## o58 statement/theorem: Theorem (geometric representation)

Theorem (geometric representation). The operators $\rho, \sigma, \tau$ are involutions on $V$, with respective eigenvectors $\mathbf{e}_{r}, \mathbf{e}_{s}, \mathbf{e}_{t}$ with simple eigenvalue −1. The products of the operators have orders corresponding to the presentation above (so $\sigma\tau$ has order $a$, etc). The operators $\rho, \sigma, \tau$ induce a representation of $\Gamma$ on $V$ which preserves $\Lambda$.
The bilinear form $\Lambda$ for the basis has matrix
$$M = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & -C & -B\\
-C & 1 & -A\\
-B & -A & 1\\
\end{pmatrix},$$
so has determinant
$$\det(M) = 1 - A^2 - B^2 - C^2 - 2ABC.$$
If $c = 2$, say, then the eigenvalues of the matrix are $1,\ 1 \pm \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}.$ The condition $\tfrac{1}{a} + \tfrac{1}{b} < \tfrac{1}{2}$ immediately forces $A^2 + B^2 > 1,$ so that $\Lambda$ must have signature $(2, 1)$. So in general $a, b, c \ge 3$. Clearly the case where all are equal to 3 is impossible. But then the determinant of the matrix is negative while its trace is positive. As a result two eigenvalues are positive and one negative, i.e. $\Lambda$ has signature $(2, 1)$. Manifestly $\rho, \sigma, \tau$ are involutions, preserving $\Lambda$ with the given −1 eigenvectors.
To check the order of the products like $\sigma\tau$, it suffices to note that:
- the reflections $\sigma$ and $\tau$ generate a finite or infinite dihedral group;
- the 2-dimensional linear span $U$ of $\mathbf{e}_{s}$ and $\mathbf{e}_{t}$ is invariant under $\sigma$ and $\tau$, with the restriction of $\Lambda$ positive-definite;
- $W$, the orthogonal complement of $U$, is negative-definite on $\Lambda$, and $\sigma$ and $\tau$ act trivially on $W$.
(1) is clear since if $\gamma = \sigma\tau$ generates a normal subgroup with $\sigma\gamma\sigma^{-1} = \gamma^{-1}$. For (2), $U$ is invariant by definition and the matrix is positive-definite since $0 < \cos\tfrac{\pi}{a} < 1.$ Since $\Lambda$ has signature $(2, 1)$, a non-zero vector $w$ in $W$ must satisfy $\Lambda(w, w) < 0$. By definition, $\sigma$ has eigenvalues 1 and −1 on $U$, so $w$ must be fixed by $\sigma$. Similarly $w$ must be fixed by $\tau$, so that (3) is proved. Finally in (1)
$$\begin{alignedat}{5}
\sigma(\mathbf{e}_s) &= -{\mathbf e}_s, &\quad \tau(\mathbf{e}_s) &= 2 \cos(\tfrac{\pi}{a}) \, \mathbf{e}_s + \mathbf{e}_t, \\[2pt]
\sigma(\mathbf{e}_t) &= 2 \cos(\tfrac{\pi}{a}) \, \mathbf{e}_s + \mathbf{e}_t, &\quad \tau(\mathbf{e}_t) &= -{\mathbf e}_t,
\end{alignedat}$$
so that, if $a$ is finite, the eigenvalues of $\sigma\tau$ are −1, $\varsigma$ and $\varsigma^{-1}$, where $\varsigma = e^{\frac{2\pi i}{a}};$ and if $a$ is infinite, the eigenvalues are −1, $X$ and $X^{-1}$, where $X = e^{2x}.$ Moreover a straightforward induction argument shows that if $\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{a}$ then
$$\begin{aligned}
(\sigma\tau)^m({\mathbf e}_s) &= \left[ \frac{\sin(2m+1)\theta}{\sin\theta} \right] {\mathbf e}_s + \left[ \frac{\sin 2m\theta}{\sin\theta} \right]{\mathbf e}_t, \\[4pt]
\tau(\sigma\tau)^m({\mathbf e}_s) &= \left[ \frac{\sin(2m+1)\theta}{\sin\theta} \right]{\mathbf e}_s + \left[ \frac{\sin(2m+2)\theta}{\sin\theta} \right] {\mathbf e}_t,
\end{aligned}$$
and if $x > 0$ then
$$\begin{aligned}
(\sigma\tau)^m({\mathbf e}_s) &= \left[ \frac{\sinh(2m+1)x}{\sinh x} \right] {\mathbf e}_s + \left[ \frac{\sinh 2mx}{\sinh x} \right] {\mathbf e}_t, \\[4pt]
\lim_{x \to 0} \ (\sigma\tau)^m(\mathbf{e}_s) &= (2m+1)\mathbf{e}_s + 2m\mathbf{e}_t; \\[12pt]
\tau(\sigma\tau)^m({\mathbf e}_s) &= \left[ \frac{\sinh(2m+1)x}{\sinh x} \right] {\mathbf e}_s + \left[ \frac{\sinh (2m+2)x}{\sinh x} \right] {\mathbf e}_t, \\[4pt]
\lim_{x \to 0} \, \tau(\sigma\tau)^m(\mathbf{e}_s) &= (2m+1)\mathbf{e}_s + (2m+2)\mathbf{e}_t.
\end{aligned}$$
Let $\Gamma_{a}$ be the dihedral subgroup of $\Gamma$ generated by $s$ and $t$, with analogous definitions for $\Gamma_{b}$ and $\Gamma_{c}$. Similarly define $\Gamma_{r}$ to be the cyclic subgroup of $\Gamma$ given by the 2-group $\{1, r\},$ with analogous definitions for $\Gamma_{s}$ and $\Gamma_{t}$. From the properties of the geometric representation, all six of these groups act faithfully on $V$. In particular
$\Gamma_{a}$ can be identified with the group generated by $\sigma$ and $\tau$; as above it decomposes explicitly as a direct sum of the 2-dimensional irreducible subspace $U$ and the 1-dimensional subspace $W$ with a trivial action. Thus there is a unique vector
$$\mathbf w = \mathbf{e}_r + \lambda\mathbf{e}_s + \mu\mathbf{e}_t$$
in $W$ satisfying $\sigma(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{w}$ and $\tau(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{w}$. Explicitly,
$$\lambda = \frac{C + AB}{1 - A^2}, \quad \mu = \frac{B + AC}{1 - A^2}.$$

## o60 remark: Remark on representations of dihedral groups

Remark on representations of dihedral groups. It is well known that, for finite-dimensional real inner product spaces, two orthogonal involutions $S$ and $T$ can be decomposed as an orthogonal direct sum of 2-dimensional or 1-dimensional invariant spaces; for example, this can be deduced from the observation of Paul Halmos and others, that the positive self-adjoint operator $(S - T)^{2}$ commutes with both $S$ and $T$. In the case above, however, where the bilinear form $\Lambda$ is no longer a positive definite inner product, different ad hoc reasoning has to be given.

## o61 statement/theorem: Theorem (Tits)

Theorem (Tits). The geometric representation of the Coxeter group is faithful.
This result was first proved by Tits in the early 1960s and first published in the text of with its numerous exercises. In the text, the fundamental chamber was introduced by an inductive argument; exercise 8 in §4 of Chapter V was expanded by Vinay Deodhar to develop a theory of positive and negative roots and thus shorten the original argument of Tits.
Let $\mathbf{X}$ be the convex cone of sums $\kappa\mathbf{e}_{r} + \lambda\mathbf{e}_{s} + \mu\mathbf{e}_{t}$ with real non-negative coefficients, not all of them zero. For $g$ in the group $\Gamma$, define
$\ell(g)$, the word length or length, to be the minimum number of reflections from $r, s, t$ required to write $g$ as an ordered composition of simple reflections. Define a positive root to be a vector $g\mathbf{e}_{r}, g\mathbf{e}_{s}$ or $g\mathbf{e}_{r}$ lying in $\mathbf{X}$, with $g$ in $\Gamma$.
It is routine to check from the definitions that
- if $\lvert \ell(gq) - \ell(g)\rvert = 1$ for a simple reflection $q$ and, if $g \ne 1$, there is always a simple reflection $q$ such that $\ell(g) = \ell(gq) + 1$;
- for $g$ and $h$ in $\Gamma$, $\ell(gh) \le \ell(g) + \ell(h)$.

## o65 statement/proposition: Proposition

Proposition. If $g$ is in $\Gamma$ and $\ell(gq) = \ell(g) \pm 1$ for a simple reflection $q$, then $g\mathbf{e}_{q}$ lies in $\pm\mathbf{X}$, and is therefore a positive or negative root, according to the sign.
Replacing $g$ by $gq$, only the positive sign needs to be considered. The assertion will be proved by induction on $\ell(g) = m$, it being trivial for $m = 0$.
Assume that $\ell(gs) = \ell(g) + 1$. If $\ell(g) = m > 0$, without less of generality it may be assumed that the minimal expression for $g$ ends with $...t$. Since $s$ and $t$ generate the dihedral group $\Gamma_{a}$, $g$ can be written as a product $g = hk$, where $k = (st)^{n}$ or $t(st)^{n}$ and $h$ has a minimal expression that ends with $...r$, but never with $s$ or $t$. This implies that $\ell(hs) = \ell(h) + 1$ and $\ell(ht) = \ell(h) + 1$. Since $\ell(h) < m$, the induction hypothesis shows that both $h\mathbf{e}_{s}, h\mathbf{e}_{t}$ lie in $\mathbf{X}$. It therefore suffices to show that $k\mathbf{e}_{s}$ has the form $\lambda\mathbf{e}_{s} + \mu\mathbf{e}_{t}$ with $\lambda, \mu \ge 0$, not both 0. But that has already been verified in the formulas above.

## o66 proof: Corollary (proof of Tits' theorem)

Corollary (proof of Tits' theorem). The geometric representation is faithful.
It suffices to show that if $g$ fixes $\mathbf{e}_{r}, \mathbf{e}_{s}, \mathbf{e}_{t}$, then $g = 1$. Considering a minimal expression for $g \ne 1$, the conditions $\ell(gq) = \ell(g) + 1$ clearly cannot be simultaneously satisfied by the three simple reflections $q$.
Note that, as a consequence of Tits' theorem, the generators (left) satisfy the conditions (right):
$$\begin{alignedat}{5}
(g &= st) \ \ \text{ s.t. } & g^a = 1,\\[4pt]
(h &= tr) \ \ \text{ s.t. } & h^b = 1,\\[4pt]
(k &= rs) \ \ \text{ s.t. } & k^c = 1, \\[4pt]
&& ghk = 1.
\end{alignedat}$$
This gives a presentation of the orientation-preserving index 2 normal subgroup $\Gamma_{1}$ of $\Gamma$. The presentation corresponds to the fundamental domain obtained by reflecting two sides of the geodesic triangle to form a geodesic parallelogram (a special case of Poincaré's polygon theorem).
Further consequences. The roots are the disjoint union of the positive roots and the negative roots. The simple reflection $q$ permutes every positive root other than $\mathbf{e}_{q}$. For $g$ in $\Gamma$, $\ell(g)$ is the number of positive roots made negative by $g$.
Fundamental domain and Tits cone.
Let $G$ be the 3-dimensional closed Lie subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}(V)$ preserving $\Lambda$. As $V$ can be identified with a 3-dimensional Lorentzian or Minkowski space with signature $(2,1)$, the group $G$ is isomorphic to the Lorentz group $O(2,1)$ and therefore $\mathrm{SL}_\pm(2,\mathbb{R}) \setminus \{\pm \,I\,\}.$ Choosing $\mathbf{e}$ to be a positive root vector in $\mathbf{X}$, the stabilizer of $\mathbf{e}$ is a maximal compact subgroup $K$ of $G$ isomorphic to $O(2)$. The homogeneous space $X = G / K$ is a symmetric space of constant negative curvature, which can be identified with the 2-dimensional hyperboloid or Lobachevsky plane $\mathfrak{H}^2$. The discrete group $\Gamma$ acts discontinuously on $G / K$: the quotient space $\Gamma \backslash G / K$ is compact if $a, b, c$ are all finite, and of finite area otherwise. Results about the Tits fundamental chamber have a natural interpretation in terms of the corresponding Schwarz triangle, which translate directly into the properties of the tessellation of the geodesic triangle through the hyperbolic reflection group $\Gamma$.
