# Splitting lemma

## o1 subject: Splitting lemma

In mathematics, and more specifically in homological algebra, the splitting lemma states that in any abelian category, the following statements are equivalent for a short exact sequence
$$0 \longrightarrow A \mathrel{\overset{q}{\longrightarrow}} B \mathrel{\overset{r}{\longrightarrow}} C \longrightarrow 0.$$

## o2 entry: Left split

Left split There exists a morphism $t: B \to A$ such that $tq$ is the identity $\operatorname{id}_{A}$ on $A$,

## o3 entry: Right split

Right split There exists a morphism $u: C \to B$ such that $ru$ is the identity $\operatorname{id}_{C}$ on $C$,

## o4 entry: Direct sum

Direct sum There is an isomorphism $h$ from $B$ to the direct sum of $A$ and $C$, such that $hq$ is the natural injection of $A$ into the direct sum, and $rh^{-1}$ is the natural projection of the direct sum onto $C$.

## o6 proof: Proof for the category of abelian groups

## Proof for the category of abelian groups
### $3. \Rightarrow 1.$ and $3. \Rightarrow 2.$
First, to show that 3. implies both 1. and 2., we assume 3. and take as $t$ the natural projection of the direct sum onto $A$, and take as $u$ the natural injection of $C$ into the direct sum.
### $1. \Rightarrow 3.$
To prove that 1. implies 3., first note that any member of B is in the set ($\ker t + \operatorname{im} q$). This follows since for all $b$ in $B$, $b = (b - qt(b)) + qt(b)$; $qt(b)$ is in $\operatorname{im} q$, and $b - qt(b)$ is in $\ker t$, since
$t(b - qt(b)) = t(b) - tqt(b) = t(b) - (tq)t(b) = t(b) - t(b) = 0.$
Next, the intersection of $\operatorname{im} q$ and $\ker t$ is 0, since if there exists $a$ in $A$ such that $q(a) = b$, and $t(b) = 0$, then $0 = tq(a) = a$; and therefore, $b = 0$.
This proves that $B$ is the direct sum of $\operatorname{im} q$ and $\ker t$. So, for all $b$ in $B$, $b$ can be uniquely identified by some $a$ in $A$, $k$ in $\ker t$, such that $b = q(a) + k$.
By exactness $\ker r = \operatorname{im} q$. The subsequence $B \longrightarrow C \longrightarrow 0$ implies that $r$ is onto; therefore for any $c$ in $C$ there exists some $b = q(a) + k$ such that $c = r(b) = r(q(a) + k) = r(k)$. Therefore, for any c in C, exists k in ker t such that c = r(k), and r(ker t) = C.
If $r(k) = 0$, then $k$ is in $\operatorname{im} q$; since the intersection of $\operatorname{im} q$ and $\ker t = 0$, then $k = 0$. Therefore, the restriction $r: \ker t \to C$ is an isomorphism; and $\ker t$ is isomorphic to $C$.
Finally, $\operatorname{im} q$ is isomorphic to $A$ due to the exactness of $0 \longrightarrow A \longrightarrow B$; so B is isomorphic to the direct sum of $A$ and $C$, which proves (3).
### $2. \Rightarrow 3.$
To show that 2. implies 3., we follow a similar argument. Any member of $B$ is in the set $\ker r + \operatorname{im} u$; since for all $b$ in $B$, $b = (b - ur(b)) + ur(b)$, which is in $\ker r + \operatorname{im} u$. The intersection of $\ker r$ and $\operatorname{im} u$ is $0$, since if $r(b) = 0$ and $u(c) = b$, then $0 = ru(c) = c$.
By exactness, $\operatorname{im} q = \ker r$, and since $q$ is an injection, $\operatorname{im} q$ is isomorphic to $A$, so $A$ is isomorphic to $\ker r$. Since $ru$ is a bijection, $u$ is an injection, and thus $\operatorname{im} u$ is isomorphic to $C$. So $B$ is again the direct sum of $A$ and $C$.
An alternative "abstract nonsense" proof of the splitting lemma may be formulated entirely in category theoretic terms.

## o11 example/counterexample: Counterexample

### Counterexample
To form a counterexample, take the smallest non-abelian group $B \cong S_{3}$, the symmetric group on three letters. Let $A$ denote the alternating subgroup, and let $C = B/A \cong \{\pm1$}. Let $q$ and $r$ denote the inclusion map and the sign map respectively, so that
$$0 \longrightarrow A \mathrel{\stackrel{q}{\longrightarrow}} B \mathrel{\stackrel{r}{\longrightarrow}} C \longrightarrow 0$$
is a short exact sequence. 3. fails, because $S_{3}$ is not abelian, but 2. holds: we may define $u: C \to B$ by mapping the generator to any two-cycle. Note for completeness that 1. fails: any map $t: B \to A$ must map every two-cycle to the identity because the map has to be a group homomorphism, while the order of a two-cycle is 2 which can not be divided by the order of the elements in A other than the identity element, which is 3 as $A$ is the alternating subgroup of $S_{3}$, or namely the cyclic group of order 3. But every permutation is a product of two-cycles, so $t$ is the trivial map, whence $tq: A \to A$ is the trivial map, not the identity.
