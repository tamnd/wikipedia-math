# Distribution of the product of two random variables

## o1 subject: Distribution of the product of two random variables

A product distribution is a probability distribution constructed as the distribution of the product of random variables having two other known distributions. Given two statistically independent random variables X and Y, the distribution of the random variable Z that is formed as the product $Z = XY$ is a product distribution.

### Proof
We first write the cumulative distribution function of $Z$ starting with its definition
$\begin{aligned}
F_Z(z) & \, \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\ \mathbb{P}(Z\leq z) \\
& = \mathbb{P}(XY\leq z) \\
& = \mathbb{P}(XY\leq z , X \geq 0) + \mathbb{P}(XY\leq z , X \leq 0)\\
& = \mathbb{P}(Y\leq z/X , X \geq 0) + \mathbb{P}(Y\geq z/X , X \leq 0)\\
& = \int^\infty_0 f_X(x) \int^{z/x}_{-\infty} f_Y(y)\, dy \,dx
+\int^0_{-\infty} f_X(x) \int^\infty_{z/x} f_Y(y)\, dy \,dx
\end{aligned}$
We find the desired probability density function by taking the derivative of both sides with respect to $z$. Since on the right hand side, $z$ appears only in the integration limits, the derivative is easily performed using the fundamental theorem of calculus and the chain rule. (Note the negative sign that is needed when the variable occurs in the lower limit of the integration.)
$\begin{aligned}
f_Z(z) & = \int^\infty_0 f_X(x) f_Y(z/x) \frac{1}{x}\,dx
-\int^0_{-\infty} f_X(x) f_Y(z/x) \frac{1}{x} \,dx \\
& = \int^\infty_0 f_X(x) f_Y(z/x) \frac{1}{|x|}\,dx
+ \int_{-\infty}^0 f_X(x) f_Y(z/x) \frac{1}{|x|} \,dx \\
& = \int^\infty_{-\infty} f_X(x) f_Y(z/x) \frac{1}{|x|}\, dx.
\end{aligned}$
where the absolute value is used to conveniently combine the two terms.

## Special cases
### Lognormal distributions
The distribution of the product of two random variables which have lognormal distributions is again lognormal. This is itself a special case of a more general set of results where the logarithm of the product can be written as the sum of the logarithms. Thus, in cases where a simple result can be found in the list of convolutions of probability distributions, where the distributions to be convolved are those of the logarithms of the components of the product, the result might be transformed to provide the distribution of the product. However this approach is only useful where the logarithms of the components of the product are in some standard families of distributions.
### Uniformly distributed independent random variables
Let $Z$ be the product of two independent variables $Z= X_1 X_2$ each uniformly distributed on the interval [0,1], possibly the outcome of a copula transformation. As noted in "Lognormal Distributions" above, PDF convolution operations in the Log domain correspond to the product of sample values in the original domain. Thus, making the transformation $u=\ln (x)$, such that $p_U(u) \, |du| = p_X(x) \, |dx|$, each variate is distributed independently on u as
$p_U (u) =\frac{ p_X(x) }{ |du/dx|} = \frac {1}{x^{-1}} = e^u, \;\; -\infty < u \le 0$.
and the convolution of the two distributions is the autoconvolution
$c(y) = \int_{u=0}^y e^u e^{y-u} du = - \int_{u=y}^0 e^y du = -y e^y , \;\; -\infty < y \le 0$
Next retransform the variable to $z=e^y$ yielding the distribution
$c_2(z) = c_Y(y)/|dz/dy| = \frac {-y e^y }{e^y} = -y = \ln (1/z)$ on the interval [0,1]
For the product of multiple (> 2) independent samples the characteristic function route is favorable. If we define $\tilde{y} = -y$ then $c(\tilde{y})$ above is a Gamma distribution of shape 1 and scale factor 1, $c(\tilde{y}) = \tilde{y} e^{-\tilde{y}}$ , and its known CF is $(1 - i t)^{-1}$. Note that $|d\tilde{y}| = |dy|$ so the Jacobian of the transformation is unity.
The convolution of $n$ independent samples from $\tilde{Y}$ therefore has CF $(1 - i t)^{-n}$ which is known to be the CF of a Gamma distribution of shape $n$:
$c_n (\tilde{y}) = \Gamma(n)^{-1} \tilde{y}^{(n-1)} e^{-\tilde{y}} = \Gamma(n)^{-1} (-y)^{(n-1)} e^y$.
Make the inverse transformation $z=e^y$ to extract the PDF of the product of the n samples:
$f_n(z) = \frac {c_n(y)}{|dz/dy|} = \Gamma(n)^{-1} \Big (-\log z \Big )^{n-1} e^y / e^y = \frac{\Big (-\log z \Big )^{n-1} }{(n-1)! \;\;\;} \;\;\; 0 < z \le 1$
The following, more conventional, derivation from Stackexchange is consistent with this result.
First of all, letting $Z_2 = X_1 X_2$ its CDF is
$\begin{aligned} F_{Z_2}(z) = \Pr \Big [ Z_2 \le z \Big ] & = \int_{x=0}^1 \Pr \Big [ X_2 \le \frac{z}{x} \Big ] f_{X_1} (x) \, dx
\\ & = \int_{x=0}^z 1 dx + \int_{x=z}^1 \frac{z}{x} \, dx
\\ & = z - z\log z, \;\; 0 < z \le 1
\end{aligned}$
The density of $z_2 \text{ is then } f(z_2) = -\log(z_2)$
Multiplying by a third independent sample gives distribution function
$\begin{aligned} F_{Z_3}(z) = \Pr \Big [Z_3 \le z \Big ] & = \int_{x=0}^1 \Pr \Big [ X_3 \le \frac{z}{x} \Big] f_{Z_2}(x) \, dx
\\ & = -\int_{x=0}^z \log(x) \, dx - \int_{x=z}^1 \frac{z}{x} \log(x) \,dx
\\ & = -z \Big (\log(z) - 1 \Big ) + \frac{1}{2}z \log^2 (z)
\end{aligned}$
Taking the derivative yields
$f_{Z_3}(z) = \frac{1}{2} \log^2 (z), \;\; 0 < z \le 1.$
The author of the note conjectures that, in general,
$f_{Z_n}(z) = \frac{( - \log z )^{n-1} }{ (n-1)! \;\;\;}, \;\; 0 < z \le 1$
The figure illustrates the nature of the integrals above. The area of the selection within the unit square and below the line z = xy, represents the CDF of z. This divides into two parts. The first is for 0 < x < z where the increment of area in the vertical slot is just equal to dx. The second part lies below the xy line, has y-height z/x, and incremental area dx z/x.
### Independent central-normal distributions
The product of two independent Normal samples follows a modified Bessel function. Let $x, y$ be independent samples from a Normal(0,1) distribution and $z=xy$.
Then
$p_Z(z) = \frac {K_0(|z|)}{\pi}, \;\;\; -\infty < z < +\infty$
The variance of this distribution could be determined, in principle, by a definite integral from Gradsheyn and Ryzhik,
$\int_0^\infty x^\mu K_\nu (ax) \, dx = 2^{\mu - 1} a^{-\mu - 1} \Gamma \Big ( \frac{1 + \mu + \nu}{2} \Big ) \Gamma \Big ( \frac{1 + \mu - \nu}{2} \Big), \;\; a>0, \;\nu + 1 \pm \mu >0$
thus
$\operatorname{E}[Z^2] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{z^2 K_0 (|z|)}{\pi} \, dz = \frac{4}{\pi} \; \Gamma^2 \Big (\frac{3}{2} \Big ) = 1$
A much simpler result, stated in a section above, is that the variance of the product of zero-mean independent samples is equal to the product of their variances. Since the variance of each Normal sample is one, the variance of the product is also one.
The product of two Gaussian samples is often confused with the product of two Gaussian PDFs. The latter simply results in a bivariate Gaussian distribution.
### Correlated central-normal distributions
The product of correlated Normal samples case was recently addressed by Nadarajaha and Pogány.
Let $X \text{, } Y$ be zero mean, unit variance, normally distributed variates with correlation coefficient $\rho \text { and let } Z = XY$
Then
$f_Z(z) = \frac{1}{\pi \sqrt{1-\rho^2} }\exp \left ( \frac{ \rho z}{ 1 - \rho^2 } \right ) K_0 \left ( \frac{|z| }{ 1 - \rho^2 } \right )$
Mean and variance: For the mean we have $\operatorname{E}[Z] = \rho$ from the definition of correlation coefficient. The variance can be found by transforming from two unit variance zero mean uncorrelated variables U, V. Let
$X = U, \;\; Y = \rho U + \sqrt{(1-\rho^2)} V$
Then X, Y are unit variance variables with correlation coefficient $\rho$ and
$(XY)^2 = U^2 \bigg ( \rho U + \sqrt {(1-\rho^2)}V \bigg )^2
= U^2 \bigg (\rho^2 U^2 + 2\rho \sqrt{1-\rho^2}UV + (1-\rho^2) V^2 \bigg )$
Removing odd-power terms, whose expectations are obviously zero, we get
$\operatorname{E}[(XY)^2] = \rho^2\operatorname{E}[U^4] + (1-\rho^2)\operatorname{E}[U^2]\operatorname{E}[V^2] = 3 \rho^2 + (1-\rho^2) = 1 + 2 \rho^2$
Since $(\operatorname{E}[Z])^2 = \rho^2$ we have
$\operatorname{Var}(Z) = \operatorname{E}[Z^2] - (\operatorname{E}[Z])^2= 1 + 2 \rho^2 - \rho^2 = 1 + \rho^2$
High correlation asymptote
In the highly correlated case, $\rho \rightarrow 1$ the product converges on the square of one sample. In this case the $K_0$ asymptote is $K_0(x) \rightarrow \sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{2x} } e^{-x} \text{ in the limit as } x = \frac {|z|}{1-\rho^2} \rightarrow \infty$
and
$\begin{aligned}
p(z) & \rightarrow \frac{1}{\pi \sqrt{1-\rho^2} } \exp \left (\frac{\rho z}{1-\rho^2 } \right)
\sqrt{\frac{\pi (1-\rho^2)}{2z} } \exp\left (-\frac{|z|}{1-\rho^2} \right) \\
& = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi z } } \exp \Bigg (\frac{ -|z| +\rho z}{(1-\rho)(1+\rho) } \Bigg) \\
& = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi z } } \exp \Bigg (\frac{ -z }{1+\rho } \Bigg), \;\; z>0 \\
& \rightarrow \frac{1}{\Gamma(\tfrac{1}{2})\sqrt{2 z } } e^{-\tfrac{ z }{2 } }, \;\; \text { as }\rho \rightarrow 1 \\
\end{aligned}$
which is a Chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom.
Multiple correlated samples. Nadarajaha et al. further show that if $Z_1, Z_2,..Z_n \text{ are } n$ iid random variables sampled from $f_Z(z)$ and $\bar{Z} = \tfrac{1}{n} \sum Z_i$ is their mean then
$f_\bar Z( z)= \frac {n^{n/2} 2^{-n/2} } { \Gamma (\frac {n}{2} ) } |z|^{n/2-1 }
\exp \left( \frac{\beta-\gamma}{2}z \right) {W}_{0,\frac{1-n}{2}}(| z |), \;\; -\infty < z < \infty.$
where W is the Whittaker function while $\beta = \frac{n}{1 - \rho}, \;\; \gamma = \frac{n}{1 + \rho}$.
Using the identity $W_{0,\nu}(x)= \sqrt {\frac{x}{\pi}}K_\nu(x/2), \;\; x \ge 0$, see for example the DLMF compilation. eqn(13.13.9), this expression can be somewhat simplified to
$f_\bar z( z)= \frac {n^{n/2} 2^{-n/2} } { \Gamma (\frac {n}{2} ) } |z|^{n/2-1 } \exp \left (\frac{\beta-\gamma}{2}z \right ) \sqrt{ \frac{ \beta+\gamma }{\pi} |z| } \;
K_{ \frac {1-n}{2}} \left ( \frac{\beta+\gamma}{2} |z| \right ), \;\; -\infty < z < \infty.$
The pdf gives the marginal distribution of a sample bivariate normal covariance, a result also shown in the Wishart Distribution article. The approximate distribution of a correlation coefficient can be found via the Fisher transformation.
Multiple non-central correlated samples. The distribution of the product of correlated non-central normal samples was derived by Cui et al. and takes the form of an infinite series of modified Bessel functions of the first kind.
Moments of product of correlated central normal samples
For a central normal distribution N(0,1) the moments are
$\operatorname{E}[X^p] =
\frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^\infty x^p \exp (-\tfrac {x^2}{2\sigma^2}) \, dx=
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if }p\text{ is odd,} \\
\sigma^p (p-1)!! & \text{if }p\text{ is even.}
\end{cases}$
where $n!!$ denotes the double factorial.
If $X,Y \sim \text{Norm}(0,1)$ are central correlated variables, the simplest bivariate case of the multivariate normal moment problem described by Kan, then
$\operatorname{E}[X^pY^q] =
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if }p+q \text{ is odd,} \\
\frac {p!q!}{2^{\tfrac{p+q}{2} } }\sum_{k=0}^t \frac { (2\rho)^{2k} }{\Big(\frac{p}{2}- k \Big)! \;
\Big(\frac{q}{2}- k \Big)! \;(2k)! } & \text{if } p \text{ and } q \text{ are even} \\
\frac {p!q!}{2^{\tfrac{p+q}{2} } }\sum_{k=0}^t \frac { (2\rho)^{2k+1} }{\Big(\frac{p-1}{2}- k \Big)! \;
\Big(\frac{q-1}{2}- k \Big)! \; (2k+1)! } & \text{if } p \text{ and } q \text{ are odd}
\end{cases}$
where
$\rho$ is the correlation coefficient and $t = \min([p,q]/2)$

### Correlated non-central normal distributions
The distribution of the product of non-central correlated normal samples was derived by Cui et al. and takes the form of an infinite series.
These product distributions are somewhat comparable to the Wishart distribution. The latter is the joint distribution of the four elements (actually only three independent elements) of a sample covariance matrix. If $x_t, y_t$ are samples from a bivariate time series then the $W = \sum_{t=1}^K \dbinom {x_t}{y_t} {\dbinom {x_t}{y_t} }^T$ is a Wishart matrix with K degrees of freedom. The product distributions above are the unconditional distribution of the aggregate of K > 1 samples of $W_{2,1}$.
### Independent complex-valued central-normal distributions
#### Product of two variables
Let $u_1, v_1, u_2, v_2$ be independent samples from a normal(0,1) distribution. Setting
$z_1 = u_1 + i v_1 \text{ and } z_2 = u_2 + i v_2 \text{ then } z_1, z_2$ are independent zero-mean complex normal samples with circular symmetry. Their complex variances are $\operatorname{Var} |z_i| = 2.$
The density functions of
$r_i \equiv |z_i| = (u_i^2 + v_i^2)^{\frac{1}{2}}, \;\;i=1,2$ are Rayleigh distributions defined as:
$f_r(r_i) = r_i e^{-r_i^2 / 2} \text{ of mean } \sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{2}} \text{ and variance}
\frac{4-\pi}{2}$
The variable $y_i \equiv r_i^2$ is clearly Chi-squared with two degrees of freedom and has PDF
$f_{y_i}(y_i) = \tfrac{1}{2} e^{-y_i/2} \text{ of mean value } 2$
Wells et al. show that the density function of $s \equiv |z_1 z_2|$ is
$f_s (s) = s K_0(s), \;\; s \ge 0$
and the cumulative distribution function of $s$ is
$P(a) = \Pr [s \le a ] = \int_{s=0}^a s K_0(s) ds = 1 - a K_1(a)$
Thus the polar representation of the product of two uncorrelated complex Gaussian samples is
$f_{s,\theta}(s,\theta) = f_s(s) p_\theta(\theta) \text{ where } p(\theta) \text{ is uniform on }[0,2\pi]$.
The first and second moments of this distribution can be found from the integral in Normal Distributions above
$m_1 = \int_0^\infty s^2 K_0 (s) \, dx = 2 \Gamma^2 ( \tfrac{3}{2} ) = 2 (\tfrac {\sqrt \pi}{2})^2 = \frac{\pi}{2}$
$m_2 = \int_0^\infty s^3 K_0 (s) \, dx = 2^2 \Gamma^2 ( \tfrac{4}{2} ) = 4$
Thus its variance is $\operatorname{Var} (s) = m_2 - m_1^2 = 4 - \frac{\pi^2}{4}$.
Further, the density of $z \equiv s^2 = {|r_1 r_2|}^2 = {|r_1 |}^2 {| r_2|}^2 = y_1 y_2$ corresponds to the product of two independent Chi-square samples $y_i$ each with two DoF. Writing these as scaled Gamma distributions $f_y(y_i) = \tfrac{1}{\theta \Gamma(1) } e^{-y_i/\theta } \text{ with } \theta = 2$ then, from the Gamma products below, the density of the product is
$f_Z(z) = \tfrac{1}{2} K_0(\sqrt{z}) \text{ with expectation } \operatorname{E}(z) = 4$
#### Sum of the product of two variables
Let $u_1, v_1, u_2, v_2, \ldots, u_{2N}, v_{2N},$ be $4N$ independent samples from a normal(0,1) distribution.Setting $z_1 = u_1 + i v_1, z_2 = u_2 + i v_2, \ldots, \text{ and } z_{2N} = u_{2N} + i v_{2N},$ then $z_1, z_2, \ldots, z_{2N}$are independent zero-mean complex normal samples with circular symmetry.
Let of $s \equiv \sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{2i-1}z_{2i}$ , Heliot et al. show that the joint density function of the real and imaginary parts of $s$ , denoted $s_\textrm{R}$ and $s_\textrm{I}$, respectively, is given by
$p_{s_{\textrm{R}},s_{\textrm{I}}}(s_{\textrm{R}},s_{\textrm{I}})=\frac{2\left(s_{\textrm{R}}^2+s_{\textrm{I}}^2\right)^{\frac{N-1}{2}}}{\pi\Gamma(n)\sigma_s^{N+1}}K_{n-1}\!\left(\!2\frac{\sqrt{s_{\textrm{R}}^2+s_{\textrm{I}}^2}}{\sigma_s}\right),$ where $\sigma_s$is the standard deviation of $s$. Note that $\sigma_s=1$ if all the $u_i, v_i$variables are normal(0,1).
Besides, they also prove that the density function of the magnitude of $s$, $|s|$, is
$p_{|s|}(s)=\frac{4}{\Gamma(N)\sigma_s^{N+1}}s^{N}K_{N-1}\left(\frac{2s}{\sigma_s}\right),$ where $s=\sqrt{s_{\textrm{R}}^2+s_{\textrm{I}}^2}$.
The first moment of this distribution, i.e. the mean of $|s|$, can be expressed as
$E\{|s|\}=\sqrt{\pi}\sigma_s\frac{\Gamma(N+\frac{1}{2})}{2\Gamma(N)},$ which further simplifies as $E\{|s|\}\sim\frac{\sigma_s\sqrt{\pi N}}{2},$when $N$is asymptotically large (i.e., $N\rightarrow\infty$) .
#### Sum of the pair product of two variables
There are $N$ pairs of real product variables $\{Y_1, Y_2, \dots, Y_{2N}\}$, where the variables within each pair are independent and identically distributed, but the variances are different among pairs. Two variables in each pair are extracted from one part (real / imaginary) of a product of two complex Gaussian-distributed variables.
Let $A\sim \mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma_A^2)$ and $B\sim \mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma_B^2)$. They are independent. The product $AB = \text{Re}(A)\text{Re}(B) - \text{Im}(A)\text{Im}(B) +i[\text{Re}(A)\text{Im}(B)+\text{Im}(A)\text{Re}(B)]$, where $\text{Re}\{\cdot\}$ and $\text{Im}\{\cdot\}$ denote real and imaginary parts, respectively. The terms $\{\text{Re}(A)\text{Re}(B), - \text{Im}(A)\text{Im}(B)\}$ can be extracted as two variables $\{Y_{2n-1}, Y_{2n}\}$ in a pair stated above, with the zero mean and variance $b_n = \sigma_A^2\sigma_B^2$ for each variable of n-th pair. The same format occurs in the imaginary part.
Junbo show that the probability density function of the sum of $N$ pairs of real independent and identically distributed product variables $y=\sum_{k=1}^{2N}y_k$ with zero mean and the variance $b_n$ for the n-th pair is
$f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{b_n^\frac{2N-3}{2}e^{-\frac{|y|}{\sqrt{b_n}}}}{\prod_{n'\neq n}^{N}(b_n-b_{n'})}, b_n\neq b_{n'}, N\geq2$
when $N=1$, the pdf is obtained without the denominator.
#### Sum of the pair product of two variables plus a Gaussian distributed variable
The probability density function of the sum of $N$ pairs of real independent and identically distributed product variables with zero mean and the variance $b_n$ for the n-th pair, plus a real Gaussian distributed variable $z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_z^2)$, $x=y+z$, is
$f_X(x) = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{b_n^\frac{2N-3}{2}}{\prod_{n'\neq n}^{N}(b_n-b_{n'})}\bigg[e^{-\frac{x}{\sqrt{b_n}}+\frac{\sigma_z^2}{2b_n}}\text{erfc}\bigg(\frac{-\sqrt{b_n}x+\sigma_z^2}{\sqrt{2\sigma_z^2b_n}}\bigg)+e^{\frac{x}{\sqrt{b_n}}+\frac{\sigma_z^2}{2b_n}}\text{erfc}\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{b_n}x+\sigma_z^2}{\sqrt{2\sigma_z^2b_n}}\bigg)\bigg]$
where $b_n\neq b_{n'}$, $N\geq2$ and $\text{erfc}\{\cdot\}$ denotes the complementary error function. When $N=1$, the pdf is obtained by setting the term $\prod_{n'\neq n}^{N}(b_n-b_{n'})=1$.
### Independent complex-valued noncentral normal distributions
The product of non-central independent complex Gaussians is described by O’Donoughue and Moura and forms a double infinite series of modified Bessel functions of the first and second types.
### Gamma distributions
The product of two independent Gamma samples, $z = x_1 x_2$, defining $\Gamma(x;k_i,\theta_i) = \frac {x^{k_i-1}e^{-x/\theta_i}}{\Gamma(k_i) \theta_i^{k_i} }$, follows
$\begin{aligned} p_Z(z) & = \frac {2}{\Gamma(k_1) \Gamma(k_2) } \frac { z ^{\frac {k_1 + k_2}{2 } -1} } { (\theta_1 \theta_2) ^{\frac {k_1 + k_2}{2 }} }
K_{k_1 -k_2 } \left( 2 \sqrt{ \frac {z}{\theta_1 \theta_2} } \right) \\ \\
& = \frac {2}{\Gamma(k_1) \Gamma(k_2) } \frac {y^{\frac {k_1 + k_2}{2 }-1}}
{\theta_1 \theta_2 } K_{k_1 -k_2 } \left(2 \sqrt y \right) \text { where } y = \frac {z}{\theta_1 \theta_2}\\
\end{aligned}$
### Beta distributions
Nagar et al. define a correlated bivariate beta distribution
$f(x,y) = \frac {x^{a-1}y^{b-1}(1-x)^{b+c-1}(1-y)^{a+c-1} } {B(a,b,c) (1-xy)^{a+b+c} }, \;\;\;0< x,y <1$
where
$B(a,b,c) = \frac {\Gamma(a) \Gamma(b) \Gamma(c)}{ \Gamma(a+b+c) }$
Then the pdf of Z = XY is given by
$f_Z(z) = \frac {B(a+c,b+c) z^{a-1} (1-z)^{c-1} } {B(a,b,c) }
{_2F_1} (a+c,a+c; a+b+2c; 1-z), \;\;\; 0< z <1$
where ${_2F_1}$ is the Gauss hypergeometric function defined by the Euler integral
${_2F_1}(a,b,c,z) = \frac {\Gamma(c) }{\Gamma(a) \Gamma (c-a) }
\int_0^1 v^{a-1} (1-v)^{c-a-1} (1-vz)^{-b} \, dv$
Arnold and Ng describe a more generalised bivariate Beta distribution consistent with the above and extend it to the bivariate Beta-prime case by variable transformations, though they find no closed form version for the Beta-prime distribution except in special cases.
The Dirichlet Distribution of order $K \geq 2$ with parameters $\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_K$ is the product of independent Beta distributions and has probability density function
$$f \left(x_1,\ldots, x_{K}; \alpha_1,\ldots, \alpha_K \right) = \frac{1}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha_1,\ldots, \alpha_K)} \prod_{i=1}^K x_i^{\alpha_i - 1}$$
### Uniform and gamma distributions
The distribution of the product of a random variable having a uniform distribution on (0,1) with a random variable having a gamma distribution with shape parameter equal to 2, is an exponential distribution. A more general case of this concerns the distribution of the product of a random variable having a beta distribution with a random variable having a gamma distribution: for some cases where the parameters of the two component distributions are related in a certain way, the result is again a gamma distribution but with a changed shape parameter.
The K-distribution is an example of a non-standard distribution that can be defined as a product distribution (where both components have a gamma distribution).
### Gamma and Pareto distributions
The product of n Gamma and m Pareto independent samples was derived by Nadarajah.
