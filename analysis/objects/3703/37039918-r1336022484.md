# Group structure and the axiom of choice

## o1 subject: Group structure and the axiom of choice

In mathematics a group is a set together with a binary operation on the set (usually called multiplication) that obeys the group axioms. The axiom of choice is an axiom of ZFC set theory which in one form states that every set can be wellordered.

## o3 section: A group structure implies the axiom of choice

## o4 statement/axiom: A group structure implies the axiom of choice

## A group structure implies the axiom of choice
In this section it is assumed that every set $X$ can be endowed with a group structure $(X, \bullet)$.
Let $X$ be a set. Let $\aleph(X)$ be the Hartogs number of $X$. This is the least cardinal number such that there is no injection from $\aleph(X)$ into $X$. It exists without the assumption of the axiom of choice. Assume here for technical simplicity of proof that $X$ has no ordinal. Let $\bullet$ denote multiplication in the group $(X \cup \aleph(X), \bullet)$.
For any $x \in X$ there is an $\alpha \in \aleph(X)$ such that $x \bullet \alpha \in \aleph(X)$. Suppose not. Then there is an $y \in X$ such that $y \bullet \alpha \in X$ for all $\alpha \in \aleph(X)$. But by elementary group theory, the $y \bullet \alpha$ are all different as α ranges over $\aleph(X)$ (i). Thus such a $y$ gives an injection from $\aleph(X)$ into $X$. This is impossible since $\aleph(X)$ is a cardinal such that no injection into $X$ exists.
Now define a map $j$ of $X$ into $\aleph(X) \times \aleph(X)$ endowed with the lexicographical wellordering by sending $x \in X$ to the least $(\alpha, \beta) \in \aleph(X) \times \aleph(X)$ such that $x \bullet \alpha = \beta$. By the above reasoning the map $j$ exists and is unique since least elements of subsets of wellordered sets are unique. It is, by elementary group theory, injective.
Finally, define a wellordering on $X$ by $x < y$ if $j(x) < j(y)$. It follows that every set $X$ can be wellordered and thus that the axiom of choice is true.
For the crucial property expressed in (i) above to hold, and hence the whole proof, it is sufficient for $X$ to be a cancellative magma, e.g. a quasigroup. The cancellation property is enough to ensure that the $y \bullet \alpha$ are all different.

## o9 statement/axiom: The axiom of choice implies a group structure

## The axiom of choice implies a group structure
Any nonempty finite set has a group structure as a cyclic group generated by any element. Under the assumption of the axiom of choice, every infinite set $X$ is equipotent with a unique cardinal number $\lvert X\rvert$ which equals an aleph. Using the axiom of choice, one can show that for any family $S$ of sets $\lvert \bigcup S\rvert \le \lvert S\rvert \times \sup \{ |s| : s \in S\}$ (A). Moreover, by Tarski's theorem on choice, another equivalent of the axiom of choice, $\lvert X\rvert^{n} = \lvert X\rvert$ for all finite $n$ (B).
Let $X$ be an infinite set and let $F$ denote the set of all finite subsets of $X$. There is a natural multiplication $\bullet$ on $F$. For $f, g \in F$, let $f \bullet g = f \Delta g$, where $\Delta$ denotes the symmetric difference. This turns $(F, \bullet)$ into a group with the empty set, $\text{Ø}$, being the identity and every element being its own inverse; $f \Delta f = \text{Ø}$. The associative property, i.e. $(f \Delta g) \Delta h = f \Delta (g \Delta h)$ is verified using basic properties of union and set difference. Thus $F$ is a group with multiplication $\Delta$.
Any set that can be put into bijection with a group becomes a group via the bijection. It will be shown that $\lvert X\rvert = \lvert F\rvert$, and hence a one-to-one correspondence between $X$ and the group $(F, \bullet)$ exists. For $n = 0,1,2, ...$, let $F_{n}$ be the subset of $F$ consisting of all subsets of cardinality exactly $n$. Then $F$ is the disjoint union of the $F_{n}$. The number of subsets of $X$ of cardinality $n$ is at most $\lvert X\rvert^{n}$ because every subset with $n$ elements is an element of the $n$-fold cartesian product $X^{n}$ of $X$. So $\lvert F_{n}\rvert \le \lvert X\rvert^{n} = \lvert X\rvert$ for all $n$ (C) by (B).
Putting these results together it is seen that $\lvert F\rvert = \lvert \bigcup_{n \in \omega}F_{n}\rvert \le \aleph_{0} \cdot \lvert X\rvert = \lvert X\rvert$ by (A) and (C). Also, $\lvert F\rvert \ge \lvert X\rvert$, since $F$ contains all singletons. Thus, $\lvert X\rvert \le \lvert F\rvert$ and $\lvert F\rvert \le \lvert X\rvert$, so, by the Schröder–Bernstein theorem, $\lvert F\rvert = \lvert X\rvert$. This means precisely that there is a bijection $j$ between $X$ and $F$. Finally, for $x, y \in X$ define $x \bullet y = j^{-1}(j(x) \Delta j(y))$. This turns $(X, \bullet)$ into a group. Hence every set admits a group structure.
