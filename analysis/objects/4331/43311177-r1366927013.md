# Closed-subgroup theorem

## o1 subject: Closed-subgroup theorem

In mathematics, the closed-subgroup theorem (sometimes referred to as Cartan's theorem) is a theorem in the theory of Lie groups. It states that if $H$ is a closed subgroup of a Lie group $G$, then $H$ is an embedded Lie group with the smooth structure (and hence the group topology) agreeing with the embedding.
One of several results known as Cartan's theorem, it was first published in 1930 by Élie Cartan, who was inspired by John von Neumann's 1929 proof of a special case for groups of linear transformations.

## o10 statement/lemma: Lemma

Take a small neighborhood $U$ of the origin in $\mathfrak{g}$ such that the exponential map sends $U$ diffeomorphically onto some neighborhood $V$ of the identity in $G$, and let $\log: V \to U$ be the inverse of the exponential map. Then there is some smaller neighborhood $W \subset V$ such that if $h$ belongs to $W \cap H$, then $\log(h)$ belongs to $\mathfrak{h}$.

## Applications
Because of the conclusion of the theorem, some authors chose to define linear Lie groups or matrix Lie groups as closed subgroups of $\operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$ or $\operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{C})$. In this setting, one proves that every element of the group sufficiently close to the identity is the exponential of an element of the Lie algebra. (The proof is practically identical to the proof of the closed subgroup theorem presented below.) It follows every closed subgroup is an embedded submanifold of $\operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{C})$
The homogeneous space construction theorem
If $H \subset G$ is a closed Lie subgroup, then $G/H$, the left coset space, has a unique real-analytic manifold structure such that the quotient map $\pi:G \to G/H$ is an analytic submersion. The left action given by $g_{1} \cdot (g_{2}H) = (g_{1}g_{2})H$ turns $G/H$ into a homogeneous $G$-space.
The closed subgroup theorem now simplifies the hypotheses considerably, a priori widening the class of homogeneous spaces. Every closed subgroup yields a homogeneous space.
In a similar way, the closed subgroup theorem simplifies the hypothesis in the following theorem.
If $X$ is a set with transitive group action and the isotropy group or stabilizer of a point $x \in X$ is a closed Lie subgroup, then $X$ has a unique smooth manifold structure such that the action is smooth.

## o33 statement/generalization: Converse

## Converse
An embedded Lie subgroup $H \subset G$ is closed so a subgroup is an embedded Lie subgroup if and only if it is closed. Equivalently, $H$ is an embedded Lie subgroup if and only if its group topology equals its relative topology.

## Proof
The proof is given for matrix groups with $G = \operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$ for concreteness and relative simplicity, since matrices and their exponential mapping are easier concepts than in the general case. Historically, this case was proven first, by John von Neumann in 1929, and inspired Cartan to prove the full closed subgroup theorem in 1930. The proof for general $G$ is formally identical, except that elements of the Lie algebra are left invariant vector fields on $G$ and the exponential mapping is the time one flow of the vector field. If $H \subset G$ with $G$ closed in $\operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$, then $H$ is closed in $\operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$, so the specialization to $\operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$ instead of arbitrary $G \subset \operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$ matters little.
### Proof of the key lemma
We begin by establishing the key lemma stated in the "overview" section above.
Endow $\mathbf{g}$ with an inner product (e.g., the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product), and let $\mathbf{h}$ be the Lie algebra of $H$ defined as $\mathbf{h} = \{X \in M_{n}(\mathbf{R}) = \mathbf{g}\mid e^{tX} \in H \forall t \in \mathbf{R}\}$. Let $\mathbf{s} = \{S \in \mathbf{g}\mid (S, T) = 0 \forall T \in \mathbf{h}\}$, the orthogonal complement of $\mathbf{h}$. Then $\mathbf{g}$ decomposes as the direct sum $\mathbf{g} = \mathbf{s} \oplus \mathbf{h}$, so each $X \in \mathbf{g}$ is uniquely expressed as $X = S + T$ with $S \in \mathbf{s}, T \in \mathbf{h}$.
Define a map $\Phi : \mathbf{g} \to \operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$ by $(S, T) \mapsto e^{S}e^{T}$. Expand the exponentials,
$$\Phi(S,T) = e^{tS}e^{tT} = I + tS + tT + O(t^2),$$
and the pushforward or differential at $0$, $\Phi_{\ast}(S, T) = \tfrac{d}{dt}\Phi(tS, tT)|_{t = 0}$ is seen to be $S + T$, i.e. $\Phi_{\ast} = \operatorname{Id}$, the identity. The hypothesis of the inverse function theorem is satisfied with $\Phi$ analytic, and thus there are open sets $U_{1} \subset \mathbf{g}, V_{1} \subset \operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$ with $0 \in U_{1}$ and $I \in V_{1}$ such that $\Phi$ is a real-analytic bijection from $U_{1}$ to $V_{1}$ with analytic inverse. It remains to show that $U_{1}$ and $V_{1}$ contain open sets $U$ and $V$ such that the conclusion of the theorem holds.
Consider a countable neighborhood basis $\mathrm{B}$ at $0 \in \mathbf{g}$, linearly ordered by reverse inclusion with $B_{1} \subset U_{1}$. Suppose for the purpose of obtaining a contradiction that for all $i$, $\Phi(B_{i}) \cap H$ contains an element $h_{i}$ that is not on the form $h_{i} = e^{T_{i}}, T_{i} \in \mathbf{h}$. Then, since $\Phi$ is a bijection on the $B_{i}$, there is a unique sequence $X_{i} = S_{i} + T_{i}$, with $0 \ne S_{i} \in \mathbf{s}$ and $T_{i} \in \mathbf{h}$ such that $X_{i} \in B_{i}$ converging to $0$ because $\mathrm{B}$ is a neighborhood basis, with $e^{S_{i}}e^{T_{i}} = h_{i}$. Since $e^{T_{i}} \in H$ and $h_{i} \in H$, $e^{S_{i}} \in H$ as well.
Normalize the sequence in $\mathbf{s}$, $Y_{i} = \tfrac{S_{i}}{||S_{i}||}$. It takes its values in the unit sphere in $\mathbf{s}$ and since it is compact, there is a convergent subsequence converging to $Y \in \mathbf{s}$. The index $i$ henceforth refers to this subsequence. It will be shown that $e^{tY} \in H, \forall t \in \mathbf{R}$. Fix $t$ and choose a sequence $m_{i}$ of integers such that $m_{i} ||S_{i}|| \to t$ as $i \to \infty$. For example, $m_{i}$ such that $m_{i} ||S_{i}|| \le t \le (m_{i} + 1) ||S_{i}||$ will do, as $S_{i} \to 0$. Then
$$(e^{S_i})^{m_i} = e^{m_iS_i} = e^{m_i\|S_i\| Y_i} \rightarrow e^{t Y}.$$
Since $H$ is a group, the left hand side is in $H$ for all $i$. Since $H$ is closed, $e^{tY} \in H, \forall t$, hence $Y \in \mathbf{h}$. This is a contradiction. Hence, for some $i$ the sets $U = \mathrm{B}_{i}$ and $V = \Phi(\mathrm{B}_{i})$ satisfy $e^{U\cap\mathbf{h}} = H \cap V$ and the exponential restricted to the open set $(U \cap \mathbf{h}) \subset \mathbf{h}$ is in analytic bijection with the open set $\Phi(U) \cap H \subset H$. This proves the lemma.
### Proof of the theorem
For $j \ge i$, the image in $H$ of $B_{j}$ under $\Phi$ form a neighborhood basis at $I$. This is, by the way it is constructed, a neighborhood basis both in the group topology and the relative topology. Since multiplication in $G$ is analytic, the left and right translates of this neighborhood basis by a group element $g \in G$ gives a neighborhood basis at $g$. These bases restricted to $H$ gives neighborhood bases at all $h \in H$. The topology generated by these bases is the relative topology. The conclusion is that the relative topology is the same as the group topology.
Next, construct coordinate charts on $H$. First define $\varphi_{1} : e^{(U)} \subset G \to \mathbf{g}, g \mapsto \log(g)$. This is an analytic bijection with analytic inverse. Furthermore, if $h \in H$, then $\varphi_{1}(h) \in \mathbf{h}$. By fixing a basis for $\mathbf{g} = \mathbf{h} \oplus \mathbf{s}$ and identifying $\mathbf{g}$ with $\mathbf{R}^{n}$, then in these coordinates $\varphi_{1}(h) = (x_{1}(h), ..., x_{m}(h), 0, ..., 0)$, where $m$ is the dimension of $\mathbf{h}$. This shows that $(e^{U}, \varphi_{1})$ is a slice chart. By translating the charts obtained from the countable neighborhood basis used above one obtains slice charts around every point in $H$. This shows that $H$ is an embedded submanifold of $G$.
Moreover, multiplication $m$, and inversion $i$ in $H$ are analytic since these operations are analytic in $G$ and restriction to a submanifold (embedded or immersed) with the relative topology again yield analytic operations $m : H \times H \to G$ and $i : H \times H \to G$. But since $H$ is embedded, $m : H \times H \to H$ and $i : H \times H \to H$ are analytic as well.
