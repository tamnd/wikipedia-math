## o18 statement/theorem: Invalid converses of the theorems

### Invalid converses of the theorems
The converses of Theorems 1, 3 and 4 do not hold, i.e., the existence of iterated limits, even if they are equal, does not imply the existence of the double limit. A counter-example is
$f(x,y) = \frac{xy}{x^2+y^2}$
near the point (0, 0). On one hand,
$\lim_{x \to 0} \lim_{y \to 0} f(x,y) = \lim_{y \to 0} \lim_{x \to 0} f(x,y) = 0.$
On the other hand, the double limit $\lim_{\begin{smallmatrix}
x \to 0 \\ y \to 0
\end{smallmatrix}} f(x, y)$ does not exist. This can be seen by taking the limit along the path (x, y) = (t, t) → (0,0), which gives
$\lim_{\begin{smallmatrix}
t \to 0 \\ t \to 0
\end{smallmatrix}} f(t,t) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{t^2}{t^2+t^2} = \frac{1}{2},$
and along the path (x, y) = (t, t2) → (0,0), which gives
$\lim_{\begin{smallmatrix}
t \to 0 \\ t^2 \to 0
\end{smallmatrix}} f(t,t^2) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{t^3}{t^2+t^4} = 0.$

## o20 statement/theorem: Moore–Osgood theorem for interchanging limits

## Moore–Osgood theorem for interchanging limits
In the examples above, we may see that interchanging limits may or may not give the same result. A sufficient condition for interchanging limits is given by the Moore–Osgood theorem. The essence of the interchangeability depends on uniform convergence.
### Interchanging limits of sequences
The following theorem allows us to interchange two limits of sequences.
Theorem 5. If $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_{n,m} = b_m$ uniformly (in m), and $\lim_{m \to \infty} a_{n,m} = c_n$ for each large n, then both $\lim_{m \to \infty} b_m$ and $\lim_{n \to \infty} c_n$ exists and are equal to the double limit, i.e.,
$\lim_{m \to \infty} \lim_{n \to \infty} a_{n,m} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \lim_{m \to \infty} a_{n,m} = \lim_{\begin{smallmatrix}
n \to \infty \\ m \to \infty
\end{smallmatrix}} a_{n,m}$.
Proof. By the uniform convergence, for any $\varepsilon > 0$ there exist $N_1(\varepsilon)\in\mathbb{N}$ such that for all $m \in \mathbb{N}$, $n, k > N_1$ implies $\left| a_{n,m} - a_{k,m} \right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$.
As $m \to \infty$, we have $\left|c_n - c_k \right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$, which means that $c_n$ is a Cauchy sequence which converges to a limit $L$. In addition, as $k \to \infty$, we have $\left|c_n - L\right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$.
On the other hand, if we take $k \to \infty$ first, we have $\left| a_{n,m} - b_m \right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$.
By the pointwise convergence, for any $\varepsilon > 0$ and $n > N_1$, there exist $N_2(\varepsilon, n) \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $m > N_2$ implies $\left| a_{n,m} - c_n \right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$.
Then for that fixed $n$, $m > N_2$ implies $\left| b_m - L \right| \le \left| b_m - a_{n,m} \right| + \left| a_{n,m} - c_n \right| + \left| c_n - L \right| \le \varepsilon$.
This proves that $\lim_{m \to \infty}b_m = L = \lim_{n \to \infty}c_n$.
Also, by taking $N = \max\{N_1, N_2\}$, we see that this limit also equals $\lim_{\begin{smallmatrix}
n \to \infty \\ m \to \infty
\end{smallmatrix}} a_{n,m}$.
A corollary is about the interchangeability of infinite sum.
Corollary 5.1. If $\sum^\infty_{n=1} a_{n,m}$ converges uniformly (in m), and $\sum^\infty_{m =1} a_{n,m}$ converges for each large n, then $\sum^\infty_{m=1} \sum^\infty_{n=1} a_{n,m} = \sum^\infty_{n=1} \sum^\infty_{m=1} a_{n,m}$.
Proof. Direct application of Theorem 5 on $S_{k,\ell} = \sum_{m=1}^k \sum_{n=1}^\ell a_{n,m}$.
### Interchanging limits of functions
Similar results hold for multivariable functions.
Theorem 6. If $\lim_{x \to a} f(x,y) = g(y)$ uniformly (in y) on $Y \smallsetminus\{b\}$, and $\lim_{y \to b} f(x,y) = h(x)$ for each x near a, then both $\lim_{y \to b} g(y)$ and $\lim_{x \to a} h(x)$ exists and are equal to the double limit, i.e.,
$\lim_{y \to b} \lim_{x \to a} f(x,y) = \lim_{x \to a} \lim_{y \to b} f(x,y) = \lim_{\begin{smallmatrix}
x \to a \\ y \to b
\end{smallmatrix}} f(x,y).$
The a and b here can possibly be infinity.
Proof. By the existence uniform limit, for any $\varepsilon > 0$ there exist $\delta_1(\varepsilon) > 0$ such that for all $y \in Y \smallsetminus \{b\}$, $0 <\left|
x - a \right| < \delta_1$ and $0 <\left|
w - a \right| < \delta_1$ implies $\left| f(x,y) - f(w,y) \right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$.
As $y \to b$, we have $\left|h(x) - h(w) \right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$. By Cauchy criterion, $\lim_{x\to a}h(x)$ exists and equals a number $L$. In addition, as $w \to a$, we have $\left|h(x) - L\right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$.
On the other hand, if we take $w \to a$ first, we have $\left| f(x,y) - g(y) \right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$.
By the existence of pointwise limit, for any $\varepsilon > 0$ and $x$ near $a$, there exist $\delta_2(\varepsilon, x) > 0$ such that $0 < \left| y - b \right| < \delta_2$ implies $\left| f(x,y) - h(x) \right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$.
Then for that fixed $x$, $0 < \left| y - b \right| < \delta_2$ implies $\left| g(y) - L \right| \le \left| g(y) - f(x,y) \right| + \left| f(x,y) - h(x) \right| + \left| h(x) - L \right| \le \varepsilon$.
This proves that $\lim_{y \to b}g(y) = L = \lim_{x \to a}h(x).$
Also, by taking $\delta = \min\{\delta_1, \delta_2\}$, we see that this limit also equals $\lim_{\begin{smallmatrix}
x \to a \\ y \to b
\end{smallmatrix}} f(x,y)$.
Note that this theorem does not imply the existence of $\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)} f(x,y)$. A counter-example is $f(x,y) = \begin{cases}
1 & \text{for } xy \ne 0 \\
0 & \text{for } xy = 0
\end{cases}$ near (0,0).
### Interchanging limits of sequences of functions
An important variation of Moore–Osgood theorem is specifically for sequences of functions.
Theorem 7. If $\lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x) = f(x)$ uniformly (in x) on $X\smallsetminus\{a\}$, and $\lim_{x \to a} f_n(x) = L_n$ for each large n, then both $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ and $\lim_{n \to \infty} L_n$ exists and are equal, i.e.,
$\lim_{n \to \infty} \lim_{x \to a} f_n(x) = \lim_{x \to a} \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x)$.
The a here can possibly be infinity.
Proof. By the uniform convergence, for any $\varepsilon > 0$ there exist $N(\varepsilon)\in\mathbb{N}$ such that for all $x \in D\smallsetminus\{a\}$, $n, m > N$ implies $\left| f_n(x) - f_m(x) \right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$.
As $x \to a$, we have $\left|L_n - L_m \right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$, which means that $L_n$ is a Cauchy sequence which converges to a limit $L$. In addition, as $m \to \infty$, we have $\left|L_n - L\right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$.
On the other hand, if we take $m \to \infty$ first, we have $\left| f_n(x) - f(x) \right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$.
By the existence of pointwise limit, for any $\varepsilon > 0$ and $n > N$, there exist $\delta(\varepsilon, n) > 0$ such that $0 < \left| x - a \right| < \delta$ implies $\left| f_n(x) - L_n \right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$.
Then for that fixed $n$, $0 < \left| x - a \right| < \delta$ implies $\left| f(x) - L \right| \le \left| f(x) - f_n(x) \right| + \left| f_n(x) - L_n \right| + \left| L_n - L \right| \le \varepsilon$.
This proves that $\lim_{x \to a}f(x) = L = \lim_{n \to \infty}L_n$.
A corollary is the continuity theorem for uniform convergence as follows:
Corollary 7.1. If $\lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x) = f(x)$ uniformly (in x) on $X$, and $f_n(x)$ are continuous at $x=a \in X$, then $f(x)$ is also continuous at $x=a$.
In other words, the uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous.
Proof. By Theorem 7, $\lim_{x\to a}f(x) = \lim_{x\to a} \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \lim_{x\to a} f_n(x) = \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(a) = f(a)$.
Another corollary is about the interchangeability of limit and infinite sum.
Corollary 7.2. If $\sum^\infty_{n=0} f_n(x)$ converges uniformly (in x) on $X \smallsetminus \{a\}$, and $\lim_{x \to a} f_n(x)$ exists for all n, then $\lim_{x \to a} \sum^\infty_{n=0} f_n(x) = \sum^\infty_{n=0} \lim_{x \to a} f_n(x)$.
Proof. Direct application of Theorem 7 on $S_k(x) = \sum_{n=0}^k f_n(x)$ near $x = a$.

## Applications
### Sum of infinite entries in a matrix
Consider a matrix with infinitely many entries
$\left[ \begin{array}{rrrrr}
1 & -1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots \\
0 & 1 & -1 & 0 & \cdots \\
0 & 0 & 1 & -1 & \cdots \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots
\end{array} \right].$
Suppose we would like to find the sum of all entries. If we sum it column by column first, we will find that the first column gives 1, while all others give 0. Hence the sum of all columns is 1. However, if we sum it row by row first, it will find that all rows give 0. Hence the sum of all rows is 0.
The explanation for this paradox is that the vertical sum to infinity and horizontal sum to infinity are two limiting processes that cannot be interchanged. Let $S_{n,m}$ be the sum of entries up to entries (n, m). Then we have $\lim_{m \to \infty} \lim_{n \to \infty} S_{n,m} = 1$, but $\lim_{n \to \infty} \lim_{m \to \infty} S_{n,m} = 0$. In this case, the double limit $\lim_{\begin{smallmatrix}
n \to \infty \\ m \to \infty
\end{smallmatrix}} S_{n,m}$ does not exist, and thus this problem is not well-defined.
### Integration over unbounded interval
By the integration theorem for uniform convergence, once we have $\lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x)$ converges uniformly on $X$, the limit in n and an integration over a bounded interval $[a, b] \subseteq X$ can be interchanged:
$\lim_{n\to \infty} \int_a^b f_n(x) \, \mathrm{d}x = \int_a^b \lim_{n\to \infty} f_n(x) \, \mathrm{d}x$.
However, such a property may fail for an improper integral over an unbounded interval $[a, \infty) \subseteq X$. In this case, one may rely on the Moore–Osgood theorem.
Consider $L = \int_0^\infty \frac{x^2}{e^x - 1} \, \mathrm{d}x = \lim_{b\to \infty} \int_0^b\frac{x^2}{e^x - 1} \, \mathrm{d}x$ as an example.
We first expand the integrand as $\frac{x^2}{e^x - 1} = \frac{x^2 e^{-x}}{1- e^{-x}} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty x^2 e^{-kx}$ for $x \in [0, \infty)$. (Here x=0 is a limiting case.)
One can prove by calculus that for $x \in [0, \infty)$ and $k \ge 1$, we have $x^2 e^{-kx} \le \frac{4}{e^2 k^2}$. By Weierstrass M-test, $\sum_{k=0}^\infty x^2 e^{-kx}$ converges uniformly on $[0, \infty)$.
Then by the integration theorem for uniform convergence, $L = \lim_{b \to \infty} \int_0^b \sum_{k=0}^\infty x^2 e^{-kx} \, \mathrm{d}x = \lim_{b \to \infty} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \int_0^b x^2 e^{-kx} \, \mathrm{d}x$.
To further interchange the limit $\lim_{b \to \infty}$ with the infinite summation $\sum_{k=0}^\infty$, the Moore–Osgood theorem requires the infinite series to be uniformly convergent.
Note that $\int_0^b x^2 e^{-kx} \, \mathrm{d}x \le \int_0^\infty x^2 e^{-kx} \, \mathrm{d}x = \frac{2}{k^3}$. Again, by Weierstrass M-test, $\sum_{k=0}^\infty \int_0^b x^2 e^{-kx} \, \mathrm{d}x$ converges uniformly on $[0, \infty)$.
Then by the Moore–Osgood theorem, $L = \lim_{b \to \infty} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \int_0^b x^2 e^{-kx} \, \mathrm{d}x = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \lim_{b \to \infty} \int_0^b x^2 e^{-kx} \,\mathrm{d}x = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{2}{k^3} = 2 \zeta(3)$. (Here is the Riemann zeta function.)
