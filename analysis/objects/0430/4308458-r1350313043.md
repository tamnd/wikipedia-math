# Union-closed sets conjecture

## o1 problem

If any two sets in some finite family of sets have a union that also belongs to the family, must some element belong to at least half of the sets in the family?

## o2 subject: Union-closed sets conjecture

The union-closed sets conjecture, also known as Frankl’s conjecture, is an open problem in combinatorics posed by Péter Frankl in 1979. A family of sets is said to be union-closed if the union of any two sets from the family belongs to the family. The conjecture states: For every finite union-closed family of sets, other than the empty family, there exists an element that belongs to at least half of the sets in the family.

## Example
The family of sets
$\varnothing, \{1\}, \{1,2\}, \{2,3,4\}, \{1,2,3,4\}$
consists of five different sets and is union-closed. The element $1$ is contained in three of the five sets (and so is the element $2$), thus the conjecture holds in this case.

## o14 proof: Proof of equivalence

### Proof of equivalence
Let L be a finite lattice, and let J(L) denote the set of join-irreducible elements of L. For each element $x \in L$, define
$T_x = \{ j \in J(L) : j \le x \}.$
The map $x \mapsto T_x$ is injective, because in a finite lattice every element is the join of the join-irreducible elements below it. Thus, if $T_x = T_y$, then x and y have the same join-irreducible elements below them, and therefore $x=y$. It follows that the family
$\mathcal{F}=\{T_x : x\in L\}$
has cardinality $|L|$.
Moreover, $\mathcal{F}$ is intersection-closed. Indeed, for any $x,y\in L$,
$j\le x\wedge y$ if and only if $j\le x$ and $j\le y$, so
$T_{x\wedge y}=T_x\cap T_y.$
Assume now that every finite intersection-closed family has an element contained in at most half of its sets. Applying this statement to $\mathcal{F}$, there exists some $j\in J(L)$ such that j belongs to at most half of the sets $T_x$. But
$j\in T_x$
holds exactly when $j\le x$. Therefore the number of members of $\mathcal{F}$ containing j is exactly the number of elements of L lying above j, namely
$|\uparrow j|$,
where
$\uparrow j = \{x\in L : j\le x\}.$
Hence
$|\uparrow j|\le \frac{|L|}{2}.$
This proves the lattice-theoretic statement.
For the converse, let $\mathcal{A}$ be a finite intersection-closed family of sets. Ordered by inclusion, $\mathcal{A}$ is a finite lattice in which the meet is set intersection and the join of two elements is the least member of $\mathcal{A}$ containing their union. Assume that every finite lattice contains a join-irreducible element j such that
$|\uparrow j|\le \frac{|L|}{2}.$
Applying this to the lattice $L=\mathcal{A}$, choose such a join-irreducible element j.
Because j is join-irreducible in a finite lattice, it has a unique lower cover, denoted $j_*$. Choose an element
$a \in j\setminus j_*.$
Such an element exists because $j_* \subsetneq j$.
It is then claimed that every member of $\mathcal{A}$ containing a lies above j. Let $A\in\mathcal{A}$ and suppose $a\in A$. If $j\nsubseteq A$, then $j\wedge A = j\cap A$ is a proper subset of j, and hence
$j\wedge A \le j_*.$
But $a\in j$ and $a\in A$, so $a\in j\cap A = j\wedge A$, which would imply $a\in j_*$, contrary to the choice of a. Therefore $j\subseteq A$, that is, $j\le A$ in the lattice order.
So every set in $\mathcal{A}$ containing a belongs to $\uparrow j$. Conversely, if $A\in\uparrow j$, then $j\subseteq A$, and since $a\in j$, the set A contains a. Thus the sets in $\mathcal{A}$ containing a are exactly the elements of $\uparrow j$.
It follows that the number of members of $\mathcal{A}$ containing a is
$|\uparrow j| \le \frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{2}.$
Therefore a belongs to at most half of the sets in $\mathcal{A}$. This proves the intersection-closed formulation.
