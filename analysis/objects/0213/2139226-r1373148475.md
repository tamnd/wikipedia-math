# Ordinal arithmetic

## o1 subject: Ordinal arithmetic

In the mathematical field of set theory, ordinal arithmetic includes binary operations on ordinal numbers such as addition, multiplication, and exponentiation. Each can be defined in two different ways: either by constructing an explicit well-ordered set that represents the result of the operation or by using transfinite recursion. In addition to these standard operations for ordinals, there are also the "natural" arithmetic operations, which are usually described using the Cantor normal form for ordinals, and nimber operations.

## o2 section: Background

## o3 section: Addition

## o4 section: Properties

## o5 statement/fact: Properties

### Properties
Ordinal addition is, in general, not commutative. For example, $3 + \omega = \omega$ since the order relation for $3 + \omega$ is $0 < 1 < 2 < 0 < 1 < 2 < ...$, which can be relabeled to $\omega$. In contrast $\omega + 3$ is not equal to $\omega$ since the order relation $0 < 1 < 2 < ... < 0 < 1 < 2$ has a largest element (namely, $2$) and $\omega$ does not ($\omega$ and $\omega + 3$ are equipotent, but not order-isomorphic).
Ordinal addition is still associative; one can see for example that $(\omega + 4) + \omega = \omega + (4 + \omega) = \omega + \omega$.
Addition is strictly increasing and continuous in the right argument:
$\alpha < \beta \Rightarrow \gamma + \alpha < \gamma + \beta$
but the analogous relation does not hold for the left argument; instead we only have:
$\alpha < \beta \Rightarrow \alpha + \gamma \le \beta + \gamma$
Ordinal addition is left-cancellative: if $\alpha + \beta = \alpha + \gamma$, then $\beta = \gamma$. Furthermore, one can define left subtraction for ordinals $\beta \le \alpha$: there is a unique $\gamma$ such that $\alpha = \beta + \gamma$. On the other hand, right cancellation does not work:
$3 + \omega = 0 + \omega = \omega$, but $3 \ne 0$
Nor does right subtraction, even when $\beta \le \alpha$: for example, there does not exist any $\gamma$ such that $\gamma + 42 = \omega$.
If the ordinals less than $\alpha$ are closed under addition and contain $0$, then $\alpha$ is occasionally called a $\gamma$-number (see Additively indecomposable ordinal). These are exactly the ordinals of the form $\omega^{\beta}$.

## o6 section: Multiplication

## o7 section: Properties

## o8 statement/fact: Properties

### Properties
$\alpha \cdot 0 = 0 \cdot \alpha = 0$, and the zero-product property holds: $\alpha \cdot \beta = 0$ implies $\alpha = 0$ or $\beta = 0$. The ordinal $1$ is a multiplicative identity, $\alpha \cdot 1 = 1 \cdot \alpha = \alpha$. Multiplication is associative, $(\alpha \cdot \beta) \cdot \gamma = \alpha \cdot (\beta \cdot \gamma)$. Multiplication is strictly increasing and continuous in the right argument: for $\gamma > 0$, $\alpha < \beta$ implies $\gamma \cdot \alpha < \gamma \cdot \beta$. Multiplication is not strictly increasing in the left argument, for example, $1 < 2$ but $1 \cdot \omega = 2 \cdot \omega = \omega$. However, it is (non-strictly) increasing, i.e. $\alpha \le \beta$ implies $\alpha \cdot \gamma \le \beta \cdot \gamma$.
Multiplication of ordinals is not in general commutative. Specifically, a natural number greater than $1$ never commutes with any infinite ordinal, and two infinite ordinals $\alpha$ and $\beta$ commute if and only if $\alpha^{m} = \beta^{n}$ for some nonzero natural numbers $m$ and $n$. The relation "$\alpha$ commutes with $\beta$ is an equivalence relation on the ordinals greater than $1$, and all equivalence classes are countably infinite.
Distributivity holds, on the left: $\alpha \cdot (\beta + \gamma) = \alpha \cdot \beta + \alpha \cdot \gamma$. However, the distributive law on the right $(\beta + \gamma) \cdot \alpha = \beta \cdot \alpha + \gamma \cdot \alpha$ is not generally true: $(1 + 1) \cdot \omega = 2 \cdot \omega = \omega$ while $1 \cdot \omega + 1 \cdot \omega = \omega + \omega$, which is different. There is a left cancellation law: If $\alpha > 0$ and $\alpha \cdot \beta = \alpha \cdot \gamma$, then $\beta = \gamma$. Right cancellation does not apply, e.g. $1 \cdot \omega = 2 \cdot \omega = \omega$, but $1 \ne 2$. A left division with remainder property holds: for all $\alpha$ and $\beta$, if $\beta > 0$, then there are unique $\gamma$ and $\delta$ such that $\alpha = \beta \cdot \gamma + \delta$ and $\delta < \beta$. Right division does not work: there is no $\alpha$ such that $\alpha \cdot \omega \le \omega^{\omega} \le (\alpha + 1) \cdot \omega$.
The ordinal numbers form a left near-semiring, but do not form a ring. Hence the ordinals are not a Euclidean domain, since they are not even a ring; furthermore the Euclidean "norm" would be ordinal-valued using the left division here.
A $\delta$-number (see Additively indecomposable ordinal#Multiplicatively indecomposable) is an ordinal $\beta$ greater than $1$ such that $\alpha \cdot \beta = \beta$ whenever $0 < \alpha < \beta$. These consist of the ordinal $2$ and the ordinals of the form $\beta = \omega^{\omega^{\gamma}}$.

## o9 section: Exponentiation

## o10 section: Properties

## o11 statement/fact: Properties

### Properties
- $\alpha^{0} = 1$.
- If $0 < \alpha$, then $0^{\alpha} = 0$.
- $1^{\alpha} = 1$.
- $\alpha^{1} = \alpha$.
- $\alpha^{\beta} \cdot \alpha^{\gamma} = \alpha^{\beta+\gamma}$.
- $(\alpha^{\beta})^{\gamma} = \alpha^{\beta\cdot\gamma}$.
- There are $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ for which $(\alpha \cdot \beta)^{\gamma} \ne \alpha^{\gamma} \cdot \beta^{\gamma}$. For instance, $(\omega \cdot 2)^{2} = \omega \cdot 2 \cdot \omega \cdot 2 = \omega^{2} \cdot 2 \ne \omega^{2} \cdot 4$.
- Ordinal exponentiation is strictly increasing and continuous in the right argument: If $\gamma > 1$ and $\alpha < \beta$, then $\gamma^{\alpha} < \gamma^{\beta}$.
- If $\alpha < \beta$, then $\alpha^{\gamma} \le \beta^{\gamma}$. Note, for instance, that $2 < 3$ and yet $2^{\omega} = 3^{\omega} = \omega$.
- If $\alpha > 1$ and $\alpha^{\beta} = \alpha^{\gamma}$, then $\beta = \gamma$. If $\alpha = 1$ or $\alpha = 0$ this is not the case.
- For all $\alpha$ and $\beta$, if $\beta > 1$ and $\alpha > 0$ then there exist unique $\gamma$, $\delta$, and $\rho$ such that $\alpha = \beta^{\gamma} \cdot \delta + \rho$ such that $0 < \delta < \beta$ and $\rho < \beta^{\gamma}$.
Jacobsthal showed that the only solutions of $\alpha^{\beta} = \beta^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha \le \beta$ are given by $\alpha = \beta$, or $\alpha = 2$ and $\beta = 4$, or $\alpha$ is any limit ordinal and $\beta = \varepsilon \cdot \alpha$ where $\varepsilon$ is an $\varepsilon$-number larger than $\alpha$.

## o20 statement/generalization: Variations

### Variations
A variation of Cantor normal form is to set all the numbers $c_{i}$ equal to $1$ and allow the exponents to be equal. In other words, every ordinal number $\alpha$ can be uniquely written as $\omega^{\beta_{1}} + \omega^{\beta_{2}} + \cdots + \omega^{\beta_{k}}$, where $k$ is a natural number, and $\beta_{1} \ge \beta_{2} \ge ... \ge \beta_{k} \ge 0$ are ordinal numbers.
Another variation of the Cantor normal form is the "base $\delta$ expansion", where $\omega$ is replaced by any ordinal $\delta \ge 2$ and the coefficients satisfy $1 \le c_{i} < \delta$.

## o22 pointer-item: Prime ordinal

## Factorization into primes
Ernst Jacobsthal showed that the ordinals satisfy a form of the unique factorization theorem: every nonzero ordinal can be written as a product of a finite number of prime ordinals. This factorization into prime ordinals is in general not unique, but there is a "minimal" factorization into primes that is unique up to changing the order of finite prime factors .
A prime ordinal is an ordinal greater than $1$ that cannot be written as a product of two smaller ordinals. Some of the first primes are $2$, $3$, $5$, ... , $\omega$, $\omega + 1$, $\omega^{2} + 1$, $\omega^{3} + 1$, ..., $\omega^{\omega}$, $\omega^{\omega} + 1$, $\omega^{\omega+1} + 1$, ... There are three types of prime ordinals:
- The finite primes $2$, $3$, $5$, ...
- The ordinals of the form $\omega^{\omega^{\alpha}}$ for any ordinal $\alpha$. These are the prime ordinals that are limits, and are the delta numbers, the transfinite ordinals that are closed under multiplication.
- The ordinals of the form $\omega^{\alpha} + 1$ for any ordinal $\alpha > 0$. These are the infinite successor primes, and are the successors of gamma numbers, the additively indecomposable ordinals.
Factorization into primes is not unique: for example, $2 \cdot 3 = 3 \cdot 2$, $2 \cdot \omega = \omega$, $(\omega + 1) \cdot \omega = \omega \cdot \omega$ and $\omega \cdot \omega^{\omega} = \omega^{\omega}$. However, there is a unique factorization into primes satisfying the following additional conditions:
- Every limit prime must occur before any successor prime
- If two consecutive primes of the prime factorization are both limits or both finite, the second one must be less than or equal to the first one.
This prime factorization can easily be read off using the Cantor normal form as follows:
- First write the ordinal as a product $\alpha \cdot \beta$, where $\alpha$ is the smallest power of $\omega$ in the Cantor normal form and $\beta$ is a successor.
- If $\alpha = \omega^{\gamma}$ then writing $\gamma$ in Cantor normal form gives an expansion of $\alpha$ as a product of limit primes.
- Now look at the Cantor normal form of $\beta$. If $\beta = \omega^{\lambda} \cdot m + \omega^{\mu} \cdot n$ + smaller terms, then $\beta = (\omega^{\mu} \cdot n + \operatorname{smaller} \operatorname{terms}) \cdot (\omega^{\lambda-\mu} + 1) \cdot m$ is a product of a smaller ordinal and a prime and a natural number $m$. Repeating this and factorizing the natural numbers into primes gives the prime factorization of $\beta$.
So the factorization of the Cantor normal form ordinal
$\omega^{\alpha_{1}} \cdot n_{1} + \cdots + \omega^{\alpha_{k}} \cdot n_{k}$ (with $\alpha_{1} > ... > \alpha_{k}$)
into a minimal product of infinite primes and natural numbers is
$(\omega^{\omega^{\beta_{1}}} \cdot \cdots \cdot \omega^{\omega^{\beta_{m}}}) \cdot n_{k} \cdot (\omega^{\alpha_{k-1}-\alpha_{k}} + 1) \cdot n_{k-1} \cdot \cdots \cdot (\omega^{\alpha_{1} - \alpha_{2}} + 1) \cdot n_{1}$
where each $n_{i}$ should be replaced by its factorization into a non-increasing sequence of finite primes and
$\alpha_{k} = \omega^{\beta_{1}} + \cdots + \omega^{\beta_{m}}$ with $\beta_{1} \ge ... \ge \beta_{m}$.

## Natural operations
The natural sum and natural product operations on ordinals were defined in 1906 by Gerhard Hessenberg, and are sometimes called the Hessenberg sum (or product) . The natural sum of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is often denoted by $\alpha \oplus \beta$ or $\alpha \# \beta$, and the natural product by $\alpha \otimes \beta$ or $\alpha \text{⨳} \beta$.
The natural sum and product are defined as follows. Let $\alpha = \omega^{\alpha_{1}} + \cdots + \omega^{\alpha_{k}}$ and $\beta = \omega^{\beta_{1}} + \cdots + \omega^{\beta_{\ell}}$ be in Cantor normal form (i.e. $\alpha_{1} \ge ... \ge \alpha_{k}$ and $\beta_{1} \ge ... \ge \beta_{\ell}$). Let $\gamma_{1}, ..., \gamma_{k+\ell}$ be the exponents $\alpha_{1}$, ..., $\alpha_{k}$, $\beta_{1}$, ..., $\beta_{\ell}$ sorted in nonincreasing order. Then $\alpha \oplus \beta$ is defined as
$\alpha \oplus \beta = \omega^{\gamma_{1}} + \cdots + \omega^{\gamma_{k+\ell}}$.
The natural product of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is defined as
$\alpha \otimes \beta = \bigoplus_{\begin{aligned}&1\le i\le k\\&1\le j\le\ell\end{align \omega^{\alpha_{i}\oplus\beta_{j}}\}\}$.
For example, suppose $\alpha = \omega^{\omega^{\omega}} + \omega$ and $\beta = \omega^{\omega} + \omega^{5}$. Then $\alpha \oplus \beta = \omega^{\omega^{\omega}} + \omega^{\omega} + \omega^{5} + \omega$, whereas $\alpha + \beta = \omega^{\omega^{\omega}} + \omega^{\omega} + \omega^{5}$. And $\alpha \otimes \beta = \omega^{\omega^{\omega}+\omega} + \omega^{\omega^{\omega}+5} + \omega^{\omega+1} + \omega^{6}$, whereas $\alpha \cdot \beta = \omega^{\omega^{\omega}+\omega} + \omega^{\omega^{\omega}+5}$.
The natural sum and product are commutative and associative, and natural product distributes over natural sum. The operations are also monotonic, in the sense that if $\alpha < \beta$ then $\alpha \oplus \gamma < \beta \oplus \gamma$; if $\alpha \le \beta$ then $\alpha \otimes \gamma \le \beta \otimes \gamma$; and if $\alpha < \beta$ and $\gamma > 0$ then $\alpha \otimes \gamma < \beta \otimes \gamma$.
We have $\underbrace{\alpha\oplus\cdots\oplus\alpha}_{n}=\alpha\otimes n$.
We always have $\alpha + \beta \le \alpha \oplus \beta$ and $\alpha \cdot \beta \le \alpha \otimes \beta$. If both $\alpha < \omega^{\gamma}$ and $\beta < \omega^{\gamma}$ then $\alpha \oplus \beta < \omega^{\gamma}$. If both $\alpha < \omega^{\omega^{\gamma}}$ and $\beta < \omega^{\omega^{\gamma}}$ then $\alpha \otimes \beta < \omega^{\omega^{\gamma}}$.
Natural sum and product are not continuous. For example, if natural sum were continuous then $\lim_{n<\omega} (1 \oplus n) = 1 \oplus \omega$, but the left-hand side equals $\omega$ and the right-hand side equals $\omega + 1$. Similarly, if natural product were continuous $\lim_{n<\omega} (2 \otimes n) = 2 \otimes \omega$, but the left-hand side equals $\omega$ and the right-hand side equals $(\omega^{0} + \omega^{0}) \otimes \omega^{1} = \omega^{1} + \omega^{1} = \omega \cdot 2$.
The natural sum and product are the same as the addition and multiplication (restricted to ordinals) of John Conway's field of surreal numbers.
The natural operations come up in the theory of well partial orders; given two well partial orders $S$ and $T$, of types (maximum linearizations) $o(S)$ and $o(T)$, the type of the disjoint union is $o(S) \oplus o(T)$, while the type of the direct product is $o(S) \otimes o(T)$. One may take this relation as a definition of the natural operations by choosing $S$ and $T$ to be ordinals $\alpha$ and $\beta$; so $\alpha \oplus \beta$ is the maximum order type of a total order extending the disjoint union (as a partial order) of $\alpha$ and $\beta$; while $\alpha \otimes \beta$ is the maximum order type of a total order extending the direct product (as a partial order) of $\alpha$ and $\beta$. A useful application of this is when $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are both subsets of some larger total order; then their union has order type at most $\alpha \oplus \beta$. If they are both subsets of some ordered abelian group, then their sum has order type at most $\alpha \otimes \beta$.
We can also define the natural sum $\alpha \oplus \beta$ by simultaneous transfinite recursion on $\alpha$ and $\beta$, as the smallest ordinal strictly greater than the natural sum of $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ for all $\gamma < \beta$ and of $\gamma$ and $\beta$ for all $\gamma < \alpha$. Similarly, we can define the natural product $\alpha \otimes \beta$ by simultaneous transfinite recursion on $\alpha$ and $\beta$, as the smallest ordinal $\gamma$ such that $(\alpha \otimes \delta) \oplus (\varepsilon \otimes \beta) < \gamma \oplus (\varepsilon \otimes \delta)$ for all $\varepsilon < \alpha$ and $\delta < \beta$. Also, see the article on surreal numbers for the definition of natural multiplication in that context; however, it uses surreal subtraction, which is not defined on ordinals.
The natural sum is associative and commutative. It is always greater or equal to the usual sum, but it may be strictly greater. For example, the natural sum of $\omega$ and $1$ is $\omega + 1$ (the usual sum), but this is also the natural sum of $1$ and $\omega$. The natural product is associative and commutative and distributes over the natural sum. The natural product is always greater or equal to the usual product, but it may be strictly greater. For example, the natural product of $\omega$ and $2$ is $\omega \cdot 2$ (the usual product), but this is also the natural product of $2$ and $\omega$.
Under natural addition, the ordinals can be identified with the elements of the free commutative monoid generated by the gamma numbers $\omega^{\alpha}$. Under natural addition and multiplication, the ordinals can be identified with the elements of the free commutative semiring generated by the delta numbers $\omega^{\omega^{\alpha}}$.
The ordinals do not have unique factorization into primes under the natural product. While the full polynomial ring does have unique factorization, the subset of polynomials with non-negative coefficients does not: for example, if $x$ is any delta number, then
$x^{5} + x^{4} + x^{3} + x^{2} + x + 1 = (x + 1) (x^{4} + x^{2} + 1) = (x^{2} + x + 1) (x^{3} + 1)$
has two incompatible expressions as a natural product of polynomials with non-negative coefficients that cannot be decomposed further.
