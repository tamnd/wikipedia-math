# Spherical law of cosines

## o1 subject: Spherical law of cosines

In spherical trigonometry, the law of cosines (or, more specifically, the law of cosines for sides) is a theorem relating the three sides and one of the angles of a spherical triangle, analogous to the planar law of cosines for a triangle in the Euclidean plane.

## Proofs
### First proof
Let $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$, and $\mathbf{w}$ denote the unit vectors from the center of a unit sphere to those corners of the triangle. The angles and distances do not change if the coordinate system is rotated, so we can rotate the coordinate system so that $\mathbf{u}$ is at the north pole and $\mathbf{v}$ is somewhere on the prime meridian (longitude of 0). With this rotation, the spherical coordinates for $\mathbf{v}$ are $(r, \theta, \phi) = (1, a, 0) ,$ where $\theta$ is the angle measured from the north pole not from the equator, and the spherical coordinates for $\mathbf{w}$ are $(r, \theta, \phi) = (1, b, C) .$ The Cartesian coordinates for $\mathbf{v}$ are $(x, y, z) = (\sin a, 0, \cos a)$ and the Cartesian coordinates for $\mathbf{w}$ are $(x, y, z) = (\sin b \cos C, \sin b \sin C, \cos b) .$ The value of $\cos c$ is the dot product of the two Cartesian vectors, which is $\sin a \sin b \cos C + \cos a \cos b .$
### Second proof
Let $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$, and $\mathbf{w}$ denote the unit vectors from the center of the sphere to those corners of the triangle. We have $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} = 1$, $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} = \cos c$, $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \cos a$, and $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{w} = \cos b$. The vectors $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{w}$ have lengths $\sin a$ and $\sin b$ respectively and the angle between them is $C$, so
$$\begin{aligned}
\sin a \sin b \cos C &= (\mathbf u \times \mathbf v) \cdot (\mathbf u \times \mathbf w) \\
&= (\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf u)(\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf w) - (\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf w)(\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf u) \\
&= \cos c - \cos a \cos b
\end{aligned}$$
using cross products, dot products, and the Binet–Cauchy identity
$$(\mathbf p \times \mathbf q) \cdot (\mathbf r \times \mathbf s) = (\mathbf p \cdot \mathbf r)(\mathbf q \cdot \mathbf s) - (\mathbf p \cdot \mathbf s)(\mathbf q \cdot \mathbf r).$$
### Third proof
There is a proof using quaternions. Let $\mathbf{u}$, $\mathbf{v}$, and $\mathbf{w}$ denote the unit vectors from the center of the unit sphere to those corners of the triangle. We define the quaternion $u = (0, \mathbf{u}) = 0 + u_{x}\mathbf{i} + u_{y}\mathbf{j} + u_{z}\mathbf{k}$. The quaternion $u$ is used to represent a rotation by 180° around the axis indicated by the vector $\mathbf{u}$. We note that using $-\mathbf{u}$ as the axis of rotation gives the same result, and that the rotation is its own inverse. We also define $v = (0, \mathbf{v})$ and $w = (0, \mathbf{w})$.
We compute the product of quaternions, which also gives the composition of the corresponding rotations:
$$q = vu^{-1} = (v)(-u) = (-(\mathbf v \cdot -\mathbf u), \mathbf v \times -\mathbf u) = (\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v, \mathbf u \times \mathbf v) = (\cos a, \mathbf w' \sin a)$$
where $(f, \mathbf{g})$ represents the real (scalar) and imaginary (vector) parts of a quaternion, $a$ is the angle between $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$, and $\mathbf{w}' = (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) / \lvert \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\rvert$ is the axis of the rotation that moves $\mathbf{u}$ to $\mathbf{v}$ along a great circle. Similarly we define:
$$\begin{aligned}
r &= wv^{-1} = (\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf w, \mathbf v \times \mathbf w) = (\cos c, \mathbf u' \sin c) \\
s &= uw^{-1} = (\mathbf w \cdot \mathbf u, \mathbf w \times \mathbf u) = (\cos b, \mathbf v' \sin b). \\
\end{aligned}$$
The quaternions $q$, $r$, and $s$ are used to represent rotations with axes of rotation $\mathbf{w}'$, $\mathbf{u}'$, and $\mathbf{v}'$, respectively, and angles of rotation $2a$, $2b$, and $2c$, respectively. (Because these are double angles, each of $q$, $r$, and $s$ represents two applications of the rotation implied by an edge of the spherical triangle.)
From the definitions, it follows that
$$qsr = vu^{-1}uw^{-1}wv^{-1} = 1,$$
which tells us that the composition of these rotations is the identity transformation. In particular, $qs = r^{-1}$ gives us
$$(\cos a, \mathbf w' \sin a) (\cos b, \mathbf v' \sin b) = (\cos c, -\mathbf u' \sin c).$$
Expanding the left-hand side, we obtain
$$(\cos a \cos b - \mathbf w' \cdot \mathbf v' \sin a \sin b, \mathbf w' \cos a \sin b + \mathbf v' \sin a \cos b + \mathbf w' \times \mathbf v' \sin a \sin b).$$
Equating the real parts on both sides of the identity, we obtain
$$\cos a \cos b - \mathbf w' \cdot \mathbf v' \sin a \sin b = \cos c.$$
Because $\mathbf{w}'$ is parallel to $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{v}'$ is parallel to $\mathbf{w} \times \mathbf{u} = -\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{w}$, and $C$ is the angle between $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{w}$, it follows that $\mathbf w' \cdot \mathbf v' = -\cos C$. Thus,
$$\cos a \cos b + \cos C \sin a \sin b = \cos c.$$
