# Napoleon points

## o1 subject: Napoleon points

In geometry, Napoleon points are a pair of special points associated with a plane triangle. It is generally believed that the existence of these points was discovered by Napoleon Bonaparte, the Emperor of the French from 1804 to 1815, but many have questioned this belief. The Napoleon points are triangle centers and they are listed as the points X(17) and X(18) in Clark Kimberling's Encyclopedia of Triangle Centers.

## Generalizations
The results regarding the existence of the Napoleon points can be generalized in different ways. In defining the Napoleon points we begin with equilateral triangles drawn on the sides of $\triangle ABC$ and then consider the centers $X, Y, Z$ of these triangles. These centers can be thought as the vertices of isosceles triangles erected on the sides of triangle ABC with the base angles equal to $\pi$/6 (30 degrees). The generalizations seek to determine other triangles that, when erected over the sides of $\triangle ABC$, have concurrent lines joining their external vertices and the vertices of $\triangle ABC$.
### Isosceles triangles
This generalization asserts the following:
If the three triangles $\triangle XBC, \triangle YCA, \triangle ZAB$, constructed on the sides of the given triangle $\triangle ABC$ as bases, are similar, isosceles and similarly situated, then the lines $AX, BY, CZ$ concur at a point $N$.
If the common base angle is $\theta$, then the vertices of the three triangles have the following trilinear coordinates.
$$\begin{array}{rccccc}
X= & -\sin\theta &:& \sin(C + \theta) &:& \sin(B + \theta) \\
Y= & \sin(C + \theta) &:& -\sin\theta &:& \sin(A + \theta) \\
Z=& \sin(B + \theta) &:& \sin(A + \theta) &:& -\sin\theta
\end{array}$$
The trilinear coordinates of $N$ are
$$\csc(A + \theta) : \csc(B + \theta) : \csc(C + \theta)$$
A few special cases are interesting.

\end{cases}$ || The vertex $C$
|}
Moreover, the locus of $N$ as the base angle $\theta$ varies between −$\pi$/2 and $\pi$/2 is the conic
$$\frac{\sin(B-C)} x + \frac{\sin(C-A)} y + \frac{\sin(A-B)} z = 0.$$
This conic is a rectangular hyperbola and it is called the Kiepert hyperbola in honor of Ludwig Kiepert (1846–1934), the mathematician who discovered this result. This hyperbola is the unique conic which passes through the five points $A, B, C, G, O$.
### Similar triangles
The three triangles $\triangle XBC, \triangle YCA, \triangle ZAB$ erected over the sides of the triangle $\triangle ABC$ need not be isosceles for the three lines $AX, BY, CZ$ to be concurrent.
If similar triangles $\triangle XBC, \triangle AYC, \triangle ABZ$ are constructed outwardly on the sides of any triangle $\triangle ABC$ then the lines $AX, BY, CZ$ are concurrent.
### Arbitrary triangles
The concurrence of the lines $AX, BY, CZ$ holds even in much relaxed conditions. The following result states one of the most general conditions for the lines $AX, BY, CZ$ to be concurrent.
If triangles $\triangle XBC, \triangle YCA, \triangle ZAB$ are constructed outwardly on the sides of any triangle $\triangle ABC$ such that
$$\angle CBX = \angle ABZ, \quad \angle ACY = \angle BCX, \quad \angle BAZ = \angle CAY;$$
then the lines $AX, BY, CZ$ are concurrent.
The point of concurrency is known as the Jacobi point.
