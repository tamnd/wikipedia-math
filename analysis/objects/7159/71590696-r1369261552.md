# Subgroup distortion

## o1 subject: Subgroup distortion

In geometric group theory, a discipline of mathematics, subgroup distortion measures the extent to which an overgroup can reduce the complexity of a group's word problem. Like much of geometric group theory, the concept is due to Misha Gromov, who introduced it in 1993.

## Examples
For example, consider the infinite cyclic group $Z = \langle b\rangle$, embedded as a normal subgroup of the Baumslag–Solitar group $\operatorname{BS}(1, 2) = \langle a, b\rangle$. With respect to the chosen generating sets, the element
$$b^{2^n}=a^nba^{-n}$$
is distance $2^{n}$ from the origin in $Z$, but distance $2n + 1$ from the origin in $\operatorname{BS}(1, 2)$. In particular, $Z$ is at least exponentially distorted with base $2$.
On the other hand, any embedded copy of $Z$ in the free abelian group on two generators $Z^{2}$ is undistorted, as is any embedding of $Z$ into itself.

## o7 section: Elementary properties

## o8 statement/fact: Elementary properties

## Elementary properties
In a tower of groups $K \le H \le G$, the distortion of $K$ in $G$ is at least the distortion of $K$ in $H$.
A normal abelian subgroup has distortion determined by the eigenvalues of the conjugation overgroup representation; formally, if $g \in G$ acts on $V \le G$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, then $V$ is at least exponentially distorted with base $\lambda$. For many non-normal but still abelian subgroups, the distortion of the normal core gives a strong lower bound.
