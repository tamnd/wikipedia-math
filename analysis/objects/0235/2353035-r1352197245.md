## Properties
The Eisenstein integers form a commutative ring $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ of algebraic integers in the algebraic number field $\mathbf{Q}(\omega)$ – the third cyclotomic field. To see that the Eisenstein integers are algebraic integers note that each $z = a + b\omega$ is a root of the monic polynomial
$z^2 - (2a - b)\;\!z + \left(a^2 - ab + b^2\right)~.$
In particular, $\omega$ satisfies the equation
$\omega^2 + \omega + 1 = 0~.$
The product of two Eisenstein integers $a + b\omega$ and $c + d\omega$ is given explicitly by
$(a + b\;\!\omega) \;\! (c + d\;\!\omega)=(ac - bd) + (bc + ad - bd)\;\!\omega~.$
The 2-norm of an Eisenstein integer is just its squared modulus, and is given by
${\left|a + b\;\!\omega\right|}^2 \,= \, {(a - \tfrac{1}{2} b)}^2 + \tfrac{3}{4} b^2 \, = \, a^2 - ab + b^2~,$
which is clearly a positive ordinary (rational) integer.
Also, the complex conjugate of $\omega$ satisfies
$\bar\omega = \omega^2~.$
The group of units in this ring is the cyclic group formed by the sixth roots of unity in the complex plane: $\{\pm1, \pm\omega, \pm\omega^{2}\}$, the Eisenstein integers of norm $1$.

## Eisenstein primes
If $x$ and $y$ are Eisenstein integers, we say that $x$ divides $y$ if there is some Eisenstein integer $z$ such that $y = zx$. A non-unit Eisenstein integer $x$ is said to be an Eisenstein prime if its only non-unit divisors are of the form $ux$, where $u$ is any of the six units. They are the corresponding concept to the Gaussian primes in the Gaussian integers.
There are two types of Eisenstein prime.
- an ordinary prime number (or rational prime) which is congruent to $2 \bmod 3$ is also an Eisenstein prime.
- $3$ and each rational prime congruent to $1 \bmod 3$ are equal to the norm $x^{2} - xy + y^{2}$ of an Eisenstein integer $x + \omega y$. Thus, such a prime may be factored as $(x + \omega y)(x + \omega^{2}y)$, and these factors are Eisenstein primes: they are precisely the Eisenstein integers whose norm is a rational prime.
In the second type, factors of $3$, $1-\omega$ and $1-\omega^2$ are associates: $1-\omega=(-\omega)(1-\omega^2)$, so it is regarded as a special type in some books.
The first few Eisenstein primes of the form $3n - 1$ are:
2, 5, 11, 17, 23, 29, 41, 47, 53, 59, 71, 83, 89, 101, ... .
Natural primes that are congruent to $0$ or $1$ modulo $3$ are not Eisenstein primes: they admit nontrivial factorizations in $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$. For example:
$3 = -(1 + 2\omega)^{2}$
$7 = (3 + \omega)(2 - \omega)$.
In general, if a natural prime $p$ is $1$ modulo $3$ and can therefore be written as $p = a^{2} - ab + b^{2}$, then it factorizes over $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ as
$p = (a + b\omega)((a - b) - b\omega)$.
Some non-real Eisenstein primes are
$2 + \omega$, $3 + \omega$, $4 + \omega$, $5 + 2\omega$, $6 + \omega$, $7 + \omega$, $7 + 3\omega$.
Up to conjugacy and unit multiples, the primes listed above, together with $2$ and $5$, are all the Eisenstein primes of absolute value not exceeding $7$.
, the largest known real Eisenstein prime is the 12th-largest known prime $10223 \times 2^{31172165} + 1$, discovered by Péter Szabolcs and PrimeGrid.
