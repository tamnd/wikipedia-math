# Voltage graph

## o1 subject: Voltage graph

In graph theory, a voltage graph is a directed graph whose edges are labelled invertibly by elements of a group. It is formally identical to a gain graph, but it is generally used in topological graph theory as a concise way to specify another graph called the derived graph of the voltage graph.

## o3 section: Definition

## o4 definition: Definition

## Definition
Formal definition of a $\Pi$-voltage graph, for a given group $\Pi$:
- Begin with a digraph G. (The direction is solely for convenience in notation.)
- A $\Pi$-voltage on an arc of G is a label of the arc by an element $x \in \Pi$. For instance, in the case where $\Pi = \mathbb{Z}_n$, the label is a number i (mod n).
- A $\Pi$-voltage assignment is a function $\alpha : E(G) \rightarrow \Pi$ that labels each arc of G with a Π-voltage.
- A $\Pi$-voltage graph is a pair $( G, \alpha: E(G) \rightarrow \Pi )$ such that G is a digraph and α is a voltage assignment.
- The voltage group of a voltage graph $( G, \alpha: E(G) \rightarrow \Pi )$ is the group $\Pi$ from which the voltages are assigned.
Note that the voltages of a voltage graph need not satisfy Kirchhoff's voltage law, that the sum of voltages around a closed path is 0 (the identity element of the group), although this law does hold for the derived graphs described below. Thus, the name may be somewhat misleading. It results from the origin of voltage graphs as dual to the current graphs of topological graph theory.

## Examples
Any Cayley graph of a group $\Pi$, with a given set $\Gamma$ of generators, may be defined as the derived graph for a $\Pi$-voltage graph having one vertex and $\Gamma$ self-loops, each labeled with one of the generators in $\Gamma$.
The Petersen graph is the derived graph for a $\mathbb{Z}_5$-voltage graph in the shape of a dumbbell with two vertices and three edges: one edge connecting the two vertices, and one self-loop on each vertex. One self-loop is labeled with 1, the other with 2, and the edge connecting the two vertices is labeled 0. More generally, the same construction allows any generalized Petersen graph GP(n,k) to be constructed as a derived graph of the same dumbbell graph with labels 1, 0, and k in the group $\mathbb{Z}_n$.
The vertices and edges of any periodic tessellation of the plane may be formed as the derived graph of a finite graph, with voltages in $\mathbb{Z}^2$.
