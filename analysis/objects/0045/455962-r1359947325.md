# Von Neumann bicommutant theorem

## o1 subject: Von Neumann bicommutant theorem

In mathematics, specifically functional analysis, the von Neumann bicommutant theorem relates the closure of a set of bounded operators on a Hilbert space in certain topologies to the bicommutant of that set. In essence, it is a connection between the algebraic and topological sides of operator theory.

## o2 section: Proof

## o3 proof: Proof

## Proof
Let $H$ be a Hilbert space and $L(H)$ the bounded operators on $H$. Consider a self-adjoint unital subalgebra $\mathbf{M}$ of $L(H)$ (this means that $\mathbf{M}$ contains the adjoints of its members, and the identity operator on $H$).
The theorem is equivalent to the combination of the following three statements:
(i) $\operatorname{cl}_{W}(\mathbf{M}) \subseteq \mathbf{M}''$
(ii) $\operatorname{cl}_{S}(\mathbf{M}) \subseteq \operatorname{cl}_{W}(\mathbf{M})$
(iii) $\mathbf{M}'' \subseteq \operatorname{cl}_{S}(\mathbf{M})$
where the $W$ and $S$ subscripts stand for closure in the weak and strong operator topologies, respectively.
### Proof of (i)
For any $x$ and $y$ in $H$, the map T → <Tx, y> is continuous in the weak operator topology, by its definition. Therefore, for any fixed operator $O$, so is the map
$T \to \langle (OT - TO)x, y\rangle = \langle Tx, O^*y\rangle - \langle TOx, y\rangle$
Let S be any subset of $L(H)$, and S′ its commutant. For any operator $T$ in S′, this function is zero for all O in S. For any $T$ not in S′, it must be nonzero for some O in S and some x and y in $H$. By its continuity there is an open neighborhood of $T$ for the weak operator topology on which it is nonzero, and which therefore is also not in S′. Hence any commutant S′ is closed in the weak operator topology. In particular, so is $\mathbf{M}''$; since it contains $\mathbf{M}$, it also contains its weak operator closure.
### Proof of (ii)
This follows directly from the weak operator topology being coarser than the strong operator topology: for every point $x$ in $\operatorname{cl}_{S}(\mathbf{M})$, every open neighborhood of $x$ in the weak operator topology is also open in the strong operator topology and therefore contains a member of $\mathbf{M}$; therefore $x$ is also a member of $\operatorname{cl}_{W}(\mathbf{M})$.
### Proof of (iii)
Fix $X \in \mathbf{M}''$. We must show that $X \in \operatorname{cl}_{S}(\mathbf{M})$, i.e. for each h ∈ H and any $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists T in $\mathbf{M}$ with $||Xh - Th|| < \varepsilon$.
Fix h in $H$. The cyclic subspace $\mathbf{M}h = \{Mh : M \in \mathbf{M}$} is invariant under the action of any T in $\mathbf{M}$. Its closure $\operatorname{cl}(\mathbf{M}h)$ in the norm of H is a closed linear subspace, with corresponding orthogonal projection $P$ : H → $\operatorname{cl}(\mathbf{M}h)$ in L(H). In fact, this P is in $\mathbf{M}'$, as we now show.
Lemma. $P \in \mathbf{M}'$.
Proof. Fix $x \in H$. As $Px \in \operatorname{cl}(\mathbf{M}h)$, it is the limit of a sequence $O_{n}h$ with $O_{n}$ in $\mathbf{M}$. For any $T \in \mathbf{M}$, $TO_{n}h$ is also in $\mathbf{M}h$, and by the continuity of $T$, this sequence converges to $TPx$. So $TPx \in \operatorname{cl}(\mathbf{M}h)$, and hence PTPx = TPx. Since x was arbitrary, we have PTP = TP for all $T$ in $\mathbf{M}$.
Since $\mathbf{M}$ is closed under the adjoint operation and P is self-adjoint, for any $x, y \in H$ we have
$\langle x,TPy\rangle = \langle x,PTPy\rangle = \langle (PTP)^*x,y\rangle = \langle PT^*Px,y\rangle = \langle T^*Px,y\rangle = \langle Px,Ty\rangle = \langle x,PTy\rangle$
So TP = PT for all $T \in \mathbf{M}$, meaning P lies in $\mathbf{M}'$.
By definition of the bicommutant, we must have XP = PX. Since $\mathbf{M}$ is unital, $h \in \mathbf{M}h$, and so $h = Ph$. Hence $Xh = XPh = PXh \in \operatorname{cl}(\mathbf{M}h)$. So for each $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists T in $\mathbf{M}$ with $||Xh - Th|| < \varepsilon$, i.e. $X$ is in the strong operator closure of $\mathbf{M}$.
### Non-unital case
A C*-algebra $\mathbf{M}$ acting on H is said to act non-degenerately if for h in $H$, $\mathbf{M}h = \{0\}$ implies $h = 0$. In this case, it can be shown using an approximate identity in $\mathbf{M}$ that the identity operator I lies in the strong closure of $\mathbf{M}$. Therefore, the conclusion of the bicommutant theorem holds for $\mathbf{M}$.
