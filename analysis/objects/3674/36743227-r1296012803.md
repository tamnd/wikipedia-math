# Matrix splitting

## o1 subject: Matrix splitting

In the mathematical discipline of numerical linear algebra, a matrix splitting is an expression which represents a given matrix as a sum or difference of matrices. Many iterative methods (for example, for systems of differential equations) depend upon the direct solution of matrix equations involving matrices more general than tridiagonal matrices. These matrix equations can often be solved directly and efficiently when written as a matrix splitting. The technique was devised by Richard S. Varga in 1960.

## Example
### Regular splitting
In equation ((1)), let
:
$\mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix}
6 & -2 & -3 \\
-1 & 4 & -2 \\
-3 & -1 & 5
\end{pmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{k} = \begin{pmatrix}
5 \\
-12 \\
10
\end{pmatrix}.$
Let us apply the splitting ((7)) which is used in the Jacobi method: we split A in such a way that B consists of all of the diagonal elements of A, and C consists of all of the off-diagonal elements of A, negated. (Of course this is not the only useful way to split a matrix into two matrices.) We have
:
$\begin{aligned}
& \mathbf{B} = \begin{pmatrix}
6 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 4 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 5
\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{C} = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & 2 & 3 \\
1 & 0 & 2 \\
3 & 1 & 0
\end{pmatrix},
\end{aligned}$
$\begin{aligned}
& \mathbf{A^{-1}} = \frac{1}{47} \begin{pmatrix}
18 & 13 & 16 \\
11 & 21 & 15 \\
13 & 12 & 22
\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B^{-1}} = \begin{pmatrix}
\frac{1}{6} & 0 & 0 \\[4pt]
0 & \frac{1}{4} & 0 \\[4pt]
0 & 0 & \frac{1}{5}
\end{pmatrix},
\end{aligned}$
$\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{D} = \mathbf{B^{-1}C} = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{2} \\[4pt]
\frac{1}{4} & 0 & \frac{1}{2} \\[4pt]
\frac{3}{5} & \frac{1}{5} & 0
\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B^{-1}k} = \begin{pmatrix}
\frac{5}{6} \\[4pt]
-3 \\[4pt]
2
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}$
Since B−1 ≥ 0 and C ≥ 0, the splitting ((11)) is a regular splitting. Since A−1 > 0, the spectral radius $\rho (\mathbf D)$ < 1. (The approximate eigenvalues of D are $\lambda_i \approx -0.4599820, -0.3397859, 0.7997679.$) Hence, the matrix D is convergent and the method ((5)) necessarily converges for the problem ((10)). Note that the diagonal elements of A are all greater than zero, the off-diagonal elements of A are all less than zero and A is strictly diagonally dominant.
The method ((5)) applied to the problem ((10)) then takes the form
:
$\mathbf x^{(m+1)} =
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{2} \\[4pt]
\frac{1}{4} & 0 & \frac{1}{2} \\[4pt]
\frac{3}{5} & \frac{1}{5} & 0
\end{pmatrix}
