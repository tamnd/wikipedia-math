# Minimal polynomial (linear algebra)

## o1 subject: Minimal polynomial

In linear algebra, the minimal polynomial $\mu_{A}$ of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over a field $\mathbf{F}$ is the monic polynomial $\mu_{A}$ over $\mathbf{F}$ of least degree such that $\mu_{A}(A)= 0$. Any other polynomial $Q$ with $Q(A) = 0$ is a (polynomial) multiple of $\mu_{A}$.

## o2 section: Formal definition

## o3 definition: Formal definition

## Formal definition
Given an endomorphism $T$ on a finite-dimensional vector space $V$ over a field $\mathbf{F}$, let $I_{T}$ be the set defined as
$\mathit{I}_T = \{ p \in \mathbf{F}[t] \mid p(T) = 0 \} ,$
where $\mathbf{F}[t]$ is the space of all polynomials over the field $\mathbf{F}$. $I_{T}$ is a proper ideal of $\mathbf{F}[t]$. Since $\mathbf{F}$ is a field, $\mathbf{F}[t]$ is a principal ideal domain, thus any ideal is generated by a single polynomial, which is unique up to a unit in $\mathbf{F}$. A particular choice among the generators can be made, since precisely one of the generators is monic. The minimal polynomial is thus defined to be the monic polynomial that generates $I_{T}$. It is the monic polynomial of least degree in $I_{T}$.

## o4 section: Applications

## o5 application: Applications

## Applications
An endomorphism $\varphi$ of a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $\mathbf{F}$ is diagonalizable if and only if its minimal polynomial factors completely over $\mathbf{F}$ into distinct linear factors. The fact that there is only one factor $X - \lambda$ for every eigenvalue $\lambda$ means that the generalized eigenspace for $\lambda$ is the same as the eigenspace for $\lambda$: every Jordan block has size $1$. More generally, if $\varphi$ satisfies a polynomial equation $P(\varphi) = 0$ where $P$ factors into distinct linear factors over $\mathbf{F}$, then it will be diagonalizable: its minimal polynomial is a divisor of $P$ and therefore also factors into distinct linear factors. In particular one has:
- $P= X^k-1$: finite order endomorphisms of complex vector spaces are diagonalizable. For the special case $k = 2$ of involutions, this is even true for endomorphisms of vector spaces over any field of characteristic other than $2$, since $X^2-1=(X-1)(X+1)$ is a factorization into distinct factors over such a field. This is a part of representation theory of cyclic groups.
- $P= X^2-X = X(X-1)$: endomorphisms satisfying $\varphi^{2} = \varphi$ are called projections, and are always diagonalizable (moreover their only eigenvalues are $0$ and $1$).
- By contrast if $\mu_\phi = X^k$ with $k \ge 2$ then $\varphi$ (a nilpotent endomorphism) is not necessarily diagonalizable, since $X^k$ has a repeated root $0$.
These cases can also be proved directly, but the minimal polynomial gives a unified perspective and proof.

## o6 section: Computation

## o7 definition/construction: Computation

## Computation
For a nonzero vector $v$ in $V$ define:
$\mathit{I}_{T, v} = \{ p \in \mathbf{F}[t] \; | \; p(T)(v) = 0 \}.$
This definition satisfies the properties of a proper ideal. Let $\mu_{T,v}$ be the monic polynomial which generates it.
### Properties
Since $I_{T,v}$ contains the minimal polynomial $\mu_{T}$, the latter is divisible by $\mu_{T,v}$.
### Example
Define $T$ to be the endomorphism of $\mathbf{R}^{3}$ with matrix, on the canonical basis,
$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & -1 \\ 1 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 \end{pmatrix}.$
Taking the first canonical basis vector $e_{1}$ and its repeated images by $T$ one obtains
$e_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
T \cdot e_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \quad
T^2\! \cdot e_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \mbox{ and}\quad
T^3\! \cdot e_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix}$
of which the first three are easily seen to be linearly independent, and therefore span all of $\mathbf{R}^{3}$. The last one then necessarily is a linear combination of the first three, in fact
$T^{\,3}\,\cdot e_{1} = -4T^{\,2}\,\cdot e_{1} - T \cdot e_{1} + e_{1}$,
so that:
$\mu_{T,e_{1}} = X^{\,3} + 4X^{\,2} + X - I$.
This is in fact also the minimal polynomial $\mu_{T}$ and the characteristic polynomial $\chi_{T}$: indeed $\mu_{T,e_{1}}$ divides $\mu_{T}$ which divides $\chi_{T}$, and since the first and last are of degree $3$ and all are monic, they must all be the same. Another reason is that in general if any polynomial in $T$ annihilates a vector $v$, then it also annihilates $T\cdot v$ (just apply $T$ to the equation that says that it annihilates $v$), and therefore by iteration it annihilates the entire space generated by the iterated images by $T$ of $v$; in the current case we have seen that for $v = e_{1}$ that space is all of $\mathbf{R}^{3}$, so $\mu_{T,e_{1}}(T) = 0$. Indeed one verifies for the full matrix that $T^{\,3} + 4T^{\,2} + T - I_{3}$ is the zero matrix:
$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & -3 \\ 3 & -13 & 23 \\ -4 & 19 & -36 \end{bmatrix}
+ 4\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 4 & -6 \\ 1 & -5 & 10 \end{bmatrix}
+ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & -1 \\ 1 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix}
+ \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
