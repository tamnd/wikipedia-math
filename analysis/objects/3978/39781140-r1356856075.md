## o2 subject: Goldberg polyhedron

In mathematics, and more specifically in polyhedral combinatorics, a Goldberg polyhedron is a convex polyhedron made from hexagons and pentagons. They were first described in 1937 by Michael Goldberg (1902–1990). They are defined by three properties: each face is either a pentagon or hexagon, exactly three faces meet at each vertex, and they have rotational icosahedral symmetry. They are not necessarily mirror-symmetric; e.g. $\operatorname{GP}(5,3)$ and $\operatorname{GP}(3,5)$ are enantiomorphs of each other. A Goldberg polyhedron is a dual polyhedron of a geodesic polyhedron.

## Construction
Most Goldberg polyhedra can be constructed using Conway polyhedron notation starting with (T)etrahedron, (C)ube, and (D)odecahedron seeds. The chamfer operator, $c$, replaces all edges by hexagons, transforming $\operatorname{GP}(m,n)$ to $\operatorname{GP}(2m,2n)$, with a $T$ multiplier of 4. The truncated kis operator, $y = tk$, generates $\operatorname{GP}(3,0)$, transforming $\operatorname{GP}(m,n)$ to $\operatorname{GP}(3m,3n)$, with a $T$ multiplier of 9.
For class 2 forms, the dual kis operator, $z = dk$, transforms $\operatorname{GP}(a,0)$ into $\operatorname{GP}(a,a)$, with a $T$ multiplier of 3. For class 3 forms, the whirl operator, $w$, generates $\operatorname{GP}(2,1)$, with a $T$ multiplier of 7. A clockwise and counterclockwise whirl generator, $w\overline{w} = wrw$ generates $\operatorname{GP}(7,0)$ in class 1. In general, a whirl can transform a $\operatorname{GP}(a,b)$ into $\operatorname{GP}(a + 3b, 2ab)$ for $a > b$ and the same chiral direction. If chiral directions are reversed, $\operatorname{GP}(a, b)$ becomes $\operatorname{GP}(2a + 3b, a - 2b)$ if $a \ge 2b$, and $\operatorname{GP}(3a + b, 2b - a)$ if $a < 2b$.
