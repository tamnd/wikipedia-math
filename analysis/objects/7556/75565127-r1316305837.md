# Distributional data analysis

## o1 subject: Distributional data analysis

Distributional data analysis is a branch of nonparametric statistics that is related to functional data analysis. It is concerned with random objects that are probability distributions, i.e., the statistical analysis of samples of random distributions where each atom of a sample is a distribution. One of the main challenges in distributional data analysis is that although the space of probability distributions is a convex space, it is not a vector space.

## o2 section: Notation

## o3 definition/notation: Notation

## Notation
Let $\nu$ be a probability measure on $D$, where $D \subset \mathbb{R}^p$ with $p \ge 1$. The probability measure $\nu$ can be equivalently characterized as cumulative distribution function $F$ or probability density function $f$ if it exists. For univariate distributions with $p = 1$, quantile function $Q=F^{-1}$ can also be used.
Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a space of distributions $\nu$ and let $d$ be a metric on $\mathcal{F}$ so that $(\mathcal{F}, d)$ forms a metric space. There are various metrics available for $d$.
For example, suppose $\nu_1, \; \nu_2 \in \mathcal{F}$, and let $f_1$ and $f_2$ be the density functions of $\nu_1$ and $\nu_2$, respectively. The Fisher-Rao metric is defined as
$$d_{FR}(f_1, f_2) = \arccos \left( \int_D \sqrt{f_1(x) f_2(x)} dx \right).$$
For univariate distributions, let $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ be the quantile functions of $\nu_1$ and $\nu_2$. Denote the $L^p$-Wasserstein space as $\mathcal{W}_p$, which is the space of distributions with finite $p$-th moments. Then, for $\nu_1, \; \nu_2 \in \mathcal{W}_p$, the $L^p$-Wasserstein metric is defined as
$$d_{W_p}(\nu_1, \nu_2) = \left( \int_0^1 [Q_1(s) - Q_2(s)]^p ds \right)^{1/p}.$$

## Hypothesis testing
### Wasserstein F-test
Wasserstein $F$-test has been proposed to test for the effects of the predictors in the Fréchet regression framework with the Wasserstein metric. Consider Euclidean predictors $X \in \mathbb{R}^p$ and distributional responses $\nu \in \mathcal{W}_2$. Denote the Wasserstein mean of $\nu$ as $\mu_\oplus^*$, and the sample Wasserstein mean as $\hat{\mu}_\oplus^*$. Consider the global Wasserstein-Fréchet regression model $m_\oplus (x)$ defined in ((1)), which is the conditional Wasserstein mean given $X=x$. The estimator of $m_\oplus (x)$, $\hat{m}_\oplus (x)$ is obtained by minimizing the empirical version of the criterion.
Let $F$, $Q$, $f$,
$F_\oplus^*$, $Q_\oplus^*$, $f_\oplus^*$,
$F_\oplus(x)$, $Q_\oplus(x)$,
and $f_\oplus(x)$ denote the cumulative distribution, quantile, and density functions of $\nu$, $\mu_\oplus^*$, and $m_\oplus(x)$, respectively. For a pair $(X, \nu)$, define $T = Q \circ F_\oplus (X)$ be the optimal transport map from $m_\oplus(X)$ to $\nu$.
Also, define $S = Q_\oplus (X) \circ F_\oplus^*$, the optimal transport map from $\mu_\oplus^*$ to $m_\oplus(x)$. Finally, define the covariance kernel $K(u, v) = \mathbb{E}[\text{Cov}((T\circ S)(u), (T\circ S)(v) )]$ and by the Mercer decomposition, $K(u, v) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \lambda_j \phi_j(u) \phi_j(v)$.
If there are no regression effects, the conditional Wasserstein mean would equal the Wasserstein mean. That is, hypotheses for the test of no effects are
$$H_0: m_\oplus (x) \equiv \mu_\oplus^* \quad \text{v.s.} \quad H_1: \text{Not }H_0.$$
To test for these hypotheses, the proposed global Wasserstein $F$-statistic and its asymptotic distribution are
$$F_G = \sum_{i=1}^n d_{W_2}^2( \hat{m}_\oplus (x), \hat{\mu}_\oplus^*), \quad F_G|X_1, \cdots, X_n \overset{d}{\longrightarrow} \sum_{j=1}^\infty \lambda_j V_j \; a.s.,$$
where $V_j \overset{iid}{\sim} \chi_p^2$. An extension to hypothesis testing for partial regression effects, and alternative testing approximations using the
Satterthwaite's approximation or a bootstrap approach are proposed.
### Tests for the intrinsic mean
The Hilbert sphere $\mathcal{S}^\infty$ is defined as $\mathcal{S}^\infty = \left\{f \in \mathbb{H} : \| f \|_{\mathbb{H}}=1 \right\}$, where $\mathbb{H}$ is a separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert space with inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_{\mathbb{H}}$ and norm $\| \cdot \|_{\mathbb{H}}$. Consider the space of square root densities $\mathcal{X} = \left\{ x:D \to \mathbb{R}: x = \sqrt{f}, \int_D f(t)dt = 1 \right\}$. Then with the Fisher-Rao metric $d_{FR}$ on $f$, $\mathcal{X}$ is the positive orthant of the Hilbert sphere $\mathcal{S}^\infty$ with $\mathbb{H} = L^2(D)$.
Let a chart $\tau: U \subset \mathcal{S}^\infty \to \mathbb{G}$ as a smooth homeomorphism that maps $U$ onto an open subset $\tau(U)$ of a separable Hilbert space $\mathbb{G}$ for coordinates. For example, $\tau$ can be the logarithm map.
Consider a random element $x = \sqrt{f} \in \mathcal{X}$ equipped with the Fisher-Rao metric, and write its Fréchet mean as $\mu$. Let the empirical estimator of $\mu$ using $n$ samples as $\hat{\mu}$. Then central limit theorem for $\hat{\mu}_\tau = \tau(\hat{\mu})$ and $\mu_\tau = \tau(\mu)$ holds: $\sqrt{n}(\hat{\mu}_\tau - \mu_\tau ) \overset{L}{\longrightarrow} Z, \; n \to \infty$,
where $Z$ is a Gaussian random element in $\mathbb{G}$ with mean 0 and covariance operator $\mathcal{T}$. Let the eigenvalue-eigenfunction pairs of $\mathcal{T}$ and the estimated covariance operator $\hat{\mathcal{T}}$ as $(\lambda_k, \phi_k)_{k=1}^\infty$ and $(\hat\lambda_k, \hat\phi_k)_{k=1}^\infty$, respectively.
Consider one-sample hypothesis testing
$$H_0: \mu = \mu_0 \quad \text{v.s.} \quad H_1: \mu \neq \mu_0,$$
with $\mu_0 \in \mathcal{S}^\infty$. Denote $\| \cdot \|_{\mathbb{G}}$ and $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_{\mathbb{G}}$ as the norm and inner product in $\mathbb{G}$. The test statistics and their limiting distributions are
$$\begin{aligned}
T_1 &= n \| \tau(\hat{\mu}) - \tau(\mu_0)\|_\mathbb{G}^2 \overset{L}{\longrightarrow} \lambda_k W_k, \\
S_1 &= n \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{\langle \tau(\hat{\mu}) - \tau(\mu_0), \hat{\phi}_k \rangle_\mathbb{G}^2}{\hat{\lambda}_k} \overset{L}{\longrightarrow} \chi_K^2,
\end{aligned}$$
where $W_k \overset{iid}{\sim} \chi_1^2$. The actual testing procedure can be done by employing the limiting distributions with Monte Carlo simulations, or bootstrap tests are possible. An extension to the two-sample test and paired test are also proposed.
