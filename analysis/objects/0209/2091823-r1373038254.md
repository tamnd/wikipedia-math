## o10 example: Example

Example Consider the Prothero-Robinson test problem
$\dot x \, = \, \lambda (x - \sin\omega t) + \omega\cos\omega t \, ; \qquad x(0)=x_0 \, ,$
with exact solution $x(t) = x_0\,{\mathrm e}^{\lambda t} + \sin\omega t$. Taking $x_0=0$, the homogeneous solution is not present in the exact solution $x(t) = \sin\omega t$, but the exponential term will show up in the local solutions passing through the points $x_n$ generated by the numerical methods. Taking $\omega = \pi$ the problem is solved on $[0,1]$, using $N=5$ and $10$ steps, respectively ($h=0.2$ and $h=0.1$). The experiment demonstrates the impact of varying $h\lambda$, which affects the stability/damping of the numerical methods. We take $\lambda = -20$, using the same computational setup for the explicit and implicit Euler methods.
The results are shown in Figure 1. With $T\lambda = -20$, there is a moderately strong exponential damping, as is evident from the fast contracting local solutions. The setup is deliberately understated for visualization purposes; in real stiff equations much larger values would be typical. Nevertheless, $\lambda$ is sufficiently large and negative to demonstrate the step size limitations of the explicit Euler method. For $N=5$, its computed solution becomes oscillatory and diverges from the exact solution, even though the initial value was taken on the exact solution. Repeating the same exercise for $T\lambda = -2000$, say, shows how the difference between the two methods grows with increasing stiffness.
In more realistic problems, the instability is often more dramatic and referred to as the solution "blowing up". Here, the maximum stable step size ($h = 0.1$) has only been exceeded by a small factor. Meanwhile, the implicit Euler method proceeds without loss of stability, and is able to produce accurate results also for the larger step size.
In real computations, stiff equations are solved using adaptive methods. These select the step size automatically to keep the computational process in check, usually controlling both accuracy and stability. Thus, if an adaptive explicit method is used, the solution may not go unstable, as the adaptivity takes measures not to exceed the maximum stable step size. Instead, the method proceeds at the (prohibitive) cost of low efficiency.
The essence of the analysis is that a problem parameter, $\lambda$, which is characteristic of the vector field and related to the transient behavior of solutions, is linked to a time scale, either the step size $h$ or the range of integration $T$ directly. The products $h\lambda$ or $T\lambda$ are both dimensionless, scaling invariant, and negative. A general stiffness concept must reflect these properties, and also be able to address stiffness in nonlinear differential equations.
