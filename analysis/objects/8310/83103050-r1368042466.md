# Fine grained complexity

## o1 subject: Fine grained complexity

Fine-grained complexity is a subfield of computational complexity theory that studies the time required to solve computational problems, especially those within the class P. While classical complexity theory distinguishes problems by whether they contain (most known through the P versus NP problem), fine-grained complexity seeks to distinguish problems solvable in $O(n)$ time from those requiring $\Theta(n^{2})$ or $\Theta(n^{3})$ time, and to identify the precise polynomial exponent of fundamental problems.

## o29 section: Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH)

## o30 statement/conjecture: Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH)

### Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH)
SETH says that for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there is some $k$ such that $k$-SAT on $n$ variables and $m$ clauses cannot be solved in time $O(2^{(1-\varepsilon)n} \cdot \operatorname{poly}(m))$. Equivalently: there is no algorithm for general CNF-SAT that beats brute force by an exponential factor.
SETH is a stronger statement than ETH (which only says 3-SAT requires $2^{\Omega(n)}$ time). It is the working hypothesis behind most fine-grained lower bounds for sequence and string problems, almost always going through OV.

## o31 section: Orthogonal Vectors (OV) hypothesis

## o32 statement/conjecture: Orthogonal Vectors (OV) hypothesis

### Orthogonal Vectors (OV) hypothesis
The Orthogonal Vectors problem asks: given $n$ Boolean vectors of dimension $d = \omega(\log n)$, are there two whose inner product (over the integers) is zero? Brute force takes $O(n^{2}d)$ time. The fastest known algorithm, from Abboud, Vassilevska Williams, and Yu (2015), runs in time $n^{2 - 1/\Theta(\log(d/\log n))}$ which is only barely subquadratic, and only for small $d$.
The OV hypothesis says that OV requires $n^{2-o(1)}$ time. Williams' 2004 reduction showed that SETH implies the OV hypothesis, so falsifying OV would also falsify SETH.

## o34 section: 3-SUM hypothesis

## o35 statement/conjecture: 3-SUM hypothesis

### 3-SUM hypothesis
The 3-SUM problem asks: given $n$ integers, are there three of them that sum to zero? The classical algorithm runs in $O(n^{2})$ time. The best known algorithms run in roughly $n^{2} / \log^{2} n$ time, both for integers and for real numbers. The 3-SUM hypothesis says that 3-SUM on integers in $\{-n^{3}, \ldots, n^{3}$} requires $n^{2-o(1)}$ time. It is the source of conditional lower bounds for many computational geometry problems and a number of dynamic data structure problems.

## o39 statement/conjecture: All-Pairs Shortest Paths (APSP) hypothesis

### All-Pairs Shortest Paths (APSP) hypothesis
APSP asks: given an $n$-vertex weighted graph, find the shortest-path distance between every pair of vertices. The classical Floyd–Warshall algorithm runs in $O(n^{3})$ time. The best known algorithm, due to Williams (2014), runs in $n^{3} / 2^{\Omega(\surd\log n)}$ time which is faster than $n^{3}$ by a subpolynomial factor, but no truly subcubic algorithm is known. The APSP hypothesis says that APSP on graphs with $O(\log n)$-bit edge weights requires $n^{3-o(1)}$ time.

## Methods
The main technical tool is the fine-grained reduction itself. Given problems $A$ and $B$ with conjectured time bounds $a(n)$ and $b(n)$, a fine-grained reduction from $A$ to $B$ is an algorithm that solves $A$-instances of size $n$ by making calls to $B$ on instances of sizes $n_{1}, \ldots, n_{k}$, such that the total work, or the time for the calls plus the overhead of the reduction, is below $a(n)$ whenever each call to $B$ runs faster than $b(n_{i})$. The point is that any subpolynomial improvement in $B$'s running time would yield a subpolynomial improvement in $A$'s.
A few techniques recur. Gadget reductions encode instances of one problem as structured instances of another, often by building graphs whose distances or other properties encode the answer to the original problem. Self-reductions reduce a problem to smaller instances of itself; the 3-SUM self-reduction via hashing is vital for worst-case-to-average-case results. On the algorithm side, the polynomial method (used by Williams in 2014 to break the $n^{3}$ barrier for APSP) and fast matrix multiplication (with the matrix multiplication exponent $\omega$) are responsible for most of the known improvements over textbook algorithms.

## o54 statement/conjecture: Open problems

## Open problems
Every major hardness hypothesis is open. Among the most consequential questions:
Is SETH true? This is the biggest one, since refuting SETH would simultaneously knock out a large fraction of the field's lower bound results. Substantially better-than-brute-force algorithms for $k$-SAT exist for any fixed $k$, but none break the $2^{(1-\varepsilon)n}$ barrier for unbounded $k$.
Are the major hypotheses independent of each other? Also open. There is no known reduction in either direction between, for example, the 3-SUM and APSP hypotheses. Carmosino et al. (2016) gave evidence that no such reduction exists in certain restricted forms, but this is not a proof of independence.
Closing gaps for specific problems. For many problems, large gaps remain between the best known upper and lower bounds. Closing these gaps — either by improving algorithms or by proving hardness from stronger hypotheses — is an active research direction.
