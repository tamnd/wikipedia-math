# Zero-symmetric graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Zero-symmetric graph"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) field of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **zero-symmetric graph** is a [connected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_graph) in which each vertex has exactly three incident edges and, for each two vertices, there is a unique [symmetry](wikipedia://en/15094186) taking one vertex to the other. Such a graph is a [vertex-transitive graph](wikipedia://en/581175) but cannot be an [edge-transitive graph](wikipedia://en/584406): the number of symmetries equals the number of vertices, too few to take every edge to every other edge.[^o2]

*Image: Two-edge-orbit GRR.svg.* The smallest zero-symmetric graph with two edge orbits

The name for this class of graphs was coined by [R. M. Foster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._M._Foster) in a 1966 letter to [H. S. M. Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Scott_MacDonald_Coxeter).[^o3] In the context of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), zero-symmetric graphs are also called **graphical regular representations** of their symmetry groups.[^o4]

:::

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The smallest zero-symmetric graph is a nonplanar graph with 18 vertices.[^o7] Its [LCF notation](wikipedia://en/24186720) is \[5,−5\]<sup>9</sup>.

Among [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314), the [truncated cuboctahedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truncated_cuboctahedral_graph) and [truncated icosidodecahedral graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truncated_icosidodecahedral_graph) are also zero-symmetric.[^o8]

These examples are all [bipartite graphs](wikipedia://en/244431). However, there exist larger examples of zero-symmetric graphs that are not bipartite.[^o9]

These examples also have three different symmetry classes (orbits) of edges. However, there exist zero-symmetric graphs with only two orbits of edges. The smallest such graph has 20 vertices, with [LCF notation](wikipedia://en/24186720) \[6,6,-6,-6\]<sup>5</sup>.[^o10]

:::

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Every finite zero-symmetric graph is a [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277), a property that does not always hold for cubic vertex-transitive graphs more generally and that helps in the solution of [combinatorial enumeration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_enumeration) tasks concerning zero-symmetric graphs. There are 97687 zero-symmetric graphs on up to 1280 vertices. These graphs form 89% of the cubic Cayley graphs and 88% of all connected vertex-transitive cubic graphs on the same number of vertices.[^o13]

::: {#o14 .problem cue=unsolved}

Does every finite zero-symmetric graph contain a [Hamiltonian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle)?

:::

All known finite connected zero-symmetric graphs contain a [Hamiltonian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle), but it is unknown whether every finite connected zero-symmetric graph is necessarily Hamiltonian.[^o15] This is a special case of the [Lovász conjecture](wikipedia://en/4367424) that (with five known exceptions, none of which is zero-symmetric) every finite connected vertex-transitive graph and every finite Cayley graph is Hamiltonian.

:::
