# Associahedron

*Image: Associahedron K5 front.svg.* Associahedron $K_{5}$ (front)

*Image: Associahedron K5 back.svg.* Associahedron $K_{5}$ (back)

*Image: Tamari lattice, ovals.svg.* $K_{5}$ is the [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199) of the [Tamari lattice](wikipedia://en/20353278) $T_{4}$.

*Image: Associahedron K5 faces, ovals.svg.* The 9 faces of $K_{5}$Each vertex in the above Hasse diagram has the ovals from the 3 adjacent faces. Faces whose ovals intersect do not touch.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Associahedron"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **associahedron** $K_{n}$ is an $(n - 2)$-dimensional [convex polytope](wikipedia://en/1817885) in which each [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) corresponds to a way of correctly inserting opening and closing [parentheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parentheses) in a string of $n$ letters, and the [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034) correspond to single application of the [associativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity) rule. Equivalently, the vertices of an associahedron correspond to the [triangulations](wikipedia://en/679384) of a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) with $n + 1$ sides, and the edges correspond to edge flips in which a single diagonal is removed from a triangulation and replaced by a different diagonal. Associahedra are also called **Stasheff polytopes** after the work of [Jim Stasheff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Stasheff), who rediscovered them in the early 1960s[^o2] after earlier work on them by [Dov Tamari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dov_Tamari_%28mathematician%29).[^o3]

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The one-dimensional associahedron *K*<sub>3</sub> represents the two parenthesizations ((*xy*)*z*) and (*x*(*yz*)) of three symbols, or the two triangulations of a square. It is itself a line segment.

The two-dimensional associahedron *K*<sub>4</sub> represents the five parenthesizations of four symbols, or the five triangulations of a regular pentagon. It is itself a pentagon and is related to the *pentagon diagram* of a [monoidal category](wikipedia://en/330604).

The three-dimensional associahedron *K*<sub>5</sub> is an [enneahedron](wikipedia://en/33329655) with nine faces (three disjoint quadrilaterals and six pentagons) and fourteen vertices, and its dual is the [triaugmented triangular prism](wikipedia://en/728019).[^o6]

:::

## Realization {#Realization}

*Image: Associahedron.stl.* 3D model of an associahedron

Initially [Jim Stasheff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Stasheff) considered these objects as [curvilinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curvilinear) polytopes. Subsequently, they were given coordinates as [convex polytopes](wikipedia://en/1817885) in several different ways; see the introduction of (Ceballos Santos Ziegler 2015) for a survey.[^o9]

One method of realizing the associahedron is as the [secondary polytope](wikipedia://en/3313527) of a regular polygon.[^o9] In this construction, each triangulation of a regular polygon with *n* + 1 sides corresponds to a point in (*n* + 1)-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), whose *i*th coordinate is the total area of the triangles incident to the *i*th vertex of the polygon. For instance, the two triangulations of the [unit square](wikipedia://en/285301) give rise in this way to two four-dimensional points with coordinates (1, 1/2, 1, 1/2) and (1/2, 1, 1/2, 1). The [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of these two points is the realization of the associahedron *K*<sub>3</sub>. Although it lives in a 4-dimensional space, it forms a line segment (a 1-dimensional polytope) within that space. Similarly, the associahedron *K*<sub>4</sub> can be realized in this way as a [regular pentagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_pentagon) in five-dimensional Euclidean space, whose vertex coordinates are the [cyclic permutations](wikipedia://en/449166) of the vector (1, 2 + φ, 1, 1 + φ, 1 + φ) where φ denotes the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386). Because the possible triangles within a [regular hexagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_hexagon) have areas that are integer multiples of each other, this construction can be used to give integer coordinates (in six dimensions) to the three-dimensional associahedron *K*<sub>5</sub>; however (as the example of *K*<sub>4</sub> already shows) this construction in general leads to irrational numbers as coordinates.

Another realization, due to [Jean-Louis Loday](wikipedia://en/36183327), is based on the correspondence of the vertices of the associahedron with *n*-leaf [rooted binary trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rooted_binary_tree) and produces integer coordinates in (*n* − 1)-dimensional space. The *i*th coordinate of Loday's realization is *a<sub>i</sub>b<sub>i</sub>*, where *a<sub>i</sub>* is the number of leaf descendants of the left child of the *i*th internal node of the tree (in left-to-right order) and *b<sub>i</sub>* is the number of leaf descendants of the right child. [^o10] The coordinates of each vertex sum to a constant and can thus be considered as [barycentric coordinates](wikipedia://en/762954) on the regular (*n* − 2)-[simplex](wikipedia://en/39781).

It is possible to realize the associahedron directly in (*n* − 2)-dimensional space as a polytope for which all of the [face normal vectors](wikipedia://en/173224) have coordinates that are 0, +1, or −1. There are exponentially many combinatorially distinct ways of doing this.[^o9][^o11]

*Image: Associahedron.gif.* *K*<sub>5</sub> as an order-4 truncated [triangular bipyramid](wikipedia://en/646933)

Because *K*<sub>5</sub> is a polyhedron only with vertices in which 3 edges come together it is possible for a [hydrocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) to exist (similar to the [Platonic hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_hydrocarbon)) whose chemical structure is represented by the skeleton of *K*<sub>5</sub>.[^o12] This "[associahedrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associahedrane)" C<sub>14</sub>H<sub>14</sub> would have the [SMILES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMILES) notation: C12-C3-C4-C1-C5-C6-C2-C7-C3-C8-C4-C5-C6-C78. Its edges would be of approximately equal length, but the vertices of each face would not necessarily be coplanar. Indeed, *K*<sub>5</sub> is a [near-miss Johnson solid](wikipedia://en/3881930): it looks like it might be possible to make from squares and regular pentagons, but it is not. If the pentagonal faces are chosen as regular pentagons, then the quadrilateral faces will not be planar, so any realization as a convex polyhedron must cause the pentagonal faces to be irregular.[^o13]

The number of (*n* − *k*)-dimensional faces of the associahedron of order *n* (K<sub>*n*+1</sub>) is given by the [number triangle](wikipedia://en/28639331) (*n*, *k*), shown on the right.

The number of vertices in *K*<sub>*n*+1</sub> is the *n*-th [Catalan number](wikipedia://en/216488) (right diagonal in the triangle).

The number of [facets](wikipedia://en/7186647) in *K*<sub>*n*+1</sub> (for *n* ≥ 2) is the *n*-th [triangular number](wikipedia://en/213915) minus one (second column in the triangle), because each facet corresponds to a 2-[subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of the *n* objects whose groupings form the Tamari lattice *T*<sub>*n*</sub>, except the 2-subset that contains the first and the last element.

The number of faces of all dimensions (including the associahedron itself as a face, but not including the empty set) is a [Schröder–Hipparchus number](wikipedia://en/33490570) (row sums of the triangle).[^o17]

## Diameter {#Diameter}

In the late 1980s, in connection with the problem of [rotation distance](wikipedia://en/62539802), [Daniel Sleator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Sleator), [Robert Tarjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Tarjan), and [William Thurston](wikipedia://en/39190) provided a proof that the diameter of the *n*-dimensional associahedron *K*<sub>*n* + 2</sub> is at most 2*n* − 4 for infinitely many *n* and for all "large enough" values of *n*.[^o19] They also proved that this upper bound is tight when *n* is large enough, and conjectured that "large enough" means "strictly greater than 9". This conjecture was proved in 2012 by Lionel Pournin.[^o20]

## Scattering amplitudes {#Scattering_amplitudes}

In 2017, Mizera[^o22] and Arkani-Hamed et al.[^o23] showed that the associahedron plays a central role in the theory of scattering amplitudes for the bi-adjoint cubic scalar theory. In particular, there exists an associahedron in the space of scattering kinematics, and the tree level scattering amplitude is the volume of the dual associahedron.[^o23] The associahedron also helps explaining the relations between scattering amplitudes of open and closed [strings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_%28physics%29) in [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305).[^o22]
