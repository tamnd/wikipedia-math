# Velocity potential

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Velocity potential"}

Within the applied mathematical study of [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034) and [continuum mechanics](wikipedia://en/5918), a **velocity potential** is a [scalar potential](wikipedia://en/461654) used in [potential flow](wikipedia://en/58320) theory. It was introduced by [Joseph-Louis Lagrange](wikipedia://en/87793) in 1788.[^o2]

:::

Suppose a smooth [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) $\mathbf{u}$ in a simple connected region represents the [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity) of a fluid at each point. This flow field is said to be [irrotational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrotational) when

$$\nabla \times \mathbf{u} =0 \,.$$

If the flow field is irrotational, then it can be also be represented as the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) of a [scalar](wikipedia://en/161879) function $\phi$:

$$\mathbf{u} = \nabla \phi\ = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} \mathbf{i} + \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} \mathbf{j} + \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial z} \mathbf{k} \,.$$

$\phi$ is known as a **velocity potential** for $\mathbf{u}$. Velocity potentials are unique up to a constant and a function solely of the temporal variable. So if $\phi(x,y,z)$ is a velocity potential, then $\phi(x,y,z)+f(t) + C$ generates the same flow field as $\phi(x,y,z)$.

The [Laplacian](wikipedia://en/174706) of a velocity potential is equal to the [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) of the corresponding flow. Hence if a velocity potential satisfies [Laplace equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace_equation), the [flow](wikipedia://en/58320) is [incompressible](wikipedia://en/461477).

Unlike a [stream function](wikipedia://en/218091), a velocity potential can exist in three-dimensional flow.

## Usage in acoustics {#Usage_in_acoustics}

In theoretical [acoustics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustics),[^o4] it is often desirable to work with the [acoustic wave equation](wikipedia://en/2630105) of the velocity potential $\phi$ instead of pressure $p$ and/or [particle velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_velocity) $\mathbf{u}$.

$$\nabla ^2 \phi - \frac{1}{c^2} \frac{ \partial^2 \phi }{ \partial t ^2 } = 0$$

Solving the wave equation for either $p$ field or $\mathbf{u}$ field does not necessarily provide a simple answer for the other field. On the other hand, when $\phi$ is solved for, not only is $\mathbf{u}$ found as given above, but $p$ is also easily found—from the (linearised) [Bernoulli equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli's_principle) for [irrotational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrotational_flow) and [unsteady flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unsteady_flow)—as

$$p = -\rho \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} \,.$$
