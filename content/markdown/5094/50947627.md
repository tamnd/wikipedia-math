# Map graph

*Image: Map graph.svg.* A map graph (top), the [cocktail party graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocktail_party_graph) $K_{2,2,2,2}$, defined by corner adjacency of eight regions in the plane (lower left), or as the half-square of a planar bipartite graph (lower right, the graph of the [rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631))

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Map graph"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a branch of mathematics, a **map graph** is an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) formed as the [intersection graph](wikipedia://en/7726759) of finitely many simply connected and internally disjoint regions of the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410). The map graphs include the [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314), but are more general. Any number of regions can meet at a common corner (as in the [Four Corners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Corners) of the United States, where four states meet), and when they do the map graph will contain a [clique](wikipedia://en/524466) connecting the corresponding vertices, unlike planar graphs in which the largest cliques have only four vertices.[^o2] Another example of a map graph is the [king's graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_graph), a map graph of the squares of the [chessboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chessboard) connecting pairs of squares between which the chess king can move.

:::

## Combinatorial representation {#Combinatorial_representation}

Map graphs can be represented combinatorially as the "half-squares of planar bipartite graphs". That is, let $G = (U,V,E)$ be a planar [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431), with bipartition $(U,V)$. The [square](wikipedia://en/38618878) of $G$ is another graph on the same vertex set, in which two vertices are adjacent in the square when they are at most two steps apart in $G$. The half-square or [bipartite half](wikipedia://en/39329230) is the [induced subgraph](wikipedia://en/1655188) of one side of the bipartition (say $V$) in the square graph: its vertex set is $V$ and it has an edge between each two vertices in $V$ that are two steps apart in $G$. The half-square is a map graph. It can be represented geometrically by finding a [planar embedding](wikipedia://en/325813) of $G$, and expanding each vertex of $V$ and its adjacent edges into a star-shaped region, so that these regions touch at the vertices of $U$. Conversely, every map graph can be represented as a half-square in this way.[^o2]

## Computational complexity {#Computational_complexity}

In 1998, [Mikkel Thorup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikkel_Thorup) claimed that map graphs can be recognized in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time).[^o5] However, the high exponent of the algorithm that he sketched makes it impractical, and Thorup has not published the details of his method and its proof.[^o6]

The [maximum independent set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_independent_set) problem has a [polynomial-time approximation scheme](wikipedia://en/666431) for map graphs, and the [chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number) can be approximated to within a factor of two in polynomial time.[^o7] The theory of [bidimensionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bidimensionality) leads to many other approximation algorithms and [fixed-parameter tractable](wikipedia://en/603026) algorithms for optimization problems on map graphs.[^o8][^o9][^o10]

## Variations and related concepts {#Variations_and_related_concepts}

A $k$-map graph is a map graph derived from a set of regions in which at most $k$ regions meet at any point. Equivalently, it is the half-square of a planar bipartite graph in which the vertex set $U$ (the side of the bipartition not used to induce the half-square) has maximum [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) $k$. A 3-map graph is a [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314), and every planar graph can be represented as a 3-map graph. Every 4-map graph is a [1-planar graph](wikipedia://en/38249632), a graph that can be drawn with at most one crossing per edge, and every optimal 1-planar graph (a graph formed from a planar quadrangulation by adding two crossing diagonals to every quadrilateral face) is a 4-map graph. However, some other 1-planar graphs are not map graphs, because (unlike map graphs) their 1-planar drawings include crossing edges that are not part of a four-vertex complete subgraph. As an example, the [utility graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_graph) $K_{3,3}$ is 1-planar, but has no four-vertex complete subgraph, so it is not a 4-map graph.[^o2]
