# Linear Operators (book)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Linear Operators"}

***Linear Operators*** is a three-volume textbook on the theory of [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operators), written by [Nelson Dunford](wikipedia://en/21022291) and [Jacob T. Schwartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_T._Schwartz). The three volumes are (I) *General Theory*; (II) *Spectral Theory, Self Adjoint Operators in Hilbert Space*; and (III) *Spectral Operators*. The first volume was published in 1958, the second in 1963, and the third in 1971. All three volumes were reprinted by [Wiley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiley_%28publisher%29) in 1988. Canonically cited as **Dunford and Schwartz**,[^o3] the textbook has been referred to as "the definitive work" on linear operators.[^o5]

:::

The work began as a written set of solutions to the problems for Dunford's graduate course in linear operators at [Yale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University).[^o2] Schwartz, a prodigy, had taken his undergraduate degree at Yale in 1948, age 18. In 1949 he began his graduate studies and enrolled in his course. Dunford recognised Schwartz's intelligence and they began a long collaboration, with Dunford acting as Schwartz's advisor for his dissertation *Linear Elliptic Differential Operators*.[^o3][^o2] One fruit of their collaboration was the [Dunford-Schwartz theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunford-Schwartz_theorem).[^o7] The work was originally intended to be a short introduction to [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) (the original material comprising what was published as Chapters 2, 4, 7 and part of 10 in Volume I) but the material ballooned.[^o2] The work enjoyed funding from the [Office of Naval Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Naval_Research) and a popular joke at the time was that every nuclear submarine had a copy.[^o2] [William G. Bade](wikipedia://en/46592286) and [Robert G. Bartle](wikipedia://en/8646865) were brought on as research assistants.[^o4] Dunford retired shortly after finishing the final volume.[^o2] Schwartz, however, went on to write similarly pathbreaking books in various other areas of mathematics.[^o3]

The book met with acclaim when published. It won the [Leroy P. Steele Prize](wikipedia://en/1252550) in 1981, awarded by the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822).[^o5] In the citation for this prize, the committee observed "This monumental work of 2,592 pages must be the most comprehensive of its kind in mathematics ... A whole generation of analysts has been trained from it."[^o6] [Peter Lax](wikipedia://en/486365) remarked that it "contained everything known, and many things not yet known, on linear functional analysis."[^o7] [Béla Szőkefalvi-Nagy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Béla_Szőkefalvi-Nagy) wrote in a review of the first volume: "the authors have created an extraordinarily important and valuable work that is distinguished in particular by its monumental completeness, clear organization, and attractive exposition".[^o7] [Gian-Carlo Rota](wikipedia://en/101891), who was involved in checking the exercises, wrote that "the contrast between the uncompromising abstraction of the text and the incredible variety of the concrete examples in the exercises is immensely beneficial to any student learning [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396)."[^o2]

Every chapter of the book ends with a section entitled "Notes and Remarks", giving historical background on the topic and informal discussion of related topics. The book contains more than a thousand exercises, wide-ranging and often difficult.[^o7] One particularly difficult exercise was not solved until Dunford assigned it to a young [Robert Langlands](wikipedia://en/25986).[^o2]
