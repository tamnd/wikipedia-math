# Subshift of finite type

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Subshift of finite type"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **subshifts of finite type** are [shift spaces](wikipedia://en/15470598) defined by a finite set of forbidden words. They are used to model [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems), and in particular are objects of study in [symbolic dynamics](wikipedia://en/1986263) and [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986). They also describe the set of all possible sequences executed by a [finite-state machine](wikipedia://en/10931). The most widely studied [shift spaces](wikipedia://en/15470598) are the subshifts of finite type.

:::

## Motivating examples {#Motivating_examples}

One example of a (one-sided) shift of finite type is the set of all sequences, infinite on one end only, that can be made up of the letters $A, B$, like $AAA\cdots, ABAB\cdots, \dots$. This is known as a [full shift](wikipedia://en/15470598#Examples) and is denoted by $\{A,B\}^\mathbb{N}$.

By forbidding the word $BB$, one defines a shift of finite type $\Sigma = \{(x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots) \mid x_ix_{i+1} \neq BB\}$ called the [golden shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_shift), so-called because the numbers of legal words of length $n$ are the [Fibonacci numbers](wikipedia://en/10918). Two-sided shifts of finite type are similar, but consist of sequences that are infinite on both ends.

A subshift can be defined by a directed graph on the letters, such as the graph $A \to B \to C \to A$. It consists of sequences whose transitions between consecutive letters are only those allowed by the graph. For this example, the subshift consists of only three one-sided sequences: $ABCABC\cdots, BCABCA\cdots, CABCAB\cdots$. Similarly, the two-sided subshift described by this graph consists of only three two-sided sequences.

Other directed graphs on the same letters produce other subshifts. For example, adding another arrow $A\to C$ to the graph produces a subshift that, instead of containing three sequences, contains [an uncountably infinite number](wikipedia://en/1574901) of sequences. Up to a local recoding of letters, every subshift of finite type can be described by such a directed graph.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a finite set of $n$ symbols (alphabet). Let $X$ denote the set $\mathcal{A}^\mathbb{Z}$ of all bi-infinite sequences of elements of $\mathcal{A}$ together with the [shift operator](wikipedia://en/467384) $T$. We endow $\mathcal{A}$ with the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology) and $X$ with the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910). A **symbolic flow** or **subshift** is a [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) $T$-invariant subset $Y$ of $X$ [^o5] and the associated language $\mathcal{L}_Y$ is the set of finite subwords of elements of $Y$.[^o6]

Let $F$ be a finite set of words in the alphabet $\mathcal{A}$, which are called **forbidden words**. The associated **subshift of finite type** is defined to be the space

$$\Sigma_F = \{(x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots) \mid \forall i,k \geq 0, x_ix_{i+1}\cdots x_{i+k} \notin F\}$$

of sequences that avoid the set of forbidden words $F$.

If the sequence extends to infinity in only one direction as above, it is called a **one-sided** subshift of finite type, and if it is [bilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi-infinite_sequence), it is called a **two-sided** subshift of finite type.

The [shift operator](wikipedia://en/467384) $T$ maps a sequence in the one- or two-sided shift to another by shifting all symbols to the left, i.e.

$$(T(x))_{i}=x_{i+1}.$$

Clearly this map is only invertible in the case of the two-sided shift.

A particularly useful subclass is given by the **edge shifts**. Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) with entries in $\{0, 1\}.$ Using these elements we construct a [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) $G = (V, E)$ with $V$ the set of vertices and $E$ the set of edges containing the directed edge $x \to y$ in $E$ if and only if $A_{x, y} = 1$. Let $Y$ be the set of all infinite **admissible** sequences of edges, where by *admissible* it is meant that the sequence is a [walk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walk_%28graph_theory%29) of the graph, and the sequence can be either one-sided or two-sided infinite. Let $T$ be the [left shift operator](wikipedia://en/467384#Sequences) on such sequences; it plays the role of the time-evolution operator of the dynamical system. An **edge shift** is then defined as a pair $(Y, T)$ obtained in this way.

Formally, one may define the sequences of edges as

$$\Sigma_{A}^{+} = \left\{ (x_0,x_1,\ldots): x_j \in V, A_{x_{j}x_{j+1}}=1, j \in \mathbb{N} \right\}.$$

This is the space of all sequences of symbols such that the symbol $p$ can be followed by the symbol $q$ only if the $(p, q)$-th entry of the matrix $A$ is 1. The space of all [bi-infinite sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi-infinite_sequence) is defined analogously:

$$\Sigma_{A} = \left\{ (\ldots, x_{-1},x_0,x_1,\ldots): x_j \in V, A_{x_{j}x_{j+1}}=1, j\in\mathbb{Z} \right\}.$$

Edge shifts are a subset of the subshifts of finite type whose set of forbidden words comprise only two-letter words. On the other hand, it can be shown that every subshift of finite type is [topologically conjugate](wikipedia://en/5506149) to an edge shift via a [local recoding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtis-Hedlund-Lyndon_theorem).[^o7]

An edge shift is called **transitive** if $G$ is [strongly connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strongly-connected_digraph): there is a sequence of edges from any one vertex to any other vertex. Subshifts of finite type with dense orbits are precisely those that are conjugate to a transitive edge shift.

An important special case is the **full $n$-shift**: it has a graph with an edge that connects every vertex to every other vertex; that is, all of the entries of the adjacency matrix are 1. The full $n$-shift corresponds to the [Bernoulli scheme](wikipedia://en/2580555) without the [measure](wikipedia://en/19873).

:::

## Terminology {#Terminology}

By convention, the term **shift** is understood to refer to the full $n$-shift. A **subshift** is then any subspace of the full shift that is shift-invariant (that is, a subspace that is invariant under the action of the shift operator), non-empty, and closed for the product topology defined below. Some subshifts can be characterized by a transition matrix, as above; such subshifts are then called subshifts of finite type. Often, subshifts of finite type are called simply **shifts of finite type**. Subshifts of finite type are also sometimes called **topological Markov shifts**.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

Many chaotic [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087) are isomorphic to subshifts of finite type; examples include systems with [transverse homoclinic connections](wikipedia://en/29764173), [diffeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/8564) of [closed manifolds](wikipedia://en/669475) with positive [metric entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_entropy), and piecewise-linear Markov maps of the interval.

:::

## Topology {#Topology}

A subshift has a natural topology, derived from the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910) on $V^\mathbb{Z},$ where

$$V^\mathbb{Z}= \prod_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} V = \{ x=(\ldots,x_{-1},x_0,x_1,\ldots) : x_k \in V \; \forall k \in \mathbb{Z} \}$$

and $V$ is given the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology). A basis for the topology of $V^\mathbb{Z},$ which induces the topology of the subshift, is the family of [cylinder sets](wikipedia://en/2613658)

$$C_t[a_0, \ldots, a_s]= \{x \in V^\mathbb{Z} : x_t = a_0, \ldots ,x_{t+s} = a_s \}$$

The cylinder sets are [clopen sets](wikipedia://en/233398) in $V^\mathbb{Z}.$ Every open set in $V^\mathbb{Z}$ is a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) union of cylinder sets. Every open set in the subshift is the intersection of an open set of $V^\mathbb{Z}$ with the subshift. With respect to this topology, the shift $T$ is a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660); that is, with respect to this topology, it is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) with continuous inverse.

The space $V^\mathbb{Z}$ is homeomorphic to a [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172).

## Metric {#Metric}

A variety of different metrics can be defined on a shift space. One can define a metric on a shift space by considering two points to be "close" if they have many initial symbols in common; this is the [$p$-adic metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_metric). In fact, both the one- and two-sided shift spaces are [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018).

## Measure {#Measure}

A subshift of finite type may be endowed with any one of several different [measures](wikipedia://en/19873), thus leading to a [measure-preserving dynamical system](wikipedia://en/398931). A common object of study is the **Markov measure**, which is an extension of a [Markov chain](wikipedia://en/60876) to the topology of the shift.

A Markov chain is a pair $(P, \pi)$ consisting of the **transition matrix**, an $n \times n$ matrix $P = (p_{ij})$ for which all $p_{ij} \ge 0$ and

$$\sum_{j=1}^np_{ij}=1$$

for all $i$. The **stationary probability vector** $\pi = (\pi_{i})$ has all $\pi_{i} \ge 0$, $\sum \pi_i = 1$ and has

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \pi_i p_{ij}= \pi_j.$$

A Markov chain, as defined above, is said to be **compatible** with the shift of finite type if $p_{ij} = 0$ whenever $A_{ij} = 0$. The **Markov measure** of a cylinder set may then be defined by

$$\mu(C_t[a_0,\ldots,a_s]) = \pi_{a_0} p_{a_0,a_1} \cdots p_{a_{s-1}, a_s}$$

The [Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov–Sinai_entropy) with relation to the Markov measure is

$$s_\mu = -\sum_{i=1}^n \pi_i \sum_{j=1}^n p_{ij} \log p_{ij}$$

### Markov and non-Markov measures {#Markov_and_non-Markov_measures}

*Image: Blackwell HMM example.png.* The hidden part of a hidden Markov model, whose observable states is non-Markovian

As above, given a [Markov transition matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_transition_matrix) and an invariant distribution on the states, we can impose a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) on the subshift. For example, consider the Markov chain given on the left on the states $A, B_1, B_2$, with invariant distribution $\pi = (2/7, 4/7, 1/7)$. If we "forget" the distinction between $B_1, B_2$, we project this subshift on $A, B_1, B_2$ onto a subshift on $A, B$, and this projection also projects the probability measure down to a probability measure on the subshift on $A, B$.

The curious thing is that the probability measure on the subshift on $A, B$ is not created by a Markov chain on $A, B$, not even multiple orders. Intuitively, this is because if one observes a long sequence of $B^n$, then one would become increasingly sure that the $Pr(A | B^n) \to \frac 23$, meaning that the observable part of the system can be affected by something infinitely in the past.[^o22][^o16]

Conversely, there exists a subshift on 6 symbols, projected to a subshift on 2 symbols, such that any Markov measure on the smaller subshift has a preimage measure that is not Markov of any order (Example 2.6 [^o16]).

## Zeta function {#Zeta_function}

The [Artin–Mazur zeta function](wikipedia://en/1342156) is defined as the [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012)

$$\zeta(z)=\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \Bigl| \textrm{Fix} (T^n) \Bigr| \frac {z^n}{n}\right),$$

where $\operatorname{Fix}(T^{n})$ is the set of [fixed points](wikipedia://en/449738) of the $n$-fold shift.[^o18] It has a product formula

$$\zeta(z) = \prod_\gamma \left(1-z^{|\gamma|} \right)^{-1} \$$

where $\gamma$ runs over the closed orbits.[^o18] For subshifts of finite type, the zeta function is a [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210) of $z$:[^o19]

$$\zeta(z) = (\det(I-zA))^{-1} \ .$$

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

A **sofic shift** is an image of a subshift of finite type where different edges of the transition graph may be mapped to the same symbol. For example, if one only watches the output from a hidden Markov chain, then the output appears to be a sofic system.[^o22] It may be regarded as the set of labellings of paths through an [automaton](wikipedia://en/103356): a subshift of finite type then corresponds to an automaton which is [deterministic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic_automaton).[^o23] Such systems correspond to [regular languages](wikipedia://en/25723).

Context-free systems are defined analogously, and are generated by [phrase structure grammars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrase_structure_grammar).

A **renewal system** is defined to be the set of all infinite concatenations of some fixed finite collection of finite words.

Subshifts of finite type are identical to free (non-interacting) one-dimensional [Potts models](wikipedia://en/1162226) ($n$-letter generalizations of [Ising models](wikipedia://en/292744)), with certain nearest-neighbor configurations excluded. Interacting Ising models are defined as subshifts together with a continuous function of the configuration space (continuous with respect to the product topology, defined below); the [partition function](wikipedia://en/16846849) and [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319) are explicitly expressible in terms of this function.

Subshifts may be quantized in a certain way, leading to the idea of the [quantum finite automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_finite_automata).

:::
