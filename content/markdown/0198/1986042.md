# Andrew Odlyzko

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Andrew Odlyzko"}

**Andrew Michael Odlyzko** (**Andrzej Odłyżko**) (born 23 July 1949) is a [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_people)-[American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) mathematician and a former head of the [University of Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Minnesota)'s Digital Technology Center and of the [Minnesota Supercomputing Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnesota_Supercomputing_Institute). He began his career in 1975 at [Bell Telephone Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Telephone_Laboratories), where he stayed for 26 years before joining the [University of Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Minnesota) in 2001.

:::

## Work in mathematics {#Work_in_mathematics}

Odlyzko received his B.S. and M.S. in mathematics from the [California Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology) and his Ph.D. from the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology) in 1975.

In the field of mathematics he has published extensively on [analytic number theory](wikipedia://en/251513), [computational number theory](wikipedia://en/511466), [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) and [computational complexity](wikipedia://en/2230), [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170), [probability](wikipedia://en/22934), and [error-correcting codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error-correcting_code). In the early 1970s, he was a co-author (with D. Kahaner and [Gian-Carlo Rota](wikipedia://en/101891)) of one of the founding papers of the modern [umbral calculus](wikipedia://en/214124). In 1985 he and [Herman te Riele](wikipedia://en/1587570) disproved the [Mertens conjecture](wikipedia://en/379847). In mathematics, he is probably known best for his work on the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809), which led to the invention of improved algorithms, including the [Odlyzko–Schönhage algorithm](wikipedia://en/14099326), and large-scale computations, which stimulated extensive research on connections between the zeta function and [random matrix](wikipedia://en/1648765) theory.

In 1990, working in [analytic combinatorics](wikipedia://en/75060288), Odlyzko and [Philippe Flajolet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippe_Flajolet) developed the theory of singularity analysis.[^o6]

As a direct collaborator of [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284), he has [Erdős number](wikipedia://en/9742) 1.[^o7][^o8]

## Work on electronic communication {#Work_on_electronic_communication}

More recently, he has worked on [communication networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_network), [electronic publishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_publishing), [economics of security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics_of_security) and [electronic commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_commerce).

In 1998, he and Kerry Coffman were the first to show that one of the great inspirations for the Internet bubble, the myth of "Internet traffic doubling every 100 days," was false.[^o10]

In the paper "Content is Not King", published in *[First Monday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Monday_%28journal%29)* in January 2001,[^o11] he argues that

1. the [entertainment industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entertainment_industry) is a small industry compared with other industries, notably the [telecommunications industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications_industry);
1. people are more interested in [communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication) than [entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entertainment);
1. and therefore that entertainment "content" is not the [killer app](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killer_app) for the [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet).

In 2012, he became a fellow of the International Association for Cryptologic Research[^o12] and in 2013 of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822).

### Network value {#Network_value}

In the 2006 paper "Metcalfe's Law is Wrong",[^o14] Andrew Odlyzko and coauthors argue that the incremental value of adding one person to a network of *n* people is approximately the *n*th [harmonic number](wikipedia://en/214729), so the total value of the network is approximately *n* \* log(*n)*. Since this curves upward (unlike [Sarnoff's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarnoff's_law)), it implies that Metcalfe's conclusion – that there is a [critical mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_mass_%28sociodynamics%29) in networks, leading to a [network effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_effect) – is qualitatively correct. But since this [linearithmic function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearithmic_function) does not grow as rapidly as [Metcalfe's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metcalfe's_law), it implies that many of the quantitative expectations based on Metcalfe's law were excessively optimistic.

For example, by Metcalfe, if a hypothetical network of 100,000 members has a value of \$1M, doubling its membership would increase its value fourfold (200,000<sup>2</sup>/100,000<sup>2</sup>). However Odlyzko predicts its value would only slightly more than double: 200,000\*log(200,000)/(100,000\*log(100,000).[^o14] Empirical tests, in part stimulated by this criticism, strongly support Metcalfe's law.[^o15]

## Financial history {#Financial_history}

In recent years, Odlyzko has published multiple papers on the financial history of bubbles, particularly the [South Sea Bubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Sea_Company) and the [English Railway Mania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_Mania) of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, respectively.[^o17][^o18][^o19][^o20]
