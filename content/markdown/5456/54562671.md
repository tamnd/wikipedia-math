# Dottie number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dottie number"}

*Image: Cosine\_fixed\_point.svg.* The Dottie number is the unique real [fixed point](wikipedia://en/449738) of the [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine) function.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Dottie number** or the **cosine constant** is a [constant](wikipedia://en/48779551) that is the unique [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) root of the equation

$$\cos x = x ,$$

where the argument of $\cos$ is in [radians](wikipedia://en/26003).

:::

The decimal expansion of the Dottie number is given by: 5 $D = 0.739085133215160641655312087673...$ .

Since $\cos(x) - x$ is [decreasing](wikipedia://en/48260) and its [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) is non-zero at $\cos(x) - x = 0$, it only crosses zero at one point. This implies that the equation $\cos(x) = x$ has only one real solution. It is the single [real-valued](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-valued) [fixed point](wikipedia://en/449738) of the [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine) function and is a [nontrivial](wikipedia://en/193053) example of a universal attracting fixed point. It is also a [transcendental number](wikipedia://en/30325) because of the [Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/348976).[^o2] The generalised case $\cos z = z$ for a complex variable $z$ has infinitely many roots, but unlike the Dottie number, they are not attracting fixed points.

*Image: quadrisection.svg.* The solution of quadrisection of circle into four parts of the same area with chords coming from the same point can be expressed via Dottie number.

## History {#History}

The constant appeared in publications as early as 1860s.[^o4] [Norair Arakelian](wikipedia://en/48552839) used lowercase [ayb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayb_%28Armenian_letter%29) (ա) from the [Armenian alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_alphabet) to denote the constant.[^o4]

The constant name was coined by Samuel R. Kaplan in 2007. It originates from a professor of French named Dottie who observed the number by repeatedly pressing the cosine button on her calculator.[^o9]

The Dottie number, for which an exact series expansion can be obtained using the [Faà di Bruno formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faà_di_Bruno_formula), has interesting connections with the Kepler and Bertrand's circle problems.[^o5]

## Identities {#Identities}

The Dottie number appears in the closed form expression of some [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532):[^o7][^o8]

$$\int _0^\infty\ln \left(\frac{4\left(x+\sinh x\right)^2+\pi^2}{4(x-\sinh x)^2+\pi ^2}\right){\rm d}x = \pi^2 - 2\pi D$$

$$\int_{0 }^\infty \frac{3\pi^2+4(x-\sinh x)^2}{(3\pi^2+4(x-\sinh x)^2)^2 + 16\pi^2(x-\sinh x)^2}\,{\rm d}x = \frac1{8+8\sqrt{1-D^2}}$$

Using the [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) of the inverse of $f(x) = \cos(x) - x$ at $\frac{\pi}{2}$ (or equivalently, the [Lagrange inversion theorem](wikipedia://en/94158)), the Dottie number can be expressed as the [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series):

$$D = \frac{\pi}{2}+\sum_{n\,\mathrm{odd}} a_n \pi^{n}$$

where each $a_n$ is a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024) defined for odd $n$ as[^o9][^o10][^o11]

$$\begin{aligned} a_n&=\frac{1}{n!2^n}\lim_{m\to\frac\pi2} \frac{\partial^{n-1}}{\partial m^{n-1}}{\left(\frac{\cos m}{m-\pi/2}-1\right)^{-n}} \\&=-\frac{1}{4},-\frac{1}{768},-\frac{1}{61440},-\frac{43}{165150720},\ldots \end{aligned}$$

The Dottie number can also be expressed as:

$$D=\sqrt{1-\left(1-2I^{-1}_\frac12\left(\frac 12,\frac 32\right)\right)^2},$$

where $I^{-1}$ is the inverse of the regularized [beta function](wikipedia://en/245660). This value can be obtained using [Kepler's equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler's_equation), along with other equivalent closed forms. $I^{-1}_\frac12\left(\tfrac 12,\tfrac 32\right) \approx 0.16319$ is the median of a [beta distribution](wikipedia://en/207074) with parameters 1/2 and 3/2. [^o5]

In [Microsoft Excel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Excel), Open Office and LibreOffice Calc spreadsheets, the Dottie number can be expressed in closed form as SQRT(1-(1-2\*BETA.INV(1/2,1/2,3/2))^2). In the [Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematica) [computer algebra](wikipedia://en/23659805) system, the Dottie number is Sqrt\[1 - (1-2 InverseBetaRegularized\[1/2, 1/2, 3/2\])^2\].

Another closed form representation:

$$D=- \tanh\left(2\operatorname{arctanh}\left(\frac1{\sqrt3} \operatorname{InvT} \left(\frac14,3\right)\right)\right)=-\frac{2\sqrt3 {\operatorname{InvT}\left(\frac14,3\right)}}{\operatorname{InvT}^2\left(\frac14,3\right)+3},$$

where $\operatorname{InvT}$ is the inverse survival function of [Student's t-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375). In Microsoft Excel, Open Office and LibreOffice Calc, due to the specifics of the realization of `TINV` function, this can be expressed as formulas 2 \*SQRT(3)\* TINV(1/2, 3)/(TINV(1/2, 3)^2+3) and TANH(2\*ATANH(1/SQRT(3) \* TINV(1/2,3))).
