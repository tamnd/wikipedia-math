# Blaney–Criddle equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Blaney–Criddle equation"}

The **Blaney–Criddle equation** (named after H. F. Blaney and W. D. Criddle) is a method for estimating reference crop [evapotranspiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evapotranspiration).

:::

## Usage {#Usage}

The Blaney–Criddle equation is a relatively simplistic method for calculating [evapotranspiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evapotranspiration). When sufficient meteorological data is available the [Penman–Monteith equation](wikipedia://en/13793909) is usually preferred. However, the Blaney–Criddle equation is ideal when only air-temperature datasets are available for a site.

Given the coarse accuracy of the Blaney–Criddle equation, it is recommended that it be used to calculate [evapotranspiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evapotranspiration) for periods of one month or greater.[^o3]

The equation calculates [evapotranspiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evapotranspiration) for a 'reference crop', which is taken as actively growing green grass of 8–15 cm height.[^o4]

## Equation {#Equation}

*ET<sub>o</sub>* = *p* ·(0.457·*T<sub>mean</sub>* + 8.128)

Where:

*ET<sub>o</sub>* is the reference evapotranspiration \[mm day<sup>−1</sup>\] (monthly)

*T<sub>mean</sub>* is the mean daily temperature \[°C\] given as *T<sub>mean</sub> = (T<sub>max</sub> + T<sub>min</sub> )/ 2*

*p* is the mean daily percentage of annual daytime hours.[^o6]

## Accuracy and bias {#Accuracy_and_bias}

Given the limited data input to the equation, the calculated [evapotranspiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evapotranspiration) should be regarded as only broadly accurate. Rather than a precise measure of [evapotranspiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evapotranspiration), the output of the equation is better thought of as providing an order of magnitude.[^o4]

The inaccuracy of the equation is exacerbated by extreme variants of weather. In particular [evapotranspiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evapotranspiration) is known to be exaggerated by up to 40% in calm, humid, clouded areas and depreciated by 60% in windy, dry, sunny areas.[^o4]
