# Strain energy density function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Strain energy density function"}

A **strain energy density function** or **stored energy density function** is a [scalar-valued](wikipedia://en/3588331) [function](wikipedia://en/185427) that relates the [strain energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_energy) density of a material to the [deformation gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_gradient).

:::

$$W = \hat{W}(\boldsymbol{C}) = \hat{W}(\boldsymbol{F}^T\cdot\boldsymbol{F}) =\bar{W}(\boldsymbol{F}) = \bar{W}(\boldsymbol{B}^{1/2}\cdot\boldsymbol{R})=\tilde{W}(\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{R})$$

Equivalently,

$$W = \hat{W}(\boldsymbol{C}) = \hat{W}(\boldsymbol{R}^T\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}) =\tilde{W}(\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{R})$$

where $\boldsymbol{F}$ is the (two-point) deformation gradient [tensor](wikipedia://en/29965), $\boldsymbol{C}$ is the [right Cauchy–Green deformation tensor](wikipedia://en/2210759#The_right_Cauchy–Green_deformation_tensor), $\boldsymbol{B}$ is the [left Cauchy–Green deformation tensor](wikipedia://en/2210759#The_left_Cauchy–Green_or_Finger_deformation_tensor),[^o2][^o3] and $\boldsymbol{R}$ is the rotation tensor from the [polar decomposition](wikipedia://en/1369241) of $\boldsymbol{F}$.

For an anisotropic material, the strain energy density function $\hat{W}(\boldsymbol{C})$ depends implicitly on reference vectors or tensors (such as the initial orientation of fibers in a composite) that characterize internal material texture. The spatial representation, $\tilde{W}(\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{R})$ must further depend explicitly on the polar rotation tensor $\boldsymbol{R}$ to provide sufficient information to convect the reference texture vectors or tensors into the spatial configuration.

For an [isotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotropic) material, consideration of the principle of material frame indifference leads to the conclusion that the strain energy density function depends only on the invariants of $\boldsymbol{C}$ (or, equivalently, the invariants of $\boldsymbol{B}$ since both have the same eigenvalues). In other words, the strain energy density function can be expressed uniquely in terms of the [principal stretches](wikipedia://en/2210759#Spectral_decompositions) or in terms of the [invariants](wikipedia://en/1126638) of the [left Cauchy–Green deformation tensor](wikipedia://en/2210759#The_left_Cauchy–Green_or_Finger_deformation_tensor) or [right Cauchy–Green deformation tensor](wikipedia://en/2210759#The_right_Cauchy–Green_deformation_tensor) and we have:

For isotropic materials,

$$W = \hat{W}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3) = \tilde{W}(I_1,I_2,I_3) = \bar{W}(\bar{I}_1,\bar{I}_2,J) = U(I_1^c, I_2^c, I_3^c)$$

with

$$\begin{aligned} \bar{I}_1 & = J^{-2/3}~I_1 ~;~~ I_1 = \lambda_1^2 + \lambda_2 ^2+ \lambda_3 ^2 ~;~~ J = \det(\boldsymbol{F}) \\ \bar{I}_2 & = J^{-4/3}~I_2 ~;~~ I_2 = \lambda_1^2 \lambda_2^2 + \lambda_2^2 \lambda_3^2 + \lambda_3^2 \lambda_1^2 \end{aligned}$$

For linear isotropic materials undergoing small strains, the strain energy density function specializes to

$W = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\sigma_{ij}\epsilon_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}(\sigma_x\epsilon_x+\sigma_y\epsilon_y+\sigma_z\epsilon_z+2\sigma_{xy}\epsilon_{xy}+2\sigma_{yz}\epsilon_{yz}+2\sigma_{xz}\epsilon_{xz})$[^o4]

A strain energy density function is used to define a [hyperelastic material](wikipedia://en/4472938) by postulating that the [stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_%28physics%29) in the material can be obtained by taking the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of $W$ with respect to the [strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_%28physics%29). For an isotropic hyperelastic material, the function relates the energy stored in an [elastic material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_%28physics%29), and thus the stress–strain relationship, only to the three [strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_%28materials_science%29) (elongation) components, thus disregarding the deformation history, heat dissipation, [stress relaxation](wikipedia://en/5202789) etc.

For [isothermal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isothermal) elastic processes, the strain energy density function relates to the specific [Helmholtz free energy](wikipedia://en/255447) function $\psi$,[^o5]

$$W = \rho_0 \psi \;.$$

For [isentropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isentropic) elastic processes, the strain energy density function relates to the [internal energy](wikipedia://en/340757) function $u$,

$$W = \rho_0 u \;.$$

## Examples {#Examples}

Some examples of hyperelastic [constitutive equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutive_equations) are:[^o8]

- [Saint Venant–Kirchhoff](wikipedia://en/4472938#Saint_Venant–Kirchhoff_model)
- [Neo-Hookean](wikipedia://en/2383470)
- [Generalized Rivlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_%28hyperelastic_model%29)
- [Mooney–Rivlin](wikipedia://en/2389236)
- [Ogden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogden_%28hyperelastic_model%29)
- [Yeoh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeoh_%28hyperelastic_model%29)
- [Arruda–Boyce model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arruda–Boyce_model)
- [Gent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gent_%28hyperelastic_model%29)
