# John R. Stallings

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="John R. Stallings"}

**John Robert Stallings Jr.** (July 22, 1935 – November 24, 2008) was a [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) known for his seminal contributions to [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164) and [3-manifold topology](wikipedia://en/621774). Stallings was a Professor Emeritus in the Department of Mathematics at the [University of California at Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California_at_Berkeley)[^o3] where he had been a faculty member since 1967.[^o3] He published over 50 papers, predominantly in the areas of [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164) and the topology of [3-manifolds](wikipedia://en/1018257). Stallings' most important contributions include a proof, in a 1960 paper, of the [Poincaré Conjecture in dimensions greater than six](wikipedia://en/8536216) and a proof, in a 1971 paper, of the [Stallings theorem about ends of groups](wikipedia://en/19196631).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

John Stallings was born on July 22, 1935, in [Morrilton, Arkansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morrilton,_Arkansas).[^o3]

Stallings received his B.Sc. from [University of Arkansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Arkansas) in 1956 (where he was one of the first two graduates in the university's Honors program)[^o5] and he received a Ph.D. in Mathematics from [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) in 1959 under the direction of [Ralph Fox](wikipedia://en/1343918).[^o3]

After completing his PhD, Stallings held a number of postdoctoral and faculty positions, including being an NSF postdoctoral fellow at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford) as well as an instructorship and a faculty appointment at Princeton. Stallings joined the University of California at Berkeley as a faculty member in 1967 where he remained until his retirement in 1994.[^o3] Even after his retirement, Stallings continued supervising UC Berkeley graduate students until 2005.[^o11] Stallings was an [Alfred P. Sloan Research fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloan_Research_Fellowship) from 1962 to 1965 and a Miller Institute fellow from 1972 to 1973.[^o3] Over the course of his career, Stallings had 22 doctoral students including [Marc Culler](wikipedia://en/29258614), [Stephen M. Gersten](wikipedia://en/61897288), and [J. Hyam Rubinstein](wikipedia://en/4983922) and has over 140 doctoral descendants. He published over 50 papers, predominantly in the areas of [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164) and the topology of [3-manifolds](wikipedia://en/1018257).

Stallings delivered an invited address as the [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) in [Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nice) in 1970[^o6] and a James K. Whittemore Lecture at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University) in 1969.[^o7]

Stallings received the [Frank Nelson Cole Prize in Algebra](wikipedia://en/1372177) from the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822) in 1970.[^o8]

The conference "Geometric and Topological Aspects of Group Theory", held at the [Mathematical Sciences Research Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Sciences_Research_Institute) in Berkeley in May 2000, was dedicated to the 65th birthday of Stallings.[^o9] In 2002 a special issue of the journal *[Geometriae Dedicata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometriae_Dedicata)* was dedicated to Stallings on the occasion of his 65th birthday. Stallings died from [prostate cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prostate_cancer) on November 24, 2008.[^o11][^o12]

## Mathematical contributions {#Mathematical_contributions}

Most of Stallings' mathematical contributions are in the areas of [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164) and [low-dimensional topology](wikipedia://en/621774) (particularly the topology of [3-manifolds](wikipedia://en/1018257)) and on the interplay between these two areas.

An early significant result of Stallings is his 1960 proof[^o14] of the [Poincaré conjecture in dimensions greater than six](wikipedia://en/8536216). (Stallings' proof was obtained independently from and shortly after the different proof of [Stephen Smale](wikipedia://en/40294) who established the same result in dimensions bigger than four[^o15]).

Using "engulfing" methods similar to those in his proof of the Poincaré conjecture for *n* > 6, Stallings proved that ordinary Euclidean *n*-dimensional space has a unique piecewise linear, hence also smooth, structure, if *n* is not equal to 4. This took on added significance when, as a consequence of work of [Michael Freedman](wikipedia://en/658970) and [Simon Donaldson](wikipedia://en/976305) in 1982, it was shown that 4-space has [exotic smooth structures](wikipedia://en/2180637), in fact uncountably many inequivalent ones.

In a 1963 paper[^o16] Stallings constructed an example of a [finitely presented group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_presented_group) with infinitely generated 3-dimensional integral [homology group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_homology) and, moreover, not of the type $F_3$, that is, not admitting a [classifying space](wikipedia://en/923556) with a finite 3-skeleton. This example came to be called the *Stallings group* and is a key example in the study of homological finiteness properties of groups. Robert Bieri later showed[^o17] that the Stallings group is exactly the kernel of the homomorphism from the direct product of three copies of the [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) $F_2$ to the additive group $\mathbb{Z}$ of integers that sends to $1\in \mathbb{Z}$ the six elements coming from the choice of free bases for the three copies of $F_2$. Bieri also showed that the Stallings group fits into a sequence of examples of groups of type $F_n$ but not of type $F_{n+1}$. The Stallings group is a key object in the version of discrete [Morse theory](wikipedia://en/348560) for cubical complexes developed by [Mladen Bestvina](wikipedia://en/26130054) and Noel Brady[^o18] and in the study of subgroups of direct products of [limit groups](wikipedia://en/78938535).[^o19][^o20][^o21]

Stallings' most famous theorem in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) is an algebraic characterization of groups with more than one [end](wikipedia://en/6862105) (that is, with more than one "connected component at infinity"), which is now known as [Stallings' theorem about ends of groups](wikipedia://en/19196631). Stallings proved that a [finitely generated group](wikipedia://en/963042) *G* has more than one end if and only if this group admits a nontrivial splitting as an [amalgamated free product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_product_with_amalgamation) or as an [HNN extension](wikipedia://en/2823319) over a finite group (that is, in terms of [Bass–Serre theory](wikipedia://en/18673708), if and only if the group admits a nontrivial action on a [tree](wikipedia://en/48560) with finite edge stabilizers). More precisely, the theorem states that a [finitely generated group](wikipedia://en/963042) *G* has more than one end if and only if either *G* admits a splitting as an amalgamated free product $G=A\ast_C B$, where the group *C* is finite and $C\ne A$, $C\ne B$, or *G* admits a splitting as an HNN extension $G=\langle H, t | t^{-1}Kt=L\rangle$ where $K, L \le H$ are finite [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) of *H*.

Stallings proved this result in a series of works, first dealing with the torsion-free case (that is, a group with no nontrivial elements of finite [order](wikipedia://en/493527))[^o22] and then with the general case.[^o7][^o23] Stalling's theorem yielded a positive solution to the long-standing open problem about characterizing finitely generated groups of cohomological dimension one as exactly the [free groups](wikipedia://en/59735).[^o24] Stallings' theorem about ends of groups is considered one of the first results in [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164) proper since it connects a geometric property of a group (having more than one end) with its algebraic structure (admitting a splitting over a finite subgroup). Stallings' theorem spawned many subsequent alternative proofs by other mathematicians (e.g.[^o25][^o26]) as well as many applications (e.g.[^o27]). The theorem also motivated several generalizations and relative versions of Stallings' result to other contexts, such as the study of the notion of relative ends of a group with respect to a subgroup,[^o28][^o29][^o30] including a connection to [CAT(0) cubical complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CAT%280%29_space).[^o31] A comprehensive survey discussing, in particular, numerous applications and generalizations of Stallings' theorem, is given in a 2003 paper of [C. T. C. Wall](wikipedia://en/2613478).[^o32]

Another influential paper of Stallings is his 1983 article "Topology of finite graphs".[^o33] Traditionally, the algebraic structure of [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) of [free groups](wikipedia://en/59735) has been studied in [combinatorial group theory](wikipedia://en/979834) using combinatorial methods, such as the [Schreier rewriting method](wikipedia://en/897724) and [Nielsen transformations](wikipedia://en/18400182).[^o34] Stallings' paper put forward a topological approach based on the methods of [covering space theory](wikipedia://en/250323) that also used a simple [graph-theoretic](wikipedia://en/12401) framework. The paper introduced the notion of what is now commonly referred to as *Stallings subgroup graph* for describing subgroups of free groups, and also introduced a foldings technique (used for approximating and algorithmically obtaining the subgroup graphs) and the notion of what is now known as a *Stallings folding*. Most classical results regarding subgroups of free groups acquired simple and straightforward proofs in this set-up and Stallings' method has become the standard tool in the theory for studying the subgroup structure of free groups, including both the algebraic and algorithmic questions (see [^o35]). In particular, Stallings subgroup graphs and Stallings foldings have been the used as a key tools in many attempts to approach the [Hanna Neumann conjecture](wikipedia://en/19296207).[^o36][^o37][^o38][^o39]

Stallings subgroup graphs can also be viewed as [finite-state automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite-state_automata)[^o35] and they have also found applications in [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799) theory and in [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science).[^o40][^o41][^o42][^o43]

Stallings' foldings method has been generalized and applied to other contexts, particularly in [Bass–Serre theory](wikipedia://en/18673708) for approximating group actions on [trees](wikipedia://en/48560) and studying the subgroup structure of the [fundamental groups of graphs of groups](wikipedia://en/18673708). The first paper in this direction was written by Stallings himself,[^o44] with several subsequent generalizations of Stallings' folding methods in the [Bass–Serre theory](wikipedia://en/18673708) context by other mathematicians.[^o45][^o46][^o47][^o48]

Stallings' 1991 paper "Non-positively curved triangles of groups"[^o49] introduced and studied the notion of a [triangle of groups](wikipedia://en/293504#Triangles_of_groups). This notion was the starting point for the theory of [complexes of groups](wikipedia://en/293504#Complexes_of_groups) (a higher-dimensional analog of [Bass–Serre theory](wikipedia://en/18673708)), developed by [André Haefliger](wikipedia://en/20280849)[^o50] and others.[^o51][^o52] Stallings' work pointed out the importance of imposing some sort of "non-positive curvature" conditions on the complexes of groups in order for the theory to work well; such restrictions are not necessary in the one-dimensional case of Bass–Serre theory.

Among Stallings' contributions to [3-manifold topology](wikipedia://en/1018257), the most well-known is the *Stallings fibration theorem*.[^o53] The theorem states that if *M* is a compact irreducible [3-manifold](wikipedia://en/1018257) whose [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) contains a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918), such that this subgroup is [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/963042) and such that the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) by this subgroup is [infinite cyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_cyclic_group), then *M* [fibers](wikipedia://en/316826) over a circle. This is an important structural result in the theory of [Haken manifolds](wikipedia://en/236567) that engendered many alternative proofs, generalizations and applications (e.g.[^o54][^o55][^o56][^o57] ), including a higher-dimensional analog.[^o58]

A 1965 paper of Stallings *"How not to prove the Poincaré conjecture"*[^o59] gave a [group-theoretic](wikipedia://en/41890) reformulation of the famous [Poincaré conjecture](wikipedia://en/23798). The paper began with a humorous admission: "I have committed the sin of falsely proving Poincaré's Conjecture. But that was in another country; and besides, until now, no one has known about it."[^o3][^o59] Despite its ironic title, Stallings' paper informed much of the subsequent research on exploring the algebraic aspects of the [Poincaré conjecture](wikipedia://en/23798) (see, for example,[^o60][^o61][^o62][^o63]).

Stallings was also interested in languages, and wrote one of the very few mathematical research papers in the constructed language [Interlingua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingua).[^o64][^o65]
