# Leslie Leland Locke

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Leslie Leland Locke"}

**Leslie Leland Locke** (1875–1943)[^o3] was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902), [historian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historian), and educator, best known for his work towards deciphering ancient Andean knot records called *[quipus](wikipedia://en/261479).*

:::

Locke's most prominent work, *The Ancient Quipu or Peruvian Knot Record* (1923), demonstrated how the [Inca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Empire) tied knots on *quipu* cords using a [base-10](wikipedia://en/8214) [positional number system](wikipedia://en/437052).[^o4] In addition to his work on *quipus*, Locke is also recognized for his research on the history of mathematics[^o5] and mathematical instruments, particularly his research and collection of [calculating machines](wikipedia://en/432897).[^o6]

## Education {#Education}

Locke earned both his B.A. (1896) and M.A. (1900) from [Grove City College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grove_City_College). He went on to study mathematics at [Pennsylvania State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania_State_University); [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University); and eventually at [Teachers College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teachers_College,_Columbia_University) at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University), where he studied under Professor [David Eugene Smith](wikipedia://en/3711980).[^o8][^o9] As a graduate student studying the history of mathematics, Locke assisted Smith and [Yoshio Mikami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshio_Mikami) with their 1914 book, *The History of Japanese Mathematics*, by taking the many photographs used throughout the book.[^o10]

## Career {#Career}

Early in his career, Locke held several short-term teaching positions, including at West Sunbury Academy in [West Sunbury, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Sunbury,_Pennsylvania); a high school in [Fredonia, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fredonia,_Pennsylvania); and at [Michigan State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_State_University) in [East Lansing, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Lansing,_Michigan). In 1902, he moved to [Brooklyn, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn), where he began teaching at [Adelphi College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adelphi_College), a position he held for six years.[^o27] In 1906, he transitioned to the Maxwell Training School for Teachers, also in Brooklyn.[^o8]

In 1933, Locke joined [Brooklyn Technical High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn_Technical_High_School) as a mechanical drawing instructor, a role he held until his retirement in 1942. Concurrently, from 1917 to 1938, he served as a professor of mathematics, teaching evening sessions at Brooklyn College.[^o8][^o27]

Aside from teaching, Locke authored several scientific publications (see section on [selected publications](wikipedia://en/78782339#Selected_publications)), often writing under the abbreviated name "L. Leland Locke." He was a "Foundation Member" of the [History of Science Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Science_Society) (HSS) and served as the society's Secretary at one point.[^o3] Additionally, he was a member of several other academic organizations, including the [American Mathematics Society](wikipedia://en/198822) (AMS), the [National Council of Teachers of Mathematics](wikipedia://en/1561691) (NCTM), and the [Mathematics Association of America](wikipedia://en/198772) (MAA).[^o9]

### *Quipu* research {#Quipu_research}

*Image: The cover of L. Leland Locke's 1923 book titled The Ancient Quipu.png.* Cover of *The Ancient Quipu* (1923) by Leslie Leland Locke.

Under the guidance of Professor [David Eugene Smith](wikipedia://en/3711980), Locke began studying Andean *[quipus](wikipedia://en/261479)*, drawing on Smith's extensive collection of rare books on South America and his access to specimens housed at the [American Museum of Natural History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Museum_of_Natural_History).[^o9] Notably, an accession card for *quipu* B/8715 in the museum's collection indicates that the specimen was lent to Smith in November 1911, likely for Locke's research.[^o13]

Locke's first major work on the Andean *[quipu](wikipedia://en/261479)* was published in 1912 as an article in *[American Anthropologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Anthropologist)*, titled "The Ancient Quipu, a Peruvian Knot Record". In this seminal work, Locke outlined a basic working model for how Inca *[quipus](wikipedia://en/261479)* recorded numbers using three types of knots: the [overhand knot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhand_knot), the [figure-eight knot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figure-eight_knot), and eight types of long knots. He showed that a knot's distance from the *quipu's* main cord was used to denote its value in a [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214) system. He argued that *quipus* were not used directly for counting or calculating—e.g., an [abacus](wikipedia://en/655)—but rather solely to record information. Finally, he strongly believed *quipu* knots were used purely for numerical purposes.[^o14]

Locke later expanded his initial 1912 article into a full-length book, publishing *The Ancient Quipu or Peruvian Knot Record* through the [American Museum of Natural History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Museum_of_Natural_History) in 1923. Early reviews hailed the book as "the first serious attempt to elucidate the quipu mystery"[^o15] and noted that "the conclusions reached by Professor Locke are very important."[^o16] In the preface to one of his own works on *quipus*, [Erland Nordenskiöld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erland_Nordenskiöld)—a leading expert in South American archaeology and anthropology of the early 20th century—praised Locke as "the founder of the modern study of the quipu".[^o17]

### Calculating machines {#Calculating_machines}

After his work on *quipus*, Locke became interested in the history of the [calculating machine](wikipedia://en/432897). He soon became an avid collector of these devices and amassed a collection of well over 100 items, at least one of which was thought to have been the first of its kind.[^o8] Several of the more rare pieces in Locke's collection included:[^o9][^o19]

- The first direct multiplication machine (designed by [Ramon Verea](wikipedia://en/41062485) in 1878)
- A lever-set barrel calculating machine (patented by George B. Grant in 1887)
- A cylindrical slide rule (invented by George Fuller in 1878)

In 1939, Locke donated his large collection to the [Smithsonian Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smithsonian_Institution).[^o27] According to the Smithsonian, Locke had initially intended his collection to go to the [Museums of the Peaceful Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_the_Peaceful_Arts) in New York, but the museum closed before he could do so.[^o6]

### Personal Library {#Personal_Library}

*Image: Bookplate of Leslie Leland Locke (cropped).png.* [Bookplate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ex_Libris_%28bookplate%29) from Locke's personal library.

Over the course of his life, Locke amassed an extensive personal library reflecting his interests in mathematics and other topics. Many of the books in Locke's collection featured a personalized [bookplate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ex_Libris_%28bookplate%29) with a circular design surrounded by the text "EX LIBRIS" at the top and "LESLIE LELAND LOCKE" at the bottom, meaning "From the library of Leslie Leland Locke." The central image depicts a figure, possibly seated or standing, engaged in scholarly or artistic activity, with a detailed rectangular object below that may represent a table, altar, or chest.[^o21]

Locke's collection included notable mathematical works such as:

- *Numerorum Mysteria* (1591) by [Pietro Bongo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietro_Bongo).[^o22]
- *Barlow’s Tables of Squares, Cubes, Square Roots, Cube Roots, Reciprocals of All Integer Numbers up to 10,000* (1840) by [Peter Barlow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Barlow_%28mathematician%29).
- *Mathematical Tables, Automatic Arithmetic: A New System for Multiplication and Division Without Mental Labour and Without the Use of Logarithms* (1878) by John Sawyer.

"Locke collected mathematical books and calculating machines. He wrote extensively on such instruments. Moreover, he graded and collected the paper-and-pencil standardized tests then relatively new in mathematics education. Many of these materials survive at the Smithsonian, offering a window into both the history of mathematics and the history of pedagogy at that time and place."[^o23]

Along with his books, Locke collected various copies of tests and examinations, including (but not limited to) a test for a fifth-grade algebra class, college entrance examinations, and New York Training School certificate examinations. He also preserved lecture notes from his time as both a student and a mathematics teacher, providing insight into early 20th-century educational practices.[^o19]

Locke's library also revealed his other interests beyond mathematics. Among these were *Tricks With Cards* (1893) by [Professor Hoffmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor_Hoffmann), a book on [sleight of hand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleight_of_hand) card tricks,[^o24] and *On the Economy of Machinery and Manufactures* (1832) by [Charles Babbage](wikipedia://en/5698), a treatise on industrial efficiency and the application of scientific principles to manufacturing.[^o25]

Following his death, Locke left his collection of books on mathematics to his alma mater, [Grove City College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grove_City_College).[^o8] However, one source also notes that Locke donated "valuable early American text-books" to the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan) during his lifetime.[^o27] The collection given to [Grove City College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grove_City_College) was later donated by the college to the [Smithsonian Libraries and Archives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smithsonian_Libraries_and_Archives).[^o19]

## Death {#Death}

Locke died at his home at 950 St. John's Place in [Brooklyn, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn), on August 28, 1943.[^o3][^o27][^o8] Some sources describe his death as sudden,[^o3] while others report that he died "after a long illness".[^o8]
