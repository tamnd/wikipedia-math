# Rule of replacement

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rule of replacement"}

In logic, a **rule of replacement**[^o2][^o3][^o4] is a [transformation rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_rule) that may be applied to only a particular segment of an [expression](wikipedia://en/404582). A [logical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_system) may be constructed so that it uses either [axioms](wikipedia://en/928), [rules of inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rules_of_inference), or both as transformation rules for [logical expressions](wikipedia://en/404582) in the system. Whereas a rule of inference is always applied to a whole logical expression, a rule of replacement may be applied to only a particular segment. Within the context of a [logical proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_proof), [logically equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logically_equivalent) expressions may replace each other. Rules of replacement are used in [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154) to manipulate [propositions](wikipedia://en/81094).

:::

Common rules of replacement include [de Morgan's laws](wikipedia://en/64669), [commutation](wikipedia://en/294390), association, distribution, [double negation](wikipedia://en/379833), [transposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposition_%28logic%29), [material implication](wikipedia://en/244516), [logical equivalence](wikipedia://en/169324), [exportation](wikipedia://en/34769960), and [tautology](wikipedia://en/34896576).

## Table: Rules of Replacement {#Table:_Rules_of_Replacement}

The rules above can be summed up in the following table.[^o6] The "[Tautology](wikipedia://en/4495335)" column shows how to interpret the notation of a given rule.

::: {#o7 .table cue=table}

| Rules of inference | Tautology | Name |
|---|---|---|
| $\begin{aligned} (p \vee q) \vee r\\ \therefore \overline{p \vee (q \vee r)} \\ \end{aligned}$ | $((p \vee q) \vee r) \rightarrow (p \vee (q \vee r))$ | [Associative](wikipedia://en/1335#Propositional_logic) |
| $\begin{aligned} p \wedge q\\ \therefore \overline{q \wedge p} \\ \end{aligned}$ | $(p \wedge q) \rightarrow (q \wedge p)$ | [Commutative](wikipedia://en/294390#Propositional_logic) |
| $\begin{aligned} (p \wedge q) \rightarrow r\\ \therefore \overline{p \rightarrow (q \rightarrow r)} \\ \end{aligned}$ | $((p \wedge q) \rightarrow r) \rightarrow (p \rightarrow (q \rightarrow r))$ | [Exportation](wikipedia://en/34769960) |
| $\begin{aligned} p \rightarrow q\\ \therefore \overline{\neg q \rightarrow \neg p} \\ \end{aligned}$ | $(p \rightarrow q) \rightarrow (\neg q \rightarrow \neg p)$ | [Transposition or contraposition law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposition_%28logic%29) |
| $\begin{aligned} p \rightarrow q\\ \therefore \overline{\neg p \vee q} \\ \end{aligned}$ | $(p \rightarrow q) \rightarrow (\neg p \vee q)$ | [Material implication](wikipedia://en/244516) |
| $\begin{aligned} (p \vee q) \wedge r\\ \therefore \overline{(p \wedge r) \vee (q \wedge r)} \\ \end{aligned}$ | $((p \vee q) \wedge r) \rightarrow ((p \wedge r) \vee (q \wedge r))$ | [Distributive](wikipedia://en/103118#Propositional_logic) |
| $\begin{aligned} p\\ q\\ \therefore \overline{p \wedge q} \\ \end{aligned}$ | $((p) \wedge (q)) \rightarrow (p \wedge q)$ | [Conjunction](wikipedia://en/18152) |
| $\begin{aligned} p\\ \therefore \overline{\neg \neg p} \\ \end{aligned}$ | $p \rightarrow (\neg \neg p)$ | [Double negation introduction](wikipedia://en/379833) |
| $\begin{aligned} {\neg \neg p}\\ \therefore \overline p\\ \end{aligned}$ | $(\neg \neg p) \rightarrow p$ | [Double negation elimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_negation_elimination) |

:::
