# Rotor (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rotor"}

A **rotor** is an object in the [geometric algebra](wikipedia://en/12939) (also called [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305)) of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) that represents a [rotation](wikipedia://en/410009) about the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432).[^o2] More precisely, for each rotation there exist two rotors that represent it. The term originated with [William Kingdon Clifford](wikipedia://en/250820),[^o3] in showing that the [quaternion](wikipedia://en/51440) algebra is just a special case of [Hermann Grassmann](wikipedia://en/99242)'s "theory of extension" (Ausdehnungslehre).[^o4] Hestenes[^o5] defined a rotor to be any element $R$ of a geometric algebra that can be written as the product of an even number of unit vectors and satisfies $R\tilde R = 1$, where $\tilde R$ is the "reverse" of $R$—that is, the product of the same vectors, but in reverse order.

:::

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

In mathematics, a rotor in the geometric algebra of a vector space *V* is the same thing as an element of the [spin group](wikipedia://en/411231) Spin(*V*). We define this group below.

Let *V* be a vector space equipped with a positive definite quadratic form *q*, and let Cl(*V*) be the geometric algebra associated to *V*. The algebra Cl(*V*) is the quotient of the [tensor algebra](wikipedia://en/363628) of *V* by the relations $v\cdot v=q(v)$ for all $v\in V$. (The tensor product in Cl(*V*) is what is called the geometric product in geometric algebra and in this article is denoted by $\cdot$.) The **Z**-grading on the tensor algebra of *V* descends to a **Z**/2**Z**-grading on Cl(*V*), which we denote by

$$\operatorname{Cl}(V) = \operatorname{Cl}^\text{even}(V) \oplus \operatorname{Cl}^\text{odd}(V).$$

Here, Cl<sup>even</sup>(*V*) is generated by even-degree [blades](wikipedia://en/21241368) and Cl<sup>odd</sup>(*V*) is generated by odd-degree blades.

There is a unique antiautomorphism of Cl(*V*) which restricts to the identity on *V*: this is called the transpose, and the transpose of any multivector *a* is denoted by $\tilde a$. On a [blade](wikipedia://en/21241368) (i.e., a simple tensor), it simply reverses the order of the factors. The spin group Spin(*V*) is defined to be the subgroup of Cl<sup>even</sup>(*V*) consisting of multivectors *R* such that $R\tilde R = 1.$ That is, it consists of multivectors that can be written as a product of an even number of unit vectors.

:::

## Action as rotation on the vector space {#Action_as_rotation_on_the_vector_space}

[Reflections along a vector](wikipedia://en/12939#Reflection) in geometric algebra may be represented as (minus) sandwiching a multivector *M* between a [non-null](wikipedia://en/1453977) vector *v* perpendicular to the [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) of reflection and that vector's [inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) *v*<sup>−1</sup>:

$$-vMv^{-1}$$

and are of even grade. Under a rotation generated by the rotor *R*, a general multivector *M* will transform double-sidedly as

$$RMR^{-1}.$$

This action gives a surjective homomorphism $\operatorname{Spin}(V)\to \operatorname{SO}(V)$ presenting Spin(*V*) as a double cover of SO(*V*). (See [Spin group](wikipedia://en/411231#Double_covering) for more details.)

### Restricted alternative formulation {#Restricted_alternative_formulation}

For a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), it may be convenient to consider an alternative formulation, and some authors define the operation of reflection as (minus) the sandwiching of a *unit* (i.e. normalized) multivector:

$$-vMv, \quad v^2=1 ,$$

forming rotors that are automatically normalised:

$$R\tilde R = \tilde RR = 1 .$$

The derived rotor action is then expressed as a sandwich product with the reverse:

$$RM\tilde R$$

For a reflection for which the associated vector squares to a negative scalar, as may be the case with a [pseudo-Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/10962546), such a vector can only be normalized up to the sign of its square, and additional bookkeeping of the sign of the application the rotor becomes necessary. The formulation in terms of the sandwich product with the inverse as above suffers no such shortcoming.

### Rotations of multivectors and spinors {#Rotations_of_multivectors_and_spinors}

However, though as multivectors also transform double-sidedly, rotors can be combined and form a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), and so multiple rotors compose single-sidedly. The alternative formulation above is not self-normalizing and motivates the definition of [spinor](wikipedia://en/29276) in geometric algebra as an object that transforms single-sidedly – i.e., spinors may be regarded as non-normalised rotors in which the reverse rather than the inverse is used in the sandwich product.

## Homogeneous representation algebras {#Homogeneous_representation_algebras}

In homogeneous representation algebras such as [conformal geometric algebra](wikipedia://en/34629138), a rotor in the representation space corresponds to a [rotation](wikipedia://en/39789) about an arbitrary [point](wikipedia://en/593693), a [translation](wikipedia://en/204682) or possibly another transformation in the base space.
