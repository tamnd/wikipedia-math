# Orthocenter

*Image: Triangle.Orthocenter.svg.* The three altitudes of a triangle intersect at the orthocenter, which for an [acute triangle](wikipedia://en/44076423) is inside the triangle.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Orthocenter"}

The **orthocenter** of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654), usually denoted by $H$, is the [point](wikipedia://en/593693) where the three (possibly extended) [altitudes](wikipedia://en/161241) intersect.[^o2][^o3] The orthocenter lies inside the triangle [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the triangle is [acute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_triangle). For a [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420), the orthocenter coincides with the [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) at the right angle.[^o3] For an [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285), all [triangle centers](wikipedia://en/12401488) (including the orthocenter) coincide at its [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926).

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Formulation"}

## Formulation {#Formulation}

Let $A, B, C$ denote the vertices and also the angles of the triangle, and let $a = \left|\overline{BC}\right|,$ $b = \left|\overline{CA}\right|,$ $c = \left|\overline{AB}\right|$ be the side lengths. The orthocenter has [trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790)[^o6]

$$\begin{aligned} & \sec A:\sec B:\sec C \\ &= \cos A-\sin B \sin C:\cos B-\sin C \sin A:\cos C-\sin A\sin B, \end{aligned}$$

and [barycentric coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycentric_coordinates_%28mathematics%29)

$$\begin{aligned} & (a^2+b^2-c^2)(a^2-b^2+c^2) : (a^2+b^2-c^2)(-a^2+b^2+c^2) : (a^2-b^2+c^2)(-a^2+b^2+c^2) \\ &= \tan A:\tan B:\tan C. \end{aligned}$$

Since barycentric coordinates are all positive for a point in a triangle's interior but at least one is negative for a point in the exterior, and two of the barycentric coordinates are zero for a vertex point, the barycentric coordinates given for the orthocenter show that the orthocenter is in an [acute triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_triangle)'s interior, on the right-angled vertex of a [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420), and exterior to an [obtuse triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obtuse_triangle).

In the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), let the points $A, B, C$ represent the [numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $z_{A}, z_{B}, z_{C}$ and assume that the [circumcenter](wikipedia://en/161262) of triangle $\triangle ABC$ is located at the origin of the plane. Then, the complex number

$$z_H=z_A+z_B+z_C$$

is represented by the point $H$, namely the orthocenter of triangle $\triangle ABC$.[^o7] From this, the following characterizations of the orthocenter $H$ by means of [free vectors](wikipedia://en/32533) can be established straightforwardly:

$$\vec{OH}=\sum\limits_{\scriptstyle\rm cyclic}\vec{OA},\qquad2\cdot\vec{HO}=\sum\limits_{\scriptstyle\rm cyclic}\vec{HA}.$$

The first of the previous vector identities is also known as the *problem of Sylvester*, proposed by [James Joseph Sylvester](wikipedia://en/234861).[^o8]

:::

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Let $D, E, F$ denote the feet of the altitudes from $A, B, C$ respectively. Then:

- The product of the lengths of the segments that the orthocenter divides an altitude into is the same for all three altitudes:[^o11][^o12] $$\overline{AH} \cdot \overline{HD} = \overline{BH} \cdot \overline{HE} = \overline{CH} \cdot \overline{HF}.$$ The circle centered at $H$ having radius the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of this constant is the triangle's [polar circle](wikipedia://en/45079434).[^o13]
- The sum of the ratios on the three altitudes of the distance of the orthocenter from the base to the length of the altitude is 1:[^o14] (This property and the next one are applications of a [more general property](wikipedia://en/6135790#Ratio_properties) of any interior point and the three [cevians](wikipedia://en/6135790) through it.) $$\frac{\overline{HD}}{\overline{AD}} + \frac{\overline{HE}}{\overline{BE}} + \frac{\overline{HF}}{\overline{CF}} = 1.$$
- The sum of the ratios on the three altitudes of the distance of the orthocenter from the vertex to the length of the altitude is 2:[^o14] $$\frac{\overline{AH}}{\overline{AD}} + \frac{\overline{BH}}{\overline{BE}} + \frac{\overline{CH}}{\overline{CF}} = 2.$$
- The [isogonal conjugate](wikipedia://en/2083100) of the orthocenter is the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) of the triangle.[^o15]
- The [isotomic conjugate](wikipedia://en/9492657) of the orthocenter is the [symmedian point](wikipedia://en/320998) of the [anticomplementary triangle](wikipedia://en/3339743#Anticomplementary_triangle).[^o16]
- Four points in the plane, such that one of them is the orthocenter of the triangle formed by the other three, is called an [orthocentric system](wikipedia://en/161263) or orthocentric quadrangle.

## Relation with circles and conics {#Relation_with_circles_and_conics}

Denote the [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) of the triangle by $R$. Then[^o19][^o20]

$$a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + \overline{AH}^2 + \overline{BH}^2 + \overline{CH}^2 = 12R^2.$$

In addition, denoting $r$ as the radius of the triangle's [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle), $r_{a}, r_{b}, r_{c}$ as the radii of its [excircles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excircle), and $R$ again as the radius of its circumcircle, the following relations hold regarding the distances of the orthocenter from the vertices:[^o21]

$$\begin{aligned} & r_a + r_b + r_c + r = \overline{AH} + \overline{BH} + \overline{CH} + 2R, \\ & r_a^2 + r_b^2 + r_c^2 + r^2 = \overline{AH}^2 + \overline{BH}^2 + \overline{CH}^2 + (2R)^2. \end{aligned}$$

If any altitude, for example, $\overline{AD}$, is extended to intersect the circumcircle at $P$, so that $\overline{AD}$ is a chord of the circumcircle, then the foot $D$ bisects segment $\overline{HP}$:[^o12]

$$\overline{HD} = \overline{DP}.$$

The [directrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directrix_%28conic_section%29) of all [parabolas](wikipedia://en/23231) that are externally tangent to one side of a triangle and tangent to the extensions of the other sides pass through the orthocenter.[^o22]

A [circumconic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumconic) passing through the orthocenter of a triangle is a [rectangular hyperbola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_hyperbola).[^o23]

## Relation to other centers, the nine-point circle {#Relation_to_other_centers,_the_nine-point_circle}

The orthocenter $H$, the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) $G$, the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) $O$, and the center $N$ of the [nine-point circle](wikipedia://en/161243) all lie on a single line, known as the [Euler line](wikipedia://en/203622).[^o25] The center of the nine-point circle lies at the [midpoint](wikipedia://en/437979) of the Euler line, between the orthocenter and the circumcenter, and the distance between the centroid and the circumcenter is half of that between the centroid and the orthocenter:[^o26]

$$\begin{aligned} & \overline{OH} = 2\overline{NH}, \\ & 2\overline{OG} = \overline{GH}. \end{aligned}$$

The orthocenter is closer to the [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) $I$ than it is to the centroid, and the orthocenter is farther than the incenter is from the centroid:

$$\begin{aligned} \overline{HI} &< \overline{HG}, \\ \overline{HG} &> \overline{IG}. \end{aligned}$$

In terms of the sides $a$, $b$, $c$, [inradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inradius) $r$ and [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) $R$,[^o27][^o28]

$$\begin{aligned} \overline{OH}^2 &= R^2 -8R^2 \cos A \cos B \cos C \\ &= 9R^2-(a^2+b^2+c^2), \\ \overline{HI}^2 &= 2r^2 -4R^2 \cos A \cos B \cos C. \end{aligned}$$

## Orthic triangle {#Orthic_triangle}

*Image: Altitudes and orthic triangle SVG.svg.* Triangle $\triangle abc$ (respectively, $\triangle DEF$ in the text) is the orthic triangle of triangle $\triangle ABC$

If the triangle $\triangle ABC$ is [oblique](wikipedia://en/44076423) (does not contain a right-angle), the [pedal triangle](wikipedia://en/152983) of the orthocenter of the original triangle is called the **orthic triangle** or **altitude triangle**. That is, the feet of the altitudes of an oblique triangle form the orthic triangle, $\triangle DEF$. Also, the incenter (the center of the inscribed circle) of the orthic triangle $\triangle DEF$ is the orthocenter of the original triangle $\triangle ABC$.[^o31]

[Trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790) for the vertices of the orthic triangle are given by

$$\begin{array}{rccccc} D =& 0 &:& \sec B &:& \sec C \\ E =& \sec A &:& 0 &:& \sec C \\ F =& \sec A &:& \sec B &:& 0 \end{array}$$

The [extended sides](wikipedia://en/43958459) of the orthic triangle meet the opposite extended sides of its reference triangle at three [collinear points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear_points).[^o32][^o33][^o31]

In any [acute triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_triangle), the inscribed triangle with the smallest perimeter is the orthic triangle.[^o34] This is the solution to [Fagnano's problem](wikipedia://en/10783043), posed in 1775.[^o35] The sides of the orthic triangle are parallel to the tangents to the circumcircle at the original triangle's vertices.[^o36]

The orthic triangle of an acute triangle gives a triangular light route.[^o37]

The tangent lines of the nine-point circle at the midpoints of the sides of $\triangle ABC$ are parallel to the sides of the orthic triangle, forming a triangle similar to the orthic triangle.[^o38]

The orthic triangle is closely related to the [tangential triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangential_triangle), constructed as follows: let $L_{A}$ be the line tangent to the circumcircle of triangle $\triangle ABC$ at vertex $A$, and define $L_{B}, L_{C}$ analogously. Let $A'' = L_B \cap L_C,$ $B'' = L_C \cap L_A,$ $C'' = L_C \cap L_A.$ The tangential triangle is $\triangle A''B''C''$, whose sides are the tangents to triangle $\triangle ABC$'s circumcircle at its vertices; it is [homothetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothetic_transformation) to the orthic triangle. The circumcenter of the tangential triangle, and the [center of similitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_similitude) of the orthic and tangential triangles, are on the [Euler line](wikipedia://en/203622).[^o28]

Trilinear coordinates for the vertices of the tangential triangle are given by

$$\begin{array}{rrcrcr} A'' =& -a &:& b &:& c \\ B'' =& a &:& -b &:& c \\ C'' =& a &:& b &:& -c \end{array}$$

The reference triangle and its orthic triangle are [orthologic triangles](wikipedia://en/69531104).

For more information on the orthic triangle, see [here](wikipedia://en/161263#The_common_orthic_triangle,_its_incenter_and_excenters).

:::

## History {#History}

The theorem that the three altitudes of a triangle concur (at the orthocenter) is not directly stated in surviving [Greek mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) texts, but is used in the *[Book of Lemmas](wikipedia://en/17941791)* (proposition 5), attributed to [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) (3rd century BC), citing the "commentary to the treatise about right-angled triangles", a work which does not survive. It was also mentioned by [Pappus](wikipedia://en/509300) (*Mathematical Collection*, VII, 62; 340).[^o40] The theorem was stated and proved explicitly by [al-Nasawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alī_ibn_Ahmad_al-Nasawī) in his (11th century) commentary on the *Book of Lemmas*, and attributed to [al-Quhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Sahl_al-Quhi) (10th century).[^o41]

This proof in Arabic was translated as part of the (early 17th century) Latin editions of the *Book of Lemmas*, but was not widely known in Europe, and the theorem was therefore proven several more times in the 17th–19th century. [Samuel Marolois](wikipedia://en/70280421) proved it in his *Geometrie* (1619), and [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) proved it in an unfinished treatise *Geometry of Curved Lines* ( 1680).[^o40] Later [William Chapple](wikipedia://en/62371614) proved it in 1749.[^o42]

A particularly elegant proof is due to [François-Joseph Servois](wikipedia://en/42809386) (1804) and independently [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) (1810): Draw a line parallel to each side of the triangle through the opposite point, and form a new triangle from the intersections of these three lines. Then the original triangle is the [medial triangle](wikipedia://en/3339743) of the new triangle, and the altitudes of the original triangle are the [perpendicular bisectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular_bisector) of the new triangle, and therefore concur (at the circumcenter of the new triangle).[^o43]
