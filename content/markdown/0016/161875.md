# Dana Scott

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dana Scott"}

**Dana Stewart Scott** (born October 11, 1932) is a retired American logician. Along with [Michael O. Rabin](wikipedia://en/298404) he received the 1976 ACM [Turing Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_Award) for their joint work on [automata theory](wikipedia://en/103356). He has made seminal contributions to automata theory, modal logic, set theory, and the theory of programming languages. He created [domain theory](wikipedia://en/325077), a branch of mathematics that provides a foundation for the theory of programming languages and computability.

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Dana Stewart Scott was born on October 11, 1932, in [Berkeley, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkeley,_California). He completed his undergraduate education at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley), receiving a Bachelor of Arts in 1954.[^o4] He then pursued graduate studies in mathematics at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University), where he held a Bell Telephone Fellowship from 1956 to 1957. Scott earned his Ph.D. in 1958 under the supervision of the mathematical logician [Alonzo Church](wikipedia://en/49731), with a dissertation titled *Convergent Sequences of Complete Boolean Algebras*.

[Solomon Feferman](wikipedia://en/355401) writes of this period [^o6]: *Scott began his studies in logic at Berkeley in the early 50s while still an undergraduate. His unusual abilities were soon recognized and he quickly moved on to graduate classes and seminars with [Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) and became part of the group that surrounded him, including me and [Richard Montague](wikipedia://en/610182); so it was at that time that we became friends. Scott was clearly in line to do a Ph. D. with Tarski, but they had a falling out for reasons explained in our biography of Tarski.[^o7] Upset by that, Scott left for Princeton where he finished with a Ph. D. under Alonzo Church. But it was not long before the relationship between them was mended to the point that Tarski could say to him, "I hope I can call you my student," and rightly so: not only did Scott's thesis deal with a problem that had been proposed by Tarski, but all of Scott's work is in the best Tarskian tradition of breadth, rigor, clarity of exposition and clarity of purpose.*

During the summer of 1957, while a graduate student, Scott worked as a research intern at [IBM Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Research) in Yorktown Heights, New York. There he began a collaboration with [Michael O. Rabin](wikipedia://en/298404), building upon work by [Stephen Cole Kleene](wikipedia://en/28805), [John Myhill](wikipedia://en/2611636), and [Anil Nerode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anil_Nerode). Their joint 1959 paper, "Finite Automata and Their Decision Problem", introduced the concept of [nondeterministic finite automata](wikipedia://en/653406), for which both authors later received the 1976 [Turing Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_Award).[^o4][^o8]

## Academic career {#Academic_career}

### Chicago and Berkeley (1958–1963) {#Chicago_and_Berkeley_(1958–1963)}

After completing his doctorate in 1958, Scott served as an instructor at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago) until 1960. He subsequently joined the faculty at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley), serving as an assistant professor and later associate professor from 1960 to 1963, during which time he was also appointed a [Miller Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_Institute) Research Fellow (1960–1961)[^o4] at Berkeley, and involved himself with classical issues in [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), especially [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) and Tarskian [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858). He proved that the [axiom of constructibility](wikipedia://en/696472) is incompatible with the existence of a [measurable cardinal](wikipedia://en/248102), a result considered *seminal* in the evolution of set theory.[^o11]

During this period he started supervising Ph.D. students, such as James Halpern (*Contributions to the Study of the Independence of the Axiom of Choice*) and Edgar Lopez-Escobar (*Infinitely Long Formulas with Countable Quantifier Degrees*).

### Modal and tense logic {#Modal_and_tense_logic}

Scott also began working on [modal logic](wikipedia://en/333365) in this period, beginning a collaboration with [John Lemmon](wikipedia://en/3924625), who moved to [Claremont, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claremont,_California), in 1963. Scott was especially interested in [Arthur Prior](wikipedia://en/308764)'s approach to [tense logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tense_logic) and the connection to the treatment of time in natural-language semantics, and began collaborating with [Richard Montague](wikipedia://en/610182) (Copeland 2004), whom he had known from his days as an undergraduate at Berkeley. Later, Scott and Montague independently discovered an important generalisation of [Kripke semantics](wikipedia://en/911960) for modal and tense logic, called [Scott-Montague semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott-Montague_semantics) (Scott 1970).

John Lemmon and Scott began work on a modal-logic textbook that was interrupted by Lemmon's death in 1966. Scott circulated the incomplete monograph amongst colleagues, introducing a number of important techniques in the semantics of model theory, most importantly presenting a refinement of the *canonical model* that became standard, and introducing the technique of constructing models through *filtrations*, both of which are core concepts in modern Kripke semantics (Blackburn, de Rijke, and Venema, 2001). Scott eventually published the work as *An Introduction to Modal Logic* (Lemmon & Scott, 1977).

## Stanford, Amsterdam and Princeton, 1963–1972 {#Stanford,_Amsterdam_and_Princeton,_1963–1972}

Following an initial observation of [Robert Solovay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Solovay), Scott formulated the concept of [Boolean-valued model](wikipedia://en/2732301), as Solovay and [Petr Vopěnka](wikipedia://en/3105466) did likewise at around the same time. In 1967, Scott published a paper, *A Proof of the Independence of the Continuum Hypothesis*, in which he used Boolean-valued models to provide an alternate analysis of the independence of the [continuum hypothesis](wikipedia://en/5705) to that provided by [Paul Cohen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Cohen_%28mathematician%29). This work led to the award of the [Leroy P. Steele Prize](wikipedia://en/1252550) in 1972.

## University of Oxford, 1972–1981 {#University_of_Oxford,_1972–1981}

Scott took up a post as Professor of Mathematical Logic on the Philosophy faculty of the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford) in 1972. He was member of [Merton College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merton_College,_Oxford) while at Oxford and is now an Honorary Fellow of the college.

### Semantics of programming languages {#Semantics_of_programming_languages}

This period saw Scott working with [Christopher Strachey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Strachey), and the two managed, despite administrative pressures, to do work on providing a mathematical foundation for the semantics of programming languages, the work for which Scott is best known. Together, their work constitutes the Scott–Strachey approach to [denotational semantics](wikipedia://en/55275), an important and seminal contribution to [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392). One of Scott's contributions is his formulation of [domain theory](wikipedia://en/325077), allowing programs involving recursive functions and looping-control constructs to be given denotational semantics. Additionally, he provided a foundation for the understanding of infinitary and continuous information through domain theory and his theory of [information systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott_information_system).

Scott's work of this period led to the bestowal of:

- The 1990 [Harold Pender Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Pender_Award) for his *application of concepts from logic and algebra to the development of mathematical semantics of programming languages*;
- The 1997 [Rolf Schock Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolf_Schock_Prize) in logic and philosophy from the [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Swedish_Academy_of_Sciences) for *his conceptually oriented logical works, especially the creation of domain theory, which has made it possible to extend Tarski's semantic paradigm to programming languages as well as to construct models of Curry's combinatory logic and Church's calculus of lambda conversion*; and
- The 2001 [Bolzano Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolzano_Prize) for Merit in the Mathematical Sciences by the [Czech Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_Academy_of_Sciences)
- The 2007 [EATCS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EATCS) Award for his contribution to theoretical computer science.

## Carnegie Mellon University, 1981–2003 {#Carnegie_Mellon_University,_1981–2003}

At [Carnegie Mellon University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Mellon_University), Scott proposed the theory of [equilogical spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilogical_spaces) as a successor theory to domain theory; among its many advantages, the category of equilogical spaces is a [cartesian closed category](wikipedia://en/237037), whereas the category of domains[^o17] is not. In 1994, he was inducted as a [Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow) of the [Association for Computing Machinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_for_Computing_Machinery). In 2012 he became a fellow of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822).[^o18]
