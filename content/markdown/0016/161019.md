# Negation

negation in linguistics

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Negation"}

In [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), **negation**, also called the **logical not** or **logical complement**, is an [operation](wikipedia://en/4140245) that takes a [proposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proposition_%28mathematics%29) $P$ to another proposition "not $P$", written $\neg P$, $\mathord{\sim} P$, $P^\prime$[^o3] or $\overline{P}$[^o4]. It is interpreted intuitively as being true when $P$ is false, and false when $P$ is true.[^o5][^o6] For example, if $P$ is "The dog runs", then "not $P$" is "The dog does not run". An operand of a negation is called a ***negand*** or ***negatum***.[^o7]

:::

Negation is a [unary](wikipedia://en/161006) [logical connective](wikipedia://en/18153). It may furthermore be applied not only to propositions, but also to [notions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notion_%28philosophy%29), [truth values](wikipedia://en/161711), or [semantic values](wikipedia://en/14511671) more generally. In [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868), negation is normally identified with the [truth function](wikipedia://en/604707) that takes *truth* to *falsity* (and vice versa). In [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262), according to the [Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation](wikipedia://en/1925846), the negation of a proposition $P$ is the proposition whose proofs are the refutations of $P$.

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

*Classical negation* is an [operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_operation) on one [logical value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_value), typically the value of a [proposition](wikipedia://en/81094), that produces a value of *true* when its operand is false, and a value of *false* when its operand is true. Thus if statement $P$ is true, then $\neg P$ (pronounced "not P") would then be false; and conversely, if $\neg P$ is true, then $P$ would be false.

The [truth table](wikipedia://en/25512250) of $\neg P$ is as follows:

{| class="wikitable" style="text-align:center; background-color: #ddffdd;"

\|- bgcolor="#ddeeff" | $P$ || $\neg P$ |- | True || False |- | False || True |}

Negation can be defined in terms of other logical operations. For example, $\neg P$ can be defined as $P \rightarrow \bot$ (where $\rightarrow$ is [logical consequence](wikipedia://en/37531624) and $\bot$ is [absolute falsehood](wikipedia://en/229823)). Conversely, one can define $\bot$ as $Q \land \neg Q$ for any proposition $Q$ (where $\land$ is [logical conjunction](wikipedia://en/18152)). The idea here is that any [contradiction](wikipedia://en/75807) is false, and while these ideas work in both classical and intuitionistic logic, they do not work in [paraconsistent logic](wikipedia://en/421085), where contradictions are not necessarily false. As a further example, negation can be defined in terms of NAND and can also be defined in terms of NOR.

Algebraically, classical negation corresponds to [complementation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complement_%28order_theory%29) in a [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959), and intuitionistic negation to pseudocomplementation in a [Heyting algebra](wikipedia://en/309343). These algebras provide a [semantics](wikipedia://en/2848519) for classical and intuitionistic logic.

## Notation {#Notation}

The negation of a proposition $p$ is notated in different ways, in various contexts of discussion and fields of application. The following table documents some of these variants:

::: {#o12 .table cue=table}

| Notation | Plain text | Vocalization |
|---|---|---|
| $\neg p$ | ¬p , 7p[^o13] | Not *p* |
| $\mathord{\sim} p$ | ~p | Not *p* |
| $-p$ | \-p | Not *p* |
| $Np$ |  | En *p* |
| $p'$ | p' | *p* prime,; *p* complement |
| $\overline{p}$ | ̅p | *p* bar,; Bar *p* |
| $!p$ | !p | Bang *p*; Not *p* |

:::

The notation $Np$ is [Polish notation](wikipedia://en/25056#Polish_notation_for_logic).

In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553#Basic_concepts_and_notation), $\setminus$ is also used to indicate 'not in the set of': $U \setminus A$ is the set of all members of $U$ that are not members of $A$.

Regardless how it is notated or [symbolized](wikipedia://en/2498388), the negation $\neg P$ can be read as "it is not the case that $P$", "not that $P$", or usually more simply as "not $P$".

### Precedence {#Precedence}

As a way of reducing the number of necessary parentheses, one may introduce [precedence rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precedence_rule): ¬ has higher precedence than ∧, ∧ higher than ∨, and ∨ higher than →. So for example, $P \vee Q \wedge{\neg R} \rightarrow S$ is short for $(P \vee (Q \wedge (\neg R))) \rightarrow S.$

Here is a table that shows a commonly used precedence of logical operators.[^o15]

::: {#o16 .table cue=table}

| Operator | Precedence |
|---|---|
| $\neg$ | 1 |
| $\land$ | 2 |
| $\lor$ | 3 |
| $\to$ | 4 |
| $\leftrightarrow$ | 5 |

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

### Double negation {#Double_negation}

Within a system of [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868), double negation, that is, the negation of the negation of a proposition $P$, is [logically equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logically_equivalent) to $P$. Expressed in symbolic terms, $\neg \neg P \equiv P$. In [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262), a proposition implies its double negation, but not conversely. This marks one important difference between classical and intuitionistic negation. Algebraically, classical negation is called an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) of period two.

However, in [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262), the weaker equivalence $\neg \neg \neg P \equiv \neg P$ does hold. This is because in intuitionistic logic, $\neg P$ is just a shorthand for $P \rightarrow \bot$, and we also have $P \rightarrow \neg \neg P$. Composing that last implication with triple negation $\neg \neg P \rightarrow \bot$ implies that $P \rightarrow \bot$ .

As a result, in the propositional case, a sentence is classically provable if its double negation is intuitionistically provable. This result is known as [Glivenko's theorem](wikipedia://en/14662815).

### Distributivity {#Distributivity}

[De Morgan's laws](wikipedia://en/64669) provide a way of [distributing](wikipedia://en/103118) negation over [disjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjunction) and [conjunction](wikipedia://en/18152):

$\neg(P \lor Q) \equiv (\neg P \land \neg Q)$, and

$$\neg(P \land Q) \equiv (\neg P \lor \neg Q).$$

### Linearity {#Linearity}

Let $\oplus$ denote the logical [xor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xor) operation. In [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/54476844), a linear function is one such that:

If there exists $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_n \in \{0,1\}$, $f(b_1, b_2, \dots, b_n) = a_0 \oplus (a_1 \land b_1) \oplus \dots \oplus (a_n \land b_n)$, for all $b_1, b_2, \dots, b_n \in \{0,1\}$.

Another way to express this is that each variable always makes a difference in the [truth-value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth-value) of the operation, or it never makes a difference. Negation is a linear logical operator.

### Self dual {#Self_dual}

In [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/54476844), a self dual function is a function such that:

$f(a_1, \dots, a_n) = \neg f(\neg a_1, \dots, \neg a_n)$ for all $a_1, \dots, a_n \in \{0,1\}$. Negation is a self dual logical operator.

### Negations of quantifiers {#Negations_of_quantifiers}

In [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983), there are two quantifiers, one is the universal quantifier $\forall$ (means "for all") and the other is the existential quantifier $\exists$ (means "there exists"). The negation of one quantifier is the other quantifier ($\neg \forall xP(x)\equiv\exists x\neg P(x)$ and $\neg \exists xP(x)\equiv\forall x\neg P(x)$). For example, with the predicate *P* as "*x* is mortal" and the domain of x as the collection of all humans, $\forall xP(x)$ means "a person x in all humans is mortal" or "all humans are mortal". The negation of it is $\neg \forall xP(x)\equiv\exists x\neg P(x)$, meaning "there exists a person *x* in all humans who is not mortal", or "there exists someone who lives forever".

## Rules of inference {#Rules_of_inference}

There are a number of equivalent ways to formulate rules for negation. One usual way to formulate classical negation in a [natural deduction](wikipedia://en/51072) setting is to take as primitive rules of inference *negation introduction* (from a derivation of $P$ to both $Q$ and $\neg Q$, infer $\neg P$; this rule also being called *[reductio ad absurdum](wikipedia://en/22456146)*), *negation elimination* (from $P$ and $\neg P$ infer $Q$; this rule also being called *ex falso quodlibet*), and *double negation elimination* (from $\neg \neg P$ infer $P$). One obtains the rules for intuitionistic negation the same way but by excluding double negation elimination.

Negation introduction states that if an absurdity can be drawn as conclusion from $P$ then $P$ must not be the case (i.e. $P$ is false (classically) or refutable (intuitionistically) or etc.). Negation elimination states that anything follows from an absurdity. Sometimes negation elimination is formulated using a primitive absurdity sign $\bot$. In this case the rule says that from $P$ and $\neg P$ follows an absurdity. Together with double negation elimination one may infer our originally formulated rule, namely that anything follows from an absurdity.

Typically the intuitionistic negation $\neg P$ of $P$ is defined as $P \rightarrow \bot$. Then negation introduction and elimination are just special cases of implication introduction ([conditional proof](wikipedia://en/6562)) and elimination (*[modus ponens](wikipedia://en/18900)*). In this case one must also add as a primitive rule *ex falso quodlibet*.

:::

## Programming language and ordinary language {#Programming_language_and_ordinary_language}

As in mathematics, negation is used in [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) to construct logical statements.

```cpp
if (!(r == t))
{
    /*...statements executed when r does NOT equal t...*/
}
```

The [exclamation mark](wikipedia://en/7712754) "`!`" signifies logical NOT in [B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B_%28programming_language%29), [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_Programming_Language), and languages with a C-inspired syntax such as [C++](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++), [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_%28programming_language%29), [JavaScript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JavaScript), [Perl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perl), and [PHP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PHP). "`NOT`" is the operator used in [ALGOL 60](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ALGOL_60), [BASIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BASIC), and languages with an ALGOL- or BASIC-inspired syntax such as [Pascal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_programming_language), [Ada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_programming_language), and [Eiffel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eiffel_%28programming_language%29). Some languages (C++, Perl, etc.) provide more than one operator for negation. A few languages like [PL/I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PL/I) and [Ratfor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratfor) use `¬` for negation. Most modern languages allow the above statement to be shortened from `if (!(r == t))` to `if (r != t)`, which allows sometimes, when the compiler/interpreter is not able to optimize it, faster programs.

In computer science there is also *[bitwise negation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitwise_negation)*. This takes the value given and switches all the [binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_numeral_system) 1s to 0s and 0s to 1s. This is often used to create [ones' complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signed_number_representations) (or "`~`" in C or C++) and [two's complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two's_complement) (just simplified to "`-`" or the [negative sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_sign), as this is equivalent to taking the [arithmetic negation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negation_%28arithmetic%29) of the number).

To get the absolute (positive equivalent) value of a given integer the following would work as the "`-`" changes it from negative to positive (it is negative because "`x < 0`" yields true)

```cpp
unsigned int abs(int x)
{
    if (x < 0)
        return -x;
    else
        return x;
}
```

To demonstrate logical negation:

```cpp
unsigned int abs(int x)
{
    if (!(x < 0))
        return x;
    else
        return -x;
}
```

Inverting the condition and reversing the outcomes produces code that is logically equivalent to the original code, i.e. will have identical results for any input (depending on the compiler used, the actual instructions performed by the computer may differ).

In C (and some other languages descended from C), double negation (`!!x`) is used as an [idiom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programming_idiom) to convert `x` to a canonical Boolean, ie. an integer with a value of either 0 or 1 and no other. Although any integer other than 0 is logically true in C and 1 is not special in this regard, it is sometimes important to ensure that a canonical value is used, for example for printing or if the number is subsequently used for arithmetic operations.[^o28]

### Usage in colloquial language {#Usage_in_colloquial_language}

The convention of using *!* to signify negation occasionally surfaces in [colloquial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colloquial) language, as computer-related [slang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slang) for *not*. For example, the phrase *!clue* is used as a synonym for "no-clue" or "clueless".[^o30][^o31]

Another example is the term *[!vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/!vote)*, which is used by [Wikipedia editors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia_editors) to refer to statements of opinion on Wikipedia's discussion pages. The term emphasizes that Wikipedia makes decisions through "consensus-building" rather than majority rule.[^o32]

## Kripke semantics {#Kripke_semantics}

In [Kripke semantics](wikipedia://en/911960) where the semantic values of formulae are sets of [possible worlds](wikipedia://en/780566), negation can be taken to mean [set-theoretic complementation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set-theoretic_complement) (see also [possible world semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Possible_world_semantics) for more).
