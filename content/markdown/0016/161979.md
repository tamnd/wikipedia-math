# Judgement

*Image: Roters Illustrators 19807 (thinking judge).svg.* Illustration of a judge evaluating evidence in court to make a **judgement**

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Judgement"}

**Judgement** (or **judgment**)[^o2] is the [evaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaluation) of given circumstances to [make a decision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision-making) or form an [opinion](wikipedia://en/1348330). It may also refer to the result of such an evaluation, or to the ability of someone to make good judgements.[^o3]

:::

In an informal context, a judgement may refer to an opinion expressed as [fact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fact). In logic, judgements assert the truth of [statements](wikipedia://en/81094). In the context of a legal [trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trial), a judgment is a final finding, statement or ruling, based on evidence, rules and precedents (see [Judgment (law)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judgment_%28law%29)). In the context of [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology), judgement informally refers to the quality of a person's [cognitive faculties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind) and adjudicational capabilities, typically called '[wisdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom)'. In formal psychology, judgement and decision making (JDM) is a cognitive process by which individuals reason, make decisions, and form opinions and beliefs.[^o4][^o5]

::: {#o7 .statement cue=named-heading name="Judgments in law"}

## Judgments in law {#Judgments_in_law}

Judgment (law)

### Etymology and origin {#Etymology_and_origin}

*Image: Scales of Justice and Wreath.svg.* The [scales of justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scales_of_justice_%28symbol%29), a common symbol of legal judgment held by [Lady Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Justice)

The term "judgment" derives from [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *iudicare* ("to judge"), entering [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) via the [Old French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_French) term *jugement* around the 13th century. It initially defined both legal trials and [eschatological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eschatology) concepts like [Judgment Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Judgment). In [English law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_law), judgments began as medieval [writs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writ) that evolved to court decisions, which was interspersed with gradual inventions like [summary judgment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summary_judgment), which originated in 19th-century [equity courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_equity) to resolve undisputed debt claims efficiently. These were later codified in systems like the [U.S. Federal Rules of Civil Procedure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Rules_of_Civil_Procedure) (1938). This mechanism allows the dismissal of meritless claims.[^o9][^o10][^o11]

:::

## Judgement in cognitive science and psychology {#Judgement_in_cognitive_science_and_psychology}

### Judgement in psychology {#Judgement_in_psychology}

In [cognitive psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_psychology) (and related fields like [experimental philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_philosophy), [social psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_psychology), [behavioral economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_economics), or [experimental economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_economics)), judgement is part of a set of cognitive processes by which individuals reason, make decisions, and form beliefs and opinions (collectively, judgement and decision making, abbreviated JDM). This involves evaluating information, weighing evidence, making choices, and coming to conclusions. Judgements are often influenced by [cognitive biases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_bias), [heuristics](wikipedia://en/27988760), prior experience, social context, abilities (e.g., [numeracy](wikipedia://en/397245), probabilistic thinking), and psychological traits (e.g., tendency toward [analytical reasoning](wikipedia://en/5163440)). In research, the [Society for Judgment and Decision Making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_for_Judgment_and_Decision_Making) is an international academic society dedicated to the topic; they publish the peer-reviewed journal *[Judgment and Decision Making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judgment_and_Decision_Making)*.

Research by [Daniel Kahneman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Kahneman) and [Amos Tversky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amos_Tversky) in the 1970s and 1980s identified [psychological heuristics](wikipedia://en/27988760), such as the [availability heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Availability_heuristic) and [anchoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchoring_effect), which often lead to predictable cognitive biases. Their theory formalized how people evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point, predicting [loss aversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loss_aversion) (valuing losses twice as much as equivalent gains). This has applications in [behavioral economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_economics) and the design of [policies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policy). Kahneman's later works, including *[Thinking, Fast and Slow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thinking,_Fast_and_Slow)* (2011), distinguish "System 1" (intuitive judgements) from "System 2" (deliberative judgements).[^o14][^o15]

### Judgement in neuroscience {#Judgement_in_neuroscience}

Recent advances in [cognitive neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_neuroscience) have mapped judgement processes to brain regions like the [prefrontal cortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefrontal_cortex), often examining how [intuitive decisions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuition_and_decision-making) are processed, as shown in [fMRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_magnetic_resonance_imaging) studies of [risk assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk_assessment). In [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence), [large language models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_model) (e.g., [GPT-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPT-4)) replicate human judgement biases such as [loss aversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loss_aversion) and the [gambler's fallacy](wikipedia://en/12970). This raises concerns for judicial prediction, though they improve accuracy in planned tasks.[^o17][^o18][^o19]

## Judgement in ethics {#Judgement_in_ethics}

Ethical judgement involves evaluating actions, intentions, or outcomes against moral norms, distinguishing it from prudential assessments. Philosophers debate whether such judgements are objective (grounded in reason) or subjective (relational), which has been the influence of theories such as [deontology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deontology) and [virtue ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtue_ethics).

### Moral and conventional judgements {#Moral_and_conventional_judgements}

A major distinction, traced to [Jean Piaget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Piaget) and refined by [Elliot Turiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliot_Turiel), separates [moral judgements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_judgement) (concerning harm, fairness, rights; e.g., "stealing is wrong regardless of rules") from conventional judgements (rules, context-dependent; e.g., "uniforms are required in school"). This idea, supported by cultural developmental psychology, posits morals as universal, while conventions are arbitrary. This conception aids explanations for why children follow moral rules more rigidly.[^o23]

In [Kantian ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kantian_ethics), moral judgements derive from the [categorical imperative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorical_imperative), creating [universalizable](wikipedia://en/1150749) axioms via practical reason, distinct from [hypothetical imperatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothetical_imperative) of skill or wisdom. Critics like [Hegel](wikipedia://en/12598) viewed such judgements as historically mediated, evolving through the general ethical views of the population.[^o24]

:::

## Judgement in philosophy {#Judgement_in_philosophy}

### Aristotle {#Aristotle}

#### Judging power or faculty {#Judging_power_or_faculty}

Aristotle observed that the ability to judge takes two forms: making assertions and thinking about definitions. He defined these powers in distinctive terms. Making an assertion as a result of judging can affirm or deny something; these are called positive and negative judgements, respectively, regardless of whether they are true or false. In a judgement, one affirms a given relationship between two things, or one denies a relationship between two things exists. The kinds of definitions that are judgements are those that are the intersection of two or more ideas rather than those indicated only by usual examples — that is, constitutive definitions.[^o28]

Later Aristotelians, like [Mortimer Adler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortimer_Adler), questioned whether "definitions of abstraction" that come from merging examples in one's mind are really analytically distinct from judgements.[^o29]

#### Distinction of parts {#Distinction_of_parts}

In informal use, words like "judgement" are often used imprecisely.[^o31] Aristotle observed that while propositions can be drawn from judgements and called "true" and "false", the objects that the terms try to represent are only "true" or "false"—with respect to the judging act or communicating that judgement—in the sense of "apt" or "inapt".[^o32]

### Kant {#Kant}

[Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant), in his *[Critique of Pure Reason](wikipedia://en/347543)* (1781) and *[Critique of the Power of Judgment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critique_of_the_Power_of_Judgment)* (1790), positioned judgement as the core of [human cognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognition), defining it as a conscious mental operation that is the base of the conceptualization of objects via [intuition](wikipedia://en/154170). Kant's [anti-psychologistic](wikipedia://en/2021691) ideas emphasize judgement unifying cognitive capabilities for objective validity. "Determining" judgements subsume particulars under universals while "reflective" judgements seek universals for given particulars.[^o34]

## Judgment in religion {#Judgment_in_religion}

### Abrahamic religions {#Abrahamic_religions}

*Image: Sistine Chapel - Last Judgment, Rom.jpg.* *[The Last Judgment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_Judgment_%28Michelangelo%29)* by [Michelangelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelangelo)*,* an artistic depiction of divine discernment in [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity)

#### Christianity {#Christianity}

In [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity), the [New Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament) addresses interpersonal judgement in the [Sermon on the Mount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sermon_on_the_Mount), where [Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus) states: "Do not judge, or you too will be judged" ([Matthew 7:1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_7:1), [NIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_International_Version)), cautioning against hypocritical condemnation.[^o38]

#### Islam {#Islam}

In [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam), judgment manifests as [Yawm al-Qiyamah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_al-Qiyamah) (Day of Judgment), where [Allah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allah) resurrects all souls for accountability based on deeds recorded in the Kitab (book of records), determining paradise or hell, showing mercy alongside justice ([Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran) 99:7–8).[^o40]

### Other religions {#Other_religions}

[Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism) views judgment as a function of [karma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma), the law of cause and effect where actions ([samskaras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samskara_%28rite_of_passage%29)) influence rebirth ([samsara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saṃsāra)) and ultimate liberation ([moksha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moksha)), with texts like the *[Bhagavad Gita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhagavad_Gita)* portraying it as self-operated moral consequence, not [divine intervention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_intervention).[^o40]

Similarly, [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism) merges judgment via [Right View](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_view) ([Pali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali): *[sammā diṭṭhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sammādiṭṭhi_Sutta)*) in the [Noble Eightfold Path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path), stressing the discernment of ethical actions as a means to break the cycle of [rebirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebirth_%28Buddhism%29) and attain [nirvana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nirvana), as taught in the *[Dhammapada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhammapada).[^o40]*
