# Barotropic vorticity equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Barotropic vorticity equation"}

The **barotropic vorticity equation** assumes the atmosphere is nearly [barotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barotropic), which means that the direction and speed of the [geostrophic wind](wikipedia://en/520674) are independent of height. In other words, there is no vertical [wind shear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_shear) of the geostrophic wind. It also implies that thickness contours (a proxy for temperature) are parallel to upper level height contours. In this type of atmosphere, high and low pressure areas are centers of warm and cold temperature anomalies. Warm-core highs (such as the [subtropical ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtropical_ridge) and the Bermuda-Azores high) and [cold-core lows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold-core_low) have strengthening winds with height, with the reverse true for cold-core highs (shallow Arctic highs) and warm-core lows (such as [tropical cyclones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_cyclone)).[^o2]

:::

A simplified form of the [vorticity equation](wikipedia://en/166365) for an inviscid, [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328)-free flow ([solenoidal](wikipedia://en/419444) velocity field), the **barotropic vorticity equation** can simply be stated as[^o3]

$$\frac{D \eta}{D t} = 0,$$

where $\tfrac{D}{Dt}$ is the [material derivative](wikipedia://en/668449) and

$$\eta = \zeta + f$$

is *absolute [vorticity](wikipedia://en/166010)*, with *ζ* being *relative vorticity*, defined as the vertical component of the [curl](wikipedia://en/6123) of the fluid velocity and *f* is the *[Coriolis parameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriolis_parameter)*

$$f = 2 \Omega \sin \varphi,$$

where Ω is the [angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency) of the planet's rotation (Ω = \0.7272 × 10<sup>\-4</sup> s<sup>−1</sup> for the earth) and *φ* is [latitude](wikipedia://en/17616).

In terms of *relative vorticity*, the equation can be rewritten as

$$\frac{D \zeta}{D t} = -v \beta,$$

where *β* = $\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ is the variation of the *Coriolis parameter* with distance *y* in the north–south direction and *v* is the component of velocity in this direction.

In 1950, Charney, Fjørtoft, and [von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) integrated this equation (with an added [diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion) term on the [right-hand side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-hand_side_and_right-hand_side_of_an_equation)) on a [computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer) for the first time, using an observed field of 500 [hPa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_%28unit%29) [geopotential height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geopotential_height) for the first timestep.[^o4] This was one of the first successful instances of [numerical weather prediction](wikipedia://en/1505381).
