# Ball (mathematics)

*Image: Blue-sphere (crop).png.* In [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), a **ball** is the volume bounded by a sphere

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ball"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **ball** is the [solid figure](wikipedia://en/507960) bounded by a *[sphere](wikipedia://en/27859)*; it is also called a **solid sphere**.[^o2] It may be a **closed ball** (including the [boundary points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_points) that constitute the sphere) or an **open ball** (excluding them).

:::

These concepts are defined not only in three-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) but also for lower and higher dimensions, and for [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018) in general. A *ball* in $n$ dimensions is called a **hyperball** or **$n$-ball** and is bounded by a *hypersphere* or [($n-1$)-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782). Thus, for example, a ball in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) is the same thing as a [disk](wikipedia://en/329542), the [planar region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planar_region) bounded by a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220). In [Euclidean 3-space](wikipedia://en/9697), a ball is taken to be the [region of space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Region_of_space) bounded by a [2-dimensional sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-sphere). In a [one-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/25584215), a ball is a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860).

In other contexts, such as in [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417) and informal use, *sphere* is sometimes used to mean *ball*. In the field of [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) the closed $n$-dimensional ball is often denoted as $B^n$ or $D^n$ while the open $n$-dimensional ball is $\operatorname{int} B^n$ or $\operatorname{int} D^n$.

## In Euclidean space {#In_Euclidean_space}

In Euclidean $n$-space, an (open) $n$-ball of radius $r$ and center $x$ is the set of all points of distance less than $r$ from $x$. A closed $n$-ball of radius $r$ is the set of all points of distance less than or equal to $r$ from $x$.

In Euclidean $n$-space, every ball is bounded by a [hypersphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere). The ball is a bounded [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) when $n = 1$, is a **[disk](wikipedia://en/329542)** bounded by a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) when $n = 2$, and is bounded by a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) when $n = 3$.

### Volume {#Volume}

The $n$-dimensional volume of a Euclidean ball of radius $r$ in $n$-dimensional Euclidean space is given by[^o5]

$$V_n(r) = \frac{\pi^\frac{n}{2}}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{n}{2} + 1\right)}} r^n,$$

where $\Gamma$ is [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902)'s [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316) (which can be thought of as an extension of the [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606) function to fractional arguments). Using explicit formulas for [particular values of the gamma function](wikipedia://en/4239318) at the integers and half integers gives formulas for the volume of a Euclidean ball that do not require an evaluation of the gamma function. These are:

$$\begin{aligned} V_{2k}(r) &= \frac{\pi^k}{k!} r^{2k}\,,\\[2pt] V_{2k+1}(r) &= \frac{2^{k+1}\pi^k}{\left(2k+1\right)!!} r^{2k+1} = \frac{2\left(k!\right) \left(4\pi\right)^k}{\left(2k+1\right)!}r^{2k+1}\,. \end{aligned}$$

In the formula for odd-dimensional volumes, the [double factorial](wikipedia://en/507208) $(2k + 1)!!$ is defined for odd integers $2k + 1$ as $(2k + 1)!! = 1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot \cdots \cdot (2k - 1) \cdot (2k + 1)$.

The surface area of an n-ball (an (n-1)-sphere) is:

$$A_n(r) =\frac{d V_n}{dr} = \frac{2\pi^\frac{n}{2}}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}} r^{n-1},$$

## In general metric spaces {#In_general_metric_spaces}

*Image: Centered\_octahedral\_number\_lattice.svg.* Balls of radii 1 (red) and 2 (blue) in [taxicab geometry](wikipedia://en/408354) are [regular octahedrons](wikipedia://en/22458)

Let $(M, d)$ be a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018), namely a set $M$ with a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) (distance function) $d$, and let $r$ be a positive real number. The open (metric) **ball of radius** $r$ centered at a point $p$ in $M$, usually denoted by $B_{r}(p)$ or $B(p; r)$, is defined the same way as a Euclidean ball, as the set of points in $M$ of distance less than $r$ away from $p$,

$$B_r(p) = \{ x \in M \mid d(x,p) < r \}.$$

The *closed* (metric) ball, sometimes denoted $B_{r}[p]$ or $B[p; r]$, is likewise defined as the set of points of distance less than or equal to $r$ away from $p$,

$$B_r[p] = \{ x \in M \mid d(x,p) \le r \}.$$

In particular, a ball (open or closed) always includes $p$ itself, since the definition requires $r > 0$. A **[unit ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_ball)** (open or closed) is a ball of radius 1.

A ball in a general metric space need not be round. For example, a ball in [real coordinate space](wikipedia://en/1482083) under the [Chebyshev distance](wikipedia://en/1503963) is a [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783), and a ball under the [taxicab distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxicab_distance) is a [cross-polytope](wikipedia://en/716401). A closed ball also need not be [compact](wikipedia://en/6042). For example, a closed ball in any infinite-dimensional [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538) is never compact. However, a ball in a normed vector space will always be [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) as a consequence of the triangle inequality.

A subset of a metric space is [bounded](wikipedia://en/48258) if it is contained in some ball. A set is [totally bounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_bounded) if, given any positive radius, it is covered by finitely many balls of that radius.

The open balls of a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) can serve as a [base](wikipedia://en/59220), giving this space a [topology](wikipedia://en/30450), the open sets of which are all possible [unions](wikipedia://en/47949) of open balls. This topology on a metric space is called the **topology induced by** the metric $d$.

Let $\overline{B_r(p)}$ denote the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) of the open ball $B_r(p)$ in this topology. While it is always the case that $B_r(p) \subseteq \overline{B_r(p)} \subseteq B_r[p],$ it is not always the case that $\overline{B_r(p)} = B_r[p].$ For example, in a metric space $X$ with the [discrete metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_metric), one has $\overline{B_1(p)} = \{p\}$ but $B_1[p] = X$ for any $p \in X.$

## In normed vector spaces {#In_normed_vector_spaces}

Any [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538) $V$ with norm $\|\cdot\|$ is also a metric space with the metric $d (x,y)= \|x - y\|.$ In such spaces, an arbitrary ball $B_r(y)$ of points $x$ around a point $y$ with a distance of less than $r$ may be viewed as a scaled (by $r$) and translated (by $y$) copy of a *unit ball* $B_1(0).$ Such "centered" balls with $y=0$ are denoted with $B(r).$

The Euclidean balls discussed earlier are an example of balls in a normed vector space.

### $p$-norm {#-norm}

In a [Cartesian space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_space) $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ with the [$p$-norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-norm) $L^{p}$, that is one chooses some $p \geq 1$ and defines

$$\left\| x \right\| _p = \left( |x_1|^p + |x_2|^p + \dots + |x_n|^p \right) ^{1/p},$$

Then an open ball around the origin with radius $r$ is given by the set

$$B(r) = \left\{ x \in \mathbb{R}^n \,:\left\| x \right\| _p = \left( |x_1|^p + |x_2|^p + \dots + |x_n|^p \right) ^{1/p} < r \right\}.$$

For $n = 2$, in a 2-dimensional plane $\mathbb{R}^2$, "balls" according to the $L^{1}$-norm (often called the *[taxicab](wikipedia://en/408354)* or *Manhattan* metric) are bounded by squares with their *diagonals* parallel to the coordinate axes; those according to the $L^{\infty}$-norm, also called the [Chebyshev](wikipedia://en/1503963) metric, have squares with their *sides* parallel to the coordinate axes as their boundaries. The $L^{2}$-norm, known as the Euclidean metric, generates the well known disks within circles, and for other values of $p$, the corresponding balls are areas bounded by [Lamé curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamé_curve) (hypoellipses or hyperellipses).

For $n = 3$, the $L^{1}$-balls are within octahedra with axes-aligned *body diagonals*, the $L^{\infty}$-balls are within cubes with axes-aligned *edges*, and the boundaries of balls for $L^{p}$ with $p > 2$ are [superellipsoids](wikipedia://en/19681409). $p = 2$ generates the inner of usual spheres.

Often can also consider the case of $p = \infty$ in which case we define

$$\lVert x \rVert_\infty = \max\{\left|x_1\right|, \dots, \left|x_n\right|\}$$

### General convex norm {#General_convex_norm}

More generally, given any [centrally symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_symmetry), [bounded](wikipedia://en/48258), [open](wikipedia://en/39358), and [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) subset $X$ of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$, one can define a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) on $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ where the balls are all translated and uniformly scaled copies of $X$. Note this theorem does not hold if "open" subset is replaced by "closed" subset, because the origin point qualifies but does not define a norm on $\mathbf{R}^{n}$.

## In topological spaces {#In_topological_spaces}

One may talk about balls in any [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$, not necessarily induced by a metric. An (open or closed) $n$-dimensional **topological ball** of $X$ is any subset of $X$ which is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to an (open or closed) Euclidean $n$-ball. Topological $n$-balls are important in [combinatorial topology](wikipedia://en/363400), as the building blocks of [cell complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_complex).

Any open topological $n$-ball is homeomorphic to the Cartesian space $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ and to the open [unit $n$-cube](wikipedia://en/39783) (hypercube) $(0, 1)^{n} \subseteq \mathbf{R}^{n}$. Any closed topological $n$-ball is homeomorphic to the closed $n$-cube $[0, 1]^{n}$.

An $n$-ball is homeomorphic to an $m$-ball if and only if $n = m$. The homeomorphisms between an open $n$-ball $B$ and $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ can be classified in two classes, that can be identified with the two possible [topological orientations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29) of $B$.

A topological $n$-ball need not be [smooth](wikipedia://en/2119219); if it is smooth, it need not be [diffeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphic) to a Euclidean $n$-ball.

## Regions {#Regions}

A number of special regions can be defined for a ball:

- *[cap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_cap)*, bounded by one plane
- *[sector](wikipedia://en/29974094)*, bounded by a conical boundary with apex at the center of the sphere
- *[segment](wikipedia://en/23023386)*, bounded by a pair of parallel planes
- *[shell](wikipedia://en/28734956)*, bounded by two concentric spheres of differing radii
- *[wedge](wikipedia://en/23117396)*, bounded by two planes passing through a sphere center and the surface of the sphere
