# Nine-point circle

*Image: Triangle.NinePointCircle.svg.* The nine points

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nine-point circle"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **nine-point circle** is a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) that can be constructed for any given [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654). It is so named because it passes through nine significant [concyclic points](wikipedia://en/504404) defined from the triangle. These nine [points](wikipedia://en/593693) are:

:::

- The [midpoint](wikipedia://en/437979) of each side of the triangle
- The [foot](wikipedia://en/76944) of each [altitude](wikipedia://en/161241)
- The **Euler points**: the midpoint of the [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) from each [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) of the triangle to the [orthocenter](wikipedia://en/161244) (where the three altitudes meet; these line segments lie on their respective altitudes).[^o2][^o3]

The nine-point circle is also known as **Feuerbach's circle** (after [Karl Wilhelm Feuerbach](wikipedia://en/161246)), **Euler's circle** (after [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902)), **Terquem's circle** (after [Olry Terquem](wikipedia://en/161565)), the **six-points circle**, the **twelve-points circle**, the **$n$-point circle**, the **medioscribed circle**, the **mid circle** or the **circum-midcircle**. Its center is the [nine-point center](wikipedia://en/4517570) of the triangle.[^o4][^o5]

## Nine Significant Points of Nine Point Circle {#Nine_Significant_Points_of_Nine_Point_Circle}

*Image: Nine-point circle.svg.*

The diagram above shows the nine significant points of the nine-point circle. Points $D, E, F$ are the midpoints of the three sides of the triangle. Points $G, H, I$ are the feet of the altitudes of the triangle. Points $J, K, L$ are the midpoints of the line segments between each altitude's [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) intersection (points $A, B, C$) and the triangle's orthocenter (point $S$).

For an [acute triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_triangle), six of the points (the midpoints and altitude feet) lie on the triangle itself; for an [obtuse triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obtuse_triangle) two of the altitudes have feet outside the triangle, but these feet still belong to the nine-point circle.

## Discovery {#Discovery}

Although he is credited for its discovery, [Karl Wilhelm Feuerbach](wikipedia://en/161246) did not entirely discover the nine-point circle, but rather the six-point circle, recognizing the significance of the midpoints of the three sides of the triangle and the feet of the altitudes of that triangle. (*See Fig. 1, points* $D, E, F, G, H, I$.) (At a slightly earlier date, [Charles Brianchon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Brianchon) and [Jean-Victor Poncelet](wikipedia://en/764818) had stated and proven the same theorem.) But soon after Feuerbach, mathematician [Olry Terquem](wikipedia://en/161565) himself proved the existence of the circle. He was the first to recognize the added significance of the three midpoints between the triangle's vertices and the orthocenter. (*See Fig. 1, points* $J, K, L$.) Thus, Terquem was the first to use the name nine-point circle.

## Tangent circles {#Tangent_circles}

*Image: Circ9pnt3.svg.* The nine-point circle is tangent to the incircle and excircles.

In 1822 Karl Feuerbach discovered that any triangle's nine-point circle is externally [tangent](wikipedia://en/19349153) to that triangle's three [excircles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excircle) and internally tangent to its [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle); this result is known as [Feuerbach's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feuerbach's_theorem). He proved that:

> ... the circle which passes through the feet of the altitudes of a triangle is tangent to all four circles which in turn are tangent to the three sides of the triangle...[^n1]

The [triangle center](wikipedia://en/12401488) at which the incircle and the nine-point circle touch is called the [Feuerbach point](wikipedia://en/547762).

:::

## Other properties of the nine-point circle {#Other_properties_of_the_nine-point_circle}

- The radius of a triangle's [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432) is twice the radius of that triangle's nine-point circle.[^o12] *Image: 9pcircle03.svg.* *Figure 3*
- A nine-point circle bisects a line segment going from the corresponding triangle's orthocenter to any point on its circumcircle. *Image: 9pcircle 04.png.* *Figure 4*
- The center $N$ of the nine-point circle bisects a segment from the orthocenter $H$ to the [circumcenter](wikipedia://en/161262) $O$ (making the orthocenter a center of [dilation](wikipedia://en/15685517) to both circles):[^o12] $$\overline{ON} = \overline{NH}.$$
- The nine-point center $N$ is one-fourth of the way along the [Euler line](wikipedia://en/203622) from the centroid $G$ to the orthocenter $H$:[^o12] $$\overline{HN} = 3\overline{NG}.$$
- Let $\omega$ be the nine-point circle of the diagonal triangle of a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131). The point of intersection of the bimedians of the cyclic quadrilateral belongs to the nine-point circle.[^o13][^o14] *Image: Nine-point circle of diagonal triangle.png.* $ABCD$ is a cyclic quadrilateral. $\triangle EFG$ is the diagonal triangle of $ABCD$. The point $T$ of intersection of the bimedians of $ABCD$ belongs to the nine-point circle of $\triangle EFG$.
- The nine-point circle of a reference triangle is the circumcircle of both the reference triangle's [medial triangle](wikipedia://en/3339743) (with vertices at the midpoints of the sides of the reference triangle) and its [orthic triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthic_triangle) (with vertices at the feet of the reference triangle's altitudes).[^o12]
- The center of all [rectangular hyperbolas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_hyperbola) that pass through the vertices of a triangle lies on its nine-point circle. Examples include the well-known rectangular hyperbolas of [Keipert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Wilhelm_August_Ludwig_Kiepert), [Jeřábek](wikipedia://en/55981030) and Feuerbach. This fact is known as the Feuerbach conic theorem. *Image: Tangent circles in Feuerbach's theorem.jpg.* The nine point circle and the 16 tangent circles of the orthocentric system
- If an [orthocentric system](wikipedia://en/161263) of four points $A, B, C, H$ is given, then the four triangles formed by any combination of three distinct points of that system all share the same nine-point circle. This is a consequence of symmetry: the *sides* of one triangle adjacent to a vertex that is an orthocenter to another triangle are *segments* from that second triangle. A third midpoint lies on their common side. (The same 'midpoints' defining separate nine-point circles, those circles must be concurrent.)
- Consequently, these four triangles have circumcircles with identical radii. Let $N$ represent the common nine-point center and $P$ be an arbitrary point in the plane of the orthocentric system. Then $$\overline{NA}^2 + \overline{NB}^2 + \overline{NC}^2 + \overline{NH}^2 = 3R^2$$ where $R$ is the common [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius); and if $$\overline{PA}^2 + \overline{PB}^2 + \overline{PC}^2 + \overline{PH}^2 = K^2,$$ where $K$ is kept constant, then the locus of $P$ is a circle centered at $N$ with a radius $\tfrac{1}{2} \sqrt{K^2-3R^2}.$ As $P$ approaches $N$ the locus of $P$ for the corresponding constant $K$, collapses onto $N$ the nine-point center. Furthermore the nine-point circle is the locus of $P$ such that $$\overline{PA}^2 + \overline{PB}^2 + \overline{PC}^2 + \overline{PH}^2 = 4R^2.$$
- The centers of the incircle and excircles of a triangle form an orthocentric system. The nine-point circle created for that orthocentric system is the circumcircle of the original triangle. The feet of the altitudes in the orthocentric system are the vertices of the original triangle.
- If four arbitrary points $A, B, C, D$ are given that do not form an orthocentric system, then the nine-point circles of $\triangle ABC, \triangle BCD, \triangle CDA, \triangle DAB$ concur at a point, the [Poncelet point](wikipedia://en/6811772) of $A, B, C, D$. The remaining six intersection points of these nine-point circles each concur with the midpoints of the four triangles. Remarkably, there exists a unique nine-point conic, centered at the centroid of these four arbitrary points, that passes through all seven points of intersection of these nine-point circles. Furthermore, because of the Feuerbach conic theorem mentioned above, there exists a unique rectangular [circumconic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumconic), centered at the common intersection point of the four nine-point circles, that passes through the four original arbitrary points as well as the orthocenters of the four triangles.
- If four points $A, B, C, D$ are given that form a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131), then the nine-point circles of $\triangle ABC, \triangle BCD, \triangle CDA, \triangle DAB$ concur at the [anticenter](wikipedia://en/194131#Anticenter_and_collinearities) of the cyclic quadrilateral. The nine-point circles are all congruent with a radius of half that of the cyclic quadrilateral's circumcircle. The nine-point circles form a set of four [Johnson circles](wikipedia://en/12087798). Consequently, the four nine-point centers are cyclic and lie on a circle congruent to the four nine-point circles that is centered at the anticenter of the cyclic quadrilateral. Furthermore, the cyclic quadrilateral formed from the four nine-pont centers is [homothetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothetic_transformation) to the reference cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$ by a factor of –$\tfrac{1}{2}$ and its homothetic center $N$ lies on the line connecting the circumcenter $O$ to the anticenter $M$ where $$\overline{ON} = 2\overline{NM}.$$
- The [orthopole](wikipedia://en/53303114) of lines passing through the circumcenter lie on the nine-point circle.
- A triangle's circumcircle, its nine-point circle, its [polar circle](wikipedia://en/45079434), and the circumcircle of its [tangential triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangential_triangle)[^o15] are [coaxal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coaxal_circles).[^o16]
- [Trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790) for the center of the [Kiepert hyperbola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiepert_hyperbola) are $$\frac{(b^2 -c^2)^2}{a} : \frac{(c^2-a^2)^2}{b} : \frac{(a^2-b^2)^2}{c}$$
- Trilinear coordinates for the center of the Jeřábek hyperbola are $$\cos(A)\sin^2(B-C) : \cos(B)\sin^2(C-A) : \cos(C)\sin^2(A-B)$$
- Letting $x : y : z$ be a variable point in trilinear coordinates, an equation for the nine-point circle is $$x^2\sin 2A + y^2\sin 2B + z^2\sin 2C-2(yz\sin A + zx\sin B + xy\sin C) = 0.$$

## Generalization {#Generalization}

The circle is an instance of a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673) and the nine-point circle is an instance of the general nine-point conic that has been constructed with relation to a triangle $\triangle ABC$ and a fourth point $P$, where the particular nine-point circle instance arises when $P$ is the orthocenter of $\triangle ABC$. The vertices of the triangle and $P$ determine a [complete quadrilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_quadrilateral) and three "diagonal points" where opposite sides of the quadrilateral intersect. There are six "sidelines" in the quadrilateral; the nine-point conic intersects the midpoints of these and also includes the diagonal points. The conic is an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) when $P$ is interior to $\triangle ABC$ or in a region sharing [vertical angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_angles) with the triangle, but a [nine-point hyperbola](wikipedia://en/18388859) occurs when $P$ is in one of the three adjacent regions, and the hyperbola is rectangular when P lies on the circumcircle of $\triangle ABC$.

:::
