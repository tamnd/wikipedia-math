# Crime clearance rate

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Crime clearance rate"}

In [criminal justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_justice), a **clearance rate** is a measure of how many crimes have been solved in a specific geographical region during a specific time period, usually given as a percentage. Various groups use clearance rates as a measure of [crimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime) solved by the police.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formula"}

## Formula {#Formula}

A clearance rate is calculated by dividing the number of crimes cleared by the total number of crimes reported and multiplying the result by 100. A crime is cleared when police make an arrest and a [criminal charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_charge) has been laid or the case is otherwise closed, such as when police have enough evidence to make an arrest but cannot because the suspect is dead.[^o4]

:::

## Limitations {#Limitations}

Clearance rates can be problematic for measuring the performance of police organizations and for comparing various police organizations. This is because police organizations may measure clearance rates differently. For example, each police organization may have a different method of recording when a "crime" has occurred and different criteria for determining when a crime has been "cleared." A given police organization may appear to have a much better clearance rate because of its calculation methodology.[^o6]

[Jerome Skolnick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome_Skolnick) found that focusing on clearance rates encourages the police to focus on *boosting* clearance rates rather than investigating crimes impartially.[^o7] Such focus on clearance rates may result in effort being expended to attribute crimes (correctly or incorrectly) to a criminal, which may not result in actual offenders being charged for their crimes.[^o7]

### United States {#United_States}

*Image: 2023 Clearance rates for crimes - US.svg.* The U.S. has a low clearance rate—cases solved or arrests made. Murder clearance rates of Australia, Britain and Germany are in the 70s and 90s percent range, compared to the U.S. in the 50s.[^o13]

The clearance rate for reported crimes varies by type of crime. For 2023 in the USA the arrest rate was 47.5% for [murder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder), 42.9% for aggravated [assault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assault), 35.4% for [shoplifting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoplifting), 24.9% for [robbery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robbery), 24.3% for [embezzlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embezzlement), 22.2% for [arson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arson), 12.0% for [rape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rape), 11.9% for [burglary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burglary) and 6.6% for [motor vehicle theft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_vehicle_theft).[^o14]

[Homicide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homicide) clearance rate in the United States of America has been decreasing from 95% in 1962 to 54% in 2020.[^o15] Some U.S. police forces have been criticized for overuse of "exceptional clearance", which is intended to classify as "cleared" cases where probable cause to arrest a suspect exists, but police are unable to do so for reasons outside their control (such as death or incarceration in a foreign country).[^o16]
