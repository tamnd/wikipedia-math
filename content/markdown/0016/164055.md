# Logarithmic scale

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Logarithmic scale"}

*Image: Internet host count 1988-2012 log scale.png.* [Semi-log plot](wikipedia://en/4826806) of the Internet host count over time shown on a logarithmic scale

A **logarithmic scale** (or **log scale**) is a method used to display numerical data that spans a broad range of values, especially when there are significant differences among the magnitudes of the numbers involved.

:::

Unlike a linear [scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_%28measurement%29) where each unit of distance corresponds to the same increment, on a logarithmic scale each unit of length is a multiple of some base value raised to a power, and corresponds to the multiplication of the previous value in the scale by the base value. In common use, logarithmic scales are in base 10 (unless otherwise specified).

A logarithmic scale is [nonlinear](wikipedia://en/146103), and as such numbers with equal distance between them such as 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 are not equally spaced. Equally spaced values on a logarithmic scale have exponents that increment uniformly. Examples of equally spaced values are 10, 100, 1000, 10000, and 100000 (i.e., 10<sup>1</sup>, 10<sup>2</sup>, 10<sup>3</sup>, 10<sup>4</sup>, 10<sup>5</sup>) and 2, 4, 8, 16, and 32 (i.e., 2<sup>1</sup>, 2<sup>2</sup>, 2<sup>3</sup>, 2<sup>4</sup>, 2<sup>5</sup>).

[Exponential growth](wikipedia://en/191933) curves are often depicted on a logarithmic scale [graph](wikipedia://en/19774918). Logarithmic\_scale.svg slide rule example3.svg

## Common uses {#Common_uses}

The markings on [slide rules](wikipedia://en/28743) are arranged in a log scale for multiplying or dividing numbers by adding or subtracting lengths on the scales.

The following are examples of commonly used logarithmic scales, where a larger quantity results in a higher value:

- [Economic growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_growth)
- [Richter magnitude scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richter_magnitude_scale) and [moment magnitude scale](wikipedia://en/631494) (MMS) for strength of [earthquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthquakes) and [movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_%28physics%29) in the [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228)

*Image: COB data Tsunami deaths.PNG.* A logarithmic scale makes it easy to compare values that cover a large range, such as in this map.

- [Sound level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_level_%28disambiguation%29), with the unit [decibel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decibel)
- [Neper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neper) for amplitude, field and power quantities
- [Frequency level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_level), with units [cent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cent_%28music%29), [minor second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_second), [major second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_second), and [octave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octave) for the relative pitch of notes in [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music)
- [Logit](wikipedia://en/172068) for [odds](wikipedia://en/172069) in [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685)
- [Palermo technical impact hazard scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palermo_Technical_Impact_Hazard_Scale)
- Logarithmic timeline
- Counting [f-stops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-stop) for ratios of [photographic exposure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photographic_exposure)
- The rule of nines used for rating low [probabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probabilities)
- [Entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) in [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952)
- [Information](wikipedia://en/18985062) in [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773)
- Particle size distribution curves of soil

*Image: Solarmap.gif.* Map of the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System) and the distance to [Proxima Centauri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxima_Centauri), using a logarithmic scale and measured in [astronomical units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_units).

The following are examples of commonly used logarithmic scales, where a larger quantity results in a lower (or negative) value:

- [pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH) for acidity
- [Stellar magnitude scale](wikipedia://en/1962) for brightness of [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star)
- [Krumbein scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krumbein_scale) for [particle size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_size_%28grain_size%29) in [geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology)
- [Absorbance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absorbance) of light by transparent samples

Some of our [senses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sense) operate in a logarithmic fashion ([Weber–Fechner law](wikipedia://en/273831)), which makes logarithmic scales for these input quantities especially appropriate. In particular, our sense of [hearing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearing_%28sense%29) perceives equal ratios of frequencies as equal differences in pitch. In addition, studies of young children in an isolated tribe have shown logarithmic scales to be the most natural display of numbers in some cultures.[^o3]

## Graphic representation {#Graphic_representation}

*Image: Logarithmic Scales-mkII.svg.* Various scales: lin–lin, [lin–log, log–lin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lin–log_graph), and [log–log](wikipedia://en/1659215). Plotted graphs are: *y* = 10<sup>*x*</sup> (red), *y* = *x* (green), *y* = log<sub>*e*</sub>(*x*) (blue).

The top left graph is linear in the X- and Y-axes, and the Y-axis ranges from 0 to 10. A base-10 log scale is used for the Y-axis of the bottom left graph, and the Y-axis ranges from 0.1 to 1000.

The top right graph uses a log-10 scale for just the X-axis, and the bottom right graph uses a log-10 scale for both the X axis and the Y-axis.

Presentation of data on a logarithmic scale can be helpful when the data:

- covers a large range of values, since the use of the logarithms of the values rather than the actual values reduces a wide range to a more manageable size;
- may contain [exponential laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_law) or [power laws](wikipedia://en/24522), since these will show up as straight lines.

A [slide rule](wikipedia://en/28743) has logarithmic scales, and [nomograms](wikipedia://en/271164) often employ logarithmic scales. The [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046) of two numbers is midway between the numbers. Before the advent of computer graphics, logarithmic [graph paper](wikipedia://en/293606) was a commonly used scientific tool.

### Log–log plots {#Log–log_plots}

*Image: 2010- Decreasing renewable energy costs versus deployment.svg.* A log–log plot condensing information that spans more than one order of magnitude along both axes

If both the vertical and horizontal axes of a plot are scaled logarithmically, the plot is referred to as a [log–log plot](wikipedia://en/1659215).

### Semi-logarithmic plots {#Semi-logarithmic_plots}

If only the [ordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinate) or [abscissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abscissa) is scaled logarithmically, the plot is referred to as a [semi-logarithmic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-logarithmic) plot.

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Extensions"}

### Extensions {#Extensions}

A modified log transform can be defined for negative input (*y* \< 0) to avoid the singularity for zero input (*y* = 0), and so produce symmetric log plots:[^o9][^o10]

$$Y=\operatorname{sgn}(y)\cdot\log_{10}(1+|y/C|)$$

for a constant *C*=1/ln(10).

:::

## Logarithmic units {#Logarithmic_units}

A **logarithmic unit** is a [unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Units_of_measurement) that can be used to express a quantity ([physical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_quantity) or mathematical) on a logarithmic scale, that is, as being proportional to the value of a [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) function applied to the ratio of the quantity and a reference quantity of the same type. The choice of unit generally indicates the type of quantity and the base of the logarithm.

::: {#o13 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

Examples of logarithmic units include [units of information](wikipedia://en/18075548) and [information entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_entropy) ([nat](wikipedia://en/3070481), [shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_%28unit%29), [ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ban_%28information%29)) and of [signal level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_level) ([decibel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decibel), bel, [neper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neper)). [Frequency levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_level) or logarithmic frequency quantities have various units are used in electronics ([decade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decade_%28log_scale%29), [octave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octave_%28electronics%29)) and for music pitch [intervals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_%28music%29) ([octave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octave), [semitone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitone), [cent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cent_%28music%29), etc.). Other logarithmic scale units include the [Richter magnitude scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richter_magnitude_scale) for earthquakes and the [pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH) value for [acidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acidity) or [basicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basicity).

In addition, some industrial measures are logarithmic, such as most [wire gauges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wire_gauge) used for wires and needles.

:::
