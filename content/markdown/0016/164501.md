# Nimber

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nimber"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **nimber**, also known as a "Grundy number" (though not to be confused with a [Grundy chromatic number](wikipedia://en/5224898), which is also known as a "Grundy number"), is a mathematical value used in [combinatorial game theory](wikipedia://en/292231) to represent the value of a position in an [impartial game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impartial_game). Nimbers are most closely associated with the game [Nim](wikipedia://en/21885), where the nimber of a heap is the number of objects in that heap. The nimbers are indexed by [ordinal numbers](wikipedia://en/26547932) and form a subclass of [surreal numbers](wikipedia://en/51432), but are endowed with [nimber addition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nimber_addition) and [nimber multiplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nimber_multiplication), which are distinct from both [ordinal arithmetic operations](wikipedia://en/2139226) and arithmetic operations of surreal numbers.

:::

Because of the [Sprague–Grundy theorem](wikipedia://en/41372), which states that every [impartial game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impartial_game) is equivalent to a Nim heap of a certain size, nimbers arise in a much larger class of impartial games. They can be used to analyse games such as [Nim](wikipedia://en/21885), [Cram](wikipedia://en/21563670), Northcott's game, and the impartial version of [Hackenbush](wikipedia://en/1613052).

Nimber addition and multiplication are [associative](wikipedia://en/1335) and [commutative](wikipedia://en/294390). Nimber addition has [0 as its additive identity](wikipedia://en/4178225), and each nimber is its own [additive inverse](wikipedia://en/228312). For finite nimbers, nimber addition can be calculated using the bitwise [exclusive or](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclusive_OR) (XOR) operation. The class of nimbers, under nimber addition and multiplication, forms a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) except that it is a [proper class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_class) rather than a set.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

As a class, **nimbers** are indexed by [ordinal numbers](wikipedia://en/26547932), and form a subclass of [surreal numbers](wikipedia://en/51432), introduced by [John Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) as part of his theory of [combinatorial games](wikipedia://en/292231).[^o4] However, nimbers are distinct from ordinal and surreal numbers in that they follow distinct [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118) rules, nim-addition and nim-multiplication. Other than that they are a proper class rather than a set, nimbers form a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) under nim-addition and nim-multiplication. The field of nimbers is denoted $\mathbf{On}_{2}$.

As a set, finite nimbers can be put [in one-to-one correspondence](wikipedia://en/3942) with finite ordinal numbers, which are the [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers). Nonetheless, their arithmetic structures are not [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828); nimber arithmetic fundamentally differs from ordinary arithmetic operations on natural numbers.

Nimbers are often denoted using a star notation $\{*0, *1, *2, ..., *\omega, *(\omega+1), ...\}$. Alternatively, expressions that are interpreted as ordinary arithmetic are customarily enclosed in square brackets; for example, $2^2 = *(2)^2 = 3$ in nim-multiplication, but $[2^2] = *(2^2) = 4$.[^o5][^o6]

:::

## Uses {#Uses}

### Nim {#Nim}

Nim is a game in which two players take turns removing objects from distinct heaps. As moves depend only on the position and not on which of the two players is currently moving, and where the payoffs are symmetric, Nim is an impartial game. On each turn, a player must remove at least one object, and may remove any number of objects provided they all come from the same heap. The goal of the game is to be the player who removes the last object. The nimber of a heap is simply the number of objects in that heap. Using nim addition, one can calculate the nimber of the game as a whole. The winning strategy is to force the nimber of the game to 0 for the opponent's turn.[^o10]

### Cram {#Cram}

Cram is a game often played on a rectangular board in which players take turns placing dominoes either horizontally or vertically until no more dominoes can be placed. The first player that cannot make a move loses. As the possible moves for both players are the same, it is an impartial game and can have a nimber value. For example, any board that is an even size by an even size will have a nimber of 0. Any board that is even by odd will have a non-zero nimber. Any $2 \times n$ board will have a nimber of 0 for all even $n$ and a nimber of 1 for all odd $n$.

::: {#o13 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Northcott's nim"}

### Northcott's game {#Northcott's_game}

In Northcott's game, pegs for each player are placed along a column with a finite number of spaces. Each turn each player must move the piece up or down the column, but may not move past the other player's piece. Several columns are stacked together to add complexity. The player that can no longer make any moves loses. Unlike many other nimber related games, the number of spaces between the two tokens on each row are the sizes of the Nim heaps. If your opponent increases the number of spaces between two tokens, just decrease it on your next move. Else, play the game of Nim and make the Nim-sum of the number of spaces between the tokens on each row be 0.[^o14]

:::

### Hackenbush {#Hackenbush}

Hackenbush is a game invented by mathematician [John Conway](wikipedia://en/15807). It may be played on any configuration of colored [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860) connected to one another by their endpoints and to a "ground" line. Players take turns removing line segments. An impartial game version, thereby a game able to be analyzed using nimbers, can be found by removing distinction from the lines, allowing either player to cut any branch. Any segments reliant on the newly removed segment in order to connect to the ground line are removed as well. In this way, each connection to the ground can be considered a nim heap with a nimber value. Additionally, all the separate connections to the ground line can also be summed for a nimber of the game state.

:::

## Addition {#Addition}

Nimber addition (also known as **nim-addition**) can be used to calculate the size of a single nim heap equivalent to a collection of nim heaps. It is defined recursively by

$$\alpha \oplus \beta = \operatorname{mex} \! \bigl( \{ \alpha' \oplus \beta : \alpha' < \alpha \} \cup \{\alpha \oplus \beta' : \beta' < \beta \} \bigr),$$

where the [minimum excludant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_excludant) $\operatorname{mex}(S)$ of a set $S$ of ordinals is defined to be the smallest ordinal that is *not* an element of $S$.

For finite ordinals, the **nim-sum** is easily evaluated on a computer by taking the [bitwise](wikipedia://en/264399) [exclusive or](wikipedia://en/105979) (XOR, denoted by $\oplus$) of the binary representations of the corresponding numbers. For example, the nim-sum of 7 and 14 can be found by writing 7 as 111 and 14 as 1110; the XOR of these two binary numbers is the binary number 1001, so their nim-sum is 9.

This property of addition follows from the fact that both $\operatorname{mex}$ and XOR yield a winning strategy for Nim and there can be only one such strategy; or it can be shown directly by induction: Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be two finite ordinals, and assume that the nim-sum of all pairs with one of them reduced is already defined. The only number whose XOR with $\alpha$ is $\alpha \oplus \beta$ is $\beta$, and vice versa; thus $\alpha \oplus \beta$ is excluded.

$$\zeta := \alpha \oplus \beta \oplus \gamma$$

On the other hand, for any ordinal $\gamma < \alpha \oplus \beta$, XORing $\zeta$ with all of $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ must lead to a reduction for one of them (since the leading 1 in $\zeta$ must be present in at least one of the three); since

$$\zeta \oplus \gamma = \alpha \oplus \beta > \gamma,$$

we must have either

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha > \zeta \oplus \alpha &= \beta \oplus \gamma, \quad\text{or}\\[4pt] \beta > \zeta \oplus \beta &= \alpha \oplus \gamma. \end{aligned}$$

Thus $\gamma$ is included as either

$$\begin{aligned} (\beta \oplus \gamma) \oplus \beta, \quad\text{or}\\[4pt] \alpha \oplus (\alpha \oplus \gamma); \end{aligned}$$

and hence $\alpha \oplus \beta$ is the minimum excluded ordinal.

Nimber addition is [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) and [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative), with $0$ as the additive [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962). Moreover, a nimber is its own [additive inverse](wikipedia://en/228312).[^o18] It follows that $\alpha \oplus \beta = 0$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $\alpha = \beta$.

## Multiplication {#Multiplication}

Nimber multiplication (**nim-multiplication**) is defined recursively by

$$\alpha \otimes \beta = \operatorname{mex} \, \{(\alpha' \otimes \beta) \oplus (\alpha \otimes \beta') \oplus (\alpha' \otimes \beta') : \alpha' < \alpha, \beta' < \beta \}.$$

Nimber multiplication is associative and commutative, with the ordinal $1$ as the multiplicative [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962). Moreover, nimber multiplication [distributes over](wikipedia://en/103118) nimber addition.[^o18]

Thus, except for the fact that nimbers form a [proper class](wikipedia://en/20696) and not a set, the class of nimbers forms a [ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_%28algebra%29). In fact, it even determines an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) 2, with the nimber multiplicative inverse of a nonzero ordinal $\alpha$ given by

$$\alpha^{-1} = \operatorname{mex}(S),$$

where $S$ is the smallest set of ordinals (nimbers) such that

1. $0$ is an element of $S$;
1. if $0 < \alpha' < \alpha$ and $\beta'$ is an element of $S$, then $(1 \oplus (\alpha' \oplus \alpha) \otimes \beta') \otimes (\alpha')^{-1}$ is also an element of $S$.

For all natural numbers $n$, the set of nimbers less than $[2^{2^{n}}]$ form the [Galois field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_field) $\operatorname{GF}(2^{2^{n}})$ of order $2^{2^{n}}$. Therefore, the set of finite nimbers is isomorphic to the [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566) as $n \to \infty$ of the fields $\operatorname{GF}(2^{2^{n}})$. This subfield is not algebraically closed, since no field $\operatorname{GF}(2^{k})$ with $k$ not a power of 2 is contained in any of those fields, and therefore not in their direct limit; for instance the polynomial $x^{3} + x + 1$, which has a root in $\operatorname{GF}(2^{3})$, does not have a root in the set of finite nimbers.

Just as in the case of nimber addition, there is a means of computing the nimber product of finite ordinals. This is determined by the rules that

1. The nimber product of a Fermat 2-power (numbers of the form $[2^{2^{n}}]$) with a smaller number is equal to their ordinary product;
1. The nimber square of a Fermat 2-power $[2^{2^{n}}]$ is equal to $[3\cdot2^{2^{n}-1}]$.

Subsequent extensions to infinite nimbers add extensions for each prime degree; for example, the next set of extensions (up to $\omega^{\omega}$) are those of degree $3^{n}$ over the finite nimbers:[^o5]

1. $$\omega^{3} = 2;$$
1. For $n \ge 1$, $[\omega^{3^{n}}]^{3} = [\omega^{3^{n-1}}]$.

The smallest algebraically closed field of nimbers is the set of nimbers less than the ordinal $\omega^{\omega^{\omega}}$, where $\omega$ is the smallest infinite ordinal. It follows that as a nimber, $\omega^{\omega^{\omega}}$ is [transcendental](wikipedia://en/30325) over the field.[^o21] The next transcendental element is unknown.[^o5]

:::

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

A similar field $\mathbf{On}_{p}$ over the class of all ordinals can be constructed for each prime characteristic $p$, in which addition is interpreted as base-$p$ addition without carry, and so on, but the definitions are more complicated.[^o6][^o5] For example, nim-addition in $\mathbf{On}_{3}$ is recursively defined as:

$$\alpha \oplus \beta = \operatorname{mex}(\{ \alpha' \oplus \beta : \alpha' < \alpha \} \cup \{ \alpha \oplus \beta' : \beta' < \beta \} \cup \{ \alpha' \oplus \beta' : \alpha' < \alpha \wedge \beta' < \beta \wedge \alpha' + \beta = \alpha + \beta' \}).$$

The structure of each $\mathbf{On}_{p}$ is similar below $\omega^{\omega^{\omega}}$: a series of quadratic extensions up to $\omega$, followed by extensions for each prime degree up to each $\omega^{\omega^{n}}$, with $\omega^{\omega^{\omega}}$ as the smallest transcendental element. The finite nimbers form the direct limit of $\operatorname{GF}(p^{2^{n}})$ for all $n$, such that the product of distinct Fermat *p*-powers (numbers of the form $[p^{2^{n}}]$) is their ordinary product, and the square of a Fermat *p*-power is defined such that there is a [primitive element](wikipedia://en/19127149). For example, for $\mathbf{On}_{3}$, we can take $3^{2} = 2$, $9^{2} = 4$, and $[3^{2^{n}}]^{2} = 3^{2^{n-1}}$ for $n \ge 2$. A general definition for nim-addition and nim-multiplication modulo *p*, including the rules for nimbers less than $\omega^{\omega^{\omega}}$, has been described.[^o5]

It is also possible to define $\mathbf{On}_{0}$ of characteristic 0 using the inductive definition of $\mathbf{On}_{p}$. In this case, the smallest field is the ordinals less than $\omega^{\omega}$, and it is isomorphic to the [rational numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_numbers).[^o5]

:::
