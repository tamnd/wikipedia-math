# Orthocentric system

*Image: Orthosystem SVG.svg.* **Orthocentric system**. Any point is the orthocenter of the triangle formed by the other three.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Orthocentric system"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), an **orthocentric system** is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of four [points](wikipedia://en/593693) on a [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852), one of which is the [orthocenter](wikipedia://en/161244) of the [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) formed by the other three. Equivalently, the lines passing through disjoint pairs among the points are [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944), and the four circles passing through any three of the four points have the same radius.[^o2]

:::

If four points form an orthocentric system, then *each* of the four points is the orthocenter of the other three. These four possible triangles will all have the same [nine-point circle](wikipedia://en/161243). Consequently these four possible triangles must all have [circumcircles](wikipedia://en/1926432) with the same [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius).

## The common nine-point circle {#The_common_nine-point_circle}

*Image: Orthocentric system nine point circle.svg.* **Common nine-point circle**, where $N$, $O_{4}$, and $A_{4}$ are the nine-point center, circumcenter, and orthocenter respectively of the triangle formed from the other three orthocentric points $A_{1}$, $A_{2}$, and $A_{3}$.

The center of this common nine-point circle lies at the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) of the four orthocentric points. The radius of the common nine-point circle is the distance from the nine-point center to the midpoint of any of the six connectors that join any pair of orthocentric points through which the common nine-point circle passes. The nine-point circle also passes through the three orthogonal intersections at the feet of the altitudes of the four possible triangles.

This common nine-point center lies at the midpoint of the connector that joins any orthocentric point to the circumcenter of the triangle formed from the other three orthocentric points.

The common nine-point circle is tangent to all 16 incircles and excircles of the four triangles whose vertices form the orthocentric system.[^o4]

## The common orthic triangle, its incenter, and its excenters {#The_common_orthic_triangle,_its_incenter,_and_its_excenters}

If the six connectors that join any set of orthocentric points are extended to six lines that intersect each other, they generate seven intersection points. Four of these points are the original orthocentric points and the additional three points are the [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) intersections at the feet of the [altitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude). The joining of these three orthogonal points into a triangle generates an [orthic triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthic_triangle) that is common to all the four possible triangles formed from the four orthocentric points taken three at a time.

The [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) of this common orthic triangle must be one of the original four orthocentric points. Furthermore, the three remaining points become the [excenters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excenter) of this common orthic triangle. The orthocentric point that becomes the incenter of the orthic triangle is that orthocentric point closest to the common nine-point center. This relationship between the orthic triangle and the original four orthocentric points leads directly to the fact that the incenter and excenters of a reference triangle form an orthocentric system.[^n1]

It is normal to distinguish one of the orthocentric points from the others, specifically the one that is the incenter of the orthic triangle; this one is denoted $H$ as the orthocenter of the outer three orthocentric points that are chosen as a reference triangle $\triangle ABC$. In this normalized configuration, the point $H$ will always lie within $\triangle ABC$, and all of the angles of $\triangle ABC$ will be acute. The four possible triangles referred above are then triangles $\triangle ABC$, $\triangle ABH$, $\triangle ACH$, and $\triangle BCH$. The six connectors referred above are $\overline{AB}$, $\overline{AC}$, $\overline{BC}$, $\overline{AH}$, $\overline{BH}$, and $\overline{CH}$. The seven intersections referred above are $A$, $B$, $C$, and $H$ (the original orthocentric points), and $H_{A}$, $H_{B}$, and $H_{C}$ (the feet of the altitudes of $\triangle ABC$ and the vertices of the orthic triangle).

## The orthocentric system and its orthic axes {#The_orthocentric_system_and_its_orthic_axes}

The orthic axis associated with a normalized orthocentric system $A$, $B$, $C$, and $H$, where $\triangle ABC$ is the reference triangle, is a line that passes through three intersection points formed when each side of the orthic triangle meets each side of the reference triangle. Now consider the three other possible triangles, $\triangle ABH$, $\triangle ACH$, and $\triangle BCH$. They each have their own orthic axis.

## Euler lines and homothetic orthocentric systems {#Euler_lines_and_homothetic_orthocentric_systems}

*Image: Orthocentric system and their circumcenters.PNG.* **Orthocentric system**. Where $O_{1}$, $O_{2}$, $O_{3}$, and $O_{4}$ are the circumcenters of the four possible triangles formed from the orthocentric points $A_{1}$, $A_{2}$, $A_{3}$, and $A_{4}$.

Let [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometry%29) $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$, $\mathbf{c}$, and $\mathbf{h}$ determine the position of each of the four orthocentric points and let

$$\mathbf n = \frac{\mathbf a + \mathbf b + \mathbf c + \mathbf h}{4}$$

be the position vector of $N$, the common nine-point center. Join each of the four orthocentric points to their common nine-point center and extend them into four lines. These four lines now represent the Euler lines of the four possible triangles where the extended line $HN$ is the Euler line of triangle $\triangle ABC$ and the extended line $AN$ is the [Euler line](wikipedia://en/203622) of triangle $\triangle BCH$ etc. If a point $P$ is chosen on the Euler line $HN$ of the reference triangle $\triangle ABC$ with a position vector $\mathbf{p}$ such that $\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{n} + \alpha(\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{n})$ where $\alpha$ is a pure constant independent of the positioning of the four orthocentric points and three more points $P_{A}$, $P_{B}$, and $P_{C}$ such that $\mathbf{p_{a}} = \mathbf{n} + \alpha(\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{n})$ etc., then $P$, $P_{A}$, $P_{B}$, and $P_{C}$ form an orthocentric system. This generated orthocentric system is always [homothetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothetic_transformation) to the original system of four points with the common nine-point center as the homothetic center and α the ratio of similitude.

When $P$ is chosen as the centroid $G$, then $\alpha = -1/3$. When $P$ is chosen as the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) $O$, then $\alpha = -1$ and the generated orthocentric system is [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) to the original system as well as being a reflection of it about the nine-point center. In this configuration $P_{A}$, $P_{B}$, and $P_{C}$ form a [Johnson triangle](wikipedia://en/12087798) of the original reference triangle $\triangle ABC$. Consequently the [circumcircles](wikipedia://en/1926432) of the four triangles $\triangle ABC$, $\triangle ABH$, $\triangle ACH$, and $\triangle BCH$ are all equal and form a set of [Johnson circles](wikipedia://en/12087798) as shown in the diagram adjacent.

## Further properties {#Further_properties}

The four Euler lines of an orthocentric system are orthogonal to the four orthic axes of an orthocentric system.

The six connectors that join any pair of the original four orthocentric points will produce pairs of connectors that are orthogonal to each other such that they satisfy the distance equations

$$\overline{AB}^2 + \overline{CH}^2 = \overline{AC}^2 + \overline{BH}^2 = \overline{BC}^2 + \overline{AH}^2 = 4R^2$$

where $R$ is the common circumradius of the four possible triangles. These equations together with the [law of sines](wikipedia://en/113222) result in the identity

$$\frac{\overline{BC}}{\sin A} = \frac{\overline{AC}}{\sin B} = \frac{\overline{AB}}{\sin C} = \frac{\overline{HA}}{|\cos A|} = \frac{\overline{HB}}{|\cos B|} = \frac{\overline{HC}}{|\cos C|} = 2R.$$

[Feuerbach's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feuerbach's_theorem) states that the nine-point circle is tangent to the incircle and the three excircles of a reference triangle. Because the nine-point circle is common to all four possible triangles in an orthocentric system it is tangent to 16 circles comprising the incircles and excircles of the four possible triangles.

Any conic that passes through the four orthocentric points can only be a rectangular [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052). This is a result of Feuerbach's conic theorem that states that for all circumconics of a reference triangle that also passes through its orthocenter, the [locus](wikipedia://en/542465) of the center of such circumconics forms the nine-point circle and that the circumconics can only be rectangular hyperbolas. The locus of the perspectors of this family of rectangular hyperbolas will always lie on the four orthic axes. So if a rectangular hyperbola is drawn through four orthocentric points it will have one fixed center on the common nine-point circle but it will have four perspectors one on each of the orthic axes of the four possible triangles. The one point on the nine-point circle that is the center of this rectangular hyperbola will have four different definitions dependent on which of the four possible triangles is used as the reference triangle.

The well documented rectangular hyperbolas that pass through four orthocentric points are the Feuerbach, [Jeřábek](wikipedia://en/55981030) and Kiepert circumhyperbolas of the reference triangle $\triangle ABC$ in a normalized system with $H$ as the orthocenter.

The four possible triangles have a set of four [inconics](wikipedia://en/9610491) known as the orthic inconics that share certain properties. The contacts of these inconics with the four possible triangles occur at the vertices of their common orthic triangle. In a normalized orthocentric system the orthic inconic that is tangent to the sides of the triangle $\triangle ABC$ is an inellipse and the orthic inconics of the other three possible triangles are hyperbolas. These four orthic inconics also share the same [Brianchon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brianchon_theorem) point $H$, the orthocentric point closest to the common nine-point center. The centers of these orthic inconics are the [symmedian points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmedian_point) $K$ of the four possible triangles.

There are many documented cubics that pass through a reference triangle and its orthocenter. The circumcubic known as the orthocubic - K006 is interesting in that it passes through three orthocentric systems as well as the three vertices of the orthic triangle (but not the orthocenter of the orthic triangle). The three orthocentric systems are the incenter and excenters, the reference triangle and its orthocenter and finally the orthocenter of the reference triangle together with the three other intersection points that this cubic has with the circumcircle of the reference triangle.

Any two [polar circles](wikipedia://en/45079434) of two triangles in an orthocentric system are [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal).[^n2]
