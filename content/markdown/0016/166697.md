# Greek numerals

37604371386

*Image: Borne milliaire Via Egnatia Galikos (crop).jpg.* Roman milestone with [Roman numerals](wikipedia://en/25657) CCↆX (100+100+50+10); at bottom is its Greek equivalent, ΣΞ (200+60); the stone marks 260 [Roman miles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_miles) from [Philippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippi).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Greek numerals"}

**Greek numerals**, also known as **Ionic**, **Ionian**, **Milesian**, or **Alexandrian numerals**, is a [system of writing numbers](wikipedia://en/21170) using the letters of the [Greek alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet)p. 30. In modern [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece), they are still used for [ordinal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinal_number_%28linguistics%29) and in contexts similar to those in which [Roman numerals](wikipedia://en/25657) are still used in the [Western world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world). For ordinary [cardinal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_number_%28linguistics%29), however, modern Greece uses [Arabic numerals](wikipedia://en/1786).

:::

## History {#History}

The [Minoan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minoans) and [Mycenaean civilizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenaean_civilization)' [Linear A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_A) and [Linear B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_B) alphabets used a different system, called [Aegean numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_numerals), which included number-only symbols for powers of ten: 𐄇 = 1, 𐄐 = 10, 𐄙 = 100, 𐄢 = 1,000, and 𐄫 = 10,000.[^o3]

*Image: EPMA-IGI(3)273-280-Tribute list 2-2.JPG.* [Tribute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribute) list using attic numerals, 430s BCE.

In the classical times two different systems of notation were used side by side: Firstly, an [acrophonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acrophonic) system called [Attic numerals](wikipedia://en/1455703)p. 278 -- also known as Herodianic signs -- and, secondly, alphabetic numeralsp. 14. The Attic numerals ran from Zeta archaic = 1, Pi archaic = 5, Delta 04 = 10, Eta classical = 100, Chi normal = 1,000, and Mu classical = 10,000p. 278p. 14. These were repeated at most four times; so, 50, 500, 5,000 and 50,000 were represented by the letter Pi archaic with minuscule powers of ten written in the top-right corner:

*Image: Attic\_00500.svg.*

*Image: Attic\_05000.svg.*

p. 278[^o3]. The Attic numerals were probably in use by the time of [Solon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solon)p. 278p. 31 in the 7th century BCE, and instances of it are found from 454 BCE to 95 BCEp. 15.

The alphabetic numerals used (all 24) letters of the [Greek alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet) together with three [Phoenician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenician_alphabet) and Ionic ones that had been dropped from the Athenian alphabet (although kept for numbers): [digamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digamma_%28letter%29), [koppa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koppa_%28letter%29), and [sampi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampi_%28letter%29). in three sets of nine letters representing ones, tens, and hundreds: From Alpha classical = 1 to Theta classical = 9, from Iota classical = 10 to Koppa normal = 90, and from Rho classical = 100 to Sampi Ionian or Sampi 1000 = 900p. 16. However, some signs vary from location to location due to different [local alphabets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaic_Greek_alphabets) being in use. This system probably developed around [Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miletus) in [Ionia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionia), and 19th century classicists placed its development in the 3rd century BCE, the occasion of its first widespread use.[^o4] This system was in limited use from at least 350 BCEp. 16, but the first official use of the alphabetic system in Greece proper is from c.a. 200 BCEp. 16.

More thorough [modern archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_archaeology) has caused the date to be pushed back at least to the 5th century BCE, a little before [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_%28city-state%29) abandoned its [pre-Eucleidean alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaic_Greek_alphabets) in favour of [Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_alphabet)'s in 402 BCE, and it may predate that by a century or two.[^o5] The position of those characters within the numbering system imply that the first two were still in use (or at least remembered as letters) while the third was not. The exact dating, particularly for [sampi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampi), is problematic since its uncommon value means the first attested representative near Miletus does not appear until the 2nd century BCE,[^o6] and its use is unattested in Athens until the 2nd century CE.[^o7] (In general, Athenians resisted using the new numerals for the longest of any Greek state, but had fully adopted them by c. 50 CE.[^o29])

## Description {#Description}

*Image: Greek minuscule numerals Cod.Const.Pal.Vet.f96r.svg.* Greek numerals in a c. 1100 [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) manuscript of [Hero of Alexandria](wikipedia://en/68274)'s *Metrika*. The first line contains the number "δ ϛ", i.e. "9,996 + $\tfrac{1}{4}$ + $\tfrac{1}{6}$". It features each of the special numeral symbols [sampi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampi) (ϡ), [koppa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koppa_%28letter%29) (ϟ), and [stigma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stigma_%28letter%29) (ϛ) in their [minuscule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_minuscule) forms.

Greek numerals form a [sign-value notation](wikipedia://en/5984280) system in [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214). The units from 1 to 9 are assigned to the first nine letters of the old [Ionic alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_alphabet) from [alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_%28letter%29) to [theta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theta_%28letter%29). Instead of reusing these numbers to form multiples of the higher powers of ten, however, each multiple of ten from 10 to 90 was assigned its own separate letter from the next nine letters of the Ionic alphabet from [iota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iota_%28letter%29) to [koppa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koppa_%28letter%29). Each multiple of one hundred from 100 to 900 was then assigned its own separate letter as well, from [rho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rho_%28letter%29) to [sampi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampi_%28letter%29).[^o10] Because the Phoenician precursor of sampi fell between what came to represent pi and koppa in Greekp. 3, its use to represent 900 is taken as a proof that the sampi had fallen into disuse as a letter by the time the system was createdp. 3.

This [alphabetic system](wikipedia://en/61315332) operates on the additive principle in which the numeric values of the letters are added together to obtain the total. For example, 241 was represented as Sigma classicalMu classicalAlpha classical (200 + 40 + 1). (It was not always the case that the numbers ran from highest to lowest: a 4th-century BCE inscription at Athens placed the units to the left of the tens. This practice continued in [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor) well into the [Roman period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Greece).[^o11]) In ancient and medieval manuscripts, these numerals were eventually distinguished from letters using [overbars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overbar): $\overline{\alpha}$, $\overline{\beta}$, $\overline{\gamma}$, etc. In medieval manuscripts of the [Book of Revelation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Revelation), the [number of the Beast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_of_the_beast) 666 is written as $\overline{\chi\xi\text{ϛ}}$ (600 + 60 + 6). (Numbers larger than 1,000 reused the same letters but included various marks to note the change.) Fractions were indicated as the denominator followed by a *keraia* (); γ indicated one third, δ one fourth and so on. As an exception, special symbol ∠ indicated one half, and γ° or γo was two-thirds. These fractions were additive (also known as [Egyptian fractions](wikipedia://en/336349)); for example δ ϛ indicated $\tfrac{1}{4}$ + $\tfrac{1}{6}$ = $\tfrac{5}{12}$.

*Image: Add 19391 19-20.png.* A 14th-century Byzantine map of the British Isles from a manuscript of [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claudius_Ptolemy)'s [*Geography*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy's_Geography), using Greek numerals for its [graticule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_coordinate_system): 52–63°N of the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) and 6–33°E from Ptolemy's [Prime Meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Meridian) at the [Fortunate Isles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortunate_Isles).

Although the [Greek alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet#Letter_shapes) began with only [majuscule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majuscule) forms, surviving [papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papyrus) manuscripts from [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Egypt) show that [uncial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncial) and [cursive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursive) [minuscule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_minuscule) forms began early. These new letter forms sometimes replaced the former ones, especially in the case of the obscure numerals. The old Q-shaped koppa (Ϙ) began to be broken up (

) and simplified (

and

). The numeral for 6 changed several times. During antiquity, the original letter form of digamma (Ϝ) came to be avoided in favour of a special numerical one (Digamma angular). By the [Byzantine era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Greek), the letter was known as [episemon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digamma#Episemon) and written as Digamma cursive 02 or Digamma cursive 06. This eventually merged with the [sigma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_%28letter%29)-[tau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tau_%28letter%29) [ligature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typographic_ligature) [stigma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stigma_%28letter%29) ϛ (Digamma cursive 07 or Digamma cursive 04).

In [modern Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Greek), a number of other changes have been made. Instead of extending an over bar over an entire number, the ***keraia*** (κεραία, lit. "hornlike projection") is marked to its upper right, a development of the short marks formerly used for single numbers and fractions. The modern *keraia* () is a symbol similar to the [acute accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_accent) (´), the [tonos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonos) (U+0384,΄) and the prime symbol (U+02B9, ʹ), but has its own [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode) character as U+0374. [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great)'s father [Philip II of Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Macedon) is thus known as Φίλιππος Β in modern Greek. A lower left *keraia* (Unicode: U+0375, "Greek Lower Numeral Sign") is now standard for distinguishing thousands: 2019 is represented as ΒΙΘ (2,000 + 10 + 9).

The declining use of ligatures in the 20th century also means that stigma is frequently written as the separate letters ΣΤ, although a single *keraia* is used for the group.[^o12]

## Isopsephy {#Isopsephy}

The practice of adding up the number values of Greek letters of words, names and phrases, thus connecting the meaning of words, names and phrases with others with equivalent numeric sums, is called *[isopsephy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isopsephy)*. Similar practices for the Hebrew and English are called *[gematria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gematria)* and [English Qaballa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Qaballa), respectively.

## Higher numbers {#Higher_numbers}

In his text *[The Sand Reckoner](wikipedia://en/276396)*, [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) gives an upper bound of the number of grains of sand required to fill the entire universe, using an estimate of its size current in his time. His essay demonstrated an easily visualized contradiction of the then-held adage that it was impossible to name a quantity "greater than the number of grains of sand on a beach", or in the entire world. In order to do that, he devised a [new enumeration scheme](wikipedia://en/276396#Naming_large_numbers) with much greater range than any of those shown above.

[Pappus of Alexandria](wikipedia://en/509300) reports that [Apollonius of Perga](wikipedia://en/257242) developed a simpler system based on [powers](wikipedia://en/99491) of the myriad:

α was 10,000 = 4

β was (10,000)<sup>2</sup> = 100,000,000 = 8

γ was (10,000)<sup>3</sup> = 12

and so on.[^o16]

## Zero {#Zero}

*Image: P. Lund, Inv. 35a.jpg.* Example of the early Greek symbol for zero (lower right corner) from a 2nd-century papyrus

As a non-positional system, Greek numerals do not have an official representation of the number zero.

[Hellenistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_civilization) [astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer) extended alphabetic Greek numerals into a [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) [positional](wikipedia://en/437052) [numbering system](wikipedia://en/21170) by limiting each position to a maximum value of $50 + 9$ which uses only the letters up through [nu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nu_%28Greek%29) ($\nu$) and included otherwise unused [omicron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omicron) ($o$) as a special symbol for [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29). Omicron as zero was only used alone for a whole table cell, rather than combined with other digits, like today's modern zero, which is a placeholder in positional numeric notation. This system was probably adapted from [Babylonian numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_numerals) by [Hipparchus](wikipedia://en/13600) c. 140 BCE. It was then used by [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979) (c. 140 BCE), [Theon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theon_of_Alexandria) (c. 380 CE) and Theon's daughter [Hypatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypatia_of_Alexandria) (415 CE). The symbol [omicron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omicron) or ['$o$'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omicron) as used for zero in astronomical and mathematical data tables is clearly different from its conventional use as the value for 70. In the 2nd century papyrus shown here, one can see the symbol for zero in the lower right, and a number of larger omicrons elsewhere in the same papyrus.

In [Ptolemy's table of chords](wikipedia://en/30684123), the first fairly extensive [trigonometric table](wikipedia://en/150170), there were 360 rows, portions of which looked as follows:

$$\begin{array}{ccc} \pi\varepsilon\varrho\iota\varphi\varepsilon\varrho\varepsilon\iota\tilde\omega\nu & \varepsilon\overset{\text{'}}\upsilon\vartheta\varepsilon\iota\tilde\omega\nu & \overset{\text{‘}}\varepsilon\xi\eta\kappao\sigma\tau\tilde\omega\nu \\ \begin{array}{|l|} \hline \pi\delta\angle' \\ \pi\varepsilon \\ \pi\varepsilon\angle' \\ \hline \pi\text{ϛ} \\ \pi\text{ϛ}\angle' \\ \pi\zeta \\ \hline \end{array} & \begin{array}{|r|r|r|} \hline \pi & \mu\alpha & \gamma \\ \pi\alpha & \delta & \iota\varepsilon \\ \pi\alpha & \kappa\zeta & \kappa\beta \\ \hline \pi\alpha & \nu & \kappa\delta \\ \pi\beta & \iota\gamma & \iota\vartheta \\ \pi\beta & \lambda\text{ϛ} & \vartheta \\ \hline \end{array} & \begin{array}{|r|r|r|r|} \hline \circ & \circ & \mu\text{ϛ} & \kappa\varepsilon \\ \circ & \circ & \mu\text{ϛ} & \iota\delta \\ \circ & \circ & \mu\text{ϛ} & \gamma \\ \hline \circ & \circ & \mu\varepsilon & \nu\beta \\ \circ & \circ & \mu\varepsilon & \mu \\ \circ & \circ & \mu\varepsilon & \kappa\vartheta \\ \hline \end{array} \end{array}$$

Each number in the first column, labeled $\operatorname{grc},$ ("regions") is the number of degrees of arc on a circle. Each number in the second column, labeled $\operatorname{grc},$ ("straight lines" or "segments") is the length of the corresponding chord of the circle, when the diameter is 120 units. Thus $\pi\delta$ represents an 84° arc, and the $\angle'$ after it means one-half, so that $\pi\delta\angle'$ means $84\tfrac{1}{2}$°. In the next column we see $| \pi | \mu\alpha | \gamma |$ , meaning 80 + $\tfrac{41}{60}$ + $\tfrac{3}{60^{2}}$. That is the length of the chord corresponding to an arc of $84\tfrac{1}{2}$° when the diameter of the circle is 120 units. The next column, labeled $\operatorname{grc},$ for "sixtieths", is the number to be added to the chord length for each 1′ increase in the arc, over the span of the next 1°. Thus that last column was used for [linear interpolation](wikipedia://en/160010).

The Greek [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) placeholder or zero symbol changed over time: The symbol used on [papyri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papyrus) during the second century was a very small circle with an overbar several diameters long ($\overline{o}$), terminated or not at both ends in various ways. Later, the overbar shortened to only one letter-diameter, similar to the modern 'o'+macron (ō). It was still being used in late medieval Arabic manuscripts whenever alphabetic numerals were used. In later [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) manuscripts even the minimal overbar was omitted, leaving a bare 'ο' (omicron).[^o19][^o20] This gradual change from an invented symbol $\overline{o}$ to 'ο' does not support the hypothesis that the omicron was the initial of $o\text{ὐ}\delta\text{έ}\nu$ meaning "nothing". Note that the letter 'ο' was still used with its original numerical value of 70; however, there was no ambiguity, as 70 could not appear in the fractional part of a [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) number, and zero was usually omitted when it was the integer.

Some of Ptolemy's true zeros appeared in the first line of each of his eclipse tables, where they were a measure of the angular separation between the center of the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon) and either the center of the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) (for [solar eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_eclipse)) or the center of [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228)'s shadow (for [lunar eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse)). All of these zeros took the form ο | ο ο, where Ptolemy actually used three of the symbols described in the previous paragraph. The vertical bar (|) indicates that the integral part on the left was in a separate column labeled in the headings of his tables as *digits* (of five arc-minutes each), whereas the fractional part was in the next column labeled *minute of immersion*, meaning sixtieths (and thirty-six-hundredths) of a digit.[^o21]

The Greek zero was added to Unicode in Version 4.1.0 at 1018A.
