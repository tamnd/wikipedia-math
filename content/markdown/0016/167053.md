# Unit vector

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unit vector"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **unit vector** in a [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538) is a [vector](wikipedia://en/23218956) (often a [spatial vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometry%29)) of [length](wikipedia://en/990534) 1. A unit vector is often denoted by a lowercase letter with a [circumflex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumflex), or "hat", as in $\hat{\mathbf{v}}$ (pronounced "v-hat"). The term *normalized vector* is sometimes used as a synonym for *unit vector*.

:::

The **normalized vector û** of a non-zero vector **u** is the unit vector in the direction of **u**, i.e.,

$$\mathbf{\hat{u}} = \frac{\mathbf{u}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|}=\left(\frac{u_1}{\|\mathbf{u}\|}, \frac{u_2}{\|\mathbf{u}\|}, ... , \frac{u_n}{\|\mathbf{u}\|}\right)$$

where ‖**u**‖ is the [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) (or length) of **u** and $\mathbf{u} = (u_1, u_2, ..., u_n)$.[^o2][^o3]

The proof is the following: $\|\mathbf{\hat{u}}\|=\sqrt{\frac{u_1}{\sqrt{u_1^2+...+u_n^2}}^2+...+\frac{u_n}{\sqrt{u_1^2+...+u_n^2}}^2}=\sqrt{\frac{u_1^2+...+u_n^2}{u_1^2+...+u_n^2}}=\sqrt{1}=1$

A unit vector is often used to represent [directions](wikipedia://en/54516626), such as [normal directions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_direction). Unit vectors are often chosen to form the [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) of a vector space, and every vector in the space may be written as a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) form of unit vectors.

## Orthogonal coordinates {#Orthogonal_coordinates}

### Cartesian coordinates {#Cartesian_coordinates}

Unit vectors may be used to represent the axes of a [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706). For instance, the standard *unit vectors* in the direction of the *x*, *y*, and *z* axes of a [three dimensional Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706) are

$$\mathbf{\hat{x}} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}, \,\, \mathbf{\hat{y}} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}, \,\, \mathbf{\hat{z}} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}$$

They form a set of mutually [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) *unit vectors*, typically referred to as a [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005) in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422).

They are often denoted using common [vector notation](wikipedia://en/5959843) (e.g., **x** or $\vec{x}$) rather than standard unit vector notation (e.g., **x̂**). In most contexts it can be assumed that **x**, **y**, and **z**, (or $\vec{x},$ $\vec{y},$ and $\vec{z}$) are versors of a 3-D [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706). The notations (**î**, **ĵ**, **k̂**), (**x̂<sub>1</sub>**, **x̂<sub>2</sub>**, **x̂<sub>3</sub>**), (**ê<sub>x</sub>**, **ê<sub>y</sub>**, **ê<sub>z</sub>**), or (**ê<sub>1</sub>**, **ê<sub>2</sub>**, **ê<sub>3</sub>**), with or without [hat](wikipedia://en/9226345), are also used,[^o2] particularly in contexts where **i**, **j**, **k** might lead to confusion with another quantity (for instance with [index](wikipedia://en/23624339) symbols such as *i*, *j*, *k*, which are used to identify an element of a set or array or sequence of variables).

When a unit vector in space is expressed in [Cartesian notation](wikipedia://en/7706#Representing_a_vector_with_Cartesian_notation) as a linear combination of **x**, **y**, **z**, its three scalar components can be referred to as [direction cosines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direction_cosines). The value of each component is equal to the cosine of the angle formed by the unit vector with the respective basis vector. This is one of the methods used to describe the [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29) (angular position) of a straight line, segment of straight line, oriented axis, or segment of oriented axis ([vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometry%29)).

### Cylindrical coordinates {#Cylindrical_coordinates}

The three [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) unit vectors appropriate to cylindrical symmetry are:

- $\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}$ (also designated $\mathbf{\hat{e}}$ or $\boldsymbol{\hat s}$), representing the direction along which the distance of the point from the axis of symmetry is measured;
- $\boldsymbol{\hat \varphi}$, representing the direction of the motion that would be observed if the point were rotating counterclockwise about the [symmetry axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_axis);
- $\mathbf{\hat{z}}$, representing the direction of the symmetry axis;

They are related to the Cartesian basis $\hat{x}$, $\hat{y}$, $\hat{z}$ by:

$$\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}} = \cos(\varphi)\mathbf{\hat{x}} + \sin(\varphi)\mathbf{\hat{y}}$$

$$\boldsymbol{\hat \varphi} = -\sin(\varphi) \mathbf{\hat{x}} + \cos(\varphi) \mathbf{\hat{y}}$$

$$\mathbf{\hat{z}} = \mathbf{\hat{z}}.$$

The vectors $\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}$ and $\boldsymbol{\hat \varphi}$ are functions of $\varphi,$ and are *not* constant in direction. When differentiating or integrating in cylindrical coordinates, these unit vectors themselves must also be operated on. The derivatives with respect to $\varphi$ are:

$$\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}} {\partial \varphi} = -\sin \varphi\mathbf{\hat{x}} + \cos \varphi\mathbf{\hat{y}} = \boldsymbol{\hat \varphi}$$

$$\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\hat \varphi}} {\partial \varphi} = -\cos \varphi\mathbf{\hat{x}} - \sin \varphi\mathbf{\hat{y}} = -\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}$$

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{\hat{z}}} {\partial \varphi} = \mathbf{0}.$$

### Spherical coordinates {#Spherical_coordinates}

The unit vectors appropriate to spherical symmetry are: $\mathbf{\hat{r}}$, the direction in which the radial distance from the origin increases; $\boldsymbol{\hat{\varphi}}$, the direction in which the angle in the *x*-*y* plane counterclockwise from the positive *x*-axis is increasing; and $\boldsymbol{\hat \theta}$, the direction in which the angle from the positive *z* axis is increasing. To minimize redundancy of representations, the polar angle $\theta$ is usually taken to lie between zero and 180 degrees. It is especially important to note the context of any ordered triplet written in [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates), as the roles of $\boldsymbol{\hat \varphi}$ and $\boldsymbol{\hat \theta}$ are often reversed. Here, the American "physics" convention[^o8] is used. This leaves the [azimuthal angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azimuthal_angle) $\varphi$ defined the same as in cylindrical coordinates. The [Cartesian](wikipedia://en/7706) relations are:

$$\mathbf{\hat{r}} = \sin \theta \cos \varphi\mathbf{\hat{x}} + \sin \theta \sin \varphi\mathbf{\hat{y}} + \cos \theta\mathbf{\hat{z}}$$

$$\boldsymbol{\hat \theta} = \cos \theta \cos \varphi\mathbf{\hat{x}} + \cos \theta \sin \varphi\mathbf{\hat{y}} - \sin \theta\mathbf{\hat{z}}$$

$$\boldsymbol{\hat \varphi} = - \sin \varphi\mathbf{\hat{x}} + \cos \varphi\mathbf{\hat{y}}$$

The spherical unit vectors depend on both $\varphi$ and $\theta$, and hence there are 5 possible non-zero derivatives. For a more complete description, see [Jacobian matrix and determinant](wikipedia://en/195351). The non-zero derivatives are:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{\hat{r}}} {\partial \varphi} = -\sin \theta \sin \varphi\mathbf{\hat{x}} + \sin \theta \cos \varphi\mathbf{\hat{y}} = \sin \theta\boldsymbol{\hat \varphi}$$

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{\hat{r}}} {\partial \theta} =\cos \theta \cos \varphi\mathbf{\hat{x}} + \cos \theta \sin \varphi\mathbf{\hat{y}} - \sin \theta\mathbf{\hat{z}}= \boldsymbol{\hat \theta}$$

$$\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}} {\partial \varphi} =-\cos \theta \sin \varphi\mathbf{\hat{x}} + \cos \theta \cos \varphi\mathbf{\hat{y}} = \cos \theta\boldsymbol{\hat \varphi}$$

$$\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}} {\partial \theta} = -\sin \theta \cos \varphi\mathbf{\hat{x}} - \sin \theta \sin \varphi\mathbf{\hat{y}} - \cos \theta\mathbf{\hat{z}} = -\mathbf{\hat{r}}$$

$$\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\hat{\varphi}}} {\partial \varphi} = -\cos \varphi\mathbf{\hat{x}} - \sin \varphi\mathbf{\hat{y}} = -\sin \theta\mathbf{\hat{r}} -\cos \theta\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}$$

## Curvilinear coordinates {#Curvilinear_coordinates}

In general, a coordinate system may be uniquely specified using a number of [linearly independent](wikipedia://en/101863) unit vectors $\mathbf{\hat{e}}_n$[^o2] (the actual number being equal to the degrees of freedom of the space). For ordinary 3-space, these vectors may be denoted $\mathbf{\hat{e}}_1, \mathbf{\hat{e}}_2, \mathbf{\hat{e}}_3$. It is nearly always convenient to define the system to be orthonormal and [right-handed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-hand_rule):

$$\mathbf{\hat{e}}_i \cdot \mathbf{\hat{e}}_j = \delta_{ij}$$

$$\mathbf{\hat{e}}_i \cdot (\mathbf{\hat{e}}_j \times \mathbf{\hat{e}}_k) = \varepsilon_{ijk}$$

where $\delta_{ij}$ is the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890) (which is 1 for *i* = *j*, and 0 otherwise) and $\varepsilon_{ijk}$ is the [Levi-Civita symbol](wikipedia://en/195982) (which is 1 for permutations ordered as *ijk*, and −1 for permutations ordered as *kji*).

## Right versor {#Right_versor}

A unit vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$ was called a **right versor** by [W. R. Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._R._Hamilton), as he developed his [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) $\mathbb{H} \subset \mathbb{R}^4$. In fact, he was the originator of the term *vector*, as every quaternion $q = s + v$ has a scalar part *s* and a vector part *v*. If *v* is a unit vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$, then the square of *v* in quaternions is −1. Thus by [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613), $\exp (\theta v) = \cos \theta + v \sin \theta$ is a [versor](wikipedia://en/2644621) in the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792). When *θ* is a [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956), the versor is a right versor: its scalar part is zero and its vector part *v* is a unit vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

Thus the right versors extend the notion of [imaginary units](wikipedia://en/52358) found in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), where the right versors now range over the [2-sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-sphere) $\mathbb{S}^2 \subset \mathbb{R}^3 \subset \mathbb{H}$ rather than the pair $\{i, -i$} in the complex plane.

By extension, a **right quaternion** is a real multiple of a right versor.

:::
