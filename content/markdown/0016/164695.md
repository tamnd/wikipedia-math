# Karl Pearson

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Karl Pearson"}

**Karl Pearson** (; 27 March 1857 – 27 April 1936[^o3]) was an English [biostatistician](wikipedia://en/3878) and [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902).[^o4][^o5] He has been credited with establishing the discipline of [mathematical statistics](wikipedia://en/888711).[^o6][^o7] He founded the world's first university statistics department at [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College,_London) in 1911, and contributed significantly to the field of [biometrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biometrics). Pearson was also a proponent of [Social Darwinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Darwinism) and [eugenics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics), and his thought is an example of what is today described as [scientific racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_racism). Pearson was a protégé and biographer of [Sir Francis Galton](wikipedia://en/49072). He edited and completed both [William Kingdon Clifford](wikipedia://en/250820)'s *Common Sense of the Exact Sciences* (1885) and [Isaac Todhunter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Todhunter)'s *History of the Theory of Elasticity*, Vol. 1 (1886–1893) and Vol. 2 (1893), following their deaths.

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Pearson was born in [Islington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islington), London, into a [Quaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaker) family. His father was William Pearson [QC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen's_Counsel) of the [Inner Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_Temple), and his mother Fanny (née Smith), and he had two siblings, Arthur and Amy. Pearson attended [University College School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_School), followed by [King's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College,_Cambridge), in 1876 to study mathematics, graduating in 1879 as Third [Wrangler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrangler_%28University_of_Cambridge%29) in the [Mathematical Tripos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Tripos). He then travelled to Germany to study physics at the [University of Heidelberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Heidelberg) under [G. H. Quincke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Hermann_Quincke) and metaphysics under [Kuno Fischer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuno_Fischer). He next visited the [University of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Berlin), where he attended the lectures of the physiologist [Emil du Bois-Reymond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_du_Bois-Reymond) on [Darwinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darwinism) (Emil was a brother of [Paul du Bois-Reymond](wikipedia://en/7457861), the mathematician). Pearson also studied Roman Law, taught by [Bruns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruns) and [Mommsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Mommsen), medieval and 16th century German Literature, and Socialism. He became an accomplished historian and [Germanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanist) and spent much of the 1880s in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin), [Heidelberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heidelberg), [Vienna](wikipedia://en/55866), [Saig bei Lenzkirch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saig_bei_Lenzkirch), and [Brixlegg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brixlegg). He wrote on [Passion plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passion_plays),[^o10] religion, [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethe), [Werther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werther), as well as sex-related themes,[^o11] and was a founder of the [Men and Women's Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Men_and_Women's_Club).[^o12]

*Image: Karl Pearson; Sir Francis Galton.jpg.* Pearson with Sir Francis Galton, 1909 or 1910

Pearson was offered a [Germanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanics) post at [King's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College,_Cambridge). Comparing Cambridge students to those he knew from Germany, Karl found German students inathletic and weak. He wrote to his mother, "I used to think athletics and sport was overestimated at Cambridge, but now I think it cannot be too highly valued."[^o13]

On returning to England in 1880, Pearson first went to Cambridge:

Back in Cambridge, I worked in the engineering shops, but drew up the schedule in Mittel- and Althochdeutsch for the Medieval Languages Tripos.[^o14]

In his first book, *The New Werther*, Pearson gives a clear indication of why he studied so many diverse subjects:

I rush from science to philosophy, and from philosophy to our old friends the poets; and then, over-wearied by too much idealism, I fancy I become practical in returning to science. Have you ever attempted to conceive all there is in the world worth knowing—that not one subject in the universe is unworthy of study? The giants of literature, the mysteries of many-dimensional space, the attempts of Boltzmann and Crookes to penetrate Nature's very laboratory, the Kantian theory of the universe, and the latest discoveries in embryology, with their wonderful tales of the development of life—what an immensity beyond our grasp! \[...\] Mankind seems on the verge of a new and glorious discovery. What Newton did to simplify the planetary motions must now be done to unite in one whole the various isolated theories of mathematical physics.[^o15]

Pearson then returned to London to study law, emulating his father. Quoting Pearson's own account: Coming to London, I read in chambers in Lincoln's Inn, drew up bills of sale, and was called to the Bar, but varied legal studies by lecturing on heat at Barnes, on Martin Luther at Hampstead, and on [Lassalle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Lassalle) and Marx on Sundays at revolutionary clubs around Soho.[^o14]

## Career {#Career}

*Image: Portrait\_of\_Karl\_Pearson.jpg.* 1890 photo of a young Karl Pearson

His next career move was to the [Inner Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_Temple), where he read law until 1881 (although he never practised). After this, he returned to mathematics, deputising for the mathematics professor at [King's College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College_London) in 1881 and for the professor at [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College,_London) in 1883. In 1884, he was appointed to the Goldsmid Chair of Applied Mathematics and Mechanics at University College London. Pearson became the editor of *Common Sense of the Exact Sciences* (1885) when [William Kingdon Clifford](wikipedia://en/250820) died. 1891 saw him also appointed to the professorship of [Geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) at [Gresham College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gresham_College); here he met [Walter Frank Raphael Weldon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Frank_Raphael_Weldon), a zoologist who had some interesting problems requiring quantitative solutions.[^o17] The collaboration, in [biometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biometry) and [evolutionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution) theory, was a fruitful one and lasted until Weldon died in 1906.[^o18] Weldon introduced Pearson to [Charles Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin)'s cousin [Francis Galton](wikipedia://en/49072), who was interested in aspects of evolution such as heredity and [eugenics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics). Pearson became Galton's protégé.[^o19]

After Galton's death in 1911, Pearson embarked on producing his definitive biography—a three-volume tome of narrative, letters, genealogies, commentaries, and photographs—published in 1914, 1924, and 1930, with much of Pearson's own money paying for their print runs. The biography, done "to satisfy myself and without regard to traditional standards, to the needs of publishers or to the tastes of the reading public", triumphed Galton's life, work and personal heredity. He predicted that Galton, rather than [Charles Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin), would be remembered as the most prodigious grandson of [Erasmus Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erasmus_Darwin).

When Galton died, he left the residue of his estate to the [University of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_London) for a chair in Eugenics. Pearson was the first holder of this chair—the *Galton Chair of Eugenics*, later the *Galton Chair of Genetics*[^o20]—in accordance with Galton's wishes. Pearson formed the Department of Applied Statistics (with financial support from the [Drapers' Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drapers'_Company)), which combined the Biometric and [Galton Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galton_Laboratory). He remained with the department until his retirement in 1933, and continued to work until his death.

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

In 1890, Pearson married Maria Sharpe. The couple had three children: Sigrid Loetitia Pearson, Helga Sharpe Pearson, and [Egon Pearson](wikipedia://en/1643759), who became a statistician himself and succeeded his father as head of the Applied Statistics Department at University College. Maria died in 1928 and in 1929 Karl married Margaret Victoria Child, a co-worker at the Biometric Laboratory. He and his family lived at 7 [Well Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well_Road) in [Hampstead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hampstead), now marked with a [blue plaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_plaque).[^o22][^o23] He died at [Coldharbour, Surrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coldharbour,_Surrey), on 27 April 1936.

## Einstein and Pearson's work {#Einstein_and_Pearson's_work}

When the 23-year-old [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736) started the [Olympia Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympia_Academy) study group in 1902, with his two younger friends, [Maurice Solovine](wikipedia://en/10779392) and [Conrad Habicht](wikipedia://en/54112749), his first reading suggestion was Pearson's *[The Grammar of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Grammar_of_Science)*. This book covered several themes that were later to become part of the theories of Einstein and other scientists.[^o25] Pearson asserted that the laws of nature are relative to the perceptive ability of the observer. Irreversibility of natural processes, he argued, is a purely relative conception. An observer who travels at the exact velocity of light would see an eternal now, or an absence of motion. He speculated that an observer who travelled faster than light would see time reversal, similar to a cinema film being run backwards. Pearson also discussed [antimatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimatter), the [fourth dimension](wikipedia://en/1364622), and wrinkles in time.

Pearson's [relativity](wikipedia://en/74327) was based on [idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idealism), in the sense of ideas or pictures in a [mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind). "There are many signs", he wrote, "that a sound idealism is surely replacing, as a basis for natural philosophy, the crude [materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materialism) of the older physicists." (Preface to second Ed., *[The Grammar of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Grammar_of_Science)*) Further, he stated, "... science is in reality a classification and analysis of the contents of the mind ... In truth, the field of science is much more [consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consciousness) than an external world." (*Ibid.*, Ch. II, § 6) "Law in the scientific sense is thus essentially a product of the human mind and has no meaning apart from man." (*Ibid.*, Ch. III, § 4)[^o26]

## Political views and eugenics {#Political_views_and_eugenics}

Pearson was a [zealous atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_and_positive_atheism), [freethinker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freethought),[^o29][^o30] and socialist. He gave lectures on such issues as "[the woman's question](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_woman_question)" (this was the era of the [suffragist movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffragette) in the UK)[^o31] and upon [Karl Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx). His commitment to socialism and its ideals led him to refuse the offer of being created an OBE ([Officer of the Order of the British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_honours_system)) in 1920 and also to refuse a [knighthood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knighthood) in 1935.

A eugenicist who applied [social Darwinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Darwinism) to entire nations, Pearson saw war against "inferior races" as a logical implication of the theory of evolution. "My view – and I think it may be called the scientific view of a nation", he wrote, "is that of an organized whole, kept up to a high pitch of internal efficiency by insuring that its numbers are substantially recruited from the better stocks, and kept up to a high pitch of external efficiency by contest, chiefly by way of war with inferior races."[^o32] He reasoned that, if [August Weismann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Weismann)'s theory of [germ plasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germ_plasm) is correct, the nation is wasting money when it tries to improve people who come from "poor stock".

Weismann argued that acquired characteristics could not be inherited. Therefore, training benefits only the trained generation. Their children will not exhibit the learned improvements and, in turn, will need to be improved. "No degenerate and feeble stock will ever be converted into healthy and sound stock by the accumulated effects of education, good laws, and sanitary surroundings. Such means may render the individual members of a stock passable if not strong members of society, but the same process will have to be gone through again and again with their offspring, and this in ever-widening circles, if the stock, owing to the conditions in which society has placed it, is able to increase its numbers."[^o33]

> History shows me one way, and one way only, in which a high state of civilization has been produced, namely, the struggle of race with race, and the survival of the physically and mentally fitter race. If you want to know whether the lower races of man can evolve a higher type, I fear the only course is to leave them to fight it out among themselves, and even then the struggle for existence between individual and individual, between tribe and tribe, may not be supported by that physical selection due to a particular climate on which probably so much of the Aryan's success depended.[^o34]

In *The Myth of the Jewish Race*[^o35] [Raphael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael_Patai) and Jennifer Patai cite Karl Pearson's 1925 opposition (in the first issue of the journal *Annals of Eugenics* which he founded) to [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews) immigration into Britain. Pearson alleged that these immigrants "will develop into a parasitic race. \[...\] Taken *on the average*, and regarding both sexes, this alien Jewish population is somewhat inferior physically and mentally to the native population".[^o36]

Pearson concluding remarks on stepping down as editor of the *Annals of Eugenics*, indicate a sense of failure of his aim to use the scientific study of eugenics as a guide for moral conduct and public policy.[^o37] My endeavour during the twenty-two years in which I have held the post of Galton Professor has been to prove in the first place that Eugenics can be developed as an academic study, and in the second place to make the conclusions drawn from that study a ground for social propagandism only when there are sound scientific reasons upon which to base our judgments and as a result our opinions as to moral conduct. Even at the present day there are far too many general impressions drawn from limited or too often wrongly interpreted experience, and far too many inadequately demonstrated and too lightly accepted theories for any nation to proceed hastily with unlimited Eugenic legislation. This statement, however, must never be taken as an excuse for indefinitely suspending all Eugenic teaching and every form of communal action in matters of sex.

Nonetheless, in June 2020 [UCL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London) announced that it was renaming two buildings which had been named after Pearson, because of his connection with eugenics.[^o38]

## Contributions to biometrics {#Contributions_to_biometrics}

Karl Pearson was important in the founding of the school of biometrics, which was one of several competing theories to describe evolution and population inheritance at the turn of the 20th century. His series of eighteen papers, "Mathematical Contributions to the Theory of Evolution" established him as the founder of the biometrical school for inheritance. In fact, Pearson devoted much time during 1893 to 1904 to developing statistical techniques for biometry.[^o40] These techniques, which are widely used today for statistical analysis, include the [chi-squared test](wikipedia://en/226680), [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590), and [correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_and_dependence) and [regression](wikipedia://en/826997) coefficients. Pearson's Law of Ancestral Heredity stated that germ plasm consisted of heritable elements inherited from the parents as well as from more distant ancestors, the proportion of which varied for different traits.[^o41] Karl Pearson was a follower of [Galton](wikipedia://en/49072), and although the two differed in some respects, Pearson used a substantial amount of Francis Galton's statistical concepts in his formulation of the biometrical school for inheritance, such as the law of regression. The biometric school, unlike the [Mendelians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendelians), focused not on providing a mechanism for inheritance, but rather on providing a mathematical description for inheritance that was not causal in nature. While Galton proposed a discontinuous theory of evolution, in which species would have to change via large jumps rather than small changes that built up over time, Pearson pointed out flaws in Galton's argument and actually used Galton's ideas to further a continuous theory of evolution, whereas the Mendelians favored a discontinuous theory of evolution. While Galton focused primarily on the application of statistical methods to the study of heredity, Pearson and his colleague Weldon expanded statistical reasoning to the fields of inheritance, variation, correlation, and natural and sexual selection.[^o42]

For Pearson, the theory of evolution was not intended to identify a biological mechanism that explained patterns of inheritance, whereas [Mendelian's theory](wikipedia://en/19595) postulated the [gene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene) as the mechanism for inheritance. Pearson criticized [Bateson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Bateson) and other biologists for their failure to adopt biometrical techniques in their study of evolution.[^o43] Pearson criticized biologists who did not focus on the statistical validity of their theories, stating that "before we can accept \[any cause of a progressive change\] as a factor we must have not only shown its plausibility but if possible have demonstrated its quantitative ability"[^o44] Biologists had succumbed to "almost metaphysical speculation as to the causes of heredity," which had replaced the process of experimental data collection that actually might allow scientists to narrow down potential theories.[^o45]

For Pearson, laws of nature were useful for making accurate predictions and for concisely describing trends in observed data.[^o42] Causation was the experience "that a certain sequence has occurred and recurred in the past".[^o44] Thus, identifying a particular mechanism of genetics was not a worthy pursuit of biologists, who should instead focus on mathematical descriptions of empirical data. This, in part led to the fierce debate between the biometricians and the Mendelians, including [Bateson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Bateson). After Bateson rejected one of Pearson's manuscripts that described a new theory for the variability of an offspring, or homotyposis, Pearson and Weldon established *[Biometrika](wikipedia://en/1734178)* in 1902.[^o46] Although the biometric approach to inheritance eventually lost to the Mendelian approach, the techniques Pearson and the biometricians at the time developed are vital to studies of biology and evolution today.

## Contributions to statistics {#Contributions_to_statistics}

Pearson's work was all-embracing in the wide application and development of mathematical statistics, and encompassed the fields of [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology), [epidemiology](wikipedia://en/66997), anthropometry, medicine, [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology) and social history.[^o48] In 1901, with Weldon and Galton, he founded the journal *[Biometrika](wikipedia://en/1734178)* whose object was the development of statistical theory.[^o49] He edited this journal until his death. Among those who assisted Pearson in his research were a number of female mathematicians who included [Beatrice Mabel Cave-Browne-Cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Mabel_Cave-Browne-Cave), [Frances Cave-Browne-Cave](wikipedia://en/14067713), and [Alice Lee](wikipedia://en/39502937). He also founded the journal *Annals of Eugenics* (now *[Annals of Human Genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annals_of_Human_Genetics)*) in 1925. He published the *[Drapers' Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drapers'_Company) Research Memoirs* largely to provide a record of the output of the Department of Applied Statistics not published elsewhere.

Pearson's thinking underpins many of the 'classical' statistical methods which are in common use today. Examples of his contributions are:

- [Correlation coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearson_product-moment_correlation_coefficient). The correlation coefficient (first developed by [Auguste Bravais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Bravais)[^o50][^o51] and [Francis Galton](wikipedia://en/49072)) was defined as a product-moment, and its relationship with [linear regression](wikipedia://en/48758386) was studied.[^o52] The correlation coefficient is abbreviated as *r*.
- [Method of moments](wikipedia://en/2175504). Pearson introduced [moments](wikipedia://en/368684), a concept borrowed from [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), as descriptive statistics and for the fitting of distributions to samples.
- [Pearson's system of continuous curves](wikipedia://en/1804023). A system of continuous univariate probability distributions that came to form the basis of the now conventional continuous probability distributions. Since the system is complete up to the fourth moment, it is a powerful complement to the Pearsonian method of moments.
- [Chi distance](wikipedia://en/799760). A precursor and special case of the [Mahalanobis distance](wikipedia://en/799760).[^o53]
- [p-value](wikipedia://en/554994). Defined as the probability measure of the complement of the [ball](wikipedia://en/160556) with the hypothesized value as center point and chi distance as radius.[^o53]
- Foundations of [statistical hypothesis testing theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing) and [statistical decision theory](wikipedia://en/446216).[^o53] In the seminal *"On the criterion..."* paper,[^o53] Pearson proposed testing the validity of hypothesized values by evaluating the chi distance between the hypothesized and the empirically observed values via the p-value, which was proposed in the same paper. The use of [preset evidence criteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_I_error), so called alpha type-I error probabilities, was later proposed by [Jerzy Neyman](wikipedia://en/2386106) and [Egon Pearson](wikipedia://en/1643759).[^o54]
- [Pearson's chi-squared test](wikipedia://en/226676). A hypothesis test using normal approximation for discrete data.
- [Principal component analysis](wikipedia://en/76340). The method of fitting a [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) to multivariate data by minimising the [chi distances](wikipedia://en/799760).[^o55][^o56]
- The first introduction of the [histogram](wikipedia://en/13266) is usually credited to Pearson.[^o57]

## Awards from professional bodies {#Awards_from_professional_bodies}

Pearson achieved widespread recognition across a range of disciplines and his membership of, and awards from, various professional bodies reflects this:

- 1896: elected [FRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society): Fellow of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society)[^o3]
- 1898: awarded the [Darwin Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darwin_Medal)[^o59]
- 1911: awarded the honorary degree of LLD from the [University of St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews)
- 1911: awarded a DSc from University of London
- 1920: offered (and refused) the [OBE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_British_Empire)
- 1932: awarded the Rudolf Virchow medal by the Berliner Anthropologische Gesellschaft
- 1934: offered (and refused) the [Guy Medal](wikipedia://en/6752269) of the Royal Statistical Society
- 1935: offered (and refused) a [knighthood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight)

He was also elected an Honorary Fellow of King's College, Cambridge, the Royal Society of Edinburgh, University College London and the Royal Society of Medicine, and a Member of the Actuaries' Club. A [sesquicentenary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesquicentenary#Latin-derived_numerical_names) conference was held in London on 23 March 2007, to celebrate the 150th anniversary of his birth.[^o6]

## Collections {#Collections}

Pearson's archive was donated to [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London) by his children Egon Pearson and Helga Hacker between 1967 and 1980.[^o61] The collection comprises personal and family papers, extensive notes on Pearson's lectures, publications, and scientific work, and correspondence.[^o61] Pearson's archive also includes the papers of scientist [F.R. Weldon](wikipedia://en/1557459), papers relating to the [Biometrika](wikipedia://en/1734178) journal, information on the [Galton Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galton_Laboratory) during wartime, and extensive research for Pearson's biography of Francis Galton.[^o61]

[University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London) also holds material relating to Pearson in other collections:

- Galton Papers - personal archive of [Sir Francis Galton](wikipedia://en/49072);[^o62]
- Galton Laboratory book collection - working library of the Galton Laboratory founded on Sir Francis Galton's library;[^o63]
- Galton Laboratory Records - records of the Galton Laboratory established and led by Pearson;[^o64]
- Modern Genetics Collections - publications from the Galton Laboratory, including many works by Pearson, and historical educational journals from the Institute of Education;[^o65]
- Hacker Papers - research collected by Pearson's daughter Helga Hacker whilst writing a biography of her father;[^o66]
- Egon Sharpe Pearson Papers - personal archive of [Pearson's son](wikipedia://en/1643759);[^o67]
- Sharpe Papers - personal archive of the Kenrick, Reid, Rogers and Sharpe families.[^o68]
