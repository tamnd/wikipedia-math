# Statistical significance

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Statistical significance"}

In [statistical hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing),[^o2][^o3] a result has **statistical significance** when a result at least as [extreme](wikipedia://en/160951) would be very infrequent if the [null hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis) were true.[^o44] More precisely, a study's defined **significance level**, denoted by $\alpha$, is the probability of the study rejecting the null hypothesis, given that the null hypothesis is true;[^o4] and the [*p*-value](wikipedia://en/554994) of a result, $p$, is the probability of obtaining a result at least as extreme, given that the null hypothesis is true.[^o5] The result is said to be **statistically significant**, by the standards of the study, when $p \le \alpha$.[^o6][^o7][^o8][^o9][^o10][^o11][^o12] The significance level for a study is chosen before data collection, and is typically set to 5%[^o13] or much lower—depending on the field of study.[^o14]

:::

In any [experiment](wikipedia://en/59861) or [observation](wikipedia://en/6326483) that involves drawing a [sample](wikipedia://en/160361) from a [population](wikipedia://en/27585), there is always the possibility that an observed effect would have occurred due to [sampling error](wikipedia://en/1955561) alone.[^o15][^o16] But if the *p*-value of an observed effect is less than (or equal to) the significance level, an investigator may conclude that the effect reflects the characteristics of the whole population,[^o2] thereby rejecting the null hypothesis.[^o17]

This technique for testing the statistical significance of results was developed in the early 20th century. The term *significance* does not imply importance here, and the term *statistical significance* is not the same as research significance, theoretical significance, or practical significance.[^o2][^o3][^o18][^o19] For example, the term [clinical significance](wikipedia://en/23658675) refers to the practical importance of a treatment effect.[^o20]

## History {#History}

Statistical significance dates to the 18th century, in the work of [John Arbuthnot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Arbuthnot) and [Pierre-Simon Laplace](wikipedia://en/344783), who computed the [*p*-value](wikipedia://en/554994) for the [human sex ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_sex_ratio) at birth, assuming a null hypothesis of equal probability of male and female births; see p-value for details.[^o22][^o23][^o24][^o25][^o26][^o27][^o28]

In 1925, [Ronald Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824) advanced the idea of statistical hypothesis testing, which he called "tests of significance", in his publication *[Statistical Methods for Research Workers](wikipedia://en/1645218)*.[^o29][^o30][^o31] Fisher suggested a probability of one in twenty (0.05) as a convenient cutoff level to reject the null hypothesis.[^o32] In a 1933 paper, [Jerzy Neyman](wikipedia://en/2386106) and [Egon Pearson](wikipedia://en/1643759) called this cutoff the *significance level*, which they named $\alpha$. They recommended that $\alpha$ be set ahead of time, prior to any data collection.[^o32][^o33]

Despite his initial suggestion of 0.05 as a significance level, Fisher did not intend this cutoff value to be fixed. In his 1956 publication *Statistical Methods and Scientific Inference,* he recommended that significance levels be set according to specific circumstances.[^o32]

### Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

The significance level $\alpha$ is the threshold for $p$ below which the null hypothesis is rejected even though by assumption it were true. This means that if indeed the null hypothesis is true, $\alpha$ is also the probability of mistakenly rejecting it anyway.[^o4] This is also called [false positive](wikipedia://en/43286898#False_positive_error) and [type I error](wikipedia://en/5657877#Type_I_error).

Sometimes researchers talk about the [confidence level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_level) $\gamma = (1 - \alpha)$ instead. This is the probability of not rejecting the null hypothesis given that it is true.[^o35][^o36] Confidence levels and confidence intervals were introduced by Neyman in 1937.[^o37]

## Role in statistical hypothesis testing {#Role_in_statistical_hypothesis_testing}

*Image: NormalDist1.96.svg.* In a [two-tailed test](wikipedia://en/1361141), the rejection region for a significance level of $\alpha = 0.05$ is partitioned to both ends of the [sampling distribution](wikipedia://en/520670) and makes up 5% of the area under the curve (white areas).

Statistical significance plays a pivotal role in statistical hypothesis testing. It is used to determine whether the [null hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis) should be rejected or retained. The null hypothesis is the hypothesis that no effect exists in the phenomenon being studied.[^o40] For the null hypothesis to be rejected, an observed result has to be statistically significant, i.e. the observed *p*-value is less than the pre-specified significance level $\alpha$.

To determine whether a result is statistically significant, a researcher calculates a *p*-value, which is the probability of observing an effect of the same magnitude or more extreme given that the null hypothesis is true.[^o5][^o12] The null hypothesis is rejected if the *p*-value is less than (or equal to) a predetermined level, $\alpha$. $\alpha$ is also called the *significance level*, and is the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis given that it is true (a [type I error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_I_error#Type_I_error)). It is usually set at or below 5%.

For example, when $\alpha$ is set to 5%, the [conditional probability](wikipedia://en/24104134) of a [type I error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_I_error#Type_I_error), *given that the null hypothesis is true*, is 5%,[^o41] and a statistically significant result is one where the observed *p*-value is less than (or equal to) 5%.[^o42] When drawing data from a sample, this means that the rejection region comprises 5% of the [sampling distribution](wikipedia://en/520670).[^o43] These 5% can be allocated to one side of the sampling distribution, as in a [one-tailed test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-tailed_test), or partitioned to both sides of the distribution, as in a [two-tailed test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-tailed_test), with each tail (or rejection region) containing 2.5% of the distribution.

The use of a one-tailed test is dependent on whether the [research question](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_question) or [alternative hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_hypothesis) specifies a direction such as whether a group of objects is *heavier* or the performance of students on an assessment is *better*.[^o44] A two-tailed test may still be used but it will be less [powerful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_power) than a one-tailed test, because the rejection region for a one-tailed test is concentrated on one end of the null distribution and is twice the size (5% vs. 2.5%) of each rejection region for a two-tailed test. As a result, the null hypothesis can be rejected with a less extreme result if a one-tailed test was used.[^o45] The one-tailed test is only more powerful than a two-tailed test if the specified direction of the alternative hypothesis is correct. If it is wrong, however, then the one-tailed test has no power.

### Significance thresholds in specific fields {#Significance_thresholds_in_specific_fields}

In specific fields such as [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics) and [manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing), statistical significance is often expressed in multiples of the [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590) or sigma (*σ*) of a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462), with significance thresholds set at a much stricter level (for example 5*σ*).[^o47][^o48] For instance, the certainty of the [Higgs boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_boson) particle's existence was based on the 5*σ* criterion, which corresponds to a *p*-value of about 1 in 3.5 million.[^o48][^o49]

In other fields of scientific research such as [genome-wide association studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genome-wide_association_study), significance levels as low as 5 × 10<sup>\-8</sup> are not uncommon[^o50][^o51]—as the number of tests performed is extremely large.

## Limitations {#Limitations}

Researchers focusing solely on whether their results are statistically significant might report findings that are not substantive[^o53] and not replicable.[^o54][^o55] There is also a difference between statistical significance and practical significance. A study that is found to be statistically significant may not necessarily be practically significant.[^o56][^o19]

### Effect size {#Effect_size}

Effect size is a measure of a study's practical significance.[^o56] A statistically significant result may have a weak effect. To gauge the research significance of their result, researchers are encouraged to always report an [effect size](wikipedia://en/437276) along with *p*-values. An effect size measure quantifies the strength of an effect, such as the distance between two means in units of standard deviation (cf. [Cohen's d](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohen's_d)), the [correlation coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearson_product-moment_correlation_coefficient) between two variables or [its square](wikipedia://en/1500869), and other measures.[^o58]

### Reproducibility {#Reproducibility}

A statistically significant result may not be easy to reproduce.[^o55] In particular, some statistically significant results will in fact be false positives. Each failed attempt to reproduce a result increases the likelihood that the result was a false positive.[^o60]

## Challenges {#Challenges}

### Overuse in some journals {#Overuse_in_some_journals}

Starting in the 2010s, some journals began questioning whether significance testing, and particularly using a threshold of $\alpha$=5%, was being relied on too heavily as the primary measure of validity of a hypothesis.[^o63] Some journals encouraged authors to do more detailed analysis than just a statistical significance test. In social psychology, the journal *[Basic and Applied Social Psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_and_Applied_Social_Psychology)* banned the use of significance testing altogether from papers it published,[^o64] requiring authors to use other measures to evaluate hypotheses and impact.[^o65][^o66]

Other editors, commenting on this ban have noted: "Banning the reporting of *p*-values, as Basic and Applied Social Psychology recently did, is not going to solve the problem because it is merely treating a symptom of the problem. There is nothing wrong with hypothesis testing and *p*-values per se as long as authors, reviewers, and action editors use them correctly."[^o67] Some statisticians prefer to use alternative measures of evidence, such as [likelihood ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Likelihood_ratio) or [Bayes factors](wikipedia://en/824552).[^o68] Using [Bayesian statistics](wikipedia://en/404412) can avoid confidence levels, but also requires making additional assumptions,[^o68] and may not necessarily improve practice regarding statistical testing.[^o69]

The widespread abuse of statistical significance represents an important topic of research in [metascience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metascience_%28research%29).[^o70]

### Redefining significance {#Redefining_significance}

In 2016, the [American Statistical Association](wikipedia://en/341988) (ASA) published a statement on *p*-values, saying that "the widespread use of 'statistical significance' (generally interpreted as **p*≤ 0.05') as a license for making a claim of a scientific finding (or implied truth) leads to considerable distortion of the [scientific process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method)".[^o68] In 2017, a group of 72 authors proposed to enhance reproducibility by changing the*p*\-value threshold for statistical significance from 0.05 to 0.005.[^o72] Other researchers responded that imposing a more stringent significance threshold would aggravate problems such as [data dredging](wikipedia://en/1311951); alternative propositions are thus to select and justify flexible*p*\-value thresholds before collecting data,[^o73] or to interpret*p*\-values as continuous indices, thereby discarding thresholds and statistical significance.[^o74] Additionally, the change to 0.005 would increase the likelihood of false negatives, whereby the effect being studied is real, but the test fails to show it.[^o75]***
