# Logical equivalence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Logical equivalence"}

In [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), statements $p$ and $q$ are said to be **logically equivalent** if they have the same [truth value](wikipedia://en/161711) in every [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_%28logic%29).[^o2] The logical equivalence of $p$ and $q$ is sometimes expressed as $p \equiv q$, $p :: q$, $\textsf{E}pq$, or $p \iff q$, depending on the notation being used. However, these symbols are also used for [material equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Material_equivalence), so proper interpretation would depend on the context. Logical equivalence is different from material equivalence, although the two concepts are intrinsically related.

:::

## Logical equivalences {#Logical_equivalences}

In logic, many common logical equivalences exist and are often listed as laws or properties. The following tables illustrate some of these.

### General logical equivalences {#General_logical_equivalences}

::: {#o5 .table cue=table}

| *Equivalence* | *Name* |
|---|---|
| $p \wedge \top \equiv p$ $p \vee \bot \equiv p$ | Identity laws |
| $p \vee \top \equiv \top$ $p \wedge \bot \equiv \bot$ | Domination laws |
| $p \vee p \equiv p$ $p \wedge p \equiv p$ | Idempotent or tautology laws |
| $\neg (\neg p) \equiv p$ | [Double negation](wikipedia://en/379833) law |
| $p \vee q \equiv q \vee p$ $p \wedge q \equiv q \wedge p$ | [Commutative laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_law) |
| $(p \vee q) \vee r \equiv p \vee (q \vee r)$ $(p \wedge q) \wedge r \equiv p \wedge (q \wedge r)$ | [Associative laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative_law) |
| $p \vee (q \wedge r) \equiv (p \vee q) \wedge (p \vee r)$ $p \wedge (q \vee r) \equiv (p \wedge q) \vee (p \wedge r)$ | [Distributive laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributive_law) |
| $\neg (p \wedge q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg q$ $\neg (p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q$ | [De Morgan's laws](wikipedia://en/64669) |
| $p \vee (p \wedge q) \equiv p$ $p \wedge (p \vee q) \equiv p$ | [Absorption laws](wikipedia://en/286172) |
| $p \vee \neg p \equiv \top$ $p \wedge \neg p \equiv \bot$ | Negation laws |

:::

### Logical equivalences involving conditional statements {#Logical_equivalences_involving_conditional_statements}

1. $$p \rightarrow q \equiv \neg p \vee q$$
1. $$p \rightarrow q \equiv \neg q \rightarrow \neg p$$
1. $$p \vee q \equiv \neg p \rightarrow q$$
1. $$p \wedge q \equiv \neg (p \rightarrow \neg q)$$
1. $$\neg (p \rightarrow q) \equiv p \wedge \neg q$$
1. $$(p \rightarrow q) \wedge (p \rightarrow r) \equiv p \rightarrow (q \wedge r)$$
1. $$(p \rightarrow q) \vee (p \rightarrow r) \equiv p \rightarrow (q \vee r)$$
1. $$(p \rightarrow r) \wedge (q \rightarrow r) \equiv (p \vee q) \rightarrow r$$
1. $$(p \rightarrow r) \vee (q \rightarrow r) \equiv (p \wedge q) \rightarrow r$$

### Logical equivalences involving biconditionals {#Logical_equivalences_involving_biconditionals}

1. $$p \leftrightarrow q \equiv (p \rightarrow q) \wedge (q \rightarrow p)$$
1. $$p \leftrightarrow q \equiv \neg p \leftrightarrow \neg q$$
1. $$p \leftrightarrow q \equiv (p \wedge q) \vee (\neg p \wedge \neg q)$$
1. $$\neg (p \leftrightarrow q) \equiv \neg p \leftrightarrow q$$
1. $$\neg (p \leftrightarrow q) \equiv p \leftrightarrow \neg q$$
1. $$\neg (p \leftrightarrow q) \equiv p \oplus q$$

Where $\oplus$ represents [XOR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XOR).

## Examples {#Examples}

### In logic {#In_logic}

The following statements are logically equivalent:

1. If Lisa is in [Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denmark), then she is in [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe) (a statement of the form $d \rightarrow e$).
1. If Lisa is not in Europe, then she is not in Denmark (a statement of the form $\neg e \rightarrow \neg d$).

Syntactically, (1) and (2) are derivable from each other via the rules of [contraposition](wikipedia://en/8556497) and [double negation](wikipedia://en/379833). Semantically, (1) and (2) are true in exactly the same models (interpretations, valuations); namely, those in which either *Lisa is in Denmark* is false or *Lisa is in Europe* is true.

(Note that in this example, [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868) is assumed. Some [non-classical logics](wikipedia://en/3192556) do not deem (1) and (2) to be logically equivalent.)

:::

## Relation to material equivalence {#Relation_to_material_equivalence}

Logical equivalence is different from material equivalence. Formulas $p$ and $q$ are logically equivalent if and only if the statement of their material equivalence ($p \leftrightarrow q$) is a [tautology](wikipedia://en/4495335).[^o12]

The material equivalence of $p$ and $q$ (often written as $p \leftrightarrow q$) is itself another statement in the same [object language](wikipedia://en/396102) as $p$ and $q$. This statement expresses the idea "'$p$ if and only if $q$'". In particular, the truth value of $p \leftrightarrow q$ can change from one model to another.

On the other hand, the claim that two formulas are logically equivalent is a statement in [metalanguage](wikipedia://en/402929), which expresses a relationship between two statements $p$ and $q$. The statements are logically equivalent if, in every model, they have the same truth value.
