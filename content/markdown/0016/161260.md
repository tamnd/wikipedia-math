# Incircle and excircles

*Image: Incircle and Excircles.svg.* **Incircle and excircles of a triangle.** solid black solid #728fce solid orange solid red solid #32cd32

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Incircle and excircles"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **incircle** or **inscribed circle** of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) is the largest [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) that can be contained in the triangle; it touches (is [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to) the three sides. The center of the incircle is a [triangle center](wikipedia://en/12401488) called the triangle's [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837).[^o2]

:::

An **excircle** or **escribed circle**[^o3] of the triangle is a circle lying outside the triangle, tangent to one of its sides and tangent to the [extensions of the other two](wikipedia://en/43958459). Every triangle has three distinct excircles, each tangent to one of the triangle's sides.[^o4]

The center of the incircle, called the **[incenter](wikipedia://en/316837)**, can be found as the intersection of the three [internal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_and_external_angle) [angle bisectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_bisector).[^o4][^o5] The center of an excircle is the intersection of the internal bisector of one angle (at vertex $A$, for example) and the [external](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_and_external_angle) bisectors of the other two. The center of this excircle is called the **excenter** relative to the vertex $A$, or the **excenter** of $A$.[^o4] Because the internal bisector of an angle is perpendicular to its external bisector, it follows that the center of the incircle together with the three excircle centers form an [orthocentric system](wikipedia://en/161263).[^n1]

## Incircle and Incenter {#Incircle_and_Incenter}

Suppose $\triangle ABC$ has an incircle with radius $r$ and center $I$. Let $a$ be the length of $\overline{BC}$, $b$ the length of $\overline{AC}$, and $c$ the length of $\overline{AB}$.

Also let $T_A$, $T_B$, and $T_C$ be the touchpoints where the incircle touches $\overline{BC}$, $\overline{AC}$, and $\overline{AB}$.

### Incenter {#Incenter}

The incenter is the point where the internal [angle bisectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_bisectors) of $\angle ABC,$ $\angle BCA,$ and $\angle BAC$ meet.

#### Trilinear coordinates {#Trilinear_coordinates}

The [trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790) for a point in the triangle is the ratio of all the distances to the triangle sides. Because the incenter is the same distance from all sides of the triangle, the trilinear coordinates for the incenter are[^o10]

$$\ 1 : 1 : 1.$$

#### Barycentric coordinates {#Barycentric_coordinates}

The [barycentric coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycentric_coordinates_%28mathematics%29) for a point in a triangle give weights such that the point is the weighted average of the triangle vertex positions. Barycentric coordinates for the incenter are given by

$$a : b : c$$

where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are the lengths of the sides of the triangle, or equivalently (using the [law of sines](wikipedia://en/113222)) by

$$\sin A : \sin B : \sin C$$

where $A$, $B$, and $C$ are the angles at the three vertices.

#### Cartesian coordinates {#Cartesian_coordinates}

The [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) of the incenter are a weighted average of the coordinates of the three vertices using the side lengths of the triangle relative to the perimeter (that is, using the barycentric coordinates given above, normalized to sum to unity) as weights. The weights are positive so the incenter lies inside the triangle as stated above. If the three vertices are located at $(x_a,y_a)$, $(x_b,y_b)$, and $(x_c,y_c)$, and the sides opposite these vertices have corresponding lengths $a$, $b$, and $c$, then the incenter is at

$$\left(\frac{a x_a + b x_b + c x_c}{a + b + c}, \frac{a y_a + b y_b + c y_c}{a + b + c}\right) = \frac{a\left(x_a, y_a\right) + b\left(x_b, y_b\right) + c\left(x_c, y_c\right)}{a + b + c}.$$

#### Distances to the vertices {#Distances_to_the_vertices}

Denote the incenter of $\triangle ABC$ as $I$.

The distance from vertex $A$ to the incenter $I$ is:

$$\overline{AI} = d(A, I) = c \, \frac{\sin\frac{B}{2}}{\cos\frac{C}{2}} = b \, \frac{\sin\frac{C}{2}}{\cos\frac{B}{2}}.$$

::: {#o15 .statement cue=named-heading name="Derivation of the formula stated above"}

#### Derivation of the formula stated above {#Derivation_of_the_formula_stated_above}

Use the [Law of sines](wikipedia://en/113222) in the triangle $\triangle IAB$.

We get $\frac{\overline{AI}}{\sin \frac{B}{2}} = \frac{c}{\sin \angle AIB}$. We have that $\angle AIB = \pi - \frac{A}{2} - \frac{B}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{C}{2}$.

It follows that $\overline{AI} = c \ \frac{\sin \frac{B}{2}}{\cos \frac{C}{2}}$.

The equality with the second expression is obtained the same way.

The distances from the incenter to the vertices combined with the lengths of the triangle sides obey the equation[^o16]

$$\frac{\overline{IA} \cdot \overline{IA}}{\overline{CA} \cdot \overline{AB}} + \frac{\overline{IB} \cdot \overline{IB}}{\overline{AB} \cdot \overline{BC}} + \frac{\overline{IC} \cdot \overline{IC}}{\overline{BC} \cdot \overline{CA}} = 1.$$

Additionally,[^o17]

$$\overline{IA} \cdot \overline{IB} \cdot \overline{IC} = 4Rr^2,$$

where $R$ and $r$ are the triangle's [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) and [inradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inradius) respectively.

:::

#### Other properties {#Other_properties}

The collection of triangle centers may be given the structure of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) under coordinate-wise multiplication of trilinear coordinates; in this group, the incenter forms the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962).[^o10]

### Incircle and its radius properties {#Incircle_and_its_radius_properties}

#### Distances between vertex and nearest touchpoints {#Distances_between_vertex_and_nearest_touchpoints}

The distances from a vertex to the two nearest touchpoints are equal; for example from vertex $A$:[^o21][^o22]

$$d\left(A, T_B\right) = d\left(A, T_C\right) = \tfrac12(b + c - a) = s - a,$$

where $s = \tfrac12(a + b + c)$ is the [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617).

Similarly, the tangency points of the incircle divide the sides into segments of lengths $s-b$ from $B$, and $s-c$ from $C$ (see [Tangent lines to a circle](wikipedia://en/19491492#Tangent_lines_to_one_circle)).

#### Radius {#Radius}

The [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) of a triangle's incircle is called the ***inradius***. For a triangle with sides of length $a$, $b$, $c$, the inradius is given by[^o24]

$$r = \sqrt{\frac{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}{s}},$$

where $s = \tfrac12(a + b + c)$ is the [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617) (see [Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123)).

::: {#o26 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Inradius"}

#### Relation to area of the triangle {#Relation_to_area_of_the_triangle}

The radius of the incircle is related to the [area](wikipedia://en/1209) of the triangle.[^o27] The ratio of the area of the incircle to the area of the triangle is less than or equal to $\pi \big/ 3\sqrt3$, with equality holding only for [equilateral triangles](wikipedia://en/173285).[^o28]

*Image: visual\_proof\_triangle\_area\_is\_inradius\_times\_semiperimeter.svg.* [Proof without words](wikipedia://en/18045517) that the area of a triangle equals the product of its inradius and its semiperimeter

Suppose $\triangle ABC$ has an incircle with radius $r$ and center $I$. Let $a$ be the length of $\overline{BC}$, $b$ the length of $\overline{AC}$, and $c$ the length of $\overline{AB}$.

Now, the incircle is tangent to $\overline{AB}$ at some point $T_C$, and so $\angle AT_CI$ is right. Thus, the radius $T_CI$ is an [altitude](wikipedia://en/161241) of $\triangle IAB$.

Therefore, $\triangle IAB$ has base length $c$ and height $r$, and so has area $\tfrac12 cr$.

Similarly, $\triangle IAC$ has area $\tfrac12 br$ and $\triangle IBC$ has area $\tfrac12 ar$.

Since these three triangles decompose $\triangle ABC$, we see that the area $\Delta$ of $\triangle ABC$ is:

$$\Delta = \tfrac12 (a + b + c)r = sr,$$

and

$$r = \frac{\Delta}{s},$$

where $\Delta$ is the area of $\triangle ABC$ and $s = \tfrac12(a + b + c)$ is its [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617).

For an alternative formula, consider $\triangle IT_CA$. This is a right-angled triangle with one side equal to $r$ and the other side equal to $r \cot \tfrac{A}{2}$. The same is true for $\triangle IB'A$. The large triangle is composed of six such triangles and the total area is:

$$\Delta = r^2 \left(\cot\tfrac{A}{2} + \cot\tfrac{B}{2} + \cot\tfrac{C}{2}\right).$$

:::

#### Other properties {#Other_properties}

If the [altitudes](wikipedia://en/161241) from sides of lengths $a$, $b$, and $c$ are $h_a$, $h_b$, and $h_c$, then the inradius $r$ is one third the [harmonic mean](wikipedia://en/14463) of these altitudes; that is,[^o30]

$$r = \frac{1}{\dfrac{1}{h_a} + \dfrac{1}{h_b} + \dfrac{1}{h_c}}.$$

The product of the incircle radius $r$ and the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432) radius $R$ of a triangle with sides $a$, $b$, and $c$ is[^n2]

$$rR = \frac{abc}{2(a + b + c)}.$$

Some relations among the sides, incircle radius, and circumcircle radius are:[^o53]

$$\begin{aligned} ab + bc + ca &= s^2 + (4R + r)r, \\ a^2 + b^2 + c^2 &= 2s^2 - 2(4R + r)r. \end{aligned}$$

Any line through a triangle that splits both the triangle's area and its perimeter in half goes through the triangle's incenter (the center of its incircle). There are either one, two, or three of these for any given triangle.[^o32]

The incircle radius is no greater than one-ninth the sum of the altitudes.[^o33]

The squared distance from the incenter $I$ to the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) $O$ is given by[^o34]

$$\overline{OI}^2 = R(R - 2r) = \frac{a\,b\,c\,}{a+b+c}\left [\frac{a\,b\,c\,}{(a+b-c)\,(a-b+c)\,(-a+b+c)}-1 \right ]$$

and the distance from the incenter to the center $N$ of the [nine point circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nine_point_circle) is[^o34]

$$\overline{IN} = \tfrac12(R - 2r) < \tfrac12 R.$$

The incenter lies in the [medial triangle](wikipedia://en/3339743) (whose vertices are the midpoints of the sides).[^o34]

::: {#o36 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Gergonne Point"}

### Gergonne triangle and point {#Gergonne_triangle_and_point}

*Image: Intouch Triangle and Gergonne Point.svg.* solid black solid #728fce solid red solid #1dc404

The **Gergonne triangle** (of $\triangle ABC$) is defined by connecting the three touchpoints of the incircle on the three sides. The touchpoint opposite $A$ is denoted $T_A$, etc.

This Gergonne triangle, $\triangle T_AT_BT_C$, is also known as the **contact triangle** or **intouch triangle** of $\triangle ABC$. Its area is

$$K_T = K\frac{2r^2 s}{abc}$$

where $K$, $r$, and $s$ are the area, radius of the incircle, and semiperimeter of the original triangle, and $a$, $b$, and $c$ are the side lengths of the original triangle. This is the same area as that of the [extouch triangle](wikipedia://en/11125142).[^o37]

The three lines $AT_A$, $BT_B$, and $CT_C$ intersect in a single point called the **Gergonne point**, denoted as $G_e$ (or [triangle center](wikipedia://en/12401488) $X_{7}$). The Gergonne point lies in the open [orthocentroidal disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthocentroidal_disk) punctured at its own center, and can be any point therein.[^o38]

The Gergonne point of a triangle has a number of properties, including that it is the [symmedian point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmedian_point) of the Gergonne triangle.[^o39]

[Trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790) for the vertices of the intouch triangle are given by

$$\begin{array}{rccccc} T_A =& 0 &:& \sec^2 \frac{B}{2} &:& \sec^2\frac{C}{2} \\[2pt] T_B =& \sec^2 \frac{A}{2} &:& 0 &:& \sec^2\frac{C}{2} \\[2pt] T_C =& \sec^2 \frac{A}{2} &:& \sec^2\frac{B}{2} &:& 0. \end{array}$$

Trilinear coordinates for the Gergonne point are given by

$$\sec^2\tfrac{A}{2} : \sec^2\tfrac{B}{2} : \sec^2\tfrac{C}{2},$$

or, equivalently, by the [law of cosines](wikipedia://en/19480890),

$$\frac{bc}{b + c - a} : \frac{ca}{c + a - b} : \frac{ab}{a + b - c}.$$

:::

## Excircles and excenters {#Excircles_and_excenters}

*Image: Incircle and Excircles.svg.* solid black solid #728fce solid orange solid red solid #32cd32

An **excircle** or **escribed circle**[^o3] of the triangle is a circle lying outside the triangle, tangent to one of its sides, and tangent to the [extensions of the other two](wikipedia://en/43958459). Every triangle has three distinct excircles, each tangent to one of the triangle's sides.[^o4]

The center of an excircle is the intersection of the internal bisector of one angle (at vertex $A$, for example) and the [external](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_and_external_angle) bisectors of the other two. The center of this excircle is called the **excenter** relative to the vertex $A$, or the **excenter** of $A$.[^o4] Because the internal bisector of an angle is perpendicular to its external bisector, it follows that the center of the incircle together with the three excircle centers form an [orthocentric system](wikipedia://en/161263).[^n1]

### Trilinear coordinates of excenters {#Trilinear_coordinates_of_excenters}

While the incenter of $\triangle ABC$ has [trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790) $1 : 1 : 1$, the excenters have trilinears

$$\begin{array}{rrcrcr} J_A = & -1 &:& 1 &:& 1 \\ J_B = & 1 &:& -1 &:& 1 \\ J_C = & 1 &:& 1 &:& -1 \end{array}$$

### Exradii {#Exradii}

The radii of the excircles are called the **exradii**.

The exradius of the excircle opposite $A$ (so touching $BC$, centered at $J_A$) is[^o44][^o45]

$$r_a = \frac{rs}{s - a} = \sqrt{\frac{s(s - b)(s - c)}{s - a}},$$

where $s = \tfrac{1}{2}(a + b + c).$

See [Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123).

::: {#o47 .statement cue=named-heading name="Derivation of exradii formula"}

#### Derivation of exradii formula {#Derivation_of_exradii_formula}

Source:[^o44]

Let the excircle at side $AB$ touch at side $AC$ extended at $G$, and let this excircle's radius be $r_c$ and its center be $J_c$. Then $J_c G$ is an altitude of $\triangle ACJ_c$, so $\triangle ACJ_c$ has area $\tfrac12 br_c$. By a similar argument, $\triangle BCJ_c$ has area $\tfrac12 ar_c$ and $\triangle ABJ_c$ has area $\tfrac12 cr_c$. Thus the area $\Delta$ of triangle $\triangle ABC$ is

$$\Delta = \tfrac12 (a + b - c)r_c = (s - c)r_c.$$

So, by symmetry, denoting $r$ as the radius of the incircle,

$$\Delta = sr = (s - a)r_a = (s - b)r_b = (s - c)r_c.$$

By the [Law of Cosines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Cosines), we have

$$\cos A = \frac{b^2 + c^2 - a^2}{2bc}$$

Combining this with the identity $\sin^2 \! A + \cos^2 \! A = 1$, we have

$$\sin A = \frac{\sqrt{-a^4 - b^4 - c^4 + 2a^2 b^2 + 2b^2 c^2 + 2 a^2 c^2}}{2bc}$$

But $\Delta = \tfrac12 bc \sin A$, and so

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta &= \tfrac14 \sqrt{-a^4 - b^4 - c^4 + 2a^2b^2 + 2b^2 c^2 + 2 a^2 c^2} \\[5mu] &= \tfrac14 \sqrt{(a + b + c)(-a + b + c)(a - b + c)(a + b - c)} \\[5mu] & = \sqrt{s(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)}, \end{aligned}$$

which is [Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123).

Combining this with $sr = \Delta$, we have

$$r^2 = \frac{\Delta^2}{s^2} = \frac{(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)}{s}.$$

Similarly, $(s - a)r_a = \Delta$ gives

$$\begin{aligned} &r_a^2 = \frac{s(s - b)(s - c)}{s - a} \\[4pt] &\implies r_a = \sqrt{\frac{s(s - b)(s - c)}{s - a}}. \end{aligned}$$

:::

#### Other properties {#Other_properties}

From the formulas above one can see that the excircles are always larger than the incircle and that the largest excircle is the one tangent to the longest side and the smallest excircle is tangent to the shortest side. Further, combining these formulas yields:[^o49]

$$\Delta = \sqrt{r r_a r_b r_c}.$$

### Other excircle properties {#Other_excircle_properties}

The circular [hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of the excircles is internally tangent to each of the excircles and is thus an [Apollonius circle](wikipedia://en/2642185).[^o51] The radius of this Apollonius circle is $\tfrac{r^2 + s^2}{4r}$ where $r$ is the incircle radius and $s$ is the semiperimeter of the triangle.[^o52]

The following relations hold among the inradius $r$, the circumradius $R$, the semiperimeter $s$, and the excircle radii $r_a$, $r_b$, $r_c$:[^o53]

$$\begin{aligned} r_a + r_b + r_c &= 4R + r, \\ r_a r_b + r_b r_c + r_c r_a &= s^2, \\ r_a^2 + r_b^2 + r_c^2 &= \left(4R + r\right)^2 - 2s^2. \end{aligned}$$

The circle through the centers of the three excircles has radius $2R$.[^o53]

If $H$ is the [orthocenter](wikipedia://en/161244) of $\triangle ABC$, then[^o53]

$$\begin{aligned} r_a + r_b + r_c + r &= \overline{AH} + \overline{BH} + \overline{CH} + 2R, \\ r_a^2 + r_b^2 + r_c^2 + r^2 &= \overline{AH}^2 + \overline{BH}^2 + \overline{CH}^2 + (2R)^2. \end{aligned}$$

### Nagel triangle and Nagel point {#Nagel_triangle_and_Nagel_point}

*Image: Extouch Triangle and Nagel Point.svg.* solid black solid orange solid red solid #728fce

The **Nagel triangle** or **extouch triangle** of $\triangle ABC$ is denoted by the vertices $T_A$, $T_B$, and $T_C$ that are the three points where the excircles touch the reference $\triangle ABC$ and where $T_A$ is opposite of $A$, etc. This $\triangle T_AT_BT_C$ is also known as the **extouch triangle** of $\triangle ABC$. The [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432) of the extouch $\triangle T_AT_BT_C$ is called the **Mandart circle** (cf. [Mandart inellipse](wikipedia://en/14974602)).

The three line segments $\overline{AT_A}$, $\overline{BT_B}$ and $\overline{CT_C}$ are called the [splitters](wikipedia://en/14961391) of the triangle; they each bisect the perimeter of the triangle,

$$\overline{AB} + \overline{BT_A} = \overline{AC} + \overline{CT_A} = \frac{1}{2}\left( \overline{AB} + \overline{BC} + \overline{AC} \right).$$

The splitters intersect in a single point, the triangle's [Nagel point](wikipedia://en/9489914) $N_a$ (or [triangle center](wikipedia://en/12401488) $X_{8}$).

Trilinear coordinates for the vertices of the extouch triangle are given by

$$\begin{array}{rccccc} T_A =& 0 &:& \csc^2\frac{B}{2} &:& \csc^2\frac{C}{2} \\[2pt] T_B =& \csc^2\frac{A}{2} &:& 0 &:& \csc^2\frac{C}{2} \\[2pt] T_C =& \csc^2\frac{A}{2} &:& \csc^2\frac{B}{2} &:& 0 \end{array}$$

Trilinear coordinates for the Nagel point are given by

$$\csc^2\tfrac{A}{2} : \csc^2\tfrac{B}{2} : \csc^2\tfrac{C}{2},$$

or, equivalently, by the [Law of Sines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Sines),

$$\frac{b + c - a}{a} : \frac{c + a - b}{b} : \frac{a + b - c}{c}.$$

Barycentric coordinates for the Nagel point are therefore

$$b + c - a: c + a - b : a + b - c,$$

or equivalently

$$s - a: s - b : s - c.$$

The Nagel point is the [isotomic conjugate](wikipedia://en/9492657) of the Gergonne point.

## Related constructions {#Related_constructions}

### Nine-point circle and Feuerbach point {#Nine-point_circle_and_Feuerbach_point}

*Image: Circ9pnt3.svg.* The nine-point circle is tangent to the incircle and excircles

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **nine-point circle** is a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) that can be constructed for any given [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654). It is so named because it passes through nine significant [concyclic points](wikipedia://en/504404) defined from the triangle. These nine [points](wikipedia://en/593693) are:[^o57][^o58]

- The [midpoint](wikipedia://en/437979) of each side of the triangle
- The [foot](wikipedia://en/76944) of each [altitude](wikipedia://en/161241)
- The midpoint of the [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) from each [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) of the triangle to the [orthocenter](wikipedia://en/161244) (where the three altitudes meet; these line segments lie on their respective altitudes).

In 1822, Karl Feuerbach discovered that any triangle's nine-point circle is externally [tangent](wikipedia://en/19349153) to that triangle's three excircles and internally tangent to its incircle; this result is known as [Feuerbach's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feuerbach's_theorem). He proved that:[^o59] ... the circle which passes through the feet of the altitudes of a triangle is tangent to all four circles which in turn are tangent to the three sides of the triangle ... (Feuerbach 1822)

The [triangle center](wikipedia://en/12401488) at which the incircle and the nine-point circle touch is called the [Feuerbach point](wikipedia://en/547762).

The incircle may be described as the [pedal circle](wikipedia://en/75329877) of the incenter. The locus of points whose pedal circles are tangent to the nine-point circle is known as the [McCay cubic](wikipedia://en/69592861).

::: {#o62 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Excentral triangle"}

### Incentral and excentral triangles {#Incentral_and_excentral_triangles}

The points of intersection of the interior angle bisectors of $\triangle ABC$ with the segments $BC$, $CA$, and $AB$ are the vertices of the **incentral triangle**. Trilinear coordinates for the vertices of the incentral triangle $\triangle A'B'C'$ are given by

$$\begin{array}{rccccc} A' =& 0 &:& 1 &:& 1 \\[2pt] B' =& 1 &:& 0 &:& 1 \\[2pt] C' =& 1 &:& 1 &:& 0 \end{array}$$

The **excentral triangle** of a reference triangle has vertices at the centers of the reference triangle's excircles. Its sides are on the external angle bisectors of the reference triangle (see figure at top of page). Trilinear coordinates for the vertices of the excentral triangle $\triangle J_AJ_BJ_C$ are given by

$$\begin{array}{rrcrcr} J_A =& -1 &:& 1 &:& 1\\[2pt] J_B =& 1 &:& -1 &:& 1 \\[2pt] J_C =& 1 &:& 1 &:& -1 \end{array}$$

:::

## Equations for four circles {#Equations_for_four_circles}

Let $x:y:z$ be a variable point in [trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790), and let $u=\cos^2\left ( A/2 \right )$, $v=\cos^2\left ( B/2 \right )$, $w=\cos^2\left ( C/2 \right )$. The four circles described above are given equivalently by either of the two given equations:[^o64]

- Incircle: $$\begin{aligned} u^2 x^2 + v^2 y^2 + w^2 z^2 - 2vwyz - 2wuzx - 2uvxy &= 0 \\[4pt] {\textstyle \pm\sqrt{x}\cos\tfrac{A}{2} \pm \sqrt{y\vphantom{t}}\cos\tfrac{B}{2} \pm \sqrt{z}\cos\tfrac{C}{2}} &= 0 \end{aligned}$$
- $A$-excircle: $$\begin{aligned} u^2 x^2 + v^2 y^2 + w^2 z^2 - 2vwyz + 2wuzx + 2uvxy &= 0 \\[4pt] {\textstyle \pm\sqrt{-x}\cos\tfrac{A}{2} \pm \sqrt{y\vphantom{t}}\cos\tfrac{B}{2} \pm \sqrt{z}\cos\tfrac{C}{2}} &= 0 \end{aligned}$$
- $B$-excircle: $$\begin{aligned} u^2 x^2 + v^2 y^2 + w^2 z^2 + 2vwyz - 2wuzx + 2uvxy &= 0 \\[4pt] {\textstyle \pm\sqrt{x}\cos\tfrac{A}{2} \pm \sqrt{-y\vphantom{t}}\cos\tfrac{B}{2} \pm \sqrt{z}\cos\tfrac{C}{2}} &= 0 \end{aligned}$$
- $C$-excircle: $$\begin{aligned} u^2 x^2 + v^2 y^2 + w^2 z^2 + 2vwyz + 2wuzx - 2uvxy &= 0 \\[4pt] {\textstyle \pm\sqrt{x}\cos\tfrac{A}{2} \pm \sqrt{y\vphantom{t}}\cos\tfrac{B}{2} \pm \sqrt{-z}\cos\tfrac{C}{2}} &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

## Euler's theorem {#Euler's_theorem}

[Euler's theorem](wikipedia://en/3338987) states that in a triangle:

$$(R - r)^2 = d^2 + r^2,$$

where $R$ and $r$ are the circumradius and inradius respectively, and $d$ is the distance between the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) and the incenter.

For excircles the equation is similar:

$$\left(R + r_\text{ex}\right)^2 = d_\text{ex}^2 + r_\text{ex}^2,$$

where $r_\text{ex}$ is the radius of one of the excircles, and $d_\text{ex}$ is the distance between the circumcenter and that excircle's center.[^o67][^n3][^o69]

:::

## Generalization to other polygons {#Generalization_to_other_polygons}

*Image: visual\_proof\_tangential\_polygon\_area\_is\_inradius\_times\_semiperimeter.svg.* The dashed edges show that the total length of even-numbered edges equal that of odd-numbered ones

Some (but not all) [quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/25278) have an incircle. These are called [tangential quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/9176412). Among their many properties, perhaps the most important is that their two pairs of opposite sides have equal sums. This is called the [Pitot theorem](wikipedia://en/27623148).[^o71]

More generally, a polygon with any number of sides that has an inscribed circle (that is, one that is tangent to each side) is called a [tangential polygon](wikipedia://en/35520654).

## Generalization to topological triangles {#Generalization_to_topological_triangles}

If [topological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological) triangles are considered, it is still possible to define an inscribed circle. It is no longer described as tangent to all sides, since the topological triangle might not be differentiable everywhere. Rather, it is defined as a circle whose center has the same minimal distance to each side. It has been proven that all topological triangles have an inscribed circle.[^o73]
