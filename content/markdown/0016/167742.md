# Scientism

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Scientism"}

**Scientism** is the belief that [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science) and the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method) are the best or only way to render [truth](wikipedia://en/29920) about the [world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World) and [reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reality).[^o2][^o3]

:::

While the term was defined originally to mean "methods and attitudes typical of or attributed to natural scientists", some scholars, as well as [political](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics) and [religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious) leaders, have also adopted it as a pejorative term with the meaning "an exaggerated trust in the efficacy of the methods of natural science applied to all areas of investigation (as in [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), the [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science), and the [humanities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanities))".[^o3][^o4]

## Overview {#Overview}

[Francis Bacon](wikipedia://en/11319) has been viewed by some scholars as an early proponent of scientism,[^o6] but this is a modern assertion as Bacon was a devout [Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican), writing in his *[Essays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essays_%28Francis_Bacon%29)*, "a little philosophy inclineth man's mind to [atheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism), but depth in philosophy bringeth men's minds about to religion."[^o7]

With respect to the [philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science), the term *scientism* frequently implies a critique of the more extreme expressions of [logical positivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_positivism)[^o8][^o9] and has been used by social scientists such as [Friedrich Hayek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Hayek),[^o10] philosophers of science such as [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper),[^o11] and philosophers such as [Mary Midgley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Midgley),[^o12] the later [Hilary Putnam](wikipedia://en/75233),[^o12][^o13] and [Tzvetan Todorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tzvetan_Todorov)[^o14] to describe (for example) the dogmatic endorsement of scientific methods and the reduction of all [knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge) to only that which is measured or [confirmatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmationism).[^o15]

More generally, scientism is often interpreted as science applied "in excess". This use of the term *scientism* has two senses:

- The improper use of science or scientific claims.[^n1] This usage applies equally in contexts where science might not apply,[^o17] such as when the topic is perceived as beyond the scope of [scientific inquiry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method), and in contexts where there is insufficient [empirical evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_evidence) to justify a scientific conclusion. It includes an excessive deference to the claims of scientists or an [uncritical](wikipedia://en/276872) eagerness to accept any result described as scientific. This can be a [counterargument](wikipedia://en/1684615) to [appeals to scientific authority](wikipedia://en/37568781). It can also address attempts to apply natural science methods and claims of certainty to the social sciences, which Friedrich Hayek described in *[The Counter-Revolution of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Counter-Revolution_of_Science)* (1952) as being impossible, because those methods attempt to eliminate the "human factor", while social sciences (including his own topic of [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics)) mainly concern the study of [human action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praxeology).
- "The belief that the methods of natural science, or the categories and things recognized in natural science, form the only proper elements in any philosophical or other inquiry",[^o18] or that "science, and only science, describes the world as it is in itself, independent of perspective"[^o13] with a concomitant "elimination of the [psychological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological) \[and [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirituality)\] dimensions of experience".[^o19][^o20] Tom Sorell provides this definition: "Scientism is a matter of putting too high a value on natural science in comparison with other branches of learning or culture."[^o21] Philosophers such as [Alexander Rosenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Rosenberg) have also adopted "scientism" as a name for the opinion that science is the only reliable source of knowledge.[^o22]

It is also sometimes used to describe the universal applicability of the scientific method, and the opinion that [empirical science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_science) constitutes the most authoritative [worldview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worldview) or the most valuable part of human learning, sometimes to the complete exclusion of other opinions, such as [historical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History), philosophical, economic or cultural opinions. It has been defined as "the view that the characteristic inductive methods of the natural sciences are the only source of genuine [factual knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factual_knowledge) and, in particular, that they alone can yield true knowledge about man and society".[^o23] The term *scientism* is also used by historians, philosophers, and cultural critics to highlight the possible dangers of lapses towards excessive [reductionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reductionism) with respect to all topics of human knowledge.[^o24][^o25][^o26][^o27][^o28]

For [social theorists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_theorist) practising the tradition of [Max Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Weber), such as [Jürgen Habermas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jürgen_Habermas) and [Max Horkheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Horkheimer), the concept of scientism relates significantly to the philosophy of [positivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positivism), but also to the cultural [rationalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationalization_%28sociology%29) for modern [Western civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture).[^o15][^o29] [Ernesto Sabato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernesto_Sabato), [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist) and [essayist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essayist), wrote in his 1951 essay Hombres y engranajes ("Man and mechanism") of the "superstition of science" as the most contradictory of all [superstitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superstition),[^o30] since this would be the "superstition that one should not be *superstitious*". He wrote: "science had become a new magic and the man in the street believed in it the more the less he understood it".[^o30]

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Reviewing the references to scientism in the works of contemporary scholars in 2003, Gregory R. Peterson[^n2] detected two main general themes:

- It is used to criticize a totalizing opinion of science as if it were capable of describing *all* reality and knowledge, or as if it were the *only* true method to acquire knowledge about reality and the nature of things;
- It is used, often pejoratively,[^o34][^o35][^o36] to denote violations by which the theories and methods of one (scientific) discipline are applied inappropriately to another (scientific or non-scientific) discipline and its domain. An example of this second usage is to term as scientism any attempt to claim science as the only or primary source of human values (a traditional domain of [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics)) or as the source of [meaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meaning_%28existential%29) and purpose (a traditional domain of [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion) and related [worldviews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worldview)).

The term *scientism* was popularized by [F. A. Hayek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._A._Hayek), who defined it in 1942 as the "slavish imitation of the method and language of Science".[^o37]

[Mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042), in his 1971 essay "The New Universal Church", characterized scientism as a religion-like ideology that advocates scientific [reductionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reductionism), scientific [authoritarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authoritarianism), political [technocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technocracy) and technological salvation, while denying the [epistemological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemological) validity of feelings and experiences such as love, emotion, beauty and fulfillment.[^o38] He predicted that "in coming years, the chief political dividing line will fall less and less among the traditional division between '[right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-wing_politics)' and '[left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-wing_politics)', but increasingly between the adherents of scientism, who advocate '[technological progress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_change) at any price', and their opponents, i.e., roughly speaking, those who regard the enhancement of life, in all its richness and variety, as being the supreme value".[^o38]

[E. F. Schumacher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._F._Schumacher), in his *[A Guide for the Perplexed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Guide_for_the_Perplexed)* (1977), criticized scientism as an impoverished [world view](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_view) confined solely to what can be counted, measured and weighed. "The architects of the modern worldview, notably [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo) and [Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes), assumed that those things that could be weighed, measured, and counted were more true than those that could not be quantified. If it couldn't be counted, in other words, it didn't count."[^o39]

In 1979, [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper) defined scientism as "the aping of what is widely mistaken for the method of science".[^o40]

In 2003, [Mikael Stenmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikael_Stenmark) proposed the expression *scientific expansionism* as a synonym of scientism.[^o41] In the *Encyclopedia of Science and Religion*, he wrote that, while the [doctrines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctrine) that are described as scientism have many possible forms and varying degrees of ambition, they share the idea that the boundaries of science (that is, typically the natural sciences) could and should be expanded so that something that has not been previously considered as a subject pertinent to science can now be understood as part of science (usually with science becoming the sole or the main arbiter regarding this area or dimension).[^o41] According to Stenmark, the strongest form of scientism states that science does not have any boundaries and that all human problems and all aspects of human endeavor, with due time, will be dealt with and solved by science alone.[^o41] This idea has also been termed the [myth of progress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myth_of_progress).[^o42]

Intellectual historian [T. J. Jackson Lears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._J._Jackson_Lears) argued in 2013 that there has been a recent reemergence of "nineteenth-century positivist faith that a reified 'science' has discovered (or is about to discover) all the important truths about human life. Precise measurement and rigorous calculation, in this view, are the basis for finally settling enduring [metaphysical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysical) and [moral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morality) controversies." Lears specifically identified Harvard psychologist [Steven Pinker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Pinker)'s work as falling in this category.[^o43] Philosophers [John N. Gray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_N._Gray) and [Thomas Nagel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Nagel) have made similar criticisms against popular works by moral psychologist [Jonathan Haidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Haidt), atheist author [Sam Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Harris_%28author%29), and writer [Malcolm Gladwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_Gladwell).[^o44][^o45][^o46]

### Strong and weak scientism {#Strong_and_weak_scientism}

There are various ways of classifying kinds of scientism.[^o3][^o48] Some authors distinguish between strong and weak scientism, as follows:

- Strong scientism: "of all the knowledge we have, scientific knowledge is the *only* 'real knowledge'"[^o49] (Moti Mizrahi), or, "the view that some proposition or theory is true and/or [rational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational) to believe if and only if it is a scientific proposition or theory"[^o50][^o51][^o52] ([J.&nbsp;P. Moreland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J.&nbsp;P._Moreland)), or, "only science yields epistemically credible data"[^o53] ([Michael W. Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_W._Austin))
- Weak scientism: "of all the knowledge we have, scientific knowledge is the *best* knowledge"[^o49] (Moti Mizrahi), or, "science is the most valuable, most serious, and most authoritative sector of human learning"[^o50][^o51][^o52] (J. P. Moreland), or, "scientific knowledge claims are the most credible knowledge claims"[^o53] (Michael W. Austin)

A 2023 research article by Rik Peels in the journal *Interdisciplinary Science Reviews* explores the concept of scientism, defining it as the belief that science is the only means of obtaining knowledge and truth. Peels distinguishes between *weak scientism*, which limits the validity of science to specific areas, and *strong scientism*, which extends this validity to all fields of knowledge. The author argues that strong scientism is untenable and self-confuting because science itself is based on [common sense](wikipedia://en/21478754) assumptions and non-scientific principles. He proposes that scientism can be considered a form of [fundamentalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamentalism), characterized by a [Manichean narrative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manichaeism) that is reactive against other sources of knowledge. The article suggests that science can learn from mainstream religion when it comes to scientific fundamentalism, by promoting a more open and tolerant approach to other forms of knowledge.[^o54]

## Relevance to debates about science and religion {#Relevance_to_debates_about_science_and_religion}

Both religious and non-religious scholars have applied the term *scientism* to individuals associated with [New Atheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Atheism).[^o56][^o57] [Theologian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theologian) [John Haught](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Haught) argued that philosopher [Daniel Dennett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Dennett) and other New Atheists subscribe to a belief system of [scientific naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_naturalism), which includes the dogma that "only nature, including humans and our creations, is real: that God does not exist; and that science alone can give us complete and reliable knowledge of reality."[^o58] Haught argued that this belief system is self-refuting since it requires its adherents to assent to beliefs that violate its own stated requirements for knowledge.[^o59] Christian philosopher Peter Williams argued in 2013 that it is only by conflating science with scientism that New Atheists feel qualified to "pontificate on metaphysical issues".[^o60] Daniel Dennett responded to religious criticism of his 2006 book *[Breaking the Spell: Religion as a Natural Phenomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breaking_the_Spell:_Religion_as_a_Natural_Phenomenon)* by saying that accusations of scientism "\[are\] an all-purpose, wild-card smear ... When someone puts forward a scientific theory that \[religious critics\] really don't like, they just try to discredit it as 'scientism'. But when it comes to [facts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fact), and explanations of facts, science is the only game in town".[^o61]

Non-religious scholars have also associated New Atheist thought with scientism and/or with positivism. Atheist philosopher [Thomas Nagel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Nagel) argued that philosopher [Sam Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Harris_%28author%29) conflated all empirical knowledge with scientific knowledge.[^o62] Marxist literary critic [Terry Eagleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_Eagleton) argued that [Christopher Hitchens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Hitchens) possessed an "old-fashioned scientistic notion of what counts as evidence" that reduces knowledge to what can and cannot be proven by scientific procedure.[^o63] [Agnostic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnostic) philosopher [Anthony Kenny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Kenny) has also criticized New Atheist philosopher [Alexander Rosenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Rosenberg)'s *The Atheist's Guide to Reality* for resurrecting a self-refuting [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology) of logical positivism and reducing all knowledge of the universe to the discipline of [physics](wikipedia://en/22939).[^o64]

[Michael Shermer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Shermer), founder of [The Skeptics Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Skeptics_Society), discussed resemblances between scientism and traditional religions, indicating the [cult of personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cult_of_personality) that develops for some scientists. He defined scientism as a worldview that encompasses natural explanations, eschews [supernatural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural) and [paranormal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paranormal) speculations, and embraces empiricism and [reason](wikipedia://en/42446).[^o65]

The Iranian scholar [Seyyed Hossein Nasr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seyyed_Hossein_Nasr) has stated that in the [Western world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world), many will accept the [ideology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology) of modern science, not as "simple ordinary science", but as a replacement for religion.[^o66]

Gregory R. Peterson wrote that "for many theologians and philosophers, scientism is among the greatest of intellectual sins".[^n2] [Genetic biologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_biology) Austin L. Hughes wrote in the conservative journal [*The New Atlantis*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Atlantis_%28journal%29) that scientism has much in common with superstition: "the stubborn insistence that something ... has powers which no evidence supports."[^o68]

Repeating common criticisms of logical positivism and [verificationism](wikipedia://en/3292195), [philosopher of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_religion) [Keith Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Ward) has said that scientism is philosophically inconsistent or even [self-refuting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-refuting), as the truth of the two statements "no statements are true unless they can be proven scientifically (or [logically](wikipedia://en/46426065))" and "no statements are true unless they can be shown empirically to be true" cannot themselves be proven scientifically, logically, or empirically.[^o69][^o70]

## Philosophy of science {#Philosophy_of_science}

### Anti-scientism {#Anti-scientism}

Philosopher [Paul Feyerabend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Feyerabend), who was an enthusiastic proponent of scientism during his youth,[^o73] later came to characterize science as "an essentially anarchic enterprise"[^n3] and argued emphatically that science merits no exclusive monopoly of "dealing in knowledge" and that scientists have never operated within a distinct and narrowly self-defined tradition. In his essay *[Against Method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Against_Method)* he depicted the process of contemporary scientific education as a mild form of [indoctrination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indoctrination), intended for "making the history of science duller, simpler, more uniform, more 'objective' and more easily accessible to treatment by strict and unchanging rules".[^n4]

## Rhetoric of science {#Rhetoric_of_science}

[Thomas M. Lessl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_M._Lessl) argued that religious themes persist in what he terms scientism, the [public rhetoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_rhetoric) of science.[^o87] There are two methods of describing this idea of scientism: the epistemological method (the assumption that the scientific method trumps other ways of knowing) and the [ontological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontological) method (that the rational mind represents the world and both operate in knowable ways). According to Lessl, the ontological method is an attempt to "resolve the conflict between rationalism and skepticism". Lessl also argued that without scientism, there would not be a scientific culture.[^o87]

## Rationalization and modernity {#Rationalization_and_modernity}

In the introduction to his collected works on the [sociology of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology_of_religion), [Max Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Weber) asked why "the scientific, the artistic, the political, or the economic development \[elsewhere\] ... did not enter upon that path of rationalization which is peculiar to the [Occident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world)?" According to the German social theorist [Jürgen Habermas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jürgen_Habermas), "For Weber, the intrinsic (that is, not merely [contingent](wikipedia://en/17753389)) relationship between [modernity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernity) and what he called 'Occidental rationalism' was still self-evident." Weber described a process of rationalisation, [disenchantment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disenchantment) and the "disintegration of religious world views" that resulted in modern [secular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secularisation) societies and [capitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism).[^o89]

Habermas is critical of pure [instrumental rationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_rationality), arguing that the "Social Life–World" of subjective experiencing is better suited to literary expression. Where the sciences select experiences that can be expressed in [formal language](wikipedia://en/10939) using general definitions, the literary arts select private, unrepeatable experiences where definitions are generated through "[intersubjectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersubjectivity) of mutual understanding in each concrete case".[^o90][^o91] Habermas quoted writer [Aldous Huxley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldous_Huxley) in order to juxtapose the "social life-world" and the "worldless universe of facts" underscoring the duality of literature and science:
