# Integer matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Integer matrix"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **integer matrix** is a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) whose entries are all [integers](wikipedia://en/14563). Examples include [binary matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_matrix), the [zero matrix](wikipedia://en/1168888), the [matrix of ones](wikipedia://en/690246), the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718), and the [adjacency matrices](wikipedia://en/244463) used in [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), amongst many others. Integer matrices find frequent application in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170).

:::

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

$\left(\begin{array}{cccr} 5 & 2 & 6 & 0\\ 4 & 7 & 3 & 8\\ 5 & 9 & 0 & 4\\ 3 & 1 & 0 & \!\!\!-3\\ 9 & 0 & 2 & 1\end{array}\right)$ and $\left(\begin{array}{ccc} 1 & 5 & 0\\ 0 & 9 & 2\\ 1 & 7 & 3\end{array}\right)$

are both examples of integer matrices.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

[Invertibility](wikipedia://en/217122) of integer matrices is in general more numerically stable than that of non-integer matrices. The [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of an integer matrix $M$ is itself an integer, and the [adjugate matrix](wikipedia://en/202840) of an integer matrix is also integer matrix. Thus, the determinant of an invertible integer matrix is $1$ or $-1$, and hence where inverses exist they do not become excessively large (see [condition number](wikipedia://en/6934)). Theorems from [matrix theory](wikipedia://en/20556859) that infer properties from determinants thus avoid the traps induced by [ill conditioned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ill-conditioned_matrix) (*nearly* zero determinant) [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [floating point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_point) valued matrices.

The inverse of an integer matrix $M$ is again an integer matrix if and only if the determinant of $M$ equals $1$ or $-1$.[^o6] Integer matrices of determinant $1$ form the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbf{Z})$, which has far-reaching applications in arithmetic and [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446). For $n=2$, it is closely related to the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019).

The intersection of the integer matrices with the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) is the group of [signed permutation matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signed_permutation_matrices).

The [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) of an integer matrix has integer coefficients. Since the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of a matrix are the [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) of this polynomial, the eigenvalues of an integer matrix are [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981). In dimension [less than 5](wikipedia://en/152518), they can thus be expressed by [radicals](wikipedia://en/235029) involving integers.

Integer matrices are sometimes called *integral matrices*, although this use is discouraged.
