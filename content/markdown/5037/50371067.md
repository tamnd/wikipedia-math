# Muisca numerals

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Muisca numerals"}

**Muisca numerals** were the numeric notation system used by the [Muisca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_people), one of the civilizations of the [Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americas) before the [Spanish conquest of the Muisca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_conquest_of_the_Muisca). Just like the [Mayas](wikipedia://en/19823), the Muisca had a [vigesimal](wikipedia://en/209196) numerical system, based on multiples of twenty ([Chibcha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chibcha_language): *gueta*). The Muisca numerals were based on counting with fingers and toes. They had specific numbers from one to ten, yet for the numbers between eleven and nineteen they used "foot one" (11) to "foot nine" (19). The number 20 was the 'perfect' number for the Muisca which is visible in their [calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_calendar). To calculate higher numbers than 20 they used multiples of their 'perfect' number; *gue-muyhica* would be "20 times 4", so 80. To describe "50" they used "20 times 2 plus 10"; *gue-bosa asaqui ubchihica*, transcribed from *guêboʒhas aſaqɣ hubchìhicâ*.[^o2] In their [calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_calendar), which was [lunisolar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunisolar), they only counted from one to ten and twenty. Each number had a special meaning, related to their [deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_religion) and certain animals, especially the abundant [toads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toad).[^o3]

:::

For the representation of their numbers they used digits inspired by their natural surroundings, especially [toads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toad); *ata* ("one") and *aca* ("nine") were both derived from the animals so abundant on the [Bogotá savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogotá_savanna) and other parts of the [Altiplano Cundiboyacense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altiplano_Cundiboyacense) where the Muisca lived in their [confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_Confederation).

The most important scholars who provided knowledge about the Muisca numerals were [Bernardo de Lugo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernardo_de_Lugo) (1619),[^o2] [Pedro Simón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_Simón) (17th century), [Alexander von Humboldt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_von_Humboldt) and [José Domingo Duquesne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/José_Domingo_Duquesne) (late 18th and 19th century) and [Liborio Zerda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liborio_Zerda).[^o4][^o5][^o6][^o7][^o8]

## Numerals {#Numerals}

The Muisca used a vigesimal counting system and counted primarily with their fingers and secondarily with their toes. Their system went from 1 to 10 and for higher numerations they used the prefix *quihicha* or *qhicha*, which means "foot" in their [Chibcha language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chibcha_language) *Muysccubun*. Eleven became thus "foot one", twelve: "foot two", etc. As in the other [pre-Columbian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_era) civilizations, the number 20 was special. It was the total number of all body extremities; fingers and toes. The Muisca used two forms to express twenty: "foot ten"; *quihícha ubchihica* or their exclusive word *gueta*, derived from *gue*, which means "house". Numbers between 20 and 30 were counted *gueta asaqui ata* ("twenty plus one"; 21), *gueta asaqui ubchihica* ("twenty plus ten"; 30). Larger numbers were counted as multiples of twenty; *gue-bosa* ("20 times 2"; 40), *gue-hisca* ("20 times 5"; 100).[^o4]

*Image: Muisca cyphers acc acosta humboldt zerda.svg.* **Muisca numerals** as depicted by [Acosta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joaquín_Acosta), [Von Humboldt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_von_Humboldt) and [Zerda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liborio_Zerda)[^o10]

The numeral symbols were first provided by Duquesne and reproduced by Humboldt,[^o4] Acosta, and Zerda. These glyphs have been criticized and their authenticity questioned, as they are “practically nonexistent” in the surviving archaeological record, including the calendar stone from Choachí. Potentially, they might represent asterisms or months instead of numerals.[^o11]
