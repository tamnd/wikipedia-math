# Fundamental groupoid

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fundamental groupoid"}

In [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), the **fundamental [groupoid](wikipedia://en/12543)** is a certain [topological invariant](wikipedia://en/1337282) of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450). It can be viewed as an extension of the more widely-known [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004); as such, it captures information about the [homotopy type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_type) of a topological space. In terms of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), the fundamental groupoid is a certain [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from the category of topological spaces to the category of [groupoids](wikipedia://en/12543).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $X$ be a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450). Consider the equivalence relation on [continuous paths](wikipedia://en/1189553) in $X$ in which two continuous paths are equivalent if they are [homotopic](wikipedia://en/1189553#Homotopy_of_paths) with fixed endpoints. The fundamental groupoid $\Pi(X)$, or $\Pi_{1}(X)$, assigns to each ordered pair of points $(p, q)$ in $X$ the collection of equivalence classes of continuous paths from $p$ to $q$. More generally, the fundamental groupoid of $X$ on a set $S$ restricts the fundamental groupoid to the points which lie in both $X$ and $S$. This allows for a generalisation of the [Van Kampen theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seifert-Van_Kampen_theorem) using two base points to compute the fundamental group of the circle.[^o4]

As suggested by its name, the fundamental groupoid of $X$ naturally has the structure of a [groupoid](wikipedia://en/12543). In particular, it forms a category; the objects are taken to be the points of $X$ and the collection of morphisms from $p$ to $q$ is the collection of equivalence classes given above. The fact that this satisfies the definition of a category amounts to the [standard fact](wikipedia://en/1189553#Path_composition) that the equivalence class of the concatenation of two paths only depends on the equivalence classes of the individual paths.[^o5] Likewise, the fact that this category is a groupoid, which asserts that every morphism is invertible, amounts to the standard fact that one can reverse the orientation of a path, and the equivalence class of the resulting concatenation contains the constant path.[^o6]

Note that the fundamental groupoid assigns, to the ordered pair $(p, p)$, the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of $X$ based at $p$.

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Basic properties"}

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

Given a topological space $X$, the [path-connected components](wikipedia://en/6233#Path_connectedness) of $X$ are naturally encoded in its fundamental groupoid; the observation is that $p$ and $q$ are in the same path-connected component of $X$ if and only if the collection of equivalence classes of continuous paths from $p$ to $q$ is nonempty. In categorical terms, the assertion is that the objects $p$ and $q$ are in the same groupoid component if and only if the set of morphisms from $p$ to $q$ is nonempty.[^o9]

Suppose that $X$ is path-connected, and fix an element $p$ of $X$. One can view the fundamental group $\pi_{1}(X, p)$ as a category; there is one object and the morphisms from it to itself are the elements of $\pi_{1}(X, p)$. The selection, for each $q$ in $M$, of a continuous path from $p$ to $q$, allows one to use concatenation to view any path in $X$ as a loop based at $p$. This defines an [equivalence of categories](wikipedia://en/379051) between $\pi_{1}(X, p)$ and the fundamental groupoid of $X$. More precisely, this exhibits $\pi_{1}(X, p)$ as a [skeleton](wikipedia://en/1812151) of the fundamental groupoid of $X$.[^o10]

The fundamental groupoid of a (path-connected) [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) $X$ is actually a [Lie groupoid](wikipedia://en/310923), arising as the gauge groupoid of the [universal cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Cover) of $X$.[^o11]

:::

## Bundles of groups and local systems {#Bundles_of_groups_and_local_systems}

Given a topological space $X$, a *[local system](wikipedia://en/3461103)* is a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from the fundamental groupoid of $X$ to a category.[^o13] As an important special case, a *bundle of (abelian) groups* on $X$ is a local system valued in the category of (abelian) groups. This is to say that a bundle of groups on $X$ assigns a group $G_{p}$ to each element $p$ of $X$, and assigns a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) $G_{p} \to G_{q}$ to each continuous path from $p$ to $q$. In order to be a functor, these group homomorphisms are required to be compatible with the topological structure, so that homotopic paths with fixed endpoints define the same homomorphism; furthermore the group homomorphisms must compose in accordance with the concatenation and inversion of paths.[^o14] One can define [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) with coefficients in a bundle of abelian groups.[^o15]

When $X$ satisfies certain conditions, a local system can be equivalently described as a [locally constant sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_constant_sheaf).

::: {#o17 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The fundamental groupoid of the [singleton](wikipedia://en/381798) space is the trivial groupoid (a groupoid with one object \* and one morphism $\operatorname{Hom}(*, *) = \{ \operatorname{id}_{*} : * \to *$}
- The fundamental groupoid of the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) is connected and all of its [vertex groups](wikipedia://en/12543#Vertex_groups_and_orbits) are isomorphic to $(\mathbb{Z},+)$, the [additive group](wikipedia://en/332229) of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563).

:::

::: {#o19 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="The homotopy hypothesis"}

## The homotopy hypothesis {#The_homotopy_hypothesis}

The [homotopy hypothesis](wikipedia://en/40965653), a well-known [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) in [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/212250) formulated by [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042), states that a suitable [generalization](wikipedia://en/12746) of the fundamental groupoid, known as the fundamental [∞-groupoid](wikipedia://en/40965675), captures *all* information about a topological space [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [weak homotopy equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_homotopy_equivalence).

:::
