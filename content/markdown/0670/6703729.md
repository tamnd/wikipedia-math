# Ramanujan's congruences

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ramanujan's congruences"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Ramanujan's congruences** are the congruences for the [partition function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_%28number_theory%29) *p*(*n*) discovered by [Srinivasa Ramanujan](wikipedia://en/47717):

:::

$$\begin{aligned} p(5k+4) & \equiv 0 \pmod 5, \\ p(7k+5) & \equiv 0 \pmod 7, \\ p(11k+6) & \equiv 0 \pmod {11}. \end{aligned}$$

In plain words, the first congruence means that if a number is 4 more than a multiple of 5, *i.e.,* it is in the sequence

4, 9, 14, 19, 24, 29, . . .

then the number of its partitions is a multiple of 5.

Later, other congruences of this type were discovered, for numbers and for [Tau-functions](wikipedia://en/1231708).

## Background {#Background}

In his 1919 paper,[^o3] he proved the first two congruences using the following identities (using [q-Pochhammer symbol](wikipedia://en/11149717) notation):

$$\begin{aligned} & \sum_{k=0}^\infty p(5k+4)q^k=5\frac{(q^5)_\infty^5}{(q)_\infty^6}, \\[4pt] & \sum_{k=0}^\infty p(7k+5)q^k=7\frac{(q^7)_\infty^3}{(q)_\infty^4}+49q\frac{(q^7)_\infty^7}{(q)_\infty^8}. \end{aligned}$$

He then stated that "It appears there are no equally simple properties for any moduli involving primes other than these".

After Ramanujan died in 1920, [G. H. Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599) extracted proofs of all three congruences from an unpublished manuscript of Ramanujan on *p*(*n*) (Ramanujan, 1921). The proof in this manuscript employs the [Eisenstein series](wikipedia://en/1270458).

In 1944, [Freeman Dyson](wikipedia://en/11397) defined the [rank function for a partition](wikipedia://en/37733253) and conjectured the existence of a ["crank" function for partitions](wikipedia://en/37749896) that would provide a [combinatorial proof](wikipedia://en/741875) of Ramanujan's congruences modulo 11. Forty years later, [George Andrews](wikipedia://en/3356273) and [Frank Garvan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Garvan) found such a function, and proved the celebrated result that the crank simultaneously "explains" the three Ramanujan congruences modulo 5, 7 and 11.

In the 1960s, [A. O. L. Atkin](wikipedia://en/3721430) of the [University of Illinois at Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Chicago) discovered additional congruences for small prime moduli. For example:

$$p(11^3 \cdot 13k + 237)\equiv 0 \pmod {13}.$$

Extending the results of A. Atkin, [Ken Ono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Ono) in 2000 proved that there are such Ramanujan congruences modulo every integer coprime to 6. For example, his results give

$$p(107^4\cdot 31k + 30064597)\equiv 0\pmod{31}.$$

Later [Ken Ono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Ono) conjectured that the elusive crank also satisfies exactly the same types of general congruences. This was proved by his Ph.D. student Karl Mahlburg in his 2005 paper *Partition Congruences and the Andrews–Garvan–Dyson Crank*, linked below. This paper won the first [Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proceedings_of_the_National_Academy_of_Sciences) Paper of the Year prize.[^o4]

A conceptual explanation for Ramanujan's observation was finally discovered in January 2011[^o5] by considering the [Hausdorff dimension](wikipedia://en/14294) of the following $P$ function in the [l-adic](wikipedia://en/51423) topology:

$$P_\ell(b;z) := \sum_{n=0}^\infty p\left(\frac{\ell^bn+1}{24}\right)q^{n/24}.$$

It is seen to have dimension 0 only in the cases where *ℓ* = 5, 7 or 11 and since the partition function can be written as a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of these functions[^o6] this can be considered a formalization and proof of Ramanujan's observation.

In 2001, R.L. Weaver gave an effective algorithm for finding congruences of the partition function, and tabulated 76,065 congruences.[^o7] This was extended in 2012 by F. Johansson to 22,474,608,014 congruences,[^o8] one large example being

$$p(999959^4\cdot29k+ 28995221336976431135321047) \equiv 0 \pmod{29}.$$
