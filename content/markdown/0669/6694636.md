# Carl Benjamin Boyer

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Carl Benjamin Boyer"}

**Carl Benjamin Boyer** (November 3, 1906 – April 26, 1976) was an American historian of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831),[^o3] dubbed the "[Gibbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Gibbon) of [math history](wikipedia://en/14220)" by novelist [David Foster Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Foster_Wallace).[^o4] He was one of few historians of mathematics of his time to "keep open links with contemporary history of science."[^o5]

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Boyer was born in [Hellertown, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellertown,_Pennsylvania), on November 3, 1906, and graduated as [valedictorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valedictorian) of his [high school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_school) class. He received a bachelor's degree from [Columbia College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_College_of_Columbia_University) in 1928 and started working as a tutor at [Brooklyn College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn_College) in the same year. From [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University), he earned his master's degree in 1929 and his doctorate in history in 1939.[^o3] He was a full professor of mathematics at Brooklyn College from 1952 until his death.[^o7]

## Career {#Career}

Along with [Carolyn Eisele](wikipedia://en/43466157) of CUNY's [Hunter College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter_College); [C. Doris Hellman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._Doris_Hellman) of the [Pratt Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_Institute), and later [City University of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_University_of_New_York)'s [Queens College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queens_College); and [Lynn Thorndike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynn_Thorndike) of Columbia University, Boyer was instrumental in the 1953 founding of the Metropolitan New York Section of the [History of Science Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Science_Society).[^o9]

In 1954, Boyer was the recipient of a [Guggenheim Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guggenheim_Fellowship) to further his work in the history of science, in particular, the history of the study of rainbows.[^o10]

Boyer wrote the books *The History of the Calculus and Its Conceptual Development* (1959),[^o11] which was originally published as *The Concepts of the Calculus* (1939).[^o12] [Richard Courant](wikipedia://en/715238) wrote the foreword to the second edition (1949).[^o13] This was his first book. In it, Boyer examined the [history of calculus](wikipedia://en/746117) from the time of the [ancient Greeks](wikipedia://en/1503369) to the nineteenth century, when calculus was put in a [rigorous](wikipedia://en/655974#Mathematics) foundation thanks to the contributions of [Augustin-Louis Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842), [Karl Weierstrass](wikipedia://en/93459), and others.[^o13][^o14]

Boyer then accomplished a similar feat with *History of [Analytic Geometry](wikipedia://en/2202)* (1956),[^o15] presenting the developments from antiquity in [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia) and [Egypt](wikipedia://en/1180105), to the independent creation of the subject proper by [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966) and [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525), and to the third quarter of the nineteenth century with the contributions of [August Ferdinand Möbius](wikipedia://en/37875), [Julius Plücker](wikipedia://en/52146), and [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034).[^o13][^o16] Boyer gave 76 key [primary sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_source) in roughly chronological order at the end of the book.[^o17] He followed this with *The Rainbow: From Myth to Mathematics* (1959).[^o18]

Boyer published *A History of Mathematics* in 1968.[^o19] This book broke new grounds by discussing mathematics developed during the early twentieth century.[^o13] After he died in 1976, [Uta Merzbach](wikipedia://en/56688455), a historian of mathematics at the [Smithsonian Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smithsonian_Institution), took responsibility for revising and updating the text. The second edition appeared in 1991, with a foreword by [Isaac Asimov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Asimov).[^o19] She released the third edition in 2011.[^o20][^o21] Since its first edition, it has become a standard text on the subject.[^o13] Reviewers praised this book for its broad and accessible coverage of interesting developments from antiquity to the modern era.[^o19][^o21] However, the third edition contains no exercises at the end of each chapter.[^o21] Jason Graham noted that readers interested in more technical details could supplement the book with *A History of Mathematics* (2009) by [Victor Katz](wikipedia://en/54447016) or *The Fontana History of the Mathematical Sciences* (1997) by [Ivor Grattan-Guinness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivor_Grattan-Guinness).[^o21]

In 1965, Boyer was appointed associate editor of the *[Dictionary of Scientific Biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_Scientific_Biography)*, which was being planned by the [American Council of Learned Societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Council_of_Learned_Societies).[^o3] He also contributed several articles to the *[Encyclopedia Britannica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopædia_Britannica)*[^o13] and served as book-review editor of *[Scripta Mathematica](wikipedia://en/13550249)*, published by [Yeshiva University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeshiva_University).[^o22]

## Personal life and death {#Personal_life_and_death}

He was married to Marjorie Boyer (née Nice), a professor of history. Boyer died of a [heart attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myocardial_infarction) at his home [Brooklyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn), New York, on April 26, 1976. He was 69 years old.[^o3]

## Legacy {#Legacy}

In 1978, Boyer's widow established the Carl B. Boyer Memorial Prize, to be awarded annually to an undergraduate at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University) who was not an American citizen for the best essay on a scientific or mathematical topic.[^o25]

According to [Morris Kline](wikipedia://en/826363), Boyer played an instrumental role in establishing the history of mathematics as a worthwhile branch of study at a time when it was largely ignored in mainstream academic circles.[^o13]
