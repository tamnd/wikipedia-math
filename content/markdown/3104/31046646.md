# Non-Archimedean ordered field

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Non-Archimedean ordered field"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **non-Archimedean ordered field** is an [ordered field](wikipedia://en/22430) that does not satisfy the [Archimedean property](wikipedia://en/264158). Such fields will contain infinitesimal and infinitely large elements, suitably defined.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Suppose $F$ is an [ordered field](wikipedia://en/22430). We say that $F$ satisfies the [Archimedean property](wikipedia://en/264158) if, for every two positive elements $x$ and $y$ of $F$, there exists a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $n$ such that $nx > y$. Here, $n$ denotes the field element resulting from forming the sum of $n$ copies of the field element $1$, so that $nx$ is the sum of $n$ copies of $x$.

An ordered field that does not satisfy the Archimedean property is a non-Archimedean ordered field.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The fields of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) and [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), with their usual orderings, satisfy the Archimedean property.

Examples of non-Archimedean ordered fields are the [Levi-Civita field](wikipedia://en/22105661), the [hyperreal numbers](wikipedia://en/51429), the [surreal numbers](wikipedia://en/51432), the [Dehn field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dehn_planes), and the field of [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) with real coefficients (where we define $f > g$ to mean that $f(t)>g(t)$ for large enough *t*).

:::

## Infinite and infinitesimal elements {#Infinite_and_infinitesimal_elements}

In a non-Archimedean ordered field, we can find two positive elements $x$ and $y$ such that, for every natural number $n$, $nx \le y$. This means that the positive element $y/x$ is greater than every natural number $n$ (so it is an "infinite element"), and the positive element $x/y$ is smaller than $1/n$ for every natural number $n$ (so it is an "infinitesimal element").

Conversely, if an ordered field contains an infinite or an infinitesimal element in this sense, then it is a non-Archimedean ordered field.

::: {#o8 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

[Hyperreal fields](wikipedia://en/51429), non-Archimedean ordered fields containing the real numbers as a subfield, are used to provide a mathematical foundation for [nonstandard analysis](wikipedia://en/21708).

[Max Dehn](wikipedia://en/570602) used the [Dehn field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dehn_planes), an example of a non-Archimedean ordered field, to construct [non-Euclidean geometries](wikipedia://en/58610) in which the [parallel postulate](wikipedia://en/33731493) fails to be true but nevertheless triangles have angles summing to [$\pi$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi_%28mathematics%29).[^o9]

The field of rational functions over $\mathbb{R}$ can be used to construct an ordered field that is [Cauchy complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_complete) (in the sense of convergence of Cauchy sequences) but is not the real numbers.[^o10] This completion can be described as the field of [formal Laurent series](wikipedia://en/60012#Formal_Laurent_series) over $\mathbb{R}$. It is a non-Archimedean ordered field. Sometimes the term "complete" is used to mean that the [least upper bound property](wikipedia://en/22038671) holds, i.e. for [Dedekind-completeness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedekind-complete). There are no Dedekind-complete non-Archimedean ordered fields. The subtle distinction between these two uses of the word complete is occasionally a source of confusion.

:::
