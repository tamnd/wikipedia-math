# Quantum Turing machine

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quantum Turing machine"}

A **quantum Turing machine** (**QTM**) or **universal quantum computer** is an [abstract machine](wikipedia://en/60492) used to [model](wikipedia://en/1773278) the effects of a [quantum computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_computer). It provides a simple model that captures all of the power of quantum computation—that is, any [quantum algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_algorithm) can be expressed formally as a particular quantum Turing machine. However, the computationally equivalent [quantum circuit](wikipedia://en/707404) is a more common model.[^o2][^o3]

:::

Quantum Turing machines can be related to classical and [probabilistic Turing machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probabilistic_Turing_machines) in a framework based on [transition matrices](wikipedia://en/217313). That is, a matrix can be specified whose [product](wikipedia://en/125280) with the matrix representing a classical or probabilistic machine provides the quantum [probability matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_matrix) representing the quantum machine. This was shown by [Lance Fortnow](wikipedia://en/25303350).[^o4]

## Informal sketch {#Informal_sketch}

::: {#o6 .problem cue=unsolved}

Is a [universal quantum computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_quantum_computer) sufficient to [efficiently](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_efficiency) [simulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_simulation) an arbitrary physical system?

:::

A way of understanding the quantum Turing machine (QTM) is that it generalizes the classical [Turing machine](wikipedia://en/30403) (TM) in the same way that the [quantum finite automaton](wikipedia://en/7926008) (QFA) generalizes the [deterministic finite automaton](wikipedia://en/653404) (DFA). In essence, the internal states of a classical TM are replaced by [pure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_state) or [mixed states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_quantum_state) in a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932); the transition function is replaced by a collection of [unitary matrices](wikipedia://en/129222) that map the Hilbert space to itself.[^o10]

That is, a classical Turing machine is described by a 7-[tuple](wikipedia://en/132729) $M = \langle Q, \Gamma, b, \Sigma, \delta, q_0, F \rangle$. See [the formal definition of a Turing Machine](wikipedia://en/30403#Formal_definition) for a more in-depth understanding of each of the elements in this tuple.

For a three-tape quantum Turing machine (one tape holding the input, a second tape holding intermediate calculation results, and a third tape holding output):

- The set of states $Q$ is replaced by a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932).
- The tape alphabet symbols $\Gamma$ are likewise replaced by a Hilbert space (usually a different Hilbert space than the set of states).
- The blank symbol $b \in \Gamma$ is an element of the Hilbert space.
- The input and output symbols $\Sigma$ are usually taken as a discrete set, as in the classical system; thus, neither the input nor output to a quantum machine need be a quantum system itself.
- The [transition function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_transition_system) $\delta : \Sigma \times Q \otimes \Gamma \to \Sigma \times Q \otimes \Gamma \times \{L, R\}$ is a generalization of a [semiautomaton](wikipedia://en/10766937) and is understood to be a collection of [unitary matrices](wikipedia://en/129222) that are [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of the Hilbert space $Q$.
- The initial state $q_0 \in Q$ may be either a [mixed state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_quantum_state) or a [pure state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_state).
- The set $F$ of *final* or *accepting states* is a [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of the Hilbert space $Q$.

The above is merely a sketch of a quantum Turing machine, rather than its formal definition, as it leaves vague several important details: for example, how often a [measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_measurement) is performed; see for example, the difference between a measure-once and a measure-many QFA. This question of measurement affects the way in which writes to the output tape are defined.

## History {#History}

In 1980 and 1982, physicist [Paul Benioff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Benioff) published articles[^o8][^o9] that first described a quantum-mechanical model of [Turing machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_machines). A 1985 article written by Oxford University physicist [David Deutsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Deutsch) further developed the idea of quantum computers by suggesting that [quantum gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_gate) could function in a similar fashion to traditional digital computing [binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_numeral_system) [logic gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_gates).[^o10]

Iriyama, [Ohya](wikipedia://en/28770303), and Volovich have developed a model of a *linear quantum Turing machine* (LQTM). This is a generalization of a classical QTM that has mixed states and that allows irreversible transition functions. These allow the representation of quantum measurements without classical outcomes.[^o11]

A *quantum Turing machine with [postselection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postselection)* was defined by [Scott Aaronson](wikipedia://en/20420243), who showed that the class of polynomial time on such a machine ([PostBQP](wikipedia://en/4787255)) is equal to the classical complexity class [PP](wikipedia://en/659322).[^o12]
