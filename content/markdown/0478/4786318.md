# Linear dynamical system

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Linear dynamical system"}

**Linear dynamical systems** are [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems) whose [evolution functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_function) are [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear).[^o2] While dynamical systems, in general, do not have [closed-form solutions](wikipedia://en/585143), linear dynamical systems can be solved exactly, and they have a rich set of mathematical properties. Linear systems can also be used to understand the qualitative behavior of general dynamical systems, by calculating the [equilibrium points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_points) of the system and approximating it as a linear system around each such point.

:::

## Introduction {#Introduction}

In a linear dynamical system, the variation of a state vector (an $N$-dimensional [vector](wikipedia://en/32370) denoted $\mathbf{x}$) equals a constant matrix (denoted $\mathbf{A}$) multiplied by $\mathbf{x}$. This variation can take two forms: either as a [flow](wikipedia://en/2619735), in which $\mathbf{x}$ varies continuously with time

$$\frac{d}{dt} \mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{x}(t)$$

or as a mapping, in which $\mathbf{x}$ varies in [discrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_time) steps

$$\mathbf{x}_{m+1} = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{x}_{m}$$

These equations are linear in the following sense: if $\mathbf{x}(t)$ and $\mathbf{y}(t)$ are two valid solutions, then so is any [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of the two solutions, e.g., $\mathbf{z}(t) \ \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\ \alpha \mathbf{x}(t) + \beta \mathbf{y}(t)$ where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are any two [scalars](wikipedia://en/3588331). The matrix $\mathbf{A}$ need not be [symmetric](wikipedia://en/2714149#Symmetry_in_linear_algebra).

Linear dynamical systems can be solved exactly, in contrast to most nonlinear ones. Occasionally, a nonlinear system can be solved exactly by a change of variables to a linear system. Moreover, the solutions of (almost) any nonlinear system can be well-approximated by an equivalent linear system near its [fixed points](wikipedia://en/449738). Hence, understanding linear systems and their solutions is a crucial first step to understanding the more complex nonlinear systems.

## Solution of linear dynamical systems {#Solution_of_linear_dynamical_systems}

If the initial vector $\mathbf{x}_{0} \ \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\ \mathbf{x}(t=0)$ is aligned with a [right eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_eigenvector) $\mathbf{r}_{k}$ of the [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $\mathbf{A}$, the dynamics are simple

$$\frac{d}{dt} \mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{r}_{k} = \lambda_{k} \mathbf{r}_{k}$$

where $\lambda_{k}$ is the corresponding [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue); the solution of this equation is

$$\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{r}_{k} e^{\lambda_{k} t}$$

as may be confirmed by substitution.

If $\mathbf{A}$ is [diagonalizable](wikipedia://en/246325), then any vector in an $N$-dimensional space can be represented by a linear combination of the right and [left eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_eigenvector) (denoted $\mathbf{l}_{k}$) of the matrix $\mathbf{A}$.

$$\mathbf{x}_{0} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \left( \mathbf{l}_{k} \cdot \mathbf{x}_{0} \right) \mathbf{r}_{k}$$

Therefore, the general solution for $\mathbf{x}(t)$ is a linear combination of the individual solutions for the right eigenvectors

$$\mathbf{x}(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left( \mathbf{l}_{k} \cdot \mathbf{x}_{0} \right) \mathbf{r}_{k} e^{\lambda_{k} t}$$

Similar considerations apply to the discrete mappings.

## Classification in two dimensions {#Classification_in_two_dimensions}

*Image: LinDynSysTraceDet.jpg.* Linear approximation of a nonlinear system: classification of 2D fixed point according to the trace and the determinant of the Jacobian matrix (the linearization of the system near an equilibrium point).

The roots of the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) det(**A** - λ**I**) are the eigenvalues of **A**. The sign and relation of these roots, $\lambda_n$, to each other may be used to determine the stability of the dynamical system

$$\frac{d}{dt} \mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{x}(t).$$

For a 2-dimensional system, the characteristic polynomial is of the form $\lambda^2-\tau\lambda+\Delta=0$ where $\tau$ is the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) and $\Delta$ is the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of **A**. Thus the two roots are in the form:

$$\lambda_1=\frac{\tau+\sqrt{\tau^2-4\Delta}}{2}$$

$$\lambda_2=\frac{\tau-\sqrt{\tau^2-4\Delta}}{2},$$

and $\Delta=\lambda_1\lambda_2$ and $\tau=\lambda_1+\lambda_2$. Thus if $\Delta<0$ then the eigenvalues are of opposite sign, and the fixed point is a saddle. If $\Delta>0$ then the eigenvalues are of the same sign. Therefore, if $\tau>0$ both are positive and the point is unstable, and if $\tau<0$ then both are negative and the point is stable. The [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607) will tell you if the point is nodal or spiral (i.e. if the eigenvalues are real or complex).
