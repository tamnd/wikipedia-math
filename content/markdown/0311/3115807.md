# Geometric genus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Geometric genus"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), the **geometric genus** is a basic [birational invariant](wikipedia://en/2605247) $p_{g}$ of [algebraic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_varieties) and [complex manifolds](wikipedia://en/509742).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The geometric genus can be defined for [non-singular](wikipedia://en/253260#Singularities) complex projective varieties and more generally for [complex manifolds](wikipedia://en/509742) of dimension $n$ as the [Hodge number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_number) $h^{n,0}$ (equal to $h^{0,n}$ by [Serre duality](wikipedia://en/359175)), that is, the dimension of the [canonical linear system](wikipedia://en/734893#General_case) plus one.

In other words, for a variety $V$ of [complex dimension](wikipedia://en/2783513) $n$ it is the number of linearly independent holomorphic $n$-[forms](wikipedia://en/221519) to be found on $V$.[^o4] This definition, as the dimension of

$$H^{0}(V,\Omega^{n})$$

then carries over to any base [field](wikipedia://en/10603), when $\Omega$ is taken to be the sheaf of [Kähler differentials](wikipedia://en/603916) and the power is the (top) [exterior power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_power), the [canonical line bundle](wikipedia://en/734893).

The geometric genus is the first invariant $p_{g} = P_{1}$ of a sequence of invariants $P_{n}$ called the [plurigenera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plurigenera).

:::

## Case of curves {#Case_of_curves}

In the case of complex varieties, (the complex loci of) non-singular curves are [Riemann surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_surfaces). The algebraic definition of genus agrees with the [topological notion](wikipedia://en/61891#Orientable_surface). On a nonsingular curve, the canonical line bundle has degree $2g - 2$.

The notion of genus features prominently in the statement of the [Riemann–Roch theorem](wikipedia://en/247261) (see also [Riemann–Roch theorem for algebraic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann–Roch_theorem_for_algebraic_curves)) and of the [Riemann–Hurwitz formula](wikipedia://en/497413). By the Riemann-Roch theorem, an irreducible plane curve of degree *d* has geometric genus

$$g=\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}{2}-s,$$

where *s* is the number of singularities when properly counted.

If $C$ is an irreducible (and smooth) hypersurface in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/31453337) cut out by a polynomial equation of degree $d$, then its normal line bundle is the [Serre twisting sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serre_twisting_sheaf) $\mathcal O$$(d)$, so by the [adjunction formula](wikipedia://en/3118117), the canonical line bundle of $C$ is given by

$$\mathcal K_C = \left[ \mathcal K_{\mathbb P^2} + \mathcal O(d) \right]_{\vert C} = \mathcal O(d-3)_{\vert C}$$

## Genus of singular varieties {#Genus_of_singular_varieties}

The definition of geometric genus is carried over classically to singular curves $C$, by decreeing that

$$p_{g}(C)$$

is the geometric genus of the [normalization](wikipedia://en/2629423#The_normalization) $C'$. That is, since the mapping

$$C' \to C$$

is [birational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birational), the definition is extended by birational invariance.
