# Bicentric quadrilateral

*Image: Bicentric quadrilateral poncelet.svg.* Poncelet's porism for bicentric quadrilaterals $ABCD$ and $EFGH$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bicentric quadrilateral"}

In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), a **bicentric quadrilateral** is a [convex](wikipedia://en/660019) [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278) that has both an [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle) and a [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432). The radii and centers of these circles are called *inradius* and *circumradius*, and *incenter* and *circumcenter* respectively. From the definition it follows that bicentric quadrilaterals have all the properties of both [tangential quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/9176412) and [cyclic quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/194131). Other names for these quadrilaterals are **chord-tangent quadrilateral**[^o2] and **inscribed and quadrilateral**. It has also rarely been called a *double circle quadrilateral*[^o36] and *double scribed quadrilateral*.[^o3]

:::

If two circles, one within the other, are the incircle and the circumcircle of a bicentric quadrilateral, then every point on the circumcircle is the vertex of a bicentric quadrilateral having the same incircle and circumcircle.[^o4] This is a special case of [Poncelet's porism](wikipedia://en/2412120), which was proved by the French mathematician [Jean-Victor Poncelet](wikipedia://en/764818) (1788–1867).

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Special cases"}

## Special cases {#Special_cases}

*Image: Bicentric kite 001.svg.* A [right kite](wikipedia://en/37263250)

Examples of bicentric quadrilaterals are [squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29), [right kites](wikipedia://en/37263250), and [isosceles tangential trapezoids](wikipedia://en/34853912#Isosceles_tangential_trapezoid).

## Characterizations {#Characterizations}

*Image: Bicentric quadrilateral.svg.* A bicentric quadrilateral ABCD and its contact quadrilateral WXYZ

A convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ with sides $a, b, c, d$ is bicentric [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) opposite sides satisfy [Pitot's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitot's_theorem) for tangential quadrilaterals *and* the cyclic quadrilateral property that opposite angles are [supplementary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supplementary_angles); that is,

$$\begin{cases} a+c=b+d\\ A+C=B+D=\pi. \end{cases}$$

Three other characterizations concern the points where the [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle) in a [tangential quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/9176412) is tangent to the sides. If the incircle is tangent to the sides $AB, BC, CD, DA$ at $W, X, Y, Z$ respectively, then a tangential quadrilateral $ABCD$ is also cyclic if and only if any one of the following three conditions holds:[^o9]

- $WY$ is [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to $XZ$
- $$\frac{\overline{AW}}{\overline{WB}}=\frac{\overline{DY}}{\overline{YC}}$$
- $$\frac{\overline{AC}}{\overline{BD}}=\frac{\overline{AW}+\overline{CY}}{\overline{BX}+\overline{DZ}}$$

The first of these three means that the *contact quadrilateral* $WXYZ$ is an [orthodiagonal quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/30425383).

If $E, F, G, H$ are the midpoints of $WX, XY, YZ, ZW$ respectively, then the tangential quadrilateral $ABCD$ is also cyclic [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the quadrilateral $EFGH$ is a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048).[^o9]

According to another characterization, if $I$ is the [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) in a [tangential quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/9176412) where the extensions of opposite sides intersect at $J$ and $K$, then the quadrilateral is also cyclic if and only if $\angle JIK$ is a [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956).[^o9]

Yet another [necessary and sufficient condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_condition) is that a tangential quadrilateral $ABCD$ is cyclic if and only if its [Newton line](wikipedia://en/35411198) is perpendicular to the Newton line of its contact quadrilateral $WXYZ$. (The Newton line of a quadrilateral is the line defined by the midpoints of its diagonals.)[^o9]

## Construction {#Construction}

*Image: 01-Bicentric quadrilateral.svg.* A bicentric quadrilateral $ABCD$ with the contact quadrilateral $WXYZ$. Animation [see here](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:01-Bicentric_quadrilateral.gif)

There is a simple method for constructing a bicentric quadrilateral:

It starts with the incircle $C_{r}$ around the [centre](wikipedia://en/4850775) $I$ with the radius $r$ and then draw two to each other [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) [chords](wikipedia://en/297947) $\overline{WY}$ and $\overline{XZ}$ in the incircle $C_{r}$. At the endpoints of the chords draw the [tangents](wikipedia://en/31482) $a, b, c, d$ to the incircle. These intersect at four points $A, B, C, D$, which are the [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) of a bicentric quadrilateral.[^o12] To draw the circumcircle, draw two [perpendicular bisectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular_bisector) $p_{1}, p_{2}$ on the sides of the bicentric quadrilateral $a$ respectively $b$. The perpendicular bisectors $p_{1}, p_{2}$ intersect in the centre $O$ of the circumcircle $C_{R}$ with the distance $x$ to the centre $I$ of the incircle $C_{r}$. The circumcircle can be drawn around the centre $O$.

The validity of this construction is due to the characterization that, in a [tangential quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/9176412) $ABCD$, the contact quadrilateral $WXYZ$ has perpendicular [diagonals](wikipedia://en/349251) if and only if the tangential quadrilateral is also [cyclic](wikipedia://en/194131).

:::

## Area {#Area}

::: {#o15 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formulas in terms of four quantities"}

### Formulas in terms of four quantities {#Formulas_in_terms_of_four_quantities}

The [area](wikipedia://en/1209) $K$ of a bicentric quadrilateral can be expressed in terms of four quantities of the quadrilateral in several different ways. If the sides are $a, b, c, d$, then the area is given by[^o30][^o17][^o18][^o28][^o41]

$$\displaystyle K = \sqrt{abcd}.$$

This is a special case of [Brahmagupta's formula](wikipedia://en/194129). It can also be derived directly from the trigonometric formula for the area of a [tangential quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/9176412#Area). Note that the converse does not hold: Some quadrilaterals that are not bicentric also have area $\displaystyle K = \sqrt{abcd}.$[^o16] One example of such a quadrilateral is a non-square [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048).

The area can also be expressed in terms of the [tangent lengths](wikipedia://en/9176412#Contact_points_and_tangent_lengths) $e, f, g, h$ as[^o17]

$$K=\sqrt[4]{efgh}(e+f+g+h).$$

A formula for the area of bicentric quadrilateral $ABCD$ with incenter $I$ is[^o18]

$$K=\overline{AI} \cdot \overline{CI} + \overline{BI} \cdot \overline{DI}.$$

If a bicentric quadrilateral has [tangency chords](wikipedia://en/9176412#Special_line_segments) $k, l$ and diagonals $p, q$, then it has area[^o17]

$$K=\frac{klpq}{k^2+l^2}.$$

If $k, l$ are the tangency chords and $m, n$ are the [bimedians](wikipedia://en/25278#Special_line_segments) of the quadrilateral, then the area can be calculated using the formula[^o18]

$$K=\left|\frac{m^2-n^2}{k^2-l^2}\right|kl$$

This formula cannot be used if the quadrilateral is a [right kite](wikipedia://en/37263250), since the denominator is zero in that case.

If $M, N$ are the midpoints of the diagonals, and $E, F$ are the intersection points of the extensions of opposite sides, then the area of a bicentric quadrilateral is given by

$$K=\frac{2\overline{MN} \cdot \overline{EI} \cdot \overline{FI}}{\overline{EF}}$$

where $I$ is the center of the incircle.[^o18]

:::

::: {#o20 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formulas in terms of three quantities"}

### Formulas in terms of three quantities {#Formulas_in_terms_of_three_quantities}

The area of a bicentric quadrilateral can be expressed in terms of two opposite sides and the angle $\theta$ between the diagonals according to[^o18]

$$K=ac\tan{\frac{\theta}{2}}=bd\cot{\frac{\theta}{2}}.$$

In terms of two adjacent angles and the radius $r$ of the incircle, the area is given by[^o18]

$$K=2r^2\left(\frac{1}{\sin{A}}+\frac{1}{\sin{B}}\right).$$

The area is given in terms of the circumradius $R$ and the inradius $r$ as

$$K=r(r+\sqrt{4R^2+r^2})\sin \theta$$

where $\theta$ is either angle between the diagonals.[^o21]

If $M, N$ are the midpoints of the diagonals, and $E, F$ are the intersection points of the extensions of opposite sides, then the area can also be expressed as

$$K=2\overline{MN}\sqrt{\overline{EQ} \cdot \overline{FQ}}$$

where $Q$ is the foot of the perpendicular to the line $EF$ through the center of the incircle.[^o18]

:::

### Inequalities {#Inequalities}

If $r$ and $R$ are the inradius and the circumradius respectively, then the [area](wikipedia://en/1209) $K$ satisfies the [inequalities](wikipedia://en/89489)[^o23]

$$\displaystyle 4r^2 \le K \le 2R^2.$$

There is equality on either side only if the quadrilateral is a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29).

Another inequality for the area is[^o24]

$$K \le \tfrac{4}{3}r\sqrt{4R^2+r^2}$$

where $r$ and $R$ are the inradius and the circumradius respectively.

A similar inequality giving a sharper upper bound for the area than the previous one is[^o21]

$$K \le r(r+\sqrt{4R^2+r^2})$$

with equality holding if and only if the quadrilateral is a [right kite](wikipedia://en/37263250).

In addition, with sides $a, b, c, d$ and [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617) $s$:

$2\sqrt{K} \leq s \leq r+ \sqrt{r^2+4R^2};$[^o24]

$6K \leq ab+ac+ad+bc+bd+cd \leq 4r^2+4R^2+ 4r\sqrt{r^2+4R^2};$[^o24]

$4Kr^2\leq abcd \leq \frac{16}{9} r^2(r^2+4R^2).$[^o24]

::: {#o26 .statement cue=named-heading name="Angle formulas"}

## Angle formulas {#Angle_formulas}

If $a, b, c, d$ are the length of the sides $AB, BC, CD, DA$ respectively in a bicentric quadrilateral $ABCD$, then its vertex angles can be calculated with the [tangent function](wikipedia://en/30367):[^o18]

$$\begin{aligned} \tan{\frac{A}{2}} &= \sqrt{\frac{bc}{ad}} = \cot{\frac{C}{2}}, \\ \tan{\frac{B}{2}} &= \sqrt{\frac{cd}{ab}} = \cot{\frac{D}{2}}. \end{aligned}$$

Using the same notations, for the [sine and cosine functions](wikipedia://en/30367) the following formulas holds:[^o27]

$$\begin{aligned} \sin{\frac{A}{2}} &= \sqrt{\frac{bc}{ad+bc}} = \cos{\frac{C}{2}}, \\ \cos{\frac{A}{2}} &= \sqrt{\frac{ad}{ad+bc}} = \sin{\frac{C}{2}}, \\ \sin{\frac{B}{2}} &= \sqrt{\frac{cd}{ab+cd}} = \cos{\frac{D}{2}}, \\ \cos{\frac{B}{2}} &= \sqrt{\frac{ab}{ab+cd}} = \sin{\frac{D}{2}}. \end{aligned}$$

The angle $\theta$ between the diagonals can be calculated from[^o28]

$$\displaystyle \tan{\frac{\theta}{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{bd}{ac}}.$$

:::

## Inradius and circumradius {#Inradius_and_circumradius}

The [inradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inradius) $r$ of a bicentric quadrilateral is determined by the sides $a, b, c, d$ according to[^o30]

$$\displaystyle r=\frac{\sqrt{abcd}}{a+c}=\frac{\sqrt{abcd}}{b+d}.$$

The [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) $R$ is given as a special case of [Parameshvara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameshvara)'s formula. It is[^o30]

$$\displaystyle R=\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{(ab+cd)(ac+bd)(ad+bc)}{abcd}}.$$

The inradius can also be expressed in terms of the consecutive [tangent lengths](wikipedia://en/9176412#Special_line_segments) $e, f, g, h$ according to[^o31]

$$\displaystyle r=\sqrt{eg}=\sqrt{fh}.$$

These two formulas are in fact [necessary and sufficient conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_condition) for a [tangential quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/9176412) with inradius $r$ to be [cyclic](wikipedia://en/194131).

The four sides $a, b, c, d$ of a bicentric quadrilateral are the four solutions of the [quartic equation](wikipedia://en/308152)

$$y^4-2sy^3+(s^2+2r^2+2r\sqrt{4R^2+r^2})y^2-2rs(\sqrt{4R^2+r^2}+r)y+r^2s^2=0$$

where $s$ is the semiperimeter, and $r$ and $R$ are the inradius and circumradius respectively.[^o32]

If there is a bicentric quadrilateral with inradius $r$ whose [tangent lengths](wikipedia://en/9176412#Special_line_segments) are $e, f, g, h$, then there exists a bicentric quadrilateral with inradius $r^{v}$ whose tangent lengths are $e^v,f^v,g^v,h^v,$ where $v$ may be any [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438).[^o35]

A bicentric quadrilateral has a greater inradius than does any other tangential quadrilateral having the same sequence of side lengths.[^o33]

### Inequalities {#Inequalities}

The circumradius $R$ and the inradius $r$ satisfy the inequality

$$R\ge \sqrt{2}r$$

which was proved by L. Fejes Tóth in 1948.[^o35] It holds with equality only when the two circles are [concentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentric) (have the same center as each other); then the quadrilateral is a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29). The inequality can be proved in several different ways, one using the double inequality for the area above.

An extension of the previous inequality is[^o36][^o37]

$$\frac{r\sqrt{2}}{R}\le \frac{1}{2}\left(\sin{\frac{A}{2}}\cos{\frac{B}{2}}+\sin{\frac{B}{2}}\cos{\frac{C}{2}}+\sin{\frac{C}{2}}\cos{\frac{D}{2}}+\sin{\frac{D}{2}}\cos{\frac{A}{2}}\right)\le 1$$

where there is equality on either side if and only if the quadrilateral is a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29).[^o27]

The [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617) $s$ of a bicentric quadrilateral satisfies[^o35]

$$\sqrt{8r\left(\sqrt{4R^2+r^2}-r\right)}\le s \le \sqrt{4R^2+r^2}+r$$

where $r$ and $R$ are the inradius and circumradius respectively.

Moreover,[^o24]

$$2sr^2\leq abc+abd+acd+bcd \leq 2r(r+\sqrt{r^2+4R^2})^2$$

and

$abc+abd+acd+bcd \leq 2\sqrt{K}(K+2R^2).$ [^o24]

## Distance between the incenter and circumcenter {#Distance_between_the_incenter_and_circumcenter}

*Image: Fuss theorem2.svg.* A bicentric quadrilateral $ABCD$ with incenter I and circumcenter O

::: {#o40 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Fuss's theorem"}

### Fuss's theorem {#Fuss's_theorem}

Fuss's theorem gives a relation between the [inradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inradius) $r$, the [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) $R$ and the distance $x$ between the [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) $I$ and the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) $O$, for any bicentric quadrilateral. The relation is[^o2][^o41][^o42]

$$\frac{1}{(R-x)^2}+\frac{1}{(R+x)^2}=\frac{1}{r^2},$$

or equivalently

$$\displaystyle 2r^2(R^2+x^2)=(R^2-x^2)^2.$$

It was derived by [Nicolaus Fuss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Fuss) (1755–1826) in 1792. Solving for $x$ yields

$$x=\sqrt{R^2+r^2-r\sqrt{4R^2+r^2}}.$$

Fuss's theorem, which is the analog of [Euler's theorem for triangles](wikipedia://en/3338987) for bicentric quadrilaterals, says that if a quadrilateral is bicentric, then its two associated circles are related according to the above equations. In fact the converse also holds: given two circles (one within the other) with radii $R$ and $r$ and distance $x$ between their centers satisfying the condition in Fuss's theorem, there exists a convex quadrilateral inscribed in one of them and tangent to the other[^o43] (and then by [Poncelet's closure theorem](wikipedia://en/2412120), there exist infinitely many of them).

Applying $x^2 \ge 0$ to the expression of Fuss's theorem for $x$ in terms of $r$ and $R$ is another way to obtain the above-mentioned inequality $R \ge \sqrt{2}r.$ A generalization is[^o35]

$$2r^2+x^2\le R^2 \le 2r^2+x^2+2rx.$$

:::

::: {#o45 .statement cue=named-heading name="Carlitz's identity"}

### Carlitz's identity {#Carlitz's_identity}

Another formula for the distance $x$ between the centers of the [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle) and the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432) is due to the American mathematician [Leonard Carlitz](wikipedia://en/10356778) (1907–1999). It states that[^o46]

$$\displaystyle x^2=R^2-2Rr\cdot \mu$$

where $r$ and $R$ are the [inradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inradius) and the [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) respectively, and

$$\displaystyle \mu=\sqrt{\frac{(ab+cd)(ad+bc)}{(a+c)^2(ac+bd)}} = \sqrt{\frac{(ab+cd)(ad+bc)}{(b+d)^2(ac+bd)}}$$

where $a, b, c, d$ are the sides of the bicentric quadrilateral.

:::

### Inequalities for the tangent lengths and sides {#Inequalities_for_the_tangent_lengths_and_sides}

For the [tangent lengths](wikipedia://en/9176412#Special_line_segments) $e, f, g, h$ the following inequalities holds:[^o35]

$$4r\le e+f+g+h \le 4r\cdot \frac{R^2+x^2}{R^2-x^2}$$

and

$$4r^2\le e^2+f^2+g^2+h^2 \le 4(R^2+x^2-r^2)$$

where $r$ is the inradius, $R$ is the circumradius, and $x$ is the distance between the incenter and circumcenter. The sides $a, b, c, d$ satisfy the inequalities[^o35]

$$8r\le a+b+c+d \le 8r\cdot \frac{R^2+x^2}{R^2-x^2}$$

and

$$4(R^2-x^2+2r^2)\le a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2 \le 4(3R^2-2r^2).$$

## Other properties of the incenter {#Other_properties_of_the_incenter}

The [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter), the [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837), and the intersection of the [diagonals](wikipedia://en/349251) in a bicentric quadrilateral are [collinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear).[^o49]

There is the following equality relating the four distances between the incenter $I$ and the vertices of a bicentric quadrilateral $ABCD$:[^o50]

$$\frac{1}{\overline{AI}^2}+\frac{1}{\overline{CI}^2}=\frac{1}{\overline{BI}^2}+\frac{1}{\overline{DI}^2}=\frac{1}{r^2}$$

where $r$ is the inradius.

If $P$ is the intersection of the diagonals in a bicentric quadrilateral $ABCD$ with incenter $I$, then[^o51]

$$\frac{\overline{AP}}{\overline{CP}}=\frac{\overline{AI}^2}{\overline{CI}^2}.$$

## Properties of the diagonals {#Properties_of_the_diagonals}

The lengths of the diagonals in a bicentric quadrilateral can be expressed in terms of [the sides](wikipedia://en/194131#Diagonals) or [the tangent lengths](wikipedia://en/9176412#Diagonals_and_tangency_chords), which are formulas that holds in a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131) and a [tangential quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/9176412) respectively.

In a bicentric quadrilateral with [diagonals](wikipedia://en/349251) $p, q$, the following identity holds:[^o41]

$$\displaystyle \frac{pq}{4r^2}-\frac{4R^2}{pq}=1$$

where $r$ and $R$ are the [inradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inradius) and the [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) respectively. This equality can be rewritten as[^o21]

$$r=\frac{pq}{2\sqrt{pq+4R^2}}$$

or, solving it as a [quadratic equation](wikipedia://en/25175) for the product of the diagonals, in the form

$$pq=2r\left(r+\sqrt{4R^2+r^2}\right).$$

An inequality for the product of the diagonals $p, q$ in a bicentric quadrilateral is[^o23]

$$\displaystyle 8pq\le (a+b+c+d)^2$$

where $a, b, c, d$ are the sides. This was proved by [Murray S. Klamkin](wikipedia://en/8881224) in 1967.

## Four incenters lie on a circle {#Four_incenters_lie_on_a_circle}

Let $ABCD$ be a bicentric quadrilateral and $O$ the center of its circumcircle. Then the incenters of the four triangles $\triangle OAB, \triangle OBC, \triangle OCD, \triangle ODA$ lie on a circle.[^o54]
