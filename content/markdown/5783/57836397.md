# Jacob Eichenbaum

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Jacob Eichenbaum"}

**Jacob Moiseyovych Eichenbaum** (יעקב בן־משה אייכענבוים, Я́ків Мойсе́йович Ейхенба́ум; 12 October 1796 – 27 December 1861), born **Jacob Gelber**, was a [Galician Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galician_Jews) [maskil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haskalah), educator, poet and mathematician.

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Jacob Gelber was born in the [Galician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galicia_%28Eastern_Europe%29) city of [Krystynopil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chervonohrad), on 12 October 1796,[^n1] in the year following the [Third Partition of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Partition_of_Poland) by the [Habsburg monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburg_monarchy).

By the age of eleven, he was already in his first marriage, but it didn't last long until the divorce, as his father-in-law suspected that he was a [secularist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_secularism).[^n2] In 1815, when he was eighteen, he remarried, adopted the name "Eichenbaum" and settled in [Zamość](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zamość). There he encountered a progressive Jewish youth circle, and began studying [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language), [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language), [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), and (in particular) [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831).[^n1] In 1819, he translated [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s *[Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)* from German into Hebrew.

He worked as a travelling private tutor, teaching Hebrew subjects and mathematics in wealthy households throughout Ukraine.[^n1] In 1835, Eichenbaum opened a private school for Jewish children in [Odesa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odesa), which had become an important [educational centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_education) for [Ukrainian Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Ukraine).[^n1] In 1836, he published *Kol Zimrah*, one of the first books of [Modern Hebrew poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Hebrew_poetry) published in the [Haskalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haskalah) period.[^n1] In 1840, he published *Ha-Kerav,* a poetry book describing a variety of [chess moves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outline_of_chess#Moves) in verse.

Eichenbaum's educational and literary work attracted the attention of the [Russian government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Russia), which advanced his position in the Jewish education system of the Russian Empire.[^n3] In 1844, Eichenbaum was appointed as director of the [Bessarabian Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Bessarabia) school in [Chișinău](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chișinău), and in 1850, he was appointed as chief inspector of a [Yeshiva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeshiva) in [Zhytomyr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhytomyr), a position which he maintained until his death.[^n3]

During his final years, he continued to publish works of mathematics and poetry.[^n3] In 1857, he published a Hebrew arithmetic textbook, *Ḥokhmat ha-Shi'urim,* which he had adapted from a work in the [French language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language).[^n2] In 1861, he wrote an allegorical poem, *Ha-Kosem,* which he published in the Hebrew newspaper *[Ha-Melitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ha-Melitz)*.[^n3]

On 27 December 1861, Jacob Eichenbaum died in [Kiev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiev).[^n1]
