# Zero-divisor graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Zero-divisor graph"}

*Image: Zero-divisor graph of Z2xZ4.svg.* The zero-divisor graph of $\mathbb{Z}_2\times\mathbb{Z}_4$, the only possible zero-divisor graph that is a [tree](wikipedia://en/48560) but not a [star](wikipedia://en/19310129)

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and more specifically in [combinatorial commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/11503412), a **zero-divisor graph** is an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) representing the [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441) of a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346). It has elements of the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) as its [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899), and pairs of elements whose product is zero as its [edges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_%28graph_theory%29).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

There are two variations of the zero-divisor graph commonly used. In the original definition of (Beck 1988), the vertices represent all elements of the ring. In a later variant studied by (Anderson Livingston 1999), the vertices represent only the [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441) of the given ring.

:::

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

If $n$ is a [semiprime number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiprime_number) (the product of two [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666)) then the zero-divisor graph of the ring of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) $n$ (with only the zero divisors as its vertices) is either a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) or a [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320). It is a complete graph $K_{p-1}$ in the case that $n=p^2$ for some prime number $p$. In this case the vertices are all the nonzero multiples of $p$, and the product of any two of these numbers is zero modulo $p^2$.

It is a complete bipartite graph $K_{p-1,q-1}$ in the case that $n=pq$ for two distinct prime numbers $p$ and $q$. The two sides of the bipartition are the $p-1$ nonzero multiples of $q$ and the $q-1$ nonzero multiples of $p$, respectively. Two numbers (that are not themselves zero modulo $n$) multiply to zero modulo $n$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) one is a multiple of $p$ and the other is a multiple of $q$, so this graph has an edge between each pair of vertices on opposite sides of the bipartition, and no other edges. More generally, the zero-divisor graph is a complete bipartite graph for any ring that is a [product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_ring) of two [integral domains](wikipedia://en/15462).

The only [cycle graphs](wikipedia://en/878962) that can be realized as zero-product graphs (with zero divisors as vertices) are the cycles of length 3 or 4. The only [trees](wikipedia://en/48560) that may be realized as zero-divisor graphs are the [stars](wikipedia://en/19310129) (complete bipartite graphs that are trees) and the five-vertex tree formed as the zero-divisor graph of $\mathbb{Z}_2\times\mathbb{Z}_4$.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

In the version of the graph that includes all elements, 0 is a [universal vertex](wikipedia://en/51352780), and the zero divisors can be identified as the vertices that have a neighbor other than 0. Because it has a universal vertex, the graph of all ring elements is always [connected](wikipedia://en/1614492) and has [diameter](wikipedia://en/2979341) at most two. The graph of all zero divisors is non-empty for every ring that is not an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462). It remains connected, has diameter at most three, and (if it contains a [cycle](wikipedia://en/168609)) has [girth](wikipedia://en/12013) at most four.

The zero-divisor graph of a ring that is not an integral domain is finite if and only if the ring is [finite](wikipedia://en/23706953). More concretely, if the graph has maximum [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) $d$, the ring has at most $(d^2-2d+2)^2$ elements. If the ring and the graph are infinite, every edge has an endpoint with infinitely many neighbors.

(Beck 1988) [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) that (like the [perfect graphs](wikipedia://en/670531)) zero-divisor graphs always have equal [clique number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clique_number) and [chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number). However, this is not true; a [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) was discovered by (Anderson Naseer 1993).
