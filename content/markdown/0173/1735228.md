# Lenstra–Lenstra–Lovász lattice basis reduction algorithm

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lenstra–Lenstra–Lovász lattice basis reduction algorithm"}

The **Lenstra–Lenstra–Lovász** (**LLL**) **lattice basis reduction algorithm** is a [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time) [lattice reduction](wikipedia://en/4786593) [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) invented by [Arjen Lenstra](wikipedia://en/6850292), [Hendrik Lenstra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lenstra) and [László Lovász](wikipedia://en/1623938) in 1982.[^o2] Given a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) $\mathbf{B} = \{ \mathbf{b}_1,\mathbf{b}_2, \dots, \mathbf{b}_d \}$ with *n*-dimensional integer coordinates, for a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) L (a discrete subgroup of **R**<sup>*n*</sup>) with $d \leq n$, the LLL algorithm calculates an *LLL-reduced* (short, nearly [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal)) lattice basis in time

$$\mathcal O(d^5n\log^3 B)$$

where $B$ is the largest length of $\mathbf{b}_i$ under the [Euclidean norm](wikipedia://en/990534#Euclidean_norm), that is, $B = \max\left(\|\mathbf{b}_1\|_2, \|\mathbf{b}_2\|_2, \dots, \|\mathbf{b}_d\|_2\right)$.[^o3][^o4]

:::

The original applications were to give polynomial-time algorithms for [factorizing polynomials with rational coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_factorization#Factoring_univariate_polynomials_over_the_integers), for finding [simultaneous rational approximations to real numbers](wikipedia://en/2770230#Simultaneous_Version), and for solving the [integer linear programming problem](wikipedia://en/43730) in fixed dimensions.

## LLL reduction {#LLL_reduction}

The precise definition of LLL-reduced is as follows: Given a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420)

$$\mathbf{B}=\{ \mathbf{b}_1,\mathbf{b}_2, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n \},$$

define its [Gram–Schmidt process](wikipedia://en/82361) orthogonal basis

$$\mathbf{B}^*=\{ \mathbf{b}^*_1, \mathbf{b}^*_2, \dots, \mathbf{b}^*_n \},$$

and the Gram-Schmidt coefficients

$$\mu_{i,j}=\frac{\langle\mathbf{b}_i,\mathbf{b}^*_j\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{b}^*_j,\mathbf{b}^*_j\rangle},$$

for any $1 \le j < i \le n$.

Then the basis $B$ is LLL-reduced if there exists a parameter $\delta$ in 0.25, 1 such that the following holds:

1. (size-reduced) For $1 \leq j < i \leq n$, we have $\left|\mu_{i,j}\right|\leq 0.5$. By definition, this property guarantees the length reduction of the ordered basis.
1. (Lovász condition) For $2 \leq k \leq n$, we have $\delta \Vert \mathbf{b}^*_{k-1}\Vert^2 \leq \Vert \mathbf{b}^*_k\Vert^2+ \mu_{k,k-1}^2\Vert \mathbf{b}^*_{k-1}\Vert^2$.

Here, estimating the value of the $\delta$ parameter, we can conclude how well the basis is reduced. Greater values of $\delta$ lead to stronger reductions of the basis. Initially, A. Lenstra, H. Lenstra and L. Lovász demonstrated the LLL-reduction algorithm for $\delta = 3/4$. Note that although LLL-reduction is well-defined for $\delta = 1$, the polynomial-time complexity is guaranteed only for $\delta$ in $(0.25,1)$.

The LLL algorithm computes LLL-reduced bases. There is no known efficient algorithm to compute a basis in which the basis vectors are as short as possible for lattices of dimensions greater than 4.[^o6] However, an LLL-reduced basis is nearly as short as possible, in the sense that there are absolute bounds $c_i > 1$ such that the first basis vector is no more than $c_1$ times as long as a shortest vector in the lattice, the second basis vector is likewise within $c_2$ of the second successive minimum, and so on.

## Applications {#Applications}

An early successful application of the LLL algorithm was its use by [Andrew Odlyzko](wikipedia://en/1986042) and [Herman te Riele](wikipedia://en/1587570) in disproving the [Mertens conjecture](wikipedia://en/379847).[^o9]

The LLL algorithm has found numerous other applications in [MIMO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIMO) detection algorithms[^o10] and cryptanalysis of [public-key encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public-key_encryption) schemes: [knapsack cryptosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naccache-Stern_knapsack_cryptosystem), [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29) with particular settings, [NTRUEncrypt](wikipedia://en/481582), and so forth. The algorithm can be used to find integer solutions to many problems.[^o11]

In particular, the LLL algorithm forms a core of one of the [integer relation algorithms](wikipedia://en/13644054). For example, if it is believed that *r*=1.618034 is a (slightly rounded) [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) to an unknown [quadratic equation](wikipedia://en/25175) with integer coefficients, one may apply LLL reduction to the lattice in $\mathbf{R}^4$ spanned by $[1,0,0,10000r^2], [0,1,0,10000r],$ and $[0,0,1,10000]$. The first vector in the reduced basis will be an integer [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of these three, thus necessarily of the form $[a,b,c,10000(ar^2+br+c)]$; but such a vector is "short" only if *a*, *b*, *c* are small and $ar^2+br+c$ is even smaller. Thus the first three entries of this short vector are likely to be the coefficients of the integral quadratic [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) which has *r* as a root. In this example the LLL algorithm finds the shortest vector to be \[1, -1, -1, 0.00025\] and indeed $x^2-x-1$ has a root equal to the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386), 1.6180339887....

:::

## Properties of LLL-reduced basis {#Properties_of_LLL-reduced_basis}

Let $\mathbf{B}=\{ \mathbf{b}_1,\mathbf{b}_2, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n \}$ be a $\delta$-LLL-reduced basis of a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) $\mathcal L$. From the definition of LLL-reduced basis, we can derive several other useful properties about $\mathbf{B}$.

1. The first vector in the basis cannot be much larger than the [shortest non-zero vector](wikipedia://en/18661117#Shortest_vector_problem_%28SVP%29): $\Vert\mathbf{b}_1 \Vert \le (2 / (\sqrt{4\delta - 1}))^{n-1} \cdot \lambda_1(\mathcal L)$. In particular, for $\delta = 3/4$, this gives $\Vert\mathbf{b}_1 \Vert \le 2^{(n-1)/2} \cdot \lambda_1(\mathcal L)$.[^o13]
1. The first vector in the basis is also bounded by the determinant of the lattice: $\Vert\mathbf{b}_1 \Vert \le (2 / (\sqrt{4\delta - 1}))^{(n-1)/2} \cdot (\det(\mathcal L))^{1/n}$. In particular, for $\delta = 3/4$, this gives $\Vert\mathbf{b}_1 \Vert \le 2^{(n-1)/4} \cdot (\det(\mathcal L))^{1/n}$.
1. The product of the norms of the vectors in the basis cannot be much larger than the determinant of the lattice: let $\delta = 3/4$, then $\prod_{i=1}^n \Vert\mathbf{b}_i \Vert \le 2^{n(n-1)/4} \cdot \det(\mathcal L)$.

## LLL algorithm pseudocode {#LLL_algorithm_pseudocode}

The following description is based on (Hoffstein Pipher Silverman 2008, Theorem 6.68), with the corrections from the errata.[^o16]

**INPUT** a lattice basis **b**<sub>1</sub>, **b**<sub>2</sub>, ..., **b**<sub>*n*</sub> in **Z**<sup>*m*</sup> a parameter *δ* with 1/4 \< *δ* \< 1, most commonly *δ* = 3/4 **PROCEDURE** **B<sup>\*</sup>** \<- GramSchmidt({**b**<sub>1</sub>, ..., **b**<sub>*n*</sub>}) = {**b**<sub>1</sub><sup>\*</sup>, ..., **b**<sub>*n*</sub><sup>\*</sup>}; *and do not normalize* *μ*<sub>*i*,*j*</sub> \<- InnerProduct(**b**<sub>*i*</sub>, **b**<sub>*j*</sub><sup>\*</sup>)/InnerProduct(**b**<sub>j</sub><sup>\*</sup>, **b**<sub>*j*</sub><sup>\*</sup>); *using the most current values of* **b**<sub>*i*</sub> and **b**<sub>*j*</sub><sup>\*</sup> *k* \<- 2; **while** *k* \<= *n* **do** **for** *j* **from** *k*−1 **to** 1 **do** **if** |*μ*<sub>*k*,*j*</sub>| > 1/2 **then** **b**<sub>*k*</sub> \<- **b**<sub>*k*</sub> − ⌊*μ*<sub>*k*,*j*</sub>⌉**b**<sub>*j*</sub>; *Update* **B<sup>\*</sup>** *and the related* *μ*<sub>*i*,*j*</sub>*'s as needed.* *(The naive method is to recompute* **B<sup>\*</sup>** *whenever* **b<sub>*i*</sub>** *changes:* **B<sup>\*</sup>** \<- GramSchmidt({**b**<sub>1</sub>, ..., **b**<sub>*n*</sub>}) = {**b**<sub>1</sub><sup>\*</sup>, ..., **b**<sub>*n*</sub><sup>\*</sup>}) **end if** **end for** **if** InnerProduct(**b**<sub>*k*</sub><sup>\*</sup>, **b**<sub>*k*</sub><sup>\*</sup>) > (*δ − μ<sup>2</sup>*<sub>*k*,*k*−1</sub>) InnerProduct(**b**<sub>*k*−1</sub><sup>\*</sup>, **b**<sub>*k*−1</sub><sup>\*</sup>) **then** *k* \<- *k* + 1; **else** Swap **b**<sub>*k*</sub> and **b**<sub>*k*−1</sub>; *Update* **B<sup>\*</sup>** *and the related* *μ*<sub>*i*,*j*</sub>*'s as needed.* *k* \<- max(*k*−1, 2); **end if** **end while** **return** **B** the LLL reduced basis of {b<sub>1</sub>, ..., b<sub>*n*</sub>} **OUTPUT** the reduced basis **b**<sub>1</sub>, **b**<sub>2</sub>, ..., **b**<sub>*n*</sub> in **Z**<sup>*m*</sup>

## Examples {#Examples}

### Example from Z<sup>3</sup> {#Example_from_Z3}

Let a lattice basis $\mathbf{b}_1,\mathbf{b}_2, \mathbf{b}_3 \in \mathbf{Z}^{3}$, be given by the columns of

$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1& 3\\ 1 & 0 & 5\\ 1 & 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

then the reduced basis is

$$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1& -1\\ 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix},$$

which is size-reduced, satisfies the Lovász condition, and is hence LLL-reduced, as described above. See W. Bosma.[^o20] for details of the reduction process.

### Example from Z\[*i*\]<sup>4</sup> {#Example_from_Z[i]4}

Likewise, for the basis over the complex integers given by the columns of the matrix below,

$$\begin{bmatrix} -2+2i & 7+3i & 7+3i & -5+4i\\ 3+3i & -2+4i & 6+2i & -1+4i\\ 2+2i & -8+0i & -9+1i & -7+5i\\ 8+2i & -9+0i & 6+3i & -4+4i \end{bmatrix},$$

then the columns of the matrix below give an LLL-reduced basis.

$$\begin{bmatrix} -6+3i & -2+2i & 2-2i & -3+6i \\ 6-1i & 3+3i & 5-5i & 2+1i \\ 2-2i & 2+2i & -3-1i & -5+3i \\ -2+1i & 8+2i & 7+1i & -2-4i \\ \end{bmatrix}.$$

## Implementations {#Implementations}

LLL is implemented in

- [Arageli](http://www.arageli.org/) as the function `lll_reduction_int`
- [fpLLL](https://github.com/fplll/fplll) as a stand-alone implementation
- [FLINT](wikipedia://en/16415701) as the function `fmpz_lll`
- [GAP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GAP_computer_algebra_system) as the function `LLLReducedBasis`
- [Macaulay2](wikipedia://en/3184149) as the function `LLL` in the package `LLLBases`
- [Magma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma_computer_algebra_system) as the functions `LLL` and `LLLGram` (taking a gram matrix)
- [Maple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maple_computer_algebra_system) as the function `IntegerRelations[LLL]`
- [Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematica) as the function `LatticeReduce`
- [Number Theory Library (NTL)](https://github.com/libntl/ntl) as the function `LLL`
- [PARI/GP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PARI/GP) as the function `qflll`
- [Pymatgen](https://pymatgen.org/) as the function `analysis.get_lll_reduced_lattice`
- [SageMath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SageMath) as the method `LLL` driven by fpLLL and NTL
- [Isabelle/HOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isabelle/HOL) in the 'archive of formal proofs' entry `LLL_Basis_Reduction`. This code exports to efficiently executable Haskell.[^o24]
