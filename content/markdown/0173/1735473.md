# Tomahawk (geometry)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Tomahawk"}

*Image: Tomahawk filled.svg.* A tomahawk, with its handle and spike thickened

The **tomahawk** is a tool in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) for [angle trisection](wikipedia://en/91111), the problem of splitting an [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) into three equal parts. The boundaries of its shape include a [semicircle](wikipedia://en/617831) and two [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860), arranged in a way that resembles a [tomahawk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomahawk_%28axe%29), a Native American axe.[^o5][^o8] The same tool has also been called the **shoemaker's knife**,[^o2] but that name is more commonly used in geometry to refer to a different shape, the [arbelos](wikipedia://en/887197) (a curvilinear triangle bounded by three mutually tangent semicircles).[^o3]

:::

## Description {#Description}

The basic shape of a tomahawk consists of a semicircle (the "blade" of the tomahawk), with a line segment the length of the radius extending along the same line as the diameter of the semicircle (the tip of which is the "spike" of the tomahawk), and with another line segment of arbitrary length (the "handle" of the tomahawk) perpendicular to the diameter. In order to make it into a physical tool, its handle and spike may be thickened, as long as the line segment along the handle continues to be part of the boundary of the shape. Unlike a related trisection using a [carpenter's square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel_square), the other side of the thickened handle does not need to be made parallel to this line segment.[^o5]

In some sources a full circle rather than a semicircle is used,[^o9] or the tomahawk is also thickened along the diameter of its semicircle,[^o7] but these modifications make no difference to the action of the tomahawk as a trisector.

## Trisection {#Trisection}

*Image: Tomahawk2.svg.* A tomahawk [trisecting an angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trisecting_an_angle). The handle $\overline{AD}$ forms one trisector and the dotted line $\overline{AC}$ to the center of the semicircle forms the other.

To use the tomahawk to [trisect an angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trisect_an_angle), it is placed with its handle line touching the apex of the angle, with the blade inside the angle, tangent to one of the two rays forming the angle, and with the spike touching the other ray of the angle. One of the two trisecting lines then lies on the handle segment, and the other passes through the center point of the semicircle.[^o5][^o7] If the angle to be trisected is too sharp relative to the length of the tomahawk's handle, it may not be possible to fit the tomahawk into the angle in this way, but this difficulty may be worked around by repeatedly doubling the angle until it is large enough for the tomahawk to trisect it, and then repeatedly [bisecting](wikipedia://en/152547) the trisected angle the same number of times as the original angle was doubled.[^o8]

If the apex of the angle is labeled $A$, the point of tangency of the blade is $B$, the center of the semicircle is $C$, the top of the handle is $D$, and the spike is $E$, then triangles $\triangle ACD$ and $\triangle ADE$ are both [right triangles](wikipedia://en/39420) with a shared base and equal height, so they are [congruent triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruent_triangles). Because the sides $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{BC}$ of triangle $\triangle ABC$ are respectively a tangent and a radius of the semicircle, they are at right angles to each other and $\triangle ABC$ is also a right triangle; it has the same [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) as $\triangle ACD$ and the same side lengths $\overline{BC} = \overline{CD}$, so again it is congruent to the other two triangles, showing that the three angles formed at the apex are equal.[^o9][^o7]

Although the tomahawk may itself be constructed using a [compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge),[^o10] and may be used to trisect an angle, it does not contradict [Pierre Wantzel](wikipedia://en/2610953)'s 1837 theorem that arbitrary angles cannot be trisected by compass and unmarked straightedge alone.[^o11] The reason for this is that placing the constructed tomahawk into the required position is a form of [neusis](wikipedia://en/3752339) that is not allowed in compass and straightedge constructions.[^o12]

## History {#History}

The inventor of the tomahawk is unknown,[^o5][^o14] but the earliest references to it come from 19th-century France. It dates back at least as far as 1835, when it appeared in a book by [Claude Lucien Bergery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Lucien_Bergery), *Géométrie appliquée à l'industrie, à l'usage des artistes et des ouvriers* (3rd edition).[^o5] Another early publication of the same trisection was made by [Henri Brocard](wikipedia://en/14897496) in 1877;[^o15] Brocard in turn attributes its invention to an 1863 memoir by French naval officer Pierre-Joseph Glotin.[^o16][^o17][^o18]
