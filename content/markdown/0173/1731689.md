# Rényi entropy

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rényi entropy"}

In [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773), the **Rényi entropy** is a quantity that generalizes various notions of [entropy](wikipedia://en/15445), including [Hartley entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartley_entropy), [Shannon entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_entropy), **collision entropy**, and [min-entropy](wikipedia://en/41299452). The Rényi entropy is named after [Alfréd Rényi](wikipedia://en/614988), who looked for the most general way to quantify information while preserving additivity for independent events.[^o2][^o3] In the context of [fractal dimension](wikipedia://en/285907) estimation, the Rényi entropy forms the basis of the concept of **generalized dimensions**.[^o4]

:::

The Rényi entropy is important in ecology and statistics as [index of diversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diversity_indices). The Rényi entropy is also important in [quantum information](wikipedia://en/25274), where it can be used as a measure of [entanglement](wikipedia://en/25336). In the Heisenberg XY [spin chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_chain) model, the Rényi entropy as a function of $\alpha$ can be calculated explicitly because it is an [automorphic function](wikipedia://en/974148) with respect to a particular subgroup of the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019).[^o5][^o6] In [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392), the min-entropy is used in the context of [randomness extractors](wikipedia://en/18362292).

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $X$ be a [discrete random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) with possible outcomes in the set $\mathcal{A} = \{x_1,x_2,...,x_n\}$ and corresponding probabilities $p_i \doteq \Pr(X=x_i)$ for $i=1,\dots,n$. Then, the Rényi entropy of order $\alpha$, where $0 < \alpha < \infty$ and $\alpha \neq 1$, is defined as[^o2]

$$\mathrm{H}_\alpha(X) = \frac{1}{1-\alpha} \log\left(\sum_{i=1}^n p_i^\alpha\right) .$$

It is further defined at $\alpha = 0, 1, \infty$ as

$$\mathrm{H}_\alpha(X) = \lim_{\gamma \to \alpha} \mathrm{H}_\gamma(X) .$$

The resulting [unit of information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_information) is determined by the base of the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860), e.g. [shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_%28unit%29) for base 2, or [nat](wikipedia://en/3070481) for base [*e*](wikipedia://en/9633). If the probabilities are $p_i=1/n$ for all $i=1,\dots,n$, then all the Rényi entropies of the distribution are equal: $\mathrm{H}_\alpha(X)=\log n$. In general, for all discrete random variables $X$, $\mathrm{H}_\alpha(X)$ is a non-increasing function in $\alpha$.

Applications often exploit the following relation between the Rényi entropy and the [*α*-norm](wikipedia://en/45194#The_p-norm_in_finite_dimensions) of the vector of probabilities:

$$\mathrm{H}_\alpha(X)=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} \log \left({\left\|P\right\|}_\alpha\right) .$$

Here, the discrete [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) $P = (p_1, \dots, p_n)$ is interpreted as a vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $p_i\geq 0$ and $\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i = 1$.

The Rényi entropy for any $\alpha \geq 0$ is [Schur concave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schur-concave_function), a property that can be proven by the [Schur–Ostrowski criterion.](wikipedia://en/3897244#Schur–Ostrowski_criterion)

:::

## Special cases {#Special_cases}

*Image: Mplwp reny entropy012inf.svg.* Rényi entropy of a random variable with two possible outcomes against $p_{1}$, where $P = (p_{1}, 1 - p_{1})$. Shown are $\mathrm{H}$<sub>0</sub>, $\mathrm{H}$<sub>1</sub>, $\mathrm{H}$<sub>2</sub> and $\mathrm{H}$<sub>∞</sub>, with the unit on the vertical axis being the [shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_%28unit%29).

As $\alpha$ approaches zero, the Rényi entropy increasingly weighs all events with nonzero probability more equally, regardless of their probabilities. In the limit for $\alpha \to 0$, the Rényi entropy is just the logarithm of the size of the [support](wikipedia://en/381013) of $X$. The limit for $\alpha \to 1$ is the [Shannon entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_entropy). For $\alpha = 2$, the Rényi entropy is related to the collision probability, and the quantity $2^{H_2} = 1/\sum p_i^2$ is known as the participation ratio, widely used in condensed matter physics and random matrix theory to measure the effective number of states contributing to a distribution. As $\alpha$ approaches infinity, the Rényi entropy is increasingly determined by the events of highest probability.

### Hartley or max-entropy {#Hartley_or_max-entropy}

$\mathrm{H}_0(X)$ is $\log n$ where $n$ is the number of non-zero probabilities.[^o12] If the probabilities are all nonzero, it is simply the logarithm of the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of the alphabet ($\mathcal{A}$) of $X$, sometimes called the [Hartley entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartley_entropy) of $X$,

$$\mathrm{H}_0 (X) = \log n = \log |\mathcal{A}|\,$$

### Shannon entropy {#Shannon_entropy}

The limiting value of $\mathrm{H}_\alpha$ as $\alpha \to 1$ is the [Shannon entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_entropy):[^o14]

$$\mathrm{H}_1 (X) \equiv \lim_{\alpha \to 1} \mathrm{H}_{\alpha} (X) = - \sum_{i=1}^n p_i \log p_i$$

### Collision entropy {#Collision_entropy}

**Collision entropy**, sometimes just called "Rényi entropy", refers to the case $\alpha = 2$,

$$\mathrm{H}_2 (X) = - \log \sum_{i=1}^n p_i^2 = - \log P(X = Y) ,$$

where $X$ and $Y$ are [independent and identically distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_and_identically_distributed). The collision entropy is related to the [index of coincidence](wikipedia://en/157932). It is the negative logarithm of the [Simpson diversity index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simpson_diversity_index). The quantity $2^{H_2} = 1/\sum p_i^2$ is also known in [random matrix theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_matrix_theory) as the **[participation ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Participation_ratio)**, where it measures the effective number of eigenstates contributing to a quantum state or, more generally, the effective number of eigenvalues contributing to a matrix spectrum.[^o16]

### Min-entropy {#Min-entropy}

In the limit as $\alpha \rightarrow \infty$, the Rényi entropy $\mathrm{H}_\alpha$ converges to the **min-entropy** $\mathrm{H}_\infty$:

$$\mathrm{H}_\infty(X) \doteq \min_i (-\log p_i) = -(\max_i \log p_i) = -\log \max_i p_i\,.$$

Equivalently, the min-entropy $\mathrm{H}_\infty(X)$ is the largest [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $b$ such that all events occur with probability at most $2^{-b}$.

The name *min-entropy* stems from the fact that it is the smallest entropy measure in the family of Rényi entropies. In this sense, it is the strongest way to measure the information content of a discrete random variable. In particular, the min-entropy is never larger than the [Shannon entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_entropy).

The min-entropy has important applications for [randomness extractors](wikipedia://en/18362292) in [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392): Extractors are able to extract randomness from random sources that have a large min-entropy; merely having a large [Shannon entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_entropy) does not suffice for this task.

:::

## Inequalities for different orders *α* {#Inequalities_for_different_orders_α}

That $\mathrm{H}_\alpha$ is non-increasing in $\alpha$ for any given distribution of probabilities $p_i$, which can be proven by differentiation,[^o19] as

$$-\frac{d \mathrm{H}_\alpha}{d\alpha} = \frac{1}{(1-\alpha)^2} \sum_{i=1}^n z_i \log(z_i / p_i) = \frac{1}{(1-\alpha)^2} D_\text{KL}(z\| p)$$

which is proportional to [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052) (which is always non-negative), where $z_i = p_i^\alpha / \sum_{j=1}^n p_j^\alpha$. In particular, it is strictly positive except when the distribution is uniform.

At the $\alpha \to 1$ limit, we have $-\frac{d \mathrm{H}_\alpha}{d\alpha} \to \frac{1}{2} \sum_i p_i {\left(\ln p_i + H(p)\right)}^2$.

In particular cases inequalities can be proven also by [Jensen's inequality](wikipedia://en/297811):[^o20][^o21]

$$\log n=\mathrm{H}_0\geq \mathrm{H}_1 \geq \mathrm{H}_2 \geq \mathrm{H}_\infty .$$

For values of $\alpha > 1$, inequalities in the other direction also hold. In particular, we have[^o22][^o23]

$$\mathrm{H}_2 \le 2 \mathrm{H}_\infty .$$

On the other hand, the Shannon entropy $\mathrm{H}_1$ can be arbitrarily high for a random variable $X$ that has a given min-entropy. An example of this is given by the sequence of random variables $X_n \sim \{0, \ldots, n\}$ for $n \geq 1$ such that $P(X_n = 0) = 1/2$ and $P(X_n = x) = 1/(2n)$ since $\mathrm{H}_\infty(X_n) = \log 2$ but $\mathrm{H}_1(X_n) = (\log 2 + \log 2n)/2$.

## Rényi divergence {#Rényi_divergence}

As well as the absolute Rényi entropies, Rényi also defined a spectrum of divergence measures generalising the [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052).[^o26]

The **Rényi divergence** of order $\alpha$ or **alpha-divergence** of a distribution $P$ from a distribution $Q$ is defined to be

$$\begin{aligned} D_\alpha (P \Vert Q) &= \frac{1}{\alpha-1} \log\left(\sum_{i=1}^n p_i^\alpha q_i^{1-\alpha}\right) \\[1ex] &= \frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log \mathbb E_{i \sim p}\left[{\left(p_i/q_i\right)}^{\alpha - 1}\right] \, \end{aligned}$$

when $0 < \alpha < \infty$ and $\alpha \ne 1$. We can define the Rényi divergence for the special values $\alpha = 0, 1, \infty$ by taking a limit, and in particular the limit $\alpha \to 1$ gives the Kullback–Leibler divergence.

Some special cases:

- $D_0(P \Vert Q) = - \log Q(\{i : p_i > 0\})$: minus the [log probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Log_probability) under $Q$ that $p_{i} > 0$;
- $D_{1/2}(P \Vert Q) = -2 \log \sum_{i=1}^n \sqrt{p_i q_i}$: minus twice the logarithm of the [Bhattacharyya coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhattacharyya_coefficient); ((Nielsen Boltz 2010))
- $D_1(P \Vert Q) = \sum_{i=1}^n p_i \log \frac{p_i}{q_i}$: the [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052);
- $D_2(P \Vert Q) = \log \Big\langle \frac{p_i}{q_i} \Big\rangle$: the log of the expected ratio of the probabilities;
- $D_\infty(P \Vert Q) = \log \sup_i \frac{p_i}{q_i}$: the log of the maximum ratio of the probabilities.

The Rényi divergence is indeed a [divergence](wikipedia://en/25896411), meaning simply that $D_\alpha (P \| Q)$ is greater than or equal to zero, and zero only when $P = Q$. For any fixed distributions $P$ and $Q$, the Rényi divergence is nondecreasing as a function of its order $\alpha$, and it is continuous on the set of $\alpha$ for which it is finite,[^o26] or for the sake of brevity, the information of order $\alpha$ obtained if the distribution $P$ is replaced by the distribution $Q$.[^o2]

:::

## Financial interpretation {#Financial_interpretation}

A pair of probability distributions can be viewed as a game of chance in which one of the distributions defines official odds and the other contains the actual probabilities. Knowledge of the actual probabilities allows a player to profit from the game. The expected profit rate is connected to the Rényi divergence as follows[^o29]

$${\rm ExpectedRate} = \frac{1}{R}\, D_1(b\|m) + \frac{R-1}{R}\, D_{1/R}(b\|m) \,,$$

where $m$ is the distribution defining the official odds (i.e. the "market") for the game, $b$ is the investor-believed distribution and $R$ is the investor's risk aversion (the [Arrow–Pratt relative risk aversion](wikipedia://en/177700#Relative_risk_aversion)).

If the true distribution is $p$ (not necessarily coinciding with the investor's belief $b$), the long-term realized rate converges to the true expectation which has a similar mathematical structure[^o29]

$${\rm RealizedRate} = \frac{1}{R}\,\Big( D_1(p\|m) - D_1(p\|b) \Big) + \frac{R-1}{R}\, D_{1/R}(b\|m) \,.$$

## Properties specific to *α* = 1 {#Properties_specific_to_α_=_1}

The value $\alpha = 1$, which gives the [Shannon entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_entropy) and the [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052), is the only value at which the [chain rule of conditional probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_information#Chain_rule) holds exactly:

$$\mathrm{H}(A,X) = \mathrm{H}(A) + \mathbb{E}_{a \sim A} \big[ \mathrm{H}(X| A=a) \big]$$

for the absolute entropies, and

$$D_\mathrm{KL}(p(x|a)p(a)\|m(x,a)) = D_\mathrm{KL}(p(a)\|m(a)) + \mathbb{E}_{p(a)}\{D_\mathrm{KL}(p(x|a)\|m(x|a))\},$$

for the relative entropies.

The latter in particular means that if we seek a distribution $p(x, a)$ which minimizes the divergence from some underlying prior measure $m(x, a)$, and we acquire new information which only affects the distribution of $a$, then the distribution of $p(x|a)$ remains $m(x|a)$, unchanged.

The other Rényi divergences satisfy the criteria of being positive and continuous, being invariant under 1-to-1 co-ordinate transformations, and of combining additively when $A$ and $X$ are independent, so that if $p(A, X) = p(A)p(X)$, then

$$\mathrm{H}_\alpha(A,X) = \mathrm{H}_\alpha(A) + \mathrm{H}_\alpha(X)\;$$

and

$$D_\alpha(P(A)P(X)\|Q(A)Q(X)) = D_\alpha(P(A)\|Q(A)) + D_\alpha(P(X)\|Q(X)).$$

The stronger properties of the $\alpha = 1$ quantities allow the definition of [conditional information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_information) and [mutual information](wikipedia://en/427282) from communication theory.

## Exponential families {#Exponential_families}

The Rényi entropies and divergences for an [exponential family](wikipedia://en/339174) admit simple expressions[^o32]

$$\mathrm{H}_\alpha(p_F(x;\theta)) = \frac{1}{1-\alpha} \left(F(\alpha\theta)-\alpha F(\theta) + \log E_p\left[e^{(\alpha-1)k(x)}\right]\right)$$

and

$$D_\alpha(p:q) = \frac{J_{F,\alpha}(\theta:\theta')}{1-\alpha}$$

where

$$J_{F,\alpha}(\theta:\theta')= \alpha F(\theta)+(1-\alpha) F(\theta')- F(\alpha\theta+(1-\alpha)\theta')$$

is a Jensen difference divergence.

## Quantum information {#Quantum_information}

The Rényi entropy can be generalized to the quantum case as

$$H_\alpha(\rho) := \frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\operatorname{tr}(\rho^\alpha)$$

where $\rho$ is normalized [density matrix](wikipedia://en/62844). Its limit as $\alpha\to 1$ is the [von Neumann entropy](wikipedia://en/2515425).[^o34]

The Rényi relative entropy (or divergence) can be generalized in two possible ways: the straightforward quantum Rényi relative entropy

$$D_\alpha(\rho\|\sigma) = \frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{tr}(\rho^\alpha\sigma^{1-\alpha})$$

and the sandwiched Rényi relative entropy

$$\tilde{D}_\alpha(\rho\|\sigma) = \frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{tr}\left[\left(\sigma^\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}\rho\sigma^\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}\right)^\alpha\right]$$

Both converge to the [quantum relative entropy](wikipedia://en/5993806) in the limit $\alpha \to 1$. However, the sandwiched Rényi relative entropy has more convenient properties when used to defined conditional entropies,[^o34] and has found applications in [quantum key distribution](wikipedia://en/51910).[^o35][^o36]

## Stabilizer Rényi entropy {#Stabilizer_Rényi_entropy}

One measure of [quantum magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_magic) is the stabilizer Rényi entropy[^o38] of order *α*:

$$M_\alpha = \frac{1}{1 - \alpha} \log \left[\frac{1}{2^N} \sum_{\hat{P} \in \mathbb{P}_N} \langle \hat{P}\rangle^{2\alpha}\right]$$

with $\hat{P}$ being the multi-qubit [Pauli group](wikipedia://en/5045613) and $\langle \hat{P} \rangle = \langle \psi | \hat{P} | \psi \rangle$ is an [expectation value (quantum mechanics)](wikipedia://en/11371560) for an $N$-qubit system.

## Ecology {#Ecology}

Hill (1973) showed that the exponential of the Rényi entropy of order *α* defines a one-parameter family of [diversity indices](wikipedia://en/3099367), now known as [Hill numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill_number): <sup>*α*</sup>*D* = exp(*H*<sub>*α*</sub>).[^o40] For *α* = 0, this gives the species richness (total count of species present); for *α* = 1, the exponential of the [Shannon entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_entropy); and for *α* = 2, the inverse [Simpson index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simpson's_diversity_index), which counts the effective number of equally common species. Hill's framework unifies several previously disparate diversity measures as members of a single parametric family, differing only in how much weight is given to rare versus common species.
