# Second moment of area

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Second moment of area"}

The **second moment of area**, or **second area moment**, or **quadratic moment of area** and also known as the **area moment of inertia**, is a geometrical property of an [area](wikipedia://en/1209) which reflects how its points are distributed with regard to an arbitrary axis. The second moment of area is typically denoted with either an $I$ (for an axis that lies in the plane of the area) or with a $J$ (for an axis perpendicular to the plane). In both cases, it is calculated with a [multiple integral](wikipedia://en/2757224) over the object in question. Its dimension is L (length) to the [fourth power](wikipedia://en/665027). Its [unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_unit) of dimension, when working with the [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units), is meters to the fourth power, [m](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre)<sup>4</sup>, or inches to the fourth power, [in](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inch)<sup>4</sup>, when working in the [Imperial System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_units) or the [US customary system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_customary_system).

:::

In [structural engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_engineering), the second moment of area of a [beam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_%28structure%29) is an important property used in the calculation of the beam's [deflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflection_%28engineering%29) and the calculation of [stress](wikipedia://en/228107) caused by a [moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment_%28physics%29) applied to the beam. In order to maximize the second moment of area, a large fraction of the [cross-sectional area](wikipedia://en/1337370) of an [I-beam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I-beam) is located at the maximum possible distance from the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) of the I-beam's cross-section. The **planar** second moment of area provides insight into a beam's [resistance to bending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stiffness) due to an applied moment, [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force), or distributed [load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_load) perpendicular to its [neutral axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_axis), as a function of its shape. The **polar** second moment of area provides insight into a beam's resistance to [torsional](wikipedia://en/3039253) deflection, due to an applied moment parallel to its cross-section, as a function of its shape.

Different disciplines use the term *[moment of inertia](wikipedia://en/157700)* (MOI) to refer to [different moments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_moments_of_inertia). It may refer to either of the **planar** second moments of area (often $I_x = \iint_{R} y^2\, dA$ or $I_y = \iint_{R} x^2\, dA,$ with respect to some reference plane), or the **polar** second moment of area ($I = \iint_{R} r^2\, dA$, where r is the distance to some reference axis). In each case the integral is over all the [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) elements of *area*, *dA*, in some two-dimensional cross-section. In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), *moment of inertia* is strictly the second moment of **mass** with respect to distance from an axis: $I = \int_{Q} r^2 dm$, where *r* is the distance to some potential rotation axis, and the integral is over all the infinitesimal elements of *mass*, *dm*, in a [three-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/3054853) occupied by an object $Q$. The MOI, in this sense, is the analog of mass for rotational problems. In engineering (especially mechanical and civil), *moment of inertia* commonly refers to the second moment of the area.[^o2]

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: Moment of area of an arbitrary shape.svg.* An arbitrary shape. *ρ* is the distance to the element d*A*, with projections *x* and *y* on the *x* and *y* axes.

The second moment of area for an arbitrary shape $R$ with respect to an arbitrary axis $BB'$ ($BB'$ axis is not drawn in the adjacent image; is an axis coplanar with *x* and *y* axes and is perpendicular to the [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) $\rho$) is defined as

$$J_{BB'} = \iint_{R} {\rho}^2 \, dA$$

where

- $dA$ is the infinitesimal area element, and
- $\rho$ is the distance from the $BB'$ axis.[^o5]

For example, when the desired reference axis is the x-axis, the second moment of area $I_{xx}$ (often denoted as $I_x$) can be computed in [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) as

$$I_{x} = \iint_{R} y^2\, dx\, dy$$

The second moment of the area is crucial in [Euler–Bernoulli theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Bernoulli_beam_equation) of slender beams.

::: {#o7 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Product Moment of Area"}

### Product moment of area {#Product_moment_of_area}

More generally, the **product moment of area** is defined as[^o8]

$$I_{xy} = \iint_{R} yx\, dx\, dy$$

:::

## Parallel axis theorem {#Parallel_axis_theorem}

*Image: Parallel axis theorem.svg.* A shape with [centroidal](wikipedia://en/187926) axis *x*. The parallel axis theorem can be used to obtain the second moment of area with respect to the *x**axis.***

It is sometimes necessary to calculate the second moment of area of a shape with respect to an $x'$ axis different to the [centroidal](wikipedia://en/187926) axis of the shape. However, it is often easier to derive the second moment of area with respect to its centroidal axis, $x$, and use the [parallel axis theorem](wikipedia://en/30876319) to derive the second moment of area with respect to the $x'$ axis. The parallel axis theorem states

$$I_{x'} = I_x + A d^2$$

where

- $A$ is the area of the shape, and
- $d$ is the perpendicular distance between the $x$ and $x'$ axes.[^o11][^o12]

A similar statement can be made about a $y'$ axis and the parallel centroidal $y$ axis. Or, in general, any centroidal $B$ axis and a parallel $B'$ axis.

:::

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Perpendicular axis theorem"}

## Perpendicular axis theorem {#Perpendicular_axis_theorem}

For the simplicity of calculation, it is often desired to define the polar moment of area (with respect to a perpendicular axis) in terms of two area moments of inertia (both with respect to in-plane axes). The simplest case relates $J_z$ to $I_x$ and $I_y$.

$$J_z = \iint_{R} \rho^2\, dA = \iint_{R} \left(x^2 + y^2\right) dA = \iint_{R} x^2 \, dA + \iint_{R} y^2 \, dA = I_x + I_y$$

This relationship relies on the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) which relates $x$ and $y$ to $\rho$ and on the [linearity of integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearity_of_integration).

:::

## Composite shapes {#Composite_shapes}

For more complex areas, it is often easier to divide the area into a series of "simpler" shapes. The second moment of area for the entire shape is the sum of the second moment of areas of all of its parts about a common axis. This can include shapes that are "missing" (i.e. holes, hollow shapes, etc.), in which case the second moment of area of the "missing" areas are subtracted, rather than added. In other words, the second moment of area of "missing" parts are considered negative for the method of composite shapes.

::: {#o17 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

See [list of second moments of area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_second_moments_of_area) for other shapes.

### Rectangle with centroid at the origin {#Rectangle_with_centroid_at_the_origin}

*Image: Moment of area of a rectangle through the centroid.svg.* Rectangle with base *b* and height *h*

Consider a rectangle with base $b$ and height $h$ whose [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) is located at the origin. $I_x$ represents the second moment of area with respect to the x-axis; $I_y$ represents the second moment of area with respect to the y-axis; $J_z$ represents the polar moment of inertia with respect to the z-axis.

$$\begin{aligned} I_x &= \iint_{R} y^2\, dA = \int^\frac{b}{2}_{-\frac{b}{2}} \int^\frac{h}{2}_{-\frac{h}{2}} y^2 \,dy \,dx = \int^\frac{b}{2}_{-\frac{b}{2}} \frac{1}{3}\frac{h^3}{4}\,dx = \frac{b h^3}{12} \\ I_y &= \iint_{R} x^2\, dA = \int^\frac{b}{2}_{-\frac{b}{2}} \int^\frac{h}{2}_{-\frac{h}{2}} x^2 \,dy \,dx = \int^\frac{b}{2}_{-\frac{b}{2}} h x^2\, dx = \frac{b^3 h}{12} \end{aligned}$$

Using the perpendicular axis theorem we get the value of $J_z$.

$$J_z = I_x + I_y = \frac{b h^3}{12} + \frac{h b^3}{12} = \frac{b h}{12}\left(b^2 + h^2\right)$$

### Annulus centered at origin {#Annulus_centered_at_origin}

*Image: Moment of area of an annulus.svg.* Annulus with inner radius *r*<sub>1</sub> and outer radius *r*<sub>2</sub>

Consider an [annulus](wikipedia://en/356158) whose center is at the origin, outside radius is $r_2$, and inside radius is $r_1$. Because of the symmetry of the annulus, the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) also lies at the origin. We can determine the polar moment of inertia, $J_z$, about the $z$ axis by the method of composite shapes. This polar moment of inertia is equivalent to the polar moment of inertia of a circle with radius $r_2$ minus the polar moment of inertia of a circle with radius $r_1$, both centered at the origin. First, let us derive the polar moment of inertia of a circle with radius $r$ with respect to the origin. In this case, it is easier to directly calculate $J_z$ as we already have $r^2$, which has both an $x$ and $y$ component. Instead of obtaining the second moment of area from [Cartesian coordinates](wikipedia://en/7706) as done in the previous section, we shall calculate $I_x$ and $J_z$ directly using [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates).

$$\begin{aligned} I_{x, \text{circle}} &= \iint_{R} y^2\,dA = \iint_{R} \left(r\sin{\theta}\right)^2\, dA = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^r \left(r\sin{\theta}\right)^2\left(r \, dr \, d\theta\right) \\ &= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^r r^3\sin^2{\theta}\,dr \, d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{r^4\sin^2{\theta}}{4}\,d\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}r^4 \\ J_{z, \text{circle}} &= \iint_{R} r^2\, dA = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^r r^2\left(r\,dr\,d\theta\right) = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^r r^3\,dr\,d\theta \\ &= \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{r^4}{4}\,d\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}r^4 \end{aligned}$$

Now, the polar moment of inertia about the $z$ axis for an annulus is simply, as stated above, the difference of the second moments of [area of a circle](wikipedia://en/1896782) with radius $r_2$ and a circle with radius $r_1$.

$$J_z = J_{z, r_2} - J_{z, r_1} = \frac{\pi}{2}r_2^4 - \frac{\pi}{2}r_1^4 = \frac{\pi}{2}\left(r_2^4 - r_1^4\right)$$

Alternatively, we could change the limits on the $dr$ integral the first time around to reflect the fact that there is a hole. This would be done like this.

$$\begin{aligned} J_{z} &= \iint_{R} r^2 \, dA = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_{r_1}^{r_2} r^2\left(r\, dr\, d\theta\right) = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_{r_1}^{r_2} r^3\, dr\, d\theta \\ &= \int_0^{2\pi}\left[\frac{r_2^4}{4} - \frac{r_1^4}{4}\right]\, d\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}\left(r_2^4 - r_1^4\right) \end{aligned}$$

### Any polygon {#Any_polygon}

*Image: Moment of area of a polygon.svg.* A simple polygon. Here, $n=6$, notice point "7" is identical to point 1.

The second moment of area about the origin for any [simple polygon](wikipedia://en/1059530) on the XY-plane can be computed in general by summing contributions from each segment of the polygon after dividing the area into a set of triangles. This formula is related to the [shoelace formula](wikipedia://en/17863612) and can be considered a special case of [Green's theorem](wikipedia://en/243334).

A polygon is assumed to have $n$ vertices, numbered in counter-clockwise fashion. If polygon vertices are numbered clockwise, returned values will be negative, but absolute values will be correct.

$$\begin{aligned} I_y &= \frac{1}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( x_i y_{i+1} - x_{i+1} y_i\right)\left( x_i^2 + x_i x_{i+1} + x_{i+1}^2 \right) \\ I_x &= \frac{1}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( x_i y_{i+1} - x_{i+1} y_i\right)\left( y_i^2 + y_i y_{i+1} + y_{i+1}^2 \right) \\ I_{xy} &= \frac{1}{24}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( x_i y_{i+1} - x_{i+1} y_i\right) \left( x_i y_{i+1} + 2 x_i y_i + 2 x_{i+1} y_{i+1} + x_{i+1} y_i \right) \end{aligned}$$

where $x_i,y_i$ are the coordinates of the $i$-th polygon vertex, for $1 \le i \le n$. Also, $x_{n+1}, y_{n+1}$ are assumed to be equal to the coordinates of the first vertex, i.e., $x_{n+1} = x_1$ and $y_{n+1} = y_1$.[^o21][^o22] [^o23] [^o24]
