# Hand's paradox

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hand's paradox"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), **Hand's paradox** arises from [ambiguity](wikipedia://en/677) when comparing two [treatments](wikipedia://en/1765232). It shows that a comparison of the effects of the treatments applied to two [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_%28statistics%29) groups that can contradict a [comparison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparison) between the effects of both treatments applied to a single group.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=named-heading name="Paradox"}

## Paradox {#Paradox}

Comparisons of two treatments often involve comparing the responses of a [random sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_sample) of [patients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patient) receiving one treatment with an independent [random sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_sample) receiving the other. One commonly used [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) of the difference is then the [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) that a randomly chosen member of one group will have a higher score than a randomly chosen member of the other group. However, in many situations, interest really lies on which of the two treatments will give a randomly chosen patient the greater probability of doing better. These two measures, a comparison between two [randomly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomly) chosen patients, one from each group, and a comparison of treatment effects on a randomly chosen patient, can lead to different conclusions.

This has been called Hand's [paradox](wikipedia://en/24390),[^o4][^o5] and appears to have first been described by [David J. Hand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_J._Hand).[^o6]

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### Example 1 {#Example_1}

Label the two treatments A and B and suppose that:

Patient 1 would have response values 2 and 3 to A and B respectively. Patient 2 would have response values 4 and 5 to A and B respectively. Patient 3 would have response values 6 and 1 to A and B respectively.

Then the probability that the response to A of a randomly chosen patient is greater than the response to B of a randomly chosen patient is 6/9 = 2/3. But the probability that a randomly chosen patient will have a greater response to A than B is 1/3. Thus a simple comparison of two independent groups may suggest that patients have a higher probability of doing better under A, whereas in fact patients have a higher probability of doing better under B.

### Example 2 {#Example_2}

Suppose we have two [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685), $x_A \sim N(1,1)$ and $x_B \sim N(0,1)$, corresponding to the effects of two treatments. If we assume that $x_A$ and $x_B$ are independent, then $\Pr(x_A > x_B) = 0.76$, suggesting that A is more likely to benefit a patient than B. In contrast, the [joint distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_distribution) which *minimizes* $Pr(x_A>x_B )$ leads to $Pr(x_A>x_B )\ge 0.38$. This means that it is possible that in up to 62% of cases treatment B is better than treatment A.

:::
