# History of combinatorics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="History of combinatorics"}

The mathematical field of [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) was studied to varying degrees in numerous ancient societies. Its study in [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe) dates to the work of [Leonardo Fibonacci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_Fibonacci) in the 13th century AD, which introduced Arabian and Indian ideas to the continent. It has continued to be studied in the modern era.

:::

## Earliest records {#Earliest_records}

*Image: Rhind Mathematical Papyrus.jpg.* A portion of the Rhind papyrus

The earliest recorded use of combinatorial techniques comes from problem 79 of the [Rhind papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhind_papyrus), which dates to the 16th century BC. The problem concerns a certain [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630), and has similarities to Fibonacci's problem of counting the number of [compositions](wikipedia://en/550741) of 1s and 2s that sum to a given total.[^o14]

In Greece, [Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch) wrote that [Xenocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenocrates) of [Chalcedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcedon) (396–314 BC) discovered the number of different [syllables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllable) possible in the [Greek language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language). This would have been the first attempt on record to solve a difficult problem in [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) and [combinations](wikipedia://en/5308).[^o3] The claim, however, is implausible: this is one of the few mentions of combinatorics in [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece), and the number they found, 1.002 × 10<sup>12</sup>, seems too round to be more than a guess.[^o15][^o4]

Later, an argument between [Chrysippus](wikipedia://en/37143) (3rd century BC) and [Hipparchus](wikipedia://en/13600) (2nd century BC) of a rather delicate [enumerative](wikipedia://en/212115) problem, which was later shown to be related to [Schröder–Hipparchus numbers](wikipedia://en/33490570), is mentioned.[^o5][^o6] There is also evidence that in the *[Ostomachion](wikipedia://en/14620737)*, [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) (3rd century BC) considered the configurations of a [tiling puzzle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiling_puzzle),[^o7] while some combinatorial interests may have been present in lost works of [Apollonius](wikipedia://en/257242).[^o8][^o9]

In [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India), the [Bhagavati Sutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vyākhyāprajñapti) had the first mention of a combinatorics problem; the problem asked how many possible combinations of tastes were possible from selecting tastes in ones, twos, threes, etc. from a selection of six different tastes (sweet, pungent, astringent, sour, salt, and bitter). The Bhagavati is also the first text to mention the [choose function](wikipedia://en/4668).[^o10] In the second century BC, [Pingala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pingala) included an enumeration problem in the [Chanda Sutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_prosody) (also Chandahsutra) which asked how many ways a six-syllable [meter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_%28music%29) could be made from short and long notes.[^o11][^o12] Pingala found the number of meters that had $n$ long notes and $k$ short notes; this is equivalent to finding the [binomial coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_coefficients).

The ideas of the Bhagavati were generalized by the Indian mathematician [Mahavira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahavira_%28mathematician%29) in 850 AD, and Pingala's work on [prosody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosody_%28poetry%29) was expanded by [Bhāskara II](wikipedia://en/480509)[^o10][^o13] and [Hemacandra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemacandra) in 1100 AD. Bhaskara was the first known person to find the generalised choice function, although [Brahmagupta](wikipedia://en/297206) may have known earlier.[^o14] Hemacandra asked how many meters existed of a certain length if a long note was considered to be twice as long as a short note, which is equivalent to finding the [Fibonacci numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_numbers).[^o11]

*Image: Iching-hexagram-37.svg.* A [hexagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Ching)

The ancient Chinese book of divination [I Ching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Ching) describes a [hexagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagram_%28I_Ching%29) as a permutation with repetitions of six lines where each line can be one of two states: solid or dashed. In describing hexagrams in this fashion they determine that there are $2^6=64$ possible hexagrams. A Chinese [monk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monk) also may have counted the number of configurations to a game similar to [Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go_%28board_game%29) around 700 AD.[^o15] Although China had relatively few advancements in enumerative combinatorics, around 100 AD they solved the [Lo Shu Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lo_Shu_Square) which is the [combinatorial design](wikipedia://en/2761968) problem of the normal [magic square](wikipedia://en/52202) of order three.[^o14][^o16] Magic squares remained an interest of China, and they began to generalize their original $3\times3$ square between 900 and 1300 AD. China corresponded with the [Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East) about this problem in the 13th century.[^o14] The Middle East also learned about binomial coefficients from Indian work and found the connection to [polynomial expansion](wikipedia://en/4173350).[^o17] The work of Hindus influenced Arabs as seen in the work of [al-Khalil ibn Ahmad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khalil_ibn_Ahmad) who considered the possible arrangements of letters to form syllables. His calculations show an understanding of permutations and combinations. In a passage from the work of Arab mathematician [Omar Khayyam](wikipedia://en/92550) that dates to around 1100, it is corroborated that the Hindus had knowledge of binomial coefficients, but also that their methods reached the middle east.

[Abū Bakr ibn Muḥammad ibn al Ḥusayn Al-Karaji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Karaji) (c. 953–1029) wrote on the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677) and [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497). In a now lost work known only from subsequent quotation by [al-Samaw'al](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Samaw'al), [Al-Karaji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Karaji) introduced the idea of argument by [mathematical induction](wikipedia://en/18881).

The [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher) and [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer) [Abraham ibn Ezra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_ibn_Ezra) (c. 1140) counted the permutations with repetitions in vocalization of [Divine Name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetragrammaton).[^o18] He also established the symmetry of binomial coefficients, while a [closed formula](wikipedia://en/585143) was obtained later by the [talmudist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talmudist) and [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Levi ben Gerson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi_ben_Gerson) (better known as Gersonides), in 1321.[^o19] The arithmetical triangle—a graphical diagram showing relationships among the [binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/4668)— was presented by mathematicians in treatises dating as far back as the 10th century, and would eventually become known as Pascal's triangle. Later, in 17th century England, [campanology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campanology) provided examples of what is now known as [Hamiltonian cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle) in certain [Cayley graphs](wikipedia://en/358277) on permutations.[^o20]

## Combinatorics in the West {#Combinatorics_in_the_West}

Combinatorics came to Europe in the 13th century through mathematicians [Leonardo Fibonacci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_Fibonacci) and [Jordanus de Nemore](wikipedia://en/4763255). Fibonacci's *[Liber Abaci](wikipedia://en/167699)* introduced many of the Arabian and Indian ideas to Europe, including that of the Fibonacci numbers.[^o22] Jordanus was the first person to arrange the binomial coefficients in a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654), as he did in proposition 70 of *De Arithmetica*. This was also done in the Middle East in 1265, and China around 1300.[^o14] Today, this triangle is known as Pascal's triangle.

[Pascal](wikipedia://en/4068)'s contribution to the triangle that bears his name comes from his work on [formal proofs](wikipedia://en/1197184) about it, and the connections he made between Pascal's triangle and [probability](wikipedia://en/22934).[^o14] From a letter [Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) sent to [Daniel Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/46784) we learn that Leibniz was formally studying the mathematical theory of [integer partitions](wikipedia://en/104790) in the 17th century, although no formal work was published. Together with Leibniz, Pascal published *[De Arte Combinatoria](wikipedia://en/2265373)* in 1666.[^o23] Pascal and Leibniz are considered the founders of modern combinatorics.[^o24]

Both Pascal and Leibniz understood that the [binomial expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_expansion) was equivalent to the choice function. The notion that [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) and combinatorics corresponded was expanded by [De Moivre](wikipedia://en/57327), who found the expansion of a [multinomial](wikipedia://en/597998).[^o25] De Moivre also found the formula for [derangements](wikipedia://en/162132) using the principle of [principle of inclusion–exclusion](wikipedia://en/342684),[^o14] a method different from [Nikolaus Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/1151030), who had found it previously.[^o26][^o27] De Moivre also managed to approximate the binomial coefficients and [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606), and found a closed form for the Fibonacci numbers by inventing [generating functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generating_functions).[^o28][^o29]

In the 18th century, [Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler) worked on problems of combinatorics, and several problems of probability which are linked to combinatorics. Problems Euler worked on include the [Knights tour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knights_tour), [Graeco-Latin square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graeco-Latin_square), [Eulerian numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eulerian_numbers), and others. To solve the [Seven Bridges of Königsberg](wikipedia://en/192753) problem he invented [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), which also led to the formation of [topology](wikipedia://en/29954). Finally, he broke ground with [partitions](wikipedia://en/340240) by the use of [generating functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generating_functions).[^o30]

## Contemporary combinatorics {#Contemporary_combinatorics}

In the 19th century, the subject of [partially ordered sets](wikipedia://en/23572) and [lattice theory](wikipedia://en/469304) originated in the work of [Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106), [Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117), and [Schröder](wikipedia://en/448015). However, it was [Garrett Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/1180916)'s seminal work in his book *Lattice Theory* published in 1967,[^o32] and the work of [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) that truly established the subjects.[^o33] In the 1930s, [Hall](wikipedia://en/1655033) (1936) and [Weisner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Weisner) (1935) independently stated the general [Möbius inversion formula](wikipedia://en/20963).[^o34] In 1964, [Gian-Carlo Rota's](wikipedia://en/101891) *On the Foundations of Combinatorial Theory I. Theory of Möbius Functions* introduced [poset](wikipedia://en/23572) and [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) theory as theories in Combinatorics.[^o33] [Richard P. Stanley](wikipedia://en/1633397) has had a big impact in contemporary combinatorics for his work in [matroid theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matroid_theory),[^o35] for introducing Zeta polynomials,[^o36] for explicitly defining [Eulerian posets](wikipedia://en/26703335),[^o37] developing the theory of [binomial posets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_poset) along with Rota and Peter Doubilet,[^o38] and more. [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) made seminal contributions to combinatorics throughout the century, winning the [Wolf Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize) in part for these contributions.[^o39]
