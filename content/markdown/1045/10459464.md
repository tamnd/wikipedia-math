# Cerf theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cerf theory"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), at the junction of [singularity theory](wikipedia://en/581763) and [differential topology](wikipedia://en/8562), **Cerf theory** is the study of families of smooth real-valued functions

:::

$$f\colon M \to \mathbb{R}$$

on a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) $M$, their generic singularities and the topology of the subspaces these singularities define, as subspaces of the [function space](wikipedia://en/340630). The theory is named after [Jean Cerf](wikipedia://en/56530120), who initiated it in the late 1960s.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="An example"}

## An example {#An_example}

[Marston Morse](wikipedia://en/856523) proved that, provided $M$ is compact, any [smooth function](wikipedia://en/1408000) $f\colon M \to \mathbb{R}$ can be approximated by a [Morse function](wikipedia://en/348560). Thus, for many purposes, one can replace arbitrary functions on $M$ by Morse functions.

As a next step, one could ask, 'if you have a one-parameter family of functions which start and end at Morse functions, can you assume the whole family is Morse?' In general, the answer is no. Consider, for example, the one-parameter family of functions on $M=\mathbb R$ given by

$$f_t(x)=(1/3)x^3-tx.$$

At time $t=-1$, it has no critical points, but at time $t=1$, it is a Morse function with two critical points at $x=\pm 1$.

Cerf showed that a one-parameter family of functions between two Morse functions can be approximated by one that is Morse at all but finitely many degenerate times. The degeneracies involve a birth/death transition of critical points, as in the above example when, at $t=0$, an index 0 and index 1 critical point are created as $t$ increases.

:::

## A *stratification* of an infinite-dimensional space {#A_stratification_of_an_infinite-dimensional_space}

Returning to the general case where $M$ is a compact manifold, let $\operatorname{Morse}(M)$ denote the space of Morse functions on $M$, and $\operatorname{Func}(M)$ the space of real-valued smooth functions on $M$. Morse proved that $\operatorname{Morse}(M) \subset \operatorname{Func}(M)$ is an open and dense subset in the $C^\infty$ topology.

For the purposes of intuition, here is an analogy. Think of the Morse functions as the top-dimensional open stratum in a [stratification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologically_stratified_space) of $\operatorname{Func}(M)$ (we make no claim that such a stratification exists, but suppose one does). Notice that in stratified spaces, the [co-dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-dimension) 0 open stratum is open and dense. For notational purposes, reverse the conventions for indexing the stratifications in a stratified space, and index the open strata not by their dimension, but by their co-dimension. This is convenient since $\operatorname{Func}(M)$ is infinite-dimensional if $M$ is not a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742). By assumption, the open co-dimension 0 stratum of $\operatorname{Func}(M)$ is $\operatorname{Morse}(M)$, i.e.: $\operatorname{Func}(M)^0=\operatorname{Morse}(M)$. In a stratified space $X$, frequently $X^0$ is disconnected. The **essential property** of the co-dimension 1 stratum $X^1$ is that any path in $X$ which starts and ends in $X^0$ can be approximated by a path that intersects $X^1$ transversely in finitely many points, and does not intersect $X^i$ for any $i>1$.

Thus Cerf theory is the study of the positive co-dimensional strata of $\operatorname{Func}(M)$, i.e.: $\operatorname{Func}(M)^i$ for $i>0$. In the case of

$$f_t(x)=x^3-tx,$$

only for $t=0$ is the function not Morse, and

$$f_0(x)=x^3$$

has a cubic [degenerate critical point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_critical_point) corresponding to the birth/death transition.

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="A single time parameter, statement of theorem"}

## A single time parameter, statement of theorem {#A_single_time_parameter,_statement_of_theorem}

The [Morse Theorem](wikipedia://en/348560#Morse_lemma) asserts that if $f \colon M \to \mathbb R$ is a Morse function, then near a critical point $p$ it is conjugate to a function $g \colon \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R$ of the form

$$g(x_1,x_2,\dotsc,x_n) = f(p) + \epsilon_1 x_1^2 + \epsilon_2 x_2^2 + \dotsb + \epsilon_n x_n^2$$

where $\epsilon_i \in \{\pm 1\}$.

Cerf's one-parameter theorem asserts the **essential property** of the co-dimension one stratum.

Precisely, if $f_t \colon M \to \mathbb R$ is a one-parameter family of smooth functions on $M$ with $t \in [0,1]$, and $f_0, f_1$ Morse, then there exists a smooth one-parameter family $F_t \colon M \to \mathbb R$ such that $F_0 = f_0, F_1 = f_1$, $F$ is uniformly close to $f$ in the $C^k$-topology on functions $M \times [0,1] \to \mathbb R$. Moreover, $F_t$ is Morse at all but finitely many times. At a non-Morse time the function has only one degenerate critical point $p$, and near that point the family $F_t$ is conjugate to the family

$$g_t(x_1,x_2,\dotsc,x_n) = f(p) + x_1^3+\epsilon_1 tx_1 + \epsilon_2 x_2^2 + \dotsb + \epsilon_n x_n^2$$

where $\epsilon_i \in \{\pm 1\}, t \in [-1,1]$. If $\epsilon_1 = -1$ this is a one-parameter family of functions where two critical points are created (as $t$ increases), and for $\epsilon_1 = 1$ it is a one-parameter family of functions where two critical points are destroyed.

:::

## Origins {#Origins}

The [PL](wikipedia://en/2174184)-[Schoenflies problem](wikipedia://en/25148935) for $S^2 \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ was solved by [J. W. Alexander](wikipedia://en/9848523) in 1924. His proof was adapted to the [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) case by Morse and [Emilio Baiada](wikipedia://en/44228628).[^o8] The **essential property** was used by Cerf in order to prove that every orientation-preserving [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564) of [$S^3$](wikipedia://en/39792) is isotopic to the identity,[^o9] seen as a one-parameter extension of the Schoenflies theorem for $S^2 \subset \mathbb{R}^3$. The corollary [$\Gamma_4 = 0$](wikipedia://en/1114931) at the time had wide implications in differential topology. The **essential property** was later used by Cerf to prove the [pseudo-isotopy theorem](wikipedia://en/25051489)[^o10] for high-dimensional simply-connected manifolds. The proof is a one-parameter extension of [Stephen Smale](wikipedia://en/40294)'s proof of the [h-cobordism theorem](wikipedia://en/681049) (the rewriting of Smale's proof into the functional framework was done by Morse, and also by [John Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632)[^o11] and by Cerf, André Gramain, and [Bernard Morin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Morin)[^o12] following a suggestion of [René Thom](wikipedia://en/61633)).

Cerf's proof is built on the work of Thom and [John Mather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_N._Mather).[^o13] A useful modern summary of Thom and Mather's work from that period is the book of [Marty Golubitsky](wikipedia://en/41446307) and [Victor Guillemin](wikipedia://en/13236636).[^o14]

::: {#o16 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Beside the above-mentioned applications, [Robion Kirby](wikipedia://en/2932424) used Cerf Theory as a key step in justifying the [Kirby calculus](wikipedia://en/1105826).

:::

::: {#o18 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

A stratification of the complement of an infinite co-dimension subspace of the space of smooth maps $\{ f \colon M \to \mathbb{R} \}$ was eventually developed by Francis Sergeraert.[^o19]

During the seventies, the classification problem for pseudo-isotopies of non-simply connected manifolds was solved by [Allen Hatcher](wikipedia://en/1112366) and John Wagoner,[^o20] discovering [algebraic $K_i$](wikipedia://en/246748)-obstructions on $\pi_1 M$ ($i=2$) and $\pi_2 M$ ($i=1$) and by [Kiyoshi Igusa](wikipedia://en/62079633), discovering obstructions of a similar nature on $\pi_1 M$ ($i=3$).[^o21]

:::
