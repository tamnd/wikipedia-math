# Nyström method

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nyström method"}

In mathematics [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506), the **Nyström method**[^o2] or **quadrature method** seeks the [numerical solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_solution) of an [integral equation](wikipedia://en/474234) by replacing the integral with a representative weighted sum. The continuous problem is broken into $n$ discrete intervals; quadrature or [numerical integration](wikipedia://en/170089) determines the weights and locations of representative points for the integral.

:::

The problem becomes a [system of linear equations](wikipedia://en/113087) with $n$ equations and $n$ unknowns, and the underlying function is implicitly represented by an interpolation using the chosen quadrature rule. This discrete problem may be ill-conditioned, depending on the original problem and the chosen quadrature rule.

Since the linear equations require $O(n^3)$ operations to solve (with default methods such as [Gaussian elimination](wikipedia://en/13035#Computational_efficiency)), high-order quadrature rules perform better because low-order quadrature rules require large $n$ for a given accuracy. [Gaussian quadrature](wikipedia://en/50579) is normally a good choice for smooth, non-singular problems.

## Discretization of the integral {#Discretization_of_the_integral}

Standard quadrature methods seek to represent an integral as a weighed sum in the following manner:

$$\int_a^b h (x) \;\mathrm d x \approx \sum_{k=1}^n w_k h (x_k)$$

where $w_k$ are the weights of the quadrature rule, and points $x_k$ are the abscissas.

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Applying this to the inhomogeneous [Fredholm equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fredholm_equation) of the second kind

$$f (x) = \lambda u (x) - \int_a^b K (x, x') f (x') \;\mathrm d x',$$

results in

$$f (x) \approx \lambda u (x) - \sum_{k=1}^n w_k K (x, x_k) f (x_k).$$

:::
