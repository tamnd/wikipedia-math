# Hassler Whitney

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hassler Whitney"}

**Hassler Whitney** (March 23, 1907 – May 10, 1989) was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902). He was one of the founders of [singularity theory](wikipedia://en/581763), and did foundational work in [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470), [embeddings](wikipedia://en/52119), [immersions](wikipedia://en/6993953), [characteristic classes](wikipedia://en/363530) and, [geometric integration theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_integration_theory).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

### Life {#Life}

Hassler Whitney was born on March 23, 1907, in New York City, where his father, [Edward Baldwin Whitney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Baldwin_Whitney), was the First District [New York Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Supreme_Court) judge.[^o5] His mother, A. [Josepha Newcomb Whitney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josepha_Newcomb_Whitney), was an artist and political activist.[^o6] He was the paternal nephew of Connecticut Governor and Chief Justice [Simeon E. Baldwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simeon_E._Baldwin), his paternal grandfather was [William Dwight Whitney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Dwight_Whitney), professor of Ancient Languages at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University), linguist and [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) scholar.[^o6] Whitney was the great-grandson of Connecticut Governor and US Senator [Roger Sherman Baldwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Sherman_Baldwin), and the great-great-grandson of American founding father [Roger Sherman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Sherman). His maternal grandparents were astronomer and mathematician [Simon Newcomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Newcomb) (1835–1909), a [Steeves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steeves) descendant, and Mary Hassler Newcomb, granddaughter of the first superintendent of the Coast Survey [Ferdinand Rudolph Hassler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Rudolph_Hassler). His great uncle [Josiah Whitney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josiah_Whitney) was the first to survey [Mount Whitney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Whitney).[^o7]

He married three times: his first wife was Margaret R. Howell, married on the 30 May 1930. They had three children, James Newcomb, Carol and Marian. After his first divorce, on January 16, 1955, he married Mary Barnett Garfield. He and Mary had two daughters, Sarah Newcomb (later a notable statistician, [Sally Thurston](wikipedia://en/76055391)), and Emily Baldwin. Finally, Whitney divorced his second wife and married Barbara Floyd Osterman on 8 February 1986.

Whitney and his first wife Margaret made an innovative decision in 1939 that influenced the history of modern architecture in New England, when they commissioned the architect [Edwin B. Goodell, Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_B._Goodell,_Jr.) to design a new residence for their family in Weston, Massachusetts. They purchased a rocky hillside site on a historic road, next door to another International Style house by Goodell from several years earlier, designed for Richard and Caroline Field.

*Image: CannonMountain-WhitneyGilman.jpg.* The Whitney–Gilman ridge on Cannon Mountain

Throughout his life he pursued two particular hobbies with excitement: music and mountain-climbing. An accomplished player of the violin and the viola, Whitney played with the Princeton Musical Amateurs. He would run outside, 6 to 12 miles every other day. As an undergraduate, with his cousin Bradley Gilman, Whitney made the first ascent of the Whitney–Gilman ridge on [Cannon Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannon_Mountain_%28New_Hampshire%29), New Hampshire in 1929. It was the hardest and most famous rock climb in the East. He was a member of the Swiss Alpine Society and the Yale Mountaineering Society (the precursor to the Yale Outdoors Club) and climbed most of the mountain peaks in Switzerland.[^n1]

#### Death {#Death}

Three years after his third marriage, on 10 May 1989, Whitney died in Princeton,[^o10] after suffering a stroke.[^o11] In accordance with his wish, Hassler Whitney's ashes rest atop [mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain) [Dent Blanche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dent_Blanche) in Switzerland where Oscar Burlet, another mathematician and member of the [Swiss Alpine Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_Alpine_Club), placed them on August 20, 1989.[^o12]

### Academic career {#Academic_career}

Whitney attended [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University), where he received baccalaureate degrees in physics and in music, respectively in 1928 and in 1929.[^o6] Later, in 1932, he earned a [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD) in mathematics at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University).[^o6] His doctoral dissertation was *The Coloring of Graphs*, written under the supervision of [George David Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/30871906).[^o14][^o15] At Harvard, Birkhoff also got him a job as instructor of Mathematics for the years 1930–31,[^o16] and an Assistant Professorship for the years 1934–35.[^o17] Later on he held the following working positions: NRC Fellow, Mathematics, 1931–33; Assistant Professor, 1935–40; Associate Professor, 1940–46, Professor, 1946–52; Professor Instructor, [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179), [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University), 1952–77; professor emeritus, 1977–89; Chairman of the Mathematics Panel, [National Science Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation), 1953–56; Exchange Professor, [Collège de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collège_de_France), 1957; Memorial Committee, Support of Research in Mathematical Sciences, National Research Council, 1966–67; President, International Commission of Mathematical Instruction, 1979–82; Research Mathematician, [National Defense Research Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Defense_Research_Committee), 1943–45; Construction of the School of Mathematics.

He was a member of the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences); Colloquium Lecturer, [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822), 1946; Vice President, 1948–50 and Editor, American Journal of Mathematics, 1944–49; Editor, [Mathematical Reviews](wikipedia://en/1485646), 1949–54; Chairman of the Committee vis. lectureship, 1946–51; Committee Summer Instructor, 1953–54;, [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822); American National Council Teachers of Mathematics, [London Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/591097) (Honorary), Swiss Mathematics Society (Honorary), [Académie des Sciences de Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Académie_des_Sciences) (Foreign Associate); [New York Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Academy_of_Sciences).

### Honors {#Honors}

In 1947 he was elected member of the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society).[^o19] In 1969 he was awarded the [Lester R. Ford Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_R._Ford_Award) for the paper in two parts "*The mathematics of Physical quantities*" (1968a, 1968b).[^o20] In 1976 he was awarded the National Medal of Science. In 1980 he was elected honorary member of the [London Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/591097).[^o21] In 1982, he received the Wolf Prize from the [Wolf Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Foundation), and finally, in 1985, he was awarded the [Steele Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steele_Prize) from the American Mathematical Society.

## Work {#Work}

### Research {#Research}

Whitney's earliest work, from 1930 to 1933, was on [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401). Many of his contributions were to graph coloring, and the ultimate computer-assisted solution to the [four-color problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-color_problem) relied on some of his results. His work in graph theory culminated in a 1933 paper[^n2] where he laid the foundations for [matroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matroids), a fundamental notion in modern [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) and [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200) independently introduced by him and [Bartel Leendert van der Waerden](wikipedia://en/91390) in the mid-1930s.[^o25] In this paper Whitney proved several theorems about [the matroid of a graph](wikipedia://en/244321#Matroids_from_graph_theory) $M(G)$: one such theorem, now called Whitney's 2-Isomorphism Theorem, states: Given $G$ and $H$ are graphs with no isolated vertices. Then $M(G)$ and $M(H)$ are [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) if and only if $G$ and $H$ are 2-isomorphic (a concept previously introduced by Whitney).[^o26]

Whitney's lifelong interest in geometric properties of functions also began around this time. His earliest work in this subject was on the possibility of extending a function defined on a closed subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ to a function on all of $\mathbb{R}^n$ with certain smoothness properties. A complete solution to this problem was found only in 2005 by [Charles Fefferman](wikipedia://en/502831).

In a 1936 paper, Whitney gave a definition of a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) of class $C^r$ and proved that, for high enough values of *r*, a smooth manifold of dimension *n* may be [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119) in $\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}$, and [immersed](wikipedia://en/6993953) in $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$. (In 1944 he managed to reduce the dimension of the ambient space by 1, provided that *n* > 2, by a technique that has come to be known as the "[Whitney trick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitney_trick)".) This basic result shows that manifolds may be treated intrinsically or extrinsically, as we wish. The intrinsic definition had been published only a few years earlier in the work of [Oswald Veblen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Veblen) and [J. H. C. Whitehead](wikipedia://en/385179). These theorems opened the way for much more refined studies of embedding, immersion and also smoothing—that is, the possibility of having various [smooth structures](wikipedia://en/1542238) on a given [topological manifold](wikipedia://en/2119193).

He was one of the major developers of [cohomology theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_theory) and [characteristic classes](wikipedia://en/363530), as these concepts emerged in the late 1930s, and his work on algebraic topology continued into the 1940s. He also returned to the study of functions in the 1940s, continuing his work on the extension problems formulated a decade earlier, and answering a question of [Laurent Schwartz](wikipedia://en/317485) in a 1948 paper "On Ideals of Differentiable Functions".

Whitney had, throughout the 1950s, an almost unique interest in the topology of singular spaces and in singularities of smooth maps. An old idea, implicit even in the notion of a simplicial complex, was to study a singular space by decomposing it into smooth pieces (nowadays called "strata"). Whitney was the first to see any subtlety in this definition and pointed out that a good "stratification" should satisfy conditions he termed "A" and "B", now referred to as [Whitney conditions](wikipedia://en/9322242). The work of [René Thom](wikipedia://en/61633) and [John Mather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Mather_%28mathematician%29) in the 1960s showed that these conditions give a very robust definition of stratified space. The singularities in low dimension of smooth mappings, later to come to prominence in the work of René Thom, were also first studied by Whitney.

In his book *Geometric Integration Theory* he gives a theoretical basis for [Stokes' theorem](wikipedia://en/25441497) applied with singularities on the boundary.[^o27] Later, his work on such topics inspired the research of [Jenny Harrison](wikipedia://en/4673481).[^o28]

These aspects of Whitney's work have looked more unified, in retrospect and with the general development of singularity theory. Whitney's purely topological work ([Stiefel–Whitney classes](wikipedia://en/877761), basic results on [vector bundles](wikipedia://en/276524)) entered the mainstream more quickly.

### Teaching {#Teaching}

In 1967, he became involved full-time in educational problems, especially at the elementary school level. He spent many years in classrooms, both teaching mathematics and observing how it is taught.[^o30] He spent four months teaching pre-algebra mathematics to a classroom of seventh graders and conducted summer courses for teachers. He traveled widely to lecture on the subject in the United States and abroad. He worked toward removing *[mathematical anxiety](wikipedia://en/8111444),* which he felt leads young pupils to avoid mathematics. Whitney spread the ideas of teaching mathematics to students in ways that relate the content to their own lives as opposed to teaching them rote memorization.
