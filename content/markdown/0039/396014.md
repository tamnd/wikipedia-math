# Local zeta function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Local zeta function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **local zeta function** $Z(V, s)$ (sometimes called the **congruent zeta function** or the [Hasse–Weil zeta function](wikipedia://en/1157842)) is defined as

:::

$$Z(V, s) = \exp\left(\sum_{k = 1}^\infty \frac{N_k}{k} (q^{-s})^k\right)$$

where $V$ is a [non-singular](wikipedia://en/981915) $n$-dimensional [projective algebraic variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_algebraic_variety) over the field $\mathbf{F}_{q}$ with $q$ elements and $N_{k}$ is the number of points of $V$ defined over the finite field extension $\mathbf{F}_{q^{k}}$ of $\mathbf{F}_{q}$.[^o2]

Making the variable transformation $t = q^{-s},$ gives

$$\mathit{Z} (V,t) = \exp \left( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} N_k \frac{t^k}{k} \right)$$

as the [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012) in the variable $t$.

Equivalently, the local zeta function is sometimes defined as follows:

$$(1)\ \ \mathit{Z} (V,0) = 1 \,$$

$$(2)\ \ \frac{d}{dt} \log \mathit{Z} (V,t) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} N_k t^{k-1}\ .$$

In other words, the local zeta function $Z(V, t)$ with coefficients in the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) $\mathbf{F}_{q}$ is defined as a function whose [logarithmic derivative](wikipedia://en/470327) generates the number $N_{k}$ of solutions of the equation defining $V$ in the degree $k$ extension $\mathbf{F}_{q^{k}}.$

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Formulation"}

## Formulation {#Formulation}

Given a finite field *F*, there is, up to [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828), only one field *F<sub>k</sub>* with

$$[ F_k : F ] = k \,,$$

for *k* = 1, 2, ... . When *F* is the unique field with *q* elements, *F<sub>k</sub>* is the unique field with $q^k$ elements. Given a set of polynomial equations — or an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) *V* — defined over *F*, we can count the number

$$N_k \,$$

of solutions in *F<sub>k</sub>* and create the generating function

$$G(t) = N_1t +N_2t^2/2 + N_3t^3/3 +\cdots \,.$$

The correct definition for *Z*(*t*) is to set log *Z* equal to *G*, so

$$Z= \exp (G(t)) \,$$

and *Z*(0) = 1, since *G*(0) = 0, and *Z*(*t*) is *a priori* a [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012).

The [logarithmic derivative](wikipedia://en/470327)

$$Z'(t)/Z(t) \,$$

equals the generating function

$$G'(t) = N_1 +N_2t^1 + N_3t^2 +\cdots \,.$$

:::

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

For example, assume all the *N<sub>k</sub>* are 1; this happens for example if we start with an equation like *X* = 0, so that geometrically we are taking *V* to be a point. Then

$$G(t) = -\log(1 - t)$$

is the expansion of a logarithm (for |*t*| \< 1). In this case we have

$$Z(t) = \frac{1}{(1 - t)}\ .$$

To take something more interesting, let *V* be the [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578) over *F*. If *F* has *q* elements, then this has *q* + 1 points, including the one [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139). Therefore, we have

$$N_k = q^k + 1$$

and

$$G(t) = -\log(1 - t) -\log(1 - qt)$$

for |*t*| small enough, and therefore

$$Z(t) = \frac{1}{(1 - t)(1 - qt)}\ .$$

The first study of these functions was in the 1923 dissertation of [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351). He obtained results for the case of a [hyperelliptic curve](wikipedia://en/499361), and conjectured the further main points of the theory as applied to curves. The theory was then developed by [F. K. Schmidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._K._Schmidt) and [Helmut Hasse](wikipedia://en/727118).[^o7] The earliest known nontrivial cases of local zeta functions were implicit in [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125)'s *[Disquisitiones Arithmeticae](wikipedia://en/736713)*, article 358. There, certain particular examples of [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225) over finite fields having [complex multiplication](wikipedia://en/537183) have their points counted by means of [cyclotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclotomy).[^o8]

For the definition and some examples, see also.[^o9]

:::

## Motivations {#Motivations}

The relationship between the definitions of *G* and *Z* can be explained in a number of ways. (See for example the infinite product formula for *Z* below.) In practice it makes *Z* a [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210) of *t*, something that is interesting even in the case of *V* an [elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/10225) over a finite field.

The local *Z* zeta functions are multiplied to get global *$\zeta$* zeta functions,

$$\zeta = \prod Z$$

These generally involve different finite fields (for example the whole family of fields **Z**/*p***Z** as *p* runs over all [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666)).

In these fields, the variable *t* is substituted by *p<sup>−s</sup>*, where *s* is the complex variable traditionally used in [Dirichlet series](wikipedia://en/393258). (For details see [Hasse–Weil zeta function](wikipedia://en/1157842).)

The global products of *Z* in the two cases used as examples in the previous section therefore come out as $\zeta(s)$ and $\zeta(s)\zeta(s-1)$ after letting $q=p$.

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Riemann hypothesis for curves over finite fields"}

## Riemann hypothesis for curves over finite fields {#Riemann_hypothesis_for_curves_over_finite_fields}

For projective curves *C* over *F* that are [non-singular](wikipedia://en/253260#Singularities), it can be shown that

$$Z(t) = \frac{P(t)}{(1 - t)(1 - qt)}\ ,$$

with *P*(*t*) a polynomial, of degree 2*g*, where *g* is the [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) of *C*. Rewriting

$$P(t)=\prod^{2g}_{i=1}(1-\omega_i t)\ ,$$

the **Riemann hypothesis for curves over finite fields** states

$$|\omega_i|=q^{1/2}\ .$$

For example, for the elliptic curve case there are two roots, and it is easy to show the absolute values of the roots are *q*<sup>1/2</sup>. [Hasse's theorem](wikipedia://en/1108758) is that they have the same absolute value; and this has immediate consequences for the number of points.

[André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019) proved this for the general case, around 1940 (*Comptes Rendus* note, April 1940): he spent much time in the years after that [writing](wikipedia://en/26402563) up the [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) involved. This led him to the general [Weil conjectures](wikipedia://en/244705). [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) developed [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) theory for the purpose of resolving these. A generation later [Pierre Deligne](wikipedia://en/394544) completed the proof. (See [étale cohomology](wikipedia://en/396231) for the basic formulae of the general theory.)

:::

::: {#o14 .statement cue=named-heading name="General formulas for the zeta function"}

## General formulas for the zeta function {#General_formulas_for_the_zeta_function}

It is a consequence of the [Lefschetz trace formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lefschetz_trace_formula) for the [Frobenius morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_morphism) that

$$Z(X,t)=\prod_{i=0}^{2\dim X}\det\big(1-t \mbox{Frob}_q |H^i_c(\overline{X},{\mathbb Q}_\ell)\big)^{(-1)^{i+1}}.$$

Here $X$ is a separated scheme of finite type over the finite field *F* with $q$ elements, and Frob<sub>q</sub> is the geometric Frobenius acting on $\ell$-adic étale cohomology with compact supports of $\overline{X}$, the lift of $X$ to the algebraic closure of the field *F*. This shows that the zeta function is a rational function of $t$.

An infinite product formula for $Z(X, t)$ is

$$Z(X, t)=\prod\ (1-t^{\deg(x)})^{-1}.$$

Here, the product ranges over all closed points *x* of *X* and deg(*x*) is the degree of *x*. The local zeta function *Z(X, t)* is viewed as a function of the complex variable *s* via the change of variables *q<sup>−s</sup>*.

In the case where *X* is the variety *V* discussed above, the closed points are the equivalence classes *x=\[P\]* of points *P* on $\overline{V}$, where two points are equivalent if they are conjugates over *F*. The degree of *x* is the degree of the field extension of *F* generated by the coordinates of *P*. The logarithmic derivative of the infinite product *Z(X, t)* is easily seen to be the generating function discussed above, namely

$$N_1 +N_2t^1 + N_3t^2 +\cdots \,.$$

:::
