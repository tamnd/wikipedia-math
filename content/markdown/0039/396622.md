# Polish space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Polish space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Polish space** is a [separable](wikipedia://en/27855), [completely metrizable](wikipedia://en/7975189) [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450); i.e., a space [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to a [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) that has a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) subset. Polish spaces are so named because they were first extensively studied by [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland) topologists and logicians, such as [Sierpiński](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpiński), [Kuratowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuratowski), [Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) and others.

:::

Polish spaces are mostly studied today because they are the primary setting for [descriptive set theory](wikipedia://en/396619), including the study of [Borel equivalence relations](wikipedia://en/2123692). Polish spaces are also a convenient setting for more advanced [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory), in particular in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542).

Common examples of Polish spaces are the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line), the [Baire space](wikipedia://en/2078245), [Cantor spaces](wikipedia://en/352376) and [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989). Additionally, some spaces that are not complete metric spaces in the usual metric may be Polish: for example, [open intervals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_interval) are Polish.

Any two [uncountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountable) Polish spaces are [Borel isomorphic](wikipedia://en/33605690). In particular, every uncountable Polish space has the [cardinality of the continuum](wikipedia://en/1574901).

**[Lusin spaces](wikipedia://en/396622#Lusin_spaces)**, **[Suslin spaces](wikipedia://en/396622#Suslin_spaces)**, and **[Radon spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon_space#Radon_spaces)** are generalizations of Polish spaces.

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

1. Every Polish space is [second countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_countable) (by virtue of being separable and metrizable).[^n1]
1. A subspace $Q$ of a Polish space $P$ is Polish (under the induced topology) if and only if $Q$ is the intersection of a sequence of open subsets of $P$ (i.e., $Q$ is a $G_\delta$-set).[^n2]
1. [Cantor–Bendixson theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantor–Bendixson_theorem): if $X$ is Polish then any closed subset of $X$ can be written as the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) of a [perfect set](wikipedia://en/5075551) and a countable set. Moreover, if $X$ is uncountable, it can be written as the disjoint union of a perfect set and a countable open set.
1. Every Polish space is homeomorphic to a $G_\delta$-subset of the [Hilbert cube](wikipedia://en/216389) $[0,1]^\mathbb{N}$.[^n3]

The following spaces are Polish:

- closed subsets of a Polish space,
- open subsets of a Polish space,
- products and disjoint unions of countable families of Polish spaces,
- [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) spaces that are metrizable and [countable at infinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-compact_space),
- countable intersections of Polish subspaces of a Hausdorff topological space,
- the set of [irrational numbers](wikipedia://en/20647689) with the topology [induced by](wikipedia://en/910926) the standard topology of the real line.

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Characterization"}

## Characterization {#Characterization}

There are many known conditions for a second-countable topological space is metrizable, such as [Urysohn's metrization theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urysohn's_metrization_theorem). The problem of determining whether a metrizable space is completely metrizable is more difficult. Topological spaces such as the open unit interval $(0,1)$ can be given both complete metrics and incomplete metrics generating their topology.

There is a characterization of complete separable metric spaces in terms of a [game](wikipedia://en/2596030#Games) known as the strong [Choquet game](wikipedia://en/43265794). A separable metric space is completely metrizable if and only if the second player has a [winning strategy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winning_strategy) in this game.

A second characterization follows from Alexandrov's theorem, which states that a separable metric space is completely metrizable if and only if it is a $G_\delta$ subset of its completion in the original metric.

:::

## Polish metric spaces {#Polish_metric_spaces}

Although Polish spaces are metrizable, they are not in and of themselves [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018); each Polish space admits many [complete metrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_metric) giving rise to the same topology, but no one of these is singled out or distinguished. A Polish space with a distinguished complete metric is called a **Polish metric space**. An alternative approach, equivalent to the one given here, is first to define "Polish metric space" to mean "complete separable metric space", and then to define a "Polish space" as the topological space obtained from a Polish metric space by [forgetting](wikipedia://en/844783) the metric.

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### Lusin spaces {#Lusin_spaces}

A [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) topological space is a **Lusin space** (named after [Nikolai Lusin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Lusin)) if some stronger topology makes it into a Polish space.

There are many ways to form Lusin spaces. In particular:

- Every Polish space is a Lusin space.[^n4]
- A subspace of a Lusin space is a Lusin space if and only if it is a Borel set.[^n5]
- Any countable union or intersection of Lusin subspaces of a [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637) is a Lusin space.[^n6]
- The product of a countable number of Lusin spaces is a Lusin space.[^n7]
- The disjoint union of a countable number of Lusin spaces is a Lusin space.[^n7]

::: {#o19 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Souslin space"}

### Suslin spaces {#Suslin_spaces}

A Hausdorff topological space is a **Suslin space** (named after [Mikhail Suslin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Suslin)) if it is the image of a Polish space under a continuous mapping. Thus, every Lusin space is Suslin. A subset of a Polish space is a Suslin space if and only if it is a [Suslin set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suslin_set) (an image of the [Suslin operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suslin_operation)).[^n8]

The following are Suslin spaces:

- closed or open subsets of a Suslin space,
- countable products and disjoint unions of Suslin spaces,
- countable intersections or countable unions of Suslin subspaces of a Hausdorff topological space,
- continuous images of Suslin spaces,
- Borel subsets of a Suslin space.

Notably, every Suslin space is [separable](wikipedia://en/27855).

:::

### Radon spaces {#Radon_spaces}

A **Radon space** (named after [Johann Radon](wikipedia://en/339331)) is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) on which every [Borel](wikipedia://en/4322) [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) is [inner regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_regular_measure). Since a probability measure is globally finite, and hence [locally finite](wikipedia://en/6621919), every probability measure on a Radon space is also a [Radon measure](wikipedia://en/521933). In particular, a [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) complete [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) is a Radon space.

Every Suslin space is a Radon space.

:::

## Polish groups {#Polish_groups}

A **Polish group** is a topological group that is also a Polish space; in other words, a group homeomorphic to a separable complete metric space. There are several classic results of [Banach](wikipedia://en/29646), [Freudenthal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Freudenthal) and [Kuratowski](wikipedia://en/46075) on homomorphisms between Polish groups.[^n9] Firstly, if $G$ and $H$ are separable metric spaces with $G$ Polish, then any Borel homomorphism from $G$ to $H$ is continuous.[^n10][^n11] Secondly, there is a version of the [open mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/17395276) or the [closed graph theorem](wikipedia://en/497834) due to Kuratowski:[^n12] a continuous surjective homomorphism of a Polish group onto another Polish group is an open mapping; a homomorphism between Polish groups is continuous if and only if its graph is closed. As a result, it is a remarkable fact about Polish groups that Baire-measurable mappings (i.e., for which the preimage of any open set has the [property of Baire](wikipedia://en/2213768)) that are homomorphisms between them are automatically continuous.[^n13] The group of homeomorphisms of the [Hilbert cube](wikipedia://en/216389) $[0,1]^\mathbb{N}$ is a universal Polish group, in the sense that every Polish group is isomorphic to a closed subgroup of it.

Examples of Polish groups are

- all finite dimensional [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945) with a countable number of components,
- the [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) of a separable [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) (with the [strong operator topology](wikipedia://en/458699)),
- the group of homeomorphisms of a compact metric space,
- the product of a countable number of Polish groups,
- the group of [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777) of a separable complete metric space.
