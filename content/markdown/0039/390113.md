# Zipf–Mandelbrot law

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Zipf–Mandelbrot law"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **Zipf–Mandelbrot law** is a [discrete probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_probability_distribution). Also known as the [Pareto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vilfredo_Pareto)–Zipf law, it is a [power-law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power-law) distribution on [ranked data](wikipedia://en/1482394), named after the [linguist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics) [George Kingsley Zipf](wikipedia://en/768770), who suggested a simpler distribution called [Zipf's law](wikipedia://en/43218), and the mathematician [Benoit Mandelbrot](wikipedia://en/3999), who subsequently generalized it.

:::

The [probability mass function](wikipedia://en/154725) is given by

$$f(k; N, q, s) = \frac{1}{H_{N,q,s}} \frac{1}{(k + q)^s},$$

where $H_{N,q,s}$ is given by

$$H_{N,q,s} = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{(i + q)^s},$$

which may be thought of as a generalization of a [harmonic number](wikipedia://en/214729). In the formula, $k$ is the rank of the data, and $q$ and $s$ are parameters of the distribution. In the limit as $N$ approaches infinity, this becomes the [Hurwitz zeta function](wikipedia://en/406952) $\zeta(s, q)$. For finite $N$ and $q = 0$ the Zipf–Mandelbrot law becomes [Zipf's law](wikipedia://en/43218). For infinite $N$ and $q = 0$ it becomes a [zeta distribution](wikipedia://en/115742).

::: {#o3 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The distribution of words ranked by their [frequency](wikipedia://en/4839019) in a random [text corpus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Text_corpus) is approximated by a [power-law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power-law) distribution, known as [Zipf's law](wikipedia://en/43218).

If one plots the frequency rank of words contained in a moderately sized corpus of text data versus the number of occurrences or actual frequencies, one obtains a [power-law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power-law) distribution, with [exponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponent) close to one (but see Powers, 1998 and Gelbukh & Sidorov, 2001). Zipf's law implicitly assumes a fixed vocabulary size, but the [Harmonic series](wikipedia://en/214729) with *s* = 1 does not converge, while the Zipf–Mandelbrot generalization with *s* > 1 does. Furthermore, there is evidence that the closed class of functional words that define a language obeys a Zipf–Mandelbrot distribution with different parameters from the open classes of contentive words that vary by topic, field and register.[^o4]

In ecological field studies, the [relative abundance distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_abundance_distribution) (i.e. the graph of the number of species observed as a function of their abundance) is often found to conform to a Zipf–Mandelbrot law.[^o5]

Within music, many metrics of measuring "pleasing" music conform to Zipf–Mandelbrot distributions.[^o6]
