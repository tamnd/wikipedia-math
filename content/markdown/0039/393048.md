# Rigged Hilbert space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rigged Hilbert space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), a **rigged Hilbert space** (**Gelfand triple**, **nested Hilbert space**, **equipped Hilbert space**) is a construction which can enlarge a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) to a bigger space containing additional objects which are not in the Hilbert space but which one would like to think of alongside the Hilbert space. For example, in the [quantum mechanical description of a non-relativistic particle](wikipedia://en/59874) using the Hilbert space of [square-integrable functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square-integrable_functions) on the real line, eigenstates of the [position](wikipedia://en/4838571) and [momentum](wikipedia://en/2288549) operators are not in the Hilbert space, but are in a suitably defined rigged Hilbert space. Informally, the term "rigged" means that the Hilbert space has been equipped to do more than it otherwise could, in analogy with [rigging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigging) a boat.[^o2]

:::

This construction is designed to link the [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) and [square-integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square-integrable) aspects of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180). Such spaces were introduced to study [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713). They bring together the '[bound state](wikipedia://en/696912)' ([eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector)) and '[continuous spectrum](wikipedia://en/976834)', in one place.

Using this notion, a version of the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495) for [unbounded operators](wikipedia://en/1422584) on Hilbert space can be formulated. "Rigged Hilbert spaces are well known as the structure which provides a proper mathematical meaning to the [Dirac formulation of quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/4542)."[^o4]

## Motivation {#Motivation}

A function such as

$$x \mapsto e^{ix} ,$$

is an [eigenfunction](wikipedia://en/228601) of the [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886)

$$-i\frac{d}{dx}$$

on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) $\mathbf{R}$, but isn't [square-integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square-integrable) for the usual ([Lebesgue](wikipedia://en/18198)) measure on $\mathbf{R}$. To properly consider this function as an eigenfunction requires some way of stepping outside the strict confines of the [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) theory. This was supplied by the apparatus of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29), and a *generalized eigenfunction* theory was developed in the years after 1950.[^n1]

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A **rigged Hilbert space** is a pair $(H, \Phi)$ with $H$ a Hilbert space, $\Phi$ a dense subspace, such that $\Phi$ is given a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) structure for which the [inclusion map](wikipedia://en/492516)

$$i : \Phi \to H,$$

is continuous.[^n2][^n3] Identifying $H$ with its dual space $H^{*}$, the adjoint to $i$ is the map

$$i^* : H = H^* \to \Phi^*.$$

The duality pairing between $\Phi$ and $\Phi^{*}$ is then compatible with the inner product on $H$, in the sense that:

$$\langle u, v\rangle_{\Phi\times\Phi^*} = (u, v)_H$$

whenever $u \in \Phi\subset H$ and $v \in H = H^* \subset \Phi^*$. In the case of complex Hilbert spaces, we use a Hermitian inner product; it will be complex linear in $u$ (math convention) or $v$ (physics convention), and conjugate-linear (complex anti-linear) in the other variable.

The triple $(\Phi,\,\,H,\,\,\Phi^*)$ is often named the *Gelfand triple* (after [Israel Gelfand](wikipedia://en/364643)). $H$ is referred to as a pivot space.

Note that even though $\Phi$ is isomorphic to $\Phi^{*}$ (via [Riesz representation](wikipedia://en/26452)) if it happens that $\Phi$ is a Hilbert space in its own right, this isomorphism is *not* the same as the composition of the inclusion $i$ with its adjoint $i*$

$$i^* i: \Phi\subset H = H^* \to \Phi^*.$$

### Functional analysis approach {#Functional_analysis_approach}

The concept of rigged Hilbert space places this idea in an abstract functional-analytic framework. Formally, a rigged Hilbert space consists of a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) $H$, together with a subspace $\Phi$ which carries a [finer topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finer_topology), that is one for which the natural inclusion

$$\Phi \subseteq H$$

is continuous. It is [no loss](wikipedia://en/296000) to assume that $\Phi$ is [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in $H$ for the Hilbert norm. We consider the inclusion of [dual spaces](wikipedia://en/7988) $H^{*}$ in $\Phi^{*}$. The latter, dual to $\Phi$ in its 'test function' topology, is realised as a space of distributions or generalised functions of some sort, and the [linear functionals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) on the subspace $\Phi$ of type

$$\phi\mapsto\langle v,\phi\rangle$$

for $v$ in $H$ are faithfully represented as distributions (because we assume $\Phi$ dense).

Now by applying the [Riesz representation theorem](wikipedia://en/26452) we can identify $H^{*}$ with $H$. Therefore, the definition of *rigged Hilbert space* is in terms of a sandwich:

$$\Phi \subseteq H \subseteq \Phi^*.$$

The most significant examples are those for which $\Phi$ is a [nuclear space](wikipedia://en/2702957); this comment is an abstract expression of the idea that $\Phi$ consists of test functions and $\Phi*$ of the corresponding [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29).

An example of a nuclear countably Hilbert space $\Phi$ and its dual $\Phi^*$ is the [Schwartz space](wikipedia://en/4277567) $\mathcal S(\mathbb R)$ and the space of [tempered distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tempered_distributions) $\mathcal S'(\mathbb R)$, respectively, rigging the Hilbert space of [square-integrable functions](wikipedia://en/28680558). As such, the rigged Hilbert space is given by[^n4]

$$\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}) \subset L^2 (\mathbb{R}) \subset \mathcal{S}'(\mathbb{R}).$$

Another example is given by [Sobolev spaces](wikipedia://en/611964): Here (in the simplest case of Sobolev spaces on $\mathbb R^n$)

$$H = L^2(\mathbb R^n),\ \Phi = H^s(\mathbb R^n),\ \Phi^* = H^{-s}(\mathbb R^n),$$

where $s > 0$.
