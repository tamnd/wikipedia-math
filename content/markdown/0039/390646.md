# Flag (linear algebra)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Flag"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), particularly in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), a **flag** is an increasing [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of [subspaces](wikipedia://en/56357) of a [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V*. Here "increasing" means each is a proper subspace of the next (see [filtration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filtration_%28abstract_algebra%29)):

:::

$$\{0\} = V_0 \subset V_1 \subset V_2 \subset \cdots \subset V_k = V.$$

The term *flag* is motivated by a particular example resembling a [flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag): the zero point, a line, and a plane correspond to a nail, a staff, and a sheet of fabric.[^o2]

If we write that dim*V*<sub>*i*</sub> = *d*<sub>*i*</sub> then we have

$$0 = d_0 < d_1 < d_2 < \cdots < d_k = n,$$

where *n* is the [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28linear_algebra%29) of *V* (assumed to be finite). Hence, we must have *k* ≤ *n*. A flag is called a **complete flag** if *d*<sub>*i*</sub> = *i* for all *i*, otherwise it is called a **partial flag**.

A partial flag can be obtained from a complete flag by deleting some of the subspaces. Conversely, any partial flag can be completed (in many different ways) by inserting suitable subspaces.

The **signature** of the flag is the sequence (*d*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *d*<sub>*k*</sub>).

## Bases {#Bases}

An ordered [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for *V* is said to be **adapted** to a flag *V*<sub>0</sub> ⊂ *V*<sub>1</sub> ⊂ ... ⊂ *V*<sub>*k*</sub> if the first *d*<sub>*i*</sub> basis vectors form a basis for *V*<sub>*i*</sub> for each 0 ≤ *i* ≤ *k*. Standard arguments from linear algebra can show that any flag has an adapted basis.

Any ordered basis gives rise to a complete flag by letting the *V*<sub>*i*</sub> be the [span](wikipedia://en/56353) of the first *i* basis vectors. For example, the **standard flag** in **R**<sup>*n*</sup> is induced from the [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005) (*e*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *e*<sub>*n*</sub>) where *e*<sub>*i*</sub> denotes the vector with a 1 in the *i*th entry and 0's elsewhere. Concretely, the standard flag is the sequence of subspaces:

$$0 < \left\langle e_1\right\rangle < \left\langle e_1,e_2\right\rangle < \cdots < \left\langle e_1,\ldots,e_n \right\rangle = K^n.$$

An adapted basis is almost never unique (the counterexamples are trivial); see below.

A complete flag on an [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856) has an essentially unique [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063): it is unique up to multiplying each vector by a unit (scalar of unit length, e.g. 1, −1, *i*). Such a basis can be constructed using the [Gram-Schmidt process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram-Schmidt_process). The uniqueness up to units follows [inductively](wikipedia://en/18881), by noting that $v_i$ lies in the one-dimensional space $V_{i-1}^\perp \cap V_i$.

More abstractly, it is unique up to an action of the [maximal torus](wikipedia://en/360136): the flag corresponds to the [Borel group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_group), and the inner product corresponds to the [maximal compact subgroup](wikipedia://en/1496726).[^o4]

## Stabilizer {#Stabilizer}

The [stabilizer subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stabilizer_subgroup) of the standard flag is the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122) [upper triangular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_triangular) [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859).

More generally, the stabilizer of a flag (the [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operators) $T$ on *V* such that $T(V_i) = V_i$ for all *i*) is, in matrix terms, the [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) of block [upper triangular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_triangular) matrices (with respect to an adapted basis), where the block sizes are $d_i-d_{i-1}$.[^o6]p. 7 The stabilizer subgroup of a complete flag is the set of invertible upper triangular matrices with respect to any basis adapted to the flag. The subgroup of [lower triangular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_triangular) matrices with respect to such a basis depends on that basis, and can therefore *not* be characterized in terms of the flag only.

The stabilizer subgroup of any complete flag is a [Borel subgroup](wikipedia://en/588356) (of the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564)), and the stabilizer of any partial flags is a [parabolic subgroup](wikipedia://en/588356#Parabolic_subgroups).

The stabilizer subgroup of a flag acts [simply transitively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_transitive) on adapted bases for the flag, and thus these are not unique unless the stabilizer is trivial. That is a very exceptional circumstance: it happens only for a vector space of dimension 0, or for a vector space over $\mathbf{F}_2$ of dimension 1 (precisely the cases where only one basis exists, independently of any flag).

## Subspace nest {#Subspace_nest}

In an infinite-dimensional space *V*, as used in [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), the flag idea generalises to a **subspace nest**, namely a collection of subspaces of *V* that is a [total order](wikipedia://en/30330) for [inclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusion_%28set_theory%29) and which further is closed under arbitrary [intersections](wikipedia://en/23476429) and closed linear spans. See [nest algebra](wikipedia://en/460461).

## Set-theoretic analogs {#Set-theoretic_analogs}

From the point of view of the [field with one element](wikipedia://en/13798040), a set can be seen as a vector space over the field with one element: this formalizes various analogies between [Coxeter groups](wikipedia://en/297004) and [algebraic groups](wikipedia://en/247924).

Under this correspondence, an ordering on a set corresponds to a maximal flag: an ordering is equivalent to a maximal filtration of a set. For instance, the filtration (flag) $\{0\} \subset \{0,1\} \subset \{0,1,2\}$ corresponds to the ordering $(0,1,2)$.
