# Stark–Heegner theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Stark–Heegner theorem"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), the **Heegner theorem** or **Stark-Heegner theorem**[^o2] establishes the complete list of the [quadratic imaginary number fields](wikipedia://en/454781) whose [rings of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) are [principal ideal domains.](wikipedia://en/60546) It solves a special case of Gauss's [class number problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_number_problem_for_imaginary_quadratic_fields) of determining the number of imaginary quadratic fields that have a given fixed [class number](wikipedia://en/150907).

:::

Let $\mathbf{Q}$ denote the set of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), and let $d$ be a [square-free integer](wikipedia://en/29525). The field $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ is a [quadratic extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_extension) of $\mathbf{Q}$. The [class number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_number_%28number_theory%29) of $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ is one [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the ring of integers of $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ is a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039). The Baker–Heegner–Stark theorem can then be stated as follows:

If $d < 0$, then the class number of $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ is one if and only if $d \in \{\, -1, -2, -3, -7, -11, -19, -43, -67, -163\,\}.$

These are known as the [Heegner numbers](wikipedia://en/522147).

By replacing $d$ with the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/3022963) $D$ of $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ this list is often written as:[^o3]

$$D \in\{ -3, -4, -7, -8, -11, -19, -43, -67, -163\}.$$

## History {#History}

This result was first conjectured by [Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) in Section 303 of his *[Disquisitiones Arithmeticae](wikipedia://en/736713)* (1798). It was essentially proven by [Kurt Heegner](wikipedia://en/621799) in 1952,[^o5] but Heegner's proof was not accepted until an academic mathematician [Harold Stark](wikipedia://en/788807) published a proof[^o6] in 1967 which had many commonalities to Heegner's work, though Stark considers the proofs to be different.[^o7] Heegner "died before anyone really understood what he had done".[^o8] In (Stark 1969a) Stark works through Heegner's proof to highlight what the gap in Heegner’s proof consisted of; other contemporary papers produced various similar proofs using modular functions.[^o10] (Heegner's paper dealt mainly with the [congruent number](wikipedia://en/2334769) problem, also using modular functions.[^o11])

[Alan Baker](wikipedia://en/18950800)'s slightly earlier 1966 proof used completely different principles which reduced the result to a finite amount of computation, with Stark's 1963/4 thesis already providing this computation; Baker won the [Fields Medal](wikipedia://en/10859) for his methods. Stark later pointed out that Baker's proof, involving linear forms in 3 logarithms, could be reduced to a statement about only 2 logarithms which was already known from 1949 by Gelfond and Linnik.[^o12]

Stark's 1969 paper (Stark 1969a) also cited the 1895 text by [Weber](wikipedia://en/2643615) and noted that if Weber had "only made the observation that the reducibility of \[a certain equation\] would lead to a [Diophantine equation](wikipedia://en/9109), the class-number one problem would have been solved 60 years ago". [Bryan Birch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryan_Birch) notes that Weber's book, and essentially the whole field of modular functions, dropped out of interest for half a century: "Unhappily, in 1952 there was no one left who was sufficiently expert in Weber's *Algebra* to appreciate Heegner's achievement."[^o14] Stark's 1969 paper can be seen as a good argument for calling the result **Heegner's Theorem**.

In the immediate years after Stark,[^o15] Deuring,[^o16] Siegel,[^o17] and Chowla[^o18] all gave slightly variant proofs by [modular functions](wikipedia://en/286000#Modular_functions). Other versions in this genre have also cropped up over the years. For instance, in 1985, Monsur Kenku gave a proof using the [Klein quartic](wikipedia://en/390976) (though again utilizing modular functions).[^o19] And again, in 1999, Imin Chen gave another variant proof by modular functions (following Siegel's outline).[^o20]

The work of Gross and Zagier (1986) (Gross Zagier 1986) combined with that of Goldfeld (1976) also gives an alternative proof.[^o22]

## Real case {#Real_case}

On the other hand, it is unknown whether there are infinitely many *d* > 0 for which $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ has class number 1. Computational results indicate that there are many such fields. [Number Fields with class number one](wikipedia://en/22779719#Quadratic_number_fields) provides a list of some of these.
