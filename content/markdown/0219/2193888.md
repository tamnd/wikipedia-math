# Force-free magnetic field

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Force-free magnetic field"}

*Image: Picturing the Sun’s Magnetic Field (25513266790).jpg.* The magnetic field in the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun)'s [corona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_corona) is often approximated as a force-free field.

In [plasma physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_physics), a **force-free magnetic field** is a [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563) in which the [Lorentz force](wikipedia://en/18631) is equal to zero and the [magnetic pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_pressure) greatly exceeds the plasma [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) such that non-magnetic forces can be neglected. For a force-free field, the [electric current density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current_density) is either zero or parallel to the magnetic field.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

When a magnetic field is approximated as force-free, all non-magnetic forces are neglected and the [Lorentz force](wikipedia://en/18631) vanishes. For non-magnetic forces to be neglected, it is assumed that the ratio of the plasma [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) to the [magnetic pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_pressure)—the [plasma *β*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_%28plasma_physics%29)—is much less than one, i.e., $\beta \ll 1$. With this assumption, magnetic pressure dominates over plasma pressure such that the latter can be ignored. It is also assumed that the magnetic pressure dominates over other non-magnetic forces, such as [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity), so that these forces can similarly be ignored.

In [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI) units, the Lorentz force condition for a static magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ can be expressed as

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{B} &= \mathbf{0}, \\ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} &= 0, \end{aligned}$$

where

$$\mathbf{j} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\nabla \times \mathbf{B}$$

is the [current density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_density) and $\mu_0$ is the [vacuum permeability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_permeability). Alternatively, this can be written as

$$\begin{aligned} \left(\nabla \times \mathbf{B}\right) \times \mathbf{B} &= \mathbf{0}, \\ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} &= 0. \end{aligned}$$

These conditions are fulfilled when the current vanishes or is parallel to the magnetic field.[^o4]

### Zero current density {#Zero_current_density}

If the current density is identically zero, then the magnetic field is the gradient of a [magnetic scalar potential](wikipedia://en/2395496) $\phi$:

$$\mathbf{B} = -\nabla\phi.$$

The substitution of this into $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$ results in [Laplace's equation](wikipedia://en/36941), $\nabla^2\phi = 0,$ which can often be readily solved, depending on the precise boundary conditions. In this case, the field is referred to as a *potential field* or *vacuum magnetic field*.

### Nonzero current density {#Nonzero_current_density}

If the current density is not zero, then it must be parallel to the magnetic field, i.e., $\mu_0 \mathbf{j} = \alpha \mathbf{B}$ where $\alpha$ is a scalar function known as the *force-free parameter* or *force-free function*. This implies that

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla \times \mathbf{B} &= \alpha\mathbf{B}, \\ \mathbf{B} \cdot \nabla\alpha &= 0. \end{aligned}$$

The force-free parameter can be a function of position but must be constant along field lines.

::: {#o8 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Linear force-free field"}

#### Linear force-free field {#Linear_force-free_field}

When the force-free parameter $\alpha$ is constant everywhere, the field is called a *linear force-free field* (LFFF). A constant $\alpha$ allows for the derivation of a vector [Helmholtz equation](wikipedia://en/1156215)

$$\nabla^2\mathbf{B} = -\alpha^2 \mathbf{B}$$

by taking the curl of the nonzero current density equations above.

:::

::: {#o10 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="NLFFF"}

#### Nonlinear force-free field {#Nonlinear_force-free_field}

When the force-free parameter $\alpha$ depends on position, the field is called a *nonlinear force-free field* (NLFFF). In this case, the equations do not possess a general solution, and usually must be solved numerically.[^o4][^o11][^o12]

:::

:::

## Physical examples {#Physical_examples}

In the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun)'s upper [chromosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromosphere) and lower [corona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_corona), the plasma *β* can locally be of order 0.01 or lower allowing for the magnetic field to be approximated as force-free.[^o4][^o14][^o15][^o16]
