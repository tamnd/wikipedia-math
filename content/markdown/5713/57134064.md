# Aggregated distribution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Aggregated distribution"}

*Image: Aggregated distribution of parasites on hosts.svg.* Parasites are distributed very unevenly among their hosts, most hosts having no parasites, and a few hosts having many. This [skewed](wikipedia://en/28212) distribution makes sampling difficult and requires careful use of statistics.

An **aggregated distribution**, commonly found among [predators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predator), [parasites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasite), and [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant) is a highly uneven ([skewed](wikipedia://en/28212)) [statistical distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_distribution) pattern in which they collect or aggregate in regions. These regions may be widely separated, particularly within animal distributions that are influenced by prey or [host](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_%28biology%29) densities. This distribution makes sampling difficult and invalidates commonly used [parametric statistics](wikipedia://en/223366). A similar pattern is found among predators that search for their prey.[^o2]

:::

## In predators {#In_predators}

When predators need to search for their prey, they could search at random, as has been assumed in models such as those made by Lotka in 1925 and Volterra in 1928. This would imply that they scatter themselves evenly across the environment.[^o2] However, prey may be concentrated at high densities in some areas and scarce elsewhere. The zoologists M. P. Hassell and R. M. May noted that predators and parasites, too, might aggregate themselves where prey was abundant, choosing some response curve: they observed for example that [redshanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_redshank) (predatory birds) adopted a sigmoid (s-shaped) response to the density of *[Corophium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corophium)* ([amphipod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphipod)) prey per square metre of [mudflats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mudflat). They noted, too, that several different behaviours of predators or parasites could cause them to aggregate selectively in areas where prey are at high density: they could be attracted by a volatile substance liberated by prey or the plants they are feeding on; they could choose to spend more time in areas where they have caught prey, as many predators appear to do; or, predators could follow an individual predator which had located prey, as is seen in feeding [terns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sterna). Aggregated distributions of predators where they tend to spend time in areas where prey are concentrated have the effect of stabilising prey populations; when the time to travel between such concentrated areas is high; and when the prey populations too are more highly clumped.[^o2]

## In parasites {#In_parasites}

Parasite aggregation with respect to hosts is, according to Robert Poulin "a defining feature of [metazoan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metazoa) parasite populations."[^o5] The main reason for this is probably either that some hosts are more exposed to parasites, or that they are more susceptible to them.[^o5] Aggregation is seen in directly transmitted parasites (those not using a [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28epidemiology%29)) from many groups: ectoparasites like [lice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louse) and mites, marine parasites like [copepods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copepod) and [cyamid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyamidae) [amphipods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphipod), and many kinds of nematode, fungi, protozoa, bacteria, and viruses. It is widespread, too, in trophically transmitted parasites of animals. The observed pattern is that most individual [hosts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_%28biology%29) are free or almost free of parasites, while a minority carry a large number of parasites.[^o6]

### Quantitative ecology {#Quantitative_ecology}

Aggregated distribution of parasites across their hosts poses considerable problems for students of parasite ecology, as it renders [parametric statistics](wikipedia://en/223366) as commonly used by biologists invalid. [Log-transformation](wikipedia://en/10056274#Logarithmic_transformation) of data before the application of parametric test, or the use of [non-parametric statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-parametric_statistics) is recommended by several authors, but this can give rise to further problems, so quantitative parasitology is based on more advanced biostatistical methods.[^o8]

## In plants {#In_plants}

Intraspecific aggregation within a plant [community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_%28ecology%29) increases competition and crowding, altering competitive interactions among and between species. Aggregated distributions tend to decrease performance of dominant plant species, while benefiting the performance of competitively inferior species.[^o10] Aggregation can exacerbate the effect of [density-dependent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_dependence) mortality, further benefiting otherwise inferior species by providing space and resources for the species growth. The presence of density-dependent mortality has been shown to shift distributions away from an aggregated pattern to a random or repulsion distribution pattern[^o11]
