# Domain (mathematical analysis)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Domain"}

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), a **domain** or **region** is a [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566), [connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_%28topology%29), and [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) in a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450). In particular, it is any non-empty connected open [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of the [real coordinate space](wikipedia://en/1482083) $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ or the [complex coordinate space](wikipedia://en/39125683) $\mathbf{C}^{n}$. A connected open subset of [coordinate space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_space) is frequently used for the [domain of a function](wikipedia://en/50263).[^o2]

:::

The basic idea of a connected subset of a space dates from the 19th century, but precise definitions vary slightly from generation to generation, author to author, and edition to edition, as concepts developed and terms were translated between German, French, and English works. In English, some authors use the term *domain*,[^o3] some use the term *region*,[^o4] some use both terms interchangeably,[^o5] and some define the two terms slightly differently;[^o6] some avoid ambiguity by sticking with a phrase such as *non-empty connected open subset*.[^o7]

::: {#o9 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Conventions"}

## Conventions {#Conventions}

One common convention is to define a *domain* as a connected open set but a *region* as the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of a domain with none, some, or all of its [limit points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point).[^o10] A **closed region** or **closed domain** is the union of a domain and all of its limit points.

Various degrees of smoothness of the [boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_%28mathematics%29) of the domain are required for various properties of functions defined on the domain to hold, such as integral theorems ([Green's theorem](wikipedia://en/243334), [Stokes theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stokes_theorem)), properties of [Sobolev spaces](wikipedia://en/611964), and to define [measures](wikipedia://en/19873) on the boundary and spaces of [traces](wikipedia://en/11199199) (generalized functions defined on the boundary). Commonly considered types of domains are domains with [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) boundary, [Lipschitz boundary](wikipedia://en/7820612), [$C^{1}$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) boundary, and so forth.

A **bounded domain** is a domain that is [bounded](wikipedia://en/48258), i.e., contained in some ball. **Bounded region** is defined similarly. An **exterior domain** or **external domain** is a domain whose [complement](wikipedia://en/54347) is bounded; sometimes smoothness conditions are imposed on its boundary.

In [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), a **complex domain** (or simply **domain**) is any connected open subset of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) $\mathbf{C}$. For example, the entire complex plane is a domain, as is the open [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738), the open [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826), and so forth. Often, a complex domain serves as the [domain of definition](wikipedia://en/50263) for a [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110). In the study of [several complex variables](wikipedia://en/360243), the definition of a domain is extended to include any connected open subset of $\mathbf{C}^{n}$.

In [Euclidean spaces](wikipedia://en/9697), [one-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-dimensional), [two-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-dimensional), and [three-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-dimensional) regions are [curves](wikipedia://en/89246), [surfaces](wikipedia://en/4342970), and [solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_%28mathematics%29), whose extent are called, respectively, *[length](wikipedia://en/1898401)*, *[area](wikipedia://en/1209)*, and *[volume](wikipedia://en/32498)*.

:::

## Historical notes {#Historical_notes}

According to [Hans Hahn](wikipedia://en/1075212),[^o13] the concept of a domain as an open connected set was introduced by [Constantin Carathéodory](wikipedia://en/470625) in his famous book (Carathéodory 1918). In this definition, Carathéodory considers obviously [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566) [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359) sets. Hahn also remarks that the word "*Gebiet*" ("*Domain*") was occasionally previously used as a [synonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synonym) of [open set](wikipedia://en/39358).[^o15] The rough concept is older. In the 19th and early 20th century, the terms *domain* and *region* were often used informally (sometimes interchangeably) without explicit definition.[^o16]

However, the term "domain" was occasionally used to identify closely related but slightly different concepts. For example, in his influential [monographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monograph) on [elliptic partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/662787), [Carlo Miranda](wikipedia://en/49974918) uses the term "region" to identify an open connected set,[^o17][^o18] and reserves the term "domain" to identify an internally connected,[^o19] [perfect set](wikipedia://en/5075551), each point of which is an accumulation point of interior points,[^o17] following his former master [Mauro Picone](wikipedia://en/10162009):[^o20] according to this convention, if a set $A$ is a region then its [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) $\overline{A}$ is a domain.[^o17]
