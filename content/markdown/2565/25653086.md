# Active placebo

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Active placebo"}

An **active placebo** is a [placebo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placebo) that produces noticeable side effects that may convince the person being treated that they are receiving a legitimate treatment, rather than an inert placebo.

:::

## Nomenclature {#Nomenclature}

According to a 1965 paper,[^o3] the term **"concealed placebo"** (German: Kaschiertes Placebo) was suggested in a 1959 paper published in German.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

An example of an active placebo is the 1964 work of Shader and colleagues who used a combination of low-dose phenobarbital plus atropine to mimic the sedation and dry mouth produced by phenothiazines.

[Morphine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphine) and [gabapentin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabapentin) are painkillers with the common side effects of sleepiness and dizziness. In a 2005 study assessing the effects of these painkillers on [neuropathic pain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuropathic_pain), [lorazepam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorazepam) was chosen as an active placebo because it is not a painkiller but it does cause sleepiness and can cause dizziness.[^o7]

Testing from the late 1950s onwards on narcotic analgesics like morphine also has used [dicyclomine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dicyclomine) as an active placebo, and on some occasions it was reported to cause the Straub mouse tail reaction, as do most narcotics. [Clonidine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clonidine) is now finding more use as an active placebo for narcotics.

:::
