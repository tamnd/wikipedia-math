# Gravitational wave

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gravitational wave"}

*Image: Warped Space and Time Around Colliding Black Holes (Courtesy Caltech-MIT-LIGO Laboratory, produced by SXS project).webm.* As two [black holes](wikipedia://en/4650) orbit closer to one another, they emit gravitational waves, the frequency of which increases to a peak as the black holes coalesce.

**Gravitational waves** are [waves](wikipedia://en/33516) of [spacetime curvature](wikipedia://en/1016422) produced by the relative [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion) of [gravitating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) [masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) and which propagate away at the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light). They were first predicted by [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736)[^o2][^o3] as a consequence of his [general theory of relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_theory_of_relativity), appearing as "ripples in spacetime curvature".[^o4][^o5][^o6] [Hundreds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_gravitational_wave_observations) of these gravitational waves have since then been observed, first [indirectly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulse–Taylor_pulsar) using [binary-pulsar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_pulsar) observations[^o7] and, since 2015,[^o8] directly through [dedicated observatories.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIGO_Scientific_Collaboration)

:::

Gravitational waves transport energy as **gravitational radiation**, a form of [radiant energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiant_energy) similar to [electromagnetic radiation](wikipedia://en/9426).[^o9] [Newton's law of universal gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation), part of [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), does not provide for their existence, instead asserting that gravity has instantaneous effect everywhere. Gravitational waves therefore stand as an important relativistic phenomenon that is absent from Newtonian physics.

[Gravitational-wave astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational-wave_astronomy) has the advantage that, unlike electromagnetic radiation, gravitational waves are not affected by intervening matter. Sources that can be studied this way include [binary star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_star) systems composed of [white dwarfs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_dwarf), [neutron stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star),[^o10][^o11] and [black holes](wikipedia://en/4650); events such as [supernovae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernova); and the formation of the [early universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronology_of_the_universe) shortly after the [Big Bang](wikipedia://en/4116).

The first indirect evidence for the existence of gravitational waves came in 1974 from the observed orbital decay of the [Hulse–Taylor binary pulsar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulse–Taylor_pulsar), which matched the decay predicted by general relativity for energy lost to gravitational radiation. In 1993, [Russell Alan Hulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Alan_Hulse) and [Joseph Hooton Taylor Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Hooton_Taylor_Jr.) received the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics) for this discovery.

The first [direct observation of gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_observation_of_gravitational_waves) was made in September 2015, when a signal generated by the merger of two black holes was received by the [LIGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIGO) [gravitational wave detectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_wave_detector) in Livingston, Louisiana, and in Hanford, Washington. The 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics was subsequently awarded to [Rainer Weiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainer_Weiss), [Kip Thorne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kip_Thorne) and [Barry Barish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Barish) for their role in the direct detection of gravitational waves.

## Introduction {#Introduction}

In [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736)'s [general theory of relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_theory_of_relativity), gravity is treated as a phenomenon resulting from the [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) of [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758),[^o13] which is caused by the presence of mass.[^o14] (See: [Stress–energy tensor](wikipedia://en/70671)) If the masses move, the curvature of spacetime changes;[^o15] and if the motion is not spherically symmetric, it can emit propagating curvature disturbances[^o15]—i.e., gravitational waves[^o16][^o17][^o18][^o19]—which radiate outward at the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light).[^o20]

A passing gravitational wave alters the relative separation of [freely falling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_fall) masses, reflecting a disturbance in local spacetime curvature. The separation varies in a pattern that mirrors the [waveform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waveform) of the disturbance. The magnitude of this effect is [inversely proportional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inversely_proportional) to the distance (not distance squared) from the source.[^o21]

Inspiraling [binary neutron stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star#Binary_neutron_star_systems) are predicted to be a powerful source of gravitational waves as they [coalesce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star_merger), due to the very large acceleration of their masses as they [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit) close to one another. However, due to the astronomical distances to these sources, the effects when measured on Earth are predicted to be very small, having strains of less than 1 part in 10<sup>20</sup>.

Scientists demonstrate the existence of these waves with highly-sensitive detectors at multiple observation sites. , the [LIGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIGO) and [Virgo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgo_interferometer) observatories were the most sensitive detectors, operating at resolutions of about one part in 5 × 10<sup>22</sup>.[^o22] The Japanese detector [KAGRA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KAGRA) was completed in 2019; its first joint detection with LIGO and VIRGO was reported in 2021.[^o23] Another European ground-based detector, the [Einstein Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_Telescope), is under development. A space-based observatory, the [Laser Interferometer Space Antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_Interferometer_Space_Antenna) (LISA), is also being developed by the [European Space Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Space_Agency).

*Image: Quadrupol Wave.gif.* Linearly polarized gravitational wave

Gravitational waves do not strongly interact with matter in the way that electromagnetic radiation does.[^o100] This allows for the observation of events involving exotic objects in the distant universe that cannot be observed with more traditional means such as [optical telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_telescope) or [radio telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_telescope); accordingly, [gravitational wave astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_wave_astronomy) gives new insights into the workings of the universe.[^o100]

In particular, gravitational waves could be of interest to [cosmologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmology) as they offer a possible way of observing the very early universe. This is not possible with conventional astronomy, since before [recombination](wikipedia://en/26244469) the universe was opaque to electromagnetic radiation.[^o24] Precise measurements of gravitational waves will also allow scientists to test more thoroughly the general theory of relativity.

In principle, gravitational waves can exist at any frequency. Very low frequency waves can be detected using [pulsar timing arrays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsar_timing_array). In this technique, the timing of approximately 100 pulsars spread widely across the [Milky Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way) is monitored over the course of years. Detectable changes in the arrival time of their signals can result from passing gravitational waves generated by merging [supermassive black holes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supermassive_black_hole) (SMBH) with wavelengths measured in [light-years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-year). These timing changes can be used to locate the source of the waves.[^o25]

Using this technique, astronomers have discovered the 'hum' of various SMBH mergers occurring in the universe. [Stephen Hawking](wikipedia://en/19376148) and [Werner Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Israel) list different frequency bands for gravitational waves that could plausibly be detected, ranging from 10<sup>−7</sup> Hz up to 10<sup>11</sup> Hz.[^o26]

## Speed of gravity {#Speed_of_gravity}

The speed of gravitational waves in the [general theory of relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_theory_of_relativity) is equal to the *[speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light)* in vacuum, $c$.[^o28] Within the theory of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962), the constant $c$ is not only about light; instead it is the highest possible speed for any interaction in nature. Formally, $c$ is a conversion factor for changing the unit of time to the unit of space.[^o29] This makes it the only speed which does not depend either on the motion of an observer or a source of light and/or gravity.

Thus, the speed of "light" is also the speed of gravitational waves, and, further, the speed of any massless particle. Such particles include the [gluon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluon) (carrier of the strong force), the [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) that make up light (hence carrier of electromagnetic force), and the hypothetical [gravitons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graviton) (which are the presumptive field particles associated with gravity; however, an understanding of the graviton, if any exist, requires an as-yet unavailable theory of [quantum gravity](wikipedia://en/25312)).

In August 2017, the [LIGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIGO) and Virgo detectors received a gravitational wave signal, [GW170817](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GW170817), at nearly the same time as gamma ray satellites and optical telescopes received signals from its source in galaxy [NGC 4993](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NGC_4993), about 130 million light years away.[^o30] This measurement constrained the experimental difference between the speed of gravitational waves and light to be smaller than one part in 10<sup>15</sup>.[^o31]

## History {#History}

*Image: History of the Universe.svg.* Primordial gravitational waves are hypothesized to arise from [cosmic inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation_%28cosmology%29), a phase of [accelerated expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accelerated_expansion) just after the [Big Bang](wikipedia://en/4116) (2014).[^o55][^o56][^o33]

The possibility of gravitational waves and that those might travel at the speed of light was discussed in 1893 by [Oliver Heaviside](wikipedia://en/22831), using the analogy between the inverse-square law of gravitation and the [electrostatic force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb's_law).[^o34] In 1905, [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) proposed gravitational waves, emanating from a body and propagating at the speed of light, as being required by the Lorentz transformations[^o35] and suggested that, in analogy to an accelerating [electrical charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_charge) producing [electromagnetic waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_wave), accelerated masses in a relativistic field theory of gravity should produce gravitational waves.[^o36][^o37]

In 1915 Einstein published his [general theory of relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), a complete relativistic theory of gravitation. He conjectured, like Poincaré, that the equation would produce gravitational waves, but, as he mentions in a letter to Schwarzschild in February 1916,[^o37] these could not be similar to electromagnetic waves. Electromagnetic waves can be produced by dipole motion, requiring both a positive and a negative charge. Gravitation has no equivalent to negative charge. Einstein continued to work through the complexity of the equations of general relativity to find an alternative wave model. The result was published in June 1916,[^o3] and there he came to the conclusion that the gravitational wave must propagate with the speed of light, and there must, in fact, be three types of gravitational waves dubbed longitudinal–longitudinal, transverse–longitudinal, and transverse–transverse by [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544).[^o37]

However, the nature of Einstein's approximations led many (including Einstein himself) to doubt the result. In 1922, [Arthur Eddington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Eddington) showed that two of Einstein's types of waves were artifacts of the coordinate system he used, and could be made to propagate at any speed by choosing appropriate coordinates, leading Eddington to jest that they "propagate at the speed of thought".[^o38] This also cast doubt on the physicality of the third (transverse–transverse) type that Eddington showed always propagate at the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) regardless of coordinate system. In 1936, Einstein and [Nathan Rosen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Rosen) submitted a paper to *[Physical Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_Review)* in which they claimed gravitational waves could not exist in the full general theory of relativity because any such solution of the field equations would have a singularity. The journal sent their manuscript to be reviewed by [Howard P. Robertson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_P._Robertson), who anonymously reported that the singularities in question were simply the harmless coordinate singularities of the employed cylindrical coordinates. Einstein, who was unfamiliar with the concept of peer review, angrily withdrew the manuscript, never to publish in *Physical Review* again. Nonetheless, his assistant [Leopold Infeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_Infeld), who had been in contact with Robertson, convinced Einstein that the criticism was correct, and the paper was rewritten with the opposite conclusion and published elsewhere.[^o37][^o38] In 1956, [Felix Pirani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Pirani) remedied the confusion caused by the use of various coordinate systems by rephrasing the gravitational waves in terms of the manifestly observable [Riemann curvature tensor](wikipedia://en/130526).[^o39]

At the time, Pirani's work was overshadowed by the community's focus on a different question: whether gravitational waves could transmit [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy). This matter was settled by a thought experiment proposed by [Richard Feynman](wikipedia://en/25523) during the first "GR" conference at [Chapel Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_North_Carolina_at_Chapel_Hill) in 1957. In short, his argument known as the "[sticky bead argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sticky_bead_argument)" notes that if one takes a rod with beads then the effect of a passing gravitational wave would be to move the beads along the rod; friction would then produce heat, implying that the passing wave had done [work](wikipedia://en/149861). Shortly after, [Hermann Bondi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Bondi) published a detailed version of the "sticky bead argument".[^o37] This later led to a series of articles (1959 to 1989) by Bondi and Pirani that established the existence of [plane wave solutions for gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_plane_wave).[^o40]

[Paul Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742) further postulated the existence of gravitational waves, declaring them to have "physical significance" in his 1959 lecture at the [Lindau Meetings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindau_Nobel_Laureate_Meetings).[^o41] Further, it was Dirac who predicted gravitational waves with a well-defined energy density in 1964.[^o42]

After the Chapel Hill conference, [Joseph Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Weber) started designing and building the first gravitational wave detectors now known as [Weber bars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weber_bar). In 1969, Weber claimed to have detected the first gravitational waves, and by 1970 he was "detecting" signals regularly from the [Galactic Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galactic_Center); however, the frequency of detection soon raised doubts on the validity of his observations as the implied rate of energy loss of the [Milky Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way) would drain the galaxy of energy on a timescale much shorter than its inferred age. These doubts were strengthened when, by the mid-1970s, repeated experiments from other groups building their own Weber bars across the globe failed to find any signals, and by the late 1970s consensus was that Weber's results were spurious.[^o37]

In the same period, the first indirect evidence of gravitational waves was discovered. In 1974, [Russell Alan Hulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Alan_Hulse) and [Joseph Hooton Taylor, Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Hooton_Taylor,_Jr.) discovered the [first binary pulsar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulse–Taylor_binary), which earned them the 1993 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics).[^o43] Pulsar timing observations over the next decade showed a gradual decay of the orbital period of the Hulse–Taylor pulsar that matched the loss of energy and angular momentum in gravitational radiation predicted by general relativity.[^o44][^o45][^o37]

This indirect detection of gravitational waves motivated further searches, despite Weber's discredited result. Some groups continued to improve Weber's original concept, while others pursued the detection of gravitational waves using laser interferometers. The idea of using a laser interferometer for this seems to have been floated independently by various people, including M.E. Gertsenshtein and V. I. Pustovoit in 1962,[^o46] and Vladimir B. Braginskiĭ in 1966. The first prototypes were developed in the 1970s by [Robert L. Forward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_L._Forward) and Rainer Weiss.[^o47][^o48] In the decades that followed, ever more sensitive instruments were constructed, culminating in the construction of [GEO600](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GEO600), [LIGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIGO), and [Virgo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgo_interferometer).[^o37]

After years of producing null results, improved detectors became operational in 2015. On 11 February 2016, the [LIGO-Virgo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIGO_Scientific_Collaboration) collaborations announced the [first observation of gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_observation_of_gravitational_waves),[^o49][^o50][^o51][^o52] from a signal (dubbed [GW150914](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GW150914)) detected at 09:50:45 GMT on 14 September 2015 of two black holes with masses of 29 and 36 [solar masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_mass) merging about 1.3 billion light-years away. During the final fraction of a second of the merger, it released more than 50 times the [power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_%28physics%29) of all the stars in the observable universe combined.[^o53] The signal increased in frequency from 35 to 250 Hz over 10 cycles (5 orbits) as it rose in strength for a period of 0.2 seconds.[^o50] The mass of the new merged black hole was 62 solar masses. Energy equivalent to three solar masses was emitted as gravitational waves.[^o54] The signal was seen by both LIGO detectors in Livingston and Hanford, with a time difference of 7 milliseconds due to the angle between the two detectors and the source. The signal came from the [Southern Celestial Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Celestial_Hemisphere), in the rough direction of (but much farther away than) the [Magellanic Clouds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magellanic_Clouds).[^o52] The confidence level of this being an observation of gravitational waves was 99.99994%.[^o54]

A year earlier, the BICEP2 collaboration claimed that they had detected the imprint of gravitational waves in the [cosmic microwave background](wikipedia://en/7376). However, they were later forced to retract this result.[^o55][^o56][^o57][^o58]

In 2017, the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics) was awarded to [Rainer Weiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainer_Weiss), [Kip Thorne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kip_Thorne) and [Barry Barish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Barish) for their role in the detection of gravitational waves.[^o59][^o60][^o61]

In 2023, results from [pulsar timing arrays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsar_timing_array) were announced suggesting evidence of a [gravitational wave background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_wave_background).[^o62] Complex analysis of radio observations over 15 years of 67 pulsars showed correlations that matches the [Hellings-Downs curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellings-Downs_curve), consistent with a stochastic gravitational wave background.[^o63][^o64] Similar results are published by [European Pulsar Timing Array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Pulsar_Timing_Array), who claimed a [$3\sigma$-significance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-sigma_rule).[^o65][^o66] The frequency dependence of this background radiation differs from models of the expected source, merging supermassive black hole binaries and other sources cannot yet be ruled out.[^o67]

## Effects of passing {#Effects_of_passing}

*Image: GravitationalWave PlusPolarization.gif.* The effect of a plus-polarized gravitational wave on a ring of particles

*Image: GravitationalWave CrossPolarization.gif.* The effect of a cross-polarized gravitational wave on a ring of particles

Gravitational waves are constantly passing [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228); however, even the strongest have a minuscule effect since their sources are generally at a great distance. For example, the waves given off by the cataclysmic final merger of [GW150914](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GW150914) reached Earth after travelling over a billion [light-years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-year), as a ripple in [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) that changed the length of a 4 km LIGO arm by a thousandth of the width of a [proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton), proportionally equivalent to changing the distance to the [nearest star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_Centauri) outside the Solar System by one hair's width.[^o69] This tiny effect from even extreme gravitational waves makes them observable on Earth only with the most sophisticated detectors.

The effects of a passing gravitational wave, in an extremely exaggerated form, can be visualized by imagining a perfectly flat region of [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) with a group of motionless test particles lying in a plane, e.g., the surface of a computer screen. As a gravitational wave passes through the particles along a line perpendicular to the plane of the particles, i.e., following the observer's line of vision into the screen, the particles will follow the distortion in spacetime, oscillating in a "[cruciform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruciform)" manner, as shown in the animations. The area enclosed by the test particles does not change and there is no motion along the direction of propagation.

The oscillations depicted in the animation are exaggerated for the purpose of discussion – in reality a gravitational wave has a very small [amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude) (as formulated in [linearized gravity](wikipedia://en/2865024)). However, they help illustrate the kind of oscillations associated with gravitational waves as produced by a pair of masses in a [circular orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_orbit). In this case the amplitude of the gravitational wave is constant, but its plane of [polarization](wikipedia://en/41564) changes or rotates at twice the orbital rate, so the time-varying gravitational wave size, or 'periodic spacetime strain', exhibits a variation as shown in the animation.[^o70] If the orbit of the masses is elliptical then the gravitational wave's amplitude also varies with time according to Einstein's [quadrupole formula](wikipedia://en/44190891).[^o3]

As with other [waves](wikipedia://en/33516), there are a number of characteristics used to describe a gravitational wave:

- Amplitude: Usually denoted *h*, this is the size of the wave – the fraction of stretching or squeezing in the animation. The amplitude shown here is roughly *h* = 0.5 (or 50%). Gravitational waves passing through the Earth are many [sextillion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sextillion) times weaker than this – *h* ≈ 10<sup>−20</sup>.
- [Frequency](wikipedia://en/10779): Usually denoted *f*, this is the frequency with which the wave oscillates (1 divided by the amount of time between two successive maximum stretches or squeezes)
- [Wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125): Usually denoted *λ*, this is the distance along the wave between points of maximum stretch or squeeze.
- [Speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed): This is the speed at which a point on the wave (for example, a point of maximum stretch or squeeze) travels. For gravitational waves with small amplitudes, this [wave speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_gravity) is equal to the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) (*c*).

The speed, wavelength, and frequency of a gravitational wave are related by the equation *c* = *λf*, just like the equation for a [light wave](wikipedia://en/9426#Wave_model). For example, the animations shown here oscillate roughly once every two seconds. This would correspond to a frequency of 0.5 Hz, and a wavelength of about 600 000 km, or 47 times the diameter of the Earth.

In the above example, it is assumed that the wave is [linearly polarized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_polarization) with a "plus" polarization, written *h*<sub>\+</sub>. Polarization of a gravitational wave is just like polarization of a light wave except that the polarizations of a gravitational wave are 45 degrees apart, as opposed to 90 degrees.[^o71] In particular, in a "cross"-polarized gravitational wave, *h*<sub>×</sub>, the effect on the test particles would be basically the same, but rotated by 45 degrees, as shown in the second animation. Just as with light polarization, the polarizations of gravitational waves may also be expressed in terms of [circularly polarized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_polarization) waves. Gravitational waves are polarized because of the nature of their source.

## Sources {#Sources}

*Image: The Gravitational wave spectrum Sources and Detectors.jpg.* The gravitational wave spectrum with sources and detectors. *Credit: NASA Goddard Space Flight Center*[^o73]

In general terms, gravitational waves are radiated by large, coherent motions of immense mass, especially in regions where gravity is so strong that [Newtonian gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_gravity) begins to fail.[^o74]

The effect does not occur in a purely spherically symmetric system.[^o75] A simple example of this principle is a spinning [dumbbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumbbell). If the dumbbell spins around its axis of symmetry, it will not radiate gravitational waves; if it tumbles end over end, as in the case of two planets orbiting each other, it will radiate gravitational waves. The heavier the dumbbell, and the faster it tumbles, the greater is the gravitational radiation it will give off. In an extreme case, such as when the two weights of the dumbbell are massive stars like neutron stars or black holes, orbiting each other quickly, then significant amounts of gravitational radiation would be given off.

Some more detailed examples:

- Two objects orbiting each other, as a planet would orbit the Sun, *will* radiate.
- A spinning non-axisymmetric planetoid – say with a large bump or dimple on the equator – *will* radiate.
- A [supernova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernova) *will* radiate except in the unlikely event that the explosion is perfectly symmetric.
- An isolated non-spinning solid object moving at a constant velocity *will not* radiate. This can be regarded as a consequence of the principle of [conservation of linear momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_conservation_of_linear_momentum).
- A spinning disk *will not* radiate. This can be regarded as a consequence of the principle of [conservation of angular momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_angular_momentum). However, it *will* show [gravitomagnetic](wikipedia://en/24794048) effects.
- A spherically pulsating spherical star (non-zero monopole moment or [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass), but zero quadrupole moment) *will not* radiate, in agreement with [Birkhoff's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birkhoff's_theorem_%28relativity%29).

More technically, the second time derivative of the [quadrupole moment](wikipedia://en/44190891) (or the *l*-th time derivative of the *l*-th [multipole moment](wikipedia://en/1425534)) of an isolated system's [stress–energy tensor](wikipedia://en/70671) must be non-zero in order for it to emit gravitational radiation. This is analogous to the changing dipole moment of charge or current that is necessary for the emission of [electromagnetic radiation](wikipedia://en/9426).

### Binaries {#Binaries}

*Image: orbit2.gif.* Two stars of dissimilar mass are in [circular orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_orbits). Each revolves about their common [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961) (denoted by the small red cross) in a circle with the larger mass having the smaller orbit.

*Image: orbit1.gif.* Two stars of similar mass in circular orbits about their center of mass

*Image: orbit5.gif.* Two stars of similar mass in highly [elliptical orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptical_orbit) about their center of mass

Gravitational waves carry energy away from their sources and, in the case of orbiting bodies, this is associated with an in-spiral or decrease in orbit.[^o77][^o78] Imagine for example a simple system of two masses – such as the Earth–Sun system – moving slowly compared to the speed of light in circular orbits. Assume that these two masses orbit each other in a circular orbit in the *x*–*y* plane. To a good approximation, the masses follow simple Keplerian [orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit). However, such an orbit represents a changing [quadrupole moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrupole#Gravitational_quadrupole). That is, the system will give off gravitational waves.

In theory, the loss of energy through gravitational radiation could eventually drop the Earth into the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun). However, the total energy of the Earth orbiting the Sun ([kinetic energy](wikipedia://en/17327) + [gravitational potential energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_potential_energy)) is about 1.14 [joules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joules) of which only 200 [watts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt) (joules per second) is lost through gravitational radiation, leading to a [decay in the orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_decay) by about 1 meters per day or roughly the diameter of a [proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton). At this rate, it would take the Earth approximately 3 times more than the current [age of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_the_universe) to spiral onto the Sun. This estimate overlooks the decrease in *r* over time, but the radius varies only slowly for most of the time and plunges at later stages, as $r(t)=r_0\left(1-\frac{t}{t_\text{coalesce}} \right)^{1/4},$ with $r_0$ the initial radius and $t_\text{coalesce}$ the total time needed to fully coalesce.[^o79]

More generally, the rate of orbital decay can be approximated by[^o80]

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\mathrm{d}t} = - \frac{64}{5}\, \frac{G^3}{c^5}\, \frac{(m_1m_2)(m_1+m_2)}{r^3}\ ,$$

where *r* is the separation between the bodies, *t* time, *G* the [gravitational constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_constant), *c* the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light), and *m*<sub>1</sub> and *m*<sub>2</sub> the masses of the bodies. This leads to an expected time to merger of [^o80]

$$t= \frac{5}{256}\, \frac{c^5}{G^3}\, \frac{r^4}{(m_1m_2)(m_1+m_2)}.$$

#### Compact binaries {#Compact_binaries}

[Compact stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_star) like [white dwarfs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_dwarf) and [neutron stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star) can be constituents of binaries. For example, a pair of [solar mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_mass) neutron stars in a circular orbit at a separation of 1.89 m (189,000 km) has an orbital period of 1,000 seconds, and an expected lifetime of 1.30 seconds or about 414,000 years. Such a system could be observed by [LISA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_Interferometer_Space_Antenna) if it were not too far away. A far greater number of white dwarf binaries exist with orbital periods in this range. White dwarf binaries have [masses in the order of the Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_mass), and diameters in the order of the Earth. They cannot get much closer together than 10,000 km before they will [merge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_collision) and explode in a [supernova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_Ia_supernova#Double_degenerate_progenitors) which would also end the emission of gravitational waves. Until then, their gravitational radiation would be comparable to that of a neutron star binary.

*Image: Artist’s impression of merging neutron stars.jpg.* Artist's impression of merging neutron stars, a source of gravitational waves[^o82]

When the orbit of a neutron star binary has decayed to 1.89 m (1890 km), its remaining lifetime is about 130,000 seconds or 36 hours. The orbital frequency will vary from 1 orbit per second at the start, to 918 orbits per second when the orbit has shrunk to 20 km at merger. The majority of gravitational radiation emitted will be at twice the orbital frequency. Just before merger, the inspiral could be observed by LIGO if such a binary were close enough. LIGO has only a few minutes to observe this merger out of a total orbital lifetime that may have been billions of years. In August 2017, LIGO and Virgo observed the first binary neutron star inspiral in [GW170817](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GW170817), and 70 observatories collaborated to detect the electromagnetic counterpart, a [kilonova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilonova) in the galaxy [NGC 4993](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NGC_4993), 40 [megaparsecs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megaparsec) away, emitting a short [gamma ray burst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_ray_burst) ([GRB 170817A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GRB_170817A)) seconds after the merger, followed by a longer optical transient ([AT 2017gfo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AT_2017gfo)) powered by [r-process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R-process) nuclei. Advanced LIGO detectors should be able to detect such events up to 200 megaparsecs away; at this range, around 40 detections per year would be expected.[^o83]

### Black hole binaries {#Black_hole_binaries}

Black hole binaries emit gravitational waves during their in-spiral, [merger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_collision), and ring-down phases. Hence, in the early 1990s the physics community rallied around a concerted effort to predict the waveforms of gravitational waves from these systems with the [Binary Black Hole Grand Challenge Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_Black_Hole_Grand_Challenge_Alliance).[^o85] The largest amplitude of emission occurs during the merger phase, which can be modeled with the techniques of numerical relativity.[^o86][^o87][^o88] The first direct detection of gravitational waves, [GW150914](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GW150914), came from the merger of two black holes.

### Supernova {#Supernova}

A supernova is a [transient astronomical event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transient_astronomical_event) that occurs during the last stellar evolutionary stages of a massive star's life, whose dramatic and catastrophic destruction is marked by one final titanic explosion. This explosion can happen in one of many ways, but in all of them a significant proportion of the matter in the star is blown away into the surrounding space at extremely high velocities (up to 10% of the speed of light). Unless there is perfect spherical symmetry in these explosions (i.e., unless matter is spewed out evenly in all directions), there will be gravitational radiation from the explosion. This is because gravitational waves are [generated by a changing quadrupole moment](wikipedia://en/44190891), which can happen only when there is asymmetrical movement of masses. Since the exact mechanism by which supernovae take place is not fully understood, it is not easy to model the gravitational radiation emitted by them.

### Spinning neutron stars {#Spinning_neutron_stars}

As noted above, a mass distribution will emit gravitational radiation only when there is spherically asymmetric motion among the masses. A [spinning neutron star](wikipedia://en/1227284) will generally emit no gravitational radiation because neutron stars are highly dense objects with a strong gravitational field that keeps them almost perfectly spherical. In some cases, however, there might be slight deformities on the surface called "mountains", which are bumps extending no more than 10 centimeters (4 inches) above the surface,[^o91] that make the spinning spherically asymmetric. This gives the star a quadrupole moment that changes with time, and it will emit gravitational waves until the deformities are smoothed out.

### Primordial gravitational wave {#Primordial_gravitational_wave}

Gravitational waves from the early universe could provide a unique probe for cosmology. This is owed to the fact that these waves interact very weakly with matter, they would propagate freely from a very early time when other signals were trapped by the large density of energy. If this gravitational radiation could be detected, it would be [gravitational wave background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_wave_background) complementary to the [cosmic microwave background](wikipedia://en/7376) data.[^o31] However, the existence of primordial gravitational waves can also be inferred from their effects. Models of "slow-roll" [cosmic inflation](wikipedia://en/5382) in the [early universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_universe) predict primordial [gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_waves) that would impact the polarisation of the cosmic microwave background, creating a specific pattern of [B-mode polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polarization_%28cosmology%29). Detection of this pattern would support the theory of inflation and their strength can confirm and exclude different models of inflation.[^o93][^o94] While claims that this characteristic pattern of B-mode polarization had been measured by the [BICEP2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BICEP_and_Keck_Array) instrument[^o95] were later attributed to [cosmic dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_dust) due to new results of the [Planck experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_%28spacecraft%29),[^o96][^o94] subsequent reanalysis with compensation for foreground dust show limits in agreement with results from [Lambda-CDM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambda-CDM) models.[^o97]

## Properties and behaviour {#Properties_and_behaviour}

### Energy, momentum, and angular momentum {#Energy,_momentum,_and_angular_momentum}

Water waves, sound waves, and electromagnetic waves are able to carry [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy), [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431), and [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839) and by doing so they carry those away from the source.[^o100] Gravitational waves perform the same function. Thus, for example, a binary system loses angular momentum as the two orbiting objects spiral towards each other – the angular momentum is radiated away by gravitational waves.

The waves can also carry off linear momentum, a possibility that has some interesting implications for [astrophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysics).[^o101] After two supermassive black holes coalesce, emission of linear momentum can produce a "kick" with amplitude as large as 4000 km/s. This is fast enough to eject the coalesced black hole completely from its host galaxy. Even if the kick is too small to eject the black hole completely, it can remove it temporarily from the nucleus of the galaxy, after which it will oscillate about the center, eventually coming to rest.[^o102] A kicked black hole can also carry a star cluster with it, forming a [hyper-compact stellar system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyper-compact_stellar_system).[^o103] Or it may carry gas, allowing the recoiling black hole to appear temporarily as a "[naked quasar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HE0450-2958)". The [quasar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasar) [SDSS J092712.65+294344.0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SDSS_J0927+2943) is thought to contain a recoiling supermassive black hole.[^o104]

### Redshifting {#Redshifting}

Like [electromagnetic waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_wave), gravitational waves should exhibit [shifting of wavelength](wikipedia://en/26262) and frequency due to the relative velocities of the source and observer (the [Doppler effect](wikipedia://en/408026)), but also due to distortions of [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758), such as [cosmic expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_expansion).[^o100][^o106] Redshifting *of* gravitational waves is different from redshifting *due to* gravity ([gravitational redshift](wikipedia://en/10346)).

### Quantum gravity, wave-particle aspects, and graviton {#Quantum_gravity,_wave-particle_aspects,_and_graviton}

In the framework of [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), the [graviton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graviton) is the name given to a hypothetical [elementary particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle) speculated to be the [force carrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_carrier) that mediates [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity). However the graviton is not yet proven to exist, and no [scientific model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_model) yet exists that successfully reconciles [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), which describes gravity, and the [Standard Model](wikipedia://en/47641), which describes all other [fundamental forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_forces). Attempts, such as [quantum gravity](wikipedia://en/25312), have been made, but are not yet accepted.

If such a particle exists, it is expected to be [massless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_in_special_relativity) (because the gravitational force has an unlimited range) and must be a [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829)-2 [boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson). It can be shown that any massless spin-2 field would give rise to a force indistinguishable from gravitation, because a massless spin-2 field must couple to (interact with) the stress-energy tensor in the same way that the gravitational field does; therefore if a massless spin-2 particle were ever discovered, it would be likely to be the graviton without further distinction from other massless spin-2 particles. Such a discovery would unite quantum theory with gravity.[^o108]

### Significance for study of the early universe {#Significance_for_study_of_the_early_universe}

Due to the weakness of the coupling of gravity to matter, gravitational waves experience very little absorption or scattering, even as they travel over astronomical distances. In particular, gravitational waves are expected to be unaffected by the opacity of the very early universe. In these early phases, space had not yet become "transparent", so observations based upon light, radio waves, and other electromagnetic radiation that far back into time are limited or unavailable. Therefore, gravitational waves are expected in principle to have the potential to provide a wealth of observational data about the very early universe.[^o117]

### Determining direction of travel {#Determining_direction_of_travel}

The difficulty in directly detecting gravitational waves means it is also difficult for a single detector to identify by itself the direction of a source. Therefore, multiple detectors are used, both to distinguish signals from other "noise" by confirming the signal is not of earthly origin, and also to determine direction by means of [triangulation](wikipedia://en/207174). This technique uses the fact that the waves travel at the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) and will reach different detectors at different times depending on their source direction. Although the differences in arrival time may be just a few [milliseconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millisecond), this is sufficient to identify the direction of the origin of the wave with considerable precision.

For example, in the case of [GW170814](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GW170814), triangulation of all three instruments estimated the position of the source within a 90% credible region of just 60 [deg<sup>2</sup>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_degree), an accuracy record for the time.[^o111] Sky localization of GW events continues to improve , for example with GW240615 localized to 6 deg<sup>2</sup>.[^o112]

## Gravitational wave astronomy {#Gravitational_wave_astronomy}

*Image: wavy.gif.* Two-dimensional representation of gravitational waves generated by two [neutron stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star) orbiting each other.

During the past century, [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) has been revolutionized by the use of new methods for observing the universe. Astronomical observations were initially made using [visible light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_light). [Galileo Galilei](wikipedia://en/29688374) pioneered the use of telescopes to enhance these observations. However, visible light is only a small portion of the [electromagnetic spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_spectrum), and not all objects in the distant universe shine strongly in this particular band. More information may be found, for example, in radio wavelengths. Using [radio telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_telescopes), astronomers have discovered [pulsars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsars) and [quasars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasars), for example. Observations in the [microwave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave) band led to the detection of [faint imprints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_microwave_background_radiation) of the [Big Bang](wikipedia://en/4116), a discovery [Stephen Hawking](wikipedia://en/19376148) called the "greatest discovery of the century, if not all time". Similar advances in observations using [gamma rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_ray), [x-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray), [ultraviolet light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet_light), and [infrared light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_light) have also brought new insights to astronomy. As each of these regions of the spectrum has opened, new discoveries have been made that could not have been made otherwise. The astronomy community hopes that the same holds true of gravitational waves.[^o115][^o116]

Gravitational waves have two important and unique properties. First, there is no need for any type of matter to be present nearby in order for the waves to be generated by a binary system of uncharged black holes, which would emit no electromagnetic radiation. Second, gravitational waves can pass through any intervening matter without being scattered significantly. Whereas light from distant stars may be blocked out by [interstellar dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_dust), for example, gravitational waves will pass through essentially unimpeded. These two features allow gravitational waves to carry information about astronomical phenomena heretofore never observed by humans.[^o117]

The sources of gravitational waves described above are in the low-frequency end of the gravitational-wave spectrum (10<sup>−7</sup> to 10<sup>5</sup> Hz). An astrophysical source at the high-frequency end of the gravitational-wave spectrum (above 10<sup>5</sup> Hz and probably 10<sup>10</sup> Hz) generates relic gravitational waves that are theorized to be faint imprints of the Big Bang like the cosmic microwave background.[^o118] At these high frequencies it is potentially possible that the sources may be "man made"[^o26] that is, gravitational waves generated and detected in the laboratory.[^o119][^o120]

A [supermassive black hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supermassive_black_hole), created from the merger of the black holes at the center of two merging galaxies detected by the [Hubble Space Telescope](wikipedia://en/40203), is theorized to have been ejected from the merger center by gravitational waves.[^o121][^o122]

## Detection {#Detection}

*Image: PIA17993-DetectorsForInfantUniverseStudies-20140317.jpg.* Now disproved evidence allegedly showing gravitational waves in the [infant universe](wikipedia://en/4116) was found by the [BICEP2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BICEP2) [radio telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_telescope). The microscopic examination of the [focal plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focal_plane) of the BICEP2 detector is shown here.[^o55][^o56] In January 2015, however, the BICEP2 findings were confirmed to be the result of [cosmic dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_dust).[^o124]

### Indirect detection {#Indirect_detection}

Although the waves from the Earth–Sun system are minuscule, astronomers can point to other sources for which the radiation should be substantial. One important example is the [Hulse–Taylor binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulse–Taylor_binary) – a pair of stars, one of which is a [pulsar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_pulsar).[^o126] The characteristics of their orbit can be deduced from the [Doppler shifting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_shift) of radio signals given off by the pulsar. Each of the stars is about 1.4 and the size of their orbits is about 1/75 of the [Earth–Sun orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_orbit), just a few times larger than the diameter of our own Sun. The combination of greater masses and smaller separation means that the energy given off by the Hulse–Taylor binary will be far greater than the energy given off by the Earth–Sun system – roughly 10<sup>22</sup> times as much.

The information about the orbit can be used to predict how much energy (and angular momentum) would be radiated in the form of gravitational waves. As the binary system loses energy, the stars gradually draw closer to each other, and the orbital period decreases. The resulting trajectory of each star is an inspiral, a spiral with decreasing radius. General relativity precisely describes these trajectories; in particular, the energy radiated in gravitational waves determines the rate of decrease in the period, defined as the time interval between successive periastrons (points of closest approach of the two stars). For the Hulse–Taylor pulsar, the predicted current change in radius is about 3 mm per orbit, and the change in the 7.75 hr period is about 2 seconds per year. Following a preliminary observation showing an orbital energy loss consistent with gravitational waves,[^o44] careful timing observations by Taylor and Joel Weisberg dramatically confirmed the predicted period decrease to within 10%.[^o44] With the improved statistics of more than 30 years of timing data since the pulsar's discovery, the observed change in the orbital period currently matches the prediction from gravitational radiation assumed by general relativity to within 0.2 percent.[^o127] In 1993, spurred in part by this indirect detection of gravitational waves, the Nobel Committee awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics to Hulse and Taylor for "the discovery of a new type of pulsar, a discovery that has opened up new possibilities for the study of gravitation".[^o128] The lifetime of this binary system, from the present to merger is estimated to be a few hundred million years.[^o129]

Inspirals are very important sources of gravitational waves. Any time two compact objects (white dwarfs, neutron stars, or [black holes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_black_hole)) are in close orbits, they send out intense gravitational waves. As they spiral closer to each other, these waves become more intense. At some point they should become so intense that direct detection by their effect on objects on Earth or in space is possible. This direct detection is the goal of several large-scale experiments.[^o130]

The only difficulty is that most systems like the Hulse–Taylor binary are so far away. The amplitude of waves given off by the Hulse–Taylor binary at Earth would be roughly *h* ≈ 10<sup>−26</sup>. There are some sources, however, that astrophysicists expect to find that produce much greater amplitudes of *h* ≈ 10<sup>−20</sup>. At least eight other binary pulsars have been discovered.[^o131]

### Difficulties {#Difficulties}

Gravitational waves are not easily detectable. When they reach the Earth, they have a small amplitude with strain approximately 10<sup>−21</sup>, meaning that an extremely sensitive detector is needed, and that other sources of noise can overwhelm the signal.[^o133] Gravitational waves are expected to have frequencies 10<sup>−16</sup> Hz \< *f* \< 10<sup>4</sup> Hz.[^o134]

### Ground-based detectors {#Ground-based_detectors}

*Image: LIGO schematic (multilang).svg.* A schematic diagram of a laser interferometer

Though the Hulse–Taylor observations were very important, they give only *indirect* evidence for gravitational waves. A more conclusive observation would be a *direct* measurement of the effect of a passing gravitational wave, which could also provide more information about the system that generated it. Any such direct detection is complicated by the [extraordinarily small](wikipedia://en/203433) effect the waves would produce on a detector. The amplitude of a spherical wave will fall off as the inverse of the distance from the source (the 1/*R* term in the formulas for *h* above). Thus, even waves from extreme systems like merging binary black holes die out to very small amplitudes by the time they reach the Earth. Astrophysicists expect that some gravitational waves passing the Earth may be as large as *h* ≈ 10<sup>−20</sup>, but generally no bigger.[^o136]

#### Resonant antennas {#Resonant_antennas}

A simple device theorised to detect the expected wave motion is called a [Weber bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weber_bar) – a large, solid bar of metal isolated from outside vibrations. This type of instrument was the first type of gravitational wave detector. Strains in space due to an incident gravitational wave excite the bar's [resonant frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resonant_frequency) and could thus be amplified to detectable levels. Conceivably, a nearby supernova might be strong enough to be seen without resonant amplification. With this instrument, [Joseph Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Weber) claimed to have detected daily signals of gravitational waves. His results, however, were contested in 1974 by physicists [Richard Garwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Garwin) and [David Douglass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Douglass_%28physicist%29). Modern forms of the Weber bar are still operated, [cryogenically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryogenically) cooled, with [superconducting quantum interference devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQUID) to detect vibration. Weber bars are not sensitive enough to detect anything but extremely powerful gravitational waves.[^o138]

[MiniGRAIL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MiniGRAIL) is a spherical gravitational wave antenna using this principle. It is based at [Leiden University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leiden_University), consisting of an exactingly machined 1,150 kg sphere cryogenically cooled to 20 millikelvins.[^o139] The spherical configuration allows for equal sensitivity in all directions, and is somewhat experimentally simpler than larger linear devices requiring high vacuum. Events are detected by measuring [deformation of the detector sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multipole_moments). MiniGRAIL is highly sensitive in the 2–4 kHz range, suitable for detecting gravitational waves from rotating neutron star instabilities or small black hole mergers.[^o140]

There are currently two detectors focused on the higher end of the gravitational wave spectrum (10<sup>−7</sup> to 10<sup>5</sup> Hz): one at [University of Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Birmingham), England,[^o141] and the other at [INFN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istituto_Nazionale_di_Fisica_Nucleare) Genoa, Italy. A third is under development at [Chongqing University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chongqing_University), China. The Birmingham detector measures changes in the polarization state of a [microwave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave) beam circulating in a closed loop about one meter across. Both detectors are expected to be sensitive to periodic spacetime strains of *h* ~ 2 × 10<sup>\-13</sup> /$\sqrt{\operatorname{Hz}}$, given as an [amplitude spectral density](wikipedia://en/202672). The INFN Genoa detector is a resonant antenna consisting of two coupled spherical [superconducting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconducting) harmonic oscillators a few centimeters in diameter. The oscillators are designed to have (when uncoupled) almost equal resonant frequencies. The system is currently expected to have a sensitivity to periodic spacetime strains of *h* ~ 2 × 10<sup>\-17</sup> /$\sqrt{\operatorname{Hz}}$, with an expectation to reach a sensitivity of *h* ~ 2 × 10<sup>\-20</sup> /$\sqrt{\operatorname{Hz}}$. The Chongqing University detector is planned to detect relic high-frequency gravitational waves with the predicted typical parameters ≈10<sup>11</sup> Hz (100 GHz) and *h* ≈10<sup>−30</sup> to 10<sup>−32</sup>.[^o142]

#### Interferometers {#Interferometers}

A more sensitive class of detector uses a laser [Michelson interferometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelson_interferometer) to measure gravitational-wave induced motion between separated 'free' masses.[^o144] This allows the masses to be separated by large distances (increasing the signal size); a further advantage is that it is sensitive to a wide range of frequencies (not just those near a resonance as is the case for Weber bars). After years of development ground-based interferometers made the first detection of gravitational waves in 2015.

Currently, the most sensitive is [LIGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIGO) – the Laser Interferometer Gravitational Wave Observatory. LIGO has three detectors: one in [Livingston, Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livingston,_Louisiana), one at the [Hanford site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanford_site) in [Richland, Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richland,_Washington) and a third (formerly installed as a second detector at Hanford) that is planned to be moved to [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INDIGO). Each observatory has two [light storage arms](wikipedia://en/196118) that are 4 kilometers in length. These are at 90 degree angles to each other, with the light passing through 1 m diameter vacuum tubes running the entire 4 kilometers. A passing gravitational wave will slightly stretch one arm as it shortens the other. This is the motion to which an interferometer is most sensitive.

Even with such long arms, the strongest gravitational waves will only change the distance between the ends of the arms by at most roughly 10<sup>−18</sup> m. LIGO should be able to detect gravitational waves as small as *h* ~ 5 × 10<sup>\-22</sup>. Upgrades to LIGO and [Virgo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgo_interferometer) should increase the sensitivity still further. Another highly sensitive interferometer, [KAGRA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KAGRA), which is located in the [Kamioka Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamioka_Observatory) in Japan, is in operation since February 2020. A key point is that a tenfold increase in sensitivity (radius of 'reach') increases the volume of space accessible to the instrument by one thousand times. This increases the rate at which detectable signals might be seen from one per tens of years of observation, to tens per year.[^o145]

Interferometric detectors are limited at high frequencies by [shot noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shot_noise), which occurs because the lasers produce photons randomly; one analogy is to rainfall – the rate of rainfall, like the laser intensity, is measurable, but the raindrops, like photons, fall at random times, causing fluctuations around the average value. This leads to noise at the output of the detector, much like radio static. In addition, for sufficiently high laser power, the random momentum transferred to the test masses by the laser photons shakes the mirrors, masking signals of low frequencies. Thermal noise (e.g., [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436)) is another limit to sensitivity. In addition to these 'stationary' (constant) noise sources, all ground-based detectors are also limited at low frequencies by [seismic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismic) noise and other forms of environmental vibration, and other 'non-stationary' noise sources; creaks in mechanical structures, lightning or other large electrical disturbances, etc. may also create noise masking an event or may even imitate an event. All of these must be taken into account and excluded by analysis before detection may be considered a true gravitational wave event.

#### Einstein@Home {#Einstein@Home}

The simplest gravitational waves are those with constant frequency. The waves given off by a spinning, non-axisymmetric neutron star would be approximately [monochromatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monochrome): a [pure tone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_tone) in [acoustics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustics). Unlike signals from supernovae or binary black holes, these signals evolve little in amplitude or frequency over the period it would be observed by ground-based detectors. However, there would be some change in the measured signal, because of [Doppler shifting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_shift) caused by the motion of the Earth. Despite the signals being simple, detection is extremely computationally expensive, because of the long stretches of data that must be analysed.

The [Einstein@Home](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein@Home) project is a [distributed computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_computing) project similar to [SETI@home](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SETI@home) intended to detect this type of gravitational wave. By taking data from LIGO and GEO, and sending it out in little pieces to thousands of volunteers for parallel analysis on their home computers, Einstein@Home can sift through the data far more quickly than would be possible otherwise.[^o147]

### Space-based interferometers {#Space-based_interferometers}

Space-based interferometers, such as [LISA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_Interferometer_Space_Antenna) and [DECIGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DECIGO), are also being developed. LISA's design calls for three test masses forming an equilateral triangle, with lasers from each spacecraft to each other spacecraft forming two independent interferometers. LISA is planned to occupy a solar orbit trailing the Earth, with each arm of the triangle being 2.5 million kilometers.[^o149] This puts the detector in an excellent vacuum far from Earth-based sources of noise, though it will still be susceptible to heat, [shot noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shot_noise), and artifacts caused by [cosmic rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_ray) and [solar wind](wikipedia://en/28538).

### Using pulsar timing arrays {#Using_pulsar_timing_arrays}

*Image: correlation\_vs\_angular\_separation\_between\_pulsars.svg.* Plot of correlation between pulsars observed by NANOGrav vs angular separation between pulsars, compared with a theoretical *Hellings-Downs* model (dashed purple) and if there were no gravitational wave background (solid green)[^o151][^o152]

[Pulsars](wikipedia://en/1227284) are highly magnetized, rapidly rotating neutron stars. A pulsar emits beams of radio waves that, like lighthouse beams, sweep through the sky as the pulsar rotates. The signal from a pulsar can be detected by radio telescopes as a series of regularly spaced pulses, essentially like the ticks of a clock. GWs affect the time it takes the pulses to travel from the pulsar to a telescope on Earth. A [pulsar timing array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsar_timing_array) uses [millisecond pulsars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millisecond_pulsar) to seek out perturbations due to GWs in measurements of the time of arrival of pulses to a telescope, in other words, to look for deviations in the clock ticks. To detect GWs, pulsar timing arrays search for a [distinct quadrupolar pattern of correlation and anti-correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellings-Downs_curve) between the time of arrival of pulses from different pulsar pairs as a function of their angular separation in the sky.[^o153] Although pulsar pulses travel through space for hundreds or thousands of years to reach us, pulsar timing arrays are sensitive to perturbations in their travel time of much less than a millionth of a second.

The most likely source of GWs to which pulsar timing arrays are sensitive are supermassive black hole binaries, which form from the collision of galaxies.[^o154] In addition to individual binary systems, pulsar timing arrays are sensitive to a stochastic background of GWs made from the sum of GWs from many galaxy mergers. Other potential signal sources include [cosmic strings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_strings) and the primordial background of GWs from [cosmic inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation_%28cosmology%29).

Globally there are seven active pulsar timing array projects. The [North American Nanohertz Observatory for Gravitational Waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Nanohertz_Observatory_for_Gravitational_Waves) (NANOGrav) uses data collected by the [Arecibo Radio Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arecibo_Observatory), [Green Bank Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Bank_Telescope), [Very Large Array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Very_Large_Array), and the [Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Hydrogen_Intensity_Mapping_Experiment). The Australian [Parkes Pulsar Timing Array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkes_Pulsar_Timing_Array) (PPTA) uses data from the [Parkes radio-telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkes_Observatory). The [European Pulsar Timing Array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Pulsar_Timing_Array) (EPTA) uses data from the four largest telescopes in Europe: the [Lovell Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lovell_Telescope), the [Westerbork Synthesis Radio Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westerbork_Synthesis_Radio_Telescope), the [Effelsberg Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effelsberg_100-m_Radio_Telescope) and the [Nancay Radio Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancay_Radio_Telescope). The [Indian Pulsar Timing Array](https://inpta.iitr.ac.in/) (InPTA) uses data from the [Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_Metrewave_Radio_Telescope), and the [MeerKAT Pulsar Timing Array](https://mpta-gw.github.io/) (MPTA) uses data from the [MeerKAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MeerKAT) radio telescope. With the [African Pulsar Timing](https://africanpulsartiming.github.io/) (APT) group, these collaborations also collaborate under the title of the [International Pulsar Timing Array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Pulsar_Timing_Array) project.[^o155] Additionally, the [Chinese Pulsar Timing Array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Pulsar_Timing_Array) (CPTA) uses data from the [Five-hundred-meter Aperture Spherical Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five-hundred-meter_Aperture_Spherical_Telescope).

### Primordial gravitational wave {#Primordial_gravitational_wave}

Primordial gravitational waves are gravitational waves observed in the [cosmic microwave background](wikipedia://en/7376). They were allegedly detected by the [BICEP2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BICEP2) instrument, an announcement made on 17 March 2014, which was withdrawn on 30 January 2015 ("the signal can be entirely attributed to [dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_dust) in the Milky Way"[^o124]).

### LIGO and Virgo observations {#LIGO_and_Virgo_observations}

*Image: LIGO measurement of gravitational waves.svg.* LIGO measurement of the gravitational waves at the Hanford (left) and Livingston (right) detectors, compared to the theoretical predicted values.

On 11 February 2016, the [LIGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIGO) collaboration announced the [first observation of gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_observation_of_gravitational_waves), from a signal detected at 09:50:45 GMT on 14 September 2015[^o49] of two black holes with masses of 29 and 36 [solar masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_mass) merging about 1.3 billion light-years away. During the final fraction of a second of the merger, it released more than 50 times the [power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_%28physics%29) of all the stars in the observable universe combined.[^o158] The signal increased in frequency from 35 to 250 Hz over 10 cycles (5 orbits) as it rose in strength for a period of 0.2 second.[^o50] The mass of the new merged black hole was 62 solar masses. Energy equivalent to three solar masses was emitted as gravitational waves.[^o54] The signal was seen by both LIGO detectors in Livingston and Hanford, with a time difference of 7 milliseconds due to the angle between the two detectors and the source. The signal came from the [Southern Celestial Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Celestial_Hemisphere), in the rough direction of (but much farther away than) the [Magellanic Clouds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magellanic_Clouds).[^o52] The gravitational waves were observed in the region more than 5 sigma[^o50] (in other words, 99.99997% chances of showing/getting the same result), the probability of finding enough to have been assessed/considered as the evidence/proof in an [experiment](wikipedia://en/59861) of [statistical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_physics).[^o159]

Since then LIGO and Virgo have reported more [gravitational wave observations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_gravitational_wave_observations) from merging black hole binaries.

On 16 October 2017, the LIGO and Virgo collaborations announced the first-ever detection of gravitational waves originating from the coalescence of a binary neutron star system. The observation of the [GW170817](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GW170817) transient, which occurred on 17 August 2017, allowed for constraining the masses of the neutron stars involved between 0.86 and 2.26 solar masses. Further analysis allowed a greater restriction of the mass values to the interval 1.17–1.60 solar masses, with the total system mass measured to be 2.73–2.78 solar masses. The inclusion of the Virgo detector in the observation effort allowed for an improvement of the localization of the source by a factor of 10. This in turn facilitated the electromagnetic follow-up of the event. The signal lasted about 100 seconds, much longer than the few seconds measured from binary black holes.[^o160] Also in contrast to the case of binary black hole mergers, binary neutron star mergers were expected to yield an electromagnetic counterpart, that is, a light signal associated with the event. A gamma-ray burst ([GRB 170817A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GRB_170817A)) was detected by the [Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_Gamma-ray_Space_Telescope), occurring 1.7 seconds after the gravitational wave transient. The signal, originating near the galaxy [NGC 4993](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NGC_4993), was associated with the neutron star merger. This was corroborated by the electromagnetic follow-up of the event ([AT 2017gfo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AT_2017gfo)), involving 70 telescopes and observatories and yielding observations over a large region of the electromagnetic spectrum which further confirmed the neutron star nature of the merged objects and the associated [kilonova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilonova).[^o161][^o162]

In 2021, the detection of the first two neutron star-black hole binaries by the LIGO and VIRGO detectors was published in the Astrophysical Journal Letters, allowing to first set bounds on the quantity of such systems. No neutron star-black hole binary had ever been observed using conventional means before the gravitational observation.[^o11]

## Microscopic sources {#Microscopic_sources}

In 1964, L. Halpern and B. Laurent theoretically proved that gravitational spin-2 electron transitions are possible in atoms. Compared to electric and magnetic transitions the emission probability is extremely low. Stimulated emission was discussed for increasing the efficiency of the process. Due to the lack of mirrors or resonators for gravitational waves, they determined that a single pass GASER (a kind of laser emitting gravitational waves) is practically unfeasible.[^o164]
