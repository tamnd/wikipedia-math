# Real number

*Image: Real number line.svg.* Real numbers can be thought of as all points on a number line.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Real number"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **real number** is a [number](wikipedia://en/21690) that can be used to [measure](wikipedia://en/19022) a [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_variable) one-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) [quantity](wikipedia://en/691277) such as a [length](wikipedia://en/18542), [duration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time) or [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050). Here, *continuous* means that pairs of values can have arbitrarily small differences. Every real number can be almost uniquely represented by an infinite [decimal expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_expansion).

:::

The real numbers are fundamental in [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) and in many other branches of mathematics, in particular by their role in the classical definitions of [limits](wikipedia://en/26551602), [continuity](wikipedia://en/6122) and [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921).

The set of real numbers, sometimes called "the reals", is usually notated as a bold $\mathbf{R}$ or the [blackboard bold](wikipedia://en/40276) $\mathbb{R}$.

The adjective *real*, used in the 17th century by [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525), distinguishes real numbers from [imaginary numbers](wikipedia://en/14554) such as the [square roots](wikipedia://en/29208) of negative numbers.[^o2]

The real numbers include the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), such as the [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $-5$ and the [fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29) $4/3$. Real numbers that are not rational are [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689). Those real numbers that are [roots](wikipedia://en/264210) of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) with rational coefficients are [algebraic numbers](wikipedia://en/1158), which include all the rational numbers and also irrational numbers such as $\surd2 = 1.414...$. Other real numbers, such as $\pi = 3.1415...$, are not roots of polynomials; these are the [transcendental numbers](wikipedia://en/30325).[^o2]

The real numbers can be thought of as the points on a [line](wikipedia://en/946975), called the [number line](wikipedia://en/287188) or [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line), on which the points corresponding to integers ($..., -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, ...$) are equally spaced.

The informal descriptions above of the real numbers are not sufficient for rigorous reasoning about real numbers. The development of a suitable formal definition was a major achievement of [19th-century mathematics](wikipedia://en/14220#19th_century) and is the foundation of [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478), the study of [real functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_function) and real-valued [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838). One modern [axiomatic](wikipedia://en/928) definition is that real numbers form the [unique](wikipedia://en/7918341) ([up to](wikipedia://en/44787) an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828)) [Dedekind-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedekind-complete) [ordered field](wikipedia://en/22430). Other common definitions of real numbers include [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of [Cauchy sequences](wikipedia://en/6085) (of rational numbers), [Dedekind cuts](wikipedia://en/45569), and infinite [decimal representations](wikipedia://en/3293594). All these definitions satisfy the axiomatic definition and are thus equivalent.

## Characterizing properties {#Characterizing_properties}

Real numbers are completely characterized by their fundamental properties that can be summarized by saying that they form an [ordered field](wikipedia://en/22430) that is [Dedekind complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedekind_complete). Here, "completely characterized" means that there is a unique [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) between any two Dedekind complete ordered fields, and thus that their elements have exactly the same properties. This implies that one can manipulate real numbers and compute with them, without knowing how they can be defined; this is what mathematicians and physicists did during several centuries before the first formal definitions were provided in the second half of the 19th century. See [Construction of the real numbers](wikipedia://en/296666) for details about these formal definitions and the proof of their equivalence.

## Arithmetic {#Arithmetic}

The real numbers form an [ordered field](wikipedia://en/22430). Intuitively, this means that methods and rules of [elementary arithmetic](wikipedia://en/1567386) apply to them. More precisely, there are two [binary operations](wikipedia://en/3948), [addition](wikipedia://en/61338) and [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845), and a [total order](wikipedia://en/30330) that have the following properties.

- The *addition* of two real numbers $a$ and $b$ produces a real number denoted $a+b,$ which is the *sum* of $a$ and $b$.
- The *multiplication* of two real numbers $a$ and $b$ produces a real number denoted $ab,$ $a\cdot b$ or $a\times b,$ which is the *product* of $a$ and $b$.
- Addition and multiplication are both [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative), which means that $a+b=b+a$ and $ab=ba$ for all real numbers $a$ and $b$.
- Addition and multiplication are both [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative), which means that $(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)$ and $(ab)c=a(bc)$ for all real numbers $a$, $b$ and $c$.
- Multiplication is [distributive](wikipedia://en/103118) over addition, which means that $a(b+c)=ab+ac$ and $(a+b)c=ac+bc$ for all real numbers $a$, $b$ and $c$.
- There is a real number called [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero) and denoted $0$ which is an [additive identity](wikipedia://en/4178225), which means that $a+0=0+a=a$ for every real number $a$.
- There is a real number denoted $1$ which is a [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity), which means that $a\times 1 =1\times a=a$ for every real number $a$.
- Every real number $a$ has an [additive inverse](wikipedia://en/228312) denoted $-a.$ This means that $a+(-a)=(-a)+a=0$ for every real number $a$.
- Every nonzero real number $a$ has a [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) denoted $a^{-1}$ or $\tfrac 1a.$ This means that $aa^{-1}=a^{-1}a=1$ for every nonzero real number $a$.
- The total order is denoted $a<b.$ Being a total order means that it has the following two properties:

<!-- -->

1. For any two real numbers $a$ and $b$, exactly one of $a<b$, $\; a=b$, $\; b<a$ is true.
1. If $a<b$ and $b<c,$ then $a<c$.

<!-- -->

- The order is compatible with addition and multiplication, which means that $a<b$ implies $a+c<b+c$ for every real number $c$, and $0<ab$ is implied by $0<a$ and $0<b.$

Many other properties can be deduced from the above properties. In particular:

- $0\cdot a=0$ for every real number $a$
- $$0<1$$
- $0<a^2$ for every nonzero real number $a$

### Auxiliary operations {#Auxiliary_operations}

Several other operations are commonly used, which can be deduced from the above properties.

- [Subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141): the subtraction of two real numbers $a$ and $b$ results in the sum of $a$ and the [additive inverse](wikipedia://en/228312) $-b$ of $b$; that is, $$a-b=a+(-b).$$
- [Division](wikipedia://en/53696): the division of a real number $a$ by a nonzero real number $b$ is denoted $\frac ab,$ or $a/b$ and defined as the multiplication of $a$ with the [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) of $b$; that is, $$\frac ab=ab^{-1}.$$
- [Absolute value](wikipedia://en/991): the absolute value of a real number $a$, denoted $|a|,$ measures its distance from zero, and is defined as $$|a|=\max(a,-a).$$

### Auxiliary order relations {#Auxiliary_order_relations}

The [total order](wikipedia://en/30330) that is considered above is denoted $a<b$ and read as "$a$ is [less than](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Less_than) $b$". Three other [order relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_relation) are also commonly used:

- [Greater than](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_than): $a>b,$ read as "$a$ is greater than $b$", is defined as $a>b$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $b<a.$
- [Less than or equal to](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Less_than_or_equal_to): $a\le b,$ read as "$a$ is less than or equal to $b$" or "$a$ is not greater than $b$", is defined as $(a<b) \text{ or } (a=b),$ or equivalently as $\text{not } (b<a).$
- [Greater than or equal to](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_than_or_equal_to): $a\ge b,$ read as "$a$ is greater than or equal to $b$" or "$a$ is not less than $b$", is defined as $(b<a) \text{ or } (a=b),$ or equivalently as $\text{not } (a<b).$

### Integers and fractions as real numbers {#Integers_and_fractions_as_real_numbers}

The real numbers $0$ and $1$ are commonly identified with the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) $0$ and $1$. This allows identifying any natural number $n$ with the sum of $n$ real numbers equal to $1$.

This identification can be pursued by identifying a negative integer $-n$ (where $n$ is a natural number) with the additive inverse $-n$ of the real number identified with $n.$ Similarly a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024) $p/q$ (where $p$ and $q$ are integers and $q\ne 0$) is identified with the division of the real numbers identified with $p$ and $q$.

These identifications make the set $\mathbb{Q}$ of the rational numbers an ordered [subfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subfield_%28mathematics%29) of the real numbers $\mathbb{R}.$ The [Dedekind completeness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedekind_completeness) described below implies that some real numbers, such as $\sqrt 2,$ are not rational numbers; they are called [irrational numbers](wikipedia://en/20647689).

The above identifications make sense, since natural numbers, integers and real numbers are generally not defined by their individual nature, but by defining properties ([axioms](wikipedia://en/928)). So, the identification of natural numbers with some real numbers is justified by the fact that [Peano axioms](wikipedia://en/25005) are satisfied by these real numbers, with the addition with $1$ taken as the [successor function](wikipedia://en/325637).

Formally, one has an injective [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) of [ordered monoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordered_monoid) from the natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$ to the integers $\mathbb{Z},$ an injective homomorphism of [ordered rings](wikipedia://en/525697) from $\mathbb{Z}$ to the rational numbers $\mathbb{Q},$ and an injective homomorphism of [ordered fields](wikipedia://en/22430) from $\mathbb{Q}$ to the real numbers $\mathbb{R}.$ The identifications consist of not distinguishing the source and the image of each injective homomorphism, and thus to write

$$\mathbb{N}\subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}.$$

These identifications are formally [abuses of notation](wikipedia://en/1698066) (since, formally, a rational number is an equivalence class of pairs of integers, and a real number is an equivalence class of Cauchy series), and are generally harmless. It is only in very specific situations, that one must avoid them and replace them by using explicitly the above homomorphisms. This is the case in [constructive mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_mathematics) and [computer programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_programming). In the latter case, these homomorphisms are interpreted as [type conversions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_conversion) that can often be done automatically by the [compiler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compiler).

## Dedekind completeness {#Dedekind_completeness}

Previous properties do not distinguish real numbers from [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024). This distinction is provided by [Dedekind completeness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedekind_completeness), which states that every non-empty set of real numbers with an [upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound) admits a [least upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_upper_bound). This means the following:

- A set of real numbers $S$ is *bounded above* if there is a real number $u$ such that $s\le u$ for all $s\in S$; such a $u$ is called an *upper bound* of $S.$ So, Dedekind completeness means that, if $S$ is non-empty and bounded above, it has an upper bound that is less than any other upper bound.

Dedekind completeness implies other sorts of completeness (see below), but also has some important consequences.

- [Archimedean property](wikipedia://en/264158): for every real number $x$, there is an integer $n$ such that $x<n$ (take $n=u+1,$ where $u$ is the least upper bound of the integers less than $x$).
- Equivalently, if $x$ is a positive real number, there is a positive integer $n$ such that $0 <\tfrac 1n <x$.
- Every positive real number $x$ has a positive [square root](wikipedia://en/29208), that is, there exists a positive real number $r$ such that $r^2=x.$
- Every [univariate polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Univariate_polynomial) of odd degree with real coefficients has at least one real [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) (if the leading coefficient is positive, take the least upper bound of real numbers for which the value of the polynomial is negative).

The last two properties are summarized by saying that the real numbers form a [real closed field](wikipedia://en/1066093). This implies the real version of the [fundamental theorem of algebra](wikipedia://en/51414), namely that every polynomial with real coefficients can be factored into polynomials with real coefficients of degree at most two.

## Decimal representation {#Decimal_representation}

The most common way of describing a real number is via its decimal representation, a sequence of [decimal digits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_digit) each representing the product of an integer between zero and nine times a [power of ten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_of_ten), extending to finitely many positive powers of ten to the left and infinitely many negative powers of ten to the right. For a number $x$ whose decimal representation extends $k$ places to the left, the standard notation is the juxtaposition of the digits $b_k b_{k-1}\cdots b_0. a_{1} a_{2}\cdots,$ in descending order by power of ten, with non-negative and negative powers of ten separated by a [decimal point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_point), representing the [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series)

$$x =b_k 10^k+b_{k-1} 10^{k-1}+ \cdots + b_0 +\frac{a_1}{10} +\frac{a_2}{10^2}+\cdots.$$

For example, for the circle constant $\pi = 3.14159\cdots,$ $k$ is zero and $b_0=3,$ $a_1=1,$ $a_2=4,$ etc.

More formally, a *decimal representation* for a nonnegative real number $x$ consists of a nonnegative integer $k$ and integers between zero and nine in the [infinite sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_sequence)

$$b_k, b_{k-1},\ldots, b_0, a_{1}, a_{2},\ldots.$$

(If $k>0,$ then by convention $b_k\neq 0.$)

Such a decimal representation specifies the real number as the least upper bound of the [decimal fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_fraction) that are obtained by [truncating](wikipedia://en/229056) the sequence: given a positive integer $n$, the truncation of the sequence at the place $n$ is the finite [partial sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_sum)

$$\begin{aligned} D_n &=b_k 10^k+b_{k-1} 10^{k-1}+ \cdots + b_0 +\frac{a_1}{10}+\cdots+\frac{a_n}{10^n}\\ &=\sum_{i=0}^k b_i 10^i + \sum_{j=1}^n a_j10^{-j} \end{aligned}$$

The real number $x$ defined by the sequence is the least upper bound of the $D_n,$ which exists by Dedekind completeness.

Conversely, given a nonnegative real number $x$, one can define a decimal representation of $x$ by [induction](wikipedia://en/18881), as follows:

Define $b_k\cdots b_0$ as decimal representation of the largest integer $D_0$ such that $D_0\le x$ (this integer exists because of the Archimedean property). Then, supposing by [induction](wikipedia://en/18881) that the decimal fraction $D_i$ has been defined for $i<n,$ one defines $a_n$ as the largest digit such that $D_{n-1}+\tfrac{a_n}{10^n} \le a,$ and one sets $D_n=D_{n-1}+\tfrac{a_n}{10^n}.$

One can use the defining properties of the real numbers to show that $x$ is the least upper bound of the $D_n.$ So, the resulting sequence of digits is called a *decimal representation* of $x$.

Another decimal representation can be obtained by replacing $\le x$ with $<x$ in the preceding construction. These two representations are identical, unless $x$ is a [decimal fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_fraction) of the form $\tfrac m {10^h}.$ In this case, in the first decimal representation, all $a_{n}$ are zero for $n>h,$ and, in the second representation, all $a_{n}$ 9. (see [0.999...](wikipedia://en/1849799) for details).

In summary, there is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between the real numbers and the decimal representations that do not end with infinitely many trailing 9.

The preceding considerations apply directly for every [numeral base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numeral_base) $B\ge 2,$ simply by replacing 10 with $B$ and 9 with $B-1.$

## Topological completeness {#Topological_completeness}

A main reason for using real numbers is so that many sequences have [limits](wikipedia://en/26551602). More formally, the reals are [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completeness_%28topology%29) (in the sense of [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018) or [uniform spaces](wikipedia://en/32339), which is a different sense than the Dedekind completeness of the order in the previous section):

A [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $(x_n)$ of real numbers is called a *[Cauchy sequence](wikipedia://en/6085)* if for any $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists an integer $N$ (possibly depending on $\varepsilon$) such that the [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) $|x_n-x_m|$ is less than $\varepsilon$ for all $n$ and $m$ that are both greater than $N$. This definition, originally provided by [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustin_Louis_Cauchy), formalizes the fact that the $x_n$ eventually come and remain arbitrarily close to each other.

A sequence $(x_n)$ *converges to the limit* $x$ if its elements eventually come and remain arbitrarily close to $x$, that is, if for any $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists an integer $N$ (possibly depending on $\varepsilon$) such that the distance $|x_n-x|$ is less than $\varepsilon$ for $n$ greater than $N$.

Every convergent sequence is a Cauchy sequence, and the converse is true for real numbers, and this means that the [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) of the real numbers is complete.

The set of rational numbers is not complete. For example, the sequence (1; 1.4; 1.41; 1.414; 1.4142; 1.41421; ...), where each term adds a digit of the decimal expansion of the positive [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of 2, is Cauchy but it does not converge to a rational number (in the real numbers, in contrast, it converges to the positive [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of 2).

The completeness property of the reals is the basis on which [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), and more generally [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), are built. In particular, the test that a sequence is a Cauchy sequence allows proving that a sequence has a limit, without computing it, and even without knowing it.

For example, the standard series of the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678)

$$e^x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n!}$$

converges to a real number for every $x$, because the sums

$$\sum_{n=N}^{M} \frac{x^n}{n!}$$

can be made arbitrarily small (independently of $M$) by choosing $N$ sufficiently large. This proves that the sequence is Cauchy, and thus converges, showing that $e^x$ is well defined for every $x$.

### "The complete ordered field" {#"The_complete_ordered_field"}

The real numbers are often described as "the complete ordered field", a phrase that can be interpreted in several ways.

First, an order can be [lattice-complete](wikipedia://en/44777). It is easy to see that no ordered field can be lattice-complete, because it can have no [largest element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_element) (given any element $z$, $z+1$ is larger).

Additionally, an order can be Dedekind-complete, see #Axiomatic approach. The uniqueness result at the end of that section justifies using the word "the" in the phrase "complete ordered field" when this is the sense of "complete" that is meant. This sense of completeness is most closely related to the construction of the reals from Dedekind cuts, since that construction starts from an ordered field (the rationals) and then forms the Dedekind-completion of it in a standard way.

These two notions of completeness ignore the field structure. However, an [ordered group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordered_group) (in this case, the additive group of the field) defines a [uniform](wikipedia://en/32339) structure, and uniform structures have a notion of [completeness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completeness_%28topology%29); the description in § Completeness is a special case. (We refer to the notion of completeness in uniform spaces rather than the related and better known notion for [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018), since the definition of metric space relies on already having a characterization of the real numbers.) It is not true that $\mathbb{R}$ is the *only* uniformly complete ordered field, but it is the only uniformly complete *[Archimedean field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedean_field)*, and indeed one often hears the phrase "complete Archimedean field" instead of "complete ordered field". Every uniformly complete Archimedean field must also be Dedekind-complete (and vice versa), justifying using "the" in the phrase "the complete Archimedean field". This sense of completeness is most closely related to the construction of the reals from Cauchy sequences (the construction carried out in full in this article), since it starts with an Archimedean field (the rationals) and forms the uniform completion of it in a standard way.

But the original use of the phrase "complete Archimedean field" was by [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302), who meant still something else by it. He meant that the real numbers form the *largest* Archimedean field in the sense that every other Archimedean field is a subfield of $\mathbb{R}$. Thus $\mathbb{R}$ is "complete" in the sense that nothing further can be added to it without making it no longer an Archimedean field. This sense of completeness is most closely related to the construction of the reals from [surreal numbers](wikipedia://en/51432), since that construction starts with a proper class that contains every ordered field (the surreals) and then selects from it the largest Archimedean subfield.

## Cardinality {#Cardinality}

The set of all real numbers is [uncountable](wikipedia://en/32248), in the sense that while both the set of all [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) $\{1, 2, 3, 4, ...\}$ and the set of all real numbers are [infinite sets](wikipedia://en/142679), there exists no [one-to-one function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-to-one_function) from the real numbers to the natural numbers. The [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of the set of all real numbers is called the *[cardinality of the continuum](wikipedia://en/1574901)* and commonly denoted by $\mathfrak c.$ It is strictly greater than the cardinality of the set of all natural numbers, denoted $\aleph_0$ and called *[aleph-zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleph-zero)* or *aleph-nought*. The cardinality of the continuum equals the cardinality of the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of the natural numbers, that is, the set of all subsets of the natural numbers.

The statement that there is no cardinality strictly greater than $\aleph_0$ and strictly smaller than $\mathfrak c$ is known as the [continuum hypothesis](wikipedia://en/5705) (CH). The axiom system most commonly used in mathematics, [Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo-Fraenkel_set_theory) with the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) (ZFC), is insufficient to decide whether CH holds: assuming that ZFC is consistent, CH can be neither proved nor disproved within ZFC, since some models of ZFC satisfy CH, while others violate it.[^o14]

## Other properties {#Other_properties}

As a topological space, the real numbers are [separable](wikipedia://en/27855). This is because the set of rationals, which is countable, is [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in the real numbers. The irrational numbers are also dense in the real numbers, however they are uncountable and have the same cardinality as the reals.

The real numbers form a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018): the distance between $x$ and $y$ is defined as the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) $|x-y|$. By virtue of being a totally ordered set, they also carry an [order topology](wikipedia://en/213650); the [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) arising from the metric and the one arising from the order are identical, but yield different presentations for the topology—in the order topology as ordered intervals, in the metric topology as epsilon-balls. The Dedekind cuts construction uses the order topology presentation, while the Cauchy sequences construction uses the metric topology presentation. The reals form a [contractible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contractible) (hence [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) and [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected)), [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) and [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) metric space of [Hausdorff dimension](wikipedia://en/14294) 1. The real numbers are [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_compactness) but not [compact](wikipedia://en/6042). There are various properties that uniquely specify them; for instance, all unbounded, connected, and separable [order topologies](wikipedia://en/30330) are necessarily [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to the reals.

Every nonnegative real number has a [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) in $\mathbb{R}$, although no negative number does. This shows that the order on $\mathbb{R}$ is determined by its algebraic structure. Also, every [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) of odd degree admits at least one real root: these two properties make $\mathbb{R}$ the premier example of a [real closed field](wikipedia://en/1066093). Proving this is the first half of one proof of the [fundamental theorem of algebra](wikipedia://en/51414).

The reals carry a canonical [measure](wikipedia://en/19873), the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198), which is the [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645) on their structure as a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) normalized such that the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) $[0;1]$ has measure 1. There exist sets of real numbers that are not Lebesgue measurable, e.g. [Vitali sets](wikipedia://en/424189).

The supremum axiom of the reals refers to subsets of the reals and is therefore a second-order logical statement. It is not possible to characterize the reals with [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) alone: the [Löwenheim–Skolem theorem](wikipedia://en/341482) implies that there exists a countable dense subset of the real numbers satisfying exactly the same sentences in first-order logic as the real numbers themselves. The set of [hyperreal numbers](wikipedia://en/51429) satisfies the same first order sentences as $\mathbb{R}$. Ordered fields that satisfy the same first-order sentences as $\mathbb{R}$ are called [nonstandard models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonstandard_model) of $\mathbb{R}$. This is what makes [nonstandard analysis](wikipedia://en/21708) work; by proving a first-order statement in some nonstandard model (which may be easier than proving it in $\mathbb{R}$), we know that the same statement must also be true of $\mathbb{R}$.

The [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $\mathbb{R}$ of real numbers is an [extension field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_field) of the field $\mathbb{Q}$ of rational numbers, and $\mathbb{R}$ can therefore be seen as a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over $\mathbb{Q}$. [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214) with the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) guarantees the existence of a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) of this vector space: there exists a set *B* of real numbers such that every real number can be written uniquely as a finite [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of elements of this set, using rational coefficients only, and such that no element of *B* is a rational linear combination of the others. However, this existence theorem is purely theoretical, as such a base has never been explicitly described.

The [well-ordering theorem](wikipedia://en/33458) implies that the real numbers can be [well-ordered](wikipedia://en/33456) if the axiom of choice is assumed: there exists a total order on $\mathbb{R}$ with the property that every nonempty [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $\mathbb{R}$ has a [least element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_element) in this ordering. (The standard ordering $\le$ of the real numbers is not a well-ordering since e.g. an [open interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_interval) does not contain a least element in this ordering.) Again, the existence of such a well-ordering is purely theoretical, as it has not been explicitly described. If the [axiom of constructibility](wikipedia://en/696472) is assumed in addition to the axioms of ZF, a well ordering of the real numbers can be shown to be explicitly definable by a formula.[^o16]

A real number may be either [computable](wikipedia://en/6206) or uncomputable; either [algorithmically random](wikipedia://en/4257548) or not; and either [arithmetically random](wikipedia://en/4257548#Stronger_than_Martin-Löf_randomness) or not.

## History {#History}

*Image: Number-systems.svg.* Real numbers $(\mathbb{R})$ include the rational numbers $(\mathbb{Q})$, which include the integers $(\mathbb{Z})$, which in turn include the natural numbers $(\mathbb{N})$

[Simple fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29#Simple,_common,_or_vulgar_fractions) were used by the [Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Egypt) around 1000 BC; the [Vedic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_civilization) "[Shulba Sutras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shulba_Sutras)" ("The rules of chords") in c. 600 BC include what may be the first "use" of irrational numbers. The concept of irrationality was implicitly accepted by early [Indian mathematicians](wikipedia://en/1848052) such as [Manava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manava) (c. 750–690 BC), who was aware that the [square roots](wikipedia://en/29208) of certain numbers, such as 2 and 61, could not be exactly determined.[^o18]

Around 500 BC, the [Greek mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) led by [Pythagoras](wikipedia://en/23275) also realized that the [square root of 2](wikipedia://en/341682) is irrational.

For Greek mathematicians, numbers were only the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474). Real numbers were called "proportions", being the ratios of two lengths, or equivalently being measures of a length in terms of another length, called unit length. Two lengths are "commensurable", if there is a unit in which they are both measured by integers, that is, in modern terminology, if their ratio is a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024). [Eudoxus of Cnidus](wikipedia://en/48388) (c. 390−340 BC) provided a definition of the equality of two irrational proportions in a way that is similar to [Dedekind cuts](wikipedia://en/45569) (introduced more than 2,000 years later), except that he did not use any [arithmetic operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_operation) other than multiplication of a length by a natural number (see [Eudoxus of Cnidus](wikipedia://en/48388)). This may be viewed as the first definition of the real numbers.

The [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) brought about the acceptance of [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero), [negative numbers](wikipedia://en/154616), integers, and [fractional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29) numbers, first by [Indian](wikipedia://en/1848052) and [Chinese mathematicians](wikipedia://en/1524543), and then by [Arabic mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_in_medieval_Islam), who were also the first to treat irrational numbers as algebraic objects (the latter being made possible by the development of algebra). Arabic mathematicians merged the concepts of "[number](wikipedia://en/21690)" and "[magnitude](wikipedia://en/577301)" into a more general idea of real numbers.[^o20] The Egyptian mathematician [Abū Kāmil Shujā ibn Aslam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abū_Kāmil_Shujā_ibn_Aslam) (c. 850–930) was the first to accept irrational numbers as solutions to [quadratic equations](wikipedia://en/25175), or as [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) in an [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) (often in the form of square roots, [cube roots](wikipedia://en/292506), and [fourth roots](wikipedia://en/235029)).[^o21] In Europe, such numbers, not commensurable with the numerical unit, were called *irrational* or [*surd*](wikipedia://en/235029) ("deaf").

In the 16th century, [Simon Stevin](wikipedia://en/72586) created the basis for modern [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214) notation, and insisted that there is no difference between rational and irrational numbers in this regard.

In the 17th century, [Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes) introduced the term "real" to describe roots of a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000), distinguishing them from "imaginary" numbers.

In the 18th and 19th centuries, there was much work on irrational and transcendental numbers. [Lambert](wikipedia://en/93085) (1761) gave a flawed proof that $\pi$ cannot be rational; [Legendre](wikipedia://en/49695) (1794) completed the proof[^o22] and showed that $\pi$ is not the square root of a rational number.[^o23] [Liouville](wikipedia://en/341810) (1840) showed that neither $e$ nor $e^{2}$ can be a root of an integer [quadratic equation](wikipedia://en/25175), and then established the existence of transcendental numbers; [Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216) (1873) extended and greatly simplified this proof.[^o24] [Hermite](wikipedia://en/22870422) (1873) proved that [$e$](wikipedia://en/9633) is transcendental, and [Lindemann](wikipedia://en/335736) (1882), showed that $\pi$ is transcendental. Lindemann's proof was much simplified by Weierstrass (1885), [Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) (1893), [Hurwitz](wikipedia://en/771690),[^o25] and [Gordan](wikipedia://en/1059850).[^o26]

The concept that many points existed between rational numbers, such as the square root of 2, was well known to the ancient Greeks. The existence of a continuous number line was considered self-evident, but the nature of this continuity, presently called [completeness](wikipedia://en/29322627), was not understood. The rigor developed for geometry did not cross over to the concept of numbers until the 1800s.[^o27]

### Modern analysis {#Modern_analysis}

The developers of [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) used real numbers and [limits](wikipedia://en/26551602) without defining them rigorously. In his *[Cours d'Analyse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cours_d'Analyse)* (1821), [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy) made calculus rigorous, but he used the real numbers without defining them, and assumed without proof that every [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy) sequence has a limit and that this limit is a real number.

In 1854 [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) highlighted the limitations of calculus in the method of [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038), showing the need for a rigorous definition of the real numbers.[^o29]

Beginning with [Richard Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106) in 1858, several mathematicians worked on the definition of the real numbers, including [Hermann Hankel](wikipedia://en/932515), [Charles Méray](wikipedia://en/17143991), and [Eduard Heine](wikipedia://en/608289), leading to the publication in 1872 of two independent definitions of real numbers, one by Dedekind, as [Dedekind cuts](wikipedia://en/45569), and the other one by [Georg Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216), as equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences. Several problems were left open by these definitions, which contributed to the [foundational crisis of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundational_crisis_of_mathematics). Firstly both definitions suppose that [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) and thus [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) are rigorously defined; this was done a few years later with [Peano axioms](wikipedia://en/25005). Secondly, both definitions involve [infinite sets](wikipedia://en/142679) (Dedekind cuts and sets of the elements of a Cauchy sequence), and Cantor's [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) was published several years later. Thirdly, these definitions imply [quantification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantification_%28logic%29) on infinite sets, and this cannot be formalized in the classical [logic](wikipedia://en/19636) of [first-order predicates](wikipedia://en/11344). This is one of the reasons for which [higher-order logics](wikipedia://en/562883) were developed in the first half of the 20th century.

In 1874 Cantor showed that the set of all real numbers is [uncountably infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountable), but the set of all algebraic numbers is [countably infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable). [Cantor's first uncountability proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantor's_first_uncountability_proof) was different from his famous [diagonal argument](wikipedia://en/51426) published in 1891.

## Formal definitions {#Formal_definitions}

The real number system $(\mathbb{R} ; {}+{} ; {}\cdot{} ; {}<{})$ can be defined [axiomatically](wikipedia://en/188401) up to an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828), which is described hereinafter. There are also many ways to construct "the" real number system, and a popular approach involves starting from natural numbers, then defining rational numbers algebraically, and finally defining real numbers as equivalence classes of their [Cauchy sequences](wikipedia://en/6085) or as Dedekind cuts, which are certain subsets of rational numbers.[^o33] Another approach is to start from some rigorous axiomatization of Euclidean geometry (say of Hilbert or of [Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920)), and then define the real number system geometrically. All these constructions of the real numbers have been shown to be equivalent, in the sense that the resulting number systems are [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic).

::: {#o35 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Axiomatic real number"}

### Axiomatic approach {#Axiomatic_approach}

Let $\mathbb{R}$ denote the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all real numbers. Then:

- The set $\mathbb{R}$ is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), meaning that [addition](wikipedia://en/61338) and [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) are defined and have the usual properties.
- The field $\mathbb{R}$ is ordered, meaning that there is a [total order](wikipedia://en/30330) $\ge$ such that for all real numbers $x$, $y$ and $z$:
  - if $x \ge y$, then $x+z \ge y+z$;
  - if $x \ge 0$ and $y \ge 0$, then $xy\ge 0$.
- The order is Dedekind-complete, meaning that every nonempty subset $S$ of $\mathbb{R}$ with an [upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound) in $\mathbb{R}$ has a [least upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) (a.k.a., supremum) in $\mathbb{R}$.

The last property applies to the real numbers but not to the rational numbers (or to [other more exotic ordered fields](wikipedia://en/22430#Examples_of_ordered_fields)). For example, $\{x \in \mathbb{Q} : x^2 < 2\}$ has a rational upper bound (e.g., 1.42), but no *least* rational upper bound, because [$\sqrt{2}$](wikipedia://en/29208) is not rational.

These properties imply the [Archimedean property](wikipedia://en/264158) (which is not implied by other definitions of completeness), which states that the set of integers has no upper bound in the reals. In fact, if this were false, then the integers would have a least upper bound $N$; then, $N-1$ would not be an upper bound, and there would be an integer $n$ such that $n > N-1$, and thus $n+1 > N$, which is a contradiction with the upper-bound property of $N$.

The real numbers are uniquely specified by the above properties. More precisely, given any two Dedekind-complete ordered fields $\mathbb{R}_1$ and $\mathbb{R}_2$, there exists a unique field [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) from $\mathbb{R}_1$ to $\mathbb{R_2}$. This uniqueness allows us to think of them as essentially the same mathematical object.

For another axiomatization of $\mathbb{R}$ see [Tarski's axiomatization of the reals](wikipedia://en/4952620).

:::

### Construction from the rational numbers {#Construction_from_the_rational_numbers}

The real numbers can be constructed as a [completion](wikipedia://en/7832) of the rational numbers, in such a way that a sequence defined by a decimal or binary expansion like (3; 3.1; 3.14; 3.141; 3.1415; ...) [converges](wikipedia://en/285773) to a unique real number—in this case $\pi$. For details and other constructions of real numbers, see *[Construction of the real numbers](wikipedia://en/296666)*.

:::

## Applications and connections {#Applications_and_connections}

### Physics {#Physics}

In the physical sciences most physical constants, such as the universal gravitational constant, and physical variables, such as position, mass, speed, and electric charge, are modeled using real numbers. In fact the fundamental physical theories such as [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism), [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), and the [Standard Model](wikipedia://en/47641) are described using mathematical structures, typically [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifolds) or [Hilbert spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_spaces), that are based on the real numbers, although actual measurements of physical quantities are of finite [accuracy and precision](wikipedia://en/41932).

Physicists have occasionally suggested that a more fundamental theory would replace the real numbers with quantities that do not form a continuum, but such proposals remain speculative.[^o39]

### Logic {#Logic}

The real numbers are most often formalized using the [Zermelo–Fraenkel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo–Fraenkel) axiomatization of set theory, but some mathematicians study the real numbers with other logical foundations of mathematics. In particular, the real numbers are also studied in [reverse mathematics](wikipedia://en/326365) and in [constructive mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructivism_%28mathematics%29).[^o41]

The [hyperreal numbers](wikipedia://en/51429) as developed by [Edwin Hewitt](wikipedia://en/7482590), [Abraham Robinson](wikipedia://en/46024), and others extend the set of the real numbers by introducing [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) and infinite numbers, allowing for building [infinitesimal calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal_calculus) in a way closer to the original intuitions of [Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz), [Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler), [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy), and others.

[Edward Nelson](wikipedia://en/387561)'s [internal set theory](wikipedia://en/865686) enriches the [Zermelo–Fraenkel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo–Fraenkel) set theory syntactically by introducing a unary predicate "standard". In this approach, infinitesimals are (non-"standard") elements of the set of the real numbers (rather than being elements of an extension thereof, as in Robinson's theory).

The [continuum hypothesis](wikipedia://en/5705) posits that the cardinality of the set of the real numbers is $\aleph_1$; i.e. the smallest infinite [cardinal number](wikipedia://en/6173) after $\aleph_0$, the cardinality of the integers. [Paul Cohen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Cohen_%28mathematician%29) proved in 1963 that it is an axiom independent of the other axioms of set theory; that is: one may choose either the continuum hypothesis or its negation as an axiom of set theory, without contradiction.

::: {#o43 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Computation"}

### Computation {#Computation}

[Electronic calculators](wikipedia://en/7593) and [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computers) cannot operate on arbitrary real numbers, because finite computers cannot directly store infinitely many digits or other infinite representations. Nor do they usually even operate on arbitrary [definable real numbers](wikipedia://en/8361), which are inconvenient to manipulate.

Instead, computers typically work with finite-precision approximations called [floating-point numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating-point_number), a representation similar to [scientific notation](wikipedia://en/59715). The achievable precision is limited by the [data storage space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_data_storage) allocated for each number, whether as [fixed-point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed-point_arithmetic), floating-point, or [arbitrary-precision numbers](wikipedia://en/600892), or some other representation. Most [scientific computation](wikipedia://en/1181008) uses [binary](wikipedia://en/238686) floating-point arithmetic, often a [64-bit representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-precision_floating-point_format) with around 16 decimal [digits of precision](wikipedia://en/317062). Real numbers satisfy the [usual rules of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/10603#Definition_and_illustration), but [floating-point numbers do not](wikipedia://en/11376#Accuracy_problems). The field of [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506) studies the [stability](wikipedia://en/233807) and [accuracy](wikipedia://en/41932) of numerical [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) implemented with approximate arithmetic.

Alternately, [computer algebra systems](wikipedia://en/54427) can operate on irrational quantities exactly by [manipulating symbolic formulas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_computation) for them (such as $\sqrt{2},$ $\arctan 5,$ or $\int_0^1 x^x \,dx$) rather than their rational or decimal approximation.[^o44] But exact and symbolic arithmetic also have limitations: for instance, they are computationally more expensive; it is not in general possible to determine whether two symbolic expressions are equal (the [constant problem](wikipedia://en/18308428)); and arithmetic operations can cause [exponential](wikipedia://en/191933) explosion in the size of representation of a single number (for instance, squaring a rational number roughly doubles the number of digits in its numerator and denominator, and squaring a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) roughly doubles its number of terms), overwhelming finite computer storage.[^o45]

A real number is called *[computable](wikipedia://en/6206)* if there exists an algorithm that yields its digits. Because there are only [countably](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_infinite) many algorithms,[^o46] but an uncountable number of reals, [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) real numbers fail to be computable. Moreover, the equality of two computable numbers is an [undecidable problem](wikipedia://en/15631055). Some [constructivists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructivism_%28mathematics%29) accept the existence of only those reals that are computable. The set of [definable numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definable_number) is broader, but still only countable.

:::

### Set theory {#Set_theory}

In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), specifically [descriptive set theory](wikipedia://en/396619), the [Baire space](wikipedia://en/2078245) is used as a surrogate for the real numbers since the latter have some topological properties (connectedness) that are a technical inconvenience. Elements of Baire space are referred to as "reals".

## Vocabulary and notation {#Vocabulary_and_notation}

*Image: Real number symbol.svg.* Variations of the real number symbol, from the letters I and R in close proximity (early math books typeset by I with backspace R), serif-symbol from blackboard bold with serif set, MathDS font in LaTeX, and MathBB in LaTeX

The [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all real numbers is denoted as $\mathbb{R}$ ([blackboard bold](wikipedia://en/40276)) or $\mathbf{R}$ (upright bold).[^o49]

As it is naturally endowed with the structure of a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), the expression *field of real numbers* is frequently used when its algebraic properties are under consideration.

The sets of positive real numbers and negative real numbers are often noted $\mathbb{R}^+$ and $\mathbb{R}^-$,[^o50] respectively; $\mathbb{R}_+$ and $\mathbb{R}_-$ are also used.[^o51] The non-negative real numbers can be noted $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ but one often sees this set noted $\mathbb{R}^+ \cup \{0\}.$[^o50]

The notation $\mathbb{R}^n$ refers to the set of the [$n$-tuples](wikipedia://en/132729) of elements of $\mathbb{R}$ ([real coordinate space](wikipedia://en/1482083)), which can be identified to the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of $n$ copies of $\mathbb{R}.$ It is an $n$-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over the field of the real numbers, often called the [coordinate space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_space) of dimension $n$; this space may be identified to the $n$-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) as soon as a [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706) has been chosen in the latter. In this identification, a [point](wikipedia://en/593693) of the Euclidean space is identified with the tuple of its [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates).

In mathematics *real* is used as an adjective, meaning that the underlying field is the field of the real numbers (or *the real field*). For example, *real [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859)*, *real polynomial* and *real [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944)*. The word is also used as a [noun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noun), meaning a real number (as in "the set of all reals").

## Generalizations and extensions {#Generalizations_and_extensions}

The real numbers can be generalized and extended in several different directions:

- The [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) contain solutions to all polynomial equations and hence are an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) unlike the real numbers. However, the complex numbers are not an ordered field. The complex numbers contain the real numbers. Sometimes a complex number which turns out to be real is said to have the property of "reality", as in "the reality of the eigenvalues".[^o53]
- The [affinely extended real number system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affinely_extended_real_number_system) adds two elements $+\infty$ and $-\infty$. It is a [compact space](wikipedia://en/6042). It is no longer a field, or even an additive group, but it still has a total order; moreover, it is a [complete lattice](wikipedia://en/44777).
- The [real projective line](wikipedia://en/46379102) adds only one value $\infty$. It is also a compact space. Again, it is no longer a field, or even an additive group. However, it allows division of a nonzero element by zero. It has [cyclic order](wikipedia://en/434673) with topology described by [point-pair separation](wikipedia://en/68571139).
- The [long real line](wikipedia://en/338403) pastes together $\aleph_1^* + \aleph_1$ copies of the real line plus a single point (here $\aleph_1^*$ denotes the reversed ordering of $\aleph_1$) to create an ordered set that is "locally" identical to the real numbers, but somehow longer; for instance, there is an order-preserving embedding of $\aleph_1$ in the long real line but not in the real numbers. The long real line is the largest ordered set that is complete and locally Archimedean. As with the previous two examples, this set is no longer a field or additive group.
- Ordered fields extending the reals are the [hyperreal numbers](wikipedia://en/51429) and the [surreal numbers](wikipedia://en/51432); both of them contain [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) and infinitely large numbers and are therefore [non-Archimedean ordered fields](wikipedia://en/31046646).
- [Self-adjoint operators](wikipedia://en/187408) on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) (for example, self-adjoint square complex [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859)) generalize the reals in many respects: they can be ordered (though not totally ordered), they are complete, all their [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) are real and they form a real [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112). [Positive-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_operator) operators correspond to the positive reals and [normal operators](wikipedia://en/190167) correspond to the complex numbers.
