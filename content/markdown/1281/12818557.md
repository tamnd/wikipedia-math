# Bryson of Heraclea

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bryson of Heraclea"}

**Bryson of Heraclea** (Βρύσων Ἡρακλεώτης, *gen*.: Βρύσωνος; fl. late 5th-century BCE) was an [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and [sophist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophist) who studied the solving the problems of [squaring the circle](wikipedia://en/201359) and calculating [pi](wikipedia://en/23601).

:::

## Life and work {#Life_and_work}

Little is known about the life of Bryson; he came from [Heraclea Pontica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclea_Pontica), and he may have been a pupil of [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates). He is mentioned in the *[13th Platonic Epistle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteenth_Letter_%28Plato%29)*,[^o3] and [Theopompus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theopompus) even claimed in his *Attack upon Plato* that [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) stole many ideas for his dialogues from Bryson of Heraclea.[^o4] He is known principally from [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308), who criticizes his method of squaring the circle.[^o5] He also upset Aristotle by asserting that [obscene language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obscene_language) does not exist.[^o6] [Diogenes Laërtius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diogenes_Laërtius)[^o7] and the [Suda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suda)[^o8] refer several times to a Bryson as a teacher of various philosophers, but since some of the philosophers mentioned lived in the late 4th-century BCE, it is possible that Bryson became confused with [Bryson of Achaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryson_of_Achaea), who may have lived around that time.[^o9]

### Pi and squaring the circle {#Pi_and_squaring_the_circle}

Bryson, along with his contemporary, [Antiphon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiphon_%28person%29), was the first to [inscribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscribe) a polygon inside a circle, find the [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621)'s area, double the number of sides of the polygon, and repeat the process, resulting in a [lower bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_bound) approximation of the [area of a circle](wikipedia://en/1896782). "Sooner or later (they figured), ...\[there would be\] so many sides that the polygon ...\[would\] be a circle."[^o11] Bryson later followed the same procedure for polygons [circumscribing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumscribe) a circle, resulting in an [upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound) approximation of the area of a circle. With these calculations, Bryson was able to approximate π and further place lower and upper bounds on π's true value. [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) criticized this method,[^o12] but [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) would later use a [method](wikipedia://en/1721284) similar to that of Bryson and Antiphon to calculate π; however, Archimedes calculated the [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636) of a polygon instead of the area.

### Robert Kilwardby on Bryson's syllogism {#Robert_Kilwardby_on_Bryson's_syllogism}

The 13th-century English philosopher [Robert Kilwardby](wikipedia://en/153666) described Bryson's attempt of proving the quadrature of the circle as a [sophistical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophism) [syllogism](wikipedia://en/491442#The_syllogism)—one which "deceives in virtue of the fact that it promises to yield a conclusion producing knowledge on the basis of specific considerations and concludes on the basis of common considerations that can produce only belief."[^o14] His account of the syllogism is as follows:

Bryson's syllogism on the squaring of the circle was of this sort, it is said: *In any genus in which one can find a greater and a lesser than something, one can find what is equal; but in the genus of squares one can find a greater and a lesser than a circle; therefore, one can also find a square equal to a circle.* This syllogism is sophistical not because the consequence is false, and not because it produces a syllogism on the basis of apparently readily believable things-for it concludes necessarily and on the basis of what is readily believable. Instead, it is called sophistical and contentious \[*litigiosus*\] because it is based on common considerations and is dialectical when it should be based on specific considerations and be demonstrative.[^o15]
