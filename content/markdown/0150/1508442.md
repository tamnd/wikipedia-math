# Logarithmically concave function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Logarithmically concave function"}

In [convex analysis](wikipedia://en/3237201), a [non-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative) function $f: \mathbf{R}^{n} \to \mathbf{R}_{+}$ is **logarithmically concave** (or **log-concave** for short) if its [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) is a [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292), and if it satisfies the inequality

:::

$$f(\theta x + (1 - \theta) y) \geq f(x)^{\theta} f(y)^{1 - \theta}$$

for all $x, y \in \operatorname{dom} f$ and $0 < \theta < 1$. If $f$ is strictly positive, this is equivalent to saying that the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) of the function, $\log \circ f$, is [concave](wikipedia://en/263321); that is,

$$\log f(\theta x + (1 - \theta) y) \geq \theta \log f(x) + (1-\theta) \log f(y)$$

for all $x, y \in \operatorname{dom} f$ and $0 < \theta < 1$.

Examples of log-concave functions are the 0-1 [indicator functions](wikipedia://en/240790) of convex sets (which requires the more flexible definition), and the [Gaussian function](wikipedia://en/245552).

Similarly, a function is *[log-convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Log-convex)* if it satisfies the reverse inequality

$$f(\theta x + (1 - \theta) y) \leq f(x)^{\theta} f(y)^{1 - \theta}$$

for all $x, y \in \operatorname{dom} f$ and $0 < \theta < 1$.

For non-negative discrete functions $f: \mathbf{Z} \to \mathbf{R}_{+}$, it is log-concave [^o2] if

$$f(k)^2 \geq f(k+1)f(k-1)$$

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- A log-concave function is also [quasi-concave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-concave_function). This follows from the fact that the logarithm is monotone implying that the [superlevel sets](wikipedia://en/559622#Sublevel_and_superlevel_sets) of this function are convex.[^o5]
- Every concave function that is nonnegative on its domain is log-concave. However, the reverse does not necessarily hold. An example is the [Gaussian function](wikipedia://en/245552) $f(x)$ = $\exp(-x^{2}/2)$ which is log-concave since $\log f(x)$ = $-x^{2}/2$ is a concave function of $x$. But $f$ is not concave since the [second derivative](wikipedia://en/3224219) is positive for |$x$| > 1:

$$f''(x)=e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}} (x^2-1) \nleq 0$$

- From above two points, [concavity](wikipedia://en/263321) $\Rightarrow$ log-concavity $\Rightarrow$ [quasiconcavity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasiconcave_function).
- A twice differentiable, nonnegative function with a convex domain is log-concave [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) for all $x$ satisfying $f(x) > 0$,

$f(x)\nabla^2f(x) \preceq \nabla f(x)\nabla f(x)^T$,[^o5]

i.e.

$f(x)\nabla^2f(x) - \nabla f(x)\nabla f(x)^T$ is

[negative semi-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix). For functions of one variable, this condition simplifies to

$$f(x)f''(x) \leq (f'(x))^2$$

:::

## Operations preserving log-concavity {#Operations_preserving_log-concavity}

- Products: The product of log-concave functions is also log-concave. Indeed, if $f$ and $g$ are log-concave functions, then $\log f$ and $\log g$ are concave by definition. Therefore

$$\log\,f(x) + \log\,g(x) = \log(f(x)g(x))$$

is concave, and hence also $f g$ is log-concave.

- [Marginals](wikipedia://en/506077): if $f(x, y)$: $\mathbf{R}^{n+m} \to \mathbf{R}$ is log-concave, then

$$g(x)=\int f(x,y) dy$$

is log-concave (see [Prékopa–Leindler inequality](wikipedia://en/10570298)).

- This implies that [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) preserves log-concavity, since $h(x, y)$ = $f(x - y) g(y)$ is log-concave if $f$ and $g$ are log-concave, and therefore

$$(f*g)(x)=\int f(x-y)g(y) dy = \int h(x,y) dy$$

is log-concave.

## Log-concave distributions {#Log-concave_distributions}

Log-concave distributions are necessary for a number of algorithms, e.g. [adaptive rejection sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptive_rejection_sampling). Every distribution with log-concave density is a [maximum entropy probability distribution](wikipedia://en/1813193) with specified mean *μ* and [Deviation risk measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deviation_risk_measure) *D*.[^o8] As it happens, many common [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) are log-concave. Some examples:[^o9]

- the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) and [multivariate normal distributions](wikipedia://en/50347),
- the [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906),
- the [uniform distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29) over any [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292),
- the [binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876),
- the [logistic distribution](wikipedia://en/542666),
- the [extreme value distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_value_distribution),
- the [Laplace distribution](wikipedia://en/1106213),
- the [chi distribution](wikipedia://en/1901933),
- the [hyperbolic secant distribution](wikipedia://en/2026043),
- the [Wishart distribution](wikipedia://en/364996), if *n* ≥ *p* + 1,[^o10]
- the [Dirichlet distribution](wikipedia://en/1117833), if all parameters are ≥ 1,[^o10]
- the [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079) if the [shape parameter](wikipedia://en/9210865) is ≥ 1,
- the [chi-square distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi-square_distribution) if the number of degrees of freedom is ≥ 2,
- the [beta distribution](wikipedia://en/207074) if both shape parameters are ≥ 1, and
- the [Weibull distribution](wikipedia://en/206948) if the shape parameter is ≥ 1.

Note that all of the parameter restrictions have the same basic source: The exponent of non-negative quantity must be non-negative in order for the function to be log-concave.

The following distributions are non-log-concave for all parameters:

- the [Student's t-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375),
- the [Cauchy distribution](wikipedia://en/7003),
- the [Pareto distribution](wikipedia://en/53057),
- the [log-normal distribution](wikipedia://en/102476), and
- the [F-distribution](wikipedia://en/332116).

Note that the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) (CDF) of all log-concave distributions is also log-concave. However, some non-log-concave distributions also have log-concave CDF's:

- the [log-normal distribution](wikipedia://en/102476),
- the [Pareto distribution](wikipedia://en/53057),
- the [Weibull distribution](wikipedia://en/206948) when the shape parameter \< 1, and
- the [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079) when the shape parameter \< 1.

The following are among the properties of log-concave distributions:

- If a density is log-concave, so is its [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) (CDF).
- If a multivariate density is log-concave, so is the [marginal density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_density) over any subset of variables.
- The sum of two independent log-concave [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) is log-concave. This follows from the fact that the convolution of two log-concave functions is log-concave.
- The product of two log-concave functions is log-concave. This means that [joint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_distribution) densities formed by multiplying two probability densities (e.g. the [normal-gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/10896234), which always has a shape parameter ≥ 1) will be log-concave. This property is heavily used in general-purpose [Gibbs sampling](wikipedia://en/509709) programs such as [BUGS](wikipedia://en/35129887) and [JAGS](wikipedia://en/21845564), which are thereby able to use [adaptive rejection sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptive_rejection_sampling) over a wide variety of [conditional distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_distribution) derived from the product of other distributions.
- If a density is log-concave, so is its [survival function](wikipedia://en/30876757).[^o9]
- If a density is log-concave, it has a monotone [hazard rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazard_rate) (MHR), and is a [regular distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_distribution_%28economics%29) since the derivative of the logarithm of the survival function is the negative hazard rate, and by concavity is monotone i.e.

$\frac{d}{dx}\log\left(1-F(x)\right) = -\frac{f(x)}{1-F(x)}$ which is decreasing as it is the derivative of a concave function.
