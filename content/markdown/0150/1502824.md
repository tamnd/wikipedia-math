# Félix Delastelle

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Félix Delastelle"}

**Félix-Marie Delastelle** (2 January 1840 – 2 April 1902) was a French cryptographer, best known for inventing the [bifid cipher](wikipedia://en/632918),[^o2] first presented in the *Revue du Génie civil* in 1895 under the name of "cryptographie nouvelle". This cipher combines [fractionation](wikipedia://en/48358#Fractionation) with [transposition](wikipedia://en/48358), and was an early cipher to implement the principles of [confusion and diffusion](wikipedia://en/537555). David Kahn described it as a "system of considerable importance in cryptology."[^o3]

:::

Delastelle's other [polygraphic substitution ciphers](wikipedia://en/48356#Polygraphic_substitution) included the [trifid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trifid_cipher)[^o4] and [four-square ciphers](wikipedia://en/1502284).[^o5] The last of these is a variant on the earlier [Playfair cipher](wikipedia://en/173586): Delastelle may have been unaware of Playfair, but he had certainly read of the fractionating cipher described by [Pliny Chase](wikipedia://en/26054310) in 1859.

There are few biographical details. Félix-Marie's father, a master mariner, was lost at sea in 1843. Félix attended the College of Saint-Malo until 1860. After leaving school, he worked in the local port, as a bonded warehouseman, for forty years, and pursued his interest in amateur cryptography as a hobby.

Following his retirement in 1900, he rented a single room in a holiday hotel where he wrote a 160-page book *Traité Élémentaire de Cryptographie* which he completed in May 1901. On hearing news of his brother's sudden death, he collapsed and died in April 1902. His book appeared three months later, published by Gauthier-Villars of Paris.

Delastelle is unusual for being an amateur cryptographer at a time when significant contributions to the subject were made by professional soldiers, diplomats and academics.
