# Orbital plane of reference

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Orbital plane of reference"}

In [celestial mechanics](wikipedia://en/102182), the **orbital plane of reference** (or **orbital reference plane**) is the [plane](wikipedia://en/84029) used to define [orbital elements](wikipedia://en/98663) (positions). The two main orbital elements that are measured with respect to the plane of reference are the [inclination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclination) and the [longitude of the ascending node](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude_of_the_ascending_node).

:::

Depending on the type of body being described, there are four different kinds of reference planes that are typically used:

- The [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264) or [invariable plane](wikipedia://en/1673352) for planets, asteroids, comets, etc. within the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System), as these bodies generally have orbits that lie close to the ecliptic.
- The [equatorial plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_plane) of the orbited body for satellites orbiting with small [semi-major axes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-major_axis)
- The local [Laplace plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace_plane) for satellites orbiting with intermediate-to-large semi-major axes
- The plane tangent to [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239) for extrasolar objects

On the plane of reference, a zero-point must be defined from which the angles of [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude) are measured. This is usually defined as the point on the celestial sphere where the plane crosses the prime hour circle (the [hour circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hour_circle) occupied by the [First Point of Aries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Point_of_Aries)), also known as the [equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox).
