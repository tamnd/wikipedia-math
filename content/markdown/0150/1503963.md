# Chebyshev distance

floatright

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Chebyshev distance"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Chebyshev distance** (or **Tchebychev distance**), **maximum metric**, or **L<sub>∞</sub> metric**[^o2] is a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) defined on a [real coordinate space](wikipedia://en/1482083) where the [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) between two [points](wikipedia://en/593693) is the greatest of their differences along any coordinate dimension.[^o3] It is named after [Pafnuty Chebyshev](wikipedia://en/149568).

:::

It is also known as **chessboard distance**, since in the game of [chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess) the minimum number of moves needed by a [king](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_%28chess%29) to go from one square on a [chessboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chessboard) to another equals the Chebyshev distance between the centers of the squares, if the squares have side length one, as represented in 2-D spatial coordinates with axes aligned to the edges of the board.[^o4] For example, the Chebyshev distance between f6 and e2 equals 4.

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The Chebyshev distance between two vectors or points *a* and *b*, with standard coordinates $a_i$ and $b_i$, respectively, is

$$D(a,b) = \max_i(|a_i - b_i|).$$

This equals the limit of the [L<sub>*p*</sub> metrics](wikipedia://en/45194):

$$D(a,b)=\lim_{p \to \infty} \bigg( \sum_{i=1}^n \left| a_i - b_i \right|^p \bigg)^{1/p},$$

hence it is also known as the L<sub>∞</sub> metric.

Mathematically, the Chebyshev distance is a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) induced by the [supremum norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum_norm) or [uniform norm](wikipedia://en/349755). It is an example of an [injective metric](wikipedia://en/7840768).

In two dimensions, i.e. [plane geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_geometry), if the points *a* and *b* have [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$, their Chebyshev distance is

$$D_{\rm Chebyshev} = \max \left ( \left | x_2 - x_1 \right | , \left | y_2 - y_1 \right | \right ) .$$

Under this metric, a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) of [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) *r*, which is the set of points with Chebyshev distance *r* from a center point, is a square whose sides have the length 2*r* and are parallel to the coordinate axes.

On a chessboard, where one is using a *discrete* Chebyshev distance, rather than a continuous one, the circle of radius *r* is a square of side lengths 2*r,* measuring from the centers of squares, and thus each side contains 2*r*+1 squares; for example, the circle of radius 1 on a chess board is a 3×3 square.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

*Image: Minkowski\_distance\_examples.svg.* Comparison of Chebyshev, Euclidean and Manhattan ('taxicab') distances for the hypotenuse of a 3-4-5 triangle on a chessboard

In one dimension, all L<sub>*p*</sub> metrics are equal – they are just the absolute value of the difference.

The two-dimensional [Manhattan distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_distance) has "circles" i.e. [level sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Level_sets) in the form of squares, with sides of length $\sqrt{2}$*r*, oriented at an angle of π/4 (45°) to the coordinate axes, so the planar Chebyshev distance can be viewed as equivalent by rotation and scaling to (i.e. a [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) of) the planar Manhattan distance.

However, this geometric equivalence between L<sub>1</sub> and L<sub>∞</sub> metrics does not generalize to higher dimensions. A [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) formed using the Chebyshev distance as a metric is a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) with each face perpendicular to one of the coordinate axes, but a sphere formed using [Manhattan distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_distance) is an [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635): these are [dual polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_polyhedra), but among cubes, only the square (and 1-dimensional line segment) are [self-dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-dual_polyhedra) [polytopes](wikipedia://en/23471). Nevertheless, it is true that in all finite-dimensional spaces the L<sub>1</sub> and L<sub>∞</sub> metrics are mathematically dual to each other.

On a grid (such as a chessboard), the points at a Chebyshev distance of 1 of a point are the [Moore neighborhood](wikipedia://en/4323874) of that point.

The Chebyshev distance is the limiting case of the order-$p$ [Minkowski distance](wikipedia://en/20326689), when $p$ reaches [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824).

:::

::: {#o10 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The Chebyshev distance is sometimes used in [warehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warehouse) [logistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logistics),[^o11] as it effectively measures the time an [overhead crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhead_crane) takes to move an object (as the crane can move on the x and y axes at the same time but at the same speed along each axis).

It is also widely used in electronic [computer-aided manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer-aided_manufacturing) (CAM) applications, in particular, in optimization algorithms for these.

:::

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

For the [sequence space](wikipedia://en/1701075) of infinite-length sequences of real or complex numbers, the Chebyshev distance generalizes to the [$\ell^\infty$-norm](wikipedia://en/46395425); this norm is sometimes called the Chebyshev norm. For the space of (real or complex-valued) functions, the Chebyshev distance generalizes to the [uniform norm](wikipedia://en/349755).

:::
