# Geodesics as Hamiltonian flows

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Geodesics as Hamiltonian flows"}

*Image: Cogeodesic flow along a Riemannian geodesic.svg.* Along a constant-speed geodesic curve, the unit velocity vector is transported, creating the geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle. Dually, the unit co-vector is also transported, creating the cogeodesic flow on the unit cotangent bundle.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the [geodesic equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_equation) are second-order non-linear [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309), and are commonly presented in the form of [Euler–Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Lagrange) equations of motion. However, they can also be presented as a set of coupled first-order equations, in the form of [Hamilton's equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton's_equations). This latter formulation is developed in this article.

:::

This can be interpreted in contact geometry as well using [Reeb vector flow](wikipedia://en/10761147). Reeb vector flow applies more generally to the geodesic flow on [Finsler manifolds](wikipedia://en/385510).[^o2]

## Overview {#Overview}

It is frequently said that [geodesics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesics) are "straight lines in [curved space](wikipedia://en/2557627)". By using the Hamilton–Jacobi approach to the [geodesic equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_equation), this statement can be given a very intuitive meaning: geodesics describe the motions of particles that are not experiencing any forces. In flat space, it is well known that a particle moving in a straight line will continue to move in a straight line if it experiences no external forces; this is [Newton's first law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_first_law). The Hamiltonian describing such motion is well known to be $H=p^2/2m$ with *p* being the [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431). It is the [conservation of momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_momentum) that leads to the straight motion of a particle. On a curved surface, exactly the same ideas are at play, except that, in order to measure distances correctly, one must use the [Riemannian metric](wikipedia://en/195795). To measure momenta correctly, one must use the inverse of the metric. The motion of a [free particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_particle) on a curved surface still has exactly the same form as above, i.e. consisting entirely of a [kinetic term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_term). The resulting motion is still, in a sense, a "straight line", which is why it is sometimes said that geodesics are "straight lines in curved space". This idea is developed in greater detail below.

## Geodesics as an application of the principle of least action {#Geodesics_as_an_application_of_the_principle_of_least_action}

Given a ([pseudo](wikipedia://en/287555)-)[Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) *M*, a [geodesic](wikipedia://en/91096) may be defined as the curve that results from the application of the [principle of least action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_least_action). A differential equation describing their shape may be derived, using [variational principles](wikipedia://en/491097), by minimizing (or finding the extremum) of the [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) of a curve. Given a [smooth curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_curve)

$$\gamma:I\to M$$

that maps an interval *I* of the [real number line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number_line) to the manifold *M*, one writes the energy

$$E(\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\int_I g(\dot \gamma(t),\dot\gamma(t))\,dt,$$

where $\dot\gamma(t)$ is the [tangent vector](wikipedia://en/191999) to the curve $\gamma$ at point $t \in I$. Here, $g(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) on the manifold *M*.

Using the energy given above as the action, one may choose to solve either the [Euler–Lagrange equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Lagrange_equations) or the [Hamilton–Jacobi equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton–Jacobi_equations). Both methods give the [geodesic equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_equation) as the solution; however, the Hamilton–Jacobi equations provide greater insight into the structure of the manifold, as shown below. In terms of the [local coordinates](wikipedia://en/304139) on *M*, the (Euler–Lagrange) geodesic equation is

$$\frac{d^2x^a}{dt^2} + \Gamma^{a}_{bc}\frac{dx^b}{dt}\frac{dx^c}{dt} = 0$$

where the *x*<sup>*a*</sup>(*t*) are the coordinates of the curve γ(*t*), $\Gamma^{a}_{bc}$ are the [Christoffel symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christoffel_symbol), and repeated indices imply the use of the [summation convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summation_convention).

:::

## Hamiltonian approach to the geodesic equations {#Hamiltonian_approach_to_the_geodesic_equations}

Geodesics can be understood to be the [Hamiltonian flows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_flow) of a special [Hamiltonian vector field](wikipedia://en/1818270) defined on the [cotangent space](wikipedia://en/6620) of the manifold. The Hamiltonian is constructed from the metric on the manifold, and is thus a [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) consisting entirely of the kinetic term.

The geodesic equations are second-order differential equations; they can be re-expressed as first-order equations by introducing additional independent variables, as shown below. Note that a coordinate neighborhood *U* with coordinates *x*<sub>*a*</sub> induces a *[local trivialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_trivialization)* of

$$T^*M|_{U}\simeq U \times \mathbb{R}^n$$

by the map which sends a point

$$\eta \in T_x^*M|_{U}$$

of the form $\eta = p_a dx^a$ to the point $(x,p_a) \in U\times\mathbb{R}^n$. Then introduce the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/1818270) as

$$H(x,p)=\frac{1}{2}g^{ab}(x)p_a p_b.$$

Here, *g*<sup>*ab*</sup>(*x*) is the inverse of the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795): *g*<sup>*ab*</sup>(*x*)*g*<sub>*bc*</sub>(*x*) = $\delta^a_c$. The behavior of the metric tensor under coordinate transformations implies that *H* is [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under a change of variable. The geodesic equations can then be written as

$$\dot{x}^a = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_a} = g^{ab}(x) p_b$$

and

$$\dot{p}_a = - \frac {\partial H}{\partial x^a} = -\frac{1}{2} \frac {\partial g^{bc}(x)}{\partial x^a} p_b p_c.$$

The [flow](wikipedia://en/2619735) determined by these equations is called the **cogeodesic flow**; a simple substitution of one into the other obtains the Euler–Lagrange equations, which give the **geodesic flow** on the tangent bundle *TM*. The geodesic lines are the projections of integral curves of the geodesic flow onto the manifold *M*. This is a [Hamiltonian flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_flow), and the Hamiltonian is constant along the geodesics:

$$\frac{dH}{dt} = \frac {\partial H}{\partial x^a} \dot{x}^a + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_a} \dot{p}_a = - \dot{p}_a \dot{x}^a + \dot{x}^a \dot{p}_a = 0.$$

Thus, the geodesic flow splits the [cotangent bundle](wikipedia://en/221600) into [level sets](wikipedia://en/559622) of constant energy

$$M_E = \{ (x,p) \in T^*M : H(x,p)=E \}$$

for each energy *E* ≥ 0, so that

$$T^*M=\bigcup_{E \ge 0} M_E.$$
