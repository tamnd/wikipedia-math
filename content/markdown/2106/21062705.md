# Crown graph

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Crown graph"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a branch of mathematics, a **crown graph** on $2n$ [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) is an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) with two sets of vertices $\{u_{1}, u_{2}, \ldots, u_{n}\}$ and $\{v_{1}, v_{2}, \ldots, v_{n}\}$ and with an edge from $u_{i}$ to $v_{j}$ whenever $i \ne j$.

:::

The crown graph can be viewed as a [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) from which the edges of a [perfect matching](wikipedia://en/44031) have been removed, as the [bipartite double cover](wikipedia://en/21241712) of a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816), as the [tensor product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_product_of_graphs) $K_{n} \times K_{2}$, as the complement of the Cartesian direct product of $K_{n}$ and $K_{2}$, or as a [bipartite Kneser graph](wikipedia://en/2724154) $H_{n,1}$ representing the 1-item and $(n - 1)$-item subsets of an $n$-item set, with an edge between two subsets whenever one is contained in the other.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The 6-vertex crown graph forms a [cycle](wikipedia://en/878962), and the 8-vertex crown graph is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) to the graph of a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285). In the [Schläfli double six](wikipedia://en/35301595), a configuration of 12 lines and 30 points in three-dimensional space, the twelve lines intersect each other in the pattern of a 12-vertex crown graph.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

*Image: Bipartite-dimension-biclique-cover.svg.* A biclique cover of the ten-vertex crown graph

The number of edges in a crown graph is the [pronic number](wikipedia://en/652733) $n(n - 1)$. Its [achromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achromatic_number) is $n$: one can find a [complete coloring](wikipedia://en/690736) by choosing each pair $\{u_{i}, v_{i}\}$ as one of the color classes.[^n1] Crown graphs are [symmetric](wikipedia://en/1577896) and [distance-transitive](wikipedia://en/7768222). (Archdeacon Debowsky Dinitz Gavlas 2004) describe partitions of the edges of a crown graph into equal-length cycles.

The $2n$-vertex crown graph may be embedded into four-dimensional Euclidean space in such a way that all of its edges have unit length. However, this embedding may also place some non-adjacent vertices a unit distance apart. An embedding in which edges are at unit distance and non-edges are not at unit distance requires at least $n - 2$ dimensions. This example shows that a graph may require very different dimensions to be represented as a [unit distance graph](wikipedia://en/7158665) and as a strict unit distance graph.[^n2]

The minimum number of [complete bipartite subgraphs](wikipedia://en/525320) needed to cover the edges of a crown graph (its [bipartite dimension](wikipedia://en/19720831), or the size of a minimum biclique cover) is

$$\sigma(n)=\min \left\{\,k \mid n \le \binom{k}{\lfloor k/2 \rfloor} \,\right\},$$

the inverse function of the [central binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/2128244).[^n3]

The [complement graph](wikipedia://en/1175649) of a $2n$-vertex crown graph is the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/2856852) of [complete graphs](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_{2} \text{▢} K_{n}$, or equivalently the $2 \times n$ [rook's graph](wikipedia://en/7324959).

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

In [etiquette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etiquette), a traditional rule for arranging guests at a dinner table is that men and women should alternate positions, and that no married couple should sit next to each other.[^o13] The arrangements satisfying this rule, for a party consisting of *n* married couples, can be described as the [Hamiltonian cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle) of a crown graph. For instance, the arrangements of vertices shown in the figure can be interpreted as seating charts of this type in which each husband and wife are seated as far apart as possible. The problem of counting the number of possible seating arrangements, or almost equivalently[^o14] the number of Hamiltonian cycles in a crown graph, is known in combinatorics as the [ménage problem](wikipedia://en/21068755); for crown graphs with 6, 8, 10, ... vertices the number of (oriented) Hamiltonian cycles is

2, 12, 312, 9600, 416880, 23879520, 1749363840, ...

Crown graphs can be used to show that [greedy coloring](wikipedia://en/21051195) algorithms behave badly in the worst case: if the vertices of a crown graph are presented to the algorithm in the order *u*<sub>0</sub>, *v*<sub>0</sub>, *u*<sub>1</sub>, *v*<sub>1</sub>, etc., then a greedy coloring uses *n* colors, whereas the optimal number of colors is two. This construction is attributed to (Johnson 1974); crown graphs are sometimes called **Johnson’s graphs** with notation *J*<sub>*n*</sub>.[^n4] (Fürer 1995) uses crown graphs as part of a construction showing hardness of approximation of coloring problems.

(Matoušek 1996) uses distances in crown graphs as an example of a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) that is difficult to embed into a [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538).

As (Miklavič Potočnik 2003) show, crown graphs are one of a small number of different types of graphs that can occur as [distance-regular](wikipedia://en/7768943) [circulant graphs](wikipedia://en/3498787).

(Agarwal Alon Aronov Suri 1994) describe polygons that have crown graphs as their [visibility graphs](wikipedia://en/1448291); they use this example to show that representing visibility graphs as unions of complete bipartite graphs may not always be space-efficient.

A crown graph with 2*n* vertices, with its edges oriented from one side of the bipartition to the other, forms the standard example of a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) with [order dimension](wikipedia://en/3121852) *n*.
