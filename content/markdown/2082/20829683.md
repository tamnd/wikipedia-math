# Noncentral distribution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Noncentral distribution"}

**Noncentral distributions** are families of [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) that are related to other "central" families of distributions by means of a **noncentrality parameter**. Whereas the central distribution describes how a [test statistic](wikipedia://en/1416975) is distributed when the difference tested is [null](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis), noncentral distributions describe the distribution of a test statistic when the null is false (so the [alternative hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_hypothesis) is true). This leads to their use in calculating [statistical power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_power).

:::

If the noncentrality parameter of a distribution is zero, the distribution is identical to a distribution in the central family.[^o2] For example, the [Student's *t*-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375) is the central family of distributions for the [noncentral *t*-distribution](wikipedia://en/3287836) family.

Noncentrality parameters are used in the following distributions:

- [Noncentral t-distribution](wikipedia://en/3287836)
- [Noncentral chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/2601749)
- [Noncentral chi-distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noncentral_chi-distribution)
- [Noncentral F-distribution](wikipedia://en/1368010)
- [Noncentral beta distribution](wikipedia://en/32632892)

In general, noncentrality parameters occur in distributions that are transformations of a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462). The "central" versions are derived from normal distributions that have a [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) of zero; the noncentral versions generalize to arbitrary means. For example, the standard (central) [chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/113424) is the distribution of a sum of squared independent [standard normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal) distributions, i.e., normal distributions with mean 0, [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) 1. The [noncentral chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/2601749) generalizes this to normal distributions with arbitrary mean and variance.

Each of these distributions has a single noncentrality parameter. However, there are extended versions of these distributions which have two noncentrality parameters: the doubly noncentral beta distribution, the doubly noncentral F distribution and the doubly noncentral *t* distribution.[^o3] These types of distributions occur for distributions that are defined as the quotient of two independent distributions. When both source distributions are central (either with a zero mean or a zero noncentrality parameter, depending on the type of distribution), the result is a central distribution. When one is noncentral, a (singly) noncentral distribution results, while if both are noncentral, the result is a doubly noncentral distribution. As an example, a [*t*-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375) is defined (ignoring constant values) as the quotient of a normal distribution and the square root of an independent [chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/113424). Extending this definition to encompass a normal distribution with arbitrary mean produces a [noncentral t-distribution](wikipedia://en/3287836), while further extending it to allow a [noncentral chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/2601749) in the denominator while produces a [doubly noncentral t-distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_noncentral_t-distribution).

There are some "noncentral distributions" that are not usually formulated in terms of a "noncentrality parameter": see [noncentral hypergeometric distributions](wikipedia://en/11815074), for example.

The noncentrality parameter of the *t*-distribution may be negative or positive while the noncentral parameters of the other three distributions must be greater than zero.
