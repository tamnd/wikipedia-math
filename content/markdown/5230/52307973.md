# Tournament solution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Tournament solution"}

A **tournament solution** is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) that maps an [oriented complete graph](wikipedia://en/1427502) to a nonempty [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of its [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899). It can informally be thought of as a way to find the "best" alternatives among all of the alternatives that are "competing" against each other in the tournament. Tournament solutions originate from [social choice theory](wikipedia://en/1745670),[^o2][^o13][^o3][^o4] but have also been considered in [sports competition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_competition), [game theory](wikipedia://en/11924),[^o5] [multi-criteria decision analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-Criteria_Decision_Analysis), [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology),[^o6][^o7] [webpage ranking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_search_engine),[^o8] and [dueling bandit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dueling_bandit) problems.[^o9]

:::

In the context of [social choice theory](wikipedia://en/1745670), tournament solutions are closely related to Fishburn's C1 social choice functions,[^o10] and thus seek to show who are the strongest candidates in some sense.

*Image: 4-tournament.svg.* A tournament on 4 vertices: $A=\{1,2,3,4\}$, $\succ = \{(1,2),(1,4),(2,4),$$(3,1),(3,2),(4,3)\}$

::: {#o12 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A [tournament graph](wikipedia://en/1427502) $T$ is a tuple $(A,\succ)$ where $A$ is a set of vertices (called *alternatives*) and $\succ$ is a [connex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connex_relation) and asymmetric [binary relation](wikipedia://en/3931) over the vertices. In social choice theory, the binary relation typically represents the [pairwise majority comparison](wikipedia://en/1427502#Majority_relations) between alternatives.

A tournament solution is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f$ that maps each tournament $T = (A,\succ)$ to a nonempty subset $f(T)$ of the alternatives $A$ (called the *choice set*[^o13]) and does not distinguish between isomorphic tournaments:

If $h: A \rightarrow B$ is a [graph isomorphism](wikipedia://en/247577) between two tournaments $T = (A,\succ)$ and $\widetilde{T} = (B,\widetilde{\succ})$, then $a \in f(T) \Leftrightarrow h(a) \in f(\widetilde{T})$

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

Common examples of tournament solutions are the:[^o2][^o13]

- [Copeland set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copeland's_method)
- [Smith set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smith_set) ( Top cycle)
- Slater set
- [Bipartisan set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bipartisan_set)
- [Landau set](wikipedia://en/6152753)
- Banks set
- Minimal covering set
- Tournament equilibrium set
