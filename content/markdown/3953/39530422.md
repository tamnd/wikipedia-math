# Icosahedral number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Icosahedral number"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **icosahedral number** is a [figurate number](wikipedia://en/325260) that represents an [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084). The *n*th icosahedral number is given by the formula

:::

$${n(5n^2-5n+2) \over 2}.$$

The first such numbers are: [1](wikipedia://en/77591639), [12](wikipedia://en/208155), [48](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/48_%28number%29), [124](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/124_%28number%29), [255](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/255_%28number%29), 456, 742, 1128, 1629, 2260, 3036, 3972, 5083, … .

## History {#History}

The first study of icosahedral numbers appears to have been by [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525), around 1630, in his *De solidorum elementis*. Prior to Descartes, figurate numbers had been studied by the ancient Greeks and by [Johann Faulhaber](wikipedia://en/9768822), but only for [polygonal numbers](wikipedia://en/213996), [pyramidal numbers](wikipedia://en/509081), and [cubes](wikipedia://en/451445). Descartes introduced the study of figurate numbers based on the [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) and some [semiregular polyhedra](wikipedia://en/1548894); his work included the icosahedral numbers. However, *De solidorum elementis* was lost, and not rediscovered until 1860. In the meantime, icosahedral numbers had been studied again by other mathematicians, including [Friedrich Wilhelm Marpurg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Wilhelm_Marpurg) in 1774, [Georg Simon Klügel](wikipedia://en/19043679) in 1808, and [Sir Frederick Pollock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Frederick_Pollock,_1st_Baronet) in 1850.[^o3]
