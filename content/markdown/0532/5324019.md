# Grand potential

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Grand potential"}

The **grand potential** or **Landau potential** or **Landau free energy** is a quantity used in [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481), especially for [irreversible processes](wikipedia://en/636094) in [open systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_system_%28systems_theory%29). The grand potential is the characteristic state function for the [grand canonical ensemble](wikipedia://en/1129074).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The grand potential is defined by

$$\Phi_\text{G} \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{{}={}} U - T S - \mu N$$

where *U* is the [internal energy](wikipedia://en/340757), *T* is the [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050) of the system, *S* is the [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891), *μ* is the [chemical potential](wikipedia://en/218628), and *N* is the number of [particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle) in the system.[^o4]

The change in the grand potential is given by

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}\Phi_\mathrm{G} & = dU - T\,dS - S\,dT - \mu d\,N - N\,d\mu \\ & = - P\,dV - S\,dT - N\,d\mu \end{aligned}$$

where *P* is [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) and *V* is [volume](wikipedia://en/32498), using the [fundamental thermodynamic relation](wikipedia://en/5529757) (combined [first](wikipedia://en/166404) and [second](wikipedia://en/166404) [thermodynamic laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_laws));

$$\mathrm{d}U = T\,dS - P\,dV + \mu\,dN$$

When the system is in [thermodynamic equilibrium](wikipedia://en/265823), $\Phi_\text{G}$ is at a minimum. This can be seen by considering that $\mathrm{d}\Phi_\mathrm{G}$ is zero if the volume is fixed and the temperature and chemical potential have stopped evolving.

### Landau free energy {#Landau_free_energy}

Some authors refer to the grand potential as the *Landau free energy* or **Landau potential** and mark it as $\Omega$,[^o6][^o7] named after Russian physicist [Lev Landau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lev_Landau).

:::

## Homogeneous systems (vs. inhomogeneous systems) {#Homogeneous_systems_(vs._inhomogeneous_systems)}

In the case of a scale-invariant type of system (where a system of volume $\lambda V$ has exactly the same set of microstates as $\lambda$ systems of volume $V$), then when the system expands new particles and energy will flow in from the reservoir to fill the new volume with a homogeneous extension of the original system. The pressure, then, must be constant with respect to changes in volume:

$$\left(\frac{\partial \langle P \rangle}{\partial V}\right)_{\mu,T} = 0,$$

and all extensive quantities (particle number, energy, entropy, potentials, ...) must grow linearly with volume, e.g.

$$\left(\frac{\partial \langle N \rangle}{\partial V}\right)_{\mu,T} = \frac{N}{V}.$$

In this case we simply have $\Phi_\text{G} = - \langle P\rangle V$,[^o9] as well as the familiar relationship $G = \langle N \rangle \mu$ for the [Gibbs free energy](wikipedia://en/238181). The value of $\Phi_\text{G}$ can be understood as the work that can be extracted from the system by shrinking it down to nothing (putting all the particles and energy back into the reservoir). The fact that $\Phi_\text{G} = - \langle P\rangle V$ is negative implies that the extraction of particles from the system to the reservoir requires energy input.

Such homogeneous scaling does not exist in many systems. For example, when analyzing the ensemble of electrons in a single molecule or even a piece of metal floating in space, doubling the volume of the space does double the number of electrons in the material.[^o10] The problem here is that, although electrons and energy are exchanged with a reservoir, the material host is not allowed to change. Generally in small systems, or systems with long range interactions (those outside the [thermodynamic limit](wikipedia://en/2262550)), $\Phi_\text{G} \neq - \langle P\rangle V$.[^o11]
