# Additive number theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Additive number theory"}

**Additive number theory** is the subfield of [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) concerning the study of subsets of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) and their behavior under [addition](wikipedia://en/61338). More abstractly, the field of additive number theory includes the study of [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) and [commutative semigroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_semigroup) with an operation of addition. Additive number theory has close ties to [combinatorial number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_number_theory) and the [geometry of numbers](wikipedia://en/250153). Principal objects of study include the [sumset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumset) of two subsets $A$ and $B$ of elements from an abelian group $G$,

:::

$$A + B = \{a+b : a \in A, b \in B\},$$

and the $h$-fold sumset of $A$,

$$hA = \underset{h}{\underbrace{A + \cdots + A}}\,.$$

## Additive number theory {#Additive_number_theory}

The field is principally devoted to consideration of *direct problems* over (typically) the integers, that is, determining the structure of $hA$ from the structure of $A$: for example, determining which elements can be represented as a sum from $hA$, where *$A$* is a fixed subset.[^o3] Two classical problems of this type are the [Goldbach conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldbach_conjecture) (which is the conjecture that $2\mathbb{P}$ contains all even numbers greater than two, where $\mathbb{P}$ is the set of [primes](wikipedia://en/23666)) and [Waring's problem](wikipedia://en/33569) (which asks how large must *$h$* be to guarantee that $hA_{k}$ contains all positive integers, where

$$A_k=\{0^k,1^k,2^k,3^k,\ldots\}$$

is the set of $k$th powers). Many of these problems are studied using the tools from the [Hardy-Littlewood circle method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardy-Littlewood_circle_method) and from [sieve methods](wikipedia://en/1035915). For example, Vinogradov proved that every sufficiently large odd number is the sum of three primes, and so every sufficiently large even integer is the sum of four primes. [Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) proved that, for every integer $k > 1$, every non-negative integer is the sum of a bounded number of $k$th powers. In general, a set *$A$* of nonnegative integers is called a *basis* of order *$h$* if $hA$ contains all positive integers, and it is called an *asymptotic basis* if $hA$ contains all sufficiently large integers. Much current research in this area concerns properties of general asymptotic bases of finite order. For example, a set *$A$* is called a *minimal asymptotic basis* of order *$h$* if *$A$* is an asymptotic basis of order $h$ but no proper subset of *$A$* is an asymptotic basis of order *$h$*. It has been proved that minimal asymptotic bases of order *$h$* exist for all *$h$*, and that there also exist asymptotic bases of order *$h$* that contain no minimal asymptotic bases of order *$h$*. Another question to be considered is how small can the number of representations of $n$ as a sum of *$h$* elements in an asymptotic basis can be. This is the content of the [Erdős–Turán conjecture on additive bases](wikipedia://en/31244492).
