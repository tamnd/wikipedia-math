# Seven states of randomness

*Image: Stable motion blue.jpeg.* Stochastic process with random increments from a symmetric [stable distribution](wikipedia://en/1117869) with *α* = 1.7. Notice the discontinuous changes.

*Image: Brow motion blue.jpeg.* Stochastic process with random increments from a standard [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Seven states of randomness"}

The **seven states of randomness** in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), [fractals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractals) and [risk analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probabilistic_risk_assessment) are extensions of the concept of [randomness](wikipedia://en/19196523) as modeled by the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462). These seven states were first introduced by [Benoît Mandelbrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benoît_Mandelbrot) in his 1997 book *Fractals and Scaling in Finance*, which applied [fractal analysis](wikipedia://en/18745015) to the study of risk and randomness.[^o2] This classification builds upon the three main states of randomness: mild, slow, and wild.

:::

The importance of **seven states of randomness** classification for [mathematical finance](wikipedia://en/33589680) is that methods such as [Markowitz mean variance portfolio](wikipedia://en/734434#Mathematical_model) and [Black–Scholes model](wikipedia://en/113515) may be invalidated as the tails of the distribution of returns are [fattened](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fat_tail): the former relies on finite [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590) ([volatility](wikipedia://en/11930108)) and stability of [correlation](wikipedia://en/157057), while the latter is constructed upon [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436).

## History {#History}

These seven states build on earlier work of Mandelbrot in 1963: "The variations of certain speculative prices"[^o4] and "New methods in statistical economics"[^o5] in which he argued that most [statistical models](wikipedia://en/27576) approached only a first stage of dealing with [indeterminism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminism) in science, and that they ignored many aspects of real world [turbulence](wikipedia://en/154664), in particular, most cases of [financial modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_modeling).[^o6][^o7] This was then presented by Mandelbrot in the International Congress for Logic (1964) in an address titled "The Epistemology of Chance in Certain Newer Sciences"[^o8]

Intuitively speaking, Mandelbrot argued[^o8] that the traditional normal distribution does not properly capture empirical and "real world" distributions and there are other forms of randomness that can be used to model extreme changes in risk and randomness. He observed that randomness can become quite "wild" if the requirements regarding finite [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) and [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) are abandoned. Wild randomness corresponds to situations in which a single observation, or a particular outcome can impact the total in a very disproportionate way.

*Image: Exponential simulation.jpeg.* Random draws from an [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) with mean = 1. (Borderline mild randomness)

*Image: Lognormal simulation.jpeg.* Random draws from a [lognormal distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lognormal_distribution) with mean = 1. (Slow randomness with finite and localized moments)

*Image: Pareto simulation.jpeg.* Random draws from a [Pareto distribution](wikipedia://en/53057) with mean = 1 and *α* = 1.5 (Wild randomness)

The classification was formally introduced in his 1997 book *Fractals and Scaling in Finance*,[^o2] as a way to bring insight into the three main states of randomness: mild, slow, and wild. Given *N* [addends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Addends), *portioning* concerns the relative contribution of the addends to their sum. By *even* portioning, Mandelbrot meant that the addends were of same [order of magnitude](wikipedia://en/22657), otherwise he considered the portioning to be *concentrated*. Given the [moment](wikipedia://en/368684#Significance_of_the_moments) of order *q* of a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685), Mandelbrot called the root of degree *q* of such moment the *scale factor* (of order *q*).

The seven states are:

1. Proper mild randomness: short-run portioning is even for *N* = 2, e.g. the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462)
1. Borderline mild randomness: short-run portioning is concentrated for *N* = 2, but eventually becomes even as *N* grows, e.g. the [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) with rate *λ* = 1 (and so with expected value 1/*λ* = 1)
1. Slow randomness with finite delocalized moments: scale factor increases faster than *q* but no faster than $\sqrt[w]{q}$, *w* \< 1
1. Slow randomness with finite and localized moments: scale factor increases faster than any power of *q*, but remains finite, e.g. the [lognormal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lognormal) distribution and importantly, the bounded uniform distribution (which by construction with finite scale for all q cannot be pre-wild randomness.)
1. Pre-wild randomness: scale factor becomes infinite for *q* > 2, e.g. the [Pareto distribution](wikipedia://en/53057) with *α* = 2.5
1. Wild randomness: infinite second moment, but finite moment of some positive order, e.g. the [Pareto distribution](wikipedia://en/53057) with $\alpha\leq 2$
1. Extreme randomness: all moments are infinite, e.g. the [log-Cauchy distribution](wikipedia://en/33450252)

Wild randomness has applications outside financial markets, e.g. it has been used in the analysis of turbulent situations such as wild [forest fires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_fire).[^o9]

Using elements of this distinction, in March 2006, before the [2008 financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_financial_crisis), and four years before the [2010 Flash Crash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Flash_Crash), during which the [Dow Jones Industrial Average](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dow_Jones_Industrial_Average) had a 1,000 point intraday swing within minutes,[^o10] Mandelbrot and [Nassim Taleb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nassim_Taleb) published an article in the *[Financial Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_Times)* arguing that the traditional "bell curves" that have been in use for over a century are inadequate for measuring risk in financial markets, given that such curves disregard the possibility of sharp jumps or discontinuities. Contrasting this approach with the traditional approaches based on [random walks](wikipedia://en/235451), they stated:[^o11]

> We live in a world primarily driven by random jumps, and tools designed for random walks address the wrong problem.

Mandelbrot and Taleb pointed out that although one can assume that the odds of finding a person who is several miles tall are extremely low, similar excessive observations cannot be excluded in other areas of application. They argued that while traditional bell curves may provide a satisfactory representation of height and weight in the population, they do not provide a suitable modeling mechanism for market risks or returns, where just ten trading days represent 63 per cent of the returns between 1956 and 2006.

## Definitions {#Definitions}

### Doubling convolution {#Doubling_convolution}

If the probability density of $U=U'+U''$ is denoted $p_2 (u)$, then it can be obtained by the double convolution $p_2 (x) = \int p(u) p(x-u)\,du$.

### Short run portioning ratio {#Short_run_portioning_ratio}

When *u* is known, the conditional probability density of *u*′ is given by the portioning ratio:

$$\frac{p(u')p(u-u')}{p_{2}(u)}$$

### Concentration in mode {#Concentration_in_mode}

In many important cases, the maximum of $p(u')p(u-u')$ occurs near $u'=u/2$, or near $u'=0$ and $u'=u$. Take the logarithm of $p(u')p(u-u')$ and write:

$$\Delta(u)=2 \log p(u/2)-[\log p(0) +\log p(u)]$$

- If $\log p(u)$ is [cap-convex](wikipedia://en/263321), the portioning ratio is maximal for $u'=u/2$
- If $\log p(u)$ is straight, the portioning ratio is constant
- If $\log p(u)$ is [cup-convex](wikipedia://en/245568), the portioning ratio is minimal for $u'=u/2$

### Concentration in probability {#Concentration_in_probability}

Splitting the doubling convolution into three parts gives:

$$p_2(x)=\int_0^x p(u)p(x-u) \, du=\left \{ \int_0^{\tilde x} + \int_{\tilde x}^{x- \tilde x} + \int_{x- \tilde x}^{x} \right \} p(u)p(x-u) \, du = I_L+I_0+I_R$$

*p*(*u*) is short-run concentrated in probability if it is possible to select $\tilde u(u)$ so that the middle interval of ($\tilde u, u-\tilde u$) has the following two properties as u→∞:

- *I*<sub>0</sub>/*p*<sub>2</sub>(*u*) → 0
- $(u-2 \tilde u) u$ does not → 0

### Localized and delocalized moments {#Localized_and_delocalized_moments}

Consider the formula $\operatorname{E}[U^{q}] = \int_0^\infty u^q p(u) \, du$, if *p*(*u*) is the [scaling distribution](wikipedia://en/24522) the integrand is maximum at 0 and ∞, on other cases the integrand may have a sharp global maximum for some value $\tilde u_q$ defined by the following equation:

$$0=\frac{d}{du} (q \log u + \log p(u))=\frac{q}{u}-\left|\frac{d \log p(u)}{du}\right|$$

One must also know $u^q p(u)$ in the neighborhood of $\tilde u_q$. The function $u^{q}p(u)$ often admits a "Gaussian" approximation given by:

$$\log[u^q p(u)]=\log p(u) +qu = \text{constant}-(u-\tilde u_q)^2 \tilde \sigma^{-2/2}_q$$

When $u^qp(u)$ is well-approximated by a Gaussian density, the bulk of $\operatorname{E}[U^{q}]$ originates in the "*q*-interval" defined as $[\tilde u_q-\tilde \sigma_q,\tilde u_q+\tilde \sigma_q]$. The Gaussian *q*-intervals greatly overlap for all values of $\sigma$. The Gaussian moments are called *delocalized*. The lognormal's *q*-intervals are uniformly spaced and their width is independent of *q*; therefore, if the log-normal is sufficiently skew, the *q*-interval and (*q* + 1)-interval do not overlap. The lognormal moments are called *uniformly localized*. In other cases, neighboring *q*-intervals cease to overlap for sufficiently high *q*, such moments are called *asymptotically localized*.
