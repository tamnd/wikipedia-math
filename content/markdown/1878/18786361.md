# Ping-pong lemma

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ping-pong lemma"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **ping-pong lemma**, or **table-tennis lemma**, is any of several mathematical statements that ensure that several elements in a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) [acting](wikipedia://en/12781) on a set freely [generates](wikipedia://en/99945) a [free](wikipedia://en/59735) [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of that group.

:::

## History {#History}

The ping-pong argument goes back to the late 19th century and is commonly attributed[^o5] to [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) who used it to study subgroups of [Kleinian groups](wikipedia://en/1362652), that is, of discrete groups of [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777) of the [hyperbolic 3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_3-space) or, equivalently [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493) of the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799). The ping-pong lemma was a key tool used by [Jacques Tits](wikipedia://en/219570) in his 1972 paper[^o3] containing the [proof](wikipedia://en/82285) of a famous result now known as the [Tits alternative](wikipedia://en/12704641). The result states that a [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/963042) [linear group](wikipedia://en/3983656) is either [virtually](wikipedia://en/10377971) [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012) or contains a free subgroup of [rank](wikipedia://en/59735) two. The ping-pong lemma and its variations are widely used in [geometric topology](wikipedia://en/621732) and [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164).

Modern versions of the ping-pong lemma can be found in many books such as Lyndon & Schupp,[^o4] de la Harpe,[^o5] Bridson & Haefliger[^o6] and others.

## Formal statements {#Formal_statements}

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=named-heading name="Ping-pong lemma for several subgroups"}

### Ping-pong lemma for several subgroups {#Ping-pong_lemma_for_several_subgroups}

This version of the ping-pong lemma ensures that several subgroups of a group [acting](wikipedia://en/12781) on a set generate a [free product](wikipedia://en/360036). The following statement appears in Olijnyk and Suchchansky (2004),[^o11] and the proof is from de la Harpe (2000).[^o5]

Let *G* be a group acting on a set *X* and let *H*<sub>1</sub>, *H*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *H*<sub>*k*</sub> be subgroups of *G* where *k* ≥ 2, such that at least one of these subgroups has [order](wikipedia://en/493527) greater than 2. Suppose there exist [pairwise disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359#Generalizations) [nonempty](wikipedia://en/9566) subsets $X_{1}, X_{2}, ...,X_{k}$ of $X$ such that the following holds:

- For any $i \ne s$ and for any $h$ in $H_{i}$, $h \ne 1$ we have $h(X_{s}) \subseteq X_{i}$.

Then

$$\langle H_1,\dots, H_k\rangle=H_1\ast\dots \ast H_k.$$

::: {#o13 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

#### Proof {#Proof}

By the definition of free product, it suffices to check that a given (nonempty) reduced word represents a nontrivial element of $G$. Let $w$ be such a word of length $m\geq 2$, and let

$$w = \prod_{i=1}^m w_i,$$

where $w_i \in H_{\alpha_i}$ for some $\alpha_i \in \{1,\dots,k\}$. Since $w$ is reduced, we have $\alpha_i \neq \alpha_{i+1}$ for any $i = 1, \dots, m-1$ and each $w_i$ is distinct from the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) of $H_{\alpha_i}$. We then let $w$ [act](wikipedia://en/12781) on an element of one of the sets $X_i$. As we assume that at least one subgroup $H_i$ has order at least 3, [without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000) we may assume that $H_1$ has order at least 3. We first make the assumption that $\alpha_1$and $\alpha_m$ are both 1 (which implies $m \geq 3$). From here we consider $w$ acting on $X_2$. We get the following chain of containments:

$$w(X_2) \subseteq \prod_{i=1}^{m-1} w_i(X_1) \subseteq \prod_{i=1}^{m-2} w_i(X_{\alpha_{m-1}}) \subseteq \dots \subseteq w_1(X_{\alpha_2}) \subseteq X_1.$$

By the assumption that different $X_i$'s are disjoint, we conclude that $w$ acts nontrivially on some element of $X_2$, thus $w$ represents a nontrivial element of $G$.

To finish the proof we must consider the three cases:

- if $\alpha_1 = 1,\,\alpha_m \neq 1$, then let $h\in H_1\setminus \{w_1^{-1},1\}$ (such an $h$ exists since by assumption $H_1$ has order at least 3);
- if $\alpha_1 \neq 1,\,\alpha_m=1$, then let $h\in H_1\setminus \{w_m,1\}$;
- and if $\alpha_1\neq 1,\,\alpha_m\neq 1$, then let $h\in H_1\setminus \{1\}$.

In each case, $hwh^{-1}$ after reduction becomes a reduced word with its first and last letter in $H_1$. Finally, $hwh^{-1}$ represents a nontrivial element of $G$, and so does $w$. This proves the claim.

:::

### The Ping-pong lemma for infinite cyclic subgroups {#The_Ping-pong_lemma_for_infinite_cyclic_subgroups}

Let *G* be a group acting on a set *X*. Let *a*<sub>1</sub>, ...,*a*<sub>*k*</sub> be elements of *G* of infinite [order](wikipedia://en/493527), where *k* ≥ 2. Suppose there exist disjoint nonempty subsets

of $X$ with the following properties:

- $a_{i}(X - X_{i}^{-}) \subseteq X_{i}^{+}$ for $i = 1, ..., k$;
- $a_{i}^{-1}(X - X_{i}^{+}) \subseteq X_{i}^{-}$ for $i = 1, ..., k$.

Then the subgroup $H = \langle a_{1}, ..., a_{k}\rangle \le G$ [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by *a*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *a*<sub>*k*</sub> is [free](wikipedia://en/59735) with free basis $\{a_{1}, ..., a_{k}\}$.

::: {#o17 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

#### Proof {#Proof}

This statement follows as a [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865) of the version for general subgroups if we let $X_{i} = X_{i}^{+} \cup X_{i}^{-}$ and let $H_{i} = \langle a_{i}\rangle$.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

### Special linear group example {#Special_linear_group_example}

One can use the ping-pong lemma to prove[^o5] that the subgroup $H = \langle A,B\rangle \le \operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})$, generated by the [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859)

$$A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\\ 0 &1 \end{pmatrix}$$

and

$$B = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 2 &1 \end{pmatrix}$$

is free of [rank](wikipedia://en/59735) two.

::: {#o22 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

#### Proof {#Proof}

Indeed, let $H_{1} = \langle A\rangle$ and $H_{2} = \langle B\rangle$ be [cyclic subgroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_subgroup) of $\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})$ generated by $A$ and $B$ accordingly. It is not hard to check that $A$ and $B$ are elements of infinite order in $\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})$ and that

$$H_1 = \{A^n \mid n\in \mathbb{Z}\} = \left\{\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2n\\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} : n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}$$

and

$$H_2 = \{B^n \mid n\in \mathbb{Z}\} = \left\{\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 2n & 1 \end{pmatrix} : n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.$$

Consider the standard [action](wikipedia://en/12781) of $\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})$ on $\mathbf{R}^{2}$ by [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation). Put

$$X_1 = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix}x \\ y \end{pmatrix}\in \mathbb{R}^2 : |x|>|y|\right\}$$

and

$$X_2 = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix}x \\ y \end{pmatrix}\in \mathbb R^2 : |x|<|y|\right\}.$$

It is not hard to check, using the above explicit descriptions of *H*<sub>1</sub> and *H*<sub>2</sub>, that for every nontrivial $g \in H_{1}$ we have $g(X_{2}) \subseteq X_{1}$ and that for every nontrivial $g \in H_{2}$ we have $g(X_{1}) \subseteq X_{2}$. Using the alternative form of the ping-pong lemma, for two subgroups, given above, we conclude that $H = H_{1}\,\ast\,H_{2}$. Since the groups $H_{1}$ and $H_{2}$ are infinite [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327), it follows that *H* is a [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) of rank two.

:::

### Word-hyperbolic group example {#Word-hyperbolic_group_example}

Let $G$ be a [word-hyperbolic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word-hyperbolic_group) which is [torsion-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion-free_group), that is, with no nonidentity elements of finite [order](wikipedia://en/493527). Let $g, h \in G$ be two non-commuting elements, that is such that $gh \ne hg$. Then there exists *M* ≥ 1 such that for any [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $n \ge M$, $m \ge M$ the subgroup $H = \langle g^{n}, h^{m}\rangle \le G$ is free of rank two.

#### Sketch of the proof {#Sketch_of_the_proof}

Source:[^o25]

The group *G* acts on its *hyperbolic boundary* ∂*G* by [homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13660). It is known that if *a* in *G* is a nonidentity element then *a* has exactly two distinct fixed points, $a^{\infty}$ and $a^{-\infty}$ in $\partial G$ and that $a^{\infty}$ is an [attracting fixed point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attracting_fixed_point) while $a^{-\infty}$ is a [repelling fixed point](wikipedia://en/449738).

Since $g$ and $h$ do not commute, basic facts about word-hyperbolic groups imply that $g^{\infty}$, $g^{-\infty}$, $h^{\infty}$ and $h^{-\infty}$ are four distinct points in $\partial G$. Take disjoint [neighborhoods](wikipedia://en/1529485) $U_{+}$, $U_{-}$, $V_{+}$, and $V_{-}$ of $g^{\infty}$, $g^{-\infty}$, $h^{\infty}$ and $h^{-\infty}$ in $\partial G$ respectively. Then the attracting/repelling properties of the fixed points of *g* and *h* imply that there exists $M \ge 1$ such that for any integers $n \ge M$, $m \ge M$ we have:

- $$g^{n}(\partial G - U_{-}) \subseteq U_{+}$$
- $$g^{-n}(\partial G - U_{+}) \subseteq U_{-}$$
- $$h^{m}(\partial G - V_{-}) \subseteq V_{+}$$
- $$h^{-m}(\partial G - V_{+}) \subseteq V_{-}$$

The ping-pong lemma now implies that $H = \langle g^{n}, h^{m}\rangle \le G$ is free of rank two.

:::

::: {#o27 .application cue=heading name="Applications of the ping-pong lemma"}

## Applications of the ping-pong lemma {#Applications_of_the_ping-pong_lemma}

- The ping-pong lemma is used in [Kleinian groups](wikipedia://en/1362652) to study their so-called [Schottky subgroups](wikipedia://en/5871034). In the Kleinian groups context the ping-pong lemma can be used to show that a particular group of isometries of the [hyperbolic 3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_3-space) is not just [free](wikipedia://en/59735) but also [properly discontinuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Properly_discontinuous) and [geometrically finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometrically_finite_group).
- Similar Schottky-type arguments are widely used in [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164), particularly for subgroups of [word-hyperbolic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word-hyperbolic_group)[^o25] and for [automorphism groups](wikipedia://en/56661772) of trees.[^o28]
- The ping-pong lemma is also used for studying Schottky-type subgroups of [mapping class groups](wikipedia://en/793295) of [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181), where the set on which the mapping class group acts is the Thurston boundary of the [Teichmüller space](wikipedia://en/1835661).[^o29] A similar argument is also utilized in the study of subgroups of the [outer automorphism group](wikipedia://en/628466) of a free group.[^o30]
- One of the most famous applications of the ping-pong lemma is in the proof of [Jacques Tits](wikipedia://en/219570) of the so-called [Tits alternative](wikipedia://en/12704641) for [linear groups](wikipedia://en/3983656).[^o3] (see also [^o31] for an overview of Tits' proof and an explanation of the ideas involved, including the use of the ping-pong lemma).
- There are generalizations of the ping-pong lemma that produce not just [free products](wikipedia://en/360036) but also [amalgamated free products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_product_with_amalgamation) and [HNN extensions](wikipedia://en/2823319).[^o4] These generalizations are used, in particular, in the proof of [Maskit's Combination Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maskit's_Combination_Theorem) for Kleinian groups.[^o32]
- There are also versions of the ping-pong lemma which guarantee that several elements in a group generate a [free semigroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_semigroup). Such versions are available both in the general context of a [group action](wikipedia://en/12781) on a set,[^o33] and for specific types of actions, e.g. in the context of linear groups,[^o34] groups [acting on trees](wikipedia://en/18673708)[^o35] and others.[^o36]

:::
