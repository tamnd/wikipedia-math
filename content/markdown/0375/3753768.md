# Philip J. Davis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Philip J. Davis"}

**Philip J. Davis** (January 2, 1923[^o5] – March 14, 2018) was an American academic [applied mathematician](wikipedia://en/24295969).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Davis was born in [Lawrence, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence,_Massachusetts). He was known for his work in [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506) and [approximation theory](wikipedia://en/1092713), as well as his investigations in the [history](wikipedia://en/14220) and [philosophy of mathematics](wikipedia://en/46439). He earned his degrees in mathematics from [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) (SB, 1943; PhD, 1950, advisor [Ralph P. Boas, Jr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_P._Boas,_Jr).), and his final position was Professor Emeritus at the Division of Applied Mathematics at [Brown University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_University).

He served briefly in an aerodynamics research position in the [Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Air_Force) in [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) before joining the [National Bureau of Standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Standards) (now the [National Institute of Standards and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology)). He became Chief of Numerical Analysis there and worked on the well-known [Abramowitz and Stegun](wikipedia://en/1206236) *Handbook of Mathematical Functions* before joining Brown in 1963.

He was awarded the [Chauvenet Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chauvenet_Prize) for mathematical writing in 1963 for an article on the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316),[^o7] and won numerous other prizes, including being chosen to deliver the 1991 Hendrick Lectures of the [MAA](wikipedia://en/198772) (which became the basis for his book *Spirals: From [Theodorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodorus_of_Cyrene) to Chaos*). He was a frequent invited lecturer and authored several books. Among the best known are *[The Mathematical Experience](wikipedia://en/13478782)* (with [Reuben Hersh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reuben_Hersh)), a popular survey of modern mathematics and its [history](wikipedia://en/14220) and [philosophy](wikipedia://en/46439); *Methods of Numerical Integration* (with [Philip Rabinowitz](wikipedia://en/15046429)),[^o8] long the standard work on the subject of [quadrature](wikipedia://en/170089); and *Interpolation and Approximation*, still an important reference in this area.

For *[The Mathematical Experience](wikipedia://en/13478782)* (1981), Davis and Hersh won a [National Book Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Book_Award) in Science.[^o9][^o10]

Davis also wrote an autobiography, *The Education of a Mathematician*; some of his other books include autobiographical sections as well. In addition, he published works of fiction. His best-known book outside the field of mathematics is *The Thread: A Mathematical Yarn* (1983, 2nd ed. 1989), which "has raised Digression into a literary form" (Gerard Piel); it takes off from the name of the Russian mathematician [Tschebyscheff](wikipedia://en/149568), and in the course of explaining why he insists on that "barbaric, Teutonic, non-standard orthography" (in the words of a reader of *Interpolation and Approximation* who wrote him to complain), he digresses in many amusing directions.

Davis died on March 14, 2018, at the age of 95.[^o11]
