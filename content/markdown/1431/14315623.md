# A-equivalence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="A-equivalence"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **$\mathcal{A}$-equivalence**, sometimes called **right-left equivalence**, is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) between [map germs](wikipedia://en/352714).

:::

Let $M$ and $N$ be two [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470), and let $f, g : (M,x) \to (N,y)$ be two smooth [map germs](wikipedia://en/352714). We say that $f$ and $g$ are $\mathcal{A}$-equivalent if there exist [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564) germs $\phi : (M,x) \to (M,x)$ and $\psi : (N,y) \to (N,y)$ such that $\psi \circ f = g \circ \phi.$

In other words, two [map germs](wikipedia://en/352714) are $\mathcal{A}$-equivalent if one can be taken onto the other by a [diffeomorphic](wikipedia://en/8564) change of co-ordinates in the source (i.e. $M$) and the target (i.e. $N$).

Let $\Omega(M_x,N_y)$ denote the space of smooth [map germs](wikipedia://en/352714) $(M,x) \to (N,y).$ Let $\mbox{diff}(M_x)$ be the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564) germs $(M,x) \to (M,x)$ and $\mbox{diff}(N_y)$ be the group of [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564) germs $(N,y) \to (N,y).$ The group $G := \mbox{diff}(M_x) \times \mbox{diff}(N_y)$ acts on $\Omega(M_x,N_y)$ in the natural way: $(\phi,\psi) \cdot f = \psi^{-1} \circ f \circ \phi.$ Under this action we see that the [map germs](wikipedia://en/352714) $f, g : (M,x) \to (N,y)$ are $\mathcal{A}$-equivalent if, and only if, $g$ lies in the [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) of $f$, i.e. $g \in \mbox{orb}_G(f)$ (or vice versa).

A map germ is called stable if its [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) under the [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of $G := \mbox{diff}(M_x) \times \mbox{diff}(N_y)$ is [open](wikipedia://en/39358) relative to the [Whitney topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitney_topology). Since $\Omega(M_x,N_y)$ is an infinite dimensional space [metric topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_topology) is no longer trivial. [Whitney topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitney_topology) compares the differences in successive derivatives and gives a notion of proximity within the infinite dimensional space. A base for the [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) of the [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) in question is given by taking $k$-jets for every $k$ and taking [open](wikipedia://en/39358) [neighbourhoods](wikipedia://en/1529485) in the ordinary Euclidean sense. [Open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) in the [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) are then [unions](wikipedia://en/47949) of these base sets.

Consider the [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) of some [map germ](wikipedia://en/352714) $orb_G(f).$ The [map germ](wikipedia://en/352714) $f$ is called simple if there are only finitely many other [orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) in a [neighbourhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighbourhood) of each of its points. [Vladimir Arnold](wikipedia://en/32490) has shown that the only simple [singular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity) [map germs](wikipedia://en/352714) $(\mathbb{R}^n,0) \to (\mathbb{R},0)$ for $1 \le n \le 3$ are the infinite sequence $A_k$ ($k \in \mathbb{N}$), the infinite sequence $D_{4+k}$ ($k \in \mathbb{N}$), $E_6,$ $E_7,$ and $E_8.$
