# Tu quoque

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Tu quoque"}

**Tu quoque**, literally "you, too", is a [rhetorical](wikipedia://en/25447) technique that intends to discredit the opponent's argument by attacking the opponent's own personal behaviour and actions as being inconsistent with their argument, so that the opponent appears [hypocritical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypocrisy). This specious reasoning is a special type of [ad hominem](wikipedia://en/2226) attack. The [Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary) cites John Cooke's 1614 stage play [The Cittie Gallant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cittie_Gallant) as the earliest known use of the term in the English language.[^n1]

:::

## Form and explanation {#Form_and_explanation}

The (fallacious) tu quoque argument follows the template (i.e., pattern):[^o3]

1. Person A claims that a statement $X$ is true.
1. Person B asserts that A's actions or past claims are inconsistent with the truth of claim $X$.
1. Therefore, $X$ is false.

For example:

Alice[^o4]

Bob reasons that because Alice is being hypocritical, her statement about the effects of smoking must be false. But the truth of Alice's claim has no connection to whether or not she is a hypocrite.

## Similar concepts {#Similar_concepts}

A similar concept in politics is that of [whataboutism](wikipedia://en/35842043); raising a counteraccusation, often in the form of a larger but unrelated issue. In the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) in the 1930s, the phrase "[and you are lynching Negroes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/And_you_are_lynching_Negroes)" was often raised against the United States. The same technique is used in [red herrings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_herrings), as [Benito Mussolini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benito_Mussolini) attempted to do after the murder of [Giacomo Matteotti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giacomo_Matteotti)[^o6].
