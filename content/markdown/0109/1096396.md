# Categorical theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Categorical theory"}

Vaught's test

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), a [theory](wikipedia://en/3512103) is **categorical** if it has exactly one [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_%28mathematical_logic%29) ([up to isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Up_to_isomorphism)). Such a theory can be viewed as *defining* its model, uniquely characterizing the model's structure.

:::

In [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983), only theories with a [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) model can be categorical, due to the upward [Löwenheim–Skolem theorem](wikipedia://en/341482). [Higher-order logic](wikipedia://en/562883) contains categorical theories with an [infinite](wikipedia://en/142679) model. For example, the second-order [Peano axioms](wikipedia://en/25005) are categorical, having a unique model whose domain is the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of natural numbers $\mathbb{N}.$

In [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858), the notion of a categorical theory is refined with respect to [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6173). A theory is $\kappa$-**categorical** (or **categorical in $\kappa$**) if it has exactly one model of cardinality $\kappa$ up to isomorphism. **Morley's categoricity theorem** is a theorem of stating that if a [first-order theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-order_theory) in a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) language is categorical in some [uncountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountable) [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174), then it is categorical in all uncountable cardinalities.

(txt) extended Morley's theorem to uncountable languages: if the language has cardinality $\kappa$ and a theory is categorical in some uncountable cardinal greater than or equal to $\kappa$ then it is categorical in all cardinalities greater than $\kappa$.

## History and motivation {#History_and_motivation}

[Oswald Veblen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Veblen) in 1904 defined a theory to be **categorical** if all of its models are isomorphic.

By the upward [Löwenheim–Skolem theorem](wikipedia://en/341482), if a [first-order theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-order_theory) has a model of infinite [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6173) $\kappa$, then it has a model of any cardinality $\kappa' > \kappa$. So it cannot be categorical. One is then immediately led to the more subtle notion of $\kappa$-categoricity, which asks: for which cardinals $\kappa$ is there exactly one model of cardinality $\kappa$ of the given theory *T* up to isomorphism? In the language of [spectrum](wikipedia://en/3820897), let $I(T, \kappa)$ be the number of non-isomorphic models of *T* of cardinality $\kappa$, then *T* is $\kappa$-categorical iff $I(T, \kappa) = 1$.

By the completeness theorem, if *T* is not complete, then it cannot be $\kappa$-categorical for any $\kappa$. Consequently, categoricity is only interesting in the case where *T* is consistent and complete.

For the special case of a consistent and complete theory *T* over a countable [language](wikipedia://en/10939), the downward and upward Löwenheim–Skolem theorems, plus the completeness theorem, implies that $I(T, \kappa) \geq 1$ for all infinite $\kappa$.

In 1954 [Jerzy Łoś](wikipedia://en/3055456) noticed that, if *T* is a consistent and complete theory over a countable [language](wikipedia://en/10939), with at least one infinite model, he could only find three ways for *T* to be $\kappa$-categorical at some $\kappa$:

- *T* is **uncountably categorical**, i.e. T is $\kappa$-categorical if and only if $\kappa$ is an [uncountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) cardinal.
- *T* is [**countably categorical**](wikipedia://en/26628083), *i.e.* *T* is $\kappa$-categorical if and only if $\kappa$ is a countable cardinal.
- *T* is **totally categorical**, *i.e.* *T* is $\kappa$-categorical for all infinite [cardinals](wikipedia://en/6173) $\kappa$.

::: {#o4 .table cue=table}

| \-categorical | countable | uncountable |
|---|---|---|
| countably | Yes | No |
| uncountably | No | Yes |
| totally | Yes | Yes |

:::

This observation spurred a great amount of research into the 1960s. [Michael Morley](wikipedia://en/2921675)'s categoricity theorem (1965) confirms that these are the only possibilities:

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Morley's categoricity theorem"}

**Morley's categoricity theorem ((Morley 1965)).**

If *T* is a consistent and complete theory over a countable language, with at least one infinite model, then *T* is $\kappa$-categorical at some uncountable $\kappa$, if and only if it is $\kappa$-categorical at all uncountable $\kappa$.

:::

The theory was subsequently extended and refined by [Saharon Shelah](wikipedia://en/331211) in the 1970s and beyond, leading to [stability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stability_%28model_theory%29) and Shelah's more general programme of [classification theory](wikipedia://en/3820897).

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

There are not many natural examples of theories that are categorical in some uncountable cardinal. The known examples include:

- Pure identity theory (with no functions, constants, predicates other than "=", or axioms).
- The classic example is the theory of [algebraically closed](wikipedia://en/1018) [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) of a given [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621). Categoricity does *not* say that all algebraically closed fields of characteristic 0 as large as the [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) **C** are the same as **C**; it only asserts that they are isomorphic *as fields* to **C**. It follows that although the completed [*p*-adic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic) closures **C**<sub>*p*</sub> are all isomorphic as fields to **C**, they may (and in fact do) have completely different [topological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological) and analytic properties. The theory of algebraically closed fields of a given characteristic is **not** categorical in $\omega$ (the countable infinite cardinal); there are models of [transcendence degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_degree) 0, 1, 2, ..., $\omega$.
- [Vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) over a given countable field. This includes [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) of given [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) [exponent](wikipedia://en/3568695) (essentially the same as vector spaces over a finite field) and [divisible](wikipedia://en/472147) [torsion-free abelian groups](wikipedia://en/11630973) (essentially the same as vector spaces over the [rationals](wikipedia://en/19727024)).
- The theory of the set of [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) with a successor function.

There are also examples of theories that are categorical in $\omega$ but not categorical in uncountable cardinals. The simplest example is the theory of an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) with exactly two [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260), both of which are infinite. Another example is the theory of [dense](wikipedia://en/6320997) [linear orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_order) with no endpoints; [Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216) proved that any such countable linear order is isomorphic to the rational numbers: see [Cantor's isomorphism theorem](wikipedia://en/68245955).

:::

## Dividing lines {#Dividing_lines}

Mathematicians working in classification theory organize first-order theories according to **dividing lines**. A **dividing line** is a property that some first-order theories have, while others do not have, such that theories having different dividing line properties have different structural behavior. The dividing lines are usually designed to make precise an intuitive sense of "how complicated the models of a complete first-order theory can be". Often, a dividing line property is defined so that *possessing* the property makes a theory wild, and a tame theory is a theory that *does not possess* any dividing line property under consideration.

For a complete theory $T$, the main dividing lines used in Shelah's categoricity theory include [stability](wikipedia://en/15828771), superstability, the dimensional order property, and the omitting types order property.[^n1]

A complete theory is **stable** in a cardinal $\kappa$ if, over each set of parameters of size $\kappa$, it has at most $\kappa$ complete types. Stability restricts the number of possible extensions of partial information over a parameter set.

A theory is **superstable** if it is stable in all sufficiently large cardinals; equivalently, in the countable case, it is stable in every uncountable cardinal. Superstability is a basic hypothesis in the structure theory of models of complete theories.

The **dimensional order property** (**DOP**) is a configuration in a superstable theory which permits the construction of many non-isomorphic models by varying independent dimensions over a two-dimensional arrangement of models. The presence of DOP is a sign that the theory has a complicated spectrum of models.

The **omitting types order property** (**OTOP**) is another configuration in a superstable theory which permits the coding of order-like information by controlling which types are omitted in models. Like DOP, the presence of OTOP gives rise to many non-isomorphic models in large cardinalities.

A complete first-order theory is called **classifiable** if it is superstable, not DOP, and not OTOP. Shelah's main structure theory can decompose classifiable models into simpler pieces. In contrast, theories with instability, unsuperstability, DOP, or OTOP have many non-isomorphic models in sufficiently large cardinalities, almost all too [wild](wikipedia://en/372742) to describe.[^n1]

Some other dividing lines are the **tree property**, the **independence property**, the **strict order property** (SOP), etc.

## Properties {#Properties}

Every categorical theory is [complete](wikipedia://en/10086335).[^n2] However, the converse does not hold.[^o15]

Any theory *T* categorical in some infinite cardinal $\kappa$ is very close to being complete. More precisely, the [Łoś–Vaught test](wikipedia://en/22229525) states that if a satisfiable theory has no finite models and is categorical in some infinite cardinal $\kappa$ at least equal to the cardinality of its language, then the theory is complete. The reason is that all infinite models are first-order equivalent to some model of cardinal $\kappa$ by the [Löwenheim–Skolem theorem](wikipedia://en/341482), and so are all equivalent as the theory is categorical in $\kappa$. Therefore, the theory is complete as all models are equivalent. The assumption that the theory have no finite models is necessary.[^o16]

:::
