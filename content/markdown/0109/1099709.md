# Hilbert's Theorem 90

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hilbert's Theorem 90"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), **Hilbert's Theorem 90** (or **Satz 90**) is an important result on [cyclic extensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_extension) of [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) (or to one of its generalizations) that leads to [Kummer theory](wikipedia://en/581610). In its most basic form, it states that if *L*/*K* is an extension of fields with cyclic [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) *G* = Gal(*L*/*K*) generated by an element $\sigma,$ and if $a$ is an element of *L* of [relative norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_norm) 1, that is

> $$N(a):=a\, \sigma(a)\, \sigma^2(a)\cdots \sigma^{n-1}(a)=1,$$

then there exists $b$ in *L* such that

> $$a=b/\sigma(b).$$

The theorem takes its name from the fact that it is the 90th theorem in [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302)'s [Zahlbericht](wikipedia://en/33462303) (), although it is originally due to (txt).

:::

Often a more general theorem due to (txt) is given the name, stating that if *L*/*K* is a finite [Galois extension](wikipedia://en/457064) of fields with arbitrary Galois group *G* = Gal(*L*/*K*), then the first [cohomology](wikipedia://en/248635) group of *G*, with coefficients in the multiplicative group of *L*, is trivial:

$$H^1(G,L^\times)=\{1\}.$$

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Let $L/K$ be the [quadratic extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_extension) $\mathbb{Q}(i)/\mathbb{Q}$. The Galois group is cyclic of order 2, its generator $\sigma$ acting via conjugation:

$$\sigma: c + di\mapsto c - di.$$

An element $a=x+yi$ in $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ has norm $a\sigma(a)=x^2+y^2$. An element of norm one thus corresponds to a rational solution of the equation $x^2+y^2=1$ or in other words, a point with rational coordinates on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071). Hilbert's Theorem 90 then states that every such element *a* of norm one can be written as

$$a=\frac{c-di}{c+di}=\frac{c^2-d^2}{c^2+d^2} - \frac{2cd}{c^2+d^2} i,$$

where $b = c+di$ is as in the conclusion of the theorem, and *c* and *d* are both integers. This may be viewed as a rational parametrization of the rational points on the unit circle. Rational points $(x,y)=(p/r,q/r)$ on the unit circle $x^2+y^2=1$ correspond to [Pythagorean triples](wikipedia://en/24172), i.e. triples $(p,q,r)$ of integers satisfying $p^2+q^2=r^2$.

:::

## Cohomology {#Cohomology}

The theorem can be stated in terms of [group cohomology](wikipedia://en/248635): if *L*<sup>×</sup> is the [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742) of any (not necessarily finite) Galois extension *L* of a field *K* with corresponding Galois group *G*, then

$$H^1(G,L^\times)=\{1\}.$$

Specifically, group cohomology is the cohomology of the [complex](wikipedia://en/138404) whose *i-*cochains are arbitrary functions from *i*-tuples of group elements to the multiplicative coefficient group, $C^i(G,L^\times) = \{\phi:G^i\to L^\times\}$, with differentials $d^i : C^i\to C^{i+1}$ defined in dimensions $i = 0,1$ by:

> $$(d^0(b))(\sigma) = b / b^\sigma, \quad \text{ and } \quad (d^1(\phi))(\sigma,\tau) \,=\,\phi(\sigma) \phi(\tau)^\sigma / \phi(\sigma\tau) ,$$

where $x^g$ denotes the image of the $G$-module element $x$ under the action of the group element $g \in G$. Note that in the first of these we have identified a 0-[cochain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochain) $\gamma = \gamma_b : G^0 = id_G \to L^\times$, with its unique image value $b \in L^\times$. The triviality of the first cohomology group is then equivalent to the 1-cocycles $Z^1$ being equal to the 1-coboundaries $B^1$, viz.:

> $$\begin{array}{rcl} Z^1 &=& \ker d^1 &=& \{\phi\in C^1\text{ satisfying }\,\,\forall \sigma , \tau \in G \, \colon\,\, \phi(\sigma\tau) = \phi(\sigma)\,\phi(\tau)^\sigma \} \\ \text{ is equal to }\\ B^1 &=& \text{im } d^0 &=& \{\phi\in C^1\ \, \colon \,\, \exists\, b\in L^\times \text{ such that } \phi(\sigma)=b / b^\sigma \ \ \forall \sigma \in G \}. \end{array}$$

For cyclic $G =\{1,\sigma,\ldots,\sigma^{n-1}\}$, a 1-cocycle is determined by $\phi(\sigma)=a\in L^\times$, with $\phi(\sigma^i) = a\,\sigma(a)\cdots\sigma^{i-1}(a)$ and:

> $$1=\phi(1)=\phi(\sigma^n)=a\,\sigma(a)\cdots\sigma^{n-1}(a)=N(a).$$

On the other hand, a 1-coboundary is determined by $\phi(\sigma)=b / b^\sigma$. Equating these gives the original version of the Theorem.

A further generalization is to cohomology with [non-abelian coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-abelian_cohomology): that if *H* is either the [general](wikipedia://en/113564) or [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) over *L*, including $\operatorname{GL}_1(L)=L^\times$, then

> $$H^1(G,H)=\{1\}.$$

Another generalization is to a scheme *X*:

$$H^1_{\text{et}}(X,\mathbb{G}_m) = H^1(X,\mathcal{O}_X^\times) = \operatorname{Pic}(X),$$

where $\operatorname{Pic}(X)$ is the group of isomorphism classes of locally free sheaves of $\mathcal{O}_X^\times$-modules of rank 1 for the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744), and $\mathbb{G}_m$ is the sheaf defined by the affine line without the origin considered as a group under multiplication. [^o8]

There is yet another generalization to [Milnor K-theory](wikipedia://en/2914128) which plays a role in [Voevodsky's](wikipedia://en/104482) proof of the [Milnor conjecture](wikipedia://en/2914126).

::: {#o10 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

Let $L/K$ be cyclic of degree $n,$ and $\sigma$ generate $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$. Pick any $a\in L$ of norm

$$N(a):=a \sigma(a) \sigma^2(a)\cdots \sigma^{n-1}(a)=1.$$

By [clearing denominators](wikipedia://en/56260115), solving $a=x/\sigma^{-1}(x) \in L$ is the same as showing that $a\sigma^{-1}(\cdot) : L \to L$ has $1$ as an eigenvalue. We extend this to a map of $L$-vector spaces via

$$\begin{cases} 1_L\otimes a\sigma^{-1}(\cdot) : L\otimes_KL \to L\otimes_K L \\ \ell \otimes\ell'\mapsto \ell\otimes a\sigma^{-1}(\ell').\end{cases}$$

The [primitive element theorem](wikipedia://en/448933) gives $L=K(\alpha)$ for some $\alpha$. Since $\alpha$ has minimal polynomial

$$f(t)=(t-\alpha)(t-\sigma(\alpha))\cdots \left (t-\sigma^{n-1}(\alpha) \right ) \in K[t],$$

we can identify

$$L\otimes_KL\stackrel{\sim}{\to} L\otimes_K K[t] /f(t) \stackrel{\sim}{\to} L[t]/f(t) \stackrel{\sim}{\to} L^{n}$$

via

$$\ell\otimes p(\alpha) \mapsto \ell \left (p(\alpha), p(\sigma \alpha ),\ldots , p(\sigma^{n-1} \alpha ) \right ).$$

Here we wrote the second factor as a $K$-polynomial in $\alpha$.

Under this identification, our map becomes

$$\begin{cases} a\sigma^{-1}(\cdot) : L^n\to L^n \\ \ell \left(p(\alpha),\ldots, p(\sigma^{n-1}\alpha)) \mapsto \ell(ap(\sigma^{n-1}\alpha), \sigma a p(\alpha), \ldots, \sigma^{n-1} a p(\sigma^{n-2}\alpha) \right ). \end{cases}$$

That is to say under this map

$$(\ell_1, \ldots ,\ell_n)\mapsto (a \ell_n, \sigma a \ell_1, \ldots, \sigma^{n-1} a \ell_{n-1}).$$

$(1, \sigma a, \sigma a \sigma^2 a, \ldots, \sigma a \cdots \sigma^{n-1}a)$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $1$ iff $a$ has norm $1$.

:::
