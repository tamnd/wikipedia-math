# List of regions by past GDP (PPP) per capita

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="List of regions by past GDP (PPP) per capita"}

These are **lists of regions and countries by their estimated [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_versus_nominal_value) [gross domestic product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_domestic_product) (GDP) per capita** in terms of [purchasing power parity](wikipedia://en/64489) (PPP), the value of all final goods and services produced within a country/region in a given year divided by population size. GDP per capita dollar ([international dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_dollar)) estimates here are derived from PPP estimates.

:::

## Characteristics {#Characteristics}

In the absence of sufficient data for nearly all economies until well into the 19th century, past GDP per capita often cannot be calculated, but only roughly estimated. A key notion in the whole process is that of [subsistence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsistence), the [income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income) level which is necessary for sustaining one's life. Since [pre-modern societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-modern_societies), by modern standards, were characterized by a very low degree of [urbanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanization) and a large majority of people working in the [agricultural sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_sector), economic historians prefer to express income in [cereal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cereal) units. To achieve comparability over space and time, these numbers are then converted into monetary units such as international dollars, a step which leaves a relatively wide margin of interpretation.

Maddison's assumptions have been criticized and admired by academics and journalists.[^o77] Bryan Haig has characterized Maddison's figures for 19th century Australia as "inaccurate and irrelevant",[^o78] John Caldwell's assessed Maddison's arguments as having a "dangerous circularity",[^o79] and [W. W. Rostow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._W._Rostow) said, "this excessive macroeconomic bias also causes him (Maddison) to mis-date, in my view, the beginning of what he calls the capitalist era at 1820 rather than, say, the mid-1780s."[^o80]

A number of economic historians have criticized Maddison's estimates for Asia. For example, W. J. MacPherson has described Maddison's work on India and Pakistan of using "dubious comparative data."[^o81] [Paul Bairoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Bairoch) has criticized Maddison's work for underestimating the per-capita incomes of non-European regions, particularly in Asia, before the 19th century; according to Bairoch, per-capita income in Asia (especially China and India) was higher than in Europe prior to the 19th century.[^o82] Others such as [Andre Gunder Frank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andre_Gunder_Frank), Robert A. Denemark, [Kenneth Pomeranz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Pomeranz) and [Amiya Kumar Bagchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amiya_Kumar_Bagchi) have criticized Maddison for grossly underestimating per-capita income and GDP growth rates in Asia (again, mainly China and India) for the three centuries up to 1820, and for refusing to take into account contemporary research demonstrating significantly higher per-capita income and growth rates in Asia. According to Frank and Denemark, his per-capita income figures for Asia up to 1820 are not credible, go "against what we know from sources" and may need to be adjusted by a factor of two.[^o83] Maddison's estimates have also been critically reviewed and revised by the Italian economists Giovanni Federico[^o84] and [Elio Lo Cascio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elio_Lo_Cascio)/[Paolo Malanima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paolo_Malanima) (see below).[^o85]

However, economist and journalist [Evan Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evan_Davis_%28journalist%29) has praised Maddison's research by saying that it a "fantastic publication" and "based on the detailed scholarship of the world expert on historical economic data Angus Maddison." He added, "One shouldn't read the book in the belief the statistics are accurate to 12 decimal places."[^o86]

According to Bairoch, in the mid-18th century, "the average [standard of living](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_of_living) in Europe was a little bit lower than that of the rest of the world."[^o90] He noted variations within both groups in 1750, citing the [Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia) civilizations of [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China) and [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent) as being the wealthiest among the Third World group, and [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia) and [Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe)/[Southeastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeastern_Europe) as being the poorest among the First World group.[^o91] He estimated that, in 1750, the average per-capita income of the [East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_world) (Asia and [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa)) was roughly equal to that of [Western Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Europe), and that China's per-capita income was on-par with the leading European economies. He estimated that it was after 1800 that Western European per-capita income pulled ahead of the East.[^o92] China was still ahead in 1800; his GNP per capita estimates for 1800, in terms of 1960 dollars, are \$228 for China (\$US in 1990 dollars) and \$213 for Western Europe (\$US in 1990 dollars). But China fell behind not long after, falling to \$204 (\$US in 1990 dollars) by 1860.[^o93]

## Indian subcontinent {#Indian_subcontinent}

According to some evidence cited by the economic historians [Immanuel Wallerstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Wallerstein), [Irfan Habib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irfan_Habib), [Percival Spear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percival_Spear), and [Ashok Desai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashok_Desai), has theorised that per-capita agricultural output and standards of consumption in 17th-century [Mughal India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_India) was on-par with 17th-century Europe and early 20th-century [British India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_India).[^o137]

According to economic historians Prasannan Parthasarathi and [Jeffrey G. Williamson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_G._Williamson), earnings data from primary sources show that mid-late 18th-century [real wages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_wages) and [living standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Living_standards) in Bengal sultanate (under the [Nawabs of Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nawabs_of_Bengal)) a South Indian [Kingdom of Mysore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Mysore) and [Maratha Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maratha_Empire) were higher than in Britain, which in turn had the highest living standards in Europe.[^o138][^o139] The economic historian Sashi Sivramkrishna estimates [Mysore's average income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_Kingdom_of_Mysore) for skilled laborers in the late 18th century to be five times higher than [subsistence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsistence) leval[^o140] Parthasarathi also slates the real wage decline occurred in the early 19th century, or possibly beginning in the very late 18th century, under [British rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_India).[^o138][^o139][^o141]

## Ottoman Egypt {#Ottoman_Egypt}

According to economic historian Jean Batou, [Ottoman Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Egypt)'s average [per-capita income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per-capita_income) in 1800 was comparable to that of leading Western European countries such as [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France), and higher than the overall average income of [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe) and [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan).[^o149] Barou estimated that, in terms of 1960 [US dollars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_dollars), [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt) in 1800 had a per-capita income of \$232 (\$US in 1990 dollars). In comparison, per-capita income in terms of 1960 dollars for France in 1800 was \$240 (\$US in 1990 dollars), for [Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe) in 1800 was \$177 (\$US in 1990 dollars), and for Japan in 1800 was \$180 (\$US in 1990 dollars).[^o150][^o151]

## Roman and Byzantine Empires {#Roman_and_Byzantine_Empires}

Much of the recent work in estimating past GDP per capita has been done in the study of the [Roman economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_economy), following the pioneering studies by [Keith Hopkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Hopkins) (1980) and [Raymond Goldsmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_W._Goldsmith) (1984).[^o153] The estimates by [Peter Temin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Temin), [Angus Maddison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angus_Maddison), [Branko Milanovic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branko_Milanovic) and [Peter Fibiger Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Fibiger_Bang) follow the basic method established by Goldsmith, varying mainly only in their set of initial numbers; these are then stepped up to estimations of the expenditure checked by those on the income side. [Walter Scheidel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Scheidel)/Steven Friesen determine GDP per capita on the relationship between certain significant economic indicators which were historically found to be plausible; two independent control assumptions provide the upper and lower limit of the probable size of the Roman GDP per capita.[^o154]

[Italia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italia_%28Roman_Empire%29) is considered the richest region, due to tax transfers from the [provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_province) and the concentration of elite income in the heartland; its GDP per capita is estimated at having been around 40%[^o163] to 66%[^o166] higher than in the rest of the empire.

The GDP per capita of the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire), the continuation of the Roman Empire in the east, has been estimated by the [World Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Bank) economist Branko Milanovic to range between \$680 and 770 (in 1990 international dollars) at its peak around 1000 AD, the reign of [Basil II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basil_II).[^o167] This is 1.7 times the subsistence level as compared to the slightly higher value of 2.1 for the Roman Empire under [Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus) (30 BC–14 AD).[^o168]
