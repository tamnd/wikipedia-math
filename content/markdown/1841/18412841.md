# Stability of the Solar System

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Stability of the Solar System"}

The **stability of the Solar System** is a subject of much inquiry in [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy). Though the [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet) have historically been stable as observed, and will be in the "short" term, their weak [gravitational effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_interaction) on one another can add up in ways that are not predictable by any simple means.

:::

For this reason (among others), the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System) is *chaotic* in the technical sense defined by mathematical [chaos theory](wikipedia://en/6295),[^o2] and that chaotic behavior degrades even the most precise long-term numerical or analytic models for the orbital motion in the Solar System, so they cannot be valid beyond more than a few tens of millions of years into the past or future – about 1% its present age.[^o3]

The Solar System is stable on the [time-scale of the existence of humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_humanity), and far beyond, given that it is unlikely any of the planets will collide with each other or be ejected from the system in the next few billion years,[^o11] and that [Earth's orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_orbit) will be relatively stable.[^o4]

Since [Newton's law of gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation) (1687), mathematicians and astronomers (such as [Pierre-Simon Laplace](wikipedia://en/344783), [Joseph-Louis Lagrange](wikipedia://en/87793), [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125), [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740), [Andrey Kolmogorov](wikipedia://en/91161), [Vladimir Arnold](wikipedia://en/32490),[^o5] and [Jürgen Moser](wikipedia://en/1752316)) have searched for evidence for the stability of the planetary motions, and this quest has led to many mathematical developments and several successive "proofs" of stability of the Solar System.[^o6]

## Overview and challenges {#Overview_and_challenges}

The orbits of the planets are open to long-term variations. Modeling the Solar System is a case of the [$n$-body problem](wikipedia://en/23859945) of physics, which is generally unsolvable except by numerical simulation. Because of the chaotic behavior embedded in the mathematics, long-term predictions can only be statistical, rather than certain.

### Resonance {#Resonance}

*Image: Semimajorhistogramofkbos.svg.* Graph showing the numbers of [Kuiper belt objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuiper_belt_object) for a given distance (in [AU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_unit); i.e., the distance from the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) to Earth) from the Sun

An [orbital resonance](wikipedia://en/22444) happens when the periods of any two objects have a simple numerical ratio. The most fundamental period for an object in the Solar System is its [orbital period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_period), and orbital resonances pervade the Solar System. In 1867, the American astronomer [Daniel Kirkwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Kirkwood) noticed that asteroids in the [main belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid_belt) are not randomly distributed.[^o9] There were distinct gaps in the belt at locations that corresponded to resonances with [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter). For example, there were no asteroids at the 3:1 resonance — a distance of \2.5 AU — or at the 2:1 resonance, at \3.3 AU. These are now known as the [Kirkwood gaps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirkwood_gap). Some asteroids were later discovered to orbit in these gaps, but when closely analyzed their orbits were determined to be unstable and they will eventually break out of the resonance due to close encounters with a major planet.

Another common form of resonance in the Solar System is spin–orbit resonance, where the [rotation period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_period) (the time it takes the planet or moon to rotate once about its axis) has a simple numerical relationship with its orbital period. An example is the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon), which is in a 1:1 spin–orbit resonance that keeps [its far side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon) away from Earth. (This feature is also known as *[tidal locking](wikipedia://en/60162)*.) Another example is [Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_%28planet%29), which is in a 3:2 spin–orbit resonance with the Sun.

### Predictability {#Predictability}

The planets' orbits are chaotic over longer time scales, in such a way that the whole Solar System possesses a [Lyapunov time](wikipedia://en/1117315) in the range of 2~230 million years.[^o11] In all cases, this means that the positions of individual planets along their orbits ultimately become impossible to predict with any certainty. In some cases, the orbits themselves may change dramatically. Such chaos manifests most strongly as changes in [eccentricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_eccentricity), with some planets' orbits becoming significantly more – or less – [elliptical](wikipedia://en/9277).[^o12]

In calculation, the unknowns include [asteroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid), the solar [quadrupole moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrupole_moment), mass loss from the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) through radiation and the [solar wind](wikipedia://en/28538), drag of the solar wind on planetary [magnetospheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetosphere), galactic [tidal forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_forces), and effects from passing [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star).[^o13]

## Scenarios {#Scenarios}

### Neptune–Pluto resonance {#Neptune–Pluto_resonance}

The [Neptune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptune)–[Pluto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluto) system lies in a 3:2 [orbital resonance](wikipedia://en/22444). [C.J. Cohen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C.J._Cohen) and [E.C. Hubbard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E.C._Hubbard) at the [Naval Surface Warfare Center Dahlgren Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Surface_Warfare_Center_Dahlgren_Division) discovered this in 1965. Although the resonance itself will remain stable in the short term, it becomes impossible to predict the position of Pluto with any degree of accuracy, as the uncertainty in the position grows by a factor $e$ with each [Lyapunov time](wikipedia://en/1117315), which for Pluto is 10–20 million years.[^o16] Thus, on a time scale of hundreds of millions of years Pluto's orbital phase becomes impossible to determine, even if Pluto's orbit appears to be perfectly stable on 10 [myr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myr) time scales.[^o17]

### Mercury–Jupiter 1:1 perihelion-precession resonance {#Mercury–Jupiter_1:1_perihelion-precession_resonance}

The planet [Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_%28planet%29) is especially susceptible to [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter)'s influence because of a small celestial coincidence: Mercury's [perihelion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perihelion), the point where it gets closest to the Sun, precesses at a rate of about 1.5 degrees every 1,000 years, and Jupiter's perihelion precesses only a little slower. At one point, the two may fall into sync, at which time Jupiter's constant gravitational tugs could accumulate and pull Mercury off course, with 1–2% probability, 3–4 billion years into the future. This could eject it from the Solar System altogether[^o2] or send it on a collision course with [Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus), the Sun, or Earth.[^o19]

Mercury's perihelion-precession rate is dominated by planet–planet interactions, but about 7.5% of Mercury's perihelion precession rate comes from the effects described by [general relativity](wikipedia://en/1784313).[^o20] The work by Laskar and Gastineau (described below) showed the importance of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) (G.R.) in long-term Solar System stability. Specifically, without G.R. the instability rate of Mercury would be 60 times higher than with G.R.[^o39] By modelling the instability time of Mercury as a one-dimensional [Fokker–Planck](wikipedia://en/166896) [diffusion process](wikipedia://en/1667059), the relationship between the instability time of Mercury and the Mercury–Jupiter 1:1 perihelion-precession resonance can be investigated statistically.[^o21] This diffusion model shows that G.R. not only distances Mercury and Jupiter from falling into a 1:1 resonance, but also decreases the rate at which Mercury diffuses through [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101).[^o22] Thus, not only does G.R. decrease the likelihood of Mercury's instability, but also extends the time at which it is likely to occur.

### Galilean moon resonance {#Galilean_moon_resonance}

Jupiter's [Galilean moons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilean_moons) experience strong [tidal dissipation](wikipedia://en/30647) and mutual interactions due to their size and proximity to Jupiter. Currently, [Io](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Io_%28moon%29), [Europa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europa_%28moon%29), and [Ganymede](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganymede_%28moon%29) are in a 4:2:1 [Laplace resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace_resonance) with each other, with each inner moon completing two orbits for every orbit of the next moon out. In around 1.5 billion years, outward migration of these moons will trap the fourth and outermost moon, [Callisto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Callisto_%28moon%29), into another 2:1 resonance with Ganymede. This 8:4:2:1 resonance will cause Callisto to migrate outward, and it may remain stable with approximately 56% probability, or become disrupted with Io usually exiting the chain.[^o24]

### Chaos from geological processes {#Chaos_from_geological_processes}

Another example is Earth's [axial tilt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_tilt), which, due to friction raised within Earth's [mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantle_%28geology%29) by tidal interactions with the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon), will be rendered chaotic between 1.5 and 4.5 billion years from now.[^o26]

### External influences {#External_influences}

Objects coming from outside the Solar System can also affect it. Though they are not technically part of the Solar System for the purposes of studying the system's intrinsic stability, they nevertheless can change it. Unfortunately, predicting the potential influences of these [extrasolar objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extrasolar_objects) is even more difficult than predicting the influences of objects within the system simply because of the sheer distances involved. Among the known objects with a potential to significantly affect the Solar System is the star [Gliese 710](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gliese_710), which is expected to pass near the system in approximately 1.281 million years.[^o47] Though the star is not expected to substantially affect the orbits of the major planets, it could substantially disrupt the [Oort cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oort_cloud), potentially causing major comet activity throughout the Solar System. There are at least a dozen other stars that have a potential to make a close approach in the next few million years.[^o28] In 2022, Garett Brown and [Hanno Rein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanno_Rein) of the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto) published a study exploring the long-term stability of the Solar System in the presence of weak perturbations from stellar flybys. They determined that if a passing star altered the [semi-major axis](wikipedia://en/23409699) of [Neptune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptune) by at least 0.03 [AU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_unit) (4.49 million km; 2.79 million miles) it would increase the chance of instability by 10 times over the subsequent 5 billion years. They also estimated that a flyby of this magnitude is not likely to occur for 100 billion years.[^o29]

## Recent studies {#Recent_studies}

### LonGStOP, 1982 {#LonGStOP,_1982}

Project LonGStOP (LOng-term Gravitational Study of the Outer Planets) was a 1982 international consortium of Solar System dynamicists led by [A.E. Roy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archie_Roy). It involved creation of a model on a supercomputer, integrating the orbits of (only) the outer planets. Its results revealed several curious exchanges of energy between the outer planets, but no signs of gross instability.[^o32]

::: {#o34 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Digital Orrery"}

### Digital Orrery, 1988 {#Digital_Orrery,_1988}

Another project involved constructing the Digital [Orrery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orrery) by [G. Sussman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerry_Sussman) and his MIT group in 1988. The group used a special-purpose computer whose multiprocessor architecture was optimized for integrating the orbits of the outer planets. It was used to integrate out to 845 million years – some 20% of the age of the Solar System. In 1988, Sussman and [Wisdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Wisdom) found data using the Orrery that revealed that Pluto's orbit shows signs of chaos, due in part to its peculiar [resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluto#Orbits_of_Pluto_and_Neptune) with [Neptune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptune).[^o16]

If Pluto's orbit is chaotic, then technically the whole Solar System is chaotic. This might be more than a technicality, since even a Solar System body as small as Pluto might affect the others to a perceptible extent through cumulative [gravitational perturbations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_perturbation).[^o35]

:::

### Laskar, 1989 {#Laskar,_1989}

In 1989, [Jacques Laskar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Laskar) of the [Bureau des Longitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_des_Longitudes) in Paris published the results of his numerical integration of the Solar System over 200 million years. These were not the full equations of motion, but rather averaged equations along the lines of those used by [Laplace](wikipedia://en/344783). Laskar's work showed that the Earth's orbit is chaotic (as are the orbits of *all* the [inner planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_planet)) and that an error as small as 15 metres in measuring the position of the Earth today would make it impossible to predict where the Earth would be in its orbit in just over 100 million years' time.

### Laskar and Gastineau, 2009 {#Laskar_and_Gastineau,_2009}

[Jacques Laskar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Laskar) and his colleague Mickaël Gastineau in 2008 took a more thorough approach by directly simulating 2,501 possible futures. Each of the 2,501 cases has slightly different initial conditions: Mercury's position varies by about 1 m between one simulation and the next.[^o38] In 20 cases, Mercury goes into a dangerous orbit and often ends up colliding with Venus or plunging into the Sun. Moving in such a warped orbit, Mercury's gravity is more likely to shake other planets out of their settled paths: In one simulated case, Mercury's perturbations sent [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars) heading toward Earth.[^o39]

### Batygin and Laughlin, 2008 {#Batygin_and_Laughlin,_2008}

Independently of Laskar and Gastineau, [Batygin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konstantin_Batygin) and [Laughlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_P._Laughlin) were also directly simulating the Solar System 20 billion years into the future. Their results reached the same basic conclusions as did Laskar and Gastineau, while additionally providing a lower bound of a billion years on the dynamical lifespan of the Solar System.[^o41]

### Brown and Rein, 2020 {#Brown_and_Rein,_2020}

In 2020, Garett Brown and Hanno Rein of the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto) published the results of their numerical integration of the Solar System over 5 billion years. Their work showed that Mercury's orbit is highly chaotic and that an error as small as \0.38 mm in measuring the position of Mercury today would make it impossible to predict the eccentricity of its orbit in just over 200 million years' time.[^o43]
