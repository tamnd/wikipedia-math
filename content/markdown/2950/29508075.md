# Group of rational points on the unit circle

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Group of rational points on the unit circle"}

*Image: Pythagorean triple and rational point on unit triangle 1.svg.* The [Pythagorean triple](wikipedia://en/24172) (4,3,5) is associated to the rational point (4/5,3/5) on the unit circle.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the [rational points](wikipedia://en/2960200) on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) are those points (*x*, *y*) such that both *x* and *y* are [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) ("fractions") and satisfy *x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> = 1. The set of such points turns out to be closely related to primitive [Pythagorean triples](wikipedia://en/24172). Consider a primitive [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420), that is, with integer side lengths *a*, *b*, *c*, with *c* the [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366), such that the sides have no common factor larger than 1. Then on the unit circle there exists the rational point (*a*/*c*, *b*/*c*), which, in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), is just *a*/*c* + *ib*/*c*, where *i* is the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358). Conversely, if (*x*, *y*) is a rational point on the unit circle in the 1st [quadrant](wikipedia://en/7706#Quadrants_and_octants) of the coordinate system (i.e. *x* > 0, *y* > 0), then there exists a primitive right triangle with sides *xc*, *yc*, *c*, with *c* being the [least common multiple](wikipedia://en/17961) of the denominators of *x* and *y*. There is a correspondence between points (*a*, *b*) in the *x*-*y* plane and points *a* + *ib* in the complex plane which is used below.

:::

## Group operation {#Group_operation}

The set of rational points on the unit circle, shortened *G* in this article, forms an [infinite abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974#Infinite_abelian_groups) under rotations. The [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) is the point (1, 0) = 1 + *i*0 = 1. The group operation, or "product" is (*x*, *y*) \* (*t*, *u*) = (*xt* − *uy*, *xu* + *yt*). This product is angle addition since *x* = [cos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine)(*A*) and *y* = [sin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine)(*A*), where *A* is the angle that the vector (*x*, *y*) makes with the vector (1,0), measured counter-clockwise. So with (*x*, *y*) and (*t*, *u*) forming angles *A* and *B* with (1, 0) respectively, their product (*xt* − *uy*, *xu* + *yt*) is just the rational point on the unit circle forming the angle *A* + *B* with (1, 0). The group operation is expressed more easily with complex numbers: identifying the points (*x*, *y*) and (*t*, *u*) with *x* + *iy* and *t* + *iu* respectively, the group product above is just the ordinary complex number multiplication (*x* + *iy*)(*t* + *iu*) = *xt* − *yu* + *i*(*xu* + *yt*), which corresponds to the point (*xt* − *uy*, *xu* + *yt*) as above.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

3/5 + 4/5*i* and 5/13 + 12/13*i* (which correspond to the two most famous Pythagorean triples (3,4,5) and (5,12,13)) are rational points on the unit circle in the complex plane, and thus are elements of *G*. Their group product is −33/65 + 56/65*i*, which corresponds to the Pythagorean triple (33,56,65). The sum of the squares of the numerators 33 and 56 is 1089 + 3136 = 4225, which is the square of the denominator 65.

:::

### Other ways to describe the group {#Other_ways_to_describe_the_group}

$$G \cong \mathrm{SO}(2, \mathbb{Q}).$$

The set of all 2×2 [rotation matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) with rational entries coincides with G. This follows from the fact that the [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177) $S^1$ is isomorphic to $\mathrm{SO}(2, \mathbb{R})$, and the fact that their rational points coincide.

## Group structure {#Group_structure}

The structure of *G* is an infinite sum of [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327). Let *G*<sub>2</sub> denote the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of *G* generated by the point 0 + 1*i*. *G*<sub>2</sub> is a [cyclic subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_subgroup) of order 4. For a prime *p* of form 4*k* + 1, let *G*<sub>*p*</sub> denote the subgroup of elements with denominator *p*<sup>*n*</sup> where *n* is a non-negative integer. *G*<sub>*p*</sub> is an infinite cyclic group, and the point (*a*<sup>2</sup> − *b*<sup>2</sup>)/*p* + (2*ab*/*p*)*i* is a generator of *G*<sub>*p*</sub>. Furthermore, by factoring the denominators of an element of *G*, it can be shown that *G* is a direct sum of *G*<sub>2</sub> and the *G*<sub>*p*</sub>. That is:

$$G \cong G_2 \oplus \bigoplus_{p \, \equiv \, 1 \, (\text{mod } 4)} G_p.$$

Since it is a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) rather than [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887), only finitely many of the values in the *G<sub>p</sub>*s are non-zero.

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

Viewing *G* as an infinite direct sum, consider the element ({*0*}; 2, 0, 1, 0, 0, ..., 0, ...) where the first coordinate *0* is in [*C*<sub>4</sub>](wikipedia://en/52327) and the other coordinates give the powers of (*a*<sup>2</sup> − *b*<sup>2</sup>)/*p*(*r*) + *i*2*ab*/*p*(*r*), where *p*(*r*) is the *r*th prime number of form 4*k* + 1. Then this corresponds to, in *G*, the rational point (3/5 + *i*4/5)<sup>2</sup> ⋅ (8/17 + *i*15/17)<sup>1</sup> = −416/425 + i87/425. The denominator 425 is the product of the denominator 5 twice, and the denominator 17 once, and as in the previous example, the square of the numerator −416 plus the square of the numerator 87 is equal to the square of the denominator 425. It should also be noted, as a connection to help retain understanding, that the denominator 5 = *p*(1) is the 1st prime of form 4*k* + 1, and the denominator 17 = *p*(3) is the 3rd prime of form 4*k* + 1.

:::

## The unit hyperbola's group of rational points {#The_unit_hyperbola's_group_of_rational_points}

There is a close connection between this group on the [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122) and the group discussed above. If $\frac {a + ib}{c}$ is a rational point on the unit circle, where *a*/*c* and *b*/*c* are [reduced fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_fraction), then (*c*/*a*, *b*/*a*) is a rational point on the unit hyperbola, since $(c/a)^2-(b/a)^2=1,$ satisfying the equation for the unit hyperbola. The group operation here is $(x, y) \times (u, v)=(xu+yv, xv+yu),$and the group identity is the same point (1, 0) as above. In this group there is a close connection with the [hyperbolic cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_cosine) and [hyperbolic sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_sine), which parallels the connection with [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine) and [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) in the unit circle group above.

### Copies inside a larger group {#Copies_inside_a_larger_group}

There are isomorphic copies of both groups, as subgroups (and as geometric objects) of the group of the rational points on the [abelian variety](wikipedia://en/247152) in four-dimensional space given by the equation $w^2+x^2-y^2+z^2=0.$ Note that this variety is the set of points with [Minkowski metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_metric) relative to the origin equal to 0. The identity in this larger group is (1, 0, 1, 0), and the group operation is $(a, b, c, d) \times (w, x, y, z)=(aw-bx,ax+bw,cy+dz,cz+dy).$

For the group on the unit circle, the appropriate subgroup is the subgroup of points of the form (*w*, *x*, 1, 0), with $w^2+x^2=1,$ and its identity element is (1, 0, 1, 0). The unit hyperbola group corresponds to points of form (1, 0, *y*, *z*), with $y^2-z^2=1,$ and the identity is again (1, 0, 1, 0). (Of course, since they are subgroups of the larger group, they both must have the same identity element.)
