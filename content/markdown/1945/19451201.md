# Pole and polar

*Image: Pole and polar.svg.* The polar line *q* to a pole **Q** with respect to a circle centered on the point **O**. The point **P** is the [inversion point](wikipedia://en/295844#Circle_inversion) of **Q**; the polar is the line through **P** that is perpendicular to the line containing **O**, **P** and **Q**.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pole and polar"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **pole** and **polar** are respectively a point and a line that have a unique reciprocal relationship with respect to a given [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673).

:::

**Polar reciprocation** in a given conic section is the transformation of each point in the plane into its polar line and each line in the plane into its pole. In [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849), this affords a one-to-one correspondence between points and lines in the projective plane. This correspondence respects incidence, in the sense that a point *$P$* lies on a line $l$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the pole of $l$ lies on the polar of $P$.

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Pole and polar have several useful properties:

- If a point $P$ lies on the polar of a point $Q$, then $Q$ lies on the polar of $P$. (La Hire's theorem)
- If a point $P$ moves along a line $l$, its polar $p$ rotates about the pole $L$ of the line $l$.
- If two tangent lines can be drawn from a point to the conic section, then its polar passes through both tangent points.
- If a point lies on the conic section, then its polar is the tangent through this point to the conic section.
- If a point $P$ lies on its own polar line, then $P$ is on the conic section.
- Each line has, with respect to a non-degenerated conic section, exactly one pole; and each point has exactly one polar line.
- If a line $l$ intersects a conic $C$ at points $A$ and $B$, and $P$ is a point on $l$, then the intersection of $l$ and the polar of $P$ with respect to $C$ is the [harmonic conjugate](wikipedia://en/16094600) of $P$ with respect to $A$ and $B$.

:::

## Special case of circles {#Special_case_of_circles}

The pole of a line *q* in a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) *C* is the point **Q** that is the [inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_inversion) in *C* of the point **P** on *q* that is closest to the center of the circle (see figure to the right). Conversely, the polar of a point **Q** in a circle *C* is the line *q* such that its closest point **P** to the center of the circle is the inversion of **Q** in *C*. The polar of the circle's center is the [line at infinity](wikipedia://en/403142) and the pole of that line is the circle's center. The poles of lines through the circle's center lie on the line at infinity.

*Image: Conjugate points and lines.svg.* If a point **A** lies on the polar line *q* of the point **Q**, then **Q** lies on the polar line *a* of **A**. This is sometimes referred to as La Hire's theorem. More generally, the polars of all the points on the line *q* must pass through its pole **Q**.

The relationship between poles and polars is reciprocal. Thus, if a point **A** lies on the polar line *q* of a point **Q**, then the point **Q** must lie on the polar line *a* of the point **A**. (La Hire's theorem) The two polar lines *a* and *q* need not be parallel.

There is another description of the polar line of a point **P** in the case that it lies outside the circle *C*. In this case, there are two lines through **P** which are [tangent to the circle](wikipedia://en/19491492), and the polar of **P** is the line joining the two points of tangency (not shown here). This shows that [pole and polar line](wikipedia://en/16094600#Projective_conics) are concepts in the [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) of the [plane](wikipedia://en/24350) and generalize with any [nonsingular conic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic) in the place of the circle *C*.

:::

## Polar reciprocation {#Polar_reciprocation}

*Image: Incidence poles polars.svg.* Illustration of the duality between points and lines, and the double meaning of "incidence". If two lines *a* and *k* pass through a single point **Q**, then the polar *q* of **Q** joins the poles **A** and **K** of the lines *a* and *k*, respectively.

The concepts of *a pole and its polar line* were advanced in [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849). For instance, the polar line can be viewed as the set of [projective harmonic conjugates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_harmonic_conjugates) of a given point, the pole, with respect to a conic. The operation of replacing every point by its polar and vice versa is known as a polarity.

A **polarity** is a [correlation](wikipedia://en/7984037) that is also an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450).

For some point *P* and its polar *p*, any other point *Q* on *p* is the pole of a line *q* through *P*. This comprises a reciprocal relationship, and is one in which incidences are preserved.[^o8]

:::

## General conic sections {#General_conic_sections}

*Image: Pool poollijn.svg.* Line *p* is the polar line to point **P**, *l* to **L** and *m* to **M**

*Image: Pool\_poollijn\_eig.svg.* *p* is the polar line to point **P** ; *m* is the polar line to **M**

The concepts of pole, polar and reciprocation can be generalized from circles to other [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673) which are the [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277#Pole-polar_relation), [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052#Pole-polar_relation_for_a_hyperbola) and [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231#Pole-polar_relation_of_a_parabola). This generalization is possible because conic sections result from a reciprocation of a circle in another circle, and the properties involved, such as [incidence](wikipedia://en/387224) and the [cross-ratio](wikipedia://en/401767), are preserved under all [projective transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_transformation).

### Calculating the polar of a point {#Calculating_the_polar_of_a_point}

A general [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673) may be written as a second-degree equation in the [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) (*x*, *y*) of the [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29)

$$A_{xx} x^2 + 2 A_{xy} xy + A_{yy} y^2 + 2 B_{x} x + 2 B_{y} y + C = 0$$

where *A*<sub>*xx*</sub>, *A*<sub>*xy*</sub>, *A*<sub>*yy*</sub>, *B*<sub>*x*</sub>, *B*<sub>*y*</sub>, and *C* are the constants defining the equation. For such a conic section, the polar line to a given pole point $(\xi, \eta)$ is defined by the equation

$$D x + E y + F = 0\,$$

where *D*, *E* and *F* are likewise constants that depend on the pole coordinates $(\xi, \eta)$

$$\begin{aligned} D &= A_{xx} \xi + A_{xy} \eta + B_{x} \\ E &= A_{xy} \xi + A_{yy} \eta + B_{y} \\ F &= B_{x} \xi + B_{y} \eta + C \end{aligned}$$

### Calculating the pole of a line {#Calculating_the_pole_of_a_line}

The pole of the line $D x + E y + F = 0$, relative to the non-degenerated conic section

$$A_{xx} x^{2} + 2 A_{xy} xy + A_{yy} y^{2} + 2 B_{x} x + 2 B_{y} y + C = 0$$

can be calculated in two steps.

First, calculate the numbers x, y and z from

$$\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} A_{xx} & A_{xy} & B_{x} \\ A_{xy} & A_{yy} & B_{y} \\ B_{x} & B_{y} & C \end{bmatrix}^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} D \\ E \\ F \end{bmatrix}$$

Now, the pole is the point with coordinates $\left( \frac{x}{z} , \frac{y}{z} \right)$

### Via complete quadrangle {#Via_complete_quadrangle}

In [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849), two lines in a plane always intersect. Thus, given four points forming a [complete quadrangle](wikipedia://en/2605272), the lines connecting the points cross in an additional three *diagonal points*.

Given a point *Z* not on conic *C*, draw two [secants](wikipedia://en/241886) from *Z* through *C* crossing at points *A*, *B*, *D*, and *E*. Then these four points form a complete quadrangle, and *Z* is at one of the diagonal points. The line joining the other two diagonal points is the polar of *Z*, and *Z* is the pole of this line.[^o16]

::: {#o18 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Poles and polars were defined by [Joseph Diaz Gergonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Diaz_Gergonne) and play an important role in his solution of the [problem of Apollonius](wikipedia://en/2642185).[^o19]

In planar dynamics a pole is a center of rotation, the polar is the force line of action and the conic is the mass–inertia matrix.[^o20] The pole–polar relationship is used to define the [center of percussion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_percussion) of a planar rigid body. If the pole is the hinge point, then the polar is the percussion line of action as described in planar [screw theory](wikipedia://en/1318037).

:::
