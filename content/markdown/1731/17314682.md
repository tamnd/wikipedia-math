# Algebraic statistics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Algebraic statistics"}

**Algebraic statistics** is a branch of [mathematical statistics](wikipedia://en/888711) that focuses on the use of algebraic, geometric, and combinatorial methods in [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685). While the use of these methods has a long history in statistics, algebraic statistics is continuously forging new interdisciplinary connections.

:::

This growing field has established itself squarely at the intersection of several areas of mathematics, including, for instance, [multilinear algebra](wikipedia://en/243946), [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990), [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), convex geometry, [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170), theoretical problems in statistics, and their practical applications. For example, algebraic statistics has been useful for [experimental design](wikipedia://en/9541), [parameter estimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameter_estimation), and [hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis_testing).

## History {#History}

Algebraic statistics can be traced back to [Karl Pearson](wikipedia://en/164695), who used polynomial algebra to study Gaussian mixture models. Subsequently, [Ronald A. Fisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_A._Fisher), [Henry B. Mann](wikipedia://en/30545778), and [Rosemary A. Bailey](wikipedia://en/26414007) applied [Abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) to the [design of experiments](wikipedia://en/9541). Experimental designs were also studied with [affine geometry](wikipedia://en/243890) over [finite fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_fields) and then with the introduction of [association schemes](wikipedia://en/2238152) by [R. C. Bose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._C._Bose). [Orthogonal arrays](wikipedia://en/17887653) were introduced by [C. R. Rao](wikipedia://en/1418009) also for experimental designs.

The field experienced a major revitalization in the 1990s. In 1998, Diaconis and Sturmfels introduced Gröbner bases for constructing Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithms for conditional sampling from discrete exponential families. Pistone and Wynn, in 1996, applied computational commutative algebra to the design and analysis of experiments, providing new tools for understanding confounding and identifiability in complex experimental settings. These works, along with the monograph by Giovanni Pistone, Eva Riccomagno, and Henry P. Wynn, in which the term “algebraic statistics” was first used, played a pivotal role in establishing this field as a unified area of research.

Modern researchers in algebraic statistics explore a wide range of topics, including [computational biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_biology), graphical models, and statistical learning.

## Active Research Areas {#Active_Research_Areas}

- Phylogenetics
- Maximum likelihood estimation
- Method of moments
- Graphical models
- Tropical statistics
- Statistical learning theory. Algebraic geometry has also recently found applications to [statistical learning theory](wikipedia://en/1053303), including a [generalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watanabe-Akaike_information_criterion) of the [Akaike information criterion](wikipedia://en/690512) to [singular statistical models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_statistical_model).[^o4]
- Algebraic analysis and abstract statistical inference. [Invariant measures](wikipedia://en/40645) on [locally compact groups](wikipedia://en/1651204) have long been used in [statistical theory](wikipedia://en/27579), particularly in [multivariate analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_analysis). [Beurling](wikipedia://en/286530)'s [factorization theorem](wikipedia://en/585826) and much of the work on (abstract) [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147) sought better understanding of the [Wold](wikipedia://en/4415141) [decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wold_decomposition) of [stationary stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary_stochastic_process), which is important in [time series](wikipedia://en/406624) statistics. Encompassing previous results on probability theory on algebraic structures, [Ulf Grenander](wikipedia://en/27178142) developed a theory of "abstract inference". Grenander's abstract inference and his [theory of patterns](wikipedia://en/3709680) are useful for [spatial statistics](wikipedia://en/9467349) and [image analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_analysis); these theories rely on [lattice theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_theory).
- Partially ordered sets and lattices. [Partially ordered vector spaces](wikipedia://en/8099221) and [vector lattices](wikipedia://en/8099018) are used throughout statistical theory. [Garrett Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/1180916) metrized the positive cone using [Hilbert's projective metric](wikipedia://en/22154619) and proved [Jentsch's theorem](wikipedia://en/1540333) using the [contraction mapping](wikipedia://en/6239) [theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contraction_mapping_theorem).[^o5] Birkhoff's results have been used for [maximum entropy](wikipedia://en/201718) [estimation](wikipedia://en/36687154) (which can be viewed as [linear programming](wikipedia://en/43730) in [infinite dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_dimensional_optimization)) by [Jonathan Borwein](wikipedia://en/1145010) and colleagues. [Vector lattices](wikipedia://en/8099018) and [conical measures](wikipedia://en/8099018) were introduced into [statistical decision theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_decision_theory) by [Lucien Le Cam](wikipedia://en/2629104).

## Introductory Example {#Introductory_Example}

Consider a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) $X$ which can take on the values $0,1,2$. Such a variable is completely characterized by the three probabilities

$$p_i=\mathrm{Pr}(X=i),\quad i=0,1,2$$

and these numbers satisfy

$$\sum_{i=0}^2 p_i = 1 \quad \mbox{and}\quad 0\leq p_i \leq 1.$$

Conversely, any three such numbers unambiguously specify a random variable, so we can identify the random variable $X$ with the tuple $(p_0,p_1,p_2)\in\mathbb{R}^3$.

Now suppose $X$ is a [binomial random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_random_variable) with parameter $q$ and $n=2$, i.e. $X$ represents the number of successes when repeating a certain experiment two times, where each experiment has an individual success probability of $q$. Then

$$p_i=\mathrm{Pr}(X=i)={2 \choose i}q^i (1-q)^{2-i}$$

and it is not hard to show that the tuples $(p_0,p_1,p_2)$ which arise in this way are precisely the ones satisfying

$$4 p_0 p_2-p_1^2=0.\$$

The latter is a [polynomial equation](wikipedia://en/23000) defining an algebraic variety (or surface) in $\mathbb{R}^3$, and this variety, when intersected with the [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) given by

$$\sum_{i=0}^2 p_i = 1 \quad \mbox{and}\quad 0\leq p_i \leq 1,$$

yields a piece of an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) which may be identified with the set of all 3-state Bernoulli variables. Determining the parameter $q$ amounts to locating one point on this curve; testing the hypothesis that a given variable $X$ is [Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/199189) amounts to testing whether a certain point lies on that curve or not.
