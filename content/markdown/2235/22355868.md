# George Sinclair (mathematician)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="George Sinclair"}

**George Sinclair** (Sinclar) (c. 1630–1696)[^o3][^o4] was a [Scottish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_people) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902), [engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineer) and [demonologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demonologist). The first [Professor of Mathematics at the University of Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor_of_Mathematics_%28Glasgow%29), he is known for *Satan's Invisible World Discovered*, (c. 1685), a work on [witchcraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witchcraft), ghosts and other supernatural phenomena. He wrote in all three areas of his interests, including an account of the "Glenluce Devil", a [poltergeist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poltergeist) case from c. 1654, in a 1672 book mainly on [hydrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrostatics) but also a pioneering study of geological structures through his experience in [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) [mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining).

:::

## Life {#Life}

Sinclair was most likely from the [East Lothian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Lothian) area, around [Haddington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haddington,_East_Lothian). He studied at the [University of St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews) between the years 1645 and 1646, but moved to complete his studies at the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh) obtaining a Master of Arts degree from there in 1649.[^o4]

He became a professor at the [University of Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Glasgow), on 18 April 1654, initially in a [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy) chair, then moved to a chair founded for mathematics. He helped to fund the building of Glasgow College in 1656.[^o6] In 1655 he made descents in a [diving bell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diving_bell) off the [Isle of Mull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_of_Mull), to look at the wreck of a ship from the [Spanish Armada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Armada) there.

Being a Presbyterian, and failing to take the [oath of allegiance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oath_of_Allegiance_%28United_Kingdom%29), he was deprived of his university post in 1666.[^o8] He then worked as a mineral surveyor and engineer, and was employed in particular by [Sir James Hope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hope_of_Hopetoun). He was brought in by the magistrates of Edinburgh, about 1670, to oversee piping of water from [Comiston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comiston) into the city.[^o9]

On 3 March 1691, University of Edinburgh appointed him again to the professorship of mathematics,[^o10] which had been newly re-funded after a long vacancy.[^o11]

Sinclair invented an early example of a [perpetual motion](wikipedia://en/54077) machine based on the principle of the [siphon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siphon). He first proposed this in a Latin work on [pneumatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumatics) in 1669.[^o12]

## Glenluce Devil {#Glenluce_Devil}

*Image: SinclairSatansWorld1685.jpg.* Title page of the first edition of *Satan's Invisible World Discovered*, published in 1685.

In his book *Satan's Invisible World Discovered* (1685), Sinclair described an alleged [poltergeist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poltergeist) incident known as the Devil of Glenluce. Sinclair described the incident as having a "usefulness for refuting atheism."[^o14]

The incident is described as having taken place at the house of weaver Gilbert Campbell in [Glenluce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenluce) during October, 1654. A beggar named Alexander Agnew was refused a handout by Campbell.[^o15] Agnew had promised to cause the family harm and over the next two years strange phenomena were alleged to have occurred at the house. This included the mysterious cutting of [warp thread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warp_and_woof), demonic voices, strange whistling noises and stones being thrown.[^o16] The poltergeist claims have been dismissed by researchers as a [hoax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoax). Magic historian [Thomas Frost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Frost_%28writer%29) suggested that the phenomena were the result of [conjuring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_%28illusion%29) trickery.[^o17] The story was given to Sinclair by Campbell's son Thomas, a philosophy student from a college in Glasgow who was living at the household. Folklorist [Andrew Lang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Lang) suggested that Thomas had produced the phenomena fraudulently.[^o18]

Historian [David Damrosch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Damrosch) has noted that Alexander Agnew commonly called the "Jock of Broad Scotland" was the first person in Scottish history to publicly deny the existence of God.[^o19] He was hanged at Dumfries for [blasphemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blasphemy) on 21 May 1656.[^o20]

## Geological pioneer {#Geological_pioneer}

A long-neglected aspect deriving from Sinclair's work as a mineral surveyor is that the last part of *Hydrostaticks* - *a short History of Coal* - includes the first published [geological cross section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_section_%28geology%29) in which he treats the strata in purely geometric terms.[^o22]

## Controversy {#Controversy}

[James Gregory](wikipedia://en/220425), then a professor at the [University of St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews), attacked Sinclair in a 1672 pamphlet *The New and Great Art of Weighing Vanity*, under the name of Patrick Mather or Mathers, [archbeadle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beadle) of the [University of St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews). Gregory was both a [Cartesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesianism) and an [Episcopalian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Episcopal_Church), and self-consciously invoked the [Hobbes-Wallis controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hobbes-Wallis_controversy) in aiming at the [non-conformist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonconformist_%28Protestantism%29) Sinclair.[^o8] The title of Gregory's pamphlet ridiculed Sinclair's 1669 book *Ars Nova et Magna Gravitatis et Levitatis* (*The New and Great Art of Heaviness and Lightness*). An appendix to the work, *Tentamina de motu penduli et projectorum*,[^o24] was a more important essay on dynamics, regarded by [D. T. Whiteside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D._T._Whiteside) as a probable source of [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627)’s theory of [resisted motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertia).[^o25] Sinclair wrote an answer to Gregory,[^o26] but it remained unpublished.

In 1684 he published as his own a work *Truth's Victory over Error*. It was in fact an English translation by Sinclair of the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) [inaugural dissertation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inaugural_dissertation) given by [David Dickson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Dickson_%28professor%29), who became [Professor of Divinity, Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor_of_Divinity,_Glasgow) in 1640, on the occasion in 1650 when he moved to Edinburgh. This was pointed out in short order.
