# White-box cryptography

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="White-box cryptography"}

In [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), the **white-box** model refers to an extreme attack scenario, in which an adversary has full unrestricted access to a cryptographic implementation, most commonly of a [block cipher](wikipedia://en/4594) such as the [Advanced Encryption Standard](wikipedia://en/1260) (AES). A variety of security goals may be posed (see the section below), the most fundamental being "unbreakability", requiring that any (bounded) attacker should not be able to extract the secret key hardcoded in the implementation, while at the same time the implementation must be fully functional. In contrast, the black-box model only provides an oracle access to the analyzed [cryptographic primitive](wikipedia://en/4928985) (in the form of encryption and/or decryption queries). There is also a model in-between, the so-called gray-box model, which corresponds to additional [information leakage](wikipedia://en/986353) from the implementation, more commonly referred to as [side-channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Side-channel) leakage.

:::

**White-box cryptography** is a practice and study of techniques for designing and attacking white-box implementations. It has many applications, including [digital rights management](wikipedia://en/18938226) (DRM), [pay television](wikipedia://en/661989), protection of cryptographic keys in the presence of [malware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malware),[^o10] mobile payments and [cryptocurrency](wikipedia://en/36662188) wallets. Examples of DRM systems employing white-box implementations include [CSS](wikipedia://en/18935238) and [Widevine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Widevine).

White-box cryptography is closely related to the more general notions of [obfuscation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obfuscation_%28software%29), in particular, to [Black-box obfuscation](wikipedia://en/65807245), proven to be impossible, and to [Indistinguishability obfuscation](wikipedia://en/59961754), constructed recently under well-founded assumptions but so far being infeasible to implement in practice.[^o2]

As of January 2023, there are no publicly known unbroken white-box designs of standard symmetric encryption schemes. On the other hand, there exist many unbroken white-box implementations of *dedicated* block ciphers designed specifically to achieve incompressibility (see ).

## Security goals {#Security_goals}

Depending on the application, different security goals may be required from a white-box implementation. Specifically, for [symmetric-key algorithms](wikipedia://en/53042) the following are distinguished:[^o4]

- **Unbreakability** is the most fundamental goal requiring that a bounded attacker should not be able to recover the secret key embedded in the white-box implementation. Without this requirement, all other security goals are unreachable since a successful attacker can simply use a [reference implementation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_implementation) of the encryption scheme together with the extracted key.
- **One-wayness** requires that a white-box implementation of an encryption scheme can not be used by a bounded attacker to decrypt ciphertexts. This requirement essentially turns a symmetric encryption scheme into a [public-key encryption scheme](wikipedia://en/24222), where the white-box implementation plays the role of the public key associated to the embedded secret key. This idea was proposed already in the famous work of Diffie and Hellman in 1976[^o5] as a potential public-key encryption candidate.
- **Code lifting** security is an informal requirement on the context, in which the white-box program is being executed. It demands that an attacker can not extract a functional copy of the program. This goal is particularly relevant in the [DRM](wikipedia://en/18938226) setting. [Code obfuscation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obfuscation_%28software%29) techniques are often used to achieve this goal.

A commonly used technique is to compose the white-box implementation with so-called **external encodings**.[^o10] These are lightweight secret encodings that modify the function computed by the white-box part of an application. It is required that their effect is canceled in other parts of the application in an obscure way, using [code obfuscation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obfuscation_%28software%29) techniques. Alternatively, the canceling counterparts can be applied on a remote server.

- **Incompressibility** requires that an attacker can not significantly compress a given white-box implementation. This can be seen as a way to achieve code lifting security (see above), since exfiltrating a large program from a constrained device (for example, an [embedded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embedded_system) or a [mobile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_device) device) can be time-consuming and may be easy to detect by a [firewall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firewall_%28computing%29).[^o6]

Examples of incompressible designs include SPACE cipher,[^o6] SPNbox,[^o7] WhiteKey and WhiteBlock.[^o8] These ciphers use large lookup tables that can be [pseudorandomly](wikipedia://en/182249) generated from a secret master key. Although this makes the recovery of the master key hard, the lookup tables themselves play the role of an equivalent secret key. Thus, unbreakability is achieved only partially.

- **Traceability** ([Traitor tracing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traitor_tracing)) requires that each distributed white-box implementation contains a [digital watermark](wikipedia://en/1061511) allowing identification of the guilty user in case the white-box program is being leaked and distributed publicly.

## History {#History}

The white-box model with initial attempts of white-box [DES](wikipedia://en/7978) and [AES](wikipedia://en/1260) implementations were first proposed by Chow, Eisen, Johnson and van Oorshot in 2003.[^o10][^o11] The designs were based on representing the cipher as a network of [lookup tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lookup_table) and obfuscating the tables by composing them with small (4- or 8-bit) random encodings. Such protection satisfied a property that each single obfuscated table individually does not contain any information about the secret key. Therefore, a potential attacker has to combine several tables in their analysis.

The first two schemes were broken in 2004 by Billet, Gilbert, and Ech-Chatbi using structural [cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715).[^o12] The attack was subsequently called "the BGE attack".

The numerous consequent design attempts (2005-2022)[^o13] were quickly broken by practical dedicated attacks.[^o14]

In 2016, Bos, Hubain, Michiels and Teuwen showed that an adaptation of standard side-channel [power analysis](wikipedia://en/754362) attacks can be used to efficiently and fully automatically break most existing white-box designs.[^o15] This result created a new research direction about generic attacks (correlation-based, algebraic, [fault injection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_injection)) and protections against them.[^o16]

## Competitions {#Competitions}

Four editions of the *WhibOx contest* were held in 2017, 2019, 2021 and 2024 respectively. These competitions invited white-box designers both from academia and industry to submit their implementation in the form of (possibly obfuscated) [C code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28programming_language%29). At the same time, everyone could attempt to attack these programs and recover the embedded secret key. Each of these competitions lasted for about 4-5 months.

- *WhibOx 2017 / CHES 2017 Capture the Flag Challenge* targeted the standard AES block cipher. Among 94 submitted implementations, all were broken during the competition, with the strongest one staying unbroken for 28 days.
- *WhibOx 2019 / CHES 2019 Capture the Flag Challenge* again targeted the AES block cipher. Among 27 submitted implementations, 3 programs stayed unbroken throughout the competition, but were broken after 51 days since the publication.[^o18][^o19]
- *WhibOx 2021 / CHES 2021 Capture the Flag Challenge* changed the target to [ECDSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ECDSA), a [digital signature](wikipedia://en/59644) scheme based on [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/9966). Among 97 submitted implementations, all were broken within at most 2 days.[^o20][^o21]
- *WhibOx 2024 / CHES 2024 Capture the Flag Challenge* again targeted [ECDSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ECDSA). Among 47 submitted implementations, all were broken during the competition, with the strongest one staying unbroken for almost 5 days.
