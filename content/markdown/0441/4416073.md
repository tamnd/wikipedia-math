# Approximations of pi

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Approximations of pi"}

*Image: PiComputationHistory.svg.* Graph showing the historical evolution of the record precision of numerical approximations to pi, measured in decimal places (depicted on a logarithmic scale; time before 1400 is not shown to scale)

[Approximations](wikipedia://en/336271#Mathematics) for the [mathematical constant](wikipedia://en/48779551) [pi](wikipedia://en/23601) ($\pi$) in the [history of mathematics](wikipedia://en/14220) reached an accuracy within 0.04% of the true value before the beginning of the [Common Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era). In [Chinese mathematics](wikipedia://en/1524543), this was improved to approximations correct to what corresponds to about seven decimal digits by the 5th century.

:::

Further progress was not made until the 14th century, when [Madhava of Sangamagrama](wikipedia://en/2793007) developed approximations correct to eleven and then thirteen digits. [Jamshīd al-Kāshī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamshīd_al-Kāshī) achieved sixteen digits next. Early modern mathematicians reached an accuracy of 35 digits by the beginning of the 17th century ([Ludolph van Ceulen](wikipedia://en/362612)), and 126 digits by the 19th century ([Jurij Vega](wikipedia://en/40546)).

The record of manual approximation of $\pi$ is held by [William Shanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shanks), who calculated 527 decimals correctly in 1853.[^o37] Since the middle of the 20th century, the approximation of $\pi$ has been the task of electronic digital computers (for a comprehensive account, see [chronology of computation of pi](wikipedia://en/4406343)). On December 11, 2025, the current record was established by [StorageReview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/StorageReview) with Alexander Yee's [y-cruncher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y-cruncher) with 314 trillion (3.14× × 10<sup>14</sup>) digits.[^o64]

## Early history {#Early_history}

The best known approximations to $\pi$ dating to [before the Common Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1st_millennium_BC) were accurate to two decimal places; this was improved upon in [Chinese mathematics](wikipedia://en/1524543) in particular by the mid-first millennium, to an accuracy of seven decimal places. After this, no further progress was made until the late medieval period.

Some Egyptologists[^o3] have claimed that the [ancient Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) used an approximation of $\pi$ as $\tfrac{22}{7}$ = 3.142857 (about 0.04% too high) from as early as the [Old Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Kingdom) (c. 2700–2200 BC).[^o4] This claim has been met with skepticism.[^o5][^o6]

[Babylonian mathematics](wikipedia://en/3861353) usually approximated $\pi$ to 3, sufficient for the architectural projects of the time (notably also reflected in the description of [Solomon's Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon's_Temple) in the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Book_of_Kings)).[^o7] The Babylonians were aware that this was an approximation, and one Old Babylonian mathematical tablet excavated near [Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa) in 1936 (dated to between the 19th and 17th centuries BCE) gives a better approximation of $\pi$ as $\tfrac{25}{8}$ = 3.125, about 0.528% below the exact value.[^o8][^o9][^o10][^o11]

At about the same time, the Egyptian [Rhind Mathematical Papyrus](wikipedia://en/9210114) (dated to the [Second Intermediate Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Intermediate_Period), c. 1600 BCE, although stated to be a copy of an older, [Middle Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Kingdom_of_Egypt) text) implies an approximation of $\pi$ as $\tfrac{256}{81}$ ≈ 3.16 (accurate to 0.6 percent) by calculating the area of a circle via approximation with the [octagon](wikipedia://en/314575).[^o5][^o12]

Astronomical calculations in the *[Shatapatha Brahmana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatapatha_Brahmana)* (c. 6th century BCE) use a fractional approximation of $\tfrac{339}{108} \approx 3.139$.[^o13]

The [Mahabharata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahabharata) (500 BCE – 300 CE) offers an approximation of 3, in the ratios offered in [Bhishma Parva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhishma_Parva) verses: 6.12.40–45.[^o14]

In the 3rd century BCE, [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) proved the sharp inequalities $\tfrac{223}{71}$ \< $\pi$ \< $\tfrac{22}{7}$ , by means of regular [96-gons](wikipedia://en/23621) (accuracies of 2·10<sup>−4</sup> and 4·10<sup>−4</sup>, respectively).[^o15]

In the 2nd century CE, [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979) used the value $\tfrac{377}{120}$, the first known approximation accurate to three decimal places (accuracy 2·10<sup>−5</sup>).[^o16] It is equal to $3+8/60+30/60^2,$ which is accurate to two [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) digits.

The [Chinese mathematician](wikipedia://en/1524543) [Liu Hui](wikipedia://en/202719) in 263 CE computed $\pi$ to between \3.141024 and \3.142708 by inscribing a 96-gon and 192-gon; the average of these two values is \3.141866 (accuracy 9·10<sup>−5</sup>). He also suggested that 3.14 was a good enough approximation for practical purposes. He has also frequently been credited with a later and more accurate result, π ≈ $\tfrac{3927}{1250}$ = 3.1416 (accuracy 2·10<sup>−6</sup>), although some scholars instead believe that this is due to the later (5th-century) Chinese mathematician [Zu Chongzhi](wikipedia://en/151762).[^o17] Zu Chongzhi is known to have computed $\pi$ to be between 3.1415926 and 3.1415927, which was correct to seven decimal places. He also gave [two other approximations of $\pi$](wikipedia://en/7315901): π ≈ $\tfrac{22}{7}$ and π ≈ $\tfrac{355}{113}$, which are not as accurate as his decimal result. The latter fraction is the best possible rational approximation of $\pi$ using fewer than five decimal digits in the numerator and denominator. Zu Chongzhi's results surpass the accuracy reached in Hellenistic mathematics, and would remain without improvement for close to a millennium.

In [Gupta-era India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gupta-era_India) (6th century), mathematician [Aryabhata](wikipedia://en/297204), in his astronomical treatise *[Āryabhaṭīya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Āryabhaṭīya)* stated:

Approximating $\pi$ to four decimal places: π ≈ $\tfrac{62832}{20000}$ = 3.1416,[^o18][^o19][^o20] Aryabhata stated that his result "approximately" (*āsanna* "approaching") gave the circumference of a circle. His 15th-century commentator [Nilakantha Somayaji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilakantha_Somayaji) ([Kerala school of astronomy and mathematics](wikipedia://en/3099132)) has argued that the word means not only that this is an approximation, but that the value is [incommensurable (irrational)](wikipedia://en/20647689).[^o21]

## Middle Ages {#Middle_Ages}

Further progress was not made for nearly a millennium, until the 14th century, when Indian mathematician and astronomer [Madhava of Sangamagrama](wikipedia://en/2793007), founder of the [Kerala school of astronomy and mathematics](wikipedia://en/3099132), found the [Maclaurin series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maclaurin_series) for arctangent, and then two [infinite series](wikipedia://en/15287) for $\pi$.[^o23][^o24][^o25] One of them is now known as the [Madhava–Leibniz series](wikipedia://en/1301559), based on $\pi=4\arctan(1):$

$$\pi=4\left(1-\frac 13+\frac 15-\frac 17 +\cdots\right)$$

The other was based on $\pi=6\arctan(1/\sqrt 3):$

$$\pi = \sqrt{12}\sum^\infty_{k=0} \frac{(-3)^{-k}}{2k+1} = \sqrt{12}\sum^\infty_{k=0} \frac{(-\frac{1}{3})^k}{2k+1} = \sqrt{12}\left(1-{1\over 3\cdot3}+{1\over5\cdot 3^2}-{1\over7\cdot 3^3}+\cdots\right)$$

300px

He used the first 21 terms to compute an approximation of $\pi$ correct to 11 decimal places as \3.14159265359.

He also improved the formula based on arctan(1) by including a correction:

$$\pi/4\approx 1-\frac 13+\frac 15-\frac 17+ \cdots -\frac{(-1)^n}{2n-1}\pm\frac{n^2 + 1}{4n^3 + 5n}$$

It is not known how he came up with this correction.[^o24] Using this he found an approximation of $\pi$ to 13 decimal places of accuracy when $n$ = 75.

Indian mathematician [Bhaskara II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhaskara_II) (12th century) used regular polygons with up to 384 sides to obtain another approximation of π, calculating it as 3.141666.[^o26]

[Jamshīd al-Kāshī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamshīd_al-Kāshī) (Kāshānī) (15th century), a [Persian astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_astronomy) and [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_mathematics), correctly computed the fractional part of 2$\pi$ to 9 [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) digits in 1424,[^o27] and translated this into 16 decimal digits[^o28] after the decimal point:

$$2\pi \approx 6.2831853071795864,$$

which gives 16 correct digits for π after the decimal point:

$$\pi \approx 3.1415926535897932$$

He achieved this level of accuracy by calculating the perimeter of a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) with 3 × 2<sup>28</sup> sides.[^o29]

## 16th to 19th centuries {#16th_to_19th_centuries}

In the second half of the 16th century, the French mathematician [François Viète](wikipedia://en/310238) discovered an [infinite product](wikipedia://en/26382006) that converged on $\pi$ known as [Viète's formula](wikipedia://en/1609504).

The German-Dutch mathematician [Ludolph van Ceulen](wikipedia://en/362612) (*circa* 1600) computed the first 35 decimal places of $\pi$ with a 2<sup>62</sup>-gon. He was so proud of this accomplishment that he had the digits inscribed on his [tombstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_stone).[^o31]

In *Cyclometricus* (1621), [Willebrord Snellius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willebrord_Snellius) demonstrated that the perimeter of the inscribed polygon converges on the circumference twice as fast as does the perimeter of the corresponding circumscribed polygon. This was proved by [Christiaan Huygens](wikipedia://en/42127) in 1654. Snellius was able to obtain seven digits of $\pi$ from a 96-sided polygon.[^o32]

In 1656, [John Wallis](wikipedia://en/239290) published the [Wallis product](wikipedia://en/1301687):

$$\frac{\pi}{2} = \prod_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{ 4n^2 }{ 4n^2 - 1 } = \prod_{n=1}^{\infty} \left(\frac{2n}{2n-1} \cdot \frac{2n}{2n+1}\right) = \Big(\frac{2}{1} \cdot \frac{2}{3}\Big) \cdot \Big(\frac{4}{3} \cdot \frac{4}{5}\Big) \cdot \Big(\frac{6}{5} \cdot \frac{6}{7}\Big) \cdot \Big(\frac{8}{7} \cdot \frac{8}{9}\Big) \cdot \; \cdots$$

In 1706, [John Machin](wikipedia://en/274756) used [Gregory's series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory's_series) (the [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) for [arctangent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctangent)) and [the identity](wikipedia://en/2724287) $\tfrac14\pi = 4\operatorname{arccot} 5 - \operatorname{arccot} 239$ to calculate 100 digits of $\pi$ (see #Machin-like formula below).[^o33][^o34] In 1719, [Thomas de Lagny](wikipedia://en/54353631) used a similar identity to calculate 127 digits (of which 112 were correct). In 1789, the Slovene mathematician [Jurij Vega](wikipedia://en/40546) improved [John Machin](wikipedia://en/274756)'s formula to calculate the first 140 digits, of which the first 126 were correct.[^o35] In 1841, [William Rutherford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Rutherford_%28mathematician%29) calculated 208 digits, of which the first 152 were correct.

The magnitude of such precision (152 decimal places) can be put into context by the fact that the circumference of the largest known object, the observable universe, can be calculated from its diameter (93 billion [light-years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-year)) to a precision of less than one [Planck length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_length) (at \1.6162 × 10<sup>\-35</sup> [meters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meter), the shortest unit of length expected to be directly measurable) using $\pi$ expressed to just 62 decimal places.[^o36]

The English amateur mathematician [William Shanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shanks) calculated $\pi$ to 530 decimal places in January 1853, of which the first 527 were correct (the last few likely being incorrect due to round-off errors).[^o37][^o38] He subsequently expanded his calculation to 607 decimal places in April 1853,[^o39] but an error introduced right at the 530th decimal place rendered the rest of his calculation erroneous; due to the nature of Machin's formula, the error propagated back to the 528th decimal place, leaving only the first 527 digits correct once again.[^o37] Twenty years later, Shanks expanded his calculation to 707 decimal places in April 1873.[^o40] Due to this being an expansion of his previous calculation, most of the new digits were incorrect as well.[^o37] Shanks was said to have calculated new digits all morning and would then spend all afternoon checking his morning's work. This was the longest expansion of $\pi$ until the advent of the electronic digital computer three-quarters of a century later.[^o41]

## 20th and 21st centuries {#20th_and_21st_centuries}

In 1910, the Indian mathematician [Srinivasa Ramanujan](wikipedia://en/47717) found several rapidly converging infinite series of $\pi$, including

$$\frac{1}{\pi} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{9801} \sum^\infty_{k=0} \frac{(4k)!(1103+26390k)}{(k!)^4 396^{4k}}$$

which computes a further eight decimal places of $\pi$ with each term in the series. His series are now the basis for the fastest algorithms currently used to calculate $\pi$. Evaluating the first term alone yields a value correct to seven decimal places:

$$\pi\approx\frac{9801}{2206\sqrt2}\approx 3.14159273$$

See [Ramanujan–Sato series](wikipedia://en/42185330).

From the mid-20th century onwards, all improvements in calculation of $\pi$ have been done with the help of [calculators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculators) or [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computers).

In 1944−45, D. F. Ferguson, with the aid of a [mechanical desk calculator](wikipedia://en/432897), found that [William Shanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shanks) had made a mistake in the 528th decimal place, and that all succeeding digits were incorrect.[^o38][^o43]

In the early years of the computer, an expansion of $\pi$ to 100000 decimal places[^o44] was computed by Maryland mathematician [Daniel Shanks](wikipedia://en/18171362) (no relation to the aforementioned William Shanks) and his team at the [United States Naval Research Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Naval_Research_Laboratory) in Washington, D.C. In 1961, Shanks and his team used two different power series for calculating the digits of $\pi$. For one, it was known that any error would produce a value slightly high, and for the other, it was known that any error would produce a value slightly low. And hence, as long as the two series produced the same digits, there was a very high confidence that they were correct. The first 100,265 digits of $\pi$ were published in 1962.[^o44] The authors outlined what would be needed to calculate $\pi$ to 1 million decimal places and concluded that the task was beyond that day's technology, but would be possible in five to seven years.[^o44]

In 1989, the [Chudnovsky brothers](wikipedia://en/1705790) computed $\pi$ to over 1 billion decimal places on the [supercomputer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercomputer) [IBM 3090](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_3090) using the following variation of Ramanujan's infinite series of $\pi$:

$$\frac{1}{\pi} = 12 \sum^\infty_{k=0} \frac{(-1)^k (6k)! (13591409 + 545140134k)}{(3k)!(k!)^3 640320^{3k + 3/2}}.$$

Records since then have all been accomplished using the [Chudnovsky algorithm](wikipedia://en/15893520). In 1999, [Yasumasa Kanada](wikipedia://en/561940) and his team at the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo) computed $\pi$ to over 200 billion decimal places on the supercomputer [HITACHI SR8000/MPP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HITACHI_SR8000/MPP) (128 nodes) using another variation of Ramanujan's infinite series of $\pi$. In November 2002, Yasumasa Kanada and a team of 9 others used the [Hitachi SR8000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitachi_SR8000), a 64-node supercomputer with 1 terabyte of main memory, to calculate $\pi$ to roughly 1.24 trillion digits in around 600 hours (25 days).[^o45]

### Recent records {#Recent_records}

1. In August 2009, a Japanese supercomputer called the [T2K Open Supercomputer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T2K_Open_Supercomputer) more than doubled the previous record by calculating $\pi$ to roughly 2.6 trillion digits in approximately 73 hours and 36 minutes.
1. In December 2009, [Fabrice Bellard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabrice_Bellard) used a home computer to compute 2.7 trillion decimal digits of $\pi$. Calculations were performed in base 2 (binary), then the result was converted to base 10 (decimal). The calculation, conversion, and verification steps took a total of 131 days.[^o47]
1. In August 2010, Shigeru Kondo used Alexander Yee's [y-cruncher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y-cruncher) to calculate 5 trillion digits of $\pi$. This was the world record for any type of calculation, but significantly it was performed on a home computer built by Kondo.[^o48] The calculation was done between 4 May and 3 August, with the primary and secondary verifications taking 64 and 66 hours respectively.[^o49]
1. In October 2011, Shigeru Kondo broke his own record by computing ten trillion (10<sup>13</sup>) and fifty digits using the same method but with better hardware.[^o50][^o51]
1. In December 2013, Kondo broke his own record for a second time when he computed 12.1 trillion digits of $\pi$.[^o52]
1. In October 2014, Sandon Van Ness, going by the pseudonym "houkouonchi" used y-cruncher to calculate 13.3 trillion digits of $\pi$.[^o53]
1. In November 2016, Peter Trueb and his sponsors computed on y-cruncher and fully verified 22.4 trillion digits of $\pi$ (22,459,157,718,361 ($\pi$<sup>$e$</sup> × 10<sup>12</sup>)).[^o54] The computation took (with three interruptions) 105 days to complete,[^o53] the limitation of further expansion being primarily storage space.[^o52]
1. In March 2019, [Emma Haruka Iwao](wikipedia://en/60230131), an employee at [Google](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google), computed 31.4 (approximately 10$\pi$) trillion digits of pi using y-cruncher and [Google Cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Cloud_Platform) machines. This took 121 days to complete.[^o55]
1. In January 2020, Timothy Mullican announced the computation of 50 trillion digits over 303 days.[^o56][^o57]
1. On 14 August 2021, a team (DAViS) at the [University of Applied Sciences of the Grisons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Applied_Sciences_of_the_Grisons) announced completion of the computation of $\pi$ to 62.8 (approximately 20$\pi$) trillion digits.[^o58][^o59]
1. On 8 June 2022, Emma Haruka Iwao announced on the Google Cloud Blog the computation of 100 trillion (10<sup>14</sup>) digits of $\pi$ over 158 days using Alexander Yee's [y-cruncher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y-cruncher).[^o60]
1. On 14 March 2024, Jordan Ranous, Kevin O’Brien and Brian Beeler computed $\pi$ to 105 trillion digits, also using y-cruncher.[^o61]
1. On 28 June 2024, the [StorageReview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/StorageReview) Team computed $\pi$ to 202 trillion digits, also using y-cruncher.[^o62]
1. On 2 April 2025, [Linus Media Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linus_Media_Group) and [Kioxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kioxia) computed $\pi$ to 300 trillion digits, also using y-cruncher.[^o63]
1. On 11 December 2025, the record returned to the [StorageReview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/StorageReview) Team, after they computed $\pi$ to 314 trillion digits, again using y-cruncher.[^o64]

## Practical approximations {#Practical_approximations}

Depending on the purpose of a calculation, $\pi$ can be approximated by using fractions for ease of calculation. The most notable such approximations are $\tfrac{22}{7}$ ([relative error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_error) of about 4·10<sup>−4</sup>) and $\tfrac{355}{113}$ (relative error of about 8·10<sup>−8</sup>).[^o66][^o67][^o68] In Chinese mathematics, the fractions 22/7 and 355/113 are known as Yuelü () and [Milü](wikipedia://en/7315901) ().

## Non-mathematical "definitions" of $\pi$ {#Non-mathematical_"definitions"_of}

Of some notability are legal or historical texts purportedly "defining $\pi$" to have some rational value, such as the "[Indiana Pi Bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_Pi_Bill)" of 1897, which stated "the ratio of the diameter and circumference is as five-fourths to four" (which would imply "$\pi = 3.2$") and a passage in the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible) that implies that $\pi = 3$.

### Indiana bill {#Indiana_bill}

The so-called "Indiana Pi Bill" from 1897 has often been characterized as an attempt to "legislate the value of Pi". Rather, the bill dealt with a purported solution to the problem of geometrically "[squaring the circle](wikipedia://en/201359)".[^o71]

The bill was nearly passed by the [Indiana General Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_General_Assembly) in the U.S., and has been claimed to imply a number of different values for $\pi$, although the closest it comes to explicitly asserting one is the wording "the ratio of the diameter and circumference is as five-fourths to four", which would make $\pi = \tfrac{16}{5} = 3.2$, a discrepancy of nearly 2 percent. A mathematics professor who happened to be present the day the bill was brought up for consideration in the Senate, after it had passed in the House, helped to stop the passage of the bill on its second reading, after which the assembly thoroughly ridiculed it before [postponing it indefinitely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postpone_indefinitely).

### Imputed biblical value {#Imputed_biblical_value}

It is sometimes claimed that the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible) implies that "$\pi$ equals three", based on a passage in 1 Kings and 2 Chronicles giving measurements for the [round basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molten_Sea) located in front of the [Temple in Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_in_Jerusalem) as having a diameter of 10 [cubits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubit) and a circumference of 30 cubits.

The issue is discussed in the [Talmud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talmud) and in [Rabbinic literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbinic_literature).[^o73] Among the many explanations and comments are these:

- [Rabbi Nehemiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbi_Nehemiah) explained this in his *Mishnat ha-Middot* (the earliest known [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew) text on [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), ca. 150 CE) by saying that the diameter was measured from the *outside* rim while the circumference was measured along the *inner* rim. This interpretation implies a brim about 0.225 cubit (or, assuming an 18-inch "cubit", some 4 inches), or one and a third "[handbreadths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handbreadth)," thick (cf. 1 and 2).
- [Maimonides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maimonides) states (ca. 1168 CE) that $\pi$ can only be known approximately, so the value 3 was given as accurate enough for religious purposes. This is taken by some[^o74] as the earliest assertion that $\pi$ is irrational.

There is still some debate on this passage in biblical scholarship.[^o75][^o76] Many reconstructions of the basin show a wider brim (or flared lip) extending outward from the bowl itself by several inches to match the description given in 1[^o77] In the succeeding verses, the rim is described as "a handbreadth thick; and the brim thereof was wrought like the brim of a cup, like the flower of a lily: it received and held three thousand baths" 2, which suggests a shape that can be encompassed with a string shorter than the total length of the brim, e.g., a [Lilium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lilium) flower or a [Teacup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teacup).

## Development of efficient formulae {#Development_of_efficient_formulae}

### Polygon approximation to a circle {#Polygon_approximation_to_a_circle}

Archimedes, in his *[Measurement of a Circle](wikipedia://en/18203202)*, created the first algorithm for the calculation of $\pi$ based on the idea that the perimeter of any (convex) polygon inscribed in a circle is less than the circumference of the circle, which, in turn, is less than the perimeter of any circumscribed polygon. He started with inscribed and circumscribed regular hexagons, whose perimeters are readily determined. He then shows how to calculate the perimeters of regular polygons of twice as many sides that are inscribed and circumscribed about the same circle. This is a recursive procedure which would be described today as follows: Let $p_{k}$ and $P_{k}$ denote the perimeters of regular polygons of $k$ sides that are inscribed and circumscribed about the same circle, respectively. Then,

$$P_{2n} = \frac{2p_nP_n}{p_n + P_n}, \quad \quad p_{2n} = \sqrt{p_n P_{2n}}.$$

Archimedes uses this to successively compute $P_{12}, p_{12}, P_{24}, p_{24}, P_{48}, p_{48}, P_{96}$ and $p_{96}$.[^o80] Using these last values he obtains

$$3 \frac{10}{71} < \pi < 3 \frac{1}{7}.$$

It is not known why Archimedes stopped at a 96-sided polygon; it only takes patience to extend the computations. [Heron](wikipedia://en/68274) reports in his *Metrica* (about 60 CE) that Archimedes continued the computation in a now lost book, but then attributes an incorrect value to him.[^o81]

Archimedes uses no trigonometry in this computation and the difficulty in applying the method lies in obtaining good approximations for the square roots that are involved. Trigonometry, in the form of a table of chord lengths in a circle, was probably used by [Claudius Ptolemy of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claudius_Ptolemaeus) to obtain the value of $\pi$ given in the *Almagest* (circa 150 CE).[^o82]

Advances in the approximation of $\pi$ (when the methods are known) were made by increasing the number of sides of the polygons used in the computation. A trigonometric improvement by [Willebrord Snell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willebrord_Snellius) (1621) obtains better bounds from a pair of bounds obtained from the polygon method. Thus, more accurate results were obtained from polygons with fewer sides.[^o83] [Viète's formula](wikipedia://en/1609504), published by [François Viète](wikipedia://en/310238) in 1593, was derived by Viète using a closely related polygonal method, but with areas rather than perimeters of polygons whose numbers of sides are powers of two.[^o84]

The last major attempt to compute $\pi$ by this method was carried out by Grienberger in 1630 who calculated 39 decimal places of $\pi$ using Snell's refinement.[^o83]

### Machin-like formula {#Machin-like_formula}

For fast calculations, one may use formulae such as [Machin's](wikipedia://en/274756):

$$\frac{\pi}{4} = 4 \arctan\frac{1}{5} - \arctan\frac{1}{239}$$

together with the [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) expansion of the function [arctan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctan)(*x*). This formula is most easily verified using [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), producing:

$$(5+i)^4\cdot(239-i)=2^2 \cdot 13^4(1+i).$$

(($x$),($y$) = {239, 13<sup>2</sup>} is a solution to the [Pell equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pell_equation) $x$<sup>2</sup> − 2$y$<sup>2</sup> = −1.)

Formulae of this kind are known as *[Machin-like formulae](wikipedia://en/2724287)*. Machin's particular formula was used well into the computer era for calculating record numbers of digits of $\pi$,[^o44] but more recently other similar formulae have been used as well.

For instance, [Shanks](wikipedia://en/18171362) and his team used the following Machin-like formula in 1961 to compute the first 100,000 digits of $\pi$:[^o44]

$$\frac{\pi}{4} = 6 \arctan\frac{1}{8} + 2 \arctan\frac{1}{57} + \arctan\frac{1}{239}$$

and they used another Machin-like formula,

$$\frac{\pi}{4} = 12 \arctan\frac{1}{18} + 8 \arctan\frac{1}{57} - 5 \arctan\frac{1}{239}$$

as a check.

The record as of December 2002 by Yasumasa Kanada of Tokyo University stood at 1,241,100,000,000 digits. The following Machin-like formulae were used for this:

$$\frac{\pi}{4} = 12 \arctan\frac{1}{49} + 32 \arctan\frac{1}{57} - 5 \arctan\frac{1}{239} + 12 \arctan\frac{1}{110443}$$

K. Takano (1982).

$$\frac{\pi}{4} = 44 \arctan\frac{1}{57} + 7 \arctan\frac{1}{239} - 12 \arctan\frac{1}{682} + 24 \arctan\frac{1}{12943}$$

[F. C. M. Størmer](wikipedia://en/1906523) (1896).

### Other classical formulae {#Other_classical_formulae}

Other formulae that have been used to compute estimates of $\pi$ include:

[Liu Hui](wikipedia://en/17829294) (see also [Viète's formula](wikipedia://en/1609504)):

$$\begin{aligned} \pi &\approx 768 \sqrt{2 - \sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2+1}}}}}}}}}\\ &\approx 3.14159046323. \end{aligned}$$

[Madhava](wikipedia://en/2793007):

$$\pi = \sqrt{12}\sum^\infty_{k=0} \frac{(-3)^{-k}}{2k+1} = \sqrt{12}\sum^\infty_{k=0} \frac{(-\frac{1}{3})^k}{2k+1} = \sqrt{12}\left({1\over 1\cdot3^0}-{1\over 3\cdot3^1}+{1\over5\cdot 3^2}-{1\over7\cdot 3^3}+\cdots\right)$$

[Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) / Euler Convergence Transformation:[^o88]

$$\begin{aligned} \arctan x &= \frac{x}{1 + x^2} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(2k)!!\,x^{2k}}{(2k+1)!!\,(1 + x^2)^k} = \frac{x}{1 + x^2} + \frac23\frac{x^3}{(1 + x^2)^2} + \frac{2\cdot 4}{3 \cdot 5}\frac{x^5}{(1 + x^2)^3} + \cdots \\[10mu] \frac{\pi}{2} &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{k!}{(2k+1)!!}= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \cfrac {2^k k!^2}{(2k + 1)!} = 1+\frac{1}{3}\left(1+\frac{2}{5}\left(1+\frac{3}{7}\left(1+\cdots\right)\right)\right) \end{aligned}$$

where $m!!$ is the [double factorial](wikipedia://en/507208), the product of the positive integers up to $m$ with the same [parity](wikipedia://en/143135).

[Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler):

$${\pi} = 20 \arctan\frac{1}{7} + 8 \arctan\frac{3}{79}$$

(Evaluated using the preceding series for $\arctan.$)

[Ramanujan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanujan):

$$\frac{1}{\pi} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{9801} \sum^\infty_{k=0} \frac{(4k)!(1103+26390k)}{(k!)^4 396^{4k}}$$

[David Chudnovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Chudnovsky_%28mathematician%29) and [Gregory Chudnovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_Chudnovsky):

$$\frac{1}{\pi} = 12 \sum^\infty_{k=0} \frac{(-1)^k (6k)! (13591409 + 545140134k)}{(3k)!(k!)^3 640320^{3k + 3/2}}$$

Ramanujan's work is the basis for the [Chudnovsky algorithm](wikipedia://en/15893520), the fastest algorithms used, as of the turn of the millennium, to calculate $\pi$.

### Modern algorithms {#Modern_algorithms}

Extremely long decimal expansions of $\pi$ are typically computed with iterative formulae like the [Gauss–Legendre algorithm](wikipedia://en/12916) and [Borwein's algorithm](wikipedia://en/233353). The latter, found in 1985 by [Jonathan](wikipedia://en/1145010) and [Peter Borwein](wikipedia://en/1339736), converges extremely quickly:

For $y_0=\sqrt2-1,\ a_0=6-4\sqrt2$ and

$$y_{k+1}=(1-f(y_k))/(1+f(y_k)) ~,~ a_{k+1} = a_k(1+y_{k+1})^4 - 2^{2k+3} y_{k+1}(1+y_{k+1}+y_{k+1}^2)$$

where $f(y)=(1-y^4)^{1/4}$, the sequence $1/a_k$ [converges quartically](wikipedia://en/999701) to $\pi$, giving about 100 digits in three steps and over a trillion digits after 20 steps. Even though the Chudnovsky series is only linearly convergent, the Chudnovsky algorithm might be faster than the iterative algorithms in practice; that depends on technological factors such as memory sizes and [access times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random-access_memory).[^o91] For breaking world records, the iterative algorithms are used less commonly than the Chudnovsky algorithm since they are memory-intensive.

The first one million digits of $\pi$ and $\tfrac{1}{\pi}$ are available from [Project Gutenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Gutenberg).[^o92][^o93] A former calculation record (December 2002) by [Yasumasa Kanada](wikipedia://en/561940) of [Tokyo University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_University) stood at 1.24 trillion digits, which were computed in September 2002 on a 64-node [Hitachi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitachi,_Ltd.) [supercomputer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercomputer) with 1 terabyte of main memory, which carries out 2 trillion operations per second, nearly twice as many as the computer used for the previous record (206 billion digits). The following Machin-like formulae were used for this:

$\frac{\pi}{4} = 12 \arctan\frac{1}{49} + 32 \arctan\frac{1}{57} - 5 \arctan\frac{1}{239} + 12 \arctan\frac{1}{110443}$ ([Kikuo Takano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kikuo_Takano) (1982))

$\frac{\pi}{4} = 44 \arctan\frac{1}{57} + 7 \arctan\frac{1}{239} - 12 \arctan\frac{1}{682} + 24 \arctan\frac{1}{12943}$ ([F. C. M. Størmer](wikipedia://en/1906523) (1896)).

These approximations have so many digits that they are no longer of any practical use, except for testing new supercomputers.[^o94] Properties like the potential [normality](wikipedia://en/204277) of [$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601) will always depend on the infinite string of digits on the end, not on any finite computation.

### Summing a circle's area {#Summing_a_circle's_area}

*Image: Pi monte carlo en.gif.* Numerical approximation of $\pi$: as points are randomly scattered inside the unit square, some fall within the unit circle. The fraction of points inside the circle approaches $\pi/4$ as points are added.

Pi can be obtained from a circle if its radius and area are known using the relationship:

$$A = \pi r^2.$$

If a circle with radius $r$ is drawn with its center at the point $(0, 0)$, any point whose distance from the origin is less than $r$ will fall inside the circle. The [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) gives the distance from any point $(x, y)$ to the center:

$$d=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.$$

Mathematical "graph paper" is formed by imagining a 1×1 square centered around each cell $(x, y)$, where $x$ and $y$ are [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) between −$r$ and $r$. Squares whose center resides inside or exactly on the border of the circle can then be counted by testing whether, for each cell $(x, y)$,

$$\sqrt{x^2+y^2} \le r.$$

The total number of cells satisfying that condition thus approximates the area of the circle, which then can be used to calculate an approximation of $\pi$. Closer approximations can be produced by using larger values of $r$.

Mathematically, this formula can be written:

$$\pi = \lim_{r \to \infty} \frac{1}{r^2} \sum_{x=-r}^{r} \; \sum_{y=-r}^{r} \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \sqrt{x^2+y^2} \le r \\ 0 & \text{if } \sqrt{x^2+y^2} > r. \end{cases}$$

In other words, begin by choosing a value for $r$. Consider all cells ($x$, $y$) in which both $x$ and $y$ are integers between −$r$ and $r$. Starting at 0, add 1 for each cell whose distance to the origin $(0, 0)$ is less than or equal to $r$. When finished, divide the sum, representing the area of a circle of radius $r$, by $r$<sup>2</sup> to find the approximation of $\pi$. For example, if $r$ is 5, then the cells considered are:

### Continued fractions {#Continued_fractions}

Besides its simple [continued fraction](wikipedia://en/739199) representation \[3; 7, 15, 1, 292, 1, 1, ...\], which displays no discernible pattern, $\pi$ has many [generalized continued fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_continued_fraction) representations generated by a simple rule, including these two.

$$\pi= {3 + \cfrac{1^2}{6 + \cfrac{3^2}{6 + \cfrac{5^2}{6 + \ddots\,}}}}$$

$$\pi = \cfrac{4}{1 + \cfrac{1^2}{3 + \cfrac{2^2}{5 + \cfrac{3^2}{7 + \cfrac{4^2}{9 + \ddots}}}}} = 3 + \cfrac{1^2}{5 + \cfrac{4^2}{7 + \cfrac{3^2}{9 + \cfrac{6^2}{11 + \cfrac{5^2}{13 + \ddots}}}}}$$

The remainder of the [Madhava–Leibniz series](wikipedia://en/1301559) can be expressed as generalized continued fraction as follows.[^o111]

$$\pi = 4\sum_{n=1}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{2n-1}+\cfrac{2(-1)^m}{2m + \cfrac{1^2}{2m + \cfrac{2^2}{2m + \cfrac{3^2}{2m + \ddots}}}} \qquad (m=1,2,3,\ldots)$$

Note that Madhava's correction term is

$$\frac{2}{2m + \frac{1^2}{2m + \frac{2^2}{2m}}} = 4\frac{m^2+1}{4m^3+5m}.$$

The well-known values $\tfrac{22}{7}$ and $\tfrac{355}{113}$ are respectively the second and fourth continued fraction approximations to π.[^o112]

### Trigonometry {#Trigonometry}

#### Gregory–Leibniz series {#Gregory–Leibniz_series}

The [Gregory–Leibniz series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz_formula_for_pi)

$$\pi = 4\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \cfrac {(-1)^n}{2n+1} = 4\left( \frac{1}{1} - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{7} +- \cdots\right)$$

is the power series for [arctan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctan)(x) specialized to $x$ = 1. It converges too slowly to be of practical interest. However, the power series converges much faster for smaller values of $x$, which leads to formulae where $\pi$ arises as the sum of small angles with rational tangents, known as [Machin-like formulae](wikipedia://en/2724287).

#### Arctangent {#Arctangent}

Knowing that 4 arctan 1 = $\pi$, the formula can be simplified to get:

$$\begin{aligned} \pi &= 2\left( 1 + \cfrac{1}{3} + \cfrac{1\cdot2}{3\cdot5} + \cfrac{1\cdot2\cdot3}{3\cdot5\cdot7} + \cfrac{1\cdot2\cdot3\cdot4}{3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot9} + \cfrac{1\cdot2\cdot3\cdot4\cdot5}{3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot9\cdot11} + \cdots\right) \\ &= 2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \cfrac {n!}{(2n + 1)!!} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \cfrac {2^{n+1} n!^2} {(2n + 1)!} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \cfrac {2^{n+1}} {\binom {2n} n (2n + 1)} \\ &= 2 + \frac{2}{3} + \frac{4}{15} + \frac{4}{35} + \frac{16}{315} + \frac{16}{693} + \frac{32}{3003} + \frac{32}{6435} + \frac{256}{109395} + \frac{256}{230945} + \cdots \end{aligned}$$

with a convergence such that each additional 10 terms yields at least three more digits.

$$\pi=2+\frac{1}{3}\left(2+\frac{2}{5}\left(2+\frac{3}{7}\left(2+\cdots\right)\right)\right)$$

This series is the basis for a decimal [spigot algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spigot_algorithm) by Rabinowitz and Wagon.[^o116]

Another formula for $\pi$ involving arctangent function is given by

$$\frac{\pi}{2^{k+1}}=\arctan \frac{\sqrt{2-a_{k-1}}}{a_k}, \qquad\qquad k\geq 2,$$

where $a_k=\sqrt{2+a_{k-1}}$ such that $a_1=\sqrt{2}$. Approximations can be made by using, for example, the rapidly convergent [Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler) formula[^o117]

$$\arctan(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{2^{2n} (n!)^2}{(2n + 1)!} \; \frac{x^{2n + 1}}{(1 + x^2)^{n + 1}}.$$

Alternatively, the following simple expansion series of the arctangent function can be used

$$\arctan(x)=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\frac{1}{2n-1}\frac{{{a}_{n}}\left(x\right)}{a_{n}^{2}\left(x\right)+b_{n}^{2}\left(x\right)}},$$

where

$$\begin{aligned} & a_1(x)=2/x,\\ & b_1(x)=1,\\ & a_n(x)=a_{n-1}(x)\,\left(1-4/x^2\right)+4b_{n-1}(x)/x,\\ & b_n(x)=b_{n-1}(x)\,\left(1-4/x^2\right)-4a_{n-1}(x)/x, \end{aligned}$$

to approximate $\pi$ with even more rapid convergence. Convergence in this arctangent formula for $\pi$ improves as integer $k$ increases.

The constant $\pi$ can also be expressed by infinite sum of arctangent functions as

$$\frac{\pi}{2} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \arctan\frac{1}{F_{2n+1}} = \arctan\frac{1}{1} + \arctan\frac{1}{2} + \arctan\frac{1}{5} + \arctan\frac{1}{13} + \cdots$$

and

$$\frac{\pi}{4}=\sum_{k \geq 2} \arctan \frac{\sqrt{2-a_{k-1}}}{a_k},$$

where $F_n$ is the *n*-th [Fibonacci number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number). However, these two formulae for $\pi$ are much slower in convergence because of set of arctangent functions that are involved in computation.

#### Arcsine {#Arcsine}

Observing an equilateral triangle and noting that

$$\sin\left (\frac{\pi}{6}\right )=\frac{1}{2}$$

yields

$$\begin{aligned} \pi &= 6 \sin^{-1} \left( \frac{1}{2} \right) = 6 \left( \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2\cdot 3\cdot 2^3} + \frac{1\cdot 3}{2\cdot 4\cdot 5\cdot 2^5} + \frac{1\cdot 3\cdot 5}{2\cdot 4\cdot 6\cdot 7\cdot 2^7} + \cdots\! \right) \\ &= \frac {3} {16^0 \cdot 1} + \frac {6} {16^1 \cdot 3} + \frac {18} {16^2 \cdot 5} + \frac {60} {16^3 \cdot 7} + \cdots\! = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac {3 \cdot \binom {2n} n} {16^n (2n+1)} \\ &= 3 + \frac{1}{8} + \frac{9}{640} + \frac{15}{7168} + \frac{35}{98304} + \frac{189}{2883584} + \frac{693}{54525952} + \frac{429}{167772160} + \cdots \end{aligned}$$

with a convergence such that each additional five terms yields at least three more digits.

## Digit extraction methods {#Digit_extraction_methods}

The [Bailey–Borwein–Plouffe formula](wikipedia://en/22684956) (BBP) for calculating $\pi$ was discovered in 1995 by Simon Plouffe. Using a [spigot algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spigot_algorithm), the formula can compute any particular [base 16](wikipedia://en/13263) digit of $\pi$—returning the hexadecimal value of the digit—without computing the intervening digits.

$$\pi=\sum_{n=0}^\infty \left(\frac{4}{8n+1}-\frac{2}{8n+4}-\frac{1}{8n+5}-\frac{1}{8n+6}\right)\left(\frac{1}{16}\right)^n$$

In 1996, Plouffe derived an algorithm to extract the $n$th decimal digit of $\pi$ (using base 10 math to extract a base 10 digit), and which can do so with an improved speed of $O(n^{3}(\log n)^{3})$ time. The algorithm does not require memory for storage of a full n-digit result, so the one-millionth digit of $\pi$ could in principle be computed using a pocket calculator.[^o121] (However, it would be quite tedious and impractical to do so.)

$$\pi+3=\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{n2^nn!^2}{(2n)!}$$

The calculation speed of Plouffe's formula was improved to $O(n^{2})$ by [Fabrice Bellard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabrice_Bellard), who derived an alternative formula (albeit only in base 2 math) for computing $\pi$.[^o122]

$$\pi=\frac{1}{2^6}\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n}{2^{10n}} \left (-\frac{2^5}{4n+1}-\frac{1}{4n+3}+\frac{2^8}{10n+1}-\frac{2^6}{10n+3}-\frac{2^2}{10n+5}-\frac{2^2}{10n+7}+\frac{1}{10n+9}\right )$$

## Efficient methods {#Efficient_methods}

Many other expressions for $\pi$ were developed and published by Indian mathematician [Srinivasa Ramanujan](wikipedia://en/47717). He worked with mathematician [Godfrey Harold Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Godfrey_Harold_Hardy) in England for a number of years.

Extremely long decimal expansions of $\pi$ are typically computed with the [Gauss–Legendre algorithm](wikipedia://en/12916) and [Borwein's algorithm](wikipedia://en/233353); the [Salamin–Brent algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salamin–Brent_algorithm), which was invented in 1976, has also been used.

In 1997, [David H. Bailey](wikipedia://en/184462), [Peter Borwein](wikipedia://en/1339736) and [Simon Plouffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Plouffe) published a paper (Bailey, 1997) on [a new formula](wikipedia://en/22684956) for $\pi$ as an [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series):

$$\pi = \sum_{k = 0}^\infty \frac{1}{16^k} \left( \frac{4}{8k + 1} - \frac{2}{8k + 4} - \frac{1}{8k + 5} - \frac{1}{8k + 6}\right).$$

This formula permits one to fairly readily compute the *k*th [binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_numeral_system) or [hexadecimal](wikipedia://en/13263) digit of $\pi$, without having to compute the preceding *k* − 1 digits. Bailey's website[^o124] contains the derivation as well as implementations in various [programming languages](wikipedia://en/23015). The [PiHex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PiHex) project computed 64 bits around the [quadrillionth](wikipedia://en/682403) bit of $\pi$ (which turns out to be 0).

[Fabrice Bellard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabrice_Bellard) further improved on BBP with [his formula](wikipedia://en/5957331):[^o125]

$$\pi = \frac{1}{2^6} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{{(-1)}^n}{2^{10n}} \left( - \frac{2^5}{4n+1} - \frac{1}{4n+3} + \frac{2^8}{10n+1} - \frac{2^6}{10n+3} - \frac{2^2}{10n+5} - \frac{2^2}{10n+7} + \frac{1}{10n+9} \right)$$

Other formulae that have been used to compute estimates of $\pi$ include:

$$\frac{\pi}{2}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{k!}{(2k+1)!!}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{2^k k!^2}{(2k+1)!} =1+\frac{1}{3}\left(1+\frac{2}{5}\left(1+\frac{3}{7}\left(1+\cdots\right)\right)\right)$$

[Newton](wikipedia://en/14627).

$$\frac{1}{\pi} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{9801} \sum^\infty_{k=0} \frac{(4k)!(1103+26390k)}{(k!)^4 396^{4k}}$$

[Srinivasa Ramanujan](wikipedia://en/47717).

This converges extraordinarily rapidly. Ramanujan's work is the basis for the fastest algorithms used, as of the turn of the millennium, to calculate $\pi$.

In 1988, [David Chudnovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Chudnovsky_%28mathematician%29) and [Gregory Chudnovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_Chudnovsky) found an even faster-converging series (the [Chudnovsky algorithm](wikipedia://en/15893520)):

$$\frac{1}{\pi} = \frac{1}{426880\sqrt{10005}} \sum^\infty_{k=0} \frac{(6k)! (13591409 + 545140134k)}{(3k)!(k!)^3 (-640320)^{3k}}.$$

The speed of various algorithms for computing pi to n correct digits is shown below in descending order of asymptotic complexity. M(n) is the complexity of the multiplication algorithm employed.

| Algorithm | Year | Time complexity or Speed |
|---|---|---|
| [Gauss–Legendre algorithm](wikipedia://en/12916) | 1975 | $O(M(n)\log(n))$[^o91] |
| [Chudnovsky algorithm](wikipedia://en/15893520) | 1988 | $O(n \log(n)^3)$[^o53] |
| [Binary splitting](wikipedia://en/4226251) of the arctan series in [Machin's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machin's_formula) |  | $O(M(n) (\log n)^2)$[^o91] |

## Projects {#Projects}

### Pi Hex {#Pi_Hex}

[Pi Hex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PiHex) was a project to compute three specific binary digits of $\pi$ using a distributed network of several hundred computers. In 2000, after two years, the project finished computing the five trillionth (5\*10<sup>12</sup>), the forty trillionth (40\*10<sup>12</sup>), and the quadrillionth (10<sup>15</sup>) bits. All three of them turned out to be 0 in base 2.

## Software for calculating $\pi$ {#Software_for_calculating}

Over the years, several programs have been written for calculating $\pi$ to [many digits](wikipedia://en/600892) on [personal computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_computer).

### General purpose {#General_purpose}

Most [computer algebra systems](wikipedia://en/54427) can calculate $\pi$ and other common [mathematical constants](wikipedia://en/48779551) to any desired precision.

Functions for calculating $\pi$ are also included in many general [libraries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_%28computing%29) for [arbitrary-precision arithmetic](wikipedia://en/600892), for instance [Class Library for Numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_Library_for_Numbers), [MPFR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MPFR) and [SymPy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SymPy).

### Special purpose {#Special_purpose}

Programs designed for calculating $\pi$ may have better performance than general-purpose mathematical software. They typically implement [checkpointing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Checkpointing) and efficient [disk swapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paging) to facilitate extremely long-running and memory-expensive computations.

- **TachusPi** by Fabrice Bellard[^o136] is the program used by himself to compute world record number of digits of pi in 2009.
- **[$y$-cruncher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y-cruncher)** by Alexander Yee[^o53] is the program which every world record holder since Shigeru Kondo in 2010 has used to compute world record numbers of digits. $y$-cruncher can also be used to calculate other constants and holds world records for several of them.
- **PiFast** by Xavier Gourdon was the fastest program for [Microsoft Windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Windows) in 2003. According to its author, it can compute one million digits in 3.5 seconds on a 2.4 GHz [Pentium 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentium_4).[^o137] PiFast can also compute other irrational numbers like $e$ and $\sqrt{2}$. It can also work at lesser efficiency with very little memory (down to a few tens of megabytes to compute well over a billion (10<sup>9</sup>) digits). This tool is a popular benchmark in the [overclocking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overclocking) community. PiFast 4.4 is available from [Stu's Pi page](https://web.archive.org/web/20090220154930/http://members.shaw.ca/francislyster/pi/pi.html). PiFast 4.3 is available from Gourdon's page.
- **QuickPi** by Steve Pagliarulo for Windows is faster than PiFast for runs of under 400 million digits. Version 4.5 is available on Stu's Pi Page below. Like PiFast, QuickPi can also compute other irrational numbers like $e$, $\sqrt{2}$, and $\sqrt{3}$. The software may be obtained from the Pi-Hacks Yahoo! forum, or from [Stu's Pi page](https://web.archive.org/web/20090220154930/http://members.shaw.ca/francislyster/pi/pi.html).
- **[Super PI](wikipedia://en/3183941)** by Kanada Laboratory[^o138] in the University of Tokyo is the program for Microsoft Windows for runs from 16,000 to 33,550,000 digits. It can compute one million digits in 40 minutes, two million digits in 90 minutes and four million digits in 220 minutes on a Pentium 90 MHz. Super PI version 1.9 is available from [Super PI 1.9 page](https://www.filecluster.com/Super-PI.html).
