# Logical hexagon

*Image: Logical-hexagon.png.* The logical hexagon extends the [square of opposition](wikipedia://en/246976) to six statements.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Logical hexagon"}

In [philosophical logic](wikipedia://en/304939), the **logical hexagon** (also called the **hexagon of opposition**) is a [conceptual model](wikipedia://en/2381958) of the relationships between the [truth values](wikipedia://en/161711) of six [statements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statement_%28logic%29). It is an extension of [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308)'s [square of opposition](wikipedia://en/246976). It was discovered independently by both [Augustin Sesmat](wikipedia://en/39796251) and [Robert Blanché](wikipedia://en/27776887).[^o2]

:::

This extension consists in introducing two statements **U** and **Y**. Whereas **U** is the [disjunction](wikipedia://en/7962) of **A** and **E**, **Y** is the [conjunction](wikipedia://en/18152) of the two traditional particulars **I** and **O**.

## Summary of relationships {#Summary_of_relationships}

The traditional square of opposition demonstrates two sets of contradictories **A** and **O**, and **E** and **I** (i.e. they cannot both be true and cannot both be false), two contraries **A** and **E** (i.e. they can both be false, but cannot both be true), and two subcontraries **I** and **O** (i.e. they can both be true, but cannot both be false) according to Aristotle’s definitions. However, the logical hexagon provides that **U** and **Y** are also contradictory.

## Interpretations {#Interpretations}

The logical hexagon may be interpreted in various ways, including as a model of [traditional logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_logic), [quantifications](wikipedia://en/43507260), [modal logic](wikipedia://en/333365), [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), or [paraconsistent logic](wikipedia://en/421085).

For instance, the statement A may be interpreted as "Whatever x may be, if x is a man, then x is white."

(x)(M(x) → W(x))

The statement E may be interpreted as "Whatever x may be, if x is a man, then x is non-white."

(x)(M(x) → ~W(x))

The statement I may be interpreted as "There exists at least one x that is both a man and white."

(∃x)(M(x) & W(x))

The statement O may be interpreted as "There exists at least one x that is both a man and non-white."

(∃x)(M(x) & ~W(x))

The statement Y may be interpreted as "There exists at least one x that is both a man and white and there exists at least one x that is both a man and non-white."

(∃x)(M(x) & W(x)) & (∃x)(M(x) & ~W(x))

The statement U may be interpreted as "One of two things, either whatever x may be, if x is a man, then x is white or whatever x may be, if x is a man, then x is non-white."

(x)(M(x) → W(x)) w (x)(M(x) → ~W(x))

### Modal logic {#Modal_logic}

The logical hexagon may be interpreted as a model of modal logic such that

- **A** is interpreted as [necessity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_condition)
- **E** is interpreted as impossibility
- **I** is interpreted as [possibility](wikipedia://en/394008)
- **O** is interpreted as non-necessity
- **U** is interpreted as non-contingency
- **Y** is interpreted as [contingency](wikipedia://en/17753389)

## Further extension {#Further_extension}

It has been proven that both the square and the hexagon, followed by a “[logical cube](wikipedia://en/37006986)”, belong to a regular series of n-dimensional objects called “logical bi-simplexes of dimension n.” The pattern also goes even beyond this.[^o7]
