# Causality conditions

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Causality conditions"}

**Causality conditions** are classifications of [Lorentzian manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentzian_manifold) according to the types of [causal structures](wikipedia://en/14246049) they admit. In the study of [spacetimes](wikipedia://en/28758), there exists a hierarchy of causality conditions which are important in proving mathematical theorems about the global structure of such manifolds. These conditions were collected during the late 1970s.[^o2]

:::

The weaker the causality condition on a spacetime, the more *unphysical* the spacetime is. Spacetimes with [closed timelike curves](wikipedia://en/287091), for example, present severe interpretational difficulties. See the [grandfather paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grandfather_paradox).

It is reasonable to believe that any physical spacetime will satisfy the strongest causality condition: [global hyperbolicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_hyperbolicity). For such spacetimes the equations in [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) can be posed as an [initial value problem](wikipedia://en/498383) on a [Cauchy surface](wikipedia://en/2534823).

## The hierarchy {#The_hierarchy}

There is a hierarchy of causality conditions, each one of which is strictly stronger than the previous. This is sometimes called the **causal ladder**. The conditions, from weakest to strongest, are:

- Non-totally vicious
- Chronological
- Causal
- Distinguishing
- Strongly causal
- Stably causal
- Causally continuous
- Causally simple
- [Globally hyperbolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globally_hyperbolic)

Given are the definitions of these causality conditions for a Lorentzian manifold $(M,g)$. Where two or more are given they are equivalent.

**Notation**:

- $p \ll q$ denotes the [chronological relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronological_relation).
- $p \prec q$ denotes the [causal relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causal_relation).

(See [causal structure](wikipedia://en/14246049#Causal_relations) for definitions of $\,I^+(x)$, $\,I^-(x)$ and $\,J^+(x)$, $\,J^-(x)$.)

## Non-totally vicious {#Non-totally_vicious}

- For some points $p \in M$ we have $p \not\ll p$.

## Chronological {#Chronological}

- There are no closed chronological (timelike) curves.
- The [chronological relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronological_relation) is [irreflexive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreflexive): $p \not\ll p$ for all $p \in M$.

## Causal {#Causal}

- There are no closed causal (non-spacelike) curves.
- If both $p \prec q$ and $q \prec p$ then $p = q$

::: {#o8 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Past and future distinguishing"}

## Distinguishing {#Distinguishing}

### Past-distinguishing {#Past-distinguishing}

- Two points $p, q \in M$ which share the same chronological past are the same point:

$$I^-(p) = I^-(q) \implies p = q$$

- Equivalently, for any neighborhood $U$ of $p \in M$ there exists a neighborhood $V \subset U, p \in V$ such that no past-directed non-spacelike curve from $p$ intersects $V$ more than once.

### Future-distinguishing {#Future-distinguishing}

- Two points $p, q \in M$ which share the same chronological future are the same point:

$$I^+(p) = I^+(q) \implies p = q$$

- Equivalently, for any neighborhood $U$ of $p \in M$ there exists a neighborhood $V \subset U, p \in V$ such that no future-directed non-spacelike curve from $p$ intersects $V$ more than once.

A [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) is called **distinguishing** when it is both future and past distinguishing, that is, when each point is determined (distinguished) by its [chronological future](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronological_future) and also by its [chronological past](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronological_past).

:::

::: {#o12 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Stably causal"}

## Strongly causal {#Strongly_causal}

- For every neighborhood $U$ of $p \in M$ there exists a neighborhood $V \subset U, p \in V$ through which no timelike curve passes more than once.
- For every neighborhood $U$ of $p \in M$ there exists a neighborhood $V \subset U, p \in V$ that is causally convex in $M$ (and thus in $U$), that is, for each $q, q'$ in $V$ any causal curve in $M$ from $q$ to $q'$ must be entirely contained in $U$.
- The [Alexandrov topology](wikipedia://en/542198) (generated by the chronological futures and pasts of all the points) satisfies any of the following equivalent conditions:
  - (a) the Alexandrov topology is equal to the topology of the manifold.
  - (b) the Alexandrov topology is Hausdorff.

:::

::: {#o14 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Stable causality"}

## Stably causal {#Stably_causal}

For each of the weaker causality conditions defined above, there are some manifolds satisfying the condition which can be made to violate it by arbitrarily small [perturbations](wikipedia://en/102140) of the metric. A spacetime $(M,g)$ is called stably causal if it satisfies any of the following equivalent conditions:

1. Causality is ($C^0$) stable: there exists a causal Lorentzian metric $g'$ on $M$ with the cones strictly wider than the original ones of $g$ (that is, all the causal vectors for $g$ are timelike vectors for $g'$).
1. It admits a time function: there exists a continuous function $t$ which is strictly increasing on causal (timelike or lightlike) curves.
1. It admits a temporal function: there exists a [smooth function](wikipedia://en/1408000) $\tau$ whose gradient $\nabla \tau$ is timelike and past directed (in particular, $\tau$ is then a time function).

Hawking (1969)[^o15] proved that condition 1 implies 2, and Bernal and Sánchez (2005)[^o16] proved that 2 implies 3, while it is not difficult to check that condition 3 implies 1. See Sánchez[^o17] for a detailed account, as well as its relation with the folk problems of [smoothability](wikipedia://en/34265825#The_"folk"_problem)

:::

## Causally continuous {#Causally_continuous}

A spacetime is called causally continuous when it satisfies: (a) it is distinguishing and (b) the chronological future $I^+(p)$ and past $I^-(p)$ varies continuously with the point $p$.

There are several ways to express formally the meaning of this continuity. One of them starts by regarding $I^+$ and $I^-$ as set-valued functions from the manifold $M$ into the set $\mathbb{P}(M)$ of parts of $M$ (i.e., the points of $\mathbb{P}(M)$ are the subsets of $M$). Notice that $M$ is endowed with a topology, and a topology on $\mathbb{P}(M)$ can be defined as follows. For any compact $K \subset M$, the subsets of $M$ not intersecting $K$ form a subset of $\mathbb{P}(M)$, which is defined as open, then, these open sets are a base for the required topology. Thus, the condition (b) means that the maps $I^\pm: M \rightarrow \mathbb{P}(M)$ are continuous as maps on topological spaces.[^o19]

## Causally simple {#Causally_simple}

A spacetime is called causally simple when it satisfies: (a) it is causal and (b) $J^+(p)$ and $J^-(p)$ are closed for every point.

Hawking and Ellis (1973), Beem, Ehrlich and Easley (1996)[^o21] and others traditionally required the condition of strong causality. However, following a result by Bernal and Sánchez (2007),[^o22] the simpler condition of causality is now used in the definition.

## Globally hyperbolic {#Globally_hyperbolic}

- $\,M$ is [causal](wikipedia://en/14346663#causal) and every set $J^+(x) \cap J^-(y)$ (for points $x,y \in M$) is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042).

[Robert Geroch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Geroch) showed[^o24] that a spacetime is globally hyperbolic [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) there exists a Cauchy surface for $M$. This means that:

- $M$ is [topologically equivalent](wikipedia://en/13660) to $\mathbb{R} \times\!\, S$ for some [Cauchy surface](wikipedia://en/2534823) $S.$ (Here $\mathbb{R}$ denotes the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line)).

Moreover, it also admits the global orthogonal splitting obtained by Bernal and Sánchez (2005), thus showing that space and time can be globally distinguished (in a highly non-unique way).

## Properties of the hierarchy {#Properties_of_the_hierarchy}

It is worth pointing out that all the levels of the ladder are preserved when narrowing the cones (that is, when the original metric $g$ is replaced by a metric $g'$ whose causal vectors are included in the causal vectors for $g$), except causal continuity and causal simplicity, as shown by García-Parrado and Sánchez with an explicit counterexample.[^o26]
