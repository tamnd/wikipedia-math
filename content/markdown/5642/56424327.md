# Paul Chernoff

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Paul Chernoff"}

**Paul Robert Chernoff** (21 June 1942, [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia) – 17 January 2017)[^o3] was an American mathematician, specializing in functional analysis and the mathematical foundations of quantum mechanics.[^o4] He is known for Chernoff's Theorem, a mathematical result in the Feynman path integral formulation of quantum mechanics.[^o5] He was also the author of [limericks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limerick_%28poetry%29).[^o6][^o7]

:::

## Education and career {#Education_and_career}

Chernoff graduated from [Central High School in Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_High_School_%28Philadelphia%29). He matriculated at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University), where he received bachelor's degree, *[summa cum laude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_cum_laude)*, in 1963, master's degree in 1965, and Ph.D. in 1968 under [George Mackey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Mackey) with thesis *Semigroup Product Formulas and Addition of Unbounded Operators*.

At the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley), he became in 1969 a lecturer, in 1971 an assistant professor, and in 1980 a full professor. U. C. Berkeley awarded him multiple Distinguished Teaching Awards and the Lili Fabilli and Eric Hoffer Essay Prize.[^o4] In 1986 he was a visiting professor at the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania).

Chernoff was elected in 1984 a Fellow of the [American Association for the Advancement of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Science)[^o10] and in 2012 a Fellow of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822).

He gave in 1981 a simplified proof of the [Groenewold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbrand_J._Groenewold)-[Van Hove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Léon_Van_Hove) theorem,[^o11][^o12][^o13] which is a [no-go theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No-go_theorem) that relates classical mechanics to quantum mechanics.[^o4]
