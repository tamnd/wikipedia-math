# Khmaladze transformation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Khmaladze transformation"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **Khmaladze transformation** is a mathematical tool used in constructing convenient [goodness of fit](wikipedia://en/2474821) tests for hypothetical [distribution functions](wikipedia://en/5793). More precisely, suppose $X_1,\ldots, X_n$ are [i.i.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I.i.d.), possibly multi-dimensional, random observations generated from an unknown [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543). A classical problem in statistics is to decide how well a given hypothetical distribution function $F$, or a given hypothetical parametric family of distribution functions $\{ F_\theta : \theta \in \Theta \}$, fits the set of observations. The Khmaladze transformation allows us to construct goodness of fit tests with desirable properties. It is named after [Estate V. Khmaladze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estate_V._Khmaladze).

:::

Consider the sequence of [empirical distribution functions](wikipedia://en/2690455) $F_n$ based on a sequence of i.i.d random variables, $X_1,\ldots, X_n$, as *n* increases. Suppose $F$ is the hypothetical [distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) of each $X_i$. To test whether the choice of $F$ is correct or not, statisticians use the normalized difference,

$$v_n(x)=\sqrt{n} [F_n(x)-F(x)].$$

This $v_n$, as a [random process](wikipedia://en/47895) in $x$, is called the [empirical process](wikipedia://en/3690433). Various [functionals](wikipedia://en/1374948) of $v_n$ are used as test statistics. The change of the variable $v_n(x)=u_n(t)$, $t=F(x)$ transforms to the so-called uniform empirical process $u_n$. The latter is an empirical processes based on independent random variables $U_i=F(X_i)$, which are [uniformly distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29) on $[0,1]$ if the $X_i$s do indeed have distribution function $F$.

This fact was discovered and first utilized by Kolmogorov (1933), Wald and Wolfowitz (1936) and Smirnov (1937) and, especially after Doob (1949) and Anderson and Darling (1952),[^o2] it led to the standard rule to choose test statistics based on $v_n$. That is, test statistics $\psi(v_n,F)$ are defined (which possibly depend on the $F$ being tested) in such a way that there exists another statistic $\varphi(u_n)$ derived from the uniform empirical process, such that $\psi(v_n,F)=\varphi(u_n)$. Examples are

$$\sup_x|v_n(x)|=\sup_t|u_n(t)|,\quad \sup_x\frac{|v_n(x)|}{a(F(x))}=\sup_t\frac {|u_n(t)|}{a(t)}$$

and

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty v_n^2(x) \, dF(x)=\int_0^1 u_n^2(t)\,dt.$$

For all such functionals, their [null distribution](wikipedia://en/8919561) (under the hypothetical $F$) does not depend on $F$, and can be calculated once and then used to test any $F$.

However, it is only rarely that one needs to test a simple hypothesis, when a fixed $F$ as a hypothesis is given. Much more often, one needs to verify parametric hypotheses where the hypothetical $F=F_{\theta_n}$, depends on some parameters $\theta_n$, which the hypothesis does not specify and which have to be estimated from the sample $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ itself.

Although the estimators $\hat \theta_n$, most commonly converge to true value of $\theta$, it was discovered that the parametric,[^o3][^o4] or estimated, empirical process

$$\hat v_n(x)=\sqrt{n} [F_n(x)-F_{\hat\theta_n}(x)]$$

differs significantly from $v_n$ and that the transformed process $\hat u_n(t)=\hat v_n(x)$, $t=F_{\hat\theta_n}(x)$ has a distribution for which the limit distribution, as $n\to\infty$, is dependent on the parametric form of $F_{\theta}$ and on the particular estimator $\hat\theta_n$ and, in general, within one [parametric family](wikipedia://en/19653337), on the value of $\theta$.

From mid-1950s to the late-1980s, much work was done to clarify the situation and understand the nature of the process $\hat v_n$.

In 1981,[^o5] and then 1987 and 1993,[^o6] Khmaladze suggested to replace the parametric empirical process $\hat v_n$ by its martingale part $w_n$ only.

$$\hat v_n(x)-K_n(x)=w_n(x)$$

where $K_n(x)$ is the compensator of $\hat v_n(x)$. Then the following properties of $w_n$ were established:

- Although the form of $K_n$, and therefore, of $w_n$, depends on $F_{\hat\theta_n}(x)$, as a function of both $x$ and $\theta_n$, the limit distribution of the time transformed process

$$\omega_n(t)=w_n(x), t=F_{\hat \theta_n}(x)$$

is that of standard [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436) on $[0,1]$, i.e., is again standard and independent of the choice of $F_{\hat\theta_n}$.

- The relationship between $\hat v_n$ and $w_n$ and between their limits, is one to one, so that the statistical inference based on $\hat v_n$ or on $w_n$ are equivalent, and in $w_n$, nothing is lost compared to $\hat v_n$.
- The construction of innovation martingale $w_n$ could be carried over to the case of vector-valued $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, giving rise to the definition of the so-called scanning martingales in $\mathbb R^d$.

For a long time the transformation was, although known, still not used. Later, the work of researchers like [Koenker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Koenker), [Stute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winfried_Stute), [Bai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jushan_Bai), [Koul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hira_L._Koul), Koening, and others made it popular in [econometrics](wikipedia://en/10390) and other fields of statistics.
