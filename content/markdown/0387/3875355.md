# Paris–Harrington theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Paris–Harrington theorem"}

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), the **Paris–Harrington theorem** states that a certain claim in [Ramsey theory](wikipedia://en/157175), namely the strengthened finite Ramsey theorem, which is expressible in [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic), is not provable in this system. That Ramsey-theoretic claim is, however, provable in slightly stronger systems.

:::

This result has been described by some (such as the editor of the *Handbook of Mathematical Logic* in the references below) as the first "natural" example of a true statement about the integers that could be stated in the language of arithmetic, but not proved in Peano arithmetic; it was already known that such statements existed by [Gödel's first incompleteness theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel's_first_incompleteness_theorem). Some authors, such as Rathjen[^o2]<sup>p. 4</sup> and [Kripke](wikipedia://en/48039)[^o3]<sup>p. 5</sup>, consider [Gentzen's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentzen's_theorem) an earlier example and work of [Reuben Goodstein](wikipedia://en/1586970) deserving some credit.

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Strengthened finite Ramsey theorem"}

## Strengthened finite Ramsey theorem {#Strengthened_finite_Ramsey_theorem}

The strengthened finite Ramsey theorem is a statement about colorings and [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) and states that:

For any positive integers *n*, *k*, *m*, such that *m ≥ n*, one can find *N* with the following property: if we color each of the *n*-element subsets of *S* = {1, 2, 3,..., *N*} with one of *k* colors, then we can find a subset *Y* of *S* with at least *m* elements, such that all *n*-element subsets of *Y* have the same color, and the number of elements of *Y* is at least the smallest element of *Y*.

Without the condition that the number of elements of *Y* is at least the smallest element of *Y*, this is a corollary of the [finite Ramsey theorem](wikipedia://en/184898) in $K_{\mathcal{P}_n(S)}$, with *N* given by:

$$\binom{N}{n} = |\mathcal{P}_n(S)| \ge R(\,\underbrace{ m, m, \ldots , m }_k\,).$$

Moreover, the strengthened finite Ramsey theorem can be deduced from the [infinite Ramsey theorem](wikipedia://en/184898#Infinite_graphs) in almost exactly the same way that the finite Ramsey theorem can be deduced from it, using a [compactness argument](wikipedia://en/152207) (see the article on [Ramsey's theorem](wikipedia://en/184898) for details). This proof can be carried out in [second-order arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3542454).

The Paris–Harrington theorem states that the strengthened finite Ramsey theorem is not provable in [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic).

:::

## Paris–Harrington theorem {#Paris–Harrington_theorem}

Roughly speaking, [Jeff Paris](wikipedia://en/11279983) and [Leo Harrington](wikipedia://en/5693354) (1977) showed that the strengthened finite Ramsey theorem is unprovable in [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic) by showing in Peano arithmetic that it implies the [consistency](wikipedia://en/75802) of Peano arithmetic itself. Assuming Peano arithmetic really is consistent, then by [Gödel's second incompleteness theorem](wikipedia://en/58863#Second_incompleteness_theorem), Peano arithmetic *cannot* prove its own consistency. This shows that Peano arithmetic cannot prove the strengthened finite Ramsey theorem.

The strengthened finite Ramsey theorem can be proven assuming [transfinite induction](wikipedia://en/30461) up to [$\varepsilon_0$](wikipedia://en/3981747) for a relevant class of formulas. Alternatively, it can be proven assuming the [reflection principle](wikipedia://en/3225574), for the arithmetic theory, for [$\Sigma_1^0$-sentences](wikipedia://en/186475). The reflection principle also implies the consistency of Peano arithmetic. It is provable in [second-order arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3542454) (or the far stronger [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214)) and so is true in the standard model of Peano arithmetic, i.e. the natural numbers.

The smallest number *N* that satisfies the strengthened finite Ramsey theorem is then a [computable function](wikipedia://en/1139338) of *n*, *m*, *k*, but grows extremely fast. In particular it is not [primitive recursive](wikipedia://en/24829), but it also grows far faster than standard examples of non-primitive recursive functions such as the [Ackermann function](wikipedia://en/2925). It dominates every computable function provably total (see [partial function](wikipedia://en/23577)) in Peano arithmetic,[^o8] which includes functions such as the Ackermann function.
