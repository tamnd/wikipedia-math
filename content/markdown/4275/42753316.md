# Main theorem of elimination theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Main theorem of elimination theory"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), the **main theorem of elimination theory** states that every [projective scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_scheme) is [proper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_scheme). A version of this theorem predates the existence of [scheme theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_theory). It can be stated, proved, and applied in the following more classical setting. Let $k$ be a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), denote by $\mathbb{P}_k^n$ the $n$-dimensional [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) over $k$. The main theorem of elimination theory is the statement that for any $n$ and any [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) $V$ defined over $k$, the projection map $V \times \mathbb{P}_k^n \to V$ sends [Zariski-closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski-closed) subsets to Zariski-closed subsets.

:::

The main theorem of elimination theory is a corollary and a generalization of [Macaulay's](wikipedia://en/3592252) theory of [multivariate resultant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_resultant). The resultant of $n$ [homogeneous polynomials](wikipedia://en/1698977) in $n$ variables is the value of a polynomial function of the coefficients, which takes the value zero if and only if the polynomials have a common non-trivial zero over some field containing the coefficients.

This belongs to [elimination theory](wikipedia://en/245978), as computing the resultant amounts to *eliminating variables* between polynomial equations. In fact, given a [system of polynomial equations](wikipedia://en/27420015), which is homogeneous in some variables, the resultant *eliminates* these homogeneous variables by providing an equation in the other variables, which has, as solutions, the values of these other variables in the solutions of the original system.

## A simple motivating example {#A_simple_motivating_example}

The [affine plane](wikipedia://en/30764139) over a field $k$ is the [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887) $A_2=L_x\times L_y$ of two copies of $k$. Let

$$\pi\colon L_x\times L_y \to L_x$$

be the projection

$$(x,y)\mapsto \pi(x,y)=x.$$

This projection is not [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_map) for the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744) (nor for the usual topology if $k= \mathbb{R}$ or $k= \mathbb{C}$), because the image by $\pi$ of the [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) $H$ of equation $xy-1=0$ is $L_x\setminus \{0\},$ which is not closed, although $H$ is closed, being an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808).

If one extends $L_y$ to a projective line $P_y,$ the equation of the [projective completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_completion) of the hyperbola becomes

$$xy_1-y_0=0,$$

and contains

$$\overline\pi(0,(1,0))=0,$$

where $\overline\pi$ is the prolongation of $\pi$ to $L_x\times P_y.$

This is commonly expressed by saying the origin of the affine plane is the projection of the point of the hyperbola that is at infinity, in the direction of the $y$-axis.

More generally, the image by $\pi$ of every algebraic set in $L_x\times L_y$ is either a finite number of points, or $L_x$ with a finite number of points removed, while the image by $\overline\pi$ of any algebraic set in $L_x\times P_y$ is either a finite number of points or the whole line $L_y.$ It follows that the image by $\overline\pi$ of any algebraic set is an algebraic set, that is that $\overline\pi$ is a closed map for Zariski topology.

The main theorem of elimination theory is a wide generalization of this property.

## Classical formulation {#Classical_formulation}

For stating the theorem in terms of [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990), one has to consider a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $R[\mathbf x]=R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ over a commutative [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129) $R$, and a [homogeneous ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_ideal) $I$ generated by [homogeneous polynomials](wikipedia://en/1698977) $f_1,\ldots, f_k.$ (In the original proof by [Macaulay](wikipedia://en/3592252), $k$ was equal to $n$, and $R$ was a polynomial ring over the integers, whose indeterminates were all the coefficients of the$f_i\mathrm s.$)

Any [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) $\varphi$ from $R$ into a field $K$, defines a ring homomorphism $R[\mathbf x] \to K[\mathbf x]$ (also denoted $\varphi$), by applying $\varphi$ to the coefficients of the polynomials.

The theorem is: there is an ideal $\mathfrak r$ in $R$, uniquely determined by $I$, such that, for every ring homomorphism $\varphi$ from $R$ into a field $K$, the homogeneous polynomials $\varphi(f_1),\ldots, \varphi(f_k)$ have a nontrivial common zero (in an algebraic closure of $K$) if and only if $\varphi(\mathfrak r)=\{0\}.$

Moreover, $\mathfrak r =0$ if $k < n$, and $\mathfrak r$ is [principal](wikipedia://en/145126) if $k = n$. In this latter case, a generator of $\mathfrak r$ is called the [resultant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macaulay's_resultant) of $f_1,\ldots, f_k.$

## Hints for a proof and related results {#Hints_for_a_proof_and_related_results}

Using above notation, one has first to characterize the condition that $\varphi(f_1),\ldots, \varphi(f_k)$ do not have any non-trivial common zero. This is the case if the maximal homogeneous ideal $\mathfrak m = \langle x_1, \ldots, x_n\rangle$ is the only homogeneous prime ideal containing $\varphi(I)=\langle \varphi(f_1),\ldots, \varphi(f_k)\rangle.$ [Hilbert's Nullstellensatz](wikipedia://en/149215) asserts that this is the case if and only if $\varphi(I)$ contains a power of each $x_i,$ or, equivalently, that $\mathfrak m^d \subseteq \varphi(I)$ for some positive integer $d$.

For this study, [Macaulay](wikipedia://en/3592252) introduced a matrix that is now called *Macaulay matrix* in degree $d$. Its rows are indexed by the [monomials](wikipedia://en/357416) of degree $d$ in $x_1, \ldots, x_n,$ and its columns are the vectors of the coefficients on the [monomial basis](wikipedia://en/976666) of the polynomials of the form $m\varphi(f_i),$ where $m$ is a monomial of degree $d-\deg(f_i).$ One has $\mathfrak m^d \subseteq \varphi(I)$ if and only if the rank of the Macaulay matrix equals the number of its rows.

If $k < n$, the rank of the Macaulay matrix is lower than the number of its rows for every $d$, and, therefore, $\varphi(f_1),\ldots, \varphi(f_k)$ have always a non-trivial common zero.

Otherwise, let $d_i$ be the degree of $f_i,$ and suppose that the indices are chosen in order that $d_2\ge d_3 \ge\cdots\ge d_k\ge d_1.$ The degree

$$D= d_1+d_2+\cdots+d_n-n+1 = 1+\sum_{i=1}^n (d_i-1)$$

is called *Macaulay's degree* or *Macaulay's bound* because Macaulay's has proved that $\varphi(f_1),\ldots, \varphi(f_k)$ have a non-trivial common zero if and only if the rank of the Macaulay matrix in degree $D$ is lower than the number to its rows. In other words, the above $d$ may be chosen once for all as equal to $D$.

Therefore, the ideal $\mathfrak r,$ whose existence is asserted by the main theorem of elimination theory, is the zero ideal if $k < n$, and, otherwise, is generated by the maximal minors of the Macaulay matrix in degree $D$.

If $k = n$, Macaulay has also proved that $\mathfrak r$ is a [principal ideal](wikipedia://en/145126) (although Macaulay matrix in degree $D$ is not a square matrix when $k > 2$), which is generated by the [resultant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macaulay's_resultant) of $\varphi(f_1),\ldots, \varphi(f_n).$ This ideal is also [generically](wikipedia://en/12886758) a [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928), as it is prime if $R$ is the ring of [integer polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_polynomial) with the all coefficients of $\varphi(f_1),\ldots, \varphi(f_k)$ as indeterminates.

## Geometrical interpretation {#Geometrical_interpretation}

In the preceding formulation, the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $R[\mathbf x]=R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ defines a [morphism](wikipedia://en/7482797) of [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) (which are algebraic varieties if $R$ is finitely generated over a field)

$$\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_R = \operatorname{Proj}(R[\mathbf x]) \to \operatorname{Spec}(R).$$

The theorem asserts that the image of the Zariski-closed set $V(I)$ defined by $I$ is the closed set $V(r)$. Thus the morphism is closed.
