# Durbin–Wu–Hausman test

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Durbin–Wu–Hausman test"}

The **Durbin–Wu–Hausman test** (also called **Hausman specification test**) is a [statistical hypothesis test](wikipedia://en/30284) in [econometrics](wikipedia://en/10390) named after [James Durbin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Durbin), [De-Min Wu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De-Min_Wu), and [Jerry A. Hausman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_A._Hausman).[^o10][^o11][^o12][^o2] The test evaluates the [consistency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_consistency) of an estimator when compared to an alternative, less [efficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efficient_estimator) estimator which is already known to be consistent.[^o3] It helps one evaluate if a statistical model corresponds to the data.

:::

## Details {#Details}

Consider the linear model *y* = *Xb* + *e*, where *y* is the dependent variable and *X* is vector of [regressors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regressor), *b* is a vector of coefficients and *e* is the [error term](wikipedia://en/4229275). We have two estimators for *b*: *b*<sub>0</sub> and *b*<sub>1</sub>. Under the [null hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis), both of these estimators are [consistent](wikipedia://en/1497569), but *b*<sub>1</sub> is [efficient](wikipedia://en/27250866) (has the smallest asymptotic variance), at least in the class of estimators containing *b*<sub>0</sub>. Under the [alternative hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_hypothesis), *b*<sub>0</sub> is consistent, whereas *b*<sub>1</sub> isn't.

Then the **Wu–Hausman [statistic](wikipedia://en/26703)** is:[^o13]

$$H=(b_{1}-b_{0})'\big(\operatorname{Var}(b_{0})-\operatorname{Var}(b_{1})\big)^\dagger(b_{1}-b_{0}),$$

where <sup>†</sup> denotes the [Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore–Penrose_pseudoinverse). Under the null hypothesis, this statistic has asymptotically the [chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/113424) with the number of degrees of freedom equal to the rank of matrix Var(*b*<sub>0</sub>) − Var(*b*<sub>1</sub>).

If we reject the null hypothesis, it means that b<sub>1</sub> is inconsistent. This test can be used to check for the [endogeneity](wikipedia://en/1908618) of a variable (by comparing [instrumental variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_variable) (IV) estimates to [ordinary least squares](wikipedia://en/1651906) (OLS) estimates). It can also be used to check the validity of extra [instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_variable) by comparing IV estimates using a full set of instruments *Z* to IV estimates that use a proper subset of *Z*. Note that in order for the test to work in the latter case, we must be certain of the validity of the subset of *Z* and that subset must have enough instruments to identify the parameters of the equation.

Hausman also showed that the covariance between an efficient estimator and the difference of an efficient and inefficient estimator is zero.

## Derivation {#Derivation}

Assuming joint normality of the estimators.[^o12][^o13]

$$\sqrt{N} \begin{bmatrix} b_1 -b\\ b_0 -b\end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N} \left(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Var}(b_1) & \operatorname{Cov}(b_1,b_0) \\ \operatorname{Cov}(b_1,b_0) & \operatorname{Var}(b_0) \end{bmatrix}\right)$$

Consider the function : $q=b_0-b_1\Rightarrow \operatorname{plim}q=0$

By the [delta method](wikipedia://en/2826026)

$$\begin{aligned} & \sqrt{N}(q-0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N} \left(0, \begin{bmatrix}1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \operatorname{Var}(b_1) & \operatorname{Cov}(b_1,b_0) \\ \operatorname{Cov}(b_1,b_0) & \operatorname{Var}(b_{0}) \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\right) \\[6pt] & \operatorname{Var}(q)=\operatorname{Var}(b_{1})+\operatorname{Var}(b_{0})-2\operatorname{Cov}(b_1,b_0) \end{aligned}$$

Using Hausman's result that the covariance of an efficient estimator with its difference from an inefficient estimator is zero,

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Cov}(b_1,b_0) &=\operatorname{Cov}(b_1,b_1+q) \\ &=\operatorname{Cov}(b_1,b_1) + \operatorname{Cov}(b_1,q) \\ &=\operatorname{Var}(b_1) + 0 \\ \operatorname{Var}(q)&=\operatorname{Var}(b_0)+\operatorname{Var}(b_{1})-2\operatorname{Var}(b_{1}) \\ &=\operatorname{Var}(b_0)-\operatorname{Var}(b_1) \end{aligned}$$

The chi-squared test is based on the Wald criterion

$$H=\chi^2[K-1]=(b_1-b_0)'\big(\operatorname{Var}(b_0)-\operatorname{Var}(b_1)\big)^\dagger(b_1-b_0),$$

where <sup>†</sup> denotes the [Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore–Penrose_pseudoinverse) and *K* denotes the dimension of vector *b*.

## Panel data {#Panel_data}

The Hausman test can be used to differentiate between [fixed effects model](wikipedia://en/4434344) and [random effects model](wikipedia://en/6709643) in [panel analysis](wikipedia://en/2369609). In this case, Random effects (RE) is preferred under the null hypothesis due to higher efficiency, while under the alternative Fixed effects (FE) is at least as consistent and thus preferred.

::: {#o7 .table cue=table}

|  | H<sub>0</sub> is true | H<sub>1</sub> is true |
|---|---|---|
| *b*<sub>1</sub> (RE estimator) | Consistent Efficient | Inconsistent |
| *b*<sub>0</sub> (FE estimator) | Consistent Inefficient | Consistent |

:::
