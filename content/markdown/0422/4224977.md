# Automatic group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Automatic group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **automatic group** is a [finitely generated group](wikipedia://en/963042) equipped with several [finite-state automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite-state_automata). These automata represent the [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of the group. That is, they can tell whether a given word representation of a group element is in a "canonical form" and can tell whether two elements given in canonical words differ by a generator.[^o2]

:::

More precisely, let *G* be a group and *A* be a finite set of generators. Then an *automatic structure* of *G* with respect to *A* is a set of finite-state automata:[^o3]

- the *word-acceptor*, which accepts for every element of *G* at least one word in $A^\ast$ representing it;
- *multipliers*, one for each $a \in A \cup \{1\}$, which accept a pair (*w*<sub>1</sub>, *w*<sub>2</sub>), for words *w*<sub>*i*</sub> accepted by the word-acceptor, precisely when $w_1 a = w_2$ in *G*.

The property of being automatic does not depend on the set of generators.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Automatic groups have [word problem](wikipedia://en/33563) solvable in quadratic time. More strongly, a given word can actually be put into canonical form in quadratic time, based on which the word problem may be solved by testing whether the canonical forms of two words represent the same element (using the multiplier for $a = 1$).[^o7]

Automatic groups are characterized by the *fellow traveler property*.[^o8] Let $d(x,y)$ denote the distance between $x, y \in G$ in the Cayley graph of $G$. Then, *G* is automatic with respect to a word acceptor *L* if and only if there is a constant $C \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all words $u, v \in L$ which differ by at most one generator, the distance between the respective prefixes of *u* and *v* is bounded by *C*. In other words, $\forall u, v \in L, d(u,v) \leq 1 \Rightarrow \forall k \in \mathbb{N}, d(u_{|k},v_{|k}) \leq C$ where $w_{|k}$ for the k-th prefix of $w$ (or $w$ itself if $k > |w|$). This means that when reading the words synchronously, it is possible to keep track of the difference between both elements with a finite number of states (the neighborhood of the identity with diameter *C* in the Cayley graph).

:::

## Examples of automatic groups {#Examples_of_automatic_groups}

The automatic groups include:

- [Finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707). To see this take the regular language to be the set of all words in the finite group.
- [Euclidean groups](wikipedia://en/652164)
- All finitely generated [Coxeter groups](wikipedia://en/297004)[^o10]
- [Geometrically finite groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometrically_finite_group)

## Examples of non-automatic groups {#Examples_of_non-automatic_groups}

- [Baumslag–Solitar groups](wikipedia://en/11485892)
- Non-[Euclidean](wikipedia://en/652164) [nilpotent groups](wikipedia://en/144569)
- Not every [CAT(0) group](wikipedia://en/12942017) is biautomatic[^o12][^o13]

::: {#o15 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Biautomatic"}

## Biautomatic groups {#Biautomatic_groups}

A group is **biautomatic** if it has two multiplier automata, for left and right multiplication by elements of the generating set, respectively. A biautomatic group is clearly automatic.[^o16]

Examples include:

- [Hyperbolic groups](wikipedia://en/3172817).[^o17]
- Any [Artin group of finite type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin_group_of_finite_type), including [braid groups](wikipedia://en/550138).[^o17]
- All finitely generated [Coxeter groups](wikipedia://en/297004)[^o18]

:::

## Automatic structures {#Automatic_structures}

The idea of describing algebraic structures with finite-automata can be generalized from groups to other structures.[^o20] For instance, it generalizes naturally to [automatic semigroups](wikipedia://en/17655204).[^o21]
