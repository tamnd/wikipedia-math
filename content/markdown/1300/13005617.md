# Flat (geometry)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Flat"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **flat** is an [affine subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_subspace), i.e. a subset of an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) that is itself an affine space.[^o2] Particularly, in the case the parent space is [Euclidean](wikipedia://en/9697), a flat is a **Euclidean subspace** which inherits the notion of [distance](wikipedia://en/53932) from its parent space.

:::

In an [$n$-dimensional space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-dimensional_space), there are $k$-flats of every [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) $k$ from 0 to $n$; flats one dimension lower than the parent space, $(n\,-\,1)$-flats, are called *[hyperplanes](wikipedia://en/99862)*.

The flats in a [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852) (two-dimensional space) are [points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_%28mathematics%29), [lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_%28mathematics%29), and the plane itself; the flats in [three-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/3054853) are points, lines, planes, and the space itself. The definition of flat excludes non-straight [curves](wikipedia://en/89246) and non-planar [surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_%28geometry%29), which are [subspaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspace_%28mathematics%29) having different notions of distance: [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) and [geodesic length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_length), respectively.

Flats occur in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), as geometric realizations of solution sets of [systems of linear equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_of_linear_equations).

A flat is a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) and an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808), and is sometimes called a *linear manifold* or *linear variety* to distinguish it from other manifolds or varieties.

## Descriptions {#Descriptions}

### By equations {#By_equations}

A flat can be described by a [system of linear equations](wikipedia://en/113087). For example, a line in two-dimensional space can be described by a single linear equation involving $x$ and $y$:

$$3x + 5y = 8.$$

In three-dimensional space, a single linear equation involving $x$, $y$, and $z$ defines a plane, while a pair of linear equations can be used to describe a line. In general, a linear equation in $n$ variables describes a hyperplane, and a system of linear equations describes the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of those hyperplanes. Assuming the equations are consistent and [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent), a system of $k$ equations describes a flat of dimension $n - k$.

### Parametric {#Parametric}

A flat can also be described by a system of linear [parametric equations](wikipedia://en/576108). A line can be described by equations involving one [parameter](wikipedia://en/25065):

$$x=2+3t,\;\;\;\;y=-1+t\;\;\;\;z=\frac{3}{2}-4t$$

while the description of a plane would require two parameters:

$$x=5+2t_1-3t_2,\;\;\;\; y=-4+t_1+2t_2\;\;\;\;z=5t_1-3t_2.\,\!$$

In general, a parameterization of a flat of dimension $k$ would require $k$ parameters, e.g. $t_{1},\,\ldots,\,t_{k}$.

## Operations and relations on flats {#Operations_and_relations_on_flats}

### Intersecting, parallel, and skew flats {#Intersecting,_parallel,_and_skew_flats}

An [intersection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_intersection) of flats is either a flat or the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566).

If each line from one flat is parallel to some line from another flat, then these two flats are [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497). Two parallel flats of the same dimension either coincide or do not intersect; they can be described by two systems of linear equations which differ only in their right-hand sides.

If flats do not intersect, and no line from the first flat is parallel to a line from the second flat, then these are [skew flats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew_flats). It is possible only if sum of their dimensions is less than dimension of the ambient space.

### Join {#Join}

For two flats of dimensions $k_{1}$ and $k_{2}$ there exists the minimal flat which contains them, of dimension at most $k_{1} + k_{2} + 1$. If two flats intersect, then the dimension of the containing flat equals to $k_{1} + k_{2}$ minus the dimension of the intersection.

### Properties of operations {#Properties_of_operations}

These two operations (referred to as *meet* and *join*) make the set of all flats in the Euclidean $n$-space a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) and can build systematic coordinates for flats in any dimension, leading to [Grassmann coordinates](wikipedia://en/1168486) or dual Grassmann coordinates. For example, a line in three-dimensional space is determined by two distinct points or by two distinct planes.

However, the lattice of all flats is not a [distributive lattice](wikipedia://en/310883). If two lines $\ell_{1}$ and $\ell_{2}$ intersect, then $\ell_{1} \cap \ell_{2}$ is a point. If $p$ is a point not lying on the same plane, then $(\ell_{1} \cap \ell_{2}) + p = (\ell_{1} + p) \cap (\ell_{2} + p)$, both representing a line. But when $\ell_{1}$ and $\ell_{2}$ are parallel, this [distributivity](wikipedia://en/103118) fails, giving $p$ on the left-hand side and a third parallel line on the right-hand side.

## Euclidean geometry {#Euclidean_geometry}

The aforementioned facts do not depend on the structure being that of Euclidean space (namely, involving [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932)) and are correct in any [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834). In a Euclidean space:

- There is the distance between a flat and a point. (See for example *[Distance from a point to a plane](wikipedia://en/8426172)* and *[Distance from a point to a line](wikipedia://en/23972889)*.)
- There is the distance between two flats, equal to 0 if they intersect. (See for example *[Distance between two parallel lines](wikipedia://en/38728858)* (in the same plane) and *Skew lines#Distance*.)
- There is the [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) between two flats, which belongs to the interval 0, π/2 between 0 and the [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956). (See for example *[Dihedral angle](wikipedia://en/338046)* (between two planes). See also *[Angles between flats](wikipedia://en/46955123)*.)
