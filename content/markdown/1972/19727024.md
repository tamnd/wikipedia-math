# Rational number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rational number"}

*Image: Number-systems\_(NZQRC).svg.* The rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ are included in the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\mathbb{R}$, which are included in the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\mathbb{C}$, while rationals include the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $\mathbb{Z}$, which in turn include the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) $\mathbb{N}$.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **rational number** is a [number](wikipedia://en/21690) that can be expressed as the [quotient](wikipedia://en/332640) or [fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29) $\tfrac p q$ of two [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), a [numerator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerator) $p$ and a nonzero [denominator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denominator) $q$.[^o2] For example, $\tfrac{3}{7}$ is a rational number, as is every integer (for example, $-5 = \tfrac{-5}{1}$). The [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all rational numbers is often referred to as "**the rationals**",[^o3] and is [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under [addition](wikipedia://en/61338), [subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141), [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845), and [division](wikipedia://en/53696) by a nonzero rational number. It is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) under these operations and therefore also called the **field of rationals**[^o4] or the **field of rational numbers**. It is usually denoted by boldface $\mathbf{Q}$, or [blackboard bold](wikipedia://en/40276) $\mathbb{Q}.$

:::

A rational number is a [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438). The real numbers that are rational are those whose [decimal expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_expansion) either terminates after a finite number of [digits](wikipedia://en/280582) (example: $3/4 = 0.75$), or eventually begins to [repeat](wikipedia://en/13612447) the same finite [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of digits over and over (example: $9/44 = 0.20454545...$).[^o5] This statement is true not only in [base 10](wikipedia://en/8214), but also in every other integer [base](wikipedia://en/701207), such as the [binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_numeral_system) and [hexadecimal](wikipedia://en/13263) ones (see Repeating decimal).

A [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) that is not rational is called [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689).[^o6] Irrational numbers include the [square root of 2](wikipedia://en/341682) ($\sqrt 2$), [$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601), $e$, and the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386) ($\varphi$). Since the set of rational numbers is [countable](wikipedia://en/6026), and the set of real numbers is [uncountable](wikipedia://en/32248), [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) real numbers are irrational.[^o2]

The field of rational numbers is the unique field that contains the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), and is contained in any field containing the integers. In other words, the field of rational numbers is a [prime field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_field). A field has [characteristic zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_zero) if and only if it contains the rational numbers as a subfield. Finite [extensions](wikipedia://en/11634) of $\mathbb{Q}$ are called [algebraic number fields](wikipedia://en/28730822), and the [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129) of $\mathbb{Q}$ is the field of [algebraic numbers](wikipedia://en/1158).[^o7]

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), the rational numbers form a [dense subset](wikipedia://en/23523594) of the real numbers. The real numbers can be constructed from the rational numbers by [completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completion_%28metric_space%29), using [Cauchy sequences](wikipedia://en/6085), [Dedekind cuts](wikipedia://en/45569), or infinite [decimals](wikipedia://en/8214) (see [Construction of the real numbers](wikipedia://en/296666)).

::: {#o9 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Terminology"}

## Terminology {#Terminology}

In mathematics, "rational" is often used as a noun abbreviating "rational number". The adjective *rational* sometimes means that the [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) are rational numbers. For example, a [rational point](wikipedia://en/2960200) is a point with rational [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinates) (i.e., a point whose coordinates are rational numbers); a *rational matrix* is a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) of rational numbers, though it sometimes also refers to a matrix whose entries are rational functions; a *rational polynomial* may be a polynomial with rational coefficients, although the term "polynomial over the rationals" is generally preferred, to avoid confusion between "[rational expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_fraction)" and "[rational function](wikipedia://en/361210)" (a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) is a rational expression and defines a rational function, even if its coefficients are not rational numbers). However, a [rational curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_curve) *is not* a curve defined over the rationals, but a curve which can be parameterized by rational functions.

### Etymology {#Etymology}

Although nowadays *rational numbers* are defined in terms of *ratios*, the term *rational* is not a [derivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphological_derivation) of *ratio*. On the contrary, it is *ratio* that is derived from *rational*: the first use of *ratio* with its modern meaning was attested in English about 1660,[^o11] while the use of *rational* for qualifying numbers appeared almost a century earlier, in 1570.[^o12] This meaning of *rational* came from the mathematical meaning of *irrational*, which was first used in 1551, and it was used in "translations of Euclid (following his peculiar use of ἄλογος)".[^o13][^o14]

This unusual history originated in the fact that [ancient Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) "avoided heresy by forbidding themselves from thinking of those \[irrational\] lengths as numbers".[^o15] So such lengths were *irrational*, in the sense of *illogical*, that is "not to be spoken about" (ἄλογος in Greek).[^o16]

## Arithmetic {#Arithmetic}

### Irreducible fraction {#Irreducible_fraction}

Every rational number may be expressed in a unique way as an [irreducible fraction](wikipedia://en/14822) $\tfrac a b,$ where $a$ and $b$ are [coprime integers](wikipedia://en/6556) and $b > 0$. This is often called the [canonical form](wikipedia://en/515096) of the rational number.

Starting from a rational number $\tfrac a b,$ its canonical form may be obtained by dividing both $a$ and $b$ by their [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354), and, if $b < 0$, changing the sign of the resulting numerator and denominator.

### Embedding of integers {#Embedding_of_integers}

Any integer $n$ can be expressed as the rational number $\tfrac n 1,$ which is its canonical form as a rational number.

### Equality {#Equality}

$\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}$ if and only if $ad = bc$

If both fractions are in canonical form, then:

$\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}$ if and only if $a = c$ and $b = d$[^o21]

### Ordering {#Ordering}

If both denominators are positive (particularly if both fractions are in canonical form):

$\frac{a}{b} < \frac{c}{d}$ if and only if $ad < bc.$

On the other hand, if either denominator is negative, then each fraction with a negative denominator must first be converted into an equivalent form with a positive denominator—by changing the signs of both its numerator and denominator.[^o21]

### Addition {#Addition}

Two fractions are added as follows:

$$\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad+bc}{bd}.$$

If both fractions are in canonical form, the result is in canonical form if and only if $b, d$ are [coprime integers](wikipedia://en/6556).[^o21][^o24]

### Subtraction {#Subtraction}

$$\frac{a}{b} - \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad-bc}{bd}.$$

If both fractions are in canonical form, the result is in canonical form if and only if $b, d$ are [coprime integers](wikipedia://en/6556).[^o24]

### Multiplication {#Multiplication}

The rule for multiplication is:

$$\frac{a}{b} \cdot\frac{c}{d} = \frac{ac}{bd}.$$

where the result may be a [reducible fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reducible_fraction)—even if both original fractions are in canonical form.[^o21][^o24]

### Inverse {#Inverse}

Every rational number $\tfrac a b$ has an [additive inverse](wikipedia://en/228312), often called its *opposite*,

$$- \left( \frac{a}{b} \right) = \frac{-a}{b}.$$

If $\tfrac a b$ is in canonical form, the same is true for its opposite.

A nonzero rational number $\tfrac a b$ has a [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940), also called its *reciprocal*,

$$\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{-1} = \frac{b}{a}.$$

If $\tfrac a b$ is in canonical form, then the canonical form of its reciprocal is either $\tfrac b a$ or $\tfrac{-b}{-a},$ depending on the sign of $a$.

### Division {#Division}

If $b, c, d$ are nonzero, the division rule is

$$\frac{\,\dfrac{a}{b}\,} {\dfrac{c}{d}} = \frac{ad}{bc}.$$

Thus, dividing $\tfrac a b$ by $\tfrac c d$ is equivalent to multiplying $\tfrac a b$ by the [reciprocal](wikipedia://en/229940) of $\tfrac c d:$[^o24]

$$\frac{ad}{bc} = \frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{d}{c}.$$

### Exponentiation to integer power {#Exponentiation_to_integer_power}

If $n$ is a non-negative integer, then

$$\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n = \frac{a^n}{b^n}.$$

The result is in canonical form if the same is true for $\tfrac a b.$ In particular,

$$\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^0 = 1.$$

If $a \ne 0$, then

$$\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{-n} = \frac{b^n}{a^n}.$$

If $\tfrac a b$ is in canonical form, the canonical form of the result is $\tfrac{b^n}{a^n}$ if $a > 0$ or $n$ is even. Otherwise, the canonical form of the result is $\tfrac{-b^n}{-a^n}.$

## Continued fraction representation {#Continued_fraction_representation}

A **finite continued fraction** is an expression such as

$$a_0 + \cfrac{1}{a_1 + \cfrac{1}{a_2 + \cfrac{1}{ \ddots + \cfrac{1}{a_n} }}},$$

where $a_{n}$ are integers. Every rational number $\tfrac a b$ can be represented as a finite continued fraction, whose [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) $a_{n}$ can be determined by applying the [Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/10377) to $(a, b)$.

## Other representations {#Other_representations}

- [common fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_fraction): $\tfrac 8 3$
- [mixed numeral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_numeral): $2\tfrac 2 3$
- [repeating decimal](wikipedia://en/13612447) using a [vinculum](wikipedia://en/673457): $2.\overline 6$
- repeating decimal using [parentheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parentheses): $2.(6)$
- [continued fraction](wikipedia://en/739199) using traditional typography: $2 + \tfrac 1 {1 + \tfrac 1 2}$
- continued fraction in abbreviated notation: $[2; 1, 2]$
- [Egyptian fraction](wikipedia://en/336349): $2 + \tfrac 1 2 + \tfrac 1 6$
- [prime power decomposition](wikipedia://en/11556#Canonical_representation_of_a_positive_integer): $2^3 \times 3^{-1}$
- [quote notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quote_notation): $3'6$

are different ways to represent the same rational value.

## Formal construction {#Formal_construction}

*Image: Rational Representation.svg.* A diagram showing a representation of the equivalent classes of pairs of integers

The rational numbers may be built as [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of [ordered pairs](wikipedia://en/22362) of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563).[^o21][^o24]

More precisely, let $(\mathbb{Z} \times (\mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\}))$ be the set of the pairs $(m, n)$ of integers such $n \ne 0$. An [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) is defined on this set by

$(m_1, n_1) \sim (m_2, n_2) \iff m_1 n_2 = m_2 n_1.$[^o21][^o24]

Addition and multiplication can be defined by the following rules:

$$(m_1, n_1) + (m_2, n_2) \equiv (m_1n_2 + n_1m_2, n_1n_2),$$

$(m_1, n_1) \times (m_2, n_2) \equiv (m_1m_2, n_1n_2).$[^o21]

This equivalence relation is a [congruence relation](wikipedia://en/48167), which means that it is compatible with the addition and multiplication defined above; the set of rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ is the defined as the [quotient set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_set) by this equivalence relation, $(\mathbb{Z} \times (\mathbb{Z} \backslash \{0\})) / \sim,$ equipped with the addition and the multiplication induced by the above operations. (This construction can be carried out with any [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) and produces its [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271).)[^o21]

The equivalence class of a pair $(m, n)$ is denoted $\tfrac m n.$ Two pairs $(m_{1}, n_{1})$ and $(m_{2}, n_{2})$ belong to the same equivalence class (that is are equivalent) if and only if

$$m_1n_2 = m_2n_1.$$

This means that

$$\frac{m_1}{n_1} = \frac{m_2}{n_2}$$

if and only if[^o21][^o24]

$$m_1n_2 = m_2n_1.$$

Every equivalence class $\tfrac m n$ may be represented by infinitely many pairs, since

$$\cdots = \frac{-2m}{-2n} = \frac{-m}{-n} = \frac{m}{n} = \frac{2m}{2n} = \cdots.$$

Each equivalence class contains a unique *[canonical representative element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_%28mathematics%29)*. The canonical representative is the unique pair $(m, n)$ in the equivalence class such that $m$ and $n$ are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime), and $n > 0$. It is called the [representation in lowest terms](wikipedia://en/14822) of the rational number.

The integers may be considered to be rational numbers identifying the integer $n$ with the rational number $\tfrac n 1.$

A [total order](wikipedia://en/30330) may be defined on the rational numbers, that extends the natural order of the integers. One has

$$\frac{m_1}{n_1} \le \frac{m_2}{n_2}$$

If

$$\begin{aligned} & (n_1n_2 > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad m_1n_2 \le n_1m_2) \\ & \qquad \text{or} \\ & (n_1n_2 < 0 \quad \text{and} \quad m_1n_2 \ge n_1m_2). \end{aligned}$$

## Properties {#Properties}

The set $\mathbb{Q}$ of all rational numbers, together with the addition and multiplication operations shown above, forms a [field](wikipedia://en/10603).[^o21]

$\mathbb{Q}$ has no [field automorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_automorphism) other than the identity. (A field automorphism must fix 0 and 1; as it must fix the sum and the difference of two fixed elements, it must fix every integer; as it must fix the quotient of two fixed elements, it must fix every rational number, and is thus the identity.)

$\mathbb{Q}$ is a [prime field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_field), which is a field that has no subfield other than itself.[^o35] The rationals are the smallest field with [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) zero. Every field of characteristic zero contains a unique subfield isomorphic to $\mathbb{Q}.$

With the order defined above, $\mathbb{Q}$ is an [ordered field](wikipedia://en/22430)[^o24] that has no subfield other than itself, and is the smallest ordered field, in the sense that every ordered field contains a unique subfield [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to $\mathbb{Q}.$

$\mathbb{Q}$ is the [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) of the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $\mathbb{Z}.$[^o36] The [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129) of $\mathbb{Q},$ i.e. the field of roots of rational polynomials, is the field of [algebraic numbers](wikipedia://en/1158).

The rationals are a [densely ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Densely_ordered) set: between any two rationals, there sits another one, and, therefore, infinitely many other ones.[^o21] For example, for any two fractions such that

$$\frac{a}{b} < \frac{c}{d}$$

(where $b,d$ are positive), we have

$$\frac{a}{b} < \frac{a + c}{b + d} < \frac{c}{d}.$$

Any [totally ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_ordered) set which is countable, dense (in the above sense), and has no least or greatest element is [order isomorphic](wikipedia://en/23916899) to the rational numbers.[^o37]

### Countability {#Countability}

*Image: Diagonal argument.svg.* Illustration of the countability of the positive rationals

The set of positive rational numbers is [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable), as is illustrated in the figure.

More precisely, one can sort the fractions by increasing values of the sum of the numerator and the denominator, and, for equal sums, by increasing numerator or denominator. This produces a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of fractions from which one can remove the reducible fractions (in red on the figure), obtaining a sequence that contains each rational number exactly once. This establishes a bijection between the rational numbers and the natural numbers, which maps each rational number to its rank in the sequence.

A similar method can be used for numbering all rational numbers (positive and negative).

As the set of all rational numbers is countable, and the set of all real numbers (as well as the set of irrational numbers) is uncountable, the set of rational numbers is a [null set](wikipedia://en/21520), that is, [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) real numbers are irrational, in the sense of [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198).[^o39]

:::

## Real numbers and topological properties {#Real_numbers_and_topological_properties}

The rationals are a [dense subset](wikipedia://en/23523594) of the [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers); every real number has rational numbers arbitrarily close to it.[^o21] A related property is that rational numbers are the only numbers with [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) expansions as [regular continued fractions](wikipedia://en/739199).[^o41]

In the usual [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) of the real numbers, the rationals are neither an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) nor a [closed set](wikipedia://en/47279).[^o42]

By virtue of their order, the rationals carry an [order topology](wikipedia://en/213650). The rational numbers, as a subspace of the real numbers, also carry a [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926). The rational numbers form a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) by using the [absolute difference](wikipedia://en/28021681) metric $d(x,y)=|x-y|,$ and this yields a third topology on $\mathbb{Q}.$ All three topologies coincide and turn the rationals into a [topological field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_field). The rational numbers are an important example of a space which is not [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact). The rationals are characterized topologically as the unique [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) [metrizable space](wikipedia://en/1337282) without [isolated points](wikipedia://en/390557). The space is also [totally disconnected](wikipedia://en/303356). The rational numbers do not form a [complete metric space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completeness_%28topology%29), and the [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) are the completion of $\mathbb{Q}$ under the metric $d(x,y)=|x-y|$ above.[^o24]

## *p*-adic numbers {#p-adic_numbers}

In addition to the absolute value metric mentioned above, there are other metrics which turn $\mathbb{Q}$ into a topological field:

Let $p$ be a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) and for any nonzero integer $a$, let $|a|_p = p^{-n},$ where $p^{n}$ is the highest power of $p$ [dividing](wikipedia://en/49492) $a$.

In addition set $|0|_p = 0.$ For any rational number $\frac a b,$ we set

$$\left|\frac{a}{b}\right|_p = \frac{|a|_p}{|b|_p}.$$

Then

$$d_p(x,y) =|x-y|_p$$

defines a [metric](wikipedia://en/20018) on $\mathbb{Q}.$[^o44]

The metric space $(\mathbb{Q}, d_p)$ is not complete, and its completion is the [$p$-adic number field](wikipedia://en/51423) $\mathbb{Q}_p.$ [Ostrowski's theorem](wikipedia://en/3360343) states that any non-trivial [absolute value](wikipedia://en/5042667) on the rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ is equivalent to either the usual real absolute value or a [$p$-adic](wikipedia://en/51423) absolute value.
