# Directed graph

*Image: Directed graph no background.svg.* A directed graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Directed graph"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and more specifically in [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **directed graph** (or **digraph**) is a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) that is made up of a set of [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) connected by directed [edges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_%28graph_theory%29), often called **arcs**.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

In formal terms, a directed graph is an ordered pair *G* = (*V*, *A*) where[^o4]

- *V* is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) whose [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29) are called *[vertices](wikipedia://en/638899)*, *nodes*, or *points*;
- *A* is a set of [ordered pairs](wikipedia://en/22362) of vertices, called *arcs*, *directed edges* (sometimes simply *edges* with the corresponding set named *E* instead of *A*), *arrows*, or *directed lines*.

It differs from an ordinary or [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph), in that the latter is defined in terms of [unordered pairs](wikipedia://en/1112432) of vertices, which are usually called *edges*, *links* or *lines*.

The aforementioned definition does not allow a directed graph to have multiple arrows with the same source and target nodes, but some authors consider a broader definition that allows directed graphs to have such multiple arcs (namely, they allow the arc set to be a [multiset](wikipedia://en/305303)). Sometimes these entities are called **[directed multigraphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_multigraph)** (or **multidigraphs**). On the other hand, the aforementioned definition allows a directed graph to have [loops](wikipedia://en/1449083) (that is, arcs that directly connect nodes with themselves), but some authors consider a narrower definition that does not allow directed graphs to have loops.[^o5] Directed graphs without loops may be called **simple directed graphs**, while directed graphs with loops may be called *loop-digraphs* (see section Types of directed graph).

:::

## Types of directed graphs {#Types_of_directed_graphs}

### Subclasses {#Subclasses}

*Image: Directed acyclic graph 2.svg.* A simple directed acyclic graph

*Image: 4-tournament.svg.* A tournament on 4 vertices

- **Symmetric directed graphs** are directed graphs where all edges appear twice, one in each direction (that is, for every arrow that belongs to the digraph, the corresponding inverse arrow also belongs to it). (Such an edge is sometimes called "bidirected" and such graphs are sometimes called "bidirected", but this conflicts with the meaning for [bidirected graphs](wikipedia://en/2699467).)
- **Simple directed graphs** are directed graphs that have no [loops](wikipedia://en/1449083) (arrows that directly connect vertices to themselves) and no multiple arrows with same source and target nodes. As already introduced, in case of multiple arrows the entity is usually addressed as *[directed multigraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_multigraph)*. Some authors describe digraphs with loops as *loop-digraphs*.[^o5]
  - **Complete directed graphs** are simple directed graphs where each pair of vertices is joined by a symmetric pair of directed arcs (it is equivalent to an undirected [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) with the edges replaced by pairs of inverse arcs). It follows that a complete digraph is symmetric.
  - **Semicomplete multipartite digraphs** are simple digraphs in which the vertex set is partitioned into sets such that for every pair of vertices *x* and *y* in different sets, there is an arc between *x* and *y*. There can be one arc between *x* and *y* or two arcs in opposite directions.[^o8]
  - **Semicomplete digraphs** are simple digraphs where there is an arc between each pair of vertices. Every semicomplete digraph is a semicomplete multipartite digraph in a trivial way, with each vertex constituting a set of the partition.[^o9]
  - **Quasi-transitive digraphs** are simple digraphs where for every triple *x*, *y*, *z* of distinct vertices with arcs from *x* to *y* and from *y* to *z*, there is an arc between *x* and *z*. There can be just one arc between *x* and *z* or two arcs in opposite directions. A semicomplete digraph is a quasi-transitive digraph. There are extensions of quasi-transitive digraphs called *k*-quasi-transitive digraphs.[^o10]
  - **[Oriented graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented_graph)** are directed graphs having no opposite pairs of directed edges (i.e. at most one of (*x*, *y*) and (*y*, *x*) may be arrows of the graph). It follows that a directed graph is an oriented graph if and only if it has no [2-cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_cycle).[^o11] Such a graph can be obtained by applying an [orientation](wikipedia://en/36619818) to an undirected graph.
    - **[Tournaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tournament_%28mathematics%29)** are oriented graphs obtained by choosing a direction for each edge in undirected [complete graphs](wikipedia://en/85816). A tournament is a semicomplete digraph.[^o9]
    - A directed graph is **acyclic** if it has no [directed cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_cycle). The usual name for such a digraph is **[directed acyclic graph](wikipedia://en/204002)** (DAG).[^o12]
      - *[Multitrees](wikipedia://en/27131455)* are DAGs in which there are no two distinct directed paths from the same starting vertex to the same ending vertex.
      - *[Oriented trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented_tree)* or *polytrees* are DAGs formed by orienting the edges of trees (connected, acyclic undirected graphs).
        - *[Rooted trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rooted_tree)* are oriented trees in which all edges of the underlying undirected tree are directed either away from or towards the root (they are called, respectively, **arborescences** or **out-trees**, and **in-trees**.

### Digraphs with supplementary properties {#Digraphs_with_supplementary_properties}

- **Weighted directed graphs** (also known as **directed networks**) are (simple) directed graphs with *weights* assigned to their arrows, similarly to [weighted graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weighted_graph) (which are also known as undirected networks or [weighted networks](wikipedia://en/24373430)).[^o5]
  - **[Flow networks](wikipedia://en/645676)** are weighted directed graphs where two nodes are distinguished, a *source* and a *sink*.
- **[Rooted directed graphs](wikipedia://en/1373168)** (also known as **flow graphs**) are digraphs in which a vertex has been distinguished as the root.
  - *[Control-flow graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control-flow_graph)* are rooted digraphs used in computer science as a representation of the paths that might be traversed through a program during its execution.
- **[Signal-flow graphs](wikipedia://en/15175696)** are directed graphs in which nodes represent system variables and branches (edges, arcs, or arrows) represent functional connections between pairs of nodes.
- **[Flow graphs](wikipedia://en/44993448)** are digraphs associated with a set of linear algebraic or differential equations.
- **[State diagrams](wikipedia://en/187337)** are [directed multigraphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_multigraph) that represent [finite-state machines](wikipedia://en/10931).
- **[Commutative diagrams](wikipedia://en/138484)** are digraphs used in [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), where the vertices represent (mathematical) objects and the arrows represent morphisms, with the property that all directed paths with the same start and endpoints lead to the same result by composition.
- In the theory of [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945), a **[quiver](wikipedia://en/355100)** *Q* is a directed graph serving as the domain of, and thus characterizing the shape of, a *representation* *V* defined as a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987), specifically an object of the [functor category](wikipedia://en/407039) FinVct<sub>*K*</sub><sup>*F*(*Q*)</sup> where *F*(*Q*) is the [free category](wikipedia://en/36769284) on *Q* consisting of paths in *Q* and FinVct<sub>*K*</sub> is the category of finite-dimensional [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K*. Representations of a quiver label its vertices with vector spaces and its edges (and hence paths) compatibly with [linear transformations](wikipedia://en/18102) between them, and transform via [natural transformations](wikipedia://en/45022).

## Basic terminology {#Basic_terminology}

*Image: Incidence matrix - directed graph.svg.* Oriented graph with corresponding incidence matrix

An arc (*x*, *y*) is considered to be directed *from* *x* *to* *y*; *y* is called the *head* and *x* is called the *tail* of the arc; *y* is said to be a *direct successor* of *x* and *x* is said to be a *direct predecessor* of *y*. If a [path](wikipedia://en/638889) leads from *x* to *y*, then *y* is said to be a *successor* of *x* and *reachable* from *x*, and *x* is said to be a *predecessor* of *y*. The arc (*y*, *x*) is called the *reversed arc* of (*x*, *y*).

The [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) of a multidigraph with loops is the integer-valued [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) with rows and columns corresponding to the vertices, where a nondiagonal entry *a*<sub>*ij*</sub> is the number of arcs from vertex *i* to vertex *j*, and the diagonal entry *a*<sub>*ii*</sub> is the number of loops at vertex *i*. The adjacency matrix of a directed graph is a [logical matrix](wikipedia://en/4109196), and is unique up to permutation of rows and columns.

Another matrix representation for a directed graph is its [incidence matrix](wikipedia://en/420919).

See [direction](wikipedia://en/325802#direction) for more definitions.

## Indegree and outdegree {#Indegree_and_outdegree}

*Image: DirectedDegrees.svg.* A directed graph with vertices labeled (indegree, outdegree)

For a vertex, the number of head ends adjacent to a vertex is called the *indegree* of the vertex and the number of tail ends adjacent to a vertex is its *outdegree* (called *[branching factor](wikipedia://en/438471)* in trees).

Let $G = (V, E)$ and $v \in V$. The indegree of *v* is denoted deg<sup>−</sup>(*v*) and its outdegree is denoted deg<sup>\+</sup>(*v*).

A vertex with $\deg^{-}(v) = 0$ is called a *source*, as it is the origin of each of its outgoing arcs. Similarly, a vertex with $\deg^{+}(v) = 0$ is called a *sink*, since it is the end of each of its incoming arcs.

The *degree sum formula* states that, for a directed graph,

$$\sum_{v \in V} \deg^-(v) = \sum_{v \in V} \deg^+(v) = |E|.$$

If for every vertex $v \in V$, $\deg^{+}(v) = \deg^{-}(v)$, the graph is called a *balanced directed graph*.[^o17]

:::

## Degree sequence {#Degree_sequence}

The degree sequence of a directed graph is the list of its indegree and outdegree pairs; for the above example we have degree sequence ((2, 0), (2, 2), (0, 2), (1, 1)). The degree sequence is a directed graph invariant so isomorphic directed graphs have the same degree sequence. However, the degree sequence does not, in general, uniquely identify a directed graph; in some cases, non-isomorphic digraphs have the same degree sequence.

The [directed graph realization problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digraph_realization_problem) is the problem of finding a directed graph with the degree sequence a given sequence of positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) pairs. (Trailing pairs of zeros may be ignored since they are trivially realized by adding an appropriate number of isolated vertices to the directed graph.) A sequence which is the degree sequence of some directed graph, i.e. for which the directed graph realization problem has a solution, is called a directed graphic or directed graphical sequence. This problem can either be solved by the [Kleitman–Wang algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleitman–Wang_algorithm) or by the [Fulkerson–Chen–Anstee theorem](wikipedia://en/43198751).

## Directed graph connectivity {#Directed_graph_connectivity}

A directed graph is *weakly connected* (or just *connected*[^o21]) if the undirected *underlying graph* obtained by replacing all directed edges of the graph with undirected edges is a [connected graph](wikipedia://en/1614492).

A directed graph is *[strongly connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strongly_connected)* or *strong* if it contains a directed path from *x* to *y* (and from *y* to *x*) for every pair of vertices (*x*, *y*). The *strong components* are the maximal strongly connected subgraphs.

A connected [rooted graph](wikipedia://en/1373168) (or *flow graph*) is one where there exists a directed path to every vertex from a distinguished *root vertex*.
