# Nørlund–Rice integral

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nørlund–Rice integral"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Nørlund–Rice integral**, sometimes called **Rice's method**, relates the *n*th [forward difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forward_difference) of a function to a [contour integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contour_integral) on the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628). It commonly appears in the theory of [finite differences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_differences) and has also been applied in [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) and [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401) to estimate [binary tree](wikipedia://en/4321) lengths. It is named in honour of [Niels Erik Nørlund](wikipedia://en/17132688) and [Stephen O. Rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_O._Rice). Nørlund's contribution was to define the integral; Rice's contribution was to demonstrate its utility by applying [saddle-point techniques](wikipedia://en/27082137) to its evaluation.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The *n*th [forward difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forward_difference) of a function *f*(*x*) is given by

$$\Delta^n[f](x)= \sum_{k=0}^n {n \choose k} (-1)^{n-k} f(x+k)$$

where ${n \choose k}$ is the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668).

The Nørlund–Rice integral is given by

$$\sum_{k=\alpha}^n {n \choose k} (-1)^{n-k} f(k) = \frac{n!}{2\pi i} \oint_\gamma \frac{f(z)}{z(z-1)(z-2)\cdots(z-n)}\, dz$$

where *f* is understood to be [meromorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meromorphic), α is an integer, $0\leq \alpha \leq n$, and the contour of integration is understood to circle the [poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) located at the integers α, ..., *n*, but encircles neither integers 0, ..., $\alpha-1$ nor any of the poles of *f*. The integral may also be written as

$$\sum_{k=\alpha}^n {n \choose k} (-1)^{k} f(k) = -\frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_\gamma B(n+1, -z) f(z)\, dz$$

where *B*(*a*,*b*) is the Euler [beta function](wikipedia://en/245660). If the function $f(z)$ is [polynomially bounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomially_bounded) on the right hand side of the complex plane, then the contour may be extended to infinity on the right hand side, allowing the transform to be written as

$$\sum_{k=\alpha}^n {n \choose k} (-1)^{n-k} f(k) = \frac{-n!}{2\pi i} \int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty} \frac{f(z)}{z(z-1)(z-2)\cdots(z-n)}\, dz$$

where the constant *c* is to the left of α.

:::

::: {#o5 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Poisson–Mellin–Newton cycle"}

## Poisson–Mellin–Newton cycle {#Poisson–Mellin–Newton_cycle}

The Poisson–Mellin–Newton cycle, noted by Flajolet et al. in 1985, is the observation that the resemblance of the Nørlund–Rice integral to the [Mellin transform](wikipedia://en/387297) is not accidental, but is related by means of the [binomial transform](wikipedia://en/3877474) and the [Newton series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton_series). In this cycle, let $\{f_n\}$ be a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838), and let *g*(*t*) be the corresponding [Poisson generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_generating_function), that is, let

$$g(t) = e^{-t} \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{t^n}{n!} f_n.$$

Taking its Mellin transform

$$\phi(s)=\int_0^\infty g(t) t^{s-1}\, dt,$$

one can then regain the original sequence by means of the Nörlund–Rice integral (see References "Mellin, seen from the sky"):

$$f_n = \frac{(-1)^n }{2\pi i} \int_\gamma \frac {\phi(-s)}{\Gamma(-s)} \frac{n!}{s(s-1)\cdots (s-n)}\, ds$$

where Γ is the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316) which cancels with the gamma from [Ramanujan's Master Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanujan's_Master_Theorem).

:::

## Riesz mean {#Riesz_mean}

A closely related integral frequently occurs in the discussion of [Riesz means](wikipedia://en/5811855). Very roughly, it can be said to be related to the Nörlund–Rice integral in the same way that [Perron's formula](wikipedia://en/4928530) is related to the Mellin transform: rather than dealing with infinite series, it deals with finite series.

## Utility {#Utility}

The integral representation for these types of series is interesting because the integral can often be evaluated using [asymptotic expansion](wikipedia://en/642090) or [saddle-point](wikipedia://en/27082137) techniques; by contrast, the forward difference series can be extremely hard to evaluate numerically, because the binomial coefficients grow rapidly for large *n*.
