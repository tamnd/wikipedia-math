# Waveguide (radio frequency)

*Image: Waveguide collection.jpg.* Collection of standard waveguide components.

Network

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Waveguide"}

In [radio-frequency engineering](wikipedia://en/13485805) and [communications engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communications_system_engineering), a **waveguide** is a hollow metal pipe used to carry [radio waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_wave).[^o2] This type of [waveguide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waveguide) is used as a [transmission line](wikipedia://en/41811) mostly at [microwave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave) frequencies, for such purposes as connecting microwave [transmitters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmitter) and [receivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_receiver) to their [antennas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antenna_%28radio%29), in equipment such as [microwave ovens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave_oven), [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar) sets, [satellite communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_communications), and microwave radio links.

:::

The [group velocity](wikipedia://en/12778) of guided [electromagnetic waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_wave) (EMW) is a fraction of the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light).[^o3] Propagation in a (metal-pipe) waveguide may be imagined as a zig-zag path, with the EMW being repeatedly reflected between opposite walls of the guide. For the particular case of **rectangular waveguide**, it is possible to base an exact analysis on this view. Propagation in a dielectric waveguide may be viewed in the same way, with the waves confined to the dielectric by [total internal reflection](wikipedia://en/30426) at its surface. Some structures, such as [non-radiative dielectric waveguides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-radiative_dielectric_waveguide) and the [Goubau line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goubau_line), use both metal walls and dielectric surfaces to confine the wave.

::: {#o5 .statement cue=named-heading name="Principle"}

## Principle {#Principle}

*Image: Diplexer1.jpg.* Example of waveguides and a [diplexer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diplexer) in an air traffic control radar

Depending on the frequency, waveguides can be constructed from either conductive or [dielectric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielectric) materials. Generally, the lower the frequency to be passed the larger the waveguide is. For example, the natural waveguide the earth forms given by the dimensions between the conductive ionosphere and the ground as well as the circumference at the median altitude of the Earth is resonant at 7.83 Hz. This is known as [Schumann resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schumann_resonance). On the other hand, waveguides used in [extremely high frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extremely_high_frequency) (EHF) communications can be less than a millimeter in width.

:::

## History {#History}

*Image: George Southworth with Waveguide (1939) (crop).jpg.* [George C. Southworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_C._Southworth) who developed waveguides in the early 1930s, in front of mile-long experimental waveguide run at Bell Labs, Holmdel, New Jersey, used in his research.[^o15]

*Image: Southworth demonstrating waveguide.jpg.* Southworth *(at left)* demonstrating waveguide at [IRE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Radio_Engineers) meeting in 1938,[^o15] showing 1.5 GHz microwaves passing through the 7.5 m flexible metal hose registering on a diode detector.

During the 1890s, theorists did the first analyses of electromagnetic waves in ducts.[^o7] Around 1893, [J. J. Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._Thomson) derived the electromagnetic modes inside a cylindrical metal cavity.[^o7] In 1897, [Lord Rayleigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Rayleigh) did a definitive analysis of waveguides; he solved the [boundary value problem](wikipedia://en/436779) of electromagnetic waves propagating through both conducting tubes and dielectric rods of arbitrary shape.[^o7][^o8][^o9][^o10] He showed that the waves could travel without attenuation only in specific [normal modes](wikipedia://en/478195) with either the [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) ([TE modes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TE_mode)) or [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563) ([TM modes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TM_mode)), perpendicular to the direction of propagation. He also showed each mode had a [cutoff frequency](wikipedia://en/40986) below which waves would not propagate. Since the cutoff wavelength for a given tube was of the same order as its width, it was clear that a hollow conducting tube could not carry radio wavelengths much larger than its diameter. In 1902, R. H. Weber observed that electromagnetic waves travel at a slower speed in tubes than in free space, and deduced the reason; that the waves travel in a "zigzag" path as they reflect from the walls.[^o7][^o9][^o11]

Prior to the 1920s, practical work on radio waves concentrated on the low frequency end of the radio spectrum, as these frequencies were better for long-range communication.[^o7] These were far below the frequencies that could propagate in even large waveguides, so there was little experimental work on waveguides during this period, although a few experiments were done. In a June 1, 1894, lecture, "The work of Hertz", before the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society), [Oliver Lodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Lodge) demonstrated the transmission of 3 inch radio waves from a [spark gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spark_gap) through a short cylindrical copper duct.[^o7][^o12] In his pioneering 1894-1900 research on microwaves, [Jagadish Chandra Bose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jagadish_Chandra_Bose) used short lengths of pipe to conduct the waves, so some sources credit him with inventing the waveguide.[^o13] However, after this, the concept of radio waves being carried by a tube or duct passed out of engineering knowledge.[^o7]

During the 1920s, the first continuous sources of high frequency radio waves were developed: the [Barkhausen–Kurz tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barkhausen–Kurz_tube),[^o14] the first oscillator which could produce power at [UHF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrahigh_frequency) frequencies; and the [split-anode magnetron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavity_magnetron) which by the 1930s had generated radio waves at up to 10 GHz.[^o7] These made possible the first systematic research on microwaves in the 1930s. It was discovered that [transmission lines](wikipedia://en/41811) used to carry lower frequency radio waves, [parallel line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_lead) and [coaxial cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coaxial_cable), had excessive power losses at microwave frequencies, creating a need for a new transmission method.[^o7][^o14]

The waveguide was developed independently between 1932 and 1936 by [George C. Southworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_C._Southworth) at [Bell Telephone Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Telephone_Laboratories)[^o15] and [Wilmer L. Barrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilmer_L._Barrow) at the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology), who worked without knowledge of one another.[^o7][^o9][^o10][^o14] Southworth's interest was sparked during his 1920s doctoral work in which he measured the [dielectric constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielectric_constant) of water with a radio frequency [Lecher line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lecher_line) in a long tank of water. He found that if he removed the Lecher line, the tank of water still showed resonance peaks, indicating it was acting as a [dielectric waveguide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielectric_waveguide).[^o7] At Bell Labs in 1931, he resumed work in dielectric waveguides. By March 1932, he observed waves in water-filled copper pipes. Rayleigh's previous work had been forgotten, and [Sergei A. Schelkunoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_A._Schelkunoff), a Bell Labs mathematician, did theoretical analyses of waveguides[^o7][^o16] and rediscovered waveguide modes. In December 1933 it was realized that with a metal sheath the dielectric is superfluous and attention shifted to metal waveguides.

Barrow had become interested in high frequencies in 1930 studying under [Arnold Sommerfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Sommerfeld) in Germany.[^o7] At MIT beginning in 1932, he worked on high frequency antennas to generate narrow beams of radio waves to locate aircraft in fog. He invented a [horn antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_antenna) and hit on the idea of using a hollow pipe as a feedline to feed radio waves to the antenna.[^o7] By March 1936, he had derived the propagation modes and cutoff frequency in a rectangular waveguide.[^o14] The source he was using had a large wavelength of 40 cm, so for his first successful waveguide experiments he used a 16-foot section of air duct, 18 inches in diameter.[^o7]

Barrow and Southworth became aware of each other's work a few weeks before both were scheduled to present papers on waveguides to a combined meeting of the [American Physical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Physical_Society) and the [Institute of Radio Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Radio_Engineers) in May 1936.[^o7][^o14] They amicably worked out credit sharing and patent division arrangements.

The development of centimeter [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar) during World War 2 and the first high power microwave tubes, the [klystron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klystron) (1938) and cavity magnetron (1940), resulted in the first widespread use of waveguides.[^o14] Standard waveguide plumbing-like components were manufactured, with flanges on the end which could be bolted together. In the 1960s, waveguides became common in commercial microwave systems, such as airport radar and [microwave relay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave_relay) networks which were built to transmit telephone calls and television programs between cities.

## Description {#Description}

*Image: Waveguide.svg.* Rectangular hollow waveguide

*Image: WaveguideJ-Band.png.* Flexible rectangular waveguide from a J-Band radar

*Image: Radar antenna feed waveguide.png.* Typical waveguide application: [antenna feed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antenna_feed) for military radar

In the [microwave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave) region of the [electromagnetic spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_spectrum), a waveguide normally consists of a hollow metallic conductor. These waveguides can take the form of single conductors with or without a dielectric coating, e.g. the [Goubau line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goubau_line) and helical waveguides. Hollow waveguides must be one-half wavelength or more in diameter in order to support one or more transverse wave modes.

Waveguides may be filled with pressurized gas to inhibit arcing and prevent [multipaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multipaction), allowing higher power transmission. Conversely, waveguides may be required to be evacuated as part of evacuated systems (e.g. electron beam systems).

A [slotted waveguide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slotted_waveguide) is generally used for radar and other similar applications. The waveguide serves as a feed path, and each slot is a separate radiator, thus forming an antenna. This structure has the capability of generating a radiation pattern to launch an [electromagnetic wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_wave) in a specific relatively narrow and controllable direction.

A **closed waveguide** is an electromagnetic waveguide (a) that is tubular, usually with a circular or rectangular cross section, (b) that has electrically conducting walls, (c) that may be hollow or filled with a dielectric material, (d) that can support a large number of discrete propagating modes, though only a few may be practical, (e) in which each discrete mode defines the [propagation constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propagation_constant) for that mode, (f) in which the [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28physics%29) at any point is describable in terms of the supported modes, (g) in which there is no [radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation) field, and (h) in which discontinuities and bends may cause mode conversion but not radiation.

The dimensions of a hollow metallic waveguide determine which wavelengths it can support, and in which modes. Typically, the waveguide is operated so that only a single mode is present. The lowest order mode possible is generally selected. Frequencies below the guide's cutoff frequency will not propagate. It is possible to operate waveguides at higher order modes, or with multiple modes present, but this is usually impractical.

Waveguides are almost exclusively made of metal and mostly rigid structures. There are certain types of "corrugated" waveguides that have the ability to flex and bend but only used where essential since they degrade propagation properties. Due to propagation of energy in mostly air or space within the waveguide, it is one of the lowest loss transmission line types and highly preferred for high frequency applications where most other types of transmission structures introduce large losses. Due to the [skin effect](wikipedia://en/261642) at high frequencies, electric current along the walls penetrates typically only a few [micrometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micrometre) into the metal of the inner surface. Since this is where most of the resistive loss occurs, it is important that the conductivity of interior surface be kept as high as possible. For this reason, most waveguide interior surfaces are plated with [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper), [silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver), or [gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold).

Voltage standing wave ratio ([VSWR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VSWR)) measurements may be taken to ensure that a waveguide is contiguous and has no leaks or sharp bends. If such bends or holes in the waveguide surface are present, this may diminish the performance of both transmitter and receiver equipment connected at either end. Poor transmission through the waveguide may also occur as a result of moisture build up which corrodes and degrades conductivity of the inner surfaces, which is crucial for low loss propagation. For this reason, waveguides are nominally fitted with [microwave windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave_windows) at the outer end that will not interfere with propagation but keep the elements out. Moisture can also cause [fungus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungus) build up or arcing in high power systems such as radio or radar transmitters. Moisture in waveguides can typically be prevented with [silica gel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silica_gel), a [desiccant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desiccant), or slight pressurization of the waveguide cavities with dry [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen) or [argon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argon). Desiccant silica gel canisters may be attached with screw-on nibs and higher power systems will have pressurized tanks for maintaining pressure including leakage monitors. Arcing may also occur if there is a hole, tear or bump in the conducting walls, if transmitting at high power (usually 200 watts or more). Waveguide plumbing[^o18] is crucial for proper waveguide performance. Voltage standing waves occur when impedance mismatches in the waveguide cause energy to reflect back in the opposite direction of propagation. In addition to limiting the effective transfer of energy, these reflections can cause higher voltages in the waveguide and damage equipment.

## In practice {#In_practice}

In practice, waveguides act as the equivalent of cables for [super high frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_high_frequency) (SHF) systems. For such applications, it is desired to operate waveguides with only one mode propagating through the waveguide. With rectangular waveguides, it is possible to design the waveguide such that the frequency band over which only one mode propagates is as high as 2:1 (i.e. the ratio of the upper band edge to lower band edge is two). The relation between the waveguide dimensions and the lowest frequency is simple: if $\scriptstyle W$ is the greater of its two dimensions, then the longest wavelength that will propagate is $\lambda\;=\;2W$ and the lowest frequency is thus $f\;=\; c/\lambda\;=\;c/2W$

With circular waveguides, the highest possible bandwidth allowing only a single mode to propagate is only 1.3601:1.[^o20]

Because rectangular waveguides have a much larger bandwidth over which only a single mode can propagate, standards exist for rectangular waveguides, but not for circular waveguides. In general (but not always), standard waveguides are designed such that

- one band starts where another band ends, with another band that overlaps the two bands[^o33]
- the lower edge of the band is approximately 25% higher than the waveguide's [cutoff frequency](wikipedia://en/40986)
- the upper edge of the band is approximately 5% lower than the cutoff frequency of the next higher order mode
- the waveguide height is half the waveguide width

The first condition is to allow for applications near band edges. The second condition limits [dispersion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispersion_%28optics%29), a phenomenon in which the velocity of propagation is a function of frequency. It also limits the loss per unit length. The third condition is to avoid [evanescent-wave coupling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evanescent_wave#Evanescent-wave_coupling) via higher order modes. The fourth condition is that which allows a 2:1 operation bandwidth. Although it is possible to have a 2:1 operating bandwidth when the height is less than half the width, having the height exactly half the width maximizes the power that can propagate inside the waveguide before [dielectric breakdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielectric_breakdown) occurs.

## Mathematical analysis {#Mathematical_analysis}

Electromagnetic waveguides are analyzed by solving [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737), or their reduced form, the [electromagnetic wave equation](wikipedia://en/2924436), with [boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_conditions) determined by the properties of the materials and their interfaces. These equations have multiple solutions, or modes, which are [eigenfunctions](wikipedia://en/228601) of the equation system. Each mode is characterized by a cutoff frequency below which the mode cannot exist in the guide. Waveguide propagation modes depend on the operating [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) and [polarization](wikipedia://en/41564) and the shape and size of the guide. The [longitudinal mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitudinal_mode) of a waveguide is a particular [standing wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standing_wave) pattern formed by waves confined in the cavity. The [transverse modes](wikipedia://en/516150) are classified into different types:

- TE modes (transverse electric) have no electric field in the direction of propagation.
- TM modes (transverse magnetic) have no magnetic field in the direction of propagation.
- TEM modes (transverse electromagnetic) have no electric nor magnetic field in the direction of propagation.
- Hybrid modes have both electric and magnetic field components in the direction of propagation.

Waveguides with certain symmetries may be solved using the method of [separation of variables](wikipedia://en/387750). Rectangular wave guides may be solved in rectangular coordinates.[^o24] Round waveguides may be solved in cylindrical coordinates.[^o24]

In hollow, single conductor waveguides, TEM waves are not possible. This contrasts with two-conductor [transmission lines](wikipedia://en/41811) used at lower frequencies; [coaxial cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coaxial_cable), [parallel wire line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_lead) and [stripline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stripline), in which TEM mode is possible. Additionally, the propagating modes (i.e. TE and TM) inside the waveguide can be mathematically expressed as the superposition of two TEM waves.[^o25]

The mode with the lowest cutoff frequency is termed the **dominant mode** of the guide. It is common to choose the size of the guide such that only this one mode can exist in the frequency band of operation. In rectangular and circular (hollow pipe) waveguides, the dominant modes are designated the TE<sub>1,0</sub> mode and TE<sub>1,1</sub> modes respectively.[^o26]

## Dielectric waveguides {#Dielectric_waveguides}

A *dielectric waveguide* employs a solid dielectric rod rather than a hollow pipe. An [optical fibre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_fibre) is a dielectric guide designed to work at optical frequencies. Transmission lines such as [microstrip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microstrip), [coplanar waveguide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coplanar_waveguide), [stripline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stripline) or [coaxial cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coaxial_cable) may also be considered to be waveguides.

Dielectric rod and slab waveguides are used to conduct radio waves, mostly at [millimeter wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millimeter_wave) frequencies and above.[^o28][^o29] These confine the radio waves by [total internal reflection](wikipedia://en/30426) from the step in [refractive index](wikipedia://en/25880) due to the change in dielectric constant at the material surface.[^o30] At millimeter wave frequencies and above, metal is not a good conductor, so metal waveguides can have increasing attenuation. At these wavelengths dielectric waveguides can have lower losses than metal waveguides. Optical fibre is a form of dielectric waveguide used at optical wavelengths.

One difference between dielectric and metal waveguides is that at a metal surface the electromagnetic waves are tightly confined; at high frequencies the electric and magnetic fields penetrate a very short distance into the metal. In contrast, the surface of the dielectric waveguide is an interface between two dielectrics, so the fields of the wave penetrate outside the dielectric in the form of an [evanescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evanescent_wave) (non-propagating) wave.[^o30]
