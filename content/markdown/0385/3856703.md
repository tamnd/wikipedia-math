# Atan2

*Image: Atan2 differs from arctan.png.* $\operatorname{atan}2(y, x)$ returns the angle $\theta$ between the positive $x$-axis and the [ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28geometry%29) from the origin to the point $(x, y)$, confined to −*π*, *π*.

*Image: Arctangent2.svg.* Graph of $\operatorname{atan2}(y, x)$ over $y / x$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Atan2"}

In [computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing) and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the [function](wikipedia://en/185427) **atan2** is the 2-[argument](wikipedia://en/32842052) [arctangent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctangent). By definition, $\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x)$ is the [angle measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_measure) (in [radians](wikipedia://en/26003), with $-\pi < \theta \leq \pi$) between the positive [$x$-axis](wikipedia://en/7706) and the [ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28geometry%29) from the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) to the point $(x,\,y)$ in the [Cartesian plane](wikipedia://en/7706). Equivalently, $\operatorname{atan2}(y, x)$ is the [argument](wikipedia://en/14699765) (also called *phase* or *angle*) of the [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $x + iy.$ (The *argument* of a function and the *argument* of a complex number, each mentioned above, should not be confused.)

:::

The $\operatorname{atan2}$ function first appeared in the programming language [Fortran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortran) in 1961. It was originally intended to return a correct and unambiguous value for the angle $\theta$ in converting from Cartesian coordinates $(x,\,y)$ to [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) $(r,\,\theta)$. If $\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x)$ and $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, then $x = r \cos \theta$ and $y = r \sin \theta.$

If $x > 0$, the desired angle measure is $\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(y,x) = \arctan\left( y / x \right).$ However, when $x < 0$, the angle $\arctan(y / x)$ is [diametrically opposite](wikipedia://en/367577) the desired angle, and $\pm\pi$ (a half [turn](wikipedia://en/855329)) must be added to place the point in the correct [quadrant](wikipedia://en/18315778).[^o2] Using the $\operatorname{atan2}$ function does away with this correction, simplifying code and mathematical formulas.

## Motivation {#Motivation}

It is common in practical applications of geometry to convert between [rectangular coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_coordinates) $(x, y)$ and [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) $(r, \theta)$ for representing points in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), for example to find the [signed angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signed_angle) representing the [orientation](wikipedia://en/2544098) of a [vector](wikipedia://en/32533) or to calculate the [argument](wikipedia://en/14699765) of a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826). The relationships between rectangular and polar coordinates can be summarized by the equations:

$$\begin{aligned} x &= r\cos\theta, & \qquad r &= {\textstyle \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} }, \\[2pt] y &= r\sin\theta, & \tan\theta &= \frac{y}{x}, \end{aligned}$$

where $\sin$, $\cos$, and $\tan$ are [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367) and $\textstyle \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ is the [Pythagorean sum](wikipedia://en/14597289) of $x$ and $y$, i.e. the [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) of a [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420) with legs $x$ and $y$.

*Image: Atan2 argument sign graph.svg.* Graph of the tangent function from $-\pi$ to $+\pi$ with the corresponding signs of $y/x$. The green arrows point to the results of $\operatorname{atan}2(-1, -1)$ and $\operatorname{atan}2(1, 1)$.

Computing $x$ and $y$ from $r$ and $\theta$ directly from these formulas is straightforward. In the other direction, though, it is helpful to provide special functions to implement the conversions. The formula to compute $r$ from $x$ and $y$ can lead to numerical overflows and underflows, avoided by the `hypot` function of many standard libraries. The computation of $\theta$ from $x$ and $y$ is more complicated, for two reasons: (1) because it could involve a division by zero and (2) because the quotient $y / x$ is equal to $(-y) / (-x)$, and therefore does not distinguish between point $(x, y)$ and the opposite point $(-x, -y)$. The ordinary single-argument [arctangent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctangent) function only returns angle measures in the interval $\left({-\tfrac12\pi}, \tfrac12\pi\right)$, so a naïve implementation of a conversion from rectangular to polar coordinates, $\theta = \arctan(y / x)$, will yield incorrect results whenever $x \le 0$.

To fully determine the direction angle from the origin given a point $(x,\,y)$ using the arctangent function, mathematical formulas or computer code must handle multiple cases; at least one for positive values of $x$ and one for negative values of $x$, and sometimes additional cases when $y$ is negative or one coordinate is zero.

To save programmers the trouble, computer [programming languages](wikipedia://en/23015) introduced the $\operatorname{atan}2$ function, at least as early as the [Fortran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortran) IV language of the 1960s.[^o4] The quantity $\operatorname{atan2}(y, x)$ is the angle measure between the $x$-axis and a ray from the origin to a point $(x,\,y)$ anywhere in the Cartesian plane. The [signs](wikipedia://en/7951270) of $x$ and $y$ are used to determine the [quadrant](wikipedia://en/18315778) of the result and select the correct branch of the [multivalued function](wikipedia://en/222367) $\operatorname{Arctan}(y/x)$.

The $\operatorname{atan}2$ function is useful in many applications involving [Euclidean vectors](wikipedia://en/32533), such as finding the direction from one point to another or converting a [rotation matrix](wikipedia://en/856005) to [Euler angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_angle).

The $\operatorname{atan}2$ function is now included in many other programming languages, and is also commonly found in mathematical formulas throughout science and engineering.

### Argument order {#Argument_order}

In 1961, Fortran introduced the $\operatorname{atan}2$ function with argument order $(y, x)$ so that the [argument](wikipedia://en/14699765) (phase angle) of a complex number is $\operatorname{arg}z = \operatorname{atan2}(\operatorname{Im}z, \operatorname{Re}z).$ This follows the left-to-right order of a fraction written $y / x,$ so that $\operatorname{atan2}(y, x) = \operatorname{atan}(y / x)$ for positive values of $x.$ However, this is the opposite of the conventional component order for complex numbers, $z = x + iy,$ or as coordinates $(\operatorname{Re}z, \operatorname{Im}z).$ See section Definition and computation.

Some other programming languages (see § Realizations of the function in common computer languages) picked the opposite order instead. For example, [Microsoft Excel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Excel) uses $\operatorname{Atan2}(x,\,y),$ [OpenOffice Calc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apache_OpenOffice#Calc) uses $\operatorname{arctan2}(x,\,y),$ and [Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematica) uses $\operatorname{ArcTan}[x,y],$ defaulting to one-argument arctangent if called with one argument.

## Definition and computation {#Definition_and_computation}

The function $\operatorname{atan}2$ computes the [principal](wikipedia://en/408225) [argument](wikipedia://en/14699765) of the [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $x + iy$, which is also the imaginary part of the principal value of the [complex logarithm](wikipedia://en/4870290). That is,

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{atan2}(y, x) &= \operatorname{arg}(x + iy)\\ &= \operatorname{Im}\operatorname{log}(x + iy). \end{aligned}$$

Adding any [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) multiple of $2\pi$ (corresponding to complete turns around the origin) gives another argument of the same complex number, but the principal argument is defined as the unique representative angle in the [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $( -\pi, \pi ]$.

In terms of the standard arctangent function, whose image is $\left({-\tfrac12\pi}, \tfrac12\pi\right)$, $\operatorname{atan}2$ can be expressed [piecewise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise-defined_function):

$$\operatorname{atan2}(y, x) = \begin{cases} \arctan\left(\frac y x\right) &\text{if } x > 0, \\[5mu] \arctan\left(\frac y x\right) + \pi &\text{if } x < 0 \text{ and } y \ge 0, \\[5mu] \arctan\left(\frac y x\right) - \pi &\text{if } x < 0 \text{ and } y < 0, \\[5mu] +\frac{\pi}{2} &\text{if } x = 0 \text{ and } y > 0, \\[5mu] -\frac{\pi}{2} &\text{if } x = 0 \text{ and } y < 0, \\[5mu] \text{undefined} &\text{if } x = 0 \text{ and } y = 0. \end{cases}$$

*Image: The principal value of the argument (-atan2- in some circles).svg.* The tangent of half an angle can be computed in terms of coordinates $x$ and $y$ and radius $r$.

In cases where the $\operatorname{atan}2$ function is unavailable or inconvenient to use, and where reducing the number of cases that must be considered is important (e.g. for [vectorized computations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Array_programming) on a GPU), it can be useful to compute $\operatorname{atan}2$ through a [tangent half-angle formula](wikipedia://en/1039889):[^o7]

$$\operatorname{atan2}(y, x) = 2 \arctan \left(\frac{y}{\textstyle \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} + x}\right),$$

except in the case that $x < 0$ and $y = 0$, when by convention atan2 is equal to $\pi$.

## Derivative {#Derivative}

As the function $\operatorname{atan}2$ is a function of two variables, it has two [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565). At points where these derivatives exist, $\operatorname{atan}2$ is, except for a constant, equal to $\arctan(y/x)$. Hence,

$$\begin{aligned} & \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\operatorname{atan2}(y,\, x) = \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \arctan\left(\frac y x \right) = -\frac{y}{x^2 + y^2}, \\[5pt] & \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\operatorname{atan2}(y,\, x) = \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \arctan\left(\frac y x \right) = \frac x {x^2 + y^2}. \end{aligned}$$

These two partial derivatives are the coordinates of the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461).

Informally representing the function $\operatorname{atan}2$ as the angle function $\theta(x, y) = \operatorname{atan}2(y, x)$ (which is only defined up to a constant) yields the following formula for the [total differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_differential):

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}\theta &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\operatorname{atan2}(y,\, x)\,\mathrm{d}x + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\operatorname{atan2}(y,\, x)\,\mathrm{d}y \\[5pt] &= -\frac{y}{x^2 + y^2}\,\mathrm{d}x + \frac{x}{x^2 + y^2}\,\mathrm{d}y. \end{aligned}$$

While the function $\operatorname{atan}2$ is discontinuous along the negative $x$-axis, reflecting the fact that angle cannot be continuously defined, this derivative is continuously defined except at the origin, reflecting the fact that [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) (and indeed local) *changes* in angle can be defined everywhere except the origin. Integrating this derivative along a path gives the total change in angle over the path, and integrating over a closed loop gives the [winding number](wikipedia://en/86113).

In the language of [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), this derivative is a [one-form](wikipedia://en/314692), and it is [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_differential_form) (its derivative is zero) but not [exact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exact_differential_form) (it is not the derivative of a 0-form, i.e., a function), and in fact it generates the first [de Rham cohomology](wikipedia://en/221530) of the [punctured plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punctured_plane). This is the most basic example of such a form, and it is fundamental in differential geometry.

The partial derivatives of $\operatorname{atan}2$ do not contain [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367), making it particularly useful in many applications (e.g. embedded systems) where trigonometric functions can be expensive to evaluate.

## Angle sum and difference identity {#Angle_sum_and_difference_identity}

The sum or difference of multiple angles to be computed by $\operatorname{atan2}$ can alternately be computed by composing them as [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826). Given two coordinate pairs $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$, their angles from the positive $x$ axis will be composed (and lengths multiplied) if they are treated as complex numbers and then multiplied together, $(x_1 + iy_1)(x_2 + iy_2)={}$$(x_1x_2 - y_1y_2) + i(y_1x_2 + x_1y_2)$. The resulting angle can be found using a single $\operatorname{atan2}$ operation, so long as the resulting angle lies in $(-\pi, \pi]$:

$$\operatorname{atan2} (y_1, x_1) \pm \operatorname{atan2} (y_2, x_2) = \operatorname{atan2} (y_1 x_2 \pm x_1 y_2, x_1 x_2 \mp y_1 y_2),$$

and likewise for more than two coordinate pairs. If the composed angle crosses the negative $x$-axis (i.e. exceeds the range $(-\pi, \pi]$), then the crossings can be counted and the appropriate integer multiple of $2\pi$ added to the final result to correct it.

This difference formula is frequently used in practice to compute the angle between two planar [vectors](wikipedia://en/32533), since the resulting angle is always in the range $(-\pi, \pi]$.

:::

## East-counterclockwise, north-clockwise and south-clockwise conventions, etc. {#East-counterclockwise,_north-clockwise_and_south-clockwise_conventions,_etc.}

The $\operatorname{atan2}$ function was originally designed for the convention in pure mathematics that can be termed *east-counterclockwise*. In practical applications, however, the *north-clockwise* and *south-clockwise* conventions are often the norm. In atmospheric sciences, for instance, the [wind direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_direction) can be calculated using the $\operatorname{atan2}$ function with the east- and north-components of the wind vector as its arguments;[^o14] the [solar azimuth angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_azimuth_angle) can be calculated similarly with the east- and north-components of the solar vector as its arguments. The wind direction is normally defined in the north-clockwise sense, and the solar azimuth angle uses both the north-clockwise and south-clockwise conventions widely.[^o15] These different conventions can be realized by swapping the positions and changing the signs of the $x$- and $y$-arguments as follows:

- $\operatorname{atan2}(y,\,x),\;\;\;\;\;$ (East-Counterclockwise Convention)
- $\operatorname{atan2}(x,\,y),\;\;\;\;\;$ (North-Clockwise Convention)
- $\operatorname{atan2}({-x},\,{-y})$. (South-Clockwise Convention)

As an example, let $x_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $y_{0}=\frac{1}{2}$, then the east-counterclockwise format gives $\operatorname{atan2}(y_{0}, x_{0})\cdot\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}=30^{\circ}$, the north-clockwise format gives $\operatorname{atan2}(x_{0}, y_{0})\cdot\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}=60^{\circ}$, and the south-clockwise format gives $\operatorname{atan2}(-x_{0}, -y_{0})\cdot\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}=-120^{\circ}$.

Changing the sign of the $x$- and/or $y$-arguments and/or swapping their positions can create 8 possible variations of the $\operatorname{atan2}$ function and they, interestingly, correspond to 8 possible definitions of the angle, namely, clockwise or counterclockwise starting from each of the 4 [cardinal directions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_direction), north, east, south and west.

## Realizations of the function in common computer languages {#Realizations_of_the_function_in_common_computer_languages}

The realization of the function differs from one computer language to another.

The $(\operatorname{Re}, \operatorname{Im})$ convention (reversed relative to the convention used in the discussion above) is used by:

- In [Microsoft Excel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Excel),[^o17] [OpenOffice.org Calc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenOffice.org_Calc), [LibreOffice Calc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LibreOffice_Calc),[^o18] [Google Spreadsheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Docs),[^o19] and [iWork Numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numbers_%28spreadsheet%29),[^o20] the 2-argument arctangent function has the two arguments in the standard sequence $(\operatorname{Re}, \operatorname{Im})$.
- In [Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematica), the form `ArcTan[''x'',''y'']` is used where the one parameter form supplies the normal arctangent. Mathematica classifies `ArcTan[0,0]` as an indeterminate expression.
- On most TI graphing calculators (excluding the [TI-85](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TI-85) and [TI-86](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TI-86)), the equivalent function is called **R►Pθ** and has the arguments $(\operatorname{Re}, \operatorname{Im})$.
- On TI-85 the $\arg$ function is called `angle(x,y)` and although it appears to take two arguments, it really only has one complex argument which is denoted by a pair of numbers: $x + iy = (x, y)$.

The $(\operatorname{Im}, \operatorname{Re})$ convention is used by:

- The C function `atan2`, and most other computer implementations, are designed to reduce the effort of transforming cartesian to polar coordinates and so always define `atan2(0, 0)`. On implementations without [signed zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signed_zero), or when given positive zero arguments, it is normally defined as 0. It will always return a value in the range −*π*, *π* rather than raising an error or returning a [NaN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NaN) (Not a Number).
- In [Common Lisp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Lisp), where optional arguments exist, the `atan` function allows one to optionally supply the $x$ coordinate: `(atan ''y'' ''x'')`.[^o21]
- In [Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_%28programming_language%29), the situation is similar to Common Lisp: instead of `atan2`, the language has a one-parameter and a two-parameter form for `atan`.[^o22] However, it has many more than two methods, to allow for aggressive optimisation at [compile time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compile_time).[^o23]
- [Mathcad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathcad) uses the argument order `atan2(x, y)`. `atan2(0, 0)` is undefined.[^o24]
- For systems implementing [signed zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signed_zero), [infinities](wikipedia://en/20110824), or [Not a Number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Not_a_Number) (for example, [IEEE floating point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_floating_point)), it is common to implement reasonable extensions that may extend the range of values produced to include $-\pi$ and −0 when $y = -0$. These also may return NaN or raise an exception when given a NaN argument.
- In the [Intel x86 Architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X86) [assembler code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assembler_code), `atan2` is known as the `FPATAN` (floating-point partial arctangent) instruction.[^o25] It can deal with infinities and results lie in the closed interval −*π*, *π*, e.g. `atan2(∞, ''x'')` = $+\pi/2$ for finite $x$. Particularly, `FPATAN` is defined when both arguments are zero:This definition is related to the concept of [signed zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signed_zero).
- In mathematical writings other than [source code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source_code), such as in books and articles, the notations **Arctan**[^o26] and **Tan<sup>−1</sup>**[^o27] have been utilized; these are capitalized variants of the [regular](wikipedia://en/374220#Notation) arctan and tan<sup>−1</sup>. This usage is consistent with the [complex argument notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_argument#Notation), such that $\operatorname{Atan}(y, x) = \operatorname{Arg}(x + iy)$.
- On [HP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hewlett-Packard) calculators, treat the coordinates as a complex number and then take the `ARG`. Or `<< C->R ARG >> 'ATAN2' STO`.
- On scientific calculators, the function can often be calculated as the angle given when $(x, y)$ is converted from [rectangular coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_coordinates) to [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates).
- Systems supporting symbolic mathematics normally return an undefined value for $\operatorname{atan}2(0, 0)$ or otherwise signal that an abnormal condition has arisen.
- The free math library FDLIBM (Freely Distributable LIBM) available from [netlib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netlib) has source code showing how it implements `atan2`, including handling the various IEEE exceptional values.
- For systems without a hardware multiplier, the function $\operatorname{atan}2$ can be implemented in a numerically reliable manner by the [CORDIC](wikipedia://en/859590) method. Thus, implementations of $\operatorname{atan}(y)$ will probably choose to compute $\operatorname{atan}2(y, 1)$.
