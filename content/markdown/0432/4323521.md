# J-integral

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="J-integral"}

The **J-integral** represents a way to calculate the [strain energy release rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_energy_release_rate), or work ([energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy)) per unit fracture surface area, in a material.[^o2] The theoretical concept of J-integral was developed in 1967 by G. P. Cherepanov[^o3] and independently in 1968 by [James R. Rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_R._Rice),[^o4] who showed that an energetic [contour path integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contour_integral) (called *J*) was independent of the path around a [crack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fracture).

:::

Experimental methods were developed using the integral that allowed the measurement of critical fracture properties in sample sizes that are too small for Linear Elastic [Fracture Mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fracture_Mechanics) (LEFM) to be valid.[^o5] These experiments allow the determination of [fracture toughness](wikipedia://en/3239291) from the critical value of fracture energy *J*<sub>Ic</sub>, which defines the point at which large-scale [plastic](wikipedia://en/240123) yielding during propagation takes place under mode I loading.[^o2][^o6]

The J-integral is equal to the [strain energy release rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_energy_release_rate) for a crack in a body subjected to [monotonic](wikipedia://en/48260) loading.[^o7] This is generally true, under quasistatic conditions, only for [linear elastic](wikipedia://en/301429) materials. For materials that experience small-scale [yielding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yield_%28engineering%29) at the crack tip, *J* can be used to compute the energy release rate under special circumstances such as monotonic loading in [mode III](wikipedia://en/843211) ([antiplane shear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiplane_shear)). The strain energy release rate can also be computed from *J* for pure power-law hardening [plastic](wikipedia://en/240123) materials that undergo small-scale yielding at the crack tip.

The quantity *J* is not path-independent for monotonic [mode I](wikipedia://en/843211) and [mode II](wikipedia://en/843211) loading of elastic-plastic materials, so only a contour very close to the crack tip gives the energy release rate. Also, Rice showed that *J* is path-independent in plastic materials when there is no non-proportional loading. Unloading is a special case of this, but non-proportional plastic loading also invalidates the path-independence. Such non-proportional loading is the reason for the path-dependence for the in-plane loading modes on elastic-plastic materials.

## Two-dimensional J-integral {#Two-dimensional_J-integral}

*Image: Integrale-J 2D.png.* Figure 1. Line J-integral around a notch in two dimensions.

The two-dimensional J-integral was originally defined as[^o4] (see Figure 1 for an illustration)

$$J := \int_\Gamma \left(W~\mathrm{d}x_2 - \mathbf{t}\cdot\cfrac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial x_1}~\mathrm{d}s\right) = \int_\Gamma \left(W~\mathrm{d}x_2 - t_i\,\cfrac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_1}~\mathrm{d}s\right)$$

where *W*(*x*<sub>1</sub>,*x*<sub>2</sub>) is the strain energy density, *x*<sub>1</sub>,*x*<sub>2</sub> are the coordinate directions, **t** = \[**σ**\]**n** is the [surface traction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_%28physics%29) vector, **n** is the normal to the curve Γ, \[**σ**\] is the [Cauchy stress tensor](wikipedia://en/3323565), and **u** is the [displacement vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_vector). The strain energy density is given by

$$W = \int_0^{[\varepsilon]} [\boldsymbol{\sigma}]:d[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}] ~;~~ [\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}] = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathbf{u}+(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathbf{u})^T\right] ~.$$

The J-integral around a crack tip is frequently expressed in a more general form[^o9] (and in [index notation](wikipedia://en/195407)) as

$$J_i := \lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0} \int_{\Gamma_\varepsilon} \left(W(\Gamma) n_i - n_j\sigma_{jk}~\cfrac{\partial u_k(\Gamma, x_i)}{\partial x_i}\right) \, d\Gamma$$

where $J_i$ is the component of the J-integral for crack opening in the $x_i$ direction and $\varepsilon$ is a small region around the crack tip. Using [Green's theorem](wikipedia://en/243334) we can show that this integral is zero when the boundary $\Gamma$ is closed and encloses a region that contains no [singularities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity) and is [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected). If the faces of the crack do not have any [surface tractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_traction) on them then the J-integral is also [path independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_independence).

Rice also showed that the value of the J-integral represents the energy release rate for planar crack growth. The J-integral was developed because of the difficulties involved in computing the [stress](wikipedia://en/228107) close to a crack in a nonlinear [elastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_%28physics%29) or elastic-[plastic](wikipedia://en/240123) material. Rice showed that if monotonic loading was assumed (without any plastic unloading) then the J-integral could be used to compute the energy release rate of plastic materials too.

{| class="toccolours collapsible collapsed" width="80%" style="text-align:left"

!Proof that the J-integral is zero over a closed path |- |To show the path independence of the J-integral, we first have to show that the value of $J$ is zero over a closed contour in a simply connected domain. Let us just consider the expression for $J_1$ which is

$$J_1 := \int_\Gamma \left(W n_1 - n_j\sigma_{jk}~\cfrac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_1}\right) \, d\Gamma$$

We can write this as

$$J_1 = \int_\Gamma \left(W \delta_{1j} - \sigma_{jk}~\cfrac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_1}\right)n_j \, d\Gamma$$

From [Green's theorem](wikipedia://en/243334) (or the two-dimensional [divergence theorem](wikipedia://en/151864)) we have

$$\int_{\Gamma} f_j~n_j~d\Gamma = \int_A \cfrac{\partial f_j}{\partial x_j}~dA$$

Using this result we can express $J_1$ as

$$\begin{aligned} J_1 & = \int_A \cfrac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\left(W \delta_{1j} - \sigma_{jk}~\cfrac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_1}\right) dA \\ & = \int_A \left[\cfrac{\partial W}{\partial x_1} - \cfrac{\partial\sigma_{jk}}{\partial x_j}~\cfrac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_1} - \sigma_{jk}~\cfrac{\partial^2 u_k}{\partial x_1 \partial x_j}\right]~dA \end{aligned}$$

where $A$ is the area enclosed by the contour $\Gamma$. Now, if there are **no body forces** present, equilibrium (conservation of linear momentum) requires that

$$\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma} = \mathbf{0} \qquad \implies \qquad \cfrac{\partial\sigma_{jk}}{\partial x_j} = 0 ~.$$

Also,

$$[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}] = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathbf{u}+(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathbf{u})^T\right] \qquad \implies \qquad \varepsilon_{jk} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\cfrac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_j} + \cfrac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_k}\right) ~.$$

Therefore,

$$\sigma_{jk}\cfrac{\partial\varepsilon_{jk}}{\partial x_1} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\sigma_{jk}\cfrac{\partial^2 u_k}{\partial x_1 \partial x_j} + \sigma_{jk}\cfrac{\partial^2 u_j}{\partial x_1 \partial x_k}\right)$$

From the balance of angular momentum we have $\sigma_{jk} = \sigma_{kj}$. Hence,

$$\sigma_{jk}\cfrac{\partial\varepsilon_{jk}}{\partial x_1} = \sigma_{jk}\cfrac{\partial^2 u_j}{\partial x_1 \partial x_k}$$

The J-integral may then be written as

$$J_1 = \int_A \left[\cfrac{\partial W}{\partial x_1} - \sigma_{jk}~\cfrac{\partial\varepsilon_{jk}}{\partial x_1}\right]~dA$$

Now, for an elastic material the stress can be derived from the stored energy function $W$ using

$$\sigma_{jk} = \cfrac{\partial W}{\partial\varepsilon_{jk}}$$

Then, if the elastic modulus tensor is homogeneous, using the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) of differentiation,

$$\sigma_{jk}~\cfrac{\partial\varepsilon_{jk}}{\partial x_1} = \cfrac{\partial W}{\partial\varepsilon_{jk}}~\cfrac{\partial\varepsilon_{jk}}{\partial x_1} = \cfrac{\partial W}{\partial x_1}$$

Therefore, we have $J_1 = 0$ for a closed contour enclosing a simply connected region without any elastic inhomogeneity, such as voids and cracks. |}

{| class="toccolours collapsible collapsed" width="80%" style="text-align:left"

!Proof that the J-integral is path-independent |- |

*Image: J integralPathIndep.png.* Figure 2. Integration paths around a notch in two dimensions.

Consider the contour $\Gamma = \Gamma_1 + \Gamma^{+} + \Gamma_2 + \Gamma^{-}$. Since this contour is closed and encloses a simply connected region, the J-integral around the contour is zero, i.e.

$$J = J_{(1)} + J^{+} - J_{(2)} - J^{-} = 0$$

assuming that counterclockwise integrals around the crack tip have positive sign. Now, since the crack surfaces are parallel to the $x_1$ axis, the normal component $n_1 = 0$ on these surfaces. Also, since the crack surfaces are traction free, $t_k = 0$. Therefore,

$$J^{+} = J^{-} = \int_{\Gamma} \left(W n_1 - t_k~\cfrac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_1}\right) \, d\Gamma = 0$$

Therefore,

$$J_{(1)} = J_{(2)}$$

and the J-integral is path independent. |}

## J-integral and fracture toughness {#J-integral_and_fracture_toughness}

For isotropic, perfectly brittle, linear elastic materials, the J-integral can be directly related to the [fracture toughness](wikipedia://en/3239291) if the crack extends straight ahead with respect to its original orientation.[^o7]

For plane strain, under [Mode I](wikipedia://en/843211) loading conditions, this relation is

$$J_{\rm Ic} = G_{\rm Ic} = K_{\rm Ic}^2 \left(\frac{1-\nu^2}{E}\right)$$

where $G_{\rm Ic}$ is the critical strain energy release rate, $K_{\rm Ic}$ is the fracture toughness in Mode I loading, $\nu$ is the Poisson's ratio, and *E* is the [Young's modulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young's_modulus) of the material.

For [Mode II](wikipedia://en/843211) loading, the relation between the J-integral and the mode II fracture toughness ($K_{\rm IIc}$) is

$$J_{\rm IIc} = G_{\rm IIc} = K_{\rm IIc}^2 \left[\frac{1-\nu^2}{E}\right]$$

For [Mode III](wikipedia://en/843211) loading, the relation is

$$J_{\rm IIIc} = G_{\rm IIIc} = K_{\rm IIIc}^2 \left(\frac{1+\nu}{E}\right)$$

## Elastic-plastic materials and the HRR solution {#Elastic-plastic_materials_and_the_HRR_solution}

*Image: HRRSingularity plain.svg.* Paths for J-integral calculation around a crack in a two-dimensional elastic-plastic material.

Hutchinson, Rice and Rosengren [^o13][^o14] subsequently showed that J characterizes the [singular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity) stress and strain fields at the tip of a crack in nonlinear (power law hardening) elastic-plastic materials where the size of the plastic zone is small compared with the crack length. Hutchinson used a material [constitutive law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutive_law) of the form suggested by [W. Ramberg and W. Osgood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramberg-Osgood_relationship):[^o15]

$$\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_y}=\frac{\sigma}{\sigma_y}+\alpha\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sigma_y}\right)^n$$

where *σ* is the [stress](wikipedia://en/3323565) in uniaxial tension, *σ*<sub>y</sub> is a [yield stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yield_%28engineering%29), *ε* is the [strain](wikipedia://en/184011), and *ε*<sub>y</sub> = *σ*<sub>y</sub>/*E* is the corresponding yield strain. The quantity *E* is the elastic [Young's modulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young's_modulus) of the material. The model is parametrized by *α*, a dimensionless constant characteristic of the material, and *n*, the coefficient of [work hardening](wikipedia://en/1590842). This model is applicable only to situations where the stress increases monotonically, the stress components remain approximately in the same ratios as loading progresses (proportional loading), and there is no [unloading](wikipedia://en/40132737).

If a far-field tensile stress *σ*<sub>far</sub> is applied to the body shown in the adjacent figure, the J-integral around the path Γ<sub>1</sub> (chosen to be completely inside the elastic zone) is given by

$$J_{\Gamma_1} = \pi\,(\sigma_{\text{far}})^2 \,.$$

Since the total integral around the crack vanishes and the contributions along the surface of the crack are zero, we have

$$J_{\Gamma_1} = -J_{\Gamma_2} \,.$$

If the path Γ<sub>2</sub> is chosen such that it is inside the fully plastic domain, Hutchinson showed that

$$J_{\Gamma_2} = -\alpha\,K^{n+1}\,r^{(n+1)(s-2)+1}\,I$$

where *K* is a stress amplitude, (*r*,*θ*) is a [polar coordinate system](wikipedia://en/25120) with origin at the crack tip, *s* is a constant determined from an asymptotic expansion of the stress field around the crack, and *I* is a dimensionless integral. The relation between the J-integrals around Γ<sub>1</sub> and Γ<sub>2</sub> leads to the constraint

$$s = \frac{2n+1}{n+1}$$

and an expression for *K* in terms of the far-field stress

$$K = \left(\frac{\beta\,\pi}{\alpha\,I}\right)^{\frac{1}{n+1}}\,(\sigma_{\text{far}})^{\frac{2}{n+1}}$$

where *β* = 1 for [plane stress](wikipedia://en/6517456) and *β* = 1 − *ν*<sup>2</sup> for [plane strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_strain) (*ν* is the [Poisson's ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson's_ratio)).

The asymptotic expansion of the stress field and the above ideas can be used to determine the stress and strain fields in terms of the J-integral:

$$\sigma_{ij}= \sigma_y \left (\frac{EJ}{r\,\alpha \sigma_y^2 I} \right )^{{1}\over{n+1}}\tilde{\sigma}_{ij}(n,\theta)$$

$$\varepsilon_{ij}=\frac{\alpha \varepsilon_y}{E} \left (\frac{EJ}{r\,\alpha \sigma_y^2 I} \right )^{{n}\over{n+1}}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{ij}(n,\theta)$$

where $\tilde{\sigma}_{ij}$ and $\tilde{\varepsilon}_{ij}$ are dimensionless functions.

These expressions indicate that *J* can be interpreted as a plastic analog to the [stress intensity factor](wikipedia://en/2547532) (*K*) that is used in linear elastic fracture mechanics, i.e., we can use a criterion such as *J* > *J*<sub>Ic</sub> as a crack growth criterion.
