# Irving Lorge

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Irving Lorge"}

**Irving Daniel Lorge** (April 19, 1905 – January 23, 1961) was an American psychologist known for his work in [psychometrics](wikipedia://en/24982). His research focused on a wide variety of topics,[^o8] including the measurement of [giftedness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giftedness), [human intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_intelligence), and [readability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Readability).[^o3] While working at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University)'s [Teachers College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teachers_College,_Columbia_University) with his mentor [Edward L. Thorndike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_L._Thorndike), he helped develop what became known as the Lorge-Thorndike Intelligence Tests.[^o4]

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Lorge was born on April 19, 1905, in [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City).[^o6] He joined the faculty at [Teachers College, Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teachers_College,_Columbia_University) in 1927 to work with [Edward L. Thorndike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_L._Thorndike). In 1938, he became Associate Professor of Education at Teachers College, and he continued to work there for the rest of his life, save for two years working as a consultant to the [United States federal government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_federal_government) during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II).[^o6][^o8] Specifically, from 1942 to 1944, he was a consultant to the special training division of the [United States Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army), where he significantly changed the way in which the Army trained illiterate soldiers.[^o7] He was promoted to Professor of Education at Teachers College in 1946, and became executive officer in the Institute of Psychological Research there in 1947; he held both of these positions until his death. He was a founder of the [Psychometric Society](wikipedia://en/20788500), and later served as its president. He died unexpectedly on January 23, 1961, of a [heart attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_attack).[^o6][^o8]
