# Stokes' paradox

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Stokes' paradox"}

In the science of [fluid flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_flow), **Stokes' paradox** is the phenomenon that there can be no creeping flow of a [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid) around a disk in two dimensions; or, equivalently, the fact there is no non-trivial steady-state solution for the [Stokes equations](wikipedia://en/2047298) around an infinitely long cylinder. This is opposed to the 3-dimensional case, where Stokes' method provides a solution to the problem of flow around a sphere.[^o2][^o3]

:::

Stokes' paradox was resolved by [Carl Wilhelm Oseen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Wilhelm_Oseen) in 1910, by introducing the [Oseen equations](wikipedia://en/19554842) which improve upon the Stokes equations – by adding [convective acceleration](wikipedia://en/48395#Convective_acceleration).

## Derivation {#Derivation}

The velocity vector $\mathbf{u}$ of the [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid) may be written in terms of the [stream function](wikipedia://en/218091) $\psi$ as

$$\mathbf{u} = \left(\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}, - \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}\right).$$

The stream function in a Stokes flow problem, $\psi$ satisfies the [biharmonic equation](wikipedia://en/3010562).[^o5] By regarding the $(x,y)$-plane as the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), the problem may be dealt with using methods of [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759). In this approach, $\psi$ is either the [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part) or [imaginary part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_part) of

$\bar{z} f(z) + g(z)$.[^o6]

Here $z = x + iy$, where $i$ is the [imaginary](wikipedia://en/14554) unit, $\bar{z} = x - iy$, and $f(z), g(z)$ are [holomorphic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_functions) outside of the disk. We will take the real part [without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000). Now the function $u$, defined by $u = u_x + iu_y$ is introduced. $u$ can be written as $u = -2i \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial \bar{z}}$, or $\frac{1}{2} iu = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial \bar{z}}$ (using the [Wirtinger derivatives](wikipedia://en/28479408)). This is calculated to be equal to

$$\frac{1}{2} iu = f(z) + z \bar{f \prime}(z) + \bar{g \prime}(z).$$

Without loss of generality, the disk may be assumed to be the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738), consisting of all [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) *z* of [absolute value](wikipedia://en/5826#Polar_form) smaller or equal to 1.

The [boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_conditions) are:

$$\lim_{z \to \infty} u = 1,$$

$$u = 0,$$

whenever $|z| = 1$,[^o2][^o7] and by representing the functions $f, g$ as [Laurent series](wikipedia://en/61213):[^o8]

$$f(z) = \sum_{n = -\infty}^\infty f_n z^n, \quad g(z) = \sum_{n = -\infty}^\infty g_n z^n,$$

the first condition implies $f_n = 0, g_n = 0$ for all $n \geq 2$.

Using the polar form of $z$ results in $z^n = r^n e^{in\theta}, \bar{z}^n = r^n e^{-in \theta}$. After deriving the series form of *u*, substituting this into it along with $r = 1$, and changing some indices, the second boundary condition translates to

$$\sum_{n = -\infty}^\infty e^{in \theta} \left( f_n + (2 - n) \bar{f}_{2-n} + (1 - n) \bar{g}_{1-n} \right) = 0.$$

Since the complex trigonometric functions $e^{in \theta}$ compose a [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) set, it follows that all coefficients in the series are zero. Examining these conditions for every $n$ after taking into account the condition at infinity shows that $f$ and $g$ are necessarily of the form

$$f(z) = az + b, \quad g(z) = -bz + c,$$

where $a$ is an imaginary number (opposite to its own [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918)), and $b$ and $c$ are complex numbers. Substituting this into $u$ gives the result that $u = 0$ globally, compelling both $u_x$ and $u_y$ to be zero. Therefore, there can be no motion – the only solution is that the cylinder is at rest relative to all points of the fluid.

## Resolution {#Resolution}

The paradox is caused by the limited validity of Stokes' approximation, as explained in [Oseen's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Wilhelm_Oseen) criticism: the validity of Stokes' equations relies on [Reynolds number](wikipedia://en/23868856) being small, and this condition cannot hold for arbitrarily large distances $r$.[^o10][^o3]

A correct solution for a cylinder was derived using [Oseen's equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oseen's_equations), and the same equations lead to an improved approximation of the [drag force on a sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stokes'_law).[^o11][^o12]

## Unsteady-state flow around a circular cylinder {#Unsteady-state_flow_around_a_circular_cylinder}

On the contrary to **Stokes' paradox**, there exists the unsteady-state solution of the same problem which models a fluid flow moving around a circular cylinder with Reynolds number being small. This solution can be given by explicit formula in terms of [vorticity](wikipedia://en/166010) of the flow's [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641).

::: {#o15 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formula of the Stokes Flow around a circular cylinder"}

### Formula of the Stokes Flow around a circular cylinder {#Formula_of_the_Stokes_Flow_around_a_circular_cylinder}

The vorticity of [Stokes' flow](wikipedia://en/2047298) is given by the following relation:[^o16]

$$w_k(t,r) = W^{-1}_{|k|,|k|-1} \left [ e^{-\lambda^2 t} W_{|k|,|k|-1} [w_k(0,\cdot)](\lambda) \right ](t,r).$$

Here $w_k(t,r)$ - are the Fourier coefficients of the [vorticity's](wikipedia://en/166010) expansion by polar angle which are defined on $(r_0,\infty)$, $r_0$ - radius of the cylinder, $W_{|k|,|k|-1}$, $W^{-1}_{|k|,|k|-1}$ are the direct and inverse special Weber's transforms,[^o17] and initial function for vorticity $w_k(0,r)$ satisfies no-slip boundary condition.

Special Weber's transform has a non-trivial kernel, but from the [no-slip condition](wikipedia://en/3855724) follows orthogonality of the vorticity flow to the kernel.[^o16]

:::

### Derivation {#Derivation}

#### Special Weber's transform {#Special_Weber's_transform}

Special Weber's transform[^o17] is an important tool in solving problems of the [hydrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodynamics). It is defined for $k\in \mathbb{R}$ as

$$W_{k,k- 1}[f](\lambda) = \int_{r_0}^\infty \frac{J_{k}(\lambda s)Y_{k- 1}(\lambda r_0) - Y_{k}(\lambda s)J_{k- 1}(\lambda r_0)} {\sqrt{J_{k- 1}^2(\lambda r_0) + Y_{k- 1}^2(\lambda r_0)}} f(s) s ds,$$

where $J_k$, $Y_k$ are the [Bessel functions](wikipedia://en/4700) of the first and second kind[^o20] respectively. For $k>1$ it has a non-trivial kernel[^o21][^o16] which consists of the functions $C/r^k \in \ker(W_{k,k-1})$.

The inverse transform is given by the formula

$$W^{-1}_{k,k- 1}[\hat f](r) = \int_{0}^\infty \frac {J_{k}(\lambda r)Y_{k- 1}(\lambda r_0) - Y_{k}(\lambda s)J_{k- 1}(\lambda r_0)}{\sqrt{J_{k- 1}^2(\lambda r_0) + Y_{k- 1}^2(\lambda r_0)}} \hat f (\lambda) \lambda d\lambda.$$

Due to non-triviality of the kernel, the inversion identity

$$f(r) = W^{-1}_{k,k-1}\left [W_{k,k-1} [f] \right ](r)$$

is valid if $k\leq 1$. Also it is valid in the case of $k> 1$ but only for functions, which are orthogonal to the kernel of $W_{k,k-1}$ in $L_2(r_0,\infty)$ with infinitesimal element $rdr$:

$$\int_{r_0}^\infty \frac 1{r^k} f(r) r dr = 0,~k>1.$$

::: {#o23 .statement cue=named-heading name="No-slip condition and Biot–Savart law"}

#### No-slip condition and Biot–Savart law {#No-slip_condition_and_Biot–Savart_law}

In exterior of the disc of radius $r_0$ $B_{r_0}=\{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^2,~\vert \mathbf{x} \vert > r_0 \}$ the [Biot-Savart law](wikipedia://en/75110)

$$\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}) =\frac 1{2\pi} \int_{B_{r_0}} \frac{(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^\perp}{\vert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\vert^2} w(\mathbf{y}) \operatorname{d\mathbf{y}} + \mathbf{v}_\infty,$$

restores the velocity field $\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})$ which is induced by the vorticity $w(\mathbf{x})$ with zero-circularity and given constant velocity $\mathbf{v}_\infty$ at infinity.

No-slip condition for $\mathbf{x}\in S_{r_0}=\{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^2,~\vert \mathbf{x} \vert = r_0 \}$

$$\frac 1{2\pi} \int_{B_{r_0}} \frac{(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^\perp}{\vert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\vert^2} w(\mathbf{y}) \operatorname{d\mathbf{y}} + \mathbf{v}_\infty =0$$

leads to the relations for $k\in \mathbf{Z}$:

$$\int_{r_0}^\infty r^{-\vert k \vert+1}w_k(r)dr = d_k,$$

where $d_k=\delta_{\vert k \vert,1} (v_{\infty,y} + i k v_{\infty,x}),$ $\delta_{\vert k \vert,1}$ is the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890), $v_{\infty,x}$, $v_{\infty,y}$ are the cartesian coordinates of $\mathbf{v}_\infty$.

In particular, from the no-slip condition follows orthogonality the vorticity to the kernel of the Weber's transform $W_{k,k- 1}$:

$$\int_{r_0}^\infty r^{-\vert k \vert+1}w_k(r)dr = 0 ~for~ |k|>1.$$

:::

#### Vorticity flow and its boundary condition {#Vorticity_flow_and_its_boundary_condition}

Vorticity $w(t,\mathbf{x})$ for Stokes flow satisfies to the [vorticity equation](wikipedia://en/166365)

$$\frac{\partial w(t,\mathbf{x})}{\partial t} - \Delta w = 0,$$

or in terms of the Fourier coefficients in the expansion by polar angle

$$\frac{\partial w_k(t,r)}{\partial t} - \Delta w_k = 0,$$

where

$$\Delta_k w_k(t,r) = \frac 1r \frac {\partial}{\partial r}\left(r \frac {\partial}{\partial r}w_k(t,r)\right) - \frac{k^2}{r^2} w_k(t,r).$$

From no-slip condition follows

$$\frac d{dt} \int_{r_0}^\infty r^{-\vert k \vert+1}w_k(t,r)dr = 0.$$

Finally, integrating by parts, we obtain the [Robin boundary condition](wikipedia://en/9945920) for the vorticity:

$$\int_{r_0}^\infty s^{-|k|+1} \Delta_k w_k(t,r)dr = - r_0^{-|k|}\left(r_0 \frac{\partial w_k(t,r)}{\partial r}\Big|_{r=r_0} + |k| w_k(t,r_0) \right ) = 0.$$

Then the solution of the boundary-value problem can be expressed via Weber's integral above.

### Remark {#Remark}

Formula for vorticity can give another explanation of the Stokes' Paradox. The functions $\frac C{r^k} \in ker(\Delta_k),~k>1$ belong to the kernel of $\Delta_k$ and generate the stationary solutions of the vorticity equation with Robin-type boundary condition. From the arguments above any Stokes' vorticity flow with no-slip boundary condition must be orthogonal to the obtained stationary solutions. That is only possible for $w\equiv 0$.
