# GF(2)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="GF(2)"}

$\operatorname{GF}(2)$ (also denoted $\mathbb F_2$, $\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z}$ or $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$) is the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) with two elements.[^o2]

:::

$\operatorname{GF}(2)$ is the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) with the smallest possible number of elements, and is unique if the [additive identity](wikipedia://en/4178225) and the [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity) are denoted respectively 0 and 1, as usual.

The elements of $\operatorname{GF}(2)$ may be identified with the two possible values of a [bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit) and to the [Boolean values](wikipedia://en/3954077) *true* and *false*. It follows that $\operatorname{GF}(2)$ is fundamental and ubiquitous in [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) and its [logical](wikipedia://en/19636) foundations.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

GF(2) is the unique field with two elements. Its [additive](wikipedia://en/4178225) and [multiplicative identities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity) are respectively denoted 0 and 1.

Its addition is defined as the usual addition of integers but modulo 2 and corresponds to the table below:

::: {#o5 .table cue=table}

| \+ | 0 | 1 |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 1 |
| 1 | 1 | 0 |

:::

If the elements of GF(2) are seen as Boolean values, then the addition is the same as that of the [logical XOR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_XOR) operation. Since each element equals its [opposite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposite_%28mathematics%29), subtraction is thus the same operation as addition.

The multiplication of GF(2) is the usual multiplication (see the table below), and on Boolean variables corresponds to the [logical AND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_AND) operation.

::: {#o6 .table cue=table}

| × | 0 | 1 |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 0 | 1 |

:::

GF(2) can be identified with the field of the [integers modulo 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers_modulo_a_prime), that is, the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) of the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) **Z** by the [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) 2**Z** of all [even numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_number): GF(2) = **Z**/2**Z**.

Notations $\mathbf{Z}_{2}$ and $\mathbb Z_2$ may be encountered although they can be confused with the notation of [2-adic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_integer).

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Because GF(2) is a field, many of the familiar properties of number systems such as the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) and [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) are retained:

- addition has an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) (0) and an inverse for every element;
- multiplication has an identity element (1) and an inverse for every element but 0;
- addition and multiplication are [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) and [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative);
- multiplication is [distributive](wikipedia://en/103118) over addition.

Properties that are not familiar from the real numbers include:

- every element *x* of GF(2) satisfies $x + x = 0$ and therefore $-x = x$; this means that the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) of GF(2) is 2;
- every element *x* of GF(2) satisfies $x^{2} = x$ (i.e. is *[idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent)* with respect to multiplication); this is an instance of [Fermat's little theorem](wikipedia://en/53453). GF(2) is the *only* field with this property (Proof: if $x^{2} = x$, then either $x = 0$ or $x \ne 0$. In the latter case, *x* must have a multiplicative inverse, in which case dividing both sides by *x* gives $x = 1$. All larger fields contain elements other than 0 and 1, and those elements cannot satisfy this property).

:::

::: {#o10 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Because of the algebraic properties above, many familiar and powerful tools of mathematics work in $\operatorname{GF}(2)$ just as well as other fields. For example, matrix operations, including [matrix inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_inversion), can be applied to matrices with elements in $\operatorname{GF}(2)$ (*see* [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541)).

Any [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $(V, +)$ with the property $v + v = 0$ for every $v$ in $V$ is necessarily [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) and can be turned into a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over $\operatorname{GF}(2)$ in a natural fashion, by defining $0v = 0$ and $1v = v$ for all $v$ in $V$. This vector space will have a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420), implying that the number of elements of $V$ must be a power of 2 (or infinite).

In modern [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer), data are represented with [bit strings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit_string) of a fixed length, called *[machine words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_word)*. These are endowed with the structure of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over $\operatorname{GF}(2)$. The addition of this vector space is the [bitwise operation](wikipedia://en/264399) called [XOR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XOR) (exclusive or). The [bitwise AND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitwise_AND) is another operation on this vector space, which makes it a [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959), a structure that underlies all [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science). These spaces can also be augmented with a multiplication operation that makes them into a field $\operatorname{GF}(2^{n})$, but the multiplication operation cannot be a bitwise operation. When $n$ is itself a power of two, the multiplication operation can be [nim-multiplication](wikipedia://en/164501); alternatively, for any $n$, one can use multiplication of polynomials over $\operatorname{GF}(2)$ modulo a [irreducible polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) (as for instance for the field $\operatorname{GF}(2^{8})$ in the description of the [Advanced Encryption Standard](wikipedia://en/1260) cipher).

[Vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) and [polynomial rings](wikipedia://en/373065) over $\operatorname{GF}(2)$ are widely used in [coding theory](wikipedia://en/321869), and in particular in [error correcting codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_correcting_code) and modern [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432). For example, many common error correcting codes (such as [BCH codes](wikipedia://en/40779)) are [linear codes](wikipedia://en/1866743) over $\operatorname{GF}(2)$ (codes defined from vector spaces over $\operatorname{GF}(2)$), or [polynomial codes](wikipedia://en/16259862) (codes defined as [quotients](wikipedia://en/184635) of polynomial rings over $\operatorname{GF}(2)$).

:::

## Algebraic closure {#Algebraic_closure}

Like any field, GF(2) has an [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129). This is a field *F* which contains GF(2) as a [subfield](wikipedia://en/11634), which is [algebraic](wikipedia://en/2125) over GF(2) (i.e. every element of *F* is a root of a polynomial with coefficients in GF(2)), and which is [algebraically closed](wikipedia://en/1018) (any non-constant polynomial with coefficients in *F* has a root in *F*). The field *F* is uniquely determined by these properties, [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) a [field automorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_automorphism) (i.e. essentially up to the notation of its elements).

*F* is countable and contains a single copy of each of the finite fields GF(2<sup>*n*</sup>); the copy of GF(2<sup>*n*</sup>) is contained in the copy of GF(2<sup>*m*</sup>) if and only if *n* divides *m.* The field *F* is countable and is the union of all these finite fields.

Conway proved that *F* is isomorphic to the field of ordinals below $\omega^{\omega^\omega}$, with the addition and multiplication operations defined by [nimber addition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nimber_addition) and [nimber multiplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nimber_multiplication) respectively. [^o12] The addition in this field is simple to perform, and [Lenstra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lenstra) has shown that the multiplication can also be performed efficiently.[^o13]
