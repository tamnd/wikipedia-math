# EFF DES cracker

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="EFF DES cracker"}

*Image: Board300.jpg.* The [EFF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation)'s US\$250,000 DES cracking machine contained 1,856 custom chips and could [brute force](wikipedia://en/53784) a DES [key](wikipedia://en/53039) in a matter of days — the photo shows a two-sided DES Cracker circuit board fitted with 64 Deep Crack chips

*Image: Chip300.jpg.* The EFF's DES cracker "Deep Crack" custom microchip

In [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), the **EFF DES cracker** (nicknamed "**Deep Crack**") is a machine built by the [Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation) (EFF) in 1998, to perform a [brute force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brute_force_attack) search of the [Data Encryption Standard](wikipedia://en/7978) (DES) cipher's [key space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_space_%28cryptography%29) – that is, to decrypt an encrypted message by trying every possible key. The aim in doing this was to prove that the [key size](wikipedia://en/5749) of DES was not sufficient to be secure.

:::

Detailed technical data of this machine, including [block diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_diagram), [circuit schematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circuit_schematics), [VHDL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VHDL) source code of the custom chips and its [emulator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emulator), have all been published in the book *Cracking DES*. Its [public domain license](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_domain_license) allows [everyone to freely copy, use, or modify its design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-source_hardware). To avoid the [export regulation on cryptography by the US Government](wikipedia://en/826003), the [source code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source_code) was distributed not in electronic form but as a hardcopy book, of which the open publication is protected by the [First Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Amendment). Machine-readable metadata is provided to facilitate the transcription of the code into a computer via [OCR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_Character_Recognition) by readers.[^o2]

## Background {#Background}

DES uses a 56-bit [key](wikipedia://en/5749), meaning that there are 2<sup>56</sup> possible keys under which a message can be encrypted. This is exactly 72,057,594,037,927,936, or approximately 72 [quadrillion](wikipedia://en/260914#1015) possible keys. One of the major criticisms of DES, when proposed in 1975, was that the key size was too short. [Martin Hellman](wikipedia://en/422387) and [Whitfield Diffie](wikipedia://en/427776) of [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University) estimated that a machine fast enough to test that many keys in a day would have cost about \$20 million in 1976, an affordable sum to national intelligence agencies such as the US [National Security Agency](wikipedia://en/21939).[^o4] Subsequent advances in the price/performance of chips kept reducing that cost until, twenty years later, it became affordable for even a small nonprofit organization such as the EFF to mount a realistic attack.[^o5]

## The DES challenges {#The_DES_challenges}

DES was a federal standard, and the [US government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_government) encouraged the use of DES for all non-classified data. [RSA Security](wikipedia://en/301240) wished to demonstrate that DES's key length was not enough to ensure security, so they set up the [DES Challenges](wikipedia://en/4426114) in 1997, offering a monetary prize. The first DES Challenge was solved in 96 days by the [DESCHALL Project](wikipedia://en/18473453) led by Rocke Verser in [Loveland, Colorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loveland,_Colorado). RSA Security set up DES Challenge II-1, which was solved by [distributed.net](wikipedia://en/337011) in 39 days in January and February 1998.[^o7]

In 1998, the EFF built Deep Crack (named in reference to IBM's [Deep Blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_Blue_%28chess_computer%29) chess computer) for less than \$250,000.[^o8] In response to DES Challenge II-2, on July 15, 1998, Deep Crack decrypted a DES-encrypted message after only 56 hours of work, winning \$10,000. The brute force attack showed that cracking DES was actually a very practical proposition. Most governments and large corporations could reasonably build a machine like Deep Crack.

Six months later, in response to RSA Security's DES Challenge III, and in collaboration with distributed.net, the EFF used Deep Crack to decrypt another DES-encrypted message, winning another \$10,000. This time, the operation took less than a day – 22 hours and 15 minutes. The decryption was completed on January 19, 1999. In October of that year, DES was reaffirmed as a federal standard, but this time the standard recommended [Triple DES](wikipedia://en/48375).

The small key space of DES and relatively high computational costs of Triple DES resulted in its replacement by [AES](wikipedia://en/1260) as a Federal standard, effective May 26, 2002.

## Technology {#Technology}

Deep Crack was designed by [Cryptography Research, Inc.](wikipedia://en/18356058), Advanced Wireless Technologies, and the [EFF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation). The principal designer was [Paul Kocher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Carl_Kocher), president of Cryptography Research. Advanced Wireless Technologies built 1,856 custom [ASIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Application-specific_integrated_circuit) DES chips (called *Deep Crack* or *AWT-4500*), housed on 29 circuit boards of 64 chips each. The boards were then fitted in six cabinets and mounted in a [Sun-4/470](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun-4) chassis.[^o10]

*Image: Paul kocher deepcrack.jpg.* Paul Kocher of Cryptography Research posing in front of Deep Crack

The search was coordinated by a single PC which assigned ranges of keys to the chips. The entire machine was capable of testing over 90 billion keys per second. It would take about 9 days to test every possible key at that rate. On average, the correct key would be found in half that time.

In 2006, another [custom hardware attack](wikipedia://en/2174032) machine was designed based on [FPGAs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FPGA). [COPACOBANA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COPACOBANA) (COst-optimized PArallel COdeBreaker) is able to crack DES at considerably lower cost.[^o11] This advantage is mainly due to progress in [integrated circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_circuit) technology.

In July 2012, security researchers David Hulton and [Moxie Marlinspike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moxie_Marlinspike) unveiled a cloud computing tool for breaking the [MS-CHAPv2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MS-CHAPv2) protocol by recovering the protocol's DES encryption keys by brute force. This tool effectively allows members of the general public to recover a DES key from a known plaintext–ciphertext pair in about 24 hours.[^o12]
