# Malliavin calculus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Malliavin calculus"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and related fields, **Malliavin calculus** is a set of mathematical techniques and ideas that extend the mathematical field of [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882) from deterministic functions to [stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/47895). In particular, it allows the computation of [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) of [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685). Malliavin calculus is also called the **[stochastic calculus](wikipedia://en/228001) of variations**. P. Malliavin first initiated the calculus on infinite dimensional space. Then, significant contributors such as [Seiichiro Kusuoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seiichiro_Kusuoka), [Daniel W. Stroock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_W._Stroock), [Jean-Michel Bismut](wikipedia://en/18887796), [Shinzo Watanabe](wikipedia://en/48084343), [Ichirō Shigekawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ichirō_Shigekawa), and so on completed the foundations for the field.

:::

Malliavin calculus is named after [Paul Malliavin](wikipedia://en/10459388) whose ideas led to a [proof](wikipedia://en/82285) that [Hörmander's condition](wikipedia://en/13659915) implies the existence and smoothness of a [density](wikipedia://en/43487) for the solution of a [stochastic differential equation](wikipedia://en/1361454); [Hörmander](wikipedia://en/500958)'s original proof was based on the theory of [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564). The calculus has been applied to [stochastic partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/12473239) as well.

The calculus allows [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252) with random variables; this operation is used in [mathematical finance](wikipedia://en/33589680) to compute the sensitivities of [financial derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative_%28finance%29). The calculus has applications in, for example, [stochastic filtering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_filtering).

## Overview and history {#Overview_and_history}

Malliavin introduced Malliavin calculus to provide a stochastic proof that [Hörmander's condition](wikipedia://en/13659915) implies the existence of a [density](wikipedia://en/43487) for the solution of a [stochastic differential equation](wikipedia://en/1361454); [Hörmander](wikipedia://en/500958)'s original proof was based on the theory of [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564). His calculus enabled Malliavin to prove regularity bounds for the solution's density. The calculus has been applied to [stochastic partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/12473239).

## Gaussian probability space {#Gaussian_probability_space}

Consider a *Wiener functional* $F$ (a functional from the [classical Wiener space](wikipedia://en/6810363)) and consider the task of finding a derivative for it. The natural idea would be to use the [Gateaux derivative](wikipedia://en/2253139)

$$D_g F[f] := \lim_{\tau \to 0} \frac{F[f + \tau g] - F[f]}{\tau},$$

however this does not always exist. Therefore it does make sense to find a new differential calculus for such spaces by limiting the directions.

The toy model of Malliavin calculus is an irreducible [Gaussian probability space](wikipedia://en/74715593) $X=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P,\mathcal{H})$. This is a (complete) probability space $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$ together with a closed [subspace](wikipedia://en/5308894) $\mathcal{H}\subset L^2(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$ such that all $H\in \mathcal{H}$ are mean zero Gaussian variables and $\mathcal{F}=\sigma(H:H\in \mathcal{H})$. If one chooses a basis for $\mathcal{H}$ then one calls $X$ a *numerical model*. On the other hand, for any separable Hilbert space $\mathcal{G}$ exists a canonical irreducible Gaussian probability space $\operatorname{Seg}(\mathcal{G})$ named the *Segal model* (named after [Irving Segal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irving_Segal)) having $\mathcal{G}$ as its Gaussian subspace. In this case for a $g\in \mathcal{G}$ one notates the associated random variable in $\operatorname{Seg}(\mathcal{G})$ as $W(g)$.

Properties of a Gaussian probability space that do not depend on the particular choice of basis are called *intrinsic* and such that do depend on the choice *extrensic*.[^o4] We denote the countably infinite product of real spaces as $\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{R}$.

Recall the modern version of the [Cameron-Martin theorem](wikipedia://en/6573013#Version_for_locall_convex_vector_spaces)

*Consider a [locally convex vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_convex_vector_space) $E$ with a cylindrical Gaussian measure $\gamma$ on it. For an element in the topological dual $f\in E'$ define the distance to the mean*

$$t_{\gamma}(f):=f-\int_E f(x) \gamma(\mathrm{d}x),$$

*which is a map $t_{\gamma}\colon E'\to L^2(E,\gamma)$, and denote the closure in $L^2(E,\gamma)$ as*

$$E_{\gamma}':=\operatorname{clos}\left\{t_{\gamma}(f)\colon\ f\in E'\right\}$$

*Let $\gamma_{m}:=\gamma(\cdot - m)$ denote the translation by $m\in E$. Then $E_{\gamma}'$ respectively the [covariance operator](wikipedia://en/33447667) $R_{\gamma}:E_{\gamma}'\to (E_{\gamma}')^*$ on it induces a [reproducing kernel Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/651196) $R$ called the Cameron-Martin space such that for any $m\in R$ there is [equivalence](wikipedia://en/6612667) $\gamma_{m}\sim \gamma$.*[^o5]

In fact one can use here the [Feldman–Hájek theorem](wikipedia://en/58507539) to find that for any other $h\not \in R$ such measure would be singular.

Let $\gamma$ be the canonical Gaussian measure, by transferring the Cameron-Martin theorem from $(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}),\gamma^{\mathbb{N}}=\otimes_{n\in\mathbb{N}} \gamma)$ into a numerical model $X$, the additive group of $\mathcal{H}$ will define a quasi-automorphism group on $\Omega$. A construction can be done as follows: choose an orthonormal basis in $\mathcal{H}$, let $\tau_{\alpha}(x)=x+\alpha$ denote the translation on $\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}$ by $\alpha$, denote the map into the Cameron-Martin space by $j:\mathcal{H}\to \ell^2$, denote

$L^{\infty-0}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)=\bigcap\limits_{p<\infty}L^p(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\quad$ and $\quad q:L^{\infty-0}(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}),\gamma^{\mathbb{N}})\to L^{\infty-0}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P),$

we get a canonical representation of the additive group $\rho:\mathcal{H}\to \operatorname{End}(L^{\infty-0}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P))$ acting on the [endomorphisms](wikipedia://en/9569) by defining

$$\rho(h)=q\circ \tau_{j(h)}\circ q^{-1}.$$

One can show that the action of $\rho$ is extrinsic meaning it does not depend on the choice of basis for $\mathcal{H}$, further $\rho(h+h')=\rho(h)\rho(h')$ for $h,h'\in \mathcal{H}$ and for the [infinitesimal generator](wikipedia://en/1644938#Infinitesimal_generator) of $(\rho(h))_{h}$ that

$$\lim\limits_{\varepsilon \to 0}\frac{\rho(\varepsilon h)-I}{\varepsilon}=M_h$$

where $I$ is the identity operator and $M_h$ denotes the multiplication operator by the random variable $h\in \mathcal{H}$ (acting on the endomorphisms). In the case of an arbitrary Hilbert space $\mathcal{G}$ and the Segal model $\operatorname{Seg}(\mathcal{G})$ one has $j:\mathcal{G}\to \ell^2$ (and thus $\rho:\mathcal{G}\to \operatorname{End}(L^{\infty-0}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P))$. Then the limit above becomes the multiplication operator by the random variable $W(g)$ associated to $g\in \mathcal{G}$.[^o6]

For $F\in L^{\infty-0}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$ and $h\in\mathcal{H}$ one now defines the directional derivative

$$\langle DF,h\rangle=D_h F=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon \to 0}\frac{\left(\rho(\varepsilon h)-I\right)F}{\varepsilon}.$$

Given a Hilbert space $H$ and a Segal model $\operatorname{Seg}(H)$ with its Gaussian space $\mathcal{H}=\{W(h): h\in H\}$. One can now deduce for $F\in L^{\infty-0}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$ the integration by parts formula

$\mathbb{E}[D_h F]=\mathbb{E}[M_{W(h)}F]=\mathbb{E}[W(h)F]$.[^o7]

## Invariance principle {#Invariance_principle}

The usual invariance principle for [Lebesgue integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_integration) over the whole real line is that, for any real number ε and integrable function *f*, the following holds

$\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\, d \lambda(x) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x+\varepsilon)\, d \lambda(x)$ and hence $\int_{-\infty}^\infty f'(x)\, d \lambda(x)=0.$

This can be used to derive the [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252) formula since, setting *f* = *gh*, it implies

$$0 = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f' \,d \lambda = \int_{-\infty}^\infty (gh)' \,d \lambda = \int_{-\infty}^\infty g h'\, d \lambda + \int_{-\infty}^\infty g' h\, d \lambda.$$

A similar idea can be applied in stochastic analysis for the differentiation along a Cameron-Martin-Girsanov direction. Indeed, let $h_s$ be a square-integrable [predictable process](wikipedia://en/33414015) and set

$$\varphi(t) = \int_0^t h_s\, d s .$$

If $W$ is a [Wiener process](wikipedia://en/149984), the [Girsanov theorem](wikipedia://en/336940) then yields the following analogue of the invariance principle:

$$E(F(W + \varepsilon\varphi))= E \left [ F(W) \exp \left ( \varepsilon\int_0^1 h_s\, d w_s - \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^2 \int_0^1 h_s^2\, ds \right ) \right ].$$

Differentiating with respect to ε on both sides and evaluating at ε=0, one obtains the following integration by parts formula:

$$E(\langle DF(W), \varphi\rangle) = E\Big[ F(W) \int_0^1 h_s\, dw_s \Big].$$

Here, the left-hand side is the [Malliavin derivative](wikipedia://en/6814674) of the random variable $F$ in the direction $\varphi$ and the integral appearing on the right hand side should be interpreted as an [Itô integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itô_integral).

:::

## Clark–Ocone formula {#Clark–Ocone_formula}

One of the most useful results from Malliavin calculus is the [Clark–Ocone theorem](wikipedia://en/6599348), which allows the process in the [martingale representation theorem](wikipedia://en/3758605) to be identified explicitly. A simplified version of this theorem is as follows:

Consider the standard Wiener measure on the canonical space $C[0,1]$, equipped with its canonical filtration. For $F: C[0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfying $E(F(X)^2) < \infty$ which is Lipschitz and such that *F* has a strong derivative kernel, in the sense that for $\varphi$ in *C*\[0,1\]

$$\lim_{\varepsilon \to 0} \frac 1 \varepsilon (F(X+\varepsilon \varphi) - F(X) ) = \int_0^1 F'(X,dt) \varphi(t)\ \mathrm{a.e.}\ X$$

then

$$F(X) = E(F(X)) + \int_0^1 H_t \,d X_t ,$$

where *H* is the previsible projection of *F*'(*x*, (*t*,1\]) which may be viewed as the derivative of the function *F* with respect to a suitable parallel shift of the process *X* over the portion (*t*,1\] of its domain.

This may be more concisely expressed by

$$F(X) = E(F(X))+\int_0^1 E (D_t F \mid \mathcal{F}_t ) \, d X_t .$$

Much of the work in the formal development of the Malliavin calculus involves extending this result to the largest possible class of functionals *F* by replacing the derivative kernel used above by the "[Malliavin derivative](wikipedia://en/6814674)" denoted $D_t$ in the above statement of the result.

:::

## Skorokhod integral {#Skorokhod_integral}

The [Skorokhod integral](wikipedia://en/18420736) operator, which is conventionally denoted δ, is defined as the adjoint of the Malliavin derivative in the [white noise](wikipedia://en/46182) case when the Hilbert space is an $L^2$ space; thus for *u* in the domain of the operator which is a subset of $L^2([0,\infty) \times \Omega)$, for *F* in the domain of the Malliavin derivative, we require

$$E (\langle DF, u \rangle ) = E (F \delta (u) ),$$

where the inner product is that on $L^2[0,\infty)$, viz.

$$\langle f, g \rangle = \int_0^\infty f(s) g(s) \, ds.$$

The existence of this adjoint follows from the [Riesz representation theorem](wikipedia://en/26452) for linear operators on [Hilbert spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_spaces).

It can be shown that if *u* is adapted then

$$\delta(u) = \int_0^\infty u_t\, d W_t ,$$

where the integral is to be understood in the Itô sense. Thus this provides a method of extending the Itô integral to non-adapted integrands.

## Applications {#Applications}

The calculus allows [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252) with [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685); this operation is used in [mathematical finance](wikipedia://en/33589680) to compute the [sensitivities](wikipedia://en/620083) of [financial derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative_%28finance%29). The calculus has applications for example in [stochastic filtering](wikipedia://en/17058216).

:::
