# Langlands–Shahidi method

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Langlands–Shahidi method"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Langlands–Shahidi method** provides the means to define [automorphic L-functions](wikipedia://en/32328300) in many cases that arise with connected [reductive groups](wikipedia://en/1088386) over a [number field](wikipedia://en/28730822). This includes [Rankin–Selberg](wikipedia://en/27989406) products for cuspidal [automorphic representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automorphic_representation) of [general linear groups](wikipedia://en/113564). The method develops the theory of the **local coefficient**, which links to the global theory via [Eisenstein series](wikipedia://en/1270458). The resulting *L*-functions satisfy a number of analytic properties, including an important functional equation.

:::

## The local coefficient {#The_local_coefficient}

The setting is in the generality of a connected quasi-split reductive group *G*, together with a **Levi** subgroup *M*, defined over a [local field](wikipedia://en/145375) *F*. For example, if *G* = *G<sub>l</sub>* is a [classical group](wikipedia://en/10172878) of rank *l*, its maximal Levi subgroups are of the form GL(*m*) × *G<sub>n</sub>*, where *G<sub>n</sub>* is a classical group of rank *n* and of the same type as *G<sub>l</sub>*, *l* = *m* + *n*. [F. Shahidi](wikipedia://en/29138216) develops the theory of the **local coefficient** for irreducible generic representations of *M(F)*.[^o3] The local coefficient is defined by means of the uniqueness property of [Whittaker models](wikipedia://en/20679744) paired with the theory of intertwining operators for representations obtained by parabolic induction from generic representations.

The global intertwining operator appearing in the functional equation of [Langlands](wikipedia://en/25986)' theory of Eisenstein series[^o4] can be decomposed as a product of local intertwining operators. When *M* is a maximal Levi subgroup, local coefficients arise from Fourier coefficients of appropriately chosen Eisenstein series and satisfy a crude functional equation involving a product of partial *L*-functions.

## Local factors and functional equation {#Local_factors_and_functional_equation}

An induction step refines the crude functional equation of a globally generic cuspidal automorphic representation $\pi = \otimes' \pi_v$ to individual functional equations of partial *L*-functions and **γ-factors**:[^o6]

$$L^S(s,\pi,r_i) = \prod_{v \in S} \gamma_i(s,\pi_v,\psi_v) L^S(1-s,\tilde{\pi},r_i).$$

The details are technical: *s* a complex variable, *S* a finite set of places (of the underlying [global field](wikipedia://en/288358)) with $\pi_v$ unramified for *v* outside of *S*, and $r = \oplus r_i$ is the adjoint action of *M* on the complex Lie algebra of a specific subgroup of the [Langlands dual group](wikipedia://en/6789133) of *G*. When *G* is the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) SL(2), and *M* = *T* is the [maximal torus](wikipedia://en/360136) of diagonal matrices, then π is a [Größencharakter](wikipedia://en/3149443) and the corresponding γ-factors are the local factors of [Tate's thesis](wikipedia://en/245933).

The γ-factors are uniquely characterized by their role in the functional equation and a list of local properties, including multiplicativity with respect to parabolic induction. They satisfy a relationship involving [Artin L-functions](wikipedia://en/2774171) and [Artin root numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin_root_number) when *v* gives an archimedean local field or when *v* is non-archimedean and $\pi_v$ is a constituent of an unramified principal series representation of *M(F)*. Local *L*-functions and root numbers ε$(s,\pi_v,r_{i,v},\psi_v)$ are then defined at every place, including $v \in S$, by means of [Langlands classification](wikipedia://en/10985992) for *p*-adic groups. The functional equation takes the form

$$L(s,\pi,r_i) = \epsilon(s,\pi,r_i) L(1-s,\tilde{\pi},r_i),$$

where $L(s,\pi,r_i)$ and $\epsilon(s,\pi,r_i)$ are the completed global *L*-function and root number.

## Examples of automorphic *L*-functions {#Examples_of_automorphic_L-functions}

- $L(s,\pi_1 \times \pi_2)$, the Rankin–Selberg *L*-function of cuspidal automorphic representations $\pi_1$ of GL(*m*) and $\pi_2$ of GL(*n*).
- $L(s,\tau \times \pi)$, where τ is a cuspidal automorphic representation of GL(*m*) and π is a globally generic cuspidal automorphic representation of a classical group *G*.
- $L(s,\tau,r)$, with τ as before and *r* a symmetric square, an exterior square, or an Asai representation of the dual group of GL(*n*).

A full list of **Langlands–Shahidi L-functions**[^o8] depends on the quasi-split group *G* and maximal Levi subgroup *M*. More specifically, the decomposition of the adjoint action $r = \oplus r_i$ can be classified using [Dynkin diagrams](wikipedia://en/277109). A first study of automorphic *L*-functions via the theory of Eisenstein Series can be found in Langlands' **Euler Products**,[^o9] under the assumption that the automorphic representations are everywhere unramified. What the Langlands–Shahidi method provides is the definition of *L*-functions and root numbers with no other condition on the representation of *M* other than requiring the existence of a Whittaker model.

## Analytic properties of *L*-functions {#Analytic_properties_of_L-functions}

Global *L*-functions are said to be **nice**[^o11] if they satisfy:

1. $L(s,\pi,r), \ L(s,\tilde{\pi}, r)$ extend to [entire functions](wikipedia://en/10372) of the complex variable *s*.
1. $L(s,\pi,r), \ L(s,\tilde{\pi},r)$ are bounded in vertical strips.
1. (Functional Equation) $L(s,\pi,r) = \epsilon(s,\pi,r) L(1-s,\tilde{\pi},r)$.

Langlands–Shahidi *L*-functions satisfy the functional equation. Progress towards boundedness in vertical strips was made by S. S. Gelbart and F. Shahidi.[^o12] And, after incorporating twists by highly ramified characters, Langlands–Shahidi *L*-functions do become entire.[^o13]

Another result is the non-vanishing of *L*-functions. For Rankin–Selberg products of general linear groups it states that $L(1+it,\pi_1 \times \pi_2)$ is non-zero for every real number *t*.[^o14]

## Applications to functoriality and to representation theory of *p*-adic groups {#Applications_to_functoriality_and_to_representation_theory_of_p-adic_groups}

- **Functoriality for the classical groups**: A cuspidal globally generic automorphic representation of a classical group admits a [Langlands functorial](wikipedia://en/171905) lift to an automorphic representation of GL(*N*),[^o16] where *N* depends on the classical group. Then, the Ramanujan bounds of W. Luo, Z. Rudnick and P. Sarnak[^o17] for GL(*N*) over number fields yield non-trivial bounds for the [generalized Ramanujan conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanujan–Peterson_conjecture) of the classical groups.
- **Symmetric powers for GL(2)**: Proofs of functoriality for the symmetric cube and for the symmetric fourth[^o18] powers of cuspidal automorphic representations of GL(2) were made possible by the Langlands–Shahidi method. Progress towards higher Symmetric powers leads to the best possible bounds towards the [Ramanujan–Peterson conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanujan–Peterson_conjecture) of automorphic cusp forms of GL(2).
- **Representations of *p*-adic groups**: Applications involving [Harish-Chandra](wikipedia://en/373190) μ functions (from the Plancherel formula) and to complementary series of *p*-adic reductive groups are possible. For example, GL(*n*) appears as the Siegel Levi subgroup of a classical group G. If π is a smooth irreducible ramified supercuspidal representation of GL(*n*, *F*) over a field *F* of *p*-adic numbers, and $I(\pi) = I(0,\pi)$ is irreducible, then:

<!-- -->

1. $I(s,\pi)$ is irreducible and in the complementary series for 0 \< *s* \< 1;
1. $I(1,\pi)$ is reducible and has a unique generic non-supercuspidal discrete series subrepresentation;
1. $I(s,\pi)$ is irreducible and never in the complementary series for *s* > 1.

Here, $I(s,\pi)$ is obtained by unitary parabolic induction from

- $\pi \otimes |\det|^s$ if *G* = SO(2*n*), Sp(2*n*), or U(*n*+1, *n*);
- $\pi \otimes |\det|^{s/2}$ if *G* = SO(2*n*+1) or U(*n*, *n*).
