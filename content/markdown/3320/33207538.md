# Mathematical manuscripts of Karl Marx

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mathematical manuscripts of Karl Marx"}

The **mathematical manuscripts of Karl Marx** are a manuscript collection of [Karl Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx)'s mathematical notes where he attempted to derive the foundations of [infinitesimal calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal_calculus) from first principles.

:::

The notes that Marx took have been collected into four independent treatises: *On the Concept of the Derived Function*, *On the Differential*, *On the History of Differential Calculus*, and *Taylor's Theorem, MacLaurin's Theorem, and Lagrange's Theory of Derived Functions*, along with several notes, additional drafts, and supplements to these four treatises. These treatises attempt to construct a rigorous foundation for calculus and use [historical materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_materialism) to analyze the [history of mathematics](wikipedia://en/14220).

Marx's contributions to mathematics did not have any impact on the [historical development of calculus](wikipedia://en/746117), and he was unaware of [many more recent developments in the field at the time](wikipedia://en/48396#Modern), such as the work of [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy). However, his work in some ways anticipated, but did not influence, some later developments in 20th century mathematics. These manuscripts, which are from around 1873–1883, were not published in any language until 1968 when they were published in the Soviet Union alongside a Russian translation. Since their publication, Marx's independent contributions to mathematics have been analyzed in terms of both his own [historical and economic theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_materialism),[^n1] and in light of their potential applications of [nonstandard analysis](wikipedia://en/21708).[^n2]

## Contents {#Contents}

Marx left over 1000 manuscript pages[^n3] of mathematical notes on his attempts at discovering the foundations of calculus. The majority of these manuscript pages have been collected into four papers, along with drafts and supplementary notes in the published editions of his collected works.[^n4] In these works, Marx attempted to draw analogies between his theories of the history of economics and the development of calculus by constructing differential calculus in terms of mathematical symbols altered by an upheaval that would reveal their meaning.[^n5]

### *On the Concept of the Derived Function* {#On_the_Concept_of_the_Derived_Function}

Marx wrote *On the Concept of the Derived Function* in 1881, just two years before his death.[^n6] In this work, he demonstrates the mechanical steps needed to calculate a derivative for several basic functions from first principles.[^n7] Despite the fact that Marx's principal sources primarily relied on geometric arguments for the [definition of the derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definition_of_the_derivative), Marx's explanations rely much more strongly on algebraic explanations than geometric ones, suggesting he likely preferred to think of things algebraically.[^n7] Fahey et al. state that although "We might be alarmed to find a student writing 0/0 ... \[Marx\] was well aware of what he was doing when he wrote '0/0'."[^n6] However, Marx was evidently disturbed by the implications of this, stating that "The closely held belief of some rationalising mathematicians that *dy* and *dx* are quantitatively actually only infinitely small, only approaching 0/0, is a chimera".[^n7]

### *On the Differential* {#On_the_Differential}

In *On the Differential*, Marx tries to construct the definition of a derivative d*y*/d*x* from first principles,[^n8] without using the definition of a [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602). He appears to have primarily used an elementary textbook written by the French mathematician Boucharlat,[^n9][^n8] who had primarily used the traditional [limit definition of the derivative](wikipedia://en/285759), but Marx appears to have intentionally avoided doing so in his definition of the derivative.[^n8]

Fahey et al. state that, as evidenced by the four separate drafts of this paper, Marx wrote it with considerable care.[^n8]

### *On the History of Differential Calculus* {#On_the_History_of_Differential_Calculus}

Fahey et al. state that although Marx never used this term in his mathematical papers, his history of calculus can be understood in terms of [thesis, antithesis, synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thesis,_antithesis,_synthesis). Marx identified three historical phases of development - the "mystical" differential calculus of [Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) and [Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz), the "rational" differential calculus of [d'Alembert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D'Alembert), and the "purely algebraic" differential calculus of [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange).[^n10] However, as Marx was not aware of the work of [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy), he did not carry his historical development any further.[^n10]

## Legacy {#Legacy}

Historian of science [Kathryn Olesko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kathryn_Olesko) states that, contrary to many claims made by both Engels and the publishers of Marx's manuscripts, Marx's work did not "solve the historical and conceptual riddle of calculus".[^n5] Mathematician [Hubert Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubert_Kennedy) observes that Marx "seems to have been unaware of the advances being made by continental mathematicians in the foundations of [differential calculus](wikipedia://en/50416), including the work of [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy)"[^n11] and that although Marx's study of differentials had "no immediate effect on the historical development of mathematics", Engels' claim of "independent discoveries" made by Marx is "certainly justified"[^n12] and Marx's definition of the differential "anticipated \[some\] 20th century developments in mathematics".[^n13]

[Joseph Dauben](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Dauben) speculates that Marx's developments in calculus may have also contributed to an interest in [nonstandard analysis](wikipedia://en/21708) among Chinese mathematicians.[^n2]

## Editions and translations {#Editions_and_translations}

Although Engels stated his intent to publish the "extremely important mathematical manuscripts left by Marx" in 1885, it was not until 1933 that parts of the manuscripts were published in Russian—for the journal *[Under the Banner of Marxism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Under_the_Banner_of_Marxism)* and collection *[Marxism and Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism_and_Science)*.[^n14] The documents were first fully published in 1968, in both German and Russian, with the latter edited by [Sofya Yanovskaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sofya_Yanovskaya).[^n15] An English translation was first published in 1983.
