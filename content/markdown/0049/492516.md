# Inclusion map

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Inclusion map"}

*Image: Venn A subset B.svg.* $A$ is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $B,$ and $B$ is a [superset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $A.$

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), if $A$ is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $B,$ then the **inclusion map** is the [function](wikipedia://en/185427) [$\iota$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ι) that sends each element $x$ of $A$ to $x,$ treated as an element of $B:$

$$\iota : A\rightarrow B, \qquad \iota(x)=x.$$

:::

An inclusion map may also be referred to as an **inclusion function**, an **insertion**,[^o2] or a **canonical injection**.

A "hooked arrow" (21AA)[^o3] is sometimes used in place of the function arrow above to denote an inclusion map; thus:

$$\iota: A\hookrightarrow B.$$

(However, some authors use this hooked arrow for any [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119).)

This and other analogous [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) functions[^o4] from [substructures](wikipedia://en/2057747) are sometimes called **natural injections**.

Given any [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) $f$ between [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) $X$ and $Y$, if there is an inclusion map $\iota : A \to X$ into the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) $X$, then one can form the [restriction](wikipedia://en/1589032) $f\circ \iota$ of $f.$ In many instances, one can also construct a canonical inclusion into the [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) $R \to Y$ known as the [range](wikipedia://en/275991) of $f.$

## Applications of inclusion maps {#Applications_of_inclusion_maps}

Inclusion maps tend to be [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13564) of [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364); thus, such inclusion maps are [embeddings](wikipedia://en/52119). More precisely, given a substructure closed under some operations, the inclusion map will be an embedding for tautological reasons. For example, for some binary operation $\star,$ to require that

$$\iota(x\star y) = \iota(x) \star \iota(y)$$

is simply to say that $\star$ is consistently computed in the sub-structure and the large structure. The case of a [unary operation](wikipedia://en/161006) is similar; but one should also look at [nullary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nullary) operations, which pick out a *constant* element. Here the point is that [closure](wikipedia://en/293802) means such constants must already be given in the substructure.

Inclusion maps are seen in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) where if $A$ is a [strong deformation retract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_deformation_retract) of $X,$ the inclusion map yields an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397) between all [homotopy groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_groups) (that is, it is a [homotopy equivalence](wikipedia://en/212250)).

Inclusion maps in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) come in different kinds: for example [embeddings](wikipedia://en/52119) of [submanifolds](wikipedia://en/525095). [Contravariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariance_and_contravariance_of_functors) objects (which is to say, objects that have [pullbacks](wikipedia://en/9413508); these are called [covariant](wikipedia://en/202886) in an older and unrelated terminology) such as [differential forms](wikipedia://en/221519) *restrict* to submanifolds, giving a mapping in the *other direction*. Another example, more sophisticated, is that of [affine schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_scheme), for which the inclusions

$$\operatorname{Spec}\left(R/I\right) \to \operatorname{Spec}(R)$$

and

$$\operatorname{Spec}\left(R/I^2\right) \to \operatorname{Spec}(R)$$

may be different [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891), where $R$ is a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) and $I$ is an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) of $R.$
