# Hyperelliptic curve

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hyperelliptic curve"}

*Image: Example of a hyperelliptic curve.svg.* Fig. 1: The graph of the hyperelliptic curve $C : y^2 = f(x)$ where$\begin{aligned}f(x)& = x^5 - 2x^4 - 7x^3 + 8x^2 + 12x\\& = x (x + 1) (x - 3) (x + 2) (x - 2).\end{aligned}$

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a **hyperelliptic curve** is an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) of [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) *g* > 1, given by an equation of the form

$$y^2 + h(x)y = f(x)$$

where *f*(*x*) is a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) of degree *n* = 2*g* + 1 > 4 or *n* = 2*g* + 2 > 4 with *n* distinct roots, and *h*(*x*) is a polynomial of degree \< *g* + 2 (if the characteristic of the ground field is not 2, one can take *h*(*x*) = 0).

:::

A **hyperelliptic function** is an element of the [function field](wikipedia://en/2783843) of such a curve, or of the [Jacobian variety](wikipedia://en/489734) on the curve; these two concepts are identical for [elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/69939), but different for hyperelliptic functions.

## Genus {#Genus}

The degree of the polynomial determines the genus of the curve: a polynomial of degree 2*g* + 1 or 2*g* + 2 gives a curve of genus *g*. When the degree is equal to 2*g* + 1, the curve is called an [imaginary hyperelliptic curve](wikipedia://en/21183334). Meanwhile, a curve of degree 2*g* + 2 is termed a [real hyperelliptic curve](wikipedia://en/30579413). This statement about genus remains true for *g* = 0 or 1, but those special cases are not called "hyperelliptic". In the case *g* = 1 (if one chooses a distinguished point), such a curve is called an [elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/10225).

## Formulation and choice of model {#Formulation_and_choice_of_model}

While this model is the simplest way to describe hyperelliptic curves, such an equation will have a [singular point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity) *at infinity* in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350). This feature is specific to the case *n* > 3. Therefore, in giving such an equation to specify a non-singular curve, it is almost always assumed that a non-singular model (also called a [smooth completion](wikipedia://en/34289679)), equivalent in the sense of [birational geometry](wikipedia://en/382733), is meant.

To be more precise, the equation defines a [quadratic extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_extension) of **C**(*x*), and it is that function field that is meant. The singular point at infinity can be removed (since this is a curve) by the normalization ([integral closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_closure)) process. It turns out that after doing this, there is an open cover of the curve by two affine charts: the one already given by

$$y^2 = f(x)$$

and another one given by

$$w^2 = v^{2g+2}f(1/v) .$$

The glueing maps between the two charts are given by

$$(x,y) \mapsto (1/x, y/x^{g+1})$$

and

$$(v,w) \mapsto (1/v, w/v^{g+1}),$$

wherever they are defined.

In fact geometric shorthand is assumed, with the curve *C* being defined as a ramified double cover of the [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578), the [ramification](wikipedia://en/384644) occurring at the roots of *f*, and also for odd *n* at the point at infinity. In this way the cases *n* = 2*g* + 1 and 2*g* + 2 can be unified, since we might as well use an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) of the projective plane to move any ramification point away from infinity.

## Using Riemann–Hurwitz formula {#Using_Riemann–Hurwitz_formula}

Using the [Riemann–Hurwitz formula](wikipedia://en/497413), the hyperelliptic curve with genus *g* is defined by an equation with degree *n* = 2*g* + 2. Suppose *f* : *X* → P<sup>1</sup> is a branched covering with ramification degree *2*, where *X* is a curve with genus *g* and P<sup>1</sup> is the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799). Let *g*<sub>1</sub> = *g* and *g*<sub>0</sub> be the genus of P<sup>1</sup> ( = 0 ), then the Riemann-Hurwitz formula turns out to be

$$2-2g_1 =2(2-2g_0)-\sum_{s \in X}(e_s-1)$$

where *s* is over all ramified points on *X*. The number of ramified points is *n*, and at each ramified point *s* we have *e<sub>s</sub>* = 2, so the formula becomes

$$2-2\times g =2(2-2\times0)-n\times(2-1)$$

so *n* = 2*g* + 2.

:::

## Occurrence and applications {#Occurrence_and_applications}

All curves of genus 2 are hyperelliptic, but for genus ≥ 3 the generic curve is not hyperelliptic. This is seen heuristically by a [moduli space](wikipedia://en/361609) dimension check. Counting constants, with *n* = 2*g* + 2, the collection of *n* points subject to the action of the automorphisms of the projective line has (2*g* + 2) − 3 degrees of freedom, which is less than 3*g* − 3, the number of moduli of a curve of genus *g*, unless *g* is 2. Much more is known about the *hyperelliptic locus* in the moduli space of curves or [abelian varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_varieties), though it is harder to exhibit *general* non-hyperelliptic curves with simple models.[^o7] One geometric characterization of hyperelliptic curves is via [Weierstrass points](wikipedia://en/3124143). More detailed geometry of non-hyperelliptic curves is read from the theory of [canonical curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_curve), the [canonical mapping](wikipedia://en/734893#Canonical_maps) being 2-to-1 on hyperelliptic curves but 1-to-1 otherwise for *g* > 2. [Trigonal curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonal_curve) are those that correspond to taking a cube root, rather than a square root, of a polynomial.

The definition by quadratic extensions of the rational function field works for fields in general except in characteristic 2; in all cases the geometric definition as a ramified double cover of the projective line is available, if the extension is assumed to be separable.

Hyperelliptic curves can be used in [hyperelliptic curve cryptography](wikipedia://en/561913) for [cryptosystems](wikipedia://en/506383) based on the [discrete logarithm problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_logarithm_problem).

Hyperelliptic curves also appear composing entire connected components of certain strata of the moduli space of Abelian differentials.[^o8]

Hyperellipticity of genus-2 curves was used to prove [Gromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Leonidovich_Gromov)'s [filling area conjecture](wikipedia://en/12083818) in the case of fillings of genus =1.

### Classification {#Classification}

Hyperelliptic curves of given genus *g* have a moduli space, closely related to the ring of [invariants of a binary form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariants_of_a_binary_form) of degree 2*g*+2.

## History {#History}

Hyperelliptic functions were first published by [Adolph Göpel](wikipedia://en/18550639) (1812-1847) in his last paper *Abelsche Transcendenten erster Ordnung* (Abelian transcendents of first order) (in [Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik](wikipedia://en/973547), vol. 35, 1847). Independently [Johann G. Rosenhain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_G._Rosenhain) worked on that matter and published *Umkehrungen ultraelliptischer Integrale erster Gattung* (in Mémoires des savants etc., vol. 11, 1851).
