# Growth rate (group theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Growth rate"}

In the mathematical subject of [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164), the **growth rate** of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) with respect to a symmetric [generating set](wikipedia://en/99945) describes how fast a group grows. Every element in the group can be written as a product of generators, and the growth rate counts the number of elements that can be written as a product of length *n*.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Suppose *G* is a finitely generated group; and *T* is a finite *symmetric* set of [generators](wikipedia://en/99945) (symmetric means that if $x \in T$ then $x^{-1} \in T$). Any element $x \in G$ can be expressed as a [word](wikipedia://en/27701#Formal_theory) in the *T*-alphabet

$$x = a_1 \cdot a_2 \cdots a_k \text{ where } a_i\in T.$$

Consider the subset of all elements of *G* that can be expressed by such a word of length ≤ *n*

$$B_n(G,T) = \{x\in G \mid x = a_1 \cdot a_2 \cdots a_k \text{ where } a_i\in T \text{ and } k\le n\}.$$

This set is just the [closed ball](wikipedia://en/160556) of radius *n* in the [word metric](wikipedia://en/1289716) *d* on *G* with respect to the generating set *T*:

$$B_n(G,T) = \{x\in G \mid d(x, e)\le n\}.$$

More geometrically, $B_n(G,T)$ is the set of vertices in the [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) with respect to *T* that are within distance *n* of the identity.

Given two nondecreasing positive functions *a* and *b* one can say that they are equivalent ($a\sim b$) if there is a constant *C* such that for all positive integers *n*,

$$a(n/ C) \leq b(n) \leq a(Cn),\,$$

for example $p^n\sim q^n$ if $p,q>1$.

Then the growth rate of the group *G* can be defined as the corresponding [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) of the function

$$\#(n)=|B_n(G,T)|,$$

where $|B_n(G,T)|$ denotes the number of elements in the set $B_n(G,T)$. Although the function $\#(n)$ depends on the set of generators *T* its rate of growth does not (see below) and therefore the rate of growth gives an invariant of a group.

The word metric *d* and therefore sets $B_n(G,T)$ depend on the generating set *T*. However, any two such metrics are [*bilipschitz*](wikipedia://en/51731#Lipschitz_continuity_in_metric_spaces) [*equivalent*](wikipedia://en/9260) in the following sense: for finite symmetric generating sets *E*, *F*, there is a positive constant *C* such that

$${1\over C} \ d_F(x,y) \leq d_E(x,y) \leq C \ d_F(x,y).$$

As an immediate corollary of this inequality we get that the growth rate does not depend on the choice of generating set.

:::

## Polynomial and exponential growth {#Polynomial_and_exponential_growth}

If

$$\#(n)\le C(n^k+1)$$

for some $C,k<\infty$ we say that *G* has a **polynomial growth rate**. The infimum $k_0$ of such *k**s is called the**order of polynomial growth**.*** According to [Gromov's theorem](wikipedia://en/635483), a group of polynomial growth is a [virtually](wikipedia://en/10377971) [nilpotent group](wikipedia://en/144569), i.e. it has a [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569) [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of finite [index](wikipedia://en/230581). In particular, the order of polynomial growth $k_0$ has to be a [natural number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers) and in fact $\#(n)\sim n^{k_0}$.

If $\#(n)\ge a^n$ for some $a>1$ we say that *G* has an **[exponential growth](wikipedia://en/191933) rate**. Every [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/963042) *G* has at most exponential growth, i.e. for some $b>1$ we have $\#(n)\le b^n$.

If $\#(n)$ grows [more slowly than any exponential function](wikipedia://en/23768353), *G* has a **subexponential growth rate**. Any such group is [amenable](wikipedia://en/657430).

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- A [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) of finite rank $k > 1$ has exponential growth rate.
- A [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) has constant growth—that is, polynomial growth of order 0—and this includes [fundamental groups](wikipedia://en/11004) of [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) whose [universal cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_cover) is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042).
- If *M* is a [closed](wikipedia://en/669475) [negatively curved](wikipedia://en/651361) [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) then its [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) $\pi_1(M)$ has exponential growth rate. [John Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632) proved this using the fact that the [word metric](wikipedia://en/1289716) on $\pi_1(M)$ is [quasi-isometric](wikipedia://en/490861#Q) to the [universal cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covering_map) of *M*.
- The [free abelian group](wikipedia://en/247296) $\mathbb{Z}^d$ has a polynomial growth rate of order *d*.
- The [discrete Heisenberg group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_Heisenberg_group) $H_3$ has a polynomial growth rate of order 4. This fact is a special case of the general theorem of [Hyman Bass](wikipedia://en/7085910) and [Yves Guivarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yves_Guivarch) that is discussed in the article on [Gromov's theorem](wikipedia://en/635483).
- The [lamplighter group](wikipedia://en/5491540) has an exponential growth.
- The existence of groups with **intermediate growth**, i.e. subexponential but not polynomial was open for many years. The question was asked by Milnor in 1968 and was finally answered in the positive by [Rostislav Grigorchuk](wikipedia://en/18697527) in 1984. There are still open questions in this area and a complete picture of which orders of growth are possible and which are not is missing.
- The [triangle groups](wikipedia://en/2069342) include infinitely many finite groups (the spherical ones, corresponding to sphere), three groups of quadratic growth (the Euclidean ones, corresponding to Euclidean plane), and infinitely many groups of exponential growth (the hyperbolic ones, corresponding to the hyperbolic plane).

:::
