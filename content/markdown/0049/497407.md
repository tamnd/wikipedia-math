# Cohen–Macaulay ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cohen–Macaulay ring"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Cohen–Macaulay ring** is a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) with some of the [algebro-geometric](wikipedia://en/1997) properties of a [smooth variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_variety), such as local [equidimensionality](wikipedia://en/21568751). Under mild assumptions, a [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) is Cohen–Macaulay exactly when it is a finitely generated [free module](wikipedia://en/326454) over a [regular local](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_local) subring. Cohen–Macaulay rings play a central role in [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990): they form a very broad class, and yet they are well understood in many ways.

:::

They are named for (txt), who proved the [unmixedness theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unmixedness_theorem) for polynomial rings, and for (txt), who proved the unmixedness theorem for [formal power series rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_power_series_ring). All Cohen–Macaulay rings have the unmixedness property.

For Noetherian local rings, there is the following chain of inclusions.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

For a [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) *R*, a finite (i.e. [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430)) *R*-module $M\neq 0$ is a *Cohen–Macaulay module* if $\mathrm{depth}(M) = \mathrm{dim}(M)$ (in general we have: $\mathrm{depth}(M) \leq \mathrm{dim}(M)$, see [Auslander–Buchsbaum formula](wikipedia://en/30774458) for the relation between [depth](wikipedia://en/9770927) and [dim](wikipedia://en/56079) of a certain kind of modules). On the other hand, $R$ is a module on itself, so we call $R$ a *Cohen–Macaulay ring* if it is a Cohen–Macaulay module as an $R$-module. A *maximal* Cohen–Macaulay module is a Cohen–Macaulay module *M* such that $\mathrm{dim}(M)=\mathrm{dim}(R)$.

The above definition was for a Noetherian local ring. But we can expand the definition for a more general Noetherian ring: If $R$ is a commutative Noetherian ring, then an *R*-module *M* is called **Cohen–Macaulay module** if $M_\mathrm{m}$ is a Cohen–Macaulay module for all [maximal ideals](wikipedia://en/48164) $\mathrm{m}\in \mathrm{Supp}(M)$. (This is a kind of [circular definition](wikipedia://en/68038) unless we define zero modules as Cohen–Macaulay. So we define zero modules as Cohen–Macaulay modules in this definition.) Now, to define *maximal* Cohen–Macaulay modules for these rings, we require that $M_\mathrm{m}$ to be such an $R_\mathrm{m}$-module for each maximal ideal $\mathrm{m}$ of *R*. As in the local case, *R* is a *Cohen–Macaulay ring* if it is a Cohen–Macaulay module (as an $R$-module on itself).[^o6]

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Noetherian rings of the following types are Cohen–Macaulay.

- Any [regular local ring](wikipedia://en/506959). This leads to various examples of Cohen–Macaulay rings, such as the integers $\mathbb{Z}$, or a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $K[x_1,\ldots ,x_n]$ over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K*, or a [power series ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_series_ring) $K[[x_1,\ldots ,x_n]]$ . In geometric terms, every [regular scheme](wikipedia://en/43016199), for example a smooth variety over a field, is Cohen–Macaulay.
- Any 0-dimensional ring (or equivalently, any [Artinian ring](wikipedia://en/489273)).
- Any 1-dimensional [reduced ring](wikipedia://en/539749), for example any 1-dimensional [domain](wikipedia://en/15462).
- Any 2-dimensional [normal ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_ring).
- Any [Gorenstein ring](wikipedia://en/515613). In particular, any [complete intersection ring](wikipedia://en/3116851).
- The [ring of invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_invariants) $R^G$ when *R* is a Cohen–Macaulay algebra over a field of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) zero and *G* is a finite group (or more generally, a [linear algebraic group](wikipedia://en/395885) whose identity component is [reductive](wikipedia://en/1088386)). This is the [Hochster–Roberts theorem](wikipedia://en/38039504).
- Any determinantal ring. That is, let *R* be the quotient of a regular local ring *S* by the ideal *I* generated by the *r* × *r* [minors](wikipedia://en/262107) of some *p* × *q* [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) of elements of *S*. If the codimension (or [height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Height_of_an_ideal)) of *I* is equal to the "expected" codimension (*p*−*r*+1)(*q*−*r*+1), *R* is called a **determinantal ring**. In that case, *R* is Cohen−Macaulay.[^o9] Similarly, coordinate rings of [determinantal varieties](wikipedia://en/22920002) are Cohen–Macaulay.

Some more examples:

1. The ring *K*\[*x*\]/(*x*²) has dimension 0 and hence is Cohen–Macaulay, but it is not reduced and therefore not regular.
1. The subring *K*\[*t*<sup>2</sup>, *t*<sup>3</sup>\] of the polynomial ring *K*\[*t*\], or its localization or [completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completion_%28ring_theory%29) at *t*=0, is a 1-dimensional domain which is Gorenstein, and hence Cohen–Macaulay, but not regular. This ring can also be described as the coordinate ring of the [cuspidal](wikipedia://en/6851758) cubic curve *y*<sup>2</sup> = *x*<sup>3</sup> over *K*.
1. The subring *K*\[*t*<sup>3</sup>, *t*<sup>4</sup>, *t*<sup>5</sup>\] of the polynomial ring *K*\[*t*\], or its localization or completion at *t*=0, is a 1-dimensional domain which is Cohen–Macaulay but not Gorenstein.

[Rational singularities](wikipedia://en/2629851) over a field of characteristic zero are Cohen–Macaulay. [Toric varieties](wikipedia://en/737087) over any field are Cohen–Macaulay.[^o10] The [minimal model program](wikipedia://en/15148924) makes prominent use of varieties with [klt](wikipedia://en/22910728#Pairs) (Kawamata log terminal) singularities; in characteristic zero, these are rational singularities and hence are Cohen–Macaulay,[^o11] One successful analog of rational singularities in positive characteristic is the notion of **F-rational singularities**; again, such singularities are Cohen–Macaulay.[^o12]

Let *X* be a [projective variety](wikipedia://en/320469) of dimension *n* ≥ 1 over a field, and let *L* be an [ample line bundle](wikipedia://en/1186896) on *X*. Then the section ring of *L*

$$R=\bigoplus_{j\geq 0}H^0(X,L^j)$$

is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if the [cohomology](wikipedia://en/23535337) group *H*<sup>*i*</sup>(*X*, *L*<sup>*j*</sup>) is zero for all 1 ≤ *i* ≤ *n*−1 and all integers *j*.[^o13] It follows, for example, that the affine cone Spec *R* over an [abelian variety](wikipedia://en/247152) *X* is Cohen–Macaulay when *X* has dimension 1, but not when *X* has dimension at least 2 (because *H*<sup>1</sup>(*X*, *O*) is not zero). See also [Generalized Cohen–Macaulay ring](wikipedia://en/60415782).

:::

## Cohen–Macaulay schemes {#Cohen–Macaulay_schemes}

We say that a [locally Noetherian](wikipedia://en/2783891) [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) $X$ is Cohen–Macaulay if at each point $x\in X$ the local ring $\mathcal{O}_{X,x}$ is Cohen–Macaulay.

### Cohen–Macaulay curves {#Cohen–Macaulay_curves}

Cohen–Macaulay curves are a special case of Cohen–Macaulay schemes, but are useful for compactifying moduli spaces of curves[^o16] where the boundary of the smooth locus $\mathcal{M}_g$ is of Cohen–Macaulay curves. There is a useful criterion for deciding whether or not curves are Cohen–Macaulay. Schemes of dimension $\leq 1$ are Cohen–Macaulay if and only if they have no embedded primes.[^o17] The singularities present in Cohen–Macaulay curves can be classified completely by looking at the plane curve case.[^o18]

::: {#o20 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=heading name="Non-examples"}

#### Non-examples {#Non-examples}

Using the criterion, there are easy examples of non-Cohen–Macaulay curves from constructing curves with embedded points. For example, the scheme

$$X = \text{Spec}\left( \frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y]}{(x^2,xy)} \right)$$

has the decomposition into prime ideals $(x)\cdot(x,y)$. Geometrically it is the $y$-axis with an embedded point at the origin, which can be thought of as a *fat point*. Given a smooth projective plane curve $C \subset \mathbb{P}^2$, a curve with an embedded point can be constructed using the same technique: find the ideal $I_x$ of a point in $x \in C$ and multiply it with the ideal $I_C$ of $C$. Then

$$X = \text{Proj}\left( \frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]}{I_C \cdot I_x} \right)$$

is a curve with an embedded point at $x$.

:::

### Intersection theory {#Intersection_theory}

Cohen–Macaulay schemes have a special relation with [intersection theory](wikipedia://en/3148602). Precisely, let *X* be a smooth variety[^o22] and *V*, *W* closed subschemes of pure dimension. Let *Z* be a [proper component](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_component) of the scheme-theoretic intersection $V \times_X W$, that is, an irreducible component of expected dimension. If the local ring *A* of $V \times_X W$ at the [generic point](wikipedia://en/3043978) of *Z* is Cohen–Macaulay, then the [intersection multiplicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersection_multiplicity) of *V* and *W* along *Z* is given as the length of *A*:[^o23]

$$i(Z, V \cdot W, X) = \operatorname{length}(A).$$

In general, that multiplicity is given as a length essentially characterizes Cohen–Macaulay ring; see #Properties. [Multiplicity one criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicity_one_criterion), on the other hand, roughly characterizes a regular local ring as a local ring of multiplicity one.

::: {#o25 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

For a simple example, if we take the intersection of a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) with a line tangent to it, the local ring at the intersection point is isomorphic to

$$\frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y]}{(y - x^2)} \otimes_{\mathbb{C}[x,y]}\frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y]}{(y)} \cong \frac{\mathbb{C}[x]}{(x^2)}$$

which is Cohen–Macaulay of length two, hence the intersection multiplicity is two, as expected.

:::

::: {#o27 .statement cue=named-heading name="Miracle flatness or Hironaka's criterion"}

## Miracle flatness or Hironaka's criterion {#Miracle_flatness_or_Hironaka's_criterion}

There is a remarkable characterization of Cohen–Macaulay rings, sometimes called **[miracle flatness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miracle_flatness)** or **[Hironaka's criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hironaka's_criterion)**. Let *R* be a local ring which is [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) as a module over some regular local ring *A* contained in *R*. Such a subring exists for any localization *R* at a [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) of a [finitely generated algebra](wikipedia://en/11985661) over a field, by the [Noether normalization lemma](wikipedia://en/3003553); it also exists when *R* is complete and contains a field, or when *R* is a complete domain.[^o28] Then *R* is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if it is [flat](wikipedia://en/492616) as an *A*-module; it is also equivalent to say that *R* is [free](wikipedia://en/326454) as an *A*-module.[^o29]

A geometric reformulation is as follows. Let *X* be a [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) [affine scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_scheme) of [finite type](wikipedia://en/70728366) over a field *K* (for example, an [affine variety](wikipedia://en/320468)). Let *n* be the dimension of *X*. By Noether normalization, there is a [finite morphism](wikipedia://en/2139357) *f* from *X* to affine space *A*<sup>*n*</sup> over *K*. Then *X* is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if all fibers of *f* have the same degree.[^o30] It is striking that this property is independent of the choice of *f*.

Finally, there is a version of Miracle Flatness for graded rings. Let *R* be a finitely generated commutative [graded algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded_algebra) over a field *K*,

$$R=K\oplus R_1 \oplus R_2 \oplus \cdots.$$

There is always a graded polynomial subring *A* ⊂ *R* (with generators in various degrees) such that *R* is finitely generated as an *A*-module. Then *R* is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if *R* is free as a graded *A*-module. Again, it follows that this freeness is independent of the choice of the polynomial subring *A*.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

- A Noetherian local ring is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if its completion is Cohen–Macaulay.[^o33]
- If *R* is a Cohen–Macaulay ring, then the polynomial ring *R*\[*x*\] and the power series ring *R*\[\[*x*\]\] are Cohen–Macaulay.[^o34][^o35]
- For a [non-zero-divisor](wikipedia://en/505119) *u* in the maximal ideal of a Noetherian local ring *R*, *R* is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if *R*/(*u*) is Cohen–Macaulay.[^o36]
- The quotient of a Cohen–Macaulay ring by any [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) is [universally catenary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universally_catenary_ring).[^o37]
- If *R* is a quotient of a Cohen–Macaulay ring, then the locus { *p* ∈ Spec *R* | *R*<sub>*p*</sub> is Cohen–Macaulay } is an open subset of Spec *R*.[^o38]
- Let (*R*, *m*, *k*) be a Noetherian local ring of embedding codimension *c*, meaning that *c* = dim<sub>*k*</sub>(*m*/*m*<sup>2</sup>) − dim(*R*). In geometric terms, this holds for a local ring of a subscheme of codimension *c* in a regular scheme. For *c*=1, *R* is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if it is a [hypersurface ring](wikipedia://en/3116851). There is also a structure theorem for Cohen–Macaulay rings of codimension 2, the [Hilbert–Burch theorem](wikipedia://en/33800100): they are all determinantal rings, defined by the *r* × *r* minors of an (*r*+1) × *r* matrix for some *r*.
- For a Noetherian local ring (*R*, *m*), the following are equivalent:[^o39]
  1. *R* is Cohen–Macaulay.
  1. For every [parameter ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameter_ideal) *Q* (an ideal generated by a [system of parameters](wikipedia://en/30823935)),

$\operatorname{length}(R/Q) = e(Q)$ := the [Hilbert–Samuel multiplicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert–Samuel_multiplicity) of *Q*.

  1. For some parameter ideal *Q*, $\operatorname{length}(R/Q) = e(Q)$.

(See [Generalized Cohen–Macaulay ring](wikipedia://en/60415782) as well as [Buchsbaum ring](wikipedia://en/34961349) for rings that generalize this characterization.)

:::

::: {#o41 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="The unmixedness theorem"}

## The unmixedness theorem {#The_unmixedness_theorem}

An ideal *I* of a Noetherian ring *A* is called **unmixed** in height if the height of *I* is equal to the height of every [associated prime](wikipedia://en/17286442) *P* of *A*/*I*. (This is stronger than saying that *A*/*I* is [equidimensional](wikipedia://en/21568751); see below.)

The **unmixedness theorem** is said to hold for the ring *A* if every ideal *I* generated by a number of elements equal to its height is unmixed. A Noetherian ring is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if the unmixedness theorem holds for it.[^o42]

The unmixed theorem applies in particular to the zero ideal (an ideal generated by zero elements) and thus it says a Cohen–Macaulay ring is an [equidimensional ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equidimensional_ring); in fact, in the strong sense: there is no embedded component and each component has the same codimension.

See also: [quasi-unmixed ring](wikipedia://en/60330511) (a ring in which the unmixed theorem holds for [integral closure of an ideal](wikipedia://en/39944913)).

:::

::: {#o44 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=heading name="Counterexamples"}

## Counterexamples {#Counterexamples}

1. If *K* is a field, then the ring *R* = *K*\[*x*,*y*\]/(*x*<sup>2</sup>,*xy*) (the coordinate ring of a line with an embedded point) is not Cohen–Macaulay. This follows, for example, by [Miracle Flatness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miracle_flatness_theorem): *R* is finite over the polynomial ring *A* = *K*\[*y*\], with degree 1 over points of the affine line Spec *A* with *y* ≠ 0, but with degree 2 over the point *y* = 0 (because the *K*-vector space *K*\[*x*\]/(*x*<sup>2</sup>) has dimension 2).
1. If *K* is a field, then the ring *K*\[*x*,*y*,*z*\]/(*xy*,*xz*) (the coordinate ring of the union of a line and a plane) is reduced, but not equidimensional, and hence not Cohen–Macaulay. Taking the quotient by the non-zero-divisor *x*−*z* gives the previous example.
1. If *K* is a field, then the ring *R* = *K*\[*w*,*x*,*y*,*z*\]/(*wy*,*wz*,*xy*,*xz*) (the coordinate ring of the union of two planes meeting in a point) is reduced and equidimensional, but not Cohen–Macaulay. To prove that, one can use [Hartshorne](wikipedia://en/5693379)'s **connectedness theorem**: if *R* is a Cohen–Macaulay local ring of dimension at least 2, then Spec *R* minus its [closed point](wikipedia://en/33086408) is connected.[^o45]

The [Segre product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Segre_product) of two Cohen–Macaulay rings need not be Cohen–Macaulay.[^o46]

:::

## Grothendieck duality {#Grothendieck_duality}

One meaning of the Cohen–Macaulay condition can be seen in [coherent duality](wikipedia://en/1815224) theory. A variety or scheme *X* is Cohen–Macaulay if the "dualizing complex", which *a priori* lies in the [derived category](wikipedia://en/985897) of [sheaves](wikipedia://en/603780) on *X*, is represented by a single sheaf. The stronger property of being [Gorenstein](wikipedia://en/48497362) means that this sheaf is a [line bundle](wikipedia://en/361366). In particular, every [regular scheme](wikipedia://en/43016199) is Gorenstein. Thus the statements of duality theorems such as [Serre duality](wikipedia://en/359175) or [Grothendieck local duality](wikipedia://en/38470542) for Gorenstein or Cohen–Macaulay schemes retain some of the simplicity of what happens for regular schemes or smooth varieties.
