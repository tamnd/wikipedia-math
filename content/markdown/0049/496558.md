# Law of the iterated logarithm

*Image: Law of large numbers.gif.* Plot of $S_n/n$ (red), its standard deviation $1/\sqrt{n}$ (blue) and its bound $\sqrt{2\log\log n/n}$ given by LIL (green). Notice the way it randomly switches from the upper bound to the lower bound. Both axes are non-linearly transformed (as explained in figure summary) to make this effect more visible.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Law of the iterated logarithm"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), the **law of the iterated logarithm** describes the magnitude of the fluctuations of a [random walk](wikipedia://en/235451). The original statement of the law of the iterated logarithm is due to [A. Ya. Khinchin](wikipedia://en/1029748) (1924).[^o2] Another statement was given by [A. N. Kolmogorov](wikipedia://en/91161) in 1929.[^o3]

:::

::: {#o5 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Let {*Y*<sub>*n*</sub>} be independent, identically distributed [random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variables) with zero means and unit variances. Let *S*<sub>*n*</sub> = *Y*<sub>1</sub> + ... + *Y*<sub>*n*</sub>. Then

$$\limsup_{n \to \infty} \frac{|S_n|}{\sqrt{2n \log\log n}} = 1 \quad \text{a.s.},$$

where "log" is the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476), "lim sup" denotes the [limit superior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_superior), and "a.s." stands for "[almost surely](wikipedia://en/351908)".[^o6][^o7]

Another statement given by [A. N. Kolmogorov](wikipedia://en/91161) in 1929[^o3] is as follows.

Let $\{ Y_n \}$ be independent [random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variables) with zero means and finite variances. Let $S_n = Y_1 + \dots + Y_n$ and $B_n = \operatorname{Var}(Y_1) + \dots + \operatorname{Var}(Y_n)$. If $B_n \to \infty$ and there exists a sequence of positive constants $\{ M_n \}$ such that $|Y_n| \le M_n$ a.s. and

$$M_n \;=\; o \left( \sqrt{\frac{B_n}{\log \log B_n}} \right),$$

then we have

$$\limsup_{n \to \infty} \frac{|S_n|}{\sqrt{2 B_n \log\log B_n}} = 1 \quad \text{a.s.}$$

Note that, the first statement covers the case of the standard normal distribution, but the second does not.

:::

## Discussion {#Discussion}

The law of iterated logarithms operates "in between" the [law of large numbers](wikipedia://en/157055) and the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406). There are two versions of the law of large numbers — [the weak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_law_of_large_numbers) and [the strong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_law_of_large_numbers) — and they both state that the sums *S*<sub>*n*</sub>, scaled by *n*<sup>−1</sup>, converge to zero, respectively [in probability](wikipedia://en/50723#Convergence_in_probability) and [almost surely](wikipedia://en/50723#Almost_sure_convergence):

$$\frac{S_n}{n} \ \xrightarrow{p}\ 0, \qquad \frac{S_n}{n} \ \xrightarrow{a.s.} 0, \qquad \text{as}\ \ n\to\infty.$$

On the other hand, the central limit theorem states that the sums *S*<sub>*n*</sub> scaled by the factor *n*<sup>−1/2</sup> converge in distribution to a standard normal distribution. By [Kolmogorov's zero–one law](wikipedia://en/191797), for any fixed *M*, the probability that the event $\limsup_n \frac{S_n}{\sqrt{n}} \geq M$ occurs is 0 or 1. Then

$$\Pr\left( \limsup_n \frac{S_n}{\sqrt{n}} \geq M \right) \geqslant \limsup_n \Pr\left( \frac{S_n}{\sqrt{n}} \geq M \right) = \Pr\left( \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \geq M \right) > 0$$

so

$$\limsup_n \frac{S_n}{\sqrt{n}}=\infty \qquad \text{with probability 1.}$$

An identical argument shows that

$$\liminf_n \frac{S_n}{\sqrt{n}}=-\infty \qquad \text{with probability 1.}$$

This implies that these quantities cannot converge almost surely. In fact, they cannot even converge in probability, which follows from the equality

$$\frac{S_{2n}}{\sqrt{2n}}-\frac{S_n}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac1{\sqrt2}\frac{S_{2n}-S_n}{\sqrt{n}} - \left (1-\frac1\sqrt2 \right )\frac{S_n}{\sqrt{n}}$$

and the fact that the random variables

$$\frac{S_n}{\sqrt{n}}\quad \text{and} \quad \frac{S_{2n}-S_n}{\sqrt{n}}$$

are independent and both converge in distribution to $\mathcal{N}(0, 1).$

The *law of the iterated logarithm* provides the scaling factor where the two limits become different:

$$\frac{S_n}{\sqrt{2n\log\log n}} \ \xrightarrow{p}\ 0, \qquad \frac{S_n}{\sqrt{2n\log\log n}} \ \stackrel{a.s.}{\nrightarrow}\ 0, \qquad \text{as}\ \ n\to\infty.$$

Thus, although the absolute value of the quantity $S_n/\sqrt{2n\log\log n}$ is less than any predefined *ε* > 0 with probability approaching one, it will nevertheless almost surely be greater than *ε* infinitely often; in fact, the quantity will be visiting the neighborhoods of any point in the interval (-1,1) almost surely.

## Generalizations and variants {#Generalizations_and_variants}

The law of the iterated logarithm (LIL) for a sum of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with zero mean and bounded increment dates back to [Khinchin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khinchin) and [Kolmogorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov) in the 1920s.

Since then, there has been a tremendous amount of work on the LIL for various kinds of dependent structures and for stochastic processes. The following is a small sample of notable developments.

[Hartman](wikipedia://en/35263118)–[Wintner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurel_Wintner) (1940) generalized LIL to random walks with increments with zero mean and finite variance. De Acosta (1983) gave a simple proof of the Hartman–Wintner version of the LIL.[^o10]

[Chung](wikipedia://en/23576590) (1948) proved another version of the law of the iterated logarithm for the absolute value of a brownian motion.[^o11]

[Strassen](wikipedia://en/771960) (1964) studied the LIL from the point of view of invariance principles.[^o12]

Stout (1970) generalized the LIL to stationary ergodic martingales.[^o13]

Wittmann (1985) generalized Hartman–Wintner version of LIL to random walks satisfying milder conditions.[^o14]

A survey up to 1986.[^o15]

[Vladimir Vovk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Vovk) (1987) derived a version of LIL valid for a single chaotic sequence (Kolmogorov random sequence).[^o16] This is notable, as it is outside the realm of classical probability theory.

[Yongge Wang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yongge_Wang) (1996) showed that the law of the iterated logarithm holds for polynomial time pseudorandom sequences also.[^o17][^o18] The Java-based software [testing tool](http://webpages.uncc.edu/yonwang/liltest/) tests whether a pseudorandom generator outputs sequences that satisfy the LIL.

Balsubramani (2014) proved a non-asymptotic LIL that holds over finite-time [martingale](wikipedia://en/274536) sample paths.[^o19] This subsumes the martingale LIL as it provides matching finite-sample concentration and anti-concentration bounds, and enables sequential testing[^o20] and other applications.[^o21]
