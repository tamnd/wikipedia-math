# Cat's cradle

*Image: Cats-cradle.svg.* "Cradle", the first (and opening) position of Cat's cradle

*Image: String Figures and How to Make Them.djvu.* "Cat's eye to fish in a dish" illustration from Jayne (1906)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cat's cradle"}

**Cat's cradle** is a game involving the creation of various [string figures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_figure) between the fingers, either individually or by passing a loop of string back and forth between two or more players. The true origin of the name is debated, though the first known reference is in *The light of nature pursued* by [Abraham Tucker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Tucker) in 1768.[^o2] The type of string, the specific figures, their order, and the names of the figures vary. Independent versions of this game have been found in [indigenous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples) cultures throughout the world, including in Africa, Eastern Asia, the Pacific Islands, Australia, the Americas, and the Arctic.[^o3][^o4][^o5]

:::

## Play {#Play}

*Image: Eishosai Choki - Cat's Cradle.jpg.* *The lovers, Okiku and Yosuke, play cat’s cradle*, by [Eishōsai Chōki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eishōsai_Chōki) (1804)

The simplest version of the game involves a player using a long string loop to make a complex figure using their fingers and hands.[^o7]

Another version of the game consists of two or more players making a sequence of string figures, each altering the figure made by the previous player. The game begins with one player making the eponymous figure *Cat's Cradle* (above). After each figure, the next player manipulates that figure and removes the string figure from the hands of the previous player with one of a few simple motions and tightens the loop to create another figure, for example, *Diamonds*. *Diamonds* might then lead to *Candles* (which is also known as *Pinkies*), for example, and then *Manger*—an inverted *Cat's Cradle*—and so on. Most of the core figures allow a choice between two or more subsequent figures: for example, *Fish in a Dish* can become *Cat's Eye* or *Manger*. The game ends when a player makes a mistake or creates a dead-end figure, such as *Two Royal Crowns* (also known as *Grandfather Clock*), which cannot be turned into anything else.[^o8]

## History {#History}

*Image: Mma two young women seated by a kotatsu playing cats cradle 55802.jpg.* "Two Young Women Seated by a [Kotatsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kotatsu) Playing Cat's Cradle", [Suzuki Harunobu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzuki_Harunobu), Japan, ca. 1765

The origin of the name "cat's cradle" is debated but the first known reference is in *The light of nature pursued* by [Abraham Tucker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Tucker) in 1768.[^o2]

An ingenious play they call cat's cradle; one ties the two ends of a packthread together, and then winds it about his fingers, another with both hands takes it off perhaps in the shape of a gridiron, the first takes it from him again in another form, and so on alternately changing the packthread into a multitude of figures whose names I forget, it being so many years since I played at it myself.[^o2]

The name may have come from a corruption of cratch-cradle, or [manger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manger) [cradle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bassinet) (although this derivation is disputed by the [OED](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary)). The connection between the two words, *cratches* and *cradle*, may come from the Christian [story of the birth of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nativity_of_Jesus), in which a manger is used as a cradle.[^o10][^o11]

In an 1858 *[Punch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punch_%28magazine%29)* cartoon,[^o12] it is referred to as "scratch cradle", a name supported by [Brewer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._Cobham_Brewer)'s 1898 *[Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brewer's_Dictionary_of_Phrase_and_Fable)*.[^o13] As "Cat's cradle" often is used to refer to string figures and games in general, Jayne uses "Real Cat's-Cradle" to refer to the specific game.[^o14]

Different [cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture) have different names for the game, and often different names for the individual figures. The [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) word for manger is crèche, and cattle feed racks are still known as cratches. In [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan), it is called . In [Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea), it is called ko. In [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia), the whole game is called simply *the game of string*, and the *diamonds* pattern is called *carpet*, with other pattern names such as *field*, *fish*, and *sawhorse* for the other figures—a *cat* isn't mentioned.[^o15] The game may have originated in China.[^o16] In [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China), the game is called .[^o14][^o15] In [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel), the game is called "Knitting Grandmother" (in [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew): סבתא סורגת, he). In some regions of the [U.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States), this game also is known as *Jack in the Pulpit*.[^o17]

## World records {#World_records}

Geneva Hultenius, Maryann Divona, and Rita Divona completed 21,200 changes of cat's cradles in 21 hours in [Chula Vista, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chula_Vista,_California) between August 17–18, 1974. The *[Guinness Book of World Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness_World_Records)* reported it as a world record in the 1975 and 1976 editions.[^o19]

Jane Muir and Robyn Lawrick completed 22,700 changes of cat's cradles in 21 hours at [Calgary Market Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_Mall), Alberta, Canada on August 25, 1976.[^o20]
