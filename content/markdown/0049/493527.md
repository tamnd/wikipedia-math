# Order (group theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Order"}

*Image: Powers of rotation, shear, and their compositions.svg.* Examples of [transformations](wikipedia://en/18102) with different orders: 90° rotation with order 4, [shearing](wikipedia://en/799405) with infinite order, and their [compositions](wikipedia://en/195947) with order 3.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **order** of a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) is the number of its elements. If a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) is not finite, one says that its order is *infinite*.

:::

The *order* of an element of a group (also called **period length** or **period**) is the order of the subgroup generated by the element. If the group operation is denoted as a [multiplication](wikipedia://en/1110742), the order of an element $a$ of a group, is thus the smallest [positive integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_integer) $m$ such that $a^{m} = e$, where $e$ denotes the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) of the group, and $a^{m}$ denotes the product of $m$ copies of $a$. If no such $m$ exists, the order of $a$ is infinite.

The order of a group $G$ is denoted by $\operatorname{ord}(G)$ or $\lvert G\rvert$, and the order of an element $a$ is denoted by $\operatorname{ord}(a)$ or $\lvert a\rvert$, instead of $\operatorname{ord}(\langle a\rangle),$ where the brackets denote the generated group.

[Lagrange's theorem](wikipedia://en/31150) states that for any subgroup $H$ of a finite group $G$, the order of the subgroup divides the order of the group; that is, $\lvert H\rvert$ is a [divisor](wikipedia://en/49492) of $\lvert G\rvert$. In particular, the order $\lvert a\rvert$ of any element is a divisor of $\lvert G\rvert$.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

The [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) S<sub>3</sub> has the following [multiplication table](wikipedia://en/1802723).

{| class="wikitable"

\|- ! • ! *e* || *s* || *t* || *u* || *v* || *w* |- ! *e* | *e* || *s* || *t* || *u* || *v* || *w* |- ! *s* | *s* || *e* || *v* || *w* || *t* || *u* |- ! *t* | *t* || *u* || *e* || *s* || *w* || *v* |- ! *u* | *u* || *t* || *w* || *v* || *e* || *s* |- ! *v* | *v* || *w* || *s* || *e* || *u* || *t* |- ! *w* | *w* || *v* || *u* || *t* || *s* || *e* |}

This group has six elements, so $\operatorname{ord}(S_{3}) = 6$. By definition, the order of the identity, $e$, is one, since $e^{1} = e$. Each of $s$, $t$, and $w$ squares to $e$, so these group elements have order two: $|s| = |t| = |w| = 2$. Finally, $u$ and $v$ have order 3, since $u^{3} = vu = e$, and $v^{3} = uv = e$.

:::

## Order and structure {#Order_and_structure}

The order of a group *G* and the orders of its elements give much information about the structure of the group. Roughly speaking, the more complicated the [factorization](wikipedia://en/82341) of |*G*|, the more complicated the structure of *G*.

For |*G*| = 1, the group is [trivial](wikipedia://en/193059). In any group, only the identity element *a = e* has ord(*a)* = 1. If every non-identity element in *G* is equal to its inverse (so that *a*<sup>2</sup> = *e*), then ord(*a*) = 2; this implies *G* is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) [since](wikipedia://en/41890#Inverse_of_ab) $ab=(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}=ba$. The converse is not true; for example, the (additive) [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) **Z**<sub>6</sub> of integers [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) 6 is abelian, but the number 2 has order 3:

$$2+2+2=6 \equiv 0 \pmod {6}.$$

The relationship between the two concepts of order is the following: if we write

$$\langle a \rangle = \{ a^{k}\colon k \in \mathbb{Z} \}$$

for the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by *a*, then

$$\operatorname{ord} (a) = \operatorname{ord}(\langle a \rangle).$$

For any integer *k*, we have

*a<sup>k</sup>* = *e* if and only if ord(*a*) [divides](wikipedia://en/49492) *k*.

In general, the order of any subgroup of *G* divides the order of *G*. More precisely: if *H* is a subgroup of *G*, then

ord(*G*) / ord(*H*) = \[*G* : *H*\], where \[*G* : *H*\] is called the [index](wikipedia://en/230581) of *H* in *G*, an integer. This is [Lagrange's theorem](wikipedia://en/31150). (This is, however, only true when G has finite order. If ord(*G*) = ∞, the quotient ord(*G*) / ord(*H*) does not make sense.)

As an immediate consequence of the above, we see that the order of every element of a group divides the order of the group. For example, in the symmetric group shown above, where ord(S<sub>3</sub>) = 6, the possible orders of the elements are 1, 2, 3 or 6.

The following partial converse is true for [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707): if *d* divides the order of a group *G* and *d* is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666), then there exists an element of order *d* in *G* (this is sometimes called [Cauchy's theorem](wikipedia://en/2838129)). The statement does not hold for [composite](wikipedia://en/82289) orders, e.g. the [Klein four-group](wikipedia://en/41928) does not have an element of order four. This can be shown by [inductive proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_proof).[^o5] The consequences of the theorem include: the order of a group *G* is a power of a prime *p* if and only if ord(*a*) is some power of *p* for every *a* in *G*.[^o6]

If *a* has infinite order, then all non-zero powers of *a* have infinite order as well. If *a* has finite order, we have the following formula for the order of the powers of *a*:

ord(*a<sup>k</sup>*) = ord(*a*) / [gcd](wikipedia://en/12354)(ord(*a*), *k*)[^o7]

for every integer *k*. In particular, *a* and its inverse *a*<sup>−1</sup> have the same order.

In any group,

$$\operatorname{ord}(ab) = \operatorname{ord}(ba)$$

There is no general formula relating the order of a product *ab* to the orders of *a* and *b*. In fact, it is possible that both *a* and *b* have finite order while *ab* has infinite order, or that both *a* and *b* have infinite order while *ab* has finite order. An example of the former is *a*(*x*) = 2−*x*, *b*(*x*) = 1−*x* with *ab*(*x*) = *x*−1 in the group $Sym(\mathbb{Z})$. An example of the latter is *a*(*x*) = *x*+1, *b*(*x*) = *x*−1 with *ab*(*x*) = *x*. If *ab* = *ba*, we can at least say that ord(*ab*) divides [lcm](wikipedia://en/17961)(ord(*a*), ord(*b*)). As a consequence, one can prove that in a finite abelian group, if *m* denotes the maximum of all the orders of the group's elements, then every element's order divides *m*.

## Counting by order of elements {#Counting_by_order_of_elements}

Suppose *G* is a finite group of order *n*, and *d* is a divisor of *n*. The number of order *d* elements in *G* is a multiple of φ(*d*) (possibly zero), where φ is [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452), giving the number of positive integers no larger than *d* and [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) to it. For example, in the case of S<sub>3</sub>, φ(3) = 2, and we have exactly two elements of order 3. The theorem provides no useful information about elements of order 2, because φ(2) = 1, and is only of limited utility for composite *d* such as *d* = 6, since φ(6) = 2, and there are zero elements of order 6 in S<sub>3</sub>.

## In relation to homomorphisms {#In_relation_to_homomorphisms}

[Group homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/12396) tend to reduce the orders of elements: if *f*: *G* → *H* is a homomorphism, and *a* is an element of *G* of finite order, then ord(*f*(*a*)) divides ord(*a*). If *f* is [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective), then ord(*f*(*a*)) = ord(*a*). This can often be used to prove that there are no homomorphisms or no injective homomorphisms, between two explicitly given groups. (For example, there can be no nontrivial homomorphism *h*: S<sub>3</sub> → **Z**<sub>5</sub>, because every number except zero in **Z**<sub>5</sub> has order 5, which does not divide the orders 1, 2, and 3 of elements in S<sub>3</sub>.) A further consequence is that [conjugate elements](wikipedia://en/49176) have the same order.

## Class equation {#Class_equation}

An important result about orders is the [class equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_equation); it relates the order of a finite group *G* to the order of its [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_a_group) Z(*G*) and the sizes of its non-trivial [conjugacy classes](wikipedia://en/49176):

$$|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum_{i}d_i\;$$

where the *d<sub>i</sub>* are the sizes of the non-trivial conjugacy classes; these are proper divisors of |*G*| bigger than one, and they are also equal to the indices of the centralizers in *G* of the representatives of the non-trivial conjugacy classes. For example, the center of S<sub>3</sub> is just the trivial group with the single element *e*, and the equation reads |S<sub>3</sub>| = 1+2+3.
