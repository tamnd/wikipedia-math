# Gabriel Cramer

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Gabriel Cramer"}

**Gabriel Cramer** (; 31 July 1704 – 4 January 1752) was a Genevan [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Cramer was born on 31 July 1704 in Geneva, [Republic of Geneva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Geneva) to Jean-Isaac Cramer, a physician, and Anne Mallet.[^o4] The progenitor of the Cramer family in Geneva was Jean-Ulrich Cramer, Gabriel's great-grandfather, who immigrated from [Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strasbourg) in 1634.[^o5] Cramer's mother, a member of the [Mallet family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mallet_family), was of [Huguenot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huguenot) origin.[^o6] Cramer showed promise in mathematics from an early age. In 1722, aged 18, he received his doctorate from the [Academy of Geneva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Geneva), and at 20 he was made co-chair (along with [Jean-Louis Calandrini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Louis_Calandrini)) of mathematics at the Academy.[^o4]

He became the sole professor of mathematics in 1734 and was appointed professor of philosophy at the Academy in 1750.[^o4] Cramer was also involved in the politics of the Republic of Geneva, entering first the [Council of Two Hundred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Two_Hundred) in 1734 then the Council of Sixty in 1750.[^o4] He was a member of the science academies of [Bologna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Sciences_of_the_Institute_of_Bologna), [Lyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Lyon), and [Montpellier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montpellier), as well as a foreign member of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society) of London and the [Royal Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_Academy_of_Sciences) of Berlin.[^o4] Cramer died on 4 January 1752 at [Bagnols-sur-Cèze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bagnols-sur-Cèze) while traveling in southern France to restore his health.[^o4]

## Contributions to mathematics {#Contributions_to_mathematics}

In 1728, Cramer proposed a solution to the [St. Petersburg Paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Petersburg_Paradox) that came very close to the concept of [expected utility theory](wikipedia://en/736803) given ten years later by [Daniel Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/46784). He did extensive travel throughout Europe in the late 1730s, which greatly influenced his works in mathematics.

Cramer published his best-known work in his forties. This included his treatise on [algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/253260) (1750). It contains the earliest demonstration that a curve of the *n*-th degree is determined by *n*(*n* + 3)/2 points on it, in [general position](wikipedia://en/383736) (see [Cramer's theorem (algebraic curves)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cramer's_theorem_%28algebraic_curves%29)). This led to the misconception that is [Cramer's paradox](wikipedia://en/22637653), concerning the number of intersections of two curves compared to the number of points that determine a curve.

Cramer edited the works of the two elder [Bernoullis](wikipedia://en/199226), and wrote on the physical cause of the spheroidal shape of the [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet) and the motion of their [apsides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apsides) (1730), and on [Newton](wikipedia://en/14627)'s treatment of [cubic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_curve) (1746).

In 1750 he published [Cramer's rule](wikipedia://en/166008), giving a general formula for the solution for any unknown in a linear equation system having a unique solution, in terms of [determinants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinant_%28mathematics%29) implied by the system. This rule is still standard.
