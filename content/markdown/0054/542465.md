# Locus (mathematics)

*Image: Locus Curve.svg.* Each curve in this example is a *locus* defined as the [conchoid](wikipedia://en/898856) of the point $P$ and the line $l$. In this example, $P$ is 8 cm from $l$.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Locus"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **locus** (plural: *loci*; [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) for place, location) is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all [points](wikipedia://en/593693) (commonly, a [line](wikipedia://en/946975), a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860), a [curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curve_%28mathematics%29) or a [surface](wikipedia://en/27865)), whose location satisfies or is determined by one or more specified conditions.[^o2][^o3]

:::

The set of the points that satisfy some property is often called the *locus of a point* satisfying this property. The use of the singular in this formulation is a witness that, until the end of the 19th century, mathematicians did not consider [infinite sets](wikipedia://en/142679). Instead of viewing lines and curves as sets of points, they viewed them as places where a point may be *located* or may move.

::: {#o5 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Singular loci"}

## History and philosophy {#History_and_philosophy}

Until the beginning of the 20th century, a geometrical shape (for example a curve) was not considered as an infinite set of points; rather, it was considered as an entity on which a point may be located or on which it moves. Thus a [circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_%28mathematics%29) in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) was defined as the *locus* of a point that is at a given distance of a fixed point, the center of the circle. In modern mathematics, similar concepts are more frequently reformulated by describing shapes as sets; for instance, one says that the circle is the set of points that are at a given distance from the center.[^o6]

In contrast to the set-theoretic view, the old formulation avoids considering infinite collections, as avoiding the [actual infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actual_infinity) was an important philosophical position of earlier mathematicians.[^o7][^o10]

Once [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) became the universal basis over which the whole mathematics is built,[^o8] the term of locus became rather old-fashioned.[^o9] Nevertheless, the word is still widely used, mainly for a concise formulation, for example:

- *[Critical locus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_locus)*, the set of the [critical points](wikipedia://en/1951424) of a [differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206).
- *Zero locus* or *vanishing locus*, the set of points where a function vanishes, in that it takes the [value](wikipedia://en/4095147) zero.
- *Singular locus*, the set of the [singular points of an algebraic variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_points_of_an_algebraic_variety).
- *[Connectedness locus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connectedness_locus)*, the subset of the parameter set of a family of [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) for which the [Julia set](wikipedia://en/56434) of the function is connected.

More recently, techniques such as the theory of [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754), and the use of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869) instead of [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) to give a foundation to mathematics, have returned to notions more like the original definition of a locus as an object in itself rather than as a set of points.[^o10]

:::

## Examples in plane geometry {#Examples_in_plane_geometry}

Examples from plane geometry include:

- The set of points equidistant from two points is a [perpendicular bisector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular_bisector) to the [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) connecting the two points.[^o12]
- The set of points equidistant from two intersecting lines is the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of their two [angle bisectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_bisector).
- All [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673) are loci:[^o13]
  - [Circle](wikipedia://en/6220): the set of points at constant distance (the [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815)) from a fixed point (the [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_%28geometry%29)).
  - [Parabola](wikipedia://en/23231): the set of points equidistant from a fixed point (the [focus](wikipedia://en/1032607)) and a line (the [directrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directrix_%28conic_section%29)).
  - [Hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052): the set of points for each of which the absolute value of the difference between the distances to two given foci is a constant.
  - [Ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277): the set of points for each of which the sum of the distances to two given foci is a constant

Other examples of loci appear in various areas of mathematics. For example, in [complex dynamics](wikipedia://en/650518), the [Mandelbrot set](wikipedia://en/19562#Formal_definition) is a subset of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) that may be characterized as the [connectedness locus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connectedness_locus) of a family of polynomial maps.

## Proof of a locus {#Proof_of_a_locus}

To prove a geometric shape is the correct locus for a given set of conditions, one generally divides the proof into two stages: the proof that all the points that satisfy the conditions are on the given shape, and the proof that all the points on the given shape satisfy the conditions.[^o16]

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Locus apollonius.svg.* (distance *PA*) = 3.(distance *PB*)

### First example {#First_example}

Find the locus of a point *P* that has a given ratio of distances *k* = *d*<sub>1</sub>/*d*<sub>2</sub> to two given points.

In this example *k* = 3, *A*(−1, 0) and *B*(0, 2) are chosen as the fixed points.

*P*(*x*, *y*) is a point of the locus

$$\Leftrightarrow |PA| = 3 |PB|$$

$$\Leftrightarrow |PA|^2 = 9 |PB|^2$$

$$\Leftrightarrow (x + 1)^2 + (y - 0)^2 = 9(x - 0)^2 + 9(y - 2)^2$$

$$\Leftrightarrow 8(x^2 + y^2) - 2x - 36y + 35 = 0$$

$$\Leftrightarrow \left(x - \frac18\right)^2 + \left(y - \frac94\right)^2 = \frac{45}{64}.$$

This equation represents a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) with center (1/8, 9/4) and radius $\tfrac{3}{8}\sqrt{5}$. It is the [circle of Apollonius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_of_Apollonius#Apollonius'_definition_of_a_circle) defined by these values of *k*, *A*, and *B*.

### Second example {#Second_example}

*Image: Locus3a.svg.* Locus of point C

A triangle *ABC* has a fixed side \[*AB*\] with length *c*. Determine the locus of the third [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) *C* such that the [medians](wikipedia://en/1072006) from *A* and *C* are [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal).

Choose an [orthonormal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthonormal) [coordinate system](wikipedia://en/81931) such that *A*(−*c*/2, 0), *B*(*c*/2, 0). *C*(*x*, *y*) is the variable third vertex. The center of \[*BC*\] is *M*((2*x* + *c*)/4, *y*/2). The median from *C* has a slope *y*/*x*. The median *AM* has [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) 2*y*/(2*x* + 3*c*).

*Image: Locus3.svg.* The locus is a circle

*C*(*x*, *y*) is a point of the locus

$\Leftrightarrow$ the medians from *A* and *C* are orthogonal

$$\Leftrightarrow \frac{y}{x} \cdot \frac{2y}{2x + 3c} = -1$$

$$\Leftrightarrow 2 y^2 + 2x^2 + 3c x = 0$$

$$\Leftrightarrow x^2 + y^2 + (3c/2) x = 0$$

$$\Leftrightarrow (x + 3c/4)^2 + y^2 = 9c^2/16.$$

The locus of the vertex *C* is a circle with center (−3*c*/4, 0) and radius 3*c*/4.

### Third example {#Third_example}

*Image: Geassocieerde rechten.svg.* The intersection point of the associated lines *k* and *l* describes the circle

A locus can also be defined by two associated curves depending on one common [parameter](wikipedia://en/25065). If the parameter varies, the intersection points of the associated curves describe the locus.

In the figure, the points *K* and *L* are fixed points on a given line *m*. The line *k* is a variable line through *K*. The line *l* through *L* is [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to *k*. The angle $\alpha$ between *k* and *m* is the parameter. *k* and *l* are associated lines depending on the common parameter. The variable intersection point *S* of *k* and *l* describes a circle. This circle is the locus of the intersection point of the two associated lines.

### Fourth example {#Fourth_example}

A locus of points need not be one-dimensional (as a circle, line, etc.). For example,[^o2] the locus of the inequality $2x + 3y - 6 < 0$ is the portion of the plane that is below the line of equation $2x + 3y - 6 = 0$.
