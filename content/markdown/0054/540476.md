# Subobject

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Subobject"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **subobject** of an object $A$ in a category is a [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538) into $A$, “up to isomorphism”. In various categories of [mathematical structures](wikipedia://en/1809181), this coincides with the standard notion of a substructure of the given type of structure. For example, subobjects correspond to [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) in the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829), to [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) in the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092), to [subrings](wikipedia://en/24873145) in the [category of rings](wikipedia://en/15300181), etc.[^n1]

:::

The [dual](wikipedia://en/310885) concept to a subobject is a **quotient object**. This generalizes concepts such as [quotient sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_set), [quotient groups](wikipedia://en/11526), [quotient rings](wikipedia://en/184635), etc.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Let $A$ be an object of a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) $\mathcal{C}$. The monomorphisms into $A$ are equipped with a canonical [preorder](wikipedia://en/23582): for $u : S \hookrightarrow A$ and $v : T \hookrightarrow A$, we put $u \leq v$ when $u$ [factors through](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_jargon#factor_through) $v$, i.e., there exists $\phi : S \to T$ such that $u = v \circ \phi$. When this is the case, $\phi$ is unique because $v$ is a monomorphism, and $\phi$ is also a monomorphism because $u$ is.

This preorder gives rise to an equivalence relation $\equiv$, namely, $u \equiv v$ when $u \leq v$ and $v \leq u$. When this holds, the unique morphism $\phi$ such that $u = v \circ \phi$ and the unique morphism $\phi'$ such that $v = u \circ \phi'$ are [isomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_%28category_theory%29) inverse to each other (by uniqueness of the factorizations $u = u \circ \operatorname{id}$, $v = v \circ \operatorname{id}$).

The **subobjects** of $A$ are defined by quotienting monomorphisms into $A$ by this equivalence relation. This means that a subobject is represented by a monomorphism into $A$, with the provision that two such monomorphisms which factor through each other are considered equal as subobjects. The subobjects are equipped with the [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) induced by the preorder $\leq$ on monomorphisms into $A$.

The collection of subobjects of an object may in fact be a [proper class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_class). If the subobject collection of every object is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691), the category $\mathcal{C}$ is called **well-powered** (or, rarely, *locally small*, but this clashes with a different usage of the term [locally small](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_small_category), namely that the morphisms between any two objects form a set).

If $\mathcal{C}$ has [pullbacks](wikipedia://en/841689), then a morphism $f : A \to B$ gives rise to an [order-preserving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order-preserving) map from subobjects of $B$ to subobjects of $A$, defined at the level of representing monomorphisms by pulling back along $f$. If $\mathcal{C}$ is additionally well-powered, this gives rise to a **subobject functor** $\operatorname{Sub}_\mathcal{C} : \mathcal{C}^\operatorname{op} \to \operatorname{Poset}$.[^n2]

The concept of a **quotient** object is formally dual: a quotient object of $A$ is an epimorphism from $A$, with the provision that two such epimorphisms which factor through each other are considered equal as quotient objects.

## Examples {#Examples}

Quotient objects

In the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829), the monomorphisms are the injective functions. Given a subset $S \subseteq A$, the [inclusion map](wikipedia://en/492516) $S \hookrightarrow A$ defines a subobject, and every subobject is of this form because two monomorphisms into $A$ are equivalent if and only if they have the same image. The subobject functor is the contravariant powerset functor $\mathcal{P} : \operatorname{Set}^\operatorname{op} \to \operatorname{Poset}$, which sends a set $A$ to its power set $\mathcal{P}(A)$ partially ordered by inclusion, and sends a function $f : A \to B$ to the function $\mathcal{P}(B) \to \mathcal{P}(A)$ that maps a subset $S \subseteq B$ to its inverse image $f^{-1}(S) \subseteq A$.

There are many other categories where subobjects correspond to a standard notion, for instance:

- A subobject of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) is a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011),
- A subobject of a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) is a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145),
- A subobject of a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) is a [submodule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule).

Likewise,

- A quotient object of a set is a [quotient set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_set),
- A quotient object of a group is a [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526),
- A quotient object of a module is a [quotient module](wikipedia://en/364820).

A subobject of a [terminal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_object) is called a [subterminal object](wikipedia://en/49651604).

## Properties {#Properties}

- There is always a greatest subobject of $A$, represented by the identity morphism $\operatorname{id} : A \to A$.
- In an [elementary topos](wikipedia://en/815802), the poset of subobjects of any object is a [Heyting algebra](wikipedia://en/309343).

:::

## Regular subobjects {#Regular_subobjects}

A **regular subobject** is a subobject represented by a [regular monomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_monomorphism), namely a morphism (automatically a monomorphism) which arises as the [equalizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equalizer_%28category_theory%29) of two parallel morphisms. Dually, a **regular quotient object** is represented by a [regular epimorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_epimorphism), namely a coequalizer of two parallel morphisms.

In some categories, the categorical notion of subobject does not concord with the usual notion whereas the categorical notion of regular subobject does. For example, in the category of rings, the inclusion $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$ is an epimorphism but is not the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) of $\mathbb{Z}$ by an [ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_%28algebra%29), whereas regular subobjects correspond to quotient rings.

Another example is the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051), where regular subobjects of $X$ correspond to [subspaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspace_%28topology%29) of $X$ because the regular monomorphisms are the subspace embeddings, whereas the monomorphisms are all injective continuous functions, and so subobjects of $X$ correspond to subsets of $X$ with a topology [refining](wikipedia://en/393069) the subspace topology. Similarly, regular quotient objects of $X$ correspond to [quotient spaces](wikipedia://en/237213) of $X$ whereas quotient objects in general correspond to quotient sets of $X$ with a topology coarser than the quotient topology.
