# Binomial (polynomial)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Binomial"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), a **binomial** is a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) that is the sum of two terms, each of which is a [monomial](wikipedia://en/357416). It is the simplest kind of a [sparse polynomial](wikipedia://en/67960783) after the monomials.

:::

A [toric ideal](wikipedia://en/45258510) is an ideal that is generated by binomials that are difference of [monomials](wikipedia://en/357416); that is, binomials whose two coefficients are $1$ and $-1$. A [toric variety](wikipedia://en/737087) is an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) defined by a toric ideal.

For every admissible [monomial ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monomial_ordering), the minimal [Gröbner basis](wikipedia://en/357328) of a toric ideal consists only of differences of monomials. (This is an immediate consequence of [Buchberger's algorithm](wikipedia://en/696317) that can produce only differences of monomials when starting with differences of monomials.

Similarly, a *binomial ideal* is an ideal generated by monomials and binomials (that is, the above constraint on the coefficient is released), and the minimal Gröbner basis of a binomial ideal contains only monomials and binomials. Monomials must be included in the definition of a binomial ideal, because, for example, if a binomial ideal contains $y-x$ and $y-2x$, it contains also $(y-x)-(y-2x)=x$.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A binomial is a polynomial which is the sum of two monomials. A binomial in a single [indeterminate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_%28variable%29) (also known as a [univariate](wikipedia://en/2398982) binomial) can be written in the form

$$a x^m - bx^n ,$$

where $a$ and $b$ are [numbers](wikipedia://en/21690), and $m$ and $n$ are distinct non-negative [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) and $x$ is a symbol which is called an [indeterminate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_%28variable%29) or, for historical reasons, a [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109). In the context of [Laurent polynomials](wikipedia://en/61351), a *Laurent binomial*, often simply called a *binomial*, is similarly defined, but the exponents $m$ and $n$ may be negative.

More generally, a binomial may be written[^o5] as:

$$a\, x_1^{n_1}\dotsb x_i^{n_i} - b\, x_1^{m_1}\dotsb x_i^{m_i}$$

:::

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

$$3x - 2x^2$$

$$xy + yx^2$$

$$0.9 x^3 + \pi y^2$$

$$2 x^3 + 7$$

$$11x - 2x^2$$

## Operations on simple binomials {#Operations_on_simple_binomials}

- The binomial $x^{2} - y^{2}$, the [difference of two squares](wikipedia://en/1102297), can be [factored](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factored) as the product of two other binomials:

$$x^2 - y^2 = (x - y)(x + y).$$

This is a [special case](wikipedia://en/31308010) of the more general formula:

$$x^{n+1} - y^{n+1} = (x - y)\sum_{k=0}^{n} x^{k} y^{n-k}.$$

When working over the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), this can also be extended to:

$$x^2 + y^2 = x^2 - (iy)^2 = (x - iy)(x + iy).$$

- The product of a pair of linear binomials $(ax + b)$ and $(cx + d\,)$ is a [trinomial](wikipedia://en/2647057):

$$(ax+b)(cx+d) = acx^2+(ad+bc)x+bd.$$

- A binomial raised to the $n$<sup>th</sup> [power](wikipedia://en/99491), represented as $(x + y)^{n}$ can be expanded by means of the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677) or, equivalently, using [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497). For example, the [square](wikipedia://en/659942) $(x + y)^{2}$ of the binomial $(x + y)$ is equal to the sum of the squares of the two terms and twice the product of the terms, that is:

$$(x + y)^2 = x^2 + 2xy + y^2.$$

The numbers (1, 2, 1) appearing as multipliers for the terms in this expansion are the [binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/4668) two rows down from the top of Pascal's triangle. The expansion of the $n$<sup>th</sup> power uses the numbers $n$ rows down from the top of the triangle.

- An application of the above formula for the square of a binomial is the "$(m,\,n)$-formula" for generating [Pythagorean triples](wikipedia://en/24172):

For $m < n$, let $a = n^{2} - m^{2}$, $b = 2mn$, and $c = n^{2} + m^{2}$; then $a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2}$.

- Binomials that are sums or differences of [cubes](wikipedia://en/451445) can be factored into smaller-[degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) polynomials as follows:

$$x^3 + y^3 = (x + y)(x^2 - xy + y^2)$$

$$x^3 - y^3 = (x - y)(x^2 + xy + y^2)$$
