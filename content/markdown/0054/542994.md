# Happy number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Happy number"}

*Image: DessinArbreHeureux01.svg.* Tree showing all happy numbers up to 100, and 130

*Image: DessinHeureux\_02b.svg.* Sad numbers up to 100 showing the repeating cycle of 8 numbers

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), a **happy number** is a number which eventually reaches 1 when the number is replaced by the sum of the square of each digit. For instance, 13 is a happy number because $1^2+3^2=10$, and $1^2+0^2=1$. On the other hand, 4 is not a happy number because the sequence starting with $4^2=16$ and $1^2+6^2=37$ eventually reaches $2^2+0^2=4$, the number that started the sequence, and so the process continues in an infinite cycle without ever reaching 1.[^o2] A number which is not happy is called **sad** or **unhappy**.

:::

More generally, a $b$-**happy number** is a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) in a given [number base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_base) $b$ that eventually reaches 1 when iterated over the [perfect digital invariant function](wikipedia://en/61883319) for $p = 2$.[^o3]

The origin of happy numbers is not clear. Happy numbers were brought to the attention of Reginald Allenby (a British author and senior lecturer in [pure mathematics](wikipedia://en/457210) at [Leeds University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeds_University)) by his daughter, who had learned of them at school. However, they "may have originated in Russia."[^o4]

## Happy numbers and perfect digital invariants {#Happy_numbers_and_perfect_digital_invariants}

Formally, let $n$ be a natural number. Given the [perfect digital invariant function](wikipedia://en/61883319)

$$F_{p, b}(n) = \sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor \log_{b}{n} \rfloor} {\left(\frac{n \bmod{b^{i+1}} - n \bmod{b^{i}}}{b^{i}}\right)}^p.$$

for base $b > 1$, a number $n$ is $b$-happy if there exists a $j$ such that $F_{2, b}^j(n) = 1$, where $F_{2, b}^j$ represents the $j$-th [iteration](wikipedia://en/1448702) of $F_{2, b}$, and $b$-unhappy otherwise. If a number is a nontrivial perfect digital invariant of $F_{2, b}$, then it is $b$-unhappy.

For example, 19 is 10-happy, as

$$F_{2, 10}(19) = 1^2 + 9^2 = 82$$

$$F_{2, 10}^2(19) = F_{2, 10}(82) = 8^2 + 2^2 = 68$$

$$F_{2, 10}^3(19) = F_{2, 10}(68) = 6^2 + 8^2 = 100$$

$$F_{2, 10}^4(19) = F_{2, 10}(100) = 1^2 + 0^2 + 0^2 = 1$$

For example, 347 is 6-happy, as

$$F_{2, 6}(347) = F_{2, 6}(1335_6) = 1^2 + 3^2 + 3^2 + 5^2 = 44$$

$$F_{2, 6}^2(347) = F_{2, 6}(44) = F_{2, 6}(112_6) = 1^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 = 6$$

$$F_{2, 6}^3(347) = F_{2, 6}(6) = F_{2, 6}(10_6) = 1^2 + 0^2 = 1$$

There are infinitely many $b$-happy numbers, as 1 is a $b$-happy number, and for every $n$, $b^n$ ($10^n$ in base $b$) is $b$-happy, since its sum is 1. The *happiness* of a number is preserved by removing or inserting zeroes at will, since they do not contribute to the cross sum.

### Natural density of *b*-happy numbers {#Natural_density_of_b-happy_numbers}

By inspection of the first million or so 10-happy numbers, it appears that they have a [natural density](wikipedia://en/1819983) of around 0.15. The 10-happy numbers do not have an asymptotic density. The upper density of the happy numbers is greater than 0.18577, and the lower density is less than 0.1138.[^o7]

### Happy bases {#Happy_bases}

::: {#o9 .problem cue=unsolved}

Are [base 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_2) and [base 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_4) the only bases that are happy?

:::

A happy base is a number base $b$ where every number is $b$-happy. The only happy integer bases less than 5e8 are [base 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_2) and [base 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_4).

## Specific *b*-happy numbers {#Specific_b-happy_numbers}

### 4-happy numbers {#4-happy_numbers}

For $b = 4$, the only positive perfect digital invariant for $F_{2, b}$ is the trivial perfect digital invariant 1, and there are no other cycles. Because all numbers are [preperiodic points](wikipedia://en/4941621#Iterated_functions) for $F_{2, b}$, all numbers lead to 1 and are happy. As a result, [base 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_4) is a happy base.

### 6-happy numbers {#6-happy_numbers}

For $b = 6$, the only positive perfect digital invariant for $F_{2, b}$ is the trivial perfect digital invariant 1, and the only cycle is the eight-number cycle

5 → 41 → 25 → 45 → 105 → 42 → 32 → 21 → 5 → ...

and because all numbers are preperiodic points for $F_{2, b}$, all numbers either lead to 1 and are happy, or lead to the cycle and are unhappy. Because base 6 has no other perfect digital invariants except for 1, no positive integer other than 1 is the sum of the squares of its own digits.

## Happy primes {#Happy_primes}

A $b$-happy prime is a number that is both $b$-happy and [prime](wikipedia://en/23666). Unlike happy numbers, rearranging the digits of a $b$-happy prime will not necessarily create another happy prime. For instance, while 19 is a 10-happy prime, 91 = 13 × 7 is not prime (but is still 10-happy).

All prime numbers are 2-happy and 4-happy primes, as [base 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_2) and [base 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_4) are happy bases.

### 6-happy primes {#6-happy_primes}

In [base 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_6), the 6-happy primes below 1296 = 6<sup>4</sup> are

The [palindromic prime](wikipedia://en/424723) 10<sup>150006</sup> + 7426247e75000 + 1 is a 10-happy prime with 150,007 digits because the many 0s do not contribute to the sum of squared digits, and 1<sup>2</sup> + 7<sup>2</sup> + 4<sup>2</sup> + 2<sup>2</sup> + 6<sup>2</sup> + 2<sup>2</sup> + 4<sup>2</sup> + 7<sup>2</sup> + 1<sup>2</sup> = 176, which is a 10-happy number. Paul Jobling discovered the prime in 2005.[^o24]

, the largest known 10-happy prime is 2<sup>42643801</sup> − 1 (a [Mersenne prime](wikipedia://en/18908)). Its decimal expansion has 12,837,064 digits.[^o25]

## Programming example {#Programming_example}

The examples below implement the perfect digital invariant function for $p = 2$ and a default base $b = 10$ described in the definition of happy given at the top of this article, repeatedly; after each time, they check for both halt conditions: reaching 1, and [repeating a number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycle_detection).

A simple test in [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29) to check if a number is happy:

```python
def pdi_function(number, base: int = 10):
    """Perfect digital invariant function."""
    total = 0
    while number > 0:
        total += pow(number % base, 2)
        number = number // base
    return total

def is_happy(number: int) -> bool:
    """Determine if the specified number is a happy number."""
    seen_numbers = set()
    while number > 1 and number not in seen_numbers:
        seen_numbers.add(number)
        number = pdi_function(number)
    return number == 1
```
