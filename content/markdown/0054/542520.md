# Flat morphism

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Flat morphism"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in particular in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a **flat morphism** *f* from a [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) *X* to a scheme *Y* is a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) such that the induced map on every [stalk](wikipedia://en/15879621) is a flat map of rings, i.e.,

:::

$$f_P\colon \mathcal{O}_{Y, f(P)} \to \mathcal{O}_{X, P}$$

is a flat map for all *P* in *X*.[^o2] A map of rings $A\to B$ is called **flat** if it is a homomorphism that makes *B* a [flat](wikipedia://en/492616) *A*-module. A morphism of schemes is called **faithfully flat** if it is both surjective and flat.[^o3]

Two basic intuitions regarding flat morphisms are:

- flatness is a [generic property](wikipedia://en/12886758); and
- the failure of flatness occurs on the jumping set of the morphism.

The first of these comes from [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990): subject to some [finiteness conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finiteness_condition_on_a_morphism_of_schemes) on *f*, it can be shown that there is a non-empty open subscheme $Y'$ of *Y*, such that *f* restricted to $Y'$ is a flat morphism ([generic flatness](wikipedia://en/26374518)). Here 'restriction' is interpreted by means of the [fiber product of schemes](wikipedia://en/56444110), applied to *f* and the [inclusion map](wikipedia://en/492516) of $Y'$ into *Y*.

For the second, the idea is that morphisms in algebraic geometry can exhibit discontinuities of a kind that are detected by flatness. For instance, the operation of [blowing down](wikipedia://en/1817790) in the [birational geometry](wikipedia://en/382733) of an [algebraic surface](wikipedia://en/648311) can give a single [fiber](wikipedia://en/56444110) that is of dimension 1 when all the others have dimension 0. It turns out (retrospectively) that flatness in morphisms is directly related to controlling this sort of [semicontinuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semicontinuity), or one-sided jumping.

Flat morphisms are used to define (more than one version of) the [flat topos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_topos), and [flat cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_cohomology) of sheaves from it. This is a deep-lying theory, and has not been found easy to handle. The concept of [étale morphism](wikipedia://en/3022771) (and so [étale cohomology](wikipedia://en/396231)) depends on the flat morphism concept: an étale morphism being flat, of [finite type](wikipedia://en/70728366), and [unramified](wikipedia://en/58653736).

## Examples/non-examples {#Examples/non-examples}

Consider the affine scheme morphism

$$\operatorname{Spec}\left(\frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y,t]}{x^2 + y^2 - t}\right) \to \operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[t])$$

induced from the morphism of algebras

$$\begin{cases} \mathbb{C}[t] \to \frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y,t]}{x^2 + y^2 - t} \\ t \mapsto t. \\ \end{cases}$$

Since flatness of this morphism is equivalent to the vanishing of the [Tor group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tor_group)[^o5]

$$\operatorname{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{C} [t]}\left(\frac{\mathbb{C} [x,y,t]}{x^2 + y^2 -t}, \mathbb{C} \right),$$

we resolve the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826)

$$\begin{array}{lcccc} 0 & \to & \mathbb{C}[t] & \xrightarrow{\cdot t} & \mathbb{C} [t] & \to & 0 \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow & & \downarrow & & \downarrow\\ 0 & \to & 0 & \to & \mathbb{C} & \to & 0 \\ \end{array}$$

and tensor by the module representing our scheme giving the sequence of $\mathbb{C} [t]$-modules

$$0 \to \frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y,t]}{x^2 + y^2 - t} \xrightarrow{\cdot t} \frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y,t]}{x^2 + y^2 - t} \to 0.$$

Because $t$ is not a [zero divisor](wikipedia://en/51441) we have a trivial kernel; hence the homology group vanishes.

### Miracle flatness {#Miracle_flatness}

Other examples of flat morphisms can be found using "miracle flatness"[^o7] which states that if a morphism $f\colon X \to Y$ between a [Cohen–Macaulay scheme](wikipedia://en/497407#Cohen–Macaulay_schemes) to a [regular scheme](wikipedia://en/43016199) has [equidimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equidimensional_scheme) fibers, then it is flat. Easy examples of this are [elliptic fibrations](wikipedia://en/3073462), [smooth morphisms](wikipedia://en/7837041), and morphisms to [stratified varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologically_stratified_space) which satisfy miracle flatness on each of the strata.

### Hilbert schemes {#Hilbert_schemes}

The universal examples of flat morphisms of schemes are given by [Hilbert schemes](wikipedia://en/6612581). This is because Hilbert schemes parameterize universal classes of flat morphisms, and every flat morphism is the pullback from some Hilbert scheme. I.e., if $f\colon X\to S$ is flat, there exists a [commutative diagram](wikipedia://en/138484)

$$\begin{matrix} X & \to & \operatorname{Hilb}_{S} \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ S & \to & S \end{matrix}$$

for the Hilbert scheme of all flat morphisms to $S$. Since $f$ is flat, the fibers $f_s\colon X_s \to s$ all have the same Hilbert polynomial $\Phi$, hence we could have similarly written $\text{Hilb}_S^\Phi$ for the Hilbert scheme above.

::: {#o10 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=heading name="Non-examples"}

### Non-examples {#Non-examples}

#### Blowup {#Blowup}

One class of non-examples are given by [blowup](wikipedia://en/1817790) maps

$$\operatorname{Bl}_I X \to X.$$

One easy example is the blowup of a point in $\mathbb{C}[x,y]$. If we take the origin, this is given by the morphism

$\mathbb{C}[x,y] \to \frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y,s,t]}{xt - ys}$ sending $x \mapsto x, y \mapsto y ,$

where the fiber over a point $(a,b) \neq (0,0)$ is a copy of $\mathbb{C}$, i.e.,

$$\frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y,s,t]}{xt - ys}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}[x,y]} \frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y]}{(x-a,y-b)} \cong \mathbb{C} ,$$

which follows from

$$M\otimes_R \frac{R}{I} \cong \frac{M}{IM} .$$

But for $a=b=0$, we get the isomorphism

$$\frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y,s,t]}{xt - ys}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}[x,y]} \frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y]}{(x,y)} \cong \mathbb{C}[s,t].$$

The reason this fails to be flat is because of the Miracle flatness lemma, which can be checked locally.

#### Infinite resolution {#Infinite_resolution}

A simple non-example of a flat morphism is $k[\varepsilon] = k[x]/(x^2) \to k.$ This is because

$$k \otimes^\mathbf{L}_{k[\varepsilon]} k$$

is an infinite complex, which we can find by taking a flat resolution of $k$,

$$\cdots ~\xrightarrow{\overset{} \cdot \varepsilon}~ k[\varepsilon] ~\xrightarrow{\cdot \varepsilon}~ k[\varepsilon] \xrightarrow{\cdot \varepsilon} k[\varepsilon] \to k$$

and tensor the resolution with $k$, we find that

$$k\otimes^\mathbf{L}_{k[\varepsilon]} k \simeq \bigoplus_{i=0}^\infty k[+i]$$

showing that the morphism cannot be flat. Another non-example of a flat morphism is a [blowup](wikipedia://en/1817790) since a flat morphism necessarily has equi-dimensional fibers.

:::

## Properties of flat morphisms {#Properties_of_flat_morphisms}

Let $f\colon X \to Y$ be a morphism of schemes. For a morphism $g\colon Y' \to Y$, let $X' = X\times_{Y} Y'$ and $f' = (f, 1_{Y'}) \colon X' \to Y'.$ The morphism *f* is flat [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) for every *g*, the [pullback](wikipedia://en/7086661) $f'^*$ is an [exact functor](wikipedia://en/453945) from the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of [quasi-coherent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-coherent_sheaf) $\mathcal{O}_{Y'}$-modules to the category of quasi-coherent $\mathcal{O}_{X'}$-modules.[^o14]

Assume $f\colon X \to Y$ and $g\colon Y \to Z$ are morphisms of schemes and *f* is flat at *x* in *X*. Then *g* is flat at $f(x)$ if and only if *gf* is flat at *x*.[^o15] In particular, if *f* is faithfully flat, then *g* is flat or faithfully flat if and only if *gf* is flat or faithfully flat, respectively.[^o16]

### Fundamental properties {#Fundamental_properties}

- The composite of two flat morphisms is flat.[^o18]
- The fiber product of two flat or faithfully flat morphisms is a flat or faithfully flat morphism, respectively.[^o19]
- Flatness and faithful flatness is preserved by base change: If *f* is flat or faithfully flat and $g\colon Y' \to Y$, then the fiber product $f\times g\colon X\times_Y Y' \to Y'$ is flat or faithfully flat, respectively.[^o20]
- The set of points where a morphism (locally of finite presentation) is flat is open.[^o21]
- If *f* is faithfully flat and of finite presentation, and if *gf* is finite type or finite presentation, then *g* is of finite type or finite presentation, respectively.[^o22]

Suppose $f\colon X \to Y$ is a flat morphism of schemes.

- If *F* is a quasi-coherent sheaf of finite presentation on *Y* (in particular, if *F* is coherent), and if *J* is the annihilator of *F* on *Y*, then $f^*J \to \mathcal{O}_X$, the pullback of the inclusion map, is an injection, and the image of $f^*J$ in $\mathcal{O}_X$ is the annihilator of $f^*F$ on *X*.[^o23]
- If *f* is faithfully flat and if *G* is a quasi-coherent $\mathcal{O}_Y$-module, then the pullback map on global sections $\Gamma(Y, G) \to \Gamma(X, f^*G)$ is injective.[^o24]

Suppose $h\colon S' \to S$ is flat. Let *X* and *Y* be *S*-schemes, and let $X'$ and $Y'$ be their base change by *h*.

- If $f\colon X \to Y$ is [quasi-compact](wikipedia://en/12410777) and dominant, then its base change $f'\colon X' \to Y'$ is quasi-compact and dominant.[^o25]
- If *h* is faithfully flat, then the pullback map $\operatorname{Hom}_S(X,Y) \to \operatorname{Hom}_{S'}(X',Y')$ is injective.[^o26]
- Assume $f\colon X \to Y$ is quasi-compact and [quasi-separated](wikipedia://en/28281191). Let *Z* be the closed image of *X*, and let $j\colon Z \to Y$ be the canonical injection. Then the closed subscheme determined by the base change $j'\colon Z' \to Y'$ is the closed image of $X'$.[^o27]

### Topological properties {#Topological_properties}

If $f\colon X \to Y$ is flat, then it possesses all of the following properties:

- For every point *x* of *X* and every generization *y* of $y = f(x)$, there is a generization *x*′ of *x* such that $y' = f(x)$.[^o29]
- For every point *x* of *X*, $f(\operatorname{Spec} \mathcal{O}_{X,x}) = \operatorname{Spec} \mathcal{O}_{Y,f(x)}$.[^o30]
- For every irreducible closed subset *Y* of *Y*, every [irreducible component](wikipedia://en/2652476) of *f*${}^{-1}$(*Y*) dominates *Y*.[^o31]
- If *Z* and *Z* are two irreducible closed subsets of *Y* with *Z* contained in *Z*, then for every irreducible component *T* of *f*${}^{-1}$(*Z*), there is an irreducible component *T* of *f*${}^{-1}$(*Z*) containing *T*.[^o32]
- For every irreducible component *T* of *X*, the closure of *f*() is an irreducible component of *Y*.[^o33]
- If *Y* is irreducible with [generic point](wikipedia://en/3043978) *y*, and if *f*${}^{-1}$(*y*) is irreducible, then *X* is irreducible.[^o34]
- If *f* is also closed, the image of every connected component of *X* is a connected component of *Y*.[^o35]
- For every pro-constructible subset *Z* of *Y*, $f^{-1}(\bar Z) = \overline{f^{-1}(Z)}$.[^o36]

If *f* is flat and locally of finite presentation, then *f* is universally open.[^o37] However, if *f* is faithfully flat and quasi-compact, it is not in general true that *f* is open, even if *X* and *Y* are [noetherian](wikipedia://en/2783891).[^o38] Furthermore, no converse to this statement holds: If *f* is the canonical map from the reduced scheme *X*<sub>red</sub> to *X*, then *f* is a universal [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660), but for *X* non-reduced and noetherian, *f* is never flat.[^o39]

If $f\colon X \to Y$ is faithfully flat and quasicompact, then:

- The topology on *Y* is the [quotient topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_topology) relative to *f*.[^o40]
- If *Z* is a subset of *Y*, then *Z* is a locally closed pro-constructible subset of *Y* if and only if *f*${}^{-1}$() is a locally closed pro-constructible subset of *X*.[^o41]

If *f* is flat and locally of finite presentation, then for each of the following properties **P**, the set of points where *f* has **P** is open:[^o42]

- Serre's condition S<sub>*k*</sub> (for any fixed *k*).
- [Geometrically](wikipedia://en/62303294) regular.
- Geometrically normal.

If in addition *f* is proper, then the same is true for each of the following properties:[^o43]

- Geometrically reduced.
- Geometrically reduced and having *k* geometric connected components (for any fixed *k*).
- Geometrically integral.

### Flatness and dimension {#Flatness_and_dimension}

Assume $X$ and $Y$ are locally noetherian, and let $f\colon X \to Y$.

- Let $x$ be a point of $X$ and $y=f(x)$. If $f$ is flat, then $\dim_{x}X=\dim_{y}Y+\dim_{x}f^{-1}(y)$.[^o45] Conversely, if this equality holds for all *x*, *X* is [Cohen–Macaulay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohen–Macaulay_scheme), and *Y* is [regular](wikipedia://en/43016199), and furthermore *f* maps closed points to closed points, then *f* is flat.[^o46]
- If *f* is faithfully flat, then for each closed subset *Z* of *Y*, $\operatorname{codim}_{Y}(Z) = \operatorname{codim}_{X}(f^{-1}(Z))$.[^o47]
- Suppose *f* is flat and *F* is a quasi-coherent module over *Y*. If *F* has [projective dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_dimension) at most *n*, then $f^*F$ has projective dimension at most *n*.[^o48]

### Descent properties {#Descent_properties}

- Assume *f* is flat at *x* in *X*. If *X* is reduced or normal at *x*, then *Y* is reduced or normal, respectively, at *f*(*x*).[^o50] Conversely, if *f* is also of finite presentation and *f*${}^{-1}$(*y*) is reduced or normal, respectively, at *x*, then *X* is reduced or normal, respectively, at *x*.[^o51]
- In particular, if *f* is faithfully flat, then *X* reduced or normal implies that *Y* is reduced or normal, respectively. If *f* is faithfully flat and of finite presentation, then all the fibers of *f* reduced or normal implies that *X* is reduced or normal, respectively.
- If *f* is flat at *x* in *X*, and if *X* is integral or integrally closed at *x*, then *Y* is integral or integrally closed, respectively, at *f*(*x*).[^o52]
- If *f* is faithfully flat, *X* is locally integral, and the [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) of *Y* is locally noetherian, then *Y* is locally integral.[^o53]
- If *f* is faithfully flat and quasi-compact, and if *X* is locally noetherian, then *Y* is also locally noetherian.[^o54]
- Assume *f* is flat and *X* and *Y* are locally noetherian. If *X* is regular at *x*, then *Y* is regular at *f*(*x*). Conversely, if *Y* is regular at *f*(*x*) and *f*${}^{-1}$(*f*(*x*)) is regular at *x*, then *X* is regular at *x*.[^o55]
- Assume *f* is flat and *X* and *Y* are locally noetherian. If *X* is normal at *x*, then *Y* is normal at *f*(*x*). Conversely, if *Y* is normal at *f*(*x*) and *f*${}^{-1}$(*f*(*x*)) is normal at *x*, then *X* is normal at *x*.[^o56]

Let $g : Y \to Y$ be faithfully flat. Let *F* be a quasi-coherent sheaf on *Y*, and let *F* be the pullback of *F* to *Y*. Then *F* is flat over *Y* if and only if *F* is flat over *Y*.[^o57]

Assume *f* is faithfully flat and quasi-compact. Let *G* be a quasi-coherent sheaf on *Y*, and let *F* denote its pullback to *X*. Then *F* is finite type, finite presentation, or locally free of rank *n* if and only if *G* has the corresponding property.[^o58]

Suppose $f : X \to Y$ is an *S*-morphism of *S*-schemes. Let $g : S \to S$ be faithfully flat and quasi-compact, and let *X*, *Y*, and *f* denote the base changes by *g*. Then for each of the following properties **P**, if *f* has **P**, then *f* has **P**.[^o59]

- Open.
- Closed.
- Quasi-compact and a homeomorphism onto its image.
- A homeomorphism.

Additionally, for each of the following properties **P**, *f* has **P** if and only if *f* has **P**.[^o60]

- Universally open.
- Universally closed.
- A universal homeomorphism.
- Quasi-compact.
- Quasi-compact and dominant.
- Quasi-compact and universally bicontinuous.
- Separated.
- Quasi-separated.
- Locally of finite type.
- Locally of finite presentation.
- Finite type.
- Finite presentation.
- Proper.
- An isomorphism.
- A [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538).
- An open immersion.
- A quasi-compact immersion.
- A [closed immersion](wikipedia://en/1623986).
- Affine.
- Quasi-affine.
- [Finite](wikipedia://en/2139357).
- [Quasi-finite](wikipedia://en/2143056).
- Integral.

It is possible for *f* to be a local isomorphism without *f* being even a local immersion.[^o61]

If *f* is quasi-compact and *L* is an [invertible sheaf](wikipedia://en/581859) on *X*, then *L* is *f*-ample or *f*-very ample if and only if its pullback *L* is *f*-ample or *f*-very ample, respectively.[^o62] However, it is not true that *f* is projective if and only if *f* is projective. It is not even true that if *f* is [proper](wikipedia://en/603782) and *f* is projective, then *f* is quasi-projective, because it is possible to have an *f*-ample sheaf on *X* which does not descend to *X*.[^o63]
