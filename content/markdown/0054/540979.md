# Magnetic moment

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Magnetic moment"}

In [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism), the **magnetic moment** or **magnetic dipole moment** is a vector quantity which characterizes the strength and orientation of a [magnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnet) or other object or system that exerts a [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563). The magnetic dipole moment of an object determines the magnitude of [torque](wikipedia://en/30400) the object experiences in a given magnetic field. When the same magnetic field is applied, objects with larger magnetic moments experience larger torques. The strength (and direction) of this torque depends not only on the magnitude of the magnetic moment but also on its orientation relative to the direction of the magnetic field. Its direction points from the south pole to the north pole of the magnet (i.e., inside the magnet).

:::

The magnetic moment also expresses the magnetic force effect of a magnet. The magnetic field of a magnetic dipole is proportional to its magnetic dipole moment. The dipole component of an object's magnetic field is symmetric about the direction of its magnetic dipole moment, and decreases as the inverse cube of the distance from the object.

Example magnetic moments for subatomic particles include [electron magnetic moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_magnetic_moment), [nuclear magnetic moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_moment), and [nucleon magnetic moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleon_magnetic_moment).

## Definition, units, and measurement {#Definition,_units,_and_measurement}

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

The magnetic moment can be defined as a vector relating the aligning [torque](wikipedia://en/30400) on the object from an externally applied [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563) to the field vector itself. The relationship is given by:[^o6]

$$\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{m} \times\mathbf{B}$$

where $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ is the torque acting on the dipole, $\mathbf{B}$ is the external magnetic field, and $\mathbf{m}$ is the magnetic moment.

This definition is based on how one could, in principle, measure the magnetic moment of an unknown sample. For a current loop, this definition leads to the magnitude of the magnetic dipole moment equaling the product of the current times the area of the loop. Further, this definition allows the calculation of the expected magnetic moment for any known macroscopic current distribution.

An alternative definition is useful for [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952) calculations of the magnetic moment. In this definition, the magnetic dipole moment of a system is the negative gradient of its intrinsic energy, $U_{\operatorname{int}}$, with respect to external magnetic field:

$$\mathbf{m} = -\hat\mathbf x \frac{\partial U_\text{int}}{\partial B_x} - \hat\mathbf y \frac{\partial U_\text{int}}{\partial B_y} - \hat\mathbf z \frac{\partial U_\text{int}}{\partial B_z}.$$

Generically, the intrinsic energy includes the self-field energy of the system plus the energy of the internal workings of the system. For example, for a hydrogen atom in a 2p state in an external field, the self-field energy is negligible, so the internal energy is essentially the eigenenergy of the 2p state, which includes Coulomb potential energy and the kinetic energy of the electron. The interaction-field energy between the internal dipoles and external fields is not part of this internal energy.[^o7]

:::

### Units {#Units}

The unit for magnetic moment in [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) (SI) [base units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_base_unit) is A⋅m<sup>2</sup>, where A is [ampere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampere) (SI base unit of current) and m is [meter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meter) (SI base unit of distance). This unit has equivalents in other SI derived units including:[^o10][^o11]

$$\mathrm{A{\cdot}m^2} = \mathrm{N{\cdot}m/T} = \mathrm{J/T},$$

where N is [newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton_%28unit%29) (SI unit of force), T is [tesla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla_%28unit%29) (SI unit of magnetic flux density), and J is [joule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule) (SI unit of [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy)).p. 20–21

In the [CGS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centimetre–gram–second_system_of_units) system, there are several different sets of electromagnetism units, of which the main ones are [ESU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centimetre–gram–second_system_of_units#Electrostatic_units_%28ESU%29), [Gaussian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_units), and [EMU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centimetre–gram–second_system_of_units#Electromagnetic_units_%28EMU%29). Among these, there are alternative (non-equivalent) units of magnetic dipole moment:

$$\mathrm{1~statA {\cdot} {cm}^2} ~\overset{\frown}{=}~ 3.33564095 \times 10^{-14} \mathrm{A {\cdot} m^2} ~~ \text{ (ESU)}$$

$$\mathrm{1~erg/G} ~\overset{\frown}{=}~ 10^{-3} \mathrm{A{\cdot}m^2} ~~ \text{ (Gaussian and EMU),}$$

where statA is [statampere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statampere), cm is [centimeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centimeter), erg is [erg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erg_%28unit%29), and G is [gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss_%28unit%29). The ratio of these two non-equivalent CGS units (EMU/ESU) is equal to the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) in free space, expressed in [cm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centimetre)⋅[s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second)<sup>−1</sup>.

All formulas in this article are correct in [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI) units; they may need to be changed for use in other unit systems. For example, in SI units, a loop of current with current $I$ and area $A$ has magnetic moment $IA$ (see below), but in [Gaussian units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_units) the magnetic moment is $IA/c$.

Other units for measuring the magnetic dipole moment include the [Bohr magneton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr_magneton) and the [nuclear magneton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magneton).

:::

### Measurement {#Measurement}

The magnetic moments of objects are typically measured with devices called [magnetometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetometer), though not all magnetometers measure magnetic moment: Some are configured to measure [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563) instead. If the magnetic field surrounding an object is known well enough, though, then the magnetic moment can be calculated from that magnetic field.

## Relation to magnetization {#Relation_to_magnetization}

The magnetic moment is a quantity that describes the magnetic strength of an entire object. Sometimes, though, it is useful or necessary to know how much of the net magnetic moment of the object is produced by a particular portion of that magnet. Therefore, it is useful to define the magnetization $\mathbf{M}$ as:

$$\mathbf{M} = \frac{\mathbf{m}_{\Delta V}}{V_{\Delta V}},$$

where $\mathbf{m}_{\Delta V}$ and $V_{\Delta V}$ are the magnetic dipole moment and volume of a sufficiently small portion of the magnet $\Delta V$. This equation is often represented using derivative notation such that

$$\mathbf M = \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{m}}{\mathrm{d}V},$$

where $d\mathbf{m}$ is the elementary magnetic moment and $dV$ is the [volume element](wikipedia://en/1920694). The net magnetic moment of the magnet $\mathbf{m}$ therefore is

$$\mathbf m = \iiint \mathbf M\,\mathrm{d}V,$$

where the triple integral denotes integration over the volume of the [magnet](wikipedia://en/36563). For uniform magnetization (where both the magnitude and the direction of $\mathbf{M}$ is the same for the entire magnet (such as a straight bar magnet) the last equation simplifies to:

$$\mathbf{m} = \mathbf{M} V,$$

where $V$ is the volume of the bar magnet.

The magnetization is often not listed as a material parameter for commercially available [ferromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetic) materials, though. Instead the parameter that is listed is [residual flux density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residual_flux_density) (or remanence), denoted $\mathbf{B}_{r}$. The formula needed in this case to calculate $\mathbf{m}$ in (units of A⋅m<sup>2</sup>) is:

$$\mathbf{m} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\mathbf{B}_\text{r} V,$$

where:

- $\mathbf{B}_{r}$ is the residual flux density, expressed in [teslas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla_%28unit%29).
- $V$ is the volume of the magnet (in m<sup>3</sup>).
- $\mu_{0}$ is the permeability of vacuum (4 × 10<sup>\-7</sup> H/m).[^o15]

## Effects of an external magnetic field {#Effects_of_an_external_magnetic_field}

### Torque on a moment {#Torque_on_a_moment}

The torque $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ on an object having a magnetic dipole moment $\mathbf{m}$ in a uniform magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ is:

$$\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{m} \times\mathbf{B}.$$

This is valid for the moment due to any localized current distribution provided that the magnetic field is uniform. For non-uniform B the equation is also valid for the torque about the center of the magnetic dipole provided that the magnetic dipole is small enough.[^o19]p. 257

An electron, nucleus, or atom placed in a uniform magnetic field will precess with a frequency known as the [Larmor frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larmor_frequency). See [Resonance](wikipedia://en/41660).

### Force on a moment {#Force_on_a_moment}

A magnetic moment in an externally produced magnetic field has a potential energy $U$:

$$U = -\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{B}$$

In a case when the external magnetic field is non-uniform, there will be a force, proportional to the magnetic field [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461), acting on the magnetic moment itself. There are two expressions for the force acting on a magnetic dipole, depending on whether the [model used for the dipole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnet#Modelling_magnets) is a current loop or two monopoles (analogous to the electric dipole).[^o21] The force obtained in the case of a current loop model is

$$\mathbf{F}_\text{loop}=\nabla \left(\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{B}\right) .$$

Assuming existence of magnetic monopole, the force is modified as follows:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{F}_\text{loop} &= \left(\mathbf{m}\times\nabla\right) \times \mathbf{B} \\[1ex] &= \nabla \left(\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{B}\right) - \left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}\right) \mathbf{m} \end{aligned}$$

In the case of a pair of monopoles being used (i.e. electric dipole model), the force is

$$\mathbf{F}_\text{dipole} = \left(\mathbf{m} \cdot \nabla \right) \mathbf{B}.$$

And one can be put in terms of the other via the relation

$$\mathbf{F}_\text{loop}=\mathbf{F}_\text{dipole} + \mathbf{m}\times \left(\nabla \times \mathbf{B} \right) - \left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}\right) \mathbf{m}.$$

In all these expressions $\mathbf{m}$ is the dipole and $\mathbf{B}$ is the magnetic field at its position. If there are no currents or time-varying electrical fields or magnetic charge, $\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = 0$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ and the two expressions agree.

### Relation to free energy {#Relation_to_free_energy}

One can relate the magnetic moment of a system to the free energy of that system.[^o23] In a uniform magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$, the [free energy](wikipedia://en/255447#Relating_free_energy_to_other_variables) $F$ can be related to the magnetic moment $\mathbf{M}$ of the system as

$$\mathrm dF = -S\,\mathrm dT - \mathbf{M}\,\cdot \mathrm d\mathbf{B}$$

where $S$ is the [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) of the system and $T$ is the temperature. Therefore, the magnetic moment can also be defined in terms of the free energy of a system as

$$m = \left. - \frac{\partial F}{\partial B} \right|_{T}.$$

### Magnetism {#Magnetism}

In addition, an applied magnetic field can change the magnetic moment of the object itself; for example by magnetizing it. This phenomenon is known as [magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism). An applied magnetic field can flip the magnetic dipoles that make up the material causing both [paramagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramagnetism) and [ferromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism). Additionally, the magnetic field can affect the currents that create the magnetic fields (such as the atomic orbits) which causes [diamagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamagnetism).

:::

## Effects on environment {#Effects_on_environment}

### Magnetic field of a magnetic moment {#Magnetic_field_of_a_magnetic_moment}

*Image: Magnetic dipole moment.jpg.* Magnetic field lines around a "magnetostatic dipole". The magnetic dipole itself is located in the center of the figure, seen from the side, and pointing upward.

Any system possessing a net magnetic dipole moment $\mathbf{m}$ will produce a [dipolar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipole) magnetic field (described below) in the space surrounding the system. While the net magnetic field produced by the system can also have higher-order [multipole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multipole) components, those will drop off with distance more rapidly, so that only the dipole component will dominate the magnetic field of the system at distances far away from it.

The magnetic field of a magnetic dipole depends on the strength and direction of a magnet's magnetic moment $\mathbf{m}$ but drops off as the cube of the distance such that:

$$\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi} \left(\frac{3\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{r})}{|\mathbf r|^5} - \frac{{\mathbf{m}}}{|\mathbf r|^3}\right),$$

where $\mathbf{H}$ is the [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563) produced by the magnet and $\mathbf{r}$ is a vector from the center of the magnetic dipole to the location where the magnetic field is measured. The inverse cube nature of this equation is more readily seen by expressing the location vector $\mathbf{r}$ as the product of its magnitude times the unit vector in its direction ($\mathbf{r} = |\mathbf{r}| \mathbf\hat{r}$) so that:

$$\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{r}) =\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{3\mathbf\hat{r}(\mathbf\hat{r}\cdot \mathbf{m})-\mathbf{m}}{|\mathbf{r}|^3}.$$

The equivalent equations for the magnetic $\mathbf{B}$-field are the same except for a multiplicative factor of *μ*<sub>0</sub> = 4 × 10<sup>\-7</sup> [H](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_%28unit%29)/[m](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre), where *μ*<sub>0</sub> is known as the [vacuum permeability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_permeability). For example:

$$\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}) =\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{3\mathbf\hat{r}(\mathbf\hat{r}\cdot \mathbf{m})-\mathbf{m}}{|\mathbf{r}|^3}.$$

### Forces between two magnetic dipoles {#Forces_between_two_magnetic_dipoles}

As discussed earlier, the force exerted by a dipole loop with moment $\mathbf{m}_{1}$ on another with moment $\mathbf{m}_{2}$ is

$$\mathbf F = \nabla \left(\mathbf m_2 \cdot \mathbf B_1\right),$$

where $\mathbf{B}_{1}$ is the magnetic field due to moment $\mathbf{m}_{1}$. The result of calculating the gradient is[^o28][^o29]

$$\mathbf F(\mathbf r,\mathbf m_1,\mathbf m_2) = \frac{3\mu_0}{4\pi|\mathbf r|^4} \left[\mathbf m_2 (\mathbf m_1\cdot\hat\mathbf r) + \mathbf m_1(\mathbf m_2\cdot\hat\mathbf r) + \hat\mathbf r(\mathbf m_1\cdot\mathbf m_2) - 5\hat\mathbf r(\mathbf m_1\cdot\hat\mathbf r)(\mathbf m_2\cdot\hat\mathbf r)\right],$$

where $\mathbf{r\text{̂}}$ is the unit vector pointing from magnet 1 to magnet 2 and $r$ is the distance. An equivalent expression is[^o29]

$$\mathbf F = \frac{3\mu_0}{4\pi|\mathbf r|^4} \left[(\hat\mathbf r\times\mathbf m_1)\times\mathbf m _2 + (\hat\mathbf r\times\mathbf m_2)\times\mathbf m_1 - 2 \hat\mathbf r(\mathbf m_1 \cdot \mathbf m_2 ) + 5\hat\mathbf r(\hat\mathbf r\times\mathbf m_1)\cdot(\hat\mathbf r\times\mathbf m_2)\right].$$

The force acting on $\mathbf{m}_{1}$ is in the opposite direction.

### Torque of one magnetic dipole on another {#Torque_of_one_magnetic_dipole_on_another}

The torque of magnet 1 on magnet 2 is

$$\boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf m_2 \times \mathbf B_1.$$

## Theory underlying magnetic dipoles {#Theory_underlying_magnetic_dipoles}

The magnetic field of any magnet can be modeled by a series of terms for which each term is more complicated (having finer angular detail) than the one before it. The first three terms of that series are called the [monopole](wikipedia://en/1425534) (represented by an isolated magnetic north or south pole) the [dipole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_dipole) (represented by two equal and opposite magnetic poles), and the [quadrupole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrupole#Magnetic_quadrupole) (represented by four poles that together form two equal and opposite dipoles). The magnitude of the magnetic field for each term decreases progressively faster with distance than the previous term, so that at large enough distances the first non-zero term will dominate.

For many magnets the first non-zero term is the magnetic dipole moment. (To date, no isolated [magnetic monopoles](wikipedia://en/175470) have been experimentally detected.) A magnetic dipole is the limit of either a current loop or a pair of poles as the dimensions of the source are reduced to zero while keeping the moment constant. As long as these limits only apply to fields far from the sources, they are equivalent. However, the two models give different predictions for the internal field (see below).

### Magnetic potentials {#Magnetic_potentials}

Traditionally, the equations for the magnetic dipole moment (and higher order terms) are derived from theoretical quantities called [magnetic potentials](wikipedia://en/1191067)[^o33] which are simpler to deal with mathematically than the magnetic fields.

In the magnetic pole model, the relevant magnetic field is the [demagnetizing field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demagnetizing_field) $\mathbf{H}$. Since the demagnetizing portion of $\mathbf{H}$ does not include, by definition, the part of $\mathbf{H}$ due to free currents, there exists a [magnetic scalar potential](wikipedia://en/2395496) such that

$${\mathbf{H}}({\mathbf{r}}) = -\nabla\psi.$$

In the amperian loop model, the relevant magnetic field is the magnetic induction $\mathbf{B}$. Since magnetic monopoles do not exist, there exists a [magnetic vector potential](wikipedia://en/1191067) such that

$$\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}) = \nabla \times \mathbf{A}.$$

Both of these potentials can be calculated for any arbitrary current distribution (for the amperian loop model) or magnetic charge distribution (for the magnetic charge model) provided that these are limited to a small enough region to give:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{A} \left(\mathbf{r}, t\right) &= \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \int \frac{\mathbf{j}\left(\mathbf{r}'\right)}{\left|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'\right|}\, \mathrm{d}V', \\[1ex] \psi \left(\mathbf{r}, t\right) &= \frac{1}{4\pi} \int \frac{\rho \left(\mathbf{r}'\right)}{\left|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'\right|}\, \mathrm{d}V', \end{aligned}$$

where $\mathbf{j}$ is the [current density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_density) in the amperian loop model, $\rho$ is the magnetic pole strength density in analogy to the electric [charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_density) that leads to the electric potential, and the integrals are the volume (triple) integrals over the coordinates that make up $\mathbf{r}'$. The denominators of these equation can be expanded using the [multipole expansion](wikipedia://en/1425534) to give a series of terms that have larger of power of distances in the denominator. The first nonzero term, therefore, will dominate for large distances. The first non-zero term for the vector potential is:

$$\mathbf{A}(\mathbf r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{\mathbf m\times\mathbf r}{|\mathbf r|^3},$$

where $\mathbf{m}$ is:

$$\mathbf{m} = \frac{1}{2} \iiint_V \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{j} \, \mathrm dV,$$

where $\times$ is the [vector cross product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_cross_product), $\mathbf{r}$ is the position vector, and $\mathbf{j}$ is the [electric current density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current_density) and the integral is a volume integral.

In the magnetic pole perspective, the first non-zero term of the [scalar potential](wikipedia://en/2395496) is

$$\psi(\mathbf r) = \frac{\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf r}{4\pi|\mathbf r|^3}.$$

Here $\mathbf{m}$ may be represented in terms of the magnetic pole strength density but is more usefully expressed in terms of the [magnetization](wikipedia://en/2404348) field as:

$$\mathbf m = \iiint \mathbf M\,\mathrm{d}V.$$

The same symbol $\mathbf{m}$ is used for both equations since they produce equivalent results outside of the magnet.

### External magnetic field produced by a magnetic dipole moment {#External_magnetic_field_produced_by_a_magnetic_dipole_moment}

The [magnetic flux density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_flux_density) for a magnetic dipole in the amperian loop model, therefore, is

$$\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}) = \nabla\times{\mathbf{A}} = \frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\left(\frac{3\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{r})}{|\mathbf r|^5}-\frac{{\mathbf{m}}}{|\mathbf r|^3}\right).$$

Further, the [magnetic field strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field_strength) $\mathbf{H}$ is

$${\mathbf{H}}({\mathbf{r}}) = -\nabla\psi = \frac{1}{4\pi}\left(\frac{3\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{r})}{|\mathbf r|^5} - \frac{{\mathbf{m}}}{|\mathbf r|^3}\right).$$

### Internal magnetic field of a dipole {#Internal_magnetic_field_of_a_dipole}

*Image: VFPt dipole magnetic3.svg.* The magnetic field of a current loop

The two models for a dipole (magnetic poles or current loop) give the same predictions for the magnetic field far from the source. However, inside the source region, they give different predictions. The magnetic field between poles (see the figure for Magnetic pole model) is in the opposite direction to the magnetic moment (which points from the negative charge to the positive charge), while inside a current loop it is in the same direction (see the figure to the right). The limits of these fields must also be different as the sources shrink to zero size. This distinction only matters if the dipole limit is used to calculate fields inside a magnetic material.[^o36]

If a magnetic dipole is formed by taking a "north pole" and a "south pole", bringing them closer and closer together but keeping the product of magnetic pole charge and distance constant, the limiting field is[^o36]

$$\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\ 1\ }{ 4\pi } \frac{\ 3\left(\mathbf\hat{r}\cdot \mathbf{m}\right)\ \mathbf\hat{r} - \mathbf{m}\ }{~ \left|\mathbf{r}\right|^3 } - \frac{\ 1\ }{ 3 } \mathbf{m}\ \delta(\mathbf{r}) ~.$$

The fields $\mathbf{H}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are related by $\ \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \left( \mathbf{H} + \mathbf{M} \right)\ ,$ where $\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}) = \mathbf{m} \delta(\mathbf{r})$ is the [magnetization](wikipedia://en/2404348).

If a magnetic dipole is formed by making a current loop smaller and smaller, but keeping the product of current and area constant, the limiting field is

$$\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}) = \mu_0\left[\ \frac{ 1 }{ 4\pi } \frac{\ 3\left(\mathbf\hat{r} \cdot \mathbf{m}\right) \mathbf\hat{r} - \mathbf{m}\ }{~ \left|\mathbf{r}\right|^3 } + \frac{\ 2\ }{ 3 } \mathbf{m}\ \delta(\mathbf{r})\ \right] ~.$$

Unlike the expressions in the previous section, this limit is correct for the internal field of the dipole.[^o36][^o33]p. 184

## Relation to angular momentum {#Relation_to_angular_momentum}

The magnetic moment has a close connection with [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839) called the *gyromagnetic effect*. This effect is expressed on a [macroscopic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroscopic_scale) in the [Einstein–de Haas effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein–de_Haas_effect), or "rotation by magnetization", and its inverse, the [Barnett effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnett_effect), or "magnetization by rotation".[^o6] Further, a [torque](wikipedia://en/30400) applied to a relatively isolated magnetic dipole such as an [atomic nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus) can cause it to [precess](wikipedia://en/24714) (rotate about the axis of the applied field). This phenomenon is used in [nuclear magnetic resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_resonance).

Viewing a magnetic dipole as current loop brings out the close connection between magnetic moment and angular momentum. Since the particles creating the current (by rotating around the loop) have charge and mass, both the magnetic moment and the angular momentum increase with the rate of rotation. The ratio of the two is called the [gyromagnetic ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyromagnetic_ratio) or $\gamma$ so that:[^o38][^o39]

$$\mathbf{m} = \gamma\,\mathbf{L},$$

where $\mathbf{L}$ is the angular momentum of the particle or particles that are creating the magnetic moment.

In the amperian loop model, which applies for macroscopic currents, the gyromagnetic ratio is one half of the [charge-to-mass ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge-to-mass_ratio). This can be shown as follows. The angular momentum of a moving charged particle is defined as:

$$\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p} = \mu \, \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{v},$$

where $\mu$ is the mass of the particle and $\boldsymbol{v}$ is the particle's [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903). The angular momentum of the very large number of charged particles that make up a current therefore is:

$$\mathbf{L} = \iiint_V \, \mathbf{r} \times (\rho \mathbf{v})\, \mathrm dV\,,$$

where $\rho$ is the [mass density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_density) of the moving particles. By convention the direction of the cross product is given by the [right-hand rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-hand_rule).[^o40]

This is similar to the magnetic moment created by the very large number of charged particles that make up that current:

$$\mathbf{m} = \tfrac{1}{2}\iiint_V \mathbf{r} \times (\rho_{Q} \mathbf{v})\, \mathrm dV\,,$$

where $\mathbf{j} = \rho_Q \mathbf{v}$ and $\rho_Q$ is the [charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_density) of the moving charged particles.

Comparing the two equations results in:

$$\mathbf{m} = \frac{e}{2\mu}\,\mathbf{L}\,,$$

where $e$ is the charge of the particle and $\mu$ is the mass of the particle.

Even though atomic particles cannot be accurately described as orbiting (and spinning) charge distributions of uniform charge-to-mass ratio, this general trend can be observed in the atomic world so that:

$$\mathbf{m} = g \, \frac{e}{2\mu} \, \mathbf{L},$$

where the [$g$-factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-factor_%28physics%29) depends on the particle and configuration. For example, the $g$-factor for the magnetic moment due to an electron orbiting a nucleus is one while the $g$-factor for the magnetic moment of electron due to its intrinsic angular momentum ([spin](wikipedia://en/19593829)) is a little larger than 2. The $g$-factor of atoms and molecules must account for the orbital and intrinsic moments of its electrons and possibly the intrinsic moment of its nuclei as well.

In the atomic world the angular momentum ([spin](wikipedia://en/19593829)) of a particle is an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) (or [half-integer](wikipedia://en/1568608) in the case of fermions) multiple of the reduced [Planck constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_constant) $\text{ħ}$. This is the basis for defining the magnetic moment units of [Bohr magneton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr_magneton) (assuming [charge-to-mass ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge-to-mass_ratio) of the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron)) and [nuclear magneton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magneton) (assuming [charge-to-mass ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge-to-mass_ratio) of the [proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton)). See [electron magnetic moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_magnetic_moment) and [Bohr magneton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr_magneton) for more details.

## Atoms, molecules, and elementary particles {#Atoms,_molecules,_and_elementary_particles}

Fundamentally, contributions to any system's magnetic moment may come from sources of two kinds: 1) motion of [electric charges](wikipedia://en/9804), such as [electric currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current); and 2) the intrinsic magnetism due to [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829#Magnetic_moments) of [elementary particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle), such as the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron).

Contributions due to the sources of the first kind can be calculated from knowing the distribution of all the electric currents (or, alternatively, of all the electric charges and their velocities) inside the system, by using the formulas below.

Contributions due to particle spin sum the [magnitude](wikipedia://en/32533) of each elementary particle's intrinsic magnetic moment, a fixed number, often measured experimentally to a great precision. For example, any electron's magnetic moment is measured to be \-9.284764 × 10<sup>\-24</sup> J/T.[^o42] The [direction](wikipedia://en/32533) of the magnetic moment of any elementary particle is entirely determined by the direction of its [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829), with the [negative value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minus_sign) indicating that any electron's magnetic moment is antiparallel to its spin.

The net magnetic moment of any system is a [vector sum](wikipedia://en/32533) of contributions from one or both types of sources. For example, the magnetic moment of an atom of [hydrogen-1](wikipedia://en/14225) (the lightest hydrogen isotope, consisting of a proton and an electron) is a vector sum of the following contributions:

1. the intrinsic moment of the electron,
1. the orbital motion of the electron around the proton,
1. the intrinsic moment of the proton.

Similarly, the magnetic moment of a [bar magnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar_magnet) is the sum of the contributing magnetic moments, which include the intrinsic and orbital magnetic moments of the unpaired [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) of the magnet's material and the nuclear magnetic moments.

### Magnetic moment of an atom {#Magnetic_moment_of_an_atom}

For an atom, individual electron spins are added to get a total spin, and individual orbital angular momenta are added to get a total orbital angular momentum. These two then are added using [angular momentum coupling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_momentum_coupling) to get a total angular momentum. For an atom with no nuclear magnetic moment, the magnitude of the atomic dipole moment, $\mathfrak m_\text{atom}$, is then[^o44]

$$\mathfrak m_\text{atom} = g_\text{J}\,\mu_\text{B}\,\sqrt{j\,(j+1)\,}$$

where $j$ is the [total angular momentum quantum number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_angular_momentum_quantum_number), $g$${}_{J}$ is the [Landé $g$-factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landé_g-factor), and $\mu$${}_{B}$ is the [Bohr magneton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr_magneton). The component of this magnetic moment along the direction of the magnetic field is then[^o45]

$$\mathfrak m_{\text{atom},z} = -m\,g_\text{J}\,\mu_\text{B} \, .$$

The negative sign occurs because electrons have negative charge.

The [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $m$ (not to be confused with the moment, $\mathfrak m$) is called the [magnetic quantum number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_quantum_number) or the *equatorial* quantum number, which can take on any of $2j + 1$ values:[^o46]

$$-j,\ -(j-1),\ \cdots,\ -1,\ 0,\ +1,\ \cdots,\ +(j-1),\ +j~.$$

Due to the angular momentum, the dynamics of a magnetic dipole in a magnetic field differs from that of an electric dipole in an electric field. The field does exert a torque on the magnetic dipole tending to align it with the field. However, torque is proportional to rate of change of angular momentum, so [precession](wikipedia://en/24714) occurs: the direction of spin changes. This behavior is described by the [Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert equation](wikipedia://en/16568967):[^o47][^o48]

$$\frac{1}{\gamma} \frac{\mathrm d\mathbf{m}}{\mathrm dt} = \mathbf{m} \times \mathbf{H}_\text{eff} - \frac{\lambda}{\gamma m} \mathbf{m} \times \frac{\mathrm d\mathbf{m}}{\mathrm dt}$$

where $\gamma$ is the [gyromagnetic ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyromagnetic_ratio), $\mathbf{m}$ is the magnetic moment, $\lambda$ is the damping coefficient and $\mathbf{H}$${}_{\operatorname{eff}}$ is the effective magnetic field (the external field plus any self-induced field). The first term describes precession of the moment about the effective field, while the second is a damping term related to dissipation of energy caused by interaction with the surroundings.

### Magnetic moment of an electron {#Magnetic_moment_of_an_electron}

[Electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) and many elementary particles also have intrinsic magnetic moments, an explanation of which requires a quantum mechanical treatment and relates to the intrinsic [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839) of the particles as discussed in the article [Electron magnetic moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_magnetic_moment). It is these intrinsic magnetic moments that give rise to the macroscopic effects of [magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism), and other phenomena, such as [electron paramagnetic resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_paramagnetic_resonance).

The magnetic moment of the electron is

$$\mathbf{m}_\text{S} = -\frac{g_\text{S} \mu_\text{B} \mathbf{S}}{\hbar},$$

where [$\mu$${}_{B}$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr_magneton) is the [Bohr magneton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr_magneton), $\mathbf{S}$ is electron [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829), and the [*g*-factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-factor_%28physics%29) $g$${}_{S}$ is 2 according to [Dirac's theory](wikipedia://en/39407), but due to [quantum electrodynamic](wikipedia://en/25268) effects it is slightly larger in reality: \2.00231930436. The deviation from 2 is known as the [anomalous magnetic dipole moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anomalous_magnetic_dipole_moment).

Again it is important to notice that $\mathbf{m}$ is a negative constant multiplied by the [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829), so the magnetic moment of the electron is antiparallel to the spin. This can be understood with the following classical picture: if we imagine that the spin angular momentum is created by the electron mass spinning around some axis, the electric current that this rotation creates circulates in the opposite direction, because of the negative charge of the electron; such current loops produce a magnetic moment which is antiparallel to the spin. Hence, for a positron (the anti-particle of the electron) the magnetic moment is parallel to its spin.

### Magnetic moment of a nucleus {#Magnetic_moment_of_a_nucleus}

The nuclear system is a complex physical system consisting of nucleons, i.e., [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton) and [neutrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron). The quantum mechanical properties of the nucleons include the spin among others. Since the electromagnetic moments of the nucleus depend on the spin of the individual nucleons, one can look at these properties with measurements of nuclear moments, and more specifically the nuclear magnetic dipole moment.

Most common nuclei exist in their [ground state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_state), although nuclei of some [isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope) have long-lived [excited states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excited_state). Each [energy state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_state) of a nucleus of a given isotope is characterized by a well-defined magnetic dipole moment, the magnitude of which is a fixed number, often measured experimentally to a great precision. This number is very sensitive to the individual contributions from nucleons, and a measurement or prediction of its value can reveal important information about the content of the nuclear wave function. There are several theoretical models that predict the value of the magnetic dipole moment and a number of experimental techniques aiming to carry out measurements in nuclei along the nuclear chart.

### Magnetic moment of a molecule {#Magnetic_moment_of_a_molecule}

Any molecule has a well-defined magnitude of magnetic moment, which may depend on the molecule's [energy state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_state). Typically, the overall magnetic moment of a molecule is a combination of the following contributions, in the order of their typical strength:

- magnetic moments due to its unpaired [electron spins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_spin) ([paramagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramagnetic) contribution), if any
- orbital motion of its electrons, which in the [ground state](wikipedia://en/4930033#Ground_state) is often proportional to the external magnetic field ([diamagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamagnetic) contribution)
- the combined magnetic moment of its [nuclear spins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_spin), which depends on the [nuclear spin configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_momentum_coupling#Spin-spin_coupling).

#### Examples of molecular magnetism {#Examples_of_molecular_magnetism}

- The [dioxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) molecule, O${}_{2}$, exhibits strong [paramagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramagnetism), due to unpaired spins of its outermost two electrons.
- The [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) molecule, CO${}_{2}$, mostly exhibits [diamagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamagnetism), a much weaker magnetic moment of the electron [orbitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_orbital) that is proportional to the external magnetic field. The nuclear magnetism of a magnetic [isotope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope) such as ${}^{13}$C or ${}^{17}$O will contribute to the molecule's magnetic moment.
- The [dihydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihydrogen) molecule, H${}_{2}$, in a weak (or zero) magnetic field exhibits nuclear magnetism, and can be in a [para-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parahydrogen) or an [ortho-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthohydrogen) nuclear spin configuration.
- Many transition metal complexes are magnetic. The spin-only formula is a good first approximation for high-spin complexes of first-row [transition metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_metal).[^o53]
