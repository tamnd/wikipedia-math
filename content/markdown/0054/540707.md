# Frattini subgroup

*Image: Dih4 subgroups (cycle graphs).svg.* [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199) of the [lattice of subgroups](wikipedia://en/3983172) of the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) [Dih<sub>4</sub>.](wikipedia://en/78749515) In the second row are the maximal subgroups; their intersection (the **Frattini subgroup**) is the central element in the third row. So Dih<sub>4</sub> has only one non-generating element beyond *e*.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Frattini subgroup"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), particularly in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), the **Frattini subgroup** $\Phi(G)$ of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$ is the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of all [maximal subgroups](wikipedia://en/1472649) of $G$. For the case that $G$ has no maximal subgroups, for example the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059) {*e*} or a [Prüfer group](wikipedia://en/4885031), it is defined by $\Phi(G)=G$. It is analogous to the [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656) in the theory of [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), and intuitively can be thought of as the subgroup of "small elements" (see the "non-generator" characterization below). It is named after [Giovanni Frattini](wikipedia://en/8606878), who defined the concept in a paper published in 1885.[^o2]

:::

## Some facts {#Some_facts}

- $\Phi(G)$ is equal to the set of all **non-generators** or **non-generating elements** of $G$. A non-generating element of $G$ is an element that can always be removed from a [generating set](wikipedia://en/99945); that is, an element *a* of $G$ such that whenever $X$ is a generating set of $G$ containing *a*, $X \setminus \{a\}$ is also a generating set of $G$.
- $\Phi(G)$ is always a [characteristic subgroup](wikipedia://en/7198) of $G$; in particular, it is always a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of $G$.
- If $G$ is [finite](wikipedia://en/323707), then $\Phi(G)$ is [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569).
- If $G$ is a finite [*p*-group](wikipedia://en/24664), then $\Phi(G)=G^p [G,G]$. Thus the Frattini subgroup is the smallest (with respect to inclusion) [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) *N* such that the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) $G/N$ is an [elementary abelian group](wikipedia://en/4843202), i.e., [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to a [direct sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_sum_of_abelian_groups) of [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) of [order](wikipedia://en/493527) *p*. Moreover, if the quotient group $G/\Phi(G)$ (also called the *Frattini quotient* of $G$) has order $p^k$, then *k* is the smallest number of generators for $G$ (that is, the smallest cardinality of a generating set for $G$). In particular, a finite *p*-group is cyclic [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its Frattini quotient is cyclic (of order *p*). A finite *p*-group is elementary abelian if and only if its Frattini subgroup is the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059), $\Phi(G)=\{e\}$.
- If $H$ and $K$ are finite, then $\Phi(H \times K) = \Phi(H) \times \Phi(K)$.

An example of a group with nontrivial Frattini subgroup is the cyclic group $G$ of order $p^2$, where *p* is [prime](wikipedia://en/23666), generated by *a*, say; here, $\Phi(G) = \left\langle a^p\right\rangle$.
