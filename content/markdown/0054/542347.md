# Specialization preorder

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Specialization preorder"}

In the branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) known as [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), the **specialization** (or **canonical**) **preorder** is a natural [preorder](wikipedia://en/23582) on the set of the points of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450). For most spaces that are considered in practice, namely for all those that satisfy the [T<sub>0</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T0_space) [separation axiom](wikipedia://en/40117302), this preorder is even a [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) (called the **specialization order**). On the other hand, for [T<sub>1</sub> spaces](wikipedia://en/148721) the order becomes trivial and is of little interest.

:::

The specialization order is often considered in applications in [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), where T<sub>0</sub> spaces occur in [denotational semantics](wikipedia://en/55275). The specialization order is also important for identifying suitable topologies on partially ordered sets, as is done in [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924).

## Definition and motivation {#Definition_and_motivation}

Consider any topological space $X$. The **specialization preorder** ≤ on $X$ relates two points of $X$ when one lies in the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) of the other. However, various authors disagree on which 'direction' the order should go. What is agreed is that if

$x$ is contained in $\mathrm{cl}\{y\}$,

(where $\mathrm{cl}\{y\}$ denotes the closure of the [singleton set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singleton_set) $\{y\}$, i.e., the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of all [closed sets](wikipedia://en/47279) containing $\{y\}$), we say that $x$ is a **specialization** of $y$ and that $y$ is a **generalization** of $x$; this is commonly written $y \rightsquigarrow x$ or $y \to x.$ The notation $y \to x$ is consistent with the fact that $x \in \mathrm{cl}\{y\}$ if and only if the constant sequence with constant value $y$ [converges](wikipedia://en/22170#Limits_of_nets) to $x$ in $X$ (in the sense that every [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of $x$ contains $y$).

Unfortunately, the property "$x$ is a specialization of $y$" is alternatively written as $x \leq y$ and as $y \leq x$ by various authors (see, respectively,[^o3] and [^o4]).

Both definitions have intuitive justifications: in the case of the former, we have

$x \leq y$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $\mathrm{cl}\{x\} \subseteq \mathrm{cl}\{y\}.$

However, in the case where our space $X$ is the [prime spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_spectrum) $\mathrm{Spec}(R)$ of a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) $R$ (which is the motivational situation in applications related to [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997)), then under our second definition of the order, we have

$y \leq x$ if and only if $y \subseteq x$ as [prime ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_ideals) of the ring $R$.

For the sake of consistency, for the remainder of this article we will take the first definition, that "$x$ is a specialization of $y$" be written as $x \leq y$. We then see that

$x \leq y$ if and only if $x$ is contained in all [closed sets](wikipedia://en/47279) that contain $y,$ and

$x \leq y$ if and only if $y$ is contained in all [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) that contain $x$.

These restatements help to explain why one speaks of a "specialization": $y$ is more general than $x$ since it is contained in more open sets. This is particularly intuitive if one views closed sets as properties that a point $x$ may or may not have. The more closed sets contain a point, the more properties the point has, and the more special it is. The usage is [consistent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consistent) with the classical logical notions of [genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genus) and [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species); and also with the traditional use of [generic points](wikipedia://en/3043978) in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), in which closed points are the most specific, while a generic point of a space is one contained in every nonempty open subset. Specialization as an idea is applied also in [valuation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valuation_theory).

The intuition of upper elements being more specific is typically found in [domain theory](wikipedia://en/325077), a branch of order theory that has ample applications in computer science.

## Upper and lower sets {#Upper_and_lower_sets}

Let *X* be a topological space and let ≤ be the specialization preorder on *X*. Every [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) is an [upper set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_set) with respect to ≤ and every [closed set](wikipedia://en/47279) is a [lower set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_set). The converses are not generally true. In fact, a topological space is an [Alexandrov-discrete space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandrov-discrete_space) if and only if every upper set is also open (or equivalently every lower set is also closed).

Let *A* be a subset of *X*. The smallest upper set containing *A* is denoted ↑*A* and the smallest lower set containing *A* is denoted ↓*A*. In case *A* = {*x*} is a singleton one uses the notation ↑*x* and ↓*x*. For *x* ∈ *X* one has:

- ↑*x* = {*y* ∈ *X* : *x* ≤ *y*} = ∩{open sets containing *x*}.
- ↓*x* = {*y* ∈ *X* : *y* ≤ *x*} = ∩{closed sets containing *x*} = cl{*x*}.

The lower set ↓*x* is always closed; however, the upper set ↑*x* need not be open or closed. The closed points of a topological space *X* are precisely the [minimal elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_element) of *X* with respect to ≤.

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- In the [Sierpinski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpinski_space) {0,1} with open sets {∅, {1}, {0,1}} the specialization order is the natural one (0 ≤ 0, 0 ≤ 1, and 1 ≤ 1).
- If *p*, *q* are elements of Spec(*R*) (the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/37291) of a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) *R*) then *p* ≤ *q* if and only if *q* ⊆ *p* (as [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928)). Thus the closed points of Spec(*R*) are precisely the [maximal ideals](wikipedia://en/48164).

:::

## Important properties {#Important_properties}

As suggested by the name, the specialization preorder is a preorder, i.e. it is [reflexive](wikipedia://en/200458) and [transitive](wikipedia://en/200463).

The [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) determined by the specialization preorder is just that of [topological indistinguishability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologically_indistinguishable). That is, *x* and *y* are topologically indistinguishable if and only if *x* ≤ *y* and *y* ≤ *x*. Therefore, the [antisymmetry](wikipedia://en/1176) of ≤ is precisely the T<sub>0</sub> separation axiom: if *x* and *y* are indistinguishable then *x* = *y*. In this case it is justified to speak of the **specialization order**.

On the other hand, the [symmetry](wikipedia://en/200459) of the specialization preorder is equivalent to the [R<sub>0</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R0_space) separation axiom: *x* ≤ *y* if and only if *x* and *y* are topologically indistinguishable. It follows that if the underlying topology is T<sub>1</sub>, then the specialization preorder is discrete, i.e. one has *x* ≤ *y* if and only if *x* = *y*. Hence, the specialization order is of little interest for T<sub>1</sub> topologies, especially for all [Hausdorff spaces](wikipedia://en/13637).

Any [continuous function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_%28topology%29) $f$ between two topological spaces is [monotone](wikipedia://en/48260) with respect to the specialization preorders of these spaces: $x\le y$ implies $f(x)\le f(y).$ The converse, however, is not true in general. In the language of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), we then have a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051) to the [category of preordered sets](wikipedia://en/540904) that assigns a topological space its specialization preorder. This functor has a [left adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_adjoint), which places the [Alexandrov topology](wikipedia://en/542198) on a preordered set.

There are spaces that are more specific than T<sub>0</sub> spaces for which this order is interesting: the [sober spaces](wikipedia://en/602490). Their relationship to the specialization order is more subtle:

For any sober space *X* with specialization order ≤, we have

- (*X*, ≤) is a [directed complete partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_complete_partial_order), i.e. every [directed subset](wikipedia://en/9165) *S* of (*X*, ≤) has a [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) sup *S*,
- for every directed subset *S* of (*X*, ≤) and every open set *O*, if sup *S* is in *O*, then *S* and *O* have [non-empty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-empty) [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429).

One may describe the second property by saying that open sets are *inaccessible by directed suprema*. A topology is **order consistent** with respect to a certain order ≤ if it induces ≤ as its specialization order and it has the above property of inaccessibility with respect to (existing) suprema of directed sets in ≤.

## Topologies on orders {#Topologies_on_orders}

The specialization order yields a tool to obtain a preorder from every topology. It is natural to ask for the converse too: Is every preorder obtained as a specialization preorder of some topology?

Indeed, the answer to this question is positive and there are in general many topologies on a set *X* that induce a given order ≤ as their specialization order. The [Alexandroff topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandroff_topology) of the order ≤ plays a special role: it is the finest topology that induces ≤. The other extreme, the coarsest topology that induces ≤, is the [upper topology](wikipedia://en/1017002), the least topology within which all complements of sets ↓*x* (for some *x* in *X*) are open.

There are also interesting topologies in between these two extremes. The finest sober topology that is order consistent in the above sense for a given order ≤ is the [Scott topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott_topology). The upper topology however is still the coarsest sober order-consistent topology. In fact, its open sets are even inaccessible by *any* suprema. Hence any [sober space](wikipedia://en/602490) with specialization order ≤ is finer than the upper topology and coarser than the Scott topology. Yet, such a space may fail to exist, that is, there exist partial orders for which there is no sober order-consistent topology. Especially, the Scott topology is not necessarily sober.
