# Feuerbach point

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Feuerbach point"}

*Image: Circ9pnt3.svg.* Feuerbach's theorem: the [nine-point circle](wikipedia://en/161243) is [tangent](wikipedia://en/19349153) to the [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle) and [excircles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excircles) of a triangle. The incircle tangency is the Feuerbach point.

In the [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) of [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654), the [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle) and [nine-point circle](wikipedia://en/161243) of a non-equilateral triangle are internally [tangent](wikipedia://en/19349153) to each other at the **Feuerbach point** of the triangle. The Feuerbach point is a [triangle center](wikipedia://en/12401488), meaning that its definition does not depend on the placement and scale of the triangle. It is listed as X(11) in [Clark Kimberling](wikipedia://en/25536421)'s [Encyclopedia of Triangle Centers](wikipedia://en/15003593), and is named after [Karl Wilhelm Feuerbach](wikipedia://en/161246).[^o2][^o3]

:::

**Feuerbach's theorem**, published by Feuerbach in 1822,[^o4] states more generally that the nine-point circle is tangent to the three [excircles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excircle) of the triangle as well as its incircle.[^o5] A very short proof of this theorem based on [Casey's theorem](wikipedia://en/14599476) on the [bitangents](wikipedia://en/2605212) of four circles tangent to a fifth circle was published by [John Casey](wikipedia://en/17297405) in 1866;[^o6] Feuerbach's theorem has also been used as a test case for [automated theorem proving](wikipedia://en/2546).[^o7] The three points of tangency with the excircles form the **Feuerbach triangle** of the given triangle.

::: {#o9 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

The [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle) of a triangle *ABC* is a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) that is tangent to all three sides of the triangle. Its center, the [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) of the triangle, lies at the point where the three internal angle bisectors of the triangle cross each other.

The [nine-point circle](wikipedia://en/161243) is another circle defined from a triangle. It is so called because it passes through nine significant points of the triangle, among which the simplest to construct are the [midpoints](wikipedia://en/437979) of the triangle's sides. The nine-point circle passes through these three midpoints; thus, it is the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432) of the [medial triangle](wikipedia://en/3339743).

These two circles meet in a single point, where they are [tangent](wikipedia://en/19349153) to each other. That point of tangency is the Feuerbach point of the triangle. In an equilateral triangle, the nine-point circle is the same as the incircle, and the Feuerbach point is therefore not defined.

Associated with the incircle of a triangle are three more circles, the [excircles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excircles). These are circles that are each tangent to the three lines through the triangle's sides. Each excircle touches one of these lines from the opposite side of the triangle, and is on the same side as the triangle for the other two lines. Like the incircle, the excircles are all tangent to the nine-point circle. Their points of tangency with the nine-point circle form a triangle, the Feuerbach triangle.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

The Feuerbach point lies on the line through the centers of the two tangent circles that define it. These centers are the [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) and [nine-point center](wikipedia://en/4517570) of the triangle.[^o2][^o3]

Let $x$, $y$, and $z$ be the three distances of the Feuerbach point to the vertices of the [medial triangle](wikipedia://en/3339743) (the midpoints of the sides *BC=a, CA=b*, and *AB=c* respectively of the original triangle). Then,[^o13]

$$x+y+z = 2\max(x,y,z),$$

or, equivalently, the largest of the three distances equals the sum of the other two. Specifically, we have $x=\frac{R}{2OI}|b-c|, \, y=\frac{R}{2OI}|c-a|, z=\frac{R}{2OI}|a-b|,$ where *O* is the reference triangle's [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) and *I* is its [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837).[^o13]

The latter property also holds for the tangency point of any of the excircles with the nine–point circle: the greatest distance from this tangency to one of the original triangle's side midpoints equals the sum of the distances to the other two side midpoints.[^o13]

If the incircle of triangle ABC touches the sides *BC, CA, AB* at *X*, *Y*, and *Z* respectively, and the midpoints of these sides are respectively *P*, *Q*, and *R*, then with Feuerbach point *F* the triangles *FPX*, *FQY*, and *FRZ* are similar to the triangles *AOI, BOI, COI* respectively.[^o13]

:::

## Coordinates {#Coordinates}

The [trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790) for the Feuerbach point are[^o3]

$$1 - \cos (B - C) : 1 - \cos (C - A) : 1 - \cos (A - B).$$

Its [barycentric coordinates](wikipedia://en/762954#Barycentric_coordinates_on_triangles) are[^o13]

$$(s-a)(b-c)^2 : (s-b)(c-a)^2 : (s-c)(a-b)^2,$$

where *s* is the triangle's [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617), $s = \tfrac12(a+b+c) {}$.

The three lines from the vertices of the original triangle through the corresponding vertices of the Feuerbach triangle meet at another triangle center, listed as X(12) in the Encyclopedia of Triangle Centers. Its trilinear coordinates are:[^o3]

$$1 + \cos (B - C) : 1 + \cos (C - A) : 1 + \cos (A - B).$$
