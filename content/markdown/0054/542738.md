# Florian Cajori

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Florian Cajori"}

**Florian Cajori** (February 28, 1859 – August 14[^o3][^o4] or 15,[^o5] 1930) was a Swiss-American [historian of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historian_of_mathematics), especially known for his comprehensive treatise about [mathematical notation](wikipedia://en/277184), *[A History of Mathematical Notations](wikipedia://en/68374755)*.

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Florian Cajori was born in [Zillis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zillis-Reischen), [Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switzerland), as the son of Georg Cajori and Catherine Camenisch. He attended schools first in Zillis and later in [Chur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chur). In 1875, Florian Cajori emigrated to the United States at the age of sixteen, and attended the [State Normal school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisconsin_State_College_of_Milwaukee) in [Whitewater, Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitewater,_Wisconsin). After graduating in 1878, he taught in a country school, and later began studying [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) at [University of Wisconsin–Madison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin–Madison).[^o9]

In 1883, Cajori received both his bachelor's and master's degrees from the University of Wisconsin–Madison, briefly attended Johns Hopkins University for 8 months in between degrees.[^o7] He taught for a few years at [Tulane University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulane_University), before being appointed as professor of applied mathematics there in 1887. He was then driven north by tuberculosis. He founded the Colorado College Scientific Society and taught at [Colorado College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_College) where he held the chair in physics from 1889 to 1898 and the chair in mathematics from 1898 to 1918. He was the position Dean of the engineering department.[^o8] While at Colorado, he received his doctorate from Tulane in 1894, and married Elizabeth G. Edwards in 1890 and had one son.[^o9]

Cajori's *A History of Mathematics* (1894) was the first popular presentation of the history of mathematics in the United States.[^o9] Based upon his reputation in the history of mathematics (even today his 1928–1929 *History of Mathematical Notations* has been described as "unsurpassed")[^o10] he was appointed in 1918 to the first history of mathematics chair in the U.S, created especially for him, at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley). He remained in Berkeley, California until his death in 1930. Cajori did no original mathematical research unrelated to the history of mathematics.[^o9] In addition to his numerous books, he also contributed highly recognized and popular historical articles to the *[American Mathematical Monthly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Monthly)*.[^o8] His last work was a revision of Andrew Motte's 1729 translation of Newton's [*Principia*](wikipedia://en/48781), vol.1 The Motion of Bodies, but he died before it was completed. The work was finished by R.T. Crawford of Berkeley, California.

## Societies and honors {#Societies_and_honors}

- 1917–1918, [Mathematical Association of America](wikipedia://en/198772) president
- 1923, American Association for the Advancement of Science vice-president
- 1924, Invited Speaker of the [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) in 1924 in [Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toronto)[^o12][^o13]
- 1924–1925, [History of Science Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Science_Society) vice-president
- 1929–1930, Comité International d'Histoire des Sciences vice-president
- The [Cajori crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cajori_%28crater%29) on the Moon was named in his honour[^o14]
