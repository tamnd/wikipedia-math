# Artinian module

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Artinian module"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), an **Artinian module** is a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) that satisfies the [descending chain condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descending_chain_condition) on its [poset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poset) of [submodules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule). They are for modules what [Artinian rings](wikipedia://en/489273) are for [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), and a ring is Artinian [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is an Artinian module over itself (with left or right multiplication). Both concepts are named for [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351).

:::

In the presence of the axiom of ([dependent](wikipedia://en/441950)) [choice](wikipedia://en/840), the descending chain condition becomes equivalent to the [minimum condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_condition), and so that may be used in the definition instead.

Like [Noetherian modules](wikipedia://en/542431), Artinian modules enjoy the following heredity property:

- If *M* is an Artinian *R*-module, then so is any submodule and any [quotient](wikipedia://en/364820) of *M*.

The [converse](wikipedia://en/155941) also holds:

- If *M* is any *R*-module and *N* any Artinian submodule such that *M*/*N* is Artinian, then *M* is Artinian.

As a consequence, any [finitely-generated module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely-generated_module) over an Artinian ring is Artinian.[^o2] Since an Artinian ring is also a [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129), and finitely-generated modules over a Noetherian ring are Noetherian,[^o2] it is true that for an Artinian ring *R*, any finitely-generated *R*-module is both Noetherian and Artinian, and is said to be of finite [length](wikipedia://en/816070). It also follows that any finitely generated Artinian module is Noetherian even without the assumption of *R* being Artinian. However, if *R* is not Artinian and *M* is not finitely-generated, there are counterexamples.

## Left and right Artinian rings, modules and bimodules {#Left_and_right_Artinian_rings,_modules_and_bimodules}

The ring *R* can be considered as a right module, where the action is the natural one given by the ring multiplication on the right. *R* is called right [Artinian](wikipedia://en/489273) when this right module *R* is an Artinian module. The definition of "left Artinian ring" is done analogously. For [noncommutative rings](wikipedia://en/24062516) this distinction is necessary, because it is possible for a ring to be Artinian on one side but not the other.

The left-right adjectives are not normally necessary for modules, because the module *M* is usually given as a left or right *R*-module at the outset. However, it is possible that *M* may have both a left and right *R*-module structure, and then calling *M* Artinian is ambiguous, and it becomes necessary to clarify which module structure is Artinian. To separate the properties of the two structures, one can abuse terminology and refer to *M* as left Artinian or right Artinian when, strictly speaking, it is correct to say that *M*, with its left *R*-module structure, is Artinian.

The occurrence of modules with a left and right structure is not unusual: for example *R* itself has a left and right *R*-module structure. In fact this is an example of a [bimodule](wikipedia://en/492612), and it may be possible for an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) *M* to be made into a left-*R*, right-*S* bimodule for a different ring *S*. Indeed, for any right module *M*, it is automatically a left module over the ring of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563#Algebraic_properties) **Z**, and moreover is a **Z**-*R*-bimodule. For example, consider the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) **Q** as a **Z**-**Q**-bimodule in the natural way. Then **Q** is not Artinian as a left **Z**-module, but it is Artinian as a right **Q**-module.

The Artinian condition can be defined on bimodule structures as well: an **Artinian bimodule** is a [bimodule](wikipedia://en/492612) whose poset of sub-bimodules satisfies the descending chain condition. Since a sub-bimodule of an *R*-*S*-bimodule *M* is a fortiori a left *R*-module, if *M* considered as a left *R*-module were Artinian, then *M* is automatically an Artinian bimodule. It may happen, however, that a bimodule is Artinian without its left or right structures being Artinian, as the following example will show.

*Example:* It is well known that a [simple ring](wikipedia://en/443387) is left Artinian if and only if it is right Artinian, in which case it is a [semisimple ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_ring). Let *R* be a simple ring which is not right Artinian. Then it is also not left Artinian. Considering *R* as an *R*-*R*-bimodule in the natural way, its sub-bimodules are exactly the [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) of *R*. Since *R* is simple there are only two: *R* and the [zero ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_ideal). Thus the bimodule *R* is Artinian as a bimodule, but not Artinian as a left or right *R*-module over itself.

:::

## Relation to the Noetherian condition {#Relation_to_the_Noetherian_condition}

Unlike the case of rings, there are Artinian modules which are not [Noetherian modules](wikipedia://en/542431). For example, consider the *p*-primary component of $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$, that is $\mathbb{Z}[1/p] / \mathbb{Z}$, which is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the *p*-[quasicyclic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasicyclic_group) $\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$, regarded as $\mathbb{Z}$-module. The chain $\langle 1/p \rangle \subset \langle 1/p^2 \rangle \subset \langle 1/p^3 \rangle \subset \cdots$ does not terminate, so $\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ (and therefore $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$) is not Noetherian. Yet every descending chain of submodules terminates, since any proper submodule has the form $\langle 1/n \rangle$ for some integer $n$ and is therefore a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742); so $\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ is Artinian.

Note that $\mathbb{Z}[1/p] / \mathbb{Z}$ is also a [faithful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faithful_module) $\mathbb{Z}$-module. So, this also provides an example of a faithful Artinian module over a non-Artinian ring. This does not happen for Noetherian case; if *M* is a faithful Noetherian module over *A* then *A* is Noetherian as well.

Over a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346), every [cyclic](wikipedia://en/4128720) Artinian module is also Noetherian, but over noncommutative rings cyclic Artinian modules can have uncountable [length](wikipedia://en/816070) as shown in the article of Hartley and summarized in the [Paul Cohn](wikipedia://en/18326967) article dedicated to Hartley's memory.

Another relevant result is the [Akizuki–Hopkins–Levitzki theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akizuki–Hopkins–Levitzki_theorem), which states that the Artinian and Noetherian conditions are equivalent for modules over a [semiprimary ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiprimary_ring).
