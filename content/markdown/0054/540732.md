# Poincaré duality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Poincaré duality"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Poincaré duality** theorem, named after [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740), is a basic result on the structure of the [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) and [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060) [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) of [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470). It states that if *M* is an *n*-dimensional [oriented](wikipedia://en/187446) [closed manifold](wikipedia://en/669475) ([compact](wikipedia://en/6042) and without boundary), then the *k*th cohomology group of *M* is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to the (*n* − *k*)th homology group of *M*, for all integers *k*

:::

$$H^k(M) \cong H_{n-k}(M).$$

Poincaré duality holds for any coefficient [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), so long as one has taken an orientation with respect to that coefficient ring; in particular, since every manifold has a unique orientation mod 2, Poincaré duality holds mod 2 without any assumption of orientation.

## History {#History}

A form of Poincaré duality was first stated, without proof, by [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) in 1893. It was stated in terms of [Betti numbers](wikipedia://en/346262): The *k*th and (*n* − *k*)th Betti numbers of a closed (i.e., compact and without boundary) orientable *n*-manifold are equal. The *cohomology* concept was at that time about 40 years from being clarified. In his 1895 paper *[Analysis Situs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_Situs_%28paper%29)*, Poincaré tried to prove the theorem using topological [intersection theory](wikipedia://en/3148602), which he had invented. Criticism of his work by [Poul Heegaard](wikipedia://en/1138214) led him to realize that his proof was seriously flawed. In the first two complements to *Analysis Situs*, Poincaré gave a new proof in terms of dual triangulations.

Poincaré duality did not take on its modern form until the advent of cohomology in the 1930s, when [Eduard Čech](wikipedia://en/859830) and [Hassler Whitney](wikipedia://en/391794) invented the [cup](wikipedia://en/848684) and [cap products](wikipedia://en/5230810) and formulated Poincaré duality in these new terms.

## Modern formulation {#Modern_formulation}

The modern statement of the Poincaré duality theorem is in terms of homology and cohomology: if *M* is a closed oriented *n*-manifold, then there is a canonically defined isomorphism $H^k(M, \mathbb{Z}) \to H_{n-k}(M, \mathbb{Z})$ for any integer *k*. To define such an isomorphism, one chooses a fixed [fundamental class](wikipedia://en/669440) \[*M*\] of *M*, which will exist if $M$ is oriented. Then the isomorphism is defined by mapping an element $\alpha \in H^k(M)$ to the [cap product](wikipedia://en/5230810) $[M]\frown \alpha$.[^o4]

Homology and cohomology groups are defined to be zero for negative degrees, so Poincaré duality in particular implies that the homology and cohomology groups of orientable closed *n*-manifolds are zero for degrees bigger than *n*.

Here, homology and cohomology are integral, but the isomorphism remains valid over any coefficient ring. In the case where an oriented manifold is not compact, one has to replace homology by [Borel–Moore homology](wikipedia://en/6495737)

$$H^i(X) \stackrel{\cong}{\to} H_{n-i}^{BM}(X),$$

or replace cohomology by [cohomology with compact support](wikipedia://en/20626603)

$$H^i_c(X) \stackrel{\cong}{\to} H_{n-i}(X).$$

## Dual cell structures {#Dual_cell_structures}

Given a triangulated manifold, there is a corresponding dual polyhedral decomposition. The dual polyhedral decomposition is a cell decomposition of the manifold such that the *k*-cells of the dual polyhedral decomposition are in bijective correspondence with the ($n-k$)-cells of the triangulation, generalizing the notion of [dual polyhedra](wikipedia://en/8815).

*Image: Dually007.png.* $\cup_{S \in T} \Delta \cap DS$ – a picture of the parts of the dual-cells in a top-dimensional simplex.

Precisely, let $T$ be a triangulation of an $n$-manifold $M$. Let $S$ be a simplex of $T$. Let $\Delta$ be a top-dimensional simplex of $T$ containing $S$, so we can think of $S$ as a subset of the vertices of $\Delta$. Define the dual cell $DS$ corresponding to $S$ so that $\Delta \cap DS$ is the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) in $\Delta$ of the barycentres of all subsets of the vertices of $\Delta$ that contain $S$. One can check that if $S$ is $i$-dimensional, then $DS$ is an $(n-i)$-dimensional cell. Moreover, the dual cells to $T$ form a CW-decomposition of $M$, and the only ($n-i$)-dimensional dual cell that intersects an $i$-cell $S$ is $DS$. Thus the pairing $C_i M \otimes C_{n-i} M \to \mathbb{Z}$ given by taking intersections induces an isomorphism $C_i M \to C^{\, n-i} M$, where $C_i$ is the [cellular homology](wikipedia://en/2113969) of the triangulation $T$, and $C_{n-i} M$ and $C^{\, n-i} M$ are the cellular homologies and cohomologies of the dual polyhedral/CW decomposition the manifold respectively. The fact that this is an isomorphism of [chain complexes](wikipedia://en/138404) is a proof of Poincaré duality. Roughly speaking, this amounts to the fact that the boundary relation for the triangulation $T$ is the incidence relation for the dual polyhedral decomposition under the correspondence $S \longmapsto DS$.

### Naturality {#Naturality}

Note that $H^k$ is a [contravariant functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contravariant_functor) while $H_{n-k}$ is [covariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariant_functor). The family of isomorphisms

$$D_M\colon H^k(M) \to H_{n-k}(M)$$

is [natural](wikipedia://en/45022) in the following sense: if

$$f\colon M\to N$$

is a [continuous map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_map) between two oriented *n*-manifolds which is compatible with orientation, i.e. which maps the fundamental class of *M* to the fundamental class of *N*, then

$$D_N = f_{*} \circ D_M \circ f^{*} ,$$

where $f_{*}$ and $f^{*}$ are the maps induced by $f$ in homology and cohomology, respectively.

Note the very strong and crucial hypothesis that $f$ maps the fundamental class of *M* to the fundamental class of *N*. Naturality does not hold for an arbitrary continuous map $f$, since in general $f^{*}$ is not an injection on cohomology. For example, if $f$ is a covering map then it maps the fundamental class of *M* to a multiple of the fundamental class of *N*. This multiple is the degree of the map $f$.

## Bilinear pairings formulation {#Bilinear_pairings_formulation}

Assuming the manifold *M* is compact, boundaryless, and [orientable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientable_manifold), let

$$\tau H_i M$$

denote the [torsion](wikipedia://en/3039330) subgroup of $H_i M$ and let

$$fH_i M = H_i M / \tau H_i M$$

be the [free](wikipedia://en/59735) part – all homology groups taken with integer coefficients in this section. Then there are [bilinear maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilinear_operator) which are [duality pairings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duality_pairing) (explained below).

$$fH_i M \otimes fH_{n-i} M \to \mathbb{Z}$$

and

$$\tau H_i M \otimes \tau H_{n-i-1} M \to \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}.$$

Here $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ is the quotient of the rationals by the integers, taken as an additive group. Notice that in the torsion linking form, there is a −1 in the dimension, so the paired dimensions add up to *n* − 1, rather than to *n*.

The first form is typically called the *[intersection product](wikipedia://en/3148602)* and the 2nd the *torsion linking form*. Assuming the manifold *M* is smooth, the intersection product is computed by perturbing the homology classes to be transverse and computing their oriented intersection number. For the torsion linking form, one computes the pairing of *x* and *y* by realizing *nx* as the boundary of some class *z*. The form then takes the value equal to the fraction whose numerator is the transverse intersection number of *z* with *y*, and whose denominator is *n*.

The statement that the pairings are duality pairings means that the adjoint maps

$$fH_i M \to \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(fH_{n-i} M,\mathbb{Z})$$

and

$$\tau H_i M \to \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\tau H_{n-i-1} M, \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})$$

are isomorphisms of groups.

This result is an application of Poincaré duality

$$H_i M \simeq H^{n-i} M,$$

together with the [universal coefficient theorem](wikipedia://en/996107), which gives an identification

$$fH^{n-i} M \equiv \mathrm{Hom}(H_{n-i} M; \mathbb{Z})$$

and

$$\tau H^{n-i} M \equiv \mathrm{Ext}(H_{n-i-1} M; \mathbb{Z}) \equiv \mathrm{Hom}(\tau H_{n-i-1} M; \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}).$$

Thus, Poincaré duality says that $fH_i M$ and $fH_{n-i} M$ are isomorphic, although there is no natural map giving the isomorphism, and similarly $\tau H_i M$ and $\tau H_{n-i-1} M$ are also isomorphic, though not naturally.

**Middle dimension**

While for most dimensions, Poincaré duality induces a bilinear *pairing* between different homology groups, in the middle dimension it induces a [bilinear *form*](wikipedia://en/449477) on a single homology group. The resulting [intersection form](wikipedia://en/3148602#Topological_intersection_form) is a very important topological invariant.

What is meant by "middle dimension" depends on parity. For even dimension *n* = 2*k*, which is more common, this is literally the middle dimension *k*, and there is a form on the free part of the middle homology:

$$fH_k M \otimes fH_k M \to \mathbb{Z}$$

By contrast, for odd dimension *n* = 2*k* + 1, which is less commonly discussed, it is most simply the lower middle dimension *k*, and there is a form on the torsion part of the homology in that dimension:

$$\tau H_k M \otimes \tau H_k M \to \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}.$$

However, there is also a pairing between the free part of the homology in the lower middle dimension *k* and in the upper middle dimension *k* + 1:

$$fH_k M \otimes fH_{k+1} M \to \mathbb{Z}.$$

The resulting groups, while not a single group with a bilinear form, are a simple chain complex and are studied in algebraic [L-theory](wikipedia://en/696568).

**Applications**

This approach to Poincaré duality was used by [Józef Przytycki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Józef_H._Przytycki) and Akira Yasuhara to give an elementary homotopy and diffeomorphism classification of 3-dimensional [lens spaces](wikipedia://en/885725).[^o8]

## Application to Euler characteristics {#Application_to_Euler_characteristics}

An immediate result from Poincaré duality is that any closed odd-dimensional manifold *M* has [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) zero, which in turn gives that any manifold that bounds has even Euler characteristic.

## Thom isomorphism formulation {#Thom_isomorphism_formulation}

Poincaré duality is closely related to the [Thom isomorphism theorem](wikipedia://en/1243451). Let $M$ be a compact, boundaryless oriented *n*-manifold, and *M* × *M* the product of *M* with itself. Let *V* be an open [tubular neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/3192042) of the diagonal in *M* × *M*. Consider the maps:

- $H_* M \otimes H_* M \to H_* (M \times M)$ the [homology cross product](wikipedia://en/918047)
- $H_* (M \times M) \to H_* \left(M \times M, (M \times M) \setminus V\right)$ inclusion.
- $H_* \left(M \times M, (M \times M) \setminus V\right) \to H_* (\nu M, \partial \nu M)$ [excision map](wikipedia://en/1248603) where $\nu M$ is the [normal disc bundle](wikipedia://en/2370200) of the diagonal in $M \times M$.
- $H_* (\nu M, \partial \nu M) \to H_{*-n} M$ the [Thom isomorphism](wikipedia://en/1243451). This map is well-defined as there is a standard identification $\nu M \equiv TM$ which is an oriented bundle, so the Thom isomorphism applies.

Combined, this gives a map $H_i M \otimes H_j M \to H_{i+j-n} M$, which is the *intersection product*, generalizing the intersection product discussed above. A similar argument with the [Künneth theorem](wikipedia://en/918047) gives the *torsion linking form*.

This formulation of Poincaré duality has become popular[^o11] as it defines Poincaré duality for any [generalized homology theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_homology_theory), given a Künneth theorem and a Thom isomorphism for that homology theory. A Thom isomorphism theorem for a homology theory is now viewed as the generalized notion of [orientability](wikipedia://en/187446) for that theory. For example, a [spin<sup>**C**</sup>-structure](wikipedia://en/2844303) on a manifold is a precise analog of an orientation within [complex topological k-theory](wikipedia://en/246748).

## Generalizations and related results {#Generalizations_and_related_results}

The [Poincaré–Lefschetz duality theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré–Lefschetz_duality_theorem) is a generalisation for manifolds with boundary. In the non-orientable case, taking into account the [sheaf](wikipedia://en/245466) of local orientations, one can give a statement that is independent of orientability: see [twisted Poincaré duality](wikipedia://en/32182870).

*Blanchfield duality* is a version of Poincaré duality which provides an isomorphism between the homology of an abelian [covering space](wikipedia://en/250323) of a manifold and the corresponding cohomology with compact supports. It is used to get basic structural results about the [Alexander module](wikipedia://en/1107647) and can be used to define the [signatures of a knot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_of_a_knot).

With the development of [homology theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_theory) to include [K-theory](wikipedia://en/246748) and other *extraordinary* theories from about 1955, it was realised that the homology $H'_*$ could be replaced by other theories, once the products on manifolds were constructed; and there are now textbook treatments in generality. More specifically, there is a general Poincaré duality theorem for a [generalized homology theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_homology_theory) which requires a notion of orientation with respect to a homology theory, and is formulated in terms of a generalized [Thom isomorphism theorem](wikipedia://en/1243451). The Thom isomorphism theorem in this regard can be considered as the germinal idea for Poincaré duality for generalized homology theories.

[Verdier duality](wikipedia://en/7190735) is the appropriate generalization to (possibly [singular](wikipedia://en/581763)) geometric objects, such as [analytic spaces](wikipedia://en/8031773) or [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754), while [intersection homology](wikipedia://en/705749) was developed by [Robert MacPherson](wikipedia://en/10238121) and [Mark Goresky](wikipedia://en/10238162) for [stratified spaces](wikipedia://en/25886609), such as real or complex algebraic varieties, precisely so as to generalise Poincaré duality to such stratified spaces.

There are many other forms of geometric duality in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), including [Lefschetz duality](wikipedia://en/16481963), [Alexander duality](wikipedia://en/3108888), [Hodge duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_duality), and [S-duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-duality_%28homotopy_theory%29).

More algebraically, one can abstract the notion of a [Poincaré complex](wikipedia://en/28277010), which is an algebraic object that behaves like the [singular chain complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_chain_complex) of a manifold, notably satisfying Poincaré duality on its homology groups, with respect to a distinguished element (corresponding to the fundamental class). These are used in [surgery theory](wikipedia://en/5961115) to algebraicize questions about manifolds. A [Poincaré space](wikipedia://en/17094227) is one whose singular chain complex is a Poincaré complex. These are not all manifolds, but their failure to be manifolds can be measured by [obstruction theory](wikipedia://en/2179639).
