# Non-commutative cryptography

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Non-commutative cryptography"}

**Non-commutative cryptography** is the area of [cryptology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptology) where the [cryptographic primitives](wikipedia://en/4928985), methods and systems are based on [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364) like [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799), [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) and [rings](wikipedia://en/48404) which are [non-commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-commutative). One of the earliest applications of a non-commutative algebraic structure for cryptographic purposes was the use of [braid groups](wikipedia://en/550138) to develop cryptographic protocols. Later several other non-commutative structures like [Thompson groups](wikipedia://en/612245), [polycyclic groups](wikipedia://en/4000598), [Grigorchuk groups](wikipedia://en/18707371), and [matrix groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_group) have been identified as potential candidates for cryptographic applications. In contrast to non-commutative cryptography, the currently widely used [public-key cryptosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public-key_cryptosystem) like [RSA cryptosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28cryptosystem%29), [Diffie–Hellman key exchange](wikipedia://en/7903) and [elliptic curve cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curve_cryptography) are based on number theory and hence depend on commutative algebraic structures.

:::

Non-commutative cryptographic protocols have been developed for solving various cryptographic problems like [key exchange](wikipedia://en/578092), [encryption](wikipedia://en/10294)-decryption, and [authentication](wikipedia://en/47967). These protocols are very similar to the corresponding protocols in the commutative case.

## Some non-commutative cryptographic protocols {#Some_non-commutative_cryptographic_protocols}

In these protocols it would be assumed that *G* is a [non-abelian](wikipedia://en/4791400) group. If *w* and *a* are elements of *G* the notation *w*<sup>*a*</sup> would indicate the element *a<sup>−1</sup>wa*.

### Protocols for key exchange {#Protocols_for_key_exchange}

#### Protocol due to Ko, Lee, et al. {#Protocol_due_to_Ko,_Lee,_et_al.}

The following protocol due to Ko, Lee, et al., establishes a common [secret key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_key) *K* for [Alice and Bob](wikipedia://en/679079).

1. An element *w* of *G* is published.
1. Two [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) *A* and *B* of *G* such that *ab* = *ba* for all *a* in *A* and *b* in *B* are published.
1. Alice chooses an element *a* from *A* and sends *w<sup>a</sup>* to Bob. Alice keeps *a* private.
1. Bob chooses an element *b* from *B* and sends *w<sup>b</sup>* to Alice. Bob keeps *b* private.
1. Alice computes *K* = (*w*<sup>*b*</sup>)<sup>a</sup> = *w*<sup>*ba*</sup>.
1. Bob computes *K**\= (*****w*<sup>*a*</sup>)<sup>*b*</sup>=*w*<sup>*ab*</sup>.***
1. Since *ab* = *ba*, *K* = *K**. Alice and Bob share the common secret key*****K*.***

#### Anshel-Anshel-Goldfeld protocol {#Anshel-Anshel-Goldfeld_protocol}

This a key exchange protocol using a non-abelian group *G*. It is significant because it does not require two commuting subgroups *A* and *B* of *G* as in the case of the protocol due to Ko, Lee, et al.

1. Elements *a*<sub>1</sub>, *a*<sub>2</sub>, . . . , *a*<sub>*k*</sub>, *b*<sub>1</sub>, *b*<sub>2</sub>, . . . , *b*<sub>*m*</sub> from *G* are selected and published.
1. Alice picks a private *x* in *G* as a [word](wikipedia://en/12792326) in *a*<sub>1</sub>, *a*<sub>2</sub>, . . . , *a*<sub>*k*</sub>; that is, *x* = *x*( *a*<sub>1</sub>, *a*<sub>2</sub>, . . . , *a*<sub>*k*</sub> ).
1. Alice sends *b*<sub>1</sub><sup>*x*</sup>, *b*<sub>2</sub><sup>*x*</sup>, . . . , *b*<sub>*m*</sub><sup>*x*</sup> to Bob.
1. Bob picks a private *y* in *G* as a [word](wikipedia://en/12792326) in *b*<sub>1</sub>, *b*<sub>2</sub>, . . . , *b*<sub>*m*</sub>; that is *y* = *y* ( *b*<sub>1</sub>, *b*<sub>2</sub>, . . . , *b*<sub>*m*</sub> ).
1. Bob sends *a*<sub>1</sub><sup>*y*</sup>, *a*<sub>2</sub><sup>*y*</sup>, . . . , *a*<sub>*k*</sub><sup>*y*</sup> to Alice.
1. Alice and Bob share the common secret key *K* = *x*<sup>−1</sup>*y*<sup>−1</sup>*xy*.
1. Alice computes *x* ( *a*<sub>1</sub><sup>*y*</sup>, *a*<sub>2</sub><sup>*y*</sup>, . . . , *a*<sub>*k*</sub><sup>*y*</sup> ) = *y*<sup>−1</sup> *xy*. Pre-multiplying it with *x*<sup>−1</sup>, Alice gets *K*.
1. Bob computes *y* ( *b*<sub>1</sub><sup>*x*</sup>, *b*<sub>2</sub><sup>*x*</sup>, . . . , *b*<sub>*m*</sub><sup>*x*</sup>) = *x*<sup>−1</sup>*yx*. Pre-multiplying it with *y*<sup>−1</sup> and then taking the inverse, Bob gets *K*.

#### Stickel's key exchange protocol {#Stickel's_key_exchange_protocol}

In the original formulation of this protocol the group used was the group of [invertible matrices](wikipedia://en/217122) over a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615).

1. Let *G* be a public non-abelian [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707).
1. Let *a*, *b* be public elements of *G* such that *ab* ≠ *ba*. Let the orders of *a* and *b* be *N* and *M* respectively.
1. Alice chooses two random numbers *n* \< *N* and *m* \< *M* and sends *u* = *a*<sup>*m*</sup>*b*<sup>*n*</sup> to Bob.
1. Bob picks two random numbers *r* \< *N* and *s* \< *M* and sends *v* = *a*<sup>*r*</sup>*b*<sup>*s*</sup> to Alice.
1. The common key shared by Alice and Bob is *K* = *a*<sup>*m* + *r*</sup>*b*<sup>*n* + *s*</sup>.
1. Alice computes the key by *K* = a<sup>*m*</sup>*vb*<sup>*n*</sup>.
1. Bob computes the key by *K* = *a*<sup>*r*</sup>*ub*<sup>*s*</sup>.

### Protocols for encryption and decryption {#Protocols_for_encryption_and_decryption}

This protocol describes how to [encrypt](wikipedia://en/10294) a secret message and then [decrypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decryption) using a non-commutative group. Let Alice want to send a secret message *m* to Bob.

1. Let *G* be a non-commutative group. Let *A* and *B* be public subgroups of *G* such that *ab* = *ba* for all *a* in *A* and *b* in *B*.
1. An element *x* from *G* is chosen and published.
1. Bob chooses a secret key *b* from *A* and publishes *z* = *x*<sup>*b*</sup> as his public key.
1. Alice chooses a random *r* from *B* and computes *t* = *z*<sup>*r*</sup>.
1. The encrypted message is *C* = (*x*<sup>*r*</sup>, *H*(*t*) $\oplus$ *m*), where *H* is some [hash function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hash_function) and $\oplus$ denotes the [XOR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XOR) operation. Alice sends *C* to Bob.
1. To decrypt *C*, Bob recovers *t* as follows: (*x*<sup>*r*</sup>)<sup>*b*</sup> = *x*<sup>*rb*</sup> = *x*<sup>*br*</sup> = (*x*<sup>*b*</sup>)<sup>*r*</sup> = *z*<sup>*r*</sup> = *t*. The plain text message send by Alice is *P* = ( *H*(*t*) $\oplus$ *m* ) $\oplus$ *H*(*t*) = *m*.

### Protocols for authentication {#Protocols_for_authentication}

Let Bob want to check whether the sender of a message is really Alice.

1. Let *G* be a non-commutative group and let *A* and *B* be subgroups of *G* such that *ab* = *ba* for all *a* in *A* and *b* in *B*.
1. An element *w* from *G* is selected and published.
1. Alice chooses a private *s* from *A* and publishes the pair ( *w*, *t* ) where *t* = *w* <sup>*s*</sup>.
1. Bob chooses an *r* from *B* and sends a challenge *w*′ = *w*<sup>*r*</sup> to Alice.
1. Alice sends the response *w*′′ = (*w*′)<sup>*s*</sup> to Bob.
1. Bob checks if *w*′′ = *t*<sup>*r*</sup>. If this true, then the identity of Alice is established.

## Security basis of the protocols {#Security_basis_of_the_protocols}

The basis for the security and strength of the various protocols presented above is the difficulty of the following two problems:

- The *[conjugacy decision problem](wikipedia://en/4942384)* (also called the *conjugacy problem*): Given two elements *u* and *v* in a group *G* determine whether there exists an element *x* in *G* such that *v* = *u*<sup>*x*</sup>, that is, such that *v* = *x*<sup>−1</sup> *ux*.
- The *conjugacy search problem*: Given two elements *u* and *v* in a group *G* find an element *x* in *G* such that *v* = *u*<sup>*x*</sup>, that is, such that *v* = *x*<sup>−1</sup> *ux*.

If no algorithm is known to solve the conjugacy search problem, then the function *x* → *u*<sup>*x*</sup> can be considered as a [one-way function](wikipedia://en/363890).

## Platform groups {#Platform_groups}

A non-commutative group that is used in a particular cryptographic protocol is called the platform group of that protocol. Only groups having certain properties can be used as the platform groups for the implementation of non-commutative cryptographic protocols. Let *G* be a group suggested as a platform group for a certain non-commutative cryptographic system. The following is a list of the properties expected of *G*.

1. The group *G* must be well-known and well-studied.
1. The [word problem](wikipedia://en/33563) in *G* should have a fast solution by a deterministic algorithm. There should be an efficiently computable "normal form" for elements of *G*.
1. It should be impossible to recover the factors *x* and *y* from the product *xy* in *G*.
1. The number of elements of length *n* in *G* should grow faster than any polynomial in *n*. (Here "length *n*" is the length of a word representing a group element.)

## Examples of platform groups {#Examples_of_platform_groups}

### Braid groups {#Braid_groups}

Let *n* be a positive integer. The braid group *B*<sub>*n*</sub> is a group generated by *x*<sub>1</sub>, *x*<sub>2</sub>, . . . , *x*<sub>*n*−1</sub> having the following presentation:

$$B_n = \left\langle x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_{n-1} \big| x_ix_j=x_jx_i \text{ if } |i-j| >1 \text{ and } x_ix_jx_i=x_jx_ix_j \text{ if } |i-j|=1 \right\rangle$$

### Thompson's group {#Thompson's_group}

Thompson's group is an infinite group *F* having the following infinite presentation:

$$F = \left\langle x_0, x_1, x_2, \ldots \big| x_k^{-1}x_nx_k=x_{n+1} \text{ for } k<n \right\rangle$$

### Grigorchuk's group {#Grigorchuk's_group}

Let *T* denote the infinite [rooted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rooted_tree) [binary tree](wikipedia://en/4321). The set *V* of vertices is the set of all finite binary sequences. Let *A*(*T*) denote the set of all automorphisms of *T*. (An automorphism of *T* permutes vertices preserving connectedness.) The Grigorchuk's group Γ is the subgroup of *A*(*T*) generated by the automorphisms *a*, *b*, *c*, *d* defined as follows:

- $$a(b_1,b_2,\ldots, b_n) = (1-b_1,b_2,\ldots, b_n)$$
- $$b(b_1,b_2,\ldots,b_n) = \begin{cases} (b_1, 1-b_2, \ldots, b_n) & \text{ if } b_1=0\\ (b_1, c(b_2,\ldots, b_n))&\text{ if } b_1=1 \end{cases}$$
- $$c(b_1,b_2,\ldots,b_n) = \begin{cases} (b_1, 1-b_2, \ldots, b_n) & \text{ if } b_1=0\\ (b_1, d(b_2,\ldots, b_n))&\text{ if } b_1=1 \end{cases}$$
- $$d(b_1,b_2,\ldots,b_n) = \begin{cases} (b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_n) & \text{ if } b_1=0\\ (b_1, b(b_2,\ldots, b_n))&\text{ if } b_1=1 \end{cases}$$

### Artin group {#Artin_group}

An Artin group *A*(Γ) is a group with the following presentation:

$$A(\Gamma) = \left\langle a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n | \mu_{ij} = \mu_{ji} \text{ for } 1 \le i < j \le n \right\rangle$$

where $\mu_{ij} = a_ia_ja_i\ldots$ ($m_{ij}$ factors) and $m_{ij} = m_{ji}$.

### Matrix groups {#Matrix_groups}

Let *F* be a finite field. Groups of matrices over *F* have been used as the platform groups of certain non-commutative cryptographic protocols.
