# Rule of three (statistics)

*Image: Rule of three.svg.* Comparison of the rule of three to the exact binomial one-sided confidence interval with no positive samples

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rule of three"}

In [statistical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_analysis), the **rule of three** states that if a certain event did not occur in a sample with $n$ [subjects](wikipedia://en/9541), the interval from 0 to 3/$n$ is a 95% [confidence interval](wikipedia://en/280911) for the rate of occurrences in the [population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_%28statistics%29). When $n$ is greater than 30, this is a good approximation of results from more sensitive tests. For example, a pain-relief drug is tested on 1500 [human subjects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_subjects_research), and no [adverse event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adverse_event) is recorded. From the rule of three, it can be concluded with 95% confidence that fewer than 1 person in 500 (or 3/1500) will experience an adverse event. By symmetry, for only successes, the 95% confidence interval is \[1 − 3/$n$,1\].

:::

The rule is useful in the interpretation of [clinical trials](wikipedia://en/241717) generally, particularly in [phase II](wikipedia://en/34382035#Phase_II) and phase III where often there are limitations in duration or [statistical power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_power). The rule of three applies well beyond medical research, to any trial done $n$ times. If 300 parachutes are randomly tested and all open successfully, then it is concluded with 95% confidence that fewer than 1 in 100 parachutes with the same characteristics (3/300) will fail.[^o2]

## Derivation {#Derivation}

A 95% [confidence interval](wikipedia://en/280911) is sought for the probability *p* of an event occurring for any randomly selected single individual in a population, given that it has not been observed to occur in *n* [Bernoulli trials](wikipedia://en/102653). Denoting the number of events by *X*, we therefore wish to find the values of the parameter *p* of a [binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876) that give Pr(*X* = 0) ≤ 0.05. The rule can then be derived[^o4] either from the [Poisson approximation to the binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876#Poisson_approximation), or from the formula (1 − *p*)<sup>*n*</sup> for the probability of zero events in the binomial distribution. In the latter case, the edge of the confidence interval is given by Pr(*X* = 0) = 0.05 and hence (1 − *p*)<sup>*n*</sup> = 0.05 so *n* [ln](wikipedia://en/21476)(1 – *p*) = ln .05 ≈ −2.996. Rounding the latter to −3 and using the approximation, for *p* close to 0, that ln(1 − *p*) ≈ −*p* (Taylor's formula), we obtain the interval's boundary 3/*n*.

By a similar argument, the numerator values of 3.51, 4.61, and 5.3 may be used for the 97%, 99%, and 99.5% confidence intervals, respectively, and in general the upper end of the confidence interval can be given as $\frac{-\ln(\alpha)}{n}$, where $1-\alpha$ is the desired confidence level.

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Extension"}

## Extension {#Extension}

The [Vysochanskij–Petunin inequality](wikipedia://en/435813) shows that the rule of three holds for [unimodal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unimodal_function) distributions with finite [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) beyond just the binomial distribution, and gives a way to change the factor 3 if a different confidence is desired. [Chebyshev's inequality](wikipedia://en/156533) removes the assumption of unimodality at the price of a higher multiplier (about 4.5 for 95% confidence). [Cantelli's inequality](wikipedia://en/36703918) is the one-tailed version of Chebyshev's inequality.

:::
