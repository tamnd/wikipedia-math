# Integral probability metric

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Integral probability metric"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), **integral probability metrics** are types of [distance functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) between [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543), defined by how well a class of functions can distinguish the two distributions. Many important [statistical distances](wikipedia://en/5187620) are integral probability metrics, including the [Wasserstein-1 distance](wikipedia://en/6704603) and the [total variation distance](wikipedia://en/683570). In addition to theoretical importance, integral probability metrics are widely used in areas of [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) and [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488).

:::

The name "integral probability metric" was given by German statistician Alfred Müller;[^o2] the distances had also previously been called "metrics with a $\zeta$-structure."[^o3]

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Integral probability metrics (IPMs) are distances on the space of distributions over a set $\mathcal X$, defined by a class $\mathcal{F}$ of real-valued functions on $\mathcal{X}$ as

$$D_{\mathcal{F}}(P, Q) = \sup_{f \in \mathcal F} \big| \mathbb E_{X \sim P} f(X) - \mathbb E_{Y \sim Q} f(Y) \big| = \sup_{f \in \mathcal F} \big| P f - Q f \big|;$$

here the notation $P f$ refers to the expectation of $f$ under the distribution $P$. The absolute value in the definition is unnecessary, and often omitted, for the usual case where for every $f \in \mathcal F$ its negation $-f$ is also in $\mathcal F$.

The functions $f$ being optimized over are sometimes called "critic" functions;[^o6] if a particular $f^* \in \mathcal F$ achieves the supremum, it is often termed a "witness function"[^o7] (it "witnesses" the difference in the distributions). These functions try to have large values for samples from $P$ and small (likely negative) values for samples from $Q$; this can be thought of as a weaker version of [classifers](wikipedia://en/2985453), and indeed IPMs can be interpreted as the optimal [risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_risk) of a particular classifier.

The choice of $\mathcal F$ determines the particular distance; more than one $\mathcal F$ can generate the same distance.[^o2]

For any choice of $\mathcal F$, $D_{\mathcal F}$ satisfies all the definitions of a metric except that we may have $D_{\mathcal F}(P, Q) = 0$ for some $P \ne Q$; this is variously termed a "[pseudometric](wikipedia://en/20018#pseudometric)" or a "semimetric" depending on the community. For instance, using the class $\mathcal F = \{ x \mapsto 0 \}$ which only contains the zero function, $D_{\mathcal F}(P, Q)$ is identically zero. $D_{\mathcal F}$ is a metric if and only if $\mathcal F$ [separates points](wikipedia://en/13972359) on the space of probability distributions, i.e. for any $P \ne Q$ there is some $f \in \mathcal F$ such that $P f \ne Q f$;[^o2] most, but not all, common particular cases satisfy this property.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

All of these examples are metrics except when noted otherwise.

- The [Wasserstein-1 distance](wikipedia://en/6704603) (also called [earth mover's distance](wikipedia://en/11915675)), via its [dual representation](wikipedia://en/6704603#Dual_representation_of_W1), has $\mathcal F$ the set of 1-[Lipschitz](wikipedia://en/51731) functions.
- The related Dudley metric is generated by the set of *bounded* 1-Lipschitz functions.
- The [total variation distance](wikipedia://en/683570) can be generated by $\mathcal F = \{ f : \mathcal X \to \{0, 1\} \}$, so that $\mathcal F$ is a set of [indicator functions](wikipedia://en/240790) for any event, or by the larger class $\mathcal F = \{ f : \mathcal X \to [0, 1] \}$.
- The closely related [Radon metric](wikipedia://en/521933#Metric_space_structure) is generated by *continuous* functions bounded in $[-1, 1]$. Total variation and the Radon metric coincide (up to scaling) on [Polish spaces](wikipedia://en/396622). [^o10]
- The Kolmogorov metric used in the [Kolmogorov-Smirnov test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov-Smirnov_test) has a function class of indicator functions, $\mathcal F = \{ 1_{(-\infty, t]} : t \in \mathbb R \}$.
- The [kernel maximum mean discrepancy (MMD)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_embedding_of_distributions#Measuring_distance_between_distributions) has $\mathcal F$ the [unit ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_ball) in a [reproducing kernel Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/651196). This distance is particularly easy to estimate from samples, requiring no optimization; it is a proper metric exactly when the underlying kernel is *characteristic.*[^o11]
- The [energy distance](wikipedia://en/30316300), as a special case of the maximum mean discrepancy,[^o12] is generated by the unit ball in a *particular* reproducing kernel Hilbert space.
- Defining $\mathcal F$ by functions with a bounded [Sobolev norm](wikipedia://en/611964) gives a useful distance for [generative modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_artificial_intelligence), among other applications.[^o13]
- Functions with bounded [Besov norm](wikipedia://en/18542594) generalize many other forms of IPM and are amenable to theoretical analysis.[^o14][^o15]
- Many variants of [generative adversarial networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_adversarial_network) and [classifer](wikipedia://en/2985453)-based [two-sample tests](wikipedia://en/60016459)[^o16][^o17] use a "neural net distance"[^o18][^o19] where $\mathcal F$ is a class of [neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_neural_network); these are not metrics for typical fixed-size networks, but could be for other classifiers. For [Wasserstein GANs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasserstein_GAN) in particular, it has been argued that analysis in terms of this distance and not the Wasserstein they approximate is very important to the behavior of these models.[^o18][^o20][^o21]

:::

## Relationship to $f$-divergences {#Relationship_to_-divergences}

The [$f$-divergences](wikipedia://en/15224289) are probably the best-known way to measure dissimilarity of probability distributions. It has been shown that the only functions which are both IPMs and $f$-divergences are of the form $c \, \operatorname{TV}(P, Q)$, where $c \in [0, \infty]$ and $\operatorname{TV}$ is the total variation distance between distributions.

One major difference between $f$-divergences and most IPMs is that when $P$ and $Q$ have disjoint support, all $f$-divergences take on a constant value;[^o23] by contrast, IPMs where functions in $\mathcal F$ are "smooth" can give "partial credit." For instance, consider the sequence $\delta_{1/n}$ of [Dirac measures](wikipedia://en/1019002) at $1/n$; this sequence [converges in distribution](wikipedia://en/50723#convergence_in_distribution) to $\delta_0$, and many IPMs satisfy $D_{\mathcal F}(\delta_{1/n}, \delta_0) \to 0$, but no nonzero $f$-divergence can satisfy this. That is, many IPMs are [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122#Continuous_functions_between_topological_spaces) in [weaker topologies](wikipedia://en/393069) than $f$-divergences. This property is sometimes of substantial importance,[^o24] although other options also exist, such as considering $f$-divergences between distributions [convolved](wikipedia://en/32251002) with continuous noise[^o24][^o25] or informal convolutions between f-divergences and integral probability metrics.[^o26][^o27]

## Estimation from samples {#Estimation_from_samples}

Because IPM values between discrete distributions are often sensible, it is often reasonable to estimate $D_{\mathcal F}(P, Q)$ using a simple "plug-in" estimator: $D_{\mathcal F}(\hat P, \hat Q)$ where $\hat P$ and $\hat Q$ are [empirical measures](wikipedia://en/5647670) of sample sets. These empirical distances can be computed exactly for some classes $\mathcal F$;[^o29] estimation quality varies depending on the distance, but can be [minimax-optimal](wikipedia://en/16976635) in certain settings.[^o19][^o30][^o31]

When exact maximization is not available or too expensive, another commonly used scheme is to [divide the samples](wikipedia://en/1514392) into "training" sets (with empirical measures $\hat P_\mathit{train}$ and $\hat Q_\mathit{train}$) and "test" sets ($\hat P_\mathit{test}$ and $\hat Q_\mathit{test}$), find $\hat f$ approximately maximizing $\big| \hat P_\mathit{train} f - \hat Q_\mathit{train} f \big|$, then use $\big| \hat P_\mathit{test} \hat f - \hat Q_\mathit{test} \hat f \big|$ as an estimate.[^o32][^o17][^o33][^o34] This estimator can possibly be [consistent](wikipedia://en/1497569), but has a negative bias. In fact, no [unbiased estimator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unbiased_estimator) can exist for any IPM, although there is for instance an unbiased estimator of the *squared* maximum mean discrepancy.
