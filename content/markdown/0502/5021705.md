# Conway polyhedron notation

*Image: Conway relational chart.png.* This example chart shows how 11 new forms can be derived from the cube using 3 operations. The new polyhedra are shown as maps on the surface of the cube so the topological changes are more apparent. Vertices are marked in all forms with circles.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Conway polyhedron notation"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) and [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), **Conway polyhedron notation**, invented by [John Horton Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) and promoted by [George W. Hart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Hart), is used to describe [polyhedra](wikipedia://en/23470) based on a seed polyhedron modified by various prefix [operations](wikipedia://en/4140245).[^o2]

:::

Conway and Hart extended the idea of using operators, like [truncation](wikipedia://en/4224429) as defined by [Kepler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler), to build related polyhedra of the same symmetry. For example, $tC$ represents a [truncated cube](wikipedia://en/280529), and $taC$, parsed as $t(aC)$, is ([topologically](wikipedia://en/29954)) a [truncated cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/285879). The simplest operator [dual](wikipedia://en/8815) swaps [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) and [face](wikipedia://en/149926) elements; e.g., a dual [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) is an [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635): $dC = O$. Applied in a series, these operators allow many [higher order polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_order_polyhedra) to be generated. Conway defined the operators $a$ (ambo), $b$ ([bevel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bevel)), $d$ ([dual](wikipedia://en/8815)), $e$ (expand), $g$ (gyro), $j$ (join), $k$ (kis), $m$ (meta), $o$ (ortho), $s$ ([snub](wikipedia://en/9428917)), and $t$ ([truncate](wikipedia://en/4224429)), while Hart added $r$ ([reflect](wikipedia://en/295829)) and $p$ (propellor).[^o4] Later implementations named further operators, sometimes referred to as "extended" operators.[^o5][^o6] Conway's basic operations are sufficient to generate the [Archimedean](wikipedia://en/1847) and [Catalan solids](wikipedia://en/564393) from the [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905). Some basic operations can be made as composites of others: for instance, ambo applied twice is the expand operation ($aa = e$), while a truncation after ambo produces bevel ($ta = b$).

Polyhedra can be studied topologically, in terms of how their vertices, edges, and faces connect together, or geometrically, in terms of the placement of those elements in space. Different implementations of these operators may create polyhedra that are geometrically different but topologically equivalent. These topologically equivalent polyhedra can be thought of as one of many [embeddings](wikipedia://en/8149170) of a [polyhedral graph](wikipedia://en/24656270) on the sphere. Unless otherwise specified, in this article (and in the literature on Conway operators in general) topology is the primary concern. Polyhedra with [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) 0 (i.e. topologically equivalent to a sphere) are often put into [canonical form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_polyhedron) to avoid ambiguity.

## Operators {#Operators}

In Conway's notation, operations on polyhedra are applied like functions, from right to left. For example, a [cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/6280) is an *ambo cube*,[^o8] i.e. $a(C) = aC$, and a [truncated cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/285879) is $t(a(C)) = t(aC) = taC$. Repeated application of an operator can be denoted with an exponent: *j<sup>2</sup>* = *o*. In general, Conway operators are not [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative).

Individual operators can be visualized in terms of [fundamental domains](wikipedia://en/469365) (or chambers), as below. Each right triangle is a [fundamental domain](wikipedia://en/469365). Each white chamber is a rotated version of the others, and so is each colored chamber. For [achiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achiral) operators, the colored chambers are a reflection of the white chambers, and all are transitive. In group terms, achiral operators correspond to [dihedral groups](wikipedia://en/100267) $D_{n}$ where *n* is the number of sides of a face, while chiral operators correspond to [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) $C_{n}$ lacking the reflective symmetry of the dihedral groups. Achiral and [chiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiral) operators are also called local symmetry-preserving operations (LSP) and local operations that preserve orientation-preserving symmetries (LOPSP), respectively.[^o9][^o10][^o11] LSPs should be understood as local operations that preserve symmetry, not operations that preserve local symmetry. Again, these are symmetries in a topological sense, not a geometric sense: the exact angles and edge lengths may differ.

Hart introduced the reflection operator *r*, that gives the mirror image of the polyhedron.[^o8] This is not strictly a LOPSP, since it does not preserve orientation: it reverses it, by exchanging white and red chambers. *r* has no effect on achiral polyhedra aside from orientation, and *rr = S* returns the original polyhedron. An overline can be used to indicate the other chiral form of an operator: $\overline{s}$ = *rsr*.

An operation is irreducible if it cannot be expressed as a composition of operators aside from *d* and *r*. The majority of Conway's original operators are irreducible: the exceptions are *e*, *b*, *o*, and *m*.

### Matrix representation {#Matrix_representation}

::: {#o14 .table cue=table}

| x | $\begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ 0 & d & 0 \\ a' & b' & c' \end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{M}_x$ |
|---|---|
| xd | $\begin{bmatrix} c & b & a \\ 0 & d & 0 \\ c' & b' & a' \end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{M}_x\mathbf{M}_d$ |
| dx | $\begin{bmatrix} a' & b' & c' \\ 0 & d & 0 \\ a & b & c \end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{M}_d\mathbf{M}_x$ |
| dxd | $\begin{bmatrix} c' & b' & a' \\ 0 & d & 0 \\ c & b & a \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{M}_d\mathbf{M}_x\mathbf{M}_d$ |

:::

The relationship between the number of vertices, edges, and faces of the seed and the polyhedron created by the operations listed in this article can be expressed as a matrix $\mathbf{M}_x$. When *x* is the operator, $v,e,f$ are the vertices, edges, and faces of the seed (respectively), and $v',e',f'$ are the vertices, edges, and faces of the result, then

$$\mathbf{M}_x \begin{bmatrix} v \\ e \\ f \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} v' \\ e' \\ f' \end{bmatrix}.$$

The matrix for the composition of two operators is just the product of the matrixes for the two operators. Distinct operators may have the same matrix, for example, *p* and *l*. The edge count of the result is an integer multiple *d* of that of the seed: this is called the inflation rate, or the edge factor.[^o9]

The simplest operators, the [identity operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_operator) *S* and the [dual operator](wikipedia://en/8815) *d*, have simple matrix forms:

$\mathbf{M}_S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{I}_3$, $\mathbf{M}_d = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$

Two dual operators cancel out; *dd* = *S*, and the square of $\mathbf{M}_d$ is the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718). When applied to other operators, the dual operator corresponds to horizontal and vertical reflections of the matrix. Operators can be grouped into groups of four (or fewer if some forms are the same) by identifying the operators *x*, *xd* (operator of dual), *dx* (dual of operator), and *dxd* (conjugate of operator). In this article, only the matrix for *x* is given, since the others are simple reflections.

### Number of operators {#Number_of_operators}

The number of LSPs for each inflation rate is $2, 2, 4, 6, 6, 20, 28, 58, 82, \cdots$ starting with inflation rate 1. However, not all LSPs necessarily produce a polyhedron whose edges and vertices form a [3-connected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph), and as a consequence of [Steinitz's theorem](wikipedia://en/19762817) do not necessarily produce a convex polyhedron from a convex seed. The number of 3-connected LSPs for each inflation rate is $2, 2, 4, 6, 4, 20, 20, 54, 64, \cdots$.[^o10] The number of 3-connected LOPSPs (chiral) for each inflation rate is $0,0,0,0,4,0,10,8,38, \cdots$.[^o16]

## Seeds {#Seeds}

Any polyhedron can serve as a seed, as long as the operations can be executed on it. Common seeds have been assigned a letter. The [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) are represented by the first letter of their name ([**T**etrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606), [**O**ctahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635), [**C**ube](wikipedia://en/6285), [**I**cosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084), [**D**odecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407)); the [**p**risms](wikipedia://en/283007) (**P**<sub>*n*</sub>) for *n*-gonal forms; [**a**ntiprisms](wikipedia://en/1851) (**A**<sub>*n*</sub>); [c**u**polae](wikipedia://en/689743) (**U**<sub>*n*</sub>); [anticupolae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticupola) (**V**<sub>*n*</sub>); and [p**y**ramids](wikipedia://en/3307045) (**Y**<sub>*n*</sub>). Any [**J**ohnson solid](wikipedia://en/16034) can be referenced as **J**<sub>*n*</sub>, for *n*=1..92.

All of the five Platonic solids can be generated from prismatic generators with zero to two operators:[^o25]

- **[Triangular pyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangular_pyramid)**: *Y*<sub>3</sub> (A tetrahedron is a special pyramid)
  - *[T](wikipedia://en/30606)* = *Y*<sub>3</sub>
  - *[O](wikipedia://en/80177635)* = *aT* (ambo tetrahedron)
  - *[C](wikipedia://en/6285)* = *jT* (join tetrahedron)
  - *[I](wikipedia://en/14968)* = *sT* (snub tetrahedron)
  - *[D](wikipedia://en/7149361)* = *gT* (gyro tetrahedron)
- **[Triangular antiprism](wikipedia://en/1851)**: *A*<sub>3</sub> (An octahedron is a special antiprism)
  - *O* = *A*<sub>3</sub>
  - *C* = *dA*<sub>3</sub>
- **[Square prism](wikipedia://en/283007)**: *P*<sub>4</sub> (A cube is a special prism)
  - *C* = *P*<sub>4</sub>
- **[Pentagonal antiprism](wikipedia://en/2860091)**: *A*<sub>5</sub>
  - *I* = *k*<sub>5</sub>*A*<sub>5</sub> (A special [gyroelongated dipyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyroelongated_dipyramid))
  - *D* = *t*<sub>5</sub>*dA*<sub>5</sub> (A special [truncated trapezohedron](wikipedia://en/3521844))

The regular Euclidean tilings can also be used as seeds:

- *Q* = Quadrille = [Square tiling](wikipedia://en/2863809)
- *H* = Hextille = [Hexagonal tiling](wikipedia://en/2863719) = *dΔ*
- *Δ* = Deltille = [Triangular tiling](wikipedia://en/2863858) = *dH*

## Indexed extended operations {#Indexed_extended_operations}

A number of operators can be grouped together by some criteria, or have their behavior modified by an index.[^o5] These are written as an operator with a subscript: *x<sub>n</sub>*.

### Augmentation {#Augmentation}

[Augmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augmentation_%28geometry%29) operations retain original edges. They may be applied to any independent subset of faces, or may be converted into a *join*-form by removing the original edges. Conway notation supports an optional index to these operators: 0 for the join-form, or 3 or higher for how many sides affected faces have. For example, *k*<sub>4</sub>*Y*<sub>4</sub>=O: taking a square-based pyramid and gluing another pyramid to the square base gives an octahedron.

The truncate operator *t* also has an index form *t<sub>n</sub>*, indicating that only vertices of a certain degree are truncated. It is equivalent to *dk<sub>n</sub>d*.

Some of the extended operators can be created in special cases with *k<sub>n</sub>* and *t<sub>n</sub>* operators. For example, a [chamfered cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamfered_cube), *cC*, can be constructed as *t*<sub>4</sub>*daC*, as a [rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631), *daC* or *jC*, with its degree-4 vertices truncated. A lofted cube, *lC* is the same as *t*<sub>4</sub>*kC*. A quinto-dodecahedron, *qD* can be constructed as *t*<sub>5</sub>*daaD* or *t*<sub>5</sub>*deD* or *t*<sub>5</sub>*oD*, a [deltoidal hexecontahedron](wikipedia://en/724753), *deD* or *oD*, with its degree-5 vertices truncated.

### Meta/Bevel {#Meta/Bevel}

Meta adds vertices at the center and along the edges, while bevel adds faces at the center, seed vertices, and along the edges. The index is how many vertices or faces are added along the edges. Meta (in its non-indexed form) is also called [cantitruncation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantitruncation) or [omnitruncation](wikipedia://en/7083690). Note that 0 here does not mean the same as for augmentation operations: it means zero vertices (or faces) are added along the edges.[^o5]

### Medial {#Medial}

Medial is like meta, except it does not add edges from the center to each seed vertex. The index 1 form is identical to Conway's ortho and expand operators: expand is also called [cantellation](wikipedia://en/6317514) and [expansion](wikipedia://en/6341070). ### Goldberg-Coxeter {#Goldberg-Coxeter}

The Goldberg-Coxeter (GC) Conway operators are two infinite families of operators that are an extension of the [Goldberg-Coxeter construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldberg-Coxeter_construction).[^o37][^o38] The GC construction can be thought of as taking a triangular section of a triangular lattice, or a square section of a square lattice, and laying that over each face of the polyhedron. This construction can be extended to any face by identifying the chambers of the triangle or square (the "master polygon").[^o9] Operators in the triangular family can be used to produce the [Goldberg polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldberg_polyhedra) and [geodesic polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_polyhedra): see [List of geodesic polyhedra and Goldberg polyhedra](wikipedia://en/53264013) for formulas.

The two families are the triangular GC family, *c<sub>a,b</sub>* and *u<sub>a,b</sub>*, and the quadrilateral GC family, *e<sub>a,b</sub>* and *o<sub>a,b</sub>*. Both the GC families are indexed by two integers $a \ge 1$ and $b \ge 0$. They possess many nice qualities:

- The indexes of the families have a relationship with certain [Euclidean domains](wikipedia://en/10376) over the complex numbers: the [Eisenstein integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eisenstein_integers) for the triangular GC family, and the [Gaussian integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integers) for the quadrilateral GC family.
- Operators in the *x* and *dxd* columns within the same family commute with each other.

The operators are divided into three classes (examples are written in terms of *c* but apply to all 4 operators):

- Class I: $b=0$. Achiral, preserves original edges. Can be written with the zero index suppressed, e.g. *c*<sub>*a*,0</sub> = *c<sub>a</sub>*.
- Class II: $a=b$. Also achiral. Can be decomposed as *c<sub>a,a</sub>* = *c<sub>a</sub>c*<sub>1,1</sub>
- Class III: All other operators. These are chiral, and *c<sub>a,b</sub>* and *c<sub>b,a</sub>* are the chiral pairs of each other.

Of the original Conway operations, the only ones that do not fall into the GC family are *g* and *s* (gyro and snub). Meta and bevel (*m* and *b*) can be expressed in terms of one operator from the triangular family and one from the quadrilateral family.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Archimedean and Catalan solids {#Archimedean_and_Catalan_solids}

Conway's original set of operators can create all of the [Archimedean solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedean_solids) and [Catalan solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan_solids), using the [Platonic solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_solids) as seeds. (Note that the *r* operator is not necessary to create both chiral forms.)

### Composite operators {#Composite_operators}

The [truncated icosahedron](wikipedia://en/31282), *tI*, can be used as a seed to create some more visually-pleasing polyhedra, although these are neither [vertex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex_transitive) nor [face-transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face-transitive).

### On the plane {#On_the_plane}

Each of the [convex uniform tilings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_convex_uniform_tilings) and their duals can be created by applying Conway operators to the [regular tilings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_tilings) *Q*, *H*, and *Δ*.

### On a torus {#On_a_torus}

Conway operators can also be applied to [toroidal polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toroidal_polyhedra) and polyhedra with multiple holes.
