# Edge (geometry)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Edge"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), an **edge** is a particular type of [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) joining two [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) in a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621), [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470), or higher-dimensional [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471).[^o2] In a polygon, an edge is a line segment on the boundary,[^o3] and is often called a **polygon side**. In a polyhedron or more generally a polytope, an edge is a line segment where two [faces](wikipedia://en/149926) (or polyhedron sides) meet.[^o4] A segment joining two vertices while passing through the interior or exterior is not an edge but instead is called a [diagonal](wikipedia://en/349251).

:::

An *edge* may also be an infinite [line](wikipedia://en/946975) separating two [half-planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-plane).[^o5] The *sides* of a [plane angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_angle) are semi-infinite [half-lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-line_%28geometry%29) (or rays).[^n1]

## Relation to edges in graphs {#Relation_to_edges_in_graphs}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), an [edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_%28graph_theory%29) is an abstract object connecting two [graph vertices](wikipedia://en/638899), unlike polygon and polyhedron edges which have a concrete geometric representation as a line segment. However, any polyhedron can be represented by its [skeleton](wikipedia://en/2977910) or edge-skeleton, a graph whose vertices are the geometric vertices of the polyhedron and whose edges correspond to the geometric edges.[^o8] Conversely, the graphs that are skeletons of three-dimensional polyhedra can be characterized by [Steinitz's theorem](wikipedia://en/19762817) as being exactly the [3-vertex-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph) [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314).[^o9]

## Number of edges in a polyhedron {#Number_of_edges_in_a_polyhedron}

Any [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron)'s surface has [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420)

$$V - E + F = 2,$$

where *V* is the number of [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417), *E* is the number of edges, and *F* is the number of [faces](wikipedia://en/149926). This equation is known as [Euler's polyhedron formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_polyhedron_formula). Thus the number of edges is 2 less than the sum of the numbers of vertices and faces. For example, a [cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube_%28geometry%29) has 8 vertices and 6 faces, and hence 12 edges.

## Incidences with other faces {#Incidences_with_other_faces}

In a polygon, two edges meet at each [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417); more generally, by [Balinski's theorem](wikipedia://en/24732291), at least *d* edges meet at every vertex of a *d*-dimensional convex polytope.[^o12] Similarly, in a polyhedron, exactly two two-dimensional faces meet at every edge,[^o13] while in higher dimensional polytopes three or more two-dimensional faces meet at every edge.

## Alternative terminology {#Alternative_terminology}

In the theory of high-dimensional [convex polytopes](wikipedia://en/1817885), a *[facet](wikipedia://en/7186647)* or *side* of a *d*-dimensional [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) is one of its (*d* − 1)-dimensional features, a *[ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ridge_%28geometry%29)* is a (*d* − 2)-dimensional feature, and a *[peak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_%28geometry%29)* is a (*d* − 3)-dimensional feature. Thus, the edges of a polygon are its facets, the edges of a 3-dimensional [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron) are its ridges, and the edges of a [4-dimensional polytope](wikipedia://en/24979) are its peaks.[^o15]
