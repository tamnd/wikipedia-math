# Regular singular point

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Regular singular point"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the theory of [ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/32742753) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) $\mathbb{C}$, the points of $\mathbb{C}$ are classified into *ordinary points*, at which the equation's coefficients are [analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478), and *singular points*, at which some coefficient has a [singularity](wikipedia://en/54240). Then amongst singular points, an important distinction is made between a **regular singular point**, where the growth of solutions is bounded (in any small sector) by an [algebraic function](wikipedia://en/974169), and an **irregular singular point**, where the full [solution set](wikipedia://en/567840) requires functions with higher growth rates. This distinction occurs, for example, between the [hypergeometric equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypergeometric_equation), with three regular singular points, and the [Bessel equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessel_equation) which is in a sense a [limiting case](wikipedia://en/40400729), but where the analytic properties are substantially different.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definitions"}

## Formal definitions {#Formal_definitions}

More precisely, consider an ordinary [linear differential equation](wikipedia://en/379868) of $n$-th order

$$f^{(n)}(z) + \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} p_i(z) f^{(i)} (z) = 0$$

with $p_{i}(z)$ [meromorphic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meromorphic_functions).

The equation should be studied on the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799) to include the [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139) as a possible singular point. A [Möbius transformation](wikipedia://en/314493) may be applied to move ∞ into the finite part of the complex plane if required, see example on Bessel differential equation below.

Then the [Frobenius method](wikipedia://en/998446) based on the [indicial equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indicial_equation) may be applied to find possible solutions that are [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) times complex powers $(z - a)^{r}$ near any given $a$ in the complex plane where $r$ need not be an integer; this function may exist, therefore, only thanks to a [branch cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branch_cut) extending out from $a$, or on a [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181) of some [punctured disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punctured_disc) around $a$. This presents no difficulty for $a$ an ordinary point ([Lazarus Fuchs](wikipedia://en/2817087) 1866). When $a$ is a **regular singular point**, which by definition means that

$$p_{n-i}(z)$$

has a [pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) of order at most $i$ at $a$, the [Frobenius method](wikipedia://en/998446) also can be made to work and provide $n$ independent solutions near $a$.

Otherwise the point $a$ is an **irregular singularity**. In that case the [monodromy group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monodromy_group) relating solutions by [analytic continuation](wikipedia://en/80842) has less to say in general, and the solutions are harder to study, except in terms of their asymptotic expansions. The irregularity of an irregular singularity is measured by the [Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) rank.[^o4]

The regularity condition is a kind of [Newton polygon](wikipedia://en/742477) condition, in the sense that the allowed poles are in a region, when plotted against $i$, bounded by a line at 45° to the axes.

An [ordinary differential equation](wikipedia://en/32742753) whose only singular points, including the point at infinity, are regular singular points is called a **[Fuchsian](wikipedia://en/57482552)** ordinary differential equation.

:::

## Examples for second order differential equations {#Examples_for_second_order_differential_equations}

In this case the equation above is reduced to:

$$f''(x) + p_1(x) f'(x) + p_0(x) f(x) = 0.$$

One distinguishes the following cases:

- Point $a$ is an **ordinary point** when functions $p_{1}(x)$ and $p_{0}(x)$ are analytic at $x = a$.
- Point $a$ is a **regular singular point** if $p_{1}(x)$ has a pole up to order 1 at $x = a$ and $p_{0}$ has a pole of order up to 2 at $x = a$.
- Otherwise point $a$ is an **irregular singular point**.

We can check whether there is an irregular singular point at infinity by using the substitution $w = 1/x$ and the relations:

$$\frac{df}{dx}=-w^2\frac{df}{dw}$$

$$\frac{d^2f}{dx^2}=w^4\frac{d^2f}{dw^2}+2w^3\frac{df}{dw}$$

We can thus transform the equation to an equation in $w$, and check what happens at $w = 0$. If $p_1(x)$ and $p_2(x)$ are quotients of polynomials, then there will be an irregular singular point at infinite *x* unless the polynomial in the denominator of $p_1(x)$ is of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) at least one more than the degree of its numerator and the denominator of $p_2(x)$ is of degree at least two more than the degree of its numerator.

Listed below are several examples from ordinary differential equations from [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416) that have singular points and known solutions.

### Bessel differential equation {#Bessel_differential_equation}

This is an ordinary differential equation of second order. It is found in the solution to [Laplace's equation](wikipedia://en/36941) in [cylindrical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylindrical_coordinates):

$$x^2 \frac{d^2 f}{dx^2} + x \frac{df}{dx} + (x^2 - \alpha^2)f = 0$$

for an arbitrary real or [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $\alpha$ (the *order* of the [Bessel function](wikipedia://en/4700)). The most common and important special case is where $\alpha$ is an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $n$.

Dividing this equation by *x*<sup>2</sup> gives:

$$\frac{d^2 f}{dx^2} + \frac{1} {x} \frac{df}{dx} + \left (1 - \frac {\alpha^2} {x^2} \right )f = 0.$$

In this case $p_{1}(x) = 1/x$ has a pole of first order at $x = 0$. When $\alpha \ne 0$, $p_{0}(x) = (1 - \alpha^{2}/x^{2})$ has a pole of second order at $x = 0$. Thus this equation has a regular singularity at 0.

To see what happens when $x \to \infty$ one has to use a [Möbius transformation](wikipedia://en/314493), for example $x = 1 / w$. After performing the algebra:

$$\frac{d^2 f}{d w^2} + \frac{1}{w} \frac{df}{dw} + \left[ \frac{1}{w^4} - \frac{\alpha ^2}{w^2} \right ] f= 0$$

Now at $w = 0$,

$$p_1(w) = \frac{1}{w}$$

has a pole of first order, but

$$p_0(w) = \frac {1} {w^4} - \frac {\alpha ^2} {w^2}$$

has a pole of fourth order. Thus, this equation has an irregular singularity at $w = 0$ corresponding to *x* at ∞.

### Legendre differential equation {#Legendre_differential_equation}

This is an ordinary differential equation of second order. It is found in the solution of [Laplace's equation](wikipedia://en/36941) in [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates):

$$\frac{d}{dx} \left[ (1-x^2) \frac{d}{dx} f \right] + \ell(\ell+1)f = 0.$$

Opening the square bracket gives:

$$\left(1-x^2\right){d^2 f \over dx^2} -2x {df \over dx } + \ell(\ell+1)f = 0.$$

And dividing by $(1 - x^{2})$:

$$\frac{d^2 f}{dx^2} - \frac{2x}{1-x^2} \frac{df}{dx} + \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{1-x^2} f = 0.$$

This differential equation has regular singular points at ±1 and ∞.

### Hermite differential equation {#Hermite_differential_equation}

One encounters this ordinary second order differential equation in solving the one-dimensional time independent [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874)

$$E\psi = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac {d^2 \psi} {d x^2} + V(x)\psi$$

for a [harmonic oscillator](wikipedia://en/50719). In this case the potential energy *V*(*x*) is:

$$V(x) = \frac{1}{2} m \omega^2 x^2.$$

This leads to the following ordinary second order differential equation:

$$\frac{d^2 f}{dx^2} - 2 x \frac{df}{dx} + \lambda f = 0.$$

This differential equation has an irregular singularity at ∞. Its solutions are [Hermite polynomials](wikipedia://en/195984).

### Hypergeometric equation {#Hypergeometric_equation}

The equation may be defined as

$$z(1-z)\frac {d^2f}{dz^2} + \left[c-(a+b+1)z \right] \frac {df}{dz} - abf = 0.$$

Dividing both sides by $z(1 - z)$ gives:

$$\frac {d^2f}{dz^2} + \frac{c-(a+b+1)z } {z(1-z)} \frac {df}{dz} - \frac {ab} {z(1-z)} f = 0.$$

This differential equation has regular singular points at 0, 1 and ∞. A solution is the [hypergeometric function](wikipedia://en/2055223).
