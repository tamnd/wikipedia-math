# Rademacher's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rademacher's theorem"}

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), **Rademacher's theorem**, named after [Hans Rademacher](wikipedia://en/1864916), states the following: If $U$ is an [open subset](wikipedia://en/39358) of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ and $f: U \to \mathbf{R}^{m}$ is [Lipschitz continuous](wikipedia://en/51731), then $f$ is differentiable [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688) in $U$; that is, the points in $U$ at which $f$ is *not* differentiable form a set of [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) zero. Differentiability here refers to infinitesimal approximability by a linear map, which in particular asserts the existence of the coordinate-wise partial derivatives.

:::

::: {#o3 .proof cue=heading name="Sketch of proof"}

## Sketch of proof {#Sketch_of_proof}

The one-dimensional case of Rademacher's theorem is a standard result in introductory texts on measure-theoretic analysis. In this context, it is natural to prove the more general statement that any single-variable function of [bounded variation](wikipedia://en/330673) is differentiable almost everywhere. (This one-dimensional generalization of Rademacher's theorem fails to extend to higher dimensions.)

One of the standard proofs of the general Rademacher theorem was found by [Charles Morrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Morrey). In the following, let $u$ denote a Lipschitz-continuous function on $\mathbf{R}^{n}$. The first step of the proof is to show that, for any fixed unit vector $v$, the $v$-directional derivative of $u$ exists almost everywhere. This is a consequence of a special case of the [Fubini theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fubini_theorem): a measurable set in $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ has Lebesgue measure zero if its restriction to every line parallel to $v$ has (one-dimensional) Lebesgue measure zero. Considering in particular the set in $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ where the $v$-directional derivative of $u$ fails to exist (which must be proved to be measurable), the latter condition is met due to the one-dimensional case of Rademacher's theorem.

The second step of Morrey's proof establishes the linear dependence of the $v$-directional derivative of $u$ upon $v$. This is based upon the following identity:

$$\int_{\mathbf{R}^n}\frac{u(x+h\nu)-u(x)}{h}\zeta(x)\,d\mathcal{L}^n(x)=-\int_{\mathbf{R}^n}\frac{\zeta(x)-\zeta(x-h\nu)}{h}u(x)\,d\mathcal{L}^n(x).$$

Using the Lipschitz assumption on $u$, the [dominated convergence theorem](wikipedia://en/351853) can be applied to replace the two [difference quotients](wikipedia://en/241863) in the above expression by the corresponding $v$-directional derivatives. Then, based upon the known linear dependence of the $v$-directional derivative of $\zeta$ upon $v$, the same can be proved of $u$ via the [fundamental lemma of calculus of variations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_lemma_of_calculus_of_variations).

At this point in the proof, the gradient (defined as the $n$-tuple of partial derivatives) is guaranteed to exist almost everywhere; for each $v$, the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) with $v$ equals the $v$-directional derivative almost everywhere (although perhaps on a smaller set). Hence, for any countable collection of unit vectors $v_{1}, v_{2}, ...$, there is a single set $E$ of measure zero such that the gradient and each $v_{i}$-directional derivative exist everywhere on the complement of $E$, and are linked by the dot product. By selecting $v_{1}, v_{2}, ...$ to be dense in the [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090), it is possible to use the Lipschitz condition to prove the existence of *every* [directional derivative](wikipedia://en/487859) everywhere on the complement of $E$, together with its representation as the dot product of the gradient with the direction.

Morrey's proof can also be put into the context of [generalized derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_derivative). Another proof, also via a reduction to the one-dimensional case, uses the technology of [approximate limits](wikipedia://en/27100784).

:::

::: {#o5 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Rademacher's theorem can be used to prove that, for any $p \ge 1$, the [Sobolev space](wikipedia://en/611964) $W^{1,p}(\Omega)$ is preserved under a bi-Lipschitz transformation of the domain, with the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) holding in its standard form. With appropriate modification, this also extends to the more general Sobolev spaces $W^{k,p}(\Omega)$.

Rademacher's theorem is also significant in the study of [geometric measure theory](wikipedia://en/12169694) and [rectifiable sets](wikipedia://en/2644238), as it allows the analysis of first-order [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), specifically [tangent planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_plane) and [normal vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_vector). Higher-order concepts such as [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) remain more subtle, since their usual definitions require more differentiability than is achieved by the Rademacher theorem. In the presence of [convexity](wikipedia://en/245568), second-order differentiability is achieved by the [Alexandrov theorem](wikipedia://en/37490344), the proof of which can be modeled on that of the Rademacher theorem. In some special cases, the Rademacher theorem is even used as part of the proof.

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

[Alberto Calderón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberto_Calderón) proved the more general fact that if $\Omega$ is an open bounded set in $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ then every function in the [Sobolev space](wikipedia://en/611964) $W^{1,p}(\Omega)$ is differentiable almost everywhere, provided that $p > n$. Calderón's theorem is a relatively direct corollary of the [Lebesgue differentiation theorem](wikipedia://en/5766547) and [Sobolev embedding theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sobolev_embedding_theorem). Rademacher's theorem is a special case, due to the fact that any Lipschitz function on $\Omega$ is an element of the space $W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)$.

There is a version of Rademacher's theorem that holds for Lipschitz functions from a Euclidean space into an arbitrary [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) in terms of [metric differentials](wikipedia://en/33004328) instead of the usual derivative.

:::
