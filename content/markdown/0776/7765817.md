# Borsuk's conjecture

*Image: Borsuk Hexagon.svg.* An example of a [hexagon](wikipedia://en/59733) cut into three pieces of smaller diameter.

::: {#o1 .problem cue=unsolved}

What is the lowest $n$ such that not every [bounded](wikipedia://en/48258) subset $E$ of the space $\mathbb{R}^n$ can be [partitioned](wikipedia://en/340240) into ($n + 1$) sets, each of which has a smaller diameter than $E$?

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Borsuk's conjecture"}

The **Borsuk problem in geometry**, for historical reasons incorrectly called **Borsuk's [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138)**, is a question in [discrete geometry](wikipedia://en/386468). It is named after [Karol Borsuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karol_Borsuk).

:::

## Problem {#Problem}

In 1932, [Karol Borsuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karol_Borsuk) showed[^o4] that an ordinary 3-dimensional [ball](wikipedia://en/160556) in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) can be easily dissected into 4 solids, each of which has a smaller [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) than the ball, and generally $n$-dimensional ball can be covered with $n + 1$ [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) of diameters smaller than the ball. At the same time he proved that $n$ [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) are not enough in general. The proof is based on the [Borsuk–Ulam theorem](wikipedia://en/4064). That led Borsuk to a general question:[^o4]

The question was answered in the positive in the following cases:

- $n = 2$ — which is the original result by Karol Borsuk (1932).
- $n = 3$ — shown by Julian Perkal (1947),[^o5] and independently, 8 years later, by H. G. Eggleston (1955).[^o6] A simple proof was found later by [Branko Grünbaum](wikipedia://en/1731976) and Aladár Heppes.
- For all $n$ for [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) convex fields — shown by [Hugo Hadwiger](wikipedia://en/630451) (1946).[^o7][^o8]
- For all $n$ for [centrally-symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_symmetry) fields — shown by A.S. Riesling (1971).[^o9]
- For all $n$ for [fields of revolution](wikipedia://en/329400) — shown by Boris Dekster (1995).[^o10]

The problem was finally solved in 1993 by [Jeff Kahn](wikipedia://en/26919476) and [Gil Kalai](wikipedia://en/19709056), who showed that the general answer to Borsuk's question is no.[^o11] They claim that their construction shows that $n + 1$ pieces do not suffice for $n = 1325$ and for each $n > 2014$. However, as pointed out by Bernulf Weißbach,[^o12] the first part of this claim is in fact false. But after improving a suboptimal conclusion within the corresponding derivation, one can indeed verify one of the constructed point sets as a counterexample for $n = 1325$ (as well as all higher dimensions up to 1560).[^o13]

Their result was improved in 2003 by Hinrichs and Richter, who constructed finite sets for $n \ge 298$, which cannot be partitioned into $n + 11$ parts of smaller diameter.[^o29]

In 2013, Andriy V. Bondarenko had shown that Borsuk's conjecture is false for all $n \ge 65$.[^o14] Shortly after, Thomas Jenrich derived a 64-dimensional counterexample from Bondarenko's construction, which stood as the record until 2026.[^o15][^o16] In May 2026, Max Grinsztajn, assisted by OpenAI's GPT-5.5 Pro, lowered the minimum known failing dimension to 63 by constructing a 321-point three-distance configuration requiring at least 65 parts.[^o17][^o18]

Apart from finding the minimum number $n$ of dimensions such that the number of pieces $\alpha(n) > n + 1$, mathematicians are interested in finding the general behavior of the function $\alpha(n)$. Kahn and Kalai show that in general (that is, for $n$ sufficiently large), one needs $\alpha(n) \ge (1.2)^\sqrt{n}$ many pieces. They also quote the upper bound by [Oded Schramm](wikipedia://en/9605580), who showed that for every $\varepsilon$, if $n$ is sufficiently large, $\alpha(n) \le \left(\sqrt{3/2} + \varepsilon\right)^n$.[^o19] The correct order of magnitude of $\alpha(n)$ is still unknown.[^o20] However, it is conjectured that there is a constant $c > 1$ such that $\alpha(n) > c^{n}$ for all $n \ge 1$.

Oded Schramm also worked on a related question, a body $K$ of constant width is said to have effective radius $r$ if $\text{Vol}(K)=r^n\text{Vol}(\mathbb{B}^{n} )$, where $\mathbb{B}^{n}$ is the unit ball in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, he proved the lower bound $\sqrt{3+2/(n+1)}-1\le r_n$, where $r_n$ is the smallest effective radius of a body of constant width 2 in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and asked if there exists $\epsilon>0$ such that $r_n\le 1-\epsilon$ for all $n\ge2$,[^o21][^o22] that is if the gap between the volumes of the smallest and largest constant-width bodies grows exponentially. In 2024 a preprint by Arman, Bondarenko, [Nazarov](wikipedia://en/21079027), Prymak, Radchenko reported to have answered this question in the affirmative giving a construction that satisfies $\text{Vol}(K)\leq (0.9)^n\text{Vol}(\mathbb{B}^{n} )$.[^o23][^o24][^o25]
