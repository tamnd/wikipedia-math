# Inverse image functor

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Inverse image functor"}

In mathematics, specifically in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), an **inverse image functor** is a [contravariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariance_and_contravariance_of_functors) construction of [sheaves](wikipedia://en/245466); here “contravariant” in the sense given a map $f : X \to Y$, the inverse image [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) is a functor from the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of sheaves on *Y* to the category of sheaves on *X*. The [direct image functor](wikipedia://en/3022744) is the primary operation on sheaves, with the simplest definition. The inverse image exhibits some relatively subtle features.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Suppose we are given a sheaf $\mathcal{G}$ on $Y$ and that we want to transport $\mathcal{G}$ to $X$ using a [continuous map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) $f\colon X\to Y$.

We will call the result the *inverse image* or [pullback](wikipedia://en/841689) [sheaf](wikipedia://en/245466) $f^{-1}\mathcal{G}$. If we try to imitate the [direct image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_image) by setting

$$f^{-1}\mathcal{G}(U) = \mathcal{G}(f(U))$$

for each open set $U$ of $X$, we immediately run into a problem: $f(U)$ is not necessarily open. The best we could do is to approximate it by open sets, and even then we will get a [presheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presheaf) and not a sheaf. Consequently, we define $f^{-1}\mathcal{G}$ to be the [sheaf associated to the presheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheaf_associated_to_the_presheaf):

$$U \mapsto \varinjlim_{V\supseteq f(U)}\mathcal{G}(V).$$

(Here $U$ is an [open subset](wikipedia://en/39358) of $X$ and the [colimit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colimit) runs over all open subsets $V$ of $Y$ containing $f(U)$.)

For example, if $f$ is just the inclusion of a point $y$ of $Y$, then $f^{-1}(\mathcal{F})$ is just the [stalk](wikipedia://en/15879621) of $\mathcal{F}$ at this point.

The restriction maps, as well as the [functoriality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functoriality) of the inverse image follows from the [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245) of [direct limits](wikipedia://en/211566).

When dealing with [morphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphisms) $f\colon X\to Y$ of [locally ringed spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_ringed_space), for example [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), one often works with [sheaves of $\mathcal{O}_Y$-modules](wikipedia://en/44887605), where $\mathcal{O}_Y$ is the structure sheaf of $Y$. Then the functor $f^{-1}$ is inappropriate, because in general it does not even give sheaves of $\mathcal{O}_X$-modules. In order to remedy this, one defines in this situation for a sheaf of $\mathcal O_Y$-modules $\mathcal G$ its inverse image by

$$f^*\mathcal G := f^{-1}\mathcal{G} \otimes_{f^{-1}\mathcal{O}_Y} \mathcal{O}_X.$$

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- While $f^{-1}$ is more complicated to define than $f_{\ast}$, the [stalks](wikipedia://en/15879621) are easier to compute: given a point $x \in X$, one has $(f^{-1}\mathcal{G})_x \cong \mathcal{G}_{f(x)}$.
- $f^{-1}$ is an [exact functor](wikipedia://en/453945), as can be seen by the above calculation of the stalks.
- $f^*$ is (in general) only right exact. If $f^*$ is exact, *f* is called [flat](wikipedia://en/542520).
- $f^{-1}$ is the [left adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_functor) of the [direct image functor](wikipedia://en/3022744) $f_{\ast}$. This implies that there are natural unit and counit morphisms $\mathcal{G} \rightarrow f_*f^{-1}\mathcal{G}$ and $f^{-1}f_*\mathcal{F} \rightarrow \mathcal{F}$. These morphisms yield a natural adjunction correspondence:

$$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf {Sh}(X)}(f^{-1} \mathcal G, \mathcal F ) = \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf {Sh}(Y)}(\mathcal G, f_*\mathcal F).$$

However, the morphisms $\mathcal{G} \rightarrow f_*f^{-1}\mathcal{G}$ and $f^{-1}f_*\mathcal{F} \rightarrow \mathcal{F}$ are *almost never* isomorphisms. For example, if $i\colon Z \to Y$ denotes the inclusion of a [closed subset](wikipedia://en/47279), the stalk of $i_* i^{-1} \mathcal G$ at a point $y \in Y$ is canonically isomorphic to $\mathcal G_y$ if $y$ is in $Z$ and $0$ otherwise. A similar adjunction holds for the case of sheaves of modules, replacing $i^{-1}$ by $i^*$.

:::
