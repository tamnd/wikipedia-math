# Quaternionic analysis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quaternionic analysis"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **quaternionic analysis** is the study of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) with [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) as the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) and/or range. Such functions can be called **functions of a quaternion variable** just as [functions of a real variable](wikipedia://en/1268958) or a [complex variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_of_a_complex_variable) are called.

:::

As with [complex](wikipedia://en/5759) and [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478), it is possible to study the concepts of [analyticity](wikipedia://en/61478), [holomorphy](wikipedia://en/14110), [harmonicity](wikipedia://en/55762) and [conformality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformality) in the context of quaternions. Unlike the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) and like the [reals](wikipedia://en/20646438), the four notions do not coincide.

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The [projections](wikipedia://en/519182) of a quaternion onto its scalar part or onto its vector part, as well as the modulus and [versor](wikipedia://en/2644621) functions, are examples that are basic to understanding quaternion structure.

An important example of a function of a quaternion variable is

$$f_1(q) = u q u^{-1}$$

which [rotates the vector part of *q*](wikipedia://en/186057) by twice the angle represented by the versor *u*.

The quaternion [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) $f_2(q) = q^{-1}$ is another fundamental function, but as with other number systems, $f_2(0)$ and related problems are generally excluded due to the nature of [dividing by zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dividing_by_zero).

[Affine transformations](wikipedia://en/38449) of quaternions have the form

$$f_3(q) = aq + b, \quad a, b, q \in \mathbb{H}.$$

[Linear fractional transformations](wikipedia://en/401769) of quaternions can be represented by elements of the [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) $M_2(\mathbb{H})$ operating on the [projective line over $\mathbb{H}$](wikipedia://en/1271901). For instance, the mappings $q \mapsto u q v,$ where $u$ and $v$ are fixed [versors](wikipedia://en/2644621) serve to produce the [motions of elliptic space](wikipedia://en/236020#elliptic_space).

Quaternion variable theory differs in some respects from complex variable theory. For example: The [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) mapping of the complex plane is a central tool but requires the introduction of a non-arithmetic, [non-analytic](wikipedia://en/14110) operation. Indeed, conjugation changes the [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29) of plane figures, something that arithmetic functions do not change.

In contrast to the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918), the quaternion conjugation can be expressed arithmetically, as $f_4(q) = - \tfrac{1}{2} (q + iqi + jqj + kqk)$

This equation can be proven, starting with the [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) {1, i, j, k}:

$$f_4(1) = -\tfrac{1}{2}(1 - 1 - 1 - 1) = 1, \quad f_4(i) = -\tfrac{1}{2}(i - i + i + i) = -i, \quad f_4(j) = -j, \quad f_4(k) = -k.$$

Consequently, since $f_4$ is [linear](wikipedia://en/18102),

$$f_4(q) = f_4(w + x i + y j + z k) = w f_4(1) + x f_4(i) + y f_4(j) + z f_4(k) = w - x i - y j - z k = q^*.$$

The success of [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759) in providing a rich family of [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110) for scientific work has engaged some workers in efforts to extend the planar theory, based on complex numbers, to a 4-space study with functions of a quaternion variable.[^o4] These efforts were summarized in (Deavours 1973).

Though $\mathbb{H}$ [appears as a union of complex planes](wikipedia://en/51440#As_a_union_of_complex_planes), the following proposition shows that extending complex functions requires special care:

Let $f_5(z) = u(x,y) + i v(x,y)$ be a function of a complex variable, $z = x + i y$. Suppose also that $u$ is an [even function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_function) of $y$ and that $v$ is an [odd function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_function) of $y$. Then $f_5(q) = u(x,y) + rv(x,y)$ is an extension of $f_5$ to a quaternion variable $q = x + yr$ where $r^2 = -1$ and $r \in \mathbb{H}$. Then, let $r^*$ represent the conjugate of $r$, so that $q = x - yr^*$. The extension to $\mathbb{H}$ will be complete when it is shown that $f_5(q) = f_5(x - yr^*)$. Indeed, by hypothesis

$u(x,y) = u(x,-y), \quad v(x,y) = -v(x,-y) \quad$ one obtains

$$f_5(x - y r^*) = u(x,-y) + r^* v(x,-y) = u(x,y) + r v(x,y) = f_5(q).$$

:::

## Homographies {#Homographies}

In the following, colons and square brackets are used to denote [homogeneous vectors](wikipedia://en/243316).

The [rotation](wikipedia://en/186057) about axis *r* is a classical application of quaternions to [space](wikipedia://en/27667) mapping.[^o7] In terms of a [homography](wikipedia://en/3051962#Over_a_ring), the rotation is expressed

$$[q:1] \begin{pmatrix}u & 0\\0 & u \end{pmatrix} = [qu : u] \thicksim [u^{-1}qu : 1] ,$$

where $u = \exp(\theta r) = \cos \theta + r \sin \theta$ is a [versor](wikipedia://en/2644621). If *p* \* = −*p*, then the translation $q \mapsto q + p$ is expressed by

$$[q : 1]\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ p & 1 \end{pmatrix} = [q + p : 1].$$

Rotation and translation *xr* along the axis of rotation is given by

$$[q : 1]\begin{pmatrix}u & 0 \\ uxr & u \end{pmatrix} = [qu + uxr : u] \thicksim [u^{-1}qu + xr : 1].$$

Such a mapping is called a [screw displacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screw_displacement). In classical [kinematics](wikipedia://en/65914), [Chasles' theorem](wikipedia://en/42392462) states that any rigid body motion can be displayed as a screw displacement. Just as the representation of a [Euclidean plane isometry](wikipedia://en/1989599) as a rotation is a matter of complex number arithmetic, so Chasles' theorem, and the [screw axis](wikipedia://en/3656192) required, is a matter of quaternion arithmetic with homographies: Let *s* be a right versor, or square root of minus one, perpendicular to *r*, with *t* = *rs*.

Consider the axis passing through *s* and parallel to *r*. Rotation about it is expressed[^o8] by the homography composition

$$\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -s & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}u & 0 \\ 0 & u \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ s & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}u & 0 \\ z & u \end{pmatrix},$$

where $z = u s - s u = \sin \theta (rs - sr) = 2 t \sin \theta .$

Now in the (*s,t*)-plane the parameter θ traces out a circle $u^{-1} z = u^{-1}(2 t \sin \theta) = 2 \sin \theta ( t \cos \theta - s \sin \theta)$ in the half-plane $\lbrace wt + xs : x > 0 \rbrace .$

Any *p* in this half-plane lies on a ray from the origin through the circle $\lbrace u^{-1} z : 0 < \theta < \pi \rbrace$ and can be written $p = a u^{-1} z , \ \ a > 0 .$

Then *up* = *az*, with $\begin{pmatrix}u & 0 \\ az & u \end{pmatrix}$ as the homography expressing [conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugation_%28group_theory%29) of a rotation by a translation p.

## The derivative for quaternions {#The_derivative_for_quaternions}

Since the time of Hamilton, it has been realized that requiring the independence of the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) from the path that a differential follows toward zero is too restrictive: it excludes even $\ f(q) = q^2\$ from differentiation. Therefore, a direction-dependent derivative is necessary for functions of a quaternion variable.[^o10][^o11] Considering the increment of [polynomial function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_function) of quaternionic argument shows that the increment is a linear map of increment of the argument. From this, a definition can be made:

A continuous function $\ f: \mathbb H \rightarrow \mathbb H\$ is called *differentiable on the set* $\ U \subset \mathbb H\ ,$ if at every point $\ x \in U\ ,$ an increment of the function $\ f\$ corresponding to a quaternion increment $\ h\$ of its argument, can be represented as

$$f(x+h) - f(x) = \frac{\operatorname d f(x)}{\operatorname d x} \circ h + o(h)$$

where

$$\frac{\operatorname d f(x)}{\operatorname d x}:\mathbb H\rightarrow\mathbb H$$

is [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) of quaternion algebra $\ \mathbb H\ ,$ and $\ o:\mathbb H\rightarrow \mathbb H\$ represents some continuous map such that

$$\lim_{a \rightarrow 0} \frac{\ \left|\ o(a)\ \right|\ }{ \left|\ a\ \right| } = 0\ ,$$

and the notation $\ \circ h\$ denotes ...

The linear map $\frac{\operatorname d f(x)}{\operatorname d x}$ is called the derivative of the map $\ f ~.$

On the quaternions, the derivative may be expressed as

$$\frac{\operatorname d f(x)}{\operatorname d x} = \sum_s \frac{\operatorname{d}_{s0} f(x)}{\operatorname d x} \otimes \frac{\operatorname{d}_{s1} f(x)}{\operatorname d x}$$

Therefore, the differential of the map $\ f\$ may be expressed as follows, with brackets on either side.

$$\frac{\operatorname d f(x)}{\operatorname d x}\circ \operatorname d x = \left(\sum_s \frac{\operatorname{d}_{s0} f(x)}{\operatorname d x} \otimes \frac{\operatorname{d}_{s1} f(x)}{\operatorname d x}\right)\circ \operatorname d x = \sum_s \frac{\operatorname{d}_{s0} f(x)}{\operatorname d x} \left( \operatorname d x \right) \frac{\operatorname{d}_{s1} f(x)}{\operatorname d x}$$

The number of terms in the sum will depend on the function $\ f ~.$ The expressions $~~ \frac{\operatorname{d}_{sp}\operatorname d f(x)}{\operatorname d x} ~~ \mathsf{\ for\ } ~~ p = 0, 1 ~~$ are called components of derivative.

The derivative of a quaternionic function is defined by the expression

$$\frac{\operatorname d f(x)}{\operatorname d x}\circ h = \lim_{t\to 0}\left(\ \frac{\ f(x + t\ h) - f(x)\ }{ t }\ \right)$$

where the variable $\ t\$ is a real scalar.

The following equations then hold:

$$\frac{\operatorname d\left( f(x) + g(x) \right)}{\operatorname d x} = \frac{\operatorname d f(x)}{\operatorname d x} + \frac{\operatorname d g(x)}{\operatorname d x}$$

$$\frac{\operatorname d\left( f(x)\ g(x)\right)}{\operatorname d x} = \frac{\operatorname d f(x) }{\operatorname d x}\ g(x) + f(x)\ \frac{\operatorname d g(x)}{\operatorname d x}$$

$$\frac{\operatorname d \left( f(x)\ g(x)\right)}{\operatorname d x} \circ h = \left(\frac{\operatorname d f(x)}{\operatorname d x}\circ h\right )\ g(x) + f(x) \left(\frac{\operatorname d g(x)}{\operatorname d x}\circ h\right)$$

$$\frac{\operatorname d \left( a\ f(x)\ b\right)}{\operatorname d x} = a\ \frac{\operatorname d f(x)}{\operatorname d x}\ b$$

$$\frac{\operatorname d \left( a\ f(x)\ b\right)}{\operatorname d x}\circ h = a \left(\frac{\operatorname d f(x)}{\operatorname d x}\circ h\right) b$$

For the function $\ f(x) = a\ x\ b\ ,$ where $\ a\$ and $\ b\$ are constant quaternions, the derivative is

::: {#o12 .table cue=table}

| $\frac{\operatorname d \left( a\ x\ b \right)}{\operatorname d x} = a \otimes b$ |  | $\operatorname d y=\frac{\operatorname d \left(a\ x\ b\right)}{\operatorname d x} \circ \operatorname d x = a\ \left(\operatorname d x\right)\ b$ |
|---|---|---|

:::

and so the components are:

::: {#o13 .table cue=table}

| $\frac{\operatorname{d}_{10} \left(a\ x\ b\right)}{\operatorname d x} = a$ |  | $\frac{\operatorname{d}_{11} \left(a\ x\ b\right)}{\operatorname d x} = b$ |
|---|---|---|

:::

Similarly, for the function $\ f(x) = x^2\ ,$ the derivative is

::: {#o14 .table cue=table}

| $\frac{\operatorname d x^2}{\operatorname d x}=x \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes x$ |  | $\operatorname d y=\frac{\operatorname d x^2}{\operatorname d x}\circ \operatorname d x = x\ \operatorname d x + (\operatorname d x)\ x$ |
|---|---|---|

:::

and the components are:

::: {#o15 .table cue=table}

| $\frac{\operatorname{d}_{10} x^2 }{\operatorname d x} = x$ |  | $\frac{\operatorname{d}_{11} x^2}{\operatorname d x} = 1$ |
|---|---|---|
| $\frac{\operatorname{d}_{20} x^2 }{\operatorname d x} = 1$ |  | $\frac{\operatorname{d}_{21} x^2}{\operatorname d x} = x$ |

:::

Finally, for the function $\ f(x) = x^{-1}\ ,$ the derivative is

::: {#o16 .table cue=table}

| $\frac{\operatorname d x^{-1} }{\operatorname d x} = -x^{-1} \otimes x^{-1}$ |  | $\operatorname d y = \frac{\operatorname d x^{-1} }{\operatorname d x} \circ \operatorname d x = -x^{-1}(\operatorname d x)\ x^{-1}$ |
|---|---|---|

:::

and the components are:

::: {#o17 .table cue=table}

| $\frac{\operatorname{d}_{10} x^{-1} }{\operatorname d x} = -x^{-1}$ |  | $\frac{\operatorname{d}_{11} x^{-1} }{\operatorname d x} = x^{-1}$ |
|---|---|---|

:::
