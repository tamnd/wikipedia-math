# Universal psychometrics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Universal psychometrics"}

**Universal psychometrics** encompasses [psychometrics](wikipedia://en/24982) instruments that could measure the [psychological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological) properties of any [intelligent agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligent_agent). Up until the early 21st century, psychometrics relied heavily on [psychological tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_tests) that require the subject to cooperate and answer questions, the most famous example being an [intelligence test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_test). Such methods are only applicable to the measurement of human psychological properties. As a result, some researchers have proposed the idea of universal psychometrics - they suggest developing testing methods that allow for the measurement of non-human entities' psychological properties.[^o2]

:::

For example, it has been suggested that the [Turing test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_test) is a form of universal psychometrics. This test involves having testers (without any foreknowledge) attempt to distinguish a [human](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human) from a [machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine) by interacting with both (while not being to see either individuals). It is supposed that if the machine is [equally intelligent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence) to a human, the testers will not be able to distinguish between the two, i.e., their guesses will not be better than chance. Thus, Turing test could measure the [intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence) (a psychological variable) of an AI.[^o3]

Other instruments proposed for universal psychometrics include [reinforcement learning](wikipedia://en/66294)[^o4] and measuring the ability to predict [complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complexity).[^o5]
