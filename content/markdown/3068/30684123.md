# Ptolemy's table of chords

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ptolemy's table of chords"}

The **table of chords**, created by the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece) astronomer, geometer, and geographer [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979) in [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt) during the 2nd century AD, is a [trigonometric table](wikipedia://en/150170) in Book I, chapter 11 of Ptolemy's *Almagest*,[^o2] a treatise on [mathematical astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_astronomy). It is essentially equivalent to a table of values of the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) function. It was the earliest trigonometric table extensive enough for many practical purposes, including those of astronomy (an earlier table of chords by [Hipparchus](wikipedia://en/13600) gave chords only for arcs that were multiples of $7\tfrac{1}{2}$° = $\tfrac{\pi}{24}$ radians).[^o3] Since the 8th and 9th centuries, the sine and other trigonometric functions have been used in Islamic mathematics and astronomy, reforming the production of sine tables.[^o4] [Khwarizmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Musa_al-Khwarizmi) and [Habash al-Hasib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habash_al-Hasib_al-Marwazi) later produced a set of trigonometric tables.

:::

## The chord function and the table {#The_chord_function_and_the_table}

*Image: Chords (circle).svg.* Example: The length of the chord subtending a ($109\tfrac{1}{2}$)° arc is approximately 98.

A [chord](wikipedia://en/297947) of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) is a line segment whose endpoints are on the circle. Ptolemy used a circle whose diameter is 120 parts. He tabulated the length of a chord whose endpoints are separated by an arc of *n* degrees, for *n* ranging from $\tfrac{1}{2}$ to 180 by increments of $\tfrac{1}{2}$. In modern notation, the length of the chord corresponding to an arc of *θ* degrees is

$$\begin{aligned} & \operatorname{chord}(\theta) = 120\sin\left(\frac{\theta^\circ} 2 \right) \\ = {} & 60 \cdot \left( 2 \sin\left(\frac{\pi\theta}{360} \text{ radians} \right) \right). \end{aligned}$$

As *θ* goes from 0 to 180, the chord of a *θ*° arc goes from 0 to 120. For tiny arcs, the chord is to the arc angle in degrees as $\pi$ is to 3, or more precisely, the ratio can be made as close as desired to $\tfrac{\pi}{3}$ ≈ \1.04719755 by making *θ* small enough. Thus, for the arc of $\tfrac{1}{2}$°, the chord length is slightly more than the arc angle in degrees. As the arc increases, the ratio of the chord to the arc decreases. When the arc reaches 60°, the chord length is exactly equal to the number of degrees in the arc, i.e. chord 60° = 60. For arcs of more than 60°, the chord is less than the arc, until an arc of 180° is reached, when the chord is only 120.

The fractional parts of chord lengths were expressed in [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) (base 60) numerals. For example, where the length of a chord subtended by a 112° arc is reported to be 99,29,5, it has a length of

$$99 + \frac{29}{60} + \frac{5}{60^2} = 99.4847\overline{2},$$

rounded to the nearest $\tfrac{1}{60^{2}}$.[^o2]

After the columns for the arc and the chord, a third column is labeled "sixtieths". For an arc of *θ*°, the entry in the "sixtieths" column is

$$\frac{\operatorname{chord} \left(\theta + \tfrac12^\circ \right) - \operatorname{chord} \left( \theta^\circ\right)}{30}.$$

This is the average number of sixtieths of a unit that must be added to chord(*θ*°) each time the angle increases by one [minute of arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minute_and_second_of_arc), between the entry for *θ*° and that for (*θ* + $\tfrac{1}{2}$)°. Thus, it is used for [linear interpolation](wikipedia://en/160010). Glowatzki and Göttsche showed that Ptolemy must have calculated chords to five [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) places in order to achieve the degree of accuracy found in the "sixtieths" column.[^o6][^o7]

$$\begin{array}{|l|rrr|rrr|} \hline \text{arc}^\circ & \text{chord} & & & \text{sixtieths} & & \\ \hline {}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\, \tfrac12 & 0 & 31 & 25 & 0 \quad 1 & 2 & 50 \\ {}\,\,\,\,\,\,\, 1 & 1 & 2 & 50 & 0 \quad 1 & 2 & 50 \\ {}\,\,\,\,\,\,\, 1\tfrac12 & 1 & 34 & 15 & 0 \quad 1 & 2 & 50 \\ {}\,\,\,\,\,\,\, \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 109 & 97 & 41 & 38 & 0 \quad 0 & 36 & 23 \\ 109\tfrac12 & 97 & 59 & 49 & 0 \quad 0 & 36 & 9 \\ 110 & 98 & 17 & 54 & 0 \quad 0 & 35 & 56 \\ 110\tfrac12 & 98 & 35 & 52 & 0 \quad 0 & 35 & 42\\ 111 & 98 & 53 & 43 & 0 \quad 0 & 35 & 29 \\ 111\tfrac12 & 99 & 11 & 27 & 0 \quad 0 & 35 & 15 \\ 112 & 99 & 29 & 5 & 0 \quad 0 & 35 & 1\\ 112\tfrac12 & 99 & 46 & 35 & 0 \quad 0 & 34 & 48 \\ 113 & 100 & 3 & 59 & 0 \quad 0 & 34 & 34 \\ {}\,\,\,\,\,\,\, \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 179 & 119 & 59 & 44 & 0 \quad 0 & 0 & 25 \\ 179\frac12 & 119 & 59 & 56 & 0 \quad 0 & 0 & 9 \\ 180 & 120 & 0 & 0 & 0 \quad 0 & 0 & 0 \\ \hline \end{array}$$

## How Ptolemy computed chords {#How_Ptolemy_computed_chords}

Chapter 10 of Book I of the *Almagest* presents [geometric](wikipedia://en/9417) theorems used for computing chords. Ptolemy used geometric reasoning based on Proposition 10 of Book XIII of [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s *[Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)* to find the chords of 72° and 36°. That [Proposition](wikipedia://en/30977#Terminology) states that if an equilateral [pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014) is inscribed in a circle, then the area of the square on the side of the pentagon equals the sum of the areas of the squares on the sides of the [hexagon](wikipedia://en/59733) and the [decagon](wikipedia://en/327393) inscribed in the same circle.

He used [Ptolemy's theorem](wikipedia://en/1521971) on quadrilaterals inscribed in a circle to derive formulas for the chord of a half-arc, the chord of the sum of two arcs, and the chord of a difference of two arcs. The theorem states that for a [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278) inscribed in a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), the product of the lengths of the diagonals equals the sum of the products of the two pairs of lengths of opposite sides. The derivations of trigonometric identities rely on a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131) in which one side is a diameter of the circle.

To find the chords of arcs of 1° and $\tfrac{1}{2}$° he used approximations based on [Aristarchus's inequality](wikipedia://en/31152478). The inequality states that for arcs *α* and *β*, if 0 \< *β* \< *α* \< 90°, then

$$\frac{\sin \alpha}{\sin \beta} < \frac\alpha\beta < \frac{\tan\alpha}{\tan\beta}.$$

Ptolemy showed that for arcs of 1° and $\tfrac{1}{2}$°, the approximations correctly give the first two sexagesimal places after the integer part.

### Accuracy {#Accuracy}

[Gerald J. Toomer](wikipedia://en/31073672) in his translation of the Almagest gives seven entries where some manuscripts have scribal errors, changing one "digit" (one letter, see below). [Glenn Elert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenn_Elert) has made a comparison between Ptolemy's values and the true values (120 times the sine of half the angle) and has found that the [root mean square](wikipedia://en/66819) error is 0.000136. But much of this is simply due to rounding off to the nearest 1/3600, since this equals 0.0002777... There are nevertheless many entries where the last "digit" is off by 1 (too high or too low) from the best rounded value. Ptolemy's values are often too high by 1 in the last place, and more so towards the higher angles. The largest errors are about 0.0004, which still corresponds to an error of only 1 in the last [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) digit.[^o10]

## The numeral system and the appearance of the untranslated table {#The_numeral_system_and_the_appearance_of_the_untranslated_table}

Lengths of arcs of the circle, in degrees, and the integer parts of chord lengths, were expressed in a [base 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_10) [numeral system](wikipedia://en/21170) that used 21 of the letters of the [Greek alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet) with the meanings given in the following table, and a symbol, "∠′, that means $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and a raised circle "○" that fills a blank space (effectively representing zero). Three of the letters, labeled "archaic" in the table below, had not been in use in the Greek language for some centuries before the *Almagest* was written, but were still in use as numerals and [musical notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_music).

$$\begin{array}{|rlr|rlr|rlr|} \hline \alpha & \mathrm{alpha} & 1 & \iota & \mathrm{iota} & 10 & \rho & \mathrm{rho} & 100 \\ \beta & \mathrm{beta} & 2 & \kappa & \mathrm{kappa} & 20 & \sigma & \mathrm{sigma} & 200 \\ \gamma & \mathrm{gamma} & 3 & \lambda & \mathrm{lambda} & 30 & \tau & \mathrm{tau} & 300 \\ \delta & \mathrm{delta} & 4 & \mu & \mathrm{mu} & 40 & \upsilon & \mathrm{upsilon} & 400 \\ \varepsilon & \mathrm{epsilon} & 5 & \nu & \mathrm{nu} & 50 & \varphi & \mathrm{phi} & 500 \\ \text{ϛ} & \mathrm{stigma\ (archaic)} & 6 & \xi & \mathrm{xi} & 60 & \chi & \mathrm{chi} & 600 \\ \zeta & \mathrm{zeta} & 7 & o & \mathrm{omicron} & 70 & \psi & \mathrm{psi} & 700 \\ \eta & \mathrm{eta} & 8 & \pi & \mathrm{pi} & 80 & \omega & \mathrm{omega} & 800 \\ \theta & \mathrm{theta} & 9 & \text{ϟ} & \mathrm{koppa\ (archaic)} & 90 & \text{ϡ} & \mathrm{sampi\ (archaic)} & 900\\ \hline \end{array}$$

Thus, for example, an arc of $143\tfrac{1}{2}$° is expressed as *ρμγ*∠′. (As the table only reaches 180°, the Greek numerals for 200 and above are not used.)

The fractional parts of chord lengths required great accuracy, and were given in [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) notation in two columns in the table: The first column gives an integer multiple of $\tfrac{1}{60}$, in the range 0–59, the second an integer multiple of $\tfrac{1}{60^{2}}$ = $\tfrac{1}{3600}$, also in the range 0–59.

Thus in Heiberg's [edition of the *Almagest* with the table of chords on pages 48–63](http://www.wilbourhall.org/pdfs/HeibergAlmagestComplete.pdf), the beginning of the table, corresponding to arcs from $\tfrac{1}{2}$° to $7\tfrac{1}{2}$°, looks like this:

$$\begin{array}{ccc} \pi\varepsilon\rho\iota\varphi\varepsilon\rho\varepsilon\iota\tilde\omega\nu & \varepsilon\overset{\text{'}}\upsilon\theta\varepsilon\iota\tilde\omega\nu & \overset{\text{‘}}\varepsilon\xi\eta\kappao\sigma\tau\tilde\omega\nu \\ \begin{array}{|l|} \hline \quad \angle' \\ \alpha \\ \alpha\;\angle' \\ \hline\beta \\ \beta\;\angle' \\ \gamma \\ \hline\gamma\;\angle' \\ \delta \\ \delta\;\angle' \\ \hline\varepsilon \\ \varepsilon\;\angle' \\ \text{ϛ} \\ \hline\text{ϛ}\;\angle' \\ \zeta \\ \zeta\;\angle' \\ \hline \end{array} & \begin{array}{|r|r|r|} \hline\circ & \lambda\alpha & \kappa\varepsilon \\ \alpha & \beta & \nu \\ \alpha & \lambda\delta & \iota\varepsilon \\ \hline \beta & \varepsilon & \mu \\ \beta & \lambda\zeta & \delta \\ \gamma & \eta & \kappa\eta \\ \hline \gamma & \lambda\theta & \nu\beta \\ \delta & \iota\alpha & \iota\text{ϛ} \\ \delta & \mu\beta & \mu \\ \hline \varepsilon & \iota\delta & \delta \\ \varepsilon & \mu\varepsilon & \kappa\zeta \\ \text{ϛ} & \iota\text{ϛ} & \mu\theta \\ \hline \text{ϛ} & \mu\eta & \iota\alpha \\ \zeta & \iota\theta & \lambda\gamma \\ \zeta & \nu & \nu\delta \\ \hline \end{array} & \begin{array}{|r|r|r|r|} \hline \circ & \alpha & \beta & \nu \\ \circ & \alpha & \beta & \nu \\ \circ & \alpha & \beta & \nu \\ \hline \circ & \alpha & \beta & \nu \\ \circ & \alpha & \beta & \mu\eta \\ \circ & \alpha & \beta & \mu\eta \\ \hline\circ & \alpha & \beta & \mu\eta \\ \circ & \alpha & \beta & \mu\zeta \\ \circ & \alpha & \beta & \mu\zeta \\ \hline \circ & \alpha & \beta & \mu\text{ϛ} \\ \circ & \alpha & \beta & \mu\varepsilon \\ \circ & \alpha & \beta & \mu\delta \\ \hline \circ & \alpha & \beta & \mu\gamma \\ \circ & \alpha & \beta & \mu\beta \\ \circ & \alpha & \beta & \mu\alpha \\ \hline \end{array} \end{array}$$

Later in the table, one can see the base-10 nature of the numerals expressing the integer parts of the arc and the chord length. Thus an arc of 85° is written as *πε* (*π* for 80 and *ε* for 5) and not broken down into 60 + 25. The corresponding chord length is 81 plus a fractional part. The integer part begins with *πα*, likewise not broken into 60 + 21. But the fractional part, $\tfrac4{60} + \tfrac{15}{60^2}$, is written as *δ*, for 4, in the $\tfrac{1}{60}$ column, followed by *ιε*, for 15, in the $\tfrac{1}{60^{2}}$ column.

$$\begin{array}{ccc} \pi\varepsilon\rho\iota\varphi\varepsilon\rho\varepsilon\iota\tilde\omega\nu & \varepsilon\overset{\text{'}}\upsilon\theta\varepsilon\iota\tilde\omega\nu & \overset{\text{‘}}\varepsilon\xi\eta\kappao\sigma\tau\tilde\omega\nu \\ \begin{array}{|l|} \hline \pi\delta\angle' \\ \pi\varepsilon \\ \pi\varepsilon\angle' \\ \hline \pi\text{ϛ} \\ \pi\text{ϛ}\angle' \\ \pi\zeta \\ \hline \end{array} & \begin{array}{|r|r|r|} \hline \pi & \mu\alpha & \gamma \\ \pi\alpha & \delta & \iota\varepsilon \\ \pi\alpha & \kappa\zeta & \kappa\beta \\ \hline \pi\alpha & \nu & \kappa\delta \\ \pi\beta & \iota\gamma & \iota\theta \\ \pi\beta & \lambda\text{ϛ} & \theta \\ \hline \end{array} & \begin{array}{|r|r|r|r|} \hline \circ & \circ & \mu\text{ϛ} & \kappa\varepsilon \\ \circ & \circ & \mu\text{ϛ} & \iota\delta \\ \circ & \circ & \mu\text{ϛ} & \gamma \\ \hline \circ & \circ & \mu\varepsilon & \nu\beta \\ \circ & \circ & \mu\varepsilon & \mu \\ \circ & \circ & \mu\varepsilon & \kappa\theta \\ \hline \end{array} \end{array}$$

The table has 45 lines on each of eight pages, for a total of 360 lines.
