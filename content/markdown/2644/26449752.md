# Thermal conductance and resistance

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Thermal conductance and resistance"}

In [heat transfer](wikipedia://en/184726), [thermal engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_engineering), and [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952)**, thermal conductance** and **thermal resistance** are fundamental concepts that describe the ability of materials or systems to conduct [heat](wikipedia://en/19593167) and the opposition they offer to the [heat current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_current). The ability to manipulate these properties allows engineers to control [temperature gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature_gradient), prevent [thermal shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_shock), and maximize the efficiency of [thermal systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_systems). Furthermore, these principles find applications in a multitude of fields, including [materials science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materials_science), [mechanical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_engineering), [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics), and [energy management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_management). Knowledge of these principles is crucial in various scientific, engineering, and everyday applications, from designing efficient [temperature control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature_control), [thermal insulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_insulation), and [thermal management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_management_%28electronics%29) in industrial processes to optimizing the performance of [electronic devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_devices).

:::

**Thermal conductance** (*G*) measures the ability of a material or system to conduct heat. It provides insights into the ease with which heat can pass through a particular system. It is measured in units of [watts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt) per [kelvin](wikipedia://en/19593121) (W/K). It is essential in the design of [heat exchangers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_exchangers), [thermally efficient materials](wikipedia://en/4024093), and various engineering systems where the controlled movement of heat is vital.

Conversely, **thermal resistance** (*R*) measures the opposition to the heat current in a material or system. It is measured in units of kelvins per watt (K/W) and indicates how much [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050) difference (in kelvins) is required to transfer a unit of heat current (in watts) through the material or object. It is essential to optimize the [building insulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building_insulation), evaluate the efficiency of electronic devices, and enhance the performance of [heat sinks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_sinks) in various applications.

Objects made of insulators like rubber tend to have very high resistance and low conductance, while objects made of conductors like metals tend to have very low resistance and high conductance. This relationship is quantified by [resistivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_resistivity) or [conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_conductivity). However, the nature of a material is not the only factor as it also depends on the [size](wikipedia://en/43537463) and [shape](wikipedia://en/169191) of an object because these properties are [extensive rather than intensive](wikipedia://en/204464). The relationship between thermal conductance and resistance is analogous to that between [electrical conductance and resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_conductance_and_resistance) in the domain of electronics.

**Thermal insulance** ([*R*-value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R-value_%28insulation%29)) is a measure of a material's resistance to the heat current. It quantifies how effectively a material can resist the transfer of heat through conduction, convection, and radiation. It has the units [square metre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_metre) kelvins per watt (m<sup>2</sup>⋅K/W) in [SI units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_units) or [square foot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_foot) [degree Fahrenheit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_Fahrenheit)–[hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hour) per [British thermal unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_thermal_unit) (ft<sup>2</sup>⋅°F⋅h/Btu) in [imperial units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_units). The higher the thermal insulance, the better a material insulates against heat transfer. It is commonly used in construction to assess the insulation properties of materials such as walls, roofs, and insulation products.

## Absolute thermal resistance {#Absolute_thermal_resistance}

Absolute thermal resistance is the [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050) difference across a structure when a unit of [heat](wikipedia://en/19593167) energy flows through it in unit [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time). It is the reciprocal of [thermal conductance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_conductivity). The [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI) unit of absolute thermal resistance is kelvins per watt (K/W) or the equivalent [degrees Celsius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_Celsius) per watt (°C/W) – the two are the same since the intervals are equal: Δ*T* = 1 K = 1 °C.

The thermal resistance of materials is of great interest to electronic engineers because most electrical components generate heat and need to be cooled. Electronic components malfunction or fail if they overheat, and some parts routinely need measures taken in the design stage to prevent this.

### Explanation from an electronics point of view {#Explanation_from_an_electronics_point_of_view}

#### Equivalent thermal circuits {#Equivalent_thermal_circuits}

*Image: Equivalient thermal circuit 2.png.* The diagram shows an equivalent thermal circuit for a semiconductor device with a [heat sink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_sink):$\dot{Q}$ is the power dissipated by the device.; $T_{\rm J}$ is the [junction temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junction_temperature) in the device.; $T_{\rm C}$ is the temperature at its case.; $T_{\rm H}$ is the temperature where the heat sink is attached.; $T_{\rm amb}$ is the ambient air temperature.; $R_{\theta \rm JC}$ is the device's absolute thermal resistance from junction to case.; $R_{\theta \rm CH}$ is the absolute thermal resistance from the case to the heatsink.; $R_{\theta \rm HA}$ is the absolute thermal resistance of the heat sink.

The heat flow can be modelled by analogy to an electrical circuit where heat flow is represented by current, temperatures are represented by voltages, heat sources are represented by constant current sources, absolute thermal resistances are represented by resistors and thermal capacitances by capacitors.

The diagram shows an equivalent thermal circuit for a semiconductor device with a [heat sink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_sink).

#### Example calculation {#Example_calculation}

Consider a component such as a silicon transistor that is bolted to the metal frame of a piece of equipment. The transistor's manufacturer will specify parameters in the datasheet called the *absolute thermal resistance from junction to case* (symbol: $R_{\theta \rm JC}$), and the maximum allowable temperature of the semiconductor junction (symbol: $T_{J\rm max}$). The specification for the design should include a maximum temperature at which the circuit should function correctly. Finally, the designer should consider how the heat from the transistor will escape to the environment: this might be by convection into the air, with or without the aid of a [heat sink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_sink), or by conduction through the [printed circuit board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printed_circuit_board). For simplicity, let us assume that the designer decides to bolt the transistor to a metal surface (or [heat sink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_sink)) that is guaranteed to be less than $\Delta T_{\rm HS}$ above the ambient temperature. Note: T<sub>HS</sub> appears to be undefined.

Given all this information, the designer can construct a model of the heat flow from the semiconductor junction, where the heat is generated, to the outside world. In our example, the heat has to flow from the junction to the case of the transistor, then from the case to the metalwork. We do not need to consider where the heat goes after that, because we are told that the metalwork will conduct heat fast enough to keep the temperature less than $\Delta T_{\rm HS}$ above ambient: this is all we need to know.

Suppose the engineer wishes to know how much power can be put into the transistor before it overheats. The calculations are as follows.

Total absolute thermal resistance from junction to ambient = $R_{\theta \rm JC}+R_{\theta \rm B}$

where $R_{\theta \rm B}$ is the absolute thermal resistance of the bond between the transistor's case and the metalwork. This figure depends on the nature of the bond - for example, a thermal bonding pad or [thermal transfer grease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_grease) might be used to reduce the absolute thermal resistance.

Maximum temperature drop from junction to ambient = $T_{J\rm max} - (T_{\rm amb} + \Delta T_{\rm HS})$.

We use the general principle that the temperature drop $\Delta T$ across a given absolute thermal resistance $R_{\theta}$ with a given heat flow $\dot{Q}$ through it is:

$$\Delta T = \dot{Q} R_\theta \,.$$

Substituting our own symbols into this formula gives:

$$T_{J\rm max} - (T_{\rm amb} + \Delta T_{\rm HS}) = \dot{Q}_{\rm max} (R_{\theta \rm JC} + R_{\theta \rm B} + R_{\theta \rm HA})\,,$$

and, rearranging,

$$\dot{Q}_{\rm max} = { { T_{J\rm max}-(T_{\rm amb} + \Delta T_{\rm HS}) } \over { R_{\theta \rm JC} + R_{\theta \rm B}+R_{\theta \rm HA} } }$$

The designer now knows $\dot{Q}_{\rm max}$, the maximum power that the transistor can be allowed to dissipate, so they can design the circuit to limit the temperature of the transistor to a safe level.

Let us substitute some sample numbers:

$T_{J\rm max} = 125 \ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ (typical for a silicon transistor)

$T_{\rm amb} = 21 \ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ (a typical specification for commercial equipment)

$R_{\theta \rm JC} = 1.5 \ ^{\circ}\text{C}/\text{W} \,$ (for a typical [TO-220](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TO-220) package)

$R_{\theta \rm B} = 0.1 \ ^{\circ}\text{C}/\text{W} \,$ (a typical value for an [elastomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastomer) heat-transfer pad for a TO-220 package)

$R_{\theta \rm HA} = 4 \ ^{\circ}\text{C}/\text{W} \,$ (a typical value for a heatsink for a TO-220 package)

The result is then:

$$\dot{Q} = {{125\ ^{\circ}\text{C}-(21\ ^{\circ}\text{C})} \over {1.5\ ^{\circ}\text{C}/\text{W}+0.1\ ^{\circ}\text{C}/\text{W}+4\ ^{\circ}\text{C}/\text{W}}} = 18.6 \ \text{W}$$

This means that the transistor can dissipate about 18 watts before it overheats. A cautious designer would operate the transistor at a lower power level to increase its [reliability](wikipedia://en/1724836).

This method can be generalized to include any number of layers of heat-conducting materials, simply by adding together the absolute thermal resistances of the layers and the temperature drops across the layers.

::: {#o18 .statement cue=named-heading name="Derived from Fourier's law for heat conduction"}

### Derived from Fourier's law for heat conduction {#Derived_from_Fourier's_law_for_heat_conduction}

From [Fourier's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_conduction#Fourier's_law) for [heat conduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_conduction), the following equation can be derived, and is valid as long as all of the parameters (x and k) are constant throughout the sample.

$$R_{\theta} = \frac{\Delta x}{A k} = \frac{\Delta x r}{A}$$

where:

- $R_{\theta}$ is the absolute thermal resistance (K/W) across the thickness of the sample
- $\Delta x$ is the thickness (m) of the sample (measured on a path parallel to the heat flow)
- $k$ is the thermal conductivity (W/(K·m)) of the sample
- $r$ is the thermal resistivity (K·m/W) of the sample
- $A$ is the cross-sectional area (m<sup>2</sup>) perpendicular to the path of heat flow.

In terms of the temperature gradient across the sample and [heat flux](wikipedia://en/14581057) through the sample, the relationship is:

$$R_{\theta} = \frac{\Delta x}{A \phi_q}\frac{\Delta T}{\Delta x} = \frac{\Delta T}{q}$$

where:

- $R_\theta$ is the absolute thermal resistance (K/W) across the thickness of the sample,
- $\Delta x$ is the thickness (m) of the sample (measured on a path parallel to the heat flow),
- $\phi_q$ is the [heat flux](wikipedia://en/14581057) through the sample ([W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt)·m<sup>−2</sup>),
- $\frac{\Delta T}{\Delta x}$ is the temperature gradient ([K](wikipedia://en/19593121)·m<sup>−1</sup>) across the sample,
- $A$ is the cross-sectional area (m<sup>2</sup>) perpendicular to the path of heat flow through the sample,
- $\Delta T$ is the temperature difference ([K](wikipedia://en/19593121)) across the sample,
- $q$ is the [rate of heat flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_of_heat_flow) ([W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt)) through the sample.

:::

### Problems with electrical resistance analogy {#Problems_with_electrical_resistance_analogy}

A 2008 review paper written by Philips researcher Clemens J. M. Lasance notes that: "Although there is an analogy between heat flow by conduction (Fourier's law) and the flow of an electric current (Ohm’s law), the corresponding physical properties of thermal conductivity and electrical conductivity conspire to make the behavior of heat flow quite unlike the flow of electricity in normal situations. \[...\] Unfortunately, although the electrical and thermal differential equations are analogous, it is erroneous to conclude that there is any practical analogy between electrical and thermal resistance. This is because a material that is considered an insulator in electrical terms is about 20 orders of magnitude less conductive than a material that is considered a conductor, while, in thermal terms, the difference between an "insulator" and a "conductor" is only about three orders of magnitude. The entire range of thermal conductivity is then equivalent to the difference in electrical conductivity of high-doped and low-doped silicon."[^o22]

## Measurement standards {#Measurement_standards}

The junction-to-air thermal resistance can vary greatly depending on the ambient conditions.[^o21] (A more sophisticated way of expressing the same fact is saying that junction-to-ambient thermal resistance is not Boundary-Condition Independent (BCI).[^o22]) [JEDEC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JEDEC) has a standard (number JESD51-2) for measuring the junction-to-air thermal resistance of electronics packages under [natural convection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_convection) and another standard (number JESD51-6) for measurement under [forced convection](wikipedia://en/3245447).

A JEDEC standard for measuring the junction-to-board thermal resistance (relevant for [surface-mount technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface-mount_technology)) has been published as JESD51-8.[^o23]

A JEDEC standard for measuring the junction-to-case thermal resistance (JESD51-14) is relatively newcomer, having been published in late 2010; it concerns only packages having a single heat flow and an exposed cooling surface.[^o24][^o25][^o26]

## Resistance in composite wall {#Resistance_in_composite_wall}

### Resistances in series {#Resistances_in_series}

When resistances are in series, the total resistance is the sum of the resistances:

$$R_{\rm tot} = R_A + R_B + R_C + ...$$

### Parallel thermal resistance {#Parallel_thermal_resistance}

*Image: Parallel Thermal Resistance.jpg.* Parallel thermal resistance in composite walls

Similarly to electrical circuits, the total thermal resistance for steady state conditions can be calculated as follows.

The total thermal resistance

::: {#o30 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$${ {1 \over R_{\rm tot}} = {1 \over R_B} + {1 \over R_C} } \tag{1}$$

:::

Simplifying the equation, we get

::: {#o31 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$${R_{\rm tot} = {R_B R_C \over R_B + R_C} } \tag{2}$$

:::

With terms for the thermal resistance for conduction, we get

::: {#o32 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$${R_{t,\rm cond} = {L \over (k_b + k_c)A}} \tag{3}$$

:::

### Resistance in series and parallel {#Resistance_in_series_and_parallel}

*Image: Equivalent thermal circuits for series-parallel composite wall.jpg.* Equivalent thermal circuits for series-parallel composite wall

It is often suitable to assume one-dimensional conditions, although the heat flow is multidimensional. Now, two different circuits may be used for this case. For case (a) (shown in picture), we presume [isothermal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isothermal) surfaces for those normal to the x- direction, whereas for case (b) we presume [adiabatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adiabatic) surfaces parallel to the x- direction. We may obtain different results for the total resistance ${R_{tot} }$ and the actual corresponding values of the heat transfer are bracketed by ${q}$. When the multidimensional effects becomes more significant, these differences are increased with increasing ${| k_f - k_g |}$.[^o34]

### Radial systems {#Radial_systems}

Spherical and cylindrical systems may be treated as one-dimensional, due to the [temperature gradients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature_gradient) in the radial direction. The standard method can be used for analyzing radial systems under steady state conditions, starting with the appropriate form of the heat equation, or the alternative method, starting with the appropriate form of [Fourier's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier's_law). For a hollow cylinder in steady state conditions with no heat generation, the appropriate form of heat equation is [^o34]

::: {#o36 .equation cue=numblk name="4"}

$${ {1 \over r}{d\over dr} \left(kr{dT \over dr} \right) = 0 } \tag{4}$$

:::

Where ${k}$ is treated as a variable. Considering the appropriate form of Fourier's law, the physical significance of treating ${k}$ as a variable becomes evident when the rate at which energy is conducted across a cylindrical surface, this is represented as

::: {#o37 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$${ q_r = -kA {dT \over dr} = -k(2\pi rL){dT \over dr}} \tag{5}$$

:::

Where ${A = 2\pi rL}$ is the area that is normal to the direction of where the heat transfer occurs. Equation 1 implies that the quantity ${kr(dT/dr)}$ is not dependent of the radius ${r}$, it follows from equation 5 that the heat transfer rate, ${q_r}$ is a constant in the radial direction.

*Image: Hollow cylinder with convective surface conditions.jpg.* Hollow cylinder with convective surface conditions in thermal conduction

In order to determine the temperature distribution in the cylinder, equation 4 can be solved applying the appropriate [boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_condition). With the assumption that ${k }$ is constant

::: {#o38 .equation cue=numblk name="6"}

$${ T(r) = C_1\ln r + C_2 } \tag{6}$$

:::

Using the following boundary conditions, the constants ${C_1}$ and ${C_2}$ can be computed

${ T(r_1) = T_{s,1} }$ and ${ T(r_2) = T_{s,2} }$

The general solution gives us

${ T_{s,1} = C_1 \ln r_1 + C_2 }$ and ${ T_{s,2} = C_1 \ln r_2 + C_2 }$

Solving for ${C_1}$ and ${C_2}$ and substituting into the general solution, we obtain

::: {#o39 .equation cue=numblk name="7"}

$${ T(r) = {T_{s,1} - T_{s,2} \over {\ln(r_1/r_2) } } \ln \left( {r \over r_2} \right) + T_{s,2} } \tag{7}$$

:::

The logarithmic distribution of the temperature is sketched in the inset of the thumbnail figure. Assuming that the temperature distribution, equation 7, is used with Fourier's law in equation 5, the heat transfer rate can be expressed in the following form

$${\dot{Q}_r = {2\pi Lk (T_{s,1} - T_{s,2}) \over \ln(r_2/r_1)} }$$

Finally, for radial conduction in a cylindrical wall, the thermal resistance is of the form

${ R_{t,\mathrm{cond}} = {\ln(r_2/r_1) \over 2\pi Lk} }$ such that ${r_2 > r_1}$
