# Quaternion algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quaternion algebra"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **quaternion algebra** over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *F* is a [central simple algebra](wikipedia://en/404082) *A* over *F*[^o2] that has [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) 4 over *F*. Every quaternion algebra becomes a [matrix algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_algebra) by *[extending scalars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_extension)* (equivalently, [tensoring](wikipedia://en/372395) with a [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634)), i.e. for a suitable field extension *K* of *F*, $A \otimes_F K$ is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the 2 × 2 matrix algebra over *K*.

:::

The notion of a quaternion algebra can be seen as a generalization of Hamilton's [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) to an arbitrary base field. The Hamilton quaternions are a quaternion algebra (in the above sense) over $F = \mathbb{R}$, and indeed the only one over $\mathbb{R}$ apart from the 2 × 2 [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) matrix algebra, up to isomorphism. When $F = \mathbb{C}$, then the [biquaternions](wikipedia://en/1207070) form the quaternion algebra over *F*.

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Split quaternion algebra"}

## Structure {#Structure}

*Quaternion algebra* here means something more general than the [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) of Hamilton's [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440). When the coefficient [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *F* does not have [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) 2, every quaternion algebra over *F* can be described as a 4-dimensional *F*-[vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) with [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) $\{ 1, i, j, k\}$, with the following multiplication rules:

$$i^2=a$$

$$j^2=b$$

$$ij=k$$

$$ji=-k$$

where *a* and *b* are any given nonzero elements of *F*. From these rules we get:[^o5]

$$k^2=ijij=-iijj=-ab.$$

The classical instances where $F=\mathbb{R}$ are Hamilton's quaternions (*a* = *b* = −1) and [split-quaternions](wikipedia://en/1461265) (*a* = −1, *b* = +1). In split-quaternions, $k^2 = +1$ and $j k = - i$, differing from Hamilton's equations.

The algebra defined in this way is denoted (*a*,*b*)<sub>*F*</sub> or simply (*a*,*b*).[^o6] When *F* has characteristic 2, a different explicit description in terms of a basis of 4 elements is also possible, but in any event the definition of a quaternion algebra over *F* as a 4-dimensional central simple algebra over *F* applies uniformly in all characteristics.

For $q = t + xi + yj +zk$ with *conjugate* $q^* = t - xi - yj - zk \ ,$ the [anticommutative property](wikipedia://en/294358) of i, j, and k imply $qq^* = t^2 - a x^2 - b y^2 + ab z^2 .$ A quaternion algebra (*a*,*b*)<sub>*F*</sub> is either a [division algebra](wikipedia://en/26549625) or isomorphic to the [matrix algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_algebra) of 2 × 2 matrices over *F*; the latter case is a [split algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_algebra).[^o7] The *norm form*

$$N(t + xi +yj + zk) = qq^* = t^2 - ax^2 - by^2 + abz^2 \$$

defines a structure of [division algebra](wikipedia://en/26549625) if and only if the norm is an [anisotropic quadratic form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anisotropic_quadratic_form), that is, zero only on the zero element. For then $q^* / N(q)$ is the [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) of *q*.

The [conic](wikipedia://en/19008673) *C*(*a*,*b*) defined by

$$a x^2 + b y^2 = z^2 \$$

has a point (*x*,*y*,*z*) with coordinates in *F* in the split case.[^o8]

:::

## Application {#Application}

Quaternion algebras are applied in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), particularly to [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478). They are concrete structures that generate the elements of [order](wikipedia://en/493527) two in the [Brauer group](wikipedia://en/404078) of *F*. For some fields, including [algebraic number fields](wikipedia://en/28730822), every element of order 2 in its Brauer group is represented by a quaternion algebra. A theorem of [Alexander Merkurjev](wikipedia://en/31902178) implies that each element of order 2 in the Brauer group of any field is represented by a [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) of quaternion algebras.[^o11] In particular, over [*p*-adic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_field) the construction of quaternion algebras can be viewed as the quadratic [Hilbert symbol](wikipedia://en/5924217) of [local class field theory](wikipedia://en/669532).

:::

## Classification {#Classification}

The products of a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) of *F*<sup>4</sup>, some of which respect the [anti-commutative property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-commutative_property), generate a [non-abelian group](wikipedia://en/4791400) of at least [order](wikipedia://en/493527) 8. When limited to 8, there are just two groups that serve: the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130) and the [dihedral group of order 8](wikipedia://en/78749515). The products of an arbitrary pair from *F*<sup>4</sup> are determined by the group generated by the basis. When Q<sub>8</sub> or D<sub>4</sub> is selected for the algebra on *F*<sup>4</sup>, a quaternion algebra over *F* is determined.

In a similar way, over any [local field](wikipedia://en/145375) *F* there are exactly two quaternion algebras: the 2 × 2 matrices over *F* and a division algebra. But the quaternion division algebra over a local field is usually *not* Hamilton's quaternions over the field. For example, over the [*p*-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423) Hamilton's quaternions are a division algebra only when *p* is 2. For odd [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) *p*, the *p*-adic Hamilton quaternions are isomorphic to the 2 × 2 matrices over the *p*-adics. To see the *p*-adic Hamilton quaternions are not a division algebra for odd prime *p*, observe that the [congruence](wikipedia://en/20087) *x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> = −1 mod *p* is solvable and therefore by [Hensel's lemma](wikipedia://en/1633368) — here is where *p* being odd is needed — the equation

*x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> = −1

is solvable in the *p*-adic numbers. Therefore the quaternion

*xi* + *yj* + *k*

has norm 0 and hence doesn't have a [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940).

One way to classify the *F*-algebra [isomorphism classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_class) of all quaternion algebras for a given field *F* is to use the one-to-one correspondence between isomorphism classes of quaternion algebras over *F* and isomorphism classes of their *norm forms*.

To every quaternion algebra *A*, one can associate a quadratic form *N* (called the *[norm form](wikipedia://en/7204666)*) on *A* such that

$$N(xy) = N(x)N(y)$$

for all *x* and *y* in *A*. It turns out that the possible norm forms for quaternion *F*-algebras are exactly the [Pfister 2-forms](wikipedia://en/7204602).

## Quaternion algebras over the rational numbers {#Quaternion_algebras_over_the_rational_numbers}

Quaternion algebras over the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) have an arithmetic theory similar to, but more complicated than, that of [quadratic extensions of $\mathbb{Q}$](wikipedia://en/454781).

Let $B$ be a quaternion algebra over $\mathbb{Q}$ and let $\nu$ be a [place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place_%28mathematics%29) of $\mathbb{Q}$, with completion $\mathbb{Q}_\nu$ (so it is either the *p*-adic numbers $\mathbb{Q}_p$ for some prime *p* or the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$). Define $B_\nu:= \mathbb{Q}_\nu \otimes_{\mathbb{Q}} B$, which is a quaternion algebra over $\mathbb{Q}_\nu$. So there are two choices for $B_\nu$: the 2 × 2 matrices over $\mathbb{Q}_\nu$ or a [division algebra](wikipedia://en/26549625).

We say that $B$ is **split** (or **unramified**) at $\nu$ if $B_\nu$ is isomorphic to the 2 × 2 matrices over $\mathbb{Q}_\nu$. We say that *B* is **non-split** (or **ramified**) at $\nu$ if $B_\nu$ is the quaternion division algebra over $\mathbb{Q}_\nu$. For example, the rational Hamilton quaternions is non-split at 2 and at $\infty$ and split at all odd primes. The rational 2 × 2 matrices are split at all places.

A quaternion algebra over the rationals which splits at $\infty$ is analogous to a [real quadratic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_quadratic_field) and one which is non-split at $\infty$ is analogous to an [imaginary quadratic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_quadratic_field). The analogy comes from a quadratic field having real embeddings when the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106) for a generator splits over the reals and having non-real embeddings otherwise. One illustration of the strength of this analogy concerns [unit groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_group) in an order of a rational quaternion algebra: it is infinite if the quaternion algebra splits at $\infty$ and it is finite otherwise, just as the unit group of an order in a quadratic ring is infinite in the real quadratic case and finite otherwise.

The number of places where a quaternion algebra over the rationals ramifies is always even, and this is equivalent to the [quadratic reciprocity law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_reciprocity_law) over the rationals. Moreover, the places where *B* ramifies determines *B* up to isomorphism as an algebra. (In other words, non-isomorphic quaternion algebras over the rationals do not share the same set of ramified places.) The product of the primes at which *B* ramifies is called the **discriminant** of *B*.
