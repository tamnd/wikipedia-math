::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Goldberg polyhedron"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and more specifically in [polyhedral combinatorics](wikipedia://en/19696519), a **Goldberg polyhedron** is a [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron) made from [hexagons](wikipedia://en/59733) and [pentagons](wikipedia://en/20741014). They were first described in 1937 by [Michael Goldberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Goldberg_%28mathematician%29) (1902–1990). They are defined by three properties: each [face](wikipedia://en/149926) is either a pentagon or hexagon, exactly three faces meet at each [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417), and they have [rotational icosahedral symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_icosahedral_symmetry). They are not necessarily [mirror-symmetric](wikipedia://en/1390873); e.g. $\operatorname{GP}(5,3)$ and $\operatorname{GP}(3,5)$ are [enantiomorphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enantiomorph) of each other. A Goldberg polyhedron is a [dual polyhedron](wikipedia://en/8815) of a [geodesic polyhedron](wikipedia://en/53024913).

:::

A consequence of [Euler's polyhedron formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_polyhedron_formula) is that a Goldberg polyhedron always has exactly 12 pentagonal faces. Icosahedral symmetry ensures that the pentagons are always [regular](wikipedia://en/333306) and that there are always 12 of them. If the vertices are not constrained to a sphere, the polyhedron can be constructed with planar equilateral (but not in general equiangular) faces.

Simple examples of Goldberg polyhedra include the [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407) and [truncated icosahedron](wikipedia://en/31282). Other forms can be described by taking a [chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess) [knight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_%28chess%29) move from one pentagon to the next: first take $m$ steps in one direction, then turn 60° to the left and take $n$ steps. Such a polyhedron is denoted $\operatorname{GP}(m,n)$. A dodecahedron is $\operatorname{GP}(1,0)$, and a [truncated icosahedron](wikipedia://en/31282) is $\operatorname{GP}(1,1)$.

A similar technique can be applied to construct polyhedra with [tetrahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2904472) and [octahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2900805). These polyhedra will have triangles or squares rather than pentagons. These variations are given Roman numeral subscripts denoting the number of sides on the non-hexagon faces: $\operatorname{GP}_{\operatorname{III}}(n,m)$, $\operatorname{GP}_{\operatorname{IV}}(n,m)$, and $\operatorname{GP}_{V}(n,m)$.

## Elements {#Elements}

The number of vertices, edges, and faces of $\operatorname{GP}(m,n)$ can be computed from $m$ and $n$, with

$$T = m^2 + mn + n^2 = (m + n)^2 - mn,$$

depending on one of three symmetry systems:[^o4] The number of non-hexagonal faces can be determined using the Euler characteristic, as demonstrated [here](wikipedia://en/148420#Soccer_ball).

## Construction {#Construction}

Most Goldberg polyhedra can be constructed using [Conway polyhedron notation](wikipedia://en/5021705) starting with (T)etrahedron, (C)ube, and (D)odecahedron seeds. The [chamfer](wikipedia://en/43468103) operator, $c$, replaces all edges by hexagons, transforming $\operatorname{GP}(m,n)$ to $\operatorname{GP}(2m,2n)$, with a $T$ multiplier of 4. The *truncated kis* operator, $y = tk$, generates $\operatorname{GP}(3,0)$, transforming $\operatorname{GP}(m,n)$ to $\operatorname{GP}(3m,3n)$, with a $T$ multiplier of 9.

For class 2 forms, the *dual kis* operator, $z = dk$, transforms $\operatorname{GP}(a,0)$ into $\operatorname{GP}(a,a)$, with a $T$ multiplier of 3. For class 3 forms, the *whirl* operator, $w$, generates $\operatorname{GP}(2,1)$, with a $T$ multiplier of 7. A clockwise and counterclockwise whirl generator, $w\overline{w} = wrw$ generates $\operatorname{GP}(7,0)$ in class 1. In general, a whirl can transform a $\operatorname{GP}(a,b)$ into $\operatorname{GP}(a + 3b, 2ab)$ for $a > b$ and the same chiral direction. If chiral directions are reversed, $\operatorname{GP}(a, b)$ becomes $\operatorname{GP}(2a + 3b, a - 2b)$ if $a \ge 2b$, and $\operatorname{GP}(3a + b, 2b - a)$ if $a < 2b$.

:::
