# Inverse Galois problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Inverse Galois problem"}

::: {#o2 .problem cue=unsolved}

Is every [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of a [Galois extension](wikipedia://en/457064) of the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024)?

:::

In [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316), the **inverse Galois problem** concerns whether or not every [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) appears as the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of some [Galois extension](wikipedia://en/457064) of the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) $\mathbb{Q}$. This problem, first posed in the early 19th century,[^o3] is unsolved.

:::

There are some [permutation groups](wikipedia://en/24634) for which [generic polynomials](wikipedia://en/1400173) are known, which define all [algebraic extensions](wikipedia://en/2125) of $\mathbb{Q}$ having a particular [group](wikipedia://en/19447) as Galois group. These groups include all of degree no greater than $5$. There also are groups known not to have generic polynomials, such as the cyclic group of [order](wikipedia://en/493527) $8$.

More generally, let $G$ be a given finite group, and $K$ a field. If there is a Galois extension field $L/K$ whose Galois group is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to $G$, one says that **$G$ is realizable over $K$**.

## Partial results {#Partial_results}

Many cases are known. It is known that every finite group is realizable over any [function field](wikipedia://en/2783843) in one variable over the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\mathbb{C}$, and more generally over function fields in one variable over any [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) zero. [Igor Shafarevich](wikipedia://en/1091815) showed that every finite [solvable group](wikipedia://en/105012) is realizable over $\mathbb{Q}$.[^o5] It is also known that every simple [sporadic group](wikipedia://en/324806) is realizable over $\mathbb{Q}$.[^o6][^o7]

[David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) showed that this question is related to a [rationality question](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationality_question) for $G$:

If $K$ is any extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ on which $G$ acts as an [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772), and the [invariant field](wikipedia://en/363762) $K^{G}$ is rational over $\mathbb{Q}$, then $G$ is realizable over $\mathbb{Q}$.

Here *rational* means that it is a [purely transcendental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purely_transcendental) extension of $\mathbb{Q}$, generated by an [algebraically independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_independent) set. This criterion can for example be used to show that all the [symmetric groups](wikipedia://en/28901) are realizable.

Much detailed work has been carried out on the question, which is in no sense solved in general. Some of this is based on constructing $G$ geometrically as a [Galois covering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_covering) of the [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578): in algebraic terms, starting with an extension of the field $\mathbb{Q}(t)$ of [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) in an indeterminate $t$. After that, one applies [Hilbert's irreducibility theorem](wikipedia://en/1951153) to specialise $t$, in such a way as to preserve the Galois group.

All 4,953 transitive permutation groups of degree 23 or less are known to be realizable over $\mathbb{Q}$.[^o8][^o9][^o7]

There are 25,000 distinct transitive permutation groups of degree $n=24$. As of June 2026, only 286 of them have been shown to be realizable over $\mathbb{Q}$.[^o8]

All 13 non-[abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) [simple groups](wikipedia://en/47600) smaller than PSL(2,25) (order 7800) are known to be realizable over $\mathbb{Q}$.[^o10]

## A simple example: cyclic groups {#A_simple_example:_cyclic_groups}

It is possible, using classical results, to construct explicitly a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) whose Galois group over $\mathbb{Q}$ is the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) $\mathbf{Z}/n\mathbf{Z}$ for any positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $n$. To do this, choose a [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) $p$ such that $p \equiv 1\pmod{n}$; this is possible by [Dirichlet's theorem](wikipedia://en/101453). Let $\mathbf{Q}(\mu)$ be the [cyclotomic extension](wikipedia://en/30872462#Cyclotomic_fields) of $\mathbb{Q}$ generated by $\mu$, where $\mu$ is a primitive $p$-th [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950); the Galois group of $\mathbf{Q}(\mu)/\mathbf{Q}$ is cyclic of order $p - 1$.

Since $n$ [divides](wikipedia://en/49492) $p - 1$, the Galois group has a cyclic [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) $H$ of order $(p - 1)/n$. The [fundamental theorem of Galois theory](wikipedia://en/1947635) implies that the corresponding fixed field, $F = \mathbf{Q}(\mu)^{H}$, has Galois group $\mathbf{Z}/n\mathbf{Z}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. By taking appropriate sums of conjugates of $\mu$, following the construction of [Gaussian periods](wikipedia://en/456085), one can find an element $\alpha$ of $F$ that generates $F$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, and compute its [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106).

This method can be extended to cover all finite [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974), since every such group appears in fact as a quotient of the Galois group of some cyclotomic extension of $\mathbb{Q}$. (This statement should not though be confused with the [Kronecker–Weber theorem](wikipedia://en/1082550), which lies significantly deeper.)

### Worked example: the cyclic group of order three {#Worked_example:_the_cyclic_group_of_order_three}

For $n = 3$, we may take $p = 7$. Then $\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbf{Q}(\mu)/\mathbf{Q})$ is cyclic of order six. Let us take the generator $\eta$ of this group which sends $\mu$ to $\mu^{3}$. We are interested in the subgroup $H = \{1, \eta^{3}$} of order two. Consider the element $\alpha = \mu + \eta^{3}(\mu)$. By construction, $\alpha$ is fixed by $H$, and only has three conjugates over $\mathbb{Q}$:

$$\alpha = \eta^{0}(\alpha) = \mu + \mu^{6},$$

$$\beta = \eta^{1}(\alpha) = \mu^{3} + \mu^{4},$$

$$\gamma = \eta^{2}(\alpha) = \mu^{2} + \mu^{5}.$$

Using the identity:

$$1 + \mu + \mu^{2} + \cdots + \mu^{6} = 0,$$

one finds that

$$\alpha + \beta + \gamma = -1,$$

$$\alpha\beta + \beta\gamma + \gamma\alpha = -2,$$

$$\alpha\beta\gamma = 1.$$

Therefore $\alpha$ is a [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) of the polynomial

$$(x - \alpha)(x - \beta)(x - \gamma) = x^{3} + x^{2} - 2x - 1,$$

which consequently has Galois group $\mathbf{Z}/3\mathbf{Z}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.

## Symmetric and alternating groups {#Symmetric_and_alternating_groups}

[Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) showed that all symmetric and [alternating](wikipedia://en/49726) groups are represented as Galois groups of polynomials with rational [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203).

The polynomial $x^{n} + ax + b$ has discriminant

$$(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} \!\left( n^n b^{n-1} + (-1)^{1-n} (n-1)^{n-1} a^n \right)\!.$$

We take the special case

$$f(x, s) = x^{n} - sx - s.$$

Substituting a prime integer for $s$ in $f(x, s)$ gives a polynomial (called a **specialization** of $f(x, s)$) that by [Eisenstein's criterion](wikipedia://en/385989) is [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725). Then $f(x, s)$ must be irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}(s)$. Furthermore, $f(x, s)$ can be written

$$x^n - \tfrac{x}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2} - \left( s - \tfrac{1}{2} \right)\!(x+1)$$

and $f(x, 1/2)$ can be factored to:

$$\tfrac{1}{2} (x-1)\!\left( 1+ 2x + 2x^2 + \cdots + 2 x^{n-1} \right)$$

whose second factor is irreducible (but not by Eisenstein's criterion). Only the reciprocal polynomial is irreducible by Eisenstein's criterion. We have now shown that the group $\operatorname{Gal}(f(x, s)/\mathbf{Q}(s))$ is [doubly transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_transitive).

We can then find that this Galois group has a transposition. Use the scaling $(1 - n)x = ny$ to get

$$y^n - \left \{ s \left ( \frac{1-n}{n} \right )^{n-1} \right \} y - \left \{ s \left ( \frac{1-n}{n} \right )^n \right \}$$

and with

$$t = \frac{s (1-n)^{n-1}}{n^n},$$

we arrive at:

$$g(y, t) = y^{n} - nty + (n - 1)t$$

which can be arranged to

$$y^{n} - y - (n - 1)(y - 1) + (t - 1)(-ny + n - 1).$$

Then $g(y, 1)$ has $1$ as a [double zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_root) and its other $n - 2$ zeros are [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_zero), and a transposition in $\operatorname{Gal}(f(x, s)/\mathbf{Q}(s))$ is implied. Any finite [doubly transitive permutation group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_transitive_permutation_group) containing a transposition is a full symmetric group.

[Hilbert's irreducibility theorem](wikipedia://en/1951153) then implies that an [infinite set](wikipedia://en/142679) of rational numbers give specializations of $f(x, t)$ whose Galois groups are $S_{n}$ over the rational field $\mathbb{Q}$. In fact this set of rational numbers is dense in $\mathbb{Q}$.

The discriminant of $g(y, t)$ equals

$$(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} n^n (n-1)^{n-1} t^{n-1} (1-t),$$

and this is not in general a perfect square.

### Alternating groups {#Alternating_groups}

Solutions for alternating groups must be handled differently for [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) and [even](wikipedia://en/143135) degrees.

#### Odd degree {#Odd_degree}

Let

$$t = 1 - (-1)^{\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}} n u^2$$

Under this substitution the discriminant of $g(y, t)$ equals

$$\begin{aligned} (-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} n^n (n-1)^{n-1} t^{n-1} (1-t) &= (-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} n^n (n-1)^{n-1} t^{n-1} \left (1 - \left (1 - (-1)^{\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}} n u^2 \right ) \right) \\ &= (-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} n^n (n-1)^{n-1} t^{n-1} \left ((-1)^{\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}} n u^2 \right ) \\ &= n^{n+1} (n-1)^{n-1} t^{n-1} u^2 \end{aligned}$$

which is a perfect square when $n$ is odd.

#### Even degree {#Even_degree}

Let:

$$t = \frac{1}{1 + (-1)^{\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}} (n-1) u^2}$$

Under this substitution the discriminant of $g(y, t)$ equals:

$$\begin{aligned} (-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} n^n (n-1)^{n-1} t^{n-1} (1-t) &= (-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} n^n (n-1)^{n-1} t^{n-1} \left (1 - \frac{1}{1 + (-1)^{\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}} (n-1) u^2} \right ) \\ &= (-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} n^n (n-1)^{n-1} t^{n-1} \left ( \frac{\left ( 1 + (-1)^{\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}} (n-1) u^2 \right ) - 1}{1 + (-1)^{\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}} (n-1) u^2} \right ) \\ &= (-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} n^n (n-1)^{n-1} t^{n-1} \left ( \frac{(-1)^{\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}} (n-1) u^2}{1 + (-1)^{\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}} (n-1) u^2} \right ) \\ &= (-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} n^n (n-1)^{n-1} t^{n-1} \left (t (-1)^{\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}} (n-1) u^2 \right ) \\ &= n^n (n-1)^n t^n u^2 \end{aligned}$$

which is a perfect square when $n$ is even.

Again, Hilbert's irreducibility theorem implies the existence of infinitely many specializations whose Galois groups are alternating groups.

## Rigid groups {#Rigid_groups}

Suppose that $C_{1}, \ldots, C_{n}$ are [conjugacy classes](wikipedia://en/49176) of a finite group $G$, and $A$ be the set of $n$-tuples $(g_{1}, \ldots, g_{n})$ of $G$ such that $g_{i}$ is in $C_{i}$ and the product $g_{1}\ldots g_{n}$ is trivial. Then $A$ is called **rigid** if it is [nonempty](wikipedia://en/9566), $G$ acts transitively on it by conjugation, and each element of $A$ generates $G$.

(Thompson 1984) showed that if a finite group $G$ has a rigid set then it can often be realized as a Galois group over a cyclotomic extension of the rationals. (More precisely, over the cyclotomic extension of the rationals generated by the values of the irreducible characters of $G$ on the conjugacy classes $C_{i}$.)

This can be used to show that many finite simple groups, including the [monster group](wikipedia://en/47422), are Galois groups of extensions of the rationals. The monster group is generated by a triad of elements of orders $2$, $3$, and $29$. All such triads are conjugate.

The prototype for rigidity is the symmetric group $S_{n}$, which is generated by an $n$-cycle and a transposition whose product is an $(n - 1)$-cycle. The construction in the preceding section used these generators to establish a polynomial's Galois group.

## A construction with an elliptic modular function {#A_construction_with_an_elliptic_modular_function}

Let $n > 1$ be any integer. A lattice $\Lambda$ in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) with period ratio $\tau$ has a sublattice $\Lambda'$ with period ratio $n\tau$. The latter lattice is one of a finite set of sublattices permuted by the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019) $\operatorname{PSL}(2, \mathbf{Z})$, which is based on changes of basis for $\Lambda$. Let $j$ denote the [elliptic modular function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_modular_function) of [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887). Define the polynomial $\varphi_{n}$ as the product of the differences $(X - j(\Lambda_{i}))$ over the conjugate sublattices. As a polynomial in $X$, $\varphi_{n}$ has coefficients that are polynomials over $\mathbb{Q}$ in $j(\tau)$.

On the conjugate lattices, the modular group acts as $\operatorname{PGL}(2, \mathbf{Z}/n\mathbf{Z})$. It follows that $\varphi_{n}$ has Galois group isomorphic to $\operatorname{PGL}(2, \mathbf{Z}/n\mathbf{Z})$ over $\mathbb{Q}(\mathrm{J}(\tau))$.

Use of Hilbert's irreducibility theorem gives an infinite (and dense) set of rational numbers specializing $\varphi_{n}$ to polynomials with Galois group $\operatorname{PGL}(2, \mathbf{Z}/n\mathbf{Z})$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. The groups $\operatorname{PGL}(2, \mathbf{Z}/n\mathbf{Z})$ include infinitely many non-solvable groups.
