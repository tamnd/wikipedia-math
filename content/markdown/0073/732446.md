# Lorentz factor

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lorentz factor"}

*Image: RelativeFactor.png.* Definition of the Lorentz factor γ

The **Lorentz factor** or **Lorentz term** (also known as the **gamma factor**[^o2]) is a [dimensionless quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless_quantity) expressing how much the measurements of time, length, and other physical properties change for an object while it moves. The expression appears in several equations in [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962), and it arises in derivations of the [Lorentz transformations](wikipedia://en/18404). The name originates from its earlier appearance in [Lorentzian electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_ether_theory) – named after the [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands) physicist [Hendrik Lorentz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lorentz).[^o3]

:::

It is generally denoted $\gamma$ (the Greek lowercase letter [gamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma)). Sometimes (especially in discussion of [superluminal motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superluminal_motion)) the factor is written as $\Gamma$ (Greek uppercase-gamma) rather than $\gamma$.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The Lorentz factor $\gamma$ is defined as[^o6]

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}} = \frac{dt}{d\tau} ,$$

where:

- $v$ is the [relative velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_velocity) between inertial reference frames,
- $c$ is the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) in vacuum,
- $\beta$ is the ratio of $v$ to $c$,
- $t$ is [coordinate time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_time),
- $\tau$ is the [proper time](wikipedia://en/494418) for an observer (measuring time intervals in the observer's own frame).

This is the most frequently used form in practice, though not the only one (see below for alternative forms).

*Image: lorentz\_factor\_pythagorean\_theorem.svg.* Lorentz factor depicted as a right triangle in a quadrant of radius 1.[^o7]

To complement the definition, some authors define the reciprocal[^o8]

$$\alpha = \frac{1}{\gamma} = \sqrt{1- \frac{v^2}{c^2}} \ = \sqrt{1- {\beta}^2} ;$$

see [velocity addition formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity_addition_formula).

:::

## Occurrence {#Occurrence}

Following is a list of formulae from Special relativity which use $\gamma$ as a shorthand:[^o6][^o10]

- The **[Lorentz transformation](wikipedia://en/18404):** The simplest case is a boost in the $x$-direction (more general forms including arbitrary directions and rotations not listed here), which describes how spacetime coordinates change from one inertial frame using coordinates $(x, y, z, t)$ to another $(x, y, z, t)$ with relative velocity $v$: $$\begin{aligned} t' &= \gamma \left( t - \tfrac{vx}{c^2} \right ), \\[1ex] x' &= \gamma \left( x - vt \right ). \end{aligned}$$

Corollaries of the above transformations are the results:

- **[Time dilation](wikipedia://en/297839):** The time ($\text{∆}t$) between two ticks as measured in the frame in which the clock is moving, is longer than the time ($\text{∆}t$) between these ticks as measured in the rest frame of the clock: $$\Delta t' = \gamma \Delta t.$$
- **[Length contraction](wikipedia://en/339024):** The length ($\text{∆}x$) of an object as measured in the frame in which it is moving, is shorter than its length ($\text{∆}x$) in its own rest frame: $$\Delta x' = \Delta x/\gamma.$$

Applying [conservation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_law_%28physics%29) of [momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_linear_momentum) and energy leads to these results:

- **[Relativistic mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_mass):** The relativistic mass $m$ of an object in motion is dependent on $\gamma$ and the [rest mass](wikipedia://en/162321) $m_{0}$: $$m = \gamma m_0.$$
- **[Relativistic momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_momentum):** The relativistic [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431) relation takes the same form as for classical momentum, but using the above relativistic mass: $$\vec p = m \vec v = \gamma m_0 \vec v.$$
- **[Relativistic kinetic energy](wikipedia://en/17327#Relativistic_kinetic_energy_of_rigid_bodies):** The relativistic kinetic [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) relation takes the slightly modified form: $$E_k = E - E_0 = (\gamma - 1) m_0 c^2$$ As $\gamma$ is a function of $\tfrac{v}{c}$, the non-relativistic limit gives $\lim_{v/c\to 0}E_k=\tfrac{1}{2}m_0v^2$, as expected from Newtonian considerations.

## Alternative representations {#Alternative_representations}

There are other ways to write the factor. Above, velocity $v$ was used, but related variables such as [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431) and [rapidity](wikipedia://en/4520673) may also be convenient.

### Momentum {#Momentum}

Solving the previous relativistic momentum equation for $\gamma$ leads to

$$\gamma = \sqrt{1+\left ( \frac{p}{m_0 c} \right )^2 } \,.$$

This form is rarely used, although it does appear in the [Maxwell–Jüttner distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell–Jüttner_distribution).[^o15]

### Rapidity {#Rapidity}

Applying the definition of [rapidity](wikipedia://en/4520673) as the [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) $\varphi$:[^o17]

$$\tanh \varphi = \beta$$

also leads to $\gamma$ (by use of [hyperbolic identities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function#Useful_relations)):

$$\gamma = \cosh \varphi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \tanh^2 \varphi}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}}.$$

Using the property of [Lorentz transformation](wikipedia://en/18404), it can be shown that rapidity is additive, a useful property that velocity does not have. Thus the rapidity parameter forms a [one-parameter group](wikipedia://en/754851), a foundation for physical models.

### Bessel function {#Bessel_function}

The Bunney identity represents the Lorentz factor in terms of an infinite series of [Bessel functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessel_functions):[^o19]

$$\sum_{m=1}^\infty \left(J^2_{m-1}(m\beta)+J^2_{m+1}(m\beta)\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}}.$$

### Series expansion (velocity) {#Series_expansion_(velocity)}

The Lorentz factor has the following [Maclaurin series](wikipedia://en/30448):

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma & = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}} \\[1ex] & = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \beta^{2n}\prod_{k=1}^n \left(\dfrac{2k - 1}{2k}\right) \\[1ex] & = 1 + \tfrac12 \beta^2 + \tfrac38 \beta^4 + \tfrac{5}{16} \beta^6 + \tfrac{35}{128} \beta^8 + \tfrac{63}{256} \beta^{10} + \cdots , \end{aligned}$$

which is a special case of a [binomial series](wikipedia://en/696619).

The approximation $\gamma \approx 1 + \frac{1}{2}\beta^2$ may be used to calculate relativistic effects at low speeds. It holds to within 1% error for $v$ \< 0.4 $c$ ($v$ \< 120,000 km/s), and to within 0.1% error for $v$ \< 0.22 $c$ ($v$ \< 66,000 km/s).

The truncated versions of this series also allow [physicists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist) to prove that [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) reduces to [Newtonian mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_mechanics) at low speeds. For example, in special relativity, the following two equations hold:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf p & = \gamma m \mathbf v, \\ E & = \gamma m c^2. \end{aligned}$$

For $\gamma \approx 1$ and $\gamma \approx 1 + \frac{1}{2}\beta^2$, respectively, these reduce to their Newtonian equivalents:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf p & = m \mathbf v, \\ E & = m c^2 + \tfrac12 m v^2. \end{aligned}$$

The Lorentz factor equation can also be inverted to yield

$$\beta = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{\gamma^2}} .$$

This has an asymptotic form

$$\beta = 1 - \tfrac12 \gamma^{-2} - \tfrac18 \gamma^{-4} - \tfrac{1}{16} \gamma^{-6} - \tfrac{5}{128} \gamma^{-8} + \cdots\,.$$

The first two terms are occasionally used to quickly calculate velocities from large $\gamma$ values. The approximation $\beta \approx 1 - \frac{1}{2}\gamma^{-2}$ holds to within 1% tolerance for $\gamma > 2$, and to within 0.1% tolerance for $\gamma > 3.5$.

## Applications in astronomy {#Applications_in_astronomy}

The standard model of long-duration gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) holds that these explosions are ultra-relativistic (initial $\gamma$ greater than approximately 100), which is invoked to explain the so-called "compactness" problem: absent this ultra-relativistic expansion, the ejecta would be optically thick to pair production at typical peak spectral energies of a few 100 keV, whereas the prompt emission is observed to be non-thermal.[^o22]

[Muons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muon), a subatomic particle, travel at a speed such that they have a relatively high Lorentz factor and therefore experience extreme [time dilation](wikipedia://en/297839). Since muons have a mean lifetime of just 2.2 [μs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsecond), muons generated from [cosmic-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_ray) collisions 10 high in Earth's atmosphere should be nondetectable on the ground due to their decay rate. However, roughly 10% of muons from these collisions are still detectable on the surface, thereby demonstrating the effects of time dilation on their decay rate.[^o23]
