::: {#o4 .subject cue=lead name="Uniform polytope"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **uniform polytope** of dimension three or higher is a [vertex-transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex-transitive) [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) bounded by uniform [facets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facet_%28mathematics%29). Here, "vertex-transitive" means that it has symmetries taking every vertex to every other vertex; the same must also be true within each lower-dimensional face of the polytope. In two dimensions (and for two-dimensional faces of higher-dimensional polytopes) a stronger definition is used: only the [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306) are considered as uniform, disallowing polygons that alternate between two different lengths of edges.

:::

This is a generalization of the older category of [*semiregular* polytopes](wikipedia://en/11684875), but also includes the [regular polytopes](wikipedia://en/518693). Further, star regular faces and [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719) ([star polygons](wikipedia://en/313303)) are allowed, which greatly expand the possible solutions. A strict definition requires uniform polytopes to be finite, while a more expansive definition allows [uniform honeycombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_honeycomb) (2-dimensional [tilings](wikipedia://en/12126635) and higher dimensional [honeycombs](wikipedia://en/6008654)) of [Euclidean](wikipedia://en/9697) and [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742) to be considered polytopes as well.

## Operations {#Operations}

Nearly every uniform polytope can be generated by a [Wythoff construction](wikipedia://en/2950777), and represented by a [Coxeter diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram). Notable exceptions include the [great dirhombicosidodecahedron](wikipedia://en/2910030) in three dimensions and the [grand antiprism](wikipedia://en/4058047) in four dimensions.

Equivalently, the Wythoffian polytopes can be generated by applying basic operations to the regular polytopes in that dimension. This approach was first used by [Johannes Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736), and is the basis of the [Conway polyhedron notation](wikipedia://en/5021705).

### Rectification operators {#Rectification_operators}

Regular n-polytopes have *n* orders of [rectification](wikipedia://en/2192366). The zeroth rectification is the original form. The (*n*−1)-th rectification is the [dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_polytope). A **rectification** reduces edges to vertices, a **birectification** reduces faces to vertices, a **trirectification** reduces cells to vertices, a **quadirectification** reduces 4-faces to vertices, a **quintirectification** reduced 5-faces to vertices, and so on.

An extended [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) can be used for representing rectified forms, with a single subscript:

- *k*-th rectification = **t<sub>k</sub>**{p<sub>1</sub>, p<sub>2</sub>, ..., p<sub>n−1</sub>} = *k***r**.

::: {#o8 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Biheptellation"}

### Truncation operators {#Truncation_operators}

Truncation operations that can be applied to regular *n*-polytopes in any combination. The resulting Coxeter diagram has two ringed nodes, and the operation is named for the distance between them. **Truncation** cuts vertices, **cantellation** cuts edges, **runcination** cuts faces, **sterication** cut cells. Each higher operation also cuts lower ones too, so a cantellation also truncates vertices.

1. **t<sub>0,1</sub>** or **t**: [**Truncation**](wikipedia://en/4224429) - applied to [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621) and higher. A truncation removes vertices, and inserts a new facet in place of each former vertex. Faces are truncated, doubling their edges. (The term, coined by [Kepler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler), comes from Latin *truncare* 'to cut off'.)

*Image: Cube truncation sequence.svg.*

   - There are higher truncations also: **[bitruncation](wikipedia://en/6792316)** **t<sub>1,2</sub>** or **2t**, **tritruncation** **t<sub>2,3</sub>** or **3t**, **quadritruncation** **t<sub>3,4</sub>** or **4t**, **quintitruncation** **t<sub>4,5</sub>** or **5t**, etc.
1. **t<sub>0,2</sub>** or **rr**: [**Cantellation**](wikipedia://en/6317514) - applied to [polyhedra](wikipedia://en/2537223) and higher. It can be seen as rectifying its [rectification](wikipedia://en/2192366). A cantellation truncates both vertices and edges and replaces them with new facets. Cells are replaced by topologically [expanded](wikipedia://en/6341070) copies of themselves. (The term, coined by Johnson, is derived from the verb *cant*, like *[bevel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bevel)*, meaning to cut with a slanted face.)

*Image: Cube cantellation sequence.svg.*

   - There are higher cantellations also: **bicantellation** **t<sub>1,3</sub>** or **r2r**, **tricantellation** **t<sub>2,4</sub>** or **r3r**, **quadricantellation** **t<sub>3,5</sub>** or **r4r**, etc.
   - **t<sub>0,1,2</sub>** or **tr**: **Cantitruncation** - applied to [polyhedra](wikipedia://en/2537223) and higher. It can be seen as truncating its [rectification](wikipedia://en/2192366). A cantitruncation truncates both vertices and edges and replaces them with new facets. Cells are replaced by topologically [expanded](wikipedia://en/6341070) copies of themselves. (The composite term combines cantellation and truncation)
     - There are higher cantellations also: **bicantitruncation** **t<sub>1,2,3</sub>** or **t2r**, **tricantitruncation** **t<sub>2,3,4</sub>** or **t3r**, **quadricantitruncation** **t<sub>3,4,5</sub>** or **t4r**, etc.
1. **t<sub>0,3</sub>**: **[Runcination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runcination)** - applied to [Uniform 4-polytope](wikipedia://en/1645899) and higher. Runcination truncates vertices, edges, and faces, replacing them each with new facets. 4-faces are replaced by topologically expanded copies of themselves. (The term, coined by Johnson, is derived from Latin *runcina* 'carpenter's [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28tool%29)'.)
   - There are higher runcinations also: **biruncination** **t<sub>1,4</sub>**, **triruncination** **t<sub>2,5</sub>**, etc.
1. **t<sub>0,4</sub>** or **2r2r**: **Sterication** - applied to [Uniform 5-polytopes](wikipedia://en/14415338) and higher. Sterication truncates vertices, edges, faces, and cells, replacing each with new facets. 5-faces are replaced by topologically expanded copies of themselves. (The term, coined by Johnson, is derived from Greek *stereos* 'solid'.)
   - There are higher sterications also: **bisterication** **t<sub>1,5</sub>** or **2r3r**, **tristerication** **t<sub>2,6</sub>** or **2r4r**, etc.
   - **t<sub>0,2,4</sub>** or **2t2r**: **Stericantellation** - applied to [Uniform 5-polytopes](wikipedia://en/14415338) and higher.
     - There are higher sterications also: **bistericantellation** **t<sub>1,3,5</sub>** or **2t3r**, **tristericantellation** **t<sub>2,4,6</sub>** or **2t4r**, etc.
1. **t<sub>0,5</sub>**: **Pentellation** - applied to [Uniform 6-polytopes](wikipedia://en/6821728) and higher. Pentellation truncates vertices, edges, faces, cells, and 4-faces, replacing each with new facets. 6-faces are replaced by topologically expanded copies of themselves. (Pentellation is derived from Greek *[pente](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penta-)* 'five'.)
   - There are also higher pentellations: **bipentellation** **t<sub>1,6</sub>**, **tripentellation** **t<sub>2,7</sub>**, etc.
1. **t<sub>0,6</sub>** or **3r3r**: **Hexication** - applied to [Uniform 7-polytopes](wikipedia://en/6821719) and higher. Hexication truncates vertices, edges, faces, cells, 4-faces, and 5-faces, replacing each with new facets. 7-faces are replaced by topologically expanded copies of themselves. (Hexication is derived from Greek *[hex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexa-)* 'six'.)
   - There are higher hexications also: **bihexication**: **t<sub>1,7</sub>** or **3r4r**, **trihexication**: **t<sub>2,8</sub>** or **3r5r**, etc.
   - **t<sub>0,3,6</sub>** or **3t3r**: **Hexiruncinated** - applied to [Uniform 7-polytopes](wikipedia://en/6821719) and higher.
     - There are also higher hexiruncinations: **bihexiruncinated**: **t<sub>1,4,7</sub>** or **3t4r**, **trihexiruncinated**: **t<sub>2,5,8</sub>** or **3t5r**, etc.
1. **t<sub>0,7</sub>**: **Heptellation** - applied to [Uniform 8-polytopes](wikipedia://en/6821715) and higher. Heptellation truncates vertices, edges, faces, cells, 4-faces, 5-faces, and 6-faces, replacing each with new facets. 8-faces are replaced by topologically expanded copies of themselves. (Heptellation is derived from Greek *[hepta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepta-)* 'seven'.)
   - There are higher heptellations also: **biheptellation** **t<sub>1,8</sub>**, **triheptellation** **t<sub>2,9</sub>**, etc.
1. **t<sub>0,8</sub>** or **4r4r**: **Octellation** - applied to [Uniform 9-polytopes](wikipedia://en/6821726) and higher.
1. **t<sub>0,9</sub>**: **Ennecation** - applied to [Uniform 10-polytopes](wikipedia://en/17925746) and higher.

In addition combinations of truncations can be performed which also generate new uniform polytopes. For example, a *runcitruncation* is a *runcination* and *truncation* applied together.

If all truncations are applied at once, the operation can be more generally called an [omnitruncation](wikipedia://en/7083690).

:::

### Alternation {#Alternation}

*Image: Snubcubes in grCO.svg.* An alternation of a [truncated cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/285879) produces a [snub cube](wikipedia://en/180249).

One special operation, called [alternation](wikipedia://en/4349117), removes alternate vertices from a polytope with only even-sided faces. An alternated omnitruncated polytope is called a *snub*.

The resulting polytopes always can be constructed, and are not generally reflective, and also do not in general have *uniform* polytope solutions.

The set of polytopes formed by alternating the [hypercubes](wikipedia://en/39783) are known as [demicubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demicube). In three dimensions, this produces a [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606); in four dimensions, this produces a [16-cell](wikipedia://en/716422), or *demitesseract*.

## Vertex figure {#Vertex_figure}

Uniform polytopes can be constructed from their [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719), the arrangement of edges, faces, cells, etc. around each vertex. Uniform polytopes represented by a [Coxeter diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram), marking active mirrors by rings, have reflectional symmetry, and can be simply constructed by recursive reflections of the vertex figure.

A smaller number of nonreflectional uniform polytopes have a single vertex figure but are not repeated by simple reflections. Most of these can be represented with operations like [alternation](wikipedia://en/4349117) of other uniform polytopes.

Vertex figures for single-ringed Coxeter diagrams can be constructed from the diagram by removing the ringed node, and ringing neighboring nodes. Such vertex figures are themselves vertex-transitive.

Multiringed polytopes can be constructed by a slightly more complicated construction process, and their topology is not a uniform polytope. For example, the vertex figure of a [truncated](wikipedia://en/4224429) regular polytope (with 2 rings) is a pyramid. An [omnitruncated](wikipedia://en/7083690) polytope (all nodes ringed) will always have an irregular [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) as its vertex figure.

## Circumradius {#Circumradius}

Uniform polytopes have equal edge-lengths, and all vertices are an equal distance from the center, called the **circumradius**.

Uniform polytopes whose circumradius is equal to the edge length can be used as [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719) for [uniform honeycombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_honeycomb). For example, the regular [hexagon](wikipedia://en/59733) divides into 6 equilateral triangles and is the vertex figure for the regular [triangular tiling](wikipedia://en/2863858). Also the [cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/6280) divides into 8 regular tetrahedra and 6 square pyramids (half [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635)), and it is the vertex figure for the [alternated cubic honeycomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternated_cubic_honeycomb).

## Uniform polytopes by dimension {#Uniform_polytopes_by_dimension}

It is useful to classify the uniform polytopes by dimension. This is equivalent to the number of nodes on the Coxeter diagram, or the number of hyperplanes in the Wythoffian construction. Because (*n*+1)-dimensional polytopes are tilings of *n*-dimensional spherical space, tilings of *n*-dimensional [Euclidean](wikipedia://en/9697) and [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742) are also considered to be (*n*+1)-dimensional. Hence, the tilings of two-dimensional space are grouped with the three-dimensional solids.

### One dimension {#One_dimension}

The only one-dimensional polytope is the line segment. It corresponds to the Coxeter family A<sub>1</sub>.

#### Constructions {#Constructions}

The Wythoffian uniform polyhedra and tilings can be defined by their [Wythoff symbol](wikipedia://en/3691548), which specifies the [fundamental region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_region) of the object. An extension of [Schläfli](wikipedia://en/489394) notation, also used by [Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Scott_MacDonald_Coxeter), applies to all dimensions; it consists of the letter 't', followed by a series of subscripted numbers corresponding to the ringed nodes of the [Coxeter diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram), and followed by the Schläfli symbol of the regular seed polytope. For example, the [truncated octahedron](wikipedia://en/279654) is represented by the notation: t<sub>0,1</sub>{3,4}.

### Four dimensions {#Four_dimensions}

In four dimensions, there are 6 [convex regular 4-polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_regular_4-polytope), 17 prisms on the Platonic and Archimedean solids (excluding the cube-prism, which has already been counted as the [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112)), and two infinite sets: the prisms on the convex antiprisms, and the [duoprisms](wikipedia://en/2114155). There are also 41 convex semiregular 4-polytopes, including the [non-Wythoffian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Wythoffian) [grand antiprism](wikipedia://en/4058047) and the [snub 24-cell](wikipedia://en/3704756). Both of these special 4-polytopes are composed of subgroups of the vertices of the [600-cell](wikipedia://en/501447).

The four-dimensional uniform star polytopes have not all been enumerated. The ones that have include the 10 regular star (Schläfli-Hess) 4-polytopes and 57 prisms on the uniform star polyhedra, as well as three infinite families: the prisms on the star antiprisms, the duoprisms formed by [multiplying](wikipedia://en/24104095) two star polygons, and the duoprisms formed by multiplying an ordinary polygon with a star polygon. There is an unknown number of 4-polytopes that do not fit into the above categories; over one thousand have been discovered so far.

*Image: Polychoron tetrahedral domain.png.* Example tetrahedron in [cubic honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3733318) cell.*There are 3 right dihedral angles (2 intersecting perpendicular mirrors): Edges 1 to 2, 0 to 2, and 1 to 3.*

*Image: Polychoron truncation chart.png.* Summary chart of truncation operations

Every regular polytope can be seen as the images of a [fundamental region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_region) in a small number of mirrors. In a 4-dimensional polytope (or 3-dimensional cubic honeycomb) the fundamental region is bounded by four mirrors. A mirror in 4-space is a three-dimensional [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862), but it is more convenient for our purposes to consider only its two-dimensional intersection with the three-dimensional surface of the [hypersphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere); thus the mirrors form an irregular [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606).

Each of the sixteen [regular 4-polytopes](wikipedia://en/505253#Four-dimensional_regular_polytopes) is generated by one of four symmetry groups, as follows:

- group \[3,3,3\]: the [5-cell](wikipedia://en/513821) {3,3,3}, which is self-dual;
- group \[3,3,4\]: [16-cell](wikipedia://en/716422) {3,3,4} and its dual [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112) {4,3,3};
- group \[3,4,3\]: the [24-cell](wikipedia://en/293485) {3,4,3}, self-dual;
- group \[3,3,5\]: [600-cell](wikipedia://en/501447) {3,3,5}, its dual [120-cell](wikipedia://en/717358) {5,3,3}, and their ten regular stellations.
- group \[3<sup>1,1,1</sup>\]: contains only repeated members of the \[3,3,4\] family.

(The groups are named in [Coxeter notation](wikipedia://en/32088502).)

Eight of the [convex uniform honeycombs](wikipedia://en/2961) in Euclidean 3-space are analogously generated from the [cubic honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3733318) {4,3,4}, by applying the same operations used to generate the Wythoffian uniform 4-polytopes.

For a given symmetry simplex, a generating point may be placed on any of the four vertices, 6 edges, 4 faces, or the interior volume. On each of these 15 elements there is a point whose images, reflected in the four mirrors, are the vertices of a uniform 4-polytope.

The extended Schläfli symbols are made by a **t** followed by inclusion of one to four subscripts 0,1,2,3. If there is one subscript, the generating point is on a corner of the fundamental region, i.e. a point where three mirrors meet. These corners are notated as

- **0**: vertex of the parent 4-polytope (center of the dual's cell)
- **1**: center of the parent's edge (center of the dual's face)
- **2**: center of the parent's face (center of the dual's edge)
- **3**: center of the parent's cell (vertex of the dual)

(For the two self-dual 4-polytopes, "dual" means a similar 4-polytope in dual position.) Two or more subscripts mean that the generating point is between the corners indicated.

### Five and higher dimensions {#Five_and_higher_dimensions}

In five and higher dimensions, there are 3 regular polytopes, the [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783), [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) and [cross-polytope](wikipedia://en/716401). They are generalisations of the three-dimensional cube, tetrahedron and octahedron, respectively. There are no regular star polytopes in these dimensions. Most uniform higher-dimensional polytopes are obtained by modifying the regular polytopes, or by taking the Cartesian product of polytopes of lower dimensions.

In six, seven and eight dimensions, the [exceptional](wikipedia://en/5280515) [simple Lie groups](wikipedia://en/292831), [E<sub>6</sub>](wikipedia://en/293725), [E<sub>7</sub>](wikipedia://en/649115) and [E<sub>8</sub>](wikipedia://en/647994) come into play. By placing rings on a nonzero number of nodes of the [Coxeter diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram), one can obtain 39 new 6-polytopes, 127 new 7-polytopes and 255 new 8-polytopes. A notable example is the [4<sub>21</sub> polytope](wikipedia://en/19236362).

## Uniform honeycombs {#Uniform_honeycombs}

Related to the subject of finite uniform polytopes are uniform honeycombs in Euclidean and hyperbolic spaces. Euclidean uniform honeycombs are generated by [affine Coxeter groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram#Affine_Coxeter_groups) and hyperbolic honeycombs are generated by the [hyperbolic Coxeter groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram#Hyperbolic_Coxeter_groups). Two affine Coxeter groups can be multiplied together.

There are two classes of hyperbolic Coxeter groups, compact and paracompact. Uniform honeycombs generated by compact groups have finite facets and vertex figures, and exist in 2 through 4 dimensions. Paracompact groups have affine or hyperbolic subgraphs, and infinite facets or vertex figures, and exist in 2 through 10 dimensions.
