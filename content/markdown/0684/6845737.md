# Disintegration theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Disintegration theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **disintegration theorem** is a result in [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory) and [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542). It rigorously defines the idea of a non-trivial "restriction" of a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) to a [measure zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_zero) subset of the [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270) in question. It is related to the existence of [conditional probability measures](wikipedia://en/20273239). In a sense, "disintegration" is the opposite process to the construction of a [product measure](wikipedia://en/2986564).

:::

## Motivation {#Motivation}

Consider the unit square $S = [0,1]\times[0,1]$ in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) $\mathbb{R}^2$. Consider the [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) $\mu$ defined on $S$ by the restriction of two-dimensional [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) $\lambda^2$ to $S$. That is, the probability of an event $E\subseteq S$ is simply the area of $E$. We assume $E$ is a measurable subset of $S$.

Consider a one-dimensional subset of $S$ such as the line segment $L_x = \{x\}\times[0, 1]$. $L_x$ has $\mu$-measure zero; every subset of $L_x$ is a $\mu$-[null set](wikipedia://en/21520); since the Lebesgue measure space is a [complete measure space](wikipedia://en/44775),

$$E \subseteq L_{x} \implies \mu (E) = 0.$$

While true, this is somewhat unsatisfying. It would be nice to say that $\mu$ "restricted to" $L_x$ is the one-dimensional Lebesgue measure $\lambda^1$, rather than the [zero measure](wikipedia://en/10122069). The probability of a "two-dimensional" event $E$ could then be obtained as an [integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_integration) of the one-dimensional probabilities of the vertical "slices" $E\cap L_x$: more formally, if $\mu_x$ denotes one-dimensional Lebesgue measure on $L_x$, then

$$\mu (E) = \int_{[0, 1]} \mu_{x} (E \cap L_{x}) \, \mathrm{d} x$$

for any "nice" $E\subseteq S$. The disintegration theorem makes this argument rigorous in the context of measures on [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018).

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Statement of the theorem"}

## Statement of the theorem {#Statement_of_the_theorem}

(Hereafter, $\mathcal{P}(X)$ will denote the collection of [Borel](wikipedia://en/4322) probability measures on a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $(X, T)$.) The assumptions of the theorem are as follows: [^o5]

- Let $Y$ and $X$ be two [Polish spaces](wikipedia://en/396622) (i.e. [separably](wikipedia://en/27855) [completely metrizable spaces](wikipedia://en/7975189)).
- Let $\mu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)$.
- Let $\pi : Y\to X$ be a Borel-[measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055). Here one should think of $\pi$ as a function to "disintegrate" $Y$, in the sense of partitioning $Y$ into $\{ \pi^{-1}(x)\ |\ x \in X\}$. For example, for the motivating example above, one can define $\pi((a,b)) = a$, $(a,b) \in [0,1]\times [0,1]$, which gives that $\pi^{-1}(a) = a \times [0,1]$, a slice we want to capture.
- Let $\nu \in\mathcal{P}(X)$ be the [pushforward measure](wikipedia://en/9412979) $\nu = \pi_{*}(\mu) = \mu \circ \pi^{-1}$. This measure provides the distribution of $x$ (which corresponds to the events $\pi^{-1}(x)$).

The conclusion of the theorem: There exists a $\nu$-[almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688) uniquely determined family of probability measures $\{\mu_x\}_{x\in X} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(Y)$, which provides a "disintegration" of $\mu$ into $\{\mu_x\}_{x \in X}$, such that:

- the function $x \mapsto \mu_{x}$ is Borel measurable, in the sense that $x \mapsto \mu_{x} (B)$ is a Borel-measurable function for each Borel-measurable set $B\subseteq Y$;
- $\mu_x$ "lives on" the [fiber](wikipedia://en/6603892) $\pi^{-1}(x)$: for $\nu$-[almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) $x\in X$, $$\mu_{x} \left( Y \setminus \pi^{-1} (x) \right) = 0,$$ and so $\mu_x(E) =\mu_x(E\cap\pi^{-1}(x))$;
- for every Borel-measurable function $f : Y \to [0,\infty]$, $$\int_{Y} f(y) \, \mathrm{d} \mu (y) = \int_{X} \int_{\pi^{-1} (x)} f(y) \, \mathrm{d} \mu_x (y) \, \mathrm{d} \nu (x).$$ In particular, for any event $E\subseteq Y$, taking $f$ to be the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of $E$,[^o6] $$\mu (E) = \int_X \mu_x (E) \, \mathrm{d} \nu (x),$$ which shows that the family $\{\mu_x\}_{x \in X}$ is a [regular conditional probability](wikipedia://en/18963326).

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

### Product spaces {#Product_spaces}

The original example was a special case of the problem of product spaces, to which the disintegration theorem applies.

When $Y$ is written as a [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) $Y = X_1\times X_2$ and $\pi_i : Y\to X_i$ is the natural [projection](wikipedia://en/3909097), then each fibre $\pi_1^{-1}(x_1)$ can be [canonically](wikipedia://en/515096) identified with $X_2$ and there exists a Borel family of probability measures $\{ \mu_{x_{1}} \}_{x_{1} \in X_{1}}$ in $\mathcal{P}(X_2)$ (which is $(\pi_1)_*(\mu)$-almost everywhere uniquely determined) such that

$$\mu = \int_{X_{1}} \mu_{x_{1}} \, \mu \left(\pi_1^{-1}(\mathrm d x_1) \right)= \int_{X_{1}} \mu_{x_{1}} \, \mathrm{d} (\pi_{1})_{*} (\mu) (x_{1}),$$

which is in particular

$$\int_{X_1\times X_2} f(x_1,x_2)\, \mu(\mathrm d x_1,\mathrm d x_2) = \int_{X_1}\left( \int_{X_2} f(x_1,x_2) \mu(\mathrm d x_2\mid x_1) \right) \mu\left( \pi_1^{-1}(\mathrm{d} x_{1})\right)$$

and

$$\mu(A \times B) = \int_A \mu\left(B\mid x_1\right) \, \mu\left( \pi_1^{-1}(\mathrm{d} x_{1})\right).$$

The relation to [conditional expectation](wikipedia://en/435099) is given by the identities

$$\operatorname E(f\mid \pi_1)(x_1)= \int_{X_2} f(x_1,x_2) \mu(\mathrm d x_2\mid x_1),$$

$$\mu(A\times B\mid \pi_1)(x_1)= 1_A(x_1) \cdot \mu(B\mid x_1).$$

### Vector calculus {#Vector_calculus}

The disintegration theorem can also be seen as justifying the use of a "restricted" measure in [vector calculus](wikipedia://en/32640). For instance, in [Stokes' theorem](wikipedia://en/25441497) as applied to a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) flowing through a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) $\Sigma \subset \mathbb{R}^3$, it is implicit that the "correct" measure on $\Sigma$ is the disintegration of three-dimensional Lebesgue measure $\lambda^3$ on $\Sigma$, and that the disintegration of this measure on ∂Σ is the same as the disintegration of $\lambda^3$ on $\partial\Sigma$.[^o11]

### Conditional distributions {#Conditional_distributions}

The disintegration theorem can be applied to give a rigorous treatment of conditional probability distributions in statistics, while avoiding purely abstract formulations of conditional probability.[^o13] The theorem is related to the [Borel–Kolmogorov paradox](wikipedia://en/443575), for example.
