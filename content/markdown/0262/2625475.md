# Philodemus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Philodemus"}

**Philodemus of Gadara** (Φιλόδημος ὁ Γαδαρεύς, *Philodēmos*, "love of the people"; c. 110 – prob. c. 40 or 35 BC) was an [Epicurean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicurean) [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher)[^o3] and [poet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poet). He studied under [Zeno of Sidon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeno_of_Sidon) in [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens), before moving to [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome), and then to [Herculaneum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herculaneum). He was once known chiefly for his poetry preserved in the *[Greek Anthology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Anthology)*, but since the 18th century, many writings of his have been discovered among the charred papyrus rolls at the [Villa of the Papyri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_of_the_Papyri) at [Herculaneum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herculaneum). The task of excavating and deciphering these rolls is difficult, and work continues to this day. The works of Philodemus so far discovered include writings on [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics), [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology), [rhetoric](wikipedia://en/25447), [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music), [poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry), and the history of various [philosophical schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_philosophy). Ethel Ross Barker suggested in 1908 that he was owner of the Villa of the Papyri Library,[^o4] although it is far more likely that the owner was in fact his wealthy Roman patron [Lucius Calpurnius Piso Caesoninus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Calpurnius_Piso_Caesoninus_%28consul_58_BC%29).

:::

## Life {#Life}

Philodemus was born c. 110 BC, in [Gadara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gadara), [Coele-Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coele-Syria) (in present-day [Jordan](wikipedia://en/7515964)).[^o6] He studied under the Epicurean philosopher, [Zeno of Sidon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeno_of_Sidon), the head ([scholarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholarch)) of the [Epicurean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicurean) school, in Athens, before settling in [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome) about 80 BC. He was a follower of Zeno, but an innovative thinker in the area of [aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics), in which conservative Epicureans had little to contribute. He was a friend of [Lucius Calpurnius Piso Caesoninus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Calpurnius_Piso_Caesoninus_%28consul_58_BC%29), and was implicated in Piso's profligacy by [Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero),[^o7] who, however, praises Philodemus warmly for his philosophic views and for the *elegans lascivia* of his poems.[^o8] Philodemus was an influence on Horace's *[Ars Poetica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ars_Poetica_%28Horace%29)*. The [Greek anthology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_anthology) contains thirty-four of his [epigrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epigram), most of them love poems.

## The Villa of the Papyri {#The_Villa_of_the_Papyri}

There was an extensive library at the [Villa of the Papyri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_of_the_Papyri) at [Herculaneum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herculaneum), a significant part of which was formed by a library of Epicurean texts, some of which were present in more than one copy, suggesting the possibility that this section of the library was Philodemus' own. The contents of the villa were buried in the [eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eruption_of_Mount_Vesuvius_in_79_AD) of [Vesuvius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Vesuvius), 79 CE, and the papyri were carbonized into black rolls but preserved.

During the 18th-century exploration of the Villa by tunnelling, from 1752 to 1754 there were recovered some 1800 full and partial carbonized [papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papyri_from_Herculaneum) rolls containing thirty-six treatises attributed to Philodemus. These works deal with music, rhetoric, ethics, signs, virtues and vices, the good king, and defend the Epicurean standpoint against the [Stoics](wikipedia://en/19224834) and the [Peripatetics](wikipedia://en/529396). The first fragments of Philodemus from Herculaneum were published in 1824. In 2019, a scroll on the history of [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954)'s [Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy), which had been unrolled and glued to cardboard in 1795, was analyzed using shortwave-infrared [hyperspectral imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperspectral_imaging). This not only revealed what was written on the back of the scroll, but also illuminated 150 new words on the front.[^o10] In 2025, the title of another carbonized scroll, also scanned using non-invasive technology, was identified as Philodemus' *On Vices* with the aid of [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488) techniques.[^o11]

"The difficulties involved in unrolling, reading, and interpreting these texts were formidable. Naples was not a particularly hospitable destination for classical scholars. Finally, the philosophies of the Hellenistic schools were neither well-known nor highly regarded until quite recently. These factors combined to cripple scholarly interest in and use of the Herculaneum papyri. Recently, however, in part due to the efforts of the International Center for the Study of the Herculaneum Papyri, these rolls have been the object of renewed scholarly work and have yielded many findings indispensable for the study of Hellenistic philosophy."[^o12] Today researchers work from digitally enhanced photographs, infra-red and multiple-imaging photography, and 18th-century transcriptions of the documents, which were being destroyed as they were being unrolled and transcribed. The actual papyri are in the National Library, Naples.

Named for the philosopher-poet, the **Philodemus Project** is an international effort, supported by a major grant from the [National Endowment for the Humanities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Endowment_for_the_Humanities) and by contributions of individuals and participating universities, to reconstruct new texts of Philodemus' works on Poetics, Rhetoric, and Music. These texts will be edited and translated and published in a series of volumes by Oxford University Press.

*Philodemus: On Poems. I*, edited with Introduction, Translation, and Commentary by [Richard Janko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Janko), appeared in 2001 and won the [Charles J. Goodwin Award of Merit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_J._Goodwin_Award_of_Merit). "Philodemus’ *On Poems*, in particular, opens a window onto a lost age of scholarship—the period between [Aristotle's Poetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetics_%28Aristotle%29) and [Horace's Art of Poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ars_Poetica_%28Horace%29), the works which define classicism for the ancient and modern worlds", Janko has written.[^o13]

The Project's next volumes are scheduled to be:

- *On Poems* V, edited and translated by David Armstrong, James Porter, Jeffrey Fish, and Cecilia Mangoni
- *On Rhetoric* I-II, edited and translated by David Blank
- *On Rhetoric* III, edited and translated by [Dirk Obbink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirk_Obbink) and Juergen Hammerstaedt.

## Inductive reasoning {#Inductive_reasoning}

In *On Methods of Inference*, Philodemus comments on the [problem of induction](wikipedia://en/177456), doubting the reliability of [inductive reasoning](wikipedia://en/393736) from the observed to the unobserved. One problem is the existence of unique events that could never be guessed from what happens elsewhere. "There are also in our experience some infrequent occurrences, as for example the man in Alexandria half a cubit high, with a colossal head that could be beaten with a hammer, who used to be exhibited by the embalmers; the person in Epidaurus who was married as a young woman and then become a man."[^o15] Induction is also unreliable if it extrapolates far beyond our experience: "We shall not, therefore, use the \[inference\] that since the men among us are mortal the men in Libya would also be mortal, much less the inference that since the living beings among us are mortal, if there are any living beings in Britain, they would be mortal."[^o16] The fourth book of Philodemus' On Death (PHerc. 1050) is an important text that sheds light on various aspects of the well-known Epicurean claim that "death is nothing to us".[^o17]
