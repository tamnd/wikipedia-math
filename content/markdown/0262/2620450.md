# Legendre's conjecture

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Legendre's conjecture"}

**Legendre's conjecture**, proposed by [Adrien-Marie Legendre](wikipedia://en/49695), states that there is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) between $n^2$ and $(n+1)^2$ for every [positive integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_integer) $n$.[^o2] The [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) is one of [Landau's problems](wikipedia://en/5785677) (1912) on prime numbers and is one of many [open problems](wikipedia://en/1333335) on the spacing of prime numbers.

:::

::: {#o3 .problem cue=unsolved}

Does there always exist at least one prime between $n^2$ and $(n+1)^2$?

:::

## Prime gaps {#Prime_gaps}

If Legendre's conjecture is true, the [gap](wikipedia://en/5075259) between any prime *p* and the next largest prime would be $O(\sqrt{p}\,)$, as expressed in [big O notation](wikipedia://en/44578). It is one of a family of results and conjectures related to [prime gaps](wikipedia://en/5075259), that is, to the spacing between prime numbers. Others include [Bertrand's postulate](wikipedia://en/232526), on the existence of a prime between $n$ and $2n$, [Oppermann's conjecture](wikipedia://en/29543748) on the existence of primes between $n^2$, $n(n+1)$, and $(n+1)^2$, [Andrica's conjecture](wikipedia://en/7878893) and [Brocard's conjecture](wikipedia://en/2620525) on the existence of primes between squares of consecutive primes, and [Cramér's conjecture](wikipedia://en/290441) that the gaps are always much smaller, of the order $(\log p)^2$. If Cramér's conjecture is true, Legendre's conjecture would follow for all sufficiently large *n*. [Harald Cramér](wikipedia://en/223129) also proved that the [Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/19344125) implies a weaker bound of $O(\sqrt p\log p)$ on the size of the largest prime gaps.[^o5]

*Image: Plot of number of primes between consecutive squares.png.* Plot of the number of primes between *n*<sup>2</sup> and (*n* + 1)<sup>2</sup>

By the [prime number theorem](wikipedia://en/23692), the expected number of primes between $n^2$ and $(n+1)^2$ is approximately $n/\ln n$, and it is additionally known that for [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) [intervals](wikipedia://en/49172) of this form the actual number of primes () is [asymptotic](wikipedia://en/641995) to this expected number.[^o6] Since this number is large for large $n$, this lends credence to Legendre's conjecture.[^o7] It is known that the prime number theorem gives an accurate count of the primes within short intervals, either unconditionally[^o8] or based on the [Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/19344125),[^o9] but the lengths of the intervals for which this has been proven are longer than the intervals between consecutive squares, too long to prove Legendre's conjecture.

## Partial results {#Partial_results}

It follows from a result by [Ingham](wikipedia://en/5380138) that for all sufficiently large $n$, there is a prime between the consecutive *[cubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube_number)* $n^3$ and $(n+1)^3$.[^o12] Dudek proved that this holds for all $n \ge e^{e^{33.217}}\approx1.01\cdot10^{115809466034000}$.[^o13]

Dudek also proved that for $m = 4.971\cdot10^9$ and any positive integer $n$, there is a prime between $n^m$ and $(n+1)^m$. Mattner lowered this to $m = 1438989$,[^o14] which was further reduced to $m = 155$ by Cully-Hugill.[^o15]

Baker, [Harman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glyn_Harman), and [Pintz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janos_Pintz) proved that there is a prime in the interval $[x-x^{21/40},\,x]$ for all large $x$.[^o16]

A [table of maximal prime gaps](wikipedia://en/5075259#Numerical_results) shows that the conjecture holds to at least $n^2=2\cdot10^{19}$, meaning $n=4.5\cdot10^9$.
