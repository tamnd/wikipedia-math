# Number needed to harm

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Number needed to harm"}

*Image: Number needed to harm plain.svg.* Group exposed to a risk factor (left) has increased risk of an adverse outcome (dark) compared to the unexposed group (right). 4 individuals need to be exposed for 1 adverse outcome to occur (NNH = 4).

In [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine), the **number needed to harm** (**NNH**) is an [epidemiological](wikipedia://en/66997) measure that indicates how many persons on average need to be exposed to a [risk factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk-factor) over a specific period to cause harm in an average of one person who would not otherwise have been harmed. It is defined as the inverse of the [absolute risk increase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_risk_increase), and computed as $1/(I_e - I_u)$, where $I_e$ is the [incidence](wikipedia://en/167008) in the treated (exposed) group, and $I_u$ is the incidence in the control (unexposed) group.[^o2] Intuitively, the lower the number needed to harm, the worse the risk factor, with 1 meaning that every exposed person is harmed.

:::

NNH is similar to [number needed to treat](wikipedia://en/1964571) (NNT), where NNT usually refers to a positive therapeutic result and NNH to a detrimental effect or risk factor.

Marginal metrics:

- NNT for an additional beneficial outcome (**NNTB**)
- NNT for an additional harmful outcome (**NNTH**)

are also used.[^o3]

## Relevance {#Relevance}

The NNH is an important measure in [evidence-based medicine](wikipedia://en/10013) and helps physicians decide whether it is prudent to proceed with a particular treatment which may expose the patient to harms while providing therapeutic benefits. If a clinical endpoint is devastating enough without the drug (e.g. [death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death), [heart attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myocardial_infarction)), drugs with a low NNH may still be indicated in particular situations if the NNT is smaller than the NNH. However, there are several important problems with the NNH, involving bias and lack of reliable confidence intervals, as well as difficulties in excluding the possibility of no difference between two treatments or groups.[^o5]
