# Brocard's conjecture

## Introduction {#Introduction}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **Brocard's conjecture** is the [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) that there are at least four [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) between (*p*<sub>*n*</sub>)<sup>2</sup> and (*p*<sub>*n*+1</sub>)<sup>2</sup>, where *p*<sub>*n*</sub> is the *n*<sup>th</sup> prime number, for every *n* ≥ 2. The conjecture is named after [Henri Brocard](wikipedia://en/14897496). It is widely believed that this conjecture is true.[^o4] However, it remains unproven as of 2025. [Legendre's conjecture](wikipedia://en/2620450), which states that there is a prime between consecutive integer squares, directly implies that there are at least two primes between prime squares for *p*<sub>*n*</sub> ≥ 3 since *p*<sub>*n*+1</sub> − *p*<sub>*n*</sub> ≥ 2.[^o5]

## Mathematical statement {#Mathematical_statement}

Let $p_n$ be the $n$-th prime, and let $\pi(x)$ be the number of prime numbers $\leq x$. Formally, Brocard's conjecture claims:

$$\pi\big((p_{n+1})^2\big)-\pi\big((p_n)^2\big)\geq 4\quad\text{for }n\geq2$$

This is equivalent to saying that there are at least four primes between squared consecutive primes other than $2$ and $3$.

## Relation to other open problems in mathematics {#Relation_to_other_open_problems_in_mathematics}

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Legendre's conjecture"}

### Legendre's conjecture {#Legendre's_conjecture}

[Legendre's conjecture](wikipedia://en/2620450) claims that there is a prime number between $(n)^2$ and $(n+1)^2$ for all natural number $n$. It is an unsolved problem in mathematics as of 2025. If Legendre's conjecture is true, it immediately implies a weak version of Brocard's conjecture:[^o10]

$$\pi\big((p_{n+1})^2\big)-\pi\big((p_n)^2\big)\geq 2\quad\text{for }n\geq 3$$

:::

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Cramér's conjecture"}

### Cramér's conjecture {#Cramér's_conjecture}

[Cramér's conjecture](wikipedia://en/290441) claims that $p_{n+1}-p_n=O((\log p_n)^2)$, which gives a bound on how far apart primes can be. Cramér's conjecture implies Brocard's conjecture for sufficiently large $n$.[^o5]

:::

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Oppermann's conjecture"}

### Oppermann's conjecture {#Oppermann's_conjecture}

[Oppermann's conjecture](wikipedia://en/29543748) claims that there is a prime in the [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $(n^2, n(n+1))$ and in the interval $(n(n+1), (n+1)^2)$. This unsolved problem directly implies Brocard's conjecture.

::: {#o15 .proof cue=collapse name="Proof that Oppermann's Conjecture implies Brocard's Conjecture"}

We begin with the fact that $p_{n+1}-p_n\geq 2$, meaning that the minimal interval between primes is $(p_n, p_n+2)$. Then, according to Oppermann's conjecture, there is a prime in the interval $(p_n^2, p_n(p_n+1))$, a prime in the interval $(p_n(p_n+1), (p_n+1)^2)$, a prime in the interval $((p_n+1)^2, (p_n+1)(p_n+2))$, and a prime in the interval $((p_n+1)(p_n+2), (p_n+2)^2)$. Then, we have:

$$\underbrace{\big(p_n^2, p_n(p_n+1)\big)}_{\text{At least }1\text{ prime}},\quad \underbrace{\big(p_n(p_n+1), (p_n+1)^2\big)}_{\text{At least } 1 \text{ prime}}, \quad \underbrace{\big((p_n+1)^2, (p_n+1)(p_n+2)\big)}_{\text{At least }1\text{ prime}}, \quad \underbrace{\big((p_n+1)(p_n+2), (p_n+2)^2\big)}_{\text{At least }1\text{ prime}}$$

Which implies at least $4$ primes between $p_n^2$ and $(p_n+2)^2$, and because $p_n+2\leq p_{n+1}$, there are at least $4$ primes between any two squared consecutive primes, which is exactly what Brocard's conjecture claims.

:::

:::

## Current research and results {#Current_research_and_results}

### Unconditional results {#Unconditional_results}

#### Bertrand's postulate {#Bertrand's_postulate}

A trivial result from [Bertrand's postulate](wikipedia://en/232526), a proven theorem, states that because there is a prime in the interval $(n, 2n)$, and the length of the interval $((p_n)^2, (p_{n+1})^2)$ is much greater than $(p_n, 2p_n)$, Bertrand's postulate suggests *many* primes in the interval $((p_n)^2, (p_{n+1})^2)$, though not a sharp bound.

#### Baker-Harman-Pintz bound {#Baker-Harman-Pintz_bound}

Using the bound proven by Baker *et al.*,[^o23] that $p_{n+1}-p_n<p_n^{0.525}$, one can show that there exist infinitely many $p_n$ such that there is at least one prime in the interval $((p_n)^2, (p_{n+1})^2)$, which is a much weaker result than Brocard's conjecture.

### Conditional results {#Conditional_results}

::: {#o26 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Legendre's Conjecture - weak version of Brocard's conjecture"}

#### Legendre's Conjecture - weak version of Brocard's conjecture {#Legendre's_Conjecture_-_weak_version_of_Brocard's_conjecture}

As shown above, Legendre's conjecture implies a weak version of Brocard's conjecture but is a strictly weaker conjecture.

:::

::: {#o28 .proof cue=named-heading name="Oppermann's Conjecture - full proof of Brocard's conjecture"}

#### Oppermann's Conjecture - full proof of Brocard's conjecture {#Oppermann's_Conjecture_-_full_proof_of_Brocard's_conjecture}

As shown [above](wikipedia://en/2620525#Opperman's_Conjecture), Oppermann's conjecture directly implies Brocard's conjecture for large enough $n$, which constitutes a proof of Brocard's conjecture.

:::

::: {#o30 .proof cue=named-heading name="Cramér's Conjecture - full proof of Brocard's conjecture"}

#### Cramér's Conjecture - full proof of Brocard's conjecture {#Cramér's_Conjecture_-_full_proof_of_Brocard's_conjecture}

As shown [above](wikipedia://en/11301521#Cramér's_Conjecture), Cramér's conjecture implies Brocard's conjecture directly.

:::

::: {#o32 .proof cue=named-heading name="The Riemann Hypothesis - full proof of Brocard's conjecture"}

#### The Riemann Hypothesis - full proof of Brocard's conjecture {#The_Riemann_Hypothesis_-_full_proof_of_Brocard's_conjecture}

The Riemann Hypothesis implies the bound $p_{n+1}-p_n=O\big(\sqrt{p_n}\log(p_n)\big)$, which implies Brocard's conjecture for sufficiently large $n$, similarly to Cramér's conjecture.[^o33]

:::
