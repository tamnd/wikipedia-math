# Rossby radius of deformation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rossby radius of deformation"}

In [atmospheric dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_dynamics) and physical [oceanography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanography), the **Rossby radius of deformation** is the length scale at which rotational effects become as important as [buoyancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buoyancy) or [gravity wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_wave) effects in the evolution of the [flow](wikipedia://en/11034) about some disturbance.[^o2]

:::

For a [barotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barotropic) [ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean), the Rossby radius is $L_R \equiv \frac{(gD)^{1/2}}{f}$, where $g$ is the [gravitational acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_acceleration), $D$ is the water depth, and $\,f$ is the [Coriolis parameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriolis_parameter).[^o3]

For *f* = 1 × 10<sup>\-4</sup> s-1 appropriate to 45° latitude, *g* = 9.81 m/s<sup>2</sup> and *D* = 4 km, *L*<sub>R</sub> ≈ 2000 km; using the same latitude and gravity but changing *D* to 40 m; *L*<sub>R</sub> ≈ 200 km.

The *n*th [baroclinic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroclinic) Rossby radius is:

$L_{R, n} \equiv \frac{NH}{n \pi f_0}$, where $N$ is the [Brunt–Väisälä frequency](wikipedia://en/2630316), $H$ is the [scale height](wikipedia://en/1994795), and *n* = 1, 2, ... .

In Earth's atmosphere, the ratio *N*/*f*<sub>0</sub> is typically of order 100, so the Rossby radius is about 100 times the vertical scale height, *H*. For a vertical scale associated with the height of the [tropopause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropopause), *L*<sub>R,1</sub> ≈ 1000 km, which is the predominant scale seen on weather charts for [cyclones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclones) and [anticyclones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticyclones). This is commonly called the [synoptic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synoptic_scale_meteorology).

In the ocean, the baroclinic Rossby radius varies dramatically with latitude. Near the equator it is larger than 200 km, while in the high latitude regions it is less than 10 km.[^o4][^o5] The size of ocean eddies varies similarly; in low latitude regions, near the equator, eddies are much larger than in high latitude regions.

The associated dimensionless parameter is the [Rossby number](wikipedia://en/459426). Both are named in honor of [Carl-Gustav Rossby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl-Gustav_Rossby).
