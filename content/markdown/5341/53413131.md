# Planigon

*Image: All Planigons.svg.* Three [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306), eight planigons, four demiregular planigons, and six not usable planigon triangles which cannot take part in dual uniform tilings; all to scale.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Planigon"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **planigon** is a [convex polygon](wikipedia://en/660019) that can fill the plane with only copies of itself ([isotopic](wikipedia://en/212250#Isotopy) to the [fundamental units](wikipedia://en/39372) of [monohedral tessellations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monohedral_tiling)). In the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) there are 3 regular planigons; [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285), [squares](wikipedia://en/659939), and [regular hexagons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_hexagon); and 8 [semiregular](wikipedia://en/2888949) planigons; and 4 [demiregular](wikipedia://en/1548669) planigons which can tile the plane only with other planigons.

:::

All angles of a planigon are whole divisors of 360°. Tilings are made by edge-to-edge connections by perpendicular bisectors of the edges of the original uniform lattice, or centroids along common edges (they coincide).

Tilings made from planigons can be seen as [dual tilings](wikipedia://en/8815) to the [regular, semiregular](wikipedia://en/2888949), and [demiregular](wikipedia://en/1548669) tilings of the plane by [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306).

## History {#History}

In the 1987 book, *[Tilings and patterns](wikipedia://en/76301624)*, [Branko Grünbaum](wikipedia://en/1731976) calls the vertex-uniform tilings *Archimedean* in parallel to the [Archimedean solids](wikipedia://en/1847). Their [dual tilings](wikipedia://en/2536864) are called ***Laves tilings*** in honor of [crystallographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography) [Fritz Laves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Laves).[^o3][^o4] They're also called **Shubnikov–Laves tilings** after Shubnikov, Alekseĭ Vasilʹevich.[^o5] [John Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) calls the uniform duals ***Catalan tilings***, in parallel to the [Catalan solid](wikipedia://en/564393) polyhedra.

The Laves tilings have vertices at the centers of the regular polygons, and edges connecting centers of regular polygons that share an edge. The [tiles](wikipedia://en/39372) of the Laves tilings are called **planigons**. This includes the 3 regular tiles (triangle, square and hexagon) and 8 irregular ones. Each vertex has edges evenly spaced around it. Three dimensional analogues of the *planigons* are called [stereohedrons](wikipedia://en/53413889).

These tilings are listed by their [face configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_configuration), the number of faces at each vertex of a face. For example *V4.8.8* (or V4.8<sup>2</sup>) means [isosceles triangle](wikipedia://en/315428) tiles with one corner with four triangles, and two corners containing eight triangles.

::: {#o8 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

The [Conway operation](wikipedia://en/5021705#Original_operations) of dual interchanges faces and vertices. In [Archimedean solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedean_solids) and [*k*-uniform tilings](wikipedia://en/1548669) alike, the new vertex coincides with the center of each [regular face](wikipedia://en/333306), or the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926). In the Euclidean (plane) case; in order to make new faces around each original vertex, the centroids must be connected by new edges, each of which must intersect exactly one of the original edges. Since regular polygons have [dihedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/100267), we see that these new centroid-centroid edges must be [perpendicular bisectors](wikipedia://en/152547#Line_segment_bisector) of the common original edges (e.g. the centroid lies on all edge perpendicular bisectors of a regular polygon). Thus, the edges of *k*-dual uniform tilings coincide with centroid-to-edge-midpoint line segments of all regular polygons in the *k*-uniform tilings.

### Using the 12-5 Dodecagram (Above) {#Using_the_12-5_Dodecagram_(Above)}

All 14 uniform usable regular vertex planigons also hail[^o11] from the [6-5 dodecagram](wikipedia://en/26173953) (where each segment subtends $5\pi/6$ radians, or 150 degrees).

The [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle_and_excircles_of_a_triangle) of this dodecagram demonstrates that all the 14 VRPs are [cocyclic](wikipedia://en/9176412), as alternatively shown by [circle packings](wikipedia://en/16879665). The ratio of the incircle to the circumcircle is:

$$\sin\frac{\pi}{12}=\sin 15^{\circ}=\frac{\sqrt{6}-\sqrt{2}}{4}\approx 0.258819$$

and the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) is precisely the [regular dodecagons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_dodecagon) in the [k-uniform tiling](wikipedia://en/66029152). The equilateral triangle, square, regular hexagon, and regular dodecagon; are shown above with the VRPs.

In fact, any group of planigons can be constructed from the edges of a $2k\text{-}(k-1)$ [polygram](wikipedia://en/45296926), where $k=\gcd(n_1,\dots,n_m)$ and $n_i$ is the number of sides in the RP adjacent to each involved vertex figure. This is because the [circumradius](wikipedia://en/333306#Circumradius) $\frac{1}{2}\csc\frac{\pi}{n_i}$ of any regular $n_i$-gon (from the vertex to the centroid) is the same as the distance from the center of the polygram to its line segments which intersect at the angle $2\pi/n_i$, since all $2k\text{-}(k-1)$ polygrams admit incircles of inradii $1/2$ tangent to all its sides.

## Derivation of all possible planigons {#Derivation_of_all_possible_planigons}

For edge-to-edge Euclidean tilings, the [interior angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_angle) of the convex polygons meeting at a vertex must add to 360 degrees. A regular *$n$*-gon has internal angle $\left(1-\frac{2}{n}\right)180^\circ$ degrees. There are seventeen combinations of regular polygons whose internal angles add up to 360 degrees, each being referred to as a *species* of vertex; in four cases there are two distinct cyclic orders of the polygons, yielding twenty-one *types* of vertex.

In fact, with the vertex (interior) angles $60^\circ,90^\circ,108^\circ,120^\circ,128\frac{4}{7}^\circ,135^\circ,140^\circ,144^\circ, 147\frac{3}{11}^\circ,150^\circ,\dots$, we can find all combinations of admissible corner angles according to the following rules:

1. Every vertex has at least degree 3 (a degree-2 vertex must have two straight angles or one reflex angle);
1. If the vertex has degree $d$, the smallest $d-1$ polygon vertex angles sum to over $180^{\circ}$;
1. The vertex angles add to $360^{\circ}$, and must be angles of regular polygons of positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) sides (of the sequence $60^\circ,90^\circ,108^\circ,120^\circ,128\frac{4}{7}^\circ,135^\circ,140^\circ,144^\circ, 147\frac{3}{11}^\circ,150^\circ,\dots$).

### Planigons in the plane {#Planigons_in_the_plane}

Only eleven of these angle combinations can occur in a [Laves Tiling](wikipedia://en/2888949#Laves_tilings) of planigons.

In particular, if three polygons meet at a vertex and one has an odd number of sides, the other two polygons must be the same. If they are not, they would have to alternate around the first polygon, which is impossible if its number of sides is odd. By that restriction these six cannot appear in any k-dual-uniform tiling:

Such periodic tilings may be classified by the number of [orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Orbits_and_stabilizers) of vertices, edges and tiles. If there are *k* orbits of planigons, a tiling is known as *k*-dual-uniform or *k*-isohedral; if there are *t* orbits of dual vertices, as *t*-isogonal; if there are *e* orbits of edges, as *e*-isotoxal.

*k*-dual-uniform tilings with the same vertex faces can be further identified by their [wallpaper group](wikipedia://en/386214) symmetry, which is identical to that of the corresponding *k*-uniform tiling.

1-dual-uniform tilings include 3 regular tilings, and 8 Laves tilings, with 2 or more types of regular degree vertices. There are 20 2-dual-uniform tilings, 61 3-dual-uniform tilings, 151 4-dual-uniform tilings, 332 5-dual-uniform tilings and 673 6--dualuniform tilings. Each can be grouped by the number *m* of distinct [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719), which are also called *m*-Archimedean tilings.[^o28]

Finally, if the number of types of planigons is the same as the uniformity (*m* = *k* below), then the tiling is said to be dual *Krotenheerdt*. In general, the uniformity is greater than or equal to the number of types of vertices (*m* ≥ *k*), as different types of planigons necessarily have different orbits, but not vice versa. Setting *m* = *n* = *k*, there are 11 such dual tilings for *n* = 1; 20 such dual tilings for *n* = 2; 39 such dual tilings for *n* = 3; 33 such dual tilings for *n* = 4; 15 such dual tilings for *n* = 5; 10 such dual tilings for *n* = 6; and 7 such dual tilings for *n* = 7.
