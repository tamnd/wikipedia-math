# Anti-integrability

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Anti-integrability"}

**Anti-integrability** is a method used to study the chaotic bounds of a [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087).[^o2] A [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087) is at the **anti-integrable limit** if it becomes [singular](wikipedia://en/54240) and trajectories of the [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087) no longer have an explicit form. In other words, the system becomes [non-deterministic](wikipedia://en/665957) and reduces to an [implicit](wikipedia://en/250896) relation with multiple solutions.[^o3] The significance of this limit is that, under some conditions, trajectories at the anti-integrable limit, referred to as anti-integrable states, can persist onto trajectories of the original [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087). Thus, anti-integrability provides a way to rigorously prove the existence of [chaotic](wikipedia://en/6295) orbits[^o4] and to study [perturbations](wikipedia://en/102140) of a [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087).[^o5]

:::

::: {#o7 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

The term anti-integrability was formalized in 1990 in the field of [solid state physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-state_physics) by Aubry and Abramovici as a method that 'opposed' [integrability](wikipedia://en/2573213#Hamiltonian_systems_and_Liouville_integrability).[^o8] It was first defined in the context of [discrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_system) [Lagrangian systems](wikipedia://en/24585634), or (locally) symplectic maps.

Trajectories of a [discrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_system) symplectic system, $\{x_t\}$ for $t\in\mathbb{Z}$, can be found via the [principle of least action](wikipedia://en/76122969). The [action](wikipedia://en/312881) of a trajectory, $S(\{x_t\})$, can be written as a sum of [discrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_system) Lagrangians $L(x_t,x_{t+1})$, which are combinations of [kinetic](wikipedia://en/17327) $K(x_t-x_{t+1})$ and [potential](wikipedia://en/23703) $V(x_t)$ energies,

$$S(\{x_t\})=\sum_tL(x_t,x_{t+1})=\sum_t \left( K(x_t-x_{t+1})-V(x_t) \right).$$

### Definition for discrete Lagrangian systems {#Definition_for_discrete_Lagrangian_systems}

According to Aubry and Abramovici,[^o8] a symplectic [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087) with trajectories $\{x_t\}$ and discrete time $t\in\mathbb{Z}$ is **anti-integrable** when its [action](wikipedia://en/312881) (or generating function) $\sum_tL(x_{t},x_{t+1})$ can be written as $\sum_tV(x_t)$.

The dynamics of the map governing $x_t\mapsto x_{t+1}$ are considered [integrable](wikipedia://en/2573213#Hamiltonian_systems_and_Liouville_integrability) when the [potential energy](wikipedia://en/23703) vanishes, and solutions can be found via the law of conservation of [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431). In contrast, the anti-integrable limit can be interpreted as the kinetic energy vanishing.[^o10] Following the [principle of least action](wikipedia://en/76122969), trajectories of the map at the anti-integrable limit must lie at [critical points](wikipedia://en/1951424) of the potential energy and the 'dynamics' become [non-deterministic](wikipedia://en/665957), reducing to the [shift operator](wikipedia://en/467384) acting on the set of these critical points. Trajectories at this limit, referred to as anti-integrable states, can then be analytically or [numerically continued](wikipedia://en/9752560) toward [integrability](wikipedia://en/2573213#Hamiltonian_systems_and_Liouville_integrability). This process is similar to perturbing an integrable system via [KAM theory](wikipedia://en/16972).[^o11] [KAM theory](wikipedia://en/16972) is used to find regular solutions that persist away from integrability, when more and more solutions become irregular. In contrast, the theory of anti-integrability is used to find irregular solutions that persist away from the anti-integrable limit, when more and more solutions become regular.

#### Example: Standard map {#Example:_Standard_map}

Written as a single difference equation, the [standard map](wikipedia://en/9503407) is given as [^o13]

$$x_{t+1}-2x_t+x_{t-1}=\lambda\sin{x_t},$$

for $x_t\in\mathbb{R}$ modulo $2\pi$ and $\lambda>0$. The Lagrangian of this difference equation takes the form

$$L(x_t,x_{t+1})= K(x_t-x_{t+1})-V(x_t)=(x_t-x_{t+1})^2-2\lambda\cos{x_t}.$$

In accordance with the [principle of least action](wikipedia://en/76122969), trajectories of the [standard map](wikipedia://en/9503407) lie at the [critical points](wikipedia://en/1951424) of the sum of Lagrangians, i.e., where

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x_n}\sum_tL(x_t,x_{t+1})=0$$

for any $n\in\mathbb{Z}$. This condition on the Lagrangian above reproduces the standard map.

The anti-integrable limit of the [standard map](wikipedia://en/9503407) is the limit $\lambda\to\infty$.[^o14] To find this limit, we rescale the [parameter](wikipedia://en/25065) as $\lambda=\frac{1}{\epsilon}$ so that it becomes

$$\epsilon(x_{t+1}-2x_t+x_{t-1})=\sin{x_t}.$$

The corresponding Lagrangian is now

$$L(x_t,x_{t+1})=\epsilon(x_t-x_{t+1})^2-2\cos{x_t}.$$

With this rescaling, the anti-integrable limit is now the limit $\epsilon\to0$. Applying this limit and the [principle of least action](wikipedia://en/76122969) leads to the [non-deterministic](wikipedia://en/665957), [implicit](wikipedia://en/250896) relation

$$\sin{x_t}=0,$$

with solutions $m\pi$ for integer $m$. Valid anti-integrable states then come in the form

$$\{\ldots, m_{-1}\pi, m_0\pi, m_1\pi, \ldots\} \quad \text{for} \quad m_n\in\mathbb{Z},$$

and the dynamics reduce to the [shift operator](wikipedia://en/467384) on these states.

Arguments using the [implicit function theorem](wikipedia://en/581005) and [contraction mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/46676) can then be made to show that anti-integrable states can persist for small $\epsilon>0$, as discussed in Aubry and Abramovici's formative work.[^o8]

The definition above was generalized by Aubry in 1995[^o15] to include all [discrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_system) maps. This generalized definition provides an alternative, and arguably easier, approach to implement anti-integrability, as can be seen in the example of the [logistic map](wikipedia://en/18137) below.

### Definition for general discrete maps {#Definition_for_general_discrete_maps}

Consider a one [parameter](wikipedia://en/25065) $\epsilon$-[continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) family of deterministic [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087) $X_{t+1}=F_\epsilon(X_t)$. The limit $\epsilon\to0$ is called the **anti-integrable limit** when

- The system can be defined as an implicit [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087), i.e., there exists a function $G_\epsilon(X,Y)$ which depends continuously on $\epsilon$ such that the [implicit equation](wikipedia://en/250896) $G_\epsilon(X,Y)=0$ is equivalent to $Y=F_\epsilon(X)$ for $\epsilon\neq0$ and such that the limit $G_0(X,Y)$ is defined.
- The solutions $\{X_t\}$ of the [implicit equations](wikipedia://en/250896) $G_0(X_{t+1},X_t)=0$ for all $t$ form a [discrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_system) set which can be characterized by an infinite [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $\{s_t\}\in\Sigma^\mathbb{Z}$ called a coding (or [symbolic](wikipedia://en/1986263)) sequence $\{s_t\}\to\{X_t\}$ where $s_t$ belongs to a [discrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_system) set $\Sigma$ of codes (or symbols).[^o15]

#### Example: Logistic map {#Example:_Logistic_map}

Following the work of Chen,[^o18] the [logistic map](wikipedia://en/18137),

$$x_{t+1}= \mu x_t(1-x_t), \quad x\in\mathbb{R}, \quad\mu>0,$$

has an anti-integrable limit $\mu\to\infty$. To see this, rewrite the map using the rescaling $\mu=\frac{1}{\epsilon}$ to obtain

$$x_t(1-x_t)=\epsilon x_{t+1}.$$

The [singular](wikipedia://en/54240) anti-integrable limit $\mu\to\infty$ now corresponds with $\epsilon\to 0$.

When $\epsilon=0$, the map becomes the [non-deterministic](wikipedia://en/665957), [implicit](wikipedia://en/250896) relation $x_t(1-x_t)=0$ and has the solutions $x_t=0$ or $x_t=1$ for every $t\in\mathbb{Z}$. Each valid anti-integrable state is associated with a [symbolic](wikipedia://en/1986263) sequence, $s=\{\ldots, s_{-1},s_0,s_1,\ldots\}$, where $s_t\in\{0,1\},$ for integer $t$. Thus, at the anti-integrable limit, the 'dynamics' become a [shift](wikipedia://en/467384) on these two symbols.

The anti-integrable states at $\epsilon=0$ persist for small $\epsilon>0$, which Chen proves analytically with an [implicit function theorem](wikipedia://en/581005) argument.[^o18]

## Extensions {#Extensions}

Arguments for persistence away from the anti-integrable limit typically utilize the [implicit function theorem](wikipedia://en/581005) or the [contraction mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/46676). One can also continue anti-integrable states away from the anti-integrable limit using [numerical continuation](wikipedia://en/9752560).[^o21]

Anti-integrability has also been used to prove the existence of a [horseshoe](wikipedia://en/449364)[^o22] and to study the development of [chaotic](wikipedia://en/6295) [attractors](wikipedia://en/191094) over changing [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065).[^o23]
