# Bernstein–Sato polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bernstein–Sato polynomial"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Bernstein–Sato polynomial** is a polynomial related to [differential operators](wikipedia://en/263886), introduced independently by Joseph Bernstein (1971)[^o2] and (txt),[^o4][^o5] Sato (1990).[^o6] It is also known as the **b-function**, the **b-polynomial**, and the **Bernstein polynomial**, though it is not related to the [Bernstein polynomials](wikipedia://en/340136) used in [approximation theory](wikipedia://en/1092713). It has applications to [singularity theory](wikipedia://en/581763), [monodromy theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monodromy_theory), and [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267).

:::

(txt)[^o8] gives an elementary introduction, while (txt)[^o10] and (txt)[^o12] give more advanced accounts.

::: {#o14 .definition cue=heading name="Definition and properties"}

## Definition and properties {#Definition_and_properties}

If $f(x)$ is a polynomial in several variables, then there are a non-zero polynomial $b(s)$ and a differential operator $P(s)$ with polynomial coefficients such that

$$P(s)f(x)^{s+1} = b(s)f(x)^s.$$

The Bernstein–Sato polynomial is the [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730) of smallest degree amongst such polynomials $b(s)$. Its existence can be shown using the notion of holonomic [D-modules](wikipedia://en/3440755).

Kashiwara (1976) proved that all roots of the Bernstein–Sato polynomial are negative [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024).[^o15]

The Bernstein–Sato polynomial can also be defined for products of powers of several polynomials.[^o16] In this case it is a product of linear factors with rational coefficients.

(txt) generalized the Bernstein–Sato polynomial to arbitrary varieties.[^o18]

The Bernstein–Sato polynomial can be computed algorithmically. However, such computations are hard in general. There are implementations of related algorithms in computer algebra systems RISA/Asir, [Macaulay2](wikipedia://en/3184149), and [SINGULAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SINGULAR).

(txt)[^o20] presented algorithms to compute the Bernstein–Sato polynomial of an affine variety together with an implementation in the computer algebra system [SINGULAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SINGULAR). (txt) described some of the algorithms for computing Bernstein–Sato polynomials by computer.[^o22]

:::

::: {#o24 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- If $f(x)=x_1^2+\cdots+x_n^2 \,$ then

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \partial_i^2 f(x)^{s+1} = 4(s+1)\left(s+\frac{n}{2}\right)f(x)^s$$

so the Bernstein–Sato polynomial is

$$b(s)=(s+1)\left(s+\frac{n}{2}\right).$$

- If $f(x)=x_1^{n_1}x_2^{n_2}\cdots x_r^{n_r}$ then

$$\prod_{j=1}^r\partial_{x_j}^{n_j}\quad f(x)^{s+1} =\prod_{j=1}^r\prod_{i=1}^{n_j}(n_js+i)\quad f(x)^s$$

so

$$b(s)=\prod_{j=1}^r\prod_{i=1}^{n_j}\left(s+\frac{i}{n_j}\right).$$

- The Bernstein–Sato polynomial of *x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>3</sup> is

$$(s+1)\left(s+\frac{5}{6}\right)\left(s+\frac{7}{6}\right).$$

- If *t*<sub>*ij*</sub> are *n*<sup>2</sup> variables, then the Bernstein–Sato polynomial of det(*t*<sub>*ij*</sub>) is given by

$$(s+1)(s+2)\cdots(s+n)$$

which follows from

$$\Omega(\det(t_{ij})^s) = s(s+1)\cdots(s+n-1)\det(t_{ij})^{s-1}$$

where Ω is [Cayley's omega process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cayley's_omega_process), which in turn follows from the [Capelli identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capelli_identity).

:::

::: {#o26 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

- If $f(x)$ is a non-negative polynomial then $f(x)^s$, initially defined for *s* with non-negative real part, can be [analytically continued](wikipedia://en/80842) to a [meromorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meromorphic) [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29)-valued function of *s* by repeatedly using the [functional equation](wikipedia://en/364377)

$$f(x)^s={1\over b(s)} P(s)f(x)^{s+1}.$$

It may have poles whenever *b*(*s* + *n*) is zero for a non-negative integer *n*.

- If *f*(*x*) is a polynomial, not identically zero, then it has an inverse *g* that is a distribution;[^o27] in other words, *f g* = 1 as distributions. If *f*(*x*) is non-negative the inverse can be constructed using the Bernstein–Sato polynomial by taking the constant term of the [Laurent expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurent_expansion) of *f*(*x*)<sup>*s*</sup> at *s* = −1. For arbitrary *f*(*x*) just take $\bar f(x)$ times the inverse of $\bar f(x)f(x).$
- The [Malgrange–Ehrenpreis theorem](wikipedia://en/14160015) states that every [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886) with [constant coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constant_coefficients) has a [Green's function](wikipedia://en/311001). By taking [Fourier transforms](wikipedia://en/52247) this follows from the fact that every polynomial has a distributional inverse, which is proved in the paragraph above.
- [Pavel Etingof](wikipedia://en/40897328) (1999)[^o28] showed how to use the Bernstein polynomial to define [dimensional regularization](wikipedia://en/1934335) rigorously, in the massive Euclidean case.
- The Bernstein-Sato functional equation is used in computations of some of the more complex kinds of singular integrals occurring in [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267).[^o29] Such computations are needed for precision measurements in elementary particle physics as practiced for instance at [CERN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN) (see the papers citing[^o29]). However, the most interesting cases require a simple generalization of the Bernstein-Sato functional equation to the product of two polynomials $(f_1(x))^{s_1}(f_2(x))^{s_2}$, with *x* having 2-6 scalar components, and the pair of polynomials having orders 2 and 3. Unfortunately, a brute force determination of the corresponding differential operators $P(s_1,s_2)$ and $b(s_1,s_2)$ for such cases has so far proved prohibitively cumbersome. Devising ways to bypass the combinatorial explosion of the brute force algorithm would be of great value in such applications.

:::
