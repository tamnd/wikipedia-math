# Radical of an integer

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Radical of an integer"}

*Image: Radical of an Integer Plot.svg.* The first thousand values of *rad*(*n*). *rad(n) = n* when *n* is [square-free](wikipedia://en/29525).

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), the **radical** of a [positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number) [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *n* is defined as the product of the distinct [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) dividing *n*. Each prime factor of *n* occurs exactly once as a factor of this product:

:::

$$\displaystyle\mathrm{rad}(n)=\prod_{\scriptstyle p\mid n\atop p\text{ prime}}p$$

The radical plays a central role in the statement of the [abc conjecture](wikipedia://en/174108).[^o2]

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Radical numbers for the first few positive integers are

1, 2, 3, 2, 5, 6, 7, 2, 3, 10, 11, 6, 13, 14, 15, 2, 17, 6, 19, 10, 21, 22, 23, 6, 5, 26, 3, 14, 29, 30, 31, 2, 33, 34, 35, 6, 37, 38, 39, 10, 41, 42, 43, 22, 15, 46, 47, 6, 7, 10, ... .

For example,

$$504 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 7$$

and therefore

$$\operatorname{rad}(504) = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 7 = 42$$

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The function $\mathrm{rad}$ is [multiplicative](wikipedia://en/20059) (but not [completely multiplicative](wikipedia://en/1055940)).

The radical of any integer $n$ is the largest [square-free](wikipedia://en/29525) divisor of $n$ and so also described as the **square-free kernel** of $n$. There is no known polynomial-time algorithm for computing the square-free part of an integer.[^o8]

The definition is generalized to the largest $t$-free divisor of $n$, $\mathrm{rad}_t$, which are multiplicative functions which act on prime powers as

$$\mathrm{rad}_t(p^e) = p^{\mathrm{min}(e, t - 1)}$$

The cases $t=3$ and $t=4$ are tabulated in and .

The notion of the radical occurs in the [abc conjecture](wikipedia://en/174108), which states that, for any $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a finite $K_\varepsilon$ such that, for all triples of [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) positive integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ satisfying $a+b=c$,[^o2]

$$c < K_\varepsilon\, \operatorname{rad}(abc)^{1 + \varepsilon}$$

For any integer $n$, the [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) elements of the [finite ring](wikipedia://en/23706953) $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ are all of the multiples of $\operatorname{rad}(n)$.

The [Dirichlet series](wikipedia://en/393258) is

$$\prod_p \left(1+\frac{p^{1-s}}{1-p^{-s}}\right) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\operatorname{rad}(n)}{n^s}$$

:::

## Notation and name {#Notation_and_name}

The notation for the function was likely introduced by [Joseph Oesterlé](wikipedia://en/1604631), in the same paper where the [abc conjecture](wikipedia://en/174108) was published.[^o10] The name is related to the [radical of an ideal](wikipedia://en/252009), as radical of $I = \langle p_1^{\alpha_1}\cdots p_k^{\alpha_k}\rangle$ in the ring $\mathbb Z$ of integers is precisely $\sqrt{I} = \langle p_1\cdots p_k\rangle$.
