# Covering system

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Covering system"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **covering system** (also called a **complete residue system**) is a collection

:::

$$\{a_1\pmod{n_1},\ \ldots,\ a_k\pmod{n_k}\}$$

of finitely many [residue classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residue_class)

$$a_i\pmod{n_i} = \{ a_i + n_ix:\ x \in \mathbb{Z} \},$$

whose union contains every integer.

## Examples and definitions {#Examples_and_definitions}

The notion of covering system was introduced by [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) in the early 1930s.

The following are examples of covering systems:

1. $$\{0\pmod{3},\ 1\pmod{3},\ 2\pmod{3}\},$$
1. $$\{1\pmod{2},\ 2\pmod{4},\ 4\pmod{8},\ 0\pmod{8}\},$$
1. $$\{0\pmod{2},\ 0\pmod{3},\ 1\pmod{4}, \ 5\pmod{6},\ 7\pmod{12} \}.$$

A covering system is called *disjoint* (or *exact*) if no two members overlap.

A covering system is called *distinct* (or *incongruent*) if all the moduli $n_i$ are different (and bigger than 1). Hough and Nielsen (2019)[^o3] proved that any distinct covering system has a modulus that is divisible by either 2 or 3.

A covering system is called *irredundant* (or *minimal*) if all the residue classes are required to cover the integers.

The first two examples are disjoint.

The third example is distinct.

A system (i.e., an unordered multi-set)

$$\{a_1\pmod{n_1},\ \ldots,\ a_k\pmod{n_k}\}$$

of finitely many residue classes is called an $m$-cover if it covers every integer at least $m$ times, and an *exact* $m$-cover if it covers each integer exactly $m$ times. It is known that for each $m=2,3,\ldots$ there are exact $m$-covers which cannot be written as a union of two covers. For example,

$$\{1\pmod{2};\ 0\pmod{3};\ 2\pmod{6};\ 0,4,6,8\pmod{10};$$

$$1,2,4,7,10,13\pmod{15};\ 5,11,12,22,23,29\pmod{30}\}$$

is an exact 2-cover which is not a union of two covers.

The first example above is an exact 1-cover (also called an *exact cover*). Another [exact cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exact_cover) in common use is that of odd and [even numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_number), or

$$\{0\pmod{2},\ 1\pmod{2}\}.$$

This is just one case of the following fact: For every positive integer modulus $m$, there is an exact cover:

$$\{0\pmod{m},\ 1\pmod{m},\ \ldots,\ {m-1}\pmod{m}\}.$$

## Mirsky–Newman theorem {#Mirsky–Newman_theorem}

The Mirsky–Newman theorem, a special case of the [Herzog–Schönheim conjecture](wikipedia://en/3603349), states that there is no disjoint distinct covering system. This result was conjectured in 1950 by [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) and proved soon thereafter by [Leon Mirsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Mirsky) and [Donald J. Newman](wikipedia://en/10419510). However, Mirsky and Newman never published their proof. The same proof was also found independently by [Harold Davenport](wikipedia://en/991111) and [Richard Rado](wikipedia://en/614984).[^o6] In 1970, the Problem 8 in the Soviet mathematical olympiad for 10th grade was essentially a polygon coloring problem equivalent to the Mirsky–Newman theorem.[^o7]

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Newman-Znám theorem"}

### Newman-Znám theorem {#Newman-Znám_theorem}

[Štefan Znám](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Štefan_Znám) in 1968[^o10] and subsequently [Morris Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris_Newman) in 1971[^o11] proved a general form of Mirsky–Newman theorem, namely given a disjoint covering system, suppose the maximum of the moduli $n$ occurs $l$ times, then $l \geqq p$, the smallest prime dividing $n$. Another non-analytic proof of this general result was given in 1986.[^o12]

## Primefree sequences {#Primefree_sequences}

Covering systems can be used to find [primefree sequences](wikipedia://en/2716293), sequences of integers satisfying the same [recurrence relation](wikipedia://en/146806) as the [Fibonacci numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number), such that consecutive numbers in the sequence are [relatively prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_prime) but all numbers in the sequence are [composite numbers](wikipedia://en/82289). For instance, a sequence of this type found by [Herbert Wilf](wikipedia://en/3782688) has initial terms

*a*<sub>1</sub> = 20615674205555510, *a*<sub>2</sub> = 3794765361567513 .

In this sequence, the positions at which the numbers in the sequence are divisible by a prime *p* form an arithmetic progression; for instance, the even numbers in the sequence are the numbers *a<sub>i</sub>* where *i* is congruent to 1 mod 3. The progressions divisible by different primes form a covering system, showing that every number in the sequence is divisible by at least one prime.

## Boundedness of the smallest modulus {#Boundedness_of_the_smallest_modulus}

[Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) asked whether for any arbitrarily large *N* there exists an incongruent covering system the minimum of whose moduli is at least *N*. It is easy to construct examples where the minimum of the moduli in such a system is 2, or 3 (Erdős gave an example where the moduli are in the set of the divisors of 120; a suitable cover is 0(3), 0(4), 0(5), 1(6), 1(8), 2(10), 11(12), 1(15), 14(20), 5(24), 8(30), 6(40), 58(60), 26(120) ) D. Swift gave an example where the minimum of the moduli is 4 (and the moduli are in the set of the divisors of 2880). S. L. G. Choi proved[^o15] that it is possible to give an example for *N* = 20, and Pace P Nielsen demonstrates[^o16] the existence of an example with *N* = 40, consisting of more than $10^{50}$ congruences. Tyler Owens[^o17] demonstrates the existence of an example with *N = 42*.

Erdős's question was resolved in the negative by Bob Hough.[^o18] Hough used the [Lovász local lemma](wikipedia://en/2211763) to show that there is some maximum *N*\<10<sup>16</sup> which can be the minimum modulus on a covering system.

## Systems of odd moduli {#Systems_of_odd_moduli}

::: {#o20 .problem cue=unsolved}

Does there exist a covering system with odd distinct moduli?

:::

There is a famous unsolved conjecture from Erdős and [Selfridge](wikipedia://en/995084): an incongruent covering system (with the minimum modulus greater than 1) whose moduli are odd, does not exist. It is known that if such a system exists with square-free moduli, the overall modulus must have at least 22 prime factors.[^o21]
