# Solving chess

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Solving chess"}

**Solving chess** consists of finding an optimal strategy for the game of [chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess); that is, one by which one of the players ([White or Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_and_Black_in_chess)) can always force either a victory or a draw (see [solved game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solved_game)). It is also related to more generally solving *chess-like* games (i.e. [combinatorial games](wikipedia://en/11924#Combinatorial_games) of [perfect information](wikipedia://en/321438)) such as [Capablanca chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capablanca_chess) and [infinite chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_chess). In a weaker sense, *solving chess* may refer to proving which one of the three possible outcomes (White wins; Black wins; draw) is the result of two perfect players, without necessarily revealing the optimal strategy itself (see [indirect proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indirect_proof)).[^o2]

:::

No complete solution for chess in either of the two senses [is known](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solved_game#Partially_solved_games), nor is it expected that chess will be solved in the near future (if ever). Progress to date is extremely limited; there are [tablebases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endgame_tablebase) of perfect endgame play with a small number of pieces (up to seven), and some [chess variants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess_variant) have been solved at least weakly. Calculated estimates of [game-tree complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_complexity) and state-space complexity of chess exist which provide a bird's eye view of the computational effort that might be required to solve the game.

## Partial results {#Partial_results}

### Endgame tablebases {#Endgame_tablebases}

floatright

[Endgame tablebases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endgame_tablebase) are computerized databases that contain precalculated exhaustive analyses of positions with small numbers of pieces remaining on the board. Tablebases have solved chess to a limited degree, determining perfect play in a number of [endgames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess_endgame), including all non-trivial endgames with no more than seven pieces or pawns (including the two kings).[^o5]

One consequence of developing the seven-piece endgame tablebase is that many interesting theoretical chess endings have been found. The longest seven-piece example is a mate-in-549 position discovered in the Lomonosov tablebase by Guy Haworth, ignoring the [50-move rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/50-move_rule).[^o6][^o7] Such a position is beyond the ability of any human to solve, and no chess engine plays it correctly, either, without access to the tablebase, which initially (in 2014) required 140 TB of storage space and the use of a supercomputer but was later reduced down to 18.4 TB through the Syzygy tablebase. Such endgames are sometimes distinguished between DTM (moves to mate) and DTZ (moves to zero ply or a reset of the 50 move rule). As of January 2023, the longest known forced winning sequence for the eight-piece tablebase (also ignoring the 50-move rule) was 584 moves. This was discovered in mid-2022 by Marc Bourzutschky.[^o8] The eight-piece tablebase is currently incomplete, though, so it is not guaranteed that this is the absolute limit for the eight-piece tablebase. floatright floatright

### Chess variants {#Chess_variants}

A variant first described by [Claude Shannon](wikipedia://en/5693) provides an argument about the game-theoretic value of chess: he proposes allowing the move of “pass”. In this variant, it is provable with a [strategy stealing argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategy_stealing_argument) that the first player has at least a draw thus: if the first player has a winning move in the initial position, let him play it, else pass. The second player now faces the same situation owing to the mirror symmetry of the initial position: if the first player had no winning move in the first instance, the second player has none now. Therefore, the second player can at best draw, and the first player can at least draw, so a perfect game results in the first player winning or drawing.[^o14]

Some [chess variants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess_Variant) which are simpler than chess have been solved. A winning strategy for Black in [Maharajah and the Sepoys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maharajah_and_the_Sepoys) can be easily memorised. The 5×5 [Gardner's Minichess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minichess#5×5_chess) variant has been [weakly solved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solved_game) as a draw.[^o10] Although [losing chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Losing_Chess) is played on an 8×8 board, its forced capture rule greatly limits its complexity, and a computational analysis managed to weakly solve this variant as a win for White.[^o11]

The prospect of solving individual, specific, chess-like games becomes more difficult as the board-size is increased, such as in large chess variants, and [infinite chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_chess).[^o12]

## Complexity of chess {#Complexity_of_chess}

[Information theorist](wikipedia://en/14773) [Claude Shannon](wikipedia://en/5693) in 1950 outlined a theoretical procedure for playing a perfect game (i.e. solving chess): "With chess it is possible, in principle, to play a perfect game or construct a machine to do so as follows: One considers in a given position all possible moves, then all moves for the opponent, etc., to the end of the game (in each variation). The end must occur, by the rules of the games after a finite number of moves (remembering the [50 move drawing rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifty-move_rule)). Each of these variations ends in win, loss or draw. By working backward from the end one can determine whether there is a forced win, the position is a draw or is lost."

Shannon then went on to estimate that solving chess according to that procedure would require comparing some 10${}^{120}$ ([Shannon number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_number)) possible game variations, or having a "dictionary" denoting an optimal move for each of the approximately 4.8x10${}^{44}$ possible board positions.[^o14][^o15] The number of mathematical operations required to solve chess, however, may be significantly different than the number of operations required to produce the entire [game-tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_tree) of chess. In particular, if White has a forced win, only a subset of the game-tree would require evaluation to confirm that a forced-win exists (i.e. with no refutations from Black). Furthermore, Shannon's calculation for the complexity of chess assumes an average game length of 40 moves, but there is no mathematical basis to say that a forced win by either side would have any relation to this game length. Indeed, some expertly played games (grandmaster-level play) have been as short as 16 moves. For these reasons, mathematicians and game theorists have been reluctant to categorically state that solving chess is an intractable problem.[^o14][^o16]

## Predictions on when or if chess will be solved {#Predictions_on_when_or_if_chess_will_be_solved}

In 1950, Shannon calculated, based on a game tree complexity of 10${}^{120}$ and a computer operating at one megahertz (a big stretch at that time: the UNIVAC 1 introduced in 1951 could perform ~2000 operations per second or 2 kilohertz) that could evaluate a terminal node in 1 microsecond would take 10${}^{90}$ years to make its first move. Even allowing for technological advances, solving chess within a practical time frame would therefore seem beyond any conceivable technology.

[Hans-Joachim Bremermann](wikipedia://en/14914165), a professor of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [biophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biophysics) at the [University of California at Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California_at_Berkeley), further argued in a 1965 paper that the "speed, memory, and processing capacity of any possible future computer equipment are limited by specific physical barriers: the *[light barrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light)*, the *quantum barrier*, and the *thermodynamical barrier*. These limitations imply, for example, that no computer, however constructed, will ever be able to examine the entire tree of possible move sequences of the game of chess." Nonetheless, Bremermann did not foreclose the possibility that a computer would someday be able to solve chess. He wrote, "In order to have a computer play a perfect or nearly perfect game, it will be necessary either to analyze the game completely ... or to analyze the game in an approximate way and combine this with a limited amount of tree searching. ... A theoretical understanding of such heuristic programming, however, is still very much wanting."[^o18]

Recent scientific advances have not significantly changed these assessments. The game of [checkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_draughts) was (weakly) solved in 2007,[^o19] but it has roughly the square root of the number of positions in chess. [Jonathan Schaeffer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Schaeffer), the scientist who led the effort, said a breakthrough such as [quantum computing](wikipedia://en/25220) would be needed before solving chess could even be attempted, but he does not rule out the possibility, saying that the one thing he learned from his 16-year effort of solving checkers "is to never underestimate the advances in technology".[^o20]
