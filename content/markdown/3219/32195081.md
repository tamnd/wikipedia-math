# Q-exponential distribution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Q-exponential distribution"}

The ***q*-exponential distribution** is a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) arising from the maximization of the [Tsallis entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsallis_entropy) under appropriate constraints, including constraining the domain to be positive. It is one example of a [Tsallis distribution](wikipedia://en/22342404). The *q*-exponential is a generalization of the [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) in the same way that Tsallis entropy is a generalization of standard [Boltzmann–Gibbs entropy](wikipedia://en/4701125) or [Shannon entropy](wikipedia://en/15445).[^o2] The exponential distribution is recovered as $q \rightarrow 1.$

:::

Originally proposed by the statisticians [George Box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Box) and [David Cox](wikipedia://en/1990771) in 1964,[^o3] and known as the reverse [Box–Cox transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box–Cox_transformation) for $q=1-\lambda,$ a particular case of [power transform](wikipedia://en/9795777) in statistics.

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Characterization"}

## Characterization {#Characterization}

### Probability density function {#Probability_density_function}

The *q*-exponential distribution has the probability density function

$$(2-q) \lambda e_q(-\lambda x)$$

where

$$e_q(x) = [1+(1-q)x]^{1/(1-q)}$$

is the [*q*-exponential](wikipedia://en/3599748#q-exponential) if *q* ≠ 1. When *q* = 1, *e*<sub>*q*</sub>(x) is just exp(*x*).

:::

## Derivation {#Derivation}

In a similar procedure to how the [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) can be derived (using the standard Boltzmann–Gibbs entropy or Shannon entropy and constraining the domain of the variable to be positive), the *q*-exponential distribution can be derived from a maximization of the Tsallis Entropy subject to the appropriate constraints.

## Relationship to other distributions {#Relationship_to_other_distributions}

The *q*-exponential is a special case of the [generalized Pareto distribution](wikipedia://en/8599355) where

$$\mu = 0,\quad \xi = \frac{q-1}{2-q} ,\quad \sigma = \frac{1}{\lambda (2-q)}.$$

The *q*-exponential is the generalization of the [Lomax distribution](wikipedia://en/32253318) (Pareto Type II), as it extends this distribution to the cases of finite support. The Lomax parameters are:

$$\alpha = \frac{2-q}{q-1} ,\quad \lambda_\mathrm{Lomax} = \frac{1}{\lambda (q-1)}.$$

As the Lomax distribution is a shifted version of the [Pareto distribution](wikipedia://en/53057), the *q*-exponential is a shifted reparameterized generalization of the Pareto. When *q* > 1, the *q*-exponential is equivalent to the Pareto shifted to have support starting at zero. Specifically, if

$$X \sim \operatorname{\mathit{q}-Exp}(q,\lambda) \text{ and } Y \sim \left[\operatorname{Pareto}\left(x_m = \frac{1}{\lambda (q-1)}, \alpha = \frac{2-q}{q-1}\right) -x_m\right],$$

then $X \sim Y.$

## Generating random deviates {#Generating_random_deviates}

Random deviates can be drawn using [inverse transform sampling](wikipedia://en/45705). Given a variable *U* that is uniformly distributed on the interval (0,1), then

$$X = \frac{-q' \ln_{q'}(U)}{\lambda} \sim \operatorname{\mathit{q}-Exp}(q,\lambda)$$

where $\ln_{q'}$ is the [*q*-logarithm](wikipedia://en/3599748#q-logarithm) and $q' = \frac{1}{2-q}.$

::: {#o11 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Being a [power transform](wikipedia://en/9795777), it is a usual technique in statistics for stabilizing the variance, making the data more normal distribution-like and improving the validity of measures of association such as the Pearson correlation between variables. It has been found to be an accurate model for train delays.[^o12] It is also found in atomic physics and quantum optics, for example processes of molecular condensate creation via transition through the Feshbach resonance.[^o13]

:::
