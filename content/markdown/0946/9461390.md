# Riemann solver

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Riemann solver"}

A **Riemann solver** is a [numerical method](wikipedia://en/1678715) used to solve a [hyperbolic partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/1575825) based on the solution of the corresponding [Riemann problem](wikipedia://en/9461236). They are heavily used in [computational fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/305924) and [computational magnetohydrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_magnetohydrodynamics).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Generally speaking, Riemann solvers are specific methods for computing the numerical flux across a discontinuity in the Riemann problem.[^o4] They form an important part of [high-resolution schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-resolution_scheme); typically the right and left states for the Riemann problem are calculated using some form of nonlinear reconstruction, such as a [flux limiter](wikipedia://en/5678338) or a [WENO method](wikipedia://en/59111950), and then used as the input for the Riemann solver.[^o5]

:::

## Exact solvers {#Exact_solvers}

[Sergei K. Godunov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_K._Godunov) is credited with introducing the first exact Riemann solver for the Euler equations,[^o7] by extending the previous CIR (Courant-Isaacson-Rees) method to non-linear systems of hyperbolic conservation laws. Modern solvers are able to simulate relativistic effects and magnetic fields.

More recent research shows that an exact series solution to the Riemann problem exists, which may converge fast enough in some cases to avoid the iterative methods required in Godunov's scheme.[^o8]

## Approximate solvers {#Approximate_solvers}

As iterative solutions are too costly, especially in magnetohydrodynamics, some approximations have to be made. Some popular solvers are:

### Roe solver {#Roe_solver}

[Philip L. Roe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_L._Roe) used the linearisation of the Jacobian, which he then solves exactly.[^o11]

### HLLE solver {#HLLE_solver}

The HLLE solver (developed by [Ami Harten](wikipedia://en/20340671), [Peter Lax](wikipedia://en/486365), [Bram van Leer](wikipedia://en/7532012) and B. Einfeldt) is an approximate solution to the Riemann problem, which is only based on the integral form of the conservation laws and the largest and smallest signal velocities at the interface.[^o13][^o14] The stability and robustness of the HLLE solver is closely related to the signal velocities and a single central average state, as proposed by Einfeldt in the original paper

### HLLC solver {#HLLC_solver}

The HLLC (Harten-Lax-van Leer-Contact) solver was introduced by Toro.[^o16] It restores the missing rarefaction wave by using an estimation technique, such as linearisation. More advanced techniques exist, like using the Roe average velocity for the middle wave speed. These schemes are quite robust and efficient but somewhat more diffusive.[^o17]

### Rotated-hybrid Riemann solvers {#Rotated-hybrid_Riemann_solvers}

These solvers were introduced by [Hiroaki Nishikawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiroaki_Nishikawa) and Kitamura,[^o19] in order to overcome the carbuncle problems of the Roe solver and the excessive diffusion of the HLLE solver at the same time. They developed robust and accurate Riemann solvers by combining the Roe solver and the HLLE/Rusanov solvers: they show that being applied in two orthogonal directions the two Riemann solvers can be combined into a single Roe-type solver (the Roe solver with modified wave speeds). In particular, the one derived from the Roe and HLLE solvers, called Rotated-RHLL solver, is extremely robust (carbuncle-free for all possible test cases on both structured and unstructured grids) and accurate (as accurate as the Roe solver for the boundary layer calculation).

### Other solvers {#Other_solvers}

There are a variety of other solvers available, including more variants of the HLL scheme[^o21] and solvers based on flux-splitting via characteristic decomposition.[^o22]
