# Natural numbers object

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Natural numbers object"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869) in mathematics, a **natural numbers object** (**NNO**) is an object endowed with a [recursive](wikipedia://en/4044867) [structure](wikipedia://en/1809181) similar to [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474). More precisely, in a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) **E** with a [terminal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_object) 1, an NNO *N* is given by:

:::

1. a [global element](wikipedia://en/14717987) *z* : 1 → *N*, and
1. an [arrow](wikipedia://en/27716891) *s* : *N* → *N*,

such that for any object *A* of **E**, global element *q* : 1 → *A*, and arrow *f* : *A* → *A*, there exists a unique arrow *u* : *N* → *A* such that:

1. *u* ∘ *z* = *q*, and
1. *u* ∘ *s* = *f* ∘ *u*.[^n1][^n2][^o4]

In other words, the triangle and square in the following diagram commute.

*Image: Natural numbers object definition.svg.* A commutative diagram expressing the equations in the definition of an NNO

The pair (*q*, *f*) is sometimes called the *recursion data* for *u*, given in the form of a [recursive definition](wikipedia://en/1047605):

1. ⊢ *u* (*z*) = *q*
1. *y* ∈<sub>**E**</sub> *N* ⊢ *u* (*s* *y*) = *f* (*u* (*y*))

The above definition is the [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245) of NNOs, meaning they are defined [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [canonical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mathematical_jargon#canonical) [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/27716891). If the arrow *u* as defined above merely has to exist, that is, uniqueness is not required, then *N* is called a *weak* NNO.

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Equivalent definitions"}

## Equivalent definitions {#Equivalent_definitions}

NNOs in [cartesian closed categories](wikipedia://en/237037) (CCCs) are sometimes defined in the following equivalent way (due to [Lawvere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawvere)): for every pair of arrows *g* : *A* → *B* and *f* : *B* → *B*, there is a unique *h* : *N* × *A* → *B* such that the squares in the following diagram commute.[^n3]

*Image: NNO definition alt.png.* alternate NNO definition

This same construction defines weak NNOs in cartesian categories that are not cartesian closed.

In a category with a terminal object 1 and binary [coproducts](wikipedia://en/310889) (denoted by +), an NNO can be defined as the [initial algebra](wikipedia://en/4116488) of the [endofunctor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endofunctor) that acts on objects by $X \mapsto 1 + X$ and on arrows by $f \mapsto \operatorname{id}_{1} + f$.[^o8]

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

- Every NNO is an initial object of the category of [diagrams](wikipedia://en/7651625) of the form

$$1 \xrightarrow{~ \quad q \quad ~} A \xrightarrow{~ \quad f \quad ~} A$$

- If a cartesian closed category has weak NNOs, then every [slice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slice_category) of it also has a weak NNO.
- NNOs can be used for [non-standard models](wikipedia://en/30875811) of [type theory](wikipedia://en/40282) in a way analogous to non-standard models of analysis. Such categories (or topoi) tend to have "infinitely many" non-standard natural numbers. (Like always, there are simple ways to get non-standard NNOs; for example, if *z* = *s z*, in which case the category or topos **E** is trivial.)
- [Freyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Freyd) showed that *z* and *s* form a [coproduct](wikipedia://en/310889) diagram for NNOs; also, !<sub>*N*</sub> : *N* → 1 is a [coequalizer](wikipedia://en/431940) of *s* and 1<sub>*N*</sub>, *i.e.*, every pair of global elements of *N* are connected by means of *s*; furthermore, this pair of facts characterize all NNOs.

:::

::: {#o12 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- In **Set**, the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829), the standard natural numbers are an NNO.[^n4] A terminal object in **Set** is a [singleton](wikipedia://en/381798), and a function out of a singleton picks out a single [element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28set_theory%29) of a set. The natural numbers 𝐍 are an NNO where $z$ is a function from a singleton to 𝐍 whose [image](wikipedia://en/579311) is zero, and $s$ is the [successor function](wikipedia://en/325637). (We could actually allow $z$ to pick out *any* element of 𝐍, and the resulting NNO would be isomorphic to this one.) One can prove that the diagram in the definition commutes using [mathematical induction](wikipedia://en/18881).
- In the category of types of [Martin-Löf type theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin-Löf_type_theory) (with types as objects and functions as arrows), the standard natural numbers type **nat** is an NNO. One can use the recursor for **nat** to show that the appropriate diagram commutes.
- Assume that $\mathcal{E}$ is a [Grothendieck topos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck_topos) with terminal object $\top$ and that $\mathcal{E} \simeq \mathbf{Shv}(\mathfrak{C},J)$ for some [Grothendieck topology](wikipedia://en/12910) $J$ on the category $\mathfrak{C}$. Then if $\Gamma_{\mathbb{N}}$ is the constant presheaf on $\mathfrak{C}$, then the NNO in $\mathcal{E}$ is the sheafification of $\Gamma_{\mathbb{N}}$ and may be shown to take the form $$\mathbb{N}_{\mathcal{E}} \cong \left(\Gamma_{\mathbb{N}}\right)^{++} \cong \coprod_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \top.$$

:::
