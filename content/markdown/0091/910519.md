# Harmonic conjugate

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Harmonic conjugate"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $u(x,y)$ defined on a [connected open set](wikipedia://en/16796643) $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ is said to have a conjugate (function) $v(x,y)$ if and only if they are respectively the [real and imaginary parts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_and_imaginary_parts) of a [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110) $f(z)$ of the [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) variable $z:=x+iy\in\Omega.$ That is, $v$ is conjugate to $u$ if $f(z):=u(x,y)+iv(x,y)$ is holomorphic on $\Omega.$ As a first consequence of the definition, they are both [harmonic](wikipedia://en/55762) real-valued functions on $\Omega$. Moreover, the conjugate of $u,$ if it exists, is unique [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) an additive constant. Also, $u$ is conjugate to $v$ if and only if $v$ is conjugate to $-u$.

:::

## Description {#Description}

Equivalently, $v$ is conjugate to $u$ in $\Omega$ if and only if $u$ and $v$ satisfy the [Cauchy–Riemann equations](wikipedia://en/7583) in $\Omega.$ As an immediate consequence of the latter equivalent definition, if $u$ is any harmonic function on $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^2,$ the function $-u_y$ is conjugate to $u_x$ for then the Cauchy–Riemann equations are just $\Delta u = 0$ and the [symmetry of the mixed second order derivatives](wikipedia://en/412094), $u_{xy}=u_{yx}.$ Therefore, a harmonic function $u$ admits a conjugated harmonic function if and only if the holomorphic function $g(z) := u_x(x,y) - i u_y(x,y)$ has a [primitive](wikipedia://en/6038197) $f(z)$ in $\Omega,$ in which case a conjugate of $u$ is, of course, $\operatorname{Im} f(x+iy).$ So any harmonic function always admits a conjugate function whenever its [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) is [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected), and in any case it admits a conjugate locally at any point of its domain.

There is an [operator](wikipedia://en/22213) taking a harmonic function *u* on a simply connected region in $\mathbb{R}^2$ to its harmonic conjugate *v* (putting e.g. *v*(*x*<sub>0</sub>) = 0 on a given *x*<sub>0</sub> in order to fix the indeterminacy of the conjugate up to constants). This is well known in applications as (essentially) the [Hilbert transform](wikipedia://en/574024); it is also a basic example in [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), in connection with [singular integral operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_integral_operator). Conjugate harmonic functions (and the transform between them) are also one of the simplest examples of a [Bäcklund transform](wikipedia://en/701077) (two [PDEs](wikipedia://en/52564) and a transform relating their solutions), in this case linear; more complex transforms are of interest in [solitons](wikipedia://en/51654) and [integrable systems](wikipedia://en/2573213).

Geometrically *u* and *v* are related as having *[orthogonal trajectories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_trajectories)*, away from the [zeros](wikipedia://en/81560) of the underlying holomorphic function; the contours on which *u* and *v* are constant cross at [right angles](wikipedia://en/76956). In this regard, *u* + *iv* would be the [complex potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_potential), where *u* is the [potential function](wikipedia://en/1431342) and *v* is the [stream function](wikipedia://en/218091).

## Examples {#Examples}

For example, consider the function

$$u(x,y) = e^x \sin y.$$

Since

$${\partial u \over \partial x } = e^x \sin y, \quad {\partial^2 u \over \partial x^2} = e^x \sin y$$

and

$${\partial u \over \partial y} = e^x \cos y, \quad {\partial^2 u \over \partial y^2} = - e^x \sin y,$$

it satisfies

$$\Delta u = \nabla^2 u = 0$$

($\Delta$ is the [Laplace operator](wikipedia://en/174706)) and is thus harmonic. Now suppose we have a $v(x,y)$ such that the Cauchy–Riemann equations are satisfied:

$${\partial u \over \partial x} = {\partial v \over \partial y} = e^x \sin y$$

and

$${\partial u \over \partial y} = -{\partial v \over \partial x} = e^x \cos y.$$

Simplifying,

$${\partial v \over \partial y} = e^x \sin y$$

and

$${\partial v \over \partial x} = -e^x \cos y$$

which when solved gives

$$v = -e^x \cos y + C.$$

Observe that if the functions related to $u$ and $v$ were interchanged, the functions would not be harmonic conjugates, since the minus sign in the Cauchy–Riemann equations makes the relationship asymmetric.

The [conformal mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_mapping) property of [analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478) (at points where the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) is not zero) gives rise to a geometric property of harmonic conjugates. Clearly the harmonic conjugate of *x* is *y*, and the lines of constant *x* and constant *y* are orthogonal. Conformality says that [contours](wikipedia://en/704359) of constant $u(x, y)$ and $v(x, y)$ will also be orthogonal where they cross (away from the zeros of $f\,'(z)$). That means that *v* is a specific solution of the [orthogonal trajectory](wikipedia://en/2564849) problem for the family of contours given by *u* (not the only solution, naturally, since we can take also functions of *v*): the question, going back to the mathematics of the seventeenth century, of finding the curves that cross a given family of non-intersecting curves at right angles.
