# Subspace topology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Subspace topology"}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) and related areas of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **subspace** of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $(X, \tau)$ is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) *S* of *X* which is equipped with a [topology](wikipedia://en/30450#Definitions) induced from that of $\tau$ called the **subspace topology**[^o2] (or the **relative topology**,[^o2] **inherited topology**,[^o3] **induced topology**,[^o2] or **trace topology**).[^o4]

:::

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given a topological space $(X, \tau)$ and a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $S$ of $X$, the **subspace topology** on $S$ is defined by

$$\tau_S = \lbrace S \cap U \mid U \in \tau \rbrace.$$

That is, a subset of $S$ is open in the subspace topology [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of $S$ with an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) in $(X, \tau)$. If $S$ is equipped with the subspace topology then it is a topological space in its own right, and is called a **subspace** of $(X, \tau)$. Subsets of topological spaces are usually assumed to be equipped with the subspace topology unless otherwise stated.

Alternatively we can define the subspace topology for a subset $S$ of $X$ as the [coarsest topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coarsest_topology) for which the [inclusion map](wikipedia://en/492516)

$$\iota: S \hookrightarrow X$$

is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_%28topology%29).

More generally, suppose $\iota$ is an [injection](wikipedia://en/45196) from a set $S$ to a topological space $X$. Then the subspace topology on $S$ is defined as the coarsest topology for which $\iota$ is continuous. The open sets in this topology are precisely the ones of the form $\iota^{-1}(U)$ for $U$ open in $X$. $S$ is then [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to its image in $X$ (also with the subspace topology) and $\iota$ is called a [topological embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_embedding).

A subspace $S$ is called an **open subspace** if the injection $\iota$ is an [open map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_map), i.e., if the forward image of an open set of $S$ is open in $X$. Likewise it is called a **closed subspace** if the injection $\iota$ is a [closed map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_map).

:::

::: {#o8 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Terminology"}

## Terminology {#Terminology}

The distinction between a set and a topological space is often blurred notationally, for convenience, which can be a source of confusion when one first encounters these definitions. Thus, whenever $S$ is a subset of $X$, and $(X, \tau)$ is a topological space, then the unadorned symbols "$S$" and "$X$" can often be used to refer both to $S$ and $X$ considered as two subsets of $X$, and also to $(S,\tau_S)$ and $(X,\tau)$ as the topological spaces, related as discussed above. So phrases such as "$S$ an open subspace of $X$" are used to mean that $(S,\tau_S)$ is an open subspace of $(X,\tau)$, in the sense used above; that is: (i) $S \in \tau$; and (ii) $S$ is considered to be endowed with the subspace topology.

:::

::: {#o10 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

In the following, $\mathbb{R}$ represents the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) with their usual topology.

- The subspace topology of the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474), as a subspace of $\mathbb{R}$, is the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology).
- The [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) $\mathbb{Q}$ considered as a subspace of $\mathbb{R}$ do not have the discrete topology ({0} for example is not an open set in $\mathbb{Q}$ because there is no open subset of $\mathbb{R}$ whose intersection with $\mathbb{Q}$ can result in *only* the [singleton](wikipedia://en/381798) {0}). If *a* and *b* are rational, then the intervals (*a*, *b*) and \[*a*, *b*\] are respectively open and closed, but if *a* and *b* are irrational, then the set of all rational *x* with *a* \< *x* \< *b* is both open and closed.
- The set \[0,1\] as a subspace of $\mathbb{R}$ is both open and closed, whereas as a subset of $\mathbb{R}$ it is only closed.
- As a subspace of $\mathbb{R}$, \[0, 1\] ∪ \[2, 3\] is composed of two disjoint *open* subsets (which happen also to be closed), and is therefore a [disconnected space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disconnected_space).
- Let *S* = \[0, 1) be a subspace of the real line $\mathbb{R}$. Then \[0, $\tfrac{1}{2}$) is open in *S* but not in $\mathbb{R}$ (as for example the intersection between (-$\tfrac{1}{2}$, $\tfrac{1}{2}$) and *S* results in \[0, $\tfrac{1}{2}$)). Likewise \[$\tfrac{1}{2}$, 1) is closed in *S* but not in $\mathbb{R}$ (as there is no open subset of $\mathbb{R}$ that can intersect with \[0, 1) to result in \[$\tfrac{1}{2}$, 1)). *S* is both open and closed as a subset of itself but not as a subset of $\mathbb{R}$.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

The subspace topology has the following characteristic property. Let $Y$ be a subspace of $X$ and let $i : Y \to X$ be the inclusion map. Then for any topological space $Z$ a map $f : Z\to Y$ is continuous [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the composite map $i\circ f$ is continuous.

*Image: Subspace-01.svg.* Characteristic property of the subspace topology

This property is characteristic in the sense that it can be used to define the subspace topology on $Y$.

We list some further properties of the subspace topology. In the following let $S$ be a subspace of $X$.

- If $f:X\to Y$ is continuous then the restriction to $S$ is continuous.
- If $f:X\to Y$ is continuous then $f:X\to f(X)$ is continuous.
- The closed sets in $S$ are precisely the intersections of $S$ with closed sets in $X$.
- If $A$ is a subspace of $S$ then $A$ is also a subspace of $X$ with the same topology. In other words, the subspace topology that $A$ inherits from $S$ is the same as the one it inherits from $X$.
- Suppose $S$ is an open subspace of $X$ (so $S\in\tau$). Then a subset of $S$ is open in $S$ if and only if it is open in $X$.
- Suppose $S$ is a closed subspace of $X$ (so $X\setminus S\in\tau$). Then a subset of $S$ is closed in $S$ if and only if it is closed in $X$.
- If $B$ is a [basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_%28topology%29) for $X$ then $B_S = \{U\cap S : U \in B\}$ is a basis for $S$.
- The topology induced on a subset of a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) by restricting the [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) to this subset coincides with subspace topology for this subset.

:::

## Preservation of topological properties {#Preservation_of_topological_properties}

If a topological space having some [topological property](wikipedia://en/1337282) implies its subspaces have that property, then we say the property is **hereditary**. If only closed subspaces must share the property we call it **weakly hereditary**.

- Every open and every closed subspace of a [completely metrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_metrizable) space is completely metrizable.
- Every open subspace of a [Baire space](wikipedia://en/200550) is a Baire space.
- Every closed subspace of a [compact space](wikipedia://en/6042) is compact.
- Being a [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637) is hereditary.
- Being a [normal space](wikipedia://en/48629) is weakly hereditary.
- [Total boundedness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_boundedness) is hereditary.
- Being [totally disconnected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_disconnected) is hereditary.
- [First countability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_countability) and [second countability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_countability) are hereditary.
