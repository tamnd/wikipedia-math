# Counterexamples in Topology

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Counterexamples in Topology"}

***Counterexamples in Topology*** (1970, 2nd ed. 1978) is a book on [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) by [topologists](wikipedia://en/29954) [Lynn Steen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynn_Steen) and [J. Arthur Seebach, Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Arthur_Seebach,_Jr.)

:::

In the process of working on problems like the [metrization problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrization_problem), topologists (including Steen and Seebach) defined a wide variety of [topological properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_properties). It is often useful in the study and understanding of abstracts such as [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) to determine that one property does not follow from another. One of the easiest ways of doing this is to find a [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) which exhibits one property but not the other. In *Counterexamples in Topology*, Steen and Seebach, together with five students in an undergraduate research project at [St. Olaf College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Olaf_College), [Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnesota) in the summer of 1967, canvassed the field of [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) for such counterexamples and compiled them in an attempt to simplify the literature.

For instance, an example of a [first-countable space](wikipedia://en/904940) which is not [second-countable](wikipedia://en/901459) is counterexample #3, the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology) on an [uncountable set](wikipedia://en/32248). This particular counterexample shows that second-countability does not follow from first-countability.

Several other "Counterexamples in ..." books and papers have followed, with similar motivations.

## Reviews {#Reviews}

In her review of the first edition, [Mary Ellen Rudin](wikipedia://en/2976742) wrote:

In other mathematical fields one restricts one's problem by requiring that the [space](wikipedia://en/30450) be [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) or [paracompact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paracompact) or [metric](wikipedia://en/20018), and usually one doesn't really care which, so long as the restriction is strong enough to avoid this dense forest of counterexamples. A usable map of the forest is a fine thing...[^o4]

In his submission[^o5] to [Mathematical Reviews](wikipedia://en/1485646) C. Wayne Patty wrote:

...the book is extremely useful, and the general topology student will no doubt find it very valuable. In addition it is very well written.

When the second edition appeared in 1978 its review in [Advances in Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advances_in_Mathematics) treated topology as territory to be explored:

[Lebesgue](wikipedia://en/49514) once said that every mathematician should be something of a [naturalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_%28philosophy%29). This book, the updated journal of a continuing expedition to the never-never land of general topology, should appeal to the latent naturalist in every mathematician.[^o6]

## Notation {#Notation}

Several of the [naming conventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naming_convention) in this book differ from more accepted modern conventions, particularly with respect to the [separation axioms](wikipedia://en/40117302). The authors use the terms T<sub>3</sub>, T<sub>4</sub>, and T<sub>5</sub> to refer to [regular](wikipedia://en/53058), [normal](wikipedia://en/48629), and [completely normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_normal_space). They also refer to [completely Hausdorff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_Hausdorff_space) as [Urysohn](wikipedia://en/327166). This was a result of the different historical development of metrization theory and [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649); see [History of the separation axioms](wikipedia://en/1481097) for more.

The [long line](wikipedia://en/338403) in example 45 is what most topologists nowadays would call the 'closed long ray'.
