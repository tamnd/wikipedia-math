# Parallel curve

*Image: Offset-definition-poss.svg.* Two definitions of a parallel curve: 1) envelope of a family of congruent circles, 2) by a fixed normal distance

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Parallel curve"}

A **parallel curve** of a given (progenitor) [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) is the [envelope](wikipedia://en/880235) of a family of [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) (equal-radius) [circles](wikipedia://en/6220) centered on the curve. It generalises the concept of *[parallel (straight) lines](wikipedia://en/664497)*. It can also be defined as a curve whose points are at a constant *[normal distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_distance)* from a given curve.[^o2] These two definitions are not entirely equivalent as the latter assumes [smoothness](wikipedia://en/1408000), whereas the former does not.[^o3]

:::

In [computer-aided design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer-aided_design) the preferred term for a parallel curve is **offset curve**.[^o3][^o8][^o4] (In other geometric contexts, the term ["offset"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offset_%28disambiguation%29) can also refer to a [translation](wikipedia://en/204682); however, a parallel curve may have a different [shape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_%28geometry%29) than its progenitor.[^o5]) Offset curves are important, for example, in [numerically controlled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerically_controlled) (NC) [machining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machining), where they describe, for example, the shape of the cut made by a round cutting tool of a two-axis machine. The shape of the cut is offset from the trajectory of the cutter by a constant distance in the direction normal to the cutter trajectory at every point.[^o6]

In the area of 2D [computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_graphics) known as [vector graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_graphics), the (approximate) computation of parallel curves is involved in one of the fundamental drawing operations, called stroking, which is typically applied to [polylines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyline) or [polybeziers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polybezier) (themselves called paths) in that field.[^o7]

*Image: Offset-curves-of-sinus-curve.svg.* Parallel curves of the graph of $y=1.5 \sin(x)$ (in red) for distances $d = 0.25, \dots, 1.5$

Except in the case of a line or [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), the parallel curves have a more complicated mathematical structure than the progenitor curve.[^o2] For example, even if the progenitor curve is [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function), its offsets may not be so; this property is illustrated in the top figure, using a [sine curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_curve) as progenitor curve.[^o3] In general, even if a curve is [rational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_curve), its offsets may not be so. For example, the offsets of a parabola are rational curves, but the offsets of an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) or of a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) are not rational, even though these progenitor curves themselves are rational.[^o8]

The notion also generalizes to 3D [surfaces](wikipedia://en/4342970), where it is called an **offset surface** or **parallel surface**.[^o10] Increasing a [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_%28geometry%29) volume by a (constant) distance offset is sometimes called *dilation* (similar to the [dilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilation_%28morphology%29) image operation).[^o9] The opposite operation is sometimes called *shelling*.[^o10] Offset surfaces are important in NC, where they describe the shape of the cut made by a ball nose [end mill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_mill) of a three-axis machine.[^o11] Other shapes of cutting bits can be modelled mathematically by general offset surfaces.[^o12]

## Parallel curve of a parametrically given curve {#Parallel_curve_of_a_parametrically_given_curve}

If there is a regular parametric representation $\vec x= (x(t),y(t))$ of the given curve available, the second definition of a parallel curve (s. above) leads to the following parametric representation of the parallel curve with distance $|d|$:

$\vec x_d(t)=\vec x(t)+d\vec n(t)$ with the unit normal $\vec n(t)$.

In cartesian coordinates:

$$x_d(t)= x(t)+\frac{d\; y'(t)}{\sqrt {x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2}}$$

$$y_d(t)= y(t)-\frac{d\; x'(t)}{\sqrt {x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2}} \ .$$

The distance parameter $d$ may be negative. In this case, one gets a parallel curve on the opposite side of the curve (see diagram on the parallel curves of a circle). One can easily check that a parallel curve of a line is a parallel line in the common sense, and the parallel curve of a circle is a concentric circle.

### Geometric properties {#Geometric_properties}

Source:[^o15]

- $\vec x'_d(t) \parallel \vec x'(t),\quad$ that means: the tangent vectors for a fixed parameter are parallel.
- $k_d(t)=\frac{k(t)}{1+dk(t)},\quad$ with $k(t)$ the [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) of the given curve and $k_d(t)$ the curvature of the parallel curve for parameter $t$.
- $R_d(t)=R(t) + d,\quad$ with $R(t)$ the [radius of curvature](wikipedia://en/60770#Curvature_of_plane_curves) of the given curve and $R_d(t)$ the radius of curvature of the parallel curve for parameter $t$.
- When they exist, the [osculating circles](wikipedia://en/1896705) to parallel curves at corresponding points are concentric. [^o16]
- As for [parallel lines](wikipedia://en/664497), a normal line to a curve is also normal to its parallels.
- When parallel curves are constructed they will have [cusps](wikipedia://en/6851758) when the distance from the curve matches the radius of [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770). These are the points where the curve touches the [evolute](wikipedia://en/842387).
- If the progenitor curve is a boundary of a planar set and its parallel curve is without self-intersections, then the latter is the boundary of the [Minkowski sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_sum) of the planar set and the disk of the given radius.

If the given curve is polynomial (meaning that $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ are polynomials), then the parallel curves are usually not polynomial. In the CAD area this is a drawback, because CAD systems use polynomials or rational curves. In order to get at least rational curves, the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of the representation of the parallel curve has to be solvable. Such curves are called [Pythagorean hodograph curves](wikipedia://en/80589686) and were investigated by R.T. Farouki.[^o17]

## Parallel curves of an implicit curve {#Parallel_curves_of_an_implicit_curve}

*Image: Offset-of-implicit-curve-c4.svg.* Parallel curves of the implicit curve (red) with equation $x^4+y^4-1=0$

Not all [implicit curves](wikipedia://en/11286809) have parallel curves with analytic representations, but this is possible in some special cases. For instance, the [Pythagorean hodograph curves](wikipedia://en/80589686) are rational curves with rational parallel curves, which can be converted to implicit representations. Another class of implicit rational curves with rational parallel curves is the [parabolas](wikipedia://en/23231).[^n1] For the simpler cases of lines and circles the parallel curves can be described easily. For example:

*Line* $\; f(x,y)=x+y-1=0\;$ → distance function: $\; h(x,y)=\frac{x+y-1}{\sqrt{2}}=d\;$ (Hesse normalform)

*Circle* $\; f(x,y)=x^2+y^2-1=0\;$ → distance function: $\; h(x,y)=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}-1=d\; .$

In general, presuming certain conditions, one can prove the existence of an [oriented distance function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented_distance_function) $h(x,y)$. In practice one has to treat it numerically.[^o20] Considering parallel curves the following is true:

- The parallel curve for distance d is the [level set](wikipedia://en/559622) $h(x,y)=d$ of the corresponding oriented distance function $h$.

### Properties of the distance function {#Properties_of_the_distance_function}

Source:[^o15][^o22]

- $$| \operatorname{grad} h (\vec x)|=1 \; ,$$
- $$h(\vec x+d\operatorname{grad} h (\vec x)) = h(\vec x)+d \; ,$$
- $$\operatorname{grad}h(\vec x+d\operatorname{grad}h (\vec x))= \operatorname{grad}h (\vec x) \; .$$

::: {#o23 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example:** The diagram shows parallel curves of the implicit curve with equation $\; f(x,y)=x^4+y^4-1=0\; .$ *Remark:* The curves $\; f(x,y)=x^4+y^4-1=d\;$ are not parallel curves, because $\; | \operatorname{grad} f (x,y)|=1 \;$ is not true in the area of interest.

## Further examples {#Further_examples}

*Image: Evolvente-kreis.svg.* Involutes of a circle

- The [involutes](wikipedia://en/840704) of a given curve are a set of parallel curves. For example: the involutes of a circle are parallel spirals (see diagram).

And:[^o26]

- A [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) has as (two-sided) offsets [rational curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_curve) of degree 6.
- A [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) or an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) has as (two-sided) offsets an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) of degree 8.
- A [Bézier curve](wikipedia://en/4664) of degree $n$ has as (two-sided) offsets [algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/253260) of degree $4n - 2$. In particular, a cubic Bézier curve has as (two-sided) offsets algebraic curves of degree 10.

## Parallel curve to a curve with a corner {#Parallel_curve_to_a_curve_with_a_corner}

*Image: Parallel curves to a curve with a discontinuous normal.png.* Parallel curves to a curve with a discontinuous normal around a corner

When determining the cutting path of part with a sharp corner for [machining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machining), one must define the parallel (offset) curve to a given curve that has a discontinuous normal at the corner. Even though the given curve is not smooth at the sharp corner, its parallel curve may be smooth with a continuous normal, or it may have [cusps](wikipedia://en/6851758) when the distance from the curve matches the radius of [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) at the sharp corner.

As described above, the parametric representation of a parallel curve, $\vec x_d(t)$, to a given curver, $\vec x(t)$, with distance $|d|$ is:

$\vec x_d(t) = \vec x(t) + d\vec n(t)$ with the unit normal $\vec n(t)$.

At a sharp corner ($t = t_c$), the normal to $\vec x(t_c)$ given by $\vec n(t_c)$ is discontinuous, meaning the [one-sided limit](wikipedia://en/379840) of the normal from the left $\vec n(t_c^-)$ is unequal to the limit from the right $\vec n(t_c^+)$. Mathematically,

$$\vec n(t_c^-) = \lim_{t \to t_c^-}\vec n(t) \ne \vec n(t_c^+) = \lim_{t \to t_c^+}\vec n(t).$$

*Image: Normal fan for defining parallel curves.png.* Normal fan for defining parallel curves around a sharp corner

However, we can define a **normal fan**[^o12] $\vec n_f(\alpha)$ that provides an [interpolant](wikipedia://en/14569) between $\vec n(t_c^-)$ and $\vec n(t_c^+)$, and use $\vec n_f(\alpha)$ in place of $\vec n(t_c)$ at the sharp corner:

$\vec n_f(\alpha) = \frac{(1 - \alpha)\vec n(t_c^-) + \alpha\vec n(t_c^+)}{\lVert (1 - \alpha)\vec n(t_c^-) + \alpha\vec n(t_c^+) \rVert},\quad$where $0 < \alpha < 1$.

The resulting definition of the parallel curve $\vec x_d(t)$ provides the desired behavior:

$$\vec x_d(t) = \begin{cases} \vec x(t) + d\vec n(t), & \text{if }t < t_c\text{ or }t > t_c \\ \vec x(t_c) + d\vec n_f(\alpha), & \text{if }t = t_c\text{ where }0 < \alpha < 1 \end{cases}$$

::: {#o29 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Algorithms"}

## Algorithms {#Algorithms}

In general, the parallel curve of a [Bézier curve](wikipedia://en/4664) is not another Bézier curve, a result proved by Tiller and Hanson in 1984.[^o30] Thus, in practice, approximation techniques are used. Any desired level of accuracy is possible by repeatedly subdividing the curve, though better techniques require fewer subdivisions to attain the same level of accuracy. A 1997 survey by Elber, Lee and Kim[^o31] is widely cited, though better techniques have been proposed more recently. A modern technique based on [curve fitting](wikipedia://en/555425), with references and comparisons to other algorithms, as well as open source JavaScript source code, was published in a blog post[^o32] in September 2022.

Another efficient algorithm for offsetting is the level approach described by [Kimmel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ron_Kimmel) and Bruckstein (1993).[^o33]

:::

## Parallel (offset) surfaces {#Parallel_(offset)_surfaces}

*Image: Offset surface.png.* Offset surface of a complex irregular shape

Offset surfaces are important in [numerically controlled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerically_controlled) [machining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machining), where they describe the shape of the cut made by a ball nose end mill of a three-axis mill.[^o11] If there is a regular parametric representation $\vec x(u,v) = (x(u,v),y(u,v),z(u,v))$ of the given surface available, the second definition of a parallel curve (see above) generalizes to the following parametric representation of the parallel surface with distance $|d|$:

$\vec x_d(u,v)=\vec x(u,v)+d\vec n(u,v)$ with the unit normal $\vec n_d(u,v) = {{{\partial \vec x \over \partial u} \times {\partial \vec x \over \partial v}} \over {|{{\partial \vec x \over \partial u} \times {\partial \vec x \over \partial v}}|}}$.

Distance parameter $d$ may be negative, too. In this case one gets a parallel surface on the opposite side of the surface (see similar diagram on the parallel curves of a circle). One easily checks: a parallel surface of a plane is a parallel plane in the common sense and the parallel surface of a sphere is a concentric sphere.

### Geometric properties {#Geometric_properties}

Source:[^o36]

- ${\partial \vec x_d \over \partial u} \parallel {\partial \vec x \over \partial u}, \quad {\partial \vec x_d \over \partial v} \parallel {\partial \vec x \over \partial v}, \quad$ that means: the tangent vectors for fixed parameters are parallel.
- $\vec n_d(u,v) = \pm\vec n(u,v), \quad$ that means: the normal vectors for fixed parameters match direction.
- $S_d = (1 + d S)^{-1} S, \quad$ where $S_d$ and $S$ are the [shape operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_operator) for $\vec x_d$ and $\vec x$, respectively.

The principal curvatures are the [eigenvalues](wikipedia://en/2161429) of the [shape operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_operator), the [principal curvature](wikipedia://en/2072472) directions are its [eigenvectors](wikipedia://en/2161429), the [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) is its [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468), and the [mean curvature](wikipedia://en/684210) is half its [trace](wikipedia://en/43270).

- $S_d^{-1} = S^{-1} + d I, \quad$ where $S_d^{-1}$ and $S^{-1}$ are the inverses of the [shape operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_operator) for $\vec x_d$ and $\vec x$, respectively.

The principal radii of curvature are the [eigenvalues](wikipedia://en/2161429) of the inverse of the [shape operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_operator), the principal curvature directions are its [eigenvectors](wikipedia://en/2161429), the reciprocal of the [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) is its [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468), and the mean radius of curvature is half its [trace](wikipedia://en/43270).

Note the similarity to the geometric properties of parallel curves.

::: {#o38 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The problem generalizes fairly obviously to higher dimensions e.g. to offset surfaces, and slightly less trivially to [pipe surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipe_surface).[^o39] Note that the terminology for the higher-dimensional versions varies even more widely than in the planar case, e.g. other authors speak of parallel fibers, ribbons, and tubes.[^o40] For curves embedded in 3D surfaces the offset may be taken along a [geodesic](wikipedia://en/91096).[^o41]

Another way to generalize it is (even in 2D) to consider a variable distance, e.g. parametrized by another curve.[^o36] One can for example stroke (envelope) with an ellipse instead of circle[^o36] as it is possible for example in [METAFONT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/METAFONT).[^o42]

*Image: Envelope of ellipses.png.* An envelope of ellipses forming two general offset curves above and below a given curve

More recently [Adobe Illustrator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe_Illustrator) has added somewhat similar facility in version [CS5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CS5), although the control points for the variable width are visually specified.[^o43] In contexts where it's important to distinguish between constant and variable distance offsetting the acronyms CDO and VDO are sometimes used.[^o9]

### General offset curves {#General_offset_curves}

Assume you have a regular parametric representation of a curve, $\vec x(t) = (x(t),y(t))$, and you have a second curve that can be parameterized by its unit normal, $\vec d(\vec n)$, where the normal of $\vec d(\vec n) = \vec n$ (this parameterization by normal exists for curves whose curvature is strictly positive or negative, and thus convex, smooth, and not straight). The parametric representation of the general offset curve of $\vec x(t)$ offset by $\vec d(\vec n)$ is:

$\vec x_d(t)=\vec x(t)+ \vec d(\vec n(t)), \quad$ where $\vec n(t)$ is the unit normal of $\vec x(t)$.

Note that the trival offset, $\vec d(\vec n) = d\vec n$, gives you ordinary parallel (aka, offset) curves.

#### Geometric properties {#Geometric_properties}

Source:[^o36]

- $\vec x'_d(t) \parallel \vec x'(t),\quad$ that means: the tangent vectors for a fixed parameter are parallel.
- As for [parallel lines](wikipedia://en/664497), a normal to a curve is also normal to its general offsets.
- $k_d(t)=\dfrac{k(t)}{1+\dfrac{k(t)}{k_n(t)}},\quad$ with $k_d(t)$ the [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) of the general offset curve, $k(t)$ the curvature of $\vec x(t)$, and $k_n(t)$ the curvature of $\vec d(\vec n(t))$ for parameter $t$.
- $R_d(t)=R(t) + R_n(t),\quad$ with $R_d(t)$ the [radius of curvature](wikipedia://en/60770#Curvature_of_plane_curves) of the general offset curve, $R(t)$ the radius of curvature of $\vec x(t)$, and $R_n(t)$ the radius of curvature of $\vec d(\vec n(t))$ for parameter $t$.
- When general offset curves are constructed they will have [cusps](wikipedia://en/6851758) when the [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) of the curve matches curvature of the offset. These are the points where the curve touches the [evolute](wikipedia://en/842387).

### General offset surfaces {#General_offset_surfaces}

General offset surfaces describe the shape of cuts made by a variety of cutting bits used by three-axis end mills in [numerically controlled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerically_controlled) [machining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machining).[^o12] Assume you have a regular parametric representation of a surface, $\vec x(u,v) = (x(u,v),y(u,v),z(u,v))$, and you have a second surface that can be parameterized by its unit normal, $\vec d(\vec n)$, where the normal of $\vec d(\vec n) = \vec n$ (this parameterization by normal exists for surfaces whose [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) is strictly positive, and thus convex, smooth, and not flat). The parametric representation of the general offset surface of $\vec x(t)$ offset by $\vec d(\vec n)$ is:

$\vec x_d(u,v)=\vec x(u,v)+ \vec d(\vec n(u,v)), \quad$ where $\vec n(u,v)$ is the unit normal of $\vec x(u,v)$.

Note that the trival offset, $\vec d(\vec n) = d\vec n$, gives you ordinary parallel (aka, offset) surfaces.

#### Geometric properties {#Geometric_properties}

Source:[^o36]

- As for [parallel lines](wikipedia://en/664497), the tangent plane of a surface is parallel to the tangent plane of its general offsets.
- As for [parallel lines](wikipedia://en/664497), a normal to a surface is also normal to its general offsets.
- $S_d = (1 + SS_n^{-1})^{-1} S, \quad$ where $S_d, S,$ and $S_n$ are the [shape operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_operator) for $\vec x_d, \vec x,$ and $\vec d(\vec n)$, respectively.

The principal curvatures are the [eigenvalues](wikipedia://en/2161429) of the [shape operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_operator), the principal curvature directions are its [eigenvectors](wikipedia://en/2161429), the [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) is its [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468), and the mean curvature is half its [trace](wikipedia://en/43270).

- $S_d^{-1} = S^{-1} + S_n^{-1}, \quad$ where $S_d^{-1}, S^{-1}$ and $S_n^{-1}$ are the inverses of the [shape operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_operator) for $\vec x_d, \vec x,$ and $\vec d(\vec n)$, respectively.

The principal radii of curvature are the [eigenvalues](wikipedia://en/2161429) of the inverse of the [shape operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_operator), the principal curvature directions are its [eigenvectors](wikipedia://en/2161429), the reciprocal of the [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) is its [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468), and the mean radius of curvature is half its [trace](wikipedia://en/43270).

Note the similarity to the geometric properties of general offset curves.

### Derivation of geometric properties for general offsets {#Derivation_of_geometric_properties_for_general_offsets}

The geometric properties listed above for general offset curves and surfaces can be derived for offsets of arbitrary dimension. Assume you have a regular parametric representation of an n-dimensional surface, $\vec x(\vec u)$, where the dimension of $\vec u$ is n-1. Also assume you have a second n-dimensional surface that can be parameterized by its unit normal, $\vec d(\vec n)$, where the normal of $\vec d(\vec n) = \vec n$ (this parameterization by normal exists for surfaces whose [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) is strictly positive, and thus convex, smooth, and not flat). The parametric representation of the general offset surface of $\vec x(\vec u)$ offset by $\vec d(\vec n)$ is:

$\vec x_d(\vec u) = \vec x(\vec u)+ \vec d(\vec n(\vec u)), \quad$ where $\vec n(\vec u)$ is the unit normal of $\vec x(\vec u)$. (The trival offset, $\vec d(\vec n) = d\vec n$, gives you ordinary parallel surfaces.)

First, notice that the normal of $\vec x(\vec u) =$ the normal of $\vec d(\vec n(\vec u)) = \vec n(\vec u),$ by definition. Now, we'll apply the differential w.r.t. $\vec u$ to $\vec x_d$, which gives us its tangent vectors spanning its tangent plane.

$$\partial\vec x_d(\vec u) = \partial\vec x(\vec u)+ \partial\vec d(\vec n(\vec u))$$

Notice, the tangent vectors for $\vec x_d$ are the sum of tangent vectors for $\vec x(\vec u)$ and its offset $\vec d(\vec n)$, which share the same unit normal. Thus, **the general offset surface shares the same tangent plane and normal with** $\vec x(\vec u)$ and $\vec d(\vec n(\vec u))$. That aligns with the nature of envelopes.

We now consider the [Weingarten equations](wikipedia://en/19149724) for the [shape operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_operator), which can be written as $\partial\vec n = -\partial\vec xS$. If $S$ is invertable, $\partial\vec x = -\partial\vec nS^{-1}$. Recall that the principal curvatures of a surface are the [eigenvalues](wikipedia://en/2161429) of the shape operator, the principal curvature directions are its [eigenvectors](wikipedia://en/2161429), the Gauss curvature is its [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468), and the mean curvature is half its [trace](wikipedia://en/43270). The inverse of the shape operator holds these same values for the radii of curvature.

Substituting into the equation for the differential of $\vec x_d$, we get:

$\partial\vec x_d = \partial\vec x - \partial\vec n S_n^{-1},\quad$ where $S_n$ is the shape operator for $\vec d(\vec n(\vec u))$.

Next, we use the [Weingarten equations](wikipedia://en/19149724) again to replace $\partial\vec n$:

$\partial\vec x_d = \partial\vec x + \partial\vec x S S_n^{-1},\quad$ where $S$ is the shape operator for $\vec x(\vec u)$.

Then, we solve for $\partial\vec x$ and multiple both sides by $-S$ to get back to the [Weingarten equations](wikipedia://en/19149724), this time for $\partial\vec x_d$:

$$\partial\vec x_d (I + S S_n^{-1})^{-1} = \partial\vec x,$$

$$-\partial\vec x_d (I + S S_n^{-1})^{-1}S = -\partial\vec xS = \partial\vec n.$$

Thus, $S_d = (I + S S_n^{-1})^{-1}S$, and inverting both sides gives us, $S_d^{-1} = S^{-1} + S_n^{-1}$.

:::
