# Jon Barwise

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Jon Barwise"}

**Kenneth Jon Barwise** (; June 29, 1942 – March 5, 2000)[^o2] was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902), [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher), and [logician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logician) who proposed some fundamental revisions to the way that [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) is understood and used.

:::

## Education and career {#Education_and_career}

He was born in [Independence, Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence,_Missouri), to Kenneth T. and Evelyn Barwise.

A student of [Solomon Feferman](wikipedia://en/355401) at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University), Barwise began his research working in [infinitary logic](wikipedia://en/630478). After positions at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University) and the [University of Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin–Madison), during which time his interests turned to [natural language](wikipedia://en/21173), he returned to Stanford in 1983 to direct the [Center for the Study of Language and Information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_the_Study_of_Language_and_Information) (CSLI). He began teaching at [Indiana University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_University_Bloomington) in 1990. He was elected a Fellow of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences) in 1999.[^o4]

In his final year, Barwise was invited to give the 2000 [Gödel Lecture](wikipedia://en/69216431); he died prior to the lecture.[^o5]

## Philosophical and logical work {#Philosophical_and_logical_work}

Barwise contended that, by being explicit about the context in which a [proposition](wikipedia://en/81094) is made, the *[situation](wikipedia://en/13279065)*, many problems in the application of logic can be eliminated. He sought "to understand meaning and inference within a general theory of information, one that takes us outside the realm of sentences and relations between sentences of any language, natural or formal."[^o7] In particular, he claimed that such an approach resolved the [liar paradox](wikipedia://en/17967). He made use of [Peter Aczel](wikipedia://en/24089125)'s [non-well-founded set theory](wikipedia://en/1091767) in understanding "vicious circles" of reasoning.[^o8]

Barwise, along with his former colleague at Stanford [John Etchemendy](wikipedia://en/5503538), was the author of the popular logic textbook *[Language, Proof and Logic](wikipedia://en/31629330)*. The text is notable for including computer-aided homework problems, some of which provide visual representations of logical problems. During his time at Stanford, he was also the first director of the [Symbolic Systems Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_Systems_Program), an interdepartmental degree program focusing on the relationships between cognition, language, logic, and computation. [The K. Jon Barwise Award for Distinguished Contributions to the Symbolic Systems Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_Systems_Program#Barwise_Award_for_Distinguished_Contributions_to_Symbolic_Systems) has been given periodically since 2001.[^o9] The [Barwise Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barwise_Prize) was established in 2002 by the [American Philosophical Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Association), awarded for contributions to [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy) and [computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing).[^o10]
