# Aztec diamond

*Image: Diamant azteque.svg.* An Aztec diamond of order 4

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Aztec diamond"}

In [combinatorial](wikipedia://en/5170) mathematics, an **Aztec diamond** of order *n* consists of all squares of a [square lattice](wikipedia://en/2874853) whose centers (*x*,*y*) satisfy |*x*| + |*y*| ≤ *n*. Here *n* is a fixed integer, and the square lattice consists of unit squares with the origin as a vertex of 4 of them, so that both *x* and *y* are [half-integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-integers).[^o2]

:::

*Image: Diamant azteque plein.svg.* One of 1024 possible domino tilings of an order 4 Aztec diamond

*Image: Aztec diamond, n=50.jpg.* A domino tiling of an order-50 Aztec diamond, chosen uniformly at random. The four corners of the diamond outside of the roughly circular area are "frozen".

The **Aztec diamond theorem** states that the number of [domino tilings](wikipedia://en/10565476) of the Aztec diamond of order *n* is 2<sup>*n*(*n*+1)/2</sup>.[^o3] The **Arctic Circle theorem** says that a random tiling of a large Aztec diamond tends to be frozen outside a certain circle.[^o4]

It is common to color the tiles in the following fashion. First consider a checkerboard coloring of the diamond. Each tile will cover exactly one black square. Vertical tiles where the top square covers a black square, is colored in one color, and the other vertical tiles in a second. Similarly for horizontal tiles.

[Knuth](wikipedia://en/8095) has also defined Aztec diamonds of order *n* + 1/2.[^o5] They are identical with the [polyominoes](wikipedia://en/72634) associated with the [centered square numbers](wikipedia://en/723297).

## Non-intersecting paths {#Non-intersecting_paths}

Something that is very useful for counting tilings is looking at the [non-intersecting paths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindström-Gessel-Viennot_lemma) through its corresponding [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986). We define the graph's vertices to lie on the left and right edges of the squares, centered vertically (so $y=k+\frac{1}{2}$ for an integer *k*). The graph's directed edges are defined by the 3 vectors (1,1), (1,0) and (1,−1): For each vertex, if addition of a vector leads to another vertex and the connecting [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) lies within the Aztec diamond, there is a corresponding directed edge. Define the sources to be the vertices of negative *y* coordinate on the left edges of the Aztec diamond, and the sinks to be the vertices of negative *y* coordinate on the right edges of the Aztec diamond. Then a tiling defines a [tuple](wikipedia://en/132729) of non-intersection paths by starting at each source and repeatedly applying the following rules:

- choose (1,1) at the bottom left vertex of a vertical tile,
- choose twice (1,0) at the left vertex of a horizontal tile,
- choose (1,−1) at a top left vertex of a vertical tile.

These movements are similar to [Schröder paths](wikipedia://en/15062158). For example, consider an Aztec diamond of order 2, and after drawing its [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) we can label its sources $a_a,a_2$ and its sinks $b_1,b_2$. On its directed graph, we can count the directed paths from $a_i$ to $b_j$ for each pair $(i,j) \in \{1,2\} \times \{1,2\}$. Call $C_{i,j}$ the result of each counting. This gives us a matrix,

$$P_2 = \begin{bmatrix} C_{1,1} & C_{1,2}\\ C_{2,1} & C_{2,2}\\ \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \\ \end{bmatrix}.$$

Then by the [Lindström-Gessel-Viennot lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindström-Gessel-Viennot_lemma)[^o10], the number of non-intersecting paths for order 2 is

det$(P_2) = 12 - 4 = 8 = 2^{2(2+1)/2},$

the same as the number of domino tilings. More generally, det$(P_n) =$ number of non-intersecting paths from sources to sinks.

It has been shown by Eu and Fu that [Schröder paths](wikipedia://en/15062158) and the tilings of the Aztec diamond are in [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942).[^o9] Hence, finding the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the path matrix $P_n$ will give us the number of tilings for the Aztec diamond of order *n*.

Another way to determine the number of tilings of an Aztec diamond is using [Hankel matrices](wikipedia://en/377537) of large and small [Schröder numbers](wikipedia://en/15062158),[^o9] using the method from [Lindstrom-Gessel-Viennot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindstrom-Gessel-Viennot_lemma) again.[^o10] Finding the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of these [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) gives us the number of [non-intersecting paths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindström-Gessel-Viennot_lemma) of small and large [Schröder numbers](wikipedia://en/15062158), which is in [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) with the tilings. The small [Schröder numbers](wikipedia://en/15062158) are $\{1,1,3,11,45,\cdots\} = \{y_0, y_1,y_2,y_3,y_4,\cdots\}$ and the large [Schröder numbers](wikipedia://en/15062158) are $\{1,2,6,22,90,\cdots\}=\{x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4,\cdots\}$, and in general our two [Hankel matrices](wikipedia://en/377537) will be

$H_n = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & x_2 & \cdots & x_n\\ x_2 & x_3 & \cdots & x_{n+1}\\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots\\ x_n & x_{n+1} & \cdots & x_{2n-1}\\ \end{bmatrix}$ and $G_n = \begin{bmatrix} y_1 & y_2 & \cdots & y_n\\ y_2 & y_3 & \cdots & y_{n+1}\\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots\\ y_n & y_{n+1} & \cdots & y_{2n-1}\\ \end{bmatrix}$

where det$(H_n) = 2^{n(n+1)/2}$ and det$(G_n) = 2^{n(n-1)/2}$ where $n \geq 1$ (It also true that det$(H_n^0) = 2^{n(n-1)/2}$ where this is the [Hankel matrix](wikipedia://en/377537) like $H_n$, but started with $x_0$ instead of $x_1$ for the first entry of the matrix in the top left corner).

## Generating valid tilings {#Generating_valid_tilings}

Finding valid tilings of the Aztec diamond involves the solution of the underlying [set-covering](wikipedia://en/870399) problem. Let $D=\{d_1,d_2,\dots,d_n\}$ be the set of 2×1 dominoes where each domino in D may be placed within the diamond (without crossing its boundaries) when no other dominoes are present. Let $S = \{s_1,s_2,\dots,s_m\}$ be the set of 1×1 squares lying within the diamond that must be covered. Two dominoes within D can be found to cover any boundary square within S, and four dominoes within D can be found to cover any non-boundary square within S.

Define $c(s_i)\subset D$ to be the set of dominoes that cover square $s_i$, and let $x_i$ be an indicator variable such that $x_i =1$ if the $i^{th}$ domino is used in the tiling, and 0 otherwise. With these definitions, the task of tiling the Aztec diamond may be reduced to a constraint satisfaction problem formulated as a binary integer program:

$$\min \sum_{1\leq i\leq m} 0\cdot x_i$$

Subject to: $\sum_{i\in c(s_i)} x_i = 1,$ for $1\leq i \leq m$, and $x_i \in \{0,1\}$.

The $i^{th}$ constraint guarantee that square $s_i$ will be covered by a single tile, and the collection of $m$ constraints ensures that each square will be covered (no holes in the covering). This formulation can be solved with standard [integer programming](wikipedia://en/411215) packages. Additional constraints can be constructed to force placement of particular dominoes, ensure a minimum number of horizontal or vertically-oriented dominoes are used, or generate distinct tilings.

An alternative approach is to apply [Knuth's Algorithm X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knuth's_Algorithm_X) to enumerate valid tilings for the problem.
