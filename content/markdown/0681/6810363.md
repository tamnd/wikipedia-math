# Classical Wiener space

*Image: Norbert wiener.jpg.* Norbert Wiener

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Classical Wiener space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **classical Wiener space** is the collection of all [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122#Continuous_functions_between_metric_spaces) on a given [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) (usually a [subinterval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subinterval) of the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line)), taking values in a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) (usually *n*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697)). Classical Wiener space is useful in the study of [stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_processes) whose sample paths are continuous functions. It is named after the [American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Norbert Wiener](wikipedia://en/63185).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Consider $E\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) $(M,d)$. The **classical Wiener space** $C(E,M)$ is the space of all continuous functions $f:E\to M.$ That is, for every fixed $t\in E,$

$d(f(s), f(t)) \to 0$ as $| s - t | \to 0.$

In almost all applications, one takes $E=[0,T]$ or $E=\mathbb{R}_+=[0, +\infty)$ and $M=\mathbb{R}^n$ for some $n\in\mathbb{N}.$ For brevity, write $C$ for $C([0,T]);$ this is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370). Write $C_0$ for the [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) consisting only of those [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) that take the value zero at the infimum of the set $E.$ Many authors refer to $C_0$ as "classical Wiener space".

## Properties of classical Wiener space {#Properties_of_classical_Wiener_space}

### Uniform topology {#Uniform_topology}

The vector space $C$ can be equipped with the [uniform norm](wikipedia://en/349755)

$$\| f \| := \sup_{t \in [0,\,T]} |f(t)|$$

turning it into a [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538) (in fact a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) since $[0,T]$ is compact). This [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) induces a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) on $C$ in the usual way: $d (f, g) := \| f-g \|$. The [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) generated by the [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) in this metric is the topology of [uniform convergence](wikipedia://en/50652) on $[0,T],$ or the [uniform topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_topology_%28disambiguation%29).

Thinking of the domain $[0,T]$ as "time" and the range $\mathbb{R}^n$ as "space", an intuitive view of the uniform topology is that two functions are "close" if we can "wiggle space slightly" and get the graph of $f$ to lie on top of the graph of $g$, while leaving time fixed. Contrast this with the [Skorokhod topology](wikipedia://en/30862839#Skorokhod_space), which allows us to "wiggle" both space and time.

If one looks at the more general domain $\mathbb{R}_{+}$ with

$$\| f \| := \sup_{t \geq 0} |f(t)|,$$

then the Wiener space is no longer a Banach space, however it can be made into one if the Wiener space is defined under the additional constraint

$$\lim\limits_{s\to\infty}s^{-1}|f(s)|=0.$$

### Separability and completeness {#Separability_and_completeness}

With respect to the uniform metric, $C$ is both a [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) and a [complete space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space):

- Separability is a consequence of the [Stone–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/28858);
- Completeness is a consequence of the fact that the uniform limit of a sequence of continuous functions is itself continuous.

Since it is both separable and complete, $C$ is a [Polish space](wikipedia://en/396622).

### Tightness in classical Wiener space {#Tightness_in_classical_Wiener_space}

Recall that the [modulus of continuity](wikipedia://en/964161) for a function $f:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^n$ is defined by

$$\omega_{f} (\delta) := \sup \left\{ |f(s) - f(t)| : s, t \in [0, T],\, |s - t| \leq \delta \right\}.$$

This definition makes sense even if $f$ is not continuous, and it can be shown that $f$ is continuous [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its modulus of continuity tends to zero as $\delta\to 0:$

$$f \in C \iff \omega_{f} (\delta) \to 0 \text{ as } \delta \to 0.$$

By an application of the [Arzelà-Ascoli theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arzelà-Ascoli_theorem), one can show that a sequence $(\mu_{n})_{n = 1}^{\infty}$ of [probability measures](wikipedia://en/45437) on classical Wiener space $C$ is [tight](wikipedia://en/6553354) if and only if both the following conditions are met:

$\lim_{a \to \infty} \limsup_{n \to \infty} \mu_{n} \{ f \in C : | f(0) | \geq a \} = 0,$ and

$\lim_{\delta \to 0} \limsup_{n \to \infty} \mu_{n} \{ f \in C : \omega_{f} (\delta) \geq \varepsilon \} = 0$ for all $\varepsilon >0.$

### Classical Wiener measure {#Classical_Wiener_measure}

There is a "standard" measure on $C_0,$ known as **classical Wiener measure** (or simply **Wiener measure**). Wiener measure has (at least) two equivalent characterizations:

If one defines [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436) to be a [Markov](wikipedia://en/306422) [stochastic process](wikipedia://en/47895) $B:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^n,$ starting at the origin, with [almost surely](wikipedia://en/351908) continuous paths and [independent increments](wikipedia://en/877315)

$$B_{t} - B_{s} \sim\, \mathrm{Normal} \left( 0, |t - s| \right),$$

then classical Wiener measure $\gamma$ is the [law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_%28stochastic_processes%29) of the process $B.$

Alternatively, one may use the [abstract Wiener space](wikipedia://en/6592746) construction, in which classical Wiener measure $\gamma$ is the [radonification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radonifying_function) of the [canonical Gaussian cylinder set measure](wikipedia://en/6571387#Cylinder_set_measures_on_Hilbert_spaces) on the [Cameron-Martin](wikipedia://en/6573013) [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) corresponding to $C_0.$

Classical Wiener measure is a [Gaussian measure](wikipedia://en/6572909): in particular, it is a [strictly positive](wikipedia://en/6607051) probability measure.

Given classical Wiener measure $\gamma$ on $C_0,$ the [product measure](wikipedia://en/2986564) $\gamma^n\times\gamma$ is a probability measure on $C$, where $\gamma^n$ denotes the standard [Gaussian measure](wikipedia://en/6572909) on $\mathbb{R}^n.$

:::

### Coordinate maps for the Wiener measure {#Coordinate_maps_for_the_Wiener_measure}

For a stochastic process $\{X_t,t\in [0,T]\}:(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\to (M,\mathcal{B})$ and the [function space](wikipedia://en/340630) $M^E\equiv\{E\to M\}$ of all functions from $E$ to $M$, one looks at the map $\varphi:\Omega\to M^E$. One can then define the *coordinate maps* or *canonical versions* $Y_t:M^E\to M$ defined by $Y_t(\omega)=\omega(t)$. The $\{Y_t,t\in E\}$ form another process. For $M=\mathbb{R}$ and $E=\mathbb{R}_{+}$, the *Wiener measure* is then the unique measure on $C_0(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R})$ such that the coordinate process is a Brownian motion.[^o12]

### Subspaces of the Wiener space {#Subspaces_of_the_Wiener_space}

Let $H\subset C_0([0,R])$ be a Hilbert space that is [continuously embedded](wikipedia://en/7277255) and let $\gamma$ be the Wiener measure then $\gamma(H)=0$. This was proven in 1973 by Smolyanov and Uglanov and in the same year independently by Guerquin.[^o14][^o15] However, there exists a Hilbert space $H\subset C_0([0,R])$ with weaker topology such that $\gamma(H)=1$ which was proven in 1993 by Uglanov.[^o16]
