# Force of mortality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Force of mortality"}

In [actuarial science](wikipedia://en/189373) and [demography](wikipedia://en/45642), **force of mortality**, also known as **death intensity**,[^o2][^o3] is a function, usually written $\mu(x)$, that gives the instantaneous rate at which deaths occur at age *x*, conditional on survival to age *x*.[^o11] In [survival analysis](wikipedia://en/419259) it corresponds to the [hazard function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazard_function), and in [reliability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reliability_theory) it corresponds to the [failure rate](wikipedia://en/1336960).[^o12][^o13] It has units of inverse time, and integrating it over an interval gives the survival probability over that interval.[^o12]

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $X$ be a non-negative [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) representing an individual's age at death (or lifetime). Write $F(x)=\Pr(X\le x)$ for its [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) and $S(x)=\Pr(X>x)$ for its [survival function](wikipedia://en/30876757).[^o12]

The force of mortality at age $x$, written $\mu(x)$, is defined as the instantaneous conditional rate of death at age $x$. Formally, it is the limit of the [conditional probability](wikipedia://en/24104134) of dying in a short interval after $x$, divided by the interval length[^o11]:

$$\mu(x)=\lim_{\Delta x\to 0^+}\frac{\Pr(x<X\le x+\Delta x\mid X>x)}{\Delta x}.$$

When $X$ is continuous with [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) $f(x)$, the force of mortality can be written in terms of $f$ and $S$ as[^o12]

$$\mu(x)=\frac{f(x)}{S(x)}=\frac{f(x)}{1-F(x)}.$$

Equivalently, where $S$ is differentiable, it is the negative derivative of the log-survival function[^o12]:

$$\mu(x)=-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\ln S(x).$$

:::

## Interpretation and related quantities {#Interpretation_and_related_quantities}

The force of mortality $\mu(x)$ is an instantaneous rate rather than a probability. For a short interval $\Delta x$, the conditional probability of dying shortly after age $x$ is approximately $\mu(x)\,\Delta x$, provided $\Delta x$ is small enough that the rate does not change much over the interval.[^o11]

In survival analysis, $\mu(x)$ is the [hazard function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazard_function).[^o12] In reliability theory, the same [mathematical object](wikipedia://en/19453961) is commonly called the [failure rate](wikipedia://en/1336960).[^o13]

The cumulative force of mortality (also called the cumulative hazard) is the integral of the force over age. Writing

$$H(x)=\int_0^x \mu(u)\,\mathrm{d}u$$

then the survival function can be expressed as[^o12]

$$S(x)=\exp\!\bigl(-H(x)\bigr).$$

These identities imply the differential relationship

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}S(x)=-\mu(x)\,S(x)$$

and, for a continuous lifetime distribution, the density can be written as[^o12]

$$f(x)=\mu(x)\,S(x).$$

## Survival probabilities and life tables {#Survival_probabilities_and_life_tables}

In [actuarial notation](wikipedia://en/852721), the probability that a life aged $x$ survives for a further $t$ years is written ${}_tp_x$. In terms of the lifetime random variable $X$, it is[^o11]

$${}_tp_x=\Pr(X>x+t\mid X>x)=\frac{S(x+t)}{S(x)}.$$

Using the force of mortality, this conditional survival probability can be expressed as an exponential of the integrated force[^o11][^o12]:

$${}_tp_x=\exp\!\left(-\int_x^{x+t}\mu(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\right).$$

Life tables often tabulate survival and death probabilities at integer ages. In that setting, the one-year survival probability is $p_x={}_1p_x$ and the one-year death probability is $q_x=1-p_x$.[^o11] The force of mortality provides a continuous-age description that can be used to relate probabilities over different intervals through the integral relationship above.[^o11]

## Examples of mortality models {#Examples_of_mortality_models}

Several parametric models are used to describe how the force of mortality varies with age. A constant force of mortality, $\mu(x)=\lambda$ for $\lambda>0$, corresponds to an [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) for $X$ and gives a memoryless survival pattern.[^o12]

In actuarial work, the [Gompertz–Makeham law of mortality](wikipedia://en/4185921) is often written as the sum of an age-independent component and an exponentially increasing component, for example

$$\mu(x)=A+B\,c^x$$

with $A\ge 0$, $B>0$, and $c>1$.[^o11] The Gompertz model is the special case $A=0$, giving:[^o11]

$$\mu(x)=B\,c^x$$

A common model in survival analysis and reliability uses a [Weibull](wikipedia://en/206948) hazard, which has the form

$$\mu(x)=\frac{k}{\lambda}\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)^{k-1}$$

for shape $k>0$ and scale $\lambda>0$. This family includes decreasing, constant, and increasing forces of mortality depending on the value of $k$.[^o12][^o13]
