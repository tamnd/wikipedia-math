# F-divergence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="F-divergence"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), an $f$**\-divergence** is a certain type of function $D_f(P\| Q)$ that measures the difference between two [probability distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distributions) $P$ and $Q$. Many common divergences, such as [KL-divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KL-divergence), [Hellinger distance](wikipedia://en/13035709), and [total variation distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_variation_distance), are special cases of $f$-divergence.

:::

## History {#History}

These divergences were introduced by [Alfréd Rényi](wikipedia://en/614988)[^o3] in the same paper where he introduced the well-known [Rényi entropy](wikipedia://en/1731689). He proved that these divergences decrease in [Markov processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_Process). *f*-divergences were studied further independently by (Csiszár 1963), (Morimoto 1963) and (Ali Silvey 1966) and are sometimes known as Csiszár $f$-divergences, Csiszár–Morimoto divergences, or Ali–Silvey distances.

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### Non-singular case {#Non-singular_case}

Let $P$ and $Q$ be two probability distributions over a space $\Omega$, such that $P\ll Q$, that is, $P$ is [absolutely continuous](wikipedia://en/252670#Absolute_continuity_of_measures) with respect to $Q$ (meaning $Q>0$ wherever $P>0$). Then, for a [convex function](wikipedia://en/245568) $f: [0, +\infty)\to(-\infty, +\infty]$ such that $f(x)$ is finite for all $x > 0$, $f(1)=0$, and $f(0)=\lim_{t\to 0^+} f(t)$ (which could be infinite), the $f$-divergence of $P$ from $Q$ is defined as

$$D_f(P\parallel Q) \equiv \int_{\Omega} f\left(\frac{dP}{dQ}\right)\,dQ.$$

We call $f$ the generator of $D_f$.

In concrete applications, there is usually a reference distribution $\mu$ on $\Omega$ (for example, when $\Omega = \mathbb{R}^n$, the reference distribution is the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198)), such that $P, Q \ll \mu$, then we can use [Radon–Nikodym theorem](wikipedia://en/338746) to take their [probability densities](wikipedia://en/43487) $p$ and $q$, giving

$$D_f(P\parallel Q) = \int_{\Omega} f\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right)q(x)\,d\mu(x).$$

When there is no such reference distribution ready at hand, we can simply define $\mu = P+Q$, and proceed as above. This is a useful technique in more abstract proofs.

### Extension to [singular measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_measures) {#Extension_to_singular_measures}

The above definition can be extended to cases where $P\ll Q$ is no longer satisfied (Definition 7.1 of [^o11]).

Since $f$ is convex, and $f(1) = 0$ , the function $\frac{f(x)}{x-1}$ must be nondecreasing, so there exists $f'(\infty) := \lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)/x$, taking value in $(-\infty, +\infty]$.

Since for any $p(x)>0$, we have $\lim_{q(x)\to 0} q(x)f \left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right) = p(x)f'(\infty)$ , we can extend f-divergence to the $P\not\ll Q$.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

### Basic relations between f-divergences {#Basic_relations_between_f-divergences}

- Linearity: $D_{\sum_i a_i f_i} = \sum_i a_i D_{f_i}$ given a finite sequence of nonnegative real numbers $a_i$ and generators $f_i$.
- $D_f = D_g$ iff $f(x) = g(x) + c(x-1)$ for some $c\in \mathbb{R}$.

::: {#o15 .proof cue=math-proof}

If $f(x) = g(x) + c(x-1)$, then $D_f = D_g$ by definition.

Conversely, if $D_f - D_g = 0$, then let $h = f-g$. For any two probability measures $P, Q$ on the set $\{0, 1\}$, since $D_f(P\| Q) - D_g(P\|Q) = 0$, we get $h(P_1/Q_1) = -\frac{Q_0}{Q_1}h(P_0/Q_0)$

Since each probability measure $P, Q$ has one degree of freedom, we can solve $\frac{P_0}{Q_0} = a, \frac{P_1}{Q_1} = x$ for every choice of $0 < a < 1 < x$.

Linear algebra yields $Q_0 = \frac{x-1}{x-a}, Q_1 = \frac{1-a}{x-a}$, which is a valid probability measure. Then we obtain $h(x) = \frac{h(a)}{a-1}(x-1), h(a) = \frac{h(x)}{x-1}(a-1)$.

Thus $h(x)=\begin{cases} c_1(x-1)\quad\text{if } x>1,\\ c_0(x-1)\quad\text{if } 0<x<1,\\ \end{cases}$ for some constants $c_0, c_1$. Plugging the formula into $h(x) = \frac{h(a)}{a-1}(x-1)$ yields $c_0 = c_1$.

:::

### Basic properties of f-divergences {#Basic_properties_of_f-divergences}

In particular, the monotonicity implies that if a [Markov process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_process) has a positive equilibrium probability distribution $P^*$ then $D_f(P(t)\parallel P^*)$ is a monotonic (non-increasing) function of time, where the probability distribution $P(t)$ is a solution of the [Kolmogorov forward equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov_equations_%28Markov_jump_process%29) (or [Master equation](wikipedia://en/840106)), used to describe the [time evolution](wikipedia://en/937739) of the probability distribution in the Markov process. This means that all *f*-divergences $D_f(P(t)\parallel P^*)$ are the [Lyapunov functions](wikipedia://en/352267) of the Kolmogorov forward equations. The converse statement is also true: If $H(P)$ is a Lyapunov function for all Markov chains with positive equilibrium $P^*$ and is of the trace-form ($H(P)=\sum_{i}f(P_{i},P_{i}^{*})$) then $H(P)= D_f(P(t)\parallel P^*)$, for some convex function *f*.[^o17][^o18] For example, [Bregman divergences](wikipedia://en/4491248) in general do not have such property and can increase in Markov processes.[^o19]

### Analytic properties {#Analytic_properties}

The f-divergences can be expressed using [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) and rewritten using a weighted sum of chi-type distances ((Nielsen Nock 2013)).

### Basic variational representation {#Basic_variational_representation}

Let $f^*$ be the [convex conjugate](wikipedia://en/1001490) of $f$. Let $\mathrm{effdom}(f^*)$ be the [effective domain](wikipedia://en/33466771) of $f^*$, that is, $\mathrm{effdom}(f^*) = \{y : f^*(y) < \infty\}$. Then we have two variational representations of $D_f$, which we describe below.

Under the above setup,

::: {#o23 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

$$D_f(P; Q) = \sup_{g: \Omega\to \mathrm{effdom}(f^*)} E_P[g] - E_Q[f^* \circ g].$$

:::

This is Theorem 7.24 in.[^o11]

#### Example applications {#Example_applications}

Using this theorem on total variation distance, with generator $f(x)= \frac 1 2 |x-1|,$ its convex conjugate is $f^*(x^*) = \begin{cases} x^* \text{ on } [-1/2, 1/2],\\ +\infty \text{ else.} \end{cases}$, and we obtain

$$TV(P\| Q) = \sup_{|g|\leq 1/2} E_P[g(X)] - E_Q[g(X)].$$

For chi-squared divergence, defined by $f(x) = (x-1)^2, f^*(y) = y^2/4 + y$, we obtain

$$\chi^2(P; Q) = \sup_g E_P[g(X)] - E_Q[g(X)^2/4 + g(X)].$$

Since the variation term is not affine-invariant in $g$, even though the domain over which $g$ varies *is* affine-invariant, we can use up the affine-invariance to obtain a leaner expression.

Replacing $g$ by $a g + b$ and taking the maximum over $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, we obtain

$$\chi^2(P; Q) = \sup_g \frac{(E_P[g(X)]-E_Q[g(X)])^2}{Var_Q[g(X)]},$$

which is just a few steps away from the [Hammersley–Chapman–Robbins bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammersley–Chapman–Robbins_bound) and the [Cramér–Rao bound](wikipedia://en/581124) (Theorem 29.1 and its corollary in [^o11]).

For $\alpha$-divergence with $\alpha \in (-\infty, 0)\cup(0, 1)$, we have $f_\alpha(x) = \frac{x^\alpha - \alpha x - (1-\alpha)}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}$, with range $x\in [0, \infty)$. Its convex conjugate is $f_\alpha^*(y)=\frac{1}{\alpha}(x(y)^\alpha - 1)$ with range $y\in(-\infty, (1-\alpha)^{-1})$, where $x(y) = ((\alpha-1)y + 1)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}$.

Applying this theorem yields, after substitution with $h = ((\alpha-1)g+1)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}$,

$$D_\alpha(P\| Q) = \frac{1}{\alpha(1-\alpha)} - \inf_{h: \Omega\to (0,\infty)}\left( E_Q\left[\frac{h^\alpha}{\alpha}\right] + E_P\left[\frac{h^{\alpha-1}}{1-\alpha}\right] \right),$$

or, releasing the constraint on $h$,

$$D_\alpha(P\| Q) = \frac{1}{\alpha(1-\alpha)} - \inf_{h: \Omega\to \mathbb{R}}\left( E_Q\left[\frac{|h|^\alpha}{\alpha}\right] + E_P\left[\frac{|h|^{\alpha-1}}{1-\alpha}\right] \right).$$

Setting $\alpha=-1$ yields the variational representation of $\chi^2$-divergence obtained above.

The domain over which $h$ varies is not affine-invariant in general, unlike the $\chi^2$-divergence case. The $\chi^2$-divergence is special, since in that case, we can remove the $|\cdot |$ from $|h|$.

For general $\alpha \in (-\infty, 0)\cup(0, 1)$, the domain over which $h$ varies is merely [scale invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance). Similar to above, we can replace $h$ by $a h$, and take minimum over $a>0$ to obtain

$$D_\alpha(P\| Q) = \sup_{h >0} \left[\frac{1}{\alpha(1-\alpha)} \left( 1-\frac{E_P[h^{\alpha-1}]^\alpha}{E_Q[h^\alpha]^{\alpha-1}} \right) \right].$$

Setting $\alpha=\frac 1 2$, and performing another substitution by $g=\sqrt h$, yields two variational representations of the squared Hellinger distance:

$$H^2(P\|Q) = \frac 1 2 D_{1/2}(P\| Q) = 2 - \inf_{h>0}\left( E_Q\left[h(X)\right] + E_P\left[h(X)^{-1}\right] \right),$$

$$H^2(P\|Q) = 2 \sup_{h > 0} \left(1-\sqrt{E_P[h^{-1}]E_Q[h]}\right).$$

Applying this theorem to the KL-divergence, defined by $f(x) = x\ln x, f^*(y) = e^{y-1}$, yields

$$D_{KL}(P; Q) =\sup_g E_P[g(X)] - e^{-1}E_Q[e^{g(X)}].$$

This is strictly less efficient than the [Donsker–Varadhan representation](wikipedia://en/1115052#Duality_formula_for_variational_inference)

$$D_{KL}(P; Q) = \sup_g E_P[g(X)]- \ln E_Q[e^{g(X)}].$$

This defect is fixed by the next theorem.

### Improved variational representation {#Improved_variational_representation}

Assume the setup in the beginning of this section ("Variational representations").

::: {#o26 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

If $f(x) = +\infty$ on $x<0$ (redefine $f$ if necessary), then

$$D_{f}(P \| Q)=f^{\prime}(\infty) P\left[S^{c}\right]+\sup _{g} \mathbb{E}_{P}\left[g 1_{S}\right]-\Psi_{Q, P}^{*}(g),$$

where $\Psi_{Q, P}^{*}(g) := \inf _{a \in \mathbb{R}} \mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[f^{*}(g(X)-a)\right]+a P[S]$ and $S:=\{q > 0\}$, where $q$ is the probability density function of $Q$ with respect to some underlying measure.

In the special case of $f^{\prime}(\infty)=+\infty$, we have

$$D_{f}(P \| Q)=\sup _{g} \mathbb{E}_{P}[g]-\Psi_{Q}^{*}(g), \quad \Psi_{Q}^{*}(g) := \inf _{a \in \mathbb{R}} \mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[f^{*}(g(X)-a)\right]+a.$$

:::

This is Theorem 7.25 in.[^o11]

#### Example applications {#Example_applications}

Applying this theorem to KL-divergence yields the Donsker–Varadhan representation.

Attempting to apply this theorem to the general $\alpha$-divergence with $\alpha \in (-\infty, 0)\cup(0, 1)$ does not yield a closed-form solution.

:::

## Common examples of *f*-divergences {#Common_examples_of_f-divergences}

The following table lists many of the common divergences between probability distributions and the possible generating functions to which they correspond. Notably, except for total variation distance, all others are special cases of $\alpha$-divergence, or linear sums of $\alpha$-divergences.

For each f-divergence $D_f$, its [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) is not uniquely defined, but only up to $c\cdot(t-1)$, where $c$ is any real constant. That is, for any $f$ that generates an f-divergence, we have $D_{f(t)} = D_{f(t) + c\cdot(t-1)}$. This freedom is not only convenient, but actually necessary.

::: {#o29 .table cue=table}

| Divergence | Corresponding *f(t)* | Discrete Form |
|---|---|---|
| $\chi^{\alpha}$-divergence, $\alpha \ge 1 \,$ | $\frac12 \|t - 1\|^{\alpha} \,$ | $\frac12 \sum_i \left\|\frac{p_i - q_i}{q_i}\right\|^\alpha q_i \,$ |
| [Total variation distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_variation_distance) ($\alpha = 1 \,$) | $\frac12\|t - 1\| \,$ | $\frac12 \sum_i \|p_i - q_i\| \,$ |
| α-divergence | $\begin{cases} \frac{t^{\alpha} - \alpha t - \left( 1 - \alpha \right)}{\alpha \left(\alpha - 1 \right)} & \text{if}\ \alpha\neq 0,\, \alpha\neq 1, \\ t\ln t-t+1, & \text{if}\ \alpha=1, \\ - \ln t +t-1, & \text{if}\ \alpha=0 \end{cases}$ |  |
| [KL-divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KL-divergence) ($\alpha=1$) | $t \ln t$ | $\sum_i p_i \ln \frac{p_i}{q_i}$ |
| reverse KL-divergence ($\alpha=0$) | $- \ln t$ | $\sum_i q_i \ln \frac{q_i}{p_i}$ |
| [Jensen–Shannon divergence](wikipedia://en/4019573) | $\frac{1}{2} \left(t \ln t -(t + 1)\ln \left(\frac{t + 1}{2}\right)\right)$ | $\frac{1}{2} \sum_i \left( p_i \ln \frac{p_i}{(p_i + q_i)/2} + q_i \ln \frac{q_i}{(p_i + q_i)/2} \right)$ |
| Jeffreys divergence (KL + reverse KL) | $(t - 1)\ln(t)$ | $\sum_i (p_i - q_i) \ln \frac{p_i}{q_i}$ |
| squared [Hellinger distance](wikipedia://en/13035709) ($\alpha=\frac 1 2$) | $\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{t} - 1)^2,\,1-\sqrt{t}$ | $\frac{1}{2}\sum_i (\sqrt{p_i} - \sqrt{q_i})^2; \; 1 - \sum_i \sqrt{p_i q_i}$ |
| Neyman $\chi^2$-divergence | $(t - 1)^2$ | $\sum_i \frac{(p_i - q_i)^2}{q_i}$ |
| Pearson $\chi^2$-divergence | $\frac{(t-1)^2}{t}$ | $\sum_i \frac{(p_i - q_i)^2}{p_i}$ |

:::

*Image: Alpha-divergence.svg.* Comparison between the generators of alpha-divergences, as alpha varies from -1 to 2.

Let $f_\alpha$ be the generator of $\alpha$-divergence, then $f_\alpha$ and $f_{1-\alpha}$ are convex inversions of each other, so $D_{\alpha}(P\| Q) = D_{1-\alpha}(Q\| P)$. In particular, this shows that the squared Hellinger distance and Jensen-Shannon divergence are symmetric.

In the literature, the $\alpha$-divergences are sometimes parametrized as

$$\begin{cases} \frac{4}{1-\alpha^2}\big(1 - t^{(1+\alpha)/2}\big), & \text{if}\ \alpha\neq\pm1, \\ t \ln t, & \text{if}\ \alpha=1, \\ - \ln t, & \text{if}\ \alpha=-1 \end{cases}$$

which is equivalent to the parametrization in this page by substituting $\alpha \leftarrow \frac{\alpha+1}{2}$.

## Relations to other statistical divergences {#Relations_to_other_statistical_divergences}

Here, we compare *f*-divergences with other [statistical divergences](wikipedia://en/25896411).

### Rényi divergence {#Rényi_divergence}

The [Rényi divergences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renyi_divergence) is a family of divergences defined by

$$R_{\alpha} (P \| Q) = \frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\Bigg( E_Q\left[\left(\frac{dP}{dQ}\right)^\alpha\right] \Bigg) \,$$

when $\alpha \in (0, 1)\cup (1, +\infty)$. It is extended to the cases of $\alpha =0, 1, +\infty$ by taking the limit.

Simple algebra shows that $R_\alpha(P\| Q) = \frac{1}{\alpha - 1}\ln (1+\alpha(\alpha-1)D_\alpha(P\|Q))$, where $D_\alpha$ is the $\alpha$-divergence defined above.

### Bregman divergence {#Bregman_divergence}

The only f-divergence that is also a [Bregman divergence](wikipedia://en/4491248) is the KL divergence.[^o37]

### Integral probability metrics {#Integral_probability_metrics}

The only f-divergence that is also an [integral probability metric](wikipedia://en/73598108) is the total variation.[^o39]

## Financial interpretation {#Financial_interpretation}

A pair of probability distributions can be viewed as a game of chance in which one of the distributions defines the official odds and the other contains the actual probabilities. Knowledge of the actual probabilities allows a player to profit from the game. For a large class of rational players the expected profit rate has the same general form as the *ƒ*-divergence.[^o41]
