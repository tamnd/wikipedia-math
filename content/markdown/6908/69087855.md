# Lorentz oscillator model

*Image: Atom-spring.svg.* Electrons are bound to the atomic nucleus analogously to springs.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lorentz oscillator model"}

The **Lorentz oscillator model** (classical electron oscillator or CEO model) describes the optical response of [bound charges](wikipedia://en/698648#Definition). The model is named after the Dutch physicist [Hendrik Lorentz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lorentz) who proposed it in 1878.[^o2] It is a [classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_physics), phenomenological model for materials with characteristic resonance frequencies (or other characteristic energy scales) for optical absorption, e.g. ionic and [molecular vibrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_vibration), interband transitions (semiconductors), [phonons](wikipedia://en/85754), and collective excitations.[^o17][^o18]

:::

## Derivation {#Derivation}

### Electron motion {#Electron_motion}

The model is derived by modeling an electron orbiting a massive, stationary nucleus as a [spring-mass-damper system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass-spring-damper_model).[^o18][^o20][^o19] The electron is modeled to be connected to the nucleus via a hypothetical spring and its motion is damped by via a hypothetical damper. The damping force ensures that the oscillator's response is finite at its resonance frequency. For a time-harmonic driving force which originates from the [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092), [Newton's second law](wikipedia://en/55212#Newton's_second_law) can be applied to the electron to obtain the motion of the electron and expressions for the [dipole moment](wikipedia://en/30876071), [polarization](wikipedia://en/698648), [susceptibility](wikipedia://en/30876071), and [dielectric function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_permittivity).[^o19]

The equation of motion for the electron oscillator is

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf F_\text{net} =\mathbf F_\text{damping} + \mathbf F_\text{spring} + \mathbf F_\text{driving} &= m\frac{\mathrm d^2 \mathbf r}{\mathrm dt^2} \\[1ex] \frac{ -m}{ \tau} \frac{\mathrm d\mathbf r}{\mathrm dt} - k \mathbf r - {e} \mathbf E(t) &= m\frac{\mathrm d^2 \mathbf r}{\mathrm dt^2} \\[1ex] \frac{\mathrm d^2 \mathbf r}{\mathrm dt^2} + \frac{ 1}{ \tau} \frac{\mathrm d\mathbf r}{\mathrm dt} + \omega_0^2 \mathbf r\; &= \; \frac{-e}{m} \mathbf E(t), \end{aligned}$$

where

- $\mathbf r$ is the displacement of charge from the rest position,
- $t$ is time,
- $\tau$ is the relaxation time/scattering time,
- $k$ is a constant factor characteristic of the spring,
- $m$ is the effective mass of the electron,
- $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k / m }$ is the resonance frequency of the oscillator,
- $e$ is the [elementary charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_charge),
- $\mathbf E(t)$ is the electric field.

For time-harmonic fields

$$\mathbf E(t) = \mathbf E_0 e^{- i \omega t},$$

$$\mathbf r(t) = \mathbf r_0 e^{- i \omega t},$$

the stationary solution of this equation of motion is

$$\mathbf r(\omega) = \frac{-e}{m} \frac{1} {\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i \omega/\tau} \mathbf E(\omega).$$

The fact that the above solution is [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) means there is a time delay (phase shift) between the driving electric field and the response of the electron's motion.[^o19]

### Dipole moment {#Dipole_moment}

The displacement, $\mathbf r$, induces a dipole moment, $\mathbf p$, given by

$$\mathbf p(\omega) = -e \mathbf r(\omega) = \hat\alpha(\omega) \mathbf E(\omega) .$$

Here, $\hat \alpha(\omega)$ is the [polarizability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polarizability) of single oscillator, given by

$$\hat \alpha(\omega) = \frac{e^2}{m} \frac{1}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2) - i \omega/\tau} .$$

Three distinct [scattering](wikipedia://en/164483) regimes can be interpreted corresponding to the dominant denominator term in the dipole moment:[^o23]

::: {#o6 .table cue=table}

| Regime | Condition | Dispersion Scaling | Phase Shift |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Thomson scattering](wikipedia://en/624714) | $\omega^2 \gg \frac{\omega}{\tau}, \omega_0^2$ | $1$ | 0° |
| [Shneider-Miles scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shneider-Miles_scattering) | $\frac{\omega}{\tau}\gg\|\omega_0^2-\omega^2\|$ | $\omega^2$ | 90° |
| [Rayleigh scattering](wikipedia://en/26176) | $\omega_0^2\gg \omega^2 ,\frac{\omega}{\tau}$ | $\omega^4$ | 180° |

:::

### Polarization and electric displacement {#Polarization_and_electric_displacement}

The polarization $\mathbf P$ is the dipole moment per unit volume. For macroscopic material properties $N$ is the density of charges (electrons) per unit volume. Considering that each electron is acting with the same dipole moment we have the polarization as

$$\mathbf P = N \mathbf p = N \hat \alpha(\omega) \mathbf E(\omega) .$$

The electric displacement $\mathbf D$ is related to the polarization density $\mathbf P$ by

$$\mathbf D = \hat\varepsilon \mathbf E = \mathbf E + 4\pi \mathbf P = (1 + 4\pi N \hat \alpha) \mathbf E.$$

## Dielectric function {#Dielectric_function}

*Image: Lorentz Oscillator Model.png.* Lorentz oscillator model. The real (blue solid line) and imaginary (orange dashed line) components of relative permittivity are plotted for a single oscillator model with parameters $\omega_0 = \mathrm{23.8\,THz}$ (12.6 μm), $s/\omega_0^2 = \mathrm{3.305}$, $\Gamma/\omega_0 = \mathrm{0.006}$, and $\varepsilon_{\infty} = \mathrm{6.7}$. These parameters approximate hexagonal silicon carbide.[^o21]

The complex dielectric function is (in [Gaussian units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_units)):

$$\hat \varepsilon(\omega) = 1 + \frac{4\pi N e^2}{m} \frac{1}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2) - i \omega/\tau},$$

where one can define $\omega_p^2 \equiv \frac{4\pi N e^2}{m}$, which is the square of the so-called [plasma frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_frequency).

In practice, the model is commonly modified to account for multiple absorption mechanisms present in a medium. This modified version is given by[^o22]

$$\hat \varepsilon(\omega) = \varepsilon_{\infty} + \sum_{j} \chi_{j}^{L}(\omega; \omega_{0,j}),$$

where

$$\chi_{j}^{L}(\omega; \omega_{0,j}) = \frac{s_j}{\omega_{0,j}^2 - \omega^2 - i \Gamma_j \omega},$$

and

- $\varepsilon_{\infty}$ is the value of the dielectric function at infinite frequency, which can be used as an adjustable parameter to account for high frequency absorption mechanisms;
- $s_{j} = \omega_p^{2} f_{j}$ and $f_{j}$ is related to the strength of the $j$th absorption mechanism;
- $$\Gamma_{j} = 1/\tau.$$

Separating the real and imaginary components,

$$\hat \varepsilon(\omega) = \varepsilon_1(\omega) + i \varepsilon_2(\omega) = \left[ \varepsilon_{\infty} + \sum_{j} \frac{s_{j} (\omega_{0,j}^2 - \omega^2)}{\left(\omega_{0,j}^{2} - \omega^{2}\right)^{2} + \left(\Gamma_{j} \omega\right)^2} \right] + i \left[ \sum_{j} \frac{s_{j} (\Gamma_{j} \omega)}{\left(\omega_{0,j}^{2} - \omega^{2}\right)^{2} + \left(\Gamma_{j} \omega\right)^{2}} \right].$$

## Complex conductivity {#Complex_conductivity}

The complex [optical conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_conductivity) in general is related to the complex dielectric function (in [Gaussian units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_units)) as

$$\hat \sigma(\omega) = \frac{\omega}{4\pi i} \left(\hat\varepsilon(\omega) - 1\right).$$

Substituting the formula of $\hat\varepsilon(\omega)$ in the equation above we obtain

$$\hat \sigma(\omega) = \frac{N e^2}{m} \frac{\omega}{\omega/\tau + i \left(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 \right)} .$$

Separating the real and imaginary components gives

$$\hat \sigma(\omega) = \sigma_1(\omega) + i \sigma_2(\omega) = \frac{N e^2}{m} \frac{\frac{\omega^2}{\tau}}{\left(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2\right)^2 + \omega^2 / \tau^2 } - i \frac{N e^2}{m} \frac{\left(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2\right) \omega}{\left(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2\right)^2 + \omega^2/\tau^2}.$$

## Zeeman effect {#Zeeman_effect}

Soon after [Zeeman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pieter_Zeeman)'s discovery of the [Zeeman effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeeman_effect), Lorentz used the oscillator model to give a theoretical explanation of it. He added a term corresponding to the [Lorentz force](wikipedia://en/18631) to the equation, which couples the equations of motions in the x- and y-directions (assuming a magnetic field in the z-direction). In Gaussian units, this gives

$$m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}=-kx+\frac{eH}{c}\frac{dy}{dt}$$

$$m\frac{d^2y}{dt^2}=-ky+\frac{eH}{c}\frac{dx}{dt}.$$

Solving these equations gives two different solutions, depending on whether the electron moves clockwise or counterclockwise. These solutions have different frequencies:

$$\omega_1^2-\frac{eH}{mc}\omega_1=\omega_0^2$$

$$\omega_2^2-\frac{eH}{mc}\omega_2=\omega_0^2.$$

In the case of a small magnetic field, this gives a small frequency difference that is linearly proportional to the magnetic field:

$$\Delta \omega = \frac{eH}{2mc}.$$

Thus, a spectral line is indeed split by a magnetic field, in qualitative correspondence to Zeeman's discovery.[^o14]
