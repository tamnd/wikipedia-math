# Projectile motion

*Image: ParabolicWaterTrajectory.jpg.* [Parabolic trajectories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabolic_trajectory) of water jets

*Image: Ferde hajitas2.svg.* Components of initial velocity of parabolic throwing

*Image: Ballistic trajectories.svg.* Ballistic trajectories are parabolic if gravity is homogeneous, and elliptic if it is radial.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Projectile motion"}

In physics, **projectile motion** describes the motion of an object that is launched into the air and moves under the influence of gravity alone, with air resistance neglected. In this idealized model, the object follows a parabolic path determined by its initial velocity and the constant acceleration due to gravity. The motion can be decomposed into horizontal and vertical components: the horizontal motion occurs at a constant velocity, while the vertical motion experiences uniform acceleration.

:::

This framework, which lies at the heart of classical mechanics, is fundamental to a wide range of applications—from engineering and ballistics to sports science and natural phenomena.

[Galileo Galilei](wikipedia://en/29688374) showed that the trajectory of a given projectile is [parabolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabolic_trajectory), but the path may also be [straight](wikipedia://en/946975) in the special case when the object is thrown directly upward or downward. The study of such motions is called [ballistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistics), and such a trajectory is described as [ballistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/External_ballistics). The force of mathematical significance that is actively exerted on the object is gravity, which acts downward, thus imparting to the object a downward [acceleration](wikipedia://en/2443) towards Earth's [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961). Due to the object's [inertia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertia), no external force is needed to maintain the horizontal velocity [component](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_component) of the object's motion.

Taking other forces into account, such as [aerodynamic drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_%28physics%29) or internal propulsion (such as in a [rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket)), requires additional analysis. A [ballistic missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistic_missile) is a [missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missile) only [guided](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missile_guidance) during the relatively brief initial [powered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powered_flight) phase of flight, and whose remaining course is governed by the laws of [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586).

Ballistics (grc) is the science of [dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_%28physics%29) that deals with the flight, behavior and effects of projectiles, especially [bullets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullet), [unguided bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unguided_bomb), [rockets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket), or the like; the science or art of designing and accelerating projectiles so as to achieve a desired performance.

*Image: Mplwp ballistic trajectories velocities.svg.* Trajectories of a projectile with air drag and varying initial velocities

The elementary equations of ballistics neglect nearly every factor except for initial velocity, the launch angle and a gravitational acceleration assumed constant. Practical solutions of a ballistics problem often require considerations of air resistance, cross winds, target motion, acceleration due to gravity varying with height, and in such problems as [launching a rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_rocket_launching_site) from one point on the Earth to another, the horizon's distance *vs* curvature R of the Earth (its local speed of rotation $v(lat)=\omega R(lat)$). Detailed mathematical solutions of practical problems typically do not have [closed-form](wikipedia://en/585143) solutions, and therefore require [numerical methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_methods) to address.

## Trajectory in vacuum {#Trajectory_in_vacuum}

In projectile motion, the horizontal motion and the vertical motion are independent of each other; that is, neither motion affects the other. This is the principle of *compound motion* established by [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo) in 1638,[^o3] and used by him to prove the parabolic form of projectile motion.[^o4]

*Image: Compound Motion.gif.* The horizontal and vertical components of a projectile's velocity are independent of each other.

A ballistic trajectory is a parabola with homogeneous acceleration, such as in a space ship with constant acceleration in absence of other forces. On Earth the acceleration changes magnitude with altitude as $g(y)=g_0/(1+y/R)^2$ and direction (faraway targets) with latitude/longitude along the trajectory. This causes an [elliptic](wikipedia://en/9277) trajectory, which is very close to a parabola on a small scale. However, if an object was thrown and the Earth was suddenly replaced with a [black hole](wikipedia://en/4650) of equal mass, it would become obvious that the ballistic trajectory is part of an elliptic [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit) around that "black hole", and not a parabola that extends to infinity. At higher speeds the trajectory can also be circular ([cosmonautics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronautics) at [LEO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit)?, [geostationary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostationary_orbit) satellites at 5$\frac{5}{6}$ R), parabolic or [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/14052) (unless distorted by other objects like the Moon or the Sun).[^o5]

In this article a *homogeneous* gravitational acceleration $(g=g_0)$ is assumed.

### Acceleration {#Acceleration}

Since there is acceleration only in the vertical direction, the velocity in the horizontal direction is constant, being equal to $\mathbf{v}_0 \cos\theta$. The vertical motion of the projectile is the motion of a particle during its free fall. Here the acceleration is constant, being equal to g.g is the [acceleration due to gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_gravity). ($9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ near the surface of the Earth). The components of the acceleration are:

$$a_x = 0,$$

$a_y = -g$.\*

*\*The y acceleration can also be referred to as the force of the earth* $(-F_g/m)$ *on the object(s) of interest.*

### Velocity {#Velocity}

Let the projectile be launched with an initial [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) $\mathbf{v}(0) \equiv \mathbf{v}_0$, which can be expressed as the sum of horizontal and vertical components as follows:

$$\mathbf{v}_0 = v_{0x}\mathbf{\hat x} + v_{0y}\mathbf{\hat y}.$$

The components $v_{0x}$ and $v_{0y}$ can be found if the initial launch angle θ is known:

$$v_{0x} = v_0\cos(\theta),$$

$$v_{0y} = v_0\sin(\theta)$$

The horizontal component of the [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) of the object remains unchanged throughout the motion. The vertical component of the velocity changes linearly,decreasing when the object goes upward, and increasing when it goes downward because the acceleration due to gravity is constant. The accelerations in the x and y directions can be integrated to solve for the components of velocity at any time t, as follows:

$$v_x = v_0 \cos(\theta),$$

$$v_y = v_0 \sin(\theta) - gt.$$

The magnitude of the velocity (under the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034), also known as the triangle law):

$$v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2 }.$$

::: {#o9 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Range (ballistics)"}

### Displacement {#Displacement}

*Image: Ferde hajitas3.svg.* Displacement and coordinates of parabolic throwing

At any time $t$, the projectile's horizontal and vertical [displacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_%28vector%29) are:

$$x = v_0 t \cos(\theta),$$

$$y = v_0 t \sin(\theta) - \frac{1}{2}gt^2.$$

The magnitude of the displacement is:

$$\Delta r=\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 }.$$

Consider the equations,

$x = v_0 t \cos(\theta)$ and $y = v_0 t\sin(\theta) - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$.[^o10]

If t is eliminated between these two equations the following equation is obtained:

$$y = \tan(\theta) \cdot x-\frac{g}{2v^2_{0}\cos^2 \theta} \cdot x^2=\tan\theta \cdot x \left(1-\frac{x}{R}\right).$$

Here R is the [range of a projectile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Range_of_a_projectile).

Since g, θ, and v<sub>0</sub> are constants, the above equation is of the form

$$y=ax+bx^2,$$

in which a and b are constants. This is the equation of a parabola, so the path is parabolic. The axis of the parabola is vertical.

If the projectile's position (x,y) and launch angle (θ or α) are known, the initial velocity can be found solving for v<sub>0</sub> in the afore-mentioned parabolic equation:

$$v_0 = \sqrt{{x^2 g} \over {x \sin 2\theta - 2y \cos^2\theta}}.$$

:::

### Displacement in polar coordinates {#Displacement_in_polar_coordinates}

The parabolic trajectory of a projectile can also be expressed in polar coordinates instead of [Cartesian](wikipedia://en/7706) coordinates. In this case, the position has the general formula

$$r( \phi ) = \frac{2v_0^2 \cos^2\theta}{|g|} \left(\tan\theta\sec\phi -\tan\phi\sec\phi \right).$$

In this equation, the origin is the midpoint of the horizontal range of the projectile, and if the ground is flat, the parabolic arc is plotted in the range $0 \leq \phi \leq \pi$. This expression can be obtained by transforming the Cartesian equation as stated above by $y = r \sin\phi$ and $x = r \cos\phi$.

### Time of flight or total time of the whole journey {#Time_of_flight_or_total_time_of_the_whole_journey}

The total time t for which the projectile remains in the air is called the time-of-flight. The y-component of the trajectory is

$$y=y_0 + v_{0y} t -\frac{1}{2} g t^2$$

After the flight, the projectile returns to the horizontal axis (x-axis), so $y=0$.

$$y_0 + v_{0y} t - \frac{1}{2} g t^2 = 0$$

Solving this using the quadratic formula yields

$$t = \frac{v_{0y} \pm \sqrt{v_{0y}^2 + 2 g y_0}}{g}$$

Note, we want the positive square root in this instance. Assuming the projectile starts from 0 ($y_0 = 0$) and using $v_{0y} = v_0 \sin (\theta)$, this gives:

$$t=\frac{2 v_0\sin(\theta)}{g}$$

Note that we have neglected air resistance on the projectile.

If the starting point is at height y<sub>0</sub> with respect to the point of impact, the time of flight is:

$$t = \frac{d}{v \cos\theta} = \frac{v \sin \theta + \sqrt{(v \sin \theta)^2 + 2gy_0}}{g}$$

As above, this expression can be reduced (y<sub>0</sub> is 0) to

$t = \frac{v\sin{\theta} + \sqrt{(v\sin{\theta})^2}}{g} = \frac{2v\sin{\theta}}{g},$ = $\frac{2v\sin{(45^\circ)}}{|g|} = \frac{\sqrt{2}v}{|g|}$

if θ equals 45°.

If $v_{y0} = 0$ e. g. for an object dropped from (vertical) rest at a height $y_0$ and free-falling under gravity, the time of flight becomes,

> $$t = \sqrt{\frac{2y_0}{g}}$$

This result is independent of any initial horizontal velocity, as illustrated in the animation above.

### Time of flight to the target's position {#Time_of_flight_to_the_target's_position}

As shown above in the **Displacement** section, the horizontal and vertical velocity of a projectile are independent of each other.

Because of this, we can find the time to reach a target using the displacement formula for the horizontal velocity:

$$x = v_0 t \cos(\theta)$$

$$\frac{x}{t}=v_0\cos(\theta)$$

$$t=\frac{x}{v_0\cos(\theta)}$$

This equation will give the total time *t* the projectile must travel for to reach the target's horizontal displacement, neglecting air resistance.

### Maximum height of projectile {#Maximum_height_of_projectile}

*Image: Ferde hajitas4.svg.* Maximum height of projectile

The greatest height that the object will reach is known as the peak of the object's motion. The increase in height will last until $v_y=0$, that is,

$$0=v_0 \sin(\theta) - gt_h.$$

Time to reach the maximum height(h):

$$t_h = \frac{v_0 \sin(\theta)}{|g|}.$$

This matches the formula found above (the time of flight) as the maximum height (in a parabola) sits in the middle of its roots. This means the projectile reaches *h* in the middle of the journey, therefore, in half of the time.

For the vertical displacement of the maximum height of the projectile:

$$h = v_0 t_h \sin(\theta) - \frac{1}{2} gt^2_h$$

$$h = \frac{v_0^2 \sin^2(\theta)}{2|g|}$$

The maximum reachable height is obtained for *θ*=90°:

$$h_{\mathrm{max}} = \frac{v_0^2}{2|g|}$$

If the projectile's position (x,y) and launch angle (θ) are known, the maximum height can be found by solving for h in the following equation:

$$h=\frac{(x\tan\theta)^2}{4(x\tan\theta-y)}.$$

Angle of [elevation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevation) (φ) at the maximum height is given by:

$$\phi = \arctan{{\tan\theta\over 2}}$$

### Relation between horizontal range and maximum height {#Relation_between_horizontal_range_and_maximum_height}

The relation between the range d on the horizontal plane and the maximum height h reached at $\frac{t_d}{2}$ is:

$$h = \frac{d\tan\theta}{4}$$

::: {#o16 .proof cue=collapse name="Proof"}

$$h = \frac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{2|g|}$$

$$d = \frac{v_0^2\sin2\theta}{|g|}$$

$\frac{h}{d} = \frac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{2|g|}$ × $\frac{g}{v_0^2\sin2\theta}$

$$\frac{h}{d} = \frac{\sin^2\theta}{4\sin\theta\cos\theta}$$

$$h = \frac{d\tan\theta}{4}.$$

If $h = R$

**$\theta = \arctan(4)\approx 76.0^\circ$**

:::

### Maximum distance of projectile {#Maximum_distance_of_projectile}

*Image: Ferde hajitas5.svg.* The maximum distance of projectile

The range and the maximum height of the projectile do not depend upon its mass. Hence range and maximum height are equal for all bodies that are thrown with the same velocity and direction. The horizontal range d of the projectile is the horizontal distance it has traveled when it returns to its initial height ($y=0$).

$$0 = v_0 t_d \sin(\theta) - \frac{1}{2}gt_d^2.$$

Time to reach ground:

$$t_d = \frac{2v_0 \sin(\theta)}{|g|}.$$

From the horizontal displacement the maximum distance of the projectile:[^o18]

$$d = v_0 t_d \cos(\theta),$$

so$2\cdot\sin(\alpha)\cdot\cos(\alpha) = \sin(2\alpha)$

$$d = \frac{v_0^2}{|g|}\sin(2\theta).$$

Note that d has its maximum value when

$$\sin(2\theta)=1,$$

which necessarily corresponds to $2\theta=90^\circ$, or $\theta=45^\circ$.

*Image: Ideal projectile motion for different angles.svg.* Trajectories of projectiles launched at different elevation angles but the same speed of 10 m/s, in a vacuum and uniform downward gravity field of 10 m/s<sup>2</sup>. Points are at 0.05 s intervals and length of their tails is linearly proportional to their speed. *t* = time from launch, *T* = time of flight, *R* = range and *H* = highest point of trajectory (indicated with arrows).

The total horizontal distance (d) traveled.[^o19]

$$d = \frac{v \cos \theta}{|g|} \left( v \sin \theta + \sqrt{(v \sin \theta)^2 + 2gy_0} \right)$$

When the surface is flat (initial height of the object is zero), the distance traveled:[^o20]

$$d = \frac{v^2 \sin(2 \theta)}{|g|}$$

Thus the maximum distance is obtained if θ is 45 degrees. This distance is:

$$d_{\mathrm{max}} = \frac{v^2}{|g|}$$

::: {#o22 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Application of the work energy theorem"}

### Application of the work energy theorem {#Application_of_the_work_energy_theorem}

According to the [work-energy theorem](wikipedia://en/149861#Work–energy_principle) the vertical component of velocity is:

$$v_y^2 = (v_0 \sin \theta)^2-2gy.$$

These formulae ignore [aerodynamic drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_%28physics%29) and also assume that the landing area is at uniform height 0.

:::

### Angle of reach {#Angle_of_reach}

The "angle of reach" is the angle (θ) at which a projectile must be launched in order to go a distance d, given the initial velocity v.

$$\sin(2\theta) = \frac{gd}{v^2}$$

There are two solutions:

$\theta = \frac{1}{2} \arcsin \left( \frac{gd}{v^2} \right)$ (shallow trajectory)

and because $\sin(2\theta) = \cos (2\theta - 90^\circ )$,

$\theta = 45^\circ + \frac{1}{2} \arccos \left( \frac{gd}{v^2} \right)$ (steep trajectory)

### Angle θ required to hit coordinate (x, y) {#Angle_θ_required_to_hit_coordinate_(x,_y)}

*Image: Trajectory for changing launch angle.gif.* Vacuum trajectory of a projectile for different launch angles. Launch speed is the same for all angles, 50 m/s, and g is 10 m/s<sup>2</sup>.

To hit a target at range x and altitude y when fired from (0,0) and with initial speed v, the required angle(s) of launch θ are:

$$\theta = \arctan{\left(\frac{v^2\pm\sqrt{v^4-g(gx^2+2yv^2)}}{gx}\right)}$$

The two roots of the equation correspond to the two possible launch angles, so long as they aren't imaginary, in which case the initial speed is not great enough to reach the point (x,y) selected. This formula allows one to find the angle of launch needed without the restriction of $y= 0$.

One can also ask what launch angle allows the lowest possible launch velocity. This occurs when the two solutions above are equal, implying that the quantity under the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) sign is zero. This, tan θ = v<sup>2</sup>/gx, requires solving a [quadratic equation](wikipedia://en/25175) for $v^2$,[^o25] and we find

<sup>$v^2/g = (2gy\pm g\sqrt{4y^2+4(1x)^2})/2g = y\pm\sqrt{y^2+x^2}.$</sup>

This gives

$$\theta=\arctan\left(y/x+\sqrt{y^2/x^2+1}\right).$$

If we denote the angle whose tangent is $y/x$ by $\alpha$,[^o26] then

$\tan\theta=\frac{\sin\alpha+1}{\cos\alpha},$ its reciprocal:

$\tan(\pi/2-\theta)=\frac{\cos\alpha}{\sin\alpha+1},$[^o27]

$$\cos^2(\pi/2-\theta)=\frac 12(\sin\alpha+1)$$

$$2\cos^2(\pi/2-\theta)-1=\cos(\pi/2-\alpha)$$

This implies

$$\theta = \pi/2 - \frac 12(\pi/2-\alpha).$$

In other words, the launch should be at the angle $(\pi/2 + \alpha)/2$ halfway between the target and [zenith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenith) (vector opposite to gravity).

### Total Path Length of the Trajectory {#Total_Path_Length_of_the_Trajectory}

The length of the parabolic arc traced by a projectile, L, given that the height of launch and landing is the same (there is no air resistance), is given by the formula:

$$L = \frac{v_0^2}{2g} \left( 2\sin\theta + \cos^2\theta\cdot\ln \frac{1 + \sin\theta}{1 - \sin\theta} \right) = \frac{v_0^2}{g} \left( \sin\theta + \cos^2\theta\cdot\tanh^{-1}(\sin\theta) \right)$$

where $v_0$ is the initial velocity, $\theta$ is the launch angle and $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity as a positive value. The expression can be obtained by evaluating the [arc length integral](wikipedia://en/1898401) for the height-distance parabola between the bounds *initial* and *final* displacement (i.e. between 0 and the horizontal range of the projectile) such that:

$$L = \int_{0}^{\mathrm{range}} \sqrt{1 + \left ( \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} \right )^2}\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{0}^{v_0^2 \sin(2\theta)/g} \sqrt{1+\left (\tan\theta -{g\over {v_0^2 \cos^2\theta}}x\right)^2}\,\mathrm{d}x .$$

If the time-of-flight is *t*,

$$L = \int_{0}^{t} \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}\,\mathrm{d}t = \int_{0}^{2v_0\sin\theta/g} \sqrt{(gt)^2-2gv_0\sin\theta t+v_0^2}\,\mathrm{d}t.$$

## Trajectory in air {#Trajectory_in_air}

*Image: Inclinedthrow2.gif.* Trajectories of a mass thrown at an angle of 70°: without [drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_%28physics%29) (a parabole) with [Stokes' drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stokes'_law) with [Newtonian drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_drag)

Air resistance creates a force that (for symmetric projectiles) is always directed against the direction of motion in the surrounding medium and has a magnitude that depends on the absolute speed: $\mathbf{F_{air}} = -f(v)\cdot\mathbf{\hat v}$. The speed-dependence of the friction force is linear ($f(v) \propto v$) at very low speeds ([Stokes drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stokes_drag)) and quadratic ($f(v) \propto v^2$) at large speeds ([Newton drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_equation)).[^o30] The transition between these behaviours is determined by the [Reynolds number](wikipedia://en/23868856), which depends on object speed and size, density $\rho$ and [dynamic viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754) $\eta$ of the medium. For Reynolds numbers below about 1 the dependence is linear, above 1000 ([turbulent flow](wikipedia://en/3087410)) it becomes quadratic. In air, which has a [kinematic viscosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinematic_viscosity) $\eta/\rho$ around 0.15 cm<sup>2</sup>/s, this means that the drag force becomes quadratic in *v* when the product of object speed and diameter is more than about 0.015 m<sup>2</sup>/s, which is typically the case for projectiles.

- Stokes drag: $\mathbf{F_{air}} = -k_{\mathrm{Stokes}}\cdot\mathbf{v}\qquad$ (for $Re\lesssim 1$)
- Newton drag: $\mathbf{F_{air}} = -k\,|\mathbf{v}|\cdot\mathbf{v}\qquad$ (for $Re\gtrsim 1000$)

*Image: Free body diagram gravity air resistance.svg.* Free body diagram of a body on which only gravity and air resistance act.

The [free body diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_body_diagram) on the right is for a projectile that experiences air resistance and the effects of gravity. Here, air resistance is assumed to be in the direction opposite of the projectile's velocity: $\mathbf{F_{\mathrm{air}}} = -f(v)\cdot\mathbf{\hat v}$

### Trajectory of a projectile with Stokes drag {#Trajectory_of_a_projectile_with_Stokes_drag}

Stokes drag, where $\mathbf{F_{air}} \propto \mathbf{v}$, only applies at very low speed in air, and is thus not the typical case for projectiles. However, the linear dependence of $F_\mathrm{air}$ on $v$ causes a very simple differential equation of motion

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\begin{pmatrix}v_x \\ v_y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-\mu\,v_x \\ -g-\mu\,v_y\end{pmatrix}$$

in which the 2 cartesian components become completely independent, and it is thus easier to solve.[^o32] Here, $v_0$,$v_x$ and $v_y$ will be used to denote the initial velocity, the velocity along the direction of x and the velocity along the direction of y, respectively. The mass of the projectile will be denoted by m, and $\mu:=k/m$. For the derivation only the case where $0^o \le \theta \le 180^o$ is considered. Again, the projectile is fired from the origin (0,0).

The relationships that represent the motion of the particle are derived by [Newton's second law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_second_law), both in the x and y directions. In the x direction $\Sigma F = -kv_x = ma_x$ and in the y direction $\Sigma F = -kv_y - mg = ma_y$.

This implies that:

$a_x = -\mu v_x = \frac{\mathrm{d}v_x}{\mathrm{d}t}$ (1),

and

$a_y = -\mu v_y - g = \frac{\mathrm{d}v_y}{\mathrm{d}t}$ (2)

Solving (1) is an elementary [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309), thus the steps leading to a unique solution for v<sub>x</sub> and, subsequently, x will not be enumerated. Given the initial conditions $v_x = v_{x0}$ (where v<sub>x0</sub> is understood to be the x component of the initial velocity) and $x=0$ for $t=0$:

$v_x = v_{x0} e^{-\mu t}$ (1a)

$x(t) = \frac{v_{x0}}{\mu}\left(1-e^{-\mu t}\right)$ (1b)

While (1) is solved much in the same way, (2) is of distinct interest because of its non-homogeneous nature. Hence, we will be extensively solving (2). Note that in this case the initial conditions are used $v_y=v_{y0}$ and $y=0$ when $t=0$.

$\frac{\mathrm{d}v_y}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\mu v_y - g$ (2)

$\frac{\mathrm{d}v_y}{\mathrm{d}t} + \mu v_y = - g$ (2a)

This first order, linear, non-homogeneous differential equation may be solved a number of ways; however, in this instance, it will be quicker to approach the solution via an [integrating factor](wikipedia://en/1912874) $e^{\int \mu \,\mathrm{d}t}$.

$e^{\mu t}(\frac{\mathrm{d}v_y}{\mathrm{d}t} + \mu v_y) = e^{\mu t}(-g)$ (2c)

$(e^{\mu t}v_y)^\prime = e^{\mu t}(-g)$ (2d)

$\int{(e^{\mu t}v_y)^\prime \,\mathrm{d}t} = e^{\mu t}v_y = \int{ e^{\mu t}(-g) \,\mathrm{d}t}$ (2e)

$e^{\mu t}v_y = \frac{1}{\mu} e^{\mu t}(-g) + C$(2f)

$v_y = \frac{-g}{\mu} + Ce^{-\mu t}$ (2g)

And by integration we find:

$y = -\frac{g}{\mu}t - \frac{1}{\mu}(v_{y0} + \frac{g}{\mu})e^{-\mu t} + C$ (3)

Solving for our initial conditions:

$v_y(t) = -\frac{g}{\mu} + (v_{y0} + \frac{g}{\mu})e^{-\mu t}$ (2h)

$y(t) = -\frac{g}{\mu}t - \frac{1}{\mu}(v_{y0} + \frac{g}{\mu})e^{-\mu t} + \frac{1}{\mu}(v_{y0} + \frac{g}{\mu})$ (3a)

With a bit of algebra to simplify (3a):

$y(t) = -\frac{g}{\mu} t + \frac{1}{\mu} \left(v_{y0} + \frac{g}{\mu} \right) (1 - e^{-\mu t} )$ (3b)

The total time of the journey in the presence of air resistance (more specifically, when $F_{air}=-kv$) can be calculated by the same strategy as above, namely, we solve the equation $y(t)=0$. While in the case of zero air resistance this equation can be solved elementarily, here we shall need the [Lambert W function](wikipedia://en/92465). The equation $y(t)= -\frac{g}{\mu}t + \frac{1}{\mu}(v_{y0} + \frac{g}{\mu})(1 - e^{-\mu t}) = 0$ is of the form $c_1t+c_2+c_3e^{c_4t}=0$, and such an equation can be transformed into an equation solvable by the $W$ function (see an example of such a transformation [here](wikipedia://en/92465#Solving_equations)). Some algebra shows that the total time of flight, in closed form, is given as[^o33]

$$t=\frac{1}{\mu} \left(1+ \frac{\mu}{g} v_{y0} + W\bigl(-(1+ \frac{\mu}{g} v_{y0}) e^{-(1+ \frac{\mu}{g} v_{y0})} \bigr)\right).$$

### Trajectory of a projectile with Newton drag {#Trajectory_of_a_projectile_with_Newton_drag}

*Image: Mplwp skydive trajectory.svg.* Trajectories of a [skydiver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skydiver) in air with Newton drag: para-flight; bomber near(ly above) target. $v.d=10^3.\nu=150$ cm<sup>2</sup>/s?

The most typical case of [air resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_resistance), in case of [Reynolds numbers](wikipedia://en/23868856) above about 1000, is Newton drag with a drag force proportional to the speed squared, $F_{\mathrm{air}} = -k v^2$. In air, which has a [kinematic viscosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinematic_viscosity) around 0.15 cm<sup>2</sup>/s, this means that the product of object speed and diameter must be more than about 0.015 m<sup>2</sup>/s.

Unfortunately, the equations of motion can ***not*** be easily solved analytically for this case. Therefore, a numerical solution will be examined.

The following assumptions are made:

- Constant [gravitational acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_acceleration) $g$
- Air resistance is given by the following [drag formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_equation),

$$\mathbf{F_D} = -\tfrac{1}{2} c \rho A\, v\,\mathbf{v}$$

Where:

- *F<sub>D</sub>* is the drag force,
- *c* is the [drag coefficient](wikipedia://en/172291),
- ρ is the [air density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_density),
- *A* is the [cross sectional area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_sectional_area) of the projectile. Again $\mu=k/m :=$ $c\rho A/(2m)$ $= c \rho/(2\rho_p l)$. Compare this with theory/practice of the [ballistic coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistic_coefficient).

::: {#o36 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Special cases"}

#### Special cases {#Special_cases}

Even though the general case of a projectile with Newton drag cannot be solved analytically, some special cases can. Here we denote the [terminal velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_velocity) in free-fall as $v_\infty=\sqrt{g/\mu}$ and the characteristic *settling time* constant $t_f\equiv1/(v_\infty \mu) =1/\sqrt{g\mu}$. (Dimension of $g$ \[m/s<sup>2</sup>\], $\mu$ \[1/m\])

- Near-horizontal motion: In case the motion is almost horizontal, $|v_x|\gg|v_y|$, such as a flying bullet. The vertical velocity component has very little influence on the horizontal motion. In this case:[^o37]

$$\dot{v}_x(t) = -\mu\,v_x^2(t)$$

$$v_x(t) = \frac{1}{1/v_{x,0}+\mu\,t}$$

$$x(t) = \frac{1}{\mu}\ln(1+\mu\,v_{x,0}\cdot t)$$

The same pattern applies for motion with friction along a line in any direction, when gravity is negligible (relatively small $g$). It also applies when vertical motion is prevented, such as for a moving car with its engine off.

- Vertical motion upward:[^o37]

$$\dot{v}_y(t) = -g-\mu\,v_y^2(t)$$

$$v_y(t) = v_\infty \tan\frac{t_{\mathrm{peak}}-t}{t_f}$$

$$y(t) = y_{\mathrm{peak}} + \frac{1}{\mu} \ln \bigl(\cos\frac{t_\mathrm{peak}-t}{t_f} \bigr)$$

Here

$v_\infty \equiv\sqrt{\frac{g}{\mu}}$ and $t_f=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu g}},$

$$t_{\mathrm{peak}} \equiv t_f \arctan{\frac{v_{y,0}}{v_\infty}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu g}} \arctan{\left(\sqrt\frac{\mu}{g}v_{y,0}\right)},$$

and

$$y_{\mathrm{peak}} \equiv -\frac{1}{\mu}\ln{\cos{\frac{t_\mathrm{peak}}{t_f}}} = \frac{1}{2\mu} \ln{ \bigl(1+\frac{\mu}{g} v_{y,0}^2 \bigr)}$$

where $v_{y,0}$ is the initial upward velocity at $t = 0$ and the initial position is $y(0) = 0$.

A projectile cannot rise longer than $t_\mathrm{rise}=\frac{\pi}{2}t_f$ in the vertical direction, when it reaches the peak (0 m, y<sub>peak</sub>) at 0 m/s.

- Vertical motion downward:[^o37]

$$\dot{v}_y(t) = -g+\mu\,v_y^2(t)$$

$$v_y(t) = -v_\infty \tanh\frac{t-t_{\mathrm{peak}}}{t_f}$$

$y(t) = y_{\mathrm{peak}} - \frac{1}{\mu} \ln(\cosh\biggl(\frac{t-t_\mathrm{peak}}{t_f}\biggr))$ With *hyperbolic* functions

After a time $t_f$ at y=0, the projectile reaches almost terminal velocity $-v_\infty$.

:::

#### Numerical solution {#Numerical_solution}

A projectile motion with drag can be computed generically by [numerical integration](wikipedia://en/272829) of the [ordinary differential equation](wikipedia://en/32742753), for instance by applying a [reduction to a first-order system](wikipedia://en/32742753#Reduction_to_a_first-order_system). The equation to be solved is

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\begin{pmatrix}x \\ y \\ v_x \\ v_y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}v_x \\ v_y \\ -\mu\,v_x\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2} \\ -g-\mu\,v_y\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}\end{pmatrix}.$$

This approach also allows to add the effects of speed-dependent drag coefficient, altitude-dependent air density (in product $c(v)\rho(y)$) and position-dependent gravity field $g(y)=g_0/(1+y/R)^2$ (when $y \ll R: g \lesssim g_0/(1+2y/R) \approx g_0(1-2y/R)$, is linear decrease).

::: {#o40 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Lofted trajectory"}

## Lofted trajectory {#Lofted_trajectory}

*Image: Trajectories of Hwasong-14.svg.* Lofted trajectories of North Korean ballistic missiles [Hwasong-14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-14), [Hwasong-15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-15) and [Hwasong-17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-17)

A special case of a ballistic trajectory for a rocket is a lofted trajectory, a trajectory with an [apogee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apogee) greater than the [minimum-energy](wikipedia://en/3596263) trajectory to the same range. In other words, the rocket travels higher and by doing so it uses more energy to get to the same landing point. This may be done for various reasons such as increasing distance to the horizon to give greater viewing/communication range or for changing the angle with which a missile will impact on landing. Lofted trajectories are sometimes used in both missile rocketry and in [spaceflight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceflight).[^o41]

:::

## Projectile motion on a planetary scale {#Projectile_motion_on_a_planetary_scale}

*Image: Ballistic-trajectories-planet2.svg.* Projectile trajectory around a planet, compared to the motion in a uniform gravity field

When a projectile travels a range that is significant compared to the Earth's radius, the [curvature of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curvature_of_the_Earth) and the non-uniform [Earth's gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_gravity) have to be considered. This is, for example, the case with spacecraft and intercontinental [missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistic_missile). The trajectory then generalizes (without air resistance) from a parabola to a Kepler-[ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) with one focus at the center of the Earth (shown in fig. 3). The projectile motion then follows [Kepler's laws of planetary motion](wikipedia://en/17553).[^o5]

The trajectory's parameters have to be adapted from the values of a uniform gravity field stated above. The [Earth radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_radius) is taken as R, and g as the standard surface gravity. Let $\tilde v:=v/\sqrt{Rg}$ be the launch velocity relative to the first cosmic velocity (the velocity required for a [circular orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_orbit)).[^o43]

Total range d between launch and impact:

$d = \frac{v^2 \sin(2 \theta)}{g} \Big/ \sqrt{1-\left(2-\tilde v^2\right)\tilde v^2\cos^2\theta}$ (where launch angle $\theta=\tfrac12\arccos\left(\tilde v^2/(2-\tilde v^2)\right)$)

Maximum range of a projectile for optimum launch angle θ=45<sup>o</sup>:

$d_{\mathrm{max}} = \frac{v^2}{g} \big/ \left(1-\tfrac12\tilde v^2\right)$ with $v<\sqrt{Rg}$, the [first cosmic velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_cosmic_velocity)

Maximum height of a projectile above the [planetary surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_surface):

$$h = \frac{v^2 \sin^2\theta}{g} \Big/ \left(1-\tilde v^2+\sqrt{1-\left(2-\tilde v^2\right)\tilde v^2\cos^2\theta}\right)$$

Maximum height of a projectile for vertical launch ($\theta=90^\circ$):

$h_{\mathrm{max}} = \frac{v^2}{2g} \big/ \left(1-\tfrac12\tilde v^2\right)$ with $v<\sqrt{2Rg}$, the [second cosmic velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_cosmic_velocity) or [escape velocity](wikipedia://en/37913),

Time of flight:

$$t = \frac{2v\sin\theta}{g} \cdot \frac{1}{2-\tilde v^2} \left(1 + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2-\tilde v^2}\,\tilde v\sin\theta}\arcsin\frac{\sqrt{2-\tilde v^2}\,\tilde v\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1-\left(2-\tilde v^2\right)\tilde v^2\cos^2\theta}}\right)$$
