# Barometric formula

Vertical pressure variation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Barometric formula"}

The **barometric formula** is a [formula](wikipedia://en/164040) used to model how the [air pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_pressure) (or [air density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_density)) changes with [altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude).

:::

## Model equations {#Model_equations}

*Image: Pressure air.svg.* Pressure as a function of the height above the sea level

The [U.S. Standard Atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Standard_Atmosphere) gives two equations for computing pressure as a function of height, valid from sea level to 86 km altitude. The first equation is applicable to the atmospheric layers in which the temperature is assumed to vary with altitude at a non-zero [temperature gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature_gradient) of $L_{M,b}$:[^o3]p. 12

$$P = P_b \cdot \left[ \frac{ T_{M,b} }{ T_{M,b} + L_{M,b} \cdot (H - H_{b}) }\right]^{\frac{g_0' \cdot M_0}{R^* \cdot L_{M,b}}}.$$

The second equation is applicable to the atmospheric layers in which the temperature is assumed not to vary with altitude (zero temperature gradient):[^o3]p. 12

$$P = P_b \cdot \exp \left[\frac{-g_0' \cdot M_0 (H - H_b)}{R^* \cdot T_{M,b}}\right].$$

In both equations:

$P_b$ = reference pressure;

$T_{M,b}$ = reference temperature ([K](wikipedia://en/19593121));

$L_{M,b}$ = temperature gradient (K/m), e.g. -6.5 K/km at sea level (this is the [lapse rate](wikipedia://en/224312) with the opposite sign convention);

$H$ = [geopotential height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geopotential_height) at which pressure is calculated (m);

$H_{b}$ = geopotential height of reference level *b* (meters, e.g., *H<sub>b</sub>* = 11000 m);

$R^*$ = [universal gas constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_gas_constant), taken to be 8.31432 J/(kmol·K)[^o3]p. 3, although the actual constant's value in those units rounds to 8.31446;

$M_0$ = mean molar mass of air at sea level = 28.9644 kg/kmol 1976;[^o3]p. 9

$g_0'$ = the [gravitational acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_gravity) in units of geopotential height = 9.80665 m/s<sup>2</sup>.[^o3]p. 3

Or converted to [imperial units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_units):[^o4]

$P_b$ = reference pressure;

$T_{M,b}$ = reference temperature ([K](wikipedia://en/19593121));

$L_{M,b}$ = temperature gradient (K/ft);

$H$ = height at which pressure is calculated (ft);

$H_b$ = height of reference level *b* (feet, e.g., *H*<sub>b</sub> = 36,089 ft);

$R^*$ = [universal gas constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_gas_constant), using feet, kelvins, and (SI) [moles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_%28unit%29), taken to be roughly e0 lb<sub>m</sub>·ft<sup>2</sup>/(lb<sub>m</sub>-mol·K·s<sup>2</sup>) by correctly converting the (incorrectly) taken constant from metric to imperial;

$g_0'$ = [gravitational acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_gravity) = 9.80665;

$M$ = mean molar mass of Earth's air = 28.9644 (both lbs are [pound-mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound-mass), *not* [pound-force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound-force)).

The value of subscript *b* ranges from 0 to 6 in accordance with each of seven successive layers of the atmosphere shown in the table below. In these equations, *g*<sub>0</sub>, *M* and *R*<sup>\*</sup> are each single-valued constants, while *P*, *L*, *T*, and *H* are multivalued constants in accordance with the table below. The values used for *M*, *g*<sub>0</sub>, and *R*<sup>\*</sup> are in accordance with the [U.S. Standard Atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Standard_Atmosphere), 1976, and the value for *R*<sup>\*</sup> in particular does not agree with standard values for this constant.[^o3]p. 3 The reference value for *P<sub>b</sub>* for *b* = 0 is the defined sea-level value, *P*<sub>0</sub> = 101325 Pa, or 29.92126 inHg. Values of *P<sub>b</sub>* of *b* = 1 through *b* = 6 are obtained from the application of the appropriate member of the pair equations 1 and 2 for the case when *H* = *H*<sub>*b*+1</sub>.[^o3]p. 12

Density can be calculated from pressure and temperature using[^o3]p. 15

$$\rho = \frac{ P \cdot M_0 }{ R^* \cdot T_M } = \frac{ P \cdot M }{ R^* \cdot T },$$

where

$M_0$ is the molecular weight at sea level;

$M$ is the mean molecular weight at the altitude of interest;

$T$ is the temperature at the altitude of interest;

$T_M = T \cdot M_0/M$ is the molecular-scale temperature.[^o3]p. 9

The atmosphere is assumed to be fully mixed up to about 80 km, so $M = M_0$ within the region of validity of the equations presented here.[^o3]p. 9

Alternatively, density equations can be derived in the same form as those for pressure, using reference densities instead of reference pressures.

This model, with its simple linearly segmented temperature profile, does not closely agree with the physically observed atmosphere at altitudes below 20 km. From 51 km to 81 km it is closer to observed conditions.[^o3]p. 1

## Derivation {#Derivation}

The barometric formula can be derived using the [ideal gas law](wikipedia://en/59881):

$$P = \frac{\rho}{M} {R^*} T$$

Assuming that all pressure is [hydrostatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrostatic_pressure):

$$dP = - \rho g\,dz$$

and dividing this equation by $P$ we get:

$$\frac{dP}{P} = - \frac{M g\,dz}{R^*T}$$

[Integrating](wikipedia://en/15532) this expression from the surface to the altitude *z* we get:

$$P = P_0 e^{-\int_{0}^{z}{M g dz/R^*T}}$$

Assuming linear temperature change $T = T_0 - L z$ and constant molar mass and gravitational acceleration, we get the first barometric formula:

$$P = P_0 \cdot \left[\frac{T}{T_0}\right]^{\textstyle \frac{M g}{R^* L}}$$

Instead, assuming constant temperature, integrating gives the second barometric formula:

$$P = P_0 e^{-M g z/R^*T}$$

In this formulation, *R<sup>\*</sup>* is the [gas constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_constant), and the term *R<sup>\*</sup>T*/*Mg* gives the [scale height](wikipedia://en/1994795) (approximately equal to 8.4 km for the [troposphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troposphere)).

The derivation shown above uses a method that relies on classical mechanics. There are several alternative derivations, the most notable are the ones based on thermodynamic forces and statistical mechanics.[^o7]

(For exact results, it should be remembered that atmospheres containing water do not behave as an *ideal gas*. See [real gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_gas) or [perfect gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_gas) or [gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas) for further understanding.)

## Barosphere {#Barosphere}

The barosphere is the region of a planetary atmosphere where the [barometric law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barometric_law) applies. It ranges from the ground to the [thermopause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermopause), also known as the baropause. Above this altitude is the [exosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exosphere), where the atmospheric velocity distribution is [non-Maxwellian](wikipedia://en/19830) due to high velocity atoms and molecules being able to escape the atmosphere.[^o9][^o10]
