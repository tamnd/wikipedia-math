# Subjective expected utility

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Subjective expected utility"}

In [decision theory](wikipedia://en/446216), **subjective expected utility** (SEU) is a framework for modeling how individuals make choices under uncertainty. In particular, it posits that decision-makers have 1) a subjective probability distribution over uncertain states of the world; and 2) a utility function over consequences such that their choice behavior can be described as maximizing [expected utility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_utility) over consequences with respect to their subjective probability.[^o2] This way, the theory of subjective expected utility combines two subjective concepts: a personal [utility](wikipedia://en/45479) function, and a personal [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) distribution (usually based on [Bayesian probability](wikipedia://en/4890) theory).[^o3]

:::

SEU is a different approach from [the one](wikipedia://en/26898094) put forward by [von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) and [Morgenstern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Morgenstern) in that it does not take (objective) probabilities (i.e., lotteries) as given. Instead, subjective probabilities are used, which are assumed to be consistent with choice behavior.[^o4]

The main contribution to formalizing SEU was done by [L. J. Savage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._J._Savage) in 1954 (see [Savage's axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savage's_subjective_expected_utility_model)),[^o5][^o6] following previous work by [Ramsey](wikipedia://en/238285)[^o7] and [von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942).[^o8] Savage proved that, if the decision-maker preferences over acts satisfy some reasonable axioms, then their choices can be explained as arising from a utility function $u(x_i)$ combined with the subjective belief that there is a probability of each outcome $P(x_i).$ The subjective expected utility is the resulting [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) of the utility:

$$\mathrm{E}[u(X)] = \sum_i \; u(x_i) \; P(x_i) .$$

Experiments have shown that many individuals do not behave in a manner consistent with Savage's axioms of subjective expected utility, e.g. most prominently [Allais](wikipedia://en/3223960) (1953)[^o9] and [Ellsberg](wikipedia://en/1912480) (1961).[^o10]
