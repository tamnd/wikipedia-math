# Rational variety

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rational variety"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **rational variety** is an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808), over a given [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K*, which is [birationally equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birationally_equivalent) to a [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) of some dimension over *K*. This means that its [function field](wikipedia://en/2783843) is isomorphic to

:::

$$K(U_1, \dots , U_d),$$

the field of all [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) for some set $\{U_1, \dots, U_d\}$ of [indeterminates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_%28variable%29), where *d* is the [dimension](wikipedia://en/253251) of the variety.

## Rationality and parameterization {#Rationality_and_parameterization}

Let *V* be an [affine algebraic variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_algebraic_variety) of dimension *d* defined by a prime ideal *I* = ⟨*f*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *f*<sub>*k*</sub>⟩ in $K[X_1, \dots , X_n]$. If *V* is rational, then there are *n* + 1 polynomials *g*<sub>0</sub>, ..., *g*<sub>*n*</sub> in $K(U_1, \dots , U_d)$ such that $f_i(g_1/g_0, \ldots, g_n/g_0)=0.$ In other words, we have a **rational parameterization** $x_i=\frac{g_i}{g_0}(u_1,\ldots,u_d)$ of the variety.

Conversely, such a rational parameterization induces a [field homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_homomorphism) of the field of functions of *V* into $K(U_1, \dots , U_d)$. But this homomorphism is not necessarily [onto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onto). If such a parameterization exists, the variety is said to be unirational. Lüroth's theorem (see below) implies that unirational curves are rational. [Castelnuovo's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castelnuovo's_theorem) implies also that, in characteristic zero, every unirational surface is rational.

## Rationality questions {#Rationality_questions}

A **rationality question** asks whether a given [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) is *rational*, in the sense of being (up to isomorphism) the function field of a rational variety; such field extensions are also described as [purely transcendental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purely_transcendental). More precisely, the rationality question for the field extension $K \subset L$ is this: is $L$ [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to a [rational function field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_function_field) over $K$ in the number of indeterminates given by the [transcendence degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_degree)?

There are several different variations of this question, arising from the way in which the fields $K$ and $L$ are constructed.

For example, let $K$ be a field, and let

$$\{y_1, \dots, y_n \}$$

be indeterminates over *K* and let *L* be the field generated over *K* by them. Consider a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) $G$ permuting those [indeterminates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_%28variable%29) over *K*. By standard [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316), the set of [fixed points](wikipedia://en/449738) of this [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) is a [subfield](wikipedia://en/11634) of $L$, typically denoted $L^G$. The rationality question for $K \subset L^G$ is called **Noether's problem** and asks if this field of fixed points is or is not a purely transcendental extension of *K*. In the paper (Noether 1918) on Galois theory she studied the problem of parameterizing the equations with given [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673), which she reduced to "Noether's problem". (She first mentioned this problem in (Noether 1913) where she attributed the problem to E. Fischer.) She showed this was true for *n* = 2, 3, or 4. (txt) found a counter-example to the Noether's problem, with *n* = 47 and *G* a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order 47.

## Lüroth's theorem {#Lüroth's_theorem}

A celebrated case is **Lüroth's problem**, which [Jacob Lüroth](wikipedia://en/33271640) solved in the nineteenth century. Lüroth's problem concerns subextensions *L* of *K*(*X*), the rational functions in the single indeterminate *X*. Any such field is either equal to *K* or is also rational, i.e. *L* = *K*(*F*) for some rational function *F*. In geometrical terms this states that a non-constant [rational map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_map) from the [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578) to a curve *C* can only occur when *C* also has [genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genus_of_a_curve) 0. That fact can be read off geometrically from the [Riemann–Hurwitz formula](wikipedia://en/497413).

## Unirationality {#Unirationality}

A **unirational variety** *V* over a field *K* is one dominated by a rational variety, so that its function field *K*(*V*) lies in a pure transcendental field of finite type (which can be chosen to be of finite degree over *K*(*V*) if *K* is infinite). The solution of Lüroth's problem shows that for algebraic curves, rational and unirational are the same, and [Castelnuovo's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castelnuovo's_theorem) implies that for complex surfaces unirational implies rational, because both are characterized by the vanishing of both the [arithmetic genus](wikipedia://en/1950390) and the second [plurigenus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plurigenus). Zariski found some examples ([Zariski surfaces](wikipedia://en/3096979)) in characteristic *p* > 0 that are unirational but not rational. (Clemens Griffiths 1972) showed that a cubic [three-fold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-fold) is in general not a rational variety, providing an example for three dimensions that unirationality does not imply rationality. Their work used an [intermediate Jacobian](wikipedia://en/3092994). (Iskovskih Manin 1971) showed that all non-singular [quartic threefolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartic_threefold) are irrational, though some of them are unirational. (Artin Mumford 1972) found some unirational 3-folds with non-trivial torsion in their third cohomology group, which implies that they are not rational.

For any field *K*, [János Kollár](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/János_Kollár) proved in 2000 that a smooth [cubic hypersurface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_hypersurface) of dimension at least 2 is unirational if it has a point defined over *K*. This is an improvement of many classical results, beginning with the case of [cubic surfaces](wikipedia://en/526126) (which are rational varieties over an [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129)). Other examples of varieties that are shown to be unirational are many cases of the [moduli space](wikipedia://en/361609) of curves.[^o14]

## Rationally connected variety {#Rationally_connected_variety}

A **rationally connected variety** *V* is a [projective algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808#Projective_variety) over an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) such that through every two points there passes the image of a [regular map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_map_%28algebraic_geometry%29) from the [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578) into *V*. Equivalently, a variety is rationally connected if every two points are connected by a [rational curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_curve) contained in the variety.[^o17]

This definition differs from that of [path connectedness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_connectedness) only by the nature of the path, but is very different, as the only algebraic curves which are rationally connected are the rational ones.

Every rational variety, including the [projective spaces](wikipedia://en/242135), is rationally connected, but the converse is false. The class of the rationally connected varieties is thus a generalization of the class of the rational varieties. Unirational varieties are rationally connected, but it is not known if the converse holds.

:::

::: {#o19 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Stably rational"}

## Stably rational varieties {#Stably_rational_varieties}

A variety *V* is called *stably rational* if $V \times \mathbf P^m$ is rational for some $m \ge 0$. Any rational variety is thus, by definition, stably rational. Examples constructed by (Beauville Colliot-Thélène Sansuc Swinnerton-Dyer 1985) show, that the converse is false however.

(Schreieder 2019) showed that very general [hypersurfaces](wikipedia://en/525101) $V \subset \mathbf P^{N+1}$ are not stably rational, provided that the [degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_%28algebraic_geometry%29) of *V* is at least $\log_2 N+2$.
