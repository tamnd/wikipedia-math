# Mean absolute error

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mean absolute error"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), **mean absolute error** (**MAE**) is a measure of [errors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_%28statistics%29) between paired observations expressing the same phenomenon. Examples of *Y* versus *X* include comparisons of predicted versus observed, subsequent time versus initial time, and one technique of measurement versus an alternative technique of measurement. MAE is calculated as the **sum of absolute errors** (i.e., the [Manhattan distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_distance)) divided by the [sample size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_size):[^o2]

$$\mathrm{MAE} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n\left| y_i - x_i\right|}{n} =\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n\left| e_i \right|}{n}.$$

It is thus the arithmetic mean of the absolute errors $|e_i| = |y_i - x_i|$, where $y_i$ is the prediction and $x_i$ the true value. Alternative formulations may include relative frequencies as weight factors. The mean absolute error uses the same scale as the data being measured. This is known as a scale-dependent accuracy measure and therefore cannot be used to make comparisons between predicted values that use different scales.[^o3] The mean absolute error is a common measure of [forecast error](wikipedia://en/4415165) in [time series analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_series_analysis),[^o15] sometimes used in confusion with the more standard definition of [mean absolute deviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_absolute_deviation). The same confusion exists more generally in technical literature.

:::

## Quantity disagreement and allocation disagreement {#Quantity_disagreement_and_allocation_disagreement}

*Image: MAE\_example.png.* Two data points for which quantity disagreement is 0 and allocation disagreement is 2 for both MAE and RMSE.

In [remote sensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_sensing) the MAE is sometimes expressed as the sum of two components: quantity disagreement and allocation disagreement. Quantity disagreement is the absolute value of the mean error:[^o5]

$$\left|\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n y_i-x_i}{n}\right|.$$

Allocation disagreement is MAE minus quantity disagreement.

It is also possible to identify the types of difference by looking at an $(x,y)$ plot. Quantity difference exists when the average of the X values does not equal the average of the Y values. Allocation difference exists if and only if points reside on both sides of the identity (Y=X) line.[^o5][^o6]

## Related measures {#Related_measures}

The mean absolute error is one of a number of ways of comparing forecasts with their eventual outcomes. Well-established alternatives are the [mean absolute scaled error](wikipedia://en/31360933) (MASE), mean absolute log error (MALE), and the [mean squared error](wikipedia://en/201816). These all summarize performance in ways that disregard the direction of over- or under- prediction; a measure that does place emphasis on this is the [mean signed difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_signed_difference).

Where a prediction model is to be fitted using a selected performance measure, in the sense that the [least squares](wikipedia://en/82359) approach is related to the [mean squared error](wikipedia://en/201816), the equivalent for mean absolute error is [least absolute deviations](wikipedia://en/19048902).

MAE is not identical to [root-mean square error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root-mean-square_deviation) (RMSE), although some researchers report and interpret it that way. The MAE is conceptually simpler and also easier to interpret than RMSE: it is simply the average absolute vertical or horizontal distance between each point in a scatter plot and the Y=X line. In other words, MAE is the average absolute difference between X and Y. Furthermore, each error contributes to MAE in proportion to the absolute value of the error. This is in contrast to RMSE which involves squaring the differences, so that a few large differences will increase the RMSE to a greater degree than the MAE.[^o5]

### Optimality property {#Optimality_property}

The *mean absolute error* of a real variable *c* with respect to the [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) *X* is

$$E(\left|X-c\right|).$$

Provided that the probability distribution of *X* is such that the above expectation exists, then *m* is a [median](wikipedia://en/18837) of *X* if and only if *m* is a minimizer of the mean absolute error with respect to *X*.[^o9] In particular, *m* is a sample median if and only if *m* minimizes the arithmetic mean of the absolute deviations.[^o10]

More generally, a median is defined as a minimum of

$$E(|X-c| - |X| ),$$

as discussed at [Multivariate median](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_median) (and specifically at [Spatial median](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatial_median)). This optimization-based definition of the median is useful in statistical data-analysis, for example, in [*k*-medians clustering](wikipedia://en/12069242).

::: {#o12 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of optimality"}

#### Proof of optimality {#Proof_of_optimality}

Statement: The classifier minimising $\mathbb{E}|y-\hat{y}|$ is $\hat{f}(x)=\text{Median}(y|X=x)$ .

Proof:

The [Loss functions for classification](wikipedia://en/44578554) is

$$\begin{aligned} L &= \mathbb{E}[|y-a||X=x]\\ &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |y-a| f_{Y|X}(y) \, dy \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^a (a-y)f_{Y|X}(y)\, dy+\int_a^{\infty}(y-a)f_{Y|X}(y)\, dy.\\ \end{aligned}$$

Differentiating with respect to $a$ gives

$$\frac{\partial }{\partial a}L = \int_{-\infty}^af_{Y|X}(y)\, dy+\int_a^{\infty}-f_{Y|X}(y)\, dy=0 .$$

This means

$$\int_{-\infty}^a f(y)\, dy = \int_a^{\infty} f(y)\, dy .$$

Hence,

$$F_{Y|X}(a)=0.5 .$$

:::
