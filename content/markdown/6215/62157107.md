# Glossary of Lie groups and Lie algebras

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Glossary of Lie groups and Lie algebras"}

This is a [glossary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary) for the terminology applied in the [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical) theories of [Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_groups) and [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944). For the topics in the representation theory of Lie groups and Lie algebras, see [Glossary of representation theory](wikipedia://en/55799101). Because of the lack of other options, the glossary also includes some generalizations such as [quantum group](wikipedia://en/340678).

:::

**Notations**:

- Throughout the glossary, $( \cdot, \cdot )$ denotes the [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) of a Euclidean space *E* and $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ denotes the rescaled inner product

$$\langle \beta, \alpha \rangle = \frac{(\beta, \alpha)}{(\alpha, \alpha)} \, \forall \alpha, \beta \in E.$$

## A {#A}

::: {#o3 .entry cue=term name="abelian"}

**abelian**

An [abelian Lie group](wikipedia://en/62891333) is a Lie group that is an abelian group.

An [abelian Lie algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_Lie_algebra) is a Lie algebra such that $[x, y] = 0$ for every $x, y$ in the algebra.

:::

::: {#o4 .entry cue=term name="adjoint"}

**adjoint**

An [adjoint representation of a Lie group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_representation_of_a_Lie_group):

$$\operatorname{Ad} : G \to \operatorname{GL}(\mathfrak g)$$

such that $\operatorname{Ad}(g)$ is the differential at the identity element of the conjugation $c_g : G \to G, x \mapsto g x g^{-1}$.

An [adjoint representation of a Lie algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_representation_of_a_Lie_algebra) is a Lie algebra representation

$\textrm{ad}: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$ where $\textrm{ad}(x)y = [x, y]$.

:::

::: {#o5 .entry cue=term name="Ado"}

**Ado**

[Ado's theorem](wikipedia://en/3422249): Any finite-dimensional Lie algebra is isomorphic to a subalgebra of $\mathfrak{gl}_V$ for some finite-dimensional vector space V.

:::

::: {#o6 .entry cue=term name="affine"}

**affine**

An [affine Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/1350865) is a particular type of Kac–Moody algebra.

An [affine Weyl group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_Weyl_group).

:::

::: {#o7 .entry cue=term name="analytic"}

**analytic**

An [analytic subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_subgroup)

:::

::: {#o8 .entry cue=term name="automorphism"}

**automorphism**

An [automorphism of a Lie algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automorphism_of_a_Lie_algebra) is a linear automorphism preserving the bracket.

## C {#C}

::: {#o14 .entry cue=term name="Cartan"}

**Cartan**

[Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) (1869 – 1951), a French mathematician

A [Cartan subalgebra](wikipedia://en/1336000) $\mathfrak{h}$ of a Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is a nilpotent subalgebra satisfying $N_\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{h}) = \mathfrak{h}$.

[Cartan criterion for solvability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartan_criterion_for_solvability): A Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is solvable [iff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iff) $\kappa( \mathfrak{g}, [\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}] ) = 0$.

[Cartan criterion for semisimplicity](wikipedia://en/3052668#Cartan's_criterion_for_semisimplicity): (1) If $\kappa( \cdot, \cdot)$ is nondegenerate, then $\mathfrak{g}$ is semisimple. (2) If $\mathfrak{g}$ is semisimple and the underlying field $F$ has characteristic 0 , then $\kappa( \cdot, \cdot)$ is nondegenerate.

The [Cartan matrix](wikipedia://en/1146433) of the root system $\Phi$ is the matrix $( \langle \alpha_i , \alpha_j \rangle )_{i,j=1}^n$, where $\Delta = \{\alpha_1 \ldots \alpha_n\}$ is a set of simple roots of $\Phi$.

[Cartan subgroup](wikipedia://en/4838153)

[Cartan decomposition](wikipedia://en/1139145)

**center**

::: {#o18 .entry cue=term name="center"}

**center**

The center of a Lie group is the [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_a_group) of the group.

The center of a Lie algebra is the centralizer of itself : $Z(L) := \{x \in \mathfrak{g} | [x, \mathfrak{g}] = 0 \}$

:::

::: {#o19 .entry cue=term name="central series"}

**central series**

A [descending central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descending_central_series) (or lower central series) is a sequence of ideals of a Lie algebra $L$ defined by $C^0(L) = L , \, C^1(L) = [L,L] , \, C^{n+1}(L) = [L, C^n(L)]$

An [ascending central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascending_central_series) (or upper central series) is a sequence of ideals of a Lie algebra $L$ defined by $C_0(L) = \{0\} , \, C_1(L) = Z(L)$ (center of L) , $C_{n+1}(L) = \pi_n^{-1} ( Z ( L / C_{n}(L) ) )$, where $\pi_i$ is the natural homomorphism $L \to L/C_n(L)$

**Chevalley**

[Claude Chevalley](wikipedia://en/366040) (1909 – 1984), a French mathematician

Chevalley used these bases to construct analogues of [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945) over [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615), called [Chevalley groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevalley_group).

::: {#o25 .entry cue=term name="derived algebra"}

**derived algebra**

The [derived algebra of a Lie algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_algebra_of_a_Lie_algebra) $\mathfrak{g}$ is $[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g} ]$. It is a subalgebra (in fact an ideal).

A derived series is a sequence of ideals of a Lie algebra $\mathfrak g$ obtained by repeatedly taking derived algebras; i.e., $D^0 \mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{g}, D^n \mathfrak{g} = D^{n-1}\mathfrak{g}$.

::: {#o56 .entry cue=term name="Lie"}

**Lie**

*Image: Portrett av Sophus Lie.jpg.* Sophus Lie

[Sophus Lie](wikipedia://en/54010) (1842 – 1899), a [Norwegian mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_mathematician)

A [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) is a group that has a compatible structure of a smooth manifold.

A [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) is a vector space $\mathfrak{g}$ over a field $F$ with a binary operation \[·, ·\] (called the **Lie bracket** or abbr. **bracket**) , which satisfies the following conditions: $\forall a,b \in F, x,y,z \in \mathfrak{g}$,

$[x,x] = 0$ ([alternating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_map))

$[[x,y], z ] + [[y,z],x] + [[z,x],y] = 0$ ([Jacobi identity](wikipedia://en/294370))

[Lie group–Lie algebra correspondence](wikipedia://en/43302095)

[Lie's theorem](wikipedia://en/2975251)

Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be a finite-dimensional complex [solvable Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/2966308) over [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) of characteristic $0$, and let $V$ be a nonzero finite dimensional [representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representation_of_Lie_algebras) of $\mathfrak{g}$. Then there exists an element of $V$ which is a simultaneous [eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) for all elements of $\mathfrak{g}$.

::: {#o75 .entry cue=term name="regular"}

**regular**

A [regular element of a Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/31861096).

A regular element with respect to a root system.

Let $\Phi$ be a root system. $\gamma \in E$ is called regular if $(\gamma, \alpha) \ne 0 \, \forall \gamma \in \Phi$.

For each set of simple roots $\Delta$ of $\Phi$, there exists a regular element $\gamma \in E$ such that $( \gamma, \alpha ) > 0 \, \forall \gamma \in \Delta$, conversely for each regular $\gamma$ there exist a unique set of base roots $\Delta(\gamma)$ such that the previous condition holds for $\Delta = \Delta(\gamma)$. It can be determined in following way: let $\Phi^+(\gamma) = \{\alpha \in \Phi | (\alpha, \gamma) > 0\}$. Call an element $\alpha$ of $\Phi^+(\gamma)$ decomposable if $\alpha = \alpha' + \alpha''$ where $\alpha', \alpha'' \in \Phi^+(\gamma)$, then $\Delta(\gamma)$ is the set of all indecomposable elements of $\Phi^+(\gamma)$

::: {#o76 .entry cue=term name="root"}

**root**

[root of a semisimple Lie algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_semisimple_Lie_algebra):

Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be a semisimple Lie algebra, $\mathfrak{h}$ be a Cartan subalgebra of $\mathfrak{g}$. For $\alpha \in \mathfrak{h}^*$, let $\mathfrak{g_\alpha} := \{ x \in \mathfrak{g} | [h,x] = \alpha(h) x \, \forall h \in \mathfrak{h} \}$. $\alpha$ is called a root of $\mathfrak{g}$ if it is nonzero and $\mathfrak{g_\alpha} \ne \{0\}$

The set of all roots is denoted by $\Phi$ ; it forms a root system.

[Root system](wikipedia://en/277087)

A subset $\Phi$ of the Euclidean space $E$ is called a root system if it satisfies the following conditions:

$\Phi$ is finite, $\textrm{span} (\Phi) = E$ and $0 \notin \Phi$.

For all $\alpha \in \Phi$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}$, $c \alpha \in \Phi$ iff $c = \pm 1$.

For all $\alpha,\beta \in \Phi$, $\langle \alpha, \beta \rangle$ is an integer.

For all $\alpha,\beta \in \Phi$, $S_\alpha(\beta)\in \Phi$, where $S_\alpha$ is the reflection through the hyperplane normal to $\alpha$, i.e. $S_\alpha(x) = x - \langle x , \alpha \rangle \alpha$.

[Root datum](wikipedia://en/6825924)

Positive root of root system $\Phi$ with respect to a set of simple roots $\Delta$ is a root of $\Phi$ which is a linear combination of elements of $\Delta$ with nonnegative coefficients.

Negative root of root system $\Phi$ with respect to a set of simple roots $\Delta$ is a root of $\Phi$ which is a linear combination of elements of $\Delta$ with nonpositive coefficients.

long root

short root

inverse of a root system: Given a root system $\Phi$. Define $\alpha^v = \frac{2 \alpha}{ ( \alpha, \alpha) }$, $\Phi^v = \{ \alpha^v | \alpha \in \Phi \}$ is called the inverse of a root system.

$\Phi^v$ is again a root system and have the identical Weyl group as $\Phi$.

base of a root system: synonymous to "set of simple roots"

dual of a root system: synonymous to "inverse of a root system"

[Serre's theorem](wikipedia://en/62858445) states that, given a (finite reduced) root system $\Phi$, there exists a unique (up to a choice of a base) semisimple Lie algebra whose root system is $\Phi$.

**simple**

A [simple Lie group](wikipedia://en/292831) is a connected Lie group that is not abelian which does not have nontrivial connected normal subgroups.

A [simple Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/59500649) is a Lie algebra that is non abelian and has only two ideals, itself and $\{0\}$.

[simply laced group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_laced_group) (a simple Lie group is simply laced when its Dynkin diagram is without multiple edges).

[simple root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_root_%28root_system%29). A subset $\Delta$ of a root system $\Phi$ is called a set of simple roots if it satisfies the following conditions:

$\Delta$ is a linear basis of $E$.

Each element of $\Phi$ is a linear combination of elements of $\Delta$ with coefficients that are either all nonnegative or all nonpositive.

**subalgebra**

A subspace $\mathfrak{g'}$ of a Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is called the subalgebra of $\mathfrak{g}$ if it is closed under bracket, i.e. $[\mathfrak{g'}, \mathfrak{g'}] \subseteq \mathfrak{g'}.$

## W {#W}

::: {#o102 .entry cue=term name="Weyl"}

**Weyl**

[Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) (1885 – 1955), a German mathematician

A [Weyl chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_chamber) is one of the connected components of the complement in *V*, a real vector space on which a root system is defined, when the hyperplanes orthogonal to the root vectors are removed.

The [Weyl character formula](wikipedia://en/5011916) gives in closed form the characters of the irreducible complex representations of the simple Lie groups.

[Weyl group](wikipedia://en/296332): Weyl group of a root system $\Phi$ is a (necessarily finite) group of orthogonal linear transformations of $E$ which is generated by reflections through hyperplanes normal to roots of $\Phi$

:::
