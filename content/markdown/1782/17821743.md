# Heroic theory of invention and scientific development

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Heroic theory of invention and scientific development"}

The **heroic theory of invention and scientific development** is the view that the principal authors of [inventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invention) and [scientific discoveries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_discoveries) are unique [heroic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hero) individuals—i.e., "great scientists" or "geniuses".[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Competing hypothesis"}

## Competing hypothesis {#Competing_hypothesis}

A competing hypothesis (that of *[multiple discovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_discovery)*) is that most inventions and scientific discoveries are made independently and simultaneously by multiple inventors and scientists.

The multiple-discovery hypothesis may be most patently exemplified in the evolution of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), since mathematical knowledge is highly unified and any advances need, as a general rule, to be built from previously established results through a process of deduction. Thus, the development of [infinitesimal calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal_calculus) into a systematic discipline did not occur until the development of [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202), the former being credited to both [Sir Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Isaac_Newton) and [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz), and the latter to both [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) and [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966).

:::
