# Banesh Hoffmann

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Banesh Hoffmann"}

**Banesh Hoffmann** (6 September 1906 – 5 August 1986) was a British [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist) known for his association with [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736).[^o19]

:::

## Life {#Life}

Banesh Hoffmann was born in [Richmond, Surrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richmond,_Surrey), on 6 September 1906, to Maurice Hoffmann, a tailor, and Leah (née Brozel), immigrants from the [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire).[^o4] He studied mathematics and [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028) at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford), where he earned a [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts) degree and went on to earn his doctorate at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University).[^o5]

While at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) in Princeton, Hoffmann collaborated with Einstein and [Leopold Infeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_Infeld) on the classic paper *Gravitational Equations and the Problem of Motion.* Einstein's original work on [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) was based on two ideas. The first was the equation of motion: that a particle would follow the shortest path in four-dimensions [space-time](wikipedia://en/28758). The second was how matter affects the geometry of space-time. What Einstein, Infeld, and Hoffmann showed was that the [equation of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equation_of_motion) followed directly from the field equation that defined the geometry.

In 1937, Hoffmann joined the mathematics department of [Queens College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queens_College), part of the [City University of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_University_of_New_York), where he remained until the late 1970s. He retired in the 1960s but continued to teach — in the fall a course on classical and quantum mechanics and an advanced math course for students who had taken pre-calculus, solid geometry and advanced algebra before entering college. This course was one semester and was called Math 3: the fusion of the year-long Math 1 and Math 2 courses required by Queens College but offered in a pressurized one-semester course. In the spring he taught the special and general theories of relativity.

In July 1938 he married Doris Marjorie Goodday in New York City. They had a son (Laurence) and a daughter ([Deborah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deborah_Hoffmann)).[^o19]

Hoffmann died on 5 August 1986. One of the Queens College mathematics department's awards for graduating seniors is named in his honor.[^o6]

## Works {#Works}

Hoffmann became Einstein's biographer in 1972 when he co-authored *[Albert Einstein: Creator and Rebel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein:_Creator_and_Rebel)* with Einstein's secretary, [Helen Dukas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_Dukas). The pair collaborated again in compiling *Albert Einstein: The Human Side*, a collection of quotations from Einstein's letters and other personal papers.[^o8][^o9]

Hoffmann was also the author of *The Strange Story of the Quantum*,[^o10] *About Vectors*,[^o11][^o12] *Relativity and Its Roots*,[^o13][^o14] and *The Tyranny of Testing*. He was a member of [The Baker Street Irregulars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Baker_Street_Irregulars) and wrote the short story "Sherlock, Shakespeare, and the Bomb," published in *[Ellery Queen's Mystery Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellery_Queen's_Mystery_Magazine)* in February 1966.[^o15]
