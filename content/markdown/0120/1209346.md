# Bicyclic semigroup

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bicyclic semigroup"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **bicyclic semigroup** is an algebraic object important for the structure theory of [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799). Although it is in fact a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652), it is usually referred to as simply a semigroup. It is perhaps most easily understood as the [syntactic monoid](wikipedia://en/670026) describing the [Dyck language](wikipedia://en/1852078) of balanced pairs of parentheses. Thus, it finds common applications in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170), such as describing [binary trees](wikipedia://en/4321) and [associative algebras](wikipedia://en/2112).

:::

## History {#History}

The first published description of this object was given by [Evgenii Lyapin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evgenii_Lyapin) in 1953. [Alfred H. Clifford](wikipedia://en/22683347) and [Gordon Preston](wikipedia://en/22694802) claim that one of them, working with [David Rees](wikipedia://en/15398568), discovered it independently (without publication) at some point before 1943.

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

There are at least three standard ways of constructing the bicyclic semigroup, and various notations for referring to it. Lyapin called it *P*; Clifford and Preston used $\mathcal{C}$; and most recent papers have tended to use *B*. This article will use the modern style throughout.

### From a free semigroup {#From_a_free_semigroup}

The bicyclic semigroup is the quotient of the [free monoid](wikipedia://en/523166) on two generators *p* and *q* by the [congruence](wikipedia://en/48167) generated by the relation *p* *q* = 1. Thus, each semigroup element is a string of those two letters, with the proviso that the [subsequence](wikipedia://en/236098) "*p* *q*" does not appear. The semigroup operation is concatenation of strings, which is clearly [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative). It can then be shown that all elements of *B* in fact have the form *q*<sup>*a*</sup> *p*<sup>*b*</sup>, for some [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) *a* and *b*. The composition operation simplifies to

(*q*<sup>*a*</sup> *p*<sup>*b*</sup>) (*q*<sup>*c*</sup> *p*<sup>*d*</sup>) = *q*<sup>*a*+*c*−min{*b*,*c*}</sup> *p*<sup>*d*+*b*−min{*b*,*c*}</sup>.

### From ordered pairs {#From_ordered_pairs}

The way in which these exponents are constrained suggests that the "*p* and *q* structure" can be discarded, leaving only operations on the "*a* and *b*" part. So *B* is the semigroup of pairs of natural numbers (including zero), with operation[^n1]

(*a*, *b*) (*c*, *d*) = (*a* + *c* − min{*b*, *c*}, *d* + *b* − min{*b*, *c*}).

This is sufficient to define *B* so that it is the same object as in the original construction. Just as *p* and *q* generated *B* originally, with the empty string as the monoid identity, this new construction of *B* has generators (1, 0) and (0, 1), with identity (0, 0).

### From functions {#From_functions}

It can be shown that *any* semigroup *S* generated by elements *e*, *a*, and *b* that satisfies the statements below is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to the bicyclic semigroup.

- *a* *e* = *e* *a* = *a*
- *b* *e* = *e* *b* = *b*
- *a* *b* = *e*
- *b* *a* ≠ *e*

It is not entirely obvious that this should be the case – perhaps the hardest task is understanding that *S* must be infinite. To see this, suppose that *a* (say) does not have infinite order, so *a*<sup>*k*+*h*</sup> = *a*<sup>*h*</sup> for some *h* and *k*. Then *a*<sup>*k*</sup> = *e*, and

*b* = *e* *b* = *a*<sup>*k*</sup> *b* = *a*<sup>*k*−1</sup> *e* = *a*<sup>*k*−1</sup>,

so

*b* *a* = *a*<sup>*k*</sup> = *e*,

which is not allowed – so there are infinitely many distinct powers of *a*. The full proof is given in Clifford and Preston's book.

Note that the two definitions given above both satisfy these properties. A third way of deriving *B* uses two appropriately-chosen functions to yield the bicyclic semigroup as a monoid of transformations of the natural numbers. Let *α*, *β*, and *ι* be elements of the [transformation semigroup](wikipedia://en/19313303) on the natural numbers, where

- *ι*(*n*) = *n*
- *α*(*n*) = *n* + 1
- *β*(*n*) = 0 if *n* = 0, and *n* − 1 otherwise.

These three functions have the required properties, so the semigroup they generate is *B*.[^o9]

## Properties {#Properties}

The bicyclic semigroup has the property that the image of any [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) *φ* from *B* to another semigroup *S* is either [cyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_semigroup), or it is an isomorphic copy of *B*. The elements *φ*(*a*), *φ*(*b*) and *φ*(*e*) of *S* will always satisfy the conditions above (because *φ* is a homomorphism) with the possible exception that *φ*(*b*) *φ*(*a*) might turn out to be φ(*e*). If this is not true, then *φ*(*B*) is isomorphic to *B*; otherwise, it is the cyclic semigroup generated by *φ*(*a*). In practice, this means that the bicyclic semigroup can be found in many different contexts.

The [idempotents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent) of *B* are all pairs (*x*, *x*), where *x* is any natural number (using the [ordered pair](wikipedia://en/22362) characterisation of *B*). Since these commute, and *B* is *regular* (for every *x* there is a *y* such that *x* *y* *x* = *x*), the bicyclic semigroup is an [inverse semigroup](wikipedia://en/1757814). (This means that each element *x* of *B* has a unique inverse *y*, in the "weak" semigroup sense that *x* *y* *x* = *x* and *y* *x* *y* = *y*.)

Every [ideal](wikipedia://en/27799#Subsemigroups_and_ideals) of *B* is principal: the left and right principal ideals of (*m*, *n*) are

- (*m*, *n*) *B* = {(*s*, *t*) : *s* ≥ *m*} and
- *B* (*m*, *n*) = {(*s*, *t*) : *t* ≥ *n*}.

Each of these contains infinitely many others, so *B* does not have minimal left or right ideals.

In terms of [Green's relations](wikipedia://en/1192012), *B* has only one *D*-class (it is *bisimple*), and hence has only one *J*-class (it is *simple*). The *L* and *R* relations are given by

- (*a*, *b*) *R* (*c*, *d*) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) *a* = *c*; and
- (*a*, *b*) *L* (*c*, *d*) if and only if *b* = *d*.[^o12]

This implies that two elements are *H*-related if and only if they are identical. Consequently, the only subgroups of *B* are infinitely many copies of the trivial group, each corresponding to one of the idempotents.

The [egg-box diagram](wikipedia://en/1192012) for *B* is infinitely large; the upper left corner begins:

{| cellpadding=4

\| (0, 0) || (1, 0) || (2, 0) || ... |- | (0, 1) || (1, 1) || (2, 1) || ... |- | (0, 2) || (1, 2) || (2, 2) || ... |- | ... || ... || ... || ... |} Each entry represents a singleton *H*-class; the rows are the *R*-classes and the columns are *L*-classes. The idempotents of *B* appear down the diagonal, in accordance with the fact that in a [regular semigroup](wikipedia://en/6797677) with commuting idempotents, each *L*-class and each *R*-class must contain exactly one idempotent.

The bicyclic semigroup is the "simplest" example of a bisimple inverse semigroup with an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962); there are many others. Where the definition of *B* from ordered pairs used the class of natural numbers (which is not only an additive semigroup, but also a commutative [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) under min and max operations), another set with appropriate properties could appear instead, and the "+", "−" and "max" operations modified accordingly.

The bicyclic semigroup satisfies the [identity](wikipedia://en/298428#Logic_and_universal_algebra) $xyyxxyxyyx=xyyxyxxyyx$ for any $x,y$ in $B$. Known as Adjan's identity (or Adian's identity), it is the shortest length identity satisfied by $B$.[^o13]
