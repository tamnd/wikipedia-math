# Spherical 3-manifold

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Spherical 3-manifold"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **spherical 3-manifold** *M* is a [3-manifold](wikipedia://en/1018257) of the form

:::

$$M=S^3/\Gamma$$

where $\Gamma$ is a [finite](wikipedia://en/323707) [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of [O(4)](wikipedia://en/173954) [acting freely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) by rotations on the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792) $S^3$. All such manifolds are [prime](wikipedia://en/19045780), [orientable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientable), and [closed](wikipedia://en/669475). Spherical 3-manifolds are sometimes called **elliptic 3-manifolds**.

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

A special case of the [Bonnet–Myers theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonnet–Myers_theorem) says that every [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) which has a smooth [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) which is both [geodesically complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesically_complete) and of [constant positive curvature](wikipedia://en/3638803) must be [closed](wikipedia://en/669475) and have finite [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004). [William Thurston](wikipedia://en/39190)'s [elliptization conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptization_conjecture), proven by [Grigori Perelman](wikipedia://en/225266) using [Richard Hamilton](wikipedia://en/758911)'s [Ricci flow](wikipedia://en/288291), states a converse: every closed three-dimensional manifold with finite fundamental group has a smooth Riemannian metric of constant positive curvature. (This converse is special to three dimensions.) As such, the spherical three-manifolds are precisely the closed 3-manifolds with finite fundamental group.

According to [Synge's theorem](wikipedia://en/23524235), every spherical 3-manifold is [orientable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientable), and in particular $\Gamma$ must be included in [SO(4)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SO%284%29). The fundamental group is either [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327), or is a central extension of a [dihedral](wikipedia://en/100267), [tetrahedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedral_group), [octahedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octahedral_group), or [icosahedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icosahedral_group) group by a cyclic group of even order. This divides the set of such manifolds into five classes, described in the following sections.

The spherical manifolds are exactly the manifolds with spherical geometry, one of the eight geometries of Thurston's [geometrization conjecture](wikipedia://en/220642).

:::

## Cyclic case (lens spaces) {#Cyclic_case_(lens_spaces)}

The manifolds $S^3/\Gamma$ with Γ [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327) are precisely the 3-dimensional [lens spaces](wikipedia://en/885725). A lens space is not determined by its fundamental group (there are non-[homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) lens spaces with [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) fundamental groups); but any other spherical manifold is.

Three-dimensional lens spaces arise as quotients of $S^3 \subset \mathbb{C}^2$ by the action of the group that is generated by elements of the form

$$\begin{pmatrix}\omega &0\\0&\omega^q\end{pmatrix}.$$

where $\omega=e^{2\pi i/p}$. Such a lens space $L(p;q)$ has fundamental group $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for all $q$, so spaces with different $p$ are not homotopy equivalent. Moreover, classifications up to homeomorphism and homotopy equivalence are known, as follows. The three-dimensional spaces $L(p;q_1)$ and $L(p;q_2)$ are:

1. homotopy equivalent if and only if $q_1 q_2 \equiv \pm n^2 \pmod{p}$ for some $n \in \mathbb{N};$
1. homeomorphic if and only if $q_1 \equiv \pm q_2^{\pm1} \pmod{p}.$

In particular, the lens spaces *L*(7,1) and *L*(7,2) give examples of two 3-manifolds that are homotopy equivalent but not homeomorphic.

The lens space *L*(1,0) is the 3-sphere, and the lens space *L*(2,1) is 3 dimensional real projective space.

Lens spaces can be represented as [Seifert fiber spaces](wikipedia://en/1631931) in many ways, usually as fiber spaces over the 2-sphere with at most two exceptional fibers, though the lens space with fundamental group of order 4 also has a representation as a Seifert fiber space over the projective plane with no exceptional fibers.

## Dihedral case (prism manifolds) {#Dihedral_case_(prism_manifolds)}

A **prism manifold** is a closed [3-dimensional manifold](wikipedia://en/1018257) *M* whose fundamental group is a central extension of a dihedral group.

The fundamental group π<sub>1</sub>(*M*) of *M* is a product of a cyclic group of order *m* with a group having presentation

$$\langle x,y\mid xyx^{-1}=y^{-1}, x^{2^k}=y^n\rangle$$

for integers *k*, *m*, *n* with *k* ≥ 1, *m* ≥ 1, *n* ≥ 2 and *m* coprime to 2*n*.

Alternatively, the fundamental group has presentation

$$\langle x,y \mid xyx^{-1}=y^{-1}, x^{2m}=y^n\rangle$$

for coprime integers *m*, *n* with *m* ≥ 1, *n* ≥ 2. (The *n* here equals the previous *n*, and the *m* here is 2<sup>*k*-1</sup> times the previous *m*.)

We continue with the latter presentation. This group is a [metacyclic group](wikipedia://en/7515561) of order 4*mn* with [abelianization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelianization) of order 4*m* (so *m* and *n* are both determined by this group). The element *y* generates a [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327) [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of order 2*n*, and the element *x* has order 4*m*. The [center](wikipedia://en/7125) is cyclic of order 2*m* and is generated by *x*<sup>2</sup>, and the quotient by the center is the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) of order 2*n*.

When *m* = 1 this group is a binary dihedral or [dicyclic group](wikipedia://en/154963). The simplest example is *m* = 1, *n* = 2, when π<sub>1</sub>(*M*) is the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130) of order 8.

Prism manifolds are uniquely determined by their fundamental groups: if a closed 3-manifold has the same fundamental group as a prism manifold *M*, it is [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) to *M*.

Prism manifolds can be represented as [Seifert fiber spaces](wikipedia://en/1631931) in two ways.

## Tetrahedral case {#Tetrahedral_case}

The fundamental group is a product of a cyclic group of order *m* with a group having presentation

$$\langle x,y,z \mid (xy)^2=x^2=y^2, zxz^{-1}=y,zyz^{-1}=xy, z^{3^k}=1\rangle$$

for integers *k*, *m* with *k* ≥ 1, *m* ≥ 1 and *m* coprime to 6.

Alternatively, the fundamental group has presentation

$$\langle x,y,z \mid (xy)^2=x^2=y^2, zxz^{-1}=y,zyz^{-1}=xy, z^{3m}=1\rangle$$

for an odd integer *m* ≥ 1. (The *m* here is 3<sup>*k*-1</sup> times the previous *m*.)

We continue with the latter presentation. This group has order 24*m*. The elements *x* and *y* generate a normal subgroup isomorphic to the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130) of order 8. The [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_%28geometry%29) is cyclic of order 2*m*. It is generated by the elements *z*<sup>3</sup> and *x*<sup>2</sup> = *y*<sup>2</sup>, and the quotient by the center is the tetrahedral group, equivalently, the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) *A*<sub>4</sub>.

When *m* = 1 this group is the [binary tetrahedral group](wikipedia://en/10997586).

These manifolds are uniquely determined by their fundamental groups. They can all be represented in an essentially unique way as [Seifert fiber spaces](wikipedia://en/1631931): the quotient manifold is a sphere and there are 3 exceptional fibers of orders 2, 3, and 3.

## Octahedral case {#Octahedral_case}

The fundamental group is a product of a cyclic group of order *m* coprime to 6 with the [binary octahedral group](wikipedia://en/10997598) (of order 48) which has the presentation

$$\langle x,y \mid (xy)^2=x^3=y^4\rangle.$$

These manifolds are uniquely determined by their fundamental groups. They can all be represented in an essentially unique way as [Seifert fiber spaces](wikipedia://en/1631931): the quotient manifold is a sphere and there are 3 exceptional fibers of orders 2, 3, and 4.

## Icosahedral case {#Icosahedral_case}

The fundamental group is a product of a cyclic group of order *m* coprime to 30 with the [binary icosahedral group](wikipedia://en/3195145) (order 120) which has the presentation

$$\langle x,y \mid (xy)^2=x^3=y^5\rangle.$$

When *m* is 1, the manifold is the [Poincaré homology sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_homology_sphere).

These manifolds are uniquely determined by their fundamental groups. They can all be represented in an essentially unique way as Seifert fiber spaces: the quotient manifold is a sphere and there are 3 exceptional fibers of orders 2, 3, and 5.
