# Abramowitz and Stegun

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Abramowitz and Stegun"}

***Abramowitz and Stegun*** (***AS***) is the informal name of a 1964 mathematical [reference work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_work) edited by [Milton Abramowitz](wikipedia://en/1206071) and [Irene Stegun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irene_Stegun) of the United States [National Bureau of Standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Standards) (NBS), now the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST). Its full title is ***Handbook of Mathematical Functions with Formulas, Graphs, and Mathematical Tables***. A digital successor to the Handbook was released as the "[Digital Library of Mathematical Functions](wikipedia://en/12647205)" (DLMF) on 11 May 2010, along with a printed version, the *[NIST Handbook of Mathematical Functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIST_Handbook_of_Mathematical_Functions)*, published by [Cambridge University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Press).[^o9]

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

Since it was first published in 1964, the 1046-page *Handbook* has been one of the most comprehensive sources of information on [special functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_function), containing definitions, identities, approximations, plots, and tables of values of numerous functions used in virtually all fields of [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969).[^o27][^o10][^o11] The notation used in the *Handbook* is the de facto standard for much of applied mathematics today.

At the time of its publication, the *Handbook* was an essential resource for practitioners. Nowadays, [scientific calculators](wikipedia://en/439711), [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506) software packages, and [computer algebra systems](wikipedia://en/54427) have replaced the [function tables](wikipedia://en/239112), but the *Handbook* remains an important reference source. The foreword discusses a meeting in 1954 in which it was agreed that "the advent of high-speed computing equipment changed the task of table making but definitely did not remove the need for tables".

More than 1,000 pages long, the *Handbook of Mathematical Functions* was first published in 1964 and reprinted many times, with yet another reprint in 1999. Its influence on science and engineering is evidenced by its popularity. In fact, when *[New Scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Scientist)* magazine recently asked some of the world's leading scientists what single book they would want if stranded on a desert island, one distinguished British physicist[^o12] said he would take the Handbook. The *Handbook* is likely the most widely distributed and most cited NIST technical publication of all time. Government sales exceed 150,000 copies, and an estimated three times as many have been reprinted and sold by commercial publishers since 1965. During the mid-1990s, the book was cited every 1.5 hours of each working day. And its influence will persist as it is currently being updated in digital format by NIST.

## Editions {#Editions}

Because the *Handbook* is the work of [U.S. federal government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_government_of_the_United_States) employees acting in their official capacity, it is [not protected by copyright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_status_of_work_by_the_U.S._government) in the United States. While it could be ordered from the [Government Printing Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Government_Publishing_Office), it has also been reprinted by commercial publishers, most notably [Dover Publications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dover_Publications) (), and can be legally viewed on and downloaded from the web.

While there was only one edition of the work, it went through many [print runs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Print_run) including a growing number of corrections.

## Related projects {#Related_projects}

[Michael Danos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Danos) and [Johann Rafelski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Rafelski) edited the *Pocketbook of Mathematical Functions*, published by [Verlag Harri Deutsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verlag_Harri_Deutsch) in 1984.[^o21][^o22] The book is an abridged version of Abramowitz's and Stegun's Handbook, retaining most of the formulas (except for the first and the two last original chapters, which were dropped), but reducing the numerical tables to a minimum,[^o21] which, by this time, could be easily calculated with [scientific pocket calculators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_pocket_calculator).[^o22] The references were removed as well.[^o22] Most known errata were incorporated, the physical constants updated and the now-first chapter saw some slight enlargement compared to the former second chapter.[^o22] The numbering of formulas was kept for easier cross-reference.[^o22]

A digital successor to the Handbook, long under development at NIST, was released as the “[Digital Library of Mathematical Functions](wikipedia://en/12647205)” (DLMF) on 11 May 2010, along with a printed version, the *[NIST Handbook of Mathematical Functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIST_Handbook_of_Mathematical_Functions)*, published by [Cambridge University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Press).[^o9]
