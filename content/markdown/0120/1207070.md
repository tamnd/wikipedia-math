# Biquaternion

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Biquaternion"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), the **biquaternions** are the numbers $w + x \mathbf{i} + y \mathbf{j} + z \mathbf{k}$, where $w, x, y$, and $z$ are [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), or variants thereof, and the elements of $\{\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}\}$ multiply as in the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130) and commute with their coefficients. There are three types of biquaternions corresponding to complex numbers and the variations thereof:

:::

- Biquaternions when the coefficients are [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826).
- [Split-biquaternions](wikipedia://en/4166765) when the coefficients are [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559).
- [Dual quaternions](wikipedia://en/8730871) when the coefficients are [dual numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_numbers).

This article is about the *ordinary biquaternions* named by [William Rowan Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817) in 1844.[^n1] Some of the more prominent proponents of these biquaternions include [Alexander Macfarlane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Macfarlane), [Arthur W. Conway](wikipedia://en/6377687), [Ludwik Silberstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwik_Silberstein), and [Cornelius Lanczos](wikipedia://en/1657748). As developed below, the unit [quasi-sphere](wikipedia://en/51406535) of the biquaternions provides a representation of the [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489), which is the foundation of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962).

The algebra of biquaternions can be considered as a [tensor product](wikipedia://en/372395) $\mathbf{C} \otimes_{\mathbf{R}} \mathbf{H}$, where $\mathbf{C}$ is the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of complex numbers and $\mathbf{H}$ is the [division algebra](wikipedia://en/26549625) of (real) [quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternions). In other words, the biquaternions are just the [complexification](wikipedia://en/755604) of the quaternions. Viewed as a complex algebra, the biquaternions are isomorphic to the algebra of $2 \times 2$ complex matrices $M_{2}(\mathbf{C})$. They are also isomorphic to several [Clifford algebras](wikipedia://en/45305) including $\mathbf{C} \otimes_{\mathbf{R}} \mathbf{H} = \operatorname{Cl}^{[0]}_{3}(\mathbf{C}) = \operatorname{Cl}_{2}(\mathbf{C}) = \operatorname{Cl}_{1,2}(\mathbf{R})$,[^n2] the [Pauli algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauli_algebra) $\operatorname{Cl}_{3,0}(\mathbf{R})$,[^n3][^n4] and the even part $\operatorname{Cl}^{[0]}_{1,3}(\mathbf{R}) = \operatorname{Cl}^{[0]}_{3,1}(\mathbf{R})$ of the [spacetime algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime_algebra).[^n5] In the [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) literature, the biquaternions, in its real Clifford algebra definition as $\operatorname{Cl}_{3,0}(\mathbf{R})$, are used under the name [algebra of physical space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_of_physical_space).[^o7]

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $\{\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}\}$ be the basis for the (real) [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) $\mathbf{H}$, and let $u, v, w, x$ be complex numbers, then

$$q = u \mathbf 1 + v \mathbf i + w \mathbf j + x \mathbf k$$

is a *biquaternion*.[^n6] To distinguish square roots of minus one in the biquaternions, Hamilton[^n7][^n8] and [Arthur W. Conway](wikipedia://en/6377687) used the convention of representing the square root of minus one in the scalar field $\mathbf{C}$ by $h$ to avoid confusion with the $\mathbf{i}$ in the quaternion group. [Commutativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity) of the scalar field with the quaternion group is assumed:

$$h \mathbf i = \mathbf i h,\ \ h \mathbf j = \mathbf j h,\ \ h \mathbf k = \mathbf k h .$$

Hamilton introduced the terms *[bivector](wikipedia://en/38379690)*, *biconjugate*, *bitensor*, and *biversor* to extend notions used with real quaternions $\mathbf{H}$.

Hamilton's primary exposition on biquaternions came in 1853 in his *Lectures on Quaternions*. The editions of *Elements of Quaternions*, in 1866 by [William Edwin Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Edwin_Hamilton) (son of Rowan), and in 1899, 1901 by [Charles Jasper Joly](wikipedia://en/21415250), reduced the biquaternion coverage in favour of the real quaternions.

Considered with the operations of component-wise addition, and multiplication according to the quaternion group, this collection forms a [4-dimensional](wikipedia://en/1364622) [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) over the complex numbers $\mathbf{C}$. The algebra of biquaternions is [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative), but not [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative). A biquaternion is either a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) or a [zero divisor](wikipedia://en/51441). The algebra of biquaternions forms a [composition algebra](wikipedia://en/3861635) and can be constructed from [bicomplex numbers](wikipedia://en/4802961). See below.

:::

## Place in ring theory {#Place_in_ring_theory}

### Linear representation {#Linear_representation}

Note that the [matrix product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_product)

$$\begin{pmatrix}h & 0\\0 & -h\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\-1 & 0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & h\\h & 0\end{pmatrix}.$$

Because $h$ is the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358), each of these three arrays has a square equal to the negative of the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718). When this matrix product is interpreted as $\mathbf{i} \mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k}$, then one obtains a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of matrices that is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130). Consequently,

$$\begin{pmatrix}u+hv & w+hx\\-w+hx & u-hv\end{pmatrix}$$

represents biquaternion $q = u \mathbf{1} + v \mathbf{i} + w \mathbf{j} + x \mathbf{k}$. Given any $2 \times 2$ complex matrix, there are complex values $u$, $v$, $w$, and $x$ to put it in this form so that the [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) $M(2, \mathbf{C})$ is isomorphic[^n9] to the biquaternion [ring](wikipedia://en/48404). This representation also shows that the 16-element group

$$\{\pm\mathbf 1, \pm h, \pm\mathbf i, \pm h\mathbf i, \pm\mathbf j, \pm h\mathbf j, \pm\mathbf k, \pm h\mathbf k \}$$

is isomorphic to the [Pauli group](wikipedia://en/5045613), the [central product](wikipedia://en/4842988) of a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order 4 and the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) of order 8. Concretely, the [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868)

$$X = \begin{pmatrix} 0&1\\ 1&0 \end{pmatrix},\quad Y = \begin{pmatrix} 0&-h\\ h&0 \end{pmatrix},\quad Z = \begin{pmatrix} 1&0\\ 0&-1 \end{pmatrix}$$

correspond respectively to the elements $\text{－}h\mathbf{k}, \text{－}h\mathbf{j}$, and $\text{－}h\mathbf{i}$.

### Subalgebras {#Subalgebras}

Considering the biquaternion algebra over the scalar field of real numbers $\mathbf{R}$, the set

$$\{\mathbf 1, h, \mathbf i, h\mathbf i, \mathbf j, h\mathbf j, \mathbf k, h\mathbf k \}$$

forms a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) so the algebra has eight real [dimensions](wikipedia://en/8398). The squares of the elements $h\mathbf{i}, h\mathbf{j}$, and $h\mathbf{k}$ are all positive one, for example, $(h\mathbf{i})^{2} = h^{2}\mathbf{i}^{2} = (-\mathbf{1})(-\mathbf{1}) = +\mathbf{1}$.

The [subalgebra](wikipedia://en/45239) given by

$$\{ x + y(h\mathbf i) : x, y \in \mathbb{R} \}$$

is [ring isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_isomorphism) to the plane of [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559), which has an algebraic structure built upon the [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122). The elements $h\mathbf{j}$ and $h\mathbf{k}$ also determine such subalgebras.

Furthermore, $\{ x + y \mathbf j : x,y \in \mathbb{C} \}$ is a subalgebra isomorphic to the [bicomplex numbers](wikipedia://en/4802961).

A third subalgebra called [coquaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coquaternion) is generated by $h\mathbf{j}$ and $h\mathbf{k}$. It is seen that $(h\mathbf{j})(h\mathbf{k}) = (-\mathbf{1})\mathbf{i}$, and that the square of this element is $-\mathbf{1}$. These elements generate the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) of the square. The [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) with basis $\{\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{i}, h\mathbf{j}, h\mathbf{k}\}$ thus is closed under multiplication, and forms the coquaternion algebra.

In the context of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) and [spinor](wikipedia://en/29276) algebra, the biquaternions $h\mathbf{i}, h\mathbf{j}$, and $h\mathbf{k}$ (or their negatives), viewed in the $M_{2}(\mathbf{C})$ representation, are called [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868).

::: {#o18 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Algebraic properties"}

## Algebraic properties {#Algebraic_properties}

The biquaternions have two *conjugations*:

- the **biconjugate** or biscalar minus [bivector](wikipedia://en/38379690) is $q^* = w - x\mathbf i - y\mathbf j - z\mathbf k \!\ ,$ and
- the [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation) of biquaternion coefficients $\bar{q} = \bar{w} + \bar{x}\mathbf i + \bar{y} \mathbf j + \bar{z}\mathbf k$

where $\bar{z} = a - bh$ when $z = a + bh,\quad a,b \in \mathbb{R},\quad h^2 = -\mathbf 1.$

Note that $(pq)^* = q^* p^*, \quad \overline{pq} = \bar{p} \bar{q}, \quad \overline{q^*} = \bar{q}^*.$

Clearly, if $q q^* = 0$ then $q$ is a zero divisor. Otherwise $\lbrace q q^* \rbrace^{-\mathbf 1}$ is a complex number. Further, $q q^* = q^* q$ is easily verified. This allows the inverse to be defined by

- $q^{-1} = q^* \lbrace q q^* \rbrace^{-1}$, if $qq^* \neq 0.$

### Relation to Lorentz transformations {#Relation_to_Lorentz_transformations}

Consider now the linear subspace[^n10]

$$M = \lbrace q\colon q^* = \bar{q} \rbrace = \lbrace t + x(h\mathbf i) + y(h \mathbf j) + z(h \mathbf k)\colon t, x, y, z \in \mathbb{R} \rbrace .$$

$M$ is not a subalgebra since it is not [closed under products](wikipedia://en/293802); for example $(h\mathbf i)(h\mathbf j) = h^2 \mathbf{ij} = -\mathbf k \notin M.$ Indeed, $M$ cannot form an algebra if it is not even a [magma](wikipedia://en/141916).

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=inline-head name="Proposition"}

**Proposition:** If $q$ is in $M$, then $q q^* = t^2 - x^2 - y^2 - z^2.$

Proof: From the definitions,

$$\begin{aligned} q q^* &= (t+xh\mathbf i+yh\mathbf j+zh\mathbf k)(t-xh\mathbf i-yh\mathbf j-zh\mathbf k)\\ &= t^2 - x^2(h\mathbf i)^2 - y^2(h\mathbf j)^2 - z^2(h\mathbf k)^2 \\ &= t^2 - x^2 - y^2 - z^2. \end{aligned}$$

:::

::: {#o22 .definition cue=inline-head name="Definition"}

**Definition:** Let biquaternion $g$ satisfy $g g^* = 1.$ Then the [Lorentz transformation](wikipedia://en/18404) associated with $g$ is given by

$$T(q) = g^* q \bar{g}.$$

:::

::: {#o23 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=inline-head name="Proposition"}

**Proposition:** If $q$ is in $M$, then $T(q)$ is also in $M$.

Proof: $(g^* q \bar{g})^* = \bar{g}^* q^* g = \overline{g^*} \bar{q} g = \overline{g^* q \bar{g})}.$

:::

::: {#o24 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=inline-head name="Proposition"}

**Proposition:** $\quad T(q) (T(q))^* = q q^*$

Proof: Note first that *gg*\* = 1 implies that the sum of the squares of its four complex components is one. Then the sum of the squares of the *complex conjugates* of these components is also one. Therefore, $\bar{g} (\bar{g})^* = 1.$ Now

$$(g^* q \bar{g})(g^* q \bar{g})^* = g^* q (\bar{g} \bar{g}^*) q^* g = g^* q q^* g = q q^*.$$

:::

:::

## Associated terminology {#Associated_terminology}

As the biquaternions have been a fixture of [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) since the beginnings of [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416), there is an array of concepts that are illustrated or represented by biquaternion algebra. The [transformation group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_group) $G = \lbrace g : g g^* = 1 \rbrace$ has two parts, $G \cap H$ and $G \cap M.$ The first part is characterized by $g = \bar{g}$ ; then the Lorentz transformation corresponding to $g$ is given by $T(q) = g^{-1} q g$ since $g^* = g^{-1}.$ Such a transformation is a [rotation by quaternion multiplication](wikipedia://en/186057), and the collection of them is $\operatorname{SO}(3)$ $\cong G \cap H .$ But this subgroup of $G$ is not a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918), so no [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) can be formed.

To view $G \cap M$ it is necessary to show some subalgebra structure in the biquaternions. Let $r$ represent an element of the [sphere of square roots of minus one](wikipedia://en/51440#Square_roots_of_−1) in the real quaternion subalgebra $\mathbf{H}$. Then $(hr)^{2} = +1$ and the plane of biquaternions given by $D_r = \lbrace z = x + yhr : x, y \in \mathbb R \rbrace$ is a commutative subalgebra isomorphic to the plane of split-complex numbers. Just as the ordinary complex plane has a unit circle, $D_r$ has a [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122) given by

$$\exp(ahr) = \cosh(a) + hr\ \sinh(a),\quad a \in R.$$

Just as the unit circle turns by multiplication through one of its elements, so the hyperbola turns because $\exp(ahr) \exp(bhr) = \exp((a+b)hr).$ Hence these algebraic operators on the hyperbola are called [hyperbolic versors](wikipedia://en/2644621#Hyperbolic_versor). The unit circle in $\mathbf{C}$ and unit hyperbola in $D_{r}$ are examples of [one-parameter groups](wikipedia://en/754851). For every square root $r$ of minus one in $\mathbf{H}$, there is a one-parameter group in the biquaternions given by $G \cap D_r.$

The space of biquaternions has a natural [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) through the [Euclidean metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_metric) on $8$-space. With respect to this topology, $G$ is a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315). Moreover, it has analytic structure making it a six-parameter [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945). Consider the subspace of bivectors $A = \lbrace q : q^* = -q \rbrace$. Then the [exponential map](wikipedia://en/44612290) $\exp:A \to G$ takes the real vectors to $G \cap H$ and the $h$-vectors to $G \cap M.$ When equipped with the [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193), $A$ forms the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) of $G$. Thus this study of a [six-dimensional space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six-dimensional_space) serves to introduce the general concepts of [Lie theory](wikipedia://en/1595681). When viewed in the matrix representation, $G$ is called the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) $\operatorname{SL}(2,C)$ in $M(2, \mathbf{C})$.

Many of the concepts of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) are illustrated through the biquaternion structures laid out. The subspace $M$ corresponds to [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space), with the four coordinates giving the time and space locations of events in a resting [frame of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference). Any hyperbolic versor $\exp(ahr)$ corresponds to a [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) in direction $r$ of speed $c \tanh a$ where $c$ is the [velocity of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity_of_light). The inertial frame of reference of this velocity can be made the resting frame by applying the [Lorentz boost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_boost) $T$ given by $g = \exp(0.5ahr)$ since then $g^{\star} = \exp(-0.5ahr) = g^*$ so that $T(\exp(ahr)) = 1 .$ Naturally the [hyperboloid](wikipedia://en/145844) $G \cap M,$ which represents the range of velocities for sub-luminal motion, is of physical interest. There has been considerable work associating this "velocity space" with the [hyperboloid model](wikipedia://en/4771189) of [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291). In special relativity, the [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) parameter of a hyperbolic versor is called [rapidity](wikipedia://en/4520673). Thus we see the biquaternion group $G$ provides a [group representation](wikipedia://en/12695) for the [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489).[^n11]

After the introduction of [spinor](wikipedia://en/29276) theory, particularly in the hands of [Wolfgang Pauli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Pauli) and [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274), the biquaternion representation of the Lorentz group was superseded. The new methods were founded on [basis vectors](wikipedia://en/18420) in the set

$$\{ q \ :\ q q^* = 0 \} = \left\{ w + x\mathbf i + y\mathbf j + z\mathbf k \ :\ w^2 + x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 0 \right\}$$

which is called the *complex light cone*. The above [representation of the Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/2249718) coincides with what physicists refer to as [four-vectors](wikipedia://en/226424). Beyond four-vectors, the [Standard Model](wikipedia://en/47641) of particle physics also includes other Lorentz representations, known as [scalars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_scalar), and the $(1, 0) \oplus (0, 1)$-representation associated with e.g. the [electromagnetic field tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field_tensor). Furthermore, particle physics makes use of the $\operatorname{SL}(2, \mathbf{C})$ representations (or [projective representations](wikipedia://en/348860) of the Lorentz group) known as left- and right-handed [Weyl spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_spinor), [Majorana spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majorana_spinor), and [Dirac spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_spinor). It is known that each of these seven representations can be constructed as invariant subspaces within the biquaternions.[^n12]

## As a composition algebra {#As_a_composition_algebra}

Although W. R. Hamilton introduced biquaternions in the 19th century, its delineation of its [mathematical structure](wikipedia://en/1809181) as a special type of [algebra over a field](wikipedia://en/191788) was accomplished in the 20th century: the biquaternions may be generated out of the [bicomplex numbers](wikipedia://en/4802961) in the same way that [Adrian Albert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrian_Albert) generated the real quaternions out of complex numbers in the so-called [Cayley–Dickson construction](wikipedia://en/175609). In this construction, a bicomplex number $(w, z)$ has conjugate $(w, z)* = (w, - z)$.

The biquaternion is then a pair of bicomplex numbers $(a, b)$, where the product with a second biquaternion $(c, d)$ is

$$(a,b)(c,d) = (a c - d^* b, d a + b c^* ).$$

If $a = (u, v), b = (w,z),$ then the *biconjugate* $(a, b)^* = (a^*, -b).$

When $(a, b)*$ is written as a 4-vector of ordinary complex numbers,

$$(u, v, w, z)^* = (u, -v, -w, -z).$$

The biquaternions form an example of a [quaternion algebra](wikipedia://en/2776890), and it has norm

$$N(u,v,w,z) = u^2 + v^2 + w^2 + z^2 .$$

Two biquaternions $p$ and $q$ satisfy $N(pq) = N(p) N(q)$, indicating that $N$ is a quadratic form admitting composition, so that the biquaternions form a [composition algebra](wikipedia://en/3861635).

## Scalar-vector representation {#Scalar-vector_representation}

An arbitrary biquaternion $A$ can be represented as a sum of a complex number (*scalar*) $\alpha = Sc(A)$ and a three-dimensional complex-valued vector $\mathbf a = Vc(A)$[^o30]:

$$A = \alpha + \mathbf a = (\alpha,\mathbf a)$$

There are two types of scalar-vector representation, based on different forms of biquaternionic product. Both representations are equivalent. In *standard representation*[^o31] the product of two biquaternions $A = (\alpha,\mathbf a)$ and $B = (\beta,\mathbf b)$ has the form:

$$A B = (\alpha \beta - (\mathbf a \mathbf b),\alpha \mathbf b + \beta \mathbf a + [\mathbf a \mathbf b]),$$

where $(\mathbf a \mathbf b)$ and $[\mathbf a \mathbf b]$ are [scalar product](wikipedia://en/157093) and [vector product](wikipedia://en/157092) of $\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b$, respectively. In *complex representation*[^o32] the product of the same biquaternions has the form:

$$A B = (\alpha \beta + (\mathbf a \mathbf b),\alpha \mathbf b + \beta \mathbf a + i [\mathbf a \mathbf b])$$

Note that in complex representation the product of two real-valued biquaternions yields a complex-valued biquaternion unless their vector parts are collinear. *Biquaternionic conjugate* of a given biquaternion $A=(\alpha,\mathbf a)$ is defined as:

$$\overline A = (\alpha, - \mathbf a)$$

*Square modulus* of a biquaternion $A=(\alpha,\mathbf a)$ is a complex number

$$|A|^2 = A \overline A = \overline A A = \alpha^2 - \mathbf a^2$$

The latter has the multiplicative property:

$$|A B|^2 = |A|^2 |B|^2$$

Applying biquaternonic or complex conjugation to the product of biquaternions turns each multiplier to its conjugate and change their order:

$$\overline { A B } = \overline B \overline A$$

$$( A B )^* = B^* A^*$$

Depending on their square modulus, all biquaternions are divided into two non-intersecting subspaces (by multiplication): nullquaternions and finite biquaternions. Nullquaternions are biquaternions with zero square modulus; finite biquaternions are biquaternions with non-zero square modulus.
