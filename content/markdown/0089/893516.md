# Core (group theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Core"}

In [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **core** is any of certain special [normal subgroups](wikipedia://en/21918) of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447). The two most common types are the **normal core** of a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) and the ***p*-core** of a group.

:::

## The normal core {#The_normal_core}

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

For a group *G*, the **normal core** or **normal interior**[^o5] of a subgroup *H* is the largest [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of *G* that is contained in *H* (or equivalently, the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of the [conjugates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_%28group_theory%29) of *H*). More generally, the core of *H* with respect to a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) *S* ⊆ *G* is the intersection of the conjugates of *H* under *S*, i.e.

$$\mathrm{Core}_S(H) := \bigcap_{s \in S}{s^{-1}Hs}.$$

Under this more general definition, the normal core is the core with respect to *S* = *G*. The normal core of any normal subgroup is the subgroup itself.

Dual to the concept of normal core is that of [normal closure](wikipedia://en/153099) which is the smallest normal subgroup of *G* containing *H*.

:::

### Significance {#Significance}

Normal cores are important in the context of [group actions](wikipedia://en/12781) on [sets](wikipedia://en/26691), where the normal core of the [isotropy subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotropy_subgroup) of any point acts as the identity on its entire [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29). Thus, in case the action is [transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_group_action), the normal core of any isotropy subgroup is precisely the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of the action.

A **core-free subgroup** is a subgroup whose normal core is the [trivial subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivial_subgroup). Equivalently, it is a subgroup that occurs as the isotropy subgroup of a transitive, [faithful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faithful_group_action) group action.

The solution for the [hidden subgroup problem](wikipedia://en/3728000) in the [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) case generalizes to finding the normal core in case of subgroups of arbitrary groups.

## The *p*-core {#The_p-core}

In this section *G* will denote a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707), though some aspects generalize to [locally finite groups](wikipedia://en/4939270) and to [profinite groups](wikipedia://en/24266).

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

For a [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) *p*, the ***p*-core** of a finite group is defined to be its largest normal [*p*-subgroup](wikipedia://en/24664). It is the normal core of every [Sylow p-subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylow_subgroup) of the group. The *p*-core of *G* is often denoted $O_p(G)$, and in particular appears in one of the definitions of the [Fitting subgroup](wikipedia://en/1416042) of a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707). Similarly, the ***p*′-core** is the largest normal subgroup of *G* whose order is coprime to *p* and is denoted $O_{p'}(G)$. In the area of finite insoluble groups, including the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445), the 2′-core is often called simply the **core** and denoted $O(G)$. This causes only a small amount of confusion, because one can usually distinguish between the core of a group and the core of a subgroup within a group. The ***p*′,*p*-core**, denoted $O_{p',p}(G)$ is defined by $O_{p',p}(G)/O_{p'}(G) = O_p(G/O_{p'}(G))$. For a finite group, the *p*′,*p*-core is the unique largest normal *p*-nilpotent subgroup.

The *p*-core can also be defined as the unique largest subnormal *p*-subgroup; the *p*′-core as the unique largest subnormal *p*′-subgroup; and the *p*′,*p*-core as the unique largest subnormal *p*-nilpotent subgroup.

The *p*′ and *p*′,*p*-core begin the **upper *p*-series**. For sets *π*<sub>1</sub>, *π*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *π*<sub>*n*+1</sub> of primes, one defines subgroups O<sub>*π*<sub>1</sub>, *π*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *π*<sub>*n*+1</sub></sub>(*G*) by:

$$O_{\pi_1,\pi_2,\dots,\pi_{n+1}}(G)/O_{\pi_1,\pi_2,\dots,\pi_{n}}(G) = O_{\pi_{n+1}}( G/O_{\pi_1,\pi_2,\dots,\pi_{n}}(G) )$$

The upper *p*-series is formed by taking *π*<sub>2*i*−1</sub> = *p*′ and *π*<sub>2*i*</sub> = *p;* there is also a [lower *p*-series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_p-series). A finite group is said to be ***p*-nilpotent** if and only if it is equal to its own *p*′,*p*-core. A finite group is said to be ***p*-soluble** if and only if it is equal to some term of its upper *p*-series; its ***p*-length** is the length of its upper *p*-series. A finite group *G* is said to be **[p-constrained](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-constrained)** for a prime *p* if $C_G(O_{p',p}(G)/O_{p'}(G)) \subseteq O_{p',p}(G)$.

Every nilpotent group is *p*-nilpotent, and every *p*-nilpotent group is *p*-soluble. Every soluble group is *p*-soluble, and every *p*-soluble group is *p*-constrained. A group is *p*-nilpotent if and only if it has a **normal *p*-complement**, which is just its *p*′-core.

:::

### Significance {#Significance}

Just as normal cores are important for [group actions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) on sets, *p*-cores and *p*′-cores are important in [modular representation theory](wikipedia://en/1110685), which studies the actions of groups on [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370). The *p*-core of a finite group is the intersection of the kernels of the [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/29430) over any field of characteristic *p*. For a finite group, the *p*′-core is the intersection of the kernels of the ordinary (complex) irreducible representations that lie in the principal *p*-block. For a finite group, the *p*′,*p*-core is the intersection of the kernels of the irreducible representations in the principal *p*-block over any field of characteristic *p*. Also, for a finite group, the *p*′,*p*-core is the intersection of the centralizers of the abelian chief factors whose order is divisible by *p* (all of which are irreducible representations over a field of size *p* lying in the principal block). For a finite, *p*-constrained group, an irreducible module over a field of characteristic *p* lies in the principal block if and only if the *p*′-core of the group is contained in the kernel of the representation.

### Solvable radicals {#Solvable_radicals}

A related subgroup in concept and notation is the solvable radical. The **solvable radical** is defined to be the largest [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012) normal subgroup, and is denoted $O_\infty(G)$. There is some variance in the literature in defining the *p*′-core of *G*. A few authors in only a few papers (for instance [John G. Thompson](wikipedia://en/862189)'s N-group papers, but not his later work) define the *p*′-core of an insoluble group *G* as the *p*′-core of its solvable radical in order to better mimic properties of the 2′-core.
