# Supermanifold

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Supermanifold"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416), **supermanifolds** are generalizations of [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) in which the algebra of functions includes both commuting and anticommuting variables. In the standard mathematical formulation, a smooth supermanifold is a [locally ringed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_ringed_space) whose structure sheaf is locally isomorphic to the [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) of the ring of ordinary smooth functions $C^\infty(\mathbb R^p)$ and a [Grassmann algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grassmann_algebra) $\Lambda(\xi_1,\dots,\xi_q)$ of the anticommuting variables. In complex-analytic and algebraic settings, smooth functions are replaced with [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110) or [algebraic functions](wikipedia://en/974169), respectively. An ordinary manifold is recovered from a supermanifold as the corresponding reduced space of commuting variables, sometimes called the *body* or *reduced manifold*.

:::

Supermanifolds provide the basic objects of [supergeometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supergeometry). They were introduced in connection with [supersymmetry](wikipedia://en/224636) and are used in areas of [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416) such as [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267) and [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305), as well as in purely mathematical subjects including the theory of [Lie superalgebras](wikipedia://en/297501) and [supergroups](wikipedia://en/293756). Alongside the standard locally ringed-space formulation, more concrete coordinate-based formalisms are also used, especially in the physics literature.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The standard mathematical definition of a supermanifold is in terms of a [locally ringed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_ringed_space). In this approach, a supermanifold is a space equipped with a sheaf of [supercommutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercommutative) algebras that is locally isomorphic to a superdomain.[^o4]

A more concrete coordinate-based formalism, used especially in parts of the physics literature, describes supermanifolds by charts with values in the even and odd parts of a Grassmann algebra.[^o4][^o5] The relationship between this formalism and the locally ringed-space definition requires some care, and is often clarified using the [functor of points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functor_of_points).[^o6][^o7]

### Algebro-geometric: as a locally ringed space {#Algebro-geometric:_as_a_locally_ringed_space}

The standard mathematical definition of a smooth supermanifold is in terms of a [locally ringed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_ringed_space). A smooth supermanifold of dimension $p|q$ is a locally ringed space $(|M|,\mathcal O_M)$ that is locally isomorphic to

$$\left(\mathbb R^p,\; C^\infty_{\mathbb R^p}\otimes \Lambda(\xi_1,\dots,\xi_q)\right).$$

More explicitly, every point of $|M|$ has an open neighbourhood $U$ such that $(U,\mathcal O_M|_U)$ is isomorphic to $(V, C^\infty_V\otimes\Lambda(\xi_1,\dots,\xi_q))$ for some open set $V\subseteq\mathbb R^p$.[^o4]

The odd elements of the structure sheaf are [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) and anti-commute, so they do not define additional points of the underlying topological space. The underlying ordinary manifold $|M|$, sometimes called the *reduced manifold* or *body*, is obtained by quotienting $\mathcal O_M$ by the sheaf of ideals generated by its odd elements.

A morphism of supermanifolds is a morphism of locally ringed spaces respecting the $\mathbb Z_2$-grading. Variants of this definition are used in the smooth, [complex-analytic](wikipedia://en/509742), and [algebraic](wikipedia://en/1997) categories. Historically, this approach is associated with the work of [Felix Berezin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Berezin), [Dimitry Leites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimitry_Leites), and [Bertram Kostant](wikipedia://en/3520590).

### Concrete: as a smooth manifold {#Concrete:_as_a_smooth_manifold}

A different, more concrete approach describes a supermanifold in a way analogous to a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold), except that the model space $\mathbb{R}^p$ is replaced by a *model superspace* built from the even and odd parts of a Grassmann algebra.[^o10][^o4]

To define this, one starts with a Grassmann algebra $\Lambda(V)$, where $V$ is an infinite-dimensional real or complex vector space. Its even and odd parts are denoted by $\Lambda(V)_{\bar 0}$ and $\Lambda(V)_{\bar 1}$. In the terminology used by DeWitt and Rogers, the even elements are called *c*-numbers and the odd elements *a*-numbers. One then writes

$$\mathbb{R}^p_c = (\Lambda(V)_{\bar 0})^p, \qquad \mathbb{R}^q_a = (\Lambda(V)_{\bar 1})^q,$$

and takes $\mathbb{R}^p_c\times\mathbb{R}^q_a$ as the local model superspace.[^o10][^o4]

As in the case of an ordinary manifold, a supermanifold is then described by an [atlas](wikipedia://en/47436) of charts with suitably smooth transition functions.[^o10] In the DeWitt approach, these model superspaces are usually equipped with a coarse topology induced from the projection onto the underlying ordinary space $\mathbb{R}^p$.[^o10]

This concrete approach is closely related to the [locally ringed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_ringed_space) definition, but the relationship requires some care. With suitable restrictions on transition functions, the two approaches are equivalent; more generally, the comparison is best understood using the [functor of points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functor_of_points).[^o4][^o6][^o11][^o12]

## Properties {#Properties}

Unlike a regular manifold, a supermanifold is not entirely composed of a set of points. Instead, one takes the dual point of view that the structure of a supermanifold **M** is contained in its sheaf *O<sub>**M**</sub>* of "smooth functions". In the dual point of view, an injective map corresponds to a surjection of sheaves, and a surjective map corresponds to an injection of sheaves.

An alternative approach to the dual point of view is to use the [functor of points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functor_of_points).

If **M** is a supermanifold of dimension (*p*,*q*), then the underlying space *M* inherits the structure of a [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) whose sheaf of smooth functions is $O_M/I$, where $I$ is the [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) generated by all odd functions. Thus *M* is called the underlying space, or the body, of **M**. The quotient map $O_M\to O_M/I$ corresponds to an injective map *M* → **M**; thus *M* is a submanifold of **M**.

:::

## Tangent sheaf {#Tangent_sheaf}

The [tangent sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_sheaf) of a supermanifold $M$ is the sheaf of [graded derivations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded_derivation) of its structure sheaf. Thus, for each open set $U$,

$$\mathcal T_M(U)=\operatorname{Der}_{\mathbb R}(\mathcal O_M(U)).$$

This is a locally free $\mathcal O_M$-module. The associated super vector bundle is called the tangent bundle of $M$. Its global sections are the vector fields on $M$.

An example is the supermanifold $\mathbb R^{1|1}$. The functions are $f_0(x) + \theta f_1(x)$ where $\theta$ is an odd generator, and $f_i$ are smooth functions on the base manifold. The vector fields then have the form $v_0(x,\theta)\partial_x + v_1(x,\theta)\partial_\theta$, where $v_i$ belong to the structure sheaf of $\mathbb R^{1|1}$, and $\partial_\theta$ is the odd derivation that extracts the coefficient of $\theta$.

## Super Riemann surfaces {#Super_Riemann_surfaces}

A super [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181) is a complex supermanifold of dimension $1|1$ equipped with a superconformal structure: an odd rank $0|1$ sub-bundle $D\subset TM$ that is maximally non-integrable. Equivalently, the Lie superbracket induces an isomorphism $D\otimes D \cong TM/D$. In suitable local coordinates $(z,\theta)$, a local generator of $D$ can be chosen as

$$D_\theta=\partial_\theta+\theta\partial_z,$$

for which

$$D_\theta^2=\tfrac12\{D_\theta,D_\theta\}=\partial_z.$$

A basic example is the genus-zero super Riemann surface $\mathbb{CP}^{1|1}$. In homogeneous coordinates $u,v\mid\theta$, all of degree $1$, its superconformal structure may be described by the homogeneous 1-form

$$\varpi=u\,dv-v\,du-\theta\,d\theta,$$

whose kernel is the superconformal distribution. In the affine chart $u=1$, writing $z=v$, this becomes $dz-\theta\,d\theta$, whose kernel is generated by $\partial_\theta+\theta\partial_z$.

More generally, for a split super Riemann surface, the reduced space is an ordinary Riemann surface together with a [spin structure](wikipedia://en/2844303), that is, a square root of its canonical bundle (equivalently, of its tangent bundle). (This is a definition of *split* super Riemann structures, although there are *non-split* examples where the superconformal structure does not come from a square root of the canonical bundle.)

Another example is a genus-one super Riemann surface with even spin structure, obtained as the quotient of $\mathbb C^{1|1}$ by

$$z\mapsto z+1,\ \theta\mapsto\theta,\qquad z\mapsto z+\tau,\ \theta\mapsto -\theta,$$

where $\tau$ lies in the upper half-plane. (The other possible twists correspond to the four different [theta characteristics](wikipedia://en/7193470) in genus 1.)

## Projective spaces {#Projective_spaces}

The projective superspace $\mathbb{CP}^{p|q}$ is defined as a quotient of the affine superspace $\mathbb C^{p+1|q}$. The affine superspace $\mathbb C^{p+1|q}$ has structure sheaf $\mathcal O_{\mathbb C^{p+1}}\otimes \Lambda(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_q)$, and one can think of the projective space roughly as the quoient of the action of even invertible $\lambda$, $(z,\theta)\sim (\lambda z,\lambda\theta)$.

More precisely, $\mathbb{CP}^{p|q}$ is obtained by the [proj construction](wikipedia://en/2285000) for the $\mathbb Z$-grading on the affine coordinate ring $\mathbb C[x_1,\dots,x_{p+1}|\theta_1,\dots,\theta_q]$. The localization in the affine patch $D(x_i)$ is

$$\mathcal O(D(x_i)) = (\mathbb C[x_1,\dots,\hat x_i,\dots,x_{p+1}|\theta_1,\dots,\theta_q][x_i^{-1}])_0.$$

The transition functions are the obvious ones.

## Examples {#Examples}

- Let *M* be a manifold. The *odd tangent bundle* ΠT*M* is a supermanifold given by the sheaf Ω(*M*) of differential forms on *M*.
- More generally, let *E* → *M* be a [vector bundle](wikipedia://en/276524). Then Π*E* is a supermanifold given by the sheaf Γ(ΛE<sup>\*</sup>). In fact, Π is a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from the [category of vector bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_vector_bundles) to the [category of supermanifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_supermanifolds).
- [Lie supergroups](wikipedia://en/293756) are examples of supermanifolds.

:::

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Batchelor's theorem"}

## Batchelor's theorem {#Batchelor's_theorem}

Batchelor's theorem states that every supermanifold is noncanonically isomorphic to a supermanifold of the form Π*E*. The word "noncanonically" prevents one from concluding that supermanifolds are simply glorified vector bundles; although the functor Π maps surjectively onto the isomorphism classes of supermanifolds, it is not an [equivalence of categories](wikipedia://en/379051). It was published by [Marjorie Batchelor](wikipedia://en/65001711) in 1979.[^o22]

The [proof](wikipedia://en/82285) of Batchelor's theorem relies in an essential way on the existence of a [partition of unity](wikipedia://en/48636), so it does not hold for complex or real-analytic supermanifolds.

:::

## Odd symplectic structures {#Odd_symplectic_structures}

### Odd symplectic form {#Odd_symplectic_form}

In many physical and geometric applications, a supermanifold comes equipped with an Grassmann-odd [symplectic structure](wikipedia://en/28356). All natural geometric objects on a supermanifold are graded. In particular, the bundle of two-forms is equipped with a grading. An odd symplectic form ω on a supermanifold is a closed, odd form, inducing a non-degenerate pairing on *TM*. Such a supermanifold is called a [P-manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-manifold). Its graded dimension is necessarily (*n*,*n*), because the odd symplectic form induces a pairing of odd and even variables. There is a version of the Darboux theorem for P-manifolds, which allows one to equip a P-manifold locally with a set of coordinates where the odd symplectic form ω is written as

$$\omega = \sum_{i} d\xi_i \wedge dx_i ,$$

where $x_i$ are even coordinates, and $\xi_i$ odd coordinates. (An odd symplectic form should not be confused with a Grassmann-even [symplectic form](wikipedia://en/28356) on a supermanifold. In contrast, the Darboux version of an even symplectic form is

$$\sum_i dp_i \wedge dq_i+\sum_j \frac{\varepsilon_j}{2}(d\xi_j)^2,$$

where $p_i,q_i$ are even coordinates, $\xi_i$ odd coordinates and $\varepsilon_j$ are either +1 or −1.)

### Antibracket {#Antibracket}

Given an odd symplectic 2-form ω one may define a [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340) known as the **antibracket** of any two functions *F* and *G* on a supermanifold by

$$\{F,G\}=\frac{\partial_rF}{\partial z^i}\omega^{ij}(z)\frac{\partial_lG}{\partial z^j}.$$

Here $\partial_r$ and $\partial_l$ are the right and left [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) respectively and *z* are the coordinates of the supermanifold. Equipped with this bracket, the algebra of functions on a supermanifold becomes an [antibracket algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibracket_algebra).

A [coordinate transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_transformation) that preserves the antibracket is called a [P-transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-transformation). If the [Berezinian](wikipedia://en/3038004) of a P-transformation is equal to one then it is called an [SP-transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SP-transformation).

### P and SP-manifolds {#P_and_SP-manifolds}

Using the [Darboux theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darboux_theorem) for odd symplectic forms one can show that P-manifolds are constructed from open sets of superspaces ${\mathcal{R}}^{n|n}$ glued together by P-transformations. A manifold is said to be an [SP-manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SP-manifold) if these transition functions can be chosen to be SP-transformations. Equivalently one may define an SP-manifold as a supermanifold with a nondegenerate odd 2-form ω and a [density function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_function) ρ such that on each [coordinate patch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_patch) there exist [Darboux coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darboux_coordinates) in which ρ is identically equal to one.

### Laplacian {#Laplacian}

One may define a [Laplacian operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian_operator) Δ on an SP-manifold as the operator which takes a function *H* to one half of the [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) of the corresponding [Hamiltonian vector field](wikipedia://en/1818270). Explicitly one defines

$$\Delta H=\frac{1}{2\rho}\frac{\partial_r}{\partial z^a}\left(\rho\omega^{ij}(z)\frac{\partial_l H}{\partial z^j}\right).$$

In Darboux coordinates this definition reduces to

$$\Delta=\frac{\partial_r}{\partial x^a}\frac{\partial_l}{\partial \theta_a}$$

where *x*<sup>*a*</sup> and *θ*<sub>*a*</sub> are even and odd coordinates such that

$$\omega=dx^a\wedge d\theta_a.$$

The Laplacian is odd and nilpotent

$$\Delta^2=0.$$

One may define the [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060) of functions *H* with respect to the Laplacian. In [Geometry of Batalin-Vilkovisky quantization](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/9205088), [Albert Schwarz](wikipedia://en/28995317) has proven that the integral of a function *H* over a [Lagrangian submanifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_submanifold) *L* depends only on the cohomology class of *H* and on the [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) class of the body of *L* in the body of the ambient supermanifold.

## SUSY {#SUSY}

A pre-SUSY-structure on a supermanifold of dimension (*n*,*m*) is an odd *m*-dimensional distribution $P \subset TM$. With such a distribution one associates its Frobenius tensor $S^2 P \mapsto TM/P$ (since *P* is odd, the skew-symmetric Frobenius tensor is a symmetric operation). If this tensor is non-degenerate, e.g. lies in an open orbit of $GL(P) \times GL(TM/P)$, *M* is called *a SUSY-manifold*. SUSY-structure in dimension (1, *k*) is the same as odd [contact structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_manifold).
