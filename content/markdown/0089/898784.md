# Quotient space (linear algebra)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quotient space"}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), the **quotient** of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $V$ by a [subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) $U$ is a vector space obtained by "collapsing" $U$ to zero. The space obtained is called a **quotient space** and is denoted $V/U$ (read "$V$ mod $U$" or "$V$ by $U$"). Alternatively phrased, the quotient space $V/U$ is the set of all [affine subsets](wikipedia://en/298834) of $V$ which are [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) to $U$.[^o2] A quotient space $A/U$ can also be defined for an affine space $A$ by a linear space $U$.[^o3]

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Formally, the construction is as follows.[^o6] Let $V$ be a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $\mathbb{K}$, and let $U$ be a [subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of $V$. We define an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) $\sim$ on $V$ by stating that $x \sim y$ iff $x - y \in U$. That is, $x$ is related to $y$ if and only if one can be obtained from the other by adding an element of $U$. This definition implies that any element of $U$ is related to the zero vector; more precisely, all the vectors in $U$ get mapped into the [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) of the zero vector.

The equivalence class – or, in this case, the [coset](wikipedia://en/98759) – of $v$ is defined as

$$[v] := \{ w : v - w \in U \}.$$

Equivalently, $[v] = \{ v + u : u \in U \}$, so it is often denoted using the shorthand $v + U$.

The quotient space $V/U$ is then defined as $V/\mathord\sim$, the set of all equivalence classes induced by $\sim$ on $U$. Scalar multiplication and addition are defined on the equivalence classes by[^o7][^o8]

- $\alpha [x] = [\alpha x]$ for all $\alpha \in \mathbb{K}$, and
- $$[x] + [y] = [x+y].$$

It is not hard to check that these operations are [well-defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-defined) (i.e. do not depend on the choice of [representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_%28mathematics%29)). These operations turn the quotient space $V/U$ into a vector space over $\mathbb{K}$ with $U$ being the zero class, $[0]$.

The mapping that associates to $v \in V$ the equivalence class $[v]$ is known as the **quotient map**.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

### Lines in Cartesian Plane {#Lines_in_Cartesian_Plane}

Let *X* = **R**<sup>2</sup> be the standard [Cartesian plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_plane), and let *Y* be a [line](wikipedia://en/946975) through the origin in *X*. Then the quotient space *X*/*Y* can be identified with the space of all lines in *X* which are parallel to *Y*. That is to say that, the elements of the set *X*/*Y* are lines in *X* parallel to *Y*. Note that the points along any one such line will satisfy the equivalence relation because their difference vectors ([relative positions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_position)) belong to *Y*. This gives a way to visualize quotient spaces geometrically. (By re-parameterising these lines, the quotient space can more conventionally be represented as the space of all points along a line through the origin that is not parallel to *Y*. Similarly, the quotient space for **R**<sup>3</sup> by a line through the origin can again be represented as the set of all co-parallel lines, or alternatively be represented as the vector space consisting of a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) which only intersects the line at the origin.)

### Subspaces of Cartesian Space {#Subspaces_of_Cartesian_Space}

Another example is the quotient of **R**<sup>*n*</sup> by the subspace spanned by the first *m* [standard basis vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_basis_vector). The space **R**<sup>*n*</sup> consists of all *n*-tuples of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) (*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>). The subspace, identified with **R**<sup>*m*</sup>, consists of all *n*-tuples such that the last *n* − *m* entries are zero: (*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*m*</sub>, 0, 0, ..., 0). Two vectors of **R**<sup>*n*</sup> are in the same equivalence class modulo the subspace [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) they are identical in the last *n* − *m* coordinates. The quotient space **R**<sup>*n*</sup>/**R**<sup>*m*</sup> is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to **R**<sup>*n*−*m*</sup> in an obvious manner.

### Polynomial Vector Space {#Polynomial_Vector_Space}

Let $\mathcal{P}_3(\mathbb{R})$ be the vector space of all cubic polynomials over the real numbers. Then $\mathcal{P}_3(\mathbb{R}) / \langle x^2 \rangle$ is a quotient space, where each element is the set corresponding to polynomials that differ by a quadratic term only. For example, one element of the quotient space is $\{x^3 + a x^2 - 2x + 3 : a \in \mathbb{R}\}$, while another element of the quotient space is $\{a x^2 + 2.7 x : a \in \mathbb{R}\}$.

### General Subspaces {#General_Subspaces}

More generally, if *V* is an (internal) [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) of subspaces *U* and *W,*

$$V=U\oplus W$$

then the quotient space *V*/*U* is [naturally isomorphic](wikipedia://en/45022) to *W*.[^o15]

### Lebesgue Integrals {#Lebesgue_Integrals}

An important example of a functional quotient space is an [L<sup>*p*</sup> space](wikipedia://en/45194#Lp_spaces_and_Lebesgue_integrals).

## Properties {#Properties}

There is a natural [epimorphism](wikipedia://en/59539) from *V* to the quotient space *V*/*U* given by sending *x* to its equivalence class \[*x*\]. The [kernel](wikipedia://en/1072915) (or nullspace) of this epimorphism is the subspace *U*. This relationship is neatly summarized by the [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence)

$$0\to U\to V\to V/U\to 0.\,$$

If *U* is a subspace of *V*, the [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) of *V*/*U* is called the **[codimension](wikipedia://en/525149)** of *U* in *V*. Since a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) of *V* may be constructed from a basis *A* of *U* and a basis *B* of *V*/*U* by adding a [representative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_%28mathematics%29) of each element of *B* to *A*, the dimension of *V* is the sum of the dimensions of *U* and *V*/*U*. If *V* is [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267), it follows that the codimension of *U* in *V* is the difference between the dimensions of *V* and *U*:[^o19][^o20]

$$\mathrm{codim}(U) = \dim(V/U) = \dim(V) - \dim(U).$$

Let *T* : *V* → *W* be a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator). The kernel of *T*, denoted ker(*T*), is the set of all *x* in *V* such that *Tx* = 0. The kernel is a subspace of *V*. The [first isomorphism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_isomorphism_theorem) for vector spaces says that the quotient space *V*/ker(*T*) is isomorphic to the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of *V* in *W*. An immediate [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865), for finite-dimensional spaces, is the [rank–nullity theorem](wikipedia://en/330310): the dimension of *V* is equal to the dimension of the kernel (the [nullity](wikipedia://en/1072915) of *T*) plus the dimension of the image (the [rank](wikipedia://en/26561) of *T*).

The [cokernel](wikipedia://en/279715) of a linear operator *T* : *V* → *W* is defined to be the quotient space *W*/im(*T*).

### First Isomorphism Theorem {#First_Isomorphism_Theorem}

Let $V,W$ be $\mathbb{K}$-Vector Spaces and $T: V \to W$ linear. Define the map $\overline T:V/\ker T \to \operatorname{im}(T)$ by $\overline T([v])=T(v).$ Then $\overline T$ is well-defined and an isomorphism.

## Quotient of a Banach space by a subspace {#Quotient_of_a_Banach_space_by_a_subspace}

If *X* is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) and *M* is a [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) subspace of *X*, then the quotient *X*/*M* is again a Banach space. The quotient space is already endowed with a vector space structure by the construction of the previous section. We define a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) on *X*/*M* by

$$\| [x] \|_{X/M} = \inf_{m \in M} \|x-m\|_X = \inf_{m \in M} \|x+m\|_X = \inf_{y\in [x]}\|y\|_X.$$

::: {#o27 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

Let *C*\[0,1\] denote the Banach space of [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) real-valued [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) on the [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) \[0,1\] with the [sup norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sup_norm). Denote the subspace of all functions *f* ∈ *C*\[0,1\] with *f*(0) = 0 by *M*. Then the equivalence class of some function *g* is determined by its value at 0, and the quotient space *C*\[0,1\]/*M* is isomorphic to **R**.

If *X* is a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), then the quotient space *X*/*M* is isomorphic to the [orthogonal complement](wikipedia://en/20598932#Orthogonal_complements_and_projections) of *M*.

:::

### Generalization to locally convex spaces {#Generalization_to_locally_convex_spaces}

The quotient of a [locally convex space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_convex_space) by a closed subspace is again locally convex.[^o29] Indeed, suppose that *X* is locally convex so that the [topology](wikipedia://en/30450) on *X* is generated by a family of [seminorms](wikipedia://en/292067) {*p*<sub>α</sub> | α ∈ *A*} where *A* is an index set. Let *M* be a closed subspace, and define seminorms *q*<sub>α</sub> on *X*/*M* by

$$q_\alpha([x]) = \inf_{v\in [x]} p_\alpha(v).$$

Then *X*/*M* is a locally convex space, and the topology on it is the [quotient topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_topology).

If, furthermore, *X* is [metrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrizable), then so is *X*/*M*. If *X* is a [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801), then so is *X*/*M*.[^o30]
