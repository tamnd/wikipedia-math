# Yamabe invariant

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Yamabe invariant"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the field of [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), the **Yamabe invariant**, also referred to as the **sigma constant**, is a real number invariant associated to a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) that is preserved under [diffeomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphisms). It was first written down independently by O. Kobayashi and [R. Schoen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Schoen) and takes its name from [H. Yamabe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidehiko_Yamabe). Used by [Vincent Moncrief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_Moncrief) and Arthur Fischer to study reduced [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319) for Einstein's equations.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $M$ be a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) smooth manifold (without boundary) of dimension $n\geq 2$. The normalized [Einstein–Hilbert functional](wikipedia://en/855776) $\mathcal{E}$ assigns to each [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) $g$ on $M$ a real number as follows:

$$\mathcal{E}(g) = \frac{\int_M R_g \, dV_g}{\left(\int_M \, dV_g\right)^{\frac{n-2}{n}}},$$

where $R_g$ is the [scalar curvature](wikipedia://en/285622) of $g$ and $dV_g$ is the [volume density](wikipedia://en/1855811#Relation_to_measures) associated to the metric $g$. The exponent in the denominator is chosen so that the functional is scale-invariant: for every positive real constant $c$, it satisfies $\mathcal{E}(cg) = \mathcal{E}(g)$. We may think of $\mathcal{E}(g)$ as measuring the average scalar curvature of $g$ over $M$. It was conjectured by Yamabe that every [conformal class](wikipedia://en/295907) of metrics contains a metric of constant scalar curvature (the so-called [Yamabe problem](wikipedia://en/11757994)); it was proven by Yamabe, [Trudinger](wikipedia://en/15173259), [Aubin](wikipedia://en/8748733), and Schoen that a minimum value of $\mathcal{E}(g)$ is attained in each conformal class of metrics, and in particular this minimum is achieved by a metric of constant scalar curvature.

We define

$$Y(g) = \inf_f \mathcal{E}(e^{2f} g),$$

where the infimum is taken over the smooth real-valued functions $f$ on $M$. This infimum is finite (not $-\infty$): [Hölder's inequality](wikipedia://en/191538) implies $Y(g) \geq -\left(\textstyle\int_M |R_g|^{n/2} \,dV_g\right)^{2/n}$. The number $Y(g)$ is sometimes called the conformal Yamabe energy of $g$ (and is constant on conformal classes).

A comparison argument due to Aubin shows that for any metric $g$, $Y(g)$ is bounded above by $\mathcal{E}(g_0)$, where $g_0$ is the standard metric on the $n$-sphere $S^n$. It follows that if we define

$$\sigma(M) = \sup_g Y(g),$$

where the supremum is taken over all metrics on $M$, then $\sigma(M) \leq \mathcal{E}(g_0)$ (and is in particular finite). The real number $\sigma(M)$ is called the Yamabe invariant of $M$.

:::

## The Yamabe invariant in two dimensions {#The_Yamabe_invariant_in_two_dimensions}

In the case that $n=2$, (so that *M* is a [closed surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_surface)) the Einstein–Hilbert functional is given by

$$\mathcal{E}(g) = \int_M R_g \, dV_g = \int_M 2K_g \, dV_g,$$

where $K_g$ is the [Gauss curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss_curvature) of *g*. However, by the [Gauss–Bonnet theorem](wikipedia://en/139229), the integral of the Gauss curvature is given by $2\pi \chi(M)$, where $\chi(M)$ is the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) of *M*. In particular, this number does not depend on the choice of metric. Therefore, for surfaces, we conclude that

$$\sigma(M) = 4\pi \chi(M).$$

For example, the 2-sphere has Yamabe invariant equal to $8\pi$, and the 2-torus has Yamabe invariant equal to zero.

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

In the late 1990s, the Yamabe invariant was computed for large classes of 4-manifolds by [Claude LeBrun](wikipedia://en/38381127) and his collaborators. In particular, it was shown that most compact complex surfaces have negative, exactly computable Yamabe invariant, and that any Kähler–Einstein metric of negative scalar curvature realizes the Yamabe invariant in dimension 4. It was also shown that the Yamabe invariant of $CP^2$ is realized by the [Fubini–Study metric](wikipedia://en/2574486), and so is less than that of the 4-sphere. Most of these arguments involve [Seiberg–Witten theory](wikipedia://en/3526032), and so are specific to dimension 4.

An important result due to Petean states that if $M$ is simply connected and has dimension $n \geq 5$, then $\sigma (M) \geq 0$. In light of Perelman's solution of the [Poincaré conjecture](wikipedia://en/23798), it follows that a simply connected $n$-manifold can have negative Yamabe invariant only if $n=4$. On the other hand, as has already been indicated, simply connected $4$-manifolds do in fact often have negative Yamabe invariants.

Below is a table of some smooth manifolds of dimension three with known Yamabe invariant. In dimension 3, the number $\mathcal{E}(g_0)$ is equal to $6(2\pi^2)^{2/3}$ and is often denoted $\sigma_1$.

::: {#o7 .table cue=table}

| $M$ | $\sigma(M)$ | notes |
|---|---|---|
| [$S^3$](wikipedia://en/27859) | $\sigma_1$ | the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) |
| $S^2 \times S^1$ | $\sigma_1$ | the trivial 2-sphere bundle over $S^1$[^o9] |
| $S^2 \stackrel{\sim}{\times} S^1$ | $\sigma_1$ | the unique non-orientable 2-sphere bundle over $S^1$ |
| [$\mathbb{R} \mathbb{P}^3$](wikipedia://en/1014694) | $\sigma_1/2^{2/3}$ | computed by Bray and Neves |
| $\mathbb{R} \mathbb{P}^2 \times S^1$ | $\sigma_1/2^{2/3}$ | computed by Bray and Neves |
| [$T^3$](wikipedia://en/74800) | $0$ | the [3-torus](wikipedia://en/74800) |

:::

By an argument due to Anderson, Perelman's results on the [Ricci flow](wikipedia://en/288291) imply that the constant-curvature metric on any [hyperbolic 3-manifold](wikipedia://en/1237700) realizes the Yamabe invariant. This provides us with infinitely many examples of 3-manifolds for which the invariant is both negative and exactly computable.

:::

## Topological significance {#Topological_significance}

The sign of the Yamabe invariant of $M$ holds important topological information. For example, $\sigma(M)$ is positive if and only if $M$ admits a metric of positive scalar curvature.[^o13] The significance of this fact is that much is known about the topology of manifolds with metrics of positive scalar curvature.
