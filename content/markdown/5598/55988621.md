# Genetic assignment methods

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Genetic assignment methods"}

**Genetic assignment methods** are powerful [statistical methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_methods) used to determine the relationship between individuals and [populations](wikipedia://en/22949).[^o2] The general principle behind these methods is to utilize [multilocus genotypes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multilocus_genotype) to assign individuals to reference populations based on their origins.[^o3]

:::

## Genetic assignment methods {#Genetic_assignment_methods}

### Frequency method {#Frequency_method}

This method was first presented by Paetkau et al. in 1995.[^o6] It assigns an individual to the reference population based on the [likelihood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Likelihood) of that individual's [genotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genotype) in the population. This method assumes [Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardy–Weinberg_equilibrium) and independence of loci, as well as an unstated assumption that is the deduced population sample [allelic frequencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allele_frequency) are close to the exact values.[^o7] This method involves three steps:[^o7]

1. Computing the required allelic frequencies in all candidate populations.
1. Computing the likelihoods of the individual's [multilocus genotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multilocus_genotype) occurring in each population.
1. Assigning the individual to the population where the likelihood of the individual's [genotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genotype) is the highest.

### Bayesian model {#Bayesian_model}

Inspired by Rannala and Mountain,[^o9] this method was presented in their 1997 paper, which emphasized a [Bayesian approach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_approach) was used to detect [immigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration). It assumes that each [locus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locus_%28genetics%29)’ allelic frequencies in each population have an equal [prior probability](wikipedia://en/472877). The [marginal probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_probability) of observing an individual with genotype $\scriptstyle A_k A_{k'}$ at locus *j* in population *i* is equal to[^o9]

$$\begin{aligned} & \frac{\left(\frac{n_{ijk}+1}{K_j+1}\right) \left(\frac{n_{ijk}+1}{K_j}\right)}{(n_{ij}+2)(n_{ij}+1)} & & \text{ for } k = k' \\[6pt] & \frac{2 \left( \frac{n_{ijk}+1}{K_j} \right) \left( \frac{n_{ijk'}+1}{K_j} \right) }{(n_{ij}+2)(n_{ij}+1)} & & \text{ for } k \neq k' \end{aligned}$$

*n*<sub>*ijk*</sub> is the number of [alleles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alleles) *k* sampled at locus *j* in population *i*, *n*<sub>*ij*</sub> is the number of [gene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene) copies sampled at locus *j* in population *i*, and *kj* is the total number of [alleles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alleles) observed in the whole populations at locus *j*.

### Distance method {#Distance_method}

Introduced by Cornuet et al. in 1999,[^o7] this method uses [genetic distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_distance) to assign the individual to the “closest” population. For the interpopulation distances, an individual is assigned as a sample of two [alleles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alleles); for shared allele distance, the measure is the average distance between the individual and the population samples. Note this method does not assume [Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardy–Weinberg_equilibrium) or independence of [loci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locus_%28genetics%29).[^o7]
