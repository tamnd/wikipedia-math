# Lovász number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lovász number"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **Lovász number** of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) is a [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) that is an [upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound) on the [Shannon capacity](wikipedia://en/39932177) of the graph. It is also known as **Lovász theta function** and is commonly denoted by $\vartheta(G)$, using a script form of the Greek letter [theta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theta) to contrast with the upright theta used for Shannon capacity. This quantity was first introduced by [László Lovász](wikipedia://en/1623938) in his 1979 paper *On the Shannon Capacity of a Graph*.[^n1]

:::

Accurate numerical approximations to this number can be computed in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time) by [semidefinite programming](wikipedia://en/4993539) and the [ellipsoid method](wikipedia://en/4331203). The Lovász number of the [complement](wikipedia://en/1175649) of any graph is sandwiched between the [chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number) and [clique number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clique_number) of the graph, and can be used to compute these numbers on graphs for which they are equal, including [perfect graphs](wikipedia://en/670531).

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $G=(V,E)$ be a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) on $n$ vertices. An ordered set of $n$ [unit vectors](wikipedia://en/167053) $U=(u_i\mid i\in V)\subset\mathbb{R}^N$ is called an **orthonormal representation** of $G$ in $\mathbb{R}^N$, if $u_i$ and $u_j$ are orthogonal whenever vertices $i$ and $j$ are not adjacent in $G$:

$$u_i^\mathrm{T} u_j = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if }i = j, \\ 0, & \text{if }(i,j) \notin E. \end{cases}$$

Clearly, every graph admits an orthonormal representation with $N=n$: just represent vertices by distinct vectors from the [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005) of $\mathbb{R}^N$.[^o5] Depending on the graph it might be possible to take $N$ considerably smaller than the number of vertices $n$.

The **Lovász number** $\vartheta$ of graph $G$ is defined as follows:

$$\vartheta(G) = \min_{c, U} \max_{i \in V} \frac{1}{(c^\mathrm{T} u_i)^2},$$

where $c$ is a unit vector in $\mathbb{R}^N$ and $U$ is an orthonormal representation of $G$ in $\mathbb{R}^N$. Here minimization implicitly is performed also over the dimension $N$, however [without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000) it suffices to consider $N=n$.[^o6] Intuitively, this corresponds to minimizing the half-angle of a rotational [cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_%28geometry%29) containing all representing vectors of an orthonormal representation of $G$. If the optimal angle is $\phi$, then $\vartheta(G)=1/\cos^2\phi$ and $c$ corresponds to the symmetry axis of the cone.[^n2]

:::

## Equivalent expressions {#Equivalent_expressions}

Let $G=(V,E)$ be a graph on $n$ vertices. Let $A$ range over all $n\times n$ [symmetric matrices](wikipedia://en/126474) such that $a_{ij}=1$ whenever $i=j$ or vertices $i$ and $j$ are not adjacent, and let $\lambda_{\max}(A)$ denote the largest [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of $A$. Then an alternative way of computing the Lovász number of $G$ is as follows:[^n3]

$$\vartheta(G) = \min_A \lambda_{\max}(A).$$

The following method is dual to the previous one. Let $B$ range over all $n\times n$ symmetric [positive semidefinite matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_semidefinite_matrix) such that $b_{ij}=0$ whenever vertices $i$ and $j$ are adjacent, and such that the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) (sum of diagonal entries) of $B$ is $\operatorname{Tr}(B)=1$. Let $J$ be the $n\times n$ [matrix of ones](wikipedia://en/690246). Then[^n4]

$$\vartheta(G) = \max_B \operatorname{Tr}(BJ).$$

Here, $\operatorname{Tr}(BJ)$ is just the sum of all entries of $B$.

The Lovász number can be computed also in terms of the [complement graph](wikipedia://en/1175649) $\bar G$. Let $d$ be a unit vector and $U=(u_i\mid i\in V)$ be an orthonormal representation of $\bar G$. Then[^n5]

$$\vartheta(G) = \max_{d,U} \sum_{i \in V} (d^\mathrm{T} u_i)^2.$$

## Value for well-known graphs {#Value_for_well-known_graphs}

The Lovász number has been computed for the following graphs:[^n6]

::: {#o14 .table cue=table}

| Graph | Lovász number |
|---|---|
| [Complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) | $\vartheta(K_n) = 1$ |
| [Empty graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empty_graph) | $\vartheta(\bar{K}_n) = n$ |
| [Pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014) graph | $\vartheta(C_5) = \sqrt{5}$ |
| [Cycle graphs](wikipedia://en/878962) | $\vartheta(C_n) = \begin{cases} \frac{n \cos(\pi/n)}{1 + \cos(\pi/n)} & \text{for odd } n, \\ \frac{n}{2} & \text{for even } n \end{cases}$ |
| [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926) | $\vartheta(KG_{5,2}) = 4$ |
| [Kneser graphs](wikipedia://en/2724154) | $\vartheta(KG_{n,k}) = \binom{n-1}{k-1}$ |
| [Complete multipartite graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_multipartite_graph) | $\vartheta(K_{n_1,\dots,n_k}) = \max_{1 \leq i \leq k} n_i$ |

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

If $G \boxtimes H$ denotes the [strong graph product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_graph_product) of graphs $G$ and $H$, then[^n7]

$$\vartheta(G \boxtimes H) = \vartheta(G) \vartheta(H).$$

If $\bar G$ is the complement of $G$, then[^n8]

$$\vartheta(G) \vartheta(\bar{G}) \geq n,$$

with equality if $G$ is [vertex-transitive](wikipedia://en/581175).

:::

::: {#o27 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Lovász \"sandwich theorem\""}

## Lovász "sandwich theorem" {#Lovász_"sandwich_theorem"}

The Lovász "sandwich theorem" states that the Lovász number always lies between two other numbers that are [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete) to compute.[^n9] More precisely,

$$\omega(G) \leq \vartheta(\bar{G}) \leq \chi(G),$$

where $\omega(G)$ is the [clique number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clique_number) of $G$ (the size of the largest [clique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clique)) and $\chi(G)$ is the [chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number) of $G$ (the smallest number of colors needed to color the vertices of $G$ so that no two adjacent vertices receive the same color).

The value of $\vartheta(G)$ can be formulated as a [semidefinite program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semidefinite_program) and numerically approximated by the [ellipsoid method](wikipedia://en/4331203) in time bounded by a [polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time) in the number of vertices of *G*.[^o29] For [perfect graphs](wikipedia://en/670531), the chromatic number and clique number are equal, and therefore are both equal to $\vartheta(\bar{G})$. By computing an approximation of $\vartheta(\bar{G})$ and then rounding to the nearest [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) value, the chromatic number and clique number of these graphs can be computed in polynomial time.

:::

## Relation to Shannon capacity {#Relation_to_Shannon_capacity}

The [Shannon capacity](wikipedia://en/39932177) of graph $G$ is defined as follows:

$$\Theta(G) = \sup_k \sqrt[k]{\alpha(G^k)} = \lim_{k \rightarrow \infty} \sqrt[k]{\alpha(G^k)},$$

where $\alpha(G)$ is the [independence number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_number) of [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) $G$ (the size of a largest [independent set](wikipedia://en/524501) of $G$) and $G^k$ is the [strong graph product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_graph_product) of $G$ with itself $k$ times. Clearly, $\Theta(G)\ge\alpha(G)$. However, the Lovász number provides an upper bound on the Shannon capacity of graph,[^n10] hence

$$\alpha(G) \leq \Theta(G) \leq \vartheta(G).$$

*Image: Cycle graph C5.png.* Pentagon graph

For example, let the confusability graph of the channel be $C_5$, a [pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014). Since the original paper of (Shannon 1956) it was an [open problem](wikipedia://en/1333335) to determine the value of $\Theta(C_5)$. It was first established by (Lovász 1979) that $\Theta(C_5)=\sqrt5$.

Clearly, $\Theta(C_5)\ge\alpha(C_5)=2$. However, $\alpha(C_5^2)\ge 5$, since "11", "23", "35", "42", "54" are five mutually non-confusable messages (forming a five-vertex independent set in the strong square of $C_5$), thus $\Theta(C_5)\ge\sqrt5$.

To show that this bound is tight, let $U=(u_1,\dots,u_5)$ be the following orthonormal representation of the pentagon:

$$u_k = \begin{pmatrix} \cos{\theta} \\ \sin{\theta} \cos{\varphi_k} \\ \sin{\theta} \sin{\varphi_k} \end{pmatrix}, \quad \cos{\theta} = \frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{5}}, \quad \varphi_k = \frac{2 \pi k}{5}$$

and let $c=(1,0,0)$. By using this choice in the initial definition of Lovász number, we get $\vartheta(C_5)\le\sqrt5$. Hence, $\Theta(C_5)=\sqrt5$.

However, there exist graphs for which the Lovász number and Shannon capacity differ, so the Lovász number cannot in general be used to compute exact Shannon capacities.[^n11]

## Quantum physics {#Quantum_physics}

The Lovász number has been generalized for "non-commutative graphs" in the context of [quantum communication](wikipedia://en/1013550).[^n12] The Lovasz number also arises in [quantum contextuality](wikipedia://en/38407148)[^n13] in an attempt to explain the power of [quantum computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_computers).[^n14]

## See also {#See_also}

- [Tardos function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tardos_function), a monotone approximation to the Lovász number of the [complement graph](wikipedia://en/1175649) that can be computed in polynomial time and has been used to prove lower bounds in monotone [circuit complexity](wikipedia://en/7641969).
