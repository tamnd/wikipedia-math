# Weak derivative

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Weak derivative"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **weak derivative** is a generalization of the concept of the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) (*strong derivative*) for functions not assumed [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206), but only [integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_function), i.e., to lie in the [L<sup>*p*</sup> space](wikipedia://en/45194) $L^1([a,b])$.

:::

The method of [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252) holds that for smooth functions $u$ and $\varphi$ we have

$$\begin{aligned} \int_a^b u(x) \varphi'(x) \, dx & = \Big[u(x) \varphi(x)\Big]_a^b - \int_a^b u'(x) \varphi(x) \, dx. \\[6pt] \end{aligned}$$

A function *u*' being the weak derivative of *u* is essentially defined by the requirement that this equation must hold for all smooth functions $\varphi$ vanishing at the boundary points ($\varphi(a)=\varphi(b)=0$).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $u$ be a function in the [Lebesgue space](wikipedia://en/45194) $L^1([a,b])$. We say that $v$ in $L^1([a,b])$ is a **weak derivative** of $u$ if

$$\int_a^b u(t)\varphi'(t) \, dt=-\int_a^b v(t)\varphi(t) \, dt$$

for *all* infinitely [differentiable functions](wikipedia://en/330206) $\varphi$ with $\varphi(a)=\varphi(b)=0$.[^o4][^o5]

Generalizing to $n$ dimensions, if $u$ and $v$ are in the space $L_\text{loc}^1(U)$ of [locally integrable functions](wikipedia://en/4258398) for some [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) $U \subset \mathbb{R}^n$, and if $\alpha$ is a [multi-index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-index), we say that $v$ is the $\alpha^\text{th}$-weak derivative of $u$ if

$$\int_U u D^\alpha \varphi=(-1)^{|\alpha|} \int_U v\varphi,$$

for all $\varphi \in C^\infty_c (U)$, that is, for all infinitely differentiable functions $\varphi$ with [compact support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support) in $U$. Here $D^{\alpha}\varphi$ is defined as

$$D^{\alpha}\varphi = \frac{\partial^{| \alpha |} \varphi }{\partial x_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots \partial x_n^{\alpha_n}}.$$

If $u$ has a weak derivative, it is often written $D^{\alpha}u$ since weak derivatives are unique (at least, up to a set of [measure zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_zero), see below).[^o6]

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) function $u : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}_+, u(t) = |t|$, which is not differentiable at $t = 0$ has a weak derivative $v: \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ known as the [sign function](wikipedia://en/505449), and given by $$v(t) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } t > 0; \\[6pt] 0 & \text{if } t = 0; \\[6pt] -1 & \text{if } t < 0. \end{cases}$$ This is not the only weak derivative for *u*: any *w* that is equal to *v* [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688) is also a weak derivative for *u*. For example, the definition of *v*(0) above could be replaced with any desired real number. Usually, the existence of multiple solutions is not a problem, since functions are considered to be equivalent in the theory of [*L*<sup>*p*</sup> spaces](wikipedia://en/45194) and [Sobolev spaces](wikipedia://en/611964) if they are equal almost everywhere.
- The [characteristic function](wikipedia://en/240790) of the rational numbers $1_{\mathbb{Q}}$ is nowhere differentiable yet has a weak derivative. Since the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) of the rational numbers is zero, $$\int 1_{\mathbb{Q}}(t) \varphi(t) \, dt = 0.$$ Thus $v(t)=0$ is a weak derivative of $1_{\mathbb{Q}}$. Note that this does agree with our intuition since when considered as a member of an L<sup>*p*</sup> space, $1_{\mathbb{Q}}$ is identified with the zero function.
- The [Cantor function](wikipedia://en/320819) *c* does not have a weak derivative, despite being differentiable almost everywhere. This is because any weak derivative of *c* would have to be equal almost everywhere to the classical derivative of *c*, which is zero almost everywhere. But the zero function is not a weak derivative of *c*, as can be seen by comparing against an appropriate test function $\varphi$. More theoretically, *c* does not have a weak derivative because its [distributional derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributional_derivative), namely the [Cantor distribution](wikipedia://en/387941), is a [singular measure](wikipedia://en/4304500) and therefore cannot be represented by a function.

:::

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

If two functions are weak derivatives of the same function, they are equal except on a set with [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) zero, i.e., they are equal [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688). If we consider [equivalence classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalence_classes) of functions such that two functions are equivalent if they are equal almost everywhere, then the weak derivative is unique.

Also, if *u* is differentiable in the conventional sense then its weak derivative is identical (in the sense given above) to its conventional (strong) derivative. Thus the weak derivative is a generalization of the strong one. Furthermore, the classical rules for derivatives of sums and products of functions also hold for the weak derivative.

:::

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Extensions"}

## Extensions {#Extensions}

This concept gives rise to the definition of [weak solutions](wikipedia://en/3555416) in [Sobolev spaces](wikipedia://en/611964), which are useful for problems of [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations) and in [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180).

:::
