# Test statistic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Test statistic"}

**Test [statistic](wikipedia://en/26703)** is a quantity derived from the [sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_%28statistics%29) for [statistical hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing).[^o2] A hypothesis test is typically specified in terms of a test statistic, considered as a numerical summary of a data-set that reduces the data to one value that can be used to perform the hypothesis test. In general, a test statistic is selected or defined in such a way as to quantify, within observed data, behaviours that would distinguish the [null](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis) from the [alternative hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_hypothesis), where such an alternative is prescribed, or that would characterize the null hypothesis if there is no explicitly stated alternative hypothesis.

:::

An important property of a test statistic is that its [sampling distribution](wikipedia://en/520670) under the null hypothesis must be calculable, either exactly or approximately, which allows [*p*-values](wikipedia://en/554994) to be calculated. A *test statistic* shares some of the same qualities of a [descriptive statistic](wikipedia://en/8187), and many statistics can be used as both test statistics and descriptive statistics. However, a test statistic is specifically intended for use in statistical testing, whereas the main quality of a descriptive statistic is that it is easily interpretable. Some informative descriptive statistics, such as the [sample range](wikipedia://en/27588), do not make good test statistics since it is difficult to determine their sampling distribution.

Two widely used test statistics are the [t-statistic](wikipedia://en/22474343) and the [F-statistic](wikipedia://en/318976).

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Suppose the task is to test whether a coin is [fair](wikipedia://en/9597100) (i.e. has equal probabilities of producing a head or a tail). If the coin is flipped 100 times and the results are recorded, the raw data can be represented as a sequence of 100 heads and tails. If there is interest in the [marginal](wikipedia://en/506077) probability of obtaining a tail, only the number *T* out of the 100 flips that produced a tail needs to be recorded. But *T* can also be used as a test statistic in one of two ways:

- the exact [sampling distribution](wikipedia://en/520670) of *T* under the null hypothesis is the [binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876) with parameters 0.5 and 100.
- the value of *T* can be compared with its expected value under the null hypothesis of 50, and since the sample size is large, a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) can be used as an approximation to the sampling distribution either for *T* or for the revised test statistic *T*−50.

Using one of these sampling distributions, it is possible to compute either a [one-tailed or two-tailed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-tailed_test) p-value for the null hypothesis that the coin is fair. The test statistic in this case reduces a set of 100 numbers to a single numerical summary that can be used for testing.

:::

## Common test statistics {#Common_test_statistics}

**One-sample tests** are appropriate when a sample is being compared to the [population](wikipedia://en/27585) from a hypothesis. The population characteristics are known from theory or are calculated from the population.

**Two-sample tests** are appropriate for comparing two samples, typically experimental and control samples from a [scientifically controlled](wikipedia://en/1640288) experiment.

**Paired tests** are appropriate for comparing two samples where it is impossible to control important variables. Rather than comparing two sets, members are paired between samples so the difference between the members becomes the sample. Typically the mean of the differences is then compared to zero. The common example scenario for when a [paired difference test](wikipedia://en/23004551) is appropriate is when a single set of test subjects has something applied to them and the test is intended to check for an effect.

[Z-tests](wikipedia://en/332213) are appropriate for comparing means under stringent conditions regarding normality and a known standard deviation.

A [*t*-test](wikipedia://en/536080) is appropriate for comparing means under relaxed conditions (less is assumed).

Tests of proportions are analogous to tests of means (the 50% proportion).

[Chi-squared tests](wikipedia://en/226680) use the same calculations and the same probability distribution for different applications:

- Chi-squared tests for variance are used to determine whether a normal population has a specified variance. The null hypothesis is that it does.
- Chi-squared tests of independence are used for deciding whether two variables are associated or are independent. The variables are [categorical](wikipedia://en/821165) rather than numeric. It can be used to decide whether [left-handedness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-handedness) is correlated with height (or not). The null hypothesis is that the variables are independent. The numbers used in the calculation are the observed and expected frequencies of occurrence (from [contingency tables](wikipedia://en/935515)).
- Chi-squared [goodness of fit](wikipedia://en/2474821) tests are used to determine the adequacy of curves fit to data. The null hypothesis is that the curve fit is adequate. It is common to determine curve shapes to minimize the [mean square error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_square_error), so it is appropriate that the goodness-of-fit calculation sums the squared errors.

[F-tests](wikipedia://en/318976) ([analysis of variance](wikipedia://en/634), ANOVA) are commonly used when deciding whether groupings of data by category are meaningful. If the variance of test scores of the left-handed in a class is much smaller than the variance of the whole class, then it may be useful to study lefties as a group. The null hypothesis is that two variances are the same – so the proposed grouping is not meaningful.

| Two-proportion z-test, pooled for $H_0\colon p_1=p_2$ | $z=\frac{(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2)}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2})}}$ $\hat{p}=\frac{x_1 + x_2}{n_1 + n_2}$ | *n*<sub>1</sub> *p*<sub>1</sub> > 5 **and** *n*<sub>1</sub>(1 − *p*<sub>1</sub>) > 5 **and** *n*<sub>2</sub> *p*<sub>2</sub> > 5 **and** *n*<sub>2</sub>(1 − *p*<sub>2</sub>) > 5 **and** independent observations, see [notes](wikipedia://en/3876#Normal_approximation). |
| Two-proportion z-test, unpooled for $\|d_0\|>0$ | $z=\frac{(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) - d_0}{\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1 - \hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{p}_2(1 - \hat{p}_2)}{n_2}}}$ | *n*<sub>1</sub> *p*<sub>1</sub> > 5 **and** *n*<sub>1</sub>(1 − *p*<sub>1</sub>) > 5 **and** *n*<sub>2</sub> *p*<sub>2</sub> > 5 **and** *n*<sub>2</sub>(1 − *p*<sub>2</sub>) > 5 **and** independent observations, see [notes](wikipedia://en/3876#Normal_approximation). |

| Chi-squared test for [goodness of fit](wikipedia://en/2474821) | $\chi^2=\sum_k\frac{(\text{observed}-\text{expected})^2}{\text{expected}}$ | *df = k* − 1 − *\# parameters estimated*, and one of these must hold. • All expected counts are at least 5.[^o14]p. 350 • All expected counts are > 1 and no more than 20% of expected counts are less than 5[^o15] |
| Two-sample F test for equality of variances | $F=\frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2}$ | Normal populations Arrange so $s_1^2 \ge s_2^2$ and reject H<sub>0</sub> for $F > F(\alpha/2,n_1-1,n_2-1)$[^o16] |
| [Regression](wikipedia://en/826997#Diagnostics) *t*-test of $H_0\colon R^2=0.$ | $t=\sqrt{\frac{R^2(n-k-1^*)}{1-R^2}}$ | Reject *H*<sub>0</sub> for $t > t(\alpha/2,n-k-1^*)$[^o14]p. 288 \*Subtract 1 for intercept; *k* terms contain independent variables. |
