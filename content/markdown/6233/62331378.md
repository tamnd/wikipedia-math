# Markov constant

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Markov constant"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), specifically in [Diophantine approximation](wikipedia://en/251558) theory, the **Markov constant** $M(\alpha)$ of an [irrational number](wikipedia://en/20647689) $\alpha$ is the factor for which [Dirichlet's approximation theorem](wikipedia://en/2770230) can be improved for $\alpha$.

:::

## History and motivation {#History_and_motivation}

Certain numbers can be approximated well by certain [rationals](wikipedia://en/19727024); specifically, the [convergents of the continued fraction](wikipedia://en/739199#Infinite_continued_fractions_and_convergents) are the best [approximations by rational numbers](wikipedia://en/251558) having denominators less than a certain bound. For example, the approximation $\pi\approx\frac{22}{7}$ is the best rational approximation among rational numbers with denominator up to 56.[^o4] Also, some numbers can be approximated more readily than others. [Dirichlet](wikipedia://en/240002) proved in 1840 that the least readily approximable numbers are the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), in the sense that for every irrational number there exists infinitely many rational numbers approximating it to a certain degree of accuracy that only finitely many such [rational approximations](wikipedia://en/251558) exist for rational numbers. Specifically, he proved that for any number $\alpha$ there are infinitely many pairs of relatively prime numbers $(p,q)$ such that $\left|\alpha - \frac{p}{q}\right| < \frac{1}{q^2}$ if and only if $\alpha$ is irrational.

51 years later, [Hurwitz](wikipedia://en/771690) further improved [Dirichlet's approximation theorem](wikipedia://en/2770230) by a factor of $\sqrt{5}$,[^o5] improving the right-hand side from $1/q^2$ to $1/\sqrt{5}q^2$ for irrational numbers:

$$\left|\alpha - \frac{p}{q}\right| < \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}q^2}.$$

The above result is best possible since the [golden ratio $\phi$](wikipedia://en/12386) is irrational but if we replace $\sqrt{5}$ by any larger number in the above expression then we will only be able to find finitely many rational numbers that satisfy the inequality for $\alpha=\phi$.

Furthermore, he showed that among the irrational numbers, the least readily approximable numbers are those of the form $\frac{a\phi+b}{c\phi+d}$ where $\phi$ is the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386), $a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $ad-bc=\pm1$.[^o6] (These numbers are said to be *[equivalent](wikipedia://en/251558#Equivalent_real_numbers)* to $\phi$.) If we omit these numbers, just as we omitted the rational numbers in Dirichlet's theorem, then we *can* increase the number $\sqrt{5}$ to 2$\sqrt{2}$. Again this new bound is best possible in the new setting, but this time the number [$\sqrt{2}$](wikipedia://en/341682), and numbers equivalent to it, limits the bound.[^o6] If we don't allow those numbers then we *can* again increase the number on the right hand side of the inequality from 2$\sqrt{2}$ to $\sqrt{221}$/5,[^o6] for which the numbers equivalent to $\frac{1+\sqrt{221}}{10}$ limit the bound. The numbers generated show how well these numbers can be approximated; this can be seen as a property of the real numbers.

However, instead of considering Hurwitz's theorem (and the extensions mentioned above) as a property of the real numbers except certain special numbers, we can consider it as a property of each excluded number. Thus, the theorem can be interpreted as "numbers equivalent to $\phi$, $\sqrt{2}$ or $\frac{1+\sqrt{221}}{10}$ are among the least readily approximable irrational numbers." This leads us to consider how accurately each number can be approximated by rationals - specifically, by how much can the factor in [Dirichlet's approximation theorem](wikipedia://en/2770230) be increased to from 1 for *that specific* number.

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Mathematically, the Markov constant of irrational $\alpha$ is defined as $M(\alpha)=\sup \left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R} : \left\vert \alpha - \frac{p}{q} \right\vert<\frac{1}{\lambda q^2} \text{ has infinitely many solutions for }p,q\in\mathbb{N} \right\}$.[^o9] If the set does not have an upper bound we define $M(\alpha)=\infty$.

Alternatively, it can be defined as $\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k^2\left\vert \alpha-\frac{f(k)}{k} \right\vert}$ where $f(k)$ is defined as the closest integer to $\alpha k$.

:::

## Properties and results {#Properties_and_results}

[Hurwitz's theorem](wikipedia://en/23190613) implies that $M(\alpha)\ge\sqrt{5}$ for all $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}-\mathbb{Q}$.

If $\alpha = [a_0; a_1, a_2, ...]$ is its [continued fraction](wikipedia://en/739199) expansion then $M(\alpha)=\limsup_{k\to\infty}{([a_{k+1}; a_{k+2}, a_{k+3}, ...] + [0; a_{k}, a_{k-1}, ...,a_2,a_1])}$.[^o9]

From the above, if $p=\limsup_{k\to\infty}{a_k}$ then $p<M(\alpha)<p+2$. This implies that $M(\alpha)=\infty$ if and only if [$(a_k)$ is not bounded](wikipedia://en/10989485). In particular, $M(\alpha)<\infty$ if $\alpha$ is a [quadratic irrational number](wikipedia://en/305331). In fact, the lower bound for $M(\alpha)$ can be strengthened to $M(\alpha)\ge\sqrt{p^2+4}$, the tightest possible.[^o11]

The values of $\alpha$ for which $M(\alpha)<3$ are families of quadratic irrationalities having the same period (but at different offsets), and the values of $M(\alpha)$ for these $\alpha$ are limited to [Lagrange numbers](wikipedia://en/16075933). There are [uncountably](wikipedia://en/32248) many numbers for which $M(\alpha)=3$, no two of which have the same ending; for instance, for each number $\alpha = [\underbrace{1;1,...,1}_{r_1},2,2,\underbrace{1,1,...,1}_{r_2},2,2,\underbrace{1,1,...,1}_{r_3},2,2,...]$ where $r_1<r_2<r_3<\cdots$, $M(\alpha)=3$.[^o9]

If $\beta=\frac{p\alpha+q}{r\alpha+s}$ where $p,q,r,s\in\mathbb{Z}$ then $M(\beta)\ge\frac{M(\alpha)}{\left\vert ps-rq \right\vert}$.[^o12] In particular if $\left\vert ps-rq \right\vert=1$ then $M(\beta)=M(\alpha)$.[^o13]

The set $L=\{M(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in\mathbb{R}-\mathbb{Q}\}$ forms the [Lagrange spectrum](wikipedia://en/19043396#Lagrange_spectrum). It contains the interval $[F,\infty]$ where *F* is Freiman's constant.[^o13] Hence, if $m>F\approx4.52783$ then there exists irrational $\alpha$ whose Markov constant is $m$.

### Numbers having a Markov constant less than 3 {#Numbers_having_a_Markov_constant_less_than_3}

Burger et al. (2002)[^o15] provides a formula for which the quadratic irrationality $\alpha_n$ whose Markov constant is the n<sup>th</sup> [Lagrange number](wikipedia://en/16075933):

$\alpha_n=\frac{2u-3m_n+\sqrt{9m_n^2-4}}{2m_n}$ where $m_n$ is the n<sup>th</sup> [Markov number](wikipedia://en/2965726), and $u$ is the smallest positive integer such that $m_n\mid u^2+1$.

Nicholls (1978)[^o16] provides a geometric proof of this (based on circles tangent to each other), providing a method that these numbers can be systematically found.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Markov constant of two numbers {#Markov_constant_of_two_numbers}

Since $\frac{\sqrt{10}}{2}=[1;\overline{1,1,2}]$,

$$\begin{aligned} M\left ( \frac{\sqrt{10}}{2} \right ) & = \max([1;\overline{2,1,1}]+[0;\overline{1,2,1}],[1;\overline{1,2,1}]+[0;\overline{2,1,1}],[2;\overline{1,1,2}]+[0;\overline{1,1,2}]) \\ & = \max\left ( \frac{2\sqrt{10}}{3},\frac{2\sqrt{10}}{3},\sqrt{10} \right ) \\ & = \sqrt{10}. \end{aligned}$$

As $e = [2; 1, 2, 1, 1, 4, 1, 1, 6, 1, \ldots, 1, 2n, 1, \ldots], M(e)=\infty$ because the [continued fraction representation of $e$](wikipedia://en/9633#Representations) is unbounded.

### Numbers α<sub>n</sub> having Markov constant less than 3 {#Numbers_&alpha;n_having_Markov_constant_less_than_3}

Consider $n=6$; Then $m_n=34$. By trial and error it can be found that $u=13$. Then

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha_6 & = \frac{2u-3m_6+\sqrt{9m_6^2-4}}{2m_6} \\[6pt] & = \frac{-76+\sqrt{10400}}{68} \\[6pt] &= \frac{-19+5\sqrt{26}}{17} \\[6pt] &=[0;\overline{2,1,1,1,1,1,1,2}]. \end{aligned}$$
