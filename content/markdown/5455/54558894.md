# Sixth power

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sixth power"}

*Image: sixth\_power\_example.svg.* 64 (2<sup>6</sup>) and 729 (3<sup>6</sup>) cubelets arranged as cubes ((2${}^{2}$)${}^{3}$ and (3${}^{2}$)${}^{3}$, respectively) and as squares ((2${}^{3}$)${}^{2}$ and (3${}^{3}$)${}^{2}$, respectively)

In [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118) and [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) the **sixth power** of a [number](wikipedia://en/21690) *n* is the result of multiplying six instances of *n* together. So:

:::

$$n^{6} = n \times n \times n \times n \times n \times n.$$

Sixth [powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_%28algebra%29) can be formed by multiplying a number by its [fifth power](wikipedia://en/40150949), multiplying the [square](wikipedia://en/659942) of a number by its [fourth power](wikipedia://en/665027), by [cubing](wikipedia://en/451445) a square, or by squaring a cube.

The sequence of sixth powers of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) are:

0, 1, 64, 729, 4096, 15625, 46656, 117649, 262144, 531441, 1000000, 1771561, 2985984, 4826809, 7529536, 11390625, 16777216, 24137569, 34012224, 47045881, 64000000, 85766121, 113379904, 148035889, 191102976, 244140625, 308915776, 387420489, 481890304, ...

They include the significant [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214) numbers 10<sup>6</sup> (a [million](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Million)), 100<sup>6</sup> (a [short-scale trillion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trillion_%28short_scale%29) and long-scale billion), 1000<sup>6</sup> (a [quintillion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintillion) and a [long-scale trillion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trillion_%28long_scale%29)) and so on.

## Squares and cubes {#Squares_and_cubes}

The sixth powers of integers can be characterized as the numbers that are simultaneously squares and cubes.[^o3] In this way, they are analogous to two other classes of [figurate numbers](wikipedia://en/325260): the [square triangular numbers](wikipedia://en/416823), which are simultaneously square and triangular, and the solutions to the [cannonball problem](wikipedia://en/28045698), which are simultaneously square and square-pyramidal.

Because of their connection to squares and cubes, sixth powers play an important role in the study of the [Mordell curves](wikipedia://en/3132697), which are [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225) of the form

$$y^2=x^3+k.$$

When $k$ is divisible by a sixth power, this equation can be reduced by dividing by that power to give a simpler equation of the same form. A well-known result in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) by [Rudolf Fueter](wikipedia://en/42010211) and [Louis J. Mordell](wikipedia://en/991484), states that, when $k$ is an integer that is not divisible by a sixth power (other than the exceptional cases $k=1$ and $k=-432$), this equation either has no [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) solutions with both $x$ and $y$ nonzero or infinitely many of them.[^o4]

In the [archaic notation](wikipedia://en/31228704) of [Robert Recorde](wikipedia://en/310247), the sixth power of a number was called the "zenzicube", meaning the square of a cube. Similarly, the notation for sixth powers used in 12th century [Indian mathematics](wikipedia://en/1848052) by [Bhāskara II](wikipedia://en/480509) also called them either the square of a cube or the cube of a square.[^o5]

## Sums {#Sums}

There are numerous known examples of sixth powers that can be expressed as the sum of seven other sixth powers, but no examples are yet known of a sixth power expressible as the sum of just six sixth powers.[^o7] This makes it unique among the powers with exponent *k* = 1, 2, ... , 8, the others of which can each be expressed as the sum of *k* other *k*-th powers, and some of which (in violation of [Euler's sum of powers conjecture](wikipedia://en/9660)) can be expressed as a sum of even fewer *k*-th powers.

In connection with [Waring's problem](wikipedia://en/33569), every sufficiently large integer can be represented as a sum of at most 24 sixth powers of integers.[^o8]

There are infinitely many different nontrivial solutions to the [Diophantine equation](wikipedia://en/9109)[^o9]

$$a^6+b^6+c^6=d^6+e^6+f^6.$$

It has not been proven whether the equation

$$a^6+b^6=c^6+d^6$$

has a nontrivial solution,[^o10] but the [Lander, Parkin, and Selfridge conjecture](wikipedia://en/40052960) would imply that it does not.

## Other properties {#Other_properties}

- $n^6-1$ is divisible by 7 if *n* is not divisible by 7.
