# Arc length

*Image: Arc length.gif.* When rectified, the curve gives a straight line segment with the same length as the curve's arc length.

*Image: Logarithmic spiral arc length.gif.* Arc length *s* of a [logarithmic spiral](wikipedia://en/42373) as a function of its parameter *θ*

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Arc length"}

**Arc length** is the length of a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) from beginning to end. Conceptually, if we imagined the curve as a string, the arc length is how long the string would be if we straightened it out. It can be formalized mathematically for [smooth curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_curve) using [vector calculus](wikipedia://en/32640) and [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), or for curves that might not necessarily be smooth as a [least upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_upper_bound) of lengths of [polygonal chains](wikipedia://en/12056032). The curves for which this limit exists are called **rectifiable curves**, and the process of determining their arc length in this way is called **curve rectification**.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: Arclength.svg.* Approximation to a curve by multiple linear segments. The length of this polygonal path is a lower bound on the length of the curve.

A [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) in the [plane](wikipedia://en/9697) can be approximated by connecting a finite number of [points](wikipedia://en/593693) on the curve, in consecutive order, using (straight) [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860) to create a [polygonal chain](wikipedia://en/12056032). One may calculate the [length](wikipedia://en/18542) of each linear segment using the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034), and sum these lengths to obtain the total length of the chain; that approximation is known as the *(cumulative) [chordal](wikipedia://en/297947) distance*.[^o4]

If additional points are placed along the curve, the chordal distance will not decrease, but may increase. If chordal distance is bounded above, the least upper bound is called the length of the curve. A curve with the property that every arc between two points of the curve has finite length (even if the whole curve has infinite length) is called a [rectifiable curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectifiable_curve).

For a [parametric curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_curve) (thought of as the trajectory of a particle, moving in the plane with position $(x(t),y(t))$ at time $t$) the arc length is the smallest quantity that is not exceeded by the length of any polygonal path along the curve. Suppose the curve is $\{\, (x(t), y(t)) : a \le t \le b\,\}$ for some numbers $a<b.$ Suppose $a = t_0 < t_1 < t_2 < \cdots < t_n = b.$ Then the sequence of points $\big( (x(t_i), y(t_i) \big)_{i=0}^n$ is a polygonal path, and its length is

$$\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \text{distance from } (x(t_i), y(t_i)) \text{ to } (x_{t_{i+1}}, y_{t_{i+1}}).$$

The length of the curve is at least as much as the length of any polygonal path, but it is the smallest distance that is not exceeded by the length of any such polygonal path.

:::

## Computation by an integral {#Computation_by_an_integral}

For a smooth, i.e. differentiable, curve, the arc length obtained by integrating [speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed) (the magnitude of the velocity vector) over the curve with respect to time. Thus the length of a [continuously differentiable curve](wikipedia://en/493403) in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), parameterized as $(x(t),y(t))$, for $a\le t\le b$, is given as the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532)[^o6]

$$L = \int_a^b \sqrt{x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2}\,dt.$$

Here the [integrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrand) (the square root inside the integral) is the particle's speed. This defining integral of arc length does not always have a [closed-form expression](wikipedia://en/585143), and [numerical integration](wikipedia://en/170089) may be used instead to obtain numerical values of arc length.

More generally, for curves that are not necessarily in the plane, let $f\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^n$ be [continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuously_differentiable) (i.e., the derivative is a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122)) function. The length of the curve is given by the formula

$$L(f) = \int_a^b |f'(t)|\,dt$$

where $|f'(t)|$ is the Euclidean norm of the tangent vector $f'(t)$ to the curve.

A curve can be parameterized in infinitely many ways. The arc length of the curve is the same regardless of the parameterization used to define the curve.

::: {#o8 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Signed arc length"}

### Sign {#Sign}

A **signed arc length** can be defined to convey a sense of [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29) or "direction" with respect to a reference point taken as [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) in the curve (see also: [curve orientation](wikipedia://en/3633706) and [signed distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signed_distance)).[^o9]

:::

## Finding arc lengths by integration {#Finding_arc_lengths_by_integration}

*Image: Quarter circle.png.* Quarter circle

If a [planar curve](wikipedia://en/971691) in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is defined by the equation $y = f(x),$ where $f$ is [continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuously_differentiable), then it is simply a special case of a parametric equation where $x = t$ and $y = f(t).$ The [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) of each infinitesimal segment of the arc can be given by:

$$\begin{aligned} & \sqrt{(dx)^2 + (dy)^2} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2} \, dt \\[6pt] \text{or } & \sqrt{ (dx)^2+(dy)^2 } = \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2 \,} \, dx. \end{aligned}$$

The arc length is then given by:

$$s=\int_a^b \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2 \,} \, dx.$$

Curves with [closed-form solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solution_in_closed_form) for arc length include the [catenary](wikipedia://en/7163), [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), [cycloid](wikipedia://en/41638), [logarithmic spiral](wikipedia://en/42373), [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231), [semicubical parabola](wikipedia://en/2669305) and [straight line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_%28mathematics%29). The lack of a closed form solution for the arc length of an [elliptic](wikipedia://en/9277#Circumference) and [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/14052) arc led to the development of the [elliptic integrals](wikipedia://en/9960).

### Numerical integration {#Numerical_integration}

In most cases, including even simple curves, there are no closed-form solutions for arc length and [numerical integration](wikipedia://en/170089) is necessary. Because of the differentiation in the arc-length formula, the integrand's [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) loses one order of precision, relative to the arc itself, with a corresponding loss of precision. But for infinitely smooth functions, numerical integration of the arc length integral is usually very efficient. For example, consider the problem of finding the length of a quarter of the unit circle by numerically integrating the arc length integral. The upper half of the unit circle can be parameterized as $y = \sqrt{1 - x^2}.$ The interval $x \in \left[-\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right]$ corresponds to a quarter of the circle. Since $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{-x}\sqrt{1 - x^2}$ and $1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{1 - x^2},$ the length of a quarter of the unit circle is

$$\int_{-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}^\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}\, .$$

Since the integrand is infinitely smooth over the domain of interest with a convergent Taylor series, the 15-point [Gauss–Kronrod](wikipedia://en/18508161) rule estimate for this integral of \1.570796326808177 differs from the true length of

$$\arcsin x\bigg|^{\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}_{-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}} = \frac{\pi}{2}$$

by \1.3e-11 and the 16-point [Gaussian quadrature](wikipedia://en/50579) rule estimate of \1.570796326794727 differs from the true length by only \1.7e-13. This means it is possible to evaluate this integral to almost [machine precision](wikipedia://en/3076863) with only 16 integrand evaluations.

### Curve on a surface {#Curve_on_a_surface}

Let $\mathbf{x}(u,v)$ be a surface mapping and let $\mathbf{C}(t) = (u(t), v(t))$ be a curve on this surface. The integrand of the arc length integral is $\left|\left(\mathbf{x}\circ\mathbf{C}\right)'(t)\right|.$ Evaluating the derivative requires the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) for vector fields:

$$D(\mathbf{x} \circ \mathbf{C}) = (\mathbf{x}_u \ \mathbf{x}_v)\binom{u'}{v'} = \mathbf{x}_u u' + \mathbf{x}_v v'.$$

The squared norm of this vector is

$$\left(\mathbf{x}_u u' + \mathbf{x}_v v'\right) \cdot (\mathbf{x}_u u' + \mathbf{x}_v v') = g_{11}\left(u'\right)^2 + 2g_{12}u'v' + g_{22}\left(v'\right)^2$$

(where $g_{ij}$ is the [first fundamental form](wikipedia://en/510355) coefficient), so the integrand of the arc length integral can be written as $\sqrt{g_{ab} \left(u^a\right)' \left(u^b\right)' \,}$ (where $u^1 = u$ and $u^2 = v$).

### Other coordinate systems {#Other_coordinate_systems}

Let $\mathbf{C}(t) = (r(t), \theta(t))$ be a curve expressed in polar coordinates. The mapping that transforms from polar coordinates to rectangular coordinates is

$$\mathbf{x}(r, \theta) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta).$$

The integrand of the arc length integral is $\left|\left(\mathbf{x}\circ\mathbf{C}\right)'(t)\right|.$ The chain rule for vector fields shows that $D(\mathbf{x} \circ \mathbf{C}) = \mathbf{x}_r r' + \mathbf{x}_{\theta} \theta'.$ So the squared integrand of the arc length integral is

$$\left(\mathbf{x_r}\cdot\mathbf{x_r}\right)\left(r'\right)^2 + 2\left(\mathbf{x}_r\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\theta}\right)r'\theta' + \left(\mathbf{x}_{\theta}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\theta}\right)\left(\theta'\right)^2 = \left(r'\right)^2 + r^2\left(\theta'\right)^2.$$

So for a curve expressed in polar coordinates, the arc length is:

$$\int_{t_1}^{t_2} \sqrt{\left(\frac{dr}{dt}\right)^2 + r^2\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2 \,} dt = \int_{\theta(t_1)}^{\theta(t_2)} \sqrt{\left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2 + r^2 \,} d\theta.$$

The second expression is for a polar graph $r = r(\theta)$ parameterized by $t=\theta$.

Now let $\mathbf{C}(t) = (r(t), \theta(t), \varphi(t))$ be a curve expressed in spherical coordinates where $\theta$ is the polar angle measured from the positive $x$-axis and $\varphi$ is the azimuthal angle. The mapping that transforms from spherical coordinates to rectangular coordinates is

$$\mathbf{x}(r, \theta, \varphi) = (r \sin\theta \cos\varphi, r\sin\theta \sin\varphi, r\cos\theta).$$

Using the chain rule again shows that $D(\mathbf{x}\circ\mathbf{C}) = \mathbf{x}_r r' + \mathbf{x}_{\theta}\theta' + \mathbf{x}_{\varphi}\varphi'.$ All [dot products](wikipedia://en/157093) $\mathbf{x}_i \cdot \mathbf{x}_j$ where $i$ and $j$ differ are zero, so the squared norm of this vector is

$$\left(\mathbf{x}_r \cdot \mathbf{x}_r\right)\left(r'^2\right) + \left(\mathbf{x}_\theta \cdot \mathbf{x}_\theta \right) \left(\theta'\right)^2 + \left(\mathbf{x}_{\varphi} \cdot \mathbf{x}_{\varphi}\right)\left(\varphi'\right)^2 = \left(r'\right)^2 + r^2\left(\theta'\right)^2 + r^2 \sin^2\theta \left(\varphi'\right)^2.$$

So for a curve expressed in spherical coordinates, the arc length is

$$\int_{t_1}^{t_2} \sqrt{\left(\frac{dr}{dt}\right)^2 + r^2\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta \left(\frac{d\varphi}{dt}\right)^2 \,} \, dt.$$

A very similar calculation shows that the arc length of a curve expressed in cylindrical coordinates is

$$\int_{t_1}^{t_2} \sqrt{\left(\frac{dr}{dt}\right)^2 + r^2\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dz}{dt}\right)^2 \,} \, dt.$$

## Simple cases {#Simple_cases}

### Arcs of circles {#Arcs_of_circles}

*Image: Circle arc lengthS.png.* Components of a circle as related to an arc

Arc length is the length of an arc along the circumference of a circle and is denoted by *s*, since the Latin word for length (or size) is *spatium*.

"Arc measure" is the angular measure of an arc and is equivalent to $\theta$ which is the arc's [central angle](wikipedia://en/462705).

In the following formulas,$r$ represents the [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), $d$ is its [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007), $C$ is its [circumference](wikipedia://en/5916), $s$ is the length of an arc on the circle, and $\theta$ is the [central angle](wikipedia://en/462705) of the arc. The measures of $r, d, C,$ and $s$ are expressed in the same units.

$$C = 2\pi r = \pi d$$

If the arc is a [semicircle](wikipedia://en/617831) then:

$$s = \frac{1}{2} C = \frac{1}{2} (2\pi r) = \pi r$$

For an arbitrary circular arc:

- If $\theta$ is in [radians](wikipedia://en/26003) then: $s = (\frac{\theta}{2\pi}) C = (\frac{\theta}{2\pi})(2\pi r) = r\theta$
- If $\theta$ is in [degrees](wikipedia://en/1195294) then: $s = \frac{\theta}{360 ^\circ} C = \frac{\theta}{360^\circ} (2\pi r) = \frac{\pi r \theta}{180^\circ}$
- If $\theta$ is in [grads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grad_%28angle%29) then: $s = \frac{\theta}{400\text{ grad}} C = \frac{\theta}{400\text{ grad}} (2\pi r) = \frac{\pi r \theta}{200\text{ grad}}$
- If $\theta$ is in [turns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_%28geometry%29) (one turn is a complete rotation, or 360°, or 400 grads, or $2\pi$ radians) then: $s = \frac{\theta}{1\text{ turn}} C$.

#### Great circles on Earth {#Great_circles_on_Earth}

Two units of length, the [nautical mile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nautical_mile) and the [metre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre) (or kilometre), were originally defined so the lengths of arcs of [great circles](wikipedia://en/48082) on the Earth's surface would be simply numerically related to the angles they subtend at its centre. The simple equation $s = \theta$ applies in the following circumstances:

- if $s$ is in nautical miles, and $\theta$ is in [arcminutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcminute) ($\tfrac{1}{60}$ [degree](wikipedia://en/1195294)), or
- if $s$ is in kilometres, and $\theta$ is in [gradians](wikipedia://en/201629).

The lengths of the distance units were chosen to make the circumference of the Earth equal 40000 kilometres, or 21600 nautical miles. Those are the numbers of the corresponding angle units in one complete turn.

Those definitions of the metre and the nautical mile have been superseded by more precise ones, but the original definitions are still accurate enough for conceptual purposes and some calculations. For example, they imply that one kilometre is exactly 0.54 nautical miles. Using official modern definitions, one nautical mile is exactly 1.852 kilometres,[^o17] which implies that 1 kilometre is about \0.53995680 nautical miles.[^o18] This modern ratio differs from the one calculated from the original definitions by less than one part in 10,000.

## Historical methods {#Historical_methods}

### Antiquity {#Antiquity}

For much of the [history of mathematics](wikipedia://en/14220), even the greatest thinkers considered it impossible to compute the length of an irregular arc. Although [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) had pioneered a way of finding the area beneath a curve with his "[method of exhaustion](wikipedia://en/1721284)", few believed it was even possible for curves to have definite lengths, as do straight lines. The first ground was broken in this field, as it often has been in [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), by [approximation](wikipedia://en/336271). People began to inscribe [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621) within the curves and compute the length of the sides for a somewhat accurate measurement of the length. By using more segments, and by decreasing the length of each segment, they were able to obtain a more and more accurate approximation. In particular, by inscribing a polygon of many sides in a circle, they were able to find approximate values of [π](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi_%28mathematical_constant%29).[^o22][^o23]

### 17th century {#17th_century}

In the 17th century, the method of exhaustion led to the rectification by geometrical methods of several [transcendental curves](wikipedia://en/519554): the [logarithmic spiral](wikipedia://en/42373) by [Evangelista Torricelli](wikipedia://en/210413) in 1645 (some sources say [John Wallis](wikipedia://en/239290) in the 1650s), the [cycloid](wikipedia://en/41638) by [Christopher Wren](wikipedia://en/52946) in 1658, and the [catenary](wikipedia://en/7163) by [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz) in 1691.

In 1659, Wallis credited [William Neile](wikipedia://en/21605403)'s discovery of the first rectification of a nontrivial [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260), the [semicubical parabola](wikipedia://en/2669305).[^o25] The accompanying figures appear on page 145. On page 91, William Neile is mentioned as *Gulielmus Nelius*.

### Integral form {#Integral_form}

Before the full formal development of calculus, the basis for the modern integral form for arc length was independently discovered by [Hendrik van Heuraet](wikipedia://en/6022646) and [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966).

In 1659 van Heuraet published a construction showing that the problem of determining arc length could be transformed into the problem of determining the area under a curve (i.e., an integral). As an example of his method, he determined the arc length of a semicubical parabola, which required finding the area under a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231).[^o27] In 1660, Fermat published a more general theory containing the same result in his *De linearum curvarum cum lineis rectis comparatione dissertatio geometrica* (Geometric dissertation on curved lines in comparison with straight lines).[^o28]

*Image: Arc length, Fermat.svg.* Fermat's method of determining arc length

Building on his previous work with tangents, Fermat used the curve

$$y = x^\frac{3}{2} \,$$

whose [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) at *x* = *a* had a [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) of

$${3 \over 2} a^\frac{1}{2}$$

so the tangent line would have the equation

$$y = {3 \over 2} a^\frac{1}{2}(x - a) + f(a).$$

Next, he increased *a* by a small amount to *a* + *ε*, making segment *AC* a relatively good approximation for the length of the curve from *A* to *D*. To find the length of the segment *AC*, he used the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034):

$$\begin{aligned} AC^2 &= AB^2 + BC^2 \\ &= \varepsilon^2 + {9 \over 4} a \varepsilon^2 \\ &= \varepsilon^2 \left(1 + {9 \over 4} a\right) \end{aligned}$$

which, when solved, yields

$$AC = \varepsilon \sqrt{1 + {9 \over 4} a \,}.$$

In order to approximate the length, Fermat would sum up a sequence of short segments.

## Curves with infinite length {#Curves_with_infinite_length}

*Image: Koch curve.svg.* The Koch curve

*Image: xsinoneoverx.svg.* The graph of $x \cdot \sin(1/x)$

As mentioned above, some curves are non-rectifiable. That is, there is no upper bound on the lengths of polygonal approximations; the length can be made [arbitrarily large](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_jargon#arbitrarily_large). Informally, such curves are said to have infinite length. There are continuous curves on which every arc (other than a single-point arc) has infinite length. An example of such a curve is the [Koch curve](wikipedia://en/46959). Another example of a curve with infinite length is the graph of the function defined by *f*(*x*) = *x* sin(1/*x*) for any [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) with 0 as one of its delimiters and *f*(0) = 0. Sometimes the [Hausdorff dimension](wikipedia://en/14294) and [Hausdorff measure](wikipedia://en/504109) are used to quantify the size of such curves.

:::

## Generalization to (pseudo-)Riemannian manifolds {#Generalization_to_(pseudo-)Riemannian_manifolds}

Let $M$ be a [(pseudo-)Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/287555), $g$ the (pseudo-) [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795), $\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow M$ a curve in $M$ defined by $n$ parametric equations

$$\gamma(t)=[\gamma^1(t), \dots, \gamma^n(t)],\quad t\in [ 0,1]$$

and

$$\gamma(0) = \mathbf x, \,\,\gamma(1) = \mathbf y$$

The length of $\gamma$, is defined to be

$$\ell(\gamma) = \int\limits_0^1 ||\gamma'(t)||_{\gamma(t)} dt,$$

or, choosing local coordinates $x$,

$$\ell(\gamma) = \int\limits_0^1 \sqrt{\pm \sum_{i,j=1}^n g_{ij}(x(\gamma(t)))\frac{dx^i(\gamma(t))}{dt}\frac{dx^j(\gamma(t))}{dt}}dt,$$

where

$$\gamma'(t) \in T_{\gamma(t)} M$$

is the tangent vector of $\gamma$ at $t.$ The sign in the square root is chosen once for a given curve, to ensure that the square root is a [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438). The positive sign is chosen for spacelike curves; in a pseudo-Riemannian manifold, the negative sign may be chosen for timelike curves. Thus the length of a curve is a non-negative real number. Usually no curves are considered which are partly spacelike and partly timelike.

In [theory of relativity](wikipedia://en/30001), arc length of timelike curves ([world lines](wikipedia://en/165487)) is the [proper time](wikipedia://en/494418) elapsed along the world line, and arc length of a spacelike curve the [proper distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_distance) along the curve.
