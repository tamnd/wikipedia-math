# Paraxial approximation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Paraxial approximation"}

*Image: Small angle compare error.svg.* The error associated with the paraxial approximation. In this plot the cosine is approximated by 1 - θ<sup>2</sup>/2.

In [geometric optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_optics), the **paraxial approximation** is a [small-angle approximation](wikipedia://en/2009207) used in [Gaussian optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_optics) and [ray tracing](wikipedia://en/17003295) of light through an optical system (such as a [lens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_%28optics%29)).[^o2][^o3]

:::

A **paraxial ray** is a [ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28optics%29) that makes a small angle (*θ*) to the [optical axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_axis) of the system, and lies close to the axis throughout the system.[^o2] Generally, this allows three important approximations (for *θ* in [radians](wikipedia://en/26003)) for calculation of the ray's path, namely:[^o2]

$$\sin \theta \approx \theta,\quad \tan \theta \approx \theta \quad \text{and}\quad\cos \theta \approx 1.$$

The paraxial approximation is used in Gaussian optics and *[first-order](wikipedia://en/346769)* ray tracing.[^o2] [Ray transfer matrix analysis](wikipedia://en/172317) is one method that uses the approximation.

In some cases, the second-order approximation is also called "paraxial". The approximations above for sine and tangent do not change for the "second-order" paraxial approximation (the second term in their [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) expansion is zero), while for cosine the second order approximation is

$$\cos \theta \approx 1 - { \theta^2 \over 2 } \ .$$

The second-order approximation is accurate within 0.5% for angles under about 10°, but its inaccuracy grows significantly for larger angles.[^o4]

For larger angles it is often necessary to distinguish between [meridional rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridional_ray), which lie in a plane containing the [optical axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_axis), and [sagittal rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sagittal_ray), which do not.

Use of the small angle approximations replaces dimensionless trigonometric functions with angles in radians. In [dimensional analysis](wikipedia://en/8267) on optics equations radians are dimensionless and therefore can be ignored.

A paraxial approximation is also commonly used in [physical optics](wikipedia://en/2460242). It is used in the derivation of the paraxial wave equation from the homogeneous [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737) and, consequently, [Gaussian beam](wikipedia://en/41206) optics.
