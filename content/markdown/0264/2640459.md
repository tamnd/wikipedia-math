# Hermitian manifold

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hermitian manifold"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and more specifically in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), a **Hermitian manifold** is the complex analogue of a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652). More precisely, a Hermitian manifold is a [complex manifold](wikipedia://en/509742) with a smoothly varying [Hermitian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_form) [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) on each (holomorphic) [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807). One can also define a Hermitian manifold as a real manifold with a [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) that preserves a [complex structure](wikipedia://en/509742).

:::

A complex structure is essentially an [almost complex structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_complex_structure) with an integrability condition, and this condition yields a unitary structure ([U(n) structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-structure)) on the manifold. By dropping this condition, we get an **almost Hermitian manifold**.

On any almost Hermitian manifold, we can introduce a **fundamental 2-form** (or **cosymplectic structure**) that depends only on the chosen metric and the almost complex structure. This form is always non-degenerate. With the extra integrability condition that it is closed (i.e., it is a [symplectic form](wikipedia://en/28356)), we get an **almost Kähler structure**. If both the almost complex structure and the fundamental form are integrable, then we have a [Kähler structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kähler_structure).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

A **Hermitian metric** on a [complex vector bundle](wikipedia://en/1538109) $E$ over a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) $M$ is a smoothly varying [positive-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_bilinear_form) [Hermitian form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_form) on each fiber.[^o4] Such a metric can be viewed as a smooth global section $h$ of the vector bundle $(E\otimes\overline{E})^*$ such that for every point $p$ in $M$,

$$h_p\mathord{\left(\eta, \bar\zeta\right)} = \overline{h_p\mathord{\left(\zeta, \bar\eta\right)}}$$

for all $\zeta$, $\eta$ in the fiber $E_{p}$ and

$$h_p\mathord{\left(\zeta, \bar\zeta\right)} > 0$$

for all nonzero $\zeta$ in $E_{p}$.

A **Hermitian manifold** is a [complex manifold](wikipedia://en/509742) with a Hermitian metric on its [holomorphic tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/27540773). Likewise, an **almost Hermitian manifold** is an [almost complex manifold](wikipedia://en/514258) with a Hermitian metric on its holomorphic tangent bundle.

On a Hermitian manifold the metric can be written in local holomorphic coordinates $(z^\alpha)$ as

$$h = h_{\alpha\bar\beta}\,dz^\alpha \otimes d\bar z^\beta$$

where $h_{\alpha\bar\beta}$ are the components of a positive-definite [Hermitian matrix](wikipedia://en/189682).

:::

## Riemannian metric and associated form {#Riemannian_metric_and_associated_form}

A Hermitian metric $h$ on an (almost) complex manifold $M$ defines a [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) $g$ on the underlying smooth manifold. The metric $g$ is defined to be the real part of $h$:

$$g = {1 \over 2}\left(h + \bar h\right).$$

The form $g$ is a symmetric bilinear form on $TM^{\mathbf{C}}$, the [complexified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complexified) tangent bundle. Since $g$ is equal to its conjugate it is the complexification of a real form on $TM$. The symmetry and positive-definiteness of $g$ on $TM$ follow from the corresponding properties of $h$. In local holomorphic coordinates the metric $g$ can be written

$$g = {1 \over 2}h_{\alpha\bar\beta}\,\left(dz^\alpha\otimes d\bar z^\beta + d\bar z^\beta\otimes dz^\alpha\right).$$

One can also associate to $h$ a [complex differential form](wikipedia://en/2497815) $\omega$ of degree (1,1). The form $\omega$ is defined as minus the imaginary part of $h$:

$$\omega = {i \over 2}\left(h - \bar h\right).$$

Again since $\omega$ is equal to its conjugate it is the complexification of a real form on $TM$. The form $\omega$ is called variously the **associated (1,1) form**, the **fundamental form**, or the **Hermitian form**. In local holomorphic coordinates $\omega$ can be written

$$\omega = {i \over 2}h_{\alpha\bar\beta}\,dz^\alpha\wedge d\bar z^\beta,$$

where $dz^{\alpha}\wedge d\bar z^{\beta}=dz^{\alpha}\otimes d\bar z^{\beta}-d\bar z^{\beta}\otimes dz^{\alpha}.$

It is clear from the coordinate representations that any one of the three forms $h$, $g$, and $\omega$ uniquely determine the other two. The Riemannian metric $g$ and associated (1,1) form $\omega$ are related by the [almost complex structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_complex_structure) $J$ as follows

$$\begin{aligned} \omega(u, v) &= g(Ju, v)\\ g(u, v) &= \omega(u, Jv) \end{aligned}$$

for all complex tangent vectors $u$ and $v$. The Hermitian metric $h$ can be recovered from $g$ and $\omega$ via the identity

$$h = g - i\omega.$$

All three forms $h$, $g$, and $\omega$ preserve the [almost complex structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_complex_structure) $J$. That is,

$$\begin{aligned} h(Ju, Jv) &= h(u, v) \\ g(Ju, Jv) &= g(u, v) \\ \omega(Ju, Jv) &= \omega(u, v) \end{aligned}$$

for all complex tangent vectors $u$ and $v$.

A Hermitian structure on an (almost) complex manifold $M$ can therefore be specified by either

1. a Hermitian metric $h$ as above,
1. a Riemannian metric $g$ that preserves the almost complex structure $J$, or
1. a [nondegenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondegenerate_form) 2-form $\omega$ which preserves $J$ and is positive-definite in the sense that $\omega(u, Ju) > 0$ for all nonzero real tangent vectors $u$.

Note that many authors call $g$ itself the Hermitian metric.

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Every (almost) complex manifold admits a Hermitian metric.[^o8] This follows directly from the analogous statement for Riemannian metric. Given an arbitrary Riemannian metric *g* on an almost complex manifold *M* one can construct a new metric *g*′ compatible with the almost complex structure *J* in an obvious manner:

$$g'(u, v) = {1 \over 2}\left(g(u, v) + g(Ju, Jv)\right).$$

Choosing a Hermitian metric on an almost complex manifold *M* is equivalent to a choice of [U(*n*)-structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-structure) on *M*; that is, a [reduction of the structure group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduction_of_the_structure_group) of the [frame bundle](wikipedia://en/366550) of *M* from GL(*n*, **C**) to the [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) U(*n*). A **unitary frame** on an almost Hermitian manifold is complex linear frame which is [orthonormal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthonormal) with respect to the Hermitian metric. The [unitary frame bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_frame_bundle) of *M* is the [principal U(*n*)-bundle](wikipedia://en/310950) of all unitary frames.

Every almost Hermitian manifold *M* has a canonical [volume form](wikipedia://en/1855811) which is just the [Riemannian volume form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_volume_form) determined by *g*. This form is given in terms of the associated (1,1)-form $\omega$ by

$$\mathrm{vol}_M = \frac{\omega^n}{n!} \in \Omega^{n,n}(M)$$

where $\omega^{n}$ is the [wedge product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedge_product) of $\omega$ with itself $n$ times. The volume form is therefore a real (*n*,*n*)-form on *M*. In local holomorphic coordinates the volume form is given by

$$\mathrm{vol}_M = \left(\frac{i}{2}\right)^n \det\left(h_{\alpha\bar\beta}\right)\, dz^1 \wedge d\bar z^1 \wedge \dotsb \wedge dz^n \wedge d\bar z^n.$$

One can also consider a hermitian metric on a [holomorphic vector bundle](wikipedia://en/4794668).

:::

## Kähler manifolds {#Kähler_manifolds}

The most important class of Hermitian manifolds are [Kähler manifolds](wikipedia://en/390538). These are Hermitian manifolds for which the Hermitian form $\omega$ is [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_differential_form):

$$d\omega = 0\,.$$

In this case the form ω is called a **Kähler form**. A Kähler form is a symplectic form, and so Kähler manifolds are naturally [symplectic manifolds](wikipedia://en/28356).

An almost Hermitian manifold whose associated (1,1)-form is closed is naturally called an **almost Kähler manifold**. Any symplectic manifold admits a compatible almost complex structure making it into an almost Kähler manifold.

### Integrability {#Integrability}

A Kähler manifold is an almost Hermitian manifold satisfying an [integrability condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrability_condition). This can be stated in several equivalent ways.

Let $(M, g, \omega, J)$ be an almost Hermitian manifold of real dimension $2n$ and let $\nabla$ be the [Levi-Civita connection](wikipedia://en/249176) of $g$. The following are equivalent conditions for $M$ to be Kähler:

- $\omega$ is closed and $J$ is integrable,
- $$\nabla J = 0,$$
- $$\nabla\omega = 0,$$
- the [holonomy group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holonomy_group) of $\nabla$ is contained in the [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) $U(n)$ associated to $J$,

The equivalence of these conditions corresponds to the "[2 out of 3](wikipedia://en/173993#2-out-of-3_property)" property of the [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993).

In particular, if $M$ is a Hermitian manifold, the condition dω = 0 is equivalent to the apparently much stronger conditions $\nabla\omega = \nabla J = 0$. The richness of Kähler theory is due in part to these properties.
