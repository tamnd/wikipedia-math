# Incentive compatibility

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Incentive compatibility"}

In [game theory](wikipedia://en/11924) and [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), a [mechanism](wikipedia://en/689895) is called **incentive-compatible** (**IC**)**[^o2]** if every participant can achieve their own best outcome by reporting their true preferences.[^o3] For example, there is incentive compatibility if high-risk clients are better off in identifying themselves as high-risk to [insurance firms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insurance_firm), who only sell discounted insurance to high-risk clients. Likewise, they would be worse off if they pretend to be low-risk. Low-risk clients who pretend to be high-risk would also be worse off.[^o4] The concept is attributed to the Russian-born American economist [Leonid Hurwicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Hurwicz).[^o3]

:::

## Typology {#Typology}

There are several different degrees of incentive-compatibility:[^o6]

- The stronger degree is **dominant-strategy incentive-compatibility** (**DSIC**).[^o2] This means that truth-telling is a weakly-[dominant strategy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominant_strategy), i.e. you fare best or at least not worse by being truthful, regardless of what the others do. In a DSIC mechanism, strategic considerations cannot help any agent achieve better outcomes than the truth; such mechanisms are called [strategyproof](wikipedia://en/886330),[^o2] truthful, or straightforward.
- A weaker degree is **Bayesian-Nash incentive-compatibility** (**BNIC**).[^o2] This means there is a [Bayesian Nash equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_Nash_equilibrium) in which all participants reveal their true preferences. In other words, *if* all other players act truthfully, *then* it is best to be truthful.[^o2]

Every DSIC mechanism is also BNIC, but a BNIC mechanism may exist even if no DSIC mechanism exists.

Typical examples of DSIC mechanisms are [second-price auctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second-price_auction) and a [simple majority vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majority_rule) between two choices. Typical examples of non-DSIC mechanisms are [ranked voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranked_voting) with three or more alternatives (by the [Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem](wikipedia://en/288224)) or [first-price auctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-price_auction).

## In randomized mechanisms {#In_randomized_mechanisms}

A randomized mechanism is a probability-distribution on deterministic mechanisms. There are two ways to define incentive-compatibility of randomized mechanisms:[^o2]

- The stronger definition is: a randomized mechanism is universally-incentive-compatible if every mechanism selected with positive probability is incentive-compatible (i.e. if truth-telling gives the agent an optimal value regardless of the coin-tosses of the mechanism).
- The weaker definition is: a randomized mechanism is incentive-compatible-in-expectation if the game induced by expectation is incentive-compatible (i.e. if truth-telling gives the agent an optimal [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653)).

::: {#o9 .statement cue=named-heading name="Revelation principles"}

## Revelation principles {#Revelation_principles}

The revelation principle comes in two variants corresponding to the two flavors of incentive-compatibility:

- The dominant-strategy revelation-principle says that every social-choice function that can be implemented in dominant-strategies can be implemented by a DSIC mechanism.
- The Bayesian–Nash revelation-principle says that every social-choice function that can be implemented in Bayesian–Nash equilibrium ([Bayesian game](wikipedia://en/1863423), i.e. game of incomplete information) can be implemented by a BNIC mechanism.

:::
