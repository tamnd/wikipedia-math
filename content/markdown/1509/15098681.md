# Cone of curves

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cone of curves"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **cone of curves** (sometimes the **Kleiman-Mori** cone) of an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) $X$ is a [combinatorial invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) of importance to the [birational geometry](wikipedia://en/382733) of $X$.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $X$ be a [proper](wikipedia://en/603782) variety. By definition, a (real) *1-cycle* on $X$ is a formal [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) $C=\sum a_iC_i$ of irreducible, reduced and proper curves $C_i$, with coefficients $a_i \in \mathbb{R}$. *Numerical equivalence* of 1-cycles is defined by intersections: two 1-cycles $C$ and $C'$ are numerically equivalent if $C \cdot D = C' \cdot D$ for every Cartier [divisor](wikipedia://en/1693055) $D$ on $X$. Denote the [real vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_vector_space) of 1-cycles modulo numerical equivalence by $N_1(X)$.

We define the *cone of curves* of $X$ to be

$$NE(X) = \left\{\sum a_i[C_i], \ 0 \leq a_i \in \mathbb{R} \right\}$$

where the $C_i$ are irreducible, reduced, proper curves on $X$, and $[C_i]$ their classes in $N_1(X)$. It is not difficult to see that $NE(X)$ is indeed a [convex cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_%28linear_algebra%29#Convex_cone) in the sense of convex geometry.

:::

::: {#o5 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

One useful application of the notion of the cone of curves is the **[Kleiman](wikipedia://en/16169105) condition**, which says that a (Cartier) divisor $D$ on a complete variety $X$ is [ample](wikipedia://en/1186896) if and only if $D \cdot x > 0$ for any nonzero element $x$ in $\overline{NE(X)}$, the closure of the cone of curves in the usual real topology. (In general, $NE(X)$ need not be closed, so taking the closure here is important.)

A more involved example is the role played by the cone of curves in the theory of [minimal models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_model_%28birational_geometry%29) of algebraic varieties. Briefly, the goal of that theory is as follows: given a (mildly singular) projective variety $X$, find a (mildly singular) variety $X'$ which is [birational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birational) to $X$, and whose [canonical divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_divisor) $K_{X'}$ is [nef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerically_effective). The great breakthrough of the early 1980s (due to [Mori](wikipedia://en/794811) and others) was to construct (at least morally) the necessary birational map from $X$ to $X'$ as a sequence of steps, each of which can be thought of as contraction of a $K_X$-negative extremal ray of $NE(X)$. This process encounters difficulties, however, whose resolution necessitates the introduction of the [flip](wikipedia://en/13001206).

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="A structure theorem"}

## A structure theorem {#A_structure_theorem}

The above process of contractions could not proceed without the fundamental result on the structure of the cone of curves known as the **Cone Theorem**. The first version of this theorem, for [smooth varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_varieties), is due to [Mori](wikipedia://en/794811); it was later generalised to a larger class of varieties by [Kawamata](wikipedia://en/47502077), [Kollár](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/János_Kollár), [Reid](wikipedia://en/5863755), [Shokurov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vyacheslav_Shokurov), and others. Mori's version of the theorem is as follows:

**Cone Theorem.** Let $X$ be a smooth [projective variety](wikipedia://en/320469). Then

\1. There are [countably many](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_many) [rational curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_curve) $C_i$ on $X$, satisfying $0< -K_X \cdot C_i \leq \operatorname{dim} X +1$, and

$$\overline{NE(X)} = \overline{NE(X)}_{K_X\geq 0} + \sum_i \mathbf{R}_{\geq0} [C_i].$$

\2. For any positive real number $\epsilon$ and any [ample divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ample_divisor) $H$,

$$\overline{NE(X)} = \overline{NE(X)}_{K_X+\epsilon H\geq0} + \sum \mathbf{R}_{\geq0} [C_i],$$

where the sum in the last term is finite.

The first assertion says that, in the [closed half-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_half-space) of $N_1(X)$ where intersection with $K_X$ is nonnegative, we know nothing, but in the complementary half-space, the cone is spanned by some countable collection of curves which are quite special: they are [rational](wikipedia://en/865939), and their 'degree' is bounded very tightly by the dimension of $X$. The second assertion then tells us more: it says that, away from the hyperplane $\{C : K_X \cdot C = 0\}$, extremal rays of the cone cannot accumulate. When $X$ is a Fano variety, $\overline{NE(X)}_{K_X\geq 0} = 0$ because $-K_X$ is ample. So the cone theorem shows that the cone of curves of a Fano variety is generated by rational curves.

If in addition the variety $X$ is defined over a field of characteristic 0, we have the following assertion, sometimes referred to as the **Contraction Theorem**:

\3. Let $F \subset \overline{NE(X)}$ be an extremal face of the cone of curves on which $K_X$ is negative. Then there is a unique [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) $\operatorname{cont}_F : X \rightarrow Z$ to a projective variety *Z*, such that $(\operatorname{cont}_F)_* \mathcal{O}_X = \mathcal{O}_Z$ and an irreducible curve $C$ in $X$ is mapped to a point by $\operatorname{cont}_F$ if and only if $[C] \in F$. (See also: [contraction morphism](wikipedia://en/52622473)).

:::
