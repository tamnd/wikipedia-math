# Exceptional divisor

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Exceptional divisor"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), an **exceptional divisor** for a [regular map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_map_%28algebraic_geometry%29)

:::

$$f: X \rightarrow Y$$

of [varieties](wikipedia://en/248808) is a kind of 'large' subvariety of $X$ which is 'crushed' by $f$, in a certain definite sense. More strictly, *f* has an associated **exceptional locus** which describes how it identifies nearby points in codimension one, and the exceptional divisor is an appropriate algebraic construction whose support is the exceptional locus. The same ideas can be found in the theory of holomorphic mappings of [complex manifolds](wikipedia://en/509742).

More precisely, suppose that

$$f: X \rightarrow Y$$

is a [regular map of varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_map_of_varieties) which is [birational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birational) (that is, it is an isomorphism between open subsets of $X$ and $Y$). A codimension-1 subvariety $Z \subset X$ is said to be *exceptional* if $f(Z)$ has codimension at least 2 as a subvariety of $Y$. One may then define the *exceptional divisor* of $f$ to be

$$\sum_i Z_i \in Div(X),$$

where the sum is over all exceptional subvarieties of $f$, and is an element of the group of [Weil divisors](wikipedia://en/1693055) on $X$.

Consideration of exceptional divisors is crucial in [birational geometry](wikipedia://en/382733): an elementary result (see for instance Shafarevich, II.4.4) shows (under suitable assumptions) that any birational regular map that is not an isomorphism has an exceptional divisor. A particularly important example is the [blowup](wikipedia://en/1817790)

$$\sigma: \tilde{X} \rightarrow X$$

of a subvariety

$W \subset X$:

in this case the exceptional divisor is exactly the preimage of $W$.
