# Vitold Belevitch

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Vitold Belevitch"}

**Vitold Belevitch** (2 March 1921 – 26 December 1999) was a Belgian mathematician and electrical engineer of Russian origin who produced some important work in the field of electrical network theory. Born to parents fleeing the [Bolsheviks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolshevik), he settled in Belgium where he worked on early computer construction projects. Belevitch is responsible for a number of circuit theorems and introduced the now well-known [scattering parameters](wikipedia://en/1972332).

:::

Belevitch had an interest in languages and found a mathematical derivation of [Zipf's law](wikipedia://en/43218). He also published on machine languages. Another field of interest was transmission lines, where he published on line coupling. He worked on telephone conferencing and introduced the mathematical construct of the [conference matrix](wikipedia://en/7770047).

## Early life {#Early_life}

Belevitch was born 2 March 1921 in [Terijoki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terijoki), [Karelia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karelia), now incorporated into Russia, but at the time part of Finland. Belevitch's parents were Russian and his mother was an ethnic Pole. They were attempting to flee from their home in Petrograd ([Saint Petersburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Petersburg)) in Russia to escape the [Bolshevik revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolshevik_revolution), which Belevitch's father opposed. Belevitch's heavily pregnant mother succeeded in crossing the border into Finland and continued on to [Helsinki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsinki) after Vitold was born, where the birth was registered. She headed for Helsinki because her husband's father was principal of the Russian school there. Belevitch's father was arrested before he could follow and was deported to [Siberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siberia), where he died without ever seeing his son.[^o4]

In 1926 Belevitch, while still a small child, emigrated with his mother to Belgium.[^o5]

## Education {#Education}

Belevitch was educated in French in Belgium, until July 1936 at the [Notre-Dame de la Paix College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facultés_universitaires_Notre-Dame_de_la_Paix) at [Namur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namur_%28city%29). In 1937, aged 16, he enrolled at the [Université Catholique de Louvain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_University_of_Leuven_%281834–1968%29) where he studied electrical and mechanical engineering, graduating in 1942.[^o7][^o8] Belevitch gained his PhD in applied sciences at the same university in 1945. His sponsor was [Charles Lambert Manneback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Lambert_Manneback) and his second advisor was [Wilhelm Cauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Cauer), the founder of the field of [network synthesis](wikipedia://en/59441761).[^o9][^o10]

From 1953 until 1985 Belevitch lectured at the university. He taught [circuit theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circuit_theory) and other mathematical subjects related to electrical science. In 1960 he became a [special professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor#Netherlands) (buitengewoon hoogleraar).[^o11] Although Belevitch worked as an electrical engineer, his primary interest was mathematics, especially algebra. There was a tradition in Belgium of the most gifted mathematicians entering engineering rather than pure mathematics or physics. Belevitch showed his mathematical leanings by preferring the use of blackboard and chalk to any [audio-visual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio-visual) aids during lectures. He even lectured in this way when presenting the opening lecture to a large audience at an international conference at the [IEE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institution_of_Electrical_Engineers) in London.[^o12]

## Career {#Career}

*Image: Belevitch young.jpg.* Young Belevitch *c*.1947

After graduating in 1942, Belevitch joined the [Bell Telephone Manufacturing Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Telephone_Manufacturing_Company) (BTMC) in [Antwerp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerp), originally part of the [International Bell Telephone Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Bell_Telephone_Company) headquartered in [Brussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels) but, along with their other European holdings, sold to [International Telephone and Telegraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Telephone_and_Telegraph) (ITT) in 1925. At BTMC Belevitch became head of the transmission department. It was here that he came into contact with Wilhelm Cauer who became a great influence on him. Cauer was one of the leading circuit theorists of the day and at the time worked for [Mix & Genest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mix_&_Genest) in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin), a sister company under the ITT umbrella. Cauer died during the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War) but Belevitch long after continued to consider his works to be the highest authority on matters of circuit theory.[^o14]

From 1951 Belevitch was involved in the design of electronic computers which BTMC were developing for the Belgian government. The purpose of this program was to "catch up" with the advances made in the English-speaking world during the war. It resulted in the construction of the Machine mathématique IRSIA-FNRS.[^o15] From 1952 Belevitch represented the electrical engineering aspect of this project. In 1955 Belevitch became director of the Belgian Computing Centre (Comité d'Étude et d'Exploitation des Calculateurs Électroniques) in Brussels which operated this computer for the government. Initially, only the 17-rack prototype was operational. One of the first tasks to which it was put was the calculation of [Bessel functions](wikipedia://en/4700). The full 34-rack machine was moved from Antwerp and put into service in 1957. Belevitch used this machine to investigate [transcendental functions](wikipedia://en/297021).[^o16]

In 1963 Belevitch became head of the newly formed Laboratoire de Recherche MBLE[^o17] (later Philips Research Laboratories Belgium) under the [Philips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philips) director of research [Hendrik Casimir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Casimir) in [Eindhoven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eindhoven). This facility specialised in applied mathematics for Philips and was heavily involved in computing research. Belevitch stayed in this post until his retirement in November 1984.[^o18]

Belevitch died on 26 December 1999. He is survived by a daughter, but not his wife.[^o19]

## Works {#Works}

Belevitch is best known for his contributions to circuit theory, particularly the mathematical basis of [filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filter_%28signal_processing%29), [modulators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulator), [coupled lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crosstalk_%28electronics%29), and [non-linear circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linear_circuits). He was on the editorial board of the *International Journal of Circuit Theory* from its foundation in 1973. He also made major contributions in [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773), electronic computers, mathematics and [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics).[^o21]

Belevitch dominated international conferences and was prone to asking searching questions of the presenters of papers, often causing them some discomfort. The organiser of one conference at [Birmingham University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_University) in 1959 made Belevitch the chairman of the session in which the organiser gave his own presentation. It seems he did this to restrain Belevitch from asking questions. Belevitch stopped attending conferences in the mid-1970s with the exception of the IEEE International Symposium on Circuits and Systems in [Montreal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreal) in 1984 in order to receive the IEEE Centennial Medal.[^o22]

### Circuit theory {#Circuit_theory}

#### Scattering matrix {#Scattering_matrix}

It was in his 1945 dissertation that Belevitch first introduced the important idea of the [scattering matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S_parameters)[^o25] (called *repartition matrix* by Belevitch). This work was reproduced in part in a later paper by Belevitch, *Transmission Losses in 2*n*\-terminal Networks*. Belgium was occupied by [Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany) for most of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) and this prevented Belevitch from any communication with American colleagues. It was only after the war that it was discovered that the same idea, under the *scattering matrix* name, had independently been used by American scientists developing military [radars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar). The American work by Montgomery, [Dicke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_H._Dicke) and [Purcell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Mills_Purcell) was published in 1948. Belevitch in his work had applied scattering matrices to [lumped-element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumped-element) circuits and was certainly the first to do so, whereas the Americans were concerned with the [distributed-element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed-element) circuits used at [microwave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave) frequencies in radar.[^o26]

Belevitch produced a textbook, *Classical Network Theory*, first published in 1968 which comprehensively covered the field of passive [one-port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-port), and multiport circuits. In this work he made extensive use of the now-established S parameters from the scattering matrix concept, thus succeeding in welding the field into a coherent whole. The eponymous [Belevitch's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belevitch's_theorem), explained in this book, provides a method of determining whether or not it is possible to construct a passive, lossless circuit from discrete elements (that is, a circuit consisting only of [inductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductor) and [capacitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacitor)) that represents a given scattering matrix.[^o27]

#### Telephone conferencing {#Telephone_conferencing}

*Image: Conference matrix 6-port.svg.* Belevitch's implementation of a 6-port conference matrix

Belevitch introduced the mathematical concept of [conference matrices](wikipedia://en/7770047) in 1950, so called because they originally arose in connection with a problem Belevitch was working on concerning telephone conferencing. However, they have applications in a range of other fields as well as being of interest to pure mathematics. Belevitch was studying setting up telephone conferencing by connecting together ideal transformers. It turns out that a necessary condition for setting up a conference with *n* telephone ports and ideal signal loss is the existence of an *n*×*n* conference matrix. *Ideal signal loss* means the loss is only that due to splitting the signal between conference subscribers – there is no dissipation within the conference network.[^o29]

The existence of conference matrices is not a trivial question; they do not exist for all values of *n*. Values of *n* for which they exist are always of the form 4*k*+2 (*k* integer) but this is not, by itself, a sufficient condition. Conference matrices exist for *n* of 2, 6, 10, 14, 18, 26, 30, 38 and 42. They do not exist for *n* of 22 or 34. Belevitch obtained complete solutions for all *n* up to 38 and also noted that *n*=66 had multiple solutions.[^o30]

#### Other work on circuits {#Other_work_on_circuits}

Belevitch wrote a comprehensive summary of the history of circuit theory. He also had an interest in transmission lines, and published several papers on the subject. They include papers on skin effects and coupling between lines ("crosstalk") due to asymmetry.[^o32]

Belevitch first introduced the *great factorization theorem* in which he gives a factorization of [paraunitary matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraunitary_matrix). Paraunitary matrices occur in the construction of filter banks used in multirate digital systems. Apparently, Belevitch's work is obscure and difficult to understand. A much more frequently cited version of this theorem was later published by P. P. Vaidyanathan.[^o33]

### Linguistics {#Linguistics}

Belevitch was educated in French but continued to speak Russian to his mother until she died. In fact, he was able to speak many languages, and could read even more. He studied [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) and the etymology of [Indo-European languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages).[^o35]

Belevitch wrote a book on human and machine languages in which he explored the idea of applying the mathematics of [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773) to obtain results regarding human languages. The book highlighted the difficulties for machine understanding of language for which there was some naive enthusiasm amongst [cybernetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybernetics) researchers in the 1950s.[^o36]

Belevitch also wrote a paper, *On the Statistical Laws of Linguistic Distribution*,[^o37] which gives a derivation for the well-known [empirical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical) relationship, [Zipf's law](wikipedia://en/43218). This law, and the more complex [Mandelbrot law](wikipedia://en/390113), provide a relationship between the frequency of word occurrence in languages and the word's [rank](wikipedia://en/1482394#Ranking_in_statistics). In the simplest form of Zipf's law, frequency is inversely proportional to rank. Belevitch expressed a large class of [statistical distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_distribution) (not only the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462)) in terms of rank and then expanded each expression into a [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448). In every case Belevitch obtained the remarkable result that a first order truncation of the series resulted in Zipf's law. Further, a second-order truncation of the Taylor series resulted in Mandelbrot's law. This gives some insight into the reason why Zipf's law has been found experimentally to hold in such a wide variety of languages.[^o38]

### Control systems {#Control_systems}

Belevitch played a part in developing a mathematical test for determining the [controllability](wikipedia://en/100638) of linear [control systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_system). A system is controllable if it can be moved from one state to another through the system [state space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_space_%28controls%29) in a finite time by application of control inputs. This test is known as the Popov-Belevitch-Hautus, or PBH, test. There is also a PBH test for determining the [observability](wikipedia://en/871280) of a system – that is, the ability to determine the state of a system in finite time solely from the system's own outputs.[^o40]

The PBH test was originally discovered by [Elmer G. Gilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elmer_G._Gilbert) in 1963, but Gilbert's version only applied to systems that could be represented by a [diagonalizable matrix](wikipedia://en/246325). The test was subsequently generalised by [Vasile M. Popov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasile_M._Popov) (in 1966), Belevitch (in *Classical Network Theory*, 1968) and [Malo Hautus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malo_Hautus) in 1969.[^o41]

## IEEE and honours {#IEEE_and_honours}

Belevitch was a Fellow of the [Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Electrical_and_Electronics_Engineers) (IEEE) and was vice-chair of the [Benelux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benelux) section when it was formed in 1959.[^o43] He was awarded the [IEEE Centennial Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_Centennial_Medal), and in 1993, the Society Award (now called Mac Van Valkenburg Award) of the [IEEE Circuits and Systems Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_Circuits_and_Systems_Society).[^o44] He was also a member of the North Rhine-Westphalian Academy of Sciences.[^o45]

Belevitch received an honorary doctoral degree in 1975 from the [Technical University of Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_University_of_Munich), and another from the [École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique_Fédérale_de_Lausanne), Switzerland, in 1978. He was also rewarded with Belgian royal medals.[^o46]

Since 2003, the IEEE Circuits and Systems Society has instituted a Vitold Belevitch award for work in circuit theory. The award is presented biennially at the *European Conference on Circuit Theory & Design*.[^o47]

Belevitch was a prolific publisher with around 4000 pages of scientific output. He was publishing throughout his career right up to, and beyond, his retirement in 1984.[^o49]
