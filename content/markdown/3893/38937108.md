# Neutral plane

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Neutral plane"}

*Image: Bending.svg.* An evenly loaded beam, bending (sagging) under load. The neutral plane is shown by the dotted line.

In [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics), the **neutral plane** or **neutral surface** is a conceptual [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) within a [beam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_%28structure%29) or [cantilever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantilever). When loaded by a bending force, the beam bends so that the inner surface is in [compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compression_%28physics%29) and the outer surface is in [tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tension_%28physics%29). The neutral plane is the surface within the beam between these zones, where the material of the beam is not under [stress](wikipedia://en/228107), either compression or tension.[^o2]

:::

As there is no lengthwise stress force on the neutral plane, there is no [strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_%28physics%29) or extension either: when the beam bends, the length of the neutral plane remains constant. Any line within the neutral plane parallel to the axis of the beam is called the **deflection curve** of the beam.

To show that every beam must have a neutral plane, the material of the beam can be imagined to be divided into narrow fibers parallel to its length. When the beam is bent, at any given cross-section the region of fibers near the concave side will be under compression, while the region near the convex side will be under tension. Because the stress in the material must be [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) across any cross section, there must be a boundary between the regions of compression and tension at which the fibers have no stress. This is the neutral plane.[^o2]

## Structural design {#Structural_design}

The location of the neutral plane can be an important factor in [monocoque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monocoque) structures and [pressure vessels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_vessel). If the structure is a membrane supported by strength ribs, then placing the skin along the neutral surface avoids either compression or tension forces upon it. If the skin is already under external pressure, then this reduces the total force to which it is subject.

In the design of submarines this has been an important, although subtle, issue. The US [Fleet submarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleet_submarine) of World War II had a [hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_hull) section that was not quite circular, causing the nodal circle to separate from the neutral plane, giving rise to additional stresses. The original design was framed internally: this needed trial-and-error design refinement to produce acceptable dimensions for the rib [scantlings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scantling). The designer [Andrew I. McKee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_McKee) at [Portsmouth Naval Shipyard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portsmouth_Naval_Shipyard) developed an improved design. By placing the frames partly inside the hull and partly outside, the neutral axis could be rearranged to coincide with the nodal circle once more. This gave no resultant bending moment on the frames and so allowed a lighter and more efficient structure.[^o4]

## Metrology {#Metrology}

*Image: Bronze Yard No. 11.jpg.* British bronze yard of 1855. The yard is defined by the distance between two lines scribed on the bottom surface of holes at each ends of the bar, on the neutral plane.[^o6]

The property of remaining a constant length under load has been made use of in length [metrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrology). When metal bars were developed as physical standards for length measures, they were calibrated as marks made on a length measured along the neutral plane. This avoided the minuscule changes in length, owing to the bar sagging under its own weight.

The first length standards to use this technique were rectangular section solid bars. A blind hole was bored at each end, to the depth of the neutral plane, and the calibration marks were made at this depth. This was inconvenient, as it was impossible to measure directly between the two marks, but only with an offset trammel down the wells.

*Image: Platinum-Iridium meter bar.jpg.* Computer generated image of platinum-iridium standard [metre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre) bars. The neutral plane of the bar is designed to coincide with the bottom surface of the central web connecting the two sides.

A more convenient approach was used for the [international prototype metre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_prototype_metre) of 1870, a bar of [platinum-iridium alloy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platinum-iridium_alloy) which served as the definition of the meter from 1889 to 1960, when the [CGPM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CGPM) [redefined the meter based on a krypton standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meter#Wavelength_definition). This bar was made with a splayed cross section called the [Tresca section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tresca_section) resembling an X with a connecting bar, or alternatively a H with the sides bent at an angle. One surface of the centre crossbar of the H was designed to coincide with the neutral plane, and the calibration marks defining the meter were scribed into this surface.[^o7]
