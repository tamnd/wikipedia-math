# Secure telephone

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Secure telephone"}

*Image: STE telephone.nsa.jpg.* A [Secure Terminal Equipment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secure_Terminal_Equipment) desk set

A **secure telephone** is a [telephone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone) that provides [voice security](wikipedia://en/1560035) in the form of [end-to-end encryption](wikipedia://en/1065362) for the telephone call, and in some cases also the mutual authentication of the call parties, protecting them against a [man-in-the-middle attack](wikipedia://en/144676). Concerns about massive growth of [telephone tapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone_tapping) incidents led to growing demand for secure telephones.

:::

The practical availability of secure telephones is restricted by several factors; notably politics, [export issues](wikipedia://en/25872370), incompatibility between different products (the devices on each side of the call have to use the same protocol), and high (though recently decreasing) price of the devices.

## Well-known products {#Well-known_products}

*Image: 20151219 154025 19th of December 2015 Constantine Palace Вертушка.jpg.* Secure telephone of the [Commandant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commandant#Russia) colloquially known as *[Vertushka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertushka)* at the [Constantine Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_Palace), one of the residences of the [President of Russian Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Russian_Federation)

The best-known product on the US government market is the [STU-III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STU-III) family. However, this system has now been replaced by the [Secure Terminal Equipment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secure_Terminal_Equipment) (STE) and [SCIP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secure_Communications_Interoperability_Protocol) standards which defines specifications for the design of equipment to secure both [data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data) and voice. The SCIP standard was developed by the [NSA](wikipedia://en/21939) and the [US DOD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Defense) to derive more [interoperability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interoperability) between secure communication equipment. A new family of standard secure phones has been developed based on Philip Zimmermann's VoIP encryption standard [ZRTP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZRTP).[^o3]

## VoIP and direct connection phones {#VoIP_and_direct_connection_phones}

As the popularity of [VoIP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VoIP) grows, secure telephony is becoming more widely used. Many major hardware and software providers offer it as a standard feature at no extra cost.

Examples include the [Gizmo5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gizmo5) and [Twinkle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twinkle_%28software%29). Both of the former work with offerings from the founder of PGP, [Phil Zimmermann](wikipedia://en/23486), and his VoIP secure protocol, [ZRTP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZRTP). ZRTP is implemented in, amongst others, [Ripcord Networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ripcord_Networks) product SecurePC with up to NSA Suite B compliant Elliptic Curve math libraries. ZRTP is also being made available for mobile [GSM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GSM) CSD as a new standard for non-VoIP secure calls.

The U.S. [National Security Agency](wikipedia://en/21939) is developing a secure phone based on Google's [Android](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Android_%28operating_system%29) called [Fishbowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishbowl_%28secure_phone%29).

## Historically significant products {#Historically_significant_products}

*Image: SIGSALY.jpg.* [SIGSALY](wikipedia://en/740918) exhibit at the [National Cryptologic Museum](wikipedia://en/734289)

[Scramblers](wikipedia://en/41680) were used to [secure voice](wikipedia://en/1560035) traffic during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) (1939-1945), but were often intercepted and decoded due to scrambling's inherent insecurity. The first true secure telephone (operational from 1943) was [SIGSALY](wikipedia://en/740918), a massive device that weighed over 50 tons. The NSA, formed in 1952, developed a series of secure telephones, including the [STU-I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STU-I) of the 1970s, [STU-II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STU-II) and [STU-III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STU-III), as well as [voice-encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_encryption) devices for military telephones.

In 1989 an Irish company called Intrepid developed one of the most advanced secure phones. Called "Milcode",[^o6] the phone was the first to implement [code-excited linear prediction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code-excited_linear_prediction) (or CELP) which dramatically improved voice quality and user-operability over previous LPC (Linear Predictive Coding) and LPC-10e versions.

Milcode also boasted significantly higher levels of security than previous secure telephones. The base model offered a proprietary encryption algorithm with a key-length of 512 bits, and a more advanced model with a key-length of 1024 bits. Key exchange used a public key, based on [Diffie-Hellman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffie-Hellman), as opposed to a plug-in datakey. A new key was generated for each phone call. Milcode was also able to encrypt fax and data and was electromagnetically shielded to NATO [TEMPEST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tempest_%28codename%29) standards.

Other products of historical significance are [PGPfone](wikipedia://en/2150535) and [Nautilus](wikipedia://en/5950590) (designed as a non-[key escrow](wikipedia://en/17114) alternative to [Clipper](wikipedia://en/409665), now officially discontinued, but still available on [SourceForge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SourceForge)), *SpeakFreely*, and the security VoIP protocol wrapper [Zfone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zfone) (developed by [Phil Zimmermann](wikipedia://en/23486), who wrote the [PGP](wikipedia://en/23080) software).

Scrambling, generally using a form of [voice inversion](wikipedia://en/5951995), was available from suppliers of electronic hobbyist kits and is common on [FRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_Radio_Service) radios. Analog scrambling is still used, as some telecommunications circuits, such as HF links and telephone lines in the developing world, are of very low quality.
