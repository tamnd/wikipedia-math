# Bézout matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bézout matrix"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Bézout matrix** (or **Bézoutian** or **Bezoutiant**) is a special [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) associated with two [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), introduced by [James Joseph Sylvester](wikipedia://en/234861) in 1853 and [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034) in 1857 and named after [Étienne Bézout](wikipedia://en/96017).[^n1][^n2] **Bézoutian** may also refer to the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of this matrix, which is equal to the [resultant](wikipedia://en/2195020) of the two polynomials. Bézout matrices are sometimes used to test the [stability](wikipedia://en/2057699) of a given polynomial.

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $f(z)$ and $g(z)$ be two [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) polynomials of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) at most *n*,

$$f(z) = \sum_{i=0}^n u_i z^i,\qquad g(z) = \sum_{i=0}^n v_i z^i.$$

(Note that any [coefficient](wikipedia://en/51203) $u_i$ or $v_i$ could be zero.) The **Bézout matrix** of order *n* associated with the polynomials *f* and *g* is

$$B_n(f,g)=\left(b_{ij}\right)_{i,j=0,\dots,n-1}$$

where the entries $b_{ij}$ result from the identity

$$\frac{f(x)g(y)-f(y)g(x)}{x-y} =\sum_{i,j=0}^{n-1} b_{ij}\,x^{i}\,y^{j}.$$

It is an *n* × *n* complex matrix, and its entries are such that if we let $\ell_{ij} = \max\{0,i-j\}$ and $m_{ij} = \min\{i,n-1-j\}$ for each $i, j = 0, \dots, n-1$, then:

$$b_{ij}=\sum_{k=\ell_{ij}}^{m_{ij}}(u_{j+k+1}v_{i-k}-u_{i-k}v_{j+k+1}).$$

To each Bézout matrix, one can associate the following [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477), called the Bézoutian:

$$\operatorname{Bez}: \mathbb{C}^n\times\mathbb{C}^n \to \mathbb{C}: (x,y)\mapsto \operatorname{Bez}(x,y) = x^{*}B_n(f,g)\,y.$$

:::

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- For *n* = 3, we have for any polynomials *f* and *g* of degree (at most) 3:

$$B_3(f,g)=\left[\begin{matrix}u_1v_0-u_0 v_1 & u_2 v_0-u_0 v_2 & u_3 v_0-u_0 v_3\\u_2 v_0-u_0 v_2 & u_2v_1-u_1v_2+u_3v_0-u_0v_3 & u_3 v_1-u_1v_3\\u_3v_0-u_0v_3 & u_3v_1-u_1v_3 & u_3v_2-u_2v_3\end{matrix}\right]\!.$$

- Let $f(x) = 3x^3-x$ and $g(x) = 5x^2+1$ be the two polynomials. Then:

$$B_4(f,g)=\left[\begin{matrix}-1 & 0 & 3 & 0\\0 &8 &0 &0 \\3 & 0 & 15 & 0\\0 & 0 & 0 & 0\end{matrix}\right]\!.$$

The last row and column are all zero as *f* and *g* have degree strictly less than *n* (which is 4). The other zero entries are because for each $i = 0, \dots, n$, either $u_i$ or $v_i$ is zero.

:::

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- $B_n(f,g)$ is [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474) (as a matrix);
- $$B_n(f,g) = -B_n(g,f);$$
- $$B_n(f,f) = 0;$$
- $(f, g) \mapsto B_n(f,g)$ is a [bilinear](wikipedia://en/4365) function;
- $B_n(f,g)$ is a real matrix if *f* and *g* have [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) coefficients;
- $B_n(f,g)$ is nonsingular with $n=\max(\deg(f),\deg(g))$ if and only if *f* and *g* have no common [roots](wikipedia://en/264210).
- $B_n(f,g)$ with $n = \max(\deg(f),\deg(g))$ has [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) which is the [resultant](wikipedia://en/2195020) of *f* and *g*.
- Bezoutians can be reduced to block diagonal form with the help of [confluent Vandermonde matrix](wikipedia://en/348641#Confluent_Vandermonde_matrices).[^o10]

:::

::: {#o12 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

An important application of Bézout matrices can be found in [control theory](wikipedia://en/7039). To see this, let *f*(*z*) be a complex polynomial of degree *n* and denote by *q* and *p* the real polynomials such that *f*(i*y*) = *q*(*y*) + i*p*(*y*) (where *y* is real). We also denote *r* for the [rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rank_of_a_matrix) and *σ* for the signature of $B_n(p,q)$. Then, we have the following statements:

- *f*(*z*) has *n* − *r* roots in common with its conjugate;
- the left *r* roots of *f*(*z*) are located in such a way that:
  - (*r* + *σ*)/2 of them lie in the open left half-plane, and
  - (*r* − *σ*)/2 lie in the open right half-plane;
- *f* is [Hurwitz stable](wikipedia://en/2057699) if and only if $B_n(p,q)$ is [positive definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix).

The third statement gives a necessary and sufficient condition concerning stability. Besides, the first statement exhibits some similarities with a result concerning [Sylvester matrices](wikipedia://en/2174514) while the second one can be related to [Routh–Hurwitz theorem](wikipedia://en/2067581).

:::
