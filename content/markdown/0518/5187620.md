# Statistical distance

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Statistical distance"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), and [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773), a **statistical distance** quantifies the [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) between two statistical objects, which can be two [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685), or two [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) or [samples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_%28statistics%29), or the distance can be between an individual sample point and a population or a wider sample of points.

:::

A distance between populations can be interpreted as measuring the distance between two [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) and hence they are essentially measures of distances between [probability measures](wikipedia://en/45437). Where statistical distance measures relate to the differences between [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685), these may have [statistical dependence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence),[^o2] and hence these distances are not directly related to measures of distances between probability measures. Again, a measure of distance between random variables may relate to the extent of dependence between them, rather than to their individual values.

Many statistical distance measures are not [metrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29), and some are not symmetric. Some types of distance measures, which generalize *squared* distance, are referred to as (statistical) *[divergences](wikipedia://en/25896411)*.

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Terminology"}

## Terminology {#Terminology}

Many terms are used to refer to various notions of distance; these are often confusingly similar, and may be used inconsistently between authors and over time, either loosely or with precise technical meaning. In addition to "distance", similar terms include [deviance](wikipedia://en/432609), [deviation](wikipedia://en/9252911), [discrepancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrepancy_%28disambiguation%29#Statistics), discrimination, and [divergence](wikipedia://en/25896411), as well as others such as [contrast function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrast_function) and [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29). Terms from [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773) include [cross entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_entropy), [relative entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_entropy), [discrimination information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrimination_information), and [information gain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_gain).

:::

## Distances as metrics {#Distances_as_metrics}

### Metrics {#Metrics}

A **metric** on a set *X* is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) (called the *distance function* or simply **distance**) *d* : *X* × *X* → **R**<sup>\+</sup> (where **R**<sup>\+</sup> is the set of non-negative [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438)). For all *x*, *y*, *z* in *X*, this function is required to satisfy the following conditions:

1. *d*(*x*, *y*) ≥ 0 (*[non-negativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative)*)
1. *d*(*x*, *y*) = 0 if and only if *x* = *y* (*[identity of indiscernibles](wikipedia://en/630398)*. Note that condition 1 and 2 together produce *[positive definiteness](wikipedia://en/649616)*)
1. *d*(*x*, *y*) = *d*(*y*, *x*) (*[symmetry](wikipedia://en/200459)*)
1. *d*(*x*, *z*) ≤ *d*(*x*, *y*) + *d*(*y*, *z*) (*[subadditivity](wikipedia://en/745789)* / *[triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941)*).

### Generalized metrics {#Generalized_metrics}

Many statistical distances are not [metrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29), because they lack one or more properties of proper metrics. For example, [pseudometrics](wikipedia://en/68503) violate property (2), identity of indiscernibles; [quasimetrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasimetric) violate property (3), symmetry; and [semimetrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semimetric) violate property (4), the triangle inequality. Statistical distances that satisfy (1) and (2) are referred to as [divergences](wikipedia://en/25896411).

::: {#o9 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Statistical close"}

## Statistically close {#Statistically_close}

The [total variation distance](wikipedia://en/683570) of two distributions $X$ and $Y$ over a finite domain $D$, (often referred to as *statistical difference*[^o10] or *statistical distance*[^o11] in cryptography) is defined as

$$\Delta(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2} \sum _{\alpha \in D} | \Pr[X=\alpha] - \Pr[Y=\alpha] |.$$

We say that two [probability ensembles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_ensembles) $\{X_k\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$ and $\{Y_k\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$ are statistically close if $\Delta(X_k,Y_k)$ is a [negligible function](wikipedia://en/8426019) in $k$.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

### Metrics {#Metrics}

- [Total variation distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_variation_distance) (sometimes just called "the" statistical distance)
- [Hellinger distance](wikipedia://en/13035709)
- [Lévy–Prokhorov metric](wikipedia://en/6789891)
- [Wasserstein metric](wikipedia://en/6704603): also known as the Kantorovich metric, or [earth mover's distance](wikipedia://en/11915675)
- [Mahalanobis distance](wikipedia://en/799760)
- [Integral probability metrics](wikipedia://en/73598108) generalize several metrics or pseudometrics on distributions

### Divergences {#Divergences}

- [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052)
- [Rényi divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rényi_divergence)
- [Jensen–Shannon divergence](wikipedia://en/4019573)
- [Ball divergence](wikipedia://en/77244347)
- [Bhattacharyya distance](wikipedia://en/831261) (despite its name it is not a distance, as it violates the triangle inequality)
- [f-divergence](wikipedia://en/15224289): generalizes several distances and divergences
- [Discriminability index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discriminability_index), specifically the [Bayes discriminability index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discriminability_index#Bayes_discriminability_index), is a positive-definite symmetric measure of the overlap of two distributions.
