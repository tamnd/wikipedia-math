# Negation introduction

::: {#o1 .statement cue=infobox-mathematical-statement name="Negation introduction"}

**Negation introduction.**

If a given antecedent implies both the consequent and its complement, then the antecedent is a contradiction.

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Negation introduction"}

**Negation introduction** is a [rule of inference](wikipedia://en/252311), or [transformation rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_rule), in the field of [propositional calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus).

:::

Negation introduction states that if a given antecedent implies both the consequent and its complement, then this implies the negated antecedent.[^o3][^o4]

## Formal notation {#Formal_notation}

This can be written as:

$$\Big((P \rightarrow Q) \land (P \rightarrow \neg Q)\Big) \rightarrow \neg P$$

An example of its use would be an attempt to prove two contradictory statements from a single fact. For example, if a person were to state "Whenever I hear the phone ringing I am happy" and then state "Whenever I hear the phone ringing I am *not* happy", one can infer that the person never hears the phone ringing.

Many [proofs by contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proofs_by_contradiction) use negation introduction as reasoning scheme: to prove ¬*P*, assume for contradiction *P*, then derive from it two contradictory inferences *Q* and ¬*Q*. Since the latter contradiction renders *P* impossible, ¬*P* must hold.

::: {#o7 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

With $\neg P$ identified as $P\to\bot$, the principle is as a special case of [Frege's theorem](wikipedia://en/4725226), already in [minimal logic](wikipedia://en/26267678).

Another derivation makes use of $A\to \neg B$ as the [curried](wikipedia://en/6600), equivalent form of $\neg (A \land B)$. Using this twice, the principle is seen equivalent to the negation of $\big(P\land(P\to Q)\big)\land \neg(P\land Q)$ which, via modus ponens and rules for conjunctions, is itself equivalent to the valid noncontradiction principle for $P\land Q$.

A classical derivation passing through the introduction of a disjunction may be given as follows:

::: {#o8 .table cue=table}

| *Step* | *Proposition* | *Derivation* |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $(P \to Q)\land(P \to \neg Q)$ | Given |
| 2 | $(\neg P \lor Q)\land(\neg P \lor \neg Q)$ | Classical equivalence of the [material implication](wikipedia://en/244516) |
| 3 | $\neg P \lor (Q \land \neg Q)$ | Distributivity |
| 4 | $\neg P \lor \bot$ | [Law of noncontradiction](wikipedia://en/17636) for $Q$ |
| 5 | $\neg P$ | [Disjunctive syllogism](wikipedia://en/7963) (3,4) |

:::
