# Jinkōki

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Jinkōki"}

is a three-volume work on [Japanese mathematics](wikipedia://en/1055369), first edited and published by [Yoshida Mitsuyoshi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshida_Mitsuyoshi) in 1627. Over his lifetime, Mitsuyoshi revised *Jinkōki* several times.[^o3] The edition released in the eleventh year of the [Kan'ei era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kan'ei_era) (1641) became particularly widespread. The last version personally published by Mitsuyoshi was the *Idai* (井大), which came out in the eighteenth year of the Kan'ei (1634). Subsequent to that, various editions of *Jinkōki* were released, one of which includes *Shinpen Jinkōki* (新編塵劫記).[^o4]

:::

*Jinkōki* is one of the most popular and influential Japanese mathematics books in history,[^o8] having influenced [Seki Takakazu](wikipedia://en/532653), [Kaibara Ekken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaibara_Ekken), and many other later Japanese mathematicians.[^o5] It is partly based on the works of [Yuan dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuan_dynasty) mathematicians in China.[^o5]

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The name *Jinkōki* is derived from *jintenkō* (塵点劫), an immeasurably long span of time mentioned in the [Lotus Sutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotus_Sutra), hence the nuance of permanence in the [Sino-Japanese word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Japanese_vocabulary) *jinkō* and its reflection in English title.[^o5]

## Content {#Content}

The book contained instructions for dividing and multiplying with a [soroban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soroban) and mathematical problems relevant to merchants and craftsmen. The book also contained several interesting mathematical problems, and was the first Japanese book to use [printing in colour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_printing). As a result, the Jinkōki became the most popular Japanese mathematics book ever and one of the most widely read books of the [Edo period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_period). Mitsuyoshi made reference to everyday problems, such as buying and selling [rice](wikipedia://en/36979).[^o8]

The book was originally published in three volumes, the first of which mainly describes [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) and [division](wikipedia://en/53696) using the soroban. The second and third volumes include an assortment of practical and recreational problems. The included problems are not arranged according to any specific order.[^o9]

The book includes ideas that aimed to keep readers from boredom by adopting a wide variety of problems such as calculations of areas of rice fields, problems related to the construction of rivers and riverbanks, geometric progression, and the [Josephus problem](wikipedia://en/1810040). The Shinpen Jinkōki was the most widespread version among the copies of Jinkōki, and widely used as a textbook for use of the soroban throughout the Edo period.[^o10]

In addition to fundamental knowledge such as numerical notation, units, and multiplication tables, it also included slightly more specialised topics, such as methods to find [square roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_roots) and [cube roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube_roots), practical calculations of area, [currency conversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_conversion), and [interest calculation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interest_%28economics%29). The content covers almost all arithmetic needed in daily life at that time, and it is a characteristic of the book that explanations are given using familiar topics, such as using the increase of mice as an example for [geometric progression](wikipedia://en/23474670).[^o8]

Many different versions were published, with slightly modified content, and by the time of the [Meiji era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_era), over 400 editions of Jinkōki had been published.[^o11][^o3]
