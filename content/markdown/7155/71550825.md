# Bretagnolle–Huber inequality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bretagnolle–Huber inequality"}

In [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773), the **Bretagnolle–Huber inequality** bounds the [total variation](wikipedia://en/683561) distance between two probability distributions $P$ and $Q$ by a concave and [bounded function](wikipedia://en/311509) of the [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052) $D_\mathrm{KL}(P \parallel Q)$. The bound can be viewed as an alternative to the well-known [Pinsker's inequality](wikipedia://en/20064401): when $D_\mathrm{KL}(P \parallel Q)$ is large (larger than 2 for instance.[^o2]), Pinsker's inequality is vacuous, while Bretagnolle–Huber remains bounded and hence non-vacuous. It is used in statistics and [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488) to prove information-theoretic lower bounds relying on [hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis_testing).[^o3] ([Bretagnolle–Huber–Carol Inequality](wikipedia://en/32023963#Bretagnolle–Huber–Carol_inequality) is a variation of Concentration inequality for multinomially distributed random variables which bounds the total variation distance.)

:::

::: {#o5 .statement cue=heading name="Formal statement"}

## Formal statement {#Formal_statement}

### Preliminary definitions {#Preliminary_definitions}

Let $P$ and $Q$ be two [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) on a [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333) $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{F})$. Recall that the [total variation](wikipedia://en/683561) between $P$ and $Q$ is defined by

$$d_\mathrm{TV}(P,Q) = \sup_{A \in \mathcal{F}} \{|P(A)-Q(A)| \}.$$

The [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052) is defined as follows:

$$D_\mathrm{KL}(P \parallel Q) = \begin{cases} \int_{\mathcal{X}} \log\bigl(\frac{dP}{dQ}\bigr)\, dP & \text{if } P \ll Q, \\[1mm] +\infty & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

In the above, the notation $P\ll Q$ stands for [absolute continuity](wikipedia://en/252670) of $P$ with respect to $Q$, and $\frac{dP}{dQ}$ stands for the [Radon–Nikodym derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon–Nikodym_derivative) of $P$ with respect to $Q$.

### General statement {#General_statement}

The Bretagnolle–Huber inequality says:

$$d_\mathrm{TV}(P,Q) \leq \sqrt{1-\exp(-D_\mathrm{KL}(P \parallel Q))} \leq 1 - \frac{1}{2}\exp(-D_\mathrm{KL}(P \parallel Q))$$

:::

## Alternative version {#Alternative_version}

The following version is directly implied by the bound above but some authors[^o3] prefer stating it this way. Let $A\in \mathcal{F}$ be any event. Then

$$P(A) + Q(\bar{A}) \geq \frac{1}{2}\exp(-D_\mathrm{KL}(P \parallel Q))$$

where $\bar{A} = \Omega \smallsetminus A$ is the complement of $A$.

Indeed, by definition of the total variation, for any $A \in \mathcal{F}$,

$$\begin{aligned} Q(A) - P(A) \leq d_\mathrm{TV}(P,Q) & \leq 1- \frac{1}{2}\exp(-D_\mathrm{KL}(P \parallel Q)) \\ & = Q(A) + Q(\bar{A}) - \frac{1}{2}\exp(-D_\mathrm{KL}(P \parallel Q)) \end{aligned}$$

Rearranging, we obtain the claimed lower bound on $P(A)+Q(\bar{A})$.

::: {#o10 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

We prove the main statement following the ideas in Tsybakov's book (Lemma 2.6, page 89),[^o11] which differ from the original proof[^o12] (see C.Canonne's note [^o2] for a modernized retranscription of their argument).

The proof is in two steps:

\1. Prove using Cauchy–Schwarz that the total variation is related to the [Bhattacharyya coefficient](wikipedia://en/831261) (right-hand side of the inequality):

$$1-d_\mathrm{TV}(P,Q)^2 \geq \left(\int \sqrt{PQ}\right)^2$$

\2. Prove by a clever application of Jensen’s inequality that

$$\left(\int \sqrt{PQ}\right)^2 \geq \exp(-D_\mathrm{KL}(P \parallel Q))$$

- Step 1:

First notice that

$$d_\mathrm{TV}(P,Q) = 1-\int \min(P,Q) = \int \max(P,Q) -1$$

To see this, denote $A^* = \arg\max_{A\in \Omega} |P(A)-Q(A)|$ and [without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000), assume that $P(A^*)>Q(A^*)$ such that $d_\mathrm{TV}(P,Q)=P(A^*)-Q(A^*)$. Then we can rewrite

$$d_\mathrm{TV}(P,Q) = \int_{A^*} \max(P,Q) - \int_{A^*} \min(P,Q)$$

And then adding and removing $\int_{\bar{A^*}} \max(P,Q) \text{ or } \int_{\bar{A^*}}\min(P,Q)$ we obtain both identities.

Then

$$\begin{aligned} 1-d_\mathrm{TV}(P,Q)^2 & = (1-d_\mathrm{TV}(P,Q))(1+d_\mathrm{TV}(P,Q)) \\ & = \int \min(P,Q) \int \max(P,Q) \\ & \geq \left(\int \sqrt{\min(P,Q) \max(P,Q)}\right)^2 \\ & = \left(\int \sqrt{PQ}\right)^2 \end{aligned}$$

because $PQ = \min(P,Q) \max(P,Q).$

- Step 2:

We write $(\cdot)^2=\exp(2\log(\cdot))$ and apply [Jensen's inequality](wikipedia://en/297811):

$$\begin{aligned} \left(\int \sqrt{PQ}\right)^2 &= \exp\left(2\log\left(\int \sqrt{PQ}\right)\right) \\ & = \exp\left(2\log\left(\int P\sqrt{\frac{Q}{P}}\right)\right) \\ & =\exp\left(2\log\left(\operatorname{E}_P \left[\left(\sqrt{\frac{P}{Q}}\right)^{-1} \, \right] \right) \right) \\ & \geq \exp\left(\operatorname{E}_P\left[-\log\left(\frac{P}{Q} \right)\right] \right) = \exp(-D_{KL}(P,Q)) \end{aligned}$$

Combining the results of steps 1 and 2 leads to the claimed bound on the total variation.

:::

## Examples of applications {#Examples_of_applications}

### Sample complexity of biased coin tosses {#Sample_complexity_of_biased_coin_tosses}

Source:[^o2]

The question is *How many coin tosses do I need to distinguish a fair coin from a biased one?*

Assume you have 2 coins, a [fair coin](wikipedia://en/9597100) ([Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/199189) distributed with mean $p_1=1/2$) and an $\varepsilon$-biased coin ($p_2=1/2+\varepsilon$). Then, in order to identify the biased coin with probability at least $1-\delta$ (for some $\delta>0$), at least

$$n\geq \frac{1}{2\varepsilon^2}\log\left(\frac{1}{2\delta}\right).$$

In order to obtain this lower bound we impose that the total variation distance between two sequences of $n$ samples is at least $1-2\delta$. This is because the total variation upper bounds the probability of under- or over-estimating the coins' means. Denote $P_1^n$ and $P_2^n$ the respective joint distributions of the $n$ coin tosses for each coin, then

We have

$$\begin{aligned} (1-2\delta)^2 & \leq d_\mathrm{TV}\left(P_1^n, P_2^n \right)^2 \\[4pt] & \leq 1-e^{-D_\mathrm{KL}(P_1^n \parallel P_2^n)} \\[4pt] &= 1-e^{-nD_\mathrm{KL}(P_1 \parallel P_2)}\\[4pt] & = 1-e^{-n\frac{\log(1/(1-4\varepsilon^2))}{2}} \end{aligned}$$

The result is obtained by rearranging the terms.

### Information-theoretic lower bound for *k*-armed bandit games {#Information-theoretic_lower_bound_for_k-armed_bandit_games}

In [multi-armed bandit](wikipedia://en/2854828), a lower bound on the minimax regret of any bandit algorithm can be proved using Bretagnolle–Huber and its consequence on hypothesis testing (see Chapter 15 of *Bandit Algorithms*[^o3]).

## History {#History}

The result was first proved in 1979 by Jean Bretagnolle and Catherine Huber, and published in the proceedings of the Strasbourg Probability Seminar.[^o12] Alexandre Tsybakov's book[^o11] features an early re-publication of the inequality and its attribution to Bretagnolle and Huber, which is presented as an early and less general version of Assouad's lemma (see notes 2.8). A constant improvement on Bretagnolle–Huber was proved in 2014 as a consequence of an extension of [Fano's Inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fano's_Inequality).[^o17]
