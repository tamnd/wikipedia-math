# Hinged dissection

*Image: Hinged dissection 3-4-6-3 loop.gif.* Loop animation of hinged dissections from triangle to square, then to hexagon, then back again to triangle. Notice that the chain of pieces can be entirely connected in a ring during the rearrangement from square to hexagon.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hinged dissection"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **hinged dissection**, also known as a **swing-hinged dissection** or **Dudeney dissection**,[^o2] is a kind of [geometric dissection](wikipedia://en/2915839) in which all of the pieces are connected into a chain by "hinged" points, such that the rearrangement from one [figure](wikipedia://en/169191) to another can be carried out by swinging the chain continuously, without severing any of the connections.[^o3] Typically, it is assumed that the pieces are allowed to overlap in the folding and unfolding process;[^o4] this is sometimes called the "wobbly-hinged" model of hinged dissection.[^o5]

:::

## History {#History}

*Image: Hinged haberdasher.svg.* Dudeney's hinged dissection of a triangle into a square.

*Image: Animated hinged dissection triangle square hexagram.gif.* Animation of hinged dissection from hexagram to triangle to square

The concept of hinged dissections was popularised by the author of [mathematical puzzles](wikipedia://en/366445), [Henry Dudeney](wikipedia://en/465317). He proposed in 1902 the hinged dissection of a square into a triangle and C.W. McElroy submitted the famous four-piece solution (pictured) that Dudeney published in his 1907 book [The Canterbury Puzzles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Canterbury_Puzzles).[^o7] The [Wallace–Bolyai–Gerwien theorem](wikipedia://en/224248), first proven in 1807, states that any two equal-area polygons must have a common dissection. However, the question of whether two such polygons must also share a *hinged* dissection remained open until 2007, when [Erik Demaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erik_Demaine) *et al.* proved that there must always exist such a hinged dissection, and provided a constructive algorithm to produce them.[^o5][^o8][^o9] This proof holds even under the assumption that the pieces may not overlap while swinging, and can be generalised to any pair of three-dimensional figures which have a common dissection (see [Hilbert's third problem](wikipedia://en/152703)).[^o8][^o10] In three dimensions, however, the pieces are not guaranteed to swing without overlap.[^o11]

## Other hinges {#Other_hinges}

*Image: Hinged square to pentagon.gif.* Hinged square to pentagon

Other types of "hinges" have been considered in the context of dissections. A **twist-hinge dissection** is one which use a three-dimensional "hinge" which is placed on the edges of pieces rather than their vertices, allowing them to be "flipped" three-dimensionally.[^o13][^o14] As of 2002, the question of whether any two polygons must have a common twist-hinged dissection remains unsolved.[^o15]
