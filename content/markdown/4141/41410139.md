# Change of rings

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Change of rings"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), **change of rings** refers to one of several related ways for changing the coefficient ring of a [module](wikipedia://en/276410). Given (possibly non-commutative) unital rings $R$ and $S$ and a ring homorphism $\rho : R \to S$,

:::

- **restriction of scalars** turns an $S$-module $V$ into an $R$-module $\rho^* V$.
- **extension of scalars** turns an $R$-module $V$ into an $S$-module $\rho_! V$, the **induced module**.
- **coextension of scalars** turns an $R$-module $V$ into an $S$-module $\rho_* V$, the **coinduced module**.

In this article we describe the constructions on left modules, although all constructions work for right modules mutatis mutandis. Each of these constructions extends to a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987), so that we have an [adjoint triple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjunction_%28category_theory%29)

*Image: Change of rings adjunction.svg.*

where ${}_R\mathsf{Mod}$ and ${}_S\mathsf{Mod}$ are the [categories of left $R$- and $S$-modules](wikipedia://en/11231812) respectively.[^n1]

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Restriction of scalars"}

## Restriction of scalars {#Restriction_of_scalars}

Of the three constructions named above, restriction of scalars is the easiest to describe. Suppose that $V$ is a left $S$-module. Then $V$ it can be regarded as an $R$-module $\rho^* V$ with the [pullback](wikipedia://en/9413508) $R$-action

$$\begin{aligned} R \times V &\longrightarrow M \\ (x , v) &\longmapsto \rho(x) \cdot v \end{aligned}$$

where $\rho(x) \cdot v$ denotes the action defined by the $S$-module structure on $V$.[^n2]

### Functoriality {#Functoriality}

Restriction of scalars $\rho^* : {}_S\mathsf{Mod} \to {}_R\mathsf{Mod}$ extends to a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) between [categories of modules](wikipedia://en/11231812). Any $S$-morphism $f : V \to W$automatically becomes an $R$-morphism between the restrictions of $V$ and $W$. Indeed, if $v \in V$ and $x \in R$, then

$$f(xv) = f(\rho(x) \cdot v) = \rho(x) \cdot f(v) = x \cdot f(v).$$

If $R$ is the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613), then this is just the [forgetful functor](wikipedia://en/844783) from modules to abelian groups.

:::

## Extension of scalars {#Extension_of_scalars}

Let ${}_SS{}_R$ denote $S$ regarded as an $(S,R)$-[bimodule](wikipedia://en/492612), where $s_1 \cdot s_2 \cdot r = s_1\,s_2\,\rho(r)$ for $s_1,s_2 \in S$ and $r \in R$. For a given left $R$-module $V$, the induced module $\rho_!V$ is defined as the [tensor product of bimodules](wikipedia://en/2993836)

$$\begin{aligned} \rho_!V = {}_SS_R \otimes_R V, \end{aligned}$$

which is to say $\rho_!V$ has the left $S$-action defined by $s_1 \cdot (s_2 \otimes v) = s_1s_2 \otimes v$ for $s_1,s_2 \in S$ and $v \in V$.

### Functoriality {#Functoriality}

Extension of scalars $\rho_! : {}_R\mathsf{Mod} \to {}_S\mathsf{Mod}$ extends to a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) between [categories of modules](wikipedia://en/11231812), so that if $f : V \to W$ is an $R$-morphism then $\rho_! V = {}_SS_R \otimes_R f$. As a functor, $\rho_!$ is determined up to natural isomorphism as the [left adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_adjoint) of $\rho^* : {}_S\mathsf{Mod} \to {}_R\mathsf{Mod}$, with [unit of adjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_adjunction)

$$\begin{aligned} \eta_V : V &\to \rho^* \rho_!V \\ v &\mapsto 1 \otimes v. \end{aligned}$$

Unfurling the corresponding [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245), this means that for any left $R$-module $V$, left $S$-module $W$, and $R$-morphism $f : V \to \rho^* W$, there exists a unique $S$-morphism $f^\sharp : \rho_* V \to W$ such that $\rho^* f^\sharp \circ \eta_V = f$.

*Image: Extension of scalars unit.svg.*

::: {#o10 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

One of the simplest examples is [complexification](wikipedia://en/755604), which is extension of scalars from the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) to the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826). More generally, given any [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) *K* \< *L,* one can extend scalars from *K* to *L.* In the language of fields, a module over a field is called a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370), and thus extension of scalars converts a vector space over *K* to a vector space over *L.* This can also be done for [division algebras](wikipedia://en/26549625), as is done in [quaternionification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternionification) (extension from the reals to the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440)).

More generally, given a homomorphism from a field or *commutative* ring *R* to a ring *S,* the ring *S* can be thought of as an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) over *R,* and thus when one extends scalars on an *R*-module, the resulting module can be thought of alternatively as an *S*-module, or as an *R*-module with an [algebra representation](wikipedia://en/854978) of *S* (as an *R*-algebra). For example, the result of complexifying a real vector space (*R* = **R**, *S* = **C**) can be interpreted either as a complex vector space (*S*-module) or as a real vector space with a [linear complex structure](wikipedia://en/1549805) (algebra representation of *S* as an *R*-module).

:::

::: {#o12 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

### Applications {#Applications}

This generalization is useful even for the study of fields – notably, many algebraic objects associated to a field are not themselves fields, but are instead rings, such as algebras over a field, as in [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200). Just as one can extend scalars on vector spaces, one can also extend scalars on [group algebras](wikipedia://en/349223) and also on modules over group algebras, i.e., [group representations](wikipedia://en/12695). Particularly useful is relating how [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982) change under extension of scalars – for example, the representation of the cyclic group of order 4, given by rotation of the plane by 90°, is an irreducible 2-dimensional *real* representation, but on extension of scalars to the complex numbers, it splits into 2 complex representations of dimension 1. This corresponds to the fact that the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) of this operator, $x^2+1,$ is irreducible of degree 2 over the reals, but factors into 2 factors of degree 1 over the complex numbers – it has no real eigenvalues, but 2 complex eigenvalues.

:::

## Coextension of scalars {#Coextension_of_scalars}

Let ${}_RS$ denote $S$ regarded as a left $R$-module, where $r \cdot s = \rho(r)\,s$ for $r \in R$ and $s \in S$. For a given left $R$-module $V$, the underlying [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) of the coinduced module $\rho_* V$ is ${}_R\mathsf{Mod}({}_RS, V)$, consisting of $R$-morphisms ${}_RS \to V$. The $S$-action on $\rho_*V$ is defined by $(s_1f)(s_2) = f(s_2s_1)$ for $s_1,s_2 \in S$ and $v \in V$.

### Functoriality {#Functoriality}

Coextension of scalars $\rho_* : {}_R\mathsf{Mod} \to {}_S\mathsf{Mod}$ extends to a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) between [categories of modules](wikipedia://en/11231812), so that if $g : V \to W$ is an $R$-morphism then $(\rho_* g)(f) = g \circ f$. As a functor, $\rho_*$ is determined up to natural isomorphism as the [right adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_adjoint) of $\rho^* : {}_S\mathsf{Mod} \to {}_R\mathsf{Mod}$, with [counit of adjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counit_of_adjunction)

$$\begin{aligned} \epsilon_V : \rho^* \rho_* V &\to V \\ f &\mapsto f(1) \end{aligned}$$

Unfurling the corresponding [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245), his means that for any left $R$-module $W$, left $S$-module $V$, and $T$-morphism $f : \rho^* W \to V$, there exists a unique $R$-morphism $f^\flat : W \to \rho^* V$ such that $\epsilon_V \circ \rho^* f^\flat = f$.
