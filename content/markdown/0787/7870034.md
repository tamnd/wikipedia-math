# Algebraic logic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Algebraic logic"}

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), **algebraic logic** is the reasoning obtained by manipulating equations with [free variables](wikipedia://en/147460).

:::

What is now usually called classical algebraic logic focuses on the identification and algebraic description of [models](wikipedia://en/19858) appropriate for the study of various logics (in the form of classes of algebras that constitute the [algebraic semantics](wikipedia://en/2848519) for these [deductive systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductive_system)) and connected problems like [representation](wikipedia://en/6355948) and duality. Well known results like the [representation theorem for Boolean algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representation_theorem_for_Boolean_algebras) and [Stone duality](wikipedia://en/379619) fall under the umbrella of classical algebraic logic (Czelakowski 2003).

Works in the more recent [abstract algebraic logic](wikipedia://en/4613861) (AAL) focus on the process of algebraization itself, like classifying various forms of algebraizability using the [Leibniz operator](wikipedia://en/4827691) (Czelakowski 2003).

## Calculus of relations {#Calculus_of_relations}

A homogeneous [binary relation](wikipedia://en/3931) is found in the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of $X \times X$ for some set *X*, while a [heterogeneous relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterogeneous_relation) is found in the power set of $X \times Y$, where $X \ne Y$. Whether a given relation holds for two individuals is one [bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit) of information, so relations are studied with Boolean arithmetic. Elements of the power set are partially ordered by [inclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusion_%28set_theory%29), and lattice of these sets becomes an algebra through *relative multiplication* or [composition of relations](wikipedia://en/7472170).

"The basic operations are set-theoretic union, intersection and complementation, the relative multiplication, and conversion."[^o5]

The *conversion* refers to the [converse relation](wikipedia://en/3941020) that always exists, contrary to function theory. A given relation may be represented by a [logical matrix](wikipedia://en/4109196); then the converse relation is represented by the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) matrix. A relation obtained as the composition of two others is then represented by the logical matrix obtained by [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) using Boolean arithmetic.

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

An example of calculus of relations arises in [erotetics](wikipedia://en/12362175), the theory of questions. In the universe of utterances there are [statements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statement_%28logic%29) *S* and [questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Question) *Q*. There are two relations $\pi$ and α from *Q* to *S*: *q* α *a* holds when *a* is a direct answer to question *q*. The other relation, *q* $\pi$ *p* holds when *p* is a [presupposition](wikipedia://en/1965727#Logical_construct) of question *q*. The converse relation $\pi$<sup>T</sup> runs from *S* to *Q* so that the composition $\pi$<sup>T</sup>α is a homogeneous relation on *S*.[^o8] The art of putting the right question to elicit a sufficient answer is recognized in [Socratic method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socratic_method) dialogue.

:::

### Functions {#Functions}

The description of the key binary relation properties has been formulated with the calculus of relations. The univalence property of functions describes a relation $R$ that satisfies the formula $R^T R \subseteq I ,$ where $I$ is the identity relation on the range of $R$. The injective property corresponds to univalence of $R^T$, or the formula $R R^T \subseteq I ,$ where this time $I$ is the identity on the domain of $R$.

But a univalent relation is only a [partial function](wikipedia://en/23577), while a univalent [total relation](wikipedia://en/1482218) is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427). The formula for totality is $I \subseteq R R^T .$ [Charles Loewner](wikipedia://en/9337483) and [Gunther Schmidt](wikipedia://en/44939247) use the term **mapping** for a total, univalent relation.[^o10][^o11]

The facility of [complementary relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_relation) inspired [Augustus De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289) and [Ernst Schröder](wikipedia://en/448015) to introduce [equivalences](wikipedia://en/7472170#Schröder_rules) using $\bar{R}$ for the complement of relation $R$. These equivalences provide alternative formulas for univalent relations ($R \bar{I} \subseteq \bar{R}$), and total relations ($\bar{R} \subseteq R \bar{I}$). Therefore, mappings satisfy the formula $\bar{R} = R \bar{I} .$ Schmidt uses this principle as "slipping below negation from the left".[^o12] For a mapping $f$, $f\bar{A} = \overline{f A} .$

### Abstraction {#Abstraction}

The **relation algebra** structure, based in set theory, was transcended by Tarski with axioms describing it. Then he asked if every algebra satisfying the axioms could be represented by a set relation. The negative answer[^o14] opened the frontier of [abstract algebraic logic](wikipedia://en/4613861).[^o15][^o16][^o41]

## Algebras as models of logics {#Algebras_as_models_of_logics}

Algebraic logic treats [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364), often [bounded lattices](wikipedia://en/469304), as models (interpretations) of certain [logics](wikipedia://en/46426065), making logic a branch of [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924).

In algebraic logic:

- Variables are tacitly [universally quantified](wikipedia://en/76174) over some [universe of discourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe_of_discourse). There are no [existentially quantified variables](wikipedia://en/91420) or [open formulas](wikipedia://en/2129527);
- [Terms](wikipedia://en/24885593) are built up from variables using primitive and defined [operations](wikipedia://en/4140245). There are no [connectives](wikipedia://en/18153);
- [Formulas](wikipedia://en/164040), built from terms in the usual way, can be equated if they are [logically equivalent](wikipedia://en/169324). To express a [tautology](wikipedia://en/4495335), equate a formula with a [truth value](wikipedia://en/161711);
- The rules of proof are the substitution of equals for equals, and uniform replacement. [Modus ponens](wikipedia://en/18900) remains valid, but is seldom employed.

In the table below, the left column contains one or more [logical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_system) or mathematical systems, and the algebraic structure which are its models are shown on the right in the same row. Some of these structures are either [Boolean algebras](wikipedia://en/3959) or [proper extensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_extension) thereof. [Modal](wikipedia://en/333365) and other [nonclassical logics](wikipedia://en/19636#Nonclassical_and_modal_logic) are typically modeled by what are called "Boolean algebras with operators."

Algebraic formalisms going beyond [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) in at least some respects include:

- [Combinatory logic](wikipedia://en/149848), having the expressive power of [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553);
- [Relation algebra](wikipedia://en/4363670), arguably the paradigmatic algebraic logic, can express [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic) and most [axiomatic set theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_set_theory), including the canonical [ZFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZFC).

## History {#History}

Algebraic logic is, perhaps, the oldest approach to formal logic, arguably beginning with a number of memoranda [Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) wrote in the 1680s, some of which were published in the 19th century and translated into English by [Clarence Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarence_Lewis) in 1918.[^o38] But nearly all of Leibniz's known work on algebraic logic was published only in 1903 after [Louis Couturat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Couturat) discovered it in Leibniz's [Nachlass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nachlass). (Parkinson 1966) and (Loemker 1969) translated selections from Couturat's volume into English.

Modern mathematical logic began in 1847, with two pamphlets whose respective authors were [George Boole](wikipedia://en/38298)[^o32] and [Augustus De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289).[^o33] In 1870 [Charles Sanders Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) published the first of several works on the [logic of relatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_of_relatives). [Alexander Macfarlane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Macfarlane) published his *Principles of the Algebra of Logic*[^o34] in 1879, and in 1883, [Christine Ladd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christine_Ladd), a student of Peirce at [Johns Hopkins University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University), published "On the Algebra of Logic".[^o35] Logic turned more algebraic when [binary relations](wikipedia://en/3931) were combined with [composition of relations](wikipedia://en/7472170). For sets *A* and *B*, a [relation](wikipedia://en/3931) over *A* and *B* is represented as a member of the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of *A*×*B* with properties described by [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/54476844). The "calculus of relations"[^o41] is arguably the culmination of Leibniz's approach to logic. At the [Hochschule Karlsruhe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hochschule_Karlsruhe) the calculus of relations was described by [Ernst Schröder](wikipedia://en/448015).[^o36] In particular he formulated [Schröder rules](wikipedia://en/7472170#Schröder_rules), though De Morgan had anticipated them with his Theorem K.

In 1903 [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) developed the calculus of relations and [logicism](wikipedia://en/350672) as his version of pure mathematics based on the operations of the calculus as [primitive notions](wikipedia://en/23706#Russell's_primitives).[^o37] The "Boole–Schröder algebra of logic" was developed at [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley) in a [textbook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textbook) by [Clarence Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarence_Lewis) in 1918.[^o38] He treated the logic of relations as derived from the [propositional functions](wikipedia://en/229146) of two or more variables.

[Hugh MacColl](wikipedia://en/3380850), [Gottlob Frege](wikipedia://en/48416), [Giuseppe Peano](wikipedia://en/13043), and [A. N. Whitehead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._N._Whitehead) all shared Leibniz's dream of combining [symbolic logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_logic), [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy).

Some writings by [Leopold Löwenheim](wikipedia://en/755668) and [Thoralf Skolem](wikipedia://en/453765) on algebraic logic appeared after the 1910–13 publication of *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)*, and Tarski revived interest in relations with his 1941 essay "On the Calculus of Relations".[^o41]

According to [Helena Rasiowa](wikipedia://en/3482432), "The years 1920-40 saw, in particular in the Polish school of logic, researches on non-classical propositional calculi conducted by what is termed the [logical matrix](wikipedia://en/4109196) method. Since logical matrices are certain abstract algebras, this led to the use of an algebraic method in logic."[^o39]

(Brady 2000) discusses the rich historical connections between algebraic logic and [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858). The founders of model theory, Ernst Schröder and Leopold Loewenheim, were logicians in the algebraic tradition. [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920), the founder of [set theoretic](wikipedia://en/27553) model theory as a major branch of contemporary mathematical logic, also:

- Initiated abstract algebraic logic with [relation algebras](wikipedia://en/4363670)[^o41]
- Invented [cylindric algebra](wikipedia://en/7852887)
- Co-discovered [Lindenbaum–Tarski algebra](wikipedia://en/990343).

In the practice of the calculus of relations, [Jacques Riguet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Riguet) used the algebraic logic to advance useful concepts: he extended the concept of an equivalence relation (on a set) to the heterogeneous case with the notion of a [difunctional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difunctional) relation. Riguet also extended ordering to the heterogeneous context by his note that a staircase logical matrix has a complement that is also a staircase, and that the theorem of [N. M. Ferrers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N._M._Ferrers) follows from interpretation of the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) of a staircase. Riguet generated *rectangular relations* by taking the [outer product](wikipedia://en/103109) of logical vectors; these contribute to the *non-enlargeable rectangles* of [formal concept analysis](wikipedia://en/313845).

Leibniz had no influence on the rise of algebraic logic because his logical writings were little studied before the Parkinson and Loemker translations. Our present understanding of Leibniz as a logician stems mainly from the work of Wolfgang Lenzen, summarized in (Lenzen 2004). To see how present-day work in logic and [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics) can draw inspiration from, and shed light on, Leibniz's thought, see (Zalta 2000).
