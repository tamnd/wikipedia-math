# Exchange matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Exchange matrix"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), the **exchange matrices** (also called the **reversal matrix**, **backward identity**, or **standard involutory permutation**) are special cases of [permutation matrices](wikipedia://en/238849), where the 1 elements reside on the [antidiagonal](wikipedia://en/194990#Antidiagonal) and all other elements are zero. In other words, they are 'row-reversed' or 'column-reversed' versions of the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718).[^o2]

:::

$$\begin{aligned} J_2 &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \\[4pt] J_3 &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \\ &\quad \vdots \\[2pt] J_n &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \,{}_{_{\displaystyle\cdot}} \!\, {}^{_{_{\displaystyle\cdot}}} \! \dot\phantom{j} & \vdots & \vdots \\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

If $J$ is an $n \times n$ exchange matrix, then the elements of $J$ are

$$J_{i,j} = \begin{cases} 1, & i + j = n + 1 \\ 0, & i + j \ne n + 1\\ \end{cases}$$

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- Premultiplying a matrix by an exchange matrix flips vertically the positions of the former's rows, i.e., $$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & 8 & 9 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$
- Postmultiplying a matrix by an exchange matrix flips horizontally the positions of the former's columns, i.e., $$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 & 1 \\ 6 & 5 & 4 \\ 9 & 8 & 7 \end{pmatrix}.$$
- Exchange matrices are [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474); that is: $$J_n^\mathsf{T} = J_n.$$
- For any [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $k$: $$J_n^k = \begin{cases} I & \text{ if } k \text{ is even,} \\[2pt] J_n & \text{ if } k \text{ is odd.} \end{cases}$$ In particular, $J_{n}$ is an [involutory matrix](wikipedia://en/7917643); that is, $$J_n^{-1} = J_n.$$
- The [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) of $J_{n}$ is 1 if $n$ is odd and 0 if $n$ is even. In other words: $$\operatorname{tr}(J_n) = \frac{1-(-1)^n}{2} = n\bmod 2.$$
- The [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of $J_{n}$ is: $$\det(J_n) = (-1)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor} = (-1)^\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$$ As a function of $n$, it has period 4, giving 1, 1, −1, −1 when $n$ is [congruent modulo 4](wikipedia://en/20087) to 0, 1, 2, and 3 respectively.
- The [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) of $J_{n}$ is: $$\det(\lambda I- J_n) = (\lambda -1)^{\lceil n/2\rceil}(\lambda +1)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}= \begin{cases} \big[(\lambda+1)(\lambda-1)\big]^\frac{n}{2} & \text{ if } n \text{ is even,} \\[4pt] (\lambda-1)^\frac{n+1}{2}(\lambda+1)^\frac{n-1}{2} & \text{ if } n \text{ is odd,} \end{cases}$$

its [eigenvalues](wikipedia://en/2161429) are 1 (with multiplicity $\lceil n/2\rceil$) and -1 (with multiplicity $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$).

- The [adjugate matrix](wikipedia://en/202840) of $J_{n}$ is: $$\operatorname{adj}(J_n) = \operatorname{sgn}(\pi_n) J_n.$$ (where $\operatorname{sgn}$ is the [sign](wikipedia://en/49727) of the [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) $\pi_{k}$ of $k$ elements).

:::

## Relationships {#Relationships}

- An exchange matrix is the simplest [anti-diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/2299135).
- Any matrix $A$ satisfying the condition $AJ = JA$ is said to be [centrosymmetric](wikipedia://en/7079248).
- Any matrix $A$ satisfying the condition $AJ = JA^{T}$ is said to be [persymmetric](wikipedia://en/10535463).
- Symmetric matrices $A$ that satisfy the condition $AJ = JA$ are called [bisymmetric](wikipedia://en/13714248) matrices. Bisymmetric matrices are both centrosymmetric and persymmetric.
