# Lucas's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lucas's theorem"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **Lucas's theorem** expresses the [remainder](wikipedia://en/502897) of division of the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668) $\tbinom{m}{n}$ by a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) *p* in terms of the [base](wikipedia://en/701207) *p* expansions of the integers *m* and *n*.

:::

Lucas's theorem first appeared in 1878 in papers by [Édouard Lucas](wikipedia://en/150102).[^o2]

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

For non-negative integers *m* and *n* and a prime *p*, the following [congruence relation](wikipedia://en/20087) holds:

$$\binom{m}{n}\equiv\prod_{i=0}^k\binom{m_i}{n_i}\pmod p,$$

where

$$m=m_kp^k+m_{k-1}p^{k-1}+\cdots +m_1p+m_0,$$

and

$$n=n_kp^k+n_{k-1}p^{k-1}+\cdots +n_1p+n_0$$

are the [base *p* expansions](wikipedia://en/437052) of *m* and *n* respectively. This uses the convention that $\tbinom{m}{n} = 0$ if *m* \< *n*.

:::

::: {#o6 .proof cue=heading name="Proofs"}

## Proofs {#Proofs}

There are several ways to prove Lucas's theorem.

::: {#o7 .proof cue=math-proof name="Combinatorial proof using a group action"}

**Combinatorial proof using a group action.**

Let *M* be a set with *m* elements, and arbitrarily divide it into *m<sub>i</sub>* cycles of length *p<sup>i</sup>* for the various values of *i*. Then each of these cycles can be rotated separately by a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) *C<sub>p<sup>i</sup></sub>*, so that the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G* which is the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of all these cyclic groups (one for each cycle) [acts on](wikipedia://en/12781) *M*. It thus also acts on the set of *n*-element subsets *N* of *M*, the number of which is $\tbinom{m}{n}$. This is the group action we consider in the sequel.

Since the number of elements in *G* is a power of *p*, the same is true of any of its [orbits](wikipedia://en/12781#Orbits_and_stabilizers), by the [orbit-stabilizer theorem](wikipedia://en/12781#Orbit–stabilizer_theorem). Hence, $\tbinom{m}{n}$ is congruent modulo *p* to the number of sets *N* whose orbit is of size 1, i.e., to the number of [fixed points](wikipedia://en/449738#Fixed_point_of_a_group_action) of the group action.

Since all cycles can be independently rotated by our group *G*, the fixed points of the action are those subsets *N* that are a union of some of the cycles. This means that *N* must consist of exactly *n<sub>i</sub>* cycles of size *p<sup>i</sup>* for each *i*, for the same reason that the integer *n* has a unique representation in base *p*. Thus the number of choices for *N* is exactly $\prod_{i=0}^k\binom{m_i}{n_i}$.

:::

::: {#o8 .proof cue=math-proof name="Proof based on generating functions"}

**Proof based on generating functions.**

This proof is due to [Nathan Fine](wikipedia://en/6042215).[^o9]

If *p* is a prime and *n* is an integer with 1 ≤ *n* ≤ *p* − 1, then the numerator of the binomial coefficient

$\binom p n = \frac{p \cdot (p-1) \cdots (p-n+1)}{n \cdot (n-1) \cdots 1}$ is divisible by *p* but the denominator is not. Hence *p* divides $\tbinom{p}{n}$. Because of the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677), this means that

$(1+X)^p\equiv1+X^p\pmod{p}.$ Continuing by [induction](wikipedia://en/18881), we have for every nonnegative integer *i* that

$$(1+X)^{p^i}\equiv1+X^{p^i}\pmod{p}.$$

Now let *m* be a nonnegative integer, and let *p* be a prime. Write *m* in base *p*, so that $m=\sum_{i=0}^{k}m_ip^i$ for some nonnegative integer *k* and integers *m*<sub>*i*</sub> with 0 ≤ *m*<sub>*i*</sub> ≤ *p* − 1. Then

$\begin{aligned} \sum_{n=0}^{m}\binom{m}{n}X^n & =(1+X)^m=\prod_{i=0}^{k}\left((1+X)^{p^i}\right)^{m_i}\\ & \equiv \prod_{i=0}^{k}\left(1+X^{p^i}\right)^{m_i}\\ &=\prod_{i=0}^{k}\left(\sum_{j_i=0}^{m_i}\binom{m_i}{j_i}X^{j_ip^i}\right)\\ & =\sum_{n=0}^{m}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k}\binom{m_i}{n_i}\right)X^n \pmod{p}. \end{aligned}$ In the last equality we use [distributivity](wikipedia://en/103118) and the fact that the representation of *n* in base *p* is unique, where *n*<sub>*i*</sub> is the *i*-th digit in the base *p* representation of *n*. Comparing the coefficients of *X*<sup>*n*</sup> in the very first and last sum, we obtain Lucas's theorem.

## Consequences {#Consequences}

One consequence of Lucas's theorem is that the binomial coefficient $\tbinom{m}{n}$ is divisible by the prime *p* [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) at least one of the digits of the base-*p* representation of *n* is greater than the corresponding digit of *m*. In particular, $\tbinom{m}{n}$ is [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) if and only if the positions of the ones in the [binary expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_expansion) of *n* are a subset of the positions of the ones in that of *m*. This leads to a peculiar distribution of odd numbers in [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497), resembling [Sierpiński 's triangle](wikipedia://en/29638), shown to the right.

## Non-prime moduli {#Non-prime_moduli}

Lucas's theorem can be generalized to give an expression for the remainder when $\tbinom mn$ is divided by a [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483) *p*<sup>*k*</sup>. However, the formulas become more complicated.

If the modulus is the square of a prime *p*, the following congruence relation holds for all 0 ≤ *s* ≤ *r* ≤ *p* − 1, *a* ≥ 0, and *b* ≥ 0:

$$\binom{pa+r}{pb+s}\equiv\binom ab\binom rs(1+pa(H_r-H_{r-s})+pb(H_{r-s}-H_s))\pmod{p^2},$$

where $H_n=1+\tfrac12+\tfrac13+\cdots+\tfrac1n$ is the *n*th [harmonic number](wikipedia://en/214729).[^o12] Generalizations of Lucas's theorem for higher prime powers *p*<sup>*k*</sup> are also given by Davis and Webb (1990)[^o13] and Granville (1997).[^o14]

[Kummer's theorem](wikipedia://en/33758554) asserts that the largest [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *k* such that *p*<sup>*k*</sup> divides the binomial coefficient $\tbinom{m}{n}$ (or in other words, the [valuation](wikipedia://en/16047618) of the binomial coefficient with respect to the prime *p*) is equal to the number of [carries](wikipedia://en/1629687) that occur when *n* and *m* − *n* are added in the [base *p*](wikipedia://en/437052#Base_of_the_numeral_system).

## *q*-binomial coefficients {#q-binomial_coefficients}

There is a generalization of Lucas's theorem for the [*q*-binomial coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q-binomial_coefficient). It asserts that if *a*, *b*, *r*, *s*, *k* are integers, where 0 ≤ *b*, *s* \< *k*, then

$$\begin{bmatrix} ka+b \\ kr+s \end{bmatrix}_q \equiv \binom{a}{r} \begin{bmatrix} b \\ s \end{bmatrix}_q \mod{\Phi_k},$$

where $\begin{bmatrix} ka+b \\ kr+s \end{bmatrix}_q$ and $\begin{bmatrix} b \\ s \end{bmatrix}_q$ are *q*-binomial coefficients, $\binom{a}{r}$ is a usual binomial coefficient, and $\Phi_k$ is the *k*th [cyclotomic polynomial](wikipedia://en/171992) (in the variable *q*).[^o16]
