# Projective line over a ring

*Image: Projectivisation\_F7P%5E1.svg.* Eight colors illustrate the projective line over Galois field GF(7)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Projective line over a ring"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **projective line over a ring** is an extension of the concept of [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603). Given a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *A* (with 1), the projective line P<sup>1</sup>(*A*) over *A* consists of points identified by [projective coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_coordinates). Let *A*<sup>×</sup> be the [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) of *A*; pairs (*a*, *b*) and (*c*, *d*) from *A* × *A* are related when there is a *u* in *A*<sup>×</sup> such that *ua* = *c* and *ub* = *d*. This relation is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259). A typical [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) is written *U*\[*a*, *b*\].

:::

P<sup>1</sup>(*A*) = $\{U[a, b]\mid aA + bA = A\}$, that is, *U*\[*a*, *b*\] is in the projective line if the [one-sided ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_ideal) generated by *a* and *b* is all of *A*.

The projective line P<sup>1</sup>(*A*) is equipped with a [group of homographies](wikipedia://en/3051962#Homography_groups). The homographies are expressed through use of the [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) over *A* and its group of units *V* as follows: If *c* is in Z(*A*<sup>×</sup>), the [center](wikipedia://en/7125) of *A*<sup>×</sup>, then the [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of matrix $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c & 0 \\ 0 & c \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ on P<sup>1</sup>(*A*) is the same as the action of the identity matrix. Such matrices represent a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) *N* of *V*. The homographies of P<sup>1</sup>(*A*) correspond to elements of the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) *V*/*N*.

P<sup>1</sup>(*A*) is considered an extension of the ring *A* since it contains a copy of *A* due to the embedding *E* : *a* → *U*\[*a*, 1\]. The [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) mapping *u* → 1/*u*, ordinarily restricted to *A*<sup>×</sup>, is expressed by a homography on P<sup>1</sup>(*A*):

$$U[a,1]\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} = U[1, a] \thicksim U[a^{-1}, 1].$$

Furthermore, for *u*,*v* ∈ *A*<sup>×</sup>, the mapping *a* → *uav* can be extended to a homography:

$$\begin{pmatrix}u & 0 \\0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} u & 0 \\ 0 & v \end{pmatrix}.$$

$$U[a,1]\begin{pmatrix}v&0\\0&u\end{pmatrix} = U[av,u] \thicksim U[u^{-1}av,1].$$

Since *u* is arbitrary, it may be substituted for *u*<sup>−1</sup>. Homographies on P<sup>1</sup>(*A*) are called **linear-fractional transformations** since

$$U[z,1] \begin{pmatrix}a&c\\b&d\end{pmatrix} = U[za+b,zc+d] \thicksim U[(zc+d)^{-1}(za+b),1].$$

## Instances {#Instances}

*Image: Projectivisation\_F5P%5E1.svg.* Six colors illustrate the projective line over Galois field GF(5)

*Image: Lines through origin finite field 25.jpg.* Six lines through the origin in F\_25, each corresponding to a point in the projective line P(F\_5).

Rings that are [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) are most familiar: The projective line over [GF(2)](wikipedia://en/11137263) has three elements: *U*\[0, 1\], *U*\[1, 0\], and *U*\[1, 1\]. Its homography group is the [permutation group](wikipedia://en/24634) on these three.[^o3]

The ring [**Z**/3**Z**](wikipedia://en/20087#Integers_modulo_m), or GF(3), has the elements 1, 0, and −1; its projective line has the four elements *U*\[1, 0\], *U*\[1, 1\], *U*\[0, 1\], *U*\[1, −1\] since both 1 and −1 are [units](wikipedia://en/289666). The homography group on this projective line has 12 elements, also described with matrices or as permutations.[^o3] For a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) GF(*q*), the projective line is the [Galois geometry](wikipedia://en/25197704) PG(1, *q*). [J. W. P. Hirschfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._W._P._Hirschfeld) has described the [harmonic tetrads](wikipedia://en/16094600#Galois_tetrads) in the projective lines for *q* = 4, 5, 7, 8, 9.[^o4]

### Over discrete rings {#Over_discrete_rings}

Consider P<sup>1</sup>(**Z**/*n***Z**) when *n* is a [composite number](wikipedia://en/82289). If *p* and *q* are distinct primes dividing *n*, then *p* and *q* are [maximal ideals](wikipedia://en/48164) in **Z**/*n***Z** and by [Bézout's identity](wikipedia://en/4947) there are *a* and *b* in **Z** such that *ap* + *bq* = *1*, so that *U*\[*p*, *q*\] is in P<sup>1</sup>(**Z**/*n***Z**) but it is not an image of an element under the canonical embedding. The whole of P<sup>1</sup>(**Z**/*n***Z**) is filled out by elements *U*\[*up*, *vq*\], where *u* ≠ *v* and *u*, *v* ∈ *A*<sup>×</sup>, *A*<sup>×</sup> being the units of **Z**/*n***Z**. The instances **Z**/*n***Z** are given here for *n* = 6, 10, and 12, where according to [modular arithmetic](wikipedia://en/20087) the group of units of the ring is (**Z**/6**Z**)<sup>×</sup> = $\{1, 5\}$, (**Z**/10**Z**)<sup>×</sup> = $\{1, 3, 7, 9\}$, and (**Z**/12**Z**)<sup>×</sup> = $\{1, 5, 7, 11\}$ respectively. Modular arithmetic will confirm that, in each table, a given letter represents multiple points. In these tables a point *U*\[*m*, *n*\] is labeled by *m* in the row at the table bottom and *n* in the column at the left of the table. For instance, the [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139) A = *U*\[*v*, 0\], where *v* is a unit of the ring.

The extra points can be associated with **Q** ⊂ **R** ⊂ **C**, the rationals in the [extended complex upper-half plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_complex_upper-half_plane). The group of homographies on P<sup>1</sup>(**Z**/*n***Z**) is called a [principal congruence subgroup](wikipedia://en/382694).[^o6]

For the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) **Q**, homogeneity of coordinates means that every element of P<sup>1</sup>(**Q**) may be represented by an element of P<sup>1</sup>(**Z**). Similarly, a homography of P<sup>1</sup>(**Q**) corresponds to an element of the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019), the automorphisms of P<sup>1</sup>(**Z**).

### Over continuous rings {#Over_continuous_rings}

The projective line over a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) results in a single auxiliary point ∞ = *U*\[1, 0\]. Examples include the [real projective line](wikipedia://en/46379102), the [complex projective line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_projective_line), and the projective line over [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440). These examples of [topological rings](wikipedia://en/60097) have the projective line as their [one-point compactifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-point_compactification). The case of the [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) field **C** has the [Möbius group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Möbius_group) as its homography group.

The projective line over the [dual numbers](wikipedia://en/42169) was described by Josef Grünwald in 1906.[^o8] This ring includes a nonzero [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) *n* satisfying *nn* = 0. The plane $\{z = x + yn\mid x, y \in \mathbf{R}\}$ of dual numbers has a projective line including a line of points *U*\[1, *xn*\], *x* ∈ **R**.[^o9] [Isaak Yaglom](wikipedia://en/11606627) has described it as an "inversive Galilean plane" that has the [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) of a [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29) when the supplementary line is included.[^o10] Similarly, if *A* is a [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391), then P<sup>1</sup>(*A*) is formed by adjoining points corresponding to the elements of the [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164) of *A*.

The projective line over the ring *M* of [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559) introduces auxiliary lines $\{U[1, x(1 + j)]\mid x \in \mathbf{R}\}$ and $\{U[1, x(1 - j)]\mid x \in \mathbf{R}\}$ Using [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431) the plane of split-complex numbers is [closed up](wikipedia://en/2580834#Compactification) with these lines to a [hyperboloid](wikipedia://en/145844) of one sheet.[^o10][^o11] The projective line over *M* may be called the [Minkowski plane](wikipedia://en/4500167) when characterized by behaviour of hyperbolas under homographic mapping.

## Modules {#Modules}

The projective line P<sup>1</sup>(*A*) over a ring *A* can also be identified as the space of [projective modules](wikipedia://en/364488) in the [module](wikipedia://en/276410) *A* ⊕ *A*. An element of P<sup>1</sup>(*A*) is then a [direct summand](wikipedia://en/58899) of *A* ⊕ *A*. This more abstract approach follows the view of [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) as the geometry of [subspaces](wikipedia://en/56357) of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370), sometimes associated with the [lattice theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_theory) of [Garrett Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/1180916)[^o13] or the book *Linear Algebra and Projective Geometry* by [Reinhold Baer](wikipedia://en/1612114). In the case of the ring of rational [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) **Z**, the module summand definition of P<sup>1</sup>(**Z**) narrows attention to the *U*\[*m*, *n*\], *m* [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) to *n*, and sheds the embeddings that are a principal feature of P<sup>1</sup>(*A*) when *A* is topological. The 1981 article by W. Benz, Hans-Joachim Samaga, & Helmut Scheaffer mentions the direct summand definition.

In an article "Projective representations: projective lines over rings"[^o14] the [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) of a [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) M<sub>2</sub>(*R*) and the concepts of module and [bimodule](wikipedia://en/492612) are used to define a projective line over a ring. The group of units is denoted by GL(2, *R*), adopting notation from the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564), where *R* is usually taken to be a field.

The projective line is the set of orbits under GL(2, *R*) of the free cyclic [submodule](wikipedia://en/276410#Submodules_and_homomorphisms) *R*(1, 0) of *R* × *R*. Extending the commutative theory of Benz, the existence of a right or left [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) of a ring element is related to P<sup>1</sup>(*R*) and GL(2, *R*). The [Dedekind-finite](wikipedia://en/1074742#Generalizations) property is characterized. Most significantly, [representation](wikipedia://en/19378200) of P<sup>1</sup>(*R*) in a projective space over a division ring *K* is accomplished with a (*K*, *R*)-bimodule *U* that is a left *K*-vector space and a right *R*-module. The points of P<sup>1</sup>(*R*) are subspaces of P<sup>1</sup>(*K*, *U* × *U*) isomorphic to their complements.

## Cross-ratio {#Cross-ratio}

A homography *h* that takes three particular ring elements *a*, *b*, *c* to the projective line points *U*\[0, 1\], *U*\[1, 1\], *U*\[1, 0\] is called the **cross-ratio homography**. Sometimes[^o16][^o17] the [cross-ratio](wikipedia://en/401767) is taken as the value of *h* on a fourth point *x* : (*x*, *a*, *b*, *c*) = *h*(*x*).

To build *h* from *a*, *b*, *c* the generator homographies

$$\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\t & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}u & 0\\0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

are used, with attention to [fixed points](wikipedia://en/449738): +1 and −1 are fixed under inversion, *U*\[1, 0\] is fixed under translation, and the "rotation" with *u* leaves *U*\[0, 1\] and *U*\[1, 0\] fixed. The instructions are to place *c* first, then bring *a* to *U*\[0, 1\] with translation, and finally to use rotation to move *b* to *U*\[1, 1\].

Lemma: If *A* is a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) and *b* − *a*, *c* − *b*, *c* − *a* are all units, then (*b* − *c*)<sup>−1</sup> + (*c* − *a*)<sup>−1</sup> is a unit.

Proof: Evidently $\frac{b-a}{(b-c)(c-a)} = \frac{(b-c)+(c-a)}{(b-c)(c-a)}$ is a unit, as required.

Theorem: If (*b* − *c*)<sup>−1</sup> + (*c* − *a*)<sup>−1</sup> is a unit, then there is a homography *h* in G(*A*) such that

*h*(*a*) = *U*\[0, 1\], *h*(*b*) = *U*\[1, 1\], and *h*(*c*) = *U*\[1, 0\].

Proof: The point *p* = (*b* − *c*)<sup>−1</sup> + (*c* − *a*)<sup>−1</sup> is the image of *b* after *a* was put to 0 and then inverted to *U*\[1, 0\], and the image of *c* is brought to *U*\[0, 1\]. As *p* is a unit, its inverse used in a rotation will move *p* to *U*\[1, 1\], resulting in *a*, *b*, *c* being all properly placed. The lemma refers to sufficient conditions for the existence of *h*.

One application of cross ratio defines the [projective harmonic conjugate](wikipedia://en/16094600) of a triple *a*, *b*, *c*, as the element *x* satisfying (*x*, *a*, *b*, *c*) = −1. Such a quadruple is a [harmonic tetrad](wikipedia://en/16094600#Galois_tetrads). Harmonic tetrads on the projective line over a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) GF(*q*) were used in 1954 to delimit the projective linear groups PGL(2, *q*) for *q* = 5, 7, and 9, and demonstrate [accidental isomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accidental_isomorphism).[^o18]

## Chains {#Chains}

The [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line#In_real_algebras) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) gets permuted with circles and other real lines under [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493), which actually permute the canonical embedding of the [real projective line](wikipedia://en/46379102) in the [complex projective line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_projective_line). Suppose *A* is an [algebra over a field](wikipedia://en/191788) *F*, generalizing the case where *F* is the real number field and *A* is the field of complex numbers. The canonical embedding of P<sup>1</sup>(*F*) into P<sup>1</sup>(*A*) is

$$U_F[x, 1] \mapsto U_A[x, 1] , \quad U_F[1, 0] \mapsto U_A[1, 0].$$

A **chain** is the image of P<sup>1</sup>(*F*) under a homography on P<sup>1</sup>(*A*). Four points lie on a chain [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) their cross-ratio is in *F*. [Karl von Staudt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_von_Staudt) exploited this property in his theory of "real strokes" \[reeler Zug\].[^o20]

### Point-parallelism {#Point-parallelism}

Two points of P<sup>1</sup>(*A*) are **parallel** if there is *no* chain connecting them. The convention has been adopted that points are parallel to themselves. This relation is [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under the action of a homography on the projective line. Given three pair-wise non-parallel points, there is a unique chain that connects the three.[^o22]

## History {#History}

[August Ferdinand Möbius](wikipedia://en/37875) investigated the [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493) between his book *Barycentric Calculus* (1827) and his 1855 paper "Theorie der Kreisverwandtschaft in rein geometrischer Darstellung". [Karl Wilhelm Feuerbach](wikipedia://en/161246) and [Julius Plücker](wikipedia://en/52146) are also credited with originating the use of homogeneous coordinates. [Eduard Study](wikipedia://en/663997) in 1898, and [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) in 1908, wrote articles on [hypercomplex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercomplex_numbers) for German and French *Encyclopedias of Mathematics*, respectively, where they use these arithmetics with [linear fractional transformations](wikipedia://en/401769) in imitation of those of Möbius. In 1902 [Theodore Vahlen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Vahlen) contributed a short but well-referenced paper exploring some linear fractional transformations of a [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305).[^o24] The ring of [dual numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_numbers) *D* gave Josef Grünwald opportunity to exhibit P<sup>1</sup>(*D*) in 1906.[^o8] [Corrado Segre](wikipedia://en/2422061) (1912) continued the development with that ring.[^o9]

[Arthur Conway](wikipedia://en/6377687), one of the early adopters of relativity via [biquaternion](wikipedia://en/1207070) transformations, considered the quaternion-multiplicative-inverse transformation in his 1911 relativity study.[^o25] In 1947 some elements of inversive quaternion geometry were described by P.G. Gormley in Ireland.[^o26] In 1968 [Isaak Yaglom](wikipedia://en/11606627)'s *Complex Numbers in Geometry* appeared in English, translated from Russian. There he uses P<sup>1</sup>(*D*) to describe [line geometry](wikipedia://en/21252759#With_complex_numbers) in the Euclidean plane and P<sup>1</sup>(*M*) to describe it for Lobachevski's plane. Yaglom's text *A Simple Non-Euclidean Geometry* appeared in English in 1979. There in pages 174 to 200 he develops *Minkowskian geometry* and describes P<sup>1</sup>(*M*) as the "inversive Minkowski plane". The Russian original of Yaglom's text was published in 1969. Between the two editions, [Walter Benz](wikipedia://en/18994043) (1973) published his book,[^o11] which included the homogeneous coordinates taken from *M*.
