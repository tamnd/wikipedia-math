# Treynor ratio

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Treynor ratio"}

In [finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance), the **Treynor reward-to-volatility model** (sometimes called the **reward-to-volatility ratio** or **Treynor measure**[^o2]), named after American economist [Jack L. Treynor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_L._Treynor),[^o3] is a measurement of the [returns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_of_return) earned in excess of that which could have been earned on an [investment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Investment) that has no [risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_risk) that can be [diversified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diversification_%28finance%29) (e.g., [Treasury bills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Treasury_security#Treasury_bill) or a completely diversified [portfolio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portfolio_%28finance%29)), per unit of market risk assumed.

:::

The Treynor ratio relates [excess return](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excess_return) over the risk-free rate to the additional risk taken; however, systematic risk is used instead of total risk. The higher the Treynor ratio, the better the performance of the portfolio under analysis.

::: {#o5 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formula"}

## Formula {#Formula}

$$T = \frac{r_i - r_f}{\beta_i},$$

where $T$ is the Treynor ratio, $r_i$ is the return of portfolio $i$, $r_f$ is the [risk free rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk_free_rate), and $\beta_i$ is the [beta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_coefficient) of portfolio $i$.

:::

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Taking the equation detailed above, let us assume that the expected portfolio return is 20%, the risk free rate is 5%, and the [beta](wikipedia://en/897558) of the portfolio is 1.5. Substituting these values, we get the following:

$$T = \frac{0.2 - 0.05}{1.5} = 0.1.$$

:::

## Limitations {#Limitations}

Like the [Sharpe ratio](wikipedia://en/934837), the Treynor ratio (*T*) does not quantify the value added, if any, of [active portfolio management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_management). It is a ranking criterion only. A ranking of portfolios based on the Treynor Ratio is only useful if the portfolios under consideration are sub-portfolios of a broader, fully diversified portfolio. If this is not the case, portfolios with identical [systematic risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematic_risk), but different total risk, will be rated the same. But the portfolio with a higher total risk is less diversified and therefore has a higher unsystematic risk which is not priced in the market.

An alternative method of ranking portfolio management is [Jensen's alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jensen's_alpha), which quantifies the added return as the excess return above the [security market line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_market_line) in the [capital asset pricing model](wikipedia://en/163062). As these two methods both determine rankings based on systematic risk alone, they will rank portfolios identically.
