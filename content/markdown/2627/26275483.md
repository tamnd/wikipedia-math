# Braikenridge–Maclaurin theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Braikenridge–Maclaurin theorem"}

*Image: Braikenridge–Maclaurin theorem.svg.* Elliptic case

*Image: Braikenridge–Maclaurin theorem 2.svg.* Hyperbolic case

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **Braikenridge–Maclaurin theorem**, named for 18th-century British mathematicians [William Braikenridge](wikipedia://en/39712514) and [Colin Maclaurin](wikipedia://en/7824), is the converse to [Pascal's theorem](wikipedia://en/699966). It states that if the three intersection points of the three pairs of lines through opposite sides of a hexagon lie on a line *L*, then the six vertices of the hexagon lie on a conic *C*; the conic may be degenerate, as in [Pappus's hexagon theorem](wikipedia://en/1493395).

:::

The Braikenridge–Maclaurin theorem may be applied in the [Braikenridge–Maclaurin construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braikenridge–Maclaurin_construction), which is a [synthetic](wikipedia://en/267484) construction of the conic defined by five points, by varying the sixth point. Namely, Pascal's theorem states that given six points on a conic (the vertices of a hexagon), the lines defined by opposite sides intersect in three collinear points. This can be reversed to construct the possible locations for a sixth point, given five existing ones.
