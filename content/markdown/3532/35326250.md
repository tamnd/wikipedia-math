# Glossary of classical algebraic geometry

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Glossary of classical algebraic geometry"}

The terminology of algebraic geometry changed drastically during the twentieth century, with the introduction of the general methods, initiated by [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) and the [Italian school of algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/383424) in the beginning of the century, and later formalized by [André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019), [Jean-Pierre Serre](wikipedia://en/205483) and [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042). Much of the classical terminology, mainly based on case study, was simply abandoned, with the result that books and papers written before this time can be hard to read. This article lists some of this classical terminology, and describes some of the changes in conventions.

:::

(txt) translates many of the classical terms in algebraic geometry into scheme-theoretic terminology. Other books defining some of the classical terminology include (txt), (Coolidge 1931), (Coxeter 1969), (Hudson 1990), (Salmon 1879), (Semple Roth 1949).

::: {#o10 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Conventions"}

## Conventions {#Conventions}

The change in terminology from around 1948 to 1960 is not the only difficulty in understanding classical algebraic geometry. There was also a lot of background knowledge and assumptions, much of which has now changed. This section lists some of these changes.

- In classical algebraic geometry, adjectives were often used as nouns: for example, "quartic" could also be short for "quartic curve" or "[quartic surface](wikipedia://en/20133775)".
- In classical algebraic geometry, all curves, surfaces, varieties, and so on came with fixed embeddings into projective space, whereas in scheme theory they are more often considered as abstract varieties. For example, a [Veronese surface](wikipedia://en/1569217) was not just a copy of the projective plane, but a copy of the projective plane together with an embedding into projective 5-space.
- Varieties were often considered only up to birational isomorphism, whereas in scheme theory they are usually considered up to biregular isomorphism. (Semple Roth 1949, p.20–21)
- Until circa 1950, many of the proofs in classical algebraic geometry were incomplete (or occasionally just wrong). In particular authors often did not bother to check degenerate cases.
- Words (such as azygetic or bifid) were sometimes formed from Latin or Greek roots without further explanation, assuming that readers would use their [classical education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classics) to figure out the meaning.
- Definitions in classical algebraic geometry were often somewhat vague, and it is futile to try to find the precise meaning of some of the older terms because many of them never had a precise meaning. In practice this did not matter much when the terms were only used to describe particular examples, as in these cases their meaning was usually clear: for example, it was obvious what the 16 tropes of a [Kummer surface](wikipedia://en/647758) were, even if "trope" was not precisely defined in general.
- Algebraic geometry was often implicitly done over the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) (or sometimes the real numbers).
- Readers were often assumed to know classical (or synthetic) [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849), and in particular to have a thorough knowledge of conics, and authors would use terminology from this area without further explanation.
- Several terms, such as "Abelian group", "complete", "complex", "flat", "harmonic", "homology", "[monoid](wikipedia://en/19652)", "normal", "pole", "regular", now have meanings that are unrelated to their original meanings. Other terms, such as "circle", have their meanings tacitly changed to work in complex projective space; for example, a circle in complex algebraic geometry is a conic passing through the [circular points at infinity](wikipedia://en/8088700) and has underlying [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) a 2-sphere rather than a 1-sphere.
- Sometimes capital letters are tacitly understood to stand for points, and small letters for lines or curves.

## A {#A}

::: {#o21 .entry cue=term name="Abelian group"}

**Abelian group**

An archaic name for the [symplectic group](wikipedia://en/173977).

A [commutative group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_group).

:::

::: {#o22 .entry cue=term name="aberrancy"}

**aberrancy**

The deviation of a curve from circular form. See (Salmon 1879, p. 356).

:::

::: {#o24 .entry cue=term name="absolute"}

**absolute**

A fixed choice of something in projective space, used to construct some other geometry from projective geometry. For example, choosing a plane, called the **absolute plane**, of projective space can be used to make its complement into a copy of affine space. Choosing a suitable conic or polarity, called the [Cayley absolute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cayley_absolute), **absolute conic** or **absolute polarity**, in the absolute plane provides the means to put a metric on affine space so that it becomes a metric space.

[Absolute geometry](wikipedia://en/699294) is roughly Euclidean geometry without the parallel postulate.

:::

::: {#o25 .entry cue=term name="accidental"}

**accidental**

An accidental (or improper) double point of a surface in 4-dimensional projective space is a double point with two distinct tangent planes. (Baker 1933b, vol 6, p. 157)

:::

::: {#o27 .entry cue=term name="acnode"}

**acnode**

An [acnode](wikipedia://en/3090256) is an isolated point of a real curve. See (Salmon 1879, p.23).

:::

::: {#o29 .entry cue=term name="adjoint"}

**adjoint**

If *C* is a curve, an adjoint of *C* is a curve such that any point of *C* of multiplicity *r* has multiplicity at least *r*–1 on the adjoint. Sometimes the multiple points of *C* are required to be ordinary, and if theis condition is not satisfied the term "sub-adjoint" is used. (Semple Roth 1949, p.55, 231)

:::

::: {#o31 .entry cue=term name="affine"}

**affine**

[Affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) is roughly a vector space where one has forgotten which point is the origin.

An [affine variety](wikipedia://en/320468) is a variety in affine space.

:::

::: {#o32 .entry cue=term name="affinity"}

**affinity**

An automorphism of affine space.

:::

::: {#o33 .entry cue=term name="aggregate"}

**aggregate**

A set.

:::

::: {#o34 .entry cue=term name="ambient"}

**ambient**

An [ambient variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambient_variety) is a large variety containing all the points, curves, divisors, and so on that one is interested in.

:::

::: {#o35 .entry cue=term name="anharmonic ratio"}

**anharmonic ratio**

[Cross-ratio](wikipedia://en/401767)

:::

::: {#o36 .entry cue=term name="antipoint"}

**antipoint**

One of a pair of points constructed from two foci of a curve. See (Salmon 1879, p.119).

:::

::: {#o38 .entry cue=term name="apparent"}

**apparent**

An apparent singularity is a singularity of a projection of a variety into a hyperplane. They are so called because they appear to be singularities to an observer at the point being projected from. (Semple Roth 1949, p.55, 231)

:::

::: {#o40 .entry cue=term name="apolar"}

**apolar**

Orthogonal under the polar pairing between the symmetric algebra of a vector space and its dual.

:::

::: {#o41 .entry cue=term name="arithmetic genus"}

**arithmetic genus**

The [arithmetic genus](wikipedia://en/1950390) of a variety is a variation of the Euler characteristic of the trivial line bundle; see [Hodge number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_number).

:::

::: {#o42 .entry cue=term name="Aronhold set"}

**Aronhold set**

One of the 288 sets of 7 of the 28 bitangents of a quartic curve corresponding to the 7 odd theta characteristics of a normal set.

:::

::: {#o43 .entry cue=term name="associated"}

**associated**

An associated curve is the image of a projective curve in a Grassmannian, given by taking the tangent lines, or osculating planes, and so on.

:::

::: {#o44 .entry cue=term name="axial"}

**axial**

:::

::: {#o45 .entry cue=term name="axis"}

**axis**

A special line or linear subspace associated with some family of geometric objects. For example, a special linear complex in 4-dimensional space consists of all lines meeting a given plane, that is called the axial plane of the complex. (Semple Roth 1949, p.274) Similar to directrix.

:::

::: {#o47 .entry cue=term name="azygetic"}

**azygetic**

Unpaired. Opposite of syzygetic, meaning paired. Example: azygetic triad, azygetic tetrad, azygetic set.

:::

## B {#B}

::: {#o49 .entry cue=term name="base"}

**base**

A **base point** is a point common to all members of a family.

The **base number** ρ is the rank of the [Neron–Severi group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neron–Severi_group).

:::

::: {#o50 .entry cue=term name="bicircular"}

**bicircular**

Having nodes at the two circular points at infinity, as in [bicircular curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicircular_curve). See (Salmon 1879, p.231).

:::

::: {#o52 .entry cue=term name="bicorn"}

**bicorn**

A [bicorn](wikipedia://en/2737646) is a curve with two cusps.

:::

::: {#o53 .entry cue=term name="bicuspidal"}

**bicuspidal**

Having two cusps

:::

::: {#o54 .entry cue=term name="bidegree"}

**bidegree**

A pair of integers giving the degrees of a bihomogeneous polynomial in two sets of variables

:::

::: {#o55 .entry cue=term name="bielliptic"}

**bielliptic**

A bielliptic curve is a branched double cover of an elliptic curve.

A bielliptic surface is the same as a [hyperelliptic surface](wikipedia://en/8358276).

:::

::: {#o56 .entry cue=term name="bifid"}

**bifid**

Split into two equal parts

A **bifid map** is an element of the vector space of dimension 2*g* over the field with 2 elements, consisting of the 2*g*+1-dimensional space of even-cardinality subsets of a set *S* of 2+2*g* elements, modulo the 1-dimensional space {0,*S*}. (Dolgachev 2012, p.215)

A **bifid substitution** is a permutation of the 28 bitangents of a quartic curve depending on one of the 35 decompositions of 8 symbols into two sets of 4 symbols. See (Salmon 1879, p.223).

:::

::: {#o59 .entry cue=term name="biflecnode"}

**biflecnode**

Same as fleflecnode. See (Salmon 1879, p.210).

:::

::: {#o61 .entry cue=term name="bigenus"}

**bigenus**

The second [plurigenus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plurigenus) *P*<sub>2</sub> of a surface.

:::

::: {#o62 .entry cue=term name="bihomogeneous"}

**bihomogeneous**

Homogeneous in each of two sets of variables, as in bihomogeneous form.

:::

::: {#o63 .entry cue=term name="binary"}

**binary**

Depending on two variables, as in [binary form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_form)

:::

::: {#o64 .entry cue=term name="binodal"}

**binodal**

Having two nodes

:::

::: {#o65 .entry cue=term name="binode"}

**binode**

A double point of a surface whose tangent cone consists of two different planes. See unode. (Semple Roth 1949, p.424)

:::

::: {#o67 .entry cue=term name="bipartite"}

**bipartite**

Having two connected components. See (Salmon 1879, p.165).

:::

::: {#o69 .entry cue=term name="bipunctual"}

**bipunctual**

Having two points

For a bipunctual conic with respect to 3 points see (Baker 1922b, vol 2, p. 123).

:::

::: {#o71 .entry cue=term name="birational"}

**birational**

Two varieties are birational if they are isomorphic off lower-dimensional subsets

A [birational map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birational_map) is a rational map with rational "inverse"

:::

::: {#o72 .entry cue=term name="biregular"}

**biregular**

A [biregular map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biregular_map) is a regular map with regular inverse

Two varieties are biregular if there is a biregular map from one to the other, in other words if they are isomorphic as abstract varieties.

:::

::: {#o73 .entry cue=term name="biscribed"}

**biscribed**

Both circumscribed and inscribed, or in other words having vertices that lie on a curve and sides that are tangent to the curve, as in biscribed triangle. (Dolgachev 2012)

:::

::: {#o75 .entry cue=term name="bitangent"}

**bitangent**

A [bitangent](wikipedia://en/2605212) is a line that is tangent to a curve at two points. See (Salmon 1879, p. 328).

:::

::: {#o77 .entry cue=term name="bitangential"}

**bitangential**

Meeting a curve at the tangency points of its bitangents

:::

::: {#o78 .entry cue=term name="Brianchon hexagon"}

**Brianchon hexagon**

A non-planar hexagon whose three diagonals meet. (Baker 1922a, vol 1, p. 47)

:::

## C {#C}

::: {#o81 .entry cue=term name="canonical"}

**canonical**

The canonical series is the linear series of the canonical line bundle

The [canonical bundle](wikipedia://en/734893) is the line bundle of differential forms of highest degree.

The **canonical map** or **canonical embedding** is the map to the projective space of the sections of the canonical bundle

A **canonical curve** (or variety) is the image of a curve (or variety) under the canonical map

The **canonical class** is the divisor class of a canonical divisor

A **canonical divisor** is a divisor of a section of the canonical line bundle.

:::

::: {#o82 .entry cue=term name="Canonizant"}

**Canonizant**

A [canonizant](wikipedia://en/35808199) is a covariant of forms.

:::

::: {#o83 .entry cue=term name="catalecticant"}

**catalecticant**

A [catalecticant](wikipedia://en/30084691) is an invariant of a binary form of degree 2*n* that vanishes when the form is a sum of powers of *n* linear forms.

:::

::: {#o84 .entry cue=term name="caustic"}

**caustic**

A [caustic](wikipedia://en/880406) is the envelope of light rays from a point reflected in a curve

:::

::: {#o85 .entry cue=term name="Cayley"}

**Cayley**

:::

::: {#o86 .entry cue=term name="Cayleyan"}

**Cayleyan**

Named after [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034)

See (Salmon 1879)

A **Cayley octad** is a set of 8 points in projective space given by the intersection of three quadrics. (Dolgachev 2012, 6.3.1)

The Cayley lines or Cayley–Salmon lines are the 20 lines passing through 3 Kirkman points.

A [Cayley absolute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cayley_absolute) is a conic or quadric used to define a metric.

:::

::: {#o89 .entry cue=term name="center"}

**center**

:::

::: {#o90 .entry cue=term name="centre"}

**centre**

A special point associated with some geometric object

The center of a perspectivity

The center of an isologue

:::

::: {#o91 .entry cue=term name="character"}

**character**

:::

::: {#o92 .entry cue=term name="characteristic"}

**characteristic**

An integer associated with a projective variety, such as its degree, rank, order, class, type. (Semple Roth 1949, p.189) In particular the **Plücker characteristics** of a curve are the order, class, number of nodes, number of bitangents, number of cusps, and number of inflections. (Coolidge 1931, p.99)

A characteristic exponent is an exponent of a power series with non-negative coefficient, that is not divisible by the highest common factor of preceding exponents with non-zero coefficients. (Coolidge 1931, p.220)

The characteristic series of a linear system of divisors on a surface is the linear system of 0-cycles on one of the divisors given by its intersections with the other divisors.

:::

::: {#o96 .entry cue=term name="chord"}

**chord**

A line joining two points of a variety

:::

::: {#o97 .entry cue=term name="chordal variety"}

**chordal variety**

A [chordal variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chordal_variety) is the union of the chords and tangent spaces of a projective variety

:::

::: {#o98 .entry cue=term name="circle"}

**circle**

A plane conic passing through the circular points at infinity. For real projective geometry this is much the same as a circle in the usual sense, but for complex projective geometry it is different: for example, circles have underlying topological spaces given by a 2-sphere rather than a 1-sphere.

:::

::: {#o99 .entry cue=term name="circuit"}

**circuit**

A component of a real algebraic curve. A circuit is called **even** or **odd** depending on whether it has an even or odd number of intersections with a generic line. (Coolidge 1931, p. 50)

:::

::: {#o101 .entry cue=term name="circular"}

**circular**

A circular point is one of the two points at infinity (1: *i*: 0), (1: −*i*: 0) through which all circles pass

A [circular algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/24215692) is a curve passing through the two circular points at infinity. See also bicircular.

:::

::: {#o102 .entry cue=term name="circumscribed"}

**circumscribed**

Having edges tangent to some curve, as in [circumscribed quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/9176412).

Passing through the vertices of something, as in [circumscribed circle](wikipedia://en/161262).

:::

::: {#o103 .entry cue=term name="cissoid"}

**cissoid**

A [cissoid](wikipedia://en/899085) is the curve generated from two curves and a point. See (Salmon 1879).

:::

::: {#o105 .entry cue=term name="class"}

**class**

The class of a plane curve is the number of proper tangents passing through a generic point of the plane. (Semple Roth 1949, p.28)

The class of a space curve is the number of osculating planes passing through a generic point of space. (Semple Roth 1949, p.85)

The class of a surface in *r*dimensional projective space is the number of tangent planes meeting a generic codimension 2 subspace in a line. (Semple Roth 1949, p.28)

The degree of a contravariant or concomitant in the covariant variables.

:::

::: {#o109 .entry cue=term name="coaxal"}

**coaxal**

:::

::: {#o110 .entry cue=term name="coaxial"}

**coaxial**

A pencil of circles is called coaxal if their centers all lie on a line (called the axis).

A family of plane circles all passing through the same two points (other than the circular points at infinity). (Baker 1922b, vol 2, p. 66)

:::

::: {#o112 .entry cue=term name="coincidence"}

**coincidence**

A coincidence quadric is a quadric associated to a correlation, given by the locus of points lying in the corresponding hyperplane. (Semple Roth 1949, p.8)

A fixed point of a correspondence, in other words a point of a variety corresponding to itself under a correspondence. (Coolidge 1931, p. 126)

:::

::: {#o115 .entry cue=term name="collinear"}

**collinear**

On the same line

:::

::: {#o116 .entry cue=term name="collineation"}

**collineation**

A [collineation](wikipedia://en/4072976) is an isomorphism from one projective space to another, often to itself. (Semple Roth 1949, p.6) See correlation.

:::

::: {#o118 .entry cue=term name="complete"}

**complete**

A linear series of divisors is called complete if it is not contained in a larger linear series.(Semple Roth 1949, p.351)

A scheme is called [complete](wikipedia://en/1047627) if the map to a point is proper

A [complete quadrangle](wikipedia://en/2605272) is 4 points and the 6 lines joining pairs

A [complete quadrilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_quadrilateral) is 4 lines meeting in pairs in 6 points

A [complete conic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_conic) in the plane is a (possibly degenerate) conic, together with a pair of (possibly equal) points on it if it is a double line

:::

::: {#o120 .entry cue=term name="complex"}

**complex**

(Noun.) A [line complex](wikipedia://en/23445252), a family of lines of codimension 1 in the family of all lines in some projective space, in particular a 3-dimensional family of lines in 3-dimensional projective space. (Semple Roth 1949, p.236) See congruence.

(Adjective.) Related to the complex numbers.

The (line) complex group is an old name for the [symplectic group](wikipedia://en/173977).

:::

::: {#o122 .entry cue=term name="composite"}

**composite**

Reducible (meaning having more than one irreducible component).

:::

::: {#o123 .entry cue=term name="conchoid"}

**conchoid**

A [conchoid](wikipedia://en/898856) is the curve given by the [cissoid](wikipedia://en/899085) of a circle and another curve. See (Salmon 1879).

:::

::: {#o125 .entry cue=term name="concomitant"}

**concomitant**

A (mixed) concomitant is an invariant homogeneous polynomial in the coefficients of a form, a covariant variable, and a contravariant variable. In other words it is a (tri)homogeneous polynomial on *SV*⊕*V*⊕*V*\* for some vector space *V*, where *SV* is some symmetric power of *V* and *V*\* its dual, that is invariant under the special linear group of *V*. In practice *V* often has dimension 2. The degree, class, and order of a concomitant are its degrees in the three types of variable. Concomitants are generalizations of covariants, contravariants, and invariants.

:::

::: {#o126 .entry cue=term name="concurrent"}

**concurrent**

Meeting at a point

:::

::: {#o127 .entry cue=term name="cone"}

**cone**

The union of the lines joining an algebraic set with a linear algebraic set. Called a point-cone, line-cone, ... if the linear set is a point, line, ...(Semple Roth 1949, p.18)

A subset of a vector space closed under multiplication by scalars.

:::

::: {#o129 .entry cue=term name="configuration"}

**configuration**

A [configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_configuration) is a finite set of points and lines (and sometimes planes), generally with equal numbers of points per line and equal numbers of lines per point.

:::

::: {#o130 .entry cue=term name="confocal"}

**confocal**

Having the same foci

:::

::: {#o131 .entry cue=term name="congruence"}

**congruence**

A family of lines in projective space such that there are a nonzero finite number of lines through a generic point (Semple Roth 1949, p.238, 288). See complex.

:::

::: {#o133 .entry cue=term name="conic"}

**conic**

A [conic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic) is a degree 2 curve. Short for "conic section", the intersection of a cone with a plane.

:::

::: {#o134 .entry cue=term name="conjugate"}

**conjugate**

A conjugate point is an [acnode](wikipedia://en/3090256). (Salmon 1879, p.23)

A conjugate point is a point lying on the hyperplane corresponding to another point under a polarity.

A conjugate line is a line containing the point corresponding to another line under a polarity (or plane conic). (Baker 1922b, vol 2, p. 26)

For **harmonic conjugate** see harmonic.

:::

::: {#o137 .entry cue=term name="connex"}

**connex**

A correspondence between a projective space and its dual.

:::

::: {#o138 .entry cue=term name="consecutive"}

**consecutive**

Infinitesimally near. For example, a tangent line to a curve is a line through two consecutive points of the curve, and a focal point is the intersection of the normals of two consecutive points.

:::

::: {#o139 .entry cue=term name="contravariant"}

**contravariant**

A bihomogeneous polynomial in dual variables of *x*, *y*, ... and the coefficients of some homogeneous form in *x*, *y*,... that is invariant under some group of linear transformations. In other words it is a bihomogeneous polynomial on *SV*⊕*V* for some vector space *V*, where *SV* is some symmetric power of *V* and *V*\* its dual, that is invariant under the special linear group of *V*. In practice *V* often has dimension at least 3, because when it has dimension 2 these are more or less the same as covariants. The degree and class of a contravariant are its degrees in the two types of variable. Contravariants generalize invariants and are special cases of concomitants, and are in some sense dual to covariants.

:::

::: {#o140 .entry cue=term name="coplanar"}

**coplanar**

In the same plane

:::

::: {#o141 .entry cue=term name="correlation"}

**correlation**

An isomorphism from a projective space to the dual of a projective space, often to the dual of itself. A correlation on the projective space of a vector space is essentially the same as a nonsingular bilinear form on the vector space, up to multiplication by constants. (Semple Roth 1949, p.7)

:::

::: {#o143 .entry cue=term name="coresidual"}

**coresidual**

See (Salmon 1879, p.131)

:::

::: {#o145 .entry cue=term name="correspondence"}

**correspondence**

A correspondence from *X* to *Y* is an algebraic subset of *X*×*Y*

:::

::: {#o146 .entry cue=term name="cosingular"}

**cosingular**

Having the same singularities

:::

::: {#o147 .entry cue=term name="couple"}

**couple**

An ordered pair

:::

::: {#o148 .entry cue=term name="covariant"}

**covariant**

A bihomogeneous polynomial in *x*, *y*, ... and the coefficients of some homogeneous form in *x*, *y*,... that is invariant under some group of linear transformations. In other words it is a bihomogeneous polynomial on *SV*⊕*V*\* for some vector space *V*, where *SV* is some symmetric power of *V* and *V*\* its dual, that is invariant under the special linear group of *V*. In practice *V* often has dimension 2. The degree and order of a covariant are its degrees in the two types of variable. Covariants generalize invariants and are special cases of concomitants, and are in some sense dual to contravariants

The variety defined by a covariant. In particular the curve defined by the Hessian or Steinerian covariants of a curve are called covariant curves. (Coolidge 1931, p.151)

:::

::: {#o150 .entry cue=term name="Cremona transformation"}

**Cremona transformation**

A [Cremona transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cremona_transformation) is a birational map from a projective space to itself

:::

::: {#o151 .entry cue=term name="cross-ratio"}

**cross-ratio**

The [cross-ratio](wikipedia://en/401767) is an invariant of 4 points on a projective line.

:::

::: {#o152 .entry cue=term name="crunode"}

**crunode**

[Crunode](wikipedia://en/3090324) is an archaic term for a node, a double point with distinct tangent directions.

:::

::: {#o153 .entry cue=term name="cubic"}

**cubic**

Degree 3, especially a degree 3 projective variety

:::

::: {#o154 .entry cue=term name="cubo-cubic"}

**cubo-cubic**

::: {#o510 .entry cue=term name="syzygy"}

**syzygy**

A point is in syzygy with some other points if it is in the linear subspace generated by them. (Baker 1922a, vol 1, p. 33) A syzygy is a linear relation between points in an affine space.

An algebraic relation between generators of a ring, especially a ring of invariants or covariants.

A linear relation between generators of a module, or more generally an element of the kernel of a homomorphism of modules.

A global syzygy is a resolution of a module or sheaf.

:::

## T {#T}

::: {#o513 .entry cue=term name="tacnode"}

**tacnode**

A [tacnode](wikipedia://en/7537723) is a point of a curve where two branches meet in the same direction. (Cayley 1852)

:::

::: {#o515 .entry cue=term name="tacnode-cusp"}

**tacnode-cusp**

A singularity of a plane curve where a tacnode and a cusp are combined at the same point. (Salmon 1879, p.207)

:::

::: {#o517 .entry cue=term name="tact-invariant"}

**tact-invariant**

An invariant of two curves that vanishes if they touch each other. See (Salmon 1879, p.76).

:::

::: {#o519 .entry cue=term name="tangent cone"}

**tangent cone**

A [tangent cone](wikipedia://en/1416915) is a cone defined by the non-zero terms of smallest degree in the Taylor series at a point of a hypersurface.

:::

::: {#o520 .entry cue=term name="tangential equation"}

**tangential equation**

The tangential equation of a plane curve is an equation giving the condition for a line to be tangent to the curve. In other words it is the equation of the dual curve. It is not the equation of a tangent to a curve.

:::

::: {#o521 .entry cue=term name="ternary"}

**ternary**

Depending on three variables, as in [ternary form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ternary_form)

:::

::: {#o522 .entry cue=term name="tetrad"}

**tetrad**

A set of 4 points

:::

::: {#o523 .entry cue=term name="tetragram"}

**tetragram**

Synonym for [complete quadrilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_quadrilateral)

:::

::: {#o524 .entry cue=term name="tetrahedroid"}

**tetrahedroid**

A [tetrahedroid](wikipedia://en/4855620) is a special kind of [Kummer surface](wikipedia://en/647758).

:::

::: {#o525 .entry cue=term name="tetrahedron"}

**tetrahedron**

A geometric configuration consisting of 4 points and the 6 lines joining pairs. This is similar to the lines and infinite edges of a polyhedral [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606), but in algebraic geometry one sometimes does not include the faces of the tetrahedron.

:::

::: {#o526 .entry cue=term name="tetrastigm"}

**tetrastigm**

Synonym for [complete quadrangle](wikipedia://en/2605272)

:::

::: {#o527 .entry cue=term name="third kind"}

**third kind**

All poles are simple (order 1)

:::

::: {#o528 .entry cue=term name="threefold"}

**threefold**

(Adjective) Three-dimensional

(Noun) A 3-dimensional variety

:::

::: {#o529 .entry cue=term name="torsal generator."}

**torsal generator.**

A generator of a scroll (ruled surface) that meets its consecutive generator. See (Semple Roth 1949, p.204).

:::

::: {#o531 .entry cue=term name="torse"}

**torse**

[Developable surface](wikipedia://en/2878111).

:::

::: {#o532 .entry cue=term name="transvectant"}

**transvectant**

An invariant depending on two forms.

:::

::: {#o533 .entry cue=term name="transversal"}

**transversal**

A line meeting several other lines. For example, 4 generic lines in projective 3-space have 2 transversals meeting all of them.

:::

::: {#o534 .entry cue=term name="triad"}

**triad**

A set of 3 points

:::

::: {#o535 .entry cue=term name="tricircular"}

**tricircular**

A [tricircular curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tricircular_curve) is one that passes through the circular points at infinity with order 3.

:::

::: {#o536 .entry cue=term name="tricuspidal"}

**tricuspidal**

Having three cusps

:::

::: {#o537 .entry cue=term name="trigonal"}

**trigonal**

A trigonal curve is one with a degree three map to the projective line. See hyperelliptic.

:::

::: {#o538 .entry cue=term name="trihedral"}

**trihedral**

A set of 3 planes A Steiner trihedral is a set of three tritangent planes of a cubic surface whose intersection point is not on the surface. (Semple Roth 1949, p.152)

:::

::: {#o540 .entry cue=term name="trilinear coordinates"}

**trilinear coordinates**

Coordinates based on distance from sides of a triangle: [Trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790).

:::

::: {#o541 .entry cue=term name="trinodal"}

**trinodal**

Having three nodes

:::

::: {#o542 .entry cue=term name="tripartite"}

**tripartite**

Having three connected components. (Salmon 1879, p.165)

:::

::: {#o544 .entry cue=term name="trisecant"}

**trisecant**

A line meeting a variety in 3 points. See [trisecant identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trisecant_identity).

:::

::: {#o545 .entry cue=term name="tritangent"}

**tritangent**

Meeting something in 3 tangent points, such as a tritangent conic to a cubic curve or a tritangent plane of a cubic surface.

:::

::: {#o546 .entry cue=term name="trope"}

**trope**

A [trope](wikipedia://en/26872956) is a singular (meaning special) tangent space. (Cayley 1869, p.202) The word is mostly used for a tangent space of a [Kummer surface](wikipedia://en/647758) touching it along a conic.

:::

::: {#o548 .entry cue=term name="twisted"}

**twisted**

A [twisted cubic](wikipedia://en/1547121) is a degree 3 embedding of the projective line in projective 3-space

:::

::: {#o549 .entry cue=term name="total"}

**total**

A set of 5 partitions of a 6-element set into three pairs, such that no two elements of the total have a pair in common. For example, {(12)(36)(45), (13)(24)(56), (14)(26)(35), (15)(23)(46), (16)(25)(34)} (Dolgachev 2012)

:::

::: {#o551 .entry cue=term name="type"}

**type**

The type of a projective surface is the number of tangent planes meeting a generic linear subspace of codimension 4. (Semple Roth 1949, p.193)

:::

## U {#U}

::: {#o554 .entry cue=term name="undulation"}

**undulation**

A point of undulation of a curve is where the tangent meets the curve to fourth order; also called a hyperflex. See inflection point. (Salmon 1879, p.35, 211)

:::

::: {#o556 .entry cue=term name="unibranch"}

**unibranch**

Having only one branch at a point. For example, a cusp of a plane curve is unibranch, while a node is not.

:::

::: {#o557 .entry cue=term name="unicursal"}

**unicursal**

A unicursal curve is one that is [rational](wikipedia://en/865939), in other words birational to the projective line. See (Salmon 1879, p. 29).

:::

::: {#o559 .entry cue=term name="unipartite"}

**unipartite**

[Connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_%28topology%29). See (Salmon 1879, p.165)

:::

::: {#o561 .entry cue=term name="unirational"}

**unirational**

A correspondence is called unirational if it is generically injective, in other words a rational map. (Semple Roth 1949, p.20)

A variety is called [unirational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unirational) if it is finitely covered by a rational variety.

:::

::: {#o563 .entry cue=term name="united point"}

**united point**

A point in the intersection of the diagonal and a correspondence from a set to itself.

:::

::: {#o564 .entry cue=term name="unode"}

**unode**

A double point of a surface whose tangent cone consists of one double plane. See binode.

:::

## V {#V}

::: {#o566 .entry cue=term name="valence"}

**valence**

:::

::: {#o567 .entry cue=term name="valency"}

**valency**

The valence or valency of a correspondence *T* on a curve is a number *k* such that the divisors *T*(*P*)+*kP* are all linearly equivalent. A correspondence need not have a valency. (Semple Roth 1949, p.368)

:::

::: {#o569 .entry cue=term name="Veronese surface"}

**Veronese surface**

An embedding of the projective plane in 5-dimensional projective space.

:::

::: {#o570 .entry cue=term name="virtual"}

**virtual**

An estimate for something that is often but not always correct, such as virtual genus, virtual dimension, and so on. If some number is given by the dimension of a space of sections of some sheaf, the corresponding virtual number is sometimes given by the corresponding Euler characteristic, and equal to the dimension when all higher cohomology groups vanish. See superabundance.

:::

## W {#W}

::: {#o572 .entry cue=term name="web"}

**web**

A 3-dimensional linear system. See "net" and "pencil". (Semple Roth 1949, p.160)

:::

::: {#o574 .entry cue=term name="Weddle surface"}

**Weddle surface**

A quartic surface in projective space given by the locus of the vertex of a cone passing through 6 points in general position.

:::

::: {#o575 .entry cue=term name="Weierstrass point"}

**Weierstrass point**

A point on a curve where the dimension of the space of rational functions whose only singularity is a pole of some order at the point is higher than normal.

:::

::: {#o576 .entry cue=term name="Wirtinger sextic"}

**Wirtinger sextic**

A degree 4 genus 6 plane curve with nodes at the 6 points of a [complete quadrangle](wikipedia://en/2605272).

:::

## XYZ {#XYZ}

::: {#o578 .entry cue=term name="Zeuthen–Segre invariant"}

**Zeuthen–Segre invariant**

The [Zeuthen–Segre invariant](wikipedia://en/35091025) is 4 less than the Euler characteristic of a non-singular projective surface.
