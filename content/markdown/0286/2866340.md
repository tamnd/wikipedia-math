# Equipotential

*Image: Equipotential of dipole.svg.* Computed electrostatic equipotentials (black contours) between two electrically charged spheres

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Equipotential"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), an **equipotential** or **isopotential** refers to a [region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Region_%28mathematics%29) in space where every point is at the same [potential](wikipedia://en/59615).[^o2][^o3][^o4] This usually refers to a [scalar potential](wikipedia://en/461654) (in that case it is a [level set](wikipedia://en/559622) of the potential), although it can also be applied to [vector potentials](wikipedia://en/422936). An equipotential of a scalar potential [function](wikipedia://en/185427) in $n$-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) space is typically an ($n - 1$)-dimensional space. The [del operator](wikipedia://en/151925) illustrates the relationship between a vector field and its associated scalar potential field. An equipotential region might be referred as being 'of equipotential' or simply be called 'an equipotential'.

:::

An equipotential region of a scalar potential in three-dimensional space is often an **equipotential surface** (or *potential [isosurface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isosurface)*), but it can also be a three-dimensional [mathematical solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_%28mathematics%29) in space. The [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) of the scalar potential (and hence also its opposite, as in the case of a vector field with an associated potential field) is everywhere perpendicular to the equipotential surface, and zero inside a three-dimensional equipotential region.

## Types {#Types}

[Electrical conductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_conductor) offer an intuitive example. If *a* and *b* are any two points within or at the surface of a given conductor, and given there is no flow of charge being exchanged between the two points, then the [potential difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_difference) is zero between the two points. Thus, an equipotential would contain both points *a* and *b* as they have the same [potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential). Extending this definition, an isopotential is the locus of all points that are of the same potential. In [electrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics) and steady [electric currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current), the [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) (and hence the current, if any) is perpendicular to the equipotential surfaces of the [electric potential](wikipedia://en/59615) ([voltage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage)). In electrostatics, a conductor is a three-dimensional equipotential region. In the case of a hollow conductor ([Faraday cage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday_cage)[^o6]), the equipotential region includes the space inside.

[Gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) is perpendicular to the equipotential surfaces of the [gravity potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_potential). In gravity, a hollow sphere has a three-dimensional equipotential region inside, with no gravity from the sphere (see [shell theorem](wikipedia://en/2025989)). A ball will not be accelerated left or right by the force of gravity if it is resting on a flat, [horizontal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_plane) surface, because it is an equipotential surface. For the [gravity of Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_of_Earth) (including its centrifugal potential owing to Earth's rotation), the corresponding [geopotential](wikipedia://en/217535) [isosurface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isosurface) (the *[equigeopotential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equigeopotential)*) that best fits [mean sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_sea_level) is called the *[geoid](wikipedia://en/176244)*.
