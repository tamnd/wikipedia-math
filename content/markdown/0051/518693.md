# Regular polytope

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Regular polytope"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **regular polytope** is a [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) whose [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) acts [transitively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_group_action) on its [flags](wikipedia://en/9352497), thus giving it the highest degree of symmetry. In particular, all its elements or $j$-faces (for all $0 \le j \le n$, where $n$ is the [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) of the polytope) — cells, faces and so on — are also transitive on the symmetries of the polytope, and are themselves regular polytopes of dimension $j\le n$.

:::

Regular polytopes are the generalised analog in any number of dimensions of [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306) (for example, the [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) or the regular pentagon) and [regular polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_polyhedra) (for example, the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285)). The strong symmetry of the regular polytopes gives them an [aesthetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics) quality that interests both mathematicians and non-mathematicians.

Classically, a regular polytope in $n$ dimensions may be defined as having regular [facets](wikipedia://en/7186647) ($[n-1]$-faces) and regular [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719). These two conditions are sufficient to ensure that all faces are alike and all [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) are alike. Note, however, that this definition does not work for [abstract polytopes](wikipedia://en/1211056).

A regular polytope can be represented by a [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) of the form $\{a, b, c, ..., y, z\},$ with regular facets as $\{a, b, c, ..., y\},$ and regular vertex figures as $\{b, c, ..., y, z\}.$

## Description {#Description}

Regular polytopes are classified primarily according to their dimension.[^n1]

- In [one dimension](wikipedia://en/25584215), the [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) simultaneously serves as the 1-simplex, the 1-hypercube and the 1-orthoplex.
- In [two dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_dimensions), there are infinitely many [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306), namely the regular $n$-sided polygon for $n \ge 3$. The triangle is the 2-simplex. The square is both the 2-hypercube and the 2-orthoplex. The $n$-sided polygons for $n \ge 5$ are exceptional.
- In [three](wikipedia://en/3054853) and [four dimensions](wikipedia://en/1364622), there are several more exceptional [regular polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_polyhedra) and [4-polytopes](wikipedia://en/24979) respectively.
- In [five dimensions](wikipedia://en/2851410) and above, the simplex, hypercube, and orthoplex are the only regular polytopes. There are no exceptional regular polytopes in these dimensions.

Regular polytopes can be further classified according to [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741). For example, the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) and the regular [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635) share the same symmetry, as do the [regular dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/7149361) and [regular icosahedron](wikipedia://en/14968). Two distinct regular polytopes with the same symmetry are [dual](wikipedia://en/609737#Dimension-reversing_dualities) to one another. Indeed, symmetry groups are sometimes named after regular polytopes, for example, the [tetrahedral](wikipedia://en/2904472) and [icosahedral symmetries](wikipedia://en/2877844).

The idea of a polytope is sometimes generalised to include related kinds of geometrical objects. Some of these have regular examples, as discussed in the section on historical discovery below.

### Schläfli symbols {#Schläfli_symbols}

A concise symbolic representation for regular polytopes was developed by [Ludwig Schläfli](wikipedia://en/1452408) in the 19th century, and a slightly modified form has become standard.[^n2] The notation is best explained by adding one dimension at a time.[^n3]

- A [convex](wikipedia://en/660019) [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) having $n$ sides is denoted by $\{n\}$. So, an [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285) is $\{3\}$, a square is $\{4\}$, and so on, indefinitely.[^n3] A regular $n$-sided [star polygon](wikipedia://en/313303) which winds $m$ times around its centre is denoted by the fractional value {*n*/*m*}, where *n* and *m* are [co-prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-prime), so a regular [pentagram](wikipedia://en/197544) is {5/2}.
- A [regular polyhedron](wikipedia://en/333299) having faces $\{n\}$ with $p$ faces joining around a vertex is denoted by $\{n,p\}$. The nine [regular polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_polyhedra) are the five Platonic solids—[tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/5242521) $\{3, 3\}$, [octahedron](wikipedia://en/22458) $\{3,4\}$, cube $\{4,3\}$, [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/14968) $\{3,5\}$, [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/7149361) $\{5,3\}$—[^n4] and [the four star polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler&ndash;Poinsot_polyhedron)—[great icosahedron](wikipedia://en/3029898) $\left\{3,5/2\right\}$, [great stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029878) $\left\{5/2, 3\right\}$, [great dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/2860441) $\left\{5, 5/2\right\}$, [small stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029843) $\left\{5/2, 5\right\}$.[^n5] This notation can be used in regular tilings, as in [triangular tiling](wikipedia://en/2863858) $\{3, 6\}$, [hexagonal tiling](wikipedia://en/2863719) $\{6,3\}$, and [square tiling](wikipedia://en/2863809) $\{4,4\}$. $\{p\}$ is the [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719) of the polyhedron.
- A regular 4-polytope having cells {*n*, *p*} with *q* cells joining around an edge is denoted by {*n*, *p*, *q*}. The vertex figure of the 4-polytope is a {*p*, *q*}.
- A regular 5-polytope is denoted by {*n*, *p*, *q*, *r*}, and so on.

### Duality of the regular polytopes {#Duality_of_the_regular_polytopes}

The [dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_polytope) of a regular polytope is also a regular polytope. The Schläfli symbol for the dual polytope is just the original symbol written backwards: {3, 3} is self-dual, {3, 4} is dual to {4, 3}, {4, 3, 3} to {3, 3, 4} and so on.

The [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719) of a regular polytope is the dual of the dual polytope's [facet](wikipedia://en/7186647). For example, the vertex figure of {3, 3, 4} is {3, 4}, the dual of which is {4, 3} — a [cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_%28geometry%29) of {4, 3, 3}.

The [measure](wikipedia://en/39783) and [cross polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_polytope) in any dimension are dual to each other.

If the Schläfli symbol is [palindromic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palindromic) (i.e. reads the same forwards and backwards), then the polytope is self-dual. The self-dual regular polytopes are:

- All [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306) - {a}.
- All regular *n*-[simplexes](wikipedia://en/39781) - {3,3,...,3}.
- The regular [24-cell](wikipedia://en/293485) - {3,4,3} - in 4 dimensions.
- The [great 120-cell](wikipedia://en/8604660) - {5,5/2,5} - and [grand stellated 120-cell](wikipedia://en/8604676) - {5/2,5,5/2} - in 4 dimensions.
- All regular *n*-dimensional [hypercubic](wikipedia://en/12237982) [honeycombs](wikipedia://en/6008654) - {4,3,...,3,4}. These may be treated as infinite polytopes.
- Hyperbolic tilings and honeycombs (tilings {p,p} with p>4 in 2 dimensions; [{4,4,4}](wikipedia://en/40319872), [{5,3,5}](wikipedia://en/3870545), [{3,5,3}](wikipedia://en/3870681), [{6,3,6}](wikipedia://en/40291952), and [{3,6,3}](wikipedia://en/40281224) in 3 dimensions; [{5,3,3,5}](wikipedia://en/41926439) in 4 dimensions; and [{3,3,4,3,3}](wikipedia://en/45690557) in 5 dimensions).

## Classifications {#Classifications}

### Regular simplices {#Regular_simplices}

The [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) is a generalization of the notion of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) or [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606) to arbitrary [dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensions). The simplex is so-named because it represents the simplest possible [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) in any given dimension. For example,[^n6]

- a [0-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0-dimensional) simplex: [point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_%28mathematics%29)
- a [1-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1-dimensional) simplex: [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860), obtained by connecting another point at a distance
- a [2-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-dimensional) simplex is a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654), obtained by connecting two points to another point
- a [3-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-dimensional) simplex is a [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606), obtained by connecting three points to another point, again, and
- a [4-dimensional](wikipedia://en/1364622) simplex is a [5-cell](wikipedia://en/513821).

This process is repeated further using new points to form higher-dimensional simplices.

### Hypercubes {#Hypercubes}

These are the **measure polytopes** or **hypercubes**. Their names are, in order of dimension:[^n7]

\0. Point

\1. Line segment

\2. [Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) (regular tetragon)

\3. [Cube](wikipedia://en/6285) (regular hexahedron)

\4. [Tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112) (regular octachoron) *or* 4-cube

\5. [Penteract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penteract) (regular decateron) *or* 5-cube

... An *n*-cube has *2<sup>n</sup>* vertices.

The process of making each hypercube can be visualised on a graph: Begin with a point *A*. Extend a line to point *B* at distance *r*, and join to form a line segment. Extend a second line of length *r*, orthogonal to *AB*, from *B* to *C*, and likewise from *A* to *D*, to form a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) *ABCD*. Extend lines of length *r* respectively from each corner, orthogonal to both *AB* and *BC* (i.e. upwards). Mark new points *E*,*F*,*G*,*H* to form the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) *ABCDEFGH*. This process is repeated further using new lines to form higher-dimensional hypercubes.

### Orthoplexes {#Orthoplexes}

These are the **cross polytopes** or **orthoplexes**. Their names are, in order of dimensionality:[^n8]

\0. Point

\1. Line segment

\2. Square (regular tetragon)

\3. Regular [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635)

\4. Regular hexadecachoron ([16-cell](wikipedia://en/716422)) *or* 4-orthoplex

\5. Regular triacontakaiditeron ([pentacross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentacross)) *or* 5-orthoplex

... An *n*-orthoplex has *2n* vertices.

The process of making each orthoplex can be visualised on a graph: Begin with a point *O*. Extend a line in opposite directions to points *A* and *B* a distance *r* from *O* and 2*r* apart. Draw a line *COD* of length 2*r*, centred on *O* and orthogonal to *AB*. Join the ends to form a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) *ACBD*. Draw a line *EOF* of the same length and centered on 'O', orthogonal to *AB* and *CD* (i.e. upwards and downwards). Join the ends to the square to form a regular [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635). This process is repeated further using new lines to form higher-dimensional orthoplices.

### Classification by Coxeter groups {#Classification_by_Coxeter_groups}

Regular polytopes can be classified by their [isometry group](wikipedia://en/1101694). These are finite [Coxeter groups](wikipedia://en/297004), but not every finite Coxeter group may be realised as the isometry group of a regular polytope. Regular polytopes are in [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) with Coxeter groups with linear [Coxeter-Dynkin diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter-Dynkin_diagram) (without branch point) and an increasing numbering of the nodes. Reversing the numbering gives the [dual polytope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_polytope).

The classification of finite Coxeter groups, which goes back to (Coxeter 1935), therefore implies the classification of regular polytopes:[^n9]

- Type $A_n$, the symmetric group, gives the regular [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781),
- Type $B_n$, gives the [measure polytope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_polytope) and the [cross polytope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_polytope) (both can be distinguished by the increasing numbering of the nodes of the Coxeter-Dynkin diagram),
- Exceptional types $I_2(n)$ give the [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306) (with $n = 3, 4, ...$),
- Exceptional type $H_3$ gives the regular [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407) and [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084) (again the numbering allows to distinguish them),
- Exceptional type $H_4$ gives the [120-cell](wikipedia://en/717358) and the [600-cell](wikipedia://en/501447),
- Exceptional type $F_4$ gives the [24-cell](wikipedia://en/293485), which is self-dual.

The bijection between regular polytopes and Coxeter groups with linear Coxeter-Dynkin diagram can be understood as follows. Consider a regular polytope $P$ of dimension $n$ and take its [barycentric subdivision](wikipedia://en/583651). The [fundamental domain](wikipedia://en/469365) of the isometry group action on $P$ is given by any simplex $\Delta$ in the barycentric subdivision. The simplex $\Delta$ has $n+1$ vertices which can be numbered from 0 to $n$ by the dimension of the corresponding face of $P$ (the face they are the barycenter of). The isometry group of $P$ is generated by the $n$ reflections around the hyperplanes of $\Delta$ containing the vertex number $n$ (since the barycenter of the whole polytope $P$ is fixed by any isometry). These $n$ hyperplanes can be numbered by the vertex of $\Delta$ they do not contain. The remaining thing to check is that any two hyperplanes with adjacent numbers cannot be orthogonal, whereas hyperplanes with non-adjacent numbers are orthogonal. This can be done using induction (since all facets of $P$ are again regular polytopes). Therefore, the Coxeter-Dynkin diagram of the isometry group of $P$ has $n$ vertices numbered from 0 to $n-1$ such that adjacent numbers are linked by at least one edge and non-adjacent numbers are not linked.

## History of discovery {#History_of_discovery}

### Convex polygons and polyhedra {#Convex_polygons_and_polyhedra}

*Image: Platonic Solids Transparent.svg.* The Platonic solids. Top left to right: [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/5242521) and [cube](wikipedia://en/6285). Middle: [regular octahedron](wikipedia://en/22458). Bottom left to right: [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/7149361) and [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/14968).

The earliest surviving mathematical treatment of regular polygons and polyhedra comes to us from [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) mathematicians. The five [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) were known to them. [Pythagoras](wikipedia://en/23275) knew of at least three of them, and [Theaetetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theaetetus_%28mathematician%29) (c. 417 BC – 369 BC) described all five.[^n10] Later, [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) wrote a systematic study of mathematics, publishing it under the title *[Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)*, which built up a logical theory of geometry and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527). His work concluded with mathematical descriptions of the five [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) inscribed in a sphere and compares the ratios of their edges to the radius of the sphere.[^n11]

### Star polygons and polyhedra {#Star_polygons_and_polyhedra}

*Image: Four Kepler-Poinsot polyhedra.svg.* Left to right: [small stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029843), [great icosahedron](wikipedia://en/3029898), [great dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/2860441), [great stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029878)

The understanding of the solids remained static for many centuries after Euclid. The subsequent history of the regular polytopes can be characterised by a gradual broadening of the basic concept, allowing more and more objects to be considered among their number. [Thomas Bradwardine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Bradwardine) (Bradwardinus) was the first to record a serious study of [star polygons](wikipedia://en/313303). Various star polyhedra appear in Renaissance art, but it was not until [Johannes Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736) studied the [small stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029843) and the [great stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029878) in 1619 that he realised these two polyhedra were regular.[^n12] [Louis Poinsot](wikipedia://en/764639) discovered the [great dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/2860441) and [great icosahedron](wikipedia://en/3029898) in 1809, and [Augustin Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustin_Cauchy) proved the list complete in 1812.[^n13] These polyhedra are collectively known as the [Kepler-Poinsot polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler-Poinsot_polyhedra).

### Higher-dimensional polytopes {#Higher-dimensional_polytopes}

It was not until the 19th century that a Swiss mathematician, [Ludwig Schläfli](wikipedia://en/1452408), examined and characterised the regular polytopes in higher dimensions. His efforts were first published in full in (Schläfli 1901), six years posthumously, although parts of it were published in (Schläfli 1855) and (Schläfli 1858).[^o32] Between 1880 and 1900, Schläfli's results were rediscovered independently by at least nine other mathematicians.[^n14] Schläfli called such a figure a "polyschem" (in English, "polyscheme" or "polyschema").[^n15] The term "polytope" was introduced by [Reinhold Hoppe](wikipedia://en/47572121), one of Schläfli's rediscoverers, in 1882, and first used in English by [Alicia Boole Stott](wikipedia://en/3325459) some twenty years later. The term "polyhedroids" was also used in earlier literature.[^n16]

(Coxeter 1973) is probably the most comprehensive printed treatment of Schläfli's and similar results to date. Schläfli showed that there are six [regular convex polytopes in 4 dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_regular_4-polytope). Five of them can be seen as analogous to the Platonic solids: the [4-simplex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-simplex) (or pentachoron) to the [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606), the [4-hypercube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-hypercube) (or 8-cell or [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112)) to the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285), the [4-orthoplex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-orthoplex) (or hexadecachoron or [16-cell](wikipedia://en/716422)) to the [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635), the [120-cell](wikipedia://en/717358) to the [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407), and the [600-cell](wikipedia://en/501447) to the [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084). The sixth, the [24-cell](wikipedia://en/293485), can be seen as a transitional form between the 4-hypercube and 16-cell, analogous to the way that the [cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/6280) and the [rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631) are transitional forms between the cube and the octahedron.

Also of interest are the star [regular 4-polytopes](wikipedia://en/18801624), partially discovered by Schläfli. By the end of the 19th century, mathematicians such as [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034) and [Ludwig Schläfli](wikipedia://en/1452408) had developed the theory of regular polytopes in four and higher dimensions, such as the [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112) and the [24-cell](wikipedia://en/293485).

*Image: 8-cell-simple.gif.* A three-dimensional projection of a rotating tesseract. This tesseract is initially oriented so that all edges are parallel to one of the four coordinate space axes. The rotation takes place in the $xw$-plane.

The latter are difficult (though not impossible) to visualise through a process of [dimensional analogy](wikipedia://en/1364622#Dimensional_analogy), since they retain the familiar symmetry of their lower-dimensional analogues. The [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112) contains 8 cubical cells. It consists of two cubes in parallel hyperplanes with corresponding vertices cross-connected in such a way that the 8 cross-edges are equal in length and orthogonal to the 12+12 edges situated on each cube. The corresponding faces of the two cubes are connected to form the remaining 6 cubical faces of the tesseract. The [24-cell](wikipedia://en/293485) can be derived from the tesseract by joining the 8 vertices of each of its cubical faces to an additional vertex to form the four-dimensional analogue of a pyramid. Both figures, as well as other 4-dimensional figures, can be directly visualised and depicted using 4-dimensional stereographs.[^o37]

Harder still to imagine are the more modern [abstract regular polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_regular_polytope) such as the [57-cell](wikipedia://en/948622) or the [11-cell](wikipedia://en/948617). From the mathematical point of view, however, these objects possess the same aesthetic qualities as their more familiar two and three-dimensional relatives.

In five and more dimensions, there are exactly three finite regular polytopes, which correspond to the tetrahedron, cube and octahedron: these are the regular simplices, measure polytopes and cross polytopes. Descriptions of these may be found in the [list of regular polytopes](wikipedia://en/505253).

### Elements and symmetry groups {#Elements_and_symmetry_groups}

At the start of the 20th century, the definition of a regular polytope was as follows.

- A regular polygon is a polygon whose edges are all equal and whose angles are all equal.
- A regular polyhedron is a polyhedron whose faces are all congruent regular polygons, and whose [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719) are all congruent and regular.
- And so on, a regular *n*-polytope is an *n*-dimensional polytope whose (*n* − 1)-dimensional faces are all regular and congruent, and whose vertex figures are all regular and congruent.

This is a "recursive" definition. It defines regularity of higher dimensional figures in terms of regular figures of a lower dimension. There is an equivalent (non-recursive) definition, which states that a polytope is regular if it has a sufficient degree of symmetry.

- An *n*-polytope is regular if any set consisting of a vertex, an edge containing it, a 2-dimensional face containing the edge, and so on up to *n*−1 dimensions, can be mapped to any other such set by a symmetry of the polytope.

So for example, the cube is regular because if we choose a vertex of the cube, and one of the three edges it is on, and one of the two faces containing the edge, then this triplet, known as a **[flag](wikipedia://en/9352497)**, (vertex, edge, face) can be mapped to any other such flag by a suitable symmetry of the cube. Thus we can define a regular polytope very succinctly:

- A regular polytope is one whose symmetry group is transitive on its flags.

In the 20th century, some important developments were made. The [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) of the classical regular polytopes were generalised into what are now called [Coxeter groups](wikipedia://en/297004). Coxeter groups also include the symmetry groups of regular [tessellations](wikipedia://en/321671) of space or of the plane. For example, the symmetry group of an infinite [chessboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chessboard) would be the Coxeter group \[4,4\].

### Apeirotopes — infinite polytopes {#Apeirotopes_—_infinite_polytopes}

In the first part of the 20th century, Coxeter and Petrie discovered three infinite structures: {4, 6}, {6, 4} and {6, 6}. They called them regular skew polyhedra, because they seemed to satisfy the definition of a regular polyhedron — all the vertices, edges and faces are alike, all the angles are the same, and the figure has no free edges. Nowadays, they are called infinite polyhedra or apeirohedra. The regular tilings of the plane [{4, 4}](wikipedia://en/2863809), [{3, 6}](wikipedia://en/2863858) and [{6, 3}](wikipedia://en/2863719) can also be regarded as infinite polyhedra.

In the 1960s [Branko Grünbaum](wikipedia://en/1731976) issued a call to the geometric community to consider more abstract types of regular polytopes that he called *polystromata*. He developed the theory of polystromata, showing examples of new objects he called [regular apeirotopes](wikipedia://en/12768648), that is, regular polytopes with [infinitely](wikipedia://en/20110824) many faces. A simple example of a [skew apeirogon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew_apeirogon) would be a zig-zag. It seems to satisfy the definition of a regular polygon — all the edges are the same length, all the angles are the same, and the figure has no loose ends (because they can never be reached). More importantly, perhaps, there are symmetries of the zig-zag that can map any pair of a vertex and attached edge to any other. Since then, other regular apeirogons and higher apeirotopes have continued to be discovered.

:::

### Regular complex polytopes {#Regular_complex_polytopes}

A [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) is a number consisting of both [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) and imaginary number $i^2 = -1$, the square root of minus one. A complex [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) has its x, y, z, etc. coordinates as complex numbers. This effectively doubles the number of dimensions. A polytope constructed in such a unitary space is called a [complex polytope](wikipedia://en/8581934).[^n17]

### Abstract polytopes {#Abstract_polytopes}

*Image: Hemicube.svg.* The [Hemicube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemi-cube_%28geometry%29) is derived from a cube by equating opposite vertices, edges, and faces. It has 4 vertices, 6 edges, and 3 faces.

Grünbaum also discovered the [11-cell](wikipedia://en/948617), a four-dimensional [self-dual](wikipedia://en/8815) object whose facets are not icosahedra, but are "hemi-icosahedra" — that is, they are the shape one gets if one considers opposite faces of the icosahedra to be actually the *same* face.[^n18] The hemi-icosahedron has only 10 triangular faces, and 6 vertices, unlike the icosahedron, which has 20 and 12.

This concept may be easier for the reader to grasp if one considers the relationship of the cube and the hemicube. An ordinary cube has 8 corners, they could be labeled A to H, with A opposite H, B opposite G, and so on. In a hemicube, A and H would be treated as the same corner. So would B and G, and so on. The edge AB would become the same edge as GH, and the face ABEF would become the same face as CDGH. The new shape has only three faces, 6 edges and 4 corners.

The 11-cell cannot be formed with regular geometry in flat (Euclidean) hyperspace, but only in positively curved (elliptic) hyperspace.

A few years after Grünbaum's discovery of the [11-cell](wikipedia://en/948617), [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328) independently discovered the same shape. He had earlier discovered a similar polytope, the [57-cell](wikipedia://en/948622).[^o45]

By 1994 Grünbaum was considering polytopes abstractly as combinatorial sets of points or vertices, and was unconcerned whether faces were planar. As he and others refined these ideas, such sets came to be called **[abstract polytopes](wikipedia://en/1211056)**. An abstract polytope is defined as a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) (poset), whose elements are the polytope's faces (vertices, edges, faces etc.) ordered by *containment*. Certain restrictions are imposed on the set that are similar to properties satisfied by the classical regular polytopes (including the Platonic solids). The restrictions, however, are loose enough that regular tessellations, hemicubes, and even objects as strange as the 11-cell or stranger, are all examples of regular polytopes.

A geometric polytope is understood to be a *realization* of the abstract polytope, such that there is a one-to-one mapping from the abstract elements to the corresponding faces of the geometric realisation. Thus, any geometric polytope may be described by the appropriate abstract poset, though not all abstract polytopes have proper geometric realizations.

The theory has since been further developed, largely by (McMullen Schulte 2002), but other researchers have also made contributions.[^n19]

#### Regularity of abstract polytopes {#Regularity_of_abstract_polytopes}

Regularity has a related, though different meaning for [abstract polytopes](wikipedia://en/1211056), since angles and lengths of edges have no meaning.

The definition of regularity in terms of the transitivity of flags as given in the introduction applies to abstract polytopes.

Any classical regular polytope has an abstract equivalent which is regular, obtained by taking the set of faces. But non-regular classical polytopes can have regular abstract equivalents, since abstract polytopes do not retain information about angles and edge lengths, for example. And a regular abstract polytope may not be realisable as a classical polytope.

*All polygons* are regular in the abstract world, for example, whereas only those having equal angles and edges of equal length are regular in the classical world.

#### Vertex figure of abstract polytopes {#Vertex_figure_of_abstract_polytopes}

The concept of *vertex figure* is also defined differently for an [abstract polytope](wikipedia://en/1211056). The vertex figure of a given abstract *n*-polytope at a given vertex *V* is the set of all abstract faces which contain *V*, including *V* itself. More formally, it is the abstract section

*F*<sub>*n*</sub> / *V* = {*F* | *V* ≤ *F* ≤ *F*<sub>*n*</sub>}

where *F*<sub>*n*</sub> is the maximal face, i.e. the notional *n*-face which contains all other faces. Note that each *i*-face, *i* ≥ 0 of the original polytope becomes an (*i* − 1)-face of the vertex figure.

Unlike the case for Euclidean polytopes, an abstract polytope with regular facets and vertex figures *may or may not* be regular itself – for example, the square pyramid, all of whose facets and vertex figures are regular abstract polygons.

The classical vertex figure will, however, be a realisation of the abstract one.

## Constructions {#Constructions}

### Polygons {#Polygons}

The traditional way to construct a regular polygon, or indeed any other figure on the plane, is by [compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge). Constructing some regular polygons in this way is very simple (the easiest is perhaps the equilateral triangle), some are more complex, and some are impossible ("not constructible"). The simplest few regular polygons that are impossible to construct are the *n*-sided polygons with *n* equal to 7, 9, 11, 13, 14, 18, 19, 21,...

[Constructibility](wikipedia://en/523430) in this sense refers only to ideal constructions with ideal tools. Of course reasonably accurate approximations can be constructed by a range of methods; while theoretically possible constructions may be impractical.

### Polyhedra {#Polyhedra}

Euclid's *Elements* gave what amount to ruler-and-compass constructions for the five Platonic solids.[^o54] However, the merely practical question of how one might draw a straight line in space, even with a ruler, might lead one to question what exactly it means to "construct" a regular polyhedron. (One could ask the same question about the polygons, of course.)

*Image: icosahedron flat.svg.* [Net](wikipedia://en/1148129) for [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084)

The English word "construct" has the connotation of systematically building the thing constructed. The most common way presented to construct a regular polyhedron is via a [fold-out net](wikipedia://en/1148129). To obtain a fold-out net of a polyhedron, one takes the surface of the polyhedron and cuts it along just enough edges so that the surface may be laid out flat. This gives a plan for the net of the unfolded polyhedron. Since the Platonic solids have only triangles, squares and pentagons for faces, and these are all constructible with a ruler and compass, there exist ruler-and-compass methods for drawing these fold-out nets. The same applies to star polyhedra, although here we must be careful to make the net for only the visible outer surface.

If this net is drawn on cardboard, or similar foldable material (for example, sheet metal), the net may be cut out, folded along the uncut edges, joined along the appropriate cut edges, and so forming the polyhedron for which the net was designed. For a given polyhedron there may be many fold-out nets. For example, there are 11 for the cube, and over 900000 for the dodecahedron.[^o55]

Numerous children's toys, generally aimed at the teen or pre-teen age bracket, allow experimentation with regular polygons and polyhedra. For example, [klikko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klikko) provides sets of plastic triangles, squares, pentagons and hexagons that can be joined edge-to-edge in a large number of different ways. A child playing with such a toy could rediscover the Platonic solids (or the [Archimedean solids](wikipedia://en/1847)), especially if given a little guidance from a knowledgeable adult.

In theory, almost any material may be used to construct regular polyhedra.[^o56] They may be carved out of wood, modeled out of wire, formed from stained glass. The imagination is the limit.

### Higher dimensions {#Higher_dimensions}

*Image: Tesseract2.svg.* [Net](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_%28polytope%29) for [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112)

*Image: Hypercube.svg.* A perspective projection ([Schlegel diagram](wikipedia://en/7201595)) for tesseract

*Image: 24cell section anim.gif.* An animated cut-away cross-section of the [24-cell](wikipedia://en/293485).

In higher dimensions, it becomes harder to say what one means by "constructing" the objects. Clearly, in a 3-dimensional universe, it is impossible to build a physical model of an object having 4 or more dimensions. There are several approaches normally taken to overcome this matter.

The first approach, suitable for four dimensions, uses four-dimensional stereography.[^o37] Depth in a third dimension is represented with horizontal relative displacement, depth in a fourth dimension with vertical relative displacement between the left and right images of the stereograph.

The second approach is to embed the higher-dimensional objects in three-dimensional space, using methods analogous to the ways in which three-dimensional objects are drawn on the plane. For example, the fold out nets mentioned in the previous section have higher-dimensional equivalents.[^o58] One might even imagine building a model of this fold-out net, as one draws a polyhedron's fold-out net on a piece of paper. Sadly, we could never do the necessary folding of the 3-dimensional structure to obtain the 4-dimensional polytope because of the constraints of the physical universe. Another way to "draw" the higher-dimensional shapes in 3 dimensions is via some kind of projection, for example, the analogue of either [orthographic](wikipedia://en/203052) or [perspective](wikipedia://en/380405) projection. Coxeter's famous book on polytopes (Coxeter 1973) has some examples of such orthographic projections.[^o60] Note that immersing even 4-dimensional polychora directly into two dimensions is quite confusing. Easier to understand are 3-d models of the projections. Such models are occasionally found in science museums or mathematics departments of universities (such as that of the [Université Libre de Bruxelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Université_Libre_de_Bruxelles)).

The intersection of a four-dimensional (or higher) regular polytope with a three-dimensional hyperplane will be a polytope (not necessarily regular). If the hyperplane is moved through the shape, the three-dimensional slices can be combined, [animated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animated) into a kind of four dimensional object, where the fourth dimension is taken to be time. In this way, we can see (if not fully grasp) the full four-dimensional structure of the four-dimensional regular polytopes, via such cutaway cross sections. This is analogous to the way a [CAT scan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CAT_scan) reassembles two-dimensional images to form a 3-dimensional representation of the organs being scanned. The ideal would be an animated [hologram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hologram) of some sort; however, even a simple animation such as the one shown can already give some limited insight into the structure of the polytope.

Another way a three-dimensional viewer can comprehend the structure of a four-dimensional polytope is through being "immersed" in the object, perhaps via some form of [virtual reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_reality) technology. To understand how this might work, a viewer might imagine what they would see if space were filled with cubes. The viewer would be inside one of the cubes, and would be able to see cubes to their left and right, above and below themselves, and to their front and back. If one could travel in these directions, one could explore the array of cubes, and gain an understanding of its geometrical structure. An [infinite array of cubes](wikipedia://en/3733318) is not a finite polytope; it is a tessellation of 3-dimensional ([Euclidean](wikipedia://en/9697)) space. However, a 4-polytope can be considered a tessellation of a 3-dimensional [non-Euclidean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Euclidean) space, namely, a tessellation of the surface of a four-dimensional [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) (a 4-dimensional [spherical tiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_tiling)).

*Image: Hyperbolic orthogonal dodecahedral honeycomb.png.* [A regular dodecahedral honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3754285), {5,3,4}, of hyperbolic space projected into 3-space.

Locally, this space seems like the one we are familiar with, and therefore, a virtual-reality system could, in principle, be programmed to allow exploration of these "tessellations", that is, of the 4-dimensional regular polytopes. The mathematics department at [UIUC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana-Champaign) has a number of pictures of what one would see if embedded in a [tessellation](wikipedia://en/321671) of [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742) with dodecahedra. Such a tessellation forms an example of an infinite abstract regular polytope.

Normally, for abstract regular polytopes, a mathematician considers that the object is "constructed" if the structure of its [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) is known. This is because of an important theorem in the study of abstract regular polytopes, providing a technique that allows the abstract regular polytope to be constructed from its symmetry group in a standard and straightforward manner.
