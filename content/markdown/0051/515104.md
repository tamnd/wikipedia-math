# Fisher's fundamental theorem of natural selection

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fisher's fundamental theorem of natural selection"}

**Fisher's fundamental theorem of natural selection** is an idea about [genetic variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_variance)[^o2][^o3] in [population genetics](wikipedia://en/219268) developed by the [statistician](wikipedia://en/26685) and [evolutionary biologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_biology) [Ronald Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824). The proper way of applying the abstract mathematics of the theorem to actual biology has been a matter of some debate, however, it is a true theorem.[^o4]

:::

It states:

"The rate of increase in [fitness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitness_%28biology%29) of any [organism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism) at any time is equal to its genetic variance in fitness at that time."[^o5]

Or in more modern terminology:

"The rate of increase in the mean fitness of any organism, at any time, that is ascribable to natural selection acting through changes in [gene frequencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_frequencies), is exactly equal to its genetic variance in fitness at that time".[^o6]

## History {#History}

The theorem was first formulated in Fisher's 1930 book *[The Genetical Theory of Natural Selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Genetical_Theory_of_Natural_Selection)*.[^o5] Fisher likened it to the [law of entropy](wikipedia://en/133017) in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), stating that "It is not a little instructive that so similar a law should hold the supreme position among the biological sciences". The model of [quasi-linkage equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-linkage_equilibrium) was introduced by [Motoo Kimura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motoo_Kimura) in 1965 as an approximation in the case of [weak selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_selection) and weak [epistasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistasis).[^o8][^o9]

Largely as a result of Fisher's feud with the American geneticist [Sewall Wright](wikipedia://en/1700593) about [adaptive landscapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptive_landscapes), the theorem was widely misunderstood to mean that the average fitness of a population would always increase, even though models showed this not to be the case.[^o10] In 1972, [George R. Price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_R._Price) [showed](wikipedia://en/1076205) that Fisher's theorem was indeed correct (and that Fisher's proof was also correct, given a typo or two), but did not find it to be of great significance. The sophistication that Price pointed out, and that had made understanding difficult, is that the theorem gives a formula for part of the change in gene frequency, and not for all of it. This is a part that can be said to be due to natural selection.[^o11]

Due to confounding factors, tests of the fundamental theorem are quite rare though Bolnick in 2007 did test this effect in a natural population.[^o12]
