# RSA numbers

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="RSA numbers"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **RSA numbers** are a set of large [semiprimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiprimes) (numbers with exactly two [prime factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor)) that were part of the [RSA Factoring Challenge](wikipedia://en/498851). The challenge was to find the prime factors of each number. It was created by [RSA Laboratories](wikipedia://en/301240) in March 1991 to encourage research into [computational number theory](wikipedia://en/511466) and the practical difficulty of [factoring](wikipedia://en/15491) large [integers](wikipedia://en/14563). The challenge was ended in 2007.[^o2]

:::

## History {#History}

[RSA Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_Laboratories) (which is an [initialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initialism) of the creators of the technique, Rivest, Shamir and Adleman) published a number of semiprimes with 100 to 617 [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214) digits. Cash prizes of varying size, up to 200000 (and prizes up to 20000 awarded), were offered for factorization of some of them. The smallest RSA number was factored in a few days. Most of the numbers have still not been factored and many of them are expected to remain unfactored for many years to come.

, the smallest 24 as well as the 26th of the 54 listed numbers have been factored.

While the RSA challenge officially ended in 2007, people are still attempting to find the factorizations. According to RSA Laboratories, "Now that the industry has a considerably more advanced understanding of the cryptanalytic strength of common symmetric-key and public-key algorithms, these challenges are no longer active."[^o4] Some of the smaller prizes had been awarded at the time. The remaining prizes were retracted.

The first RSA numbers generated, from RSA-100 to RSA-500, were labeled according to their number of decimal digits. Later, beginning with RSA-576, [binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_numeral_system) digits are counted instead. An exception to this is RSA-617, which was created before the change in the numbering scheme. The numbers are listed in increasing order below.

### RSA-100 {#RSA-100}

RSA-100 has 100 decimal digits (330 bits). Its factorization was announced on April 1, 1991, by [Arjen K. Lenstra](wikipedia://en/6850292).[^o12][^o13] Reportedly, the factorization took a few days using [the multiple-polynomial quadratic sieve algorithm](wikipedia://en/582340) on a [MasPar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MasPar) parallel computer.[^o14]

The value and factorization of RSA-100 are as follows:

RSA-100 = 1522605027922533360535618378132637429718068114961380688657908494580122963258952897654000350692006139

RSA-100 = 37975227936943673922808872755445627854565536638199 × 40094690950920881030683735292761468389214899724061

RSA-100 is often used to benchmark new factorization software or new hardware.

As of December 2009, it took four hours to repeat this factorization using the program Msieve on a 2200 MHz [Athlon 64](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athlon_64) processor.

As of June 2015, the number could be factorized in 72 minutes on overclocked to 3.5 GHz Intel Core2 Quad q9300, using GGNFS and Msieve binaries running by distributed version of the factmsieve Perl script.[^o15]

As of June 2025, the number was factored in 108 seconds on 32 [Epyc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epyc) 9174 server cores using YAFU's implementation of the self initializing quadratic sieve.[^o16]

As of January 2026, the number was reported to have been factored in 4 minutes and 57 seconds on an [NVIDIA RTX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nvidia_RTX) 5070 Ti, the first such factorization on a complete quadratic sieve factorization pipeline on GPU.[^o17]

### RSA-129 {#RSA-129}

RSA-129, having 129 decimal digits (426 bits), was not part of the 1991 RSA Factoring Challenge, but rather related to [Martin Gardner](wikipedia://en/20435)'s [Mathematical Games column](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Games_column) in the August 1977 issue of *[Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American)*.[^o12]

RSA-129 was factored in April 1994 by a team led by [Derek Atkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek_Atkins), [Michael Graff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Graff), [Arjen K. Lenstra](wikipedia://en/6850292) and [Paul Leyland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Leyland), using approximately 1600 computers[^o25] from around 600 volunteers connected over the [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet).[^o26] A [US\$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_dollar)100 token prize was awarded by RSA Security for the factorization, which was donated to the [Free Software Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Software_Foundation).

The value and factorization are as follows:

RSA-129 = 114381625757888867669235779976146612010218296721242362562561842935706935245733897830597123563958705058989075147599290026879543541

RSA-129 = 3490529510847650949147849619903898133417764638493387843990820577 × 32769132993266709549961988190834461413177642967992942539798288533

The factorization was found using the [multiple polynomial quadratic sieve](wikipedia://en/582340#Multiple_polynomials) algorithm.

The factoring challenge included a message encrypted with RSA-129. When decrypted using the factorization the message was revealed to be "[The Magic Words are Squeamish Ossifrage](wikipedia://en/551707)".

In 2015, RSA-129 was factored in about one day, with the CADO-NFS open source implementation of number field sieve, using a commercial [cloud computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_computing) service for about \$30.[^o27]
