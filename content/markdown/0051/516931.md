# Map (mathematics)

*Image: Function\_color\_example\_3.svg.* A map is a function, as in the association of any of the four colored shapes in *X* to its color in *Y*

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Map"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **map** or **mapping** is synonymous to a [function](wikipedia://en/185427),[^o2][^o3] though various branches add further nuances to differentiate them. The terms may have originated as from the process of making a [geographical map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Map): *mapping* the Earth surface to a sheet of paper.[^o4]

:::

## Maps and functions {#Maps_and_functions}

The term *map* may be used to distinguish some special types of functions, such as [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13564). For example, a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) is a homomorphism of [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370), while the term [linear function](wikipedia://en/152111) may have this meaning or it may mean a [linear polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_polynomial).[^o6][^o7] In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a map may refer to a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891).[^o4] The term *transformation* can be used interchangeably,[^o4] but *[transformation](wikipedia://en/1904373)* often refers to a function from a set to itself.

Certain branches will often define a *map* with a property of particular importance to that branch. For instance, a *map* is a "[continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122)" in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), a "[linear transformation](wikipedia://en/18102)" in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), etc. Maps of certain kinds have also been given specific names. These include [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13564) in [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), [isometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometries) in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), [operators](wikipedia://en/22213) in [analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) and [representations](wikipedia://en/12695) in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890).[^o4]

In the theory of [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087), a map denotes an [evolution function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete-time_dynamical_system) used to create [discrete dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087#Maps).

Some authors, such as [Serge Lang](wikipedia://en/872991),[^o8] use "function" only to refer to maps in which the [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) is a set of numbers (i.e. a subset of [**R**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) or [**C**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers)), and reserve the term *mapping* for more general functions.

## As morphisms {#As_morphisms}

In category theory, "map" is often used as a synonym for "[morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891)" or "arrow", which is a structure-respecting function and thus may imply more structure than "function" does.[^o10] For example, a morphism $f\colon X \to Y$ in a [concrete category](wikipedia://en/167001) (i.e. a morphism that can be viewed as a function) carries with it the information of its domain (the source $X$ of the morphism) and its codomain (the target $Y$). In the widely used definition of a function $f\colon X \to Y$, $f$ is a subset of $X \times Y$ consisting of all the pairs $\big(x, f(x)\big)$ for $x \in X$. In this sense, the function does not capture the set $Y$ that is used as the codomain; only the range $f(X)$ is determined by the function.
