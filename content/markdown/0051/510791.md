# List of cognitive biases

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="List of cognitive biases"}

In [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology) and [cognitive science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science), [cognitive biases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_bias) are systematic patterns of deviation from norm and/or rationality in judgment.[^o3][^o2] They are often studied in [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology), [sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology) and [behavioral economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_economics).[^o3]

:::

A memory bias is a [cognitive bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_bias) that either enhances or impairs the recall of a [memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory) (either the chances that the memory will be recalled at all, or the amount of time it takes for it to be recalled, or both), or that alters the content of a reported memory.

Explanations include information-processing rules (i.e., mental shortcuts), called *[heuristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristics_in_judgment_and_decision-making)*, that the brain uses to produce [decisions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision-making) or judgments. Biases have a variety of forms and appear as cognitive ("cold") bias, such as mental noise,[^o12] or motivational ("hot") bias, such as when beliefs are distorted by [wishful thinking](wikipedia://en/386062). Both effects can be present at the same time.[^o4][^o5]

There are also controversies over some of these biases as to whether they count as useless or [irrational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrationality), or whether they result in useful attitudes or behavior. For example, when getting to know others, people tend to ask [leading questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading_question) which seem biased towards confirming their assumptions about the person. However, this kind of [confirmation bias](wikipedia://en/59160) has also been argued to be an example of [social skill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_skills); a way to establish a connection with the other person.[^o6]

Although this research overwhelmingly involves human subjects, some studies have found bias in non-human animals as well. For example, [loss aversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loss_aversion) has been shown in monkeys and [hyperbolic discounting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_discounting) has been observed in rats, pigeons, and monkeys.[^o7]

## Organization of cognitive biases {#Organization_of_cognitive_biases}

Although the reality of these biases is confirmed by [reproducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproducible) research,[^o9][^o10] there are often controversies about how to classify these biases or how to explain them.[^o11] Several [theoretical causes are known for some cognitive biases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_bias#Common_theoretical_causes_of_some_cognitive_biases), which provides a classification of biases by their common generative mechanism (such as noisy information-processing[^o12]). [Gerd Gigerenzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerd_Gigerenzer) has criticized the framing of cognitive biases as errors in judgment, and favors interpreting them as arising from rational deviations from logical thought.[^o13] This list is organized based on the task-based classification proposed by (Dimara Franconeri Plaisant Bezerianos Dragicevic). This classification defines 6 tasks, namely estimation, decision, hypothesis assessment, causal attribution, recall, and opinion reporting. The biases are further loosely classified into 5 sub-categories or "flavors":[^o15]

1. **Association:** a connection between different pieces of information
1. **Baseline:** comparing something to a perceived standard or starting point
1. **Inertia:** the reluctance to change something that is already in place
1. **Outcome:** how well something aligns with an expected or hoped-for result
1. **Self-perspective:** influenced by one's own personal point of view

## Estimation {#Estimation}

In estimation or judgement tasks, people are asked to assess the value of a quantity.

### Association {#Association}

::: {#o18 .entry cue=list-entry name="Aesthetic–usability effect"}

- [Aesthetic–usability effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetic–usability_effect): A tendency for people to perceive attractive things as more usable.[^o19]

:::

::: {#o20 .entry cue=list-entry name="Attribute substitution"}

- [Attribute substitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attribute_substitution): Occurs when a judgment has to be made (of a target attribute) that is computationally complex, and instead a more easily calculated heuristic attribute is substituted. This substitution is thought of as taking place in the automatic intuitive judgment system, rather than the more self-aware reflective system.[^o21]

:::

::: {#o22 .entry cue=list-entry name="Availability heuristic"}

- The [availability heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Availability_heuristic) (also known as the availability bias) is the tendency to overestimate the likelihood of events with greater "availability" in memory, which can be influenced by how recent the memories are or how unusual or emotionally charged they may be.[^o23] There is a greater likelihood of recalling recent, nearby, or otherwise immediately available examples, and the imputation of importance to those examples over others.

:::

::: {#o24 .entry cue=list-entry name="Conjunction fallacy"}

- [Conjunction fallacy](wikipedia://en/732729), the tendency to assume that specific conditions are more probable than a more general version of those same conditions.[^o25]

:::

::: {#o26 .entry cue=list-entry name="Hot-cold empathy gap"}

- [Hot-cold empathy gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot-cold_empathy_gap), the tendency to underestimate the influence of [visceral drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot-cold_empathy_gap#Visceral_factors) on one's attitudes, preferences, and behaviors.[^o27]

:::

::: {#o28 .entry cue=list-entry name="Identifiable victim effect"}

- [Identifiable victim effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identifiable_victim_effect), people tend to be more persuaded by a story rather than a statistic or data set.

:::

::: {#o29 .entry cue=list-entry name="Tachypsychia"}

- [Tachypsychia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tachypsychia): When time perceived by the individual either lengthens or contracts, making events appear to slow down or speed up.[^o30]

:::

::: {#o31 .entry cue=list-entry name="Time-saving bias"}

- [Time-saving bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-saving_bias), a tendency to underestimate the time that could be saved (or lost) when increasing (or decreasing) from a relatively low speed, and to overestimate the time that could be saved (or lost) when increasing (or decreasing) from a relatively high speed.[^o32]

:::

::: {#o33 .entry cue=list-entry name="Chronological snobbery"}

- Travis syndrome: Overestimating the significance of the present.[^o34] It is related to [chronological snobbery](wikipedia://en/2432024) with possibly an [appeal to novelty](wikipedia://en/219435) [logical fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_fallacy) being part of the bias.

:::

### Baseline {#Baseline}

::: {#o36 .entry cue=list-entry name="anchoring bias"}

- The [anchoring bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchoring_%28cognitive_bias%29), or focalism, is the tendency to rely too heavily—to "anchor"—on one trait or piece of information when making decisions (usually the first piece of information acquired on that subject).[^o37][^o234]

:::

::: {#o38 .entry cue=list-entry name="Base rate fallacy"}

- [Base rate fallacy](wikipedia://en/732873) or base rate neglect, the tendency to ignore general information and focus on information only pertaining to the specific case, even when the general information is more important.[^o39]

:::

::: {#o40 .entry cue=list-entry name="Dunning–Kruger effect"}

- [Dunning–Kruger effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunning–Kruger_effect), the tendency for unskilled individuals to overestimate their own ability and the tendency for experts to underestimate their own ability.[^o41]

:::

::: {#o42 .entry cue=list-entry name="Gambler's fallacy"}

- [Gambler's fallacy](wikipedia://en/12970), the tendency to think that future probabilities are altered by past events, when in reality they are unchanged. The fallacy arises from an erroneous conceptualization of the [law of large numbers](wikipedia://en/157055). For example, "I've flipped heads with this coin five times consecutively, so the chance of tails coming out on the sixth flip is much greater than heads."[^o43]

:::

::: {#o44 .entry cue=list-entry name="Hard–easy effect"}

- [Hard–easy effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard–easy_effect), the tendency to overestimate one's ability to accomplish hard tasks, and underestimate one's ability to accomplish easy tasks.[^o12][^o45][^o46][^o47]

:::

::: {#o48 .entry cue=list-entry name="Hot-hand fallacy"}

- [Hot-hand fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot-hand_fallacy) (also known as "hot hand phenomenon" or "hot hand"), the belief that a person who has experienced success with a random event has a greater chance of further success in additional attempts.[^o49]

:::

::: {#o50 .entry cue=list-entry name="Insensitivity to sample size"}

- [Insensitivity to sample size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insensitivity_to_sample_size), the tendency to under-expect variation in small samples.[^o43]

:::

::: {#o51 .entry cue=list-entry name="Hungry judge effect"}

- Interoceptive bias or [hungry judge effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungry_judge_effect): The tendency for sensory input about the body itself to affect one's judgement about external, unrelated circumstances. (As for example, in parole judges who are more lenient when fed and rested.)[^o52][^o53][^o54][^o55]

:::

- Conservatism or regressive bias: Tendency to remember high values and high likelihoods/probabilities/frequencies as lower than they actually were and low ones as higher than they actually were. Based on the evidence, memories are not extreme enough.[^o56][^o57]

::: {#o58 .entry cue=list-entry name="Subadditivity effect"}

- [Subadditivity effect](wikipedia://en/4713918): The tendency to estimate that the likelihood of a remembered event is less than the sum of its (more than two) mutually exclusive components.[^o59]

:::

- Systematic bias: Judgement that arises when targets of differentiating judgement become subject to effects of regression that are not equivalent.[^o60]
- Unit bias: The standard suggested amount of consumption (e.g., food serving size) is perceived to be appropriate, and a person would consume it all even if it is too much for this particular person.[^o61]

::: {#o62 .entry cue=list-entry name="Weber–Fechner law"}

- [Weber–Fechner law](wikipedia://en/273831): Difficulty in perceiving and comparing small differences in large quantities.[^o63]

:::

### Inertia {#Inertia}

::: {#o65 .entry cue=list-entry name="Conservatism bias"}

- [Conservatism bias](wikipedia://en/34980746), the tendency to insufficiently [revise one's belief](wikipedia://en/1187311) when presented with new evidence.[^o12][^o66][^o67]

:::

### Outcome {#Outcome}

- Exaggerated expectation: The tendency to expect or predict more extreme outcomes than those outcomes that actually happen.[^o12]
- Hedonic recall bias: The tendency for people who are satisfied with their wage to overestimate how much they earn, and conversely, for people who are unsatisfied with their wage to underestimate it.[^o69]

::: {#o70 .entry cue=list-entry name="Illusion of validity"}

- [Illusion of validity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_validity), the tendency to overestimate the accuracy of one's judgments, especially when available information is consistent or inter-correlated.[^o71]

:::

::: {#o72 .entry cue=list-entry name="Impact bias"}

- [Impact bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_bias): The tendency to overestimate the length or the intensity of the impact of future feeling states.[^o73]

:::

::: {#o74 .entry cue=list-entry name="Outcome bias"}

- [Outcome bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outcome_bias): The tendency to judge a decision by its eventual outcome instead of the quality of the decision at the time it was made.[^o75]

:::

::: {#o76 .entry cue=list-entry name="Planning fallacy"}

- [Planning fallacy](wikipedia://en/903029), the tendency for people to underestimate the time it will take them to complete a given task.[^o73]

:::

::: {#o77 .entry cue=list-entry name="Restraint bias"}

- [Restraint bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restraint_bias), the tendency to overestimate one's ability to show restraint in the face of temptation.[^o78]

:::

::: {#o79 .entry cue=list-entry name="Sexual overperception bias"}

- [Sexual overperception bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_overperception_bias), the tendency to overestimate sexual interest of another person in oneself, and [sexual underperception bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_underperception_bias), the tendency to underestimate it.[^o80]

:::

### Self-perspective {#Self-perspective}

::: {#o82 .entry cue=list-entry name="Curse of knowledge"}

- [Curse of knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curse_of_knowledge): When better-informed people find it extremely difficult to think about problems from the perspective of lesser-informed people.[^o83]

:::

::: {#o84 .entry cue=list-entry name="Extrinsic incentives bias"}

- [Extrinsic incentives bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extrinsic_incentives_bias), an exception to the *fundamental attribution error*, where people view others as having (situational) extrinsic motivations, while viewing themselves as having (dispositional) intrinsic motivations.[^o85]

:::

::: {#o86 .entry cue=list-entry name="False consensus effect"}

- [False consensus effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_consensus_effect), the tendency for people to overestimate the degree to which others agree with them.[^o87]

:::

::: {#o88 .entry cue=list-entry name="Illusion of transparency"}

- [Illusion of transparency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_transparency), the tendency for people to overestimate the degree to which their personal mental state is known by others, and to overestimate how well they understand others' personal mental states.[^o89]

:::

::: {#o90 .entry cue=list-entry name="Naïve cynicism"}

- [Naïve cynicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naïve_cynicism), expecting more egocentric bias in others than in oneself.[^o91]

:::

::: {#o92 .entry cue=list-entry name="Optimism bias"}

- [Optimism bias](wikipedia://en/7224456): The tendency to be over-optimistic, underestimating greatly the probability of undesirable outcomes and overestimating favorable and pleasing outcomes (see also [wishful thinking](wikipedia://en/386062), [valence effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_effect), [positive outcome bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_effect), and compare [pessimism bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pessimism_bias)).[^o93][^o94]

:::

::: {#o95 .entry cue=list-entry name="Outgroup homogeneity bias"}

- [Outgroup homogeneity bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outgroup_homogeneity_bias), where individuals see members of other groups as being relatively less varied than members of their own group.[^o96]

:::

::: {#o97 .entry cue=list-entry name="Pessimism bias"}

- [Pessimism bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pessimism_bias): The tendency to overestimate the likelihood that bad things will happen. (compare [optimism bias](wikipedia://en/7224456)).[^o98]

:::

::: {#o99 .entry cue=list-entry name="Spotlight effect"}

- [Spotlight effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spotlight_effect): The tendency to overestimate the amount that other people notice one's appearance or behavior.[^o100]

:::

::: {#o101 .entry cue=list-entry name="Worse-than-average effect"}

- [Worse-than-average effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worse-than-average_effect): A tendency to believe ourselves to be worse than others at tasks which are difficult.[^o102]

:::

## Decision {#Decision}

In decision or *choice* tasks, people select one option out of several.

### Association {#Association}

::: {#o105 .entry cue=list-entry name="Ambiguity effect"}

- [Ambiguity effect](wikipedia://en/4740896), the tendency to avoid options for which the probability of a favorable outcome is unknown.[^o106]

:::

::: {#o107 .entry cue=list-entry name="Authority bias"}

- [Authority bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authority_bias), the tendency to attribute greater accuracy to the opinion of an authority figure (unrelated to its content) and be more influenced by that opinion.[^o108]

:::

::: {#o109 .entry cue=list-entry name="Automation bias"}

- [Automation bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automation_bias), the tendency to depend excessively on automated systems which can lead to erroneous automated information overriding correct decisions.[^o110]

:::

::: {#o111 .entry cue=list-entry name="Default effect"}

- [Default effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Default_effect), the tendency to favor the default option when given a choice between several options.[^o112]

:::

- Dread aversion, just as losses yield double the emotional impact of gains, dread yields double the emotional impact of savouring.[^o113][^o114]

::: {#o115 .entry cue=list-entry name="framing effect"}

- The [framing effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framing_effect_%28psychology%29) is the tendency to draw different conclusions from the same information, depending on how that information is presented.[^o116]

:::

::: {#o117 .entry cue=list-entry name="Hyperbolic discounting"}

- [Hyperbolic discounting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_discounting), where discounting is the tendency for people to have a stronger preference for more immediate payoffs relative to later payoffs. Hyperbolic discounting leads to choices that are inconsistent over time—people make choices today that their future selves would prefer not to have made, despite using the same reasoning.[^o118] Also known as current moment bias or present bias, and related to [Dynamic inconsistency](wikipedia://en/1494164). A good example of this is a study showed that when making food choices for the coming week, 74% of participants chose fruit, whereas when the food choice was for the current day, 70% chose chocolate.[^o119][^o120]

:::

::: {#o121 .entry cue=list-entry name="Compassion fade"}

- [Compassion fade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compassion_fade), the tendency to behave more compassionately towards a small number of identifiable victims than to a large number of anonymous ones.[^o122]

:::

::: {#o123 .entry cue=list-entry name="Loss aversion"}

- [Loss aversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loss_aversion), where the perceived disutility of giving up an object is greater than the utility associated with acquiring it.[^o124] (see also [Sunk cost fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunk_cost_fallacy))

:::

::: {#o125 .entry cue=list-entry name="Neglect of probability"}

- [Neglect of probability](wikipedia://en/4724667), the tendency to completely disregard probability when making a decision under uncertainty.[^o126]

:::

- Non-adaptive choice switching: After experiencing a bad outcome with a decision problem, the tendency to avoid the choice previously made when faced with the same decision problem again, even though the choice was optimal. Also known as "once bitten, twice shy" or "hot stove effect".[^o127]
- Prevention bias: When investing money to protect against risks, decision makers perceive that a dollar spent on prevention buys more security than a dollar spent on timely detection and response, even when investing in either option is equally effective.[^o128]

::: {#o129 .entry cue=list-entry name="Pseudocertainty effect"}

- [Pseudocertainty effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudocertainty_effect), the tendency to make risk-averse choices if the expected outcome is good but risk-seeking choices if it is bad.[^o130]

:::

::: {#o131 .entry cue=list-entry name="Risk compensation"}

- [Risk compensation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk_compensation) or [Peltzman effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peltzman_effect): The tendency to take greater risks when perceived safety increases.[^o132]

:::

::: {#o133 .entry cue=list-entry name="Zero-risk bias"}

- [Zero-risk bias](wikipedia://en/4741644), the preference for reducing a small risk to zero over a greater reduction in a larger risk.[^o134]

:::

### Baseline {#Baseline}

::: {#o136 .entry cue=list-entry name="Action bias"}

- [Action bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_bias): The tendency for someone to act when faced with a problem even when inaction would be more effective, or to act when no evident problem exists.[^o137][^o138]

:::

::: {#o139 .entry cue=list-entry name="Additive bias"}

- [Additive bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additive_bias): The tendency to solve problems through addition, even when subtraction is a better approach.[^o140][^o141]

:::

::: {#o142 .entry cue=list-entry name="Decoy effect"}

- [Decoy effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decoy_effect), where preferences for either option A or B change in favor of option B when option C is presented, which is completely dominated by option B (inferior in all respects) and partially dominated by option A.[^o143]

:::

::: {#o144 .entry cue=list-entry name="Ballot order effect"}

- [Ballot order effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballot_order_effect), where candidates who are listed first often receive a small but statistically significant increase in votes compared to those listed in lower positions.[^o145]

:::

::: {#o146 .entry cue=list-entry name="Cheerleader effect"}

- [Cheerleader effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheerleader_effect), the tendency for people to appear more attractive in a group than in isolation.[^o147]

:::

- Compromise effect, choices affected if presented as extreme or average[^o15]

::: {#o148 .entry cue=list-entry name="Denomination effect"}

- [Denomination effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denomination_effect), the tendency to spend more money when it is denominated in small amounts (e.g., coins) rather than large amounts (e.g., bills).[^o149]

:::

::: {#o150 .entry cue=list-entry name="Disposition effect"}

- [Disposition effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disposition_effect), the tendency to sell an asset that has accumulated in value and resist selling an asset that has declined in value.[^o151]

:::

::: {#o152 .entry cue=list-entry name="Distinction bias"}

- [Distinction bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distinction_bias), the tendency to view two options as more dissimilar when evaluating them simultaneously than when evaluating them separately.[^o153]

:::

::: {#o154 .entry cue=list-entry name="Less-is-better effect"}

- [Less-is-better effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Less-is-better_effect), the tendency to prefer a smaller set to a larger set judged separately, but not jointly.

:::

::: {#o155 .entry cue=list-entry name="Money illusion"}

- [Money illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_illusion): The tendency to concentrate on the nominal value (face value) of money rather than its value in terms of purchasing power.[^o156]

:::

- Phantom effect: choices affected by dominant but unavailable options.[^o15]

::: {#o157 .entry cue=list-entry name="Normalcy bias"}

- [Normalcy bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalcy_bias), a form of cognitive dissonance, is the refusal to plan for, or react to, a disaster which has never happened before.[^o158]

:::

::: {#o159 .entry cue=list-entry name="Projection bias"}

- [Projection bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projection_bias): The tendency to overestimate how much one's future selves will share one's current preferences, thoughts and values, thus leading to sub-optimal choices.[^o160]

:::

::: {#o161 .entry cue=list-entry name="Scope neglect"}

- [Scope neglect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scope_neglect) or scope insensitivity, the tendency to be insensitive to the size of a problem when evaluating it. For example, being willing to pay as much to save 2,000 children or 20,000 children.[^o162]

:::

### Inertia {#Inertia}

- Doubling-back aversion, the tendency for people to avoid retracing their steps or restarting a task, even when doing so would clearly save time or effort, because it feels like undoing past progress rather than making future gains.[^o164]

::: {#o165 .entry cue=list-entry name="Endowment effect"}

- [Endowment effect](wikipedia://en/804737), the tendency for people to demand much more to give up an object than they would be willing to pay to acquire it.[^o166]

:::

::: {#o167 .entry cue=list-entry name="Escalation of commitment"}

- [Escalation of commitment](wikipedia://en/1704142), irrational escalation, or [sunk cost fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunk_cost_fallacy), where people justify increased investment in a decision, based on the cumulative prior investment, despite new evidence suggesting that the decision was probably wrong.

:::

::: {#o168 .entry cue=list-entry name="Functional fixedness"}

- [Functional fixedness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_fixedness), a tendency limiting a person to using an object only in the way it is traditionally used.[^o169]

:::

::: {#o170 .entry cue=list-entry name="Mere exposure effect"}

- [Mere exposure effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mere_exposure_effect) or familiarity principle (in social psychology): The tendency to express undue liking for things merely because of familiarity with them.[^o171]

:::

::: {#o172 .entry cue=list-entry name="Plan continuation bias"}

- [Plan continuation bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plan_continuation_bias), failure to recognize that the original plan of action is no longer appropriate for a changing situation or for a situation that is different from anticipated.[^o173]

:::

::: {#o174 .entry cue=list-entry name="Semmelweis reflex"}

- [Semmelweis reflex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semmelweis_reflex), the tendency to reject new evidence that contradicts a paradigm.[^o67]

:::

::: {#o175 .entry cue=list-entry name="Shared information bias"}

- [Shared information bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shared_information_bias): The tendency for group members to spend more time and energy discussing information that all members are already familiar with (i.e., shared information), and less time and energy discussing information that only some members are aware of (i.e., unshared information).[^o176]

:::

::: {#o177 .entry cue=list-entry name="Status quo bias"}

- [Status quo bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Status_quo_bias), the tendency to prefer things to stay relatively the same.[^o178][^o179]

:::

::: {#o180 .entry cue=list-entry name="Well travelled road effect"}

- [Well travelled road effect](wikipedia://en/25725314), the tendency to underestimate the duration taken to traverse oft-travelled routes and overestimate the duration taken to traverse less familiar routes.[^o181]

:::

### Outcome {#Outcome}

::: {#o183 .entry cue=list-entry name="Present bias"}

- [Present bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Present_bias): The tendency of people to give stronger weight to payoffs that are closer to the present time when considering trade-offs between two future moments.[^o184]

:::

::: {#o185 .entry cue=list-entry name="Reactance"}

- [Reactance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactance_%28psychology%29): The urge to do the opposite of what someone wants one to do out of a need to resist a perceived attempt to constrain one's freedom of choice (see also [Reverse psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse_psychology)).

:::

### Self-perspective {#Self-perspective}

::: {#o187 .entry cue=list-entry name="Effort justification"}

- [Effort justification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effort_justification), a person's tendency to attribute greater value to an outcome if they had to put effort into achieving it. This can result in more value being applied to an outcome than it actually has. An example of this is the [IKEA effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IKEA_effect), the tendency for people to place a disproportionately high value on objects that they partially assembled themselves, such as furniture from [IKEA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IKEA), regardless of the quality of the end product.[^o188]

:::

::: {#o189 .entry cue=list-entry name="Law of the instrument"}

- [Law of the instrument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_instrument), an over-reliance on a familiar tool or methods, ignoring or under-valuing alternative approaches. "If all you have is a hammer, everything looks like a nail."

:::

::: {#o190 .entry cue=list-entry name="Not invented here"}

- [Not invented here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Not_invented_here), an aversion to contact with or use of products, research, standards, or knowledge developed outside a group.

:::

::: {#o191 .entry cue=list-entry name="Reactive devaluation"}

- [Reactive devaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactive_devaluation): Devaluing proposals only because they purportedly originated with an adversary.

::: {#o192 .entry cue=list-entry name="Social comparison bias"}

- [Social comparison bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_comparison_bias): The tendency, when making decisions, to favour potential candidates who do not compete with one's own particular strengths.[^o193][^o194]

## Hypothesis assessment {#Hypothesis_assessment}

In hypothesis assessment, people determine whether a statement is true or false.

### Association {#Association}

::: {#o197 .entry cue=list-entry name="Agent detection bias"}

- [Agent detection bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agent_detection_bias), the inclination to presume the purposeful intervention of a sentient or intelligent [agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agency_%28philosophy%29).

:::

::: {#o198 .entry cue=list-entry name="Availability cascade"}

- [Availability cascade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Availability_cascade), a self-reinforcing process in which a [collective belief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_belief) gains more and more plausibility through its increasing repetition in public discourse (or "repeat something long enough and it will become true").[^o199] See also [availability heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Availability_heuristic).

:::

::: {#o200 .entry cue=list-entry name="Cognitive dissonance"}

- [Cognitive dissonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_dissonance) is the perception of contradictory information and the mental toll of it.

:::

::: {#o201 .entry cue=list-entry name="Common source bias"}

- [Common source bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_source_bias), the tendency to combine or compare research studies from the same source, or from sources that use the same methodologies or data.[^o202]

:::

- False priors are initial beliefs and knowledge which interfere with the unbiased evaluation of factual evidence and lead to incorrect conclusions.

::: {#o203 .entry cue=list-entry name="Fluency heuristic"}

- [Fluency heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluency_heuristic): If one object is [processed more fluently](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Processing_fluency), faster, or more smoothly than another, the mind infers that this object has the higher value with respect to the question being considered. In other words, the more skillfully or elegantly an idea is communicated, the more likely it is to be considered seriously, whether or not it is logical

:::

::: {#o204 .entry cue=list-entry name="Groupthink"}

- [Groupthink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groupthink), the psychological [phenomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenon) that occurs within a [group of people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_group) in which the desire for harmony or conformity in the group results in an irrational or dysfunctional [decision-making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision-making) outcome. Group members try to minimize conflict and reach a consensus decision without [critical evaluation](wikipedia://en/276872) of alternative viewpoints by actively suppressing dissenting viewpoints, and by isolating themselves from outside influences.

:::

::: {#o205 .entry cue=list-entry name="Groupshift"}

- [Groupshift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groupshift), the tendency for decisions to be more risk-seeking or risk-averse than the group as a whole, if the group is already biased in that direction

:::

::: {#o206 .entry cue=list-entry name="Illusion of explanatory depth"}

- [Illusion of explanatory depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_explanatory_depth), the tendency to believe that one understands a topic much better than one actually does.[^o207][^o208] The effect is strongest for explanatory knowledge, whereas people tend to be better at self-assessments for procedural, narrative, or factual knowledge.[^o208][^o209]

:::

::: {#o210 .entry cue=list-entry name="Illusory truth effect"}

- [Illusory truth effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusory_truth_effect), the tendency to believe that a statement is true if it is [easier to process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluency_heuristic), or if it has been [stated multiple times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Processing_fluency), regardless of its actual veracity. People are more likely to identify as true statements those they have previously heard (even if they cannot consciously remember having heard them), regardless of the actual validity of the statement. In other words, a person is more likely to believe a familiar statement than an unfamiliar one.

:::

::: {#o211 .entry cue=list-entry name="Probability matching"}

- [Probability matching](wikipedia://en/9731945): Sub-optimal matching of the probability of choices with the probability of reward in a stochastic context.

:::

::: {#o212 .entry cue=list-entry name="Rhyme as reason effect"}

- [Rhyme as reason effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhyme_as_reason_effect), where rhyming statements are perceived as more truthful.

:::

::: {#o213 .entry cue=list-entry name="Quantification bias"}

- [Quantification bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantification_bias), the tendency to ascribe more weight to measured/quantified metrics than to unquantifiable values.[^o214] See also: [McNamara fallacy](wikipedia://en/24290703).

:::

::: {#o215 .entry cue=list-entry name="Salience bias"}

- [Salience bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salience_bias), the tendency to focus on items that are more prominent or emotionally striking and ignore those that are unremarkable, even though this difference is often irrelevant by objective standards.[^o216] See also [von Restorff effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Restorff_effect).

:::

::: {#o217 .entry cue=list-entry name="Saying is believing effect"}

- [Saying is believing effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saying_is_believing_effect): Communicating a socially tuned message to an audience can lead to a bias of identifying the tuned message as one's own thoughts.[^o218][^o219]

:::

::: {#o220 .entry cue=list-entry name="Selection bias"}

- [Selection bias](wikipedia://en/394392), which happens when the members of a [statistical sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_%28statistics%29) are not chosen completely at random, which leads to the sample not being representative of the population.

:::

::: {#o221 .entry cue=list-entry name="Subadditivity effect"}

- [Subadditivity effect](wikipedia://en/4713918), the tendency to judge the probability of the whole to be less than the probabilities of the parts.[^o222]

:::

::: {#o223 .entry cue=list-entry name="Truth bias"}

- [Truth bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth_bias) is people's inclination towards believing, to some degree, the communication of another person, regardless of whether or not that person is actually lying or being untruthful.[^o224][^o225]

:::

### Outcome {#Outcome}

::: {#o227 .entry cue=list-entry name="Barnum effect"}

- [Barnum effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect) or Forer effect, the tendency for individuals to give high accuracy ratings to descriptions of their personality that supposedly are tailored specifically for them, but are in fact vague and general enough to apply to a wide range of people. This effect can provide a partial explanation for the widespread acceptance of some beliefs and practices, such as [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology), [fortune telling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_telling), [graphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphology), and some types of [personality tests](wikipedia://en/609194).[^o228]

:::

::: {#o229 .entry cue=list-entry name="Belief bias"}

:::

::: {#o231 .entry cue=list-entry name="Berkson's paradox"}

:::

::: {#o233 .entry cue=list-entry name="Clustering illusion"}

:::

::: {#o235 .entry cue=list-entry name="Confirmation bias"}

:::

::: {#o237 .entry cue=list-entry name="Congruence bias"}

- [Congruence bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruence_bias), the tendency to test hypotheses exclusively through direct testing, instead of testing possible alternative hypotheses.[^o234]

:::

::: {#o238 .entry cue=list-entry name="Extension neglect"}

- [Extension neglect](wikipedia://en/34952656) occurs where the quantity of the sample size is not sufficiently taken into consideration when assessing the outcome, relevance or judgement.

:::

::: {#o239 .entry cue=list-entry name="Gender bias"}

- [Gender bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_bias), a widespread[^o240] set of implicit biases that discriminate against a gender. For example, the assumption that women are less suited to jobs requiring high intellectual ability.[^o241] Or the assumption that people or animals are male in the absence of any indicators of gender.[^o242]

:::

::: {#o243 .entry cue=list-entry name="Illusory correlation"}

- [Illusory correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusory_correlation): Inaccurately seeing a relationship between two events related by coincidence.[^o244]

:::

::: {#o245 .entry cue=list-entry name="Information bias"}

- [Information bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_bias_%28psychology%29): The tendency to seek information even when it cannot affect action.[^o246]

:::

::: {#o247 .entry cue=list-entry name="Observer-expectancy effect"}

- [Observer-expectancy effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observer-expectancy_effect), when a researcher expects a given result and therefore unconsciously manipulates an experiment or misinterprets data in order to find it (see also [subject-expectancy effect](wikipedia://en/3192289)).

:::

::: {#o248 .entry cue=list-entry name="Overconfidence effect"}

- [Overconfidence effect](wikipedia://en/3080323), a tendency to have excessive confidence in one's own answers to questions. For example, for certain types of questions, answers that people rate as "99% certain" turn out to be wrong 40% of the time.[^o12][^o249][^o250][^o251]

:::

::: {#o252 .entry cue=list-entry name="Pareidolia"}

- [Pareidolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pareidolia), a tendency to perceive a vague and random stimulus (often an image or sound) as significant, e.g., seeing images of animals or faces in clouds, the [man in the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man_in_the_Moon), and hearing non-existent [hidden messages](wikipedia://en/172270) on [records played in reverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backmasking).[^o253]

:::

::: {#o254 .entry cue=list-entry name="Subjective validation"}

- [Subjective validation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subjective_validation), where statements are perceived as true if a subject's belief demands it to be true. Also assigns perceived connections between coincidences. (Compare [confirmation bias](wikipedia://en/59160).)

:::

::: {#o255 .entry cue=list-entry name="Survivorship bias"}

- [Survivorship bias](wikipedia://en/1745325), which is concentrating on the people or things that "survived" some process and inadvertently overlooking those that did not because of their lack of visibility.

:::

::: {#o256 .entry cue=list-entry name="Unconscious bias"}

- [Unconscious bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unconscious_bias) or implicit bias: The underlying attitudes and stereotypes that people unconsciously attribute to another person or group of people that affect how they understand and engage with them. Many researchers suggest that unconscious bias occurs automatically as the brain makes quick judgments based on past experiences and background.[^o257]

:::

::: {#o258 .entry cue=list-entry name="Value selection bias"}

- [Value selection bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_selection_bias): The tendency to rely on existing numerical data when reasoning in an unfamiliar context, even if calculation or numerical manipulation is required.[^o259][^o260]

## Causal attribution {#Causal_attribution}

In a causal attribution task, people are asked to explain the causes of behavior and events.

### Outcome {#Outcome}

::: {#o263 .entry cue=list-entry name="Apophenia"}

:::

::: {#o265 .entry cue=list-entry name="Assumed similarity bias"}

:::

::: {#o267 .entry cue=list-entry name="Context neglect bias"}

::: {#o281 .entry cue=list-entry name="Hostile attribution bias"}

::: {#o283 .entry cue=list-entry name="Illusory correlation"}

::: {#o286 .entry cue=list-entry name="Illusion of control"}

::: {#o291 .entry cue=list-entry name="Motonormativity"}

- [Motonormativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motonormativity): Also known as windshield bias, car blindness or car brain. The assumption that motor vehicle use is an unremarkable social norm, causing people to discount harms caused by motor vehicle use compared to similar harms caused by other behaviors.

::: {#o292 .entry cue=list-entry name="Plant blindness"}

::: {#o298 .entry cue=list-entry name="Puritanical bias"}

::: {#o300 .entry cue=list-entry name="Surrogation"}

Existing social, economic, and political arrangements tend to be preferred, and alternatives disparaged, sometimes even at the expense of individual and collective self-interest.

::: {#o305 .entry cue=list-entry name="Turkey illusion"}

### Self-perspective {#Self-perspective}

::: {#o307 .entry cue=list-entry name="Actor-observer bias"}

- [Actor-observer bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actor-observer_bias), the tendency for explanations of other individuals' behaviors to overemphasize the influence of their personality and underemphasize the influence of their situation (see also [Fundamental attribution error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_attribution_error)), and for explanations of one's own behaviors to do the opposite (that is, to overemphasize the influence of our situation and underemphasize the influence of our own personality).

::: {#o311 .entry cue=list-entry name="Experimenter's"}

::: {#o313 .entry cue=list-entry name="False uniqueness bias"}

:::

::: {#o315 .entry cue=list-entry name="Fundamental attribution error"}

- [Fundamental attribution error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_attribution_error), the tendency for people to overemphasize personality-based explanations for behaviors observed in others while under-emphasizing the role and power of situational influences on the same behavior[^o275] (see also actor-observer bias, [group attribution error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_attribution_error), positivity effect, and [negativity effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negativity_effect)).[^o316]

:::

::: {#o317 .entry cue=list-entry name="Ingroup bias"}

:::

::: {#o318 .entry cue=list-entry name="Objectivity illusion"}

:::

::: {#o320 .entry cue=list-entry name="Ostrich effect"}

:::

::: {#o321 .entry cue=list-entry name="Outgroup favoritism"}

:::

::: {#o323 .entry cue=list-entry name="Pygmalion effect"}

:::

::: {#o324 .entry cue=list-entry name="Selective perception"}

:::

::: {#o325 .entry cue=list-entry name="Self-serving bias"}

## Recall {#Recall}

In a recall or memory task, people are asked to recall or recognize previous material.

### Association {#Association}

::: {#o330 .entry cue=list-entry name="Boundary extension"}

- [Boundary extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_extension): Remembering the background of an image as being larger or more expansive than the foreground[^o331]

:::

::: {#o332 .entry cue=list-entry name="Childhood amnesia"}

- [Childhood amnesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Childhood_amnesia): The retention of few memories from before the age of four.

:::

- Consistency bias: Incorrectly remembering one's past attitudes and behaviour as resembling present attitudes and behaviour.[^o333]

::: {#o334 .entry cue=list-entry name="Contrast effect"}

- [Contrast effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrast_effect), the enhancement or reduction of a certain stimulus's perception when compared with a recently observed, contrasting object.[^o335]

:::

::: {#o336 .entry cue=list-entry name="Cryptomnesia"}

- [Cryptomnesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptomnesia), where a memory is mistaken for novel thought or imagination, because there is no subjective experience of it being a memory.[^o337]

:::

::: {#o338 .entry cue=list-entry name="Cue-dependent forgetting"}

- [Cue-dependent forgetting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cue-dependent_forgetting) context effect: That cognition and memory are dependent on context, such that out-of-context memories are more difficult to retrieve than in-context memories (e.g., recall time and accuracy for a work-related memory will be lower at home, and vice versa).

:::

::: {#o339 .entry cue=list-entry name="Google effect"}

- [Google effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_effect): The tendency to forget information that can be found readily online by using Internet search engines.

:::

::: {#o340 .entry cue=list-entry name="Duration neglect"}

- [Duration neglect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duration_neglect), the neglect of the duration of an episode in determining its value.[^o341]

:::

::: {#o342 .entry cue=list-entry name="Fading affect bias"}

- [Fading affect bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fading_affect_bias): A bias in which the emotion associated with unpleasant memories fades more quickly than the emotion associated with pleasant ones.[^o343]

:::

::: {#o344 .entry cue=list-entry name="False memory"}

- [False memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_memory), where imagination is mistaken for a memory.

:::

- Humor effect: That humorous items are more easily remembered than non-humorous ones, which might be explained by the distinctiveness of humor, the increased cognitive processing time to understand the humor, or the emotional arousal caused by the humor.[^o345]

::: {#o346 .entry cue=list-entry name="Implicit association"}

- [Implicit association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicit_association_test), where the speed with which people can match words depends on how closely they are associated.

:::

::: {#o347 .entry cue=list-entry name="Spacing effect"}

- Lag effect: The phenomenon whereby learning is greater when studying is spread out over time, as opposed to studying the same amount of time in a single session. See also [spacing effect](wikipedia://en/903495).

:::

::: {#o348 .entry cue=list-entry name="Levels-of-processing effect"}

- [Levels-of-processing effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levels-of-processing_effect): That different methods of encoding information into memory have different levels of effectiveness.[^o349]

:::

::: {#o350 .entry cue=list-entry name="Leveling and sharpening"}

- [Leveling and sharpening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leveling_and_sharpening): Memory distortions introduced by the loss of details in a recollection over time, often concurrent with sharpening or selective recollection of certain details that take on exaggerated significance in relation to the details or aspects of the experience lost through leveling. Both biases may be reinforced over time, and by repeated recollection or re-telling of a memory.[^o351]

:::

::: {#o352 .entry cue=list-entry name="Memory inhibition"}

- [Memory inhibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_inhibition): Being shown some items from a list makes it harder to retrieve the other items (e.g., Slamecka, 1968).

:::

::: {#o353 .entry cue=list-entry name="Misinformation effect"}

- [Misinformation effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misinformation_effect): Memory becoming less accurate because of interference from *post-event information*.[^o354] [cf.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cf.) *[continued influence effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continued_influence_effect)*, where misinformation about an event, despite later being corrected, continues to influence memory about the event.

:::

::: {#o355 .entry cue=list-entry name="Modality effect"}

- [Modality effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modality_effect): That memory recall is higher for the last items of a list when the list items were received via speech than when they were received through writing.

:::

::: {#o356 .entry cue=list-entry name="Repetition blindness"}

- [Repetition blindness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repetition_blindness): Unexpected difficulty in remembering more than one instance of a visual sequence

:::

::: {#o357 .entry cue=list-entry name="Mood-congruent memory bias"}

- [Mood-congruent memory bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State-dependent_memory) (state-dependent memory): The improved recall of information congruent with one's current mood.

:::

::: {#o358 .entry cue=list-entry name="Next-in-line effect"}

- [Next-in-line effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Next-in-line_effect): When taking turns speaking in a group using a predetermined order (e.g. going clockwise around a room, taking numbers, etc.) people tend to have diminished recall for the words of the person who spoke immediately before them.[^o359]

:::

::: {#o360 .entry cue=list-entry name="Part-list cueing effect"}

- [Part-list cueing effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Part-list_cueing_effect): That being shown some items from a list and later retrieving one item causes it to become harder to retrieve the other items.[^o361]

:::

::: {#o362 .entry cue=list-entry name="Peak–end rule"}

- [Peak–end rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak–end_rule): That people seem to perceive not the sum of an experience but the average of how it was at its peak (e.g., pleasant or unpleasant) and how it ended.

:::

::: {#o363 .entry cue=list-entry name="traumatic event"}

- Persistence: The unwanted recurrence of memories of a [traumatic event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_trauma).

:::

::: {#o364 .entry cue=list-entry name="Perky effect"}

- The [Perky effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perky_effect), where real images can influence imagined images, or be misremembered as imagined rather than real

:::

::: {#o365 .entry cue=list-entry name="Picture superiority effect"}

- [Picture superiority effect](wikipedia://en/903460): The notion that concepts that are learned by viewing pictures are more easily and frequently recalled than are concepts that are learned by viewing their written word form counterparts.[^o366][^o367][^o368][^o369][^o370][^o371]

:::

::: {#o372 .entry cue=list-entry name="Socioemotional selectivity theory"}

- Positivity effect ([Socioemotional selectivity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socioemotional_selectivity_theory)): Older adults' tendency to favor good over bad information in their memories. See also [euphoric recall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphoric_recall)

:::

::: {#o373 .entry cue=list-entry name="Levels-of-processing effect"}

- Processing difficulty effect: That information that takes longer to read and is thought about more (processed with more difficulty) is more easily remembered.[^o374] See also [levels-of-processing effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levels-of-processing_effect).

:::

::: {#o375 .entry cue=list-entry name="Reminiscence bump"}

- [Reminiscence bump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reminiscence_bump): The recalling of more personal events from adolescence and early adulthood than personal events from other lifetime periods.[^o376]

:::

::: {#o377 .entry cue=list-entry name="Social cryptomnesia"}

- [Social cryptomnesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_cryptomnesia), a failure by people and society in general to remember the origin of a change, in which people know that a change has occurred in society, but forget how this change occurred; that is, the steps that were taken to bring this change about, and who took these steps. This has led to reduced social credit towards the minorities who made major sacrifices that led to a change in societal values.[^o378]

:::

::: {#o379 .entry cue=list-entry name="Source confusion"}

- [Source confusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misattribution_of_memory#Source_confusion), episodic memories are confused with other information, creating distorted memories.[^o380]

:::

::: {#o381 .entry cue=list-entry name="Spacing effect"}

- [Spacing effect](wikipedia://en/903495): That information is better recalled if exposure to it is repeated over a long span of time rather than a short one.

:::

::: {#o382 .entry cue=list-entry name="Recency effect"}

- Suffix effect: Diminishment of the [recency effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recency_effect) because a sound item is appended to the list that the subject is *not* required to recall.[^o383][^o384] A form of [serial position effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_position_effect). cf. [recency effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recency_effect) and [primacy effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primacy_effect).

:::

::: {#o385 .entry cue=list-entry name="Suggestibility"}

- [Suggestibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suggestibility#External), where ideas suggested by a questioner are mistaken for memory.

:::

::: {#o386 .entry cue=list-entry name="Telescoping effect"}

- [Telescoping effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescoping_effect): The tendency to displace recent events backwards in time and remote events forward in time, so that recent events appear more remote, and remote events, more recent.

:::

::: {#o387 .entry cue=list-entry name="Testing effect"}

- [Testing effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testing_effect): The fact that one more easily recall information one has read by rewriting it instead of rereading it.[^o388] Frequent testing of material that has been committed to memory improves memory recall.

:::

::: {#o389 .entry cue=list-entry name="Tip of the tongue"}

- [Tip of the tongue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tip_of_the_tongue) phenomenon: When a subject is able to recall parts of an item, or related information, but is frustratingly unable to recall the whole item. This is thought to be an instance of "blocking" where multiple similar memories are being recalled and interfere with each other.[^o337]

:::

- Verbatim effect: That the "gist" of what someone has said is better remembered than the verbatim wording.[^o390] This is because memories are representations, not exact copies.

::: {#o391 .entry cue=list-entry name="Zeigarnik effect"}

- [Zeigarnik effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeigarnik_effect): That uncompleted or interrupted tasks are remembered better than completed ones.

:::

### Baseline {#Baseline}

::: {#o393 .entry cue=list-entry name="Bizarreness effect"}

- [Bizarreness effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bizarreness_effect): Bizarre material is better remembered than common material.

:::

::: {#o394 .entry cue=list-entry name="Frequency illusion"}

- [Frequency illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_illusion) or Baader–Meinhof phenomenon. The frequency illusion is that once something has been noticed then every instance of that thing is noticed, leading to the belief it has a high frequency of occurrence (a form of [selection bias](wikipedia://en/394392)).[^o395] The Baader–Meinhof phenomenon is the illusion where something that has recently come to one's attention suddenly seems to appear with improbable frequency shortly afterwards.[^o396][^o397] It was named after an incidence of frequency illusion in which the [Baader–Meinhof Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Army_Faction) was mentioned.[^o398]

:::

- List-length effect: A smaller percentage of items are remembered in a longer list, but as the length of the list increases, the absolute number of items remembered increases as well.[^o399]

::: {#o400 .entry cue=list-entry name="Negativity bias"}

- [Negativity bias](wikipedia://en/9445847) or Negativity effect: The phenomenon of having better [recall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recall_%28memory%29) of unpleasant memories than of pleasant ones.[^o401][^o275] (see also actor-observer bias, [group attribution error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_attribution_error), positivity effect, and [negativity effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negativity_effect)).[^o316]

:::

::: {#o402 .entry cue=list-entry name="Primacy effect"}

- [Primacy effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primacy_effect): Where an item at the beginning of a list is more easily recalled. A form of [serial position effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_position_effect). See also [recency effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recency_effect) and [suffix effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffix_effect).

:::

::: {#o403 .entry cue=list-entry name="Recency effect"}

- [Recency effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recency_effect): A form of [serial position effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_position_effect) where an item at the end of a list is easier to recall. This can be disrupted by the [suffix effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffix_effect). See also [primacy effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primacy_effect).

:::

::: {#o404 .entry cue=list-entry name="Serial position effect"}

- [Serial position effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_position_effect): That items near the end of a sequence are the easiest to recall, followed by the items at the beginning of a sequence; items in the middle are the least likely to be remembered.[^o405] See also [recency effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recency_effect), [primacy effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primacy_effect) and [suffix effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffix_effect).

:::

::: {#o406 .entry cue=list-entry name="Von Restorff effect"}

- [von Restorff effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Restorff_effect): That an item that sticks out is more likely to be remembered than other items.[^o407]

:::

### Inertia {#Inertia}

::: {#o409 .entry cue=list-entry name="Attentional bias"}

- [Attentional bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attentional_bias), the tendency of perception to be affected by recurring thoughts.[^o410]

:::

::: {#o411 .entry cue=list-entry name="Misinformation effect"}

- Continued influence effect: Misinformation continues to influence memory and reasoning about an event, despite the misinformation having been corrected.[^o412] cf. *[misinformation effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misinformation_effect)*, where the original memory is affected by incorrect information received later.

:::

- Stereotype bias or stereotypical bias: Memory distorted towards stereotypes (e.g., racial or gender).

### Outcome {#Outcome}

::: {#o414 .entry cue=list-entry name="Choice-supportive bias"}

- [Choice-supportive bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choice-supportive_bias): The tendency to remember one's choices as better than they actually were.[^o415]

:::

::: {#o416 .entry cue=list-entry name="Declinism"}

- [Declinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declinism): The predisposition to view the past favorably ([rosy retrospection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosy_retrospection)) and the future unfavorably.[^o417]

:::

::: {#o418 .entry cue=list-entry name="Euphoric recall"}

- [Euphoric recall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphoric_recall): The tendency of people to remember past experiences favorably while overlooking bad experiences associated with them.

:::

::: {#o419 .entry cue=list-entry name="Hindsight bias"}

- [Hindsight bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindsight_bias): Sometimes called the "I-knew-it-all-along" effect, or the "Hindsight is 20/20" effect, is the tendency to see past events as having been predictable[^o420] before they happened.

:::

::: {#o421 .entry cue=list-entry name="Recency illusion"}

- [Recency illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recency_illusion): The illusion that a phenomenon one has noticed only recently is itself recent. Often used to refer to linguistic phenomena; the illusion that a word or language usage that one has noticed only recently is an innovation when it is, in fact, long-established (see also [frequency illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_illusion)). Also [recency bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recency_bias) is a [cognitive bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_bias) that favors recent events over historic ones. A [memory bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_bias), recency bias gives "greater importance to the most recent event",[^o422] such as the final lawyer's closing argument a jury hears before being dismissed to deliberate.

:::

::: {#o423 .entry cue=list-entry name="Rosy retrospection"}

- [Rosy retrospection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosy_retrospection): The remembering of the past as having been better than it really was.

:::

### Self-perspective {#Self-perspective}

::: {#o425 .entry cue=list-entry name="Cross-race effect"}

- [Cross-race effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-race_effect): The tendency for people of one race to have difficulty identifying members of a race other than their own.

:::

- Gender differences in eyewitness memory: The tendency for a witness to remember more details about someone of the same gender.

::: {#o426 .entry cue=list-entry name="Generation effect"}

- [Generation effect](wikipedia://en/6256328) (Self-generation effect): That self-generated information is remembered best. For instance, people are better able to recall memories of statements that they have generated than similar statements generated by others.

:::

::: {#o427 .entry cue=list-entry name="Illusory superiority"}

- Placement bias: Tendency to remember ourselves to be better than others at tasks at which we rate ourselves above average (also [Illusory superiority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusory_superiority) or [Better-than-average effect](wikipedia://en/3080323#Better-than-average_effects))[^o41] and tendency to remember ourselves to be worse than others at tasks at which we rate ourselves below average (also [Worse-than-average effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worse-than-average_effect)).[^o428]

:::

::: {#o429 .entry cue=list-entry name="Self-relevance effect"}

- [Self-relevance effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-reference_effect): That memories relating to the self are better recalled than similar information relating to others.

:::

## Opinion reporting {#Opinion_reporting}

In an opinion reporting task, people answer questions regarding their beliefs or opinions on political, moral, or social issues.

### Association {#Association}

::: {#o432 .entry cue=list-entry name="Halo effect"}

- [Halo effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halo_effect), the tendency for a person's good or bad traits to "spill over" from one personality area to another in others' perceptions of them (see also [physical attractiveness stereotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_attractiveness_stereotype)).[^o433]

:::

::: {#o434 .entry cue=list-entry name="Moral credential"}

- [Moral credential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_credential) effect: Occurs when someone who does something good gives themselves permission to be less good in the future.

:::

::: {#o435 .entry cue=list-entry name="Negativity bias"}

- [Negativity bias](wikipedia://en/9445847): Social judgments are more influenced by negative information than positive information.[^o15]

:::

### Inertia {#Inertia}

::: {#o437 .entry cue=list-entry name="Backfire effect"}

- [Backfire effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backfire_effect), a tendency to react to disconfirming evidence by strengthening one's previous beliefs.[^o438]

:::

::: {#o439 .entry cue=list-entry name="End-of-history illusion"}

- [End-of-history illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End-of-history_illusion): The age-independent belief that one will change less in the future than one has in the past.[^o440]

:::

::: {#o441 .entry cue=list-entry name="Omission bias"}

- [Omission bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omission_bias): The tendency to judge harmful actions (commissions) as worse, or less moral, than equally harmful inactions (omissions).[^o442]

:::

### Outcome {#Outcome}

::: {#o444 .entry cue=list-entry name="Bandwagon effect"}

- [Bandwagon effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandwagon_effect), the tendency to do (or believe) things because many other people do (or believe) the same. Related to [groupthink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groupthink) and [herd behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herd_behavior).[^o445]

:::

- Courtesy bias, the tendency to give an opinion that is more socially correct than one's true opinion, so as to avoid offending anyone.[^o446]

::: {#o447 .entry cue=list-entry name="Illusion of learning"}

- [Illusion of learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_learning), a false belief that if you understand something you learned and acquired a knowledge about it.[^o448]

:::

::: {#o449 .entry cue=list-entry name="Moral luck"}

- [Moral luck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_luck), the tendency for people to ascribe greater or lesser moral standing based on the outcome of an event.

:::

::: {#o450 .entry cue=list-entry name="Misinterpreted-effort hypothesis"}

- [Misinterpreted-effort hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misinterpreted-effort_hypothesis): Perceiving effort as a poor learning.[^o451]

:::

::: {#o452 .entry cue=list-entry name="Social desirability bias"}

- [Social desirability bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_desirability_bias), the tendency to over-report socially desirable characteristics or behaviours in oneself and under-report socially undesirable characteristics or behaviours.[^o453] See also: .

:::

::: {#o454 .entry cue=list-entry name="Stereotyping"}

- [Stereotyping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereotyping), expecting a member of a group to have certain characteristics without having actual information about that individual.

:::

::: {#o455 .entry cue=list-entry name="Women are wonderful effect"}

- [Women are wonderful effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_are_wonderful_effect): A tendency to associate more good attributes with women than with men.

:::

### Self-perspective {#Self-perspective}

::: {#o457 .entry cue=list-entry name="Anthropocentric thinking"}

- [Anthropocentric thinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocentric_thinking), the tendency to use human analogies as a basis for reasoning about other, less familiar, biological phenomena.[^o458]

:::

::: {#o459 .entry cue=list-entry name="Anthropomorphism"}

- [Anthropomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropomorphism#Psychology) is characterization of animals, objects, and abstract concepts as possessing human traits, emotions, or intentions.[^o460] The opposite bias, of not attributing feelings or thoughts to another person, is [dehumanised perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dehumanization),[^o461] a type of [objectification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objectification).

:::

::: {#o462 .entry cue=list-entry name="Ben Franklin effect"}

- [Ben Franklin effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Franklin_effect), where a person who has performed a favor for someone is more likely to do another favor for that person than they would be if they had *received* a favor from that person.[^o463]

:::

::: {#o464 .entry cue=list-entry name="Bias blind spot"}

- [Bias blind spot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bias_blind_spot), the tendency to see oneself as less biased than other people, or to be able to identify more cognitive biases in others than in oneself.[^o465]

:::

::: {#o466 .entry cue=list-entry name="Illusion of asymmetric insight"}

- [Illusion of asymmetric insight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_asymmetric_insight), where people perceive their knowledge of their peers to surpass their peers' knowledge of them.[^o467]

:::

::: {#o468 .entry cue=list-entry name="Illusory superiority"}

- [Illusory superiority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusory_superiority), the tendency to overestimate one's desirable qualities, and underestimate undesirable qualities, relative to other people. (Also known as "Lake Wobegon effect", "better-than-average effect", or "superiority bias".)[^o469]

:::

::: {#o470 .entry cue=list-entry name="Impostor Syndrome"}

- [Impostor Syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impostor_Syndrome), a psychological occurrence in which an individual doubts their skills, talents, or accomplishments and has a persistent internalized fear of being exposed as a fraud. Also known as impostor phenomenon.[^o471]

:::

::: {#o472 .entry cue=list-entry name="Naïve realism"}

- [Naïve realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naïve_realism_%28psychology%29), the belief that we see reality as it really is—objectively and without bias; that the facts are plain for all to see; that rational people will agree with us; and that those who do not are either uninformed, lazy, irrational, or biased.

:::

::: {#o473 .entry cue=list-entry name="Third-person effect"}

- [Third-person effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third-person_effect), a tendency to believe that mass-communicated media messages have a greater effect on others than on themselves.

:::

::: {#o474 .entry cue=list-entry name="Trait ascription bias"}

- [Trait ascription bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trait_ascription_bias), the tendency for people to view themselves as relatively variable in terms of personality, behavior, and mood while viewing others as much more predictable.

:::

::: {#o475 .entry cue=list-entry name="Zero-sum bias"}

- [Zero-sum bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-sum_bias), where a situation is incorrectly perceived to be like a zero-sum game, in which any gain by one person necessarily comes at the expense of another.

:::
