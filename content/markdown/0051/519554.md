# Transcendental curve

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Transcendental curve"}

In [analytical geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_geometry), a **transcendental curve** is a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) that is not an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260).[^o2] Here for a curve, *C*, what matters is the point set (typically in the [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852)) underlying *C*, not a given parametrisation. For example, the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) is an algebraic curve (pedantically, the real points of such a curve); the usual parametrisation by [trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function) may involve those [transcendental functions](wikipedia://en/297021), but certainly the unit circle is defined by a polynomial equation. (The same remark applies to [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225) and [elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/69939); and in fact to curves of [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) > 1 and [automorphic functions](wikipedia://en/974148).)

:::

The properties of algebraic curves, such as [Bézout's theorem](wikipedia://en/149217), give rise to criteria for showing curves actually are transcendental. For example, an algebraic curve *C* either meets a given line *L* in a finite number of points, or possibly contains all of *L*. Thus a curve intersecting any line in an infinite number of points, while not containing it, must be transcendental. This applies not just to [sinusoidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinusoidal) curves, therefore; but to large classes of curves showing oscillations.

The term is originally attributed to [Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281).
