# Pisot–Vijayaraghavan number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pisot–Vijayaraghavan number"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Pisot–Vijayaraghavan number** (or **Pisot number** or **PV number**) is a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [algebraic integer](wikipedia://en/98981) greater than 1, all of whose [Galois conjugates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_conjugate) are less than 1 in [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991). These numbers were discovered by [Axel Thue](wikipedia://en/635533) in 1912 and rediscovered by [G. H. Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599) in 1919 within the context of [Diophantine approximation](wikipedia://en/251558). They became widely known after the publication of [Charles Pisot](wikipedia://en/10796294)'s dissertation in 1938. They also occur in the uniqueness problem for [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038). [Tirukkannapuram Vijayaraghavan](wikipedia://en/3985689) and [Raphael Salem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael_Salem) continued their study in the 1940s. [Salem numbers](wikipedia://en/1066694) are a closely related set of numbers.

:::

A characteristic property of PV numbers is that their powers [approach integers](wikipedia://en/14041933) at an exponential rate. Pisot [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) a remarkable [converse](wikipedia://en/155941): if *α* > 1 is a real number such that the [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838)

$$\|\alpha^n\|$$

measuring the distance from its consecutive powers to the nearest [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) is [square-summable](wikipedia://en/1701075), or *ℓ*<sup>2</sup>, then *α* is a Pisot number (and, in particular, algebraic). Building on this characterization of PV numbers, Salem showed that the set *S* of all PV numbers is [closed](wikipedia://en/47279). Its minimal element is a [cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_polynomial) irrationality known as the [plastic ratio](wikipedia://en/2148532). Much is known about the [accumulation points](wikipedia://en/226505) of *S*. The smallest of them is the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386).

## Definition and properties {#Definition_and_properties}

*Image: Pisot2 Newton map.svg.* [Newton's method](wikipedia://en/22145) for $p(z) = z4 - z3 - 1.$ The second Pisot number $\chi$ (right) and its conjugates at the nuclei of their [basins of attraction](wikipedia://en/191094#Basins_of_attraction) in the complex plane. [Julia set](wikipedia://en/56434) of the Newton map in orange, with unit circle and real curve for reference.

An **algebraic integer** of degree *n* is a root *α* of an [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725) [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730) *P*(*x*) of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) *n* with integer coefficients, its **[minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106)**. The other roots of *P*(*x*) are called the **[conjugates](wikipedia://en/458467)** of *α*. If *α* > 1 but all other roots of *P*(*x*) are real or [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) numbers of absolute value less than 1, so that they lie strictly inside the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), then *α* is called a **Pisot number**, **Pisot–Vijayaraghavan number**, or simply **PV number**. For example, the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386), *φ* ≈ 1.618, is a real [quadratic integer](wikipedia://en/21834785) that is greater than 1, while the absolute value of its conjugate, −*φ*<sup>−1</sup> ≈ −0.618, is less than 1. Therefore, *φ* is a Pisot number. Its minimal polynomial is *x*<sup>2</sup> − *x* − 1.

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Elementary properties"}

### Elementary properties {#Elementary_properties}

- Every integer greater than 1 is a PV number. Conversely, every [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) PV number is an integer greater than 1.
- If α is an [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689) PV number whose minimal polynomial ends in *k* then α is greater than |*k*|.
- If α is a PV number then so are its powers α<sup>*k*</sup>, for all positive integer exponents *k*.
- Every real [algebraic number field](wikipedia://en/28730822) **K** of degree *n* contains a PV number of degree *n*. This number is a field generator. The set of all PV numbers of degree *n* in **K** is closed under multiplication.
- Given an upper bound *M* and degree *n*, there are only [finitely many](wikipedia://en/11742) PV numbers of degree *n* that are less than *M*.
- Every PV number is a [Perron number](wikipedia://en/5362893) (a real algebraic number greater than one all of whose conjugates have smaller absolute value).

:::

### Diophantine properties {#Diophantine_properties}

The main interest in PV numbers is due to the fact that their powers have a very "biased" distribution (mod 1). If *α* is a PV number and *λ* is any algebraic integer in the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ then the sequence

$$\|\lambda\alpha^n\|,$$

where ||*x*|| denotes the distance from the real number *x* to the nearest integer, approaches 0 at an exponential rate. In particular, it is a square-summable sequence and its terms converge to 0.

Two converse statements are known: they characterize PV numbers among all real numbers and among the algebraic numbers (but under a weaker Diophantine assumption).

- Suppose *α* is a real number greater than 1 and *λ* is a non-zero real number such that

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty \|\lambda\alpha^n\|^2 < \infty.$$

Then *α* is a Pisot number and *λ* is an algebraic number in the field $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ (**Pisot's theorem**).

- Suppose *α* is an algebraic number greater than 1 and *λ* is a non-zero real number such that

$$\|\lambda\alpha^n\| \to 0, \quad n\to\infty.$$

Then *α* is a Pisot number and *λ* is an algebraic number in the field $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$.

A longstanding **Pisot–Vijayaraghavan problem** asks whether the assumption that *α* is algebraic can be dropped from the last statement. If the answer is affirmative, Pisot's numbers would be characterized *among all real numbers* by the simple convergence of ||*λα*<sup>*n*</sup>|| to 0 for some auxiliary real *λ*. It is known that there are only [countably many](wikipedia://en/6026) numbers *α* with this property.[^o7][^o8] The problem is to decide whether any of them is [transcendental](wikipedia://en/30325).

### Topological properties {#Topological_properties}

The set of all Pisot numbers is denoted *S*. Since Pisot numbers are algebraic, the set *S* is countable. Raphael Salem proved that this set is [closed](wikipedia://en/47279): it contains all its [limit points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point).[^o10] His proof uses a constructive version of the main diophantine property of Pisot numbers:[^o11] given a Pisot number *α*, a real number *λ* can be chosen so that 0 \< *λ* ≤ *α* and

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty \|\lambda\alpha^n\|^2 \leq 9.$$

Thus the *ℓ*<sup>2</sup> norm of the sequence ||*λα*<sup>*n*</sup>|| can be bounded by a uniform constant independent of *α*. In the last step of the proof, Pisot's characterization is invoked to conclude that the limit of a sequence of Pisot numbers is itself a Pisot number.

Closedness of *S* implies that it has a [minimal element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_element). [Carl Siegel](wikipedia://en/716602) proved that it is the positive root of the equation *x*<sup>3</sup> − *x* − 1 = 0 ([plastic constant](wikipedia://en/2148532)) and is isolated in *S*.[^o12] He constructed two sequences of Pisot numbers converging to the golden ratio *φ* from below and asked whether *φ* is the smallest limit point of *S*. This was later proved by Dufresnoy and Pisot, who also determined all elements of *S* that are less than *φ*; not all of them belong to Siegel's two sequences. Vijayaraghavan proved that *S* has infinitely many limit points; in fact, the sequence of [derived sets](wikipedia://en/1567335)

$$S, S', S'', \ldots$$

does not terminate. On the other hand, the intersection $S^{(\omega)}$ of these sets is [empty](wikipedia://en/9566), meaning that the [Cantor–Bendixson rank](wikipedia://en/1567335#Cantor–Bendixson_rank) of *S* is *ω*. Even more accurately, the [order type](wikipedia://en/4499021) of *S* has been determined:[^o13] Let $a_0 = 1$, and for all *n* ≥ 1, define the order type

$$a_{n + 1} = a_n \omega + 1 + (a_n \omega)^*,$$

where *ω* is the order type of the natural numbers, *a*\* means *a* in [reverse order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_order), and addition and multiplication are interpreted the same as for [ordinal numbers](wikipedia://en/26547932). Then *S* has order type

$$\mathrm{ord}(S) = \sum_{n = 0}^\infty a_n.$$

The set of [Salem numbers](wikipedia://en/1066694), denoted by *T*, is intimately related with *S*. It has been proved that *S* is contained in the set *T**of the limit points of*****T*.[^o14][^o15] It has been [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) that the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of*S*and*T*is closed.[^o16]***

:::

## Quadratic irrationals {#Quadratic_irrationals}

If $\alpha\,$ is a [quadratic irrational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_irrational) there is only one other conjugate, $\alpha'$, obtained by changing the sign of the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) in $\alpha$ from

$$\alpha = a + \sqrt D \text{ to } \alpha' = a - \sqrt D\,$$

or from

$$\alpha = \frac{a + \sqrt D}{2}\text{ to }\alpha' = \frac{a - \sqrt D}{2}.\,$$

Here *a* and *D* are integers and in the second case *a* is [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) and *D* is [congruent](wikipedia://en/20087) to 1 modulo 4.

The required conditions are *α* > 1 and −1 \< *α**\< 1.*** These are satisfied in the first case exactly when *a* > 0 and either $(a-1)^2 < D < a^2$ or $a^2 < D < (a+1)^2$, and are satisfied in the second case exactly when $a>0$ and either $(a-2)^2 < D < a^2$ or $a^2 < D < (a+2)^2$.

Thus, the first few quadratic irrationals that are PV numbers are:

## Powers of PV-numbers {#Powers_of_PV-numbers}

Pisot–Vijayaraghavan numbers can be used to generate [almost integers](wikipedia://en/14041933): the *n*th power of a Pisot number approaches integers as *n* grows. For example,

$$(3+\sqrt{10})^6=27379+8658\sqrt{10}=54757.9999817\dots \approx 54758-\frac{1}{54758}.$$

Since $27379 \,$ and $8658\sqrt{10} \,$ differ by only $0.0000182\dots,\,$

$$\frac{27379}{8658}=3.162277662\dots$$

is extremely close to

$$\sqrt{10}=3.162277660\dots .$$

Indeed

$$\left( \frac{27379}{8658}\right)^2=10+\frac{1}{8658^2}.$$

Higher powers give correspondingly better rational approximations.

This property stems from the fact that for each *n*, the sum of *n*th powers of an algebraic integer *x* and its conjugates is exactly an integer; this follows from an application of [Newton's identities](wikipedia://en/2468892). When *x* is a Pisot number, the *n*th powers of the other conjugates tend to 0 as *n* tends to infinity. Since the sum is an integer, the distance from *x<sup>n</sup>* to the nearest integer tends to 0 at an exponential rate.

## Small Pisot numbers {#Small_Pisot_numbers}

Since these PV numbers are less than 2, they are all units: their minimal polynomials end in 1 or −1. The polynomials in this table,[^o21] with the exception of

$$x^6 - 2x^5 + x^4 - x^2 + x - 1,$$

are factors of either

$$x^n(x^2 - x - 1) + 1$$

or

$$x^n(x^2 - x - 1) + (x^2 - 1).$$

The first polynomial is divisible by *x*<sup>2</sup> − 1 when *n* is odd and by *x* − 1 when *n* is [even](wikipedia://en/143135). It has one other real zero, which is a PV number. Dividing either polynomial by *x*<sup>*n*</sup> gives expressions that approach *x*<sup>2</sup> − *x* − 1 as *n* grows very large and have zeros that [converge](wikipedia://en/285773) to *φ*. A complementary pair of polynomials,

$$x^n(x^2 - x - 1) - 1$$

and

$$x^n(x^2-x-1) - (x^2-1)\,$$

yields Pisot numbers that approach φ from above.

Two-dimensional [turbulence](wikipedia://en/154664) modeling using [logarithmic spiral](wikipedia://en/42373) chains with [self-similarity](wikipedia://en/28782) defined by a constant scaling factor can be reproduced with some small Pisot numbers.[^o22]
