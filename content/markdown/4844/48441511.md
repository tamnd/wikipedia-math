::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Overlapping circles grid"}

An **overlapping circles grid** is a geometric pattern of [repeating](wikipedia://en/321671), overlapping [circles](wikipedia://en/6220) of an equal [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) in [two-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/70691232). Commonly, designs are based on circles centered on [triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangular_lattice) (with the simple, two circle form named *[vesica piscis](wikipedia://en/471830)*) or on the [square lattice](wikipedia://en/2874853) pattern of points.

:::

Patterns of seven overlapping circles appear in historical artefacts from the 7th century BCE onward; they become a frequently used ornament in the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) period, and survive into medieval artistic traditions both in [Islamic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_art) ([girih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girih) decorations) and in [Gothic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_art). The name **Flower of Life** is given to the overlapping circles pattern in [New Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Age) publications.

Of special interest is the [hexafoil](wikipedia://en/31636714) or six-petal [rosette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosette_%28design%29) derived from the **seven overlapping circles** pattern, also known as the *[Sun of the Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_of_the_Alps)* from its frequent use in alpine [folk art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_art) in the 17th and 18th century.

## Triangular grid of overlapping circles {#Triangular_grid_of_overlapping_circles}

*Image: Flower of life 6-levels.svg.* This pattern can be extended indefinitely, seen here with [hexagonal](wikipedia://en/59733) rings of [1, 7, 19, 37, 61, and 91](wikipedia://en/497485) circles

The [triangular lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangular_lattice) form, with circle [radii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radii) equal to their separation is called a **seven overlapping circles grid**.[^o4] It contains six circles intersecting at a point, with a seventh circle centered on that intersection.

Overlapping circles with similar geometrical constructions have been used infrequently in various of the [decorative arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decorative_arts) since ancient times. The pattern has found a wide range of usage in popular culture, in [fashion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fashion), [jewelry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewelry), [tattoos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tattoo) and decorative products.

### Cultural significance {#Cultural_significance}

#### Near East {#Near_East}

The oldest known occurrence of the overlapping circles pattern is dated to the 7th or 6th century [BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCE), found on the threshold of the palace of Assyrian king [Aššur-bāni-apli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aššur-bāni-apli) in [Dur Šarrukin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dur_Šarrukin) (now in the [Louvre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louvre)).[^o7]

The design became more widespread in the early centuries of the [Common Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era). One early example is a set of 5 patterns of 19 overlapping circles drawn on the granite columns at the [Temple of Osiris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Osiris_Temple) in [Abydos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abydos,_Egypt), Egypt, and a further five on a column opposite the building. They are drawn in red [ochre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ochre) and some are very faint and difficult to distinguish.[^o9] The patterns are [graffiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graffiti), and not found in natively Egyptian ornaments. They are mostly dated to the early centuries CE[^o10] although medieval or even modern (early 20th century) origin cannot be ruled out with certainty, as the drawings are not mentioned in the extensive listings of graffiti at the temple compiled by [Margaret Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Murray) in 1904.[^o11]

Similar patterns were sometimes used in England as [apotropaic marks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apotropaic_mark) to keep witches from entering buildings.[^o12] [Consecration crosses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consecration_crosses) indicating points in churches anointed with holy water during a church's dedication also take the form of overlapping circles.

In [Islamic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_art), the pattern is one of several arrangements of circles (others being used for fourfold or fivefold designs) used to construct grids for [Islamic geometric patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_geometric_patterns). It is used to design patterns with 6- and 12-pointed stars as well as [hexagons](wikipedia://en/59733) in the style called *[girih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girih)*. The resulting patterns however characteristically conceal the construction grid, presenting instead a [design of interlaced strapwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_ornament).[^o13]

#### Europe {#Europe}

Patterns of seven overlapping circles are found on [Roman mosaics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_mosaic), for example at [Herod's Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod's_Palace_%28Herodium%29) in the 1st century BCE.[^o15] The design is found on one of the silver plaques of the Late Roman hoard of [Kaiseraugst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiseraugst) (discovered 1961).[^o16] It is later found as an ornament in [Gothic architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_architecture), and still later in European [folk art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_art) of the early modern period. High medieval examples include the [Cosmati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmati) pavements in [Westminster Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Abbey) (13th century).[^o17] [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci) explicitly discussed the mathematical proportions of the design.[^o18]

#### Modern usage {#Modern_usage}

*Image: Flower of Life pendant (2).jpg.* 19-circle with arcs:27 silver [pendant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendant) (2013)

The name *Flower of Life* is modern, associated with the [New Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Age) movement, and commonly attributed specifically to Drunvalo Melchizedek in his book *The Ancient Secret of the Flower of Life* (1999).[^o21][^o22]

The pattern and modern name have propagated into wide range of usage in popular culture, in fashion, jewelry, tattoos, and decorative products. The pattern in [quilting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quilting) has been called the **diamond wedding ring** or *triangle wedding ring* to contrast it from the square pattern. Besides occasional use in fashion,[^o23] it is also used in the decorative arts. For example, the album *[Sempiternal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sempiternal_%28album%29)* (2013) by [Bring Me the Horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bring_Me_the_Horizon) uses the 61 overlapping circles grid as the main feature of its album cover,[^o24] and the album *[A Head Full of Dreams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Head_Full_of_Dreams)* (2015) by [Coldplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coldplay) features the 19 overlapping circles grid as the central part of its album cover. Teaser posters illustrating the cover art to *A Head Full of Dreams* were widely displayed on the [London Underground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Underground) in the last week of October 2015.[^o25]

The *Sun of the Alps* (Sole delle Alpi) symbol has been used as the emblem of [Padanian nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padanian_nationalism) in northern Italy since the 1990s.[^o26] It resembles a pattern often found in that area on buildings.[^o27]

A seven-circle Flower of Life is also used in the coat of arms of [Asgardia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asgardia) the space nation.

### Construction {#Construction}

Martha Bartfeld, author of geometric art tutorial books, described her independent discovery of the design in 1968. Her original definition said, "This design consists of circles having a 1-\[inch; 25 mm\] radius, with each point of intersection serving as a new center. The design can be expanded *[ad infinitum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ad_infinitum)* depending upon the number of times the odd-numbered points are marked off."

The figure can be drawn by [pen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pen) and [compass](wikipedia://en/492445), by creating multiple series of interlinking circles of the same diameter touching the previous circle's center. The second circle is centered at any point on the first circle. All following circles are centered on the intersection of two other circles.

*Image: Flower-construction.svg.*

#### Progressions {#Progressions}

The pattern can be extended outward in concentric hexagonal rings of circles, as in the following table. The first row shows rings of circles. The second row shows cubes of stacked spheres in [isometric projections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometric_projection). The third row shows the pattern completed with partial circle arcs within a set of completed circles.

Expanding sets have 1, 7, 19, 37, 61, 91, 127 circles, and continuing ever larger hexagonal rings of circles. The number of circles is $n^3-(n-1)^3=3n^2-3n+1=3n(n-1)+1$, where *n* is the number of rings, forming the [centered hexagonal numbers](wikipedia://en/497485).

These overlapping circles can also be seen as a [projection](wikipedia://en/313741) of an *n*-unit [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) of spheres in 3-dimensional space, viewed on the diagonal axis. There are more spheres than circles because some are overlapping in 2 dimensions.

### Other variations {#Other_variations}

Another [triangular lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangular_lattice) form is common, with circle separation as the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of 3 times their radius. Richard Kershner showed in 1939 that no [arrangement of circles can cover the plane](wikipedia://en/9451796) more efficiently than this hexagonal lattice arrangement.[^o36]

Two offset copies of this circle pattern makes a [rhombic tiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhombic_tiling) pattern, while three copies make the original triangular pattern.

### Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

The center [lens](wikipedia://en/2038396) of the two-circle figure is called a [vesica piscis](wikipedia://en/471830), from the mathematician [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331). Two circles are also called [Villarceau circles](wikipedia://en/1547360) as a plane intersection of a [torus](wikipedia://en/74800). The area inside one circle and outside the other is called a [lune](wikipedia://en/12935671).

The three-circle figure resembles the [Borromean rings](wikipedia://en/873118) and is also used in the [three-set](wikipedia://en/23476429) variant of [Venn diagrams](wikipedia://en/61701). The combined path of the intersections forms a [unicursal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicursal_curve) path called a [triquetra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triquetra). The center of the three-circle figure is called a [Reuleaux triangle](wikipedia://en/580252).

## Square grid of overlapping circles {#Square_grid_of_overlapping_circles}

| Square lattice form *Image: Square overlapping circle grid4.svg.* Each circle's radius is the reciprocal of the square root of twice the distance between their centers *Image: Double Wedding Ring Quilt.jpg.* A [quilt design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quilt#Block_designs) called a double wedding ring pattern |
|---|
| Centered square lattice form *Image: Square overlapping circle rotated grid4.svg.* It can be visualized as two half-offset square grids of [tangent circles](wikipedia://en/19349153) *Image: Wallpaper group-p4m-5.jpg.* [Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt) design, from Owen Jones's *[The Grammar of Ornament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Grammar_of_Ornament)* (1856) |

The [square lattice](wikipedia://en/2874853) form can be seen with circles that line up horizontally and vertically, while intersecting on their diagonals. The pattern appears slightly different when rotated on its diagonal, also called a *centered square lattice* form because it can be seen as two square lattices with each centered on the gaps of the other.

It is called a **Kawung motif** in Indonesian [batik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batik), and is found on the walls of the 8th-century Hindu temple [Prambanan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prambanan) in [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java).

It is called an **Apsamikkum** from ancient [Mesopotamian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamian) mathematics.[^o42]
