# Resolvent formalism

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Resolvent formalism"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **resolvent formalism** is a technique for applying concepts from [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759) to the study of the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137) of [operators](wikipedia://en/22213) on [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989) and more general spaces. Formal justification for the manipulations can be found in the framework of [holomorphic functional calculus](wikipedia://en/3131706).

:::

The **resolvent** captures the spectral properties of an operator in the analytic structure of the [functional](wikipedia://en/1374948). Given an operator $A$, the resolvent may be defined as

$$R(z;A)= (A-zI)^{-1}~.$$

Among other uses, the resolvent may be used to solve the inhomogeneous [Fredholm integral equations](wikipedia://en/363377); a commonly used approach is a series solution, the [Liouville–Neumann series](wikipedia://en/203807).

The resolvent of $A$ can be used to directly obtain information about the [spectral decomposition](wikipedia://en/123495) of $A$. For example, suppose $\lambda$ is an isolated [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) in the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137) of $A$. That is, suppose there exists a simple closed curve $C_\lambda$ in the complex plane that separates $\lambda$ from the rest of the spectrum of $A$. Then the [residue](wikipedia://en/73102)

$$-\frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_{C_\lambda} (A- z I)^{-1}~ dz$$

defines a [projection operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projection_operator) onto the $\lambda$ [eigenspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenspace) of $A$. The [Hille–Yosida theorem](wikipedia://en/1578212) relates the resolvent through a [Laplace transform](wikipedia://en/18610) to an integral over the [one-parameter group](wikipedia://en/754851) of transformations generated by $A$.[^o2] Thus, for example, if $A$ is a [skew-Hermitian matrix](wikipedia://en/509788), then $U(t) = \exp(tA)$ is a one-parameter group of unitary operators. Whenever $|z|>\|A\|$, the resolvent of *A* at *z* can be expressed as the [Laplace transform](wikipedia://en/18610)

$$R(z;A)= \int_0^\infty e^{-zt}U(t)~dt,$$

where the integral is taken along the ray $\arg t=-\arg\lambda$.[^o3]

## History {#History}

The first major use of the resolvent operator as a series in $A$ (cf. [Liouville–Neumann series](wikipedia://en/203807)) was by [Ivar Fredholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivar_Fredholm), in a landmark 1903 paper in *Acta Mathematica* that helped establish modern [operator theory](wikipedia://en/712675).

The name *resolvent* was given by [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302).

::: {#o6 .statement cue=named-heading name="Resolvent identity"}

## Resolvent identity {#Resolvent_identity}

For all $z, w$ in $\rho(A)$, the [resolvent set](wikipedia://en/8721698) of an operator $A$, we have that the **first resolvent identity** (also called Hilbert's identity) holds:[^o7]

$$R(z; A) - R(w; A) = (z - w) R(z;A) R(w;A)\, .$$

(Note that [Dunford and Schwartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunford_and_Schwartz), cited, define the resolvent as $(zI -A)^{-1}$, instead, so that the formula above differs in sign from theirs.)

The **second resolvent identity** is a generalization of the first resolvent identity, above, useful for comparing the resolvents of two distinct operators. Given operators $A$ and $B$, both defined on the same linear space, and $z$ in $\rho(A) \cap \rho(B)$ the following identity holds,[^o8]

$$R(z;A) - R(z;B) = R(z;A)(B-A) R(z;B) \, .$$

A one-line proof goes as follows:

$$(A-zI)^{-1}-(B-zI)^{-1}=(A-zI)^{-1}((B-zI)-(A-zI))(B-zI)^{-1}=(A-zI)^{-1}(B-A)(B-zI)^{-1} \, .$$

:::

::: {#o10 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Compact resolvent"}

## Compact resolvent {#Compact_resolvent}

When studying a closed [unbounded operator](wikipedia://en/1422584) $A$: $H$ → $H$ on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) $H$, if there exists $z\in\rho(A)$ such that $R(z;A)$ is a [compact operator](wikipedia://en/577441), we say that $A$ has compact resolvent. The spectrum $\sigma(A)$ of such $A$ is a discrete subset of $\mathbb{C}$. If furthermore $A$ is [self-adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-adjoint), then $\sigma(A)\subset\mathbb{R}$ and there exists an orthonormal basis $\{v_i\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}$ of eigenvectors of $A$ with eigenvalues $\{\lambda_i\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}$ respectively. Also, $\{\lambda_i\}$ has no finite [accumulation point](wikipedia://en/226505).[^o11]
