# Carleman's inequality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Carleman's inequality"}

**Carleman's inequality** is an [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489) in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), named after [Torsten Carleman](wikipedia://en/6063370), who proved it in 1923[^o2] and used it to prove the Denjoy–Carleman theorem on [quasi-analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-analytic) classes.[^o3][^o4]

:::

::: {#o6 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Let $a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots$ be a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of [non-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative) [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), then

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty \left(a_1 a_2 \cdots a_n\right)^{1/n} \le \mathrm{e} \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n.$$

The constant [$\mathrm{e}$](wikipedia://en/9633) (euler number) in the inequality is optimal, that is, the inequality does not always hold if $\mathrm{e}$ is replaced by a smaller number. The inequality is strict (it holds with "\<" instead of "≤") if some element in the sequence is non-zero.

:::

## Integral version {#Integral_version}

Carleman's inequality has an integral version, which states that

$$\int_0^\infty \exp\left\{ \frac{1}{x} \int_0^x \ln f(t) \,\mathrm{d}t \right\} \,\mathrm{d}x \leq \mathrm{e} \int_0^\infty f(x) \,\mathrm{d}x$$

for any *f* ≥ 0.

::: {#o9 .statement cue=named-heading name="Carleson's inequality"}

## Carleson's inequality {#Carleson's_inequality}

A generalisation, due to [Lennart Carleson](wikipedia://en/1669541), states the following:[^o10]

for any [convex function](wikipedia://en/245568) *g* with *g*(0) = 0, and for any -1 \< *p* \< ∞,

$$\int_0^\infty x^p \mathrm{e}^{-g(x)/x} \,\mathrm{d}x \leq \mathrm{e}^{p+1} \int_0^\infty x^p \mathrm{e}^{-g'(x)} \,\mathrm{d}x.$$

Carleman's inequality follows from the case *p* = 0.

:::

::: {#o12 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

### Direct proof {#Direct_proof}

An [elementary proof](wikipedia://en/9318685) is sketched below. From the [inequality of arithmetic and geometric means](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inequality_of_arithmetic_and_geometric_means) applied to the numbers $1\cdot a_1,2\cdot a_2,\dots,n \cdot a_n$

$$\mathrm{MG}(a_1,\dots,a_n)=\mathrm{MG}(1a_1,2a_2,\dots,na_n)(n!)^{-1/n}\le \mathrm{MA}(1a_1,2a_2,\dots,na_n)(n!)^{-1/n}$$

where MG stands for geometric mean, and MA — for arithmetic mean. The [Stirling-type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirling_formula) inequality $n!\ge \sqrt{2\pi n}\, n^n \mathrm{e}^{-n}$ applied to $n+1$ implies

$(n!)^{-1/n} \le \frac{\mathrm{e}}{n+1}$ for all $n\ge1.$

Therefore,

$$MG(a_1,\dots,a_n) \le \frac{\mathrm{e}}{n(n+1)}\, \sum_{1\le k \le n} k a_k \, ,$$

whence

$$\sum_{n\ge1}MG(a_1,\dots,a_n) \le\, \mathrm{e}\, \sum_{k\ge1} \bigg( \sum_{n\ge k} \frac{1}{n(n+1)}\bigg) \, k a_k =\, \mathrm{e}\, \sum_{k\ge1}\, a_k \, ,$$

proving the inequality. Moreover, the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means of $n$ non-negative numbers is known to be an equality [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) all the numbers coincide, that is, in the present case, if and only if $a_k= C/k$ for $k=1,\dots,n$. As a consequence, Carleman's inequality is never an equality for a convergent series, unless all $a_n$ vanish, just because the [harmonic series](wikipedia://en/142488) is divergent.

::: {#o15 .statement cue=named-heading name="By Hardy’s inequality"}

### By Hardy’s inequality {#By_Hardy’s_inequality}

One can also prove Carleman's inequality by starting with [Hardy's inequality](wikipedia://en/7015763)[^o16]

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty \left (\frac{a_1+a_2+\cdots +a_n}{n}\right )^p\le \left (\frac{p}{p-1}\right )^p\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n^p$$

for the non-negative numbers $a_1$, $a_2$,… and $p > 1$, replacing each $a_n$ with $a_n^{1/p}$, and letting $p \to \infty$.

:::

:::

## Versions for specific sequences {#Versions_for_specific_sequences}

Christian Axler and Mehdi Hassani investigated Carleman's inequality for the specific cases of $a_i= p_i$ where $p_i$ is the $i$th [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666). They also investigated the case where $a_i=\frac{1}{p_i}$.[^o18] They found that if $a_i=p_i$ one can replace $e$ with $\frac{1}{e}$ in Carleman's inequality, but that if $a_i=\frac{1}{p_i}$ then $e$ remained the best possible constant.
