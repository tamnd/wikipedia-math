# Boltzmann's entropy formula

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Boltzmann's entropy formula"}

*Image: Boltzmann equation.JPG.* **Boltzmann's entropy formula**—carved on his gravestone.[^o2]

In [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481), **Boltzmann's entropy formula** (also known as the **Boltzmann–Planck equation**, not to be confused with the more general [Boltzmann equation](wikipedia://en/1026522), which is a [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564)) is a probability equation relating the [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) $S$, also written as $S_\mathrm{B}$, of an [ideal gas](wikipedia://en/65905) to the [multiplicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicity_%28statistical_mechanics%29) (commonly denoted as $\Omega$ or $W$), the number of real [microstates](wikipedia://en/3066350) corresponding to the gas's [macrostate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macrostate):

:::

::: {#o3 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$S = k_\mathrm{B} \ln W \tag{1}$$

:::

where $k_\mathrm B$ is the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant) (also written as simply $k$) and equal to 1.380649 × 10<sup>−23</sup> J/K, and $\ln$ is the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476) function (or [log](wikipedia://en/17860) base [e](wikipedia://en/9633), as in the image above).

In short, the [Boltzmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann) formula shows the relationship between entropy and the number of ways the [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom) or [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule) of a certain kind of [thermodynamic system](wikipedia://en/425850) can be arranged. What is important to note is that W is not all possible states of the system, but ways the system can be arranged and still have the same properties from perspective of external observer. So for example when system contains 5 particles of gas and given amount of energy distributed between them for example \[1,1,2,3,4\]. Energy distribution can be realized as \[1,2,1,3,4\] where index represent a particle, but the distribution can also be realized as \[2,1,1,3,4\] after swapping first two and so forth. W is measure of all possible way the distribution can be realized. When W is small for given distribution that distribution has small entropy, when W is large for given distribution it has a large entropy.

## History {#History}

*Image: Zentralfriedhof Vienna - Boltzmann.JPG.* Boltzmann's grave in the [Zentralfriedhof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zentralfriedhof), Vienna, with bust and entropy formula.

The equation was originally formulated by [Ludwig Boltzmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Boltzmann) between 1872 and 1875, but later put into its current form by [Max Planck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck) in about 1900.[^o5][^o6] To quote Planck, "the [logarithmic](wikipedia://en/17860) connection between [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) and [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) was first stated by L. Boltzmann in his [kinetic theory](wikipedia://en/64204) of gases".[^o7]

A 'microstate' is a state specified in terms of the constituent particles of a body of matter or radiation that has been specified as a macrostate in terms of such variables as [internal energy](wikipedia://en/340757) and pressure. A macrostate is experimentally observable, with at least a finite extent in [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758). A microstate can be instantaneous, or can be a trajectory composed of a temporal progression of instantaneous microstates. In experimental practice, such are scarcely observable. The present account concerns instantaneous microstates.

The value of $W$ was originally intended to be proportional to the *Wahrscheinlichkeit* (the German word for probability) of a [macroscopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroscopic) state for some [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of possible [microstates](wikipedia://en/3066350)—the collection of (unobservable microscopic single particle) "ways" in which the (observable macroscopic) [thermodynamic](wikipedia://en/29952) state of a system can be realized by assigning different [positions](wikipedia://en/81931) and [momenta](wikipedia://en/20431) to the respective molecules.

There are many instantaneous microstates that apply to a given macrostate. Boltzmann considered collections of such microstates. For a given macrostate, he called the collection of all possible instantaneous microstates of a certain kind by the name *monode*, for which Gibbs' term *ensemble* is used nowadays. For single particle instantaneous microstates, Boltzmann called the collection an *ergode*. Subsequently, Gibbs called it a *microcanonical ensemble*, and this name is widely used today, perhaps partly because Bohr was more interested in the writings of Gibbs than of Boltzmann.[^o8]

Interpreted in this way, Boltzmann's formula is the most basic formula for the thermodynamic [entropy](wikipedia://en/4701125). Boltzmann's [paradigm](wikipedia://en/175308) was an [ideal gas](wikipedia://en/65905) of $N$ *identical* particles, of which $N_{i}$ are in the $i$-th microscopic condition (range) of position and momentum. For this case, the probability of each microstate of the system is equal, so it was equivalent for Boltzmann to calculate the number of microstates associated with a macrostate. $W$ was historically misinterpreted as literally meaning the number of microstates, and that is what it usually means today. $W$ can be counted using the formula for [permutations](wikipedia://en/170167#Derivation_of_the_Maxwell–Boltzmann_distribution)

::: {#o9 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$W = \frac{N!}{\prod_i N_i!} \tag{2}$$

:::

where $i$ ranges over all possible molecular conditions and "$!$" denotes [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606). The "correction" in the denominator is due to the fact that identical particles in the same condition are [indistinguishable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identical_particles). $W$ is sometimes called the "thermodynamic probability" since it is an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) greater than one, while [mathematical probabilities](wikipedia://en/23542) are always [numbers](wikipedia://en/21690) between zero and one.

## Introduction of the natural logarithm {#Introduction_of_the_natural_logarithm}

In Boltzmann’s 1877 paper, he clarifies molecular state counting to determine the state distribution number introducing the logarithm to simplify the equation.

Boltzmann writes: “The first task is to determine the permutation number, previously designated by 𝒫 , for any state distribution. Denoting by J the sum of the permutations 𝒫 for all possible state distributions, the quotient 𝒫 /J is the state distribution’s probability, henceforth denoted by W. We would first like to calculate the permutations 𝒫 for the state distribution characterized by w<sub>0</sub> molecules with [kinetic energy](wikipedia://en/17327) 0, w<sub>1</sub> molecules with kinetic energy ϵ, etc. …

“The most likely state distribution will be for those w<sub>0</sub>, w<sub>1</sub> … values for which 𝒫 is a maximum or since the numerator is a constant, for which the denominator is a minimum. The values w<sub>0</sub>, w<sub>1</sub> must simultaneously satisfy the two constraints (1) and (2). Since the denominator of 𝒫 is a product, it is easiest to determine the minimum of its logarithm, …”

Therefore, by making the denominator small, he maximizes the number of states. So to simplify the product of the factorials, he uses their natural logarithm to add them. This is the reason for the natural logarithm in Boltzmann’s entropy formula.[^o11]

## Generalization {#Generalization}

Boltzmann's formula applies to microstates of a system, each possible microstate of which is presumed to be equally probable.

But in thermodynamics, the universe is divided into a [system](wikipedia://en/425850) of interest, plus its surroundings; then the entropy of Boltzmann's microscopically specified system can be identified with the system entropy in classical thermodynamics. The microstates of such a thermodynamic system are *not* equally probable—for example, high energy microstates are less probable than low energy microstates for a thermodynamic system kept at a fixed temperature by allowing contact with a heat bath. For thermodynamic systems where microstates of the system may not have equal probabilities, the appropriate generalization, called the [Gibbs entropy formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs_entropy_formula), is:

::: {#o14 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$S_{\mathrm G} = - k_{\mathrm B} \sum p_i \ln p_i \tag{3}$$

:::

This reduces to equation ((1)) if the probabilities *p*<sub>i</sub> are all equal.

Boltzmann used a $\rho\ln\rho$ formula as early as 1866.[^o15] He interpreted $\rho$ as a density in phase space—without mentioning probability—but since this satisfies the axiomatic definition of a probability measure we can retrospectively interpret it as a probability anyway. [Gibbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Willard_Gibbs) gave an explicitly probabilistic interpretation in 1878.

Boltzmann himself used an expression equivalent to ((3)) in his later work[^o16] and recognized it as more general than equation ((1)). That is, equation ((1)) is a corollary of equation ((3))—and not vice versa. In every situation where equation ((1)) is valid, equation ((3)) is valid also—and not vice versa.

:::

## Boltzmann entropy excludes statistical dependencies {#Boltzmann_entropy_excludes_statistical_dependencies}

The term **Boltzmann entropy** is also sometimes used to indicate entropies calculated based on the approximation that the overall probability can be factored into an identical separate term for each particle—i.e., assuming each particle has an identical independent probability distribution, and ignoring interactions and correlations between the particles. This is exact for an ideal gas of identical particles that move independently apart from instantaneous collisions, and is an approximation, possibly a poor one, for other systems.[^o18]

The Boltzmann entropy is obtained if one assumes one can treat all the component particles of a [thermodynamic system](wikipedia://en/425850) as statistically independent. The probability distribution of the system as a whole then factorises into the product of *N* separate identical terms, one term for each particle; and when the summation is taken over each possible state in the 6-dimensional [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) of a *single* particle (rather than the 6*N*-dimensional phase space of the system as a whole), the Gibbs entropy formula

::: {#o19 .equation cue=numblk name="4"}

$$S_\mathrm{G} = - N k_\mathrm{B} \sum_i p_i \ln p_i \tag{4}$$

:::

simplifies to the Boltzmann entropy $S_{\mathrm B}$.

This reflects the original statistical entropy function introduced by Ludwig Boltzmann in 1872. For the special case of an [ideal gas](wikipedia://en/65905) it exactly corresponds to the proper [thermodynamic entropy](wikipedia://en/9891).

For anything but the most dilute of real gases, $S_{\mathrm B}$ leads to increasingly wrong predictions of entropies and physical behaviours, by ignoring the interactions and correlations between different molecules. Instead one must consider the [ensemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_ensemble) of states of the system as a whole, called by Boltzmann a *holode*, rather than single particle states.[^o20] Gibbs considered several such kinds of ensembles; relevant here is the *canonical* one.[^o18]
