# Baranyai's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Baranyai's theorem"}

*Image: Complete-edge-coloring.svg.* A partition of a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) on 8 vertices into 7 colors ([perfect matchings](wikipedia://en/44031)), the case *r* = 2 of Baranyai's theorem

In [combinatorial](wikipedia://en/5170) mathematics, **Baranyai's theorem** (proved by and named after [Zsolt Baranyai](wikipedia://en/23926237)) deals with the [decompositions](wikipedia://en/325802#Independence) of complete [hypergraphs](wikipedia://en/232535).

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement of the theorem"}

## Statement of the theorem {#Statement_of_the_theorem}

The statement of the result is that if $2\le r<k$ are integers and *r* divides *k*, then the complete [hypergraph](wikipedia://en/232535) $K^k_r$ decomposes into [1-factors](wikipedia://en/3298854). $K^k_r$ is a hypergraph with *k* vertices, in which every subset of *r* vertices forms a hyperedge; a 1-factor of this hypergraph is a set of hyperedges that touches each vertex exactly once, or equivalently a [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) of the vertices into subsets of size *r*. Thus, the theorem states that the *k* vertices of the hypergraph may be partitioned into subsets of *r* vertices in $\binom{k}{r}\frac{r}{k} = \binom{k-1}{r-1}$ different ways, in such a way that each *r*-element subset appears in exactly one of the partitions.

:::

## The case $r = 2$ {#The_case}

In the special case $r = 2$, we have a complete graph $K_n$ on $n$ vertices, and we wish to color the edges with $\binom{n}{2}\frac{2}{n} = n-1$ colors so that the edges of each color form a perfect matching. Baranyai's theorem says that we can do this whenever $n$ is even.

## History {#History}

The *r* = 2 case can be rephrased as stating that every [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) with an even number of vertices has an [edge coloring](wikipedia://en/690647) whose number of colors equals its [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448), or equivalently that its edges may be partitioned into [perfect matchings](wikipedia://en/44031). It may be used to schedule [round-robin tournaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round-robin_tournament), and its solution was already known in the 19th century. The case that *k* = 2*r* is also easy.

The *r* = 3 case was established by R. Peltesohn in 1936. The general case was proved by [Zsolt Baranyai](wikipedia://en/23926237) in 1975.
