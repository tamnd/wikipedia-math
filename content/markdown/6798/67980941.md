# Coordination sequence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Coordination sequence"}

In [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography) and [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **coordination sequence** of a [vertex](wikipedia://en/638899) $v$ is an [integer sequence](wikipedia://en/51421) that counts how many vertices are at each possible [distance](wikipedia://en/1020021) from $v$. That is, it is a sequence

$$n_0, n_1, n_2,\dots$$

where each $n_i$ is the number of vertices that are $i$ steps away from $v$. If the graph is [vertex-transitive](wikipedia://en/581175), then the sequence is an [invariant of the graph](wikipedia://en/1655142) that does not depend on the specific choice of $v$. Coordination sequences can also be defined for [sphere packings](wikipedia://en/368621), by using either the [contact graph](wikipedia://en/51513238) of the spheres or the [Delaunay triangulation](wikipedia://en/8864) of their centers, but these two choices may give rise to different sequences.

:::

*Image: Distance countours in a square grid.svg.* A square grid, shaded by distance from the central blue point. The number of grid points at distance exactly $i$ (for any positive $i$) is $4i$, so the coordination sequence of the grid is the sequence of multiples of four, modified to start with one instead of zero.

As an example, in a [square grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_grid), for each positive integer $i$, there are $4i$ grid points that are $i$ steps away from the origin. Therefore, the coordination sequence of the square grid is the sequence

$$1,4,8,12,16,20,\dots\ .$$

in which, except for the initial value of one, each number is a multiple of four.

The concept was proposed by Georg O. Brunner and [Fritz Laves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Laves) and later developed by [Michael O'Keefe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_O'Keeffe_%28chemist%29). The coordination sequences of many low-dimensional lattices and [uniform tilings](wikipedia://en/12126635) are known.

The coordination sequences of periodic structures are known to be [quasi-polynomial](wikipedia://en/10609701).
