# The Equidistribution of Lattice Shapes of Rings of Integers of Cubic, Quartic, and Quintic Number Fields

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="The Equidistribution of Lattice Shapes of Rings of Integers of Cubic, Quartic, and Quintic Number Fields"}

***The Equidistribution of Lattice Shapes of Rings of Integers of Cubic, Quartic, and Quintic Number Fields: An Artist's Rendering*** is a mathematics book by Piper Harron (also known as Piper H and Piper Harris), based on her [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) [doctoral thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctoral_thesis) of the same title. It has been described as "feminist",[^o7] "unique",[^o4] "honest",[^o4] "generous",[^o9] and "refreshing".[^o8]

:::

## Thesis and reception {#Thesis_and_reception}

Harron was advised by [Fields Medalist](wikipedia://en/10859) [Manjul Bhargava](wikipedia://en/5102088), and her thesis deals with the properties of [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_fields), specifically the shape of their [rings of integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rings_of_integers).[^o4][^o5] Harron and Bhargava showed that, viewed as a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) in [real vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_vector_space), the ring of integers of a random number field does not have any special symmetries.[^o5][^o6] Rather than simply presenting the [proof](wikipedia://en/82285), Harron intended for the thesis and book to explain both the mathematics and the process (and struggle) that was required to reach this result.[^o5]

The writing is accessible and informal, and the book features sections targeting three different audiences: laypeople, people with general mathematical knowledge, and experts in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527).[^o7] Harron intentionally departs from the typical academic format as she is writing for a community of mathematicians who "do not feel that they are encouraged to be themselves".[^o7] Unusually for a mathematics thesis, Harron intersperses her rigorous analysis and proofs with cartoons, poetry, pop-culture references, and humorous diagrams.[^o4] Science writer Evelyn Lamb, in *[Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American)*, expresses admiration for Harron for explaining the process behind the mathematics in a way that is accessible to non-mathematicians, especially "because as a woman of color, she could pay a higher price for doing it."[^o8] Mathematician Philip Ording calls her approach to communicating [mathematical abstractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_abstraction) "generous".[^o9]

Her thesis [went viral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viral_phenomenon) in late 2015, especially within the mathematical community, in part because of the prologue which begins by stating that "respected research math is dominated by men of a certain attitude".[^o4][^o8] Harron had left academia for several years, later saying that she found the atmosphere oppressive and herself miserable and verging on failure.[^o10] She returned determined that, even if she did not do math the "right way", she "could still contribute to the community".[^o10] Her prologue states that the community lacks diversity and discourages diversity of thought.[^o8] "It is not my place to make the system comfortable with itself", she concludes.[^o8]

A concise proof was published in *[Compositio Mathematica](wikipedia://en/31882322)* in 2016.[^o11]
