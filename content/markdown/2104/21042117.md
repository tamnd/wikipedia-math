# Brooks' theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Brooks' theorem"}

*Image: Graph exact coloring.svg.* [Complete graphs](wikipedia://en/85816) need one more color than their maximum degree. They and the odd cycles are the only exceptions to Brooks' theorem.

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), **Brooks' theorem** states a relationship between the maximum [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) of a graph and its [chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number). According to the theorem, in a connected graph in which every vertex has at most Δ neighbors, the vertices can be [colored](wikipedia://en/426743) with only Δ colors, except for two cases, [complete graphs](wikipedia://en/85816) and [cycle graphs](wikipedia://en/878962) of odd length, which require Δ + 1 colors.

:::

The theorem is named after [R. Leonard Brooks](wikipedia://en/28209555), who published a proof of it in 1941.[^n1] A coloring with the number of colors described by Brooks' theorem is sometimes called a *Brooks coloring*[^n2] or a Δ-*coloring*.[^n3]

::: {#o6 .statement cue=heading name="Formal statement"}

## Formal statement {#Formal_statement}

For any [connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_graph) [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) *G* with maximum [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) Δ, the [chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number) of *G* is at most Δ, unless *G* is a complete graph or an odd cycle, in which case the chromatic number is Δ + 1.[^n1]

:::

::: {#o9 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

[László Lovász](wikipedia://en/1623938) gives a simplified proof of Brooks' theorem. If the graph is not [biconnected](wikipedia://en/4989397), its biconnected components may be colored separately and then the colorings combined. If the graph has a vertex *v* with degree less than Δ, then a [greedy coloring](wikipedia://en/21051195) algorithm that colors vertices farther from *v* before closer ones uses at most Δ colors. This is because at the time that each vertex other than *v* is colored, at least one of its neighbors (the one on a shortest path to *v*) is uncolored, so it has fewer than Δ colored neighbors and has a free color. When the algorithm reaches *v*, its small number of neighbors allows it to be colored. Therefore, the most difficult case of the proof concerns biconnected Δ-[regular](wikipedia://en/85821) graphs with Δ ≥ 3. In this case, Lovász shows that one can find a [spanning tree](wikipedia://en/455770) such that two nonadjacent neighbors *u* and *w* of the root *v* are leaves in the tree. A greedy coloring starting from *u* and *w* and processing the remaining vertices of the spanning tree in bottom-up order, ending at *v*, uses at most Δ colors. For, when every vertex other than *v* is colored, it has an uncolored parent, so its already-colored neighbors cannot use up all the free colors, while at *v* the two neighbors *u* and *w* have equal colors so again a free color remains for *v* itself.[^n4]

:::

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Extensions"}

## Extensions {#Extensions}

A more general version of the theorem applies to [list coloring](wikipedia://en/690669): given any connected undirected graph with maximum degree Δ that is neither a [clique](wikipedia://en/524466) nor an odd cycle, and a list of Δ colors for each vertex, it is possible to choose a color for each vertex from its list so that no two adjacent vertices have the same color. In other words, the list chromatic number of a connected undirected graph G never exceeds Δ, unless G is a clique or an odd cycle.[^n5]

For certain graphs, even fewer than Δ colors may be needed. Δ − 1 colors suffice if and only if the given graph has no Δ-clique, *provided* Δ is large enough.[^n6] For [triangle-free graphs](wikipedia://en/7667996), or more generally graphs in which the [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28graph_theory%29) of every vertex is sufficiently [sparse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparse_graph), O(Δ/log Δ) colors suffice.[^o15]

The degree of a graph also appears in upper bounds for other types of coloring; for [edge coloring](wikipedia://en/690647), the result that the chromatic index is at most Δ + 1 is [Vizing's theorem](wikipedia://en/5449464). An extension of Brooks' theorem to [total coloring](wikipedia://en/690702), stating that the total chromatic number is at most Δ + 2, has been conjectured by [Mehdi Behzad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehdi_Behzad) and Vizing. The Hajnal–Szemerédi theorem on [equitable coloring](wikipedia://en/21796230) states that any graph has a (Δ + 1)-coloring in which the sizes of any two color classes differ by at most one.

:::

::: {#o17 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Algorithms"}

## Algorithms {#Algorithms}

A Δ-coloring, or even a Δ-list-coloring, of a degree-Δ graph may be found in linear time.[^o18] Efficient algorithms are also known for finding Brooks colorings in parallel and distributed models of computation.[^o19]

:::
