# Thomas Blundeville

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Thomas Blundeville"}

**Thomas Blundeville** (c. 1522 – c. 1606) was an English [humanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanism) writer and mathematician. He is known for work on [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), education and [horsemanship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horsemanship), as well as for translations from the Italian. His interests were both wide-ranging and directed towards practical ends, and he adapted freely a number of the works he translated.

:::

He was a pioneer writer in English in several areas, and inventor of a standard classroom geometrical instrument, the [protractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protractor).

## Life {#Life}

He lived as a country gentleman on his estate at [Newton Flotman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton_Flotman), in [Norfolk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norfolk). He inherited from his father Edward Blundeville in 1568, having possibly studied at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge).[^o3]

He had connections with court circles, and London scientific intellectuals. He was an associate of [Henry Briggs](wikipedia://en/210399), at [Gresham College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gresham_College), and enjoyed the patronage of [Robert Dudley, 1st Earl of Leicester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Dudley,_1st_Earl_of_Leicester), among other aristocrats.[^o4]

Other indications on his life are more tenuous. It has been plausibly suggested that as "T. B." he added a prefatory poem to [John Studley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Studley)'s *Agamemnon*;[^o5] he was certainly alluded to by [Jasper Heywood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jasper_Heywood), in the preface to his *Thyestes* of 1560, as a translator of Plutarch.[^o3] From this it is argued that he had connection to the [Inns of Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inns_of_Court). He may have travelled to Italy, an inference from his familiarity with Italian literature. He was a mathematics tutor, to households including that of [Nicholas Bacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Bacon_%28courtier%29) and [Francis Wyndham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Wyndham_%28judge%29); and Cecil may have recommended Blundeville to Leicester.[^o6][^o7] [W. W. Rouse Ball](wikipedia://en/308290) gives a date of death of 1595, and possible connections to mathematicians: "Thomas Blundeville was resident at Cambridge about the same time as [Dee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dee) and [Digges](wikipedia://en/628083)—possibly he was a non-collegiate student, and if so must have been one of the last of them."[^o8]

He married twice, and his male heir Andrew was killed in the Flemish wars.[^o3] His daughter Elizabeth married Rowland Meyrick, son of [Sir Gelli Meyrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Gelli_Meyrick) who was steward to [Robert Devereux, 2nd Earl of Essex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Devereux,_2nd_Earl_of_Essex) and caught up in his fall.[^o9]

## Writings {#Writings}

### Early works {#Early_works}

He made a partial verse translation of the *Moralia* of [Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch), to which [Roger Ascham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Ascham) added verses.[^o12] It appeared as *Three Moral Treatises* in 1561, his first work, to mark the accession of [Elizabeth I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_I), to whom one of the pieces was dedicated. Another was dedicated to the courtiers [John Harington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Harington_%28treasurer%29) and [John Astley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Astley_%28courtier%29).[^o13]

His book on horsemanship, *The arte of ryding and breakinge greate horses*, was published about 1560 and is the first work on [equitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equitation) published in English. It is an abridged and adapted translation, made at the suggestion of John Astley, of *Gli ordini di cavalcare* by [Federico Grisone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federico_Grisone), and is directed towards the use of horses in war.[^o14][^o15] He followed it with *The fower chiefyst offices belonging to Horsemanshippe* (1565–6), which included a revised translation of Grisone together with other treatises.[^o16] It was praised as "Xenophontean" by [Gabriel Harvey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Harvey).[^o17]

### Historiography {#Historiography}

His expressed views on history are considered standard for Elizabethan England.[^o19] The approach is causal, invoking the "meanes and instrumentes" of history, and mechanism in politics.[^o20] Influentially, he compromised between "linear" (traditional Christian medieval) and "cyclic" (classical) overall views of the working-out of history, for a "spiral" model. For him, the providential is not incompatible with the moral order as it asserts itself in the details of exemplary political history, and he has been compared to [Edmund Bolton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Bolton) in combining the medieval and humanist traditions.[^o21][^o22] He commented that to be a good historiographer is a prerequisite for a counsellor, in his 1570 book on [counsel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counsel).[^o23]

His *True Order and Methode* was dedicated to the Earl of Leicester and was a loose translation and summary of historiographical works by [Jacopo Aconcio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacopo_Aconcio) and [Francesco Patrizzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Patrizzi). It endorsed the realist writing of history as process, and was one of the few English contributions of the period to the *[artes historicae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artes_historicae)*.[^o24][^o25] He translated also a manuscript of Aconcio on [fortification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortification), for the [Earl of Bedford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Russell,_2nd_Earl_of_Bedford).[^o26]

### Logic {#Logic}

His *Arte of Logike* (written 1575, published 1599) is somewhat [Ramist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramist) in approach, but strongly so in discussing method.[^o28] Besides [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308), it also shows the influence of [Galen](wikipedia://en/12326), [Melanchthon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melanchthon), [Cornelius Valerius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelius_Valerius) the *De Methodo* of Aconcio of 1558, and [Thomas Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Wilson_%28rhetorician%29).[^o29][^o30]

It contains a section on [fallacies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallacies). Under *[petitio principii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petitio_principii)*, it uses an even-handed example of [Aristotelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelianism) and [Copernican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernican_principle) arguments on the motion of the Earth.[^o31]

### Scientific, mathematical and geographical {#Scientific,_mathematical_and_geographical}

These later works are directed towards geography, [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation) and travel, geography in Blundeville's view being a necessary support to history; their content is very mixed.[^o33]

*Image: Blundeville's Exercises on Cosmophraphy, Astronomy, etc. 1613 Edition.jpg.* Blundeville's Exercises containing eight treatises including Cosmography, Astronomy, Geography and Navigation. Printed by Stansby, 1613.

The *Exercises* (1594) collected six treatises on practical skills, with a serious effort to be up-to-date. One of the parts described the world map of [Petrus Plancius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrus_Plancius), published two years earlier. Other topical matters covered were [Molyneux's globes](wikipedia://en/15442947), the work of [John Blagrave](wikipedia://en/1393581) and [Gemma Frisius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemma_Frisius), and the [cross-staff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-staff) of [Thomas Hood](wikipedia://en/21652686).[^o34] According to Rouse Ball:

In 1594 he published his Exercises in six parts, containing a brief account of arithmetic, cosmography, the use of the globes, a universal map, the astrolabe, and navigation. The arithmetic is taken from [Recorde](wikipedia://en/310247), but to it are added trigonometrical tables (copied from [Clavius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clavius)) of the natural sines, tangents, and secants of all angles in the first quadrant; the difference between consecutive angles being one minute. These are worked out to seven places of decimals. This is the earliest English work in which plane trigonometry is introduced.[^o8]

A later edition (1613) showed the circumnavigations of [Francis Drake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Drake) and [Thomas Cavendish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Cavendish).[^o35]

He collaborated on an astronomy book, *The Theoriques of the Seuen Planets* (1602), assisted by [Lancelot Browne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancelot_Browne) as he notes in the preface.[^o36] It contained also information about the recent research of [William Gilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Gilbert_%28astronomer%29) on the Earth's magnetic field, which he included with help from [Edward Wright](wikipedia://en/17214829) and Henry Briggs. Wright had earlier supplied some of the innovative material for his writing on [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation) in the *Exercises*. He had worked with [William Barlow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Barlow_%28archdeacon%29) and others on the required scientific instruments; according to Hill Blundeville invented the protractor.[^o4] In fact he described a semicircular instrument for measuring angles in 1589, in his *Briefe Description of Universal Mappes and Cardes*.[^o37]
