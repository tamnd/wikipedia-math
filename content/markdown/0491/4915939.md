# Midy's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Midy's theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Midy's theorem**, named after [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) E. Midy,[^o2] is a statement about the [decimal expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_expansion) of [fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29) *a*/*p* where *p* is a [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) and *a*/*p* has a [repeating decimal](wikipedia://en/13612447) expansion with an [even](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_number) period . If the period of the decimal representation of *a*/*p* is 2*n*, so that

:::

$$\frac{a}{p}=0.\overline{a_1a_2a_3\dots a_na_{n+1}\dots a_{2n}}$$

then the digits in the second half of the repeating decimal period are the [9s complement](wikipedia://en/358754#Numeric_complements) of the corresponding digits in its first half. In other words,

$$a_i+a_{i+n}=9$$

$$a_1\dots a_n+a_{n+1}\dots a_{2n}=10^n-1.$$

For example,

$$\frac{1}{13}=0.\overline{076923}\text{ and }076+923=999.$$

$$\frac{1}{17}=0.\overline{0588235294117647}\text{ and }05882352+94117647=99999999.$$

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Extended Midy's theorem"}

## Extended Midy's theorem {#Extended_Midy's_theorem}

If *k* is any [divisor](wikipedia://en/49492) of *h* (where *h* is the number of digits of the period of the decimal expansion of *a*/*p* (where *p* is again a prime)), then Midy's theorem can be generalised as follows. The **extended Midy's theorem**[^o5] states that if the repeating portion of the decimal expansion of *a*/*p* is divided into *k*-digit numbers, then their sum is a multiple of 10<sup>*k*</sup> − 1.

For example,

$$\frac{1}{19}=0.\overline{052631578947368421}$$

has a period of 18. Dividing the repeating portion into 6-digit numbers and summing them gives

$$052631+578947+368421=999999.$$

Similarly, dividing the repeating portion into 3-digit numbers and summing them gives

$$052+631+578+947+368+421=2997=3\times999.$$

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Midy's theorem in other bases"}

## Midy's theorem in other bases {#Midy's_theorem_in_other_bases}

Midy's theorem and its extension do not depend on special properties of the decimal expansion, but work equally well in any [base](wikipedia://en/701207) *b*, provided we replace 10<sup>*k*</sup> − 1 with *b*<sup>*k*</sup> − 1 and carry out addition in base *b*.

For example, in [octal](wikipedia://en/22330)

$$\begin{aligned} & \frac{1}{19}=0.\overline{032745}_8 \\[8pt] & 032_8+745_8=777_8 \\[8pt] & 03_8+27_8+45_8=77_8. \end{aligned}$$

In [dozenal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dozenal) (using inverted two and three for ten and eleven, respectively)

$$\begin{aligned} & \frac{1}{19}=0.\overline{076\mathcal{E}45}_{12} \\[8pt] & 076_{12}+\mathcal{E}45_{12}=\mathcal{EEE}_{12} \\[8pt] & 07_{12}+6\mathcal{E}_{12}+45_{12}=\mathcal{EE}_{12} \end{aligned}$$

:::

## Proof of Midy's theorem {#Proof_of_Midy's_theorem}

Short proofs of Midy's theorem can be given using results from [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890). However, it is also possible to prove Midy's theorem using [elementary algebra](wikipedia://en/9710) and [modular arithmetic](wikipedia://en/20087):

Let *p* be a prime and *a*/*p* be a fraction between 0 and 1. Suppose the expansion of *a*/*p* in base *b* has a period of *ℓ*, so

$$\begin{aligned} & \frac{a}{p} = [0.\overline{a_1a_2\dots a_\ell}]_b \\[6pt] & \Rightarrow\frac{a}{p}b^\ell = [a_1a_2\dots a_\ell.\overline{a_1a_2\dots a_\ell}]_b \\[6pt] & \Rightarrow\frac{a}{p}b^\ell = N+[0.\overline{a_1a_2\dots a_\ell}]_b=N+\frac{a}{p} \\[6pt] & \Rightarrow\frac{a}{p} = \frac{N}{b^\ell-1} \end{aligned}$$

where *N* is the [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) whose expansion in base *b* is the string *a*<sub>1</sub>*a*<sub>2</sub>...*a*<sub>*ℓ*</sub>.

Note that *b*<sup>*ℓ*</sup> − 1 is a multiple of *p* because (*b*<sup>*ℓ*</sup> − 1)*a*/*p* is an integer. Also *b*<sup>*n*</sup>−1 is *not* a multiple of *p* for any value of *n* less than *ℓ*, because otherwise the repeating period of *a*/*p* in base *b* would be less than *ℓ*.

Now suppose that *ℓ* = *hk*. Then *b*<sup>*ℓ*</sup> − 1 is a multiple of *b*<sup>*k*</sup> − 1. (To see this, substitute *x* for *b*<sup>*k*</sup>; then *b*<sup>*ℓ*</sup> = *x*<sup>*h*</sup> and *x* − 1 is a factor of *x*<sup>*h*</sup> − 1. ) Say *b*<sup>*ℓ*</sup> − 1 = *m*(*b*<sup>*k*</sup> − 1), so

$$\frac{a}{p}=\frac{N}{m(b^k-1)}.$$

But *b*<sup>*ℓ*</sup> − 1 is a multiple of *p*; *b*<sup>*k*</sup> − 1 is *not* a multiple of *p* (because *k* is less than *ℓ* ); and *p* is a prime; so *m* must be a multiple of *p* and

$$\frac{am}{p}=\frac{N}{b^k-1}$$

is an integer. In other words,

$$N\equiv0\pmod{b^k-1}.$$

Now split the string *a*<sub>1</sub>*a*<sub>2</sub>...*a*<sub>*ℓ*</sub> into *h* equal parts of length *k*, and let these represent the integers *N*<sub>0</sub>...*N*<sub>*h* − 1</sub> in base *b*, so that

$$\begin{aligned} N_{h-1} & = [a_1\dots a_k]_b \\ N_{h-2} & = [a_{k+1}\dots a_{2k}]_b \\ & {}\ \ \vdots \\ N_0 & = [a_{l-k+1}\dots a_l]_b \end{aligned}$$

To prove Midy's extended theorem in base *b* we must show that the sum of the *h* integers *N*<sub>*i*</sub> is a multiple of *b*<sup>*k*</sup> − 1.

Since *b*<sup>*k*</sup> is congruent to 1 modulo *b*<sup>*k*</sup> − 1, any power of *b*<sup>*k*</sup> will also be congruent to 1 modulo *b*<sup>*k*</sup> − 1. So

$$N=\sum_{i=0}^{h-1}N_ib^{ik}=\sum_{i=0}^{h-1}N_i(b^{k})^i$$

$$\Rightarrow N \equiv \sum_{i=0}^{h-1}N_i \pmod{b^k-1}$$

$$\Rightarrow \sum_{i=0}^{h-1}N_i \equiv 0 \pmod{b^k-1}$$

which proves Midy's extended theorem in base *b*.

To prove the original Midy's theorem, take the special case where *h* = 2. Note that *N*<sub>0</sub> and *N*<sub>1</sub> are both represented by strings of *k* digits in base *b* so both satisfy

$$0 \leq N_i \leq b^k-1.$$

*N*<sub>0</sub> and *N*<sub>1</sub> cannot both equal 0 (otherwise *a*/*p* = 0) and cannot both equal *b*<sup>*k*</sup> − 1 (otherwise *a*/*p* = 1), so

$$0 < N_0+N_1 < 2(b^k-1)$$

and since *N*<sub>0</sub> + *N*<sub>1</sub> is a multiple of *b*<sup>*k*</sup> − 1, it follows that

$$N_0+N_1 = b^k-1.$$

:::

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/corollary cue=named-heading name="Corollary"}

## Corollary {#Corollary}

From the above,

$$\frac{am}{p}$$

is an integer

Thus $m \equiv 0 \pmod p$

And thus for $k = \frac{\ell}{2}$

$$b^{\ell/2}+ 1 \equiv 0 \pmod p$$

For $k = \frac{\ell}{3}$ and is an integer

$$b^{2\ell/3} + b^{\ell/3} + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod p$$

and so on.

:::
