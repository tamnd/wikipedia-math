# Arrow (symbol)

3

*Image: Arrows in Okazaki.jpeg.* Advertising [billboards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboards) in [Okazaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okazaki,_Aichi), Japan, featuring many different arrow symbols

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Arrow"}

An **arrow** is a graphical [symbol](wikipedia://en/37673), such as ←, ↑, ↓ or →, or a [pictogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pictogram), used to point or indicate direction. In its simplest form, an arrow is a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654), [chevron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevron_%28insignia%29), or [concave kite](wikipedia://en/84840#Characterizations), usually affixed to a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) or [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048),[^o3] and in more complex forms a representation of an actual [arrow](wikipedia://en/51513) (e.g., ➵ U+27B5). The direction indicated by an arrow is the one along the length of the line or rectangle toward the single pointed end.

:::

## History {#History}

An older (medieval) convention is the [manicule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manicule) (pointing hand, ☚). [Pedro Reinel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_Reinel) in c. 1505 first used the [fleur-de-lis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleur-de-lis) as indicating north in a [compass rose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_rose); the convention of marking the eastern direction with a cross is older (medieval).[^o5]

Use of the arrow symbol does not appear to pre-date the 18th century. An early arrow symbol is found in an illustration of [Bernard Forest de Bélidor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Forest_de_Bélidor)'s treatise *L'architecture hydraulique*, printed in France in 1737. The arrow is here used to illustrate the direction of the flow of water and of the water wheel's rotation. At about the same time, arrow symbols were used to indicate the flow of rivers in maps.[^o6]

A trend toward abstraction, in which the arrow's [fletching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fletching) is removed, can be observed in the mid-to-late 19th century. The arrow can be seen in the work of [Paul Klee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Klee). In a further refinement of the symbol, John Richard Green's *[A Short History of the English People](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Short_History_of_the_English_People)* of 1874 contained maps by cartographer Emil Reich, which indicated army movements by curved lines, with solid triangular arrowheads placed intermittently along the lines.[^o7]

## Main usage {#Main_usage}

*Image: Fire exit.svg.* An [exit sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exit_sign) with an arrow to indicate the exit is to the left

Arrows are universally recognised for indicating directions.[^o3] They are widely used on [signage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signage) and for [wayfinding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayfinding),[^o3] and are often used in [road surface markings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road_surface_marking). A [two-way road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-way_road) may be indicated by "↕" or "⇅".

## In mathematics and sciences {#In_mathematics_and_sciences}

Upward arrows are often used to indicate an increase in a numerical value, and downward arrows indicate a decrease.

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), a right-facing arrow indicates [material conditional](wikipedia://en/658808), and a left-right (bidirectional) arrow indicates [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922), an upwards arrow indicates the [NAND operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAND_operator) (negation of conjunction), an downwards arrow indicates the [NOR operator](wikipedia://en/209154) (negation of disjunction).

Use of arrow symbols in [mathematical notation](wikipedia://en/277184) developed in the first half of the 20th century.[^o10] [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) in 1922 introduced the arrow symbol representing [logical implication](wikipedia://en/658808). The double-headed arrow representing [logical equivalence](wikipedia://en/169324) was introduced by Albrecht Becker in *Die Aristotelische Theorie der Möglichkeitsschlüsse*, Berlin, 1933.[^o7]

[Knuth's up-arrow notation](wikipedia://en/258733) uses multiple up arrows, such as ⇈, for iterated, or repeated, exponentiation (*[tetration](wikipedia://en/482379)*).

The quantum theory of [electron spin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_spin) uses either upward or downward arrows.

A [vector](wikipedia://en/23218956) may be denoted with an overhead arrow, such as in $\vec{x}$ or $\stackrel \longrightarrow{AB}$.

Some computer programming languages, like Haskell, use [arrows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow_%28computer_science%29) (typed as ->) to denote a type class.

In chemistry, arrows indicate a chemical reaction. For example, 2H<sub>2</sub> + O<sub>2</sub> → 2H<sub>2</sub>O shows that hydrogen and oxygen react to produce water.

## Graffiti {#Graffiti}

Arrows are regularly used in contemporary [graffiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graffiti) designs, incorporated as an element in both simplistic [tags](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tag_%28graffiti%29) and complex [wildstyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildstyle) [pieces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piece_%28graffiti%29). Arrows are used as a visual technique to make a graffito stand out or give it a sense of movement.[^o12] The graffiti theoretician [RAMM:ΣLL:ZΣΣ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rammellzee) described adornments, such as arrows, in [wildstyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildstyle) paintings as ornaments that ‘armed’ the letters of a piece.[^o13] The [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia) graffiti artist Cool Earl began using arrows in 1967,[^o14] although the New York graffiti writer [SJK 171](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SJK_171) may have been the first to do so.[^o15] The graffiti artist [Mare139](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mare139) is known for creating 3D sculptures of arrows.[^o16]
