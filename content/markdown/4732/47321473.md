# Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem"}

In [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478) and [approximation theory](wikipedia://en/1092713), the **Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem** (or **superposition theorem**) states that every [multivariate](wikipedia://en/615222) [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_%28mathematics%29) [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f\colon[0,1]^n\to \mathbb{R}$ can be represented as a [superposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superposition) of continuous single-variable functions.

:::

The works of [Vladimir Arnold](wikipedia://en/32490) and [Andrey Kolmogorov](wikipedia://en/91161) established that if *f* is a multivariate [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122), then *f* can be written as a finite [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of continuous functions of a single variable and the [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) of [addition](wikipedia://en/61338).[^o2] More specifically,

$$f(\mathbf x) = f(x_1,\ldots ,x_n) = \sum_{q=0}^{2n} \Phi_{q}\!\left(\sum_{p=1}^{n} \phi_{q,p}(x_{p})\right),$$

where $\phi_{q,p}\colon[0,1]\to \mathbb{R}$ and $\Phi_{q}\colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$.

In this representation, the inner functions $\phi_{q,p}$ are continuous and universal, that is, independent of $f$, while the outer functions $\Phi_q$ depend on the specific function being represented. The same representation formula extends to all multivariate functions $f$, including discontinuous ones, as discussed in [^o3]. If $f$ is continuous, then the corresponding outer functions $\Phi_q$ are continuous; if $f$ is discontinuous, the outer functions are generally discontinuous, while the inner functions $\phi_{q,p}$ remain unchanged, being the same universal functions.

There are proofs with specific constructions.[^o4]

It solved a more constrained form of [Hilbert's thirteenth problem](wikipedia://en/2336190), so the original Hilbert's thirteenth problem is a corollary.[^o5][^o6][^o7] In a sense, they showed that the only true continuous multivariate function is the sum, since every other continuous function can be written using [univariate](wikipedia://en/2398982) continuous functions and summing.[^o8]

## History {#History}

The Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem is closely related to [Hilbert's 13th problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_13th_problem). In his [Paris](wikipedia://en/22989) lecture at the [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) in 1900, [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) formulated [23 problems](wikipedia://en/154584) which in his opinion were important for the further development of mathematics.[^o10] The 13th of these problems dealt with the solution of general equations of higher degrees. It is known that for algebraic equations of degree 4 the solution can be computed by formulae that only contain radicals and arithmetic operations. For higher orders, [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316) shows us that the solutions of algebraic equations cannot be expressed in terms of basic algebraic operations. It follows from the so called [Tschirnhaus transformation](wikipedia://en/595824) that the general algebraic equation

$$x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots +a_{0}=0$$

can be translated to the form $y^{n}+b_{n-4}y^{n-4}+\cdots +b_{1}y+1=0$. The Tschirnhaus transformation is given by a formula containing only radicals and arithmetic operations and transforms. Therefore, the solution of an [algebraic equation](wikipedia://en/404001) of degree $n$ can be represented as a superposition of functions of two variables if $n<7$ and as a superposition of functions of $n-4$ variables if $n\geq 7$. For $n=7$ the solution is a superposition of arithmetic operations, radicals, and the solution of the equation $y^{7}+b_{3}y^{3}+b_{2}y^{2}+b_{1}y+1=0$.

A further simplification with algebraic transformations seems to be impossible which led to Hilbert's conjecture that "A solution of the general equation of degree 7 cannot be represented as a superposition of continuous functions of two variables". This explains the relation of [Hilbert's thirteenth problem](wikipedia://en/2336190) to the representation of a higher-dimensional function as superposition of lower-dimensional functions. In this context, it has stimulated many studies in the theory of functions and other related problems by different authors.[^o11]

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Variants"}

## Variants {#Variants}

A variant of Kolmogorov's theorem that reduces the number of outer functions $\Phi_{q}$ is due to [George Lorentz](wikipedia://en/58614550).[^o14] He showed in 1962 that the outer functions $\Phi_{q}$ can be replaced by a single function $\Phi$. More precisely, Lorentz proved the existence of functions $\phi _{q,p}$, $q=0,1,\ldots, 2n$, $p=1,\ldots,n,$ such that

$$f(\mathbf x) = \sum_{q=0}^{2n} \Phi\!\left(\sum_{p=1}^{n} \phi_{q,p}(x_{p})\right).$$

David Sprecher [^o15] replaced the inner functions $\phi_{q,p}$ by one single inner function with an appropriate shift in its argument. He proved that there exist real values $\eta, \lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$, a continuous function $\Phi\colon \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, and a real increasing continuous function $\phi\colon [0,1] \rightarrow [0,1]$ with $\phi \in \operatorname{Lip}(\ln 2/\ln (2N+2))$, for $N \geq n \geq 2$, such that

$$f(\mathbf x) = \sum_{q=0}^{2n} \Phi\!\left(\sum_{p=1}^{n} \lambda_p \phi(x_{p}+\eta q)+q \right).$$

Phillip A. Ostrand [^o16] generalized the Kolmogorov superposition theorem to compact metric spaces. For $p=1,\ldots,m$ let $X_p$ be compact metric spaces of finite dimension $n_p$ and let $n = \sum_{p=1}^{m} n_p$. Then there exists continuous functions $\phi_{q,p}\colon X_p \rightarrow [0,1], q=0,\ldots,2n, p=1,\ldots,m$ and continuous functions $G_q\colon [0,1] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}, q=0,\ldots,2n$ such that any continuous function $f\colon X_1 \times \dots \times X_m \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is representable in the form

$$f(x_1,\ldots,x_m) = \sum_{q=0}^{2n} G_{q}\!\left(\sum_{p=1}^{m} \phi_{q,p}(x_{p})\right).$$

The Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem and its aforementioned variants also hold for discontinuous multivariate functions.[^o17]

:::

## Continuous form {#Continuous_form}

In its classic form the Kolmogorov–Arnold representation has two layers, where the first, called the inner layer, is a vector to vector mapping

$$s_q = \sum_{p=1}^{n} \phi_{q,p}(x_{p}), \quad q=0,1,..,2n$$

and the second, outer layer, is a vector to scalar mapping

$$f(x_1, ... , x_n) = \sum_{q=0}^{2n} \Phi_q\left(s_q\right).$$

The transition from discrete to continuous form for the inner layer gives the equation of Urysohn with 3D kernel

$$s(q) = \int_{p_1}^{p_2} F[x(p),p,q]dp, \quad q \in [q_1, q_2],$$

the same transition for the outer layer gives its particular case

$$f = \int_{q_1}^{q_2} G[s(q),q]dq.$$

The generalization of the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation known as the Kolmogorov-Arnold network in continuous form is a chain of Urysohn equations, where the outer equation may also return a function or a vector as multiple related targets.

The Urysohn equation was introduced in 1924 for a different purpose, as function to function mapping with the problem of finding a function $x(p)$, provided $s(q)$ and $F[x(p),p,q]$.

## Limitations {#Limitations}

The theorem does not hold in general for complex multi-variate functions, as discussed here.[^o6] Furthermore, the non-smoothness of the inner functions and their "wild behavior" has limited the practical use of the representation,[^o20] although there is some debate on this.[^o21]

## Applications {#Applications}

In the field of [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488), there have been various attempts to use [neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_networks) modeled on the Kolmogorov–Arnold representation, known as [Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov-Arnold_Networks).[^o24][^o25][^o26][^o27][^o28][^o29][^o30] In these works, the Kolmogorov–Arnold theorem plays a role analogous to that of the [universal approximation theorem](wikipedia://en/18543448) in the study of [multilayer perceptrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multilayer_perceptron).

## Proof {#Proof}

Here one example is proved.[^o33] A proof for the case of functions depending on two variables is given, as the generalization is immediate.

### Setup {#Setup}

- Let $I$ be the unit interval $[0, 1]$.
- Let $C[I]$ be the set of continuous functions of type $[0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$. It is a [function space](wikipedia://en/340630) with [supremum norm](wikipedia://en/349755) (it is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989)).
- Let $f$ be a continuous function of type $[0, 1]^2 \to \mathbb{R}$, and let $\|f\|$ be the supremum of it on $[0, 1]^2$.
- Let $t$ be a positive [irrational number](wikipedia://en/20647689). Its exact value is irrelevant.

We say that a 5-tuple $(\phi_1, \dots, \phi_5) \in C[I]^5$ is a Kolmogorov–Arnold [tuple](wikipedia://en/132729) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) for any $f \in C[I^2]$ there exists a continuous function $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, such that

$$f(x,y) = \sum_{i=1}^5 g(\phi_i(x) + t \phi_i(y))$$

In the notation, we have the following:

::: {#o35 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

The Kolmogorov–Arnold tuples make up an open and dense subset of $C[I]^5$.

:::

::: {#o37 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

### Proof {#Proof}

Fix a $f \in C[I^2]$. We show that a certain subset $U_f \subset C[I]^5$ is open and dense: There exists continuous $g$ such that $\|g\| < \frac{1.01}{7} \|f\|$, and

$$\Big\| f(x,y) - \sum_{i=1}^5 g(\phi_i(x) + t \phi_i(y)) \Big\| < \frac{6.01}{7} \|f\|$$

We can assume that $\|f\| = 1$ with no loss of generality.

By continuity, the set of such 5-tuples is open in $C[I]^5$. It remains to prove that they are dense.

The key idea is to divide $[0, 1]^2$ into an overlapping system of small squares, each with a unique address, and define $g$ to have the appropriate value at each address.

#### Grid system {#Grid_system}

Let $\psi_1 \in C[I]$. For any $\epsilon > 0$, for all large $N$, we can discretize $\psi_1$ into a continuous function $\phi_1$ satisfying the following properties:

- $\phi_1$ is constant on each of the intervals $[0/5N, 4/5N], [5/5N, 9/5N], \dots, [1-5/5N, 1-1/5N]$.
- These values are different rational numbers.
- $$\|\psi_1 - \phi_1\| < \epsilon.$$

This function $\phi_1$ creates a grid address system on $[0, 1]^2$, divided into streets and blocks. The blocks are of form $[0/5N, 4/5N] \times [0/5N, 4/5N], [0/5N, 4/5N] \times [5/5N, 9/5N], \dots$.

*Image: Kolmogorov-Arnold grid.svg.* An example construction of $\phi$ and the corresponding grid system

Since $f$ is continuous on $[0, 1]^2$, it is uniformly continuous. Thus, we can take $N$ large enough, so that $f$ varies by less than $1/7$ on any block.

On each block, $\phi_1(x) + t \phi_1(y)$ has a constant value. The key property is that, because $t$ is irrational, and $\phi_1$ is rational on the blocks, each block has a different value of $\phi_1(x) + t \phi_1(y)$.

So, given any 5-tuple $(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_5)$, we construct such a 5-tuple $(\phi_1, \dots, \phi_5)$. These create 5 overlapping grid systems.

Enumerate the blocks as $R_{i, r}$, where $R_{i,r}$ is the $r$ -th block of the grid system created by $\phi_i$. The address of this block is $a_{i, r} := \phi_i(x) + t \phi_i(y)$, for any $(x, y) \in R_{i, r}$. By adding a small and linearly independent irrational number (the construction is similar to that of the [Hamel basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamel_basis)) to each of $(\phi_1, \dots, \phi_5)$, we can ensure that every block has a unique address.

By plotting out the entire grid system, one can see that every point in $[0, 1]^2$ is contained in 3 to 5 blocks, and 2 to 0 streets.

#### Construction of *g* {#Construction_of_g}

For each block $R_{i, r}$, if $f > 0$ on all of $R_{i, r}$ then define $g(a_{i, r}) = +1/7$; if $f < 0$ on all of $R_{i, r}$ then define $g(a_{i, r}) = -1/7$. Now, linearly interpolate $g$ between these defined values. It remains to show this construction has the desired properties.

For any $(x, y) \in I^2$, we consider three cases.

If $f(x, y) \in [1/7, 7/7]$, then by [uniform continuity](wikipedia://en/32337), $f > 0$ on every block $R_{i, r}$ that contains the point $(x, y)$. This means that $g = 1/7$ on 3 to 5 of the blocks, and have an unknown value on 2 to 0 of the streets. Thus, we have

$$\sum_{i=1}^5 g(\phi_i(x) + t \phi_i(y)) \in [1/7, 5/7]$$

giving

$$\Big| f(x,y) - \sum_{i=1}^5 g(\phi_i(x) + t \phi_i(y)) \Big| \in [0, 6/7]$$

Similarly for $f(x, y) \in [-7/7, -1/7]$.

If $f(x, y) \in [-1/7, 1/7]$, then since $\|g\| \leq 1/7$, we still have

$$\Big| f(x,y) - \sum_{i=1}^5 g(\phi_i(x) + t \phi_i(y)) \Big| \in [0, 6/7]$$

::: {#o41 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Baire category theorem"}

#### Baire category theorem {#Baire_category_theorem}

Iterating the above construction, then applying the [Baire category theorem](wikipedia://en/55227), we find that the following kind of 5-tuples are open and dense in $C[I]^5$: There exists a sequence of $g_1, g_2, \dots$ such that $\|g_1\| < \frac{1.01}{7} \|f\|$, $\|g_2\| < \frac{1.01}7 \frac{6.01}7 \|f\|$, etc. This allows their sum to be defined: $g := \sum_n g_n$, which is still continuous and bounded, and it satisfies

$$f(x,y) = \sum_{i=1}^5 g(\phi_i(x) + t \phi_i(y))$$

Since $C[I^2]$ [has a countable dense subset](wikipedia://en/27855), we can apply the Baire category theorem again to obtain the full theorem.

:::

:::

::: {#o43 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Extensions"}

### Extensions {#Extensions}

The above proof generalizes for $n$-dimensions: Divide the cube $[0, 1]^n$ into $(2n+1)$ interlocking grid systems, such that each point in the cube is on $(n+1)$ to $(2n + 1)$ blocks, and $0$ to $n$ streets. Now, since $(n+1) > n$, the above construction works.

Indeed, this is the best possible value.

::: {#o45 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem (Sternfeld, 1985)"}

**Theorem (Sternfeld, 1985 [^o44]).**

Let $X$ be a compact metric space with $\operatorname{dim} X \geq 2$, and let $X \subset \mathbb{R}^m$ be an embedding such that every $f \in C[X]$ can be represented as

$$f\left(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_m\right)=\sum_{i=1}^m g_i\left(x_i\right), \quad\left(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_m\right) \in X, \quad g_i \in C[\mathbb{R}].$$

Then $m \geq 2 \operatorname{dim} X+1$.

:::

A relatively short proof is given in [^o46] via [dimension theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_theory).

In another direction of generality, more conditions can be imposed on the Kolmogorov–Arnold tuples.

::: {#o47 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

There exists a Kolmogorov–Arnold tuple where each function is strictly monotonically increasing.

:::

The proof is given in.[^o48]

(Vituškin, 1954)[^o49] showed that the theorem is false if we require all functions $f, g,\phi_i$ to be continuously differentiable. The theorem remains true if we require all $\phi_i$ to be 1-[Lipschitz continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipschitz_continuous).[^o7]
