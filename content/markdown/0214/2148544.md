# Hereditarianism

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hereditarianism"}

**Hereditarianism** is the view that [heredity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heredity) plays a central role in determining [human nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_nature) and character traits, such as [intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_%28trait%29) and personality. Hereditarians believe in the power of [genetic influences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_influences) to [explain human behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_genetics) and solve human social-political problems. They stress the value of [evolutionary explanations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_explanations) in all areas of the [human sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_sciences).

:::

Most prominently in [intelligence research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_research), proponents argue that [genetic predisposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_predisposition) determines individual life outcomes more than do either structured [environmental influences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environment_and_intelligence) (i.e. [nurture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nurture)) or [developmental noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developmental_noise).

On the other hand, the idea that genetic predisposition determines differences in life outcomes between [racial groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Race_%28human_categorization%29) –– sometimes termed **racial hereditarianism** –– is rejected by mainstream science on [empirical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_evidence) and [methodological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodology) grounds, and considered a form of [scientific racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_racism).[^o2]

## Overview {#Overview}

Social scientist [Barry Mehler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Mehler) defines hereditarianism as "the belief that a substantial part of both group and individual differences in human behavioral traits are caused by genetic differences".[^o4] Hereditarianism is sometimes used as a [synonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synonym) for [biological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_determinism) or [genetic determinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_determinism), though some scholars distinguish the two terms. When distinguished, biological determinism is used to mean that heredity is the only factor. Supporters of hereditarianism reject this sense of biological determinism for most cases. However, in some cases genetic determinism is true; for example, [Matt Ridley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matt_Ridley) describes [Huntington's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huntington's_disease) as "pure fatalism, undiluted by environmental variability".[^o5] In other cases, hereditarians would see no role for genes; for example, the condition of "*not knowing a word of Chinese*" has nothing to do (directly) with genes.[^o6]

Hereditarians point to the heritability of cognitive ability, and the outsized influence that cognitive ability has on life outcomes, as evidence in favor of the hereditarian viewpoint.[^o7] According to [Plomin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Plomin) and Van Stumm (2018), "Intelligence is highly heritable and predicts important educational, occupational and health outcomes better than any other trait."[^o8] Estimates for the [heritability of intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heritability_of_intelligence) range from 20% in infancy to 80% in adulthood.[^o9][^o10]

## History {#History}

[Francis Galton](wikipedia://en/49072) is generally considered the father of hereditarianism.[^o4] In his book *[Hereditary Genius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hereditary_Genius)* (1869), Galton pioneered research on the heredity of [intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence). Galton continued research into the heredity of human behavior in his later works, including "The History of Twins" (1875)[^o12] and *[Inquiries into Human Faculty and Its Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inquiries_into_Human_Faculty_and_Its_Development)* (1883).

*[The Bell Curve](wikipedia://en/31277)* (1994), by psychologist [Richard Herrnstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Herrnstein) and political scientist [Charles Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Murray_%28political_scientist%29), argued that the heritability of cognitive ability, combined with a modern American society in which cognitive ability is the leading determinant of success, was leading to an increasingly rich and segregated "[cognitive elite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_elite)".[^o13][^o14] Herrnstein and Murray also examined how cognitive ability predicts socially desirable behavior.[^o13] They also discussed the debate regarding [race and intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Race_and_intelligence), concluding that the evidence to date didn't justify an estimate on the degree of influence of genetics versus environmental causes for average differences in IQ test performance between racial groups.[^o15] Today the scientific consensus is that genetics does not explain such differences, and that they are rather environmental in origin.[^o2][^o16][^o17][^o18][^o19][^o20][^o21]

Cognitive psychologist [Steven Pinker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Pinker), in his book *[The Blank Slate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Blank_Slate)* (2002), argues that biology explains much more about human nature than people generally acknowledge.[^o22]

## Political implications {#Political_implications}

In 1949, Nicolas Pastore claimed that hereditarians were more likely to be [conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservatism),[^o24] that they view social and economic inequality as a natural result of variation in talent and character. Consequently, they explain class and race differences as the result of partly genetic group differences. Pastore contrasted this with the claim that [behaviorists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behaviorism) were more likely to be [liberals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalism) or [leftists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leftist), that they believe economic disadvantage and structural problems in the social order were to blame for group differences.[^o24]

However, the historical correspondence between hereditarianism and conservatism has broken down at least among proponents of hereditarianism. Philosopher [Peter Singer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Singer) describes his vision of a new liberal political view that embraces hereditarianism in his 1999 book, *[A Darwinian Left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Darwinian_Left)*.[^o25]

## Criticism {#Criticism}

Ronald C. Bailey argues that hereditarianism is based on five fallacious assumptions. In a 1997 paper, he also wrote that "...behavior geneticists will continue to be very limited in their ability to partition the effects of genes, the environment, and their covariance and interaction on human behavior and cognitive ability."[^o27]
