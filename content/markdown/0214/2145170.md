# Multicategory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Multicategory"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) (especially [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869)), a **multicategory** is a generalization of the concept of [category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_%28math%29) that allows [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) of multiple [arity](wikipedia://en/42301). If morphisms in a category are viewed as analogous to [functions](wikipedia://en/185427), then morphisms in a multicategory are analogous to functions of several variables. Multicategories are also sometimes called [operads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operad_theory#Operad_without_permutations), or colored operads.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A (non-symmetric) multicategory consists of

- a collection (often a [proper class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_class)) of *objects*;
- for every [finite sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_sequence) $(X_i)_{i \in [n]}$ of objects ($[n] = \{0,1,2,...,n\}$) and every object *Y*, a set of *morphisms* from $(X_i)_{i \in n}$ to *Y*; and
- for every object *X*, a special identity morphism (with *n* = 1) from *X* to *X*.

Additionally, there are composition operations: Given a sequence of sequences $((X_{ij})_{i \in n_j})_{j \in m}$ of objects, a sequence $(Y_j)_{j \in m}$ of objects, and an object *Z*: if

- for each $j \in m$, *f*<sub>*j*</sub> is a morphism from $(X_{ij})_{i \in n_j}$ to *Y*<sub>*j*</sub>; and
- *g* is a morphism from $(Y_j)_{j \in m}$ to *Z*:

then there is a composite morphism $g(f_j)_{j \in m}$ from $(X_{ij})_{i \in n_j, j \in m}$ to *Z*. This must satisfy certain axioms:

- If *m* = 1, *Z* = *Y*<sub>0</sub>, and *g* is the identity morphism for *Y*<sub>0</sub>, then *g*(*f*<sub>0</sub>) = *f*<sub>0</sub>;
- if for each $j \in m$, *n*<sub>*j*</sub> = 1, $X_{0j} = Y_j$, and *f*<sub>*j*</sub> is the identity morphism for *Y*<sub>*j*</sub>, then $g(f_j)_{j \in m} = g$; and
- an [associativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity) condition: if for each $j \in m$ and $i \in n_j$, $e_{ij}$ is a morphism from $(W_{hij})_{h \in o_{ij}}$ to $X_{ij}$, then $g\left(f_j(e_{ij})_{i \in n_j}\right)_{j \in m} = g(f_j)_{j \in m}(e_{ij})_{i \in n_j, j \in m}$ are identical morphisms from $(W_{hij})_{h \in o_{ij}, i \in n_j, j \in m}$ to *Z*.

:::

## Comcategories {#Comcategories}

A *comcategory* (co-multicategory) is a [totally ordered set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_ordered_set) *O* of objects, a set *A* of *multiarrows* with two functions

$$\mathrm{head}:A\rightarrow O,$$

$$\mathrm{ground}:A\rightarrow O^\%,$$

where *O*<sup>%</sup> is the set of all finite ordered sequences of elements of *O*. The dual image of a multiarrow *f* may be summarized

$$f:\mathrm{head}(f) \Leftarrow \mathrm{ground}(f).$$

A comcategory **C** also has a *multiproduct* with the usual character of a composition operation. **C** is said to be associative if there holds a *multiproduct axiom* in relation to this operator.

Any multicategory, symmetric *or* non-symmetric, together with a total-ordering of the object set, can be made into an equivalent comcategory.

A *multiorder* is a comcategory satisfying the following conditions.

- There is at most one multiarrow with given head and ground.
- Each object *x* has a unit multiarrow.
- A multiarrow is a unit if its ground has one entry.

Multiorders are a generalization of partial orders (posets), and were first introduced (in passing) by [Tom Leinster](wikipedia://en/78103499).[^o5]

## Examples {#Examples}

There is a multicategory whose objects are (small) [sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_%28math%29), where a morphism from the sets *X*<sub>1</sub>, *X*<sub>2</sub>, ..., and *X*<sub>*n*</sub> to the set *Y* is an [*n*-ary function](wikipedia://en/3943), that is a function from the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) *X*<sub>1</sub> × *X*<sub>2</sub> × ... × *X*<sub>*n*</sub> to *Y*.

There is a multicategory whose objects are [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) (over the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), say), where a morphism from the vector spaces *X*<sub>1</sub>, *X*<sub>2</sub>, ..., and *X*<sub>*n*</sub> to the vector space *Y* is a [multilinear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multilinear_operator), that is a [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) from the [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) *X*<sub>1</sub> ⊗ *X*<sub>2</sub> ⊗ ... ⊗ *X*<sub>*n*</sub> to *Y*.

More generally, given any [monoidal category](wikipedia://en/330604) **C**, there is a multicategory whose objects are objects of **C**, where a morphism from the **C**-objects *X*<sub>1</sub>, *X*<sub>2</sub>, ..., and *X*<sub>*n*</sub> to the **C**-object *Y* is a **C**-morphism from the monoidal product of *X*<sub>1</sub>, *X*<sub>2</sub>, ..., and *X*<sub>*n*</sub> to *Y*.

An [operad](wikipedia://en/3126358) is a multicategory with one unique object; except in degenerate cases, such a multicategory does not come from a monoidal category.

Examples of multiorders include *pointed [multisets](wikipedia://en/305303)* , *integer partitions* , and *combinatory separations* . The triangles (or compositions) of any multiorder are morphisms of a (not necessarily associative) category of *contractions* and a comcategory of *decompositions*. The contraction category for the multiorder of *multimin partitions* is the simplest known category of multisets.[^o8]

:::

::: {#o10 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Multicategories are often incorrectly considered to belong to [higher category theory](wikipedia://en/5274013), as their original application was the observation that the operators and identities satisfied by higher categories are the objects and multiarrows of a multicategory. The study of *n*-categories was in turn motivated by applications in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) and attempts to describe the [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831) of higher-dimensional [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470).

The correspondence between contractions and decompositions of triangles in a multiorder allows one to construct an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) called its *incidence algebra*. Any element that is nonzero on all unit arrows has a compositional inverse, and the *Möbius function* of a multiorder is defined as the compositional inverse of the zeta function (constant-one) in its incidence algebra.

:::

## History {#History}

Multicategories were first introduced under that name by [Jim Lambek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Lambek) in "Deductive systems and categories II" (1969).[^o12] He mentions (p. 108) that he was "told that multicategories have also been studied by [\[Jean\] Benabou](wikipedia://en/70081438) and [\[Pierre\] Cartier](wikipedia://en/1470549)", and indeed Leinster opines that "the idea might have occurred to anyone who knew what both a category and a multilinear map were".p. 63
