# Coxeter element

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Coxeter element"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Coxeter element** is an element of an irreducible [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004) which is a product of all simple reflections. The product depends on the order in which they are taken, but different orderings produce [conjugate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugation_%28group_theory%29) elements, which have the same [order](wikipedia://en/493527). This order is known as the **Coxeter number**. They are named after British-Canadian geometer [H.S.M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328), who introduced the groups in 1934 as abstractions of [reflection groups](wikipedia://en/2422023).[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Note that this article assumes a finite [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004). For infinite Coxeter groups, there are multiple [conjugacy classes](wikipedia://en/49176) of Coxeter elements, and they have infinite order.

There are many different ways to define the Coxeter number $h$ of an irreducible root system.

- The Coxeter number is the order of any **Coxeter element;**.
- The Coxeter number is $\tfrac{2m}{n},$ where $n$ is the rank, and $m$ is the number of reflections. In the crystallographic case, $m$ is half the number of [roots](wikipedia://en/277087); and $2m+n$ is the dimension of the corresponding semisimple [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944).
- If the highest root is $\sum m_i \alpha_i$ for simple roots $\alpha_{i}$, then the Coxeter number is $1 + \sum m_i.$
- The Coxeter number is the highest degree of a fundamental invariant of the Coxeter group acting on polynomials.

The invariants of the Coxeter group acting on polynomials form a polynomial algebra whose generators are the fundamental invariants; their degrees are given in the table above. Notice that if $m$ is a degree of a fundamental invariant then so is $h + 2 - m$.

The eigenvalues of a Coxeter element are the numbers $e^{2\pi i\frac{m-1}{h}}$ as $m$ runs through the degrees of the fundamental invariants. Since this starts with $m = 2$, these include the [primitive $h$th root of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_root_of_unity), $\zeta_h = e^{2\pi i\frac{1}{h}},$ which is important in the Coxeter plane, below.

The **dual Coxeter number** is 1 plus the sum of the coefficients of simple roots in the highest [short root](wikipedia://en/277087#properties_of_the_irreducible_root_systems) of the [dual root system](wikipedia://en/277087#the_dual_root_system).

:::

## Group order {#Group_order}

There are relations between the order $g$ of the Coxeter group and the Coxeter number $h$:[^o8]

$$\begin{aligned} {} [p]:& \quad \frac{2h}{g_p} = 1 \\[4pt] [p,q]:& \quad \frac{8}{g_{p,q}} = \frac{2}{p} + \frac{2}{q} -1 \\[4pt] [p,q,r]:& \quad \frac{64h}{g_{p,q,r}} = 12 - p - 2q - r + \frac{4}{p} + \frac{4}{r} \\[4pt] [p,q,r,s]:& \quad \frac{16}{g_{p,q,r,s}} = \frac{8}{g_{p,q,r}} + \frac{8}{g_{q,r,s}} + \frac{2}{ps} - \frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{q} - \frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{s} +1 \\[4pt] \vdots \qquad & \qquad \vdots \end{aligned}$$

For example, $[3,3,5]$ has $h = 30$:

$$\begin{aligned} &\frac{64 \times 30}{g_{3,3,5}} = 12 - 3 - 6 - 5 + \frac{4}{3} + \frac{4}{5} = \frac{2}{15}, \\[4pt] &\therefore g_{3,3,5} = \frac{1920\times 15}{2} = 960 \times 15 = 14400. \end{aligned}$$

## Coxeter elements {#Coxeter_elements}

Distinct Coxeter elements correspond to orientations of the Coxeter diagram (i.e. to Dynkin [quivers](wikipedia://en/355100)): the simple reflections corresponding to source vertices are written first, downstream vertices later, and sinks last. (The choice of order among non-adjacent vertices is irrelevant, since they correspond to commuting reflections.) A special choice is the alternating orientation, in which the simple reflections are partitioned into two sets of non-adjacent vertices, and all edges are oriented from the first to the second set.[^o10] The alternating orientation produces a special Coxeter element $w$ satisfying $w^{h/2}= w_0,$ where $w_{0}$ is the [longest element](wikipedia://en/5355571), provided the Coxeter number $h$ is even.

For $A_{n-1} \cong S_n,$ the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) on $n$ elements, Coxeter elements are certain $n$-cycles: the product of simple reflections $(1,2) (2,3) \cdots (n-1,n)$ is the Coxeter element $(1,2,3,\dots, n)$.[^o11] For $n$ even, the alternating orientation Coxeter element is:

$$(1,2)(3,4)\cdots (2,3)(4,5) \cdots = (2,4,6,\ldots,n{-}2,n, n{-}1,n{-}3,\ldots,5,3,1).$$

There are $2^{n-2}$ distinct Coxeter elements among the $(n{-}1)!$ $n$-cycles.

The [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) $\operatorname{Dih}_{p}$ is generated by two reflections that form an angle of $\tfrac{2\pi}{2p},$ and thus the two Coxeter elements are their product in either order, which is a rotation by $\pm \tfrac{2\pi}{p}.$

## Coxeter plane {#Coxeter_plane}

*Image: E8Petrie.svg.* Projection of $E_{8}$ root system onto Coxeter plane, showing 30-fold symmetry.

For a given Coxeter element $w$, there is a unique plane $P$ on which $w$ acts by rotation by $\tfrac{2\pi}{h}.$ This is called the **Coxeter plane**[^o13] and is the plane on which $P$ has eigenvalues $e^{2\pi i\frac{1}{h}}$ and $e^{-2\pi i\frac{1}{h}} = e^{2\pi i\frac{h-1}{h}}.$[^o14] This plane was first systematically studied in (Coxeter 1948),[^o16] and subsequently used in (Steinberg 1959) to provide uniform proofs about properties of Coxeter elements.[^o16]

The Coxeter plane is often used to draw diagrams of higher-dimensional polytopes and root systems – the vertices and edges of the polytope, or roots (and some edges connecting these) are [orthogonally projected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_projection) onto the Coxeter plane, yielding a [Petrie polygon](wikipedia://en/18571869) with $h$-fold rotational symmetry.[^o18] For root systems, no root maps to zero, corresponding to the Coxeter element not fixing any root or rather axis (not having eigenvalue 1 or −1), so the projections of orbits under $w$ form $h$-fold circular arrangements[^o18] and there is an empty center, as in the $E_{8}$ diagram at above right. For polytopes, a vertex may map to zero, as depicted below. Projections onto the Coxeter plane are depicted below for the [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905).

In three dimensions, the symmetry of a [regular polyhedron](wikipedia://en/333299), $\{p, q\},$ with one directed Petrie polygon marked, defined as a composite of 3 reflections, has [rotoinversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotoinversion) symmetry $S_{h}$, $[2^{+},h^{+}]$, order $h$. Adding a mirror, the symmetry can be doubled to antiprismatic symmetry, $D_{hd}$, $[2^{+},h]$, order $2h$. In orthogonal 2D projection, this becomes [dihedral symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihedral_symmetry), $\operatorname{Dih}_{h}$, $[h]$, order $2h$.
