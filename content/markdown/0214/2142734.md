# Notation in probability and statistics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Notation in probability and statistics"}

[Probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) have some commonly used conventions, in addition to standard [mathematical notation](wikipedia://en/277184) and [mathematical symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Table_of_mathematical_symbols).

:::

## Probability theory {#Probability_theory}

- [Random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) are usually written in [upper case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_case) Roman letters, such as $X$ or $Y$ and so on. Random variables, in this context, usually refer to something in words, such as "the height of a subject" for a continuous variable, or "the number of cars in the school car park" for a discrete variable, or "the colour of the next bicycle" for a categorical variable. They do not represent a single number or a single category. For instance, if $P(X = x)$ is written, then it represents the probability that a particular realisation of a random variable (e.g., height, number of cars, or bicycle colour), *X*, would be equal to a particular value or category (e.g., 1.735 m, 52, or purple), $x$. It is important that $X$ and $x$ are not confused into meaning the same thing. $X$ is an idea, $x$ is a value. Clearly they are related, but they do not have identical meanings.
- Particular realisations of a random variable are written in corresponding [lower case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_case) letters. For example, $x_1,x_2, \ldots,x_n$ could be a [sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_sample) corresponding to the random variable $X$. A cumulative probability is formally written $P(X\le x)$ to distinguish the random variable from its realization.[^o3]
- The probability is sometimes written $\mathbb{P}$ to distinguish it from other functions and measure *P* to avoid having to define "*P* is a probability" and $\mathbb{P}(X\in A)$ is short for $P(\{\omega \in\Omega: X(\omega) \in A\})$, where $\Omega$ is the sample space, $X$ is a random variable that is a function of $\omega$ (i.e., it depends upon $\omega$), and $\omega$ is some outcome of interest within the domain specified by $\Omega$ (say, a particular height, or a particular colour of a car). $\Pr(A)$ notation is used alternatively.
- $\mathbb{P}(A \cap B)$ or $\mathbb{P}[B \cap A]$ indicates the probability that events *A* and *B* both occur. The [joint probability distribution](wikipedia://en/879637) of random variables *X* and *Y* is denoted as $P(X, Y)$, while joint probability mass function or probability density function as $f(x, y)$ and joint cumulative distribution function as $F(x, y)$.
- $\mathbb{P}(A \cup B)$ or $\mathbb{P}[B \cup A]$ indicates the probability of either event *A* or event *B* occurring ("or" in this case means [one or the other or both](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusive_or)).
- [σ-algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-algebra) are usually written with uppercase [calligraphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calligraphy) (e.g. $\mathcal F$ for the set of sets on which we define the probability *P*)
- [Probability density functions](wikipedia://en/43487) (pdfs) and [probability mass functions](wikipedia://en/154725) are denoted by lowercase letters, e.g. $f(x)$, or $f_X(x)$.
- [Cumulative distribution functions](wikipedia://en/5793) (cdfs) are denoted by uppercase letters, e.g. $F(x)$, or $F_X(x)$.
- [Survival functions](wikipedia://en/30876757) or complementary cumulative distribution functions are often denoted by placing an [overbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overbar) over the symbol for the cumulative:$\overline{F}(x) =1-F(x)$, or denoted as $S(x)$,
- In particular, the pdf of the [standard normal distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal_distribution) is denoted by $\varphi(z)$, and its cdf by $\Phi(z)$.
- Some common operators:
  - $\mathrm{E}[X]$: [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) of *X*
  - $\operatorname{var}[X]$: [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) of *X*
  - $\operatorname{cov}[X,Y]$: [covariance](wikipedia://en/157059) of *X* and *Y*
- X is independent of Y is often written $X \perp Y$ or $X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y$, and X is independent of Y given W is often written

$X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \,|\, W$ or

$$X \perp Y \,|\, W$$

- $\textstyle P(A\mid B)$, the *[conditional probability](wikipedia://en/24104134)*, is the probability of $\textstyle A$ *given* $\textstyle B$ [^o4]

## Statistics {#Statistics}

- Greek letters (e.g. *θ*, *β*) are commonly used to denote unknown parameters (population parameters).[^o6]
- Some commonly used symbols for [population](wikipedia://en/27585) or [distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) [parameters](wikipedia://en/413291) are given below:
  - the population mean $\mu$,
  - the population variance $\sigma^2$,
  - the population standard deviation *$\sigma$*,
  - the population [correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearson_product-moment_correlation_coefficient) *$\rho$*,
  - the population [cumulants](wikipedia://en/359684) *$\kappa_r$*,
- A tilde (~) denotes "has the probability distribution of".
- Placing a hat, or caret (also known as a circumflex), over a true parameter denotes an [estimator](wikipedia://en/10043) of it, e.g., $\widehat{\theta}$ is an estimator for $\theta$.
- The [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612) of a series of values $x_1,x_2, \ldots,x_n$ is often denoted by placing an "[overbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overbar)" over the symbol, e.g. $\bar{x}$, pronounced "$x$ bar".
- Some commonly used symbols for [sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_%28statistics%29) statistics are given below:
  - the [sample mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_mean) $\bar{x}$,
  - the [sample variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_variance) $s^2$,
  - the [sample standard deviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_standard_deviation) *$s$*,
  - the [sample correlation coefficient](wikipedia://en/221708) *$r$*,
  - the sample cumulants $k_r$.
- $x_{(k)}$ is used for the $k^\text{th}$ [order statistic](wikipedia://en/160986), where $x_{(1)}$ is the sample minimum and $x_{(n)}$ is the sample maximum from a total sample size $n$.[^o7]

## Critical values {#Critical_values}

The *α*-level upper [critical value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_value_%28statistics%29) of a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) is the value exceeded with probability $\alpha$, that is, the value $x_\alpha$ such that $F(x_\alpha) = 1-\alpha$, where $F$ is the cumulative distribution function. There are standard notations for the upper critical values of some commonly used distributions in statistics:

- $z_\alpha$ or $z(\alpha)$ for the [standard normal distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal_distribution)
- $t_{\alpha,\nu}$ or $t(\alpha,\nu)$ for the [*t*-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375) with $\nu$ [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321606)
- ${\chi_{\alpha,\nu}}^2$ or ${\chi}^{2}(\alpha,\nu)$ for the [chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/113424) with $\nu$ degrees of freedom
- $F_{\alpha,\nu_1,\nu_2}$ or $F(\alpha,\nu_1,\nu_2)$ for the [F-distribution](wikipedia://en/332116) with $\nu_1$ and $\nu_2$ degrees of freedom

## Linear algebra {#Linear_algebra}

- [Matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) are usually denoted by boldface capital letters, e.g. $\mathbf{A}$.
- [Column vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_vector) are usually denoted by boldface lowercase letters, e.g. **$\mathbf{x}$**.
- The [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) operator is denoted by either a superscript T (e.g. **$\mathbf{A}^\mathrm{T}$**) or a [prime symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_%28symbol%29) (e.g. **$\mathbf{A}'$**).
- A [row vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Row_vector) is written as the transpose of a column vector, e.g. **$\mathbf{x}^\mathrm{T}$** or **$\mathbf{x}'$**.

## Abbreviations {#Abbreviations}

Common abbreviations include:

- **a.e.** [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688)
- **a.s.** [almost surely](wikipedia://en/351908)
- **cdf** [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793)
- **cmf** [cumulative mass function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumulative_mass_function)
- **df** [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321606) (also $\nu$)
- **i.i.d.** [independent and identically distributed](wikipedia://en/30876902)
- **pdf** [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487)
- **pmf** [probability mass function](wikipedia://en/154725)
- **r.v.** [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685)
- **w.p.** with probability; **wp1** [with probability 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/With_probability_1)
- **i.o.** infinitely often, i.e. $\{ A_n\text{ i.o.} \} = \bigcap_N\bigcup_{n\geq N} A_n$
- **ult.** ultimately, i.e. $\{ A_n \text{ ult.} \} = \bigcup_N\bigcap_{n\geq N} A_n$
