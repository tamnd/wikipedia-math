# Point process

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Point process"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) and [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), a **point process** or **point field** is a set of a random number of [mathematical points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_%28mathematics%29) randomly located on a mathematical space such as the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) or [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697).[^o2][^o3]

:::

Point processes on the real line form an important special case that is particularly amenable to study,[^o4] because the points are ordered in a natural way, and the whole point process can be described completely by the (random) intervals between the points. These point processes are frequently used as models for random events in time, such as the arrival of customers in a queue ([queueing theory](wikipedia://en/50578)), of impulses in a neuron ([computational neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_neuroscience)), particles in a [Geiger counter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geiger_counter), location of radio stations in a [telecommunication network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunication_network)[^o5] or of searches on the [world-wide web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World-wide_web).

General point processes on a Euclidean space can be used for [spatial data analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatial_data_analysis),[^o6][^o7] which is of interest in such diverse disciplines as forestry, plant ecology, epidemiology, geography, seismology, materials science, astronomy, telecommunications, computational neuroscience,[^o8] economics[^o9] and others.

::: {#o11 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Conventions"}

## Conventions {#Conventions}

Since point processes were historically developed by different communities, there are different mathematical interpretations of a point process, such as a [random counting measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_counting_measure) or a random set,[^o12][^o13] and different notations. The notations are described in detail on the [point process notation](wikipedia://en/40765261) page.

Some authors regard a point process and stochastic process as two different objects such that a point process is a random object that arises from or is associated with a stochastic process,[^o14][^o15] though it has been remarked that the difference between point processes and stochastic processes is not clear.[^o15] Others consider a point process as a stochastic process, where the process is indexed by sets of the underlying space on which it is defined, such as the real line or $n$-dimensional Euclidean space.[^o16][^o17] Other stochastic processes such as renewal and counting processes are studied in the theory of point processes.[^o18][^o15] Sometimes the term "point process" is not preferred, as historically the word "process" denoted an evolution of some system in time, so point process is also called a random point field.[^o19]

:::

## Mathematics {#Mathematics}

In mathematics, a point process is a [random element](wikipedia://en/5215393) whose values are "point patterns" on a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) *S*. While in the exact mathematical definition a point pattern is specified as a [locally finite](wikipedia://en/6621919) [counting measure](wikipedia://en/191786), it is sufficient for more applied purposes to think of a point pattern as a [countable](wikipedia://en/6026) subset of *S* that has no [limit points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point).

::: {#o22 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

To define general point processes, we start with a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$, and a measurable space $(S, \mathcal{S})$ where $S$ is a [locally compact](wikipedia://en/48632) [second countable](wikipedia://en/901459) [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637) and $\mathcal{S}$ is its [Borel σ-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_sigma-algebra). Consider now an integer-valued locally finite kernel $\xi$ from $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$ into $(S, \mathcal{S})$, that is, a mapping $\Omega \times \mathcal{S} \mapsto \mathbb{Z}_{+}$ such that:

1. For every $\omega \in \Omega$, $\xi(\omega, \cdot)$ is a (integer-valued) [locally finite measure](wikipedia://en/6621919) on $S$.
1. For every $B \in \mathcal{S}$, $\xi(\cdot, B): \Omega \to \mathbb{Z}_+$ is a random variable over $\mathbb{Z}_+$.

This kernel defines a [random measure](wikipedia://en/10040846) in the following way. We would like to think of $\xi$ as defining a mapping which maps $\omega \in \Omega$ to a measure $\xi_\omega \in \mathcal{M}(\mathcal{S})$ (namely, $\Omega \mapsto \mathcal{M}(\mathcal{S})$), where $\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{S})$ is the set of all locally finite measures on $S$. Now, to make this mapping measurable, we need to define a $\sigma$-field over $\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{S})$. This $\sigma$-field is constructed as the minimal algebra so that all evaluation maps of the form $\pi_B: \mu \mapsto \mu(B)$, where $B \in \mathcal{S}$ is [relatively compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_compact_subset), are measurable. Equipped with this $\sigma$-field, then $\xi$ is a random element, where for every $\omega \in \Omega$, $\xi_\omega$ is a locally finite measure over $S$.

Now, by *a point process* on $S$ we simply mean *an integer-valued random measure* (or equivalently, integer-valued kernel) $\xi$ constructed as above. The most common example for the state space *S* is the Euclidean space **R**<sup>*n*</sup> or a subset thereof, where a particularly interesting special case is given by the real half-line \[0,∞). However, point processes are not limited to these examples and may among other things also be used if the points are themselves compact subsets of **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, in which case *ξ* is usually referred to as a *particle process*.

Despite the name *point process* since *S* might not be a subset of the real line, as it might suggest that ξ is a [stochastic process](wikipedia://en/47895).

:::

::: {#o24 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Representation"}

### Representation {#Representation}

Every instance (or event) of a point process ξ can be represented as

$$\xi=\sum_{i=1}^n \delta_{X_i},$$

where $\delta$ denotes the [Dirac measure](wikipedia://en/1019002), *n* is an integer-valued random variable and $X_i$ are random elements of *S*. If $X_i$'s are [almost surely](wikipedia://en/351908) distinct (or equivalently, almost surely $\xi(x) \leq 1$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$), then the point process is known as *[simple](wikipedia://en/57430168)*.

Another different but useful representation of an event (an event in the event space, i.e. a series of points) is the counting notation, where each instance is represented as an $N(t)$ function, a continuous function which takes an interval:

$$N\begin{cases}{\mathbb{R}^2}\to{\mathbb{N}}\\ (t_1, t_2) \mapsto \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \xi(t) \, dt \end{cases}$$

which is the number of events in the observation interval $(t_1,t_2]$. It is sometimes denoted by $N_{t_1,t_2}$, and $N_T$ or $N(T)$ mean $N_{0,T}$.

:::

### Expectation measure {#Expectation_measure}

The *expectation measure* *Eξ* (also known as *mean measure*) of a point process ξ is a measure on *S* that assigns to every Borel subset *B* of *S* the expected number of points of *ξ* in *B*. That is,

$$E \xi (B) := E \bigl( \xi(B) \bigr) \quad \text{for every } B \in \mathcal{B}.$$

### Laplace functional {#Laplace_functional}

The *Laplace functional* $\Psi_{N}(f)$ of a point process *N* is a map from the set of all positive valued functions *f* on the state space of *N*, to $[0,\infty)$ defined as follows:

$$\Psi_N(f)=E[\exp(-N(f))]$$

They play a similar role as the [characteristic functions](wikipedia://en/3229401) for [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685). One important theorem says that: two point processes have the same law if their Laplace functionals are equal.

::: {#o28 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Intensity function"}

### Moment measure {#Moment_measure}

The $n$th power of a point process, $\xi^n,$ is defined on the product space $S^n$ as follows :

$$\xi^n(A_1 \times \cdots \times A_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n \xi(A_i)$$

By [monotone class theorem](wikipedia://en/4719864), this uniquely defines the product measure on $(S^n,B(S^n)).$ The expectation $E \xi^n(\cdot)$ is called the $n$ th [moment measure](wikipedia://en/40969212). The first moment measure is the mean measure.

Let $S = \mathbb{R}^d$ . The *joint intensities* of a point process $\xi$ w.r.t. the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) are functions $\rho^{(k)} :(\mathbb{R}^d)^k \to [0,\infty)$ such that for any disjoint bounded Borel subsets $B_1,\ldots,B_k$

$$E\left(\prod_i \xi(B_i)\right) = \int_{B_1 \times \cdots \times B_k} \rho^{(k)}(x_1,\ldots,x_k) \, dx_1\cdots dx_k .$$

Joint intensities do not always exist for point processes. Given that [moments](wikipedia://en/368684) of a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) determine the random variable in many cases, a similar result is to be expected for joint intensities. Indeed, this has been shown in many cases.[^o3]

:::

### Stationarity {#Stationarity}

A point process $\xi \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ is said to be *stationary* if $\xi + x := \sum_{i=1}^N \delta_{X_i + x}$ has the same distribution as $\xi$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}^d.$ For a stationary point process, the mean measure $E \xi (\cdot) = \lambda \|\cdot\|$ for some constant $\lambda \geq 0$ and where $\|\cdot\|$ stands for the Lebesgue measure. This $\lambda$ is called the *intensity* of the point process. A stationary point process on $\mathbb{R}^d$ has almost surely either 0 or an infinite number of points in total. For more on stationary point processes and random measure, refer to Chapter 12 of Daley & Vere-Jones.[^o3] Stationarity has been defined and studied for point processes in more general spaces than $\mathbb{R}^d$.

### Transformations {#Transformations}

A point process transformation is a function that maps a point process to another point process.

::: {#o32 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

We shall see some examples of point processes in $\mathbb{R}^d.$

### Poisson point process {#Poisson_point_process}

The simplest and most ubiquitous example of a point process is the *Poisson point process*, which is a spatial generalisation of the [Poisson process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_process). A Poisson (counting) process on the line can be characterised by two properties : the number of points (or events) in disjoint intervals are independent and have a [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144). A Poisson point process can also be defined using these two properties. Namely, we say that a point process $\xi$ is a Poisson point process if the following two conditions hold

\1) $\xi(B_1),\ldots,\xi(B_n)$ are independent for disjoint subsets $B_1,\ldots,B_n.$

\2) For any bounded subset $B$, $\xi(B)$ has a [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) with parameter $\lambda \|B\|,$ where $\|\cdot\|$ denotes the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198).

The two conditions can be combined and written as follows : For any disjoint bounded subsets $B_1,\ldots,B_n$ and non-negative integers $k_1,\ldots,k_n$ we have that

$$\Pr[\xi(B_i) = k_i, 1 \leq i \leq n] = \prod_i e^{-\lambda \|B_i\|}\frac{(\lambda \|B_i\|)^{k_i}}{k_i!}.$$

The constant $\lambda$ is called the intensity of the Poisson point process. Note that the Poisson point process is characterised by the single parameter $\lambda.$ It is a simple, stationary point process. To be more specific one calls the above point process a homogeneous Poisson point process. An [inhomogeneous Poisson process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inhomogeneous_Poisson_process) is defined as above but by replacing $\lambda \|B\|$ with $\int_B\lambda(x) \, dx$ where $\lambda$ is a non-negative function on $\mathbb{R}^d.$

### Cox point process {#Cox_point_process}

A [Cox process](wikipedia://en/6196078) (named after [Sir David Cox](wikipedia://en/1990771)) is a generalisation of the Poisson point process, in that we use [random measures](wikipedia://en/10040846) in place of $\lambda \|B\|$. More formally, let $\Lambda$ be a [random measure](wikipedia://en/10040846). A Cox point process driven by the [random measure](wikipedia://en/10040846) $\Lambda$ is the point process $\xi$ with the following two properties :

1. Given $\Lambda(\cdot)$, $\xi(B)$ is Poisson distributed with parameter $\Lambda(B)$ for any bounded subset $B.$
1. For any finite collection of disjoint subsets $B_1,\ldots,B_n$ and conditioned on $\Lambda(B_1),\ldots,\Lambda(B_n),$ we have that $\xi(B_1),\ldots,\xi(B_n)$ are independent.

It is easy to see that Poisson point process (homogeneous and inhomogeneous) follow as special cases of Cox point processes. The mean measure of a Cox point process is $E \xi(\cdot) = E \Lambda(\cdot)$ and thus in the special case of a Poisson point process, it is $\lambda\|\cdot\|.$

For a Cox point process, $\Lambda(\cdot)$ is called the *intensity measure*. Further, if $\Lambda(\cdot)$ has a (random) density ([Radon–Nikodym derivative](wikipedia://en/338746)) $\lambda(\cdot)$ i.e.,

$$\Lambda(B) \,\stackrel{\text{a.s.}}{=}\, \int_B \lambda(x) \, dx,$$

then $\lambda(\cdot)$ is called the *intensity field* of the Cox point process. Stationarity of the intensity measures or intensity fields imply the stationarity of the corresponding Cox point processes.

There have been many specific classes of Cox point processes that have been studied in detail such as:

- Log-Gaussian Cox point processes:[^o35] $\lambda(y) = \exp(X(y))$ for a [Gaussian random field](wikipedia://en/1803858) $X(\cdot)$
- Shot noise Cox point processes:,[^o36] $\lambda(y)= \sum_{X \in \Phi} h(X,y)$ for a Poisson point process $\Phi(\cdot)$ and kernel $h(\cdot , \cdot)$
- Generalised shot noise Cox point processes:[^o37] $\lambda(y)= \sum_{X \in \Phi} h(X,y)$ for a point process $\Phi(\cdot)$ and kernel $h(\cdot , \cdot)$
- Lévy based Cox point processes:[^o38] $\lambda(y)= \int h(x,y)L(dx)$ for a Lévy basis $L(\cdot)$ and kernel $h(\cdot , \cdot)$, and
- Permanental Cox point processes:[^o39] $\lambda(y) = X_1^2(y) + \cdots + X_k^2(y)$ for *k* independent Gaussian random fields $X_i(\cdot)$'s
- Sigmoidal Gaussian Cox point processes:[^o40] $\lambda(y) = \lambda^{\star}/(1+\exp(-X(y)))$ for a Gaussian random field $X(\cdot)$ and random $\lambda^\star > 0$

By Jensen's inequality, one can verify that Cox point processes satisfy the following inequality: for all bounded Borel subsets $B$,

$$\operatorname{Var}(\xi(B)) \geq \operatorname{Var}(\xi_{\alpha}(B)) ,$$

where $\xi_\alpha$ stands for a Poisson point process with intensity measure $\alpha(\cdot) := E \xi(\cdot) = E \Lambda(\cdot).$ Thus points are distributed with greater variability in a Cox point process compared to a Poisson point process. This is sometimes called *clustering* or *attractive property* of the Cox point process.

### Determinantal point processes {#Determinantal_point_processes}

An important class of point processes, with applications to [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), [random matrix theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_matrix_theory), and [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170), is that of [determinantal point processes](wikipedia://en/31225305).[^o42]

### Hawkes (self-exciting) processes {#Hawkes_(self-exciting)_processes}

A Hawkes process $N_t$, also known as a self-exciting counting process, is a simple point process whose conditional intensity can be expressed as

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda (t) & = \mu (t) + \int_{-\infty}^t \nu (t - s) \, dN_s\\[5pt] & = \mu (t) + \sum_{T_k < t} \nu (t - T_k) \end{aligned}$$

where $\nu : \mathbb{R}^+ \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^+$ is a kernel function which expresses the positive influence of past events $T_i$ on the current value of the intensity process $\lambda (t)$, $\mu (t)$ is a possibly non-stationary function representing the expected, predictable, or deterministic part of the intensity, and $\{ T_i : T_i < T_{i + 1} \} \in \mathbb{R}$ is the time of occurrence of the *i*-th event of the process.[^o44]

### Geometric processes {#Geometric_processes}

Given a sequence of non-negative random variables $\{X_k,k=1,2, \dots\}$, if they are independent and the cdf of $X_k$ is given by $F(a^{k-1}x)$ for $k=1,2, \dots$, where $a$ is a positive constant, then $\{X_k,k=1,2,\ldots\}$ is called a geometric process (GP).[^o46]

The geometric process has several extensions, including the *α- series process*[^o47] and the *doubly geometric process*.[^o48]

:::

## Point processes on the real half-line {#Point_processes_on_the_real_half-line}

Historically the first point processes that were studied had the real half line **R**<sub>\+</sub> = \[0,∞) as their state space, which in this context is usually interpreted as time. These studies were motivated by the wish to model telecommunication systems,[^o50] in which the points represented events in time, such as calls to a telephone exchange.

Point processes on **R**<sub>\+</sub> are typically described by giving the sequence of their (random) inter-event times (*T*<sub>1</sub>, *T*<sub>2</sub>, ...), from which the actual sequence (*X*<sub>1</sub>, *X*<sub>2</sub>, ...) of event times can be obtained as

$$X_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} T_j \quad \text{for } k \geq 1.$$

If the inter-event times are independent and identically distributed, the point process obtained is called a [*renewal process*](wikipedia://en/1945387).

::: {#o52 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Conditional intensity function"}

### Intensity of a point process {#Intensity_of_a_point_process}

The *intensity* *λ*(*t* | *H*<sub>*t*</sub>) of a point process on the real half-line with respect to a filtration *H*<sub>*t*</sub> is defined as

$$\lambda(t \mid H_t)=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\Pr(\text{One event occurs in the time-interval}\,[t,t+\Delta t] \mid H_t) ,$$

*H*<sub>*t*</sub> can denote the history of event-point times preceding time *t* but can also correspond to other filtrations (for example in the case of a Cox process).

In the $N(t)$-notation, this can be written in a more compact form:

$$\lambda(t \mid H_t)=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\Pr(N(t+\Delta t)-N(t)=1 \mid H_t).$$

The *compensator* of a point process, also known as the *dual-predictable projection*, is the integrated conditional intensity function defined by

$$\Lambda (s, u) = \int_s^u \lambda (t \mid H_t) \, \mathrm{d} t$$

:::

## Related functions {#Related_functions}

### Papangelou intensity function {#Papangelou_intensity_function}

The *Papangelou intensity function* of a point process $N$ in the $n$-dimensional Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$ is defined as

$$\lambda_p(x)=\lim_{\delta \to 0}\frac{1}{|B_\delta (x)|}{P}\{\text{One event occurs in } \,B_\delta(x)\mid \sigma[N(\mathbb{R}^n \setminus B_\delta(x))] \} ,$$

where $B_\delta (x)$ is the ball centered at $x$ of a radius $\delta$, and $\sigma[N(\mathbb{R}^n \setminus B_\delta(x))]$ denotes the information of the point process $N$ outside $B_\delta(x)$.

### Likelihood function {#Likelihood_function}

The logarithmic likelihood of a parameterized simple point process conditional upon some observed data is written as

$\ln \mathcal{L} (N (t)_{t \in [0, T]})=\int_0^T (1 - \lambda (s)) \, ds + \int_0^T \ln \lambda (s) \, dN_s$[^o56]

## Point processes in spatial statistics {#Point_processes_in_spatial_statistics}

The analysis of point pattern data in a compact subset *S* of **R**<sup>*n*</sup> is a major object of study within [spatial statistics](wikipedia://en/9467349). Such data appear in a broad range of disciplines,[^o58] amongst which are

- forestry and plant ecology (positions of trees or plants in general)
- epidemiology (home locations of infected patients)
- zoology (burrows or nests of animals)
- geography (positions of human settlements, towns or cities)
- seismology (epicenters of earthquakes)
- materials science (positions of defects in industrial materials)
- astronomy (locations of stars or galaxies)
- computational neuroscience (spikes of neurons).

The need to use point processes to model these kinds of data lies in their inherent spatial structure. Accordingly, a first question of interest is often whether the given data exhibit [complete spatial randomness](wikipedia://en/21447866) (i.e. are a realization of a spatial [Poisson process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_process)) as opposed to exhibiting either spatial aggregation or spatial inhibition.

In contrast, many datasets considered in classical [multivariate statistics](wikipedia://en/19384) consist of independently generated datapoints that may be governed by one or several covariates (typically non-spatial).

Apart from the applications in spatial statistics, point processes are one of the fundamental objects in [stochastic geometry](wikipedia://en/22037813). Research has also focussed extensively on various models built on point processes such as [Voronoi tessellations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voronoi_tessellation), [random geometric graphs](wikipedia://en/12106740), and [Boolean models](wikipedia://en/22025356).
