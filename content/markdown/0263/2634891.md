# Alastair Denniston

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Alastair Denniston"}

[Commander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander_%28Royal_Navy%29) **Alexander Guthrie Denniston** (1 December 1881 – 1 January 1961) was a Scottish codebreaker, deputy head of the [Government Code and Cypher School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Code_and_Cypher_School) (GC&CS) and [hockey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_hockey) player.[^o3][^o4] Denniston was appointed operational head of GC&CS in 1919 and remained so until February 1942.[^o5][^o6]

:::

## Early life {#Early_life}

Denniston was born in [Greenock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenock), [Renfrewshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renfrewshire_%28historic%29), the son of a medical practitioner.[^o5] He studied at the [University of Bonn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bonn) and the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris).[^o5] Denniston was a member of the Scottish Olympic hockey team in [1908](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1908_Summer_Olympics) and won a bronze medal. He played as a half-back, and his club team was listed as [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh). In the [IOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Olympic_Committee)'s official 1908 report, he is listed as "Dennistoun" rather than Denniston.[^o4]

## First World War and after {#First_World_War_and_after}

In 1914, Denniston helped form [Room 40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_40) in the [Admiralty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiralty_%28United_Kingdom%29), an organisation responsible for intercepting and decrypting enemy messages. In 1917, he married a fellow Room 40 worker, Dorothy Mary Gilliat.[^o5]

After the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War), Denniston, recognising the strategic importance of codebreaking, kept the Room 40 activity functioning.[^o11] Room 40 was merged with its counterpart in the Army, [MI1b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MI1b) in 1919, renamed the [Government Code and Cypher School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Code_and_Cypher_School) (GC&CS) in 1920 and transferred from the Navy to the Foreign Office. Denniston was chosen to run the new organisation.[^o12]

With the rise of [Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler), Denniston began making preparations. Following the practice of his superiors at Room 40, he contacted scientists from Oxford and Cambridge (including [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208) and [Gordon Welchman](wikipedia://en/2385693)), asking if they would be willing to serve if war broke out. [Bletchley Park](wikipedia://en/4037) was chosen by MI6 chief Admiral [Hugh Sinclair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Sinclair) as the location for the codebreaking effort because it was at a [rail junction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bletchley_railway_station) on the west coast main line 47 mi north of London with good rail connections to Oxford and Cambridge. Sinclair acquired the Bletchley Park property, and Denniston was assigned to prepare the site and design the huts to be built on the grounds. The GC&CS moved to the new location in August 1939, just before the [invasion of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Poland) and the start of the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War). Its name changed to [Government Communications Headquarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Communications_Headquarters) (GCHQ).[^o11]

On 26 July 1939, five weeks before the outbreak of war, Denniston was one of three Britons (along with [Dilly Knox](wikipedia://en/905870) and Humphrey Sandwith) who participated in the trilateral Polish-French-British conference held in the [Kabaty Woods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabaty_Woods) south of [Warsaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw), at which the [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland) [Biuro Szyfrów](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biuro_Szyfrów) (Cipher Bureau) initiated the French and British into the [decryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decryption) of [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany) military [Enigma](wikipedia://en/9256) [ciphers](wikipedia://en/5244).[^o13]

## Second World War {#Second_World_War}

Denniston remained in command until he was admitted to hospital in June 1940 for a [bladder stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bladder_stone). Although ill, he flew to the United States in 1941 to make contact with American cryptographers including [William Friedman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Friedman). Denniston returned to Bletchley Park for a while but moved to London later in 1941 to work on diplomatic traffic.[^o11]

Despite his knowledge of the success of Polish cryptologists against Enigma, Denniston shared the general pessimism about the prospects of breaking the more complex Naval Enigma encryption until as late as the summer of 1940, having told the Head of Naval Section at Bletchley: "You know, the Germans don't mean you to read their stuff, and I don't expect you ever will."[^o15] The advent of [Banburismus](wikipedia://en/1530481) shortly afterwards showed his pessimism to be misplaced.

In October 1941, the originator of the technique, [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208), along with fellow senior cryptologists [Gordon Welchman](wikipedia://en/2385693), [Stuart Milner-Barry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Milner-Barry) and [Hugh Alexander](wikipedia://en/2784711) wrote to Churchill, over the head of Denniston, to alert Churchill to the fact that a shortage of staff at Bletchley Park was preventing them from deciphering many messages. An addition of personnel, small by military standards, could make a big difference to the effectiveness of the fighting effort. The slow response to previous requests had convinced them that the strategic value of their work was not understood in the right quarters. In the letter, there was praise for the 'energy and foresight' of Commander [Edward Travis](wikipedia://en/1465928).[^o16]

Churchill reacted to the letter immediately, ordering "Action this day". Resources were transferred as fast as possible.[^o17]

In February 1942, GC&CS was reorganised. Travis, Denniston's second in command and chief of the Naval section, succeeded Denniston at Bletchley Park, overseeing the work on military codes and ciphers. When Travis took over, he "presided over an administrative revolution which at last brought the management of Intelligence into line with its mode of production".[^o16]

## Personal and postwar life {#Personal_and_postwar_life}

Denniston and his wife had two children: a son and daughter. Their son, [Robin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Denniston), was educated at [Westminster School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_School) and [Christ Church, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_Church,_Oxford). After Denniston's demotion and resulting decreased income, Robin's school fees were paid by benefactors. However, the Dennistons' daughter had to leave her school due to lack of funds.[^o19]

Denniston retired in 1945, and later taught French and Latin in [Leatherhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leatherhead).[^o5]

[William Friedman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Friedman), the American cryptographer who broke the Japanese [Purple code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_code), later wrote to Denniston's daughter: "Your father was a great man in whose debt all English-speaking people will remain for a very long time, if not forever. That so few should know exactly what he did ... is the sad part."[^o11]

Robin Denniston became a publisher. In 2007, he published *Thirty Secret Years*, a [biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biography) of his father that consolidated his reputation in GCHQ history.[^o19]

## Honours and awards {#Honours_and_awards}

- 2 January 1933 appointed a [Commander of the Order of the British Empire (CBE)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_British_Empire) *Commander Alexander Guthrie Denniston, O.B.E., R.N.V.R. Head of a Department, Foreign Office.*
