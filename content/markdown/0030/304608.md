# Polytrope

*Image: Polytropes.gif.* The normalized density as a function of scale length for a wide range of polytropic indices

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Polytrope"}

In [astrophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysics), a **polytrope** is a thermodynamic system with [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) dependent upon [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density), leaving only one independent state variable. A [polytropic process](wikipedia://en/4151504) is intermediate between an [isothermal](wikipedia://en/409951) process and [adiabatic](wikipedia://en/1419) one.[^o2] The dependence of pressure on density is a solution to the [Lane–Emden equation](wikipedia://en/341818):

$$P = K \rho^{(n+1)/n} = K \rho^{1 + 1/n},$$

where $P$ is pressure, $\rho$ is density and $K$ is a [constant](wikipedia://en/24758132) of [proportionality](wikipedia://en/81863).[^o2] The constant $n$ is known as the [polytropic index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytropic_index).[^o2]

:::

This relation need not be interpreted as an [equation of state](wikipedia://en/9908), which states *P* as a function of both ρ and *T* (the [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050)); however in the particular case described by the polytrope equation there are other additional relations between these three quantities, which together determine the equation. Thus, this is simply a relation that expresses an assumption about the change of pressure with [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) in terms of the change of density with radius, yielding a solution to the Lane–Emden equation.

Sometimes the word *polytrope* may refer to an equation of state that looks similar to the [thermodynamic](wikipedia://en/29952) relation above. It is preferable to refer to the [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid) itself (as opposed to the solution of the Lane–Emden equation) as a **polytropic fluid** or **polytropic gas**. Specifically, the polytropic gas is a gas for which the [specific heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_heat) is constant.[^o3][^o4] The equation of state of a polytropic fluid is general enough that such idealized fluids find wide use outside of the limited problem of polytropes.

The polytropic exponent (of a polytrope) has been shown to be equivalent to the pressure [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of the [bulk modulus](wikipedia://en/1092110)[^o5] where its relation to the [Murnaghan equation of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murnaghan_equation_of_state) has also been demonstrated. The polytrope relation is therefore best suited for relatively low-pressure (below 10<sup>7</sup> [Pa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_%28unit%29)) and high-pressure (over 10<sup>14</sup> Pa) conditions when the pressure derivative of the bulk modulus, which is equivalent to the polytrope index, is near constant.

## Example models by polytropic index {#Example_models_by_polytropic_index}

*Image: Polytrope3n.svg.* Density (normalized to average density) versus radius (normalized to external radius) for a polytrope with index n=3.

- An index $n = 0$ polytrope is often used to model [rocky planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_planet). The reason is that $n = 0$ polytrope has constant density, i.e., incompressible interior. This is a zero order approximation for rocky (solid/liquid) planets.
- [Neutron stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star) are well [modeled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_%28abstract%29) by polytropes with index between $n = 0.5$ and $n = 1$.
- A polytrope with index $n = 1.5$ is a good model for fully convective [star cores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_core)[^o3][^o7] (like those of [red giants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_giant)), [brown dwarfs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_dwarf), [giant gaseous planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_giant) (like [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter)). With this index, the polytropic exponent is 5/3, which is the [heat capacity ratio](wikipedia://en/1949009) (γ) for [monatomic gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monatomic_gas). For the interior of gaseous stars (consisting of either [ionized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionization) [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) or [helium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium)), this follows from an [ideal gas](wikipedia://en/1419#Ideal_gas_%28reversible_process%29) approximation for [natural convection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_convection) conditions.
- A polytrope with index $n = 1.5$ is also a good model for [white dwarfs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_dwarf) of low mass, according to the [equation of state](wikipedia://en/9908) of non-[relativistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_particle) [degenerate matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_matter).[^o8]
- A polytrope with index $n = 3$ is a good model for the cores of white dwarfs of higher masses, according to the equation of state of [relativistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_particle) [degenerate matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_matter).[^o8]
- A polytrope with index $n = 3$ is usually also used to model [main-sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_sequence) [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star) like the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun), at least in the [radiation zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_zone), corresponding to the [Eddington standard model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_zone#Eddington_stellar_model) of [stellar structure](wikipedia://en/1039124).[^o9]
- A polytrope with index $n = 5$ has an [infinite](wikipedia://en/20110824) radius. It corresponds to the simplest plausible model of a self-consistent stellar system, first studied by [Arthur Schuster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Schuster) in 1883, and it has an [exact solution](wikipedia://en/341818#For_n_=_5).
- A polytrope with index $n = \infty$ corresponds to what is called an *isothermal sphere*, that is an [isothermal](wikipedia://en/409951) [self-gravitating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-gravitation) sphere of gas, whose structure is identical to the structure of a collisionless system of stars like a [globular cluster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globular_cluster). This is because for an ideal gas, the temperature is proportional to ρ<sup>1/n</sup>, so infinite *n* corresponds to a constant temperature.

In general as the polytropic index increases, the density distribution is more heavily weighted toward the center ($r = 0$) of the body.
