# Max Black

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Max Black"}

**Max Black** (February 24, 1909 – August 27, 1988) was a Russian-born British-American philosopher who was a leading figure in [analytic philosophy](wikipedia://en/159211) in the years after [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II). He made contributions to the [philosophy of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_language), the [philosophy of mathematics](wikipedia://en/46439) and [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science), and the [philosophy of art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_art), also publishing studies of the work of philosophers such as [Frege](wikipedia://en/48416). His translation (with [Peter Geach](wikipedia://en/302884)) of Frege's published philosophical writing is a classic text.

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Black was born **Max Tcherny** on February 24, 1909, in [Baku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baku), then within the [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire) and now the capital of [Azerbaijan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azerbaijan), to a wealthy [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish) family. The family left Baku whilst he was very young, emigrating to England in 1912 after spending a short time in Paris, France. The family name was changed to Black (a direct translation of "Tcherny") in 1911 or 1912. A younger brother, [Misha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misha_Black), became an architect.[^o4][^o5]

He first attended a free school in [north London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_London), but at age nine was awarded a scholarship to [Dame Alice Owen's School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dame_Alice_Owen's_School), where he remained until the age of eighteen.[^o12][^o6]

He studied mathematics at [Queens' College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queens'_College,_Cambridge) at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge), where he was drawn to [analytic philosophy](wikipedia://en/159211). [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163), [Ludwig Wittgenstein](wikipedia://en/17741), [G. E. Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._E._Moore), and [Frank P. Ramsey](wikipedia://en/238285) were all at Cambridge at that time and were influential on much of Black's later thought.[^o7]

He graduated in 1930, and was awarded a fellowship for research at the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen) for a year.[^o8]

## Career {#Career}

From 1931 to 1936, Black was mathematics master at the [Royal Grammar School, Newcastle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Grammar_School,_Newcastle).[^o10]

His first book was *The Nature of Mathematics* (1933), a critical exposition of *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)* and a critical analysis of the [formalist](wikipedia://en/16258342) and [intuitionist](wikipedia://en/19513) schools of mathematics.[^o11]

Black lectured in mathematics at the [Institute of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Education) in London from 1936 to 1940.[^o10] During this time he also undertook graduate work at the [University of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_London), being awarded a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D.) in 1939 for his dissertation on theories of logical positivism.[^o12]

In 1940 he moved to the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) and joined the Philosophy Department at the [University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana–Champaign). In 1946, he accepted a professorship in philosophy at [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University). In 1948, he became a [naturalized citizen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalized_citizen) of the United States.[^o12]

Black became the Susan Linn Sage Professor of Philosophy and Humane Letters at Cornell in 1954, a position he held until his retirement in 1978.[^o7] He served as President of the Eastern Division of the American Philosophical Association in 1958–1959 and as President of the [International Institute of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Institute_of_Philosophy) 1981– 1984.[^o4]

He was elected a Fellow of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences) in 1963.[^o13]

Amongst the [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) dissertations he supervised was that of novelist [William H. Gass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_H._Gass).[^o14]

## Death and family {#Death_and_family}

Black was the elder brother of the architect [Misha Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misha_Black) and the public relations manager [Sam Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Black_%28public_relations%29).[^o5][^o16]

In 1933 he married Michal (or Mabel) Landesberg (1911–1985) with whom he had a daughter and a son.[^o6]

Black died in [Ithaca, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ithaca,_New_York) age 79, from a heart attack that followed surgery for cancer.[^o6]

## Philosophical contributions {#Philosophical_contributions}

Black made notable contributions to the metaphysics of [identity](wikipedia://en/89532). In a 1952 paper,[^o18] Black presents an objection to [Leibniz' law](wikipedia://en/630398) – the principle of the [identity of indiscernibles](wikipedia://en/630398) that states that no two distinct things can exactly resemble each other. He does so, as Peter Forrest notes, by suggesting "there could be a universe containing nothing but two [exactly resembling](wikipedia://en/66431011#Exact_similarity_and_identity) spheres" and that in "such a completely symmetrical universe the two spheres would be indiscernible".[^o19]

He is also known for his interaction theory of [metaphor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphor).[^o20]
