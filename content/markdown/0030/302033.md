# Frequency response

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Frequency response"}

In [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324), [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics) and [mechanical vibration](wikipedia://en/20646772), the **frequency response** of a system is the quantitative measure of the magnitude and [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29) of the output as a function of input frequency.[^o2] The frequency response is widely used in the design and analysis of systems, such as [audio equipment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio_equipment) and [control systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_system), where they simplify mathematical analysis by converting governing [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations) into [algebraic equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_equations). In an audio system, it may be used to minimize audible [distortion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distortion) by designing components (such as [microphones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microphones), [amplifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio_power_amplifier) and [loudspeakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loudspeakers)) so that the overall response is as flat (uniform) as possible across the system's [bandwidth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandwidth_%28signal_processing%29). In control systems, such as a vehicle's [cruise control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruise_control), it may be used to assess system [stability](wikipedia://en/5493795), often through the use of [Bode plots](wikipedia://en/155562). Systems with a specific frequency response can be designed using [analog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analog_filter) and [digital filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_filter).

:::

The frequency response characterizes systems in the [frequency domain](wikipedia://en/370346), just as the [impulse response](wikipedia://en/545825) characterizes systems in the [time domain](wikipedia://en/173419). In [linear systems](wikipedia://en/722503) (or as an approximation to a real system, neglecting second-order non-linear properties), either response completely describes the system, and thus there is a one-to-one correspondence: the frequency response is the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of the impulse response. The frequency response allows simpler analysis of cascaded systems such as [multistage amplifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistage_amplifier), as the response of the overall system can be found through multiplication of the individual stages' frequency responses (as opposed to [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) of the impulse response in the time domain). The frequency response is closely related to the [transfer function](wikipedia://en/31146) in linear systems, which is the [Laplace transform](wikipedia://en/18610) of the impulse response. They are equivalent when the real part $\sigma$ of the transfer function's complex variable $s = \sigma + j\omega$ is zero.[^o3]

In the context of mechanical vibration, the frequency response of a system is usually referred to as the **frequency response function** (FRF), with the (complex) ratio of harmonic displacement to force input referred to as *receptance*, and acceleration and velocity FRFs referred to respectively as *mobility* and *accelerance*.[^o4]

## Measurement and plotting {#Measurement_and_plotting}

*Image: Butterworth response.svg.* Magnitude response of a low pass filter with 6 dB per octave or 20 dB per decade [roll-off](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roll-off)

Measuring the frequency response typically involves exciting the system with an input signal and measuring the resulting output signal, calculating the [frequency spectra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_spectrum) of the two signals (for example, using the [fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512) for discrete signals), and comparing the spectra to isolate the effect of the system. In linear systems, the frequency range of the input signal should cover the frequency range of interest.

Several methods using different input signals may be used to measure the frequency response of a system, including:

- Applying constant amplitude sinusoids stepped through a range of frequencies and comparing the amplitude and phase shift of the output relative to the input. The frequency sweep must be slow enough for the system to reach its [steady-state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steady-state) at each point of interest
- Applying an [impulse](wikipedia://en/37021) signal and taking the Fourier transform of the [system's response](wikipedia://en/545825)
- Applying a [wide-sense stationary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wide-sense_stationary) [white noise](wikipedia://en/46182) signal over a long period of time and taking the Fourier transform of the system's response. With this method, the [cross-spectral density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-spectral_density) (rather than the [power spectral density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_spectral_density)) should be used if phase information is required

The frequency response is characterized by the *magnitude*, typically in [decibels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decibel) (dB) or as a generic [amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude) of the dependent variable, and the *[phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29)*, in [radians](wikipedia://en/26003) or degrees, measured against frequency, in [radian/s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radians_per_second), [Hertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertz) (Hz) or as a fraction of the [sampling frequency](wikipedia://en/41435).

There are three common ways of plotting response measurements:

- [Bode plots](wikipedia://en/155562) graph magnitude and phase against frequency on two rectangular plots
- [Nyquist plots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nyquist_plot) graph magnitude and phase [parametrically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_plot) against frequency in polar form
- [Nichols plots](wikipedia://en/2017203) graph magnitude and phase parametrically against frequency in rectangular form

For the design of control systems, any of the three types of plots may be used to infer closed-loop stability and stability margins from the open-loop frequency response. In many frequency domain applications, the phase response is relatively unimportant, and the magnitude response of the Bode plot may be all that is required. In digital systems (such as [digital filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_filters)), the frequency response often contains a main lobe with multiple periodic sidelobes, due to [spectral leakage](wikipedia://en/545288) caused by digital processes such as [sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampling_%28signal_processing%29) and [windowing](wikipedia://en/244097).[^o6]

## Applications {#Applications}

In the audible range, frequency response is usually referred to in connection with [electronic amplifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_amplifier), [microphones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microphone) and [loudspeakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loudspeakers). Radio spectrum frequency response can refer to measurements of [coaxial cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coaxial_cable), [twisted-pair cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_6_cable), [video switching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_switching) equipment, [wireless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless) communications devices, and antenna systems. Infrasonic frequency response measurements include [earthquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthquakes) and [electroencephalography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroencephalography) (brain waves).

Frequency response curves are often used to indicate the accuracy of electronic components or systems.[^o9] When a system or component reproduces all desired input signals with no emphasis or attenuation of a particular frequency band, the system or component is said to be "flat", or to have a flat frequency response curve.[^o9] In other cases, 3D-form of frequency response graphs are sometimes used.

Frequency response requirements differ depending on the application.[^o10] In [high fidelity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_fidelity) audio, an amplifier requires a flat frequency response of at least 20–20,000 Hz, with a tolerance as tight as ±0.1 dB in the mid-range frequencies around 1000 Hz; however, in [telephony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephony), a frequency response of 400–4,000 Hz, with a tolerance of ±1 dB is sufficient for intelligibility of speech.[^o10]

Once a frequency response has been measured (e.g., as an impulse response), provided the system is [linear and time-invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LTI_system_theory), its characteristic can be approximated with arbitrary accuracy by a [digital filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_filter). Similarly, if a system is demonstrated to have a poor frequency response, a digital or [analog filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analog_filter) can be applied to the signals prior to their reproduction to compensate for these deficiencies.

The form of a frequency response curve is very important for [anti-jamming protection of radars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_jamming_and_deception), communications and other systems.

Frequency response analysis can also be applied to biological domains, such as the detection of hormesis in repeated behaviors with opponent process dynamics,[^o11] or in the optimization of drug treatment regimens.[^o12]
