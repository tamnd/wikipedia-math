# Detonation

*Image: TNT detonation on Kaho'olawe Island during Operation Sailor Hat, shot Bravo, 1965.jpg.* Detonation of [TNT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TNT), and [shock wave](wikipedia://en/146253)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Detonation"}

**Detonation** (la)[^o2] is a type of [combustion](wikipedia://en/5638) involving a [supersonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersonic) exothermic front accelerating through a medium that eventually drives a [shock front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shock_front) propagating directly in front of it. Detonations propagate supersonically through [shock waves](wikipedia://en/146253) with speeds about 1 km/sec and differ from [deflagrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflagration) which have subsonic flame speeds about 1 m/sec.[^o3] Detonation may form from an [explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explosion) of fuel-oxidizer mixture. Compared with deflagration, detonation doesn't need to have an external oxidizer. Oxidizers and fuel mix when deflagration occurs. Detonation is more destructive than deflagration. In detonation, the flame front travels through the air-fuel faster than sound; while in deflagration, the flame front travels through the air-fuel slower than sound.

:::

Detonations occur in both conventional solid and liquid explosives,[^o4] as well as in reactive gases. TNT, dynamite, and C4 are examples of high power explosives that detonate. The [velocity of detonation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detonation_velocity) in solid and liquid explosives is much higher than that in gaseous ones, which allows the wave system to be observed with greater detail (higher [resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_resolution)).

A very wide variety of fuels may occur as gases (e.g. [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen)), droplet fogs, or dust suspensions. In addition to dioxygen, oxidants can include halogen compounds, ozone, hydrogen peroxide, and [oxides of nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_oxide). Gaseous detonations are often associated with a mixture of fuel and oxidant in a composition somewhat below conventional flammability ratios. They happen most often in confined systems, but they sometimes occur in large vapor clouds. Other materials, such as [acetylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetylene), [ozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone), and [hydrogen peroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_peroxide), are detonable in the absence of an oxidant (or reductant). In these cases the energy released results from the rearrangement of the molecular constituents of the material.[^o5][^o6]

Detonation was discovered in 1881 by four French scientists [Marcellin Berthelot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcellin_Berthelot) and [Paul Marie Eugène Vieille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Marie_Eugène_Vieille)[^o7] and [Ernest-François Mallard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest-François_Mallard) and [Henry Louis Le Chatelier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Louis_Le_Chatelier).[^o8] The mathematical predictions of propagation were carried out first by [David Chapman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Chapman_%28chemist%29) in 1899[^o9] and by [Émile Jouguet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Émile_Jouguet) in 1905,[^o16] 1906 and 1917.[^o10] The next advance in understanding detonation was made by [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942)[^o13] and [Werner Döring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Döring)[^o14] in the early 1940s and [Yakov B. Zel'dovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakov_B._Zel'dovich) and [Aleksandr Solomonovich Kompaneets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleksandr_Solomonovich_Kompaneets) in the 1960s.[^o12]

## Theories {#Theories}

The simplest theory to predict the behaviour of detonations in gases is known as the [Chapman–Jouguet](wikipedia://en/3873144) (CJ) condition, developed around the turn of the 20th century. This theory, described by a relatively simple set of algebraic equations, models the detonation as a propagating shock wave accompanied by exothermic heat release. Such a theory describes the chemistry and diffusive transport processes as occurring abruptly as the shock passes.

A more complex theory was advanced during World War II independently by [Zel'dovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakov_B._Zel'dovich), [von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942), and [Döring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Döring).[^o12][^o13][^o14] This theory, now known as [ZND theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZND_theory), admits finite-rate chemical reactions and thus describes a detonation as an infinitesimally thin shock wave, followed by a zone of exothermic chemical reaction. With a reference frame of a stationary shock, the following flow is subsonic, so that an acoustic reaction zone follows immediately behind the lead front, the [Chapman–Jouguet condition](wikipedia://en/3873144).[^o15][^o16]

There is also some evidence that the reaction zone is [semi-metallic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semimetal) in some explosives.[^o17]

Both theories describe one-dimensional and steady wavefronts. However, in the 1960s, experiments revealed that gas-phase detonations were most often characterized by unsteady, three-dimensional structures, which can only, in an averaged sense, be predicted by one-dimensional steady theories. Indeed, such waves are quenched as their structure is destroyed.[^o18][^o19] The Wood-Kirkwood detonation theory can correct some of these limitations.[^o20]

Experimental studies have revealed some of the conditions needed for the propagation of such fronts. In confinement, the range of composition of mixes of fuel and oxidant and self-decomposing substances with inerts are slightly below the flammability limits and, for spherically expanding fronts, well below them.[^o21] The influence of increasing the concentration of diluent on expanding individual detonation cells has been elegantly demonstrated.[^o22] Similarly, their size grows as the initial pressure falls.[^o23] Since cell widths must be matched with minimum dimension of containment, any wave overdriven by the initiator will be quenched.

Mathematical modeling has steadily advanced to predicting the complex flow fields behind shocks inducing reactions.[^o24][^o25] To date, none has adequately described how the structure is formed and sustained behind unconfined waves.

## Applications {#Applications}

*Image: Iraqi Bomb Disposal Company DVIDS19881.jpg.* A controlled [bomb disposal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bomb_disposal) in [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq), 2006; detonating the bomb causes fire and smoke to propel upward.

When used in explosive devices, the main cause of damage from a detonation is the supersonic blast front (a powerful [shock wave](wikipedia://en/146253)) in the surrounding area. This is a significant distinction from [deflagrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflagration) where the exothermic wave is subsonic and maximum pressures for non-metal specks of dust are approximately 7–10 times atmospheric pressure.[^o28] Therefore, detonation is a feature for destructive purposes while deflagration is favored for the acceleration of [firearms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firearm)' projectiles. However, detonation waves may also be used for less destructive purposes, including deposition of coatings to a surface[^o29] or cleaning of equipment (e.g. slag removal[^o30]) and even [explosively welding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explosive_welding) together metals that would otherwise fail to fuse. [Pulse detonation engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulse_detonation_engine) use the detonation wave for aerospace propulsion.[^o31] The first flight of an aircraft powered by a pulse detonation engine took place at the [Mojave Air & Space Port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mojave_Airport_&_Spaceport) on 31 January 2008.[^o32]

:::

## In engines and firearms {#In_engines_and_firearms}

Unintentional detonation when [deflagration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflagration) is desired is a problem in some devices. In [Otto cycle](wikipedia://en/147566), or gasoline engines it is called [engine knocking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engine_knocking) or pinging, and it causes a loss of power. It can also cause excessive heating, and harsh mechanical shock that can result in eventual engine failure.[^o34] In firearms, it may cause catastrophic and potentially lethal failure.

[Pulse detonation engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulse_detonation_engine) are a form of pulsed jet engine that has been experimented with on several occasions as this offers the potential for good fuel efficiency.
