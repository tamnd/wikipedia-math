# Mercer's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mercer's theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), **Mercer's theorem** is a representation of a symmetric [positive-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_bilinear_form) function on a square as a sum of a convergent sequence of product functions. This theorem, presented in (Mercer 1909), is one of the most notable results of the work of [James Mercer](wikipedia://en/303923) (1883–1932). It is an important theoretical tool in the theory of [integral equations](wikipedia://en/474234); it is used in the [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) theory of [stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/47895), for example the [Karhunen–Loève theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karhunen–Loève_theorem); and it is also used in the [reproducing kernel Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/651196) theory where it characterizes a symmetric [positive-definite kernel](wikipedia://en/10273855) as a reproducing kernel.[^o3]

:::

## Introduction {#Introduction}

To explain Mercer's theorem, we first consider an important special case; see below for a more general formulation. A *kernel*, in this context, is a [symmetric](wikipedia://en/22510359) continuous function

$$K: [a,b] \times [a,b] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$$

where $K(x,y) = K(y,x)$ for all $x,y \in [a,b]$.

*K* is said to be a [positive-definite kernel](wikipedia://en/10273855) if and only if

$$\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n K(x_i, x_j) c_i c_j \geq 0$$

for all finite sequences of points *x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub> of \[*a*, *b*\] and all choices of real numbers *c*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *c*<sub>*n*</sub>. Note that the term "positive-definite" is well-established in literature despite the weak inequality in the definition.[^o5][^o6]

The fundamental characterization of stationary positive-definite kernels (where $K(x,y) = K(x-y)$) is given by **[Bochner's theorem](wikipedia://en/2369850)**. It states that a continuous function $K(x-y)$ is positive-definite if and only if it can be expressed as the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of a finite non-negative measure $\mu$:

$$K(x-y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{i(x-y)\omega} \, d\mu(\omega)$$

This spectral representation reveals the connection between positive definiteness and [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147), providing a stronger and more direct characterization of positive definiteness than the abstract definition in terms of inequalities when the kernel is stationary, e.g., when it can be expressed as a 1-variable function of the distance between points rather than the 2-variable function of the positions of pairs of points.

Associated to *K* is a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) (more specifically a [Hilbert–Schmidt integral operator](wikipedia://en/12366193) when the interval is compact) on functions defined by the integral

$$[T_K \varphi](x) =\int_a^b K(x,s) \varphi(s)\, ds.$$

We assume $\varphi$ can range through the space of real-valued [square-integrable functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square-integrable_functions) *L*<sup>2</sup>\[*a*, *b*\]; however, in many cases the associated [reproducing kernel Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/651196) can be strictly larger than *L*<sup>2</sup>\[*a*, *b*\]. Since *T<sub>K</sub>* is a linear operator, the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) and [eigenfunctions](wikipedia://en/228601) of *T<sub>K</sub>* exist.

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem**. Suppose *K* is a continuous symmetric positive-definite kernel. Then there is an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) {*e*<sub>i</sub>}<sub>i</sub> of *L*<sup>2</sup>\[*a*, *b*\] consisting of eigenfunctions of *T*<sub>*K*</sub> such that the corresponding sequence of eigenvalues {λ<sub>*i*</sub>}<sub>*i*</sub> is nonnegative. The eigenfunctions corresponding to non-zero eigenvalues are continuous on \[*a*, *b*\] and *K* has the representation

$$K(s,t) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \lambda_j \, e_j(s) \, e_j(t)$$

where the convergence is absolute and uniform.

:::

## Details {#Details}

We now explain in greater detail the structure of the proof of Mercer's theorem, particularly how it relates to [spectral theory of compact operators](wikipedia://en/4597914).

- The map *K* ↦ *T*<sub>*K*</sub> is [injective](wikipedia://en/45196).
- *T*<sub>*K*</sub> is a non-negative symmetric compact operator on *L*<sup>2</sup>\[*a*,*b*\]; moreover *K*(*x*, *x*) ≥ 0.

To show compactness, show that the image of the [unit ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_ball) of *L*<sup>2</sup>\[*a*,*b*\] under *T*<sub>*K*</sub> is [equicontinuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equicontinuous) and apply [Ascoli's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascoli's_theorem), to show that the image of the unit ball is relatively compact in C(\[*a*,*b*\]) with the [uniform norm](wikipedia://en/349755) and *a fortiori* in *L*<sup>2</sup>\[*a*,*b*\].

Now apply the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495) for compact operators on Hilbert spaces to *T*<sub>*K*</sub> to show the existence of the orthonormal basis {*e*<sub>i</sub>}<sub>i</sub> of *L*<sup>2</sup>\[*a*,*b*\]

$$\lambda_i e_i(t)= [T_K e_i](t) = \int_a^b K(t,s) e_i(s)\, ds.$$

If λ<sub>i</sub> ≠ 0, the eigenvector ([eigenfunction](wikipedia://en/228601)) *e*<sub>i</sub> is seen to be continuous on \[*a*,*b*\]. Now

$$\sum_{i=1}^\infty \lambda_i |e_i(t) e_i(s)| \leq \sup_{x \in [a,b]} |K(x,x)|,$$

which shows that the sequence

$$\sum_{i=1}^\infty \lambda_i e_i(t) e_i(s)$$

converges absolutely and uniformly to a kernel *K*<sub>0</sub> which is easily seen to define the same operator as the kernel *K*. Hence *K*=*K*<sub>0</sub> from which Mercer's theorem follows.

Finally, to show non-negativity of the eigenvalues one can write $\lambda \langle f,f \rangle= \langle f, T_{K}f \rangle$ and expressing the right hand side as an integral well-approximated by its Riemann sums, which are non-negative by positive-definiteness of *K*, implying $\lambda \langle f,f \rangle \geq 0$, implying $\lambda \geq 0$.

## Trace {#Trace}

The following is immediate:

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem**. Suppose *K* is a continuous symmetric positive-definite kernel; *T*<sub>*K*</sub> has a sequence of nonnegative eigenvalues {λ<sub>i</sub>}<sub>i</sub>. Then

$$\int_a^b K(t,t)\, dt = \sum_i \lambda_i.$$

This shows that the operator *T*<sub>*K*</sub> is a [trace class](wikipedia://en/192266) operator and

$$\operatorname{trace}(T_K) = \int_a^b K(t,t)\, dt.$$

:::

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Mercer's theorem itself is a generalization of the result that any [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474) [positive-semidefinite matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-semidefinite_matrix) is the [Gramian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gramian_matrix) of a set of vectors.

The first generalization replaces the interval \[*a*, *b*\] with any [compact Hausdorff space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Hausdorff_space) and Lebesgue measure on \[*a*, *b*\] is replaced by a finite countably additive measure μ on the [Borel algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_algebra) of *X* whose support is *X*. This means that μ(*U*) > 0 for any nonempty open subset *U* of *X*.

A recent generalization replaces these conditions by the following: the set *X* is a [first-countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-countable) topological space endowed with a Borel (complete) measure μ. *X* is the support of μ and, for all *x* in *X*, there is an open set *U* containing *x* and having finite measure. Then essentially the same result holds:

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem**. Suppose *K* is a continuous symmetric positive-definite kernel on *X*. If the function κ is *L*<sup>1</sup><sub>μ</sub>(*X*), where κ(x) := K(x,x) for all *x* in *X*, then there is an [orthonormal set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthonormal_set) {*e*<sub>i</sub>}<sub>i</sub> of *L*<sup>2</sup><sub>μ</sub>(*X*) consisting of eigenfunctions of *T*<sub>*K*</sub> such that corresponding sequence of eigenvalues {λ<sub>i</sub>}<sub>i</sub> is nonnegative. The eigenfunctions corresponding to non-zero eigenvalues are continuous on *X* and *K* has the representation

$$K(s,t) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \lambda_j \, e_j(s) \, e_j(t)$$

where the convergence is absolute and uniform on compact subsets of *X*.

The next generalization deals with representations of *measurable* kernels.

Let (*X*, *M*, μ) be a σ-finite measure space. An *L*<sup>2</sup> (or square-integrable) kernel on *X* is a function

$$K \in L^2_{\mu \otimes \mu}(X \times X).$$

*L*<sup>2</sup> kernels define a bounded operator *T*<sub>*K*</sub> by the formula

$$\langle T_K \varphi, \psi \rangle = \int_{X \times X} K(y,x) \varphi(y) \psi(x) \,d[\mu \otimes \mu](y,x).$$

*T*<sub>*K*</sub> is a compact operator (actually it is even a [Hilbert–Schmidt operator](wikipedia://en/976673)). If the kernel *K* is symmetric, by the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/6690773#Compact_self_adjoint_operator), *T*<sub>*K*</sub> has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. Those eigenvectors that correspond to non-zero eigenvalues can be arranged in a sequence {*e*<sub>*i*</sub>}<sub>*i*</sub> (regardless of separability).

:::

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem**. If *K* is a symmetric positive-definite kernel on (*X*, *M*, μ), then

$$K(y,x) = \sum_{i \in \mathbb{N}} \lambda_i e_i(y) e_i(x)$$

where the convergence in the *L*<sup>2</sup> norm. Note that when continuity of the kernel is not assumed, the expansion no longer converges uniformly.

:::

:::

## Mercer's condition {#Mercer's_condition}

A [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued [function](wikipedia://en/185427) *K*(*x*,*y*) is said to fulfill **Mercer's condition** if for all [square-integrable functions](wikipedia://en/28680558) *g*(*x*) one has

$$\iint g(x)K(x,y)g(y)\,dx\,dy \geq 0.$$

### Discrete analog {#Discrete_analog}

This is analogous to the definition of a [positive-semidefinite matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-semidefinite_matrix). This is a matrix $K$ of dimension $N$, which satisfies, for all vectors $g$, the property

$$(g,Kg)=g^{T}{\cdot}Kg=\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^N\,g_i\,K_{ij}\,g_j\geq0.$$

::: {#o18 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

A positive constant function

$$K(x, y)=c\,$$

satisfies Mercer's condition, as then the integral becomes by [Fubini's theorem](wikipedia://en/255245)

$$\iint g(x)\,c\,g(y)\,dx \, dy = c\int\! g(x) \,dx \int\! g(y) \,dy = c\left(\int\! g(x) \,dx\right)^2$$

which is indeed [non-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative).

:::
