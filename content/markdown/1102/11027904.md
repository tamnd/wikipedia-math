# Epsilon calculus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Epsilon calculus"}

In logic, [Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302)'s **epsilon calculus** is an extension of a [formal language](wikipedia://en/10939) by the epsilon operator, where the epsilon operator substitutes for [quantifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantification_%28logic%29) in that language as a method leading to a [proof of consistency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consistency_proof) for the extended formal language. The *epsilon operator* and *epsilon substitution method* are typically applied to a [first-order predicate calculus](wikipedia://en/10983), followed by a demonstration of consistency. The epsilon-extended calculus is further extended and generalized to cover those mathematical objects, classes, and categories for which there is a desire to show consistency, building on previously-shown consistency at earlier levels.[^o2]

:::

## Epsilon operator {#Epsilon_operator}

### Hilbert notation {#Hilbert_notation}

For any formal language $L$, extend $L$ by adding the epsilon operator to redefine quantification:

- $$(\exists x) A(x)\ \equiv \ A(\epsilon x\ A)$$
- $$(\forall x) A(x)\ \equiv \ \neg \exists x \neg A(x) \leftrightarrow \neg \bigl(\neg A(\epsilon x\ \neg A)\bigr) \leftrightarrow A\bigl(\epsilon x\ (\neg A)\bigr)$$

The intended interpretation of $\epsilon x\ A$ is *some* $x$ that satisfies $A$, if it exists. In other words, $\epsilon x\ A$ returns some [term](wikipedia://en/24885593) $t$ such that $A(t)$ is true, otherwise it returns some default or arbitrary term. If more than one term can satisfy $A$, then any one of these terms (which make $A$ true) can be [chosen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_of_Choice), non-deterministically. Equality is required to be defined under $L$, and the only rules required for $L$ extended by the epsilon operator are [modus ponens](wikipedia://en/18900) and the substitution of $A(t)$ to replace $A(x)$ for any term $t$.[^o5]

### Bourbaki notation {#Bourbaki_notation}

In tau-square notation from [N. Bourbaki's](wikipedia://en/167394) *Theory of Sets*, the quantifiers are defined as follows:

- $$(\exists x) A(x)\ \equiv \ \bigl(\tau_x(A)|x\bigr)A$$
- $$(\forall x) A(x)\ \equiv \ \neg \bigl(\tau_x(\neg A)|x\bigr)\neg A\ \equiv \ \bigl(\tau_x(\neg A)|x\bigr)A$$

where $A$ is a relation in $L$, $x$ is a variable, and $\tau_x(A)$ juxtaposes a $\tau$ at the front of $A$, replaces all instances of $x$ with $\square$, and links them back to $\tau$. Then let $Y$ be an assembly, $(Y|x)A$ denotes the replacement of all variables $x$ in $A$ with $Y$.

This notation is equivalent to the Hilbert notation and is read the same. It is used by Bourbaki to define [cardinal assignment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_assignment) since they do not use the [axiom of replacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_of_replacement).

Defining quantifiers in this way leads to great inefficiencies. For instance, the expansion of Bourbaki's original definition of the number one, using this notation, has length approximately 4.5 × 10<sup>12</sup>, and for a later edition of Bourbaki that combined this notation with the [Kuratowski](wikipedia://en/46075) definition of [ordered pairs](wikipedia://en/22362), this number grows to approximately 2.4 × 10<sup>54</sup>.[^o7]

## Modern approaches {#Modern_approaches}

[Hilbert's program](wikipedia://en/607286) for mathematics was to justify those [formal systems](wikipedia://en/396102) as consistent in relation to constructive or semi-constructive systems. While [Gödel's results on incompleteness](wikipedia://en/58863) mooted Hilbert's Program to a great extent, modern researchers find the epsilon calculus to provide alternatives for approaching proofs of systemic consistency as described in the epsilon substitution method.

### Epsilon substitution method {#Epsilon_substitution_method}

A theory to be checked for consistency is first embedded in an appropriate epsilon calculus. Second, a process is developed for re-writing quantified theorems to be expressed in terms of epsilon operations via the epsilon substitution method. Finally, the process must be shown to normalize the re-writing process, so that the re-written theorems satisfy the axioms of the theory.[^o10]
