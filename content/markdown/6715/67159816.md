# Osserman–Xavier–Fujimoto theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Osserman–Xavier–Fujimoto theorem"}

In the mathematical field of [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), the **Osserman–Xavier–Fujimoto theorem** concerns the [Gauss maps](wikipedia://en/378881) of [minimal surfaces](wikipedia://en/276734) in the three-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). It says that if a minimal surface is [immersed](wikipedia://en/6993953) and [geodesically complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesically_complete), then the image of the Gauss map either consists of a single point (so that the surface is a plane) or contains all of the sphere except for at most four points.

:::

[Bernstein's theorem](wikipedia://en/30486183) says that a minimal *graph* in $\mathbf{R}^{3}$ which is geodesically complete must be a plane. This can be rephrased to say that the Gauss map of a complete immersed minimal surface in $\mathbf{R}^{3}$ is either constant or not contained within an open hemisphere. As [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) by [Louis Nirenberg](wikipedia://en/1814725) and proved by [Robert Osserman](wikipedia://en/19663336) in 1959, in this form Bernstein's theorem can be generalized to say that the image of the Gauss map of a complete immersed minimal surface in $\mathbf{R}^{3}$ either consists of a single point or is [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) within the sphere.

Osserman's theorem was improved by Frederico Xavier and Hirotaka Fujimoto in the 1980s. They proved that if the image of the Gauss map of a complete immersed minimal surface in $\mathbf{R}^{3}$ omits more than four points of the sphere, then the surface is a plane. This is optimal, since it was shown by [Konrad Voss](wikipedia://en/79511023) in the 1960s that for any subset $A$ of the sphere whose complement consists of zero, one, two, three, or four points, there exists a complete immersed minimal surface in $\mathbf{R}^{3}$ whose Gauss map has image $A$. Particular examples include [Riemann's minimal surface](wikipedia://en/36747670), whose Gauss map is surjective, the [Enneper surface](wikipedia://en/4688830), whose Gauss map omits one point, the [catenoid](wikipedia://en/273329) and [helicoid](wikipedia://en/684207), whose Gauss maps omit two points, and [Scherk's first surface](wikipedia://en/8235776), whose Gauss map omits four points.

It is also possible to study the Gauss map of minimal surfaces of higher [codimension](wikipedia://en/525149) in higher-dimensional Euclidean spaces. There are a number of variants of the results of Osserman, Xavier, and Fujimoto which can be studied in this setting.
