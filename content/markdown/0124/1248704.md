# Ideal theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ideal theory"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **ideal theory** is the theory of [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) in [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346). While the notion of an ideal exists also for [non-commutative rings](wikipedia://en/24062516), a much more substantial theory exists only for commutative rings (and this article therefore only considers ideals in commutative rings.)

:::

Throughout the articles, rings refer to commutative rings. See also the article [ideal (ring theory)](wikipedia://en/25977) for basic operations such as sum or products of ideals.

## Ideals in a finitely generated algebra over a field {#Ideals_in_a_finitely_generated_algebra_over_a_field}

Ideals in a finitely generated algebra over a field (that is, a quotient of a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) over a field) behave somehow nicer than those in a general commutative ring. First, in contrast to the general case, if $A$ is a finitely generated algebra over a field, then the [radical of an ideal](wikipedia://en/252009) in $A$ is the intersection of all maximal ideals containing the ideal (because $A$ is a [Jacobson ring](wikipedia://en/4607512)). This may be thought of as an extension of [Hilbert's Nullstellensatz](wikipedia://en/149215), which concerns the case when $A$ is a polynomial ring.

## Topology determined by an ideal {#Topology_determined_by_an_ideal}

If *I* is an ideal in a ring *A*, then it determines the topology on *A* where a subset *U* of *A* is open if, for each *x* in *U*,

$$x + I^n \subset U.$$

for some integer $n > 0$. This topology is called the *I*-adic topology. It is also called an *a*-adic topology if $I = aA$ is generated by an element $a$.

For example, take $A = \mathbb{Z}$, the ring of integers and $I = pA$ an ideal generated by a prime number *p*. For each integer $x$, define $|x|_p = p^{-n}$ when $x = p^n y$, $y$ [prime to](wikipedia://en/6556) $p$. Then, clearly,

$$x + p^n A = B(x, p^{-(n-1)})$$

where $B(x, r) = \{ z \in \mathbb{Z} \mid |z - x|_p < r \}$ denotes an open ball of radius $r$ with center $x$. Hence, the $p$-adic topology on $\mathbb{Z}$ is the same as the [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) topology given by $d(x, y) = |x - y|_p$. As a metric space, $\mathbb{Z}$ can be [completed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completion_of_a_metric_space). The resulting complete metric space has a structure of a ring that extended the ring structure of $\mathbb{Z}$; this ring is denoted as $\mathbb{Z}_p$ and is called the [ring of *p*-adic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_p-adic_integers).

## Ideal class group {#Ideal_class_group}

In a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132) *A* (e.g., a ring of integers in a number field or the coordinate ring of a smooth affine curve) with the field of fractions $K$, an ideal $I$ is invertible in the sense: there exists a [fractional ideal](wikipedia://en/460700) $I^{-1}$ (that is, an *A*-submodule of $K$) such that $I \, I^{-1} = A$, where the product on the left is a product of submodules of *K*. In other words, fractional ideals form a group under a product. The quotient of the group of fractional ideals by the subgroup of principal ideals is then the [ideal class group](wikipedia://en/150907) of *A*.

In a general ring, an ideal may not be invertible (in fact, already the definition of a fractional ideal is not clear). However, over a Noetherian [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462), it is still possible to develop some theory generalizing the situation in Dedekind domains. For example, Ch. VII of Bourbaki's *[Algèbre commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algèbre_commutative)* gives such a theory.

The ideal class group of *A*, when it can be defined, is closely related to the [Picard group](wikipedia://en/1850134) of the [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_spectrum) of *A* (often the two are the same; e.g., for Dedekind domains).

In [algebraic number](wikipedia://en/1158) theory, especially in [class field theory](wikipedia://en/242695), it is more convenient to use a generalization of an ideal class group called an [idele class group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idele_class_group).

## Closure operations {#Closure_operations}

There are several operations on ideals that play roles of closures. The most basic one is the [radical of an ideal](wikipedia://en/252009). Another is the [integral closure of an ideal](wikipedia://en/39944913). Given an irredundant primary decomposition $I = \cap Q_i$, the intersection of $Q_i$'s whose radicals are minimal (don’t contain any of the radicals of other $Q_j$'s) is uniquely determined by $I$; this intersection is then called the unmixed part of $I$. It is also a closure operation.

Given ideals $I, J$ in a ring $A$, the ideal

$$(I : J^{\infty}) = \{ f \in A \mid fJ^n \subset I, n \gg 0 \} = \bigcup_{n > 0} \operatorname{Ann}_A((J^n + I)/I)$$

is called the saturation of $I$ with respect to $J$ and is a closure operation (this notion is closely related to the study of local cohomology).

See also [tight closure](wikipedia://en/6921893).

## Local cohomology in ideal theory {#Local_cohomology_in_ideal_theory}

Local cohomology can sometimes be used to obtain information on an ideal. This section assumes some familiarity with sheaf theory and scheme theory.

Let $M$ be a module over a ring $R$ and $I$ an ideal. Then $M$ determines the sheaf $\widetilde{M}$ on $Y = \operatorname{Spec}(R) - V(I)$ (the restriction to *Y* of the sheaf associated to *M*). Unwinding the definition, one sees:

$$\Gamma_I(M) := \Gamma(Y, \widetilde{M}) = \varinjlim \operatorname{Hom}(I^n, M).$$

Here, $\Gamma_I(M)$ is called the **ideal transform** of $M$ with respect to $I$.
