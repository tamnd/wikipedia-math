# Thom space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Thom space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Thom space,** **Thom complex,** or **Pontryagin–Thom construction** (named after [René Thom](wikipedia://en/61633) and [Lev Pontryagin](wikipedia://en/582075)) of [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) and [differential topology](wikipedia://en/8562) is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) associated to a [vector bundle](wikipedia://en/276524), over any [paracompact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paracompact) space.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction of the Thom space"}

## Construction of the Thom space {#Construction_of_the_Thom_space}

One way to construct this space is as follows. Let

$$p\colon E \to B$$

be a rank *n* [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [vector bundle](wikipedia://en/276524) over the [paracompact space](wikipedia://en/48631) *B*. Then for each point *b* in *B*, the [fiber](wikipedia://en/6603892#Fiber_in_naive_set_theory) $E_b$ is an *n*-dimensional real [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370). We can form an *n*-[sphere bundle](wikipedia://en/48880896) $\operatorname{Sph}(E) \to B$ by taking the [one-point compactification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-point_compactification) of each fiber and gluing them together to get the total space.What are the open sets of the new total space? Finally, from the total space $\operatorname{Sph}(E)$ we obtain the **Thom space** $T(E)$ as the quotient of $\operatorname{Sph}(E)$ by *B*; that is, by identifying all the new points to a single point $\infty$, which we take as the [basepoint](wikipedia://en/1236666) of $T(E)$. If *B* is compact, then $T(E)$ is the one-point compactification of *E*.

For example, if *E* is the trivial bundle $B\times \mathbb{R}^n$, then $\operatorname{Sph}(E)$ is $B\times S^n$ and, writing $B_+$ for *B* with a disjoint basepoint, $T(E)$ is the [smash product](wikipedia://en/698010) of $B_+$ and $S^n$; that is, the *n*-th reduced [suspension](wikipedia://en/1251702) of $B_+$.

Alternatively, since *B* is paracompact, *E* can be given a Euclidean metric and then $T(E)$ can be defined as the quotient of the unit disk bundle of *E* by the unit $(n-1)$-sphere bundle of *E*.

:::

## The Thom isomorphism {#The_Thom_isomorphism}

The significance of this construction begins with the following result, which belongs to the subject of [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060) of [fiber bundles](wikipedia://en/276526). (We have stated the result in terms of $\mathbb{Z}_2$ [coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficients) to avoid complications arising from [orientability](wikipedia://en/187446); see also [Orientation of a vector bundle#Thom space](wikipedia://en/44844703#Thom_space).)

Let $p: E\to B$ be a real vector bundle of rank *n*. Then there is an isomorphism called a **Thom isomorphism**

$$\Phi : H^k(B; \mathbb{Z}_2) \to \widetilde{H}^{k+n}(T(E); \mathbb{Z}_2),$$

for all *k* greater than or equal to 0, where the [right hand side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-hand_side_and_right-hand_side_of_an_equation) is [reduced cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_cohomology).

This theorem was formulated and proved by [René Thom](wikipedia://en/61633) in his famous 1952 thesis.

We can interpret the theorem as a global generalization of the suspension isomorphism on local trivializations, because the Thom space of a trivial bundle on *B* of rank *k* is isomorphic to the *k*th suspension of $B_+$, *B* with a disjoint point added (cf. #Construction of the Thom space.) This can be more easily seen in the formulation of the theorem that does not make reference to Thom space:

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Thom isomorphism"}

**Thom isomorphism.**

Let $\Lambda$ be a ring and $p: E\to B$ be an [oriented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented_bundle) real vector bundle of rank *n*. Then there exists a class

$u \in H^n(E, E \setminus B; \Lambda),$ where *B* is embedded into *E* as a zero section, such that for any fiber *F* the restriction of *u*

$u|_{(F, F \setminus 0)} \in H^n(F, F \setminus 0; \Lambda)$ is the class induced by the orientation of *F*. Moreover,

$\begin{cases} H^k(E; \Lambda) \to H^{k+n}(E, E \setminus B; \Lambda) \\ x \longmapsto x \smile u \end{cases}$ is an isomorphism.

:::

In concise terms, the last part of the theorem says that *u* freely generates $H^*(E, E \setminus B; \Lambda)$ as a right $H^*(E; \Lambda)$-module. The class *u* is usually called the **Thom class** of *E*. Since the pullback $p^*: H^*(B; \Lambda) \to H^*(E; \Lambda)$ is a [ring isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_isomorphism), $\Phi$ is given by the equation:

$$\Phi(b) = p^*(b) \smile u.$$

In particular, the Thom isomorphism sends the [identity](wikipedia://en/298428) element of $H^*(B)$ to *u*. Note: for this formula to make sense, *u* is treated as an element of (we drop the ring $\Lambda$)

$\tilde{H}^n(T(E)) = H^n(\operatorname{Sph}(E), B) \simeq H^n(E, E \setminus B).$[^o6]

The standard reference for the Thom isomorphism is the book by Bott and Tu.

## Significance of Thom's work {#Significance_of_Thom's_work}

In his 1952 paper, Thom showed that the Thom class, the [Stiefel–Whitney classes](wikipedia://en/877761), and the [Steenrod operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steenrod_operation) were all related. He used these ideas to prove in the 1954 paper *Quelques propriétés globales des variétés differentiables* that the [cobordism](wikipedia://en/391832) groups could be computed as the [homotopy groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_groups) of certain Thom spaces *MG*(*n*). The proof depends on and is intimately related to the [transversality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transversality_%28mathematics%29) properties of [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifolds)—see [Thom transversality theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thom_transversality_theorem). By reversing this construction, [John Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632) and [Sergei Novikov](wikipedia://en/628515) (among many others) were able to answer questions about the existence and uniqueness of high-dimensional manifolds: this is now known as [surgery theory](wikipedia://en/5961115). In addition, the spaces *MG(n)* fit together to form [spectra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_%28homotopy_theory%29) *MG* now known as **Thom spectra**, and the cobordism groups are in fact [stable](wikipedia://en/2946655). Thom's construction thus also unifies [differential topology](wikipedia://en/8562) and stable homotopy theory, and is in particular integral to our knowledge of the [stable homotopy groups of spheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_homotopy_groups_of_spheres).

If the Steenrod operations are available, we can use them and the isomorphism of the theorem to construct the Stiefel–Whitney classes. Recall that the Steenrod operations (mod 2) are [natural transformations](wikipedia://en/45022)

$$Sq^i : H^m(-; \mathbb{Z}_2) \to H^{m+i}(-; \mathbb{Z}_2),$$

defined for all nonnegative integers *m*. If $i=m$, then $Sq^i$ coincides with the cup square. We can define the *i*th Stiefel–Whitney class $w_i(p)$ of the vector bundle $p: E\to B$ by:

$$w_i(p) = \Phi^{-1}(Sq^i(\Phi(1))) = \Phi^{-1}(Sq^i(u)).$$

## Consequences for differentiable manifolds {#Consequences_for_differentiable_manifolds}

If we take the bundle in the above to be the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) of a smooth manifold, the conclusion of the above is called the [Wu formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wu_formula), and has the following strong consequence: since the Steenrod operations are invariant under homotopy equivalence, we conclude that the Stiefel–Whitney classes of a manifold are as well. This is an extraordinary result that does not generalize to other characteristic classes. There exists a similar famous and difficult result establishing topological invariance for rational [Pontryagin classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontryagin_classes), due to [Sergei Novikov](wikipedia://en/628515).

## Thom spectrum {#Thom_spectrum}

### Real cobordism {#Real_cobordism}

There are two ways to think about bordism: one as considering two $n$-manifolds $M,M'$ are cobordant if there is an $(n+1)$-manifold with boundary $W$ such that

$$\partial W = M \coprod M'$$

Another technique to encode this kind of information is to take an embedding $M \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^{N + n}$ and considering the normal bundle

$$\nu: N_{\mathbb{R}^{N+n}/M} \to M$$

The embedded manifold together with the isomorphism class of the normal bundle actually encodes the same information as the cobordism class $[M]$. This can be shown[^o11] by using a cobordism $W$ and finding an embedding to some $\mathbb{R}^{N_W + n}\times [0,1]$ which gives a homotopy class of maps to the Thom space $MO(n)$ defined below. Showing the isomorphism of

$$\pi_nMO \cong \Omega^O_n$$

requires a little more work.[^o12]

### Definition of Thom spectrum {#Definition_of_Thom_spectrum}

By definition, the **Thom spectrum**[^o14] is a sequence of Thom spaces

$$MO(n) = T(\gamma^n)$$

where we wrote $\gamma^n\to BO(n)$ for the [universal vector bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_vector_bundle) of rank *n*. The sequence forms a [spectrum](wikipedia://en/1033697).[^o15] A theorem of Thom says that $\pi_*(MO)$ is the unoriented [cobordism ring](wikipedia://en/44846510);[^o16] the proof of this theorem relies crucially on [Thom’s transversality theorem](wikipedia://en/22667049).[^o17] The lack of transversality requires that alternative methods be found to compute cobordism rings of, say, [topological manifolds](wikipedia://en/2119193) from Thom spectra.
