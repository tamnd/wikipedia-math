# Lagrangian (field theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lagrangian"}

**Lagrangian field theory** is a formalism in [classical field theory](wikipedia://en/1293340). It is the field-theoretic analogue of [Lagrangian mechanics](wikipedia://en/23371726). Lagrangian mechanics is used to analyze the motion of a system of discrete particles each with a finite number of [degrees of freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_of_freedom_%28physics_and_chemistry%29). Lagrangian field theory applies to continua and [fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28physics%29), which have an infinite number of degrees of freedom.

:::

One motivation for the development of the Lagrangian formalism on fields, and more generally, for [classical field theory](wikipedia://en/1293340), is to provide a clear mathematical foundation for [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), which is infamously beset by formal difficulties that make it unacceptable as a mathematical theory. The Lagrangians presented here are identical to their quantum equivalents, but, in treating the fields as classical fields, instead of being quantized, one can provide definitions and obtain solutions with properties compatible with the conventional formal approach to the mathematics of [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564). This enables the formulation of solutions on spaces with well-characterized properties, such as [Sobolev spaces](wikipedia://en/611964). It enables various theorems to be provided, ranging from proofs of existence to the [uniform convergence](wikipedia://en/50652) of formal series to the general settings of [potential theory](wikipedia://en/1431342). In addition, insight and clarity is obtained by generalizations to [Riemannian manifolds](wikipedia://en/144652) and [fiber bundles](wikipedia://en/276526), allowing the geometric structure to be clearly discerned and disentangled from the corresponding equations of motion. A clearer view of the geometric structure has in turn allowed highly abstract theorems from geometry to be used to gain insight, ranging from the [Chern–Gauss–Bonnet theorem](wikipedia://en/393115) and the [Riemann–Roch theorem](wikipedia://en/247261) to the [Atiyah–Singer index theorem](wikipedia://en/324752) and [Chern–Simons theory](wikipedia://en/391908).

## Overview {#Overview}

In field theory, the independent variable is replaced by an event in [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) $(x, y, z, t)$, or more generally still by a point *s* on a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652). The dependent variables are replaced by the value of a field at that point in spacetime $\varphi (x, y, z, t)$ so that the [equations of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations_of_motion) are obtained by means of an [action](wikipedia://en/312881) principle, written as:

$$\frac{\delta \mathcal{S}}{\delta \varphi_i} = 0,$$

where the *action*, $\mathcal{S}$, is a [functional](wikipedia://en/1374948) of the dependent variables $\varphi_i (s)$, their derivatives and *s* itself

$$\mathcal{S}\left[\varphi_i\right] = \int{ \mathcal{L} \left(\varphi_i (s), \left\{ \frac{\partial\varphi_i(s)}{\partial s^\alpha} \right\}, \{ s^\alpha \} \right) \, \mathrm{d}^n s },$$

where the brackets denote $\{\cdot~\forall\alpha\}$; and *s* = {*s<sup>α</sup>*} denotes the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of *n* [independent variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_variable) of the system, including the time variable, and is indexed by *α* = 1, 2, 3, ..., *n*. The calligraphic typeface, $\mathcal{L}$, is used to denote the [density](wikipedia://en/3428935), and $\mathrm{d}^n s$ is the [volume form](wikipedia://en/1855811) of the field function, i.e., the measure of the domain of the field function.

In mathematical formulations, it is common to express the Lagrangian as a function on a [fiber bundle](wikipedia://en/276526), wherein the Euler–Lagrange equations can be interpreted as specifying the [geodesics](wikipedia://en/91096) on the fiber bundle, leading to topics like [tangent manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_manifold), [symplectic manifolds](wikipedia://en/28356) and [contact geometry](wikipedia://en/476398).[^o3] The field theories of physics can be developed in terms of gauge invariant fiber bundles.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

In Lagrangian field theory, the Lagrangian as a function of [generalized coordinates](wikipedia://en/473514) is replaced by a Lagrangian density, a function of the fields in the system and their derivatives, and possibly the space and time coordinates themselves. In field theory, the independent variable *t* is replaced by an event in spacetime $(x, y, z, t)$ or still more generally by a point *s* on a manifold.

Often, a "Lagrangian density" is simply referred to as a "Lagrangian".

### Scalar fields {#Scalar_fields}

For one scalar field $\varphi$, the Lagrangian density will take the form:[^o8][^o9]

$$\mathcal{L}(\varphi, \boldsymbol{\nabla}\varphi, \partial \varphi/\partial t , \mathbf{x},t)$$

For many scalar fields

$$\mathcal{L}(\varphi_1, \boldsymbol{\nabla}\varphi_1, \partial \varphi_1/\partial t ,\ldots,\varphi_n, \boldsymbol{\nabla}\varphi_n, \partial \varphi_n/\partial t ,\ldots, \mathbf{x},t)$$

In mathematical formulations, the scalar fields are understood to be [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_chart) on a [fiber bundle](wikipedia://en/276526), and the derivatives of the field are understood to be [sections](wikipedia://en/367621) of the [jet bundle](wikipedia://en/928060).

### Vector fields, tensor fields, spinor fields {#Vector_fields,_tensor_fields,_spinor_fields}

The above can be generalized for [vector fields](wikipedia://en/62641), [tensor fields](wikipedia://en/256738), and [spinor fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinor_field). In physics, [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion) are described by spinor fields. [Bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson) are described by tensor fields, which include scalar and vector fields as special cases.

For example, if there are $m$ [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued [scalar fields](wikipedia://en/161879), $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_m$, then the field manifold is $\mathbb{R}^m$. If the field is a real [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641), then the field manifold is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to $\mathbb{R}^n$.

### Action {#Action}

The [time integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_integral) of the Lagrangian is called the [action](wikipedia://en/312881) denoted by $S$. In field theory, a distinction is occasionally made between the **Lagrangian** $L$, of which the time integral is the action

$$\mathcal{S} = \int L \, \mathrm{d}t \,,$$

and the **Lagrangian density** $\mathcal{L}$, which one integrates over all [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) to get the action:

$$\mathcal{S} [\varphi] = \int \mathcal{L} (\varphi,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\varphi,\partial\varphi/\partial t , \mathbf{x},t) \, \mathrm{d}^3 \mathbf{x} \, \mathrm{d}t .$$

The spatial [volume integral](wikipedia://en/1247265) of the Lagrangian density is the Lagrangian; in 3D,

$$L = \int \mathcal{L} \, \mathrm{d}^3 \mathbf{x} \,.$$

The action is often referred to as the "action [functional](wikipedia://en/1374948)", in that it is a function of the fields (and their derivatives).

### Volume form {#Volume_form}

In the presence of gravity or when using general curvilinear coordinates, the Lagrangian density $\mathcal{L}$ will include a factor of $\sqrt{g}$. This ensures that the action is invariant under general coordinate transformations. In mathematical literature, spacetime is taken to be a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) $M$ and the integral then becomes the [volume form](wikipedia://en/1855811)

$$\mathcal{S}=\int_M \sqrt{|g|} dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m \mathcal{L}$$

Here, the $\wedge$ is the [wedge product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedge_product) and $\sqrt{|g|}$ is the square root of the determinant $|g|$ of the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) $g$ on $M$. For flat spacetime (e.g., [Minkowski spacetime](wikipedia://en/230488)), the unit volume is one, i.e. $\sqrt{|g|}=1$ and so it is commonly omitted, when discussing field theory in flat spacetime. Likewise, the use of the wedge-product symbols offers no additional insight over the ordinary concept of a volume in multivariate calculus, and so these are likewise dropped. Some older textbooks, e.g., Landau and Lifschitz write $\sqrt{-g}$ for the volume form, since the minus sign is appropriate for metric tensors with signature (+−−−) or (−+++) (since the determinant is negative, in either case). When discussing field theory on general Riemannian manifolds, the volume form is usually written in the abbreviated notation $*(1)$ where $*$ is the [Hodge star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_star). That is,

$$*(1) = \sqrt{|g|} dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m$$

and so

$$\mathcal{S} = \int_M *(1) \mathcal{L}$$

Not infrequently, the notation above is considered to be entirely superfluous, and

$$\mathcal{S} = \int_M \mathcal{L}$$

is frequently seen. Do not be misled: the volume form is implicitly present in the integral above, even if it is not explicitly written.

### Euler–Lagrange equations {#Euler–Lagrange_equations}

The [Euler–Lagrange equations](wikipedia://en/294995) describe the [geodesic flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_flow) of the field $\varphi$ as a function of time. Taking the [variation](wikipedia://en/301504) with respect to $\varphi$, one obtains

$$0 = \frac{\delta\mathcal{S}}{\delta\varphi} = \int_M *(1) \left(-\partial_\mu \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\varphi)}\right)+ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\varphi}\right).$$

Solving, with respect to the [boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_condition), one obtains the [Euler–Lagrange equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Lagrange_equations):

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\varphi} = \partial_\mu \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\varphi)}\right) .$$

### Lagrangian terms {#Lagrangian_terms}

Often the Lagrangian consists of a sum of [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) terms, with the [symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_%28physics%29) of the theory and the fields involved dictating the types of terms that are allowed. For example, in [relativistic theories](wikipedia://en/30001), each term must be [Lorentz invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_covariance) while in a theory with a [gauge field](wikipedia://en/23912155), they must be gauge invariant.

Terms that contain the product of two fields and no [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) are known as *mass terms*, with these giving [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) to the fields.[^o15] For example, a single real scalar field $\phi(x)$ of mass $m$ has a mass term given by

$$\mathcal L_m = -\frac{1}{2}m^2 \phi^2(x).$$

The other terms that have two fields, those with at least one derivative, are known as *[kinetic terms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_term)*. They make fields [dynamical](wikipedia://en/9087), with most theories requiring a restriction of at most two derivatives in kinetic terms to [preserve probabililties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitarity_%28physics%29) in a [quantum theory](wikipedia://en/25202). They are also usually [positive-definite](wikipedia://en/40326) to ensure positive energies. For example, the kinetic term for a relativistic real scalar field is given by

$$\mathcal L_k = \frac{1}{2}\partial_\mu \phi \partial^\mu \phi.$$

Fields with no kinetic terms can also be found, playing the role of [auxiliary fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auxiliary_field), background fields, or [currents](wikipedia://en/1097925). Theories with only kinetic and mass terms, form [free field theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_field).

Any term with more than two fields per term is known as an *interaction term*.[^o16] The presence of these gives rise to interacting theories where particles can [scatter](wikipedia://en/164483) off each other. The coefficients in front of these terms are known as [coupling constants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupling_constant) and they dictate the strength of the interaction. For example, a [quartic interaction](wikipedia://en/1382023) in a real scalar field theory is given by

$$\mathcal L_i = -\frac{g}{4!} \phi^4,$$

where $g$ is its coupling constant. This term gives rise to scattering processes whereby two scalar fields can scatter off each other. Interacting terms can have any number of derivatives, with each derivative providing a [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431) dependence to the scattering term as can be seen by going into [momentum space](wikipedia://en/36740699).

Terms with only one field are known as *tadpole terms* since they give rise to [tadpole Feynman diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tadpole_%28physics%29).[^o15] In theories with [translational symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translational_symmetry), such terms can usually be eliminated by redefining some of the fields though a shift.

Constant terms, those with no fields, have no physical consequences in non-gravitational theories.[^o17] In classical field theories, the equations of motion only depend on variations of the Lagrangian, so constant terms play no role. In quantum field theories they only provide an irrelevant overall multiplicative term to the [partition function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_function_%28quantum_field_theory%29), so again play no role. Physically this is because in these theories there is no absolute [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) scale as the [potential energy](wikipedia://en/23703) can always be shifted by an arbitrary constant without altering the physics. However, in [gravitational systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) the constant terms are multiplied by the metric determinant, coupling them to the spacetime. They play the role of the [cosmological constant](wikipedia://en/38992), directly affecting the dynamics of the theory at both a classical and quantum level.

Polynomial terms are often expressed with certain [canonical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical) normalizations, used to simplify the [Feynman rules](wikipedia://en/11617) that are derived from them. Usually one divides by the product of the [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606) of the [multipicity](wikipedia://en/19211790) of the fields. For example, in a theory with two real scalar fields, a term of the form $g\phi^n \varphi^m$ term would be divided by $n!m!$. Particles and [antiparticles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiparticle) are distinguished in this counting, so that a complex scalar field term of the form $g'\bar \phi^p\phi^p$ is divided by $p!p!$ rather than $(2p)!$.

## Examples {#Examples}

A large variety of physical systems have been formulated in terms of Lagrangians over fields. Below is a sampling of some of the most common ones found in physics textbooks on field theory.

### Newtonian gravity {#Newtonian_gravity}

The Lagrangian density for Newtonian gravity is:

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},t)= - {1 \over 8 \pi G} (\nabla \Phi (\mathbf{x},t))^2 - \rho (\mathbf{x},t) \Phi (\mathbf{x},t)$$

where $\Phi$ is the [gravitational potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_potential), $\rho$ is the mass density, and $G$ in m<sup>3</sup>·kg<sup>−1</sup>·s<sup>−2</sup> is the [gravitational constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_constant). The density $\mathcal{L}$ has units of J·m<sup>−3</sup>. Here the interaction term involves a continuous mass density *ρ* in kg·m<sup>−3</sup>. This is necessary because using a point source for a field would result in mathematical difficulties.

This Lagrangian can be written in the form of $\mathcal{L} = T - V$, with the $T = -(\nabla \Phi)^2 / 8\pi G$ providing a kinetic term, and the interaction $V=\rho \Phi$ the potential term. See also [Nordström's theory of gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordström's_theory_of_gravitation) for how this could be modified to deal with changes over time. This form is reprised in the next example of a scalar field theory.

The variation of the integral with respect to $\Phi$ is:

$$\delta \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},t) = - \rho (\mathbf{x},t) \delta\Phi (\mathbf{x},t) - {2 \over 8 \pi G} (\nabla \Phi (\mathbf{x},t)) \cdot (\nabla \delta\Phi (\mathbf{x},t)) .$$

After integrating by parts, discarding the total integral, and dividing out by $\delta\Phi$ the formula becomes:

$$0 = - \rho (\mathbf{x},t) + \frac{1}{4 \pi G} \nabla \cdot \nabla \Phi (\mathbf{x},t)$$

which is equivalent to:

$$4 \pi G \rho (\mathbf{x},t) = \nabla^2 \Phi (\mathbf{x},t)$$

which yields [Gauss's law for gravity](wikipedia://en/16704344).

### Scalar field theory {#Scalar_field_theory}

The Lagrangian for a scalar field moving in a potential $V(\phi)$ can be written as

$$\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\partial^\mu\phi\partial_\mu\phi - V(\phi) = \frac{1}{2}\partial^\mu\phi\partial_\mu\phi - \frac{1}{2}m^2\phi^2 - \sum_{n=3}^\infty \frac{1}{n!} g_n\phi^n$$

It is not at all an accident that the scalar theory resembles the undergraduate textbook Lagrangian $L=T-V$ for the [kinetic term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_term) of a free point particle written as $T=mv^2/2$. The scalar theory is the field-theory generalization of a particle moving in a potential. When the $V(\phi)$ is the [Mexican hat potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_hat_potential), the resulting fields are termed the [Higgs fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_field).

### Sigma model Lagrangian {#Sigma_model_Lagrangian}

The [sigma model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_model) describes the motion of a scalar point particle constrained to move on a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652), such as a circle or a sphere. It generalizes the case of scalar and vector fields, that is, fields constrained to move on a flat manifold. The Lagrangian is commonly written in one of three equivalent forms:

$$\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2} \mathrm{d}\phi \wedge {*\mathrm{d}\phi}$$

where the $\mathrm{d}$ is the [differential](wikipedia://en/1558510). An equivalent expression is

$$\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n g_{ij}(\phi) \; \partial^\mu \phi_i \partial_\mu \phi_j$$

with $g_{ij}$ the [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) on the manifold of the field; i.e. the fields $\phi_i$ are just [local coordinates](wikipedia://en/304139) on the [coordinate chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_chart) of the manifold. A third common form is

$$\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\left(L_\mu L^\mu\right)$$

with

$$L_\mu=U^{-1}\partial_\mu U$$

and $U \in \mathrm{SU}(N)$, the [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) [SU(N)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SU%28N%29). This group can be replaced by any Lie group, or, more generally, by a [symmetric space](wikipedia://en/1642877). The trace is just the [Killing form](wikipedia://en/982386) in hiding; the Killing form provides a quadratic form on the field manifold, the lagrangian is then just the pullback of this form. Alternately, the Lagrangian can also be seen as the pullback of the [Maurer–Cartan form](wikipedia://en/964177) to the base spacetime.

In general, sigma models exhibit [topological soliton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_soliton) solutions. The most famous and well-studied of these is the [Skyrmion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skyrmion), which serves as a model of the [nucleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleon) that has withstood the test of time.

### Electromagnetism in special relativity {#Electromagnetism_in_special_relativity}

Consider a point particle, a charged particle, interacting with the [electromagnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field). The interaction terms

$$- q \phi (\mathbf{x}(t),t) + q \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) \cdot \mathbf{A} (\mathbf{x}(t),t)$$

are replaced by terms involving a continuous charge density ρ in A·s·m<sup>−3</sup> and current density $\mathbf{j}$ in A·m<sup>−2</sup>. The resulting Lagrangian density for the electromagnetic field is:

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},t) = - \rho (\mathbf{x},t) \phi (\mathbf{x},t) + \mathbf{j} (\mathbf{x},t) \cdot \mathbf{A} (\mathbf{x},t) + {\epsilon_0 \over 2} {E}^2 (\mathbf{x},t) - {1 \over {2 \mu_0}} {B}^2 (\mathbf{x},t) .$$

Varying this with respect to $\phi$, we get

$$0 = - \rho (\mathbf{x},t) + \epsilon_0 \nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} (\mathbf{x},t)$$

which yields [Gauss' law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss'_law).

Varying instead with respect to $\mathbf{A}$, we get

$$0 = \mathbf{j} (\mathbf{x},t) + \epsilon_0 \dot{\mathbf{E}} (\mathbf{x},t) - {1 \over \mu_0} \nabla \times \mathbf{B} (\mathbf{x},t)$$

which yields [Ampère's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampère's_law).

Using [tensor notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_notation), we can write all this more compactly. The term $- \rho \phi (\mathbf{x},t) + \mathbf{j} \cdot \mathbf{A}$ is actually the inner product of two [four-vectors](wikipedia://en/226424). We package the charge density into the current 4-vector and the potential into the potential 4-vector. These two new vectors are

$$j^\mu = (\rho,\mathbf{j})\quad\text{and}\quad A_\mu = (-\phi,\mathbf{A})$$

We can then write the interaction term as

$$- \rho \phi + \mathbf{j} \cdot \mathbf{A} = j^\mu A_\mu$$

Additionally, we can package the E and B fields into what is known as the [electromagnetic tensor](wikipedia://en/1912367) $F_{\mu\nu}$. We define this tensor as

$$F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\mu$$

The term we are looking out for turns out to be

$${\epsilon_0 \over 2} {E}^2 - {1 \over {2 \mu_0}} {B}^2 = -\frac{1}{4\mu_0} F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}= -\frac{1}{4\mu_0} F_{\mu\nu} F_{\rho\sigma}\eta^{\mu\rho}\eta^{\nu\sigma}$$

We have made use of the [Minkowski metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_metric) to raise the indices on the EMF tensor. In this notation, Maxwell's equations are

$$\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=-\mu_0 j^\nu\quad\text{and}\quad \epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}\partial_\nu F_{\lambda\sigma}=0$$

where ε is the [Levi-Civita tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi-Civita_tensor). So the Lagrange density for electromagnetism in special relativity written in terms of Lorentz vectors and tensors is

$$\mathcal{L}(x) = j^\mu(x) A_\mu(x) - \frac{1}{4\mu_0} F_{\mu\nu}(x) F^{\mu\nu}(x)$$

In this notation it is apparent that classical electromagnetism is a Lorentz-invariant theory. By the [equivalence principle](wikipedia://en/857235), it becomes simple to extend the notion of electromagnetism to curved spacetime.[^o36][^o24]

### Electromagnetism and the Yang–Mills equations {#Electromagnetism_and_the_Yang–Mills_equations}

Using [differential forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_forms), the electromagnetic action *S* in vacuum on a (pseudo-) Riemannian manifold $\mathcal M$ can be written (using [natural units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_units), $c = \varepsilon_{0} = 1$) as

$$\mathcal S[\mathbf{A}] = -\int_{\mathcal{M}} \left(\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{F} \wedge \ast\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{A} \wedge\ast \mathbf{J}\right) .$$

Here, **A** stands for the electromagnetic potential 1-form, **J** is the current 1-form, $\mathbf{F}$ is the field strength 2-form and the star denotes the [Hodge star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_star) operator. This is exactly the same Lagrangian as in the section above, except that the treatment here is coordinate-free; expanding the integrand into a basis yields the identical, lengthy expression. Note that with forms, an additional integration measure is not necessary because forms have coordinate differentials built in. Variation of the action leads to

$$\mathrm{d} {\ast}\mathbf{F} = {\ast}\mathbf{J} .$$

These are Maxwell's equations for the electromagnetic potential. Substituting $\mathbf{F} = d\mathbf{A}$ immediately yields the equation for the fields,

$$\mathrm{d}\mathbf{F} = 0$$

because $\mathbf{F}$ is an [exact form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exact_form).

The **A** field can be understood to be the [affine connection](wikipedia://en/607690) on a [U(1)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U%281%29)-[fiber bundle](wikipedia://en/276526). That is, classical electrodynamics, all of its effects and equations, can be *completely* understood in terms of a [circle bundle](wikipedia://en/2794417) over [Minkowski spacetime](wikipedia://en/230488).

The [Yang–Mills equations](wikipedia://en/35544664) can be written in exactly the same form as above, by replacing the [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) [U(1)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U%281%29) of electromagnetism by an arbitrary Lie group. In the [Standard Model](wikipedia://en/47641), it is conventionally taken to be $\mathrm{SU}(3) \times \mathrm{SU}(2) \times \mathrm{U}(1)$ although the general case is of general interest. In all cases, there is no need for any quantization to be performed. Although the Yang–Mills equations are historically rooted in quantum field theory, the above equations are purely classical.[^o4]

### Chern–Simons functional {#Chern–Simons_functional}

In the same vein as the above, one can consider the action in one dimension less, i.e. in a [contact geometry](wikipedia://en/476398) setting. This gives the [Chern–Simons functional](wikipedia://en/294383). It is written as

$$\mathcal S[\mathbf{A}] = \int_{\mathcal{M}} \mathrm {tr} \left(\mathbf{A} \wedge d\mathbf{A} + \frac{2}{3}\mathbf{A} \wedge \mathbf{A} \wedge \mathbf{A}\right) .$$

[Chern–Simons theory](wikipedia://en/391908) was deeply explored in physics, as a toy model for a broad range of geometric phenomena that might be expected to be found in a [Grand Unified Theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Unified_Theory).

### Ginzburg–Landau Lagrangian {#Ginzburg–Landau_Lagrangian}

The Lagrangian density for [Ginzburg–Landau theory](wikipedia://en/396286) combines the Lagrangian for the [scalar field theory](wikipedia://en/5173456) with the Lagrangian for the [Yang–Mills action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang–Mills_action). It may be written as:[^o28]

$$\mathcal{L}(\psi, A)=\vert F \vert^2 + \vert D \psi\vert^2 + \frac{1}{4} \left( \sigma-\vert\psi\vert^2\right)^2$$

where $\psi$ is a [section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_%28fibre_bundle%29) of a [vector bundle](wikipedia://en/276524) with fiber $\mathbb{C}^n$. The $\psi$ corresponds to the order parameter in a [superconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductor); equivalently, it corresponds to the [Higgs field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_field), after noting that the second term is the famous ["Sombrero hat" potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_hat_potential). The field $A$ is the (non-Abelian) gauge field, i.e. the [Yang–Mills field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang–Mills_field) and $F$ is its field-strength. The [Euler–Lagrange equations](wikipedia://en/294995) for the Ginzburg–Landau functional are the [Yang–Mills equations](wikipedia://en/35544664)

$$D {\star} D\psi = \frac{1}{2}\left(\sigma - \vert\psi\vert^2\right)\psi$$

and

$$D {\star} F=-\operatorname{Re}\langle D\psi, \psi\rangle$$

where ${\star}$ is the [Hodge star operator](wikipedia://en/310914), i.e. the fully antisymmetric tensor. These equations are closely related to the [Yang–Mills–Higgs equations](wikipedia://en/25723035). Another closely related Lagrangian is found in [Seiberg–Witten theory](wikipedia://en/3526032).

### Dirac Lagrangian {#Dirac_Lagrangian}

The Lagrangian density for a [Dirac field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_field) is:[^o30]

$$\mathcal{L} = \bar \psi ( i \hbar c {\partial}\!\!\!/\ - mc^2) \psi$$

where $\psi$ is a [Dirac spinor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_spinor), $\bar \psi = \psi^\dagger \gamma^0$ is its [Dirac adjoint](wikipedia://en/695917), and ${\partial}\!\!\!/$ is [Feynman slash notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feynman_slash_notation) for $\gamma^\sigma \partial_\sigma$. There is no particular need to focus on Dirac spinors in the classical theory. The [Weyl spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_spinor) provide a more general foundation; they can be constructed directly from the [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305) of spacetime; the construction works in any number of dimensions,[^o31] and the Dirac spinors appear as a special case. Weyl spinors have the additional advantage that they can be used in a [vielbein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vielbein) for the metric on a Riemannian manifold; this enables the concept of a [spin structure](wikipedia://en/2844303), which, roughly speaking, is a way of formulating spinors consistently in a curved spacetime.

### Quantum electrodynamic Lagrangian {#Quantum_electrodynamic_Lagrangian}

The Lagrangian density for [QED](wikipedia://en/25268) combines the Lagrangian for the Dirac field together with the Lagrangian for electrodynamics in a gauge-invariant way. It is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QED}} = \bar \psi (i\hbar c {D}\!\!\!\!/\ - mc^2) \psi - {1 \over 4\mu_0} F_{\mu \nu} F^{\mu \nu}$$

where $F^{\mu \nu}$ is the [electromagnetic tensor](wikipedia://en/1912367), *D* is the [gauge covariant derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_covariant_derivative), and ${D}\!\!\!\!/$ is [Feynman notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feynman_slash_notation) for $\gamma^\sigma D_\sigma$ with $D_\sigma = \partial_\sigma - i e A_\sigma$ where $A_\sigma$ is the [electromagnetic four-potential](wikipedia://en/731884). Although the word "quantum" appears in the above, this is a historical artifact. The definition of the Dirac field requires no quantization whatsoever, it can be written as a purely classical field of anti-commuting [Weyl spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_spinor) constructed from first principles from a [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305).[^o31] The full gauge-invariant classical formulation is given in Bleecker.[^o4]

### Quantum chromodynamic Lagrangian {#Quantum_chromodynamic_Lagrangian}

The Lagrangian density for [quantum chromodynamics](wikipedia://en/25264) combines the Lagrangian for one or more massive [Dirac spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_spinor) with the Lagrangian for the [Yang–Mills action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang–Mills_action), which describes the dynamics of a gauge field; the combined Lagrangian is gauge invariant. It may be written as:[^o34]

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QCD}} = \sum_n \bar\psi_n \left( i\hbar c{D}\!\!\!\!/\ - m_n c^2 \right) \psi_n - {1\over 4} G^\alpha {}_{\mu\nu} G_\alpha {}^{\mu\nu}$$

where *D* is the QCD [gauge covariant derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_covariant_derivative#Quantum_chromodynamics), *n* = 1, 2, ...6 counts the [quark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark) types, and $G^\alpha {}_{\mu\nu}\!$ is the [gluon field strength tensor](wikipedia://en/38184895). As for the electrodynamics case above, the appearance of the word "quantum" above only acknowledges its historical development. The Lagrangian and its gauge invariance can be formulated and treated in a purely classical fashion.[^o4][^o31]

### Einstein gravity {#Einstein_gravity}

The Lagrange density for general relativity in the presence of matter fields is

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{GR} = \mathcal{L}_\text{EH}+\mathcal{L}_\text{matter} = \frac{c^4}{16\pi G} \left(R-2\Lambda\right) + \mathcal{L}_\text{matter}$$

where $\Lambda$ is the [cosmological constant](wikipedia://en/38992), $R$ is the [curvature scalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curvature_scalar), which is the [Ricci tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_tensor) contracted with the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795), and the [Ricci tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_tensor) is the [Riemann tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_tensor) contracted with a [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890). The integral of $\mathcal{L}_\text{EH}$ is known as the [Einstein–Hilbert action](wikipedia://en/855776). The Riemann tensor is the [tidal force](wikipedia://en/30719) tensor, and is constructed out of [Christoffel symbols](wikipedia://en/1401020) and derivatives of Christoffel symbols, which define the [metric connection](wikipedia://en/4794503) on spacetime. The gravitational field itself was historically ascribed to the metric tensor; the modern view is that the connection is "more fundamental". This is due to the understanding that one can write connections with non-zero [torsion](wikipedia://en/4342484). These alter the metric without altering the geometry one bit. As to the actual "direction in which gravity points" (e.g. on the surface of the Earth, it points down), this comes from the Riemann tensor: it is the thing that describes the "gravitational force field" that moving bodies feel and react to. (This last statement must be qualified: there is no "force field" *per se*; moving bodies follow [geodesics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesics) on the manifold described by the connection. They move in a "[straight line](wikipedia://en/285595)".)

The Lagrangian for general relativity can also be written in a form that makes it manifestly similar to the Yang–Mills equations. This is called the Einstein–Yang–Mills action principle. This is done by noting that most of differential geometry works "just fine" on bundles with an [affine connection](wikipedia://en/607690) and arbitrary Lie group. Then, plugging in SO(3,1) for that symmetry group, i.e. for the [frame fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_field), one obtains the equations above.[^o4][^o31]

Substituting this Lagrangian into the Euler–Lagrange equation and taking the metric tensor $g_{\mu\nu}$ as the field, we obtain the [Einstein field equations](wikipedia://en/424540)

$$R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}Rg_{\mu\nu}+g_{\mu\nu}\Lambda=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}T_{\mu\nu}\,.$$

$T_{\mu\nu}$ is the [energy momentum tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_momentum_tensor) and is defined by

$$T_{\mu\nu} \equiv \frac{-2}{\sqrt{-g}}\frac{\delta (\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{matter}} \sqrt{-g}) }{\delta g^{\mu\nu}} = -2 \frac{\delta \mathcal{L}_\mathrm{matter}}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}} + g_{\mu\nu} \mathcal{L}_\mathrm{matter}\,.$$

where $g$ is the determinant of the metric tensor when regarded as a matrix. Generally, in general relativity, the integration measure of the action of Lagrange density is $\sqrt{-g}\,d^4x$. This makes the integral coordinate independent, as the root of the metric determinant is equivalent to the [Jacobian determinant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_determinant). The minus sign is a consequence of the metric signature (the determinant by itself is negative).[^o36] This is an example of the [volume form](wikipedia://en/1855811), previously discussed, becoming manifest in non-flat spacetime.

### Electromagnetism in general relativity {#Electromagnetism_in_general_relativity}

The Lagrange density of electromagnetism in general relativity also contains the Einstein–Hilbert action from above. The pure electromagnetic Lagrangian is precisely a matter Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}_\text{matter}$. The Lagrangian is

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(x) &= j^\mu (x) A_\mu (x) - {1 \over 4\mu_0} F_{\mu \nu}(x) F_{\rho\sigma}(x) g^{\mu\rho}(x) g^{\nu\sigma}(x) + \frac{c^4}{16\pi G}R(x)\\ &= \mathcal{L}_\text{Maxwell} + \mathcal{L}_\text{Einstein–Hilbert}. \end{aligned}$$

This Lagrangian is obtained by simply replacing the Minkowski metric in the above flat Lagrangian with a more general (possibly curved) metric $g_{\mu\nu}(x)$. We can generate the Einstein Field Equations in the presence of an EM field using this lagrangian. The energy-momentum tensor is

$$T^{\mu\nu}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{-g(x)}}\frac{\delta}{\delta g_{\mu\nu}(x)}\mathcal{S}_\text{Maxwell}=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\left(F^{\mu}_{\text{ }\lambda}(x)F^{\nu\lambda}(x)-\frac{1}{4}g^{\mu\nu}(x)F_{\rho\sigma}(x)F^{\rho\sigma}(x)\right)$$

It can be shown that this energy momentum tensor is traceless, i.e. that

$$T = g_{\mu\nu}T^{\mu\nu} = 0$$

If we take the trace of both sides of the Einstein Field Equations, we obtain

$$R = -\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}T$$

So the tracelessness of the energy momentum tensor implies that the curvature scalar in an electromagnetic field vanishes. The Einstein equations are then

$$R^{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\frac{1}{\mu_0}\left({F^{\mu}}_{\lambda}(x)F^{\nu\lambda}(x) - \frac{1}{4} g^{\mu\nu}(x)F_{\rho\sigma}(x)F^{\rho\sigma}(x)\right)$$

Additionally, Maxwell's equations are

$$D_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu} = -\mu_0 j^\nu$$

where $D_\mu$ is the [covariant derivative](wikipedia://en/431848). For free space, we can set the current tensor equal to zero, $j^\mu = 0$. Solving both Einstein and Maxwell's equations around a spherically symmetric mass distribution in free space leads to the [Reissner–Nordström charged black hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reissner–Nordström_black_hole), with the defining line element (written in [natural units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_units) and with charge $Q$):[^o36]

$$\mathrm{d}s^2 = \left(1-\frac{2M}{r}+\frac{Q^2}{r^2}\right)\mathrm{d}t^2- \left(1-\frac{2M}{r}+\frac{Q^2}{r^2}\right)^{-1}\mathrm{d}r^2 -r^2\mathrm{d}\Omega^2$$

One possible way of unifying the electromagnetic and gravitational Lagrangians (by using a fifth dimension) is given by [Kaluza–Klein theory](wikipedia://en/17070).[^o4] Effectively, one constructs an affine bundle, just as for the Yang–Mills equations given earlier, and then considers the action separately on the 4-dimensional and the 1-dimensional parts. Such [factorizations](wikipedia://en/580384), such as the fact that the 7-sphere can be written as a product of the 4-sphere and the 3-sphere, or that the 11-sphere is a product of the 4-sphere and the 7-sphere, accounted for much of the early excitement that a [theory of everything](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_everything) had been found. Unfortunately, the 7-sphere proved not large enough to enclose all of the [Standard Model](wikipedia://en/47641), dashing these hopes.

### Additional examples {#Additional_examples}

- The [BF model](wikipedia://en/1428123) Lagrangian, short for "Background Field", describes a system with trivial dynamics, when written on a flat spacetime manifold. On a topologically non-trivial spacetime, the system will have non-trivial classical solutions, which may be interpreted as [solitons](wikipedia://en/51654) or [instantons](wikipedia://en/296077). A variety of extensions exist, forming the foundations for [topological field theories](wikipedia://en/443757).
