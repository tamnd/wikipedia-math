# Vertical and horizontal

*Image: Horizontal (PSF).png.* A diagram showing vertical and horizontal lines

*Image: Horizontal vertical and diagonal.svg.* Horizontal (left), vertical (center) and diagonal (right) double arrows.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Vertical and horizontal"}

In [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), [geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography) and related sciences, a [line](wikipedia://en/946975) or [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) passing by a given point is said to be **vertical** if it contains the local [gravity direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_direction) at that point.[^o2] Conversely, a line or plane is said to be **horizontal** (or *leveled*) if it is perpendicular to the vertical at a given point. By extension, the concept applies to finite objects contained by a line or a plane, such as [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860), [plane regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_region), [vectors](wikipedia://en/23218956), [directions](wikipedia://en/54516626), etc.

:::

A [surface](wikipedia://en/50328015) is horizontal if its [tangent planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_plane) are everywhere perpendicular to the gravity vector at the tangent point or, equivalently, if the surface [normal vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_vector) is everywhere parallel to gravity, as in an [equigeopotential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equigeopotential) surface.

More generally, something that is *vertical* can be drawn from "up" to "down" (or down to up), such as the y-axis in the [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706).

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The word *horizontal* is derived from the Latin horizon, which derives from the Greek ὁρῐ́ζων, meaning 'separating' or 'marking a boundary'. The word *vertical* is derived from the late Latin *verticalis*, which is from the same root as *vertex*, meaning 'highest point' or more literally the 'turning point' such as in a whirlpool.

[Girard Desargues](wikipedia://en/358484) defined the vertical to be perpendicular to the [horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon) in his 1636 book *Perspective*.

## Geophysical definition {#Geophysical_definition}

### The plumb line and spirit level {#The_plumb_line_and_spirit_level}

*Image: Bubble on a marble shelf.PNG.* Spirit level bubble on a marble shelf tests for horizontality

*Image: Plumb bob.jpg.* A plumb bob

In physics, engineering and construction, the orientation designated as vertical is usually that along which a [plumb-bob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plumb-bob) hangs. Alternatively, a [spirit level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirit_level) that exploits the buoyancy of an air bubble and its tendency to go vertically upwards may be used to test for horizontality. A [water level device](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_level_device) may also be used to establish horizontality.

Modern rotary [laser levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_level) work on the same fundamental principle.[^o8][^o9]

### The spherical Earth {#The_spherical_Earth}

*Image: Vertical wall are never parallel.PNG.* Strictly, vertical orientations are never parallel on the surface of a spherical planet (except at opposite poles, where they are in a line).

When the curvature of the Earth is taken into account, the concepts of vertical and horizontal take on yet another meaning. On the surface of a smoothly spherical, homogenous, non-rotating planet, the plumb bob picks out as vertical the radial orientation. Strictly speaking, it is now no longer possible for vertical walls to be parallel: *all* verticals intersect. This fact has real practical applications in construction and civil engineering, e.g., the tops of the towers of a suspension bridge are further apart than at the bottom. [^o11]

*Image: Horizontal planes can intersect.PNG.* On a spherical planet, horizontal planes intersect. In the example shown, the blue line represents the horizontal tangent plane at the North pole, the red the horizontal tangent plane at an equatorial point. The two intersect at a right angle.

Also, horizontal planes can intersect when they are [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) planes to separated points on the surface of the Earth. In particular, a plane tangent to a point on the equator intersects the plane tangent to the [North Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pole) at a [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956). (See diagram). Furthermore, the [equatorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) plane is parallel to the tangent plane at the North Pole and as such has claim to be a horizontal plane. But it is. at the same time, a vertical plane for points on the equator. In this sense, a plane can, arguably, be *both* horizontal and vertical, horizontal *at one place*, and vertical *at another*.

### Further complications {#Further_complications}

For a spinning earth, the plumb line deviates from the radial orientation as a function of latitude.[^o13] Only on the equator and at the North and South Poles does the plumb line align with the local radius. The situation is actually even more complicated because Earth is not a [homogeneous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous) smooth sphere. It is a non homogeneous, non spherical, knobby planet in motion, and the vertical not only need not lie along a radial, it may even be curved and be varying with time. On a smaller scale, a mountain to one side may deflect the plumb bob away from the true [zenith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenith).[^o14]

On a larger scale the gravitational field of the Earth, which is at least approximately radial near the Earth, is not radial when it is affected by the Moon at higher altitudes.[^o15][^o16]

### Independence of horizontal and vertical motions {#Independence_of_horizontal_and_vertical_motions}

Neglecting the curvature of the earth, horizontal and vertical motions of a projectile moving under gravity are independent of each other.[^o18] Vertical displacement of a projectile is not affected by the horizontal component of the launch velocity, and, conversely, the horizontal displacement is unaffected by the vertical component. The notion dates at least as far back as Galileo.[^o19]

When the curvature of the Earth is taken into account, the independence of the two motions does *not* hold. For example, even a projectile fired in a horizontal orientation (i.e., with a zero vertical component) may leave the surface of the spherical Earth and indeed escape altogether.[^o20]

## Mathematical definition {#Mathematical_definition}

### In two dimensions {#In_two_dimensions}

*Image: Vertical horizontal.PNG.* In two dimensions. 1. The vertical orientation is designated. 2. The horizontal is perpendicular to the vertical. Through any point P, there is exactly one vertical and exactly one horizontal. Alternatively, one can start by designating the horizontal orientation.

In the context of a 1-dimensional orthogonal [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706) on a Euclidean plane, to say that a line is horizontal or vertical, an initial designation has to be made. One can start off by designating the vertical orientation, usually labelled the Y orientation.[^o23] The horizontal orientation, usually labelled the X orientation,[^o24] is then automatically determined. Or, one can do it the other way around, i.e., nominate the *x*-axis, in which case the *y*-axis is then automatically determined. There is no special reason to choose the horizontal over the vertical as the initial designation: the two orientation are on par in this respect.

The following hold in the two-dimensional case:

1. Through any point P in the plane, there is one and only one *vertical line* within the plane and one and only one *horizontal line* within the plane. This symmetry breaks down as one moves to the three-dimensional case.
1. A *vertical line* is any line parallel to the vertical orientation. A *horizontal line* is any line normal to a vertical line.
1. Horizontal lines do not cross each other.
1. Vertical lines do not cross each other.

Not all of these elementary geometric facts are true in the 3-D context.

### In three dimensions {#In_three_dimensions}

In the three-dimensional case, the situation is more complicated as now one has horizontal and vertical planes in addition to horizontal and vertical lines. Consider a point P and designate a orientation through P as vertical. A plane which contains P and is normal to the designated orientation is the *[horizontal plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_plane)* at P. Any plane going through P, normal to the horizontal plane is a *[vertical plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_plane)* at P. Through any point P, there is one and only one horizontal plane but a *multiplicity* of vertical planes. This is a new feature that emerges in three dimensions. The symmetry that exists in the two-dimensional case no longer holds.

### In the classroom {#In_the_classroom}

*Image: Mystery of the horizontal y-axis.PNG.* The *y*-axis on the wall is vertical, but the one on the table is horizontal.

In the 2-dimension case, as mentioned already, the usual designation of the vertical coincides with the [y-axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y-axis) in co-ordinate geometry. This convention can cause confusion in the classroom. For the teacher, writing perhaps on a white board, the *y*-axis really is vertical in the sense of the plumbline verticality but for the student the axis may well lie on a horizontal table.

## Discussion {#Discussion}

Horizontal plane section

*Image: Bubble on a marble shelf.PNG.* A spirit level on a shelf

Although the word horizontal is commonly used in daily life and language (see below), it is subject to many misconceptions.

- The concept of horizontality only makes sense in the context of a clearly measurable gravity field, i.e., in the 'neighborhood' of a planet, star, etc. When the gravity field becomes very weak (the masses are too small or too distant from the point of interest), the notion of being horizontal loses its meaning.

*Image: Verticals at separate points.PNG.* Verticals at two separate points are not parallel. The same holds for their associated horizontal planes.

- A plane is horizontal only at the chosen point. Horizontal planes at two separate points are not parallel, they intersect.
- In general, a horizontal plane will only be perpendicular to a vertical orientation if both are specifically defined with respect to the same point: a orientation is only vertical at the point of reference. Thus both horizontality and verticality are strictly speaking local concepts, and it is always necessary to state to which location the orientation or the plane refers to. (1) the same restriction applies to the straight lines contained within the plane: they are horizontal only at the point of reference, and (2) those straight lines contained in the plane but not passing by the reference point are not necessarily horizontal anywhere.

*Image: Gravitational field due to knobbly planet.PNG.* Field lines for a non-homogeneous knobbly planet in motion may be curved. The white, red and blue bits illustrate the planet's heterogeneity.

- In reality, the gravity field of a heterogeneous planet such as [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228) is deformed due to the inhomogeneous spatial distribution of materials with different [densities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density). Actual horizontal planes are thus not even parallel even if their reference points are along the same vertical line, since a vertical line is slightly curved.
- At any given location, the total gravitational force is not quite constant over [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time), because the objects that generate the gravity are moving. For instance, on Earth the horizontal plane at a given point (as determined by a pair of [spirit levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirit_level)) changes with the position of the Moon (air, sea and land [tides](wikipedia://en/30718)).
- On a rotating planet such as Earth, the strictly gravitational pull of the planet (and other celestial objects such as the Moon, the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun), etc.) is different from the apparent net force (e.g., on a free-falling object) that can be measured in the laboratory or in the field. This difference is the [centrifugal force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrifugal_force_%28fictitious%29) associated with the planet's rotation. This is a [fictitious force](wikipedia://en/1082841): it only arises when calculations or experiments are conducted in [non-inertial frames of reference](wikipedia://en/14838), such as the surface of the Earth.

In general or in practice, something that is horizontal can be [drawn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drawing) from left to right (or right to left), such as the x-axis in the [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706).

### Practical use in daily life {#Practical_use_in_daily_life}

*Image: Mystery of the horizontal y-axis.PNG.* The y-axis on the wall is vertical, but the one on the table is horizontal.

The concept of a horizontal plane is thus anything but simple, although, in practice, most of these effects and variations are rather small: they are measurable and can be predicted with great accuracy, but they may not greatly affect our daily life.

This dichotomy between the apparent simplicity of a concept and an actual complexity of defining (and measuring) it in scientific terms arises from the fact that the typical linear scales and dimensions of relevance in daily life are 3 [orders of magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orders_of_magnitude) (or more) smaller than the size of the Earth. Hence, the world appears to be flat locally, and horizontal planes in nearby locations appear to be parallel. Such statements are nevertheless approximations; whether they are acceptable in any particular context or application depends on the applicable requirements, in particular in terms of accuracy. In graphical contexts, such as [drawing and drafting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_drawing) and Co-ordinate geometry on rectangular paper, it is very common to associate one of the dimensions of the paper with a horizontal, even though the entire sheet of paper is standing on a flat horizontal (or slanted) table. In this case, the horizontal orientation is typically from the left side of the paper to the right side. This is purely conventional (although it is somehow 'natural' when drawing a natural scene as it is seen in reality), and may lead to misunderstandings or misconceptions, especially in an educational context.
