# Dyadic derivative

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dyadic derivative"}

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), the **dyadic derivative** is a concept that extends the notion of [classical differentiation](wikipedia://en/7921) to functions defined on the [dyadic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyadic_group) or the dyadic field. Unlike classical differentiation, which is based on the [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602) of difference quotients, dyadic differentiation is defined using dyadic (binary) addition and reflects the discontinuous nature of [Walsh functions](wikipedia://en/380839).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### Pointwise dyadic derivative {#Pointwise_dyadic_derivative}

For a function $f$ defined on \[0,1), the first *pointwise dyadic derivative* of $f$ at a point $x$ is defined as:

$$f^{[1]}(x) = \lim_{m \to \infty} \sum_{j=0}^{m-1} 2^{j-1}[f(x) - f(x \oplus 2^{-j-1})]$$

if this limit exists. Here, $\oplus$ denotes the dyadic addition operation, which is defined using the [dyadic (binary) representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_representation) of numbers.[^o6] That is, if

$x = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} x_j 2^{-j-1}$ and $y = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} y_j 2^{-j-1}$ with $x_j, y_j \in \{0, 1\}$,

then

$$x \oplus y = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} (x_j \oplus y_j) 2^{-j-1},$$

where

$x_j \oplus y_j = (x_j + y_j) \pmod 2$.[^o6][^o12]

Higher-order dyadic derivatives are defined [recursively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursion_%28mathematics%29): $f^{[r]}(x) = (f^{[r-1]})^{[1]}(x)$ for $r \in \mathbb N$.[^o6]

### Strong dyadic derivative {#Strong_dyadic_derivative}

The *strong dyadic derivative* is defined in the context of [function spaces](wikipedia://en/340630). Let $X(0, 1)$ denote one of the function spaces $L^p(0, 1)$ for $1 \le p \le \infty$ ([*L*<sup>*p*</sup> space](wikipedia://en/45194)); $L^\infty(0, 1)$ ([*L*<sup>*∞*</sup> space](wikipedia://en/46395425)); or $C^\oplus[0, 1]$ (the space of dyadically continuous functions). If $f \in X(0, 1)$ and there exists $g \in X(0, 1)$ such that

$$\lim_{m \to \infty} \left\| \sum_{j=0}^{m-1} 2^{j-1}[f(\cdot) - f(\cdot \oplus 2^{-j-1})] - g(\cdot) \right\|_X = 0,$$

then $g$ is called the first strong dyadic derivative of $f$, denoted by $g = D^{[1]}f$.[^o6] Higher-order derivatives can be defined recursively similar to pointwise dyadic derivatives.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

Similar to the classic derivative in calculus, the dyadic derivative possesses several properties.

### Linearity {#Linearity}

The dyadic derivative is a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator). If functions $f$ and $g$ are dyadically differentiable and $\alpha, \beta$ are [constants](wikipedia://en/48779551), then $\alpha f + \beta g$ is dyadically differentiable:

$(\alpha f + \beta g)^{[1]} = \alpha f^{[1]} + \beta g^{[1]}$.[^o10]

### Closure {#Closure}

The dyadic differentiation operator is closed; that is, if $f$ is in the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of the [operator](wikipedia://en/22213), then its dyadic derivative also belongs to the same function space.[^o12]

### Inverse operator {#Inverse_operator}

There exists a dyadic [integration operator](wikipedia://en/63688957) that serves as an [inverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_operator) to the dyadic differentiation operator, analogous to the [fundamental theorem of calculus](wikipedia://en/19468696).[^o14]

### Relationship to the Walsh-Fourier transform {#Relationship_to_the_Walsh-Fourier_transform}

For functions $f$ where $D^{[1]}f \in L_1(G)$ exists, the [Walsh-Fourier transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walsh-Fourier_transform) satisfies:

$$[D^{[1]}f]^{\wedge}(\chi) = |\chi|\hat{f}(\chi)$$

for all [characters](wikipedia://en/293418) $\chi$, where $|\chi|$ represents the [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) of the character.[^o16]

### Eigenfunctions {#Eigenfunctions}

The Walsh functions $\psi_k$ are [eigenfunctions](wikipedia://en/228601) of the dyadic differentiation operator with corresponding eigenvalues related to their index:

$$D^{[1]}\psi_k = \psi_k^{[1]} = k\psi_k$$

and

$$D^{[r]}\psi_k = \psi_k^{[r]} = k^r \psi_k.$$

This eigenfunction property makes Walsh functions naturally suited for analysis involving dyadic derivatives, similar to how [complex exponentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_exponential) $e^{ikx}$ are eigenfunctions of classical differentiation.[^o6]

### Characterization of differentiable functions {#Characterization_of_differentiable_functions}

Thanks to a generalization of a result of Butzer and Wagner,[^o6]

> **Theorem (Skvorcov—Wade).** Let $f$ be continuous on $[0, 1)$, and let $f^{[1]}$ exist for all but countably many points $x \in (0, 1)$. Then $f$ is [constant](wikipedia://en/320861).[^o19]

This result implies that it is more interesting to consider functions that are *not* continuous over the entire interval. A generalization of the above result shows that:

> **Theorem.** A [bounded function](wikipedia://en/311509) defined on $[0,1)$ with a [countable set](wikipedia://en/6026) of [discontinuities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discontinuity_%28mathematics%29) (exclusively of [jump discontinuities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jump_discontinuities)) that have at most a finite number of cluster points is pointwise dyadically differentiable *except* on a countable set if and only if it is a [piecewise constant function](wikipedia://en/170939).[^o6]

## Examples {#Examples}

::: {#o22 .table cue=table}

| Function type | Example | Dyadic derivative | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Constant functions](wikipedia://en/320861) | $f(x) = C$ for all $x \in [0,1)$ | $f^{[1]}(x) = 0$ | Similar to classical calculus[^o6] |
| [Step functions](wikipedia://en/170939) | $g(x) = \begin{cases} 3, & x \in [0,1/4) \\ -1, & x \in [1/4,1) \end{cases}$ | $g^{[1]}(x) = \begin{cases} 6, & x \in [0,1/4) \\ -4, & x \in [1/4,1/2) \\ -2, & x \in [1/2,3/4) \\ 0, & x \in [3/4,1) \end{cases}$ | Creates additional [discontinuities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discontinuity_%28mathematics%29)[^o6] |
| [Dirichlet function](wikipedia://en/630005) | $d(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x \in [0,1) \cap \mathbb{Q} \\ 0, & x \in [0,1) \setminus \mathbb{Q} \end{cases}$ | $d^{[1]}(x) = 0$ for all $x \in [0,1)$ | Differentiable despite [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) discontinuities[^o6] |
| [Walsh functions](wikipedia://en/380839) | $\psi_k(x)$ | $\psi_k^{[1]}(x) = k\psi_k(x)$ | [Eigenfunctions](wikipedia://en/228601) of the dyadic derivative[^o10] |
| [Linear functions](wikipedia://en/152111) | $f(x) = x$ | Not dyadic differentiable | Contrasts with classical calculus[^o6] |

## History {#History}

The dyadic derivative was introduced by mathematician James Edmund Gibbs in the context of [Walsh functions](wikipedia://en/380839) and further developed by [Paul Butzer](wikipedia://en/74356919) and Heinz-Joseph Wagner.[^o29][^o30]

Further contributions came from C. W. Onneweer, who extended the concept to [fractional differentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_differentiation) and [*p*-adic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic) fields.[^o16] In 1979, Onneweer provided alternative definitions to the dyadic derivatives.[^o12]
