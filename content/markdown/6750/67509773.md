# Fibonacci group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fibonacci group"}

In mathematics, for a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $n \ge 2$, the *n*th **Fibonacci group**, denoted $F(2,n)$ or sometimes $F(n)$, is defined by *n* [generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generators_and_relations) $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ and *n* [relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generators_and_relations):

:::

- $$a_1 a_2 = a_3,$$
- $$a_2 a_3 = a_4,$$
- $$\dots$$
- $$a_{n-2} a_{n-1} = a_n,$$
- $$a_{n-1}a_n = a_1,$$
- $$a_n a_1 = a_2.$$

These [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) were introduced by [John Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Conway) in 1965.

The group $F(2,n)$ is of finite [order](wikipedia://en/493527) for $n=2,3,4,5,7$ (order 1,8,5,11,29, respectively), and infinite order for $n = 6$ and $n \ge 8$. The infinitude of $F(2,9)$ was proved by computer in 1990.

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Kaplansky's unit conjecture"}

## Kaplansky's unit conjecture {#Kaplansky's_unit_conjecture}

From a group $G$ and a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $K$ (or more generally a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404)), the [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) $K[G]$ is defined as the set of all finite formal $K$-linear combinations of elements of $G$ − that is, an element $a$ of $K[G]$ is of the form $a = \sum_{g \in G} \lambda_g g$, where $\lambda_g = 0$ for all but finitely many $g \in G$ so that the linear combination is finite. The (size of the) *support* of an element $a = \sum\nolimits_g \lambda_g g$ in $K[G]$, denoted $|\operatorname{supp} a\,|$, is the number of elements $g \in G$ such that $\lambda_g \neq 0$, i.e. the number of terms in the linear combination. The ring structure of $K[G]$ is the "obvious" one: the linear combinations are added "component-wise", i.e. as $\sum\nolimits_g \lambda_g g + \sum\nolimits_g \mu_g g = \sum\nolimits_g (\lambda_g \!+\! \mu_g) g$, whose support is also finite, and multiplication is defined by $\left(\sum\nolimits_g \lambda_g g\right)\!\!\left(\sum\nolimits_h \mu_h h\right) = \sum\nolimits_{g,h} \lambda_g\mu_h \, gh$, whose support is again finite, and which can be written in the form $\sum_{x \in G} \nu_x x$ as $\sum_{x \in G}\Bigg(\sum_{g,h \in G \atop gh = x} \lambda_g\mu_h \!\Bigg) x$.

*[Kaplansky's unit conjecture](wikipedia://en/4025490#Group_rings)* states that given a field $K$ and a [torsion-free group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion-free_group) $G$ (a group in which all non-[identity](wikipedia://en/14962) elements have infinite [order](wikipedia://en/493527)), the group ring $K[G]$ does not contain any non-trivial [units](wikipedia://en/289666) – that is, if $ab = 1$ in $K[G]$ then $a = kg$ for some $k \in K$ and $g \in G$. Giles Gardam disproved this [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) in February 2021 by providing a [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151).[^o4][^o5][^o6] He took $K = \mathbb{F}_2$, the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) with two elements, and he took $G$ to be the 6th Fibonacci group $F(2,6)$. The non-trivial unit $\alpha \in \mathbb{F}_2[F(2, 6)]$ he discovered has $|\operatorname{supp} \alpha\,| = |\operatorname{supp} \alpha^{-1}| = 21$.[^o4]

The 6th Fibonacci group $F(2,6)$ has also been variously referred to as the *Hantzsche-Wendt group*, the *Passman group*, and the *Promislow group*.[^o4][^o7]
