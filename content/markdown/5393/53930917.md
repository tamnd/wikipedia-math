# Georg Joseph Sidler

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Georg Joseph Sidler"}

**Georg Joseph Sidler** (31 August 1831 – 9 November 1907) was a Swiss [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902), professor at the [university of Bern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bern).

:::

## Life and work {#Life_and_work}

Sidler was born on 31 August 1831 in [Zug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zug), Switzerland to Georg Joseph Sidler (1782 - 1861) and Verena Maria Sidler (née Moos) (1806 - 1886). He was their only son. He also had two half-sisters from his father's first marriage. One of the girls died at the age of seven.[^o4] Both of Georg's parents came from old-established families in [Canton of Zug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canton_of_Zug); Many of his ancestors had been involved in local and cantonal politics. His paternal grandfather, Georg Damian held a number of offices; most notably he served as a Landvogt (bailiff) in the Valle Maggia in [Ticino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ticino). Sidler's father became a politician himself, first in his home, Canton of Zug, then in [Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zürich) and in the newly established federal government.[^o5] From 1810-1833, he served as delegate of Canton of Zug in the [Tagsatzung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagsatzung). Georg Joseph was a liberal politician, but as the conservatives gained power in Zug his influence dwindled, and as a result the family moved to [Unterstrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unterstrass), near Zürich, in 1839.[^o6] Georg Joseph also had a great interest in [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), having attended lectures in both subjects during his law studies. In his house, both in Zug and in Unterstrass, he had a little observatory, and Graf reports in that 'he always took Kästner's *Foundations of Mathematics* or Lacroix's *Introduction to Differential and Integral Calculus* along to meetings of the National Court'. Georg Joseph did not publish any scientific papers, but he might have played a role in sparking his son's interest in mathematics and astronomy.[^o7] Sidler attended primary school first in Zug and then in Unterstrass. He attended the [Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_%28school%29) in Zürich from 1843-1850. There, he continued to excel in mathematics and ancient languages.[^o8] After having obtained his [Matura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matura), he matriculated at the [University of Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Zurich) to study mathematics. Sidler was awarded with a doctorate at the University of Zurich in 1854. His doctoral dissertation, *Sur les inégalités du moyen mouvement d'Uranus dues à l'action perturbatrice de Neptun ("On the inequalities of the mean motion of Uranus due to the disturbing action of Neptune")*, was inspired by mathematician and astronomer, [Victor Puiseux](wikipedia://en/3868862).[^o9] The following years he completed studies at the Universities of Zurich and [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Berlin). In 1857, he was appointed Assistant Professor of [University of Bern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bern) and in 1880, he became full professor. He retired in 1898.[^o10]

His most important works were about [spherical harmonic functions](wikipedia://en/55762) and on perturbation theory. In 1861 he published his main book in Bern: *[Die Theorie der Kugelfunktionen](https://books.google.com/books?id=2BZNAAAAMAAJ&pg=PA1)*.

Although described as lank and bookish, Sidler enjoyed good health throughout his lifetime. On 9 November 1907, Sidler died from a heart attack, and was buried in Zürich three days later.[^o11]
