# Winsorized mean

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Winsorized mean"}

A **winsorized mean** is a [winsorized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winsorising) [statistical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical) [measure of central tendency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_of_central_tendency), much like the [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) and [median](wikipedia://en/18837), and even more similar to the [truncated mean](wikipedia://en/430588). It involves the calculation of the mean after [winsorizing](wikipedia://en/1767926) — replacing given parts of a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) or [sample](wikipedia://en/160361) at the high and low end with the most extreme remaining values,[^o2] typically doing so for an equal amount of both extremes; often 10 to 25 percent of the ends are replaced. The winsorized mean can equivalently be expressed as a [weighted average](wikipedia://en/189822) of the truncated mean and the quantiles at which it is limited, which corresponds to replacing parts with the corresponding quantiles.

:::

## Advantages {#Advantages}

The winsorized mean is a useful estimator because by retaining the [outliers](wikipedia://en/160951) without taking them too literally, it is less sensitive to observations at the extremes than the straightforward mean, and will still generate a reasonable estimate of central tendency or mean for almost all statistical models. In this regard it is referred to as a [robust estimator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robust_estimator).

## Drawbacks {#Drawbacks}

The winsorized mean uses more information from the distribution or sample than the [median](wikipedia://en/18837). However, unless the underlying distribution is [symmetric](wikipedia://en/39284103), the winsorized mean of a sample is unlikely to produce an [unbiased estimator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unbiased_estimator) for either the mean or the median.

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

For a sample of 10 numbers (from *x*<sub>(1)</sub>, the smallest, to *x*<sub>(10)</sub> the largest; [order statistic](wikipedia://en/160986) notation) the 10% winsorized mean is

$$\frac{\overbrace{x_{(2)} + x_{(2)}} + x_{(3)} + x_{(4)} + x_{(5)} + x_{(6)} + x_{(7)} + x_{(8)} + \overbrace{x_{(9)} + x_{(9)}}}{10}. \,$$

The key is in the repetition of *x*<sub>(2)</sub> and *x*<sub>(9)</sub>: the extras substitute for the original values *x*<sub>(1)</sub> and *x*<sub>(10)</sub> which have been discarded and replaced.

This is equivalent to a weighted average of 0.1 times the 5th percentile (*x*<sub>(2)</sub>), 0.8 times the 10% [trimmed mean](wikipedia://en/430588), and 0.1 times the 95th percentile (*x*<sub>(9)</sub>).

:::
