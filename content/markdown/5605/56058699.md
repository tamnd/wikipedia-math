# William of Soissons

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="William of Soissons"}

**William of Soissons** (Guillaume de Soissons) was a French [logician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logician) who lived in [Paris](wikipedia://en/22989) in the 12th century. He belonged to a school of logicians called the [Parvipontians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parvipontians).[^o2]

:::

## William of Soissons fundamental logical problem and solution {#William_of_Soissons_fundamental_logical_problem_and_solution}

William of Soissons[^o4] seems to have been the first one to answer the question, "Why is a contradiction not accepted in logic reasoning?" by the [principle of explosion](wikipedia://en/591394). Exposing a contradiction was already in the ancient days of [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) a way of showing that some reasoning was wrong, but there was no explicit argument as to why contradictions were incorrect. William of Soissons gave a proof in which he showed that from a contradiction any assertion can be inferred as true.[^o2] In example from: *It is raining (P) and it is not raining (¬P)* you may infer *that there are trees on the moon (or whatever else)(E)*. In symbolic language: P & ¬P → E.

If a contradiction makes anything true then it makes it impossible to say anything meaningful: whatever you say, its contradiction is also true.

## C. I. Lewis's reconstruction of his proof {#C._I._Lewis's_reconstruction_of_his_proof}

William's contemporaries compared his proof with a siege engine (12th century).[^o6] [Clarence Irving Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarence_Irving_Lewis)[^o7] formalized this proof as follows:[^o8]

::: {#o9 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof**

- V : or
- & : and
- → : inference
- P : proposition
- ¬ P : denial of P
- P &¬ P : contradiction.
- E : any possible assertion (Explosion).

(1) P &¬ P → P (If P and ¬ P are both true then P is true) (2) P → P∨E (If P is true then P or E is true) (3) P &¬ P → P∨E (If P and ¬ P are both true then P or E are true (from (2)) (4) P &¬ P → ¬P (If P and ¬ P are both true then ¬P is true) (5) P &¬ P → (P∨E) &¬P (If P and ¬ P are both true then (P∨E) is true (from (3)) and ¬P is true (from (4))) (6) (P∨E) &¬P → E (If (P∨E) is true and ¬P is true then E is true) (7) P &¬ P → E (From (5) and (6) one after the other follows (7))

:::

## Acceptance and criticism in later ages {#Acceptance_and_criticism_in_later_ages}

In the 15th century this proof was rejected by a school in [Cologne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne). They didn't accept step (6).[^o11] In 19th-century [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868), the Principle of Explosion was widely accepted as self-evident, e.g. by logicians like [George Boole](wikipedia://en/38298) and [Gottlob Frege](wikipedia://en/48416), though the formalization of the Soissons proof by Lewis provided additional grounding for the Principle of Explosion.
