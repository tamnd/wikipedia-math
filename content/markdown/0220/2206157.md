# Concurrent lines

*Image: Concurrent Lines.svg.* Lines A, B and C are concurrent in Y.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Concurrent lines"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), [lines](wikipedia://en/946975) in a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) or higher-dimensional [space](wikipedia://en/27667) are **concurrent** if they [intersect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line-line_intersection) at a single [point](wikipedia://en/593693).

:::

The set of all lines through a point is called a *[pencil](wikipedia://en/2906829)*, and their common intersection is called the *[vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417)* of the pencil.

In any [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) (including a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697)) the set of lines [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) to a given line (sharing the same [direction](wikipedia://en/54516626)) is also called a *pencil*, and the vertex of each pencil of parallel lines is a distinct [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139); including these points results in a [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) in which every pair of lines has an intersection.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### Triangles {#Triangles}

In a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654), four basic types of sets of concurrent lines are [altitudes](wikipedia://en/161241), [angle bisectors](wikipedia://en/152547#Angle_bisector), [medians](wikipedia://en/1072006), and [perpendicular bisectors](wikipedia://en/152547#Perpendicular_bisectors):

- A triangle's altitudes run from each [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) and meet the opposite side at a [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956). The point where the three altitudes meet is the [orthocenter](wikipedia://en/161244).
- Angle bisectors are rays running from each vertex of the triangle and bisecting the associated [angle](wikipedia://en/1196). They all meet at the [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837).
- Medians connect each vertex of a triangle to the midpoint of the opposite side. The three medians meet at the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926).
- Perpendicular bisectors are lines running out of the midpoints of each side of a triangle at 90 degree angles. The three perpendicular bisectors meet at the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter).

Other sets of lines associated with a triangle are concurrent as well. For example:

- Any median (which is necessarily a [bisector of the triangle's area](wikipedia://en/152547#Area_bisectors_and_perimeter_bisectors)) is concurrent with two other area bisectors each of which is parallel to a side.[^o5]
- A [cleaver](wikipedia://en/14925692) of a triangle is a line segment that [bisects the perimeter](wikipedia://en/152547#Area_bisectors_and_perimeter_bisectors) of the triangle and has one endpoint at the midpoint of one of the three sides. The three cleavers concur at the center of the [Spieker circle](wikipedia://en/9502005), which is the [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle) of the [medial triangle](wikipedia://en/3339743).
- A [splitter](wikipedia://en/14961391) of a triangle is a line segment having one endpoint at one of the three vertices of the triangle and bisecting the perimeter. The three splitters concur at the [Nagel point](wikipedia://en/9489914) of the triangle.
- Any line through a triangle that splits both the triangle's area and its perimeter in half goes through the triangle's [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837), and each triangle has one, two, or three of these lines.[^o6] Thus if there are three of them, they concur at the incenter.
- The [Tarry point](wikipedia://en/14662924) of a triangle is the point of concurrency of the lines through the vertices of the triangle perpendicular to the corresponding sides of the triangle's first [Brocard triangle](wikipedia://en/14884335).
- The [Schiffler point](wikipedia://en/2012094) of a triangle is the point of concurrence of the [Euler lines](wikipedia://en/203622) of four triangles: the triangle in question, and the three triangles that each share two vertices with it and have its [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) as the other vertex.
- The [Napoleon points](wikipedia://en/35718816) and generalizations of them are points of concurrency. For example, the first Napoleon point is the point of concurrency of the three lines each from a vertex to the centroid of the equilateral triangle drawn on the exterior of the opposite side from the vertex. A generalization of this notion is the [Jacobi point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_point).
- The [de Longchamps point](wikipedia://en/14593776) is the point of concurrence of several lines with the [Euler line](wikipedia://en/203622).
- Three lines, each formed by drawing an external equilateral triangle on one of the sides of a given triangle and connecting the new vertex to the original triangle's opposite vertex, are concurrent at a point called the [first isogonal center](wikipedia://en/12401488#1st_isogonic_center). In the case in which the original triangle has no angle greater than 120°, this point is also the [Fermat point](wikipedia://en/2666205).
- The [Apollonius point](wikipedia://en/35841146) is the point of concurrence of three lines, each of which connects a point of tangency of the circle to which the triangle's [excircles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excircle) are internally tangent, to the opposite vertex of the triangle.

### Quadrilaterals {#Quadrilaterals}

- The two [bimedians](wikipedia://en/25278#Special_line_segments) of a [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278) (segments joining midpoints of opposite sides) and the line segment joining the midpoints of the diagonals are concurrent and are all bisected by their point of intersection.[^o9]
- In a [tangential quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/9176412), the four [angle bisectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_bisector) concur at the center of the [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle).[^o8]
- Other concurrencies of a tangential quadrilateral are given [here](wikipedia://en/9176412#Concurrent_and_perpendicular_lines).
- In a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131), four line segments, each [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to one side and passing through the opposite side's [midpoint](wikipedia://en/437979), are concurrent.[^o9][^o10] These line segments are called the *maltitudes*, which is an abbreviation for midpoint altitude. Their common point is called the *anticenter*.
- A convex quadrilateral is [ex-tangential](wikipedia://en/32791638) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) there are six concurrent angles bisectors: the internal [angle bisectors](wikipedia://en/152547#Angle_bisector) at two opposite vertex angles, the external angle bisectors at the other two vertex angles, and the external angle bisectors at the angles formed where the extensions of opposite sides intersect.

### Hexagons {#Hexagons}

- If the successive sides of a [cyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_polygon) [hexagon](wikipedia://en/59733) are *a*, *b*, *c*, *d*, *e*, *f*, then the three main diagonals concur at a single point if and only if *ace* = *bdf*.[^o13]
- If a hexagon has an [inscribed](wikipedia://en/4470203) [conic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic), then by [Brianchon's theorem](wikipedia://en/1554065) its principal [diagonals](wikipedia://en/349251) are concurrent (as in the above image).
- Concurrent lines arise in the dual of [Pappus's hexagon theorem](wikipedia://en/1493395).
- For each side of a cyclic hexagon, extend the adjacent sides to their intersection, forming a triangle exterior to the given side. Then the segments connecting the circumcenters of opposite triangles are concurrent.[^o14]

### Regular polygons {#Regular_polygons}

- If a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) has an [even number](wikipedia://en/143135) of sides, the [diagonals](wikipedia://en/349251) connecting opposite vertices are concurrent at the center of the polygon.

### Circles {#Circles}

- The [perpendicular bisectors](wikipedia://en/152547#Line_segment_bisector) of all [chords](wikipedia://en/6220#Chord) of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) are concurrent at the [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_%28geometry%29) of the circle.
- The lines perpendicular to the tangents to a circle at the points of tangency are concurrent at the center.
- All [area](wikipedia://en/1209) [bisectors](wikipedia://en/152547) and [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636) bisectors of a circle are [diameters](wikipedia://en/8007), and they are concurrent at the circle's center.

### Ellipses {#Ellipses}

- All area bisectors and perimeter bisectors of an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) are concurrent at the center of the ellipse.

### Hyperbolas {#Hyperbolas}

- In a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) the following are concurrent: (1) a circle passing through the hyperbola's [foci](wikipedia://en/1032607) and centered at the hyperbola's center; (2) either of the lines that are tangent to the hyperbola at the vertices; and (3) either of the asymptotes of the hyperbola.
- The following are also concurrent: (1) the circle that is centered at the hyperbola's center and that passes through the hyperbola's vertices; (2) either directrix; and (3) either of the asymptotes.

### Tetrahedrons {#Tetrahedrons}

- In a [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606), the four medians and three bimedians are all concurrent at a point called the *centroid* of the tetrahedron.[^o20]
- An [isodynamic tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606#Irregular_tetrahedra) is one in which the [cevians](wikipedia://en/6135790) that join the vertices to the [incenters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle_and_excircles_of_a_triangle) of the opposite faces are concurrent, and an [isogonic tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606#Irregular_tetrahedra) has concurrent cevians that join the vertices to the points of contact of the opposite faces with the [inscribed sphere](wikipedia://en/991786) of the tetrahedron.
- In an [orthocentric tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/34286384) the four altitudes are concurrent.

## Algebra {#Algebra}

According to the [Rouché–Capelli theorem](wikipedia://en/28648000), a system of equations is [consistent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consistent_equations) if and only if the [rank](wikipedia://en/26561) of the [coefficient matrix](wikipedia://en/4636611) is equal to the rank of the [augmented matrix](wikipedia://en/3676187) (the coefficient matrix augmented with a column of intercept terms), and the system has a *unique* solution if and only if that common rank equals the number of variables. Thus with two variables the *k* lines in the plane, associated with a set of *k* equations, are concurrent if and only if the rank of the *k* × 2 coefficient matrix and the rank of the *k* × 3 augmented matrix are both 2. In that case only two of the *k* equations are [independent](wikipedia://en/5957182), and the point of concurrency can be found by solving any two mutually independent equations simultaneously for the two variables.

## Projective geometry {#Projective_geometry}

In [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849), in two dimensions concurrency is the [dual](wikipedia://en/539925) of [collinearity](wikipedia://en/3189581); in three dimensions, concurrency is the dual of [coplanarity](wikipedia://en/1508434).
