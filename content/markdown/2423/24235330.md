# Behavioural genetics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Behavioural genetics"}

**Behavioural genetics**, also referred to as **behaviour genetics**, is a field of [scientific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science) [research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research) that uses [genetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics) [methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method) to investigate the [nature and origins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etiology) of [individual differences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_psychology) in [behaviour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behaviour). While the name "behavioural genetics" connotes a focus on genetic influences, the field broadly investigates the extent to which genetic and environmental factors influence individual differences, and the development of [research designs](wikipedia://en/3345681) that can remove the [confounding](wikipedia://en/3105999) of genes and environment.

:::

Behavioural genetics was founded as a [scientific discipline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branches_of_science) by [Francis Galton](wikipedia://en/49072) in the late 19th century, only to be discredited through association with [eugenics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics) movements before and during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II). In the latter half of the 20th century, the field saw renewed prominence with research on [inheritance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inheritance) of behaviour and [mental illness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_illness) in humans (typically using [twin and family studies](wikipedia://en/297382)), as well as research on genetically informative [model organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_organism) through [selective breeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selective_breeding) and [crosses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diallel_cross). In the late 20th and early 21st centuries, technological advances in molecular genetics made it possible to measure and modify the [genome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genome) directly. This led to major advances in model organism research (e.g., [knockout mice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knockout_mice)) and in human studies (e.g., [genome-wide association studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genome-wide_association_studies)).

Findings from behavioural genetic research have broadly impacted modern understanding of the role of genetic and environmental influences on behaviour. These include evidence that nearly all researched behaviours are under a significant degree of genetic influence, and that influence tends to increase as individuals develop into adulthood. Further, most researched human behaviours are influenced by a [very large number of genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygenic) and the [individual effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penetrance) of these genes are very small. Environmental influences also play a strong role, but they tend to make family members more different from one another, not more similar.

## History {#History}

*Image: Maler der Grabkammer des Menna 012.jpg.* Farmers with wheat and cattle—Ancient Egyptian art 1,422 BCE displaying domesticated animals

[Selective breeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selective_breeding) and the [domestication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestication) of animals is perhaps the earliest evidence that humans considered the idea that individual differences in behaviour could be due to natural causes.[^o4] [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) and [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) each speculated on the basis and mechanisms of [inheritance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heredity) of behavioural characteristics.[^o5] Plato, for example, argued in *[The Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_%28Plato%29)* that selective breeding among the citizenry to encourage the development of some traits and discourage others, what today might be called [eugenics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics), was to be encouraged in the pursuit of an ideal society.[^o5][^o7] Behavioural genetic concepts also existed during the [English Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Renaissance), where [William Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shakespeare) perhaps first coined the phrase "[nature versus nurture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_versus_nurture)" in *[The Tempest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tempest)*, where he wrote in Act IV, Scene I, that [Caliban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliban) was "A devil, a born devil, on whose nature Nurture can never stick".[^o7][^o6]

Modern-day behavioural genetics began with [Sir Francis Galton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Francis_Galton), a nineteenth-century intellectual and cousin of [Charles Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin).[^o7] Galton was a [polymath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath) who studied many subjects, including the [heritability](wikipedia://en/155624) of human abilities and mental characteristics. One of Galton's investigations involved a large [pedigree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedigree_chart) study of social and [intellectual achievement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence) in the [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England) [upper class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_class). In 1869, 10 years after Darwin's *[On the Origin of Species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Origin_of_Species)*, Galton published his results in *Hereditary Genius*.[^o8] In this work, Galton found that the rate of "eminence" was highest among close relatives of eminent individuals, and decreased as the degree of relationship to eminent individuals decreased. While Galton could not rule out the role of environmental influences on eminence, a fact which he acknowledged, the study served to initiate an important debate about the [relative roles of genes and environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_versus_nurture) on behavioural characteristics. Through his work, Galton also "introduced [multivariate analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_analysis) and paved the way towards modern [Bayesian statistics](wikipedia://en/404412)" that are used throughout the sciences—launching what has been dubbed the "Statistical Enlightenment".[^o9]

*Image: Francis Galton2.jpg.* Galton in his later years

The field of behavioural genetics, as founded by Galton, was ultimately undermined by another of Galton's intellectual contributions, the founding of the [eugenics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics) movement in 20th century society.[^o7] The primary idea behind [eugenics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics) was to use selective breeding combined with knowledge about the inheritance of behaviour to improve the human species.[^o7] The [eugenics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics) movement was subsequently discredited by scientific [corruption and genocidal actions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_eugenics) in [Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany). Behavioural genetics was thereby discredited through its association to eugenics.[^o7] The field once again gained status as a distinct scientific discipline through the publication of early texts on behavioural genetics, such as [Calvin S. Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvin_S._Hall)'s 1951 book chapter on behavioural genetics, in which he introduced the term "psychogenetics",[^o10] which enjoyed some limited popularity in the 1960s and 1970s.[^o11][^o12] However, it eventually disappeared from usage in favour of "behaviour genetics".

The start of behaviour genetics as a well-identified field was marked by the publication in 1960 of the book *Behavior Genetics* by [John L. Fuller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_L._Fuller) and William Robert (Bob) Thompson.[^o4][^o13] It is widely accepted now that many if not most behaviours in animals and humans are under significant genetic influence, although the extent of genetic influence for any particular trait can differ widely.[^o73][^o14] A decade later, in February 1970, the first issue of the journal [*Behavior Genetics*](wikipedia://en/24891663) was published and in 1972 the [Behavior Genetics Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavior_Genetics_Association) was formed with [Theodosius Dobzhansky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodosius_Dobzhansky) elected as the association's first president. The field has since grown and diversified, touching many scientific disciplines.[^o7][^o15]

## Methods {#Methods}

The primary goal of behavioural genetics is to investigate the nature and origins of [individual differences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_psychology) in behaviour.[^o7] A wide variety of different methodological approaches are used in behavioural genetic research,[^o18] only a few of which are outlined below.

### Animal studies {#Animal_studies}

Investigators in animal behaviour genetics can carefully control for environmental factors and can experimentally manipulate genetic variants, allowing for a degree of causal inference that is not available in studies on [human behavioural genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_behaviour_genetics).[^o20] In animal research [selection experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_selection) have often been employed. For example, laboratory [house mice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_mice) have been bred for [open-field behaviour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_field_test),[^o21] [thermoregulatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermoregulation) [nesting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nesting_instinct),[^o22] and voluntary [wheel-running](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamster_wheel) behaviour.[^o23] A range of methods in these designs are covered on those pages. Behavioural geneticists using [model organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_organism) employ a range of [molecular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_biology) techniques to alter, insert, or delete genes. These techniques include [knockouts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_knockout), [floxing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floxing), [gene knockdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_knockdown), or [genome editing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genome_editing) using methods like [CRISPR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR)-Cas9.[^o24] These techniques allow behavioural geneticists different levels of control in the model organism's genome, to evaluate the molecular, [physiological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology), or behavioural outcome of genetic changes.[^o25] Animals commonly used as model organisms in behavioural genetics include mice,[^o26] [zebra fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zebra_fish),[^o27] [Drosophila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drosophila),[^o28] and the [nematode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nematode) species *[C. elegans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._elegans)*.[^o29]

Machine learning and A.I. developments are allowing researchers to design experiments that are able to manage the complexity and large data sets generated, allowing for increasingly complex behavioural experiments.[^o30]

### Human studies {#Human_studies}

Some research designs used in behavioural genetic research are variations on [family designs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genealogy) (also known as [pedigree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedigree_chart) designs), including [twin studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_study) and [adoption studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adoption_study).[^o18] [Quantitative genetic](wikipedia://en/297382) modelling of individuals with known genetic relationships (e.g., parent-child, sibling, dizygotic and monozygotic [twins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twins)) allows one to estimate to what extent genes and environment contribute to [phenotypic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenotype) differences among individuals.[^o32]

#### Twin and family studies {#Twin_and_family_studies}

*Image: Autosomal Dominant Pedigree Chart2.svg.* [Pedigree chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genogram) showing an [inheritance pattern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heredity) consistent with [autosomal dominant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autosomal_dominant) transmission. Behavioural geneticists have used [pedigree studies](wikipedia://en/297382#Pedigree_analysis) to investigate the genetic and environmental basis of behaviour.

The basic intuition of the twin study is that [monozygotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monozygotic) twins share 100% of their genome and [dizygotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dizygotic) twins share, on average, 50% of their segregating genome. Thus, differences between the two members of a monozygotic twin pair can only be due to differences in their environment, whereas dizygotic twins will differ from one another due to genes in addition to the environment. Under this simplistic model, if dizygotic twins differ more than monozygotic twins it can only be attributable to genetic influences. An important assumption of the twin model is the [equal environment assumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_study#Assumptions)[^o34] that monozygotic twins have the same shared environmental experiences as dizygotic twins. If, for example, monozygotic twins tend to have more similar experiences than dizygotic twins—and these experiences themselves are not genetically mediated through [gene-environment correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene-environment_correlation) mechanisms—then monozygotic twins will tend to be more similar to one another than dizygotic twins for reasons that have nothing to do with genes.[^o35] While this assumption should be kept in mind when interpreting the results of twin studies, research tends to support the equal environment assumption.[^o36]

Twin studies of monozygotic and dizygotic twins use a biometrical formulation to describe the influences on twin similarity and to infer heritability.[^o32][^o37] The formulation rests on the basic observation that the [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) in a phenotype is due to two sources, genes and environment. More formally, $Var(P) = g + (g \times \epsilon) + \epsilon$, where $P$ is the phenotype, $g$ is the effect of genes, $\epsilon$ is the effect of the environment, and $(g \times \epsilon)$ is a [gene by environment interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene-environment_interaction). The $g$ term can be expanded to include [additive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additive_genetic_effects) ($a^2$), [dominance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominance_%28genetics%29) ($d^2$), and [epistatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistasis) ($i^2$) genetic effects. Similarly, the environmental term $\epsilon$ can be expanded to include shared environment ($c^2$) and non-shared environment ($e^2$), which includes any [measurement error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurement_error). Dropping the gene by environment interaction for simplicity (typical in twin studies) and fully decomposing the $g$ and $\epsilon$ terms, we now have $Var(P) = (a^2 + d^2 + i^2) + (c^2 + e^2)$. Twin research then models the similarity in monozygotic twins and dizygotic twins using simplified forms of this decomposition, shown in the table.[^o32]

*Decomposing the genetic and environmental contributions to twin similarity.[^o32]*

::: {#o38 .table cue=table name="Decomposing the genetic and environmental contributions to twin similarity."}

| Type of relationship | Full decomposition | Falconer's decomposition |
|---|---|---|
| Perfect similarity between siblings | $1.0 = a^2 + d^2 + i^2 + c^2 + e^2$ | $1.0 = a^2 + c^2 + e^2$ |
| Monozygotic twin correlation($r_{MZ}$) | $r_{MZ} = a^2 + d^2 + i^2 + c^2$ | $r_{MZ} = a^2 + c^2$ |
| Dizygotic twin correlation ($r_{DZ}$) | $r_{DZ} = \frac{1}{2}a^2 + \frac{1}{4}d^2 + (k)i^2 + c^2$ | $r_{DZ} = \frac{1}{2}a^2 + c^2$ |
| Where $k$ is an unknown (probably very small) quantity. |  |  |

:::

The simplified Falconer formulation can then be used to derive estimates of $a^2$, $c^2$, and $e^2$. Rearranging and substituting the $r_{MZ}$ and $r_{DZ}$ equations one can obtain an estimate of the additive genetic variance, or [heritability](wikipedia://en/155624), $a^2 = 2(r_{MZ} - r_{DZ})$, the non-shared environmental effect $e^2 = 1- r_{MZ}$ and, finally, the shared environmental effect $c^2 = 2r_{DZ} - r_{MZ}$.[^o32] The Falconer formulation is presented here to illustrate how the twin model works. Modern approaches use [maximum likelihood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_likelihood) to estimate the genetic and environmental [variance components](wikipedia://en/6709643).[^o39]

#### Measured genetic variants {#Measured_genetic_variants}

The [Human Genome Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Genome_Project) has allowed scientists to directly [genotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genotyping) the [sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleic_acid_sequence) of human [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA) [nucleotides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleotide).[^o41] Once genotyped, [genetic variants](wikipedia://en/4816754) can be tested for [association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_association) with a behavioural [phenotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenotype), such as [mental disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_disorder), [cognitive ability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence), [personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality), and so on.[^o42]

- **Candidate Genes.** One popular approach has been to test for association [candidate genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candidate_gene) with behavioural phenotypes, where the candidate gene is selected based on some [a priori](wikipedia://en/6679056) theory about biological mechanisms involved in the manifestation of a behavioural trait or phenotype.[^o99] In general, such studies have proven difficult to broadly [replicate](wikipedia://en/47651)[^o43][^o44][^o45][^o46] and there has been concern raised that the [false positive rate](wikipedia://en/23067942) in this type of research is high.[^o99][^o47]
- **Genome-wide association studies** In [genome-wide association studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genome-wide_association_studies), researchers test the relationship of millions of [genetic polymorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymorphism_%28biology%29#Genetics) with behavioural phenotypes across the [genome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genome).[^o42] This approach to genetic association studies is largely [atheoretical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_theory), and typically not guided by a particular biological hypothesis regarding the phenotype.[^o42] Genetic association findings for behavioural traits and [psychiatric disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_illness) have been found to be highly [polygenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygenic) (involving many small genetic effects).[^o48][^o49][^o50][^o51][^o100] *Image: Genetic correlation results about traits, IQ and language skills.jpg.* Study results about which and to what degree various traits, IQ and language-related [skills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skill) appear to be [influenced by genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_correlation)[^o52][^o53] Genetic variants identified to be associated with some trait or disease through GWAS may be used to improve disease risk predictions. However, the genetic variants identified through GWAS of common genetic variants are most likely to have a modest effect on disease risk or development of a given trait. This is different from the strong genetic contribution seen in [Mendelian conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_disorder#Single_gene_disorder) or for some rare variants that may have a larger effect on disease.
- **SNP heritability and co-heritability** Recently, researchers have begun to use similarity between classically unrelated people at their measured [single nucleotide polymorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_nucleotide_polymorphisms) (SNPs) to estimate [genetic variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_variation) or covariation that is tagged by SNPs, using [mixed effects models](wikipedia://en/5431921) implemented in [software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software) such as [genome-wide complex trait analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genome-wide_complex_trait_analysis) (GCTA).[^o54][^o70] To do this, researchers find the average genetic relatedness over all SNPs between all individuals in a (typically large) sample, and use [Haseman–Elston regression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haseman–Elston_regression) or [restricted maximum likelihood](wikipedia://en/15211981) to estimate the genetic variation that is "tagged" by, or predicted by, the SNPs. The proportion of [phenotypic variation](wikipedia://en/32344) that is accounted for by the genetic relatedness has been called "SNP heritability".[^o55] Intuitively, SNP heritability increases to the degree that phenotypic similarity is predicted by genetic similarity at measured SNPs, and is expected to be lower than the true [narrow-sense heritability](wikipedia://en/155624) to the degree that measured SNPs fail to tag (typically rare) causal variants.[^o56] The value of this method is that it is an independent way to estimate heritability that does not require the same assumptions as those in twin and family studies, and that it gives insight into the [allelic frequency spectrum](wikipedia://en/47912905) of the causal variants underlying trait variation.[^o57]

### Quasi-experimental designs {#Quasi-experimental_designs}

Some behavioural genetic designs are useful not to understand genetic influences on behaviour, but to [control](wikipedia://en/1640288) for genetic influences to test environmentally-mediated influences on behaviour.[^o59] Such behavioural genetic designs may be considered a subset of [natural experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_experiments),[^o60] [quasi-experiments](wikipedia://en/11864322) that attempt to take advantage of naturally occurring situations that mimic true [experiments](wikipedia://en/59861) by providing some [control](wikipedia://en/1640288) over an [independent variable](wikipedia://en/437701). Natural experiments can be particularly useful when [experiments](wikipedia://en/59861) are infeasible, due to practical or [ethical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethical) limitations.[^o60]

A general limitation of [observational studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_studies) is that the relative influences of genes and environment are [confounded](wikipedia://en/3105999). A simple demonstration of this fact is that measures of 'environmental' influence are heritable.[^o61] Thus, observing a [correlation](wikipedia://en/157057) between an environmental risk factor and a health outcome is not necessarily evidence for environmental influence on the health outcome. Similarly, in [observational studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_studies) of parent-child behavioural transmission, for example, it is impossible to know if the transmission is due to genetic or environmental influences, due to the problem of passive [gene–environment correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene–environment_correlation).[^o60] The simple observation that the children of parents who use [drugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug) are more likely to use drugs as adults does not indicate *why* the children are more likely to use drugs when they grow up. It could be because the children are [modelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Role_model) their parents' behaviour. Equally plausible, it could be that the children inherited drug-use-predisposing genes from their parent, which put them at increased risk for drug use as adults regardless of their parents' behaviour. Adoption studies, which parse the relative effects of rearing environment and genetic inheritance, find a small to negligible effect of rearing environment on [smoking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoking), [alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcoholic_drink), and [marijuana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marijuana) use in adopted children,[^o62] but a larger effect of rearing environment on [harder drug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_harmfulness) use.[^o63]

Other behavioural genetic designs include discordant twin studies,[^o59] children of twins designs,[^o64] and [Mendelian randomization](wikipedia://en/10453294).[^o65]

## General findings {#General_findings}

There are many broad conclusions to be drawn from behavioural genetic research about the nature and origins of behaviour.[^o7][^o67] Three major conclusions include:[^o7]

1. all behavioural traits and disorders are influenced by genes
1. environmental influences tend to make members of the same family more different, rather than more similar
1. the influence of genes tends to increase in relative importance as individuals age.

### Genetic influences on behaviour are pervasive {#Genetic_influences_on_behaviour_are_pervasive}

It is clear from multiple lines of evidence that all researched behavioural traits and [disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_illness) are influenced by [genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene); that is, they are [heritable](wikipedia://en/155624). The single largest source of evidence comes from [twin studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_study), where it is routinely observed that monozygotic (identical) [twins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin) are more similar to one another than are same-sex dizygotic (fraternal) twins.[^o73][^o14]

The conclusion that genetic influences are pervasive has also been observed in research designs that do not depend on the assumptions of the twin method. [Adoption studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adoption_study) show that [adoptees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adoption) are routinely more similar to their biological relatives than their adoptive relatives for a wide variety of traits and disorders.[^o7] In the [Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnesota_Twin_Family_Study#Twins_reared_apart), monozygotic twins separated shortly after birth were reunited in adulthood.[^o69] These adopted, reared-apart twins were as similar to one another as were twins reared together on a wide range of measures including general [cognitive ability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_ability), [personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality), [religious attitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religiosity), and vocational interests, among others.[^o69] Approaches using genome-wide [genotyping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genotyping) have allowed researchers to measure genetic relatedness between individuals and estimate heritability based on millions of genetic variants. Methods exist to test whether the extent of genetic similarity (aka, relatedness) between nominally unrelated individuals (individuals who are not close or even distant relatives) is associated with phenotypic similarity.[^o70] Such methods do not rely on the same assumptions as twin or adoption studies, and routinely find evidence for heritability of behavioural traits and disorders.[^o50][^o100][^o71]

### Nature of environmental influence {#Nature_of_environmental_influence}

Just as all researched human behavioural phenotypes are influenced by [genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene) (i.e., are [heritable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heritable)), all such phenotypes are also influenced by the environment.[^o73][^o67] The basic fact that monozygotic [twins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin) are genetically identical but are never perfectly concordant for [psychiatric disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_disorder) or perfectly [correlated](wikipedia://en/157057) for behavioural [traits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenotypic_trait), indicates that the environment shapes human behaviour.[^o67]

The nature of this environmental influence, however, is such that it tends to make individuals in the same family more different from one another, not more similar to one another.[^o7] That is, estimates of shared environmental effects ($c^2$) in human studies are small, negligible, or zero for the vast majority of behavioural traits and psychiatric disorders, whereas estimates of non-shared environmental effects ($e^2$) are moderate to large.[^o73] From twin studies $c^2$ is typically estimated at 0 because the correlation ($r_{MZ}$) between monozygotic twins is at least twice the correlation ($r_{DZ}$) for dizygotic twins. When using the [Falconer variance decomposition](wikipedia://en/7862454) ($1.0 = a^2 + c^2 + e^2$) this difference between monozygotic and dizygotic twin similarity results in an estimated $c^2=0$. The Falconer decomposition is simplistic.[^o32] It removes the possible influence of dominance and epistatic effects which, if present, will tend to make monozygotic twins more similar than dizygotic twins and mask the influence of shared environmental effects.[^o32] This is a limitation of the twin design for estimating $c^2$. However, the general conclusion that shared environmental effects are negligible does not rest on twin studies alone. [Adoption research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adoption_study) also fails to find large ($c^2$) components; that is, adoptive parents and their adopted children tend to show much less resemblance to one another than the adopted child and his or her non-rearing biological parent.[^o7] In studies of adoptive families with at least one biological child and one adopted child, the sibling resemblance also tends to be nearly zero for most traits that have been studied.[^o73][^o74]

*Image: Behavioral Genetics Twin Adoption Personality Similarity.pdf.* Similarity in twins and adoptees indicates a small role for shared environment in [personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality).

The figure provides an example from [personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality) research, where twin and adoption studies converge on the conclusion of zero to small influences of shared environment on broad personality traits measured by the [Multidimensional Personality Questionnaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multidimensional_Personality_Questionnaire) including positive emotionality, negative emotionality, and constraint.[^o75]

Given the conclusion that all researched behavioural traits and psychiatric disorders are heritable, biological siblings will always tend to be more similar to one another than will adopted siblings. However, for some traits, especially when measured during adolescence, adopted siblings do show some significant similarity (e.g., correlations of .20) to one another. Traits that have been demonstrated to have significant shared environmental influences include internalizing and [externalizing psychopathology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Externalizing_disorders),[^o76] substance use[^o77] and [dependence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substance_dependence),[^o63] and [intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence).[^o77]

### Nature of genetic influence {#Nature_of_genetic_influence}

Genetic effects on human behavioural outcomes can be described in multiple ways.[^o32] One way to describe the effect is in terms of how much [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) in the behaviour can be accounted for by [alleles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allele) in the [genetic variant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-nucleotide_polymorphism), otherwise known as the [coefficient of determination](wikipedia://en/1500869) or $R^2$. An intuitive way to think about $R^2$ is that it describes the extent to which the genetic variant makes individuals, who harbour different alleles, different from one another on the behavioural [outcome](wikipedia://en/39778851). A complementary way to describe effects of individual genetic variants is in how much change one expects on the behavioural outcome given a change in the number of risk alleles an individual harbours, often denoted by the Greek letter $\beta$ (denoting the slope in a [regression](wikipedia://en/826997) equation), or, in the case of binary disease outcomes by the [odds ratio](wikipedia://en/406880) $OR$ of disease given allele status. Note the difference: $R^2$ describes the population-level effect of alleles within a genetic variant; $\beta$ or $OR$ describe the effect of having a risk allele on the individual who harbours it, relative to an individual who does not harbour a risk allele.[^o79]

When described on the $R^2$ metric, the effects of individual genetic variants on *complex* human behavioural traits and disorders are vanishingly small, with each variant accounting for $R^2<0.3\%$ of variation in the phenotype.[^o7] This fact has been discovered primarily through [genome-wide association studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genome-wide_association_studies) of complex behavioural phenotypes, including results on substance use,[^o104][^o103] [personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality),[^o102] [fertility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertility),[^o80] [schizophrenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schizophrenia),[^o49] [depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depression_%28mood%29),[^o102][^o81] and [endophenotypes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endophenotype) including brain structure[^o101] and function.[^o82] There are a small handful of [replicated and robustly](wikipedia://en/47651) studied exceptions to this rule, including the effect of *[APOE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apolipoprotein_E)* on [Alzheimer's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alzheimer's_disease),[^o83] and *[CHRNA5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CHRNA5)* on [smoking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoking) behaviour,[^o104] and *[ALDH2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ALDH2)* (in individuals of [East Asian ancestry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asian_people)) on [alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_%28drug%29) use.[^o84]

On the other hand, when assessing effects according to the $\beta$ metric, there are a large number of genetic variants that have very large effects on complex behavioural phenotypes. The risk alleles within such variants are exceedingly rare, such that their large behavioural effects impact only a small number of individuals. Thus, when assessed at a population level using the $R^2$ metric, they account for only a small amount of the differences in risk between individuals in the population. Examples include variants within *[APP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amyloid_precursor_protein)* that result in familial forms of severe early onset Alzheimer's disease but affect only relatively few individuals. Compare this to risk alleles within *APOE*, which pose much smaller risk compared to *APP*, but are far more common and therefore affect a much greater proportion of the population.[^o85]

Finally, there are classical behavioural disorders that are genetically simple in their etiology, such as [Huntington's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huntington's) disease. Huntington's is caused by a single [autosomal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autosomal) [dominant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominance_%28genetics%29) variant in the *[HTT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huntingtin)* gene, which is the only variant that accounts for any differences among individuals in their risk for developing the disease, assuming they live long enough.[^o86] In the case of genetically simple and rare diseases such as Huntington's, the variant $R^2$ and the $OR$ are simultaneously large.[^o79]

### Additional general findings {#Additional_general_findings}

In response to general concerns about the [replicability of psychological research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replication_crisis#In_psychology), behavioural geneticists [Robert Plomin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Plomin), [John C. DeFries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_C._DeFries), [Valerie Knopik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valerie_Knopik), and [Jenae Neiderhiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jenae_Neiderhiser) published a [review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature_review) of the ten most well-replicated findings from behavioural genetics research.[^o67] The ten findings were:

1. "All psychological traits show significant and substantial genetic influence."
1. "No behavioural traits are 100% heritable."
1. "Heritability is caused by many genes of small effect."
1. "Phenotypic correlations between psychological traits show significant and substantial genetic mediation."
1. "The heritability of intelligence increases throughout development."
1. "Age-to-age stability is mainly due to genetics."
1. "Most measures of the 'environment' show significant genetic influence."
1. "Most associations between environmental measures and psychological traits are significantly mediated genetically."
1. "Most environmental effects are not shared by children growing up in the same family."
1. "Abnormal is normal."

## Criticisms and controversies {#Criticisms_and_controversies}

Behavioural genetic research and findings have at times been controversial. Some of this controversy has arisen because behavioural genetic findings can challenge [societal beliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_view) about the nature of human behaviour and abilities. Major areas of controversy have included genetic research on topics such as racial differences, [intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence), [violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violence), and [human sexuality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_sexuality).[^o89] Other controversies have arisen due to misunderstandings of behavioural genetic research, whether by the [lay public](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laity) or the researchers themselves.[^o7] For example, the notion of heritability is easily misunderstood to imply causality, or that some behaviour or condition is determined by one's genetic endowment.[^o94] When behavioural genetics researchers say that a behaviour is X% heritable, that does not mean that genetics causes, determines, or fixes up to X% of the behaviour. Instead, heritability is a statement about genetic differences correlated with trait differences on the population level.

Historically, perhaps the most controversial subject has been on [race and genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Race_and_genetics).[^o89] [Race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Race_%28human_categorization%29) is not a scientifically exact term, and its interpretation can depend on one's culture and country of origin.[^o90] Instead, geneticists use concepts such as [ancestry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_genealogy), which is more rigorously defined.[^o91] For example, a so-called "Black" race may include all individuals of relatively recent [African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa) descent ("recent" because all humans are [descended from African ancestors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_hominin_expansions_out_of_Africa)). However, there is more genetic diversity in Africa than the rest of the world combined,[^o92] so speaking of a "Black" race is without a precise genetic meaning.[^o91]

[Qualitative research](wikipedia://en/371299) has fostered arguments that behavioural genetics is an ungovernable field without scientific [norms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_norm) or [consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_consensus), which fosters [controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_controversy). The argument continues that this state of affairs has led to controversies including race, intelligence, instances where variation within a single gene was found to very strongly influence a controversial phenotype (e.g., the "[gay gene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology_and_sexual_orientation#Chromosome_linkage_studies)" controversy), and others. This argument further states that because of the persistence of controversy in behaviour genetics and the failure of disputes to be resolved, behaviour genetics does not conform to the standards of good science.[^o93]

The scientific assumptions on which parts of behavioural genetic research are based have also been criticized as flawed.[^o94] Genome wide association studies are often implemented with simplifying statistical assumptions, such as [additivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additive_genetic_effects), which may be statistically robust but unrealistic for some behaviours. Critics further contend that, in humans, behaviour genetics represents a misguided form of [genetic reductionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_reductionism) based on inaccurate interpretations of statistical analyses.[^o95] Studies comparing monozygotic (MZ) and dizygotic (DZ) twins assume that [environmental influences will be the same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_study#Assumptions) in both types of twins, but this assumption may also be unrealistic. MZ twins may be treated more alike than DZ twins,[^o94] which itself may be an example of evocative [gene–environment correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene–environment_correlation), suggesting that one's genes influence their treatment by others. It is also not possible in twin studies to eliminate effects of the shared womb environment, although studies comparing twins who experience [monochorionic and dichorionic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chorion) environments in utero do exist, and indicate limited impact.[^o96] Studies of twins separated in early life include children who were separated not at birth but part way through childhood.[^o94] The effect of early rearing environment can therefore be evaluated to some extent in such a study, by comparing twin similarity for those twins separated early and those separated later.[^o69]
