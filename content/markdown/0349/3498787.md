::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Circulant graph"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **circulant graph** is an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) acted on by a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of [symmetries](wikipedia://en/15094186) which [takes any vertex to any other vertex](wikipedia://en/581175). It is sometimes called a **[cyclic graph](wikipedia://en/3498846)**,[^o10] but this term has other meanings.

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Equivalent definitions"}

## Equivalent definitions {#Equivalent_definitions}

Circulant graphs can be described in several equivalent ways:[^o6]

- The [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of the graph includes a [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327) [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) that [acts transitively](wikipedia://en/12781) on the graph's vertices. In other words, the graph has an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) which is a [cyclic permutation](wikipedia://en/449166) of its vertices.
- The graph has an [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) that is a [circulant matrix](wikipedia://en/889936).
- The $n$ vertices of the graph can be numbered from 0 to $n - 1$ in such a way that, if some two vertices numbered $x$ and $(x + d) \bmod n$ are adjacent, then every two vertices numbered $z$ and $(z + d) \bmod n$ are adjacent.
- The graph can be drawn (possibly with crossings) so that its vertices lie on the corners of a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306), and every rotational symmetry of the [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) is also a symmetry of the drawing.
- The graph is a [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327).[^o7]

:::

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Every [cycle graph](wikipedia://en/878962) is a circulant graph, as is every [crown graph](wikipedia://en/21062705) with number of vertices congruent to 2 modulo 4.

The [Paley graphs](wikipedia://en/5719307) of order $n$ (where $n$ is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) congruent to 1 modulo 4) is a graph in which the vertices are the numbers from 0 to $n - 1$ and two vertices are adjacent if their difference is a [quadratic residue](wikipedia://en/200091) modulo $n$. Since the presence or absence of an edge depends only on the difference modulo $n$ of two vertex numbers, any Paley graph is a circulant graph.

Every [Möbius ladder](wikipedia://en/7355278) is a circulant graph, as is every [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816). A [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) is a circulant graph if it has the same number of vertices on both sides of its bipartition.

If two numbers $m$ and $n$ are [relatively prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_prime), then the $m \times n$ [rook's graph](wikipedia://en/7324959) (a graph that has a vertex for each square of an $m \times n$ [chessboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chessboard) and an edge for each two squares that a [rook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rook_%28chess%29) can move between in a single move) is a circulant graph. This is because its symmetries include as a subgroup the cyclic group *C<sub>mn</sub>* $\simeq$ *C<sub>m</sub>*×*C<sub>n</sub>*. More generally, in this case, the [tensor product of graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_product_of_graphs) between any $m$- and $n$-vertex circulants is itself a circulant.[^o6]

Many of the known [lower bounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_bound) on [Ramsey numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsey_number) come from examples of circulant graphs that have small [maximum cliques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_clique) and small [maximum independent sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_independent_set).[^o10]

:::

## A specific example {#A_specific_example}

The circulant graph $C_n^{s_1,\ldots,s_k}$ with jumps $s_1, \ldots, s_k$ is defined as the graph with $n$ nodes labeled $0, 1, \ldots, n-1$ where each node *i* is adjacent to 2*k* nodes $i \pm s_1, \ldots, i \pm s_k \mod n$.

- The graph $C_n^{s_1, \ldots, s_k}$ is [connected](wikipedia://en/1614492) if and only if $\gcd(n, s_1, \ldots, s_k) = 1$.
- If $1 \leq s_1 < \cdots < s_k$ are fixed [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) then the number of [spanning trees](wikipedia://en/455770) $t(C_n^{s_1,\ldots,s_k}) = na_n^2$ where $a_n$ satisfies a [recurrence relation](wikipedia://en/146806) of order $2^{s_k-1}$.
  - In particular, $t(C_n^{1,2}) = nF_n^2$ where $F_n$ is the *n*-th [Fibonacci number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number).

## Self-complementary circulants {#Self-complementary_circulants}

A [self-complementary graph](wikipedia://en/15093884) is a graph in which replacing every edge by a non-edge and vice versa produces an [isomorphic graph](wikipedia://en/247577). For instance, a five-vertex cycle graph is self-complementary, and is also a circulant graph. More generally every [Paley graph](wikipedia://en/5719307) of prime order is a self-complementary circulant graph.[^o13] [Horst Sachs](wikipedia://en/15095266) showed that, if a number $n$ has the property that every prime factor of $n$ is congruent to 1 modulo 4, then there exists a self-complementary circulant with $n$ vertices. He [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) that this condition is also necessary: that no other values of $n$ allow a self-complementary circulant to exist.[^o6][^o13] The conjecture was [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) some 40 years later, by Vilfred.[^o6]

## Ádám's conjecture {#Ádám's_conjecture}

Define a *circulant numbering* of a circulant graph to be a labeling of the vertices of the graph by the numbers from 0 to $n - 1$ in such a way that, if some two vertices numbered $x$ and $y$ are adjacent, then every two vertices numbered $z$ and $(z - x + y) \bmod n$ are adjacent. Equivalently, a circulant numbering is a numbering of the vertices for which the adjacency matrix of the graph is a circulant matrix.

Let $a$ be an integer that is relatively prime to $n$, and let $b$ be any integer. Then the [linear function](wikipedia://en/152111) that takes a number $x$ to $ax + b$ transforms a circulant numbering to another circulant numbering. András Ádám conjectured that these linear maps are the only ways of renumbering a circulant graph while preserving the circulant property: that is, if $G$ and $H$ are isomorphic circulant graphs, with different numberings, then there is a linear map that transforms the numbering for $G$ into the numbering for $H$. However, Ádám's conjecture is now known to be false. A [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) is given by graphs $G$ and $H$ with 16 vertices each; a vertex $x$ in $G$ is connected to the six neighbors $x \pm 1$, $x \pm 2$, and $x \pm 7$ modulo 16, while in $H$ the six neighbors are $x \pm 2$, $x \pm 3$, and $x \pm 5$ modulo 16. These two graphs are isomorphic, but their isomorphism cannot be realized by a linear map.[^o6]

[Toida's conjecture](wikipedia://en/29249172) refines Ádám's conjecture by considering only a special class of circulant graphs, in which all of the differences between adjacent graph vertices are relatively prime to the number of vertices. According to this refined conjecture, these special circulant graphs should have the property that all of their symmetries come from symmetries of the underlying additive group of numbers modulo $n$. It was proven by two groups in 2001 and 2002.[^o16][^o17]

:::

## Algorithmic questions {#Algorithmic_questions}

There is a [polynomial-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial-time) recognition algorithm for circulant graphs, and the isomorphism problem for circulant graphs can be solved in polynomial time.[^o19][^o20]
