# Cauchy condensation test

Series

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cauchy condensation test"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Cauchy condensation test**, named after [Augustin-Louis Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842), is a standard [convergence test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergence_test) for [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series). For a [non-increasing](wikipedia://en/27838#Increasing_and_decreasing) [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $f(n)$ of non-negative [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), the series $\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty} f(n)$ [converges](wikipedia://en/1743431) if and only if the "condensed" series $\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty} 2^{n} f(2^{n})$ converges. Moreover, if they converge, the sum of the condensed series is no more than twice as large as the sum of the original.

:::

## Estimate {#Estimate}

The Cauchy condensation test follows from the stronger estimate,

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f(n) \leq \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} 2^n f(2^n) \leq\ 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f(n),$$

which should be understood as an [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489) of [extended real numbers](wikipedia://en/51698). The essential thrust of a [proof](wikipedia://en/82285) follows, patterned after [Oresme's](wikipedia://en/593659) proof of the [divergence](wikipedia://en/876428) of the [harmonic series](wikipedia://en/142488).

To see the first inequality, the terms of the original series are rebracketed into runs whose lengths are [powers of two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powers_of_two), and then each run is bounded above by replacing each term by the largest term in that run. That term is always the first one, since by assumption the terms are non-increasing.

$$\begin{array}{rcccccccl}\displaystyle \sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty} f(n) & = &f(1) & + & f(2) + f(3) & + & f(4) + f(5) + f(6) + f(7) & + & \cdots \\ & = &f(1) & + & \Big(f(2) + f(3)\Big) & + & \Big(f(4) + f(5) + f(6) + f(7)\Big) & + &\cdots \\ & \leq &f(1) & + & \Big(f(2) + f(2)\Big) & + & \Big(f(4) + f(4) + f(4) + f(4)\Big) & + &\cdots \\ & = &f(1) & + & 2 f(2) & + & 4 f(4)& + &\cdots = \sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty} 2^{n} f(2^{n}) \end{array}$$

To see the second inequality, these two series are again rebracketed into runs of power of two length, but "offset" as shown below, so that the run of $2 \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f(n)$ which *begins* with $f(2^{n})$ lines up with the end of the run of $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} 2^{n} f(2^{n})$ which *ends* with $f(2^{n})$, so that the former stays always "ahead" of the latter.

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} 2^{n}f(2^{n}) & = f(1) + \Big(f(2) + f(2)\Big) + \Big(f(4) + f(4) + f(4) +f(4)\Big) + \cdots \\ & = \Big(f(1) + f(2)\Big) + \Big(f(2) + f(4) + f(4) + f(4)\Big) + \cdots \\ & \leq \Big(f(1) + f(1)\Big) + \Big(f(2) + f(2)\Big) + \Big( f(3) + f(3)\Big) + \cdots = 2 \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f(n) \end{aligned}$$

## Analogy with Integral Comparison {#Analogy_with_Integral_Comparison}

The condensation transformation $f(n) \rightarrow 2^{n} f(2^{n})$ recalls the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) variable substitution $x \rightarrow e^{x}$ yielding $f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \rightarrow e^{x} f(e^{x})\,\mathrm{d}x$.

The [integral test for convergence](wikipedia://en/454016) gives us, in the case of [monotone](wikipedia://en/48260) $f$, that $\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}f(n)$ converges if and only if $\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$ converges. The [substitution](wikipedia://en/193748) $x = 2^u$ yields the integral $\displaystyle \log 2\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\!2^{u}f(2^{u})\,\mathrm{d}u$. If we also have that the function $u\mapsto 2^u f(2^u)$ is monotone, the integral test applies and gives the convergence of the condensed series$\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty} 2^{n}f(2^{n})$.

## Examples {#Examples}

The test can be useful for series where $n$ appears as in a denominator in $f$. For the most basic example of this sort, the harmonic series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1/n$ is transformed into the series $\sum 1$, which clearly diverges.

For a more complex example, take

$$f(n) := n^{-a} (\log n)^{-b} (\log \log n)^{-c}.$$

Here the series definitely converges for $a > 1$, and diverges for $a < 1$. When $a = 1$, the condensation transformation gives the [Bertrand series](wikipedia://en/83783425)

$$\sum n^{-b} (\log n)^{-c}.$$

The [logarithms](wikipedia://en/17860) "shift to the left". So when $a = 1$, we have convergence for $b > 1$, divergence for $b < 1$. When $b = 1$ the value of $c$ enters.

This result readily generalizes: the condensation test, applied repeatedly, can be used to show that for $k = 1,2,3,\ldots$, the generalized [Bertrand series](wikipedia://en/83783425)

$$\sum_{n\geq N} \frac{1}{n \cdot \log n \cdot \log\log n \cdots \log^{\circ (k-1)} n \cdot(\log^{\circ k} n)^\alpha} \quad\quad (N=\lfloor \exp^{\circ k} (0) \rfloor+1)$$

converges for $\alpha > 1$ and diverges for $0 < \alpha \leq 1$.[^o6] Here $f^{\circ m}$ denotes the $m$th [iterate](wikipedia://en/1448702) of a function $f$, so that

$$f^{\circ m} (x) := \begin{cases} f(f^{\circ(m-1)}(x)), & m=1, 2, 3,\ldots; \\ x, & m = 0. \end{cases}$$

The lower limit of the sum, $N$, was chosen so that all terms of the series are positive. Notably, these series provide examples of infinite sums that converge or diverge arbitrarily slowly. For instance, in the case of $k = 2$ and $\alpha = 1$, the partial sum exceeds 10 only after $10^{10^{100}}$(a [googolplex](wikipedia://en/12365)) terms; yet the series diverges nevertheless.

:::

## Schlömilch's generalization {#Schlömilch's_generalization}

A generalization of the condensation test was given by [Oskar Schlömilch](wikipedia://en/3615367).[^o8] Let $u(n)$ be a strictly increasing sequence of positive [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) such that the ratio of successive [differences](wikipedia://en/39147) is bounded: there is a positive real number $N$, for which

$${\Delta u(n) \over \Delta u(n{-}1)} \ =\ {u(n{+}1)-u(n) \over u(n)-u(n{-}1)} \ <\ N \ \text{ for all } n.$$

Then, provided that $f(n)$ meets the same preconditions as in [Cauchy's convergence test](wikipedia://en/3738733), the convergence of the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f(n)$ is equivalent to the convergence of

$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} {\Delta u(n)}\, f(u(n)) \ =\ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \Big(u(n{+}1)-u(n)\Big) f(u(n)).$$

Taking $u(n) = 2^n$ so that $\Delta u(n) = u(n{+}1)-u(n) = 2^n$, the Cauchy condensation test emerges as a special case.
