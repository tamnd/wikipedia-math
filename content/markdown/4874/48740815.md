# Non-surveyable proof

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Non-surveyable proof"}

In the [philosophy of mathematics](wikipedia://en/46439), a **non-surveyable proof** is a [mathematical proof](wikipedia://en/82285) that is considered infeasible for a human mathematician to [verify](wikipedia://en/21304742#Arguments) and so of controversial [validity](wikipedia://en/21304742). The term was coined by [Thomas Tymoczko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Tymoczko) in 1979 in criticism of [Kenneth Appel](wikipedia://en/311544) and [Wolfgang Haken](wikipedia://en/311546)'s [computer-assisted proof](wikipedia://en/2840305) of the [four color theorem](wikipedia://en/10949), and has since been applied to other arguments, mainly those with excessive [case splitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_splitting) and/or with portions dispatched by a difficult-to-verify computer program. Surveyability remains an important consideration in [computational mathematics](wikipedia://en/10433833).

:::

## Tymoczko's argument {#Tymoczko's_argument}

Tymoczko argued that three criteria determine whether an argument is a mathematical proof:

- *Convincingness*, which refers to the proof's ability to persuade a rational prover of its conclusion;
- *Surveyability*, which refers to the proof's accessibility for verification by members of the human mathematical community; and
- *Formalizability*, which refers to the proof's appealing to only logical relationships between concepts to substantiate its argument.[^o3]

In Tymoczko's view, the Appel–Haken proof failed the surveyability criterion by, he argued, substituting [experiment](wikipedia://en/59861) for [deduction](wikipedia://en/61093):

Without surveyability, a proof may serve its first purpose of convincing a reader of its result and yet fail at its second purpose of enlightening the reader as to why that result is true—it may play the role of an observation rather than of an argument.[^o4][^o5]

This distinction is important because it means that non-surveyable proofs expose mathematics to a much higher potential for error. Especially in the case where non-surveyability is due to the use of a computer program (which may have [bugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_bug)), most especially when that program is not published, convincingness may suffer as a result.[^o5] As Tymoczko wrote:

## Counterarguments to Tymoczko's claims of non-surveyability {#Counterarguments_to_Tymoczko's_claims_of_non-surveyability}

Tymoczko's view is contested, however, by arguments that difficult-to-survey proofs are not necessarily as invalid as impossible-to-survey proofs.

[Paul Teller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Teller_%28mathematician%29) claimed that surveyability was a matter of degree and reader-dependent, not something a proof does or does not have. As proofs are not rejected when students have trouble understanding them, Teller argues, neither should proofs be rejected (though they may be criticized) simply because professional mathematicians find the argument hard to follow.[^o7][^o5] (Teller disagreed with Tymoczko's assessment that "\[The Four-Color Theorem\] has not been checked by mathematicians, step by step, as all other proofs have been checked. Indeed, it cannot be checked that way.")

An argument along similar lines is that [case splitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_splitting) is an accepted proof method, and the Appel–Haken proof is only an extreme example of case splitting.[^o4]

## Countermeasures against non-surveyability {#Countermeasures_against_non-surveyability}

On the other hand, Tymoczko's point that proofs must be at least possible to survey and that errors in difficult-to-survey proofs are less likely to fall to scrutiny is generally not contested; instead methods have been suggested to improve surveyability, especially of computer-assisted proofs. Among early suggestions was that of parallelization: the verification task could be split across many readers, each of which could survey a portion of the proof.[^o9] But modern practice, as made famous by [Flyspeck](wikipedia://en/368311#A_formal_proof), is to render the dubious portions of a proof in a restricted formalism and then verify them with a [proof checker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automated_proof_checking) that is available itself for survey. Indeed, the Appel–Haken proof has been thus verified.[^o10]

Nonetheless, automated verification has yet to see widespread adoption.[^o11]
