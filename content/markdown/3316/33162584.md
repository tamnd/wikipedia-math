# Weyl equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Weyl equation"}

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), particularly in [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), the **Weyl equation** ( ) is a [relativistic wave equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_wave_equation) for describing massless [spin-1/2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin-1/2) particles which have an inherent handedness, or [chirality](wikipedia://en/32703814), called **Weyl fermions**. The equation is named after [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544). The Weyl fermions are one of the three possible types of elementary fermions, the other two being the [Dirac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_fermion) and the [Majorana fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majorana_fermion).

:::

None of the [elementary particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle) in the [Standard Model](wikipedia://en/47641) are Weyl fermions. Previous to the confirmation of the [neutrino oscillations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino_oscillation), it was considered possible that the [neutrino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino) might be a Weyl fermion (it is now expected to be either a Dirac or a Majorana fermion). In [condensed matter physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_matter_physics), some materials can display [quasiparticles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasiparticle) that behave as Weyl fermions, leading to the notion of [Weyl semimetals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_semimetal).

Mathematically, any Dirac fermion can be decomposed as two Weyl fermions of opposite chirality coupled by the mass term.[^o22]

## History {#History}

*Image: ETH-BIB-Weyl, Hermann (1885-1955)-Portr 10803-023-AL.tif.* [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) c. 1928

The [Dirac equation](wikipedia://en/39407) was published in 1928 by [Paul Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742), and was first used to model [spin-1/2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin-1/2) particles in the framework of [relativistic quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/19389837).[^o19] Hermann Weyl published his equation in 1929 as a simplified version of the Dirac equation.[^o19][^o24] [Wolfgang Pauli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Pauli) wrote in 1933 against Weyl's equation because it violated [parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_%28physics%29).[^o18] However, three years before, Pauli had predicted the existence of a new elementary [fermion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion), the [neutrino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino), to explain the [beta decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_decay), which eventually was described using the Weyl equation.

In 1937, [Conyers Herring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conyers_Herring) proposed that Weyl fermions may exist as [quasiparticles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasiparticles) in condensed matter.[^o23]

Neutrinos were experimentally observed in 1956 as particles with extremely small masses (and historically were even sometimes thought to be massless).[^o18] The same year the [Wu experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wu_experiment) showed that [parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_%28physics%29) could be violated by the [weak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction), addressing Pauli's criticism.[^o3] This was followed by the measurement of the neutrino's [helicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helicity_%28particle_physics%29) in 1958.[^o18] As experiments showed no signs of a neutrino mass, interest in the Weyl equation resurfaced. Thus, the [Standard Model](wikipedia://en/47641) was built under the assumption that neutrinos were Weyl fermions.[^o18]

While Italian physicist [Bruno Pontecorvo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruno_Pontecorvo) had proposed in 1957 the possibility of neutrino masses and [neutrino oscillations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino_oscillation),[^o18] it was not until 1998 that [Super-Kamiokande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super-Kamiokande) eventually confirmed the existence of neutrino oscillations, and their non-zero mass.[^o18] This discovery confirmed that Weyl's equation cannot completely describe the propagation of neutrinos, as the equations can only describe massless particles.[^o19]

In 2015, the first [Weyl semimetal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_semimetal) was demonstrated experimentally in crystalline [tantalum arsenide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tantalum_arsenide) (TaAs) by the collaboration of [M.Z. Hasan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._Zahid_Hasan)'s ([Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University)) and H. Ding's ([Chinese Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Academy_of_Sciences)) teams.[^o23] Independently, the same year, [M. Soljačić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marin_Soljačić) team ([Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology)) also observed Weyl-like excitations in [photonic crystals](wikipedia://en/355377).[^o23]

## Equation {#Equation}

The Weyl equation comes in two forms. The right-handed form can be written as follows:[^o20][^o25][^o21]

$$\sigma^\mu\partial_\mu \psi = 0.$$

Expanding this equation, and inserting $c$ for the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light), it becomes

$$I_2 \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} + \sigma_x\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} + \sigma_y\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y} + \sigma_z\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial z} = 0,$$

where

$$\sigma^\mu = \begin{pmatrix}\sigma^0 & \sigma^1 & \sigma^2 & \sigma^3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}I_2 & \sigma_x & \sigma_y & \sigma_z\end{pmatrix}$$

is a [vector](wikipedia://en/23218956) whose components are the 2×2 [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) $I_2$ for $\mu = 0$ and the [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868) for $\mu = 1, 2, 3$, and $\psi$ is the [wavefunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavefunction) – one of the Weyl [spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinors). The left-handed form of the Weyl equation is usually written as

$$\bar{\sigma}^\mu\partial_\mu \psi = 0,$$

where

$$\bar{\sigma}^\mu = \begin{pmatrix}I_2 & -\sigma_x & -\sigma_y & -\sigma_z\end{pmatrix}.$$

The solutions of the right- and left-handed Weyl equations are different: they have right- and left-handed [helicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helicity_%28particle_physics%29), and thus [chirality](wikipedia://en/32703814), respectively. It is convenient to indicate this explicitly, as follows: $\sigma^\mu\partial_\mu \psi_{\rm R} = 0$ and $\bar{\sigma}^\mu\partial_\mu \psi_{\rm L} = 0$.

## Plane wave solutions {#Plane_wave_solutions}

The [plane-wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane-wave) solutions to the Weyl equation are referred to as the left- and right-handed Weyl spinors, each is with two components. Both have the form

$$\psi\left(\mathbf{r}, t\right) = \begin{pmatrix} \psi_1 \\ \psi_2 \\ \end{pmatrix} = \chi e^{-i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega t)} = \chi e^{-i(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r} - Et)/\hbar},$$

where

$$\chi = \begin{pmatrix} \chi_1 \\ \chi_2 \\ \end{pmatrix}$$

is a momentum-dependent two-component spinor which satisfies

$$\sigma^\mu p_\mu \chi = \left( I_2 E - \vec{\sigma} \cdot \vec{p} \right) \chi = 0$$

or

$$\bar{\sigma}^\mu p_\mu \chi = \left( I_2 E + \vec{\sigma} \cdot \vec{p} \right) \chi = 0.$$

By direct manipulation, one obtains that

$$\left(\bar{\sigma}^\nu p_\nu\right) \left(\sigma^\mu p_\mu\right) \chi = \left(\sigma^\nu p_\nu\right) \left(\bar{\sigma}^\mu p_\mu\right) \chi = p_\mu p^\mu \chi = \left(E^2 - \vec{p}\cdot\vec{p}\right) \chi = 0,$$

and concludes that the equations correspond to a particle that is [massless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass). As a result, the magnitude of [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431) $\mathbf{p}$ relates directly to the [wave-vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave-vector) $\mathbf{k}$ by the [de Broglie relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Broglie_relations) as

$$|\mathbf{p}| = \hbar |\mathbf{k}| = \frac{\hbar\omega}{c} \, \Rightarrow \, |\mathbf{k}| = \frac{\omega}{c}.$$

The equation can be written in terms of left- and right-handed spinors as

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma^\mu \partial_\mu \psi_{\rm R} &= 0, \\ \bar{\sigma}^\mu \partial_\mu \psi_{\rm L} &= 0. \end{aligned}$$

### Helicity {#Helicity}

The left and right components correspond to the helicity $\lambda$ of the particles, the projection of [angular momentum operator](wikipedia://en/3797203) $\mathbf{J}$ onto the linear momentum $\mathbf{p}$:

$$\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{J}\left|\mathbf{p},\lambda\right\rangle = \lambda |\mathbf{p}|\left|\mathbf{p},\lambda\right\rangle.$$

Here $\textstyle \lambda = \pm\frac{1}{2}$.

## Lorentz invariance {#Lorentz_invariance}

Both equations are [Lorentz invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_invariant) under the [Lorentz transformation](wikipedia://en/18404) $x \mapsto x^\prime = \Lambda x$ where $\Lambda \in \mathrm{SO}(1,3)$. More precisely, the equations transform as

$$\sigma^\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu} \psi_{\rm R}(x) \mapsto \sigma^\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime\mu}} \psi^\prime_{\rm R}\left(x^\prime\right) = \left(S^{-1}\right)^\dagger \sigma^\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu} \psi_{\rm R}(x),$$

where $S^\dagger$ is the [Hermitian transpose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_transpose), provided that the right-handed field transforms as

$$\psi_{\rm R}(x) \mapsto \psi^\prime_{\rm R}\left(x^\prime\right) = S\psi_{\rm R}(x).$$

The matrix $S \in SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ is related to the Lorentz transform by means of the [double covering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cover_%28mathematics%29) of the [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489) by the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{C})$ given by

$$\sigma_\mu {\Lambda^\mu}_\nu = \left(S^{-1}\right)^\dagger \sigma_\nu S^{-1}.$$

Thus, if the untransformed differential vanishes in one Lorentz frame, then it also vanishes in another. Similarly

$$\overline{\sigma}^\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu} \psi_{\rm L}(x) \mapsto \overline{\sigma}^\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime\mu}} \psi^\prime_{\rm L}\left(x^\prime\right) = S \overline{\sigma}^\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu} \psi_{\rm L}(x),$$

provided that the left-handed field transforms as

$$\psi_{\rm L}(x) \mapsto \psi^\prime_{\rm L}\left(x^\prime\right) = \left(S^\dagger\right)^{-1}\psi_{\rm L}(x).$$

Proof: Neither of these transformation properties are in any way "obvious", and so deserve a careful derivation. Begin with the form

$$\psi_{\rm R}(x) \mapsto \psi^\prime_{\rm R}\left(x^\prime\right) = R\psi_{\rm R}(x)$$

for some unknown $R \in \mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{C})$ to be determined. The Lorentz transform, in coordinates, is

$$x^{\prime\mu} = {\Lambda^\mu}_\nu x^\nu,$$

or, equivalently,

$$x^\nu = {\left(\Lambda^{-1}\right)^\nu}_\mu x^{\prime\mu}.$$

This leads to

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma^\mu \partial^\prime_\mu \psi^\prime_{\rm R}\left(x^\prime\right) &= \sigma^\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime\mu}} \psi^\prime_{\rm R}\left(x^\prime\right) \\ &= \sigma^\mu \frac{\partial x^\nu}{\partial x^{\prime\mu}} \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\nu} R \psi_{\rm R}(x) \\ &= \sigma^\mu {\left(\Lambda^{-1}\right)^\nu}_\mu \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\nu} R\psi_{\rm R}(x) \\ &= \sigma^\mu {\left(\Lambda^{-1}\right)^\nu}_\mu \partial_\nu R\psi_{\rm R}(x). \end{aligned}$$

In order to make use of the Weyl map

$$\sigma_\mu{\Lambda^\mu}_\nu = \left(S^{-1}\right)^\dagger\sigma_\nu S^{-1},$$

a few indexes must be raised and lowered. This is easier said than done, as it invokes the identity

$$\eta\Lambda^\mathsf{T}\eta = \Lambda^{-1},$$

where $\eta = \mbox{diag}(+1, -1, -1, -1)$ is the flat-space [Minkowski metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_metric). The above identity is often used to define the elements $\Lambda\in \mathrm{SO}(1,3)$. One takes the transpose

$${\left(\Lambda^{-1}\right)^\nu}_\mu = {\left(\Lambda^{-1\mathsf{T}}\right)_\mu}^\nu$$

to write

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma^\mu {\left(\Lambda^{-1}\right)^\nu}_\mu \partial_\nu R\psi_{\rm R}(x) &= \sigma^\mu {\left(\Lambda^{-1\mathsf{T}}\right)_\mu}^\nu \partial_\nu R\psi_{\rm R}(x) \\ &= \sigma_\mu {\Lambda^\mu}_\nu \partial^\nu R\psi_{\rm R}(x) \\ &= \left(S^{-1}\right)^\dagger \sigma_\mu \partial^\mu S^{-1} R\psi_{\rm R}(x). \end{aligned}$$

One thus regains the original form if $S^{-1} R = 1$, that is, $R = S$. Performing the same manipulations for the left-handed equation, one concludes that

$$\psi_{\rm L}(x)\mapsto \psi^\prime_{\rm L}\left(x^\prime\right) = L\psi_{\rm L}(x)$$

with $L = \left(S^\dagger\right)^{-1}$.

### Relationship to Majorana {#Relationship_to_Majorana}

The Weyl equation is conventionally interpreted as describing a [massless particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massless_particle). However, with a slight alteration, one may obtain a two-component version of the [Majorana equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majorana_equation).[^o17] This arises because the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the [symplectic group](wikipedia://en/173977) $\mathrm{Sp}(2,\mathbb{C})$. The symplectic group is defined as the set of all complex 2×2 matrices that satisfy

$$S^\mathsf{T} \omega S = \omega,$$

where

$$\omega = i\sigma_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$

The defining relationship can be rewritten as $\omega S^* = \left( S^\dagger \right)^{-1} \omega$, where $S^*$ is the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918). The right-handed field, as noted earlier, transforms as

$$\psi_{\rm R}(x) \mapsto \psi^\prime_{\rm R}\left(x^\prime\right) = S\psi_{\rm R}(x),$$

and so the complex conjugate field transforms as

$$\psi^*_{\rm R}(x) \mapsto \psi^{\prime *}_{\rm R}\left(x^\prime\right) = S^*\psi^*_{\rm R}(x).$$

Applying the defining relationship, one concludes that

$$m \omega \psi^*_{\rm R}(x) \mapsto m \omega \psi^{\prime *}_{\rm R} \left(x^\prime\right) = \left(S^\dagger\right)^{-1} m \omega \psi^*_{\rm R}(x),$$

which is exactly the same [Lorentz covariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_covariance) property noted earlier. Thus, the linear combination, using an arbitrary complex phase factor $\eta = e^{i\phi}$,

$$i\sigma^\mu \partial_\mu \psi_{\rm R}(x) + \eta m \omega \psi^*_{\rm R}(x),$$

transforms in a covariant fashion; setting this to zero gives the complex two-component [Majorana equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majorana_equation). The Majorana equation is conventionally written as a four-component real equation, rather than a two-component complex equation; the above can be brought into four-component form (see that article for details). Similarly, the left-chiral Majorana equation (including an arbitrary phase factor $\zeta$) is

$$i\overline{\sigma}^\mu \partial_\mu \psi_{\rm L}(x) + \zeta m \omega \psi^*_{\rm L}(x) = 0.$$

As noted earlier, the left and right chiral versions are related by a parity transformation. The skew complex conjugate $\omega\psi^* = i\sigma^2\psi$ can be recognized as the [charge conjugate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_conjugation) form of $\psi$. Thus, the Majorana equation can be read as an equation that connects a spinor to its charge-conjugate form. The two distinct phases on the mass term are related to the two distinct eigenvalues of the charge conjugation operator; see [charge conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_conjugation) and Majorana equation for details.

Define a pair of operators, the Majorana operators,

$$D_{\rm L} = i\overline{\sigma}^\mu \partial_\mu +\zeta m\omega K,\qquad D_{\rm R} = i\sigma^\mu \partial_\mu + \eta m\omega K,$$

where $K$ is a short-hand reminder to take the complex conjugate. Under Lorentz transformations, these transform as

$$D_{\rm L} \mapsto D^\prime_{\rm L} = S D_{\rm L} S^\dagger, \qquad D_{\rm R} \mapsto D^\prime_{\rm R} = \left(S^\dagger\right)^{-1} D_{\rm R} S^{-1},$$

whereas the Weyl spinors transform as

$$\psi_{\rm L} \mapsto \psi^\prime_{\rm L} = \left(S^\dagger\right)^{-1} \psi_{\rm L}, \qquad \psi_{\rm R} \mapsto \psi^\prime_{\rm R} = S \psi_{\rm R},$$

just as above. Thus, the matched combinations of these are Lorentz covariant, and one may take

$$D_{\rm L} \psi_{\rm L} = 0 \qquad D_{\rm R} \psi_{\rm R} = 0$$

as a pair of complex 2-spinor Majorana equations.

The products $D_{\rm L} D_{\rm R}$ and $D_{\rm R} D_{\rm L}$ are both Lorentz covariant. The product is explicitly

$$D_{\rm R}D_{\rm L} = \left(i\sigma^\mu \partial_\mu + \eta m\omega K\right) \left(i\overline{\sigma}^\mu \partial_\mu + \zeta m\omega K\right) = -\left(\partial_t^2 - \vec\nabla \cdot \vec\nabla + \eta\zeta^* m^2\right) = -\left(\square + \eta\zeta^* m^2\right).$$

Verifying this requires keeping in mind that $\omega^2 = -1$ and that $K i = -i K$. The RHS reduces to the [Klein–Gordon operator](wikipedia://en/209627) provided that $\eta\zeta^* = 1$, that is, $\eta = \zeta$. These two Majorana operators are thus "square roots" of the Klein–Gordon operator.

## Lagrangian densities {#Lagrangian_densities}

The equations are obtained from the [Lagrangian densities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_density)

$$\mathcal L = i \psi_{\rm R}^\dagger \sigma^\mu \partial_\mu \psi_{\rm R},$$

$$\mathcal L = i \psi_{\rm L}^\dagger \bar\sigma^\mu \partial_\mu \psi_{\rm L}.$$

By treating the spinor and its [conjugate](wikipedia://en/667175) (denoted by $\dagger$) as independent variables, the relevant Weyl equation is obtained.

## Weyl spinors {#Weyl_spinors}

The term **Weyl spinor** is also frequently used in a more general setting, as an element of a [Clifford module](wikipedia://en/709999). This is closely related to the solutions given above, and gives a natural geometric interpretation to [spinors](wikipedia://en/29276) as geometric objects living on a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470). This general setting has multiple strengths: it clarifies their interpretation as [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion) in physics, and it shows precisely how to define spin in [General Relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Relativity), or, indeed, for any [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) or [pseudo-Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/287555). This is informally sketched as follows.

The Weyl equation is [invariant](wikipedia://en/202886) under the action of the Lorentz group. This means that, as [boosts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_boost) and [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789) are applied, the form of the equation itself does not change. However, the form of the [spinor](wikipedia://en/29276) $\psi$ itself does change. Ignoring [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) entirely, the algebra of the spinors is described by a (complexified) [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305). The spinors transform under the action of the [spin group](wikipedia://en/411231). This is entirely analogous to how one might talk about a vector, and how it transforms under the [rotation group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_group), except that now, it has been adapted to the case of spinors.

Given an arbitrary [pseudo-Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/287555) $M$ of dimension $(p,q)$, one may consider its [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) $TM$. At any given point $x \in M$, the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) $T_x M$ is a $(p,q)$-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370). Given this vector space, one can construct the Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(p,q)$ on it. If $\{e_i\}$ are a [vector space basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_space_basis) on $T_x M$, one may construct a pair of Weyl spinors as[^o11]

$$w_j = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(e_{2j} + ie_{2j+1}\right)$$

and

$$w_j^* = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(e_{2j} - ie_{2j+1}\right).$$

When properly examined in light of the Clifford algebra, these are naturally [anti-commuting](wikipedia://en/294358), that is, one has that $w_j w_m = -w_m w_j$. This can be happily interpreted as the mathematical realization of the [Pauli exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/24669), thus allowing these abstractly defined formal structures to be interpreted as fermions. For $(p,q)=(1,3)$-dimensional [Minkowski space-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space-time), there are only two such spinors possible, by convention labelled "left" and "right", as described above. A more formal, general presentation of Weyl spinors can be found in the article on the [spin group](wikipedia://en/411231).

The abstract, general-relativistic form of the Weyl equation can be understood as follows: Given a pseudo-Riemannian manifold $M$, one constructs a [fiber bundle](wikipedia://en/276526) above it, with the spin group as the fiber. The spin group $\mathrm{Spin}(p,q)$ is a [double cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cover_%28mathematics%29) of the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) $\mathrm{SO}(p,q)$, and so one can identify the spin group fiber-wise with the [frame bundle](wikipedia://en/366550) over $M$. When this is done, the resulting structure is called a [spin structure](wikipedia://en/2844303).

Selecting a single point on the fiber corresponds to selecting a [local coordinate frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_reference_frame) for spacetime; two different points on the fiber are related by a (Lorentz) boost/rotation, that is, by a local change of coordinates. The natural inhabitants of the spin structure are the Weyl spinors, in that the spin structure completely describes how the spinors behave under (Lorentz) boosts/rotations.

Given a [spin manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_manifold), the analog of the [metric connection](wikipedia://en/4794503) is the [spin connection](wikipedia://en/2915190); this is effectively "the same thing" as the normal connection, just with spin indexes attached to it in a consistent fashion. The [covariant derivative](wikipedia://en/431848) can be defined in terms of the connection in an entirely conventional way. It acts naturally on the [Clifford bundle](wikipedia://en/8687388); the Clifford bundle is the space in which the spinors live. The general exploration of such structures and their relationships is termed [spin geometry](wikipedia://en/32013990).

### Mathematical definition {#Mathematical_definition}

For even $n$, the even subalgebra $\mathbb{C}l^0(n)$ of the complex Clifford algebra $\mathbb{C}l(n)$ is isomorphic to $\mathrm{End}(\mathbb{C}^{N/2}) \oplus \mathrm{End}(\mathbb{C}^{N/2}) =: \Delta^+_n \oplus \Delta^-_n$, where $N = 2^{n/2}$. A left-handed (respectively, right-handed) **complex Weyl spinor** in $n$-dimensional space is an element of $\Delta^+_n$ (respectively, $\Delta^-_n$).

### Special cases {#Special_cases}

There are three important special cases that can be constructed from Weyl spinors. One is the [Dirac spinor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_spinor), which can be taken to be a pair of Weyl spinors, one left-handed, and one right-handed. These are coupled together in such a way as to represent an electrically charged fermion field. The [electric charge](wikipedia://en/9804) arises because the Dirac field transforms under the action of the complexified spin group $\mathrm{Spin}^\mathbb{C}(p,q)$. This group has the structure

$$\mathrm{Spin}^\mathbb{C}(p,q)\cong\mathrm{Spin}(p,q)\times_{\mathbb{Z}_2} S^1,$$

where $S^1\cong \mathrm{U}(1)$ is the circle, and can be identified with the $\mathrm{U}(1)$ of [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism). The product $\times_{\mathbb{Z}_2}$ is just fancy notation denoting the product $\mathrm{Spin}(p,q)\times S^1$ with opposite points $(s,u) = (-s,-u)$ identified (a double covering).

The [Majorana spinor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majorana_spinor) is again a pair of Weyl spinors, but this time arranged so that the left-handed spinor is the charge conjugate of the right-handed spinor. The result is a field with two less degrees of freedom than the Dirac spinor. It is unable to interact with the electromagnetic field, since it transforms as a scalar under the action of the $\mathrm{spin}^\mathbb{C}$ group. That is, it transforms as a spinor, but transversally, such that it is invariant under the $\mathrm{U}(1)$ action of the spin group.

The third special case is the [ELKO spinor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ELKO_spinor), constructed much as the Majorana spinor, except with an additional minus sign between the charge-conjugate pair. This again renders it electrically neutral, but introduces a number of other quite surprising properties.
