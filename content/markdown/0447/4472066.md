# Atomic formula

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Atomic formula"}

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), an **atomic formula** (also known as an **atom** or a **prime formula**) is a [formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formula_%28mathematical_logic%29) with no deeper [propositional](wikipedia://en/81094) structure, that is, a formula that contains no [logical connectives](wikipedia://en/18153) or equivalently a formula that has no strict subformulas. Atoms are thus the simplest [well-formed formulas](wikipedia://en/404582) of the logic. Compound formulas are formed by combining the atomic formulas using the logical connectives.

:::

The precise form of atomic formulas depends on the logic under consideration; for [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154), for example, a [propositional variable](wikipedia://en/1557562) is often more briefly referred to as an "atomic formula", but, more precisely, a propositional variable is not an atomic formula but a formal expression that denotes an atomic formula. For [predicate logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_logic), the atoms are predicate symbols together with their arguments, each argument being a [term](wikipedia://en/10983#Formation_rules). In [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858), atomic formulas are merely [strings](wikipedia://en/27701) of symbols with a given [signature](wikipedia://en/7788156), which may or may not be [satisfiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satisfiable) with respect to a given model.[^o2]

::: {#o4 .statement cue=named-heading name="Atomic formula in first-order logic"}

## Atomic formula in first-order logic {#Atomic_formula_in_first-order_logic}

The well-formed terms and propositions of ordinary [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) have the following [syntax](wikipedia://en/26860):

[Terms](wikipedia://en/2216678):

- $$t \equiv c \mid x \mid f (t_{1},\dotsc, t_{n}),$$

that is, a term is [recursively defined](wikipedia://en/1047605) to be a constant *c* (a named object from the [domain of discourse](wikipedia://en/229180)), or a variable *x* (ranging over the objects in the domain of discourse), or an *n*-ary function *f* whose arguments are terms *t*<sub>*k*</sub>. Functions map [tuples](wikipedia://en/132729) of objects to objects.

Propositions:

- $$A, B, ... \equiv P (t_{1},\dotsc, t_{n}) \mid A \wedge B \mid \top \mid A \vee B \mid \bot \mid A \supset B \mid \forall x.\ A \mid \exists x.\ A,$$

that is, a proposition is recursively defined to be an *n*-ary [predicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_%28mathematics%29) *P* whose arguments are terms *t*<sub>*k*</sub>, or an expression composed of [logical connectives](wikipedia://en/18153) (and, or) and [quantifiers](wikipedia://en/43507260) (for-all, there-exists) used with other propositions.

An **atomic formula** or **atom** is simply a predicate applied to a tuple of terms; that is, an atomic formula is a formula of the form *P* (*t*<sub>1</sub> ,…, *t*<sub>*n*</sub>) for *P* a predicate, and the *t*<sub>*n*</sub> terms.

All other well-formed formulas are obtained by composing atoms with logical connectives and quantifiers.

For example, the formula ∀*x. P* (*x*) ∧ ∃*y. Q* (*y*, *f* (*x*)) ∨ ∃*z. R* (*z*) contains the atoms

- $$P (x)$$
- $$Q (y, f (x))$$
- $$R (z).$$

As there are no quantifiers appearing in an atomic formula, all occurrences of variable symbols in an atomic formula are free.[^o5]
