# Lagrange, Euler, and Kovalevskaya tops

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lagrange, Euler, and Kovalevskaya tops"}

In [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), the [rotation](wikipedia://en/39789) of a [rigid body](wikipedia://en/455769) such as a [spinning top](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinning_top) under the influence of [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) is not, in general, an [integrable problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_problem). There are however three famous cases that are integrable, the **Euler**, the **Lagrange**, and the **Kovalevskaya top**, which are in fact the only integrable cases when the system is subject to [holonomic constraints](wikipedia://en/9777067).[^o2][^o3][^o4] In addition to the energy, each of these tops involves two additional [constants of motion](wikipedia://en/5391037) that give rise to the [integrability](wikipedia://en/2573213#Hamiltonian_systems_and_Liouville_integrability).

:::

The Euler top describes a free top without any particular symmetry moving in the absence of any external [torque](wikipedia://en/30400), and for which the fixed point is the [center of gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_gravity). The Lagrange top is a symmetric top, in which two moments of [inertia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertia) are the same and the center of gravity lies on the [symmetry axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_axis). The Kovalevskaya top[^o5][^o6][^o7] is a special symmetric top with a unique ratio of the [moments of inertia](wikipedia://en/157700) which satisfy the relation

$$I_1=I_2= 2 I_3,$$

That is, two moments of inertia are equal, the third is half as large, and the center of gravity is located in the [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) perpendicular to the symmetry axis (parallel to the plane of the two degenerate principal axes).

## Hamiltonian formulation of classical tops {#Hamiltonian_formulation_of_classical_tops}

The configuration of a classical top[^o9] is described at time $t$ by three time-dependent [principal axes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_axis_%28mechanics%29), defined by the three [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) vectors $\hat{\mathbf{e}}^1$, $\hat {\mathbf{e}}^2$ and $\hat{\mathbf{e}}^3$ with corresponding moments of inertia $I_1$, $I_2$ and $I_3$ and the [angular velocity](wikipedia://en/65927) about those axes. In a [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319) formulation of classical tops, the [conjugate dynamical variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_variable) are the components of the [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839) vector $\bf{L}$ along the principal axes

$$(\ell_1, \ell_2, \ell_3)= (\mathbf{L}\cdot \hat {\bf{e}}^1,\bf{L}\cdot \hat {\mathbf{e}}^2,\bf{L}\cdot \hat {\mathbf{e}}^3)$$

and the *z*-components of the three principal axes,

$$(n_1, n_2, n_3)= (\mathbf{\hat{z}}\cdot \hat {\mathbf{e}}^1,\mathbf{\hat{z}}\cdot \hat {\mathbf{e}}^2,\mathbf{\hat{z}}\cdot \hat {\mathbf{e}}^3)$$

The [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340) relations of these variables is given by

$$\{ \ell_a, \ell_b\} = \varepsilon_{abc} \ell_c, \ \{\ell_a, n_b\} = \varepsilon_{abc} n_c, \ \{n_a, n_b\} = 0$$

If the position of the center of mass is given by $\vec{R}_{cm} = (a \mathbf{\hat e}^1 + b \mathbf{\hat e}^2 + c\mathbf{\hat e}^3)$, then the Hamiltonian of a top is given by

$$H = \frac{(\ell_1)^2}{2I_1}+\frac{(\ell_2)^2}{2I_2}+\frac{(\ell_3)^2}{2I_3} + mg (a n_1 + bn_2 + cn_3) = \frac{(\ell_1)^2}{2I_1}+\frac{(\ell_2)^2}{2I_2}+\frac{(\ell_3)^2}{2I_3} + mg \vec{R}_{cm}\cdot \mathbf{\hat{z}},$$

The equations of motion are then determined by

$$\dot{\ell}_a = \{ H, \ell_a\}, \dot{n}_a = \{H, n_a\}.$$

Explicitly, these are

$$\dot \ell_1 = \left(\frac{1}{I_3} - \frac{1}{I_2}\right)\ell_2 \ell_3 + mg(c n_2 - b n_3)$$

$$\dot n_1 = \frac{\ell_3}{I_3} n_2 - \frac{\ell_2}{I_2} n_3$$

and cyclic permutations of the indices.

### Mathematical description of phase space {#Mathematical_description_of_phase_space}

In mathematical terms, the spatial configuration of the body is described by a point on the [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) $SO(3)$, the three-dimensional [rotation group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_group), which is the rotation matrix from the lab frame to the body frame. The full configuration space or phase space is the [cotangent bundle](wikipedia://en/221600) $T^*SO(3)$, with the fibers $T^*_RSO(3)$ parametrizing the angular momentum at spatial configuration $R$. The Hamiltonian is a function on this phase space.

## Euler top {#Euler_top}

The Euler top, named after [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902), is an untorqued top (for example, a top in free fall), with Hamiltonian

$$H_{\rm E} = \frac{(\ell_1)^2}{2I_1}+\frac{(\ell_2)^2}{2I_2}+\frac{(\ell_3)^2}{2I_3},$$

The four constants of motion are the energy $H_{\rm E}$ and the three components of angular momentum in the lab frame,

$$(L_1,L_2,L_3) = \ell_1 \mathbf{\hat e}^1 +\ell_2\mathbf{\hat e}^2+ \ell_3 \mathbf{\hat e}^3.$$

:::

## Lagrange top {#Lagrange_top}

The Lagrange top,[^o14] named after [Joseph-Louis Lagrange](wikipedia://en/87793), is a symmetric top with the center of mass along the symmetry axis at location, $\mathbf{R}_{\rm cm} = h\mathbf{\hat e}^3$, with Hamiltonian

$$H_{\rm L}= \frac{(\ell_1)^2+(\ell_2)^2}{2I}+\frac{(\ell_3)^2}{2I_3}+ mgh n_3.$$

The four constants of motion are the energy $H_{\rm L}$, the angular momentum component along the symmetry axis, $\ell_3$, the angular momentum in the *z*-direction

$$L_z = \ell_1n_1+\ell_2n_2+\ell_3n_3,$$

and the magnitude of the *n*-vector

$$n^2 = n_1^2 + n_2^2 + n_3^2$$

## Kovalevskaya top {#Kovalevskaya_top}

The Kovalevskaya top[^o5][^o7] is a symmetric top in which $I_1=I_2=2I$, $I_3=I$ and the center of mass lies in the plane perpendicular to the symmetry axis $\mathbf {R}_{\rm cm} = h \mathbf{\hat e}^1$. It was discovered by [Sofia Kovalevskaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sofia_Kovalevskaya) in 1888 and presented in her paper "Sur le problème de la rotation d'un corps solide autour d'un point fixe", which won her the Prix Bordin from the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences) in 1888. The Hamiltonian is

$$H_{\rm K}= \frac{(\ell_1)^2+(\ell_2)^2+ 2 (\ell_3)^2}{2I}+ mgh n_1.$$

The four constants of motion are the energy $H_{\rm K}$, the Kovalevskaya invariant

$$K = \xi_+ \xi_-$$

where the variables $\xi_\pm$ are defined by

$$\xi_\pm = (\ell_1\pm i \ell_2 )^2- 2 mgh I(n_1\pm i n_2),$$

the angular momentum component in the *z*-direction,

$$L_z = \ell_1n_1+\ell_2n_2+\ell_3n_3,$$

and the magnitude of the *n*-vector

$$n^2 = n_1^2 + n_2^2 + n_3^2.$$

## Nonholonomic constraints {#Nonholonomic_constraints}

If the constraints are relaxed to allow [nonholonomic](wikipedia://en/554248) constraints, there are other possible integrable tops besides the three well-known cases. The nonholonomic **Goryachev–Chaplygin top** (introduced by D. Goryachev in 1900[^o17] and integrated by [Sergey Chaplygin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergey_Chaplygin) in 1948[^o18][^o19]) is also integrable ($I_1=I_2=4I_3$). Its center of gravity lies in the [equatorial plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_plane).[^o20]
