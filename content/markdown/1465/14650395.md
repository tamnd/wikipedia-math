# Monadic second-order logic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Monadic second-order logic"}

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), **monadic second-order logic** (**MSO**) is the fragment of [second-order logic](wikipedia://en/341428) where the second-order quantification is limited to quantification over sets.[^o2] It is particularly important in the [logic of graphs](wikipedia://en/47857242), because of [Courcelle's theorem](wikipedia://en/35810608), which provides algorithms for evaluating monadic second-order formulas over graphs of bounded [treewidth](wikipedia://en/3987086). It is also of fundamental importance in [automata theory](wikipedia://en/103356), where the [Büchi–Elgot–Trakhtenbrot theorem](wikipedia://en/68241562) gives a logical characterization of the [regular languages](wikipedia://en/25723).

:::

Second-order logic allows quantification over [predicates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_%28mathematical_logic%29). However, MSO is the [fragment](wikipedia://en/43571557) in which second-order quantification is limited to monadic predicates (predicates having a single argument). This is often described as quantification over "sets" because monadic predicates are equivalent in expressive power to sets (the set of elements for which the predicate is true).

Monadic second-order logic is expressively equivalent to [plural logic](wikipedia://en/444789).

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Variants"}

## Variants {#Variants}

Monadic second-order logic comes in two variants. In the variant considered over structures such as graphs and in Courcelle's theorem, the formula may involve non-monadic predicate constants (in this case the binary edge predicate $E(x, y)$), but quantification is restricted to be over monadic predicate variables only. In the variant considered in automata theory and the Büchi–Elgot–Trakhtenbrot theorem, all predicates, constant or variable, must be monadic, with the exceptions of equality ($=$) and ordering ($<$) relations.

:::

## Computational complexity of evaluation {#Computational_complexity_of_evaluation}

Existential monadic second-order logic (EMSO) is the fragment of MSO in which all quantifiers over sets must be [existential quantifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_quantifier), outside of any other part of the formula. The first-order quantifiers are not restricted. That is, one can speak of "there exists some *x*, such that..." and "for all *x*, we have..." where *x* is a term, but one can only speak of "there exists some monadic predicate *P*, such that..." not "for all monadic predicate *P*, we have...".

[Fagin's theorem](wikipedia://en/3122050) states that existential second-order logic (ESO) captures precisely the [descriptive complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descriptive_complexity) of the complexity class [NP](wikipedia://en/21562). By analogy, the class of problems that may be expressed in existential monadic second-order logic has been called **monadic NP**. In other words, EMSO captures precisely the descriptive complexity of monadic NP (MNP).

In the [logic of graphs](wikipedia://en/47857242), testing whether a graph is [disconnected](wikipedia://en/1614492) is in MNP, as the test can be represented by a formula that describes the existence of a proper subset of vertices with no edges connecting them to the rest of the graph. The complementary problem, testing whether a graph is connected, does not belong to monadic NP. That is, it is a problem in [co](wikipedia://en/1929955)-MNP \\ MNP. By symmetry, graph disconnection testing is in MNP \\ co-MNP, showing that neither complexity class contains the other.[^o6][^o7] The addition of "monadic" makes the question easier. The analogous question, of whether NP = [co-NP](wikipedia://en/6184), is an open question in computational complexity.

By contrast, when we wish to check whether a Boolean MSO formula is satisfied by an input finite [tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_%28data_structure%29), this problem can be solved in linear time in the tree, by translating the Boolean MSO formula to a [tree automaton](wikipedia://en/98748)[^o8] and evaluating the automaton on the tree. In terms of the query, however, the complexity of this process is generally [nonelementary](wikipedia://en/2284654).[^o9][^o14] Thanks to [Courcelle's theorem](wikipedia://en/35810608), we can also evaluate a Boolean MSO formula in linear time on an input graph if the [treewidth](wikipedia://en/3987086) of the graph is bounded by a constant.

For MSO formulas that have [free variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_variable), when the input data is a tree or has bounded treewidth, there are efficient [enumeration algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enumeration_algorithm) to produce the set of all solutions,[^o10] ensuring that the input data is preprocessed in linear time and that each solution is then produced in a delay linear in the size of each solution, i.e., constant-delay in the common case where all free variables of the query are first-order variables (i.e., they do not represent sets). There are also efficient algorithms for counting the number of solutions of the MSO formula in that case.[^o11]

## Decidability and complexity of satisfiability {#Decidability_and_complexity_of_satisfiability}

The satisfiability problem for monadic second-order logic is undecidable in general because this logic subsumes [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983).

The monadic second-order theory of the infinite complete [binary tree](wikipedia://en/4321), called [S2S](wikipedia://en/72252525), is [decidable](wikipedia://en/913118).[^o13] As a consequence of this result, the following theories are decidable:

- The monadic second-order theory of trees.
- S1S, The monadic second-order theory with one successor (that is, of $\mathbb{N}$)
- WS2S and WS1S, which restrict quantification to finite subsets (weak monadic second-order logic).
  - By binary-coding natural numbers as finite subsets, addition is definable even in WS1S.

For each of these theories (S2S, S1S, WS2S, WS1S), the complexity of the decision problem is [nonelementary](wikipedia://en/2284654).[^o9][^o14] They could be obtained by performing a reduction of the [emptiness problem](wikipedia://en/57342784) of the [star-free languages](wikipedia://en/4922792) to WS1S.[^o15] The WS1S is specifically known to be [TOWER](wikipedia://en/2284654)-complete.[^o16]

### Use of satisfiability of MSO on trees in verification {#Use_of_satisfiability_of_MSO_on_trees_in_verification}

Monadic second-order logic of trees has applications in [formal verification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_verification). Decision procedures for MSO satisfiability[^o18][^o19][^o20] have been used to prove properties of programs manipulating linked [data structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_structures),[^o21] as a form of [shape analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_analysis_%28program_analysis%29), and for [symbolic reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_reasoning) in [hardware verification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardware_verification).[^o22]
