# Pierpont prime

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Pierpont prime"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), a **Pierpont prime** is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) of the form

$$2^u\cdot 3^v + 1\,$$

for some nonnegative [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $u$ and $v$. That is, they are the prime numbers $p$ for which $p - 1$ is [3-smooth](wikipedia://en/1035039). They are named after the mathematician [James Pierpont](wikipedia://en/2594993), who used them to characterize the [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306) that can be constructed using [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673). The same characterization applies to polygons that can be constructed using ruler, compass, and [angle trisector](wikipedia://en/91111), or using [paper folding](wikipedia://en/232840).

:::

Except for 2 and the [Fermat primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_prime), every Pierpont prime must be 1 modulo 6. The first few Pierpont primes are: [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2_%28number%29), [3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3_%28number%29), [5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5_%28number%29), [7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7_%28number%29), [13](wikipedia://en/37231), [17](wikipedia://en/208288), [19](wikipedia://en/208292), [37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/37_%28number%29), [73](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/73_%28number%29), [97](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/97_%28number%29), [109](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/109_%28number%29), [163](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/163_%28number%29), [193](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/193_%28number%29), [257](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/257_%28number%29), [433](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/433_%28number%29), 487, 577, 769, 1153, 1297, 1459, 2593, 2917, 3457, 3889, 10369, 12289, 17497, 18433, 39367, 52489, 65537, 139969, 147457, 209953, 331777, 472393, 629857, 746497, 786433, 839809, 995329, ... It has been conjectured that there are infinitely many Pierpont primes, but this remains unproven.

## Distribution {#Distribution}

::: {#o4 .problem cue=unsolved}

Are there infinitely many Pierpont primes?

:::

A Pierpont prime with $v = 0$ is of the form $2^u+1$, and is therefore a [Fermat prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_prime) (unless $u = 0$). If $v$ is [positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number) then $u$ must also be positive (because $3^v+1$ would be an [even number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_number) greater than 2 and therefore not prime), and therefore the non-Fermat Pierpont primes all have the form $6k + 1$, when $k$ is a positive integer (except for 2, when $u = v = 0$).

*Image: Pierpont exponent distribution.png.* Distribution of the exponents for the smaller Pierpont primes

Empirically, the Pierpont primes do not seem to be particularly rare or sparsely distributed; there are 42 Pierpont primes less than 10<sup>6</sup>, 65 less than 10<sup>9</sup>, 157 less than 10<sup>20</sup>, and 795 less than 10<sup>100</sup>. There are few restrictions from algebraic factorisations on the Pierpont primes, so there are no requirements like the [Mersenne prime](wikipedia://en/18908) condition that the exponent must be prime. Thus, it is expected that among $n$-digit numbers of the correct form $2^u\cdot3^v+1$, the fraction of these that are prime should be proportional to $1/n$, a similar proportion as the proportion of prime numbers among all $n$-digit numbers. As there are $\Theta(n^{2})$ numbers of the correct form in this range, there should be $\Theta(n)$ Pierpont primes.

[Andrew M. Gleason](wikipedia://en/4244871) made this reasoning explicit, [conjecturing](wikipedia://en/6138) there are infinitely many Pierpont primes, and more specifically that there should be approximately $9n$ Pierpont primes up to $10^{n}$.[^o5] According to Gleason's conjecture there are $\Theta(\log N)$ Pierpont primes smaller than *N*, as opposed to the smaller conjectural number $O(\log \log N)$ of Mersenne primes in that range.

## Primality testing {#Primality_testing}

When $2^u > 3^v$, $2^u\cdot 3^v + 1$ is a [Proth number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proth_number) and thus its primality can be tested by [Proth's theorem](wikipedia://en/3225985). On the other hand, when $2^u < 3^v$ alternative primality tests for $M=2^u\cdot 3^v + 1$ are possible based on the factorization of $M-1$ as a small even number multiplied by a large [power of 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_of_3).[^o7]

## Polygon construction {#Polygon_construction}

In the [mathematics of paper folding](wikipedia://en/232840), the [Huzita axioms](wikipedia://en/232844) define six of the seven types of fold possible. It has been shown that these folds are sufficient to allow the construction of the points that solve any [cubic equation](wikipedia://en/180787).[^o13] It follows that they allow any [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) of $N$ sides to be formed, as long as $N \ge 3$ and is of the form $2^{m}3^{n}\rho$, where $\rho$ is a product of distinct Pierpont primes. This is the same class of regular polygons as those that can be constructed with a [compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass), [straightedge](wikipedia://en/147854), and [angle trisector](wikipedia://en/91111).[^o5] Regular polygons which can be constructed with only compass and straightedge ([constructible polygons](wikipedia://en/523430)) are the special case where $n = 0$ and $\rho$ is a product of distinct [Fermat primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_prime), themselves a subset of Pierpont primes.

In 1895, [James Pierpont](wikipedia://en/2594993) studied the same class of regular polygons; his work is what gives the name to the Pierpont primes. Pierpont generalized compass and straightedge constructions in a different way, by adding the ability to draw [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673) whose coefficients come from previously constructed points. As he showed, the regular $N$-gons that can be constructed with these operations are the ones such that the [totient](wikipedia://en/53452) of $N$ is 3-smooth. Since the totient of a prime is formed by subtracting one from it, the primes $N$ for which Pierpont's construction works are exactly the Pierpont primes. However, Pierpont did not describe the form of the composite numbers with 3-smooth totients.[^o14] As Gleason later showed, these numbers are exactly the ones of the form $2^{m}3^{n}\rho$ given above.[^o5]

The smallest prime that is not a Pierpont (or Fermat) prime is 11; therefore, the [hendecagon](wikipedia://en/614840) is the first regular polygon that cannot be constructed with compass, straightedge and angle trisector (or origami, or conic sections). However, Benjamin and Snyder showed in 2014 that the regular 11-gon is [neusis-constructible](wikipedia://en/3752339).[^o15] All other regular $N$-gons with $3 \le N \le 21$ can be constructed with compass, straightedge and trisector.[^o5]

## Generalization {#Generalization}

A **Pierpont prime of the second kind** is a prime number of the form 2<sup>*u*</sup>3<sup>*v*</sup> − 1. These numbers are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 17, 23, 31, 47, 53, 71, 107, 127, 191, 383, 431, 647, 863, 971, 1151, 2591, 4373, 6143, 6911, 8191, 8747, 13121, 15551, 23327, 27647, 62207, 73727, 131071, 139967, 165887, 294911, 314927, 442367, 472391, 497663, 524287, 786431, 995327, ...

The largest known primes of this type are [Mersenne primes](wikipedia://en/18908); currently the largest known is $2^{136279841}-1$ (41,024,320 decimal digits). The largest known Pierpont prime of the second kind that is not a Mersenne prime is $3\cdot 2^{22103376}-1$, found by [PrimeGrid](wikipedia://en/3255603).[^o18]

A **generalized Pierpont prime** is a prime of the form $p_1^{n_1} \!\cdot p_2^{n_2} \!\cdot p_3^{n_3} \!\cdot \ldots \cdot p_k^{n_k} + 1$ with *k* fixed primes *p*<sub>1</sub> \< *p*<sub>2</sub> \< *p*<sub>3</sub> \< ... \< *p*<sub>*k*</sub>. A **generalized Pierpont prime of the second kind** is a prime of the form $p_1^{n_1} \!\cdot p_2^{n_2} \!\cdot p_3^{n_3} \!\cdot \ldots \cdot p_k^{n_k} - 1$ with *k* fixed primes *p*<sub>1</sub> \< *p*<sub>2</sub> \< *p*<sub>3</sub> \< ... \< *p*<sub>*k*</sub>. Since all primes greater than 2 are [odd](wikipedia://en/143135), in both kinds *p*<sub>1</sub> must be 2. The sequences of such primes in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OEIS) are:
