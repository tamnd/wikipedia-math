# Menger curvature

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Menger curvature"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Menger curvature** of a triple of points in *n*-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) **R**<sup>*n*</sup> is the [reciprocal](wikipedia://en/229940) of the [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) of the circle that passes through the three points. It is named after the [Austrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria)-[American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Karl Menger](wikipedia://en/149431).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let *x*, *y* and *z* be three points in **R**<sup>*n*</sup>; for simplicity, assume for the moment that all three points are distinct and do not lie on a single straight line. Let Π ⊆ **R**<sup>*n*</sup> be the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/19469852) spanned by *x*, *y* and *z* and let *C* ⊆ Π be the unique [Euclidean circle](wikipedia://en/6220) in Π that passes through *x*, *y* and *z* (the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/161262) of *x*, *y* and *z*). Let *R* be the radius of *C*. Then the **Menger curvature** *c*(*x*, *y*, *z*) of *x*, *y* and *z* is defined by

$$c (x, y, z) = \frac1{R}.$$

If the three points are [collinear](wikipedia://en/946975), *R* can be informally considered to be +∞, and it makes rigorous sense to define *c*(*x*, *y*, *z*) = 0. If any of the points *x*, *y* and *z* are coincident, again define *c*(*x*, *y*, *z*) = 0.

Using the well-known formula relating the side lengths of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) to its area, it follows that

$$c (x, y, z) = \frac1{R} = \frac{4 A}{|x - y ||y - z ||z - x |},$$

where *A* denotes the area of the triangle spanned by *x*, *y* and *z*.

Another way of computing Menger curvature is the identity

$$c(x,y,z)=\frac{2\sin \angle xyz}{|x-z|}$$

where $\angle xyz$ is the angle made at the *y*-corner of the triangle spanned by *x*,*y*,*z*.

Menger curvature may also be defined on a general [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018). If *X* is a metric space and *x*,*y*, and *z* are distinct points, let *f* be an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) from $\{x,y,z\}$ into $\mathbb{R}^{2}$. Define the Menger curvature of these points to be

$$c_{X} (x,y,z)=c(f(x),f(y),f(z)).$$

Note that *f* need not be defined on all of *X*, just on *{x,y,z}*, and the value *c*<sub>*X*</sub> *(x,y,z)* is independent of the choice of *f*.

:::

## Integral Curvature Rectifiability {#Integral_Curvature_Rectifiability}

Menger curvature can be used to give quantitative conditions for when sets in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ may be [rectifiable](wikipedia://en/2644238). For a [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322) $\mu$ on a Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ define

$$c^{p}(\mu)=\int\int\int c(x,y,z)^{p}d\mu(x)d\mu(y)d\mu(z).$$

- A Borel set $E\subseteq \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is rectifiable if $c^{2}(H^{1}|_{E})<\infty$, where $H^{1}|_{E}$ denotes one-dimensional [Hausdorff measure](wikipedia://en/504109) restricted to the set $E$.[^o5]

The basic intuition behind the result is that Menger curvature measures how straight a given triple of points are (the smaller $c(x,y,z)\max\{|x-y|,|y-z|,|z-y|\}$ is, the closer x,y, and z are to being collinear), and this integral quantity being finite is saying that the set E is flat on most small scales. In particular, if the power in the integral is larger, our set is smoother than just being rectifiable[^o6]

- Let $p>3$, $f:S^{1}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{n}$ be a homeomorphism and $\Gamma=f(S^{1})$. Then $f\in C^{1,1-\frac{3}{p}}(S^{1})$ if $c^{p}(H^{1}|_{\Gamma})<\infty$.
- If $0<H^{s}(E)<\infty$ where $0<s\leq\frac{1}{2}$, and $c^{2s}(H^{s}|_{E})<\infty$, then $E$ is rectifiable in the sense that there are countably many $C^{1}$ curves $\Gamma_{i}$ such that $H^{s}(E\backslash \bigcup\Gamma_{i})=0$. The result is not true for $\frac{1}{2}<s<1$, and $c^{2s}(H^{s}|_{E})=\infty$ for $1<s\leq n$.:[^o7]

In the opposite direction, there is a result of Peter Jones:[^o8]

- If $E\subseteq\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}$, $H^{1}(E)>0$, and $\Gamma$ is rectifiable. Then there is a positive Radon measure $\mu$ supported on $E$ satisfying $\mu B(x,r)\leq r$ for all $x\in E$ and $r>0$ such that $c^{2}(\mu)<\infty$ (in particular, this measure is the [Frostman measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frostman's_lemma) associated to E). Moreover, if $H^{1}(B(x,r)\cap\Gamma)\leq Cr$ for some constant *C* and all $x\in \Gamma$ and *r>0*, then $c^{2}(H^{1}|_{E})<\infty$. This last result follows from the [Analyst's Traveling Salesman Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analyst's_Traveling_Salesman_Theorem).
