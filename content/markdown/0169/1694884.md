# Planar algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Planar algebra"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **planar algebras** first appeared in the work of [Vaughan Jones](wikipedia://en/1227628) on the [standard invariant](wikipedia://en/2780098#Standard_invariant) of a [II<sub>1</sub> subfactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/II-1_subfactor).[^o2] They also provide an appropriate algebraic framework for many [knot invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_invariants) (in particular the [Jones polynomial](wikipedia://en/1107642)), and have been used in describing the properties of [Khovanov homology](wikipedia://en/2639906) with respect to [tangle](wikipedia://en/30858815) composition.[^o3][^o4] Any subfactor planar algebra provides a family of unitary representations of [Thompson groups](wikipedia://en/612245).[^o5] Any [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) (and quantum generalization) can be encoded as a planar algebra.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The idea of the planar algebra is to be a diagrammatic axiomatization of the [standard invariant](wikipedia://en/2780098#Standard_invariant).[^o2][^o8][^o9]

### Planar tangle {#Planar_tangle}

A (shaded) **planar tangle** is the data of finitely many *input* disks, one *output* disk, non-intersecting strings giving an even number, say $2n$, intervals per disk and one $\star$-marked interval per disk.

*Image: Tangle.png.*

Here, the mark is shown as a $\star$-shape. On each input disk it is placed between two adjacent outgoing strings, and on the output disk it is placed between two adjacent incoming strings. A planar tangle is defined up to [isotopy](wikipedia://en/212250#Isotopy).

### Composition {#Composition}

To *compose* two planar tangles, put the output disk of one into an input of the other, having as many intervals, same shading of marked intervals and such that the $\star$-marked intervals coincide. Finally we remove the coinciding circles. Note that two planar tangles can have zero, one or several possible compositions.

*Image: Composition.png.*

### Planar operad {#Planar_operad}

The **planar operad** is the set of all the planar tangles (up to isomorphism) with such compositions.

### Planar algebra {#Planar_algebra}

A **planar algebra** is a *representation* of the planar [operad](wikipedia://en/3126358); more precisely, it is a family of vector spaces $(\mathcal{P}_{n,\pm})_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$, called $n$-box spaces, on which *acts* the planar operad, i.e. for any tangle $T$ (with one output disk and $r$ input disks with $2n_0$ and $2n_1, \dots, 2n_r$ intervals respectively) there is a [multilinear map](wikipedia://en/221525)

$$Z_T : \mathcal{P}_{n_1,\epsilon_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{P}_{n_r,\epsilon_r} \to \mathcal{P}_{n_0,\epsilon_0}$$

with $\epsilon_i \in \{+,-\}$ according to the shading of the $\star$-marked intervals, and these maps (also called partition functions) respect the composition of tangle in such a way that all the diagrams as below commute.

*Image: PlanarDiagram.png.*

## Examples {#Examples}

### Planar tangles {#Planar_tangles}

The family of vector spaces $(\mathcal{T}_{n,\pm})_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ generated by the planar tangles having $2n$ intervals on their *output* disk and a white (or black) $\star$-marked interval, admits a planar algebra structure.

### Temperley–Lieb {#Temperley–Lieb}

The Temperley-Lieb planar algebra $\mathcal{TL}(\delta)$ is generated by the planar tangles without input disk; its $3$-box space $\mathcal{TL}_{3,+}(\delta)$ is generated by

*Image: TLJ3.png.*

Moreover, a closed string is replaced by a multiplication by $\delta$.

*Image: DeltaMultiplication.png.*

Note that the dimension of $\mathcal{TL}_{n,\pm}(\delta)$ is the [Catalan number](wikipedia://en/216488) $\frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}$. This planar algebra encodes the notion of [Temperley–Lieb algebra](wikipedia://en/2802848).

### Hopf algebra {#Hopf_algebra}

A semisimple and cosemisimple [Hopf algebra](wikipedia://en/325714) over an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) is encoded in a planar algebra defined by generators and relations, and "corresponds" (up to isomorphism) to a connected, irreducible, spherical, non degenerate planar algebra with non zero modulus $\delta$ and of depth two.[^o19]

Note that *connected* means $\dim(\mathcal{P}_{0,\pm}) = 1$ (as for *evaluable* below), *irreducible* means $\dim(\mathcal{P}_{1,+}) = 1$, *spherical* is defined below, and *non-degenerate* means that the traces (defined below) are non-degenerate.

:::

## Subfactor planar algebra {#Subfactor_planar_algebra}

::: {#o22 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

A **subfactor planar algebra** is a planar $\star$-algebra $(\mathcal{P}_{n,\pm})_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ which is:

(1) Finite-dimensional: $\dim (\mathcal{P}_{n,\pm}) < \infty$

(2) Evaluable: $\mathcal{P}_{0,\pm} = \mathbb{C}$

(3) Spherical: $tr:=tr_r = tr_l$

(4) Positive: $\langle a \vert b \rangle = tr(b^{\star}a)$ defines an inner product.

Note that by (2) and (3), any closed string (shaded or not) counts for the same constant $\delta$.

*Image: PlanarOperations.png.*

The tangle action deals with the adjoint by:

$$Z_T(a_1 \otimes a_2 \otimes \cdots \otimes a_r)^{\star} = Z_{T^{\star}}(a_1^{\star} \otimes a_2^{\star} \otimes \cdots \otimes a_r^{\star})$$

with $T^{\star}$ the mirror image of $T$ and $a_i^{\star}$ the adjoint of $a_i$ in $\mathcal{P}_{n_i,\epsilon_i}$.

:::

### Examples and results {#Examples_and_results}

*No-ghost theorem*: The planar algebra $\mathcal{TL}(\delta)$ has no ghost (i.e. element $a$ with $\langle a \vert a \rangle < 0$) if and only if

$$\delta \in \{ 2\cos(\pi/n) | n=3,4,5,... \} \cup [2, +\infty]$$

For $\delta$ as above, let $\mathcal{I}$ be the null ideal (generated by elements $a$ with $\langle a \vert a \rangle = 0$). Then the quotient $\mathcal{TL}(\delta)/\mathcal{I}$ is a subfactor planar algebra, called the **Temperley–Lieb-Jones subfactor planar algebra** $\mathcal{TLJ}(\delta)$. Any subfactor planar algebra with constant $\delta$ admits $\mathcal{TLJ}(\delta)$ as planar subalgebra.

A planar algebra $(\mathcal{P}_{n,\pm})$ is a subfactor planar algebra if and only if it is the [standard invariant](wikipedia://en/2780098#Standard_invariant) of an extremal [subfactor](wikipedia://en/2780098) $N \subseteq M$ of index $[M:N] = \delta^2$, with $\mathcal{P}_{n,+}= N' \cap M_{n-1}$ and $\mathcal{P}_{n,-}= M' \cap M_{n}$.[^o24][^o25][^o26] A finite depth or irreducible [subfactor](wikipedia://en/2780098) is extremal ($tr_{N'} = tr_{M}$ on $N' \cap M$).

There is a subfactor planar algebra encoding any finite group (and more generally, any finite dimensional [Hopf ${\rm C}^{\star}$-algebra](wikipedia://en/325714), called Kac algebra), defined by generators and relations. A (finite dimensional) Kac algebra "corresponds" (up to isomorphism) to an irreducible subfactor planar algebra of depth two.[^o27][^o28]

The subfactor planar algebra associated to an inclusion of finite groups,[^o29] does not always remember the (core-free) inclusion.[^o30][^o31]

A Bisch-Jones subfactor planar algebra $\mathcal{BJ}(\delta_1, \delta_2)$ (sometimes called Fuss-Catalan) is defined as for $\mathcal{TLJ}(\delta)$ but by allowing two colors of string with their own constant $\delta_1$ and $\delta_2$, with $\delta_i$ as above. It is a planar subalgebra of any subfactor planar algebra with an intermediate such that $[K:N] = \delta_1^2$ and $[M:K] = \delta_2^2$.[^o32][^o33]

The first finite depth subfactor planar algebra of index $\delta^2 > 4$ is called the [Haagerup](wikipedia://en/46705302) subfactor planar algebra.[^o34] It has index $(5+\sqrt{13})/2 \sim 4.303$.

The subfactor planar algebras are completely classified for index at most $5$[^o35] and a bit beyond.[^o36] This classification was initiated by [Uffe Haagerup](wikipedia://en/46705302).[^o37] It uses (among other things) a listing of possible principal graphs, together with the embedding theorem[^o38] and the jellyfish algorithm.[^o39]

A subfactor planar algebra remembers the subfactor (i.e. its standard invariant is complete) if it is amenable.[^o40] A finite depth hyperfinite subfactor is amenable.

About the non-amenable case: there are unclassifiably many irreducible hyperfinite subfactors of index 6 that all have the same standard invariant.[^o41]

### Fourier transform and biprojections {#Fourier_transform_and_biprojections}

Let $N \subset M$ be a finite index subfactor, and $\mathcal{P}$ the corresponding subfactor planar algebra. Assume that $\mathcal{P}$ is irreducible (i.e. $\mathcal{P}_{1,+} = N' \cap M_1 = \mathbb{C}$). Let $N \subset K \subset M$ be an intermediate subfactor. Let the Jones projection $e^M_K: L^2(M) \to L^2(K)$. Note that $e^M_K \in \mathcal{P}_{2,+}$. Let $id:=e^M_M$ and $e_1:=e^M_N$.

*Image: E1id.png.*

Note that $tr(e_1) = \delta^{-2} = [M:N]^{-1}$ and $tr(id) = 1$.

Let the bijective linear map $\mathcal{F}: \mathcal{P}_{2,\pm} \to \mathcal{P}_{2,\mp}$ be the **Fourier transform**, also called $1$-click (of the outer star) or $90^{\circ}$ rotation; and let $a * b$ be the **coproduct** of $a$ and $b$.

*Image: FourierCoproduct.png.*

Note that the word *coproduct* is a diminutive of *convolution product*. It is a [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948).

The coproduct satisfies the equality $a * b = \mathcal{F}(\mathcal{F}^{-1}(a) \mathcal{F}^{-1}(b)).$

For any positive operators $a,b$, the coproduct $a*b$ is also positive; this can be seen diagrammatically:[^o43]

*Image: CoproductPositive.png.*

Let $\overline{a} := \mathcal{F}(\mathcal{F}(a))$ be the **contragredient** $a$ (also called $180^{\circ}$ rotation). The map $\mathcal{F}^{4}$ corresponds to four $1$-clicks of the outer star, so it's the identity map, and then $\overline{\overline{a}} = a$.

In the Kac algebra case, the contragredient is exactly the antipode,[^o28] which, for a finite group, correspond to the inverse.

A **biprojection** is a projection $b \in \mathcal{P}_{2,+} \setminus \{ 0\}$ with $\mathcal{F}(b)$ a multiple of a projection. Note that $e_1=e^M_N$ and $id=e^M_M$ are biprojections; this can be seen as follows:

*Image: F(e1).png.*

A projection $b$ is a biprojection iff it is the Jones projection $e^M_K$ of an intermediate subfactor $N \subset K \subset M$,[^o44] iff $e_1 \le b=\overline{b} = \lambda b * b, \text{ with } \lambda^{-1} = \delta tr(b)$.[^o45][^o43]

**Galois correspondence**:[^o46] in the Kac algebra case, the biprojections are 1-1 with the left coideal subalgebras, which, for a finite group, correspond to the subgroups.

For any irreducible subfactor planar algebra, the set of biprojections is a finite lattice,[^o47] of the form $[e_1,id]$, as for an interval of finite groups $[H,G]$.

Using the biprojections, we can make the intermediate subfactor planar algebras.[^o48][^o49]

The [uncertainty principle](wikipedia://en/31883) extends to any irreducible subfactor planar algebra $\mathcal{P}$:

Let $\mathcal{S}(x) = Tr(R(x))$ with $R(x)$ the range projection of $x$ and $Tr$ the unnormalized trace (i.e. $Tr = \delta^n tr$ on $\mathcal{P}_{n,\pm}$).

**Noncommutative uncertainty principle**:[^o50] Let $x \in \mathcal{P}_{2,\pm}$, nonzero. Then

$$\mathcal{S}(x) \mathcal{S}(\mathcal{F}(x)) \ge \delta^{2}$$

Assuming $x$ and $\mathcal{F}(x)$ positive, the equality holds if and only if $x$ is a biprojection. More generally, the equality holds if and only if $x$ is the *bi-shift* of a biprojection.
