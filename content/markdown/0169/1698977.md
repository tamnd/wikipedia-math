# Homogeneous polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Homogeneous polynomial"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **homogeneous polynomial**, sometimes called quantic in older texts, is a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) whose nonzero terms all have the same [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652).[^o2] For example, $x^5 + 2 x^3 y^2 + 9 x y^4$ is a homogeneous polynomial of degree 5, in two variables; the sum of the exponents in each term is always 5. The polynomial $x^3 + 3 x^2 y + z^7$ is not homogeneous, because the sum of exponents does not match from term to term. The function defined by a homogeneous polynomial is always a [homogeneous function](wikipedia://en/622844).

:::

An **algebraic form**, or simply **form**, is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) defined by a homogeneous polynomial.[^o3] A **binary form** is a form in two variables. A *form* is also a function defined on a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370), which may be expressed as a homogeneous function of the coordinates over any [basis](wikipedia://en/18420).

A polynomial of degree 0 is always homogeneous; it is simply an element of the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) or [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) of the coefficients, usually called a constant or a scalar. A form of degree 1 is a [linear form](wikipedia://en/214137).[^o4] A form of degree 2 is a [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478). In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) is the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of a quadratic form.

Homogeneous polynomials are ubiquitous in mathematics and physics.[^o5] They play a fundamental role in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), as a [projective algebraic variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_algebraic_variety) is defined as the set of the common zeros of a set of homogeneous polynomials.

## Properties {#Properties}

A homogeneous polynomial defines a [homogeneous function](wikipedia://en/622844). This means that, if a [multivariate polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_polynomial) *P* is homogeneous of degree *d*, then

$$P(\lambda x_1, \ldots, \lambda x_n)=\lambda^d\,P(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\,,$$

for every $\lambda$ in any [field](wikipedia://en/10603) containing the [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) of *P*. Conversely, if the above relation is true for infinitely many $\lambda$ then the polynomial is homogeneous of degree *d*.

In particular, if *P* is homogeneous then

$$P(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad P(\lambda x_1, \ldots, \lambda x_n)=0,$$

for every $\lambda.$ This property is fundamental in the definition of a [projective variety](wikipedia://en/320469).

Any nonzero polynomial may be decomposed, in a unique way, as a sum of homogeneous polynomials of different degrees, which are called the **homogeneous components** of the polynomial.

Given a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $R=K[x_1, \ldots,x_n]$ over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) (or, more generally, a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404)) *K*, the homogeneous polynomials of degree *d* form a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) (or a [module](wikipedia://en/276410)), commonly denoted $R_d.$ The above unique decomposition means that $R$ is the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) of the $R_d$ (sum over all [nonnegative integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonnegative_integer)).

The dimension of the vector space (or [free module](wikipedia://en/326454)) $R_d$ is the number of different monomials of degree *d* in *n* variables (that is the maximal number of nonzero terms in a homogeneous polynomial of degree *d* in *n* variables). It is equal to the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668)

$$\binom{d+n-1}{n-1}=\binom{d+n-1}{d}=\frac{(d+n-1)!}{d!(n-1)!}.$$

Homogeneous polynomial satisfy [Euler's identity for homogeneous functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_homogeneous_function_theorem). That is, if $P$ is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $d$ in the indeterminates $x_1, \ldots, x_n,$ one has, whichever is the [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) of the coefficients,

$$dP=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_i},$$

where $\textstyle \frac{\partial P}{\partial x_i}$ denotes the [formal partial derivative](wikipedia://en/3832917) of $P$ with respect to $x_i.$

## Homogenization {#Homogenization}

A non-homogeneous polynomial *P*(*x*<sub>1</sub>,...,*x*<sub>*n*</sub>) can be homogenized by introducing an additional variable *x*<sub>0</sub> and defining the homogeneous polynomial sometimes denoted <sup>*h*</sup>*P*:[^o10]

$${^h\!P}(x_0,x_1,\dots, x_n) = x_0^d P \left (\frac{x_1}{x_0},\dots, \frac{x_n}{x_0} \right ),$$

where *d* is the [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) of *P*. For example, if

$$P(x_1,x_2,x_3)=x_3^3 + x_1 x_2+7,$$

then

$$^h\!P(x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3)=x_3^3 + x_0 x_1x_2 + 7 x_0^3.$$

A homogenized polynomial can be dehomogenized by setting the additional variable *x*<sub>0</sub> = 1. That is

$$P(x_1,\dots, x_n)={^h\!P}(1,x_1,\dots, x_n).$$
