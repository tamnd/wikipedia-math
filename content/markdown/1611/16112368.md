# Earth ellipsoid

*Image: Earth oblateness to scale.svg.* A scale diagram of the [oblateness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblateness) of the 2003 [IERS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IERS) [reference ellipsoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_ellipsoid). solid blue \#6594e0 solid red orange

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Earth ellipsoid"}

An **Earth ellipsoid** or **Earth spheroid** is a mathematical figure approximating the [Earth's shape and size](wikipedia://en/233668), used as part of a [reference frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference) for [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatial_reference_system) and computations in [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), and the [geosciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoscience). Various different [reference ellipsoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_ellipsoid) have been used as approximations.

:::

It is an [oblate spheroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblate_spheroid) (an ellipsoid of [revolution](wikipedia://en/329549)) whose [minor axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_axis) (polar diameter), connecting the [geographical poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographical_pole), is approximately aligned with the [Earth's axis of rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_axis_of_rotation). The ellipsoid is also defined by the major axis (*equatorial axis*); the difference between the two axes is slightly more than 21 km or 0.335%.

Many methods exist for determination of the axes of an Earth ellipsoid, ranging from [meridian arcs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_arc) up to modern [satellite geodesy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_geodesy) or the analysis and interconnection of continental [geodetic networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodetic_network). Amongst the different set of data used in [national surveys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_survey) are several of special importance: the [Bessel ellipsoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessel_ellipsoid) of 1841, the international [Hayford ellipsoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hayford_ellipsoid) of 1924, and (for [GPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPS) positioning) the [WGS84](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WGS84) ellipsoid.

## Types {#Types}

There are two types of ellipsoid: mean and reference.

A data set which describes the global [average](wikipedia://en/60167) of the Earth's surface curvature is called the *mean Earth ellipsoid*. It refers to a theoretical coherence between the [geographic latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_latitude) and the meridional curvature of the [geoid](wikipedia://en/176244). The latter is close to the [mean sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_sea_level), and therefore an ideal Earth ellipsoid has the same [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) as the geoid.

While the mean Earth ellipsoid is the ideal basis of global geodesy, for [regional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional) networks a so-called *reference ellipsoid* may be the better choice.[^o3] When geodetic measurements have to be computed on a mathematical reference surface, this surface should have a similar curvature as the regional geoid; otherwise, [reduction](wikipedia://en/1033865) of the measurements will get small distortions.

This is the reason for the "long life" of former reference ellipsoids like the [Hayford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hayford_ellipsoid) or the [Bessel ellipsoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessel_ellipsoid), despite the fact that their main axes deviate by several hundred meters from the modern values. Another reason is a judicial one: the [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate) of millions of boundary stones should remain fixed for a long period. If their reference surface changes, the coordinates themselves also change.

However, for international networks, [GPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPS) positioning, or [astronautics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronautics), these regional reasons are less relevant. As knowledge of [the Earth's figure](wikipedia://en/233668) is increasingly accurate, the International Geoscientific Union [IUGG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IUGG) usually adapts the axes of the Earth ellipsoid to the best available data.

### Reference ellipsoid {#Reference_ellipsoid}

*Image: OblateSpheroid.PNG.* Flattened sphere

In [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608), a **reference ellipsoid** is a mathematically defined surface that approximates the [geoid](wikipedia://en/176244), which is the truer, imperfect [figure of the Earth](wikipedia://en/233668), or other planetary body, as opposed to a perfect, smooth, and unaltered sphere, which factors in the undulations of the bodies' [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) due to variations in the composition and density of the [interior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_core), as well as the subsequent [flattening](wikipedia://en/2043875) caused by the [centrifugal force](wikipedia://en/19265670) from the rotation of these massive objects (for planetary bodies that do rotate). Because of their relative simplicity, reference ellipsoids are used as a preferred surface on which [geodetic network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodetic_network) computations are performed and point coordinates such as [latitude](wikipedia://en/17616), [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude), and [elevation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevation) are defined.

In the context of standardization and geographic applications, a *geodesic reference ellipsoid* is the mathematical model used as foundation by [spatial reference system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatial_reference_system) or [geodetic datum](wikipedia://en/995417) definitions.

:::

## Ellipsoid parameters {#Ellipsoid_parameters}

In geophysics, [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608), and related areas, the word 'ellipsoid' is understood to mean 'oblate ellipsoid of revolution', and the older term 'oblate spheroid' is hardly used.[^o7][^o8] For bodies that cannot be well approximated by an ellipsoid of revolution a [triaxial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triaxial_ellipsoid) (or scalene) ellipsoid is used.

The shape of an ellipsoid of revolution is determined by the shape parameters of that [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277). The [semi-major axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-major_axis) of the ellipse, $a$, becomes the equatorial radius of the ellipsoid: the [semi-minor axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-minor_axis) of the ellipse, $b$, becomes the distance from the centre to either pole. These two lengths completely specify the shape of the ellipsoid.

In geodesy publications, however, it is common to specify the semi-major axis (equatorial radius) $a$ and the [flattening](wikipedia://en/2043875) $f$, defined as:

$$f=\frac{a-b}{a}.$$

That is, $f$ is the amount of flattening at each pole, relative to the radius at the equator. This is often expressed as a fraction 1/$m$; $m = 1/f$ then being the "inverse flattening". A great many other [ellipse parameters](wikipedia://en/9277) are used in [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608) but they can all be related to one or two of the set $a$, $b$ and $f$.

A great many ellipsoids have been used to model the Earth in the past, with different assumed values of $a$ and $b$ as well as different assumed positions of the center and different axis orientations relative to the solid Earth. Starting in the late twentieth century, improved measurements of satellite orbits and star positions have provided extremely accurate determinations of the Earth's center of mass and of its axis of revolution; and those parameters have been adopted also for all modern reference ellipsoids.

The ellipsoid [WGS-84](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Geodetic_System), widely used for mapping and [satellite navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPS) has $f$ close to 1/300 (more precisely, 1/298.257223563, by definition), corresponding to a difference of the major and minor semi-axes of approximately 21 km (more precisely, 21.3846857548205 km). For comparison, Earth's [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon) is even less elliptical, with a flattening of less than 1/825, while [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter) is visibly oblate at about 1/15 and one of [Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn)'s triaxial moons, [Telesto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telesto_%28moon%29), is highly flattened, with $f$ between 1/3 and 1/2 (meaning that the polar diameter is between 50% and 67% of the equatorial.

## Determination {#Determination}

[Arc measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_measurement) is the historical method of determining the ellipsoid. Two meridian arc measurements will allow the derivation of two parameters required to specify a reference ellipsoid. For example, if the measurements were hypothetically performed exactly over the equator plane and either geographical pole, the radii of curvature so obtained would be related to the equatorial radius and the polar radius, respectively *a* and *b* (see: [Earth polar and equatorial radius of curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_radius#Polar_and_equatorial_radius_of_curvature)). Then, the [flattening](wikipedia://en/2043875) would readily follow from its definition:

$$f=(a-b)/a.$$

For two arc measurements each at arbitrary average latitudes $\varphi_i$, $i=1,\,2$, the solution starts from an initial approximation for the equatorial radius $a_0$ and for the flattening $f_0$. The theoretical [Earth's meridional radius of curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_meridional_radius_of_curvature) $M_0(\varphi_i)$ can be calculated at the latitude of each arc measurement as:

$$M_0(\varphi_i)=\frac{a (1-e^2)}{(1-e_0^2\sin^2\varphi_i)^\frac{3}{2}}$$

where $e_0^2 = 2f_0-f_0^2$.[^o10] Then discrepancies between empirical and theoretical values of the radius of curvature can be formed as $\delta M_i=M_i-M_0(\varphi_i)$. Finally, corrections for the initial equatorial radius $\delta a$ and the flattening $\delta f$ can be solved by means of a [system of linear equations](wikipedia://en/113087) formulated via [linearization](wikipedia://en/1118789) of $M$:[^o11]

$$\delta M_i \approx \delta a (\partial M / \partial a) + \delta f (\partial M / \partial f)$$

where the partial derivatives are:[^o11]

$$\partial M / \partial a \approx 1$$

$$\partial M / \partial f \approx -2 a_0 (1-1.5 \sin^2\varphi_i)$$

Longer arcs with multiple intermediate-latitude determinations can completely determine the ellipsoid that best fits the surveyed region. In practice, multiple arc measurements are used to determine the ellipsoid parameters by the method of [least squares adjustment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_squares_adjustment). The parameters determined are usually the semi-major axis, $a$, and any of the semi-minor axis, $b$, [flattening](wikipedia://en/2043875), or eccentricity.

Regional-scale [systematic effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematic_effect) observed in the radius of curvature measurements reflect the [geoid undulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoid_undulation) and the [deflection of the vertical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflection_of_the_vertical), as explored in [astrogeodetic leveling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrogeodetic_leveling).

[Gravimetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravimetry) is another technique for determining Earth's flattening, as per [Clairaut's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clairaut's_theorem_%28gravity%29).

Modern [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608) no longer uses simple meridian arcs or ground triangulation networks, but the methods of [satellite geodesy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_geodesy), especially [satellite gravimetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_gravimetry).

## History {#History}

In 1687 [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) published the [Principia](wikipedia://en/48781) in which he included a proof that a rotating self-gravitating fluid body in equilibrium takes the form of a flattened ("oblate") [ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/145381) of revolution, generated by an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) rotated around its minor diameter; a shape which he termed an [oblate spheroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblate_spheroid).[^o14][^o15]

In 1669, [Jean Picard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Picard) found the first accurate and reliable value for the [radius of Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_radius) as 6,365.6 [kilometres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilometre).[^o16][^o17] Picard's geodetic observations had been confined to the determination of the magnitude of the Earth considered as a sphere, but the discovery made by [Jean Richer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Richer) turned the attention of mathematicians to the Earth's deviation from a spherical form.[^o18][^o19] [Christiaan Huygens](wikipedia://en/42127) found out the [centrifugal force](wikipedia://en/19265670) which explained variations of gravitational acceleration depending on [latitude](wikipedia://en/17616).[^o20]

In 1743, [Alexis Clairaut](wikipedia://en/196273) proposed a theorem which suggested that the study of variations in [gravitational acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_acceleration) was a way to determine the [figure of the Earth](wikipedia://en/233668), whose crucial parameter was the [flattening](wikipedia://en/2043875) of the Earth ellipsoid.[^o20][^o17] Towards the end of the 18th century, the geodesists sought to reconcile the values of flattening drawn from the measurements of [meridian arcs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_arc) with that given by [Clairaut's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clairaut's_theorem_%28gravity%29) drawn from the measurement of [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity).[^o17][^o21] The Weights and Measures Commission would, in 1799, adopt a flattening of $\tfrac{1}{334}$ based on analysis by [Pierre-Simon Laplace](wikipedia://en/344783) who combined the [arc of Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Geodesic_Mission_to_the_Equator) and the data of the [meridian arc of Delambre and Méchain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_measurement_of_Delambre_and_Méchain).[^o22][^o22][^o23]

### Historical Earth ellipsoids {#Historical_Earth_ellipsoids}

*Image: WGS84\_mean\_Earth\_radius.svg.* Equatorial ($a$), polar ($b$) and mean Earth radii as defined in the 1984 [World Geodetic System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Geodetic_System) revision (not to scale)

The reference ellipsoid models listed below have had utility in geodetic work and many are still in use. The older ellipsoids are named for the individual who derived them and the year of development is given. In 1887 the English surveyor Colonel [Alexander Ross Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Ross_Clarke) CB FRS RE was awarded the Gold Medal of the Royal Society for his work in determining the figure of the Earth. The international ellipsoid was developed by [John Fillmore Hayford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Fillmore_Hayford) in 1910 and adopted by the International Union of Geodesy and Geophysics (IUGG) in 1924, which recommended it for international use.

At the 1967 meeting of the IUGG held in Lucerne, Switzerland, the ellipsoid called GRS-67 ([Geodetic Reference System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodetic_system) 1967) in the listing was recommended for adoption. The new ellipsoid was not recommended to replace the International Ellipsoid (1924), but was advocated for use where a greater degree of accuracy is required. It became a part of the GRS-67 which was approved and adopted at the 1971 meeting of the IUGG held in Moscow. It is used in Australia for the Australian Geodetic Datum and in the [South American Datum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_American_Datum) 1969.

The GRS-80 (Geodetic Reference System 1980) as approved and adopted by the IUGG at its Canberra, Australia meeting of 1979 is based on the equatorial radius (semi-major axis of Earth ellipsoid) $a$, total mass $GM$, dynamic form factor $J_2$ and angular velocity of rotation $\omega$, making the inverse flattening $1/f$ a derived quantity. The minute difference in $1/f$ seen between GRS-80 and WGS-84 results from an unintentional truncation in the latter's defining constants: while the WGS-84 was designed to adhere closely to the GRS-80, incidentally the WGS-84 derived flattening turned out to differ slightly from the GRS-80 flattening because the normalized second degree zonal harmonic gravitational coefficient, that was derived from the GRS-80 value for $J_2$, was truncated to eight significant digits in the normalization process.[^o25]

An ellipsoidal model describes only the ellipsoid's geometry and a [normal gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_gravity) field formula to go with it. Commonly an ellipsoidal model is part of a more encompassing [geodetic datum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Datum_%28geodesy%29). For example, the older ED-50 ([European Datum 1950](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ED50)) is based on the Hayford or [International Ellipsoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Ellipsoid). WGS-84 is peculiar in that the same name is used for both the complete geodetic reference system and its component ellipsoidal model. Nevertheless, the two concepts—ellipsoidal model and geodetic reference system—remain distinct.

Note that the same ellipsoid may be known by different names. It is best to mention the defining constants for unambiguous identification.
