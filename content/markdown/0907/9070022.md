# Flexible polyhedron

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Flexible polyhedron"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **flexible polyhedron** is a [polyhedral surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedral_surface) without any boundary edges, whose shape can be continuously changed while keeping the shapes of all of its faces unchanged. The [Cauchy rigidity theorem](wikipedia://en/8317687) shows that in dimension 3 such a polyhedron cannot be [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) (this is also true in higher dimensions).

:::

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The first examples of flexible polyhedra were discovered by (txt), which are called [Bricard octahedra](wikipedia://en/18605515). They are self-intersecting surfaces [isometric](wikipedia://en/254777) to a [regular octahedron](wikipedia://en/22458). The first example of a flexible non-self-intersecting surface in three-dimensional Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^3$, the **Connelly sphere**, was discovered by (txt).

[Steffen's polyhedron](wikipedia://en/53375446) is another non-self-intersecting flexible polyhedron with nine vertices derived from Bricard's octahedra.[^n1] It is claimed as the smallest number of vertices among any other flexible polyhedra, although there is another polyhedron with eight vertices by combining two Bricard octahedra to form a self-crossing flexible [pentagonal bipyramid](wikipedia://en/728038).[^n2]

[Kaleidocycle](wikipedia://en/41738353) or (flextangle) is another example of a flexible polyhedron, constructed by connecting six [tetragonal disphenoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetragonal_disphenoid) on opposite edges into a cycle. This polyhedron can twist continuously around an axis at the ring.[^n3]

:::

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Bellows conjecture"}

## Bellows conjecture {#Bellows_conjecture}

In the late 1970s Connelly and [D. Sullivan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D._Sullivan) formulated the **bellows conjecture** stating that the [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) of a flexible polyhedron is [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under flexing. This conjecture was proved for polyhedra [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) by (txt, 223–224) using [elimination theory](wikipedia://en/245978), and then proved for general [orientable](wikipedia://en/187446) 2-dimensional polyhedral surfaces by (txt, 1–10).[^n4][^n5] The proof extends [Piero della Francesca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_della_Francesca)'s formula for the [volume of a tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606#Volume) to a formula for the volume of any polyhedron. The extended formula shows that the volume must be a root of a polynomial whose coefficients depend only on the lengths of the polyhedron's edges. Since the edge lengths cannot change as the polyhedron flexes, the volume must remain at one of the finitely many roots of the polynomial, rather than changing continuously.[^n6]

:::

::: {#o17 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Strong bellows conjecture"}

## Scissor congruence {#Scissor_congruence}

Connelly conjectured that the [Dehn invariant](wikipedia://en/338051) of a flexible polyhedron is invariant under flexing. This was known as the **strong bellows conjecture** or (after it was proven in 2018) the **strong bellows theorem**.[^n7] Because all configurations of a flexible polyhedron have both the same volume and the same Dehn invariant, they are [scissors congruent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scissors_congruence) to each other, meaning that for any two of these configurations it is possible to [dissect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissection_puzzle) one of them into polyhedral pieces that can be reassembled to form the other. The total [mean curvature](wikipedia://en/684210) of a flexible polyhedron, defined as the sum of the products of edge lengths with exterior dihedral angles, is a function of the Dehn invariant that is also known to stay constant while a polyhedron flexes.[^n8]

:::

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Flexible [4-polytopes](wikipedia://en/24979) in 4-dimensional Euclidean space and 3-dimensional [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/241291) were studied by (txt).[^n9] In dimensions $n\geq 5$, flexible polytopes were constructed by (Gaifullin 2014).[^n10]
