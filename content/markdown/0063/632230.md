# Colin McGinn

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Colin McGinn"}

**Colin McGinn** (born 10 March 1950) is a British philosopher. He has held teaching posts and professorships at [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London), the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford), [Rutgers University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutgers_University), and the [University of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Miami).[^o10]

:::

McGinn is best known for his work in [philosophy of mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_mind), and in particular for what is known as [new mysterianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_mysterianism), the idea that the [human mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_mind) is not equipped to solve the [problem of consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind-body_problem). He has written over 20 books on this and other areas of philosophy, including *The Character of Mind* (1982), *The Problem of Consciousness* (1991), *Consciousness and Its Objects* (2004), and *The Meaning of Disgust* (2011).[^o10]

In 2013, McGinn resigned from his [tenured position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenured_position) at the University of Miami after a graduate student accused him of [sexual harassment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_harassment). His resignation touched off a debate about the prevalence of [sexism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexism) and sexual harassment within academic philosophy.[^o3]

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

McGinn was born in [West Hartlepool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Hartlepool), a town in County Durham, England. Several of his relatives, including both grandfathers, were miners. His father, Joseph, left school to become a miner but put himself through night school and became a building manager instead. McGinn was the eldest of three children, all boys. When he was three, the family moved to [Gillingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gillingham,_Kent), Kent, and eight years later to [Blackpool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackpool), [Lancashire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancashire). Having failed his [11-plus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleven_plus_exam), he attended a [technical school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_Technical_School) in Kent, then a [secondary modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_modern_school) in Blackpool, but did well enough in his [O-levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GCE_Ordinary_Level) to transfer to the local [grammar school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammar_school) for his [A-levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GCE_Advanced_Level).[^o5]

In 1968, McGinn began a degree in [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology) at the [University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Manchester), obtaining a first-class honours degree in 1971 and an MA in 1972, also in psychology.[^o10] In 1972, he was admitted to [Jesus College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_College,_Oxford), initially to study for a [Bachelor of Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Letters) postgraduate degree. He switched to the [Bachelor of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Philosophy) (BPhil) postgraduate programme on the recommendation of his advisor, [Michael R. Ayers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_R._Ayers). In 1973, McGinn received the university's John Locke Prize in Mental Philosophy; one of the examiners was [A. J. Ayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._J._Ayer).[^o6] He received his BPhil in 1974, writing a thesis under the supervision of Ayers and [P. F. Strawson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._F._Strawson) on the semantics of [Donald Davidson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Davidson_%28philosopher%29).[^o7]

## Teaching career {#Teaching_career}

### Posts {#Posts}

McGinn taught at University College London for 11 years, first as a lecturer in philosophy (1974–84), then as [reader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reader_%28academic_rank%29) (1984–85). In 1985, he succeeded [Gareth Evans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gareth_Evans_%28philosopher%29) as [Wilde Reader in Mental Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilde_Professor_of_Mental_Philosophy) at the University of Oxford, a position he held until 1990. He held visiting professorships at the [University of California, Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles) (1979), [Bielefeld University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bielefeld_University) (1982), [University of Southern California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Southern_California) (1983), [Rutgers University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutgers_University) (1984), [University of Helsinki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Helsinki) (1986), [City University of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_University_of_New_York) (1988) and [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) (1992). In 1990, he joined the philosophy department at Rutgers as a full professor, working alongside [Jerry Fodor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_Fodor).[^o10]

In 2006, he joined the [University of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Miami) as Professor of Philosophy and Cooper Fellow.[^o10]

### Sexual harassment complaint {#Sexual_harassment_complaint}

McGinn resigned his position at the University of Miami in January 2013, effective at the end of the calendar year, after a graduate student complained that he had been sexually harassing her, including by text and email. These documents have since been released and include explicit references to McGinn's desire to have sex with the student.[^o12] He denied any wrongdoing.[^o13]

Represented by [Ann Olivarius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ann_Olivarius), the student complained in April 2014 to the [Equal Employment Opportunity Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_Employment_Opportunity_Commission) that the university had mishandled the case.[^o14] She filed a lawsuit in October 2015 against the university, McGinn, and Edward Erwin, another philosophy professor at the [University of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Miami). The complaint accused McGinn of sexual harassment, civil assault, and defamation, and Erwin of defamation. It alleged that the university had violated [Title IX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_IX) of the [Education Amendments of 1972](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_Amendments_of_1972) (which requires that women have equal access to education) by failing to investigate the student's complaint adequately or protect her from retaliation, including from McGinn on his blog before his resignation came into effect.[^o15][^o16] McGinn's lawyer, Andrew Berman, said that McGinn denied the claim.[^o15] The lawsuit was settled in October 2016. All parties are prohibited from disclosing the terms of the settlement.[^o17]

The incident triggered a debate about the extent to which sexism remains prevalent in academia, particularly in academic philosophy, and the effect on students and teachers of harassment and harassment-related complaints.[^o18]

In 2014, [East Carolina University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Carolina_University) offered McGinn a visiting professorship but university administrators later rescinded the offer. McGinn blamed the sexual-harassment allegations for East Carolina's decision.[^o19]

In 2024, McGinn wrote: "I have existed in a state of professional cancellation for over ten years now. Before that I had normal access to teaching positions, publishers, conferences, professional contacts, and so on. Not anymore."[^o20]

In 2025, McGinn wrote that his resignation 12 years earlier was not an admission of guilt, but that he had had no desire to stay at the University of Miami. "I judged it wiser to resign and go elsewhere, which I fully expected to do. What I didn't expect was the complete stupidity and ill-will of my colleagues in the American philosophy profession", he wrote.[^o21]

## Writing {#Writing}

### Philosophy of mind {#Philosophy_of_mind}

McGinn has written extensively on [philosophical logic](wikipedia://en/304939), [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics), and [philosophy of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_language), but is best known for his work in [philosophy of mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_mind). He is known in particular for the development of the idea that human minds are incapable of solving the [problem of consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind-body_problem), a position known as [new mysterianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_mysterianism). In addition to his academic publications on [consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consciousness), including *The Character of Mind* (1982), *The Problem of Consciousness* (1991) and *Consciousness and Its Objects* (2004), he has written a popular introduction, *The Mysterious Flame: Conscious Minds in a Material World* (1999).[^o24]

[Owen Flanagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owen_Flanagan) introduced the term "new mysterians" in 1991 (named after the band [Question Mark & the Mysterians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Question_Mark_&_the_Mysterians)) to describe McGinn's position and that of [Thomas Nagel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Nagel), first described in Nagel's "[What Is It Like to Be a Bat?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_Is_It_Like_to_Be_a_Bat?)" (1974).[^o25] McGinn introduced his position in "Can We Solve the Mind-Body Problem?" (*Mind*, 1989), and in *The Problem of Consciousness* (1991), arguing that the human mind is incapable of comprehending itself entirely.[^o27] [Mark Rowlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Rowlands) writes that the 1989 article was largely responsible for reviving the debate about [phenomenal consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenal_consciousness), or the nature of experience.[^o26] McGinn argued in the paper for the idea of [cognitive closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_closure_%28philosophy%29):

A type of mind M is cognitively closed with respect to a property P (or theory T), if and only if the concept-forming procedures at M's disposal cannot extend to a grasp of P (or an understanding of T). Conceiving minds come in different kinds, equipped with varying powers and limitations, biases and blindspots, so that properties (or theories) may be accessible to some minds but not to others. What is closed to the mind of a rat may be open to the mind of a monkey, and what is open to us may be closed to the monkey. ... But such closure does not reflect adversely on the reality of the properties that lie outside the representational capacities in question; a property is no less real for not being reachable from a certain kind of perceiving and conceiving mind.[^o27]

Although human beings might grasp the concept of consciousness, McGinn argues that we cannot understand its causal basis: neither direct examination of consciousness nor of the brain can identify the properties that cause or provide the mechanism for consciousness, or how "technicolour phenomenology \[can\] arise from soggy grey matter."[^o28] Thus, his answer to the [hard problem of consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_problem_of_consciousness) is that the answer is inaccessible to us.[^o29]

New, or [epistemological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemological), mysterianism is contrasted with the old, or [ontological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontological), form, namely that consciousness is inherently mysterious or supernatural. The new mysterians are not [Cartesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesianism) [dualists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dualism_%28philosophy_of_mind%29).[^o25] The argument holds that human minds cannot understand consciousness, not that there is anything [supernatural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural) about it.[^o30] The [mind-body problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind-body_problem) is simply "the perimeter of our conceptual anatomy making itself felt."[^o31] McGinn describes this as existential [naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysical_naturalism).[^o32]

### Animal rights {#Animal_rights}

McGinn is a supporter of [animal rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_rights), calling our treatment of non-humans "deeply and systematically immoral".[^o34] His position is that we make the mistake of seeing the non-human only in relation to the human, because of "species solipsism": the farmer sees animals as food, the pet owner as companions for humans, the activist as victims of humans, the evolutionary biologist as "gene survival machines". But "their *esse* is not human *percipi*" – "The rhino looks at us with the same skewed solipsism we bring to him", McGinn writes, "and surely we do not want to be as limited in our outlook as he is." He argues that "we need to improve our *manners*" toward animals by recognizing that they have their own lives, and that those lives ought to be respected.[^o35]

### Novels and articles {#Novels_and_articles}

McGinn has regularly contributed reviews and short stories to the *[London Review of Books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Review_of_Books)* and *[The New York Review of Books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Review_of_Books)*,[^o37][^o38] and has written occasionally for *[Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_%28journal%29)*, *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times)*, *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)*, *[The Wall Street Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wall_Street_Journal)*, *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times)* and *[The Times Literary Supplement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times_Literary_Supplement)*. He has also written two novels, *The Space Trap* (1992) and *Bad Patches* (2012).[^o10]

## Radio and television {#Radio_and_television}

In 1984, McGinn discussed [John Searle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Searle)'s Reith lectures on BBC Radio Three with Searle, [Richard Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Gregory) and [Colin Blakemore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Blakemore). The next year, he and Sir [Andrew Huxley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Huxley) debated animal rights with [Bernard Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Williams) as the moderator.[^o10] He was interviewed for [Jonathan Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Miller)'s 2003 documentary miniseries *[Atheism: A Rough History of Disbelief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism:_A_Rough_History_of_Disbelief)*, later broadcast as *[The Atheism Tapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Atheism_Tapes)* (2004),[^o40] and published an article titled "Why I am an Atheist".[^o41] He has also appeared in 11 episodes of *[Closer to Truth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closer_to_Truth)* hosted by [Robert Lawrence Kuhn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Lawrence_Kuhn), discussing consciousness, personal identity, free will, and materialism.[^o42]
