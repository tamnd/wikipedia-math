# Paley–Wiener theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Paley–Wiener theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Paley–Wiener theorem** is a theorem that relates decay properties of a function or [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) at infinity with [analyticity](wikipedia://en/61478) of its [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247). It is named after [Raymond Paley](wikipedia://en/2788568) (1907–1933) and [Norbert Wiener](wikipedia://en/63185) (1894–1964) who, in 1934, introduced various versions of the theorem.[^n1] The original theorems did not use the language of [distributions](wikipedia://en/1580246), and instead applied to [square-integrable functions](wikipedia://en/45194). The first such theorem using distributions was due to [Laurent Schwartz](wikipedia://en/317485). These theorems heavily rely on the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941) (to interchange the absolute value and integration).

:::

The original work by Paley and Wiener is also used as a namesake in the fields of [control theory](wikipedia://en/7039) and [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147); introducing the **[Paley–Wiener condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jensen%27s_formula#Applications)** for [spectral factorization](wikipedia://en/54112321) and the **[Paley–Wiener criterion](wikipedia://en/4795751#Paley-Wiener_criterion)** for [non-harmonic Fourier series](wikipedia://en/4504771#Non-harmonic_Fourier_series) respectively.[^n2] These are related mathematical concepts that place the decay properties of a function in context of [stability problems](wikipedia://en/5493795).

## Holomorphic Fourier transforms {#Holomorphic_Fourier_transforms}

The classical Paley–Wiener theorems make use of the [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110) Fourier transform on classes of [square-integrable functions](wikipedia://en/28680558) supported on the real line. Formally, the idea is to take the integral defining the (inverse) Fourier transform

$$f(\zeta) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty F(x)e^{i x \zeta}\,dx$$

and allow $\zeta$ to be a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) in the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826). One may then expect to differentiate under the integral in order to verify that the [Cauchy–Riemann equations](wikipedia://en/7583) hold, and thus that $f$ defines an analytic function. However, this integral may not be well-defined, even for $F$ in $L^2(\mathbb{R})$; indeed, since $\zeta$ is in the upper half plane, the modulus of $e^{ix\zeta}$ grows exponentially as $x \to -\infty$; so [differentiation under the integral sign](wikipedia://en/2558855) is out of the question. One must impose further restrictions on $F$ in order to ensure that this integral is well-defined.

The first such restriction is that $F$ be supported on $\mathbb{R}_+$: that is, $F\in L^2(\mathbb{R}_+)$. The Paley–Wiener theorem now asserts the following:[^o5] The holomorphic Fourier transform of $F$, defined by

$$f(\zeta) = \int_0^\infty F(x) e^{i x\zeta}\, dx$$

for $\zeta$ in the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826) is a holomorphic function. Moreover, by [Plancherel's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plancherel's_theorem), one has

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \left |f(\xi+i\eta) \right|^2\, d\xi \le \int_0^\infty |F(x)|^2\, dx$$

and by [dominated convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominated_convergence),

$$\lim_{\eta\to 0^+}\int_{-\infty}^\infty \left|f(\xi+i\eta)-f(\xi) \right|^2\,d\xi = 0.$$

Conversely, if $f$ is a holomorphic function in the upper half-plane satisfying

$$\sup_{\eta>0} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left |f(\xi+i\eta) \right|^2\,d\xi = C < \infty$$

then there exists $F\in L^2(\mathbb{R}_+)$ such that $f$ is the holomorphic Fourier transform of $F$.

In abstract terms, this version of the theorem explicitly describes the [Hardy space](wikipedia://en/314780) [$H^2(\mathbb{R})$](wikipedia://en/20041584). The theorem states that

$$\mathcal{F}H^2(\mathbb{R})=L^2(\mathbb{R_+}).$$

This is a very useful result as it enables one to pass to the Fourier transform of a function in the Hardy space and perform calculations in the easily understood space $L^2(\mathbb{R}_+)$ of square-integrable functions supported on the positive axis.

By imposing the alternative restriction that $F$ be [compactly supported](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support), one obtains another Paley–Wiener theorem.[^o6] Suppose that $F$ is supported in $[-A,A]$, so that $F\in L^2(-A,A)$. Then the holomorphic Fourier transform

$$f(\zeta) = \int_{-A}^A F(x)e^{i x\zeta}\,dx$$

is an [entire function](wikipedia://en/10372) of [exponential type](wikipedia://en/4081509) $A$, meaning that there is a constant $C$ such that

$$|f(\zeta)|\le Ce^{A|\zeta|},$$

and moreover, $f$ is square-integrable over horizontal lines:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |f(\xi+i\eta)|^2\,d\xi < \infty.$$

Conversely, any entire function of exponential type $A$ which is square-integrable over horizontal lines is the holomorphic Fourier transform of an $L^2$ function supported in $[-A,A]$.

## Schwartz's Paley–Wiener theorem {#Schwartz's_Paley–Wiener_theorem}

Schwartz's Paley–Wiener theorem asserts that the Fourier transform of a [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) of [compact support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support) on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is an [entire function](wikipedia://en/10372) on $\mathbb{C}^n$ and gives estimates on its growth at infinity. It was proven by [Laurent Schwartz](wikipedia://en/317485) (1952). The formulation presented here is from (Hörmander 1976).

Generally, the Fourier transform can be defined for any [tempered distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29#Tempered_distributions_and_Fourier_transform); moreover, any distribution of compact support $v$ is a tempered distribution. If $v$ is a distribution of compact support and $f$ is an infinitely differentiable function, the expression

$$v(f) = v(x\mapsto f(x))$$

is well defined.

It can be shown that the Fourier transform of $v$ is a function (as opposed to a general tempered distribution) given at the value $s$ by

$$\hat{v}(s) = (2 \pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}} v\left(x\mapsto e^{-i \langle x, s\rangle}\right)$$

and that this function can be extended to values of $s$ in the complex space $\mathbb{C}^n$. This extension of the Fourier transform to the complex domain is called the [Fourier–Laplace transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier–Laplace_transform).

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Schwartz's theorem"}

**Schwartz's theorem.**

An entire function $F$ on $\mathbb{C}^n$ is the Fourier–Laplace transform of a distribution $v$ of compact support if and only if for all $z\in\mathbb{C}^n$,

$$|F(z)| \leq C (1 + |z|)^{N} e^{B|\text{Im}(z)|}$$

for some positive constants $C$, $N$, $B$. The distribution $v$ in fact will be supported in the closed ball of center $0$ and radius $B$.

:::

Additional growth conditions on the entire function $F$ impose regularity properties on the distribution $v$. For instance:[^o11]

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

If for every positive $N$ there is a constant $C_N$ such that for all $z\in\mathbb{C}^n$,

$$|F(z)| \leq C_N (1 + |z|)^{-N} e^{B|\mathrm{Im}(z)|}$$

then $v$ is an infinitely differentiable function, and vice versa.

:::

Sharper results giving good control over the [singular support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_support) of $v$ have been formulated by (Hörmander 1990). In particular,[^o14] let $K$ be a convex [compact set](wikipedia://en/6042) in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with supporting function $H$, defined by

$$H(x) = \sup_{y\in K} \langle x,y\rangle.$$

Then the singular support of $v$ is contained in $K$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) there is a constant $N$ and sequence of constants $C_m$ such that

$$|\hat{v}(\zeta)| \le C_m(1+|\zeta|)^Ne^{H(\mathrm{Im}(\zeta))}$$

for $|\mathrm{Im}(\zeta)| \le m \log(| \zeta |+1).$

:::
