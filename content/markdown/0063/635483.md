# Gromov's theorem on groups of polynomial growth

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gromov's theorem on groups of polynomial growth"}

In [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164), **Gromov's theorem on groups of polynomial growth**, first proved by [Mikhail Gromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gromov_%28mathematician%29),[^o2] characterizes finitely generated [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) of *polynomial* growth, as those groups which have [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569) subgroups of finite [index](wikipedia://en/230581).

:::

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

The [growth rate](wikipedia://en/498727) of a group is a [well-defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-defined) notion from [asymptotic analysis](wikipedia://en/641995). To say that a finitely generated group has **polynomial growth** means the number of elements of [length](wikipedia://en/18542) at most *n* (relative to a symmetric generating set) is bounded above by a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) function *p*(*n*). The *order of growth* is then the least degree of any such polynomial function *p*.

A [nilpotent group](wikipedia://en/144569) *G* is a group with a [lower central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_central_series) terminating in the identity subgroup.

Gromov's theorem states that a finitely generated group has polynomial growth if and only if it has a nilpotent subgroup that is of finite index.

:::

## Growth rates of nilpotent groups {#Growth_rates_of_nilpotent_groups}

There is a vast literature on growth rates, leading up to Gromov's theorem. An earlier result of [Joseph A. Wolf](wikipedia://en/41199481)[^o6] showed that if *G* is a finitely generated nilpotent group, then the group has polynomial growth. [Yves Guivarc'h](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yves_Guivarc'h)[^o7] and independently [Hyman Bass](wikipedia://en/7085910)[^o8] (with different proofs) computed the exact order of polynomial growth. Let *G* be a finitely generated nilpotent group with lower central series

$$G = G_1 \supseteq G_2 \supseteq \cdots.$$

In particular, the quotient group *G*<sub>*k*</sub>/*G*<sub>*k*+1</sub> is a finitely generated abelian group.

The **Bass–Guivarc'h formula** states that the order of polynomial growth of *G* is

$$d(G) = \sum_{k \geq 1} k \operatorname{rank}(G_k/G_{k+1})$$

where:

*rank* denotes the [rank of an abelian group](wikipedia://en/244541), i.e. the largest number of independent and torsion-free elements of the abelian group.

In particular, Gromov's theorem and the Bass–Guivarc'h formula imply that the order of polynomial growth of a finitely generated group is always either an integer or infinity (excluding for example, fractional powers).

Another nice application of Gromov's theorem and the Bass–Guivarch formula is to the [quasi-isometric rigidity](wikipedia://en/955164#Modern_themes_and_development) of finitely generated abelian groups: any group which is [quasi-isometric](wikipedia://en/12747259) to a finitely generated abelian group contains a free abelian group of finite index.

::: {#o10 .proof cue=heading name="Proofs of Gromov's theorem"}

## Proofs of Gromov's theorem {#Proofs_of_Gromov's_theorem}

In order to prove this theorem Gromov introduced a convergence for metric spaces. This convergence, now called the [Gromov&ndash;Hausdorff convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gromov&ndash;Hausdorff_convergence), is currently widely used in geometry.

A relatively simple proof of the theorem was found by [Bruce Kleiner](wikipedia://en/8488020).[^o11] Later, [Terence Tao](wikipedia://en/1118498) and [Yehuda Shalom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yehuda_Shalom) modified Kleiner's proof to make an essentially elementary proof as well as a version of the theorem with explicit bounds.[^o12][^o13] Gromov's theorem also follows from the classification of [approximate groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approximate_group) obtained by Breuillard, Green and Tao. A simple and concise proof based on [functional analytic methods](wikipedia://en/11180) is given by [Ozawa](wikipedia://en/43440581).[^o14]

:::

::: {#o16 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="The gap conjecture"}

## The gap conjecture {#The_gap_conjecture}

Beyond Gromov's theorem one can ask whether there exists a gap in the growth spectrum for finitely generated group just above polynomial growth, separating virtually nilpotent groups from others. Formally, this means that there would exist a function $f: \mathbb N \to \mathbb N$ such that a finitely generated group is virtually nilpotent if and only if its growth function is an $O(f(n))$. Such a theorem was obtained by Shalom and Tao, with an explicit function $n^{\log\log(n)^c}$ for some $c > 0$. All known groups with intermediate growth (i.e. both superpolynomial and subexponential) are essentially generalizations of [Grigorchuk's group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grigorchuk's_group), and have faster growth functions; so all known groups have growth faster than $e^{n^{\alpha-o(1)}}$, with $\alpha = \log(2)/\log(2/\eta ) \approx 0.767$, where $\eta$ is the real root of the polynomial $x^3+x^2+x-2$.[^o17]

It is conjectured that the true lower bound on growth rates of groups with intermediate growth is $e^{\sqrt n}$. This is known as the *Gap conjecture*.[^o18]

:::
