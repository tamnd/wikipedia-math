# Khufu and Khafre

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Khufu and Khafre"}

In [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), **Khufu** and **Khafre** are two [block ciphers](wikipedia://en/4594) designed by [Ralph Merkle](wikipedia://en/25849) in 1989 while working at [Xerox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox)'s [Palo Alto Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palo_Alto_Research_Center). Along with [Snefru](wikipedia://en/450613), a [cryptographic hash function](wikipedia://en/439526), the ciphers were named after the Egyptian [Pharaohs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharaoh) [Khufu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khufu), [Khafre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khafre) and [Sneferu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sneferu).

:::

Under a voluntary scheme, Xerox submitted Khufu and Khafre to the US [National Security Agency](wikipedia://en/21939) (NSA) prior to publication. NSA requested that Xerox not publish the algorithms, citing concerns about national security. Xerox, a large contractor to the US government, complied. However, a reviewer of the paper passed a copy to [John Gilmore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gilmore_%28activist%29), who made it available via the [sci.crypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sci.crypt) [newsgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newsgroup).[^o2][^o3] It would appear this was against Merkle's wishes.[^o4] The scheme was subsequently published at the 1990 [CRYPTO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRYPTO) conference (Merkle, 1990).

Khufu and Khafre were patented by Xerox; the patent was issued on March 26, 1991.[^o5]

## Khufu {#Khufu}

Khufu is a [64-bit block](wikipedia://en/294065) cipher which, unusually, uses [keys](wikipedia://en/53039) of [size](wikipedia://en/5749) 512 bits; block ciphers typically have much smaller keys, rarely exceeding 256 bits. Most of the key material is used to construct the cipher's [S-boxes](wikipedia://en/240991). Because the key-setup time is quite time consuming, Khufu is not well suited to situations in which many small messages are handled. It is better suited to bulk encryption of large amounts of data.

Khufu is a [Feistel cipher](wikipedia://en/352709) with 16 rounds by default (other multiples of eight between 8 and 64 are allowed). Each set of eight rounds is termed an *octet*; a different S-box is used in each octet. In a round, the least significant byte of half of the block is passed into the 8×32-bit S-box. The S-box output is then combined (using [XOR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XOR)) with the other 32-bit half. The left half is rotated to bring a new byte into position, and the halves are swapped. At the start and end of the algorithm, extra key material is XORed with the block ([key whitening](wikipedia://en/2990178)). Other than this, all the key is contained in the S-boxes.

There is a [differential attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_attack) on 16 rounds of Khufu which can recover the secret key. It requires 2<sup>43</sup> [chosen plaintexts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chosen_plaintext) and has a 2<sup>43</sup> time complexity (Gilbert and Chauvaud, 1994). 2<sup>32</sup> plaintexts and complexity are required merely to distinguish the cipher from random. A [boomerang attack](wikipedia://en/2137332) (Wagner, 1999) can be used in an adaptive chosen plaintext / chosen ciphertext scenario with 2<sup>18</sup> queries and a similar time complexity. Khufu is also susceptible to an [impossible differential attack](wikipedia://en/3329225), which can break up to 18 rounds of the cipher (Biham *et al.*, 1999).

[Schneier](wikipedia://en/36732) and Kelsey (1996) categorise Khafre and Khufu as "even incomplete heterogeneous target-heavy [Unbalanced Feistel Networks](wikipedia://en/352709#Unbalanced_Feistel_cipher)".

## Khafre {#Khafre}

Khafre is similar to Khufu, but uses a standard set of S-boxes, and does not compute them from the key. (Rather, they are generated from the [RAND tables](wikipedia://en/1354812), used as a source of "[nothing up my sleeve numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nothing_up_my_sleeve_number)".) An advantage is that Khafre can encrypt a small amount of data very rapidly — it has good *key agility*. However, Khafre probably requires a greater number of rounds to achieve a similar [level of security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Level_of_security) as Khufu, making it slower at bulk encryption. Khafre uses a key whose size is a multiple of 64 bits. Because the S-boxes are not key-dependent, Khafre XORs subkeys every eight rounds.

[Differential cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/8536) is effective against Khafre: 16 rounds can be broken using either 1500 chosen plaintexts or 2<sup>38</sup> [known plaintexts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Known_plaintext). Similarly, 24 rounds can be attacked using 2<sup>53</sup> chosen plaintexts or 2<sup>59</sup> known plaintexts.
