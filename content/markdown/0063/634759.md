# Hausdorff paradox

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hausdorff paradox"}

The **Hausdorff paradox** is a paradox in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) named after [Felix Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/10989). It involves the [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) ${ S^2}$ (the surface of a 3-dimensional ball in [${ \mathbb{R}^3 }$](wikipedia://en/3054853)). It states that if a certain [countable](wikipedia://en/6026) subset is removed from ${ S^2 }$, then the remainder can be divided into three disjoint subsets ${ A,B }$ and ${ C }$ such that ${ A, B, C }$ and ${ B \cup C }$ are all [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330). In particular, it follows that on $S^2$ there is no [finitely additive measure](wikipedia://en/19873#Generalizations) defined on all subsets such that the measure of congruent sets is equal (because this would imply that the measure of ${ B \cup C }$ is simultaneously $1/3$, $1/2$, and $2/3$ of the non-zero measure of the whole sphere).

:::

The paradox was published in *[Mathematische Annalen](wikipedia://en/1423381)* in 1914 and also in Hausdorff's book, *[Grundzüge der Mengenlehre](wikipedia://en/2561202)*, the same year. The proof of the much more famous [Banach–Tarski paradox](wikipedia://en/19759220) uses Hausdorff's ideas. The proof of this paradox relies on the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840). This paradox shows that there is no finitely additive measure on a sphere defined on *all* subsets which is equal on congruent pieces. (Hausdorff first showed in the same paper the easier result that there is no *countably* additive measure defined on all subsets.) The structure of the [group of rotations on the sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SO%283%29) plays a crucial role here – the statement is not true on the plane or the line. In fact, as was later shown by [Banach](wikipedia://en/29646),[^o2] it is possible to define an "area" for *all* bounded subsets in the Euclidean plane (as well as "length" on the real line) in such a way that congruent sets will have equal "area". (This [Banach measure](wikipedia://en/1339598), however, is only finitely additive, so it is not a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) in the full sense, but it equals the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on sets for which the latter exists.) This implies that if two open subsets of the plane (or the real line) are [equi-decomposable](wikipedia://en/19759220) then they have equal area.
