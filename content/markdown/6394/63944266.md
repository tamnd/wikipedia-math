# Differentiable vector-valued functions from Euclidean space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Differentiable vector-valued functions from Euclidean space"}

In the mathematical discipline of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), a **differentiable vector-valued function from Euclidean space** is a [differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable) function valued in a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVS) whose [domains](wikipedia://en/50263) is a subset of some [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). It is possible to generalize the notion of [derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative_%28mathematics%29) to functions whose domain and codomain are subsets of arbitrary [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVSs) in multiple ways. But when the domain of a TVS-valued function is a subset of a finite-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) then many of these notions become [logically equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logically_equivalent) resulting in a much more limited number of generalizations of the derivative and additionally, differentiability is also more [well-behaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-behaved) compared to the general case. This article presents the theory of $k$-times continuously differentiable functions on an open subset $\Omega$ of Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$ ($1 \leq n < \infty$), which is an important special case of [differentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiation_%28mathematics%29) between arbitrary TVSs. This importance stems partially from the fact that every finite-dimensional vector subspace of a Hausdorff topological vector space is [TVS isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TVS_isomorphism) to Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$ so that, for example, this special case can be applied to any function whose domain is an arbitrary Hausdorff TVS by [restricting it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restriction_of_a_function) to finite-dimensional vector subspaces.

:::

All vector spaces will be assumed to be over the field $\mathbb{F},$ where $\mathbb{F}$ is either the [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) $\mathbb{R}$ or the [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) $\mathbb{C}.$

## Continuously differentiable vector-valued functions {#Continuously_differentiable_vector-valued_functions}

A map $f,$ which may also be denoted by $f^{(0)},$ between two [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) is said to be **$0$-times continuously differentiable** or **$C^0$** if it is continuous. A [topological embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_embedding) may also be called a **$C^0$-embedding**.

### Curves {#Curves}

Differentiable curves are an important special case of differentiable vector-valued (i.e. TVS-valued) functions which, in particular, are used in the definition of the [Gateaux derivative](wikipedia://en/2253139). They are fundamental to the analysis of maps between two arbitrary [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) $X \to Y$ and so also to the analysis of TVS-valued maps from [Euclidean spaces](wikipedia://en/9697), which is the focus of this article.

A continuous map $f : I \to X$ from a subset $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ that is valued in a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) $X$ is said to be (**once** or **$1$-time**) **differentiable** if for all $t \in I,$ it is **differentiable at $t,$** which by definition means the following [limit in $X$](wikipedia://en/285759#Functions_on_topological_spaces) exists:

$$f^{\prime}(t) := f^{(1)}(t) := \lim_{\stackrel{r \to t}{t \neq r \in I}} \frac{f(r) - f(t)}{r - t} = \lim_{\stackrel{h \to 0}{t \neq t + h \in I}} \frac{f(t + h) - f(t)}{h}$$

where in order for this limit to even be well-defined, $t$ must be an [accumulation point](wikipedia://en/226505) of $I.$ If $f : I \to X$ is differentiable then it is said to be **continuously differentiable** or **$C^1$** if its **derivative**, which is the induced map $f^{\prime} = f^{(1)} : I \to X,$ is continuous. Using induction on $1 < k \in \mathbb{N},$ the map $f : I \to X$ is **$k$-times continuously differentiable** or **$C^k$** if its $k-1^{\text{th}}$ derivative $f^{(k-1)} : I \to X$ is continuously differentiable, in which case the **$k^{\text{th}}$-derivative of $f$** is the map $f^{(k)} := \left(f^{(k-1)}\right)^{\prime} : I \to X.$ It is called **smooth**, $C^\infty,$ or **infinitely differentiable** if it is $k$-times continuously differentiable for every integer $k \in \mathbb{N}.$ For $k \in \mathbb{N},$ it is called **$k$-times differentiable** if it is $k-1$-times continuous differentiable and $f^{(k-1)} : I \to X$ is differentiable.

A continuous function $f : I \to X$ from a non-empty and non-degenerate interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ into a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$ is called a **curve** or a **$C^0$ curve** in $X.$ A **[path](wikipedia://en/1189553)** in $X$ is a curve in $X$ whose domain is compact while an **[arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_%28topology%29)** or **$C$<sup>0</sup>-arc** in $X$ is a path in $X$ that is also a [topological embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_embedding). For any $k \in \{ 1, 2, \ldots, \infty \},$ a curve $f : I \to X$ valued in a topological vector space $X$ is called a **$C^k$-embedding** if it is a [topological embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_embedding) and a $C^k$ curve such that $f^{\prime}(t) \neq 0$ for every $t \in I,$ where it is called a **$C^k$-arc** if it is also a path (or equivalently, also a $C^0$-arc) in addition to being a $C^k$-embedding.

### Differentiability on Euclidean space {#Differentiability_on_Euclidean_space}

The definition given above for curves are now extended from functions valued defined on subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ to functions defined on open subsets of finite-dimensional [Euclidean spaces](wikipedia://en/9697).

Throughout, let $\Omega$ be an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n,$ where $n \geq 1$ is an integer. Suppose $t = \left( t_1, \ldots, t_n \right) \in \Omega$ and $f : \operatorname{domain} f \to Y$ is a function such that $t \in \operatorname{domain} f$ with $t$ an accumulation point of $\operatorname{domain} f.$ Then $f$ is **differentiable at $t$**[^n1] if there exist $n$ vectors $e_1, \ldots, e_n$ in $Y,$ called the **partial derivatives of $f$ at $t$**, such that

$$\lim_{\stackrel{p \to t}{t \neq p \in \operatorname{domain} f}} \frac{f(p) - f(t) - \sum_{i=1}^n \left(p_i - t_i \right) e_i}{\|p - t\|_2} = 0 \text{ in } Y$$

where $p = \left(p_1, \ldots, p_n\right).$ If $f$ is differentiable at a point then it is continuous at that point.[^n1] If $f$ is differentiable at every point in some subset $S$ of its domain then $f$ is said to be (**once** or **$1$-time**) **differentiable in $S$**, where if the subset $S$ is not mentioned then this means that it is differentiable at every point in its domain. If $f$ is differentiable and if each of its partial derivatives is a continuous function then $f$ is said to be (**once** or **$1$-time**) **continuously differentiable** or **$C^1.$**[^n1] For $k \in \mathbb{N},$ having defined what it means for a function $f$ to be $C^k$ (or $k$ times continuously differentiable), say that $f$ is **$k + 1$ times continuously differentiable** or that **$f$ is $C^{k+1}$** if $f$ is continuously differentiable and each of its partial derivatives is $C^k.$ Say that $f$ is $C^{\infty},$ **smooth**, $C^\infty,$ or **infinitely differentiable** if $f$ is $C^k$ for all $k = 0, 1, \ldots.$ The **[support](wikipedia://en/381013)** of a function $f$ is the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) (taken in its domain $\operatorname{domain} f$) of the set $\{ x \in \operatorname{domain} f : f(x) \neq 0 \}.$

## Spaces of *C*<sup>*k*</sup> vector-valued functions {#Spaces_of_Ck_vector-valued_functions}

In this section, the [space of smooth test functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_of_smooth_test_functions) and its canonical LF-topology are generalized to functions valued in general [complete](wikipedia://en/53695856) Hausdorff locally convex [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVSs). After this task is completed, it is revealed that the topological vector space $C^k(\Omega;Y)$ that was constructed could (up to TVS-isomorphism) have instead been defined simply as the completed [injective tensor product](wikipedia://en/63566619) $C^k(\Omega) \widehat{\otimes}_{\epsilon} Y$ of the usual [space of smooth test functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_of_smooth_test_functions) $C^k(\Omega)$ with $Y.$

Throughout, let $Y$ be a Hausdorff [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVS), let $k \in \{ 0, 1, \ldots, \infty \},$ and let $\Omega$ be either:

1. an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n,$ where $n \geq 1$ is an integer, or else
1. a [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) topological space, in which case $k$ can only be $0.$

### Space of *C*<sup>*k*</sup> functions {#Space_of_Ck_functions}

For any $k = 0, 1, \ldots, \infty,$ let $C^k(\Omega;Y)$ denote the vector space of all $C^k$ $Y$-valued maps defined on $\Omega$ and let $C_c^k(\Omega;Y)$ denote the vector subspace of $C^k(\Omega;Y)$ consisting of all maps in $C^k(\Omega;Y)$ that have compact support. Let $C^k(\Omega)$ denote $C^k(\Omega;\mathbb{F})$ and $C_c^k(\Omega)$ denote $C_c^k(\Omega; \mathbb{F}).$ Give $C_c^k(\Omega;Y)$ the topology of uniform convergence of the functions together with their derivatives of order $< k + 1$ on the compact subsets of $\Omega.$[^n1] Suppose $\Omega_1 \subseteq \Omega_2 \subseteq \cdots$ is a sequence of [relatively compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_compact) open subsets of $\Omega$ whose union is $\Omega$ and that satisfy $\overline{\Omega_i} \subseteq \Omega_{i+1}$ for all $i.$ Suppose that $\left(V_\alpha\right)_{\alpha \in A}$ is a basis of neighborhoods of the origin in $Y.$ Then for any integer $\ell < k + 1,$ the sets:

$$\mathcal{U}_{i, \ell, \alpha} := \left\{ f \in C^k(\Omega;Y) : \left(\partial / \partial p\right)^q f (p) \in U_\alpha \text{ for all } p \in \Omega_i \text{ and all } q \in \mathbb{N}^n, | q | \leq \ell \right\}$$

form a basis of neighborhoods of the origin for $C^k(\Omega;Y)$ as $i,$ $\ell,$ and $\alpha \in A$ vary in all possible ways. If $\Omega$ is a countable union of compact subsets and $Y$ is a [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801), then so is $C^(\Omega;Y).$ Note that $\mathcal{U}_{i, l, \alpha}$ is convex whenever $U_{\alpha}$ is convex. If $Y$ is [metrizable](wikipedia://en/64278078) (resp. [complete](wikipedia://en/53695856), [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844), [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637)) then so is $C^k(\Omega;Y).$[^n1][^n2] If $(p_\alpha)_{\alpha \in A}$ is a basis of continuous seminorms for $Y$ then a basis of continuous seminorms on $C^k(\Omega;Y)$ is:

$$\mu_{i, l, \alpha}(f) := \sup_{y \in \Omega_i} \left(\sum_{| q | \leq l} p_\alpha\left(\left(\partial / \partial p\right)^q f (p)\right)\right)$$

as $i,$ $\ell,$ and $\alpha \in A$ vary in all possible ways.[^n1]

### Space of *C*<sup>*k*</sup> functions with support in a compact subset {#Space_of_Ck_functions_with_support_in_a_compact_subset}

The definition of the topology of the [space of test functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_of_test_functions) is now duplicated and generalized. For any compact subset $K \subseteq \Omega,$ denote the set of all $f$ in $C^k(\Omega;Y)$ whose support lies in $K$ (in particular, if $f \in C^k(K;Y)$ then the domain of $f$ is $\Omega$ rather than $K$) and give it the subspace topology induced by $C^k(\Omega;Y).$[^n1] If $K$ is a compact space and $Y$ is a Banach space, then $C^0(K;Y)$ becomes a Banach space normed by $\| f \| := \sup_{\omega \in \Omega} \| f(\omega) \|.$[^n2] Let $C^k(K)$ denote $C^k(K;\mathbb{F}).$ For any two compact subsets $K \subseteq L \subseteq \Omega,$ the inclusion

$$\operatorname{In}_{K}^{L} : C^k(K;Y) \to C^k(L;Y)$$

is an embedding of TVSs and that the union of all $C^k(K;Y),$ as $K$ varies over the compact subsets of $\Omega,$ is $C_c^k(\Omega;Y).$

### Space of compactly support *C*<sup>*k*</sup> functions {#Space_of_compactly_support_Ck_functions}

For any compact subset $K \subseteq \Omega,$ let

$$\operatorname{In}_K : C^k(K;Y) \to C_c^k(\Omega;Y)$$

denote the inclusion map and endow $C_c^k(\Omega;Y)$ with the strongest topology making all $\operatorname{In}_K$ continuous, which is known as the [final topology](wikipedia://en/1819736) induced by these map. The spaces $C^k(K;Y)$ and maps $\operatorname{In}_{K_1}^{K_2}$ form a [direct system](wikipedia://en/211566) (directed by the compact subsets of $\Omega$) whose limit in the category of TVSs is $C_c^k(\Omega;Y)$ together with the injections $\operatorname{In}_{K}.$[^n1] The spaces $C^k\left(\overline{\Omega_i}; Y\right)$ and maps $\operatorname{In}_{\overline{\Omega_i}}^{\overline{\Omega_j}}$ also form a [direct system](wikipedia://en/211566) (directed by the total order $\mathbb{N}$) whose limit in the category of TVSs is $C_c^k(\Omega;Y)$ together with the injections $\operatorname{In}_{\overline{\Omega_i}}.$[^n1] Each embedding $\operatorname{In}_K$ is an embedding of TVSs. A subset $S$ of $C_c^k(\Omega;Y)$ is a neighborhood of the origin in $C_c^k(\Omega;Y)$ if and only if $S \cap C^k(K;Y)$ is a neighborhood of the origin in $C^k(K;Y)$ for every compact $K \subseteq \Omega.$ This direct limit topology (i.e. the final topology) on $C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ is known as the **canonical LF topology**.

If $Y$ is a Hausdorff locally convex space, $T$ is a TVS, and $u : C_c^k(\Omega;Y) \to T$ is a linear map, then $u$ is continuous if and only if for all compact $K \subseteq \Omega,$ the restriction of $u$ to $C^k(K;Y)$ is continuous.[^n1] The statement remains true if "all compact $K \subseteq \Omega$" is replaced with "all $K := \overline{\Omega}_i$".

### Properties {#Properties}

::: {#o24 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem[^n1].**

Let $m$ be a positive integer and let $\Delta$ be an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^m.$ Given $\phi \in C^k(\Omega \times \Delta),$ for any $y \in \Delta$ let $\phi_y : \Omega \to \mathbb{F}$ be defined by $\phi_y(x) = \phi(x, y)$ and let $I_k(\phi) : \Delta \to C^k(\Omega)$ be defined by $I_k(\phi)(y) := \phi_y.$ Then

$$I_\infty : C^\infty(\Omega \times \Delta) \to C^\infty(\Delta; C^\infty(\Omega))$$

is a surjective isomorphism of TVSs. Furthermore, its restriction

$$I_{\infty}\big\vert_{C_c^{\infty}\left(\Omega \times \Delta\right)} : C_c^\infty(\Omega \times \Delta) \to C_c^\infty\left(\Delta; C_c^\infty(\Omega)\right)$$

is an isomorphism of TVSs (where $C_c^\infty\left(\Omega \times \Delta\right)$ has its canonical LF topology).

:::

::: {#o26 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem[^n3].**

Let $Y$ be a Hausdorff [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) and for every continuous linear form $y^{\prime} \in Y$ and every $f \in C^\infty(\Omega;Y),$ let $J_{y^{\prime}}(f) : \Omega \to \mathbb{F}$ be defined by $J_{y^{\prime}}(f)(p) = y^{\prime}(f(p)).$ Then

$$J_{y^{\prime}} : C^\infty(\Omega;Y) \to C^\infty(\Omega)$$

is a continuous linear map; and furthermore, its restriction

$$J_{y^{\prime}}\big\vert_{C_c^\infty(\Omega;Y)} : C_c^\infty(\Omega;Y) \to C^\infty(\Omega)$$

is also continuous (where $C_c^\infty(\Omega;Y)$ has the canonical LF topology).

:::

### Identification as a tensor product {#Identification_as_a_tensor_product}

Suppose henceforth that $Y$ is Hausdorff. Given a function $f \in C^k(\Omega)$ and a vector $y \in Y,$ let $f \otimes y$ denote the map $f \otimes y : \Omega \to Y$ defined by $(f \otimes y)(p) = f(p) y.$ This defines a bilinear map $\otimes : C^k(\Omega) \times Y \to C^k(\Omega;Y)$ into the space of functions whose image is contained in a finite-dimensional vector subspace of $Y;$ this bilinear map turns this subspace into a tensor product of $C^k(\Omega)$ and $Y,$ which we will denote by $C^k(\Omega) \otimes Y.$[^n1] Furthermore, if $C_c^k(\Omega) \otimes Y$ denotes the vector subspace of $C^k(\Omega) \otimes Y$ consisting of all functions with compact support, then $C_c^k(\Omega) \otimes Y$ is a tensor product of $C_c^k(\Omega)$ and $Y.$[^n1]

If $X$ is locally compact then $C_c^{0}(\Omega) \otimes Y$ is dense in $C^0(\Omega;X)$ while if $X$ is an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ then $C_c^{\infty}(\Omega) \otimes Y$ is dense in $C^k(\Omega;X).$[^n2]

::: {#o31 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

If $Y$ is a complete Hausdorff locally convex space, then $C^k(\Omega;Y)$ is canonically isomorphic to the [injective tensor product](wikipedia://en/63566619) $C^k(\Omega) \widehat{\otimes}_{\epsilon} Y.$[^n4]

:::
