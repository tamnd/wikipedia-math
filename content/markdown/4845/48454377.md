# Frobenius's theorem (group theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Frobenius's theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), **Frobenius's theorem** states that if $n$ [divides](wikipedia://en/49492) the [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) of a [finite](wikipedia://en/323707) [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$, then the number of solutions of $x^n=1$ in $G$ is a multiple of $n$. It was introduced by (txt).

:::

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

A more general version of Frobenius's theorem states that if $C$ is a [conjugacy class](wikipedia://en/49176) with $h$ elements of a finite group $G$ with $g$ elements and $n$ is a positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563), then the number of elements $k$ such that $k^n$ is in $C$ is a multiple of the [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) $(hn,g)$ (Hall 1959, theorem 9.1.1).

:::

::: {#o7 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

One application of Frobenius's theorem is to show that the coefficients of the [Artin–Hasse exponential](wikipedia://en/5753249) are $p$-integral, by interpreting them in terms of the number of elements of order a power of $p$ in the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_n$.

:::

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Frobenius's conjecture"}

## Frobenius's conjecture {#Frobenius's_conjecture}

Frobenius [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) that if, in addition, the number of solutions to $x^n=1$ is exactly $n$, where $n$ divides the order of $G$, then these solutions form a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918). This was [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) by Iiyori and Yamaki[^o10] as a consequence of the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445).

The symmetric group $S_3$ has exactly $4$ solutions to $x^4=1$ but these do not form a normal subgroup; this is not a [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) to the conjecture as $4$ does not divide $|S_3| = 6$.

:::
