# André-Michel Guerry

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="André-Michel Guerry"}

**André-Michel Guerry** (; December 24, 1802 – April 9, 1866) was a French lawyer and amateur statistician. Together with [Adolphe Quetelet](wikipedia://en/184215) he may be regarded as the founder of [moral statistics](wikipedia://en/32446932) which led to the development of [criminology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminology), [sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology) and ultimately, modern [social science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science).

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Guerry was born in [Tours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tours), [Indre-et-Loire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indre-et-Loire), the only child of Michel Guerry, a building contractor, whose family had a long history as innkeepers, merchants, farmers and trades-people. About 1817-1820 he studied at the Imperial College of Tours (now the Lyceum Descartes, founded in 1807) and subsequently studied law at the [University of Poitiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Poitiers).

About 18241825 he moved to Paris and was admitted to the bar as a royal advocate. Shortly after, he was employed by the Ministry of Justice. Guerry worked with the data on crime statistics in France collected as part of the *General office for administration of criminal justice* in France, the first centralized national system of crime reporting. Guerry was so fascinated with these data, and the possibility to discover empirical regularities and laws that might govern them, that he gave up the active practice of law to devote the rest of his life to study crime and its relation to other moral variables.

## Moral statistics and criminology {#Moral_statistics_and_criminology}

Guerry's first work on what would come to be called *[moral statistics](wikipedia://en/32446932)* was a large, one page sheet containing three shaded maps of France, prepared together with the Venetian geographer, [Adriano Balbi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adriano_Balbi) in 1829. These showed the departments of France, shaded according to crimes against persons, crimes against property, and school instruction. Such statistical maps, now called [choropleth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choropleth) maps had just been invented in 1826 by Baron [Charles Dupin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dupin).

Guerry is best known for his *Essay on moral statistics of France,* presented to the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences) on July 2, 1832, and published in 1833 after it was awarded the Prix Montyon in statistics. His presentation, in tables and [thematic maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thematic_map), showed that rates of crime and suicide remained remarkably stable over time, when broken down by age, sex, region of France and even season of the year. Yet, these numbers also varied systematically across departments of France. This regularity of *social numbers* created the possibility to conceive that human actions could be described by social laws, just as inanimate actions were governed by physical laws.

Throughout his career, Guerry was particularly interested in uncovering the relation between social and moral variables. How are personal crime and property related to each other, and to suicide, donations to the poor, illegitimate births, wealth, and so forth? How do different types of crimes vary with age of the accused? Statistical methods ([correlation](wikipedia://en/157057) and [regression](wikipedia://en/826997)) were still in their infancy, so Guerry relied on graphic comparisons of maps and semi-graphic tables. ### Suicide {#Suicide}

In addition to a map of France showing rates of suicide by department, Guerry collected all the [suicide notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suicide_note) found by the police in Paris over a four-year period. He classified these by the apparent motive expressed for taking one's life, perhaps the first [content analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content_analysis) in the social sciences.

### Ordonnateur statistique {#Ordonnateur_statistique}

Around 1851, Guerry invented the [Ordonnateur Statistique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordonnateur_Statistique), probably the first mechanical device designed to aid in statistical calculations and the assessment of relationships among moral variables.[^o8] This device is now known to have been used for sorting one target variable (e.g., crimes of different types) in relation to other possibly explanatory variables (e.g., [population density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_density)).

### Polar area diagram {#Polar_area_diagram}

In 1829, Guerry invented a new graphic form, the [polar area diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_area_diagram) to show variations in weather and other phenomena over calendar cycles. This variation of the [pie chart](wikipedia://en/728487) uses equi-angular sectors of differing radii, in contrast to the pie chart that uses sectors with varying angle, but equal radii. The polar area diagram is often mistakenly credited to [Florence Nightingale](wikipedia://en/100127), who used it to great effect to illustrate needless mortality in the [Crimean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_War) due to unsanitary medical conditions. Nightingale most probably got the idea from [William Farr](wikipedia://en/253197), who in turn was acquainted with Guerry's work.

### Other activities {#Other_activities}

Guerry also resided in [Beaumont-sur-Dême](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaumont-sur-Dême), where he was mayor of the village from 1846 to 1855.
