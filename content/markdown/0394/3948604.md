# Hyperbolic equilibrium point

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hyperbolic equilibrium point"}

In the study of [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087), a **hyperbolic equilibrium point** or **hyperbolic fixed point** is a [fixed point](wikipedia://en/449738) that does not have any [center manifolds](wikipedia://en/3948656). Near a [hyperbolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function) point the orbits of a two-dimensional, [non-dissipative](wikipedia://en/198319) system resemble hyperbolas. This fails to hold in general. [Strogatz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Strogatz) notes that "hyperbolic is an unfortunate name—it sounds like it should mean '[saddle point](wikipedia://en/640249)'—but it has become standard."[^o2] Several properties hold about a neighborhood of a hyperbolic point, notably[^o3]

*Image: Phase Portrait Sadle.svg.* Orbits near a two-dimensional saddle point, an example of a hyperbolic equilibrium.

:::

- A [stable manifold](wikipedia://en/5466649) and an unstable manifold exist,
- [Shadowing](wikipedia://en/27431403) occurs,
- The dynamics on the invariant set can be represented via [symbolic dynamics](wikipedia://en/1986263),
- A natural [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) can be defined,
- The system is [structurally stable](wikipedia://en/2515807).

## Maps {#Maps}

If $T \colon \mathbb{R}^{n} \to \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is a *C*<sup>1</sup> map and *p* is a [fixed point](wikipedia://en/449738) then *p* is said to be a **hyperbolic fixed point** when the [Jacobian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix) $\operatorname{D} T (p)$ has no [eigenvalues](wikipedia://en/2161429) on the complex unit circle.

One example of a [map](wikipedia://en/516931) whose only fixed point is hyperbolic is [Arnold's cat map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold's_cat_map):

$$\begin{bmatrix} x_{n+1}\\ y_{n+1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_n\\ y_n\end{bmatrix}$$

Since the eigenvalues are given by

$$\lambda_1=\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}$$

$$\lambda_2=\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}$$

We know that the Lyapunov exponents are:

$$\lambda_1=\frac{\ln(3+\sqrt{5})}{2}>1$$

$$\lambda_2=\frac{\ln(3-\sqrt{5})}{2}<1$$

Therefore, it is a saddle point.

## Flows {#Flows}

Let $F \colon \mathbb{R}^{n} \to \mathbb{R}^{n}$ be a *C*<sup>1</sup> [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) with a critical point *p*, i.e., *F*(*p*) = 0, and let *J* denote the [Jacobian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix) of *F* at *p*. If the matrix *J* has no eigenvalues with zero real parts then *p* is called **hyperbolic**. Hyperbolic fixed points may also be called **hyperbolic critical points** or **elementary critical points**.[^o6]

The [Hartman–Grobman theorem](wikipedia://en/9933752) states that the orbit structure of a dynamical system in a [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of a hyperbolic equilibrium point is [topologically equivalent](wikipedia://en/5506149) to the orbit structure of the [linearized](wikipedia://en/1118789) dynamical system.

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

Consider the nonlinear system

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{dx}{dt} & = y, \\[5pt] \frac{dy}{dt} & = -x-x^3-\alpha y,~ \alpha \ne 0 \end{aligned}$$

(0, 0) is the only equilibrium point. The Jacobian matrix of the linearization at the equilibrium point is

$$J(0,0) = \left[ \begin{array}{rr} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & -\alpha \end{array} \right].$$

The eigenvalues of this matrix are $\frac{-\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2-4}}{2}$. For all values of *α* ≠ 0, the eigenvalues have non-zero real part. Thus, this equilibrium point is a hyperbolic equilibrium point. The linearized system will behave similar to the non-linear system near (0, 0). When *α* = 0, the system has a nonhyperbolic equilibrium at (0, 0).

:::

## Comments {#Comments}

In the case of an infinite dimensional system—for example systems involving a time delay—the notion of the "hyperbolic part of the spectrum" refers to the above property.
