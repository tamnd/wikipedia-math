# Generalized quadrangle

*Image: GQ(2,2).svg.* GQ(2,2), the Doily

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Generalized quadrangle"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **generalized quadrangle** is an [incidence structure](wikipedia://en/723125) whose main feature is the lack of any ([non-degenerate](wikipedia://en/232952)) [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654) yet containing many [quadrangles](wikipedia://en/2605272). A generalized quadrangle is by definition a [polar space](wikipedia://en/3948557) of rank two. They are the [generalized n-gons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_n-gon) with *n* = 4 and [near 2n-gons](wikipedia://en/41024203) with *n* = 2. They are also precisely the [partial geometries](wikipedia://en/5744042) pg(*s*,*t*,α) with α = 1.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A generalized quadrangle is an incidence structure (*P*,*B*,I), with I ⊆ *P* × *B* an [incidence relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incidence_relation), satisfying certain [axioms](wikipedia://en/928). Elements of *P* are by definition the *points* of the generalized quadrangle, elements of *B* the *lines*. The axioms are the following:

- There is an *s* (*s* ≥ 1) such that on every line there are exactly *s* + 1 points. There is at most one point on two distinct lines.
- There is a *t* (*t* ≥ 1) such that through every point there are exactly *t* + 1 lines. There is at most one line through two distinct points.
- For every point *p* not on a line *L*, there is a unique line *M* and a unique point *q*, such that *p* is on *M*, and *q* on *M* and *L*.

(*s*,*t*) are the *parameters* of the generalized quadrangle. The parameters are allowed to be infinite. If either *s* or *t* is one, the generalized quadrangle is called *trivial*. For example, the 3x3 grid with *P* = {1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9} and *B* = {123, 456, 789, 147, 258, 369} is a trivial GQ with *s* = 2 and *t* = 1. A generalized quadrangle with parameters (*s*,*t*) is often denoted by GQ(*s*,*t*).

The smallest non-trivial generalized quadrangle is [GQ(2,2)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cremona-Richmond_configuration), whose representation was dubbed "the doily" by Stanley Payne in 1973.

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- $$|P|=(s t+1)(s+1)$$
- $$|B|=(s t+1)(t+1)$$
- $$(s+t)|st(s+1)(t+1)$$
- $$s\neq 1 \Longrightarrow t\leq s^2$$
- $$t\neq 1 \Longrightarrow s\leq t^2$$

:::

## Graphs {#Graphs}

*Image: GQ24.svg.* [Line graph](wikipedia://en/675231) of **generalized quadrangle** GQ(2,4)

There are two interesting graphs that can be obtained from a generalized quadrangle.

- The *collinearity graph* having as vertices the points of a generalized quadrangle, with the collinear points connected. This graph is a [strongly regular graph](wikipedia://en/1399856) with parameters ((s+1)(st+1), s(t+1), s-1, t+1) where (s,t) is the order of the GQ.
- The *incidence graph* whose vertices are the points and lines of the generalized quadrangle and two vertices are adjacent if one is a point, the other a line and the point lies on the line. The incidence graph of a generalized quadrangle is characterized by being a [connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_graph), [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431) with [diameter](wikipedia://en/2979341) four and [girth](wikipedia://en/12013) eight. Therefore, it is an example of a [Cage](wikipedia://en/7735022). Incidence graphs of configurations are today generally called [Levi graphs](wikipedia://en/1396888), but the original Levi graph was the incidence graph of the GQ(2,2).

## Duality {#Duality}

If (*P*,*B*,I) is a generalized quadrangle with parameters (*s*,*t*), then (*B*,*P*,I<sup>−1</sup>), with I<sup>−1</sup> the inverse incidence relation, is also a generalized quadrangle. This is the *dual generalized quadrangle*. Its parameters are (*t*,*s*). Even if *s* = *t*, the dual structure need not be isomorphic with the original structure.

## Generalized quadrangles with lines of size 3 {#Generalized_quadrangles_with_lines_of_size_3}

There are precisely five (possibly degenerate) generalized quadrangles where each line has three points incident with it, the quadrangle with empty line set, the quadrangle with all lines through a fixed point corresponding to the [windmill graph Wd(3,n)](wikipedia://en/24373456), grid of size 3x3, the GQ(2,2) quadrangle and the unique GQ(2,4). These five quadrangles corresponds to the five [root systems](wikipedia://en/277087) in the [ADE classes](wikipedia://en/648042) *A*<sub>*n*</sub>, *D*<sub>*n*</sub>, *E*<sub>*6*</sub>, *E*<sub>*7*</sub> and *E*<sub>*8*</sub> , i.e., the simply laced root systems.[^o9][^o10]

## Classical generalized quadrangles {#Classical_generalized_quadrangles}

When looking at the different cases for [polar spaces](wikipedia://en/3948557) of rank at least three, and extrapolating them to rank 2, one finds these (finite) generalized quadrangles :

- A hyperbolic [quadric](wikipedia://en/145570) $Q^+(3,q)$, a parabolic quadric $Q(4,q)$ and an elliptic quadric $Q^-(5,q)$ are the only possible quadrics in projective spaces over finite fields with projective index 1. We find these parameters respectively :

$Q^+(3,q) :\ s=q,t=1$ (this is just a grid)

$$Q(4,q) :\ s=q,t=q$$

$$Q^-(5,q) :\ s=q,t=q^2$$

- A [hermitian variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_variety) $H(n,q^2)$ has projective index 1 if and only if n is 3 or 4. We find :

$$H(3,q^2) :\ s=q^2,t=q$$

$$H(4,q^2) :\ s=q^2,t=q^3$$

- A symplectic polarity in $PG(2d+1,q)$ has a maximal isotropic subspace of dimension 1 if and only if $d=1$. Here, we find a generalized quadrangle $W(3,q)$, with $s=q,t=q$.

The generalized quadrangle derived from $Q(4,q)$ is always isomorphic with the dual of $W(3,q)$, and they are both self-dual and thus isomorphic to each other if and only if $q$ is even.

## Non-classical examples {#Non-classical_examples}

- Let *O* be a [hyperoval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperoval) in $PG(2,q)$ with *q* an even [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483), and embed that projective (desarguesian) plane $\pi$ into $PG(3,q)$. Now consider the incidence structure $T_2^{*}(O)$ where the points are all points not in $\pi$, the lines are those not on $\pi$, intersecting $\pi$ in a point of *O*, and the incidence is the natural one. This is a *(q-1,q+1)*-generalized quadrangle.
- Let *q* be a [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483) (odd or even) and consider a symplectic polarity $\theta$ in $PG(3,q)$. Choose an arbitrary point *p* and define $\pi=p^{\theta}$. Let the lines of our incidence structure be all absolute lines not on $\pi$ together with all lines through *p* which are not on $\pi$, and let the points be all points of $PG(3,q)$ except those in $\pi$. The incidence is again the natural one. We obtain once again a *(q-1,q+1)*-generalized quadrangle

## Restrictions on parameters {#Restrictions_on_parameters}

By using grids and dual grids, any [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *z*, *z* ≥ 1 allows generalized quadrangles with parameters (1,*z*) and (*z*,1). Apart from that, only the following parameters have been found possible until now, with *q* an arbitrary [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483) :

$$(q,q)$$

$(q,q^2)$ and $(q^2,q)$

$(q^2,q^3)$ and $(q^3,q^2)$

$(q-1,q+1)$ and $(q+1,q-1)$
