# Heteroclinic orbit

*Image: Heteroclinic orbit in pendulum phaseportrait.png.* The [phase portrait](wikipedia://en/4519252) of the [pendulum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendulum) equation $x\,'' + \sin x = 0$. The highlighted curve shows the heteroclinic orbit from $(x, x') = (-\pi, 0)$ to $(x, x') = (\pi, 0)$. This orbit corresponds with the (rigid) pendulum starting upright, making one revolution through its lowest position, and ending upright again.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Heteroclinic orbit"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the [phase portrait](wikipedia://en/4519252) of a [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087), a **heteroclinic orbit** (sometimes called a *[heteroclinic connection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heteroclinic_connection)*) is a path in [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) which joins two different [equilibrium points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_point). If the equilibrium points at the start and end of the orbit are the same, the orbit is a [homoclinic orbit](wikipedia://en/3948734).

:::

Consider the continuous dynamical system described by the [ordinary differential equation](wikipedia://en/32742753)

$$\dot x = f(x).$$

Suppose there are equilibria at $x=x_0,x_1.$ Then a solution $\phi(t)$ is a heteroclinic orbit from $x_0$ to $x_1$ if both [limits](wikipedia://en/285759) are satisfied:

$$\begin{array}{rcl} \phi(t) \rightarrow x_0 &\text{as}& t \rightarrow -\infty, \\[4pt] \phi(t) \rightarrow x_1 &\text{as}& t \rightarrow +\infty. \end{array}$$

This implies that the orbit is contained in the [stable manifold](wikipedia://en/5466649) of $x_1$ and the [unstable manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unstable_manifold) of $x_0$.

## Symbolic dynamics {#Symbolic_dynamics}

By using the [Markov partition](wikipedia://en/11491735), the long-time behaviour of [hyperbolic system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_system) can be studied using the techniques of [symbolic dynamics](wikipedia://en/1986263). In this case, a heteroclinic orbit has a particularly simple and clear representation. Suppose that $S=\{1,2,\ldots,M\}$ is a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) of *M* symbols. The dynamics of a point *x* is then represented by a [bi-infinite string](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi-infinite_string) of symbols

$$\sigma =\{(\ldots,s_{-1},s_0,s_1,\ldots) : s_k \in S \; \forall k \in \mathbb{Z} \}$$

A [periodic point](wikipedia://en/4941621) of the system is simply a recurring sequence of letters. A heteroclinic orbit is then the joining of two distinct periodic orbits. It may be written as

$$p^\omega s_1 s_2 \cdots s_n q^\omega$$

where $p= t_1 t_2 \cdots t_k$ is a sequence of symbols of length *k*, (of course, $t_i\in S$), and $q = r_1 r_2 \cdots r_m$ is another sequence of symbols, of length *m* (likewise, $r_i\in S$). The notation $p^\omega$ simply denotes the repetition of *p* an infinite number of times. Thus, a heteroclinic orbit can be understood as the transition from one periodic orbit to another. By contrast, a [homoclinic orbit](wikipedia://en/3948734) can be written as

$$p^\omega s_1 s_2 \cdots s_n p^\omega$$

with the intermediate sequence $s_1 s_2 \cdots s_n$ being non-empty, and, of course, not being *p*, as otherwise, the orbit would simply be $p^\omega$.
