# SL2(R)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="SL2(R)"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) **SL(2, R)** or **SL<sub>2</sub>(R)** is the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of 2 × 2 [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) with [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) one:

:::

$$\mbox{SL}(2,\mathbf{R}) = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \colon a,b,c,d \in \mathbf{R}\mbox{ and }ad-bc=1\right\}.$$

It is a [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) [non-compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [simple](wikipedia://en/292831) real [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) of dimension 3 with applications in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200), and [physics](wikipedia://en/22939).

SL(2, **R**) acts on the [complex upper half-plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_upper_half-plane) by [fractional linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_linear_transformation). The [group action](wikipedia://en/12781) factors through the [quotient](wikipedia://en/11526) **PSL(2, R)** (the 2 × 2 [projective special linear group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_special_linear_group) over **R**). More specifically,

PSL(2, **R**) = SL(2, **R**) / {±*I*},

where *I* denotes the 2 × 2 [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718). It contains the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019) PSL(2, **Z**).

Also closely related is the 2-fold [covering group](wikipedia://en/1378699), Mp(2, **R**), a [metaplectic group](wikipedia://en/2981795) (thinking of SL(2, **R**) as a [symplectic group](wikipedia://en/173977)).

Another related group is SL<sup>±</sup>(2, **R**), the group of real 2 × 2 matrices with determinant ±1; this is more commonly used in the context of the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019), however.

## Descriptions {#Descriptions}

SL(2, **R**) is the group of all [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) of **R**<sup>2</sup> that preserve [oriented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29) [area](wikipedia://en/1209). It is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to the [symplectic group](wikipedia://en/173977) Sp(2, **R**) and the special unitary group [SU(1, 1)](wikipedia://en/173997#SU%281,_1%29). It is also isomorphic to the group of unit-length [coquaternions](wikipedia://en/1461265). The group SL<sup>±</sup>(2, **R**) preserves unoriented area: it may reverse orientation.

The quotient PSL(2, **R**) has several interesting descriptions, up to Lie group isomorphism:

- It is the group of [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29)-preserving [projective transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_transformation) of the [real projective line](wikipedia://en/46379102) **R** ∪ {∞}.
- It is the group of [conformal](wikipedia://en/50627) [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of the [unit disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_disc).
- It is the group of [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29)-preserving [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777) of the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/318742).
- It is the restricted [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489) of three-dimensional [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space). Equivalently, it is isomorphic to the [indefinite orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/293609) SO<sup>\+</sup>(1,2). It follows that SL(2, **R**) is isomorphic to the [spin group](wikipedia://en/411231) Spin(2,1)<sup>\+</sup>.

Elements of the modular group PSL(2, **Z**) have additional interpretations, as do elements of the group SL(2, **Z**) (as linear transforms of the torus), and these interpretations can also be viewed in light of the general theory of SL(2, **R**).

### Homographies {#Homographies}

Elements of PSL(2, **R**) are [homographies](wikipedia://en/3051962) on the [real projective line](wikipedia://en/46379102) **R** ∪ {∞}:

$$[x,1] \mapsto [x,\ 1] \begin{pmatrix}a & c \\ b & d \end{pmatrix} \ = \ [ax + b,\ cx + d] \ = \, \left[\frac{ax+b}{cx+d},\ 1\right] .$$

These projective transformations form a subgroup of PSL(2, **C**), which acts on the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799) by [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493).

When the real line is considered the boundary of the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/461942), PSL(2, **R**) expresses [hyperbolic motions](wikipedia://en/1229416).

### Möbius transformations {#Möbius_transformations}

Elements of PSL(2, **R**) act on the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) by Möbius transformations:

$$z \mapsto \frac{az+b}{cz+d}\;\;\;\;\mbox{ (where }a,b,c,d\in\mathbf{R}\mbox{)}.$$

This is precisely the set of Möbius transformations that preserve the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826). It follows that PSL(2, **R**) is the group of conformal automorphisms of the upper half-plane. By the [Riemann mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/26215), it is also isomorphic to the group of conformal automorphisms of the unit disc.

These Möbius transformations act as the [isometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometries) of the [upper half-plane model](wikipedia://en/461942) of hyperbolic space, and the corresponding Möbius transformations of the disc are the hyperbolic isometries of the [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836).

The above formula can be also used to define Möbius transformations of [dual](wikipedia://en/42169) and [double (aka split-complex) numbers](wikipedia://en/893559). The corresponding geometries are in non-trivial relations[^o5] to [Lobachevskian geometry](wikipedia://en/241291).

### Adjoint representation {#Adjoint_representation}

The group SL(2, **R**) acts on its Lie algebra sl(2, **R**) by [conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugation_%28group_theory%29) (remember that the Lie algebra elements are also 2 × 2 matrices), yielding a faithful 3-dimensional linear [representation](wikipedia://en/19378200) of PSL(2, **R**). This can alternatively be described as the action of PSL(2, **R**) on the space of [quadratic forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_forms) on **R**<sup>2</sup>. The result is the following representation:

$$\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \begin{bmatrix} a^2 & 2ab & b^2 \\ ac & ad+bc & bd \\ c^2 & 2cd & d^2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

The [Killing form](wikipedia://en/982386) on sl(2, **R**) has [signature](wikipedia://en/463225) (2,1), and induces an isomorphism between PSL(2, **R**) and the [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489) SO<sup>\+</sup>(2,1). This action of PSL(2, **R**) on [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space) restricts to the isometric action of PSL(2, **R**) on the [hyperboloid model](wikipedia://en/4771189) of the hyperbolic plane.

## Classification of elements {#Classification_of_elements}

The [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of an element *A* ∈ SL(2, **R**) satisfy the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268)

$$\lambda^2 \,-\, \mathrm{tr}(A)\,\lambda \,+\, 1 \,=\, 0$$

and therefore

$$\lambda = \frac{\mathrm{tr}(A) \pm \sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(A)^2 - 4}}{2}.$$

This leads to the following classification of elements, with corresponding action on the Euclidean plane:

- If $|\mathrm{tr}(A)| < 2$, then *A* is called **elliptic,** and is conjugate to a [rotation](wikipedia://en/410009).
- If $|\mathrm{tr}(A)| = 2$, then *A* is called **parabolic,** and is a [shear mapping](wikipedia://en/799405).
- If $|\mathrm{tr}(A)| > 2$, then *A* is called **hyperbolic,** and is a [squeeze mapping](wikipedia://en/1140043).

The names correspond to the classification of [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673) by [eccentricity](wikipedia://en/1239472): if one defines eccentricity as half the absolute value of the trace (ε = $\tfrac{1}{2}$ |tr|; dividing by 2 corrects for the effect of dimension, while absolute value corresponds to ignoring an overall factor of ±1 such as when working in PSL(2, **R**)), then this yields: $\epsilon < 1$, elliptic; $\epsilon = 1$, parabolic; $\epsilon > 1$, hyperbolic.

The identity element 1 and negative identity element −1 (in PSL(2, **R**) they are the same), have trace ±2, and hence by this classification are parabolic elements, though they are often considered separately.

[The same classification](wikipedia://en/314493#Classification) is used for SL(2, **C**) and PSL(2, **C**) ([Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493)) and PSL(2, **R**) (real Möbius transformations), with the addition of "loxodromic" transformations corresponding to complex traces; [analogous classifications](wikipedia://en/1239472#Analogous_classifications) are used elsewhere.

A subgroup that is contained with the elliptic (respectively, parabolic, hyperbolic) elements, plus the identity and negative identity, is called an **elliptic subgroup** (respectively, **[parabolic subgroup](wikipedia://en/588356),** **hyperbolic subgroup**).

The trichotomy of SL(2, **R**) into elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic elements is a classification into *subsets,* not *subgroups:* these sets are not closed under multiplication (the product of two parabolic elements need not be parabolic, and so forth). However, each element is conjugate to a member of one of 3 standard [one-parameter subgroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-parameter_subgroup) (possibly times ±1), as detailed below.

Topologically, as trace is a continuous map, the elliptic elements (excluding ±1) form an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358), as do the hyperbolic elements (excluding ±1). By contrast, the parabolic elements, together with ±1, form a [closed set](wikipedia://en/47279) that is not open.

### Elliptic elements {#Elliptic_elements}

The [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) for an elliptic element are both complex, and are [conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) values on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071). Such an element is conjugate to a [rotation](wikipedia://en/39789) of the Euclidean plane – they can be interpreted as rotations in a possibly non-orthogonal basis – and the corresponding element of PSL(2, **R**) acts as (conjugate to) a [rotation](wikipedia://en/39789) of the hyperbolic plane and of [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space).

Elliptic elements of the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019) must have eigenvalues {ω, ω<sup>−1</sup>}, where *ω* is a primitive 3rd, 4th, or 6th [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950). These are all the elements of the modular group with finite [order](wikipedia://en/493527), and they act on the [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) as periodic diffeomorphisms.

Elements of trace 0 may be called "circular elements" (by analogy with eccentricity) but this is rarely done; they correspond to elements with eigenvalues ±*i*, and are conjugate to rotation by 90°, and square to -*I*: they are the non-identity [involutions](wikipedia://en/293450) in PSL(2).

Elliptic elements are conjugate into the subgroup of rotations of the Euclidean plane, the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) SO(2); the angle of rotation is [arccos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arccos_%28trigonometry%29) of half of the trace, with the sign of the rotation determined by orientation. (A rotation and its inverse are conjugate in GL(2) but not SL(2).)

### Parabolic elements {#Parabolic_elements}

A parabolic element has only a single eigenvalue, which is either 1 or -1. Such an element acts as a [shear mapping](wikipedia://en/799405) on the Euclidean plane, and the corresponding element of PSL(2, **R**) acts as a [limit rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_rotation) of the hyperbolic plane and as a [null rotation](wikipedia://en/230489#Conjugacy_classes) of [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space).

Parabolic elements of the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019) act as [Dehn twists](wikipedia://en/765970) of the torus.

Parabolic elements are conjugate into the 2 component group of standard shears × ±*I*: $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1 & \lambda \\ & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right) \times \{\pm I\}$. In fact, they are all conjugate (in SL(2)) to one of the four matrices $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1 & \pm 1 \\ & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-1 & \pm 1 \\ & -1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ (in GL(2) or SL<sup>±</sup>(2), the ± can be omitted, but in SL(2) it cannot).

### Hyperbolic elements {#Hyperbolic_elements}

The [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) for a hyperbolic element are both real, and are reciprocals. Such an element acts as a [squeeze mapping](wikipedia://en/1140043) of the Euclidean plane, and the corresponding element of PSL(2, **R**) acts as a [translation](wikipedia://en/204682) of the hyperbolic plane and as a [Lorentz boost](wikipedia://en/18404) on [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space).

Hyperbolic elements of the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019) act as [Anosov diffeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/2320078) of the torus.

Hyperbolic elements are conjugate into the 2 component group of standard squeezes × ±*I*: $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\lambda \\ & \lambda^{-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right) \times \{\pm I\}$; the [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) of the hyperbolic rotation is given by [arcosh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcosh) of half of the trace, but the sign can be positive or negative: in contrast to the elliptic case, a squeeze and its inverse are conjugate in SL₂ (by a rotation in the axes; for standard axes, a rotation by 90°).

### Conjugacy classes {#Conjugacy_classes}

By [Jordan normal form](wikipedia://en/312877), matrices are classified up to conjugacy (in GL(*n*, **C**)) by eigenvalues and nilpotence (concretely, nilpotence means where 1s occur in the Jordan blocks). Thus elements of SL(2) are classified up to conjugacy in GL(2) (or indeed SL<sup>±</sup>(2)) by trace (since determinant is fixed, and trace and determinant determine eigenvalues), except if the eigenvalues are equal, so ±I and the parabolic elements of trace +2 and trace -2 are not conjugate (the former have no off-diagonal entries in Jordan form, while the latter do).

Up to conjugacy in SL(2) (instead of GL(2)), there is an additional datum, corresponding to orientation: a clockwise and counterclockwise (elliptical) rotation are not conjugate, nor are a positive and negative shear, as detailed above; thus for absolute value of trace less than 2, there are two conjugacy classes for each trace (clockwise and counterclockwise rotations), for absolute value of the trace equal to 2 there are three conjugacy classes for each trace (positive shear, identity, negative shear), and for absolute value of the trace greater than 2 there is one conjugacy class for a given trace.

## Iwasawa or KAN decomposition {#Iwasawa_or_KAN_decomposition}

The [Iwasawa decomposition](wikipedia://en/1139148) of a group is a method to construct the group as a product of three Lie subgroups *K*, *A*, *N*. For $\mbox{SL}(2,\mathbf{R})$ these three subgroups are

$$\mathbf{K} = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} \cos \theta & -\sin \theta \\ \sin \theta & \cos \theta \end{pmatrix} \in SL(2,\mathbb{R}) \ | \ \theta\in\mathbf{R} \right\} \cong SO(2) ,$$

$$\mathbf{A} = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} r & 0 \\ 0 & r^{-1} \end{pmatrix} \in SL(2,\mathbb{R}) \ | \ r > 0 \right\},$$

$$\mathbf{N} = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & x \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \in SL(2,\mathbb{R}) \ | \ x\in\mathbf{R} \right\}.$$

These three elements are the generators of the Elliptic, Hyperbolic, and Parabolic subsets respectively.

## Topology and universal cover {#Topology_and_universal_cover}

As a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), PSL(2, **R**) can be described as the [unit tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/7932644) of the hyperbolic plane. It is a [circle bundle](wikipedia://en/2794417), and has a natural [contact structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_structure) induced by the [symplectic structure](wikipedia://en/28356) on the hyperbolic plane. SL(2, **R**) is a 2-fold cover of PSL(2, **R**), and can be thought of as the bundle of [spinors](wikipedia://en/29276) on the hyperbolic plane.

The fundamental group of SL(2, **R**) is the infinite [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) **Z**. The [universal covering group](wikipedia://en/1378699#Universal_covering_group), denoted $\overline{\mbox{SL}(2,\mathbf{R})}$, is an example of a finite-dimensional Lie group that is not a [matrix group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_group). That is, $\overline{\mbox{SL}(2,\mathbf{R})}$ admits no [faithful](wikipedia://en/2617872), finite-dimensional [representation](wikipedia://en/12695).

As a topological space, $\overline{\mbox{SL}(2,\mathbf{R})}$ is a line bundle over the hyperbolic plane. When imbued with a left-invariant [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric), the [3-manifold](wikipedia://en/1018257) $\overline{\mbox{SL}(2,\mathbf{R})}$ becomes one of the [eight Thurston geometries](wikipedia://en/220642#The_eight_Thurston_geometries). For example, $\overline{\mbox{SL}(2,\mathbf{R})}$ is the universal cover of the unit tangent bundle to any [hyperbolic surface](wikipedia://en/173181). Any manifold modeled on $\overline{\mbox{SL}(2,\mathbf{R})}$ is orientable, and is a [circle bundle](wikipedia://en/2794417) over some 2-dimensional hyperbolic [orbifold](wikipedia://en/293504) (a [Seifert fiber space](wikipedia://en/1631931)).

*Image: Braid-modular-group-cover.svg.* The [braid group](wikipedia://en/550138) *B*<sub>3</sub> is the [universal central extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_central_extension) of the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019).

Under this covering, the preimage of the modular group PSL(2, **Z**) is the [braid group](wikipedia://en/550138) on 3 generators, *B*<sub>3</sub>, which is the [universal central extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_central_extension) of the modular group. These are lattices inside the relevant algebraic groups, and this corresponds algebraically to the universal covering group in topology.

The 2-fold covering group can be identified as Mp(2, **R**), a [metaplectic group](wikipedia://en/2981795), thinking of SL(2, **R**) as the symplectic group Sp(2, **R**).

The aforementioned groups together form a sequence:

$$\overline{\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf{R})} \to \cdots \to \mathrm{Mp}(2,\mathbf{R}) \to \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf{R}) \to \mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbf{R}).$$

However, there are other covering groups of PSL(2, **R**) corresponding to all *n*, as *n* **Z** \< **Z** ≅ π<sub>1</sub> (PSL(2, **R**)), which form a [lattice of covering groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_of_covering_groups) by divisibility; these cover SL(2, **R**) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) *n* is even.

## Algebraic structure {#Algebraic_structure}

The [center](wikipedia://en/7125) of SL(2, **R**) is the two-element group {±1}, and the [quotient](wikipedia://en/11526) PSL(2, **R**) is [simple](wikipedia://en/47600).

Discrete subgroups of PSL(2, **R**) are called [Fuchsian groups](wikipedia://en/471365). These are the hyperbolic analogue of the Euclidean [wallpaper groups](wikipedia://en/386214) and [Frieze groups](wikipedia://en/375503). The most famous of these is the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019) PSL(2, **Z**), which acts on a tessellation of the hyperbolic plane by ideal triangles.

The [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177) [SO(2)](wikipedia://en/173954) is a [maximal compact subgroup](wikipedia://en/1496726) of SL(2, **R**), and the circle SO(2) / {±1} is a maximal compact subgroup of PSL(2, **R**).

The [Schur multiplier](wikipedia://en/1121587) of the [discrete group](wikipedia://en/468536) PSL(2, **R**) is much larger than **Z**, and the universal [central extension](wikipedia://en/345807#Central_extension) is much larger than the universal covering group. However these large central extensions do not take the [topology](wikipedia://en/30450) into account and are somewhat pathological.

## Representation theory {#Representation_theory}

SL(2, **R**) is a real, non-compact [simple Lie group](wikipedia://en/292831), and is the split-real form of the [complex Lie group](wikipedia://en/2187847) SL(2, **C**). The [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) of SL(2, **R**), denoted sl(2, **R**), is the algebra of all real, [traceless](wikipedia://en/43270) 2 × 2 matrices. It is the [Bianchi algebra](wikipedia://en/7811800) of type VIII.

The finite-dimensional representation theory of SL(2, **R**) is equivalent to the [representation theory of SU(2)](wikipedia://en/1378619), which is the compact real form of SL(2, **C**). In particular, SL(2, **R**) has no nontrivial finite-dimensional unitary representations. This is a feature of every connected simple non-compact Lie group. For outline of proof, see [non-unitarity of representations](wikipedia://en/2249718#Non-unitarity).

The infinite-dimensional representation theory of SL(2, **R**) is quite interesting. The group has several families of unitary representations, which were worked out in detail by [Gelfand](wikipedia://en/364643) and [Naimark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Naimark) (1946), [V. Bargmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V._Bargmann) (1947), and [Harish-Chandra](wikipedia://en/373190) (1952).
