# Graph embedding

*Image: Heawood graph and map on torus.png.* The [Heawood graph](wikipedia://en/1396870) and associated map embedded in the torus.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Graph embedding"}

In [topological graph theory](wikipedia://en/1631772), an **embedding** (also spelled **imbedding**) of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) $G$ on a [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) $\Sigma$ is a representation of $G$ on $\Sigma$ in which points of $\Sigma$ are associated with [vertices](wikipedia://en/12401) and simple arcs ([homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) images of $[0,1]$) are associated with [edges](wikipedia://en/12401) in such a way that:

:::

- the endpoints of the arc associated with an edge $e$ are the points associated with the end vertices of $e,$
- no arcs include points associated with other vertices,
- two arcs never intersect at a point which is interior to either of the arcs.

Here a surface is a [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) $2$-[manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470).

Informally, an embedding of a graph into a surface is a drawing of the graph on the surface in such a way that its edges may intersect only at their endpoints. It is well known that any finite graph can be embedded in 3-dimensional Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^3$.[^o2] A [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314) is one that can be embedded in 2-dimensional Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^2.$

Often, an **embedding** is regarded as an equivalence class (under homeomorphisms of $\Sigma$) of representations of the kind just described.

Some authors define a weaker version of the definition of "graph embedding" by omitting the non-intersection condition for edges. In such contexts the stricter definition is described as "non-crossing graph embedding".[^o3]

This article deals only with the strict definition of graph embedding. The weaker definition is discussed in the articles "[graph drawing](wikipedia://en/325813)" and "[crossing number](wikipedia://en/18298594)".

::: {#o5 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Terminology"}

## Terminology {#Terminology}

If a graph $G$ is embedded on a closed surface $\Sigma$, the complement of the union of the points and arcs associated with the vertices and edges of $G$ is a family of **regions** (or **[faces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_%28graph_theory%29)**).[^o6] A **2-cell embedding**, **cellular embedding** or **map** is an embedding in which every face is homeomorphic to an open disk.[^o7] A **closed 2-cell embedding** is an embedding in which the closure of every face is homeomorphic to a closed disk.

The **genus** of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) is the minimal integer $n$ such that the graph can be embedded in a surface of [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) $n$. In particular, a [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314) has genus $0$, because it can be drawn on a sphere without self-crossing. A graph that can be embedded on a [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) is called a [toroidal graph](wikipedia://en/1633057).

The **non-orientable genus** of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) is the minimal integer $n$ such that the graph can be embedded in a non-orientable surface of (non-orientable) genus $n$.[^o6]

The **Euler genus** of a graph is the minimal integer $n$ such that the graph can be embedded in an orientable surface of (orientable) genus $n/2$ or in a non-orientable surface of (non-orientable) genus $n$. A graph is **orientably simple** if its Euler genus is smaller than its non-orientable genus.

The **maximum genus** of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) is the maximal integer $n$ such that the graph can be $2$-cell embedded in an orientable surface of [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) $n$.

:::

## Combinatorial embedding {#Combinatorial_embedding}

An embedded graph uniquely defines [cyclic orders](wikipedia://en/434673) of edges incident to the same vertex. The set of all these cyclic orders is called a [rotation system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_system). Embeddings with the same rotation system are considered to be equivalent and the corresponding equivalence class of embeddings is called **combinatorial embedding** (as opposed to the term **topological embedding**, which refers to the previous definition in terms of points and curves). Sometimes, the rotation system itself is called a "combinatorial embedding".[^o9][^o10][^o11]

An embedded graph also defines natural cyclic orders of edges which constitutes the boundaries of the faces of the embedding. However handling these face-based orders is less straightforward, since in some cases some edges may be traversed twice along a face boundary. For example this is always the case for embeddings of trees, which have a single face. To overcome this combinatorial nuisance, one may consider that every edge is "split" lengthwise in two "half-edges", or "sides". Under this convention in all face boundary traversals each half-edge is traversed only once and the two half-edges of the same edge are always traversed in opposite directions.

Other equivalent representations for cellular embeddings include the [ribbon graph](wikipedia://en/56039802), a topological space formed by gluing together topological disks for the vertices and edges of an embedded graph, and the [graph-encoded map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph-encoded_map), an edge-colored [cubic graph](wikipedia://en/980508) with four vertices for each edge of the embedded graph.

## Computational complexity {#Computational_complexity}

The problem of finding the graph genus is [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard) (the problem of determining whether an $n$-vertex graph has genus $g$ is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete)).[^o13]

At the same time, the graph genus problem is [fixed-parameter tractable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed-parameter_tractability), i.e., [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time) algorithms are known to check whether a graph can be embedded into a surface of a given fixed genus as well as to find the embedding.

The first breakthrough in this respect happened in 1979, when algorithms of [time complexity](wikipedia://en/405944) *O*(*n*<sup>*O*(*g*)</sup>) were independently submitted to the Annual [ACM Symposium on Theory of Computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACM_Symposium_on_Theory_of_Computing): one by I. Filotti and [G.L. Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Miller_%28computer_scientist%29) and another one by [John Reif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Reif). Their approaches were quite different, but upon the suggestion of the program committee they presented a joint paper.[^o14] However, [Wendy Myrvold](wikipedia://en/61886633) and [William Kocay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lawrence_Kocay) proved in 2011 that the algorithm given by Filotti, Miller and Reif was incorrect.[^o15]

In 1999 it was reported that the fixed-genus case can be solved in time [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time) in the graph size and [doubly exponential](wikipedia://en/8541166) in the genus.[^o16]

## Embeddings of graphs into higher-dimensional spaces {#Embeddings_of_graphs_into_higher-dimensional_spaces}

It is known that any finite graph can be embedded into a three-dimensional space.[^o2]

One method for doing this is to place the points on any line in space and to draw the edges as curves each of which lies in a distinct [halfplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halfplane), with all halfplanes having that line as their common boundary. An embedding like this in which the edges are drawn on halfplanes is called a [book embedding](wikipedia://en/3987703) of the graph. This [metaphor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphor) comes from imagining that each of the planes where an edge is drawn is like a page of a book. It was observed that in fact several edges may be drawn in the same "page"; the *book thickness* of the graph is the minimum number of halfplanes needed for such a drawing.

Alternatively, any finite graph can be drawn with straight-line edges in three dimensions without crossings by placing its vertices in [general position](wikipedia://en/383736) so that no four are coplanar. For instance, this may be achieved by placing the *i*th vertex at the point (*i*,*i*<sup>2</sup>,*i*<sup>3</sup>) of the [moment curve](wikipedia://en/29536414).

An embedding of a graph into three-dimensional space in which no two of the cycles are topologically linked is called a [linkless embedding](wikipedia://en/772031). A graph has a linkless embedding if and only if it does not have one of the seven graphs of the [Petersen family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petersen_family) as a [minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_%28graph_theory%29).
