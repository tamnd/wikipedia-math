# Abstract simplicial complex

*Image: Simplicial complex example.svg.* Geometric realization of a 3-dimensional abstract simplicial complex

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Abstract simplicial complex"}

In [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170), an **abstract simplicial complex** (ASC), often called an **abstract complex** or just a **complex**, is a [family of sets](wikipedia://en/723043) that is closed under taking [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631), i.e., every subset of a set in the family is also in the family. It is a purely combinatorial description of the geometric notion of a [simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/252686).[^o2] For example, in a 2-dimensional simplicial complex, the sets in the family are the triangles (sets of size 3), their edges (sets of size 2), and their vertices (sets of size 1).

:::

In the context of [matroids](wikipedia://en/244321) and [greedoids](wikipedia://en/666526), abstract simplicial complexes are also called **[independence systems](wikipedia://en/2471386)**.[^o3]

An abstract simplex can be studied algebraically by forming its [Stanley–Reisner ring](wikipedia://en/24394535); this sets up a powerful relation between [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) and [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990).

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

A collection $\Delta$ of non-empty finite subsets of a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) *S* is called a set-family.

A set-family $\Delta$ is called an **abstract simplicial complex** if, for every set $X$ in $\Delta$, and every non-empty subset $Y \subseteq X$, the set $Y$ also belongs to $\Delta$.

The finite sets that belong to $\Delta$ are called **faces** of the complex, and a face $Y$ is said to belong to another face $X$ if $Y \subseteq X$, so the definition of an abstract simplicial complex can be restated as saying that every face of a face of a complex $\Delta$ is itself a face of $\Delta$. The **vertex set** of $\Delta$ is defined as $V(\Delta) = \cup\Delta$, the union of all faces of $\Delta$. The elements of the vertex set are called the **vertices** of the complex. For every vertex *v* of $\Delta$, the set {*v*} is a face of the complex, and every face of the complex is a finite subset of the vertex set.

The maximal faces of $\Delta$ (i.e., faces that are not subsets of any other faces) are called **facets** of the complex. The **dimension of a face** $X$ in $\Delta$ is defined as $\dim(X) = |X| - 1$: faces consisting of a single element are zero-dimensional, faces consisting of two elements are one-dimensional, etc. The **dimension of the complex** $\dim(\Delta)$ is defined as the largest dimension of any of its faces, or infinity if there is no finite bound on the dimension of the faces.

The complex $\Delta$ is said to be **finite** if it has finitely many faces, or equivalently if its vertex set is finite. Also, $\Delta$ is said to be **pure** if it is finite-dimensional (but not necessarily finite) and every facet has the same dimension. In other words, $\Delta$ is pure if $\dim(\Delta)$ is finite and every face is contained in a facet of dimension $\dim(\Delta)$.

One-dimensional abstract simplicial complexes are mathematically equivalent to [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_graph) [undirected graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph): the vertex set of the complex can be viewed as the vertex set of a graph, and the two-element facets of the complex correspond to undirected edges of a graph. In this view, one-element facets of a complex correspond to isolated vertices that do not have any incident edges.

A **subcomplex** of $\Delta$ is an abstract simplicial complex *L* such that every face of *L* belongs to $\Delta$; that is, $L \subseteq \Delta$ and *L* is an abstract simplicial complex. A subcomplex that consists of all of the subsets of a single face of $\Delta$ is often called a **simplex** of $\Delta$. (However, some authors use the term "simplex" for a face or, rather ambiguously, for both a face and the subcomplex associated with a face, by analogy with the non-abstract (geometric) [simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/252686) terminology. To avoid ambiguity, we do not use in this article the term "simplex" for a face in the context of abstract complexes).

The **[*d*-skeleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D-skeleton)** of $\Delta$ is the subcomplex of $\Delta$ consisting of all of the faces of $\Delta$ that have dimension at most *d*. In particular, the [1-skeleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skeleton_%28topology%29) is called the **underlying graph** of $\Delta$. The 0-skeleton of $\Delta$ can be identified with its vertex set, although formally it is not quite the same thing (the vertex set is a single set of all of the vertices, while the 0-skeleton is a family of single-element sets).

The **link** of a face $Y$ in $\Delta$, often denoted $\Delta/Y$ or $\operatorname{lk}_{\Delta}(Y)$, is the subcomplex of $\Delta$ defined by

$$\Delta/Y := \{ X\in \Delta \mid X\cap Y = \varnothing,\, X\cup Y \in \Delta \}.$$

Note that the link of the empty set is $\Delta$ itself.

### Simplicial maps {#Simplicial_maps}

Given two abstract simplicial complexes, $\Delta$ and $\Gamma$, a **[simplicial map](wikipedia://en/25738537)** is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f\,$ that maps the vertices of $\Delta$ to the vertices of $\Gamma$ and that has the property that for any face $X$ of $\Delta$, the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) $f\,(X)$ is a face of $\Gamma$. There is a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) **SCpx** with abstract simplicial complexes as objects and simplicial maps as [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891). This is equivalent to a suitable category defined using non-abstract [simplicial complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicial_complexes).

Moreover, the categorical point of view allows us to tighten the relation between the underlying set *S* of an abstract simplicial complex $\Delta$ and the vertex set $V(\Delta) \subseteq S$ of $\Delta$: for the purposes of defining a category of abstract simplicial complexes, the elements of *S* not lying in $V(\Delta)$ are irrelevant. More precisely, **SCpx** is equivalent to the category where:

- an object is a set *S* equipped with a collection of non-empty finite subsets $\Delta$ that contains all singletons and such that if $X$ is in $\Delta$ and $Y \subseteq X$ is non-empty, then $Y$ also belongs to $\Delta$.
- a morphism from $(S, \Delta)$ to $(T, \Gamma)$ is a function $f : S \to T$ such that the image of any element of $\Delta$ is an element of $\Gamma$.

## Geometric realization {#Geometric_realization}

We can associate to any abstract simplicial complex (ASC) *K* a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $|K|$, called its **geometric realization**. There are several ways to define $|K|$.

### Geometric definition {#Geometric_definition}

Every [geometric simplicial complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_simplicial_complex) (GSC) determines an ASC:*[^o9]*p. 14 the vertices of the ASC are the vertices of the GSC, and the faces of the ASC are the vertex-sets of the faces of the GSC. For example, consider a GSC with 4 vertices {1,2,3,4}, where the maximal faces are the triangle between {1,2,3} and the lines between {2,4} and {3,4}. Then, the corresponding ASC contains the sets {1,2,3}, {2,4}, {3,4}, and all their subsets. We say that the GSC is the **geometric realization** of the ASC.

Every ASC has a geometric realization. This is easy to see for a finite ASC.*[^o9]*p. 14 Let $N := |V(K)|$. Identify the vertices in $V(K)$ with the vertices of an (*N* − 1)-dimensional simplex in $\mathbb{R}^N$. Construct the GSC {[conv](wikipedia://en/40634)(F): F is a face in K}. Clearly, the ASC associated with this GSC is identical to *K*, so we have indeed constructed a geometric realization of *K.* In fact, an ASC can be realized using much fewer dimensions. If an ASC is *d*-dimensional (that is, the maximum cardinality of a simplex in it is *d*+1), then it has a geometric realization in $\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}$, but might not have a geometric realization in $\mathbb{R}^{2d}$ *[^o9]p. 16* The special case *d*=1 corresponds to the well-known fact, that any [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) can be plotted in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ where the edges are straight lines that do not intersect each other except in common vertices, but not any [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) can be plotted in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ in this way.

If *K* is the standard combinatorial *n*-simplex, then $|K|$ can be naturally identified with $\Delta^{n}$.

Every two geometric realizations of the same ASC, even in Euclidean spaces of different dimensions, are [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660).*[^o9]*p. 14 Therefore, given an ASC *K,* one can speak of *the* geometric realization of *K*.

### Topological definition {#Topological_definition}

The construction goes as follows. First, define $|K|$ as a subset of $[0, 1]^S$ consisting of functions $t\colon S\to [0, 1]$ satisfying the two conditions:

$$\{s\in S:t_s>0\}\in K$$

$$\sum_{s\in S}t_s=1$$

Now think of the set of elements of $[0, 1]^S$ with finite support as the [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566) of $[0, 1]^A$ where *A* ranges over finite subsets of *S*, and give that direct limit the [induced topology](wikipedia://en/1819736). Now give $|K|$ the [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926).

### Categorical definition {#Categorical_definition}

Alternatively, let $\mathcal{K}$ denote the category whose objects are the faces of $K$ and whose morphisms are inclusions. Next choose a [total order](wikipedia://en/30330) on the vertex set of $K$ and define a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) *F* from $\mathcal{K}$ to the category of topological spaces as follows. For any face *X* in *K* of dimension *n*, let $F(X) = \Delta^{n}$ be the standard *n*-simplex. The order on the vertex set then specifies a unique [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between the elements of $X$ and vertices of $\Delta^{n}$, ordered in the usual way $e_{0} < e_{1} < ... < e_{n}$. If $Y \subseteq X$ is a face of dimension $m < n$, then this bijection specifies a unique *m*-dimensional face of $\Delta^{n}$. Define $F(Y) \to F(X)$ to be the unique [affine](wikipedia://en/38449) linear [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119) of $\Delta^{m}$ as that distinguished face of $\Delta^{n}$, such that the map on vertices is order-preserving.

We can then define the geometric realization $|K|$ as the [colimit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colimit) of the functor *F*. More specifically $|K|$ is the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) of the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946)

$$\coprod_{X \in K}{F(X)}$$

by the [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) that identifies a point $y \in F(Y)$ with its image under the map $F(Y) \to F(X)$, for every inclusion $Y \subseteq X$.

## Examples {#Examples}

\1. Let *V* be a finite set of [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) $n + 1$. The **combinatorial *n*-simplex** with vertex-set *V* is an ASC whose faces are all nonempty subsets of *V* (i.e., it is the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of *V*). If $V = S = \{0, 1, ..., n\},$ then this ASC is called the **standard combinatorial *n*-simplex**.

\2. Let *G* be an undirected graph. The **[clique complex](wikipedia://en/20751674)** **of *G*** is an ASC whose faces are all [cliques](wikipedia://en/524466) (complete subgraphs) of *G*. The **independence complex of *G*** is an ASC whose faces are all [independent sets](wikipedia://en/524501) of *G* (it is the clique complex of the [complement graph](wikipedia://en/1175649) of G). Clique complexes are the prototypical example of [flag complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_complex). A **flag complex** is a complex *K* with the property that every set, all of whose 2-element subsets are faces of *K*, is itself a face of *K*.

\3. Let *H* be a [hypergraph](wikipedia://en/232535). A [matching](wikipedia://en/63030703) in *H* is a set of edges of *H*, in which every two edges are [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359). The **matching complex of *H*** is an ASC whose faces are all [matchings](wikipedia://en/63030703) in *H*. It is the [independence complex](wikipedia://en/64659151) of the [line graph](wikipedia://en/16181442) of *H*.

\4. Let *P* be a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) (poset). The **order complex** of *P* is an ASC whose faces are all finite [chains](wikipedia://en/30330#Chains) in *P*. Its [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) groups and other [topological invariants](wikipedia://en/1337282) contain important information about the poset *P*.

\5. Let *M* be a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) and *δ* a real number. The **[Vietoris–Rips complex](wikipedia://en/13056778)** is an ASC whose faces are the finite subsets of *M* with diameter at most *δ*. It has applications in [homology theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_theory), [hyperbolic groups](wikipedia://en/3172817), [image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processing), and [mobile ad hoc networking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_ad_hoc_network). It is another example of a flag complex.

\6. Let $I$ be a square-free [monomial ideal](wikipedia://en/43023424) in a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $S = K[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ (that is, an ideal generated by products of subsets of variables). Then the exponent vectors of those square-free monomials of $S$ that are not in $I$ determine an abstract simplicial complex via the map $\mathbf{a}\in \{0,1\}^n \mapsto \{i \in [n] : a_i = 1\}$. In fact, there is a bijection between (non-empty) abstract simplicial complexes on $n$ vertices and square-free monomial ideals in $S$. If $I_{\Delta}$ is the square-free ideal corresponding to the simplicial complex $\Delta$ then the [quotient](wikipedia://en/184635) $S/I_{\Delta}$ is known as the [Stanley–Reisner ring](wikipedia://en/24394535) of ${\Delta}$.

\7. For any [open covering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_covering) *C* of a topological space, the **[nerve complex](wikipedia://en/587678)** of *C* is an abstract simplicial complex containing the sub-families of *C* with a non-empty [intersection](wikipedia://en/41780044).

:::

## Enumeration {#Enumeration}

The number of abstract simplicial complexes on up to *n* labeled elements (that is on a set *S* of size *n*) is one less than the *n*th [Dedekind number](wikipedia://en/21452705). These numbers grow very rapidly, and are known only for $n \le 9$; the Dedekind numbers are (starting with *n* = 0):

1, 2, 5, 19, 167, 7580, 7828353, 2414682040997, 56130437228687557907787, 286386577668298411128469151667598498812365 . This corresponds to the number of non-empty [antichains](wikipedia://en/348973) of subsets of an $n$ set.

The number of abstract simplicial complexes whose vertices are exactly *n* labeled elements is given by the sequence "1, 2, 9, 114, 6894, 7785062, 2414627396434, 56130437209370320359966, 286386577668298410623295216696338374471993" , starting at *n* = 1. This corresponds to the number of antichain covers of a labeled *n*-set; there is a clear bijection between antichain covers of an *n*-set and simplicial complexes on *n* elements described in terms of their maximal faces.

The number of abstract simplicial complexes on exactly *n* unlabeled elements is given by the sequence "1, 2, 5, 20, 180, 16143, 489996795, 1392195548399980210" , starting at *n* = 1.

:::

## Computational problems {#Computational_problems}

The [simplicial complex recognition problem](wikipedia://en/72353274) is: given a finite ASC, decide whether its geometric realization is homeomorphic to a given geometric object. This problem is [undecidable](wikipedia://en/15631055) for any *d*-dimensional manifolds for *d* ≥ 5.[^o17]

## Relation to other concepts {#Relation_to_other_concepts}

An abstract simplicial complex with an additional property called the **augmentation property** or the **exchange property** yields a **[matroid](wikipedia://en/244321)**. The following expression shows the relations between the terms:

HYPERGRAPHS = SET-FAMILIES ⊃ INDEPENDENCE-SYSTEMS = ABSTRACT-SIMPLICIAL-COMPLEXES ⊃ MATROIDS.
