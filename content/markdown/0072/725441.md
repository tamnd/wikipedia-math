# Atwood machine

*Image: Atwoods machine.png.* Illustration of the Atwood machine, 1905.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Atwood machine"}

The **Atwood machine** (or **Atwood's machine**) was invented in 1784 by the English [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [George Atwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Atwood) as a laboratory experiment to verify the [mechanical laws of motion](wikipedia://en/55212) with constant [acceleration](wikipedia://en/2443). Atwood's machine is a common classroom demonstration used to illustrate principles of [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586).

:::

The ideal Atwood machine consists of two objects of mass $m_{1}$ and $m_{2}$, connected by an [inextensible](wikipedia://en/65914#Inextensible_cord) massless string over an ideal massless [pulley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulley).[^o2]

Both masses experience uniform acceleration. When $m_{1} = m_{2}$, the machine is in [neutral equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_equilibrium) regardless of the position of the weights.

## Equation for constant acceleration {#Equation_for_constant_acceleration}

*Image: Atwood.svg.* The [free body diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_body_diagram) of the two hanging masses of the Atwood machine. Our [sign convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_convention), depicted by the [acceleration](wikipedia://en/2443) [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_vectors) is that $m_{1}$ accelerates downward and that $m_{2}$ accelerates upward, as would be the case if $m_{1} > m_{2}$

An equation for the acceleration can be derived by analyzing forces. Assuming a massless, inextensible string and an ideal massless pulley, the only forces to consider are: tension force ($T$), and the weight of the two masses ($W_{1}$ and $W_{2}$). To find an acceleration, consider the forces affecting each individual mass. Using [Newton's second law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_second_law) (with a [sign convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_convention) of $m_1 > m_2$) derive a [system of equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simultaneous_equations) for the acceleration ($a$).

As a sign convention, assume that *a* is positive when downward for $m_1$ and upward for $m_2$. Weight of $m_1$ and $m_2$ is simply $W_1 = m_1 g$ and $W_2 = m_2 g$ respectively.

Forces affecting m<sub>1</sub>:

$$m_1 g - T = m_1 a$$

Forces affecting m<sub>2</sub>:

$$T - m_2 g = m_2 a$$

and adding the two previous equations yields

$$m_1 g - m_2 g = m_1 a + m_2 a,$$

and the concluding formula for acceleration

$$a = g \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}$$

The Atwood machine is sometimes used to illustrate the [Lagrangian method](wikipedia://en/23371726) of deriving equations of motion.[^o4]
