# Ad hoc hypothesis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ad hoc hypothesis"}

In [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science) and [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), an ***ad hoc* hypothesis** is a [hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis) added to a [theory](wikipedia://en/30746) in order to save it from being [falsified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falsified).

:::

For example, a person that wants to believe in [leprechauns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leprechaun) can avoid ever being proven wrong by using *ad hoc* hypotheses (e.g., by adding "they are invisible", then "their motives are complex", and so on).[^o2]

Often, *ad hoc* hypothesizing is employed to compensate for anomalies not anticipated by the theory in its unmodified form.

## In the scientific community {#In_the_scientific_community}

Scientists are often [skeptical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_skepticism) of theories that rely on frequent, unsupported adjustments to sustain them. This is because, if a theorist so chooses, there is no limit to the number of *ad hoc* hypotheses that they could add. Thus the theory becomes more and more complex, but is never falsified. This is often at a cost to the theory's [predictive power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predictive_power), however.[^o2] *Ad hoc* hypotheses are often characteristic of [pseudoscientific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoscientific) subjects.[^o4]

[Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736)'s addition of the [cosmological constant](wikipedia://en/38992) to [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) in order to allow a [static universe](wikipedia://en/3050160) was *ad hoc*. Although he later referred to it as his "greatest blunder", it may correspond to theories of [dark energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_energy).[^o5]
