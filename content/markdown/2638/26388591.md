# Arcsine laws (Wiener process)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Arcsine laws"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), the **arcsine laws** are a collection of results for one-dimensional [random walks](wikipedia://en/235451) and Brownian motion (the [Wiener process](wikipedia://en/149984)). The best known of these is attributed to (txt).

:::

All three laws relate path properties of the Wiener process to the [arcsine distribution](wikipedia://en/26400022). A random variable *X* on \[0,1\] is arcsine-distributed if

$$\Pr \left[ X \leq x \right] = \frac{2}{\pi} \arcsin\left(\sqrt{x}\right), \qquad \forall x \in [0,1].$$

::: {#o4 .statement cue=named-heading name="Statement of the laws"}

## Statement of the laws {#Statement_of_the_laws}

Throughout we suppose that (*W*<sub>*t*</sub>)<sub>0 ≤ *t* ≤ 1</sub> ∈ **R** is the one-dimensional Wiener process on \[0,1\]. [Scale invariance](wikipedia://en/149984#Self-similarity) ensures that the results can be generalised to Wiener processes run for *t* ∈\[0,∞).

::: {#o6 .statement cue=named-heading name="First (Lévy's) arcsine law"}

### First (Lévy's) arcsine law {#First_(Lévy's)_arcsine_law}

The first arcsine law states that the proportion of time that the one-dimensional Wiener process is positive follows an arcsine distribution. Let

$$T_+ = \left| \{\, t \in [0,1] \, \colon \, W_t > 0 \,\}\right|$$

be the [measure](wikipedia://en/18198) of the set of times in \[0,1\] at which the Wiener process is positive. Then $T_+$ is arcsine distributed.

:::

::: {#o8 .statement cue=named-heading name="Second arcsine law"}

### Second arcsine law {#Second_arcsine_law}

The second arcsine law describes the distribution of the last time the Wiener process changes sign. Let

$$L=\sup\left\{t \in [0,1] \, \colon \, W_t = 0 \right\}$$

be the time of the last zero. Then *L* is arcsine distributed.

:::

::: {#o10 .statement cue=named-heading name="Third arcsine law"}

### Third arcsine law {#Third_arcsine_law}

The third arcsine law states that the time at which a Wiener process achieves its maximum is arcsine distributed.

The statement of the law relies on the fact that the Wiener process has an almost surely unique maxima,[^o11] and so we can define the random variable *M* which is the time at which the maxima is achieved. i.e. the unique *M* such that

$$W_M = \sup \{ W_s \, \colon \, s \in [0,1] \}.$$

Then *M* is arcsine distributed.

:::

:::

::: {#o13 .statement cue=named-heading name="Equivalence of the second and third laws"}

## Equivalence of the second and third laws {#Equivalence_of_the_second_and_third_laws}

Defining the running maximum process *M*<sub>*t*</sub> of the Wiener process

$$M_t = \sup \{ W_s \, \colon \, s \in [0,t] \},$$

then the law of *X*<sub>*t*</sub> = *M*<sub>*t*</sub> − *W*<sub>*t*</sub> has the same law as a reflected Wiener process |*B*<sub>*t*</sub>| (where *B*<sub>*t*</sub> is a Wiener process independent of *W*<sub>*t*</sub>).[^o11]

Since the zeros of *B* and |*B*| coincide, the last zero of *X* has the same distribution as *L*, the last zero of the Wiener process. The last zero of *X* occurs exactly when *W* achieves its maximum.[^o11] It follows that the second and third laws are equivalent.

:::
