# Hierarchy of beliefs

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hierarchy of beliefs"}

The **hierarchy of beliefs** is a mathematical construct in [game theory](wikipedia://en/11924) used to model [incomplete information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incomplete_information) situations, where players are uncertain about other players' private information. Each player is modeled as having a privately known "type" that determines their preferences and beliefs, which in turn guide their strategic decisions. This approach builds upon [John Harsanyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Harsanyi)’s foundational work on games with [incomplete information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incomplete_information).

:::

In this framework, a player's first-order beliefs are [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) over other players’ types. Second-order beliefs are beliefs about others’ first-order beliefs, and this recursive structure continues indefinitely, forming a hierarchy of beliefs.

[Jean-François Mertens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-François_Mertens) and [Shmuel Zamir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shmuel_Zamir)’s key contribution in 1985 was the construction of a **universal type space**—a mathematical structure encompassing all possible hierarchies of beliefs consistent with the model.[^o2] This universal space enables a rigorous treatment of beliefs at all levels and provides a foundation for practical approximations using finite type spaces.

The concept has become central in [Bayesian game theory](wikipedia://en/1863423), with applications in [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), [AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence), and [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy). It is particularly useful in analyzing strategic interactions under [asymmetric information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymmetric_information) and [uncertainty](wikipedia://en/63778), and in exploring notions like [common knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_knowledge), as formalized by [Robert Aumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Aumann), and [induction puzzles](wikipedia://en/2062017) involving recursive reasoning.[^o3]
