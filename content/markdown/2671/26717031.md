# Osgood curve

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Osgood curve"}

*Image: Osgood curve.svg.* Example of an Osgood curve, constructed by recursively removing wedges from triangles. The wedge angles shrink exponentially, as does the fraction of area removed in each level, leaving nonzero area in the final curve.

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), an **Osgood curve** is a non-self-intersecting [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) that has positive [area](wikipedia://en/1209). Despite its area, it is not possible for such a curve to cover any [two-dimensional region](wikipedia://en/16796643), distinguishing them from [space-filling curves](wikipedia://en/352398). Osgood curves are named after [William Fogg Osgood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Fogg_Osgood).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition and properties"}

## Definition and properties {#Definition_and_properties}

A curve in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) is defined to be an Osgood curve when it is non-self-intersecting (that is, it is either a [Jordan curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_curve) or a [Jordan arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_arc)) and it has positive area.[^n1] More formally, it must have positive two-dimensional [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198).

Osgood curves have [Hausdorff dimension](wikipedia://en/14294) two, like [space-filling curves](wikipedia://en/352398). However, they cannot be space-filling curves: by [Netto's theorem](wikipedia://en/70997704), covering all of the points of the plane, or of any [two-dimensional region](wikipedia://en/16796643) of the plane, would lead to self-intersections.[^o5]

:::

## History {#History}

The first examples of Osgood curves were found by (txt) and (txt). Both examples have positive area in parts of the curve, but zero area in other parts; this flaw was corrected by (Knopp 1917), who found a curve that has positive area in every neighborhood of each of its points, based on an earlier construction of [Wacław Sierpiński](wikipedia://en/34122). Knopp's example has the additional advantage that its area can be made arbitrarily close to the area of its [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634).[^o10]

::: {#o12 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

It is possible to modify the recursive construction of certain [fractals](wikipedia://en/10913) and space-filling curves to obtain an Osgood curve.[^o13] For instance, Knopp's construction involves recursively splitting triangles into pairs of smaller triangles, meeting at a shared vertex, by removing triangular wedges. When each level of this construction removes the same fraction of the area of its triangles, the result is a [Cesàro fractal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesàro_fractal) such as the [Koch snowflake](wikipedia://en/46959). Instead, reducing the fraction of area removed per level, rapidly enough to leave a constant fraction of the area unremoved, produces an Osgood curve.[^o10]

Another way to construct an Osgood curve is to form a two-dimensional version of the [Smith–Volterra–Cantor set](wikipedia://en/922382), a [totally disconnected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_disconnected) point set with non-zero area, and then apply the [Denjoy–Riesz theorem](wikipedia://en/48130890) according to which every [bounded](wikipedia://en/48258) and totally disconnected subset of the plane is a subset of a Jordan curve.[^n2]

:::
