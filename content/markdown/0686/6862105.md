# End (topology)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="End"}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **ends** of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) are, roughly speaking, the [connected components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28topology%29) of the "ideal boundary" of the space. That is, each end represents a topologically distinct way to move to [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824) within the space. Adding a point at each end yields a [compactification](wikipedia://en/6617) of the original space, known as the **end compactification**.

:::

The notion of an end of a topological space was introduced by (txt).

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $X$ be a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), and suppose that

is an ascending sequence of [compact subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_subset) of $X$ whose [interiors](wikipedia://en/55610) [cover](wikipedia://en/317552) $X$. Then $X$ has one **end** for every sequence

where each $U_n$ is a [connected component](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28topology%29) of $X\setminus K_n$. The number of ends does not depend on the specific sequence $(K_i)$ of compact sets; there is a [natural](wikipedia://en/45022) [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between the sets of ends associated with any two such sequences.

Using this definition, a **neighborhood** of an end $(U_i)$ is an open set $V$ such that $V\supset U_n$ for some $n$. Such neighborhoods represent the neighborhoods of the corresponding point at infinity in the **end compactification** (this "compactification" is not always compact; the topological space *X* has to be connected and [locally connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_connected)).

The definition of ends given above applies only to spaces $X$ that possess an [exhaustion by compact sets](wikipedia://en/3521238) (that is, $X$ must be [hemicompact](wikipedia://en/5076058)). However, it can be generalized as follows: let $X$ be any topological space, and consider the [direct system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_system_%28mathematics%29) $\{K\}$ of compact subsets of $X$ and [inclusion maps](wikipedia://en/492516). There is a corresponding [inverse system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_system) $\{\pi_0(X\setminus K)\}$, where $\pi_0(Y)$ denotes the set of connected components of a space $Y$, and each inclusion map $Z\to Y$ induces a function $\pi_0(Y)\to\pi_0(Z)$. Then the **set of ends** of $X$ is defined to be the [inverse limit](wikipedia://en/15109) of this inverse system.

Under this definition, the set of ends is a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051), where morphisms are only *proper* continuous maps, to the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829). Explicitly, if $\varphi:X\to Y$ is a proper map and $x=(x_K)_K$ is an end of $X$ (i.e. each element $x_K$ in the family is a connected component of $X\setminus K$ and they are compatible with maps induced by inclusions) then $\varphi(x)$ is the family $\varphi_*(x_{\varphi^{-1}(K')})$ where $K'$ ranges over compact subsets of *Y* and $\varphi_*$ is the map induced by $\varphi$ from $\pi_0(X \setminus \varphi^{-1}(K'))$ to $\pi_0(Y \setminus K')$. Properness of $\varphi$ is used to ensure that each $\varphi^{-1}(K')$ is compact in $X$.

The original definition above represents the special case where the direct system of compact subsets has a [cofinal sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofinal_sequence).

## Examples {#Examples}

- The set of ends of any [compact space](wikipedia://en/6042) is the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566).
- The [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) $\mathbb{R}$ has two ends. For example, if we let *K*<sub>*n*</sub> be the [closed interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_interval) \[−*n*, *n*\], then the two ends are the sequences of open sets *U*<sub>*n*</sub> = (*n*, ∞) and *V*<sub>*n*</sub> = (−∞, −*n*). These ends are usually referred to as "infinity" and "minus infinity", respectively.
- If *n* > 1, then Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$ has only one end. This is because $\mathbb{R}^n \smallsetminus K$ has only one unbounded component for any compact set *K*.
- More generally, if *M* is a compact [manifold with boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold_with_boundary), then the number of ends of the interior of *M* is equal to the number of connected components of the boundary of *M*.
- The union of *n* distinct [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28mathematics%29) emanating from the origin in $\mathbb{R}^2$ has *n* ends.
- The [infinite complete binary tree](wikipedia://en/4321#Types_of_binary_trees) has uncountably many ends, corresponding to the uncountably many different descending paths starting at the root. (This can be seen by letting *K*<sub>*n*</sub> be the complete binary tree of depth *n*.) These ends can be thought of as the "leaves" of the infinite tree. In the end compactification, the set of ends has the topology of a [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172).

:::

## Ends of graphs and groups {#Ends_of_graphs_and_groups}

In [infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_graph) [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), an end is defined slightly differently, as an equivalence class of semi-infinite paths in the graph, or as a [haven](wikipedia://en/5133456), a function mapping finite sets of vertices to connected components of their complements. However, for locally finite graphs (graphs in which each vertex has finite [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448)), the ends defined in this way correspond one-for-one with the ends of topological spaces defined from the graph (Diestel Kühn 2003).

The ends of a [finitely generated group](wikipedia://en/963042) are defined to be the ends of the corresponding [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277); this definition is insensitive to the choice of generating set. Every finitely-generated infinite group has either 1, 2, or infinitely many ends, and [Stallings theorem about ends of groups](wikipedia://en/19196631) provides a decomposition for groups with more than one end.

## Ends of a CW complex {#Ends_of_a_CW_complex}

For a [path connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_connected) [CW-complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CW-complex), the ends can be characterized as [homotopy classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_classes) of [proper maps](wikipedia://en/1190262) $\mathbb{R}^+\to X$, called [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_%28mathematics%29) in *X*: more precisely, if between the restriction —to the subset $\mathbb{N}$— of any two of these maps exists a proper homotopy we say that they are equivalent and they define an equivalence class of proper rays. This set is called **an end** of *X*.
