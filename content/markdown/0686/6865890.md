# Vertex (curve)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Vertex"}

*Image: Ellipse evolute.svg.* An ellipse (red) and its [evolute](wikipedia://en/842387) (blue). The dots are the vertices of the curve, each corresponding to a cusp on the evolute.

In the geometry of [plane curves](wikipedia://en/971691), a **vertex** is a point of where the first derivative of [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) is zero.[^o2] This is typically a local [maximum or minimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxima_and_minima) of curvature,[^o6] and some authors define a vertex to be more specifically a [local extremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_extremum) of curvature.[^o3] However, other special cases may occur, for instance when the second derivative is also zero, or when the curvature is constant. For [space curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_curve), on the other hand, a **vertex** is a point where the [torsion](wikipedia://en/3039281) vanishes.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

A hyperbola has two vertices, one on each branch; they are the closest of any two points lying on opposite branches of the hyperbola, and they lie on the principal axis. On a parabola, the sole vertex lies on the axis of symmetry and in a quadratic of the form:

$$ax^2 + bx + c\,\!$$

it can be found by [completing the square](wikipedia://en/303500) or by [differentiation](wikipedia://en/7921).[^o6] On an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277#Vertex), two of the four vertices lie on the major axis and two lie on the minor axis.[^o7]

For a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), which has constant curvature, every point is a vertex.

:::

## Cusps and osculation {#Cusps_and_osculation}

Vertices are points where the curve has [4-point contact](wikipedia://en/396068#Contact_between_curves) with the [osculating circle](wikipedia://en/1896705) at that point.[^o9][^o10] In contrast, generic points on a curve typically only have 3-point contact with their osculating circle. The [evolute](wikipedia://en/842387) of a curve will generically have a [cusp](wikipedia://en/6851758) when the curve has a vertex;[^o10] other, more degenerate and non-stable singularities may occur at higher-order vertices, at which the osculating circle has contact of higher order than four.[^o9] Although a single generic curve will not have any higher-order vertices, they will generically occur within a one-parameter family of curves, at the curve in the family for which two ordinary vertices coalesce to form a higher vertex and then annihilate.

The [symmetry set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_set) of a curve has endpoints at the cusps corresponding to the vertices, and the [medial axis](wikipedia://en/4537039), a subset of the [symmetry set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_set), also has its endpoints in the cusps.

## Other properties {#Other_properties}

According to the classical [four-vertex theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-vertex_theorem), every simple closed planar smooth curve must have at least four vertices.[^o12] A more general fact is that every simple closed space curve which lies on the boundary of a convex body, or even bounds a locally convex disk, must have four vertices.[^o13] Every [curve of constant width](wikipedia://en/580238) must have at least six vertices.[^o14]

If a planar curve is [bilaterally symmetric](wikipedia://en/1390873), it will have a vertex at the point or points where the axis of symmetry crosses the curve. Thus, the notion of a vertex for a curve is closely related to that of an [optical vertex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex_%28optics%29), the point where an optical axis crosses a [lens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_%28optics%29) surface.
