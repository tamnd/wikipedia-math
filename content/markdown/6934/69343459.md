# Innovation method

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Innovation method"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **Innovation method** provides an [estimator](wikipedia://en/10043) for the [parameters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameters) of [stochastic differential equations](wikipedia://en/1361454) given a [time series](wikipedia://en/406624) of ([potentially noisy](wikipedia://en/19196523)) [observations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation) of the [state variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_variable). In the framework of continuous-discrete state space models, the **innovation estimator** is obtained by [maximizing the log-likelihood](wikipedia://en/140806) of the corresponding [discrete-time](wikipedia://en/40240263) innovation process with respect to the parameters. The innovation estimator can be classified as a [M-estimator](wikipedia://en/4225388), a [quasi-maximum likelihood estimator](wikipedia://en/17518635) or a [prediction error](wikipedia://en/3244288) estimator depending on the inferential considerations that want to be emphasized. The innovation method is a [system identification](wikipedia://en/552466) technique for developing mathematical models of [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087) from measured data and for the optimal [design of experiments](wikipedia://en/9541).

:::

## Background {#Background}

Stochastic differential equations (SDEs) have become an important mathematical tool for describing the [time evolution](wikipedia://en/937739) of several [random](wikipedia://en/19196523) [phenomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenon) in [natural, social and applied sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science). [Statistical inference](wikipedia://en/27577) for SDEs is thus of great importance in applications for model building, [model selection](wikipedia://en/3664073), [model identification](wikipedia://en/22884398) and [forecasting](wikipedia://en/246074). To carry out statistical inference for SDEs, [measurements](wikipedia://en/19022) of the [state variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_variable) of these random phenomena are indispensable. Usually, in practice, only a few state variables are measured by physical devices that introduce random measurement errors ([observational errors](wikipedia://en/22346)).

### Mathematical model for inference {#Mathematical_model_for_inference}

The innovation estimator.[^o4] for SDEs is defined in the framework of continuous-discrete state space models.[^o5] These models arise as natural mathematical representation of the temporal evolution of continuous random phenomena and their measurements in a succession of time instants. In the simplest formulation, these continuous-discrete models [^o5] are expressed in term of a SDE of the form

$$\qquad\qquad d\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{f} ( t,\mathbf{x}(t);\theta) dt +\sum_{i=1}^m \mathbf{g}_{i}\ (t, \mathbf{x}(t);\theta)\ d\mathbf{w}^{i}(t) \qquad \qquad (1)$$

describing the time evolution of $d$ state variables $\mathbf{x}$ of the phenomenon for all time instant $t \ge t_0$, and an observation equation

$$\qquad\qquad \mathbf{z}_{t_k} = \mathbf{Cx}(t_k)+\mathbf{e}_{t_k} \qquad\qquad (2)$$

describing the time series of measurements $\mathbf{z}_{t_0},...,\mathbf{z}_{t_{M-1}}$of at least one of the variables $\mathbf{x}$ of the random phenomenon on $M$ time instants $t_0 \ ,..., \ t_{M-1}$. In the model (1)-(2), $\mathbf{f}$ and $\mathbf{g}_i$ are [differentiable functions](wikipedia://en/330206), $\mathbf{w}=(\mathbf{w}^1,...,\mathbf{w}^m)$ is an $m$-dimensional standard [Wiener process](wikipedia://en/149984), $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^p$ is a vector of $p$ parameters, $\{\mathbf{e}_{t_k} : \mathbf{e}_{t_k}\sim \mathrm{N} ( 0,\Pi_{t_k}) \}_{k=0,...,M-1}$ is a sequence of $r$-dimensional [i.i.d.](wikipedia://en/30876902) [Gaussian random vectors](wikipedia://en/21462) independent of $\mathbf{w}$, $\Pi_{t_k}$ an $r \times r$ [positive definite matrix](wikipedia://en/40326), and $\mathbf{C}$ an $r \times d$ matrix.

### Statistical problem to solve {#Statistical_problem_to_solve}

Once the dynamics of a phenomenon is described by a state equation as (1) and the way of measurement the state variables specified by an observation equation as (2), the inference problem to solve is the following:[^o4][^o7] given $M$ partial and noisy observations $\mathbf{z}_{t_0},...,\mathbf{z}_{t_{M-1}}$ of the stochastic process $\mathbf{x}$ on the observation times $t_0,...,t_{M-1}$, estimate the unobserved state variable of $\mathbf{x}$ and the unknown parameters $\theta$ in (1) that better fit to the given observations.

## Discrete-time innovation process {#Discrete-time_innovation_process}

Let $\{t\}_M$ be the sequence of $M$ observation times $t_0,\ldots,t_{M-1}$ of the states of (1), and $Z_\rho =\{ \mathbf{z}_{t_k} : t_k\leq \rho, t_k \in \{ t \}_M \}$ the time series of partial and noisy measurements of $\mathbf{x}$ described by the observation equation (2).

Further, let $\mathbf{x}_{t/\rho} = \mathrm{E} (\mathbf{x}(t)|Z_\rho)$ and $\mathbf{U}_{t/\rho }=E(\mathbf{x}(t)\mathbf{x}^{\intercal }(t)|Z_{\rho })-\mathbf{x}_{t/\rho }\mathbf{x}_{t/\rho }^{\intercal }$ be the [conditional mean and variance](wikipedia://en/435099) of $\mathbf{x}$ with $\rho \leq t$, where $E (\cdot)$ denotes the [expected value](wikipedia://en/21462) of random vectors.

The random sequence $\{ \nu_{t_k}\} _{k=1,\ldots,M-1},$ with

$$\qquad \qquad \nu_{t_k} = \mathbf{z}_{t_k}- \mathbf{Cx}_{{t_k}/{t_{k-1}}}(\theta), \qquad \qquad (3)$$

defines the discrete-time innovation process,[^o9][^o4][^o14] where $\nu_{t_k}$ is proved to be an independent [normally distributed](wikipedia://en/21462) random vector with zero mean and variance

$$\qquad \qquad \Sigma_{t_k}= \mathbf{CU}_{{t_k}/t_{k-1}}(\theta)\ \mathbf{C}^\intercal + \Pi_{t_k}, \qquad \qquad (4)$$

for small enough $\Delta=\underset{k}{\max } \{t_{k+1}-t_k\}$, with $t_k,t_{k+1} \in \{t\}_{M}$. In practice,[^o10] this distribution for the discrete-time innovation is valid when, with a suitable selection of both, the number $M$ of observations and the time distance $t_{k+1}-t_k$ between consecutive observations, the time series of observations $\mathbf{z}_{t_0},...,\mathbf{z}_{t_{M-1}}$ of the SDE contains the main information about the continuous-time process $\mathbf{x}$. That is, when the [sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampling_%28signal_processing%29) of the continuous-time process $\mathbf{x}$ has low distortion ([aliasing](wikipedia://en/151474)) and when there is a suitable [signal-noise ratio](wikipedia://en/41706).

## Innovation estimator {#Innovation_estimator}

The innovation estimator for the parameters of the SDE (1) is the one that maximizes the [likelihood function](wikipedia://en/44968) of the discrete-time innovation process $\{\nu_{t_k}\}_ {k=1,\ldots,M-1}$ with respect to the parameters.[^o4] More precisely, given $M$ measurements $Z_{t_{M-1}}$of the state space model (1)-(2) with $\theta = \theta_0$ on $\{t\}_M,$ the **innovation estimator** for the parameters $\theta_0$ of (1) is defined by

$$\qquad\qquad \hat{\theta}_M = \operatorname\arg\{\underset{\theta}{\min} \ U_M(\theta, Z_{t_{M-1}})\}, \qquad\qquad (5)$$

where

$$\qquad\qquad U_M(\theta,Z_{t_{M-1}}) = (M-1)\ln(2\pi)+\sum_{k=1}^{M-1} \ln(\det(\Sigma_{t_k})) + \nu_{t_k}^\intercal\Sigma_{t_k}^{-1}\nu_{t_k},$$

being $\nu_{t{_k}}$the discrete-time innovation (3) and $\Sigma_{t_k}$the innovation variance (4) of the model (1)-(2) at $t_k$, for all $k=1,...,M-1.$ In the above expression for $U_M (\theta, Z_{t_{M-1}}),$ the conditional mean $\mathbf{x}_{t_k/t_{k-1}}(\theta)$ and variance $\mathbf{U}_{t_k/t_{k-1}}(\theta)$ are computed by the continuous-discrete [filtering](wikipedia://en/12433418) algorithm for the evolution of the moments (Section 6.4 in[^o5]), for all $k=1,...,M-1.$

### Differences with the maximum likelihood estimator {#Differences_with_the_maximum_likelihood_estimator}

The maximum likelihood estimator of the parameters $\theta$ in the model (1)-(2) involves the evaluation of the - usually unknown - [transition density function](wikipedia://en/166896) $p_\theta (t_{k+1}- t_k, \mathbf{x}(t_k), \mathbf{x}(t_{k+1}))$ between the states $\mathbf{x}(t_k)$ and $\mathbf{x}(t_{k+1})$ of the [diffusion process](wikipedia://en/1667059) $\mathbf{x}$ for all the observation times $t_k$ and $t_{k+1}$.[^o13] Instead of this, the innovation estimator (5) is obtained by maximizing the likelihood of the discrete-time innovation process $\{ \nu_{t_k} \}_{k=1,...,M-1} ,$ taking into account that $\nu _{t_1},...,\nu _{t_{M-1}}$ are Gaussian and independent random vectors. Remarkably, whereas the transition density function $p_{\theta}(t_{k+1}-t_k, \mathbf{x}(t_k),\mathbf{x}(t_{k+1}))$ changes when the SDE for $\mathbf{x}$ does, the transition density function $\mathfrak{p}_\theta(t_{k+1}-t_k, \nu_{t_{k}},\nu_{t_{k+1}} )$ for the innovation process remains Gaussian independently of the SDEs for $\mathbf{x}$. Only in the case that the diffusion $\mathbf{x}$ is described by a linear SDE with additive noise, the density function $p_\theta(t_{k+1}-t_k, \mathbf{x}(t_k),\mathbf{x}(t_{k+1}))$ is Gaussian and equal to $\mathfrak{p}_\theta(t_{k+1}-t_k, \nu_{t_k}, \nu_{t_{k+1}}),$ and so the maximum likelihood and the innovation estimator coincide.[^o14] Otherwise,[^o14] the innovation estimator is an approximation to the maximum likelihood estimator and, in this sense, the innovation estimator is a Quasi-Maximum Likelihood estimator. In addition, the innovation method is a particular instance of the Prediction Error method according to the definition given in.[^o15] Therefore, the asymptotic results obtained in for that general class of estimators are valid for the innovation estimators.[^o4][^o16][^o17] Intuitively, by following the typical control engineering viewpoint, it is expected that the innovation process - viewed as a measure of the prediction errors of the fitted model - be approximately a white noise process when the models fit the data,[^o18][^o7] which can be used as a practical tool for designing of models and for [optimal experimental design](wikipedia://en/1292142).[^o10]

::: {#o20 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

The **innovation estimator** (5) has a number of important attributes:

- Under conventional regularity conditions, the innovation estimator (5) is [consistent](wikipedia://en/1497569) and [asymptoticaly normal distributed](wikipedia://en/3001287).[^o4][^o17][^o21]
- For [selecting models](wikipedia://en/3664073),[^o18] the maximum log-likelihood $-U_{M,h} ( \widehat{\theta}_M, Z_{t_{M-1}} )$ of the innovation estimator (5) can be used to compute the [Akaike or Bayesian information criterion](wikipedia://en/690512).
- The $100 (1-\alpha)\%$ [confidence limits](wikipedia://en/280911) $\widehat{\theta}_M\pm\bigtriangleup$ for the innovation estimator $\widehat{\theta}_M$ is estimated with[^o10]

$$\ \qquad \qquad \bigtriangleup = t_{1-\alpha, M-\rho-1}\sqrt{\frac{diag(Var(\widehat{\theta}_M))}{M-p}},$$

where $t_{1-\alpha, M-p-1}$ is the [t-student distribution](wikipedia://en/105375) with $100 (1-\alpha)\%$ [significance level](wikipedia://en/160995), and $M-p-1$ [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321606) . Here, $\text{V} ar (\widehat{\theta}_M)= (I (\widehat{\theta}_M))^{-1}$ denotes the variance of the innovation estimator $\widehat{\theta}_M$, where

$$\ \qquad \qquad I(\widehat{\theta }_{M})=\sum_{k=1}^{M-1}I_{k}(\widehat{\theta }_{M})$$

is the [Fisher Information matrix](wikipedia://en/598971) the innovation estimator $\widehat{\theta }_{M}$ of $\theta _{0}$ and

$$\qquad \qquad \lbrack I_{k}(\widehat{\theta }_{M})]_{m,n}=\frac{\partial \mu ^{\intercal }}{\partial \theta _{m}}\Sigma ^{-1}\frac{\partial \mu }{\partial \theta _{n}} +\frac{1}{2}trace(\Sigma ^{-1}\frac{\partial \Sigma }{\partial \theta _{m}}\Sigma ^{-1}\frac{\partial \Sigma }{\partial \theta _{n}})$$

is the entry $(m,n)$ of the matrix $I_{k}(\widehat{\theta }_{M})$ with $\mu = \mathbf{Cx}_{t_{k}/t_{k-1}}(\widehat{\theta }_{M})$ and $\Sigma =\mathbf{\Sigma }_{t_{k}}(\widehat{\theta }_{M})$, for $1\leq m,n\leq p$.

- The [distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of the fitting-innovation process $\{\mathbf{\nu }_{t_{k}}:\mathbf{\nu }_{t_{k}}=\mathbf{z}_{t_{k}}-\mathbf{Cx} _{t_{k}/t_{k-1}}(\widehat{\theta }_{M})\}_{k=1,\ldots M-1}$ measures the [goodness of fit](wikipedia://en/2474821) of the model to the data.[^o4][^o7][^o18][^o10]
- For smooth enough function $\mathbf{h}$, nonlinear observation equations of the form

$$\qquad \qquad \mathbf{z}_{t_{k}}=\mathbf{h}(t_{k}\text{, }\mathbf{x}(t_{k}))+\mathbf{e}_{t_{k}}, \qquad \qquad (6)$$

can be transformed to the simpler one (2), and the innovation estimator (5) can be applied.[^o14]

:::

## Approximate Innovation estimators {#Approximate_Innovation_estimators}

In practice, close form expressions for computing $\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}/t_{k-1}}(\theta )$ and $\mathbf{U}_{t_{k}/t_{k-1}}(\theta )$ in (5) are only available for a few models (1)-(2). Therefore, approximate filtering algorithms as the following are used in applications.

Given $M$ measurements $Z_{t_{M-1}}$ and the initial filter estimates $\mathbf{y}_{t_{0}/t_{0}}=\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}/t_{0}}$, $\mathbf{V}_ {t_{0}/t_{0}}=\mathbf{U}_{t_{0}/t_{0}}$, the **approximate Linear Minimum Variance (LMV) filter** for the model (1)-(2) is iteratively defined at each observation time $t_{k}\in \{t\}_{M}$ by the prediction estimates[^o5][^o23]

$\qquad \qquad \mathbf{y}_{t_{k+1}/t_{k}}=E(\mathbf{y}(t_{k+1})|Z_{t_{k}}) \quad$ and $\quad \mathbf{V}_{t_{k+1}/t_{k}}=E(\mathbf{y}(t_{k+1})\mathbf{y} ^{\intercal }(t_{k+1})|Z_{t_{k}})-\mathbf{y}_{t_{k+1}/t_{k}}\mathbf{y}_{t_{k+1}/t_{k}}^{\intercal }, \qquad (7)$

with initial conditions $\mathbf{y}_{t_{k}/t_{k}}$ and $\mathbf{V}_{t_{k}/t_{k}}$, and the filter estimates

$\qquad \qquad \mathbf{y}_{t_{k+1}/t_{k+1}}=\mathbf{y}_{t_{k+1}/t_{k}}+\mathbf{K}_{t_{k+1}} \mathbf{(\mathbf{z}}_{t_{k+1}}-\mathbf{\mathbf{C}y}_{t_{k+1}/t_{k}}\mathbf{)} \quad$ and $\quad \mathbf{V}_{t_{k+1}/t_{k+1}}=\mathbf{V}_{t_{k+1}/t_{k}}-\mathbf{K}_{t_{k+1}}\mathbf{CV}_{t_{k+1}/t_{k}} \qquad (8)$

with filter gain

$$\qquad \qquad \mathbf{K}_{t_{k+1}}=\mathbf{V}_{t_{k+1}/t_{k}}\mathbf{C}^{\intercal } \mathbf{CV}_{t_{k+1}/t_{k}} (\mathbf{C}^{\intercal }+\mathbf{\Pi }_{t_{k+1}})^{-1}$$

for all $t_{k},t_{k+1}\in \{t\}_{M}$, where $\mathbf{y}$ is an approximation to the solution $\mathbf{x}$ of (1) on the observation times $\{t\}_{M}$.

Given $M$ measurements $Z_{t_{M-1}}$ of the state space model (1)-(2) with $\mathbf{\theta =\theta }_{0}$ on $\{t\}_{M}$, the **approximate innovation estimator** for the parameters $\mathbf{\theta }_{0}$ of (1) is defined by[^o4][^o21]

$$\qquad \qquad \widehat{\mathbf{\vartheta }}_{M}=\arg \{\underset{\mathbf{\theta \in } \mathcal{D}_{\theta }}{\mathbf{\min }}\text{ }\widetilde{U}_{M}\mathbf{(\theta },Z_{t_{M-1}})\}, \qquad \qquad (9)$$

where

$$\qquad \qquad \widetilde{U}_{M}(\mathbf{\theta },Z_{t_{M-1}})=(M-1)\ln (2\pi)+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{M-1}\ln (\det (\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma }}_{t_{k}}))+\widetilde{\mathbf{\nu }}_{t_{k}}^{\intercal }(\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma }}_{t_{k}})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{\nu }}_{t_{k}},$$

being

$\qquad \qquad \widetilde{\mathbf{\nu }}_{t_{k}}=\mathbf{z}_{t_{k}}-\mathbf{Cy}_{t_{k}/t_{k-1}}(\theta ) \qquad$ and $\qquad \widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma }}_{t_{k}}=\mathbf{CV}_{t_{k}/t_{k-1}}(\theta )\mathbf{C}^{\intercal }+\mathbf{\Pi }_{t_{k}}$

approximations to the discrete-time innovation (3) and innovation variance (4), respectively, resulting from the filtering algorithm (7)-(8).

For models with complete observations free of noise (i.e., with $\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{I}$ and $\mathbf{\Pi }_{t_{k}}=0$ in (2)), the approximate innovation estimator (9) reduces to the known Quasi-Maximum Likelihood estimators for SDEs.[^o21]

### Main conventional-type estimators {#Main_conventional-type_estimators}

Conventional-type innovation estimators are those (9) derived from conventional-type continuous-discrete or discrete-discrete approximate filtering algorithms. With approximate continuous-discrete filters there are the innovation estimators based on Local Linearization (LL) filters,[^o4][^o25][^o14] on the [extended Kalman filter](wikipedia://en/18436459),[^o26][^o27] and on the [second order filters](wikipedia://en/18436459).[^o7][^o27] Approximate innovation estimators based on discrete-discrete filters result from the discretization of the SDE (1) by means of a numerical scheme.[^o28][^o29] Typically, the effectiveness of these innovation estimators is directly related to the [stability](wikipedia://en/233807) of the involved filtering algorithms.

A shared drawback of these conventional-type filters is that, once the observations are given, the error between the approximate and the exact innovation process is fixed and completely settled by the time distance between observations.[^o21] This might set a large [bias](wikipedia://en/184099) of the approximate innovation estimators in some applications, bias that cannot be corrected by increasing the number of observations. However, the conventional-type innovation estimators are useful in many practical situations for which only medium or low [accuracy](wikipedia://en/41932) for the parameter estimation is required.[^o21]

### Order-β innovation estimators {#Order-β_innovation_estimators}

Let us consider the finer time discretization $\left( \tau \right) _{h>0}=\{\tau _{n}:\tau _{n+1}-\tau _{n}\leq h\text{ for }n=0,1,\ldots ,N\}$ of the time interval $[t_{0},t_{M-1}]$ satisfying the condition $\left( \tau\right) _{h}\supset \{t\}_{M}$. Further, let $\mathbf{y}_n$ be the approximate value of $\mathbf{x}(\tau_n)$ obtained from a discretization of the equation (1) for all $\left(\tau \right)_{h}$, and

$\qquad \qquad \mathbf{y}=\{\mathbf{y}(t),t\in \lbrack t_{0},t_{M-1}] : \mathbf{y}(\tau _{n})=\mathbf{y}_{n}, \quad$ for all $\quad \tau _{n}\in \left( \tau \right) _{h}\} \qquad \qquad (10)$

a continuous-time approximation to $\mathbf{x}$.

A **order-**$\beta$ **LMV filter**.[^o23] is an approximate LMV filter for which $\mathbf{y}$ is an **order-**$\beta$ **weak approximation** to $\mathbf{x}$ satisfying (10) and the [weak convergence condition](wikipedia://en/50723)

$$\qquad \qquad \underset{t_{k}\leq t\leq t_{k+1}}{\sup }\left\vert E\left( g(\mathbf{x} (t))|Z_{t_{k}}\right) -E\left( g(\mathbf{y}(t))|Z_{t_{k}}\right) \right\vert \leq L_{k}h^{\beta }$$

for all $t_{k},t_{k+1}\in \{t\}_{M}$ and any $2(\beta +1)$ times continuously differentiable functions $g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ for which $g$ and all its partial derivatives up to order $2(\beta +1)$ have polynomial growth, being $L_{k}$ a positive constant. This order-$\beta$ LMV filter converges with rate $\beta$ to the exact LMV filter as $h$ goes to zero,[^o23] where $h$ is the maximum stepsize of the time discretization $(\tau )_{h}\supset \{t\}_{M}$ on which the approximation $\mathbf{y}$ to $\mathbf{x}$ is defined.

A **order-**$\beta$ **innovation estimator** is an approximate innovation estimator (9) for which the approximations to the discrete-time innovation (3) and innovation variance (4), respectively, resulting from an order-$\beta$ LMV filter.[^o21]

Approximations $\mathbf{y}$ of any kind converging to $\mathbf{x}$ in a weak sense (as, e.g., those in [^o31][^o23]) can be used to design an order-$\beta$ LMV filter and, consequently, an order-$\beta$ innovation estimator. These order-$\beta$ innovation estimators are intended for the recurrent practical situation in which a diffusion process should be identified from a reduced number of observations distant in time or when high accuracy for the estimated parameters is required.

::: {#o33 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

#### Properties {#Properties}

An **order-**$\beta$ **innovation estimator** $\widehat{\mathbf{\theta }}_{M}(h)$ has a number of important properties:[^o21][^o10]

- For each given data $Z_{t_{M-1}}$ of $M$ observations, $\widehat{\mathbf{\theta }}_{M}(h)$ converges to the exact innovation estimator $\widehat{\mathbf{\theta }}_{M}$ as the maximum stepsize $h$ of the time discretization $\left( \tau \right) _{h}\supset \{t\}_{M}$ goes to zero.
- For finite [samples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_%28statistics%29) of $M$ observations, the expected value of $\widehat{\mathbf{\theta }}_{M}(h)$ converges to the expected value of the exact innovation estimator $\widehat{\mathbf{\theta }}_{M}$ as $h$ goes to zero.
- For an increasing number of observations, $\widehat{\mathbf{\theta }}_{M}(h)$ is asymptotically normal distributed and its bias decreases when $h$ goes to zero.
- Likewise to the convergence of the order-$\beta$ LMV filter to the exact LMV filter, for the convergence and asymptotic properties of $\widehat{\mathbf{\theta }}_{M}(h)$ there are no constraints on the time distance $t_{k+1}-t_{k}$ between two consecutive observations $\mathbf{z}_{t_{k}}$ and $\mathbf{z}_{t_{k+1}}$, nor on the time discretization $(\tau )_{h} \supset \{t\}_{M}.$
- Approximations for the Akaike or Bayesian information criterion and confidence limits are directly obtained by replacing the exact estimator $\widehat{\mathbf{\theta }}_{M}$ by its approximation $\widehat{\mathbf{\theta }}_{M}(h)$. These approximations converge to the corresponding exact one when the maximum stepsize $h$ of the time discretization $\left( \tau \right) _{h}\supset \{t\}_{M}$ goes to zero.
- The distribution of the approximate fitting-innovation process $\{\widetilde{\mathbf{\nu }}_{t_{k}}:\widetilde{\mathbf{\nu }}_{t_{k}}=\mathbf{z}_{t_{k}}-\mathbf{Cy}_{t_{k}/t_{k-1}}(\widehat{\theta }_{M}(h))\}_{k=1,\ldots M-1}$ measures the goodness of fit of the model to the data, which is also used as a practical tool for designing of models and for optimal experimental design.
- For smooth enough function $\mathbf{h}$, nonlinear observation equations of the form (6) can be transformed to the simpler one (2), and the order-$\beta$ innovation estimator can be applied.

#### Deterministic approximations {#Deterministic_approximations}

The order-$\beta$ innovation estimators overcome the drawback of the conventional-type innovation estimators concerning the impossibility of reducing bias.[^o21] However, the viable bias reduction of an order-$\beta$ innovation estimators might eventually require that the associated order-$\beta$ LMV filter performs a large number of stochastic simulations.[^o23] In situations where only low or medium precision approximate estimators are needed, an alternative deterministic filter algorithm - called deterministic order-$\beta$ LMV filter [^o23] - can be obtained by tracking the first two conditional moments $\mu$ and $\Lambda$ of the order-$\beta$ weak approximation $\mathbf{y}$ at all the time instants $\tau _{n}\in \left( \tau \right) _{h}$ in between two consecutive observation times $t_{k}$ and $t_{k+1}$. That is, the value of the predictions $\mathbf{y}_{t_{k+1}/t_{k}}$ and $\mathbf{P}_{t_{k+1}/t_{k}}$ in the filtering algorithm are computed from the recursive formulas

$\qquad \qquad \mathbf{y}_{\tau _{n+1}/t_{k}}=\mu (\tau _{n},\mathbf{y}_{\tau_{n}/t_{k}};h_{n})\quad$ and $\quad \mathbf{P}_{\tau_{n+1}/t_{k}}=\Lambda (\tau _{n},\mathbf{P}_{\tau _{n}/t_{k}};h_{n}),\quad$ with $\tau _{n},\tau _{n+1}\in (\tau )_{h}\cap \lbrack t_{k,}t_{k+1}],$

and with $h_n=\tau _{n+1}-\tau _{n}$. The approximate innovation estimators $\widehat{\mathbf{\theta }}_{h,M}$ defined with these deterministic order-$\beta$ LMV filters not longer converge to the exact innovation estimator, but allow a significant bias reduction in the estimated parameters for a given finite sample with a lower computational cost.

## Software {#Software}

A [Matlab](wikipedia://en/20412) implementation of various approximate innovation estimators is provided by the **SdeEstimation toolbox**.[^o36] This toolbox has Local Linearization filters, including deterministic and stochastic options with fixed step sizes and sample numbers. It also offers adaptive time stepping and sampling algorithms, along with local and [global optimization](wikipedia://en/563854) algorithms for innovation estimation. For models with complete observations free of noise, various approximations to the Quasi-Maximum Likelihood estimator are implemented in [R](wikipedia://en/376707).[^o37]
