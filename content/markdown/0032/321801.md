# Multiply perfect number

*Image: Multiply perfect number Cuisenaire rods 6.png.* Demonstration, with [Cuisenaire rods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuisenaire_rods), of the 2-perfection of the number 6

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Multiply perfect number"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **multiply perfect number** (also called **multiperfect number** or **pluperfect number**) is a generalization of a [perfect number](wikipedia://en/23670).

:::

For a given [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) *k*, a number *n* is called *k*-perfect (or *k*-fold perfect) if the sum of all positive [divisors](wikipedia://en/49492) of *n* (the [divisor function](wikipedia://en/407249), *σ*(*n*)) is equal to *kn*; a number is thus [perfect](wikipedia://en/23670) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is 2-perfect. A number that is *k*-perfect for a certain *k* is called a multiply perfect number. , *k*-perfect numbers are known to exist for each value of *k* up to 11.[^o6]

It is unknown whether there are any [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) multiply perfect numbers other than 1. The first few multiply perfect numbers are:

1, 6, 28, 120, 496, 672, 8128, 30240, 32760, 523776, 2178540, 23569920, 33550336, 45532800, 142990848, 459818240, 14182439040, 4364925090, 8589869056 ... .

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

The sum of the divisors of 120 is

1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 + 8 + 10 + 12 + 15 + 20 + 24 + 30 + 40 + 60 + 120 = 360

which is 3 × 120. Therefore 120 is a 3-perfect number.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

It can be [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) that:

- For a given [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) *p*, if *n* is *p*-perfect and *p* does not divide *n*, then *pn* is (*p* + 1)-perfect. This implies that an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *n* is a 3-perfect number divisible by 2 but not by 4, if and only if *n*/2 is an odd [perfect number](wikipedia://en/23670), of which none are known.
- If 3*n* is 4*k*-perfect and 3 does not divide *n*, then *n* is 3*k*-perfect.

:::

## Odd multiply perfect numbers {#Odd_multiply_perfect_numbers}

::: {#o10 .problem cue=unsolved}

Are there any odd multiply perfect numbers?

:::

It is unknown whether there are any odd multiply perfect numbers other than 1. However if an odd *k*-perfect number *n* exists where *k* > 2, then it must satisfy the following conditions:[^o14]

- The largest prime factor is ≥ 100129
- The second largest prime factor is ≥ 1009
- The third largest prime factor is ≥ 101

If an odd triperfect number exists, it must be greater than 10<sup>128</sup>.[^o11]

Tóth found several numbers that would be odd multiperfect, if one of their factors was a square. An example is 8999757, which would be an odd multiperfect number, if only one of its prime factors, 61, was a square.[^o12] This is closely related to the concept of [Descartes numbers](wikipedia://en/40093498).

## Bounds {#Bounds}

In [little-o notation](wikipedia://en/44578#Little-o_notation), the number of multiply perfect numbers less than *x* is $o(x^\varepsilon)$ for all ε > 0.[^o14]

The number of *k*-perfect numbers *n* for *n* ≤ *x* is less than $cx^{c'\log\log\log x/\log\log x}$, where *c* and *c**are constants independent of*****k*.[^o14]***

Under the assumption of the [Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/19344125), the following [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489) is true for all *k*-perfect numbers *n*, where *k* > 3

$$\log\log n > k\cdot e^{-\gamma}$$

where $\gamma$ is [Euler's gamma constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Mascheroni_constant). This can be proven using [Robin's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin's_theorem).

The [number of divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_of_divisors) τ(*n*) of a *k*-perfect number *n*, where *k* > 2, satisfies the inequality[^o15]

$$\tau(n) > e^{k - \gamma}.$$

The [number of distinct prime factors](wikipedia://en/57197043) ω(*n*) of *n* satisfies[^o16]

$$\omega(n) \ge k^2-1, ~~ \text{if }n\text{ is odd}$$

$$\omega(n) \ge k^2/4, ~~ \text{if }n\text{ is even}$$

If the distinct prime factors of *n* are $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_r$, then:[^o17]

$$r \left(\sqrt[r]{3/2} - 1\right) < \sum_{i=1}^{r} \frac{1}{p_i} < r \left(1 - \sqrt[r]{6/(k\pi^2)}\right), ~~ \text{if }n\text{ is even}$$

$$r \left(\sqrt[3r]{k^2} - 1\right) < \sum_{i=1}^{r} \frac{1}{p_i} < r \left(1 - \sqrt[r]{8/(k\pi^2)}\right), ~~ \text{if }n\text{ is odd}$$

## Specific values of *k* {#Specific_values_of_k}

### Perfect numbers {#Perfect_numbers}

A number *n* with σ(*n*) = 2*n* is **perfect**.

::: {#o21 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Triperfect"}

### Triperfect numbers {#Triperfect_numbers}

A number *n* with σ(*n*) = 3*n* is **triperfect**. There are only six known triperfect numbers and these are believed to comprise all such numbers:

120, 672, 523776, 459818240, 1476304896, 51001180160

If there exists an odd perfect number *m* (a famous [open problem](wikipedia://en/1333335)) then 2*m* would be 3-perfect, since σ(2*m*) = σ(2)σ(*m*) = 3×2*m*. An odd triperfect number must be a [square number](wikipedia://en/213919) exceeding 10<sup>70</sup> and have at least 12 distinct prime factors, the largest exceeding 10<sup>5</sup>.[^o22]

:::

## Variations {#Variations}

### Unitary multiply perfect numbers {#Unitary_multiply_perfect_numbers}

A similar extension can be made for [unitary perfect numbers](wikipedia://en/553027). A positive integer *n* is called a **unitary multi** *k*-**perfect** **number** if σ<sup>\*</sup>(*n*) = *kn* where σ<sup>\*</sup>(*n*) is the sum of its [unitary divisors](wikipedia://en/5488222). A **unitary multiply perfect number** is a unitary multi *k*-perfect number for some positive integer *k*. A unitary multi 2-perfect number is also called a **unitary perfect number**.

In the case *k* > 2, no example of a unitary multi *k*-perfect number is yet known. It is known that if such a number exists, it must be [even](wikipedia://en/143135) and greater than 10<sup>102</sup> and must have at least 45 odd prime factors.[^o26]

The first few unitary multiply perfect numbers are:

1, 6, 60, 90, 87360

### Bi-unitary multiply perfect numbers {#Bi-unitary_multiply_perfect_numbers}

A positive integer *n* is called a **bi-unitary multi** *k*-**perfect** **number** if σ<sup>\*\*</sup>(*n*) = *kn* where σ<sup>\*\*</sup>(*n*) is the sum of its [bi-unitary divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi-unitary_divisor). A **bi-unitary multiply perfect number** is a bi-unitary multi *k*-perfect number for some positive integer *k*.[^o28] A bi-unitary multi 2-perfect number is also called a **bi-unitary perfect number**, and a bi-unitary multi 3-perfect number is called a **bi-unitary triperfect number**.

In 1987, Peter Hagis proved that there are no odd bi-unitary multiperfect numbers other than 1.[^o28]

In 2020, Haukkanen and Sitaramaiah studied bi-unitary triperfect numbers of the form 2<sup>*a*</sup>*u* where *u* is odd. They completely resolved the cases 1 ≤ *a* ≤ 6 and *a* = 8, and partially resolved the case *a* = 7.[^o29][^o30][^o31][^o32][^o33][^o34]

In 2024, Tomohiro Yamada proved that 2160 is the only bi-unitary triperfect number divisible by 27 = 3<sup>3</sup>.[^o35] This means that Yamada found all biunitary triperfect numbers of the form 3<sup>*a*</sup>*u* with 3 ≤ *a* and *u* not divisible by 3.

The first few bi-unitary multiply perfect numbers are:

1, 6, 60, 90, 120, 672, 2160, 10080, 22848, 30240

:::
