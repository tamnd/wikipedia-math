# Sine wave

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sine wave"}

*Image: Sine and cosine animation.gif.* Tracing the y component of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) while going around the circle results in a sine wave (red). Tracing the x component results in a [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine) wave (blue). Both waves are sinusoids of the same frequency but different phases.

A **sine wave**, **sinusoidal wave**, or **sinusoid** (symbol: **∿**) is a [periodic wave](wikipedia://en/76848) whose [waveform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waveform) (shape) is the [trigonometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function) [sine function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine). In [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics), as a linear [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion) over time, this is *[simple harmonic motion](wikipedia://en/28437)*; as [rotation](wikipedia://en/39789), it corresponds to *[uniform circular motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_circular_motion)*. Sine waves occur often in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), including [wind waves](wikipedia://en/710251), [sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound) waves, and [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light) waves, such as [monochromatic radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monochromatic_radiation). In [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324), and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), [Fourier analysis](wikipedia://en/11659) decomposes general functions into a sum of sine waves of various frequencies, relative phases, and magnitudes.

:::

When any two sine waves of the same [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779) (but arbitrary [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29)) are [linearly combined](wikipedia://en/55632), the result is another sine wave of the same frequency; this property is unique among periodic waves. Conversely, if some phase is chosen as a zero reference, a sine wave of arbitrary phase can be written as the linear combination of two sine waves with phases of zero and a quarter cycle, the *sine* and *cosine* [components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_component), respectively.

## Audio example {#Audio_example}

A sine wave represents a single frequency with no [harmonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic) and is considered an [acoustically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustics) [pure tone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_tone).[^o3][^o4] Adding sine waves of different frequencies results in a different waveform. Presence of higher harmonics in addition to the [fundamental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_frequency) causes variation in the [timbre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbre), which is the reason why the same [musical pitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_%28music%29) played on different instruments sounds different.

## Sinusoid form {#Sinusoid_form}

Sine waves of arbitrary phase and amplitude are called *sinusoids* and have the general form:[^o6]

$$y(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \varphi) = A\sin(2 \pi f t + \varphi)$$

[^o7] where:

- *$A$*, *[amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude)*, the peak deviation of the function from zero.
- $t$, the [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [independent variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_variable), usually representing [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time) in [seconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seconds).
- $\omega$, *[angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency)*, the rate of change of the function argument in units of [radians per second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radians_per_second).
- *$f$*, *[ordinary frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_frequency)*, the *[number](wikipedia://en/20646438)* of oscillations ([cycles](wikipedia://en/855329)) that occur each second of time.
- $\varphi$, *[phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29)*, specifies (in [radians](wikipedia://en/26003)) where in its cycle the oscillation is at *t* = 0.
  - When $\varphi$ is non-zero, the entire waveform appears to be shifted backwards in time by the amount $\tfrac{\varphi}{\omega}$ seconds. A negative value represents a delay, and a positive value represents an advance.
  - Adding or subtracting $2\pi$ (one cycle) to the phase results in an equivalent wave.

A sine wave can be written in the form of its complementary function, the cosine, with a phase shift of $\pi/2$ radians ($90^\circ$).[^o8]

$$x(t) = \sin(\omega t + \pi/2) = \cos(\omega t)$$

### Combining sinusoids {#Combining_sinusoids}

#### Summing sinusoids with the same frequency {#Summing_sinusoids_with_the_same_frequency}

The resultant sum of a linear combination of sinusoids of the same frequency is a single sinusoid of that frequency, regardless of amplitude and phase. An important implication of this is that sinusoids are [eigenfunctions](wikipedia://en/228601) of [linear time-invariant (LTI) systems](wikipedia://en/1383899):[^o11]

*Image: Sinus und Kosinus am Einheitskreis 1.svg.* Sine and cosine components of an arbitrary angle on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071).

$$x(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \varphi_1) + B\sin(\omega t + \varphi_2)$$

(Expand each term using the trigonometric sine [angle-addition identity](wikipedia://en/25804104#Angle_sum_and_difference_identities) $\sin(\alpha + \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta \; + \; \cos\alpha\sin\beta$:)

$$\begin{aligned} x(t) &= A [ \sin(\omega t)\cos\varphi_1 + \cos(\omega t)\sin\varphi_1 ] \; + \; B [ \sin(\omega t)\cos\varphi_2 + \cos(\omega t)\sin\varphi_2 ]. \\ &= [ A\cos\varphi_1 + B\cos\varphi_2 ] \sin(\omega t) \; + \; [ A\sin\varphi_1 + B\sin\varphi_2 ] \cos(\omega t) \\ &= C\sin(\omega t) + D\cos(\omega t) \end{aligned}$$

Take $C$ and $D$ to be components of a right-angled triangle via [SOH-CAH-TOA](wikipedia://en/18717261#Mnemonics). [^o12]

$$\begin{aligned} x(t) &= R [\cos\theta\sin(\omega t) + \sin\theta\cos(\omega t) ] \\ &= R\sin(\omega t + \theta) \end{aligned}$$

## As a function of both position and time {#As_a_function_of_both_position_and_time}

*Image: Animated-mass-spring.gif.* The displacement of an undamped [spring-mass system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_mass_system) oscillating around the equilibrium over time is a sine wave.

Sinusoids that exist in both position and time also have:

- a spatial variable $x$ that represents the *position* on the dimension on which the wave propagates.
- a [wave number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_number) (or angular wave number) $k$, which represents the proportionality between the [angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency) $\omega$ and the linear speed ([speed of propagation](wikipedia://en/25098)) $v$:
  - wavenumber is related to the angular frequency by $k {=} \frac{\omega}{v} {=} \frac{2 \pi f}{v} {=} \frac{2 \pi}{\lambda}$ where $\lambda$ ([lambda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambda)) is the [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125).

Depending on their direction of travel, they can take the form:

- $y(x, t) = A\sin(kx - \omega t + \varphi)$, if the wave is moving to the right, or
- $y(x, t) = A\sin(kx + \omega t + \varphi)$, if the wave is moving to the left.

Since sine waves propagate without changing form in *distributed linear systems*, they are often used to analyze [wave propagation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_propagation).

### Standing waves {#Standing_waves}

When two waves with the same [amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude) and [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779) traveling in opposite directions [superpose](wikipedia://en/1201321) each other, then a [standing wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standing_wave) pattern is created.

On a plucked string, the superimposing waves are the waves reflected from the fixed endpoints of the string. The string's [resonant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resonant) frequencies are the string's only possible standing waves, which only occur for wavelengths that are twice the string's length (corresponding to the [fundamental frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_frequency)) and integer divisions of that (corresponding to higher harmonics).

### Multiple spatial dimensions {#Multiple_spatial_dimensions}

The earlier equation gives the displacement $y$ of the wave at a position $x$ at time $t$ along a single line. This could, for example, be considered the value of a wave along a wire.

In two or three spatial dimensions, the same equation describes a travelling [plane wave](wikipedia://en/41559) if position $x$ and wavenumber $k$ are interpreted as vectors, and their product as a [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093). For more complex waves such as the height of a water wave in a pond after a stone has been dropped in, more complex equations are needed.

## Fourier analysis {#Fourier_analysis}

*Image: Fourier series and transform.gif.* Function $s_6(x)$ (in red) is a Fourier series sum of 6 harmonically related sine waves (in blue). Its Fourier transform $S(f)$ is a frequency-domain representation that reveals the amplitudes of the summed sine waves.

French mathematician [Joseph Fourier](wikipedia://en/25767401) discovered that sinusoidal waves can be summed as simple building blocks to approximate any periodic waveform, including [square waves](wikipedia://en/314652). These [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) are frequently used in [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324) and the statistical analysis of [time series](wikipedia://en/406624). The [Fourier transform (FT)](wikipedia://en/52247) then extended Fourier series to handle general functions, and birthed the field of [Fourier analysis](wikipedia://en/11659). The sine function specifically is an [odd function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_function).[^o18] Applications of Fourier analysis include [acoustics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustics), [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics), [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324), [image compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_compression), [finding exoplanets in astronomy](wikipedia://en/7290120#Modified_interferometry), etc.

## Differentiation and integration {#Differentiation_and_integration}

### Differentiation {#Differentiation}

[Differentiating](wikipedia://en/7921) any sinusoid with respect to time can be viewed as multiplying its amplitude by its [angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency) and advancing it by a quarter cycle:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt} [A\sin(\omega t + \varphi)] &= A \omega \cos(\omega t + \varphi) \\ &= A \omega \sin(\omega t + \varphi + \tfrac{\pi}{2}) \, . \end{aligned}$$

A [differentiator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiator) has a [zero](wikipedia://en/81560) at the origin of the [complex frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_frequency) plane. The [gain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gain_%28electronics%29) of its [frequency response](wikipedia://en/302033) increases at a rate of +20 [dB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decibel) per [decade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decade_%28log_scale%29) of frequency (for [root-power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root-power) quantities), the same positive slope as a 1${}^{\operatorname{st}}$ order [high-pass filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-pass_filter)'s [stopband](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stopband), although a differentiator does not have a [cutoff frequency](wikipedia://en/40986) or a flat [passband](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passband). A n${}^{\operatorname{th}}$-order high-pass filter approximately applies the n${}^{\operatorname{th}}$ time derivative of [signals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signals) whose frequency band is significantly lower than the filter's cutoff frequency.

### Integration {#Integration}

[Integrating](wikipedia://en/15532) any sinusoid with respect to time can be viewed as dividing its amplitude by its angular frequency and delaying it a quarter cycle:

$$\begin{aligned} \int A \sin(\omega t + \varphi) dt &= -\frac{A}{\omega} \cos(\omega t + \varphi) + C\\ &= -\frac{A}{\omega} \sin(\omega t + \varphi + \tfrac{\pi}{2}) + C\\ &= \frac{A}{\omega} \sin(\omega t + \varphi - \tfrac{\pi}{2}) + C \, . \end{aligned}$$

The [constant of integration](wikipedia://en/147939) $C$ will be zero if the [bounds of integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounds_of_integration) is an integer multiple of the sinusoid's period.

An [integrator](wikipedia://en/1047173) has a [pole](wikipedia://en/81560) at the origin of the complex frequency plane. The gain of its frequency response falls off at a rate of -20 dB per decade of frequency (for root-power quantities), the same negative slope as a 1${}^{\operatorname{st}}$ order [low-pass filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-pass_filter)'s stopband, although an integrator does not have a cutoff frequency or a flat passband. A n${}^{\operatorname{th}}$-order low-pass filter approximately performs the n${}^{\operatorname{th}}$ time integral of signals whose frequency band is significantly higher than the filter's cutoff frequency.
