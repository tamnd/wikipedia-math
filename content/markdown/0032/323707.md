# Finite group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Finite group"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **finite group** is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) whose [underlying set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underlying_set) is [finite](wikipedia://en/11742). Finite groups often arise when considering symmetry of [mathematical](wikipedia://en/2714149) or [physical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_%28physics%29) objects, when those objects admit just a finite number of structure-preserving transformations. Important examples of finite groups include [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) and [permutation groups](wikipedia://en/24634).

:::

The study of finite groups has been an integral part of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) since it arose in the 19th century. One major area of study has been classification: the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445) (those with no nontrivial [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918)) was completed in 2004.

## History {#History}

During the twentieth century, mathematicians investigated some aspects of the theory of finite groups in great depth, especially the [local theory](wikipedia://en/312867) of finite groups and the theory of [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012) and [nilpotent groups](wikipedia://en/144569).[^o3][^o4] As a consequence, the complete [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445) was achieved, meaning that all those [simple groups](wikipedia://en/47600) from which all finite groups can be built are now known.

During the second half of the twentieth century, mathematicians such as [Chevalley](wikipedia://en/366040) and [Steinberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Steinberg) also increased the understanding of finite analogs of [classical groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_groups), and other related groups. One such family of groups is the family of [general linear groups](wikipedia://en/113564) over [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615).

Finite groups often occur when considering [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) of mathematical or physical objects, when those objects admit just a finite number of structure-preserving transformations. The theory of [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945), which may be viewed as dealing with "[continuous symmetry](wikipedia://en/3667668)", is strongly influenced by the associated [Weyl groups](wikipedia://en/296332). These are finite groups generated by reflections which act on a finite-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). The properties of finite groups can thus play a role in subjects such as [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028) and [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry).[^o5]

## Examples {#Examples}

### Permutation groups {#Permutation_groups}

*Image: Symmetric group 4; Cayley graph 4,9.svg.* A [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of the symmetric group S<sub>4</sub>

The **symmetric group** S<sub>*n*</sub> on a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) of *n* symbols is the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) whose elements are all the [permutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutations) of the *n* symbols, and whose [group operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_operation) is the [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of such permutations, which are treated as [bijective functions](wikipedia://en/3942) from the set of symbols to itself.[^o9] Since there are *n*! (*n* [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606)) possible permutations of a set of *n* symbols, it follows that the [order](wikipedia://en/493527) (the number of elements) of the symmetric group S<sub>*n*</sub> is *n*!.

### Cyclic groups {#Cyclic_groups}

A cyclic group Z<sub>*n*</sub> is a group all of whose elements are powers of a particular element *a* where *a*${}^{n}$ = *a*${}^{0}$ = e, the identity. A typical realization of this group is as the complex [*n* roots of unity](wikipedia://en/171950). Sending *a* to a [primitive root of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_root_of_unity) gives an isomorphism between the two. This can be done with any finite cyclic group.

### Finite abelian groups {#Finite_abelian_groups}

An **[abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974)**, also called a **commutative group**, is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) in which the result of applying the group [operation](wikipedia://en/4140245) to two group elements does not depend on their order (the axiom of [commutativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity)). They are named after [Niels Henrik Abel](wikipedia://en/21573).[^o12]

An arbitrary finite abelian group is isomorphic to a direct sum of finite cyclic groups of prime power order, and these orders are uniquely determined, forming a complete system of invariants. The [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of a finite abelian group can be described directly in terms of these invariants. The theory had been first developed in the 1879 paper of [Georg Frobenius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Frobenius) and [Ludwig Stickelberger](wikipedia://en/18870377) and later was both simplified and generalized to finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain, forming an important chapter of [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422).

### Groups of Lie type {#Groups_of_Lie_type}

A **group of Lie type** is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) closely related to the group *G*(*k*) of rational points of a reductive [linear algebraic group](wikipedia://en/395885) *G* with values in the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *k*. Finite groups of Lie type give the bulk of nonabelian [finite simple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_simple_groups). Special cases include the [classical groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_groups), the [Chevalley groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevalley_groups), the Steinberg groups, and the Suzuki–Ree groups.

Finite groups of Lie type were among the first groups to be considered in mathematics, after [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327), [symmetric](wikipedia://en/28901) and [alternating](wikipedia://en/49726) groups, with the [projective special linear groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_special_linear_group) over prime finite fields, PSL(2, *p*) being constructed by [Évariste Galois](wikipedia://en/9815) in the 1830s. The systematic exploration of finite groups of Lie type started with [Camille Jordan](wikipedia://en/355931)'s theorem that the [projective special linear group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_special_linear_group) PSL(2, *q*) is simple for *q* ≠ 2, 3. This theorem generalizes to projective groups of higher dimensions and gives an important infinite family PSL(*n*, *q*) of [finite simple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_simple_groups). Other classical groups were studied by [Leonard Dickson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Dickson) in the beginning of 20th century. In the 1950s [Claude Chevalley](wikipedia://en/366040) realized that after an appropriate reformulation, many theorems about [semisimple Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_Lie_group) admit analogues for algebraic groups over an arbitrary field *k*, leading to construction of what are now called *Chevalley groups*. Moreover, as in the case of compact simple Lie groups, the corresponding groups turned out to be almost simple as abstract groups (*Tits simplicity theorem*). Although it was known since 19th century that other finite simple groups exist (for example, [Mathieu groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathieu_groups)), gradually a belief formed that nearly all finite simple groups can be accounted for by appropriate extensions of Chevalley's construction, together with cyclic and alternating groups. Moreover, the exceptions, the [sporadic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sporadic_groups), share many properties with the finite groups of Lie type, and in particular, can be constructed and characterized based on their *geometry* in the sense of Tits.

The belief has now become a theorem – the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445). Inspection of the list of finite simple groups shows that groups of Lie type over a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) include all the finite simple groups other than the cyclic groups, the alternating groups, the [Tits group](wikipedia://en/606719), and the 26 [sporadic simple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sporadic_simple_group).

## Main theorems {#Main_theorems}

::: {#o17 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Lagrange's theorem"}

### Lagrange's theorem {#Lagrange's_theorem}

For any finite group *G*, the [order](wikipedia://en/493527) (number of elements) of every [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) *H* of *G* divides the order of *G*. The theorem is named after [Joseph-Louis Lagrange](wikipedia://en/87793).

:::

::: {#o19 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Sylow theorems"}

### Sylow theorems {#Sylow_theorems}

This provides a partial converse to Lagrange's theorem giving information about how many subgroups of a given order are contained in *G*.

:::

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Cayley's theorem"}

### Cayley's theorem {#Cayley's_theorem}

**Cayley's theorem**, named in honour of [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034), states that every [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G* is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) acting on *G*.[^o22] This can be understood as an example of the [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of *G* on the elements of *G*.[^o23]

:::

::: {#o25 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Burnside's theorem"}

### Burnside's theorem {#Burnside's_theorem}

**Burnside's theorem** in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) states that if *G* is a finite group of [order](wikipedia://en/493527) *p*${}^{a}$*q*${}^{b}$, where *p* and *q* are [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666), and *a* and *b* are [non-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_and_positive_numbers) [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), then *G* is [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012). Hence each non-Abelian [finite simple group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_simple_group) has order divisible by at least three distinct primes.

### Classification of finite simple groups {#Classification_of_finite_simple_groups}

The [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445) is a theorem stating that every [finite simple group](wikipedia://en/1749665) belongs to one of the following families:

- A [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) with prime order;
- An [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) of degree at least 5;
- A [simple group of Lie type](wikipedia://en/1691490);
- One of the 26 [sporadic simple groups](wikipedia://en/324806);
- The [Tits group](wikipedia://en/606719) (sometimes considered as a 27th sporadic group).

The finite simple groups can be seen as the basic building blocks of all finite groups, in a way reminiscent of the way the [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) are the basic building blocks of the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474). The [Jordan–Hölder theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan–Hölder_theorem) is a more precise way of stating this fact about finite groups. However, a significant difference with respect to the case of [integer factorization](wikipedia://en/15491) is that such "building blocks" do not necessarily determine uniquely a group, since there might be many non-isomorphic groups with the same [composition series](wikipedia://en/297493) or, put in another way, the [extension problem](wikipedia://en/345807#Extension_problem) does not have a unique solution.

The proof of the theorem consists of tens of thousands of pages in several hundred journal articles written by about 100 authors, published mostly between 1955 and 2004. [Gorenstein](wikipedia://en/1569478) (d.1992), [Lyons](wikipedia://en/35256697), and [Solomon](wikipedia://en/35255960) are gradually publishing a simplified and revised version of the proof.

## Number of groups of a given order {#Number_of_groups_of_a_given_order}

Given a positive integer *n*, it is not at all a routine matter to determine how many [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) types of groups of [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_order) *n* there are. Every group of [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) order is [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327), because [Lagrange's theorem](wikipedia://en/31150) implies that the cyclic subgroup generated by any of its non-identity elements is the whole group. If *n* is the square of a prime, then there are exactly two possible isomorphism types of group of order *n*, both of which are abelian. If *n* is a higher power of a prime, then results of [Graham Higman](wikipedia://en/2600152) and [Charles Sims](wikipedia://en/20022166) give asymptotically correct estimates for the number of isomorphism types of groups of order *n*, and the number grows very rapidly as the power increases.

Depending on the prime factorization of *n*, some restrictions may be placed on the structure of groups of order *n*, as a consequence, for example, of results such as the [Sylow theorems](wikipedia://en/53993). For example, every group of order *pq* is cyclic when *q* \< *p* are primes with *p* − 1 not divisible by *q*. For a necessary and sufficient condition, see [cyclic number](wikipedia://en/30495448).

If *n* is [squarefree](wikipedia://en/29525), then any group of order *n* is solvable. [Burnside's theorem](wikipedia://en/1912650), proved using [group characters](wikipedia://en/691741), states that every group of order *n* is solvable when *n* is divisible by fewer than three distinct primes, i.e. if *n* = *p*<sup>*a*</sup>*q*<sup>*b*</sup>, where *p* and *q* are prime numbers, and *a* and *b* are non-negative integers. By the [Feit–Thompson theorem](wikipedia://en/1656473), which has a long and complicated proof, every group of order *n* is solvable when *n* is odd.

For every positive integer *n*, most groups of order *n* are [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012). To see this for any particular order is usually not difficult (for example, there is, up to isomorphism, one non-solvable group and 12 solvable groups of order 60) but the proof of this for all orders uses the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445). For any positive integer *n* there are at most two simple groups of order *n*, and there are infinitely many positive integers *n* for which there are two non-isomorphic simple groups of order *n*.
