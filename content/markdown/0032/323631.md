# Wieferich prime

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Wieferich prime"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), a **Wieferich prime** is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) *p* such that *p*<sup>2</sup> divides $2^{p - 1} - 1$,[^o6] therefore connecting these primes with [Fermat's little theorem](wikipedia://en/53453), which states that every odd prime *p* divides $2^{p - 1} - 1$. Wieferich primes were first described by [Arthur Wieferich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Wieferich) in 1909 in works pertaining to [Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953), at which time both of Fermat's theorems were already well known to mathematicians.[^o7][^o8]

:::

Since then, connections between Wieferich primes and various other topics in mathematics have been discovered, including other types of numbers and primes, such as [Mersenne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_number) and [Fermat](wikipedia://en/91127) numbers, specific types of [pseudoprimes](wikipedia://en/28787420) and some types of numbers generalized from the original definition of a Wieferich prime. Over time, those connections discovered have extended to cover more properties of certain prime numbers as well as more general subjects such as [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) and the [*abc* conjecture](wikipedia://en/174108).

, the only known Wieferich primes are 1093 and 3511 .

## History and search status {#History_and_search_status}

::: {#o10 .problem cue=unsolved}

Are there infinitely many Wieferich primes?

:::

In 1902, [Meyer](wikipedia://en/57755859) proved a theorem about solutions of the congruence *a*<sup>*p* − 1</sup> ≡ 1 (mod *p*<sup>*r*</sup>).[^o11][^o12] Later in that decade [Arthur Wieferich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Wieferich) showed specifically that if the [first case of Fermat's last theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_case_of_Fermat's_last_theorem) has solutions for an odd prime exponent, then that prime must satisfy that congruence for *a* = 2 and *r* = 2.[^o13] In other words, if there exist solutions to *x*<sup>*p*</sup> + *y*<sup>*p*</sup> + *z*<sup>*p*</sup> = 0 in integers *x*, *y*, *z* and *p* an [odd prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_prime) with *p* [∤](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mathematical_symbols#∤) *xyz*, then *p* satisfies 2<sup>*p* − 1</sup> ≡ 1 (mod *p*<sup>2</sup>). In 1913, [Bachmann](wikipedia://en/641951) examined the [residues](wikipedia://en/502897) of $\tfrac{2^{p-1}-1}{p}\,\bmod\,p$. He asked the question when this residue [vanishes](wikipedia://en/264210) and tried to find expressions for answering this question.[^o14]

The prime 1093 was found to be a Wieferich prime by Waldemar Meissner in 1913 and confirmed to be the only such prime below 2000. He calculated the smallest residue of $\tfrac{2^{t}-1}{p}\,\bmod\,p$ for all primes *p* \< 2000 and found this residue to be zero for *t* = 364 and *p* = 1093, thereby providing a counterexample to a [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) by [Grave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitry_Grave) about the impossibility of the Wieferich congruence.[^o15] Emil Haentzschel later ordered verification of the correctness of Meissner's congruence via only elementary calculations.[^o16] Inspired by an earlier work of [Euler](wikipedia://en/17902), he simplified Meissner's proof by showing that 1093<sup>2</sup> | (2<sup>182</sup> + 1) and remarked that (2<sup>182</sup> + 1) is a factor of (2<sup>364</sup> − 1).[^o17] It was also shown that it is possible to prove that 1093 is a Wieferich prime without using [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) contrary to the method used by Meissner,[^o18] although Meissner himself hinted at that he was aware of a proof without complex values.[^o15]

The prime [3511](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3511_%28number%29) was first found to be a Wieferich prime by [N. G. W. H. Beeger](wikipedia://en/1550971) in 1922[^o19] and another proof of it being a Wieferich prime was published in 1965 by [Guy](wikipedia://en/816308).[^o20] In 1960, Kravitz[^o21] doubled a previous record set by Carl-Erik Fröberg[^o22] and in 1961 [Riesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Riesel) extended the search to 500000 with the aid of the computer [BESK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BESK).[^o23] Around 1980, [Lehmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derrick_Henry_Lehmer) was able to reach the search limit of 6.[^o24] This limit was extended to over 2.5 in 2006,[^o25] finally reaching 3. Eventually, it was shown that if any other Wieferich primes exist, they must be greater than 6.7.[^o26]

In 2007–2016, a search for Wieferich primes was performed by the [distributed computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_computing) project Wieferich@Home.[^o27] In 2011–2017, another search was performed by the [PrimeGrid](wikipedia://en/3255603) project, although later the work done in this project was claimed wasted.[^o28] While these projects reached search bounds above 1, neither of them reported any sustainable results.

In 2020, PrimeGrid started another project that searched for Wieferich and [Wall–Sun–Sun primes](wikipedia://en/323651) simultaneously. The new project used checksums to enable independent double-checking of each subinterval, thus minimizing the risk of missing an instance because of faulty hardware.[^o29] The project ended in December 2022, definitely proving that a third Wieferich prime must exceed 2<sup>64</sup> (about 18).[^o30]

It has been conjectured (as for [Wilson primes](wikipedia://en/323646)) that infinitely many Wieferich primes exist, and that the number of Wieferich primes below *x* is approximately log(log(*x*)), which is a [heuristic result](wikipedia://en/43491) that follows from the plausible assumption that for a prime *p*, the $(p - 1)-\operatorname{th}$ degree [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity) modulo *p*<sup>2</sup> are [uniformly distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28discrete%29) in the [multiplicative group of integers modulo *p*<sup>2</sup>](wikipedia://en/1195234).[^o75]

## Properties {#Properties}

::: {#o34 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Connection with Fermat's Last Theorem"}

### Connection with Fermat's Last Theorem {#Connection_with_Fermat's_Last_Theorem}

The following theorem connecting Wieferich primes and [Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953) was proven by Wieferich in 1909:[^o13]

Let *p* be prime, and let *x*, *y*, *z* be [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) such that $x^{p} + y^{p} + z^{p} = 0$. Furthermore, assume that *p* does not divide the [product](wikipedia://en/24977) *xyz*. Then *p* is a Wieferich prime.

The above case (where *p* does not divide any of *x*, *y* or *z*) is commonly known as the [first case of Fermat's Last Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_case_of_Fermat's_Last_Theorem) (FLTI)[^o35][^o36] and FLTI is said to fail for a prime *p*, if solutions to the Fermat equation exist for that *p*, otherwise FLTI holds for *p*.[^o37] In 1910, [Mirimanoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirimanoff) expanded[^o38] the theorem by showing that, if the preconditions of the theorem hold true for some prime *p*, then *p*<sup>2</sup> must also divide $3^{p - 1} - 1$. Granville and Monagan further proved that *p*<sup>2</sup> must actually divide $m^{p - 1} - 1$ for every prime *m* ≤ 89.[^o39] Suzuki extended the proof to all primes *m* ≤ 113.[^o40]

Let *H<sub>p</sub>* be a set of pairs of integers with 1 as their [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354), *p* being prime to *x*, *y* and *x* + *y*, (*x* + *y*)<sup>*p*−1</sup> ≡ 1 (mod p<sup>2</sup>), (*x* + *ξy*) being the *p*th power of an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) of *K* with *ξ* defined as cos 2*π*/*p* + *i* sin 2*π*/*p*. *K* = **Q**(*ξ*) is the [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) obtained by adjoining all [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) in the [algebraic number](wikipedia://en/1158) *ξ* to the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) (such an extension is known as a [number field](wikipedia://en/28730822) or in this particular case, where *ξ* is a [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950), a [cyclotomic number field](wikipedia://en/30872462)).[^o39] From [uniqueness of factorization of ideals in **Q**(ξ)](wikipedia://en/36099150) it follows that if the first case of Fermat's last theorem has solutions *x*, *y*, *z* then *p* divides *x*+*y*+*z* and (*x*, *y*), (*y*, *z*) and (*z*, *x*) are elements of *H<sub>p</sub>*.[^o39] Granville and Monagan showed that (1, 1) ∈ *H<sub>p</sub>* if and only if *p* is a Wieferich prime.[^o39]

:::

::: {#o42 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Connection with the abc conjecture and non-Wieferich primes"}

### Connection with the *abc* conjecture and non-Wieferich primes {#Connection_with_the_abc_conjecture_and_non-Wieferich_primes}

A non-Wieferich prime is a prime *p* satisfying $2^{p-1}\not\equiv 1\bmod{p^2}$. [J. H. Silverman](wikipedia://en/2288703) showed in 1988 that if the [*abc* conjecture](wikipedia://en/174108) holds, then there exist infinitely many non-Wieferich primes.[^o43] More precisely he showed that the *abc* conjecture implies the existence of a constant only depending on *α* such that the number of non-Wieferich primes to base *α* with *p* less than or equal to a variable *X* is greater than log(*X*) as *X* goes to infinity.[^o44] Numerical evidence suggests that very few of the prime numbers in a given interval are Wieferich primes. The set of Wieferich primes and the set of non-Wieferich primes, sometimes denoted by *W<sub>2</sub>* and *W<sub>2</sub><sup>c</sup>* respectively,[^o45] are [complementary sets](wikipedia://en/54347), so if one of them is shown to be finite, the other one would necessarily have to be infinite. It was later shown that the existence of infinitely many non-Wieferich primes already follows from a weaker version of the *abc* conjecture, called the *ABC*-(*k*, *ε*) *conjecture*.[^o46] Additionally, the existence of infinitely many non-Wieferich primes would also follow if there exist infinitely many square-free Mersenne numbers[^o47] as well as if there exists a real number *ξ* such that the set {*n* ∈ **N** : λ(2<sup>*n*</sup> − 1) \< 2 − *ξ*} is of [density](wikipedia://en/1819983) one, where the *index of composition* *λ*(*n*) of an integer *n* is defined as $\tfrac{\log n}{\log \gamma (n)}$ and $\gamma (n) = \prod_{p \mid n} p$, meaning $\gamma (n)$ gives the product of all [prime factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor) of *n*.[^o45]

:::

### Connection with Mersenne and Fermat primes {#Connection_with_Mersenne_and_Fermat_primes}

It is known that the *n*th [Mersenne number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_number) $M_{n} = 2^{n} - 1$ is prime only if *n* is prime. [Fermat's little theorem](wikipedia://en/53453) implies that if $p > 2$ is prime, then *M*<sub>*p*−1</sub> $(= 2^{p - 1} - 1)$ is always divisible by *p*. Since Mersenne numbers of prime indices *M*<sub>*p*</sub> and *M*<sub>*q*</sub> are co-prime,

A prime divisor *p* of *M*<sub>*q*</sub>, where *q* is prime, is a Wieferich prime if and only if *p*<sup>2</sup> divides *M*<sub>*q*</sub>.[^o49]

Thus, a Mersenne prime cannot also be a Wieferich prime. A notable [open problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unsolved_problems_in_mathematics) is to determine whether or not all Mersenne numbers of prime index are [square-free](wikipedia://en/29525). If *q* is prime and the Mersenne number *M*<sub>*q*</sub> is *not* square-free, that is, there exists a prime *p* for which *p*<sup>2</sup> divides *M*<sub>*q*</sub>, then *p* is a Wieferich prime. Therefore, if there are only finitely many Wieferich primes, then there will be at most finitely many Mersenne numbers with prime index that are not square-free. Rotkiewicz showed a related result: if there are infinitely many square-free Mersenne numbers, then there are infinitely many non-Wieferich primes.[^o50]

Similarly, if *p* is prime and *p*<sup>2</sup> divides some [Fermat number](wikipedia://en/91127) *F*<sub>*n*</sub> $= 2^{2^{n}} + 1$, then *p* must be a Wieferich prime.[^o51]

For the primes 1093 and 3511, it was shown that neither of them is a divisor of any Mersenne number with prime index nor a divisor of any Fermat number, because 364 and 1755 are neither prime nor powers of 2.[^o52]

### Connection with other equations {#Connection_with_other_equations}

Scott and Styer showed that the equation *p*<sup>x</sup> − 2<sup>y</sup> = *d* has at most one solution in positive integers (*x*, *y*), unless when *p*<sup>4</sup> | 2<sup>ord<sub>*p*</sub> 2</sup> − 1 if *p* ≢ 65 (mod 192) or unconditionally when *p*<sup>2</sup> | 2<sup>ord<sub>*p*</sub> 2</sup> − 1, where ord<sub>*p*</sub> 2 denotes the [multiplicative order](wikipedia://en/344542) of 2 modulo *p*.[^o54] They also showed that a solution to the equation ±*a*<sup>*x*<sub>1</sub></sup> ± 2<sup>*y*<sub>1</sub></sup> = ±*a*<sup>*x*<sub>2</sub></sup> ± 2<sup>*y*<sub>2</sub></sup> = *c* must be from a specific set of equations but that this does not hold, if *a* is a Wieferich prime greater than 1.25 × 10<sup>15</sup>.[^o55]

::: {#o57 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Wieferich@Home"}

### Binary periodicity of *p* − 1 {#Binary_periodicity_of_p_−_1}

Johnson observed[^o58] that the two known Wieferich primes are one greater than numbers with periodic [binary expansions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_expansion) (1092 = 010001000100<sub>2</sub>=444<sub>16</sub>; 3510 = 110110110110<sub>2</sub>=6666<sub>8</sub>). The Wieferich@Home project searched for Wieferich primes by testing numbers that are one greater than a number with a periodic binary expansion, but up to a "bit pseudo-length" of 3500 of the tested binary numbers generated by combination of bit strings with a bit length of up to 24 it has not found a new Wieferich prime.[^o59]

:::

### Abundancy of *p* − 1 {#Abundancy_of_p_−_1}

The known Wieferich primes are one greater than mutually [friendly numbers](wikipedia://en/3821872) (the shared abundancy index being 112/39).

### Connection with pseudoprimes {#Connection_with_pseudoprimes}

It was observed that the two known Wieferich primes are the square factors of all [non-square free](wikipedia://en/29525) base-2 [Fermat pseudoprimes](wikipedia://en/45891) up to 25.[^o63] Later computations showed that the only repeated factors of the pseudoprimes up to 10<sup>12</sup> are 1093 and 3511.[^o64] In addition, the following connection exists:

Let *n* be a base 2 pseudoprime and *p* be a prime divisor of *n*. If $\tfrac{2^{n-1}-1}{n}\not\equiv 0 \pmod{p}$, then also $\tfrac{2^{p-1}-1}{p}\not\equiv 0 \pmod{p}$.[^o37]

### Connection with directed graphs {#Connection_with_directed_graphs}

For all primes $p$ up to $100000$, $L(p^{n+1}) = L(p^{n})$ only in two cases: $L(1093^{2}) = L(1093) = 364$ and $L(3511^{2}) = L(3511) = 1755$, where $L(m)$ is the number of vertices in the cycle of 1 in the *doubling diagram* modulo $m$. Here the doubling diagram represents the [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) with the non-negative integers less than *m* as vertices and with directed edges going from each vertex *x* to vertex 2*x* reduced modulo *m*.[^o66] It was shown, that for all odd prime numbers either $L(p^{n+1}) = p \cdot L(p^{n})$ or $L(p^{n+1}) = L(p^{n})$.[^o66]

### Properties related to number fields {#Properties_related_to_number_fields}

It was shown that $\chi_{D_{0}} \big(p \big) = 1$ and $\lambda\,\!_p \big( \mathbb{Q} \big(\sqrt{D_{0}} \big) \big) = 1$ if and only if $2^{p - 1} \not\equiv 1\pmod{p^{2}}$ where *p* is an odd prime and $D_{0} < 0$ is the [fundamental discriminant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_discriminant) of the imaginary [quadratic field](wikipedia://en/454781) $\mathbb{Q} \big(\sqrt{1 - p^2} \big)$. Furthermore, the following was shown: Let *p* be a Wieferich prime. If $p \equiv 3\pmod{4}$, let $D_{0} < 0$ be the fundamental discriminant of the imaginary quadratic field $\mathbb{Q} \big(\sqrt{1 - p} \big)$ and if $p \equiv 1\pmod{4}$, let $D_{0} < 0$ be the fundamental discriminant of the imaginary quadratic field $\mathbb{Q} \big(\sqrt{4 - p} \big)$. Then $\chi_{D_{0}} \big(p \big) = 1$ and $\lambda\,\!_p \big( \mathbb{Q} \big(\sqrt{D_{0}} \big) \big) = 1$ (*χ* and *λ* in this context denote Iwasawa [invariants](wikipedia://en/1126638)).[^o68]

Furthermore, the following result was obtained: Let *q* be an odd prime number, *k* and *p* are primes such that $p = 2k + 1,$ $k \equiv 3\pmod{4},$ $p \equiv -1\pmod{q},$ $p \not\equiv -1\pmod{q^{3}}$ and the order of *q* modulo *k* is $\tfrac{k-1}{2}$. Assume that *q* divides *h*<sup>\+</sup>, the [class number](wikipedia://en/150907) of the real [cyclotomic field](wikipedia://en/30872462) $\mathbb{Q} \big( \zeta\,\!_p + \zeta\,\!_p^{-1} \big)$, the cyclotomic field obtained by adjoining the sum of a *p*-th [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950) and its [reciprocal](wikipedia://en/229940) to the field of rational numbers. Then *q* is a Wieferich prime.[^o69] This also holds if the conditions $p \equiv -1\pmod{q}$ and $p \not\equiv -1\pmod{q^{3}}$ are replaced by $p \equiv -3\pmod{q}$ and $p \not\equiv -3\pmod{q^{3}}$ as well as when the condition $p \equiv -1\pmod{q}$ is replaced by $p \equiv -5\pmod{q}$ (in which case *q* is a [Wall–Sun–Sun prime](wikipedia://en/323651)) and the incongruence condition replaced by $p \not\equiv -5\pmod{q^{3}}$.[^o70]

:::

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

::: {#o74 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Near-Wieferich prime"}

### Near-Wieferich primes {#Near-Wieferich_primes}

A prime *p* satisfying the congruence 2<sup>(*p*−1)/2</sup> $\equiv \pm1 + Ap$ (mod *p*<sup>2</sup>) with small |*A*| is commonly called a *near-Wieferich prime* .[^o75][^o76] Near-Wieferich primes with *A* = 0 represent Wieferich primes. Recent searches, in addition to their primary search for Wieferich primes, also tried to find near-Wieferich primes.[^o26]

:::

### Wieferich pairs {#Wieferich_pairs}

A [Wieferich pair](wikipedia://en/6832884) is a pair of primes *p* and *q* that satisfy

*p*<sup>*q* − 1</sup> ≡ 1 (mod *q*<sup>2</sup>) and *q*<sup>*p* − 1</sup> ≡ 1 (mod *p*<sup>2</sup>)

so that a Wieferich prime *p* ≡ 1 (mod 4) will form such a pair (*p*, 2): the only known instance in this case is $p = 1093$. There are only 7 known Wieferich pairs.

(2, 1093), (3, 1006003), (5, 1645333507), (5, 188748146801), (83, 4871), (911, 318917), and (2903, 18787) (sequence in [OEIS](wikipedia://en/500004))

::: {#o80 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Wieferich number"}

### Wieferich numbers {#Wieferich_numbers}

A **Wieferich number** is an odd natural number *n* satisfying the congruence 2<sup>(*n*)</sup> ≡ 1 (mod *n*<sup>2</sup>), where denotes the [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452) (according to [Euler's theorem](wikipedia://en/69566), 2<sup>(*n*)</sup> ≡ 1 (mod *n*) for every odd natural number *n*). If Wieferich number *n* is prime, then it is a Wieferich prime. The first few Wieferich numbers are:

1, 1093, 3279, 3511, 7651, 10533, 14209, 17555, 22953, 31599, 42627, 45643, 52665, 68859, 94797, 99463, ...

It can be shown that if there are only finitely many Wieferich primes, then there are only finitely many Wieferich numbers. In particular, if the only Wieferich primes are 1093 and 3511, then there exist exactly 104 Wieferich numbers, which matches the number of Wieferich numbers currently known.[^o4]

More generally, a natural number *n* is a **Wieferich number to base** *a*, if *a*<sup>(*n*)</sup> ≡ 1 (mod *n*<sup>2</sup>).[^o81]

Another definition specifies a **Wieferich number** as odd natural number *n* such that *n* and $\tfrac{2^m-1}{n}$ are not [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime), where *m* is the [multiplicative order](wikipedia://en/344542) of 2 modulo *n*. The first of these numbers are:[^o82]

21, 39, 55, 57, 105, 111, 147, 155, 165, 171, 183, 195, 201, 203, 205, 219, 231, 237, 253, 273, 285, 291, 301, 305, 309, 327, 333, 355, 357, 385, 399, ...

As above, if Wieferich number *q* is prime, then it is a Wieferich prime.

:::

### Wieferich places {#Wieferich_places}

Let *K* be a [global field](wikipedia://en/288358), i.e. a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) or a [function field](wikipedia://en/447645) in one variable over a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) and let *E* be an [elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/10225). If *v* is a [non-archimedean place](wikipedia://en/28730822#Nonarchimedian_or_ultrametric_places) of [norm](wikipedia://en/450555) *q<sub>v</sub>* of *K* and a ∈ K, with *v*(*a*) = 0 then $v(a^{q_{v} - 1} - 1)$ ≥ 1. *v* is called a *Wieferich place* for base *a*, if $v(a^{q_{v} - 1} - 1)$ > 1, an *elliptic Wieferich place* for base *P* ∈ *E*, if *N<sub>v</sub>P* ∈ *E*<sub>2</sub> and a *strong elliptic Wieferich place* for base *P* ∈ *E* if *n<sub>v</sub>P* ∈ *E*<sub>2</sub>, where *n<sub>v</sub>* is the order of *P* modulo *v* and *N<sub>v</sub>* gives the number of [rational points](wikipedia://en/2960200) (over the [residue field](wikipedia://en/10789680) of *v*) of the reduction of *E* at *v*.[^o84]

:::
