# Figurate number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Figurate number"}

The term **figurate number** is used by different writers for members of different sets of numbers, generalizing from [triangular numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangular_numbers) to different shapes ([polygonal numbers](wikipedia://en/213996)) and different dimensions (polyhedral numbers). The ancient Greek mathematicians already considered [triangular numbers](wikipedia://en/213915), [polygonal numbers](wikipedia://en/213996), [tetrahedral numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedral_number), and [pyramidal numbers](wikipedia://en/509081),[^o2] and subsequent mathematicians have included other classes of these numbers including numbers defined from other types of polyhedra and from their analogs in other dimensions.

:::

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Terminology"}

## Terminology {#Terminology}

Some kinds of figurate number were discussed in the 16th and 17th centuries under the name "figural number".[^o5]

In historical works about [Greek mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) the preferred term used to be *figured number*.[^o6][^o7]

In a use going back to [Jacob Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/64740)'s [Ars Conjectandi](wikipedia://en/17554277),[^o2] the term *figurate number* is used for [triangular numbers](wikipedia://en/213915) made up of successive [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), [tetrahedral numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedral_number) made up of successive triangular numbers, etc. These turn out to be the [binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/4668). In this usage the [square numbers](wikipedia://en/213919) (4, 9, 16, 25, ...) would not be considered figurate numbers when viewed as arranged in a square.

A number of other sources use the term *figurate number* as synonymous for the [polygonal numbers](wikipedia://en/213996), either just the usual kind or both those and the [centered polygonal numbers](wikipedia://en/860507).[^o8]

:::

## History {#History}

The mathematical study of figurate numbers is said to have originated with [Pythagoras](wikipedia://en/23275), possibly based on Babylonian or Egyptian precursors. Generating whichever class of figurate numbers the Pythagoreans studied using [gnomons](wikipedia://en/6673051) is also attributed to Pythagoras. Unfortunately, there is no trustworthy source for these claims, because all surviving writings about the Pythagoreans[^o10] are from centuries later.[^o11] [Speusippus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speusippus) is the earliest source to expose the view that ten, as the fourth triangular number, was in fact the [tetractys](wikipedia://en/1570530), supposed to be of great importance for [Pythagoreanism](wikipedia://en/291170).[^o12] Figurate numbers were a concern of the Pythagorean worldview. It was well understood that some numbers could have many figurations, e.g. [36](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/36_%28number%29) is a both a square and a triangle and also various rectangles.

The modern study of figurate numbers goes back to [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966), specifically the [Fermat polygonal number theorem](wikipedia://en/847886). Later, it became a significant topic for [Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler), who gave an explicit formula for all [triangular numbers that are also perfect squares](wikipedia://en/416823), among many other discoveries relating to figurate numbers.

Figurate numbers have played a significant role in modern recreational mathematics.[^o13] In research mathematics, figurate numbers are studied by way of the [Ehrhart polynomials](wikipedia://en/316904), [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) that count the number of integer points in a polygon or polyhedron when it is expanded by a given factor.[^o14]

These are the binomial coefficients $\textstyle \binom{n+1}{2}$. This is the case $r = 2$ of the fact that the $r$th diagonal of [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497#Patterns_and_properties) for $r \ge 0$ consists of the figurate numbers for the $r$-dimensional analogs of triangles ($r$-dimensional [simplices](wikipedia://en/39781)).

The simplicial polytopic numbers for $r = 1, 2, 3, 4, ...$ are:

- $P_1(n) = \frac{n}{1} = \binom{n+0}{1}=\binom{n}{1}$ (linear numbers),
- $P_2(n) = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} = \binom{n+1}{2}$ ([triangular numbers](wikipedia://en/213915)),
- $P_3(n) = \frac{n(n+1)(n+2)}{6} = \binom{n+2}{3}$ ([tetrahedral numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedral_number)),
- $P_4(n) = \frac{n(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}{24} = \binom{n+3}{4}$ (pentachoric numbers, [pentatopic numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentatopic_number), 4-simplex numbers),

$$\qquad\vdots$$

- $P_r(n) = \frac{n(n+1)(n+2)\cdots(n+r-1)}{r!} = \binom{n+(r-1)}{r}$ ($r$-topic numbers, $r$-[simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) numbers).

The terms *[square number](wikipedia://en/213919)* and *[cubic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_number)* derive from their geometric representation as a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) or [cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube_%28geometry%29). The difference of two positive triangular numbers is a [trapezoidal number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trapezoidal_number).

## Gnomon {#Gnomon}

The **gnomon** is the piece added to a figurate number to transform it to the next larger one.

For example, the gnomon of the square number is the [odd number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_number), of the general form $2n + 1$, $n = 0, 1, 2, 3, ...$. The square of size 8 composed of gnomons looks like this:

$$\begin{matrix}1&2&3&4&5&6&7&8\\2&2&3&4&5&6&7&8\\3&3&3&4&5&6&7&8\\4&4&4&4&5&6&7&8\\5&5&5&5&5&6&7&8\\6&6&6&6&6&6&7&8\\7&7&7&7&7&7&7&8\\8&8&8&8&8&8&8&8\end{matrix}$$

To transform from the *$n$-square* (the square of size $n$) to the $(n + 1)$-square, one adjoins $2n + 1$ elements: one to the end of each row ($n$ elements), one to the end of each column ($n$ elements), and a single one to the corner. For example, when transforming the 7-square to the 8-square, we add 15 elements; these adjunctions are the 8s in the above figure.

This gnomonic technique also provides a [mathematical proof](wikipedia://en/82285) that the sum of the first $n$ odd numbers is $n^{2}$; the figure illustrates 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 + 11 + 13 + 15 = 64 = 8<sup>2</sup>.

There is a similar **gnomon** with [centered hexagonal numbers](wikipedia://en/497485) adding up to make cubes of each integer number.
