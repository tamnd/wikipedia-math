# Isoperimetric inequality

*Image: Isoperimetric inequality illustr1.svg.* If a region is not convex, a "dent" in its boundary can be "flipped" to increase the area of the region while keeping the perimeter unchanged.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Isoperimetric inequality"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **isoperimetric inequality** is a [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489) involving the square of the [circumference](wikipedia://en/5916) of a [closed curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_curve) in the plane and the [area](wikipedia://en/1209) of a [plane region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_region) it encloses, as well as its various generalizations. *Isoperimetric* literally means "having the same [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636)". Specifically, the isoperimetric inequality states, for the length *L* of a closed [plane curve](wikipedia://en/971691) and the area *A* of the region that it encloses, that

:::

$$4\pi A \le L^2,$$

and that equality holds [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the curve is a circle.

The **isoperimetric problem** is to determine a [plane figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_figure) of the largest possible area whose [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) has a specified length.[^o2] The closely related *Dido's problem (geometry)* asks for a region of the maximal area bounded by a straight line and a curvilinear [arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_%28geometry%29) whose endpoints belong to that line. It is named after [Dido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dido_%28Queen_of_Carthage%29), the legendary founder and first queen of [Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carthage). The solution to the isoperimetric problem is given by a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) and was known already in [Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece). However, the first mathematically rigorous proof of this fact was obtained only in the 19th century. Since then, many other proofs have been found.

The isoperimetric problem has been extended in multiple ways, for example, to curves on [surfaces](wikipedia://en/15513875) and to regions in higher-dimensional spaces. Perhaps the most familiar physical manifestation of the 3-dimensional isoperimetric inequality is the shape of a drop of water. Namely, a drop will typically assume a symmetric round shape. Since the amount of water in a drop is fixed, [surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension) forces the drop into a shape which minimizes the surface area of the drop, namely a round sphere.

## The isoperimetric problem in the plane {#The_isoperimetric_problem_in_the_plane}

*Image: Isoperimetric inequality illustr2.svg.* An elongated shape can be made more round while keeping its perimeter fixed and increasing its area.

The classical *isoperimetric problem* dates back to antiquity.[^o4] The problem can be stated as follows: Among all closed [curves](wikipedia://en/89246) in the plane of fixed perimeter, which curve (if any) maximizes the area of its enclosed region? This question can be shown to be equivalent to the following problem: Among all closed curves in the plane enclosing a fixed area, which curve (if any) minimizes the perimeter?

This problem is conceptually related to the [principle of least action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_least_action) in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), in that it can be restated: what is the principle of action which encloses the greatest area, with the greatest economy of effort? The 15th-century philosopher and scientist, Cardinal [Nicholas of Cusa](wikipedia://en/282522), considered [rotational](wikipedia://en/39789) action, the process by which a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) is generated, to be the most direct reflection, in the realm of sensory impressions, of the process by which the universe is created. German astronomer and astrologer [Johannes Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736) invoked the isoperimetric principle in discussing the morphology of the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System), in *[Mysterium Cosmographicum](wikipedia://en/3194945)* (*The Sacred Mystery of the Cosmos*, 1596).

Although the circle appears to be an obvious solution to the problem, proving this fact is rather difficult. The first progress toward the solution was made by Swiss geometer [Jakob Steiner](wikipedia://en/567445) in 1838, using a geometric method later named [*Steiner symmetrisation*](wikipedia://en/48578727#Steiner_Symmetrization).[^o5] Steiner showed that if a solution existed, then it must be the circle. Steiner's proof was completed later by several other mathematicians.

Steiner begins with some geometric constructions which are easily understood; for example, it can be shown that any closed curve enclosing a region that is not fully [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) can be modified to enclose more area, by "flipping" the concave areas so that they become convex. It can further be shown that any closed curve which is not fully symmetrical can be "tilted" so that it encloses more area. The one shape that is perfectly convex and symmetrical is the circle, although this, in itself, does not represent a rigorous proof of the isoperimetric theorem (see external links).

## On a plane {#On_a_plane}

The solution to the isoperimetric problem is usually expressed in the form of an [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489) that relates the length *L* of a closed curve and the area *A* of the planar region that it encloses. The **isoperimetric inequality** states that

$$4\pi A \le L^2,$$

and that the equality holds if and only if the curve is a circle. The [area of a disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area_of_a_disk) of radius *R* is *πR*<sup>2</sup> and the circumference of the circle is 2*πR*, so both sides of the inequality are equal to 4*π*<sup>2</sup>*R*<sup>2</sup> in this case.

Dozens of proofs of the isoperimetric inequality have been found. In 1902, [Hurwitz](wikipedia://en/771690) published a short proof using the [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) that applies to arbitrary [rectifiable curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectifiable_curve) (not assumed to be smooth). An elegant direct proof based on comparison of a smooth simple closed curve with an appropriate circle was given by E. Schmidt in 1938. It uses only the [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) formula, expression for the area of a plane region from [Green's theorem](wikipedia://en/243334), and the [Cauchy–Schwarz inequality](wikipedia://en/38128).

For a given closed curve, the **isoperimetric quotient** is defined as the ratio of its area and that of the circle having the same perimeter. This is equal to

$$Q=\frac{4\pi A}{L^2}$$

and the isoperimetric inequality says that *Q* ≤ 1. Equivalently, the [isoperimetric ratio](wikipedia://en/48509016) $L^{2}/A$ is at least 4$\pi$ for every curve.

The isoperimetric quotient of a regular *n*-gon is

$$Q_n=\frac{\pi}{n \tan(\pi/n)}.$$

Let $C$ be a smooth regular convex closed curve. Then the **improved isoperimetric inequality** states the following

$$L^2\geqslant 4\pi A+8\pi\left|\widetilde{A}_{0.5}\right|,$$

where $L, A, \widetilde{A}_{0.5}$ denote the length of $C$, the area of the region bounded by $C$ and the oriented area of the [Wigner caustic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wigner_caustic) of $C$, respectively, and the equality holds if and only if $C$ is a [curve of constant width](wikipedia://en/580238).[^o7]

## On a sphere {#On_a_sphere}

Let *C* be a simple closed curve on a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) of radius 1. Denote by *L* the length of *C* and by *A* the area enclosed by *C*. The **spherical isoperimetric inequality** states that

$$L^2 \ge A (4\pi - A),$$

and that the equality holds if and only if the curve is a circle. There are, in fact, two ways to measure the spherical area enclosed by a simple closed curve, but the inequality is symmetric with the respect to taking the complement.

This inequality was discovered by [Paul Lévy](wikipedia://en/556462) (1919) who also extended it to higher dimensions and general surfaces.[^o9]

In the more general case of arbitrary radius *R*, it is known[^o10] that

$$L^2\ge 4\pi A - \frac{A^2}{R^2}.$$

## In Euclidean space {#In_Euclidean_space}

The isoperimetric inequality states that a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) has the smallest surface area per given volume. Given a bounded open set $S\subset\mathbb{R} ^n$ with $C^1$ boundary, having [surface area](wikipedia://en/27725) $\operatorname{per}(S)$ and [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) $\operatorname{vol}(S)$, the isoperimetric inequality states

$$\operatorname{per}(S)\geq n \operatorname{vol}(S)^{(n-1)/n} \, \operatorname{vol}(B_1)^{1/n},$$

where $B_1\subset\mathbb{R} ^n$ is a [unit ball](wikipedia://en/27033090). The equality holds when $S$ is a ball in $\mathbb{R} ^n$. Under additional restrictions on the set (such as [convexity](wikipedia://en/6292), [regularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_regular_set), [smooth boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_surface)), the equality holds for a ball only. But in full generality the situation is more complicated. The relevant result of (Schmidt 1949, Sect. 20.7) (for a simpler proof see (Baebler 1957)) is clarified in (Hadwiger 1957, Sect. 5.2.5) as follows. An extremal set consists of a ball and a "corona" that contributes neither to the volume nor to the surface area. That is, the equality holds for a compact set $S$ if and only if $S$ contains a closed ball $B$ such that $\operatorname{vol}(B) = \operatorname{vol}(S)$ and $\operatorname{per}(B) = \operatorname{per}(S).$ For example, the "corona" may be a curve.

The proof of the inequality follows directly from [Brunn–Minkowski inequality](wikipedia://en/7500545) between a set $S$ and a ball with radius $\epsilon$, i.e. $B_\epsilon=\epsilon B_1$. Indeed, $\operatorname{vol}(A + B_\epsilon) \ge (\operatorname{vol}(A)^{1/n} + \operatorname{vol}(B_\epsilon)^{1/n})^n \ge \operatorname{vol}(A) + n \operatorname{vol}(A)^{(n-1)/n} \epsilon\operatorname{vol}(B_1)^{1/n}.$ The isoperimetric inequality follows by subtracting $\operatorname{vol}(A)$, dividing by $\epsilon$, and taking the limit as $\epsilon\to 0.$ ((Osserman 1978); (Federer 1969, §3.2.43)).

In full generality (Federer 1969, §3.2.43), the isoperimetric inequality states that for any set $S\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ whose [closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closure_of_a_set) has finite [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198)

$$n\,\omega_n^{1/n} L^n(\bar{S})^{(n-1)/n} \le M^{n-1}_*(\partial S)$$

where $M_*^{n-1}$ is the (*n*-1)-dimensional [Minkowski content](wikipedia://en/15667957), *L<sup>n</sup>* is the *n*-dimensional Lebesgue measure, and *ω<sub>n</sub>* is the volume of the [unit ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_ball) in $\mathbb{R}^n$. If the boundary of *S* is [rectifiable](wikipedia://en/2644238), then the Minkowski content is the (*n*-1)-dimensional [Hausdorff measure](wikipedia://en/504109).

The *n*-dimensional isoperimetric inequality is equivalent (for sufficiently smooth domains) to the [Sobolev inequality](wikipedia://en/4665038) on $\mathbb{R}^n$ with optimal constant:

$$\left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} |u|^{n/(n-1)}\right)^{(n-1)/n} \le n^{-1}\omega_{n}^{-1/n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}|\nabla u|$$

for all $u\in W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^n)$.

## In Hadamard manifolds {#In_Hadamard_manifolds}

[Hadamard manifolds](wikipedia://en/12471936) are complete simply connected manifolds with nonpositive curvature. Thus they generalize the Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$, which is a Hadamard manifold with curvature zero. In 1970's and early 80's, [Thierry Aubin](wikipedia://en/8748733), [Misha Gromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Leonidovich_Gromov), [Yuri Burago](wikipedia://en/8688682), and [Viktor Zalgaller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Zalgaller) conjectured that the Euclidean isoperimetric inequality

$$\operatorname{per}(S)\geq n \operatorname{vol}(S)^{(n-1)/n}\operatorname{vol}(B_1)^{1/n}$$

holds for bounded sets $S$ in Hadamard manifolds, which has become known as the [Cartan–Hadamard conjecture](wikipedia://en/62358280). In dimension 2 this had already been established in 1926 by [André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019), who was a student of [Hadamard](wikipedia://en/312675) at the time. In dimensions 3 and 4 the conjecture was proved by [Bruce Kleiner](wikipedia://en/8488020) in 1992, and [Chris Croke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Croke) in 1984 respectively.

## In a metric measure space {#In_a_metric_measure_space}

Most of the work on isoperimetric problem has been done in the context of smooth regions in [Euclidean spaces](wikipedia://en/9697), or more generally, in [Riemannian manifolds](wikipedia://en/144652). However, the isoperimetric problem can be formulated in much greater generality, using the notion of *Minkowski content*. Let $(X, \mu, d)$ be a *metric measure space*: *X* is a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) with [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) *d*, and *μ* is a [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322) on *X*. The *boundary measure*, or [Minkowski content](wikipedia://en/15667957), of a [measurable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurable) subset *A* of *X* is defined as the [lim inf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lim_inf)

$$\mu^+(A) = \liminf_{\varepsilon \to 0+} \frac{\mu(A_\varepsilon) - \mu(A)}{\varepsilon},$$

where

$$A_\varepsilon = \{ x \in X | d(x, A) \leq \varepsilon \}$$

is the ε-*extension* of *A*.

The isoperimetric problem in *X* asks how small can $\mu^+(A)$ be for a given *μ*(*A*). If *X* is the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/19469852) with the usual distance and the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) then this question generalizes the classical isoperimetric problem to planar regions whose boundary is not necessarily smooth, although the answer turns out to be the same.

The function

$$I(a) = \inf \{ \mu^+(A) | \mu(A) = a\}$$

is called the *isoperimetric profile* of the metric measure space $(X, \mu, d)$. Isoperimetric profiles have been studied for [Cayley graphs](wikipedia://en/358277) of [discrete groups](wikipedia://en/468536) and for special classes of Riemannian manifolds (where usually only regions *A* with regular boundary are considered).

## For graphs {#For_graphs}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), isoperimetric inequalities are at the heart of the study of [expander graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expander_graphs), which are [sparse graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparse_graph) that have strong connectivity properties. Expander constructions have spawned research in pure and applied mathematics, with several applications to [complexity theory](wikipedia://en/7543), design of robust [computer networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_network), and the theory of [error-correcting codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error-correcting_code).[^o21]

Isoperimetric inequalities for graphs relate the size of vertex subsets to the size of their boundary, which is usually measured by the number of edges leaving the subset (edge expansion) or by the number of neighbouring vertices (vertex expansion). For a graph $G$ and a number $k$, the following are two standard isoperimetric parameters for graphs.[^o22]

- The edge isoperimetric parameter: $$\Phi_E(G,k)=\min_{S\subseteq V} \left\{|E(S,\overline{S})| : |S|=k \right\}$$
- The vertex isoperimetric parameter: $$\Phi_V(G,k)=\min_{S\subseteq V} \left\{|\Gamma(S)\setminus S| : |S|=k \right\}$$

Here $E(S,\overline{S})$ denotes the set of edges leaving $S$ and $\Gamma(S)$ denotes the set of vertices that have a neighbour in $S$. The isoperimetric problem consists of understanding how the parameters $\Phi_E$ and $\Phi_V$ behave for natural families of graphs.

### Example: Isoperimetric inequalities for hypercubes {#Example:_Isoperimetric_inequalities_for_hypercubes}

The $d$-dimensional [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783) $Q_d$ is the graph whose vertices are all Boolean vectors of length $d$, that is, the set $\{0,1\}^d$. Two such vectors are connected by an edge in $Q_d$ if they are equal up to a single bit flip, that is, their [Hamming distance](wikipedia://en/41227) is exactly one. The following are the isoperimetric inequalities for the Boolean hypercube.[^o24]

::: {#o26 .statement cue=named-heading name="Edge isoperimetric inequality"}

#### Edge isoperimetric inequality {#Edge_isoperimetric_inequality}

The edge isoperimetric inequality of the hypercube is $\Phi_E(Q_d,k) \geq k(d-\log_2 k)$. This bound is tight, as is witnessed by each set $S$ that is the set of vertices of any subcube of $Q_d$.

:::

::: {#o28 .statement cue=named-heading name="Vertex isoperimetric inequality"}

#### Vertex isoperimetric inequality {#Vertex_isoperimetric_inequality}

Harper's theorem[^o29] says that *Hamming balls* have the smallest vertex boundary among all sets of a given size. Hamming balls are sets that contain all points of [Hamming weight](wikipedia://en/1127884) at most $r$ and no points of Hamming weight larger than $r+1$ for some integer $r$. This theorem implies that any set $S\subseteq V$ with

$$|S|\geq\sum_{i=0}^{r} {d\choose i}$$

satisfies

$|S\cup\Gamma(S)|\geq \sum_{i=0}^{r+1}{d\choose i}.$[^o30]

As a special case, consider set sizes $k=|S|$ of the form

$$k={d \choose 0} + {d \choose 1} + \dots + {d \choose r}$$

for some integer $r$. Then the above implies that the exact vertex isoperimetric parameter is

$\Phi_V(Q_d,k) = {d\choose r+1}.$[^o31]

:::

::: {#o33 .statement cue=named-heading name="Isoperimetric inequality for triangles"}

## Isoperimetric inequality for triangles {#Isoperimetric_inequality_for_triangles}

The isoperimetric inequality for triangles in terms of perimeter *p* and area *T* states that[^o34]

$$p^2 \ge 12\sqrt{3} \cdot T,$$

with equality for the [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285). This is implied, via the [AM–GM inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inequality_of_arithmetic_and_geometric_means), by a stronger inequality which has also been called the isoperimetric inequality for triangles:[^o35]

$$T \le \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}(abc)^{2/3}.$$

:::
