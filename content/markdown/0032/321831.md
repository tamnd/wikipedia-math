# Abundant number

*Image: Abundant number Cuisenaire rods 12.png.* Demonstration, with [Cuisenaire rods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuisenaire_rods), of the abundance of the number 12

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Abundant number"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), an **abundant number** or **excessive number** is a [positive integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_integer) for which the sum of its [proper divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_divisor) is greater than the number. The integer 12 is the first abundant number: its proper divisors are 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6, and their sum 16 is larger than 12. The amount by which the sum exceeds the number is the **abundance**. The number 12 has an abundance of 4, for example.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition and examples"}

## Definition and examples {#Definition_and_examples}

An *abundant number* is a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $n$ for which the [sum of divisors](wikipedia://en/407249) $\sigma(n)$ satisfies $\sigma(n) > 2n$, or, equivalently, the sum of proper divisors (or [aliquot sum](wikipedia://en/12374274)) $s(n)$ satisfies $s(n) > n$.p. 84p. 693p. 273p. 185 The *abundance* of a natural number is the [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $\sigma(n) - 2n$ (equivalently, $s(n) - n$).

The abundant numbers smaller than 100 are

12, 18, 20, 24, 30, 36, 40, 42, 48, 54, 56, 60, 66, 70, 72, 78, 80, 84, 88, 90, and 96 .

For example, the proper divisors of 24 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, and 12, whose sum is 36. Because 36 is greater than 24, the number 24 is abundant, and its abundance is $36 - 24 = 12$.

Numbers that are not abundant are either [perfect](wikipedia://en/23670) (if $\sigma(n) = 2n$) or [deficient](wikipedia://en/321843) (if $\sigma(n) < 2n$).

## Properties {#Properties}

*Image: Proportion of abundant numbers.svg.* Let $a(n)$ be the number of abundant numbers not exceeding $n$. Plot of $a(n)/n$ for $n < 10^6$ (with $n$ log-scaled)

Every multiple of an abundant number is abundant.p. 695 For example, every multiple of 20 (including 20 itself) is abundant because if $n$ is a multiple of 20 then $\sigma(n) \geq \tfrac{n}{2} + \tfrac{n}{4} + \tfrac{n}{5} + \tfrac{n}{10} + \tfrac{n}{20} = n + \tfrac{n}{10}.$ Similarly, every multiple of a [perfect number](wikipedia://en/23670) (except the perfect number itself) is abundant.p. 134 For example, every multiple $n$ of 6 greater than 6 is abundant because $\sigma(n) \geq 1 + \tfrac{n}{2} + \tfrac{n}{3} + \tfrac{n}{6} = n + 1.$ An abundant number that is not the multiple of an abundant number or perfect number (i.e., whose proper divisors are all deficient) is called a [primitive abundant number](wikipedia://en/27077452).

Unlike for perfect numbers, [even and odd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_and_odd_numbers) abundant numbers are known to exist. The smallest odd abundant number is 945.p. 199 Consequently, infinitely many abundant numbers exist with each [parity](wikipedia://en/143135). The smallest abundant number that is not divisible by 2 or by 3 is 5391411025; its distinct [prime factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor) are 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23, and 29. An algorithm given by Iannucci in 2005 shows how to find the smallest abundant number not divisible by the first *k* [primes](wikipedia://en/23666) .[^o7] If $A(k)$ represents the smallest abundant number not divisible by the first *k* primes then for all $\epsilon>0$ we have

$$(1-\epsilon)(k\ln k)^{2-\epsilon}<\ln A(k)<(1+\epsilon)(k\ln k)^{2+\epsilon}$$

for sufficiently large *k*.

The set of abundant numbers has a non-zero [natural density](wikipedia://en/1819983): that is, as $N$ grows large, the fraction of the natural numbers less than $N$ that are abundant approaches a constant. This limiting fraction lies between 0.2476171 and 0.2476475.[^o8][^o9][^o10]

The first pair of consecutive abundant numbers is (5775, 5776), and the first consecutive triple is (171078830, 171078831, 171078832). Let $E(n)$ be the length of the longest run of consecutive abundant numbers not exceeding $n$. [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) (1935) showed that there exists two constants $c_1, c_2$ such that $c_1\log\log\log n \le E(n) \le c_2\log\log\log n$ for all sufficiently large $n$.[^o12] As a matter of fact, the limit

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{E(n)}{\log\log\log n}$$

exists, with value lying between 3.24 and 3.54.[^o13]

Every [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) greater than 20161 can be written as the sum of two abundant numbers.p. 273 The largest even number that is not the sum of two abundant numbers is 46.

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

Numbers whose sum of proper factors equals the number itself (such as 6 and 28) are called [perfect numbers](wikipedia://en/23670),p. 11p. 84 while numbers whose sum of proper factors is less than the number itself are called [deficient numbers](wikipedia://en/321843).p. 185p. 84 The first known classification of numbers as deficient, perfect or abundant was by [Nicomachus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicomachus) in his *[Introductio Arithmetica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introduction_to_Arithmetic)* (ca. 100 CE) and by [Theon of Smyrna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theon_of_Smyrna) in his *On Mathematics Useful for the Understanding of Plato* (ca. 100 CE).p. 74p. 128

The **abundancy index** of $n$ is the ratio $\sigma(n)/n$.p. 84 A number whose abundancy index is greater than any lower number is called a [superabundant number](wikipedia://en/1759187) .p. 88 Distinct numbers *n*<sub>1</sub>, *n*<sub>2</sub>, ... (whether abundant or not) with the same abundancy index are called [friendly numbers](wikipedia://en/3821872).

The sequence (*a*<sub>*k*</sub>) of least numbers *n* such that *σ*(*n*) > *kn*, in which *a*<sub>2</sub> = 12 corresponds to the first abundant number, grows very quickly . The smallest odd integer with abundancy index exceeding 3 is 1018976683725 = 3<sup>3</sup> × 5<sup>2</sup> × 7<sup>2</sup> × 11 × 13 × 17 × 19 × 23 × 29.[^o17]

If **p** = (*p*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *p<sub>n</sub>*) is a list of primes, then **p** is termed *abundant* if some integer composed only of primes in **p** is abundant. A necessary and sufficient condition for this is that the product of the numbers $\frac{p_k}{p_k - 1}$ be larger than 2.[^o18]

A number $n$ for which the sum of its divisors (including itself) is greater than the sum of the divisors of any smaller natural number is called a [highly abundant number](wikipedia://en/1759247).

An abundant number which is not a [semiperfect number](wikipedia://en/321918) is called a [weird number](wikipedia://en/321930).p. 144 An abundant number with abundance 1 is called a [quasiperfect number](wikipedia://en/321913); it is not known whether any quasiperfect numbers exist.
