# Disk (mathematics)

*Image: Circle-withsegments.svg.* Disk with black solid 3px blue solid 2px red solid 2px green solid 2px

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Disk"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **disk** ([also spelled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spelling_of_disc) **disc**)[^o2] is the region in a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) bounded by a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220). A disk is said to be *closed* if it contains the circle that constitutes its boundary, and *open* if it does not.[^o3]

:::

For a radius $r$, an open disk is usually denoted as $D_r$, and a closed disk is $\overline{D_r}$. However in the field of [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) the closed disk is usually denoted as $D^2$, while the open disk is $\operatorname{int} D^2$.

::: {#o5 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formulas"}

## Formulas {#Formulas}

In [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates), the *open disk* with center $(a, b)$ and radius *R* is given by the formula[^o2]

$$D = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : (x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 < R^2\},$$

while the *closed disk* with the same center and radius is given by

$$\overline{D} = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : (x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 \le R^2\}.$$

The [area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area_%28geometry%29) of a closed or open disk of radius *R* is π*R*<sup>2</sup> (see [area of a disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area_of_a_disk)).[^o6]

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The disk has [circular symmetry](wikipedia://en/2217599).[^o9]

The open disk and the closed disk are not topologically equivalent (that is, they are not [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660)), as they have different topological properties from each other. For instance, every closed disk is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) whereas every open disk is not compact.[^o10] However from the viewpoint of [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) they share many properties: both of them are [contractible](wikipedia://en/1752414)[^o11] and so are [homotopy equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_equivalent) to a single point. This implies that their [fundamental groups](wikipedia://en/11004) are trivial, and all [homology groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_group) are trivial except the 0th one, which is isomorphic to **Z**. The [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) of a point (and therefore also that of a closed or open disk) is 1.[^o12]

Every [continuous map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_map) from the closed disk to itself has at least one [fixed point](wikipedia://en/449738) (we don't require the map to be [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) or even [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective)); this is the case *n*=2 of the [Brouwer fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/4101).[^o13] The statement is false for the open disk:[^o14]

Consider for example the function $f(x,y)=\left(\frac{x+\sqrt{1-y^2}}{2},y\right)$ which maps every point of the open unit disk to another point on the open unit disk to the right of the given one. But for the closed unit disk it fixes every point on the half circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1 , x >0 .$

:::

## As a statistical distribution {#As_a_statistical_distribution}

*Image: Discdist.svg.* The average distance to a location from points on a disc

A uniform distribution on a unit circular disk is occasionally encountered in statistics. It most commonly occurs in operations research in the mathematics of urban planning, where it may be used to model a population within a city. Other uses may take advantage of the fact that it is a distribution for which it is easy to compute the probability that a given set of linear inequalities will be satisfied. ([Gaussian distributions](wikipedia://en/50347) in the plane require [numerical quadrature](wikipedia://en/170089).)

"An ingenious argument via elementary functions" shows the mean [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) between two points in the disk to be $\tfrac{128}{45\pi} \approx 0.90541$,[^o16] while direct integration in polar coordinates shows the mean squared distance to be $1$.

If we are given an arbitrary location at a distance $q$ from the center of the disk, it is also of interest to determine the average distance $b(q)$ from points in the distribution to this location and the average square of such distances. The latter value can be computed directly as $q^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}$.

### Average distance to an arbitrary internal point {#Average_distance_to_an_arbitrary_internal_point}

*Image: Cjcdiscin.svg.* The average distance from a disk to an internal point

To find $b(q)$ we need to look separately at the cases in which the location is internal or external, i.e. in which $q \text{≶} 1$, and we find that in both cases the result can only be expressed in terms of [complete elliptic integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_integrals).

If we consider an internal location, our aim (looking at the diagram) is to compute the expected value of $r$ under a distribution whose density is $\tfrac{1}{\pi}$ for $0 \le r \le s(\theta)$, integrating in polar coordinates centered on the fixed location for which the area of a cell is $r dr d\theta$ ; hence

$$b(q) = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} \textrm{d}\theta \int_0^{s(\theta)} r^2 \textrm{d}r = \frac{1}{3\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} s(\theta)^3 \textrm{d}\theta.$$

Here $s(\theta)$ can be found in terms of $q$ and $\theta$ using the [Law of cosines](wikipedia://en/19480890). The steps needed to evaluate the integral, together with several references, will be found in the paper by Lew et al.;[^o16] the result is that

$$b(q) = \frac{4}{9\pi}\biggl\{ 4(q^2-1)K(q^2) + (q^2+7)E(q^2)\biggr\}$$

where $K$ and $E$ are complete elliptic integrals of the first and second kinds.[^o18] $b(0) = \tfrac{2}{3}$; $b(1) = \tfrac{32}{9\pi} \approx 1.13177$.

### Average distance to an arbitrary external point {#Average_distance_to_an_arbitrary_external_point}

*Image: Cjcdiscex.svg.* The average distance from a disk to an external point

Turning to an external location, we can set up the integral in a similar way, this time obtaining

$$b(q) = \frac{2}{3\pi} \int_0^{\textrm{sin}^{-1}\tfrac{1}{q}} \biggl\{ s_{+}(\theta)^3-s_{-}(\theta)^3\biggr\} \textrm{d}\theta$$

where the law of cosines tells us that $s_{+}(\theta)$ and $s_{-}(\theta)$ are the roots for $s$ of the equation

$$s^2-2qs\,\textrm{cos}\theta+q^2\!-\!1=0.$$

Hence

$$b(q) = \frac{4}{3\pi} \int_0^{\textrm{sin}^{-1}\tfrac{1}{q}} \biggl\{ 3q^2\textrm{cos}^2\theta \sqrt{1-q^2 \textrm{sin}^2\theta} + \Bigl( 1-q^2 \textrm{sin}^2\theta\Bigr)^{\tfrac{3}{2}} \biggl\} \textrm{d}\theta.$$

We may substitute $u = q \sin\theta$ to get

$$\begin{aligned}b(q) &= \frac{4}{3\pi} \int_0^1 \biggl\{ 3\sqrt{q^2-u^2} \sqrt{1-u^2} + \frac{(1-u^2)^{\tfrac{3}{2}}}{\sqrt{q^2-u^2}} \biggr\} \textrm{d}u \\[0.6ex] &= \frac{4}{3\pi} \int_0^1 \biggl\{ 4\sqrt{q^2-u^2} \sqrt{1-u^2} - \frac{q^2-1}{q} \frac{\sqrt{1-u^2}}{\sqrt{q^2-u^2}} \biggr\} \textrm{d}u \\[0.6ex] &= \frac{4}{3\pi} \biggl\{ \frac{4q}{3} \biggl( (q^2+1)E(\tfrac{1}{q^2})-(q^2-1)K(\tfrac{1}{q^2}) \biggr) - (q^2-1) \biggl(qE(\tfrac{1}{q^2})-\frac{q^2-1}{q}K(\tfrac{1}{q^2}) \biggr) \biggr\} \\[0.6ex] &= \frac{4}{9\pi} \biggl\{ q(q^2+7)E(\tfrac{1}{q^2}) - \frac{q^2-1}{q}(q^2+3)K(\tfrac{1}{q^2}) \biggr\} \end{aligned}$$

using standard integrals.[^o20]

Hence again $b(1) = \tfrac{32}{9\pi}$, while also[^o21]

$$\lim_{q \to \infty} b(q) = q + \tfrac{1}{8q}.$$
