# Perfect information

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Perfect information"}

**Perfect information** is a concept in [game theory](wikipedia://en/11924) and [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics) that describes a situation where all players in a game or all participants in a market have knowledge of all relevant information in the system. This is different than [complete information](wikipedia://en/1603001), which implies [common knowledge](wikipedia://en/1409006) of each agent's utility functions, payoffs, strategies and "types". A system with perfect information may or may not have complete information.

:::

In economics this is sometimes described as "no hidden information" and is a feature of [perfect competition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_competition). In a market with perfect information all consumers and producers would have complete and instantaneous knowledge of all market prices, their own utility and cost functions.

In game theory, a [sequential game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequential_game) has perfect information if each player, when making any decision, is perfectly informed of all the events that have previously occurred, including the "initialisation event" of the [game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game) (e.g. the starting hands of each player in a card game).[^o2][^o3][^o4][^o5]

Games where some aspect of play is *hidden* from opponents – such as the cards in [poker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poker) and [bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contract_bridge) – are examples of games with **imperfect information**.[^o6][^o7]

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Go-Equipment.png.* [Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go_%28game%29) is a game of perfect information.

[Chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess) is an example of a game with perfect information, as each player can see all the pieces on the board at all times.[^o3] Other games with perfect information include [tic-tac-toe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tic-tac-toe), [Reversi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reversi), [checkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draughts), [shogi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shogi), and [Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go_%28game%29).[^o4]

Academic literature has not produced consensus on a standard definition of perfect information which defines whether games with chance, *but no secret information*, and games with [*simultaneous moves*](wikipedia://en/23992863) are games of perfect information.[^o5][^o10][^o11][^o12]

Games which are [sequential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequential_game) (players alternate in moving) and which have [chance events](wikipedia://en/2927358) (with known probabilities to all players) but *no secret information*, are sometimes considered games of perfect information. This includes games such as [backgammon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backgammon). However, some academic papers do not regard such games as games of perfect information because the results of chance themselves are unknown prior to them occurring.[^o5][^o10][^o11][^o12]

Games with *simultaneous moves* are generally not considered games of perfect information. This is because each player holds information, which is secret, and must play a move without knowing the opponent's secret information. Nevertheless, some such games are [symmetrical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_game), and fair. An example of a game in this category is [rock paper scissors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_paper_scissors).[^o5][^o10][^o11][^o12]

:::
