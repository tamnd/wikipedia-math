# Deficient number

*Image: Deficient number Cuisenaire rods 8.png.* Demonstration, with [Cuisenaire rods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuisenaire_rods), of the deficiency of the number 8

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Deficient number"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), a **deficient number** or **defective number** is a [positive integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_integer) $n$ for which the [sum of divisors](wikipedia://en/407249#Definition) of $n$ is less than $2n$. Equivalently, it is a number for which the sum of [proper divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_divisor) (or [aliquot sum](wikipedia://en/12374274)) is less than $n$. For example, the proper divisors of 8 are 1, 2, and 4, and their sum is less than 8, so 8 is deficient.

:::

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The first few deficient numbers are

1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 19, 21, 22, 23, 25, 26, 27, 29, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 37, 38, 39, 41, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 49, 50, ...

As an example, consider the number 21. Its proper divisors are 1, 3 and 7, and their sum is 11. Because 11 is less than 21, the number 21 is deficient. Its deficiency is 21 − 11 = 10.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

Since the aliquot sums of prime numbers equal 1, all [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) are deficient.[^n1] More generally, all odd numbers with one or two distinct prime factors are deficient. It follows that there are infinitely many [odd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_number) deficient numbers. There are also an infinite number of [even](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_number) deficient numbers as all [powers of two](wikipedia://en/376948) have the sum ($1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + ... + 2^{x - 1} = 2^{x} - 1$). The infinite family of numbers of form 2<sup>*n* − 1</sup> × *p*<sup>*m*</sup> where *m* > 0 and *p* is a prime > 2<sup>*n*</sup> − 1 are also deficient.

More generally, all [prime powers](wikipedia://en/1630483) $p^k$ are deficient, because their only proper divisors are $1, p, p^2, \dots, p^{k-1}$ which sum to $\frac{p^k-1}{p-1}$, which is at most $p^k-1$.[^n2]

All proper [divisors](wikipedia://en/49492) of deficient numbers are deficient.[^n3] Moreover, all proper divisors of [perfect numbers](wikipedia://en/23670) are deficient.[^n4]

There exists at least one deficient number in the interval $[n, n + (\log n)^2]$ for all sufficiently large *n*.[^n5]

:::

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

Closely related to deficient numbers are [perfect numbers](wikipedia://en/23670) with *σ*(*n*) = 2*n*, and [abundant numbers](wikipedia://en/321831) with *σ*(*n*) > 2*n*.

[Nicomachus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicomachus) was the first to subdivide numbers into deficient, perfect, or abundant, in his *[Introduction to Arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introduction_to_Arithmetic)* (circa 100 CE). However, he applied this classification only to the [even numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_number).[^n6]
