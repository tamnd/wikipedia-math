# Free module

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Free module"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **free module** is a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) that has a *basis*, that is, a [generating set](wikipedia://en/50718738) that is [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent). Every [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) is a free module,[^o2] but, if the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) of the coefficients is not a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) (not a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) in the [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346) case), then there exist non-free modules.

:::

Given any [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $S$ and ring $R$, there is a free $R$-module with basis $S$, which is called the *free module on* $S$ or *module of formal* $R$-*linear combinations* of the elements of $S$.

A [free abelian group](wikipedia://en/247296) is precisely a free module over the ring $\mathbb{Z}$ of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563).

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

For a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$ and an $R$-[module](wikipedia://en/276410) $M$, the set $E\subseteq M$ is a basis for $M$ if:

- $E$ is a [generating set](wikipedia://en/50718738) for $M$; that is to say, every element of $M$ is a finite sum of elements of $E$ multiplied by coefficients in $R$; and
- $E$ is [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent): for every set $\{e_1,\dots,e_n\}\subset E$ of distinct elements, $r_1 e_1 + r_2 e_2 + \cdots + r_n e_n = 0_M$ implies that $r_1 = r_2 = \cdots = r_n = 0_R$ (where $0_M$ is the zero element of $M$ and $0_R$ is the zero element of $R$).

A free module is a module with a basis.[^o5]

An immediate consequence of the second half of the definition is that the coefficients in the first half are unique for each element of *M*.

If $R$ has [invariant basis number](wikipedia://en/3327998), then by definition any two bases have the same cardinality. For example, nonzero commutative rings have invariant basis number. The cardinality of any (and therefore every) basis is called the **rank** of the free module $M$. If this cardinality is finite, the free module is said to be *free of finite rank*, or *free of rank* $n$ if the rank is known to be $n$.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

Let *R* be a ring.

- *R* is a free module of rank one over itself (either as a left or right module); any unit element is a basis.
- More generally, If *R* is commutative, a nonzero ideal *I* of *R* is free if and only if it is a [principal ideal](wikipedia://en/145126) generated by a [nonzerodivisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonzerodivisor), with a generator being a basis.[^o8]
- Over a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039) (e.g., $\mathbb{Z}$), a submodule of a free module is free.
- If *R* is commutative, the polynomial ring $R[X]$ in indeterminate *X* is a free module with a possible basis 1, *X*, *X*<sup>2</sup>, ....
- Let $A[t]$ be a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) over a commutative ring *A*, *f* a [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730) of degree *d* there, $B = A[t]/(f)$ and $\xi$ the image of *t* in *B*. Then *B* contains *A* as a subring and is free as an *A*-module with a basis $1, \xi, \dots, \xi^{d-1}$.
- For any non-negative integer *n*, $R^n = R \times \cdots \times R$, the [cartesian product](wikipedia://en/8887#Direct_product_of_modules) of *n* copies of *R* as a left *R*-module, is free. If *R* has [invariant basis number](wikipedia://en/3327998), then its [rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rank_of_a_module) is *n*.
- A [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) of free modules is free, while an infinite cartesian product of free modules is generally *not* free (cf. the [Baer–Specker group](wikipedia://en/19200651)).
- A finitely generated module over a commutative [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) is free if and only if it is [faithfully flat](wikipedia://en/492616#Faithful_flatness).[^o9] Also, [Kaplansky's theorem](wikipedia://en/62610482) states a projective module over a (possibly non-commutative) local ring is free.
- Sometimes, whether a module is free or not is [undecidable](wikipedia://en/15631055#Examples_of_undecidable_statements) in the set-theoretic sense. A famous example is the [Whitehead problem](wikipedia://en/212619), which asks whether a Whitehead group is free or not. As it turns out, the problem is independent of ZFC.

:::

## Formal linear combinations {#Formal_linear_combinations}

Given a set $E$ and ring $R$, there is a free $R$-module that has $E$ as a basis: namely, the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) of copies of *R* indexed by *E*

$$R^{(E)} = \bigoplus_{e \in E} R.$$

Explicitly, it is the submodule of the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/8887#Direct_product_of_modules) $\prod_E R$ (*R* is viewed as say a left module) that consists of the elements that have only finitely many nonzero components. One can [embed](wikipedia://en/52119) *E* into $R^{(E)}$ as a subset by identifying an element *e* with that of $R^{(E)}$ whose *e*-th component is 1 (the unity of *R*) and all the other components are zero. Then each element of $R^{(E)}$ can be written uniquely as

$$\sum_{e \in E} c_e e ,$$

where only finitely many $c_e$ are nonzero. It is called a *[formal linear combination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_linear_combination)* of elements of $E$.

A similar argument shows that every free left (resp. right) *R*-module is isomorphic to a direct sum of copies of *R* as left (resp. right) module.

### Another construction {#Another_construction}

The free module $R^{(E)}$ may also be constructed in the following equivalent way.

Given a ring *R* and a set *E*, first as a set we let

$$R^{(E)} = \{ f: E \to R \mid f(x) = 0 \text { for all but finitely many } x \in E \}.$$

We equip it with a structure of a left module such that the addition is defined by: for *x* in *E*,

$$(f+g)(x) = f(x) + g(x)$$

and the scalar multiplication by: for *r* in *R* and *x* in *E*,

$$(r f)(x) = r f(x)$$

Now, as an *R*-valued [function](wikipedia://en/185427) on *E*, each *f* in $R^{(E)}$ can be written uniquely as

$$f = \sum_{e \in E} c_e \delta_e$$

where $c_e$ are in *R* and only finitely many of them are nonzero and $\delta_e$ is given as

$$\delta_e(x) = \begin{cases} 1_R \quad\mbox{if } x=e \\ 0_R \quad\mbox{if } x\neq e \end{cases}$$

(this is a variant of the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890)). The above means that the subset $\{ \delta_e \mid e \in E \}$ of $R^{(E)}$ is a basis of $R^{(E)}$. The mapping $e \mapsto \delta_e$ is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between $E$ and this basis. Through this bijection, $R^{(E)}$ is a free module with the basis *E*.

## Universal property {#Universal_property}

The inclusion mapping $\iota : E\to R^{(E)}$ defined above is [universal](wikipedia://en/32245) in the following sense. Given an arbitrary function $f : E\to N$ from a set $E$ to a left $R$-module $N$, there exists a unique [module homomorphism](wikipedia://en/474118) $\overline{f}: R^{(E)}\to N$ such that $f = \overline{f} \circ\iota$; namely, $\overline{f}$ is defined by the formula:

$$\overline{f}\left (\sum_{e \in E} r_e e \right) = \sum_{e \in E} r_e f(e)$$

and $\overline{f}$ is said to be obtained by *extending $f$ by linearity.* The uniqueness means that each *R*-linear map $R^{(E)} \to N$ is uniquely determined by its [restriction](wikipedia://en/1589032) to *E*.

As usual for universal properties, this defines $R^{(E)}$ [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) a [canonical isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_isomorphism). Also the formation of $\iota : E\to R^{(E)}$ for each set *E* determines a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987)

$$R^{(-)}: \textbf{Set} \to R\text{-}\mathsf{Mod}, \, E \mapsto R^{(E)},$$

from the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829) to the category of left $R$-modules. It is called the [free functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_functor) and satisfies a natural relation: for each set *E* and a left module *N*,

$$\operatorname{Hom}_{\textbf{Set}}(E, U(N)) \simeq \operatorname{Hom}_R(R^{(E)}, N), \, f \mapsto \overline{f}$$

where $U: R\text{-}\mathsf{Mod} \to \textbf{Set}$ is the [forgetful functor](wikipedia://en/844783), meaning $R^{(-)}$ is a [left adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_adjoint) of the forgetful functor.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Many statements true for free modules extend to certain larger classes of modules. [Projective modules](wikipedia://en/364488) are direct summands of free modules. [Flat modules](wikipedia://en/492616) are defined by the property that tensoring with them preserves exact sequences. [Torsion-free modules](wikipedia://en/38400572) form an even broader class. For a finitely generated module over a PID (such as **Z**), the properties free, projective, flat, and torsion-free are equivalent.
