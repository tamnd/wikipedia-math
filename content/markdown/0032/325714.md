# Hopf algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hopf algebra"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Hopf algebra**, named after [Heinz Hopf](wikipedia://en/543082), is a structure that is simultaneously a ([unital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_algebra) associative) [algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) and a (counital coassociative) [coalgebra](wikipedia://en/310886), with these structures' compatibility making it a [bialgebra](wikipedia://en/310887), and that moreover is equipped with an [antihomomorphism](wikipedia://en/706374) satisfying a certain property. The [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200) of a Hopf algebra is particularly nice, since the existence of compatible comultiplication, counit, and antipode allows for the construction of [tensor products of representations](wikipedia://en/43079145), trivial representations, and dual representations.

:::

Hopf algebras occur naturally in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), where they originated and are related to the [H-space](wikipedia://en/600570) concept, in [group scheme](wikipedia://en/510523) theory, in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) (via the concept of a [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223)), and in numerous other places, making them probably the most familiar type of [bialgebra](wikipedia://en/310887). Hopf algebras are also studied in their own right, with much work on specific classes of examples on the one hand and classification problems on the other. They have diverse applications ranging from [condensed matter physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_matter_physics) and [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267)[^o2] to [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305)[^o3] and [LHC phenomenology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_Hadron_Collider).[^o4]

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Let $(H,\nabla,\eta,\Delta,\varepsilon)$ be an (associative and coassociative) [bialgebra](wikipedia://en/310887) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $K.$ One can consider the convolution algebra $\operatorname{Hom}_K(H,H)$ of [*K*-linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) maps with product given by: $(f\star g)(h)=\nabla\circ(f\otimes g)\circ\Delta(h).$ The identity of the convolution algebra is $\eta\circ\varepsilon.$

The bialgebra $H$ is said to be a Hopf algebra if the identity of $H,$ $\operatorname{id}_H\in\operatorname{Hom}_K(H,H)$ has a convolutive inverse $S\in\operatorname{Hom}_K(H,H)$ (called the **antipode**). The statement that $S$ is the inverse of $\operatorname{id}_H$ is equivalent to the [commutativity](wikipedia://en/138484) of the following diagram :

*Image: Hopf algebra.svg.* antipode commutative diagram

In the sumless [Sweedler notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweedler_notation), this property can also be expressed as

$$S(c_{(1)})c_{(2)}=c_{(1)}S(c_{(2)})=\varepsilon(c)1\qquad\mbox{ for all }c\in H.$$

As for [algebras](wikipedia://en/2112), one can replace the underlying field *K* with a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) *R* in the above definition.[^o7]

The definition of Hopf algebra is [self-dual](wikipedia://en/310885) (as reflected in the symmetry of the above diagram), so if one can define a [dual](wikipedia://en/7988) of *H* (which is always possible if *H* is finite-dimensional), then it is automatically a Hopf algebra.[^o8]

### Structure constants {#Structure_constants}

Fixing a basis $\{e_k\}$ for the underlying vector space, one may define the algebra in terms of [structure constants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_constant) for multiplication:

$$e_i\nabla e_j = \sum_k \mu^k_{\;ij} e_k$$

for co-multiplication:

$$\Delta e_i = \sum_{j,k} \nu^{\;jk}_i e_j\otimes e_k$$

and the antipode:

$$S e_i = \sum_j \tau_i^{\;j} e_j$$

Associativity then requires that

$$\mu^k_{\;ij}\mu^m_{\;kn}=\mu^k_{\;jn}\mu^m_{\;ik}$$

while co-associativity requires that

$$\nu_k^{\;ij}\nu_i^{\;mn}=\nu_k^{\;mi}\nu_i^{\;nj}$$

The connecting axiom requires that

$$\nu_k^{\;ij}\tau_j^{\;m}\mu^n_{\;im}=\nu_k^{\;jm}\tau_j^{\,\;i}\mu^n_{\;im}$$

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Antipode (algebra)"}

### Properties of the antipode {#Properties_of_the_antipode}

The antipode *S* is sometimes required to have a *K*-linear inverse, which is automatic in the finite-dimensional case,[^o12] or if *H* is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) or [cocommutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocommutative) (or more generally [quasitriangular](wikipedia://en/3727020)).

In general, *S* is an [antihomomorphism](wikipedia://en/706374),[^o13] so *S*<sup>2</sup> is a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564), which is therefore an automorphism if *S* was invertible (as may be required).

If *S*<sup>2</sup> = id<sub>*H*</sub>, then the Hopf algebra is said to be **involutive** (and the underlying algebra with involution is a [\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/293426)). If *H* is finite-dimensional semisimple over a field of characteristic zero, commutative, or cocommutative, then it is involutive.

If a bialgebra *B* admits an antipode *S*, then *S* is unique ("a bialgebra admits at most 1 Hopf algebra structure").[^o14] Thus, the antipode does not pose any extra structure which we can choose: Being a Hopf algebra is a property of a bialgebra.

The antipode is an analog to the inversion map on a group that sends *g* to *g*<sup>−1</sup>.[^o15]

:::

### Hopf subalgebras {#Hopf_subalgebras}

A subalgebra *A* of a Hopf algebra *H* is a Hopf subalgebra if it is a subcoalgebra of *H* and the antipode *S* maps *A* into *A*. In other words, a Hopf subalgebra A is a Hopf algebra in its own right when the multiplication, comultiplication, counit and antipode of *H* are restricted to *A* (and additionally the identity 1 of *H* is required to be in A). The Nichols–Zoeller freeness theorem of Warren Nichols and [Bettina Zoeller](wikipedia://en/75056172) (1989) established that the natural *A*-module *H* is free of finite rank if *H* is finite-dimensional: a generalization of [Lagrange's theorem for subgroups](wikipedia://en/31150).[^o18] As a corollary of this and integral theory, a Hopf subalgebra of a semisimple finite-dimensional Hopf algebra is automatically semisimple.

A Hopf subalgebra *A* is said to be right normal in a Hopf algebra *H* if it satisfies the condition of stability, *ad<sub>r</sub>*(*h*)(*A*) ⊆ *A* for all *h* in *H*, where the right adjoint mapping *ad<sub>r</sub>* is defined by *ad<sub>r</sub>*(*h*)(*a*) = *S*(*h*<sub>(1)</sub>)*ah*<sub>(2)</sub> for all *a* in *A*, *h* in *H*. Similarly, a Hopf subalgebra *A* is left normal in *H* if it is stable under the left adjoint mapping defined by *ad<sub>l</sub>*(*h*)(*a*) = *h*<sub>(1)</sub>*aS*(*h*<sub>(2)</sub>). The two conditions of normality are equivalent if the antipode *S* is bijective, in which case *A* is said to be a normal Hopf subalgebra.

A normal Hopf subalgebra *A* in *H* satisfies the condition (of equality of subsets of H): *HA*<sup>\+</sup> = *A*<sup>\+</sup>*H* where *A*<sup>\+</sup> denotes the kernel of the counit on *A*. This normality condition implies that *HA*<sup>\+</sup> is a Hopf ideal of *H* (i.e. an algebra ideal in the kernel of the counit, a coalgebra coideal and stable under the antipode). As a consequence one has a quotient Hopf algebra *H*/*HA*<sup>\+</sup> and epimorphism *H* → *H*/*A*<sup>\+</sup>*H*, a theory analogous to that of normal subgroups and quotient groups in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890).[^o19]

:::

::: {#o21 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Hopf order"}

### Hopf orders {#Hopf_orders}

A **Hopf order** *O* over an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) *R* with [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) *K* is an [order](wikipedia://en/1114171) in a Hopf algebra *H* over *K* which is closed under the algebra and coalgebra operations: in particular, the comultiplication Δ maps *O* to *O*⊗*O*.[^o22]

:::

### Group-like elements {#Group-like_elements}

A **group-like element** is a nonzero element *x* such that Δ(*x*) = *x*⊗*x*. The group-like elements form a group with inverse given by the antipode.[^o24] A **[primitive element](wikipedia://en/43337163)** *x* satisfies Δ(*x*) = *x*⊗1 + 1⊗*x*.[^o25][^o26]

:::

### Cohomology of Lie groups {#Cohomology_of_Lie_groups}

The cohomology algebra (over a field $K$) of a Lie group $G$ is a Hopf algebra: the multiplication is provided by the [cup product](wikipedia://en/848684), and the comultiplication

$$H^*(G,K) \rightarrow H^*(G\times G,K) \cong H^*(G,K)\otimes H^*(G,K)$$

by the group multiplication $G\times G\to G$. This observation was actually a source of the notion of Hopf algebra. Using this structure, Hopf proved a structure theorem for the cohomology algebra of Lie groups.

::: {#o41 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem (Hopf)"}

**Theorem (Hopf)**[^o42] Let $A$ be a finite-dimensional, [graded commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded-commutative), graded cocommutative Hopf algebra over a field of characteristic 0. Then $A$ (as an algebra) is a free exterior algebra with generators of odd degree.

:::

### Quantum groups and non-commutative geometry {#Quantum_groups_and_non-commutative_geometry}

Most examples above are either commutative (i.e. the multiplication is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative)) or co-commutative (i.e.[^o44] Δ = *T* ∘ Δ where the *twist map*[^o45] *T*: *H* ⊗ *H* → *H* ⊗ *H* is defined by *T*(*x* ⊗ *y*) = *y* ⊗ *x*). Other interesting Hopf algebras are certain "deformations" or "[quantizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantization_%28physics%29)" of those from example 3 which are neither commutative nor co-commutative. These Hopf algebras are often called *[quantum groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_groups)*, a term that is so far only loosely defined. They are important in [noncommutative geometry](wikipedia://en/295917), the idea being the following: a standard algebraic group is well described by its standard Hopf algebra of regular functions; we can then think of the deformed version of this Hopf algebra as describing a certain "non-standard" or "quantized" algebraic group (which is not an algebraic group at all). While there does not seem to be a direct way to define or manipulate these non-standard objects, one can still work with their Hopf algebras, and indeed one *identifies* them with their Hopf algebras. Hence the name "quantum group".

:::

## Representation theory {#Representation_theory}

Let *A* be a Hopf algebra, and let *M* and *N* be *A*-modules. Then, *M* ⊗ *N* is also an *A*-module, with

$$a(m\otimes n):=\Delta(a)(m \otimes n)=(a_1\otimes a_2)(m\otimes n)=(a_1 m \otimes a_2 n)$$

for *m* ∈ *M*, *n* ∈ *N* and Δ(*a*) = (*a*<sub>1</sub>, *a*<sub>2</sub>). Furthermore, we can define the trivial representation as the base field *K* with

$$a(m):=\epsilon(a)m$$

for *m* ∈ *K*. Finally, the dual representation of *A* can be defined: if *M* is an *A*-module and *M\** is its dual space, then

$$(af)(m):=f(S(a)m)$$

where *f* ∈ *M\** and *m* ∈ *M*.

The relationship between Δ, ε, and *S* ensure that certain natural homomorphisms of vector spaces are indeed homomorphisms of *A*-modules. For instance, the natural isomorphisms of vector spaces *M* → *M* ⊗ *K* and *M* → *K* ⊗ *M* are also isomorphisms of *A*-modules. Also, the map of vector spaces *M\** ⊗ *M* → *K* with *f* ⊗ *m* → *f*(*m*) is also a homomorphism of *A*-modules. However, the map *M* ⊗ *M\** → *K* is not necessarily a homomorphism of *A*-modules.

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

[Graded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded_algebra) Hopf algebras are often used in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801): they are the natural algebraic structure on the direct sum of all [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) or [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060) groups of an [H-space](wikipedia://en/600570).

[Locally compact quantum groups](wikipedia://en/1845113) generalize Hopf algebras and carry a [topology](wikipedia://en/30450). The algebra of all [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) on a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) is a locally compact quantum group.

[Quasi-Hopf algebras](wikipedia://en/4899676) are generalizations of Hopf algebras, where coassociativity only holds up to a twist. They have been used in the study of the [Knizhnik–Zamolodchikov equations](wikipedia://en/16119106).[^o48]

[Multiplier Hopf algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplier_Hopf_algebra) introduced by Alfons Van Daele in 1994[^o49] are generalizations of Hopf algebras where comultiplication from an algebra (with or without unit) to the [multiplier algebra](wikipedia://en/13542720) of tensor product algebra of the algebra with itself.

[Hopf group-(co)algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopf_group-%28co%29algebra) introduced by V. G. Turaev in 2000 are also generalizations of Hopf algebras.

### Weak Hopf algebras {#Weak_Hopf_algebras}

[Weak Hopf algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_Hopf_algebra), or quantum groupoids, are generalizations of Hopf algebras. Like Hopf algebras, weak Hopf algebras form a self-dual class of algebras; i.e., if *H* is a (weak) Hopf algebra, so is *H*\*, the dual space of linear forms on *H* (with respect to the algebra-coalgebra structure obtained from the natural pairing with *H* and its coalgebra-algebra structure). A weak Hopf algebra *H* is usually taken to be a

- finite-dimensional algebra and coalgebra with coproduct Δ: *H* → *H* ⊗ *H* and counit ε: *H* → *k* satisfying all the axioms of Hopf algebra except possibly Δ(1) ≠ 1 ⊗ 1 or ε(*ab*) ≠ ε(*a*)ε(*b*) for some *a,b* in *H*. Instead one requires the following:

$$(\Delta(1) \otimes 1)(1 \otimes \Delta(1)) = (1 \otimes \Delta(1))(\Delta(1) \otimes 1) = (\Delta \otimes \mbox{Id})\Delta(1)$$

$$\epsilon(abc) = \sum \epsilon(ab_{(1)})\epsilon(b_{(2)}c) = \sum \epsilon(ab_{(2)})\epsilon(b_{(1)}c)$$

for all *a*, *b*, and *c* in *H*.

- *H* has a weakened antipode *S*: *H* → *H* satisfying the axioms:

<!-- -->

1. $S(a_{(1)})a_{(2)} = 1_{(1)} \epsilon(a 1_{(2)})$ for all *a* in *H* (the right-hand side is the interesting projection usually denoted by Π<sup>*R*</sup>(*a*) or ε<sub>*s*</sub>(*a*) with image a separable subalgebra denoted by *H<sup>R</sup>* or *H<sub>s</sub>*);
1. $a_{(1)}S(a_{(2)}) = \epsilon(1_{(1)}a)1_{(2)}$ for all *a* in *H* (another interesting projection usually denoted by Π<sup>*R*</sup>(*a*) or ε<sub>*t*</sub>(*a*) with image a separable algebra *H<sup>L</sup>* or *H<sub>t</sub>*, anti-isomorphic to *H<sup>L</sup>* via *S*);
1. $S(a_{(1)})a_{(2)}S(a_{(3)}) = S(a)$ for all *a* in *H*.

Note that if Δ(1) = 1 ⊗ 1, these conditions reduce to the two usual conditions on the antipode of a Hopf algebra.

The axioms are partly chosen so that the category of *H*-modules is a [rigid monoidal category](wikipedia://en/25383437). The unit *H*-module is the separable algebra *H<sup>L</sup>* mentioned above.

For example, a finite [groupoid](wikipedia://en/12543) algebra is a weak Hopf algebra. In particular, the groupoid algebra on \[n\] with one pair of invertible arrows *e<sub>ij</sub>* and *e<sub>ji</sub>* between *i* and *j* in \[*n*\] is isomorphic to the algebra *H* of *n* x *n* matrices. The weak Hopf algebra structure on this particular *H* is given by coproduct Δ(*e<sub>ij</sub>*) = *e<sub>ij</sub>* ⊗ *e<sub>ij</sub>*, counit ε(*e<sub>ij</sub>*) = 1 and antipode *S*(*e<sub>ij</sub>*) = *e<sub>ji</sub>*. The separable subalgebras *H<sup>L</sup>* and *H<sup>R</sup>* coincide and are non-central commutative algebras in this particular case (the subalgebra of diagonal matrices).

## Analogy with groups {#Analogy_with_groups}

Groups can be axiomatized by the same diagrams (equivalently, operations) as a Hopf algebra, where *G* is taken to be a set instead of a module. In this case:

- the field *K* is replaced by the 1-point set
- there is a natural counit (map to 1 point)
- there is a natural comultiplication (the diagonal map)
- the unit is the identity element of the group
- the multiplication is the multiplication in the group
- the antipode is the inverse

In this philosophy, a group can be thought of as a Hopf algebra over the "[field with one element](wikipedia://en/13798040)".[^o55]

## Hopf algebras in braided monoidal categories {#Hopf_algebras_in_braided_monoidal_categories}

The definition of Hopf algebra is naturally extended to arbitrary [braided monoidal categories](wikipedia://en/1470466).[^n1][^n2] A Hopf algebra in such a category $(C,\otimes,I,\alpha,\lambda,\rho,\gamma)$ is a sextuple $(H,\nabla,\eta,\Delta,\varepsilon,S)$ where $H$ is an object in $C$, and

$\nabla:H\otimes H\to H$ (multiplication),

$\eta:I\to H$ (unit),

$\Delta:H\to H\otimes H$ (comultiplication),

$\varepsilon:H\to I$ (counit),

$S:H\to H$ (antipode)

— are morphisms in $C$ such that

\1) the triple $(H,\nabla,\eta)$ is a [monoid](wikipedia://en/3955446) in the monoidal category $(C,\otimes,I,\alpha,\lambda,\rho,\gamma)$, i.e. the following diagrams are commutative:

*Image: Minoid.png.* monoid in a monoidal category

\2) the triple $(H,\Delta,\varepsilon)$ is a [comonoid](wikipedia://en/3955446) in the monoidal category $(C,\otimes,I,\alpha,\lambda,\rho,\gamma)$, i.e. the following diagrams are commutative:

*Image: Cominoid.png.* comonoid in a monoidal category

\3) the structures of monoid and comonoid on $H$ are compatible: the multiplication $\nabla$ and the unit $\eta$ are morphisms of comonoids, and (this is equivalent in this situation) at the same time the comultiplication $\Delta$ and the counit $\varepsilon$ are morphisms of monoids; this means that the following diagrams must be commutative:

*Image: Multiplication-comultiplication.png.* coherence between multiplication and comultiplication

*Image: Unit-counit.png.* unit and counit in bialgebras

*Image: Uinit-counit-1.png.* unit and counit in bialgebras

where $\lambda_I:I\otimes I\to I$ is the left unit morphism in $C$, and $\theta$ the natural transformation of functors $(A\otimes B)\otimes (C\otimes D)\stackrel{\theta}{\rightarrowtail} (A\otimes C)\otimes (B\otimes D)$ which is unique in the class of natural transformations of functors composed from the structural transformations (associativity, left and right units, transposition, and their inverses) in the category $C$.

The quintuple $(H,\nabla,\eta,\Delta,\varepsilon)$ with the properties 1),2),3) is called a **bialgebra** in the category $(C,\otimes,I,\alpha,\lambda,\rho,\gamma)$;

\4) the diagram of antipode is commutative:

*Image: Antipode-1.png.* unit and counit in bialgebras

The typical examples are the following.

- **Groups**. In the monoidal category $(\text{Set},\times,1)$ of [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) (with the [cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) $\times$ as the tensor product, and an arbitrary singletone, say, $1=\{\varnothing\}$, as the unit object) a triple $(H,\nabla,\eta)$ is a [monoid in the categorical sense](wikipedia://en/3955446) if and only if it is a [monoid in the usual algebraic sense](wikipedia://en/19652), i.e. if the operations $\nabla(x,y)=x\cdot y$ and $\eta(1)$ behave like usual multiplication and unit in $H$ (but possibly without the invertibility of elements $x\in H$). At the same time, a triple $(H,\Delta,\varepsilon)$ is a comonoid in the categorical sense iff $\Delta$ is the diagonal operation $\Delta(x)=(x,x)$ (and the operation $\varepsilon$ is defined uniquely as well: $\varepsilon(x)=\varnothing$). And any such a structure of comonoid $(H,\Delta,\varepsilon)$ is compatible with any structure of monoid $(H,\nabla,\eta)$ in the sense that the diagrams in the section 3 of the definition always commute. As a corollary, each monoid $(H,\nabla,\eta)$ in $(\text{Set},\times,1)$ can naturally be considered as a bialgebra $(H,\nabla,\eta,\Delta,\varepsilon)$ in $(\text{Set},\times,1)$, and vice versa. The existence of the antipode $S:H\to H$ for such a bialgebra $(H,\nabla,\eta,\Delta,\varepsilon)$ means exactly that every element $x\in H$ has an inverse element $x^{-1}\in H$ with respect to the multiplication $\nabla(x,y)=x\cdot y$. Thus, in the category of sets $(\text{Set},\times,1)$ Hopf algebras are exactly [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) in the usual algebraic sense.
- **Classical Hopf algebras**. In the special case when $(C,\otimes,s,I)$ is the category of vector spaces over a given field $K$, the Hopf algebras in $(C,\otimes,s,I)$ are exactly the classical Hopf algebras described above.
- **Functional algebras on groups**. The standard [functional algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereotype_algebra#Examples) ${\mathcal C}(G)$, ${\mathcal E}(G)$, ${\mathcal O}(G)$, ${\mathcal P}(G)$ (of continuous, smooth, holomorphic, regular functions) on groups are Hopf algebras in the category (**Ste**,$\odot$) of [stereotype spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereotype_space),[^n3]
- **Group algebras**. The [stereotype group algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereotype_group_algebra) ${\mathcal C}^\star(G)$, ${\mathcal E}^\star(G)$, ${\mathcal O}^\star(G)$, ${\mathcal P}^\star(G)$ (of measures, distributions, analytic functionals and currents) on groups are Hopf algebras in the category (**Ste**,$\circledast$) of [stereotype spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereotype_space).[^n3] These Hopf algebras are used in the [duality theories for non-commutative groups](wikipedia://en/366136#Dualities_for_non-commutative_topological_groups).[^n4]
