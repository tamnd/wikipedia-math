# Semi-simplicity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Semi-simplicity"}

In mathematics, **semi-simplicity** is a widespread concept in disciplines such as [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200), [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997). A **semi-simple object** is one that can be decomposed into a sum of [simple objects](wikipedia://en/3875683), and simple objects are those that do not contain non-trivial proper sub-objects. The precise definitions of these words depends on the context.

:::

For example, if *G* is a [finite](wikipedia://en/323707) [group](wikipedia://en/19447), then a nontrivial finite-dimensional [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) *V* over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) is said to be *simple* if the only subrepresentations it contains are either {0} or *V* (these are also called [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982)). Now [Maschke's theorem](wikipedia://en/30876007) says that any finite-dimensional representation of a finite group is a direct sum of simple representations (provided the [characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_%28field%29) of the base field does not divide the order of the group). So in the case of finite groups with this condition, every finite-dimensional representation is semi-simple. Especially in algebra and representation theory, "semi-simplicity" is also called **complete reducibility**. For example, [Weyl's theorem on complete reducibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl's_theorem_on_complete_reducibility) says a finite-dimensional representation of a [semisimple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_Lie_group) [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) is semisimple.

A [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) (in other words a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) $T:V \to V$ with *V* a finite-dimensional vector space) is said to be *simple* if its only [invariant](wikipedia://en/585826) [linear subspaces](wikipedia://en/56357) under *T* are {0} and *V*. If the field is [algebraically closed](wikipedia://en/1018) (such as the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826)), then the only simple matrices are of size 1-by-1. A *semi-simple matrix* is one that is [similar](wikipedia://en/341149) to a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/26237950) of simple matrices; if the field is algebraically closed, this is the same as being [diagonalizable](wikipedia://en/246325).

These notions of semi-simplicity can be unified using the language of [semi-simple](wikipedia://en/1001361) [modules](wikipedia://en/276410), and generalized to semi-simple [categories](wikipedia://en/53759).

## Introductory example of vector spaces {#Introductory_example_of_vector_spaces}

If one considers all [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) (over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), such as the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438)), the simple vector spaces are those that contain no proper nontrivial subspaces. Therefore, the one-[dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28linear_algebra%29) vector spaces are the simple ones. So it is a basic result of linear algebra that any finite-dimensional vector space is the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) of simple vector spaces; in other words, all finite-dimensional vector spaces are semi-simple.

## Semi-simple matrices {#Semi-simple_matrices}

A [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) or, equivalently, a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) *T* on a finite-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V* is called *semi-simple* if every *T*-[invariant subspace](wikipedia://en/585826) has a [complementary](wikipedia://en/58899#Internal_direct_sum) *T*-invariant subspace.[^o4][^o5] This is equivalent to the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667107) of *T* being square-free.

For vector spaces over an [algebraically closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_closed) field *F*, semi-simplicity of a matrix is equivalent to [diagonalizability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagonalizable).[^o4] This is because such an operator always has an eigenvector; if it is, in addition, semi-simple, then it has a complementary invariant [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862), which itself has an eigenvector, and thus by induction is diagonalizable. Conversely, diagonalizable operators are easily seen to be semi-simple, as invariant subspaces are direct sums of eigenspaces, and any eigenbasis for this subspace can be extended to an eigenbasis of the full space.

## Semi-simple modules and rings {#Semi-simple_modules_and_rings}

For a fixed [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *R*, a nontrivial *R*-module *M* is simple, if it has no submodules other than 0 and *M*. An *R*-module *M* is [semi-simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-simple_module) if every *R*-submodule of *M* is an *R*-module direct summand of *M* (the trivial module 0 is semi-simple, but not simple). For an *R*-module *M*, *M* is semi-simple if and only if it is the direct sum of simple modules (the trivial module is the empty direct sum). Finally, *R* is called a [semi-simple ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-simple_ring) if it is semi-simple as an *R*-module. As it turns out, this is equivalent to requiring that any [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) *R*-module *M* is semi-simple.[^o7]

Examples of semi-simple rings include fields and, more generally, finite direct products of fields. For a finite group *G* [Maschke's theorem](wikipedia://en/30876007) asserts that the [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) *R*\[*G*\] over some ring *R* is semi-simple if and only if *R* is semi-simple and |*G*| is invertible in *R*. Since the theory of modules of *R*\[*G*\] is the same as the [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200) of *G* on *R*-modules, this fact is an important dichotomy, which causes [modular representation theory](wikipedia://en/1110685), i.e., the case when |*G*| *does* divide the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) of *R* to be more difficult than the case when |*G*| does not divide the characteristic, in particular if *R* is a field of characteristic zero. By the [Artin–Wedderburn theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin–Wedderburn_theorem), a unital [Artinian ring](wikipedia://en/489273) *R* is semisimple if and only if it is (isomorphic to) $M_{n_1}(D_1) \times M_{n_2}(D_2) \times \cdots \times M_{n_r}(D_r)$, where each $D_i$ is a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) and $M_n(D)$ is the ring of *n*-by-*n* matrices with entries in *D*.

An operator *T* is semi-simple in the sense above if and only if the subalgebra $F[T] \subseteq \operatorname{End}_F(V)$ generated by the powers (i.e., iterations) of *T* inside the ring of [endomorphisms](wikipedia://en/9569) of *V* is semi-simple.

As indicated above, the theory of semi-simple rings is much more easy than the one of general rings. For example, any [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence)

$$0 \to M' \to M \to M'' \to 0$$

of modules over a semi-simple ring must split, i.e., $M \cong M' \oplus M''$. From the point of view of [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410), this means that there are no non-trivial [extensions](wikipedia://en/840758). The ring **Z** of integers is not semi-simple: **Z** is not the direct sum of *n***Z** and **Z**/*n*.

## Semi-simple categories {#Semi-simple_categories}

Many of the above notions of semi-simplicity are recovered by the concept of a *semi-simple* category *C*. Briefly, a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) is a collection of objects and maps between such objects, the idea being that the maps between the objects preserve some structure inherent in these objects. For example, *R*-modules and *R*-linear maps between them form a category, for any ring *R*.

An [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063)[^o9] *C* is called semi-simple if there is a collection of simple objects $X_\alpha \in C$, i.e., ones with no [subobject](wikipedia://en/540476) other than the [zero object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_object) 0 and $X_\alpha$ itself, such that *any* object *X* is the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) (i.e., [coproduct](wikipedia://en/310889) or, equivalently, product) of finitely many simple objects. It follows from [Schur's lemma](wikipedia://en/521696) that the [endomorphism ring](wikipedia://en/59623)

$$\operatorname{End}_C(X)=\operatorname{Hom}_C(X, X)$$

in a semi-simple category is a product of matrix rings over division rings, i.e., semi-simple.

Moreover, a ring *R* is semi-simple if and only if the category of finitely generated *R*-modules is semisimple.

An example from [Hodge theory](wikipedia://en/501590) is the category of *polarizable pure [Hodge structures](wikipedia://en/10297305)*, i.e., pure Hodge structures equipped with a suitable [positive definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_bilinear_form) [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477). The presence of this so-called polarization causes the category of polarizable Hodge structures to be semi-simple.[^o10] Another example from algebraic geometry is the category of *pure [motives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motive_%28mathematics%29)* of [smooth](wikipedia://en/7837041) [projective varieties](wikipedia://en/320469) over a field *k* $\operatorname{Mot}(k)_\sim$ modulo an [adequate equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/25384599) $\sim$. As was conjectured by [Grothendieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_Grothendieck) and shown by [Jannsen](wikipedia://en/56877096), this category is semi-simple if and only if the equivalence relation is [numerical equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_equivalence).[^o11] This fact is a conceptual cornerstone in the theory of motives.

Semisimple abelian categories also arise from a combination of a [*t*-structure](wikipedia://en/1798923) and a (suitably related) [weight structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weight_structure) on a [triangulated category](wikipedia://en/1679700).[^o12]

## Semi-simplicity in representation theory {#Semi-simplicity_in_representation_theory}

One can ask whether the [category of finite-dimensional representations](wikipedia://en/55872661) of a group or a Lie algebra is semisimple, that is, whether every finite-dimensional representation decomposes as a direct sum of irreducible representations. The answer, in general, is no. For example, the representation of $\mathbb{R}$ given by

$$\Pi(x)=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & x\\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

is not a direct sum of irreducibles.[^o14] (There is precisely one nontrivial invariant subspace, the span of the first basis element, $e_1$.) On the other hand, if $G$ is [compact](wikipedia://en/750326), then every finite-dimensional representation $\Pi$ of $G$ admits an [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) with respect to which $\Pi$ is unitary, showing that $\Pi$ decomposes as a sum of irreducibles.[^o15] Similarly, if $\mathfrak{g}$ is a complex semisimple Lie algebra, every finite-dimensional representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ is a sum of irreducibles.[^o16] Weyl's original proof of this used the [unitarian trick](wikipedia://en/1569065): Every such $\mathfrak{g}$ is the complexification of the Lie algebra of a [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) compact Lie group $K$. Since $K$ is simply connected, there is a one-to-one correspondence between the finite-dimensional representations of $K$ and of $\mathfrak{g}$.[^o17] Thus, the just-mentioned result about representations of compact groups applies. It is also possible to prove semisimplicity of representations of $\mathfrak{g}$ directly by algebraic means, as in Section 10.3 of Hall's book.
