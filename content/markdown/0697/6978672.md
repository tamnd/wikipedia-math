# Comparability graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Comparability graph"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401) and [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), a **comparability graph** is an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) that connects pairs of elements that are [comparable](wikipedia://en/3834818) to each other in a [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order). Comparability graphs have also been called **transitively orientable graphs**, **partially orderable graphs**, **containment graphs**,[^o2] and **divisor graphs**.[^n1] An **incomparability graph** is an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) that connects pairs of elements that are not [comparable](wikipedia://en/3834818) to each other in a [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order).

:::

## Definitions and characterization {#Definitions_and_characterization}

*Image: Poset et graphe de comparabilité.svg.* Hasse diagram of a poset (left) and its comparability graph (right)

*Image: Forbidden interval subgraph.svg.* One of the forbidden induced subgraphs of a comparability graph. The generalized cycle $a-b-d-f-d-c-e-c-b-a$ in this graph has odd length (nine) but has no triangular chords.

For any [strict partially ordered set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strict_partial_order) $(S,<)$, the **comparability graph** of $(S, <)$ is the graph $(S, \perp)$ of which the vertices are the elements of $S$ and the edges are those pairs $\{u, v\}$ of elements such that $u < v$. That is, for a partially ordered set, take the [directed acyclic graph](wikipedia://en/204002), apply [transitive closure](wikipedia://en/292239), and remove orientation.

Equivalently, a comparability graph is a graph that has a **transitive orientation**,[^o5] an assignment of directions to the edges of the graph (i.e. an [orientation](wikipedia://en/36619818) of the graph) such that the [adjacency relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjacency_relation) of the resulting [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) is [transitive](wikipedia://en/200463): whenever there exist directed edges $(x,y)$ and $(y,z)$, there must exist an edge $(x,z)$.

One can represent any finite partial order as a family of sets, such that $x < y$ in the partial order whenever the set corresponding to $x$ is a subset of the set corresponding to $y$. In this way, comparability graphs can be shown to be equivalent to containment graphs of set families; that is, a graph with a vertex for each set in the family and an edge between two sets whenever one is a subset of the other.[^o6] Alternatively, one can represent the partial order by a family of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), such that $x < y$ whenever the integer corresponding to $x$ is a [divisor](wikipedia://en/49492) of the integer corresponding to $y$. Because of this construction, comparability graphs have also been called divisor graphs.[^n1]

Comparability graphs can be characterized as the graphs such that, for every *generalized cycle* (see below) of odd length, one can find an edge $(x,y)$ connecting two vertices that are at distance two in the cycle. Such an edge is called a *triangular chord*. In this context, a generalized cycle is defined to be a [closed walk](wikipedia://en/325802#Walks) that uses each edge of the graph at most once in each direction.[^o8] Comparability graphs can also be characterized by a list of [forbidden induced subgraphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forbidden_induced_subgraph).[^o9]

## Cocomparability graph {#Cocomparability_graph}

*Image: Cocomparability graph.svg.* The cocomparability graph (right) of a poset (left)

A **cocomparability graph** is the [complement](wikipedia://en/1175649) of a comparability graph. That is, given a comparability graph $G = (V, E)$, its cocomparability graph $G\text{̅} = (V, E\text{̅})$ has the same vertex set but complementary edge set: two vertices are adjacent in $G\text{̅}$ if and only if they are not adjacent in $G$.

Cocomparability graphs are precisely the intersection graphs of continuous curves between two parallel lines, or equivalently, the intersection graphs of intervals on two parallel lines.[^o12] A graph is a cocomparability graph if and only if its complement admits a transitive orientation.

Cocomparability graphs form an important subclass of [perfect graphs](wikipedia://en/670531), inheriting this property from the fact that both comparability graphs and their complements are perfect (by [Dilworth's theorem](wikipedia://en/749033) and [Mirsky's theorem](wikipedia://en/28848438) respectively).[^o13]

Every cocomparability graph is [asteroidal triple-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroidal_triple-free_graph) (AT-free).[^o14] This places them within the hierarchy: [interval](wikipedia://en/363423) ⊂ [trapezoid](wikipedia://en/31675608) ⊂ cocomparability ⊂ [AT-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AT-free_graph), and [permutation](wikipedia://en/17625375) ⊂ [trapezoid](wikipedia://en/31675608) ⊂ cocomparability ⊂ [AT-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AT-free_graph).

The class of cocomparability graphs is self-complementary in the sense that the complement of a cocomparability graph is a comparability graph, and vice versa.

**[Interval graphs](wikipedia://en/363423)** are exactly the graphs that are [chordal](wikipedia://en/744165) and have cocomparability complements; that is, the [complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complement_%28graph_theory%29) of any [interval graph](wikipedia://en/363423) is a comparability graph, and the comparability relation is called an [interval order](wikipedia://en/11680645).[^o15]

Cocomparability graphs are a subclass of **[string graphs](wikipedia://en/16081202)**; the [complement](wikipedia://en/1175649) of every comparability graph is a string graph.[^o16]

:::

## Relation to other graph families {#Relation_to_other_graph_families}

Every [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) is a comparability graph, the comparability graph of a [total order](wikipedia://en/30330). All acyclic orientations of a complete graph are transitive. Every [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431) is also a comparability graph. Orienting the edges of a bipartite graph from one side of the bipartition to the other results in a transitive orientation, corresponding to a partial order of height two. As (Seymour 2006) observes, every comparability graph that is neither complete nor bipartite has a [skew partition](wikipedia://en/36707156).

A [permutation graph](wikipedia://en/17625375) is a containment graph on a set of intervals.[^o19] Therefore, permutation graphs are another subclass of comparability graphs.

The [trivially perfect graphs](wikipedia://en/18033364) are the comparability graphs of [rooted trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rooted_tree).[^o20] [Cographs](wikipedia://en/1175666) can be characterized as the comparability graphs of [series-parallel partial orders](wikipedia://en/28826384); thus, cographs are also comparability graphs.[^o21]

[Threshold graphs](wikipedia://en/11503485) are another special kind of comparability graph.

Every comparability graph is [perfect](wikipedia://en/670531). The perfection of comparability graphs is [Mirsky's theorem](wikipedia://en/28848438), and the perfection of their complements is [Dilworth's theorem](wikipedia://en/749033); these facts, together with the [perfect graph theorem](wikipedia://en/670602) can be used to prove Dilworth's theorem from Mirsky's theorem or vice versa.[^o22] More specifically, comparability graphs are [perfectly orderable graphs](wikipedia://en/21051205), a subclass of perfect graphs: a [greedy coloring](wikipedia://en/21051195) algorithm for a [topological ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_ordering) of a transitive orientation of the graph will optimally color them.[^o23]

## Algorithms {#Algorithms}

A transitive orientation of a graph, if it exists, can be found in linear time.[^o26] However, the algorithm for doing so will assign orientations to the edges of any graph, so to complete the task of testing whether a graph is a comparability graph, one must test whether the resulting orientation is transitive, a problem provably equivalent in complexity to [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280).

Because comparability graphs (and cocomparability graphs) are perfect, many problems that are hard on more general classes of graphs, including [graph coloring](wikipedia://en/426743) and the [independent set problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_set_problem), can be solved for these graphs in polynomial time.

:::
