# Asymmetric graph

*Image: Asym-graph.PNG.* The eight 6-vertex asymmetric graphs

*Image: Frucht graph.dot.svg.* The [Frucht graph](wikipedia://en/23784721), one of the five smallest asymmetric [cubic graphs](wikipedia://en/980508).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Asymmetric graph"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) is called an **asymmetric graph** if it has no nontrivial [symmetries](wikipedia://en/15094186).

:::

Formally, an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/15094186) of a graph is a [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) $p$ of its [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) with the property that any two vertices $u$ and $v$ are adjacent [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $p(u)$ and $p(v)$ are adjacent. The [identity mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_mapping) of a graph is always an automorphism, and is called the [trivial automorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivial_automorphism) of the graph. An asymmetric graph is a graph for which there are no other automorphisms.

Note that the term "asymmetric graph" is not a negation of the term "[symmetric graph](wikipedia://en/1577896)," as the latter refers to a stronger condition than possessing nontrivial symmetries.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The smallest asymmetric [non-trivial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivial_graph) graphs have 6 vertices.[^o11] The smallest asymmetric [regular graphs](wikipedia://en/85821) have ten vertices; there exist 10-vertex asymmetric graphs that are 4-regular and 5-regular.[^o4][^o5] One of the five smallest asymmetric [cubic graphs](wikipedia://en/980508)[^o6] is the twelve-vertex [Frucht graph](wikipedia://en/23784721) discovered in 1939.[^o7] According to a strengthened version of [Frucht's theorem](wikipedia://en/26259599), there are infinitely many asymmetric cubic graphs.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

The class of asymmetric graphs is closed under [complements](wikipedia://en/1175649): a graph *G* is asymmetric if and only if its complement is.[^o11] Any *n*-vertex asymmetric graph can be made symmetric by adding and removing a total of at most *n*/2 + o(*n*) edges.[^o11]

:::

## Random graphs {#Random_graphs}

The proportion of graphs on *n* vertices with a nontrivial automorphism tends to zero as *n* grows, which is informally expressed as "[almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) finite graphs are asymmetric". In contrast, again informally, "almost all infinite graphs have nontrivial symmetries." More specifically, [countably](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) infinite [random graphs](wikipedia://en/358477) in the [Erdős–Rényi model](wikipedia://en/10477221) are, with [probability 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_1), [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) to the highly symmetric [Rado graph](wikipedia://en/7785594).[^o11]

## Trees {#Trees}

The smallest asymmetric [tree](wikipedia://en/48560) has seven vertices: it consists of three paths of lengths 1, 2, and 3, linked at a common endpoint.[^o13] In contrast to the situation for graphs, almost all trees are symmetric. In particular, if a tree is chosen [uniformly at random](wikipedia://en/1699214) among all trees on *n* labeled nodes, then with [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) tending to 1 as *n* increases, the tree will contain some two leaves adjacent to the same node and will have symmetries exchanging these two leaves.[^o11]
