# Logical equality

the logical operator in propositional calculus

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Logical equality"}

**Logical equality** is a [logical operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_operator) that compares two [truth values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth_values), or more generally, two [formulas](wikipedia://en/404582), such that it gives the value *[True](wikipedia://en/29920)* if both arguments have the same truth value, and *[False](wikipedia://en/229823)* if they are different. In the case where formulas have [free variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_variables), we say two formulas are equal when their truth values are equal for all possible resolutions of free variables. It corresponds to [equality](wikipedia://en/90446) in [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/54476844) and to the [logical biconditional](wikipedia://en/228783) in [propositional calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus).

:::

It is customary practice in various applications, if not always technically precise, to indicate the operation of **logical equality** on the logical operands *x* and *y* by any of the following forms:

$$\begin{aligned} x &\leftrightarrow y & x &\Leftrightarrow y & \mathrm Exy \\ x &\mathrm{~EQ~} y & x &= y \end{aligned}$$

Some logicians, however, draw a firm distinction between a *functional form*, like those in the left column, which they interpret as an application of a function to a pair of arguments — and thus a mere indication that the value of the compound expression depends on the values of the component expressions — and an *equational form*, like those in the right column, which they interpret as an assertion that the arguments have equal values, in other words, that the functional value of the compound expression is *true*.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

**Logical equality** is an [operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_operation) on two [logical values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_value), typically the values of two [propositions](wikipedia://en/81094), that produces a value of *true* if and only if both operands are false or both operands are true.

## Inequality {#Inequality}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the plus sign "+" almost invariably indicates an operation that satisfies the axioms assigned to addition in the type of [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) that is known as a *[field](wikipedia://en/10603)*. For Boolean algebra, this means that the logical operation signified by "+" is not the same as the [inclusive disjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusive_disjunction) signified by "∨" but is actually equivalent to the logical inequality operator signified by "≠", or what amounts to the same thing, the [exclusive disjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclusive_disjunction) signified by "XOR" or "⊕". Naturally, these variations in usage have caused some failures to communicate between mathematicians and switching engineers over the years. At any rate, one has the following array of corresponding forms for the symbols associated with logical inequality:

$$\begin{aligned} x &+ y & x &\not\equiv y & Jxy \\ x &\mathrm{~XOR~} y & x &\ne y \end{aligned}$$

This explains why "EQ" is often called "[XNOR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XNOR)" in the [combinational logic](wikipedia://en/89371) of circuit engineers, since it is the *negation* of the *[XOR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XOR)* operation; "NXOR" is a less commonly used alternative.[^o8] Another rationalization of the admittedly circuitous name "XNOR" is that one begins with the "both false" operator NOR and then adds the eXception "or both true".
