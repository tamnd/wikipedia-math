# Wiener deconvolution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Wiener deconvolution"}

*Image: Image restoration (motion blur, Wiener filtering).png.* From left: Original image, blurred image, image [deblurred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deblurring) using Wiener deconvolution.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Wiener deconvolution** is an application of the [Wiener filter](wikipedia://en/1216721) to the [noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise) problems inherent in [deconvolution](wikipedia://en/275626). It works in the [frequency domain](wikipedia://en/370346), attempting to minimize the impact of deconvolved noise at frequencies which have a poor [signal-to-noise ratio](wikipedia://en/41706).

:::

The Wiener deconvolution method has widespread use in [image](wikipedia://en/71925) deconvolution applications, as the frequency spectrum of most visual images is fairly well behaved and may be estimated easily.

Wiener deconvolution is named after [Norbert Wiener](wikipedia://en/63185).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given a system:

$$\ y(t) = (h*x)(t) + n(t)$$

where $*$ denotes [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) and:

- $\ x(t)$ is some unknown original signal at time $\ t$.
- $\ h(t)$ is the known [impulse response](wikipedia://en/545825) of a [linear time-invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LTI_system_theory) system
- $\ n(t)$ is some unknown additive noise, [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence) of $\ x(t)$
- $\ y(t)$ is our known observed signal

Our goal is to find some $\ g(t)$ so that we can estimate $\ x(t)$ as follows:

$$\ \hat{x}(t) = (g*y)(t)$$

where $\ \hat{x}(t)$ is an estimate of $\ x(t)$ that minimizes the [mean square error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_square_error)

$$\ \epsilon(t) = \mathbb{E} \left| x(t) - \hat{x}(t) \right|^2,$$

with $\ \mathbb{E}$ denoting the [expectation](wikipedia://en/9653). The Wiener deconvolution filter provides such a $\ g(t)$. The filter is most easily described in the [frequency domain](wikipedia://en/370346):

$$\ G(f) = \frac{H^*(f)S(f)}{ |H(f)|^2S(f) + N(f) }$$

where:

- $\ G(f)$ and $\ H(f)$ are the [Fourier transforms](wikipedia://en/52247) of $\ g(t)$ and $\ h(t)$,
- $\ S(f) = \mathbb{E}|X(f)|^2$ is the mean [power spectral density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_spectral_density) of the original signal $\ x(t)$,
- $\ N(f) = \mathbb{E}|V(f)|^2$ is the mean power spectral density of the noise $\ n(t)$,
- $X(f)$, $Y(f)$, and $V(f)$ are the Fourier transforms of $x(t)$, $y(t)$, and $n(t)$, respectively,
- the superscript ${}^*$ denotes [complex conjugation](wikipedia://en/173918).

The filtering operation may either be carried out in the time-domain, as above, or in the frequency domain:

$$\ \hat{X}(f) = G(f)Y(f)$$

and then performing an [inverse Fourier transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_Fourier_transform) on $\ \hat{X}(f)$ to obtain $\ \hat{x}(t)$.

Note that in the case of images, the arguments $\ t$ and $\ f$ above become two-dimensional; however the result is the same.

:::

## Interpretation {#Interpretation}

The operation of the Wiener filter becomes apparent when the filter equation above is rewritten:

$$\begin{aligned} G(f) & = \frac{1}{H(f)} \left[ \frac{ 1 }{ 1 + 1/(|H(f)|^2 \mathrm{SNR}(f))} \right] \end{aligned}$$

Here, $\ 1/H(f)$ is the inverse of the original system, $\ \mathrm{SNR}(f) = S(f)/N(f)$ is the [signal-to-noise ratio](wikipedia://en/41706), and $\ |H(f)|^2 \mathrm{SNR}(f)$ is the ratio of the pure filtered signal to [noise spectral density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise_spectral_density). When there is zero noise (i.e. infinite signal-to-noise), the term inside the square brackets equals 1, which means that the Wiener filter is simply the inverse of the system, as we might expect. However, as the noise at certain frequencies increases, the signal-to-noise ratio drops, so the term inside the square brackets also drops. This means that the Wiener filter attenuates frequencies according to their filtered signal-to-noise ratio.

The Wiener filter equation above requires us to know the spectral content of a typical image, and also that of the noise. Often, we do not have access to these exact quantities, but we may be in a situation where good estimates can be made. For instance, in the case of photographic images, the signal (the original image) typically has strong low frequencies and weak high frequencies, while in many cases the noise content will be relatively flat with frequency.

## Derivation {#Derivation}

As mentioned above, we want to produce an estimate of the original signal that minimizes the mean square error, which may be expressed:

$\ \epsilon(f) = \mathbb{E} \left| X(f) - \hat{X}(f) \right|^2$ .

The equivalence to the previous definition of $\epsilon$, can be derived using [Plancherel theorem](wikipedia://en/454315) or [Parseval's theorem](wikipedia://en/706435) for the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247).

If we substitute in the expression for $\ \hat{X}(f)$, the above can be rearranged to

$$\begin{aligned} \epsilon(f) & = \mathbb{E} \left| X(f) - G(f)Y(f) \right|^2 \\ & = \mathbb{E} \left| X(f) - G(f) \left[ H(f)X(f) + V(f) \right] \right|^2 \\ & = \mathbb{E} \big| \left[ 1 - G(f)H(f) \right] X(f) - G(f)V(f) \big|^2 \end{aligned}$$

If we expand the quadratic, we get the following:

$$\begin{aligned} \epsilon(f) & = \Big[ 1-G(f)H(f) \Big] \Big[ 1-G(f)H(f) \Big]^*\, \mathbb{E}|X(f)|^2 \\ & {} - \Big[ 1-G(f)H(f) \Big] G^*(f)\, \mathbb{E}\Big\{X(f)V^*(f)\Big\} \\ & {} - G(f) \Big[ 1-G(f)H(f) \Big]^*\, \mathbb{E}\Big\{V(f)X^*(f)\Big\} \\ & {} + G(f) G^*(f)\, \mathbb{E}|V(f)|^2 \end{aligned}$$

However, we are assuming that the noise is independent of the signal, therefore:

$$\ \mathbb{E}\Big\{X(f)V^*(f)\Big\} = \mathbb{E}\Big\{V(f)X^*(f)\Big\} = 0$$

Substituting the power spectral densities $\ S(f)$ and $\ N(f)$, we have:

$$\epsilon(f) = \Big[ 1-G(f)H(f) \Big]\Big[ 1-G(f)H(f) \Big]^ * S(f) + G(f)G^*(f)N(f)$$

To find the minimum error value, we calculate the [Wirtinger derivative](wikipedia://en/28479408) with respect to $\ G(f)$ and set it equal to zero.

$$\ \frac{d\epsilon(f)}{dG(f)} = 0 \Rightarrow G^*(f)N(f) - H(f)\Big[1 - G(f)H(f)\Big]^* S(f) = 0$$

This final equality can be rearranged to give the Wiener filter.
