# Engquist–Majda absorbing boundary condition

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Engquist–Majda absorbing boundary condition"}

*Image: Mur absorbing boundary condition in 1D FDTD.gif.* Demonstration of Mur absorbing boundary condition in a 1D finite-difference time-domain simulation

In [numerical methods for partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/1634583), **Engquist–Majda absorbing boundary conditions** or **Lindman–Engquist–Majda absorbing boundary conditions** are a hierarchy of [absorbing boundary conditions](wikipedia://en/82452187) for the numerical solution of [wave equations](wikipedia://en/33691). Named after mathematicians [Björn Engquist](wikipedia://en/2070192) and [Andrew Majda](wikipedia://en/23295125), they are designed to allow waves to exit a finite computational domain with minimal artificial [reflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_%28physics%29) through the use of [one-way wave equations](wikipedia://en/63245755), essentially making the boundaries transparent to outgoing [radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation). Within the context of [computational electromagnetics](wikipedia://en/3849994), they are known as **Mur absorbing boundary condition** after Gerrit Mur, who introduced a discretized version of the boundary conditions for [finite-difference time-domain method](wikipedia://en/1908142) in 1981.

:::

## Theory {#Theory}

A simple form of Engquist–Majda absorbing boundary condition can be formulated through one-dimensional scalar wave equation:[^o17]

$$\left(L_x^2- v^{-2} L_t^2\right) U(x,t)=G U(x,t)=0$$

where $L_{\{x,t\}}=\partial_{\{x,t\}}$ are the partial [differential operators](wikipedia://en/263886) and $v$ is the [wave velocity](wikipedia://en/33516#Wave_velocity); $G$ is the resultant wave function operator. This operator can be split into two [one-way wave operators](wikipedia://en/63245755), resulting in [advection](wikipedia://en/166377) equations:

$$G U(x,t)=G^+ G^- U(x,t)=0$$

$$G^+=L_x+v^{-1} L_t$$

$$G^-=L_x-v^{-1} L_t$$

Applying the following boundary conditions at a simulation region from $x=0$ to $x=L$ results in the cancellation of outgoing waves:

$$G^+ U(x,t)|_{x=0}=\left(L_x+v^{-1} L_t\right)U(x,t)|_{x=0}=0$$

$$G^- U(x,t)|_{x=L}=\left(L_x-v^{-1} L_t\right)U(x,t)|_{x=L}=0$$

In the case of higher dimensions, i.e. the two-dimensional scalar wave equation, the operator form can be written as:[^o9]

$$\left(L_x^2+L_y^2 - v^{-2} L_t^2\right) U(x,y,t)=G^+ G^- U(x,y,t)=0$$

For waves traveling along the $x$-axis, the operator splitting results in:

$$G^+ U(x,y,t)|_{x=0}=\left(L_x+v^{-1} L_t \sqrt{1 - v^{2}\frac{L_y^2}{L_t^2}}\right)U(x,t)|_{x=0}=0$$

$$G^- U(x,y,t)|_{x=L}=\left(L_x-v^{-1} L_t \sqrt{1 - v^{2}\frac{L_y^2}{L_t^2}}\right)U(x,t)|_{x=L}=0$$

The terms in square brackets constitute [pseudo-differential operators](wikipedia://en/973479) and their theoretical exact forms are [nonlocal](wikipedia://en/61563184). In practice, they can be locally approximated with their [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) representations. First-order approximation $(\sqrt{1-s^2} \approx 1)$ yields:

$$G^+ \approx L_x+v^{-1} L_t$$

$$G^- \approx L_x-v^{-1} L_t$$

which is equivalent to the Engquist–Majda condition for one-dimensional wave equation: in the theoretical limit, it perfectly absorbs the normally incident waves while causing reflections for waves impinging on other angles. A second-order approximation $(\sqrt{1-s^2} \approx 1-s^2/2)$ yields a better performance:

$$G^+ \approx L_x+v^{-1} L_t \left[1-v^{2}\frac{L_y^2}{2 L_t^2}\right]=L_x+v^{-1} L_t-\frac{v^2 L_y^2}{2L_t^2}$$

$$G^- \approx L_x-v^{-1} L_t \left[1-v^{2}\frac{L_y^2}{2 L_t^2}\right]=L_x-v^{-1} L_t+\frac{v^2 L_y^2}{2L_t^2}$$

Higher-order boundary conditions can also be obtained through [Padé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padé_approximation) or [Chebyshev approximation](wikipedia://en/184539) of the pseudo-differential operators;[^o19][^o8] these are known as generalized Trefethen–Halpern absorbing boundary conditions.[^o17]

## History and applications {#History_and_applications}

Engquist and Majda's seminal paper on the absorbing boundary conditions was published in 1977.[^o9] An earlier form of the absorbing boundary conditions was previously reported in 1973 by physicist and [Los Alamos National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Alamos_National_Laboratory) staff member Erick L. Lindman,[^o14][^o15] who first suggested the use of one-way equations to construct absorbing boundaries.[^o12] Since their introduction, Engquist–Majda absorbing boundary conditions have been applied to numerical solutions of various different problems in areas ranging from [acoustics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustics) to [seismology](wikipedia://en/28935), particularly within [finite difference](wikipedia://en/6054681) and [finite element](wikipedia://en/18233581) formulations.[^o7][^o18][^o10][^o11][^o13] In [computational electromagnetics](wikipedia://en/3849994), they are known as Mur boundary conditions, named after Gerrit Mur's extension of the Engquist–Majda operators to the [finite-difference time-domain method](wikipedia://en/1908142) (FDTD).[^o16] While Mur boundary condition enjoyed popularity in the FDTD applications for a decade following its introduction, it was subsequently superseded by more efficient [perfectly matched layers](wikipedia://en/4262792)[^o6] and more accurate exact absorbing conditions[^o21] in the 1990s.

Engquist–Majda absorbing boundary conditions, alongside the [Dirac equation](wikipedia://en/39407), were instrumental in the development of an exact one-way wave equation in 2025.[^o20]
