# Kneser graph

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Kneser graph"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **Kneser graph** $K(n, k)$ (alternatively $KG_{n,k}$) is the [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) whose [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) correspond to the [$k$-element subsets of a set of $n$ elements](wikipedia://en/5308), and where two vertices are adjacent [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the two corresponding sets are [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359). Kneser graphs are named after [Martin Kneser](wikipedia://en/17620650), who first investigated them in 1956.

:::

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Kneser graph KG(7,3).jpg.* Kneser graph $O_{4} = K(7, 3)$

The Kneser graph $K(n, 1)$ is the [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) on $n$ vertices.

The Kneser graph $K(n, 2)$ is the [complement](wikipedia://en/1175649) of the [line graph](wikipedia://en/675231) of the complete graph on $n$ vertices.

The Kneser graph $K(2n - 1, n - 1)$ is the [odd graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_graph) $O_{n}$; in particular $O_{3} = K(5, 2)$ is the [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926) (see top right figure).

The Kneser graph $O_{4} = K(7, 3)$, visualized on the right.

## Properties {#Properties}

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Basic properties"}

### Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

The Kneser graph $K(n,k)$ has $\tbinom{n}{k}$ vertices. Each vertex has exactly $\tbinom{n-k}{k}$ neighbors.

The Kneser graph is [vertex transitive](wikipedia://en/581175) and [arc transitive](wikipedia://en/1577896). When $k=2$, the Kneser graph is a [strongly regular graph](wikipedia://en/1399856), with parameters $( \tbinom{n}{2}, \tbinom{n-2}{2}, \tbinom{n-4}{2}, \tbinom{n-3}{2} )$. However, it is not strongly regular when $k>2$, as different pairs of nonadjacent vertices have different numbers of common neighbors depending on the size of the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of the corresponding pairs of sets.

Because Kneser graphs are [regular](wikipedia://en/85821) and [edge-transitive](wikipedia://en/584406), their [vertex connectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph) equals their [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448), except for $K(2k,k)$ which is [disconnected](wikipedia://en/1614492). More precisely, the connectivity of $K(n,k)$ is $\tbinom{n-k}{k},$ the same as the number of neighbors per vertex.[^n1]

:::

### Chromatic number {#Chromatic_number}

As (txt) [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138), the [chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number) of the Kneser graph $K(n,k)$ for $n\geq 2k$ is exactly $n - 2k + 2$; for instance, the Petersen graph requires three colors in any [proper coloring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_coloring). This conjecture was [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) in several ways.

- [László Lovász](wikipedia://en/1623938) proved this in 1978 using [topological](wikipedia://en/29954) methods,[^n2] giving rise to the field of [topological combinatorics](wikipedia://en/17633579).
- Soon thereafter [Imre Bárány](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imre_Bárány) gave a simple proof, using the [Borsuk–Ulam theorem](wikipedia://en/4064) and a lemma of [David Gale](wikipedia://en/1227675).[^n3]
- Joshua E. Greene won the 2002 [Morgan Prize](wikipedia://en/4180157) for outstanding undergraduate research for his further-simplified but still topological proof.[^n4]
- In 2004, [Jiří Matoušek](wikipedia://en/20938051) found a purely [combinatorial proof](wikipedia://en/741875).[^n5]

In contrast, the [fractional chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_chromatic_number) of these graphs is $n/k$.[^n6] When $n< 2k$, $K(n,k)$ has no edges and its chromatic number is 1. When $n=2k$, the graph is a perfect matching and its chromatic number is 2.

### Hamiltonian cycles {#Hamiltonian_cycles}

It is well-known that the [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926) is not [Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_graph), but it was long conjectured that this was the sole exception and that every other connected Kneser graph $K(n, k)$ is Hamiltonian.

In 2003, Ya-Chen Chen showed that the Kneser graph $K(n, k)$ contains a [Hamiltonian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle) if[^n7]

$$n \geq \frac{1}{2} \left(3k+1+\sqrt{5k^2-2k+1} \right).$$

Since

$$\frac{1}{2} \left (3k+1+\sqrt{5k^2-2k+1} \right )< \left (\frac{3 + \sqrt{5}}{2} \right) k+1$$

holds for all $k$, this condition is satisfied if

$$n \geq \left(\frac{3 + \sqrt{5}}{2} \right) k+1 \approx 2.62k+1.$$

Around the same time, Shields showed (computationally) that, except the Petersen graph, all connected Kneser graphs $K(n, k)$ with $n \le 27$ are Hamiltonian.[^n8]

In 2021, Mütze, Nummenpalo, and Walczak proved that the Kneser graph $K(n, k)$ contains a Hamiltonian cycle if there exists a non-negative [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $a$ such that $n=2k+2^a$.[^n9] In particular, the [odd graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_graph) $O_{n}$ has a Hamiltonian cycle if $n \ge 4$. Finally, in 2023, Merino, Mütze and Namrata completed the proof of the conjecture.[^n10]

### Cliques {#Cliques}

When $n < 3k$, the Kneser graph $K(n, k)$ contains no triangles. More generally, when $n < ck$ it does not contain [cliques](wikipedia://en/524466) of size $c$, whereas it does contain such cliques when $n \ge ck$. Moreover, although the Kneser graph always contains [cycles](wikipedia://en/168609) of length four whenever $n \ge 2k + 2$, for values of $n$ close to $2k$ the shortest [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) cycle may have variable length.[^n11]

### Diameter {#Diameter}

The [diameter](wikipedia://en/2979341) of a connected Kneser graph $K(n, k)$ is[^n12]

$$\left\lceil \frac{k-1}{n-2k} \right\rceil + 1.$$

### Spectrum {#Spectrum}

The [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_spectrum) of the Kneser graph $K(n, k)$ consists of *k* + 1 distinct [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue):

$$\lambda_j=(-1)^j\binom{n-k-j}{k-j}, \qquad j=0, \ldots,k.$$

Moreover $\lambda_j$ occurs with [multiplicity](wikipedia://en/19211790) $\tbinom{n}{j}-\tbinom{n}{j-1}$ for $j >0$ and $\lambda_0$ has multiplicity 1.[^o27][^o28]

### Independence number {#Independence_number}

The [Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem](wikipedia://en/296188) states that the [independence number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_number) of the Kneser graph $K(n, k)$ for $n\geq 2k$ is

$$\alpha(K(n,k))=\binom{n-1}{k-1},$$

but if $n > 10000k$, then every vertex subset $S$ of size beyond the independence number will contain a vertex adjacent to at least

$$\left(\frac{1}{2}-O\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\right)\right) \binom{n-k-1}{k-1}$$

other vertices in $S$.[^n13]

## Related graphs {#Related_graphs}

The **[Johnson graph](wikipedia://en/2948666)** $J(n, k)$ is the graph whose vertices are the $k$-element subsets of an $n$-element set, two vertices being adjacent when they meet in a $(k - 1)$-element set. The Johnson graph $J(n, 2)$ is the complement of the Kneser graph $K(n, 2)$. Johnson graphs are closely related to the [Johnson scheme](wikipedia://en/22649423), both of which are named after [Selmer M. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selmer_M._Johnson).

The **generalized Kneser graph** $K(n, k, s)$ has the same vertex set as the Kneser graph $K(n, k)$, but connects two vertices whenever they correspond to sets that intersect in $s$ or fewer items.[^n11] Thus $K(n, k, 0) = K(n, k)$.

The **bipartite Kneser graph** $H(n, k)$ has as vertices the sets of $k$ and $n - k$ items drawn from a collection of $n$ elements. Two vertices are connected by an edge whenever one set is a subset of the other. Like the Kneser graph it is vertex transitive with degree $\tbinom{n-k}{k}.$ The bipartite Kneser graph can be formed as a [bipartite double cover](wikipedia://en/21241712) of $K(n, k)$ in which one makes two copies of each vertex and replaces each edge by a pair of edges connecting corresponding pairs of vertices.[^n14] The bipartite Kneser graph $H(5, 2)$ is the [Desargues graph](wikipedia://en/1578059) and the bipartite Kneser graph $H(n, 1)$ is a [crown graph](wikipedia://en/21062705).

:::
