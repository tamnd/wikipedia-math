# Inverse recovery in EEG

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Inverse recovery in EEG"}

The **inverse recovery in EEG** is a [Calderón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberto_Calderón)-type [inverse problem](wikipedia://en/203956)[^o2] with the goal of recovering source terms and/or conductivity in layers of the human head from [electroencephalographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroencephalography) measurements. Fundamentally, this inverse recovery seeks to solve the [elliptic partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/662787) given by

:::

$\nabla \cdot (\sigma \nabla u) = \mathcal{S}$ or $\hbox{div }(\sigma \hbox{ grad } u) = \mathcal{S}$

where $u$ is the electric potential, $\sigma$ is the (possibly [anisotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anisotropy)) conductivity, and $\mathcal{S}$ represents primary current sources in the brain. Depending on the application, the inverse problem consists of either recovering $\mathcal{S}$ from $u$ (the inverse source problem) or recovering $\sigma$ from $u$ (the inverse conductivity problem).

Because the human head is highly inhomogeneous and composed of multiple layers with different conductivities, the inverse EEG problem is severely [ill-posed](wikipedia://en/176673) and requires either analytical techniques or numerical approximations to obtain stable solutions (such as the [finite element method](wikipedia://en/18233581)).[^o3]

Due to the form of the governing equation being a sort of "generalization" of the Laplace-Beltrami operator, this problem has deep connections to [generalized analytic function](wikipedia://en/30411190) theory, [heat conduction](wikipedia://en/72536), and broader [electromagnetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism). In fact, the problem can be seen as solving the [Poisson equation](wikipedia://en/193735) for an [inhomogeneous](wikipedia://en/4603613) media, which is indeed how it is derived in the EEG problem.

## Problem derivation {#Problem_derivation}

An overview on the physical foundations of the problem is given by Darbas and Lohrengel.[^o5]

Consider the current density $\mathbf{J}(r)$ produced by neural activity,

$$\mathbf{J}(r) = \mathbf{J}^p(r)+\sigma(r) \mathbf{E}(r)$$

where $\mathbf{J}^p(r)$ denotes primary current and $\sigma(r)\mathbf{E}(r)$ the return current (composed of macroscopic conductivity and the brain's electric field). Using the quasi-static approximation of [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737),[^o6]

$$\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = 0 \quad \hbox{and} \quad \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J}=0$$

The first of the above equation implies that the electric field is path-independent and thus we may write $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla u$ with $u$ the electric potential function. Then,

$$\nabla \cdot (\mathbf{J}^p + \sigma \mathbf{E}) = -\nabla \cdot (\sigma \mathbf{E}) = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J}^p$$

which gives

$$\nabla \cdot(\sigma \nabla u) = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J}^p$$

The primary current is modeled by $Q$ pointwise sources located at some coordinate $C_q$ with dipolar moments $p_q$ (note that $p_q$ is a vector quantity).[^o5][^o7] Using the [Dirac delta](wikipedia://en/37021) distribution,

$$\mathbf{J}^p = \sum_{q=1}^Q p_q \delta_{C_q}$$

Thus, the source term is

$$\mathcal{S} := \nabla \mathbf{J}^p = \sum_{q=1}^Q p_q \cdot \nabla \delta_{C_q}.$$

## Exact solutions {#Exact_solutions}

### Constant conductivity {#Constant_conductivity}

Many analytical studies assume that conductivity is piecewise constant in layers representing the brain, skull, and scalp.[^o5][^o13][^o10] Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ denote the head domain decomposed into three concentric spherical domains $\Omega_i,\,i=0,1,2$, where $\Omega_0$ is a ball and $\Omega_1,\Omega_2$ are spherical shells. When $\sigma$ is assumed piecewise constant, the governing equation reduces to a set of Laplace and Poisson equations:[^o13]

$$\begin{cases} \Delta u = 0 & \text{on} \quad \Omega_1,\Omega_2\\ \Delta u = \displaystyle \frac{1}{\sigma_0}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}p_q\cdot \nabla \delta_{C_q} & \text{on} \quad \Omega_0\\ u = g & \text{on} \quad \partial\Omega \end{cases}$$

Solutions can be obtained using [Green's functions](wikipedia://en/311001), spherical harmonics, and separation of variables.[^o11]

A benchmark tool in the problem of piecewise constant conductivity is FindSource3D,[^o12] a source localization tool which has shown perfect reconstruction when negligible error is assumed in source location. Such a tool has also been used for conductivity reconstruction, again with perfect accuracy under suitable error assumptions.[^o13]

### Non-constant conductivity {#Non-constant_conductivity}

For non-constant or anisotropic conductivity, the inverse problem becomes substantially more difficult.[^o15] Classical uniqueness and stability results for Calderón-type inverse problems apply under varying regularity assumptions on the boundary of $\Omega$; for example, Alessandrini established uniqueness with [Lipschitz boundaries](wikipedia://en/7820612), while Kohn and Vogelius proved stability under smooth boundary conditions.[^o16][^o17]

Exact solutions for spatially varying $\sigma$ are generally unavailable. Modern work therefore typically focuses on [perturbative](wikipedia://en/102140) methods, [integral equation](wikipedia://en/474234) formulations, or regularization techniques for ensuring stable recovery.[^o7][^o18][^o19]

## Numerical solutions {#Numerical_solutions}

Because analytical solutions are available only in highly idealized geometries, numerical methods play a central role in practical EEG inverse recovery. Common approaches include typical Finite Element or [Boundary Element Methods](wikipedia://en/1678795).[^o18]

Numerical inverse recovery typically requires regularization methods such as Tikhonov regularization, sparsity constraints, or Bayesian inverse modeling to counteract ill-posedness.[^o21] Practical EEG systems also incorporate noise modeling and electrode placement uncertainty.[^o22]
