# Proof (truth)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Proof"}

A **proof** is [sufficient](wikipedia://en/169319) [evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence) or a sufficient [argument](wikipedia://en/13018310) for the [truth](wikipedia://en/29920) of a [proposition](wikipedia://en/81094).[^o2][^o3][^o4][^o5]

:::

The concept applies in a variety of disciplines,[^o6] with both the nature of the evidence or justification and the criteria for sufficiency being area-dependent. In the area of oral and written [communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication) such as [conversation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversation), [dialog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogue), [rhetoric](wikipedia://en/25447), etc., a proof is a [persuasive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persuasive) [perlocutionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perlocutionary_act) [speech act](wikipedia://en/44836), which demonstrates the truth of a proposition.[^o7] In any area of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) defined by its assumptions or [axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axioms), a proof is an argument establishing a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) of that area via accepted [rules of inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rules_of_inference) starting from those axioms and from other previously established theorems.[^o8] The subject of [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), in particular [proof theory](wikipedia://en/183478), formalizes and studies the notion of [formal proof](wikipedia://en/1197184).[^o9] In some areas of [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_justification) and [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existence_of_god), the notion of [justification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_justification) plays approximately the role of proof,[^o10] while in [jurisprudence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisprudence) the corresponding term is [evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence_%28law%29),[^o11] with "burden of proof" as a concept common to both [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophic_burden_of_proof) and [law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_burden_of_proof).

In most disciplines, evidence is required to prove something. Evidence is drawn from the experience of the world around us, with [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science) obtaining its evidence from [nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_%28science%29),[^o12] law obtaining its evidence from [witnesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witness) and [forensic investigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forensics),[^o13] and so on. A notable exception is mathematics, whose proofs are drawn from a mathematical world begun with axioms and further developed and enriched by theorems proved earlier.

Exactly what evidence is sufficient to prove something is also strongly area-dependent, usually with no absolute threshold of sufficiency at which evidence becomes proof.[^o14][^o15] In law, the same evidence that may convince one [jury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jury) may not persuade another. [Formal proof](wikipedia://en/1197184) provides the main exception, where the criteria for proofhood are ironclad and it is impermissible to defend any step in the reasoning as "obvious" (except for the necessary ability of the one proving and the one being proven to, to correctly identify any symbol used in the proof.);[^o16] for a [well-formed formula](wikipedia://en/404582) to qualify as part of a formal proof, it must be the result of applying a rule of the deductive apparatus of some [formal system](wikipedia://en/396102) to the previous well-formed formulae in the proof sequence.

Proofs have been presented since antiquity. [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) used the observation that patterns of nature never display the machine-like uniformity of [determinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinism) as proof that [chance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chance_%28Ancient_Greek_concept%29) is an inherent part of nature.[^o18] On the other hand, [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas) used the observation of the existence of [rich patterns in nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_nature_%28science%29) as proof that nature is *not* ruled by chance.[^o19]

Proofs need not be verbal. Before [Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicus), people took the apparent motion of the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) across the sky as proof that [the Sun went round the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentric_model).[^o20] Suitably incriminating evidence left at the scene of a [crime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime) may serve as proof of the identity of the perpetrator. Conversely, a verbal entity need not assert a proposition to constitute a proof of that proposition. For example, a [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature) constitutes direct proof of [authorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authorship); less directly, [handwriting analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handwriting_analysis) may be submitted as proof of authorship of a document.[^o21] [Privileged information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret) in a document can serve as proof that the document's author had access to that information; such access might in turn establish the location of the author at certain time, which might then provide the author with an [alibi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alibi).

## Proof vs evidence {#Proof_vs_evidence}

Eighteenth-century Scottish philosopher [David Hume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hume) built on [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308)'s separation of [belief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief) from [knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge),[^o24] recognizing that one can be said to "know" something only if one has firsthand experience with it, in a strict sense proof, while one can infer that something is true and therefore "believe" it without knowing, via evidence or supposition. This speaks to one way of separating proof from evidence:

If one cannot find their chocolate bar, and sees chocolate on their napping roommate's face, this evidence can cause one to believe their roommate ate the chocolate bar. But they do not *know* their roommate ate it. It may turn out that the roommate put the candy away when straightening up, but was thus inspired to go eat their own chocolate. Only if one directly experiences *proof* of the roommate eating it, perhaps by walking in on them doing so, would one have certain *knowledge*, in Hume's sense, that the roommate did it.

In a more strict sense of sure knowledge, one may be unable to prove anything to a rational certainty beyond that of the existence of one's immediate sensory awareness. [Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes) famously raised a similarly strict standard with his first principle *[Cogito, ergo sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cogito,_ergo_sum)* (I think, therefore I am). While Descartes' larger project in *[Meditations on First Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meditations_on_First_Philosophy)* has knowledge of God and the external world—founded on the certainty of the cogito—as its aim, his legacy in doing so is to have shown that one cannot have such proof, because all perceptions could be false (such as under the [evil demon](wikipedia://en/773618) or [simulated reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulated_reality) hypotheses). One nevertheless can still have clear proof of the existence of one's thought, even if belief in the external world lacks the certainty of demonstration beyond that of one's own firsthand experience.
