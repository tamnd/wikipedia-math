# Tautological one-form

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Tautological one-form"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **tautological one-form** is a special [1-form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1-form) defined on the [cotangent bundle](wikipedia://en/221600) $T^{*}Q$ of a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) $Q.$ In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), it is used to create a correspondence between the velocity of a point in a mechanical system and its momentum, thus providing a bridge between [Lagrangian mechanics](wikipedia://en/23371726) and [Hamiltonian mechanics](wikipedia://en/198319) (on the manifold $Q$).

:::

The [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) of this form defines a [symplectic form](wikipedia://en/28356), giving $T^{*}Q$ the structure of a [symplectic manifold](wikipedia://en/28356). The tautological one-form plays an important role in relating the formalism of [Hamiltonian mechanics](wikipedia://en/198319) and [Lagrangian mechanics](wikipedia://en/23371726). The tautological one-form is sometimes also called the **Liouville one-form**, the **Poincaré one-form**, the **[canonical](wikipedia://en/515096) one-form**, or the **symplectic potential**. A similar object is the [canonical vector field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_vector_field) on the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794).

## Definition in coordinates {#Definition_in_coordinates}

To define the tautological one-form, select a coordinate chart $U$ on $T^*Q$ and a [canonical coordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_coordinate) system on $U.$ Pick an arbitrary point $m \in T^*Q.$ By definition of cotangent bundle, $m = (q,p),$ where $q \in Q$ and $p \in T_q^*Q.$ The tautological one-form $\theta_m : T_mT^*Q \to \mathbb{R}$ is given by

$$\theta_m = \sum^n_{i=1} p_i \, dq^i,$$

with $n = \mathop{\text{dim}}Q$ and $(p_1, \ldots, p_n) \in U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ being the coordinate representation of $p.$

Any coordinates on $T^*Q$ that preserve this definition, up to a total differential ([exact form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exact_form)), may be called canonical coordinates; transformations between different canonical coordinate systems are known as [canonical transformations](wikipedia://en/514534).

The **canonical symplectic form**, also known as the **Poincaré two-form**, is given by

$$\omega = -d\theta = \sum_i dq^i \wedge dp_i$$

The extension of this concept to general [fibre bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibre_bundle) is known as the [solder form](wikipedia://en/9792297). By convention, one uses the phrase "canonical form" whenever the form has a unique, canonical definition, and one uses the term "solder form", whenever an arbitrary choice has to be made. In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) and [complex geometry](wikipedia://en/186101) the term "canonical" is discouraged, due to confusion with the [canonical class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_class), and the term "tautological" is preferred, as in [tautological bundle](wikipedia://en/2913502).

## Coordinate-free definition {#Coordinate-free_definition}

The tautological 1-form can also be defined rather abstractly as a form on [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101). Let $Q$ be a manifold and $M=T^*Q$ be the [cotangent bundle](wikipedia://en/221600) or [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101). Let

$$\pi : M \to Q$$

be the canonical fiber bundle projection, and let

$$\mathrm{d} \pi : TM \to TQ$$

be the [induced](wikipedia://en/17018463) [tangent map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_map). Let $m$ be a point on $M.$ Since $M$ is the cotangent bundle, we can understand $m$ to be a map of the tangent space at $q=\pi(m)$:

$$m : T_qQ \to \mathbb{R}.$$

That is, we have that $m$ is in the fiber of $q.$ The tautological one-form $\theta_m$ at point $m$ is then defined to be

$$\theta_m = m \circ \mathrm{d}_m \pi.$$

It is a linear map

$$\theta_m : T_mM \to \mathbb{R}$$

and so

$$\theta : M \to T^*M.$$

### Intuition {#Intuition}

Visually, the tautological 1-form can be described as follows. Like how a vector can be pictured as an ordered pair of points, a 1-form can be pictured as an ordered pair of hyperplanes.

Consider any vector in the cotangent bundle $V \in T_\omega(T^* Q)$, where $\omega \in T^*_q Q$ is its base point (a covector), and $q \in Q$ is *its* base point. Then, there are 3 effects of moving infinitesimally from $\omega$ to $\omega + V \delta t$: shifting the base point $q$, rotating the hyperplane of the covector $\ker\omega$, and changing the distance separating between the hyperplane pairs. In particular, the shifting of the base point creates a vector $d\pi(V) \in T_q Q$, which can be fed into the covector.

The tautological 1-form computes $\theta(V)$ by feeding to $\omega$ the vector created by shifting the base point, and ignoring the other two effects, which cannot be fed into the covector, giving $\theta(V) = \omega(d\pi(V))$.

## Symplectic potential {#Symplectic_potential}

The symplectic potential is generally defined a bit more freely, and also only defined locally: it is any one-form $\phi$ such that $\omega=-d\phi$; in effect, symplectic potentials differ from the canonical 1-form by a [closed form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_differential_form).

## Properties {#Properties}

The tautological one-form is the unique one-form that "cancels" [pullback](wikipedia://en/494995). That is, let $\beta$ be a 1-form on $Q.$ $\beta$ is a [section](wikipedia://en/367621) $\beta: Q \to T^*Q.$ For an arbitrary 1-form $\sigma$ on $T^*Q,$ the pullback of $\sigma$ by $\beta$ is, by definition, $\beta^*\sigma := \sigma \circ \beta_*.$ Here, $\beta_* : TQ\to TT^*Q$ is the [pushforward](wikipedia://en/1558510) of $\beta.$ Like $\beta,$ $\beta^*\sigma$ is a 1-form on $Q.$ The tautological one-form $\theta$ is the only form with the property that $\beta^*\theta = \beta,$ for every 1-form $\beta$ on $Q.$

## Action functional {#Action_functional}

If $H$ is a [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319) on the [cotangent bundle](wikipedia://en/221600) and $X_H$ is its [Hamiltonian vector field](wikipedia://en/1818270), then the corresponding [action](wikipedia://en/312881) $S$ is given by

$$S = \theta(X_H).$$

In more prosaic terms, the Hamiltonian flow represents the classical trajectory of a mechanical system obeying the [Hamilton-Jacobi equations of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton-Jacobi_equations_of_motion). The Hamiltonian flow is the integral of the Hamiltonian vector field, and so one writes, using traditional notation for [action-angle variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action-angle_variables):

$$S(E) = \sum_i \oint p_i\,dq^i$$

with the integral understood to be taken over the manifold defined by holding the energy $E$ constant: $H=E=\text{const}.$

## On Riemannian and Pseudo-Riemannian Manifolds {#On_Riemannian_and_Pseudo-Riemannian_Manifolds}

If the manifold $Q$ has a Riemannian or pseudo-Riemannian [metric](wikipedia://en/195795) $g,$ then corresponding definitions can be made in terms of [generalized coordinates](wikipedia://en/473514). Specifically, if we take the metric to be a map

$$g : TQ \to T^*Q,$$

then define

$$\Theta = g^*\theta$$

and

$$\Omega = -d\Theta = g^*\omega$$

In generalized coordinates $(q^1,\ldots,q^n,\dot q^1,\ldots,\dot q^n)$ on $TQ,$ one has

$$\Theta = \sum_{ij} g_{ij} \dot q^i dq^j$$

and

$$\Omega = \sum_{ij} g_{ij} \; dq^i \wedge d\dot q^j + \sum_{ijk} \frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial q^k} \; \dot q^i\, dq^j \wedge dq^k$$

The metric allows one to define a unit-radius sphere in $T^*Q.$ The canonical one-form restricted to this sphere forms a [contact structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_structure); the contact structure may be used to generate the [geodesic flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_flow) for this metric.
