# How Round Is Your Circle?

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="How Round Is Your Circle?"}

***How Round Is Your Circle? Where Engineering and Mathematics Meet*** is a book on the mathematics of physical objects, for a popular audience. It was written by chemical engineer John Bryant and mathematics educator Chris Sangwin, and published by the [Princeton University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University_Press) in 2008.

:::

## Topics {#Topics}

The book has 13 chapters, whose topics include:

- [Lines](wikipedia://en/946975), the thickness of physically drawn or cut lines, and the problem of testing straightness of physical objects
- The construction of physical measuring and calculating devices including [rulers](wikipedia://en/65593), [protractors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protractor), [pantographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantograph), [planimeters](wikipedia://en/1103376), [integrators](wikipedia://en/1047173), and [slide rules](wikipedia://en/28743)
- [Mechanical linkages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linkage_%28mechanical%29), [pantographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantograph), [four-bar linkages](wikipedia://en/1442361), and the problem of converting rotary to linear motion, solved by the [Peaucellier–Lipkin linkage](wikipedia://en/2163562) and by [Hart's inversor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hart's_inversor)
- Geometric dissections, [straightedge and compass constructions](wikipedia://en/61229), [angle trisection](wikipedia://en/91111), and [mathematical origami](wikipedia://en/232844)
- The [catenary](wikipedia://en/7163) and the [tractrix](wikipedia://en/841685), curves formed from physical forces, and their use in bridges and bearings
- [Approximation by rational numbers](wikipedia://en/251558), [discretization](wikipedia://en/330017) and [pixelization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixelization), gear ratios, and the approximations involved in [calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendar) systems
- The [roundness of objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundness_%28object%29), non-circular [objects of constant width](wikipedia://en/580238), including the [Reuleaux triangle](wikipedia://en/580252) and certain coins, and their use in drilling square holes
- Stability and [mechanical equilibrium](wikipedia://en/92290) of objects, overhanging objects and the [block-stacking problem](wikipedia://en/3338254), [supereggs](wikipedia://en/10405346), and objects with only one stable resting position (unfortunately not including the [Gömböc](wikipedia://en/9493560), which was discovered too recently to be included)

The book emphasizes the construction of physical models, and includes many plates of the authors' own models, detailed construction plans, and illustrations.

## Audience and reception {#Audience_and_reception}

Doug Manchester characterizes the topic of the book as "recreational engineering". It only requires a standard background in mathematics including basic geometry, trigonometry, and a small amount of calculus. Owen Smith calls it "a great book for engineers and mathematicians, as well as the interested lay person", writing that it is particularly good at laying bare the mathematical foundations of seemingly-simple problems. Similarly, Ronald Huston recommends it to "mathematicians, engineers, and physicists", as well as interested members of the general public.

Matthew Killeya writes approvingly of the book's intuitive explanations for its calculations and the motivation it adds to the mathematics it applies. However, although reviewer Tim Erickson calls the book "exuberant and eclectic", reviewers Andrew Whelan and William Satzer disagree, both finding fault with the book's lack of focus.
