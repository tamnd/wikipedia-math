# Coppersmith's attack

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Coppersmith's attack"}

**Coppersmith's attack** describes a class of [cryptographic attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptographic_attack) on the [public-key cryptosystem](wikipedia://en/24222) [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29) based on the [Coppersmith method](wikipedia://en/25974284). Particular applications of the Coppersmith method for attacking RSA include cases when the public exponent *e* is small or when partial knowledge of a [prime factor](wikipedia://en/23666) of the secret key is available.

:::

## RSA basics {#RSA_basics}

The public key in the RSA system is a [tuple](wikipedia://en/132729) of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $(N, e)$, where *N* is the product of two primes *p* and *q*. The secret key is given by an integer *d* satisfying $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{(p - 1)(q - 1)}$; equivalently, the secret key may be given by $d_p \equiv d \pmod{p - 1}$ and $d_q \equiv d \pmod{q - 1}$ if the [Chinese remainder theorem](wikipedia://en/7713) is used to improve the speed of decryption, see [CRT-RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29#Using_the_Chinese_remainder_algorithm). Encryption of a [message](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Message) *M* produces the [ciphertext](wikipedia://en/175560) $C \equiv M^e \pmod{N}$, which can be decrypted using $d$ by computing $C^d \equiv M \pmod{N}$.

## Low public exponent attack {#Low_public_exponent_attack}

In order to reduce [encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29#Encryption) or signature verification time, it is useful to use a small public [exponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponent) ($e$).[^o4] In practice, common choices for $e$ are 3, 17 and [65537](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/65537) $(2^{16} + 1)$. These values for *e* are [Fermat primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_primes), sometimes referred to as $F_0, F_2$ and $F_4$ respectively $(F_x = 2^{2^x} + 1)$. They are chosen because they make the [modular exponentiation](wikipedia://en/903032) operation faster. Also, having chosen such $e$, it is simpler to test whether $\gcd(e, p - 1) = 1$ and $\gcd(e, q - 1) = 1$ while generating and testing the primes in step 1 of the [key generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29#Key_generation). Values of $p$ or $q$ that fail this test can be rejected there and then. (Even better: if *e* is prime and greater than 2, then the test $p \bmod e \ne 1$ can replace the more expensive test $\gcd(p - 1, e) = 1$.)

If the public exponent is small and the [plaintext](wikipedia://en/157935) $m$ is very short, then the RSA function may be easy to invert, which makes certain attacks possible. [Padding schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29#Padding_schemes) ensure that messages have full lengths, but additionally choosing the public exponent $e = 2^{16} + 1$ is recommended. When this value is used, signature verification requires 17 multiplications, as opposed to about 25 when a random $e$ of similar size is used. Unlike low private exponent (see [Wiener's attack](wikipedia://en/30639146)), attacks that apply when a small $e$ is used are far from a total [break](wikipedia://en/5715), which would recover the secret key *d*. The most powerful attacks on low public exponent [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29) are based on the following theorem, which is due to [Don Coppersmith](wikipedia://en/540090).

## Håstad's broadcast attack {#Håstad's_broadcast_attack}

The simplest form of Håstad's attack is presented to ease understanding.[^o6] The general case uses the Coppersmith method.

Suppose one sender sends the same message $M$ in encrypted form to a number of people $P_1;P_2;\dots ;P_k$, each using the same small public exponent $e$, say $e=3$, and different moduli $\left\langle N_i, e \right\rangle$. A simple argument shows that as soon as $k \ge 3$ ciphertexts are known, the message $M$ is no longer secure: Suppose Eve intercepts $C_1, C_2$, and $C_3$, where $C_i \equiv M^3 \pmod{N_i}$. We may assume $\gcd(N_i, N_j) = 1$ for all $i, j$ (otherwise, it is possible to compute a [factor](wikipedia://en/82341) of one of the numbers $N_i$ by computing $\gcd(N_i, N_j)$.) By the [Chinese remainder theorem](wikipedia://en/7713), she may compute $C \in \mathbb{Z}^*_{N_1N_2N_3}$ such that $C \equiv C_i \pmod{N_i}$. Then $C \equiv M^3\pmod{N_1 N_2 N_3}$; however, since $M < N_i$ for all $i$, we have $M^3 < N_1N_2N_3$. Thus $C = M^3$ holds over the integers, and Eve can compute the [cube root](wikipedia://en/292506) of $C$ to obtain $M$.

For larger values of $e$, more ciphertexts are needed, particularly, $e$ ciphertexts are sufficient.

### Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Håstad also showed that applying a [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear) [padding](wikipedia://en/592505) to $M$ prior to encryption does not protect against this attack. Assume the attacker learns that $C_i = f_i(M)^e$ for $1 \leq i \leq k$ and some linear function $f_i$, i.e., Bob applies a [pad](wikipedia://en/592505) to the [message](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Message) $M$ prior to [encrypting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encrypt) it so that the recipients receive slightly different messages. For instance, if $M$ is $m$ bits long, Bob might [encrypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encrypt) $M_i = i2^m + M$ and send this to the $i$-th recipient.

If a large enough group of people is involved, the attacker can recover the [plaintext](wikipedia://en/157935) $M_i$ from all the [ciphertext](wikipedia://en/175560) with similar methods. In more generality, Håstad proved that a system of [univariate](wikipedia://en/2398982) [equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations) [modulo](wikipedia://en/9259) [relatively prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_prime) composites, such as applying any fixed [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) $g_i(M) \equiv 0 \pmod{N_i}$, could be solved if sufficiently many [equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations) are provided. This [attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyber_attack) suggests that randomized [padding](wikipedia://en/592505) should be used in [RSA encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_encryption).

:::

## Franklin–Reiter related-message attack {#Franklin–Reiter_related-message_attack}

Franklin and Reiter identified an attack against [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29) when multiple related messages are encrypted: If two [messages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messages) differ only by a known fixed difference between the two messages and are [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29)-[encrypted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encrypted) under the same [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29) [modulus](wikipedia://en/20087) $N$, then it is possible to recover both of them. The attack was originally described with public [exponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponent) $e = 3$, but it works more generally (with increasing cost as $e$ grows).

Let $\left\langle N; e_i \right\rangle$ be Alice's public key. Suppose $M_1; M_2 \in \mathbb{Z}_N$ are two distinct [messages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messages) satisfying $M_1 \equiv f(M_2) \pmod{N}$ for some publicly known [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) $f \in \mathbb{Z}_N[x]$. To send $M_1$ and $M_2$ to Alice, Bob may naively [encrypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encrypt) the [messages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messages) and transmit the resulting [ciphertexts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciphertexts) $C_1; C_2$. Eve can easily recover $M_1; M_2$, given $C_1; C_2$, by using the following theorem: Let $g_1(x) = f(x)^e - C_1 \in \mathbb{Z}_N[x]$ and $g_2(x) = x^e - C_2 \in \mathbb{Z}_N[x]$. Then $(x - M_2)$ is a factor of both $g_1(x)$ and $g_2(x)$. In most cases, computing the [polynomial GCD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_common_divisor_of_two_polynomials) reveals the message directly:

$$\gcd(g_1(x), g_2(x)) = x - M_2 \pmod{N}$$

Once $M_2$ is found, $M_1$ is recovered by computing $f(M_2) \pmod{N}$.

## Coppersmith’s short-pad attack {#Coppersmith’s_short-pad_attack}

Like Håstad’s and Franklin–Reiter’s attacks, this attack exploits a weakness of [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29) with public exponent $e = 3$. Coppersmith showed that if randomized padding suggested by Håstad is used improperly, then [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29) [encryption](wikipedia://en/10294) is not secure.

Suppose Bob sends a message $M$ to Alice using a small random padding before [encrypting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encrypting) it. An attacker, Eve, intercepts the [ciphertext](wikipedia://en/175560) and prevents it from reaching its destination. Bob decides to resend $M$ to Alice because Alice did not respond to his message. He randomly pads $M$ again and transmits the resulting ciphertext. Eve now has two ciphertexts corresponding to two encryptions of the same message using two different random pads.
