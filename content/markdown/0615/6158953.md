# Modal μ-calculus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Modal μ-calculus"}

In [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392), the **modal μ-calculus** (**Lμ**, **L<sub>μ</sub>**, or **propositional mu-calculus**[^o2], sometimes just **μ-calculus**, although this can have a more general meaning) is an extension of [propositional](wikipedia://en/18154) [modal logic](wikipedia://en/333365) (with [many modalities](wikipedia://en/11233252)) by adding the [least fixed point](wikipedia://en/1211913) operator μ and the [greatest fixed point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_fixed_point) operator ν, thus a [fixed-point logic](wikipedia://en/50026488).

:::

The (propositional, modal) μ-calculus originates with [Dana Scott](wikipedia://en/161875) and [Jaco de Bakker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaco_de_Bakker),[^o3] and was further developed by [Dexter Kozen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dexter_Kozen)[^o4] into the version most used nowadays. It is used to describe properties of [labelled transition systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labelled_transition_system) and for [verifying](wikipedia://en/321157) these properties. Many [temporal logics](wikipedia://en/321481) can be encoded in the μ-calculus, including [CTL\*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CTL*) and its widely used fragments—[linear temporal logic](wikipedia://en/710307) and [computational tree logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_tree_logic).[^o5]

An algebraic view is to see it as an [algebra](wikipedia://en/45200) of [monotonic functions](wikipedia://en/48260) over a [complete lattice](wikipedia://en/44777), with operators consisting of [functional composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_composition) plus the least and greatest fixed point operators; from this viewpoint, the modal μ-calculus is over the lattice of a [power set algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_set_algebra).[^o6] The [game semantics](wikipedia://en/617121) of μ-calculus is related to [two-player games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-player_game) with [perfect information](wikipedia://en/321438), particularly infinite [parity games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_game).[^o7]

## Syntax {#Syntax}

Let *P* (propositions) and *A* (actions) be two finite sets of symbols, and let Var be a countably infinite set of variables. The set of formulas of (propositional, modal) μ-calculus is defined as follows:

- each proposition and each variable is a formula;
- if $\phi$ and $\psi$ are formulas, then $\phi \wedge \psi$ is a formula;
- if $\phi$ is a formula, then $\neg \phi$ is a formula;
- if $\phi$ is a formula and $a$ is an action, then $[a] \phi$ is a formula; (pronounced either: $a$ box $\phi$ or after $a$ necessarily $\phi$)
- if $\phi$ is a formula and $Z$ a variable, then $\nu Z. \phi$ is a formula, provided that every free occurrence of $Z$ in $\phi$ occurs positively, i.e. within the scope of an even number of negations.

(The notions of free and bound variables are as usual, where $\nu$ is the only binding operator.)

Given the above definitions, we can enrich the syntax with:

- $\phi \lor \psi$ meaning $\neg (\neg \phi \land \neg \psi)$
- $\langle a \rangle \phi$ (pronounced either: $a$ diamond $\phi$ or after $a$ possibly $\phi$) meaning $\neg [a] \neg \phi$
- $\mu Z. \phi$ means $\neg \nu Z. \neg \phi [Z:=\neg Z]$, where $\phi [Z:=\neg Z]$ means substituting $\neg Z$ for $Z$ in all [free occurrences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_occurrence) of $Z$ in $\phi$.

The first two formulas are the familiar ones from the classical [propositional calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus) and respectively the minimal [multimodal logic](wikipedia://en/11233252) **K**.

The notation $\mu Z. \phi$ (and its dual) are inspired from the [lambda calculus](wikipedia://en/18203); the intent is to denote the least (and respectively greatest) fixed point of the expression $\phi$ where the "minimization" (and respectively "maximization") are in the variable $Z$, much like in lambda calculus $\lambda Z. \phi$ is a function with formula $\phi$ in [bound variable](wikipedia://en/18203#Free_and_bound_variables) $Z$;[^o9] see the denotational semantics below for details.

## Denotational semantics {#Denotational_semantics}

Models of (propositional) μ-calculus are given as [labelled transition systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labelled_transition_system) $(S, R, V)$ where:

- $S$ is a set of states;
- $R$ maps to each label $a$ a binary relation on $S$;
- $V : P \to 2^S$, maps each proposition $p \in P$ to the set of states where the proposition is true.

Given a labelled transition system $(S, R, V)$ and an interpretation $i$ of the variables $Z$ of the $\mu$-calculus, $[\![\cdot]\!]_i:\phi \to 2^S$, is the function defined by the following rules:

- $$[\![p]\!]_i = V(p);$$
- $$[\![Z]\!]_i = i(Z);$$
- $$[\![\phi \wedge \psi]\!]_i = [\![\phi]\!]_i \cap [\![\psi]\!]_i;$$
- $$[\![\neg \phi]\!]_i = S \smallsetminus [\![\phi]\!]_i;$$
- $$[\![[a] \phi]\!]_i = \{s \in S \mid \forall t \in S, (s, t) \in R_a \rightarrow t \in [\![\phi]\!]_i\};$$
- $[\![\nu Z. \phi]\!]_i = \bigcup \{T \subseteq S \mid T \subseteq [\![\phi]\!]_{i[Z := T]}\}$, where $i[Z := T]$ maps $Z$ to $T$ while preserving the mappings of $i$ everywhere else.

By duality, the interpretation of the other basic formulas is:

- $$[\![\phi \vee \psi]\!]_i = [\![\phi]\!]_i \cup [\![\psi]\!]_i;$$
- $$[\![\langle a \rangle \phi]\!]_i = \{s \in S \mid \exists t \in S, (s, t) \in R_a \wedge t \in [\![\phi]\!]_i\};$$
- $$[\![\mu Z. \phi]\!]_i = \bigcap \{T \subseteq S \mid [\![\phi]\!]_{i[Z := T]} \subseteq T \}$$

Less formally, this means that, for a given transition system $(S, R, V)$:

- $p$ holds in the set of states $V(p)$;
- $\phi \wedge \psi$ holds in every state where $\phi$ and $\psi$ both hold;
- $\neg \phi$ holds in every state where $\phi$ does not hold.
- $[a] \phi$ holds in a state $s$ if every $a$-transition leading out of $s$ leads to a state where $\phi$ holds.
- $\langle a\rangle \phi$ holds in a state $s$ if there exists $a$-transition leading out of $s$ that leads to a state where $\phi$ holds.
- $\nu Z.\phi$ holds in any state in any set $T$ such that, when the variable $Z$ is set to $T$, then $\phi$ holds for all of $T$. (From the [Knaster–Tarski theorem](wikipedia://en/181417) it follows that $[\![\nu Z.\phi]\!]_i$ is the [greatest fixed point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_fixed_point) of $T\mapsto[\![\phi]\!]_{i[Z := T]}$, and $[\![\mu Z. \phi]\!]_i$ its [least fixed point](wikipedia://en/1211913).)

The interpretations of $[a] \phi$ and $\langle a\rangle \phi$ are in fact the "classical" ones from [dynamic logic](wikipedia://en/31631871). Additionally, the operator $\mu$ can be interpreted as [liveness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liveness) ("something good eventually happens") and $\nu$ as [safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safety_%28computer_science%29) ("nothing bad ever happens") in [Leslie Lamport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leslie_Lamport)'s informal classification.[^o11]

::: {#o13 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

- $\nu Z.\phi \wedge [a]Z$ is interpreted as "$\phi$ is true along every *a*-path".[^o11] The idea is that "$\phi$ is true along every *a*-path" can be defined axiomatically as that (weakest) sentence $Z$ which implies $\phi$ and which remains true after processing any *a*-label. [^o14]
- $\mu Z.\phi \vee \langle a \rangle Z$ is interpreted as the existence of a path along *a*-transitions to a state where $\phi$ holds.[^o15]
- The property of a state being [deadlock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deadlock_%28computer_science%29)-free, meaning no path from that state reaches a dead end, is expressed by the formula[^o15] $$\nu Z.\left (\bigvee_{a\in A}\langle a\rangle\top\wedge \bigwedge_{a\in A}[a]Z \right)$$

:::

## Decision problems {#Decision_problems}

[Satisfiability](wikipedia://en/23401166) of a modal μ-calculus formula is [EXPTIME-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EXPTIME-complete).[^o17] Like for linear temporal logic,[^o18] the [model checking](wikipedia://en/321157), satisfiability and validity problems of linear modal μ-calculus are [PSPACE-complete](wikipedia://en/54685).[^o19]

Actually, the complexity of the satisfiability problem of *graded* modal μ-calculus is also EXPTIME-complete, even if the number in the modalities are written in binary (graded modal μ-calculus is an extension of standard modal μ-calculus with modalities "there are at least k successors such that...).[^o20]

## Comparison to other logics {#Comparison_to_other_logics}

Recall that modal logic can be translated in [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) (FO) via the [standard translation](wikipedia://en/25310346) denoted by $ST$ and indexed by a first-order variable $x$ denoting the current state:

$$\begin{aligned} ST_x(p) & := p(x) \\ ST_x(\lnot \phi) & := \lnot ST_x(\phi) \\ ST_x(\phi \land \psi) &:= ST_x(\phi) \land ST_y(\psi) \\ ST_x([a]\phi) & := \forall y, x R_a y \rightarrow ST_y(\phi) \end{aligned}$$

Recall that [monadic second order logic](wikipedia://en/14650395) (MSO) extends [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) (FO) with second order quantifications over subsets. The standard translation extends to mu-calculus that can be translated in monadic second order logic by adding the following translation rules for the fix-point operators[^o22]:

$$\begin{aligned} ST_x(\mu X. \phi) & := \forall X, (\forall y, ST_y(\phi) \rightarrow y \in X) \rightarrow x \in X) \\ ST_x(\nu X. \phi) & := \exists X, (\forall y, y \in X \rightarrow ST_y(\phi)) \land x \in X) \end{aligned}$$

Janin and Walukiewicz proved in 1996 that any formula of [monadic second order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monadic_second_order) that is invariant by [bisimulation](wikipedia://en/397571) is equivalent to some mu-calculus formula[^o2].
