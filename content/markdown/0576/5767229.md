# Zsigmondy's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Zsigmondy's theorem"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **Zsigmondy's theorem**, named after [Karl Zsigmondy](wikipedia://en/12162423), states that if $a>b>0$ are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), then for any integer $n \ge 1$, there is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) *p* (called a *primitive prime divisor*) that divides $a^n-b^n$ and does not divide $a^k-b^k$ for any positive integer $k<n$, with the following exceptions:

:::

- $n=1$, $a-b=1$; then $a^n-b^n=1$ which has no prime divisors
- $n=2$, $a+b$ a [power of two](wikipedia://en/376948); then any [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) prime factors of $a^2-b^2=(a+b)(a^1-b^1)$ must be contained in $a^1-b^1$, which is also [even](wikipedia://en/143135)
- $n=6$, $a=2$, $b=1$; then $a^6-b^6=63=3^2\times 7=(a^2-b^2)^2 (a^3-b^3)$

This generalizes Bang's theorem,[^o2] which states that if $n>1$ and $n$ is not equal to 6, then $2^n-1$ has a prime divisor not dividing any $2^k-1$ with $k<n$.

Similarly, $a^n+b^n$ has at least one primitive prime divisor with the exception $2^3+1^3=9$.

Zsigmondy's theorem is often useful, especially in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), where it is used to [prove](wikipedia://en/82285) that various [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) have distinct [orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) except when they are known to be the same. [^o3][^o4] It is also used in [mathematical olympiads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_olympiad).

## History {#History}

The [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) was discovered by [Karl Zsigmondy](wikipedia://en/12162423) who proved it in 1892.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Let $(a_n)_{n\ge1}$ be a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of nonzero integers. The **Zsigmondy set** associated to the sequence is the [set](wikipedia://en/26691)

$$\mathcal{Z}(a_n) = \{n \ge 1 : a_n \text{ has no primitive prime divisors}\}.$$

i.e., the set of indices $n$ such that every prime dividing $a_n$ also divides some $a_m$ for some $m < n$. Thus Zsigmondy's theorem implies that $\mathcal{Z}(a^n-b^n)\subset\{1,2,6\}$, and [Carmichael's theorem](wikipedia://en/932711) says that the Zsigmondy set of the [Fibonacci sequence](wikipedia://en/10918) is $\{1,2,6,12\}$, and that of the [Pell sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pell_sequence) is $\{1\}$. In 2001 Bilu, Hanrot, and Voutier[^o8] proved that in general, if $(a_n)_{n\ge1}$ is a [Lucas sequence](wikipedia://en/734635) or a [Lehmer sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lehmer_number), then $\mathcal{Z}(a_n) \subseteq \{ 1 \le n \le 30 \}$ (see , there are only 13 such $n$s, namely 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 12, 13, 18, 30). Lucas and Lehmer sequences are examples of [divisibility sequences](wikipedia://en/29875125).

It is also known that if $(W_n)_{n\ge1}$ is an [elliptic divisibility sequence](wikipedia://en/21298611), then its Zsigmondy set $\mathcal{Z}(W_n)$ is [finite](wikipedia://en/11742).[^o9] However, the result is ineffective in the sense that the proof does not give an explicit [upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound) for the largest element in $\mathcal{Z}(W_n)$, although it is possible to give an effective upper bound for the number of elements in $\mathcal{Z}(W_n)$.[^o10]
