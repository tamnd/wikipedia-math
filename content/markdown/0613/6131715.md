# Zhoubi Suanjing

周髀算經

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Zhoubi Suanjing"}

The ***Zhoubi Suanjing***, also known by many other names, is an ancient Chinese astronomical and mathematical work. The *Zhoubi* is most famous for its presentation of [Chinese cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_cosmology) and a form of the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034). It claims to present 246 problems worked out by the [Duke of Zhou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_of_Zhou) as well as members of his court, placing its composition during the 11th century BC. However, the present form of the book does not seem to be earlier than the [Eastern Han](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Han) (25–220 AD), with some additions and commentaries continuing to be added for several more centuries.

:::

The book was included as part of the *[Ten Computational Canons](wikipedia://en/30269292)*.

## Names {#Names}

The work's original title was simply the ***Zhoubi***: the character 髀 is a literary term for the [femur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Femur) or [thighbone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thighbone) but in context only refers to one or more [gnomons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnomon), large sticks whose shadows were used for [Chinese calendrical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_calendar) and [astronomical calculations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_astronomy).[^n1] Because of the ambiguous nature of the character 周, it has been alternately understood and translated as 'On the gnomon and the circular paths of [Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tian)',[^n1] the 'Zhou shadow gauge manual',[^n2] the 'Gnomon of the Zhou sundial',[^n3] and 'Gnomon of the [Zhou dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhou_dynasty)'.[^n4] The honorific ***Suanjing***—'Arithmetical classic',[^n5] 'Sacred book of arithmetic',[^n6] 'Mathematical canon',[^n4] 'Classic of computations',[^n7]—was added later.

## Dating {#Dating}

Examples of the [gnomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnomon) described in the work have been found from as early as 2300 BC and the [Duke of Zhou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_of_Zhou), was an 11th-century BC regent and noble during the first generation of the [Zhou dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhou_dynasty). The *Zhoubi* was traditionally dated to the Duke of Zhou's own life[^n8] and considered to be the oldest Chinese mathematical treatise.[^n1] However, although some passages seem to come from the [Warring States period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warring_States_period) or earlier,[^n8] the current text of the work mentions [Lü Buwei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lü_Buwei) and is believed to have received its current form no earlier than the [Eastern Han](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Han), during the 1st or 2nd century. The earliest known mention of the text is from a memorial dedicated to the astronomer [Cai Yong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cai_Yong) in 178 AD.[^o17] It does not appear at all in the [Book of Han](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Han)'s account of calendrical, astronomical, and mathematical works, although [Joseph Needham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Needham) allows that this may have been from its current contents having previously been provided in several different works listed in the Han history which are otherwise unknown.[^n1]

## Contents {#Contents}

*Image: NAJDA-305-0214 周髀算経 乾.pdf.* Title page from a Ming dynasty printed edition of the *Zhoubi Suanjing*

*Image: 周髀算經.jpg.* Pages of the *Zhoubi Suanjing*

The *Zhoubi* is an anonymous collection of 246 problems encountered by the Duke of Zhou and figures in his court, including the [astrologer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_astrology) Shang Gao. Each problem includes an answer and a corresponding arithmetic [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775).

It is an important source on early [Chinese cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_cosmology), glossing the ancient idea of a round heaven over a square earth (天, *tiānyuán dìfāng*) as similar to the round parasol suspended over some ancient [Chinese chariots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_chariot)[^n9] or a [Chinese chessboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_chess).[^n10] All things measurable were considered variants of the [square](wikipedia://en/659939), while the expansion of a polygon to infinite sides approaches the immeasurable [circle](wikipedia://en/6220).[^n2] This concept of a 'canopy heaven' (蓋, *gàitiān*) had earlier produced the jade [*bi*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi_%28jade%29) (璧) and [*cong*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cong_%28vessel%29) objects and [myths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_mythology) about [Gonggong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonggong), [Mount Buzhou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Buzhou), [Nüwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nüwa), and [repairing the sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nüwa_Mends_the_Heavens). Although this eventually developed into an idea of a 'spherical heaven' (渾, *hùntiān*),[^n11] the *Zhoubi* offers numerous explorations of the geometric relationships of simple circles [circumscribed by squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumscribed_quadrilateral) and squares [circumscribed by circles](wikipedia://en/161262).[^n12] A large part of this involves analysis of [solar declination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_declination) in the Northern Hemisphere at various points throughout the year.[^n1]

At one point during its discussion of the shadows cast by gnomons, the work presents a form of the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) known as the **gougu theorem** (勾股)[^n13] from the Chinese names—lit. 'hook' and 'thigh'—of the two sides of the [carpenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpenter_square) or [try square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Try_square).[^n14] In the 3rd century, Zhao Shuang's commentary on the *Zhoubi* included a diagram effectively proving the theorem[^n15] for the case of a [3-4-5 triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-4-5_triangle),[^n16] whence it can be generalized to all [right triangles](wikipedia://en/39420). The original text being ambiguous on its own, there is disagreement as to whether this proof was established by Zhao or merely represented an illustration of a previously understood concept earlier than [Pythagoras](wikipedia://en/23275).[^n16][^n13] Shang Gao concludes the gougu problem saying "He who understands the earth is a wise man, and he who understands the heavens is a sage. Knowledge is derived from the shadow \[straight line\], and the shadow is derived from the gnomon \[right angle\]. The combination of the gnomon with numbers is what guides and rules the ten thousand things."[^n17]

## Commentaries {#Commentaries}

The *Zhoubi* has had a prominent place in [Chinese mathematics](wikipedia://en/1524543) and was the subject of specific commentaries by Zhao Shuang in the 3rd century, [Liu Hui](wikipedia://en/202719) in 263, by [Zu Gengzhi](wikipedia://en/3359266) in the early 6th century, [Li Chunfeng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_Chunfeng) in the 7th century, and [Yang Hui](wikipedia://en/1716898) in 1270.

## Translation {#Translation}

A translation to English was published in 1996 by Christopher Cullen, through the Cambridge University Press, entitled *Astronomy and mathematics in ancient China: the Zhou bi suan jing*.[^o35] The work includes a preface attributed to Zhao Shuang, as well as his discussions and diagrams for the gougu theorem, the height of the sun, the seven *heng* and his gnomon shadow table, restored.
