# Theodosius' Spherics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Theodosius' Spherics"}

The ***Spherics*** ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek): τὰ σφαιρικά, τὰ σφαιρικά) is a three-volume [treatise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise) on [spherical geometry](wikipedia://en/174026) written by the [Hellenistic mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) [Theodosius of Bithynia](wikipedia://en/2828050) in the 2nd or 1st century BC.

:::

Book I and the first half of Book II establish basic [geometric constructions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_construction) needed for spherical geometry using the tools of [Euclidean](wikipedia://en/9417) [solid geometry](wikipedia://en/507960), while the second half of Book II and Book III contain propositions relevant to [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) as modeled by the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239).

Primarily consisting of theorems which were known at least informally a couple centuries earlier, the *Spherics* was a foundational treatise for geometers and astronomers from its origin until the 19th century. It was continuously studied and copied in Greek manuscript for more than a millennium. It was translated into [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) in the 9th century during the [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age), and thence translated into [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo_Latin) [in 12th century Iberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_translations_of_the_12th_century), though the text and diagrams were somewhat corrupted. In the 16th century printed editions in Greek were published along with better translations into Latin.

## History {#History}

Several of the definitions and theorems in the *Spherics* were used without mention in [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s *Phenomena* and two extant works by [Autolycus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autolycus_of_Pitane) concerning motions of the celestial sphere, all written about two centuries before Theodosius. It has been speculated that this tradition of Greek "spherics" – founded in the axiomatic system and using the methods of proof of solid geometry exemplified by [Euclid's *Elements*](wikipedia://en/244107) but extended with additional definitions relevant to the sphere – may have originated in a now-unknown work by [Eudoxus](wikipedia://en/48388), who probably established a two-sphere model of the cosmos (spherical Earth and celestial sphere) sometime between 370–340 BC.[^o3]

The *Spherics* is a supplement to the *Elements*, and takes its content for granted as a prerequisite. The *Spherics* follows the general presentation style of the *Elements*, with definitions followed by a list of theorems (propositions), each of which is first stated abstractly as prose, then restated with [points](wikipedia://en/593693) lettered for the proof. It analyses [spherical circles](wikipedia://en/349704) as flat circles lying in planes intersecting the sphere and provides geometric constructions for various configurations of spherical circles. Spherical distances and radii are treated as Euclidean distances in the surrounding 3-dimensional space. The relationship between planes is described in terms of [dihedral angle](wikipedia://en/338046). As in the *Elements*, there is no concept of [angle measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_measure) or [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261) per se.

This approach differs from other quantitative methods of Greek astronomy such as the analemma ([orthographic projection](wikipedia://en/203052)),[^o4] [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431), or trigonometry (a fledgling subject introduced by Theodosius' contemporary [Hipparchus](wikipedia://en/13600)). It also differs from the approach taken in [Menelaus'](wikipedia://en/46301) *Spherics*, a treatise of the same title written 3 centuries later, which treats the geometry of the sphere *intrinsically*, analyzing the inherent structure of the spherical surface and circles drawn on it rather than primarily treating it as a surface embedded in three-dimensional space.

In [late antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_antiquity), the *Spherics* was part of a collection of treatises now called the *[Little Astronomy](wikipedia://en/79872883)*, an assortment of shorter works on geometry and astronomy building on Euclid's *Elements*. Other works in the collection included [Aristarchus](wikipedia://en/74857)' *[On the Sizes and Distances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Sizes_and_Distances_%28Aristarchus%29)*, Autolycus' *On Rising and Settings* and *On the Moving Sphere*, Euclid's *Catoptrics*, *[Data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid's_Data)*, *[Optics](wikipedia://en/24877937)*, and *Phenomena*, [Hypsicles](wikipedia://en/12815034)' *On Ascensions*, Theodosius' *On Geographic Places* and *On Days and Nights*, and Menelaus' *Spherics*. Often several of these were bound together in a single volume. During the [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age), the books in the collection were translated into [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic), and with the addition of a few new works, were known as the *Middle Books*, intended to fit between the *Elements* and [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979)'s *[Almagest](wikipedia://en/148060)*.[^o5]

Authoritative critical editions of the Greek text, compiled from several manuscripts, were made by (Heiberg 1927) and (Czinczenheim 2000). (Sidoli Thomas 2023) is an English translation by modern scholars.
