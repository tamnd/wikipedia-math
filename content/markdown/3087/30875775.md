# Null result

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Null result"}

In [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science), a **null result** is a result without the expected content: that is, the proposed result is absent.[^o2] It is an experimental outcome which does not show an otherwise expected effect. This does not imply a result of zero or nothing, simply a result that does not support the [hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis).

:::

In [statistical hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing), a null result occurs when an experimental result is not significantly different from what is to be expected under the [null hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis); its probability (under the null hypothesis) does not exceed the [significance level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Significance_level), i.e., the threshold set prior to testing for rejection of the null hypothesis. The significance level varies, but common choices include 0.10, 0.05, and 0.01.[^o3] However, a non-significant result does not necessarily mean that an effect is absent.[^o4][^o5][^o6][^o7]

As an example in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), the results of the [Michelson–Morley experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelson–Morley_experiment) were of this type, as it did not detect the expected velocity relative to the postulated [luminiferous aether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminiferous_aether). This experiment's famous failed detection, commonly referred to as the *null result*, contributed to the development of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962). The experiment did appear to measure a non-zero "drift", but the value was far too small to account for the theoretically expected results; it is generally thought to be inside the [noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise_%28signal_processing%29) level of the experiment.[^o8]

## Publishing bias {#Publishing_bias}

Despite similar quality of execution and [design](wikipedia://en/9541),[^o10] papers with statistically significant results are three times more likely to be published than those with null results.[^o11] This unduly motivates researchers to manipulate their practices to ensure statistically significant results, such as by [data dredging](wikipedia://en/1311951).[^o12]

Many factors contribute to publication bias.[^o13][^o14] For instance, once a scientific finding is well established, it may become newsworthy to publish reliable papers that fail to reject the null hypothesis.[^o15] Most commonly, investigators simply decline to submit results, leading to [non-response bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-response_bias). Investigators may also assume they made a mistake, find that the null result fails to support a known finding, lose interest in the topic, or anticipate that others will be uninterested in the null results.[^o10]

There are several scientific journals dedicated to the publication of negative or null results, including the following:

- *[Journal of Negative Results in Biomedicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Negative_Results_in_Biomedicine)* (defunct)
- *[Journal of Pharmaceutical Negative Results](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Pharmaceutical_Negative_Results)*
- *[Journal of Unsolved Questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Unsolved_Questions)*

While it is not exclusively dedicated to publishing negative results, [BMC Research Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BioMed_Central) also publishes negative results in the form of research or data notes.
