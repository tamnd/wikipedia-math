# Maschke's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Maschke's theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Maschke's theorem**,[^o2][^o3] named after [Heinrich Maschke](wikipedia://en/33372083), is a theorem in [group representation](wikipedia://en/12695) theory that concerns the decomposition of representations of a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) into [irreducible](wikipedia://en/385982) pieces. Maschke's theorem allows one to make general conclusions about representations of a finite group *G* without actually computing them. It reduces the task of classifying all representations to a more manageable task of classifying [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982), since when the theorem applies, any representation is a [direct sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_sum_of_representations) of irreducible pieces (constituents). Moreover, it follows from the [Jordan–Hölder theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan–Hölder_theorem) that, while the decomposition into a direct sum of irreducible subrepresentations may not be unique, the irreducible pieces have well-defined [multiplicities](wikipedia://en/19211790). In particular, a representation of a finite group over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) zero is determined up to [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) by its [character](wikipedia://en/691741).

:::

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Formulations"}

## Formulations {#Formulations}

Maschke's theorem addresses the question: when is a general (finite-dimensional) representation built from irreducible [subrepresentations](wikipedia://en/55943952) using the [direct sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_sum_of_representations) operation? This question (and its answer) are formulated differently for different perspectives on group representation theory.

### Group-theoretic {#Group-theoretic}

Maschke's theorem is commonly formulated as a [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865) to the following result:

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

Let $V$ be a representation of a finite group $G$ over a field $\mathbb{F}$ with [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) not dividing the [order](wikipedia://en/493527) of $G$. If $V$ has a subrepresentation $W$, then it has another subrepresentation $U$ such that $V=W\oplus U$.[^n1][^n2]

:::

Then the corollary is

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/corollary cue=math-theorem name="Corollary (Maschke's theorem)"}

**Corollary (Maschke's theorem).**

Every representation of a finite group $G$ over a field $\mathbb{F}$ with characteristic not dividing the order of $G$ is a direct sum of irreducible representations.[^n3][^n4]

:::

The [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of complex-valued [class functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_function_%28algebra%29) of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$ has a natural $G$-invariant [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) structure, described in the article [Schur orthogonality relations](wikipedia://en/7947411). Maschke's theorem was originally [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) for the case of representations over $\mathbb{C}$ by constructing $U$ as the [orthogonal complement](wikipedia://en/1058833) of $W$ under this inner product.

### Module-theoretic {#Module-theoretic}

One of the approaches to representations of finite groups is through [module theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Module_theory). *Representations* of a group $G$ are replaced by *modules* over its [group algebra](wikipedia://en/349223) $K[G]$ (to be precise, there is an [isomorphism of categories](wikipedia://en/489935) between $K[G]\text{-Mod}$ and $\operatorname{Rep}_{G}$, the [category of representations](wikipedia://en/55872661) of $G$). Irreducible representations correspond to [simple modules](wikipedia://en/29430). In the module-theoretic language, Maschke's theorem asks: is an arbitrary module [semisimple](wikipedia://en/1001361)? In this context, the theorem can be reformulated as follows:

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Maschke's Theorem"}

**Maschke's Theorem.**

Let $G$ be a finite group and $K$ a field whose characteristic does not divide the order of $G$. Then $K[G]$, the group algebra of $G$, is [semisimple](wikipedia://en/3876151).[^o16][^o17]

:::

The importance of this result stems from the well developed theory of semisimple rings, in particular, their classification as given by the [Wedderburn–Artin theorem](wikipedia://en/397264). When $K$ is the field of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), this shows that the algebra $K[G]$ is a product of several copies of complex [matrix algebras](wikipedia://en/20556859), one for each irreducible representation.[^o18] If the field $K$ has characteristic zero, but is not [algebraically closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_closed), for example if $K$ is the field of [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) numbers, then a somewhat more complicated statement holds: the group algebra $K[G]$ is a product of matrix algebras over [division rings](wikipedia://en/9067) over $K$. The summands correspond to irreducible representations of $G$ over $K$.[^o19]

### Category-theoretic {#Category-theoretic}

Reformulated in the language of [semi-simple categories](wikipedia://en/45256442), Maschke's theorem states

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Maschke's theorem"}

**Maschke's theorem.**

If $G$ is a group and $F$ is a field with characteristic not dividing the order of $G$, then the [category of representations](wikipedia://en/55872661) of $G$ over $F$ is semi-simple.

:::

:::

## Proofs {#Proofs}

### Group-theoretic {#Group-theoretic}

Let *U* be a subspace of *V* complement of *W*. Let $p_0 : V \to W$ be the projection function, i.e., $p_0(w + u) = w$ for any $u \in U, w \in W$.

Define $p(x) = \frac{1}{\#G} \sum_{g \in G} g \cdot p_0 \cdot g^{-1} (x)$, where $g \cdot p_0 \cdot g^{-1}$ is an abbreviation of $\rho_W{g} \cdot p_0 \cdot \rho_V{g^{-1}}$, with $\rho_W{g}, \rho_V{g^{-1}}$ being the representation of *G* on *W and* *V*. Then, $\ker p$ is preserved by *G* under representation $\rho_V$: for any $w' \in \ker p, h \in G$,

$$\begin{aligned} p(hw') &= h \cdot h^{-1} \frac{1}{\#G} \sum_{g \in G} g \cdot p_0 \cdot g^{-1} (hw') \\ &= h \cdot \frac{1}{\#G} \sum_{g \in G} (h^{-1} \cdot g) \cdot p_0 \cdot (g^{-1} h) w' \\ &= h \cdot \frac{1}{\#G} \sum_{g \in G} g \cdot p_0 \cdot g^{-1} w' \\ &= h \cdot p(w') \\ &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

so $w' \in \ker p$ implies that $hw' \in \ker p$. So the restriction of $\rho_V$ on $\ker p$ is also a representation.

By the definition of $p$, for any $w \in W$, $p(w) = w$, so $W \cap \ker\ p = \{0\}$, and for any $v \in V$, $p(p(v)) = p(v)$. Thus, $p(v-p(v)) = 0$, and $v - p(v) \in \ker p$. Therefore, $V = W \oplus \ker p$.

### Module-theoretic {#Module-theoretic}

Let *V* be a *K*\[*G*\]-submodule. We will prove that *V* is a direct summand. Let *π* be any *K*-linear projection of *K*\[*G*\] onto *V*. Consider the map

$$\begin{cases} \varphi:K[G]\to V \\ \varphi:x \mapsto \frac{1}{\#G}\sum_{s \in G} s\cdot \pi(s^{-1} \cdot x) \end{cases}$$

Then *φ* is again a projection: it is clearly *K*-linear, maps *K*\[*G*\] to *V*, and induces the identity on *V* (therefore, maps *K*\[*G*\] onto *V*). Moreover we have

$$\begin{aligned} \varphi(t\cdot x) &= \frac{1}{\#G}\sum_{s \in G} s\cdot \pi(s^{-1}\cdot t\cdot x)\\ &= \frac{1}{\#G}\sum_{u \in G} t\cdot u\cdot \pi(u^{-1}\cdot x)\\ &= t\cdot\varphi(x), \end{aligned}$$

so *φ* is in fact *K*\[*G*\]-linear. By the [splitting lemma](wikipedia://en/138378), $K[G]=V \oplus \ker \varphi$. This proves that every submodule is a direct summand, that is, *K*\[*G*\] is semisimple.

:::

## Converse statement {#Converse_statement}

The above proof depends on the fact that #*G* is [invertible](wikipedia://en/289666) in *K*. This might lead one to ask if the [converse](wikipedia://en/155941) of Maschke's theorem also holds: if the characteristic of *K* divides the order of *G*, does it follow that *K*\[*G*\] is not semisimple? The answer is *yes*.[^n5]

::: {#o28 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof.** For $x = \sum\lambda_g g\in K[G]$ define $\epsilon(x) = \sum\lambda_g$. Let $I=\ker\epsilon$. Then *I* is a *K*\[*G*\]-submodule. We will prove that for every nontrivial submodule *V* of *K*\[*G*\], $I \cap V \neq 0$. Let *V* be given, and let $v=\sum\mu_gg$ be any nonzero element of *V*. If $\epsilon(v)=0$, the claim is immediate. Otherwise, let $s = \sum 1 g$. Then $\epsilon(s) = \#G \cdot 1 = 0$ so $s \in I$ and

$$sv = \left(\sum1g\right)\!\left(\sum\mu_gg\right) = \sum\epsilon(v)g = \epsilon(v)s$$

so that $sv$ is a nonzero element of both *I* and *V*. This proves *V* is not a direct complement of *I* for all *V*, so *K*\[*G*\] is not semisimple.

:::

::: {#o30 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=heading name="Non-examples"}

## Non-examples {#Non-examples}

The theorem can not apply to the case where *G* is infinite, or when the field *K* has characteristics dividing #*G*. For example,

- Consider the infinite group $\mathbb{Z}$ and the representation $\rho: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{C})$ defined by $\rho(n) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}^n = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & n \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Let $W = \mathbb{C} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, a 1-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{C}^2$ spanned by $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Then the restriction of $\rho$ on *W* is a [trivial subrepresentation](wikipedia://en/450573) of $\mathbb{Z}$. However, there's no *U* such that both *W, U* are subrepresentations of $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{C}^2 = W \oplus U$: any such *U* needs to be 1-dimensional, but any 1-dimensional subspace preserved by $\rho$ has to be spanned by an [eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) for $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, and the only eigenvector for that is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$.
- Consider a [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) *p*, and the group $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, field $K = \mathbb{F}_p$, and the representation $\rho: \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \to \mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{F}_p)$ defined by $\rho(n) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & n \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Simple calculations show that there is only one eigenvector for $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ here, so by the same argument, the 1-dimensional subrepresentation of $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is unique, and $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ cannot be decomposed into the direct sum of two 1-dimensional subrepresentations.

:::
