# Riemann sphere

*Image: RiemannKugel.svg.* The Riemann sphere can be visualized as the complex number plane wrapped around a sphere (by some form of [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431) – details are given below).

*Image: Riemann sphere1.svg.* [Stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431) of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $A$ and $B$ onto the points $\alpha$ and $\beta$ of the **Riemann sphere**. The point $\alpha$ is the intersection of the line through the two points $P(\infty)$ and *A* with the sphere.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Riemann sphere"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Riemann sphere**, named after [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980), [^n1] is a [model](wikipedia://en/20590) of the **extended complex plane** (also called the **closed complex plane**): the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) plus one [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139). This extended plane represents the **extended complex numbers**, that is, the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) plus a value $\infty$ for [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824). With the Riemann model, the point $\infty$ is near to very large numbers, just as the point $0$ is near to very small numbers.

:::

The extended complex numbers are useful in [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759) because they allow for [division by zero](wikipedia://en/185663) in some circumstances, in a way that makes expressions such as $1/0=\infty$ [well-behaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-behaved). For example, any [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210) on the complex plane can be extended to a [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110) on the Riemann sphere, with the [poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) of the rational function mapping to infinity. More generally, any [meromorphic function](wikipedia://en/80857) can be thought of as a holomorphic function whose [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) is the Riemann sphere.

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the Riemann sphere is the prototypical example of a [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181), and is one of the simplest [complex manifolds](wikipedia://en/509742). In [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849), the sphere is an example of a [complex projective space](wikipedia://en/762977) and can be thought of as the complex [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578) $\mathbf{P}^1(\mathbf{C})$, the [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) of all [complex lines](wikipedia://en/7975294) in $\mathbf{C}^2$. As with any [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) Riemann surface, the sphere may also be viewed as a projective [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260), making it a fundamental example in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997). It also finds utility in other disciplines that depend on analysis and geometry, such as the [Bloch sphere](wikipedia://en/729572) of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) and in other [branches of physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branches_of_physics).

## Extended complex numbers {#Extended_complex_numbers}

The extended complex numbers consist of the complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$ together with $\infty$. The set of extended complex numbers may be written as $\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}$, and is often denoted by adding some decoration to the letter $\mathbb{C}$, such as

$$\widehat{\mathbb{C}},\quad\overline{\mathbb{C}},\quad\text{or}\quad\mathbb{C}_\infty.$$

The notation $\mathbb{C}^{*}$ has also seen use, but as this notation is also used for the punctured plane $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$, it can lead to ambiguity.[^o4]

Geometrically, the set of extended complex numbers is referred to as the Riemann sphere (or extended complex plane).

### Arithmetic operations {#Arithmetic_operations}

[Addition](wikipedia://en/61338) of complex numbers may be extended by defining, for $z\in\mathbb{C}$,

$$z+\infty=\infty$$

and [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) may be defined by

$$z\times\infty=\infty$$

for all nonzero complex numbers $z$, with $\infty\times\infty = \infty$. Note that $\infty +\infty$, $\infty -\infty$, and $0\times \infty$ are left [undefined](wikipedia://en/366723). Unlike the complex numbers, the extended complex numbers do not form a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), since $\infty$ does not have an [additive](wikipedia://en/228312) nor [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940). Nonetheless, it is customary to define [division](wikipedia://en/53696) on $\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}$ by

$$\frac{z}{0} = \infty\quad\text{and}\quad \frac{z}{\infty} = 0$$

for all nonzero complex numbers $z$ with $\infty/0 = \infty$ and $0/\infty =0$. The quotients $0/0$ and $\infty/\infty$ are left undefined.

### Rational functions {#Rational_functions}

Any [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210) $f(z) = g(z)/h(z)$ (in other words, $f(z)$ is the ratio of polynomial functions $g(z)$ and $h(z)$ of $z$ with complex coefficients, such that $g(z)$ and $h(z)$ have no common factor) can be extended to a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) on the Riemann sphere. Specifically, if $z_0$ is a complex number such that the denominator $h(z_0)$ is zero but the numerator $g(z_0)$ is nonzero, then $f(z_0)$ can be defined as $\infty$. Moreover, $f(\infty)$ can be defined as the [limit](wikipedia://en/285759) of $f(z)$ as $z\to\infty$, which may be finite or infinite.

The set of complex rational functions—whose mathematical symbol is $\mathbb{C}(z)$—form all possible [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110) from the Riemann sphere to itself, when it is viewed as a [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181), except for the constant function taking the value $\infty$ everywhere. The functions of $\mathbb{C}(z)$ form an algebraic field, known as *the field of rational functions on the sphere*.

For example, given the function

$$f(z) = \frac{6z^2 + 1}{2z^2 - 50}$$

we may define $f(\pm 5) = \infty$, since the denominator is zero at $\pm 5$, and $f(\infty) = 3$ since $f(z)\to 3$ as $z\to\infty$. Using these definitions, $f$ becomes a continuous function from the Riemann sphere to itself.

## As a complex manifold {#As_a_complex_manifold}

As a one-dimensional [complex manifold](wikipedia://en/509742), the Riemann sphere can be described by two [charts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chart_%28topology%29), both with domain equal to the complex number plane $\mathbf{C}$. Let $\zeta$ be a complex number in one copy of $\mathbf{C}$, and let $\xi$ be a complex number in another copy of $\mathbf{C}$. Identify each nonzero complex number $\zeta$ of the first $\mathbf{C}$ with the nonzero complex number $1/\xi$ of the second $\mathbf{C}$. Then the map

$$f(z) = \frac{1}{z}$$

is called the [transition map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_map) between the two copies of $\mathbf{C}$—the so-called charts—glueing them together. Since the transition maps are [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110), they define a complex manifold, called the **Riemann sphere**. As a complex manifold of 1 complex dimension (i.e. 2 real dimensions), this is also called a **Riemann surface**.

Intuitively, the transition maps indicate how to glue two planes together to form the Riemann sphere. The planes are glued in an "inside-out" manner, so that they overlap almost everywhere, with each plane contributing just one point (its origin) missing from the other plane. In other words, (almost) every point in the Riemann sphere has both a $\zeta$ value and a $\xi$ value, and the two values are related by $\zeta=1/\xi$. The point where $\xi=0$ should then have $\zeta$-value "$1/0$"; in this sense, the origin of the $\xi$-chart plays the role of $\infty$ in the $\zeta$-chart. Symmetrically, the origin of the $\zeta$-chart plays the role of $\infty$ in the $\xi$-chart.

[Topologically](wikipedia://en/29954), the resulting space is the [one-point compactification](wikipedia://en/6617) of a plane into the sphere. However, the Riemann sphere is not merely a topological sphere. It is a sphere with a well-defined [complex structure](wikipedia://en/509742), so that around every point on the sphere there is a neighborhood that can be [biholomorphically](wikipedia://en/6077963) identified with $\mathbf{C}$.

On the other hand, the [uniformization theorem](wikipedia://en/288270), a central result in the classification of Riemann surfaces, states that every [simply-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply-connected) Riemann surface is biholomorphic to the complex plane, the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/318742), or the Riemann sphere. Of these, the Riemann sphere is the only one that is a [closed surface](wikipedia://en/669475) (a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) surface without [boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold_with_boundary)). Hence the two-dimensional sphere admits a unique complex structure turning it into a one-dimensional complex manifold.

## As the complex projective line {#As_the_complex_projective_line}

The Riemann sphere can also be defined as the **complex projective line**. The points of the complex projective line can be defined as [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of *non-null vectors* in the complex vector space $\mathbf{C}^2$: two non-null vectors $(w, z)$ and $(u, v)$ are equivalent iff $(w, z) = (\lambda u, \lambda v)$ for some non-zero coefficient $\lambda \in \mathbf{C}$.

In this case, the equivalence class is written $[w,z]$ using [projective coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_coordinates). Given any point $[w,z]$ in the complex projective line, one of $w$ and $z$ must be non-zero, say $w \ne 0$. Then by the notion of equivalence, $[w, z] = \left[1, z/w \right]$, which is in a chart for the Riemann sphere manifold.[^n2]

This treatment of the Riemann sphere connects most readily to projective geometry. For example, any line (or smooth conic) in the [complex projective plane](wikipedia://en/737155) is biholomorphic to the complex projective line. It is also convenient for studying the sphere's [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160), later in this article.

:::

## As a sphere {#As_a_sphere}

*Image: RiemannSphere.png.* Stereographic projection of a complex number *A* onto a point α of the Riemann sphere.

*Image: Earth as riemann sphere large 500mio 254dpi.jpg.* Riemann sphere: almost the whole earth in stereographic azimuthal projection 1:500.000.000 (254 dpi)

The Riemann sphere can be visualized as the unit sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1$ in the three-dimensional real space $\mathbf{R}^3$. To this end, consider the [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431) from the unit sphere minus the point $(0,0,1)$ onto the plane $z = 0,$ which we identify with the complex plane by $\zeta=x+iy$. In [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) $(x,y,z)$ and [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates) $(\theta,\varphi)$ on the sphere (with $\theta$ the [zenith angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenith_angle) and $\varphi$ the [azimuth](wikipedia://en/47487)), the projection is

$$\zeta = \frac{x + i y}{1 - z} = {\cot}\bigl(\tfrac12 \theta\bigr) \, e^{i \varphi}.$$

Similarly, stereographic projection from $(0,0,-1)$ onto the plane $z = 0,$ identified with another copy of the complex plane by $\xi = x - iy,$ is written

$$\xi = \frac{x - i y}{1 + z} = {\tan}\bigl(\tfrac12 \theta\bigr) \, e^{-i \varphi}.$$

The inverses of these two stereographic projections are maps from the complex plane to the sphere. The first inverse covers the sphere except the point $(0, 0, 1)$, and the second covers the sphere except the point $(0, 0, -1)$. The two complex planes, that are the domains of these maps, are identified differently with the plane $z = 0$, because an [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29)-reversal is necessary to maintain consistent orientation on the sphere.

The transition maps between $\zeta$-coordinates and $\xi$-coordinates are obtained by composing one projection with the inverse of the other. They turn out to be $\zeta = 1 / \xi$ and $\xi = 1 / \zeta$, as described above. Thus the unit sphere is [diffeomorphic](wikipedia://en/8564) to the Riemann sphere.

Under this diffeomorphism, the unit circle in the $\zeta$-chart, the unit circle in the $\xi$-chart, and the equator of the unit sphere are all identified. The unit disk $|\zeta| < 1$ is identified with the southern hemisphere $z < 0$, while the unit disk $|\xi| < 1$ is identified with the northern hemisphere $z > 0$.

## Metric {#Metric}

A Riemann surface does not come equipped with any particular [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric). The Riemann surface's conformal structure does, however, determine a class of metrics: all those whose subordinate conformal structure is the given one. In more detail: The complex structure of the Riemann surface does uniquely determine a metric up to [conformal equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_equivalence). (Two metrics are said to be conformally equivalent if they differ by multiplication by a positive [smooth function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function).) Conversely, any metric on an [oriented surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented_surface) uniquely determines a complex structure, which depends on the metric only up to conformal equivalence. Complex structures on an oriented surface are therefore in one-to-one correspondence with conformal classes of metrics on that surface.

Within a given conformal class, one can use conformal symmetry to find a representative metric with convenient properties. In particular, there is always a complete metric with [constant curvature](wikipedia://en/3638803) in any given conformal class.

In the case of the Riemann sphere, the [Gauss–Bonnet theorem](wikipedia://en/139229) implies that a constant-curvature metric $\gamma$ must have positive [curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) $K$. It follows that the metric must be [isometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometry_%28Riemannian_geometry%29) to the sphere of radius $1/\sqrt{K}$ in $\mathbf{R}^3$ via stereographic projection. In the $\zeta$-chart on the Riemann sphere, the metric with $K=1$ is given by

$$ds^2 = 2\gamma_{\zeta\overline\zeta} \,d\zeta \,d\overline \zeta = \frac{4}{\left(1 + \zeta \overline \zeta\right)^2}\,d\zeta \,d\overline \zeta = \left(\frac{2}{1+|\zeta|^2}\right)^2\,|d\zeta|^2.$$

In real coordinates $\zeta=u+iv$, the formula is

$$ds^2 = \frac{4}{\left(1 + u^2 + v^2\right)^2} \left(du^2 + dv^2\right).$$

Up to a constant factor, this metric agrees with the standard [Fubini–Study metric](wikipedia://en/2574486) on complex projective space (of which the Riemann sphere is an example). The two non-vanishing [Christoffel symbols](wikipedia://en/1401020) of its [Levi-Civita connection](wikipedia://en/249176) are $\Gamma^\zeta_{\zeta\zeta} = -2\overline\zeta/(1 + |\zeta|^2)$ and its conjugate. This metric is therefore equal to its own [Ricci curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_curvature_tensor), $\gamma = \mathrm{Ric}$.

Up to scaling, this is the *only* metric on the sphere whose group of orientation-preserving isometries is 3-dimensional (and none is more than 3-dimensional); that group is called [$\mbox{SO}(3)$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SO%283%29). In this sense, this is by far the most symmetric metric on the sphere. (The group of all isometries, known as [$\mbox{O}(3)$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O%283%29), is also 3-dimensional, but unlike $\mbox{SO}(3)$ is not a connected space.)

Conversely, let $S$ denote the sphere (as an abstract [smooth](wikipedia://en/2119219) or [topological manifold](wikipedia://en/2119193)). By the uniformization theorem there exists a unique complex structure on $S$ up to conformal equivalence. It follows that any metric on $S$ is conformally equivalent to the [round metric](wikipedia://en/195795#The_round_metric_on_a_sphere). All such metrics determine the same conformal geometry. The round metric is therefore not intrinsic to the Riemann sphere, since "roundness" is not an invariant of conformal geometry. The Riemann sphere is only a [conformal manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_manifold), not a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652). However, if one needs to do Riemannian geometry on the Riemann sphere, the round metric is a natural choice (with any fixed radius, though radius $1$ is the simplest and most common choice). That is because only a round metric on the Riemann sphere has its isometry group be a 3-dimensional group. (Namely, the group known as [$\mbox{SO}(3)$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_group_SO%283%29), a continuous ("Lie") group that is topologically the 3-dimensional [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) $\mathbf{P}^3$.)

## Automorphisms {#Automorphisms}

*Image: Mob3d-elip-opp-200.png.* A [Möbius transformation](wikipedia://en/314493) acting on the sphere, and on the plane by [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431).

The study of any [mathematical object](wikipedia://en/19453961) is aided by an understanding of its [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of automorphisms, meaning the maps from the object to itself that preserve the essential structure of the object. In the case of the Riemann sphere, an automorphism is an invertible [conformal map](wikipedia://en/50627) (i.e. biholomorphic map) from the Riemann sphere to itself. It turns out that the only such maps are the [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493). These are functions of the form

$$f(\zeta) = \frac{a \zeta + b}{c \zeta + d},$$

where $a$, $b$, $c$, and $d$ are complex numbers such that $ad-bc\ne 0$. Examples of Möbius transformations include [dilations](wikipedia://en/651752), [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789), [translations](wikipedia://en/204682), and complex inversion. In fact, any Möbius transformation can be written as a composition of these.

The Möbius transformations are [homographies](wikipedia://en/3051962) on the complex projective line. In [projective coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_coordinates), the transformation *f* can be written

$$[\zeta,\ 1 ] \begin{pmatrix} a & c \\ b & d \end{pmatrix} \ = \ [ a\zeta + b, \ c\zeta + d ] \ = \ \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac{a\zeta + b}{c\zeta + d} & 1 \end{pmatrix} \ = \ [ f(\zeta),\ 1 ].$$

Thus the Möbius transformations can be described as two-by-two complex matrices with nonzero [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468). Since they act on projective coordinates, two matrices yield the same Möbius transformation if and only if they differ by a nonzero factor. The [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of Möbius transformations is the [projective linear group](wikipedia://en/382667) $\mbox{PGL}(2,\mathbf{C})$.

If one endows the Riemann sphere with the [Fubini–Study metric](wikipedia://en/2574486), then not all Möbius transformations are isometries; for example, the dilations and translations are not. The isometries form a proper subgroup of $\mbox{PGL}(2,\mathbf{C})$, namely $\mbox{PSU}(2)$. This subgroup is isomorphic to the [rotation group $\mbox{SO}(3)$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_group_SO%283%29), which is the group of symmetries of the unit sphere in $\mathbf{R}^3$ (which, when restricted to the sphere, become the isometries of the sphere).

## Applications {#Applications}

In complex analysis, a meromorphic function on the complex plane (or on any Riemann surface, for that matter) is a ratio $f/g$ of two holomorphic functions $f$ and $g$. As a map to the complex numbers, it is undefined wherever $g$ is zero. However, it induces a holomorphic map $(f,g)$ to the complex projective line that is well-defined even where $g=0$. This construction is helpful in the study of holomorphic and meromorphic functions. For example, on a compact Riemann surface there are no non-constant holomorphic maps to the complex numbers, but holomorphic maps to the complex projective line are abundant.

The Riemann sphere is often cited as a construction on which one can easily visualise [generalised circles](wikipedia://en/10766404), [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493) and conformal maps between connected open subsets of the extended complex plane.

The Riemann sphere has many uses in physics. In quantum mechanics, points on the complex projective line are natural values for [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) [polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon_polarization) states, [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829) states of [massive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) [particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subatomic_particle) of spin $1/2$, and 2-state particles in general (see also [Quantum bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_bit) and [Bloch sphere](wikipedia://en/729572)). The Riemann sphere has been suggested as a [relativistic](wikipedia://en/12024) model for the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239).[^n3] In [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305), the [worldsheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worldsheet) of strings are Riemann surfaces, and the Riemann sphere, being the simplest Riemann surface, plays a significant role. It is also important in [twistor theory](wikipedia://en/260836).
