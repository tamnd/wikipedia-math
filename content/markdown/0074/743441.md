# Bouguer anomaly

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bouguer anomaly"}

In [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608) and [geophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysics), the **Bouguer anomaly** (named after [Pierre Bouguer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Bouguer)) is a [gravity anomaly](wikipedia://en/937535), corrected for the height at which it is measured and the attraction of terrain.[^o2] The height correction alone gives a [free-air gravity anomaly](wikipedia://en/569071).

:::

*Image: Nj cboug.jpg.* Bouguer anomaly map of the state of [New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey) (USGS)

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The Bouguer anomaly $g_B$ defined as:

$$g_B = g_{F} - \delta g_B + \delta g_T$$

Here,

- $g_F$ is the free-air gravity anomaly.
- $\delta g_B$ is the *Bouguer correction* which allows for the gravitational attraction of rocks between the measurement point and sea level;
- $\delta g_T$ is a *terrain correction* which allows for deviations of the surface from an infinite horizontal plane

The free-air anomaly $g_F$, in its turn, is related to the observed gravity $g_{obs}$ as follows:

$$g_F = g_{obs} - g_\lambda + \delta g_F$$

where:

- $g_\lambda$ is the correction for latitude (because the Earth is not a perfect sphere; see [normal gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_gravity));
- $\delta g_F$ is the [free-air correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free-air_correction).

:::

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Bouguer reduction"}

## Reduction {#Reduction}

A **Bouguer reduction** is called *simple* (or *incomplete*) if the terrain is approximated by an infinite flat plate called the **Bouguer plate**. A *refined* (or *complete*) Bouguer reduction removes the effects of [terrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrain) more precisely. The difference between the two is called the *(residual) terrain effect* (or *(residual) terrain correction*) and is due to the differential gravitational effect of the unevenness of the terrain; it is always negative.[^o7]

### Simple reduction {#Simple_reduction}

The gravitational acceleration $g$ outside a Bouguer plate is perpendicular to the plate and towards it, with magnitude *2πG* times the mass per unit area, where $G$ is the [gravitational constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_constant). It is independent of the distance to the plate (as can be proven most simply with [Gauss's law for gravity](wikipedia://en/16704344), but can also be proven directly with [Newton's law of gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation)). The value of $G$ is \6.67 × 10<sup>\-11</sup> N m<sup>2</sup> kg<sup>−2</sup>, so $g$ is \4.191 × 10<sup>\-10</sup> N m<sup>2</sup> kg<sup>−2</sup> times the mass per unit area. Using 1 Gal = \0.01 m s<sup>−2</sup> (1 cm s<sup>−2</sup>) we get \4.191 × 10<sup>\-5</sup> mGal m<sup>2</sup> kg<sup>−1</sup> times the mass per unit area. For mean [rock](wikipedia://en/199661) [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) (\2.67 g cm<sup>−3</sup>) this gives \0.1119 mGal m<sup>−1</sup>

The Bouguer reduction for a Bouguer plate of thickness $H$ is

$$\delta g_B = 2\pi\rho G H$$

where $\rho$ is the density of the material and $G$ is the constant of gravitation.[^o7] On Earth the effect on gravity of elevation is 0.3086 mGal m<sup>−1</sup> decrease when going up, minus the gravity of the Bouguer plate, giving the *Bouguer gradient* of 0.1967 mGal m<sup>−1</sup>.

More generally, for a [mass distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_distribution) with the density depending on one Cartesian coordinate *z* only, gravity for any *z* is 2π*G* times the difference in mass per unit area on either side of this *z* value. A combination of two parallel infinite if equal mass per unit area plates does not produce any gravity between them.
