# Hamiltonian decomposition

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hamiltonian decomposition"}

*Image: Walecki decomposition.svg.* Walecki's Hamiltonian decomposition of the complete graph $K_9$

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a branch of mathematics, a **Hamiltonian decomposition** of a given graph is a [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) of the edges of the graph into [Hamiltonian cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle). Hamiltonian decompositions have been studied both for [undirected graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) and for [directed graphs](wikipedia://en/19721986). In the undirected case a Hamiltonian decomposition can also be described as a [2-factorization](wikipedia://en/3298854) of the graph such that each factor is connected.

:::

## Necessary conditions {#Necessary_conditions}

For a Hamiltonian decomposition to exist in an undirected graph, the graph must be connected and [regular](wikipedia://en/85821) of even [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448). A directed graph with such a decomposition must be [strongly connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strongly_connected_graph) and all vertices must have the same in-degree and out-degree as each other, but this degree does not need to be even.

## Special classes of graphs {#Special_classes_of_graphs}

### Complete graphs {#Complete_graphs}

Every [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) with an odd number $n$ of vertices has a Hamiltonian decomposition. This result, which is a special case of the [Oberwolfach problem](wikipedia://en/55972236) of decomposing complete graphs into isomorphic 2-factors, was attributed to Walecki by [Édouard Lucas](wikipedia://en/150102) in 1892. The original formulation of the Oberwolfach problem asks how to make a seating chart for $n$ people over $(n-1)/2$ dinners in a given set of circular tables of different sizes, such that each participant sits next to each other exactly once. Walecki's theorem corresponds to the case where there is one single table.

Walecki's original construction places $n-1$ of the vertices into a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306), and covers the complete graph in this subset of vertices with $(n-1)/2$ Hamiltonian paths that zigzag across the polygon, with each path rotated from each other path by a multiple of $\pi/(n-1)$. The paths can then all be completed to Hamiltonian cycles by connecting their ends through the remaining vertex.

Expanding a vertex of a $2k$-regular graph into a [clique](wikipedia://en/524466) of $2k$ vertices, one for each endpoint of an edge at the replaced vertex, cannot change whether the graph has a Hamiltonian decomposition. The reverse of this expansion process, collapsing a clique to a single vertex, will transform any Hamiltonian decomposition in the larger graph into a Hamiltonian decomposition in the original graph. Conversely, Walecki's construction can be applied to the clique to expand any Hamiltonian decomposition of the smaller graph into a Hamiltonian decomposition of the expanded graph.

One kind of analogue of a complete graph, in the case of directed graphs, is a [tournament](wikipedia://en/1427502). This is a graph in which every pair of distinct vertices is connected by a single directed edge, from one to the other; for instance, such a graph may describe the outcome of a [round-robin tournament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round-robin_tournament) in sports, where each competitor in the tournament plays each other competitor, and edges are directed from the loser of each game to the winner. Answering a conjecture by [Paul Kelly](wikipedia://en/45092620) from 1968, [Daniela Kühn](wikipedia://en/37003499) and [Deryk Osthus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deryk_Osthus) proved in 2012 that every sufficiently large regular tournament has a Hamiltonian decomposition.

### Planar graphs {#Planar_graphs}

*Image: Medial Herschel graph.svg.* The [medial graph](wikipedia://en/28280731) of the [Herschel graph](wikipedia://en/24670148) is a 4-regular [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314) with no Hamiltonian decomposition. The shaded regions correspond to the vertices of the underlying Herschel graph.

For 4-regular [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314), additional necessary conditions can be derived from [Grinberg's theorem](wikipedia://en/22816079). An example of a 4-regular planar graph that does not meet these conditions, and does not have a Hamiltonian decomposition, is given by the [medial graph](wikipedia://en/28280731) of the [Herschel graph](wikipedia://en/24670148).

### Prisms {#Prisms}

::: {#o7 .problem cue=unsolved}

Does every prism over a 3-connected 3-regular graph have a Hamiltonian decomposition?

:::

The *prism* over a graph is its [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/2856852) with the two-vertex complete graph. For instance, the prism over a [cycle graph](wikipedia://en/878962) is the graph of a [geometric prism](wikipedia://en/283007). The 4-regular graphs obtained as prisms over 3-regular graphs have been particularly studied with respect to Hamiltonian decomposition. When the underlying 3-regular graph is [3-vertex-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph), the resulting 4-regular prism always has a Hamiltonian cycle and, in all examples that have been tested, a Hamiltonian decomposition. Based on this observation, Alspach and Rosenfeld conjectured in 1986 that all prisms over 3-regular 3-vertex-connected graphs have a Hamiltonian decomposition.

Many classes of 3-regular 3-vertex-connected graphs are known to have prisms with Hamiltonian decompositions. In particular this occurs when the 3-regular graph is planar and bipartite, when it is a [Halin graph](wikipedia://en/19658548), when it is itself a prism or [Möbius ladder](wikipedia://en/7355278), or when it is a [generalized Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/23716451) of order divisible by four.

### Symmetric graphs {#Symmetric_graphs}

There are infinitely many [vertex-transitive graphs](wikipedia://en/581175) (graphs in which every vertex is symmetric to every other vertex) that do not have a Hamiltonian decomposition. In particular this applies to the [Cayley graphs](wikipedia://en/358277) whose vertices describe the elements of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) and whose elements describe multiplication by [generators of the group](wikipedia://en/99945). Infinitely many 6-regular Cayley graphs have no Hamiltonian decomposition, and there exist Cayley graphs of arbitrarily large even degree with no Hamiltonian decomposition. One way of constructing these graphs is to use repeated expansions by cliques, which preserve symmetry and cannot change the existence of a Hamiltonian decomposition.

## Uniform Hypergraphs {#Uniform_Hypergraphs}

**Decomposition problems** for hypergraphs are in general much harder than for graphs. Unlike graphs, hypergraphs admit multiple non-equivalent notions of cycles (see [Hypergraph cycles](wikipedia://en/232535#Cycles)).

The simplest of these notions is the [Berge-cycle](wikipedia://en/232535#Cycles#Cycles). In 2014, Kühn and Osthus[^o10] showed that the complete $k$-uniform hypergraph $K_n^{(k)}$ admits a decomposition into Hamilton Berge-cycles whenever $n$ divides ${\tbinom{n}{k}}$.

::: {#o11 .problem cue=unsolved}

For integers $n$ and $k$ where $n$ divides $\tbinom{n}{k}$, does the complete $k$-uniform hypergraph $K_n^{(k)}$ admit a tight Hamiltonian decomposition?

:::

However, for the most commonly used notion of cycle in hypergraph —the [tight cycle](wikipedia://en/232535#Cycles)— it remains an [open problem](wikipedia://en/1333335) whether the complete $k$-uniform hypergraph $K_n^{(k)}$ admits a Hamiltonian decomposition.[^o12] Formulated as the seating arrangement issue from the [Oberwolfach problem](wikipedia://en/55972236) presented above, finding these decompositions corresponds to the case in which every set of $k$ people sits consecutively exactly once. In this case the number of dinners has to be $\tbinom{n-1}{k-1}/k$.

[Baranyai's theorem](wikipedia://en/24011805) addresses a similar problem, by finding a decomposition of the complete $k$-uniform hypergraph into perfect matchings.

## Number of decompositions {#Number_of_decompositions}

Every 4-regular undirected graph has an even number of Hamiltonian decompositions. More strongly, for every two edges $e$ and $f$ of a 4-regular graph, the number of Hamiltonian decompositions in which $e$ and $f$ belong to the same cycle is even. If a $2k$-regular graph has a Hamiltonian decomposition, it has at least a [triple factorial](wikipedia://en/507208#Generalizations) number of decompositions,

$$(3k-2)\cdot(3k-5)\cdots 7\cdot 4 \cdot 1.$$

For instance, 4-regular graphs that have a Hamiltonian decomposition have at least four of them; 6-regular graphs that have a Hamiltonian decomposition have at least 28, etc. It follows that the only graphs whose Hamiltonian decompositions are unique are the [cycle graphs](wikipedia://en/878962).

## Computational complexity {#Computational_complexity}

Testing whether an arbitrary graph has a Hamiltonian decomposition is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete), both in the directed and undirected cases. In particular, the question is NP-complete for regular graphs of a specified even degree; e.g., for 4-regular graphs.

The [line graphs](wikipedia://en/675231) of [cubic graphs](wikipedia://en/980508) are 4-regular and have a Hamiltonian decomposition [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the underlying cubic graph has a Hamiltonian cycle. As a consequence, Hamiltonian decomposition remains NP-complete for classes of graphs that include line graphs of hard instances of the [Hamiltonian cycle problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle_problem). For instance, Hamiltonian decomposition is NP-complete for the 4-regular planar graphs, because they include the line graphs of cubic planar graphs. On the other hand, this equivalence also implies that Hamiltonian decomposition is easy for 4-regular line graphs when their underlying cubic graphs have easy Hamiltonian cycle problems.

[Random regular graphs](wikipedia://en/10151936) of even degree [almost surely](wikipedia://en/351908) have a Hamiltonian decomposition, and more strongly there is a [randomized polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomized_polynomial_time) algorithm which, when given as input a random regular graph of even degree, almost surely finds a Hamiltonian decomposition in it.
