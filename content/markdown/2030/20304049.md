# Harris–Benedict equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Harris–Benedict equation"}

The **Harris–Benedict equation** (also called the **Harris-Benedict principle**) is a method used to estimate an individual's [basal metabolic rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basal_metabolic_rate) (BMR).[^o2]

:::

The estimated BMR value may be multiplied by a number that corresponds to the individual's activity level; the resulting number is the approximate daily [kilocalorie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilocalorie) intake to maintain current [body weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_weight).

The Harris-Benedict equation may be used to assist weight loss — by reducing the kilocalorie intake number below the estimated maintenance intake of the equation.

## Calculating the Harris-Benedict BMR {#Calculating_the_Harris-Benedict_BMR}

The original Harris–Benedict equations were published in 1918 and 1919.[^o4][^o5]

::: {#o6 .table cue=table}

| Sex | Calculations of BMR |
|---|---|
| Male | ${BMR}={a_m}M+{b_m}H-{c_m}T+d_m$ |
| Female | ${BMR}={a_f}M+{b_f}H-{c_f}T+d_f$ |

:::

where M is the subject's mass, H is the subject's height, T is the subject's age, $a_m=13.7516 {kg}^{-1} {kcal}\ d^{-1}$, $b_m = 5.0033 {cm}^{-1} {kcal}\ d^{-1}$, $c_m=6.755 {kcal}\ a^{-1} d^{-1}$, $d_m=66.473 {kcal}\ d^{-1}$, $a_f=9.5634 {kg}^{-1} {kcal}\ d^{-1}$, $b_f = 1.8496 {cm}^{-1} {kcal}\ d^{-1}$, $c_f=4.6756 {kcal}\ a^{-1} d^{-1}$, and $d_f=655.0955 {kcal}\ d^{-1}$.

The coefficients were revised by Roza and Shizgal in 1984.[^o7]

::: {#o8 .table cue=table}

| a<sub>m</sub> | $13.397 {kg}^{-1} {kcal}\ d^{-1}$ |
|---|---|
| a<sub>f</sub> | $9.247 {kg}^{-1} {kcal}\ d^{-1}$ |
| b<sub>m</sub> | $4.799 {cm}^{-1} {kcal}\ d^{-1}$ |
| b<sub>f</sub> | $3.098 {cm}^{-1} {kcal}\ d^{-1}$ |
| c<sub>m</sub> | $5.677 {kcal}\ a^{-1} d^{-1}$ |
| c<sub>f</sub> | $4.330 {kcal}\ a^{-1} d^{-1}$ |
| d<sub>m</sub> | $88.362 {kcal}\ d^{-1}$ |
| d<sub>f</sub> | $447.593 {kcal}\ d^{-1}$ |

:::

The 95% confidence range for men is ±213.0 kcal/day, and ±201.0 kcal/day for women.

The coefficients were again revised by [Mifflin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._D._Mifflin) and St Jeor in 1990:[^o9]

::: {#o10 .table cue=table}

| a<sub>m</sub>=a<sub>f</sub> | $10 {kg}^{-1} {kcal}\ d^{-1}$ |
|---|---|
| b<sub>m</sub>=b<sub>f</sub> | $6.25 {cm}^{-1} {kcal}\ d^{-1}$ |
| c<sub>m</sub>=c<sub>f</sub> | $5 {kcal}\ a^{-1} d^{-1}$ |
| d<sub>m</sub> | $5 {kcal}\ d^{-1}$ |
| d<sub>f</sub> | $-161 {kcal}\ d^{-1}$ |

:::

## History {#History}

The Harris-Benedict equation sprang from a study by [James Arthur Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Arthur_Harris) and [Francis Gano Benedict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Gano_Benedict), which was published in 1919 by the [Carnegie Institution of Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Institution_for_Science) in the [monograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monograph) [A Biometric Study Of Basal Metabolism In Man](https://archive.org/details/biometricstudyof00harruoft). A 1984 [revision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basal_metabolic_rate) improved its accuracy. Mifflin et al. published an equation more predictive for modern lifestyles in 1990.[^o9] Later work produced BMR estimators that accounted for lean body mass.

## Issues in dietary use {#Issues_in_dietary_use}

As the BMR equations do not attempt to take into account body composition, identical results can be calculated for a very muscular person, and an overweight person, who are both the same height, weight, age and gender. As muscle and fat require differing amounts of calories to maintain, the TEE estimates will not be accurate for such cases.

The paper behind the latest update (Mifflin et al) to the BMR formula states all participants in their study fall within the 'normal' and 'overweight' [body mass index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_mass_index) (BMI) categories, and so the results also do not necessarily apply to those in the 'underweight' or 'obese' BMI categories.[^o13]
