# Largest known prime number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Largest known prime number"}

The **largest known prime number** is 2<sup>136,279,841</sup> − 1, a number that has 41,024,320 digits when written in the [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214) system. It was found on October 12, 2024, on a cloud-based [virtual machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_machine) volunteered by Luke Durant, a researcher from San Jose, California, to the [Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search](wikipedia://en/12917) (GIMPS).[^o2][^o3]

:::

*Image: Digits in largest prime found as a function of time.svg.* A plot of the number of digits in the largest known prime by year since the advent of the [electronic computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_computer). The vertical scale is [logarithmic](wikipedia://en/164055).

A [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) is a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) greater than 1 with no [divisors](wikipedia://en/49492) other than 1 and itself. [Euclid's theorem](wikipedia://en/3143591) proves that for any given prime number, there will always be a higher one, and thus there are infinitely many; there is no largest prime.

Many of the largest known primes are [Mersenne primes](wikipedia://en/18908), numbers that are one less than a [power of two](wikipedia://en/376948), because they can be verified by a [specialized primality test](wikipedia://en/325739) that is faster than the general one. , the seven largest known primes are Mersenne primes.[^o4] The last 18 record primes were Mersenne primes.[^o5][^o6] The [binary](wikipedia://en/238686) representation of any Mersenne prime is [composed of all ones](wikipedia://en/215056), since the binary form of 2*<sup>k</sup>* − 1 is simply *k* ones.[^o7]

Finding larger prime numbers is sometimes presented as a means to stronger [encryption](wikipedia://en/10294), but this is not true. While large primes with hundreds of digits are indeed used for [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), primes with millions of digits are not.[^o8][^o9]

## Prizes {#Prizes}

The [Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation) (EFF) offers several prizes for record primes.[^o15] A prime with one million digits was found in 1999, earning the discoverer a \$50,000 prize.[^o14] In 2008, a ten-million-digit prime won a \$100,000 prize and a [Cooperative Computing Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_Computing_Award) from the EFF.[^o15] *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_%28magazine%29)* called this prime the 29th top invention of 2008.[^o16]

Both these primes were discovered through the [Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search](wikipedia://en/12917) (GIMPS), which coordinates long-range search efforts among tens of thousands of computers and thousands of volunteers. The \$50,000 prize went to the discoverer and the \$100,000 prize went to GIMPS. GIMPS will split the \$150,000 prize for the first prime of over 100 million digits with the winning participant. A \$250,000 prize awaits the first prime with one billion digits.[^o15]

GIMPS also offers a \$3,000 research discovery award for participants who discover a new Mersenne prime of fewer than 100 million digits.[^o17]

## History {#History}

*Image: MersennePrimeStamp.gif.* Commemorative postmark used by the [UIUC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana–Champaign) Math Department after proving that M<sub>11213</sub> is prime

The following table lists the progression of the largest known prime number in ascending order.[^o5] Here M<sub>*p*</sub> = 2<sup>*p*</sup> − 1 is the Mersenne number with exponent *p*, where *p* is a prime number. The longest record-holder was M<sub>19</sub> = 524,287, which held the record for 184 years.

The primes up to and including $\tfrac{2^{148}+1}{17}$ were found without a computer, while the primes starting with 180×(M<sub>127</sub>)<sup>2</sup>+1 were found using computers.

[GIMPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GIMPS) volunteers found the 16 latest records, all of them [Mersenne primes](wikipedia://en/18908). They were found on ordinary [personal computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_computer) until the most recent one, found by ex-[Nvidia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nvidia) employee Luke Durant using a network of thousands of dedicated [graphics processing units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphics_processing_unit) (GPUs).[^o2] Durant spent about a year and \$2 million on the hunt.[^o19] This is the first time a Mersenne prime has been discovered using GPUs instead of [central processing units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_processing_unit) (CPUs).[^o20][^o21]
