# Mapping cylinder

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mapping cylinder"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), the **mapping cylinder**[^o2] of a [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f$ between [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$ and $Y$ is the [quotient](wikipedia://en/237213)

:::

$$M_f = (([0,1]\times X) \amalg Y)\,/\,\sim$$

where the $\amalg$ denotes the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/1564380), and ~ is the [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) [generated](wikipedia://en/9259#Generating_equivalence_relations) by

$$(0,x)\sim f(x)\quad\text{for each }x\in X.$$

That is, the mapping cylinder $M_f$ is obtained by gluing one end of $X\times[0,1]$ to $Y$ via the map $f$. Notice that the "top" of the cylinder $\{1\}\times X$ is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to $X$, while the "bottom" is the space $f(X)\subset Y$. It is common to write $Mf$ for $M_f$, and to use the notation $\sqcup_f$ or $\cup_f$ for the mapping cylinder construction. That is, one writes

$$Mf = ([0,1]\times X) \cup_f Y$$

with the subscripted cup symbol denoting the equivalence. The mapping cylinder is commonly used to construct the [mapping cone](wikipedia://en/1707756) $Cf$, obtained by collapsing one end of the cylinder to a point. Mapping cylinders are central to the definition of [cofibrations](wikipedia://en/1697216).

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Basic properties"}

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

The bottom *Y* is a [deformation retract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_retract) of $M_f$. The projection $M_f \to Y$ splits (via $Y \ni y \mapsto y \in Y \subset M_f$), and the deformation retraction $R$ is given by:

$$R: M_f \times I \rightarrow M_f$$

$$([t,x],s) \mapsto [s\cdot t,x], (y, s) \mapsto y$$

(where points in $Y$ stay fixed because $[0,x]=[s\cdot 0,x]$ for all $s$).

The map $f:X \to Y$ is a [homotopy equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_equivalence) if and only if the "top" $\{1\}\times X$ is a strong deformation retract of $M_f$.[^o5] An explicit formula for the strong deformation retraction can be worked out.[^o6]

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

### Mapping cylinder of a fiber bundle {#Mapping_cylinder_of_a_fiber_bundle}

For a [fiber bundle](wikipedia://en/276526) $\pi:P \to X$ with fiber $F$, the mapping cylinder

$$M_\pi = (([0,1]\times P) \coprod X)/\sim$$

has the equivalence relation

$$(0,p_{x}) \sim (0, q_{x})$$

for $p_{x},q_{x} \in F_x$. Then, there is a canonical map sending a point $[i, p_{x}, x] \in M_\pi$ to the point $x \in X$, giving a fiber bundle

$$p:M_\pi \to X$$

whose fiber is the cone $CF_x$. To see this, notice the fiber over a point $x\in X$ is the quotient space

$$[0,1]\times F_x \coprod \{x\}/\sim$$

where every point in $\{0\}\times F_x$ is equivalent.

:::

## Interpretation {#Interpretation}

The mapping cylinder may be viewed as a way to replace an arbitrary map by an equivalent [cofibration](wikipedia://en/1697216), in the following sense:

Given a map $f\colon X \to Y$, the mapping cylinder is a space $M_f$, *together with* a cofibration $\tilde f\colon X \to M_f$ and a [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) [homotopy equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_equivalence) $M_f \to Y$ (indeed, *Y* is a [deformation retract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_retract) of $M_f$), such that the [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) $X \to M_f \to Y$ equals *f*.

*Image: Mapping cylinder.png.*

Thus the space *Y* gets replaced with a homotopy equivalent space $M_f$, and the map *f* with a lifted map $\tilde f$. Equivalently, the diagram

$$f\colon X \to Y$$

gets replaced with a diagram

$$\tilde f\colon X \to M_f$$

together with a homotopy equivalence between them.

The construction serves to replace any map of topological spaces by a homotopy equivalent cofibration.

Note that pointwise, a [cofibration](wikipedia://en/1697216) is a closed [inclusion](wikipedia://en/45196).

## Applications {#Applications}

Mapping cylinders are quite common homotopical tools. One use of mapping cylinders is to apply theorems concerning inclusions of spaces to general maps, which might not be [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective).

Consequently, theorems or techniques (such as [homology](wikipedia://en/142432), [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060) or [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831)) which are only dependent on the homotopy class of spaces and maps involved may be applied to $f\colon X\rightarrow Y$ with the assumption that $X \subset Y$ and that $f$ is actually the inclusion of a [subspace](wikipedia://en/910926).

Another, more intuitive appeal of the construction is that it accords with the usual mental image of a function as "sending" points of $X$ to points of $Y,$ and hence of embedding $X$ within $Y,$ despite the fact that the function need not be one-to-one.

### Categorical application and interpretation {#Categorical_application_and_interpretation}

One can use the mapping cylinder to construct [homotopy colimits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_colimit): this follows from the general statement that any [category](wikipedia://en/53759) with all [pushouts](wikipedia://en/582024) and [coequalizers](wikipedia://en/431940) has all [colimits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colimit_%28category_theory%29). That is, given a diagram, replace the maps by cofibrations (using the mapping cylinder) and then take the ordinary pointwise limit (one must take a bit more care, but mapping cylinders are a component).

Conversely, the mapping cylinder is the [homotopy pushout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_pushout) of the diagram where $f\colon X \to Y$ and $\text{id}_X\colon X \to X$.

::: {#o15 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Mapping telescope"}

### Mapping telescope {#Mapping_telescope}

Given a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of maps

$$X_1 \xrightarrow{f_1} X_2 \xrightarrow{f_2} X_3 \to\cdots$$

the mapping telescope is the homotopical [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566). If the maps are all already cofibrations (such as for the [orthogonal groups](wikipedia://en/173954) $O(n) \subset O(n+1)$), then the direct limit is the union, but in general one must use the mapping telescope. The mapping telescope is a sequence of mapping cylinders, joined end-to-end. The picture of the construction looks like a stack of increasingly large cylinders, like a telescope.

Formally, one defines it as

$$\Bigl(\coprod_i [0,1] \times X_i\Bigr) / ((0,x_i) \sim (1,f_i(x_i))).$$
