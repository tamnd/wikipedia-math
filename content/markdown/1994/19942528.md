# Systolic freedom

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Systolic freedom"}

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), **systolic freedom** refers to the fact that closed [Riemannian manifolds](wikipedia://en/144652) may have arbitrarily small [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) regardless of their [systolic](wikipedia://en/2545455) invariants. That is, systolic invariants or products of systolic invariants do not in general provide universal (i.e. curvature-free) lower bounds for the total volume of a closed Riemannian manifold.

:::

Systolic freedom was first detected by [Mikhail Gromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gromov_%28mathematician%29) in an [I.H.É.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institut_des_Hautes_Études_Scientifiques) preprint in 1992 (which eventually appeared as (Gromov 1996)), and was further developed by [Mikhail Katz](wikipedia://en/25830095), [Michael Freedman](wikipedia://en/658970) and others. Gromov's observation was elaborated on by (txt). One of the first publications to study systolic freedom in detail is by (Katz 1995).

Systolic freedom has applications in [quantum error correction](wikipedia://en/892803). (Croke Katz 2003) survey the main results on systolic freedom.

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

The [complex projective plane](wikipedia://en/737155) admits Riemannian metrics of arbitrarily small volume, such that every essential surface is of area at least 1. Here a surface is called "essential" if it cannot be contracted to a point in the ambient 4-manifold.

:::

## Systolic constraint {#Systolic_constraint}

The opposite of systolic freedom is systolic constraint, characterized by the presence of systolic inequalities such as [Gromov's systolic inequality for essential manifolds](wikipedia://en/12066797).
