# Sparse polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sparse polynomial"}

In mathematics, a **sparse polynomial** (also **lacunary polynomial** or **fewnomial**) is a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) that has far fewer terms than its [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) and number of [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109) would suggest. For example, $x^{10} + 3x^3 + 1$ is a sparse polynomial, as it is a [trinomial](wikipedia://en/2647057) with a [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) of $10$.

:::

The motivation for studying sparse polynomials is to concentrate on the structure of a polynomial's monomials instead of its degree, as one can see, for instance, by comparing [Bernstein–Kushnirenko theorem](wikipedia://en/45428442) with [Bézout's theorem](wikipedia://en/149217). Research on sparse polynomials has also included work on [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) whose running time grows as a function of the number of terms rather than on the degree, for problems including [polynomial multiplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_multiplication), [division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_division), [root-finding algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root-finding_algorithms), and [polynomial greatest common divisors](wikipedia://en/9927028). Sparse polynomials have also been used in pure mathematics, especially in the study of [Galois groups](wikipedia://en/12673), because it has been easier to determine the Galois groups of certain families of sparse polynomials than it is for other polynomials.

The [algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/248808) determined by sparse polynomials have a simple structure, which is also reflected in the structure of the solutions of certain related [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309). Additionally, a sparse [positivstellensatz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positivstellensatz) exists for univariate sparse polynomials. It states that the non-negativity of a polynomial can be certified by [SOS polynomials](wikipedia://en/17001761) whose degree only depends on the number of monomials of the polynomial.[^o2]

Sparse polynomials often come up in sum or difference of powers equations. The [sum of two cubes](wikipedia://en/72077175) states that $(x + y)(x^2 - xy + y^2) = x^3 + y^3$. Here $x^3 + y^3$ is a sparse polynomial, since out of the $16$ possible terms, only $2$ appear. Other examples include the identities $(x - y) \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} x^k y^{N-1-k} = x^N - y^N$ and also $(x + y) \sum_{k=0}^{2n}(-1)^k x^k y^{2n-k} = x^{2n+1} + y^{2n+1},$ where the product of two polynomials give a sparse polynomial. The [Bring–Jerrard normal form](wikipedia://en/3191883#Bring–Jerrard_normal_form) of a quintic, $x^5 + px + q,$ is also a sparse polynomial.
