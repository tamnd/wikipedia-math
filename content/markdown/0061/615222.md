# Multivariable calculus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Multivariable calculus"}

**Multivariable calculus** (also known as **multivariate calculus**) is the extension of [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) in one [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109) to [functions of several variables](wikipedia://en/39783039): the [differentiation](wikipedia://en/50416) and [integration](wikipedia://en/15532) of functions involving multiple variables (*[multivariate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_%28mathematics%29)*), rather than just one.[^o2]

:::

Multivariable calculus may be thought of as an elementary part of [calculus on Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/64965378). The special case of calculus in three dimensional space is often called *[vector calculus](wikipedia://en/32640)*.

## Introduction {#Introduction}

In single-variable calculus, operations like differentiation and integration are made to functions of a single variable. In multivariate calculus, it is required to generalize these to multiple variables, and the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) is therefore multi-dimensional. Care is therefore required in these generalizations, because of two key differences between 1D and higher dimensional spaces:

1. There are infinite ways to approach a single point in higher dimensions, as opposed to two (from the positive and negative direction) in 1D;
1. There are multiple extended objects associated with the dimension; for example, a 1D function is represented as a curve on the 2D [Cartesian plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_plane), but a [scalar](wikipedia://en/3588331)-valued function of two variables is a surface in 3D, while curves can also live in 3D space.

The consequence of the first difference is the difference in the definition of the limits and continuity. Directional [limits](wikipedia://en/285759) and [derivatives](wikipedia://en/487859) define the limit and differential along a 1D parametrized curve, reducing the problem to the 1D case. Further higher-dimensional objects can be constructed from these operators.

The consequence of the second difference is the existence of multiple types of integration, including [line integrals](wikipedia://en/21245414), [surface integrals](wikipedia://en/528867) and [volume integrals](wikipedia://en/1247265). Due to the non-uniqueness of these integrals, an [antiderivative](wikipedia://en/2823) or [indefinite integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_integral) cannot be properly defined.

## Limits {#Limits}

A study of [limits](wikipedia://en/285759) and [continuity](wikipedia://en/6122) in multivariable calculus yields many counterintuitive results not demonstrated by single-variable functions.

A limit along a path may be defined by considering a parametrised path $s(t): \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ in n-dimensional Euclidean space. Any function $f(\overrightarrow{x}): \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ can then be projected on the path as a 1D function $f(s(t))$. The limit of $f$ to the point $s(t_0)$ along the path $s(t)$ can hence be defined as

::: {#o5 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\lim_{\overrightarrow{x} \to s(t_0)} f(\overrightarrow{x}) = \lim_{t \to t_0} f(s(t)) \tag{1}$$

:::

Note that the value of this limit can be dependent on the form of $s(t)$, i.e. the path chosen, not just the point which the limit approaches.[^o2] For example, consider the function

$$f(x,y) = \frac{x^2y}{x^4+y^2}.$$

If the point $(0,0)$ is approached through the line $y=kx$, or in parametric form:

*Image: ((x^2)(y))⁄((x^4)+(y^2)).png.* Plot of the function $f(x, y) = (x^{2}y)/(x^{4} + y^{2})$

::: {#o6 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$x(t) = t,\, y(t) = kt \tag{2}$$

:::

Then the limit along the path will be:

::: {#o7 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$\lim_{t \to 0} f(x(t),y(t)) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{k t^3}{t^4 + k^2 t^2} = 0 \tag{3}$$

:::

On the other hand, if the path $y=\pm x^2$ (or parametrically, $x(t)=t,\, y(t)=\pm t^2$) is chosen, then the limit becomes:

::: {#o8 .equation cue=numblk name="4"}

$$\lim_{t \to 0} f(x(t),y(t)) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{\pm t^4}{t^4 + t^4} = \pm \frac{1}{2} \tag{4}$$

:::

Since taking different paths towards the same point yields different values, a general limit at the point $(0,0)$ cannot be defined for the function.

A general limit can be defined if the limits to a point along all possible paths converge to the same value, i.e. we say for a function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ that the limit of $f$ to some point $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is L, if and only if

::: {#o9 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$$\lim_{t \to t_0} f(s(t)) = L \tag{5}$$

:::

for all continuous functions $s(t): \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ such that $s(t_0)=x_0$.

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Continuity"}

### Continuity {#Continuity}

From the concept of limit along a path, we can then derive the definition for multivariate continuity in the same manner, that is: we say for a function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ that $f$ is continuous at the point $x_0$, if and only if

::: {#o12 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$$\lim_{t \to t_0} f(s(t)) = f(x_0) \tag{5}$$

:::

for all continuous functions $s(t): \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ such that $s(t_0)=x_0$.

As with limits, being continuous along *one* path $s(t)$ does not imply multivariate continuity.

Continuity in each argument not being sufficient for multivariate continuity can also be seen from the following example.[^o2] For example, for a real-valued function $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ with two real-valued parameters, $f(x,y)$, continuity of $f$ in $x$ for fixed $y$ and continuity of $f$ in $y$ for fixed $x$ does not imply continuity of $f$.

Consider

$$f(x,y)= \begin{cases} \frac{y}{x}-y & \text{if}\quad 0 \leq y < x \leq 1 \\ \frac{x}{y}-x & \text{if}\quad 0 \leq x < y \leq 1 \\ 1-x & \text{if}\quad 0 < x=y \\ 0 & \text{everywhere else}. \end{cases}$$

It is easy to verify that this function is zero by definition on the boundary and outside of the quadrangle $(0,1)\times (0,1)$. Furthermore, the functions defined for constant $x$ and $y$ and $0 \le a \le 1$ by

$g_a(x) = f(x,a)\quad$ and $\quad h_a(y) = f(a,y)\quad$

are continuous. Specifically,

$g_0(x) = f(x,0) = h_0(0,y) = f(0,y) = 0$ for all $x$ and $y$. Therefore, $f(0,0)=0$ and moreover, along the coordinate axes, $\lim_{x \to 0} f(x,0) = 0$ and $\lim_{y \to 0} f(0,y) = 0$. Therefore the function is continuous along both individual arguments.

However, consider the parametric path $x(t) = t,\, y(t) = t$. The parametric function becomes

::: {#o13 .equation cue=numblk name="6"}

$$f(x(t),y(t))= \begin{cases} 1-t & \text{if}\quad t > 0 \\ 0 & \text{everywhere else}. \end{cases} \tag{6}$$

:::

Therefore,

::: {#o14 .equation cue=numblk name="7"}

$$\lim_{t \to 0^+} f(x(t),y(t)) = 1 \neq f(0,0) = 0 \tag{7}$$

:::

It is hence clear that the function is not multivariate continuous, despite being continuous in both coordinates.

:::

::: {#o16 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorems regarding multivariate limits and continuity"}

### Theorems regarding multivariate limits and continuity {#Theorems_regarding_multivariate_limits_and_continuity}

- All properties of linearity and superposition from single-variable calculus carry over to multivariate calculus.
- **Composition**: If $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ and $g: \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^p$ are both multivariate continuous functions at the points $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $f(x_0) \in \mathbb{R}^m$ respectively, then $g \circ f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^p$ is also a multivariate continuous function at the point $x_0$.
- **Multiplication**: If $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ and $g: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ are both continuous functions at the point $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$, then $fg: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous at $x_0$, and $f/g : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is also continuous at $x_0$ provided that $g(x_0) \neq 0$.
- If $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is a continuous function at point $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$, then $|f|$ is also continuous at the same point.
- If $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is [Lipschitz continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipschitz_continuous) (with the appropriate normed spaces as needed) in the neighbourhood of the point $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$, then $f$ is multivariate continuous at $x_0$.

::: {#o17 .proof cue=collapse name="Proof"}

From the Lipschitz continuity condition for $f$ we have

::: {#o18 .equation cue=numblk name="8"}

$$|f(s(t))-f(s(t_0))| \leq K|s(t)-s(t_0)| \tag{8}$$

:::

where $K$ is the Lipschitz constant. Note also that, as $s(t)$ is continuous at $t_0$, for every $\delta > 0$ there exists a $\epsilon > 0$ such that $|s(t)-s(t_0)| < \delta$ $\forall |t-t_0| < \epsilon$.

Hence, for every $\alpha > 0$, choose $\delta = \frac{\alpha}{K}$; there exists an $\epsilon > 0$ such that for all $t$ satisfying $|t-t_0| < \epsilon$, $|s(t)-s(t_0)| < \delta$, and $|f(s(t)) - f(s(t_0))| \leq K|s(t)-s(t_0)| < K\delta = \alpha$. Hence $\lim_{t \to t_0} f(s(t))$ converges to $f(s(t_0))$ regardless of the precise form of $s(t)$.

:::

:::

## Differentiation {#Differentiation}

### Directional derivative {#Directional_derivative}

The derivative of a single-variable function is defined as

::: {#o21 .equation cue=numblk name="9"}

$$\frac{df}{dx} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \tag{9}$$

:::

Using the extension of limits discussed above, one can then extend the definition of the derivative to a scalar-valued function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ along some path $s(t): \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n$:

::: {#o22 .equation cue=numblk name="10"}

$$\left . \frac{df}{dx} \right |_{s(t),t=t_0} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(s(t_0+h))-f(s(t_0))}{|s(t_0+h)-s(t_0)|} \tag{10}$$

:::

Unlike limits, for which the value depends on the exact form of the path $s(t)$, it can be shown that the derivative along the path depends only on the tangent vector of the path at $s(t_0)$, i.e. $s'(t_0)$, provided that $f$ is [Lipschitz continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipschitz_continuous) at $s(t_0)$, and that the limit exists for at least one such path.

::: {#o23 .proof cue=collapse name="Proof"}

For $s(t)$ continuous up to the first derivative (this statement is well defined as $s$ is a function of one variable), we can write the [Taylor expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_expansion) of $s$ around $t_0$ using [Taylor's theorem](wikipedia://en/51714) to construct the remainder:

::: {#o24 .equation cue=numblk name="11"}

$$s(t) = s(t_0) + s'(\tau) (t-t_0) \tag{11}$$

:::

where $\tau \in [t_0,t]$.

Substituting this into (10),

::: {#o25 .equation cue=numblk name="12"}

$$\left . \frac{df}{dx} \right |_{s(t),t=t_0} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(s(t_0)+s'(\tau)h)-f(s(t_0))}{|s'(\tau)h|} \tag{12}$$

:::

where $\tau(h) \in [t_0,t_0+h]$.

Lipschitz continuity gives us $|f(x)-f(y)| \leq K|x-y|$ for some finite $K$, $\forall x,y\in \mathbb{R}^n$. It follows that $|f(x+O(h))-f(x)| \sim O(h)$.

Note also that given the continuity of $s'(t)$, $s'(\tau) = s'(t_0)+O(h)$ as $h \to 0$.

Substituting these two conditions into (12),

::: {#o26 .equation cue=numblk name="13"}

$$\left . \frac{df}{dx} \right |_{s(t),t=t_0} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(s(t_0)+s'(t_0)h)-f(s(t_0))+O(h^2)}{|s'(t_0)h|+O(h^2)} \tag{13}$$

:::

whose limit depends only on $s'(t_0)$ as the dominant term.

:::

It is therefore possible to generalize the definition of the directional derivative as follows: The directional derivative of a scalar-valued function $f:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ along the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{u}}$ at some point $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is

::: {#o27 .equation cue=numblk name="14"}

$$\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{u}}} f(x_0) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f(x_0+\hat{\mathbf{u}} t) - f(x_0)}{t} \tag{14}$$

:::

or, when expressed in terms of ordinary differentiation,

::: {#o28 .equation cue=numblk name="15"}

$$\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{u}}} f(x_0) = \left . \frac{df(x_0+\hat{\mathbf{u}}t)}{dt} \right |_{t=0} \tag{15}$$

:::

which is a well defined expression because $f(x_0+\hat{\mathbf{u}}t)$ is a scalar function with one variable in $t$.

It is not possible to define a unique scalar derivative without a direction; it is clear for example that $\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{u}}}f(x_0) = - \nabla_{-\hat{\mathbf{u}}}f(x_0)$. It is also possible for directional derivatives to exist for some directions but not for others.

### Partial derivative {#Partial_derivative}

The partial derivative generalizes the notion of the derivative to higher dimensions. A partial derivative of a multivariable function is a [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) with respect to one variable with all other variables held constant.[^o2]

A partial derivative may be thought of as the directional derivative of the function along a coordinate axis.

Partial derivatives may be combined in interesting ways to create more complicated expressions of the derivative. In [vector calculus](wikipedia://en/32640), the [del](wikipedia://en/151925) operator ($\nabla$) is used to define the concepts of [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461), [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328), and [curl](wikipedia://en/6123) in terms of partial derivatives. A matrix of partial derivatives, the **[Jacobian](wikipedia://en/195351)** matrix, may be used to represent the derivative of a function between two spaces of arbitrary dimension. The derivative can thus be understood as a [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) which directly varies from point to point in the domain of the function.

[Differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations) containing partial derivatives are called [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations) or PDEs. These equations are generally more difficult to solve than [ordinary differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_differential_equations), which contain derivatives with respect to only one variable.[^o2]

## Multiple integration {#Multiple_integration}

The multiple integral extends the concept of the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) to functions of any number of variables. Double and triple integrals may be used to calculate areas and volumes of regions in the plane and in space. [Fubini's theorem](wikipedia://en/255245) guarantees that a multiple integral may be evaluated as a *repeated integral* or *iterated integral* as long as the integrand is continuous throughout the domain of integration.[^o2]

The [surface integral](wikipedia://en/528867) and the [line integral](wikipedia://en/21245414) are used to integrate over curved [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) such as [surfaces](wikipedia://en/4342970) and [curves](wikipedia://en/89246).

::: {#o32 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Fundamental theorem of calculus in multiple dimensions"}

### Fundamental theorem of calculus in multiple dimensions {#Fundamental_theorem_of_calculus_in_multiple_dimensions}

In single-variable calculus, the [fundamental theorem of calculus](wikipedia://en/19468696) establishes a link between the derivative and the integral. The link between the derivative and the integral in multivariable calculus is embodied by the integral theorems of vector calculus:[^o2]

- [Gradient theorem](wikipedia://en/5337437)
- [Stokes' theorem](wikipedia://en/25441497#Special_cases)
- [Divergence theorem](wikipedia://en/151864)
- [Green's theorem](wikipedia://en/243334).

In a more advanced study of multivariable calculus, it is seen that these four theorems are specific incarnations of a more general theorem, the [generalized Stokes' theorem](wikipedia://en/29040), which applies to the integration of [differential forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_forms) over [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2119219).[^o33]

:::

## Applications and uses {#Applications_and_uses}

Techniques of multivariable calculus are used to study many objects of interest in the material world. In particular,

::: {#o35 .table cue=table}

|  |  | Type of functions | Applicable techniques |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Curves](wikipedia://en/89246) | *Image: Osculating circle.svg.* | $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ for $n > 1$ | Lengths of curves, [line integrals](wikipedia://en/21245414), and [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770). |
| [Surfaces](wikipedia://en/4342970) | *Image: Helicoid.svg.* | $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^n$ for $n > 2$ | [Areas](wikipedia://en/1209) of surfaces, [surface integrals](wikipedia://en/528867), [flux](wikipedia://en/43590) through surfaces, and curvature. |
| [Scalar fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_fields) | *Image: Surface-plot.png.* | $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ | Maxima and minima, [Lagrange multipliers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange_multipliers), [directional derivatives](wikipedia://en/487859), [level sets](wikipedia://en/559622). |
| [Vector fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_fields) | *Image: Vector field.svg.* | $f: \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^n$ | Any of the operations of [vector calculus](wikipedia://en/32640) including [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461), [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328), and [curl](wikipedia://en/6123). |

:::

Multivariable calculus can be applied to analyze [deterministic systems](wikipedia://en/522958) that have multiple [degrees of freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_of_freedom_%28physics_and_chemistry%29). Functions with [independent variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_variable) corresponding to each of the degrees of freedom are often used to model these systems, and multivariable calculus provides tools for characterizing the [system dynamics](wikipedia://en/153208).

Multivariate calculus is used in the [optimal control](wikipedia://en/362565) of [continuous time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_time) [dynamic systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_systems). It is used in [regression analysis](wikipedia://en/826997) to derive formulas for estimating relationships among various sets of [empirical data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_data).

Multivariable calculus is used in many fields of [natural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science) and [social science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science) and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) to model and study high-dimensional systems that exhibit deterministic behavior. In [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), for example, [consumer choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_choice) over a variety of goods, and [producer choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profit_maximization) over various inputs to use and outputs to produce, are modeled with multivariate calculus.

Non-deterministic, or [stochastic](wikipedia://en/47895) systems can be studied using a different kind of mathematics, such as [stochastic calculus](wikipedia://en/228001).
