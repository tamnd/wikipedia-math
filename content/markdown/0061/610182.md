# Richard Montague

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Richard Montague"}

**Richard Merritt Montague** (September 20, 1930 – March 7, 1971) was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher) who made contributions to [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636) and the [philosophy of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_language). He is known for proposing [Montague grammar](wikipedia://en/709308) to formalize the semantics of [natural language](wikipedia://en/21173). As a student of [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920), he also contributed early developments to [axiomatic set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) ([ZFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZFC)). For the latter half of his life, he was a professor at the [University of California, Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles) until his early death, believed to be a [homicide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homicide), at age 40.

:::

## Career {#Career}

At the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley), Montague earned a BA in philosophy in 1950, an MA in mathematics in 1953, and a [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD) in Philosophy in 1957, the latter under the direction of the mathematician and logician [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920). Montague spent his entire career teaching in the [UCLA Department of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCLA_Department_of_Philosophy), where he supervised the dissertations of [Nino Cocchiarella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nino_Cocchiarella) and [Hans Kamp](wikipedia://en/17054475).

Montague wrote on the foundations of [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) and [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), as would befit a student of Tarski. His PhD dissertation, titled *Contributions to the Axiomatic Foundations of Set Theory*,[^o4] contained the first proof that all possible axiomatizations of the standard [axiomatic set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) [ZFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZFC) must contain infinitely many axioms. In other words, ZFC cannot be finitely axiomatized.

He pioneered a logical approach to natural language [semantics](wikipedia://en/29107) that became known as [Montague grammar](wikipedia://en/709308). This approach to language has been especially influential among certain [computational linguists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_linguist)—perhaps more so than among more traditional [philosophers of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_Language). In particular, Montague's influence lives on in grammar approaches like [categorial grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorial_grammar) (such as Unification Categorial Grammar, Left-Associative Grammar, or [Combinatory Categorial Grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatory_categorial_grammar)), which attempt a derivation of syntactic and semantic representation in tandem and the semantics of quantifiers, scope and discourse ([Hans Kamp](wikipedia://en/17054475), a student of Montague's, co-developed [Discourse Representation Theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourse_Representation_Theory)).

Montague was an accomplished organist and a successful real estate investor. He died violently in his own home; the crime is unsolved to this day. [Anita Feferman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anita_Feferman) and [Solomon Feferman](wikipedia://en/355401) argue that he usually went to bars "cruising" and bringing people home with him.[^o5] On the day that he was murdered, he brought home several people "for some kind of soirée", but they strangled him.[^o5]

## In popular culture {#In_popular_culture}

Three novels have been inspired by the life and death of Richard M. Montague:

- *[The Mad Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mad_Man)* by American science fiction author [Samuel R. Delany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_R._Delany) (1994)
- *Less Than Meets the Eye* by American philosopher [David Berlinski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Berlinski) (1994)
- *The Semantics of Murder* by Irish writer [Aifric Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aifric_Campbell) (2008)
