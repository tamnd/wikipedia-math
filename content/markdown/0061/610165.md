# Cusp form

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cusp form"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), a **cusp form** is a particular kind of [modular form](wikipedia://en/286000) with zero constant coefficient in its [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) [expansion](wikipedia://en/1575813).

:::

## Introduction {#Introduction}

A cusp form is distinguished in the case of modular forms for the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019) by the vanishing of the constant coefficient *a*<sub>0</sub> in the [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) expansion (see [*q*-expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q-expansion))

$$\sum a_n q^n.$$

This Fourier expansion exists as a consequence of the presence in the modular group's action on the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826) via the transformation

$$z\mapsto z+1.$$

For other groups, there may be some translation through several units, in which case the Fourier expansion is in terms of a different parameter. In all cases, though, the limit as *q* → 0 is the limit in the upper half-plane as the [imaginary part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_part) of *z* → ∞. Taking the quotient by the modular group, this limit corresponds to a [cusp](wikipedia://en/6851758) of a [modular curve](wikipedia://en/669552) (in the sense of a point added for [compactification](wikipedia://en/6617)). So, the definition amounts to saying that a cusp form is a modular form that vanishes at a cusp. In the case of other groups, there may be several cusps, and the definition becomes a modular form vanishing at *all* cusps. This may involve several expansions.

## Dimension {#Dimension}

The dimensions of spaces of cusp forms are, in principle, computable via the [Riemann–Roch theorem](wikipedia://en/247261). For example, the [Ramanujan tau function](wikipedia://en/1231708) *τ*(*n*) arises as the sequence of Fourier coefficients of the cusp form of weight 12 for the modular group, with *a*<sub>1</sub> = 1. The space of such forms has dimension 1, which means this definition is possible; and that accounts for the action of [Hecke operators](wikipedia://en/521837) on the space being by [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373) (Mordell's proof of Ramanujan's identities). Explicitly it is the **modular discriminant**

$$\Delta(z,q),$$

which represents (up to a [normalizing constant](wikipedia://en/398189)) the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607) of the cubic on the right side of the [Weierstrass equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_equation) of an [elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/10225); and the 24-th power of the [Dedekind eta function](wikipedia://en/447020). The Fourier coefficients here are written

$$\tau(n)$$

and called '[Ramanujan's tau function](wikipedia://en/1231708)', with the normalization *τ*(1) = 1.

## Petersson inner product {#Petersson_inner_product}

The fact that cusp forms decay at the cusps, rather than merely being holomorphic there as the case with more general [modular forms](wikipedia://en/286000), means that there is an invariant inner product on the cusp forms of weight $k$, given by

$$\langle f,g\rangle_k = \int_{\Gamma\setminus\mathcal H} f(z)\overline{g(z)}y^k\frac{dx\,dy}{y^2}.$$

Cuspidality is what is required to make this integral finite.

More generally, the integral remains finite if $f$ is a cusp form and $g$ is a modular form, both of weight $k$. With respect to this inner product, there is an orthogonal decomposition

$$M_k(\Gamma) = E_k(\Gamma) \oplus S_k(\Gamma)$$

where $M_k(\Gamma)$ is the space of modular forms, $S_k(\Gamma)$ the space of cusp forms, and $E_k(\Gamma)$ is the space of [Eisenstein series](wikipedia://en/1270458), all of weight $k$.

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

In the larger picture of [automorphic forms](wikipedia://en/782146), the cusp forms are complementary to [Eisenstein series](wikipedia://en/1270458), in a *discrete spectrum*/*continuous spectrum*, or *discrete series representation*/*induced representation* distinction typical in different parts of [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713). That is, Eisenstein series can be 'designed' to take on given values at cusps. There is a large general theory, depending though on the quite intricate theory of parabolic subgroups, and corresponding [cuspidal representations](wikipedia://en/6043458).

Consider $P=MU$ a standard parabolic subgroup of some reductive group $G$ (over $\mathbb{A}$, the [adele ring](wikipedia://en/247978)), an automorphic form $\phi$ on $U(\mathbb{A})M(k)\backslash G$ is called cuspidal if for all parabolic subgroups $P'$ such that $P_0\subset P'\subsetneq P$ we have $\phi_{P'}=0$, where $P_0$ is the standard minimal parabolic subgroup. The notation $\phi_{P}$ for $P=MU$ is defined as $\phi_P (g) =\int_{U(k)\backslash U(\mathbb{A})} \phi(ug) du$.
