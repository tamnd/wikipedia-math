# Richard Rado

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Richard Rado"}

**Richard Rado** [FRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society)[^o3] (28 April 1906 – 23 December 1989) was a German-born British mathematician whose research concerned [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) and [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401). He was Jewish and left Germany to escape [Nazi persecution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_persecution_of_the_Catholic_Church_in_Germany).[^o4] He earned two PhDs: in 1933 from the [University of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Berlin), and in 1935 from the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge).[^o7] He was interviewed in Berlin by [Lord Cherwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Cherwell) for a scholarship given by the chemist Sir [Robert Mond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Mond) which provided financial support to study at [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge). After he was awarded the scholarship, Rado and his wife left for the UK in 1933. He was appointed Professor of Mathematics at the [University of Reading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Reading) in 1954 and remained there until he retired in 1971.

:::

## Contributions {#Contributions}

Rado made contributions in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) and [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401) including 18 papers with [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284).

In graph theory, the [Rado graph](wikipedia://en/7785594), a countably infinite graph containing all countably infinite graphs as induced subgraphs, is named after Rado. He rediscovered it in 1964 after previous works on the same graph by [Wilhelm Ackermann](wikipedia://en/62727), Erdős, and [Alfréd Rényi](wikipedia://en/614988).

In [combinatorial set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_set_theory), the [Erdős–Rado theorem](wikipedia://en/22844355) extends [Ramsey's theorem](wikipedia://en/184898) to infinite sets. It was published by Erdős and Rado in 1956. [Rado's theorem](wikipedia://en/1706360) is another Ramsey-theoretic result concerning systems of linear equations, proved by Rado in his thesis. The [Milner–Rado paradox](wikipedia://en/25392586), also in set theory, states the existence of a partition of an [ordinal](wikipedia://en/26547932) into subsets of small order-type; it was published by Rado and E. C. Milner in 1965.

The [Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem](wikipedia://en/296188) can be described either in terms of set systems or [hypergraphs](wikipedia://en/232535). It gives an upper bound on the number of sets in a family of finite sets, all the same size, that all intersect each other. Rado published it with Erdős and [Chao Ko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chao_Ko) in 1961, but according to Erdős it was originally formulated in 1938.

In [matroid](wikipedia://en/244321) theory, Rado proved a fundamental result of [transversal theory](wikipedia://en/897733) by generalizing the [Marriage Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marriage_Theorem) for [matchings](wikipedia://en/581797) between sets *S* and *X* to the case where *X* has a matroid structure and matchings must match to an independent set in the matroid on *X*.[^o9][^o10]

The Klarner–Rado Sequence is named after Rado and [David A. Klarner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_A._Klarner).[^o11]

## Awards and honours {#Awards_and_honours}

In 1972, Rado was awarded the [Senior Berwick Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berwick_Prizes).
