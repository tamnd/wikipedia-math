# Sinc function

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Sinc function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), the **sinc function** (), denoted by $\operatorname{sinc}(x)$, is defined as either

$$\operatorname{sinc}(x) = \frac{\sin x}{x}$$

or

$$\operatorname{sinc}(x) = \frac{\sin \pi x}{\pi x},$$

:::

the latter of which is sometimes referred to as the **normalized sinc function**. The only difference between the two definitions is in the scaling of the [independent variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_variable) (the [$x$ axis](wikipedia://en/7706)) by a factor of $\pi$. In both cases, the value of the function at the [removable singularity](wikipedia://en/81644) at zero is understood to be the limit value 1. The sinc function is then [analytic](wikipedia://en/61478) everywhere and hence an [entire function](wikipedia://en/10372).

The normalized sinc function is the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of the [rectangular function](wikipedia://en/1261615) with no scaling. It is used in the concept of [reconstructing](wikipedia://en/105499) a continuous bandlimited signal from uniformly spaced [samples](wikipedia://en/37864) of that signal. The [sinc filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinc_filter) is used in signal processing.

The function itself was first mathematically derived in this form by [Lord Rayleigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Rayleigh) in his expression ([Rayleigh's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessel_functions#Rayleigh's_formulas)) for the zeroth-order spherical [Bessel function](wikipedia://en/4700) of the first kind.

The *sinc* function is also called the **cardinal sine** function.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

*Image: Sinc.wav.* The sinc function as audio, at 2000 Hz (±1.5 seconds around zero)

The sinc function has two forms, normalized and unnormalized.[^o5]

In mathematics, the historical **unnormalized sinc function** is defined for $x \ne 0$ by

$$\operatorname{sinc}(x) = \frac{\sin x}{x}.$$

Alternatively, the unnormalized sinc function is often called the [sampling function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampling_function), indicated as Sa(*x*).[^o6]

In [digital signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_processing) and [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773), the **normalized sinc function** is commonly defined for $x \ne 0$ by

$$\operatorname{sinc}(x) = \frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x}.$$

In either case, the value at $x = 0$ is defined to be the limiting value

$$\operatorname{sinc}(0) := \lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin(a x)}{a x} = 1$$

for all real $a \ne 0$ (the limit can be proven using the [squeeze theorem](wikipedia://en/330994#Second_example)).

The [normalization](wikipedia://en/398189) causes the [definite integral](wikipedia://en/15532) of the function over the real numbers to equal 1 (whereas the same integral of the unnormalized sinc function has a value of [$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601)). As a further useful property, the zeros of the normalized sinc function are the nonzero integer values of $x$.

:::

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The function has also been called the **cardinal sine** or **sine [cardinal](wikipedia://en/14189688)** [**function**](wikipedia://en/14189688)[^o8][^o9], since it returns 1 (a cardinal number) for $x = 0$ and 0 (another cardinal number) for every other integer.[^o10] The term "sinc" is a contraction of the function's full Latin name, the sinus cardinalis[^o12] and was introduced by [Philip M. Woodward](wikipedia://en/9986270) and I.L Davies in their 1952 article "Information theory and [inverse probability](wikipedia://en/908703) in telecommunication", saying "This function occurs so often in Fourier analysis and its applications that it does seem to merit some notation of its own".[^o11] It is also used in Woodward's 1953 book *Probability and Information Theory, with Applications to Radar*.[^o12][^o13]

## Properties {#Properties}

*Image: Si cos.svg.* The local extrema (small white dots) of the unnormalized, red sinc function correspond to its intersections with the blue cosine function.

The [zero crossings](wikipedia://en/567723) of the unnormalized sinc are at non-zero integer multiples of $\pi$, while zero crossings of the normalized sinc occur at non-zero integers.

The [local extrema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_extrema) of the unnormalized sinc correspond to its intersections with the [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine) function. That is, $\tfrac{\sin(\xi)}{\xi} = \cos(\xi)$ for all points $\xi$ where the derivative of $\tfrac{\sin(x)}{x}$ is zero and thus a local extremum is reached. This follows from the derivative of the sinc function:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\operatorname{sinc}(x) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\cos(x) - \operatorname{sinc}(x)}{x}, & x \ne 0 \\0, & x = 0\end{cases}.$$

The first few terms of the infinite series for the $x$ coordinate of the $n$th extremum with positive $x$ coordinate are

$$x_n = q - q^{-1} - \frac{2}{3} q^{-3} - \frac{13}{15} q^{-5} - \frac{146}{105} q^{-7} - \cdots,$$

where

$$q = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right) \pi,$$

and where odd $n$ lead to a local minimum, and even $n$ to a local maximum. Because of symmetry around the $y$ axis, there exist extrema with $x$ coordinates $-x_{n}$. In addition, there is an absolute maximum at $\xi_{0} = (0, 1)$.

The normalized sinc function has a simple representation as the [infinite product](wikipedia://en/26382006):

$$\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x} = \prod_{n=1}^\infty \left(1 - \frac{x^2}{n^2}\right)$$

*Image: The cardinal sine function sinc(z) plotted in the complex plane from -2-2i to 2+2i.svg.* The cardinal sine function $\operatorname{sinc}(z)$ plotted in the complex plane from $-2 - 2i$ to $2 + 2i$

and is related to the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316) $\Gamma(x)$, as well as to Gauss' [Pi function](wikipedia://en/12316#Pi_function), through [Euler's reflection formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_reflection_formula):

$$\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x} = \frac{1}{\Gamma(1 + x)\Gamma(1 - x)} = \frac{1}{\Pi(x)\Pi(-x)}.$$

[Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler) discovered[^o16] that

$$\frac{\sin(x)}{x} = \prod_{n=1}^\infty \cos\left(\frac{x}{2^n}\right),$$

and because of the product-to-sum identity[^o17]

*Image: Sinc cplot.svg.* [Domain coloring](wikipedia://en/5280990) plot of $\operatorname{sinc} z = \tfrac{\sin z}{z}$

$$\prod_{n=1}^k \cos\left(\frac{x}{2^n}\right) = \frac{1}{2^{k-1}} \sum_{n=1}^{2^{k-1}} \cos\left(\frac{n - 1/2}{2^{k-1}} x \right),\quad \forall k \ge 1,$$

Euler's product can be recast as a sum

$$\frac{\sin(x)}{x} = \lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N \cos\left(\frac{n - 1/2}{N} x\right).$$

The [continuous Fourier transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_Fourier_transform) of the normalized sinc (to ordinary frequency) is $\operatorname{rect}(f)$:

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \operatorname{sinc}(t) \, e^{-i 2 \pi f t}\,dt = \operatorname{rect}(f),$$

where the [rectangular function](wikipedia://en/1261615) is 1 for argument between −$\tfrac{1}{2}$ and $\tfrac{1}{2}$, and zero otherwise. This corresponds to the fact that the [sinc filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinc_filter) is the ideal ([brick-wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brick-wall_filter), meaning rectangular [frequency response](wikipedia://en/302033)) [low-pass filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-pass_filter).

This Fourier integral, including the special case

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x} \, dx = \operatorname{rect}(0) = 1$$

is an [improper integral](wikipedia://en/346030) (see [Dirichlet integral](wikipedia://en/1235977)) and not a convergent [Lebesgue integral](wikipedia://en/26064288), as

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \left|\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x} \right| \,dx = +\infty.$$

The normalized sinc function has properties that make it ideal in relationship to [interpolation](wikipedia://en/14569) of [sampled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampling_%28signal_processing%29) [bandlimited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandlimited) functions:

- It is an interpolating function, i.e., $\operatorname{sinc}(0) = 1$, and $\operatorname{sinc}(k) = 0$ for nonzero [integer](wikipedia://en/21690#Integers) $k$.
- The functions $x_{k}(t) = \operatorname{sinc}(t - k)$ ($k$ integer) form an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) for [bandlimited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandlimited) functions in the [function space](wikipedia://en/45194) $\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\mathbf{R})$, with highest angular frequency $\omega_{H} = \pi$ (that is, highest cycle frequency $f_{H} = \tfrac{1}{2}$).

Other properties of the two sinc functions include:

- The unnormalized sinc is the zeroth-order spherical [Bessel function](wikipedia://en/4700) of the first kind, $j_{0}(x)$. The normalized sinc is $j_{0}(\pi x)$.
- where $\operatorname{Si}(x)$ is the [sine integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_integral), $$\int_0^x \frac{\sin(\theta)}{\theta}\,d\theta = \operatorname{Si}(x).$$
- $\lambda \operatorname{sinc}(\lambda x)$ (not normalized) is one of two linearly independent solutions to the linear [ordinary differential equation](wikipedia://en/32742753) $$x \frac{d^2 y}{d x^2} + 2 \frac{d y}{d x} + \lambda^2 x y = 0.$$ The other is $\tfrac{\cos(\lambda x)}{x}$, which is not bounded at $x = 0$, unlike its sinc function counterpart.
- Using normalized sinc, $$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{\sin^2(\theta)}{\theta^2}\,d\theta = \pi \quad \Rightarrow \quad \int_{-\infty}^\infty \operatorname{sinc}^2(x)\,dx = 1,$$
- $$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{\sin(\theta)}{\theta}\,d\theta = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left( \frac{\sin(\theta)}{\theta} \right)^2 \,d\theta = \pi.$$
- $$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{\sin^3(\theta)}{\theta^3}\,d\theta = \frac{3\pi}{4}.$$
- $$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{\sin^4(\theta)}{\theta^4}\,d\theta = \frac{2\pi}{3}.$$
- The following improper integral involves the (not normalized) sinc function: $$\int_0^\infty \frac{dx}{x^n + 1} = 1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{(kn)^2 - 1} = \frac{1}{\operatorname{sinc}(\frac{\pi}{n})}.$$

:::

## Relationship to the Dirac delta distribution {#Relationship_to_the_Dirac_delta_distribution}

The normalized sinc function can be used as a *[nascent delta function](wikipedia://en/37021#Representations)*, meaning that the following [weak limit](wikipedia://en/2119266) holds:

$$\lim_{a \to 0} \frac{\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{a}\right)}{\pi x} = \lim_{a \to 0}\frac{1}{a} \operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) = \delta(x).$$

This is not an ordinary limit, since the left side does not converge. Rather, it means that

$$\lim_{a \to 0}\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{1}{a} \operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) \varphi(x) \,dx = \varphi(0)$$

for every [Schwartz function](wikipedia://en/4277567), as can be seen from the [Fourier inversion theorem](wikipedia://en/382445). In the above expression, as $a \to 0$, the number of oscillations per unit length of the sinc function approaches infinity. Nevertheless, the expression always oscillates inside an envelope of $\pm\tfrac{1}{\pi x}$, regardless of the value of $a$.

This complicates the informal picture of $\delta(x)$ as being zero for all $x$ except at the point $x = 0$, and illustrates the problem of thinking of the delta function as a function rather than as a distribution. A similar situation is found in the [Gibbs phenomenon](wikipedia://en/373986).

We can also make an immediate connection with the standard Dirac representation of $\delta(x)$ by writing $b=1/a$ and

$$\lim_{b \to \infty} \frac{\sin\left(b\pi x\right)}{\pi x} = \lim_{b \to \infty} \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-b\pi}^{b\pi} e^{ik x}dk= \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{i k x} dk=\delta(x),$$

which makes clear the recovery of the delta as an infinite bandwidth limit of the integral.

## Summation {#Summation}

All sums in this section refer to the unnormalized sinc function.

The sum of $\operatorname{sinc}(n)$ over integer $n$ from 1 to $\infty$ equals $\tfrac{\pi - 1}{2}$:

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty \operatorname{sinc}(n) = \operatorname{sinc}(1) + \operatorname{sinc}(2) + \operatorname{sinc}(3) + \operatorname{sinc}(4) +\cdots = \frac{\pi - 1}{2}.$$

The sum of the squares also equals $\tfrac{\pi - 1}{2}$:[^o20][^o21]

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty \operatorname{sinc}^2(n) = \operatorname{sinc}^2(1) + \operatorname{sinc}^2(2) + \operatorname{sinc}^2(3) + \operatorname{sinc}^2(4) + \cdots = \frac{\pi - 1}{2}.$$

When the signs of the [addends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Addend) alternate and begin with +, the sum equals $\tfrac{1}{2}$:

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty (-1)^{n+1}\,\operatorname{sinc}(n) = \operatorname{sinc}(1) - \operatorname{sinc}(2) + \operatorname{sinc}(3) - \operatorname{sinc}(4) + \cdots = \frac{1}{2}.$$

The alternating sums of the squares and cubes also equal $\tfrac{1}{2}$:[^o22]

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty (-1)^{n+1}\,\operatorname{sinc}^2(n) = \operatorname{sinc}^2(1) - \operatorname{sinc}^2(2) + \operatorname{sinc}^2(3) - \operatorname{sinc}^2(4) + \cdots = \frac{1}{2},$$

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty (-1)^{n+1}\,\operatorname{sinc}^3(n) = \operatorname{sinc}^3(1) - \operatorname{sinc}^3(2) + \operatorname{sinc}^3(3) - \operatorname{sinc}^3(4) + \cdots = \frac{1}{2}.$$

## Series expansion {#Series_expansion}

The [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) of the unnormalized $\operatorname{sinc}$ function can be obtained from that of the sine (which also yields its value of 1 at $x = 0$):

$$\frac{\sin x}{x} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n x^{2n}}{(2n+1)!} = 1 - \frac{x^2}{3!} + \frac{x^4}{5!} - \frac{x^6}{7!} + \cdots$$

The series converges for all $x$. The normalized version follows easily:

$$\frac{\sin \pi x}{\pi x} = 1 - \frac{\pi^2x^2}{3!} + \frac{\pi^4x^4}{5!} - \frac{\pi^6x^6}{7!} + \cdots$$

[Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) famously compared this series to the expansion of the infinite product form to solve the [Basel problem](wikipedia://en/490303).

## Higher dimensions {#Higher_dimensions}

The product of 1-D sinc functions readily provides a [multivariate](wikipedia://en/615222) sinc function for the square Cartesian grid ([lattice](wikipedia://en/19635706)): $\operatorname{sinc}_{C}(x, y) = \operatorname{sinc}(x) \operatorname{sinc}(y)$, whose [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) is the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of a square in the frequency space (i.e., the brick wall defined in 2-D space). The sinc function for a non-Cartesian [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) (e.g., [hexagonal lattice](wikipedia://en/2868762)) is a function whose [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) is the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of the [Brillouin zone](wikipedia://en/562067) of that lattice. For example, the sinc function for the hexagonal lattice is a function whose [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) is the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of the unit hexagon in the frequency space. For a non-Cartesian lattice this function can not be obtained by a simple [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844). However, the explicit formula for the sinc function for the [hexagonal](wikipedia://en/2868762), [body-centered cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body-centered_cubic), [face-centered cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face-centered_cubic) and other higher-dimensional lattices can be explicitly derived[^o25] using the geometric properties of Brillouin zones and their connection to [zonotopes](wikipedia://en/669402).

For example, a [hexagonal lattice](wikipedia://en/2868762) can be generated by the (integer) [linear span](wikipedia://en/56353) of the vectors

$$\mathbf{u}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{2} \\ \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{u}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{2} \\ -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \end{bmatrix}.$$

Denoting

$$\boldsymbol{\xi}_1 = \tfrac{2}{3} \mathbf{u}_1, \quad \boldsymbol{\xi}_2 = \tfrac{2}{3} \mathbf{u}_2, \quad \boldsymbol{\xi}_3 = -\tfrac{2}{3} (\mathbf{u}_1 + \mathbf{u}_2), \quad \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y\end{bmatrix},$$

one can derive[^o25] the sinc function for this hexagonal lattice as

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{sinc}_\text{H}(\mathbf{x}) = \tfrac{1}{3} \big( & \cos\left(\pi\boldsymbol{\xi}_1\cdot\mathbf{x}\right) \operatorname{sinc}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_2\cdot\mathbf{x}\right) \operatorname{sinc}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_3\cdot\mathbf{x}\right) \\ & {} + \cos\left(\pi\boldsymbol{\xi}_2\cdot\mathbf{x}\right) \operatorname{sinc}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_3\cdot\mathbf{x}\right) \operatorname{sinc}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_1\cdot\mathbf{x}\right) \\ & {} + \cos\left(\pi\boldsymbol{\xi}_3\cdot\mathbf{x}\right) \operatorname{sinc}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_1\cdot\mathbf{x}\right) \operatorname{sinc}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_2\cdot\mathbf{x}\right) \big). \end{aligned}$$

This construction can be used to design [Lanczos window](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanczos_window) for general multidimensional lattices.[^o25]

## Sinhc {#Sinhc}

Some authors, by analogy, define the [hyperbolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function) sine [cardinal function](wikipedia://en/14189688).[^o28][^o29][^o30]

$$\mathrm{sinhc}(x) = \begin{cases} {\displaystyle \frac{\sinh(x)}{x},} & \text{if }x \ne 0 \\ {\displaystyle 1,} & \text{if }x = 0 \end{cases}$$
