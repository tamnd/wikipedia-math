# Koopman–von Neumann classical mechanics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Koopman–von Neumann classical mechanics"}

The **Koopman–von Neumann (KvN) theory** is a description of classical mechanics as an [operatorial](wikipedia://en/22213) theory similar to [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), based on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) of [complex](wikipedia://en/5826), [square-integrable](wikipedia://en/28680558) functions representing classical observables on phase spaces. As its name suggests, the KvN theory was developed by [Bernard Koopman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Koopman) and [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942).[^o2][^o3]

:::

The KvN theory is mathematically related to the conceptually distinct **method of classical wavefunctions** originally introduced by [Mario Schönberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mário_Schenberg)[^o11][^o12] and others.[^o4] These two methods will collectively be called **Hilbert-space formulations of classical mechanics** in what follows.

## History {#History}

section

### Statistical Mechanics and Ergodic Theory {#Statistical_Mechanics_and_Ergodic_Theory}

[Statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481) describes macroscopic systems in terms of [statistical ensembles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_ensemble_%28mathematical_physics%29), such as the macroscopic properties of an [ideal gas](wikipedia://en/65905). [Ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986) is a branch of mathematics arising from the study of statistical mechanics.

The origins of the Koopman–von Neumann theory are tightly connected with the rise of [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986) as an independent branch of mathematics, in particular with [Ludwig Boltzmann](wikipedia://en/544255)'s [ergodic hypothesis](wikipedia://en/258980).

In 1931, Koopman observed that the phase space of the classical system can be converted into a Hilbert space.[^o7] According to this formulation, functions representing physical observables become vectors, with an inner product defined in terms of a natural integration rule over the system's probability density on phase space. This reformulation makes it possible to draw interesting conclusions about the evolution of physical observables from [Stone's theorem](wikipedia://en/893698), which had been proved shortly before. This finding inspired von Neumann to apply the novel formalism to the ergodic problem in 1932.[^o8][^o9] Subsequently, he published several seminal results in modern ergodic theory, including the proof of his [mean ergodic theorem](wikipedia://en/258986#Mean_ergodic_theorem).

### Schönberg's Method of Classical Wavefunctions {#Schönberg's_Method_of_Classical_Wavefunctions}

Independently, several researchers developed a framework for representing classical probability distributions on phase spaces with complex-valued wavefunctions. This method of classical wavefunctions is conceptually distinct from the KvN theory, which was based on treating functions representing classical observables on phase spaces as the elementary vectors of Hilbert spaces.

The method of classical wavefunctions was developed by [Mário Schönberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mário_Schenberg) in 1952-1953,[^o11][^o12] Angelo Loinger in 1962,[^o13] Giacomo Della Riccia and [Norbert Wiener](wikipedia://en/63185) in 1966,[^o14] and [E. C. George Sudarshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._C._George_Sudarshan) in 1976.[^o15] In particular, Loinger[^o13] established an isomorphism between the Hilbert spaces of classical wavefunctions and KvN Hilbert spaces, yielding an overall Hilbert-space framework for classical mechanics.

In 2001, Danilo Mauro made important advances[^o16][^o17] to the line of work tracing back to Schönberg but Mauro attributed the concepts to Koopman and von Neumann. Since that time the method of classical wave functions became conflated with the KvN theory, despite being conceptually different theories with different origins.[^o4]

## Definition and dynamics {#Definition_and_dynamics}

### Derivation starting from the Liouville equation {#Derivation_starting_from_the_Liouville_equation}

In the method of classical wavefunctions, as part of Hilbert-space classical mechanics, dynamics in [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) is described by a (classical) probability density, recovered from an underlying wavefunction – the classical wavefunction – as the square of its absolute value (more precisely, as the amplitude multiplied with its own [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918)). This stands in analogy to the [Born rule](wikipedia://en/4313746) in quantum mechanics. In Hilbert-space classical mechanics, observables are represented by commuting self-adjoint operators acting on the [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) of classical wavefunctions. The commutativity physically implies that all observables are simultaneously measurable. Contrast this with quantum mechanics, where observables need not commute, which underlines the [uncertainty principle](wikipedia://en/31883), [Kochen–Specker theorem](wikipedia://en/3790812), and [Bell inequalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_inequalities).[^o20]

The classical wavefunction is postulated to evolve according to exactly the same [Liouville equation](wikipedia://en/312301) as the classical probability density.[^o11] From this postulate it can be shown that indeed probability density dynamics is recovered.

::: {#o21 .proof cue=math-proof name="Dynamics of the probability density (proof)"}

**Dynamics of the probability density (proof).**

In classical statistical mechanics, the probability density (with respect to [Liouville measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liouville_measure)) obeys the Liouville equation[^o11][^o22][^o23]

$$i\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \rho (x, p, t) = \hat{L} \rho(x, p, t)$$

with the self-adjoint Liouvillian

$$\hat{L} = - i\frac{\partial H(x, p)}{\partial p} \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + i\frac{\partial H(x, p)}{\partial x} \frac{\partial}{\partial p},$$

where $H(x,p)$ denotes the [classical Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319) (i.e. the Liouvillian is $i$ times the [Hamiltonian vector field](wikipedia://en/1818270) considered as a first order differential operator). The same dynamical equation is postulated for the classical wavefunction

$$i\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \psi (x, p, t) = \hat{L} \psi (x, p, t),$$

thus

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \psi(x, p, t) = \left[- \frac{\partial H(x, p)}{\partial p} \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial H(x, p)}{\partial x} \frac{\partial}{\partial p} \right] \psi(x, p, t),$$

and for its complex conjugate

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \psi^*(x, p, t) = \left[- \frac{\partial H(x, p)}{\partial p} \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial H(x, p)}{\partial x} \frac{\partial}{\partial p} \right] \psi^*(x, p, t).$$

From

$$\rho(x, p, t) = \psi^*(x, p, t) \psi(x, p, t)$$

follows using the [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988#Higher_partial_derivatives) that

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \rho(x, p, t) = \left[- \frac{\partial H(x, p)}{\partial p} \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial H(x, p)}{\partial x} \frac{\partial}{\partial p} \right] \rho(x, p, t)$$

which proves that probability density dynamics can be recovered from the classical wavefunction.

Remark

The last step of this derivation relies on the classical Liouville operator containing only first-order derivatives in the coordinate and momentum; this is not the case in quantum mechanics where the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874) contains second-order derivatives. [^o22] [^o23]

:::

::: {#o25 .statement kind=statement/axiom cue=named-heading name="Derivation starting from operator axioms"}

### Derivation starting from operator axioms {#Derivation_starting_from_operator_axioms}

Conversely, it is possible to start from operator postulates, similar to the [Hilbert-space axioms of quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_%28physics%29#Operators_in_quantum_mechanics), and derive the equation of motion by specifying how expectation values evolve.[^o26]

The relevant axioms are that as in quantum mechanics (i) the states of a system are represented by normalized vectors of a complex Hilbert space, and the observables are given by [self-adjoint operators](wikipedia://en/187408) acting on that space, (ii) the expectation value of an observable is obtained in the manner as the [expectation value in quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/11371560#Formalism_in_quantum_mechanics), (iii) the probabilities of measuring certain values of some observables are calculated by the [Born rule](wikipedia://en/4313746), and (iv) the state space of a composite system is the [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) of the subsystem's spaces.

::: {#o27 .proof cue=math-proof name="Mathematical form of the operator axioms"}

**Mathematical form of the operator axioms.**

The above axioms (i) to (iv), with the [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) written in the [bra–ket notation](wikipedia://en/4542), are

$\langle \psi(t) | \psi(t) \rangle = 1$, The expectation value of an observable $\hat{A}$ at time $t$ is $\langle A (t)\rangle = \langle \Psi (t)| \hat{A} | \Psi(t) \rangle.$ The probability that a measurement of an observable $\hat{A}$ at time $t$ yields $A$ is $\left|\langle A | \Psi(t)\rangle \right|^2$, where $\hat{A} |A\rangle = A |A \rangle$. (This axiom is an analogue of the [Born rule](wikipedia://en/4313746) in quantum mechanics.[^o28]) (see [Tensor product of Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/2728973#Definition)).

:::

These axioms allow us to recover the formalism of both classical and quantum mechanics.[^o26] Specifically, under the assumption that the classical position and momentum operators [commute](wikipedia://en/294390), the Liouville equation for the classical wavefunction is recovered from averaged [Newton's laws of motion](wikipedia://en/55212). However, if the coordinate and momentum obey the [canonical commutation relation](wikipedia://en/706295), the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874) of quantum mechanics is obtained.

::: {#o29 .proof cue=math-proof name="Derivation of classical mechanics from the operator axioms"}

**Derivation of classical mechanics from the operator axioms.**

We begin from the following equations for expectation values of the coordinate *x* and momentum *p*

$m\frac{d}{dt} \langle x \rangle = \langle p \rangle, \qquad \frac{d}{dt} \langle p \rangle =\langle -U'(x) \rangle,$ aka, [Newton's laws of motion](wikipedia://en/55212) averaged over ensemble. With the help of the operator axioms, they can be rewritten as

$\begin{aligned} m\frac{d}{dt} \langle \Psi(t) | \hat{x} | \Psi(t) \rangle &= \langle \Psi(t) | \hat{p} | \Psi(t) \rangle, \\ \frac{d}{dt} \langle \Psi(t) | \hat{p} | \Psi(t) \rangle &= \langle \Psi(t) | -U'(\hat{x}) | \Psi(t) \rangle. \end{aligned}$ Notice a close resemblance with [Ehrenfest theorems](wikipedia://en/2585991) in quantum mechanics. Applications of the [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988) leads to

$\begin{aligned} \langle d\Psi/dt | \hat{x} | \Psi \rangle + \langle \Psi | \hat{x} | d\Psi/dt \rangle &= \langle \Psi | \hat{p}/m | \Psi \rangle, \\ \langle d\Psi/dt | \hat{p} | \Psi \rangle + \langle \Psi | \hat{p} | d\Psi/dt \rangle & = \langle \Psi | -U'(\hat{x}) | \Psi \rangle, \end{aligned}$ into which we substitute a consequence of [Stone's theorem](wikipedia://en/893698) $i | d\Psi(t)/dt \rangle = \hat{L} | \Psi(t) \rangle$ and obtain

$\begin{aligned} im \langle \Psi(t) | [\hat{L}, \hat{x} ] | \Psi(t) \rangle &= \langle \Psi(t)| \hat{p} |\Psi(t)\rangle, \\ i \langle \Psi(t) | [\hat{L}, \hat{p}] | \Psi(t)\rangle &= - \langle \Psi(t)| U'(\hat{x}) |\Psi(t)\rangle. \end{aligned}$ Since these identities must be valid for any initial state, the averaging can be dropped and the system of commutator equations for the unknown $\hat{L}$ is derived

**Assume that the coordinate and momentum commute $[ \hat{x}, \hat{p} ] = 0$.** This assumption physically means that the classical particle's coordinate and momentum can be measured simultaneously, implying absence of the [uncertainty principle](wikipedia://en/31883).

The solution $\hat{L}$ cannot be simply of the form $\hat{L} = L(\hat{x}, \hat{p})$ because it would imply the contractions $im [L(\hat{x}, \hat{p}), \hat{x}] = 0 = \hat{p}$ and $i [L(\hat{x}, \hat{p}), \hat{p}] = 0 = -U'(\hat{x})$. Therefore, we must utilize additional operators $\hat{\lambda}_x$ and $\hat{\lambda}_p$ obeying

The need to employ these auxiliary operators arises because all classical observables commute. Now we seek $\hat{L}$ in the form $\hat{L} = L(\hat{x}, \hat{\lambda}_x, \hat{p}, \hat{\lambda}_p)$. Utilizing (Hilbert-space algebra), the (commutator eqs for L) can be converted into the following differential equations:[^o26][^o30]

$m L'_{\lambda_x} (x, \lambda_x, p, \lambda_p) = p, \qquad L'_{\lambda_p} (x, \lambda_x, p, \lambda_p) = -U'(x).$ Whence, we conclude that the classical wave function $|\Psi(t)\rangle$ evolves according to the [Schrödinger-like](wikipedia://en/59874) equation of motion

Let us explicitly show that (Hilbert-space dynamical eq) is equivalent to the [classical Liouville mechanics](wikipedia://en/312301#Liouville_equations).

Since $\hat{x}$ and $\hat{p}$ commute, they share the common [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvectors)

with the [resolution of the identity](wikipedia://en/187408#Resolution_of_the_identity) $1 = \int dx \, dp \, |x,p\rangle \langle x,p|.$ Then, one obtains from equation ((Hilbert-space algebra))

$$\langle x,p| \hat{\lambda}_x | \Psi \rangle = -i \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \langle x,p | \Psi \rangle, \qquad \langle x,p| \hat{\lambda}_p | \Psi \rangle = -i \frac{\partial}{\partial p} \langle x,p | \Psi \rangle.$$

Projecting equation ((Hilbert-space dynamical eq)) onto $\langle x,p|$, we get the equation of motion for the classical wave function in the xp-representation

The quantity $\langle x,\, p |\Psi(t) \rangle$ is the probability amplitude for a classical particle to be at point $x$ with momentum $p$ at time $t$. According to the axioms above, the probability density is given by $\rho(x,p;t) = \left| \langle x, p |\Psi(t) \rangle \right|^2$. Utilizing the identity

$$\frac{\partial }{\partial t} \rho(x,p;t) = \langle \Psi(t) | x, p \rangle \frac{\partial }{\partial t} \langle x, p |\Psi(t) \rangle + \langle x, p |\Psi(t) \rangle \left( \frac{\partial }{\partial t} \langle x, p |\Psi(t) \rangle \right)^*$$

as well as ((Hilbert-space dynamical eq in xp)), we recover the classical Liouville equation

Moreover, according to the operator axioms and ((xp eigenvec)),

$$\begin{aligned} \langle A \rangle &= \langle \Psi (t)| A(\hat{x}, \hat{p}) | \Psi(t) \rangle = \int dx \, dp \, \langle \Psi (t)| x,p\rangle A(x, p) \langle x,p | \Psi(t) \rangle \\ & = \int dx \, dp \, A(x, p) \langle \Psi (t)| x,p\rangle \langle x,p | \Psi(t) \rangle = \int dx \, dp \, A(x, p) \rho(x,p;t). \end{aligned}$$

Therefore, the rule for calculating averages of observable $A(x,p)$ in classical statistical mechanics has been recovered from the operator axioms with the additional assumption $[ \hat{x}, \hat{p} ] = 0$. As a result, the phase of a classical wave function does not contribute to observable averages. Contrary to quantum mechanics, the phase of a classical wave function is physically irrelevant. Hence, nonexistence of the [double-slit experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-slit_experiment)[^o23][^o31][^o32] as well as [Aharonov–Bohm effect](wikipedia://en/277664)[^o33] is established in Hilbert-space classical mechanics.

Projecting (Hilbert-space dynamical eq) onto the common eigenvector of the operators $\hat{x}$ and $\hat{\lambda}_p$ (i.e., $x\lambda_p$-representation), one obtains classical mechanics in the doubled configuration space,[^o34] whose generalization leads [^o34] [^o35] [^o36] [^o37] [^o38] to the [phase space formulation of quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_space_formulation).

:::

::: {#o39 .proof cue=math-proof name="Derivation of quantum mechanics from the operator axioms"}

**Derivation of quantum mechanics from the operator axioms.**

As in the derivation of classical mechanics, we begin from the following equations for averages of coordinate *x* and momentum *p*

$m\frac{d}{dt} \langle x \rangle = \langle p \rangle, \qquad \frac{d}{dt} \langle p \rangle =\langle -U'(x) \rangle.$ With the help of the operator axioms, they can be rewritten as

$\begin{aligned} m\frac{d}{dt} \langle \Psi(t) | \hat{x} | \Psi(t) \rangle &= \langle \Psi(t) | \hat{p} | \Psi(t) \rangle, \\ \frac{d}{dt} \langle \Psi(t) | \hat{p} | \Psi(t) \rangle &= \langle \Psi(t) | -U'(\hat{x}) | \Psi(t) \rangle. \end{aligned}$ These are the [Ehrenfest theorems](wikipedia://en/2585991) in quantum mechanics. Applications of the [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988) leads to

$\begin{aligned} \langle d\Psi/dt | \hat{x} | \Psi \rangle + \langle \Psi | \hat{x} | d\Psi/dt \rangle &= \langle \Psi | \hat{p}/m | \Psi \rangle, \\ \langle d\Psi/dt | \hat{p} | \Psi \rangle + \langle \Psi | \hat{p} | d\Psi/dt \rangle & = \langle \Psi | -U'(\hat{x}) | \Psi \rangle, \end{aligned}$ into which we substitute a consequence of [Stone's theorem](wikipedia://en/893698)

$i\hbar | d \Psi(t)/dt \rangle = \hat{H} | \Psi(t) \rangle,$ where $\hbar$ was introduced as a normalization constant to balance dimensionality. Since these identities must be valid for any initial state, the averaging can be dropped and the system of commutator equations for the unknown quantum generator of motion $\hat{H}$ are derived

$im [\hat{H}, \hat{x}] = \hbar \hat{p}, \qquad i [\hat{H}, \hat{p}] = -\hbar U'(\hat{x}).$ Contrary to the case of classical mechanics, we **assume that observables of the coordinate and momentum obey the [canonical commutation relation](wikipedia://en/706295)** $[ \hat{x}, \hat{p} ] = i\hbar$. Setting $\hat{H} = H(\hat{x}, \hat{p})$, the commutator equations can be converted into the differential equations [^o26][^o30]

$m H'_p (x,p) = p, \qquad H'_x (x,p) = U'(x),$ whose solution is the familiar [quantum Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381)

$\hat{H} = \frac{\hat{p}^2}{2m} + U(\hat{x}).$ Whence, the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874) was derived from the Ehrenfest theorems by assuming the canonical commutation relation between the coordinate and momentum. This derivation as well as the derivation of Hilbert-space classical mechanics shows that the difference between quantum and classical mechanics essentially boils down to the value of the commutator $[ \hat{x}, \hat{p} ]$.

:::

### Measurements {#Measurements}

In Hilbert-space classical mechanics, the classical wavefunction takes the form of a superposition of eigenstates, and measurement collapses the classical wavefunction to the eigenstate which is associated the measurement result, in analogy to the [wave function collapse](wikipedia://en/71138) of quantum mechanics.

However, Mauro showed that, in his approach, *non-selective measurements* leave the classical wavefunction unchanged.[^o22]

## Hilbert-space classical mechanics vs Liouville mechanics {#Hilbert-space_classical_mechanics_vs_Liouville_mechanics}

The dynamical equation for Hilbert-space classical mechanics ((Hilbert-space dynamical eq in xp)) and the [Liouville equation](wikipedia://en/312301#Liouville_equations) ((Liouville eq)) are [first-order linear partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/4722013). One recovers [Newton's laws of motion](wikipedia://en/55212) by applying the [method of characteristics](wikipedia://en/751933) to either of these equations. Hence, the key difference between Hilbert-space classical mechanics and Liouville mechanics lies in weighting individual trajectories: Arbitrary weights, underlying the classical wave function, can be utilized in Hilbert-space classical mechanics, while only positive weights, representing the probability density, are permitted in the Liouville mechanics (see this scheme).

## Quantum analogy {#Quantum_analogy}

Being explicitly based on the Hilbert space language, the Hilbert-space approach to classical mechanics adopts many techniques from quantum mechanics, for example, [perturbation](wikipedia://en/297069) and [diagram techniques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feynman_diagrams)[^o43] as well as [functional integral methods](wikipedia://en/301521).[^o44][^o45][^o46] Hilbert-space classical mechanics is very general, and it has been extended to [dissipative systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissipative_systems),[^o47] [relativistic mechanics](wikipedia://en/2021419),[^o55] and [classical field theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_field_theories).[^o26][^o48][^o49][^o50]

The Hilbert-space classical mechanics is fruitful in studies on the [quantum-classical correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_limit)[^o26][^o28][^o51][^o52][^o53] as it reveals that the use of Hilbert spaces is not exclusively quantum mechanical.[^o54] Even [Dirac spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_spinor) are not exceptionally quantum as they are utilized in the relativistic generalization of Hilbert-space classical mechanics.[^o55] Similarly as the more well-known [phase space formulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_space_formulation) of quantum mechanics, Hilbert-space classical mechanics can be understood as an attempt to bring classical and quantum mechanics into a common mathematical framework. In fact, the time evolution of the [Wigner function](wikipedia://en/1884336) approaches, in the classical limit, the time evolution of the classical wavefunction of a classical particle.[^o55][^o56] However, a mathematical resemblance to quantum mechanics does not imply the presence of hallmark quantum effects. In particular, impossibility of [double-slit experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-slit_experiment)[^o23][^o31][^o32] and [Aharonov–Bohm effect](wikipedia://en/277664)[^o33] are explicitly demonstrated in Hilbert-space classical mechanics.
