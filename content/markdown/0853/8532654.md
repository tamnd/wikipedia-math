# Mice problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mice problem"}

*Image: Four point pursuit curve.gif.* Four mice

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **mice problem** is a continuous [pursuit–evasion](wikipedia://en/3857398) problem in which a number of mice (or insects, dogs, missiles, etc.) are considered to be placed at the corners of a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306). In the classic setup, each then begins to move towards its immediate neighbour (clockwise or anticlockwise). The goal is often to find out at what time the mice meet.

:::

The most common version has the mice starting at the corners of a unit square, moving at unit speed. In this case they meet after a time of one unit, because the distance between two neighboring mice always decreases at a speed of one unit. More generally, for a regular polygon of $n$ unit-length sides, the distance between neighboring mice decreases at a speed of $1 - \cos(2\pi/n)$, so they meet after a time of $1/\bigl(1 - \cos(2\pi/n)\bigr)$.[^o2][^o3]

## Path of the mice {#Path_of_the_mice}

For all regular polygons, each mouse traces out a [pursuit curve](wikipedia://en/587650) in the shape of a [logarithmic spiral](wikipedia://en/42373). These curves meet in the center of the polygon.[^o5]

In *[Dara Ó Briain: School of Hard Sums](wikipedia://en/35607118)*, the mice problem is discussed. Instead of 4 mice, 4 ballroom dancers are used.[^o7]

## Whirl {#Whirl}

*Image: 1deg\_square\_whirl.svg.* A whirl with alternating black and white squares

Connecting the locations of the mice at different intervals forms a whirl: a figure consisting of a sequence of nested polygons, each smaller and rotated relative to the previous.[^o9][^o10][^o11][^o12][^o13][^o14]
