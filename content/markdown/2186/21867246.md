# Mahler volume

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mahler volume"}

In [convex geometry](wikipedia://en/4165882), the **Mahler volume** of a [centrally symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_symmetry) [convex body](wikipedia://en/8652020) is a [dimensionless quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless_quantity) that is associated with the body and is [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation). It is named after German-English mathematician [Kurt Mahler](wikipedia://en/4421496). It is known that the shapes with the largest possible Mahler volume are the [balls](wikipedia://en/160556) and solid [ellipsoids](wikipedia://en/145381); this is now known as the **Blaschke–Santaló inequality**. The still-unsolved **Mahler conjecture** states that the minimum possible Mahler volume is attained by a [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A convex body in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is defined as a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292) with [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566) interior. If $B$ is a centrally symmetric convex body in $n$-dimensional Euclidean space, the [polar body](wikipedia://en/1764022) $B^\circ$ is another centrally symmetric body in the same space, defined as the set

$$\left\{ x\mid x\cdot y\le 1 \text{ for all } y\in B \right\}.$$

The Mahler volume of $B$ is the product of the volumes of $B$ and $B^\circ$.[^o4]

If $T$ is an [invertible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible) linear transformation, then $(TB)^\circ = (T^{-1})^\ast B^\circ$. Applying $T$ to $B$ multiplies its volume by $\det T$ and multiplies the volume of $B^\circ$ by $\det (T^{-1})^\ast$. As these determinants are [multiplicative inverses](wikipedia://en/229940), the overall Mahler volume of $B$ is preserved by linear transformations.

:::

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The polar body of an [$n$-dimensional unit ball](wikipedia://en/39782) is itself another unit ball. Thus, its Mahler volume is just the square of its volume,

$$\frac{\Gamma(3/2)^{2n}4^n}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}+1)^2}$$

where $\Gamma$ is the [Gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316). By affine invariance, any solid [ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/145381) has the same Mahler volume.[^o4]

The polar body of a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) or [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) is its [dual polyhedron](wikipedia://en/8815) or [dual polytope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_polytope). In particular, the polar body of a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) or [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783) is an [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635) or [cross polytope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_polytope). Its Mahler volume can be calculated as[^o4]

$$\frac{4^n}{\Gamma(n+1)}.$$

The Mahler volume of the ball is larger than the Mahler volume of the hypercube by a factor of approximately $\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)^n$.[^o4]

:::

## Extreme shapes {#Extreme_shapes}

::: {#o8 .problem cue=unsolved}

Is the Mahler volume of a centrally symmetric convex body always at least that of the hypercube of the same dimension?

:::

The Blaschke–Santaló inequality states that the shapes with maximum Mahler volume are the balls and solid ellipsoids. The three-dimensional case of this result was [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) by (txt); the full result was proven much later by (txt) using a technique known as [Steiner symmetrization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steiner_symmetrization) by which any centrally symmetric convex body can be replaced with a more sphere-like body without decreasing its Mahler volume.[^o4]

The shapes with the minimum known Mahler volume are [hypercubes](wikipedia://en/39783), [cross polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_polytope), and more generally the [Hanner polytopes](wikipedia://en/39408400) which include these two types of shapes, as well as their affine transformations. The Mahler conjecture states that the Mahler volume of these shapes is the smallest of any *n*-dimensional symmetric convex body; it remains unsolved when $n\geq4$. As [Terry Tao](wikipedia://en/1118498) writes:[^o4] The main reason why this [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) is so difficult is that unlike the upper [bound](wikipedia://en/42693), in which there is essentially only one extremiser up to affine transformations (namely the ball), there are many distinct extremisers for the lower bound - not only the cube and the octahedron, but also products of cubes and octahedra, polar bodies of products of cubes and octahedra, products of polar bodies of… well, you get the idea. It is really difficult to conceive of any sort of flow or optimisation procedure which would converge to exactly these bodies and no others; a radically different type of argument might be needed.

(Bourgain Milman 1987) proved that the Mahler volume is bounded below by $c^n$ times the volume of a sphere for some absolute constant $c > 0$, matching the scaling behavior of the hypercube volume but with a smaller constant. (Kuperberg 2008) proved that, more concretely, one can take $c=\tfrac{1}{2}$ in this bound. A result of this type is known as a **reverse Santaló inequality**.

## Partial results {#Partial_results}

- The 2-dimensional case of the Mahler conjecture has been solved by Mahler[^o14] and the 3-dimensional case by Iriyeh and Shibata.[^o15]
- It is known that each of the [Hanner polytopes](wikipedia://en/39408400) is a strict local minimizer for the Mahler volume in the class of origin-symmetric convex bodies endowed with the [Banach–Mazur distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banach–Mazur_distance). This was first proven by Nazarov, Petrov, Ryabogin, and Zvavitch for the unit cube,[^o16] and later generalized to all Hanner polytopes by Jaegil Kim.[^o17]
- The Mahler conjecture holds for [zonotopes](wikipedia://en/669406).[^o18]
- The Mahler conjecture holds in the class of [unconditional bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unconditional_bodies), that is, convex bodies invariant under reflection on each coordinate hyperplane {*x*<sub>*i*</sub> = 0}. This was first proven by Saint-Raymond in 1980.[^o19] Later, a much shorter proof was found by Meyer.[^o20] This was further generalized to convex bodies with [symmetry groups](wikipedia://en/44362809) that are more general [reflection groups](wikipedia://en/2422023). The minimizers are then not necessarily Hanner polytopes, but were found to be regular polytopes corresponding to the reflection groups.[^o21]
- Reisner et al. (2010) showed that a minimizer of the Mahler volume must have [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) equal to zero almost everywhere on its boundary, suggesting strongly that a minimal body is a polytope.[^o22]

## For asymmetric bodies {#For_asymmetric_bodies}

The Mahler volume can be defined in the same way, as the product of the volume and the polar volume, for convex bodies whose interior contains the origin regardless of symmetry. Mahler conjectured that, for this generalization, the minimum volume is obtained by a [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781), with its centroid at the origin. As with the symmetric Mahler conjecture, reverse Santaló inequalities are known showing that the minimum volume is at least within an exponential factor of the simplex.[^n1]
