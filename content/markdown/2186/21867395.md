# Ax–Grothendieck theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ax–Grothendieck theorem"}

In mathematics, the **Ax–Grothendieck theorem** is a result about [injectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injectivity) and [surjectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjectivity) of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) that was proved independently by [James Ax](wikipedia://en/6165715) and [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042).[^o2][^o3][^o4][^o5]

:::

The theorem is often given as this special case: If $P$ is an [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) polynomial function from an $n$-dimensional [complex vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_vector_space) to itself then $P$ is [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective). That is, if $P$ always maps distinct arguments to distinct values, then the values of $P$ cover all of $\mathbb{C}^n$.[^o4][^o5]

The full theorem generalizes to [regular maps](wikipedia://en/24457573) on any [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) over an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018).[^o6]

::: {#o8 .proof cue=heading name="Proof via finite fields"}

## Proof via finite fields {#Proof_via_finite_fields}

Grothendieck's proof of the theorem[^o4][^o5] is based on proving the analogous theorem for [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615) and their [algebraic closures](wikipedia://en/3129). That is, for any field $F$ that is itself finite or that is the closure of a finite field, if a polynomial $P$ from $F^n$ to itself is injective then it is bijective.

If $F$ is a finite field, then $F^n$ is finite. In this case the theorem is true for trivial reasons having nothing to do with the representation of the function as a polynomial: any injection of a finite set to itself is a bijection. When $F$ is the algebraic closure of a finite field, the result follows from [Hilbert's Nullstellensatz](wikipedia://en/149215). The Ax–Grothendieck theorem for complex numbers can therefore be proven by showing that a counterexample over $\mathbb{C}$ would translate into a counterexample in some algebraic extension of a finite field.

This method of proof is noteworthy in that it is an example of the idea that finitistic algebraic relations in fields of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) 0 translate into algebraic relations over finite fields with large characteristic.[^o4] Thus, one can use the arithmetic of finite fields to prove a statement about $\mathbb{C}$ even though there is no [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) from any finite field to $\mathbb{C}$. The proof thus uses [model-theoretic principles](wikipedia://en/19858) such as the [compactness theorem](wikipedia://en/152207) to prove an elementary statement about polynomials. The proof for the general case uses a similar method.

:::

## Other proofs {#Other_proofs}

There are other proofs of the theorem. [Armand Borel](wikipedia://en/365672) gave a proof using topology.[^o5] The case of $n=1$ and field $\mathbb{C}$ follows since $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed and can also be thought of as a special case of the result that for any [analytic function](wikipedia://en/61478) $f$ on $\mathbb{C}$, injectivity of $f$ implies surjectivity of $f$. This is a corollary of [Picard's theorem](wikipedia://en/674050).

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Related results"}

## Related results {#Related_results}

The inverse of $f$ is always also a polynomial function in the case of complex numbers, i.e., $f$ is an [isomorphism of varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_of_varieties).[^o12] More generally, a bijective morphism $f\colon Y\to X$ of complex varieties is an isomorphism if $X$ is [normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_variety), which can be deduced from [Zariski's main theorem](wikipedia://en/13969950).[^o13]

Another example of reducing theorems about [morphisms of finite type](wikipedia://en/70728366) to finite fields can be found in [EGA IV](wikipedia://en/471387): There, it is proved that a [radicial](wikipedia://en/14483490) $S$-endomorphism of a scheme $X$ of finite type over $S$ is bijective (10.4.11), and that if $X/S$ is of finite presentation, and the endomorphism is a monomorphism, then it is an automorphism (17.9.6). Therefore, a scheme of finite presentation over a base $S$ is a cohopfian object in the category of $S$-schemes.

The Ax–Grothendieck theorem may also be used to prove the [Garden of Eden theorem](wikipedia://en/1144206), a result that like the Ax–Grothendieck theorem relates injectivity with surjectivity but in [cellular automata](wikipedia://en/54342) rather than in algebraic fields. Although direct proofs of this theorem are known, the proof via the Ax–Grothendieck theorem extends more broadly, to automata acting on [amenable groups](wikipedia://en/657430).[^o14]

Some partial converses to the Ax-Grothendieck Theorem:

- A generically surjective polynomial map of $n$-dimensional affine space over a finitely generated extension of $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}[t]$ is bijective with a polynomial inverse rational over the same ring (and therefore bijective on affine space of the algebraic closure).
- A generically surjective rational map of $n$-dimensional affine space over a Hilbertian field is generically bijective with a rational inverse defined over the same field. ("Hilbertian field" being defined here as a field for which Hilbert's Irreducibility Theorem holds, such as the rational numbers and function fields.)[^o15]
