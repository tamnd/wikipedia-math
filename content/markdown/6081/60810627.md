# Newton–Gauss line

*Image: Newton-Gauss Line Default Figure.svg.* solid red solid lime

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Newton–Gauss line"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **Newton–Gauss line** (or **Gauss–Newton line**) is the [line](wikipedia://en/946975) joining the [midpoints](wikipedia://en/437979) of the three [diagonals](wikipedia://en/349251) of a [complete quadrilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_quadrilateral).

:::

The midpoints of the two diagonals of a [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278) with at most two parallel sides are distinct and thus determine a line, the [Newton line](wikipedia://en/35411198). If the sides of such a quadrilateral are extended to form a complete quadrangle, the diagonals of the quadrilateral remain diagonals of the complete quadrangle and the Newton line of the quadrilateral is the Newton–Gauss line of the complete quadrangle.

## Complete quadrilaterals {#Complete_quadrilaterals}

Any four lines in [general position](wikipedia://en/383736) (no two lines are parallel, and no three are concurrent) form a [complete quadrilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_quadrilateral). This [configuration](wikipedia://en/7200539) consists of a total of six points, the intersection points of the four lines, with three points on each line and precisely two lines through each point.[^o3] These six points can be split into pairs so that the [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860) determined by any pair do not intersect any of the given four lines except at the endpoints. These three line segments are called [diagonals](wikipedia://en/349251) of the complete quadrilateral.

### Existence of the Newton−Gauss line {#Existence_of_the_Newton−Gauss_line}

*Image: Labeling for a complete quadrilateral.svg.* Labels used in proof concerning complete quadrilateral

It is a well-known theorem that the three midpoints of the diagonals of a complete quadrilateral are [collinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear).[^o5] There are several proofs of the result based on areas [^o5] or wedge products[^o6] or, as the following proof, on [Menelaus's theorem](wikipedia://en/1480484), due to Hillyer and published in 1920.[^o7]

Let the complete quadrilateral $ABCA'B'C'$ be labeled as in the diagram with diagonals $\overline{AA'}, \overline{BB'}, \overline{CC'}$ and their respective midpoints $L, M, N$. Let the midpoints of $\overline{BC}, \overline{CA'}, \overline{A'B}$ be $P, Q, R$ respectively. Using similar triangles it is seen that $QR$ intersects $\overline{AA'}$ at $L$, $RP$ intersects $\overline{BB'}$ at $M$ and $PQ$ intersects $\overline{CC'}$ at $N$. Again, similar triangles provide the following proportions,

$$\frac{\overline{RL}}{\overline{LQ}} = \frac{\overline{BA}}{\overline{AC}}, \quad \frac{\overline{QN}}{\overline{NP}} = \frac{\overline{A'C'}}{\overline{C'B}}, \quad \frac{\overline{PM}}{\overline{MR}} = \frac{\overline{CB'}}{\overline{B'A'}}.$$

However, the line $A'B'C$ intersects the sides of triangle $\triangle ABC$, so by Menelaus's theorem the product of the terms on the right hand sides is −1. Thus, the product of the terms on the left hand sides is also −1 and again by Menelaus's theorem, the points $L, M, N$ are collinear on the sides of triangle $\triangle PQR$.

## Applications to cyclic quadrilaterals {#Applications_to_cyclic_quadrilaterals}

The following are some results that use the Newton–Gauss line of complete quadrilaterals that are associated with [cyclic quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/194131), based on the work of Barbu and Patrascu.[^o9]

### Equal angles {#Equal_angles}

*Image: Newton-Gauss\_Line\_Figure\_1\_subtext.png.* *Figure 1:* An angle equality.

Given any cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$, let point $F$ be the [point of intersection](wikipedia://en/8240558) between the two diagonals $\overline{AC}$ and $\overline{BD}$. Extend the diagonals $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{CD}$ until they meet at the point of intersection, $E$. Let the [midpoint](wikipedia://en/437979) of the [segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) $\overline{EF}$ be $N$, and let the midpoint of the segment $\overline{BC}$ be $M$ (Figure 1).

::: {#o12 .statement cue=heading name="Theorem"}

#### Theorem {#Theorem}

If the midpoint of the line segment $\overline{BF}$ is $P$, the Newton–Gauss line of the complete quadrilateral $ABCDEF$ and the line $PM$ determine an angle $\angle PMN$ equal to $\angle EFD$.

::: {#o14 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

##### Proof {#Proof}

First show that the [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654) $\triangle NPM, \triangle EDF$ are [similar](wikipedia://en/39377).

Since $BE \parallel PN$ and $FC \parallel PM$, we know $\angle NPM = \angle EAC$. Also, $\tfrac{\overline{BE}}{\overline{PN}} = \tfrac{\overline{FC}}{\overline{PM}} = 2.$

In the cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$, these [equalities](wikipedia://en/90446) hold:

$$\begin{aligned} \angle EDF &= \angle ADF + \angle EDA, \\ &= \angle ACB + \angle ABC, \\ &= \angle EAC. \end{aligned}$$

Therefore, $\angle NPM = \angle EDF$.

Let $R_{1}, R_{2}$ be the [radii](wikipedia://en/1780815) of the [circumcircles](wikipedia://en/161262) of $\triangle EDB, \triangle FCD$ respectively. Apply the [law of sines](wikipedia://en/113222) to the triangles, to obtain:

$$\frac {\overline{BE}}{\overline{FC}}=\frac {2R_1\sin \angle EDB}{2R_2\sin \angle FDC}=\frac {R_1}{R_2}=\frac {2R_1\sin \angle EBD}{2R_2\sin \angle FCD} = \frac{\overline{DE}}{\overline{DF}}.$$

Since $\overline{BE} = 2 \cdot \overline{PN}$ and $\overline{FC} = 2 \cdot \overline{PM}$, this shows the equality $\tfrac{\overline{PN}}{\overline{PM}} = \tfrac{\overline{DE}}{\overline{DF}}.$ The similarity of triangles $\triangle PMN, \triangle DFE$ follows, and $\angle NMP = \angle EFD$.

:::

##### Remark {#Remark}

If $Q$ is the midpoint of the line segment $\overline{FC}$, it follows by the same reasoning that $\angle NMQ = \angle EFA$.

:::

### Isogonal lines {#Isogonal_lines}

::: {#o18 .statement cue=heading name="Theorem"}

#### Theorem {#Theorem}

The line through $E$ [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) to the Newton–Gauss line of the complete quadrilateral $ABCDEF$ and the line $EF$ are isogonal lines of $\angle BEC$, that is, each line is a [reflection](wikipedia://en/295829) of the other about the [angle bisector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_bisector).[^o9] (Figure 2)

::: {#o20 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

##### Proof {#Proof}

Triangles $\triangle EDF, \triangle NPM$ are similar by the above argument, so $\angle DEF = \angle PNM$. Let $E'$ be the point of intersection of $BC$ and the line parallel to the Newton–Gauss line $NM$ through $E$.

Since $PN \parallel BE$ and $NM \parallel EE',$ $\angle BEF = \angle PNF$, and $\angle FNM = \angle E'EF$.

Therefore,

$$\begin{aligned} \angle CEE' &= \angle DEF - \angle E'EF, \\ &= \angle PNM - \angle FNM, \\ &= \angle PNF = \angle BEF. \end{aligned}$$

:::

:::

### Two cyclic quadrilaterals sharing a Newton-Gauss line {#Two_cyclic_quadrilaterals_sharing_a_Newton-Gauss_line}

*Image: Newton-Gauss\_Line\_Subtext\_3.1.png.* *Figure 3:* Showing that the quadrilaterals $MPGN, MQHN$ are cyclic.

::: {#o23 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=named-heading name="Lemma"}

#### Lemma {#Lemma}

Let $G$ and $H$ be the [orthogonal projections](wikipedia://en/519182) of the point $F$ on the lines $AB$ and $CD$ respectively.

The [quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/25278) $MPGN$ and $MQHN$ are cyclic quadrilaterals.[^o9]

::: {#o25 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

##### Proof {#Proof}

$\angle EFD = \angle PMN$, as previously shown. The points $P$ and $N$ are the respective [circumcenters](wikipedia://en/161262) of the [right triangles](wikipedia://en/39420) $\triangle BFG, \triangle EFG$. Thus, $\angle PGF = \angle PFG$ and $\angle FGN = \angle GFN$.

Therefore,

$$\begin{aligned} \angle PGN + \angle PMN & = (\angle PGF + \angle FGN) + \angle PMN \\[4pt] & = \angle PFG + \angle GFN + \angle EFD \\[4pt] &= 180^\circ \end{aligned}.$$

Therefore, $MPGN$ is a cyclic quadrilateral, and by the same reasoning, $MQHN$ also lies on a circle.

*Image: Newton-Gauss\_Line\_Figure\_3\_subtext\_2.png.* *Figure 4:* Showing that the complete quadrilaterals $EDGHIJ, ABCDEF$ have the same Newton–Gauss line.

:::

:::

::: {#o27 .statement cue=heading name="Theorem"}

#### Theorem {#Theorem}

Extend the lines $GF, HF$ to intersect $EC, EB$ at $I, J$ respectively (Figure 4).

The complete quadrilaterals $EFGHIJ$ and $ABCDEF$ have the same Newton–Gauss line.[^o9]

::: {#o29 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

##### Proof {#Proof}

The two complete quadrilaterals have a shared diagonal, $\overline{EF}$. $N$ lies on the Newton–Gauss line of both quadrilaterals. $N$ is [equidistant](wikipedia://en/1532579) from $G$ and $H$, since it is the [circumcenter](wikipedia://en/161262) of the cyclic quadrilateral $EGFH$.

If triangles $\triangle GMP, \triangle HMQ$ are [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330), and it will follow that $M$ lies on the [perpendicular bisector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular_bisector) of the line $HG$. Therefore, the line $MN$ contains the midpoint of $\overline{GH}$, and is the Newton–Gauss line of $EFGHIJ$.

To show that the triangles $\triangle GMP, \triangle HMQ$ are congruent, first observe that $PMQF$ is a [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496), since the points $M, P$ are midpoints of $\overline{BF}, \overline{BC}$ respectively.

Therefore,

$$\begin{aligned} & \overline{MP} = \overline{QF} = \overline{HQ}, \\ & \overline{GP} = \overline{PF} = \overline{MQ}, \\ & \angle MPF = \angle FQM. \end{aligned}$$

Also note that

$$\angle FPG = 2 \angle PBG = 2 \angle DBA = 2 \angle DCA = 2 \angle HCF = \angle HQF.$$

Hence,

$$\begin{aligned} \angle MPG &= \angle MPF + \angle FPG, \\ &= \angle FQM + \angle HQF, \\ &= \angle HQF + \angle FQM, \\ &= \angle HQM. \end{aligned}$$

Therefore, $\triangle GMP$ and $\triangle HMQ$ are congruent by SAS.

:::

##### Remark {#Remark}

Due to $\triangle GMP, \triangle HMQ$ being congruent triangles, their [circumcircles](wikipedia://en/161262) $MPGN, MQHN$ are also [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330).

:::

## Relation with the Miquel point {#Relation_with_the_Miquel_point}

The [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139) along the Newton–Gauss line is the [isogonal conjugate](wikipedia://en/2083100) of the Miquel point.

## Generalization {#Generalization}

Dao Thanh Oai showed a generalization of the Newton–Gauss line.[^o34]

For a triangle $ABC$, let $l$ an arbitrary line and $A_{0}B_{0}C_{0}$ the [Cevian triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cevian_triangle) of an arbitrary point $P$. $l$ intersects $BC, CA$, and $AB$ at $A_{1}, B_{1},$ and $C_{1}$ respectively. Then $AA_{1}\cap B_{0}C_{0}, BB_{1}\cap C_{0}A_{0}$, and $CC_{1}\cap A_{0}B_{0}$ are [colinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colinear).

If $P$ is the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) of the triangle $ABC$, the line is Newton–Gauss line of the quadrilateral composed of $AB, BC, CA,$ and $l$.

:::

## History {#History}

The Newton–Gauss line proof was developed by the two mathematicians it is named after: [Sir Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) and [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125). The initial framework for this theorem is from the work of Newton, in his previous theorem on the [Newton line](wikipedia://en/35411198), in which Newton showed that the center of a conic inscribed in a quadrilateral lies on the Newton–Gauss line.[^o36]

The theorem of Gauss and Bodenmiller states that the three circles whose diameters are the diagonals of a complete quadrilateral are [coaxal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coaxal_circles).[^o37]
