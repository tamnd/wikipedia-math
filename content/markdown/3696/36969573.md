# Eisenstein reciprocity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Eisenstein reciprocity"}

In [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705) **Eisenstein's reciprocity law** is a [reciprocity law](wikipedia://en/247622) that extends the [law of quadratic reciprocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_quadratic_reciprocity) and the [cubic reciprocity law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_reciprocity_law) to residues of higher powers. It is one of the earliest and simplest of the higher reciprocity laws, and is a consequence of several later and stronger reciprocity laws such as the [Artin reciprocity law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin_reciprocity_law). It was introduced by (txt), though Jacobi had previously announced (without proof) a similar result for the special cases of 5th, 8th and 12th powers in 1839.[^o3]

:::

## Background and notation {#Background_and_notation}

Let $m > 1$ be an integer, and let $\mathcal{O}_m$ be the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of the *m*-th [cyclotomic field](wikipedia://en/30872462) $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_m),$ where $\zeta_m=e^{2 \pi i\frac{1}{m}}$ is a [primitive *m*-th root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950).

The numbers $\zeta_m, \zeta_m^2,\dots\zeta_m^m=1$ are [units](wikipedia://en/289666) in $\mathcal{O}_m.$ (There are [other units](wikipedia://en/5991396) as well.)

### Primary numbers {#Primary_numbers}

A number $\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_m$ is called **primary**[^o6][^o7] if it is not a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666), is [relatively prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_prime) to $m$, and is congruent to a rational (i.e. in $\mathbb{Z}$) integer $(\!\bmod{(1-\zeta_m)^2}).$

The following lemma[^o8][^o9] shows that primary numbers in $\mathcal{O}_m$ are analogous to positive integers in $\mathbb{Z}.$

Suppose that $\alpha, \beta\in\mathcal{O}_m$ and that both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are relatively prime to $m.$ Then

- There is an integer $c$ making $\zeta_m^c\alpha$ primary. This integer is unique $(\!\bmod{m}).$
- if $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are primary then $\alpha\pm\beta$ is primary, provided that $\alpha\pm\beta$ is coprime with $m$.
- if $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are primary then $\alpha\beta$ is primary.
- $\alpha^m$ is primary.

> The significance of the $1-\zeta_m$ that appears in the definition is most easily seen when $m=l$ is a prime. In that case $l=(1-\zeta_l)(1-\zeta_l^2)\dots(1-\zeta_l^{l-1}).$ Furthermore, the prime ideal $(l)$ of $\mathbb{Z}$ is totally ramified in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_l)$
>
> $(l)=(1-\zeta_l)^{l-1},$ and the ideal $(1-\zeta_l)$ is prime of degree 1.[^o10][^o11]

### *m*-th power residue symbol {#m-th_power_residue_symbol}

For $\alpha, \beta\in\mathcal{O}_m,$ the *m*-th power residue symbol for $\mathcal{O}_m$ is either zero or an *m*-th root of unity:

$$\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta }\right)_m = \begin{cases} \zeta \mbox{ where }\zeta^m=1&\mbox{ if }\alpha\mbox{ and }\beta\mbox{ are relatively prime}\\ 0 &\mbox{ otherwise}.\\ \end{cases}$$

It is the *m*-th power version of the classical (quadratic, *m* = 2) [Jacobi symbol](wikipedia://en/97313) (assuming $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are relatively prime):

- If $\eta\in\mathcal{O}_m$ and $\alpha\equiv\eta^m\;(\bmod{\beta})$ then $\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta }\right)_m = 1.$
- If $\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta }\right)_m \neq1$ then $\alpha$ is not an *m*-th power $(\!\bmod{\beta}).$
- If $\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta }\right)_m =1$ then $\alpha$ may or may not be an *m*-th power $(\!\bmod{\beta}).$

::: {#o14 .statement cue=heading name="Statement of the theorem"}

## Statement of the theorem {#Statement_of_the_theorem}

Let $m\in\mathbb{Z}$ be an odd prime and $a\in\mathbb{Z}$ an integer [relatively prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_prime) to $m.$ Then

### First supplement {#First_supplement}

$\left(\frac{\zeta_m}{a }\right)_m = \zeta_m^{\frac{a^{m-1}-1}{m}}.$[^o16]

### Second supplement {#Second_supplement}

$\left(\frac{1-\zeta_m}{a }\right)_m = \left(\frac{\zeta_m}{a }\right)_m^{\frac{m+1}{2}}.$[^o16]

### Eisenstein reciprocity {#Eisenstein_reciprocity}

Let $\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_m$ be primary (and therefore relatively prime to $m$), and assume that $\alpha$ is also relatively prime to $a$. Then[^o16][^o19]

$$\left(\frac{\alpha}{a }\right)_m = \left(\frac{a}{\alpha }\right)_m.$$

:::

::: {#o21 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

The theorem is a consequence of the [Stickelberger relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stickelberger_relation).[^o22][^o23]

(Weil 1975) gives a historical discussion of some early reciprocity laws, including a proof of Eisenstein's law using Gauss and Jacobi sums that is based on Eisenstein's original proof.

:::

::: {#o26 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

In 1922 [Takagi](wikipedia://en/174754) proved that if $K\supset\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_l)$ is an arbitrary [algebraic number field](wikipedia://en/28730822) containing the $l$-th roots of unity for a prime $l$, then Eisenstein's law for $l$-th powers holds in $K.$[^o27]

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

::: {#o31 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="First case of Fermat's Last Theorem"}

### First case of Fermat's Last Theorem {#First_case_of_Fermat's_Last_Theorem}

Assume that $p$ is an odd prime, that $x^p+y^p+z^p=0$ for pairwise relatively prime integers (i.e. in $\mathbb{Z}$) $x,y,z$ and that $p\nmid xyz.\;\;$

This is the [first case of Fermat's Last Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_case_of_Fermat's_Last_Theorem). (The second case is when $p\mid xyz.\;$) Eisenstein reciprocity can be used to prove the following theorems

**(Wieferich 1909)**[^o32][^o33] Under the above assumptions, $2^{p-1}\equiv 1 \; (\!\bmod{p^2}).$

The only primes below 6.7×10<sup>15</sup> that satisfy this are 1093 and 3511. See [Wieferich primes](wikipedia://en/323631) for details and current records.

**(Mirimanoff 1911)**[^o34] Under the above assumptions $3^{p-1}\equiv 1 \; (\!\bmod{p^2}).$

Analogous results are true for all primes ≤ 113, but the proof does not use Eisenstein's law. See [Wieferich prime#Connection with Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/323631#Connection_with_Fermat's_Last_Theorem).

**(Furtwängler 1912)**[^o35][^o36] Under the above assumptions, for every prime $r\mid x,\;\;r^{p-1}\equiv1 \; (\!\bmod{p^2}).$

**(Furtwängler 1912)**[^o37] Under the above assumptions, for every prime $r\mid (x-y),\;\;r^{p-1}\equiv1 \; (\!\bmod{p^2}).$

**(Vandiver)**[^o38] Under the above assumptions, if in addition $p>3,$ then $x^p\equiv x,\; y^p\equiv y$, and $z^p\equiv z \; (\!\bmod{p^3}).$

:::

### Powers mod most primes {#Powers_mod_most_primes}

Eisenstein's law can be used to prove the following theorem (Trost, [Ankeny](wikipedia://en/7677262), [Rogers](wikipedia://en/33382489)).[^o40] Suppose $a\in\mathbb{Z}$ and that $l\nmid a$ where $l$ is an odd prime. If $x^l\equiv a \; (\!\bmod{p})$ is solvable for all but finitely many primes $p$, then $a=b^l.$

:::
