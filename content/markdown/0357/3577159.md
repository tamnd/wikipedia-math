# Cycle per second

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Cycle per second"}

The **cycle per second** is a once-common English name for the unit of [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779) now known as the *[hertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertz)* (Hz). Cycles per second may be denoted by **c.p.s.**, **c/s**, or, ambiguously, just "cycles" (Cyc., Cy., C, or c). The term comes from repetitive phenomena such as [sound waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_wave) having a frequency measurable as a number of oscillations, or cycles, per second.[^o3]

:::

With the organization of the [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) in 1960, the cycle per second was officially replaced by the [hertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertz), or [reciprocal second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_second), "s<sup>−1</sup>" or "1/s". Symbolically, "cycle per second" units are "cycle/second", while hertz is "Hz" or "s<sup>−1</sup>".[^o4] For higher frequencies, *kilocycles* (kc), as an abbreviation of *kilocycles per second* were often used on components or devices. Other higher units like *megacycle* (Mc) and less commonly *kilomegacycle* (kMc) were used before 1960[^o5] and in some later documents.[^o6] These have modern equivalents such as kilohertz (kHz), megahertz (MHz), and gigahertz (GHz). Following the introduction of the SI standard, use of these terms began to fall off in favor of the new unit, with hertz becoming the dominant convention in both academic and colloquial speech by the 1970s.[^o7]

Cycle can also be a unit for measuring usage of [reciprocating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocating_motion) machines, especially [presses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_press), in which cases *cycle* refers to one complete revolution of the mechanism being measured (i.e. the shaft of a [reciprocating engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocating_engine)).

Derived units include **cycles per day** (**cpd**) and **cycles per year** (**cpy**).
