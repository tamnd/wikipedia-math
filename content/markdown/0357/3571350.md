# Socle (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Socle"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the term **socle** has several related meanings.

:::

::: {#o3 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Socle of a group"}

## Socle of a group {#Socle_of_a_group}

In the context of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), the **socle of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447)** *G*, denoted soc(*G*), is the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) generated by the [minimal normal subgroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_normal_subgroup) of *G*. It can happen that a group has no minimal non-trivial [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) (that is, every non-trivial normal subgroup properly contains another such subgroup) and in that case the socle is defined to be the subgroup generated by the identity. The socle is a [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of minimal normal subgroups.[^n1]

As an example, consider the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) **Z**<sub>12</sub> with [generator](wikipedia://en/99945) *u*, which has two minimal normal subgroups, one generated by *u*<sup>4</sup> (which gives a normal subgroup with 3 elements) and the other by *u*<sup>6</sup> (which gives a normal subgroup with 2 elements). Thus the socle of **Z**<sub>12</sub> is the group generated by *u*<sup>4</sup> and *u*<sup>6</sup>, which is just the group generated by *u*<sup>2</sup>.

The socle is a [characteristic subgroup](wikipedia://en/7198), and hence a normal subgroup. It is not necessarily [transitively normal](wikipedia://en/4938714), however.

If a group *G* is a [finite](wikipedia://en/323707) [solvable group](wikipedia://en/105012), then the socle can be expressed as a product of [elementary abelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_abelian) [*p*-groups](wikipedia://en/24664). Thus, in this case, it is just a product of copies of **Z**/*p***Z** for various *p*, where the same *p* may occur multiple times in the product.

## Socle of a module {#Socle_of_a_module}

In the context of [module theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Module_theory) and [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424) the **socle of a [module](wikipedia://en/276410)** $M$ over a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$ is defined to be the sum of the minimal nonzero [submodules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule) of $M$. It can be considered as a [dual notion](wikipedia://en/609737) to that of the [radical of a module](wikipedia://en/9379691). In set notation,

$$\mathrm{soc}(M) = \sum_{N \text{ is a simple submodule of }M} N.$$

Equivalently,

$$\mathrm{soc}(M) = \bigcap_{E \text{ is an essential submodule of }M} E.$$

The **socle of a ring** $R$ can refer to one of two sets in the ring. Considering $R$ as a right $R$-module, $\mathrm{soc}(R_R)$ is defined, and considering $R$ as a left $R$-module, $\mathrm{soc}(_RR)$ is defined. Both of these socles are [ring ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_ideal), and it is known they are not necessarily equal.

- If $M$ is an [Artinian module](wikipedia://en/542336), $\mathrm{soc}(M)$ is itself an [essential submodule](wikipedia://en/10119238) of $M$.

In fact, if $M$ is a [semiartinian module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-Artinian_ring), then $\mathrm{soc}(M)$ is itself an [essential submodule](wikipedia://en/10119238) of $M$. Additionally, if $M$ is a non-zero module over a left [semi-Artinian ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-Artinian_ring), then $\mathrm{soc}(M)$ is itself an [essential submodule](wikipedia://en/10119238) of $M$. This is because any non-zero module over a left semi-Artinian ring is a semiartinian module.

- A module is [semisimple](wikipedia://en/1001361) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $\mathrm{soc}(M)=M$. Rings for which $\mathrm{soc}(M)=M$ for all module $M$ are precisely [semisimple rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_ring).
- $$\mathrm{soc}(\mathrm{soc}(M))=\mathrm{soc}(M).$$
- $M$ is a [finitely cogenerated module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_cogenerated_module) if and only if $\mathrm{soc}(M)$ is [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) and $\mathrm{soc}(M)$ is an [essential submodule](wikipedia://en/10119238) of $M$.
- Since the sum of semisimple modules is semisimple, the socle of a module could also be defined as the unique maximal semisimple submodule.
- From the definition of $\mathrm{rad}(R)$, it is easy to see that $\mathrm{rad}(R)$ [annihilates](wikipedia://en/328684) $\mathrm{soc}(R)$. If $R$ is a finite-dimensional unital [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788#Generalization:_algebra_over_a_ring) and $M$ a finitely generated $R$-module then the socle consists precisely of the elements annihilated by the [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656) of $R$.[^o7]

:::

## Socle of a Lie algebra {#Socle_of_a_Lie_algebra}

In the context of [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944), a **socle of a [symmetric Lie algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_Lie_algebra)** is the [eigenspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenspace) of its structural [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) that corresponds to the [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) −1. (A symmetric Lie algebra decomposes into the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) of its socle and [cosocle](wikipedia://en/24762871).)[^o9]
