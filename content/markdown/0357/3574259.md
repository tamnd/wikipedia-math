# Brillouin and Langevin functions

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Brillouin and Langevin functions"}

The **Brillouin and Langevin functions** are a pair of [special functions](wikipedia://en/23709110) that appear when studying an idealized [paramagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramagnetic) material in [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481). These functions are named after French physicists [Paul Langevin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Langevin) and [Léon Brillouin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Léon_Brillouin), who contributed to the microscopic understanding of magnetic properties of matter.

:::

The Langevin function is derived using statistical mechanics and describes how magnetic dipoles are aligned by an applied field.[^o2] The Brillouin function was developed later to give an explanation that considers quantum physics.[^o3] The Langevin function could then be seen as a special case of the more general Brillouin function if the quantum number $J$ was infinite ($J \to \infty$).[^o4]

## Brillouin function for paramagnetism {#Brillouin_function_for_paramagnetism}

The **Brillouin function**[^o6][^o7][^o3][^o8] arises when studying [magnetization](wikipedia://en/2404348) of an ideal [paramagnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramagnetism). In particular, it describes the dependency of the magnetization $M$ on the applied [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563) $B$, defined by the following equation:

The function $B_J$ is usually applied in the context where $x$ is a real variable and a function of the applied field $B$. In this case, the function varies from -1 to 1, approaching +1 as $x \to +\infty$ and -1 as $x \to -\infty$.

The [total angular momentum quantum number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_angular_momentum_quantum_number) $J$ is a positive integer or [half-integer](wikipedia://en/1568608). Considering the microscopic [magnetic moments](wikipedia://en/540979) of the material. The magnetization is given by:[^o6]

$$M = N g \mu_\text{B} J B_J(x)$$

where

- $N$ is the number of atoms per unit volume,
- $g$ the [g-factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-factor_%28physics%29),
- $\mu_\text{B}$ the [Bohr magneton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr_magneton),
- $x$ is the ratio of the [Zeeman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeeman_effect) energy of the magnetic moment in the external field to the thermal energy $k_\text{B} T$:[^o6] $$x = J \frac{g\mu_\text{B} B}{k_\text{B} T}$$
- $k_\text{B}$ is the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant) and $T$ the temperature.

Note that in the SI system of units $B$ given in tesla stands for the [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563), $B = \mu_0 H$, where $H$ is the auxiliary magnetic field given in A/m and $\mu_0$ is the [permeability of vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permeability_of_vacuum).

### High-field saturation limit {#High-field_saturation_limit}

When $x\to\infty$, the Brillouin function goes to 1. The magnetization saturates with the magnetic moments completely aligned with the applied field:

$$M = N g \mu_\text{B} J$$

### Low field/high-temperature limit {#Low_field/high-temperature_limit}

For low fields the curve appears almost linear, and could be replaced by a linear slope as in [Curie's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curie's_law) of paramagnetism. When $x \ll 1$ (i.e. when $x = \mu_\text{B} B / k_\text{B} T$ is small) the expression of the magnetization can be approximated by:

$$M = C \cdot \frac{B}{T}$$

and equivalent to [Curie's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curie's_law) with the constant given by

$$C = \frac{N g^2 J(J+1) \mu_\text{B}^2}{3k_\text{B}} = \frac{N \mu_{\text{eff}}^2 }{3k_\text{B}}$$

Using $\mu_{\text{eff}} = g \sqrt{J(J+1)} \mu_\text{B}$ as the effective number of Bohr magnetons.

Note that this is only valid for low fields in [paramagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramagnetism).[^o12] [Ferromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism) materials still has a [spontaneous magnetization](wikipedia://en/2562358) at low fields (below the Curie-temperature), and the susceptibility must then instead be explained by [Curie–Weiss law](wikipedia://en/1561792).

### Two-state case (spin-1/2) {#Two-state_case_(spin-1/2)}

The most simple case of the Brillouin function would be the case of $J = 1/2$, when the function simplifies to the shape of a tanh-function.[^o14][^o15][^o16] Then written as

$$M = N g \mu_\text{B} J \tanh\frac{g J \mu_\text{B} B}{k_\text{B}T},$$

This could be linked to Ising's model, for a case with two possible spins: either up or down.[^o17] Directed in parallel or antiparallel to the applied field.

This is then equivalent to a *2-state* particle: it may either align its magnetic moment with the magnetic field or against it. So the only possible values of magnetic moment are then $\mu_\text{B}$ and $-\mu_\text{B}$. If so, then such a particle has only two possible energies, $-\mu_\text{B} B$ when it is aligned with the field and $+\mu_\text{B} B$ when it is oriented opposite to the field.

## Langevin function for classical paramagnetism {#Langevin_function_for_classical_paramagnetism}

The Langevin function can also be derived as the [classical limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_limit) of the Brillouin function, if the magnetic moments can be continuously aligned in the field and the quantum number $J$ would be able to assume all values ($J \to \infty$). The Brillouin function is then simplified into the Langevin function.

### Classical or quantum approach? {#Classical_or_quantum_approach?}

Langevin function is often seen as the classical theory of paramagnetism,[^o2] while the Brillouin function is the quantum theory of paramagnetism.[^o4] When Langevin published the theory paramagnetism in 1905[^o24][^o25] it was before the adoption of quantum physics. Meaning that Langevin only used concepts of [classical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_physics).[^o26]

[Niels Bohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr) showed in his thesis that classical statistical mechanics can not be used to explain paramagnetism, and that quantum theory has to be used.[^o26] This would later be known as the [Bohr–Van Leeuwen theorem](wikipedia://en/19968510). The magnetic moment would later be explained in quantum theory by the [Bohr magneton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr_magneton) ($\mu_\text{B}$), which is used in the Brillouin function.

It could be noted that there is a difference in the approaches of Langevin and Bohr, since Langevin assumes a magnetic polarization $\mu$ as the basis for the derivation, while Bohr start the derivation from motions of electrons and a model of an atom.[^o26] Langevin is still assuming a fix magnetic dipole. This could be expressed as by [J. H. Van Vleck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hasbrouck_Van_Vleck): "When Langevin assumed that the magnetic moment of the atom or molecule had a fixed value $\mu$, he was quantizing the system without realizing it [^o26]". This makes the Langevin function to be in the borderland between classical statistical mechanics and quantum theory (as either semi-classical or semi-quantum).[^o26]

## Langevin function for electric polarization {#Langevin_function_for_electric_polarization}

The Langevin function could also be used to describe electric polarization, in the specific case when the polarization is explained by orientation of (electrically polarized) dipoles.[^o28][^o29] So that the electric polarization is given by:[^o30]

$$P = P_s \cdot L(x)$$

but here for an electric dipole moment $p$ and an [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) $E_L$ (instead of the magnetic equivalents), that is

$$x = \frac{pE_L}{k_\text{B}T}$$

## Simplified functions {#Simplified_functions}

For small values of $x$, the Langevin function can be approximated by a truncation of its [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448):

$$L(x) = \tfrac{1}{3} x - \tfrac{1}{45} x^3 + \tfrac{2}{945} x^5 - \tfrac{1}{4725} x^7 + \dots$$

The first term of this series expansion is equivalent to [Curie's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curie's_law),[^o2] when writing it as

$$L(x) \approx \frac{x}{3}$$

An alternative, better behaved approximation can be derived from the [Lambert's continued fraction](wikipedia://en/11771113#The_series_0F1_2) expansion of $\tanh(x)$:

$$L(x) = \frac{x}{3+\tfrac{x^2}{5 + \tfrac{x^2}{7+\tfrac{x^2}{9+\ldots}}}}$$

For small enough $x$, both approximations are numerically better than a direct evaluation of the actual analytical expression, since the latter suffers from [catastrophic cancellation](wikipedia://en/19985542) for $x \approx 0$ where $\coth(x) \approx 1/x$.

## Inverse Langevin function {#Inverse_Langevin_function}

The inverse Langevin function $L^{-1}(x)$ is without an explicit analytical form, but there exist several approximations.[^o33]

The inverse Langevin function $L^{-1}(x)$ is defined on the open interval (−1, 1). For small values of $x$, it can be approximated by a truncation of its [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448)[^o34]

$$L^{-1}(x) = 3 x + \tfrac{9}{5} x^3 + \tfrac{297}{175} x^5 + \tfrac{1539}{875} x^7 + \dots$$

and by the [Padé approximant](wikipedia://en/3090820)

$$L^{-1}(x) = 3x \frac{35-12x^2}{35-33x^2} + O(x^7).$$

*Image: Cohen and Jedynak approximations.gif.* Graphs of relative error for x ∈ 0, 1 for Cohen and Jedynak approximations

Since this function has no closed form, it is useful to have approximations valid for arbitrary values of $x$. One popular approximation, valid on the whole range (−1, 1), has been published by A. Cohen:[^o35]

$$L^{-1}(x) \approx x \frac{3-x^2}{1-x^2}.$$

This has a maximum relative error of 4.9% at the vicinity of $x = \pm 0.8$. Greater accuracy can be achieved by using the formula given by R. Jedynak:[^o36]

$$L^{-1}(x) \approx x \frac{3.0-2.6x+0.7x^2}{(1-x)(1+0.1x)},$$

valid for $x \ge 0$. The maximal relative error for this approximation is 1.5% at the vicinity of $x = 0.85$. Even greater accuracy can be achieved by using the formula given by M. Kröger:[^o37]

$$L^{-1}(x) \approx \frac{3x-x(6x^2 + x^4 - 2x^6)/5}{1-x^2}$$

The maximal relative error for this approximation is less than 0.28%. More accurate approximation was reported by R. Petrosyan:[^o38]

$$L^{-1}(x) \approx 3x + \frac{x^2}{5}\sin\left(\frac{7x}{2}\right) + \frac{x^3}{1-x},$$

valid for $x \ge 0$. The maximal relative error for the above formula is less than 0.18%.[^o38]

New approximation given by R. Jedynak,[^o39] is the best reported approximant at complexity 11:

$$L^{-1}(x) \approx \frac{x (3 -1.00651x^2 -0.962251x^4+ 1.47353x^6-0.48953 x^8)}{(1 - x) (1 + 1.01524 x)},$$

valid for $x \ge 0$. Its maximum relative error is less than 0.076%.[^o39]

### Inverse Brillouin function {#Inverse_Brillouin_function}

Approximations could also be used to express the inverse Brillouin function ($B_J(x)^{-1}$). Takacs[^o44] proposed the following approximation to the inverse of the Brillouin function:

$$B_J(x)^{-1} = \frac{axJ^2}{1 - bx^2}$$

where the constants $a$ and $b$ are defined to be

- $$a = \frac{0.5(1+2J)(1-0.055)}{(J-0.27)2J} + \frac{0.1}{J^2}$$
- $$b = 0.8$$
