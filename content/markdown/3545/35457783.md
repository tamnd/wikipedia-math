# Glossary of algebraic geometry

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Glossary of algebraic geometry"}

This is a **glossary of algebraic geometry**.

:::

See also [glossary of commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/38256214), [glossary of classical algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/35326250), and [glossary of ring theory](wikipedia://en/251424). For the number-theoretic applications, see [glossary of arithmetic and Diophantine geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_arithmetic_and_Diophantine_geometry).

For simplicity, a reference to the base scheme is often omitted; i.e., a scheme will be a scheme over some fixed base scheme *S* and a morphism an *S*-morphism.

## !\$@ {#!$@}

::: {#o3 .entry cue=term name="\\eta"}

**$\eta$**

A [generic point](wikipedia://en/3043978). For example, the point associated to the zero ideal for any integral affine scheme.

:::

::: {#o4 .entry cue=term}

If *X* is a projective scheme with [Serre's twisting sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serre's_twisting_sheaf) $\mathcal{O}_X(1)$ and if *F* is an $\mathcal{O}_X$-module, then $F(n) = F \otimes_{\mathcal{O}_X} \mathcal{O}_X(n).$

If *D* is a Cartier divisor and *F* is an $\mathcal{O}_X$-module (*X* arbitrary), then $F(D) = F \otimes_{\mathcal{O}_X} \mathcal{O}_X(D).$ If *D* is a Weil divisor and *F* is reflexive, then one replaces *F*(*D*) by its reflexive hull (and calls the result still *F*(*D*)).

:::

::: {#o5 .entry cue=term name="|D|"}

**\|*D*|**

The [complete linear system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_linear_system) of a [Weil divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weil_divisor) *D* on a normal complete variety *X* over an algebraically closed field *k*; that is, $|D| = \mathbf{P}(\Gamma(X, \mathcal{O}_X(D)))$. There is a bijection between the set of *k*-rational points of |*D*| and the set of effective Weil divisors on *X* that are linearly equivalent to *D*.[^o6] The same definition is used if *D* is a [Cartier divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartier_divisor) on a complete variety over *k*.

:::

::: {#o7 .entry cue=term name="[X/G]"}

**\[X/G\]**

The [quotient stack](wikipedia://en/40579087) of, say, an algebraic space *X* by an action of a group scheme *G*.

:::

::: {#o8 .entry cue=term name="X/\\!/G"}

**$X/\!/G$**

The [GIT quotient](wikipedia://en/41139312) of a scheme *X* by an [action of a group scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_of_a_group_scheme) *G*.

:::

::: {#o9 .entry cue=term}

An ambiguous notation. It usually means an *n*-th tensor power of *L* but can also mean the self-intersection number of *L*. If $L = \mathcal{O}_X$, the structure sheaf on *X*, then it means the direct sum of *n* copies of $\mathcal{O}_X$.

:::

::: {#o10 .entry cue=term}

The [tautological line bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tautological_line_bundle). It is the dual of [Serre's twisting sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serre's_twisting_sheaf) $\mathcal{O}_X(1)$.

:::

::: {#o11 .entry cue=term}

[Serre's twisting sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serre's_twisting_sheaf). It is the dual of the [tautological line bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tautological_line_bundle) $\mathcal{O}_X(-1)$. It is also called the hyperplane bundle.

:::

::: {#o12 .entry cue=term}

If *D* is an [effective Cartier divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_Cartier_divisor) on *X*, then it is the inverse of the ideal sheaf of *D*.

Most of the times, $\mathcal{O}_X(D)$ is the image of *D* under the natural group homomorphism from the group of Cartier divisors to the Picard group $\operatorname{Pic}(X)$ of *X*, the group of isomorphism classes of line bundles on *X*.

In general, $\mathcal{O}_X(D)$ is the sheaf corresponding to a [Weil divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weil_divisor) *D* (on a [normal scheme](wikipedia://en/2629423)). It need not be locally free, only [reflexive](wikipedia://en/45159116).

If *D* is a $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor, then $\mathcal{O}_X(D)$ is $\mathcal{O}_X$ of the integral part of *D*.

:::

::: {#o13 .entry cue=term}

$\Omega_X^1$ is the sheaf of [Kähler differentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kähler_differentials) on *X*.

$\Omega_X^p$ is the *p*-th [exterior power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_power) of $\Omega_X^1$.

:::

::: {#o14 .entry cue=term}

If *p* is 1, this is the sheaf of [logarithmic Kähler differentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_Kähler_differential) on *X* along *D* (roughly differential forms with simple poles along a divisor *D*.)

$\Omega_X^p(\log D)$ is the *p*-th exterior power of $\Omega_X^1(\log D)$.

:::

::: {#o15 .entry cue=term name="P(V)"}

**P**(*V*)**

The notation is ambiguous. Its traditional meaning is the [projectivization](wikipedia://en/21861125) of a finite-dimensional *k*-vector space *V*; i.e., $\mathbf{P}(V) = \operatorname{Proj}(k[V]) = \operatorname{Proj}(\operatorname{Sym}(V^*))$ (the [Proj](wikipedia://en/2285000) of the [ring of polynomial functions](wikipedia://en/38608533) *k*\[*V*\]) and its *k*-points correspond to lines in *V*. In contrast, Hartshorne and EGA write **P**(*V*) for the Proj of the symmetric algebra of *V*.

:::

::: {#o16 .entry cue=term name="Q-factorial"}

**Q-factorial**

A normal variety is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial if every $\mathbb{Q}$-Weil divisor is $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier.

:::

::: {#o17 .entry cue=term name="Spec(R)"}

**Spec(*R*)**

The set of all prime ideals in a ring *R* with Zariski topology; it is called the [prime spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_spectrum) of *R*.

:::

::: {#o18 .entry cue=term}

The [relative Spec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_Spec) of the *O*<sub>*X*</sub>-algebra *F*. It is also denoted by **Spec**(*F*) or simply Spec(*F*).

:::

::: {#o19 .entry cue=term}

The set of all valuations for a ring *R* with a certain weak topology; it is called the [Berkovich spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkovich_spectrum) of *R*.

:::

## A {#A}

::: {#o21 .entry cue=term name="abelian"}

**abelian**

An [abelian variety](wikipedia://en/247152) is a complete group variety. For example, consider the complex variety $\mathbb{C}^n/\mathbb{Z}^{2n}$ or an elliptic curve $E$ over a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$.

An [abelian scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_scheme) is a (flat) family of abelian varieties.

:::

::: {#o22 .entry cue=term name="adjunction formula"}

**adjunction formula**

If *D* is an effective Cartier divisor on an algebraic variety *X*, both admitting [dualizing sheaves](wikipedia://en/47021614) $\omega_D, \omega_X$, then the [adjunction formula](wikipedia://en/3118117) says: $\omega_D = (\omega_X \otimes \mathcal{O}_X(D))|_D$.

If, in addition, *X* and *D* are smooth, then the formula is equivalent to saying: $K_D = (K_X + D)|_D$ where $K_D, K_X$ are [canonical divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_divisor) on *D* and *X*.

:::

::: {#o23 .entry cue=term name="affine"}

**affine**

[Affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) is roughly a vector space where one has forgotten which point is the origin

An [affine variety](wikipedia://en/320468) is a variety in affine space

An [affine scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_scheme) is a scheme that is the [prime spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_spectrum) of some commutative ring.

A morphism is called **[affine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_morphism)** if the preimage of any open affine subset is again affine. In more fancy terms, affine morphisms are defined by the [global **Spec**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Spec) construction for sheaves of *O<sub>X</sub>*-algebras, defined by analogy with the [spectrum of a ring](wikipedia://en/37291). Important affine morphisms are [vector bundles](wikipedia://en/276524), and [finite morphisms](wikipedia://en/2139357).

The [affine cone](wikipedia://en/50394182) over a closed subvariety *X* of a projective space is the Spec of the homogeneous coordinate ring of *X*.

:::

Algebraic geometry occupied a central place in the mathematics of the last century. The deepest results of Abel, Riemann, Weierstrass, many of the most important papers of Klein and Poincare belong to this domain. At the end of the last and the beginning of the present century the attitude towards algebraic geometry changed abruptly. ... The style of thinking that was fully developed in algebraic geometry at that time was too far removed from the set-theoretical and axiomatic spirit, which then determined the development of mathematics. ... Around the middle of the present century algebraic geometry had undergone to a large extent such a reshaping process. As a result, it can again lay claim to the position it once occupied in mathematics.

::: {#o30 .entry cue=term name="algebraic group"}

**algebraic group**

An [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924) is an algebraic variety that is also a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) in such a way the group operations are morphisms of varieties.

:::

::: {#o31 .entry cue=term name="algebraic scheme"}

**algebraic scheme**

A separated scheme of finite type over a field. For example, an algebraic variety is a reduced irreducible algebraic scheme.

:::

::: {#o32 .entry cue=term name="algebraic set"}

**algebraic set**

An [algebraic set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_set) over a field *k* is a reduced separated scheme of finite type over $\operatorname{Spec}(k)$. An irreducible algebraic set is called an algebraic variety.

:::

::: {#o33 .entry cue=term name="algebraic space"}

**algebraic space**

An [algebraic space](wikipedia://en/2394027) is a quotient of a scheme by the [étale equivalence relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Étale_equivalence_relation).

:::

::: {#o34 .entry cue=term name="algebraic variety"}

**algebraic variety**

An [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) over a field *k* is an integral separated scheme of finite type over $\operatorname{Spec}(k)$. Note, not assuming *k* is algebraically closed causes some pathology; for example, $\operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{C} \times_{\mathbb{R}} \operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{C}$ is not a variety since the coordinate ring $\mathbb{C} \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C}$ is not an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462).

:::

::: {#o35 .entry cue=term name="algebraic vector bundle"}

**algebraic vector bundle**

A [locally free sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_free_sheaf) of a finite rank.

:::

::: {#o36 .entry cue=term name="ample"}

**ample**

A line bundle on a projective variety is [ample](wikipedia://en/1186896) if some tensor power of it is very ample.

:::

::: {#o37 .entry cue=term name="Arakelov geometry"}

**Arakelov geometry**

Algebraic geometry over the compactification of Spec of the [ring of rational integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_rational_integers) $\mathbb{Z}$. See [Arakelov geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arakelov_geometry).[^o38]

:::

::: {#o39 .entry cue=term name="arithmetic genus"}

**arithmetic genus**

The [arithmetic genus](wikipedia://en/1950390) of a projective variety *X* of dimension *r* is $(-1)^r (\chi(\mathcal{O}_X) - 1)$.

:::

::: {#o40 .entry cue=term name="Artin stack"}

**Artin stack**

Another term for an [algebraic stack](wikipedia://en/717377).

:::

::: {#o41 .entry cue=term name="artinian"}

**artinian**

0-dimensional and Noetherian. The definition applies both to a scheme and a ring.

:::

## B {#B}

::: {#o43 .entry cue=term name="base change"}

**base change**

A [fiber product of schemes](wikipedia://en/56444110), in particular one of a *k*-scheme *X* with Spec *E* for a field extension *E*/*k*.

:::

::: {#o44 .entry cue=term name="Behrend function"}

**Behrend function**

The [weighted Euler characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weighted_Euler_characteristic) of a (nice) stack *X* with respect to the [Behrend function](wikipedia://en/50529465) is the degree of the [virtual fundamental class](wikipedia://en/50529391) of *X*.

:::

::: {#o45 .entry cue=term name="Behrend's trace formula"}

**Behrend's trace formula**

[Behrend's trace formula](wikipedia://en/41791807) generalizes [Grothendieck's trace formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck's_trace_formula); both formulas compute the trace of the [Frobenius](wikipedia://en/6988121) on *l*-adic cohomology.

:::

::: {#o46 .entry cue=term name="big"}

**big**

A [big line bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_line_bundle) *L* on *X* of dimension *n* is a line bundle such that $\displaystyle \limsup_{l \to \infty} \operatorname{dim} \Gamma(X, L^l) / l^n > 0$.

:::

::: {#o47 .entry cue=term name="birational morphism"}

**birational morphism**

A [birational morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birational_morphism) between schemes is a morphism that becomes an isomorphism after restricted to some open dense subset. One of the most common examples of a birational map is the map induced by a blowup.

:::

::: {#o48 .entry cue=term name="blow-up"}

**blow-up**

A [blow-up](wikipedia://en/1817790) is a birational transformation that replaces a closed subscheme with an effective Cartier divisor. Precisely, given a noetherian scheme *X* and a closed subscheme $Z \subset X$, the blow-up of *X* along *Z* is a proper morphism $\pi: \widetilde{X} \to X$ such that (1) $\pi^{-1}(Z) \hookrightarrow \widetilde{X}$ is an effective Cartier divisor, called the [exceptional divisor](wikipedia://en/14271694), and (2) $\pi$ is universal with respect to (1). Concretely, it is constructed as the relative Proj of the Rees algebra of $O_X$ with respect to the ideal sheaf determining *Z*.

:::

## C {#C}

::: {#o50 .entry cue=term name="Calabi–Yau"}

**Calabi–Yau**

The [Calabi–Yau metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calabi–Yau_metric) is a Kähler metric whose Ricci curvature is zero.

:::

::: {#o51 .entry cue=term name="canonical"}

**canonical**

The [canonical sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_sheaf) on a normal variety *X* of dimension *n* is $\omega_X = i_* \Omega_U^n$ where *i* is the inclusion of the [smooth locus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_locus) *U* and $\Omega_U^n$ is the sheaf of differential forms on *U* of degree *n*. If the base field has characteristic zero instead of normality, then one may replace *i* by a resolution of singularities.

The [canonical class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_class) $K_X$ on a normal variety *X* is the divisor class such that $\mathcal{O}_X(K_X) = \omega_X$.

The [canonical divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_divisor) is a representative of the canonical class $K_X$ denoted by the same symbol (and not well-defined.)

The [canonical ring](wikipedia://en/1866872) of a normal variety *X* is the section ring of the [canonical sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_sheaf).

:::

::: {#o52 .entry cue=term name="canonical model"}

**canonical model**

The [canonical model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_model_%28algebraic_geometry%29) is the [Proj](wikipedia://en/2285000) of a canonical ring (assuming the ring is finitely generated.)

:::

::: {#o53 .entry cue=term name="Cartier"}

**Cartier**

An effective [Cartier divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartier_divisor) *D* on a scheme *X* over *S* is a closed subscheme of *X* that is flat over *S* and whose ideal sheaf is invertible (locally free of rank one).

:::

::: {#o54 .entry cue=term name="Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity"}

**Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity**

The [Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity](wikipedia://en/24560170) of a coherent sheaf *F* on a projective space $f: \mathbf{P}^n_S \to S$ over a scheme *S* is the smallest integer *r* such that

$R^i f_*F(r-i) = 0$ for all *i* > 0.

:::

::: {#o55 .entry cue=term name="catenary"}

**catenary**

A scheme is [catenary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catenary_%28ring_theory%29), if all chains between two irreducible closed subschemes have the same length. Examples include virtually everything, e.g. varieties over a field, and it is hard to construct examples that are not catenary.

:::

::: {#o56 .entry cue=term name="central fiber"}

**central fiber**

A special fiber.

:::

::: {#o57 .entry cue=term name="Chow group"}

**Chow group**

The *k*-th [Chow group](wikipedia://en/4282051) $A_k(X)$ of a smooth variety *X* is the free abelian group generated by closed subvarieties of dimension *k* (group of *k*-[cycles](wikipedia://en/9520954)) modulo [rational equivalences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_equivalence).

:::

::: {#o58 .entry cue=term name="classification"}

**classification**

[Classification](wikipedia://en/4576710) is a guiding principle in all of mathematics where one tries to describe all objects satisfying certain properties up to given equivalences by more accessible data such as [invariants](wikipedia://en/9903220) or even some constructive process. In algebraic geometry one distinguishes between discrete and continuous invariants. For continuous classifying invariants one additionally attempts to provide some geometric structure which leads to [moduli spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_spaces).

[Complete](wikipedia://en/1047627) [smooth](wikipedia://en/981915) [curves](wikipedia://en/253260) over an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) are classified up to [rational equivalence](wikipedia://en/382733#Birational_equivalence_and_rationality) by their [genus](wikipedia://en/61891#Algebraic_geometry) $g$. (a) $g=0$. [rational](wikipedia://en/865939) curves, i.e. the curve is [birational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birational) to the projective line $\mathbb{P}^1$. (b) $g=1$. [Elliptic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curves), i.e. the curve is a complete 1-dimensional [group scheme](wikipedia://en/510523) after choosing any point on the curve as identity. (c) $g\geq2$. **Hyperbolic curves**, also called **curves of general type**. See [algebraic curves for examples](wikipedia://en/253260#Examples_of_curves). The classification of smooth curves can be refined by the [degree](wikipedia://en/2776558) for [projectively embedded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_embedding) curves, in particular when restricted to [plane curves](wikipedia://en/253260#Projective_plane_curves). Note that all complete smooth curves are projective in the sense that they admit embeddings into projective space, but for the degree to be well-defined a choice of such an embedding has to be explicitly specified. The arithmetic of a complete smooth curve over a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) (in particular number and structure of its rational points) is governed by the classification of the associated curve [base changed](wikipedia://en/56444110#Interpretations_and_special_cases) to an algebraic closure. See [Faltings' theorem](wikipedia://en/172199#Background) for details on the arithmetic implications.

Classification of complete smooth [surfaces](wikipedia://en/648311) over an algebraically closed field up to rational equivalence. See an [overview of the classification](wikipedia://en/648311#Classification_by_the_Kodaira_dimension) or [Enriques–Kodaira classification](wikipedia://en/3029260) for details.

Classification of [singularities](wikipedia://en/981915) resp. associated [Zariski neighboorhoods](wikipedia://en/241744#Zariski_topology_of_varieties) over algebraically closed fields up to isomorphism. (a) In characteristic 0 [Hironaka's resolution result](wikipedia://en/9914115#Hironaka's_method_2) attaches invariants to a singularity which classify them. (b) For curves and surfaces resolution is known in any characteristic which also yields a classification. See [here for curves](wikipedia://en/253260#Classification_of_singularities) or [here for curves and surfaces](wikipedia://en/9914115#Resolution_for_schemes_and_status_of_the_problem).

Classification of [Fano varieties](wikipedia://en/3095929#Classification_in_small_dimensions) in small dimension.

The [minimal model program](wikipedia://en/15148924) is an approach to birational classification of complete smooth varieties in higher dimension (at least 2). While the original goal is about smooth varieties, [terminal singularites](wikipedia://en/22910728) naturally appear and are part of a wider classification.

Classification of [split reductive groups](wikipedia://en/1088386#Classification_of_split_reductive_groups) up to isomorphism over algebraically closed fields.

:::

::: {#o59 .entry cue=term name="classifying stack"}

**classifying stack**

An analog of a [classifying space](wikipedia://en/923556) for [torsors](wikipedia://en/41910974) in algebraic geometry; see [classifying stack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classifying_stack).

:::

::: {#o60 .entry cue=term name="closed"}

**closed**

**Closed subschemes** of a scheme *X* are defined to be those occurring in the following construction. Let *J* be a [quasi-coherent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-coherent_sheaf) sheaf of $\mathcal{O}_X$-[ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheaf_of_ideals). The [support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Support_of_a_sheaf) of the [quotient sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_sheaf) $\mathcal{O}_X/J$ is a closed subset *Z* of *X* and $(Z,(\mathcal{O}_X/J)|_Z)$ is a scheme called the **closed subscheme defined by the quasi-coherent sheaf of ideals *J***.[^o61] The reason the definition of closed subschemes relies on such a construction is that, unlike open subsets, a closed subset of a scheme does not have a unique structure as a subscheme.

:::

A scheme is called Cohen-Macaulay if all local rings are [Cohen-Macaulay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohen-Macaulay_ring). For example, regular schemes, and *Spec k*\[*x,y*\]/(*xy*) are Cohen–Macaulay, but

:::

::: {#o63 .entry cue=term name="coherent sheaf"}

**coherent sheaf**

A [coherent sheaf](wikipedia://en/603780) on a Noetherian scheme *X* is a quasi-coherent sheaf that is finitely generated as *O*<sub>*X*</sub>-module.

:::

::: {#o64 .entry cue=term name="conic"}

**conic**

An [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) of degree two.

:::

::: {#o65 .entry cue=term name="connected"}

**connected**

The scheme is *[connected](wikipedia://en/6233)* as a topological space. Since the [connected components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28topology%29) refine the [irreducible components](wikipedia://en/2652476) any irreducible scheme is connected but not vice versa. An [affine scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_scheme) *Spec(R)* is connected [iff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iff) the ring *R* possesses no [idempotents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent) other than 0 and 1; such a ring is also called a **connected ring**.

Examples of connected schemes include [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834), [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135), and an example of a scheme that is not connected is *Spec*(*k*\[*x*\]×*k*\[*x*\])

:::

::: {#o66 .entry cue=term name="compactification"}

**compactification**

See for example [Nagata's compactification theorem](wikipedia://en/37613671).

:::

::: {#o67 .entry cue=term name="Cox ring"}

**Cox ring**

A generalization of a homogeneous coordinate ring. See [Cox ring](wikipedia://en/42058042).

:::

::: {#o68 .entry cue=term name="crepant"}

**crepant**

A [crepant morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crepant_morphism) $f: X \to Y$ between normal varieties is a morphism such that $f^* \omega_Y = \omega_X$.

:::

::: {#o69 .entry cue=term name="curve"}

**curve**

An algebraic variety of dimension one.

## D {#D}

::: {#o71 .entry cue=term name="deformation"}

**deformation**

Let $S \to S'$ be a morphism of schemes and *X* an *S*-scheme. Then a deformation *X*' of *X* is an *S*'-scheme together with a pullback square in which *X* is the pullback of *X*' (typically *X*' is assumed to be [flat](wikipedia://en/542520)).

:::

::: {#o72 .entry cue=term name="degeneracy locus"}

**degeneracy locus**

Given a vector-bundle map $f: E \to F$ over a variety *X* (that is, a scheme *X*-morphism between the total spaces of the bundles), the [degeneracy locus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degeneracy_locus) is the (scheme-theoretic) locus $X_k(f) = \{ x \in X | \operatorname{rk}(f(x)) \le k \}$.

:::

::: {#o73 .entry cue=term name="degeneration"}

**degeneration**

A scheme *X* is said to [degenerate](wikipedia://en/50650350) to a scheme $X_0$ (called the limit of *X*) if there is a scheme $\pi: Y \to \mathbf{A}^1$ with [generic fiber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generic_fiber) *X* and [special fiber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_fiber) $X_0$.

A [flat degeneration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_degeneration) is a degeneration such that $\pi$ is flat.

::: {#o74 .entry cue=term name="dimension"}

**dimension**

The [dimension](wikipedia://en/253251), by definition the maximal length of a chain of irreducible closed subschemes, is a global property. It can be seen locally if a scheme is irreducible. It depends only on the topology, not on the structure sheaf. See also [Global dimension](wikipedia://en/2291739).

Examples: [equidimensional schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equidimensional_scheme) in dimension 0: [Artinian](wikipedia://en/489273) schemes, 1: [algebraic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_curves), 2: [algebraic surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_surfaces).

::: {#o75 .entry cue=term name="degree"}

**degree**

The degree of a line bundle *L* on a complete variety is an integer *d* such that $\chi(L^{\otimes m}) = {d \over n!} m^n + O(m^{n-1})$.

If *x* is a cycle on a complete variety $f: X \to \operatorname{Spec} k$ over a field *k*, then its degree is $f_*(x) \in A_0(\operatorname{Spec} k) = \mathbb{Z}$.

For the degree of a finite morphism, see [morphism of varieties#Degree of a finite morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphism_of_varieties#Degree_of_a_finite_morphism).

::: {#o77 .entry cue=term name="divisorial"}

**divisorial**

A [divisorial sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisorial_sheaf) on a normal variety is a reflexive sheaf of the form *O*<sub>*X*</sub>(*D*) for some [Weil divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weil_divisor) *D*.

A [divisorial scheme](wikipedia://en/62736148) is a scheme admitting an ample family of invertible sheaves. A scheme admitting an ample invertible sheaf is a basic example.

**dominant**

A morphism *f* : *X* → *Y* is called *[dominant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominant_morphism)*, if the image *f*(*X*) is [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594). A morphism of affine schemes *Spec A* → *Spec B* is dense if and only if the kernel of the corresponding map *B* → *A* is contained in the nilradical of *B*.

**dualizing sheaf**

::: {#o93 .entry cue=term name="fiber"}

**fiber**

Given $f: X \to Y$ between schemes, the fiber of *f* over *y* is, as a set, the pre-image $f^{-1}(y) = \{x \in X|f(x)=y\}$; it has the natural structure of a scheme over the [residue field](wikipedia://en/10789680) of *y* as the fiber product $X \times_Y \{y\}$, where $\{y\}$ has the natural structure of a scheme over *Y* as Spec of the residue field of *y*.

::: {#o94 .entry cue=term name="fiber product"}

**fiber product**

Another term for the "[pullback](wikipedia://en/841689)" in the category theory. In particular, [fiber product of schemes](wikipedia://en/56444110).

A stack $F \times_G H$ given for $f: F \to G, g:H \to G$: an object over *B* is a triple (*x*, *y*, ψ), *x* in *F*(*B*), *y* in *H*(*B*), ψ an isomorphism $f(x) \overset{\sim}\to g(y)$ in *G*(*B*); an arrow from (*x*, *y*, ψ) to (*x**,*****y*', ψ') is a pair of morphisms $\alpha: x \to x', \beta: y \to y'$ such that $\psi' \circ f(\alpha) = g(\beta) \circ \psi$. The resulting square with obvious projections*does not*commute; rather, it commutes up to natural isomorphism; i.e., it [2-commutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-commute).***

:::

::: {#o95 .entry cue=term name="final"}

**final**

One of Grothendieck's fundamental ideas is to emphasize *relative* notions, i.e. conditions on morphisms rather than conditions on schemes themselves. The category of schemes has a [final object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Final_object), the spectrum of the ring $\mathbb{Z}$ of integers; so that any scheme $S$ is *over* $\textrm{Spec} (\mathbb{Z})$, and in a unique way.

::: {#o96 .entry cue=term name="finite"}

**finite**

The morphism *f* : *Y* → *X* is **finite** if $X$ may be covered by affine open sets $\text{Spec }B$ such that each $f^{-1}(\text{Spec }B)$ is affine — say of the form $\text{Spec }A$ — and furthermore $A$ is finitely generated as a $B$-module. See [finite morphism](wikipedia://en/2139357).

Finite morphisms are quasi-finite, but not all morphisms having finite fibers are quasi-finite, and morphisms of finite type are usually not quasi-finite.

:::

::: {#o97 .entry cue=term name="finite type (locally)"}

**finite type (locally)**

The morphism *f* : *Y* → *X* is **locally of finite type** if $X$ may be covered by affine open sets $\text{Spec }B$ such that each inverse image $f^{-1}(\text{Spec }B)$ is covered by affine open sets $\text{Spec }A$ where each $A$ is finitely generated as a $B$-algebra.

The morphism *f* : *Y* → *X* is [of finite type](wikipedia://en/70728366) if $X$ may be covered by affine open sets $\text{Spec }B$ such that each inverse image $f^{-1}(\text{Spec }B)$ is covered by finitely many affine open sets $\text{Spec }A$ where each $A$ is finitely generated as a $B$-algebra.

:::

::: {#o98 .entry cue=term name="finite fibers"}

**finite fibers**

The morphism *f* : *Y* → *X* has **finite fibers** if the fiber over each point $x \in X$ is a finite set. A morphism is **[quasi-finite](wikipedia://en/2143056)** if it is of finite type and has finite fibers.

:::

::: {#o99 .entry cue=term name="finite presentation"}

**finite presentation**

If *y* is a point of *Y*, then the morphism *f* is **of finite presentation at *y*** (or **finitely presented at *y***) if there is an open affine neighborhood *U* of *f(y)* and an open affine neighbourhood *V* of *y* such that *f*(*V*) ⊆ *U* and $\mathcal{O}_Y(V)$ is a [finitely presented algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_presented_algebra) over $\mathcal{O}_X(U)$. The morphism *f* is **locally of finite presentation** if it is finitely presented at all points of *Y*. If *X* is locally Noetherian, then *f* is locally of finite presentation if, and only if, it is locally of finite type.[^o100]

The morphism *f* : *Y* → *X* is **of finite presentation** (or ***Y* is finitely presented over *X***) if it is locally of finite presentation, quasi-compact, and quasi-separated. If *X* is locally Noetherian, then *f* is of finite presentation if, and only if, it is of finite type.[^o101]

**flat**

## I {#I}

::: {#o132 .entry cue=term name="image"}

**image**

If *f* : *Y* → *X* is any morphism of schemes, the **scheme-theoretic image** of *f* is the unique *closed* subscheme *i* : *Z* → *X* which satisfies the following [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245):

*f* factors through *i*,

if *j* : *Z*′ → *X* is any closed subscheme of *X* such that *f* factors through *j*, then *i* also factors through *j*.[^o133][^o134] This notion is distinct from that of the usual set-theoretic image of *f*, *f*(*Y*). For example, the underlying space of *Z* always contains (but is not necessarily equal to) the Zariski closure of *f*(*Y*) in *X*, so if *Y* is any open (and not closed) subscheme of *X* and *f* is the inclusion map, then *Z* is different from *f*(*Y*). When *Y* is reduced, then *Z* is the Zariski closure of *f*(*Y*) endowed with the structure of reduced closed subscheme. But in general, unless *f* is quasi-compact, the construction of *Z* is not local on *X*.

:::

::: {#o135 .entry cue=term name="immersion"}

**immersion**

**Immersions** *f* : *Y* → *X* are maps that factor through isomorphisms with subschemes. Specifically, an **open immersion** factors through an isomorphism with an open subscheme and a **[closed immersion](wikipedia://en/1623986)** factors through an isomorphism with a closed subscheme.[^o136] Equivalently, *f* is a closed immersion if, and only if, it induces a homeomorphism from the underlying topological space of *Y* to a closed subset of the underlying topological space of *X*, and if the morphism $f^\sharp: \mathcal{O}_X \to f_* \mathcal{O}_Y$ is surjective.[^o178] A composition of immersions is again an immersion.[^o137]

Some authors, such as Hartshorne in his book *Algebraic Geometry* and Q. Liu in his book *Algebraic Geometry and Arithmetic Curves*, define immersions as the composite of an open immersion followed by a closed immersion. These immersions are immersions in the sense above, but the converse is false. Furthermore, under this definition, the composite of two immersions is not necessarily an immersion. However, the two definitions are equivalent when *f* is quasi-compact.[^o138]

Note that an open immersion is completely described by its image in the sense of topological spaces, while a closed immersion is not: $\operatorname{Spec} A/I$ and $\operatorname{Spec} A/J$ may be homeomorphic but not isomorphic. This happens, for example, if *I* is the radical of *J* but *J* is not a radical ideal. When specifying a closed subset of a scheme without mentioning the scheme structure, usually the so-called *reduced* scheme structure is meant, that is, the scheme structure corresponding to the unique radical ideal consisting of all functions vanishing on that closed subset.

::: {#o155 .entry cue=term name="local"}

**local**

Most important properties of schemes are *local in nature*, i.e. a scheme *X* has a certain property *P* if and only if for any cover of *X* by open subschemes *X<sub>i</sub>*, i.e. *X*=$\cup$ *X<sub>i</sub>*, every *X<sub>i</sub>* has the property *P*. It is usually the case that it is enough to check one cover, not all possible ones. One also says that a certain property is *Zariski-local*, if one needs to distinguish between the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744) and other possible topologies, like the [étale topology](wikipedia://en/5707590).

Consider a scheme *X* and a cover by affine open subschemes *Spec A<sub>i</sub>*. Using the dictionary between [(commutative) rings](wikipedia://en/61346) and [affine schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_scheme) local properties are thus properties of the rings *A<sub>i</sub>*. A property *P* is local in the above sense, iff the corresponding property of rings is stable under [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring).

For example, we can speak of *[locally Noetherian](wikipedia://en/2783891)* schemes, namely those which are covered by the spectra of [Noetherian rings](wikipedia://en/56129). The fact that localizations of a Noetherian ring are still noetherian then means that the property of a scheme of being locally Noetherian is local in the above sense (whence the name). Another example: if a ring is [reduced](wikipedia://en/539749) (i.e., has no non-zero [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) elements), then so are its localizations.

An example for a non-local property is *separatedness* (see below for the definition). Any affine scheme is separated, therefore any scheme is locally separated. However, the affine pieces may glue together pathologically to yield a non-separated scheme.

The following is a (non-exhaustive) list of local properties of rings, which are applied to schemes. Let *X* = $\cup$ *Spec A<sub>i</sub>* be a covering of a scheme by open affine subschemes. For definiteness, let *k* denote a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) in the following. Most of the examples also work with the integers **Z** as a base, though, or even more general bases. Connected, irreducible, reduced, integral, normal, regular, Cohen-Macaulay, locally noetherian, dimension, catenary, Gorenstein.

**moduli**

See for example [moduli space](wikipedia://en/361609).While much of the early work on moduli, especially since \[Mum65\], put the emphasis on the construction of fine or coarse moduli spaces, recently the emphasis shifted towards the study of the families of varieties, that is towards moduli functors and moduli stacks. ::: {#o172 .entry cue=term name="normal"}

**normal**

An integral scheme is called *[normal](wikipedia://en/2629423)*, if the local rings are [integrally closed domains](wikipedia://en/21420209). For example, all regular schemes are normal, while singular curves are not.

A smooth curve $C \subset \mathbf{P}^r$ is said to be *k*-normal if the hypersurfaces of degree *k* cut out the complete linear series $|\mathcal{O}_C(k)|$. It is [projectively normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectively_normal) if it is *k*-normal for all *k* > 0. One thus says that "a curve is projectively normal if the linear system that embeds it is complete." The term "linearly normal" is synonymous with 1-normal.

A closed subvariety $X \subset \mathbf{P}^r$ is said to be projectively normal if the [affine cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_cover) over *X* is a [normal scheme](wikipedia://en/2629423); i.e., the homogeneous coordinate ring of *X* is an integrally closed domain. This meaning is consistent with that of 2.

::: {#o173 .entry cue=term name="normal"}

**normal**

If *X* is a closed subscheme of a scheme *Y* with ideal sheaf *I*, then the [normal sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_sheaf) to *X* is $(I/I^2)^* = \mathcal{H}om_{\mathcal{O}_Y}(I/I^2, \mathcal{O}_Y)$. If the embedded of *X* into *Y* is [regular](wikipedia://en/47089144), it is locally free and is called the [normal bundle](wikipedia://en/2370200).

The [normal cone](wikipedia://en/2370200#Algebraic_geometry) to *X* is $\operatorname{Spec}_X(\oplus_0^{\infty} I^n/I^{n+1})$. if *X* is regularly embedded into *Y*, then the normal cone is isomorphic to $\operatorname{Spec}_X(\mathcal{S}ym(I/I^2))$, the total space of the normal bundle to *X*.

**normally generated**

::: {#o188 .entry cue=term name="point"}

**point**

A scheme $S$ is a [locally ringed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_ringed_space), so *a fortiori* a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), but the meanings of *point of $S$* are threefold:

a point $P$ of the underlying topological space;

a $T$-valued point of $S$ is a morphism from $T$ to $S$, for any scheme $T$;

a *geometric point*, where $S$ is defined over (is equipped with a morphism to) $\textrm{Spec}(K)$, where $K$ is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), is a morphism from $\textrm{Spec} (\overline{K})$ to $S$ where $\overline{K}$ is an [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129) of $K$.

Geometric points are what in the most classical cases, for example [algebraic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_varieties) that are [complex manifolds](wikipedia://en/509742), would be the ordinary-sense points. The points $P$ of the underlying space include analogues of the [generic points](wikipedia://en/3043978) (in the sense of [Zariski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski), not that of [André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019)), which specialise to ordinary-sense points. The $T$-valued points are thought of, via [Yoneda's lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoneda's_lemma), as a way of identifying $S$ with the [representable functor](wikipedia://en/844461) $h_{S}$ it sets up. Historically there was a process by which [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) added more points (*e.g.* complex points, [line at infinity](wikipedia://en/403142)) to simplify the geometry by refining the basic objects. The $T$-valued points were a massive further step.

As part of the predominating [Grothendieck approach](wikipedia://en/1816636), there are three corresponding notions of *fiber* of a morphism: the first being the simple [inverse image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_image) of a point. The other two are formed by creating [fiber products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber_product) of two morphisms. For example, a **geometric fiber** of a morphism $S^{\prime} \to S$ is thought of as $S^{\prime} \times_{S} \textrm{Spec}(\overline{K})$. This makes the extension from [affine schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_scheme), where it is just the [tensor product of R-algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_product_of_R-algebras), to all schemes of the fiber product operation a significant (if technically anodyne) result.

::: {#o191 .entry cue=term name="projection formula"}

**projection formula**

The [projection formula](wikipedia://en/38275478) says that, for a morphism $f:X \to Y$ of schemes, an $\mathcal{O}_X$-module $\mathcal{F}$ and a [locally free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_free_sheaf) $\mathcal{O}_Y$-module $\mathcal{E}$ of finite rank, there is a natural isomorphism $f_* (F \otimes f^* E) = (f_* F) \otimes E$ (in short, $f_*$ is linear with respect to the action of locally free sheaves.)

::: {#o192 .entry cue=term name="projective"}

**projective**

A [projective variety](wikipedia://en/320469) is a closed subvariety of a projective space.

A [projective scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_scheme) over a scheme *S* is an *S*-scheme that factors through some projective space $\mathbf{P}^N_S \to S$ as a closed subscheme.

Projective morphisms are defined similarly to affine morphisms: *f* : *Y* → *X* is called **[projective](wikipedia://en/320469)** if it factors as a closed immersion followed by the projection of a [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) $\mathbb{P}^{n}_X := \mathbb{P}^n \times_{\mathrm{Spec}\mathbb Z} X$ to $X$.[^o193] Note that this definition is more restrictive than that of [EGA](wikipedia://en/471387), II.5.5.2. The latter defines $f$ to be projective if it is given by the [global **Proj**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Proj) of a quasi-coherent [graded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded_algebra) *O<sub>X</sub>*-algebra $\mathcal S$ such that $\mathcal S_1$ is finitely generated and generates the algebra $\mathcal S$. Both definitions coincide when $X$ is affine or more generally if it is quasi-compact, separated and admits an ample sheaf,[^o194] e.g. if $X$ is an open subscheme of a projective space $\mathbb P^n_A$ over a ring $A$.

:::

::: {#o195 .entry cue=term name="projective bundle"}

**projective bundle**

If *E* is a locally free sheaf on a scheme *X*, the [projective bundle](wikipedia://en/35183882) **P**(*E*) of *E* is the [global Proj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Proj) of the symmetric algebra of the dual of *E*: $\mathbf{P}(E) = \mathbf{Proj}(\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathcal{O}_X}(E^{\vee})).$ Note this definition is standard nowadays (e.g., Fulton's *Intersection theory*) but differs from EGA and Hartshorne (they don't take a dual).

::: {#o197 .entry cue=term name="proper"}

**proper**

A morphism is **[proper](wikipedia://en/603782)** if it is separated, *universally closed* (i.e. such that fiber products with it are closed maps), and of finite type. Projective morphisms are proper; but the converse is not in general true. See also [complete variety](wikipedia://en/1047627). A deep property of proper morphisms is the existence of a *[Stein factorization](wikipedia://en/1205940)*, namely the existence of an intermediate scheme such that a morphism can be expressed as one with connected fibres, followed by a finite morphism.

**property P**

Let **P** be a property of a scheme that is stable under base change (finite-type, proper, smooth, étale, etc.). Then a representable morphism $f: F \to G$ is said to have property **P** if, for any $B \to G$ with *B* a scheme, the base change $F \times_G B \to B$ has property **P**.

**rational**

Over an algebraically closed field, a variety is [rational](wikipedia://en/865939) if it is birational to a projective space. Given a field *k* and a relative scheme *X* → *S*, a [*k*-rational point](wikipedia://en/2960200) of *X* is an *S*-morphism $\operatorname{Spec}(k) \to X$.

::: {#o213 .entry cue=term name="rational function"}

**rational function**

An element in the [function field](wikipedia://en/2783843) $k(X) = \varinjlim k[U]$ where the limit runs over all coordinates rings of open subsets *U* of an (irreducible) algebraic variety *X*. See also [function field (scheme theory)](wikipedia://en/381301).

::: {#o214 .entry cue=term name="rational normal curve"}

**rational normal curve**

A [rational normal curve](wikipedia://en/2380117) is the image of $\mathbf{P}^1 \to \mathbf{P}^d, \, (s:t) \mapsto (s^d : s^{d-1} t : \cdots : t^d)$. If *d* = 3, it is also called the [twisted cubic](wikipedia://en/1547121).

::: {#o215 .entry cue=term name="rational singularities"}

**rational singularities**

A variety *X* over a field of characteristic zero has [rational singularities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_singularities) if there is a resolution of singularities $f:X' \to X$ such that $f_*(\mathcal{O}_{X'}) = \mathcal{O}_X$ and $R^i f_*(\mathcal{O}_{X'}) = 0, \, i \ge 1$.

::: {#o216 .entry cue=term name="reduced"}

**reduced**

A commutative ring $R$ is [reduced](wikipedia://en/539749) if it has no nonzero nilpotent elements, i.e., its nilradical is the zero ideal, $\sqrt{(0)} = (0)$. Equivalently, $R$ is reduced if $\operatorname{Spec}(R)$ is a reduced scheme.

A scheme X is reduced if its stalks $\mathcal{O}_{X,x}$ are reduced rings. Equivalently X is reduced if, for each open subset $U \subset X$, $\mathcal{O}_X(U)$ is a reduced ring, i.e., $X$ has no nonzero nilpotent sections.

::: {#o224 .entry cue=term name="Riemann–Hurwitz formula"}

**Riemann–Hurwitz formula**

Given a finite separable morphism $\pi:X \to Y$ between smooth projective curves, if $\pi$ is [tamely ramified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamely_ramified) (no wild ramification), for example, over a field of characteristic zero, then the [Riemann–Hurwitz formula](wikipedia://en/497413) relates the degree of π, the genera of *X*, *Y* and the [ramification indices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramification_index): $2g(X) - 2 = \operatorname{deg}(\pi) (2g(Y) - 2) + \sum_{y \in Y} (e_y - 1)$. Nowadays, the formula is viewed as a consequence of the more general formula (which is valid even if π is not tame): $K_X \sim \pi^*K_Y + R$ where $\sim$ means a [linear equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_equivalence) and $R = \sum_{P \in X} \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_P} (\Omega_{X/Y})P$ is the divisor of the relative cotangent sheaf $\Omega_{X/Y}$ (called the [different](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Different_%28mathematics%29)).

:::

::: {#o225 .entry cue=term name="Riemann–Roch formula"}

**Riemann–Roch formula**

If *L* is a line bundle of degree *d* on a smooth projective curve of genus *g*, then the [Riemann–Roch formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann–Roch_formula) computes the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) of *L*: $\chi(L) = d - g + 1$. For example, the formula implies the degree of the canonical divisor *K* is 2*g* - 2.

The general version is due to Grothendieck and called the [Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch_formula). It says: if $\pi:X \to S$ is a proper morphism with smooth *X*, *S* and if *E* is a vector bundle on *X*, then as equality in the rational [Chow group](wikipedia://en/4282051) $\operatorname{ch}(\pi_! E) \cdot \operatorname{td}(S) = \pi_*(\operatorname{ch}(E) \cdot \operatorname{td}(X))$ where $\pi_! = \sum_i (-1)^i R^i \pi_*$, $\operatorname{ch}$ means a [Chern character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chern_character) and $\operatorname{td}$ a [Todd class](wikipedia://en/1568489) of the tangent bundle of a space, and, over the complex numbers, $\pi_*$ is an [integration along fibers](wikipedia://en/40730681). For example, if the base *S* is a point, *X* is a smooth curve of genus *g* and *E* is a line bundle *L*, then the left-hand side reduces to the Euler characteristic while the right-hand side is $\pi_*(e^{c_1(L)}(1-c_1(T^*X)/2)) = \operatorname{deg}(L) - g + 1.$

:::

::: {#o226 .entry cue=term name="rigid"}

**rigid**

Every [infinitesimal deformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal_deformation) is trivial. For example, the [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) is rigid since $\operatorname{H}^1(\mathbf{P}^n, T_{\mathbf{P}^n}) = 0$ (and using the [Kodaira–Spencer map](wikipedia://en/35205007)).

:::

::: {#o227 .entry cue=term name="rigidify"}

**rigidify**

A heuristic term, roughly equivalent to "killing automorphisms". For example, one might say "we introduce [level structures](wikipedia://en/47016783) resp. [marked points](wikipedia://en/3010057#Moduli_of_marked_curves) to rigidify the geometric situation."

:::

## S {#S}

On Grothendieck's own view there should be almost no history of schemes, but only a history of the resistance to them: ... There is no serious historical question of how Grothendieck found his definition of schemes. It was in the air. Serre has well said that no one invented schemes (conversation 1995). The question is, what made Grothendieck believe he should use this definition to simplify an 80 page paper by Serre into some 1000 pages of *[Éléments de géométrie algébrique](wikipedia://en/471387)*?

::: {#o229 .entry cue=term name="scheme"}

**scheme**

A [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) is a [locally ringed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_ringed_space) that is locally a [prime spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_spectrum) of a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346).

:::

::: {#o230 .entry cue=term name="Schubert"}

**Schubert**

A [Schubert cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schubert_cell) is a *B*-orbit on the Grassmannian $\operatorname{Gr}(d, n)$ where *B* is the standard Borel; i.e., the group of upper triangular matrices.

A [Schubert variety](wikipedia://en/10469987) is the closure of a Schubert cell.

:::

::: {#o231 .entry cue=term name="scroll"}

**scroll**

A [rational normal scroll](wikipedia://en/34956805) is a [ruled surface](wikipedia://en/705158#Ruled_surfaces_in_algebraic_geometry) which is of [degree](wikipedia://en/2776558) $n$ in a [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135#Algebraic_geometry) $\mathbb{P}^{n+1}$ for some $n\in\mathbb{N}_{>1}$.

:::

::: {#o232 .entry cue=term name="secant variety"}

**secant variety**

The [secant variety](wikipedia://en/17761472) to a projective variety $V \subset \mathbb{P}^r$ is the closure of the union of all secant lines to *V* in $\mathbb{P}^r$.

:::

::: {#o233 .entry cue=term name="section ring"}

**section ring**

The [section ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_ring) or the ring of sections of a line bundle *L* on a scheme *X* is the graded ring $\oplus_0^\infty \Gamma(X, L^n)$.

:::

::: {#o234 .entry cue=term}

See [Serre's conditions on normality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serre's_conditions_on_normality). See also https://mathoverflow.net/q/22228

:::

::: {#o235 .entry cue=term name="Serre duality"}

**Serre duality**

See #dualizing sheaf

:::

::: {#o236 .entry cue=term name="separated"}

**separated**

A [separated morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separated_morphism) is a morphism $f$ such that the [fiber product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber_product) of $f$ with itself along $f$ has its [diagonal](wikipedia://en/349251) as a closed subscheme — in other words, the [diagonal morphism](wikipedia://en/58638781) is a *[closed immersion](wikipedia://en/1623986)*.

:::

::: {#o237 .entry cue=term name="sheaf generated by global sections"}

**sheaf generated by global sections**

A sheaf with a set of global sections that span the stalk of the sheaf at every point. See [Sheaf generated by global sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheaf_generated_by_global_sections).

:::

::: {#o238 .entry cue=term name="simple"}

**simple**

The term "simple point" is an old term for a "smooth point".

A [simple normal crossing (snc) divisor](wikipedia://en/23634474) is another name for a smooth normal crossing divisor, i.e. a divisor that has only smooth normal crossing singularities. They appear in [strong desingularization](wikipedia://en/9914115#Definitions) as well as in stabilization for compactifying moduli problems.

In the context of [linear algebraic groups](wikipedia://en/395885) there are [semisimple groups](wikipedia://en/395885#Semisimple_and_reductive_groups) and [simple groups](wikipedia://en/1088386#Simple_reductive_groups) which are themselves semisimple groups with additional properties. Since all simple groups are reductive, a split simple group is a simple group that is split-reductive.

:::

::: {#o239 .entry cue=term name="smooth"}

**smooth**

The higher-dimensional analog of étale morphisms are *smooth morphisms*. There are many different characterisations of smoothness. The following are equivalent definitions of smoothness of the morphism *f* : *Y* → *X*:

for any *y* ∈ *Y*, there are open affine neighborhoods *V* and *U* of *y*, *x*=*f*(*y*), respectively, such that the restriction of *f* to *V* factors as an étale morphism followed by the projection of [affine *n*-space](wikipedia://en/298834) over *U*.

*f* is flat, locally of finite presentation, and for every geometric point $\bar{y}$ of *Y* (a morphism from the spectrum of an algebraically closed field $k(\bar{y})$ to *Y*), the geometric fiber $X_{\bar{y}}:=X\times_Y \mathrm{Spec} (k(\bar{y}))$ is a smooth *n*-dimensional variety over $k(\bar{y})$ in the sense of classical algebraic geometry.

A [smooth scheme](wikipedia://en/35033819) over a perfect field *k* is a scheme *X* that is locally of finite type and [regular](wikipedia://en/43016199) over *k*.

A smooth scheme over a field *k* is a scheme *X* that is geometrically smooth: $X \times_k \overline{k}$ is smooth.

:::

::: {#o240 .entry cue=term name="special"}

**special**

A divisor *D* on a smooth curve *C* is [special](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_divsor) if $h^0(\mathcal{O}(K - D))$, which is called the index of speciality, is positive.

:::

::: {#o241 .entry cue=term name="spherical variety"}

**spherical variety**

A [spherical variety](wikipedia://en/39305846) is a normal *G*-variety (*G* connected reductive) with an open dense orbit by a Borel subgroup of *G*.

:::

::: {#o242 .entry cue=term name="split"}

**split**

In the context of an [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924) $G$ for certain properties $P$ there is the derived property split-$P$. Usually $P$ is a property that is automatic or more common over algebraically closed fields $\overline{k}$. If this property holds already for $G$ defined over a not necessarily algebraically closed field $k$ then $G$ is said to satisfy split-$P$.

A linear algebraic group $G$ defined over a field $k$ is a [torus](wikipedia://en/395885#Tori) if only if its base change $G_{\overline{k}}$ to an algebraic closure $\overline{k}$ is isomorphic to a product of multiplicative groups $G_{m,\overline{k}}^{n}$. $G$ is a **split torus** if and only if it is isomorphic to $G_{m,k}^{n}$ without any base change. $G$ is said to **split** over an intermediate field $k\subseteq L\subseteq\overline{k}$ if and only if its base change $G_{L}$ to $L$ is isomorphic to $G_{m,L}^{n}$.

A [reductive group](wikipedia://en/1088386) $G$ defined over a field $k$ is **split-reductive** if and only if a maximal torus $T\subseteq G$ defined over $k$ is a split torus. Since any [simple group](wikipedia://en/1088386#Simple_reductive_groups) is reductive a **split simple group** means a simple group that is split-reductive.

A [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012) linear algebraic group $G$ defined over a field $k$ is **split** if and only if it has [composition series](wikipedia://en/297493) $B=B_0\supset B_1\supset \ldots\supset B_t=\{1\}$ defined over $k$ such that each successive quotient $B_i/B_{i+1}$ is isomorphic to either the multiplicative group $G_{m,k}$ or the additive group $G_{m,a}$ over $k$.

A linear algebraic group $G$ defined over a field $k$ is **split** if and only if it has a [Borel subgroup](wikipedia://en/588356) $B\subseteq G$ defined over $k$ that is split in the sense of connected solvable linear algebraic groups.

In the classification of [real Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/22385144) [split Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/25264571) play an important role. There is a close connection between linear Lie groups, their associated Lie algebras and linear algebraic groups over $k=\mathbb{R}$ resp. $\mathbb{C}$. The term **split** has similar meanings for Lie theory and linear algebraic groups.

:::

::: {#o243 .entry cue=term name="stable"}

**stable**

A [stable curve](wikipedia://en/18948788) is a curve with some "mild" singularity, used to construct a good-behaving [moduli space of curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_space_of_curves).

A [stable vector bundle](wikipedia://en/18909129) is used to construct the [moduli space of vector bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_space_of_vector_bundles).

:::

::: {#o244 .entry cue=term name="stack"}

**stack**

A [stack](wikipedia://en/13185596) parametrizes sets of points together with automorphisms.

:::

::: {#o245 .entry cue=term name="strict transform"}

**strict transform**

Given a blow-up $\pi: \widetilde{X} \to X$ along a closed subscheme *Z* and a morphism $f: Y \to X$, the [strict transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strict_transform) of *Y* (also called proper transform) is the [blow-up](wikipedia://en/1817790) $\widetilde{Y} \to Y$ of *Y* along the closed subscheme $f^{-1} Z$. If *f* is a closed immersion, then the induced map $\widetilde{Y} \hookrightarrow \widetilde{X}$ is also a closed immersion.

:::

::: {#o246 .entry cue=term name="subscheme"}

**subscheme**

A **subscheme**, without qualifier, of *X* is a closed subscheme of an open subscheme of *X*.

:::

::: {#o247 .entry cue=term name="surface"}

**surface**

An algebraic variety of dimension two.

**symmetric variety**

An analog of a [symmetric space](wikipedia://en/1642877). See [symmetric variety](wikipedia://en/37635994).

## U {#U}

::: {#o258 .entry cue=term name="universal"}

**universal**

If a [moduli functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_functor) *F* is represented by some scheme or algebraic space *M*, then a [universal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_object) is an element of *F*(*M*) that corresponds to the identity morphism *M* → *M* (which is an *M*-point of *M*). If the values of *F* are isomorphism classes of curves with extra structure, say, then a universal object is called a [universal curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_of_curves). A [tautological bundle](wikipedia://en/2913502) would be another example of a universal object.

Let $\mathcal{M}_{g}$ be the moduli of smooth projective curves of genus *g* and $\mathcal{C}_g = \mathcal{M}_{g, 1}$ that of smooth projective curves of genus *g* with single marked points. In literature, the forgetful map $\pi: \mathcal{C}_{g} \to \mathcal{M}_{g}$ is often called a universal curve.

:::

::: {#o259 .entry cue=term name="universally"}

**universally**

A morphism has some property universally if all base changes of the morphism have this property. Examples include [universally catenary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universally_catenary), [universally injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universally_injective).

:::

::: {#o260 .entry cue=term name="unramified"}

**unramified**

For a point $y$ in $Y$, consider the corresponding morphism of local rings $f^\# \colon \mathcal{O}_{X, f(y)} \to \mathcal{O}_{Y, y}$.

Let $\mathfrak{m}$ be the maximal ideal of $\mathcal{O}_{X,f(y)}$, and let $\mathfrak{n} = f^\#(\mathfrak{m}) \mathcal{O}_{Y,y}$

be the ideal generated by the image of $\mathfrak{m}$ in $\mathcal{O}_{Y,y}$. The morphism $f$ is **[unramified](wikipedia://en/58653736)** (resp. **G-unramified**) if it is locally of finite type (resp. locally of finite presentation) and if for all $y$ in $Y$, $\mathfrak{n}$ is the maximal ideal of $\mathcal{O}_{Y,y}$ and the induced map $\mathcal{O}_{X,f(y)}/\mathfrak{m} \to \mathcal{O}_{Y,y}/\mathfrak{n}$

is a [finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_field_extension) [separable field extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separable_field_extension).[^o261] This is the geometric version (and generalization) of an [unramified field extension](wikipedia://en/384644) in [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705).

:::
