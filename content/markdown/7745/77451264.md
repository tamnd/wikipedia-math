# Women cryptanalysts at MI1(b)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Women cryptanalysts at MI1(b)"}

[MI1(b)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MI1) was a department of [British Military Intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Military_Intelligence) set up during [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I) for the interception and [cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715) of coded messages. Unlike its equivalent in the Admiralty, [Room 40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_40), where women were employed in clerical roles, MI1(b) employed women as linguists, translators, and cryptanalysts.[^o2] By 1919, a third of MI1(b)’s civilian cryptanalysts and linguists were women.[^o3]

:::

Most of MI1(b)'s female codebreakers are known to have been university-educated, and several came from teaching backgrounds. Some of them had previously served in the [Hush WAACs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hush_WAACs), a small group of codebreakers working near the front lines in France in 1917–18, and some went on to work at the [Government Code and Cypher School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Code_and_Cypher_School), formed in 1919 to continue codebreaking work during peacetime. At least one member, [Emily Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emily_Anderson), also worked as a codebreaker during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) at [Bletchley Park](wikipedia://en/4037).

At first, recruitment to MI1(b) was done on a basis of personal contact, with women being selected for their knowledge of languages, particularly German. In 1917, the fourteen-strong staff of MI1(b) under the leadership of [Malcolm Vivian Hay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_Vivian_Hay) moved to new premises at Cork Street and its workforce expanded.[^o4] Recruitment became more formal and Hay introduced his own testing for candidates who applied.[^o3] Details are limited due to the destruction of records, both accidental and deliberate, pertaining to MI1(b).[^o5]

## Women known to have worked at MI1(b) {#Women_known_to_have_worked_at_MI1(b)}

Women known to have worked at MI1(b) are (in alphabetical order):

### Emily Anderson {#Emily_Anderson}

Emily Anderson (1891–1962) was an Irish translator and music historian who was Professor of German at [University College Galway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Galway) when she was called on by the war effort for her language skills. Initially trained for the Hush WAACs, she instead took a post at MI1(b) in July 1918. In 1920, she joined GC&CS at the rank of Junior Assistant along with Florence Hayllar. Head of the Italian Diplomatic Section, she became a trainer of other decoders. Her decoding work continued throughout the 1930s and 1940s, including [service at Bletchley Park](wikipedia://en/54254691). She was awarded an [OBE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_British_Empire) in 1943.

### Miss J.F. Carleton and Miss Chichester {#Miss_J.F._Carleton_and_Miss_Chichester}

Miss J.F. Carleton and Miss Chichester are known to have been working for MI1(b) as linguists or cryptanalysts at the time of its change of premises in 1917.[^o4] Miss Chichester worked on Uruguayan, Spanish, and Argentine codes.[^o9] J.F. Carleton worked on American codes, and went on to become a Junior Assistant at GC&CS.[^o10]

### Florence Mabel Hannam {#Florence_Mabel_Hannam}

Florence Mabel Hannam was [mentioned in despatches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mentioned_in_dispatches) for her service as a Hush WAAC, for which she won the Victory Medal and the British War Medal in 1919.[^o12][^o13] She worked at MI1(b) on non-alphabetical Romanian field codes,[^o9] and then at GC&CS from 1918 to 1920.[^o14]

### Florence Hayllar {#Florence_Hayllar}

Florence Hayllar Hayllar[^o16] (b. 1868) was a professional writer from Brighton.[^o17] A university graduate, she published poetry collections[^o18][^o19] and a novel[^o20] as well as articles on social issues.[^o21] She was editor of the linguistics journal [Notes and Queries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notes_and_Queries) from 1912 to 1942.[^o22][^o23]

Hayllar served as a Hush WAAC and joined MI1(b) in 1917.[^o24][^o25] While at MI1(b), she composed poetry mocking the sexist attitudes of her male colleagues[^o24] and expressing a hope that the 'shadowy Entity' of MI1(b) would be lent form by memory one day.[^o4]

She was appointed Junior Assistant at GC&CS in January 1920 along with Emily Anderson, and left the position after a month.[^o26][^o27][^o17]

### Barbara Freire-Marreco {#Barbara_Freire-Marreco}

The 'Miss Marreco' known to have worked on Spanish, French, German and South American codes for MI1(b) was probably Barbara Freire-Marreco (1879–1967), an anthropologist known for her fieldwork among the [Pueblo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puebloans) peoples. She also worked for the War Trade Intelligence Department during WWI.[^o25]

### Alda Milner-Barry {#Alda_Milner-Barry}

[Alda Milner-Barry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alda_Milner-Barry) (1893–1938) worked as a translator in the Intelligence Department of the War Office in 1916 after studying Medieval and Modern Languages at [Newnham College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newnham_College,_Cambridge). After a stint as a German professor, she started work at MI1(b) in 1917. She was deputy to Emily Anderson. After the war she worked as an English lecturer and vice-principal of Newnham College.[^o30]

### Claribel Spurling {#Claribel_Spurling}

[Claribel Spurling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claribel_Spurling) (1875–1941) was a headmistress and German teacher at [Birkenhead High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birkenhead_High_School_Academy) when her brother was killed in action in 1917, and she subsequently joined the [WRNS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_Royal_Naval_Service). She is known for being the only person to score 100% on a supposedly impossible test set for candidates for the expanded MI1(b) by Major Hay. She worked on German, French, and Italian codes, and went on to work at GC&CS as a Junior Assistant. After her codebreaking career she worked as a warden of several university halls and co-wrote children's stories and drama with [Beatrice Clay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Clay).[^o32]

### Gwendoline Watkins {#Gwendoline_Watkins}

Gwendoline Edith Gwyllyam Watkins (b. 1895) was one of the first six women to be selected as a Hush WAAC for her 'somewhat rusty' knowledge of German, and the youngest of the group at the age of twenty-two.[^o34][^o13] She was [mentioned in despatches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mentioned_in_dispatches) for her work in France, and was awarded the Victory Medal and the British War Medal in 1919.[^o12] After nine months working at the War Office at the close of the war,[^o35] she, like her fellow WAAC Florence Hannam, worked at MI1(b) on Romanian non-alphabetical field codes and at GC&CS, departing in 1920.[^o36]
