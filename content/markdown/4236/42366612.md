# Rainbow matching

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rainbow matching"}

In the mathematical discipline of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **rainbow matching** in an [edge-colored graph](wikipedia://en/690647) is a [matching](wikipedia://en/581797) in which all the edges have distinct colors.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given an edge-colored graph $G = (V, E)$, a rainbow matching $M$ in $G$ is a set of pairwise non-adjacent edges, that is, no two edges share a common vertex, such that all the edges in the set have distinct colors.

A maximum rainbow matching is a rainbow matching that contains the largest possible number of edges.

:::

## History {#History}

*Image: Rainbow matching.svg.* On the top left a [Latin square](wikipedia://en/37447), on the bottom left the relative proper $n$-[edge coloring](wikipedia://en/690647). On the top right a [Latin transversal](wikipedia://en/12437622#Bounds_on_maximal_number_of_transversals_in_a_Latin_square) and on the bottom right the relative **rainbow matching**.

Rainbow matchings are of particular interest given their connection to transversals of [Latin squares](wikipedia://en/37447).

Denote by $K_{n,n}$ the [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) on $n + n$ vertices. Every proper $n$-[edge coloring](wikipedia://en/690647) of $K_{n,n}$ corresponds to a Latin square of order $n$. A rainbow matching then corresponds to a [transversal](wikipedia://en/37447#Transversals_and_rainbow_matchings) of the Latin square, meaning a selection of $n$ positions, one in each row and each column, containing distinct entries.

This connection between transversals of Latin squares and rainbow matchings in $K_{n,n}$ has inspired additional interest in the study of rainbow matchings in [triangle-free graphs](wikipedia://en/7667996).[^o5]

## Existence when each edge has a single color {#Existence_when_each_edge_has_a_single_color}

An edge-coloring is called *proper* if each edge has a single color, and each two edges of the same color have no vertex in common.

A proper edge-coloring does not guarantee the existence of a perfect rainbow matching. For example, consider the graph $K_{2,2}$: the complete bipartite graph on 2+2 vertices. Suppose the edges $(x_{1},y_{1})$ and $(x_{2},y_{2})$ are colored green, and the edges $(x_{1},y_{2})$ and $(x_{2},y_{1})$ are colored blue. This is a proper coloring, but there are only two perfect matchings, and each of them is colored by a single color. This invokes the question: when does a large rainbow matching is guaranteed to exist?

### Bounds depending only on the number of vertices {#Bounds_depending_only_on_the_number_of_vertices}

Much of the research on this question was published using the terminology of [Latin transversals in Latin squares](wikipedia://en/37447#Transversals_and_rainbow_matchings). Translated into the rainbow matching terminology:

- In 1967, [H. J. Ryser](wikipedia://en/20825197) conjectured that, when $n$ is **odd**, every proper edge-coloring of $K_{n,n}$ has a rainbow matching of size $n$.[^o8]
- In 1975, S. K. Stein and Brualdi conjectured that, when $n$ is **even**, every proper edge-coloring of $K_{n,n}$ has a rainbow matching of size $n - 1$.[^o9] (it is known that a rainbow matching of size $n$ need not exist in this case).

A more general conjecture of Stein is that a rainbow matching of size $n - 1$ exists not only for a proper edge-coloring, but for any coloring in which each color appears on exactly $n$ edges.[^o8]

Some weaker versions of these conjectures have been proved:

- Every proper edge-coloring of $K_{n,n}$ has a rainbow matching of size $2n/3$.[^o10]
- Every proper edge-coloring of $K_{n,n}$ has a rainbow matching of size $n - \sqrt{n}.$[^o11]
- Every proper edge-coloring of $K_{n,n}$ has a rainbow matching of size $n - 11 \log_{2}^{2}(n)$.[^o12]
- Every proper edge-coloring of $K_{n,n}$ has a rainbow matching of size $n - O(\log n/\log \log n)$.[^o13]
- Every proper edge-coloring of $K_{n,n}$ has a rainbow matching of size $n - 1$.[^o14] (Preprint)

### Bounds depending on the minimum degree {#Bounds_depending_on_the_minimum_degree}

Wang asked if there is a function $f(d)$ such that every properly edge-colored graph $G$ with minimum [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) $d$ and at least $f(d)$ vertices must have a rainbow matching of size $d$.[^o16] Obviously at least $2d$ vertices are necessary, but how many are sufficient?

- Diemunsch, et al. answered this question in the affirmative and showed that given a properly edge-colored graph $G$ with minimum degree $d$ and order at least $f(d) = 13d/2-23/2+41/(8d)$, there exists a rainbow matching of size $d$ in $G$.[^o17]
- This bound was later improved to $f(d) = 4d - 3$ by Andras Gyarfas and Gabor N. Sarkozy.[^o18] They also show that any graph with at least $2d$ vertices has a rainbow matching of size at least $d - 2d^{2/3}$. These are the best known estimate to date.

## Existence when the same edge may have different colors {#Existence_when_the_same_edge_may_have_different_colors}

Suppose that each edge may have several different colors, while each two edges of the same color must still have no vertex in common. In other words, each color is a [matching](wikipedia://en/581797). How many colors are needed in order to guarantee the existence of a rainbow matching?

### In complete bipartite graphs {#In_complete_bipartite_graphs}

Drisko[^o21] studied this question using the terminology of [Latin rectangles](wikipedia://en/26464655). He proved that, for any $n \le k$, in the complete bipartite graph $K_{n,k}$, any family of $2n - 1$ matchings (=colors) of size $n$ has a perfect rainbow matching (of size $n$). He applied this theorem to questions about [group actions](wikipedia://en/12781) and [difference sets](wikipedia://en/2992310).

Drisko also showed that $2n - 1$ matchings may be necessary: consider a family of $2n - 2$ matchings, of which $n - 1$ are $\{ (x_{1}, y_{1}), (x_{2}, y_{2}), ..., (x_{n}, y_{n})\}$ and the other $n - 1$ are $\{(x_{1}, y_{2}), (x_{2}, y_{3}), \ldots, (x_{n}, y_{1}) \}.$ Then the largest rainbow matching is of size $n - 1$ (e.g. take one edge from each of the first $n - 1$ matchings).

Alon[^o22] showed that Drisko's theorem implies an older result[^o23] in [additive number theory](wikipedia://en/4460333).

### In general bipartite graphs {#In_general_bipartite_graphs}

Aharoni and Berger[^o25] generalized Drisko's theorem to any bipartite graph, namely: any family of $2n - 1$ matchings of size $n$ in a bipartite graph has a rainbow matching of size $n$.

Aharoni, Kotlar and Ziv[^o26] showed that Drisko's extremal example is unique in any bipartite graph.

### In general graphs {#In_general_graphs}

In general graphs, $2n - 1$ matchings are no longer sufficient. When $n$ is even, one can add to Drisko's example the matching $\{ (x_{1}, x_{2}), (y_{1}, y_{2}), (x_{2}, x_{3}), (y_{2}, y_{3}), \ldots \}$ and get a family of $2n - 1$ matchings without any rainbow matching.

Aharoni, Berger, Chudnovsky, Howard and Seymour[^o28] proved that, in a general graph, $3n - 2$ matchings (=colors) are always sufficient. It is not known whether this is tight: currently the best lower bound for even $n$ is $2n$ and for odd $n$ it is $2n - 1$.[^o29]

## Rainbow fractional matchings {#Rainbow_fractional_matchings}

A [fractional matching](wikipedia://en/64410778) is a set of edges with a non-negative weight assigned to each edge, such that the sum of weights adjacent to each vertex is at most 1. The size of a fractional matching is the sum of weights of all edges. It is a generalization of a matching, and can be used to generalize both the colors and the rainbow matching:

- Instead of requiring that each color be a matching of size $n$, the requirement is weakened: each "color" can be an arbitrary set of edges, but it should admit a fractional matching of size at least $n$.
- Instead of looking for a rainbow matching, we look for a **rainbow fractional matching** - a fractional matching in which each edge with a positive weight has a different color.

It is known that, in a bipartite graph, the maximum fractional matching size equals the [maximum matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_cardinality_matching) size. Therefore, the theorem of Aharoni and Berger[^o25] is equivalent to the following. Let $n$ be any positive integer. Given any family of $2n - 1$ fractional-matchings (=colors) of size $n$ in a bipartite graph, there exists a rainbow-fractional-matching of size $n$.

Aharoni, Holzman and Jiang extend this theorem to arbitrary graphs as follows. Let $n$ be any positive integer or [half-integer](wikipedia://en/1568608). Any family of $2n$ fractional-matchings (=colors) of size at least $n$ in an arbitrary graph has a rainbow-fractional-matching of size $n$.[^o29] The $2n$ is the smallest possible for fractional matchings in arbitrary graphs: the extremal case is constructed using an odd-length cycle.

### Partial proof {#Partial_proof}

For the case of perfect fractional matchings, both the above theorems can derived from the [colorful Carathéodory theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorful_caratheodory_theorem).

For every edge $e$ in $E$, let $\mathbf{1}_{e}$ be a vector of size $\lvert V\rvert$, where for each vertex $v$ in $V$, element $v$ in $\mathbf{1}_{e}$ equals 1 if $e$ is adjacent to $v$, and 0 otherwise (so each vector $\mathbf{1}_{e}$ has 2 ones and $\lvert V\rvert$-2 zeros). Every fractional matching corresponds to a [conical combination](wikipedia://en/19634578) of edges, in which each element is at most 1. A conical combination in which each element is *exactly* 1 corresponds to a *perfect* fractional matching. In other words, a collection $F$ of edges admits a perfect fractional matching, [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $\mathbf{1}_{v}$ (the vector of $\lvert V\rvert$ ones) is contained in the [conical hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conical_hull) of the vectors $\mathbf{1}_{e}$ for $e$ in $F$.

Consider a graph with $2n$ vertices, and suppose there are $2n$ subsets of edges, each of which admits a perfect fractional matching (of size $n$). This means that the vector $\mathbf{1}_{v}$ is in the conical hull of each of these $n$ subsets. By the [colorful Carathéodory theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorful_caratheodory_theorem), there exists a selection of $2n$ edges, one from each subset, that their conical hull contains $\mathbf{1}_{v}$. This corresponds to a rainbow perfect fractional matching. The expression $2n$ is the dimension of the vectors $\mathbf{1}_{e}$ - each vector has $2n$ elements.

Now, suppose that the graph is bipartite. In a bipartite graph, there is a constraint on the vectors $\mathbf{1}_{e}$: the sum of elements corresponding to each part of the graph must be 1. Therefore, the vectors $\mathbf{1}_{e}$ live in a $(2n - 1)$-dimensional space. Therefore, the same argument as above holds when there are only $2n - 1$ subsets of edges.

## Rainbow matching in hypergraphs {#Rainbow_matching_in_hypergraphs}

An *r-uniform [hypergraph](wikipedia://en/232535)* is a set of hyperedges each of which contains exactly $r$ vertices (so a 2-uniform hypergraph is a just a graph without self-loops). Aharoni, Holzman and Jiang extend their theorem to such hypergraphs as follows. Let $n$ be any positive [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024). Any family of $\lceil r\cdot n\rceil$ fractional-matchings (=colors) of size at least $n$ in an $r$-uniform hypergraph has a rainbow-fractional-matching of size $n$.[^o29] The $\lceil r\cdot n\rceil$ is the smallest possible when $n$ is an integer.

An *r-partite hypergraph* is an $r$-uniform hypergraph in which the vertices are partitioned into $r$ [disjoint sets](wikipedia://en/39359) and each hyperedge contains exactly one vertex of each set (so a 2-partite hypergraph is a just bipartite graph). Let $n$ be any positive integer. Any family of $rn - r + 1$ fractional-matchings (=colors) of size at least $n$ in an $r$-partite hypergraph has a rainbow-fractional-matching of size $n$.[^o29] The $rn - r + 1$ is the smallest possible: the extremal case is when $n = r - 1$ is a [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483), and all colors are edges of the truncated [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) of order $n$. So each color has $n^{2} = rn - r + 1$ edges and a fractional matching of size $n$, but any fractional matching of that size requires all $rn - r + 1$ edges.[^o33]

### Partial proof {#Partial_proof}

For the case of perfect fractional matchings, both the above theorems can derived from the colorful Carathéodory theorem in the previous section. For a general $r$-uniform hypergraph (admitting a perfect matching of size $n$), the vectors $\mathbf{1}_{e}$ live in a $(rn)$-dimensional space. For an $r$-uniform $r$-partite hypergraph, the $r$-partiteness constraints imply that the vectors $\mathbf{1}_{e}$ live in a $(rn - r + 1)$-dimensional space.

### Notes {#Notes}

The above results hold only for rainbow *fractional* matchings. In contrast, the case of rainbow *integral* matchings in $r$-uniform hypergraphs is much less understood. The number of required matchings for a rainbow matching of size $n$ grows at least exponentially with $n$.

## Computation {#Computation}

[Garey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Garey) and [Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_S._Johnson) have shown that computing a maximum rainbow matching is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete) even for edge-colored [bipartite graphs](wikipedia://en/244431).[^o38]

:::

## Applications {#Applications}
