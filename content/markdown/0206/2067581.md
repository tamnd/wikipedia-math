# Routh–Hurwitz theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Routh–Hurwitz theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Routh–Hurwitz theorem** gives a test to determine whether all [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) of a given [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) lie in the left-half [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628). Polynomials with this property are called [Hurwitz stable polynomials](wikipedia://en/2057699). The Routh–Hurwitz theorem is important in [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087) and [control theory](wikipedia://en/7039), because the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) of the [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309) of a [stable](wikipedia://en/3652079), [linear system](wikipedia://en/722503) has roots limited to the left half plane (negative [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue)). Thus the theorem provides a mathematical test, the [Routh–Hurwitz stability criterion](wikipedia://en/1705432), to determine whether a linear dynamical system is stable without solving the system. [The Routh–Hurwitz theorem was proved](wikipedia://en/16187387) in 1895, and it was named after [Edward John Routh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_John_Routh) and [Adolf Hurwitz](wikipedia://en/771690).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notations"}

## Notations {#Notations}

Let $f(z)$ be a polynomial (with [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203)) of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) $n$ with no roots on the [imaginary axis](wikipedia://en/217628) (i.e. the line $z = ic$ where $i$ is the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358) and $c$ is a [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438)). Let us define real polynomials $P_{0}(y)$ and $P_{1}(y)$ by $f(iy) = P_{0}(y) + iP_{1}(y)$, respectively the [real and imaginary parts](wikipedia://en/5826) of $f$ on the imaginary line.

Furthermore, let us denote by:

- $p$ the number of roots of $f$ in the left [half-plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-plane) (taking into account multiplicities);
- $q$ the number of roots of $f$ in the right half-plane (taking into account multiplicities);
- $\Delta \arg f(iy)$ the variation of the argument of $f(iy)$ when $y$ runs from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$;
- $w(x)$ is the number of variations of the [generalized Sturm chain](wikipedia://en/1221919#Generalized_Sturm_chains) obtained from $P_{0}(y)$ and $P_{1}(y)$ by applying the [Euclidean algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid's_algorithm);
- $I^{+\infty}_{-\infty} r$ is the [Cauchy index](wikipedia://en/2079538) of the [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210) $r$ over the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line).

:::

::: {#o5 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

With the notations introduced above, the **Routh–Hurwitz theorem** states that:

$$p-q=\frac{1}{\pi}\Delta\arg f(iy)= \left.\begin{cases} +I_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{P_0(y)}{P_1(y)} & \text{for odd degree} \\[10pt] -I_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{P_1(y)}{P_0(y)} & \text{for even degree} \end{cases}\right\} = w(+\infty)-w(-\infty).$$

From the first equality we can for instance conclude that when the variation of the argument of $f(iy)$ is positive, then $f(z)$ will have more roots to the left of the imaginary axis than to its right. The equality $p - q = w(+\infty) - w(-\infty)$ can be viewed as the complex counterpart of [Sturm's theorem](wikipedia://en/1221919). Note the differences: in Sturm's theorem, the left member is $p + q$ and the $w$ from the right member is the number of variations of a Sturm chain (while $w$ refers to a generalized Sturm chain in the present theorem).

:::

::: {#o7 .statement cue=named-heading name="Routh–Hurwitz stability criterion"}

## Routh–Hurwitz stability criterion {#Routh–Hurwitz_stability_criterion}

We can easily determine a stability criterion using this theorem as it is trivial that $f(z)$ is [Hurwitz-stable](wikipedia://en/2057699) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $p - q = n$. We thus obtain conditions on the coefficients of $f(z)$ by imposing $w(+\infty) = n$ and $w(-\infty) = 0$.

:::
