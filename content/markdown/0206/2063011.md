# Love wave

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Love wave"}

*Image: Love\_wave.svg.* How Love waves work

In [solid mechanics](wikipedia://en/458866), **Love waves**, named after [Augustus Edward Hough Love](wikipedia://en/1115798), are horizontally [polarized](wikipedia://en/41564) [surface waves](wikipedia://en/41763). The Love wave is a result of the [interference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interference_%28wave_propagation%29) of many shear waves ([S-waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-wave)) guided by an elastic layer, which is *welded* to an elastic half space on one side while bordering a vacuum on the other side. In [seismology](wikipedia://en/28935), **Love waves** (also known as **Q waves** (Quer, "lateral" in German)) are [surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_waves) [seismic waves](wikipedia://en/89830) that cause horizontal shifting of the Earth during an [earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthquake). Augustus Edward Hough Love predicted the existence of Love waves mathematically in 1911. They form a distinct class, different from other types of [seismic waves](wikipedia://en/89830), such as [P-waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-wave) and [S-waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-wave) (both [body waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_wave_%28seismology%29)), or [Rayleigh waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayleigh_waves) (another type of surface wave). Love waves travel with a lower velocity than P- or S- waves, but faster than Rayleigh waves. These waves are observed only when there is a low velocity layer overlying a high velocity layer/sub–layers.

:::

## Description {#Description}

The particle motion of a Love wave forms a horizontal line, perpendicular to the direction of [propagation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_propagation) (i.e. are [transverse waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transverse_waves)). Moving deeper into the material, motion can decrease to a "node" and then alternately increase and decrease as one examines deeper layers of particles. The [amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude), or maximum particle motion, often decreases rapidly with depth.

Since Love waves travel on the Earth's surface, the strength (or amplitude) of the waves decrease exponentially with the depth of an earthquake. However, given their confinement to the surface, their amplitude decays only as $\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}$, where $r$ represents the distance the wave has travelled from the earthquake. Surface waves therefore decay more slowly with distance than do body waves, which travel in three dimensions. Large earthquakes may generate Love waves that travel around the Earth several times before dissipating.

Since they decay so slowly, Love waves are the most destructive outside the immediate area of the focus or [epicentre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicentre) of an earthquake. They are what most people feel directly during an earthquake.

In the past, it was often thought that animals like cats and dogs could predict an earthquake before it happened. However, they are simply more sensitive to [ground vibrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_vibrations) than humans and are able to detect the subtler body waves that precede Love waves, like the P-waves and the S-waves.[^o3]

## Basic theory {#Basic_theory}

The conservation of [linear momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_momentum) of a [linear elastic](wikipedia://en/301429) material can be written as:[^o5]

$$\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot(\mathsf{C}:\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathbf{u}) = \rho~\ddot{\mathbf{u}}$$

where $\mathbf{u}$ is the [displacement vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_%28vector%29) and $\mathsf{C}$ is the [stiffness tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stiffness_tensor). Love waves are a special solution ($\mathbf{u}$) that satisfy this system of equations. We typically use a Cartesian coordinate system ($x,y,z$) to describe Love waves.

Consider an isotropic linear elastic medium in which the elastic properties are functions of only the $z$ coordinate, i.e., the [Lamé parameters](wikipedia://en/8983708) and the mass [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) can be expressed as $\lambda(z), \mu(z), \rho(z)$. Displacements $(u,v,w)$ produced by Love waves as a function of time ($t$) have the form

$$u(x,y,z,t) = 0 ~,~~ v(x,y,z,t) = \hat{v}(x,z,t) ~,~~ w(x,y,z,t) = 0 \,.$$

These are therefore [antiplane shear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiplane_shear) waves perpendicular to the $(x,z)$ plane. The function $\hat{v}(x,z,t)$ can be expressed as the superposition of [harmonic waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_wave) with varying [wave numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_number) ($k$) and [frequencies](wikipedia://en/10779) ($\omega$). Consider a single harmonic wave, i.e.,

$$\hat{v}(x,z,t) = V(k, z, \omega)\,\exp[i(k x - \omega t)]$$

where $i$ is the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358), i.e. $i^2 = -1$. The [stresses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_%28physics%29) caused by these displacements are

$$\sigma_{xx} = 0 ~,~~ \sigma_{yy} = 0 ~,~~ \sigma_{zz} = 0 ~, ~~ \tau_{zx} = 0 ~,~~ \tau_{yz} = \mu(z)\,\frac{dV}{dz}\,\exp[i(k x - \omega t)] ~,~~ \tau_{xy} = i k \mu(z) V(k, z, \omega) \,\exp[i(k x - \omega t)] \,.$$

If we substitute the assumed displacements into the equations for the conservation of momentum, we get a simplified equation

$$\frac{d}{dz}\left[\mu(z)\,\frac{dV}{dz}\right] = [k^2\,\mu(z) - \omega^2\,\rho(z)]\,V(k,z,\omega) \,.$$

The boundary conditions for a Love wave are that the [surface tractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_traction) at the free surface $(z = 0)$ must be zero. Another requirement is that the stress component $\tau_{yz}$ in a layer medium must be continuous at the interfaces of the layers. To convert the second order [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309) in $V$ into two first order equations, we express this stress component in the form

$$\tau_{yz} = T(k, z, \omega)\,\exp[i(k x - \omega t)]$$

to get the first order conservation of momentum equations

$$\frac{d}{dz}\begin{bmatrix} V \\ T \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1/\mu(z) \\ k^2\,\mu(z) - \omega^2\,\rho(z) & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} V \\ T \end{bmatrix} \,.$$

The above equations describe an [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) problem whose solution [eigenfunctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenfunctions) can be found by a number of [numerical methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_methods). Another common, and powerful, approach is the [propagator matrix](wikipedia://en/698759) method (also called the [matricant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matricant) approach).
