# Equiareal map

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Equiareal map"}

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), an **equiareal map**, sometimes called an **authalic map**, is a [smooth map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_map) from one [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) to another that preserves the [areas](wikipedia://en/1209) of figures.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

If *M* and *N* are two [Riemannian](wikipedia://en/144652) (or [pseudo-Riemannian](wikipedia://en/287555)) surfaces, then an equiareal map *f* from *M* to *N* can be characterized by any of the following equivalent conditions:

- The [surface area](wikipedia://en/27725) of *f*(*U*) is equal to the area of *U* for every [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) *U* on *M*.
- The [pullback](wikipedia://en/494995) of the [area element](wikipedia://en/1920694) *μ*<sub>*N*</sub> on *N* is equal to *μ*<sub>*M*</sub>, the area element on *M*.
- At each point *p* of *M*, and [tangent vectors](wikipedia://en/191999) *v* and *w* to *M* at *p*,

$$\bigl|df_p(v)\wedge df_p(w)\bigr| = |v\wedge w|\,$$

where $\wedge$ denotes the Euclidean [wedge product](wikipedia://en/221537) of vectors and *df* denotes the [pushforward](wikipedia://en/1558510) along *f*.

:::

## Linear transformations {#Linear_transformations}

*Image: Hyperbolic\_rotation.gif.* Repeated [squeeze mapping](wikipedia://en/1140043) applied to a [hyperbolic sector](wikipedia://en/1139926)

Every [Euclidean isometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_isometry) of the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) is equiareal, but the [converse](wikipedia://en/155941) is not true. In fact, [shear mapping](wikipedia://en/799405) and [squeeze mapping](wikipedia://en/1140043) are [counterexamples](wikipedia://en/143151) to the converse.

Shear mapping takes a rectangle to a parallelogram of the same area. Written in matrix form, a shear mapping along the $x$-axis is

$$\begin{pmatrix}1 & v \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \,\begin{pmatrix}x\\y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}x+vy\\y \end{pmatrix}.$$

Squeeze mapping lengthens and contracts the sides of a rectangle in a reciprocal manner so that the area is preserved. Written in matrix form, with λ > 1 the squeeze reads

$$\begin{pmatrix}\lambda & 0 \\ 0 & 1/\lambda \end{pmatrix}\,\begin{pmatrix}x\\y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\lambda x\\ y/\lambda.\end{pmatrix}$$

A linear transformation $\begin{pmatrix}a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ multiplies areas by the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of its [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) $\lvert ad - bc\rvert$.

[Gaussian elimination](wikipedia://en/13035) shows that every equiareal linear transformation ([rotations](wikipedia://en/410009) included) can be obtained by composing at most two shears along the axes, a squeeze and (if the determinant is negative), a [reflection](wikipedia://en/295829).

## In map projections {#In_map_projections}

In the context of [geographic maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Map#Geographic_maps), a [map projection](wikipedia://en/51784) is called **[equal-area](wikipedia://en/51784#Equal-area)**, **equivalent**, **authalic**, **equiareal**, or **area-preserving**, if areas are preserved up to a constant factor; embedding the target map, usually considered a subset of **R**<sup>2</sup>, in the obvious way in **R**<sup>3</sup>, the requirement above then is weakened to:

$$|df_p(v)\times df_p(w)|=\kappa|v\times w|$$

for some *κ* > 0 not depending on $v$ and $w$. For examples of such projections, see [equal-area map projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal-area_map_projection).
