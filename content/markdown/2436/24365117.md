# Thermodynamic square

*Image: Thermodynamic square.svg.* The thermodynamic square with potentials highlighted in red. Key: G = [Gibbs free energy](wikipedia://en/238181) p = [Pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) H = [Enthalpy](wikipedia://en/10274) S = [Entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) U = [Internal energy](wikipedia://en/340757) V = [Volume](wikipedia://en/32498) F = [Helmholtz free energy](wikipedia://en/255447) T = [Temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Thermodynamic square"}

The **thermodynamic square** (also known as the **thermodynamic wheel**, **Guggenheim scheme (after [Edward A. Guggenheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_A._Guggenheim))** or **Born square**) is a [mnemonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mnemonic) diagram attributed to [Max Born](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Born) and used to help determine thermodynamic relations. Born presented the thermodynamic square in a 1929 lecture.[^o2] The symmetry of thermodynamics appears in a paper by F.O. Koenig.[^o3] The corners represent common [conjugate variables](wikipedia://en/3177451) while the sides represent [thermodynamic potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_potentials). The placement and relation among the variables serves as a key to recall the relations they constitute.

:::

A mnemonic used by students to remember the [Maxwell relations](wikipedia://en/921525) (in [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952)) is "**G**ood **P**hysicists **H**ave **S**tudied **U**nder **V**ery **F**ine **T**eachers", which helps them remember the order of the variables in the square, in clockwise direction. Another mnemonic used here is "**V**alid **F**acts and **T**heoretical **U**nderstanding **G**enerate **S**olutions to **H**ard **P**roblems", which gives the letter in the normal left-to-right writing direction. Both times **A** has to be identified with **F**, another common symbol for [Helmholtz free energy](wikipedia://en/255447). To prevent the need for this switch the following mnemonic is also widely used:"**G**ood **P**hysicists **H**ave **S**tudied **U**nder **V**ery **A**mbitious **T**eachers"; another one is **G**ood **P**hysicists **H**ave **SUVAT**, in reference to the [equations of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations_of_motion). One other useful variation of the mnemonic when the symbol **E** is used for internal energy instead of **U** is the following: "**S**ome **H**ard **P**roblems **G**o **T**o **F**inish **V**ery **E**asy".[^o4]

::: {#o6 .application cue=heading name="Use"}

## Use {#Use}

### Derivatives of thermodynamic potentials {#Derivatives_of_thermodynamic_potentials}

The thermodynamic square is mostly used to compute the derivative of any thermodynamic potential of interest. Suppose for example one desires to compute the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of the [internal energy](wikipedia://en/340757) $U$. The following procedure should be considered:

1. Place oneself in the thermodynamic potential of interest, namely ($G$, $H$, $U$, $F$). In our example, that would be $U$.
1. The two opposite corners of the potential of interest represent the coefficients of the overall result. If the coefficient lies on the left hand side of the square, a negative sign should be added. In our example, an intermediate result would be $dU = - p\,[\text{Differential}] + T\,[\text{Differential}]$.
1. In the opposite corner of each coefficient, you will find the associated differential. In our example, the opposite corner to $p$ would be $V$ ([volume](wikipedia://en/32498)) and the opposite corner for $T$ would be $S$ ([entropy](wikipedia://en/9891)). In our example, an interim result would be: $dU=-p\,dV+T\,dS$. Notice that the sign convention will affect only the coefficients, not the differentials.
1. Finally, always add $\mu\,dN$, where $\mu$ denotes the [chemical potential](wikipedia://en/218628). Therefore, we would have: $dU=-p\,dV+T\,dS+\mu\,dN$.

The other three equations that can be derived using this technique are as follows:

1. $$\mathrm{d}F = -p \mathrm{d}V - S \mathrm{d} T + \mu \mathrm{d}N$$
1. $$\mathrm{d} G = +V \mathrm{d}p - S \mathrm{d}T + \mu \mathrm{d}N$$
1. $$\mathrm{d}H = + V \mathrm{d}p + T \mathrm{d}S + \mu \mathrm{d}N$$

The [Gibbs–Duhem equation](wikipedia://en/8891344) can be derived by using this technique. Notice though that the final addition of the differential of the chemical potential has to be generalized.

### Maxwell relations {#Maxwell_relations}

The thermodynamic square can also be used to find the first-order derivatives in the common [Maxwell relations](wikipedia://en/921525). The following procedure should be considered:

1. Looking at the four corners of the square and make a $\sqcup$ shape with the quantities of interest.
1. Read the $\sqcup$ shape in two different ways by seeing it as L and ⅃. The L will give one side of the relation and the ⅃ will give the other. Note that the partial derivative is taken along the vertical stem of L (and ⅃) while the last corner is held constant.
1. Use L to find $LHS = \left ({\partial S\over\partial p} \right)_T$.
1. Similarly, use ⅃ to find $RHS=-\left ({\partial V\over\partial T} \right)_p$. Again, notice that the sign convention affects only the variable held constant in the partial derivative, not the differentials.
1. Finally, use above equations to get the Maxwell relation: $\left ({\partial S\over\partial p} \right)_T = -\left ({\partial V\over\partial T} \right)_p$.

By rotating the $\sqcup$ shape (randomly, for example by 90 degrees counterclockwise into a $\sqsupset$ shape) other relations such as: $\left ({\partial p\over\partial T} \right)_V = \left ({\partial S\over\partial V} \right)_T$ can be found.

### Natural variables of thermodynamic potentials {#Natural_variables_of_thermodynamic_potentials}

Finally, the potential at the center of each side is a [natural function](wikipedia://en/255446#Natural_variables) of the variables at the corner of that side. So, $G$ is a natural function of $p$ and $T$, and $U$ is a natural function of $S$ and $V$.
