# Law of the unconscious statistician

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Law of the unconscious statistician"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **law of the unconscious statistician**, or **LOTUS**, is a theorem which expresses the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $g(X)$ of a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) $X$ in terms of $g$ and the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of $X$.

:::

The form of the law depends on the type of random variable $X$ in question. If the distribution of $X$ is [discrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_probability_distribution) and one knows its [probability mass function](wikipedia://en/154725) $p_{X}$, then the expected value of $g(X)$ is

$$\operatorname{E}[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x) p_X(x), \,$$

where the sum is over all possible values $x$ of $X$. If instead the distribution of $X$ is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_probability_distribution) with [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) $f_{X}$, then the expected value of $g(X)$ is

$$\operatorname{E}[g(X)] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty g(x) f_X(x) \, \mathrm{d}x$$

Both of these special cases can be expressed in terms of the [cumulative probability distribution function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumulative_probability_distribution_function) $F_{X}$ of $X$, with the expected value of $g(X)$ now given by the [Lebesgue&ndash;Stieltjes integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue&ndash;Stieltjes_integral)

$$\operatorname{E}[g(X)] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty g(x) \, \mathrm{d}F_X(x).$$

In even greater generality, $X$ could be a [random element](wikipedia://en/5215393) in any [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333), in which case the law is given in terms of [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory) and the [Lebesgue integral](wikipedia://en/26064288). In this setting, there is no need to restrict the context to [probability measures](wikipedia://en/45437), and the law becomes a general theorem of [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) on Lebesgue integration relative to a [pushforward measure](wikipedia://en/9412979).

## Etymology {#Etymology}

This proposition is (sometimes) known as the *law of the unconscious statistician* because of a purported tendency to think of the aforementioned law as the very definition of the expected value of a function $g(X)$ and a random variable $X$, rather than (more formally) as a consequence of the true definition of expected value. The naming is sometimes attributed to [Sheldon Ross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheldon_Ross)' 1972 textbook *Introduction to Probability Models*, although he removed the reference in later editions. Many statistics textbooks do present the result as the definition of expected value.

## Joint distributions {#Joint_distributions}

A similar property holds for [joint distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_distribution), or equivalently, for [random vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_vector). For discrete random variables *X* and *Y*, a function of two variables *g*, and joint probability mass function $p_{X, Y}(x, y)$:

$$\operatorname{E}[g(X, Y)] = \sum_y \sum_x g(x, y) p_{X, Y}(x, y)$$

In the [absolutely continuous](wikipedia://en/252670#Absolute_continuity_of_functions) case, with $f_{X, Y}(x, y)$ being the joint probability density function,

$$\operatorname{E}[g(X, Y)] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \int_{-\infty}^\infty g(x, y) f_{X, Y}(x, y) \, \mathrm{d}x \, \mathrm{d}y$$

## Special cases {#Special_cases}

A number of special cases are given here. In the simplest case, where the random variable $X$ takes on countably many values (so that its distribution is discrete), the proof is particularly simple, and holds without modification if $X$ is a discrete [random vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_vector) or even a discrete [random element](wikipedia://en/5215393).

The case of a [continuous random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_random_variable) is more subtle, since the proof in generality requires subtle forms of the change-of-variables formula for integration. However, in the framework of [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory), the discrete case generalizes straightforwardly to general (not necessarily discrete) [random elements](wikipedia://en/5215393), and the case of a continuous random variable is then a special case by making use of the [Radon–Nikodym theorem](wikipedia://en/338746).

### Discrete case {#Discrete_case}

Suppose that $X$ is a random variable which takes on only finitely or countably many different values $x_{1}, x_{2}, ...$, with probabilities $p_{1}, p_{2}, ...$. Then for any function $g$ of these values, the random variable $g(X)$ has values $g(x_{1}), g(x_{2}), ...$, although some of these may coincide with each other. For example, this is the case if $X$ can take on both values $1$ and $-1$ and $g(x) = x^{2}$.

Let $y_{1}, y_{2}, ...$ enumerate the possible *distinct* values of $g(X)$, and for each $i$ let $I_{i}$ denote the collection of all $j$ with $g(x_{j}) = y_{i}$. Then, according to the definition of expected value, there is

$$\operatorname{E}[g(X)]=\sum_i y_i p_{g(X)}(y_i).$$

Since a $y_i$ can be the image of multiple, distinct $x_j$, it holds that

$$p_{g(X)}(y_i) = \sum_{j \in I_i} p_X(x_j).$$

Then the expected value can be rewritten as

$$\sum_i y_i p_{g(X)}(y_i) = \sum_i y_i \sum_{j \in I_i} p_X(x_j) = \sum_i \sum_{j \in I_i} g(x_j) p_X(x_j) = \sum_x g(x)p_X(x).$$

This equality relates the average of the outputs of $g(X)$ as weighted by the probabilities of the outputs themselves to the average of the outputs of $g(X)$ as weighted by the probabilities of the outputs of $X$.

If $X$ takes on only finitely many possible values, the above is fully rigorous. However, if $X$ takes on countably many values, the last equality given does not always hold, as seen by the [Riemann series theorem](wikipedia://en/1895800). Because of this, it is necessary to assume the [absolute convergence](wikipedia://en/61532) of the sums in question.

### Continuous case {#Continuous_case}

Suppose that $X$ is a random variable whose distribution has a continuous density $f$. If $g$ is a general function, then the probability that $g(X)$ is valued in a set of real numbers $K$ equals the probability that $X$ is valued in $g^{-1}(K)$, which is given by

$$\int_{g^{-1}(K)} f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.$$

Under various conditions on $g$, the [change-of-variables formula for integration](wikipedia://en/193748) can be applied to relate this to an integral over $K$, and hence to identify the density of $g(X)$ in terms of the density of $X$. In the simplest case, if $g$ is differentiable with nowhere-vanishing derivative, then the above integral can be written as

$$\int_K f(g^{-1}(y))(g^{-1})'(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,$$

thereby identifying $g(X)$ as possessing the density $f (g^{-1}(y))(g^{-1})'(y)$. The expected value of $g(X)$ is then identified as

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty yf(g^{-1}(y))(g^{-1})'(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{-\infty}^\infty g(x)f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,$$

where the equality follows by another use of the change-of-variables formula for integration. This shows that the expected value of $g(X)$ is encoded entirely by the function $g$ and the density $f$ of $X$.

The assumption that $g$ is differentiable with nonvanishing derivative, which is necessary for applying the usual change-of-variables formula, excludes many typical cases, such as $g(x) = x^{2}$. The result still holds true in these broader settings, although the proof requires more sophisticated results from [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) such as [Sard's theorem](wikipedia://en/914901) and the [coarea formula](wikipedia://en/17915846). In even greater generality, using the [Lebesgue theory](wikipedia://en/26064288) as below, it can be found that the identity

$$\operatorname{E}[g(X)]=\int_{-\infty}^\infty g(x)f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$$

holds true whenever $X$ has a density $f$ (which does not have to be continuous) and whenever $g$ is a [measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055) for which $g(X)$ has finite expected value. (Every continuous function is measurable.) Furthermore, without modification to the proof, this holds even if $X$ is a [random vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_vector) (with density) and $g$ is a multivariable function; the integral is then taken over the multi-dimensional range of values of $X$.

### Measure-theoretic formulation {#Measure-theoretic_formulation}

An abstract and general form of the result is available using the framework of [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory) and the [Lebesgue integral](wikipedia://en/26064288). Here, the setting is that of a [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270) $(\Omega, \mu)$ and a [measurable map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurable_map) $X$ from $\Omega$ to a [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333) $\Omega'$. The theorem then says that for any measurable function $g$ on $\Omega'$ which is valued in [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) (or even the [extended real number line](wikipedia://en/51698)), there is

$$\int_\Omega g \circ X \, \mathrm{d}\mu = \int_{\Omega'} g \, \mathrm{d}(X_\sharp \mu),$$

(interpreted as saying, in particular, that either side of the equality exists if the other side exists). Here $X_{\sharp} \mu$ denotes the [pushforward measure](wikipedia://en/9412979) on $\Omega'$. The 'discrete case' given above is the special case arising when $X$ takes on only countably many values and $\mu$ is a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437). In fact, the discrete case (although without the restriction to probability measures) is the first step in proving the general measure-theoretic formulation, as the general version follows therefrom by an application of the [monotone convergence theorem](wikipedia://en/146263). Without any major changes, the result can also be formulated in the setting of [outer measures](wikipedia://en/488605).

If $\mu$ is a [σ-finite measure](wikipedia://en/4316174), the theory of the [Radon–Nikodym derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon–Nikodym_derivative) is applicable. In the special case that the measure $X_{\sharp} \mu$ is [absolutely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_continuous) relative to some background σ-finite measure $\nu$ on $\Omega'$, there is a real-valued function $f_{X}$ on $\Omega'$ representing the [Radon–Nikodym derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon–Nikodym_derivative) of the two measures, and then

$$\int_{\Omega'} g \, \mathrm{d}(X_\sharp \mu)=\int_{\Omega'}gf_X\,\mathrm{d}\nu.$$

In the further special case that $\Omega'$ is the [real number line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number_line), as in the contexts discussed above, it is natural to take $\nu$ to be the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198), and this then recovers the 'continuous case' given above whenever $\mu$ is a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437). (In this special case, the condition of σ-finiteness is vacuous, since Lebesgue measure and every probability measure are trivially σ-finite.)
