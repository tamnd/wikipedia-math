# Nilmanifold

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nilmanifold"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **nilmanifold** is a [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) which has a [transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_%28group_action%29) [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569) group of [diffeomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphisms) acting on it. As such, a nilmanifold is an example of a [homogeneous space](wikipedia://en/363325) and is diffeomorphic to the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) $N/H$, the quotient of a nilpotent [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) *N* modulo a [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_%28topology%29) [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) *H*. This notion was introduced by [Anatoly Mal'cev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Maltsev) in 1949.[^o9]

:::

In the Riemannian category, there is also a good notion of a nilmanifold. A [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) is called a **homogeneous nilmanifold** if there exist a nilpotent group of [isometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometry_%28Riemannian_geometry%29) acting transitively on it. The requirement that the transitive nilpotent group acts by isometries leads to the following rigid characterization: every homogeneous nilmanifold is isometric to a nilpotent Lie group with left-invariant metric (see Wilson[^o2]).

Nilmanifolds are important geometric objects and often arise as concrete examples with interesting properties; in Riemannian geometry these spaces always have mixed curvature,[^o3] [almost flat spaces](wikipedia://en/933946) arise as quotients of nilmanifolds,[^o4] and compact nilmanifolds have been used to construct elementary examples of collapse of Riemannian metrics under the [Ricci flow](wikipedia://en/288291).[^o5]

In addition to their role in geometry, nilmanifolds are increasingly being seen as having a role in [arithmetic combinatorics](wikipedia://en/18243381) (see Green–Tao[^o6]) and [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986) (see, e.g., Host–Kra[^o7]).

## Compact nilmanifolds {#Compact_nilmanifolds}

One way to construct a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) nilmanifold is to start with a [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) nilpotent Lie group *N* and a [discrete subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_subgroup) $\Gamma$. If the subgroup $\Gamma$ acts cocompactly (via right multiplication) on *N*, then the quotient manifold $N/ \Gamma$ will be a compact nilmanifold. As Mal'cev has shown, every compact nilmanifold is obtained this way.[^o9]

Such a subgroup $\Gamma$ as above is called a [lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_%28group_theory%29) in *N*. It is well known that a nilpotent Lie group admits a lattice if and only if [its Lie algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_algebra_of_a_Lie_group) admits a basis with rational [structure constants](wikipedia://en/20285142): this is [Mal'cev's criterion](wikipedia://en/75315168). Not all nilpotent Lie groups admit lattices; for more details, see also [M. S. Raghunathan](wikipedia://en/18730540).[^o10]

A **compact Riemannian nilmanifold** is a compact Riemannian manifold which is locally isometric to a nilpotent Lie group with left-invariant metric. These spaces are constructed as follows. Let $\Gamma$ be a lattice in a simply connected nilpotent Lie group *N*, as above. Endow *N* with a left-invariant (Riemannian) metric. Then the subgroup $\Gamma$ acts by isometries on *N* via left-multiplication. Thus the quotient $\Gamma \backslash N$ is a compact space locally isometric to *N*. Note: this space is naturally diffeomorphic to $N / \Gamma$.

Compact nilmanifolds also arise as [principal bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_bundles). For example, consider a 2-step [nilpotent Lie group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilpotent_Lie_group) *N* which admits a lattice (see above). Let $Z=[N,N]$ be the commutator subgroup of *N*. Denote by p the dimension of *Z* and by q the codimension of *Z*; i.e. the dimension of *N* is p+q. It is known (see Raghunathan) that $Z \cap \Gamma$ is a lattice in *Z*. Hence, $G = Z/(Z \cap \Gamma )$ is a *p*-dimensional compact torus. Since *Z* is central in *N*, the group *G* acts on the compact nilmanifold $P = N/ \Gamma$ with quotient space $M=P/G$. This base manifold *M* is a *q*-dimensional compact torus. It has been shown that every principal torus bundle over a torus is of this form.[^o11] More generally, a compact nilmanifold is a torus bundle, over a torus bundle, over...over a torus.

As mentioned above, [almost flat manifolds](wikipedia://en/933946) are intimately compact nilmanifolds. See that article for more information.

## Complex nilmanifolds {#Complex_nilmanifolds}

Historically, a **complex nilmanifold** meant a quotient of a complex nilpotent Lie group over a [cocompact lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocompact_lattice). An example of such a nilmanifold is an [Iwasawa manifold](wikipedia://en/14961545). From the 1980s, another (more general) notion of a complex nilmanifold gradually replaced this one.

An [almost complex structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_complex_structure) on a real Lie algebra *g* is an endomorphism $I:\; g \rightarrow g$ which squares to −Id<sub>*g*</sub>. This operator is called a [complex structure](wikipedia://en/509742) if its eigenspaces, corresponding to eigenvalues $\pm \sqrt{-1}$, are subalgebras in $g \otimes {\mathbb C}$. In this case, *I* defines a left-invariant complex structure on the corresponding Lie group. Such a manifold (*G*,*I*) is called a **complex group manifold**. It is easy to see that every connected complex [homogeneous manifold](wikipedia://en/363325) equipped with a free, transitive, holomorphic action by a real Lie group is obtained this way.

Let *G* be a real, nilpotent Lie group. A **complex nilmanifold** is a quotient of a complex group manifold (*G*,*I*), equipped with a left-invariant complex structure, by a discrete, cocompact lattice, acting from the right.

Complex nilmanifolds are usually not homogeneous, as complex varieties.

In complex dimension 2, the only complex nilmanifolds are a complex torus and a [Kodaira surface](wikipedia://en/21256202).[^o13]

## Properties {#Properties}

Compact nilmanifolds (except a torus) are never [homotopy formal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_space).[^o16] This implies immediately that compact nilmanifolds (except a torus) cannot admit a [Kähler structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kähler_structure) (see also [^o17]).

Topologically, all nilmanifolds can be obtained as iterated torus bundles over a torus. This is easily seen from a filtration by [ascending central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_central_series).[^o18]

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

### Nilpotent Lie groups {#Nilpotent_Lie_groups}

From the above definition of homogeneous nilmanifolds, it is clear that any nilpotent Lie group with left-invariant metric is a homogeneous nilmanifold. The most familiar nilpotent Lie groups are matrix groups whose diagonal entries are 1 and whose lower diagonal entries are all zeros.

For example, the [Heisenberg group](wikipedia://en/448518) is a 2-step nilpotent Lie group. This nilpotent Lie group is also special in that it admits a compact quotient. The group $\Gamma$ would be the upper triangular matrices with integral coefficients. The resulting nilmanifold is 3-dimensional. One possible [fundamental domain](wikipedia://en/469365) is (isomorphic to) \[0,1\]<sup>3</sup> with the faces identified in a suitable way. This is because an element $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & x & z \\ & 1 & y \\ & & 1\end{pmatrix}\Gamma$ of the nilmanifold can be represented by the element $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \{x\} & \{z-x \lfloor y \rfloor \} \\ & 1 & \{y\} \\ & & 1\end{pmatrix}$ in the fundamental domain. Here $\lfloor x \rfloor$ denotes the [floor function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_function) of *x*, and $\{ x \}$ the [fractional part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_function#Fractional_part). The appearance of the floor function here is a clue to the relevance of nilmanifolds to additive combinatorics: the so-called bracket polynomials, or generalised polynomials, seem to be important in the development of higher-order Fourier analysis.[^o6]

### Abelian Lie groups {#Abelian_Lie_groups}

A simpler example would be any abelian Lie group. This is because any such group is a nilpotent Lie group. For example, one can take the group of real numbers under addition, and the discrete, cocompact subgroup consisting of the integers. The resulting 1-step nilmanifold is the familiar circle $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$. Another familiar example might be the compact 2-torus or Euclidean space under addition.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

A parallel construction based on [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012) Lie groups produces a class of spaces called **[solvmanifolds](wikipedia://en/4292247)**. An important example of a solvmanifolds are [Inoue surfaces](wikipedia://en/21870938), known in [complex geometry](wikipedia://en/186101).
