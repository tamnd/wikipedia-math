# Bivariant theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bivariant theory"}

In mathematics, a **bivariant theory** was introduced by [Fulton](wikipedia://en/9642149) and [MacPherson](wikipedia://en/10238121) (Fulton MacPherson 1981), in order to put a [ring structure](wikipedia://en/48404) on the [Chow group](wikipedia://en/4282051) of a [singular variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_variety), the resulting ring called an **[operational Chow ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operational_Chow_ring)**.

:::

On technical levels, a bivariant theory is a mix of a [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) theory, specifically [Borel-Moore homology](wikipedia://en/6495737), and a [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060) theory.[^o3] In general, a homology theory is a [covariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariance_and_contravariance_%28computer_science%29) [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from the category of spaces to the category of [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974), while a cohomology theory is a [contravariant functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contravariant_functor) from the category of (nice) spaces to the category of rings. A bivariant theory is a functor both covariant and contravariant; hence, the name “bivariant”.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Unlike a homology theory or a cohomology theory, a bivariant class is defined for a map not a space.

Let $f : X \to Y$ be a map. For such a map, we can consider the fiber square

$$\begin{matrix} X' & \to & Y' \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ X & \to & Y \end{matrix}$$

(for example, a blow-up.) Intuitively, the consideration of all the fiber squares like the above can be thought of as an approximation of the map $f$.

Now, a **birational class** of $f$ is a family of group homomorphisms indexed by the fiber squares:

$$A_k Y' \to A_{k-p} X'$$

satisfying the certain compatibility conditions.

:::

::: {#o7 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Operational Chow ring"}

## Operational Chow ring {#Operational_Chow_ring}

The basic question was whether there is a [cycle map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycle_map):

$$A^*(X) \to \operatorname{H}^*(X, \mathbb{Z}).$$

If *X* is smooth, such a map exists since $A^*(X)$ is the usual [Chow ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chow_ring) of *X*. (Totaro 2014) has shown that rationally there is no such a map with good properties even if *X* is a [linear variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_variety_%28algebraic_geometry%29), roughly a variety admitting a cell decomposition. He also notes that Voevodsky's [motivic cohomology ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motivic_cohomology_ring) is "probably more useful" than the operational Chow ring for a singular scheme (§ 8 of loc. cit.)

:::
