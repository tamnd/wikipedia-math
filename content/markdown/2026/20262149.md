# Trinomial tree

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Trinomial tree"}

The **trinomial tree** is a [lattice-based](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_model_%28finance%29) [computational model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_model) used in [financial mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_mathematics) to price [options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Option_%28finance%29) on [equity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock). It was developed by [Phelim Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phelim_Boyle) in 1986. It is an extension of the [binomial options pricing model](wikipedia://en/250074), and is conceptually similar. It can also be shown that the approach is equivalent to the [explicit](wikipedia://en/6054681#Explicit_method) [finite difference method for option pricing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_difference_methods_for_option_pricing).[^o2]

:::

Trinomial trees [are also deployed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_model_%28finance%29#Approaches) [^o3] for [fixed income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_income) and [interest rate derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interest_rate_derivative); see under [Lattice model (finance)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_model_%28finance%29).

::: {#o5 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formula"}

## Formula {#Formula}

Under the trinomial method, the [underlying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underlying) stock price is modeled as a recombining tree, where, at each node the price has three possible paths: an up, down and stable or middle path.[^o6] These values are found by multiplying the value at the current node by the appropriate factor $u\,$, $d\,$ or $m\,$ where

$$u = e^{\sigma\sqrt {2\Delta t}}$$

$d = e^{-\sigma\sqrt {2\Delta t}} = \frac{1}{u} \,$ (the structure is recombining)

$$m = 1 \,$$

and the corresponding probabilities are:

$$p_u = \left(\frac{e^{(r - q) \Delta t / 2}- e^{-\sigma\sqrt {\Delta t/2}}}{e^{\sigma\sqrt {\Delta t/2}}- e^{-\sigma\sqrt {\Delta t/2}}}\right)^2 \,$$

$$p_d = \left(\frac{e^{\sigma\sqrt {\Delta t/2}}-e^{(r - q) \Delta t / 2}}{e^{\sigma\sqrt {\Delta t/2}}- e^{-\sigma\sqrt {\Delta t/2}}}\right)^2 \,$$

$$p_m = 1 - (p_u + p_d) \,.$$

In the above formulae: $\Delta t \,$ is the length of time per step in the tree and is simply time to maturity divided by the number of time steps; $r\,$ is the [risk-free interest rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk-free_interest_rate) over this maturity; $\sigma\,$ is the corresponding [volatility of the underlying](wikipedia://en/11930108); $q\,$ is its corresponding [dividend yield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dividend_yield).[^o7]

As with the binomial model, these factors and probabilities are specified so as to ensure that the price of the [underlying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underlying) evolves as a [martingale](wikipedia://en/274536), while the [moments –](wikipedia://en/368684) considering node spacing and probabilities – are matched to those of the [log-normal distribution](wikipedia://en/102476)[^o8] (and with increasing accuracy for smaller time-steps). Note that for $p_u$, $p_d$, and $p_m$ to be in the interval $(0,1)$ the following condition on $\Delta t$ has to be satisfied $\Delta t < 2\frac{\sigma^2}{(r-q)^2}$.

Once the tree of prices has been calculated, the option price is found at each node largely [as for the binomial model](wikipedia://en/250074#Methodology), by working backwards from the final nodes to the present node ($t_{0}$). The difference being that the option value at each non-final node is determined based on the three – as opposed to *two* – later nodes and their corresponding probabilities.[^o9]

If the length of time-steps $\Delta t$ is taken as an exponentially distributed random variable and interpreted as the waiting time between two movements of the stock price then the resulting stochastic process is a [birth–death process](wikipedia://en/2276797). The resulting [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korn–Kreer–Lenssen_model) is soluble and there exist analytic pricing and hedging formulae for various options.

:::

::: {#o11 .application cue=heading name="Application"}

## Application {#Application}

The trinomial model is considered[^o12] to produce more accurate results than the binomial model when fewer time steps are modelled, and is therefore used when computational speed or resources may be an issue. For [vanilla options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanilla_option), as the number of steps increases, the results rapidly converge, and the binomial model is then preferred due to its simpler implementation. For [exotic options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exotic_option) the trinomial model (or adaptations[^o13]) is sometimes more stable and accurate, regardless of step-size.
