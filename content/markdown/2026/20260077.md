# Wind wave model

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Wind wave model"}

Wind waves

*Image: NOAA Wavewatch III Sample Forecast.gif.* NOAA WAVEWATCH III (R) 120-hour Forecast for the North Atlantic

In [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034), **wind wave modeling** describes the effort to depict the [sea state](wikipedia://en/1212631) and predict the evolution of the [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) of wind waves using [numerical techniques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_model). These simulations consider atmospheric wind forcing, nonlinear wave interactions, and frictional dissipation, and they output [statistics](wikipedia://en/26703) describing [wave heights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_height), [periods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_period), and propagation directions for regional seas or global oceans. Such **wave [hindcasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindcast)** and **wave [forecasts](wikipedia://en/246074)** are extremely important for commercial interests on the high seas.[^o2] For example, the [shipping industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shipping_industry) requires guidance for operational planning and tactical [seakeeping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seakeeping) purposes.[^o2]

:::

For the specific case of predicting wind wave statistics on the ocean, the term **ocean surface wave model** is used.

Other applications, in particular [coastal engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastal_engineering), have led to the developments of wind wave models specifically designed for coastal applications.

## Historical overview {#Historical_overview}

Early forecasts of the [sea state](wikipedia://en/1212631) were created manually based upon [empirical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical) relationships between the present state of the sea, the expected wind conditions, the fetch/duration, and the direction of the wave propagation.[^o4] Alternatively, the [swell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swell_%28ocean%29) part of the state has been forecasted as early as 1920 using remote observations.[^o5]

During the 1950s and 1960s, much of the theoretical groundwork necessary for numerical descriptions of wave evolution was laid. For forecasting purposes, it was realized that the random nature of the sea state was best described by a spectral decomposition in which the energy of the waves was attributed to as many wave trains as necessary, each with a specific direction and period. This approach allowed to make combined forecasts of [wind seas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_sea) and swells. The first numerical model based on the spectral decomposition of the sea state was operated in 1956 by the French Weather Service, and focused on the North Atlantic.[^o6] The 1970s saw the first operational, hemispheric wave model: the spectral wave ocean model (SWOM) at the [Fleet Numerical Oceanography Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleet_Numerical_Oceanography_Center).[^o7]

First generation wave models did not consider nonlinear wave interactions. Second generation models, available by the early 1980s, parameterized these interactions. They included the "coupled hybrid" and "coupled discrete" formulations.[^o8] Third generation models explicitly represent all the physics relevant for the development of the sea state in two dimensions. The wave modeling project (WAM), an international effort, led to the refinement of modern wave modeling techniques during the decade 1984-1994.[^o9] Improvements included two-way coupling between wind and waves, assimilation of satellite wave data, and medium-range operational forecasting.

Wind wave models are used in the context of a forecasting or hindcasting system. Differences in model results arise (with decreasing order of importance) from: differences in wind and sea ice forcing, differences in parameterizations of physical processes, the use of [data assimilation](wikipedia://en/2458875) and associated methods, and the numerical techniques used to solve the wave energy evolution equation.

In the aftermath of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), the study of wave growth garnered significant attention. The global nature of the war, encompassing battles in the Pacific, Atlantic, and Mediterranean seas, necessitated the execution of landing operations on enemy-held coasts. Safe landing was paramount, given that choppy waters posed the danger of capsizing [landing craft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_craft_tank). Consequently, the precise forecasting of weather and wave conditions became essential, prompting the recruitment of [meteorologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorologist) and [oceanographers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanography) by the warring nations.[^o10][^o11]

During this period, both [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan) and the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) embarked on wave prediction research. In the U.S., comprehensive studies were carried out at the [Scripps Institution of Oceanography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scripps_Institution_of_Oceanography) affiliated with the [University of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California). Under the guidance of [Harald Svedrup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Sverdrup_%28oceanographer%29), [Walter Munk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Munk) devised an avant-garde wave calculation methodology for the [United States Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Navy) and later refined this approach for the [Office of Naval Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Naval_Research).

This pioneering effort led to the creation of the [significant wave method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Significant_wave_height), which underwent subsequent refinements and data integrations. The method, in due course, came to be popularly referred to as the SMB method, an acronym derived from its founders Sverdrup, Munk, and Charles L. Bretschneider.[^o12][^o13]

Between 1950 and 1980, various formulae were proposed. Given that two-dimensional field models had not been formulated during that time, studies were initiated in the Netherlands by [Rijkswaterstaat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rijkswaterstaat) and the Technische Adviescommissie voor de Waterkeringen (TAW - Technical Advisory Committee for Flood Defences) to discern the most appropriate formula to compute wave height at the base of a [dike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levee).[^o14] This work concluded that the 1973 Bretschneider formula was the most suitable. However, subsequent studies by Young and Verhagen in 1997 suggested that adjusting certain coefficients enhanced the formula's efficacy in shallow water regions.

## General strategy {#General_strategy}

### Input {#Input}

A wave model requires as initial conditions information describing the state of the sea. An analysis of the sea or ocean can be created through data assimilation, where observations such as buoy or satellite altimeter measurements are combined with a background guess from a previous forecast or climatology to create the best estimate of the ongoing conditions. In practice, many forecasting system rely only on the previous forecast, without any assimilation of observations.[^o17]

A more critical input is the "forcing" by wind fields: a time-varying map of wind speed and directions. The most common sources of errors in wave model results are the errors in the wind field. [Ocean currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_currents) can also be important, in particular in western boundary currents such as the Gulf Stream, Kuroshio or Agulhas current, or in coastal areas where tidal currents are strong. Waves are also affected by sea ice and icebergs, and all operational global wave models take at least the sea ice into account.

### Representation {#Representation}

The sea state is described as a [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_spectrum); the sea surface can be decomposed into waves of varying [frequencies](wikipedia://en/10779) using the principle of [superposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_superposition). The waves are also separated by their direction of propagation. The model domain size can range from regional to the global ocean. Smaller domains can be nested within a global domain to provide higher resolution in a region of interest. The sea state evolves according to physical equations – based on a spectral representation of the conservation of [wave action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_action_%28continuum_mechanics%29) – which include: [wave propagation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_propagation) / advection, [refraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refraction) (by bathymetry and currents), [shoaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_shoaling), and a source function which allows for wave energy to be augmented or diminished. The source function has at least three terms: wind forcing, nonlinear transfer, and dissipation by whitecapping.[^o8] Wind data are typically provided from a separate atmospheric model from an operational weather forecasting center.

For intermediate water depths the effect of bottom friction should also be added.[^o20] At ocean scales, the dissipation of swells - without breaking - is a very important term.[^o21]

:::

### Output {#Output}

The output of a wind wave model is a description of the wave spectra, with amplitudes associated with each frequency and propagation direction. Results are typically summarized by the [significant wave height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Significant_wave_height), which is the average height of the one-third largest waves, and the period and propagation direction of the dominant wave.

### Coupled models {#Coupled_models}

Wind waves also act to modify atmospheric properties through frictional drag of near-surface winds and heat fluxes.[^o24] Two-way coupled models allow the wave activity to feed back upon the atmosphere. The [European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Centre_for_Medium-Range_Weather_Forecasts) (ECMWF) coupled atmosphere-wave forecast system described below facilitates this through exchange of the [Charnock parameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charnock_parameter) which controls the [sea surface roughness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_roughness). This allows the atmosphere to respond to changes in the surface roughness as the [wind sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_sea) builds up or decays.

## Examples {#Examples}

::: {#o28 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="WAVEWATCH"}

### WAVEWATCH {#WAVEWATCH}

The operational wave forecasting systems at [NOAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NOAA) are based on the WAVEWATCH III model.[^o29] This system has a global domain of approximately 50 km resolution, with nested regional domains for the northern hemisphere oceanic basins at approximately 18 km and approximately 7 km resolution. Physics includes wave field refraction, [nonlinear resonant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_resonance) interactions, sub-grid representations of unresolved islands, and dynamically updated ice coverage. Wind data is provided from the GDAS data assimilation system for the GFS weather model. Up to 2008, the model was limited to regions outside the surf zone where the waves are not strongly impacted by shallow depths.[^o30]

The model can incorporate the effects of currents on waves from its early design by [Hendrik Tolman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Tolman) in the 1990s, and is now extended for near shore applications.

:::

### WAM {#WAM}

The wave model WAM was the first so-called third generation prognostic wave model where the two-dimensional [wave spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_spectrum) was allowed to evolve freely (up to a cut-off frequency) with no constraints on the spectral shape.[^o32] The model underwent a series of software updates from its inception in the late 1980s.[^o33] The last official release is Cycle 4.5, maintained by the German [Helmholtz Zentrum, Geesthacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmholtz_Association_of_German_Research_Centres).[^o34]

ECMWF has incorporated WAM into its deterministic and [ensemble forecasting system](wikipedia://en/2451045).,[^o35] known as the [Integrated Forecast System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_Forecast_System) (IFS). The model currently comprises 36 frequency bins and 36 propagation directions at an average spatial resolution of 25 km. The model has been coupled to the atmospheric component of IFS since 1998.[^o36][^o37]

### Other models {#Other_models}

Wind wave forecasts are issued regionally by [Environment Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environment_Canada).[^o39]

Regional wave predictions are also produced by universities, such as [Texas A&M University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_A&M_University)'s use of the SWAN model (developed by [Delft University of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delft_University_of_Technology)) to forecast waves in the Gulf of Mexico.[^o40]

Another model, CCHE2D-COAST is a processes-based integrated model which is capable of simulating coastal processes in different coasts with complex shorelines such as irregular wave deformation from offshore to onshore, nearshore currents induced by radiation stresses, wave set-up, wave set-down, sediment transport, and seabed morphological changes.[^o41]

Other wind wave models include the [U.S. Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Navy) Standard Surf Model (NSSM).[^o42]

:::

## The formulae of Bretschneider, [Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basil_Wrigley_Wilson), and Young & Verhagen {#The_formulae_of_Bretschneider,_Wilson,_and_Young_&_Verhagen}

For determining wave growth in deep waters subjected to prolonged [fetch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_fetch), the basic formula set is:

$${{gH_s}\over{u_w^2}}=0.283$$

$${{gT_s}\over{u_w}}=7.54$$

Where:

$g$ = gravitational acceleration (m/s<sup>2</sup>)

$H_s$ = significant wave height (m)

$T_s$ = significant wave period (s)

$u_w$ = wind speed (m/s)

The constants in these formulas are deduced from empirical data. Factoring in water depth, wind fetch, and storm duration complicates the equations considerably. However, the application of [dimensionless values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless_quantity) facilitates the identification of patterns for all these variables. The dimensionless parameters employed are:

$$\widehat{H}={gH_s/u_w^2}$$

$$\widehat{T}={gT_s/u_w}$$

$$\widehat{d}={gd/u_w^2}$$

$$\widehat{F}={gF/u_w^2}$$

$$\widehat{t}={gt/u_w}$$

Where:

$d$ = water depth (m)

$F$ = wind fetch (m)

$t$ = storm duration (s)

When plotted against the dimensionless wind fetch, both dimensionless wave height and wave period tend to align linearly. However, this trend becomes notably more flattened for more extended dimensionless wind fetches. Various researchers have endeavoured to formulate equations capturing this observed behaviour.

*Image: DataWilson-Golfgroei.jpg.* Dimensionless wave height and period against the backdrop of the dimensionless fetch (data courtesy of Wilson, 1965)[^o44]

::: {#o46 .statement cue=named-heading name="Common Formulas for Deep Water"}

### Common Formulas for Deep Water {#Common_Formulas_for_Deep_Water}

Bretschneider (1952, 1977):

$$\widehat{H}=0.283 \tanh(0.0125\widehat{F})^{0.42}$$

$$\widehat{T}=7.54 \tanh(0.077\widehat{F})^{0.25}$$

Wilson (1965):[^o47]

$$\widehat{H}= 0.30 \{1-[1+0.004 \widehat{F}^{1/2}]^{-2}\}$$

$$\widehat{T}= 1.37 \{1-[1+0.008 \widehat{F}^{1/3}]^{-5}\}$$

*Image: Wave forecasting nomogram.png.* Wave growth chart based on the formulas by Groen & Dorrestein[^o48]

*Image: Youngverhagen.png.* Graph depicting the variation of significant wave height with dimensionless fetch based on the Young & Verhagen wave growth formula, set against a specific water depth and wind speed.

In the Netherlands, a formula devised by Groen & Dorrestein (1976) is also in common use:[^o48]

$\widehat{H}=0.24\tanh\left(0.015\widehat{F}\right)^{0.45}$ for $\widehat{F}>10$

$\widehat{T}=2\pi\tanh\left(0.0345\widehat{F}\right)^{0.37}$ for $\widehat{F}>400$

$\widehat{T}=0.502\widehat{F}^{0.225}$ for $10<\widehat{F}<400$

During periods when programmable computers weren't commonly utilised, these formulas were cumbersome to use. Consequently, for practical applications, [nomograms](wikipedia://en/271164) were developed which did away with dimensionless units, instead presenting wave heights in metres, storm duration in hours, and the wind fetch in km.

Integrating the water depth into the same chart was problematic as it introduced too many input parameters. Therefore, during the primary usage of nomograms, separate nomograms were crafted for distinct depths. The use of computers has resulted in reduced reliance on nomograms.

For deep water, the distinctions between the various formulas are subtle. However, for shallow water, the formula modified by Young & Verhagen[^o49] proves more suitable. It's defined as:

$$\widehat {H}=0.241 \left(\tanh{A_H}\tanh{{B_H}\over{\tanh{A_H}}} \right)^{0.87}$$

$A_H=0.493 \widehat{d}^{0.75}$ and $B_H=0.00313\widehat{F}^{0.57}$

and

$$\widehat {T}=7.519 \left(\tanh{A_T}\tanh{{B_T}\over{\tanh{A_T}}} \right)^{0.387}$$

$A_T=0.331 \widehat{d}^{1.01}$ and $B_T=0.0005215\widehat{F}^{0.73}$

Research by Bart demonstrated that, under Dutch conditions (for example, in the [IJsselmeer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IJsselmeer)), this formula is reliable.[^o50]

#### Example: Lake Garda {#Example:_Lake_Garda}

[Lake Garda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Garda) in Italy is a deep, elongated lake, measuring about 350 m in depth and spanning 45 km in length. With a wind speed of 25 m/s from the SSW, the Bretschneider and Wilson formulas suggest an H<sub>s</sub> of 3.5 m and a period of roughly 7 s (assuming the storm persists for at least 4 hours). The Young and Verhagen formula, however, predicts a lower wave height of 2.6 m. This diminished result is attributed to the formula's calibration for shallow waters, whilst Lake Garda is notably deep.

## Shallow and coastal waters {#Shallow_and_coastal_waters}

Global wind wave models such as [WAVEWATCH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WAVEWATCH) and WAM are not reliable in shallow water areas near the coast. To address this issue, the SWAN (Simulating WAves Nearshore) program was developed in 1993 by [Delft University of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delft_University_of_Technology), in collaboration with [Rijkswaterstaat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rijkswaterstaat) and the [Office of Naval Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Naval_Research) in the United States.[^o59][^o60] Initially, the main focus of this development was on wave changes due to the effects of [breaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breaking_wave), [refraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refraction), and the like. The program was subsequently developed to include analysis of wave growth.[^o61]

SWAN essentially calculates the energy of a wave field (in the form of a [wave spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_spectrum)) and derives the significant wave height from this spectrum. SWAN lacks a [user interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User_environment) for easily creating input files and presenting the output. The program is [open-source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-source_software), and many institutions and companies have since developed their own user environments for SWAN. The program has become a global standard for such calculations, and can be used in both one-dimensional and two-dimensional modes.[^o62]

### One-dimensional approach {#One-dimensional_approach}

*Image: Golfprofiel-Westerschelde-en.jpg.* Wave growth in the [Western Scheldt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Scheldt)

The computation time for a calculation with SWAN is in the order of seconds. In one-dimensional mode, results are available from the input of a cross-sectional profile and wind information. In many cases, this can yield a sufficiently reliable value for the local wave spectrum, particularly when the wind path crosses shallow areas.

#### Example: wave growth calculation in The Netherlands {#Example:_wave_growth_calculation_in_The_Netherlands}

As an example, a calculation of the wave growth in the Westerschelde has been made. For this example, the one-dimensional version of SWAN and the open-source user interface SwanOne were used.[^o65] The wave height at the base of the sea dike near Goudorpe on [South Beveland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Beveland), just west of the [Westerscheldetunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westerscheldetunnel), was calculated, with the wind coming from the SW at a speed of 25 m/s (force 9 to 10). In the graph, this is from left to right. The dike is quite far from deep water, with a [salt marsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_marsh) in front of it.

The calculation was made for low water, average water level, and high water. At high tide, the salt marsh is under water; at low tide, only the salt marsh is submerged (the tidal difference here is about 5 metres). At high tide, there is a constant increase in wave height, which is faster in deep water than in shallow water. At low tide, some plates are dry, and wave growth has to start all over again. Close to the shore (beyond the Gat van Borssele), there's a tall salt marsh; at low tide, there are no waves there, at average tide, the wave height decreases to almost nothing at the dike, and at high tide, there's still a wave height of 1 m present. The measure of period shown in these graphs is the [spectral period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_spectrum#Spectral_moment) (T<sub>m-1,0</sub>).

## Validation {#Validation}

Comparison of the wave model forecasts with observations is essential for characterizing model deficiencies and identifying areas for improvement. In-situ observations are obtained from buoys, ships and oil platforms. [Altimetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altimetry) data from satellites, such as [GEOSAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosat) and [TOPEX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TOPEX), can also be used to infer the characteristics of wind waves.

Hindcasts of wave models during extreme conditions also serves as a useful test bed for the models.[^o70]

## Reanalyses {#Reanalyses}

A retrospective analysis, or reanalysis, combines all available observations with a physical model to describe the state of a system over a time period of decades. Wind waves are a part of both the NCEP Reanalysis[^o72] and the [ERA-40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ERA-40) from the ECMWF.[^o73] Such resources permit the creation of monthly wave climatologies, and can track the variation of wave activity on interannual and multi-decadal time scales. During the northern hemisphere winter, the most intense wave activity is located in the central North Pacific south of the Aleutians, and in the central North Atlantic south of Iceland. During the southern hemisphere winter, intense wave activity circumscribes the pole at around 50°S, with 5 m significant wave heights typical in the southern Indian Ocean.[^o73]
