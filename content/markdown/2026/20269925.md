# Free convolution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Free convolution"}

**Free convolution** is the [free probability](wikipedia://en/679696) analog of the classical notion of [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) of [probability measures](wikipedia://en/45437). Due to the non-commutative nature of free probability theory, one has to talk separately about additive and multiplicative free convolution, which arise from addition and multiplication of free random variables (see below; in the classical case, what would be the analog of free multiplicative convolution can be reduced to additive convolution by passing to logarithms of random variables). These operations have some interpretations in terms of [empirical spectral measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_spectral_measures) of [random matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_matrices).[^o2]

:::

The notion of free convolution was introduced by [Dan-Virgil Voiculescu](wikipedia://en/7656821).[^o3][^o4]

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Free additive convolution"}

## Free additive convolution {#Free_additive_convolution}

Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be two probability measures on the real line, and assume that $X$ is a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) in a non commutative [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325) with law $\mu$ and $Y$ is a random variable in the same non commutative probability space with law $\nu$. Assume finally that $X$ and $Y$ are [freely independent](wikipedia://en/25130943). Then the **free additive convolution** $\mu\boxplus\nu$ is the law of $X+Y$. [Random matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_matrices) interpretation: if $A$ and $B$ are some independent $n$ by $n$ Hermitian (resp. real symmetric) random matrices such that at least one of them is invariant, in law, under conjugation by any unitary (resp. orthogonal) matrix and such that the [empirical spectral measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_spectral_measures) of $A$ and $B$ tend respectively to $\mu$ and $\nu$ as $n$ tends to infinity, then the empirical spectral measure of $A+B$ tends to $\mu\boxplus\nu$.[^o7]

In many cases, it is possible to compute the probability measure $\mu\boxplus\nu$ explicitly by using complex-analytic techniques and the [R-Transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R-Transform) of the measures $\mu$ and $\nu$.

:::

## Relationship with classical convolution {#Relationship_with_classical_convolution}

There exists a precise functional relationship between the free convolution and classical convolution of compactly-supported probability measures: the expectation of $f$ over the free convolution $\mu\boxplus\nu$ is at most the expectation of $f$ over the classical convolution, provided that the fourth derivative of $f$ is non-negative. The non-negativity of the fourth derivative is also a necessary condition for the comparison to hold true for all compactly-supported $\mu$ and $\nu$.[^o9]

## Rectangular free additive convolution {#Rectangular_free_additive_convolution}

The rectangular free additive convolution (with ratio $c$) $\boxplus_c$ has also been defined in the non commutative probability framework by Benaych-Georges[^o11] and admits the following [random matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_matrices) interpretation. For $c\in [0,1]$, for $A$ and $B$ are some independent $n$ by $p$ complex (resp. real) random matrices such that at least one of them is invariant, in law, under multiplication on the left and on the right by any unitary (resp. orthogonal) matrix and such that the [empirical singular values distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_singular_values_distribution) of $A$ and $B$ tend respectively to $\mu$ and $\nu$ as $n$ and $p$ tend to infinity in such a way that $n/p$ tends to $c$, then the [empirical singular values distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_singular_values_distribution) of $A+B$ tends to $\mu\boxplus_c\nu$.[^o12]

In many cases, it is possible to compute the probability measure $\mu\boxplus_c\nu$ explicitly by using complex-analytic techniques and the rectangular R-transform with ratio $c$ of the measures $\mu$ and $\nu$.

::: {#o14 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Free multiplicative convolution"}

## Free multiplicative convolution {#Free_multiplicative_convolution}

Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be two probability measures on the interval $[0,+\infty)$, and assume that $X$ is a random variable in a non commutative probability space with law $\mu$ and $Y$ is a random variable in the same non commutative probability space with law $\nu$. Assume finally that $X$ and $Y$ are [freely independent](wikipedia://en/25130943). Then the **free multiplicative convolution** $\mu\boxtimes\nu$ is the law of $X^{1/2}YX^{1/2}$ (or, equivalently, the law of $Y^{1/2}XY^{1/2}$. [Random matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_matrices) interpretation: if $A$ and $B$ are some independent $n$ by $n$ non negative Hermitian (resp. real symmetric) random matrices such that at least one of them is invariant, in law, under conjugation by any unitary (resp. orthogonal) matrix and such that the [empirical spectral measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_spectral_measures) of $A$ and $B$ tend respectively to $\mu$ and $\nu$ as $n$ tends to infinity, then the empirical spectral measure of $AB$ tends to $\mu\boxtimes\nu$.[^o15]

A similar definition can be made in the case of laws $\mu,\nu$ supported on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) $\{z:|z|=1\}$, with an orthogonal or unitary [random matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_matrices) interpretation.

Explicit computations of multiplicative free convolution can be carried out using complex-analytic techniques and the S-transform.

:::

::: {#o17 .application cue=heading name="Applications of free convolution"}

## Applications of free convolution {#Applications_of_free_convolution}

- Free convolution can be used to give a proof of the free [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406).
- Free convolution can be used to compute the laws and spectra of sums or products of random variables which are free. Such examples include: [random walk](wikipedia://en/235451) operators on free groups (Kesten measures); and [asymptotic distribution](wikipedia://en/3001287) of eigenvalues of sums or products of independent [random matrices](wikipedia://en/1648765).

Through its applications to random matrices, free convolution has some strong connections with other works on G-estimation of Girko.

The applications in [wireless communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_communication), [finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance) and [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology) have provided a useful framework when the number of observations is of the same order as the dimensions of the system.
