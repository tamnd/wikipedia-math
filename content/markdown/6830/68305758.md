# Urey–Bigeleisen–Mayer equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Urey–Bigeleisen–Mayer equation"}

In [stable isotope geochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_isotope_geochemistry), the **Urey–Bigeleisen–Mayer equation**, also known as the **Bigeleisen–Mayer equation** or the **Urey model**,[^o2] is a model describing the approximate equilibrium [isotope fractionation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope_fractionation) in an isotope exchange reaction.[^o3][^o4][^o5][^o6][^o7] While the equation itself can be written in numerous forms, it is generally presented as a ratio of [partition functions](wikipedia://en/312252) of the isotopic molecules involved in a given reaction.[^o8][^o9] The Urey–Bigeleisen–Mayer equation is widely applied in the fields of [quantum chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chemistry) and [geochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geochemistry) and is often modified or paired with other quantum chemical modelling methods (such as [density functional theory](wikipedia://en/209874)) to improve accuracy and precision and reduce the [computational cost](wikipedia://en/6511) of calculations.[^o2][^o7][^o10]

:::

The equation was first introduced by [Harold Urey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Urey) and, independently, by [Jacob Bigeleisen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Bigeleisen) and [Maria Goeppert Mayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Goeppert_Mayer) in 1947.[^o3][^o8][^o9]

## Description {#Description}

Since its original descriptions, the Urey–Bigeleisen–Mayer equation has taken many forms. Given an isotopic exchange reaction $A+B^*=A^*+B$, such that $^*$ denotes a molecule containing an isotope of interest, the equation can be expressed by relating the [equilibrium constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_constant), $K_{{eq}}$, to the product of [partition function](wikipedia://en/16846849) ratios, namely the [translational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translational_partition_function), [rotational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_partition_function), [vibrational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibrational_partition_function), and sometimes electronic partition functions.[^o12][^o13][^o14] Thus the equation can be written as: $K_{eq} = \frac{[A^*][B]}{[A][B^*]}$ where $[A]=\prod^n Q_{n,A}$ and $Q_n$ is each respective partition function of molecule or atom $A$.[^o14][^o15] It is typical to approximate the rotational partition function ratio as quantized [rotational energies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_energy) in a [rigid rotor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigid_rotor) system.[^o13][^o16] The Urey model also treats [molecular vibrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_vibration) as simplified [harmonic oscillators](wikipedia://en/13899) and follows the [Born–Oppenheimer approximation](wikipedia://en/68946).[^o13][^o16][^o17]

Isotope partitioning behavior is often reported as a **reduced partition function ratio**, a simplified form of the Bigeleisen–Mayer equation notated mathematically as $\frac{s}{s'}f$ or $(\frac{Q^*}{Q})_r$.[^o18][^o19] The reduced partition function ratio can be derived from [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) expansion of the function and allows the partition functions to be expressed in terms of frequency.[^o18][^o20][^o21] It can be used to relate molecular vibrations and intermolecular forces to equilibrium isotope effects.[^o22]

As the model is an approximation, many applications append corrections for improved accuracy.[^o17] Some common, significant modifications to the equation include accounting for pressure effects,[^o23] nuclear geometry,[^o24] and corrections for [anharmonicity](wikipedia://en/3075539) and quantum mechanical effects.[^o2][^o3][^o25][^o26] For example, [hydrogen isotope exchange reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen–deuterium_exchange) have been shown to disagree with the requisite assumptions for the model but correction techniques using [path integral methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_integral_formulation) have been suggested.[^o2][^o9][^o27]

## History of discovery {#History_of_discovery}

One aim of the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project) was increasing the availability of concentrated radioactive and stable isotopes, in particular [<sup>14</sup>C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon-14), [<sup>35</sup>S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur-35), [<sup>32</sup>P](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus-32), and [deuterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deuterium) for [heavy water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_water).[^o29] [Harold Urey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Urey), Nobel laureate physical chemist known for his discovery of deuterium,[^o30] became its head of isotope separation research while a professor at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University).[^o31][^o32] In 1945, he joined [The Institute for Nuclear Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi_Institute) at the University of Chicago, where he continued to work with chemist [Jacob Bigeleisen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Bigeleisen) and physicist [Maria Mayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Goeppert_Mayer), both also veterans of isotopic research in the Manhattan Project.[^o13][^o31][^o33][^o34] In 1946, Urey delivered the [Liversidge lecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liversidge_Award) at the then-[Royal Institute of Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institute_of_Chemistry), where he outlined his proposed model of stable isotope fractionation.[^o3][^o8][^o13] Bigeleisen and Mayer had been working on similar work since at least 1944 and, in 1947, published their model independently from Urey.[^o3][^o9][^o13] Their calculations were mathematically equivalent to a 1943 derivation of the reduced partition function by German physicist [Ludwig Waldmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Waldmann).[^o9][^o13]

## Applications {#Applications}

Initially used to approximate chemical [reaction rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_rate),[^o8][^o9] models of isotope fractionation are used throughout the [physical sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_sciences). In [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry), the Urey–Bigeleisen–Mayer equation has been used to predict [equilibrium isotope effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_constant#Effect_of_isotopic_substitution) and interpret the distributions of isotopes and [isotopologues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopologue) within systems, especially as deviations from their [natural abundance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_abundance).[^o37][^o38] The model is also used to explain [isotopic shifts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopic_shift) in [spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy), such as those from nuclear field effects or [mass independent effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass-independent_fractionation).[^o2][^o24][^o37] In biochemistry, it is used to model [enzymatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme) [kinetic isotope effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_isotope_effect).[^o39][^o40] [Simulation testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model-based_testing) in [computational systems biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_systems_biology) often uses the Bigeleisen–Mayer model as a [baseline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseline_%28science%29) in the development of more complex [models of biological systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modelling_biological_systems).[^o41][^o42] Isotope fractionation modeling is a critical component of [isotope geochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope_geochemistry) and can be used to reconstruct [past Earth environments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoecology) as well as examine [surface processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomorphology).[^o43][^o44][^o45][^o46]
