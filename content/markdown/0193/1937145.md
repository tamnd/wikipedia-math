# S wave

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="S wave"}

In [solid mechanics](wikipedia://en/458866), **S waves**, **secondary waves**, or **shear waves** (sometimes called **elastic S waves**) are a type of [elastic wave](wikipedia://en/301429#Elastic_wave) and are one of the two main types of elastic [body waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_wave_%28seismology%29), so named because they move through the body of an object, unlike [surface waves](wikipedia://en/41763).[^o2]

:::

S waves are [transverse waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transverse_wave), meaning that the direction of [particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle) movement of an S wave is perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation, and the main restoring force comes from [shear stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_stress).[^o3] Therefore, S waves cannot propagate in liquids[^o4] with zero (or very low) [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754); however, they may propagate in liquids with high viscosity.[^o5][^o6] Similarly, S waves cannot travel through gases.

The name *secondary wave* comes from the fact that they are the second type of wave to be detected by an earthquake [seismograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismograph), after the [compressional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compressional_wave) primary wave, or [P wave](wikipedia://en/1343550), because S waves travel more slowly in solids. Unlike P waves, S waves cannot travel through the molten [outer core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_outer_core) of the Earth, and this causes a [shadow zone](wikipedia://en/5035905) for S waves opposite to their origin. They can still propagate through the solid [inner core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_inner_core): when a P wave strikes the boundary of molten and solid cores at an oblique angle, S waves will form and propagate in the solid medium. When these S waves hit the boundary again at an oblique angle, they will in turn create P waves that propagate through the liquid medium. This property allows [seismologists](wikipedia://en/28935) to determine some physical properties of the Earth's inner core.[^o7]

## History {#History}

In 1830, the mathematician [Siméon Denis Poisson](wikipedia://en/44670) presented to the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences) an essay ("memoir") with a theory of the propagation of elastic waves in solids. In his memoir, he states that an earthquake would produce two different waves: one having a certain speed $a$ and the other having a speed $\frac{a}{\sqrt 3}$. At a sufficient distance from the source, when they can be considered [plane waves](wikipedia://en/41559) in the region of interest, the first kind consists of expansions and compressions in the direction perpendicular to the wavefront (that is, parallel to the wave's direction of motion); while the second consists of stretching motions occurring in directions parallel to the front (perpendicular to the direction of motion).[^o9]

## Theory {#Theory}

### Isotropic medium {#Isotropic_medium}

*Image: Speeds of seismic waves.svg.* Velocity of [seismic waves](wikipedia://en/89830) in the Earth versus depth. The negligible S wave velocity in the outer core occurs because it is liquid, while in the solid inner core the S wave velocity is non-zero.

For the purpose of this explanation, a solid medium is considered [isotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotropic) if its [strain (deformation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_%28mechanics%29) in response to [stress](wikipedia://en/228107) is the same in all directions. Let $\boldsymbol{u} = (u_1,u_2,u_3)$ be the displacement [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28mathematics%29) of a particle of such a medium from its "resting" position $\boldsymbol{x}=(x_1,x_2,x_3)$ due elastic vibrations, understood to be a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) of the rest position $\boldsymbol{x}$ and time $t$. The deformation of the medium at that point can be described by the [strain tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_tensor) $\boldsymbol{e}$, the 3×3 matrix whose elements are

$$e_{i j} = \tfrac{1}{2} \left( \partial_i u_j + \partial_j u_i \right)$$

where $\partial_i$ denotes partial derivative with respect to position coordinate $x_i$. The strain tensor is related to the 3×3 [stress tensor](wikipedia://en/3323565) $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ by the equation

$$\tau_{i j} = \lambda\delta_{i j}\sum_{k} e_{k k} + 2\mu e_{i j}$$

Here $\delta_{ij}$ is the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890) (1 if $i = j$, 0 otherwise) and $\lambda$ and $\mu$ are the [Lamé parameters](wikipedia://en/8983708) ($\mu$ being the material's [shear modulus](wikipedia://en/1575447)). It follows that

$$\tau_{i j} = \lambda\delta_{i j} \sum_{k} \partial_k u_k + \mu \left( \partial_i u_j + \partial_j u_i \right)$$

From [Newton's law of inertia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_2nd_law), one also gets

$$\rho \partial_t^2 u_i = \sum_j \partial_j\tau_{i j}$$

where $\rho$ is the [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) (mass per unit volume) of the medium at that point, and $\partial_t$ denotes partial derivative with respect to time. Combining the last two equations one gets the *seismic wave equation in homogeneous media*

$$\rho \partial_t^2 u_i = \lambda\partial_i \sum_k \partial_k u_k + \mu\sum_j \bigl(\partial_i\partial_j u_j + \partial_j\partial_j u_i\bigr)$$

Using the [nabla operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabla_operator) notation of [vector calculus](wikipedia://en/32640), $\nabla = (\partial_1, \partial_2, \partial_3)$, with some approximations, this equation can be written as

$$\rho \partial_t^2 \boldsymbol{u} = \left(\lambda + 2\mu \right) \nabla\left(\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u}\right) - \mu\nabla \times \left(\nabla \times \boldsymbol{u}\right)$$

Taking the [curl](wikipedia://en/6123) of this equation and applying vector identities, one gets

$$\partial_t^2(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{u}) = \frac{\mu}{\rho}\nabla^2 \left(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{u}\right)$$

This formula is the [wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691) applied to the vector quantity $\nabla\times \boldsymbol{u}$, which is the material's shear strain. Its solutions, the S waves, are [linear combinations](wikipedia://en/55632) of [sinusoidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinusoidal) [plane waves](wikipedia://en/41559) of various [wavelengths](wikipedia://en/33125) and directions of propagation, but all with the same speed $\beta = \sqrt{\mu/\rho}$. Assuming that the medium of propagation is linear, elastic, isotropic, and homogeneous, this equation can be rewritten as $\mu=\rho \beta^2=\rho \omega^2 / k^2$[^o12] where *ω* is the angular frequency and *$k$* is the wavenumber. Thus, $\beta = \omega / k$.

Taking the [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) of seismic wave equation in homogeneous media, instead of the curl, yields a wave equation describing propagation of the quantity $\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u}$, which is the material's compression strain. The solutions of this equation, the P waves, travel at the faster speed $\alpha = \sqrt{(\lambda + 2\mu)/\rho}$.

The [steady state](wikipedia://en/1326107) SH waves are defined by the [Helmholtz equation](wikipedia://en/1156215)[^o13]

$$\left(\nabla^2 + k^2 \right) \boldsymbol{u}=0$$

where $k$ is the wave number.

### S waves in viscoelastic materials {#S_waves_in_viscoelastic_materials}

Similar to in an elastic medium, in a [viscoelastic](wikipedia://en/711288) material, the speed of a shear wave is described by a similar relationship $c(\omega) = \omega / k(\omega)=\sqrt{\mu(\omega)/\rho}$, however, here, $\mu$ is a complex, frequency-dependent shear modulus and $c(\omega)$ is the frequency dependent phase velocity.[^o12] One common approach to describing the shear modulus in viscoelastic materials is through the [Voigt Model](wikipedia://en/2061865) which states: $\mu(\omega)=\mu_0+i\omega\eta$, where $\mu_0$ is the stiffness of the material and $\eta$ is the viscosity.[^o12]

## S wave technology {#S_wave_technology}

### Magnetic resonance elastography {#Magnetic_resonance_elastography}

[Magnetic resonance elastography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_elastography) (MRE) is a method for studying the properties of biological materials in living organisms by propagating shear waves at desired frequencies throughout the desired organic tissue.[^o17] This method uses a vibrator to send the shear waves into the tissue and [magnetic resonance imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging) to view the response in the tissue.[^o18] The measured wave speed and wavelengths are then measured to determine elastic properties such as the [shear modulus](wikipedia://en/1575447#:~:text=In_materials_science,_shear_modulus,Common_symbols). MRE has seen use in studies of a variety of human tissues including liver, brain, and bone tissues.[^o17]
