# William Fleetwood Sheppard

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="William Fleetwood Sheppard"}

**William Fleetwood Sheppard** (20 November 1863 – 12 October 1936) was an Australian-British [civil servant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_servant), [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and [statistician](wikipedia://en/48661) remembered for his work in [finite differences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_differences), [interpolation](wikipedia://en/14569) and statistical theory, known in particular for the eponymous [Sheppard's corrections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheppard's_corrections).

:::

## Life {#Life}

William Fleetwood Sheppard was born near [Sydney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney), Australia. He was the second child of [Edmund Sheppard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Sheppard), an Englishman who had gone to Australia in 1859, and his wife Mary Grace Murray; the couple had married in 1860. Edmund Sheppard was a lawyer and became a judge of the [Supreme Court of Queensland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Queensland). When he was about ten William was sent to [Brisbane Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brisbane_Grammar_School). However he stayed for only one term for the headmaster believed that the school could not do justice to such a brilliant pupil and that he had better go to school in [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England).

In England Sheppard went to [Charterhouse School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charterhouse_School) where he had a very successful academic career and was finally head of the school. He went to [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge) as a Foundation Scholar. When he graduated in 1884 it was as the [Senior Wrangler](wikipedia://en/19355609) (the mathematics student with the highest mark). Shortly after the results came out, a letter found him, addressed only to "The Senior Wrangler, Cambridge." The third wrangler was [William Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Bragg) and the fourth was [W. H. Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Young). All the top wranglers that year were coached by [E. J. Routh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Routh).

After some years preparing to become a [barrister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrister) and doing legal work, Sheppard joined the Education Department as a Junior Examiner in 1896. By 1914 he had been made an Assistant Secretary at the Board of Education. Sheppard had an extraordinary capacity for work; he would get up at 5 or 6 in the morning and work until 10 or 11 in the evening. He retired in 1921 at the age of 58. To save money, the government had asked all civil servants who would be retiring in the next few years to retire immediately; this was part of the [Geddes Axe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geddes_Axe). Sheppard could not afford to retire and so he took on work as an examiner for the Cambridge School Certificate and the [University of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_London). This was a great burden because he was amazingly thorough, recording all the errors that were made and the marks given to ensure consistency of marking across thousands of scripts.

Sheppard had a parallel and equally distinguished career as a mathematician, distinguished enough for him to receive the degree of Sc. D. from Cambridge in 1908 and was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society_of_Edinburgh) in 1932. His proposers were Sir [Edmund Taylor Whittaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Taylor_Whittaker), [George James Lidstone](wikipedia://en/25446803), [Alexander Aitken](wikipedia://en/3348690) and [William Ogilvy Kermack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Ogilvy_Kermack).[^o4]

Sheppard was not unique in combining a civil service career with mathematical research; an almost exact contemporary was [Thomas Little Heath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Little_Heath), the historian of Greek mathematics, who went higher in the Civil Service and became much better known as an author. Another contemporary was the civil servant and statistician [R H Hooker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reginald_Hawthorn_Hooker). Sheppard published his first paper in 1888 and then he published nothing during his legal years. Then he resumed publishing in 1897 and kept going until 1931.

Sheppard was encouraged to turn his mathematical skills to statistics by [Francis Galton](wikipedia://en/49072), whom he had met during his Cambridge days when he visited Galton's Anthropometric Laboratory. In his memorial piece [Aitken](wikipedia://en/3348690) placed Sheppard with [Edgeworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Ysidro_Edgeworth), [Pearson](wikipedia://en/164695) and [Yule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Udny_Yule) as a contributor to the development of statistics at the turn of the 20th. century. The assessment was based on the series of papers on correlation and the calculation of moments that Sheppard produced between 1897 and 1907. The first Sheppard's correction paper was in 1897. After 1907 the focus of Sheppard's work moved from statistics to interpolation and graduation and he published in mathematical and actuarial journals. The characteristics of Sheppard's work that Aitken emphasised were "thoroughness and independence." "Agility, shafts of brilliance, these are not to be found; but there is not a trace of the superficiality which sometimes goes with these qualities." At the fifth [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) held in 1912 in [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge), Sampson presented a paper entitled *Reduction of errors by means of negligible differences*.[^o5]

When Sheppard died he was remembered in the Mathematical Gazette with a one-page note; Sheppard had been president of the Mathematical Association in 1928. The [Annals of Eugenics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annals_of_Eugenics) published a long family memoir by Sheppard's son and an account of Sheppard's scientific work by Aitken. (These pieces form the basis of the present article.) The editor of the journal, [Ronald Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824), also contributed a piece. Fisher knew Sheppard through his work on mathematical tables. Of Sheppard's work, Fisher wrote, "We find practically nothing that ought to be retracted, and very little that is now obsolete." However, it is clear from Fisher's article and from the notes he added to Aitken's that Sheppard's main virtue was that he was not [Karl Pearson](wikipedia://en/164695). Pearson had died some months previously and this appreciation of Sheppard's life is uncomfortably like a disappreciation of Pearson's.

## Some publications of W. F. Sheppard {#Some_publications_of_W._F._Sheppard}

- W. F. Sheppard (1897) ["On the Calculation of the Average Square, Cube, of a Large Number of Magnitudes"](https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/pt?id=uc1.b4148221&view=1up&seq=734), *[Journal of the Royal Statistical Society](wikipedia://en/13327143)*, 60, 698–703.
- W. F. Sheppard (1898) "On the Calculation of the Most Probable Values of Frequency Constants for data arranged according to Equidistant Divisions of a Scale", *Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society*, 29, 353–80.
- W. F. Sheppard (1899) "On the Application of the Theory of Error to Cases of Normal Distribution and Normal Correlation", *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society, A*, 192,101–167+531.
- W. F. Sheppard (1907) "Table of Deviates of the Normal Curve", *Biometrika*, v. 404–406.
- W. F. Sheppard (1921) "Reduction of Error by Linear Compounding", *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society, A*, 221, 199–237.
- W. F. Sheppard (1923) [*From Determinant to Tensor*](https://books.google.com/books?id=5GEgAAAAIAAJ), Clarendon Press Oxford.[^o9]

A bibliography appears with the obituaries in the *Annals of Eugenics*. It contains 40 publications, the first in 1888 and the last in 1931.

- "William Fleetwood Sheppard", *Mathematical Gazette*, 20, December 1936.

- [R. A. Fisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._A._Fisher) (1937) "The Character of Sheppard’s Work", *Annals of Eugenics*, 8, 11–12.
- Donald A. MacKenzie, *Statistics in Britain 1865–1930 : the Social Construction of Scientific Knowledge*, Edinburgh University Press. 1981.

MacKenzie is particularly interested in the relationship between Sheppard and Galton. The standard modern histories and encyclopedias pay little attention to Sheppard. See, however,

- A. Hald (2001) "On the History of the Correction for Grouping, 1873–1922", *Scandinavian Journal of Statistics*, 28, 417–428.

This puts Sheppard's work into a story that begins with [Thiele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorvald_Thiele) (1873) and ends with [Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824) (1922).
