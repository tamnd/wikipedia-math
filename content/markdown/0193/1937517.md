# XDH assumption

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="XDH assumption"}

The **external Diffie–Hellman (XDH) assumption** is a [computational hardness assumption](wikipedia://en/6158383) used in [elliptic curve cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curve_cryptography). The XDH assumption holds if there exist certain [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) of elliptic curves which have useful properties for cryptography. Specifically, XDH implies the existence of two distinct [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) $\langle{\mathbb G}_1, {\mathbb G}_2\rangle$ with the following properties:

:::

1. The [discrete logarithm problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_logarithm_problem) (DLP), the [computational Diffie–Hellman problem](wikipedia://en/1937391) (CDH), and the [co-computational Diffie–Hellman problem](wikipedia://en/1937391#Variations_of_the_computational_Diffie–Hellman_assumption_in_product_groups) are all intractable in ${\mathbb G}_1$ and ${\mathbb G}_2$.
1. There exists an efficiently computable [bilinear map](wikipedia://en/4365) (pairing) $e(\cdot, \cdot) : {\mathbb G}_1 \times {\mathbb G}_2 \rightarrow {\mathbb G}_T$.
1. The [decisional Diffie–Hellman problem](wikipedia://en/1198015) (DDH) is intractable in ${\mathbb G}_1$.

The above formulation is referred to as **asymmetric XDH**. A stronger version of the assumption (**symmetric XDH**, or **SXDH**) holds if [DDH](wikipedia://en/1198015) is *also* intractable in ${\mathbb G}_2$.

The XDH assumption is used in some [pairing-based](wikipedia://en/8605953) cryptographic protocols. In certain elliptic curve subgroups, the existence of an efficiently-computable [bilinear map](wikipedia://en/4365) (pairing) can allow for practical solutions to the [DDH](wikipedia://en/1198015) problem. These groups, referred to as [gap Diffie–Hellman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gap_Diffie–Hellman) (GDH) groups, facilitate a variety of novel cryptographic protocols, including tri-partite [key exchange](wikipedia://en/578092), [identity based encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_based_encryption), and [secret handshakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_handshakes) (to name a few). However, the ease of computing DDH within a GDH group can also be an obstacle when constructing cryptosystems; for example, it is not possible to use DDH-based cryptosystems such as [ElGamal](wikipedia://en/59458) within a GDH group. Because the DDH assumption holds within at least one of a pair of XDH groups, these groups can be used to construct pairing-based protocols which allow for ElGamal-style encryption and other novel cryptographic techniques.

In practice, it is believed that the XDH assumption may hold in certain subgroups of [MNT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MNT_curve) elliptic curves. This notion was first proposed by Scott (2002), and later by [Boneh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Boneh), Boyen and Shacham (2002) as a means to improve the efficiency of a signature scheme. The assumption was formally defined by Ballard, Green, de Medeiros and Monrose (2005), and full details of a proposed implementation were advanced in that work. Evidence for the validity of this assumption is the proof by Verheul (2001) and Galbraith and Rotger (2004) of the non-existence of [distortion maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distortion_map) in two specific elliptic curve subgroups which possess an efficiently computable pairing. As pairings and distortion maps are currently the only known means to solve the DDH problem in elliptic curve groups, it is believed that the DDH assumption therefore holds in these subgroups, while pairings are still feasible between elements in distinct groups.
