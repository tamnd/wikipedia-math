# Radius of curvature

*Image: Radius of curvature.svg.* Radius of curvature and [center of curvature](wikipedia://en/5816420)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Radius of curvature"}

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), the **radius of curvature**, $R$, is the [reciprocal](wikipedia://en/229940) of the [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770). For a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246), it equals the [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) of the [circular arc](wikipedia://en/5578920) which best approximates the curve at that point. For [surfaces](wikipedia://en/4342970), the radius of curvature is the radius of a circle that best fits a [normal section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_section) or [combinations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_radius#Combinations) thereof.[^o2][^o3][^o4]

:::

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

In the case of a [space curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_curve), the radius of curvature is the length of the [curvature vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curvature_vector).

In the case of a [plane curve](wikipedia://en/971691), then $R$ is the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of[^o4]

$$R \equiv \left|\frac{ds}{d\varphi} \right| = \frac{1}{\kappa},$$

where $s$ is the [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) from a fixed point on the curve, $\varphi$ is the [tangential angle](wikipedia://en/15785733) and $\kappa$ is the [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770#Curvature).

:::

::: {#o8 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formula"}

## Formula {#Formula}

### In two dimensions {#In_two_dimensions}

If the curve is given in [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) as $y(x)$, i.e., as the [graph of a function](wikipedia://en/87352), then the radius of curvature is (assuming the curve is differentiable up to order 2)

$$R =\left| \frac { \left(1 + y'^{\,2}\right)^\frac32}{y''}\right|\,,$$

where $y' = \frac{dy}{dx}\,,$ $y'' = \frac{d^2y}{dx^2},$ and $\lvert z\rvert$ denotes the absolute value of $z$.

If the curve is given [parametrically](wikipedia://en/576108) by functions $x(t)$ and $y(t)$, then the radius of curvature is

$$R = \left|\frac{ds}{d\varphi}\right| = \left|\frac {\left({\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2}\right)^\frac32}{\dot {x}\ddot{y} - \dot{y}\ddot{x}}\right|$$

where $\dot{x} = \frac{dx}{dt},$ $\ddot{x} = \frac{d^2x}{dt^2},$ $\dot{y} = \frac{dy}{dt},$ and $\ddot{y} = \frac{d^2y}{dt^2}.$

Heuristically, this result can be interpreted as[^o3]

$$R = \frac{\left|\mathbf{v}\right|^3}{\left| \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{ \dot v} \right|}\,,$$

where

$$\left| \mathbf{v} \right| = \big| (\dot x, \dot y) \big| = R \frac{d\varphi}{dt}\,.$$

### In $n$ dimensions {#In_dimensions}

If $\mathbf{\gamma} : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is a parametrized curve in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ then the radius of curvature at each point of the curve, $\rho : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, is given by[^o4]

$$\rho = \frac{\left|\boldsymbol\gamma'\right|^3}{\sqrt{\left|\boldsymbol\gamma'\right|^2 \, \left|\boldsymbol\gamma''\right|^2 - \left(\boldsymbol\gamma' \cdot \boldsymbol\gamma''\right)^2}}\,.$$

As a special case, if $f(t)$ is a function from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{R}$, then the radius of curvature of its [graph](wikipedia://en/87352), $\mathbf{\gamma}(t) = (t, f (t))$, is

$$\rho(t)=\frac{\left|1+f'^{\,2}(t)\right|^\frac32}{\left|f''(t)\right|}.$$

### Derivation {#Derivation}

Let $\mathbf{\gamma}$ be as above, and fix $t$. We want to find the radius $\rho$ of a parametrized circle which matches $\gamma$ in its zeroth, first, and second derivatives at $t$. Clearly the radius will not depend on the position $\mathbf{\gamma}(t)$, only on the velocity $\mathbf{\gamma}'(t)$ and acceleration $\mathbf{\gamma}''(t)$. There are only three independent [scalars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_product) that can be obtained from two vectors $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$, namely $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w}$, and $\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{w}$. Thus the radius of curvature must be a function of the three scalars $\lvert \mathbf{\gamma}'(t)\rvert^{2}$, $\lvert \mathbf{\gamma}''(t)\rvert^{2}$ and $\mathbf{\gamma}'(t) \cdot \mathbf{\gamma}''(t)$.[^o4]

The general equation for a parametrized circle in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is

$$\mathbf{g}(u) = \mathbf a \cos (h(u)) + \mathbf b \sin (h(u)) + \mathbf c$$

where $\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is the center of the circle (irrelevant since it disappears in the derivatives), $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ are perpendicular vectors of length $\rho$ (that is, $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \rho^{2}$ and $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0$), and $h : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is an arbitrary function which is twice differentiable at $t$.

The relevant derivatives of $\mathbf{g}$ work out to be

$$\begin{aligned} |\mathbf g'|^2 &= \rho^2 (h')^2 \\ \mathbf g' \cdot \mathbf g'' &= \rho^2 h' h'' \\ |\mathbf g''|^2 &= \rho^2 \left((h')^4 + (h'')^2 \right) \end{aligned}$$

If we now equate these derivatives of $\mathbf{g}$ to the corresponding derivatives of $\mathbf{\gamma}$ at $t$ we obtain

$$\begin{aligned} |\boldsymbol\gamma'(t)|^{2} &= \rho^2 h'^{\,2}(t) \\ \boldsymbol\gamma'(t) \cdot \boldsymbol\gamma''(t) &= \rho^2 h'(t) h''(t) \\ |\boldsymbol\gamma''(t)|^{2} &= \rho^2 \left(h'^{\,4}(t) + h''^{\,2}(t)\right) \end{aligned}$$

These three equations in three unknowns ($\rho$, $h'(t)$ and $h''(t)$) can be solved for $\rho$, giving the formula for the radius of curvature:

$$\rho(t) = \frac{\left|\boldsymbol\gamma'(t)\right|^{3}}{\sqrt{\left|\boldsymbol\gamma'(t)\right|^{2} \, \left|\boldsymbol\gamma''(t)\right|^{2} - \big(\boldsymbol\gamma'(t) \cdot \boldsymbol\gamma''(t)\big)^2}}\,,$$

or, omitting the parameter $t$ for readability,

$$\rho = \frac{\left|\boldsymbol\gamma'\right|^3}{\sqrt{\left|\boldsymbol\gamma'\right|^2 \; \left|\boldsymbol\gamma''\right|^2 - \left(\boldsymbol\gamma' \cdot \boldsymbol\gamma''\right)^2}}\,.$$

## Examples {#Examples}

### Semicircles and circles {#Semicircles_and_circles}

For a [semi-circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-circle) of radius $a$ in the upper half-plane with $R=|-a| =a\,,$

$$\begin{aligned} y &= \sqrt{a^2-x^2} \\ y' &= \frac{-x}{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}} \\ y'' &= \frac{-a^2}{\left(a^2-x^2\right)^\frac{3}{2}}\,. \end{aligned}$$

For a semi-circle of radius $a$ in the lower half-plane

$$y = -\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\,.$$

The [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) of radius $a$ has a radius of curvature equal to $a$.

### Ellipses {#Ellipses}

*Image: Ellipse evolute.svg.* An ellipse (red) and its [evolute](wikipedia://en/842387) (blue). The dots are the vertices of the ellipse, at the points of greatest and least curvature.

In an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) with major axis $2a$ and minor axis $2b$, the [vertices](wikipedia://en/6865890) on the major axis have the smallest radius of curvature of any points, $R={b^2 \over a}$; and the vertices on the minor axis have the largest radius of curvature of any points, $R={a^2 \over b}$.

The radius of curvature of an ellipse as a function of the geocentric coordinate $t$ with $\tan t = \frac{y}{x}$ is

$$R(t)= \frac{(b^2 \cos^2t + a^2\sin^2t)^{3/2}}{ab}.$$

It has its minima at $t=0$ and $t=180^\circ$ and its maxima at $t= \pm 90^\circ$.

### Stress in semiconductor structures {#Stress_in_semiconductor_structures}

[Stress](wikipedia://en/228107) in the [semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor) structure involving evaporated [thin films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin_film) usually results from the [thermal expansion](wikipedia://en/1569600) (thermal stress) during the manufacturing process. Thermal stress occurs because film depositions are usually made above room temperature. Upon cooling from the deposition temperature to room temperature, the difference in the [thermal expansion coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficient_of_thermal_expansion) of the substrate and the film cause thermal stress.[^o19]

[Intrinsic stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intrinsic_stress) results from the microstructure created in the film as atoms are deposited on the substrate. Tensile stress results from microvoids (small holes, considered to be defects) in the thin film, because of the attractive interaction of atoms across the voids.

The stress in thin film semiconductor structures results in the [buckling](wikipedia://en/815969) of the wafers. The radius of the curvature of the stressed structure is related to stress tensor in the structure, and can be described by modified [Stoney formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoney_formula).[^o20] The topography of the stressed structure including radii of curvature can be measured using optical scanner methods. The modern scanner tools have capability to measure full topography of the substrate and to measure both principal radii of curvature, while providing the accuracy of the order of 0.1% for radii of curvature of 90 meters and more.[^o21]
