# Isocline

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Isocline"}

*Image: Isocline 3.png.* Fig. 1: Isoclines (blue), slope field (black), and some solution curves (red) of *y**\=*****xy*. The solution curves are $y = C e^{x^2/2}$.***

Given a [family of curves](wikipedia://en/5726601), assumed to be [differentiable](wikipedia://en/2119219), an **isocline** for that family is formed by the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of points at which some member of the family attains a given [slope](wikipedia://en/29368). The word comes from the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) words ἴσος (isos), meaning "same", and the κλίνειν (klenein), meaning "make to slope". Generally, an isocline will itself have the shape of a curve or the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of a small number of curves.

:::

Isoclines are often used as a graphical method of solving [ordinary differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_differential_equations). In an equation of the form *y' = f*(*x*, *y*), the isoclines are lines in the (*x*, *y*) plane obtained by setting *f*(*x*, *y*) equal to a constant. This gives a series of lines (for different constants) along which the solution curves have the same gradient. By calculating this gradient for each isocline, the [slope field](wikipedia://en/1866685) can be visualised; making it relatively easy to sketch approximate solution curves; as in fig. 1.

## Other uses {#Other_uses}

In [population dynamics](wikipedia://en/501466), the term "zero-growth isocline" refers to the set of population sizes at which the rate of change for one population in a pair of interacting populations is zero.[^o3] However, this is rare and a more common term is [nullcline](wikipedia://en/4879924).
