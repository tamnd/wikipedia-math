# Frank Vigor Morley

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Frank Vigor Morley"}

**Frank Vigor Morley** (4 January 1899 – 8 October 1980) was an American mathematician, author, editor and publishing executive. As had his two older brothers, [Christopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Morley) and [Felix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Morley), Morley attended [Haverford College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haverford_College) and then studied at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford) as a [Rhodes Scholar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodes_Scholar). Morley worked in book publishing in London and New York and played a significant role in the early history of the publishing firm [Faber and Faber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faber_and_Faber), where he became a close friend of the poet [T. S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._S._Eliot).

:::

## Life {#Life}

Morley was born on 4 January 1899 in [Haverford, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haverford,_Pennsylvania)[^o3] where his father [Frank Morley](wikipedia://en/589167) was Professor of Mathematics at [Haverford College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haverford_College). In 1900 his father was named chairman of the mathematics department at [Johns Hopkins University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University) and the family removed to [Baltimore, Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltimore,_Maryland).[^o4] As had his two, older brothers, [Christopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Morley) and [Felix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Morley), Frank returned to Haverford College for his undergraduate education which, however, was interrupted in 1917 when Morley left school to serve as 2nd lieutenant in an Engineering unit based at [Hog Island, Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hog_Island,_Philadelphia), during World War I.[^o5][^o6]

Morley received his [bachelor's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor's_degree) in 1918 from Johns Hopkins University.[^o7][^o8] Morley won a [Rhodes Scholarship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodes_Scholarship), as did his two brothers, and attended [New College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_College,_Oxford); they were the only family of three brothers to receive this prestigious scholarship.[^o9] Morley earned a doctorate in mathematics from Oxford in 1923.[^o10] Morley remained in England, working at *[The Times Literary Supplement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times_Literary_Supplement)*, and then as London manager for the publishers of *[The Century Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Century_Magazine).*[^o11] [Geoffrey Faber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Faber) hired Morley in 1929 to be co-director of the newly re-organized publishing firm [Faber and Faber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faber_and_Faber).[^o12] In this capacity Morley worked with and became close friends with [T. S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._S._Eliot), then an editor at Faber and Faber. Morley assisted Eliot in quickly finding new accommodation when Eliot separated from his wife.[^o13] Eliot wrote the poem "Cows" for Morley's children.[^o14]

In 1933 Morley and his father [Frank Morley](wikipedia://en/589167) published the "stimulating volume," *Inversive Geometry*.[^o15][^o16] The book develops [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) as a tool for geometry and [function theory](wikipedia://en/5759).

In 1936 Morley's novel *War Paint* was published. Reviewing the book in *[The New English Weekly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_English_Weekly)*, [George Orwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Orwell) described it as "an exceedingly naïve adventure story, and at the same time a sort of [Chelsea Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chelsea_Hospital) for superannuated jokes."[^o17]

In 1939 Morley left London for New York to become Vice-President, and Director of the Trade Editorial Department at [Harcourt, Brace, Jovanovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harcourt_%28publisher%29).[^o18] Shortly thereafter Harcourt published *My Sister and I* by Dirk van der Heide (1941), the story of the German [bombing of Rotterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_bombing_of_Rotterdam_in_World_War_II) ostensibly written by an 11-year-old refugee to England and thence to the United States. *My Sister and I* enjoyed considerable commercial and critical success, but in the 1980s was discovered to have been a literary fabrication conducted by Harcourt, under Morley's supervision, to encourage American participation in [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), possibly with guidance or encouragement from British intelligence services.[^o19] During the war, Morley also served on the [National War Labor Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_War_Labor_Board_%281942-1945%29).[^o20] Following the war, Morley returned to England to become Director of the publisher [Eyre & Spottiswoode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyre_&_Spottiswoode) from which he ultimately retired.[^o21][^o22]

## Literary connections {#Literary_connections}

As an editor and publisher, Morley enjoyed a wide correspondence and friendships with many prominent authors and literary figures of the day. These included Eliot, [Ezra Pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezra_Pound), [Lewis Mumford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Mumford), and [Walter De la Mare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_De_la_Mare), as well as Morley's eldest brother [Christopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Morley), who was a notable author and powerful promoter of literature. It was Frank Morley who suggested to Pound in 1933 that he write an autobiography, which resulted in Pound's 1938 work *[Guide to Kulchur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guide_to_Kulchur)*.[^o24] Morley's literary correspondence is now held chiefly by the [New York Public Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Public_Library)'s [Berg Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berg_Collection).[^o25]

With his brother Christopher, Morley had long been an admirer of the [Sherlock Holmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes) stories by [Sir Arthur Conan Doyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Arthur_Conan_Doyle). Christopher originated [The Baker Street Irregulars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Baker_Street_Irregulars) in 1934 as an informal meeting of his friends and others interested in Holmes, but in order to restrict membership to those who were truly knowledgeable if not expert in the stories, and eliminate mere casual readers of Conan Doyle, a membership test in the form of a crossword puzzle was devised by Frank V. Morley. This was published in Christopher's "Bowling Green" column in the May 1934 issue of the *[Saturday Review of Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturday_Review_of_Literature)*.[^o26]

Morley traveled widely throughout England before and after the Second World War, incorporating his reading and observations into a number of non-fiction works of literary history and tourism, the last of which, *Literary Britain*, was published only days before his death on 8 October 1980 at age 82 in [Buckinghamshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckinghamshire), England.[^o27]

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Morley married Christina McLeod Innes 14 May 1925. They had two sons and two daughters: Donald, Oliver, Peregrine, and Susannah.[^o29]
