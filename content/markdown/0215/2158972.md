# Jacopo Zabarella

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Jacopo Zabarella"}

*Image: Portret van Jacopo Zabarella, RP-P-1909-5623 (cropped).jpg.* Engraving of Zabarella, 1630

**Giacomo** (or **Jacopo**) **Zabarella** (5 September 1533 – 15 October 1589) was an Italian [Aristotelian](wikipedia://en/308) [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher) and [logician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logician).

:::

## Life {#Life}

Zabarella was born into a noble Paduan family. He received a [humanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_Humanism) education and entered the [University of Padua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Padua), where he received a doctorate in 1553. His teachers included [Francesco Robortello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Robortello) in humanities, Bernardino Tomitano in logic, [Marcantonio Genua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcantonio_Genua) in physics and metaphysics, and [Pietro Catena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietro_Catena) in mathematics. In 1564 he succeeded Tomitano in the chair of logic. In 1577 he was promoted to the first extraordinary chair of [natural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy). He died in Padua at the age of 56 in 1589. His entire teaching career was spent at his native university. His successor was [Cesare Cremonini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesare_Cremonini_%28philosopher%29).

## Works {#Works}

*Image: Opera Logica 1578.png.* Title page of *Opera logica* (1578)

Zabarella's works reflect his teaching in the Aristotelian tradition. His first published work was *Opera logica* (Venice 1578), followed by *Tabula logicae* (1578).[^o4] His [commentary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commentary_%28philology%29) on Aristotle's *[Posterior Analytics](wikipedia://en/1692809)* appeared in 1582.[^o4] His great work in [natural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy) was *De rebus naturalibus*, published posthumously in 1590. It constituted 30 treatises on Aristotelian natural philosophy, the introduction to which was written only weeks before his death. His two sons edited his incomplete commentaries on Aristotle's texts, also published posthumously (the commentary on the *[Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_%28Aristotle%29)* in 1601 and the commentary on *[On the Soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Soul)* (1605).[^o4]

Zabarella consulted newly recovered Greek commentators such as [Alexander of Aphrodisias](wikipedia://en/1599), [Philoponus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philoponus), [Simplicius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicius_of_Cilicia), and [Themistius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Themistius), as well as medieval commentators like [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas), [Walter Burley](wikipedia://en/3909460), and [Averroes](wikipedia://en/47836). Unlike some earlier [scholastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism) philosophers, he was literate in Greek and was therefore able to use the Greek texts of Aristotle. He devoted much effort to presenting what he considered to be the true meaning of Aristotle's texts.
