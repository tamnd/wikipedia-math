# Complete theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complete theory"}

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), a [theory](wikipedia://en/3512103) of a [language](wikipedia://en/10939) is **complete** if it is [consistent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consistent_theory) and it [proves](wikipedia://en/7954712) every [closed formula](wikipedia://en/404582#Closed_formulas) with which it is not inconsistent. That is to say, a consistent theory $T$ is complete if, for every [sentence](wikipedia://en/2129527) $\varphi$ in the language, either $T \vdash \varphi$ holds or $T \cup \{\varphi\}$ is inconsistent. Another common definition, that is equivalent if the [formal system](wikipedia://en/396102) satisfies the [principle of explosion](wikipedia://en/591394), requires instead that either $\varphi$ or its negation $\neg \varphi$ is provable from $T$. Using this definition, consistency of $T$ follows automatically if "either" and "or" are read as exclusive disjunction, and it thus can be omitted from the definition. If $T$ is furthermore [deductively closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductively_closed), completeness reduces to the concise condition that exactly one of $\varphi$ and $\neg \varphi$ is contained in $T$ for every sentence $\varphi$. Recursively axiomatizable [first-order theories](wikipedia://en/3690584) that are consistent and rich enough to allow general mathematical reasoning to be formulated cannot be complete, as demonstrated by [Gödel's first incompleteness theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel's_first_incompleteness_theorem).[^o2]

:::

This syntactic sense of *complete* is distinct from the semantic notion of a [complete formal system](wikipedia://en/31271451#Semantic_completeness), which asserts that for every theory that can be formulated in the formal system, all semantically valid statements are provable theorems (for an appropriate sense of "semantically valid"). [Gödel's completeness theorem](wikipedia://en/12450) is about this latter kind of completeness.

## Complete theories {#Complete_theories}

Complete theories are closed under a number of conditions internally modelling the [T-schema](wikipedia://en/1063436):

- For a set of formulas $S$: $A \land B \in S$ if and only if $A \in S$ and $B \in S$,
- For a set of formulas $S$: $A \lor B \in S$ if and only if $A \in S$ or $B \in S$.

Maximal consistent sets are a fundamental tool in the [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858) of [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868) and [modal logic](wikipedia://en/333365). Their existence in a given case is usually a straightforward consequence of [Zorn's lemma](wikipedia://en/51442), based on the idea that a [contradiction](wikipedia://en/75807) involves use of only finitely many premises. In the case of modal logics, the collection of maximal consistent sets extending a theory *T* (closed under the necessitation rule) can be given the structure of a [model](wikipedia://en/911960) of *T*, called the canonical model.

## Examples {#Examples}

Some examples of complete theories are:

- [Presburger arithmetic](wikipedia://en/23756)
- [Tarski's axioms](wikipedia://en/2181360) for [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417)
- The theory of [dense](wikipedia://en/6320997) [linear orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_order) without endpoints
- The theory of [algebraically closed fields](wikipedia://en/1018) of a given [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621)
- The theory of [real closed fields](wikipedia://en/1066093)
- Every [uncountably categorical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morley's_categoricity_theorem) [countable](wikipedia://en/6026) theory
- Every [countably categorical](wikipedia://en/26628083) countable theory
- A [group of three elements](https://math.stackexchange.com/q/159124)
- [True arithmetic](wikipedia://en/22156658) or any other [elementary diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_diagram) or complete theory of a [structure](wikipedia://en/4055928) (see Theory (mathematical logic))
