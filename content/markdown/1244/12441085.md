# Noncontracting grammar

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Noncontracting grammar"}

In [formal language theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_language_theory), a **noncontracting grammar** (also called **monotonic grammar**) is a type of [formal grammar](wikipedia://en/18020716) whose [production rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production_rule_%28formal_languages%29) never decrease the total length of a string during [derivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphological_derivation). This means that when applying any rule to transform one string into another, the resulting string must have at least as many symbols as the original.

:::

Noncontracting grammars are significant because they are equivalent in [expressive power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expressive_power_%28computer_science%29) to [context-sensitive grammars](wikipedia://en/6211) and define the same class of languages (the [context-sensitive languages](wikipedia://en/6212)) in the [Chomsky hierarchy](wikipedia://en/6011). This equivalence makes them important for understanding the computational limits of [natural language processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_language_processing) and [compiler design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compiler_design), as they can model complex linguistic phenomena while maintaining certain desirable mathematical properties. Some authors use the term **context-sensitive grammar** to refer to noncontracting grammars in general, though this usage varies in the literature.[^o4]

A closely related concept is the **essentially noncontracting grammar**, which allows one special exception: a rule that produces the [empty string](wikipedia://en/835827) from the [start symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Start_symbol_%28formal_languages%29), provided that the start symbol never appears elsewhere in the grammar.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definitions"}

## Formal definitions {#Formal_definitions}

A [grammar](wikipedia://en/18020716) is noncontracting if for all of its production rules, α → β (where α and β are [strings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_%28formal_languages%29#Formal_theory) of [nonterminal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonterminal) and terminal symbols), it holds that |α| ≤ |β|, that is β has at least as many symbols as α.

A grammar is essentially noncontracting if there may be one exception, namely, a rule *S* → ε where *S* is the [start symbol](wikipedia://en/18020716#The_syntax_of_grammars) and ε the [empty string](wikipedia://en/835827), and furthermore, *S* never occurs in the right-hand side of any rule.

A [context-sensitive grammar](wikipedia://en/6211) is a noncontracting grammar in which all rules are of the form αAβ → αγβ, where A is a nonterminal, and γ is a nonempty string of nonterminal and/or terminal symbols. However, some authors use the term context-sensitive grammar to refer to noncontracting grammars in general.[^o4]

A noncontracting grammar in which |α| \< |β| for all rules is called a [growing context-sensitive grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growing_context-sensitive_grammar).

:::

## History {#History}

Chomsky (1959) introduced the [Chomsky hierarchy](wikipedia://en/6011), in which context-sensitive grammars occur as "type 1" grammars; general noncontracting grammars do not occur.[^o6]

Chomsky (1963) calls a noncontracting grammar a "type 1 grammar", and a context-sensitive grammar a "type 2 grammar", and by presenting a conversion from the former into the latter, [proves](wikipedia://en/82285) the two [weakly equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_equivalence_%28formal_languages%29) .[^o7]

Kuroda (1964) introduced Kuroda normal form, into which all noncontracting grammars can be converted.[^o8]

## Expressive power {#Expressive_power}

Every [context-sensitive grammar](wikipedia://en/6211) is a noncontracting grammar.

There are easy procedures for

- bringing any noncontracting grammar into [Kuroda normal form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuroda_normal_form),[^o8][^o13] and
- converting any noncontracting grammar in [Kuroda normal form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuroda_normal_form) into a context-sensitive grammar.

Hence, these three types of grammar are equal in expressive power, all describing exactly the [context-sensitive languages](wikipedia://en/6212) that do not include the empty string; the essentially noncontracting grammars describe exactly the set of [context-sensitive languages](wikipedia://en/6212).

### A direct conversion {#A_direct_conversion}

A direct conversion into context-sensitive grammars, avoiding Kuroda normal form:

For an arbitrary noncontracting grammar (*N*, Σ, *P*, *S*), construct the context-sensitive grammar (*N*’, Σ, *P*’, *S*) as follows:

1. For every terminal symbol *a* ∈ Σ, introduce a new nonterminal symbol \[*a*\] ∈ *N*’, and a new rule (\[*a*\] → *a*) ∈ *P*’.
1. In the rules of *P*, replace every terminal symbol *a* by its corresponding nonterminal symbol \[*a*\]. As a result, all these rules are of the form *X*<sub>1</sub>...*X*<sub>*m*</sub> → *Y*<sub>1</sub>...*Y*<sub>*n*</sub> for nonterminals *X*<sub>*i*</sub>, *Y*<sub>*j*</sub> and *m*≤*n*.
1. Replace each rule *X*<sub>1</sub>...*X*<sub>*m*</sub> → *Y*<sub>1</sub>...*Y*<sub>*n*</sub> with *m*>1 by 2*m* rules:[^o15]
