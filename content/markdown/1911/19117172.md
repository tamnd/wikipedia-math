# K-equivalence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="K-equivalence"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **$\mathcal{K}$-equivalence**, or **contact equivalence**, is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) between [map germs](wikipedia://en/352714). It was introduced by [John Mather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Mather_%28mathematician%29) in his seminal work in [Singularity theory](wikipedia://en/581763) in the 1960s as a technical tool for studying stable maps. Since then it has proved important in its own right. Roughly speaking, two map germs *ƒ*, *g* are $\scriptstyle\mathcal{K}$-equivalent if *ƒ*<sup>−1</sup>(0) and *g*<sup>−1</sup>(0) are [diffeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphic).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Two map germs $f,g:X \to (Y,0)$ are $\scriptstyle\mathcal{K}$-equivalent if there is a [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564)

$$\Psi: X \times Y \to X\times Y$$

of the form Ψ(x,y) = (φ(x),ψ(x,y)), satisfying,

$\Psi(x,0) = (\varphi(x), 0)$, and

$$\Psi(x,f(x)) = (\varphi(x), g(\varphi(x))).$$

In other words, &Psi. Maps the graph of *f* to the graph of *g*, as well as the graph of the zero map to itself. In particular, the diffeomorphism φ maps *f*<sup>−1</sup>(0) to *g*<sup>−1</sup>(0). The name *contact* is explained by the fact that this equivalence is measuring the contact between the graph of *f* and the graph of the zero map.

Contact equivalence is the appropriate equivalence relation for studying the sets of solutions of equations, and finds many applications in [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems) and [bifurcation theory](wikipedia://en/2326042), for example.

It is easy to see that this equivalence relation is *weaker* than [A-equivalence](wikipedia://en/14315623), in that any pair of $\scriptstyle\mathcal{A}$-equivalent map germs are necessarily $\scriptstyle\mathcal{K}$-equivalent.

:::

## K<sub>V</sub>-equivalence {#KV-equivalence}

This modification of $\scriptstyle\mathcal{K}$-equivalence was introduced by [James Damon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Damon) in the 1980s. Here *V* is a subset (or subvariety) of *Y*, and the diffeomorphism Ψ above is required to preserve not $X\times\{0\}$ but $X\times V$ (that is, $y\in V \Rightarrow \psi(x,y)\in V$). In particular, Ψ maps *f*<sup>−1</sup>(V) to *g*<sup>−1</sup>(V).
