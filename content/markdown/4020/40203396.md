# Material inference

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Material inference"}

In [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), [inference](wikipedia://en/317465) is the process of deriving logical conclusions from premises known or assumed to be true. In checking a logical inference for **formal** and **material** validity, the meaning of only its logical vocabulary and of both its logical and extra-logical vocabulary is considered, respectively.

:::

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

For example, the inference "*Socrates is a human, and each human must eventually die, therefore Socrates must eventually die*" is a formally valid inference; it remains valid if the nonlogical vocabulary "*Socrates*", "*is human*", and "*must eventually die*" is arbitrarily, but consistently replaced.[^o4]

In contrast, the inference "*Montreal is north of New York, therefore New York is south of Montreal*" is materially valid only; its validity relies on the extra-logical relations "*is north of*" and "*is south of*" being converse to each other.[^o5]

:::

## Material inferences vs. enthymemes {#Material_inferences_vs._enthymemes}

Classical formal logic considers the above "north/south" inference as an [enthymeme](wikipedia://en/515973), that is, as an incomplete inference; it can be made formally valid by supplementing the tacitly used conversity relationship explicitly: "*Montreal is north of New York, and whenever a location x is north of a location y, then y is south of x; therefore New York is south of Montreal*".

In contrast, the notion of a **material inference** has been developed by [Wilfrid Sellars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilfrid_Sellars)[^o7] in order to emphasize his view that such supplements are not necessary to obtain a correct argument.

## Brandom on material inference {#Brandom_on_material_inference}

### Non-monotonic inference {#Non-monotonic_inference}

[Robert Brandom](wikipedia://en/875857) adopted Sellars' view,[^o10] arguing that everyday (practical) reasoning is usually [non-monotonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonmonotonic_logic), i.e. additional premises can turn a practically valid inference into an invalid one, e.g.

1. "If I rub this [match](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Match) along the striking surface, then it will ignite." (*p*→*q*)
1. "If *p*, but the match is inside a strong [electromagnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field), then it will not ignite." (*p*∧*r*→¬*q*)
1. "If *p* and *r*, but the match is in a [Faraday cage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday_cage), then it will ignite." (*p*∧*r*∧*s*→*q*)
1. "If *p* and *r* and *s*, but there is no [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) in the room, then the match will not ignite." (*p*∧*r*∧*s*∧*t*→¬*q*)
1. ...

Therefore, practically valid inference is different from formally valid inference (which is monotonic - the above argument that *Socrates must eventually die* cannot be challenged by whatever additional information), and should better be modelled by materially valid inference. While a classical logician could add a [ceteris paribus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceteris_paribus) clause to 1. to make it usable in formally valid inferences:

1. "If I rub this match along the striking surface, then, ceteris paribus,[^o11] it will inflame."

However, Brandom doubts that the meaning of such a clause can be made explicit, and prefers to consider it as a hint to non-monotony rather than a miracle drug to establish monotony.

Moreover, the "match" example shows that a typical everyday inference can hardly be ever made formally complete. In a similar way, [Lewis Carroll](wikipedia://en/26586083)'s dialogue "*[What the Tortoise Said to Achilles](wikipedia://en/154227)*" demonstrates that the attempt to make every inference fully complete can lead to an infinite regression.[^o12]

## See also {#See_also}

Material inference should not be confused with the following concepts, which refer to *formal*, not **material** validity:

- [Material conditional](wikipedia://en/658808) — the logical connective "→" (i.e. "formally implies")
- [Material implication (rule of inference)](wikipedia://en/244516) — a rule for formally replacing "→" by "¬" (negation) and "∨" (disjunction)
