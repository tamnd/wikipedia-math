# Root mean square deviation of atomic positions

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Root mean square deviation of atomic positions"}

In [bioinformatics](wikipedia://en/4214), the **root mean square deviation of atomic positions**, or simply **root mean square deviation (RMSD)**, is the measure of the average distance between the atoms (usually the backbone atoms) of [superimposed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein_structural_alignment) molecules.[^o2] In the study of globular protein conformations, one customarily measures the similarity in three-dimensional structure by the RMSD of the [Cα](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cα) atomic coordinates after optimal rigid body superposition.

:::

When a [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087) fluctuates about some well-defined average position, the RMSD from the average over time can be referred to as the *RMSF* or [root mean square fluctuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_mean_square_fluctuation). The size of this fluctuation can be measured, for example using [Mössbauer spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mössbauer_spectroscopy) or [nuclear magnetic resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_resonance), and can provide important physical information. The [Lindemann index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindemann_index) is a method of placing the RMSF in the context of the parameters of the system.

A widely used way to compare the structures of biomolecules or solid bodies is to translate and rotate one structure with respect to the other to minimize the RMSD. Coutsias, *et al.* presented a simple derivation, based on [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440), for the optimal solid body transformation (rotation-translation) that minimizes the RMSD between two sets of vectors.[^o3] They proved that the quaternion method is equivalent to the well-known [Kabsch algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabsch_algorithm).[^o4] The solution given by Kabsch is an instance of the solution of the *d*-dimensional problem, introduced by Hurley and Cattell.[^o5] The [quaternion](wikipedia://en/51440) solution to compute the optimal rotation was published in the appendix of a paper of Petitjean.[^o6] This [quaternion](wikipedia://en/51440) solution and the calculation of the optimal isometry in the *d*-dimensional case were both extended to infinite sets and to the continuous case in the appendix A of another paper of Petitjean.[^o7]

## The equation {#The_equation}

$$\mathrm{RMSD}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\delta_i^2}$$

where *δ<sub>i</sub>* is the distance between atom *i* and either a reference structure or the mean position of the *N* equivalent atoms. This is often calculated for the backbone heavy atoms *C*, *N*, *O*, and *C<sub>α</sub>* or sometimes just the *C<sub>α</sub>* atoms.

Normally a rigid superposition which minimizes the RMSD is performed, and this minimum is returned. Given two sets of $n$ points $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$, the RMSD is defined as follows:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{RMSD}(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}) & = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \|v_i - w_i\|^2} \\ & = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n ((v_{ix} - w_{ix})^2 + (v_{iy} - w_{iy})^2 + (v_{iz} - w_{iz})^2}) \end{aligned}$$

An RMSD value is expressed in length units. The most commonly used unit in [structural biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_biology) is the [Ångström](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ångström) (Å) which is equal to 10<sup>−10</sup> m.

::: {#o10 .application cue=heading name="Uses"}

## Uses {#Uses}

Typically RMSD is used as a quantitative measure of similarity between two or more protein structures. For example, the [CASP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CASP) [protein structure prediction](wikipedia://en/306769) competition uses RMSD as one of its assessments of how well a submitted structure matches the known target structure. Thus the lower RMSD, the better the model is in comparison to the target structure.[^o11]

Also some scientists who study [protein folding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein_folding) by computer simulations use RMSD as a [reaction coordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_coordinate) to quantify where the protein is between the folded state and the unfolded state.

The study of RMSD for small organic molecules (commonly called [ligands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligand_%28biochemistry%29) when they're binding to macromolecules, such as proteins, is studied) is common in the context of [docking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Docking_%28molecular%29),[^o2] as well as in other methods to study the [configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_configuration) of ligands when bound to macromolecules. Note that, for the case of ligands (contrary to proteins, as described above), their structures are most commonly not superimposed prior to the calculation of the RMSD.

RMSD is also one of several metrics that have been proposed for quantifying evolutionary similarity between proteins, as well as the quality of sequence alignments.[^o12][^o13]
