# Doubly stochastic matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Doubly stochastic matrix"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially in [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) and [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170), a **doubly stochastic matrix** (also called **bistochastic matrix**) is a [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) $X=(x_{ij})$ of nonnegative [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), each of whose rows and columns sums to 1, i.e.,

:::

$$\sum_i x_{ij}=\sum_j x_{ij}=1,$$

Thus, a doubly stochastic matrix is both left [stochastic](wikipedia://en/217313) and right stochastic.[^o2]

Indeed, any matrix that is both left and right stochastic must be [square](wikipedia://en/166022): if every row sums to 1 then the sum of all entries in the matrix must be equal to the number of rows, and since the same holds for columns, the number of rows and columns must be equal.

## Birkhoff polytope {#Birkhoff_polytope}

The class of $n\times n$ doubly stochastic matrices is a [convex polytope](wikipedia://en/1817885) known as the **Birkhoff polytope** $B_n$. Using the matrix entries as [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates), it lies in an $(n-1)^2$-dimensional affine subspace of $n^2$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) defined by $2n-1$ independent linear constraints specifying that the row and column sums all equal 1. (There are $2n-1$ constraints rather than $2n$ because one of these constraints is dependent, as the sum of the row sums must equal the sum of the column sums.) Moreover, the entries are all constrained to be non-negative and less than or equal to 1.

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem"}

## Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem {#Birkhoff–von_Neumann_theorem}

The **Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem** (often known simply as **Birkhoff's theorem**[^o16][^o19][^o6]) states that the polytope $B_n$ is the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of the set of $n\times n$ [permutation matrices](wikipedia://en/238849), and furthermore that the [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) of $B_n$ are precisely the permutation matrices. In other words, if $X$ is a doubly stochastic matrix, then there exist $\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_k \ge 0, \sum_{i=1}^k \theta_i = 1$ and permutation matrices $P_1,\ldots,P_k$ such that

$$X = \theta_1 P_1 + \cdots + \theta_k P_k.$$

(Such a decomposition of *X* is known as a 'convex combination'.) A proof of the theorem based on [Hall's marriage theorem](wikipedia://en/186969) is given [below](wikipedia://en/2906180#birkhoffproof).

This representation is known as the **Birkhoff–von Neumann decomposition**, and may not be unique. It is often described as a real-valued generalization of [Kőnig's theorem](wikipedia://en/5465118), where the correspondence is established through adjacency matrices of graphs.

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- The product of two doubly stochastic matrices is doubly stochastic. However, the inverse of a nonsingular doubly stochastic matrix need not be doubly stochastic (indeed, the inverse is doubly stochastic if it has nonnegative entries).
- The stationary distribution of an irreducible aperiodic finite [Markov chain](wikipedia://en/60876) is uniform if and only if its transition matrix is doubly stochastic.
- [Sinkhorn's theorem](wikipedia://en/24742938) states that any matrix with strictly positive entries can be made doubly stochastic by pre- and post-multiplication by [diagonal matrices](wikipedia://en/174080).
- For $n=2$, all bistochastic matrices are [unistochastic](wikipedia://en/13269420) and [orthostochastic](wikipedia://en/13676913), but for larger $n$ this is not the case.
- [Van der Waerden's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_der_Waerden's_conjecture) that the minimum [permanent](wikipedia://en/215889) among all *n* × *n* doubly stochastic matrices is $n!/n^n$, achieved by the matrix for which all entries are equal to $1/n$.[^o9] Proofs of this conjecture were published in 1980 by B. Gyires[^o10] and in 1981 by G. P. Egorychev[^o11] and D. I. Falikman;[^o12] for this work, Egorychev and Falikman won the [Fulkerson Prize](wikipedia://en/1425916) in 1982.[^o13]

:::

## Proof of the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem {#Proof_of_the_Birkhoff–von_Neumann_theorem}

Let *X* be a doubly stochastic matrix. Then we will show that there exists a permutation matrix *P* such that *x<sub>ij</sub>* ≠ 0 whenever *p<sub>ij</sub>* ≠ 0. Thus if we let λ be the smallest *x<sub>ij</sub>* corresponding to a non-zero *p<sub>ij</sub>*, the difference *X* – λ*P* will be a scalar multiple of a doubly stochastic matrix and will have at least one more zero cell than *X*. Accordingly we may successively reduce the number of non-zero cells in *X* by removing scalar multiples of permutation matrices until we arrive at the zero matrix, at which point we will have constructed a convex combination of permutation matrices equal to the original *X*.[^o16]

For instance if $X=\frac{1}{12}\begin{pmatrix} 7 & 0 & 5 \\ 2 & 6 & 4 \\ 3 & 6 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ then $P=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $\lambda = \frac{2}{12}$, and $X-\lambda P=\frac{1}{12}\begin{pmatrix} 7 & 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 6 & 4 \\ 3 & 4 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$.

::: {#o17 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

***Proof:*** Construct a [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431) in which the rows of *X* are listed in one part and the columns in the other, and in which row *i* is connected to column *j* iff *x<sub>ij</sub>* ≠ 0. Let $A$ be any set of rows, and define $A'$ as the set of columns joined to rows in *A* in the graph. We want to express the sizes $|A|$ and $|A'|$ of the two sets in terms of the *x<sub>ij</sub>*.

For every *i* in *A*, the sum over *j* in *A**of*****x<sub>ij</sub>*is 1, since all columns*j*for which*x<sub>ij</sub>*≠ 0 are included in*A'*, and*X*is doubly stochastic; hence $|A|$ is the sum over all*i*∈*A*,*j*∈*A'*of*x<sub>ij</sub>*.***

Meanwhile $|A'|$ is the sum over all *i* (whether or not in *A*) and all *j* in *A'* of *x<sub>ij</sub>* ; and this is ≥ the corresponding sum in which the *i* are limited to rows in *A*. Hence $|A'|\ge|A|$.

It follows that the conditions of [Hall's marriage theorem](wikipedia://en/186969) are satisfied, and that we can therefore find a set of edges in the graph which join each row in *X* to exactly one (distinct) column. These edges define a permutation matrix whose non-zero cells correspond to non-zero cells in *X*.

:::

### Generalisations {#Generalisations}

There is a simple generalisation to matrices with more columns and rows such that the *i<sup>th</sup>* row sum is equal to *r<sub>i</sub>* (a positive integer), the column sums are equal to 1, and all cells are non-negative (the sum of the row sums being equal to the number of columns). Any matrix in this form can be expressed as a convex combination of matrices in the same form made up of 0s and 1s. The proof is to replace the *i<sup>th</sup>* row of the original matrix by *r<sub>i</sub>* separate rows, each equal to the original row divided by *r<sub>i</sub>* ; to apply Birkhoff's theorem to the resulting square matrix; and at the end to additively recombine the *r<sub>i</sub>* rows into a single *i<sup>th</sup>* row.

In the same way it is possible to replicate columns as well as rows, but the result of recombination is not necessarily limited to 0s and 1s. A different generalisation (with a significantly harder proof) has been put forward by R. M. Caron et al.[^o19]

:::
