# Incenter–excenter lemma

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Incenter–excenter lemma"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **incenter–excenter lemma** is the [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) that the [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) between the [incenter and any excenter](wikipedia://en/161260) of a triangle, or between two excenters, is the [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) (an *incenter–excenter* or *excenter–excenter circle*) also passing through two triangle [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) with its center on the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432). This theorem is best known in Russia, where it is called the **trillium theorem** (теорема трилистника) or **trident lemma** (лемма о трезубце), based on the geometric figure's resemblance to a [trillium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trillium) flower or [trident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trident); these names have sometimes also been adopted in English.

:::

These relationships arise because the incenter and excenters of any triangle form an [orthocentric system](wikipedia://en/161263) whose [nine-point circle](wikipedia://en/161243) is the circumcircle of the original triangle. The theorem is helpful for solving competitive Euclidean geometry problems, and can be used to reconstruct a triangle starting from one vertex, the incenter, and the circumcenter.

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

*Image: Trillium theorem2.svg.* incenter–excenter lemma with incenter $I$ and excenter $E$

Let $ABC$ be an arbitrary [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654). Let $I$ be its [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) and let $D$ be the point where line $BI$ (the [angle bisector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_bisector) of $\angle ABC$) crosses the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/161262) of $ABC$. Then, the theorem states that $D$ is [equidistant](wikipedia://en/1532579) from $A$, $C$, and $I$. Equivalently:

- The circle through $A$, $C$, and $I$ has its center at $D$. In particular, this implies that the center of this circle lies on the circumcircle.
- The three triangles $AID$, $CID$, and $ACD$ are [isosceles](wikipedia://en/315428), with $D$ as their apex.

A fourth point $E$, the [excenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excenter) of $ABC$ relative to $B$, also lies at the same distance from $D$, diametrically opposite from $I$.

:::

::: {#o5 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

By the [inscribed angle theorem](wikipedia://en/462730), $\angle IBA = \angle DCA, \ \angle IBC = \angle DAC.$

Since $BI$ is an angle bisector, $\angle DCA = \angle DAC \implies AD = CD.$

We also get

$$\begin{aligned} \angle DIA &= 180^\circ - \angle AIB \\ &= 180^\circ - (180^\circ - \angle IAB - \angle IBA) \\ &= \angle IAB + \angle IBA \\ &= \angle IAC + \angle DAC \\ &= \angle IAD \\ \implies AD &= DI. \end{aligned}$$

:::

## Application to triangle reconstruction {#Application_to_triangle_reconstruction}

This theorem can be used to reconstruct a triangle starting from the locations only of one vertex, the [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837), and the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) of the triangle. For, let $B$ be the given vertex, $I$ be the incenter, and $O$ be the circumcenter. This information allows the successive construction of:

- the circumcircle of the given triangle, as the circle with center $O$ and radius $OB$,
- point $D$ as the intersection of the circumcircle with line $BI$,
- the circle of the theorem, with center $D$ and radius $DI$, and
- vertices $A$ and $C$ as the intersection points of the two circles.

However, for some triples of points $B$, $I$, and $O$, this construction may fail, either because line $IB$ is tangent to the circumcircle or because the two circles do not have two crossing points. It may also produce a triangle for which the given point $I$ is an excenter rather than the incenter. In these cases, there can be no triangle having $B$ as vertex, $I$ as incenter, and $O$ as circumcenter.[^o23]

Other triangle reconstruction problems, such as the reconstruction of a triangle from a vertex, incenter, and center of its [nine-point circle](wikipedia://en/161243), can be solved by reducing the problem to the case of a vertex, incenter, and circumcenter.[^o23]

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

Let $I$ and $J$ be any two of the four points given by the incenter and the three excenters of a triangle $ABC$. Then $I$ and $J$ are collinear with one of the three triangle vertices. The circle with $IJ$ as diameter passes through the other two vertices and is centered on the circumcircle of $ABC$. When one of $I$ or $J$ is the incenter, this is the trillium theorem, with line $IJ$ as the (internal) angle bisector of one of the triangle's angles. However, it is also true when $I$ and $J$ are both excenters; in this case, line $IJ$ is the external angle bisector of one of the triangle's angles.

:::
