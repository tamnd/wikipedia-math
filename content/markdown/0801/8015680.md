# Monadic predicate calculus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Monadic predicate calculus"}

In [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), the **monadic predicate calculus** (also called **monadic first-order logic**) is the [fragment](wikipedia://en/43571557) of **[first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983)** (also called **predicate calculus**) in which all [relation symbols](wikipedia://en/1011332) in the [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_%28mathematical_logic%29) are [monadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monadic_%28arity%29) (that is, they take only one argument), and there are no function symbols. In other words, all [atomic formulas](wikipedia://en/4472066) are of the form $P(t)$, where $P$ is a relation symbol and $t$ is a [term](wikipedia://en/24885593).

:::

Monadic predicate calculus contrasts with the standard predicate calculus, which allows relation symbols that take two or more arguments. When we wish to emphasize the fact that the standard predicate calculus is not monadic, we would call it "**[polyadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyadic)** predicate calculus".

Without [polyadic relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyadic_relation) symbols, monadic predicate calculus is weaker than the full predicate calculus. Indeed, it is even a [decidable](wikipedia://en/913118) logic. That is, there exists a [decision algorithm](wikipedia://en/8336) that determines whether a given formula of monadic predicate calculus is [logically valid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_validity) (true for all nonempty [domains](wikipedia://en/229180)).[^o2][^o3] Adding a *single* binary relation symbol to monadic logic, however, results in an undecidable logic.

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Variants"}

## Variants {#Variants}

The formal system described above is sometimes called the **pure** monadic predicate calculus, where "pure" signifies the absence of function symbols. Allowing monadic function symbols changes the logic only superficially, whereas admitting even a single binary function symbol results in an undecidable logic.

[Monadic second-order logic](wikipedia://en/14650395) allows predicates of higher [arity](wikipedia://en/42301) in formulas. However, it only allows the second-order quantification to range over sets. That is, it allows one to speak of "For all monadic relations *P*, we have..." or "There exists a monadic relation *P*, such that...", but not "For all dyadic relations *Q*, we have...", etc.

:::

## Relationship with term logic {#Relationship_with_term_logic}

The need to go beyond monadic logic was not appreciated until the work on the logic of [relations](wikipedia://en/22517558), by [Augustus De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289) and [Charles Sanders Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) in the nineteenth century, and by [Frege](wikipedia://en/48416) in his 1879 *[Begriffsschrift](wikipedia://en/900125)*. Prior to the work of these three, [term logic](wikipedia://en/321652) (syllogistic logic) was widely considered adequate for formal deductive reasoning.

Inferences in term logic can all be represented in the monadic predicate calculus. For example the argument

All dogs are mammals.

No mammal is a bird.

Thus, no dog is a bird.

can be notated in the language of monadic predicate calculus as

$$[(\forall x\,D(x)\Rightarrow M(x))\land \neg(\exists y\,M(y)\land B(y))] \Rightarrow \neg(\exists z\,D(z)\land B(z))$$

where $D$, $M$ and $B$ denote the predicates of being, respectively, a dog, a mammal, and a bird.

Conversely, monadic predicate calculus is not significantly more expressive than term logic. Each formula in the monadic predicate calculus is [equivalent](wikipedia://en/169324) to a formula in which [quantifiers](wikipedia://en/43507260) appear only in closed subformulas of the form

$$\forall x\,P_1(x)\lor\cdots\lor P_n(x)\lor\neg P'_1(x)\lor\cdots\lor \neg P'_m(x)$$

or

$$\exists x\,\neg P_1(x)\land\cdots\land\neg P_n(x)\land P'_1(x)\land\cdots\land P'_m(x),$$

These formulas slightly generalize the basic judgements considered in term logic. For example, this form allows statements such as "*Every mammal is either a herbivore or a carnivore (or both)*", $\forall x\, M(x) \Rightarrow (H(x)\lor C(x))$. Reasoning about such statements can, however, still be handled within the framework of term logic, although not by the 19 classical Aristotelian [syllogisms](wikipedia://en/48046) alone.

Taking [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154) as given, every formula in the monadic predicate calculus expresses something that can likewise be formulated in term logic. On the other hand, a modern view of the [problem of multiple generality](wikipedia://en/931381) in traditional logic concludes that quantifiers cannot nest usefully if there are no polyadic predicates to relate the bound variables.
