# Trivialism

*Image: Trivialisme en logique symbolique.svg.* Trivialism in [symbolic logic](wikipedia://en/10983#Logical_symbols); Read as "given any proposition, it is a true proposition."

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Trivialism"}

**Trivialism** is the [logical theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_theory) that all [statements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statement_%28logic%29) (also known as [propositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositions)) are [true](wikipedia://en/161711) and, consequently, that all [contradictions](wikipedia://en/75807) of the form "p and not p" (e.g. the ball is red and not red) are true. In accordance with this, a **trivialist** is a person who believes everything is true.[^o2][^o3]

:::

In [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868), trivialism follows from a violation of the [law of noncontradiction](wikipedia://en/17636) due to the [principle of explosion](wikipedia://en/591394). In [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), trivialism is considered by some to be the complete opposite of [radical skepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_skepticism). [Paraconsistent logics](wikipedia://en/421085) may use "the law of non-triviality" to abstain from trivialism in logical practices that involve [true contradictions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_contradiction).

Theoretical arguments and anecdotes have been offered for trivialism to contrast it with theories such as [modal realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_realism), [dialetheism](wikipedia://en/591359) and paraconsistent logics.

## Overview {#Overview}

### Etymology {#Etymology}

*Trivialism*, as a term, is derived from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) word *trivialis,* meaning commonplace, in turn derived from the *[trivium](wikipedia://en/30666)*, the three introductory educational topics (grammar, logic, and rhetoric) expected to be learned by all [freemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_tenant). In [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), from this meaning, a "trivial" theory is something regarded as defective in the face of a complex [phenomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenon) that needs to be completely represented. Thus, literally, the trivialist theory is something expressed in the simplest possible way.[^o10]

### Theory {#Theory}

In [symbolic logic](wikipedia://en/10983#Logical_symbols), trivialism may be expressed as the following:[^o7]

$$\forall pTp$$

The above would be read as "given any proposition, it is a true proposition" through [universal quantification](wikipedia://en/76174) (∀).

A claim of trivialism may always apply its fundamental truth, otherwise known as a [truth predicate](wikipedia://en/4807326):

$$p \leftrightarrow Tp$$

The above would be read as a "proposition [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) a true proposition", meaning that all propositions are believed to be inherently proven as true. Without consistent use of this concept, a claim of advocating trivialism may not be seen as genuine and complete trivialism; as to claim a proposition is true but deny it as probably true may be considered inconsistent with the assumed theory.[^o7]

### Taxonomy of trivialisms {#Taxonomy_of_trivialisms}

Luis Estrada-González in "Models of Possibilism and Trivialism" lists four types of trivialism through the concept of [possible worlds](wikipedia://en/780566), with a "world" being a possibility and "the actual world" being reality. It is theorized a trivialist simply designates a value to all propositions in equivalence to seeing all propositions and their negations as true. This taxonomy is used to demonstrate the different strengths and plausibility of trivialism in this context:

- (T0) Minimal trivialism: At some world, all propositions have a designated value.
- (T1) Pluralist trivialism: In some worlds, all propositions have a designated value.
- (T2) [Actualist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actualism) trivialism: In the actual world, all propositions have a designated value.
- (T3) Absolute trivialism: In all worlds, all propositions have a designated value.[^o10]

## Arguments against trivialism {#Arguments_against_trivialism}

The consensus among the majority of philosophers is descriptively a denial of trivialism, termed as non-trivialism or anti-trivialism.[^o10] This is due to it being unable to produce a sound argument through the [principle of explosion](wikipedia://en/591394) and it being considered an [absurdity](wikipedia://en/1745810) ([reductio ad absurdum](wikipedia://en/22456146)).[^o3][^o7]

### Aristotle {#Aristotle}

[Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308)'s defense of the [law of noncontradiction](wikipedia://en/17636) is sometimes considered to be against trivialism. Luis Estrada-González in "Models of Possiblism and Trivialism" has interpreted Aristotle's *[Metaphysics Book IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics_%28Aristotle%29)* as such: "A family of arguments between 1008a26 and 1007b12 of the form 'If trivialism is right, then X is the case, but if X is the case then all things are one. But it is impossible that all things are one, so trivialism is impossible.' ... these Aristotelian considerations are the seeds of virtually all subsequent suspicions against trivialism: Trivialism has to be rejected because it identifies what should not be identified, and is undesirable from a logical point of view because it identifies what is not identical, namely, truth and falsehood."[^o10]

This view however is contentious. Aristotle said the law of non-contradiction was indemonstrable; that trying to prove the law of non-contradiction shows "want of education."[^o12] Others note for Aristotle the law of noncontradiction is a principle of *metaphysics* rather than one of logic. Aristotle notes his logic would still work even if the law of non contradiction were false.[^o13][^o14] This seems to mean Aristotle's logic denies explosion and so is paraconsistent rather than classical.

[Avicenna](wikipedia://en/1130)'s [commentary](wikipedia://en/145227#Philosophy) on the *[Metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics_%28Aristotle%29)* similarly says the law of non-contradiction is indemonstrable; that it "and their like are among the things that do not require our elaboration." However, like Aristotle, he then seems to go on to give a demonstration against those who deny the law, albeit a facetious one: "he must be subjected to the conflagration of fire, since 'fire' and 'not fire' are one. Pain must be inflicted on him through beating, since 'pain' and 'no pain' are one. And he must be denied food and drink, since eating and drinking and the abstention from both are one \[and the same\]."[^o15]

::: {#o17 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Law of non-triviality"}

### Priest {#Priest}

[Graham Priest](wikipedia://en/3659382) considers trivialism untenable: "a substantial case can be made for dialetheism; belief in \[trivialism\], though, would appear to be grounds for certifiable insanity".[^o18]

He formulated the "law of non-triviality" as a replacement for the [law of non-contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_non-contradiction) in paraconsistent logic and dialetheism.[^o27]

:::

## Arguments for trivialism {#Arguments_for_trivialism}

There are theoretical arguments for trivialism argued from the position of a [devil's advocate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devil's_advocate):

### Argument from possibilism {#Argument_from_possibilism}

Paul Kabay has argued for trivialism in "On the Plenitude of Truth" from the following:

[Possibilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Possibilism_%28philosophy%29) is true \[premise\]

If possibilism is true, then there is a world (either possible or impossible or both), *w*, in which trivialism is true \[premise\]

*w* is a possible world \[premise\]

It is true in *w* that *w* is identical to the actual world, A \[2\]

If it is true that there is a world, *w*, and *w* is a possible world, and it is true in *w* that *w* is identical to A, then trivialism is true \[premise\]

Trivialism is true \[1–5\][^o3][^o7]

Above, possibilism ([modal realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_realism); related to [possible worlds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Possible_worlds)) is the oft-debated theory that every proposition is possible. With this assumed to be true, trivialism can be assumed to be true as well according to Kabay.

### Paradoxes {#Paradoxes}

The [liar's paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liar's_paradox), [Curry's paradox](wikipedia://en/154163), the [principle of explosion](wikipedia://en/591394), and the Characterization Principle all can be asserted as valid and not required to be resolved and used to defend trivialism.[^o3][^o7]

## Philosophical implications {#Philosophical_implications}

### Comparison to skepticism {#Comparison_to_skepticism}

In Paul Kabay's comparison of trivialism to schools of philosophical skepticism (in "On the Plenitude of Truth")—such as [Pyrrhonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrrhonism)—who seek to attain a form of [ataraxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ataraxia), or state of imperturbability; it is purported the figurative trivialist inherently attains this state. This is claimed to be justified by the figurative trivialist seeing every [state of affairs](wikipedia://en/25718122) being true, even in a state of anxiety. Once universally accepted as true, the trivialist is free from any further anxieties regarding whether any state of affairs is true.

Kabay compares the Pyrrhonian skeptic to the figurative trivialist and claims that as the skeptic reportedly attains a state of imperturbability through a [suspension of belief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoché), the trivialist may attain such a state through an abundance of belief.

In this case—and according to independent claims by Graham Priest—trivialism is considered the complete opposite of [skepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skepticism).[^o3][^o7][^o24] However, insofar as the trivialist affirms all states of affairs as universally true, the Pyrrhonist neither affirms nor denies the truth (or falsity) of such affairs.[^o25]

### Impossibility of action {#Impossibility_of_action}

It is asserted by both Priest and Kabay that it is impossible for a trivialist to truly choose and thus act.[^o27][^o28] Priest argues this by the following in *Doubt Truth to Be a Liar*: "One cannot intend to act in such a way as to bring about some state of affairs, *s*, if one believes *s* already to hold. Conversely, if one acts with the purpose of bringing *s* about, one cannot believe that *s* already obtains." Due to their suspension of determination upon striking equipollence between claims, the Pyrrhonist has also remained subject to apraxia charges.[^o29][^o30][^o31]

## Advocates {#Advocates}

Paul Kabay, an Australian philosopher, in his book *A Defense of Trivialism* has argued that various philosophers in history have held views resembling trivialism, although he stops short of calling them trivialists. He mentions various pre-Socratic Greek philosophers as philosophers holding views resembling trivialism. He mentions that Aristotle in his book *Metaphysics* appears to suggest that Heraclitus and Anaxagoras advocated trivialism. He quotes Anaxagoras as saying that all things are one. Kabay also suggests Heraclitus' ideas are similar to trivialism because Heraclitus believed in a union of opposites, shown in such quotes as "the way up and down is the same".[^o7] Kabay also mentions a fifteenth century Roman Catholic cardinal, [Nicholas of Cusa](wikipedia://en/282522), stating that what Cusa wrote in *De Docta Ignorantia* is interpreted as stating that God contained every fact, which Kabay argues would result in trivialism, but Kabay admits that mainstream Cusa scholars would not agree with interpreting Cusa as a trivialist.[^o7] Kabay also mentions Spinoza as a philosopher whose views resemble trivialism. Kabay argues Spinoza was a trivialist because Spinoza believed everything was made of one substance which had infinite attributes.[^o7] Kabay also mentions Hegel as a philosopher whose views resemble trivialism, quoting Hegel as stating in *The Science of Logic* "everything is inherently contradictory."[^o7]

### Azzouni {#Azzouni}

[Jody Azzouni](wikipedia://en/1987344) is a purported advocate of trivialism in his article *The Strengthened Liar* by claiming that [natural language](wikipedia://en/21173) is [trivial](wikipedia://en/193053) and inconsistent through the existence of the [liar paradox](wikipedia://en/17967) ("This sentence is false"), and claiming that natural language has developed without central direction. Azzouni implies that every sentence in any natural language is true. "According to Azzouni, natural language is trivial, that is to say, every sentence in natural language is true...And, of course, trivialism follows straightforwardly from the triviality of natural language: after all, 'trivialism is true' is a sentence in natural language."[^o7][^o34][^o35]

### Anaxagoras {#Anaxagoras}

The Greek philosopher [Anaxagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaxagoras) is suggested as a possible trivialist by [Graham Priest](wikipedia://en/3659382) in his 2005 book *Doubt Truth to Be a Liar*. Priest writes, "He held that, at least at one time, everything was all mixed up so that no predicate applied to any one thing more than a contrary predicate."[^o27]

## Anti-trivialism {#Anti-trivialism}

*Image: Absolute anti-trivialism (or maximal logical nihilism) in symbolic logic.svg.* Absolute anti-trivialism (or maximal logical nihilism) in [symbolic logic](wikipedia://en/10983#Logical_symbols); Read as "given any proposition, it is neither a true or false proposition."

Luis Estrada-González in "Models of Possibilism and Trivialism" lists eight types of anti-trivialism (or non-trivialism) through the use of [possible worlds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Possible_worlds):

(AT0) [Actualist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actualist) minimal anti-trivialism: In the actual world, some propositions do not have a value of true or false.

(AT1) Actualist absolute anti-trivialism: In the actual world, all propositions do not have a value of true or false.

(AT2) Minimal anti-trivialism: In some worlds, some propositions do not have a value of true or false.

(AT3) Pointed anti-trivialism (or minimal logical [nihilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nihilism)): In some worlds, every proposition does not have a value of true or false.

(AT4) Distributed anti-trivialism: In every world, some propositions do not have a value of true or false.

(AT5) Strong anti-trivialism: Some propositions do not have a value of true or false in every world.

(AT6) Super anti-trivialism (or moderate logical nihilism): All propositions do not have a value of true or false at some world.

(AT7) Absolute anti-trivialism (or maximal logical nihilism): All propositions do not have a value of true or false in every world.[^o10]
