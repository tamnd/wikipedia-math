# Two-dimensional space

*Image: Noneuclid.svg.* Euclidean space has parallel lines which extend infinitely while remaining equidistant. In non-Euclidean spaces, lines perpendicular to a traversal either converge or diverge.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Two-dimensional space"}

A **two-dimensional space** is a [mathematical space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_space) with two [dimensions](wikipedia://en/8398), meaning [points](wikipedia://en/593693) have two [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/176622): their locations can be [locally](wikipedia://en/1462457) described with two [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate) or they can move in two independent directions. Common two-dimensional spaces are often called *[planes](wikipedia://en/19469852)* (especially the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410)), or, more generally, *[surfaces](wikipedia://en/4342970)*. These include analogs to physical spaces, like flat planes, and curved surfaces like spheres, cylinders, and cones, which can be infinite or finite. Some two-dimensional mathematical spaces are not used to represent physical positions, like an [affine plane](wikipedia://en/30764139) or [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628).

:::

## Flat {#Flat}

The most basic example is the flat [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), an idealization of a flat surface in [physical space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_space) such as a sheet of paper or a chalkboard. On the Euclidean plane, any two points can be joined by a unique [straight line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_line) along which the [distance](wikipedia://en/53932) can be measured. The space is flat because any two lines [transversed](wikipedia://en/13295107) by a third line [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to both of them are [parallel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_lines), meaning they never [intersect](wikipedia://en/41604289) and stay at uniform distance from each-other.

## Curved {#Curved}

Two-dimensional spaces can also be [curved](wikipedia://en/2557627), for example the [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) and [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane), sufficiently small portions of which appear like the flat plane, but on which straight lines which are locally parallel do not stay equidistant from each-other but eventually converge or diverge, respectively. Two-dimensional spaces with a locally Euclidean concept of distance but which can have non-uniform [curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) are called [Riemannian surfaces](wikipedia://en/15513875). (Not to be confused with [Riemann surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_surfaces).) Some surfaces are [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119) in [three-dimensional Euclidean space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-dimensional_Euclidean_space) or some other [ambient space](wikipedia://en/2854628), and inherit their structure from it; for example, [ruled surfaces](wikipedia://en/705158) such as the [cylinder](wikipedia://en/859283) and [cone](wikipedia://en/782427) contain a straight line through each point, and [minimal surfaces](wikipedia://en/276734) locally minimize their area, as is done physically by [soap films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap_film).

## Relativistic {#Relativistic}

[Lorentzian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentzian_manifold) surfaces look locally like a two-dimensional slice of [relativistic](wikipedia://en/26962) [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) with one spatial and one time dimension; constant-curvature examples are the flat Lorentzian plane (a two-dimensional subspace of [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space)) and the curved [de Sitter](wikipedia://en/301666) and [anti-de Sitter](wikipedia://en/301670) planes.

## Non-Euclidean {#Non-Euclidean}

Other types of mathematical planes and surfaces modify or do away with the structures defining the Euclidean plane. For example, the [affine plane](wikipedia://en/30764139) has a notion of parallel lines but no notion of distance; however, [signed areas](wikipedia://en/39433938) can be meaningfully compared, as they can in a more general [symplectic](wikipedia://en/28356) surface. The [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) does away with both distance and parallelism. A two-dimensional [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) has some concept of distance but it need not match the Euclidean version. A [topological surface](wikipedia://en/27865) can be stretched, twisted, or bent without changing its essential properties. An [algebraic surface](wikipedia://en/648311) is a two-dimensional set of solutions of a [system of polynomial equations](wikipedia://en/27420015).

## Information-holding {#Information-holding}

Some mathematical spaces have additional arithmetical structure associated with their points. A [vector plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_plane) is an affine plane whose points, called *[vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_vector)*, include a special designated [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) or zero vector. Vectors can be added together or [scaled](wikipedia://en/651752) by a number, and optionally have a Euclidean, Lorentzian, or Galilean concept of distance. The [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), [hyperbolic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_number) plane, and [dual number](wikipedia://en/42169) plane each have points which are considered numbers themselves, and can be added and multiplied. A [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181) or [Lorentz surface](wikipedia://en/31742143) appear locally like the complex plane or hyperbolic number plane, respectively.

## Definition and meaning {#Definition_and_meaning}

Mathematical spaces are often defined or represented using numbers rather than [geometric axioms](wikipedia://en/21317755). One of the most fundamental two-dimensional spaces is the [real coordinate space](wikipedia://en/1482083), denoted $\mathbb{R}^2,$ consisting of pairs of [real-number](wikipedia://en/20646438) coordinates. Sometimes the space represents arbitrary quantities rather than geometric positions, as in the [parameter space](wikipedia://en/2882813) of a mathematical model or the [configuration space](wikipedia://en/473596) of a physical system.

### Non-real numbers {#Non-real_numbers}

More generally, other types of numbers can be used as coordinates. The [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) is two-dimensional when considered to be formed from real-number coordinates, but [one-dimensional](wikipedia://en/25584215) in terms of [complex-number](wikipedia://en/5826) coordinates. A two-dimensional complex space – such as the two-dimensional [complex coordinate space](wikipedia://en/39125683), the [complex projective plane](wikipedia://en/737155), or a [complex surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_surface) – has two complex dimensions, which can alternately be represented using four real dimensions. A [two-dimensional lattice](wikipedia://en/469246#Lattices_in_two_dimensions:_detailed_discussion) is an infinite grid of points which can be represented using [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) coordinates. Some two-dimensional spaces, such as [finite planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_plane), have only a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) of elements.
