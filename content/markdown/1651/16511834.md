# Noli turbare circulos meos!

la

It depicts a mechanical drawing on a [roll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scroll) rather than in sand.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Noli turbare circulos meos!"}

"**Nōlī turbāre circulōs meōs!** is a [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) phrase, meaning "Do not disturb my circles!" It is said to have been uttered by [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844)—in reference to a geometric figure he had outlined on the sand—as he was about to be killed by a Roman soldier during the [Siege of Syracuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Syracuse_%28213–212_BC%29).[^o2]

:::

## Origin {#Origin}

According to [Valerius Maximus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valerius_Maximus), the phrase was uttered by the [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) mathematician and astronomer [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844). When the [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) conquered the city of [Syracuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syracuse,_Sicily) after the [siege of 214–212 BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Syracuse_%28214–212_BC%29), the Roman general [Marcus Claudius Marcellus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Claudius_Marcellus) gave the order to retrieve Archimedes. Some soldiers entered the house of Archimedes and one of the soldiers asked Archimedes who he was. But, according to Valerius Maximus (*Facta et dicta memorabilia*, Book VIII.7), Archimedes just answered "Noli, obsecro, istum disturbare[^o5] ("Do not, I entreat you, disturb that (sand)"), because he was so engrossed in the circles drawn on the sand in front of him. After that, one of the soldiers killed Archimedes, despite the order of Marcus Claudius Marcellus.

## Authenticity {#Authenticity}

[Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch) does not mention the quote in his *[Parallel Lives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_Lives)*.[^o5] Valerius Maximus (*Facta et dicta memorabilia*, Book VIII.7) attests the Latin form "noli ... istum disturbare ("I ask you not to disturb that sand").[^o5] Valerius's is the only version of the phrase that survives from antiquity. In the modern era, it was paraphrased as "Noli turbare circulos meos and then translated to [Katharevousa Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katharevousa_Greek) as "μὴ μου τοὺς κύκλους τάραττε!" ("el).[^o5]
