# MD4

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="MD4"}

The **MD4 Message-Digest Algorithm** is a [cryptographic hash function](wikipedia://en/439526) developed by [Ronald Rivest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Rivest) in 1990.[^o4] The digest length is 128 bits. The algorithm has influenced later designs, such as the [MD5](wikipedia://en/18826), [SHA-1](wikipedia://en/26672) and [RIPEMD](wikipedia://en/26363) algorithms. The initialism "MD" stands for "Message Digest".

:::

*Image: MD4.svg.* One MD4 operation. MD4 consists of 48 of these operations, grouped in three rounds of 16 operations. *F* is a nonlinear function; one function is used in each round. *M<sub>i</sub>* denotes a 32-bit block of the message input, and *K<sub>i</sub>* denotes a 32-bit constant, different for each round.

The security of MD4 has been severely compromised. The first full [collision attack](wikipedia://en/969624) against MD4 was published in 1995, and several newer attacks have been published since then. As of 2007, an attack can generate collisions in less than two MD4 hash operations.[^o5] A theoretical [preimage attack](wikipedia://en/969477) also exists.

A variant of MD4 is used in the [ed2k URI scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed2k:_URI_scheme) to provide a unique identifier for a file in the popular eDonkey2000 / eMule P2P networks. MD4 was also used by the [rsync](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rsync) protocol (prior to version 3.0.0).

MD4 is used to compute [NTLM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NTLM) password-derived key digests on Microsoft Windows NT, XP, Vista, 7, 8, 10 and 11.[^o6]

## Security {#Security}

Weaknesses in MD4 were demonstrated by Den Boer and Bosselaers in a paper published in 1991.[^o8] The first full-round MD4 [collision attack](wikipedia://en/969624) was found by [Hans Dobbertin](wikipedia://en/2210027) in 1995, which took only seconds to carry out at that time.[^o9] In August 2004, [Wang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_Xiaoyun) et al. found a very efficient collision attack, alongside attacks on later hash function designs in the MD4/MD5/SHA-1/RIPEMD family. This result was improved later by Sasaki et al., and generating a collision is now as cheap as verifying it (a few microseconds).[^o5]

In 2008, the [preimage resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preimage_resistance) of MD4 was also broken by Gaëtan Leurent, with a 2<sup>102</sup> attack.[^o10] In 2010 Guo et al. published a 2<sup>\99.7</sup> attack.[^o11]

In 2011, RFC 6150 stated that RFC 1320 (MD4) is **historic** (obsolete).

## MD4 hashes {#MD4_hashes}

The 128-bit (16-byte) MD4 hashes (also termed *message digests*) are typically represented as 32-digit [hexadecimal](wikipedia://en/13263) numbers. The following demonstrates a 43-byte [ASCII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASCII) input and the corresponding MD4 hash:

MD4("The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy #87CEEBog") = 1bee69a46ba811185c194762abaeae90

Even a small change in the message will (with overwhelming probability) result in a completely different hash, e.g. changing `d` to `c`:

MD4("The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy #87CEEBog") = b86e130ce7028da59e672d56ad0113df

The hash of the zero-length string is:

MD4("") = 31d6cfe0d16ae931b73c59d7e0c089c0

## MD4 collision example {#MD4_collision_example}

Let:

k1 = 839c7a4d7a92cb5678a5d5b9eea5a7573c8a74deb366c3dc20a083b69f5d2a3bb3719dc69891e9f95e809fd7e8b23ba6318edd45e51fe39708bf9427e9c3e8b9 k2 = 839c7a4d7a92cbd678a5d529eea5a7573c8a74deb366c3dc20a083b69f5d2a3bb3719dc69891e9f95e809fd7e8b23ba6318edc45e51fe39708bf9427e9c3e8b9

MD4(k1) = MD4(k2) = 4d7e6a1defa93d2dde05b45d864c429b

Note that two hex-digits of k1 and k2 define one byte of the input string, whose length is 64 bytes .
