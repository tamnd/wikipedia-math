# Gilbert Vernam

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gilbert Vernam"}

**Gilbert Sandford Vernam** (April 3, 1890 – February 7, 1960) was a [Worcester Polytechnic Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worcester_Polytechnic_Institute) 1914 graduate and [AT&T](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AT&T_Corporation) [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs) engineer who, in 1917, invented an additive [polyalphabetic](wikipedia://en/157918) [stream cipher](wikipedia://en/49007) and later co-invented an automated [one-time pad](wikipedia://en/22210) [cipher](wikipedia://en/5244). Vernam proposed a teleprinter cipher in which a previously prepared [key](wikipedia://en/53039), kept on [paper tape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_tape), is combined character by character with the [plaintext](wikipedia://en/157935) message to produce the [ciphertext](wikipedia://en/175560). To decipher the ciphertext, the same key would be again combined character by character, producing the [plaintext](wikipedia://en/157935). Vernam later worked for the [Postal Telegraph Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postal_Telegraph_Company), and became an employee of [Western Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Union) when that company acquired Postal in 1943. His later work was largely with automatic switching systems for [telegraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telegraphy) networks.

:::

## Vernam's patent {#Vernam's_patent}

*Image: USpatent1310719.fig1.png.* Figure 1 from Vernam's patent.

The combining function Vernam specified in 1310719, issued July 22, 1919, is the [XOR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XOR) operation, applied to the individual impulses or [bits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit) used to encode the characters in the [Baudot code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baudot_code). Vernam did not use the term "XOR" in the patent, but he implemented that operation in [relay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relay) logic. In the example Vernam gave, the [plaintext](wikipedia://en/157935) is *A*, encoded as "++---" in Baudot, and the key character is *B*, encoded as "+--++". The resulting ciphertext will be "-+-++", which encodes a *G*. Combining the *G* with the key character *B* at the receiving end produces "++---", which is the original plaintext *A*. The [NSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NSA) has called this patent "perhaps one of the most important in the history of cryptography.".[^o4]

## One-time pad {#One-time_pad}

Shortly thereafter, [Joseph Mauborgne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Mauborgne), at that time a captain in the [US Army Signal Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Signal_Corps), proposed, in addition, that the paper tape key contain [random](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random) information. The two ideas, when themselves combined, implement an automatic form of the [one-time pad](wikipedia://en/22210), though neither inventor used the name then.

[Claude Shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Elwood_Shannon), also at Bell Labs, proved that the one-time pad, properly implemented, is unbreakable in his [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) research that was later published in October 1949. He also proved that any unbreakable system must have essentially the same characteristics as the one-time pad: the key must be truly random, as large as the plaintext, never reused in whole or part, and kept secret.[^o6]

## The Vernam cipher {#The_Vernam_cipher}

In modern terminology, a **Vernam cipher** is a symmetrical [stream cipher](wikipedia://en/49007) in which the plaintext is combined with a random or [pseudorandom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudorandom) stream of data (the "keystream") to generate the ciphertext, using the [Boolean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_algebra_%28logic%29) ["exclusive or" (XOR)](wikipedia://en/105979) function. This is symbolised by ⊕ [^o8] and is represented by the following "[truth table](wikipedia://en/25512250)", where + represents "true" and − represents "false".

::: {#o9 .table cue=table}

| **INPUT** |  | **OUTPUT** |
|---|---|---|
| ***A*** | ***B*** | ***A* ⊕ *B*** |
| **−** | **−** | **−** |
| **−** | **\+** | **\+** |
| **\+** | **−** | **\+** |
| **\+** | **\+** | **−** |

:::

Other names for this function are: Not equal (NEQ), [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) 2 addition (without 'carry') and modulo 2 subtraction (without 'borrow').

The cipher is reciprocal in that the identical keystream is used both to encipher plaintext to ciphertext and to decipher ciphertext to yield the original plaintext:

Plaintext ⊕ Key = Ciphertext

and:

Ciphertext ⊕ Key = Plaintext

If the keystream is truly random and used only once, this is effectively a [one-time pad](wikipedia://en/22210). Substituting pseudorandom data generated by a [cryptographically secure pseudo-random number generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptographically_secure_pseudo-random_number_generator) is a common and effective construction for a stream cipher. [RC4](wikipedia://en/25831) is an example of a Vernam cipher that is widely used on the Internet.

If, however, the same keystream is used for two messages, known to cryptanalysts as a [depth](wikipedia://en/5715#Depth), the effect of the keystream can be eliminated, leaving the two plaintexts XORed together. The result is equivalent to a [Running key cipher](wikipedia://en/450657) and the two plaintexts may be separated by linguistic cryptanalytical techniques.

Ciphertext1 ⊕ Ciphertext2 = Plaintext1 ⊕ Plaintext2

An operator's mistake of this sort famously allowed the [Cryptanalysis of the Lorenz cipher](wikipedia://en/33647361) by the British at [Bletchley Park](wikipedia://en/4037) during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II). They diagnosed how the keystream was generated, worked out how to break the cipher, and read vast quantities of high-level messages to and from German high command without ever seeing an actual Lorenz machine.[^o10]
