# Magnitude (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Magnitude"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **magnitude** or **size** of a [mathematical object](wikipedia://en/19453961) is a property which determines whether the object is larger or smaller than other objects of the same kind. More formally, an object's magnitude is the displayed result of an [ordering](wikipedia://en/361924) (or ranking) of the [class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_%28mathematics%29) of objects to which it belongs. Magnitude as a concept dates to [Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) and has been applied as a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) of distance from one object to another. For numbers, the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of a number is commonly applied as the measure of units between a number and zero.

:::

In vector spaces, the [Euclidean norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_norm) is a measure of magnitude used to define a distance between two points in space. In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), magnitude can be defined as quantity or distance. An [order of magnitude](wikipedia://en/22657) is typically defined as a unit of distance between one number and another's numerical places on the decimal scale.

## History {#History}

[Ancient Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greeks) distinguished between several types of magnitude,[^o3] including:

- Positive [fractions](wikipedia://en/1704824)
- [Line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860) (ordered by [length](wikipedia://en/18542))
- [Plane figures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_shape) (ordered by [area](wikipedia://en/1209))
- [Solids](wikipedia://en/507960) (ordered by [volume](wikipedia://en/32498))
- [Angles](wikipedia://en/1196) (ordered by angular measure)

They proved that the first two could not be the same, or even [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) systems of magnitude.[^o4] They did not consider [negative](wikipedia://en/154616) magnitudes to be meaningful, and *magnitude* is still primarily used in contexts in which [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero) is either the smallest size or less than all possible sizes.

## In geometry {#In_geometry}

[Length](wikipedia://en/18542), [area](wikipedia://en/1209), and [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) describe the [size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Size_%28mathematics%29) or extent of an object in one dimension, two dimension, and three dimensions respectively.

## Numbers {#Numbers}

The magnitude of any [number](wikipedia://en/21690) $x$ is usually called its *[absolute value](wikipedia://en/991)* or *modulus*, denoted by $|x|$.[^o7]

### Real numbers {#Real_numbers}

The absolute value of a [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) *r* is defined by:[^o9]

$$\left| r \right| = r, \text{ if } r \text{ ≥ } 0$$

$$\left| r \right| = -r, \text{ if } r < 0 .$$

Absolute value may also be thought of as the number's [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) from zero on the real [number line](wikipedia://en/287188). For example, the absolute value of both 70 and −70 is 70.

### Complex numbers {#Complex_numbers}

A [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) *z* may be viewed as the position of a point *P* in a [2-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/9697), called the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628). The absolute value (or *[modulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulus_of_complex_number)*) of *z* may be thought of as the distance of *P* from the origin of that space. The formula for the absolute value of *z* = *a* + *bi* is similar to that for the [Euclidean norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_norm) of a vector in a 2-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697):[^o11]

$$\left| z \right| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$$

where the real numbers *a* and *b* are the [real part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part) and the [imaginary part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_part) of *z*, respectively. For instance, the modulus of −3 + 4*i* is $\sqrt{(-3)^2+4^2} = 5$. Alternatively, the magnitude of a complex number *z* may be defined as the square root of the product of itself and its [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918), $\bar{z}$, where for any complex number $z = a + bi$, its complex conjugate is $\bar{z} = a -bi$.

$$\left| z \right| = \sqrt{z\bar{z} } = \sqrt{(a+bi)(a-bi)} = \sqrt{a^2 -abi + abi - b^2i^2} = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2 }$$

(where $i^2 = -1$).

## Vector spaces {#Vector_spaces}

### Euclidean vector space {#Euclidean_vector_space}

A [Euclidean vector](wikipedia://en/32533) represents the position of a point *P* in a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). Geometrically, it can be described as an arrow from the origin of the space (vector tail) to that point (vector tip). Mathematically, a vector **x** in an *n*-dimensional Euclidean space can be defined as an ordered list of *n* real numbers (the [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinate) of *P*): *x* = \[*x*<sub>1</sub>, *x*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>\]. Its **magnitude** or **length**, denoted by $\|x\|$,[^o14] is most commonly defined as its [Euclidean norm](wikipedia://en/990534#Euclidean_norm) (or Euclidean length):[^o15]

$$\|\mathbf{x}\| = \sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots + x_n^2}.$$

For instance, in a 3-dimensional space, the magnitude of \[3, 4, 12\] is 13 because $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{169} = 13.$ This is equivalent to the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) of the vector with itself:

$$\|\mathbf{x}\| = \sqrt{\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{x}}.$$

The Euclidean norm of a vector is just a special case of [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932): the distance between its tail and its tip. Two similar notations are used for the Euclidean norm of a vector *x*:

1. $$\left \| \mathbf{x} \right \|,$$
1. $$\left | \mathbf{x} \right |.$$

A disadvantage of the second notation is that it can also be used to denote the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of [scalars](wikipedia://en/3588331) and the [determinants](wikipedia://en/8468) of [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859), which introduces an element of ambiguity.

### Normed vector spaces {#Normed_vector_spaces}

By definition, all Euclidean vectors have a magnitude (see above). However, a vector in an abstract [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) does not possess a magnitude.

A [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) endowed with a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534), such as the Euclidean space, is called a [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538).[^o17] The norm of a vector *v* in a normed vector space can be considered to be the magnitude of *v*.

### Pseudo-Euclidean space {#Pseudo-Euclidean_space}

In a [pseudo-Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/10962546), the magnitude of a vector is the value of the [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) for that vector.

## Logarithmic magnitudes {#Logarithmic_magnitudes}

When comparing magnitudes, a [logarithmic scale](wikipedia://en/164055) is often used. Examples include the [loudness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loudness) of a [sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound) (measured in [decibels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decibel)), the [brightness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brightness) of a [star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star), and the [Richter scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richter_magnitude_scale) of earthquake intensity. Logarithmic magnitudes can be negative. In the [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_sciences), a logarithmic magnitude is typically referred to as a *[level](wikipedia://en/39999171)*.

:::

## Order of magnitude {#Order_of_magnitude}

Orders of magnitude denote differences in numeric quantities, usually measurements, by a factor of 10—that is, a difference of one digit in the location of the decimal point.
