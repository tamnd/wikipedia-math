# McKelvey–Schofield chaos theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="McKelvey–Schofield chaos theorem"}

The **McKelvey–Schofield chaos theorem** is a result in [social choice theory](wikipedia://en/1745670). It states that if preferences are defined over a multidimensional policy space, then choosing policies using [majority rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majority_rule) is unstable. There will in most cases be no [Condorcet winner](wikipedia://en/1133911) and any policy can be enacted through a sequence of votes, regardless of the original policy. This means that adding more policies and changing the order of votes ("agenda manipulation") can be used to arbitrarily pick the winner.[^o11]

:::

Versions of the theorem have been proved for different types of preferences, with different classes of exceptions. A version of the theorem was first proved by [Richard McKelvey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_McKelvey) in 1976, for preferences based on [Euclidean distances](wikipedia://en/53932) in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$. Another version of the theorem was proved by [Norman Schofield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Schofield) in 1978, for [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) preferences.

The theorem can be thought of as showing that [Arrow's impossibility theorem](wikipedia://en/89425) holds when preferences are restricted to be [concave](wikipedia://en/263321) in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$. The [median voter theorem](wikipedia://en/1233715) shows that when preferences are restricted to be single-peaked on the real line, Arrow's theorem does not hold, and the median voter's ideal point is a Condorcet winner. In higher dimensions the median voter's ideal point can be found by the [geometric median](wikipedia://en/10324687).[^o2]

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

The theorem considers a finite number of voters, $n$, who vote for policies which are represented as points in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) of dimension $m$. Each vote is between two policies using [majority rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majority_rule). Each voter, $i$, has a [utility function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_function), $U_{i}$, which measures how much they value different policies.

:::

## Euclidean preferences {#Euclidean_preferences}

[Richard McKelvey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_McKelvey) considered the case when preferences are "Euclidean metrics".[^o6] That means every voter's utility function has the form

$$U_i(x)=\Phi_i \cdot d(x,x_i)$$

for all policies $x$ and some $x_{i}$, where $d$ is the [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) and $\Phi_i$ is a [monotone decreasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotone_decreasing) function.

Under these conditions, there could be a collection of policies which don't have a [Condorcet winner](wikipedia://en/1133911) using majority rule. This means that, given a number of policies $X_{a}$, $X_{b}$, $X_{c}$, there could be a series of pairwise elections where:

1. $X_{a}$ wins over $X_{b}$
1. $X_{b}$ wins over $X_{c}$
1. $X_{c}$ wins over $X_{a}$

McKelvey proved that elections can be even more "chaotic" than that: If there is no equilibrium outcome then any two policies, e.g. $A$ and $B$, have a sequence of policies, $X_1,X_2,...,X_s$, where each one pairwise wins over the other in a series of elections, meaning:

1. $A$ wins over $X_{1}$
1. $X_{1}$ wins over $X_{2}$
1. ...
1. $X_{s}$ wins over $B$

This is true regardless of whether $A$ would beat $B$ or *vice versa*.

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

*Image: McKelvey theorem example.svg.* An example of McKelvey's theorem

The simplest illustrating example is in [two dimensions](wikipedia://en/15575410), with three voters. Each voter will then have a maximum preferred policy, and any other policy will have a corresponding circular [indifference curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indifference_curve) centered at the preferred policy. If a policy was proposed, then any policy in [the intersection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle–circle_intersection) of two voters indifference curves would beat it. Any point in the plane will almost always have a set of points that are preferred by 2 out of 3 voters.

:::

## Generalisations {#Generalisations}

[Norman Schofield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Schofield) extended the theorem to more general classes of utility functions, requiring only that they are [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206). He also established conditions for the existence of a directed [continuous path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_path) of policies, where each policy further along the path would win against one earlier.[^o10][^o11] Some of Schofield's proofs were later found to be incorrect by Jeffrey S. Banks, who corrected his proofs.[^o12][^o13]
