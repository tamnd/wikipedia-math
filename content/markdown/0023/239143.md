# Isaac Watts

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Isaac Watts"}

*Image: Statue of Isaac Watts, Abney Park Cemetery.jpg.* Statue of Watts, Abney Park Cemetery

**Isaac Watts** (17 July 1674 – 25 November 1748) was an English [Congregational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congregational_church) minister, [hymn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hymn) writer, [theologian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theologian), and [logician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logician). He was a prolific and popular hymn writer and is credited with some 750 hymns. His works include "[When I Survey the Wondrous Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/When_I_Survey_the_Wondrous_Cross)", "[Joy to the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joy_to_the_World)", and "[O God, Our Help in Ages Past](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_God,_Our_Help_in_Ages_Past)". He is recognised as the "Godfather of English Hymnody"; many of his hymns remain in use today and have been translated into numerous languages.

:::

## Life {#Life}

Watts was born in [Southampton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southampton), [Hampshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hampshire), England, in 1674 and was brought up in the home of a committed religious [nonconformist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonconformist_%28Protestantism%29); his father, also Isaac Watts, had been incarcerated twice for his views. Watts had a classical education at [King Edward VI School, Southampton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Edward_VI_School,_Southampton), learning [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin), [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language), and [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew).

Watts could not attend [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University) or [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge) because he was a nonconformist and these universities were restricted to Anglicans—as were government positions at the time. He went to the [Dissenting Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissenting_academies) at [Stoke Newington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoke_Newington) in 1690. Much of the remainder of his life centred on that village, which is now part of [Inner London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_London).

Following his education, Watts was called as pastor of a large independent chapel in London, Mark Lane Congregational Chapel, where he helped train preachers, despite his poor health. He held religious opinions that were more nondenominational or [ecumenical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical) than was common for a nonconformist Congregationalist. He had a greater interest in promoting education and scholarship than preaching for any particular sect.

Watts took work as a private tutor and lived with the nonconformist Hartopp family at [Fleetwood House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abney_Park) on [Church Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoke_Newington_Church_Street) in [Stoke Newington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoke_Newington). Through them, he became acquainted with their immediate neighbours Sir [Thomas Abney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Abney) and [Lady Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Mary_Abney). He eventually lived for a total of 36 years in the Abney household, most of the time at Abney House, their second residence. (Lady Mary had inherited the manor of [Stoke Newington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoke_Newington) in 1701 from her late brother Thomas Gunston.)

On the death of Sir Thomas Abney in 1722, his widow Lady Mary and her unmarried daughter Elizabeth moved all her household to Abney House from Hertfordshire, and she invited Watts to continue with them. He particularly enjoyed the grounds at [Abney Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abney_Park), which Lady Mary planted with two elm walks leading down to an island [heronry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heronry) in the [Hackney Brook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hackney_Brook), and he often sought inspiration there for the many books and hymns that he wrote.

Watts lived at Abney Hall in Stoke Newington until his death in 1748; he was buried in [Bunhill Fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunhill_Fields). He left an extensive legacy of hymns, treatises, educational works, and essays. His work was influential amongst nonconformist independents and religious revivalists of the 18th century, such as [Philip Doddridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Doddridge), who dedicated his best-known work to Watts.

## Watts and his hymns {#Watts_and_his_hymns}

Sacred music scholars Stephen Marini, Denny Prutow and Michael LeFebvre describe the ways in which Watts contributed to English hymnody and the previous tradition of the Church. Watts led the change in practice by including new poetry for "original songs of Christian experience" to be used in worship, according to Marini.[^n1] The older tradition was based on the poetry of the Bible: the [Psalms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psalm). According to LeFebvre, Psalms had been sung by God's people from the time of King David, who with a large staff over many years assembled the complete book of Psalms in a form appropriate for singing (by the Levites, during Temple sacrifices at the time). The practice of singing Psalms in worship was continued by Biblical command in the New Testament Church from its beginnings in Acts through the time of Watts, as documented by Prutow. The teachings of 16th-century [Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Reformation) leaders such as [John Calvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Calvin), who translated the Psalms in the vernacular for congregational singing, followed this historic worship practice.[^n2] Watts was not the first Protestant to promote the singing of hymns; however, his prolific hymn writing helped usher in a new era of English worship as many other poets followed in his path.[^o9]

Watts also introduced a new way of rendering the Psalms in verse for church services, proposing that they be adapted for hymns with a specifically Christian perspective. As Watts put it in the title of his 1719 metrical Psalter, the Psalms should be "imitated in the language of the New Testament."[^n1] Besides writing hymns, Isaac Watts was also a [theologian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theologian) and [logician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logician), writing books and essays on these subjects. Isaac Watts is credited by many with introducing hymns to the English churches when his Hymns and Spiritual Songs was first published in 1707. They are extensively used today due to his poetic gifts, but also because he interpreted the Old Testament using knowledge of the New. This set an example for later hymn writers. Isaac Watts explained his methods as follows: “Where the Psalmist describes religion by the fear of God, I have often joined faith and love to it. Where he speaks of pardon of sin through the mercies of God, I have added the merits of a Saviour. Where he talks of sacrificing goats and bullocks, I rather mention the sacrifice of Christ, the Lamb of God. When he attends the ark with shouting into Zion, I sing of the ascension of my Saviour into heaven, or His presence in His church on earth. Where he promises abundance of wealth, honor, and long life, I have changed some of these typical blessings for grace, glory, and life eternal, which are brought to light in the Gospel, and promised in the New Testament.”[^n3]

## Logic and science {#Logic_and_science}

*Image: Isaac Watts.jpg.* Isaac Watts

Watts wrote a textbook on [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) which was particularly popular; its full title was, *Logick, or The Right Use of Reason in the Enquiry After Truth With a Variety of Rules to Guard Against Error in the Affairs of Religion and Human Life, as well as in the Sciences*. This was first published in 1724, and it was printed in twenty editions.

Watts wrote this work for beginners of logic, and arranged the book methodically. He divided the content of his elementary treatment of logic into four parts: [perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perception), [judgement](wikipedia://en/161979), [reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reasoning), and method, which he treated in this order. Each of these parts is divided into chapters, and some of these chapters are divided into sections. The content of the chapters and sections is subdivided by the following devices: divisions, distributions, notes, observations, directions, rules, illustrations, and remarks. Every contentum of the book comes under one or more of these headings, and this methodical arrangement serves to make the exposition clear.

In Watts's *Logic,* there are notable departures from other works of the time, and some notable innovations. The influence of British [empiricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricism) may be seen, especially that of contemporary [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher) and empiricist [John Locke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Locke). *Logic* includes several references to Locke and his *[Essay Concerning Human Understanding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essay_Concerning_Human_Understanding)*,[^n4] in which he espoused his empiricist views. Watts was careful to distinguish between [judgements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judgment) and [propositions](wikipedia://en/81094), unlike some other logic authors. According to Watts, judgement is "to compare ... ideas together, and to join them by [affirmation](wikipedia://en/81094), or disjoin then by [negation](wikipedia://en/161019), according as we find them to agree or disagree".[^n5] He continues, "when mere ideas are joined in the mind without words, it is rather called a judgement; but when clothed with words it is called a proposition".[^n6] Watts's *Logic* follows the [scholastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism) tradition and divides propositions into universal affirmative, universal negative, particular affirmative, and particular negative.

In the third part, Watts discusses [reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reasoning) and [argumentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argumentation), with particular emphasis on the theory of [syllogism](wikipedia://en/48046). This was considered a centrally important part of [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868). According to Watts, and in keeping with logicians of his day, Watts defined logic as an art (see [liberal arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_arts)), as opposed to a [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science). Throughout *Logic,* Watts revealed his high conception of logic by stressing the [practical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Practical) side of logic, rather than the speculative side. According to Watts, as a practical art, logic can be really useful in any inquiry, whether it is an inquiry in the arts, or inquiry in the sciences, or inquiry of an [ethical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics) kind. Watts's emphasis on logic as a practical art distinguishes his book from others.

By stressing a practical and non-formal part of logic, Watts gave rules and directions for any kind of inquiry, including the inquiries of science and the inquiries of [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy). These rules of inquiry were given in addition to the formal content of classical logic common to textbooks on logic from that time. Watts's conception of logic as being divided into its practical part and its speculative part marks a departure from the conception of logic of most other authors. His conception of logic is more akin to that of the later, nineteenth-century logician, [Charles Sanders Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117).

Isaac Watts's *Logic* became the standard text on logic at [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University), [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University), [Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) and [Yale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University), being used at Oxford for well over 100 years. Charles Sanders Peirce wrote favourably of Watts's *Logic*. When preparing his own textbook, titled *A Critick of Arguments: How to Reason* (also known as the *Grand Logic*), Peirce wrote, "I shall suppose the reader to be acquainted with what is contained in Dr Watts' *Logick*, a book ... far superior to the treatises now used in colleges, being the production of a man distinguished for good sense."[^o17]

Watts followed the *Logic* in 1741 by a supplement, *The Improvement of the Mind.* This also went through numerous editions and later inspired [Michael Faraday](wikipedia://en/19727). It was also widely used as a moral textbook in schools.

### "Element" vs. "compound" {#"Element"_vs._"compound"}

In *Logic*, Watts gave an early definition of [chemical element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element), and contrasted "element" with [chemical "compound"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound) in clear, modern terms. He also provided an early list of elements then recognized by chemists—five in number—as he understood it. Watts did also, however, note the lack of consensus among chemists.[^o19]

## Legacy, honours and memorials {#Legacy,_honours_and_memorials}

*Image: Isaac Watts DD tomb in Bunhill Fields.jpg.* Watts's tomb in [Bunhill Fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunhill_Fields)

*Image: Abney Park Cemetery Isaac Watts 2020.jpg.* London's only public statue to Watts is in [Abney Park Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abney_Park_Cemetery), [Stoke Newington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoke_Newington).

*Image: Watts Park.jpg.* Statue of Watts in [Watts Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watts_Park), Southampton (city of his birth)

On his death, Isaac Watts's papers were given to [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University) in the [Colony of Connecticut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colony_of_Connecticut), which nonconformists (Puritans/Congregationalists) had established. [King Edward VI School, Southampton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Edward_VI_School,_Southampton), which he attended, named one of its [houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_system) "Watts" in his honour and has [Our God, Our Help in Ages Past](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_God,_Our_Help_in_Ages_Past) as its school hymn.

The [Church of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_England) and [Lutheran Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutheran_Church) remember Watts (and his ministerial service) annually in the [Calendar of Saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_%28Church_of_England%29) with a [commemoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commemoration_%28Anglicanism%29) on 25 November.[^o21]

The earliest surviving monument to Watts is in [Westminster Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Abbey); this was completed shortly after his death. His much-visited chest tomb at [Bunhill Fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunhill_Fields) dates from 1808, replacing the original that had been paid for and erected by [Lady Mary Abney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Mary_Abney) and the Hartopp family.

Another early memorial may be lost: a bust to Watts commissioned on his death for the London chapel with which he was associated. The chapel was demolished in the late 18th century: remaining parts of the memorial were rescued at the last minute by a wealthy landowner for installation in his chapel near [Liverpool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liverpool), but it is not known whether the bust survives. Another bust is installed at the nonconformist [Dr Williams's Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dr_Williams's_Library), in central London.

The first public statue stands at [Abney Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abney_Park), where Watts lived for more than 30 years at the manor house, and where he also died. The park later became [Abney Park Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abney_Park_Cemetery), opened in 1840; and the statue of Watts was erected here by public subscription in 1845. It stands in Dr Watts' Walk, in front of the [Abney Park Chapel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abney_Park_Chapel), and was designed by the leading British sculptor, [Edward Hodges Baily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Hodges_Baily). A scheme for a commemorative statue on this spot had been promoted in the late 1830s by [George Collison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Collison), who in 1840 published an engraving as the frontispiece of his book about cemetery design in Europe and America, and at Abney Park in particular. Collison's proposal was never commissioned, and Baily's design was adopted instead.

A later, rather similar statue was also funded by public subscription and erected in a new Victorian public park named after Watts in Southampton, the city of his birth. In the mid-nineteenth century, the Congregational Dr Watts Memorial Hall was built in Southampton and also named after him. It was lost to redevelopment after the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War), but the Isaac Watts Memorial United Reformed Church was built on the site.

In 1974, the City of Southampton commemorated the tercentenary of Watts's birth by commissioning the biography *Isaac Watts Remembered*, written by David G. Fountain, who like Watts, was a nonconformist minister from Southampton. The clock on Southampton Civic Centre chimes the tune of the opening line of 'Our God, our help in ages past', three times a day, at 8 am, 12 noon and 4 pm.[^o23]

## Cultural or contemporary influences {#Cultural_or_contemporary_influences}

*Image: Watts-BusyBee.png.* "Against Idleness and Mischief"

In his novel *[David Copperfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Copperfield_%28novel%29)* (1850), [Charles Dickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens) has school master Dr. Strong quote from Watts's "Against Idleness and Mischief".

In [Herman Melville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Melville)'s epic novel *[Moby-Dick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moby-Dick)* (1851), a minor investor in the whaling ship *Pequod* is Charity Bildad, "a lean old lady of a most determined and indefatigable spirit, but withal very kindhearted" (chapter 20). Her brother, the captain, had forbidden the sailors to sing "profane songs" such as [sea shanties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_shanty), so she "placed a small choice copy of Watts in each seaman’s berth" (chapter 22).

One of Watts's best-known poems was an exhortation "Against Idleness and Mischief" in *[Divine Songs for Children](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Songs_for_Children).* This was [parodied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parody) by [Lewis Carroll](wikipedia://en/26586083) in the poem "[How Doth the Little Crocodile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_Doth_the_Little_Crocodile)", included in Chapter 2 of *[Alice's Adventures in Wonderland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice's_Adventures_in_Wonderland)* (1865). His parody is better known than Watts's original poem. The poem was also featured in the segment on the cartoon programme "Rocky and His Friends" called "Bullwinkle's Corner", in which Bullwinkle Moose recites poetry. In this case, the poem was titled "The Bee", with no author credit. A second example appears in Chapter 10 of *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*, the poem "['Tis the Voice of the Lobster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/'Tis_the_Voice_of_the_Lobster)", which parodies the opening lines of "The Sluggard": "'Tis the voice of the sluggard; I heard him complain, / 'You have waked me too soon, I must slumber again.'"

The 1884 [comic opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comic_opera) *[Princess Ida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princess_Ida)* includes a punning reference to Watts in Act I. At Princess Ida's women's university, no men are allowed. Her father King Gama says that "She'll scarcely suffer Dr. Watts' 'hymns'".

A poem often referred to as "False Greatness" by [Joseph Merrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Merrick) ("The Elephant Man"), which was used in writing or "signature block" by Merrick, starting "Tis true, my form is something odd/but blaming me, is blaming God ..." is often (incorrectly) quoted or cited as a work by Isaac Watts. In fact only the last few sentences were penned by Watts ("False Greatness", book II – Horae lyricae 1743) starting "Mylo, forbear to call him bless'd / That only boasts a large estate ..."[^o25]

Many of Watts's hymns are included in the Anglican [Hymns Ancient and Modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hymns_Ancient_and_Modern), the Oxford [Book of Common Praise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Common_Praise), the non-denominational, evangelical [Mission Praise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission_Praise), the [Christadelphian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christadelphian) hymnal, the Episcopal Church's *Hymnal 1982*, *Evangelical Lutheran Worship*, the [Baptist Hymnal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptist_Hymnal), the Presbyterian [Trinity Hymnal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_Hymnal), and the Methodist [Hymns and Psalms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hymns_and_Psalms). Many of his texts are also used in the American hymnal, *The [Sacred Harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_Harp),* using what is known as the [shape note](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_note) notation used for teaching non-musicians. Several of his hymns are used in the hymnals of the [Church of Christ, Scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Christ,_Scientist) and [the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Church_of_Jesus_Christ_of_Latter-day_Saints).
