# Mathematics of paper folding

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mathematics of paper folding"}

*Image: MapFoldings-2x2.png.* [Map folding](wikipedia://en/26185707) for a 2×2 grid of squares: there are eight different ways to fold such a map along its creases

The discipline of [origami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origami) or paper folding has received a considerable amount of [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) study. Fields of interest include a given paper model's flat-foldability (whether the model can be flattened without damaging it), and the use of paper folds to solve [mathematical equations](wikipedia://en/9284) up to the third order.[^o7]

:::

Computational origami is a recent branch of [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) that is concerned with studying algorithms that solve paper-folding problems. The field of computational origami has also grown significantly since its inception in the 1990s with [Robert Lang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_J._Lang)'s TreeMaker algorithm to assist in the precise folding of bases.[^o2] Computational origami results either address origami design or origami foldability.[^o3] In origami design problems, the goal is to design an object that can be folded out of paper given a specific target configuration. In origami foldability problems, the goal is to fold something using the creases of an initial configuration. Results in origami design problems have been more accessible than in origami foldability problems.[^o3]

## History {#History}

In 1893, Indian [civil servant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_service) T. Sundara Row published *[Geometric Exercises in Paper Folding](wikipedia://en/63640292)* which used paper folding to demonstrate proofs of geometrical constructions. This work was inspired by the use of origami in the [kindergarten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kindergarten) system. Row demonstrated an approximate trisection of angles and implied that the construction of a cube root was impossible.[^o5]

In 1922, [Harry Houdini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Houdini) published "Houdini's Paper Magic," which described origami techniques that drew informally from mathematical approaches that were later formalized.[^o6]

*Image: Group1-Origami The Beloch Fold.png.* The Beloch fold

In 1936 [Margharita P. Beloch](wikipedia://en/41887951) showed that use of the '[Beloch fold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beloch_fold)', later used in the sixth of the [Huzita–Hatori axioms](wikipedia://en/232844), allowed the general [cubic equation](wikipedia://en/180787) to be solved using origami.[^o7]

In 1949, R C Yeates' book "Geometric Methods" described three allowed constructions corresponding to the first, second, and fifth of the Huzita–Hatori axioms.[^o8][^o9]

The [Yoshizawa–Randlett system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshizawa–Randlett_system) of instruction by diagram was introduced in 1961.[^o10]

*Image: Miura-Ori CP.svg.* [Crease pattern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crease_pattern) for a Miura fold. The parallelograms of this example have 84° and 96° angles.

In 1980 a construction was reported which enabled an angle to be trisected. Trisections are impossible under Euclidean rules.[^o53]

Also in 1980, [Kōryō Miura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kōryō_Miura) and Masamori Sakamaki demonstrated a novel map-folding technique whereby the folds are made in a prescribed parallelogram pattern, which allows the map to be expandable without any right-angle folds in the conventional manner. Their pattern allows the fold lines to be interdependent, and hence the map can be unpacked in one motion by pulling on its opposite ends, and likewise folded by pushing the two ends together. No unduly complicated series of movements are required, and folded *Miura-ori* can be packed into a very compact shape.[^o11] In 1985 Miura reported a method of packaging and deployment of large membranes in outer space,[^o12] and as early as 2012 this technique had been applied to [solar panels on spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_panels_on_spacecraft).[^o13][^o14]

*Image: Group1-Origami Doubling the Cube .png.* A diagram showing the first and last step of how origami can double the cube

In 1986, Messer reported a construction by which one could [double the cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_the_cube), which is impossible with Euclidean constructions.[^o49]

The first complete statement of the seven axioms of origami by French folder and mathematician [Jacques Justin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Justin) was written in 1986, but were overlooked until the first six were rediscovered by [Humiaki Huzita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humiaki_Huzita) in 1989.[^o15] The first International Meeting of Origami Science and Technology (now known as the International Conference on Origami in Science, Math, and Education) was held in 1989 in Ferrara, Italy. At this meeting, a construction was given by Scimemi for the [regular heptagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_heptagon).[^o16]

Around 1990, [Robert J. Lang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_J._Lang) and others first attempted to write computer code that would solve origami problems.[^o17]

*Image: Maekawas Theorem.svg.* Mountain-valley counting

In 1996, [Marshall Bern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Bern) and [Barry Hayes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Hayes) showed that the problem of assigning a crease pattern of mountain and valley folds in order to produce a flat origami structure starting from a flat sheet of paper is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete).[^o31]

In 1999, a theorem due to Haga provided constructions used to divide the side of a square into rational fractions.[^o41][^o42]

In 2002, [sarah-marie belcastro](wikipedia://en/70807222) and [Tom Hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Hull_%28mathematician%29) brought to the theoretical origami the language of [affine transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_transformations), with an extension from $R$<sup>2</sup> to $R$<sup>3</sup> in only the case of single-vertex construction.[^o18]

In 2002, Alperin solved [Alhazen's problem](wikipedia://en/26855522) of spherical optics.[^o19] In the same paper, Alperin showed a construction for a regular heptagon.[^o19] In 2004, was proven algorithmically the fold pattern for a regular heptagon.[^o20] Bisections and trisections were used by Alperin in 2005 for the same construction.[^o21]

In 2005, principles and concepts from mathematical and computational origami were applied to solve *Countdown*, a game popularized in British television in which competitors used a list of source numbers to build an arithmetic expression as close to the target number as possible.[^o22]

In 2009, Alperin and Lang extended the theoretical origami to rational equations of arbitrary degree, with the concept of manifold creases.[^o23][^o24] This work was a formal extension of Lang's unpublished 2004 demonstration of angle quintisection.[^o24][^o25]

## Pure origami {#Pure_origami}

::: {#o28 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Flat-foldability"}

### Flat folding {#Flat_folding}

*Image: Lang rule one.png.* Two-colorability

*Image: Lang rule three.png.* Angles around a vertex

The construction of origami models is sometimes shown as crease patterns. The major question about such crease patterns is whether a given crease pattern can be folded to a flat model, and if so, how to fold it; this is an [NP-complete problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete_problem).[^o29] Related problems when the creases are orthogonal are called [map folding](wikipedia://en/26185707) problems. There are three mathematical rules for producing flat-foldable origami [crease patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crease_pattern):[^o30]

1. [Maekawa's theorem](wikipedia://en/39459296): at any vertex the number of valley and mountain folds always differ by two.

It follows from this that every vertex has an even number of creases, and therefore also the regions between the creases can be colored with two colors.

1. [Kawasaki's theorem](wikipedia://en/14511776) or Kawasaki-Justin theorem: at any vertex, the sum of all the odd angles (see image) adds up to 180 degrees, as do the even.
1. A sheet can never penetrate a fold.

Paper exhibits zero [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) at all points on its surface, and only folds naturally along lines of zero curvature. Curved surfaces that can't be flattened can be produced using a non-folded crease in the paper, as is easily done with wet paper or a fingernail.

Assigning a crease pattern mountain and valley folds in order to produce a flat model has been proven by [Marshall Bern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Bern) and [Barry Hayes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Hayes) to be NP-complete.[^o31] Further references and technical results are discussed in Part II of *[Geometric Folding Algorithms](wikipedia://en/63007621)*.[^o32]

:::

::: {#o34 .statement kind=statement/axiom cue=named-heading name="Huzita–Justin axioms"}

### Huzita–Justin axioms {#Huzita–Justin_axioms}

Some [classical construction problems of geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge_constructions#Impossible_constructions) — namely [trisecting an arbitrary angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trisecting_an_arbitrary_angle) or [doubling the cube](wikipedia://en/91110) — are proven to be unsolvable using [compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge), but can be solved using only a few paper folds.[^o35] Paper fold strips can be constructed to solve equations up to degree 4. The Huzita–Justin axioms or Huzita–Hatori axioms are an important contribution to this field of study. These describe what can be constructed using a sequence of creases with at most two point or line alignments at once. Complete methods for solving all equations up to degree 4 by applying methods satisfying these axioms are discussed in detail in *[Geometric Origami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_Origami)*.[^o36]

:::

## Constructions {#Constructions}

As a result of origami study through the application of geometric principles, methods such as Haga's theorem have allowed paperfolders to accurately fold the side of a square into thirds, fifths, sevenths, and ninths. Other theorems and methods have allowed paperfolders to get other shapes from a square, such as equilateral [triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangles), [pentagons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentagons), [hexagons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagons), and special rectangles such as the [golden rectangle](wikipedia://en/219374) and the [silver rectangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_rectangle). Methods for folding most regular polygons up to and including the regular Enneadecagon have been developed.[^o36] A regular *n*-gon can be constructed by paper folding [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) *n* is a product of distinct [Pierpont primes](wikipedia://en/2595060), [powers of two](wikipedia://en/376948), and [powers of three](wikipedia://en/14214617).

::: {#o40 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Haga's theorems"}

### Haga's theorems {#Haga's_theorems}

*Image: Haga theorem 1.svg.* BQ is always rational if AP is.

The side of a square can be divided at an arbitrary rational fraction in a variety of ways. Haga's theorems say that a particular set of constructions can be used for such divisions.[^o41][^o42] Surprisingly few folds are necessary to generate large odd fractions. For instance $\tfrac{1}{5}$ can be generated with three folds; first halve a side, then use Haga's theorem twice to produce first $\tfrac{2}{3}$ and then $\tfrac{1}{5}$.

The accompanying diagram shows Haga's first theorem:

$$BQ = \frac{2 AP}{1 + AP}.$$

The function changing the length *AP* to *QC* is [self inverse](wikipedia://en/293450). Let *x* be *AP* then a number of other lengths are also rational functions of *x*. For example:

*Haga's first theorem*

| AP | BQ | QC | AR | PQ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $x$ | $\frac{2 x}{1+x}$ | $\frac{1-x}{1+x}$ | $\frac{1-x^2}{2}$ | $\frac{1+x^2}{1+x}$ |
| $\tfrac{1}{2}$ | $\tfrac{2}{3}$ | $\tfrac{1}{3}$ | $\tfrac{3}{8}$ | $\tfrac{5}{6}$ |
| $\tfrac{1}{3}$ | $\tfrac{1}{2}$ | $\tfrac{1}{2}$ | $\tfrac{4}{9}$ | $\tfrac{5}{6}$ |
| $\tfrac{2}{3}$ | $\tfrac{4}{5}$ | $\tfrac{1}{5}$ | $\tfrac{5}{18}$ | $\tfrac{13}{15}$ |
| $\tfrac{1}{5}$ | $\tfrac{1}{3}$ | $\tfrac{2}{3}$ | $\tfrac{12}{25}$ | $\tfrac{13}{15}$ |

### A generalization of Haga's theorems {#A_generalization_of_Haga's_theorems}

Haga's theorems are generalized as follows:

$$\frac{BQ}{CQ} = \frac{2 AP}{BP}.$$

Therefore, BQ:CQ=k:1 implies AP:BP=k:2 for a positive real number k. Also,

Each side is 1 $PB=1-AP$ $AP=1/2$ By triangle congruence, AR~PB $BQ=(AP/AR)*(PB)$ $=(2*AP/(1-AP^2))*(1-AP)$ [^o46]

### Doubling the cube {#Doubling_the_cube}

*Image: Delian origami.svg.* Doubling the cube: PB/PA = cube root of 2

The classical problem of [doubling the cube](wikipedia://en/91110) can be solved using origami. This construction is due to Peter Messer:[^o48] A square of paper is first creased into three equal strips as shown in the diagram. Then the bottom edge is positioned so the corner point P is on the top edge and the crease mark on the edge meets the other crease mark Q. The length PB will then be the cube root of 2 times the length of AP.[^o49]

The edge with the crease mark is considered a marked straightedge, something which is not allowed in [compass and straightedge constructions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge_constructions). Using a marked straightedge in this way is called a [neusis construction](wikipedia://en/3752339) in geometry.

### Trisecting an angle {#Trisecting_an_angle}

*Image: Origami Trisection of an angle.svg.* Trisecting the angle CAB

[Angle trisection](wikipedia://en/91111) is another of the classical problems that cannot be solved using a compass and unmarked ruler but can be solved using origami.[^o51] This construction, which was reported in 1980, is due to Hisashi Abe.[^o48][^o53] The angle CAB is trisected by making two folds: PP', parallel to the base, and QQ', halfway in between. Then point P is folded over to lie on line AC and at the same time point A is made to lie on line QQ' at A'. The angle A'AB is one third of the original angle CAB. This is because PAQ, A'AQ and A'AR are three [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330#Congruence_of_triangles) triangles. Aligning the two points on the two lines is another neusis construction as in the solution to doubling the cube.[^o52][^o53]

:::

## Related problems {#Related_problems}

The problem of [rigid origami](wikipedia://en/20332029), treating the folds as hinges joining two flat, rigid surfaces, such as [sheet metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheet_metal), has great practical importance. For example, the [Miura map fold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miura_map_fold) is a rigid fold that has been used to deploy large solar panel arrays for space satellites.

The [napkin folding problem](wikipedia://en/20688523) is the problem of whether a square or rectangle of paper can be folded so the perimeter of the flat figure is greater than that of the original square.

The placement of a point on a curved fold in the pattern may require the solution of elliptic integrals. Curved origami allows the paper to form [developable surfaces](wikipedia://en/2878111) that are not flat.[^o55] [Wet-folding](wikipedia://en/3628411) origami is a technique evolved by Yoshizawa that allows curved folds to create an even greater range of shapes of higher order complexity.

The maximum number of times an incompressible material can be folded has been derived. With each fold a certain amount of paper is lost to potential folding. The [loss function](wikipedia://en/442137) for folding paper in half in a single direction was given to be $L=\tfrac{\pi t}{6} (2^n + 4)(2^n - 1)$, where *L* is the minimum length of the paper (or other material), *t* is the material's thickness, and *n* is the number of folds possible.[^o56] The distances *L* and *t* must be expressed in the same units, such as inches. This result was derived by Britney Gallivan, a high schooler from [California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California), in December 2001. In January 2002, she folded a 4000 ft piece of toilet paper twelve times in the same direction, debunking a long-standing myth that paper cannot be folded in half more than eight times.[^o58]

The [fold-and-cut problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fold-and-cut_problem) asks what shapes can be obtained by folding a piece of paper flat, and making a single straight complete cut. The solution, known as the fold-and-cut theorem, states that any shape with straight sides can be obtained.

A practical problem is how to fold a map so that it may be manipulated with minimal effort or movements. The [Miura fold](wikipedia://en/2894962) is a solution to the problem, and several others have been proposed.[^o59]

## Computational origami {#Computational_origami}

Computational origami is a branch of computer science that is concerned with studying algorithms for solving paper-folding problems. In the early 1990s, origamists participated in a series of origami contests called the [Bug Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bug_Wars) in which artists attempted to out-compete their peers by adding complexity to their origami bugs. Most competitors in the contest belonged to the Origami Detectives, a group of acclaimed Japanese artists.[^o61] [Robert Lang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_J._Lang), a research-scientist from [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University) and the [California Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology), also participated in the contest. The contest helped initialize a collective interest in developing universal models and tools to aid in origami design and foldability.[^o61]

### Research {#Research}

Paper-folding problems are classified as either origami design or origami foldability problems. There are predominantly three current categories of computational origami research: universality results, [efficient decision algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_efficiency), and [computational intractability results](wikipedia://en/7543).[^o63] A universality result defines the bounds of possibility given a particular model of folding. For example, a large enough piece of paper can be folded into any tree-shaped origami base, polygonal silhouette, and polyhedral surface.[^o64] When universality results are not attainable, efficient decision algorithms can be used to test whether an object is foldable in polynomial time.[^o63] Certain paper-folding problems do not have efficient algorithms. Computational intractability results show that there are no such polynomial-time algorithms that currently exist to solve certain folding problems. For example, it is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete) to determine whether a given finite crease pattern folds flat.[^o65] It is an [undecidable problem](wikipedia://en/15631055) to test whether a [recursively](wikipedia://en/4044867)-defined [fractal](wikipedia://en/10913) crease pattern, on a square sheet of origami paper, folds flat.[^o66]

In 2017, Erik Demaine of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Tomohiro Tachi of the University of Tokyo published a new universal algorithm that generates practical paper-folding patterns to produce any 3-D structure. The new algorithm built upon work that they presented in their paper in 1999 that first introduced a universal algorithm for folding origami shapes that guarantees a minimum number of seams. The algorithm will be included in Origamizer, a free software for generating origami crease patterns that was first released by Tachi in 2008.[^o67]

### Software & tools {#Software_&_tools}

*Image: Origami kabuto.gif.* Animation of folds to make a Samurai helmet, also called a kabuto. (On a laptop computer, Julia and GLMakie generated[^o69] the 66 second .mp4 video in 10 seconds.)

There are several software design tools that are used for origami design. Users specify the desired shape or functionality and the software tool constructs the fold pattern and/or 2D or 3D model of the result. Researchers at the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology), [Georgia Tech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgia_Tech), [University of California Irvine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Irvine), [University of Tsukuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tsukuba), and [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo) have developed and posted publicly available tools in computational origami. TreeMaker, ReferenceFinder, OrigamiDraw, and Origamizer are among the tools that have been used in origami design.[^o70]

There are other software solutions associated with building computational origami models using non-paper materials such as Cadnano in [DNA origami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_origami).[^o71]

::: {#o73 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

### Applications {#Applications}

Computational origami has contributed to applications in robotics, engineering, biotechnology & medicine, industrial design.[^o74] Applications for origami have also been developed in the study of programming languages and programming paradigms, particular in the setting of functional programming.[^o75]

Robert Lang participated in a project with researchers at EASi Engineering in Germany to develop automotive airbag folding designs.[^o76] In the mid-2000s, Lang worked with researchers at the [Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Livermore_National_Laboratory) to develop a solution for the [James Webb Space Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope), particularly its large mirrors, to fit into a rocket using principles and algorithms from computational origami.[^o77]

In 2014, researchers at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Harvard University, and the Wyss Institute for Biologically Inspired Engineering published a method for building self-folding machines and credited advances in computational origami for the project's success. Their origami-inspired robot was reported to fold itself in 4 minutes and walk away without human intervention, which demonstrated the potential for autonomous self-controlled assembly in robotics.[^o78]

Other applications include [DNA origami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_origami) and [RNA origami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA_origami), folding of manufacturing instruments, and surgery by tiny origami robots.[^o79]

Applications of computational origami have been featured by various production companies and commercials. Lang famously worked with Toyota Avalon to feature an animated origami sequence, Mitsubishi Endeavor to create a world entirely out of origami figures, and McDonald's to form numerous origami figures from cheeseburger wrappers.[^o80]
