# Hexahedron

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hexahedron"}

A **hexahedron** (: **hexahedra** or **hexahedrons**) or **sexahedron** (: **sexahedra** or **sexahedrons**) is any [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) with six [faces](wikipedia://en/149926). A [cube](wikipedia://en/6285), for example, is a [regular](wikipedia://en/333299) hexahedron with all its faces [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29), and three squares around each [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417).

:::

There are seven [topologically](wikipedia://en/29954) distinct *convex* hexahedra,[^o2] one of which exists in two mirror image forms. Additional non-convex hexahedra exist, with their number depending on how polyhedra are defined. Two polyhedra are "topologically distinct" if they have intrinsically different arrangements of faces and vertices, such that it is impossible to distort one into the other simply by changing the lengths of edges or the angles between edges or faces.

## Convex {#Convex}

### Cuboid {#Cuboid}

A hexahedron that is combinatorially equivalent to a cube may be called a [cuboid](wikipedia://en/156802), although this term is often used more specifically to mean a [rectangular cuboid](wikipedia://en/38848329), a hexahedron with six rectangular sides. A cuboid possess 8 vertices, 6 faces and 12 edges. Different types of cuboids include the ones depicted and linked below.

## Concave {#Concave}

Three further topologically distinct hexahedra can only be realised as *concave* [acoptic polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoptic_polyhedron). These are defined as the surfaces formed by non-crossing [simple polygon](wikipedia://en/1059530) faces, with each edge shared by exactly two faces and each vertex surrounded by a cycle of three or more faces.[^o10]

::: {#o11 .table cue=table}

| Concave |  |  |
|---|---|---|

These cannot be convex because they do not meet the conditions of [Steinitz's theorem](wikipedia://en/19762817), which states that convex polyhedra have vertices and edges that form [3-vertex-connected graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph).[^o12] For other types of polyhedra that allow faces that are not simple polygons, such as the *spherical polyhedra* of Hong and Nagamochi, more possibilities exist.[^o13]
