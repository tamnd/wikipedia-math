# Falling and rising factorials

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Falling and rising factorials"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **falling factorial** (sometimes called the **descending factorial**,[^n1] **falling sequential product**, or **lower factorial**) is defined as the polynomial

$$(x)_n = x^\underline{n} = \overbrace{x(x-1)(x-2)\cdots(x-n+1)}^{n\text{ factors}} = \prod_{k=1}^n(x-k+1) = \prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(x-k).$$

The **rising factorial** (sometimes called the **Pochhammer function**, **Pochhammer polynomial**, **ascending factorial**,[^n1] **rising sequential product**, or **upper factorial**) is defined as

$$x^{(n)} = x^\overline{n} = \overbrace{x(x+1)(x+2)\cdots(x+n-1)}^{n\text{ factors}} = \prod_{k=1}^n(x+k-1) = \prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(x+k).$$

The value of each is taken to be 1 (an [empty product](wikipedia://en/181289)) when $n=0$. These symbols are collectively called **factorial powers**.[^n2]

:::

The **Pochhammer symbol**, introduced by [Leo August Pochhammer](wikipedia://en/3363015), is the notation $(x)_n$, where $n$ is a [non-negative integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative_integer). It may represent *either* the rising or the falling factorial, with different articles and authors using different conventions. Pochhammer himself actually used $(x)_n$ with yet another meaning, namely to denote the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668) $\tbinom{x}{n}$.[^n3]

In this article, the symbol $(x)_n$ is used to represent the falling factorial, and the symbol $x^{(n)}$ is used for the rising factorial. These conventions are used in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170),[^n4] although [Knuth](wikipedia://en/8095)'s underline and [overline](wikipedia://en/4780165) notations $x^\underline{n}$ and $x^\overline{n}$ are increasingly popular.[^n2][^n5] In the theory of [special functions](wikipedia://en/23709110) (in particular the [hypergeometric function](wikipedia://en/2055223)) and in the standard reference work *[Abramowitz and Stegun](wikipedia://en/1206236)*, the Pochhammer symbol $(x)_n$ is used to represent the rising factorial.[^n6][^n7]

When $x$ is a positive integer, the falling factorial $(x)_n$ gives the number of [$n$-permutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-permutation) (sequences of $n$ distinct elements) from an $x$-element set, or equivalently the number of [injective functions](wikipedia://en/45196) from a set of size $n$ to a set of size $x$. The rising factorial $x^{(n)}$ gives the number of [partitions](wikipedia://en/340240) of an $n$-element set into $x$ ordered sequences (possibly empty).

## Examples and combinatorial interpretation {#Examples_and_combinatorial_interpretation}

The first few falling factorials are as follows:

$$\begin{alignedat}{2} (x)_0 &= 1 \\ (x)_1 &= x \\ (x)_2 &= x(x-1) = x^2-x \\ (x)_3 &= x(x-1)(x-2) = x^3-3x^2+2x \\ (x)_4 &= x(x-1)(x-2)(x-3) = x^4-6x^3+11x^2-6x. \end{alignedat}$$

The first few rising factorials are as follows:

$$\begin{alignedat}{2} x^{(0)} &= 1 \\ x^{(1)} &= x \\ x^{(2)} &= x(x+1) =x^2+x \\ x^{(3)} &= x(x+1)(x+2) =x^3+3x^2+2x \\ x^{(4)} &= x(x+1)(x+2)(x+3) =x^4+6x^3+11x^2+6x. \end{alignedat}$$

The coefficients that appear in the expansions are [Stirling numbers of the first kind](wikipedia://en/2229296); see #Connection coefficients and identities below.

When the variable $x$ is a positive integer, the number $(x)_n$ is equal to the number of [$n$-permutations from a set of $x$ items](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-permutation), that is, the number of ways of choosing an ordered list of length $n$ consisting of distinct elements drawn from a collection of size $x$. For example, $(8)_3 = 8\times7\times6 = 336$ is the number of possible different podiums—assignments of gold, silver, and bronze medals—in an eight-person race. On the other hand, $x^{(n)}$ is "the number of ways to arrange $n$ flags on $x$ flagpoles",[^n8] where all flags must be used and each flagpole can have any number of flags. Equivalently, this is the number of ways to partition a set of size $n$ (the flags) into $x$ distinguishable parts (the poles), with a linear order on the elements assigned to each part (the order of the flags on a given pole).

## Properties {#Properties}

Rising and falling factorials are related to one another by

$$\begin{alignedat}{2} {(x)}_n &= {(x-n+1)}^{(n)} &&= (-1)^n (-x)^{(n)},\\[3pt] x^{(n)} &= {(x+n-1)}_{n} &&= (-1)^n (-x)_n. \end{alignedat}$$

Rising and falling factorials of integers are directly related to the ordinary [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606):

$$n! = 1^{(n)} = (n)_n,\qquad (m)_n = \frac{m!}{(m-n)!},\qquad m^{(n)} = \frac{(m+n-1)!}{(m-1)!}.$$

A useful identity for the sums of falling factorials is[^o15]

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(k)_m = \frac{(n)_{m+1}}{m+1}.$$

Rising factorials of half-integers are directly related to the [double factorial](wikipedia://en/507208) $m!! = m(m-2)(m-4)\cdots$ by

$$\begin{aligned} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{(n)} = \frac{(2n-1)!!}{2^n},\quad \left(\frac{2m+1}{2}\right)^{(n)} = \frac{(2(n+m)-1)!!}{2^n(2m-1)!!}. \end{aligned}$$

Rising and falling factorials can be used to express [binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/4668):

$$\frac{(x)_n}{n!} = \binom{x}{n},\qquad \frac{x^{(n)}}{n!} = \binom{x+n-1}{n}.$$

Thus many identities involving binomial coefficients carry over to rising and falling factorials.

Rising and falling factorials are well defined in any [unital ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_ring), and therefore $x$ can be taken to be, for example, a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826), or a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) with complex coefficients, or any [complex-valued function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex-valued_function).

### Calculus {#Calculus}

Falling factorials appear in multiple [differentiation](wikipedia://en/7921) of simple power functions:

$$\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^n x^a = (a)_n \cdot x^{a-n}.$$

The rising factorial is also integral to the definition of the [hypergeometric function](wikipedia://en/2055223), which is defined for $|z| < 1$ by the [power series](wikipedia://en/59958)

$${}_2F_1(a,b;c;z) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{a^{(n)} b^{(n)}}{c^{(n)}} \frac{z^n}{n!}$$

provided that $c$ is not zero or a negative integer. Note, however, that the hypergeometric function literature typically uses the notation $(a)_n$ for rising factorials.

## Connection coefficients and identities {#Connection_coefficients_and_identities}

Falling and rising factorials are closely related to [Stirling numbers](wikipedia://en/95465). Expanding the product reveals [Stirling numbers of the first kind](wikipedia://en/2229296):

$$(x)_n = \sum_{k=0}^n s(n,k) x^k = \sum_{k=0}^n \begin{bmatrix}n \\ k \end{bmatrix} (-1)^{n-k}x^k, \qquad x^{(n)} = \sum_{k=0}^n \begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix} x^k.$$

The inverse relations use [Stirling numbers of the second kind](wikipedia://en/2229292):

$$x^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \begin{Bmatrix} n \\ k \end{Bmatrix} (x)_{k} = \sum_{k=0}^n \begin{Bmatrix} n \\ k \end{Bmatrix} (-1)^{n-k} x^{(k)}.$$

The falling and rising factorials are related to one another through the [Lah numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lah_numbers) $L(n, k) = \textstyle\binom{n-1}{k-1} \frac{n!}{k!}$ by the relations[^o18]

$$x^{(n)} = \sum_{k=0}^n L(n,k) (x)_k$$

and

$$(x)_n = \sum_{k=0}^n L(n,k) (-1)^{n-k} x^{(k)}.$$

Since falling factorials are a basis for the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065), their product can be expressed as a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of falling factorials:[^n9]

$$(x)_m (x)_n = \sum_{k=0}^m \binom{m}{k} \binom{n}{k} k! \cdot (x)_{m+n-k}.$$

The coefficients $\tbinom{m}{k} \tbinom{n}{k} k!$ are called *connection coefficients*, and have a combinatorial interpretation as the number of ways to identify (or "glue together") $k$ elements each from a set of size $m$ and a set of size $n$.

There is also a connection formula for the ratio of two rising factorials given by

$$\frac{x^{(n)}}{x^{(i)}} = (x+i)^{(n-i)} ,\quad \text{for }n \geq i .$$

Additionally, we can expand generalized exponent laws and negative rising and falling powers through the following identities:[^o20]p. 52

$$\begin{aligned} (x)_{m+n} & = (x)_m (x-m)_n = (x)_n (x-n)_m \\[6pt] x^{(m+n)} & = x^{(m)} (x+m)^{(n)} = x^{(n)} (x+n)^{(m)} \\[6pt] x^{(-n)} & = \frac{\Gamma(x-n)}{\Gamma(x)} = \frac{(x-n-1)!}{(x-1)!} = \frac{1}{(x-n)^{(n)}} = \frac{1}{(x-1)_n} = \frac{1}{(x-1)(x-2) \cdots (x-n)} \\[6pt] (x)_{-n} & = \frac{\Gamma(x+1)}{\Gamma(x+n+1)} = \frac{x!}{(x+n)!} = \frac{1}{(x+n)_n} = \frac{1}{(x+1)^{(n)}} = \frac{1}{(x+1)(x+2) \cdots (x+n)} \end{aligned}$$

Finally, [duplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duplication_formula) and [multiplication formulas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplication_formula) for the falling and rising factorials provide the next relations:

$$\begin{aligned} (x)_{k+mn} &= x^{(k)} m^{mn} \prod_{j=0}^{m-1} \left(\frac{x-k-j}{m}\right)_{n}& \text{ for } m &\in \mathbb{N} \\[6pt] x^{(k+mn)} &= x^{(k)} m^{mn} \prod_{j=0}^{m-1} \left(\frac{x+k+j}{m}\right)^{(n)}& \text{ for } m &\in \mathbb{N} \\[6pt] (ax+b)^{(n)} &= x^n \prod_{j=0}^{n-1} \left(a+\frac{b+j}{x}\right)& \text{ for } x &\neq 0 \\[6pt] (2x)^{(2n)} &= 2^{2n} x^{(n)} \left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{(n)} . \end{aligned}$$

## Relation to umbral calculus {#Relation_to_umbral_calculus}

The falling factorial occurs in a formula which represents [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) using the forward [difference operator](wikipedia://en/39147) $\Delta f(x) := f(x+1) - f(x),$ which is an exact analogue to [Taylor's theorem](wikipedia://en/51714): compare the [series expansion](wikipedia://en/1575813) from [umbral calculus](wikipedia://en/214124)

$$f(t) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{1}{n!} \Delta_x^n f(x)\,\Big\vert_{x = 0}(t)_n$$

with the corresponding series from [differential calculus](wikipedia://en/50416)

$$f(t) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{1}{n!} \left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^n f(x)\,\Big\vert_{x = 0}t^n.$$

In this formula, the falling factorial $(x)_n$ in the calculus of finite differences plays the role of $x^n$ in differential calculus. For another example, note the similarity between $\Delta (x)_n = n(x)_{n-1}$ and

$$\frac{d}{dx}x^n = n x^{n-1}.$$

A corresponding relation holds for the rising factorial and the [backward difference](wikipedia://en/39147#Basic_types) operator.

The study of analogies of this type is known as [umbral calculus](wikipedia://en/214124). A general theory covering such relations, including the falling and rising factorial functions, is given by the theory of [polynomial sequences of binomial type](wikipedia://en/200077) and [Sheffer sequences](wikipedia://en/218596). Falling and rising factorials are Sheffer sequences of binomial type, as shown by the relations:

$$\ \begin{aligned} (a + b)_n &= \sum_{j=0}^n \binom{n}{j}(a)_{n-j}(b)_{j}\\[6pt] (a + b)^{(n)} &= \sum_{j=0}^n\binom{n}{j} a^{(n-j)} b^{(j)} \end{aligned}\$$

where the coefficients are the same as those in the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677).

Similarly, the [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) of Pochhammer polynomials then amounts to the umbral exponential,

$$\ \sum_{n=0}^\infty (x)_n \frac{t^n}{n!}=(1+t)^x,$$

since

$$\ \operatorname\Delta_x (1+t)^x=t\cdot(1+t)^x ~.$$

## Alternative notations {#Alternative_notations}

An alternative notation for the rising factorial

$$x^\overline{m} \equiv (x)_{+m} \equiv (x)_m = \underbrace{x(x+1)\ldots(x+m-1)}_{m \text{ factors}} \quad \text{for integer } m \geq 0$$

and for the falling factorial

$$x^\underline{m} \equiv (x)_{-m} = \overbrace{x(x-1)\ldots(x-m+1)}^{m \text{ factors}} \quad \text{for integer } m \geq 0$$

goes back to A. Capelli (1893) and L. Toscano (1939), respectively.[^n2] Graham, Knuth, and Patashnik[^o20]p. 47, 48 propose to pronounce these expressions as "$x$ to the $m$ rising" and "$x$ to the $m$ falling", respectively.

An alternative notation for the rising factorial $x^{(n)}$ is the less common $(x)_n^{+}~.$ When $(x)_n^{+}$ is used to denote the rising factorial, the notation $(x)_n^{-}$ is typically used for the ordinary falling factorial, to avoid confusion.[^n3]

:::

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The Pochhammer symbol has a generalized version called the [generalized Pochhammer symbol](wikipedia://en/8664662), used in multivariate [analysis](wikipedia://en/48396). There is also a [$q$-analogue](wikipedia://en/1619396), the [$q$-Pochhammer symbol](wikipedia://en/11149717).

For any fixed [arithmetic function](wikipedia://en/3170) $f : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{C}$ and parameters $x$ and $t$, related generalized factorial products of the form

$$(x)_{n,f,t} := \prod_{k=0}^{n-1} \left(x+\frac{f(k)}{t^k}\right)$$

may be studied from the point of view of the classes of generalized [Stirling numbers of the first kind](wikipedia://en/2229296) defined by the following coefficients of the powers of $x$ in the expansions of $(x)_{n,f,t}$ and then by the next corresponding triangular recurrence relation:

$$\begin{aligned} \left[\begin{matrix} n \\ k \end{matrix} \right]_{f,t} & = \left[x^{k-1}\right] (x)_{n,f,t} \\ & = f(n-1) t^{1-n} \left[\begin{matrix} n-1 \\ k \end{matrix} \right]_{f,t} + \left[\begin{matrix} n-1 \\ k-1 \end{matrix} \right]_{f,t} + \delta_{n,0} \delta_{k,0}. \end{aligned}$$

These coefficients satisfy a number of analogous properties to those for the Stirling numbers of the first kind as well as recurrence relations and functional equations related to $f$-harmonic numbers:[^n10]

$$F_n^{(r)}(t) := \sum_{k \leq n} \frac{ t^k }{ f(k)^r }.$$

:::
