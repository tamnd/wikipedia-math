# Dissipative system

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dissipative system"}

A **dissipative system** is a thermodynamically [open system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_system_%28systems_theory%29) which is operating out of, and often far from, [thermodynamic equilibrium](wikipedia://en/265823) in an environment with which it exchanges [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) and [matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter). A [tornado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tornado) may be thought of as a dissipative system. Dissipative systems stand in contrast to [conservative systems](wikipedia://en/8918323).

:::

A **dissipative structure** is a dissipative system that has a dynamical regime that is in some sense in a reproducible [steady state](wikipedia://en/1326107). This reproducible steady state may be reached by natural evolution of the system, by artifice, or by a combination of these two.

## Overview {#Overview}

A [dissipative](wikipedia://en/164572) structure is characterized by the spontaneous appearance of symmetry breaking ([anisotropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anisotropy)) and the formation of complex, sometimes [chaotic](wikipedia://en/6295), structures where interacting particles exhibit long range correlations. Examples in everyday life include [convection](wikipedia://en/47526), [turbulent flow](wikipedia://en/154664), [cyclones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclone), [hurricanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_cyclone) and [living organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life). Less common examples include [lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser), [Bénard cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bénard_cells), [droplet cluster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Droplet_cluster), and the [Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction](wikipedia://en/186262).[^o3]

One way of mathematically modeling a dissipative system is given in the article on *[wandering sets](wikipedia://en/2870010)*: it involves the action of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) on a [measurable set](wikipedia://en/19873).

Dissipative systems can also be used as a tool to study economic systems and [complex systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_systems).[^o4] For example, a dissipative system involving [self-assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-assembly) of nanowires has been used as a model to understand the relationship between entropy generation and the robustness of biological systems.[^o5]

The [Hopf decomposition](wikipedia://en/53972682) states that [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087) can be decomposed into a conservative and a dissipative part; more precisely, it states that every [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270) with a [non-singular transformation](wikipedia://en/8918323) can be decomposed into an invariant [conservative set](wikipedia://en/8918323) and an invariant dissipative set.

## Dissipative structures in thermodynamics {#Dissipative_structures_in_thermodynamics}

Russian-Belgian physical chemist [Ilya Prigogine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilya_Prigogine), who coined the term *dissipative structure,* received the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry) in 1977 for his pioneering work on these structures, which have dynamical regimes that can be regarded as thermodynamic steady states, and sometimes at least can be described by suitable [extremal principles in non-equilibrium thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extremal_principles_in_non-equilibrium_thermodynamics).

In his Nobel lecture,[^o7] Prigogine explains how thermodynamic systems far from equilibrium can have drastically different behavior from systems close to equilibrium. Near equilibrium, the *local equilibrium* hypothesis applies and typical thermodynamic quantities such as free energy and entropy can be defined locally. One can assume linear relations between the (generalized) flux and forces of the system. Two celebrated results from linear thermodynamics are the [Onsager reciprocal relations](wikipedia://en/466164) and the principle of minimum [entropy production](wikipedia://en/35482259).[^o8] After efforts to extend such results to systems far from equilibrium, it was found that they do not hold in this regime and opposite results were obtained.

One way to rigorously analyze such systems is by studying the stability of the system far from equilibrium. Close to equilibrium, one can show the existence of a [Lyapunov function](wikipedia://en/352267) which ensures that the entropy tends to a stable maximum. Fluctuations are damped in the neighborhood of the fixed point and a macroscopic description suffices. However, far from equilibrium stability is no longer a universal property and can be broken. In chemical systems, this occurs with the presence of [autocatalytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocatalytic) reactions, such as in the example of the [Brusselator](wikipedia://en/27379198). If the system is driven beyond a certain threshold, oscillations are no longer damped out, but may be amplified. Mathematically, this corresponds to a [Hopf bifurcation](wikipedia://en/3626981) where increasing one of the parameters beyond a certain value leads to [limit cycle](wikipedia://en/452950) behavior. If spatial effects are taken into account through a [reaction–diffusion equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction–diffusion_equation), long-range correlations and spatially ordered patterns arise,[^o9] such as in the case of the [Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction](wikipedia://en/186262). Systems with such dynamic states of matter that arise as the result of irreversible processes are dissipative structures.

Recent research has seen reconsideration of Prigogine's ideas of dissipative structures in relation to biological systems.[^o10]

## Dissipative systems in control theory {#Dissipative_systems_in_control_theory}

[Willems](wikipedia://en/40331164) first introduced the concept of dissipativity in systems theory[^o12] to describe dynamical systems by input-output properties. Considering a dynamical system described by its state $x(t)$, its input $u(t)$ and its output $y(t)$, the input-output correlation is given a supply rate $w(u(t),y(t))$. A system is said to be dissipative with respect to a supply rate if there exists a continuously differentiable storage function $V(x(t))$ such that $V(0)=0$, $V(x(t))\ge 0$ and

$\dot{V}(x(t)) \le w(u(t),y(t))$.[^o13]

As a special case of dissipativity, a system is said to be passive if the above dissipativity inequality holds with respect to the passivity supply rate $w(u(t),y(t)) = u(t)^Ty(t)$.

The physical interpretation is that $V(x)$ is the energy stored in the system, whereas $w(u(t),y(t))$ is the energy that is supplied to the system.

This notion has a strong connection with [Lyapunov stability](wikipedia://en/363360), where the storage functions may play, under certain conditions of controllability and observability of the dynamical system, the role of Lyapunov functions.

Roughly speaking, dissipativity theory is useful for the design of feedback control laws for linear and nonlinear systems. Dissipative systems theory has been discussed by [V. M. Popov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasile_M._Popov), [J. C. Willems](wikipedia://en/40331164), D. J. Hill, and P. Moylan. In the case of linear invariant systems, this is known as positive real transfer functions, and a fundamental tool is the so-called [Kalman–Yakubovich–Popov lemma](wikipedia://en/6267642) which relates the state space and the frequency domain properties of positive real systems.[^o14] Dissipative systems are still an active field of research in systems and control, due to their important applications.

## Quantum dissipative systems {#Quantum_dissipative_systems}

As [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), and any classical [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087), relies heavily on [Hamiltonian mechanics](wikipedia://en/198319) for which [time is reversible](wikipedia://en/1937658), these approximations are not intrinsically able to describe dissipative systems. It has been proposed that in principle, one can couple weakly the system – say, an oscillator – to a bath, i.e., an assembly of many oscillators in thermal equilibrium with a broad band spectrum, and trace (average) over the bath. This yields a [master equation](wikipedia://en/840106) which is a special case of a more general setting called the [Lindblad equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindblad_equation) that is the quantum equivalent of the classical [Liouville equation](wikipedia://en/312301). The well-known form of this equation and its quantum counterpart takes time as a reversible variable over which to integrate, but the very foundations of dissipative structures imposes an [irreversible](wikipedia://en/424440) and constructive role for time.

Recent research has seen the quantum extension of [Jeremy England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_England)'s theory of dissipative adaptation[^o10] (which generalizes Prigogine's ideas of dissipative structures to far-from-equilibrium statistical mechanics, as stated above). The notion of quantum dissipative adaptation[^o16][^o17] has proven fruitful in a number of contexts. In living matter, we find both the notions of self-organization (repeating patterns) and of diversification and open-endedness (novel patterns). Quantum dissipative adaptation has been shown to be able to reconcile these two aspects, as it takes place in a minimal model of self-replication.[^o18] Beyond the emergence of self-organized states, the concept of quantum dissipative adaptation has led to the findings of emergent functionalities (such as energy-seeking and energy-avoiding behaviors) in some driven-dissipative quantum systems.[^o19] Photosynthesis has also inspired the search for some kind of quantum dissipative adaptation, as it raises the question of how relevant are quantum aspects to the absorption of photons by light-harvesting complexes. It turns out that the presence of a delocalized excitation in matter can cost some additional amount of energy from the incoming photon, which leads to a more subtle generalization of the standard quantum dissipative adaptation: instead of the absorbed work being directly proportional to the probability of excitation transfer from one site to another (as expected from the standard version), the absorbed work can, under certain circumstances, be partly used by the system in the transfer of excitation, while another part is dissipated during the buildup of a transient quantum coherence.[^o20]

## Applications on dissipative systems of dissipative structure concept {#Applications_on_dissipative_systems_of_dissipative_structure_concept}

The framework of dissipative structures as a mechanism to understand the behavior of systems in constant interexchange of energy has been successfully applied on different science fields and applications, as in optics,[^o22][^o23] population dynamics and growth[^o24][^o25][^o26] and chemomechanical structures.[^o27][^o28][^o29]
