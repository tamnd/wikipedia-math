# Product (category theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Product"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), the **product** of two (or more) [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) in a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) is a notion designed to capture the essence behind constructions in other areas of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) such as the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of [sets](wikipedia://en/26691), the [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887) of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) or [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), and the [product](wikipedia://en/24910) of [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450). Essentially, the product of a [family](wikipedia://en/23624339) of objects is the "most general" object which admits a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) to each of the given objects.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

::: {#o5 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Binary product (category theory)"}

### Product of two objects {#Product_of_two_objects}

Fix a category $C.$ Let $X_1$ and $X_2$ be objects of $C.$ A product of $X_1$ and $X_2$ is an object $X,$ typically denoted $X_1 \times X_2,$ equipped with a pair of morphisms $\pi_1 : X \to X_1,$ $\pi_2 : X \to X_2$ satisfying the following [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245):

- For every object $Y$ and every pair of morphisms $f_1 : Y \to X_1,$ $f_2 : Y \to X_2,$ there exists a unique morphism $f : Y \to X_1 \times X_2$ such that the following diagram [commutes](wikipedia://en/138484):

*Image: CategoricalProduct-03.svg.* Universal property of the product

Whether a product exists may depend on $C$ or on $X_1$ and $X_2.$ If it does exist, it is unique [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [canonical isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_isomorphism), because of the universal property, so one may speak of *the* product. This has the following meaning: if $X', \pi_1', \pi_2'$ is another product, there exists a unique isomorphism $h : X' \to X_1 \times X_2$ such that $\pi_1' = \pi_1 \circ h$ and $\pi_2' = \pi_2 \circ h$.

The morphisms $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$ are called the **canonical projections** or **projection morphisms**; the letter $\pi$ alliterates with projection. Given $Y$ and $f_1,$ $f_2,$ the unique morphism $f$ is called the **product of morphisms** $f_1$ and $f_2$ and may be denoted $\langle f_1, f_2 \rangle$, $f_1 \times f_2$, or $f_1 \otimes f_2$.

:::

### Product of an arbitrary family {#Product_of_an_arbitrary_family}

Instead of two objects, we can start with an arbitrary family of objects [indexed](wikipedia://en/393055) by a set $I.$

Given a family $\left(X_i\right)_{i \in I}$ of objects, a **product** of the family is an object $X$ equipped with morphisms $\pi_i : X \to X_i,$ satisfying the following universal property:

- For every object $Y$ and every $I$-indexed family of morphisms $f_i : Y \to X_i,$ there exists a unique morphism $f : Y \to X$ such that the following diagrams commute for all $i \in I:$

*Image: Cat product.svg.* Universal product of the product

The product is denoted $\prod_{i \in I} X_i.$ If $I = \{1, \ldots, n\},$ then it is denoted $X_1 \times \cdots \times X_n$ and the product of morphisms is denoted $\langle f_1, \ldots, f_n \rangle.$

### Equational definition {#Equational_definition}

Alternatively, the product may be defined through equations. So, for example, for the binary product:

- Existence of $f$ is guaranteed by existence of the operation $\langle \cdot,\cdot \rangle.$
- Commutativity of the diagrams above is guaranteed by the equality: for all $f_1, f_2$ and all $i \in \{1, 2\},$ $\pi_i \circ \left\langle f_1, f_2 \right\rangle = f_i$
- Uniqueness of $f$ is guaranteed by the equality: for all $g : Y \to X_1 \times X_2,$ $\left\langle \pi_1 \circ g, \pi_2 \circ g \right\rangle = g.$[^o8]

### As a limit {#As_a_limit}

The product is a special case of a [limit](wikipedia://en/39248). This may be seen by using a [discrete category](wikipedia://en/808519) (a family of objects without any morphisms, other than their identity morphisms) as the [diagram](wikipedia://en/7651625) required for the definition of the limit. The discrete objects will serve as the index of the components and projections. If we regard this diagram as a functor, it is a functor from the index set $I$ considered as a discrete category. The definition of the product then coincides with the definition of the limit, $\{f\}_i$ being a [cone](wikipedia://en/7533645) and projections being the limit (limiting cone).

### Universal property {#Universal_property}

Just as the limit is a special case of the [universal construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_construction), so is the product. Starting with the definition given for the [universal property of limits](wikipedia://en/39248#Universal_property), take $\mathbf{J}$ as the discrete category with two objects, so that $\mathbf{C}^{\mathbf{J}}$ is simply the [product category](wikipedia://en/4587226) $\mathbf{C} \times \mathbf{C}.$ The [diagonal functor](wikipedia://en/4606683) $\Delta : \mathbf{C} \to \mathbf{C} \times \mathbf{C}$ assigns to each object $X$ the [ordered pair](wikipedia://en/22362) $(X, X)$ and to each morphism $f$ the pair $(f, f).$ The product $X_1 \times X_2$ in $C$ is given by a [universal morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_morphism) from the functor $\Delta$ to the object $\left(X_1, X_2\right)$ in $\mathbf{C} \times \mathbf{C}.$ This universal morphism consists of an object $X$ of $C$ and a morphism $(X, X) \to \left(X_1, X_2\right)$ which contains projections.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

In the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829), the product (in the category theoretic sense) is the Cartesian product. Given a family of sets $X_i$ the product is defined as

$$\prod_{i \in I} X_i := \left\{\left(x_i\right)_{i \in I} : x_i \in X_i \text{ for all } i \in I\right\}$$

with the canonical projections

$$\pi_j : \prod_{i \in I} X_i \to X_j, \quad \pi_j\left(\left(x_i\right)_{i \in I}\right) := x_j.$$

Given any set $Y$ with a family of functions $f_i : Y \to X_i,$ the universal arrow $f : Y \to \prod_{i \in I} X_i$ is defined by $f(y) := \left(f_i(y)\right)_{i \in I}.$

Other examples:

- In the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051), the product is the space whose underlying set is the Cartesian product and which carries the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910). The product topology is the [coarsest topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coarsest_topology) for which all the projections are [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29).
- In the [category of modules](wikipedia://en/11231812) over some ring $R,$ the product is the Cartesian product with addition defined componentwise and distributive multiplication.
- In the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092), the product is the [direct product of groups](wikipedia://en/3706015) given by the Cartesian product with multiplication defined componentwise.
- In the [category of graphs](wikipedia://en/676328#Structure_of_homomorphisms), the product is the [tensor product of graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_product_of_graphs).
- In the [category of relations](wikipedia://en/5930730), the product is given by the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946). (This may come as a bit of a surprise given that the category of sets is a [subcategory](wikipedia://en/310959) of the category of relations.)
- In the category of [algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/248808), the product is given by the [Segre embedding](wikipedia://en/1623980).
- In the category of [semi-abelian monoids](wikipedia://en/10739141), the product is given by the [history monoid](wikipedia://en/10833335).
- In the category of [Banach spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banach_spaces) and [short maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_map), the product carries the [$l^{\infty}$](wikipedia://en/46395425) norm.[^o13]
- A [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) can be treated as a category, using the order relation as the morphisms. In this case the products and [coproducts](wikipedia://en/310889) correspond to greatest lower bounds ([meets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meet_%28mathematics%29)) and least upper bounds ([joins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Join_%28mathematics%29)).

:::

## Discussion {#Discussion}

An example in which the product does not exist: In the category of fields, the product $\mathbb{Q} \times F_p$ does not exist, since there is no field with homomorphisms to both $\mathbb{Q}$ and $F_p.$

Another example: An [empty product](wikipedia://en/181289) (that is, $I$ is the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566)) is the same as a [terminal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_object), and some categories, such as the category of [infinite groups](wikipedia://en/4587030), do not have a terminal object: given any infinite group $G$ there are infinitely many morphisms $\mathbb{Z} \to G,$ so $G$ cannot be terminal.

If $I$ is a set such that all products for families indexed with $I$ exist, then one can treat each product as a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) $\mathbf{C}^I \to \mathbf{C}.$[^o15] How this functor maps objects is obvious. Mapping of morphisms is subtle, because the product of morphisms defined above does not fit. First, consider the binary product functor, which is a [bifunctor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bifunctor). For $f_1 : X_1 \to Y_1, f_2 : X_2 \to Y_2$ we should find a morphism $X_1 \times X_2 \to Y_1 \times Y_2.$ We choose $\left\langle f_1 \circ \pi_1, f_2 \circ \pi_2 \right\rangle.$ This operation on morphisms is called **Cartesian product of morphisms**.[^o16] Second, consider the general product functor. For families $\left\{X\right\}_i, \left\{Y\right\}_i, f_i : X_i \to Y_i$ we should find a morphism $\prod_{i \in I} X_i \to \prod_{i \in I} Y_i.$ We choose the product of morphisms $\left\{f_i \circ \pi_i\right\}_i.$

A category where every finite set of objects has a product is sometimes called a **Cartesian category**[^o16] (although some authors use this phrase to mean "a category with all finite limits").

The product is [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative). Suppose $C$ is a Cartesian category, product functors have been chosen as above, and $1$ denotes a terminal object of $C.$ We then have [natural isomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_isomorphism)

$$X \times (Y \times Z) \simeq (X\times Y) \times Z \simeq X \times Y \times Z,$$

$$X \times 1 \simeq 1 \times X \simeq X,$$

$$X \times Y \simeq Y \times X.$$

These properties are formally similar to those of a commutative [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652); a Cartesian category with its finite products is an example of a [symmetric monoidal category](wikipedia://en/3865249).

## Distributivity {#Distributivity}

For any objects $X, Y, \text{ and } Z$ of a category with finite products and coproducts, there is a [canonical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mathematical_jargon#canonical) morphism $X \times Y + X \times Z \to X \times (Y + Z),$ where the plus sign here denotes the [coproduct](wikipedia://en/310889). To see this, note that the universal property of the coproduct $X \times Y + X \times Z$ guarantees the existence of unique arrows filling out the following diagram (the induced arrows are dashed):

*Image: Product-Coproduct Distributivity SVG.svg.*

The universal property of the product $X \times (Y + Z)$ then guarantees a unique morphism $X \times Y + X \times Z \to X \times (Y + Z)$ induced by the dashed arrows in the above diagram. A [distributive category](wikipedia://en/6053993) is one in which this morphism is actually an isomorphism. Thus in a distributive category, there is the canonical isomorphism

$$X\times (Y + Z)\simeq (X\times Y) + (X \times Z).$$
