# Abel's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Abel's theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Abel's theorem** for [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) relates a [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602) of a power series to the sum of its [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203). It is named after Norwegian mathematician [Niels Henrik Abel](wikipedia://en/21573), who proved it in 1826.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Theorem"}

## Theorem {#Theorem}

Let the [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448)

$$G (x) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k x^k$$

be a power series with [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) coefficients $a_k$. Suppose that the series

$$\sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k$$

[converges](wikipedia://en/1743431). Then $G(x)$ is [continuous from the left](wikipedia://en/6122#Directional_and_semi-continuity) at $x = 1,$ that is,

$$\lim_{x\to 1^-} G(x) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k.$$

The same [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) holds for [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) power series

$$G(z) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k z^k,$$

provided that $z \to 1$ entirely within a single *Stolz sector*, that is, a region of the [open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_disk) [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738) where

$$|1-z| \leq M(1-|z|)$$

for some fixed finite $M > 1$. Without this restriction, the limit may fail to exist: for example, the power series

$$\sum_{n>0} \frac{z^{3^n}-z^{2\cdot 3^n}} n$$

converges to $0$ at $z = 1,$ but is [unbounded](wikipedia://en/311509) near any other point of the form $e^{\pi i/3^n},$ so the value at $z = 1$ is not the limit as $z$ tends to 1 in the whole open disk.

The convergence of $\sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k$ implies that the [radius of convergence](wikipedia://en/61476) of the power series $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a_k z^k$ is at least 1, ensuring convergence for $|z|<1$.

Also, by the [uniform limit theorem](wikipedia://en/29230765), $G(z)$ is continuous on the real [closed interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_interval) $[0, t]$ for $t < 1,$ by virtue of the [uniform convergence](wikipedia://en/50652) of the series on [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) subsets of the disk of convergence (by the [Weierstrass M-test](wikipedia://en/444456)). Abel's theorem allows us to say more, namely that the restriction of $G(z)$ to $[0, 1]$ is continuous.

### Stolz sector {#Stolz_sector}

*Image: Stolz sector plot.svg.* 20 Stolz sectors, for $M$ ranging from 1.01 to 10. The red lines are the tangents to the cone at the right end.

The Stolz sector $|1-z|\leq M(1-|z|)$ has explicit equation

$$y^2 = -\frac{M^4 (x^2 - 1) - 2 M^2 ((x - 1) x + 1) + 2 \sqrt{M^4 (-2 M^2 (x - 1) + 2 x - 1)} + (x - 1)^2}{(M^2 - 1)^2}$$

and is plotted on the right for various values.

The left end of the sector is $x = \frac{1-M}{1+M}$, and the right end is $x=1$. On the right end, it becomes a cone with [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) $2\theta$ where $\cos\theta = \frac{1}{M}$.

:::

## Remarks {#Remarks}

As an immediate consequence of this theorem, if $z$ is any nonzero complex number for which the series

$$\sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k z^k$$

converges, then it follows that

$$\lim_{t\to 1^{-}} G(tz) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty a_kz^k$$

in which the limit is taken [from below](wikipedia://en/379840).

The theorem can also be generalized to account for sums which diverge to infinity; see below. If

$$\sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k = \infty$$

then

$$\lim_{x \to 1^{-}} G(x) \to \infty.$$

However, if the series is only known to be divergent, but for reasons other than diverging to infinity, then the claim of the theorem may fail: take, for example, the power series for

$$\frac{1}{1+z}.$$

At $z = 1$ the series is equal to $1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + \cdots,$ but $\tfrac{1}{1+1} = \tfrac{1}{2}.$

We also remark the theorem holds for radii of convergence other than $R = 1$: let

$$G(x) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty a_kx^k$$

be a power series with radius of convergence $R > 0,$ and suppose the series converges at $x = R.$ Then $G(x)$ is continuous from the left at $x = R,$ that is,

$$\lim_{x\to R^-}G(x) = G(R).$$

## Applications {#Applications}

Abel's theorem allows us to evaluate many series in closed form if we can first prove that they converge. For example, consider the sequence

$$a_k = \frac{(-1)^k}{k+1}.$$

The sum $\sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k$ converges by the [alternating series test](wikipedia://en/1598759), so we can apply Abel's theorem to the corresponding [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) $G_a(z)$. We obtain

$$G_a(z) = \frac{\ln(1+z)}{z}, \qquad 0 < z < 1,$$

by integrating the uniformly convergent geometric power series term by term on $[-z, 0]$; thus the series

$$\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k}{k+1}$$

converges to $\lim \limits_{z \to 1^-} G_a(z) = \ln 2$ by Abel's theorem. Similarly,

$$\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1}$$

converges to $\arctan 1 = \tfrac{\pi}{4}.$

$G_a(z)$ is called the [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) of the sequence $a.$ Abel's theorem is frequently useful in dealing with generating functions of real-valued and non-negative [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838), such as [probability-generating functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability-generating_function). In particular, it is useful in the theory of [Galton&ndash;Watson processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galton&ndash;Watson_process).

:::

## Outline of proof {#Outline_of_proof}

Source:[^o10]

Let $S = \sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k$ and $s_n= \left({\sum_{k=0}^n a_k}\right) - S\!.$ Then substituting $a_k=s_k-s_{k-1}$ and performing a simple manipulation of the series ([summation by parts](wikipedia://en/246188)) results in

$$G_a(z) = S + (1-z)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} s_k z^k.$$

Given $\varepsilon > 0,$ pick $n$ large enough so that $|s_k| < \varepsilon$ for all $k \geq n.$ We have

$$\left|(1-z)\sum_{k=n}^\infty s_kz^k \right| \leq \varepsilon |1-z|\sum_{k=n}^\infty |z|^k = \varepsilon|1-z|\frac{|z|^n}{1-|z|} < \varepsilon M$$

when $z$ lies within the given Stolz angle. Whenever $z$ is sufficiently close to $1$ we have

$$\left|(1-z)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} s_kz^k \right| < \varepsilon,$$

so that $\left|G_a(z) - S\right| < (M+1) \varepsilon$ when $z$ is both sufficiently close to $1$ and within the Stolz angle.

## Divergent case {#Divergent_case}

To prove the case for $\sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k = +\infty$, let $x \in (0,1)$ and $s_n = \sum_{k=0}^n a_k$. Since $(s_k)_k$ diverges to $+\infty$, we can find, for any $M \in \mathbb{R}_+$, a $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $s_k > 3M$ for all $k \geq N$. We write, for $n > N$:

$$\sum_{k=0}^n a_k x^k = \left[{(1-x) \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} s_k x^k}\right] + \left\{{ (1-x) \left[{ \sum_{k=N}^{n-1} s_k x^k }\right] + s_n x^n }\right\}$$

Since the first term on the right hand side vanishes as $x \to 1$, we can find an $x_1 \in (0, 1)$ such that it exceeds $-M$ whenever $x_1 < x < 1$. The second term may be estimated by:

$$(1-x) \left[{ \sum_{k=N}^{n-1} s_k x^k }\right] + s_n x^n > 3M \left\{{ (1-x) \left[{\sum_{k=N}^{n-1} x^k}\right] + x^n}\right\} = 3M x^N.$$

Hence, if we let $x_2 = (2/3)^{1/N}$, then for $x_2 < x < 1$ this exceeds $2M$. Combining, we get, for any $n>N$ and $\max(x_1, x_2) < x < 1$:

$$\sum_{k=0}^n a_k x^k > M.$$

This establishes:

$$\lim_{x \to 1^{-}} \liminf_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=0}^n a_k x^k = +\infty$$

In the absence of additional assumptions, the series $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a_k x^k$ might not converge when $|x|<1$, hence the use of the [limit inferior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_inferior).

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

[Converses](wikipedia://en/155941) to a theorem like Abel's are called [Tauberian theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tauberian_theorems): There is no exact converse, but results conditional on some hypothesis. The field of [divergent series](wikipedia://en/876428), and their summation methods, contains many theorems *of abelian type* and *of tauberian type*.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

In the real case, the functions $x^k$ may be replaced by [bounded](wikipedia://en/311509) non-negative functions $u_k(x)$ monotonically decreasing with $k$ (i.e., $u_k(x) \ge u_{k+1}(x)$) such that $\lim_{x \to 1^{-}} u_k(x) = 1$ for all $k$.[^o15] This follows from [Abel's uniform convergence test](wikipedia://en/7088921#Abel's_uniform_convergence_test) and applying the [uniform limit theorem](wikipedia://en/29230765).

:::
