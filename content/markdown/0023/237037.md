# Cartesian closed category

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cartesian closed category"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) is **Cartesian closed** if, roughly speaking, any [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) defined on a [product](wikipedia://en/237868) of two [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) can be naturally identified with a morphism defined on one of the factors. These categories are particularly important in [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636) and the theory of programming, in that their [internal language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_language) is the [simply typed lambda calculus](wikipedia://en/1986011). They are generalized by [closed monoidal categories](wikipedia://en/1396924), whose internal language, [linear type systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_type_system), are suitable for both [quantum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_computation) and classical computation.[^o2]

:::

## Etymology {#Etymology}

Named after [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) (1596–1650), French philosopher, mathematician, and scientist, whose formulation of analytic geometry gave rise to the concept of [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095), which was later generalized to the notion of [categorical product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorical_product).

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The category **C** is called Cartesian closed[^o6] if it satisfies the following three properties:

- It has a [terminal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_object).
- Any two objects *X* and *Y* of **C** have a [product](wikipedia://en/237868) *X*×*Y* in **C**.
- Any two objects *Y* and *Z* of **C** have an [exponential](wikipedia://en/2237858) *Z<sup>Y</sup>* in **C**.

The first two conditions can be combined to the single requirement that any finite (possibly empty) family of objects of *C* admit a product in *C*, because of the natural [associativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity) of the categorical product and because the [empty product](wikipedia://en/181289) in a category is the terminal object of that category.

The third condition is equivalent to the requirement that the [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) –×*Y* (i.e. the functor from **C** to **C** that maps objects *X* to *X*×*Y* and morphisms φ to φ×id<sub>*Y*</sub>) has a [right adjoint](wikipedia://en/53991), usually denoted –<sup>*Y*</sup>, for all objects *Y* in **C**. For [locally small](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_small_category) categories, this can be expressed by the existence of a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between the [hom-sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hom-set)

$$\mathrm{Hom}(X\times Y,Z) \cong \mathrm{Hom}(X,Z^Y)$$

which is [natural](wikipedia://en/45022) in *X*, *Y*, and *Z*.[^o7]

Take care to note that a Cartesian closed category need not have finite [limits](wikipedia://en/39248); only finite products are guaranteed.

If a category has the property that all its [slice categories](wikipedia://en/840950#Category_of_objects_over_A) are Cartesian closed, then it is called *locally cartesian closed*. Note that if **C** is locally Cartesian closed, it need not actually be Cartesian closed; that happens if and only if **C** has a terminal object.

:::

## Basic constructions {#Basic_constructions}

### Evaluation {#Evaluation}

For each object *Y*, the counit of the exponential adjunction is a [natural transformation](wikipedia://en/45022)

$$\mathrm{ev}_{Y,Z} : Z^Y \times Y \to Z$$

called the (internal) **evaluation** map. More generally, we can construct the **[partial application](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_application)** map as the composite

$$\mathrm{papply}_{X,Y,Z} : Z^{X \times Y} \times X \cong (Z^Y)^{X} \times X \,\xrightarrow\overset{}{\mathrm{ev}_{X, Z^Y}}\, Z^Y.$$

In the particular case of the category **Set**, these reduce to the ordinary operations:

$$\mathrm{ev}_{Y,Z}(f,y) = f(y).$$

### Composition {#Composition}

Evaluating the exponential in one argument at a morphism *p* : *X* → *Y* gives morphisms

$$\begin{aligned} p^Z : X^Z \to Y^Z,\\ Z^p &: Z^Y \to Z^X, \end{aligned}$$

corresponding to the operation of composition with *p*. Alternate notations for the operation *p*<sup>*Z*</sup> include *p*<sub>\*</sub> and *p*∘-. Alternate notations for the operation *Z*<sup>*p*</sup> include *p*<sup>\*</sup> and -∘*p*.

Evaluation maps can be chained as

$$Z^Y \times Y^X \times X \,\xrightarrow{\mathrm{id} \times \mathrm{ev}_{X,Y}}\, Z^Y \times Y \,\xrightarrow{\mathrm{ev}_{Y,Z}}\, Z$$

the corresponding arrow under the exponential adjunction

$$c_{X,Y,Z} : Z^Y \times Y^X \to Z^X$$

is called the (internal) **composition** map.

In the particular case of the category **Set**, this is the ordinary [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) operation:

$$c_{X,Y,Z}(g,f) = g \circ f.$$

### Sections {#Sections}

For a morphism *p*:*X* → *Y*, suppose the following [pullback](wikipedia://en/841689) square exists, which defines the subobject of *X*<sup>*Y*</sup> corresponding to maps whose composite with *p* is the identity:

$$\begin{array}{ccc} \Gamma_Y(p) &\to& X^Y \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ 1 &\to& Y^Y \end{array}$$

where the arrow on the right is *p*<sup>*Y*</sup> and the arrow on the bottom corresponds to the identity on *Y*. Then Γ<sub>*Y*</sub>(*p*) is called the **[object of sections](wikipedia://en/367621)** of *p*. It is often abbreviated as Γ<sub>*Y*</sub>(*X*).

If Γ<sub>*Y*</sub>(*p*) exists for every morphism *p* with codomain *Y*, then it can be assembled into a functor Γ<sub>*Y*</sub> : *C*/*Y* → *C* on the slice category, which is right adjoint to a variant of the product functor:

$$\hom_{C/Y}(X \times Y \,\xrightarrow\overset{}{\pi_2}\, Y, Z \,\xrightarrow\overset{}{p}\, Y) \cong \hom_C(X, \Gamma_Y(p)).$$

The exponential by *Y* can be expressed in terms of sections:

$$Z^Y \cong \Gamma_Y(Z \times Y \,\xrightarrow\overset{}{\pi_2}\, Y).$$

## Examples {#Examples}

Examples of Cartesian closed categories include:

- The category **Set** of all [sets](wikipedia://en/26691), with [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) as morphisms, is Cartesian closed. The product *X*×*Y* is the Cartesian product of *X* and *Y*, and *Z*<sup>*Y*</sup> is the set of all functions from *Y* to *Z*. The adjointness is expressed by the following fact: the function *f* : *X*×*Y* → *Z* is naturally identified with the [curried](wikipedia://en/6600) function *g* : *X* → *Z*<sup>*Y*</sup> defined by *g*(*x*)(*y*) = *f*(*x*,*y*) for all *x* in *X* and *y* in *Y*.
- The [subcategory](wikipedia://en/310959) of [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) sets, with functions as morphisms, is also Cartesian closed for the same reason.
- If *G* is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), then the category of all [*G*-sets](wikipedia://en/12781) is Cartesian closed. If *Y* and *Z* are two *G*-sets, then *Z*<sup>*Y*</sup> is the set of all functions from *Y* to *Z* with *G* action defined by (*g*.*F*)(*y*) = *g*.F(*g*<sup>−1</sup>.*y*) for all *g* in *G*, *F*:*Y* → *Z* and *y* in *Y*.
- The subcategory of finite *G*-sets is also Cartesian closed.
- The category **Cat** of all small categories (with functors as morphisms) is Cartesian closed; the exponential *C*<sup>*D*</sup> is given by the [functor category](wikipedia://en/407039) consisting of all functors from *D* to *C*, with natural transformations as morphisms.
- If *C* is a [small category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_category), then the functor category **Set**<sup>*C*</sup> consisting of all covariant functors from *C* into the category of sets, with natural transformations as morphisms, is Cartesian closed. If *F* and *G* are two functors from *C* to **Set**, then the exponential *F*<sup>*G*</sup> is the functor whose value on the object *X* of *C* is given by the set of all natural transformations from $(X, -) \times G$ to *F*.
  - The earlier example of *G*-sets can be seen as a special case of functor categories: every group can be considered as a one-object category, and *G*-sets are nothing but functors from this category to **Set**
  - The category of all [directed graphs](wikipedia://en/19721986) is Cartesian closed; this is a functor category as explained under functor category.
  - In particular, the category of [simplicial sets](wikipedia://en/1027784) (which are functors *X* : Δ<sup>op</sup> → **Set**) is Cartesian closed.
- Even more generally, every [elementary topos](wikipedia://en/815802) is Cartesian closed.
- In [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), Cartesian closed categories are particularly easy to work with. Neither the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051) with [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) maps nor the [category of smooth manifolds](wikipedia://en/10059981) with smooth maps is Cartesian closed. Substitute categories have therefore been considered: the category of [compactly generated](wikipedia://en/899382) [Hausdorff spaces](wikipedia://en/13637) is Cartesian closed, as is the category of [Frölicher spaces](wikipedia://en/2229523).
- In [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), [complete partial orders](wikipedia://en/572352) (*cpo*s) have a natural [topology](wikipedia://en/30450), the [Scott topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott_topology), whose continuous maps do form a Cartesian closed category (that is, the objects are the cpos, and the morphisms are the [Scott continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott_continuous) maps). Both [currying](wikipedia://en/6600) and *[apply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apply)* are continuous functions in the Scott topology, and currying, together with apply, provide the adjoint.[^o15]
- A [Heyting algebra](wikipedia://en/309343) is a Cartesian closed (bounded) [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304). An important example arises from topological spaces. If *X* is a topological space, then the [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) in *X* form the objects of a category O(*X*) for which there is a unique morphism from *U* to *V* if *U* is a subset of *V* and no morphism otherwise. This [poset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poset) is a Cartesian closed category: the "product" of *U* and *V* is the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of *U* and *V* and the exponential *U*<sup>*V*</sup> is the [interior](wikipedia://en/55610) of $U\cup(X\backslash V)$.
- A category with a [zero object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_object) is Cartesian closed if and only if it is [equivalent](wikipedia://en/379051) to a category with only one object and one identity morphism. Indeed, if 0 is an initial object and 1 is a final object and we have $0 \cong 1$, then $\mathrm{Hom}(X, Y) \cong \mathrm{Hom}(1, Y^X) \cong \mathrm{Hom}(0, Y^X) \cong 1$ which has only one element.[^o16]
  - In particular, any non-trivial category with a zero object, such as an [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063), is not Cartesian closed. So the [category of modules over a ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_modules_over_a_ring) is not Cartesian closed. However, the functor [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) $-\otimes M$ with a fixed [module](wikipedia://en/276410) does have [a right adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor-hom_adjunction). The tensor product is not a categorical product, so this does not contradict the above. We obtain instead that the category of modules is [monoidal closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoidal_closed_category).

Examples of locally Cartesian closed categories include:

- Every [elementary topos](wikipedia://en/815802) is locally Cartesian closed. This example includes **Set**, *FinSet*, *G*-sets for a group *G*, as well as **Set**<sup>*C*</sup> for small categories *C*.
- The category *LH* whose objects are topological spaces and whose morphisms are [local homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/248582) is locally Cartesian closed, since *LH*/*X* is equivalent to the category of [sheaves](wikipedia://en/245466) $Sh(X)$. However, *LH* does not have a terminal object, and thus is not Cartesian closed.
- If *C* has pullbacks and for every arrow *p* : *X* → *Y*, the functor *p*<sup>\*</sup> : *C/Y* → *C/X* given by taking pullbacks has a right adjoint, then *C* is locally Cartesian closed.
- If *C* is locally Cartesian closed, then all of its slice categories *C*/*X* are also locally Cartesian closed.

Non-examples of locally Cartesian closed categories include:

- **Cat** is not locally Cartesian closed.

## Applications {#Applications}

In Cartesian closed categories, a "function of two variables" (a morphism *f* : *X*×*Y* → *Z*) can always be represented as a "function of one variable" (the morphism λ*f* : *X* → *Z*<sup>*Y*</sup>). In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) applications, this is known as [currying](wikipedia://en/6600); it has led to the realization that simply-typed lambda calculus can be interpreted in any Cartesian closed category.

The [Curry–Howard–Lambek correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curry–Howard–Lambek_correspondence) provides a deep [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) between [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262), simply-typed lambda calculus and Cartesian closed categories.

Certain Cartesian closed categories, the [topoi](wikipedia://en/21391464), have been proposed as a general setting for mathematics, instead of traditional [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553).

Computer scientist [John Backus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Backus) has advocated a variable-free notation, or [Function-level programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function-level_programming), which in retrospect bears some similarity to the [internal language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_language) of Cartesian closed categories.[^o19] [CAML](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorical_Abstract_Machine_Language) is more consciously modelled on Cartesian closed categories.

:::

## Dependent sum and product {#Dependent_sum_and_product}

Let *C* be a locally Cartesian closed category. Then *C* has all pullbacks, because the pullback of two arrows with codomain *Z* is given by the product in *C*/*Z*.

For every arrow *p* : *X* → *Y*, let *P* denote the corresponding object of *C/Y*. Taking pullbacks along *p* gives a functor *p*<sup>\*</sup> : *C*/*Y* → *C*/*X* which has both a left and a right adjoint.

The left adjoint $\Sigma_p : C/X \to C/Y$ is called the **dependent sum** and is given by composition $p \circ (-)$.

The right adjoint $\Pi_p : C/X \to C/Y$ is called the **dependent product**.

The exponential by *P* in *C*/*Y* can be expressed in terms of the dependent product by the formula $Q^P \cong \Pi_p(p^*(Q))$.

The reason for these names is because, when interpreting *P* as a [dependent type](wikipedia://en/1949487) $y : Y \vdash P(y) : \mathrm{Type}$, the functors $\Sigma_p$ and $\Pi_p$ correspond to the type formations $\Sigma_{x : P(y)}$ and $\Pi_{x : P(y)}$ respectively.

## Equational theory {#Equational_theory}

In every Cartesian closed category (using exponential notation), (*X*<sup>*Y*</sup>)<sup>*Z*</sup> and (*X*<sup>*Z*</sup>)<sup>*Y*</sup> are isomorphic for all objects *X*, *Y* and *Z*. We write this as the "equation"

$$(x^y)^z = (x^z)^y.$$

One may ask what other such equations are valid in all Cartesian closed categories. It turns out that all of them follow logically from the following axioms (where $1$ denotes the terminal object of $C$:[^o22]

- the product is associative: $x\times(y\times z) = (x\times y)\times z$
- the product is commutative: $x\times y = y\times x$
- the terminal object is the product identity: $x\times 1 = x$
- the terminal object is the (algebraic) left zero for exponentiation: $1^x = 1$
- the terminal object is the right identity for exponentiation: $x^1 = x$
- exponentiation distributes on the right over products: $(x\times y)^z = x^z\times y^z$
- currying: $(x^y)^z = x^{y\times z}$

::: {#o24 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Bicartesian closed category"}

### Bicartesian closed categories {#Bicartesian_closed_categories}

Bicartesian closed categories extend Cartesian closed categories with binary [coproducts](wikipedia://en/310889) and an [initial object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_object), with products distributing over coproducts. Their equational theory is extended with the following axioms, yielding something similar to [Tarski's high school axioms](wikipedia://en/21394895) but with a zero:

- coproducts are commutative: $x + y = y + x$
- coproducts are associative: $(x + y) + z = x + (y + z)$
- products distribute over coproducts: $x\times(y + z) = x\times y + x\times z$
- exponentiation by coproducts is the same as a product of exponents: $x^{y + z} = x^y\times x^z$
- the initial object is the coproduct identity: $0 + x = x$
- the initial object is the product zero: $x\times 0 = 0$
- exponentiation by the initial object is the terminal object: $x^0 = 1$

Note however that the above list is not complete; type isomorphism in the free BCCC is not finitely axiomatizable, and its decidability is still an [open problem](wikipedia://en/1333335).[^o25]

:::
