# Degeneracy (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Degeneracy"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **degenerate case** is a [limiting case](wikipedia://en/40400729) of a class of objects which appears to be qualitatively different from (and usually simpler than) the rest of the class;[^o2] "**degeneracy**" is the condition of being a degenerate case.[^o3] A given class of objects may be defined by several different systems of equations in most scenarios, and these different systems of equations may lead to different degenerate cases, while characterizing the same non-degenerate cases. This may be the reason for which there is no general definition of degeneracy, despite the fact that the concept is widely used and defined (if needed) in each specific situation.

:::

## Exceptionality {#Exceptionality}

Often, the degenerate cases are the exceptional cases where changes to the usual [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) or the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of the object (or of some part of it) occur. For example, a triangle is an object of dimension two, and a degenerate triangle is contained in a [line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_%28mathematics%29),[^o7] which makes its dimension one. This is similar to the case of a circle, whose dimension shrinks from two to zero as it degenerates into a point.[^o2] As another example, the [solution set](wikipedia://en/567840) of a [system of equations](wikipedia://en/249617) that depends on [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065) generally has a fixed cardinality and dimension, but cardinality and/or dimension may be different for some exceptional values, called degenerate cases. In such a degenerate case, the [solution set](wikipedia://en/567840) is said to be degenerate.

A degenerate case thus has special features which makes it [non-generic](wikipedia://en/12886758), or a [special case](wikipedia://en/31308010). However, not all non-generic or special cases are degenerate. For example, [right triangles](wikipedia://en/39420), [isosceles triangles](wikipedia://en/315428) and [equilateral triangles](wikipedia://en/173285) are non-generic and non-degenerate. In fact, degenerate cases often correspond to [singularities](wikipedia://en/581763), either in the object or in some [configuration space](wikipedia://en/54981574). For example, a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673) is degenerate if and only if it has singular points (e.g., point, line, intersecting lines).[^o5]

## In geometry {#In_geometry}

The definitions of many classes of composite or structured objects often implicitly include inequalities. For example, the [angles](wikipedia://en/1196) and the side lengths of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) are supposed to be positive. The limiting cases, where one or several of these inequalities become equalities, are degeneracies. A triangle, for example, is degenerate if at least one side length or angle is zero. Equivalently, it becomes a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860).[^o7] For some classes of composite objects, the degenerate cases depend on the properties that are specifically studied. In particular, the class of objects may often be defined or characterized by systems of equations.

### Conic section {#Conic_section}

A degenerate conic is a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673) (a second-degree [plane curve](wikipedia://en/971691), defined by a [polynomial equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_equation) of degree two) that fails to be an [irreducible curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreducible_variety).

- A [point](wikipedia://en/593693) is a degenerate [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), namely one with radius 0.[^o2]
- The [line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_%28mathematics%29) is a degenerate case of a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) if the parabola resides on a [tangent plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_plane). In [inversive geometry](wikipedia://en/295844), a line is a degenerate case of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), with infinite radius.
- Two [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) lines also form a degenerate parabola.
- A [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) can be viewed as a degenerate case of an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) in which the [semiminor axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiminor_axis) goes to zero, the [foci](wikipedia://en/1032607) go to the endpoints, and the [eccentricity](wikipedia://en/1239472) goes to one.
- A circle can be thought of as a degenerate ellipse, as the [eccentricity](wikipedia://en/1239472) approaches 0 and the foci merge.[^o2]
- An ellipse can also degenerate into a single point.
- A [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) can degenerate into two lines crossing at a point, through a family of hyperbolae having those lines as common [asymptotes](wikipedia://en/3107).

### Triangle {#Triangle}

*Image: Degenerate Triangles.png.* The three types of degenerate triangles, all of which contain zero area.

A degenerate [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) is a "flat" triangle in the sense that it is contained in a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860). In other words, a degenerate triangle is a triangle that does not satisfy the strict triangle inequality: $a+b>c$, instead, it satisfies: $a+b=c$[^o11]. It has thus [collinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear) vertices[^o7] and zero area. If the three vertices are all distinct, it has two 0° angles and one 180° angle. If two vertices are equal, it has one 0° angle and two undefined angles. If all three vertices are equal, all three angles are undefined.

:::

### Rectangle {#Rectangle}

A [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048) with one pair of opposite sides of length zero degenerates to a line segment, with zero area. If both of the rectangle's pairs of opposite sides have length zero, the rectangle degenerates to a point.

### Hyperrectangle {#Hyperrectangle}

A [hyperrectangle](wikipedia://en/1688550) is the $n$-dimensional analog of a rectangle. If its sides along any of the $n$ axes has length zero, it degenerates to a lower-dimensional hyperrectangle, all the way down to a point if the sides aligned with every axis have length zero.

### Convex polygon {#Convex_polygon}

A [convex polygon](wikipedia://en/660019) is degenerate if at least two consecutive sides coincide at least partially, or at least one side has zero length, or at least one angle is 180°. Thus a degenerate convex polygon of *n* sides looks like a polygon with fewer sides. In the case of triangles, this definition coincides with the one that has been given above.

### Convex polyhedron {#Convex_polyhedron}

A [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron) is degenerate if either two adjacent facets are [coplanar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coplanar) or two edges are aligned. In the case of a [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606), this is equivalent to saying that all of its [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) lie in the same [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29), giving it a [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) of zero.

### Standard torus {#Standard_torus}

- In contexts where self-intersection is allowed, a double-covered [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) is a degenerate [standard torus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_torus) where the axis of revolution passes through the center of the generating circle, rather than outside it.
- A torus degenerates to a circle when its minor radius goes to 0.

### Sphere {#Sphere}

When the radius of a sphere goes to zero, the resulting degenerate sphere of zero volume is a [point](wikipedia://en/593693).

See [general position](wikipedia://en/383736) for other examples.

## Elsewhere {#Elsewhere}

- A set containing a single point is a degenerate [continuum](wikipedia://en/17368856).
- Objects such as the [digon](wikipedia://en/5442728) and [monogon](wikipedia://en/5483767) can be viewed as degenerate cases of [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621): valid in a general abstract mathematical sense, but not part of the original Euclidean conception of polygons.
- A [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) which can only take one value has a [degenerate distribution](wikipedia://en/101843); if that value is the real number 0, then its [probability density](wikipedia://en/43487) is the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021).
- A [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) of a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) is sometimes said to be *degenerate* if it is a [multiple root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_root), since generically the $n$ roots of an $n$th degree polynomial are all distinct.[^o2] This usage carries over to eigenproblems: a degenerate [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) is a multiple root of the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268).
- In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), any such [multiplicity](wikipedia://en/19211790) in the eigenvalues of the [Hamiltonian operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_operator) gives rise to [degenerate energy levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_energy_level). Usually any such degeneracy indicates some underlying [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) in the system.
