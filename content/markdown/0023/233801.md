# Fréchet space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fréchet space"}

In [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) and related areas of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Fréchet spaces**, named after [Maurice Fréchet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Fréchet), are special [topological vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_vector_spaces). They are generalizations of [Banach spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banach_spaces) ([normed vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_vector_spaces) that are [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) with respect to the [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) induced by the [norm](wikipedia://en/990534)). All [Banach](wikipedia://en/3989) and [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932) are Fréchet spaces. Spaces of [infinitely differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitely_differentiable) [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) are typical examples of Fréchet spaces, many of which are typically not Banach spaces.

:::

A Fréchet space $X$ is defined to be a [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) [metrizable](wikipedia://en/64278078) [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVS) that is [complete as a TVS](wikipedia://en/53695856),[^n1] meaning that every [Cauchy sequence](wikipedia://en/6085) in $X$ converges to some point in $X$ (see footnote for more details).[^o3]

**Important note**: Not all authors require that a Fréchet space be locally convex (discussed below).

The topology of every Fréchet space is induced by some [translation-invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation-invariant) [complete metric](wikipedia://en/7832). Conversely, if the topology of a locally convex space $X$ is induced by a translation-invariant complete metric then $X$ is a Fréchet space.

[Fréchet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_René_Fréchet) was the first to use the term "[Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989)" and [Banach](wikipedia://en/29646) in turn then coined the term "Fréchet space" to mean a [complete](wikipedia://en/53695856) [metrizable topological vector space](wikipedia://en/64278078), without the local convexity requirement (such a space is today often called an "[F-space](wikipedia://en/200440)").[^n1] The local convexity requirement was added later by [Nicolas Bourbaki](wikipedia://en/167394).[^n1] A sizable number of authors (e.g. Schaefer) use "F-space" to mean a (locally convex) Fréchet space while others do not require that a "Fréchet space" be locally convex. Moreover, some authors even use "*F*-space" and "Fréchet space" interchangeably. When reading mathematical literature, it is recommended that a reader always check whether the book's or article's definition of "F-space" and "Fréchet space" requires local convexity.[^n1]

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Fréchet spaces can be defined in two equivalent ways: the first employs a [translation-invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation-invariant) [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29), the second a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) family of [seminorms](wikipedia://en/292067).

### Invariant metric definition {#Invariant_metric_definition}

A topological vector space $X$ is a **Fréchet space** if and only if it satisfies the following three properties:

It is [locally convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_convex).[^o10] Its topology **can** be [induced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induced_topology) by a translation-invariant metric, that is, a metric $d : X \times X \to \mathbb{R}$ such that $d(x, y) = d(x + z, y + z)$ for all $x, y, z \in X.$ This means that a subset $U$ of $X$ is [open](wikipedia://en/39358) if and only if for every $u \in U$ there exists an $r > 0$ such that $\{v : d(v, u) < r\}$ is a subset of $U.$ Some (or equivalently, every) translation-invariant metric on $X$ inducing the topology of $X$ is [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_metric).

Assuming that the other two conditions are satisfied, this condition is equivalent to $X$ being a [complete topological vector space](wikipedia://en/53695856), meaning that $X$ is a complete [uniform space](wikipedia://en/32339) when it is endowed with its [canonical uniformity](wikipedia://en/53695856) (this canonical uniformity is independent of any metric on $X$ and is defined entirely in terms of vector subtraction and $X$'s neighborhoods of the origin; moreover, the uniformity induced by any (topology-defining) translation invariant metric on $X$ is identical to this canonical uniformity).

Note there is no natural notion of distance between two points of a Fréchet space: many different translation-invariant metrics may induce the same topology.

### Countable family of seminorms definition {#Countable_family_of_seminorms_definition}

The alternative and somewhat more practical definition is the following: a topological vector space $X$ is a **Fréchet space** if and only if it satisfies the following three properties:

1. It is a [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637).
1. Its topology may be induced by a countable family of seminorms $(\|\cdot\|_k)_{k \in \mathbb{N}_0}$. This means that a subset $U \subseteq X$ is open if and only if for every $u \in U$ there exist $K \geq 0$ and $r > 0$ such that $\{ v \in X : \|v-u\|_k < r \text{ for all } k \le K\}$ is a subset of $U$.
1. It is complete with respect to the family of seminorms.

A family $\mathcal P$ of seminorms on $X$ yields a Hausdorff topology if and only if[^o12]

$$\bigcap_{\|\cdot\| \in \mathcal{P}} \{x \in X : \|x\| = 0\} = \{0\}.$$

A sequence $\left(x_n\right)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ in $X$ converges to $x$ in the Fréchet space defined by a family of seminorms if and only if it converges to $x$ with respect to each of the given seminorms.

### As webbed Baire spaces {#As_webbed_Baire_spaces}

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem (de Wilde 1978)"}

**Theorem[^n2] (de Wilde 1978).**

A [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) $X$ is a Fréchet space if and only if it is both a [webbed space](wikipedia://en/41717785) and a [Baire space](wikipedia://en/200550).

:::

### Comparison to Banach spaces {#Comparison_to_Banach_spaces}

In contrast to [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989), the complete translation-invariant metric need not arise from a norm. The topology of a Fréchet space does, however, arise from both a [total paranorm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paranorm) and an [F-norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-seminorm) (the F stands for Fréchet).

Even though the [topological structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_structure) of Fréchet spaces is more complicated than that of Banach spaces due to the potential lack of a norm, many important results in functional analysis, like the [open mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/17395276), the [closed graph theorem](wikipedia://en/497834), and the [Banach–Steinhaus theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banach–Steinhaus_theorem), still hold.

:::

## Constructing Fréchet spaces {#Constructing_Fréchet_spaces}

Recall that a seminorm $\|\cdot\|$ is a function from a vector space $X$ to the real numbers satisfying three properties. For all $x, y \in X$ and all scalars $c,$

$$\|x\| \geq 0$$

$$\|x+y\| \le \|x\| + \|y\|$$

$$\|c\cdot x\| = |c|\|x\|$$

If $\| x\| = 0 \iff x = 0$, then $\|\cdot\|$ is in fact a norm. However, seminorms are useful in that they enable us to construct Fréchet spaces, as follows:

To construct a Fréchet space, one typically starts with a vector space $X$ and defines a countable family of seminorms $\|\cdot\|_k$ on $X$ with the following two properties:

- if $x \in X$ and $\|x\|_k = 0$ for all $k \geq 0,$ then $x = 0$;
- if $x_{\bullet} = \left(x_n\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}$ is a sequence in $X$ which is [Cauchy](wikipedia://en/6085) with respect to each seminorm $\|\cdot\|_k,$ then there exists $x \in X$ such that $x_{\bullet} = \left(x_n\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}$ converges to $x$ with respect to each seminorm $\|\cdot\|_k.$

Then the topology induced by these seminorms (as explained above) turns $X$ into a Fréchet space; the first property ensures that it is Hausdorff, and the second property ensures that it is complete. A translation-invariant complete metric inducing the same topology on $X$ can then be defined by

$$d(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty 2^{-k}\frac{\|x-y\|_k}{1+\|x-y\|_k} \qquad x, y \in X.$$

The function $u \mapsto \frac{u}{1 + u}$ maps $[0, \infty)$ monotonically to $[0, 1),$ and so the above definition ensures that $d(x, y)$ is "small" if and only if there exists $K$ "large" such that $\|x - y\|_k$ is "small" for $k = 0, \ldots, K.$

## Examples {#Examples}

### From pure functional analysis {#From_pure_functional_analysis}

- Every Banach space is a Fréchet space, as the norm induces a translation-invariant metric and the space is complete with respect to this metric.
- The [space $\mathbb{R}^{\omega}$ of all real valued sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_of_real_valued_sequences) (also denoted $\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}$) becomes a Fréchet space if we define the $k$-th seminorm of a sequence to be the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of the $k$-th element of the sequence. Convergence in this Fréchet space is equivalent to element-wise convergence.

### From smooth manifolds {#From_smooth_manifolds}

The [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $C^\infty([0,1])$ of all infinitely differentiable functions $f : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ becomes a Fréchet space with the seminorms

$$\|f\|_k = \sup\{|f^{(k)}(x)| : x \in [0,1]\}$$

for every non-negative integer $k.$ Here, $f^{(k)}$ denotes the $k$-th derivative of $f,$ and $f^{(0)} = f.$ In this Fréchet space, a sequence $\left(f_n\right) \to f$ of functions [converges](wikipedia://en/26551602) towards the element $f \in C^\infty([0,1])$ if and only if for every non-negative integer $k \geq 0,$ the sequence $\left(f_n^{(k)}\right) \to f^{(k)}$ [converges uniformly](wikipedia://en/50652).

The vector space $C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ of all infinitely differentiable functions $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ becomes a Fréchet space with the seminorms

$$\|f\|_{k, a} = \sup \{ |f^{(k)}(x)| : x \in [-a, a]\}$$

for all integers $k, a \geq 0.$ Then, a sequence of functions $\left(f_n\right) \to f$ converges if and only if for every $k \geq 0,$ the sequence $\left(f_n^{(k)}\right) \to f^{(k)}$ [converges compactly](wikipedia://en/7442564).

The vector space $C^m(\mathbb{R})$ of all $m$-times continuously differentiable functions $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ becomes a Fréchet space with the seminorms

$$\|f\|_{k, a} = \sup \{ |f^{(k)}(x)| : x \in [-a, a]\}$$

for all integers $a \geq 0$ and $k = 0, \ldots, m.$

If $M$ is a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) $C^{\infty}$-[manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) and $B$ is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), then the set $C^{\infty}(M, B)$ of all infinitely-often differentiable functions $f : M \to B$ can be turned into a Fréchet space by using as seminorms the suprema of the norms of all partial derivatives. If $M$ is a (not necessarily compact) $C^{\infty}$-manifold which admits a countable sequence $K^n$ of compact subsets, so that every compact subset of $M$ is contained in at least one $K^n,$ then the spaces $C^m(M, B)$ and $C^{\infty}(M, B)$ are also Fréchet space in a natural manner.

As a special case, every smooth finite-dimensional [complete manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_manifold) $M$ can be made into such a nested union of compact subsets: equip it with a [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) $g$ which induces a metric $d(x, y),$ choose $x \in M,$ and let

$$K_n = \{y \in M : d(x,y) \le n\} \ .$$

Let $X$ be a compact $C^{\infty}$-[manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) and$V$ a [vector bundle](wikipedia://en/276524) over $X.$ Let $C^{\infty}(X, V)$ denote the space of smooth sections of $V$ over $X.$ Choose Riemannian metrics $g$ and connections $D$, which are guaranteed to exist, on the bundles $TX$ and $V.$ If $s$ is a section, denote its *j*<sup>th</sup> covariant derivative by $D^j s.$ Then

$$\|s\|_n = \sum_{j=0}^n \sup_{x\in M} |D^js|_g$$

(where $|\,\cdot\,|_g$ is the norm induced by the Riemannian metric $g$) is a family of seminorms making $C^{\infty}(M, V)$ into a Fréchet space.

### From holomorphicity {#From_holomorphicity}

Let $H$ be the space of entire (everywhere [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110)) functions on the complex plane. Then the family of seminorms

$$\left|f\right|_{n} = \sup \{\left|f(z)\right| : |z| \leq n\}$$

makes $H$ into a Fréchet space.

Let $H$ be the space of entire (everywhere holomorphic) functions of [exponential type](wikipedia://en/4081509) $\tau.$ Then the family of seminorms

$$\left|f\right|_{n} = \sup_{z \in \mathbb{C}} \exp \left[- \left(\tau + \frac{1}{n} \right) |z|\right] \left|f(z)\right|$$

makes $H$ into a Fréchet space.

Not all vector spaces with complete translation-invariant metrics are Fréchet spaces. An example is the [space $L^p([0, 1])$](wikipedia://en/45194) with $p < 1.$ Although this space fails to be locally convex, it is an [F-space](wikipedia://en/200440).

:::

## Properties and further notions {#Properties_and_further_notions}

If a Fréchet space admits a continuous norm then all of the seminorms used to define it can be replaced with norms by adding this continuous norm to each of them. A Banach space, $C^{\infty}([a, b]),$ $C^{\infty}(X, V)$ with $X$ compact, and $H$ all admit norms, while $\mathbb{R}^{\omega}$ and $C(\mathbb{R})$ do not.

A closed subspace of a Fréchet space is a Fréchet space. A quotient of a Fréchet space by a closed subspace is a Fréchet space. The direct sum of a finite number of Fréchet spaces is a Fréchet space.

A product of [countably many](wikipedia://en/6026) Fréchet spaces is always once again a Fréchet space. However, an arbitrary product of Fréchet spaces will be a Fréchet space if and only if all except for at most countably many of them are trivial (that is, have dimension 0). Consequently, a product of uncountably many non-trivial Fréchet spaces can not be a Fréchet space (indeed, such a product is not even metrizable because its origin can not have a countable neighborhood basis). So for example, if $I \neq \varnothing$ is any set and $X$ is any non-trivial Fréchet space (such as $X = \mathbb{R}$ for instance), then the product $X^I = \prod_{i \in I} X$ is a Fréchet space if and only if $I$ is a countable set.

Several important tools of functional analysis which are based on the [Baire category theorem](wikipedia://en/55227) remain true in Fréchet spaces; examples are the [closed graph theorem](wikipedia://en/497834) and the [open mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/17395276). The [open mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/17395276) implies that if $\tau \text{ and } \tau_2$ are topologies on $X$ that make both $(X, \tau)$ and $\left(X, \tau_2\right)$ into [complete](wikipedia://en/53695856) [metrizable TVSs](wikipedia://en/64278078) (such as Fréchet spaces) and if one topology is [finer or coarser](wikipedia://en/393069) than the other then they must be equal (that is, if $\tau \subseteq \tau_2 \text{ or } \tau_2 \subseteq \tau \text{ then } \tau = \tau_2$).[^n3]

Every [bounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_map) linear operator from a Fréchet space into another [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVS) is continuous.[^n4]

There exists a Fréchet space $X$ having a [bounded](wikipedia://en/1789812) subset $B$ and also a dense vector subspace $M$ such that $B$ is not contained in the closure (in $X$) of any bounded subset of $M.$[^n5]

All Fréchet spaces are [stereotype spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereotype_space). In the theory of stereotype spaces Fréchet spaces are dual objects to [Brauner spaces](wikipedia://en/40958342). All [metrizable](wikipedia://en/64278078) [Montel spaces](wikipedia://en/1796319) are [separable](wikipedia://en/27855).[^n6] A [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) Fréchet space is a Montel space if and only if each [weak-\* convergent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak-*_topology) sequence in its continuous dual converges is [strongly convergent](wikipedia://en/63951372).[^n6]

The [strong dual space](wikipedia://en/63951372) $X_b^{\prime}$ of a Fréchet space (and more generally, of any metrizable locally convex space[^n7]) $X$ is a [DF-space](wikipedia://en/63703274).[^o30] The strong dual of a DF-space is a Fréchet space.[^n8] The strong dual of a [reflexive](wikipedia://en/210091) Fréchet space is a [bornological space](wikipedia://en/1804457)[^n7] and a [Ptak space](wikipedia://en/63688717). Every Fréchet space is a Ptak space. The strong bidual (that is, the [strong dual space](wikipedia://en/63951372) of the strong dual space) of a metrizable locally convex space is a Fréchet space.[^n9]

### Norms and normability {#Norms_and_normability}

If $X$ is a locally convex space then the topology of $X$ can be a defined by a family of continuous norms on $X$ (a **[norm](wikipedia://en/990534)** is a [positive-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_functional) [seminorm](wikipedia://en/292067)) if and only if there exists at least one continuous norm on $X.$[^n10] Even if a Fréchet space has a topology that is defined by a (countable) family of norms (all norms are also seminorms), then it may nevertheless still fail to be [normable space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normable_space) (meaning that its topology cannot be defined by any single norm). The [space of all sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_of_real_sequences) $\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{N}}$ (with the product topology) is a Fréchet space. There does not exist any Hausdorff [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) topology on $\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{N}}$ that is [strictly coarser](wikipedia://en/393069) than this product topology.[^n11] The space $\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{N}}$ is not [normable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normable_space), which means that its topology can not be defined by any [norm](wikipedia://en/990534).[^n11] Also, there does not exist any [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) norm on $\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{N}}.$ In fact, as the following theorem shows, whenever $X$ is a Fréchet space on which there does not exist any continuous norm, then this is due entirely to the presence of $\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{N}}$ as a subspace.

::: {#o39 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem[^n11].**

Let $X$ be a Fréchet space over the field $\mathbb{K}.$ Then the following are equivalent:

$X$ does not admit a continuous norm (that is, any continuous seminorm on $X$ can not be a norm). $X$ contains a vector subspace that is TVS-isomorphic to $\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{N}}.$ $X$ contains a [complemented vector subspace](wikipedia://en/63033543) that is TVS-isomorphic to $\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{N}}.$

:::

If $X$ is a non-normable Fréchet space on which there exists a continuous norm, then $X$ contains a closed vector subspace that has no [topological complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_complement).[^n12]

A metrizable [locally convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_convex) space is [normable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normable_space) if and only if its [strong dual space](wikipedia://en/63951372) is a [Fréchet–Urysohn](wikipedia://en/28480560) locally convex space.[^o41] In particular, if a locally convex metrizable space $X$ (such as a Fréchet space) is not normable (which can only happen if $X$ is infinite dimensional) then its [strong dual space](wikipedia://en/63951372) $X^{\prime}_b$ is not a [Fréchet–Urysohn space](wikipedia://en/28480560) and consequently, this [complete](wikipedia://en/53695856) Hausdorff locally convex space $X^{\prime}_b$ is also neither metrizable nor normable.

The [strong dual space](wikipedia://en/63951372) of a Fréchet space (and more generally, of [bornological spaces](wikipedia://en/1804457) such as metrizable TVSs) is always a [complete TVS](wikipedia://en/53695856) and so like any complete TVS, it is [normable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normable_space) if and only if its topology can be induced by a [complete norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_norm) (that is, if and only if it can be made into a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) that has the same topology). If $X$ is a Fréchet space then $X$ is [normable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normable_space) if (and only if) there exists a complete [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) on its continuous dual space $X'$ such that the norm induced topology on $X'$ is [finer](wikipedia://en/393069) than the weak-\* topology.[^o42] Consequently, if a Fréchet space is not normable (which can only happen if it is infinite dimensional) then neither is its strong dual space.

::: {#o44 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Anderson–Kadec theorem"}

### Anderson–Kadec theorem {#Anderson–Kadec_theorem}

::: {#o45 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Anderson–Kadec theorem"}

**[Anderson–Kadec theorem](wikipedia://en/58422881).**

Every infinite-dimensional, separable real Fréchet space is homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}},$ the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of [countably many](wikipedia://en/6026) copies of the real line $\mathbb{R}.$

:::

Note that the homeomorphism described in the Anderson–Kadec theorem is not necessarily linear.

::: {#o46 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Eidelheit theorem"}

**[Eidelheit](wikipedia://en/58995960) theorem.**

A Fréchet space is either isomorphic to a Banach space, or has a quotient space isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}.$

:::

:::

## Differentiation of functions {#Differentiation_of_functions}

If $X$ and $Y$ are Fréchet spaces, then the space $L(X,Y)$ consisting of all [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) [linear maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) from $X$ to $Y$ is not a Fréchet space in any natural manner. This is a major difference between the theory of Banach spaces and that of Fréchet spaces and necessitates a different definition for continuous differentiability of functions defined on Fréchet spaces, the [Gateaux derivative](wikipedia://en/2253139):

Suppose $U$ is an open subset of a Fréchet space $X,$ $P : U \to Y$ is a function valued in a Fréchet space $Y,$ $x \in U$ and $h \in X.$ The map $P$ is **differentiable at $x$ in the direction $h$** if the [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602)

$$D(P)(x)(h) = \lim_{t \to 0} \,\frac{1}{t}\left(P(x + th) - P(x)\right)$$

exists. The map $P$ is said to be **continuously differentiable** in $U$ if the map

$$D(P) : U \times X \to Y$$

is continuous. Since the [product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_%28topology%29) of Fréchet spaces is again a Fréchet space, we can then try to differentiate $D(P)$ and define the higher derivatives of $P$ in this fashion.

The derivative operator $P : C^{\infty}([0, 1]) \to C^{\infty}([0, 1])$ defined by $P(f) = f'$ is itself infinitely differentiable. The first derivative is given by

$$D(P)(f)(h) = h'$$

for any two elements $f, h \in C^{\infty}([0, 1]).$ This is a major advantage of the Fréchet space $C^{\infty}([0, 1])$ over the Banach space $C^k([0, 1])$ for finite $k.$

If $P : U \to Y$ is a continuously differentiable function, then the [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309)

$$x'(t) = P(x(t)),\quad x(0) = x_0\in U$$

need not have any solutions, and even if does, the solutions need not be unique. This is in stark contrast to the situation in Banach spaces.

In general, the [inverse function theorem](wikipedia://en/287229) is not true in Fréchet spaces, although a partial substitute is the [Nash–Moser theorem](wikipedia://en/2286045).

## Fréchet manifolds and Lie groups {#Fréchet_manifolds_and_Lie_groups}

One may define **Fréchet manifolds** as spaces that "locally look like" Fréchet spaces (just like ordinary manifolds are defined as spaces that locally look like [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$), and one can then extend the concept of [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) to these manifolds. This is useful because for a given (ordinary) compact $C^{\infty}$ manifold $M,$ the set of all $C^{\infty}$ [diffeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/8564) $f : M \to M$ forms a generalized Lie group in this sense, and this Lie group captures the symmetries of $M.$ Some of the relations between [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944) and Lie groups remain valid in this setting.

Another important example of a Fréchet Lie group is the loop group of a compact Lie group $G,$ the smooth ($C^{\infty}$) mappings $\gamma : S^1 \to G,$ multiplied pointwise by $\left(\gamma_1 \gamma_2\right)(t) = \gamma_1(t) \gamma_2(t).$[^o49][^o50]

::: {#o52 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

If we drop the requirement for the space to be locally convex, we obtain [F-spaces](wikipedia://en/200440): vector spaces with complete translation-invariant metrics.

[LF-spaces](wikipedia://en/2314852) are countable inductive limits of Fréchet spaces.

:::
