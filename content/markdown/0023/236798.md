# Roger Cotes

::: {#o4 .subject cue=lead name="Roger Cotes"}

**Roger Cotes** (10 July 1682 – 5 June 1716) was an English [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902), known for working closely with [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) by proofreading the second edition of his famous book, the *[Principia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophiae_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica)*, before publication. He also devised the [quadrature](wikipedia://en/1618671) formulas known as [Newton–Cotes formulas](wikipedia://en/236795), which originated from Newton's research,[^o5] and made a geometric argument that can be interpreted as a logarithmic version of [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613).[^o6] He was the first [Plumian Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plumian_Professor_of_Astronomy_and_Experimental_Philosophy) at [Cambridge University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University) from 1707 until his death.

:::

## Early life {#Early_life}

Cotes was born in [Burbage, Leicestershire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burbage,_Leicestershire). His parents were Robert, the [rector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rector_%28ecclesiastical%29) of Burbage, and his wife, Grace, *née* Farmer. Roger had an elder brother, Anthony (born 1681), and a younger sister, Susanna (born 1683), both of whom died young. At first Roger attended Leicester School, where his mathematical talent was recognised. His aunt Hannah had married Rev. John Smith, and Smith took on the role of tutor to encourage Roger's talent. The Smiths' son, [Robert Smith](wikipedia://en/447816), became a close associate of Roger Cotes throughout his life. Cotes later studied at [St Paul's School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Paul's_School_%28London%29) in [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London) and entered [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge), in 1699. He [graduated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graduation) [BA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts) in 1702 and [MA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts) in 1706.[^o9]

## Astronomy {#Astronomy}

Roger Cotes's contributions to modern [computational methods](wikipedia://en/10433833) lie heavily in the fields of [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) and mathematics. Cotes began his educational career with a focus on [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy). He became a [fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow#Oxford,_Cambridge_and_Dublin) of Trinity College in 1707, and at age 26 he became the first Plumian Professor of Astronomy and Experimental Philosophy. On his appointment to professor, he opened a subscription list in an effort to provide an [observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observatory) for Trinity. Unfortunately, the observatory was still unfinished when Cotes died, and was demolished in 1797.[^o9]

In correspondence with Isaac Newton, Cotes designed a [heliostat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliostat) telescope with a mirror revolving by clockwork.[^o11][^o12] He recomputed the solar and planetary tables of [Giovanni Domenico Cassini](wikipedia://en/5215428) and [John Flamsteed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Flamsteed), and he intended to create tables of the [moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon)'s [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_motion), based on Newtonian principles. Finally, in 1707 he formed a school of physical sciences at Trinity in partnership with [William Whiston](wikipedia://en/188885).[^o9]

## The *Principia* {#The_Principia}

From 1709 to 1713, Cotes became heavily involved with the second edition of Newton's *Principia*, a book that explained Newton's theory of [universal gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_gravitation). The first edition of *Principia* had only a few copies printed and was in need of revision to include Newton's works and principles of lunar and planetary theory.[^o9] Newton at first had a casual approach to the revision, since he had all but given up scientific work. However, through the vigorous passion displayed by Cotes, Newton's scientific hunger was once again reignited. The two spent nearly three and half years collaborating on the work, in which they fully deduce, from [Newton's laws of motion](wikipedia://en/55212), the theory of the [moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon), the [equinoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox), and the [orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit) of [comets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet). Only 750 copies of the second edition were printed[^o9] although pirated copies from [Amsterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amsterdam) were also distributed to meet the demand for the work. As a reward to Cotes, he was given a share of the profits and 12 copies of his own. Cotes's original contribution to the work was a preface which supported the scientific superiority of Newton's principles over the then popular [vortex theory of gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_explanations_of_gravitation) advocated by [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525). Cotes concluded that the Newton's law of gravitation was confirmed by observation of celestial phenomena that were inconsistent with the vortex theory.[^o9]

## Mathematics {#Mathematics}

Cotes's major original work was in mathematics, especially in the fields of [integral calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_calculus), [logarithms](wikipedia://en/17860), and [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506). He published only one [scientific paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_paper) in his lifetime, titled *Logometria*, in which he successfully constructs the [logarithmic spiral](wikipedia://en/42373).[^o15][^o16] After his death, many of Cotes's mathematical papers were edited by his cousin Robert Smith and published in a book, *Harmonia mensurarum*.[^o9][^o17] Cotes's additional works were later published in [Thomas Simpson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Simpson)'s *The Doctrine and Application of Fluxions*.[^o15] Although Cotes's style was somewhat obscure, his systematic approach to [integration](wikipedia://en/15532) and mathematical theory was highly regarded by his peers. Cotes discovered an important theorem on the *n*-th [roots of unity](wikipedia://en/171950),[^o18] foresaw the method of [least squares](wikipedia://en/82359),[^o19] and discovered a method for integrating [rational fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_fraction) with [binomial](wikipedia://en/542399) [denominators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denominator).[^o15][^o20] He was also praised for his efforts in numerical methods, especially in [interpolation](wikipedia://en/14569) methods and his table construction techniques.[^o15] He was regarded as one of the few British mathematicians capable of following the powerful work of Sir Isaac Newton.

## Death and assessment {#Death_and_assessment}

Cotes died from a violent fever in [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge) in 1716 at the early age of 33. Isaac Newton remarked, "If he had lived we would have known something."[^o9]

That is, consider a circle having center *E* (at the origin of the (x, y) plane) and radius *CE*. Consider an angle *θ* with its vertex at *E* having the positive x-axis as one side and a radius *CE* as the other side. The perpendicular from the point *C* on the circle to the x-axis is the "sinus" *CX*; the line between the circle's center *E* and the point *X* at the foot of the perpendicular is *XE*, which is the "sinus of the complement to the quadrant" or "cosinus". The ratio between $EX + XC \sqrt{-1}$ and *CE* is thus $\cos \theta + \sqrt{-1} \sin \theta \$. In Cotes' terminology, the "measure" of a quantity is its natural logarithm, and the "modulus" is a conversion factor that transforms a measure of angle into circular arc length (here, the modulus is the radius (*CE*) of the circle). According to Cotes, the product of the modulus and the measure (logarithm) of the ratio, when multiplied by $\sqrt{-1}$, equals the length of the circular arc subtended by *θ*, which for any angle measured in radians is *CE* • *θ*. Thus, $\sqrt{-1} CE \ln{\left ( \cos \theta + \sqrt{-1} \sin \theta \right ) \ } = (CE) \theta$. This equation has the wrong sign: the factor of $\sqrt{-1}$ should be on the right side of the equation, not the left. If this change is made, then, after dividing both sides by *CE* and exponentiating both sides, the result is: $\cos \theta + \sqrt{-1} \sin \theta = e^{\sqrt{-1} \theta}$, which is Euler's formula.
