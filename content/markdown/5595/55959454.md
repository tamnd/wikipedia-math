# Intensity-duration-frequency curve

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Intensity-duration-frequency curve"}

An **intensity-duration-frequency curve** (**IDF curve**) is a mathematical function that relates the intensity of an event (e.g. [rainfall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainfall)) with its [duration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time) and [frequency](wikipedia://en/1039962) of occurrence.[^o2] Frequency is the inverse of the probability of occurrence. These curves are commonly used in [hydrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrological_modelling) for [flood forecasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flood#Flood_forecasting) and [civil engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_engineering#Water_resources_engineering) for [urban drainage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_runoff) design. However, the *IDF curves* are also analysed in [hydrometeorology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrometeorology) because of the interest in the *time concentration* or *time-structure* of the [rainfall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainfall),[^o3][^o4] but it is also possible to define IDF curves for drought events.[^o5][^o6] Additionally, applications of IDF curves to risk-based design are emerging outside of hydrometeorology, for example some authors developed IDF curves for food supply chain inflow shocks to US cities.[^o7]

:::

## Mathematical approaches {#Mathematical_approaches}

The IDF curves can take different mathematical expressions, theoretical or empirically fitted to observed event data. For each duration (e.g. 5, 10, 60, 120, 180 ... minutes), the [empirical cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/2690455) (ECDF), and a determined frequency or [return period](wikipedia://en/1039962) is set. Therefore, the empirical IDF curve is given by the union of the points of equal frequency of occurrence and different duration and intensity[^o9] Likewise, a theoretical or semi-empirical IDF curve is one whose mathematical expression is physically justified, but presents parameters that must be estimated by empirical fits.

### Empirical approaches {#Empirical_approaches}

There is a large number of empirical approaches that relate the intensity (*I*), the duration (*t*) and the return period (*p*), from fits to power laws such as:

- Sherman's formula,[^o11] with three parameters (*a*, *c* and *n*), which are a function of the return period, *p*:

$$I(t)=\frac a {(t+c)^n}$$

- Chow's formula,[^o12] also with three parameters (*a*, *c* and *n*), for a particular return period *p*:

$$I(t)= \frac a {t^n+c}$$

- [Power law](wikipedia://en/24522) according to Aparicio (1997),[^o13] with four parameters (*a*, *c*, *m* and *n*), already adjusted for all return periods of interest:

$$I(t,p)=a \cdot \frac{p^m}{(t+c)^n}$$

In [hydrometeorology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrometeorology), the simple power law (taking $\ c = 0$) is used as a measure of the time-structure of the rainfall:[^o3]

$$I(t)=\frac a {t^n} = I_o\left( \frac{t_o} t \right)^n$$

where $\ I_o$ is defined as an intensity of reference for a fixed time $\ t_o$, i.e. $\ a=I_o t_o^n$, and $\ n$ is a non-dimensional parameter known as ***n*-index**.[^o3][^o4] In a rainfall event, the equivalent to the IDF curve is called *Maximum Averaged Intensity* (MAI) curve.[^o14]

### Theoretical approaches {#Theoretical_approaches}

To get an **IDF curves** from a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543), $\ F(x)$ it is necessary to mathematically isolate the total amount or depth of the event$\ x$, which is directly related to the average intensity $\ I$ and the duration $\ t$, by the equation $\ x = It$, and since the [return period](wikipedia://en/1039962) $p$ is defined as the inverse of $\ 1 - F(x)$, the function $\ f(p)$ is found as the inverse of $\ F(x)$, according to:

$$I t = f(p) \quad \Leftarrow \quad p = \frac{1}{1-F(I t)}$$

- Power law with the return period, derived from the [Pareto distribution](wikipedia://en/53057), for a fixed duration $\ t$:

$$\ I(p) = kp^m \quad \Leftarrow \quad F(It) = 1 - \left( \frac{kt}{It} \right)^{1/m} = 1 - \frac{1}{p}$$

where the Pareto [distribution constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_constant) has been redefined as$\ k' = k t$, since it is a valid distribution for a specific duration of the event, it has been taken as$\ x = It$.

- Function derived from the [generalized Pareto distribution](wikipedia://en/53057), for a given duration $\ t$:

$$I(p) = \begin{cases} \mu + \frac \sigma m \cdot (p^m-1) \quad \Leftarrow \quad F(I) = 1 - \left(1+ \frac{m(I-\mu)}{\sigma}\right)^{-1/m} = 1 - \frac{1}{p} & \text{if } m > 0, \\ \quad \mu + \sigma\ln(p) \quad \quad \Leftarrow \quad F(I) = 1 - \exp \left( - \frac{I-\mu}{\sigma}\right) = 1 - \frac{1}{p} & \text{if } m = 0. \end{cases}$$

Note that for $\ m > 0$ y $\ \mu = \frac \sigma m$, the **generalized Pareto distribution** retrieves the simple form of the Pareto distribution, with $\ k' = \frac \sigma m$. However, with $\ m = 0$ the [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) is retrieved.

- Function deduced from the [Gumbel distribution](wikipedia://en/270827) and the [opposite Gumbel distribution](wikipedia://en/270827), for a given duration $\ t$:

$$I(p) = \mu + \sigma\ln \left( \ln \left( 1 - \frac{1}{p} \right) \right) \quad \Leftarrow \quad \quad F(I) = \exp \left( - \exp \left( - \frac{I-\mu} \sigma \right) \right) = 1 - \frac{1}{p}$$

$$I(p) = \mu + \sigma\ln(\ln p) \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \Leftarrow \quad \quad F(I) = 1 - \exp \left( - \exp \left( \frac{I-\mu}{\sigma} \right) \right) = 1 - \frac{1}{p}$$
