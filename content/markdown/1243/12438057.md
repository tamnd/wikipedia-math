# Frobenius covariant

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Frobenius covariant"}

In [matrix theory](wikipedia://en/20556859), the **Frobenius covariants** of a [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) $A$ are special polynomials of it, namely [projection](wikipedia://en/519182) matrices $F_{i}(A)$ associated with the [eigenvalues and eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue,_eigenvector_and_eigenspace) of $A$.[^o2] They are named after the mathematician [Ferdinand Frobenius](wikipedia://en/341048).

:::

Each covariant is a [projection](wikipedia://en/519182) on the [eigenspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue,_eigenvector_and_eigenspace) associated with the eigenvalue *λ*<sub>*i*</sub>. Frobenius covariants are the coefficients of [Sylvester's formula](wikipedia://en/8768758), which expresses a [function of a matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_function) $f(A)$ as a [matrix polynomial](wikipedia://en/15663294), namely a linear combination of that function's values on the eigenvalues of $A$.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Let $A$ be a [diagonalizable matrix](wikipedia://en/246325) with eigenvalues *λ*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *λ*<sub>*k*</sub>.

The Frobenius covariant $F_{i}(A)$, for *i* = 1,..., *k*, is the matrix

$$F_i (A) \equiv \prod_{j=1 \atop j \ne i}^k \frac{1}{\lambda_i-\lambda_j} (A - \lambda_j I)~.$$

It is essentially the [Lagrange polynomial](wikipedia://en/217523) with matrix argument. If the eigenvalue *λ*<sub>*i*</sub> is simple, then as an idempotent projection matrix to a one-dimensional subspace, $F_{i}(A)$ has a unit [trace](wikipedia://en/43270).

:::

## Computing the covariants {#Computing_the_covariants}

*Image: GeorgFrobenius.jpg.* [Ferdinand Georg Frobenius](wikipedia://en/341048) (1849–1917), German mathematician. His main interests were [elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/69939), [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309), and later [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890).

The Frobenius covariants of a matrix $A$ can be obtained from any [eigendecomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigendecomposition) $A = SDS^{-1}$, where $S$ is non-singular and $D$ is diagonal with $D_{i,i} = \lambda_{i}$. The matrix $S$ is defined up to multiplication on the right by a [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080). If $A$ has no multiple eigenvalues, then let *c*<sub>*i*</sub> be the $i$th right eigenvector of $A$, that is, the $i$th column of $S$; and let *r*<sub>*i*</sub> be the $i$th left eigenvector of $A$, namely the $i$th row of $S$<sup>−1</sup>. Then $F_{i}(A) = c_{i} r_{i}$. As a projection matrix, the Frobenius covariant satisfies the relation

$$F_i (A)F_j (A) = \delta_{ij}F_i (A),$$

which leads to $r_{i} c_{j} = \delta_{ij}.$

Given that $v$ and $w$ are the right and left vectors satisfying $w F_{i}(A) v 0$, the right and left eigenvectors of $A$ may be written as $c_{i} = N_{ci} F_{i}(A) v$ and $r_{i} = N_{ri} w F_{i}(A)$. The orthonormality of the eigenvectors gives one constraint for the normalization coefficients. The remaining freedom is related to the choice of representation for the matrix $S$.

If $A$ has an eigenvalue *λ*<sub>*i*</sub> appearing multiple times, then $F_{i}(A) = \Sigma_{j} c_{j} r_{j}$, where the sum is over all rows and columns associated with the eigenvalue *λ*<sub>*i*</sub>.[^o2]

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Consider the two-by-two matrix:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 4 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

This matrix has two eigenvalues, 5 and −2, which can be found by solving the [characteristic equation](wikipedia://en/218268). By virtue of the [Cayley–Hamilton theorem](wikipedia://en/173547), $(A - 5)(A + 2) = 0$.

The corresponding eigen decomposition is

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1/7 \\ 4 & -1/7 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1/7 \\ 4 & -1/7 \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1/7 \\ 4 & -1/7 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1/7 & 1/7 \\ 4 & -3 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Hence the Frobenius covariants, manifestly projections, are

$$\begin{array}{rl} F_1(A) &= c_1 r_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1/7 & 1/7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3/7 & 3/7 \\ 4/7 & 4/7 \end{bmatrix} = F_1^2(A)\\ F_2(A) &= c_2 r_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1/7 \\ -1/7 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4/7 & -3/7 \\ -4/7 & 3/7 \end{bmatrix}=F_2^2(A) ~, \end{array}$$

with

$$F_1(A) F_2(A) = 0 , \qquad F_1(A) + F_2(A) = I ~.$$

Note $\operatorname{tr}F_{1}(A) = \operatorname{tr}F_{2} (A)= 1$, as required.

:::

## Generalization {#Generalization}

When a square matrix $A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$ has repeated eigenvalues (such as a [defective matrix](wikipedia://en/5302952) or a diagonalizable one), the standard definition of Frobenius covariants based on Lagrange polynomials becomes undefined due to division by zero.

In this general case, let $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$ be the distinct eigenvalues of $A$, each with an [algebraic multiplicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_multiplicity) of $m_i + 1$. The generalized Frobenius covariants $A_i \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$ are projection matrices ($A_i^2 = A_i$) that project $\mathbb{C}^n$ onto the [generalized eigenspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_eigenspace) corresponding to $\lambda_i$ along the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) of the remaining generalized eigenspaces, satisfying $\sum_{i=1}^k A_i = I$.

The generalized Frobenius covariants $A_i$ can be constructed using several equivalent methods.

### Construction via Hermite interpolation {#Construction_via_Hermite_interpolation}

To generalize the projection matrices for cases with repeated eigenvalues, one can use [Hermite interpolating polynomials](wikipedia://en/2085700). The generalized Frobenius covariant $A_i$ is evaluated by applying the polynomial to $A$ as a matrix polynomial:

$$A_i = f_{i,0}(A),$$

where $f_{i,0}(\lambda)$ is the degree-0 [Hermite interpolating basis polynomial](wikipedia://en/2085700#Basis_polynomials) corresponding to node $\lambda_i$ satisfying the interpolation conditions:

$$f_{i,0}^{(j)}(\lambda_r) = \delta_{ir}\delta_{j0} \quad (1 \le r \le k, \; 0 \le j \le m_r),$$

where $\delta_{a b}$ denotes the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890).

Let $f_{i,j}(\lambda)$ denote the $j$-th order derivative Hermite interpolating basis polynomial corresponding to node $\lambda_i$ for $0 \le j \le m_i$. In terms of the [Jordan–Chevalley decomposition](wikipedia://en/11578785), applying a higher-order basis polynomial $f_{i,j}(\lambda)$ to matrix $A$ isolates the $j$-th power of the [nilpotent matrix](wikipedia://en/1167800) $N_i = A_i (A - \lambda_i I)$ associated with the Jordan blocks of $\lambda_i$ (which is not a projection matrix for $j > 0$). When $j = 0$, the 0th power of the nilpotent matrix acts as the identity on that generalized eigenspace, reducing $f_{i,0}(A)$ to the projection matrix $A_i$.

The degree-0 Hermite interpolating basis polynomial $f_{i,0}(\lambda)$ can be constructed using any standard Hermite interpolation method, such as [divided differences with confluent nodes](wikipedia://en/2085700#Using_divided_differences), [solving a confluent Vandermonde system](wikipedia://en/2085700#A_perspective_from_linear_algebra), or [via the Chinese remainder theorem](wikipedia://en/2085700#Basis_polynomials_via_Chinese_remainder_theorem).

::: {#o12 .statement cue=named-heading name="Closed-form explicit formula"}

#### Closed-form explicit formula {#Closed-form_explicit_formula}

Based on the [explicit formula for degree-0 Hermite interpolating basis polynomials](wikipedia://en/2085700#Basis_polynomials_in_closed_form), define the complementary factor polynomial $q_i(\lambda)$ for eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ as $q_i(\lambda) = \prod_{r \neq i} (\lambda - \lambda_r)^{m_r + 1}$. The degree-0 Hermite interpolating basis polynomial $f_{i,0}(\lambda)$ can be expressed directly in closed form by expanding the local Taylor polynomial:

$$f_{i,0}(\lambda) = \left( \sum_{s=0}^{m_i} \frac{1}{s!} \left. \left( \frac{1}{q_i(z)} \right)^{(s)} \right|_{z = \lambda_i} (\lambda - \lambda_i)^s \right) q_i(\lambda).$$

Evaluating this degree-0 Hermite interpolating basis polynomial $f_{i,0}(\lambda)$ at $A$ constructs the generalized Frobenius covariant $A_i = f_{i,0}(A)$.

:::

### Construction without Hermite interpolation {#Construction_without_Hermite_interpolation}

Besides constructing Hermite interpolating basis polynomials, the generalized Frobenius covariants $A_i$ can also be constructed directly using several non-interpolation methods.

#### Jordan normal form {#Jordan_normal_form}

This approach generalizes the eigendecomposition method for distinct eigenvalues described in § Computing the covariants to arbitrary matrices with non-trivial Jordan blocks. If the [Jordan normal form](wikipedia://en/312877) decomposition $A = P J P^{-1}$ is known, $A_i$ can be evaluated directly as $A_i = P E_i P^{-1}$, where $E_i$ is a block diagonal matrix whose blocks corresponding to $\lambda_i$ are identity matrices of matching dimensions, with all other entries set to zero.

#### Nullspace projection {#Nullspace_projection}

Without finding [Jordan chains](wikipedia://en/1137612#Jordan_chains), the generalized eigenspace $\mathcal{V}_i = \ker((A - \lambda_i I)^{m_i + 1})$ of dimension $d_i = m_i + 1$ and its complementary space $\mathcal{V}_i^c = \ker ( \prod_{r \neq i} (A - \lambda_r I)^{m_r + 1} )$ of dimension $n - d_i$ decompose the vector space into a direct sum $\mathbb{C}^n = \mathcal{V}_i \oplus \mathcal{V}_i^c$. In general, $\mathcal{V}_i$ and $\mathcal{V}_i^c$ are not orthogonal unless $A$ is a [normal matrix](wikipedia://en/170366).

Let $Q_{i,1} \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times d_i}$ be a matrix whose columns form a basis for $\mathcal{V}_i$, and $Q_{i,2} \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times (n - d_i)}$ be a matrix whose columns form a basis for $\mathcal{V}_i^c$. Assembling the partitioned basis matrix $Q_i = \begin{bmatrix} Q_{i,1} & Q_{i,2} \end{bmatrix}$ yields

$$A_i = Q_i \begin{bmatrix} I_{d_i} & 0 \\ 0 & 0_{n - d_i} \end{bmatrix} Q_i^{-1}.$$

#### Holomorphic matrix functional calculus {#Holomorphic_matrix_functional_calculus}

Under the [holomorphic matrix functional calculus](wikipedia://en/3131706#Functional_calculus_for_a_bounded_operator), the generalized Frobenius covariant $A_i$ can be defined directly without polynomial interpolation as a [contour integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contour_integral) of the [resolvent matrix](wikipedia://en/3378540) $(zI - A)^{-1}$:

$$A_i = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_{\Gamma_i} (zI - A)^{-1} \, dz,$$

where $\Gamma_i$ is a simple closed contour in the complex plane that encloses $\lambda_i$ in its interior while excluding all other eigenvalues of $A$.

:::
