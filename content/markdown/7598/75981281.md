# Graphic statics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Graphic statics"}

In a broad sense, **graphic statics** is the technique of solving particular practical problems of [statics](wikipedia://en/28767) using graphical means.[^n1] Actively used in the [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture) of the 19th century, the methods of graphic statics were largely abandoned in the second half of the 20th century, primarily due to widespread use of frame structures of [steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel_frame) and [reinforced concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforced_concrete) that facilitated analysis based on [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422). The beginning of the 21st century was marked by a "renaissance" of the technique driven by its addition to [computer-aided design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer-aided_design) tools thus enabling engineers to instantly visualize [form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Form_%28architecture%29) and [forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force).[^n2]

:::

## History {#History}

*Image: Analogy between an arch and a hanging chain and comparison to the dome of St Peter's Cathedral in Rome.png.* Poleni's analysis of the dome (right)

Markou and Ruan[^n2] trace the origins of the graphic statics to [da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Da_Vinci) and [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo) who used the graphical means to calculate the sum of forces, [Simon Stevin](wikipedia://en/72586)'s [parallelogram of forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallelogram_of_forces) and the 1725 introduction of the [force polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_polygon) and [funicular polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funicular_polygon) by [Pierre Varignon](wikipedia://en/1193511). [Giovanni Poleni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Poleni) used the graphical calculations (and [Robert Hooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hooke)'s analogy between the hanging chain and standing structure) while studying the dome of the [Saint Peter's Basilica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Peter's_Basilica) in Rome (1748). [Gabriel Lamé](wikipedia://en/52145) and [Émile Clapeyron](wikipedia://en/203175) studied of the dome of the [Saint Isaac's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Isaac's_Cathedral) with the help of the force and funicular polygons (1823).[^n2]

Finally, [Carl Culmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Culmann) had established the new discipline (and gave it a name) in his 1864 work *Die Graphische Statik*. Culmann was inspired by preceding work by [Jean-Victor Poncelet](wikipedia://en/764818) on earth pressure and *Lehrbuch der Statik* by [August Möbius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Möbius). The next twenty years saw rapid development of methods that involved, among others, major physicists like [James Clerk Maxwell](wikipedia://en/28989696) and [William Rankine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Rankine). In 1872 [Luigi Cremona](wikipedia://en/1846002) introduced the [Cremona diagram](wikipedia://en/14523851) to calculate [trusses](wikipedia://en/397263),[^n3] in 1873 Robert H. Bow established the "Bow's notation" that is still in use.[^n2] Graphic statics fell out of use in the 20th century, especially since construction methods, such as [concrete post and beam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete_frame), allowed for familiar numerical calculations. Access to powerful computation gave structural engineers new tools to compute stresses for shell structures such as [Finite element method](wikipedia://en/18233581).

While the method is not commonly used for construction today, graphic statics was proposed as an educational tool to teach intuition in engineering education.[^o9] It is employed in classes at [MIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT)[^o10] and [ETH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETH_Zurich).[^o11] for architecture and structural engineering students.

## Concepts {#Concepts}

### Polygon of forces {#Polygon_of_forces}

*Image: Polygon of forces.png.* A force polygon for the forces P<sub>1</sub> to P<sub>6</sub> applied to point O

To graphically determine the resultant force of multiple forces, the acting forces can be arranged as [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034) of a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) by attaching the beginning of one force vector to the end of another in an arbitrary order. Then the vector value of the resultant force would be determined by the missing edge of the polygon.[^o14] In the diagram, the forces P<sub>1</sub> to P<sub>6</sub> are applied to the point O. The polygon is constructed starting with P<sub>1</sub> and P<sub>2</sub> using the [parallelogram of forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallelogram_of_forces) ([vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) a). The process is repeated (adding P<sub>3</sub> yields the vertex b, etc.). The remaining edge of the polygon O-e represents the resultant force R.

In the case of two applied forces, their sum ([resultant force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resultant_force)) can be found graphically using a [parallelogram of forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallelogram_of_forces).
