# Prevalent and shy sets

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Prevalent and shy sets"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the notions of **prevalence and shyness** are notions of "[almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688)" and "[measure zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_zero)" that are well-suited to the study of [infinite](wikipedia://en/20110824)-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) spaces and make use of the translation-invariant [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on finite-dimensional real spaces. The term "shy" was suggested by the [American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [John Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

### Prevalence and shyness {#Prevalence_and_shyness}

Let $V$ be a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) and let $S$ be a [Borel-measurable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_sigma_algebra) [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $V.$ $S$ is said to be **prevalent** if there exists a finite-dimensional subspace $P$ of $V,$ called the **probe set**, such that for all $v \in V$ we have $v + p \in S$ for $\lambda_P$-[almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) $p \in P,$ where $\lambda_P$ denotes the $\dim (P)$-dimensional Lebesgue measure on $P.$ Put another way, for every $v \in V,$ Lebesgue-almost every point of the [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) $v + P$ lies in $S.$

A non-Borel subset of $V$ is said to be prevalent if it contains a prevalent Borel subset.

A Borel subset of $V$ is said to be **shy** if its [complement](wikipedia://en/54347) is prevalent; a non-Borel subset of $V$ is said to be shy if it is contained within a shy Borel subset.

An alternative, and slightly more general, definition is to define a set $S$ to be shy if there exists a [transverse measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transverse_measure) for $S$ (other than the [trivial measure](wikipedia://en/10122069)).

### Local prevalence and shyness {#Local_prevalence_and_shyness}

A subset $S$ of $V$ is said to be **locally shy** if every point $v \in V$ has a [neighbourhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighbourhood_%28topology%29) $N_v$ whose [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) with $S$ is a shy set. $S$ is said to be **locally prevalent** if its complement is locally shy.

## Theorems involving prevalence and shyness {#Theorems_involving_prevalence_and_shyness}

- If $S$ is shy, then so is every subset of $S$ and every translate of $S.$
- Every shy Borel set $S$ admits a transverse measure that is finite and has [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [support](wikipedia://en/6591796). Furthermore, this measure can be chosen so that its support has arbitrarily small [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007).
- Any finite or [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of shy sets is also shy. Analogously, countable intersection of prevalent sets is prevalent.
- Any shy set is also locally shy. If $V$ is a [separable space](wikipedia://en/27855), then every locally shy subset of $V$ is also shy.
- A subset $S$ of $n$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$ is shy [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it has Lebesgue measure zero.
- Any prevalent subset $S$ of $V$ is [dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_%28topology%29) in $V.$
- If $V$ is infinite-dimensional, then every compact subset of $V$ is shy.

In the following, "almost every" is taken to mean that the stated property holds of a prevalent subset of the space in question.

- Almost every [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) from the [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $[0, 1]$ into the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) $\mathbb{R}$ is [nowhere differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nowhere_differentiable); here the space $V$ is $C([0, 1]; \mathbb{R})$ with the topology induced by the [supremum norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum_norm).
- Almost every function $f$ in the [$L^p$ space](wikipedia://en/45194) $L^1([0, 1]; \mathbb{R})$ has the property that $$\int_0^1 f(x) \, \mathrm{d} x \neq 0.$$ Clearly, the same property holds for the spaces of $k$-times [differentiable functions](wikipedia://en/330206) $C^k([0, 1]; \mathbb{R}).$
- For $1 < p \leq +\infty,$ almost every sequence $a = \left(a_n\right)_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \in \ell^p$ has the property that the series $$\sum_{n \in \mathbb{N}} a_n$$ diverges.
- Prevalence version of the [Whitney embedding theorem](wikipedia://en/477578): Let $M$ be a compact [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) of class $C^1$ and dimension $d$ contained in $\mathbb{R}^n.$ For $1 \leq k \leq +\infty,$ almost every $C^k$ function $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^{2d+1}$ is an [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119) of $M.$
- If $A$ is a compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ with [Hausdorff dimension](wikipedia://en/14294) $d,$ $m \geq ,$ and $1 \leq k \leq +\infty,$ then, for almost every $C^k$ function $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m,$ $f(A)$ also has Hausdorff dimension $d.$
- For $1 \leq k \leq +\infty,$ almost every $C^k$ function $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ has the property that all of its [periodic points](wikipedia://en/4941621) are hyperbolic. In particular, the same is true for all the period $p$ points, for any integer $p.$
