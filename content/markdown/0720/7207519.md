# Functional square root

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Functional square root"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **functional square root** (sometimes called a **half iterate**) is a [square root](wikipedia://en/29208#In_rings_in_general) of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) with respect to the operation of [function composition](wikipedia://en/195947). In other words, a functional square root of a function $g$ is a function $f$ satisfying $f(f(x)) = g(x)$ [for all](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/For_all) $x$.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

## Notation {#Notation}

Notations expressing that $f$ is a functional square root of $g$ are $f = g_{[1/2]}$ and $f = g_{1/2}$, or rather $f = g^{1/2}$ (see [Iterated function](wikipedia://en/1448702#Fractional_iterates_and_flows,_and_negative_iterates)), although this leaves the usual ambiguity with taking the function to that power in the multiplicative sense, just as *f ² = f ∘ f* can be misinterpreted as *x ↦ f*(*x*)².

:::

## History {#History}

- The functional square root of the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) (now known as a [half-exponential function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-exponential_function)) was studied by [Hellmuth Kneser](wikipedia://en/1368525) in 1950,[^o5] later providing the basis for extending [tetration](wikipedia://en/482379) to non-integer heights in 2017.[^o6]
- The solutions of $f(f(x)) = x$ over $\mathbb{R}$ (the [involutions](wikipedia://en/293450) of the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438)) were first studied by [Charles Babbage](wikipedia://en/5698) in 1815, and this equation is called Babbage's [functional equation](wikipedia://en/364377).[^o7] A particular solution is $f(x) = (b - x)/(1 + cx)$ for $bc \ne -1$. Babbage noted that for any given solution $f$, its [functional conjugate](wikipedia://en/5506149) $\Psi^{-1}\circ f \circ\ \Psi$ by an arbitrary [invertible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible_function) function $\Psi$ is also a solution. In other words, the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of all invertible functions on the real line [acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) on the subset consisting of solutions to Babbage's functional equation by [conjugation](wikipedia://en/49176).

## Solutions {#Solutions}

A systematic procedure to produce *arbitrary* functional $n$-roots (including arbitrary real, negative, and infinitesimal $n$) of functions $g: \mathbb{C}\rightarrow \mathbb{C}$ relies on the solutions of [Schröder's equation](wikipedia://en/5834541).[^o9][^o10][^o11] Infinitely many trivial solutions exist when the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of a root function *f* is allowed to be sufficiently larger than that of *g*.

::: {#o13 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- $f(x) = 2x^{2}$ is a functional square root of $g(x) = 8x^{4}$.
- A functional square root of the $n$th [Chebyshev polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chebyshev_polynomial), $g(x)=T_n(x)$, is $f(x) = \cos{(\sqrt{n}\arccos(x))}$, which in general is not a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000).
- $f(x) = x / (\sqrt{2} + x(1-\sqrt{2}))$ is a functional square root of $g(x)=x / (2-x)$.

Half-iterate (orange), i.e., the sine's functional square root; the functional square root of that, the quarter-iterate (black) above it, and further fractional iterates up to the 1/64th iterate. The functions below sine are six integral iterates below it, starting with the second iterate (red) and ending with the 64th iterate. The green envelope triangle represents the limiting null iterate, the sawtooth function serving as the starting point leading to the sine function. The dashed line is the negative first iterate, i.e. the [inverse](wikipedia://en/14907) of sine ([arcsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcsin)).

$\sin_{[2]}(x) = \sin(\sin(x))$ \[red curve\]

$\sin_{[1]}(x) = \sin(x) = \operatorname{rin}(\operatorname{rin}(x))$ \[blue curve\]

$\sin_{[\tfrac{1}{2}]}(x) = \operatorname{rin}(x) = \operatorname{qin}(\operatorname{qin}(x))$ \[orange curve\], although this is not unique, the opposite $- \operatorname{rin}$ being a solution of $\sin = \operatorname{rin} \circ \operatorname{rin}$, too.

$\sin_{[\tfrac{1}{4}]}(x) = \operatorname{qin}(x)$ \[black curve above the orange curve\]

$\sin_{[-1]}(x) = \arcsin(x)$ \[dashed curve\]
