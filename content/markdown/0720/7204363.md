# Interchange of limiting operations

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Interchange of limiting operations"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the study of **interchange of limiting operations** is one of the major concerns of [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), in that two given limiting operations, say *L* and *M*, cannot be *assumed* to give the same result when applied in either order. One of the historical sources for this theory is the study of [trigonometric series](wikipedia://en/4099125).

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Formulation"}

## Formulation {#Formulation}

In symbols, the assumption

*LM* = *ML*,

where the [left-hand side](wikipedia://en/166352) means that *M* is applied first, then *L*, and *vice versa* on the [right-hand side](wikipedia://en/166352), is not a valid [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) between [mathematical operators](wikipedia://en/22213), under all circumstances and for all operands. An algebraist would say that the operations do not [commute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity). The approach taken in analysis is somewhat different. Conclusions that assume limiting operations do 'commute' are called *formal*. The analyst tries to delineate conditions under which such conclusions are valid; in other words [mathematical rigour](wikipedia://en/655974) is established by the specification of some set of sufficient conditions for the formal analysis to hold. This approach justifies, for example, the notion of [uniform convergence](wikipedia://en/50652).[^o5] It is relatively rare for such sufficient conditions to be also necessary, so that a sharper piece of analysis may extend the domain of validity of formal results.

Professionally speaking, therefore, analysts push the envelope of techniques, and expand the meaning of *[well-behaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-behaved)* for a given context. [G. H. Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599) wrote that "The problem of deciding whether two given limit operations are commutative is one of the most important in mathematics".[^o6] An opinion apparently not in favour of the piece-wise approach, but of leaving analysis at the level of [heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic), was that of [Richard Courant](wikipedia://en/715238).

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Examples abound, one of the simplest being that for a [double sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_sequence) *a*<sub>*m*,*n*</sub>: it is not necessarily the case that the operations of taking the limits as *m* → ∞ and as *n* → ∞ can be freely interchanged.[^o9] For example take

*a*<sub>*m*,*n*</sub> = 2<sup>*m* − *n*</sup>

in which taking the limit first with respect to *n* gives 0, and with respect to *m* gives ∞.

Many of the fundamental results of [infinitesimal calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) also fall into this category: the [symmetry of partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/412094), [differentiation under the integral sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiation_under_the_integral_sign), and [Fubini's theorem](wikipedia://en/255245) deal with the interchange of [differentiation](wikipedia://en/7921) and [integration](wikipedia://en/15532) operators.

One of the major reasons why the [Lebesgue integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_integration) is used is that theorems exist, such as the [dominated convergence theorem](wikipedia://en/351853), that give sufficient conditions under which integration and limit operation can be interchanged. Necessary and sufficient conditions for this interchange were discovered by [Federico Cafiero](wikipedia://en/38489810).[^o10]

:::

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="List of related theorems"}

## List of related theorems {#List_of_related_theorems}

- Interchange of limits:
  - [Moore-Osgood theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore-Osgood_theorem)
- Interchange of limit and infinite summation:
  - [Tannery's theorem](wikipedia://en/59113779)
- Interchange of limit and derivatives:
  - If a sequence of functions $(f_n)$ converges at at least one point and the derivatives converge uniformly, then $(f_n)$ converges uniformly as well, say to some function $f$ and the limiting function of the derivatives is $f'$.[^o13] While this is often shown using the [mean value theorem](wikipedia://en/19662) for real-valued functions, the same method can be applied for higher-dimensional functions by using the [mean value inequality](wikipedia://en/19662#Mean_value_theorem_for_vector-valued_functions) instead.
- Interchange of partial derivatives:
  - [Schwarz's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwarz's_theorem)
- Interchange of integrals:
  - [Fubini's theorem](wikipedia://en/255245)
- Interchange of limit and integral:
  - [Dominated convergence theorem](wikipedia://en/351853)
  - [Vitali convergence theorem](wikipedia://en/13197969)
  - [Fichera convergence theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fichera_convergence_theorem)
  - [Cafiero convergence theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cafiero_convergence_theorem)
  - [Fatou's lemma](wikipedia://en/291614)
  - [Monotone convergence theorem for integrals (Beppo Levi's lemma)](wikipedia://en/146263#Beppo_Levi's_lemma)
- Interchange of derivative and integral:
  - [Leibniz integral rule](wikipedia://en/2558855)
