# Schlegel diagram

*Image: Polyhedral schlegel diagrams.svg.* Examples colored by the number of sides on each face. Yellow [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654), red [squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29), and green [pentagons](wikipedia://en/20741014).

*Image: Hypercube.svg.* A [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112) projected into 3-space as a Schlegel diagram. There are 8 [cubic cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_cellulation) visible: the outer cell into which the others are projected, one below each of the six exterior faces, and one in the center.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Schlegel diagram"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **Schlegel diagram** is a [projection](wikipedia://en/3909097) of a [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) from $\mathbb{R}^d$ into $\mathbb{R}^{d-1}$ through a [point](wikipedia://en/593693) just outside one of its [facets](wikipedia://en/7186647). The resulting entity is a [polytopal subdivision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytopal_subdivision) of the facet in $\mathbb{R}^{d-1}$ that, together with the original facet, is combinatorially equivalent to the original polytope. The diagram is named for [Victor Schlegel](wikipedia://en/34212179), who in 1886 introduced this tool for studying [combinatorial](wikipedia://en/5170) and [topological](wikipedia://en/29954) properties of polytopes. In [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) 3, a Schlegel diagram is a projection of a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) into a [plane figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_figure); in [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) 4, it is a projection of a [4-polytope](wikipedia://en/24979) to [3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-space). As such, Schlegel diagrams are commonly used as a means of visualizing [four-dimensional](wikipedia://en/1364622) polytopes.

:::

## Construction {#Construction}

The most elementary Schlegel diagram, that of a polyhedron, was described by [Duncan Sommerville](wikipedia://en/8276753) as follows:[^o4]

A very useful method of representing a convex polyhedron is by plane projection. If it is projected from any external point, since each ray cuts it twice, it will be represented by a polygonal area divided twice over into polygons. It is always possible by suitable choice of the centre of projection to make the projection of one face completely contain the projections of all the other faces. This is called a *Schlegel diagram* of the polyhedron. The Schlegel diagram completely represents the morphology of the polyhedron. It is sometimes convenient to project the polyhedron from a vertex; this vertex is projected to infinity and does not appear in the diagram, the edges through it are represented by lines drawn outwards.

Sommerville also considers the case of a [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) in four dimensions:[^o5] "The Schlegel diagram of simplex in S<sub>4</sub> is a [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606) divided into four tetrahedra." More generally, a polytope in n-dimensions has a Schlegel diagram constructed by a [perspective projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perspective_projection) viewed from a point outside of the polytope, above the center of a facet. All vertices and edges of the polytope are projected onto a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) of that facet. If the polytope is convex, a point near the facet will exist which maps the facet outside, and all other facets inside, so no edges need to cross in the projection.

## See also {#See_also}

- [Net (polyhedron)](wikipedia://en/1148129) – A different approach for visualization by lowering the dimension of a polytope is to build a net, disconnecting facets, and *unfolding* until the facets can exist on a single hyperplane. This maintains the geometric scale and shape, but makes the topological connections harder to see.
