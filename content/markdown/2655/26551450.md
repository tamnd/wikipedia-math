# Martin Feinberg

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Martin Feinberg"}

**Martin Feinberg** is an American [chemical engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_engineering) and [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) known for his work in [chemical reaction network theory](wikipedia://en/36996546).

:::

## Life {#Life}

Born in New York, Feinberg received his undergraduate degree in chemical engineering from The [Cooper Union for the Advancement of Science and Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooper_Union_for_the_Advancement_of_Science_and_Art) in 1962. A year later, he obtained his master's degree from [Purdue University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_University). In 1968, he received his PhD degree from [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University). The subject of the doctoral thesis is [fluid mechanics](wikipedia://en/2684988) and the advisor is William Schowalter. After completing the PhD, he went to work at the [University of Rochester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Rochester), Rochester, NY, where he was a professor of chemical engineering until 1997. He then moved to The [Ohio State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_State_University), where he serves as Richard M. Morrow Professor of Chemical Engineering and professor of mathematics. Feinberg was a member of the editorial board of the [Archive for Rational Mechanics and Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archive_for_Rational_Mechanics_and_Analysis) from 1978–1991.

## Research {#Research}

Together with F. J. M. Horn and Roy Jackson, Feinberg created [chemical reaction network theory](wikipedia://en/36996546), a field of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) that connects the graphical and algebraic structure of chemical reaction networks with their [dynamic behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_kinetics). He is best known for stating and proving the deficiency zero theorem (together with Horn and Jackson) and the deficiency one theorem. He has also articulated complete necessary and sufficient conditions for detailed balancing in [mass-action systems](wikipedia://en/318051). More recently, Feinberg has turned his attention to problems arising from biology. Together with Gheorghe Craciun, he developed the theory of injective reaction networks and explored its implications for [biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry). A current research focus (together with Guy Shinar) is the application of chemical reaction network theory to questions of robustness in biochemical reaction networks. He has also worked with Richard Lavine on foundations of classical [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952). Feinberg is the author of "Foundations of Chemical Reaction Network Theory," published in 2019 by Springer in its Applied Mathematical Sciences series.
