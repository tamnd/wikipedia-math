# Cahn–Hilliard equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cahn–Hilliard equation"}

The **Cahn–Hilliard equation** (after [John W. Cahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_W._Cahn) and [John E. Hilliard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_E._Hilliard))[^o2] is an [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) of [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416) which describes the process of [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28matter%29) separation, [spinodal decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinodal_decomposition), by which the two [components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Component_%28thermodynamics%29) of a binary [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid) spontaneously separate and form domains pure in each component. If $c$ is the concentration of the fluid, with $c=\pm1$ indicating both domains, then the equation is written as

$$\frac{\partial c}{\partial t} = D\nabla^2\left(c^3-c-\gamma\nabla^2 c\right),$$

where $D$ is a [diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion) coefficient with units of $\rm{L}^2/\rm{T}$, $\sqrt{\gamma}$ gives the length of the transition regions between the domains, $\partial/{\partial t}$ is the partial [time derivative](wikipedia://en/1783069) and $\nabla^2$ is the [Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian) in $n$ dimensions (typically three). Additionally, the quantity $\mu \equiv c^3-c-\gamma\nabla^2 c$ is sometimes identified as a [chemical potential](wikipedia://en/218628).

:::

It is related to the [Allen–Cahn equation](wikipedia://en/37054750), as well as the stochastic Allen–Cahn and the stochastic Cahn–Hilliard equations.

## Features and applications {#Features_and_applications}

Of interest to mathematicians is the existence of a unique solution of the Cahn–Hilliard equation, given by smooth initial data. The proof relies essentially on the existence of a [Lyapunov functional](wikipedia://en/352267). Specifically, if we identify

$$F[c]=\int d^n x \left[\frac{1}{4}\left(c^2-1\right)^2+\frac{\gamma}{2}\left|\nabla c\right|^2\right],$$

as a free energy functional, then

$$\frac{d F}{dt} = -\int d^n x \left|\nabla\mu\right|^2,$$

so that the free energy does not grow in time. This also indicates segregation into domains is the [asymptotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic) outcome of the evolution of this equation.

In real experiments, the segregation of an initially mixed binary fluid into domains is observed. The segregation is characterized by the following facts.

*Image: CahnHilliard g0.5.gif.* Evolution of random initial data under the Cahn–Hilliard equation with $\gamma=0.5$ and $C=0$ (equal amounts of each phase), demonstrating phase separation.

- There is a transition layer between the segregated domains, with a profile given by the function $c(x) = \tanh\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2\gamma}}\right),$ and hence a typical width $\sqrt{\gamma}$ because this function is an equilibrium solution of the Cahn–Hilliard equation.
- Of interest also is the fact that the segregated domains grow in time as a [power law](wikipedia://en/24522). That is, if $L(t)$ is a typical domain size, then $L(t)\propto t^{1/3}$. This is the Lifshitz–Slyozov law, and has been proved rigorously for the Cahn–Hilliard equation and observed in numerical simulations and real experiments on binary fluids.
- The Cahn–Hilliard equation has the form of a [conservation law](wikipedia://en/6956), $\frac{\partial c}{\partial t} = -\nabla \cdot \mathbf{j}(x),$ with $\mathbf{j}(x)=-D\nabla \mu$. Thus the phase separation process conserves the total concentration $C=\int d^n x c\left(x,t\right)$, so that $\frac{dC}{dt}=0$.
- *Image: CahnHilliard OstwaldRipening.gif.* Evolution of random initial data with $\gamma=0.5$ and $C=-0.2$ (60/40 mix of the blue and red phases, respectively), demonstrating [Ostwald ripening](wikipedia://en/3508610) When one phase is significantly more abundant, the Cahn–Hilliard equation can show the phenomenon known as [Ostwald ripening](wikipedia://en/3508610), where the minority phase forms spherical droplets, and the smaller droplets are absorbed through diffusion into the larger ones.

The Cahn–Hilliard equation finds applications in diverse fields: in complex fluids and soft matter (interfacial fluid flow, polymer science and in industrial applications). The solution of the Cahn–Hilliard equation for a binary mixture demonstrated to coincide well with the solution of a [Stefan problem](wikipedia://en/679186) and the model of Thomas and Windle.[^o4] Of interest to researchers at present is the coupling of the phase separation of the Cahn–Hilliard equation to the [Navier–Stokes equations](wikipedia://en/48395) of fluid flow.
