# R. Tyrrell Rockafellar

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="R. Tyrrell Rockafellar"}

**Ralph Tyrrell Rockafellar** (born February 10, 1935) is an American mathematician and one of the leading scholars in [optimization theory](wikipedia://en/52033) and related fields of [analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) and [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/24338041). He is the author of four major books including the landmark text "Convex Analysis" (1970),[^o3] which has been cited more than 27,000 times according to Google Scholar and remains the standard reference on the subject, and "Variational Analysis" (1998, with [Roger J-B Wets](wikipedia://en/26298695)) for which the authors received the [Frederick W. Lanchester Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_W._Lanchester_Prize) from the [Institute for Operations Research and the Management Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Operations_Research_and_the_Management_Sciences) (INFORMS).

:::

He is professor emeritus at the departments of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969) at the [University of Washington, Seattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Washington,_Seattle).

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Ralph Tyrrell Rockafellar was born in [Milwaukee, Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milwaukee,_Wisconsin).[^o5] He is named after his father Ralph Rockafellar, with Tyrrell being his mother's maiden name. Since his mother was fond of the name Terry, the parents adopted it as a nickname for Tyrrell and soon everybody referred to him as Terry.[^o6]

Rockafellar is a distant relative of the American business magnate and philanthropist [John D. Rockefeller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_D._Rockefeller). They both can trace their ancestors back to two brothers named Rockenfelder that came to America from the Rhineland-Pfaltz region of Germany in 1728. Soon the spelling of the family name evolved, resulting in Rockafellar, Rockefeller, and many other versions of the name.[^o7]

Rockafellar moved to [Cambridge, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge,_Massachusetts) to attend [Harvard College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_College) in 1953. Majoring in mathematics, he graduated from Harvard in 1957 with [summa cum laude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_cum_laude). He was also elected for the [Phi Beta Kappa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phi_Beta_Kappa) honor society. Rockafellar was a [Fulbright Scholar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulbright_Scholar) at the [University of Bonn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bonn) in 1957–58 and completed a [Master of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Science) degree at [Marquette University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marquette_University) in 1959. Formally under the guidance of Professor [Garrett Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/1180916), Rockafellar completed his [Doctor of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) degree in mathematics from [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) in 1963 with the dissertation "Convex Functions and Dual Extremum Problems." However, at the time there was little interest in convexity and optimization at Harvard and Birkhoff was neither involved with the research nor familiar with the subject.[^o8] The dissertation was inspired by the duality theory of [linear programming](wikipedia://en/43730) developed by [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942), which Rockafellar learned about through volumes of recent papers compiled by [Albert W. Tucker](wikipedia://en/935841) at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University).[^o9] Rockafellar's dissertation together with the contemporary work by [Jean-Jacques Moreau](wikipedia://en/34674927) in France are regarded as the birth of [convex analysis](wikipedia://en/3237201).

## Career {#Career}

After graduating from Harvard, Rockafellar became Assistant Professor of Mathematics at the [University of Texas, Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas,_Austin), where he also was affiliated with the Department of Computer Science. After two years, he moved to [University of Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Washington) in Seattle where he filled joint positions in the Departments of Mathematics and Applied Mathematics from 1966 to 2003 when he retired. He is presently Professor Emeritus at the university. He has held adjunct positions at the [University of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Florida) and [Hong Kong Polytechnic University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong_Polytechnic_University).

Rockafellar was a visiting professor at the Mathematics Institute, Copenhagen (1964), [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) (1965–66), [University of Grenoble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Grenoble) (1973–74), [University of Colorado, Boulder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Colorado,_Boulder) (1978), International Institute of Applied Systems Analysis, Vienna (1980–81), [University of Pisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pisa) (1991), University of Paris-Dauphine (1996), University of Pau (1997), Keio University (2009), [National University of Singapore](wikipedia://en/286880) (2011), [University of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Vienna) (2011), and [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University) (2012).

Rockafellar received the [Dantzig Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dantzig_Prize) from the [Society for Industrial and Applied Mathematics](wikipedia://en/1883376) (SIAM) and the [Mathematical Optimization Society](wikipedia://en/5751407) in 1982, delivered the 1992 [John von Neumann Lecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann_Lecture), received with [Roger J-B Wets](wikipedia://en/26298695) the [Frederick W. Lanchester Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_W._Lanchester_Prize) from the [Institute for Operations Research and the Management Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Operations_Research_and_the_Management_Sciences) (INFORMS) in 1998 for the book "Variational Analysis." In 1999, he was awarded the [John von Neumann Theory Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann_Theory_Prize) from INFORMS. He was elected to the 2002 class of [Fellows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow) of INFORMS.[^o11] He is the recipient of honorary doctoral degrees from University of Groningen (1984), University of Montpellier (1995), University of Chile (1998), and University of Alicante (2000). The [Institute for Scientific Information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Scientific_Information) (ISI) lists Rockafellar as a [highly cited researcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Scientific_Information_highly_cited_researcher).[^o12]

## Research {#Research}

Rockafellar's research is motivated by the goal of organizing mathematical ideas and concepts into robust frameworks that yield new insights and relations.[^o14] This approach is most salient in his seminal book "Variational Analysis" (1998, with [Roger J-B Wets](wikipedia://en/26298695)), where numerous threads developed in the areas of convex analysis, nonlinear analysis, calculus of variation, mathematical optimization, equilibrium theory, and control systems were brought together to produce a unified approach to variational problems in finite dimensions. These various fields of study are now referred to as [variational analysis](wikipedia://en/40237498). In particular, the text dispenses of differentiability as a necessary property in many areas of analysis and embraces nonsmoothness, set-valuedness, and extended real-valuedness, while still developing far-reaching calculus rules.

### Contributions to Mathematics {#Contributions_to_Mathematics}

The approach of extending the real line with the values [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824) and negative infinity and then allowing (convex) functions to take these values can be traced back to Rockafellar's dissertation and, independently, the work by [Jean-Jacques Moreau](wikipedia://en/34674927) around the same time. The central role of [set-valued mappings](wikipedia://en/12506030) (also called multivalued functions) was also recognized in Rockafellar's dissertation and, in fact, the standard notation ∂*f*(*x*) for the set of [subgradients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subgradient) of a function *f* at *x* originated there.

Rockafellar contributed to nonsmooth analysis by extending the rule of [Fermat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat), which characterizes solutions of [optimization problems](wikipedia://en/1126536), to composite problems using subgradient calculus and variational geometry and thereby bypassing the [implicit function theorem](wikipedia://en/581005). The approach broadens the notion of [Lagrange multipliers](wikipedia://en/159974) to settings beyond smooth equality and inequality systems. In his doctoral dissertation and numerous later publications, Rockafellar developed a general duality theory based on [convex conjugate](wikipedia://en/1001490) functions that centers on embedding a problem within a family of problems obtained by a perturbation of parameters. This encapsulates [linear programming](wikipedia://en/43730) duality and Lagrangian duality, and extends to general convex problems as well as nonconvex ones, especially when combined with an augmentation.

### Contributions to Applications {#Contributions_to_Applications}

Rockafellar also worked on applied problems and computational aspects. In the 1970s, he contributed to the development of the proximal point method, which underpins several successful algorithms including the [proximal gradient method](wikipedia://en/39587805) often used in statistical applications. He placed the analysis of expectation functions in [stochastic programming](wikipedia://en/501898) on solid footing by defining and analyzing normal integrands. Rockafellar also contributed to the analysis of [control systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_system) and [general equilibrium theory](wikipedia://en/45938) in economics.

Since the late 1990s, Rockafellar has been actively involved with organizing and expanding the mathematical concepts for risk assessment and decision making in [financial engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_engineering) and [reliability engineering](wikipedia://en/1724836). This includes examining the mathematical properties of [risk measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk_measures) and coining the terms "conditional value-at-risk", in 2000 as well as "superquantile" and "buffered failure probability" in 2010, which either coincide with or are closely related to [expected shortfall](wikipedia://en/8307819).
