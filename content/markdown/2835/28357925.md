# Calculus of moving surfaces

*Image: Dannebrog.jpg.* The surface of a flag in the wind is an example of a deforming manifold.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Calculus of moving surfaces"}

The **calculus of moving surfaces** (**CMS**) [^o2] is an extension of the classical [tensor calculus](wikipedia://en/29965) to deforming [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470). Central to the CMS is the tensorial time derivative $\dot{\nabla}$ whose original definition [^o3] was put forth by [Jacques Hadamard](wikipedia://en/312675). It plays the role analogous to that of the [covariant derivative](wikipedia://en/431848) $\nabla _{\alpha }$ on [differential manifolds](wikipedia://en/2119219) in that it produces a [tensor](wikipedia://en/29965) when applied to a tensor.

:::

Suppose that $\Sigma_t$ is the evolution of the [surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_%28differential_geometry%29) $\Sigma$ indexed by a time-like parameter $t$. The definitions of the surface [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) $C$ and the [operator](wikipedia://en/22213) $\dot{\nabla}$ are the [geometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric) foundations of the CMS. The velocity C is the [rate](wikipedia://en/332372) of deformation of the surface $\Sigma$ in the instantaneous [normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_normal) direction. The value of $C$ at a point $P$ is defined as the [limit](wikipedia://en/285759)

$$C=\lim_{h\to 0} \frac{\text{Distance}(P,P^*)}{h}$$

where $P^{*}$ is the point on $\Sigma_{t+h}$ that lies on the straight line perpendicular to $\Sigma_{t}$ at point P. This definition is illustrated in the first geometric figure below. The velocity $C$ is a signed quantity: it is positive when $\overline{PP^{*}}$ points in the direction of the chosen normal, and negative otherwise. The relationship between $\Sigma_{t}$ and $C$ is analogous to the relationship between location and velocity in elementary calculus: knowing either quantity allows one to construct the other by [differentiation](wikipedia://en/7921) or [integration](wikipedia://en/498383).

*Image: C3x3t.png.* Geometric construction of the surface velocity C

*Image: DFdt3x3t.png.* Geometric construction of the $\delta/\delta t$-derivative of an invariant field F

The tensorial time derivative $\dot{\nabla}$ for a [scalar field](wikipedia://en/161879) F defined on $\Sigma_{t}$ is the [rate of change](wikipedia://en/7921) in $F$ in the instantaneously normal direction:

$$\frac{\delta F}{\delta t}=\lim_{h\to 0} \frac{F(P^*)-F(P)}{h}$$

This definition is also illustrated in second geometric figure.

The above definitions are *[geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446)*. In analytical settings, direct application of these definitions may not be possible. The CMS gives *analytical* definitions of C and $\dot{\nabla}$ in terms of elementary operations from [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) and [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625).

## Analytical definitions {#Analytical_definitions}

For [analytical](wikipedia://en/48396) definitions of $C$ and $\dot{\nabla}$, consider the evolution of $S$ given by

$$Z^i = Z^i \left( t ,S \right)$$

where $Z^{i}$ are general [curvilinear space coordinates](wikipedia://en/755300) and $S^{\alpha }$ are the surface coordinates. By convention, tensor indices of function arguments are dropped. Thus the above equations contains $S$ rather than $S^\alpha$. The velocity object $\textbf{V}=V^i\textbf{Z}_i$ is defined as the [partial derivative](wikipedia://en/52565)

$$V^i =\frac{\partial Z^i \left( t ,S \right)}{\partial t }$$

The velocity $C$ can be computed most directly by the formula

$$C=V^i N_i$$

where $N_i$ are the covariant components of the normal vector $\vec{N}$.

Also, defining the shift tensor representation of the surface's tangent space $Z^{\alpha}_{i}=\textbf{S}^\alpha\cdot\textbf{Z}_i$ and the tangent velocity as $V^\alpha=Z^\alpha_iV^i$ , then the definition of the $\dot{\nabla}$ derivative for an [invariant](wikipedia://en/256738) *F* reads

$$\dot{\nabla}F=\frac{\partial F\left( t ,S \right)}{\partial t }-V^\alpha\nabla _{\alpha }F$$

where $\nabla_\alpha$ is the covariant derivative on S.

For *tensors*, an appropriate generalization is needed. The proper definition for a representative tensor $T^{i\alpha }_{j\beta }$ reads

$$\dot{\nabla}T^{i\alpha }_{j\beta }=\frac{\partial T^{i\alpha }_{j\beta }}{\partial t}-V^{\eta }\nabla _{\eta }T^{i\alpha }_{j\beta }+V^{m}\Gamma ^{i}_{mk}T^{k\alpha }_{j\beta }-V^{m}\Gamma ^{k}_{mj}T^{i\alpha }_{k\beta }+\dot{\Gamma}^\alpha_\eta T^{i\eta }_{j\beta }-\dot{\Gamma}^\eta_\beta T^{i\alpha }_{j\eta }$$

where $\Gamma^k_{mj}$ are [Christoffel symbols](wikipedia://en/1401020) and $\dot{\Gamma}^\alpha_\beta=\nabla_\beta V^\alpha-C B^\alpha_\beta$ is the surface's appropriate temporal symbols ($B^\alpha_\beta$ is a matrix representation of the surface's curvature shape operator)

## Properties of the $\dot{\nabla}$-derivative {#Properties_of_the_\dot{\nabla}-derivative}

The $\dot{\nabla}$-derivative commutes with contraction, satisfies the [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988) for any collection of indices

$$\dot{\nabla}(S^i_\alpha T^\beta_j )=T^\beta_j\dot{\nabla}S^i_\alpha + S^i_\alpha\dot{\nabla}T^\beta_j$$

and obeys a [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) for surface [restrictions](wikipedia://en/185427#Restrictions_and_extensions) of spatial tensors:

$$\dot{\nabla}F^j_k(Z,t) =\frac{\partial F^j_k}{\partial t}+CN^i \nabla _i F^j_k$$

Chain rule shows that the $\dot{\nabla}$-derivatives of spatial "metrics" vanishes

$$\dot{\nabla}\delta^i_j=0,\dot{\nabla}Z_{ij}=0,\dot{\nabla}Z^{ij}=0,\dot{\nabla}\varepsilon _{ijk}=0,\dot{\nabla}\varepsilon^{ijk}=0$$

where $Z_{ij}$ and $Z^{ij}$ are covariant and contravariant [metric tensors](wikipedia://en/195795), $\delta ^{i}_{j}$ is the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890) symbol, and $\varepsilon _{ijk}$ and $\varepsilon ^{ijk}$ are the [Levi-Civita symbols](wikipedia://en/195982). The [main article](wikipedia://en/195982) on Levi-Civita symbols describes them for [Cartesian coordinate systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinate_systems). The preceding rule is valid in general coordinates, where the definition of the Levi-Civita symbols must include the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the covariant metric tensor $Z_{ij}$.

## Differentiation table for the $\dot{\nabla}$-derivative {#Differentiation_table_for_the_\dot{\nabla}-derivative}

The $\dot{\nabla}$ derivative of the key surface objects leads to highly concise and attractive formulas. When applied to the [covariant](wikipedia://en/202886) surface [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) $S_{\alpha \beta }$ and the [contravariant](wikipedia://en/202886) metric tensor $S^{\alpha \beta }$, the following identities result

$$\begin{aligned} \dot{\nabla}S_{\alpha \beta } &=0 \\[8pt] \dot{\nabla}S^{\alpha \beta } &=0 \end{aligned}$$

where $B_{\alpha \beta }$ and $B^{\alpha \beta }$ are the doubly covariant and doubly contravariant [curvature tensors](wikipedia://en/285618). These curvature tensors, as well as for the mixed curvature tensor $B^\alpha_\beta$, satisfy

$$\begin{aligned} \dot{\nabla}B_{\alpha \beta }& = \nabla_\alpha \nabla_\beta C + CB_{\alpha \gamma }B^\gamma_\beta \\[8pt] \dot{\nabla}B^\alpha_\beta& = \nabla_\beta\nabla^\alpha C + CB^\alpha_\gamma B^\gamma_\beta \\[8pt] \dot{\nabla}B^{\alpha \beta }& = \nabla ^\alpha \nabla^\beta C + CB^{\gamma \alpha}B^\beta_\gamma \end{aligned}$$

The shift tensor $Z^i_\alpha$ and the normal $N^i$ satisfy

$$\begin{aligned} \dot{\nabla}Z^i_\alpha & = N^i\nabla _\alpha C \\[8pt] \dot{\nabla}N^i & = -Z^i_\alpha \nabla^\alpha C \end{aligned}$$

Finally, the surface [Levi-Civita symbols](wikipedia://en/195982) $\varepsilon _{\alpha \beta }$ and $\varepsilon ^{\alpha \beta }$ satisfy

$$\begin{aligned} \dot{\nabla} \varepsilon _{\alpha \beta } & = 0 \\[8pt] \dot{\nabla} \varepsilon ^{\alpha \beta } & = 0 \end{aligned}$$
