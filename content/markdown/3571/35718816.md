# Napoleon points

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Napoleon points"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), **Napoleon points** are a pair of special points associated with a plane [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654). It is generally believed that the existence of these points was discovered by [Napoleon Bonaparte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_Bonaparte), the [Emperor of the French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_of_the_French) from 1804 to 1815, but many have questioned this belief.[^o16] The Napoleon points are [triangle centers](wikipedia://en/12401488) and they are listed as the points *X*(17) and *X*(18) in [Clark Kimberling](wikipedia://en/25536421)'s [Encyclopedia of Triangle Centers](wikipedia://en/15003593).

:::

The name "Napoleon points" has also been applied to a different pair of triangle centers, better known as the [isodynamic points](wikipedia://en/5102885).[^o2]

## Definition of the points {#Definition_of_the_points}

### First Napoleon point {#First_Napoleon_point}

*Image: First Napoleon Point.svg.* First Napoleon point

Let $\triangle ABC$ be any given [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654). On the sides $\operatorname{BC}, \operatorname{CA}, \operatorname{AB}$ of the triangle, construct outwardly drawn [equilateral triangles](wikipedia://en/173285) $\triangle DBC, \triangle ECA, \triangle FAB$ respectively. Let the [centroids](wikipedia://en/187926) of these triangles be $X, Y, Z$ respectively. Then the lines $AX, BY, CZ$ are [concurrent](wikipedia://en/2206157). The point of concurrence $N_{1}$ is the first Napoleon point, or the outer Napoleon point, of the triangle $\triangle ABC$.

The triangle $\triangle XYZ$ is called the outer Napoleon triangle of $\triangle ABC$. [Napoleon's theorem](wikipedia://en/2728914) asserts that this triangle is an [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285).

In [Clark Kimberling](wikipedia://en/25536421)'s [Encyclopedia of Triangle Centers](wikipedia://en/15003593), the first Napoleon point is denoted by *X*(17).[^o5]

- The [trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790) of $N_{1}$: $$\begin{aligned} & \csc(A + \tfrac{\pi}{6}) : \csc(B + \tfrac{\pi}{6}) : \csc(C + \tfrac{\pi}{6}) \\[2pt] =\ & \sec(A -\tfrac{\pi}{3}) : \sec(B -\tfrac{\pi}{3}) : \sec(C - \tfrac{\pi}{3}) \end{aligned}$$
- The [barycentric coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycentric_coordinate_system_%28mathematics%29) of $N_{1}$: $$a \csc(A + \tfrac{\pi}{6}) : b \csc(B + \tfrac{\pi}{6}) : c \csc(C + \tfrac{\pi}{6})$$

### Second Napoleon point {#Second_Napoleon_point}

*Image: Second Napoleon Point.svg.* Second Napoleon point

Let $\triangle ABC$ be any given [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654). On the sides $BC, CA, AB$ of the triangle, construct inwardly drawn equilateral triangles $\triangle DBC, \triangle ECA, \triangle FAB$ respectively. Let the [centroids](wikipedia://en/187926) of these triangles be $X, Y, Z$ respectively. Then the lines $AX, BY, CZ$ are concurrent. The point of concurrence $N_{2}$ is the second Napoleon point, or the inner Napoleon point, of $\triangle ABC$.

The triangle $\triangle XYZ$ is called the inner Napoleon triangle of $\triangle ABC$. [Napoleon's theorem](wikipedia://en/2728914) asserts that this triangle is an equilateral triangle.

In Clark Kimberling's Encyclopedia of Triangle Centers, the second Napoleon point is denoted by *X*(18).[^o5]

- The trilinear coordinates of $N_{2}$: $$\begin{aligned} & \csc(A - \tfrac{\pi}{6}) : \csc(B - \tfrac{\pi}{6}) : \csc(C - \tfrac{\pi}{6}) \\[2pt] =\ & \sec(A + \tfrac{\pi}{3}) : \sec(B + \tfrac{\pi}{3}) : \sec(C + \tfrac{\pi}{3}) \end{aligned}$$
- The barycentric coordinates of $N_{2}$: $$a \csc(A - \tfrac{\pi}{6}) : b \csc(B -\tfrac{\pi}{6}) : c \csc(C - \tfrac{\pi}{6})$$

Two points closely related to the Napoleon points are the [Fermat-Torricelli points](wikipedia://en/2666205) (ETC's *X*(13) and *X*(14)). If instead of constructing lines joining the equilateral triangles' centroids to the respective vertices one now constructs lines joining the equilateral triangles' apices to the respective vertices of the triangle, the three lines so constructed are again concurrent. The points of concurrence are called the Fermat-Torricelli points, sometimes denoted $F_{1}$ and $F_{2}$. The intersection of the Fermat line (i.e., that line joining the two Fermat-Torricelli points) and the Napoleon line (i.e., that line joining the two Napoleon points) is the triangle's [symmedian point](wikipedia://en/320997) (ETC's *X*(6)).

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The results regarding the existence of the Napoleon points can be [generalized](wikipedia://en/12746) in different ways. In defining the Napoleon points we begin with equilateral triangles drawn on the sides of $\triangle ABC$ and then consider the centers $X, Y, Z$ of these triangles. These centers can be thought as the vertices of [isosceles triangles](wikipedia://en/315428) erected on the sides of triangle ABC with the base angles equal to $\pi$/6 (30 degrees). The generalizations seek to determine other triangles that, when erected over the sides of $\triangle ABC$, have concurrent lines joining their external vertices and the vertices of $\triangle ABC$.

::: {#o10 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Kiepert's hyperbola"}

### Isosceles triangles {#Isosceles_triangles}

*Image: KiepertPoint.svg.* A point on the Kiepert hyperbola.

*Image: Kiepert Hyperbola.svg.* Kiepert hyperbola of $\triangle ABC$. The hyperbola passes through the vertices $A, B, C$, the orthocenter $O$ and the centroid $G$ of the triangle.

This generalization asserts the following:[^o11]

If the three triangles $\triangle XBC, \triangle YCA, \triangle ZAB$, constructed on the sides of the given triangle $\triangle ABC$ as bases, are [similar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similar_triangle), [isosceles](wikipedia://en/315428) and similarly situated, then the lines $AX, BY, CZ$ concur at a point $N$.

If the common base angle is $\theta$, then the vertices of the three triangles have the following trilinear coordinates.

$$\begin{array}{rccccc} X= & -\sin\theta &:& \sin(C + \theta) &:& \sin(B + \theta) \\ Y= & \sin(C + \theta) &:& -\sin\theta &:& \sin(A + \theta) \\ Z=& \sin(B + \theta) &:& \sin(A + \theta) &:& -\sin\theta \end{array}$$

The trilinear coordinates of $N$ are

$$\csc(A + \theta) : \csc(B + \theta) : \csc(C + \theta)$$

A few special cases are interesting.

{| class="wikitable"

\|- ! Value of $\theta$ !! Moreover, the [locus](wikipedia://en/542465) of $N$ as the base angle $\theta$ varies between −$\pi$/2 and $\pi$/2 is the [conic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic)

$$\frac{\sin(B-C)} x + \frac{\sin(C-A)} y + \frac{\sin(A-B)} z = 0.$$

This [conic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic) is a [rectangular hyperbola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_hyperbola) and it is called the **[Kiepert hyperbola](wikipedia://en/69991093)** in honor of [Ludwig Kiepert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Kiepert) (1846–1934), the mathematician who discovered this result.[^o11] This hyperbola is the unique conic which passes through the five points $A, B, C, G, O$.

### Similar triangles {#Similar_triangles}

*Image: GeneralisationOfNapoleonPointSpecialCase.svg.* Generalization of Napoleon point: A special case

The three triangles $\triangle XBC, \triangle YCA, \triangle ZAB$ erected over the sides of the triangle $\triangle ABC$ need not be isosceles for the three lines $AX, BY, CZ$ to be concurrent.[^o13]

If similar triangles $\triangle XBC, \triangle AYC, \triangle ABZ$ are constructed outwardly on the sides of any triangle $\triangle ABC$ then the lines $AX, BY, CZ$ are concurrent.

### Arbitrary triangles {#Arbitrary_triangles}

The concurrence of the lines $AX, BY, CZ$ holds even in much relaxed conditions. The following result states one of the most general conditions for the lines $AX, BY, CZ$ to be concurrent.[^o13]

If triangles $\triangle XBC, \triangle YCA, \triangle ZAB$ are constructed outwardly on the sides of any triangle $\triangle ABC$ such that

$$\angle CBX = \angle ABZ, \quad \angle ACY = \angle BCX, \quad \angle BAZ = \angle CAY;$$

then the lines $AX, BY, CZ$ are concurrent.

The point of concurrency is known as the [Jacobi point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_point).

:::

## History {#History}

Coxeter and Greitzer state the Napoleon Theorem thus: *If equilateral triangles are erected externally on the sides of any triangle, their centers form an equilateral triangle*. They observe that Napoleon Bonaparte was a bit of a mathematician with a great interest in geometry. However, they doubt whether Napoleon knew enough geometry to discover the theorem attributed to him.[^o16]

The earliest recorded appearance of the result embodied in Napoleon's theorem is in an article in [The Ladies' Diary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ladies'_Diary) appeared in 1825. The Ladies' Diary was an annual periodical which was in circulation in London from 1704 to 1841. The result appeared as part of a question posed by W. Rutherford, Woodburn.

VII. Quest.(1439); by Mr. W. Rutherford, Woodburn." *Describe equilateral triangles (the vertices being either all outward or all inward) upon the three sides of any triangle ABC: then the lines which join the centers of gravity of those three equilateral triangles will constitute an equilateral triangle. Required a demonstration.*"

However, there is no reference to the existence of the so-called Napoleon points in this question. [Christoph J. Scriba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christoph_J._Scriba), a German [historian of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historian_of_mathematics), has studied the problem of attributing the Napoleon points to [Napoleon](wikipedia://en/69880) in a paper in [Historia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/10841658).[^o17]
