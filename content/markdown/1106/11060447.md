# Bahmanyar

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bahmanyar"}

**Abu al-Hasan (or Abu al-Husayn) Bahmanyar ibn al-Marzban**, better simply known as **Bahmanyar** (بهمنیار; died 1066) was an [Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_peoples) scholar and a pupil of [Avicenna](wikipedia://en/1130) (d. 1037).

:::

## Background {#Background}

Bahmanyar was from a [Zoroastrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrian) family, reportedly from [Azerbaijan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azerbaijan_%28Iran%29) in northern [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran).[^n1][^n2] His father may have been the [Bavandid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bavandid) prince [al-Marzuban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Marzuban), who ruled in [Mazandaran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazandaran) in the late 10th-century, and was the author of the *[Marzban-nama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marzban-nama)*.[^n1][^n2] Bahmanyar's knowledge of [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language) was not perfect.[^n3]

## Life {#Life}

Little is known of Bahmanyar's life. He most likely started his studies in philosophy along with Abu al-Qasim al-Kirmani in the [Buyid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buyid_dynasty) city of [Ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray,_Iran) in northern Iran. There they both became involved in the administration, while they read [Avicenna](wikipedia://en/1130) works.[^n2] Bahmanyar was possibly part of the Buyid court in Ray through family ties with the Bavandid princess [Sayyida Shirin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyida_Shirin), her husband [Fakhr al-Dawla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fakhr_al-Dawla) (984) and son [Majd al-Dawla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majd_al-Dawla) (997), both Buyid rulers of Ray.[^n2]

Bahmanyar eventually started to interact with Avicenna, which would later result in the latter's creation of the*al-Mubāḥathāt* ("The Discussions"), which were mainly answers to questions made by Bahmanyar.[^n2] The work was compiled sometime between 1024 and 1037, during Avicenna's stay in [Isfahan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isfahan), the capital of the [Kakuyid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kakuyid) ruler [Muhammad ibn Rustam Dushmanziyar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Rustam_Dushmanziyar) (1008).[^n2][^n4] In the work, Bahmanyar is referred to as *al-Shaykh al-fāḍil* ("the aristocratic gentleman").[^n2][^n1] Bahmanyar and Avicenna possibly encountered each other in 1014/5 at Ray, when the latter worked for Sayyida Shirin and Majd al-Dawla.[^n2]

Bahmanyar's main work, the *Kitāb al-taḥṣīl* ("The Summation"), which summarises Avicenna's logic, physics and metaphysics was written between 1024 and 1037 and dedicated to his Zoroastrian uncle, Abu Mansur Bahram ibn Khurshid ibn Yazdyar, who was possibly the son of the [treasurer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasurer) of the Buyid emperor [Adud al-Dawla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adud_al-Dawla) (949).[^n2][^n3] [Abu'l-Hasan Bayhaqi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu'l-Hasan_Bayhaqi) (d. 1169) writes that Bahmanyar also wrote a book on logic and one on music and other works are attributed to him.[^n3]

## Beliefs {#Beliefs}

Bahmanyar reportedly converted to Islam.[^n2] While his main work *Kitāb al-taḥṣīl* is ambiguous about his beliefs, the introduction and conclusion of the book are made in a Muslim manner and character, though it is unknown if these were part of the original version of the book or later added by secretaries.[^n2] However, his conversion is considered plausible due to his way of thinking on questions regarding [divine unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism) and the struggle between good and evil, which Bahmanyar places inside the created order, contrary to the Zoroastrian belief that associates it with the divine essence.[^n2] Furthermore, his *[kunya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kunya_%28Arabic%29)* Abu al-Husayn may be a possible sign of a conversion to [Shia Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shia_Islam).[^n2]
