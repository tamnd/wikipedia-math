# Projection (relational algebra)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Projection"}

In [relational algebra](wikipedia://en/175285), a **projection** is a [unary operation](wikipedia://en/161006) written as $\Pi_{a_1, ...,a_n}( R )$, where $R$ is a [relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relation_%28database%29) and $a_1,...,a_n$ are attribute names. Its result is defined as the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) obtained when the components of the [tuples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuples) in $R$ are restricted to the set $\{a_1,...,a_n\}$ – it *discards* (or *excludes*) the other attributes.[^o2]

:::

In practical terms, if a relation is thought of as a table, then projection can be thought of as picking a subset of its columns. For example, if the attributes are (name, age), then projection of the relation {(Alice, 5), (Bob, 8)} onto attribute list (age) yields {5,8} – we have discarded the names, and only know what ages are present.

Projections may also modify attribute values. For example, if $R$ has attributes $a$, $b$, $c$, where the values of $b$ are numbers, then $\Pi_{a,\ b \times 0.5,\ c}( R )$ is like $R$, but with all $b$-values halved.[^o3]

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

The closely related concept in [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) (see: [projection (set theory)](wikipedia://en/3913145)) differs from that of [relational algebra](wikipedia://en/175285) in that, in set theory, one projects onto ordered components, not onto attributes. For instance, projecting $(3,7)$ onto the second component yields 7.

Projection is relational algebra's counterpart of [existential quantification](wikipedia://en/91420) in [predicate logic](wikipedia://en/10983). The attributes *not* included correspond to existentially quantified variables in the predicate whose [extension](wikipedia://en/3934566) the operand relation represents. The example below illustrates this point.

Because of the correspondence with existential quantification, some authorities prefer to define projection in terms of the excluded attributes. In a [computer language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_language) it is of course possible to provide notations for both, and that was done in [ISBL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISBL) and several languages that have taken their cue from ISBL.

A nearly identical concept occurs in the category of [monoids](wikipedia://en/19652), called a [string projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_projection), which consists of removing all of the letters in the [string](wikipedia://en/27701) that do not belong to a given [alphabet](wikipedia://en/4292269).

When implemented in [SQL](wikipedia://en/29004) standard the "default projection" returns a [multiset](wikipedia://en/305303) instead of a set, and the $\pi$ projection is obtained by the addition of the [`DISTINCT` keyword](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Select_%28SQL%29) to eliminate duplicate data.

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

For an example, consider the relations depicted in the following two tables which are the relation $\operatorname{Person}$ and its projection on (some say "over") the attributes $\operatorname{Age}$ and $\operatorname{Weight}$:

| $\text{Person}$ | $\Pi_\text{Age,Weight}(\text{Person})$ |
|---|---|
| Name Age Weight Harry 34 180 Sally 28 164 George 28 170 Helena 54 154 Peter 34 180 | Age Weight 34 180 28 164 28 170 54 154 |

Suppose the predicate of Person is "*Name* is *age* years old and weighs *weight*." Then the given projection represents the predicate, "There exists *Name* such that *Name* is *age* years old and weighs *weight*."

Note that Harry and Peter have the same age and weight, but since the result is a relation, and therefore a set, this combination only appears once in the result.

:::

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

More formally the semantics of projection are defined as follows:

$$\Pi_{a_1, ...,a_n}( R ) = \{ \ t[a_1,...,a_n] : \ t \in R \ \},$$

where $t[a_1,...,a_n]$ is the [restriction](wikipedia://en/1589032) of the tuple $t$ to the set $\{a_1,...,a_n\}$ so that

$$t[a_1,...,a_n] = \{ \ ( a', v ) \ | \ ( a', v ) \in t, \ a' \in \{a_1,...,a_n \} \},$$

where $(a', v)$ is an attribute value, $a'$ is an attribute name, and $v$ is an element of that attribute's domain — see [Relation (database)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relation_%28database%29).

The result of a projection $\Pi_{a_1, ...,a_n}( R )$ is defined only if $\{a_1,...,a_n\}$ is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of the header of $R$.

Projection over no attributes at all is possible, yielding a [relation of degree zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relation_of_degree_zero). In this case the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of the result is zero if the operand is empty, otherwise one. The two relations of degree zero are the only ones that cannot be depicted as tables.

:::
