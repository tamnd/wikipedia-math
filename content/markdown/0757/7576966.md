# Pierre de Fermat

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Pierre de Fermat"}

**Pierre de Fermat** (; ; 17 August 1601 – 12 January 1665) was a French magistrate, [polymath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath), and [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) credited for early developments that led to [infinitesimal calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal_calculus), including his technique of [adequality](wikipedia://en/30609033). In particular, he is recognized for his discovery of an original method of finding the greatest and the smallest [ordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinate) of curved lines, which is analogous to that of [differential calculus](wikipedia://en/50416), then unknown, and his research into [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527). He made notable contributions to [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202), [probability](wikipedia://en/22934), and [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics). He is best known for his [Fermat's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_principle) for light propagation and his [Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953) in number theory, which he described in a note at the margin of a copy of [Diophantus](wikipedia://en/9110)' *[Arithmetica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetica)*. He was also a lawyer[^o5] at the *[parlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parlement)* of [Toulouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toulouse), [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France), a poet, a skilled [Latinist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latinist), and a [Hellenist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenist).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

*Image: Pierre de Fermat3.jpg.* Pierre de Fermat, 17th century painting by [Rolland Lefebvre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roland_Lefebvre_%28painter%29)

Fermat was born in 1601 in [Beaumont-de-Lomagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaumont-de-Lomagne), France — the late 15th-century mansion where Fermat was born is now a museum. He was from [Gascony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gascony), where his father, Dominique Fermat, was a wealthy leather merchant and served three one-year terms as one of the four consuls of Beaumont-de-Lomagne. His mother was Claire de Long.[^o7] Pierre had one brother and two sisters and was almost certainly brought up in the town of his birth.

He attended the [University of Orléans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Orléans) from 1623 and received a bachelor in civil law in 1626, before moving to [Bordeaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bordeaux). In Bordeaux, he began his first serious mathematical researches. In 1629, he gave a copy of his restoration of [Apollonius](wikipedia://en/257242)'s *[De Locis Planis](wikipedia://en/257242#De_Locis_Planis)* to one of the mathematicians there. In Bordeaux, he was in contact with [Beaugrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_de_Beaugrand), and during this time, he produced important work on [maxima and minima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxima_and_minima) which he gave to [Étienne d'Espagnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Étienne_d'Espagnet) who shared mathematical interests with Fermat. There, he became much influenced by the work of [François Viète](wikipedia://en/310238).[^o8]

In 1630, he bought the office of a [councilor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Councilor) at the [Parlement de Toulouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parlement_de_Toulouse), one of the High Courts of Judicature in France, and was sworn in by the Grand Chambre in May 1631. He held this office for the rest of his life. Fermat thereby became entitled to change his name from Pierre Fermat to Pierre de Fermat. On 1 June 1631, Fermat married Louise de Long, a fourth cousin of his mother Claire de Fermat (née de Long). The Fermats had eight children, five of whom survived to adulthood: Clément-Samuel, Jean, Claire, Catherine, and Louise.[^o9][^o10][^o11]

Fluent in six languages ([French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language), [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin), [Occitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occitan_language), [classical Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Greek), [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_language) and [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_language)), Fermat was praised for his written verse in several languages and his advice was eagerly sought regarding the emendation of Greek texts. He communicated most of his work in letters to friends, often with little or no proof of his theorems. In some of these letters to his friends, he explored many of the fundamental ideas of calculus before [Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) or [Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281). He was a trained lawyer making mathematics more of a hobby than a profession. Nevertheless, he made important contributions to [analytical geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_geometry), probability, number theory and calculus.[^o12] Secrecy was common in European mathematical circles at the time. This naturally led to priority disputes with contemporaries such as [Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) and [Wallis](wikipedia://en/239290).[^o13]

[Anders Hald](wikipedia://en/1374460) writes that, "The basis of Fermat's mathematics was the classical Greek treatises combined with Vieta's [new algebraic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_algebra) methods."[^o14]

### Work {#Work}

*Image: Diophantus-II-8-Fermat.jpg.* The 1670 edition of [Diophantus](wikipedia://en/9110)'s *[Arithmetica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetica)* includes Fermat's commentary, referred to as his "Last Theorem" (*Observatio Domini Petri de Fermat*), posthumously published by his son.

Fermat's pioneering work in [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202) (*Methodus ad disquirendam maximam et minimam et de tangentibus linearum curvarum*) was circulated in manuscript form in 1636 (based on results achieved in 1629),[^o17] predating the publication of Descartes' *[La géométrie](wikipedia://en/239981)* (1637), which exploited the work.[^o18] This manuscript was published posthumously in 1679 in *Varia opera mathematica*, as *Ad Locos Planos et Solidos Isagoge* (*Introduction to Plane and Solid Loci*).[^o19]

In *Methodus ad disquirendam maximam et minimam et de tangentibus linearum curvarum*, Fermat developed a method ([adequality](wikipedia://en/30609033)) for determining maxima, minima, and [tangents](wikipedia://en/31482) to various curves that was equivalent to [differential calculus](wikipedia://en/50416).[^o20][^o21] In these works, Fermat obtained a technique for finding the centers of gravity of various plane and solid figures, which led to his further work in [quadrature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrature_%28geometry%29).

Fermat was the first person known to have evaluated the integral of general power functions. With his method, he was able to reduce this evaluation to the sum of [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630).[^o22] The resulting formula was helpful to [Newton](wikipedia://en/14627), and then [Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281), when they independently developed the [fundamental theorem of calculus](wikipedia://en/19468696).

In number theory, Fermat studied [Pell's equation](wikipedia://en/24877), [perfect numbers](wikipedia://en/23670), [amicable numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amicable_number) and what would later become [Fermat numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_numbers). It was while researching perfect numbers that he discovered [Fermat's little theorem](wikipedia://en/53453). He invented a factorization method — [Fermat's factorization method](wikipedia://en/2132433) — and popularized the proof by [infinite descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_descent), which he used to prove [Fermat's right triangle theorem](wikipedia://en/29559494) which includes as a corollary Fermat's Last Theorem for the case *n*=4. Fermat developed the [two-square theorem](wikipedia://en/1850216), and the [polygonal number theorem](wikipedia://en/847886), which states that each number is a sum of three [triangular numbers](wikipedia://en/213915), [four square numbers](wikipedia://en/326395), five [pentagonal numbers](wikipedia://en/504786), and so on.

Although Fermat claimed to have proven all his arithmetic theorems, few records of his proofs have survived. Many mathematicians, including [Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125), doubted several of his claims, especially given the difficulty of some of the problems and the limited mathematical methods available to Fermat. His [Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953) was first discovered by his son in the margin in his father's copy of an edition of [Diophantus](wikipedia://en/9110), and included the statement that the margin was too small to include the proof. It seems that he had not written to [Marin Mersenne](wikipedia://en/72947) about it. It was first proven in 1994, by [Sir Andrew Wiles](wikipedia://en/21950759), using techniques unavailable to Fermat.

Through their correspondence in 1654, Fermat and [Blaise Pascal](wikipedia://en/4068) helped lay the foundation for the theory of probability. From this brief but productive collaboration on the [problem of points](wikipedia://en/11285518), they are now regarded as joint founders of [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542).[^o23] Fermat is credited with carrying out the first-ever rigorous probability calculation. In it, he was asked by a professional [gambler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gambler) why if he bet on rolling at least one six in four throws of a die he won in the long term, whereas betting on throwing at least one double-six in 24 throws of two [dice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dice) resulted in his losing. Fermat showed mathematically why this was the case.[^o24]

The first [variational principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_variational_principles_in_physics) in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) was articulated by [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) in his *Catoptrica*. It says that, for the path of light reflecting from a mirror, the [angle of incidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_incidence_%28optics%29) equals the [angle of reflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_reflection). [Hero of Alexandria](wikipedia://en/68274) later showed that this path gave the shortest length and the least time.[^o25] Fermat refined and generalized this to "light travels between two given points along the path of shortest *time*" now known as the *[principle of least time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_least_time)*.[^o26] For this, Fermat is recognized as a key figure in the historical development of the fundamental [principle of least action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_least_action) in physics. The terms [Fermat's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_principle) and *Fermat functional* were named in recognition of this role.[^o27]

### Death {#Death}

Pierre de Fermat died on 12 January 1665, at [Castres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castres), in the present-day department of [Tarn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarn_%28department%29).[^o29] The oldest and most prestigious high school in [Toulouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toulouse) is named after him: the Lycée Pierre-de-Fermat. French sculptor [Théophile Barrau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Théophile_Barrau) made a marble statue named *Hommage à Pierre Fermat* as a tribute to Fermat, now at the [Capitole de Toulouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitole_de_Toulouse).

## Assessment of his work {#Assessment_of_his_work}

Together with [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525), Fermat was one of the two leading mathematicians of the first half of the 17th century. According to [Peter L. Bernstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_L._Bernstein), in his 1996 book *Against the Gods*, Fermat "was a mathematician of rare power. He was an independent inventor of [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202), he contributed to the early development of calculus, he did research on the weight of the earth, and he worked on light refraction and optics. In the course of what turned out to be an extended correspondence with [Blaise Pascal](wikipedia://en/4068), he made a significant contribution to the theory of probability. But Fermat's crowning achievement was in the theory of numbers."[^o31]

Regarding Fermat's work in analysis, [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) wrote that his own early ideas about calculus came directly from "Fermat's way of drawing tangents".[^o32]

Of Fermat's number theoretic work, the 20th-century mathematician [André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019) wrote that: "what we possess of his methods for dealing with [curves](wikipedia://en/253260) of [genus 1](wikipedia://en/10225) is remarkably coherent; it is still the foundation for the modern theory of such curves. It naturally falls into two parts; the first one ... may conveniently be termed a method of ascent, in contrast with the [descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_descent) which is rightly regarded as Fermat's own".[^o33] Regarding Fermat's use of ascent, Weil continued: "The novelty consisted in the vastly extended use which Fermat made of it, giving him at least a partial equivalent of what we would obtain by the systematic use of the [group theoretical](wikipedia://en/41890) properties of the [rational points](wikipedia://en/2960200) on a standard cubic".[^o34] With his gift for number relations and his ability to find proofs for many of his theorems, Fermat essentially created the modern theory of numbers.

Fermat made a number of mistakes. Some mistakes were pointed out by Schinzel and Sierpinski.[^o35] In his letter to [Pierre de Carcavi](wikipedia://en/47366237), Fermat said that he had proved that the [Fermat numbers](wikipedia://en/91127) are all prime. [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) pointed out that 4,294,967,297 is divisible by 641. Also, see Weil, in "Number Theory".[^o36]
