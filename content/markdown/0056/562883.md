# Higher-order logic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Higher-order logic"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), a **higher-order logic** (**HOL**) is a form of logic that is distinguished from [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) by additional [quantifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantification_%28logic%29) and, sometimes, stronger [semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantics_of_logic). Higher-order logics with their standard semantics are more expressive, but their [model-theoretic](wikipedia://en/19858) properties are less well-behaved than those of first-order logic.

:::

The term "higher-order logic" is commonly used to mean **higher-order simple predicate logic**. Here, "simple" indicates that the underlying [type theory](wikipedia://en/40282) is the *theory of simple types*, also called the *simple theory of types*. [Leon Chwistek](wikipedia://en/1029740) and [Frank P. Ramsey](wikipedia://en/238285) proposed this as a simplification of *ramified theory of types* specified in the *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)* by [Alfred North Whitehead](wikipedia://en/43395) and [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163). *Simple types* is sometimes also meant to exclude [polymorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_polymorphism) and [dependent](wikipedia://en/1949487) types.[^o2]

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Order (logic)"}

## Quantification scope {#Quantification_scope}

[First-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) quantifies only variables that range over individuals; *[second-order logic](wikipedia://en/341428)*, also quantifies over sets; *third-order logic* also quantifies over sets of sets, and so on.

*Higher-order logic* is the union of first-, second-, third-, ..., *n*th-order logic; *i.e.,* higher-order logic admits quantification over sets that are nested arbitrarily deeply.

:::

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Semantics of higher-order logic"}

## Semantics {#Semantics}

There are two possible semantics for higher-order logic.

In the *standard* or *full semantics*, quantifiers over higher-type objects range over *all* possible objects of that type. For example, a quantifier over sets of individuals ranges over the entire [powerset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powerset) of the set of individuals. Thus, in standard semantics, once the set of individuals is specified, this is enough to specify all the quantifiers. HOL with standard semantics is more expressive than first-order logic. For example, HOL admits [categorical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morley's_categoricity_theorem) axiomatizations of the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474), and of the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), which are impossible with first-order logic. However, by a result of [Kurt Gödel](wikipedia://en/16736), HOL with standard semantics does not admit an [effective](wikipedia://en/1139338), [sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soundness_%28logic%29), and [complete](wikipedia://en/12450) [proof calculus](wikipedia://en/1250665).[^o7] The model-theoretic properties of HOL with standard semantics are also more complex than those of first-order logic. For example, the [Löwenheim number](wikipedia://en/28877484) of [second-order logic](wikipedia://en/341428) is already larger than the first [measurable cardinal](wikipedia://en/248102), if such a cardinal exists.[^o8] The Löwenheim number of first-order logic, in contrast, is [ℵ<sub>0</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleph_nought), the smallest infinite cardinal.

In **Henkin semantics**, a separate domain is included in each interpretation for each higher-order type. Thus, for example, quantifiers over sets of individuals may range over only a subset of the [powerset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powerset) of the set of individuals. HOL with these semantics is equivalent to [many-sorted first-order logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Many-sorted_first-order_logic), rather than being stronger than first-order logic. In particular, HOL with Henkin semantics has all the model-theoretic properties of first-order logic, and has a complete, sound, effective proof system inherited from first-order logic.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

Higher-order logics include the offshoots of [Church](wikipedia://en/49731)'s [simple theory of types](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_theory_of_types)[^o11] and the various forms of [intuitionistic type theory](wikipedia://en/345023). [Gérard Huet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gérard_Huet) has shown that [unifiability](wikipedia://en/54432#Higher-order_unification) is [undecidable](wikipedia://en/15631055) in a [type-theoretic](wikipedia://en/345023) flavor of third-order logic,[^o12][^o13][^o14][^o15] that is, there can be no algorithm to decide whether an arbitrary equation between second-order (let alone arbitrary higher-order) terms has a solution.

Up to a certain notion of [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828), the power set operation is definable in second-order logic. Using this observation, [Jaakko Hintikka](wikipedia://en/1710903) established in 1955 that second-order logic can simulate higher-order logics in the sense that for every formula of a higher-order logic, one can find an [equisatisfiable](wikipedia://en/15895296) formula for it in second-order logic.[^o16]

The term "higher-order logic" is assumed in some context to refer to *[classical](wikipedia://en/55868)* higher-order logic. However, [modal](wikipedia://en/333365) higher-order logic has been studied as well. According to several logicians, [Gödel's ontological proof](wikipedia://en/12420) is best studied (from a technical perspective) in such a context.[^o17]

:::
