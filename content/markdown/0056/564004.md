# Graph reduction

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Graph reduction"}

In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), **graph reduction** implements an efficient version of non-strict evaluation, an [evaluation strategy](wikipedia://en/2977119) where the arguments to a function are not immediately evaluated. This form of non-strict evaluation is also known as [lazy evaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lazy_evaluation) and used in [functional programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_programming). The technique was first developed by [Chris Wadsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Wadsworth) in 1971.

:::

## Motivation {#Motivation}

A simple example of evaluating an arithmetic expression follows:

$$\begin{aligned} & {} & &((2+2)+(2+2))+(3+3) \\ & {} &=&((2+2)+(2+2))+ 6 \\ & {} &=&((2+2)+ 4)+6 \\ & {} &=&(4+4)+6 \\ & {} &=&8+6 \\ & {} &=&14 \end{aligned}$$

The above reduction sequence employs a strategy known as [outermost tree reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outermost_tree_reduction). The same expression can be evaluated using [innermost tree reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innermost_tree_reduction), yielding the reduction sequence:

$$\begin{aligned} & {} & &((2+2)+(2+2))+(3+3) \\ & {} &= &((2+2)+4)+(3+3) \\ & {} &= &(4+4)+(3+3) \\ & {} &= &(4+4)+6 \\ & {} &= &8+6 \\ & {} &= &14 \end{aligned}$$

Notice that the reduction order is made explicit by the addition of parentheses. This expression could also have been simply evaluated right to left, because addition is an [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) operation.

Represented as a [tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_data_structure), the expression above looks like this:

*Image: Expression Tree.svg.*

This is where the term tree reduction comes from. When represented as a tree, we can think of innermost reduction as working from the bottom up, while outermost works from the top down.

The expression can also be represented as a [directed acyclic graph](wikipedia://en/204002), allowing sub-expressions to be shared:

*Image: Expression Graph.svg.*

As for trees, outermost and innermost reduction also applies to graphs. Hence we have **graph reduction**.

Now evaluation with outermost graph reduction can proceed as follows:

*Image: Expression Graph Reduction.svg.*

Notice that evaluation now only requires four steps. Outermost graph reduction is referred to as [lazy evaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lazy_evaluation) and innermost graph reduction is referred to as [eager evaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eager_evaluation).

## Combinator graph reduction {#Combinator_graph_reduction}

**Combinator graph reduction** is a fundamental implementation technique for [functional programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_programming) languages, in which a program is converted into a [combinator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinator) representation which is mapped to a [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) [data structure](wikipedia://en/8519) in computer memory, and program execution then consists of rewriting parts of this graph ("reducing" it) so as to move towards useful results.

## History {#History}

The concept of a graph reduction that allows evaluated values to be shared was first developed by [Chris Wadsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Wadsworth) in his 1971 Ph.D. dissertation.[^o5] This dissertation was cited by Peter Henderson and James H. Morris Jr. in 1976 paper, “A lazy evaluator”[^o6] that introduced the notion of lazy evaluation. In 1976 [David Turner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Turner_%28computer_scientist%29) incorporated lazy evaluation into [SASL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SASL_programming_language) using combinators.[^o7] SASL was an early functional programming language first developed by Turner in 1972.
