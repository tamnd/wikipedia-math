# Leonid Khachiyan

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Leonid Khachiyan"}

**Leonid Genrikhovich Khachiyan**[^o22] (;[^o20] Леони́д Ге́нрихович Хачия́н; May 3, 1952 – April 29, 2005) was a Soviet and American mathematician and [computer scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_scientist).

:::

He was most famous for his [ellipsoid algorithm](wikipedia://en/4331203) (1979) for [linear programming](wikipedia://en/43730),[^o3] which was the first such [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) known to have a [polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time) running time. Even though this algorithm was shown to be impractical, it has inspired other [randomized algorithms](wikipedia://en/495383) for [convex programming](wikipedia://en/1674411) and is considered a significant theoretical breakthrough.

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Khachiyan was born on May 3, 1952, in [Leningrad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leningrad) to [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians_in_Russia) parents Genrikh Borisovich Khachiyan, a mathematician and professor of [theoretical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_mechanics), and Zhanna Saakovna Khachiyan, a [civil engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_engineer).[^o21][^o22] His grandparents were [Artsakh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artsakh_%28historical_province%29) Armenians.[^o23][^o5] He had two brothers: Boris and Yevgeniy (Eugene).[^o21][^o20] His family moved to [Moscow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow) in 1961, when he was nine.[^o22][^o21] He received a master's degree from the [Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_Institute_of_Physics_and_Technology).[^o20] In 1978 he earned his Ph.D. in [computational mathematics](wikipedia://en/10433833)/[theoretical mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_mathematics) from the [Computer Center of the Soviet Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorodnitsyn_Computing_Centre) and in 1984 a D.Sc. in [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) from the same institution.[^o21][^o20][^o22]

## Career {#Career}

Khachiyan began his career at the Soviet Academy of Sciences,[^o20] working as a researcher at the academy's [Computer Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorodnitsyn_Computing_Centre) in Moscow.[^o22] He also worked as an [adjunct professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjunct_professor) at the [Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_Institute_of_Physics_and_Technology).[^o27] In 1979 he stated: "I am a theoretical mathematician and I'm just working on a class of very difficult mathematical problems."[^o22] Khachiyan immigrated to the United States in 1989.[^o25][^o21] He first taught at [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University) as a visiting professor. In 1990 he joined [Rutgers University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutgers_University) as a visiting professor.[^o20][^o21][^o27] He became [professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenure)[^o19] of [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) at Rutgers in 1992.[^o20][^o21] By 2005, he held the position of Professor II at Rutgers, reserved for those faculty who have achieved scholarly eminence in their discipline.[^o21]

## Work on linear programming {#Work_on_linear_programming}

### Ellipsoid method {#Ellipsoid_method}

Khachiyan is best known for his four-page February 1979 paper[^o9] that indicated how an [ellipsoid method](wikipedia://en/4331203) for [linear programming](wikipedia://en/43730) can be implemented in polynomial time.[^o30][^o27] The paper was translated into several languages and spread around the world unusually quickly. Authors of a 1981 survey of his work noted that it "has caused great excitement and stimulated a flood of technical papers" and was covered by major newspapers.[^o30] It was originally published without proofs, which were provided by Khachiyan in a later paper published in 1980[^o10] and by [Peter Gács](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Gács) and [Laszlo Lovász](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laszlo_Lovász) in 1981.[^o11][^o27][^o30] It was Gács and Lovász who first brought attention to Khachiyan's paper at the International Symposium on Mathematical Programming in Montreal in August 1979.[^o30][^o21] It was further popularized when [Gina Kolata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gina_Kolata) reported it in *[Science Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_%28journal%29)* on November 2, 1979.[^o12][^o19]

Khachiyan's theory is considered a groundbreaking one that "helped advance the field of linear programming."[^o19] [Giorgio Ausiello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giorgio_Ausiello) noted that the method was not practical, "but it was a real breakthrough for the world of operations research and computer science, since it proved that the design of polynomial time algorithms for linear programming was possible and in fact opened the way to other, more practical, algorithms that were designed in the following years."[^o13]

## Personal life and death {#Personal_life_and_death}

Khachiyan spoke Russian and English, but not [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_language).[^o23] Bahman Kalantari noted that "For some, his English accent wasn’t always easy to understand."[^o29] A 1979 *[New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Times)* profile of him described Khachiyan as "a relaxed, friendly young man in a sweater who speaks a little English, which he learned in high school."[^o22]

He was known as "Leo"[^o23][^o24] and "Lenya" to his friends and colleagues.[^o28] [Václav Chvátal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Václav_Chvátal) described him as "selfless, open, patient, sympathetic, understanding, considerate."[^o24] Michael Todd, another colleague, described him as "cynical about politics," "very modest and kind to his friends," and "intolerant of condescension and pomposity."[^o27]

Khachiyan married Olga Pischikova Reynberg, of [Russian-Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Russia) origin,[^o15] in 1985.[^o21][^o27] They had two daughters, [Anna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Khachiyan) and Nina,[^o21][^o20] who were teenagers at the time of his death.[^o27] He became a [naturalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalization) U.S. citizen in 2000.[^o20][^o19] He died of a [heart attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_attack) in [South Brunswick, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Brunswick,_New_Jersey) on April 29, 2005, at the age of 52.[^o20][^o21][^o19]

## Recognition {#Recognition}

In 1982 he was awarded the prestigious [Fulkerson Prize](wikipedia://en/1425916) by the [Mathematical Programming Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Programming_Society) and the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822)[^o25] for outstanding papers in the area of discrete mathematics,[^o21] particularly his 1979 article "A polynomial algorithm in linear programming."[^o17]

Khachiyan was considered a "noted expert in computer science whose work helped computers process extremely complex problems."[^o25] He was called one of the world's most famous computer scientists at the time of his death by Haym Hirsh, chair of the computer science department at Rutgers.[^o21][^o26] "Computer scientists and mathematicians say his work helped revolutionize his field," noted his *[New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Times)* obituary.[^o20] Bahman Kalantari, a friend and colleague at Rutgers, wrote: "Surely, Khachiyan shall always remain to be among the greatest and most legendary figures in the field of mathematical programming."[^o29]
