# Charles Howard Hinton

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Charles Howard Hinton"}

**Charles Howard Hinton** (1853 – 30 April 1907) was a British mathematician and writer of [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction) works titled *Scientific Romances*. He was interested in [higher dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-dimensional_space), particularly the [fourth dimension](wikipedia://en/1364622). He is known for coining the word "[tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112)" and for his work on methods of visualising the geometry of higher dimensions.

:::

## Life {#Life}

Hinton's father, [James Hinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hinton_%28surgeon%29), was a surgeon and advocate of [polygamy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygamy). Charles Hinton was born in England. His siblings included the costume designer [Ada Nettleship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_Nettleship) (1856 – 1932).[^o4]

Hinton taught at [Cheltenham College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheltenham_College)[^o5] while he studied at [Balliol College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balliol_College,_Oxford), where he obtained his B.A. in 1877. From 1880 to 1886, he taught at [Uppingham School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uppingham_School) in [Rutland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutland), where Howard Candler, a friend of [Edwin Abbott Abbott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Abbott_Abbott)'s, also taught.[^o6] Hinton received his M.A. from Oxford in 1886.

In 1880, Hinton married Mary Ellen Boole, daughter of [Mary Everest Boole](wikipedia://en/2770094) and [George Boole](wikipedia://en/38298), the founder of mathematical logic.[^o7] The couple had four children: George (1882–1943), Eric (born 1884), William (1886–1909)[^o8] and [Sebastian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebastian_Hinton) (1887–1923) (inventor of the [jungle gym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungle_gym) and father of [William](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_H._Hinton) and [Joan Hinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Hinton)).

In 1883, he went through a marriage ceremony with Maud Florence Weldon, by whom he had had twin children, under the assumed identity of John Weldon. He was subsequently convicted of [bigamy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bigamy) and spent three days in prison, losing his job at Uppingham.[^o9] His father [James Hinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hinton_%28surgeon%29) was a radical advocate of polygamous relationships,[^o10] and according to Charles' mother James had once remarked to her: "Christ was the saviour of Men but I am the saviour of Women and I don't envy him a bit."[^o11]

In 1887, Charles moved with Mary Ellen to Japan to work in a mission before accepting a job as headmaster of the Victoria Public School. In 1893, he sailed to the United States on the SS *Tacoma* to take up a post at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) as an instructor in mathematics.[^o9]

In 1897, he designed a gunpowder-powered baseball [pitching machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitching_machine) for the Princeton baseball team's batting practice.[^o9][^o12] The machine was versatile, capable of variable speeds with an adjustable breech size, and firing curve balls by the use of two rubber-coated steel fingers at the muzzle of the pitcher.[^o13] He successfully introduced the machine to the [University of Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Minnesota), where Hinton worked as an assistant professor until 1900, when he resigned to move to the [U.S. Naval Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Naval_Observatory) in [Washington, D.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_D.C.)[^o9]

At the end of his life, Hinton worked as an examiner of chemical patents for the [United States Patent Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Patent_Office). At age 54, he died unexpectedly of a [cerebral hemorrhage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerebral_hemorrhage) on 30 April 1907 in [Washington, D.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_D.C.)[^o14][^o15] After Hinton's sudden death his wife, Mary Ellen, committed suicide, also in Washington, D.C., in May 1908.[^o16]

## Fourth dimension {#Fourth_dimension}

*Image: Hinton-1904-Views of the Tessaract-aka Tesseract.jpg.* Frontispiece to Charles Howard Hinton's 1904 book *The Fourth Dimension*, illustrating the tesseract, the four-dimensional analog of the cube. Hinton's spelling varied: also known, as here, "tessaract".

In an 1880 article entitled "What is the Fourth Dimension?", Hinton suggested that points moving around in three dimensions might be imagined as successive cross-sections of a static four-dimensional arrangement of lines passing through a three-dimensional plane, an idea that anticipated the notion of [world lines](wikipedia://en/165487). Hinton's explorations of higher space had a moral basis:

> Hinton argues that gaining an intuitive perception of higher space required that we rid ourselves of the ideas of right and left, up and down, that inheres in our position as observers in a three-dimensional world. Hinton calls the process "casting out the self", equates it with the process of sympathizing with another person, and implies the two processes are mutually reinforcing.[^o18]

Hinton created several [new words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neologisms) to describe elements in the fourth dimension. According to the [OED](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OED), he first used the word *[tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112)* in 1888 in his book *[A New Era of Thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_New_Era_of_Thought)*. [Alicia Boole Stott](wikipedia://en/3325459), his sister in law who knew him at Oxford, supervised the publication of the book whilst he was abroad.[^o19] He also invented the words *kata* (from the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) for "down from") and *ana* (from the Greek for "up toward") to describe the additional two opposing fourth-dimensional directions (an additional 4th axis of motion analogous to left-right (x), up-down (y), and forwards-backwards (z)).[^o20]

Hinton's *[Scientific romances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_romance)*, including "What is the Fourth Dimension?" and "A Plane World", were published as a series of nine pamphlets by Swan Sonnenschein & Co. during 1884–1886. In the introduction to "A Plane World", Hinton referred to Abbott's recent *[Flatland](wikipedia://en/75214)* as having similar design but different intent. Abbott used the stories as "a setting wherein to place his satire and his lessons. But we wish in the first place to know the physical facts." Hinton's world existed along the perimeter of a circle rather than on an infinite flat plane.[^o21] He extended the connection to Abbott's work with *An Episode of Flatland: Or How a Plane Folk Discovered the Third Dimension* (1907).

## *An Episode of Flatland or How a Plane Folk Discovered the Third Dimension…* (1907) {#An_Episode_of_Flatland_or_How_a_Plane_Folk_Discovered_the_Third_Dimension…_(1907)}

*An Episode of Flatland or How a Plane Folk Discovered the Third Dimension, to which is bound up An Outline of the History of Unæa*[^o23] made its public debut in 1907, It received an unflattering paragraph review in the British scientific journal *[Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_%28journal%29)* (1907).[^o24] The action story takes place on the planar world of two-dimensional Astria on which the primary characters partake in pursuits of a scientific and romantic nature. Ultimately, some Astrians come to accept and comprehend the reality and fullness of three-dimensions in a world beyond their immediate comprehension. The book consists of a preface, an introduction, "The History of Astria", and the "episode" referred to in the title, composed of twenty short chapters. At 53,720 words, Hinton's work is longer than [Edwin A. Abbott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_A._Abbott)'s earlier, and more famous, novella *Flatland* (1884).

Hinton's work combines various literary and scientific features, with the author intent on popularizing the idea of higher dimensions among educated [Edwardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwardian) readers including such diverse groups as religious thinkers and believers, experimental scientists, artists, stodgy academics, engineers, politicians, and others of various persuasions and agendas. Recognizing the existence of, and even reaching, a higher [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) was not simply part and parcel to a strictly mathematical game; for Charles H. Hinton (1907), during an era when [spiritualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiritualism_%28movement%29) (with the obligatory séances) was running rampant, it was important to point the way toward a higher realm of existence in both intellectual and genuinely spiritual terms.

## Influence {#Influence}

Hinton's advocacy of the [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112) as a means to perceive higher dimensions spawned a long lineage of science fiction, fantasy, and spiritual works that similarly refer to the tesseract as a way to understand—or even access—higher dimensions, including [Charles Leadbeater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Webster_Leadbeater)'s *Clairvoyance* (1899), [Claude Bragdon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Fayette_Bragdon)'s *A Primer of Higher Space* (1913), [Algernon Blackwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algernon_Blackwood)'s *Victim of Higher Space* (1914), [H. P. Lovecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._P._Lovecraft)'s "[The Shadow Out of Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shadow_Out_of_Time)" (1935), [Robert Heinlein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Heinlein)'s "["—And He Built a Crooked House—"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/"—And_He_Built_a_Crooked_House—")" (1941), [Madeleine L'Engle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madeleine_L'Engle)'s *[A Wrinkle in Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Wrinkle_in_Time)* (1962), and [Christopher Nolan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Nolan)'s film *[Interstellar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_%28film%29)* (2014).[^o26]

Hinton was one of the many thinkers who circulated in [Jorge Luis Borges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jorge_Luis_Borges)'s pantheon of writers. Hinton is mentioned in Borges' short stories "[Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tlön,_Uqbar,_Orbis_Tertius)", "[There Are More Things](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_Are_More_Things)" and "El milagro secreto" ("[The Secret Miracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Secret_Miracle)"): He judged *A Vindication of Eternity* to be less unsatisfactory, perhaps. The first volume documents the diverse eternities that mankind has invented, from [Parmenides](wikipedia://en/23575)' static Being to Hinton's modifiable past; the second denies (with [Francis Bradley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Herbert_Bradley)) that all the events of the universe constitute a temporal series.[^o27]

Hinton influenced [P. D. Ouspensky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._D._Ouspensky)'s thinking. Hinton’s works are mentioned in relation to many of the ideas Ouspensky presents in *Tertium Organum*. He also influenced the work of [occultist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occultist) [Aleister Crowley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleister_Crowley), whose novel *[Moonchild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonchild_%28novel%29)* mentions Hinton, though incorrectly by the name of his father, James Hinton.

[John Dewey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dewey) cited Hinton's concept of the Unlearner in *Art as Experience*, chapter 3. The story Unlearner appears in actually "An Unfinished Communication", part of the second series of "Scientific Romances". (Dewey mistakenly gives an incorrect name for the story.)

Spanish playwright [Carlos Atanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlos_Atanes) wrote the comedy *Un genio olvidado (Un rato en la vida de Charles Howard Hinton)* ("A Forgotten Genius (A While in the Life of Charles Howard Hinton)"), a fictionalized play inspired by Hinton's life and centred on his stay in Yokohama in 1893. It premiered in Madrid at La Pensión de las Pulgas in July 2015.[^o28][^o29]
