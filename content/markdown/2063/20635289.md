# Spectral geometry

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Spectral geometry"}

**Spectral geometry** is a field in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) which concerns relationships between geometric structures of domains and [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) and [spectra](wikipedia://en/206137) of canonically defined [differential operators](wikipedia://en/263886). The case of the [Laplace–Beltrami operator](wikipedia://en/1763356) on a [closed](wikipedia://en/669475) [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) has been most intensively studied, although other [Laplace operators in differential geometry](wikipedia://en/15659323) have also been examined. The field concerns itself with two kinds of questions: direct problems and inverse problems.

:::

Inverse problems seek to identify features of the geometry from information about the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of the Laplacian. One of the earliest results of this kind was due to [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) who used [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302)'s theory of [integral equation](wikipedia://en/474234) in 1911 to show that the volume of a bounded domain in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) can be determined from the [asymptotic behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_behavior) of the eigenvalues for the [Dirichlet boundary value problem](wikipedia://en/423504) of the [Laplace operator](wikipedia://en/174706). This question is usually expressed as "[Can one hear the shape of a drum?](wikipedia://en/1072325)", the popular phrase due to [Mark Kac](wikipedia://en/1062425). A refinement of Weyl's asymptotic formula obtained by Pleijel and Minakshisundaram produces a series of local [spectral invariants](wikipedia://en/41404099) involving [covariant differentiations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariant_differentiation) of the [curvature tensor](wikipedia://en/130526), which can be used to establish spectral rigidity for a special class of manifolds. However as the example given by [John Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632) tells us, the information of eigenvalues is not enough to determine the [isometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometry_%28Riemannian_geometry%29) class of a manifold (see [isospectral](wikipedia://en/386031)). A general and systematic method due to [Toshikazu Sunada](wikipedia://en/22019166) gave rise to a plethora of such examples which clarifies the phenomenon of isospectral manifolds.

Direct problems attempt to infer the behavior of the eigenvalues of a Riemannian manifold from knowledge of the geometry. The solutions to direct problems are typified by the [Cheeger](wikipedia://en/7343761) inequality which gives a relation between the first positive eigenvalue and an [isoperimetric constant](wikipedia://en/326182) (the [Cheeger constant](wikipedia://en/16531739)). Many versions of the inequality have been established since Cheeger's work (by [R. Brooks](wikipedia://en/34336906) and P. Buser for instance).
