# Primitive permutation group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Primitive permutation group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [permutation group](wikipedia://en/24634) *G* [acting](wikipedia://en/12781) on a non-empty finite set *X* is called **primitive** if *G* acts [transitively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_action) on *X* and the only [partitions](wikipedia://en/340240) the *G*-action preserves are the trivial partitions into either a single set or into |*X*| singleton sets. Otherwise, if *G* is transitive and *G* does preserve a nontrivial partition, *G* is called **imprimitive**.

:::

While primitive permutation groups are transitive, not all transitive permutation groups are primitive. The simplest example is the [Klein four-group](wikipedia://en/41928) acting on the vertices of a square, which preserves the partition into diagonals. On the other hand, if a permutation group preserves only trivial partitions, it is transitive, except in the case of the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059) acting on a 2-element set. This is because for a non-transitive action, either the [orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) of *G* form a nontrivial partition preserved by *G*, or the group action is trivial, in which case *all* nontrivial partitions of *X* (which exist for |*X*| ≥ 3) are preserved by *G*.

This terminology was introduced by [Évariste Galois](wikipedia://en/9815) in his last letter, in which he used the French term *équation primitive* for an equation whose [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) is primitive.[^o2]

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

In the same letter in which he introduced the term "primitive", Galois stated the following theorem:[^o5]

> If *G* is a primitive [solvable group](wikipedia://en/105012) acting on a finite set *X*, then the order of *X* is a power of a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) *p*. Further, *X* may be identified with an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) over the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) with *p* elements, and *G* acts on *X* as a subgroup of the [affine group](wikipedia://en/298828).

If the set *X* on which *G* acts is finite, its [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) is called the *degree* of *G*.

A corollary of this result of Galois is that, if $p$ is an odd prime number, then the order of a solvable transitive group of degree $p$ is a divisor of $p(p-1).$ In fact, every transitive group of prime degree is primitive (since the number of elements of a partition fixed by $G$ must be a divisor of $p$), and $p(p-1)$ is the cardinality of the affine group of an affine space with $p$ elements.

It follows that, if $p$ is a prime number greater than 3, the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) and the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) of degree $p$ are not solvable, since their order are greater than $p(p-1).$ The [Abel–Ruffini theorem](wikipedia://en/152518) results from this and the fact that there are polynomials with a symmetric Galois group.

An equivalent definition of primitivity relies on the fact that every transitive action of a group *G* is isomorphic to an action arising from the canonical action of *G* on the set *G*/*H* of [cosets](wikipedia://en/98759) for *H* a subgroup of *G*. A group action is primitive if it is isomorphic to *G*/*H* for a [*maximal* subgroup](wikipedia://en/1472649) *H* of *G*, and imprimitive otherwise (that is, if there is a proper subgroup *K* of *G* of which *H* is a proper subgroup). These imprimitive actions are examples of [induced representations](wikipedia://en/357452).

The numbers of primitive groups of small degree were stated by [Robert Carmichael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Carmichael) in 1937:

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number | 1 | 2 | 2 | 5 | 4 | 7 | 7 | 11 | 9 | 8 | 6 | 9 | 4 | 6 | 22 | 10 | 4 | 8 | 4 | 9 | 4 | 7 | 5 |  |

There are a large number of primitive groups of degree 16. As Carmichael notes, all of these groups, except for the [symmetric](wikipedia://en/28901) and [alternating](wikipedia://en/49726) group, are subgroups of the [affine group](wikipedia://en/298828) on the 4-dimensional space over the 2-element [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615).

## Examples {#Examples}

- Consider the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_3$ acting on the set $X=\{1,2,3\}$ and the permutation

$$\eta=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Both $S_3$ and the group generated by $\eta$ are primitive.

- Now consider the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_4$ acting on the set $\{1,2,3,4\}$ and the permutation

$$\sigma=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 2 & 3 & 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The group generated by $\sigma$ is not primitive, since the partition $(X_1, X_2)$ where $X_1 = \{1,3\}$ and $X_2 = \{2,4\}$ is preserved under $\sigma$, i.e. $\sigma(X_1) = X_2$ and $\sigma(X_2)=X_1$.

- Every transitive group of prime degree is primitive
- The [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_n$ acting on the set $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ is primitive for every *n* and the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) $A_n$ acting on the set $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ is primitive for every *n* > 2.
