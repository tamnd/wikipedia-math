# Folium of Descartes

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Folium of Descartes"}

*Image: Kartesisches-Blatt.svg.* The folium of Descartes (green) with asymptote (blue), when $a=1$

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **folium of Descartes** (la) is an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) defined by the [implicit equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicit_equation) $x^3 + y^3 - 3ayx = 0$. It is named after the mathematician and philosopher [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525).

:::

## History {#History}

The curve was first proposed and studied in 1638 by René Descartes,[^o3] who was interested in classifying curves by the [degrees](wikipedia://en/5930652) of their algebraic equations.[^o4] Its claim to fame lies in an incident in the development of [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176). Descartes challenged [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966) to find the [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) line to the curve at an arbitrary point, since Fermat had recently discovered a method for finding tangent lines. Fermat solved the problem easily, something Descartes was unable to do.[^o5] Since the invention of calculus, the slope of the tangent line can be found easily using [implicit differentiation](wikipedia://en/470397).[^o6]

## Graphing the curve {#Graphing_the_curve}

The folium of Descartes can be expressed in [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) as

$$r = \frac{3 a \sin \theta \cos \theta}{\sin^3 \theta + \cos^3 \theta },$$

which is plotted on the left. This is equivalent to[^o6]

$$r = \frac{3a \sec\theta \tan\theta}{1 + \tan^3 \theta}.$$

Another technique is to write $y = px$ and solve for $x$ and $y$ in terms of $p$. This yields the [rational](wikipedia://en/361210) [parametric equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_equations):[^o8]

$$x = {{3ap} \over {1 + p^3}},\, y = {{3ap^2} \over {1 + p^3}}.$$

We can see that the parameter is related to the position on the curve as follows:

- $p < -1$ corresponds to $x>0$, $y<0$: the right, lower, "wing".
- $-1 < p < 0$ corresponds to $x<0$, $y>0$: the left, upper "wing".
- $p>0$ corresponds to $x>0$, $y>0$: the loop of the curve.

Another way of plotting the function can be derived from symmetry over $y = x$. The symmetry can be seen directly from its equation (x and y can be interchanged). By applying rotation of 45° clockwise for example, one can plot the function symmetric over rotated x axis.

*Image: Декартов лист в полярных координатах.png.* Folium of Descartes in polar coordinates *r-φ:* $r(\phi)/a,\,a=1$

This operation is equivalent to a substitution:

$$x = {{u+v} \over {\sqrt{2}}},\, y = {{u-v} \over {\sqrt{2}}}$$

and yields

$$v=\pm u\sqrt{\frac{3a\sqrt{2}-2u}{6u+3a\sqrt{2}}}\,,\,u<3a/\sqrt{2}.$$

Plotting in the Cartesian system of $(u,v)$ gives the *folium* rotated by 45° and therefore symmetric by $u$-axis.

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

It forms a loop in the first quadrant with a [double point](wikipedia://en/4230456) at the origin and has [asymptote](wikipedia://en/3107)

$$x+y=-a \,.$$

It is symmetrical about the line $y = x$. As such, the curve and this line intersect at the origin and at the point $(3a/2,3a/2).$

Implicit differentiation gives the formula for the slope of the tangent line to this curve to be[^o6]

$$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{ay-x^2}{y^2-ax}\,,$$

with poles $x=y^2/a$ and value 0 or $\pm \infty$ at the origin.

Using either one of the polar representations above, the area of the interior of the loop is found to be $3a^2 / 2$. Moreover, the area between the "wings" of the curve and its slanted asymptote is also $3a^2/2.$[^o3]

:::

## Relationship to the trisectrix of Maclaurin {#Relationship_to_the_trisectrix_of_Maclaurin}

*Image: MaclaurinTrisectrix.SVG.* Trisectrix of Maclaurin

The folium of Descartes is related to the [trisectrix of Maclaurin](wikipedia://en/2738114) by [affine transformation](wikipedia://en/38449). To see this, start with the equation

$$x^3+y^3=3a\cdot xy\,,$$

and change variables to find the equation in a coordinate system rotated 45 degrees. This amounts to setting

$$x = {{X+Y} \over \sqrt{2}}, y = {{X-Y} \over \sqrt{2}}.$$

In the $X,Y$ plane the equation is

$$2X(X^2 + 3Y^2) = 3 \sqrt{2}a(X^2-Y^2).$$

If we stretch the curve in the $Y$ direction by a factor of $\sqrt{3}$ this becomes

$$2X(X^2 + Y^2) = a \sqrt{2}(3X^2-Y^2),$$

which is the equation of the trisectrix of Maclaurin.
