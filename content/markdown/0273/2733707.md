# Boole's expansion theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Boole's expansion theorem"}

**Boole's expansion theorem**, often referred to as the **Shannon expansion** or **Shannon decomposition**, is the [identity](wikipedia://en/298428) $F = x \cdot F_x + x' \cdot F_{x'}$, where $F$ is any [Boolean function](wikipedia://en/753349), $x$ is a variable, $x'$ is the complement of $x$, and $F_x$and $F_{x'}$ are $F$ with the argument $x$ set equal to $1$ and to $0$ respectively.

:::

The terms $F_x$ and $F_{x'}$ are sometimes called the positive and negative *Shannon cofactors*, respectively, of $F$ with respect to $x$. These are functions, computed by the $\operatorname{restrict}$ operator, $\operatorname{restrict}(F, x, 0)$ and $\operatorname{restrict}(F, x, 1)$. (See also [valuation](wikipedia://en/17456938) and [partial application](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_application).)

The theorem has been called the "fundamental theorem of Boolean algebra".[^o12] Besides its theoretical importance, it paved the way for [binary decision diagrams](wikipedia://en/576855) (BDDs), [satisfiability solvers](wikipedia://en/4715), and many other techniques relevant to [computer engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_engineering) and [formal verification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_verification) of digital circuits. In such contexts, particularly in BDDs, the expansion is interpreted as an [if-then-else](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_%28computer_programming%29), with the variable $x$ being the condition and the cofactors being the branches ($F_x$ when $x$ is true and respectively $F_{x'}$ when $x$ is false).[^o2]

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Statement of the theorem"}

## Statement of the theorem {#Statement_of_the_theorem}

A more explicit way of stating the theorem is:

$$f(X_1, X_2, \dots , X_n) = X_1 \cdot f(1, X_2, \dots , X_n) + X_1' \cdot f(0, X_2, \dots , X_n)$$

:::

## Variations and implications {#Variations_and_implications}

**XOR-Form**

The statement also holds when the [disjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjunction) "+" is replaced by the [XOR](wikipedia://en/105979) operator:

$$f(X_1, X_2, \dots , X_n) = X_1 \cdot f(1, X_2, \dots , X_n) \oplus X_1' \cdot f(0, X_2, \dots , X_n)$$

**Dual form**

There is a dual form of the Shannon expansion (which does not have a related XOR form):

$$f(X_1, X_2, \dots , X_n) = (X_1 + f(0, X_2, \dots , X_n)) \cdot (X_1' + f(1, X_2, \dots , X_n))$$

Repeated application for each argument leads to the [Sum of Products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sum_of_Products) (SoP) canonical form of the Boolean function $f$. For example, for $n=2$ that would be

$$\begin{aligned} f(X_1, X_2) & = X_1 \cdot f(1, X_2) + X_1' \cdot f(0, X_2)\\ & = X_1 X_2 \cdot f(1, 1) + X_1 X_2' \cdot f(1, 0) + X_1' X_2 \cdot f(0, 1) + X_1' X_2' \cdot f(0, 0) \end{aligned}$$

Likewise, application of the dual form leads to the [Product of Sums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_of_Sums) (PoS) canonical form (using the [distributivity law](wikipedia://en/103118#Truth_functional_connectives) of $+$ over $\cdot$):

$$\begin{aligned} f(X_1, X_2) & = (X_1 + f(0, X_2)) \cdot (X_1' + f(1, X_2))\\ & = (X_1 + X_2 + f(0, 0)) \cdot (X_1 + X_2' + f(0, 1)) \cdot (X_1' + X_2 + f(1, 0)) \cdot (X_1' + X_2' + f(1, 1)) \end{aligned}$$

## Properties of cofactors {#Properties_of_cofactors}

**Linear properties of cofactors**

For a Boolean function *F* which is made up of two Boolean functions *G* and *H* the following are true:

If $F = H'$ then $F_x = H'_x$

If $F = G \cdot H$ then $F_x = G_x \cdot H_x$

If $F = G + H$ then $F_x = G_x + H_x$

If $F = G \oplus H$ then $F_x = G_x \oplus H_x$

**Characteristics of unate functions**

If *F* is a [unate function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unate_function) and...

If *F* is positive unate then $F = x \cdot F_x + F_{x'}$

If *F* is negative unate then $F = F_x + x' \cdot F_{x'}$

## Operations with cofactors {#Operations_with_cofactors}

**Boolean difference**

The Boolean difference or [Boolean derivative](wikipedia://en/55539147) of the function F with respect to the literal x is defined as:

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial x} = F_x \oplus F_{x'}$$

**Universal quantification**

The [universal quantification](wikipedia://en/76174) of F is defined as:

$$\forall x F = F_x \cdot F_{x'}$$

**Existential quantification**

The [existential quantification](wikipedia://en/91420) of F is defined as:

$$\exists x F = F_x + F_{x'}$$

## History {#History}

[George Boole](wikipedia://en/38298) presented this expansion as his Proposition II, "To expand or develop a function involving any number of logical symbols", in his *[Laws of Thought](wikipedia://en/1993328)* (1854),[^o13] and it was "widely applied by Boole and other nineteenth-century logicians".[^o14]

[Claude Shannon](wikipedia://en/5693) mentioned this expansion, among other Boolean identities, in a 1949 paper,[^o15] and showed the switching network interpretations of the identity. In the literature of computer design and switching theory, the identity is often incorrectly attributed to Shannon.[^o16][^o14]

## Application to switching circuits {#Application_to_switching_circuits}

1. [Binary decision diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_decision_diagrams) follow from systematic use of this theorem
1. Any Boolean function can be implemented directly in a [switching circuit](wikipedia://en/32182026) using a hierarchy of basic [multiplexer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplexer) by repeated application of this theorem.
