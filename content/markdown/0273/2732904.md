# Cantor's paradox

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cantor's paradox"}

In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), **Cantor's paradox** states that there is no [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all [cardinalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinalities). This is derived from the [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) that there is no greatest [cardinal number](wikipedia://en/6173). In informal terms, the paradox is that the collection of all possible "infinite sizes" is not only infinite, but so infinitely large that its own infinite size cannot be any of the infinite sizes in the collection. The difficulty is handled in [axiomatic set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_set_theory) by declaring that this collection is not a set but a [proper class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_class); in [von Neumann–Bernays–Gödel set theory](wikipedia://en/528491) it follows from this and the [axiom of limitation of size](wikipedia://en/5322450) that this proper class must be in [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) with the class of all sets. Thus, not only are there infinitely many infinities, but this infinity is larger than any of the infinities it enumerates.

:::

This [paradox](wikipedia://en/24390) is named for [Georg Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216), who is often credited with first identifying it in 1899 (or between 1895 and 1897). Like a number of "paradoxes" it is not actually contradictory but merely indicative of a mistaken intuition, in this case about the nature of infinity and the notion of a set. Put another way, it *is* paradoxical within the confines of [naïve set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naïve_set_theory) and therefore demonstrates that a careless axiomatization of this theory is inconsistent.

## Statements and proofs {#Statements_and_proofs}

In order to state the paradox it is necessary to understand that the cardinal numbers are [totally ordered](wikipedia://en/30330), so that one can speak about one being greater or less than another. Then Cantor's paradox is:

*Theorem:* There is no greatest cardinal number.

This fact is a direct consequence of [Cantor's theorem](wikipedia://en/341442) on the cardinality of the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of a set.

*Proof:* Assume the contrary, and let *C* be the largest cardinal number. Then (in the [von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) formulation of cardinality) *C* is a set and therefore has a power set 2<sup>*C*</sup> which, by Cantor's theorem, has cardinality strictly larger than *C*. Demonstrating a cardinality (namely that of 2<sup>*C*</sup>) larger than *C*, which was assumed to be the greatest cardinal number, falsifies the definition of C. This contradiction establishes that such a cardinal cannot exist.

Another consequence of [Cantor's theorem](wikipedia://en/341442) is that the cardinal numbers constitute a [proper class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_class). That is, they cannot all be collected together as elements of a single set. Here is a somewhat more general result.

*Theorem:* If *S* is any set then *S* cannot contain elements of all cardinalities. In fact, there is a strict upper bound on the cardinalities of the elements of *S*.

*Proof:* Let *S* be a set, and let *T* be the union of the elements of *S*. Then every element of *S* is a subset of *T*, and hence is of cardinality less than or equal to the cardinality of *T*. [Cantor's theorem](wikipedia://en/341442) then implies that every element of *S* is of cardinality strictly less than the cardinality of 2<sup>*T*</sup>.

## Discussion and consequences {#Discussion_and_consequences}

Since the cardinal numbers are well-ordered by indexing with the [ordinal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinal_numbers) (see [Cardinal number, formal definition](wikipedia://en/6173#Formal_definition)), this also establishes that there is no greatest ordinal number; conversely, the latter statement implies Cantor's paradox. By applying this indexing to the [Burali-Forti paradox](wikipedia://en/51653) we obtain another proof that the cardinal numbers are a [proper class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_class) rather than a set, and (at least in [ZFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZFC) or in [von Neumann–Bernays–Gödel set theory](wikipedia://en/528491)) it follows from this that there is a bijection between the class of cardinals and the class of all sets. Since every set is a subset of this latter class, and every cardinality is the cardinality of a set (by definition!) this intuitively means that the "cardinality" of the collection of cardinals is greater than the cardinality of any set: it is more infinite than any true infinity. This is the paradoxical nature of Cantor's "paradox".

## Historical notes {#Historical_notes}

While Cantor is usually credited with first identifying this property of cardinal sets, some mathematicians award this distinction to [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163), who defined a similar theorem in 1899 or 1901.
