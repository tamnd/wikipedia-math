# Congeneric reliability

Reliability (statistics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Congeneric reliability"}

In statistical models applied to [psychometrics](wikipedia://en/24982), **congeneric reliability** $\rho_C$ ("rho C")[^o2] a single-administration test score reliability (i.e., the reliability of persons over items holding occasion fixed) coefficient, commonly referred to as **composite reliability**, **construct reliability**, and **coefficient omega**. $\rho_C$ is a [structural equation model](wikipedia://en/2007748) (SEM)-based reliability coefficients and is obtained from a unidimensional model. $\rho_C$ is the second most commonly used reliability factor after [Cronbach's alpha](wikipedia://en/379269)), and is often recommended as its alternative.

:::

## History and names {#History_and_names}

A quantity similar (but not mathematically equivalent) to congeneric reliability first appears in the appendix to McDonald's 1970 paper on [factor analysis](wikipedia://en/253492), labeled $\theta$.[^o4] In McDonald's work, the new quantity is primarily a mathematical convenience: a well-behaved intermediate that [separates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separating_hyperplane_theorem) two values. Seemingly unaware of McDonald's work, Jöreskog first analyzed a quantity equivalent to congeneric reliability in a paper the following year.[^o6][^o7] Jöreskog defined congeneric reliability (now labeled ρ) with [coordinate-free](wikipedia://en/18858579) notation,[^o7] and three years later, Werts gave the modern, coordinatized formula for the same.[^o8] Both of the latter two papers named the new quantity simply "reliability".[^o7][^o8] The modern name originates with Jöreskog's name for the model whence he derived $\rho_{C}$: a "congeneric model".[^o2][^o9][^o10]

Applied statisticians have subsequently coined many names for ${\rho}_{C}$. "Composite reliability" emphasizes that ${\rho}_{C}$ measures the [statistical reliability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_reliability) of composite scores.[^o2][^o11] As psychology calls "[constructs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construct_%28psychology%29)" any [latent characteristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latent_variable) only measurable through composite scores,[^o12] ${\rho}_{C}$ has also been called "construct reliability".[^o13] Following McDonald's more recent expository work on [testing theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testing_theory), some SEM-based reliability coefficients, including congeneric reliability, are referred to as "reliability coefficient $\omega$", often without a definition.[^o2][^o14][^o15]

## Formula and calculation {#Formula_and_calculation}

*Image: Congeneric measurement model.png.* Congeneric measurement model

Congeneric reliability applies to [datasets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dataset) of [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28math%29): each row $X$ in the dataset is a list $X_{i}$ of numerical scores corresponding to one individual. The congeneric model supposes that there is a single underlying property ("factor") of the individual $F$, such that each numerical score $X_{i}$ is a noisy measurement of $F$. Moreover, that the relationship between $X$ and $F$ is [approximately linear](wikipedia://en/1118789): there exist (non-random) vectors $\lambda$ and $\mu$ such that

$$X_i=\lambda_iF+\mu_i+E_i\text{,}$$

where $E_{i}$ is a [statistically independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistically_independent) noise term.[^o7]

In this context, $\lambda_{i}$ is often referred to as the [factor loading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_loading) on item $i$.

Because $\lambda$ and $\mu$ are free parameters, the model exhibits [affine invariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_symmetry), and $F$ may be normalized to [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_%28statistics%29) $0$ and [variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variance_%28statistics%29) $1$ [without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000). The [fraction of variance explained](wikipedia://en/1500869) in item $X_{i}$ by $F$ is then simply

$$\rho_i=\frac{\lambda_i^2}{\lambda_i^2+\mathbb{V}[E_i]}\text{.}$$

More generally, given any [covector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covector) $w$, the proportion of variance in $wX$ explained by $F$ is

$$\rho=\frac{(w\lambda)^2}{(w\lambda)^2+\mathbb{E}[(wE)^2]}\text{,}$$

which is maximized when $w \propto E[EE^{*}]^{-1}\lambda$.[^o7]

$\rho_{C}$ is this proportion of [explained variance](wikipedia://en/14921993) in the case where $w \propto [1 1 ... 1]$ (all components of $X$ equally important):

$$\rho_C = \frac{ \left( \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i \right)^2 }{ \left( \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i \right)^2 + \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma^2_{E_i} }$$

These are the estimates of the factor loadings and errors:

*Factor loadings and errors*

|  | $\hat{\lambda}_i$ | $\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{e_i}$ |
|---|---|---|
| $X_1$ | $1.96$ | $6.13$ |
| $X_2$ | $2.25$ | $5.92$ |

$$\hat{\rho}_{C} = \frac{ \left( \sum_{i=1}^k \hat{\lambda}_i \right)^2 }{ \hat{\sigma}^{2}_{X} } = \frac{ 106.22 }{ 124.23 } = .8550$$

$$\hat{\rho}_{C} = \frac{ \left( \sum_{i=1}^k \hat{\lambda}_i \right)^2 }{ \left( \sum_{i=1}^k \hat{\lambda}_i \right)^2 + \sum_{i=1}^k \hat{\sigma}^{2}_{e_i} } = \frac{ 106.22 }{ 106.22 + 18.01 } = .8550$$

Compare this value with the value of applying [Cronbach's alpha](wikipedia://en/379269) to the same data.

## Related coefficients {#Related_coefficients}

[Cronbach's alpha](wikipedia://en/379269) ($\alpha$) assumes that all factor loadings are equal (i.e. $\lambda_1=\lambda_2=...=\lambda_k$). In reality, this is rarely the case and, thus, it systematically underestimates the reliability. In contrast, congeneric reliability ($\rho_C$) explicitly acknowledges the existence of different factor loadings. According to Bagozzi & Yi (1988), $\rho_C$ should have a value of at least around 0.6.[^o23] Often, higher values are desirable. However, such values should not be misunderstood as strict cutoff boundaries between "good" and "bad".[^o24] Moreover, $\rho_{C}$ values close to 1 might indicate that items are too similar. Another property of a "good" measurement model besides reliability is [construct validity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construct_validity).

A related coefficient is [average variance extracted](wikipedia://en/53750318).
