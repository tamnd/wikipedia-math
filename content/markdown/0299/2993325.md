# Henry M. Sheffer

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Henry M. Sheffer"}

**Henry Maurice Sheffer** (1 September 1882 – 17 March 1964)[^o2] was an American [logician](wikipedia://en/19636).

:::

## Life and career {#Life_and_career}

Sheffer was a [Polish Jew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Jew) born in [Odessa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odessa) who immigrated to the U.S. in 1892 with his parents and six siblings. He studied at the [Boston Latin School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_Latin_School) before entering [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University), learning logic from [Josiah Royce](wikipedia://en/494060), and completing his undergraduate degree in 1905, his master's in 1907, and his Ph.D. in philosophy under Royce in 1908.

Sheffer was a postdoctoral fellow at Harvard, and then taught at the [University of Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Washington), [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University), the [University of Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Minnesota), the [University of Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Missouri), and [City College of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_College_of_New_York) for one year each. In 1916, he was hired by Harvard as a philosophy professor, where he stayed until he retired in 1952. Scanlan (2000) is a study of Sheffer's life and work.

Sheffer proved in 1913 that [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/54476844) could be defined using a single primitive binary operation, "not both . . . and . . .", now abbreviated [NAND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_NAND), or its dual [NOR](wikipedia://en/209154), (in the sense of "neither . . . nor").[^o4] Likewise, the [propositional calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus) could be formulated using a single connective, having the [truth table](wikipedia://en/25512250) either of the [logical NAND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_NAND), usually symbolized with a vertical line called the [Sheffer stroke](wikipedia://en/29388), or its dual [logical NOR](wikipedia://en/209154) (usually symbolized with a vertical arrow or with a [dagger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagger_%28typography%29) symbol). [Charles Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) had also discovered these facts in 1880, but the relevant paper was not published until 1933. Sheffer also proposed axioms formulated solely in terms of his stroke.

Sheffer introduced what is now known as the Sheffer stroke in 1913; it became well known only after its use in the 1925 edition of [Whitehead](wikipedia://en/43395) and [Russell's](wikipedia://en/4163) *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)*. Sheffer's discovery won great praise from Bertrand Russell, who used it extensively to simplify his own logic, in the second edition of his *Principia Mathematica*. Because of this comment, Sheffer was something of a mystery man to logicians, especially because Sheffer, who published little in his career, never published the details of this method, only describing it in mimeographed notes and in a brief published abstract. [W. V. Quine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._V._Quine)'s *Mathematical Logic* also made much of the Sheffer stroke. A [Sheffer connective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheffer_connective), subsequently, is any connective in a [logical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_system) that functions analogously: one in terms of which all other possible connectives in the language can be expressed. For example, they have been developed for quantificational and [modal](wikipedia://en/333365) logics as well.[^o5]

Sheffer died in [Boston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston).
