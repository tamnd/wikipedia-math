# Polynomial transformation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Polynomial transformation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **polynomial transformation** consists of computing the polynomial whose [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) are a given function of the roots of a polynomial. Polynomial transformations such as [Tschirnhaus transformations](wikipedia://en/595824) are often used to simplify the solution of [algebraic equations](wikipedia://en/404001).

:::

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Simple examples"}

## Simple examples {#Simple_examples}

### Translating the roots {#Translating_the_roots}

Let

$$P(x) = a_0x^n + a_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_{n}$$

be a polynomial, and

$$\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n$$

be its complex roots (not necessarily distinct).

For any constant $c$, the polynomial whose roots are

$$\alpha_1+c, \ldots, \alpha_n+c$$

is

$$Q(y) = P(y-c)= a_0(y-c)^n + a_1 (y-c)^{n-1} + \cdots + a_{n}.$$

If the coefficients of $P$ are [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) and the constant $c=\frac{p}{q}$ is a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024), the coefficients of $Q$ may be not integers, but the polynomial $c^{n} Q$ has integer coefficients and has the same roots as $Q$.

A special case is when $c=\frac{a_1}{na_0}.$ The resulting polynomial $Q$ does not have any term in $y^{n - 1}$.

### Reciprocals of the roots {#Reciprocals_of_the_roots}

Let

$$P(x) = a_0x^n + a_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_{n}$$

be a polynomial. The polynomial whose roots are the [reciprocals](wikipedia://en/229940) of the roots of $P$ is its [reciprocal polynomial](wikipedia://en/1106042)

$$Q(y)= y^nP\left(\frac{1}{y}\right)= a_ny^n + a_{n-1} y^{n-1} + \cdots + a_{0}.$$

### Scaling the roots {#Scaling_the_roots}

Let

$$P(x) = a_0x^n + a_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_{n}$$

be a polynomial, and $c$ be a non-zero constant. A polynomial whose roots are the product by $c$ of the roots of $P$ is

$$Q(y)=c^nP\left(\frac{y}{c} \right) = a_0y^n + a_1 cy^{n-1} + \cdots + a_{n}c^n.$$

The factor $c^{n}$ appears here because, if $c$ and the coefficients of $P$ are integers or belong to some [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462), the same is true for the coefficients of $Q$.

In the special case where $c=a_0$, all coefficients of $Q$ are multiples of $c$, and $\frac{Q}{c}$ is a [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730), whose coefficients belong to any integral domain containing $c$ and the coefficients of $P$. This polynomial transformation is often used to reduce questions on [algebraic numbers](wikipedia://en/1158) to questions on [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981).

Combining this with a translation of the roots by $\frac{a_1}{na_0}$, allows to reduce any question on the roots of a polynomial, such as [root-finding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root-finding), to a similar question on a simpler polynomial, which is monic and does not have a term of degree $n - 1$. For examples of this, see [Cubic function § Reduction to a depressed cubic](wikipedia://en/243709#Reduction_to_a_depressed_cubic) or [Quartic function § Converting to a depressed quartic](wikipedia://en/308152#Converting_to_a_depressed_quartic).

:::

## Transformation by a rational function {#Transformation_by_a_rational_function}

All preceding examples are polynomial transformations by a [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210), also called [Tschirnhaus transformations](wikipedia://en/595824). Let

$$f(x)=\frac{g(x)}{h(x)}$$

be a rational function, where $g$ and $h$ are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) polynomials. The polynomial transformation of a polynomial $P$ by $f$ is the polynomial $Q$ (defined [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) the product by a non-zero constant) whose roots are the images by $f$ of the roots of $P$.

Such a polynomial transformation may be computed as a [resultant](wikipedia://en/2195020). In fact, the roots of the desired polynomial $Q$ are exactly the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $y$ such that there is a complex number $x$ such that one has simultaneously (if the coefficients of $P, g$ and $h$ are not real or complex numbers, *"complex number"* has to be replaced by *"element of an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) containing the coefficients of the input polynomials"*)

$$\begin{aligned} P(x)&=0\\ y\,h(x)-g(x)&=0\,. \end{aligned}$$

This is exactly the defining property of the resultant

$$\operatorname{Res}_x(y\,h(x)-g(x),P(x)).$$

This is generally difficult to compute by hand. However, as most [computer algebra systems](wikipedia://en/54427) have a built-in function to compute resultants, it is straightforward to compute it with a [computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer).

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

If the polynomial $P$ is [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725), then either the resulting polynomial $Q$ is irreducible, or it is a power of an irreducible polynomial. Let $\alpha$ be a root of $P$ and consider $L$, the [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) generated by $\alpha$. The former case means that $f(\alpha)$ is a [primitive element](wikipedia://en/4771726) of $L$, which has $Q$ as [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106). In the latter case, $f(\alpha)$ belongs to a subfield of $L$ and its minimal polynomial is the irreducible polynomial that has $Q$ as power.

:::

## Transformation for equation-solving {#Transformation_for_equation-solving}

Polynomial transformations have been applied to the simplification of polynomial equations for solution, where possible, by radicals. Descartes introduced the transformation of a polynomial of degree $d$ which eliminates the term of degree $d - 1$ by a translation of the roots. Such a polynomial is termed **depressed**. This already suffices to solve the quadratic by square roots. In the case of the cubic, Tschirnhaus transformations replace the variable by a quadratic function, thereby making it possible to eliminate two terms, and so can be used to eliminate the linear term in a depressed cubic to achieve the solution of the cubic by a combination of square and cube roots. The Bring–Jerrard transformation, which is quartic in the variable, brings a quintic into [Bring-Jerrard normal form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bring-Jerrard_normal_form) with terms of degree 5,1, and 0.
