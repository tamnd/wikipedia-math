# Sparse Fourier transform

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sparse Fourier transform"}

The **sparse Fourier transform** (**SFT**) is a kind of [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811) (DFT) for handling [big data](wikipedia://en/27051151) signals. Specifically, it is used in [GPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPS) synchronization, spectrum sensing and [analog-to-digital converters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analog-to-digital_converter).:[^o2]

:::

The [fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512) (FFT) plays an indispensable role on many scientific domains, especially on signal processing. It is one of the top-10 algorithms in the twentieth century.[^o3] However, with the advent of big data era, the FFT still needs to be improved in order to save more computing power. Recently, the sparse Fourier transform (SFT) has gained a considerable amount of attention, for it performs well on analyzing the long sequence of data with few signal components.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Consider a sequence *x*<sub>*n*</sub> of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826). By [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038), *x*<sub>*n*</sub> can be written as

$$x_n=(F^*X)_n=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}X_k e^{j\frac{2\pi}{N}kn}.$$

Similarly, *X*<sub>*k*</sub> can be represented as

$$X_k=\frac{1}{N}(Fx)_k=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x_n e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}kn}.$$

Hence, from the equations above, the mapping is $F:\mathbb C^N\to \mathbb C^N$.

### Single frequency recovery {#Single_frequency_recovery}

Assume only a single frequency exists in the sequence. In order to recover this frequency from the sequence, it is reasonable to utilize the relationship between adjacent points of the sequence.

#### Phase encoding {#Phase_encoding}

The phase *k* can be obtained by dividing the adjacent points of the sequence. In other words,

$$\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n}=e^{j\frac{2\pi}{N}k} = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi k}{N}\right)+j \sin\left(\frac{2\pi k}{N}\right).$$

Notice that $x_n \in \mathbb C^N$.

#### An aliasing-based search {#An_aliasing-based_search}

*Image: Aliasing-based search.png.* An aliasing-based search

Seeking phase *k* can be done by [Chinese remainder theorem](wikipedia://en/7713) (CRT).[^o9]

Take $k=104{,}134$ for an example. Now, we have three relatively prime integers 100, 101, and 103. Thus, the equation can be described as

$$k=104{,}134\equiv \left\{ \begin{array}{rl} 34 & \bmod 100, \\ 3 & \bmod 101, \\ 1 & \bmod 103. \end{array} \right.$$

By CRT, we have

$$k=104{,}134\bmod (100\cdot101\cdot103)=104{,}134\bmod 1{,}040{,}300$$

#### Randomly binning frequencies {#Randomly_binning_frequencies}

*Image: Randomlybinning.pdf.* Spread all frequencies

Now, we desire to explore the case of multiple frequencies, instead of a single frequency. The adjacent frequencies can be separated by the scaling *c* and modulation *b* properties. Namely, by randomly choosing the parameters of *c* and *b*, the distribution of all frequencies can be almost a uniform distribution. The figure *Spread all frequencies* reveals by randomly binning frequencies, we can utilize the single frequency recovery to seek the main components.

$$x_n'=X_k e^{j\frac{2\pi}{N}(c\cdot k+b)},$$

where *c* is scaling property and *b* is modulation property.

By randomly choosing *c* and *b*, the whole spectrum can be looked like [uniform distribution](wikipedia://en/1699214). Then, taking them into [filter banks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filter_bank) can separate all frequencies, including Gaussians,[^o11] indicator functions,[^o12][^o13] spike trains,[^o14][^o15][^o16][^o17] and Dolph-Chebyshev filters.[^o18] Each bank only contains a single frequency.

:::

## The prototypical SFT {#The_prototypical_SFT}

Generally, all SFT follows the three stages[^o2]

### Identifying frequencies {#Identifying_frequencies}

By randomly bining frequencies, all components can be separated. Then, taking them into filter banks, so each band only contains a single frequency. It is convenient to use the methods we mentioned to recover this signal frequency.

### Estimating coefficients {#Estimating_coefficients}

After identifying frequencies, we will have many frequency components. We can use Fourier transform to estimate their coefficients.

$$X_k'=\frac 1 L \sum_{l=1}^L x_n'e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}n'\ell}$$

### Repeating {#Repeating}

Finally, repeating these two stages can we extract the most important components from the original signal.

$$x_n-\sum_{k'=1}^k X_k' e^{j\frac{2\pi}{N}k'n}$$

## Sparse Fourier transform in the discrete setting {#Sparse_Fourier_transform_in_the_discrete_setting}

In 2012, Hassanieh, Indyk, Katabi, and Price [^o18] proposed an algorithm that takes $O ( k \log n \log (n/k) )$ samples and runs in the same running time.

## Sparse Fourier transform in the high dimensional setting {#Sparse_Fourier_transform_in_the_high_dimensional_setting}

In 2014, Indyk and Kapralov [^o25] proposed an algorithm that takes $2^{O(d \log d)} k \log n$ samples and runs in nearly linear time in $n$. In 2016, Kapralov [^o26] proposed an algorithm that uses sublinear samples $2^{O(d^2)} k \log n \log \log n$ and sublinear decoding time $k \log^{O(d)} n$. In 2019, Nakos, Song, and Wang [^o27] introduced a new algorithm which uses nearly optimal samples $O( k \log n \log k )$ and requires nearly linear time decoding time. A dimension-incremental algorithm was proposed by Potts, Volkmer [^o28] based on sampling along rank-1 lattices.

## Sparse Fourier transform in the continuous setting {#Sparse_Fourier_transform_in_the_continuous_setting}

There are several works about generalizing the discrete setting into the continuous setting.[^o30][^o31]
