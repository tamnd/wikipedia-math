# Arithmetic derivative

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Arithmetic derivative"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), the **Lagarias arithmetic derivative** or **number derivative** is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) defined for [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), based on [prime factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factorization), by analogy with the [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988) for the [derivative of a function](wikipedia://en/7921) that is used in [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396).

:::

There are many versions of "arithmetic derivatives", including the one discussed in this article (the Lagarias arithmetic derivative), such as Ihara's arithmetic derivative and Buium's arithmetic derivatives.

## Early history {#Early_history}

The arithmetic derivative was introduced by Spanish mathematician Josè Mingot Shelly in 1911.[^o3][^o4] The arithmetic derivative also appeared in the 1950 [Putnam Competition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lowell_Putnam_Competition).[^o5]

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

For [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) $n$, the arithmetic derivative $D(n)$[^o8] is defined as follows:

- $D(p) = 1$ for any [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) $p$.
- $D(mn) = D(m)n + mD(n)$ for any $m, n \in \mathbb{N}$ ([Leibniz rule](wikipedia://en/248988)).

:::

## Extensions beyond natural numbers {#Extensions_beyond_natural_numbers}

[Edward J. Barbeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Barbeau) extended the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) to all integers by showing that the choice $D(-n) = -D(n)$ uniquely extends the domain to the integers and is consistent with the product formula. Barbeau also further extended it to the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), showing that the familiar [quotient rule](wikipedia://en/209128) gives a well-defined derivative on $\mathbb{Q}$:

$$D\!\left(\frac{m}{n}\right) = \frac{D(m)n-m D(n)}{n^2} .$$

[^o10][^o11]

[Victor Ufnarovski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Ufnarovski) and [Bo Åhlander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bo_Åhlander) expanded it to the [irrationals](wikipedia://en/20647689) that can be written as the product of primes raised to arbitrary rational powers, allowing expressions like $D(\sqrt{3}\,)$ to be computed.[^o12]

The arithmetic derivative can also be extended to any [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546) (UFD),[^o12] such as the [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628) and the [Eisenstein integers](wikipedia://en/2353035), and its associated [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271). If the UFD is a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065), then the arithmetic derivative is the same as the [derivation](wikipedia://en/897658) over said polynomial ring. For example, the regular [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) is the arithmetic derivative for the rings of [univariate](wikipedia://en/2398982) [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) and [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) and [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210), which can be proven using the [fundamental theorem of algebra](wikipedia://en/51414).

The arithmetic derivative has also been extended to the [ring of integers modulo *n*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_integers_modulo_n).[^o13]

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Elementary properties"}

## Elementary properties {#Elementary_properties}

The Leibniz rule implies that $D(0) = 0$ (take $m = n = 0$) and $D(1) = 0$ (take $m = n = 1$).

The *power rule* is also valid for the arithmetic derivative. For any integers $k$ and $n \ge 0$:

$$D(k^n) = nk^{n-1} D(k).$$

This allows one to compute the derivative from the prime factorization of an integer, $x = \prod\limits_{p \in \mathbb{P}} p^{n_p}$ (in which $n_p = \nu_p(x)$ is the [*p*-adic valuation](wikipedia://en/16047618) of $x$) :

$$D(x) = \sum\limits_{p \in \mathbb{P}} \frac{x}{p^{n_p}} n_p \, p^{n_p-1} D(p) = \sum_{\stackrel{p \vert x}{p \in \mathbb{P}}} n_p \frac x p D(p) = x \sum_{\stackrel{p \vert x}{p \in \mathbb{P}}} \frac {n_p} {p} D(p).$$

This shows that if one knows the derivative for all prime numbers, then the derivative is fully known. In fact, the family of arithmetic partial derivatives $\frac \partial {\partial p}$ relative to the prime number $p$, defined by $\frac \partial {\partial p}(q)=0$ for all primes $q$, except for $q=p$ for which $\frac \partial {\partial p}(p)=1$ allows to write any derivative as an (improper) infinite sum (see below for an example). Note that, for this derivative, we have $\frac {\partial x}{\partial p} = n_p \frac x p$.

Usually, one takes the derivative such that $D(p)=1$ for all primes $p$, so that

$$D=\sum\limits_{p \in \mathbb{P}}\frac \partial {\partial p} \text{, and } D(x)= x \sum\limits_{p \in \mathbb{P}} \frac {n_p} p.$$

With this derivative, we have for example:

$$D(60) = D(2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5) = 60 \cdot \left(\frac{2}{2} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5}\right) = 92,$$

or

$$D(81) = D(3^4) = 4\cdot 3^3\cdot D(3) = 4\cdot 27\cdot 1 = 108.$$

And the [sequence](wikipedia://en/51421) of number derivatives for $x = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ begins :

$$0, 0, 1, 1, 4, 1, 5, 1, 12, 6, 7, 1, 16, 1, 9, \ldots$$

:::

## Related functions {#Related_functions}

The *logarithmic derivative*

$$\operatorname{ld}(x)=\frac{D(x)}{x} = \sum_{\stackrel{p \,\mid\, x}{p \in \mathbb{P}}} \frac {\nu_p(x)} {p}$$

is a [totally additive function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_additive_function):

$$\operatorname{ld}(x \cdot y) = \operatorname{ld}(x)+\operatorname{ld}(y).$$

Let $p$ be a prime. The *arithmetic partial derivative* of $x$ with respect to $p$ is defined as $D_p(x) = \frac {\nu_p(x)} {p} x.$ So, the arithmetic derivative of $x$ is given as $D(x) = \sum_{\stackrel{p \,\mid\, x}{p \in \mathbb{P}}} D_p(x).$

Let $S$ be a nonempty set of primes. The *arithmetic subderivative* of $x$ with respect to $S$ is defined as $D_S(x) = \sum_{\stackrel{p \,\mid\, x}{p \in S}} D_p(x).$ If $S$ is the set of all primes, then $D_S(x) = D(x),$ the usual arithmetic derivative. If $S=\{p\}$, then $D_S(x) = D_p(x),$ the arithmetic partial derivative.

An arithmetic function $f$ is *Leibniz-additive* if there is a [totally multiplicative function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_multiplicative_function) $h_f$ such that $f(mn) = f(m)h_f(n)+f(n)h_f(m)$ for all positive integers $m$ and $n$. A motivation for this concept is the fact that Leibniz-additive functions are generalizations of the arithmetic derivative $D$; namely, $D$ is Leibniz-additive with $h_D(n)=n$.

The function $\delta$ given in Section 3.5 of the book by Sandor and Atanassov is, in fact, exactly the same as the usual arithmetic derivative $D$.

## Inequalities and bounds {#Inequalities_and_bounds}

E. J. Barbeau examined bounds on the arithmetic derivative[^o18] and found that

$$D(n) \leq \frac{n \log_2 n}{2}$$

and

$$D(n) \geq \Omega(n)\, n^{\frac{\Omega(n)-1}{\Omega(n)}}$$

where $\Omega(n)$, a [prime omega function](wikipedia://en/57197043), is the number of prime factors in $n$. In both bounds above, equality always occurs when $n$ is a [power of 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_of_2).

Dahl, Olsson and Loiko found the arithmetic derivative of natural numbers is bounded by[^o19]

$$D(n) \leq \frac{n \log_p n}{p}$$

where $p$ is the least prime in $n$ and equality holds when $n$ is a power of $p$.

[Alexander Loiko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Loiko), [Jonas Olsson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonas_Ernst_Olsson) and [Niklas Dahl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niklas_Dahl) found that it is impossible to find similar bounds for the arithmetic derivative extended to rational numbers by proving that between any two rational numbers there are other rationals with arbitrary large or small derivatives (note that this means that the arithmetic derivative is not a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) from $\mathbb{Q}$ to $\mathbb{Q}$).

## Order of the average {#Order_of_the_average}

We have

$$\sum_{n \le x} \frac{D(n)}{n} = T_0 x + O(\log x \log\log x)$$

and

$$\sum_{n \le x} D(n) = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)T_0 x^2 + O(x^{1+\delta})$$

for any *δ* > 0, where

$$T_0 = \sum_p \frac{1}{p(p-1)}.$$

## Relevance to number theory {#Relevance_to_number_theory}

[Victor Ufnarovski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Ufnarovski) and [Bo Åhlander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bo_Åhlander) have detailed the function's connection to famous number-theoretic [conjectures](wikipedia://en/6138) like the [twin prime conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_prime_conjecture), the prime triples conjecture, and [Goldbach's conjecture](wikipedia://en/44758). For example, Goldbach's conjecture would imply, for each $k > 1$ the existence of an $n$ so that $D(n) = 2k$. The twin prime conjecture would imply that there are infinitely many $k$ for which $D^{2}(k) = 1$.[^o12]
