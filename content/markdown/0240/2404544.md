# Poincaré recurrence theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Poincaré recurrence theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), the **Poincaré Recurrence Theorem** is a foundational result in both [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems) and [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481). The theorem is named after [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740), who discussed it in 1890.[^o2][^o3] A proof was presented by [Constantin Carathéodory](wikipedia://en/470625) using [measure theory](wikipedia://en/19873) in 1919.[^o4][^o5]

:::

In simple terms, it states that if a system has a **finite volume** (or total probability measure) and preserves that volume over time, then almost every initial state will eventually return arbitrarily close to its starting position, and it will do so infinitely many times.

## Definitions and Poincaré's formulation {#Definitions_and_Poincaré's_formulation}

Any [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087) defined by an [ordinary differential equation](wikipedia://en/32742753) determines a [flow map](wikipedia://en/2619735) *f*<sup>*t*</sup> mapping [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) to itself. Each point of the phase space describes the entire state of the system, typically the positions and velocities of all involved particles. The flow map tells us how the system evolves: if it starts in state *x*, then after time *t* the system will be in state *f*<sup>*t*</sup>(*x*). If we follow this point for all times *t*, we obtain the *[orbit](wikipedia://en/252250)* of *x*. The system is said to be [volume-preserving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume-preserving) if the volume of each set in phase space is invariant under the flow. Poincaré's formulation of the theorem then states:

If a flow preserves volume and has only bounded orbits, then, for each [open set](wikipedia://en/39358), there is an orbit that intersects this open set infinitely often.[^o7]

A consequence of the theorem is that conservative [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems) will, after a sufficiently long but finite time, almost certainly return to a state arbitrarily close to their initial state. The result applies to isolated mechanical systems subject to some constraints, e.g., all particles must be bound to a finite volume. Systems to which the Poincaré recurrence theorem applies are called [conservative systems](wikipedia://en/8918323).

The **Poincaré recurrence time** estimated by [Kac's lemma](wikipedia://en/63490326), is the length of time elapsed until the recurrence. This time may vary greatly depending on the exact initial state and required degree of closeness.

## Discussion, example and applications {#Discussion,_example_and_applications}

We may think of the open set as tiny; the claim is that there will be orbits that return to this tiny set. (In fact, according to more modern formulations of the theorem given below, *almost all* orbits that start in the tiny set will return infinitely often.)

The assumption that all orbits be bounded is crucial: otherwise the orbits could "go off to infinity", never to return.

The other important assumption is that the system is volume-preserving. This is a common feature of physical systems when there is no friction or air resistance, no energy is pumped in and no energy is ever lost to heat. Technically, all [Hamiltonian systems](wikipedia://en/1197531) are volume-preserving because of [Liouville's theorem](wikipedia://en/312301). (Systems that don't preserve phase space volume and lose energy over time could start in a tiny open set and then slowly come to a standstill somewhere else, never to return.)

As an example of the theorem, consider a billiard table (without pockets) and a set of billiard balls, moving on the table without friction and undergoing completely [elastic collisions](wikipedia://en/65907) with the cushions and the other balls. This ensures that no energy is ever lost and the flow preserves phase space volume. We can even allow for hills and valleys on the table. A point in phase space specifies the positions and velocities of all balls. If we impose a certain maximum total energy on the balls, then the system has bounded orbits and Poincaré's theorem applies. It states: when specifying the initial positions and velocities of all balls with arbitrary (but finite) precision, there will be orbits that start according to these specifications and that will return infinitely often to conditions that also accord with those specifications. Note however that not *all* orbits will necessarily return: it's possible to find orbits so finely tuned that some balls will slowly come to rest at the top of hills, never to return.

The Poincaré recurrence theorem has applications in [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481), [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087), and [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986). In statistical mechanics, it is used to predict the long-term behavior of [isolated systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isolated_system), such as gases confined to a finite volume, where the system is expected to return arbitrarily close to its initial state after sufficient length.

In the study of dynamical systems, the theorem applies to [conservative systems](wikipedia://en/8918323) and provides insight into recurrence properties of [measure-preserving transformations](wikipedia://en/398931). Related concepts also appear in ergodic theory, where recurrence plays a fundamental role in understanding the behavior of trajectories over time.[^o9]

## Proof idea and further discussion {#Proof_idea_and_further_discussion}

The qualitative proof hinges on two premises:[^o12]

1. A finite upper bound can be set on the total potentially accessible phase space volume. For a mechanical system, this bound can be provided by requiring that the system is contained in a bounded *physical* region of space (so that it cannot, for example, eject particles that never return)–combined with the conservation of energy, this locks the system into a finite region in [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101).
1. The phase volume of a finite element under dynamics is conserved.

Imagine any finite starting volume $D$ of the [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) and follow its path under the dynamics of the system. ("Finite" here means that the volume is positive.) The volume evolves through a "phase tube" in the phase space, keeping its size constant. Since the phase space is finite, after some number of steps $k_1$ the phase tube must intersect itself in some positive volume. Translating this intersection backwards in time, we find that at least a finite fraction of the starting volume is recurring.

Now, consider the size of the non-returning portion $D_1$ of the starting phase volume–that portion that never returns to the starting volume $D$. Using the principle in the last paragraph, we know that if $D_1$ is finite, then a finite part of it must return to $D_1$ after a number of steps, and therefore also returns to $D$. This contradicts the fact that all of $D_1$ is non-returning. We must conclude that the non-returning portion $D_1$has volume 0.

The theorem does not comment on certain aspects of recurrence that this proof cannot guarantee:

- There may be some special phase space points that never return to the starting phase volume, or that only return to the starting volume a finite number of times then never return again. These are extremely "rare", making up an infinitesimal part of any starting volume.
- What *can* be said is that for "almost any" starting phase, a system shall eventually return arbitrarily close to that starting phase. The recurrence time varies as the required degree of closeness (the smallness of the phase volume).
- Not all parts of the phase volume need to return at the same time. Some "miss" the starting volume on the first pass, only to make their return later.
- Nothing prevents the phase tube from returning completely to its starting volume before all the possible phase volume is exhausted. A trivial example of this is the [harmonic oscillator](wikipedia://en/13899). Systems that do cover all accessible phase volume are called [ergodic](wikipedia://en/258980). (This of course depends on the definition of "accessible volume".)
- For a given phase in a volume, the recurrence is not necessarily periodic. The second recurrence time does not need to be double the first recurrence time.
- The theorem does not guarantee the existence of a *periodic orbit,* i.e. of a phase space point that exactly returns to itself after a certain amount of time.

## Formal statement {#Formal_statement}

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Poincare recurrence theorem"}

**Poincare recurrence theorem.**

Let $(X, \mathcal{B}, \mu, T)$ be a [measure-preserving dynamical system](wikipedia://en/398931). This means:

$X$ is the state space.

$\mathcal{B}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra of measurable subsets of $X$.

$\mu$ is a **finite** measure on $X$ (meaning $0 < \mu(X) < \infty$). Without loss of generality, we usually assume $\mu(X) = 1$, making it a probability space.

$T: X \to X$ is a measure-preserving transformation, meaning $\mu(T^{-1}(A)) = \mu(A)$ for any $A \in \mathcal{B}$.

Let $A \in \mathcal{B}$ be a subset with positive measure ($\mu(A) > 0$). A point $x \in A$ is called **recurrent** with respect to $A$ if there exists some integer $n \ge 1$ such that $T^n(x) \in A$.

Poincaré's Recurrence Theorem states that **almost every point in $A$ returns to $A$**. More precisely, the set of points in $A$ that *never* return to $A$ has a measure of exactly zero. By extension, these points must return to $A$ infinitely many times.

:::

::: {#o17 .statement cue=named-heading name="The physical paradox"}

### The physical paradox {#The_physical_paradox}

In physics, this theorem creates a philosophical paradox (the Zermelo paradox) against the [second law of thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/133017). If you open a bottle of perfume in a sealed, finite room, thermodynamics says that the gas molecules will disperse and never spontaneously go back into the bottle.

However, because classical mechanics in a closed box preserves phase space volume (by [Liouville's theorem](wikipedia://en/312301)), Poincaré's theorem guarantees that if you wait long enough, the molecules *will* spontaneously reassemble back inside the bottle. The catch? The "Poincaré recurrence time" for macroscopic systems is staggeringly long—vastly longer than the current age of the universe.

:::

:::

## The Proof {#The_Proof}

::: {#o20 .proof cue=math-proof}

The beauty of the Poincaré Recurrence Theorem lies in its proof: it is elegant, surprisingly brief, and relies almost entirely on the pigeonhole principle applied to geometry.

\=== Step 1: Define the non-returning set === Let $A$ be a measurable set with $\mu(A) > 0$. Let $B$ be the subset of points in $A$ that **never** return to $A$ under positive iterations of $T$:

$$B = \{ x \in A : T^n(x) \notin A \text{ for all } n \ge 1 \}$$

Our goal is to show that $\mu(B) = 0$.

\=== Step 2: Consider forward images === Notice that by definition, if $x \in B$, then $T^n(x)$ cannot be in $A$ for any $n \ge 1$. Because $B$ is a subset of $A$, this implies that $T^n(x)$ can never be in $B$ either!

Therefore, the forward images of $B$ under the transformation $T$ can never intersect $B$:

$$B \cap T^{-n}(B) = \emptyset \quad \text{for all } n \ge 1$$

\=== Step 3: Show mutual disjointness === We can extend this to show that *all* forward images of $B$ are mutually disjoint. Suppose there were two integers $k > m \ge 0$ such that:

$$T^{-k}(B) \cap T^{-m}(B) \neq \emptyset$$

If we apply $T^m$ to both sides, we get:

$$T^{-(k-m)}(B) \cap B \neq \emptyset$$

But since $k - m > 0$, this directly contradicts our observation from Step 2 that $B$ never intersects its future self. Thus, the sets $B, T^{-1}(B), T^{-2}(B), T^{-3}(B), \dots$ must all be **mutually disjoint**.

\=== Step 4: Utilize the finite measure === Because $T$ is a measure-preserving transformation, all of these disjoint sets must have the exact same measure:

$$\mu(T^{-n}(B)) = \mu(B) \quad \text{for all } n \ge 0$$

Now, we sum the measures of this infinite sequence of disjoint sets. Because they are all contained within the total space $X$, their combined measure cannot exceed the total measure of $X$:

$$\mu\left( \bigcup_{n=0}^\infty T^{-n}(B) \right) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \mu(T^{-n}(B)) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \mu(B) \le \mu(X)$$

Since $\mu(X) < \infty$, we are summing an infinite number of identical constants $\mu(B)$, and the result is finite. The only way an infinite sum of a single constant can be finite is if that constant is exactly zero.

Therefore:

$$\mu(B) = 0$$

This proves that the set of points in $A$ that never return to $A$ has measure zero. $\blacksquare$

:::

## Extensions and generalizations {#Extensions_and_generalizations}

Over the years, Poincaré's theorem has been extended significantly to apply to more complex mathematical frameworks:

1. **Topological recurrence:** If $X$ is a compact metric space and $T: X \to X$ is a continuous map, a point $x$ is topologically recurrent if $\liminf_{n \to \infty} d(T^n(x), x) = 0$. While measure theory guarantees this *almost everywhere* for measure-preserving maps, topological dynamics looks for sets of points that are dense or form "residual sets" (Baire category theorem).
1. **Multiple recurrence (Furstenberg’s theorem):** Hillel Furstenberg generalized Poincaré recurrence in 1977 to show that for any system with $\mu(A) > 0$ and any integer $k \ge 1$, there exists an $n \ge 1$ such that: ::$\mu(A \cap T^{-n}A \cap T^{-2n}A \cap \dots \cap T^{-kn}A) > 0$ This groundbreaking extension was used to provide an entirely ergodic-theoretic proof of **Szemerédi's Theorem** (which states that any subset of integers with positive upper density contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions).
1. **Quantitative recurrence (Barreira, Saussol, etc.):** Instead of just asking *if* a point returns, quantitative recurrence asks *how fast* it returns. In many chaotic systems, if $B(x, r)$ is a ball of radius $r$ around $x$, the first return time $R(x, r)$ scales as $r^{-d}$, where $d$ is the local dimension of the measure.
1. **Infinite measure spaces:** If $\mu(X) = \infty$, the classic theorem fails (e.g., $T(x) = x+1$ on $\mathbb{R}$). Extensions to infinite measures require imposing conservative properties on the operator $T$ (leading to Hopf's decomposition into conservative and dissipative parts).

## Quantum mechanical version {#Quantum_mechanical_version}

For time-independent quantum mechanical systems with discrete energy eigenstates, a similar theorem holds. For every $\varepsilon >0$ and $T_0>0$ there exists a time *T* larger than $T_0$, such that $|| \psi(T) \rangle - |\psi(0)\rangle| < \varepsilon$, where $| \psi(t)\rangle$ denotes the state vector of the system at time *t*.[^o23][^o24][^o25]

The essential elements of the proof are as follows. The system evolves in time according to:

$$|\psi(t)\rangle = \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n \exp(-i E_n t)|\phi_n\rangle$$

where the $E_n$ are the energy eigenvalues (we use [natural units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_units), so $\hbar = 1$ ), and the $|\phi_n \rangle$ are the energy [eigenstates](wikipedia://en/30876419). The squared norm of the difference of the state vector at time *$T$* and time zero, can be written as:

$$| |\psi(T) \rangle - |\psi(0)\rangle|^2 = 2\sum_{n=0}^\infty |c_n|^2 [1-\cos(E_n T)]$$

We can truncate the summation at some *n* = *N* independent of *T*, because

$$\sum_{n=N+1}^\infty |c_n|^2 [1-\cos(E_n T)] \leq 2\sum_{n=N+1}^\infty |c_n|^2$$

which can be made arbitrarily small by increasing *N*, as the summation $\sum_{n=0}^\infty |c_n|^2$, being the squared norm of the initial state, converges to 1.

The finite sum

$$\sum_{n=0}^N |c_n|^2 [1-\cos(E_n T)]$$

can be made arbitrarily small for specific choices of the time *T*, according to the following construction. Choose an arbitrary $\delta>0$, and then choose *T* such that there are integers $k_n$ that satisfies

$$|E_n T -2\pi k_n|<\delta,$$

for all numbers $0 \leq n \leq N$. For this specific choice of *T*,

$$1-\cos(E_n T)<\frac{\delta^2}{2}.$$

As such, we have:

$$2\sum_{n=0}^N |c_n|^2 [1-\cos(E_n T)] < \delta^2 \sum_{n=0}^N |c_n|^2<\delta^2.$$

The state vector $|\psi(T)\rangle$ thus returns arbitrarily close to the initial state $|\psi(0)\rangle$.
