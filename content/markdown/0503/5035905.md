# Shadow zone

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Shadow zone"}

*Image: Earthquake wave shadow zone.svg.* Seismic shadow zone (from [USGS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USGS))

A seismic **shadow zone** is an area of the [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228)'s surface where [seismographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismograph) cannot detect direct [P waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P_waves) and/or [S waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S_waves) from an [earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthquake). This is due to liquid layers or structures within the Earth's surface. The most recognized shadow zone is due to the [core-mantle boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core-mantle_boundary) where [P waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P_waves) are refracted and [S waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S_waves) are stopped at the liquid outer core; however, any liquid boundary or body can create a shadow zone. For example, [magma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma) reservoirs with a high enough percent melt can create seismic shadow zones.

:::

## Background {#Background}

The earth is made up of different structures: the [crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crust_%28geology%29), the [mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantle_%28geology%29), the [inner core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_core) and the [outer core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_core). The crust, mantle, and inner core are typically solid; however, the outer core is entirely liquid.[^o3] A liquid outer core was first shown in 1906 by [Geologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologist) [Richard Oldham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Dixon_Oldham).[^o4] Oldham observed [seismograms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismogram) from various earthquakes and saw that some seismic stations did not record direct S waves, particularly ones that were 120° away from the hypocenter of the earthquake.[^o5]

In 1913, [Beno Gutenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beno_Gutenberg) noticed the abrupt change in seismic velocities of the P waves and disappearance of S waves at the core-mantle boundary. Gutenberg attributed this due to a solid mantle and liquid outer core, calling it the [Gutenberg discontinuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gutenberg_discontinuity).[^o6]

## Seismic wave properties {#Seismic_wave_properties}

The main observational constraint on identifying liquid layers and/or structures within the earth come from [seismology](wikipedia://en/28935). When an earthquake occurs, [seismic waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismic_waves) radiate out spherically from the earthquake's [hypocenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypocentre).[^o8] Two types of body waves travel through the Earth: primary seismic waves (P waves) and secondary seismic waves (S waves). P waves travel with motion in the same direction as the wave propagates and S waves travel with motion perpendicular to the wave propagation (transverse).[^o9]

The [P waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P_waves) are [refracted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refraction) by the liquid outer core of the Earth and are not detected between 104° and 140° (between approximately 11,570 and 15,570 km or 7,190 and 9,670 mi) from the hypocenter.[^o10][^o11] This is due to [Snell's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snell's_law), where a seismic wave encounters a boundary and either [refracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refract) or [reflects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_%28physics%29). In this case, the P waves refract due to [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) differences and greatly reduce in [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903).[^o10][^o12] This is considered the P wave shadow zone.[^o13]

The [S waves](wikipedia://en/1937145) cannot pass through the liquid outer core and are not detected more than 104° (approximately 11,570 km or 7,190 mi) from the [epicenter](wikipedia://en/173900).[^o10][^o14][^o15] This is considered the S wave shadow zone.[^o13] However, P waves that travel refract through the outer core and refract to another P wave (PKP wave) on leaving the outer core can be detected within the shadow zone. Additionally, S waves that refract to P waves on entering the outer core and then refract to an S wave on leaving the outer core can also be detected in the shadow zone ([SKS waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SKS_wave)).[^o10][^o16]

The reason for this is P wave and S wave velocities are governed by different properties in the material which they travel through and the different mathematical relationships they share in each case. The three properties are: [incompressibility](wikipedia://en/166084) ($k$), [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) ($p$) and [rigidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stiffness) ($u$).[^o14][^o17]

P wave velocity is equal to:

$$\sqrt{(k+\tfrac{4}{3}u)/p}$$

S wave velocity is equal to:

$$\sqrt{u/p}$$

S wave velocity is entirely dependent on the rigidity of the material it travels through. Liquids have zero rigidity, making the S wave velocity zero when traveling through a liquid. Overall, S waves are [shear waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_waves), and [shear stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_stress) is a type of [deformation](wikipedia://en/19194778) that cannot occur in a liquid.[^o14][^o15][^o17] Conversely, P waves are compressional waves and are only partially dependent on rigidity. P waves still maintain some velocity (can be greatly reduced) when traveling through a liquid.[^o10][^o11][^o17][^o18]

## Other observations and implications {#Other_observations_and_implications}

Although the core-mantle boundary casts the largest shadow zone, smaller structures, such as magma bodies, can also cast a shadow zone. For example, in 1981, Páll Einarsson conducted a seismic investigation on the [Krafla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krafla) Caldera in Northeast Iceland.[^o20] In this study, Einarsson placed a dense array of seismometers over the caldera and recorded earthquakes that occurred. The resulting seismograms showed both an absence of S waves and/or small S wave amplitudes. Einarsson attributed these results to be caused by a magma reservoir. In this case, the magma reservoir has enough percent melt to cause S waves to be directly affected.[^o20] In areas where there are no S waves being recorded, the S waves are encountering enough liquid, that no solid grains are touching.[^o21] In areas where there are highly [attenuated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anelastic_attenuation_factor) (small aptitude) S waves, there is still a percentage of melt, but enough solid grains are touching where S waves can travel through the part of the magma reservoir.[^o15][^o18][^o22]

Between 2014 and 2018, a geophysicist in Taiwan, Cheng-Horng Lin investigated the magma reservoir beneath the [Tatun Volcanic Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tatun_Volcanic_Group) in Taiwan.[^o23][^o24] Lin's research group used deep earthquakes and seismometers on or near the Tatun Volcanic Group to identify changes P and S [waveforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waveforms). Their results showed P wave delays and the absence of S waves in various locations. Lin attributed this finding to be due to a magma reservoir with at least 40% melt that casts an S wave shadow zone.[^o23][^o24] However, a recent study done by National Chung Cheng University used a dense array of seismometers and only saw S wave attenuation associated with the magma reservoir.[^o25] This research study investigated the cause of the S wave shadow zone Lin observed and attributed it to either a magma diapir above the subducting [Philippine Sea plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine_Sea_plate). Though it was not a magma reservoir, there was still a structure with enough melt/liquid to cause an S wave shadow zone.[^o25]

The existence of shadow zones, more specifically S wave shadow zones, could have implications on the eruptibility of volcanoes throughout the world. When volcanoes have enough percent melt to go below the rheological lockup (percent crystal fraction when a volcano is eruptive or not eruptive), this makes the volcanoes eruptible.[^o26][^o27] Determining the percent melt of a volcano could help with predictive modeling and assess current and future hazards. In an actively erupting volcano, [Mt. Etna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mt._Etna) in Italy, a study was done in 2021 that showed both an absence of S waves in some regions and highly attenuated S waves in others, depending on where the receivers are located above the magma chamber.[^o28] Previously, in 2014, a study was done to model the mechanism leading to December 28, 2014, eruption. This study showed that an eruption could be triggered between 30 and 70% melt.[^o29]
