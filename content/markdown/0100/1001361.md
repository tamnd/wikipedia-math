# Semisimple module

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Semisimple module"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially in the area of [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) known as [module theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Module_theory), a **semisimple module** or **completely reducible module** is a type of module that can be understood easily from its parts. A [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) that is a semisimple module over itself is known as an Artinian **semisimple ring**. Some important rings, such as [group rings](wikipedia://en/349223) of [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707) over [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) of [characteristic zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_zero), are semisimple rings. An [Artinian ring](wikipedia://en/489273) is initially understood via its largest semisimple quotient. The structure of Artinian semisimple rings is well understood by the [Artin–Wedderburn theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin–Wedderburn_theorem), which exhibits these rings as finite [direct products](wikipedia://en/8887) of [matrix rings](wikipedia://en/487541).

:::

For a group-theory analog of the same notion, see *[Semisimple representation](wikipedia://en/55823265)*.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A [module](wikipedia://en/276410) over a (not necessarily commutative) ring is said to be **semisimple** (or **completely reducible**) if it is the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) of [simple](wikipedia://en/29430) (irreducible) submodules.

For a module *M*, the following are equivalent:

1. *M* is semisimple; i.e., a direct sum of irreducible modules.
1. *M* is the sum of its irreducible submodules.
1. Every submodule of *M* is a [direct summand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_summand): for every submodule *N* of *M*, there is a complement *P* such that *M* = *N* ⊕ *P*.

For the proof of the equivalences, see *Semisimple representation#Equivalent characterizations*.

The most basic example of a semisimple module is a module over a field, i.e., a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370). On the other hand, the ring **Z** of integers is not a semisimple module over itself, since the submodule 2**Z** is not a direct summand.

Semisimple is stronger than [completely decomposable](wikipedia://en/810549), which is a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) of [indecomposable submodules](wikipedia://en/810549).

Let *A* be an algebra over a field *K*. Then a left module *M* over *A* is said to be **absolutely semisimple** if, for any field extension *F* of *K*, *F* ⊗<sub>*K*</sub> *M* is a semisimple module over *F* ⊗<sub>*K*</sub> *A*.

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- If *M* is semisimple and *N* is a [submodule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule), then *N* and *M* / *N* are also semisimple.
- The [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) of (possibly infinitely many) semisimple modules is semisimple.
- A module *M* is [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) and semisimple if and only if it is Artinian and its [radical](wikipedia://en/9379691) is zero.

:::

## Endomorphism rings {#Endomorphism_rings}

- A semisimple module *M* over a ring *R* can also be thought of as a [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) from *R* into the ring of [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) [endomorphisms](wikipedia://en/9569) of *M*. The image of this homomorphism is a [semiprimitive ring](wikipedia://en/485855), and every semiprimitive ring is isomorphic to such an image.
- The [endomorphism ring](wikipedia://en/59623) of a semisimple module is not only semiprimitive, but also [von Neumann regular](wikipedia://en/907524).[^n1]

## Semisimple rings {#Semisimple_rings}

A ring is said to be (left-)**semisimple** if it is semisimple as a left module over itself.[^n2] Surprisingly, a left-semisimple ring is also right-semisimple and vice versa. The left/right distinction is therefore unnecessary, and one can speak of semisimple rings without ambiguity.

A semisimple ring may be characterized in terms of [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410): namely, a ring *R* is semisimple if and only if any [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) of left (or right) *R*-modules [splits](wikipedia://en/1531404). That is, for a short exact sequence

$$0 \to A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C \to 0$$

there exists *s* : *C* → *B* such that the composition *g* ∘ *s* : *C* → *C* is the identity. The map *s* is known as a section. From this it follows that

$$B \cong A \oplus C$$

or in more exact terms

$$B \cong f(A) \oplus s(C).$$

In particular, any module over a semisimple ring is [injective](wikipedia://en/473775) and [projective](wikipedia://en/364488). Since "projective" implies "[flat](wikipedia://en/492616)", a semisimple ring is a [von Neumann regular ring](wikipedia://en/907524).

Semisimple rings are of particular interest to algebraists. For example, if the base ring *R* is semisimple, then all *R*-modules would automatically be semisimple. Furthermore, every simple (left) *R*-module is isomorphic to a minimal left ideal of *R*, that is, *R* is a left [Kasch ring](wikipedia://en/34212423).

Semisimple rings are both [Artinian](wikipedia://en/489273) and [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129). From the above properties, a ring is semisimple if and only if it is Artinian and its [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656) is zero.

If an Artinian semisimple ring contains a field as a [central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_a_ring) [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145), it is called a [semisimple algebra](wikipedia://en/3876151).

::: {#o11 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

- For a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346), the four following properties are equivalent: being a [semisimple ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_ring); being [Artinian](wikipedia://en/489273) and [reduced](wikipedia://en/539749);[^n3] being a [reduced](wikipedia://en/539749) [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129) of [Krull dimension](wikipedia://en/56079) 0; and being (isomorphic to) a finite direct product of fields.
- If *K* is a field and *G* is a finite group of order *n*, then the [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) *K*\[*G*\] is semisimple if and only if the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) of *K* does not divide *n*. This is [Maschke's theorem](wikipedia://en/30876007), an important result in [group representation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_representation_theory).
- By the [Wedderburn–Artin theorem](wikipedia://en/397264), a unital ring *R* is semisimple if and only if it is (isomorphic to) M<sub>*n*<sub>1</sub></sub>(*D*<sub>1</sub>) × M<sub>*n*<sub>2</sub></sub>(*D*<sub>2</sub>) × ... × M<sub>*n*<sub>*r*</sub></sub>(*D*<sub>*r*</sub>), where each *D*<sub>*i*</sub> is a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) and each *n*<sub>*i*</sub> is a positive integer, and M<sub>*n*</sub>(*D*) denotes the ring of *n*-by-*n* matrices with entries in *D*.
- An example of a semisimple non-unital ring is M<sub>∞</sub>(*K*), the row-finite, column-finite, infinite matrices over a field *K*.

:::

### Simple rings {#Simple_rings}

One should beware that despite the terminology, *not all simple rings are semisimple*. The problem is that the ring may be "too big", that is, not (left/right) Artinian. In fact, if *R* is a simple ring with a minimal left/right ideal, then *R* is semisimple.

Classic examples of simple, but not semisimple, rings are the [Weyl algebras](wikipedia://en/491095), such as the **Q**-algebra

$$A=\mathbf{Q}{\langle x,y \rangle }/\langle xy-yx-1\rangle\ ,$$

which is a simple noncommutative [domain](wikipedia://en/487627). These and many other nice examples are discussed in more detail in several noncommutative ring theory texts, including chapter 3 of Lam's text, in which they are described as nonartinian simple rings. The [module theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Module_theory) for the Weyl algebras is well studied and differs significantly from that of semisimple rings.

### Jacobson semisimple {#Jacobson_semisimple}

A ring is called *Jacobson semisimple* (or *J-semisimple* or *[semiprimitive](wikipedia://en/485855)*) if the intersection of the maximal left ideals is zero, that is, if the [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656) is zero. Every ring that is semisimple as a module over itself has zero Jacobson radical, but not every ring with zero Jacobson radical is semisimple as a module over itself. A J-semisimple ring is semisimple if and only if it is an [Artinian ring](wikipedia://en/489273), so semisimple rings are often called *Artinian semisimple rings* to avoid confusion.

For example, the ring of integers, **Z**, is J-semisimple, but not Artinian semisimple.
