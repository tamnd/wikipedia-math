# KW-26

*Image: kw-26.jpg.* An array of KW-26s

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="KW-26"}

The **TSEC/KW-26**, code named **ROMULUS**, was an [encryption system](wikipedia://en/18934432) used by the [U.S. Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Government) and, later, by [NATO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO) countries. It was developed in the 1950s by the [National Security Agency](wikipedia://en/21939) (NSA) to secure fixed [teleprinter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teleprinter) circuits that operated 24 hours a day. It used [vacuum tubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_tube) and [magnetic core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core_memory) logic, replacing older systems, like [SIGABA](wikipedia://en/145035) and the British [5-UCO](wikipedia://en/1198691), that used rotors and electromechanical [relays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relay).

:::

A KW-26 system (transmitter or receiver) contained over 800 cores and approximately 50 vacuum-tube driver circuits, occupying slightly more than one half of a standard [19-inch rack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/19-inch_rack). Most of the space in the rack and most of the 1 kW input power were required for the special-purpose vacuum tube circuits needed to provide compatibility with multiple input and output circuit configurations. The military services' requirements for numerous modes and speeds significantly increased costs and delayed delivery. NSA says it is doubtful that more than three or four of the possible configurations were ever used.

The KW-26 used an NSA-developed encryption algorithm based on [shift registers](wikipedia://en/58992). The algorithm produced a continuous stream of [bits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit) that were [xored](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xor) with the five bit [Baudot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baudot_code) teleprinter code to produce [ciphertext](wikipedia://en/175560) on the transmitting end and [plaintext](wikipedia://en/157935) on the receiving end. In NSA terminology, this stream of bits is called the [key](wikipedia://en/53039). The information needed to initialize the algorithm, what most [cryptographers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptographers) today would call the key, NSA calls a **cryptovariable**. Typically each KW-26 was given a new cryptovariable once a day.

*Image: kw-26-2.jpg.* KW-26 model C; the receiver is at the top, the transmitter at the bottom. Card reader is in upper right of each unit.

NSA designed a [common fill device](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_fill_device) (CFD), for loading the cryptovariable. It used a [Remington Rand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remington_Rand) (UNIVAC) format [punched card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punched_card) (45 columns, round holes). The operator inserted the daily key card into the CFD and closed the door securely, locking the card in place. Decks of cards were created by NSA and sent by courier. The cards were strictly accounted for.

Because the KW-26 used a [stream cipher](wikipedia://en/49007), if the same key card was ever used twice, the [encryption could be broken.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream_cipher_attack) To prevent re-use, the card was automatically cut in half upon reopening the CFD. As the units aged, the card reader contacts became less dependable, and operators resorted to various tricks, such as hitting the card reader cover with a screwdriver, to get them to work properly. Card readers were cleaned and the spring loading of the contacts checked as part of the routine maintenance of the device.

The KW-26 required a significant amount of technician time to maintain. Routine maintenance involved weekly air filter cleaning to less frequent recurring checks of tube equipped "driver packages" waveforms using an oscilloscope, voltage checks and adjustments, and oscillator frequency adjustments. Maintaining the equipment was only permitted after completion of a formal technical school of nine months as evidenced by a written certification. U.S. Air Force circa 1975 manning documents allocated one technician for each seven KW-26 transceivers.

Because the KW-26 sent a continuous stream of bits, it offered [traffic-flow security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic-flow_security). Someone intercepting the ciphertext stream had no way to judge how many real messages were being sent, making [traffic analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_analysis) impossible. One problem with the KW-26 was the need to keep the receiver and transmitter units synchronized. The crystal controlled clock in the KW-26 was capable of keeping both ends of the circuit in sync for many hours, even when signal contact was lost between the sending and receiving units. This capability made the KW-26 ideally suited for use on unreliable HF radio circuits. However, when the units did get out of sync, a new key card had to be inserted at each end. The benefit of [traffic-flow security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic-flow_security) was lost each time new cards were inserted. In practice, operational protocol led to the cards being replaced more often than was desirable to maintain maximum security of the circuit. This was especially so on radio circuits, where operators often changed the cards many times each day in response to a loss of radio connectivity. In any case, it was necessary to change the cards at least once per day to prevent the cypher pattern from repeating.

Early KW-26 units protected the **CRITICOMM** network, used to protect communications circuits used to coordinate [signals intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signals_intelligence) gathering. The initial production order for this application, awarded to [Burroughs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burroughs_Corporation) in 1957, was for 1500 units. Other services demanded KW-26's and some 14000 units were eventually built, beginning in the early 1960s, for the U.S. Navy, Army, Air Force, Defense Communications Agency, State Department and the [CIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIA). It was provided to U.S. allies as well.

When the [USS *Pueblo*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Pueblo_%28AGER-2%29) was captured by [North Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Korea) in 1968, KW-26's were on board. In response, the NSA had modifications made to other units in the field, presumably changing the crypto algorithm in some way, perhaps by changing the shift register feedback taps. Starting in the mid-1980s, the KW-26 system was decommissioned by NSA, being replaced by the more advanced solid-state data encryptor, TSEC/[KG-84](wikipedia://en/1637220).
