# Vertex figure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Vertex figure"}

*Image: Cube-vertex-figure-middle.svg.* "Half-edge" vertex figure of the cube

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **vertex figure**, broadly speaking, is the figure exposed when a corner of a general $n$-[polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) is sliced off.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

*Image: Cube-vertex-figure-large.svg.* "Whole-edge" vertex figure of the cube

*Image: Cube-vertex-figure-sphere.svg.* Spherical vertex figure of the cube

*Image: Cube-vertex-figure-points.svg.* Point-set vertex figure of the cube

Take some corner or [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) of a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470). Mark a point somewhere along each connected edge. Draw lines across the connected faces, joining adjacent points around the face. When done, these lines form a complete circuit, i.e. a polygon, around the vertex. This polygon is the vertex figure.

More precise formal definitions can vary quite widely, according to circumstance. For example [Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter) (e.g. 1948, 1954) varies his definition as convenient for the current area of discussion. Most of the following definitions of a vertex figure apply equally well to infinite [tilings](wikipedia://en/321671) or, by extension, to [space-filling tessellation](wikipedia://en/6008654) with [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) [cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_%28geometry%29) and other higher-dimensional [polytopes](wikipedia://en/23471).

### As a flat slice {#As_a_flat_slice}

Make a slice through the corner of the polyhedron, cutting through all the edges connected to the vertex. The cut surface is the vertex figure (a [plane figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_figure)). This is perhaps the most common approach, and the most easily understood. Different authors make the slice in different places. Wenninger (2003) cuts each edge a unit distance from the vertex, as does Coxeter (1948). For uniform polyhedra the [Dorman Luke construction](wikipedia://en/51069784#Dorman_Luke_construction) cuts each connected edge at its midpoint. Other authors make the cut through the vertex at the other end of each edge.[^o5][^o6]

For an irregular polyhedron, cutting all edges incident to a given vertex at equal distances from the vertex may produce a figure that does not lie in a plane. A more general approach, valid for arbitrary convex polyhedra, is to make the cut along any plane which separates the given vertex from all the other vertices, but is otherwise arbitrary. This construction determines the combinatorial structure of the vertex figure, similar to a set of connected vertices (see below), but not its precise geometry; it may be generalized to [convex polytopes](wikipedia://en/1817885) in any dimension. However, for non-convex polyhedra, there may not exist a plane near the vertex that cuts all of the faces incident to the vertex.

### As a spherical polygon {#As_a_spherical_polygon}

Cromwell (1999) forms the vertex figure by intersecting the polyhedron with a sphere centered at the vertex, small enough that it intersects only edges and faces incident to the vertex. This can be visualized as making a spherical cut or scoop, centered on the vertex. The cut surface or vertex figure is thus a spherical polygon marked on this sphere. One advantage of this method is that the shape of the vertex figure is fixed (up to the scale of the sphere), whereas the method of intersecting with a plane can produce different shapes depending on the angle of the plane. Additionally, this method works for non-convex polyhedra.

### As the set of connected vertices {#As_the_set_of_connected_vertices}

Many combinatorial and computational approaches (e.g. Skilling, 1975) treat a vertex figure as the ordered (or partially ordered) set of points of all the neighboring (connected via an edge) vertices to the given vertex.

### Abstract definition {#Abstract_definition}

In the theory of [abstract polytopes](wikipedia://en/1211056), the vertex figure at a given vertex *V* comprises all the elements which are incident on the vertex; edges, faces, etc. More formally it is the (*n*−1)-section *F<sub>n</sub>*/*V*, where *F<sub>n</sub>* is the greatest face.

This set of elements is elsewhere known as a *vertex star*. The geometrical vertex figure and the vertex star may be understood as distinct *realizations* of the same abstract section.

:::

## General properties {#General_properties}

A vertex figure of an *n*-polytope is an (*n*−1)-polytope. For example, a vertex figure of a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) is a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621), and the vertex figure for a [4-polytope](wikipedia://en/24979) is a polyhedron.

In general a vertex figure need not be planar.

For nonconvex polyhedra, the vertex figure may also be nonconvex. Uniform polytopes, for instance, can have [star polygons](wikipedia://en/313303) for faces and/or for vertex figures.

### Isogonal figures {#Isogonal_figures}

Vertex figures are especially significant for [uniforms](wikipedia://en/6842392) and other [isogonal](wikipedia://en/581560) (vertex-transitive) polytopes because one vertex figure can define the entire polytope.

For polyhedra with regular faces, a vertex figure can be represented in [vertex configuration](wikipedia://en/3603745) notation, by listing the faces in sequence around the vertex. For example 3.4.4.4 is a vertex with one triangle and three squares, and it defines the uniform [rhombicuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/26213).

If the polytope is isogonal, the vertex figure will exist in a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) surface of the *n*-space.

## Constructions {#Constructions}

### From the adjacent vertices {#From_the_adjacent_vertices}

By considering the connectivity of these neighboring vertices, a vertex figure can be constructed for each vertex of a polytope:

- Each [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) of the *vertex figure* coincides with a vertex of the original polytope.
- Each [edge](wikipedia://en/12401) of the *vertex figure* exists on or inside of a face of the original polytope connecting two alternate vertices from an original face.
- Each [face](wikipedia://en/149926) of the *vertex figure* exists on or inside a cell of the original *n*-polytope (for *n* > 3).
- ... and so on to higher order elements in higher order polytopes.

### Dorman Luke construction {#Dorman_Luke_construction}

For a uniform polyhedron, the face of the [dual polyhedron](wikipedia://en/8815) may be found from the original polyhedron's vertex figure using the "[Dorman Luke](wikipedia://en/8815#Dorman_Luke_construction)" construction.

### Regular polytopes {#Regular_polytopes}

*Image: Great icosahedron vertfig.svg.* The vertex figure of the [great icosahedron](wikipedia://en/3029898) is a regular [pentagram](wikipedia://en/197544) or [star polygon](wikipedia://en/313303) {5/2}.

If a polytope is regular, it can be represented by a [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) and both the [cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_%28geometry%29) and the vertex figure can be trivially extracted from this notation.

In general a regular polytope with Schläfli symbol {*a*,*b*,*c*,...,*y*,*z*} has cells as {*a*,*b*,*c*,...,*y*}, and *vertex figures* as {*b*,*c*,...,*y*,*z*}.

1. For a [regular polyhedron](wikipedia://en/333299) {*p*,*q*}, the vertex figure is {*q*}, a *q*-gon.
   - Example, the vertex figure for a cube {4,3}, is the triangle {3}.
1. For a [regular 4-polytope](wikipedia://en/18801624) or [space-filling tessellation](wikipedia://en/6008654) {*p*,*q*,*r*}, the vertex figure is {*q*,*r*}.
   - Example, the vertex figure for a hypercube {4,3,3}, the vertex figure is a regular tetrahedron {3,3}.
   - Also the vertex figure for a [cubic honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3733318) {4,3,4}, the vertex figure is a regular octahedron {3,4}.

Since the dual polytope of a regular polytope is also regular and represented by the Schläfli symbol indices reversed, it is easy to see the dual of the vertex figure is the cell of the dual polytope. For regular polyhedra, this is a special case of the [Dorman Luke construction](wikipedia://en/8815).

:::

## Edge figure {#Edge_figure}

*Image: Truncated cubic honeycomb1.jpg.* The *truncated cubic honeycomb* has two edge types, one with four *truncated cubes*, and the others with one octahedron, and two truncated cubes. These can be seen as two types of *edge figures*. These are seen as the vertices of the *vertex figure*.

Related to the *vertex figure*, an *edge figure* is the *vertex figure* of a *vertex figure*.[^o24] Edge figures are useful for expressing relations between the elements within regular and uniform polytopes.

An *edge figure* will be a (*n*−2)-polytope, representing the arrangement of [facets](wikipedia://en/7186647) around a given edge. Regular and single-ringed [coxeter diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram) uniform polytopes will have a single edge type. In general, a uniform polytope can have as many edge types as active mirrors in the construction, since each active mirror produces one edge in the fundamental domain.

Regular polytopes (and honeycombs) have a single *edge figure* which is also regular. For a regular polytope {*p*,*q*,*r*,*s*,...,*z*}, the *edge figure* is {*r*,*s*,...,*z*}.

In four dimensions, the edge figure of a [4-polytope](wikipedia://en/24979) or [3-honeycomb](wikipedia://en/6008654) is a polygon representing the arrangement of a set of facets around an edge. For example, the *edge figure* for a regular [cubic honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3733318) {4,3,4} is a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29), and for a regular 4-polytope {*p*,*q*,*r*} is the polygon {*r*}.

Less trivially, the [truncated cubic honeycomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truncated_cubic_honeycomb) t<sub>0,1</sub>{4,3,4}, has a [square pyramid](wikipedia://en/1128455) vertex figure, with [truncated cube](wikipedia://en/280529) and [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635) cells. Here there are two types of *edge figures*. One is a square edge figure at the apex of the pyramid. This represents the four *truncated cubes* around an edge. The other four edge figures are isosceles triangles on the base vertices of the pyramid. These represent the arrangement of two truncated cubes and one octahedron around the other edges.
