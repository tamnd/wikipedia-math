# Apex graph

*Image: Apex graph.svg.* An apex graph. The [subgraph](wikipedia://en/325802#Subgraphs) formed by removing the red vertex is [planar](wikipedia://en/24314).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Apex graph"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a branch of mathematics, an **apex graph** is a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) that can be made [planar](wikipedia://en/24314) by the removal of a single [vertex](wikipedia://en/638899). The deleted vertex is called an apex of the graph. It is *an* apex, not *the* apex because an apex graph may have more than one apex; for example, in the minimal nonplanar graphs $K_{5}$ or $K_{3,3}$, every vertex is an apex. The apex graphs include graphs that are themselves planar, in which case again every vertex is an apex. The [null graph](wikipedia://en/1520758) is also counted as an apex graph even though it has no vertex to remove.

:::

Apex graphs are [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under the operation of taking [minors](wikipedia://en/353042) and play a role in several other aspects of graph minor theory: [linkless embedding](wikipedia://en/772031),[^o24] [Hadwiger's conjecture](wikipedia://en/1657860),[^o2] YΔY-reducible graphs,[^o26] and relations between [treewidth](wikipedia://en/3987086) and [graph diameter](wikipedia://en/2979341).[^o15]

## Characterization and recognition {#Characterization_and_recognition}

Apex graphs are [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under the operation of taking [minors](wikipedia://en/353042): contracting any edge, or removing any edge or vertex, leads to another apex graph. For, if $G$ is an apex graph with apex $v$, then any contraction or removal that does not involve $v$ preserves the planarity of the remaining graph, as does any edge removal of an edge incident to $v$. If an edge incident to $v$ is contracted, the effect on the remaining graph is equivalent to the removal of the other endpoint of the edge. And if $v$ itself is removed, any other vertex may be chosen as the apex.[^o4]

By the [Robertson–Seymour theorem](wikipedia://en/351769), because they form a minor-closed family of graphs, the apex graphs have a [forbidden graph characterization](wikipedia://en/13547826). There are only finitely many graphs that are neither apex graphs nor have another non-apex graph as a minor. These graphs are *forbidden minors* for the property of being an apex graph. Any other graph $G$ is an apex graph [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) none of the forbidden minors is a minor of $G$. These forbidden minors include the seven graphs of the [Petersen family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petersen_family), three disconnected graphs formed from the disjoint unions of two of $K_{5}$ and $K_{3,3}$, and many other graphs. However, a complete description of them remains unknown.[^o4][^n1]

Despite the complete set of forbidden minors remaining unknown, it is possible to test whether a given graph is an apex graph, and if so, to find an apex for the graph, in [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time). More generally, for any fixed constant $k$, it is possible to recognize in linear time the **$k$-apex graphs**, the graphs in which the removal of some carefully chosen set of at most $k$ vertices leads to a planar graph.[^o6] If $k$ is variable, however, the problem is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete).[^o7]

## Chromatic number {#Chromatic_number}

Every apex graph has [chromatic number](wikipedia://en/426743) at most five: the underlying planar graph requires at most four colors by the [four color theorem](wikipedia://en/10949), and the remaining vertex needs at most one additional color. (Robertson Seymour Thomas 1993a) used this fact in their proof of the case $k = 6$ of the [Hadwiger conjecture](wikipedia://en/1657860), the statement that every 6-chromatic graph has the [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_{6}$ as a minor: they showed that any minimal [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) to the conjecture would have to be an apex graph, but since there are no 6-chromatic apex graphs such a counterexample cannot exist.

::: {#o10 .problem cue=unsolved}

Is every 6-vertex-connected $K_{6}$-minor-free graph an apex graph?

:::

(Jørgensen 1994) conjectured that every [6-vertex-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph) graph that does not have $K_{6}$ as a minor must be an apex graph. If this were proved, the Robertson–Seymour–Thomas result on the Hadwiger conjecture would be an immediate consequence.[^o2] Jørgensen's conjecture remains unproven.[^o12] However, if false, it has only finitely many counterexamples.[^n2]

## Local treewidth {#Local_treewidth}

A graph family $F$ has *bounded local treewidth* if the graphs in $F$ obey a functional relationship between [diameter](wikipedia://en/1020021) and [treewidth](wikipedia://en/3987086): there exists a function $f$ such that the treewidth of a diameter-$d$ graph in $F$ is at most $f (d)$. The apex graphs do not have bounded local treewidth: the apex graphs formed by connecting an apex vertex to every vertex of an $n \times n$ [grid graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grid_graph) have treewidth $n$ and diameter 2, so the treewidth is not bounded by a function of diameter for these graphs. However, apex graphs are intimately connected to bounded local treewidth: the minor-closed graph families $F$ that have bounded local treewidth are exactly the families that have an apex graph as one of their forbidden minors.[^o15] A minor-closed family of graphs that has an apex graph as one of its forbidden minors is known as *apex-minor-free*. With this terminology, the connection between apex graphs and local treewidth can be restated as the fact that apex-minor-free graph families are the same as minor-closed graph families with bounded local treewidth.

The concept of bounded local treewidth forms the basis of the theory of [bidimensionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bidimensionality), and allows for many algorithmic problems on apex-minor-free graphs to be solved exactly by a polynomial-time algorithm or a [fixed-parameter tractable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed-parameter_tractability) algorithm, or approximated using a [polynomial-time approximation scheme](wikipedia://en/666431).[^o16] Apex-minor-free graph families obey a strengthened version of the [graph structure theorem](wikipedia://en/21318521), leading to additional approximation algorithms for [graph coloring](wikipedia://en/426743) and the [travelling salesman problem](wikipedia://en/31248).[^o17] However, some of these results can also be extended to arbitrary minor-closed graph families via structure theorems relating them to apex-minor-free graphs.[^o18]

## Embeddings {#Embeddings}

If $G$ is an apex graph with apex $v$, and $\tau$ is the minimum number of faces needed to cover all the neighbors of $v$ in a planar embedding of $G \backslash \{v\},$ then $G$ may be embedded onto a two-dimensional surface of [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) $\tau - 1$: simply add that number of bridges to the planar embedding, connecting together all the faces into which $v$ must be connected. For instance, adding a single vertex to an [outerplanar graph](wikipedia://en/352343) (a graph with $\tau = 1$) produces a planar graph. When $G \backslash \{v\}$ is 3-connected, this bound is within a constant factor of optimal: every surface embedding of $G$ requires genus at least $\tfrac{\tau}{160}$. However, it is [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard) to determine the optimal genus of a surface embedding of an apex graph.[^o20]

By using [SPQR trees](wikipedia://en/11220797) to encode the possible embeddings of the planar part of an apex graph, it is possible to compute a [drawing](wikipedia://en/325813) of the graph in the plane in which the only crossings involve the apex vertex, minimizing the total number of crossings, in polynomial time.[^o21] However, if arbitrary crossings are allowed, it becomes NP-hard to minimize the number of crossings, even in the special case of apex graphs formed by adding a single edge to a planar graph.[^o22]

Apex graphs are also [linklessly embeddable](wikipedia://en/772031) in [three-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/3054853): they can be embedded in such a way that each cycle in the graph is the boundary of a disk that is not crossed by any other feature of the graph.[^o23] A drawing of this type may be obtained by drawing the planar part of the graph in a plane, placing the apex above the plane, and connecting the apex by straight-line edges to each of its neighbors. Linklessly embeddable graphs form a minor-closed family with the seven graphs in the [Petersen family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petersen_family) as their minimal forbidden minors;[^o24] therefore, these graphs are also forbidden as minors for the apex graphs. However, there exist linklessly embeddable graphs that are not apex graphs.

## YΔY-reducibility {#YΔY-reducibility}

*Image: Apex rhombic dodecahedron.svg.* Robertson's example of a non-YΔY-reducible apex graph.

A connected graph is YΔY-reducible if it can be reduced to a single vertex by a sequence of steps, each of which is a [Δ-Y or Y-Δ transform](wikipedia://en/268145), the removal of a self-loop or multiple adjacency, the removal of a vertex with one neighbor, and the replacement of a vertex of degree two and its two neighboring edges by a single edge.[^o26]

Like the apex graphs and the linkless embeddable graphs, the YΔY-reducible graphs are closed under graph minors. And, like the linkless embeddable graphs, the YΔY-reducible graphs have the seven graphs in the [Petersen family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petersen_family) as forbidden minors, prompting the question of whether these are the only forbidden minors and whether the YΔY-reducible graphs are the same as the linkless embeddable graphs. However, Neil Robertson provided an example of an apex graph that is not YΔY-reducible. Since every apex graph is linkless embeddable, this shows that there are graphs that are linkless embeddable but not YΔY-reducible and therefore that there are additional forbidden minors for the YΔY-reducible graphs.[^o26]

Robertson's apex graph is shown in the figure. It can be obtained by connecting an apex vertex to each of the degree-three vertices of a [rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631), or by merging two diametrally opposed vertices of a four-dimensional [hypercube graph](wikipedia://en/6706815). Because the rhombic dodecahedron's graph is planar, Robertson's graph is an apex graph. It is a [triangle-free graph](wikipedia://en/7667996) with minimum [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) four, so it cannot be changed by any YΔY-reduction.[^o26]

## Nearly planar graphs {#Nearly_planar_graphs}

*Image: Moebius-ladder-16.svg.* The 16-vertex [Möbius ladder](wikipedia://en/7355278), an example of a nearly planar graph.

If a graph is an apex graph, it is not necessarily the case that it has a unique apex. For instance, in the minor-minimal nonplanar graphs *K*<sub>5</sub> and *K*<sub>3,3</sub>, any of the vertices can be chosen as the apex. (txt) defined a **nearly planar graph** to be a nonplanar apex graph with the property that all vertices can be the apex of the graph; thus, *K*<sub>5</sub> and *K*<sub>3,3</sub> are nearly planar. He provided a classification of these graphs into four subsets, one of which consists of the graphs that (like the [Möbius ladders](wikipedia://en/7355278)) can be embedded onto the [Möbius strip](wikipedia://en/37817) in such a way that the single edge of the strip coincides with a [Hamiltonian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle) of the graph. Prior to the proof of the [four color theorem](wikipedia://en/10949), he proved that every nearly planar graph can be colored with at most four colors, except for the graphs formed from a [wheel graph](wikipedia://en/1655876) with an odd outer cycle by replacing the hub vertex with two adjacent vertices, which require five colors. Additionally, he proved that, with a single exception (the eight-vertex [complement graph](wikipedia://en/1175649) of the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285)) every nearly planar graph has an embedding onto the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350).

However, the phrase "nearly planar graph" is highly ambiguous: it has also been used to refer to apex graphs,[^o29] graphs formed by adding one edge to a planar graph,[^o30] and graphs formed from a planar embedded graph by replacing a bounded number of faces by "vortexes" of bounded [pathwidth](wikipedia://en/5133142),[^o31] as well as for other less precisely defined sets of graphs.

## Related graph classes {#Related_graph_classes}

An abstract graph is said to be *n*-apex if it can be made planar by deleting *n* or fewer vertices. A 1-apex graph is also said to be apex.

According to (Lipton Mackall Mattman Pierce 2018), a graph is **edge-apex** if there is some edge in the graph that can be deleted to make the graph planar. A graph is **contraction-apex** if there is some edge in the graph that can be contracted to make the graph planar.

In general, if **X** is a class of graphs, an "apex-**X**" graph is a graph that can be brought into the class **X** by deleting some one vertex. For example, an apex-[cograph](wikipedia://en/1175666) is a graph *G* that has a vertex *v* such that *G―v* is a cograph.
