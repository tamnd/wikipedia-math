# Morphism of algebraic varieties

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Morphism of algebraic varieties"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a morphism between [algebraic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_varieties) is a function between the varieties that is given locally by [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000). It is also called a **regular map**. A morphism from an algebraic variety to the [affine line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_line) is also called a **regular function**. A regular map whose inverse is also regular is called **biregular**, and the biregular maps are the [isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/14828) of algebraic varieties. Because regular and biregular are very restrictive conditions – there are no non-constant regular functions on [projective varieties](wikipedia://en/320469) – the concepts of [rational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_map) and [birational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birational) maps are widely used as well; they are [partial functions](wikipedia://en/23577) that are defined locally by [rational fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_fraction) instead of polynomials.

:::

An algebraic variety has naturally the structure of a [locally ringed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_ringed_space); a morphism between algebraic varieties is precisely a morphism of the underlying locally ringed spaces.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

If *X* and *Y* are closed subvarieties of $\mathbb{A}^n$ and $\mathbb{A}^m$ (so they are [affine varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_varieties)), then a regular map $f\colon X\to Y$ is the restriction of a [polynomial map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_map) $\mathbb{A}^n\to \mathbb{A}^m$. Explicitly, it has the form:[^n1]

$$f = (f_1, \dots, f_m)$$

where the $f_i$s are in the [coordinate ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_ring) of *X*:

$$k[X] = k[x_1, \dots, x_n]/I,$$

where *I* is the [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) defining *X* (note: two polynomials *f* and *g* define the same function on *X* if and only if *f* − *g* is in *I*). The image *f*(*X*) lies in *Y*, and hence satisfies the defining equations of *Y*. That is, a regular map $f: X \to Y$ is the same as the restriction of a polynomial map whose components satisfy the defining equations of $Y$.

More generally, a map *f* : *X*→*Y* between two [varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_variety) is **regular at a point** *x* if there is a neighbourhood *U* of *x* and a neighbourhood *V* of *f*(*x*) such that *f*(*U*) ⊂ *V* and the restricted function *f* : *U*→*V* is regular as a function on some affine charts of *U* and *V*. Then *f* is called **regular**, if it is regular at all points of *X*.

- **Note:** It is not immediately obvious that the two definitions coincide: if *X* and *Y* are affine varieties, then a map *f* : *X*→*Y* is regular in the first sense if and only if it is so in the second sense. Also, it is not immediately clear whether regularity depends on a choice of affine charts (it does not.) This kind of a consistency issue, however, disappears if one adopts the formal definition. Formally, an (abstract) algebraic variety is defined to be a particular kind of a locally [ringed space](wikipedia://en/248014). When this definition is used, a morphism of varieties is just a morphism of locally ringed spaces.

The composition of regular maps is again regular; thus, algebraic varieties form the [category of algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/1997#Morphism_of_affine_varieties) where the morphisms are the regular maps.

Regular maps between affine varieties correspond contravariantly in one-to-one to [algebra homomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_homomorphism) between the coordinate rings: if *f* : *X*→*Y* is a morphism of affine varieties, then it defines the algebra homomorphism

$$f^{\#}: k[Y] \to k[X], \, g \mapsto g \circ f$$

where $k[X], k[Y]$ are the coordinate rings of *X* and *Y*; it is well-defined since $g \circ f = g(f_1, \dots, f_m)$ is a polynomial in elements of $k[X]$. Conversely, if $\phi: k[Y] \to k[X]$ is an algebra homomorphism, then it induces the morphism

$$\phi^a: X \to Y$$

given by: writing $k[Y] = k[y_1, \dots, y_m]/J,$

$$\phi^a = (\phi(\overline{y_1}), \dots, \phi(\overline{y_m}))$$

where $\overline{y}_i$ are the images of $y_i$'s. Note ${\phi^a}^{\#} = \phi$ as well as ${f^{\#}}^a = f.$ In particular, *f* is an isomorphism of affine varieties if and only if *f*<sup>\#</sup> is an isomorphism of the coordinate rings.

For example, if *X* is a closed subvariety of an affine variety *Y* and *f* is the inclusion, then *f*<sup>\#</sup> is the restriction of regular functions on *Y* to *X*. See #Examples below for more examples.

:::

## Regular functions {#Regular_functions}

In the particular case that $Y$ equals $\mathbb{A}^1$ the regular maps $f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{A}^1$ are called **regular functions**, and are algebraic analogs of [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) studied in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625). The [ring of regular functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_regular_functions) (that is the [coordinate ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_ring) or more abstractly the ring of global sections of the structure sheaf) is a fundamental object in affine algebraic geometry. The only regular function on a [projective variety](wikipedia://en/320469) is constant (this can be viewed as an algebraic analogue of [Liouville's theorem](wikipedia://en/312293) in [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759)).

A scalar function $f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{A}^1$ is regular at a point $x$ if, in some open affine neighborhood of $x$, it is a [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210) that is regular at $x$; i.e., there are regular functions $g$, $h$ near $x$ such that $f=g/h$ and $h$ does not vanish at $x$. Caution: the condition is for some pair (*g*, *h*) not for all pairs (*g*, *h*); see Examples.

If *X* is a [quasi-projective variety](wikipedia://en/1321785); i.e., an open subvariety of a projective variety, then the function field *k*(*X*) is the same as that of the closure $\overline{X}$ of *X* and thus a rational function on *X* is of the form *g*/*h* for some homogeneous elements *g*, *h* of the same degree in the [homogeneous coordinate ring](wikipedia://en/23833909) $k[\overline{X}]$ of $\overline{X}$ (cf. [Projective variety#Variety structure](wikipedia://en/320469#Variety_structure)). Then a rational function *f* on *X* is regular at a point *x* if and only if there are some homogeneous elements *g*, *h* of the same degree in $k[\overline{X}]$ such that *f* = *g*/*h* and *h* does not vanish at *x*. This characterization is sometimes taken as the definition of a regular function.[^n2]

:::

## Comparison with a morphism of schemes {#Comparison_with_a_morphism_of_schemes}

If $X=\operatorname{Spec}A$ and $Y=\operatorname{Spec}B$ are [affine schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_scheme), then each ring homomorphism $\phi:B\rightarrow A$ determines a morphism

$$\phi^a: X \to Y, \, \mathfrak{p} \mapsto \phi^{-1}(\mathfrak{p})$$

by taking the [pre-images](wikipedia://en/579311) of [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928). All morphisms between affine schemes are of this type and gluing such morphisms gives a [morphism of schemes](wikipedia://en/7482797) in general.

Now, if *X*, *Y* are affine varieties; i.e., *A*, *B* are [integral domains](wikipedia://en/15462) that are finitely generated algebras over an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) *k*, then, working with only the closed points, the above coincides with the definition given at #Definition. (Proof: If *f* : *X* → *Y* is a morphism, then writing $\phi = f^{\#}$, we need to show

$$\mathfrak{m}_{f(x)} = \phi^{-1}(\mathfrak{m}_x)$$

where $\mathfrak{m}_x, \mathfrak{m}_{f(x)}$ are the [maximal ideals](wikipedia://en/48164) corresponding to the points *x* and *f*(*x*); i.e., $\mathfrak{m}_x = \{ g \in k[X] \mid g(x) = 0 \}$. This is immediate.)

This fact means that the category of affine varieties can be identified with a [full subcategory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_subcategory) of affine schemes over *k*. Since morphisms of varieties are obtained by gluing morphisms of affine varieties in the same way morphisms of schemes are obtained by gluing morphisms of affine schemes, it follows that the category of varieties is a full subcategory of the category of schemes over *k*.

For more details, see <https://math.stackexchange.com/q/101038>.

## Examples {#Examples}

- The regular functions on $\mathbb{A}^n$ are exactly the polynomials in $n$ variables and the regular functions on $\mathbb{P}^n$ are exactly the constants.
- Let $X$ be the affine curve $y = x^2$. Then $$f: X \to \mathbf{A}^1, \, (x, y) \mapsto x$$ is a morphism; it is bijective with the inverse $g(x) = (x, x^2)$. Since $g$ is also a morphism, $f$ is an isomorphism of varieties.
- Let $X$ be the affine curve $y^2 = x^3 + x^2$. Then $$f: \mathbf{A}^1 \to X, \, t \mapsto (t^2 - 1, t^3 - t)$$ is a morphism. It corresponds to the ring homomorphism $$f^{\#}: k[X] \to k[t], \, g \mapsto g(t^2 - 1, t^3 - t),$$ which is seen to be injective (since *f* is surjective).
- Continuing the preceding example, let *U* = **A**<sup>1</sup> − {1}. Since *U* is the complement of the hyperplane *t* = 1, *U* is affine. The restriction $f: U \to X$ is bijective. But the corresponding ring homomorphism is the inclusion $k[X] = k[t^2 - 1, t^3 - t] \hookrightarrow k[t, (t - 1)^{-1}]$, which is not an isomorphism and so the restriction *f* |<sub>*U*</sub> is not an isomorphism.
- Let *X* be the affine curve *x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> = 1 and let $$f(x, y) = {1 - y \over x}.$$ Then *f* is a rational function on *X*. It is regular at (0, 1) despite the expression since, as a rational function on *X*, *f* can also be written as $f(x, y) = {x \over 1 + y}$.
- Let *X* = **A**<sup>2</sup> − (0, 0). Then *X* is an algebraic variety since it is an open subset of a variety. If *f* is a regular function on *X*, then *f* is regular on $D_{\mathbf{A}^2}(x) = \mathbf{A}^2 - \{ x = 0 \}$ and so is in $k[D_{\mathbf{A}^2}(x)] = k[\mathbf{A}^2][x^{-1}] = k[x, x^{-1}, y]$. Similarly, it is in $k[x, y, y^{-1}]$. Thus, we can write: $$f = {g \over x^n} = {h \over y^m}$$ where *g*, *h* are polynomials in *k*\[*x*, *y*\]. But this implies *g* is divisible by *x*<sup>*n*</sup> and so *f* is in fact a polynomial. Hence, the ring of regular functions on *X* is just *k*\[*x*, *y*\]. (This also shows that *X* cannot be affine since if it were, *X* is determined by its coordinate ring and thus *X* = **A**<sup>2</sup>.)
- Suppose $\mathbf{P}^1 = \mathbf{A}^1 \cup \{ \infty \}$ by identifying the points (*x* : 1) with the points *x* on **A**<sup>1</sup> and ∞ = (1 : 0). There is an automorphism σ of **P**<sup>1</sup> given by σ(x : y) = (y : x); in particular, σ exchanges 0 and ∞. If *f* is a rational function on **P**<sup>1</sup>, then $$\sigma^{\#}(f) = f(1/z)$$ and *f* is regular at ∞ if and only if *f*(1/*z*) is regular at zero.
- Taking the [function field](wikipedia://en/2783843) *k*(*V*) of an [irreducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreducible_variety) [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) *V*, the functions *F* in the function field may all be realised as morphisms from *V* to the [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578) over *k*. (cf. #Properties) The image will either be a single point, or the whole projective line (this is a consequence of the [completeness of projective varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completeness_of_projective_varieties)). That is, unless *F* is actually constant, we have to attribute to *F* the value ∞ at some points of *V*.
- For any algebraic varieties *X*, *Y*, the projection $$p: X \times Y \to X, \, (x, y) \mapsto x$$ is a morphism of varieties. If *X* and *Y* are affine, then the corresponding ring homomorphism is $$p^{\#}: k[X] \to k[X \times Y] = k[X] \otimes_k k[Y], \, f \mapsto f \otimes 1$$ where $(f \otimes 1)(x, y) = f(p(x, y)) = f(x)$.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

A morphism between varieties is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_map) with respect to Zariski topologies on the source and the target.

The image of a morphism of varieties need not be open nor closed (for example, the image of $\mathbf{A}^2 \to \mathbf{A}^2, \, (x, y) \mapsto (x, xy)$ is neither open nor closed). However, one can still say: if *f* is a morphism between varieties, then the image of *f* contains an open [dense subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_subset) of its closure (cf. [constructible set](wikipedia://en/23234121)).

A morphism *f*:*X*→*Y* of algebraic varieties is said to be *dominant* if it has dense image. For such an *f*, if *V* is a nonempty open affine subset of *Y*, then there is a nonempty open affine subset *U* of *X* such that *f*(*U*) ⊂ *V* and then $f^{\#}: k[V] \to k[U]$ is injective. Thus, the dominant map *f* induces an injection on the level of function fields:

$$k(Y) = \varinjlim k[V] \hookrightarrow k(X), \, g \mapsto g \circ f$$

where the [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566) runs over all nonempty open affine subsets of *Y*. (More abstractly, this is the induced map from the [residue field](wikipedia://en/10789680) of the [generic point](wikipedia://en/3043978) of *Y* to that of *X*.) Conversely, every inclusion of fields $k(Y) \hookrightarrow k(X)$ is induced by a dominant [rational map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_map) from *X* to *Y*.[^o13] Hence, the above construction determines a contravariant-equivalence between the category of algebraic varieties over a field *k* and dominant rational maps between them and the category of finitely generated field extension of *k*.[^n3]

If *X* is a smooth complete curve (for example, **P**<sup>1</sup>) and if *f* is a rational map from *X* to a projective space **P**<sup>*m*</sup>, then *f* is a regular map *X* → **P**<sup>*m*</sup>.[^n4] In particular, when *X* is a smooth complete curve, any rational function on *X* may be viewed as a morphism *X* → **P**<sup>1</sup> and, conversely, such a morphism as a rational function on *X*.

On a [normal variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_variety) (in particular, a [smooth variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_variety)), a rational function is regular if and only if it has no poles of codimension one. This is an algebraic analog of [Hartogs' extension theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartogs'_extension_theorem). There is also a relative version of this fact; see <https://mathoverflow.net/q/87350>.

A morphism between algebraic varieties that is a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) between the underlying topological spaces need not be an isomorphism (a counterexample is given by a [Frobenius morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_morphism) $t \mapsto t^p$.) On the other hand, if *f* is bijective birational and the target space of *f* is a [normal variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_variety), then *f* is biregular. (cf. [Zariski's main theorem](wikipedia://en/13969950).)

A regular map between [complex algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/39219687) is a [holomorphic map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_map). (There is actually a slight technical difference: a regular map is a meromorphic map whose singular points are [removable](wikipedia://en/81644), but the distinction is usually ignored in practice.) In particular, a regular map into the complex numbers is just a usual [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110) (complex-analytic function).

:::

## Morphisms to a projective space {#Morphisms_to_a_projective_space}

Let

$$f: X \to \mathbf{P}^m$$

be a morphism from a [projective variety](wikipedia://en/320469) to a projective space. Let *x* be a point of *X*. Then some *i*-th homogeneous coordinate of *f*(*x*) is nonzero; say, *i* = 0 for simplicity. Then, by continuity, there is an open affine neighborhood *U* of *x* such that

$$f: U \to \mathbf{P}^m - \{ y_0 = 0 \}$$

is a morphism, where *y*<sub>*i*</sub> are the homogeneous coordinates. Note the target space is the affine space **A**<sup>*m*</sup> through the identification $(a_0 : \dots : a_m) = (1 : a_1 / a_0 : \dots : a_m / a_0) \sim (a_1 / a_0, \dots, a_m / a_0)$. Thus, by definition, the restriction *f* |<sub>*U*</sub> is given by

$$f|_U(x) = (g_1(x), \dots, g_m(x))$$

where *g*<sub>*i*</sub>'s are regular functions on *U*. Since *X* is projective, each *g*<sub>*i*</sub> is a fraction of homogeneous elements of the same degree in the homogeneous coordinate ring *k*\[*X*\] of *X*. We can arrange the fractions so that they all have the same homogeneous denominator say *f*<sub>0</sub>. Then we can write *g*<sub>*i*</sub> = *f*<sub>*i*</sub>/*f*<sub>0</sub> for some homogeneous elements *f*<sub>*i*</sub>'s in *k*\[*X*\]. Hence, going back to the homogeneous coordinates,

$$f(x) = (f_0(x) : f_1(x) : \dots : f_m(x))$$

for all *x* in *U* and by continuity for all *x* in *X* as long as the *f*<sub>*i*</sub>'s do not vanish at *x* simultaneously. If they vanish simultaneously at a point *x* of *X*, then, by the above procedure, one can pick a different set of *f*<sub>*i*</sub>'s that do not vanish at *x* simultaneously (see Note at the end of the section.)

In fact, the above description is valid for any [quasi-projective variety](wikipedia://en/1321785) *X*, an open subvariety of a projective variety $\overline{X}$; the difference being that *f*<sub>*i*</sub>'s are in the homogeneous coordinate ring of $\overline{X}$.

**Note**: The above does not say a morphism from a projective variety to a projective space is given by a single set of polynomials (unlike the affine case). For example, let *X* be the conic $y^2 = xz$ in **P**<sup>2</sup>. Then two maps $(x : y : z) \mapsto (x : y)$ and $(x : y : z) \mapsto (y : z)$ agree on the open subset $\{ (x : y : z) \in X \mid x \ne 0 , z \ne 0 \}$ of *X* (since $(x : y) = (xy : y^2) = (xy: xz) = (y : z)$) and so defines a morphism $f: X \to \mathbf{P}^1$.

## Fibers of a morphism {#Fibers_of_a_morphism}

The important fact is:[^o18]

::: {#o19 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

Let *f*: *X* → *Y* be a dominating (i.e., having dense image) morphism of algebraic varieties, and let *r* = dim *X* − dim *Y*. Then

For every irreducible closed subset *W* of *Y* and every irreducible component *Z* of $f^{-1}(W)$ dominating *W*,

$$\dim Z \ge \dim W + r.$$

There exists a nonempty open subset *U* in *Y* such that (a) $U \subset f(X)$ and (b) for every irreducible closed subset *W* of *Y* intersecting *U* and every irreducible component *Z* of $f^{-1}(W)$ intersecting $f^{-1}(U)$,

$$\dim Z = \dim W + r.$$

:::

::: {#o20 .statement kind=statement/corollary cue=math-theorem name="Corollary"}

**Corollary.**

Let *f*: *X* → *Y* be a morphism of algebraic varieties. For each *x* in *X*, define

$e(x) = \max \{ \dim Z \mid Z \text{ an irreducible component of } f^{-1}(f(x)) \text{ containing } x \}.$ Then *e* is [upper-semicontinuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper-semicontinuous); i.e., for each integer *n*, the set

$X_n = \{ x \in X \mid e(x) \ge n \}$ is closed.

:::

In Mumford's red book, the theorem is proved by means of [Noether's normalization lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether's_normalization_lemma). For an algebraic approach where the [generic freeness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generic_freeness) plays a main role and the notion of "[universally catenary ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universally_catenary_ring)" is a key in the proof, see Eisenbud, Ch. 14 of "Commutative algebra with a view toward algebraic geometry." In fact, the proof there shows that if *f* is [flat](wikipedia://en/542520), then the dimension equality in 2. of the theorem holds in general (not just generically).

::: {#o22 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Degree of a finite morphism"}

## Degree of a finite morphism {#Degree_of_a_finite_morphism}

Let *f*: *X* → *Y* be a [finite](wikipedia://en/2139357) surjective morphism between algebraic varieties over a field *k*. Then, by definition, the degree of *f* is the degree of the finite field extension of the function field *k*(*X*) over *f*<sup>\*</sup>*k*(*Y*). By [generic freeness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generic_freeness), there is some nonempty open subset *U* in *Y* such that the restriction of the structure sheaf *O*<sub>*X*</sub> to *f*<sup>−1</sup>(*U*) is free as [*O*<sub>*Y*</sub>$|$<sub>*U*</sub>-module](wikipedia://en/44887605). The degree of *f* is then also the rank of this free module.

If *f* is [étale](wikipedia://en/3022771) and if *X*, *Y* are [complete](wikipedia://en/1047627), then for any [coherent sheaf](wikipedia://en/603780) *F* on *Y*, writing χ for the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420#Generalizations),

$\chi(f^* F) = \deg(f) \chi (F).$[^n5]

(The [Riemann–Hurwitz formula](wikipedia://en/497413) for a ramified covering shows the "étale" here cannot be omitted.)

In general, if *f* is a finite surjective morphism, if *X*, *Y* are [complete](wikipedia://en/1047627) and *F* a coherent sheaf on *Y*, then from the [Leray spectral sequence](wikipedia://en/5174983) $\operatorname{H}^p(Y, R^q f_* f^* F) \Rightarrow \operatorname{H}^{p+q}(X, f^* F)$, one gets:

$$\chi(f^* F) = \sum_{q=0}^{\infty} (-1)^{q} \chi(R^q f_* f^* F).$$

In particular, if *F* is a tensor power $L^{\otimes n}$ of a line bundle, then $R^q f_*(f^* F) = R^q f_* \mathcal{O}_X \otimes L^{\otimes n}$ and since the support of $R^q f_* \mathcal{O}_X$ has positive codimension if *q* is positive, comparing the leading terms, one has:

$$\operatorname{deg}(f^* L) = \operatorname{deg}(f) \operatorname{deg}(L)$$

(since the [generic rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generic_rank) of $f_* \mathcal{O}_X$ is the degree of *f*.)

If *f* is étale and *k* is algebraically closed, then each geometric fiber *f*<sup>−1</sup>(*y*) consists exactly of deg(*f*) points.
