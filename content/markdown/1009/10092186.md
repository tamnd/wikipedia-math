# Nagata ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nagata ring"}

In [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990), an **N-1 ring** is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) $A$ whose [integral closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_closure) in its [quotient field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_field) is a [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) $A$-[module](wikipedia://en/276410). It is called a **Japanese ring** (or an **N-2 ring**) if for every [finite extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_extension) $L$ of its quotient field $K$, the integral closure of $A$ in $L$ is a finitely generated $A$-module (or equivalently a finite $A$-[algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_over_a_ring)). A [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) is called **universally Japanese** if every finitely generated integral domain over it is Japanese, and is called a **Nagata ring**, named for [Masayoshi Nagata](wikipedia://en/3086117), or a **pseudo-geometric ring** if it is [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) and universally Japanese (or, which turns out to be the same, if it is Noetherian and all of its [quotients](wikipedia://en/184635) by a [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) are N-2 rings). A ring is called **geometric** if it is the [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) of an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) or a [completion](wikipedia://en/9477975) of such a local ring,[^o2] but this concept is not used much.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

[Fields](wikipedia://en/10603) and rings of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) or [power series](wikipedia://en/60012) in finitely many indeterminates over fields are examples of Japanese rings. Another important example is a Noetherian [integrally closed domain](wikipedia://en/21420209) (e.g. a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132)) having a [perfect](wikipedia://en/505088) [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271). On the other hand, a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039) or even a [discrete valuation ring](wikipedia://en/848633) is not necessarily Japanese.

Any [quasi-excellent ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-excellent_ring) is a Nagata ring, so in particular almost all Noetherian rings that occur in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) are Nagata rings. The first example of a Noetherian domain that is not a Nagata ring was given by (Akizuki 1935).

Here is an example of a discrete valuation ring that is not a Japanese ring. Choose a prime $p$ and an infinite [degree](wikipedia://en/4382697) [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) $K$ of a [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) $p$ field $k$, such that $K^p\subseteq k$. Let the discrete valuation ring $R$ be the [ring of formal power series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_formal_power_series) over $K$ whose coefficients generate a finite extension of $k$. If $y$ is any formal power series not in $R$ then the ring $R[y]$ is not an N-1 ring (its integral closure is not a finitely generated module) so $R$ is not a Japanese ring.

If $R$ is the [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $k[x_1, x_2, ...]$ in infinitely many generators generated by the squares and cubes of all generators, and $S$ is obtained from $R$ by adjoining inverses to all elements not in any of the [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) generated by some $x_n$, then $S$ is a 1-dimensional Noetherian domain that is not an N-1 ring, in other words its integral closure in its quotient field is not a finitely generated $S$-module. Also $S$ has a cusp singularity at every [closed point](wikipedia://en/33086408), so the set of singular points is not closed.
