# Hardy–Littlewood maximal function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hardy–Littlewood maximal function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Hardy–Littlewood maximal operator** *M* is a significant non-linear [operator](wikipedia://en/4140245) used in [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478) and [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The operator takes a [locally integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_integrable) function $f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb C$ and returns another function $Mf : \mathbb{R}^d \to [0, \infty]$, where $Mf (x)$ is the supremum of the average of $f$ among all possible balls centered on $x$. Formally,

$$Mf(x)=\sup_{r>0} \frac{1}{|B(x, r)|}\int_{B(x, r)} |f(y)|\, dy,$$

where |*E*| denotes the [*d*-dimensional Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) of a subset *E* ⊂ **R**<sup>*d*</sup>, and $B(x,\,r)$ is the ball of radius, $r>0$, centered at the point $x\in\mathbb{R}^d$.

Since $f$ is locally integrable, the averages are jointly [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) in *x* and *r*, so the maximal function *Mf*, being the supremum over *r* > 0, is [measurable](wikipedia://en/44055).

A nontrivial corollary of the **Hardy–Littlewood maximal inequality** states that $Mf$ is finite [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688) for functions in $L^1$.

:::

::: {#o5 .statement cue=named-heading name="Hardy–Littlewood maximal inequality"}

## Hardy–Littlewood maximal inequality {#Hardy–Littlewood_maximal_inequality}

This theorem of [G. H. Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599) and [J. E. Littlewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._E._Littlewood) states that *M* is [bounded](wikipedia://en/455961) as a [sublinear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublinear_operator) from [*L<sup>p</sup>*(**R**<sup>*d*</sup>)](wikipedia://en/45194) to itself for *p* > 1. That is, if *f* ∈ *L<sup>p</sup>*(**R**<sup>*d*</sup>) then the maximal function *Mf* is weak *L*<sup>1</sup>-bounded and *Mf* ∈ *L<sup>p</sup>*(**R**<sup>*d*</sup>). Before stating the theorem more precisely, for simplicity, let {*f* > *t*} denote the set {*x* | *f*(*x*) > *t*}. Now we have:

> **Theorem (Weak Type Estimate).** For *d* ≥ 1, there is a constant *C<sub>d</sub>* > 0 such that for all λ > 0 and *f* ∈ *L*<sup>1</sup>(**R**<sup>*d*</sup>), we have:
>
> $$\left |\{Mf > \lambda\} \right |< \frac{C_d}{\lambda} \Vert f\Vert_{L^1 (\mathbf{R}^d)}.$$

With the Hardy–Littlewood maximal inequality in hand, the following *strong-type* estimate is an immediate consequence of the [Marcinkiewicz interpolation theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcinkiewicz_theorem):

> **Theorem (Strong Type Estimate).** For *d* ≥ 1, 1 \< *p* ≤ ∞, and *f* ∈ *L<sup>p</sup>*(**R**<sup>*d*</sup>), there is a constant *C<sub>p,d</sub>* > 0 such that
>
> $$\Vert Mf\Vert_{L^p (\mathbf{R}^d)}\leq C_{p,d}\Vert f\Vert_{L^p(\mathbf{R}^d)}.$$

In the strong type estimate the best bounds for *C<sub>p,d</sub>* are unknown.[^o6] However subsequently [Elias M. Stein](wikipedia://en/418073) used the Calderón-Zygmund method of rotations to prove the following:

> **Theorem (Dimension Independence).** For 1 \< *p* ≤ ∞ one can pick *C<sub>p,d</sub>* = *C<sub>p</sub>* independent of *d*.[^o6][^o7]

:::

::: {#o9 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

While there are several proofs of this theorem, a common one is given below, that uses the following version of the [Vitali covering lemma](wikipedia://en/10102876) to prove the weak-type estimate. (See the article for the proof of the lemma.)

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=math-theorem name="Lemma"}

**Lemma.**

Let *X* be a separable metric space and $\mathcal{F}$ a family of open balls with bounded diameter. Then $\mathcal{F}$ has a countable subfamily $\mathcal{F}'$ consisting of disjoint balls such that

$\bigcup_{B \in \mathcal{F}} B \subset \bigcup_{B \in \mathcal{F'}} 5B$ where 5*B* is *B* with 5 times radius.

:::

::: {#o11 .proof cue=math-proof name="Proof of weak-type estimate"}

**Proof of weak-type estimate.**

For *p* = ∞, the inequality is trivial (since the average of a function is no larger than its [essential supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essential_supremum)). For 1≤ *p* \< ∞, we use the lemma.

For every *x* such that *Mf*(*x*) > *t*, by definition, we can find a ball *B<sub>x</sub>* centered at *x* such that

$\int_{B_x} |f|dy > t|B_x|.$ Thus {*Mf* > *t*} is a subset of the union of such balls, as *x* varies in {*Mf* > *t*}. This is trivial since *x* is contained in *B<sub>x</sub>*. By the lemma, we can find, among such balls, a sequence of disjoint balls *B<sub>j</sub>* such that the union of 5*B<sub>j</sub>* covers {*Mf* > *t*}. It follows:

$|\{Mf > t\}| \le 5^d \sum_j |B_j| \le {5^d \over t} \int |f|dy.$ This completes the proof of the weak-type estimate.

The *L<sup>p</sup>* bounds for *p* > 1 can be deduced from the weak $L^1$ bound by the [Marcinkiewicz interpolation theorem](wikipedia://en/1033045). Here is how the argument goes in this particular case. Define the function $f_t \colon \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{C}$ by $f_t (x) = f(x)$ if $|f(x)| > t/2$ and 0 otherwise. We have then

$|f(x)| \le |f_t (x)| + \frac{t}{2}$ and, by the definition of maximal function $Mf$

$M f (x) \le Mf_t (x) + \frac{t}{2}$ By the weak-type estimate applied to $f_t$, we have:

$|\{Mf > t\}| \le |\{Mf_t > t/2\}| \le \frac{2 \cdot 5^d}{t} \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} |f_t (x)|dx = \frac{2 \cdot 5^d}{t} \int_{|f(x)| > \frac{t}{2}} |f(x)|dx.$ Then

$\|Mf\|_p^p = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \vert M f (x)\vert^p dx = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \int_0^{Mf(x)} pt^{p-1} dt dx = p \int_0^\infty t^{p-1} |\{ Mf > t \}| dt$ By the estimate above we have:

$$\|Mf\|_p^p \leq p \int_0^\infty t^{p-1} \left ({2\cdot 5^d \over t} \int_{|f(x)| > \frac{t}{2}} |f(x)|dx \right ) dt = 2\cdot 5^d p \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \int_{0}^{2|f(x)|} t^{p-2} dt |f(x)| dx = \frac{2^p \cdot 5^d p}{p - 1} \|f\|_p^p.$$

:::

Note that the constant $C=5^d$ in the proof can be improved to $3^d$ by using the [inner regularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_regular_measure) of the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198), and the finite version of the [Vitali covering lemma](wikipedia://en/10102876#Statement_of_the_lemma). See the [Discussion section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardy-Littlewood_maximal_function#Discussion) below for more about optimizing the constant.

:::

::: {#o13 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Some applications of the Hardy–Littlewood Maximal Inequality include proving the following results:

- [Lebesgue differentiation theorem](wikipedia://en/5766547)
- [Rademacher differentiation theorem](wikipedia://en/7764195)
- [Fatou's theorem](wikipedia://en/10738834) on nontangential convergence.
- [Fractional integration theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_integration_theorem)

Here we use a standard trick involving the maximal function to give a quick proof of Lebesgue differentiation theorem. (But remember that in the proof of the maximal theorem, we used the Vitali covering lemma.) Let *f* ∈ *L*<sup>1</sup>(**R**<sup>*n*</sup>) and

$$\Omega f (x) = \limsup_{r \to 0} f_r(x) - \liminf_{r \to 0} f_r(x)$$

where

$$f_r(x) = \frac{1}{|B(x, r)|} \int_{B(x, r)} f(y) dy.$$

We write *f* = *h* + *g* where *h* is continuous and has compact support and *g* ∈ *L*<sup>1</sup>(**R**<sup>*n*</sup>) with norm that can be made arbitrary small. Then

$$\Omega f \le \Omega g + \Omega h = \Omega g$$

by continuity. Now, Ω*g* ≤ 2*Mg* and so, by the theorem, we have:

$$\left | \{ \Omega g > \varepsilon \} \right | \le \frac{2\,M}{\varepsilon} \|g\|_1$$

Now, we can let $\|g\|_1 \to 0$ and conclude Ω*f* = 0 almost everywhere; that is, $\lim_{r \to 0} f_r(x)$ exists for almost all *x*. It remains to show the limit actually equals *f*(*x*). But this is easy: it is known that $\|f_r - f\|_1 \to 0$ ([approximation of the identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approximation_of_the_identity)) and thus there is a subsequence $f_{r_k} \to f$ almost everywhere. By the uniqueness of limit, *f<sub>r</sub>* → *f* almost everywhere then.

:::

## Discussion {#Discussion}

It is still unknown what the smallest constants *C<sub>p,d</sub>* and *C<sub>d</sub>* are in the above inequalities. However, a result of [Elias Stein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elias_Stein) about spherical maximal functions can be used to show that, for 1 \< *p* \< ∞, we can remove the dependence of *C<sub>p,d</sub>* on the dimension, that is, *C<sub>p,d</sub>* = *C<sub>p</sub>* for some constant *C<sub>p</sub>* > 0 only depending on *p*. It is unknown whether there is a weak bound that is independent of dimension.

There are several common variants of the Hardy-Littlewood maximal operator which replace the averages over centered balls with averages over different families of sets. For instance, one can define the *uncentered* HL maximal operator (using the notation of Stein-Shakarchi)

$$f^*(x) = \sup_{x \in B_x} \frac{1}{|B_x|} \int_{B_x} |f(y)| dy$$

where the balls *B<sub>x</sub>* are required to merely contain x, rather than be centered at x. There is also the *dyadic* HL maximal operator

$$M_\Delta f(x) = \sup_{x \in Q_x} \frac{1}{|Q_x|} \int_{Q_x} |f(y)| dy$$

where *Q<sub>x</sub>* ranges over all [dyadic cubes](wikipedia://en/30151810) containing the point *x*. Both of these operators satisfy the HL maximal inequality.
