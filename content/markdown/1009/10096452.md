# Mittag-Leffler Institute

*Image: IntitutMittagLeffler.jpg.* Mittag Leffler Institute.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mittag-Leffler Institute"}

The **Mittag-Leffler Institute** (Swedish: **Institut Mittag-Leffler**) is a [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) research institute in Sweden.[^o2] Located in [Djursholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djursholm), a suburb of [Stockholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm), it invites scholars to participate in half-year programs in specialized mathematical subjects. The Institute is run by the [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Swedish_Academy_of_Sciences) on behalf of research societies representing all the [Scandinavian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scandinavia) countries. The institute focuses on hosting half-year programmes in specialised mathematical subjects, inviting scholars from around the world to collaborate in a focused, contemplative atmosphere. It houses a mathematical library with rare historical collections and continues Mittag-Leffler's vision of mathematics as an intellectual pursuit that transcends national boundaries. The institute is housed in the former [villa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa) of Mittag-Leffler, which was converted into a research facility.

:::

## History {#History}

[Gösta Mittag-Leffler](wikipedia://en/1438792) (1846–1927) originally envisaged the institute, announcing on his seventieth birthday in 1916 that he and his wife would donate their fortune to found a centre for mathematics. During his lifetime he assembled an extensive mathematical library and hosted visiting scholars at his villa. After earning his doctorate from [Uppsala University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uppsala_University) in 1872, he studied in Paris and Berlin, where he was influenced by [Karl Weierstrass](wikipedia://en/93459). He held professorships at the [University of Helsinki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Helsinki) from 1875 and at [Stockholm University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_University) from 1880, and contacts with [Charles Hermite](wikipedia://en/22870422) and others shaped his belief that mathematics should transcend national boundaries.[^o12]

Following Mittag-Leffler's death in 1927, realisation of his dream faced financial setbacks when his fortune—invested largely in German [government bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_bond)—declined after the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War). [Torsten Carleman](wikipedia://en/6063370) was appointed director and maintained the library and occasional lectures but was unable to establish a full institute before his death in 1948. For two decades the library persisted under the Academy's mathematics class, and in the 1960s the sale of complete runs of *[Acta Mathematica](wikipedia://en/6860708)* provided fresh funding.[^o12]

In 1967, [Lennart Carleson](wikipedia://en/1669541) revitalised the project using a personal professorship from the Swedish government. He relocated to [Djursholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djursholm) and inaugurated the first scientific programme on [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147) in 1969–70. Rather than adopting a permanent-faculty model, Carleson followed Mittag-Leffler's vision of year-long gatherings of visiting researchers, securing support from the [Academy of Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Finland), the [Knut and Alice Wallenberg Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knut_and_Alice_Wallenberg_Foundation) and other Scandinavian bodies.[^o12]

## Organisation and activities {#Organisation_and_activities}

The institute is one of seven under the [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Swedish_Academy_of_Sciences), with an endowment of about 125 million [Swedish krona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_krona) in the late 1990s covering nearly half its budget. Additional funds come from foundations, the Swedish Natural Sciences Research Council and contributions from other [Nordic countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordic_countries). A small administrative staff manage daily operations, while the Academy's mathematics class, national representatives and international advisors form the board.[^o12]

Each year-long programme, running from September to May, focuses on a single mathematical theme and is organised by a committee that must include at least one or two Scandinavian scholars. A scientific leader oversees seminars and spends at least one term on site. The institute hosts five to ten [postdoctoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postdoctoral) researchers and a varying number of senior visitors—accommodating up to twenty-eight residents at once—and emphasises informal interaction through twice-weekly seminars and weekly tea gatherings.[^o12]

## Facilities {#Facilities}

*Image: IML-Kuskvillan.jpg.* The Kuskvillan house at the Mittag Leffler Institute.

The institute occupies the [villa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa) that Mittag-Leffler built in the 1890s and remodelled in 1907 by [Ferdinand Boberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Boberg), set within seven acres of parkland in [Djursholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djursholm). The Swedish-style interior features an oak-panelled library with a gallery, a round "flower room" with marble flooring and distinctive details such as [dachshund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dachshund)-shaped fireplace gratings and owl motifs. Visitor housing includes two historic cottages and two modern buildings added during the institute's expansion.[^o12]

## Library and archival collections {#Library_and_archival_collections}

Mittag-Leffler's original collection, acquired across Europe, forms the core of a remarkable library with rare books and complete leather-bound runs of key journals. The institute holds about 250 current journal subscriptions—fewer than major university libraries—but receives additional titles in exchange for issues of *Acta Mathematica* and *[Arkiv för Matematik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arkiv_för_Matematik)*.[^o12]

The Institute's archives contain correspondence and personal papers of several prominent figures in nineteenth- and early twentieth-century mathematics. More than forty boxes hold the *[Nachlass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nachlass)* of [Karl Weierstrass](wikipedia://en/93459), comprising letters, lecture manuscripts and drafts of his work on analytic and [elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/69939), together with correspondence with contemporaries such as [Sophie Kovalevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophie_Kovalevsky) and [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887). Separate collections preserve the papers of [Sophie Kovalevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophie_Kovalevsky), [Georg Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216), [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) and [Philip Jourdain](wikipedia://en/3966802), including letters, unpublished manuscripts and family documents.[^o11]

In addition to personal papers, the archives include fifty volumes of [Weierstrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Theodor_Wilhelm_Weierstrass)'s lecture manuscripts (circa 1860s–1880s), numerous boxes of mathematical notes and offprints, and the [facsimile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facsimile) collection of Prince [Baldassarre Boncompagni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldassarre_Boncompagni), which comprises about five thousand reproductions of medieval and early modern mathematical manuscripts. The holdings also feature early nineteenth-century materials such as [Niels H. Abel's](wikipedia://en/21573) original papers on [transcendental functions](wikipedia://en/297021) and correspondence between [Carl Jacobi](wikipedia://en/210340) and [Adrien-Marie Legendre](wikipedia://en/49695) on elliptic function theory.[^o11]

## Legacy and significance {#Legacy_and_significance}

Despite its modest size, the institute has been instrumental in the growth of Swedish mathematics. From only a handful of professors when Mittag-Leffler began teaching, Sweden now has over one hundred in the field. The range of research has broadened from classical analysis to [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) and [computational mathematics](wikipedia://en/10433833), aided by the institute's role in introducing Scandinavian scholars to international developments.[^o12]

## Notable connections {#Notable_connections}

The institute maintains a close link to *Acta Mathematica*, founded by Mittag-Leffler in 1882. Originally conceived for Scandinavian contributions at [Sophus Lie](wikipedia://en/54010)'s suggestion, it quickly became international through efforts to secure papers from [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) and others. In 1889, after publication of Poincaré's prize-winning work on the [three-body problem](wikipedia://en/1208420), Mittag-Leffler famously recalled and corrected the entire issue—a testament to his commitment to precision.[^o12]

A first-edition copy of [Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicus)'s "*[De revolutionibus orbium coelestium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_revolutionibus_orbium_coelestium)*" (On the Revolutions of Heavenly Spheres) was stolen from the Mittag-Leffler Institute in 2000.[^o9]
