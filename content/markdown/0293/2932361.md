# GCD domain

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="GCD domain"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **GCD domain** is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) *R* with the property that any two elements have a [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) (GCD); i.e., there is a minimum [principal ideal](wikipedia://en/145126) containing the ideal [generated by](wikipedia://en/24873145#Subring_generated_by_a_set) two given elements. Equivalently, any two elements of *R* have a [least common multiple](wikipedia://en/17961) (LCM).[^o2]

:::

A GCD domain generalizes a [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546) (UFD) to a non-[Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) setting in the following sense: an integral domain is a UFD [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is a GCD domain satisfying the [ascending chain condition on principal ideals](wikipedia://en/22243102) (and in particular if it is Noetherian).

## Properties {#Properties}

Every [irreducible element](wikipedia://en/60693) of a GCD domain is [prime](wikipedia://en/60692). A GCD domain is [integrally closed](wikipedia://en/21420209), and every nonzero element is [primal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primal_element). In other words, every GCD domain is a [Schreier domain](wikipedia://en/22284043).

For every pair of elements *x*, *y* of a GCD domain *R*, a GCD *d* of *x* and *y* and an LCM *m* of *x* and *y* can be chosen such that *dm* = *xy*, or stated differently, if *x* and *y* are nonzero elements and *d* is any GCD *d* of *x* and *y*, then *xy*/*d* is an LCM of *x* and *y*, and vice versa. It [follows](wikipedia://en/310883#Characteristic_properties) that the operations of GCD and LCM make the quotient *R*/~ into a [distributive lattice](wikipedia://en/310883), where "~" denotes the [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) of being [associate elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associate_elements). The equivalence between the existence of GCDs and the existence of LCMs is not a corollary of the similar result on [complete lattices](wikipedia://en/44777), as the quotient *R*/~ need not be a complete lattice for a GCD domain *R*.

If *R* is a GCD domain, then the polynomial ring *R*\[*X*<sub>1</sub>,...,*X*<sub>*n*</sub>\] is also a GCD domain.[^o5]

R is a GCD domain if and only if finite intersections of its [principal ideals](wikipedia://en/145126) are principal. In particular, $(a) \cap (b) = (c)$, where $c$ is the LCM of $a$ and $b$.

For a polynomial in *X* over a GCD domain, one can define its content as the GCD of all its coefficients. Then the content of a product of polynomials is the product of their contents, as expressed by [Gauss's lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_lemma_%28polynomial%29), which is valid over GCD domains.

## Examples {#Examples}

- A [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546) is a GCD domain. Among the GCD domains, the unique factorization domains are precisely those that are also [atomic domains](wikipedia://en/9599592) (which means that at least one factorization into irreducible elements exists for any nonzero nonunit).
- A [Bézout domain](wikipedia://en/3131407) (i.e., an integral domain where every finitely generated ideal is principal) is a GCD domain. Unlike [principal ideal domains](wikipedia://en/25039) (where *every* ideal is principal), a Bézout domain need not be a unique factorization domain; for instance the ring of [entire functions](wikipedia://en/10372) is a non-atomic Bézout domain, and there are many other examples. An integral domain is a [Prüfer](wikipedia://en/12367835) GCD domain if and only if it is a Bézout domain.[^o8]
- If *R* is a non-atomic GCD domain, then *R*\[*X*\] is an example of a GCD domain that is neither a unique factorization domain (since it is non-atomic) nor a Bézout domain (since *X* and a non-invertible and non-zero element *a* of *R* generate an ideal not containing 1, but 1 is nevertheless a GCD of *X* and *a*); more generally any ring *R*\[*X*<sub>1</sub>,...,*X*<sub>*n*</sub>\] has these properties.
- A [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346) [monoid ring](wikipedia://en/20781) $R[X; S]$ is a GCD domain iff $R$ is a GCD domain and $S$ is a [torsion-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion-free_group) [cancellative](wikipedia://en/22772051) GCD-[semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799). A GCD-semigroup is a semigroup with the additional property that for any $a$ and $b$ in the semigroup $S$, there exists a $c$ such that $(a + S) \cap (b + S) = c + S$. In particular, if $G$ is an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974), then $R[X;G]$ is a GCD domain iff $R$ is a GCD domain and $G$ is torsion-free.[^o9]
- The ring $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-d}]$ is not a GCD domain for all [square-free integers](wikipedia://en/29525) $d\ge 3$.[^o10]

:::

## G-GCD domains {#G-GCD_domains}

Many of the properties of GCD domain carry over to Generalized GCD domains,[^o12] where principal ideals are generalized to [invertible ideals](wikipedia://en/460700) and where the intersection of two invertible ideals is invertible, so that the group of invertible ideals forms a lattice. In GCD rings, ideals are invertible if and only if they are principal, meaning the GCD and LCM operations can also be treated as operations on invertible ideals.

Examples of G-GCD domains include GCD domains, polynomial rings over GCD domains, [Prüfer domains](wikipedia://en/12367835), and π-domains (domains where every principal ideal is the product of prime ideals), which generalizes the GCD property of [Bézout domains](wikipedia://en/3131407) and [unique factorization domains](wikipedia://en/60546).
