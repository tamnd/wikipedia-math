# Chevalley–Warning theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Chevalley–Warning theorem"}

In number theory, the **Chevalley–Warning theorem** implies that certain [polynomial equations](wikipedia://en/23000) in sufficiently many variables over a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) have solutions. It was proved by (txt) and a slightly weaker form of the theorem, known as **Chevalley's theorem**, was proved by (txt). Chevalley's theorem implied [Artin's](wikipedia://en/245351) and [Dickson's](wikipedia://en/1790247) conjecture that finite fields are [quasi-algebraically closed fields](wikipedia://en/2934213) (Artin 1982, page x).

:::

::: {#o6 .statement cue=heading name="Statement of the theorems"}

## Statement of the theorems {#Statement_of_the_theorems}

Let $\mathbb{F}$ be a finite field and $\{f_j\}_{j=1}^r\subseteq\mathbb{F}[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$ be a set of polynomials such that the number of variables satisfies

$$n>\sum_{j=1}^r d_j$$

where $d_j$ is the [total degree](wikipedia://en/5930652#Extension_to_polynomials_with_two_or_more_variables) of $f_j$. The theorems are statements about the solutions of the following system of polynomial equations

$$f_j(x_1,\dots,x_n)=0\quad\text{for}\, j=1,\ldots, r.$$

- The *Chevalley–Warning theorem* states that the number of common solutions $(a_1,\dots,a_n) \in \mathbb{F}^n$ is divisible by the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) $p$ of $\mathbb{F}$. Or in other words, the cardinality of the vanishing set of $\{f_j\}_{j=1}^r$ is $0$ modulo $p$.
- The *Chevalley theorem* states that if the system has the trivial solution $(0,\dots,0) \in \mathbb{F}^n$, that is, if the polynomials have no constant terms, then the system also has a non-trivial solution $(a_1,\dots,a_n) \in \mathbb{F}^n \backslash \{(0,\dots,0)\}$.

Chevalley's theorem is an immediate consequence of the Chevalley–Warning theorem since $p$ is at least 2.

Both theorems are best possible in the sense that, given any $n$, the list $f_j = x_j, j=1,\dots,n$ has total degree $n$ and only the trivial solution. Alternatively, using just one polynomial, we can take *f*<sub>1</sub> to be the degree *n* polynomial given by the [norm](wikipedia://en/450555) of *x*<sub>1</sub>*a*<sub>1</sub> + ... + *x*<sub>*n*</sub>*a*<sub>*n*</sub> where the elements *a* form a basis of the finite field of order *p*<sup>*n*</sup>.

Warning proved another theorem, known as Warning's second theorem, which states that if the system of polynomial equations has the trivial solution, then it has at least $q^{n-d}$ solutions where $q$ is the size of the finite field and $d := d_1 + \dots + d_r$. Chevalley's theorem also follows directly from this.

:::

::: {#o8 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of Warning's theorem"}

## Proof of Warning's theorem {#Proof_of_Warning's_theorem}

*Remark:*[^o9] If $i<q-1$ then

$$\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}}x^i=0$$

so the sum over $\mathbb{F}^n$ of any polynomial in $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ of degree less than $n(q-1)$ also vanishes.

The total number of common solutions modulo $p$ of $f_1, \ldots, f_r = 0$ is equal to

$$\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}^n}(1-f_1^{q-1}(x))\cdot\ldots\cdot(1-f_r^{q-1}(x))$$

because each term is 1 for a solution and 0 otherwise. If the sum of the degrees of the polynomials $f_i$ is less than *n* then this vanishes by the remark above.

:::

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Artin's conjecture"}

## Artin's conjecture {#Artin's_conjecture}

It is a consequence of Chevalley's theorem that finite fields are [quasi-algebraically closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-algebraically_closed). This had been conjectured by [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351) in 1935. The motivation behind Artin's conjecture was his observation that quasi-algebraically closed fields have trivial [Brauer group](wikipedia://en/404078), together with the fact that finite fields have trivial Brauer group by [Wedderburn's theorem](wikipedia://en/7132002).

:::

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="The Ax–Katz theorem"}

## The Ax–Katz theorem {#The_Ax–Katz_theorem}

The **Ax–Katz theorem**, named after [James Ax](wikipedia://en/6165715) and [Nicholas Katz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Katz), determines more accurately a power $q^b$ of the cardinality $q$ of $\mathbb{F}$ dividing the number of solutions; here, if $d$ is the largest of the $d_j$, then the exponent $b$ can be taken as the [ceiling function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceiling_function) of

$$\frac{n - \sum_j d_j}{d}.$$

The Ax–Katz result has an interpretation in [étale cohomology](wikipedia://en/396231) as a divisibility result for the (reciprocals of) the zeroes and poles of the [local zeta-function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_zeta-function). Namely, the same power of $q$ divides each of these [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981).

:::
