# Quantum calculus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quantum calculus"}

**Quantum calculus**, sometimes called **calculus without limits**, is equivalent to traditional [infinitesimal calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal_calculus) without the notion of [limits](wikipedia://en/285759). The two types of calculus in quantum calculus are *q*-calculus and *h*-calculus. The goal of both types is to find "analogs" of mathematical objects, where, after taking a certain [limit](wikipedia://en/285759), the original object is returned. In *q*-calculus, the limit as *q* tends to 1 is taken of the [*q*-analog](wikipedia://en/1619396). Likewise, in *h*-calculus, the limit as h tends to 0 is taken of the *h*-analog. The parameters $q$ and $h$ can be related by the formula $q = e^h$.

:::

## Differentiation {#Differentiation}

The *q*-differential and *h*-differential are defined as:

$$d_q(f(x)) = f(qx) - f(x)$$

and

$$d_h(f(x)) = f(x + h) - f(x),$$

respectively. The [*q*-derivative](wikipedia://en/3921324) and *h*-derivative are then defined as

$$D_q(f(x)) = \frac{d_q(f(x))}{d_q(x)} = \frac{f(qx) - f(x)}{qx - x}$$

and

$$D_h(f(x)) = \frac{d_h(f(x))}{d_h(x)} = \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}$$

respectively. By taking the [limit](wikipedia://en/285759) as $q \rightarrow 1$ of the *q*-derivative or as $h \rightarrow 0$ of the *h*-derivative, one can obtain the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921):

$$\lim_{q \rightarrow 1} D_{q}f(x) = \lim_{h \rightarrow 0}D_h f(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl( f(x) \Bigr)$$

## Integration {#Integration}

### q-integral {#q-integral}

A function *F*(*x*) is a q-antiderivative of *f*(*x*) if *D*<sub>q</sub>*F*(*x*) = *f*(*x*). The q-antiderivative (or q-integral) is denoted by $\int f(x) \, d_qx$ and an expression for *F*(*x*) can be found from:$\int f(x) \, d_qx = (1-q) \sum_{j=0}^\infty xq^j f(xq^j)$, which is called the [Jackson integral](wikipedia://en/10335099) of *f*(*x*). For 0 \< *q* \< 1, the series converges to a function *F*(*x*) on an interval (0,*A*\] if |*f*(*x*)*x*<sup>*α*</sup>| is bounded on the interval 0, *A* for some 0 ≤ *α* \< 1.

The q-integral is a [Riemann–Stieltjes integral](wikipedia://en/330491) with respect to a [step function](wikipedia://en/170939) having infinitely many points of increase at the points *q*<sup>*j.*</sup>.The jump at the point *q*<sup>*j*</sup> is *q*<sup>*j*</sup>. Calling this step function *g*<sub>*q*</sub>(*t*) gives *dg*<sub>*q*</sub>(*t*) = *d*<sub>*q*</sub>*t*.[^o5]

### h-integral {#h-integral}

A function *F*(*x*) is an h-antiderivative of *f*(*x*) if *D*<sub>*h*</sub>*F*(*x*) = *f*(*x*). The h-integral is denoted by $\int f(x) \, d_hx$. If *a* and *b* differ by an integer multiple of *h* then the definite integral $\int_a^b f(x) \, d_hx$ is given by a [Riemann sum](wikipedia://en/176478) of *f*(*x*) on the interval *a*, *b*, partitioned into sub-intervals of equal width *h*. The motivation of h-integral comes from the Riemann sum of f(x). Following the idea of the motivation of classical integrals, some of the properties of classical integrals hold in h-integral. This notion has broad applications in [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506), and especially [finite difference calculus](wikipedia://en/39147).

## Example {#Example}

In infinitesimal calculus, the derivative of the function $x^n$ is $nx^{n-1}$ (for some positive integer $n$). The corresponding expressions in *q*-calculus and *h*-calculus are:

$$D_q(x^n) = \frac{1- q^n}{1 - q} x^{n - 1} = [n]_q\ x^{n - 1}$$

where $[n]_q$ is the [*q*-bracket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q-bracket)

$$[n]_q = \frac{1 - q^n}{1 - q}$$

and

$$\begin{aligned} D_h(x^n) &= \frac{(x+h)^n - x^n}{h} \\ &= \frac{1}{h}\left(\sum_{k=0}^n{\binom{n}{k} x^{n-k} h^k - x^n} \right) \\ &= \frac{1}{h}\sum_{k=1}^n{\binom{n}{k} x^{n-k} h^k} \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^n{\binom{n}{k} x^{n-k} h^{k-1}} \\ &= n x^{n - 1} + \frac{n(n-1)}{2} h x^{n - 2} + \cdots + n h^{n-2}x + h^{n - 1}, \end{aligned}$$

respectively. The expression $[n]_q x^{n - 1}$ is then the [*q*-analog](wikipedia://en/1619396) and $\sum_{k=1}^n{\binom{n}{k} x^{n-k} h^{k-1}}$ is the *h*-analog of the power rule for positive integral powers. The [q-Taylor expansion](wikipedia://en/3921324) allows for the definition of *q*-analogs of all of the usual functions, such as the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) function, whose *q*-derivative is the *q*-analog of [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine).

:::

## History {#History}

The *h*-calculus is the [calculus of finite differences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus_of_finite_differences), which was studied by [George Boole](wikipedia://en/38298) and others, and has proven useful in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) and [fluid mechanics](wikipedia://en/2684988). In a sense, *q*-calculus dates back to [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) and [Carl Gustav Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Gustav_Jacobi), but has only recently begun to find usefulness in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), given its intimate connection with commutativity relations and [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944), specifically [quantum groups](wikipedia://en/340678).
