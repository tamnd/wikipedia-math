# Hurwitz's theorem (composition algebras)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hurwitz's theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Hurwitz's theorem** is a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) of [Adolf Hurwitz](wikipedia://en/771690), published posthumously in 1923, solving the [Hurwitz problem](wikipedia://en/36830673) for [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [unital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_algebra) [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [non-associative algebras](wikipedia://en/6533841) endowed with a nondegenerate [positive-definite](wikipedia://en/20437320) [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478). The theorem states that if the quadratic form defines a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) into the positive real numbers on the non-zero part of the algebra, then the algebra must be [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440), or the [octonions](wikipedia://en/51436), and that there are no other possibilities. Such algebras, sometimes called **Hurwitz algebras**, are examples of [composition algebras](wikipedia://en/3861635).

:::

The theory of composition algebras has subsequently been generalized to arbitrary quadratic forms and arbitrary [fields](wikipedia://en/10603).[^o2] Hurwitz's theorem implies that multiplicative formulas for sums of squares can only occur in 1, 2, 4 and 8 dimensions, a result originally [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) by Hurwitz in 1898. It is a special case of the [Hurwitz problem](wikipedia://en/36830673), solved also in (Radon 1922). Subsequent proofs of the restrictions on the dimension have been given by (Eckmann 1943) using the [representation theory of finite groups](wikipedia://en/874400) and by (Lee 1948) and (Chevalley 1954) using [Clifford algebras](wikipedia://en/45305). Hurwitz's theorem has been applied in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) to problems on [vector fields on spheres](wikipedia://en/3100908#Radon–Hurwitz_numbers) and the [homotopy groups](wikipedia://en/297506) of the [classical groups](wikipedia://en/10172878)[^o7] and in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) to the [classification of simple Jordan algebras](wikipedia://en/39156141#Simple_Euclidean_Jordan_algebras).[^o8]

## Euclidean Hurwitz algebras {#Euclidean_Hurwitz_algebras}

::: {#o11 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

A **Hurwitz algebra** or **composition algebra** is a finite-dimensional not necessarily associative algebra $A$ with identity endowed with a nondegenerate quadratic form $q$ such that $q(a\,b) = q(a)\,q(b)$. If the underlying coefficient field is the reals and $q$ is positive-definite, so that $(a,\,b) = \tfrac{1}{2}[q(a + b) - q(a) - q(b)]$ is an [inner product](wikipedia://en/14856), then $A$ is called a **Euclidean Hurwitz algebra** or (finite-dimensional) **normed division algebra**.[^o12]

If $A$ is a Euclidean Hurwitz algebra and $a$ is in $A$, define the [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) and right and left multiplication operators by

$$a^* = -a + 2(a,1)1,\quad L(a)b = ab,\quad R(a)b = ba.$$

Evidently the involution has period two and preserves the inner product and norm. These operators have the following properties:

- the involution is an [antiautomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiautomorphism), i.e. $(ab)* = b*a*$
- $$aa* = \|a\|^{2}\,1 = a*a$$
- $L(a*) = L(a)*$, $R(a*) = R(a)*$, so that the involution on the algebra corresponds to taking [adjoints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_operator)
- $\operatorname{Re} (ab) = \operatorname{Re} (ba)$ if $\operatorname{Re}\,x = (x + x*)/2 = (x,\,1)1$
- $$\operatorname{Re} (ab)c = \operatorname{Re}\,a(bc)$$
- $L(a^{2}) = L(a)^{2}$, $R(a^{2}) = R(a)^{2}$, so that $A$ is an [alternative algebra](wikipedia://en/3160).

These properties are proved starting from the polarized version of the identity $(ab,\,ab) = (a,\,a)(b,\,b)$:

$$\displaystyle{2(a,b)(c,d) = (ac,bd) + (ad,bc).}$$

Setting $b = 1$ or $d = 1$ yields $L(a*) = L(a)*$ and $R(c*) = R(c)*$.

Hence $\operatorname{Re}(ab) = (ab,\,1)1 = (a,\,b*)1 = (ba,\,1)1 = \operatorname{Re}(ba)$.

Similarly $\operatorname{Re} (ab)c = ((ab)c,1)1 = (ab,\,c*)1 = (b,\,a*\,c*)1 = (bc,a*)1 = (a(bc),1)1 = \operatorname{Re} a(bc)$.

Hence $((ab)*, c) = (ab, c*) = (b, a*c*) = (1, b*(a*c*)) = (1, (b*a*)c*) = (b*a*, c)$, so that $(ab)* = b*a*$.

By the polarized identity $\|a\|^{2}\,(c,\,d) = (ac,\,ad) = (a*\,(ac),\,d)$ so $L(a*)\,L(a) = L(\|a\|^{2})$. Applied to 1 this gives $a*a = \|a\|^{2} 1$. Replacing $a$ by $a*$ gives the other identity.

Substituting the formula for $a*$ in $L(a*)\,L(a) = L(a*a)$ gives $L(a)^{2} = L(a^{2})$. The formula $R(a^{2}) = R(a)^{2}$ is proved analogously.

:::

### Classification {#Classification}

It is routine to check that the real numbers $\mathbf{R}$, the complex numbers $\mathbf{C}$ and the quaternions $\mathbf{H}$ are examples of associative Euclidean Hurwitz algebras with their standard norms and involutions. There are moreover natural inclusions $\mathbf{R} \subset \mathbf{C} \subset \mathbf{H}$.

Analysing such an inclusion leads to the [Cayley–Dickson construction](wikipedia://en/175609), formalized by [A.A. Albert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Adrian_Albert). Let $A$ be a Euclidean Hurwitz algebra and $B$ a proper unital subalgebra, so a Euclidean Hurwitz algebra in its own right. Pick a [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) $j$ in $A$ orthogonal to $B$. Since $(j,\,1) = 0$, it follows that $j* = -j$ and hence $j^{2} = -1$. Let $C$ be subalgebra generated by $B$ and $j$. It is unital and is again a Euclidean Hurwitz algebra. It satisfies the following [Cayley–Dickson multiplication laws](wikipedia://en/175609):

$$\displaystyle{C=B\oplus Bj, \,\,\, (a+bj)^*=a^* - bj, \,\,\, (a+bj)(c+dj)=(ac -d^*b) +(bc^*+da)j.}$$

$B$ and $Bj$ are orthogonal, since $j$ is orthogonal to $B$. If $a$ is in $B$, then $j\,a = a*\,j$, since by orthogonal $0 = 2(j,\,a*) = ja - a*j$. The formula for the involution follows. To show that $B \oplus B\,j$ is closed under multiplication $Bj = jB$. Since $Bj$ is orthogonal to 1, $(bj)* = -bj$.

- $b(cj) = (cb)j$ since $(b,\,j) = 0$ so that, for $x$ in $A$, $(b(cj),\,x) = (b(jx),\,j(cj)) = -(b(jx),\,c*) = -(cb,\,(jx)*) = -((cb)j,\,x*) = ((cb)j,\,x)$.
- $(jc)b = j(bc)$ taking adjoints above.
- $(bj)(cj) = -c*b$ since $(b,\,cj)$ = 0, so that, for $x$ in $A$, $((bj)(cj),\,x) = -((cj)x*,\,bj) = (bx*,\,(cj)j) = -(c*b,\,x)$.

Imposing the multiplicativity of the norm on $C$ for $a + bj$ and $c + dj$ gives:

$$\displaystyle{(\|a\|^2 + \|b\|^2)(\|c\|^2 + \|d\|^2) = \|ac - d^*b\|^2 + \|bc^* + da\|^2,}$$

which leads to

$$\displaystyle{(ac,d^*b) = (bc^*,da).}$$

Hence $d(ac) = (da)c$, so that $B$ *must be associative*.

This analysis applies to the inclusion of $\mathbf{R}$ in $\mathbf{C}$ and $\mathbf{C}$ in $\mathbf{H}$. Taking $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{H} \oplus \mathbf{H}$ with the product and inner product above gives a noncommutative nonassociative algebra generated by $\mathbf{J} = (0,\,1)$. This recovers the usual definition of the [octonions](wikipedia://en/51436) or [Cayley numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cayley_number). If $A$ is a Euclidean algebra, it must contain $\mathbf{R}$. If it is strictly larger than $\mathbf{R}$, the argument above shows that it contains $\mathbf{C}$. If it is larger than $\mathbf{C}$, it contains $\mathbf{H}$. If it is larger still, it must contain $\mathbf{O}$. But there the process must stop, because $\mathbf{O}$ is not associative. In fact $\mathbf{H}$ is not commutative and $a(bj) = (ba)j \ne (ab)j$ in $\mathbf{O}$.[^o14]

**Theorem.** The only Euclidean Hurwitz algebras are the real numbers, the complex numbers, the quaternions and the octonions.

## Other proofs {#Other_proofs}

The proofs of (Lee 1948) and (Chevalley 1954) use [Clifford algebras](wikipedia://en/45305) to show that the dimension $N$ of $A$ must be 1, 2, 4 or 8. In fact the operators $L(a)$ with $(a,\,1) = 0$ satisfy $L(a)^{2} = -\|a\|^{2}$ and so form a real Clifford algebra. If $a$ is a unit vector, then $L(a)$ is skew-adjoint with square $-I$. So $N$ must be either [even](wikipedia://en/143135) or 1 (in which case $A$ contains no unit vectors orthogonal to 1). The real Clifford algebra and its [complexification](wikipedia://en/755604) act on the complexification of $A$, an $N$-dimensional complex space. If $N$ is even, $N - 1$ is [odd](wikipedia://en/143135), so the Clifford algebra has exactly two complex [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982) of dimension $2^{N/2 - 1}$. So this [power of 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_of_2) must divide $N$. It is easy to see that this implies $N$ can only be 1, 2, 4 or 8.

The proof of (Eckmann 1943) uses the [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200) of [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707), or the projective representation theory of [elementary abelian 2-groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_abelian_2-group), known to be equivalent to the representation theory of real Clifford algebras. Indeed, taking an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) $e_{i}$ of the [orthogonal complement](wikipedia://en/1058833) of 1 gives rise to operators $U_{i} = L(e_{i})$ satisfying

$$U_i^2 = -I,\quad U_i U_j = -U_j U_i \,\, (i \ne j).$$

This is a [projective representation](wikipedia://en/348860) of a direct product of $N - 1$ [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) of [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) 2. ($N$ is assumed to be greater than 1.) The operators $U_{i}$ by construction are skew-symmetric and orthogonal. In fact Eckmann constructed operators of this type in a slightly different but equivalent way. It is in fact the method originally followed in (Hurwitz 1923).[^o20] Assume that there is a composition law for two forms

$$\displaystyle{(x_1^2 + \cdots +x_N^2)(y_1^2 + \cdots + y_N^2) =z_1^2 + \cdots + z_N^2,}$$

where $z_{i}$ is bilinear in $x$ and $y$. Thus

$$\displaystyle{z_i=\sum_{j=1}^N a_{ij}(x)y_j}$$

where the [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $T(x) = (a_{ij})$ is linear in $x$. The relations above are equivalent to

$$\displaystyle{T(x)T(x)^t=x_1^2 +\cdots + x_N^2.}$$

Writing

$$\displaystyle{T(x)=T_1x_1 + \cdots + T_Nx_N,}$$

the relations become

$$\displaystyle{T_iT^t_j+T_jT_i^t =2\delta_{ij}I.}$$

Now set $V_{i} = (T_{N})^{t} T_{i}$. Thus $V_{N} = I$ and the $V_{1},\,...\,,\,V_{N - 1}$ are skew-adjoint, orthogonal satisfying exactly the same relations as the $U_{i}$'s:

$$\displaystyle{V_i^2 = -I,\quad V_iV_j = -V_jV_i \,\, (i \ne j).}$$

Since $V_{i}$ is an [orthogonal matrix](wikipedia://en/105620) with square $-I$ on a real [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370), $N$ is even.

Let $G$ be the finite group generated by elements $v_{i}$ such that

$$\displaystyle{v_i^2 = \varepsilon,\quad v_iv_j = \varepsilon v_jv_i \,\, (i \ne j),}$$

where $\varepsilon$ is [central](wikipedia://en/7125) of order 2. The [commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847) $[G,\,G]$ is just formed of 1 and $\varepsilon$. If $N$ is odd this coincides with the [center](wikipedia://en/7125) while if $N$ is even the center has order 4 with extra elements $\gamma = v_{1}...v_{N -\,1}$ and $\varepsilon\gamma$. If $g$ in $G$ is not in the center its [conjugacy class](wikipedia://en/49176) is exactly $g$ and $\varepsilon g$. Thus there are $2^{N -\,1} + 1$ conjugacy classes for $N$ odd and $2^{N -\,1} + 2$ for $N$ even. $G$ has $|G / [G,\,G]\,| = 2^{N -\,1}$ 1-dimensional complex representations. The total number of irreducible complex representations is the number of conjugacy classes. So since $N$ is even, there are two further irreducible complex representations. Since the sum of the squares of the dimensions equals $|G|$ and the dimensions divide $|G|$, the two irreducibles must have dimension $2^{(N - 2)/2}$. When $N$ is even, there are two and their dimension must divide the order of the group, so is a power of two, so they must both have dimension $2^{(N - 2)/2}$. The space on which the $V_{i}$'s act can be complexified. It will have complex dimension $N$. It breaks up into some of complex irreducible representations of $G$, all having dimension $2^{(N - 2)/2}$. In particular this dimension is $\le N$, so $N$ is less than or equal to 8. If $N = 6$, the dimension is 4, which does not divide 6. So *N* can only be 1, 2, 4 or 8.

## Applications to Jordan algebras {#Applications_to_Jordan_algebras}

Let $A$ be a Euclidean Hurwitz algebra and let $M_{n}(A)$ be the algebra of $n$-by-$n$ matrices over $A$. It is a unital nonassociative algebra with an involution given by

$$\displaystyle{(x_{ij})^*=(x_{ji}^*).}$$

The trace $\operatorname{Tr}(X)$ is defined as the sum of the diagonal elements of $X$ and the real-valued trace by $\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbf{R}}(X) = \operatorname{Re}\,\operatorname{Tr}(X)$. The real-valued trace satisfies:

$$\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbf{R}} XY = \operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbf{R}} YX, \qquad \operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbf{R}} (XY)Z = \operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbf{R}} X(YZ).$$

These are immediate consequences of the known identities for $n = 1$.

In $A$ define the *[associator](wikipedia://en/943321)* by

$$\displaystyle{[a,b,c]=a(bc) - (ab)c.}$$

It is trilinear and vanishes identically if $A$ is associative. Since $A$ is an [alternative algebra](wikipedia://en/3160) $[a,\,a,\,b] = 0$ and $[b,\,a,\,a] = 0$. Polarizing it follows that the associator is antisymmetric in its three entries. Furthermore, if $a$, $b$ or $c$ lie in $\mathbf{R}$ then $[a,\,b,\,c] = 0$. These facts imply that $M_{3}(A)$ has certain commutation properties. In fact if $X$ is a matrix in $M_{3}(A)$ with real entries on the diagonal then

$$\displaystyle{[X,X^2] = aI,}$$

with $a$ in $A$. In fact if $Y = [X,\,\,X^{\,2}]$, then

$$\displaystyle{y_{ij} = \sum_{k,\ell} [x_{ik},x_{k\ell},x_{\ell j}].}$$

Since the diagonal entries of $X$ are real, the off-diagonal entries of $Y$ vanish. Each diagonal entry of $Y$ is a sum of two associators involving only off diagonal terms of $X$. Since the associators are invariant under [cyclic permutations](wikipedia://en/449166), the diagonal entries of $Y$ are all equal.

Let $H_{n}(A)$ be the space of self-adjoint elements in $M_{n}(A)$ with product $X\circ Y = \tfrac{1}{2}(X\,Y + Y\,X)$ and inner product $(X,\,Y) = \operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbf{R}}(X\,Y)$.

**Theorem.** $H_{n}(A)$ is a [Euclidean Jordan algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_Jordan_algebra) if $A$ is associative (the real numbers, complex numbers or quaternions) and $n \ge 3$ or if $A$ is nonassociative (the octonions) and $n = 3$.

The [exceptional](wikipedia://en/5280515) Jordan algebra $H_{3}(\mathbf{O})$ is called the **[Albert algebra](wikipedia://en/9153180)** after [A.A. Albert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Adrian_Albert).

To check that $H_{n}(A)$ satisfies the axioms for a Euclidean Jordan algebra, the real trace defines a [symmetric bilinear form](wikipedia://en/1803375) with $(X,\,X) = \Sigma\,\|x_{ij}\|^{2}$. So it is an inner product. It satisfies the associativity property $(Z\circ X,\,Y) = (X,\,Z\circ Y)$ because of the properties of the real trace. The main axiom to check is the Jordan condition for the operators $L(X)$ defined by $L(X)Y = X\circ Y$:

$$\displaystyle{[L(X),L(X^2)]=0.}$$

This is easy to check when $A$ is associative, since $M_{n}(A)$ is an associative algebra so a Jordan algebra with $X\circ Y = \tfrac{1}{2}(X\,Y + Y\,X)$. When $A = \mathbf{O}$ and $n = 3$ a special argument is required, one of the shortest being due to (Freudenthal 1951).[^o24]

In fact if $T$ is in $H_{3}(\mathbf{O})$ with $\operatorname{Tr}\,T = 0$, then

$$\displaystyle{D(X) = TX -XT}$$

defines a skew-adjoint derivation of $H_{3}(\mathbf{O})$. Indeed,

$$\operatorname{Tr}(T(X(X^2)) -T(X^2(X))) = \operatorname{Tr} T(aI) = \operatorname{Tr}(T)a=0,$$

so that

$$(D(X),X^2) = 0.$$

Polarizing yields:

$$(D(X),Y\circ Z) + (D(Y),Z\circ X) + (D(Z),X\circ Y) = 0.$$

Setting $Z = 1$ shows that $D$ is skew-adjoint. The derivation property $D(X\,\circ Y) = D(X)\circ Y + X\circ D(Y)$ follows by this and the associativity property of the inner product in the identity above.

With $A$ and $n$ as in the statement of the theorem, let $K$ be the group of [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of $E = H_{n}(A)$ leaving invariant the inner product. It is a closed subgroup of $O(E)$ so a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945). Its [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) consists of skew-adjoint derivations. (Freudenthal 1951) showed that given $X$ in $E$ there is an automorphism $k$ in $K$ such that $k(X)$ is a [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080). (By self-adjointness the diagonal entries will be real.) Freudenthal's diagonalization theorem immediately implies the Jordan condition, since Jordan products by real diagonal matrices commute on $M_{n}(A)$ for any non-associative algebra $A$.

To prove the diagonalization theorem, take $X$ in $E$. By compactness $k$ can be chosen in $K$ minimizing the sums of the squares of the norms of the off-diagonal terms of $k(X)$. Since $K$ preserves the sums of all the squares, this is equivalent to maximizing the sums of the squares of the norms of the diagonal terms of $k(X)$. Replacing $X$ by $k\,X$, it can be assumed that the maximum is attained at $X$. Since the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_{n}$, acting by permuting the coordinates, lies in $K$, if $X$ is not diagonal, it can be supposed that $x_{12}$ and its adjoint $x_{21}$ are non-zero. Let $T$ be the skew-adjoint matrix with $(2,\,1)$ entry $a$, $(1,\,2)$ entry $-a*$ and 0 elsewhere and let $D$ be the derivation ad $T$ of $E$. Let $k_{t} = \exp\,tD$ in $K$. Then only the first two diagonal entries in $X(t) = k_{t}X$ differ from those of $X$. The diagonal entries are real. The derivative of $x_{11}(t)$ at $t = 0$ is the $(1,\,1)$ coordinate of $[T,\,X]$, i.e. $a*\,x_{21} + x_{12}\,a = 2(x_{21},\,a)$. This derivative is non-zero if $a = x_{21}$. On the other hand, the group $k_{t}$ preserves the real-valued trace. Since it can only change $x_{11}$ and $x_{22}$, it preserves their sum. However, on the line $x + y =$ constant, $x^{2} + y^{2}$ has no local maximum (only a global minimum), a contradiction. Hence $X$ must be diagonal.
