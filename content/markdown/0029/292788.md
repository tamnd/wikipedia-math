# Unitary representation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unitary representation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **unitary representation** of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G* is a [linear representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_representation) π of *G* on a complex [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) *V* such that π(*g*) is a [unitary operator](wikipedia://en/225999) for every *g* ∈ *G*. The general theory is well-developed in the case that *G* is a [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) ([Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637)) [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) and the representations are [strongly continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strongly_continuous).

:::

The theory has been widely applied in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) since the 1920s, particularly influenced by [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544)'s 1928 book *[Gruppentheorie und Quantenmechanik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gruppentheorie_und_Quantenmechanik)*. One of the pioneers in constructing a general theory of unitary representations, for any group *G* rather than just for particular groups useful in applications, was [George Mackey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Mackey).

## Context in harmonic analysis {#Context_in_harmonic_analysis}

The theory of unitary representations of topological groups is closely connected with [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147). In the case of an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) *G*, a fairly complete picture of the representation theory of *G* is given by [Pontryagin duality](wikipedia://en/366136). In general, the unitary equivalence classes (see below) of [irreducible](wikipedia://en/385982) unitary representations of *G* make up its **unitary dual**. This set can be identified with the [spectrum of the C\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/918414) associated with *G* by the [group C\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/349223) construction. This is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450).

The general form of the [Plancherel theorem](wikipedia://en/454315) tries to describe the [regular representation](wikipedia://en/360011) of *G* on *L*<sup>2</sup>(*G*) using a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) on the unitary dual. For *G* abelian this is given by the Pontryagin duality theory. For *G* [compact](wikipedia://en/750326), this is done by the [Peter–Weyl theorem](wikipedia://en/294474); in that case, the unitary dual is a [discrete space](wikipedia://en/56061), and the measure attaches an atom to each point of mass equal to its degree.

## Formal definitions {#Formal_definitions}

Let *G* be a topological group. A **strongly continuous unitary representation** of *G* on a Hilbert space *H* is a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) from *G* into the unitary group of *H*,

$$\pi: G \rightarrow \operatorname{U}(H)$$

such that *g* → π(*g*) ξ is a norm continuous function for every ξ ∈ *H*.

Note that if G is a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945), the Hilbert space also admits underlying smooth and analytic structures. A vector ξ in *H* is said to be **smooth** or **analytic** if the map *g* → π(*g*) ξ is smooth or analytic (in the norm or weak topologies on *H*).[^o5] Smooth vectors are dense in *H* by a classical argument of [Lars Gårding](wikipedia://en/8000370), since convolution by smooth functions of [compact support](wikipedia://en/381013#compact_support) yields smooth vectors. Analytic vectors are dense by a classical argument of [Edward Nelson](wikipedia://en/387561), amplified by Roe Goodman, since vectors in the image of a heat operator *e*<sup>–tD</sup>, corresponding to an [elliptic differential operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_differential_operator) *D* in the [universal enveloping algebra](wikipedia://en/363551) of *G*, are analytic. Not only do smooth or analytic vectors form dense subspaces; but they also form common cores for the unbounded skew-adjoint operators corresponding to the elements of the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944), in the sense of [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713).[^o6]

Two unitary representations π<sub>1</sub>: *G* → U(*H*<sub>1</sub>), π<sub>2</sub>: *G* → U(*H*<sub>2</sub>) are said to be **unitarily equivalent** if there is a [unitary transformation](wikipedia://en/236007) *A*:*H*<sub>1</sub> → *H*<sub>2</sub> such that π<sub>1</sub>(*g*) = *A*<sup>\*</sup> ∘ π<sub>2</sub>(*g*) ∘ *A* for all *g* in *G*. When this holds, *A* is said to be an [intertwining operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intertwining_operator) for the representations $(\pi_1,H_1),(\pi_2,H_2)$.[^o7]

If $\pi$ is a representation of a connected Lie group $G$ on a *finite-dimensional* Hilbert space $H$, then $\pi$ is unitary if and only if the associated Lie algebra representation $d\pi:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathrm{End}(H)$ maps into the space of skew-self-adjoint operators on $H$.[^o8]

:::

## Complete reducibility {#Complete_reducibility}

A unitary representation is [completely reducible](wikipedia://en/55823265), in the sense that for any closed [invariant subspace](wikipedia://en/585826), the [orthogonal complement](wikipedia://en/1058833) is again a closed invariant subspace. This is at the level of an observation, but is a fundamental property. For example, it implies that finite-dimensional unitary representations are always a direct sum of irreducible representations, in the algebraic sense.

Since unitary representations are much easier to handle than the general case, it is natural to consider **unitarizable representations**, those that become unitary on the introduction of a suitable complex Hilbert space structure. This works very well for [finite groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representations_of_a_finite_group), and more generally for [compact groups](wikipedia://en/750326), by an averaging argument applied to an arbitrary hermitian structure (more specifically, a new inner product defined by an averaging argument over the old one, w.r.t which the representation is unitary).[^o10] For example, a natural proof of [Maschke's theorem](wikipedia://en/30876007) is by this route.

## Unitarizability and the unitary dual question {#Unitarizability_and_the_unitary_dual_question}

In general, for non-compact groups, it is a more serious question which representations are unitarizable. One of the important unsolved problems in mathematics is the description of the **unitary dual**, the effective classification of irreducible unitary representations of all real [reductive](wikipedia://en/1088386) [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945). All [irreducible](wikipedia://en/385982) unitary representations are [admissible](wikipedia://en/11055227) (or rather their [Harish-Chandra modules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harish-Chandra_module) are), and the admissible representations are given by the [Langlands classification](wikipedia://en/10985992), and it is easy to tell which of them have a non-trivial invariant [sesquilinear form](wikipedia://en/860138). The problem is that it is in general hard to tell when the quadratic form is [positive definite](wikipedia://en/20437320). For many reductive Lie groups this has been solved; see [representation theory of SL2(R)](wikipedia://en/11103925) and [representation theory of the Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/2249718) for examples.
