# Composition series

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Composition series"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **composition series** provides a way to break up an [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364), such as a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) or a [module](wikipedia://en/276410), into simple pieces. The need for considering composition series in the context of modules arises from the fact that many naturally occurring modules are not [semisimple](wikipedia://en/1001361), hence cannot be decomposed into a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) of [simple modules](wikipedia://en/29430). A composition series of a module *M* is a finite increasing [filtration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filtration_%28abstract_algebra%29) of *M* by [submodules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule) such that the successive quotients are [simple](wikipedia://en/3875683) and serves as a replacement of the direct sum decomposition of *M* into its simple constituents.

:::

A composition series may not exist, and when it does, it need not be unique. Nevertheless, a group of results known under the general name **Jordan–Hölder theorem** asserts that whenever composition series exist, the *[isomorphism classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_class)* of simple pieces (although, perhaps, not their *location* in the composition series in question) and their multiplicities are uniquely determined. Composition series may thus be used to define invariants of [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707) and [Artinian modules](wikipedia://en/542336).

A related but distinct concept is a [chief series](wikipedia://en/12505519): a composition series is a maximal [*subnormal* series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subnormal_series), while a chief series is a maximal *[normal series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_series)*.

## For groups {#For_groups}

If a group *G* has a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) *N*, then the factor group *G*/*N* may be formed, and some aspects of the study of the structure of *G* may be broken down by studying the "smaller" groups *G/N* and *N*. If *G* has no normal subgroup that is different from *G* and from the trivial group, then *G* is a [simple group](wikipedia://en/47600). Otherwise, the question naturally arises as to whether *G* can be reduced to simple "pieces", and if so, whether there are any unique features of the way this can be done.

More formally, a **composition series** of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G* is a [subnormal series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subnormal_series) of finite length

$$1 = H_0\triangleleft H_1\triangleleft \cdots \triangleleft H_n = G,$$

with strict inclusions, such that each *H*<sub>*i*</sub> is a [maximal](wikipedia://en/1472649) proper normal subgroup of *H*<sub>*i*+1</sub>. Equivalently, a composition series is a subnormal series such that each factor group *H*<sub>*i*+1</sub> / *H*<sub>*i*</sub> is [simple](wikipedia://en/47600). The factor groups are called **composition factors**.

A subnormal series is a composition series [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is of maximal length. That is, there are no additional subgroups which can be "inserted" into a composition series. The length *n* of the series is called the **composition length**.

If a composition series exists for a group *G*, then any subnormal series of *G* can be *refined* to a composition series, informally, by inserting subgroups into the series up to maximality. Every [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) has a composition series, but not every [infinite group](wikipedia://en/4587030) has one. For example, $\mathbb{Z}$ has no composition series.

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Uniqueness: Jordan–Hölder theorem"}

### Uniqueness: Jordan–Hölder theorem {#Uniqueness:_Jordan–Hölder_theorem}

A group may have more than one composition series. However, the **Jordan–Hölder theorem** (named after [Camille Jordan](wikipedia://en/355931) and [Otto Hölder](wikipedia://en/191652)) states that any two composition series of a given group are equivalent. That is, they have the same composition length and the same composition factors, [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) and [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828). This theorem can be proved using the [Schreier refinement theorem](wikipedia://en/947167). The Jordan–Hölder theorem is also true for [transfinite](wikipedia://en/30461) *ascending* composition series, but not transfinite *descending* composition series (Birkhoff 1934). (Baumslag 2006) gives a short proof of the Jordan–Hölder theorem by intersecting the terms in one subnormal series with those in the other series.

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

#### Example {#Example}

For a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order *n*, composition series correspond to ordered prime factorizations of *n*, and in fact yields a proof of the [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556).

For example, the cyclic group $C_{12}$ has $C_1\triangleleft C_2\triangleleft C_6 \triangleleft C_{12}, \ \, C_1\triangleleft C_2\triangleleft C_4\triangleleft C_{12},$ and $C_1\triangleleft C_3\triangleleft C_6 \triangleleft C_{12}$ as three different composition series. The sequences of composition factors obtained in the respective cases are $C_2,C_3,C_2, \ \, C_2,C_2,C_3,$ and $C_3,C_2,C_2.$

:::

## For modules {#For_modules}

The definition of composition series for modules restricts all attention to submodules, ignoring all additive subgroups that are *not* submodules. Given a ring *R* and an *R*-module *M*, a composition series for *M* is a series of submodules

$$\{0\} = J_0 \subset \cdots \subset J_n = M$$

where all inclusions are strict and *J*<sub>*k*</sub> is a maximal submodule of *J*<sub>*k*+1</sub> for each *k*. As for groups, if *M* has a composition series at all, then any finite strictly increasing series of submodules of *M* may be refined to a composition series, and any two composition series for *M* are equivalent. In that case, the (simple) quotient modules *J*<sub>*k*+1</sub>/*J*<sub>*k*</sub> are known as the **composition factors** of *M,* and the Jordan–Hölder theorem holds, ensuring that the number of occurrences of each isomorphism type of simple *R*-module as a composition factor does not depend on the choice of composition series.

It is well known[^n1] that a module has a finite composition series if and only if it is both an [Artinian module](wikipedia://en/542336) and a [Noetherian module](wikipedia://en/542431). If *R* is an [Artinian ring](wikipedia://en/489273), then every finitely generated *R*-module is Artinian and Noetherian, and thus has a finite composition series. In particular, for any field *K*, any finite-dimensional module for a finite-dimensional algebra over *K* has a composition series, unique up to equivalence.

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

[Groups with a set of operators](wikipedia://en/4276393) generalize group actions and ring actions on a group. A unified approach to both groups and modules can be followed as in (Bourbaki 1974, Ch. 1) or (Isaacs 1994, Ch. 10), simplifying some of the exposition. The group *G* is viewed as being acted upon by elements (operators) from a set Ω. Attention is restricted entirely to subgroups invariant under the action of elements from Ω, called Ω-subgroups. Thus Ω-composition series must use only Ω-subgroups, and Ω-composition factors need only be Ω-simple. The standard results above, such as the Jordan–Hölder theorem, are established with nearly identical proofs.

The special cases recovered include when Ω = *G* so that *G* is acting on itself. An important example of this is when elements of *G* act by conjugation, so that the set of operators consists of the [inner automorphisms](wikipedia://en/61839). A composition series under this action is exactly a [chief series](wikipedia://en/12505519). Module structures are a case of Ω-actions where Ω is a ring and some additional axioms are satisfied.

:::

## For objects in an abelian category {#For_objects_in_an_abelian_category}

A **composition series** of an [object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) *A* in an [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063) is a sequence of subobjects

$$A=X_0\supsetneq X_1\supsetneq \dots \supsetneq X_n=0$$

such that each [quotient object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_object) *X<sub>i</sub>* /*X*<sub>*i* + 1</sub> is [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_object) (for 0 ≤ *i* \< *n*). If *A* has a composition series, the [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *n* only depends on *A* and is called the [length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Length_of_an_object) of *A*.[^o16]
