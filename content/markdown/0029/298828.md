# Affine group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Affine group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **affine group** or **general affine group** of any [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) is the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of all invertible [affine transformations](wikipedia://en/38449) from the space into itself. In the case of a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) (where the associated field of scalars is the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438)), the affine group consists of those functions from the space to itself such that the image of every line is a line.

:::

Over any field, the affine group may be viewed as a matrix group in a natural way. If the associated field of scalars is the real or complex field, then the affine group is a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945).

## Relation to general linear group {#Relation_to_general_linear_group}

### Construction from general linear group {#Construction_from_general_linear_group}

Concretely, given a vector space $V$, it has an underlying [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) $A$ obtained by "forgetting" the origin, with $V$ acting by translations, and the affine group of $A$ can be described concretely as the [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) of $V$ by $\operatorname{GL}(V)$, the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) of $V$:

$$\operatorname{Aff}(V) = V \rtimes \operatorname{GL}(V)$$

The action of $\operatorname{GL}(V)$ on $V$ is the natural one (linear transformations are automorphisms), so this defines a [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246).

In terms of matrices, one writes:

$$\operatorname{Aff}(n,K) = K^n \rtimes \operatorname{GL}(n,K)$$

where here the natural action of $\operatorname{GL}(n, K)$ on $K^{n}$ is [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) of a vector.

### Stabilizer of a point {#Stabilizer_of_a_point}

Given the affine group of an affine space $A$, the [stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Orbits_and_stabilizers) of a point $p$ is isomorphic to the general linear group of the same dimension (so the stabilizer of a point in $\operatorname{Aff}(2, \mathbf{R})$ is isomorphic to $\operatorname{GL}(2, \mathbf{R})$); formally, it is the general linear group of the vector space $(A, p)$: recall that if one fixes a point, an affine space becomes a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370).

All these subgroups are conjugate, where conjugation is given by translation from $p$ to $q$ (which is uniquely defined), however, no particular subgroup is a natural choice, since no point is special – this corresponds to the multiple choices of transverse subgroup, or [splitting](wikipedia://en/1531404) of the [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence)

$$1 \to V \to V \rtimes \operatorname{GL}(V) \to \operatorname{GL}(V) \to 1\,.$$

In the case that the affine group was constructed by *starting* with a vector space, the subgroup that stabilizes the origin (of the vector space) is the original $\operatorname{GL}(V)$.

## Matrix representation {#Matrix_representation}

Representing the affine group as a semidirect product of $V$ by $\operatorname{GL}(V)$, then [by construction of the semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246#Semidirect_products_and_group_homomorphisms), the elements are pairs $(v, M)$, where $v$ is a vector in $V$ and $M$ is a linear transform in $\operatorname{GL}(V)$, and multiplication is given by

$$(v, M) \cdot (w, N) = (v+Mw, MN)\,.$$

This can be represented as the $(n + 1) \times (n + 1)$ [block matrix](wikipedia://en/457464)

$$\left( \begin{array}{c|c} M & v\\ \hline 0 & 1 \end{array}\right)$$

where $M$ is an $n \times n$ matrix over $K$, $v$ an $n \times 1$ column vector, 0 is a $1 \times n$ row of zeros, and 1 is the 1 × 1 identity block matrix.

Formally, $\operatorname{Aff}(V)$ is naturally isomorphic to a subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}(V \oplus K)$, with $V$ embedded as the affine plane $\{(v, 1) | v \in V\}$, namely the stabilizer of this affine plane; the above matrix formulation is the (transpose of the) realization of this, with the $n \times n$ and $1 \times 1$ blocks corresponding to the direct sum decomposition $V \oplus K$.

A [similar](wikipedia://en/341149) representation is any $(n + 1) \times (n + 1)$ matrix in which the entries in each column sum to 1.[^o6] The [similarity](wikipedia://en/341149) $P$ for passing from the above kind to this kind is the $(n + 1) \times (n + 1)$ [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) with the bottom row replaced by a row of all ones.

Each of these two classes of matrices is closed under matrix multiplication.

The simplest paradigm may well be the case $n = 1$, that is, the upper triangular 2 × 2 matrices representing the affine group in one dimension. It is a two-parameter [non-Abelian](wikipedia://en/4791400) [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945), so with merely two generators (Lie algebra elements), $A$ and $B$, such that $[A, B] = B$, where

$$A= \left( \begin{array}{cc} 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 \end{array}\right), \qquad B= \left( \begin{array}{cc} 0 & 1\\ 0 & 0 \end{array}\right)\,,$$

so that

$$e^{aA+bB}= \left( \begin{array}{cc} e^a & \tfrac{b}{a}(e^a-1)\\ 0 & 1 \end{array}\right)\,.$$

## Character table of $\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbf{F}_{p})$ {#Character_table_of}

$\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbf{F}_{p})$ has order $p(p - 1)$. Since

$$\begin{pmatrix} c & d \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} c & d \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix} a & (1-a)d+bc \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\,,$$

we know $\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbf{F}_{p})$ has $p$ [conjugacy classes](wikipedia://en/49176), namely

$$\begin{aligned} C_{id} &= \left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\,, \\[6pt] C_{1} &= \left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & b \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\Bigg|b\in \mathbf{F}_p^*\right\}\,, \\[6pt] \Bigg\{C_{a} &= \left\{\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\Bigg| b\in \mathbf{F}_p\right\}\Bigg|a\in \mathbf{F}_p\setminus\{0,1\}\Bigg\}\,. \end{aligned}$$

Then we know that $\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbf{F}_{p})$ has $p$ [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982). By above paragraph (#Matrix representation), there exist $p - 1$ one-dimensional representations, decided by the homomorphism

$$\rho_k:\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbf{F}_p)\to\mathbb{C}^*$$

for $k = 1, 2,\ldots p - 1$, where

$$\rho_k\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}=\exp\left(\frac{2i kj\pi}{p-1}\right)$$

and $i^{2} = -1$, $a = gj$, $g$ is a generator of the group $\mathbf{F}^{\ast}_{p}$. Then compare with the order of $\mathbf{F}_{p}$, we have

$$p(p-1)=p-1+\chi_p^2\,,$$

hence $\chi_{p} = p - 1$ is the dimension of the last irreducible representation. Finally using the orthogonality of irreducible representations, we can complete the [character table](wikipedia://en/911520) of $\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbf{F}_{p})$:

$$\begin{array}{c|cccccc} & {\color{Blue}C_{id}} & {\color{Blue}C_1} & {\color{Blue}C_g} & {\color{Blue}C_{g^2}} & {\color{Gray}\dots} & {\color{Blue}C_{g^{p-2}}} \\ \hline {\color{Blue}\chi_1} & {\color{Gray}1} & {\color{Gray}1} & {\color{Blue}e^{\frac{2\pi i}{p-1}}} & {\color{Blue}e^{\frac{4\pi i}{p-1}}} & {\color{Gray}\dots} & {\color{Blue}e^{\frac{2\pi (p-2)i}{p-1}}} \\ {\color{Blue}\chi_2} & {\color{Gray}1} & {\color{Gray}1} & {\color{Blue}e^{\frac{4\pi i}{p-1}}} & {\color{Blue}e^{\frac{8\pi i}{p-1}}} & {\color{Gray}\dots} & {\color{Blue}e^{\frac{4\pi (p-2)i}{p-1}}} \\ {\color{Blue}\chi_3} & {\color{Gray}1} & {\color{Gray}1} & {\color{Blue}e^{\frac{6\pi i}{p-1}}} & {\color{Blue}e^{\frac{12\pi i}{p-1}}} & {\color{Gray}\dots} & {\color{Blue}e^{\frac{6\pi (p-2)i}{p-1}}} \\ {\color{Gray}\dots} & {\color{Gray}\dots} & {\color{Gray}\dots} & {\color{Gray}\dots} & {\color{Gray}\dots} & {\color{Gray}\dots} & {\color{Gray}\dots} \\ {\color{Blue}\chi_{p-1}} & {\color{Gray}1} & {\color{Gray}1} & {\color{Gray}1} & {\color{Gray}1} & {\color{Gray}\dots} & {\color{Gray}1} \\ {\color{Blue}\chi_{p}} & {\color{Gray}p-1} & {\color{Gray}-1} & {\color{Gray}0} & {\color{Gray}0} & {\color{Gray}\dots} & {\color{Gray}0} \end{array}$$

## Planar affine group over the reals {#Planar_affine_group_over_the_reals}

The elements of $\operatorname{Aff}(2,\mathbb R)$ can take a simple form on a well-chosen [affine coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_coordinate_system). More precisely, given an affine transformation of an [affine plane](wikipedia://en/30764139) over the [reals](wikipedia://en/20646438), an affine coordinate system exists on which it has one of the following forms, where $a$, $b$, and $t$ are real numbers (the given conditions insure that transformations are invertible, but not for making the classes distinct; for example, the identity belongs to all the classes).

$$\begin{aligned} \text{1.}&& (x, y) &\mapsto (x +a,y+b),\\[3pt] \text{2.}&& (x, y) &\mapsto (ax,by), &\qquad \text{where } ab\ne 0,\\[3pt] \text{3.}&& (x, y) &\mapsto (ax,y+b), &\qquad \text{where } a\ne 0,\\[3pt] \text{4.}&& (x, y) &\mapsto (ax+y,ay), &\qquad \text{where } a\ne 0,\\[3pt] \text{5.}&& (x, y) &\mapsto (x+y,y+a)\\[3pt] \text{6.}&& (x, y) &\mapsto (a(x\cos t + y\sin t), a(-x\sin t+y\cos t)), &\qquad \text{where } a\ne 0. \end{aligned}$$

Case 1 corresponds to [translations](wikipedia://en/204682).

Case 2 corresponds to [scalings](wikipedia://en/651752) that may differ in two different directions. When working with a [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) these directions need not be [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944), since the [coordinate axes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_axes) need not be perpendicular.

Case 3 corresponds to a scaling in one direction and a translation in another one.

Case 4 corresponds to a [shear mapping](wikipedia://en/799405) combined with a dilation.

Case 5 corresponds to a [shear mapping](wikipedia://en/799405) combined with a dilation.

Case 6 corresponds to [similarities](wikipedia://en/39377) when the coordinate axes are perpendicular.

The affine transformations without any [fixed point](wikipedia://en/449738) belong to cases 1, 3, and 5. The transformations that do not preserve the orientation of the plane belong to cases 2 (with $ab < 0$) or 3 (with $a < 0$).

The proof may be done by first remarking that if an affine transformation has no fixed point, then the matrix of the associated linear map has an [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) equal to one, and then using the [Jordan normal form theorem for real matrices](wikipedia://en/312877#Real_matrices).

## Other affine groups and subgroups {#Other_affine_groups_and_subgroups}

### General case {#General_case}

Given any subgroup $G < \operatorname{GL}(V)$ of the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564), one can produce an affine group, sometimes denoted $\operatorname{Aff}(G)$, analogously as $\operatorname{Aff}(G) := V \rtimes G$.

More generally and abstractly, given any group $G$ and a [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) $\rho : G \to \operatorname{GL}(V)$ of $G$ on a vector space $V$, one gets[^o11] an associated affine group $V \rtimes_{\rho} G$: one can say that the affine group obtained is "a [group extension](wikipedia://en/345807) by a vector representation", and, as above, one has the short exact sequence

$$1 \to V \to V \rtimes_\rho G \to G \to 1.$$

::: {#o13 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Special affine group"}

### Special affine group {#Special_affine_group}

The subset of all invertible affine transformations that preserve a fixed [volume form](wikipedia://en/1855811) up to sign is called the *special affine group*. (The transformations themselves are sometimes called *equiaffinities*.) This group is the affine analogue of the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823). In terms of the semi-direct product, the special affine group consists of all pairs $(M, v)$ with $|\det(M)| = 1$, that is, the affine transformations

$$x \mapsto Mx + v$$

where $M$ is a linear transformation of whose determinant has absolute value 1 and $v$ is any fixed translation vector.[^o14][^o15]

The subgroup of the special affine group consisting of those transformations whose linear part has determinant 1 is the group of orientation- and volume-preserving maps. Algebraically, this group is a semidirect product $SL(V) \ltimes V$ of the special linear group of $V$ with the translations. It is generated by the [shear mappings](wikipedia://en/799405).

:::

### Projective subgroup {#Projective_subgroup}

Presuming knowledge of [projectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectivity) and the projective group of [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849), the affine group can be easily specified. For example, Günter Ewald wrote:[^o17]

The set $\mathfrak{P}$ of all projective collineations of $Pn$ is a group which we may call the **projective group** of $Pn$. If we proceed from $Pn$ to the affine space $An$ by declaring a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) $\omega$ to be a [hyperplane at infinity](wikipedia://en/713946), we obtain the **affine group** $\mathfrak{A}$ of $An$ as the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of $\mathfrak{P}$ consisting of all elements of $\mathfrak{P}$ that leave $\omega$ fixed.

$$\mathfrak{A} \subset \mathfrak{P}$$

### Isometries of Euclidean space {#Isometries_of_Euclidean_space}

When the affine space $A$ is a Euclidean space (over the field of real numbers), the group $\mathcal{E}$ of distance-preserving maps (*[isometries](wikipedia://en/254777)*) of $A$ is a subgroup of the affine group. Algebraically, this group is a semidirect product $O(V) \ltimes V$ of the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) of $V$ with the translations. Geometrically, it is the subgroup of the affine group generated by the orthogonal reflections.

### Poincaré group {#Poincaré_group}

The [Poincaré group](wikipedia://en/230487) is the affine group of the [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489) $O(1,3)$:

$$\mathbf{R}^{1,3}\rtimes \operatorname{O}(1,3)$$

This example is very important in [relativity](wikipedia://en/30001).
