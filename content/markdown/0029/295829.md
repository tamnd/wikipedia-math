# Reflection (mathematics)

*Image: SimmetriainvOK.svg.* A reflection of a [chiral](wikipedia://en/32703814) shape through an axis (a diagonal red line here)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Reflection"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **reflection** (also spelled **reflexion**)[^o2] is a [mapping](wikipedia://en/185427) from a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) to itself that is an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) with a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) as the set of [fixed points](wikipedia://en/449738); this set is called the [axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_of_symmetry) (in dimension 2) or [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852) (in dimension 3) of reflection. The image of a figure by a reflection is its [mirror image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror_image) in the axis or plane of reflection.

:::

For example, the mirror image of the small Latin letter **p** for a reflection with respect to a [vertical axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_axis) (a *vertical reflection*) would look like **q**. Its image by reflection in a [horizontal axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_axis) (a *horizontal reflection*) would look like **b**. A reflection is an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450): when applied twice in succession, every point returns to its original location, and every geometrical object is restored to its original state.

The term *reflection* is sometimes used for a larger class of mappings from a Euclidean space to itself, namely the non-identity isometries that are involutions. The set of fixed points (the "mirror") of such an isometry is an [affine subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_subspace), but is possibly smaller than a hyperplane.

For instance, a [reflection through a point](wikipedia://en/12487536) is an involutive isometry with just one fixed point; the image of the letter **p** under it would look like a **d**. This operation is also known as a [central inversion](wikipedia://en/12487536) (Coxeter 1969, §7.2), and exhibits Euclidean space as a [symmetric space](wikipedia://en/1642877).

In a [Euclidean vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_vector_space), the reflection in the point situated at the origin is the same as vector negation. Other examples include reflections in a line in three-dimensional space. Typically, however, unqualified use of the term "reflection" means reflection in a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862). Some mathematicians use "**flip**" as a synonym for "reflection".[^o4][^o5][^o6]

## Construction {#Construction}

*Image: Perpendicular-construction.svg.* Point $Q$ is the reflection of point $P$ through the line $AB$.

In a plane (or, respectively, 3-dimensional) geometry, to find the reflection of a point drop a [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) from the point to the line (plane) used for reflection, and extend it the same distance on the other side. To find the reflection of a figure, reflect each point in the figure.

To reflect point $P$ through the line $\operatorname{AB}$ using [compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge), proceed as follows (see figure):

- Step 1 (red): construct a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) with center at $P$ and some fixed radius $r$ to create points $A'$ and $B'$ on the line $\operatorname{AB}$, which will be [equidistant](wikipedia://en/1532579) from $P$.
- Step 2 (green): construct circles centered at $A'$ and $B'$ having radius $r$. $P$ and $Q$ will be the points of intersection of these two circles.

Point $Q$ is then the reflection of point $P$ through line $\operatorname{AB}$.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

The [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) for a reflection is [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/105620) with [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) −1 and [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) −1, 1, 1, ..., 1. The product of two such matrices is a special orthogonal matrix that represents a rotation. Every [rotation](wikipedia://en/410009) is the result of reflecting in an even number of reflections in hyperplanes through the origin, and every [improper rotation](wikipedia://en/244324) is the result of reflecting in an odd number. Thus reflections generate the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954), and this result is known as the [Cartan–Dieudonné theorem](wikipedia://en/4048180).

Similarly the [Euclidean group](wikipedia://en/652164), which consists of all isometries of Euclidean space, is generated by reflections in affine hyperplanes. In general, a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) generated by reflections in affine hyperplanes is known as a [reflection group](wikipedia://en/2422023). The [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707) generated in this way are examples of [Coxeter groups](wikipedia://en/297004).

:::

## Reflection across a line in the plane {#Reflection_across_a_line_in_the_plane}

Reflection across an arbitrary line through the origin in [two dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_dimensions) can be described by the following formula

$$\operatorname{Ref}_l(v) = 2\frac{v \cdot l}{l \cdot l}l - v,$$

where $v$ denotes the vector being reflected, $l$ denotes any vector in the line across which the reflection is performed, and $v\cdot l$ denotes the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) of $v$ with $l$. Note the formula above can also be written as

$$\operatorname{Ref}_l(v) = 2\operatorname{Proj}_l(v) - v,$$

saying that a reflection of $v$ across $l$ is equal to 2 times the [projection](wikipedia://en/1508507) of $v$ on $l$, minus the vector $v$. Reflections in a line have the eigenvalues of 1, and −1.

## Reflection through a hyperplane in *n* dimensions {#Reflection_through_a_hyperplane_in_n_dimensions}

More generally, if $S$ is a linear subspace and $P_S$ is the orthogonal projection onto $S$, then reflection across $S$ is given by

$$R_S = 2P_S - I.$$

*Image: Reflection across a plane in R3 using a projection matrix.gif.* Reflection across a plane in ℝ³ using an orthogonal projection matrix. Here $P=U(U^TU)^{-1}U^T$ projects onto $\operatorname{col}(U)$, and $R=2P-I$ is the corresponding reflection.

This fixes every vector in $S$ and negates every vector in $S^\perp$. Since

$$P_S + P_{S^\perp} = I,$$

the same reflection may equivalently be written as

$$R_S = I - 2P_{S^\perp}.$$

Thus a reflection can be described either by the subspace that remains fixed or by its orthogonal complement, whose vectors are reversed. In the preceding two-dimensional section, $l$ spans the subspace that remains fixed; in the formula below, $a$ spans its orthogonal complement, whose vectors are reversed.

Given a vector $v$ in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb R^n$, the formula for the reflection in the [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) through the origin, [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) to $a$, is given by

$$\operatorname{Ref}_a(v) = v - 2\frac{v\cdot a}{a\cdot a}a,$$

where $v\cdot a$ denotes the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) of $v$ with $a$. Note that the second term in the above equation is just twice the [vector projection](wikipedia://en/1508507) of $v$ onto $a$. One can easily check that

- $\operatorname{Ref}_{a}(v) = -v$, if $v$ is parallel to $a$, and
- $\operatorname{Ref}_{a}(v) = v$, if $v$ is perpendicular to $a$.

Using the [geometric product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_product), the formula is

$$\operatorname{Ref}_a(v) = -\frac{a v a}{a^2} .$$

Since these reflections are isometries of Euclidean space fixing the origin they may be represented by [orthogonal matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_matrices). The orthogonal matrix corresponding to the above reflection is the [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859)

$$R = I-2\frac{aa^T}{a^Ta},$$

where $I$ denotes the $n \times n$ [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) and $a^T$ is the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) of a. Its entries are

$$R_{ij} = \delta_{ij} - 2\frac{a_i a_j}{ \left\| a \right\| ^2 },$$

where $\delta_{ij}$ is the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890).

The formula for the reflection in the affine hyperplane $v\cdot a=c$ not through the origin is

$$\operatorname{Ref}_{a,c}(v) = v - 2\frac{v \cdot a - c}{a\cdot a}a.$$
