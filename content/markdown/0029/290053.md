# Airfoil

*Image: Examples of Airfoils.svg.* Examples of airfoils in nature, aircraft (ULM = [ultralight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultralight_aviation)/microlight), jet engine, and sailing boat. The dolphin flipper works in the same way in water and is an example of a [hydrofoil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrofoil).

*Image: Flow over aerofoils.webm.* Streamlines past an airfoil made visible with smoke in a [wind tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_tunnel). Video demonstrates air velocity differences, angle of attack, and stall.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Airfoil"}

An **airfoil** ([American English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English)) or **aerofoil** ([British English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English)) is a streamlined body that is capable of generating significantly more [lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lift_%28force%29) than [drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_%28physics%29).[^n1] Wings, sails and propeller blades are examples of airfoils. [Foils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foil_%28fluid_mechanics%29) which work in a similar way submerged in water are called [hydrofoils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrofoil).

:::

When oriented at a suitable angle, a solid body moving through a [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid) deflects the passing fluid, resulting in a force on the airfoil in the direction opposite to the deflection.[^o3][^o4] This force is known as an [aerodynamic force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamic_force) and can be resolved into two [components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_component): lift ([perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to the [freestream velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freestream_velocity)) and drag ([parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) to the freestream velocity).

The lift on an airfoil is primarily the result of its [angle of attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_attack). Most foil shapes require a positive angle of attack to generate lift, but [cambered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camber_%28aerodynamics%29) airfoils can generate lift at zero angle of attack. Airfoils have different shapes for different speeds: those for [subsonic flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsonic_flight) have a rounded [leading edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading_edge), while those for [supersonic flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersonic_aircraft) tend to be slimmer. All have a sharp [trailing edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trailing_edge).[^o5]

The deflection of air by an airfoil generates a lower-pressure region above and behind itself. This pressure difference is accompanied by a velocity difference, as explained by [Bernoulli's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli's_principle), so the resulting flowfield about the airfoil has a higher average velocity on the upper surface than on the lower surface.[^n2] In some situations (e.g., [inviscid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inviscid) [potential flow](wikipedia://en/58320)) the lift force can be related directly to the average top/bottom velocity difference without computing the pressure by using the concept of [circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulation_%28fluid_dynamics%29) and the [Kutta–Joukowski theorem](wikipedia://en/8606325).[^o7]

## Overview {#Overview}

*Image: Streamlines around a NACA 0012.svg.* Streamlines around a NACA 0012 airfoil at moderate angle of attack

*Image: Lift drag graph.JPG.* Lift and drag curves for a typical airfoil

The wings and stabilizers of [fixed-wing aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed-wing_aircraft), as well as [helicopter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helicopter) rotor blades, are built with airfoil-shaped cross sections. Airfoils are also found in propellers, [fans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fan_%28mechanical%29), [compressors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_compressor) and [turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbines). Sails are also airfoils, and the underwater surfaces of sailboats, such as the [centerboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centerboard), [rudder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudder), and [keel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keel), are similar in cross-section and operate on the same principles as airfoils. Swimming and flying creatures and even many plants and [sessile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sessility_%28zoology%29) organisms employ airfoils/hydrofoils, common examples being bird wings, the bodies of fish, and the shape of [sand dollars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sand_dollar). An airfoil-shaped wing can create [downforce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downforce) on an [automobile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automobile) or other motor vehicle, improving [traction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traction_%28engineering%29).

When the wind is obstructed by an object such as a flat plate, a building, or the deck of a bridge, the object will experience [drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_%28physics%29) and also an aerodynamic force perpendicular to the wind. This does not mean the object qualifies as an airfoil. Airfoils are highly-efficient lifting shapes, able to generate more lift than similarly sized flat plates of the same area, and able to generate lift with significantly less drag. Airfoils are used in the design of aircraft, propellers, rotor blades, wind turbines and other applications of aeronautical engineering.

A lift and drag curve obtained in [wind tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_tunnel) testing is shown on the right. The curve represents an airfoil with a positive [camber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camber_%28aerodynamics%29) so some lift is produced at zero angle of attack. With increased angle of attack, lift increases in a roughly linear relation, called the *slope* of the lift curve. At about 18 degrees this airfoil [stalls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stall_%28fluid_dynamics%29), and lift falls off quickly beyond that. The drop in lift can be explained by the action of the upper-surface [boundary layer](wikipedia://en/224301), which separates and greatly thickens over the upper surface at and past the stall angle. The thickened boundary layer's [displacement thickness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_thickness) changes the airfoil's effective shape, in particular it reduces its effective [camber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camber_%28aerodynamics%29), which modifies the overall flow field so as to reduce the [circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulation_%28fluid_dynamics%29) and the lift. The thicker boundary layer also causes a large increase in [pressure drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_drag), so that the overall drag increases sharply near and past the stall point.

Airfoil design is a major facet of [aerodynamics](wikipedia://en/2819). Various airfoils serve different flight regimes. Asymmetric airfoils can generate lift at zero angle of attack, while a symmetric airfoil may better suit frequent inverted flight as in an [aerobatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerobatic) airplane. In the region of the [ailerons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ailerons) and near a [wingtip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wingtip) a symmetric airfoil can be used to increase the range of angles of attack to avoid [spin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_%28flight%29)–[stall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stall_%28flight%29). Thus a large range of angles can be used without [boundary layer separation](wikipedia://en/4871070). Subsonic airfoils have a round leading edge, which is naturally insensitive to the angle of attack. The cross section is not strictly circular, however: the [radius of curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radius_of_curvature_%28mathematics%29) is increased before the wing achieves maximum thickness to minimize the chance of boundary layer separation. This elongates the wing and moves the point of maximum thickness back from the leading edge.

[Supersonic airfoils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersonic_airfoils) are much more angular in shape and can have a very sharp leading edge, which is very sensitive to angle of attack. A [supercritical airfoil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercritical_airfoil) has its maximum thickness close to the leading edge to have a lot of length to slowly shock the supersonic flow back to subsonic speeds. Generally such [transonic](wikipedia://en/247737) airfoils and also the supersonic airfoils have a low camber to reduce [drag divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_divergence_Mach_number). Modern aircraft wings may have different airfoil sections along the wing span, each one optimized for the conditions in each section of the wing.

Movable high-lift devices, [flaps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flap_%28aircraft%29) and sometimes [slats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading_edge_slats), are fitted to airfoils on almost every aircraft. A trailing edge flap acts similarly to an aileron; however, it, as opposed to an aileron, can be retracted partially into the wing if not used.

For many wings, the [boundary layer](wikipedia://en/224301) of the air flow close to the upper surface rapidly becomes turbulent past the maximum thickness point, which increases the skin friction drag. A **laminar flow wing** moves the maximum thickness point well back along the chord from a typical 25% chord position from to 60% from the leading edge or more.[^o9] This maintains smooth laminar flow over a larger percentage of the wing and significantly reduces drag. However, surface contamination will disrupt the boundary layer, making it turbulent. Insects, for example, impacting and sticking onto the wing will cause the loss of wedge shaped regions of laminar flow across the wing's surface. This is a particular problem for aircraft with high take off speeds, since many insects are found near the ground making it unlikely that laminar flow can be sustained into the flight.[^o10] [Gliders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glider_%28sailplane%29) have seen widespread uptake of laminar flow airfoils due to their low speeds and need for low drag aerodynamic structures.[^o9]

Before NASA's research in the 1970s and 1980s laminar flow wing designs were not practical using common manufacturing tolerances and surface imperfection, until new manufacturing methods were developed with machined metal and composite materials (e.g. laminar-flow airfoils developed by Professor [Franz Wortmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Xaver_Wortmann) for use with wings made of [fibre-reinforced plastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibre-reinforced_plastic)). NASA's research in the 1980s revealed the practicality and usefulness of laminar flow wing designs and opened the way for laminar-flow applications on modern practical aircraft surfaces, from subsonic general aviation aircraft to transonic large transport aircraft, to supersonic designs.[^o11]

Schemes have been devised to define airfoils – an example is the [NACA system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NACA_airfoil). Various airfoil generation systems are also used. An example of a general purpose airfoil that finds wide application, and pre–dates the NACA system, is the [Clark-Y](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clark-Y). Today, airfoils can be designed for specific functions by the use of computer programs.

## Airfoil terminology {#Airfoil_terminology}

*Image: Wing profile nomenclature.svg.* Airfoil nomenclature

The various terms related to airfoils are defined below:[^o14]

- The *suction surface* (typically upper surface) is generally associated with higher velocity and lower static pressure.
- The *pressure surface* (typically lower surface) has a comparatively higher static pressure than the suction surface. The pressure gradient between these two surfaces contributes to the lift force generated for a given airfoil.

The geometry of the airfoil is described with a variety of terms :

- The *[leading edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading_edge)* is the point at the front of the airfoil that has maximum curvature (minimum radius).[^n3]
- The *[trailing edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trailing_edge)* is the point on the airfoil most remote from the leading edge. The angle between the upper and lower surfaces at the trailing edge is the *trailing edge angle*.
- The *[chord line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chord_%28aircraft%29)* is the straight line connecting leading and trailing edges. The *chord length*, or simply **chord**, $c$, is the length of the chord line. That is the **reference dimension** of the airfoil section.

*Image: Airfoil thickness definition.svg.* Different definitions of airfoil thickness

*Image: PSU-90-125.PNG.* An airfoil designed for [winglets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wingtip_device) (PSU 90-125WL)

The shape of the airfoil is defined using the following geometrical parameters:

- The *mean camber line* or *mean line* is the locus of points midway between the upper and lower surfaces. Its shape depends on the thickness distribution along the chord;
- The *thickness* of an airfoil varies along the chord. It may be measured in either of two ways:
  - Thickness measured perpendicular to the camber line.[^n4][^n5] This is sometimes described as the "American convention";[^n4]
  - Thickness measured perpendicular to the chord line.[^n6] This is sometimes described as the "British convention".

Some important parameters to describe an airfoil's shape are its *[camber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camber_%28aerodynamics%29)* and its *thickness*. For example, an airfoil of the NACA 4-digit series such as the NACA 2415 (to be read as 2 – 4 – 15) describes an airfoil with a camber of 0.02 chord located at 0.40 chord, with 0.15 chord of maximum thickness.

Finally, important concepts used to describe the airfoil's behaviour when moving through a fluid are:

- The *[aerodynamic center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamic_center)*, which is the chord-wise location about which the pitching moment is independent of the lift coefficient and the angle of attack.
- The *[center of pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_pressure_%28fluid_mechanics%29)*, which is the chord-wise location about which the *[pitching moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitching_moment)* is momentarily zero. On a cambered airfoil, the center of pressure is not a fixed location as it moves in response to changes in angle of attack and lift coefficient.

In two-dimensional flow around a uniform wing of infinite span, the slope of the [lift curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lift_coefficient#Section_lift_coefficient) is determined primarily by the *trailing edge angle*. The slope is greatest if the angle is zero; and decreases as the angle increases.[^o20][^o21] For a wing of finite span, the [aspect ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspect_ratio_%28aeronautics%29) of the wing also significantly influences the slope of the curve. As aspect ratio decreases, the slope also decreases.[^o22]

## Thin airfoil theory {#Thin_airfoil_theory}

*Image: denney.kitfox.g-foxc.arp.jpg.* An airfoil section is displayed at the tip of this [Denney Kitfox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denney_Kitfox) aircraft, built in 1991.

*Image: Helikopter forgószárnyának keresztmetszete 2.jpg.* Airfoil profile of a [Kamov Ka-26](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamov_Ka-26) helicopter's lower rotor blade

**Thin airfoil theory** is a simple theory of airfoils that relates [angle of attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_attack) to lift for incompressible, [inviscid flows](wikipedia://en/5877457). It was devised by German mathematician [Max Munk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Munk) and further refined by British aerodynamicist [Hermann Glauert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Glauert) and others[^n7] in the 1920s. The theory idealizes the flow around an airfoil as two-dimensional flow around a thin airfoil. It can be imagined as addressing an airfoil of zero thickness and infinite [wingspan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wingspan).

Thin airfoil theory was particularly notable in its day because it provided a sound theoretical basis for the following important properties of airfoils in two-dimensional inviscid flow:[^n8][^n9]

1. on a symmetric airfoil, the [center of pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_pressure_%28fluid_mechanics%29) and [aerodynamic center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamic_center) are coincident and lie exactly one quarter of the [chord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chord_%28aircraft%29) behind the leading edge.
1. on a [cambered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camber_%28aerodynamics%29) airfoil, the aerodynamic center lies exactly one quarter of the chord behind the leading edge, but the position of the center of pressure moves when the angle of attack changes.
1. the slope of the *lift coefficient versus angle of attack* line is $2 \pi\!$ units per radian.

As a consequence of (3), the section [lift coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lift_coefficient) of a thin symmetric airfoil of infinite wingspan is:

$$\ c_l = 2\pi \alpha$$

where $c_l\!$ is the section lift coefficient,

$\alpha\!$ is the [angle of attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_attack) in radians, measured relative to the [chord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chord_%28aircraft%29) line.

(The above expression is also applicable to a cambered airfoil where $\alpha\!$ is the angle of attack measured relative to the [zero-lift line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-lift_line) instead of the chord line.)

Also as a consequence of (3), the section lift coefficient of a cambered airfoil of infinite wingspan is:

$$\ c_l = c_{l_0} + 2\pi\alpha$$

where $\ c_{l_0}$ is the section lift coefficient when the angle of attack is zero.

Thin airfoil theory assumes the air is an [inviscid fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inviscid_fluid) so does not account for the [stall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stall_%28flight%29) of the airfoil, which usually occurs at an angle of attack between 10° and 15° for typical airfoils.[^o28] In the mid-late 2000s, however, a theory predicting the onset of leading-edge stall was proposed by Wallace J. Morris II in his doctoral thesis.[^n10] Morris's subsequent refinements contain the details on the current state of theoretical knowledge on the leading-edge stall phenomenon.[^n11][^n12] Morris's theory predicts the critical angle of attack for leading-edge stall onset as the condition at which a global separation zone is predicted in the solution for the inner flow.[^o32] Morris's theory demonstrates that a subsonic flow about a thin airfoil can be described in terms of an outer region, around most of the airfoil chord, and an inner region, around the nose, that asymptotically match each other. As the flow in the outer region is dominated by classical thin airfoil theory, Morris's equations exhibit many components of thin airfoil theory.

### Derivation {#Derivation}

*Image: Aerofoils for different aeroplanes.svg.* From top to bottom:Laminar flow airfoil for a RC [park flyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park_flyer); Laminar flow airfoil for a RC [pylon racer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_racing); Laminar flow airfoil for a crewed propeller aircraft; Laminar flow at a jet airliner airfoil; Stable airfoil used for flying wings; Aft loaded airfoil allowing for a large main spar and late stall; Transonic supercritical airfoil; Supersonic leading edge airfoil black red \#939393 \#2ba3ff

In thin airfoil theory, the width of the (2D) airfoil is assumed negligible, and the airfoil itself replaced with a 1D blade along its camber line, oriented at the [angle of attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_attack) $\alpha$. Let the position along the blade be $x$, ranging from $0$ at the wing's front to $c$ at the trailing edge; the camber of the airfoil, $\tfrac{dy}{dx}$, is assumed sufficiently small that one need not distinguish between $x$ and position relative to the fuselage.[^o34][^n13]

The flow across the airfoil generates a circulation around the blade, which can be modeled as a [vortex sheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vortex_sheet) of position-varying strength $\gamma(x)$. The [Kutta condition](wikipedia://en/4265190) implies that $\gamma(c)=0$, but the strength is singular at the bladefront, with $\gamma(x)\propto\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$ for $x \approx 0$.[^n14] If the [main flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freestream) $V$ has [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) $\rho$, then the [Kutta–Joukowski theorem](wikipedia://en/8606325) gives that the total lift force $F$ is proportional to[^n15][^o38]

$$\rho V \int_{0}^{c} \gamma (x) \, dx$$

and its moment $M$ about the leading edge proportional to[^n14]

$$\rho V \int_{0}^{c} x \; \gamma (x) \, dx.$$

From the [Biot–Savart law](wikipedia://en/75110), the vorticity $\gamma(x)$ produces a flow field

$$w(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{0}^{c} \frac{\gamma (x')}{x-x'}\, dx'\text{,}$$

oriented [normal](wikipedia://en/173224) to the airfoil at $x$. Since the airfoil is an [impermeable surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic_conductivity), the flow $w(x)$ must balance an inverse flow from $V$. By the [small-angle approximation](wikipedia://en/2009207), $V$ is inclined at angle $\alpha-\tfrac{dy}{dx}$ relative to the blade at position $x$, and the normal component is correspondingly $(\alpha-\tfrac{dy}{dx})V$. Thus, $\gamma(x)$ must satisfy the [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) [equation](wikipedia://en/474234)

$$\left(\alpha - \frac{dy}{dx}\right)V = -w(x) = -\frac{1}{2 \pi} \int_{0}^{c} \frac {\gamma(x')}{x-x'} \,dx'\text{,}$$

which uniquely determines it in terms of known quantities.[^n15][^n16]

An explicit solution can be obtained through first the [change of variables](wikipedia://en/2146848)

$$x = c \cdot \frac{1 + \cos(\theta)}{2},$$

and then expanding both $\tfrac{dy}{dx}$ and $\gamma(x)$ as a nondimensionalized [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) in $\theta$ with a modified lead term:

$$\begin{aligned} &\frac{dy}{dx}=A_0+A_1\cos(\theta)+A_2\cos(2\theta)+\dots \\ &\gamma(x)=2(\alpha+A_0)\left(\frac{\sin\theta}{1+\cos\theta}\right)+2A_1\sin(\theta)+2A_2\sin(2\theta)+\dots\text{.} \end{aligned}$$

The resulting lift and moment depend on only the first few terms of this series.[^n17]

The [lift coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lift_coefficient) satisfies

$$C_L = 2\pi\left(\alpha+A_0+\frac{A_1}{2}\right)=2\pi\alpha+2\int_0^\pi{\frac{dy}{dx}\cdot(1+\cos\theta)\,d\theta}$$

and the moment coefficient[^n18]

$$C_M = -\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\alpha+A_0+A_1-\frac{A_2}{2}\right)=-\frac{\pi}{2}\alpha-\int_0^\pi{\frac{dy}{dx}\cdot\cos(\theta)(1+\cos\theta)\,d\theta}\text{.}$$

The moment about the 1/4 chord point will thus be

$$C_M(1/4c) = - \pi /4 (A_1 - A_2)\text{.}$$

From this it follows that the [center of pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_pressure_%28fluid_mechanics%29) is aft of the 'quarter-chord' point $0.25c$, by

$$\Delta x /c = \pi /4 ((A_1-A_2)/C_L)\text{.}$$

The [aerodynamic center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamic_center) is the position at which the pitching moment $M$ does not *vary* with a change in lift coefficient:[^n15]

$$\frac{ \partial (C_{M'}) }{ \partial (C_L)} = 0\text{.}$$

Thin-airfoil theory shows that, in two-dimensional inviscid flow, the aerodynamic center is at the quarter-chord position.
