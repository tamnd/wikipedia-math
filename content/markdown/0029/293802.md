# Closure (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Closure"}

In mathematics, a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of a larger [set](wikipedia://en/26691) is **closed** under a given [operation](wikipedia://en/4140245) on the larger set if performing that operation on members of the subset always produces a member of that subset. For example, the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) are closed under addition, but not under subtraction: 1 − 2 is not a natural number, although both 1 and 2 are.

:::

Similarly, a subset is said to be closed under a *collection* of operations if it is closed under each of the operations individually.

The **closure** of a subset is the result of a [closure operator](wikipedia://en/483120) applied to the subset. The *closure* of a subset under some operations is the smallest superset that is closed under these operations. It is often called the *span* (for example [linear span](wikipedia://en/56353)) or the *generated set*.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Let $S$ be a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) equipped with one or several methods for producing elements of $S$ from other elements of $S$.[^o4] A subset $X$ of $S$ is said to be *closed* under these methods if an input of purely elements of $X$ always results in an element still in $X$. Sometimes, one may also say that $X$ has the **closure property**.

The main property of closed sets, which results immediately from the definition, is that every [intersection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_intersection) of closed sets is a [closed set](wikipedia://en/47279). It follows that for every subset $Y$ of $S$, there is a smallest closed subset $X$ of $S$ such that $Y\subseteq X$ (it is the intersection of all closed subsets that contain $Y$). Depending on the context, $X$ is called the *closure* of $Y$ or the set [generated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generating_set) or [spanned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Span_%28linear_algebra%29) by $Y$.

The concepts of closed sets and closure are often extended to any property of subsets that are stable under intersection; that is, every intersection of subsets that have the property has also the property. For example, in $\mathbb{C}^n,$ a *[Zariski-closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski-closed) set*, also known as an [algebraic set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_set), is the set of the common zeros of a family of polynomials, and the [Zariski closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski_closure) of a set $V$ of points is the smallest algebraic set that contains $V$.

### In algebraic structures {#In_algebraic_structures}

An [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) is a set equipped with [operations](wikipedia://en/4140245) that satisfy some [axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axioms). These axioms may be [identities](wikipedia://en/298428). Some axioms may contain [existential quantifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_quantifier) $\exists;$ in this case it is worth to add some auxiliary operations in order that all axioms become identities or purely [universally quantified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universally_quantified) formulas. See [Algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) for details. A set with a single [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) that is closed is called a [magma](wikipedia://en/141916).

In this context, given an algebraic structure $S$, a [substructure](wikipedia://en/2057747) of $S$ is a subset that is closed under all operations of $S$, including the auxiliary operations that are needed for avoiding existential quantifiers. A substructure is an algebraic structure of the same type as $S$. It follows that, in a specific example, when closeness is proved, there is no need to check the axioms for proving that a substructure is a structure of the same type.

Given a subset $X$ of an algebraic structure $S$, the closure of $X$ is the smallest substructure of $S$ that is closed under all operations of $S$. In the context of algebraic structures, this closure is generally called the substructure *generated* or *spanned* by $X$, and one says that $X$ is a [generating set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generating_set) of the substructure.

For example, a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) is a set with an [associative operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative_operation), often called *multiplication*, with an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962), such that every element has an [inverse element](wikipedia://en/45199). Here, the auxiliary operations are the [nullary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nullary) operation that results in the identity element and the [unary operation](wikipedia://en/161006) of inversion. A subset of a group that is closed under multiplication and inversion is also closed under the nullary operation (that is, it contains the identity) if and only if it is non-empty. So, a non-empty subset of a group that is closed under multiplication and inversion is a group that is called a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011). The subgroup generated by a single element, that is, the closure of this element, is called a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327).

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), the closure of a non-empty subset of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) (under vector-space operations, that is, addition and [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373)) is the [linear span](wikipedia://en/56353) of this subset. It is a vector space by the preceding general result, and it can be proved easily that is the set of [linear combinations](wikipedia://en/55632) of elements of the subset.

Similar examples can be given for almost every algebraic structures, with, sometimes some specific terminology. For example, in a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346), the closure of a single element under [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) operations is called a [principal ideal](wikipedia://en/145126).

## Binary relations {#Binary_relations}

A [binary relation](wikipedia://en/3931) $R$ on a set $A$ is a subset of $A\times A$, which is the set of all [ordered pairs](wikipedia://en/22362) on $A$. The [infix notation](wikipedia://en/199014) $xRy$ is commonly used for $(x,y)\in R$. We can define different kinds of closures of $R$ on $A$ by the properties and operations of it. For examples:[^o8]

**[Reflexivity](wikipedia://en/200458#Etymology)**

As every intersection of reflexive relations is reflexive, we define the [reflexive closure](wikipedia://en/22603618#Definition) of $R$ on $A$ as the smallest reflexive relation on $A$ that contains $R$.

**[Symmetry](wikipedia://en/200459)**

As we can define a [unary operation](wikipedia://en/161006) on $A\times A$ that maps $(x,y)$ to $(y,x)$, we define the [symmetric closure](wikipedia://en/22603634#Definition) of $R$ on $A$ as the smallest relation on $A$ that contains $R$ and is closed under this unary operation.

**[Transitivity](wikipedia://en/200463)**

As we can define a [partial binary operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_operation) on $A\times A$ that maps $(x,y)$ and $(y,z)$ to $(x,z)$, we define the [transitive closure](wikipedia://en/292239#Definition) of $R$ on $A$ as the smallest relation on $A$ that contains $R$ and is closed under this partial binary operation.

A [preorder](wikipedia://en/23582) is a relation that is reflective and transitive. It follows that the **reflexive transitive closure** of a relation is the smallest preorder containing it. Similarly, the **reflexive transitive symmetric closure** or **equivalence closure** of a relation is the smallest [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) that contains it.

:::

## Other examples {#Other_examples}

- In [matroid](wikipedia://en/244321) theory, the closure of *X* is the largest superset of *X* that has the same rank as *X*.
- The [transitive closure](wikipedia://en/2462396#Transitive_closure) of a [set](wikipedia://en/26691).[^o10]
- The [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129) of a [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28algebra%29).[^o11]
- The [integral closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_closure) of an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) in a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) that contains it.
- The [radical of an ideal](wikipedia://en/252009) in a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346).
- In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of a set *S* of points is the smallest [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292) of which *S* is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631).[^o12]
- In [formal languages](wikipedia://en/10939), the [Kleene closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleene_closure) of a language can be described as the set of strings that can be made by concatenating zero or more strings from that language.
- In [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), the [conjugate closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_closure) or normal closure of a set of [group](wikipedia://en/19447) elements is the smallest [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) containing the set.
- In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) and in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), the closure of a collection of subsets of *X* under [countably many](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) [set operations](wikipedia://en/961805) is called the [σ-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-algebra) generated by the collection.

## Closure operator {#Closure_operator}

In the preceding sections, closures are considered for subsets of a given set. The subsets of a set form a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) (poset) for [inclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_inclusion). *Closure operators* allow generalizing the concept of closure to any partially ordered set.

Given a poset $S$ whose partial order is denoted with $\le$, a *closure operator* on $S$ is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $C:S\to S$ that is

- *increasing* ($x\le C(x)$ for all $x\in S$),
- [idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent) ($C(C(x))=C(x)$), and
- [monotonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonic) ($x\le y \implies C(x)\le C(y)$).[^o14]

Equivalently, a function from $S$ to $S$ is a closure operator if $x \le C(y) \iff C(x) \le C(y)$ for all $x,y\in S.$

An element of $S$ is *closed* if it is its own closure, that is, if $x=C(x).$ By idempotency, an element is closed [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is the closure of some element of $S$.

An example is the [topological closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_closure) operator; in [Kuratowski's characterization](wikipedia://en/331597), axioms K2, K3, K4' correspond to the above defining properties. An example not operating on subsets is the [ceiling function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceiling_function), which maps every real number $x$ to the smallest integer that is not smaller than $x$.

### Closure operator vs. closed sets {#Closure_operator_vs._closed_sets}

A closure on the subsets of a given set may be defined either by a closure operator or by a set of closed sets that is stable under intersection and includes the given set. These two definitions are equivalent.

Indeed, the defining properties of a closure operator $C$ implies that an intersection of closed sets is closed: if $X = \bigcap X_i$ is an intersection of closed sets, then $C(X)$ must contain $X$ and be contained in every $X_i.$ This implies $C(X) = X$ by definition of the intersection.

Conversely, if closed sets are given and every intersection of closed sets is closed, then one can define a closure operator $C$ such that $C(X)$ is the intersection of the closed sets containing $X$.

This equivalence remains true for partially ordered sets with the [greatest-lower-bound property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest-lower-bound_property), if one replace "closed sets" by "closed elements" and "intersection" by "greatest lower bound".
