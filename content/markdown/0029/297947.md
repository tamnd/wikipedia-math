# Chord (geometry)

*Image: CIRCLE LINES-en.svg.* Common lines and line segments on a circle, including a chord in blue

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Chord"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **chord** (la) of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) is a [straight line segment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_line_segment) whose endpoints both lie on a [circular arc](wikipedia://en/5578920). If a chord were to be extended [infinitely](wikipedia://en/20110824) on both directions into a [line](wikipedia://en/946975), the object is a *[secant line](wikipedia://en/241886)*. The perpendicular line passing through the chord's [midpoint](wikipedia://en/437979) is called *[sagitta](wikipedia://en/24947530)* (Latin for "arrow").

:::

More generally, a chord is a line segment joining two points on any [curve](wikipedia://en/89246), for instance, on an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277#Chords). A chord that passes through a circle's center point is the circle's *[diameter](wikipedia://en/8007)*.

## In circles {#In_circles}

Among properties of chords of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) are the following:

1. Chords are equidistant from the center if and only if their lengths are equal.
1. Equal chords are subtended by equal angles from the center of the circle.
1. A chord that passes through the center of a circle is called a diameter and is the longest chord of that specific circle.
1. If the line extensions (secant lines) of chords $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{CD}$ intersect at a point $P$, then their lengths satisfy $\overline{AP} \cdot \overline{PB} = \overline{CP} \cdot \overline{PD}$ ([power of a point theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_of_a_point_theorem)).

## In conics {#In_conics}

The midpoints of a set of parallel chords of a [conic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic) are [collinear](wikipedia://en/3189581) ([midpoint theorem for conics](wikipedia://en/70959639)).[^o4]

## In trigonometry {#In_trigonometry}

*Image: TrigonometricChord.svg.*

Chords were used extensively in the early development of [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261). The first known trigonometric table, compiled by [Hipparchus](wikipedia://en/13600) in the 2nd century BC, is no longer extant but [tabulated the value of the chord function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Table_of_chords) for every $7\tfrac{1}{2}$ [degrees](wikipedia://en/1195294). In the 2nd century AD, [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979) compiled a more extensive table of chords in [his book on astronomy](wikipedia://en/148060), giving the value of the chord for angles ranging from $\tfrac{1}{2}$ to 180 degrees by increments of $\tfrac{1}{2}$ degree. Ptolemy used a circle of diameter 120, and gave chord lengths accurate to two [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) (base sixty) digits after the integer part.[^o6]

The chord function is defined geometrically as shown in the picture. The chord of an [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) is the [length](wikipedia://en/18542) of the chord between two points on a unit circle separated by that [central angle](wikipedia://en/462705). The angle $\theta$ is taken in the positive sense and must lie in the interval $0 < \theta \le \pi$ (radian measure). The chord function can be related to the modern [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) function, by taking one of the points to be $(1,0)$, and the other point to be $(\cos \theta, \sin \theta)$, and then using the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) to calculate the chord length:[^o6]

$$\operatorname{crd} \theta = \sqrt{(1-\cos \theta)^2+\sin^2 \theta} = \sqrt{2-2\cos \theta} =2 \sin \frac{\theta }{2}$$

[^o7]

The last step uses the [half-angle formula](wikipedia://en/25804104#Half-angle_formulae). Much as modern trigonometry is built on the sine function, ancient trigonometry was built on the chord function. Hipparchus is purported to have written a twelve-volume work on chords, all now lost, so presumably, a great deal was known about them. In the table below (where $c$ is the chord length, and $D$ the diameter of the circle) the chord function can be shown to satisfy many identities analogous to well-known modern ones:

::: {#o8 .table cue=table}

| Name | Sine-based | Chord-based |
|---|---|---|
| Pythagorean | $\sin^2 \theta + \cos^2 \theta = 1 \,$ | $\operatorname{crd}^2 \theta + \operatorname{crd}^2 (\pi- \theta) = 4 \,$ |
| Half-angle | $\sin\frac{\theta}{2} = \pm\sqrt{\frac{1-\cos \theta}{2}} \,$ | $\operatorname{crd}\ \frac{\theta}{2} = \sqrt{2-\operatorname{crd}(\pi - \theta)} \,$ |
| **[Apothem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apothem) ($a$)** | $c=2 \sqrt{r^2- a^2}$ | $c=\sqrt{D ^2-4 a^2}$ |
| **Angle ($\theta$)** | $c=2 r \sin \frac{\theta }{2}$ | $c=\frac{D}{2} \operatorname{crd} \theta$ |

:::

The inverse function exists as well:[^o10]

$$\theta = 2\arcsin\frac{c}{2r}$$
