# Inversive geometry

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Inversive geometry"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), **inversive geometry** is the study of *inversion*, a transformation of the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) that maps [circles](wikipedia://en/6220) or [lines](wikipedia://en/946975) to other circles or lines and that preserves the angles between crossing curves. Many difficult problems in geometry become much more tractable when an inversion is applied. Inversion seems to have been discovered by a number of people contemporaneously, including [Steiner](wikipedia://en/567445) (1824), [Quetelet](wikipedia://en/184215) (1825), [Bellavitis](wikipedia://en/23435437) (1836), [Stubbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_William_Stubbs) and [Ingram](wikipedia://en/6550356) (1842–3) and [Kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thomson,_1st_Baron_Kelvin) (1845).[^o2] The concept of inversion can be generalized to higher-dimensional spaces.

:::

## Inversion in a circle {#Inversion_in_a_circle}

*Image: Inversion of lambda Mandelbrot set with different translations.gif.* Inversion of lambda [Mandelbrot set](wikipedia://en/19562) with different translations

### Inverse of a point {#Inverse_of_a_point}

*Image: Inversion illustration1.svg.* *P*' is the inverse of *P* with respect to the circle.

To invert a number in arithmetic usually means to take its [reciprocal](wikipedia://en/229940). A closely related idea in geometry is that of "inverting" a point. In the [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29), the **inverse** of a point *P* with respect to a *reference circle (Ø)* with center *O* and radius *r* is a point *P*', lying on the ray from *O* through *P* such that

$$OP \cdot OP^{\prime} = r^2.$$

This is called **circle inversion** or **plane inversion**. The inversion taking any point *P* (other than *O*) to its image *P*' also takes *P*' back to *P*, so the result of applying the same inversion twice is the identity transformation which makes it a [self-inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-inversion) (i.e. an involution).[^o5][^o6] To make the inversion a [total function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_function) that is also defined for *O*, it is necessary to introduce a [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139), a single point placed on all the lines, and extend the inversion, by definition, to interchange the center *O* and this point at infinity.

It follows from the definition that the inversion of any point inside the reference circle must lie outside it, and vice versa, with the center and the point at infinity changing positions, whilst any point on the circle is unaffected (is *invariant* under inversion). In summary, for a point inside the circle, the nearer the point to the center, the further away its transformation. While for any point (inside or outside the circle), the nearer the point to the circle, the closer its transformation.

#### Compass and straightedge construction {#Compass_and_straightedge_construction}

*Image: Inversion in circle.svg.* To construct the inverse *P'* of a point *P* outside a circle *Ø*: Let *r* be the radius of *Ø*. Right triangles *OPN* and *ONP'* are similar. *OP* is to *r* as *r* is to *OP'*.

##### Point outside circle {#Point_outside_circle}

To [construct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge_constructions) the inverse *P'* of a point *P* outside a circle *Ø*:

- Draw the segment from *O* (center of circle *Ø*) to *P*.
- Let *M* be the midpoint of *OP*. (Not shown)
- Draw the circle *c* with center *M* going through *P*. (Not labeled. It's the blue circle)
- Let *N* and *N'* be the points where *Ø* and *c* intersect.
- Draw segment *NN'*.
- *P'* is where *OP* and *NN'* intersect.

##### Point inside circle {#Point_inside_circle}

To construct the inverse *P* of a point *P'* inside a circle *Ø*:

- Draw ray *r* from *O* (center of circle *Ø*) through *P'*. (Not labeled, it's the horizontal line)
- Draw line *s* through *P'* perpendicular to *r*. (Not labeled. It's the vertical line)
- Let *N* be one of the points where *Ø* and *s* intersect.
- Draw the segment *ON*.
- Draw line *t* through *N* perpendicular to *ON*.
- *P* is where ray *r* and line *t* intersect.

### Dutta's construction {#Dutta's_construction}

There is a construction of the inverse point to *A* with respect to a circle *Ø* that is *independent* of whether *A* is inside or outside *Ø*.[^o11]

Consider a circle *Ø* with center *O* and a point *A* which may lie inside or outside the circle *Ø*.

- Take the intersection point *C* of the ray *OA* with the circle *Ø*.
- Connect the point *C* with an arbitrary point *B* on the circle *Ø* (different from *C* and from the point on *Ø* antipodal to *C*)
- Let *h* be the reflection of ray *BA* in line *BC*. Then *h* cuts ray *OC* in a point *A'*. *A'* is the inverse point of *A* with respect to circle *Ø*.[^o11]

### Properties {#Properties}

The inversion of a set of points in the plane with respect to a circle is the set of inverses of these points. The following properties make circle inversion useful.

- A circle that passes through the center *O* of the reference circle inverts to a line not passing through *O*, but parallel to the tangent to the original circle at *O*, and vice versa; whereas a line passing through *O* is inverted into itself (but not pointwise invariant).[^o14]
- A circle not passing through *O* inverts to a circle not passing through *O*. If the circle meets the reference circle, these invariant points of intersection are also on the inverse circle. A circle (or line) is unchanged by inversion [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/74072119) to the reference circle at the points of intersection.[^o14]

Additional properties include:

- If a circle *q* passes through two distinct points A and A' which are inverses with respect to a circle *k*, then the circles *k* and *q* are orthogonal.
- If the circles *k* and *q* are orthogonal, then a straight line passing through the center O of *k* and intersecting *q*, does so at inverse points with respect to *k*.
- Given a triangle OAB in which O is the center of a circle *k*, and points A' and B' inverses of A and B with respect to *k*, then

$$\angle OAB = \angle OB'A' \ \text{ and }\ \angle OBA = \angle OA'B'.$$

- The points of intersection of two circles *p* and *q* orthogonal to a circle *k*, are inverses with respect to *k*.
- If M and M' are inverse points with respect to a circle *k* on two curves m and m', also inverses with respect to *k*, then the tangents to m and m' at the points M and M' are either perpendicular to the straight line MM' or form with this line an isosceles triangle with base MM'.
- Inversion leaves the measure of angles unaltered, but reverses the orientation of oriented angles.[^o15]

:::

### Application {#Application}

For a circle not passing through the center of inversion, the center of the circle being inverted and the center of its image under inversion are [collinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear) with the center of the reference circle. This fact can be used to prove that the [Euler line](wikipedia://en/203622) of the [intouch triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intouch_triangle) of a triangle coincides with its OI line. The proof roughly goes as below:

Invert with respect to the [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle) of triangle *ABC*. The [medial triangle](wikipedia://en/3339743) of the intouch triangle is inverted into triangle *ABC*, meaning the circumcenter of the medial triangle, that is, the nine-point center of the intouch triangle, the incenter and circumcenter of triangle *ABC* are [collinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear).

Any two non-intersecting circles may be inverted into [concentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentric) circles. Then the [inversive distance](wikipedia://en/18849148) (usually denoted δ) is defined as the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476) of the ratio of the radii of the two concentric circles.

In addition, any two non-intersecting circles may be inverted into [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) circles, using circle of inversion centered at a point on the [circle of antisimilitude](wikipedia://en/18827986).

The [Peaucellier–Lipkin linkage](wikipedia://en/2163562) is a mechanical implementation of inversion in a circle. It provides an exact solution to the important problem of converting between linear and circular motion.

#### Pole and polar {#Pole_and_polar}

*Image: Pole and polar.svg.* The polar line *q* to a point **Q** with respect to a circle of radius *r* centered on the point **O**. The point **P** is the [inversion point](wikipedia://en/295844#Circle_inversion) of **Q**; the polar is the line through **P** that is perpendicular to the line containing **O**, **P** and **Q**.

If point *R* is the inverse of point *P* then the lines [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to the line *PR* through one of the points is the [polar](wikipedia://en/19451201) of the other point (the [pole](wikipedia://en/19451201)).

Poles and polars have several useful properties:

- If a point **P** lies on a line *l*, then the pole **L** of the line *l* lies on the polar *p* of point **P**.
- If a point **P** moves along a line *l*, its polar *p* rotates about the pole **L** of the line *l*.
- If two tangent lines can be drawn from a pole to the circle, then its polar passes through both tangent points.
- If a point lies on the circle, its polar is the tangent through this point.
- If a point **P** lies on its own polar line, then **P** is on the circle.
- Each line has exactly one pole.

## In three dimensions {#In_three_dimensions}

*Image: Inv-kugel.svg.* Inversion of a sphere at the red sphere

*Image: Inv-ellipsoid.svg.* Inversion of a spheroid (at the red sphere)

*Image: Inv-hyperboloid.svg.* Inversion of a hyperboloid of one sheet

Circle inversion is generalizable to sphere inversion in three dimensions. The inversion of a point *P* in 3D with respect to a reference sphere centered at a point *O* with radius *R* is a point *P* ' on the ray with direction *OP* such that $OP \cdot OP^{\prime} = ||OP|| \cdot ||OP^{\prime}|| = R^2$. As with the 2D version, a sphere inverts to a sphere, except that if a sphere passes through the center *O* of the reference sphere, then it inverts to a plane. Any plane passing through *O*, inverts to a sphere touching at *O*. A circle, that is, the intersection of a sphere with a secant plane, inverts into a circle, except that if the circle passes through *O* it inverts into a line. This reduces to the 2D case when the secant plane passes through *O*, but is a true 3D phenomenon if the secant plane does not pass through *O*.

### Stereographic projection as the inversion of a sphere {#Stereographic_projection_as_the_inversion_of_a_sphere}

*Image: Inv-stereogr-proj.svg.* Stereographic projection as an inversion of a sphere

A [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431) usually projects a sphere from a point $N$ (north pole) of the sphere onto the tangent plane at the opposite point $S$ (south pole). This mapping can be performed by an inversion of the sphere onto its tangent plane. If the sphere (to be projected) has the equation $x^2+y^2+z^2 = -z$ (alternately written $x^2+y^2+(z+\tfrac{1}{2})^2=\tfrac{1}{4}$; center $(0,0,-0.5)$, radius $0.5$, green in the picture), then it will be mapped by the inversion at the unit sphere (red) onto the tangent plane at point $S=(0,0,-1)$. The lines through the center of inversion (point $N$) are mapped onto themselves. They are the projection lines of the stereographic projection.

### 6-sphere coordinates {#6-sphere_coordinates}

The [6-sphere coordinates](wikipedia://en/12267953) are a coordinate system for three-dimensional space obtained by inverting the [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates).

## Axiomatics and generalization {#Axiomatics_and_generalization}

One of the first to consider foundations of inversive geometry was [Mario Pieri](wikipedia://en/2452874) in 1911 and 1912.[^o33] [Edward Kasner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Kasner) wrote his thesis on "[Invariant theory](wikipedia://en/363762) of the inversion group".[^o34]

More recently the [mathematical structure](wikipedia://en/1809181) of inversive geometry has been interpreted as an [incidence structure](wikipedia://en/723125) where the generalized circles are called "blocks": In [incidence geometry](wikipedia://en/1576323), any [affine plane](wikipedia://en/649724) together with a single [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139) forms a [Möbius plane](wikipedia://en/38245519), also known as an *inversive plane*. The point at infinity is added to all the lines. These Möbius planes can be described axiomatically and exist in both finite and infinite versions.

A [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_%28model_theory%29) for the Möbius plane that comes from the Euclidean plane is the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799).

## Invariant {#Invariant}

The [cross-ratio](wikipedia://en/401767) between 4 points $x, y, z, w$ is invariant under an inversion. In particular if O is the centre of the inversion and $r_1$ and $r_2$ are distances to the ends of a line L, then length of the line $d$ will become $d/(r_1 r_2)$ under an inversion with radius 1. The invariant is:

$$I =\frac{|x-y| |w-z|}{|x-w| |y-z|}$$

## Relation to Erlangen program {#Relation_to_Erlangen_program}

According to Coxeter,[^o37] the transformation by inversion in circle was invented by [L. I. Magnus](wikipedia://en/340313) in 1831. Since then this mapping has become an avenue to higher mathematics. Through some steps of application of the circle inversion map, a student of [transformation geometry](wikipedia://en/473774) soon appreciates the significance of [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887)'s [Erlangen program](wikipedia://en/243382), an outgrowth of certain models of [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291).

### Dilation {#Dilation}

The combination of two inversions in concentric circles results in a [similarity](wikipedia://en/39377), [homothetic transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothetic_transformation), or dilation characterized by the ratio of the circle radii.

$$x \mapsto R^2 \frac {x} {|x|^2} = y \mapsto T^2 \frac {y} {|y|^2} = \left( \frac {T} {R} \right)^2 x.$$

### Reciprocation {#Reciprocation}

When a point in the plane is interpreted as a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $z=x+iy,$ with [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) $\bar{z}=x-iy,$ then the [reciprocal](wikipedia://en/229940) of *z* is

$$\frac{1}{z} = \frac{\bar{z}}{|z|^2}.$$

Consequently, the algebraic form of the inversion in a [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) is given by $z \mapsto w$ where:

$$w=\frac{1}{\bar z}=\overline{\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)}.$$

Reciprocation is key in transformation theory as a [generator](wikipedia://en/99945) of the [Möbius group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Möbius_group). The other generators are translation and rotation, both familiar through physical manipulations in the ambient 3-space. Introduction of reciprocation (dependent upon circle inversion) is what produces the peculiar nature of Möbius geometry, which is sometimes identified with inversive geometry (of the Euclidean plane). However, inversive geometry is the larger study since it includes the raw inversion in a circle (not yet made, with conjugation, into reciprocation). Inversive geometry also includes the [conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation) mapping. Neither conjugation nor inversion-in-a-circle are in the Möbius group since they are non-conformal (see below). Möbius group elements are [analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478) of the whole plane and so are necessarily [conformal](wikipedia://en/50627).

### Transforming circles into circles {#Transforming_circles_into_circles}

Consider, in the complex plane, the circle of radius $r$ around the point $a$

$$(z-a)(z-a)^* = r^2$$

where without loss of generality, $a \in \mathbb{R}.$ Using the definition of inversion

$$w = \frac{1}{z^*}$$

it is straightforward to show that $w$ obeys the equation

$$w w^*-\frac{a}{(a^2-r^2)}(w+w^{*})+\frac{a^2}{(a^2-r^2)^2}=\frac{r^2}{(a^2-r^2)^2}$$

and hence that $w$ describes the circle of center $\frac{a}{a^2-r^2}$ and radius $\frac{r}{|a^2-r^2|} .$

When $a\to r,$ the circle transforms into the line parallel to the imaginary axis $w+w^* = \tfrac{1}{a}.$

For $a \not\in \mathbb{R}$ and $a a^* \ne r^2$ the result for $w$ is

$$\begin{aligned} & w w^*-\frac{a w+a^* w^*}{(a^* a-r^2)}+\frac{a a^*}{(a a^*-r^2)^2}=\frac{r^2}{(a a^*-r^2)^2} \\[4pt] \Longleftrightarrow {} & \left(w-\frac{a^*}{a a^*-r^2}\right)\left(w^*-\frac{a}{a^* a-r^2}\right)=\left(\frac{r}{\left|a a^*-r^2\right|}\right)^2 \end{aligned}$$

showing that the $w$ describes the circle of center $\frac{a^*}{(a a^*-r^2)}$ and radius $\frac{r}{\left|a^* a-r^2\right|}$.

When $a^* a\to r^2,$ the equation for $w$ becomes

$$\begin{aligned} & a w+ a^* w^*=1 \Longleftrightarrow 2\operatorname{Re}\{a w\}=1 \Longleftrightarrow \operatorname{Re} \{a\} \operatorname{Re}\{w\} - \operatorname{Im}\{a\}\operatorname{Im}\{w\}=\frac{1}{2} \\[4pt] \Longleftrightarrow {} & \operatorname{Im} \{w\}=\frac{\operatorname{Re}\{a\}}{\operatorname{Im}\{a\}}\cdot \operatorname{Re}\{w\}-\frac{1}{2\cdot\operatorname{Im}\{a\}}. \end{aligned}$$

### Higher geometry {#Higher_geometry}

As mentioned above, zero, the origin, requires special consideration in the circle inversion mapping. The approach is to adjoin a point at infinity designated ∞ or 1/0 . In the complex number approach, where reciprocation is the apparent operation, this procedure leads to the [complex projective line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_projective_line), often called the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799). It was subspaces and subgroups of this space and group of mappings that were applied to produce early models of hyperbolic geometry by [Beltrami](wikipedia://en/543760), [Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034), and [Klein](wikipedia://en/41887). Thus inversive geometry includes the ideas originated by [Lobachevsky](wikipedia://en/648527) and [Bolyai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolyai) in their plane geometry. Furthermore, [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) was so overcome by this facility of mappings to identify geometrical phenomena that he delivered a manifesto, the [Erlangen program](wikipedia://en/243382), in 1872. Since then many mathematicians reserve the term *geometry* for a [space](wikipedia://en/5308894) together with a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of mappings of that space. The significant properties of figures in the geometry are those that are invariant under this group.

For example, Smogorzhevsky[^o42] develops several theorems of inversive geometry before beginning Lobachevskian geometry.

## In higher dimensions {#In_higher_dimensions}

In a real *n*-dimensional Euclidean space, an **inversion in the sphere** of radius $r$ centered at the point $O = (o_1, ..., o_n)$ is a map of an arbitrary point $P = (p_1, ..., p_n)$ found by inverting the length of the [displacement vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_vector) $P - O$ and multiplying by $r^2$:

$$\begin{aligned} P &\mapsto P' = O + \frac{r^2(P - O)}{\|P - O\|^2}, \\[5mu] p_j &\mapsto p_j' = o_j + \frac{r^2(p_j - o_j)}{\sum_k (p_k - o_k)^2}. \end{aligned}$$

The transformation by inversion in [hyperplanes](wikipedia://en/99862) or [hyperspheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere) in E<sup>*n*</sup> can be used to generate dilations, translations, or rotations. Indeed, two concentric hyperspheres, used to produce successive inversions, result in a [dilation](wikipedia://en/1591770) or [homothety](wikipedia://en/386138) about the hyperspheres' center.

When two parallel hyperplanes are used to produce successive reflections, the result is a [translation](wikipedia://en/204682). When two hyperplanes intersect in an (*n*−2)-[flat](wikipedia://en/13005617), successive reflections produce a [rotation](wikipedia://en/39789) where every point of the (*n*−2)-flat is a [fixed point](wikipedia://en/449738) of each reflection and thus of the composition.

Any combination of reflections, translations, and rotations is called an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777). Any combination of reflections, dilations, translations, and rotations is a [similarity](wikipedia://en/39377).

All of these are [conformal maps](wikipedia://en/50627), and in fact, where the space has three or more dimensions, the mappings generated by inversion are the only conformal mappings. [Liouville's theorem](wikipedia://en/12458499) is a classical theorem of [conformal geometry](wikipedia://en/295907).

The addition of a [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139) to the space obviates the distinction between hyperplane and hypersphere; higher dimensional inversive geometry is frequently studied then in the presumed context of an [*n*-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) as the base space. The transformations of inversive geometry are often referred to as [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493#Higher_dimensions). Inversive geometry has been applied to the study of colorings, or partitionings, of an *n*-sphere.[^o44]

## Anticonformal mapping property {#Anticonformal_mapping_property}

The circle inversion map is anticonformal, which means that at every point it preserves angles and reverses orientation (a map is called [conformal](wikipedia://en/50627) if it preserves *oriented* angles). Algebraically, a map is anticonformal if at every point the [Jacobian](wikipedia://en/195351) is a scalar times an [orthogonal matrix](wikipedia://en/105620) with negative determinant: in two dimensions the Jacobian must be a scalar times a reflection at every point. This means that if *J* is the Jacobian, then $J \cdot J^T = k I$ and $\det(J) = -\sqrt{k}.$ Computing the Jacobian in the case *z*<sub>*i*</sub> = *x*<sub>*i*</sub>/$\lVert \mathbf{x}\rVert$<sup>2</sup>, where $\lVert \mathbf{x}\rVert$<sup>2</sup> = *x*<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup> + ... + *x*<sub>*n*</sub><sup>2</sup> gives *JJ*<sup>T</sup> = *kI*, with *k* = 1/$\lVert \mathbf{x}\rVert$<sup>4n</sup>, and additionally det(*J*) is negative; hence the inversive map is anticonformal.

In the complex plane, the most obvious circle inversion map (i.e., using the unit circle centered at the origin) is the complex conjugate of the complex inverse map taking *z* to 1/*z*. The complex analytic inverse map is conformal and its conjugate, circle inversion, is anticonformal. In this case a [homography](wikipedia://en/3051962) is conformal while an [anti-homography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-homography) is anticonformal.

## Hyperbolic geometry {#Hyperbolic_geometry}

The [(*n* − 1)-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) with equation

$$x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2 + 2a_1x_1 + \cdots + 2a_nx_n + c = 0$$

will have a positive radius if *a*<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup> + ... + *a*<sub>*n*</sub><sup>2</sup> is greater than *c*, and on inversion gives the sphere

$$x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2 + 2\frac{a_1}{c}x_1 + \cdots + 2\frac{a_n}{c}x_n + \frac{1}{c} = 0.$$

Hence, it will be invariant under inversion if and only if *c* = 1. But this is the condition of being orthogonal to the unit sphere. Hence we are led to consider the (*n* − 1)-spheres with equation

$$x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2 + 2a_1x_1 + \cdots + 2a_nx_n + 1 = 0,$$

which are invariant under inversion, orthogonal to the unit sphere, and have centers outside of the sphere. These together with the subspace hyperplanes separating hemispheres are the hypersurfaces of the [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836) of hyperbolic geometry.

Since inversion in the unit sphere leaves the spheres orthogonal to it invariant, the inversion maps the points inside the unit sphere to the outside and vice versa. This is therefore true in general of orthogonal spheres, and in particular inversion in one of the spheres orthogonal to the unit sphere maps the unit sphere to itself. It also maps the interior of the unit sphere to itself, with points outside the orthogonal sphere mapping inside, and vice versa; this defines the reflections of the Poincaré disc model if we also include with them the reflections through the diameters separating hemispheres of the unit sphere. These reflections generate the group of isometries of the model, which tells us that the isometries are conformal. Hence, the angle between two curves in the model is the same as the angle between two curves in the hyperbolic space.
