# 3-Way

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="3-Way"}

In [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), **3-Way** is a [block cipher](wikipedia://en/4594) designed in 1994 by [Joan Daemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Daemen). It is closely related to [BaseKing](wikipedia://en/8367899); the two are variants of the same general cipher technique.

:::

3-Way has a [block size](wikipedia://en/294065) of 96 [bits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit), notably not a [power of two](wikipedia://en/376948) such as the more common 64 or 128 bits. The [key length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_length) is also 96 bits. The figure 96 arises from the use of three 32 bit [words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_%28computer_architecture%29) in the algorithm, from which also is derived the [cipher](wikipedia://en/5244)'s name. When 3-Way was invented, 96-bit keys and blocks were quite strong, but more recent ciphers have a 128-bit block, and few now have keys shorter than 128 bits. 3-Way is an 11-round [substitution–permutation network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substitution–permutation_network).

3-Way is designed to be very efficient in a wide range of platforms from 8-bit processors to specialized [hardware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_hardware), and has some elegant mathematical features which enable nearly all the decryption to be done in exactly the same circuits as did the encryption.

3-Way, just as its counterpart BaseKing, is vulnerable to [related key](wikipedia://en/1516144) [cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715). [John Kelsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Kelsey_%28cryptanalyst%29), [Bruce Schneier](wikipedia://en/36732), and [David Wagner](wikipedia://en/298253) showed how it can be broken with one related key query and about $2^{22}$ [chosen plaintexts](wikipedia://en/63852).
