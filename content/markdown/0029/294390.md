# Commutative property

::: {#o1 .statement cue=infobox-mathematical-statement name="Commutative property"}

**Commutative property.**

A [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) is *commutative* if changing the order of the [operands](wikipedia://en/22656) does not change the result.

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Commutative property"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) is **commutative** if changing the order of the [operands](wikipedia://en/22656) does not change the result. It is a fundamental property of many binary operations, and many [mathematical proofs](wikipedia://en/82285) depend on it. Perhaps most familiar as a property of arithmetic, e.g. "3 + 4 = 4 + 3" or "2 × 5 = 5 × 2", the property can also be used in more advanced settings. The name is needed because there are operations, such as [division](wikipedia://en/53696) and [subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141), that do not have it (for example, "3 − 5 ≠ 5 − 3"); such operations are *not* commutative, and so are referred to as **noncommutative operations**.

:::

The idea that simple operations, such as the [multiplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplication_%28mathematics%29) and [addition](wikipedia://en/61338) of numbers, are commutative was for many centuries implicitly assumed. Thus, this property was not named until the 19th century, when new [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364) started to be studied.[^n1]

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) $*$ on a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) *S* is *commutative* if

$$x * y = y * x$$

for all $x,y \in S$.[^n2] An operation that is not commutative is said to be *noncommutative*.[^n3]

One says that $x$ *commutes* with $y$ or that $x$ and $y$ *commute* under $*$ if[^n4]

$$x * y = y * x.$$

So, an operation is commutative if every two elements commute.[^n4] An operation is noncommutative if there are two elements such that $x * y \ne y * x.$ This does not exclude the possibility that some pairs of elements commute.[^n3]

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Commutative Addition.svg.* The cumulation of apples, which can be seen as an addition of natural numbers, is commutative.

### Commutative operations {#Commutative_operations}

*Image: Vector Addition.svg.* The addition of vectors is commutative, because $\vec a+\vec b=\vec b+ \vec a.$

- [Addition](wikipedia://en/61338) and [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) are commutative in most [number systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_system), and, in particular, between [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474), [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) and [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826). This is also true in every [field](wikipedia://en/10603).[^n5]
- Addition is commutative in every [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) and in every [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788).[^n6]
- [Union](wikipedia://en/47949) and [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) are commutative operations on [sets](wikipedia://en/26691).[^n7]
- "[And](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/And_%28logic%29)" and "[or](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Or_%28logic%29)" are commutative [logical operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_operation).[^n8]

### Noncommutative operations {#Noncommutative_operations}

- [Division](wikipedia://en/53696) is noncommutative, since $1 \div 2 \neq 2 \div 1$. [Subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141) is noncommutative, since $0 - 1 \neq 1 - 0$. However it is classified more precisely as [anti-commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticommutativity), since $x - y = - (y - x)$ for every $x$ and $y$. [Exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491) is noncommutative, since $2^3\neq3^2$ (see \[\[Equation xy = yx|Equation *x<sup>y</sup>* = *y<sup>x</sup>*\]\]).[^n9]
- Some [truth functions](wikipedia://en/604707) are noncommutative, since their [truth tables](wikipedia://en/25512250) are different when one changes the order of the operands.[^n10] For example, the truth tables for $(A \Rightarrow B) = (\neg A \lor B)$ and $(B \Rightarrow A) = (A \lor \neg B)$ are

- [Function composition](wikipedia://en/195947) is generally noncommutative.[^n11] For example, if $f(x)=2x+1$ and $g(x)=3x+7$. Then $$(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x)) = 2(3x+7)+1 = 6x+15$$ and $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = 3(2x+1)+7 = 6x+10.$$
- [Matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) of [square matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_matrices) of a given dimension is a noncommutative operation, except for $1\times 1$ matrices. For example:[^n12] $$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \neq \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
- The vector product (or [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092)) of two vectors in three dimensions is [anti-commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticommutativity); i.e., $\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{a} = -(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b})$.[^n13]
- The [quaternion](wikipedia://en/51440) multiplication is noncommutative, since $\mathbf {ij}= -\mathbf {ji}$, where $\mathbf i$ and $\mathbf j$ are two of the basis quaternions.

## Commutative structures {#Commutative_structures}

Some types of [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364) involve an operation that does not require commutativity. If this operation is commutative for a specific structure, the structure is often said to be *commutative*. So,

- a [commutative semigroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_semigroup) is a [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799) whose operation is commutative;[^n14]
- a [commutative monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_monoid) is a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) whose operation is commutative;[^n15]
- a *commutative group* or [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) whose operation is commutative;[^n16]
- a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) whose [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) is commutative. (Addition in a ring is always commutative.)[^n17]

However, in the case of [algebras](wikipedia://en/191788), the phrase "[commutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_algebra_%28structure%29)" refers only to [associative algebras](wikipedia://en/2112) that have a commutative multiplication.[^n18]

## History and etymology {#History_and_etymology}

*Image: Commutative Word Origin.PNG.* The first known use of the term was in a French Journal published in 1814

Records of the implicit use of the commutative property go back to ancient times. The [Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt) used the commutative property of [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) to simplify computing [products](wikipedia://en/24977).[^n19] [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) is known to have assumed the commutative property of multiplication in his book [*Elements*](wikipedia://en/244107).[^o32] Formal uses of the commutative property arose in the late 18th and early 19th centuries when mathematicians began to work on a theory of functions. Nowadays, the commutative property is a well-known and basic property used in most branches of mathematics.[^n2]

The first recorded use of the term *commutative* was in a memoir by [François Servois](wikipedia://en/42809386) in 1814, which used the word *commutatives* when describing functions that have what is now called the commutative property.[^n20] *Commutative* is the feminine form of the French adjective *commutatif*, which is derived from the French noun *commutation* and the French verb *commuter*, meaning "to exchange" or "to switch", a cognate of *to commute*. The term then appeared in English in 1838 in [Duncan Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duncan_Gregory)'s article entitled "On the real nature of symbolical algebra" published in 1840 in the [Transactions of the Royal Society of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Edinburgh).
