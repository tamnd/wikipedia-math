# Indefinite orthogonal group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Indefinite orthogonal group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **indefinite orthogonal group**, $\operatorname{O}(p,q)$ is the [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) of all [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) of an $n$-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) that leave invariant a [nondegenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondegenerate_form), [symmetric bilinear form](wikipedia://en/1803375) of [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_of_a_quadratic_form) $(p,q)$, where $n=p+q$. It is also called the **pseudo-orthogonal group**[^o2] or **generalized orthogonal group**.[^o3] The dimension of the group is $n(n-1)/2$.

:::

The **indefinite special orthogonal group**, $\operatorname{SO}(p,q)$ is the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of $\operatorname{O}(p,q)$ consisting of all elements with [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) $1$. Unlike in the definite case, $\operatorname{SO}(p,q)$ is not [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) – it has 2 [components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28topology%29) – and there are two additional finite [index](wikipedia://en/230581) subgroups, namely the connected $\operatorname{SO}^+(p,q)$ and $\operatorname{O}^+(p,q)$, which has 2 components – see for definition and discussion.

The signature of the form determines the group up to [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828); interchanging $p$ with $q$ amounts to replacing the metric by its negative, and so gives the same group. If either $p$ or $q$ equals zero, then the group is isomorphic to the ordinary [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) $\operatorname{O}(n)$. We assume in what follows that both $p$ and $q$ are positive.

The group $\operatorname{O}(p,q)$ is defined for vector spaces over the reals. On [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) spaces, all nondegenerate symmetric bilinear forms are the same up to change of coordinates; however, one can define the [indefinite unitary group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_unitary_group) $\operatorname{U}(p,q)$ which preserves a [sesquilinear form](wikipedia://en/860138) of signature $(p,q)$.

In even dimension $n=2p$, $\operatorname{O}(p,p)$ is known as the split orthogonal group.

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Squeeze r=1.5.svg.* [Squeeze mappings](wikipedia://en/1140043), here $r=3/2$, are the basic hyperbolic symmetries.

The basic example is the [squeeze mappings](wikipedia://en/1140043), which is the group $\operatorname{SO}^+(1,1)$ of (the identity component of) linear transforms preserving the [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122). Concretely, these are the [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) $\left[\begin{smallmatrix} \cosh(\alpha) & \sinh(\alpha) \\ \sinh(\alpha) & \cosh(\alpha) \end{smallmatrix}\right],$ and can be interpreted as *hyperbolic rotations,* just as the group $\operatorname{SO}(2)$ can be interpreted as *circular rotations.*

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), the [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489) $\operatorname{O}(1,3)$ is of central importance, being the setting for [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism) and [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962). (Some texts use $\operatorname{O}(3,1)$ for the Lorentz group; however, $\operatorname{O}(1,3)$ is prevalent in [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267) because the geometric properties of the [Dirac equation](wikipedia://en/39407) are more natural in $\operatorname{O}(1,3)$.)

:::

## Matrix definition {#Matrix_definition}

One can define $\operatorname{O}(p,q)$ as a group of matrices, just as for the classical [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) $\operatorname{O}(n)$. Consider the $(p+q)\times(p+q)$ [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080) $g$ given by

$$g = \mathrm{diag}(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{p},\underbrace{-1,\ldots,-1}_{q}).$$

Then we may define a [symmetric bilinear form](wikipedia://en/1803375) $[\cdot,\cdot]_{p,q}$ on $\mathbb R^{p+q}$ by the formula

$$[x,y]_{p,q}=\langle x,gy\rangle=x_1y_1+\cdots +x_py_p-x_{p+1}y_{p+1}-\cdots -x_{p+q}y_{p+q},$$

where $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is the standard [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) on $\mathbb R^{p+q}$.

We then define $\mathrm{O}(p,q)$ to be the group of $(p+q)\times(p+q)$ matrices that preserve this bilinear form:[^o7]

$$\mathrm{O}(p,q)=\{A\in M_{p+q}(\mathbb R):[Ax,Ay]_{p,q}=[x,y]_{p,q}\,\forall x,y\in\mathbb R^{p+q}\}.$$

More explicitly, $\mathrm{O}(p,q)$ consists of matrices $A$ such that[^o8]

$$gA^Tg = A^{-1},$$

where $A^T$ is the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) of $A$.

One obtains an isomorphic group (indeed, a conjugate subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}(p+q)$) by replacing $g$ with any [symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/126474) with $p$ positive [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) and $q$ negative ones. [Diagonalizing](wikipedia://en/246325) this matrix gives a conjugation of this group with the standard group $\operatorname{O}(p,q)$.

### Subgroups {#Subgroups}

The group $\operatorname{SO}^+(p,q)$ and related subgroups of $\operatorname{O}(p,q)$ can be described algebraically. Partition a matrix $L$ in $\operatorname{O}(p,q)$ as a [block matrix](wikipedia://en/457464):

$$L = \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix}$$

where $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ are $p\times p$, $p\times q$, $q\times p$, and $q\times q$ blocks, respectively. It can be shown that the set of matrices in $\operatorname{O}(p,q)$ whose upper-left $p\times p$ block $A$ has positive determinant is a subgroup. Or, to put it another way, if

$$L = \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix} \;\mathrm{and}\; M = \begin{pmatrix} W & X \\ Y & Z \end{pmatrix}$$

are in $\operatorname{O}(p,q)$, then

$$(\operatorname{sgn} \det A)(\operatorname{sgn} \det W) = \operatorname{sgn} \det (AW+BY).$$

The analogous result for the bottom-right $q\times q$ block also holds. The subgroup $\operatorname{SO}^+(p,q)$ consists of matrices $L$ such that $\det A$ and $\det D$ are both positive.[^o10][^o11]

For all matrices $L$ in $\operatorname{O}(p,q)$, the determinants of $A$ and $D$ have the property that $\frac{\det A}{\det D} = \det L$ and that $|{\det A}| = |{\det D}| \ge 1$.[^o12] In particular, the subgroup $\operatorname{SO}(p,q)$ consists of matrices $L$ such that $\det A$ and $\det D$ have the same sign.[^o10]

## Topology {#Topology}

Assuming both $p$ and $q$ are positive, neither of the groups $\operatorname{O}(p,q)$ nor $\operatorname{SO}(p,q)$ are [connected](wikipedia://en/6233), having $4$ and $2$ components respectively. $\pi_0(\operatorname{O}(p,q))\cong C_2\times C_2$ is the [Klein four-group](wikipedia://en/41928), with each factor being whether an element preserves or reverses the respective orientations on the $p$ and $q$ dimensional subspaces on which the form is definite; note that reversing orientation on only one of these subspaces reverses orientation on the whole space. The special orthogonal group has components $\pi_0(\operatorname{SO}(p,q))=\{(1,1),(-1,-1)\}$, each of which either preserves both orientations or reverses both orientations, in either case preserving the overall orientation.

The [identity component](wikipedia://en/1088787) of $\operatorname{O}(p,q)$ is often denoted $\operatorname{SO}^+(p,q)$ and can be identified with the set of elements in $\operatorname{SO}(p,q)$ that preserve both orientations. This notation is related to the notation $\operatorname{O}^+(1,3)$ for the [orthochronous Lorentz group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthochronous_Lorentz_group), where the $+$ refers to preserving the orientation on the first (temporal) dimension.

The group $\operatorname{O}(p,q)$ is also not [compact](wikipedia://en/750326), but contains the compact subgroups $\operatorname{O}(p)$ and $\operatorname{O}(q)$ acting on the subspaces on which the form is definite. In fact, $\operatorname{O}(p)\times\operatorname{O}(q)$ is a [maximal compact subgroup](wikipedia://en/1496726) of $\operatorname{O}(p,q)$, while $\operatorname{S}(\operatorname{O}(p)\times\operatorname{O}(q))$ (pairs from $\operatorname{O}(p)$ and $\operatorname{O}(q)$ having equal determinant) is a maximal compact subgroup of $\operatorname{SO}(p,q)$. Likewise, $\operatorname{SO}(p)\times\operatorname{SO}(q)$ is a maximal compact subgroup of $\operatorname{SO}^+(p,q)$. Thus, the spaces are [homotopy equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_equivalent) to products of (special) orthogonal groups, from which algebro-topological invariants can be computed. (See [Maximal compact subgroup](wikipedia://en/1496726#Topology).)

In particular, the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of $\operatorname{SO}^+(p,q)$ is the product of the fundamental groups of the components, $\pi_1(\operatorname{SO}^+(p,q))=\pi_1(\operatorname{SO}(p))\times\pi_1(\operatorname{SO}(q))$, and is given by:

!style="background:#efefef;"| $\pi_1(\operatorname{SO}^+(p,q))$ !style="background:#efefef;"| $p=1$ !style="background:#efefef;"| $p=2$ !style="background:#efefef;"| $p\geq 3$ |- !style="background:#efefef;"| $q=1$ | $C_1$ || $\mathbb{Z}$ || $C_2$ |- !style="background:#efefef;"| $q=2$ | $\mathbb{Z}$ || $\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}$ || $\mathbb{Z}\times C_2$ |- !style="background:#efefef;"| $q\geq 3$ | $C_2$ || $C_2\times\mathbb{Z}$ || $C_2\times C_2$ |}

## Split orthogonal group {#Split_orthogonal_group}

In even dimensions, the middle group $\operatorname{O}(n,n)$ is known as the **split orthogonal group**, and is of particular interest, as it occurs as the group of [T-duality](wikipedia://en/432615) transformations in [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305), for example. It is the [split Lie group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_Lie_group) corresponding to the complex [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) $\mathfrak{so}_{2n}$ (the Lie group of the [split real form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_real_form) of the Lie algebra); more precisely, the [identity component](wikipedia://en/1088787) is the split Lie group, as non-identity components cannot be reconstructed from the Lie algebra. In this sense it is opposite to the definite orthogonal group $\operatorname{O}(n):=\operatorname{O}(n,0)=\operatorname{O}(0,n)$, which is the [*compact* real form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_real_form) of the [complex Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/62595897).

The group $\operatorname{SO}(1,1)$ may be identified with the [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122) group, a subgroup of the [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) in [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559).

In terms of being a [group of Lie type](wikipedia://en/1691490) – i.e., construction of an [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924) from a Lie algebra – split orthogonal groups are [Chevalley groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevalley_group), while the non-split orthogonal groups require a slightly more complicated construction, and are [Steinberg groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steinberg_group_%28Lie_theory%29).

Split orthogonal groups are used to construct the [generalized flag variety](wikipedia://en/385339) over non-[algebraically closed fields](wikipedia://en/1018).
