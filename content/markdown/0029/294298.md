# Symplectic geometry

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Symplectic geometry"}

*Image: Limitcycle.svg.* [Phase portrait](wikipedia://en/4519252) of the [Van der Pol oscillator](wikipedia://en/1961786), a one-dimensional system. [Phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) was the original object of study in symplectic geometry.

**Symplectic geometry** is a branch of [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) and [differential topology](wikipedia://en/8562) that studies [symplectic manifolds](wikipedia://en/28356); that is, [differentiable manifolds](wikipedia://en/2119219) equipped with a [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_differential_form), [nondegenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondegenerate_form) [2-form](wikipedia://en/221519). Symplectic geometry has its origins in the [Hamiltonian formulation](wikipedia://en/198319) of [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586) where the [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) of certain classical systems takes on the structure of a symplectic manifold.[^o2]

:::

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The term "symplectic", as adopted into mathematics by [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544),[^o4][^o5] is a neo-Greek [calque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calque) of "complex". Previously, the "[symplectic group](wikipedia://en/173977)" had been called the "line complex group". "Complex" comes from the Latin *com-plexus*, meaning "braided together" (co- + plexus), while "symplectic" represents the corresponding Greek grc (συμπλεκτικός "twining or plaiting together, copulative"). In both cases, the stems come from the Indo-European root ine-x-proto, expressing the concept of folding or weaving, and the prefixes suggest "togetherness". The name reflects the deep connections between complex and symplectic structures.

By [Darboux's theorem](wikipedia://en/1543358), symplectic manifolds are locally isomorphic to the standard [symplectic vector space](wikipedia://en/292852). Hence they have only global (topological) invariants. The term "symplectic topology" is often used interchangeably with "symplectic geometry".

## Overview {#Overview}

A symplectic geometry is defined on a smooth even-dimensional space that is a [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219). On this space a geometric object is defined, the [symplectic 2-form](wikipedia://en/28356#Definition), that allows for the measurement of sizes of two-dimensional objects in the [space](wikipedia://en/5308894). The symplectic form in symplectic geometry plays a role analogous to that of the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) in [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243). Where the metric tensor measures lengths and angles, the symplectic form measures oriented areas.[^o8]

Symplectic geometry arose from the study of [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586) and an example of a symplectic structure is the motion of an object in one dimension. To specify the trajectory of the object, one requires both the [position](wikipedia://en/1345771) *q* and the [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431) *p*, which form a point (*p*,*q*) in the [Euclidean plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-dimensional_Euclidean_space) $\mathbb{R}^{2}$. In this case, the symplectic [form](wikipedia://en/221519) is

$$\omega = dp \wedge dq$$

and is an [area form](wikipedia://en/1855811) that measures the area *A* of a region *S* in the plane through [integration](wikipedia://en/221519#Integration):

$$A = \int_S \omega.$$

The area is important because as [conservative dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/8918323) evolve in time, this area is invariant.[^o8]

Higher dimensional symplectic geometries are defined analogously. A 2*n*-dimensional symplectic geometry is formed of pairs of directions

$$((x_1,x_2), (x_3,x_4),\ldots(x_{2n-1},x_{2n}))$$

in a 2*n*-dimensional manifold along with a symplectic form

$$\omega = dx_1 \wedge dx_2 + dx_3 \wedge dx_4 + \cdots + dx_{2n-1} \wedge dx_{2n}.$$

This symplectic form yields the size of a 2*n*-dimensional region *V* in the space as the sum of the areas of the projections of *V* onto each of the planes formed by the pairs of directions[^o8]

$$A = \int_V \omega = \int_V dx_1 \wedge dx_2 + \int_V dx_3 \wedge dx_4 + \cdots + \int_V dx_{2n-1} \wedge dx_{2n}.$$

## Comparison with Riemannian geometry {#Comparison_with_Riemannian_geometry}

[Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243) is the study of [differentiable manifolds](wikipedia://en/2119219) equipped with nondegenerate, symmetric 2-tensors (called [metric tensors](wikipedia://en/195795)). Symplectic geometry has a number of similarities with and differences from Riemannian geometry.

Unlike in the Riemannian case, symplectic manifolds have no local invariants such as [curvature](wikipedia://en/651361). This is a consequence of [Darboux's theorem](wikipedia://en/1543358) which states that a neighborhood of any point of a 2*n*-dimensional symplectic manifold is isomorphic to the standard symplectic structure on an open set of $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$.

Another difference with Riemannian geometry is that not every differentiable manifold can admit a symplectic form; there are certain topological restrictions. For example, every symplectic manifold is even-dimensional and [orientable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientable). Additionally, if *M* is a closed symplectic manifold, then the 2nd [de Rham cohomology](wikipedia://en/221530) [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *H*<sup>2</sup>(*M*) is nontrivial (even more - every even-dimensional cohomology group is nontrivial); this implies, for example, that the only [*n*-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) that admits a symplectic form is the [2-sphere](wikipedia://en/27859).

A parallel that one can draw between the two subjects is the analogy between [geodesics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesics) in Riemannian geometry and [pseudoholomorphic curves](wikipedia://en/2294542) in symplectic geometry. Geodesics are curves of shortest length (locally), while pseudoholomorphic curves are surfaces of minimal area. Both concepts play a fundamental role in their respective disciplines.

## Examples and structures {#Examples_and_structures}

Every [Kähler manifold](wikipedia://en/390538) is also a symplectic manifold. Well into the 1970s, symplectic experts were unsure whether any compact non-Kähler symplectic manifolds existed, but since then many examples have been constructed (the first was due to [William Thurston](wikipedia://en/39190)); in particular, [Robert Gompf](wikipedia://en/42024874) has shown that every [finitely presented group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_presented_group) occurs as the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of some symplectic [4-manifold](wikipedia://en/1362795), in marked contrast with the Kähler case.

Most symplectic manifolds, one can say, are not Kähler; and so do not have an integrable [complex structure](wikipedia://en/1549805) compatible with the symplectic form. [Mikhail Gromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gromov_%28mathematician%29), however, made the important observation that symplectic manifolds do admit an abundance of compatible [almost complex structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_complex_structure), so that they satisfy all the axioms for a Kähler manifold *except* the requirement that the [transition maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_map) be [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110).

Gromov used the existence of almost complex structures on symplectic manifolds to develop a theory of [pseudoholomorphic curves](wikipedia://en/2294542),[^o11] which has led to a number of advancements in symplectic topology, including a class of symplectic invariants now known as [Gromov–Witten invariants](wikipedia://en/2301291). Later, using the pseudoholomorphic curve technique [Andreas Floer](wikipedia://en/2280157) invented another important tool in symplectic geometry known as the [Floer homology](wikipedia://en/2672856).[^o12]
