# Antilinear map

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Antilinear map"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f : V \to W$ between two [complex vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_vector_space) is said to be **antilinear** or **conjugate-linear** if

$$\begin{alignedat}{9} f(x + y) &= f(x) + f(y) && \qquad \text{ (additivity) } \\ f(s x) &= \overline{s} f(x) && \qquad \text{ (conjugate homogeneity) } \\ \end{alignedat}$$

hold for all vectors $x, y \in V$ and every [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $s,$ where $\overline{s}$ denotes the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) of $s.$

:::

Antilinear maps stand in contrast to [linear maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator), which are [additive maps](wikipedia://en/22473529) that are [homogeneous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_map) rather than [conjugate homogeneous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_homogeneity). If the vector spaces are [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_vector_space) then antilinearity is the same as linearity.

Antilinear maps occur in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) in the study of [time reversal](wikipedia://en/151013) and in [spinor calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinor_calculus), where it is customary to replace the bars over the basis vectors and the components of geometric objects by dots put above the indices. Scalar-valued antilinear maps often arise when dealing with [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [inner products](wikipedia://en/14856) and [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932).

## Definitions and characterizations {#Definitions_and_characterizations}

A function is called antilinear or conjugate linear if it is [additive](wikipedia://en/22473529) and [conjugate homogeneous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_homogeneous). An antilinear functional on a vector space $V$ is a scalar-valued antilinear map.

A function $f$ is called [additive](wikipedia://en/22473529) if

$$f(x + y) = f(x) + f(y) \quad \text{ for all vectors } x, y$$

while it is called [conjugate homogeneous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_homogeneity) if

$$f(ax) = \overline{a} f(x) \quad \text{ for all vectors } x \text{ and all scalars } a.$$

In contrast, a linear map is a function that is additive and [homogeneous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous), where $f$ is called homogeneous if

$$f(ax) = a f(x) \quad \text{ for all vectors } x \text{ and all scalars } a.$$

An antilinear map $f : V \to W$ may be equivalently described in terms of the [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) $\overline{f} : V \to \overline{W}$ from $V$ to the [complex conjugate vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugate_vector_space) $\overline{W}.$

### Examples {#Examples}

#### Anti-linear dual map {#Anti-linear_dual_map}

Given a complex vector space $V$ of rank 1, we can construct an anti-linear dual map which is an anti-linear map

$$l:V \to \mathbb{C}$$

sending an element $x_1 + iy_1$ for $x_1,y_1 \in \mathbb{R}$ to

$$x_1 + iy_1 \mapsto a_1 x_1 - i b_1 y_1$$

for some fixed real numbers $a_1,b_1.$ We can extend this to any finite dimensional complex vector space, where if we write out the [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005) $e_1, \ldots, e_n$ and each standard basis element as

$$e_k = x_k + iy_k$$

then an anti-linear complex map to $\mathbb{C}$ will be of the form

$$\sum_k x_k + iy_k \mapsto \sum_k a_k x_k - i b_k y_k$$

for $a_k,b_k \in \mathbb{R}.$

#### Isomorphism of anti-linear dual with real dual {#Isomorphism_of_anti-linear_dual_with_real_dual}

The anti-linear dual[^o7]<sup>pg 36</sup> of a complex vector space $V$

$$\operatorname{Hom}_{\overline{\mathbb{C}}}(V,\mathbb{C})$$

is a special example because it is isomorphic to the real dual of the underlying real vector space of $V,$ $\text{Hom}_\mathbb{R}(V,\mathbb{R}).$ This is given by the map sending an anti-linear map

$$\ell: V \to \mathbb{C}$$

to

$$\operatorname{Im}(\ell) : V \to \mathbb{R}$$

In the other direction, there is the inverse map sending a real dual vector

$$\lambda : V \to \mathbb{R}$$

to

$$\ell(v) = -\lambda(iv) + i\lambda(v)$$

giving the desired map.

## Properties {#Properties}

The [composite](wikipedia://en/7472170) of two antilinear maps is a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102). The class of [semilinear maps](wikipedia://en/24918060) generalizes the class of antilinear maps by generalizing the field.

## Anti-dual space {#Anti-dual_space}

The vector space of all antilinear forms on a vector space $X$ is called the algebraic [anti-dual space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-dual_space) of $X.$ If $X$ is a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752), then the vector space of all continuous antilinear functionals on $X,$ denoted by $\overline{X}^{\prime},$ is called the continuous anti-dual space or simply the anti-dual space of $X$[^n1] if no confusion can arise.

When $H$ is a [normed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space) then the canonical norm on the (continuous) anti-dual space $\overline{X}^{\prime},$ denoted by $\|f\|_{\overline{X}^{\prime}},$ is defined by using this same equation:[^n2]

$$\|f\|_{\overline{X}^{\prime}} ~:=~ \sup_{\|x\| \leq 1, x \in X} |f(x)| \quad \text{ for every } f \in \overline{X}^{\prime}.$$

This formula is identical to the formula for the [dual norm](wikipedia://en/13525027) on the [continuous dual space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_dual_space) $X^{\prime}$ of $X,$ which is defined by[^n2]

$$\|f\|_{X^{\prime}} ~:=~ \sup_{\|x\| \leq 1, x \in X} |f(x)| \quad \text{ for every } f \in X^{\prime}.$$

**Canonical isometry between the dual and anti-dual**

The [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) $\overline{f}$ of a functional $f$ is defined by sending $x \in \operatorname{domain} f$ to $\overline{f(x)}.$ It satisfies

$$\|f\|_{X^{\prime}} ~=~ \left\|\overline{f}\right\|_{\overline{X}^{\prime}} \quad \text{ and } \quad \left\|\overline{g}\right\|_{X^{\prime}} ~=~ \|g\|_{\overline{X}^{\prime}}$$

for every $f \in X^{\prime}$ and every $g \in \overline{X}^{\prime}.$ This says exactly that the canonical antilinear [bijection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective_map) defined by

$$\operatorname{Cong} ~:~ X^{\prime} \to \overline{X}^{\prime} \quad \text{ where } \quad \operatorname{Cong}(f) := \overline{f}$$

as well as its inverse $\operatorname{Cong}^{-1} ~:~ \overline{X}^{\prime} \to X^{\prime}$ are antilinear [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777) and consequently also [homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13660).

If $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{R}$ then $X^{\prime} = \overline{X}^{\prime}$ and this canonical map $\operatorname{Cong} : X^{\prime} \to \overline{X}^{\prime}$ reduces down to the [identity map](wikipedia://en/15069).

**Inner product spaces**

If $X$ is an [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856) then both the canonical norm on $X^{\prime}$ and on $\overline{X}^{\prime}$ satisfies the [parallelogram law](wikipedia://en/362953), which means that the [polarization identity](wikipedia://en/2047965) can be used to define a canonical inner product on $X^{\prime}$ and also on $\overline{X}^{\prime},$ which this article will denote by the notations

$$\langle f, g \rangle_{X^{\prime}} := \langle g \mid f \rangle_{X^{\prime}} \quad \text{ and } \quad \langle f, g \rangle_{\overline{X}^{\prime}} := \langle g \mid f \rangle_{\overline{X}^{\prime}}$$

where this inner product makes $X^{\prime}$ and $\overline{X}^{\prime}$ into Hilbert spaces. The inner products $\langle f, g \rangle_{X^{\prime}}$ and $\langle f, g \rangle_{\overline{X}^{\prime}}$ are antilinear in their second arguments. Moreover, the canonical norm induced by this inner product (that is, the norm defined by $f \mapsto \sqrt{\left\langle f, f \right\rangle_{X^{\prime}}}$) is consistent with the dual norm (that is, as defined above by the [supremum](wikipedia://en/39382) over the unit ball); explicitly, this means that the following holds for every $f \in X^{\prime}:$

$$\sup_{\|x\| \leq 1, x \in X} |f(x)| = \|f\|_{X^{\prime}} ~=~ \sqrt{\langle f, f \rangle_{X^{\prime}}} ~=~ \sqrt{\langle f \mid f \rangle_{X^{\prime}}}.$$

If $X$ is an [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856) then the inner products on the dual space $X^{\prime}$ and the anti-dual space $\overline{X}^{\prime},$ denoted respectively by $\langle \,\cdot\,, \,\cdot\, \rangle_{X^{\prime}}$ and $\langle \,\cdot\,, \,\cdot\, \rangle_{\overline{X}^{\prime}},$ are related by

$$\langle \,\overline{f}\, | \,\overline{g}\, \rangle_{\overline{X}^{\prime}} = \overline{\langle \,f\, | \,g\, \rangle_{X^{\prime}}} = \langle \,g\, | \,f\, \rangle_{X^{\prime}} \qquad \text{ for all } f, g \in X^{\prime}$$

and

$$\langle \,\overline{f}\, | \,\overline{g}\, \rangle_{X^{\prime}} = \overline{\langle \,f\, | \,g\, \rangle_{\overline{X}^{\prime}}} = \langle \,g\, | \,f\, \rangle_{\overline{X}^{\prime}} \qquad \text{ for all } f, g \in \overline{X}^{\prime}.$$
