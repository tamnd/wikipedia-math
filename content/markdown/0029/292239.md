# Transitive closure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Transitive closure"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **transitive closure** $R^{+}$ of a [homogeneous binary relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_binary_relation) $R$ on a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $X$ is the smallest [relation](wikipedia://en/22517558) on $X$ that contains $R$ and is [transitive](wikipedia://en/200463). For finite sets, "smallest" can be taken in its usual sense, of having the fewest related pairs; for infinite sets $R^{+}$ is the unique [minimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_element) transitive [superset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superset) of $R$.

:::

For example, if $X$ is a set of airports and $x R y$ means "there is a direct flight from airport $x$ to airport $y$" (for $x$ and $y$ in $X$), then the transitive closure of $R$ on $X$ is the relation $R^{+}$ such that $x R^{+} y$ means "it is possible to fly from $x$ to $y$ in one or more flights".

More formally, the transitive closure of a binary relation $R$ on a set $X$ is the smallest (w.r.t. ⊆) transitive relation $R^{+}$ on $X$ such that $R$ ⊆ $R^{+}$; see (Lidl Pilz 1998, 337). We have $R^{+}$ = $R$ if, and only if, $R$ itself is transitive.

Conversely, [transitive reduction](wikipedia://en/3757117) reduces a minimal relation $S$ from a given relation $R$ such that they have the same closure, that is, $S^{+} = R^{+}$; however, many different $S$ with this property may exist.

Both transitive closure and transitive reduction are also used in the closely related area of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401).

## Transitive relations and examples {#Transitive_relations_and_examples}

A relation *R* on a set *X* is transitive if, for all *x*, *y*, *z* in *X*, whenever *x R y* and *y R z* then *x R z*. Examples of transitive relations include the equality relation on any set, the "less than or equal" relation on any linearly ordered set, and the relation "*x* was born before *y*" on the set of all people. Symbolically, this can be denoted as: if *x* \< *y* and *y* \< *z* then *x* \< *z*.

One example of a non-transitive relation is "city *x* can be reached via a direct flight from city *y*" on the set of all cities. Simply because there is a direct flight from one city to a second city, and a direct flight from the second city to the third, does not imply there is a direct flight from the first city to the third. The transitive closure of this relation is a different relation, namely "there is a sequence of direct flights that begins at city *x* and ends at city *y*". Every relation can be extended in a similar way to a transitive relation.

An example of a non-transitive relation with a less meaningful transitive closure is "*x* is the [day of the week](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_of_the_week) after *y*". The transitive closure of this relation is "some day *x* comes after a day *y* on the calendar", which is trivially true for all days of the week *x* and *y* (and thus equivalent to the [Cartesian square](wikipedia://en/24104095), which is "*x* and *y* are both days of the week").

## Existence and description {#Existence_and_description}

For any relation *R*, the transitive closure of *R* always exists. To see this, note that the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of any [family](wikipedia://en/23624339) of transitive relations is again transitive. Furthermore, [there exists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_exists) at least one transitive relation containing *R*, namely the trivial one: *X* × *X*. The transitive closure of *R* is then given by the intersection of all transitive relations containing *R*.

For finite sets, we can construct the transitive closure step by step, starting from *R* and adding transitive edges. This gives the intuition for a general construction. For any set *X*, we can prove that transitive closure is given by the following expression

$$R^{+}=\bigcup_{i = 1}^{\infty} R^i.$$

where $R^i$ is the *i*-th power of *R*, defined inductively by

$$R^1 = R$$

and, for $i>0$,

$$R^{i+1} = R \circ R^i$$

where $\circ$ denotes [composition of relations](wikipedia://en/7472170).

To show that the above definition of *R*<sup>\+</sup> is the least transitive relation containing *R*, we show that it contains *R*, that it is transitive, and that it is the smallest set with both of those characteristics.

- $R \subseteq R^{+}$: $R^+$ contains all of the $R^i$, so in particular $R^+$ contains $R$.
- $R^{+}$ is transitive: If $(s_1, s_2), (s_2, s_3)\in R^+$, then $(s_1, s_2)\in R^j$ and $(s_2, s_3)\in R^k$ for some $j,k$ by definition of $R^+$. Since composition is associative, $R^{j+k} = R^j \circ R^k$; hence $(s_1, s_3)\in R^{j+k} \subseteq R^+$ by definition of $\circ$ and $R^+$.
- $R^{+}$ is minimal, that is, if $T$ is any transitive relation containing $R$, then $R^{+} \subseteq T$: Given any such $T$, [induction](wikipedia://en/18881) on $i$ can be used to show $R^i\subseteq T$ for all $i$ as follows: *Base:* $R^1 = R \subseteq T$ by assumption. *Step:* If $R^i\subseteq T$ holds, and $(s_1, s_3)\in R^{i+1} = R \circ R^i$, then $(s_1, s_2) \in R$ and $(s_2, s_3)\in R^i$ for some $s_2$, by definition of $\circ$. Hence, $(s_1, s_2), (s_2, s_3)\in T$ by assumption and by induction hypothesis. Hence $(s_1, s_3)\in T$ by transitivity of $T$; this completes the induction. Finally, $R^i\subseteq T$ for all $i$ implies $R^{+} \subseteq T$ by definition of $R^{+}$.

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of two transitive relations is transitive.

The [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of two transitive relations need not be transitive. To preserve transitivity, one must take the transitive closure. This occurs, for example, when taking the union of two [equivalence relations](wikipedia://en/9259) or two [preorders](wikipedia://en/23582). To obtain a new equivalence relation or preorder one must take the transitive closure (reflexivity and symmetry—in the case of equivalence relations—are automatic).

:::

## In graph theory {#In_graph_theory}

*Image: Transitive-closure.svg.* Transitive closure constructs the output graph from the input graph.

In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), the concept of transitive closure can be thought of as constructing a data structure that makes it possible to answer [reachability](wikipedia://en/2833097) questions. That is, can one get from node *a* to node *d* in one or more hops? A binary relation tells you only that node a is connected to node *b*, and that node *b* is connected to node *c*, etc. After the transitive closure is constructed, as depicted in the following figure, in an [O(1)-time](wikipedia://en/44578#Orders_of_common_functions) operation one may determine that node *d* is reachable from node *a*. The data structure is typically stored as a Boolean matrix, so if matrix\[1\]\[4\] = true, then it is the case that node 1 can reach node 4 through one or more hops.

The transitive closure of the adjacency relation of a [directed acyclic graph](wikipedia://en/204002) (DAG) is the reachability relation of the DAG and a [strict partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strict_partial_order).

*Image: Equivalentie.svg.* A [cluster graph](wikipedia://en/50931136), the transitive closure of an undirected graph

The transitive closure of an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) produces a [cluster graph](wikipedia://en/50931136), a [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/9938459) of [cliques](wikipedia://en/524466). Constructing the transitive closure is an equivalent formulation of the problem of finding the [components](wikipedia://en/246223) of the graph.[^o8]

## In logic and computational complexity {#In_logic_and_computational_complexity}

The transitive closure of a binary relation cannot, in general, be expressed in [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) (FO). This means that one cannot write a formula using predicate symbols *R* and *T* that will be satisfied in any model if and only if *T* is the transitive closure of *R*. In [finite model theory](wikipedia://en/1773596), first-order logic (FO) extended with a transitive closure operator is usually called **transitive closure logic**, and abbreviated FO(TC) or just TC. TC is a sub-type of [fixpoint logics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixpoint_logic). The fact that FO(TC) is strictly more expressive than FO was discovered by [Ronald Fagin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Fagin) in 1974; the result was then rediscovered by [Alfred Aho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Aho) and [Jeffrey Ullman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_Ullman) in 1979, who proposed to use fixpoint logic as a [database query language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database_query_language).[^o10] With more recent concepts of finite model theory, proof that FO(TC) is strictly more expressive than FO follows immediately from the fact that FO(TC) is not [Gaifman-local](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaifman-local).[^o11]

In [computational complexity theory](wikipedia://en/7543), the [complexity class](wikipedia://en/502426) [NL](wikipedia://en/1145955) corresponds precisely to the set of logical sentences expressible in TC. This is because the transitive closure property has a close relationship with the [NL-complete](wikipedia://en/4595166) problem [STCON](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STCON) for finding [directed paths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_path) in a graph. Similarly, the class [L](wikipedia://en/1149999) is first-order logic with the commutative, transitive closure. When transitive closure is added to [second-order logic](wikipedia://en/341428) instead, we obtain [PSPACE](wikipedia://en/39351).

## In database query languages {#In_database_query_languages}

Since the 1980s [Oracle Database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oracle_Database) has implemented a proprietary [SQL](wikipedia://en/29004) extension `CONNECT BY... START WITH` that allows the computation of a transitive closure as part of a declarative query. The [SQL 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQL_3) (1999) standard added a more general `WITH RECURSIVE` construct also allowing transitive closures to be computed inside the query processor; as of 2011 the latter is implemented in [IBM Db2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Db2), [Microsoft SQL Server](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_SQL_Server), [Oracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oracle_Database), [PostgreSQL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PostgreSQL), and [MySQL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MySQL) (v8.0+). [SQLite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQLite) released support for this in 2014.

[Datalog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Datalog) also implements transitive closure computations.[^o13]

[MariaDB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MariaDB) implements Recursive Common Table Expressions, which can be used to compute transitive closures. This feature was introduced in release 10.2.2 of April 2016.[^o14]

## Algorithms {#Algorithms}

Efficient algorithms for computing the transitive closure of the adjacency relation of a graph can be found in (Nuutila 1995). Reducing the problem to multiplications of adjacency matrices achieves the time complexity of [fast matrix multiplication algorithms](wikipedia://en/24107400),[^o18] $O(n^{2.3728596})$. However, this approach is not practical since both the constant factors and the memory consumption for sparse graphs are high (Nuutila 1995, 22–23). The problem can also be solved by the [Floyd–Warshall algorithm](wikipedia://en/230401) in $O(n^3)$, or by repeated [breadth-first search](wikipedia://en/97026) or [depth-first search](wikipedia://en/97034) starting from each node of the graph.

For directed graphs, [Purdom's algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdom's_algorithm) solves the problem by first computing its condensation DAG and its transitive closure, then lifting it to the original graph. Its runtime is $O(m+\mu n)$, where $\mu$ is the number of edges between its [strongly connected components](wikipedia://en/684680).[^o20][^o21][^o22][^o23]

More recent research has explored efficient ways of computing transitive closure on distributed systems based on the [MapReduce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MapReduce) paradigm.[^o24]
