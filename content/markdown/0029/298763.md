# Richard M. Karp

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Richard M. Karp"}

**Richard Manning Karp** (born January 3, 1935) is an American [computer scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_scientist) and [computational theorist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_theorist) at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley). He is most notable for his research in the [theory of algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_algorithms), for which he received the 1985 ACM [Turing Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_Award), [The Benjamin Franklin Medal in Computer and Cognitive Science in 2004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Franklin_Institute_Awards), and the [Kyoto Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto_Prize) in 2008.[^o4]

:::

Karp was elected a member of the [National Academy of Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Engineering) (1992) for major contributions to the theory and application of NP-completeness, constructing efficient combinatorial algorithms, and applying probabilistic methods in computer science.

## Biography {#Biography}

Born to parents Abraham and Rose Karp in [Boston, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston,_Massachusetts), Karp has three younger siblings: Robert, [David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_A._Karp), and Carolyn. His family was [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish),[^o6] and he grew up in a small apartment, in a then mostly Jewish neighborhood of [Dorchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorchester,_Boston) in Boston.

Both his parents were Harvard graduates (his mother eventually obtaining her Harvard degree at age 57 after taking evening courses), while his father had had ambitions to go to medical school after Harvard, but became a mathematics teacher as he could not afford the medical school fees.[^o6] He attended [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University), where he received his bachelor's degree in 1955, his master's degree in 1956, and his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) in [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969) in 1959. He started working at [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM)'s [Thomas J. Watson Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_J._Watson_Research_Center).

In 1968, Karp became professor of computer science, mathematics, and operations research at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley). Karp was the first associate chair of the Computer Science Division within the Department of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science.[^o7] Apart from a 4-year period as a professor at the [University of Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Washington), he has remained at Berkeley. From 1988 to 1995 and 1999 to the present he has also been a research scientist at the [International Computer Science Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Computer_Science_Institute) in Berkeley, where he currently leads the Algorithms Group.

Richard Karp was awarded the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science), and was the recipient of the [Harvey Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvey_Prize) of the [Technion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technion) and the 2004 [Benjamin Franklin Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_Institute#The_Benjamin_Franklin_Awards) in Computer and Cognitive Science for his insights into [computational complexity](wikipedia://en/7543). In 1994 he was inducted as a [Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow) of the [Association for Computing Machinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_for_Computing_Machinery). He was elected to the 2002 class of [Fellows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow) of the [Institute for Operations Research and the Management Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Operations_Research_and_the_Management_Sciences).[^o8] He is the recipient of several honorary degrees and a member of the U.S. [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences),[^o9] the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences),[^o10] and the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society).[^o11]

In 2012, Karp became the founding director of the [Simons Institute for the Theory of Computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simons_Institute_for_the_Theory_of_Computing) at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley).[^o12]

## Work {#Work}

Karp has made many important discoveries in computer science, [combinatorial algorithms](wikipedia://en/420555), and [operations research](wikipedia://en/43476). His major current research interests include [bioinformatics](wikipedia://en/4214).

In 1962 he co-developed with Michael Held the [Held–Karp algorithm](wikipedia://en/45036001), an [exact](wikipedia://en/48448587) exponential-time algorithm for the [travelling salesman problem](wikipedia://en/31248).

In 1971 he co-developed with [Jack Edmonds](wikipedia://en/3625459) the [Edmonds–Karp algorithm](wikipedia://en/239230) for solving the [maximum flow problem](wikipedia://en/403165) on networks, and in 1972 he published a landmark paper in complexity theory, "Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems", in which he proved [21 problems to be NP-complete](wikipedia://en/2012564).[^o14]

In 1973 he and [John Hopcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hopcroft) published the [Hopcroft–Karp algorithm](wikipedia://en/5944391), the fastest known method for finding maximum cardinality [matchings](wikipedia://en/581797) in [bipartite graphs](wikipedia://en/244431).

In 1980, along with [Richard J. Lipton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_J._Lipton), Karp proved the [Karp–Lipton theorem](wikipedia://en/2855729) (which proves that if [SAT](wikipedia://en/4715) can be solved by [Boolean circuits](wikipedia://en/7087423) with a polynomial number of [logic gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_gate), then the [polynomial hierarchy](wikipedia://en/658651) collapses to its second level).

In 1987 he co-developed with [Michael O. Rabin](wikipedia://en/298404) the [Rabin–Karp string search algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabin–Karp_string_search_algorithm).

### Turing Award {#Turing_Award}

His citation[^o16] for the *(1985)* Turing Award was as follows:

For his continuing contributions to the theory of algorithms including the development of efficient algorithms for network flow and other combinatorial optimization problems, the identification of polynomial-time computability with the intuitive notion of [algorithmic efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_efficiency), and, most notably, contributions to the theory of [NP-completeness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete). Karp introduced the now standard methodology for proving problems to be NP-complete which has led to the identification of many theoretical and practical problems as being computationally difficult.
