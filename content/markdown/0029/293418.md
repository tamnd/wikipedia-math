# Character (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Character"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **character** is (most commonly) a special kind of [function](wikipedia://en/185427) from a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) to a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) (such as the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826)). There are at least two distinct, but overlapping meanings.[^o2] Other uses of the word "character" are almost always qualified.

:::

## Multiplicative character {#Multiplicative_character}

A **multiplicative character** (or **linear character**, or simply **character**) on a group *G* is a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) from *G* to the [multiplicative group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_group) of a field (Artin 1966), usually the field of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826). If *G* is any group, then the set Ch(*G*) of these morphisms forms an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) under pointwise multiplication.

This group is referred to as the [character group](wikipedia://en/1384568) of *G*. Sometimes only *unitary* characters are considered (thus the image is in the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071)); other such homomorphisms are then called *quasi-characters*. [Dirichlet characters](wikipedia://en/174515) can be seen as a special case of this definition.

Multiplicative characters are [linearly independent](wikipedia://en/101863), i.e. if $\chi_1,\chi_2, \ldots , \chi_n$ are different characters on a group *G* to the same field then from $a_1\chi_1+a_2\chi_2 + \dots + a_n \chi_n = 0$ it follows that $a_1=a_2=\cdots=a_n=0$.

::: {#o6 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Character of a representation"}

## Character of a representation {#Character_of_a_representation}

The **character** $\chi : G \to F$ of a [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) $\phi \colon G\to\mathrm{GL}(V)$ of a group *G* on a [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V* over a field *F* is the [trace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace_%28matrix%29) of the representation $\phi$ (Serre 1977), i.e.

$\chi_\phi(g) = \operatorname{Tr}(\phi(g))$ for $g \in G$

In general, the trace is not a group homomorphism, nor does the set of traces form a group. The characters of one-dimensional representations are identical to one-dimensional representations, so the above notion of multiplicative character can be seen as a special case of higher-dimensional characters. The study of representations using characters is called "[character theory](wikipedia://en/691741)" and one-dimensional characters are also called "linear characters" within this context.

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Alternative definition"}

### Alternative definition {#Alternative_definition}

If restricted to [finite](wikipedia://en/323707) abelian group with $1 \times 1$ representation in $\mathbb{C}$ (i.e. $\mathrm{GL}(V) = \mathrm{GL}(1, \mathbb{C})$), the following alternative definition would be equivalent to the above (For abelian groups, every matrix representation decomposes into a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) of $1 \times 1$ representations. For non-abelian groups, the original definition would be more general than this one):

A character $\chi$ of group $(G, \cdot)$ is a group homomorphism $\chi: G \rightarrow \mathbb{C}^*$ i.e. $\chi (x \cdot y)=\chi (x) \chi (y)$ for all $x, y \in G.$

If $G$ is a finite abelian group, the characters play the role of [harmonics](wikipedia://en/14147). For infinite abelian groups, the above would be replaced by $\chi: G \to \mathbb{T}$ where $\mathbb{T}$ is the [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177).
