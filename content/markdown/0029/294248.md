# Observable

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Observable"}

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), an **observable** is a [physical property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_property) or [physical quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_quantity) that can be [measured](wikipedia://en/19022). In [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), an observable is a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued "function" on the set of all possible system states, e.g., [position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_%28vector%29) and [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431). In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), an observable is described by a linear [operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_operator). For example, these operators might represent submitting the system to various [electromagnetic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field) and eventually reading a value.

:::

Physically meaningful observables must also satisfy [transformation](wikipedia://en/18102) laws that relate observations performed by different [observers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation) in different [frames of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frames_of_reference). These transformation laws are [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of the [state space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_space_%28physics%29), that is [bijective](wikipedia://en/3942) [transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_%28mathematics%29) that preserve certain mathematical properties of the space in question.

::: {#o3 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Observable (quantum mechanics)"}

## Quantum mechanics {#Quantum_mechanics}

Every observable quantity in a quantum system is represented by a linear operator. [John Archibald Wheeler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Archibald_Wheeler) used the analogy of a machine to describe operators: a quantum state goes in to the machine and the result state comes out. The result state will be one of the [eigenstates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenstate) of the operator. If the input was an eigenstate, the output will also be that eigenstate. In all other cases the output will be non-deterministic: one of the eigenstates will result with a probability depending on the operator and input.[^o4]

In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), observables correspond to linear [self-adjoint operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-adjoint_operators) on a [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) representing the [quantum state space](wikipedia://en/9718351).[^n1] Observables assign values to outcomes of *particular measurements*, corresponding to the [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of the operator. If these outcomes represent physically allowable states (i.e. those that belong to the Hilbert space) the eigenvalues are [real](wikipedia://en/20646438); however, the converse is not necessarily true.[^o6][^n2][^o8] As a consequence, only certain measurements can determine the value of an observable for some state of a quantum system. In classical mechanics, *any* measurement can be made to determine the value of an observable.

The relation between the state of a quantum system and the value of an observable requires some [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) for its description. In the [mathematical formulation of quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/20728), up to a [phase constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_factor), [pure states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_state) are given by non-zero [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometry%29) in a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) *V*. Two vectors **v** and **w** are considered to specify the same state if and only if $\mathbf{w} = c\mathbf{v}$ for some non-zero $c \in \mathbb{C}$. Observables are given by [self-adjoint operators](wikipedia://en/187408) on *V*. Not every self-adjoint operator corresponds to a physically meaningful observable.[^o9][^o10][^o11][^o12] Also, not all physical observables are associated with non-trivial self-adjoint operators. For example, in quantum theory, mass appears as a parameter in the Hamiltonian, not as a non-trivial operator.[^o13]

In the case of transformation laws in quantum mechanics, the requisite automorphisms are [unitary](wikipedia://en/225999) (or [antiunitary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiunitary)) [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) of the Hilbert space *V*. Under [Galilean relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilean_relativity) or [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962), the mathematics of frames of reference is particularly simple, considerably restricting the set of physically meaningful observables.

In quantum mechanics, measurement of observables exhibits some seemingly unintuitive properties. Specifically, if a system is in a state described by a vector in a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), the measurement process affects the state in a non-deterministic but statistically predictable way. In particular, after a measurement is applied, the state description by a single vector may be destroyed, being replaced by a [statistical ensemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_ensemble). The [irreversible](wikipedia://en/416681) nature of measurement operations in quantum physics is sometimes referred to as the [measurement problem](wikipedia://en/773292) and is described mathematically by [quantum operations](wikipedia://en/666107). By the structure of quantum operations, this description is mathematically equivalent to that offered by the [relative state interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Many-worlds_interpretation) where the original system is regarded as a subsystem of a larger system and the state of the original system is given by the [partial trace](wikipedia://en/814148) of the state of the larger system.

In quantum mechanics, dynamical variables $A$ such as position, translational (linear) [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431), [orbital angular momentum](wikipedia://en/3797203), [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829), and [total angular momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_angular_momentum) are each associated with a [self-adjoint operator](wikipedia://en/187408) $\hat{A}$ that acts on the [state](wikipedia://en/30876419) of the quantum system. The [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) of operator $\hat{A}$ correspond to the possible values that the dynamical variable can be observed as having. For example, suppose $|\psi_{a}\rangle$ is an eigenket ([eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector)) of the observable $\hat{A}$, with eigenvalue $a$, and exists in a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932). Then

$$\hat{A}|\psi_a\rangle = a|\psi_a\rangle.$$

This eigenket equation says that if a [measurement](wikipedia://en/19022) of the observable $\hat{A}$ is made while the system of interest is in the state $|\psi_a\rangle$, then the observed value of that particular measurement must return the eigenvalue $a$ with certainty. However, if the system of interest is in the general state $|\phi\rangle \in \mathcal{H}$ (and $|\phi\rangle$ and $|\psi_a\rangle$ are [unit vectors](wikipedia://en/167053), and the [eigenspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenspace) of $a$ is one-dimensional), then the eigenvalue $a$ is returned with probability $|\langle \psi_a|\phi\rangle|^2$, by the [Born rule](wikipedia://en/4313746).

### Compatible and incompatible observables in quantum mechanics {#Compatible_and_incompatible_observables_in_quantum_mechanics}

A crucial difference between classical quantities and quantum mechanical observables is that some pairs of quantum observables may not be simultaneously measurable, a property referred to as [complementarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementarity_%28physics%29). This is mathematically expressed by non-[commutativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity) of their corresponding operators, to the effect that the [commutator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutator_%28physics%29)

$$\left[\hat{A}, \hat{B}\right] := \hat{A}\hat{B} - \hat{B}\hat{A} \neq \hat{0}.$$

This inequality expresses a dependence of measurement results on the order in which measurements of observables $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{B}$ are performed. A measurement of $\hat{A}$ alters the quantum state in a way that is incompatible with the subsequent measurement of $\hat{B}$ and vice versa.

Observables corresponding to commuting operators are called *compatible observables*. For example, momentum along say the $x$ and $y$ axes are compatible. Observables corresponding to non-commuting operators are called *incompatible observables* or *complementary variables*. For example, the position and momentum along the same axis are incompatible.[^o15]

Incompatible observables cannot have a complete set of common [eigenfunctions](wikipedia://en/228601). Note that there can be some simultaneous eigenvectors of $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{B}$, but not enough in number to constitute a complete [basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_%28vector_space%29).[^o16][^n3]
