# Block size (cryptography)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Block size"}

In modern [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), [symmetric key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_key_algorithm) [ciphers](wikipedia://en/5244) are generally divided into [stream ciphers](wikipedia://en/49007) and [block ciphers](wikipedia://en/4594). [Block ciphers](wikipedia://en/4594) operate on a fixed length string of [bits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit). The length of this bit string is the **block size**.[^o2] Both the input ([plaintext](wikipedia://en/157935)) and output ([ciphertext](wikipedia://en/175560)) are the same length; the output cannot be shorter than the input – this follows logically from the [pigeonhole principle](wikipedia://en/54217) and the fact that the cipher must be reversible – and it is undesirable for the output to be longer than the input.

:::

Until the announcement of [NIST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIST)'s [AES contest](wikipedia://en/99431), the majority of block ciphers followed the example of the [DES](wikipedia://en/7978) in using a block size of 64 bits (8 [bytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BytesForAll)). However, the [birthday paradox](wikipedia://en/73242) indicates that after accumulating several blocks equal to the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of the total number possible, there will be an approximately 50% chance of two or more being the same, which would start to leak information about the message contents. Thus even when used with a proper [encryption mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_cipher_modes_of_operation) (e.g. CBC or OFB), only 2<sup>32</sup> × 8 B = 32 GB of data can be safely sent under one key. In practice a greater margin of security is desired, restricting a single key to the encryption of much less data — say a few hundred megabytes. At one point that seemed like a fair amount of data, but today it is easily exceeded. If the [cipher mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_cipher_modes_of_operation) does not properly randomise the input, the limit is even lower.

Consequently, AES candidates were required to support a block length of 128 bits (16 bytes). This should be acceptable for up to 2<sup>64</sup> × 16 B = 256 [exabytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exabyte) of data, and would suffice for many years after introduction. The winner of the AES contest, [Rijndael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rijndael), supports block and key sizes of 128, 192, and 256 bits, but in AES the block size is always 128 bits. The extra block sizes were not adopted by the [AES](wikipedia://en/1260) standard.

Many block ciphers, such as [RC5](wikipedia://en/102600), support a variable block size. The [Luby-Rackoff](wikipedia://en/352709) construction and the [Outerbridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Outerbridge) construction can both increase the effective block size of a cipher.

[Joan Daemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Daemen)'s [3-Way](wikipedia://en/294099) and [BaseKing](wikipedia://en/8367899) have unusual block sizes of 96 and 192 bits, respectively.
