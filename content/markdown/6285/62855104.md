# John Williams Calkin

*Image: Calkin-john w.jpg.* Photo from Calkin's identification badge at Los Alamos.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="John Williams Calkin"}

**John Williams Calkin**[^o2] (11 October 1909, [New Rochelle, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Rochelle,_New_York) – 5 August 1964, [Westhampton, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westhampton,_New_York)) was an American mathematician, specializing in [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180). The [Calkin algebra](wikipedia://en/945565) is named after him.[^o3]

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Calkin received his bachelor's degree from [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University) in 1933 and his master's degree in 1934 and his Ph.D. in 1937 from [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University). His doctoral dissertation *Applications of the Theory of Hilbert Space to Partial Differential Equations; the Self-Adjoint Transformations in Hilbert Space Associated with a Formal Partial Differential Operator of the Second Order and Elliptic Type*) was supervised by [Marshall H. Stone](wikipedia://en/758331). In the dissertation, Calkin acknowledges useful discussions with [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942).[^o5] At the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179), Calkin was a research assistant for the academic year 1937–1938 (working with [Oswald Veblen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Veblen) and von Neumann) and in the first eight months of 1942.[^o6][^o5] From 1938 to 1942 he was an assistant professor at the [University of New Hampshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_New_Hampshire) and then at Chicago's [Illinois Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois_Institute_of_Technology). During the late 1930s and early 1940s he wrote several important papers on operator theory and its applications to partial differential equations.[^o7]

During World War II Calkin was part of a mine warfare operations analysis group with [J.L. Doob](wikipedia://en/1547599), J. von Neumann, and [M.H. Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Harvey_Stone). Von Neumann and Calkin worked on shock waves and damage by explosives; they were sent to England to learn of the progress under way there. When it appeared that their special knowledge would be useful for the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project), they moved to Los Alamos.[^o5]

From Los Alamos, Calkin went in 1946 as a [Guggenheim Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guggenheim_Fellow)[^o8] to the [California Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology). He later taught at the [Rice Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice_University) (renamed Rice University in 1960), before returning in 1949 to [Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Alamos_Scientific_Laboratory) as a member of the theoretical division.[^o7] There he worked on the development of the H-bomb.

In 1958, he accepted a consulting appointment at New York University and at Brookhaven National Laboratory and, in 1961, was named head, and then chairman of the Applied Mathematics Department.[^o7]

Upon his death he was survived by his widow, Emilienne Calkin (1922–2000), and his son, Brant Calkin (born 1934), from a previous marriage (to Eileen Calkin).[^o9] Brant Calkin is an environmental activist in New Mexico and Utah and a former president of the [Sierra Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Club).[^o10]
