# Separable polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Separable polynomial"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) *P*(*X*) over a given [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K* is **separable** if its [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) are [distinct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distinct_%28mathematics%29) in an [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129) of *K*, that is, the number of distinct roots is equal to the [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) of the polynomial.[^o2]

:::

This concept is closely related to [square-free polynomial](wikipedia://en/3816655). If *K* is a [perfect field](wikipedia://en/505088) then the two concepts coincide. In general, *P*(*X*) is separable if and only if it is square-free over any field that contains *K*, which holds if and only if *P*(*X*) is [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime_polynomials) to its [formal derivative](wikipedia://en/3832917) *D* *P*(*X*).

## Older definition {#Older_definition}

In an older definition, *P*(*X*) was considered separable if each of its [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725) factors in *K*\[*X*\] is separable in the modern definition.[^o4] In this definition, separability depended on the field *K*; for example, any polynomial over a [perfect field](wikipedia://en/505088) would have been considered separable. This definition, although it can be convenient for [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316), is no longer in use.[^o5]

## Separable field extensions {#Separable_field_extensions}

Separable polynomials are used to define [separable extensions](wikipedia://en/363340): A [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) $K \subset L$ is a separable extension if and only if for every $\alpha$ in $L$ which is [algebraic](wikipedia://en/186291) over $K$, the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106) of $\alpha$ over $K$ is a separable polynomial.

[Inseparable extensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inseparable_extension) (that is, extensions which are not separable) may occur only in positive [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621).

The criterion above leads to the quick conclusion that if *P* is irreducible and not separable, then *D* *P*(*X*) = 0. Thus we must have

*P*(*X*) = *Q*(*X*<sup>*p*</sup>)

for some polynomial *Q* over *K*, where the [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) *p* is the characteristic.

With this clue we can construct an example:

*P*(*X*) = *X*<sup>*p*</sup> − *T*

with *K* the field of [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) in the indeterminate *T* over the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) with *p* elements. Here one can [prove](wikipedia://en/82285) directly that *P*(*X*) is irreducible and not separable. This is actually a typical example of why *inseparability* matters; in geometric terms *P* represents the mapping on the [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578) over the finite field, taking co-ordinates to their *p*th power. Such mappings are fundamental to the [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) of finite fields. Put another way, there are coverings in that setting that cannot be 'seen' by Galois theory. (See [Radical morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_morphism) for a higher-level discussion.)

If *L* is the field extension

*K*(*T*<sup>1/*p*</sup>),

in other words the [splitting field](wikipedia://en/174440) of *P*, then *L*/*K* is an example of a [purely inseparable field extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purely_inseparable_field_extension). It is of degree *p*, but has no [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) fixing *K*, other than the identity, because *T*<sup>1/*p*</sup> is the unique root of *P*. This shows directly that Galois theory must here break down. A field such that there are no such extensions is called *perfect*. That finite fields are perfect follows *a posteriori* from their known structure.

One can show that the [tensor product of fields](wikipedia://en/372240) of *L* with itself over *K* for this example has [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) elements that are non-zero. This is another manifestation of inseparability: that is, the tensor product operation on fields need not produce a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) that is a product of fields (so, not a [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346) [semisimple ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_ring)).

If *P*(*x*) is separable, and its roots form a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) (a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of the field *K*), then *P*(*x*) is an [additive polynomial](wikipedia://en/1374699).

## Applications in Galois theory {#Applications_in_Galois_theory}

Separable polynomials occur frequently in [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316).

For example, let *P* be an irreducible polynomial with [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) and *p* be a prime number which does not divide the leading coefficient of *P*. Let *Q* be the polynomial over the finite field with *p* elements, which is obtained by reducing [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) *p* the coefficients of *P*. Then, if *Q* is separable (which is the case for every *p* but a finite number) then the degrees of the irreducible factors of *Q* are the lengths of the [cycles](wikipedia://en/449166) of some [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) of the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of *P*.

Another example: *P* being as above, a **resolvent** *R* for a group *G* is a polynomial whose coefficients are polynomials in the coefficients of *P*, which provides some information on the Galois group of *P*. More precisely, if *R* is separable and has a [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) root then the Galois group of *P* is contained in *G*. For example, if *D* is the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607) of *P* then $X^2-D$ is a resolvent for the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726). This resolvent is always separable (assuming the characteristic is not 2) if *P* is irreducible, but most resolvents are not always separable.
