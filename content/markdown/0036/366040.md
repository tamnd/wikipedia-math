# Claude Chevalley

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Claude Chevalley"}

**Claude Chevalley** (; 11 February 1909 – 28 June 1984) was a French mathematician who made important contributions to [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), [class field theory](wikipedia://en/242695), [finite group theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_group_theory) and the theory of [algebraic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_groups). He was a founding member of the [Bourbaki](wikipedia://en/167394) group.

:::

## Life {#Life}

His father, Abel Chevalley, was a French diplomat who, jointly with his wife Marguerite Chevalley née [Sabatier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Auguste_Sabatier), wrote *The Concise Oxford French Dictionary*.[^o4] Chevalley graduated from the [École Normale Supérieure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Normale_Supérieure) in 1929, where he studied under [Émile Picard](wikipedia://en/42000). He then spent time at the [University of Hamburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Hamburg), studying under [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351) and at the [University of Marburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Marburg), studying under [Helmut Hasse](wikipedia://en/727118). In Germany, Chevalley discovered Japanese mathematics in the person of [Shokichi Iyanaga](wikipedia://en/18357287). Chevalley was awarded a doctorate in 1933 from the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris) for a thesis on [class field theory](wikipedia://en/242695).

When World War II broke out, Chevalley was at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University). After reporting to the French Embassy, he stayed in the U.S., first at Princeton and then (after 1947) at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University). His American students included [Leon Ehrenpreis](wikipedia://en/14162704) and [Gerhard Hochschild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerhard_Hochschild). During his time in the U.S., Chevalley became an American citizen and wrote a substantial part of his lifetime's output in English.

When Chevalley applied for a chair at the [Sorbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris), the difficulties he encountered were the subject of a polemical piece by his friend and fellow *Bourbakiste* [André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019), titled "Science Française?" and published in the *[Nouvelle Revue Française](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nouvelle_Revue_Française)*. Chevalley was the "professeur B" of the piece, as confirmed in the endnote to the reprint in Weil's collected works, *Oeuvres Scientifiques, tome II*. Chevalley eventually did obtain a position in 1957 at the faculty of sciences of the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris) and after 1970 at the [Université de Paris VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Université_de_Paris_VII).

Chevalley had artistic and political interests, and was a minor member of the French [non-conformists of the 1930s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-conformists_of_the_1930s). The following quote by the co-editor of Chevalley's collected works attests to these interests:

> "Chevalley was a member of various avant-garde groups, both in politics and in the arts... Mathematics was the most important part of his life, but he did not draw any boundary between his mathematics and the rest of his life."[^o5]

## Work {#Work}

In his PhD thesis, Chevalley made an important contribution to the technical development of [class field theory](wikipedia://en/242695), removing a use of [L-functions](wikipedia://en/245283) and replacing it by an algebraic method. At that time use of [group cohomology](wikipedia://en/248635) was implicit, cloaked by the language of [central simple algebras](wikipedia://en/404082). In the introduction to [André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019)'s *Basic Number Theory*, Weil attributed the book's adoption of that path to an unpublished manuscript by Chevalley.

Around 1950, Chevalley wrote a three-volume treatment of [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945). A few years later, he published the work for which he is best remembered, his investigation into what are now called [Chevalley groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevalley_group). Chevalley groups make up 9 of the 18 families of [finite simple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_simple_group).

Chevalley's accurate discussion of integrality conditions in the [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944) of [semisimple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_group) enabled abstracting their theory from the [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) and [complex fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_field). As a consequence, analogues over [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615) could be defined. This was an essential stage in the evolving [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445). After Chevalley's work, the distinction between "classical groups" falling into the [Dynkin diagram](wikipedia://en/277109) classification, and [sporadic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sporadic_groups) which did not, became sharp enough to be useful. What are called 'twisted' groups of the classical families could be fitted into the picture.

"Chevalley's theorem" (also called the [Chevalley–Warning theorem](wikipedia://en/2934314)) usually refers to his result on the solubility of equations over a finite field. Another theorem of his concerns the [constructible sets](wikipedia://en/23234121) in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), i.e. those in the [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959) generated by the [Zariski-open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski-open) and [Zariski-closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski-closed) sets. It states that the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of such a set by a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) of [algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/248808) is of the same type. Logicians call this an [elimination of quantifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elimination_of_quantifiers).

In the 1950s, Chevalley led some Paris seminars of major importance: the *Séminaire Cartan–Chevalley* of the academic year 1955-6, with [Henri Cartan](wikipedia://en/362536) and the *Séminaire Chevalley* of 1956-7 and 1957-8. These dealt with topics on [algebraic groups](wikipedia://en/247924) and the foundations of algebraic geometry, as well as pure [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384). The Cartan–Chevalley seminar was the genesis of [scheme theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_theory), but its subsequent development in the hands of [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) was so rapid, thorough and inclusive that its historical tracks can appear well covered. Grothendieck's work subsumed the more specialised contribution of [Serre](wikipedia://en/205483), Chevalley, [Gorō Shimura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gorō_Shimura) and others such as [Erich Kähler](wikipedia://en/383501) and [Masayoshi Nagata](wikipedia://en/3086117).

## Recognition {#Recognition}

The American Mathematical Society established the [Chevalley Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevalley_Prize) in Lie Theory[^o8] in 2014, with the first recipient being [Geordie Williamson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geordie_Williamson) in 2016.
