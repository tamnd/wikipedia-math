# Barhebraeus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Barhebraeus"}

**Gregory Barhebraeus** or **Bar Hebraeus** (ܓܪܝܓܘܪܝܘܣ ܒܪ ܥܒܪܝܐ; 1226 – 30 July 1286), also known as **Abu al-Faraj** and in Latin, **Abulpharagius**, was the [maphrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maphrian) [Catholicos of the East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicos_of_the_East) (regional primate) of the [Catholicate of the East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicate_of_the_East) under the [Syriac Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox_Church) from 1264 until his death in 1286. He is recognised as one of the most accomplished and multifaceted academics of the medieval [Syriac Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Christian) world, with important contributions to the fields of theology, philosophy, history, linguistics, medicine, and the natural sciences.

:::

Barhebraeus was born in Melitene (modern-day [Malatya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malatya)) during the [Seljuk Sultanate of Rum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuk_Sultanate_of_Rum). He experienced the shifting borders of the early [Mongol era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_Empire), [Ayyubid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayyubid) rule, and [Crusader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades) dominions. Barhebraeus's early education in medicine and logic was influenced by his father Aaron's experience serving in the upper echelons of the Mongol armies as a physician and [deacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deacon). Later in life, he was ordained bishop and soon elevated to maphrian, under which he travelled across the Middle East, engaged in scholarship, and sought to support his community through the difficult 13th-century period.

The monumental chronicle, a universal history written in [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language) and translated into [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) under the title *Tārīkh Mukhtaṣar al-Duwal*, was part of Barhebraeus's vast body of work. In addition, he produced important theological and ascetic treatises, medical books, grammars, and encyclopaedic works like the "Cream of Wisdom" (*Hewath Hekhemtho*). Barhebraeus's writings were read by a wide range of intellectual circles outside of his Syriac Orthodox community, including Christians of different denominations, Muslim scholars, Latin orientalists, and later found in academic archives in Europe.

The immense scholarship brought forth by Barhebraeus revitalised [Syriac literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_literature) at a time when it was in decline and bridged Christian, Islamic, and classical traditions, which earned him epithets such as the "Ocean of Wisdom", "Light of East and West", and "King of Learned Men". He is commemorated with great honour as a saint in the [Oriental Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Orthodox_Church) and especially in the Syriac Orthodox tradition where his feast is celebrated on 30 July, the date of his repose, and his relics at [Mor Mattai Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mor_Mattai_Monastery) remain a popular pilgrimage site.

## Name {#Name}

The name *Barhebraeus* (ܒܪ ܥܒܪܝܐ‎) literally means 'Son of the Hebrew', and although the phrase might suggest Jewish ancestry, this interpretation is almost unanimously rejected by modern scholars.[^n1] There is no evidence of Jewish elements in his writings and his family background was Christian, noble, and clerical; his father, Aaron, was a deacon—whose name, despite its Hebrew origin, was common among Christians of the region and period.[^n2][^n3]

Barhebraeus himself addressed the misunderstanding in a short epigram:

> If the Lord called himself a [Samaritan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaritans), do not be ashamed when they call you *Bar ʿEbrāyā*. For the name has to do with the [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates) and with the river, not with the false religion or with the language.[^n4]

Patriarch [Ignatius Aphrem I Barsoum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignatius_Aphrem_I_Barsoum) interpreted the epithet as originating from the circumstances of his birth, that his mother gave birth to him while crossing the Euphrates, the Syriac word *ʿbar* also meaning 'crossing'. Modern scholars, however, associate it with a village named *ʿEbra* (modern-day [Elazığ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elazığ)) near Melitene, situated on the banks of the Euphrates River.[^n1][^n5]

His Arabic name Abu'l-Faraj appears in his own usage for the first time on the eve of his episcopal ordination, which means that Gregory (*Grigorios*) was his episcopal name, while Abu'l-Faraj was his earlier personal designation.[^n6]

[E. W. Budge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._A._Wallis_Budge) states that Barhebraeus was given the baptismal name John (ܝܘܚܢܢ),[^n3] but modern scholarship suggests that this might be the result of confusion with Gregory John of Bartelli. Still, the inscription on his grave at [Mor Mattai Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mor_Mattai_Monastery) reads: "This is the grave of Mar Gregory John, and of Mar Bar Sawma, his brother, the children of the Hebrew \[plural of *Bar ʿEbrāyā*\] on [Mount Elpeph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Alfaf)".[^n7][^n8]

## Life {#Life}

Barhebraeus was born in 1225 or 1226 AD under the rule of the [Rum Seljuks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Rum) and lived through a succession of regimes including the [Crusaders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusaders), the [Ayyubids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayyubids), and later the [Mongols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_Empire). He was a native of [Melitene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melitene) (modern-day Malatya), then a major metropolitan and cultural centre of the Syriac Orthodox Church.[^n9]

His father, Aaron (هارون بن توما الملطي)[^n10] was both a deacon and a renowned physician, known to have treated the Mongol general [Yasaʾur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasa'ur) during the siege of Melitene. This profession placed the family among the patrician class, to which his mother also belonged.[^n11] He had at least one brother Safi (often called Barsauma) and probably three more named Michael, Muwaffaq, and Quphar. Barsauma's monastic name was later changed to Gregory upon ordination.[^n12]

The family remained in Melitene until 1242–1243. After Aaron's medical service to Yasaʾur, they moved to [Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antioch), where Barhebraeus continued his studies, and at around seventeen years of age, he became a monk and began to live as a [hermit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermit) upon ordination by Patriarch Ignatius III David.[^n6][^n13] Influenced by his father, Barhebraeus left Antioch and travelled to [Tripoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_Tripoli), a [Crusader state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusader_state) at the time, to begin his study of medicine[^n14] alongside Saliba bar Jacob Wajih of Edessa—the future Maphrian Ignatius IV (1253–1258).[^n15] His expertise eventually secured him the position of personal physician to [Hulagu Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulagu_Khan), which was a remarkable achievement.[^n16] He later undertook practical training in Damascus under Jamal al-Din ibn al-Rahbi al-Dimashqi at the Nur al-Din Hospital.[^n17]

### Episcopate {#Episcopate}

Barhebraeus was first ordained bishop of [Gubbos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gubos_%28West_Syriac_diocese%29) by Patriarch [Ignatius David III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignatius_III_David),[^n13] and soon after transferred to [Laqabin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laqabin_%28West_Syriac_diocese%29), both near Melitene,[^n18] where he was ordained maphrian.[^n19] He supported Dionysius VII ʿAngur (1252) in his conflict with [John XIII bar Maʿdani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_XIII_bar_Ma'dani) (1232; 1252). As a reward, Dionysius appointed him bishop of [Aleppo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleppo).[^n20] His tenure was short-lived, however, as his former colleague Saliba bar Jacob Wajih (now Maphrian Ignatius IV) sided with John bar Maʿdani and succeeded in having him expelled. Barhebraeus briefly resided with his father in Aleppo, then moved to [Mar Barsawma Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mor_Bar_Sauma_Monastery), where Dionysius resided.[^n21] In 1258 he travelled to [Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus) to secure reinstatement for himself and Dionysius, aided by the Melitene-born physician Qir Michael bar Gabras. He was present in Aleppo in January 1260 when the Mongols invaded; his attempt to spare his people, in vain, led to temporary detainment in [Qalʿat al-Najm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qal'at_Najm). He was eventually released and reconciled with John bar Maʿdani.[^n21]

Unlike earlier maphrians who had resided in the Levant to escape persecution, thereby neglecting their home dioceses, Barhebraeus chose to live among his flock in [Tagrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagrit) and at times in the nearby Monastery of Mor Mattai, even amidst the violent persecutions under the [Mongols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongols) and [Mamluks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamluks) in the thirteenth century.[^n22]

From 1260 to 1264 Barhebraeus served at the Mongol court as personal physician to [Hulagu Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulegu_Khan) before being elected maphrian by [Ignatius IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignatius_IV_of_Antioch) at [Sis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sis_%28ancient_city%29) in [Cilicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cilicia) on 19/20 January 1264.[^n23] The ceremony was grand, attended by King [Hethum II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hethum_I), his brothers and sons, [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Apostolic_Church) high priests, and dignitaries from many nations and faiths, along with a large assembly of Syriac Orthodox bishops and laymen.[^n3] Afterwards, he travelled widely throughout [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia) and [Iranian Azerbaijan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Azerbaijan), particularly between [Nineveh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineveh_region) (where he probably stayed at Mor Mattai Monastery) and the cities of [Tabriz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabriz) and [Maragha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maragheh), centres of the [Ilkhanid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilkhanid) court and learning.[^n24]

### Death {#Death}

Barhebraeus wrote in one poem: "O' net of the world, in 1226 your trap caught me, and I think in 1286 I will not be in you". In 1286, he travelled to Tabriz and completed an Arabic translation of the Syriac work *The Political History of the World* within thirty days.[^n3]

Soon after, in Maragha, he fell ill with a fever two days before his death and, as he predicted, died on the night of Tuesday, 30 July, 1286, in the presence of his brother Barsauma and many others. A grand funeral was held in Maragha, and Catholicos [Yahballaha III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahballaha_III) (1282–1317) of the Church of the East declared a public day of mourning attended by Christians of various denominations. His remains were interred beneath an altar in the Maragha church and later translated to Mor Mattai Monastery, where they are kept today alongside his brother's.[^n3][^n25][^n26]

#### Legacy {#Legacy}

He was ordained monk in 1244, bishop in 1246 (taking the name Gregory), and maphrian on 19 January 1264. He founded two churches, two monasteries, two episcopal residences, and a guesthouse for travellers.[^n27] During his tenure, he consecrated twelve bishops, including his biographer and disciple Dioscorus of Gozarto and Philoxenus I Nemrud (later Patriarch, 1283–1292), respectively.[^n26]

One of the most distinguished holders of the title of [Maphrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maphrian) was Barhebraeus. He spent most of his life, including all his later years, within Mongol territory until his death in 1286. While based mainly in [Nineveh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineveh_region) and the environs of [Mosul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosul) and Mor Mattai Monastery, he maintained friendships with scholars of diverse faiths and frequently travelled to major learning centres, most importantly Maragha in Iranian Azerbaijan, then-capital of the Mongol khanate. His close ties with Mongols and other communities enabled him to provide detailed first‑hand accounts, which is why the events of the 13th century are so well documented in both his secular and ecclesiastical chronicles.[^n28]

Barhebraeus also highlights the crucial part Syriac scholars played in bringing Greek philosophy to the Arab world in his reflections on intellectual history. He places Syriac scholarship as an important bridge in the larger evolution of medieval intellectual culture, attributing the medieval Arab interest in philosophy and the sciences to the translation efforts of earlier Syriac writers.[^n29]

## Writings {#Writings}

*Image: Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, MS Or. 83, fol. 32r.png.* A page from a copy of the *Hewath Hekhemtho,* 1340

Barhebraeus was among the most prolific writers of the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), with his surviving corpus being vast and diverse, spanning theology, philosophy, history, medicine, grammar, astronomy, and the natural sciences. His brother Barsauma reports a total of 31 works by him, though his disciple Dioscorus of Gozarto and various manuscripts have variations on this total.[^n30] His writings were so extensive and refined that [J. S. Assemani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._S._Assemani) called them "easily the chief of the writings of the [Jacobites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobite_Syriac_Orthodox_Church)" and [J. P. Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Pol_Martin) described him as "one of the most prolific writers to have ever emerged". [Jean Maurice Fiey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Maurice_Fiey) said that while Barhebraeus was "often just a populariser", he was a "genius populariser, and one can only admire the amount of work he has produced, especially when considering the often difficult circumstances in which he had to operate".[^n31]

Barhebraeus's corpus reflects the full breadth of intellectual traditions available in his time. Göttsberger compared him with [Ephrem the Syrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephrem_the_Syrian) and [Jacob of Edessa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_of_Edessa) as the third great pillar of Syriac intellectual history—Ephrem representing pure Syriac thought, Jacob Greek influence, and Barhebraeus the synthesis of Arabic and Islamic science and philosophy.[^n32] His works display familiarity with Islamic scholarship by drawing from [Ibn Sina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Sina), [al-Ghazali](wikipedia://en/146607), and [al-Abhari](wikipedia://en/1740825).[^n33]

Barhebraeus's writings circulated widely. They were found in virtually every Syriac-speaking region to the extent that any single library might contain up to 10 per cent of its manuscripts authored by him. His writings were translated into Arabic and cited as a major authority. They were read across confessional line by the Church of the East, the [Maronites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronites), the [Coptic Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_Orthodox), and the [Melkites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melkites); they were read even by Muslims, especially his medical works.[^n34] His texts also reached Europe where early [Orientalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientalists) and [Syriacists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriacists) studied them extensively.[^n35]

Barhebraeus's works largely consist of compendia that synthesise material from older Syriac and more recent Arabo‑Persian literature. He was previously castigated as a skilful but unoriginal compiler of earlier works, but that judgement is misplaced, as his originality lies in his choice of sources and his openness to knowledge found in Islamic and non‑Orthodox texts. His writings remained standard across [Syriac Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Christianity) including its [Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_East), [Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_orthodox) and [Maronite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronite_church) branches. They were translated into Arabic mainly by Daniel of Mardin (1326/27 – after 1382) and Grigorios Yuhanna bin al-Ghurayr al-Zurbabi (bishop of Damascus, 1668–1684).[^n26]

### Chronography {#Chronography}

Barhebraeus's most celebrated work is his chronicle (ܡܟܬܒܢܘܬ ܙܒܢܐ), the largest piece of classical [Syriac historiography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_literature) ever written, no later work surpassing it in scale.[^n36] The chronicle consists of two complementary parts, a *Universal History* and an *Ecclesiastical History*. The *Universal History* (also known as the *Chronicon Syriacum*) covers world events from [Creation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creation_in_genesis) to Barhebraeus's own era and was translated into Arabic as *Tārīkh Mukhtaṣar al-Duwal*, adapted for a Muslim audience.[^n37] His brother Barsauma addended it to 1297, while Priest Addai of Basibrina continued the *Ecclesiastical History* to 1496.[^n36] It runs from Creation to his death in 1286. Though intended to instruct "his people" (the Syriac Orthodox), Barhebraeus knew it would reach a wide readership, which is why he supplemented the work with material of general interest while retaining scholarly sense for specialists.[^n38]

The chronicle is divided into eleven dynastic sections (*yubbālē*), following the model of the [Book of Daniel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Daniel), with the final section devoted to the contemporary Mongols (whom Barhebraeus calls '[Huns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huns)'). The second part was translated into Latin in 1789 by Paul Jakob Bruns and Georg Wilhelm Kirsch, and a superior edition was produced by [Paul Bedjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Bedjan) in 1890 from the Syriac. [Budge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EA_Wallis_Budge) published an English translation in 1932 based on Bedjan's French with a [facsimile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facsimile) of the Syriac original from the [Bodleian Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodleian_Library). Although the secular sections were extensively used by medieval historians, neither part has been the subject of major analysis.[^n36]

It treats history, religion, language, and the customs of peoples; includes biographies of notable warriors and physicians; describes battles, sieges and the capture of cities; records comets and other unusual celestial phenomena; and notes earthquakes, famines, heavy snowfall, and the freezing of the [Tigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris) and [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates), with the associated food prices in times of scarcity. He also recounts court scandals, repeats various rumours, and relates 'laughable stories'.[^n39]

Structurally, Barhebraeus adopts and innovates upon the chronological tables of earlier historians like [Eusebius of Caesarea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eusebius_of_Caesarea) and [Michael the Syrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_the_Syrian), replacing them with successions of rulers and patriarchs, with expansions to incorporate Muslim, Persian and Armenian sources while diminishing the [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) focus and reflecting the multicultural milieu of the thirteenth century.[^n40] This arrangement separated secular and ecclesiastical history, preventing the reader from easily associating secular events with their corresponding ecclesiastical ones and forcing them to cross‑reference manually or rely on memory.[^n41] Early section begins with "After such-and-such, another such-and-such",[^n42] but it later changes, becoming "After the persecution of the orthodox \[i.e., [Miaphysites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miaphysites)\] of the East, [Ahudhemmeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahudemmeh) became the metropolitan of the East".[^n43]

Within the ecclesiastical portion, the lemmata for Jewish hierarchs begin with [Aaron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaron) (not [Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses), since Aaron was the [true priest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Priest_of_Israel)), followed by his son [Phinehas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phinehas), and continue through to [Caiaphas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caiaphas) and [Annas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annas). After this, the old high‑priesthood 'disappeared' and was superseded by [Jesus Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_Christ)'s, with [Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_peter) as the new hierarch. Afterwards, he traces the patriarchs of [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_of_Alexandria), [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_of_Constantinople), [Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox_Archbishop_of_Jerusalem), [Ephesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Ephesus), and [Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_of_Antioch), and then continues only as Antioch, since his "eastern lands are subject to the authority of the throne of Antioch".[^n44]

Barhebraeus records various [Church Fathers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Fathers) such as [Athanasius of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athanasius_of_Alexandria) and Ephrem the Syrian, in addition to heretics like [Arius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arius) and [Apollinaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollinaris_of_Laodicea). He discusses the [Chalcedonian schism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcedonian_schism), noting that Justin deposed [Severus of Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severus_of_Antioch) using military force and that his successor, the 'excellent' [Sergius of Tella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergius_of_Tella), was ordained by John of Anazarbus (in fact, [Jacob Baradeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Baradeus) performed the ordination). He mentions Jacob Baradeus's efforts across the East to preserve the Syriac Orthodox hierarchy by ordaining many bishops, which led to the Church bearing his name. The list of patriarchs continues to Barhebraeus's time with addenda by anonymous authors.[^n45]

The apostolic foundation by [Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_the_apostle), [Addai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Addai), [Aggai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Aggai), [Mari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Mari), and [Abrosius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrosius) is described in the *Ecclesiastical History*'s 'Eastern' section, where the evangelism of Thomas, the 'high priest of the East', and the translation of his relics to Edessa, are described. Barhebraeus then discusses the line of Church of the East patriarchs with notable ecumenical sympathy;[^n46] still, he views the maphrianate of the Syriac Orthodox in the East as the rightful continuation of the ancient see of Seleucia-Ctesiphon,[^n47] where the ecclesiastical succession of that see, following Isho'yabh, continues through Syriac Orthodox maphrians, culminating with Barhebraeus himself. The lineage extends through his brother, Barsauma, whose pontificate was documented by an anonymous author writing after his time.[^n43]

#### Arabic version {#Arabic_version}

*Image: Cavalry during the Assyrian Genocide.jpg.* The persecution of [Assyrian Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_Christians) in Mesopotamia has been rampant throughout history, peaking with the [Seyfo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seyfo) genocide in 1915; [ongoing oppression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Assyrian_sentiment) continues to affect this community

Barhebraeus's *Chronicon Syriacum* was purposefully modified for Arabic in *Tārīkh Mukhtaṣar al-Duwal* to suit his target audience. For instance, he omitted accounts of Arab persecutions, softened Christian-Mongol connections, used the [Hijri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_calendar) calendar exclusively, and replaced overtly Christian expressions with inclusive wording. Both the Syriac and Arabic versions exhibit linguistic and ideological variations that signify Barhebraeus's purpose to tailor the work for different audiences, as the Arabic version carefully adjusts the text to accommodate Muslim ears.

A more accurate translation of the Arabic recension would be "*The Abridged Histories of the States or Empires*", although many contemporary scholars have translated this as "*The Abridged History of the Dynasties*". This is because the Arabic word *al-duwal* (plural of *dawla*) literally means 'states' or 'political powers', while Barhebraeus frequently used *ansāb* to refer to 'dynasties'. An 18th-century [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_translations_of_the_12th_century) translation that used the word dynastiarum to convey the idea of political succession is probably what gave rise to the popular translation 'dynasties'.[^n48]

Though their emphases differ, the sources underpinning both texts are essentially the same. The chronicle of Michael the Syrian, East-Syriac historical writings, various Arabic and Persian material—particularly [Juvayni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ata-Malik_Juvayni)'s *Tārīkh-i Jahāngushāy*, which Barhebraeus probably encountered while at Maragha—and potential [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_literature) sources constitute the primary sources of inspiration.[^n49]

In descriptions of the terminology employed, the Syriac edition applies the title '[Caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliph)' to multiple Muslim rulers, including the [Fatimids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimids), while the Arabic work reserves this title exclusively for the [Abbasid Caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliph), aligning with mainstream Islamic convention of Barhebraeus's time and region.[^n50] Omitted are accounts describing the [persecution of Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_Christians#Abbasid_Caliphate) at Tagrit by Arab forces and details regarding Christians taking refuge in a church before their liberation by two Armenians. Additionally omitted is the use of explicitly Christian formulations like referring to Hulagu's wife as "the believing queen and lover of Christ", and all uses of the [Greek calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_calendar).[^n51]

Further on, the Arabic chronicle also shortens or softens depictions of Muslim aggression toward Christians, such as by condensing passages describing the plundering of the Monastery of the Sisters of Beth Kūdida, the [months-long siege of Mor Mattai Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mor_Mattai_Monastery#12th–16th_centuries) by [Kurds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurds) with looting of the monastery's valuables by them and their altercations with the monks, the murder of Christians at the Habsusyotho monastery, and the killing of ʿAlam al-Din Sanjar and his Kurdish allies.[^n52]

Both versions praise Hulagu's successor [Kublai Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kublai_Khan), but the Arabic version omits the phrase "lover of Christians" in favour of "lover of all religious people from all confessions and nations", with various descriptions admiring the Mongol rulers.[^n53] Additionally, in further demonstration of the good relations between Christians and Mongols, the Arabic text excises a passage about a Syriac priest rising to a high-ranking position as a physician in Hulagu's palace.[^n54]

While both recount stories of Crusaders in the Middle East, the Arabic version specifies the Crusaders as "Franks", while the Syriac version refers to everyone as "Christians" regardless of denomination or political position.[^n55]

### Theology {#Theology}

*Image: Syriac MS 7. Unknown Date, by Barhebraeus.jpg.* Syriac MS 7 by Barhebraeus from "The Lamp of the Sanctuaries" (*Mnorath Qudshe*), a theological work; the damaged right page (likely from candlelight) depicts a semicircular world map

Barhebraeus remained steadfast in his adherence to [Miaphysite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miaphysite) Christology even as he adopted an approach characterised by intellectual openness and ecumenical awareness. In his large theological summa, he devotes several sections to [Christology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christology). He offers an explanation and a refutation of the [Dyophysite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyophysite) doctrine and its arguments, but does not mention the Crusaders in that context. In some passages, he treats these theological differences as substantive rather than merely semantic; for example, he reproaches a bishop who dismissed a Nestorian's theological objections as sophistry and supplies the responses the bishop should have given. The book ends with a list of thirty heresies, most of which are Christological in nature, but it noticeably leaves out the [Chalcedonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcedonian_schism) (including the Latins) and [Nestorians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nestorian_schism), categorising their differences as mainly terminological rather than substantive. This contrasts with the preceding chapter where Miaphysitism is strongly affirmed, but aligns with his other works that display an open ecumenical attitude, including those that place less emphasis on dogma and more on spiritual matters.[^n56]

Among Barhebraeus's theological works are the "Book of the Dove", which emphasises ascetic living, and the *Ethikon*, which provides monks, priests and laypeople with moral and spiritual guidance. In the *Ethikon*, he cautions against disputation as verbal cunning intended to subdue an adversary lest it causes one to sin, and counsels ascetics to abstain from speculative discussions about "natures and persons", referring to Christological debates.[^n57]

*Image: Book of the Dove WDL7076.pdf.* A page from "The Book of the Dove" (*Kthobo d-Yawno*), detailing monastic life and is an important work of [mystical theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystical_theology)

Barhebraeus has been described as a "supporter of the validity of a certain theological pluralism".[^n58] Using the framework of Greek [patristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patristic) and [philosophical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_philosophical_tradition) thought, Barhebraeus discussed traditional [Christological issues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nestorian_schism) in his theological correspondence with Catholicos [Denha I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denha_I). Notwithstanding their differences in doctrine, Barhebraeus urged Denha to look beyond the rifts that had long divided their churches as a result of the disputes between [Nestorius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nestorius) and [Cyril](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyril_of_Alexandria). His historical writings draw heavily on the East‑Syrian Chronicle tradition, which demonstrates his willingness to engage with traditions outside his own communion and his scholarly respect for them.[^n59]

Scholars differ in interpreting the motivation behind Barhebraeus's ecumenical outlook. Herman Teule considers it a sincere expression of theological fraternity and genuine openness toward other Christian denominations; on the other hand, Hidemi Takahashi sees it as pragmatic to a degree and meant to improve the Christian community's social and political standing during Mongol rule—though not devoid of genuine elements of goodwill and conciliation.[^n60]

Barhebraeus's works, while rooted in the Syriac literary and poetic tradition, also show a significant interest in Greek philosophy, especially Aristotle's ideas. This synthesis is most clearly reflected in his encyclopaedic work "The Cream of Wisdom" (*Hewath Hekhmetho*).[^n61]

### Medicine {#Medicine}

Barhebraeus's initial path in life appears to have been set toward [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world) rather than theology. He was remembered as first "the father of physicians" and *then* "the glory of pastors" in a manuscript colophon, showing the dual nature of his vocation but emphasising his role as a healer.[^n62] The fact that his father was a Mongol general's personal physician probably contributed to his early academic direction. Barhebraeus himself pursued formal studies in 'medicine and logic' under a Nestorian teacher in Tripoli, and later worked with the physician Jamal al-Din in Damascus. By 1263, records place him working within [Mongol military camps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasions_of_the_Levant) alongside other physicians. Barhebraeus remained steadfast in his commitment to the medical sciences even after being promoted to the high ecclesiastical office of maphrian, in an effort to pass on medical knowledge to future generations.[^n63]

Eight works on [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine) and [pharmacology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacology) are known to be by him. These treatises, which avoid the elaborate or poetic style that permeates much of his literary output, have simple, descriptive titles, in contrast with many of his other works with more ornate titles. Four of these works were written in Arabic and the rest in Syriac.[^n64]

Barhebraeus held that the medical profession offered Christians a means of attaining influence and respect within the largely Muslim society of his day, and perhaps this motivated him to compose and disseminate his medical works among [Syriac Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Christians).[^n65] Nevertheless, his writings were not intended solely for that community; Barhebraeus records using the Hijri dating system himself and composed some works in Arabic at the request of Muslims (*ṭayyāyē*) in Maragha, while his openings include an Islamic [basmala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basmala) with references to [Christian Apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostles_in_the_New_Testament) phrased so as to suit a Muslim audience. Thus, Barhebraeus's works transcend the needs of a single religious community in favour of the greater good.[^n66]

### Astronomy {#Astronomy}

*Image: Maragha Observatory.jpg.* The [Maragha Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maragheh_observatory) in [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran)

Besides his knowledge of history and theology, Barhebraeus was also a skilled mathematician and astronomer. [Nasir al-Din al-Tusi](wikipedia://en/33642424) and other researchers at the [Maragha Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maragheh_observatory) had a major influence on his primary scientific work, "Ascent of the Mind" (*Sullaqā Hawnānāyā*), which he wrote in 1279 and used as a textbook of mathematics and astronomy. It was partially based on al-Tusi's *Tadhkira fī ʿIlm al-Hayʾa*, but it also included Syriac scientific traditions, especially those of [Severus Sebokht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severus_Sebokht), whom Barhebraeus specifically mentions. Barhebraeus's command of Arabo-Persian astronomical literature is evident in other related works such as "On Creation" and "Candelabrum of the Sanctuary" (*Mnārath Qudshē*), which also reference [al-Biruni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Biruni)'s *Kitāb al-Tafhīm li-Awāʾil Ṣināʿat al-Tanjīm*.[^n67]

### Identity and geography {#Identity_and_geography}

The identity and language of the Syriac Orthodox and other Syriac-speaking communities took shape largely in contrast with the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greeks) and [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab) populations, and were increasingly influenced by contact with the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latins) West where [notions of national identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism) were already established. Such awareness was reinforced by the composition of Syriac-language chronicles that went beyond mere record-keeping to include reflection on communal origins and identity,[^n68] such as [Michael the Great's *Chronicle*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_the_Syrian#Identity_and_geography).[^n69]

Barhebraeus, along with the Syriac Orthodox people of his time, did not identify as 'Arameans' and rejected the term as a self-designation,[^n69] even entirely omitting it from the genealogy of Noah's sons.[^n70] He instead speaks of "ancient Syrians" (‏ܣܘܖ̈ܝܝܐ ܥܬܝܩ̈ܐ), a term collectively used for all ancient [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic) speakers i.e., the [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Assyrians), [Babylonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonians), and [Arameans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arameans).[^n71] Among intellectual [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrians) as Barhebraeus, the usage of 'Assyria' did not disappear after [Islamic conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_conquests), and he frequently mentioned [Assyria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_homeland) (ܐܬܘܪ) as a contemporary geopolitical designation, clearly differentiating it from [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria_%28region%29) (a distinction also observed by the anonymous author known as pseudo-Barhebraeus), which indicates that the names 'Assyria' and 'Assyrian' remained active designations for Syriac-speaking Mesopotamians and their homeland.[^n72] He used '[Chaldeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaldea)' as an umbrella term for the Assyrians and Babylonians whom he equated with the ancient Syrians,[^n69] and also described the kings of ancient Mesopotamia as "All these are Chaldeans; that is, Syrians (*Suryāyē*)".[^n73] Nevertheless, he denounced 'Chaldeanhood' (*Kaldōyūtō*) as "barbaric".[^n74]

Generally, 'Syrian' is used inclusively for all Aramaic‑speaking Christians, including those in [Mount Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Lebanon),[^n75] regardless of precise ethnic origin, although occasional distinctions are made.[^n76]

*Image: Syriac World Map by BarHebraeus.jpg.* Syriac world map by Barhebraeus, depicting the eastern hemisphere with the north being at the bottom following Islamic [cartographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartographic) tradition

Geographically, since Barhebraeus and his audience were mainly located in the east, his preference for [Tagrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagrit) over [Edessa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edessa) becomes evident. Edessa, while still honoured, appears far less frequently in his writings than [Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad) and [Mosul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosul); Tagrit, however, held supreme importance for him—it was the first city mentioned in his *Chronicon*.[^n77] When recounting the missionary route of [Thomas the Apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_the_Apostle), he lists Tagrit, then [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_India), and only afterwards does he return to Edessa and its king [Abgar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abgar_V), which highlights the spiritual significance of Tagrit.[^n78] He saw Thomas as linked to Tagrit, which he hailed as the defender of orthodoxy during the spread of [Nestorianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nestorianism) in the East. Tagrit and [Tur ‘Abdin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tur_Abdin), he wrote, remained steadfast against the persecutions initiated by [Barsauma of Nisibis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barsauma_of_Nisibis).[^n78] Consequently, Barhebraeus portrayed Tagrit as the true successor to the See of [Seleucia-Ctesiphon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucia-Ctesiphon), in contrast to the Church of the East whose catholicos had been allowed by the caliphs to move to Baghdad, whereas the Tagritian maphrian was not. Thus, Tagrit came to surpass even Edessa and Baghdad in honour.[^n79]

Barhebraeus rarely used the term 'Jacobite' (after [Jacob Baradaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Baradaeus)) except when quoting adversaries who used it, or when referring to certain localised, Arabised communities of the Syriac Orthodox Church.[^n80] Instead, he preferred expressions such as "our people", "the faithful", and "the [orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodoxy)" to denote his own religious community in opposition to the [Chalcedonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcedonian) Greeks and the [Nestorians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nestorians). The broader term "Christians" he reserved for Christendom as a whole going beyond denominational divisions.[^n81]

Despite his insistence on [Miaphysitism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miaphysitism) and his position as the heir of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, he maintained cordial, fraternal relations with the Church of the East and its patriarch.[^n82] He deliberately used the term "Easterners" for Christians in both the Syriac Orthodox Church and the Church of the East, adopting a single self‑designation for both milieus and emphasising the shared heritage of the East.[^n71]

### Relations {#Relations}

Like the Syriac Orthodox Church in general, Barhebraeus maintained good relations with diverse religious and intellectual communities, such as the [Crusaders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades). He probably encountered the Latins during his residence in Antioch and Tripoli (both under Crusader rule) and later continued his visits to the "quasi-Crusader" state of Cilicia after the Crusaders' influence waned.[^n83] His familiarity with Latin circles led some scholars to suggest that his thought may have been subtly influenced by Roman Catholic [scholasticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism), but this influence was never dominant.[^n84] Other scholars do not consider him to be aware of these Latin works despite spending time in Crusader‑held Antioch, Tripoli and Cilicia.[^n85]

Disillusioned by the Arabs and their conduct, Barhebraeus may have viewed the Mongols as liberators from oppressive Islamic forces, his writings suggesting a pragmatic appreciation of the Mongols' relative tolerance towards Christianity. In this interpretation, he perceived Hulagu as a kind of new [Cyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_the_Great), freeing God's people from "Babylon", or as a new [Constantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_the_Great) through likening Hulagu's Christian mother, [Sorghoqtani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghaghtani_Beki), to [Empress Helena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empress_Helena). The Mongol rulers' comparative tolerance towards Christians inspired hopes that a Christian polity might arise under their protection with Syriac as its language.[^n86]

Within the broader intellectual sphere, Barhebraeus has frequently been paralleled with major European thinkers of the Greek and Latin traditions, described as the Eastern/Syriac counterpart to figures such as the near‑contemporary [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas)—his *Candelabrum of the Sanctuary* likened to Aquinas's *[Summa Theologica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_Theologica)*. Other comparisons were made to [Albertus Magnus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albertus_Magnus), [Pico della Mirandola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pico_della_Mirandola), [Isidore of Seville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidore_of_Seville), and [Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero).[^n87]

*Image: Ecumenical Memorial Plaque at Cologne Cathedral.jpg.* Ecumenical Memorial Plaque at [Cologne Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne_Cathedral), depicting two clergymen exchanging symbols of unity

Throughout his life, Barhebraeus maintained genial relations with the [Church of the East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_East). While sometimes described as an [ecumenist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenist), this is an overstatement, as he remained a firm [Miaphysite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miaphysite) and did not advocate doctrinal union.[^n88] Nevertheless, he expressed fraternal sentiments towards East Syrians and hoped for the restoration of a unified [Syriac Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Christianity) rooted in the ancient [Edessan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osroene) tradition. His familiarity with East Syrian scholars and contact with Latin Christians (who at the time had achieved communion with the [Maronites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronites)) may have influenced this outlook. He corresponded courteously with Catholicoi [Makkika II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makkikha_II) (1257–1265) and [Denha I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denha_I) (1265–1281), criticising their [Christological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christological) formulations but emphasising shared [Nicene faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicene_Christianity). He even cited Church of the East commentaries as authoritative in his *[Nomocanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomocanon)*, and clergy from the Church of the East attended his consecration of the chrism in Baghdad in 1265. His belief that disputes were merely terminological rather than doctrinal has served as a model for the current [ecumenical dialogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical_dialogue) between churches today. Upon his death, Catholicos [Yahbalaha III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahballaha_III) (1282–1317) declared a public day of mourning in Maragha attended by "[Nestorians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nestorian_Church), [Armenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_church), and [Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Patriarchate_of_Antioch)".[^n88]

Barhebraeus's intellectual vision embraced both the [East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Syriac_Rite) and [West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Syriac_Rite) Syriac traditions which he distinguished as "two illustrious traditions". His writings display a strong familiarity with East-Syriac vocalisation and pronunciation systems, and he frequently cited [Church Fathers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Fathers) of the Church of the East such as [Narsai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narsai) and [Theodore of Mopsuestia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_of_Mopsuestia), whom his own Church formally regards as heretical. Overall, his attitude towards these figures and their traditions was one of generous appreciation and respect.[^n89] Barhebraeus also saw that unity among Syriac Christians was critical to gaining the favour of the Mongols, who were already inclined towards Christianity.[^n90]

### Linguistics {#Linguistics}

*Image: Syriac MS 11. 1699? by Barhebraeus.jpg.* Syriac MS 11. (1699) by Barhebraeus, from his *Nomocanon* or "Book of Directions" (*Kthobo d-Hudoye*)

Apart from his native [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language), Barhebraeus was proficient in [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_language), [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) and [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language).[^n91] While some scholars have hypothesised the possibility of [Turkic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkic_languages) or [Mongolian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongolian_language) fluency, and have even mentioned that he probably understood something of these languages, he was scarcely proficient in either and wrote in neither.[^n92] The notion of slight understanding of [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) has been suggested, but it seems unlikely, as by Barhebraeus's time, Greek was in disuse in nearby regions and the liturgy was already wholly conducted in Arabic among the [Melkites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melkites). Moreover, his writings bear no trace of Greek linguistic influence, for extant Syriac translations of Greek works such as Aristotle and the [Septuagint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septuagint) had sufficed.[^n93]

Barhebraeus considered the [Edessan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edessa) dialect (today termed 'Classical Syriac') the most eloquent of the Aramaic varieties,[^n94] while he disapproved of the dialect of the "Easterners" found in the Aramaic-language [Talmud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talmud) (the [Targum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Targum)), features of which persist in modern [Neo‑Aramaic dialects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Aramaic_dialects).[^n95] He regarded the Aramaic of the [Kashkar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashkar) region (i.e., [Nabatean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataeans_of_Iraq) in Babylonia), on the shore of the [Tigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris) in southern [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq), as the worst of all.[^n96] Like most early and medieval Christians of the time, Barhebraeus believed that [Syriac Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Aramaic) was the original tongue of mankind, the very language God spoke to [Adam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam).[^n70][^n97]

## Works {#Works}

In addition to being a scholar, historian, and physician, Barhebraeus was also a mathematician, astronomer, and lecturer. He authored numerous books on intricate subjects like philosophy, religion, and grammar, presenting them in an easily understandable manner.[^n38]

The following is a list of only some of his works, a few of which are lost, others unpublished:[^n98][^n99][^n100]

- "Storehouse of Secrets" (*Awṣar Roze*) – A large biblical commentary (philological, literal, and spiritual) drawing on multiple biblical versions, citing authoritative Church Fathers such as [Dionysius the Areopagite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysius_the_Areopagite), [John Chrysostom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Chrysostom), [Cyril of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyril_of_Alexandria), [Jacob of Edessa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_of_Edessa), [Michael the Syrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_the_Syrian), and many others
- "The Lamp of the Sanctuaries" (*Mnorath Qudshe*) – A theological and apologetic work defending Christianity against various heresies in Barhebraeus's time
- "The Book of Rays" (*Kthobo d-Zalgē*) – A compendium of *The Lamp of the Sanctuaries*, encompassing doctrines of [creation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creation_in_Christianity), [incarnation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incarnation_%28Christianity%29), [angelology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angelology), [ecclesiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiology), [sacramentology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrament), [eschatology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eschatology), and the [afterlife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaven_in_Christianity), drawing upon numerous Church Fathers, both orthodox and otherwise
- *Nomocanon* or "Book of Directions" (*Kthobo d-Hudoye*) – A vast canonical collection in forty parts, treating subjects such as ecclesiology, sacramentology, economics, law, and ethics. It draws heavily from the patristic corpus and served as an authoritative reference for resolving church disputes. It was later praised by European authorities
- The *Ethikon*; "Ethics" (*Ktābā d-īthīqōn*) – Addresses spiritual and moral matters, providing guidance for both ascetics and laypeople
- "The Book of the Dove" (*Kthobo d-Yawno*) – Named after the [Holy Spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Spirit) (the [Dove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_of_the_Dove#Symbolism_in_Christianity)), this treatise explores the life of asceticism and spiritual perfection
- Various large volumes on prayer and spirituality – Some written in poetic form, focusing on moral and devotional themes
- "The Cream of Wisdom" (*Hewath Hekhemtho*) – His magnum opus in philosophy and the natural sciences, comprising multiple volumes and covering [Aristotelian philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelian_philosophy), [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics), astronomy, [meteorology](wikipedia://en/19904), [mineralogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineralogy), [botany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botany), [zoology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoology), [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology), [theological anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theological_anthropology), [alchemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy), [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology), politics, economics, and ethics
- "The Cream of Secrets" (*Kitab Zubdat al-Asrār*) – A Syriac philosophical translation of [Athir al-Din al-Abhari](wikipedia://en/1740825) (1266); now lost
- "The Book of *Tegrath Tegrotho"* (*Mercatura Mercaturarum*) – An abridgement of *The Cream of Wisdom* that deals with theology and [dialectics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectics)
- "Book of the Speech of Wisdom" (*Kthobo da-Swodh Sophia*) – On dialectics, linguistics, and philosophy
- "Book of the Pupils of the Eyes" (*Kthobo d-Bobotho*) – A concise treatise on logic and philosophy
- "On the Human Soul" – Two Arabic treatises, the larger composed at the request of Dionysius ‘Anjur, Metropolitan of Melitene before 1252
- "The Book of Indications and Prognostications" (*Kitab al-Isharāt wa al-Tanbihāt*) – A Syriac translation of [Ibn Sina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Sina), undertaken at the request of Simon Thomas the Easterner, chief physician of Hulegu
- "Ascent of the Mind" (*Kthobo d-Ṣuloqo Hawnonoyo*) – A scientific work on astronomy and cosmography with accompanying illustrations and diagrams
- "On the Benefits of the Members of the Body" – A medical work in Arabic, now lost
- Commentary on the [Aphorisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphorisms) of [Hippocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocrates) – Written in Arabic, showing Barhebraeus's medical and philosophical synthesis
- Various commentaries and translations – Covering geometry, astronomy, medicine, and [oneiromancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oneiromancy) (dream interpretation)
- "The Ecclesiastical History" – A detailed account of the patriarchs of Antioch from Peter up to 1285, also including lists of the maphrians and Nestorian [catholicoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicoi)
- "Chronography" (*Makhthbonuth Zabne*) – A universal chronicle beginning with creation and extending to 1285, regarded as his most significant historical work
- *Tarikh Mukhtaṣar al-Duwal* – The Arabic version of the *Chronography*, a compendium of world history with adaptations for Arabic-speaking audience
- "The Book of Lights" (*Kthobo d-Ṣinḥe*) – A Syriac grammar addressing both [East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Syriac_Rite) and [West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Syriac_Rite) Syriac traditions; two further related grammatical books were published
- "Introduction to Grammar" (*Kthobo da-Gramatiki*) – A grammatical introduction composed in [heptasyllabic metre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexasyllable)
- "Book of the Sparks" (*Kthobo d-Balṣuṣitho*) – A large grammar treatise, unfinished and now lost
- "The Book of Humorous Stories" – A collection of tales featuring people and animals, written in a lively narrative style
- Large collections of poems, riddles, and [Biblical exegesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_exegesis)

## Veneration and legacy {#Veneration_and_legacy}

Barhebraeus stands as one of the most venerated saints of the [Oriental Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Orthodox_Church), and a towering figure of pride within the [Syriac Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox_Church). Renowned as both a blessed monastic saint and a genius polymath, he combined sanctity with scholarship and excelled in theology, philosophy, medicine, and the Syriac language. His intellectual output revived [Syriac literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_literature) and learning in an age when it had declined, thus contributing to what has often been termed the Syriac Renaissance.[^n101][^n102] His feast day in the Syriac Orthodox Church is observed on 30 July, the date of his death. His relics, interred at [Mor Mattai Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mor_Mattai_Monastery), remain a major pilgrimage site for the faithful.[^n103]

In March 1986, Patriarch [Ignatius Zakka I Iwas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignatius_Zakka_I_Iwas) celebrated the "700th anniversary of the passing of Maphrian Barhebraeus to the heavenly abode", dedicating that year's patriarchal encyclical to him. The encyclical included a short biography and encouraged the faithful to emulate his life and teachings, in addition to invoking his intercession.[^n104] In February 2026, coinciding with the 800th anniversary of Barhebraeus's birth, Patriarch [Ignatius Aphrem II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignatius_Aphrem_II) issued that year's [Lent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lent) encyclical, urging the faithful to read the Bible, especially in times of need and danger, in line with Barhebraeus's life and teachings, which emphasised a steadfast dedication to the [Gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospels).[^n105]

### Descriptions {#Descriptions}

Patriarch [Ignatius Aphrem I Barsoum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignatius_Aphrem_I_Barsoum) devoted numerous pages of *The Scattered Pearls* to praising Barhebraeus, calling him one of the "great philosophers and theologians of the Orient as well as the world" and "the most luminous star that ever shone on the firmament of the Syrian nations and his encyclopaedic knowledge makes him all the more unique and unequalled".[^n106] He also used many laudatory titles such as "Ocean of Wisdom", "Light of East and West", "King of Learned Men", "Greatest Sage", "Holy Father", and "The Most Learned Man Possessing Divine Knowledge".[^n107] Barsoum further commended Barhebraeus's mastery of Syriac, Arabic, Armenian, and Persian, writing, "His Syriac style is very powerful, lucid and attractive. Whenever his reader dives into his books he finds unique and precious pearls. He would end his reading by bowing his head in great reverence to the prince of writers, the king of learned men, and without exception, the most famous Syrian scholar".[^n107]

*Image: Mar Mattai monastery.jpg.* The Monastery of St. [Matthew the Hermit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_the_Hermit) in Iraq, where the relics of many saints, including Barhebraeus and his brother, are interred

Other commendatory descriptions include the [Syriacist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriacist) [Sebastian P. Brock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebastian_P._Brock)'s, who classed Barhebraeus "alongside [Ephrem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephrem_the_Syrian), perhaps the most famous of all Syriac writers" and described him as "a polymath of extraordinarily wide learning in virtually every subject that was studied in his time".[^n108] Lucas Van Rompay also called him "a man of an exceptional breadth and open-mindedness",[^n109] and Rev. Joseph Tarzi hailed him as "the Church's greatest and boldest knight, never to be challenged or matched in all branches of learning—theological and otherwise" and listed his many epithets: "the sea of wisdom, the light of the East and West, king of the learned, the greatest of the wise men, the holy father, the father versed in theology, the adornment of composers, the crown of Maphrians, the crown of chiefs".[^n3] Paul Bedjan further described his chronicle as "one of the most interesting books that were produced in my country".[^n110]

In modern commemoration, Barhebraeus continues to be honoured. In 2010, a symposium dedicated to him was organised in [Aleppo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleppo), Syria by the previous archbishop of Aleppo, [Yohanna Ibrahim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yohanna_Ibrahim).[^n111]

Contemporary scholars frequently refer to Barhebraeus as the "Encyclopaedia of the 13th century" which entirely captures his unmatched knowledge and lasting influence throughout the Syriac, Arabic, and wider Eastern intellectual sphere.[^n3]
