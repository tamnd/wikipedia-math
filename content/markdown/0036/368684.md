# Moment (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Moment"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **moments** of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) are certain quantitative measures related to the shape of the function's [graph](wikipedia://en/87352). For example, [in physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment_%28physics%29), if the function represents [mass density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_density), then the zeroth moment is the total mass, the first moment is the [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961) (multiplied by total mass), and the second moment is the [moment of inertia](wikipedia://en/157700). [In statistics](wikipedia://en/32960532), the function is a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543), then the first moment is the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653), the second [central moment](wikipedia://en/19983) is the [variance](wikipedia://en/32344), the third [standardized moment](wikipedia://en/202713) is the [skewness](wikipedia://en/28212), and the fourth standardized moment is the [kurtosis](wikipedia://en/16848).

:::

For a distribution of mass or probability on a [bounded interval](wikipedia://en/48258), the collection of all the moments (of all orders, from $0$ to $\infty$) uniquely determines the distribution ([Hausdorff moment problem](wikipedia://en/3216387)). The same is not true on unbounded intervals ([Hamburger moment problem](wikipedia://en/3216491)).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formulation"}

## Formulation {#Formulation}

The $n$th moment of a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued continuous density function $f(x)$ about a reference value $c$ is the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532)

$$\mu_n = \int_{-\infty}^\infty (x - c)^n\,f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.$$

It is possible to define moments in a more general fashion than moments for real-valued functions – see moments in metric spaces. The moment of a function, without further explanation, usually refers to the above expression with $c=0$. For the second and higher moments, the [central moment](wikipedia://en/19983) (moments about the mean, with *c* being the mean) are usually used rather than the moments about zero, because they provide clearer information about the distribution's shape.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

### Transformation of center {#Transformation_of_center}

Since

$$(x - b)^n = (x - a + a - b)^n = \sum_{i=0}^n {n \choose i}(x - a)^i(a - b)^{n-i}$$

where $\binom{n}{i}$ is the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668), it follows that the moments about *b* can be calculated from the moments about *a* by:

$$E\left[(x - b)^n\right] = \sum_{i=0}^n {n \choose i} E\left[(x - a)^i\right](a - b)^{n-i}.$$

As a corollary of this result, the lowest nonzero moment is independent of the choice of center. We can see this from the formula above by considering the case where the *i*th moments (about some point *a*) are zero for all $i < n$.

### Moment of a convolution of function {#Moment_of_a_convolution_of_function}

The raw moment of a convolution $h(t) = (f * g)(t) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(\tau) g(t - \tau) \, d\tau$ reads

$$\mu_n[h] = \sum_{i=0}^{n} {n\choose i} \mu_i[f] \mu_{n-i}[g]$$

where $\mu_n[\,\cdot\,]$ denotes the $n$th moment of the function given in the brackets. This identity follows by the convolution theorem for moment generating function and applying the chain rule for [differentiating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiation_%28mathematics%29) a product.

:::

## Partial moments {#Partial_moments}

Partial moments are sometimes referred to as "one-sided moments". The $n$th order lower and upper partial moments with respect to a reference point *r* may be expressed as

$$\mu_n^- (r) = \int_{-\infty}^r (r - x)^n\,f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,$$

$$\mu_n^+ (r) = \int_r^\infty (x - r)^n\,f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.$$

If the integral function does not converge, the partial moment does not exist.

Partial moments are normalized by being raised to the power $1/n$. The [upside potential ratio](wikipedia://en/1451419) may be expressed as a ratio of a first-order upper partial moment to a normalized second-order lower partial moment.

## Central moments in metric spaces {#Central_moments_in_metric_spaces}

Let $(M, d)$ be a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018), and let $B(M)$ be the [Borel $\sigma$-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_sigma_algebra) on $M$, the [$\sigma$-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_algebra) generated by the $d$-[open subsets](wikipedia://en/39358) of $M$. (For technical reasons, it is also convenient to assume that $M$ is a [separable space](wikipedia://en/27855) with respect to the [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) $d$.) Let $1 \le p \le \infty$.

The **$p$th central moment** of a measure $\mu$ on the [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333) $(M, B(M))$ about a given point $x_{0} \in M$ is defined to be

$$\int_{M} d\left(x, x_0\right)^p \, \mathrm{d} \mu (x).$$

$\mu$ is said to have **finite $p$th central moment** if the $p$th central moment of $\mu$ about $x_{0}$ is finite for some $x_{0} \in M$.

This terminology for measures carries over to random variables in the usual way: if $(\Omega, \Sigma, \mathbf{P})$ is a [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325) and $X : \Omega \to M$ is a random variable, then the **$p$th central moment** of $X$ about $x_{0} \in M$ is defined to be

$$\int_M d \left(x, x_0\right)^p \, \mathrm{d} \left( X_* \left(\mathbf{P}\right) \right) (x) = \int_\Omega d \left(X(\omega), x_0\right)^p \, \mathrm{d} \mathbf{P} (\omega) = \operatorname{\mathbf{E}}[d(X, x_0)^p],$$

and *X* has **finite $p$th central moment** if the $p$th central moment of $X$ about $x_{0}$ is finite for some $x_{0} \in M$.
