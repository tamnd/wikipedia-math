# Homogeneous space

*Image: Torus.png.* A [torus](wikipedia://en/74800). The standard torus is homogeneous under its [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564) and [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) groups, and the [flat torus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_torus) is homogeneous under its diffeomorphism, homeomorphism, and [isometry groups](wikipedia://en/1101694).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Homogeneous space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **homogeneous space** is, very informally, a space that looks the same everywhere as one moves through it, with movement given by the [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447). Homogeneous spaces occur in the theories of [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945), [algebraic groups](wikipedia://en/247924) and [topological groups](wikipedia://en/42315). More precisely, a homogeneous space for a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G* is a [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566) [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) or [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X* on which *G* [acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) [transitively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Transitivity_properties). The elements of *G* are called the **symmetries** of *X*.

:::

A special case of this is when the group *G* in question is the [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of the space *X* – here "automorphism group" can mean [isometry group](wikipedia://en/1101694), [diffeomorphism group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphism_group), or [homeomorphism group](wikipedia://en/1531409). In this case, *X* is homogeneous if intuitively *X* looks locally the same at each point, either in the sense of isometry (rigid geometry), diffeomorphism ([differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625)), or homeomorphism ([topology](wikipedia://en/29954)).

Some authors insist that the action of *G* be [faithful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_group_action) (non-identity elements act non-trivially), although the present article does not. Thus there is a [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of *G* on *X* that can be thought of as preserving some "geometric structure" on *X*, and making *X* into a single [*G*-orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Let *X* be a non-empty set and *G* a group. Then *X* is called a *G*-space if it is equipped with an action of *G* on *X*.[^o4] Note that automatically *G* acts by automorphisms (bijections) on the set. If *X* in addition belongs to some [category](wikipedia://en/53759), then the elements of *G* are assumed to act as [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) in the same category. That is, the maps on *X* coming from elements of *G* preserve the structure associated with the category (for example, if *X* is an object in **Diff** then the action is required to be by [diffeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/8564)). A homogeneous space is a *G*-space on which *G* acts transitively.

If *X* is an object of the category **C**, then the structure of a *G*-space is a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564):

$$\rho : G \to \mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbf{C}}(X)$$

into the group of [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of the object *X* in the category **C**. The pair (*X*, *ρ*) defines a homogeneous space provided *ρ*(*G*) is a transitive group of symmetries of the underlying set of *X*.

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

For example, if *X* is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), then group elements are assumed to act as [homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13660) on *X*. The structure of a *G*-space is a group homomorphism *ρ* : *G* → Homeo(*X*) into the [homeomorphism group](wikipedia://en/1531409) of *X*.

Similarly, if *X* is a [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219), then the group elements are [diffeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/8564). The structure of a *G*-space is a group homomorphism *ρ* : *G* → Diffeo(*X*) into the diffeomorphism group of *X*.

[Riemannian symmetric spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_symmetric_space) are an important class of homogeneous spaces, and include many of the examples listed below.

Concrete examples include:

**Isometry groups**

- Positive curvature:
  1. Sphere ([orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954)): *S*<sup>*n*−1</sup> ≅ O(*n*) / O(*n*−1). This is true because of the following observations: First, *S*<sup>*n*−1</sup> is the set of vectors in **R**<sup>*n*</sup> with norm 1. If we consider one of these vectors as a base vector, then any other vector can be constructed using an orthogonal transformation. If we consider the span of this vector as a one dimensional subspace of **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, then the complement is an (*n* − 1)-dimensional vector space that is invariant under an orthogonal transformation from O(*n* − 1). This shows us why we can construct *S*<sup>*n*−1</sup> as a homogeneous space.
  1. Oriented sphere ([special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group)): *S*<sup>*n*−1</sup> ≅ SO(*n*) / SO(*n* − 1)
  1. Projective space ([projective orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/4845690)): P<sup>*n*−1</sup> ≅ PO(*n*) / PO(*n* − 1)
- Flat (zero curvature):
  1. Euclidean space ([Euclidean group](wikipedia://en/652164), point stabilizer is orthogonal group): E<sup>*n*</sup> ≅ E(*n*) / O(*n*)
- Negative curvature:
  1. Hyperbolic space ([orthochronous Lorentz group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthochronous_Lorentz_group), point stabilizer orthogonal group, corresponding to [hyperboloid model](wikipedia://en/4771189)): H<sup>*n*</sup> ≅ O<sup>\+</sup>(1, *n*) / O(*n*)
  1. Oriented hyperbolic space: SO<sup>\+</sup>(1, *n*) / SO(*n*)
  1. [Anti-de Sitter space](wikipedia://en/301670): AdS<sub>*n*+1</sub> = O(2, *n*) / O(1, *n*)

**Others**

- [Affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) over [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K* (for [affine group](wikipedia://en/298828), point stabilizer [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564)): A<sup>*n*</sup> = Aff(*n*, *K*) / GL(*n*, *K*).
- [Grassmannian](wikipedia://en/373810): Gr(*r*, *n*) = O(*n*) / (O(*r*) × O(*n* − *r*))
- [Topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) (in the sense of topology)
- There are other interesting homogeneous spaces, in particular with relevance in physics: This includes [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space) M<sup>*n*</sup> ≅ ISO(*n-1,1*) / SO(*n,1*) or Galilean and Carrollian spaces.[^o8]

## Geometry {#Geometry}

From the point of view of the [Erlangen program](wikipedia://en/243382), one may understand that "all points are the same", in the [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) of *X*. This was true of essentially all geometries proposed before [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243), in the middle of the nineteenth century.

Thus, for example, [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) and [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) are all in natural ways homogeneous spaces for their respective [symmetry groups](wikipedia://en/28186). The same is true of the models found of [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610) of constant [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770), such as [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742).

A further classical example is the space of lines in projective space of three dimensions (equivalently, the space of two-dimensional subspaces of a four-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370)). It is simple [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) to show that GL<sub>4</sub> acts transitively on those. We can parameterize them by *line co-ordinates*: these are the 2×2 [minors](wikipedia://en/262107) of the 4×2 matrix with columns two basis vectors for the subspace. The geometry of the resulting homogeneous space is the [line geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_geometry) of [Julius Plücker](wikipedia://en/52146).

## Homogeneous spaces as coset spaces {#Homogeneous_spaces_as_coset_spaces}

In general, if *X* is a homogeneous space of *G*, and *H*<sub>*o*</sub> is the [stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stabilizer_%28group_theory%29) of some marked point *o* in *X* (a choice of [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432)), the points of *X* correspond to the left [cosets](wikipedia://en/98759) *G*/*H*<sub>*o*</sub>, and the marked point *o* corresponds to the coset of the identity. Conversely, given a coset space *G*/*H*, it is a homogeneous space for *G* with a distinguished point, namely the coset of the identity. Thus a homogeneous space can be thought of as a coset space without a choice of origin.

For example, if *H* is the identity subgroup $\{e\}$, then *X* is the [*G*-torsor](wikipedia://en/363441), which explains why *G*-torsors are often described intuitively as "*G* with forgotten identity".

In general, a different choice of origin *o* will lead to a quotient of *G* by a different subgroup *H<sub>o′</sub>* that is related to *H<sub>o</sub>* by an [inner automorphism](wikipedia://en/61839) of *G*. Specifically,

::: {#o11 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$H_{o'} = gH_og^{-1} \tag{1}$$

:::

where *g* is any element of *G* for which *go* = *o*′. Note that the inner automorphism (1) does not depend on which such *g* is selected; it depends only on *g* modulo *H*<sub>*o*</sub>.

If the action of *G* on *X* is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_map) and *X* is [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637), then *H* is a [closed subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_subgroup) of *G*. In particular, if *G* is a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945), then *H* is a [Lie subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_subgroup) by [Cartan's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_subgroup_theorem). Hence *G* / *H* is a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) and so *X* carries a unique [smooth structure](wikipedia://en/1542238) compatible with the group action.

One can go further to [*double* coset](wikipedia://en/982771) spaces, notably [Clifford–Klein forms](wikipedia://en/797238) Γ\\*G*/*H*, where Γ is a discrete subgroup (of *G*) acting [properly discontinuously](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Properly_discontinuously).

::: {#o13 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

For example, in the line geometry case, we can identify *H* as a 12-dimensional subgroup of the 16-dimensional [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564), GL(4), defined by conditions on the matrix entries

*h*<sub>13</sub> = *h*<sub>14</sub> = *h*<sub>23</sub> = *h*<sub>24</sub> = 0,

by looking for the stabilizer of the subspace spanned by the first two standard basis vectors. That shows that *X* has dimension 4.

Since the [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316) given by the minors are 6 in number, this means that the latter are not independent of each other. In fact, a single quadratic relation holds between the six minors, as was known to nineteenth-century geometers.

This example was the first known example of a [Grassmannian](wikipedia://en/373810), other than a projective space. There are many further homogeneous spaces of the classical linear groups in common use in mathematics.

:::

## Prehomogeneous vector spaces {#Prehomogeneous_vector_spaces}

The idea of a prehomogeneous vector space was introduced by [Mikio Sato](wikipedia://en/373336).

It is a finite-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V* with a [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of an [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924) *G*, such that there is an orbit of *G* that is open for the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744) (and so, dense). An example is GL(1) acting on a one-dimensional space.

The definition is more restrictive than it initially appears: such spaces have remarkable properties, and there is a classification of irreducible prehomogeneous vector spaces, up to a transformation known as "castling".

## Homogeneous spaces in physics {#Homogeneous_spaces_in_physics}

Given the [Poincaré group](wikipedia://en/230487) *G* and its subgroup the [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489) *H*, the space of [cosets](wikipedia://en/98759) *G* / *H* is the [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space).[^o16] Together with [de Sitter space](wikipedia://en/301666) and [anti-de Sitter space](wikipedia://en/301670) these are the maximally symmetric [lorentzian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentzian_metric) spacetimes. There are also homogeneous spaces of relevance in physics that are non-lorentzian, for example Galilean, Carrollian or Aristotelian spacetimes.[^o8]

[Physical cosmology](wikipedia://en/5378) using the [general theory of relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_theory_of_relativity) makes use of the [Bianchi classification](wikipedia://en/7811800) system. Homogeneous spaces in relativity represent the [space part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_%28physics%29) of background [metrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) for some [cosmological models](wikipedia://en/5378); for example, the three cases of the [Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric](wikipedia://en/343031) may be represented by subsets of the Bianchi I (flat), V (open), VII (flat or open) and IX (closed) types, while the [Mixmaster universe](wikipedia://en/8014489) represents an [anisotropic](wikipedia://en/14865) example of a Bianchi IX cosmology.[^o17]

A homogeneous space of *N* dimensions admits a set of at least *N* and up to $\tfrac{1}{2}$*N*(*N* + 1) [Killing vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killing_vectors).[^o18] For three dimensions, this gives a total of up to six linearly independent Killing vector fields; homogeneous 3-spaces have the property that one may use linear combinations of these to find three everywhere non-vanishing Killing vector fields *ξ*${}^{(a)}_{i}$,

$$\xi^{(a)}_{[i;k]}=C^a_{\ bc}\xi^{(b)}_i \xi^{(c)}_k ,$$

where the object *C*<sup>*a*</sup><sub>*bc*</sub>, the "[structure constants](wikipedia://en/20285142)", form a [constant](wikipedia://en/24758132) [order-three tensor](wikipedia://en/29965) [antisymmetric](wikipedia://en/696955) in its lower two indices (on the left-hand side, the brackets denote antisymmetrisation and ";" represents the [covariant differential operator](wikipedia://en/431848)). In the case of a [flat isotropic universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambda-CDM), one possibility is *C*<sup>*a*</sup><sub>*bc*</sub> = 0 (type I), but in the case of a closed FLRW universe, *C*<sup>*a*</sup><sub>*bc*</sub> = *ε*<sup>*a*</sup><sub>*bc*</sub>, where *ε*<sup>*a*</sup><sub>*bc*</sub> is the [Levi-Civita symbol](wikipedia://en/195982).
