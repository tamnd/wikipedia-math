# Idempotent (ring theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Idempotent"}

In [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **idempotent element** or simply **idempotent** of a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) is an element $a$ such that $a^{2} = a$.[^n1] That is, the element is [idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent) under the ring's multiplication. [Inductively](wikipedia://en/18881) then, one can also conclude that $a = a^{2} = a^{3} = a^{4} = ... = a^{n}$ for any positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $n$. For example, an idempotent element of a [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) is precisely an [idempotent matrix](wikipedia://en/2974863).

:::

For general rings, elements idempotent under multiplication are involved in decompositions of [modules](wikipedia://en/276410), and connected to [homological](wikipedia://en/139410) properties of the ring. In [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/54476844), the main objects of study are rings in which all elements are idempotent under both addition and multiplication.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### Quotients of Z {#Quotients_of_Z}

One may consider the [ring of integers modulo $n$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_integers_modulo_n), where $n$ is [square-free](wikipedia://en/29525). By the [Chinese remainder theorem](wikipedia://en/7713), this ring factors into the [product of rings](wikipedia://en/25063) of integers modulo $p$, where $p$ is [prime](wikipedia://en/23666). Now each of these factors is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), so it is clear that the factors' only idempotents will be $0$ and $1$. That is, each factor has two idempotents. So if there are $m$ factors, there will be $2^{m}$ idempotents.

We can check this for the integers $\bmod 6$, $R = \mathbf{Z} / 6\mathbf{Z}$. Since $6$ has two prime factors ($2$ and $3$) it should have $2^{2}$ idempotents.

$$0^{2} \equiv 0 \equiv 0\pmod{6}$$

$$1^{2} \equiv 1 \equiv 1\pmod{6}$$

$$2^{2} \equiv 4 \equiv 4\pmod{6}$$

$$3^{2} \equiv 9 \equiv 3\pmod{6}$$

$$4^{2} \equiv 16 \equiv 4\pmod{6}$$

$$5^{2} \equiv 25 \equiv 1\pmod{6}$$

From these computations, $0$, $1$, $3$, and $4$ are idempotents of this ring, while $2$ and $5$ are not. This also demonstrates the decomposition properties described below: because $3 + 4 \equiv 1\pmod{6}$, there is a ring decomposition $3\mathbf{Z} / 6\mathbf{Z} \oplus 4\mathbf{Z} / 6\mathbf{Z}$. In $3\mathbf{Z} / 6\mathbf{Z}$ the multiplicative identity is $3 + 6\mathbf{Z}$ and in $4\mathbf{Z} / 6\mathbf{Z}$ the multiplicative identity is $4 + 6\mathbf{Z}$.

### Quotient of polynomial ring {#Quotient_of_polynomial_ring}

Given a ring $R$ and an element $f \in R$ such that $f^{2} \ne 0$, the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635)

$$R / (f^{2} - f)$$

has the idempotent $f$. For example, this could be applied to $x \in \mathbf{Z}[x]$, or any [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) $f \in k[x_{1}, ..., x_{n}]$.

### Idempotents in the ring of split-quaternions {#Idempotents_in_the_ring_of_split-quaternions}

There is a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) of idempotents in the ring of [split-quaternions](wikipedia://en/1461265). Split quaternions have the structure of a [real algebra](wikipedia://en/191788), so elements can be written *w* + *x*i + *y*j + *z*k over a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) {1, i, j, k}, with j<sup>2</sup> = k<sup>2</sup> = +1. For any θ,

$s = j \cos \theta + k \sin \theta$ satisfies s<sup>2</sup> = +1 since j and k satisfy the [anticommutative property](wikipedia://en/294358). Now

$\left(\frac{1+s}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{1 + 2s + s^2}{4} = \frac{1+s}{2},$ the idempotent property.

The element *s* is called a [hyperbolic unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_unit) and so far, the i-coordinate has been taken as zero. When this coordinate is non-zero, then there is a [hyperboloid of one sheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperboloid_of_one_sheet) of [hyperbolic units in split-quaternions](wikipedia://en/1461265#Hyperbolic_units). The same equality shows the idempotent property of $\frac{1 + s}{2}$ where *s* is on the hyperboloid.

## Types of ring idempotents {#Types_of_ring_idempotents}

A partial list of important types of idempotents includes:

- Two idempotents $a$ and $b$ are called **orthogonal** if $ab = ba = 0$. If $a$ is idempotent in the ring $R$ (with [unity](wikipedia://en/48404#Notes_on_the_definition)), then so is $b = 1 - a$; moreover, $a$ and $b$ are orthogonal.
- An idempotent $a$ in $R$ is called a **central idempotent** if $ax = xa$ for all $x$ in $R$, that is, if $a$ is in the [center](wikipedia://en/53796787) of $R$.
- A **trivial idempotent** refers to either of the elements $0$ and $1$, which are always idempotent.
- A **primitive idempotent** of a ring $R$ is a nonzero idempotent $a$ such that $aR$ is [indecomposable](wikipedia://en/810549) as a right $R$-module; that is, such that $aR$ is not a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) of two [nonzero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_module) [submodules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule). Equivalently, $a$ is a primitive idempotent if it cannot be written as $a = e + f$, where $e$ and $f$ are nonzero orthogonal idempotents in $R$.
- A **local idempotent** is an idempotent $a$ such that $aRa$ is a [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391). This implies that $aR$ is directly indecomposable, so local idempotents are also primitive.
- A **right irreducible idempotent** is an idempotent $a$ for which $aR$ is a [simple module](wikipedia://en/29430). By [Schur's lemma](wikipedia://en/521696), $\operatorname{End}_{R}(aR) = aRa$ is a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067), and hence is a local ring, so right (and left) irreducible idempotents are local.
- A **centrally primitive** idempotent is a central idempotent $a$ that cannot be written as the sum of two nonzero orthogonal central idempotents.
- An idempotent $a + I$ in the quotient ring $R / I$ is said to **lift modulo $I$** if there is an idempotent $b$ in $R$ such that $b + I = a + I$.
- An idempotent $a$ of $R$ is called a **full idempotent** if $RaR = R$.
- A **separability idempotent**; see *[Separable algebra](wikipedia://en/13205367)*.

Any non-trivial idempotent $a$ is a [zero divisor](wikipedia://en/51441) (because $ab = 0$ with neither $a$ nor $b$ being zero, where $b = 1 - a$). This shows that [integral domains](wikipedia://en/15462) and [division rings](wikipedia://en/9067) do not have such idempotents. [Local rings](wikipedia://en/153391) also do not have such idempotents, but for a different reason. The only idempotent contained in the [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656) of a ring is $0$.

## Rings characterized by idempotents {#Rings_characterized_by_idempotents}

- A ring in which *all* elements are idempotent is called a [Boolean ring](wikipedia://en/54356). Some authors use the term "idempotent ring" for this type of ring. In such a ring, multiplication is [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346) and every element is its own [additive inverse](wikipedia://en/228312).
- A ring is [semisimple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_ring) if and only if every right (or every left) [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) is generated by an idempotent.
- A ring is [von Neumann regular](wikipedia://en/907524) if and only if every [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) right (or every finitely generated left) ideal is generated by an idempotent.
- A ring for which the [annihilator](wikipedia://en/328684) $r.\operatorname{Ann}(S)$ every subset $S$ of $R$ is generated by an idempotent is called a [Baer ring](wikipedia://en/7455223). If the condition only holds for all [singleton](wikipedia://en/381798) subsets of $R$, then the ring is a right [Rickart ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rickart_ring). Both of these types of rings are interesting even when they [lack a multiplicative identity](wikipedia://en/3405004).
- A ring in which all idempotents are [central](wikipedia://en/53796787) is called an **abelian ring**. Such rings need not be commutative.
- A ring is [directly irreducible](wikipedia://en/23169189) if and only if $0$ and $1$ are the only central idempotents.
- A ring $R$ can be written as $e_{1}R \oplus e_{2}R \oplus ... \oplus e_{n}R$ with each $e_{i}$ a local idempotent if and only if $R$ is a [semiperfect ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiperfect_ring).
- A ring is called an **[SBI ring](wikipedia://en/37641195)** or **Lift/rad** ring if all idempotents of $R$ lift modulo the [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656).
- A ring satisfies the [ascending chain condition](wikipedia://en/3189) on right direct summands if and only if the ring satisfies the [descending chain condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descending_chain_condition) on left direct summands if and only if every set of pairwise orthogonal idempotents is finite.
- If $a$ is idempotent in the ring $R$, then $aRa$ is again a ring, with multiplicative identity $a$. The ring $aRa$ is often referred to as a **corner ring** of $R$. The corner ring arises naturally since the [ring of endomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_endomorphisms) $\operatorname{End}_{R}(aR) \cong aRa$.

:::

## Role in decompositions {#Role_in_decompositions}

The idempotents of $R$ have an important connection to decomposition of $R$-[modules](wikipedia://en/276410). If $M$ is an $R$-module and $E = \operatorname{End}_{R}(M)$ is its [ring of endomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_endomorphisms), then $A \oplus B = M$ if and only if there is a unique idempotent $e$ in $E$ such that $A = eM$ and $B = (1 - e)M$. Clearly then, $M$ is directly indecomposable if and only if $0$ and $1$ are the only idempotents in $E$.[^n2]

In the case when $M = R$ (assumed unital), the endomorphism ring $\operatorname{End}_{R}(R) = R$, where each [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569) arises as left multiplication by a fixed ring element. With this modification of notation, $A \oplus B = R$ as right modules if and only if there exists a unique idempotent $e$ such that $eR = A$ and $(1 - e)R = B$. Thus every direct summand of $R$ is generated by an idempotent.

If $a$ is a central idempotent, then the corner ring $aRa = Ra$ is a ring with multiplicative identity $a$. Just as idempotents determine the direct decompositions of $R$ as a module, the central idempotents of $R$ determine the decompositions of $R$ as a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) of rings. If $R$ is the direct sum of the rings $R_{1}$, ..., $R_{n}$, then the identity elements of the rings $R_{i}$ are central idempotents in $R$, pairwise orthogonal, and their sum is $1$. Conversely, given central idempotents $a_{1}$, ..., $a_{n}$ in $R$ that are pairwise orthogonal and have sum $1$, then $R$ is the direct sum of the rings $Ra_{1}$, ..., $Ra_{n}$. So in particular, every central idempotent $a$ in $R$ gives rise to a decomposition of $R$ as a direct sum of the corner rings $aRa$ and $(1 - a)R(1 - a)$. As a result, a ring $R$ is directly indecomposable as a ring if and only if the identity $1$ is centrally primitive.

Working inductively, one can attempt to decompose $1$ into a sum of centrally primitive elements. If $1$ is centrally primitive, we are done. If not, it is a sum of central orthogonal idempotents, which in turn are primitive or sums of more central idempotents, and so on. The problem that may occur is that this may continue without end, producing an infinite family of central orthogonal idempotents. The condition "$R$*does not contain infinite sets of central orthogonal idempotents*" is a type of finiteness condition on the ring. It can be achieved in many ways, such as requiring the ring to be right [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129). If a decomposition $R = c_{1}R \oplus c_{2}R \oplus ... \oplus c_{n}R$ exists with each $c_{i}$ a centrally primitive idempotent, then $R$ is a direct sum of the corner rings $c_{i}Rc_{i}$, each of which is ring irreducible.[^n3]

For [associative algebras](wikipedia://en/2112) or [Jordan algebras](wikipedia://en/936371) over a field, the [Peirce decomposition](wikipedia://en/30189901) is a decomposition of an algebra as a sum of eigenspaces of commuting idempotent elements.

## Relation with involutions {#Relation_with_involutions}

If $a$ is an idempotent of a ring $R$, then the square of $f = 1 - 2a$ equals 1. So, for every left $R$-module, the multiplication by $f$ is an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) of $M$; that is, it is an $R$-[module homomorphism](wikipedia://en/474118) such that $f^{2}$ is the identity endomorphism of $M$.

If $M$ is an $R$-[bimodule](wikipedia://en/492612), and, in particular, if $M=R$, the left and the right multiplications with $f$ gives rise to two involutions of the module.

Conversely, if $b$ is an element of $R$ such that $b^2=1$, then $(1- b)^2= 2(1- b)$, and, if $2$ is an [invertible element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible_element) in $R$, $a=2^{-1}(1 - b)$ is an idempotent such that $b = 1 - 2a$. Thus, for a ring in which $2$ is invertible, the idempotent elements are in [one-to-one correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-to-one_correspondence) with the elements whose square is 1.

## Category of *R*-modules {#Category_of_R-modules}

Lifting idempotents also has major consequences for the [category of $R$-modules](wikipedia://en/11231812). All idempotents lift modulo $I$ if and only if every $R$ direct summand of $R/I$ has a [projective cover](wikipedia://en/10273917) as an $R$-module.[^n4] Idempotents always lift modulo [nil ideals](wikipedia://en/3100140) and rings for which $R$ is [$I$-adically complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completion_%28ring_theory%29#Krull_topology).

Lifting is most important when $I = J(R)$, the [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656) of $R$. Yet another characterization of [semiperfect rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiperfect_ring) is that they are [semilocal rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semilocal_ring) whose idempotents lift modulo $J(R)$.[^n5]

## Lattice of idempotents {#Lattice_of_idempotents}

One may define a [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) on the idempotents of a ring as follows: if $a$ and $b$ are idempotents, we write $a \le b$ if and only if $ab = ba = a$. With respect to this order, $0$ is the smallest and $1$ the largest idempotent. For orthogonal idempotents $a$ and $b$, $a + b$ is also idempotent, and we have $a \le a + b$ and $b \le a + b$. The [atoms](wikipedia://en/998824) of this partial order are precisely the primitive idempotents.[^n6]

When the above partial order is restricted to the central idempotents of $R$, a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) structure, or even a [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/54476844) structure, can be given. For two central idempotents $e$ and $f$, the [complement](wikipedia://en/54476844#Operations) is given by

$$\neg e = 1 - e,$$

the [meet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meet_%28mathematics%29) is given by

$$e \land f = ef.$$

and the [join](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Join_%28mathematics%29) is given by

$$e \lor f = \neg(\neg e \land \neg f) = e + f - ef$$

The ordering now becomes simply $e \le f$ if and only if $eR \subseteq fR$, and the join and meet satisfy $(e \lor f)R = eR + fR$ and $(e \land f)R = eR \cap fR = (eR)(fR)$. It is shown in (Goodearl 1991, 99) that if $R$ is [von Neumann regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Neumann_regular) and right [self-injective](wikipedia://en/473775#Self-injective_rings), then the lattice is a [complete lattice](wikipedia://en/44777).
