# Ford circle

*Image: Ford\_circles\_colour.svg.* Ford circles for $p/q$ with $q$ from 1 to 20. Circles with $q \le 10$ are labelled as $\tfrac{p}{q}$ and color-coded according to $q$. Each circle is [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to the base line and its neighboring circles. Irreducible fractions with the same denominator have circles of the same size.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ford circle"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Ford circle** is a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), in a family of circles that are all tangent to the $x$-axis at [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) points. For each rational number $p/q$, expressed in lowest terms, there is a Ford circle whose [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_%28geometry%29) is at the point $(p/q,1/(2q^2))$ and whose radius is $1/(2q^2)$. It is tangent to the $x$-axis at its bottom point, $(p/q,0)$. The two Ford circles for rational numbers $p/q$ and $r/s$ (both in lowest terms) are [tangent circles](wikipedia://en/19349153) when $|ps-qr|=1$ and otherwise these two circles are disjoint.[^o5]

:::

## History {#History}

Ford circles are a special case of mutually tangent circles; the base line can be thought of as a circle with infinite radius. Systems of mutually tangent circles were studied by [Apollonius of Perga](wikipedia://en/257242), after whom the [problem of Apollonius](wikipedia://en/2642185) and the [Apollonian gasket](wikipedia://en/1049256) are named.[^o3] In the 17th century [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) discovered [Descartes' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes'_theorem), a relationship between the reciprocals of the radii of mutually tangent circles.[^o3]

Ford circles also appear in the [Sangaku](wikipedia://en/1055365) (geometrical puzzles) of [Japanese mathematics](wikipedia://en/1055369). A typical problem, which is presented on an 1824 tablet in the [Gunma Prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunma_Prefecture), covers the relationship of three touching circles with a common [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482). Given the size of the two outer large circles, what is the size of the small circle between them? The answer is equivalent to a Ford circle:[^o4]

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{r_\text{middle}}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r_\text{left}}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{r_\text{right}}}.$$

Ford circles are named after the American mathematician [Lester R. Ford, Sr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_R._Ford), who wrote about them in 1938.[^o5]

## Properties {#Properties}

*Image: Comparison\_Ford\_circles\_Farey\_diagram.svg.* Comparison of Ford circles and a Farey diagram with circular arcs for *n* from 1 to 9. Note that each arc intersects its corresponding circles at right angles. In \[ the SVG image,\] hover over a circle or curve to highlight it and its terms.

The Ford circle associated with the fraction $p/q$ is denoted by $C[p/q]$ or $C[p,q].$ There is a Ford circle associated with every [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024). In addition, the line $y=1$ is counted as a Ford circle – it can be thought of as the Ford circle associated with [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824), which is the case $p=1,q=0.$

Two different Ford circles are either [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359) or [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to one another. No two interiors of Ford circles intersect, even though there is a Ford circle tangent to the [*x*-axis](wikipedia://en/7706) at each point on it with [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) coordinates. If $p/q$ is between 0 and 1, the Ford circles that are tangent to $C[p/q]$ can be described variously as

1. the circles $C[r/s]$ where $|p s-q r|=1,$[^o5]
1. the circles associated with the fractions $r/s$ that are the neighbors of $p/q$ in some [Farey sequence](wikipedia://en/350164),[^o5] or
1. the circles $C[r/s]$ where $r/s$ is the next larger or the next smaller ancestor to $p/q$ in the [Stern–Brocot tree](wikipedia://en/2546511) or where $p/q$ is the next larger or next smaller ancestor to $r/s$.[^o5]

If $C[p/q]$ and $C[r/s]$ are two tangent Ford circles, then the circle through $(p/q,0)$ and $(r/s,0)$ (the x-coordinates of the centers of the Ford circles) and that is perpendicular to the $x$-axis (whose center is on the x-axis) also passes through the point where the two circles are tangent to one another.

The centers of the Ford circles constitute a discrete (and hence countable) subset of the plane, whose closure is the real axis - an uncountable set.

Ford circles can also be thought of as curves in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628). The [modular group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_group_Gamma) of transformations of the complex plane maps Ford circles to other Ford circles.[^o5]

Ford circles are a sub-set of the circles in the [Apollonian gasket](wikipedia://en/1049256) generated by the lines $y=0$ and $y=1$ and the circle $C[0/1].$[^o8]

By interpreting the upper half of the complex plane as a model of the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/241291) (the [Poincaré half-plane model](wikipedia://en/461942)), Ford circles can be interpreted as [horocycles](wikipedia://en/11084460). In [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291) any two horocycles are [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330). When these [horocycles](wikipedia://en/11084460) are [circumscribed](wikipedia://en/35520654) by [apeirogons](wikipedia://en/5658261) they [tile](wikipedia://en/321671) the hyperbolic plane with an [order-3 apeirogonal tiling](wikipedia://en/38758064).

## Total area of Ford circles {#Total_area_of_Ford_circles}

There is a link between the area of Ford circles, [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452) $\varphi,$ the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809) $\zeta,$ and [Apéry's constant](wikipedia://en/2216711) $\zeta(3).$[^o10] As no two Ford circles intersect, it follows immediately that the total area of the Ford circles

$$\left\{ C[p,q]: 0 < \frac{p}{q} \le 1 \right\}$$

is less than 1. In fact the total area of these Ford circles is given by a convergent sum, which can be evaluated. From the definition, the area is

$$A = \sum_{q\ge 1} \sum_{ (p, q)=1 \atop 1 \le p < q }\pi \left( \frac{1}{2 q^2} \right)^2.$$

Simplifying this expression gives

$$A = \frac{\pi}{4} \sum_{q\ge 1} \frac{1}{q^4} \sum_{ (p, q)=1 \atop 1 \le p < q } 1 = \frac{\pi}{4} \sum_{q\ge 1} \frac{\varphi(q)}{q^4} = \frac{\pi}{4} \frac{\zeta(3)}{\zeta(4)},$$

where the last equality reflects the [Dirichlet generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_generating_function) for [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452) $\varphi(q).$ Since $\zeta(4)=\pi^4/90,$ this finally becomes

$$A = \frac{45}{2} \frac{\zeta(3)}{\pi^3}\approx 0.872284041.$$

Note that as a matter of convention, the previous calculations excluded the circle of radius $\frac{1}{2}$ corresponding to the fraction $\frac{0}{1}$. It includes the complete circle for $\frac{1}{1}$, half of which lies outside the unit interval, hence the sum is still the fraction of the unit square covered by Ford circles.

## Ford spheres (3D) {#Ford_spheres_(3D)}

*Image: Ford-Kugeln.png.* Ford spheres above the complex domain

The concept of Ford circles can be generalized from the rational numbers to the [Gaussian rationals](wikipedia://en/318052), giving Ford spheres. In this construction, the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) are embedded as a plane in a three-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), and for each Gaussian rational point in this plane one constructs a sphere tangent to the plane at that point. For a Gaussian rational represented in lowest terms as $p/q$, the diameter of this sphere should be $1/2q\bar q$ where $\bar q$ represents the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) of $q$. The resulting spheres are [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) for pairs of Gaussian rationals $P/Q$ and $p/q$ with $|Pq-pQ|=1$, and otherwise they do not intersect each other.[^o12][^o13]
