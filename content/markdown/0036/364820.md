# Quotient module

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quotient module"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), given a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) and a [submodule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule), one can construct their **quotient module**.[^o2][^o3] This construction, described below, is very similar to that of a [quotient vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_vector_space).[^o4] It differs from analogous quotient constructions of [rings](wikipedia://en/48404) and [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) by the fact that in the latter cases, the [subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) that is used for defining the quotient is not of the same nature as the ambient space (that is, a [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) is the quotient of a ring by an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977), not a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145), and a [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) is the quotient of a group by a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918), not by a general [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011)).

:::

Given a module $A$ over a ring $R$, and a submodule $B$ of $A$, the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) $A/B$ is defined by the [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259)

$a \sim b$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $b - a \in B,$

for any $a, b$ in $A$.[^o5] The elements of $A/B$ are the [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) $[a] = a+B = \{a+b:b \in B\}.$ The [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $\pi: A \to A/B$ sending $a$ in $A$ to its equivalence class $a + B$ is called the *[quotient map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_map_%28topology%29)* or the *projection map*, and is a [module homomorphism](wikipedia://en/474118).

The [addition](wikipedia://en/61338) [operation](wikipedia://en/4140245) on $A/B$ is defined for two equivalence classes as the equivalence class of the sum of two [representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_%28mathematics%29) from these classes; and [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373) of elements of $A/B$ by elements of $R$ is defined similarly. Note that it has to be shown that these operations are [well-defined](wikipedia://en/253456). Then $A/B$ becomes itself an $R$-module, called the *quotient module*. In symbols, for all $a, b$ in $A$ and $r$ in $R$:

$$\begin{aligned} & (a+B)+(b+B) := (a+b)+B, \\ & r \cdot (a+B) := (r \cdot a)+B. \end{aligned}$$

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Consider the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065), $\mathbb{R}[X]$ with real [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203), and the $\mathbb{R}[X]$-module $A=\mathbb{R}[X],$ . Consider the submodule

$$B = (X^2+1) \mathbb{R}[X]$$

of $A$, that is, the submodule of all polynomials divisible by $X^{2} + 1$. It follows that the equivalence relation determined by this module will be

$P(X) \sim Q(X)$ if and only if $P(X)$ and $Q(X)$ give the same remainder when divided by $X^{2} + 1$.

Therefore, in the quotient module $A/B$, $X^{2} + 1$ is the same as 0; so one can view $A/B$ as obtained from $\mathbb{R}[X]$ by setting $X^{2} + 1 = 0$. This quotient module is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), viewed as a module over the real numbers $\mathbb{R}.$
