# Free product

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Free product"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), the **free product** is an operation that takes two [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$ and $H$ and constructs a new group $G * H$. The result contains both $G$ and $H$ as [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011), is [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by the elements of these subgroups, and is the “[universal](wikipedia://en/32245)” group having these properties, in the sense that any two homomorphisms from $G$ and $H$ into a group $K$ factor uniquely through a homomorphism from $G * H$ to $K$. Unless one of the groups $G$ and $H$ is trivial, the free product is always infinite. The construction of a free product is similar in spirit to the construction of a [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) (the universal group with a given set of generators).

:::

The free product is the [coproduct](wikipedia://en/310889) in the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092). That is, the free product plays the same role in group theory that [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) plays in [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), or that the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) plays in [module theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Module_theory). Even if the groups are commutative, their free product is not, unless one of the two groups is the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059). Therefore, the free product is not the coproduct in the [category of abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454748).

The free product is important in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) because of [van Kampen's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Kampen's_theorem), which states that the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of two [path-connected](wikipedia://en/6233) [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) whose intersection is also path-connected is always an **amalgamated free product** of the fundamental groups of the spaces. In particular, the fundamental group of the [wedge sum](wikipedia://en/684143) of two spaces (i.e. the space obtained by joining two spaces together at a single point) is, under certain conditions given in the Seifert van-Kampen theorem, the free product of the fundamental groups of the spaces.[^o2]

Free products are also important in [Bass–Serre theory](wikipedia://en/18673708), the study of groups [acting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) by automorphisms on [trees](wikipedia://en/48560). Specifically, any group acting with finite vertex stabilizers on a tree may be constructed from [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707) using amalgamated free products and [HNN extensions](wikipedia://en/2823319). Using the action of the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019) on a certain [tessellation](wikipedia://en/321671) of the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/241291), it follows from this theory that the modular group is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the free product of [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) of orders 4 and 6 amalgamated over a cyclic group of order 2.

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

If $G$ and $H$ are groups, a *[word](wikipedia://en/12792326)* on $G$ and $H$ is a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of the form $s_1 s_2 \cdots s_n$, where each $s_i$ is either an element of $G$ or an element of $H$. Such a word may be *reduced* using the following operations:

- remove an instance of the identity element (of either $G$ or $H$), or
- replace a pair $g_1g_2$ by its product in $G$, or a pair $h_1h_2$ by its product in $H$.

Every reduced word is either the empty sequence, contains exactly one element of $G$ or $H$, or is an alternating sequence of elements of $G$ and elements of $H$, e.g.

$$g_1 h_1 g_2 h_2 \cdots g_k h_k.$$

The **free product** $G * H$ is the group whose elements are the reduced words in $G$ and $H$, under the operation of concatenation followed by reduction.

For example, if $G$ is the infinite cyclic group $\langle x\rangle$, and $H$ is the infinite cyclic group $\langle y\rangle$, then every element of $G * H$ is an alternating product of powers of $x$ with powers of $y$. In this case, $G * H$ is isomorphic to the free group generated by $x$ and $y$.

:::

## Presentation {#Presentation}

Suppose that

$$G = \langle S_G \mid R_G \rangle$$

is a [presentation](wikipedia://en/99494) for *G* (where *S*<sub>*G*</sub> is a set of generators and *R*<sub>*G*</sub> is a set of relations), and suppose that

$$H = \langle S_H \mid R_H \rangle$$

is a presentation for *H*. Then

$$G * H = \langle S_G \cup S_H \mid R_G \cup R_H \rangle.$$

That is, *G* ∗ *H* is generated by the generators for *G* together with the generators for *H*, with relations consisting of the relations from *G* together with the relations from *H* (assume here no notational clashes so that these are in fact [disjoint unions](wikipedia://en/188946)).

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

For example, suppose that *G* is a cyclic group of order 4,

$$G = \langle x \mid x^4 = 1 \rangle,$$

and *H* is a cyclic group of order 5

$$H = \langle y \mid y^5 = 1 \rangle.$$

Then *G* ∗ *H* is the infinite group

$$G * H = \langle x, y \mid x^4 = y^5 = 1 \rangle.$$

Because there are no relations in a free group, the free product of free groups is always a free group. In particular,

$$F_m * F_n \cong F_{m+n},$$

where *F*<sub>*n*</sub> denotes the free group on *n* generators.

Another example is the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019) $PSL_2(\mathbf Z)$. It is isomorphic to the free product of two cyclic groups:[^o8]

$$PSL_2(\mathbf Z) \cong (\mathbf Z / 2 \mathbf Z) \ast (\mathbf Z / 3 \mathbf Z).$$

:::

## Generalization: Free product with amalgamation {#Generalization:_Free_product_with_amalgamation}

The more general construction of **free product with amalgamation** is correspondingly a special kind of [pushout](wikipedia://en/582024) in the same [category](wikipedia://en/5869). Suppose $G$ and $H$ are given as before, along with monomorphisms (i.e. injective [group homomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_homomorphisms)):

$\varphi : F \rightarrow G \ \,$ and $\ \, \psi : F \rightarrow H,$

where $F$ is some arbitrary group. Start with the free product $G * H$ and adjoin as relations

$$\varphi(f)\psi(f)^{-1}=1$$

for every $f$ in $F$. In other words, take the [smallest normal subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_closure) $N$ of $G * H$ containing all elements on the [left-hand side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-hand_side_and_right-hand_side_of_an_equation) of the above equation, which are tacitly being considered in $G * H$ by means of the inclusions of $G$ and $H$ in their free product. The free product with amalgamation of $G$ and $H$, with respect to $\varphi$ and $\psi$, is the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526)

$$(G * H)/N.\,$$

The amalgamation has forced an identification between $\varphi(F)$ in $G$ with $\psi(F)$ in $H$, element by element. This is the construction needed to compute the fundamental group of two connected spaces joined along a path-connected subspace, with $F$ taking the role of the fundamental group of the subspace. See: [Seifert–van Kampen theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seifert–van_Kampen_theorem).

Karrass and [Solitar](wikipedia://en/43651852) have given a description of the subgroups of a free product with amalgamation.[^o11] For example, the homomorphisms from $G$ and $H$ to the quotient group $(G * H)/N$ that are induced by $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are both injective, as is the induced homomorphism from $F$.

Free products with amalgamation and a closely related notion of [HNN extension](wikipedia://en/2823319) are basic building blocks in Bass–Serre theory of groups acting on trees.

:::

## In other branches {#In_other_branches}

One may similarly define free products of other algebraic structures than groups, including [algebras over a field](wikipedia://en/191788). Free products of algebras of [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) play the same role in defining "[freeness](wikipedia://en/25130943)" in the theory of [free probability](wikipedia://en/679696) that [Cartesian products](wikipedia://en/24104095) play in defining [statistical independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence) in classical [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542).
