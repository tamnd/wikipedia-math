## Classical notation {#Classical_notation}

::: {#o3 .entry cue=glossary-term name="Ricci calculus"}

**[Ricci calculus](wikipedia://en/35456546)**

The earliest foundation of tensor theory – tensor index notation.[^o4]

:::

::: {#o5 .entry cue=glossary-term name="Order of a tensor"}

**[Order of a tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_order)**

The components of a tensor with respect to a basis is an indexed array. The *order* of a tensor is the number of indices needed. Some texts may refer to the tensor order using the term *degree* or *rank*.

:::

::: {#o6 .entry cue=glossary-term name="Rank of a tensor"}

**[Rank of a tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_rank)**

The rank of a tensor is the minimum number of rank-one tensor that must be summed to obtain the tensor. A rank-one tensor may be defined as expressible as the outer product of the number of nonzero vectors needed to obtain the correct order.

:::

::: {#o7 .entry cue=glossary-term name="Dyadic tensor"}

**[Dyadic tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyadic_tensor)**

A *dyadic* tensor is a tensor of order two, and may be represented as a square [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859). In contrast, a *dyad* is specifically a dyadic tensor of rank one.

:::

::: {#o8 .entry cue=glossary-term name="Einstein notation"}

**[Einstein notation](wikipedia://en/195407)**

This notation is based on the understanding that whenever a multidimensional array contains a repeated index letter, the default interpretation is that the product is summed over all permitted values of the index. For example, if *a<sub>ij</sub>* is a matrix, then under this convention *a<sub>ii</sub>* is its [trace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace_%28matrix%29). The Einstein convention is widely used in physics and engineering texts, to the extent that if summation is not to be applied, it is normal to note that explicitly.

:::

::: {#o11 .entry cue=glossary-term name="Covariant tensor"}

:::

::: {#o12 .entry cue=glossary-term name="Contravariant tensor"}

**[Contravariant](wikipedia://en/202886) tensor**

The classical interpretation is by components. For example, in the differential form *a<sub>i</sub>dx<sup>i</sup>* the **components** *a<sub>i</sub>* are a covariant vector. That means all indices are lower; contravariant means all indices are upper.

:::

::: {#o13 .entry cue=glossary-term name="Mixed tensor"}

**[Mixed tensor](wikipedia://en/202772)**

This refers to any tensor that has both lower and upper indices.

:::

::: {#o14 .entry cue=glossary-term name="Cartesian tensor"}

**Cartesian tensor**

Cartesian tensors are widely used in various branches of [continuum mechanics](wikipedia://en/5918), such as [fluid mechanics](wikipedia://en/2684988) and [elasticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_%28physics%29). In classical [continuum mechanics](wikipedia://en/5918), the space of interest is usually 3-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), as is the tangent space at each point. If we restrict the local coordinates to be [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) with the same scale centered at the point of interest, the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) is the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890). This means that there is no need to distinguish covariant and contravariant components, and furthermore there is no need to distinguish tensors and [tensor densities](wikipedia://en/1763396). All [Cartesian-tensor](wikipedia://en/1824845) indices are written as subscripts. [Cartesian tensors](wikipedia://en/1824845) achieve considerable computational simplification at the cost of generality and of some theoretical insight.

## Algebraic notation {#Algebraic_notation}

This avoids the initial use of components, and is distinguished by the explicit use of the tensor product symbol.

::: {#o21 .entry cue=glossary-term name="Tensor product"}

**Tensor product**

If *v* and *w* are vectors in [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) *V* and *W* respectively, then

$$v \otimes w$$

is a tensor in

$$V \otimes W.$$

That is, the ⊗ operation is a [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948), but it takes values into a fresh space (it is in a strong sense *external*). The ⊗ operation is a [bilinear map](wikipedia://en/4365); but no other conditions are applied to it.

:::

::: {#o22 .entry cue=glossary-term name="Pure tensor"}

**Pure tensor**

A pure tensor of *V* ⊗ *W* is one that is of the form *v* ⊗ *w*.

It could be written dyadically *a<sup>i</sup>b<sup>j</sup>*, or more accurately *a<sup>i</sup>b<sup>j</sup>* **e**<sub>*i*</sub> ⊗ **f**<sub>*j*</sub>, where the **e**<sub>*i*</sub> are a basis for *V* and the **f**<sub>*j*</sub> a basis for *W*. Therefore, unless *V* and *W* have the same dimension, the array of components need not be square. Such *pure* tensors are not generic: if both *V* and *W* have dimension greater than 1, there will be tensors that are not pure, and there will be non-linear conditions for a tensor to satisfy, to be pure. For more see [Segre embedding](wikipedia://en/1623980).

:::

::: {#o23 .entry cue=glossary-term name="Tensor algebra"}

**Tensor algebra**

In the tensor algebra *T*(*V*) of a vector space *V*, the operation $\otimes$ becomes a normal (internal) [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948). A consequence is that *T*(*V*) has infinite dimension unless *V* has dimension 0. The [free algebra](wikipedia://en/494669) on a set *X* is for practical purposes the same as the tensor algebra on the vector space with *X* as basis.

:::

::: {#o24 .entry cue=glossary-term name="Hodge star operator"}

**Hodge star operator**

:::

::: {#o25 .entry cue=glossary-term name="Exterior power"}

**Exterior power**

:::

The [wedge product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedge_product) is the anti-symmetric form of the ⊗ operation. The quotient space of *T*(*V*) on which it becomes an internal operation is the *[exterior algebra](wikipedia://en/221537)* of *V*; it is a [graded algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded_algebra), with the graded piece of weight *k* being called the *k*-th **exterior power** of *V*.

::: {#o26 .entry cue=glossary-term name="Symmetric power, symmetric algebra"}

**Symmetric power, symmetric algebra**

This is the invariant way of constructing [polynomial algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_algebra).

:::
