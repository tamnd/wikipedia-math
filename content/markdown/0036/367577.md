# Antipodal point

*Image: Great circle, axis, and poles.svg.* The two points $P$ and $P$ (red) are *antipodal* because they are ends of a diameter $PP$, a segment of the *axis* $a$ (purple) passing through the sphere's center $O$ (black). $P$ and $P$ are the *poles* of a great circle $g$ (green) whose points are equidistant from each (with a central right angle). Any great circle $s$ (blue) passing through the poles is *secondary* to $g$.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Antipodal point"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), two points of a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) (or [n-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782), including a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220)) are called **antipodal** or **diametrically opposite** if they are the endpoints of a [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007), a straight [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) between two points on a sphere and passing through its [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_%28geometry%29).[^o2]

:::

Given any point on a sphere, its antipodal point is the unique point at greatest [distance](wikipedia://en/39378), whether measured intrinsically ([great-circle distance](wikipedia://en/379733) on the surface of the sphere) or extrinsically ([chordal](wikipedia://en/297947) distance through the sphere's interior). Every [great circle](wikipedia://en/48082) on a sphere passing through a point also passes through its antipodal point, and there are infinitely many great circles passing through a pair of antipodal points (unlike the situation for any non-antipodal pair of points, which have a unique great circle passing through both). Many results in spherical geometry depend on choosing non-antipodal points, and [degenerate](wikipedia://en/232952) if antipodal points are allowed; for example, a [spherical triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_triangle) degenerates to an underspecified [lune](wikipedia://en/23024648) if two of the vertices are antipodal.

The point antipodal to a given point is called its **antipodes**, from the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) ἀντίποδες (grc) meaning "opposite feet"; see Antipodes#Etymology. Sometimes the *s* is dropped, and this is rendered **antipode**, a [back-formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Back-formation).

## Higher mathematics {#Higher_mathematics}

The concept of *antipodal points* is generalized to [spheres](wikipedia://en/27859) of any dimension: two points on the sphere are antipodal if they are opposite *through the centre*. Each line through the centre intersects the sphere in two points, one for each [ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28geometry%29) emanating from the centre, and these two points are antipodal.

The [Borsuk–Ulam theorem](wikipedia://en/4064) is a result from [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) dealing with such pairs of points. It says that any [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) from $S^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^n$ maps some pair of antipodal points in $S^n$ to the same point in $\mathbb{R}^n.$ Here, $S^n$ denotes the $n$-dimensional sphere and $\mathbb{R}^n$ is $n$-dimensional [real coordinate space](wikipedia://en/1482083).

The **antipodal map** $A : S^n \to S^n$ sends every point on the sphere to its antipodal point. If points on the $n$-sphere are represented as [displacement vectors](wikipedia://en/1345771) from the sphere's center in Euclidean $(n+1)$-space, then two antipodal points are represented by additive inverses $\mathbf{v}$ and $-\mathbf{v},$ and the antipodal map can be defined as $A(\mathbf{x}) = -\mathbf{x}.$ The antipodal map preserves [orientation](wikipedia://en/187446) (is [homotopic](wikipedia://en/212250) to the [identity map](wikipedia://en/15069))[^o4] when $n$ is odd, and reverses it when $n$ is even. Its [degree](wikipedia://en/3094450) is $(-1)^{n+1}.$

If antipodal points are identified (considered equivalent), the sphere becomes a model of [real projective space](wikipedia://en/1014694).
