# Timothy Gowers

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Timothy Gowers"}

**Sir William Timothy Gowers**, (; born 20 November 1963)[^o3] is a British [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902). He is the holder of the [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) chair at the [Collège de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collège_de_France), a research professor at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge) and a fellow of [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge). In 1998, he received the [Fields Medal](wikipedia://en/10859) for research connecting the fields of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) and [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170).

:::

## Education {#Education}

Gowers attended [King's College School, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College_School,_Cambridge), as a choirboy in the [King's College choir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choir_of_King's_College,_Cambridge), and then [Eton College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eton_College)[^o3] as a [King's Scholar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_Scholar), where he was taught mathematics by [Norman Routledge](wikipedia://en/63664151).[^o8] In 1981, Gowers won a gold medal at the [International Mathematical Olympiad](wikipedia://en/82208) with a perfect score.[^o9][^o10] He completed his PhD, with a [dissertation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissertation) on *Symmetric Structures in Banach Spaces*[^o11] at [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge) in 1990, supervised by [Béla Bollobás](wikipedia://en/614956).[^o11][^o4]

## Career and research {#Career_and_research}

After his PhD, Gowers was elected to a Junior Research Fellowship at Trinity College. From 1991 until his return to Cambridge in 1995 he was lecturer at [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London). He was elected to the Rouse Ball Professorship at Cambridge in 1998. During 2000–2 he was visiting professor at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University). In May 2020 it was announced[^o13] that he would be taking up the *Chaire de Combinatoire* at the [College de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_de_France) beginning in October 2020, though he [^o14] continues to reside in Cambridge and maintain a part-time affiliation at the university, as well as enjoy the privileges of his life fellowship of Trinity College.

Gowers initially worked on [Banach spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banach_spaces). He used combinatorial tools in proving several of [Stefan Banach](wikipedia://en/29646)'s conjectures in the subject, in particular constructing a Banach space with almost no symmetry, serving as a counterexample to several other conjectures.[^o15] With [Bernard Maurey](wikipedia://en/56469825) he resolved the "unconditional basic sequence problem" in 1992, showing that not every infinite-dimensional Banach space has an infinite-dimensional subspace that admits an [unconditional Schauder basis](wikipedia://en/3460662).[^o16]

After this, Gowers turned to combinatorics and combinatorial number theory. In 1997 he proved[^o17] that the [Szemerédi regularity lemma](wikipedia://en/1962417) necessarily comes with tower-type bounds.

In 1998, Gowers proved[^o18] the first effective bounds for [Szemerédi's theorem](wikipedia://en/591703), showing that any subset $A \subset \{1,\dots, N\}$ free of *k*-term arithmetic progressions has cardinality $O(N (\log \log N)^{-c_k})$ for an appropriate $c_k > 0$. One of the ingredients in Gowers's argument is a tool now known as the Balog–Szemerédi–Gowers theorem, which has found many further applications. He also introduced the [Gowers norms](wikipedia://en/38041703), a tool in [arithmetic combinatorics](wikipedia://en/18243381), and provided the basic techniques for analysing them. This work was further developed by [Ben Green](wikipedia://en/1927952) and [Terence Tao](wikipedia://en/1118498), leading to the [Green–Tao theorem](wikipedia://en/7006166).

In 2003, Gowers established a regularity lemma for [hypergraphs](wikipedia://en/232535),[^o19] analogous to the [Szemerédi regularity lemma](wikipedia://en/1962417) for [graphs](wikipedia://en/325806).

In 2005, he introduced[^o20] the notion of a [quasirandom group](wikipedia://en/69381645).

More recently, Gowers has worked on [Ramsey theory](wikipedia://en/157175) in [random graphs](wikipedia://en/358477) and random sets with [David Conlon](wikipedia://en/36837348), and has turned his attention[^o21] to other problems such as the [P versus NP problem](wikipedia://en/6115). He has also developed an interest, in joint work with Mohan Ganesalingam,[^o22] in automated problem solving.

Gowers has an [Erdős number](wikipedia://en/9742) of three.[^o23]

### Popularisation work {#Popularisation_work}

Gowers has written several works popularising mathematics, including *Mathematics: A Very Short Introduction* (2002),[^o25] which describes modern mathematical research for the general reader. He was consulted about the 2005 film *[Proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_%282005_film%29)*, starring [Gwyneth Paltrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwyneth_Paltrow) and [Anthony Hopkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Hopkins). He edited *[The Princeton Companion to Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Princeton_Companion_to_Mathematics)* (2008), which traces the development of various branches and concepts of modern mathematics.[^o26] For his work on this book, he won the 2011 [Euler Book Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_Book_Prize) of the [Mathematical Association of America](wikipedia://en/198772).[^o27] In May 2020 he was made a professor at the Collège de France, a historic institution dedicated to popularising science.[^o28]

### Blogging {#Blogging}

After asking on his blog whether "massively collaborative mathematics" was possible,[^o30] he solicited comments on his blog from people who wanted to try to solve mathematical problems collaboratively.[^o31] The first problem in what is called the Polymath Project, Polymath1, was to find a new combinatorial proof to the density version of the [Hales–Jewett theorem](wikipedia://en/640714). After seven weeks, Gowers wrote on his blog that the problem was "probably solved".[^o32]

In 2009, with Olof Sisask and Alex Frolkin, he invited people to post comments to his blog to contribute to a collection of methods of mathematical problem solving.[^o33] Contributors to this Wikipedia-style project, called Tricki.org, include [Terence Tao](wikipedia://en/1118498) and [Ben Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_J._Green).[^o34]

### Elsevier boycott {#Elsevier_boycott}

In 2012, Gowers posted to his blog to call for a boycott of the publishing house [Elsevier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elsevier).[^o36][^o37] A petition ensued, branded the [Cost of Knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cost_of_Knowledge) project, in which researchers commit to stop supporting Elsevier journals. Commenting on the petition in *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)*, Alok Jha credited Gowers with starting an [Academic Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_Spring).[^o38][^o39]

In 2016, Gowers started [Discrete Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_Analysis) to demonstrate that a high-quality mathematics journal could be inexpensively produced outside of the traditional academic publishing industry.[^o40]

### Awards and honours {#Awards_and_honours}

In 1994, Gowers was an invited speaker at the [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) in [Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zurich) where he discussed the theory of infinite-dimensional Banach spaces.[^o42] In 1996, Gowers received the Prize of the [European Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/969470), and in 1998 the [Fields Medal](wikipedia://en/10859) for research on [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) and [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170).[^o43][^o44] In 1999 he became a fellow of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society) and a member of the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society) in 2010.[^o45] In 2012 he was knighted by the British monarch for services to mathematics.[^o47] He also sits on the selection committee for the mathematics award, given under the auspices of the [Shaw Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaw_Prize). He was listed in [Nature's 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature's_10) people who mattered in 2012.[^o37]

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Timothy Gowers was born on 20 November 1963, in [Marlborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marlborough,_Wiltshire), [Wiltshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiltshire), [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England).[^o42]

Gowers's father was [Patrick Gowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Gowers), a composer; his great-grandfather was [Sir Ernest Gowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Gowers), a British civil servant who was best known for guides to English usage; and his great-great-grandfather was [Sir William Gowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Richard_Gowers), a [neurologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurology). He has two siblings, the writer Rebecca Gowers, and the violinist Katharine Gowers. He has five children[^o49] and plays jazz piano.[^o3]

In 1988, Gowers married [Emily Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emily_Gowers), a classicist and Cambridge academic: they divorced in 2007. Together they had three children. In 2008, he married for a second time, to Julie Barrau, a University Lecturer in British Medieval History at the University of Cambridge. They have two children together.[^o50]

In November 2012, Gowers opted to undergo [catheter ablation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catheter_ablation) to treat a sporadic [atrial fibrillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrial_fibrillation), after performing a mathematical risk–benefit analysis to decide whether to have the treatment.[^o51]
