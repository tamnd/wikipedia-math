# Emil Leon Post

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Emil Leon Post"}

**Emil Leon Post** (; February 11, 1897 – April 21, 1954) was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and [logician](wikipedia://en/46426065). He is best known for his work in the field that eventually became known as [computability theory](wikipedia://en/155414).

:::

## Life {#Life}

Post was born in [Augustów](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustów), [Suwałki Governorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suwałki_Governorate), [Congress Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_Poland), [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire) (now Poland) into a [Polish-Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Jews) family that immigrated to New York City in May 1904. His parents were Arnold and Pearl Post.

Post had been interested in astronomy, but at the age of twelve lost his left arm in a car accident. This loss was a significant obstacle to being a professional astronomer, leading to his decision to pursue mathematics rather than astronomy.[^n1]

Post attended the [Townsend Harris High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Townsend_Harris_High_School) and continued on to graduate from [City College of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_College_of_New_York) in 1917 with a B.S. in mathematics.[^o2]

After completing his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_philosophy) in mathematics in 1920 at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University), supervised by [Cassius Jackson Keyser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassius_Jackson_Keyser), he did a post-doctorate at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) in the 1920–1921 academic year. Post then became a high school mathematics teacher in New York City.

Post married Gertrude Singer (1900–1956) in 1929, with whom he had a daughter, Phyllis Post Goodman (1932–1995).[^o7] Post spent at most three hours a day on research on the advice of his doctor in order to avoid manic attacks, which he had been experiencing since his year at Princeton.[^o8]

In 1936, he was appointed to the mathematics department at the City College of New York. He died in April 1954 of a [heart attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myocardial_infarction) following [electroshock treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroconvulsive_therapy) for [depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depression_%28mood%29).[^o8][^o9]

## Early work {#Early_work}

In his doctoral thesis, later shortened and published as "Introduction to a General Theory of Elementary Propositions" (1921), Post proved, among other things, that the [propositional calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus) of *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)* was complete: all [tautologies](wikipedia://en/4495335) are [theorems](wikipedia://en/30977), given the *Principia* axioms and the rules of [substitution](wikipedia://en/5438948) and [modus ponens](wikipedia://en/18900). Post also devised [truth tables](wikipedia://en/25512250) independently of [C. S. Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) and [Ludwig Wittgenstein](wikipedia://en/17741) and put them to good mathematical use. [Jean van Heijenoort](wikipedia://en/2137251)'s well-known source book on mathematical logic (1966) reprinted Post's classic 1921 article setting out these results.

While at Princeton, Post came very close to discovering the incompleteness of *Principia Mathematica*, which [Kurt Gödel](wikipedia://en/16736) proved in 1931. Post initially failed to publish his ideas as he believed he needed a 'complete analysis' for them to be accepted.[^o5] As Post said in a postcard to Gödel in 1938:

I would have discovered Gödel's theorem in 1921—if I had been Gödel.[^o11]

## Recursion theory {#Recursion_theory}

In 1936, Post developed, independently of [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208), a mathematical model of computation that was essentially equivalent to the [Turing machine](wikipedia://en/30403) model. Intending this as the first of a series of models of equivalent power but increasing complexity, he titled his paper [Formulation 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formulation_1). This model is sometimes called "Post's machine" or a [Post–Turing machine](wikipedia://en/3688147), but is not to be confused with [Post's tag machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tag_system) or other special kinds of [Post canonical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post_canonical_system), a computational model using [string rewriting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_rewriting) and developed by Post in the 1920s but first published in 1943. Post's rewrite technique is now ubiquitous in programming language specification and design, and so with [Church](wikipedia://en/49731)'s [lambda calculus](wikipedia://en/18203) is a salient influence of classical modern logic on practical computing. Post devised a method of 'auxiliary symbols' by which he could canonically represent any Post-generative language, and indeed any [computable function](wikipedia://en/1139338) or [computable set](wikipedia://en/332264) at all.

Correspondence systems were introduced by Post in 1946 to give simple examples of [undecidability](wikipedia://en/15631055).[^o13] He showed that the [Post correspondence problem](wikipedia://en/64685) (PCP) of satisfying their constraints is, in general, undecidable. The undecidability of the correspondence problem turned out to be exactly what was needed to obtain undecidability results in the theory of [formal languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_languages).

In an influential address to the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822) in 1944, he raised the question of the existence of an uncomputable [recursively enumerable set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursively_enumerable_set) whose [Turing degree](wikipedia://en/764405) is less than that of the [halting problem](wikipedia://en/21391870). This question, which became known as [Post's problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post's_problem), stimulated much research. It was solved in the affirmative in the 1950s by the introduction of the powerful [priority method](wikipedia://en/764405#Post's_problem_and_the_priority_method) in [computability theory](wikipedia://en/155414).

## Polyadic groups {#Polyadic_groups}

Post made a fundamental and still influential contribution to the theory of [polyadic, or *n*-ary, groups](wikipedia://en/23340612) in a long paper published in 1940. His major theorem showed that a polyadic group is the iterated product of elements of a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), such that the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) is [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327) of order *n* − 1. He also demonstrated that a polyadic group operation on a set can be expressed in terms of a group operation on the same set. The paper contains many other important results.
