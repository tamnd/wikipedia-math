# Functional equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Functional equation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **functional equation** is, in the broadest meaning, an [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) in which one or several functions appear as [unknowns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unknown_%28mathematics%29).[^o2][^o3] So, [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309) and [integral equations](wikipedia://en/474234) are functional equations. However, a more restricted meaning is often used, where a *functional equation* is an equation that relates several values of the same function. For example, the [logarithm functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithm_function) are [essentially characterized](wikipedia://en/17860#Characterization_by_the_product_formula) by the *logarithmic functional equation* $\log(xy)=\log(x) + \log(y)$.

:::

If the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of the unknown function is supposed to be the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474), the function is generally viewed as a [sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence_%28mathematics%29), and, in this case, a functional equation (in the narrower meaning) is called a [recurrence relation](wikipedia://en/146806). Thus the term *functional equation* is used mainly for [real functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_function) and [complex functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_function). Moreover a [smoothness condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) is often assumed for the solutions, since without such a condition, most functional equations have highly irregular solutions. For example, the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316) is a function that satisfies the functional equation $f (x + 1) = x f (x)$ and the initial value $f (1) = 1.$ There are many functions that satisfy these conditions, but the gamma function is the unique one that is [meromorphic](wikipedia://en/80857) in the whole complex plane, and [logarithmically convex](wikipedia://en/1503566) for $x$ real and positive ([Bohr–Mollerup theorem](wikipedia://en/780918)).

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- [Recurrence relations](wikipedia://en/146806) can be seen as functional equations in functions over the integers or natural numbers, in which the differences between terms' indexes can be seen as an application of the [shift operator](wikipedia://en/467384). For example, the recurrence relation defining the [Fibonacci numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_numbers), $F_{n} = F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}$, where $F_0=0$ and $F_1=1$
- $f(x+P) = f(x)$, which characterizes the [periodic functions](wikipedia://en/76848)
- $f(x) = f(-x)$, which characterizes the [even functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_function), and likewise $f(x) = -f(-x)$, which characterizes the [odd functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_function)
- $f(f(x)) = g(x)$, which characterizes the [functional square roots](wikipedia://en/7207519) of a function $g$
- $f(x + y) = f(x) + f(y)$ ([Cauchy's functional equation](wikipedia://en/5270898)), satisfied by [linear maps](wikipedia://en/18102). The equation may, contingent on the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840), also have other pathological nonlinear solutions, whose existence can be proven with a [Hamel basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamel_basis) for the real numbers
- $f(x + y) = f(x)f(y),$ satisfied by all [exponential functions](wikipedia://en/9678). Like Cauchy's additive functional equation, this too may have pathological, discontinuous solutions
- $f(xy) = f(x) + f(y)$, satisfied by all [logarithmic](wikipedia://en/17860) functions and, over coprime integer arguments, [additive functions](wikipedia://en/87372)
- $f(xy) = f(x) f(y)$, satisfied by all [power functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_function) and, over coprime integer arguments, [multiplicative functions](wikipedia://en/20059)
- $f(x + y) + f(x - y) = 2[f(x) + f(y)]$ (quadratic equation or [parallelogram law](wikipedia://en/362953))
- $f((x + y)/2) = (f(x) + f(y))/2$ ([Jensen's functional equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jensen's_functional_equation))
- $g(x + y) + g(x - y) = 2[g(x) g(y)]$ ([d'Alembert's functional equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D'Alembert's_functional_equation))
- $f(h(x)) = h(x + 1)$ ([Abel equation](wikipedia://en/5834526))
- $f(h(x)) = cf(x)$ ([Schröder's equation](wikipedia://en/5834541)).
- $f(h(x)) = (f(x))^c$ ([Böttcher's equation](wikipedia://en/34458555)).
- $f(h(x)) = h'(x)f(x)$ ([Julia's equation](wikipedia://en/5834541#Functional_significance)).
- $f(xy) = \sum g_l(x) h_l(y)$ (Levi-Civita),
- $f(x+y) = f(x)g(y)+f(y)g(x)$ ([sine addition formula](wikipedia://en/25804104#Angle_sum_and_difference_identities) and [hyperbolic sine addition formula](wikipedia://en/56567)),
- $g(x+y) = g(x)g(y)-f(y)f(x)$ ([cosine addition formula](wikipedia://en/25804104#Angle_sum_and_difference_identities)),
- $g(x+y) = g(x)g(y)+f(y)f(x)$ ([hyperbolic cosine addition formula](wikipedia://en/56567)).
- The [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_law) and [associative laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative_law) are functional equations. In its familiar form, the associative law is expressed by writing the [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) in [infix notation](wikipedia://en/199014), $$(a \circ b) \circ c = a \circ (b \circ c),$$ but if we write *f*(*a*, *b*) instead of $a \circ b$ then the associative law looks more like a conventional functional equation, $$f(f(a, b),c) = f(a, f(b, c)).$$
- The functional equation $$f(s) = 2^s\pi^{s-1}\sin\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\Gamma(1-s)f(1-s)$$ is satisfied by the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809). The capital $\Gamma$ denotes the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316).
- The gamma function is the unique solution of the following system of three equations:
  - $$f(x)={f(x+1) \over x}$$
  - $$f(y)f\left(y+\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2^{2y-1}}f(2y)$$
  - $f(z)f(1-z)={\pi \over \sin(\pi z)}$10([Euler's](wikipedia://en/17902) [reflection formula](wikipedia://en/4025531))
- The functional equation $$f\left({az+b\over cz+d}\right) = (cz+d)^k f(z)$$ where $a, b, c, d$ are [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) satisfying $ad - bc = 1$, i.e. $\begin{vmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{vmatrix}$ = 1, defines $f$ to be a [modular form](wikipedia://en/286000) of order $k$.

One feature that all of the examples listed above have in common is that, in each case, two or more known functions (sometimes multiplication by a constant, sometimes addition of two variables, sometimes the [identity function](wikipedia://en/15069)) are inside the argument of the unknown functions to be solved for.

When it comes to asking for *all* solutions, it may be the case that conditions from [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) should be applied; for example, in the case of the *Cauchy equation* mentioned above, the solutions that are [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) are the 'reasonable' ones, while other solutions that are not likely to have practical application can be constructed (by using a [Hamel basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamel_basis) for the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) as [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024)). The [Bohr–Mollerup theorem](wikipedia://en/780918) is another well-known example.

### Involutions {#Involutions}

The [involutions](wikipedia://en/293450) are characterized by the functional equation $f(f(x)) = x$. These appear in [Babbage's](wikipedia://en/5698) functional equation (1820),[^o7]

$$f(f(x)) = 1-(1-x) = x \, .$$

Other involutions, and solutions of the equation, include

- $$f(x) = a-x\, ,$$
- $f(x) = \frac{a}{x}\, ,$ and
- $$f(x) = \frac{b-x}{1+cx} ~ ,$$

which includes the previous three as [special cases](wikipedia://en/31308010) or limits.

## Solution {#Solution}
