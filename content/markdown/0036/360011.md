# Regular representation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Regular representation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and in particular the theory of [group representations](wikipedia://en/12695), the **regular representation** of a group *G* is the [linear representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_representation) afforded by the [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of *G* on itself by [translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_%28group_theory%29).

:::

One distinguishes the **left regular representation** λ given by left translation and the **right regular representation** ρ given by the inverse of right translation.

## Finite groups {#Finite_groups}

For a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) *G*, the left regular representation λ (over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K*) is a linear representation on the [*K*-vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V* freely generated by the elements of *G*, i.e. elements of *G* can be identified with a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) of *V*. Given *g* ∈ *G*, λ<sub>*g*</sub> is the linear map determined by its action on the basis by left translation by *g*, i.e.

$$\lambda_{g}:h\mapsto gh,\text{ for all }h\in G.$$

For the right regular representation ρ, an inversion must occur in order to satisfy the axioms of a representation. Specifically, given *g* ∈ *G*, ρ<sub>*g*</sub> is the linear map on *V* determined by its action on the basis by right translation by *g*<sup>−1</sup>, i.e.

$$\rho_{g}:h\mapsto hg^{-1},\text{ for all }h\in G.\$$

Alternatively, these representations can be defined on the *K*-vector space *W* of all functions *G* → *K*. It is in this form that the regular representation is generalized to [topological groups](wikipedia://en/42315) such as [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945).

The specific definition in terms of *W* is as follows. Given a function *f* : *G* → *K* and an element *g* ∈ *G*,

$$(\lambda_{g}f)(x)=f(\lambda_{g}^{-1}(x))=f({g}^{-1}x)$$

and

$$(\rho_{g}f)(x)=f(\rho_{g}^{-1}(x))=f(xg).$$

## Significance of the regular representation of a group {#Significance_of_the_regular_representation_of_a_group}

Every group *G* acts on itself by translations. If we consider this action as a [permutation representation](wikipedia://en/59487764) it is characterised as having a single [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) and [stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) the identity subgroup {*e*} of *G*. The regular representation of *G*, for a given field *K*, is the linear representation made by taking this permutation representation as a set of [basis vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_vector) of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over *K*. The significance is that while the permutation representation doesn't decompose – it is [transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) – the regular representation in general breaks up into smaller representations. For example, if *G* is a finite group and *K* is the [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) field, the regular representation decomposes as a [direct sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_sum_of_representations) of [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982), with each irreducible representation appearing in the decomposition with multiplicity its dimension. The number of these irreducibles is equal to the number of [conjugacy classes](wikipedia://en/49176) of *G*.

The above fact can be explained by [character theory](wikipedia://en/691741). Recall that the character of the regular representation χ*(g)* is the number of fixed points of *g* acting on the regular representation *V*. It means the number of fixed points χ*(g)* is zero when *g* is not *id* and |*G*| otherwise. Let *V* have the decomposition ⊕*a*<sub>*i*</sub>*V*<sub>*i*</sub> where *V*<sub>*i*</sub>'s are irreducible representations of *G* and *a*<sub>*i*</sub>'s are the corresponding multiplicities. By [character theory](wikipedia://en/691741), the multiplicity *a*<sub>*i*</sub> can be computed as

$a_i= \langle \chi,\chi_i \rangle =\frac{1}{|G|}\sum \overline{\chi(g)}\chi_i(g)=\frac{1}{|G|}\chi(1)\chi_i(1)=\operatorname{dim} V_i,$ which means the multiplicity of each irreducible representation is its dimension.

The article on [group rings](wikipedia://en/349223) articulates the regular representation for [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707), as well as showing how the regular representation can be taken to be a [module](wikipedia://en/276410).

## Module theory point of view {#Module_theory_point_of_view}

To put the construction more abstractly, the [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) *K*\[*G*\] is considered as a module over itself. (There is a choice here of left-action or right-action, but that is not of importance except for notation.) If *G* is finite and the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) of K doesn't divide |*G*|, this is a [semisimple ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_ring) and we are looking at its left (right) [ring ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_ideal). This theory has been studied in great depth. It is known in particular that the direct sum decomposition of the regular representation contains a representative of every [isomorphism class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_class) of irreducible linear representations of *G* over *K*. You can say that the regular representation is *comprehensive* for representation theory, in this case. The modular case, when the characteristic of *K* does divide |*G*|, is harder mainly because with *K*\[*G*\] not semisimple, a representation can fail to be irreducible without splitting as a direct sum.

## Structure for finite cyclic groups {#Structure_for_finite_cyclic_groups}

For a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) *C* generated by *g* of order *n*, the matrix form of an element of *K*\[*C*\] acting on *K*\[*C*\] by multiplication takes a distinctive form known as a *[circulant matrix](wikipedia://en/889936)*, in which each row is a shift to the right of the one above (in [cyclic order](wikipedia://en/434673), i.e. with the right-most element appearing on the left), when referred to the natural basis

1, *g*, *g*<sup>2</sup>, ..., *g*<sup>*n*−1</sup>.

When the field *K* contains a [primitive *n*-th root of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_n-th_root_of_unity), one can [diagonalise](wikipedia://en/246325) the representation of *C* by writing down *n* linearly independent simultaneous [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) for all the *n*×*n* circulants. In fact if ζ is any *n*-th root of unity, the element

1 + ζ*g* + ζ<sup>2</sup>*g*<sup>2</sup> + ... + ζ<sup>*n*−1</sup>*g*<sup>*n*−1</sup>

is an eigenvector for the action of *g* by multiplication, with eigenvalue

ζ<sup>−1</sup>

and so also an eigenvector of all powers of *g*, and their linear combinations.

This is the explicit form in this case of the abstract result that over an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) *K* (such as the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826)) the regular representation of *G* is [completely reducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_reducible), provided that the characteristic of *K* (if it is a prime number *p*) doesn't divide the order of *G*. That is called *[Maschke's theorem](wikipedia://en/30876007)*. In this case the condition on the characteristic is implied by the existence of a *primitive* *n*-th root of unity, which cannot happen in the case of prime characteristic *p* dividing *n*.

Circulant [determinants](wikipedia://en/8468) were first encountered in nineteenth century mathematics, and the consequence of their diagonalisation drawn. Namely, the determinant of a circulant is the product of the *n* eigenvalues for the *n* eigenvectors described above. The basic work of [Frobenius](wikipedia://en/341048) on [group representations](wikipedia://en/12695) started with the motivation of finding analogous factorisations of the **group determinants** for any finite *G*; that is, the determinants of arbitrary matrices representing elements of *K*\[*G*\] acting by multiplication on the basis elements given by *g* in *G*. Unless *G* is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974), the factorisation must contain non-linear factors corresponding to [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982) of *G* of degree > 1.

## Topological group case {#Topological_group_case}

For a topological group *G*, the regular representation in the above sense should be replaced by a suitable space of functions on *G*, with *G* acting by translation. See [Peter–Weyl theorem](wikipedia://en/294474) for the [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) case. If *G* is a Lie group but not compact nor [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974), this is a difficult matter of [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147). The [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) abelian case is part of the [Pontryagin duality](wikipedia://en/366136) theory.

## Normal bases in Galois theory {#Normal_bases_in_Galois_theory}

In [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316) it is shown that for a field *L*, and a finite group *G* of [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of *L*, the fixed field *K* of *G* has \[*L*:*K*\] = |*G*|. In fact we can say more: *L* viewed as a *K*\[*G*\]-module is the regular representation. This is the content of the [normal basis theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_basis_theorem), a **normal basis** being an element *x* of *L* such that the *g*(*x*) for *g* in *G* are a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) basis for *L* over *K*. Such *x* exist, and each one gives a *K*\[*G*\]-isomorphism from *L* to *K*\[*G*\]. From the point of view of [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705) it is of interest to study *normal integral bases*, where we try to replace *L* and *K* by the rings of [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981) they contain. One can see already in the case of the [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628) that such bases may not exist: *a* + *bi* and *a* − *bi* can never form a **Z**-module basis of **Z**\[*i*\] because 1 cannot be an integer combination. The reasons are studied in depth in [Galois module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_module) theory.

## More general algebras {#More_general_algebras}

The regular representation of a group ring is such that the left-hand and right-hand regular representations give isomorphic modules (and we often need not distinguish the cases). Given an [algebra over a field](wikipedia://en/191788) *A*, it doesn't immediately make sense to ask about the relation between *A* as left-module over itself, and as right-module. In the group case, the mapping on basis elements *g* of *K*\[*G*\] defined by taking the [inverse element](wikipedia://en/45199) gives an isomorphism of *K*\[*G*\] to its *opposite* ring. For *A* general, such a structure is called a [Frobenius algebra](wikipedia://en/1814652). As the name implies, these were introduced by [Frobenius](wikipedia://en/341048) in the nineteenth century. They have been shown to be related to [topological quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/443757) in 1 + 1 dimensions by a particular instance of the [cobordism hypothesis](wikipedia://en/41419738).
