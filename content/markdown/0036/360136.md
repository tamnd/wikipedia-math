# Maximal torus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Maximal torus"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) theory of [compact Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Lie_group) a special role is played by torus subgroups, in particular by the **maximal torus** subgroups.

:::

A **torus** in a compact [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) *G* is a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), [connected](wikipedia://en/6233), [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) [Lie subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_subgroup) of *G* (and therefore isomorphic to[^o2] the standard torus **T**<sup>*n*</sup>). A **maximal torus** is one which is maximal among such subgroups. That is, *T* is a maximal torus if for any torus *T*′ containing *T* we have *T* = *T*′. Every torus is contained in a maximal torus simply by [dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28mathematics_and_physics%29) considerations. A noncompact Lie group need not have any nontrivial tori (e.g. **R**<sup>*n*</sup>).

The dimension of a maximal torus in *G* is called the **rank** of *G*. The rank is [well-defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-defined) since all maximal tori turn out to be [conjugate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_%28group_theory%29). For [semisimple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_Lie_group) groups the rank is equal to the number of nodes in the associated [Dynkin diagram](wikipedia://en/277109).

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) U(*n*) has as a maximal torus the subgroup of all [diagonal matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagonal_matrices). That is,

$$T = \left\{\operatorname{diag}\left(e^{i\theta_1},e^{i\theta_2},\dots,e^{i\theta_n}\right) : \forall j, \theta_j \in \mathbb{R}\right\}.$$

*T* is clearly isomorphic to the product of *n* circles, so the unitary group U(*n*) has rank *n*. A maximal torus in the [special unitary group](wikipedia://en/173997) SU(*n*) ⊂ U(*n*) is just the intersection of *T* and SU(*n*) which is a torus of dimension *n* − 1.

A maximal torus in the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) SO(2*n*) is given by the set of all simultaneous [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789) in any fixed choice of *n* pairwise orthogonal planes (i.e., two dimensional vector spaces). Concretely, one maximal torus consists of all block-diagonal matrices with $2\times 2$ diagonal blocks, where each diagonal block is a rotation matrix. This is also a maximal torus in the group SO(2*n*+1) where the action fixes the remaining direction. Thus both SO(2*n*) and SO(2*n*+1) have rank *n*. For example, in the [rotation group SO(3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_group_SO%283%29) the maximal tori are given by rotations about a fixed axis.

The [symplectic group](wikipedia://en/173977) Sp(*n*) has rank *n*. A maximal torus is given by the set of all diagonal matrices whose entries all lie in a fixed complex subalgebra of [**H**](wikipedia://en/51440).

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

Let *G* be a compact, connected Lie group and let $\mathfrak g$ be the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) of *G*. The first main result is the torus theorem, which may be formulated as follows:[^o7]

**Torus theorem**: If *T* is one fixed maximal torus in *G*, then every element of *G* is conjugate to an element of *T*.

This theorem has the following consequences:

- All maximal tori in *G* are conjugate.[^o8]
- All maximal tori have the same dimension, known as the *rank* of *G*.
- A maximal torus in *G* is a maximal abelian subgroup, but the converse need not hold.[^o9]
- The maximal tori in *G* are exactly the Lie subgroups corresponding to the maximal abelian subalgebras of $\mathfrak g$[^o10] (cf. [Cartan subalgebra](wikipedia://en/1336000))
- Every element of *G* lies in some maximal torus; thus, the [exponential map](wikipedia://en/44612290) for *G* is surjective.
- If *G* has dimension *n* and rank *r* then *n* − *r* is even.

:::

## Root system {#Root_system}

If *T* is a maximal torus in a compact Lie group *G*, one can define a [root system](wikipedia://en/277087) as follows. The roots are the [weights](wikipedia://en/750326#Representation_theory_of_a_connected_compact_Lie_group) for the adjoint action of *T* on the complexified Lie algebra of *G*. To be more explicit, let $\mathfrak t$ denote the Lie algebra of *T*, let $\mathfrak g$ denote the Lie algebra of $G$, and let $\mathfrak g_{\mathbb C}:=\mathfrak g\oplus i\mathfrak g$ denote the complexification of $\mathfrak g$. Then we say that an element $\alpha\in\mathfrak t$ is a **root** for *G* relative to *T* if $\alpha\neq 0$ and there exists a nonzero $X\in\mathfrak g_{\mathbb C}$ such that

$$\mathrm{Ad}_{e^H}(X)=e^{i\langle\alpha,H\rangle}X$$

for all $H\in\mathfrak t$. Here $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is a fixed inner product on $\mathfrak g$ that is invariant under the adjoint action of connected compact Lie groups.

The root system, as a subset of the Lie algebra $\mathfrak t$ of *T*, has all the usual properties of a root system, except that the roots may not span $\mathfrak t$.[^o12] The root system is a key tool in understanding the [classification](wikipedia://en/750326#Classification) and [representation theory](wikipedia://en/750326#Representation_theory_of_a_connected_compact_Lie_group) of *G*.

## Weyl group {#Weyl_group}

Given a torus *T* (not necessarily maximal), the [Weyl group](wikipedia://en/296332) of *G* with respect to *T* can be defined as the [normalizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalizer) of *T* modulo the [centralizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centralizer) of *T*. That is,

$$W(T,G) := N_G(T)/C_G(T).$$

Fix a maximal torus $T = T_0$ in *G;* then the corresponding Weyl group is called the Weyl group of *G* (it depends up to isomorphism on the choice of *T*).

The first two major results about the Weyl group are as follows.

- The centralizer of *T* in *G* is equal to *T*, so the Weyl group is equal to *N*(*T*)/*T*.[^o14]
- The Weyl group is generated by reflections about the roots of the associated Lie algebra.[^o15] Thus, the Weyl group of *T* is isomorphic to the [Weyl group](wikipedia://en/296332) of the [root system](wikipedia://en/277087) of the Lie algebra of *G*.

We now list some consequences of these main results.

- Two elements in *T* are conjugate if and only if they are conjugate by an element of *W*. That is, each conjugacy class of *G* intersects *T* in exactly one Weyl [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29).[^o16] In fact, the space of conjugacy classes in *G* is homeomorphic to the [orbit space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_space) *T*/*W*.
- The Weyl group acts by ([outer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_automorphism)) [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) on *T* (and its Lie algebra).
- The [identity component](wikipedia://en/1088787) of the normalizer of *T* is also equal to *T*. The Weyl group is therefore equal to the [component group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Component_group) of *N*(*T*).
- The Weyl group is finite.

The [representation theory](wikipedia://en/750326#Representation_theory_of_a_connected_compact_Lie_group) of *G* is essentially determined by *T* and *W*.

As an example, consider the case $G=SU(n)$ with $T$ being the diagonal subgroup of $G$. Then $x\in G$ belongs to $N(T)$ if and only if $x$ maps each standard basis element $e_i$ to a multiple of some other standard basis element $e_j$, that is, if and only if $x$ permutes the standard basis elements, up to multiplication by some constants. The Weyl group in this case is then the [permutation group](wikipedia://en/24634) on $n$ elements.

## Weyl integral formula {#Weyl_integral_formula}

Suppose *f* is a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) on *G*. Then the integral over *G* of *f* with respect to the normalized Haar measure *dg* may be computed as follows:

$$\displaystyle{\int_G f(g)\, dg = |W|^{-1} \int_T |\Delta(t)|^2\int_{G/T}f\left(yty^{-1}\right)\,d[y]\, dt,}$$

where $d[y]$ is the normalized volume measure on the quotient manifold $G/T$ and $dt$ is the normalized Haar measure on *T*.[^o19] Here Δ is given by the [Weyl denominator formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_denominator_formula) and $|W|$ is the order of the Weyl group. An important special case of this result occurs when *f* is a [class function](wikipedia://en/1001329), that is, a function invariant under conjugation. In that case, we have

$$\displaystyle{\int_G f(g)\, dg = |W|^{-1} \int_T f(t) |\Delta(t)|^2\, dt.}$$

Consider as an example the case $G=SU(2)$, with $T$ being the diagonal subgroup. Then the Weyl integral formula for class functions takes the following explicit form:[^o20]

$$\displaystyle{\int_{SU(2)} f(g)\, dg = \frac{1}{2} \int_0^{2\pi} f\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(e^{i\theta}, e^{-i\theta}\right)\right)\, 4\,\mathrm{sin}^2(\theta) \, \frac{d\theta}{2\pi}.}$$

Here $|W|=2$, the normalized Haar measure on $T$ is $\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}$, and $\mathrm{diag}\left(e^{i\theta}, e^{-i\theta}\right)$ denotes the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries $e^{i\theta}$ and $e^{-i\theta}$.

:::
