# Marinus of Neapolis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Marinus of Neapolis"}

**Marinus** (Μαρῖνος ὁ Νεαπολίτης; born c. 440 AD) was a [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine) [Neoplatonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonic) [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher), mathematician and [rhetorician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetorician)[^n1] born in [Flavia Neapolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nablus) (modern Nablus), [Palaestina Secunda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaestina_Secunda). He was a student of [Proclus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclus) in Athens. His surviving works are an introduction to [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s *[Data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_%28Euclid%29)*; a *Life of Proclus*, and two astronomical texts.

:::

Most of what we know of his life comes from an epitome of a work by [Damascius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascius) conserved in the Byzantine [Suda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suda) encyclopedia.[^n2]

## Life {#Life}

He was, according to his pupil Damascius, born a [Samaritan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaritan).[^n3] Whether this information is correct is disputed, but it is quite possible. Damascius also adds that he had converted from [Samaritanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaritanism).[^n4]

He came to [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens) at a time when, with the exception of [Proclus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclus), there was a great dearth of eminent men in the [Neoplatonist school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy). He was appointed as successor (*diadochos*) to Proclus, sometime before the latter's death, during the period of the teacher's infirmity. Proclus dedicated to Marinus his commentary on [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954)'s [Myth of Er](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myth_of_Er).[^o7]

Proclus himself, it is reported, worried that Marinus himself was of delicate constitution.[^n5] During this period, the professors of the old [Greek religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_ancient_Greece) suffered persecution at the hands of the Christians and Marinus was compelled to seek refuge at [Epidaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidaurus), where he died, at a date unknown.[^n6]

## Works {#Works}

Only a remnant of his output survives.[^n7] His chief surviving work was a biography of Proclus, since it is the main source of information on Proclus' life. This was written in a combination of prose and epic [hexameters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexameter), of which only the former survives.[^n6]

The publication of the biography is fixed by internal evidence to the year of Proclus's death; for he mentions an [eclipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclipse) which will happen when the first year after that event is completed. It was first published with the works of [Marcus Aurelius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Aurelius) in 1559; it was republished separately by [Fabricius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Albert_Fabricius) at Hamburg in 1700, and re-edited in 1814 by [Boissonade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_François_Boissonade_de_Fontarabie) with emendations and notes.[^n8] He is also the author of a commentary (or introduction) on the *[Data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_%28Euclid%29)* of [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331), and a commentary on [Theon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theon_of_Alexandria)'s *Little Commentary*.[^n9] There is also a surviving astronomical text which discusses the [Milky Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way).[^n9]

His lost works included [commentaries on Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commentaries_on_Aristotle), on [Theon of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theon_of_Alexandria) and on some of the dialogues of [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954). He is said to have destroyed his commentary on Plato's *[Philebus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philebus)* on the advice of a pupil he was tutoring, [Isidorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidore_of_Alexandria).[^n10] According to a version of the story written by Damascius, when Marinus showed his student, to whom he taught [Aristotelianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelianism),[^n11] this commentary, which he had just completed, Isidorus prevailed on him to destroy it, arguing that since the 'divine' Proclus had himself written a definitive commentary which was the final word on the topic.[^n12] Current scholarship suspects that this advice arose from fears that Marinus' commentary would, despite his best efforts, betray traces of material that might undermine the reigning [Neoplatonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism) [paradigm](wikipedia://en/175308).[^n12]
