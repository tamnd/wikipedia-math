# Negentropy

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Negentropy"}

In [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), **negative entropy** is used as a measure of distance to [normality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normality_%28statistics%29). It is also known as **negentropy** or **syntropy**. It is a counter-construct to [entropy](wikipedia://en/15445).

:::

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The concept and phrase "*negative entropy*" was introduced by [Erwin Schrödinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Schrödinger) in his 1944 book *[What is Life?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_Is_Life?).*[^o3] Later, the [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_people) [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist) [Léon Brillouin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Léon_Brillouin) shortened the phrase to néguentropie (negentropy).[^o4][^o5] In 1974, [Albert Szent-Györgyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Szent-Györgyi) proposed replacing the term *negentropy* with *syntropy* (Greek, 'together with change'). That term may have originated in the 1940s with the Italian mathematician [Luigi Fantappiè](wikipedia://en/7255073), who tried to construct a unified theory of [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology) and [physics](wikipedia://en/22939). [Buckminster Fuller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckminster_Fuller) tried to popularize this usage, but *negentropy* remains common.

In a note to *What is Life?,* Schrödinger explained his use of this phrase: ... if I had been catering for them \[physicists\] alone I should have let the discussion turn on *[free energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_free_energy)* instead. It is the more familiar notion in this context. But this highly technical term seemed linguistically too near to *[energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy)* for making the average reader alive to the contrast between the two things.

## Information theory {#Information_theory}

section In [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), negentropy is used as a measure of distance to normality.[^o7][^o8][^o9] Out of all [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) with a given [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) and [variance](wikipedia://en/32344), the Gaussian or [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) is the one with the highest [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891). Negentropy measures the difference in entropy between a given distribution and the Gaussian distribution with the same mean and variance. Thus, negentropy is always nonnegative, is invariant by any linear invertible change of coordinates, and vanishes [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the signal is Gaussian.

Negentropy is defined as

$$J(Y) = h(Y_G) - h(Y),$$

where $h(Y_G) = \tfrac{1}{2} \log \left(2\pi\mathrm{e} \cdot \sigma^2\right)$ is the [differential entropy](wikipedia://en/3504168) of a normal distribution $Y_G \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with the same mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ as $Y$, and $h(Y)$ is the differential entropy of $Y$, with $p_Y$ as its [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487):

$$h(Y) = - \int p_Y(u) \log p_Y(u) \, \mathrm{d}u$$

Negentropy is used in [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) and [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324). It is related to [network entropy](wikipedia://en/66323235), which is used in [independent component analysis](wikipedia://en/598031).[^o10][^o11]

The negentropy of a distribution is equal to the [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052) between $Y$ and a Gaussian distribution with the same mean and variance as $Y$ (see *Differential entropy#Maximization in the normal distribution* for a proof):

$$J(Y)=D_{KL}(Y\ \Vert\ Y_G)$$

In particular, it is always nonnegative (unlike differential entropy, which can be negative).

## Correlation between statistical negentropy and Gibbs free energy {#Correlation_between_statistical_negentropy_and_Gibbs_free_energy}

*Image: Wykres Gibbsa.svg.* [Willard Gibbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willard_Gibbs)' 1873 **available energy** ([free energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_free_energy)) graph, which shows a plane perpendicular to the axis of *v* ([volume](wikipedia://en/32498)) and passing through point A, which represents the initial state of the body. MN is the section of the surface of [dissipated energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissipated_energy). Qε and Qη are sections of the planes *η* = 0 and *ε* = 0, and therefore parallel to the axes of ε ([internal energy](wikipedia://en/340757)) and η ([entropy](wikipedia://en/9891)) respectively. AD and AE are the energy and entropy of the body in its initial state, AB and AC its *available energy* ([Gibbs energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs_energy)) and its *capacity for entropy* (the amount by which the entropy of the body can be increased without changing the energy of the body or increasing its volume) respectively.

There is a physical quantity closely linked to [free energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_free_energy) ([free enthalpy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_enthalpy)), with a unit of entropy and isomorphic to negentropy known in statistics and information theory. In 1873, [Willard Gibbs](wikipedia://en/37332) created a diagram illustrating the concept of free energy corresponding to [free enthalpy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_enthalpy). On the diagram one can see the quantity called [capacity for entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacity_for_entropy). This quantity is the amount of entropy that may be increased without changing an internal energy or increasing its volume.[^o13] In other words, it is a difference between maximum possible, under assumed conditions, entropy and its actual entropy. It corresponds exactly to the definition of negentropy adopted in statistics and information theory. A similar physical quantity was introduced in 1869 by [Massieu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_Jacques_Dominique_Massieu) for the [isothermal process](wikipedia://en/409951)[^o14][^o15][^o16] (both quantities differs just with a figure sign) and by then [Planck](wikipedia://en/19848) for the [isothermal](wikipedia://en/409951)-[isobaric](wikipedia://en/414765) process.[^o17] More recently, the Massieu–Planck [thermodynamic potential](wikipedia://en/255446), known also as *[free entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_entropy)*, has been shown to play a great role in the so-called entropic formulation of [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481),[^o18] applied among the others in molecular biology[^o19] and thermodynamic non-equilibrium processes.[^o20]

$$J = S_\max - S = -\Phi = -k \ln Z\,$$

where:

$S$ is [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891)

$J$ is negentropy (Gibbs "capacity for entropy")

$\Phi$ is the [Massieu potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_entropy#Massieu_potential_/_Helmholtz_free_entropy)

$Z$ is the [partition function](wikipedia://en/312252)

$k$ the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant)

In particular, mathematically the negentropy (the negative entropy function, in physics interpreted as free entropy) is the [convex conjugate](wikipedia://en/1001490) of [LogSumExp](wikipedia://en/47422895) (in physics interpreted as the free energy).

## Brillouin's negentropy principle of information {#Brillouin's_negentropy_principle_of_information}

In 1953, [Léon Brillouin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Léon_Brillouin) derived a general equation[^o23] stating that the changing of an information bit value requires at least $kT\ln 2$ energy. This is the same energy as the work [Leó Szilárd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leó_Szilárd)'s engine produces in the idealistic case. In his book,[^o24] he further explored this problem concluding that any cause of this bit value change (measurement, decision about a yes/no question, erasure, display, etc.) will require the same amount of energy.

:::
