# Witold Hurewicz

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Witold Hurewicz"}

**Witold Hurewicz** (Polish: '; June 29, 1904 – September 6, 1956) was a Polish [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who worked in [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649), [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) and dimension theory. A graduate of the [University of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Vienna), Hurewicz moved to the United States where he held positions at various American institutions of higher learning including the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) and the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology). He is best known for introducing higher [homotopy groups](wikipedia://en/297506), proving the [Hurewicz theorem](wikipedia://en/591587).

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Witold Hurewicz was born on 29 June 1904 in [Łódź](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Łódź),[^o4] at the time one of the main Polish industrial hubs with economy focused on the textile industry. His father Mieczysław Hurewicz was an industrialist born in [Wilno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilno), which until 1939 was mainly populated by Poles and Jews.[^o5] His mother Katarzyna Finkelsztain hailed from [Biała Cerkiew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biała_Cerkiew), a town that belonged to the [Kingdom of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_of_the_Kingdom_of_Poland) until the [Second Partition of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Partition_of_Poland) (1793) when it was taken by [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia).[^o4]

Hurewicz attended school in a German-controlled Poland but with [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I) beginning before he had begun [secondary school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_school), major changes occurred in Poland. In August 1915 the Russian forces that had held Poland for many years withdrew. [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany) and [Austria-Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria-Hungary) took control of most of the country and the [University of Warsaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Warsaw) was refounded and it began operating as a Polish university. Rapidly, a [strong school of mathematics](wikipedia://en/1028194) grew up in the University of Warsaw, with [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) one of the main topics. Although Hurewicz knew intimately the topology that was being studied in Poland, in 1921, he chose to go to [Vienna](wikipedia://en/55866) to continue his studies.[^o6]

He studied under [Hans Hahn](wikipedia://en/1075212) and [Karl Menger](wikipedia://en/149431) in [Vienna](wikipedia://en/55866), receiving a [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD) in 1926 on the basis of his thesis *Über eine Verallgemeinerung des Borelschen Theorems* (On a Generalization of Borel's Theorem).[^o7] Hurewicz was awarded a [Rockefeller scholarship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_Foundation), which allowed him to spend the year 1927–28 in [Amsterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amsterdam). He was assistant to [L. E. J. Brouwer](wikipedia://en/172896) in Amsterdam from 1928 to 1936. He was given study leave for a year, which he decided to spend in the United States. He visited the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey) and then decided to remain in the United States and not return to his position in Amsterdam.[^o6] Hurewicz collaborated with the [Warsaw School of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw_School_of_Mathematics) maintaining close contacts with [Karol Borsuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karol_Borsuk), [Samuel Eilenberg](wikipedia://en/362501) and [Bronisław Knaster](wikipedia://en/1003125), co-authoring with Knaster *Ein Einbettungessatz über henkelfreie Kontinua* in 1933.[^o6]

## Career {#Career}

Hurewicz worked first at the [University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_North_Carolina_at_Chapel_Hill) but during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) he contributed to the war effort with research on [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969). In particular, the work he did on [servomechanisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servomechanisms) at that time was classified because of its military importance. From 1945 until his death he worked at the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology).[^o6]

Hurewicz's early work was on [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) and [topology](wikipedia://en/29954). The *[Dictionary of Scientific Biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_Scientific_Biography)* states: "...a remarkable result of this first period \[1930\] is his [topological embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_embedding) of [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) [metric spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_spaces) into [compact spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_spaces) of the same ([finite](wikipedia://en/11742)) [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398).\*"

In the field of [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649) his contributions are centred on [dimension theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_theory). He wrote an important text with [Henry Wallman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Wallman), *Dimension Theory*, published in 1941.[^o9] A reviewer writes that the book "...is truly a classic. It presents the theory of dimension for separable metric spaces with what seems to be an impossible mixture of depth, clarity, precision, succinctness, and comprehensiveness."

Hurewicz is best remembered for three remarkable contributions to mathematics: his discovery of the [higher homotopy groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_homotopy_groups) in 1935–36,[^o10] his discovery of the [long exact homotopy sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_long_exact_sequence#Long_exact_sequence_of_a_fibration) for [fibrations](wikipedia://en/316826) in 1941, and the [Hurewicz theorem](wikipedia://en/591587) connecting homotopy and [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) groups.[^o11] His work led to [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410). It was during Hurewicz's time as Brouwer's assistant in Amsterdam that he did the work on the higher homotopy groups; "...the idea was not new, but until Hurewicz nobody had pursued it as it should have been. Investigators did not expect much new information from [groups](wikipedia://en/19447), which were obviously [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative)..."

Hurewicz was also first to represent a function by an arrow, in about 1940. This rapidly displaced the old notation. It was proven fundamental for the development of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869).[^o12]

In the late 1940s, he was the doctoral advisor of [Yael Dowker](wikipedia://en/48217935).[^o13]

Hurewicz had a second textbook published, but this was not until 1958 after his death. *Lectures on [ordinary differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_differential_equations)*[^o14] is an introduction to ordinary differential equations that again reflects the clarity of his thinking and the quality of his writing. He also published a number of papers in Polish scientific journals including 11 works in *[Fundamenta Mathematicae](wikipedia://en/1576701)*.[^o15]

He died after participating in the International Symposium on Algebraic Topology [^o16] at the [National Autonomous University of Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Autonomous_University_of_Mexico) in [Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico_City). He tripped and fell off the top of a [Mayan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_civilization) [step pyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Step_pyramid) during an outing in [Uxmal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uxmal), [Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico). In the *[Dictionary of Scientific Biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_Scientific_Biography)* it is suggested that he was "...a paragon of absentmindedness, a failing that probably led to his death."[^o17]
