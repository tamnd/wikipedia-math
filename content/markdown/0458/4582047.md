# Pólya conjecture

*Image: Liouville-big.svg.* Summatory Liouville function *L*(*n*) up to *n* = 10<sup>7</sup>. The (disproven) conjecture states that this function is always negative. The readily visible oscillations are due to the first non-trivial zero of the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809).

*Image: Liouville-polya.svg.* Closeup of the summatory Liouville function *L*(*n*) in the region where the Pólya conjecture fails to hold.

*Image: Liouville-log.svg.* Logarithmic graph of the negative of the summatory Liouville function *L*(*n*) up to *n* = 2 × 10<sup>9</sup>. The green spike shows the function itself (not its negative) in the narrow region where the conjecture fails; the blue curve shows the oscillatory contribution of the first Riemann zero.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pólya conjecture"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), the **Pólya conjecture** (or **Pólya's conjecture**) stated that "most" (i.e., 50% or more) of the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) [less than](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Less_than) any given number have an *odd* number of [prime factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor). The [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) was set forth by the Hungarian mathematician [George Pólya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Pólya) in 1919,[^o2] and proven false in 1958 by [C. Brian Haselgrove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._Brian_Haselgrove). Though mathematicians typically refer to this statement as the Pólya conjecture, Pólya never actually conjectured that the statement was true; rather, he showed that the truth of the statement would imply the [Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/19344125). For this reason, it is more accurately called "**Pólya's problem**".

:::

The size of the smallest [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) is often used to demonstrate the fact that a conjecture can be true for many cases and still fail to hold in general,[^o3] providing an illustration of the [strong law of small numbers](wikipedia://en/18093481).

::: {#o5 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

The Pólya conjecture states that for any $n>1$, if the [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers) less than or equal to $n$ (excluding 0) are partitioned into those with an *odd* number of prime factors and those with an *even* number of prime factors, then the former set has at least as many members as the latter set. Repeated prime factors are counted repeatedly; for instance, we say that 18 = 2 × 3 × 3 has an odd number of prime factors, while 60 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5 has an even number of prime factors.

Equivalently, it can be stated in terms of the summatory [Liouville function](wikipedia://en/104463), with the conjecture being that

$$L(n) = \sum_{k=1}^n \lambda(k) \leq 0$$

for all $n>1$. Here, $\lambda(k)=(-1)^{\Omega(k)}$, where $\Omega(k)$ counts the total number of prime factors of $k$. Thus $\lambda(k)$ is positive if the number of prime factors of $k$ is even, and is negative if it is odd.

:::

## Disproof {#Disproof}

The Pólya conjecture was disproven by [C. Brian Haselgrove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._Brian_Haselgrove) in 1958. He showed that the conjecture has a counterexample, which he estimated to be around 1.845 × 10<sup>361</sup>.[^o7]

A (much smaller) explicit counterexample, of *n* = 906,180,359 was given by [R. Sherman Lehman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._Sherman_Lehman) in 1960;[^o8] the smallest counterexample is *n* = 906,150,257, found by Minoru Tanaka in 1980.[^o9]

The conjecture fails to hold for most values of *n* in the region of 906,150,257 ≤ *n* ≤ 906,488,079. In this region, the summatory [Liouville function](wikipedia://en/104463) reaches a maximum value of 829 at *n* = 906,316,571.

Peter Humphries showed that

$$\liminf_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{L(n)}{\sqrt{n}}\le -1.389278414\cdots,\quad \limsup_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{L(n)}{\sqrt{n}}\ge 0.061867262\cdots.$$

He also showed that assuming certain hypotheses including the [Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/19344125), the set of numbers $n$ such that $L(n)>0$ has logarithm density $0<\delta\le\dfrac{1}{2}$. A heuristic argument suggests that this set has logarithm density of around 0.00012.
