# Path graph

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Path graph"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) field of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **path graph** (or **linear graph**) is a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) whose [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) can be listed in the order $v_{1}, v_{2}, ..., v_{n}$ such that the [edges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_%28graph_theory%29) are $\{v_{i}, v_{i+1}$} where $i = 1, 2, ..., n - 1$. Equivalently, a path with at least two vertices is [connected](wikipedia://en/1614492) and has two terminal vertices (vertices of [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) 1), while all others (if any) have degree 2.

:::

Paths are often important in their role as [subgraphs](wikipedia://en/325802#Subgraphs) of other graphs, in which case they are called [paths](wikipedia://en/638889) in that graph. A path is a particularly simple example of a [tree](wikipedia://en/48560), and in fact the paths are exactly the trees in which no vertex has degree 3 or more. A [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/9938459) of paths is called a [linear forest](wikipedia://en/36344169).

Paths are fundamental concepts of graph theory, described in the introductory sections of most graph theory texts. See, for example, Bondy and Murty (1976), Gibbons (1985), or Diestel (2005).

## As Dynkin diagrams {#As_Dynkin_diagrams}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), path graphs appear as the [Dynkin diagrams](wikipedia://en/277109) of type A. As such, they classify the [root system](wikipedia://en/277087) of type A and the [Weyl group](wikipedia://en/296332) of type A, which is the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901).
