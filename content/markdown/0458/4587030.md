# Infinite group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Infinite group"}

In [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), an area of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **infinite group** is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) whose [underlying set](wikipedia://en/844783) contains [infinitely many](wikipedia://en/142679) [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29). In other words, it is a group of infinite [order](wikipedia://en/493527). The structure of infinite groups is often a question of [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) of the [asymptotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic) of how various invariants grow relative to a generating set, or how a group [acts](wikipedia://en/12781) on a [topological](wikipedia://en/30450) or [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270). In contrast, the structure of [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707) is determined largely by methods of [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384).

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- (**Z**, +), the group of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) with addition is infinite, as is (**Q**, +) the group of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) with addition
- Non-discrete [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945) are infinite. For example, (**R**, +), the group of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) with addition is an infinite group
- The [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) of order *n* > 0 over an infinite [field](wikipedia://en/10603) is infinite
- The [Infinite dihedral group](wikipedia://en/4296050)
- The [Tarski monster group](wikipedia://en/727196)
- The [Prüfer p-group](wikipedia://en/4885031)
- A [free group](wikipedia://en/59735)
- [SL(2,Z)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SL%282,Z%29)
- [Amenable groups](wikipedia://en/657430)
- [Thompson groups](wikipedia://en/612245)
- [Grigorchuk group](wikipedia://en/18707371)
- [Lamplighter groups](wikipedia://en/5491540)

## Torsion {#Torsion}

An infinite group is called a [torsion group](wikipedia://en/3568695) if every element has finite order. Examples include the Prüfer *p*-group and certain Burnside groups. In contrast, a group is torsion-free if no non-identity element has finite order, such as $\mathbb{Z}$ or free groups.

## Structure and classification {#Structure_and_classification}

Many infinite groups are given in terms of a set of generators and relations. For example, a [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) is a group on a set of generators with no relations, whereas a [braid group](wikipedia://en/550138) is a group on generators $g_i$, where $i$ is an integer in $[0,n]$, with relations $g_i g_{i+1} g_i = g_{i+1} g_i g_{i+1}$ and $g_ig_j=g_jg_i$ if $|i-j|>1$.

Infinite groups can be finitely generated, such as $\mathbb Z$ or $\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb Z)$, or infinitely generated such as $(\mathbb Q,+)$ or any [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945); finitely presented such as any free group (on a finite set of generators) or braid groups. Groups may also be infinitely presented, etc.

An infinite group may be [residually finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residually_finite), meaning that every element is non-trivial in some finite quotient. Many groups, like $\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb Z)$, are residually finite; whereas others like the [Tarski monster groups](wikipedia://en/727196), are not.

Many infinite groups are [linear groups](wikipedia://en/3983656), meaning that they have a faithful representation on a finite-dimensional vector space. This includes groups like $\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb Z)$ and every [classical group](wikipedia://en/10172878) (via its [adjoint representation](wikipedia://en/302188)), and every finitely-generated torsion-free nilpotent group, by [Malcev's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcev's_theorem), but not groups like the [metaplectic group](wikipedia://en/2981795).

Some infinite groups are [simple](wikipedia://en/47600), such as the [Thompson groups](wikipedia://en/612245).

## Asymptotics {#Asymptotics}

An infinite group equipped with a generating set $\{\gamma_i \mid i \in I\}$ inherits a natural metric structure via the [word metric](wikipedia://en/1289716). This is the unique left-invariant distance function such that $d(\gamma_i, e) = d(\gamma_i^{-1}, e) = 1$ for each generator $\gamma_i$, and distances extend by minimal word length. The resulting metric space is locally a [discrete topological space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topological_space), but its large-scale geometry exhibits meaningful structure. For instance, the volume of a ball of radius $r$ (i.e., the number of group elements expressible using at most $r$ generators) grows in a way that reflects intrinsic properties of the group—such as polynomial growth in [nilpotent groups](wikipedia://en/144569) or exponential growth in [free groups](wikipedia://en/59735).[^o7]

A finitely generated group has polynomial growth if and only if it is [virtually nilpotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtually_nilpotent), and that the group's large-scale geometry can often be understood via its [asymptotic cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_cone)—a kind of limiting metric space that captures the group's behavior "at infinity".

Many other aspects of infinite groups can be expressed in terms of the word metric, such as [amenability](wikipedia://en/657430): a group is *not* amenable, if and only if there exist constants $d$ and $C$ such that, for every finite subset $S$, $|S|\le C |\partial_d S|$, where $\partial_d S$ is the set of points within a distance $d$ of any element of $S$.
