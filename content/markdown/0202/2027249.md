# Oval

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Oval"}

*Image: oval1.svg.* This oval, with only one [axis of symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_of_symmetry), resembles a [chicken egg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicken_egg).

An **oval** (la) is a [closed curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_curve) in a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) which resembles the outline of an [egg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egg).[^o2][^o3][^o4] The term is not very specific, but in some areas of mathematics ([projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849), [technical drawing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_drawing), etc.), it is given a more precise definition, which may include either one or two [axes of symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axes_of_symmetry) of an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277). In common English, the term is used in a broader sense: any shape which reminds one of an egg.[^o5] The [three-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-dimensional) version of an oval is called an **ovoid**.[^o3]

:::

## Oval in geometry {#Oval_in_geometry}

The term **oval** when used to describe [curves](wikipedia://en/89246) in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) is not well defined, except in the context of projective geometry. Many distinct curves are commonly called ovals or are said to have an "oval shape". Generally, to be called an oval, a plane curve should *resemble* the outline of an egg or an ellipse. In particular, these are common traits of ovals:

- they are [differentiable](wikipedia://en/493403) (smooth-looking),[^o7] [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_curve) (not self-intersecting), [convex](wikipedia://en/29747989), [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_curve), and [plane curves](wikipedia://en/971691);
- their [shape](wikipedia://en/169191) does not depart much from that of an ellipse; and

*Image: Ovoid-definition.svg.* To the definition of an ovoid.

- an oval typically has an [axis of symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_of_symmetry), but this is not required.

Here are examples of ovals described elsewhere:

- [Cassini ovals](wikipedia://en/2040906)
- portions of some [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225)
- [Moss's egg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moss's_egg)
- [superellipse](wikipedia://en/52211)
- [Cartesian oval](wikipedia://en/29131481)
- [stadium](wikipedia://en/38383047)

An **ovoid** is the surface in [three-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/3054853) generated by rotating an oval curve about an axis of symmetry. The adjectives **ovoidal** and **ovate** mean having the characteristic of being an ovoid, and are often used as [synonyms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synonym) for "egg-shaped".

*Image: Oval-definition-en.svg.* To the definition of an oval in a [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350).

## Projective geometry {#Projective_geometry}

- In a [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) a set $\Omega$ of points is called an **[oval](wikipedia://en/4255174)**, if:

<!-- -->

1. Any line $l$ meets $\Omega$ in at most two points, and
1. For any point $P \in \Omega$, there exists exactly one tangent line $t$ through $P$, *[i.e.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Id_est)*, $t \cap \Omega = \{P$}.

For [*finite* planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_plane) (*i.e.*, the set of points is finite) there is a more convenient characterization:[^n1]

- For a [finite projective plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_projective_plane) of *order* $n$ (*i.e.*, any line contains $n + 1$ points) a set $\Omega$ of points is an oval if and only if $\lvert \Omega\rvert = n + 1$ and no three points are [collinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear) (on a common line).

An **[ovoid](wikipedia://en/4264509)** in a [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) is a set $\Omega$ of points such that:

1. Any line intersects $\Omega$ in at most 2 points,
1. The [tangents](wikipedia://en/31482) at a point cover a hyperplane (and nothing more), and
1. $\Omega$ contains no lines.

*Image: Oval 1 (PSF).svg.* An oval [egg shape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egg_shape).

In the *finite* case only for dimension 3, there exist ovoids. A convenient characterization is:

- In a [3-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-dimension) finite projective space of order $n > 2$ any pointset $\Omega$ is an ovoid if and only if |$\Omega$|$=n^2+1$ and no three points are collinear.[^n2]

## Egg shape {#Egg_shape}

The shape of an egg is approximated by the "long" half of a [prolate spheroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prolate_spheroid), joined to a "short" half of a roughly [spherical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical) [ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/145381), or even a slightly [oblate spheroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblate_spheroid). These are joined at the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator_%28disambiguation%29) and share a [principal axis](wikipedia://en/11376126) of [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096), as illustrated above. Although the term *egg-shaped* usually implies a lack of [reflection symmetry](wikipedia://en/1390873) across the [equatorial plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_plane), it may also refer to true [prolate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prolate) ellipsoids. It can also be used to describe the [two-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-dimensional) figure that, if revolved around its [major axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_axis), produces the three-dimensional surface.

*Image: Oval outline and its geometrical parameters.png.* Oval and its four key [geometrical](wikipedia://en/18973446) [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065): [length](wikipedia://en/18542) *L*, maximum [breadth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breadth) *B*, the shift *w* of the maximum breadth from the [center](wikipedia://en/4850775), that is, from the point *x* = *L*/2, and the breadth *D<sub>p</sub>* at a point $\tfrac{1}{4}$ of the length from the pointed end.

## Mathematical definition of an oval {#Mathematical_definition_of_an_oval}

Based on the meaning of the word "oval" (*i.e.*, "egg"), an oval refers to a flat representation of an egg. Since there are a variety of [egg shapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egg_shape) in nature, a mathematical description should graphically reproduce the oval shape of any egg profile.[^o14] Four key dimensions ([measurements](wikipedia://en/19022)) can be adopted as the defining [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065) of an oval:[^o15]

- length *L*,
- maximum breadth *B*,
- the shift *w* of the maximum breadth from the [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_%28geometry%29), that is, from the point *x* = *L*/2, and
- the breadth *D<sub>p</sub>* at a point $\tfrac{1}{4}$ of the length from the pointed end.

Accordingly, the [mathematical formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_formula) for an oval can be written as follows:[^o15]

$$y = \pm \frac{B}{2} \sin^n \left (\frac{\pi (1 - p) x}{x - pL} \right ),$$

in which

$$p = \frac{1}{2} \left [1 + \frac{1}{2} \left (\frac{w}{L} \right )^{-1} \right ],$$

$$n = \frac{\ln \left (\frac{D_{p}}{B} \right )}{\ln \left [\sin \left (\pi \frac{1 - p}{1 - 4p} \right ) \right ]}.$$

It is more convenient to operate with various modifications of oval shapes when a unit length (*L* = 1) is used, and the oval parameters are presented as their combinations conventionally expressed by [indices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indices), that is, the shape index (*B*/*L*), the [asymmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymmetry) index (*w*/*L*), and the [conicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conicity) index (*D<sub>p</sub>*/*B*).[^o15] Herewith, the mathematical formula for a unit oval can be written as:[^o15]

$$\frac{y}{L} = \pm \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{B}{L} \sin^n \left (\pi \frac{1 - p}{\frac{x}{L} - p} \cdot \frac{x}{L}\right ).$$

The variety of index values allows for countless variations of oval shapes, from standard [geometric shapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_shape) such as the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) and ellipse, to classic egg-shaped ovals and even more exotic [pyriform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyriform) (pear-shaped) and [biconical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biconic) shapes.[^o15]

## Technical drawing {#Technical_drawing}

*Image: Owal by Zureks.svg.* An oval with two [axes of symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axes_of_symmetry) constructed from four [arcs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_%28geometry%29) (top), and comparison of blue oval and red [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) with the same dimensions of short and long axes (bottom).

In technical drawing, an **oval** is a figure that is constructed from two pairs of [arcs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_%28geometry%29), with two different [radii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radii) (see image on the right). The arcs are joined at a point in which lines [tangential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangential) to both joining arcs lie on the same line, thus making the joint smooth. Any point of an oval belongs to an arc with a constant [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) (shorter or longer), but in an ellipse, the radius is continuously changing.

## In common speech {#In_common_speech}

In common speech, "oval" means a shape rather like an egg or an ellipse, which may be two-dimensional or three-dimensional. It also often refers to a figure that resembles two [semicircles](wikipedia://en/617831) joined by a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048), like a [cricket infield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infield_%28cricket%29), [speed skating rink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_skating_rink) or an [athletics track](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athletics_track). However, this is most correctly called a stadium.

*Image: Speedskating rink 400 meters with dimensions.svg.* A [speed skating rink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_skating_rink) is often called an oval.

The term "ellipse" is often used interchangeably with oval, but it has a more specific mathematical meaning.[^o18] The term "oblong" is also used to mean oval,[^o19] though in geometry an oblong refers to rectangle with unequal adjacent sides, not a curved figure.[^o20]
