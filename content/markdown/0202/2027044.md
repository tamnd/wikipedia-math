# A. C. Grayling

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="A. C. Grayling"}

**Anthony Clifford Grayling** (; born 3 April 1949) is a British [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher) and [author](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Author). He was born in [Northern Rhodesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Rhodesia) (now [Zambia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zambia)) and spent most of his childhood there and in Nyasaland (now [Malawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malawi)).[^o4] Until June 2011, he was Professor of Philosophy at [Birkbeck, University of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birkbeck,_University_of_London), where he taught from 1991. In 2011 he founded and became the first Master of [New College of the Humanities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_College_of_the_Humanities) (now Northeastern University London), an independent undergraduate college in London. He is also a supernumerary fellow of [St Anne's College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Anne's_College,_Oxford), where he formerly taught.[^o5]

:::

Grayling is the author of about 30 books on [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), [biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biography), [history of ideas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_ideas), [human rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights) and [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics), including *The Refutation of Scepticism* (1985), *The Future of Moral Values* (1997), *Wittgenstein* (1992), *What Is Good?* (2000), *[The Meaning of Things](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Meaning_of_Things)* (2001), *[The Good Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Good_Book_%28book%29)* (2011), *[The God Argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_God_Argument)* (2013), *The Age of Genius: The Seventeenth Century and the Birth of the Modern Mind* (2016) and *Democracy and its Crisis* (2017).

Grayling was a trustee of the [London Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Library) and a fellow of the [World Economic Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Economic_Forum), and is a fellow of the [Royal Society of Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Literature) and the [Royal Society of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_for_the_encouragement_of_Arts,_Manufactures_&_Commerce).[^o6] For a number of years he was a columnist for *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* newspaper,[^o7] and presented the [BBC World Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_World_Service) series *Exchanges at the Frontier*[^o8] on [science and society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_society).

Grayling was a director and contributor at *[Prospect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prospect_%28magazine%29)* magazine from its foundation until 2016. He is a vice-president of [Humanists UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanists_UK), honorary associate of the [National Secular Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Secular_Society),[^o9] and Patron of the Defence Humanists.[^o10] His main academic interests lie in [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology), [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics), and [philosophical logic](wikipedia://en/304939) and he has published works in these subjects.[^o6] His political affiliations lie on the [centre-left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre-left), and he has defended human rights and politically liberal values in print and by activism.[^o11] He is associated in Britain with other [New Atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Atheists).[^o12] He frequently appears in British media discussing philosophy and public affairs.[^o13] In his book, *Democracy and Its Crisis*, Grayling argues that voting systems must be reformed to prevent certain results, such as [Brexit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brexit) and the 2016 election of [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump).[^o53][^o54]

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

The son of Henry Clifford and Ursula Adelaide Grayling ( Burns),[^o15][^o16] Grayling was born and raised in [Luanshya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luanshya), [Northern Rhodesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Rhodesia) (now [Zambia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zambia)), within the British expatriate enclave, and raised there and in [Nyasaland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nyasaland) (now [Malawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malawi))[^o4] where his father worked as manager[^o17] for the [Standard Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Bank_%28historic%29).[^o18] He attended several boarding schools, including [Falcon College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_College) in [Southern Rhodesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Rhodesia) (now [Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwe)), from which he ran away after being regularly caned.[^o19] His first exposure to philosophical writing was at the age of twelve, when he found an English translation of the *[Charmides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charmides_%28dialogue%29)*, one of [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954)'s dialogues, in a local library.[^o18] At age fourteen, he read [G. H. Lewes's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Henry_Lewes) *Biographical History of Philosophy* (1846), which confirmed his ambition to study philosophy; he said it "superinduced order on the random reading that had preceded it, and settled my vocation".[^o20]

Grayling had an elder sister Jennifer and brother John.[^o21] When he was 19 years old, his elder sister Jennifer was murdered in [Johannesburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannesburg). She had been born with brain damage, and after brain surgery to alleviate it at the age of 20 had experienced personality problems that led to emotional difficulties[^o21] and a premature marriage. She was found dead in a river shortly after the marriage; she had been stabbed. When her parents went to identify her, her mother—already ill—had a heart attack and died. Grayling said he dealt with his grief by becoming a workaholic.[^o22]

After moving to England in his teens, he spent three years at the [University of Sussex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Sussex), but said that although he applauded their intention to educate generalists, he wished to be a scholar, so in addition to his BA from Sussex, he also completed one in philosophy as a [University of London external student](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_London_International_Programmes).[^o23] He went on to obtain an MA from Sussex, then attended [Magdalen College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magdalen_College,_Oxford), where he was taught by [P. F. Strawson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._F._Strawson) and [A. J. Ayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._J._Ayer), obtaining his doctorate in 1981 for a thesis on *Epistemological Scepticism and Transcendental Arguments*. A part of that thesis is published as *The Refutation of Scepticism* (1985) and its themes are further developed in *Scepticism and the Possibility of Knowledge* (2008).[^o24]

### Career {#Career}

Grayling lectured in philosophy at [Bedford College, London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedford_College,_London), and [St Anne's College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Anne's_College,_Oxford),[^o5] before taking up a post in 1991 at [Birkbeck, University of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birkbeck,_University_of_London), where in 1998 he became a [reader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reader_%28academic_rank%29) in philosophy, and in 2005 a professor.[^o26] In addition to his work on Berkeley, philosophical logic, the theory of knowledge, and the history of ideas,[^o27] the latter including (as chief editor) the four-volume *The Continuum Encyclopedia of British Philosophy*,[^o28] he wrote and edited several pedagogical works in philosophy, including *An Introduction to Philosophical Logic* (3rd ed., 1999)[^o29] and the two volumes *Philosophy: A Guide Through the Subject* (1995)[^o30] and *Philosophy: Further Through the Subject* (1998).[^o31]

In his philosophical work, Grayling connected solutions to the problem of [scepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scepticism) in [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology) with the questions about assertability and the problem of meaning in the philosophy of language and [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065). A principal theme in his work is that considerations of [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics), which relate to what exists, has to be kept separate from the two connected questions of the relation of thought to its objects in the variety of domains over which thought ranges, and the mastery of discourses about those domains, where a justificationist approach is required.[^o32]

Grayling resigned from Birkbeck in June 2011 to found and become the first master of the [New College of the Humanities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_College_of_the_Humanities), an independent undergraduate college in London. In February 2019, [Northeastern University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeastern_University), a private research university in [Boston, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston,_Massachusetts), US, purchased the New College of the Humanities.[^o33] He is a supernumerary fellow of St Anne's College, Oxford. He was a judge on the [Man Booker Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man_Booker_Prize) for 2003[^o34] and chairman of the judges for the 2014 Man Booker Prize.[^o35] He has also been a judge on the [Wellcome Trust Book Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellcome_Trust_Book_Prize)[^o36] and the Art Fund prize.[^o37]

In 2013 he was awarded the Forkosch Literary Prize,[^o38] and in 2015 he received the Bertrand Russell Society Award.[^o39] Grayling was appointed [Commander of the Order of the British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander_of_the_Order_of_the_British_Empire) (CBE) in the [2017 New Year Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_New_Year_Honours) for services to philosophy.

## Public advocacy {#Public_advocacy}

For Grayling, work on technical problems is only one aspect of philosophy. Another aspect, one which has been at the centre of philosophy's place in history, has more immediate application to daily life: the questions of ethics, which revolve upon what Grayling calls the great Socratic question, 'How should one live?'. In pursuit of what he describes as 'contributing to the conversation society has with itself about possibilities for good lives in good societies', Grayling writes widely on contemporary issues, including [war crimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_crimes), the [legalisation of drugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illegal_drugs_trade), [euthanasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euthanasia), [secularism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secularism), human rights and other topics in the tradition of [Polemics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polemics). He has articulated positions on [humanist ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanist_ethics) and on the history and nature of concepts of liberty as applied in civic life. In support of his belief that the philosopher should engage in public debate, he brings these philosophical perspectives to issues of the day in his work as a writer and as a commentator on radio and television.[^o42]

Among his contributions to the discussion about religion in contemporary society he argues that there are three separable, though naturally connected debates:

(a) a metaphysical debate about what the universe contains; denying that it contains supernatural agencies of any kind makes him an atheist;

(b) a debate about the basis of ethics; taking the world to be a natural realm of natural law requires that humanity thinks for itself about the right and the good, based on our best understanding of human nature and the human condition; this makes him a humanist;

(c) a debate about the place of religious movements and organisations in the public domain; as a secularist Grayling argues that these should see themselves as civil society organisations on a par with trade unions and other [NGOs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NGOs), with every right to exist and to have their say, but no greater right than any other self-constituted, self-selected interest group.

On this last point, Grayling's view is that for historical reasons religions have an inflated place in the public domain out of all proportion to the numbers of their adherents or their intrinsic merits, so that their voice and influence is amplified disproportionately: with the result that they can distort such matters as public policy (e.g. on abortion) and science research and education (e.g. [stem cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stem_cells), [teaching of evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teaching_of_evolution)). He argues that winning the metaphysical and ethical debates is already abating the problems associated with (c) in more advanced Western societies, even the US. He sees his own major contribution as being the promotion of understanding of humanist ethics deriving from the philosophical tradition.[^o43]

Between 1999 and 2002 Grayling wrote a weekly column in *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* called "The Last Word", on a different topic every week. In these columns, which also formed the basis of a series of books for a general readership, commencing with *[The Meaning of Things](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Meaning_of_Things)* in 2001, Grayling made the basics of philosophy available to the layperson. He is a regular contributor to *The Guardian's* "Comment is free" group blog, and writes columns for, among others, the [*Prospect*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prospect_%28magazine%29) and *[New Scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Scientist)* magazines.

Grayling is accredited with the [United Nations Human Rights Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Human_Rights_Council), and is a patron of [Humanists UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanists_UK), an Honorary Associate of the [National Secular Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Secular_Society), Patron of the Defence Humanists,[^o10] was a Trustee of the [London Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Library), and a board member of the [Society of Authors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_Authors) and an Honorary Patron of the Philosophy Foundation, a charity whose aim is to bring philosophy to the wider community, and particularly to disadvantaged schools. In 2003 he was a [Booker Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booker_Prize) judge[^o44] and Chairman of the Judges for the 2014 [Man Booker Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man_Booker_Prize).[^o35] In 2005, Grayling debated with Christian philosopher [William Lane Craig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lane_Craig) on whether God can exist in an evil world.[^o45][^o46] Grayling is also a Patron of the right to die organisation, [My Death My Decision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/My_Death_My_Decision).[^o47]

Grayling wrote a book on the , *[Among the Dead Cities: The History and Moral Legacy of the WWII Bombing of Civilians in Germany and Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Among_the_Dead_Cities)* (2006), as a contribution to the debate on the [ethics of war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics_of_war).[^o48][^o49] In September 2010, Grayling was one of 55 public figures who sent a letter to *The Guardian* expressing their opposition to [Pope Benedict XVI's state visit to the UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI's_state_visit_to_the_UK).[^o50] In August 2014, Grayling was one of 200 public figures who were signatories to a letter to *The Guardian* opposing [Scottish independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_independence) in the run-up to September's [referendum on that issue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Scottish_independence_referendum).[^o51]

A. C. Grayling was one of the contributors to the book, *We Are One: A Celebration of Tribal Peoples*, released in October 2009.[^o52] The book explores the cultures of peoples around the world, portraying both their diversity and the threats they face. Other contributors included not only western writers, such as [Laurens van der Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurens_van_der_Post), [Noam Chomsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noam_Chomsky), [Claude Lévi-Strauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Lévi-Strauss), but also indigenous people, such as [Davi Kopenawa Yanomami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davi_Kopenawa_Yanomami) and [Roy Sesana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Sesana). The royalties from the sale of this book go to the indigenous rights organisation, [Survival International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survival_International).

In recent years Grayling has been campaigning against the UK Government's response to the [2016 United Kingdom European Union membership referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_Kingdom_European_Union_membership_referendum) result. In his book, *Democracy and Its Crisis*, Grayling argues that voting systems must be reformed to prevent certain results, such as [Brexit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brexit) and the election of [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump).[^o53][^o54] Grayling has tweeted that Brexit must be made to disappear like a "nasty, temporary, hiccup, soon forgotten".[^o55][^o56]

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Grayling lives in central London. He has two children, Anthony Jolyon Clifford Grayling and Georgina Eveline Ursula Grayling, from his first marriage and one daughter, Madeline Catherine Jennifer Grayling, from his second marriage to the novelist [Katie Hickman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katie_Hickman).[^o58]
