# Mason–Stothers theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mason–Stothers theorem"}

The **Mason–Stothers theorem**, or simply **Stothers theorem**, is a mathematical [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) about [polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomials), analogous to the [*abc* conjecture](wikipedia://en/174108) for integers. It is named after [Walter Wilson Stothers](wikipedia://en/63845773), who published it in 1981,[^o2] and [R. C. Mason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._C._Mason), who rediscovered it shortly thereafter.[^o3]

:::

The theorem states:

Let $a(t)$, $b(t)$, and $c(t)$ be [relatively prime polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_prime_polynomials) over a field such that $a + b = c$ and such that not all of them have vanishing derivative. Then

$$\max\{\deg(a),\deg(b),\deg(c)\} \le \deg(\operatorname{rad}(abc))-1.$$

Here $\operatorname{rad}(f)$ is the product of the distinct irreducible factors of $f$. For algebraically closed fields it is the polynomial of minimum degree that has the same [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) as $f$; in this case $\deg(\operatorname{rad}(f))$ gives the number of distinct roots of $f$.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- Over fields of characteristic 0 the condition that $a$, $b$, and $c$ do not all have vanishing derivative is equivalent to the condition that they are not all constant. Over fields of characteristic $p > 0$ it is not enough to assume that they are not all constant. For example, considered as polynomials over some field of characteristic *p*, the identity $t^{p} + 1 = (t + 1)^{p}$ gives an example where the maximum degree of the three polynomials ($a = t^{p}$ and $b = 1$ as the summands on the left hand side, and $c = (t + 1)^{p}$ as the right hand side) is $p$, but the degree of the radical is only $2$.
- Taking $a(t) = t^{n}$ and $c(t) = (t+1)^{n}$ gives an example where equality holds in the Mason–Stothers theorem, showing that the inequality is in some sense the best possible.
- A corollary of the Mason–Stothers theorem is the analog of [Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953) for function fields: if $a(t)^{n} + b(t)^{n} = c(t)^{n}$ for $a$, $b$, $c$ relatively prime polynomials over a field of characteristic not dividing $n$ and $n > 2$ then either at least one of $a$, $b$, or $c$ is 0 or they are all constant.

:::

::: {#o8 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

(Snyder 2000) gave the following elementary proof of the Mason–Stothers theorem.[^o10]

Step 1. The condition $a + b + c = 0$ implies that the [Wronskians](wikipedia://en/302846) $W(a, b) = ab' - a'b$, $W(b, c)$, and $W(c, a)$ are all equal. Write $W$ for their common value.

Step 2. The condition that at least one of the derivatives $a'$, $b'$, or $c'$ is nonzero and that $a$, $b$, and $c$ are coprime is used to show that $W$ is nonzero. For example, if $W = 0$ then $ab' = a'b$ so $a$ divides $a'$ (as $a$ and $b$ are coprime) so $a' = 0$ (as $\deg a > \deg a'$ unless $a$ is constant).

Step 3. $W$ is divisible by each of the greatest common divisors $(a, a')$, $(b, b')$, and $(c, c')$. Since these are coprime it is divisible by their product, and since $W$ is nonzero we get

$$\deg (a, a') + \deg (b, b') + \deg (c, c') \le \deg W.$$

Step 4. Substituting in the inequalities

$\deg (a, a') \ge \deg a$ − (number of distinct roots of $a$)

$\deg (b, b') \ge \deg b$ − (number of distinct roots of $b$)

$\deg (c, c') \ge \deg c$ − (number of distinct roots of $c$)

(where the roots are taken in some algebraic closure) and

$$\deg W \le \deg a + \deg b - 1$$

we find that

$$\deg c \le (\operatorname{number}\text{ of } \operatorname{distinct} \operatorname{roots}\text{ of } abc) - 1$$

which is what we needed to prove.

:::

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

There is a natural generalization in which the ring of polynomials is replaced by a one-dimensional [function field](wikipedia://en/447645). Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field of characteristic 0, let $C/k$ be a [smooth projective curve](wikipedia://en/320469) of [genus](wikipedia://en/3115807) $g$, let

$a,b\in k(C)$ be rational functions on $C$ satisfying $a+b=1$,

and let $S$ be a set of points in $C(k)$ containing all of the zeros and poles of $a$ and $b$. Then

$$\max\bigl\{ \deg(a),\deg(b) \bigr\} \le \max\bigl\{|S| + 2g - 2,0\bigr\}.$$

Here the degree of a function in $k(C)$ is the degree of the map it induces from $C$ to **P**<sup>1</sup>. This was proved by Mason, with an alternative short proof published the same year by [J. H. Silverman](wikipedia://en/2288703) .[^o13]

There is a further generalization, due independently to [J. F. Voloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/José_Felipe_Voloch)[^o14] and to [W. D. Brownawell](wikipedia://en/45211789) and [D. W. Masser](wikipedia://en/2172777),[^o15] that gives an upper bound for $n$-variable $S$-unit equations $a_{1} + a_{2} + ... + a_{n} = 1$ provided that no subset of the $a_{i}$ are $k$-linearly dependent. Under this assumption, they prove that

$$\max\bigl\{ \deg(a_1),\ldots,\deg(a_n) \bigr\} \le \frac{1}{2}n(n-1)\max\bigl\{|S| + 2g - 2,0\bigr\}.$$

:::
