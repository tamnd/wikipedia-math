# Geopotential spherical harmonic model

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Geopotential spherical harmonic model"}

In [geophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysics) and [physical geodesy](wikipedia://en/176813), a geopotential model is the theoretical analysis of measuring and calculating the effects of [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228)'s [gravitational field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_field) (the [geopotential](wikipedia://en/217535)). The Earth is not exactly spherical, mainly because of its rotation around the polar axis that makes its shape slightly oblate. However, a [spherical harmonics](wikipedia://en/203056) [series expansion](wikipedia://en/1575813) captures the actual field with increasing fidelity.

:::

If [Earth's shape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_shape) were perfectly known together with the exact mass density ρ = ρ(*x*, *y*, *z*), it could be [integrated numerically](wikipedia://en/170089) (when combined with a [reciprocal distance kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_potential#Mathematical_form)) to find an accurate model for Earth's gravitational field. However, the situation is in fact the opposite: by observing the orbits of spacecraft and the Moon, Earth's gravitational field can be determined quite accurately. The best estimate of [Earth's mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_mass) is obtained by dividing the product *GM* as determined from the analysis of spacecraft orbit with a value for the [gravitational constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_constant) *G*, determined to a lower relative accuracy using other physical methods.

## Background {#Background}

From the defining equations ((1)) and ((2)) it is clear (taking the partial derivatives of the integrand) that outside the body in empty space the following differential equations are valid for the field caused by the body:

::: {#o3 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$$\frac{\partial F_x }{\partial x} + \frac{\partial F_y }{\partial y} + \frac{\partial F_z }{\partial z} = 0 \tag{5}$$

:::

::: {#o4 .equation cue=numblk name="6"}

$$\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial z^2} = 0 \tag{6}$$

:::

Functions of the form $\phi = R(r)\, \Theta(\theta)\, \Phi(\varphi)$ where (*r*, θ, φ) are the [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates) which satisfy the [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564) ((6)) (the [Laplace equation](wikipedia://en/36941)) are called [spherical harmonic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_harmonic_function).

They take the forms:

::: {#o5 .equation cue=numblk name="7"}

$$\begin{aligned} g(x, y, z) &= \frac{1}{r^{n+1}} P^m_n(\sin \theta) \cos m\varphi \,,& 0 &\le m \le n \,,& n &= 0, 1, 2, \dots \\ h(x, y, z) &= \frac{1}{r^{n+1}} P^m_n(\sin \theta) \sin m\varphi \,,& 1 &\le m \le n \,,& n &= 1, 2, \dots \end{aligned} \tag{7}$$

:::

where [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates) (*r*, θ, φ) are used, given here in terms of cartesian (*x, y, z*) for reference:

::: {#o6 .equation cue=numblk name="8"}

$$\begin{aligned} x &= r \cos \theta \cos \varphi \\ y &= r \cos \theta \sin \varphi \\ z &= r \sin \theta\,, \end{aligned} \tag{8}$$

:::

also *P*<sup>0</sup><sub>*n*</sub> are the [Legendre polynomials](wikipedia://en/100349) and *P<sup>m</sup><sub>n</sub>* for 1 ≤ *m* ≤ *n* are the [associated Legendre functions](wikipedia://en/1062015).

## Formulation {#Formulation}

The model for Earth's gravitational potential is a sum

::: {#o9 .equation cue=numblk name="9"}

$$u = -\frac{\mu}{r} + \sum_{n=2}^{N_z} \frac{J_n P^0_n(\sin\theta) }{r^{n+1}} + \sum_{n=2}^{N_t} \sum_{m=1}^n \frac{ P^m_n(\sin\theta) \left(C_n^m \cos m\varphi + S_n^m \sin m\varphi\right)}{r^{n+1}} \tag{9}$$

:::

where $\mu = GM$ and the coordinates ((8)) are relative to the standard geodetic reference system extended into space with origin in the center of the [reference ellipsoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_ellipsoid) and with *z*-axis in the direction of the polar axis.

The **zonal terms** refer to terms of the form:

$$\frac{P^0_n(\sin\theta)}{r^{n+1}} \quad n=0,1,2,\dots$$

and the **tesseral terms** terms refer to terms of the form:

$$\frac{P^m_n(\sin\theta) \cos m\varphi}{r^{n+1}}\,, \quad 1 \le m \le n \quad n=1,2,\dots$$

$$\frac{P^m_n(\sin\theta) \sin m\varphi}{r^{n+1}}$$

The zonal and tesseral terms for *n* = 1 are left out in ((9)). The coefficients for the n=1 with both m=0 and m=1 term correspond to an arbitrarily oriented dipole term in the multi-pole expansion. Gravity does not physically exhibit any dipole character and so the integral characterizing *n* = 1 must be zero.

The different coefficients *J<sub>n</sub>*, *C<sub>n</sub><sup>m</sup>*, *S<sub>n</sub><sup>m</sup>*, are then given the values for which the best possible agreement between the computed and the observed spacecraft orbits is obtained.

As *P*<sup>0</sup><sub>n</sub>(*x*) = −*P*<sup>0</sup><sub>n</sub>(−*x*) non-zero coefficients *J<sub>n</sub>* for **odd *n*** correspond to a lack of symmetry "north–south" relative the equatorial plane for the mass distribution of Earth. Non-zero coefficients *C<sub>n</sub><sup>m</sup>*, *S<sub>n</sub><sup>m</sup>* correspond to a lack of [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096) around the polar axis for the mass distribution of Earth, i.e. to a "tri-axiality" of Earth.

For large values of *n* the coefficients above (that are divided by *r*<sup>(*n* + 1)</sup> in ((9))) take very large values when for example kilometers and seconds are used as units. In the literature it is common to introduce some arbitrary "reference radius" *R* close to Earth's radius and to work with the dimensionless coefficients

$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{J_n} &= -\frac{J_n}{\mu\ R^n}, & \tilde{C_{n}^m} &= -\frac{C_{n}^m}{\mu\ R^n}, & \tilde{S_{n}^m} &= -\frac{S_{n}^m}{\mu\ R^n} \end{aligned}$$

and to write the potential as

::: {#o10 .equation cue=numblk name="10"}

$$u = -\frac{\mu }{r} \left(1 + \sum_{n=2}^{N_z} \frac{\tilde{J_n} P^0_n(\sin\theta) }{{(\frac{r}{R})}^n} + \sum_{n=2}^{N_t} \sum_{m=1}^n \frac{ P^m_n(\sin\theta) (\tilde{C_{n}^m} \cos m\varphi + \tilde{S_{n}^m} \sin m\varphi)}{{(\frac{r}{R})}^n}\right) \tag{10}$$

:::

### Derivation {#Derivation}

The spherical harmonics are derived from the approach of looking for harmonic functions of the form

::: {#o12 .equation cue=numblk name="16"}

$$\phi = R(r)\ \Theta(\theta)\ \Phi(\varphi) \tag{16}$$

:::

where (*r*, θ, φ) are the [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates) defined by the equations ((8)). By straightforward calculations one gets that for any function *f*

::: {#o13 .equation cue=numblk name="17"}

$$\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}\ +\ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}\ +\ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z^2}\ =\ {1 \over r^2}{\partial \over \partial r}\left(r^2 {\partial f \over \partial r}\right) + {1 \over r^2\cos\theta}{\partial \over \partial \theta}\left(\cos\theta {\partial f \over \partial \theta}\right) + {1 \over r^2\cos^2\theta}{\partial^2 f \over \partial \varphi^2} \tag{17}$$

:::

Introducing the expression ((16)) in ((17)) one gets that

::: {#o14 .equation cue=numblk name="18"}

$$\frac{r^2}{\phi}\left(\frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial z^2}\right)\ = \frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) + \frac{1}{\Theta\cos\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\left(\cos\theta \frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{\Phi\cos^2\theta}\frac{d^2\Phi}{d\varphi^2} \tag{18}$$

:::

As the term

$$\frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right)$$

only depends on the variable $r$ and the sum

$$\frac{1}{\Theta\cos\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\left(\cos\theta \frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{\Phi\cos^2\theta}\frac{d^2\Phi}{d\varphi^2}$$

only depends on the variables θ and φ. One gets that φ is harmonic if and only if

::: {#o15 .equation cue=numblk name="19"}

$$\frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) = \lambda \tag{19}$$

:::

and

::: {#o16 .equation cue=numblk name="20"}

$$\frac{1}{\Theta\cos\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\left(\cos\theta \frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{\Phi\cos^2\theta}\frac{d^2\Phi}{d\varphi^2} = -\lambda \tag{20}$$

:::

for some constant $\lambda$.

From ((20)) then follows that

$$\frac{1}{\Theta}\ \cos\theta\ \frac{d}{d\theta}\left(\cos\theta \frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\right)\ + \lambda\ \cos^2\theta\ +\ \frac{1}{\Phi}\frac{d^2\Phi}{d\varphi^2}\ =\ 0$$

The first two terms only depend on the variable $\theta$ and the third only on the variable $\varphi$.

From the definition of φ as a spherical coordinate it is clear that Φ(φ) must be periodic with the period 2π and one must therefore have that

::: {#o17 .equation cue=numblk name="21"}

$$\frac{1}{\Phi}\frac{d^2\Phi}{d\varphi^2} = -m^2 \tag{21}$$

:::

and

::: {#o18 .equation cue=numblk name="22"}

$$\frac{1}{\Theta}\ \cos\theta\ \frac{d}{d\theta}\left(\cos\theta \frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\right)\ + \lambda\ \cos^2\theta = m^2 \tag{22}$$

:::

for some integer *m* as the family of solutions to ((21)) then are

::: {#o19 .equation cue=numblk name="23"}

$$\Phi(\varphi)\ =a\ \cos m\varphi\ +\ b\ \sin m\varphi \tag{23}$$

:::

With the variable substitution

$$x=\sin \theta$$

equation ((22)) takes the form

::: {#o20 .equation cue=numblk name="24"}

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(\left(1 - x^2\right) \frac{d\Theta}{dx}\right) + \left(\lambda -\frac{m^2}{1 - x^2}\right)\Theta = 0 \tag{24}$$

:::

From ((19)) follows that in order to have a solution $\phi$ with

$$R(r) = \frac{1}{r^{n+1}}$$

one must have that

$$\lambda = n (n + 1)$$

If *P<sub>n</sub>*(*x*) is a solution to the differential equation

::: {#o21 .equation cue=numblk name="25"}

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(\left(1 - x^2\right)\ \frac{dP_n}{dx}\right)\ +\ n(n + 1)\ P_n = 0 \tag{25}$$

:::

one therefore has that the potential corresponding to *m* = 0

$$\phi = \frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\ P_n(\sin\theta)$$

**which is rotationally symmetric around the z-axis** is a harmonic function

If $P_{n}^{m}(x)$ is a solution to the differential equation

::: {#o22 .equation cue=numblk name="26"}

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(\left(1 - x^2\right)\ \frac{dP_{n}^{m}}{dx}\right)\ +\ \left(n(n + 1) - \frac{m^2}{1 - x^2} \right)\ P_{n}^{m}\ =\ 0 \tag{26}$$

:::

with *m* ≥ 1 one has the potential

::: {#o23 .equation cue=numblk name="27"}

$$\phi = \frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\ P_{n}^{m}(\sin\theta)\ (a\ \cos m\varphi\ +\ b\ \sin m\varphi) \tag{27}$$

:::

where *a* and *b* are arbitrary constants is a harmonic function that depends on φ and therefore is **not** rotationally symmetric around the z-axis

The differential equation ((25)) is the Legendre differential equation for which the [Legendre polynomials](wikipedia://en/100349) defined

::: {#o24 .equation cue=numblk name="28"}

$$\begin{aligned} P_{0}(x) &= 1 \\ P_{n}(x) &= \frac{1}{2^n n!}\ \frac{d^n\left(x^2 - 1\right)^n}{dx^n} \quad n \ge 1 \\ \end{aligned} \tag{28}$$

:::

are the solutions.

The arbitrary factor 1/(2<sup>n</sup>*n*!) is selected to make *P<sub>n</sub>*(−1) = −1 and *P<sub>n</sub>*(1) = 1 for odd *n* and *P<sub>n</sub>*(−1) = *P<sub>n</sub>*(1) = 1 for even *n*.

The first six Legendre polynomials are:

::: {#o25 .equation cue=numblk name="29"}

$$\begin{aligned} P_{0}(x) &= 1 \\ P_{1}(x) &= x \\ P_{2}(x) &= \frac{1}{2} \left(3x^2 - 1\right) \\ P_{3}(x) &= \frac{1}{2} \left(5x^3 - 3x\right) \\ P_{4}(x) &= \frac{1}{8} \left(35x^4 - 30x^2 + 3\right) \\ P_{5}(x) &= \frac{1}{8} \left(63x^5 - 70x^3 + 15x\right) \\ \end{aligned} \tag{29}$$

:::

The solutions to differential equation ((26)) are the associated [Legendre functions](wikipedia://en/100349)

::: {#o26 .equation cue=numblk name="30"}

$$P_n^m(x) = \left(1 - x^2\right)^\frac{m}{2}\ \frac{d^m P_n}{dx^m} \quad 1 \le m \le n \tag{30}$$

:::

One therefore has that

$$P_n^m (\sin\theta) = \cos^m \theta\ \frac{d^n P_n}{dx^n} (\sin\theta)$$

:::

## Largest terms {#Largest_terms}

The dominating term (after the term −μ/*r*) in ((9)) is the *J*<sub>2</sub> coefficient, the *[second dynamic form factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_dynamic_form_factor)* representing the oblateness of Earth:

$$u = \frac{J_2\ P^0_2(\sin\theta)}{r^3} = J_2 \frac{1}{r^3} \frac{1}{2} (3\sin^2\theta -1) = J_2 \frac{1}{r^5} \frac{1}{2} (3 z^2 -r^2)$$

Relative the [coordinate system](wikipedia://en/81931)

::: {#o28 .equation cue=numblk name="11"}

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\varphi} &= -\sin \varphi \hat{x} + \cos \varphi \hat{y} \\ \hat{\theta} &= -\sin \theta\, (\cos \varphi \hat{x} + \sin \varphi \hat{y}) + \cos\theta \hat{z} \\ \hat{r} &= \cos \theta\, (\cos \varphi \hat{x} + \sin \varphi \hat{y}) + \sin\theta \hat{z} \end{aligned} \tag{11}$$

:::

illustrated in figure 1 the components of the force caused by the "*J*<sub>2</sub> term" are

::: {#o29 .equation cue=numblk name="12"}

$$\begin{aligned} F_\theta &= -\frac{1}{r}\ \frac{\partial u }{\partial \theta} = -J_2\ \frac{1}{r^4} 3\ \cos\theta\ \sin\theta \\ F_r &= -\frac{\partial u }{\partial r} = J_2\ \frac{1}{r^4} \frac{3}{2}\ \left(3\sin^2\theta\ -\ 1\right) \end{aligned} \tag{12}$$

:::

In the rectangular coordinate system (*x, y, z*) with unit vectors (*x̂ ŷ ẑ*) the force components are:

::: {#o30 .equation cue=numblk name="13"}

$$\begin{aligned} F_x &= -\frac{\partial u }{\partial x} = J_2\ \frac{x}{r^7} \left(6z^2 - \frac{3}{2} (x^2 + y^2)\right) \\ F_y &= -\frac{\partial u }{\partial y} = J_2\ \frac{y}{r^7} \left(6z^2 - \frac{3}{2} (x^2 + y^2)\right) \\ F_z &= -\frac{\partial u }{\partial z} = J_2\ \frac{z}{r^7} \left(3z^2 - \frac{9}{2} (x^2 + y^2)\right) \end{aligned} \tag{13}$$

:::

The components of the force corresponding to the "*J*<sub>3</sub> term"

$$u = \frac{J_3 P^0_3(\sin\theta) }{r^4} = J_3 \frac{1}{r^4} \frac{1}{2} \sin\theta \left(5\sin^2\theta - 3\right) = J_3 \frac{1}{r^7} \frac{1}{2} z \left(5 z^2 - 3 r^2\right)$$

are

::: {#o31 .equation cue=numblk name="14"}

$$\begin{aligned} F_\theta &= -\frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial u }{\partial \theta} = -J_3 \frac{1}{r^5} \frac{3}{2} \cos\theta \left(5 \sin^2\theta - 1\right) \\ F_r &= -\frac{\partial u }{\partial r} = J_3 \frac{1}{r^5} 2 \sin\theta \left(5\sin^2\theta - 3\right) \end{aligned} \tag{14}$$

:::

and

::: {#o32 .equation cue=numblk name="15"}

$$\begin{aligned} F_x &= -\frac{\partial u }{\partial x} = J_3 \frac{x z}{r^9} \left(10 z^2 - \frac{15}{2} (x^2 + y^2)\right) \\ F_y &= -\frac{\partial u }{\partial y} = J_3 \frac{y z}{r^9} \left(10 z^2 - \frac{15}{2} (x^2 + y^2)\right) \\ F_z &= -\frac{\partial u }{\partial z} = J_3 \frac{1}{r^9} \left(4 z^2\ \left( z^2 - 3 (x^2 + y^2)\right) + \frac{3}{2} (x^2 + y^2)^2\right) \end{aligned} \tag{15}$$

:::

The exact numerical values for the coefficients deviate (somewhat) between different Earth models but for the lowest coefficients they all agree almost exactly.

For the JGM-3 model (see below) the values are:

μ = 398600.440 km<sup>3</sup>⋅s<sup>−2</sup>

*J*<sub>2</sub> = 1.75553 × 10<sup>10</sup> km<sup>5</sup>⋅s<sup>−2</sup>

*J*<sub>3</sub> = −2.61913 × 10<sup>11</sup> km<sup>6</sup>⋅s<sup>−2</sup>

For example, at a radius of 6600 km (about 200 km above Earth's surface) *J*<sub>3</sub>/(*J*<sub>2</sub>*r*) is about 0.002; i.e., the correction to the "*J*<sub>2</sub> force" from the "*J*<sub>3</sub> term" is in the order of 2 parts per thousand. The negative value of *J*<sub>3</sub> implies that for a point mass in Earth's equatorial plane the gravitational force is tilted slightly towards the south due to the lack of symmetry for the mass distribution of Earth's "north–south".

## Recursive algorithms used for the numerical propagation of spacecraft orbits {#Recursive_algorithms_used_for_the_numerical_propagation_of_spacecraft_orbits}

Spacecraft orbits are computed by the [numerical integration](wikipedia://en/272829) of the [equation of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations_of_motion). For this the gravitational force, i.e. the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) of the potential, must be computed. Efficient [recursive algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursion_%28mathematics%29) have been designed to compute the gravitational force for any $N_z$ and $N_t$ (the max degree of zonal and tesseral terms) and such algorithms are used in standard orbit propagation software.

## Available models {#Available_models}

The earliest Earth models in general use by [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA) and [ESRO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Space_Research_Organisation)/[ESA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Space_Agency) were the "Goddard Earth Models" developed by [Goddard Space Flight Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goddard_Space_Flight_Center) (GSFC) denoted "GEM-1", "GEM-2", "GEM-3", and so on. Later the "Joint Earth Gravity Models" denoted "JGM-1", "JGM-2", "JGM-3" developed by GSFC in cooperation with universities and private companies became available. The newer models generally provided higher order terms than their precursors. The [EGM96](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EGM96) uses *N<sub>z</sub>* = *N<sub>t</sub>* = 360 resulting in 130317 coefficients. An EGM2008 model is available as well.

For a normal Earth satellite requiring an orbit determination/prediction accuracy of a few meters the "JGM-3" truncated to *N<sub>z</sub>* = *N<sub>t</sub>* = 36 (1365 coefficients) is usually sufficient. Inaccuracies from the modeling of the air-drag and to a lesser extent the solar [radiation pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_pressure) will exceed the inaccuracies caused by the gravitation modeling errors.
