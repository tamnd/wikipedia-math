# Weakly chained diagonally dominant matrix

*Image: WCDD Venn Diagram.png.* Venn Diagram showing the containment of weakly chained diagonally dominant (WCDD) matrices relative to weakly diagonally dominant (WDD) and strictly diagonally dominant (SDD) matrices.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Weakly chained diagonally dominant matrix"}

In mathematics, the **weakly chained diagonally dominant matrices** are a family of [nonsingular matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonsingular_matrices) that include the strictly [diagonally dominant matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagonally_dominant_matrices).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### Preliminaries {#Preliminaries}

We say row $i$ of a complex matrix $A = (a_{ij})$ is **strictly diagonally dominant** (SDD) if $|a_{ii}|>\textstyle{\sum_{j\neq i}}|a_{ij}|$. We say $A$ is SDD if all of its rows are SDD. **Weakly diagonally dominant** (WDD) is defined with $\geq$ instead.

The **[directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986)** associated with an $m \times m$ complex matrix $A = (a_{ij})$ is given by the vertices $\{1, \ldots, m\}$ and edges defined as follows: there exists an edge from $i \rightarrow j$ if and only if $a_{ij} \neq 0$.

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

A complex square matrix $A$ is said to be **weakly chained diagonally dominant** (WCDD) if

- $A$ is WDD and
- for each row $i_1$ that is *not* SDD, there exists a [walk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walk_%28graph_theory%29) $i_1 \rightarrow i_2 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow i_k$ in the directed graph of $A$ ending at an SDD row $i_k$.

:::

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

*Image: Graph of a WCDD matrix.png.* The directed graph associated with the WCDD matrix in the example. The first row, which is SDD, is highlighted. Note that regardless of which node $i$ we start at, we can find a walk $i \rightarrow (i - 1) \rightarrow (i - 2) \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow 1$.

The $m \times m$ matrix

$$\begin{pmatrix}1\\ -1 & 1\\ & -1 & 1\\ & & \ddots & \ddots\\ & & & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

is WCDD.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

### Nonsingularity {#Nonsingularity}

A WCDD matrix is nonsingular.[^o12]

*Proof*:[^o13] Let $A=(a_{ij})$ be a WCDD matrix. Suppose there exists a nonzero $x$ in the [null space](wikipedia://en/1072915) of $A$. [Without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000), let $i_1$ be such that $|x_{i_1}|=1\geq|x_j|$ for all $j$. Since $A$ is WCDD, we may pick a walk $i_1\rightarrow i_2\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow i_k$ ending at an SDD row $i_k$.

Taking moduli on both sides of

$$-a_{i_1 i_1}x_{i_1} = \sum_{j\neq i_1} a_{i_{1} j}x_j$$

and applying the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941) yields

$$\left|a_{i_1 i_1}\right|\leq\sum_{j\neq i_1}\left|a_{i_1 j}\right|\left|x_j\right|\leq\sum_{j\neq i_1}\left|a_{i_1 j}\right|,$$

and hence row $i_1$ is not SDD. Moreover, since $A$ is WDD, the above chain of inequalities holds with equality so that $|x_{j}|=1$ whenever $a_{i_1 j}\neq0$. Therefore, $|x_{i_2}|=1$. Repeating this argument with $i_2$, $i_3$, etc., we find that $i_k$ is not SDD, a contradiction. $\square$

Recalling that an [irreducible](wikipedia://en/1001293) matrix is one whose associated directed graph is [strongly connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strongly_connected), a trivial corollary of the above is that an *irreducibly diagonally dominant matrix* (i.e., an irreducible WDD matrix with at least one SDD row) is nonsingular.[^o14]

### Relationship with nonsingular M-matrices {#Relationship_with_nonsingular_M-matrices}

The following are equivalent:[^o16]

- $A$ is a nonsingular WDD [M-matrix](wikipedia://en/7939066).
- $A$ is a nonsingular WDD [L-matrix](wikipedia://en/51718642);
- $A$ is a WCDD [L-matrix](wikipedia://en/51718642);

In fact, WCDD L-matrices were studied (by [James H. Bramble](wikipedia://en/26993300) and B. E. Hubbard) as early as 1964 in a journal article[^o17] in which they appear under the alternate name of *matrices of positive type*.

Moreover, if $A$ is an $n\times n$ WCDD L-matrix, we can bound its inverse as follows:[^o18]

$\left\Vert A^{-1}\right\Vert _{\infty}\leq\sum_{i}\left[a_{ii}\prod_{j=1}^{i}(1-u_{j})\right]^{-1}$ where $u_{i}=\frac{1}{\left|a_{ii}\right|}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\left|a_{ij}\right|.$

Note that $u_n$ is always zero and that the right-hand side of the bound above is $\infty$ whenever one or more of the constants $u_i$ is one.

Tighter bounds for the inverse of a WCDD L-matrix are known.[^o19][^o20][^o21][^o22]

## Applications {#Applications}

Due to their relationship with [M-matrices](wikipedia://en/7939066) (see above), WCDD matrices appear often in practical applications. An example is given below.

### Monotone numerical schemes {#Monotone_numerical_schemes}

WCDD L-matrices arise naturally from monotone approximation schemes for [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations).

For example, consider the one-dimensional [Poisson problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_problem)

$u^{\prime \prime}(x) + g(x)= 0$ for $x \in (0,1)$

with [Dirichlet boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_boundary_conditions) $u(0)=u(1)=0$. Letting $\{0,h,2h,\ldots,1\}$ be a numerical grid (for some positive $h$ that divides unity), a monotone finite difference scheme for the Poisson problem takes the form of

$-\frac{1}{h^2}A\vec{u} + \vec{g} = 0$ where $[\vec{g}]_j = g(jh)$

and

$$A = \begin{pmatrix}2 & -1\\ -1 & 2 & -1\\ & -1 & 2 & -1\\ & & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots\\ & & & -1 & 2 & -1\\ & & & & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Note that $A$ is a WCDD L-matrix.
