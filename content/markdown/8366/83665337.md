# Synathroesmus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Synathroesmus"}

**Synathroesmus** () is a [rhetorical device](wikipedia://en/430274) which involves the collection or listing of words, often adjectives, in a list; it also may be spelled **synathroismos** or **sinathrismus** and is also referred to as ***frequentatio*** or **the heaping figure**.[^o2] The gathered words may or may not be synonyms and may involve the collection of previous phrases or ideas from an oral or written work. Often, synathroesmus is used invectively: to emphasize blame or negative traits.

:::

## Etymology {#Etymology}

Synathroesmus is derived from Greek *synathroismos* (*συναθροισμός*; "a collection, union")[^o4]

## Rhetorical use {#Rhetorical_use}

Synathroesmus, being a collection of like terms, is meant to emphasize a description of the subject. When the subject is an inanimate object, synathroesmus may enliven it and create vivid [imagery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imagery).[^o4] The piling up of descriptions can accentuate a cumulative favoring or disfavoring towards its subject;[^o6] in the latter case, where is it a heap of insults, it is referred to as [bdelygmia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bdelygmia)[^o7]

According to [Mark Forsyth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Forsyth), part of the effectiveness of such listings is because of their unnaturalness in normal oral language which emphasizes imagery or tone in a manner unfound in human speech: "Nobody but a God or a fool talks in lists."[^o7]

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- In "[Nicholas Nickleby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Nickleby)," [Dickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens) uses synathroesmus by describing a character as "a proud, haughty, consequential, turned-up-nose peacock." The device is used to exacerbate the snobbyness of the character.[^o4]
- [Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shakespeare), in *[The Tempest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tempest)*, uses synathroesmus in the following lines: "The cloud-capp'd towers, the gorgeous palaces, / The solemn temples, the great glope itself"[^o7]
- The following quote (with liberty taken to modernize archaic spelling) is an example given by [Richard Sherry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Sherry): "He is an harlot of his own body, he lies in wait for others, greedy, intemperate, wanton, proud, unnatural to his parents, unkind to his friends, troublesome to his kinsfolk, stubborn to his betters, disdainful to his equals, cruel to his inferiors, finally, intolerable to all men." Note the device, similar to the above example, being used to degrade its subject; Sherry describes his use of synathroesmus more akin to [accumulatio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accumulatio).[^o10]
- In *[The Path to Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Path_to_Rome)*, [Belloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilaire_Belloc) declares, "So this threat about the heavy task of beginning breeds discouragement, anger, vexation, irritability, bad style, pomposity and infinitives split from helm to saddle, and metaphors mixed as the Carlton."[^o11]
- [Belloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilaire_Belloc), again in *[The Path to Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Path_to_Rome)*, jokes, "I see. You would not pile words one on the other, qualifying, exaggerating, conditioning, superlative, diminishing, connecting, amplifying, condensing, mouthing, and glorifying the mere sound: you would be terse."[^o11]

:::

## Subcategories {#Subcategories}

Synathroesmus is a broad rhetorical category that describes types of listings; those subcategories include the following:

::: {#o14 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Synonymia"}

### Synonymia {#Synonymia}

Synonymia is synathroesmus in which the listed terms are [synonymous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synonym) with each other[^o10] with the purpose of emphasizing emotional meaning or increasing clarity.[^o15]

For example, in [*Julius Caesar*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar_%28play%29), a Roman tribune named Marullus insults the Caesar-supporting Romans by exclaiming, "You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things!" This [bdelygmia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bdelygmia) is an example of synonymia because it involves repeated synonyms and aims to intensify the pejorative name-calling.[^o15]

Synonymia comes from [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *synōnymia* which was [borrowed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borrowing_%28linguistics%29) from sunōnumía (συνωνυμία) which comes from *sunṓnumos* (συνώνυμος; "of like name" ) and the noun-forming *\-ία* [suffix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffix).[^o16] Ultimately, the word derives from *syn-* (σῠν-; alike, same) and *onyma* (ὄνομα / ὄνυμα; "name").[^o17]

:::

::: {#o19 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Congeries"}

### Congeries {#Congeries}

Congeries refers to synathroesmus involving words of differing meaning— though this distinction is not always made, and congeries may be viewed as synonymous with synathroesmus or the same as synonymia. While its constituents may not be synonymous, the elements of a congeries typically invoke the same meaning.

[Galatians 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galatians_5) is an example of a congeries: "${}^{22}$But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, forbearance, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, ${}^{23}$gentleness and self-control. Against such things there is no law" ([NIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_International_Version)).[^o20] Note how the individual nouns used to described the "fruit of the Spirit" are diverse and non-synonymous, but, nonetheless, have the same positive connotation.[^o7]

Congeries, in English, literally means a collection or heap. The term is derived from [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *congeries* ("heap, pile") which comes from the verb *congerere* ("to pile up") from *con-* ("with, together") and *gerere* ("to carry").[^o21] Congeries is an invariant noun as its singular is identical to the plural.[^o7]

:::

### Accumulatio {#Accumulatio}

[Accumulatio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accumulatio), unlike the former two, involves the culmination of previously stated points or arguments via synathroesmus.[^o23] It is often used to summarize, emphasize, or strengthen the main points of an argument.[^o24]

The fourth book of [Rhetorica ad Herennium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetorica_ad_Herennium) provides an example of accumulatio in a courtroom argument: "From what vice, I ask, is this defendant free? What ground have you for wishing to acquit him of the suit? He is the betrayer of his own self respect, and the waylayer of the self-respect of others; covetous, intemperate, irascible, arrogant; disloyal to his parents, ungrateful to his friends, troublesome to his kin; insulting to his betters, disdainful of his equals and mates, cruel to his inferiors; in short he is intolerable to every one." Accumulatio, unlike some other uses of synathroesmus, draws from previously made arguments but has a similar rhetorical structure of listing elements in order to emphasize a point.[^o25]

*Accumlatio,* sometimes anglicely referred to as its "accumulation" (which is also its [doublet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doublet_%28linguistics%29)), is derived directly from its homonymous [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) noun meaning "accumulation, heaping, piling up" which comes from the verb *accumulare* ("to amass") which further is derived from the prefix *\-ad*, ("to") and *cumulare* ("to heap;" from noun *cumulus*: "a heap").[^o26][^o27][^o28]
