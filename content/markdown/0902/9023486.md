# Aircraft principal axes

*Image: Yaw Axis Corrected.svg.* The position of all three axes, with the [right-hand rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-hand_rule) for describing the angle of its rotations

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Aircraft principal axes"}

An [aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft) in flight is free to [rotate](wikipedia://en/39789) in three dimensions: ***yaw***, nose left or right about an axis running up and down; ***pitch***, nose up or down about an axis running from wing to wing; and ***roll***, rotation about an axis running from nose to tail. The yaw, pitch, and roll axes are alternatively designated as *vertical*, *lateral* (or *transverse*), and *longitudinal* respectively. These axes [move with the vehicle](wikipedia://en/439082) and rotate relative to the Earth along with the craft. These definitions were analogously applied to [spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacecraft) when the first crewed spacecraft were designed in the late 1950s.

:::

These rotations are produced by [torques](wikipedia://en/30400) (or [moments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment_%28physics%29)) about the principal axes. On an aircraft, these are intentionally produced by means of moving control surfaces, which vary the distribution of the net [aerodynamic](wikipedia://en/2819) force about the vehicle's [center of gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_gravity). [Elevators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevator_%28aeronautics%29) (moving flaps on the horizontal tail) produce pitch, a [rudder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudder) on the vertical tail produces yaw, and [ailerons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aileron) (flaps on the wings that move in opposing directions) produce roll. On a spacecraft, the movements are usually produced by a [reaction control system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_control_system) consisting of small rocket thrusters used to apply asymmetrical thrust on the vehicle.

## Principal axes {#Principal_axes}

- [Vertical axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_control_surfaces#Vertical_axis) or yaw axis — an axis drawn from top to bottom and perpendicular to the other two axes.
- [Transverse axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_control_surfaces#Transverse_axis), lateral axis, or pitch axis — an axis running from the pilot's left to right in piloted aircraft, and parallel to the wings of a winged aircraft, parallel to the [buttock line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttock_line).
- [Longitudinal axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_control_surfaces#Longitudinal_axis), or roll axis — an axis drawn through the body of the vehicle from tail to nose in the normal direction of flight, or the direction the pilot faces, similar to a ship's [waterline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterline).

Normally, these axes are represented by the letters X, Y and Z in order to compare them with some reference frame, usually named x, y, z. Normally, this is made in such a way that the X is used for the longitudinal axis, but there are [other possibilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axes_conventions) to do it.

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Yaw (aviation)"}

### Vertical axis (yaw) {#Vertical_axis_(yaw)}

The **yaw axis** has its origin at the center of gravity and is directed towards the bottom of the aircraft, [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to the wings and to the fuselage reference line. Motion about this axis is called **yaw**. A positive yawing motion moves the nose of the aircraft to the right.[^o5] The [rudder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudder#Aircraft_rudders) is the primary control of yaw.[^o11]

The term *yaw* was originally applied in sailing, and referred to the motion of an unsteady ship rotating about its vertical axis. Its [etymology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etymology) is uncertain.

:::

::: {#o7 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Pitch (aviation)"}

### Lateral axis (pitch) {#Lateral_axis_(pitch)}

The **pitch axis** (also called **transverse** or **lateral axis**)[^o8] passes through an aircraft from wingtip to wingtip. Rotation about this axis is called **pitch**. Pitch changes the vertical direction that the aircraft's nose is pointing (a positive pitching motion raises the nose of the aircraft up and lowers the tail). The [elevators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevator_%28aeronautics%29) are the primary control surfaces for pitch.[^o11]

:::

::: {#o10 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Angle of roll"}

### Longitudinal axis (roll) {#Longitudinal_axis_(roll)}

The **roll axis** (or **longitudinal axis**[^o8]) has its origin at the center of gravity and is directed forward, parallel to the fuselage reference line. Motion about this axis is called **roll**. An angular displacement about this axis is called **bank**.[^o11] A positive rolling motion lifts the left wing and lowers the right wing. The pilot rolls by increasing the lift on one wing and decreasing it on the other. This changes the bank angle.[^o12] The [ailerons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aileron) are the primary control of roll. The rudder also has a secondary effect on roll.[^o13]

:::

## Relationship with other systems of axes {#Relationship_with_other_systems_of_axes}

*Image: MISB ST 0601.8 - Yaw, Pitch & Roll.png.* Yaw/heading, pitch and roll [angles](wikipedia://en/1196) and associated vertical (down), transverse and longitudinal axes

These axes are related to the [principal axes of inertia](wikipedia://en/157700#Principal_axes), but are not the same. They are geometrical symmetry axes, regardless of the mass distribution of the aircraft.

In aeronautical and aerospace engineering intrinsic rotations around these axes are often called [Euler angles](wikipedia://en/411492), but this conflicts with existing usage elsewhere. The calculus behind them is similar to the [Frenet–Serret formulas](wikipedia://en/666987). Performing a rotation in an intrinsic reference frame is equivalent to right-multiplying its characteristic matrix (the matrix that has the vectors of the reference frame as columns) by the matrix of the rotation.

## History {#History}

The first aircraft to demonstrate active control about all three axes was the [Wright brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wright_brothers)' [1902 glider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wright_Glider#1902_glider).[^o16]
