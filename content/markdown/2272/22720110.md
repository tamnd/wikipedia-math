# Taylor microscale

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Taylor microscale"}

In [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034), the **Taylor microscale**, which is sometimes called the **turbulence length scale**, is a [length scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Length_scale) used to characterize a [turbulent](wikipedia://en/154664) fluid flow.[^o2] This microscale is named after [Geoffrey Ingram Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Ingram_Taylor). The Taylor microscale is the intermediate length scale at which fluid [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754) significantly affects the dynamics of turbulent [eddies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eddy_%28fluid_dynamics%29) in the flow. This length scale is traditionally applied to turbulent flow which can be characterized by a [Kolmogorov](wikipedia://en/91161) spectrum of velocity fluctuations. In such a flow, length scales which are larger than the Taylor microscale are not strongly affected by viscosity. These larger length scales in the flow are generally referred to as the [inertial range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_range). Below the Taylor microscale the turbulent motions are subject to strong viscous forces and [kinetic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbulence_kinetic_energy) is [dissipated](wikipedia://en/164572) into heat. These shorter length scale motions are generally termed the [dissipation range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissipation_range).

:::

Calculation of the Taylor microscale is not entirely straightforward, requiring formation of certain flow correlation function(s),[^o3] then expanding in a [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) and using the first non-zero term to characterize an [osculating parabola](wikipedia://en/3432879). The Taylor microscale is proportional to $\text{Re}^{-1/2}$, while the [Kolmogorov microscale](wikipedia://en/2092749) is proportional to $\text{Re}^{-3/4}$, where $\text{Re}$ is the integral scale [Reynolds number](wikipedia://en/23868856). A turbulence Reynolds number calculated based on the Taylor microscale $\lambda$ is given by

$$\text{Re}_\lambda = \frac{\langle \mathbf{v'} \rangle_{rms} \lambda}{\nu},$$

where $\langle \mathbf{v'} \rangle_{rms} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \sqrt{ (v'_1)^2 + (v'_2)^2 + (v'_3)^2 }$ is the [root mean square](wikipedia://en/66819) of the velocity fluctuations. The Taylor microscale is given as

$$\lambda = \sqrt{15 \frac{\nu}{\epsilon}} \langle \mathbf{v'} \rangle_{rms},$$

where $\nu$ is the [kinematic viscosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinematic_viscosity), and $\epsilon$ is the rate of energy dissipation. A relation with [turbulence kinetic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbulence_kinetic_energy) $k$ can be derived as

$$\lambda \approx \sqrt{10 \nu \frac{k}{\epsilon}}.$$

The Taylor microscale gives a convenient estimation for the fluctuating strain rate field

$$\left( \frac{\partial \langle \mathbf{v} '\rangle_{rms}}{\partial \mathbf{x} }\right)^2 = \frac{\langle \mathbf{v}' \rangle_{rms}^2}{\lambda^2}.$$

## Other relations {#Other_relations}

The Taylor microscale falls in between the large-scale eddies and the small-scale eddies, which can be seen by calculating the ratios between $\lambda$ and the Kolmogorov microscale $\eta$. Given the length scale of the larger eddies $l \propto \frac{k^{3/2}}{\epsilon}$, and the turbulence Reynolds number $\text{Re}_{l}$ referred to these eddies, the following relations can be obtained:[^o5]

$$\frac{\lambda}{l} = \sqrt{10} \, \text{Re}_{l}^{-1/2}$$

$$\frac{\eta}{l} = \text{Re}_{l}^{-3/4}$$

$$\frac{\lambda}{\eta} = \sqrt{10} \, \text{Re}_{l}^{1/4}$$

$$\lambda = \sqrt{10} \, \eta^{2/3} l^{1/3}$$
