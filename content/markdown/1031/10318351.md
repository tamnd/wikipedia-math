# Span (category theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Span"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a **span**, **roof** or **correspondence** is a generalization of the notion of [relation](wikipedia://en/3931) between two [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) of a [category](wikipedia://en/53759). When the category has all [pullbacks](wikipedia://en/841689) (and satisfies a small number of other conditions), spans can be considered as [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) in a [category of fractions](wikipedia://en/611460).

:::

The notion of a span is due to [Nobuo Yoneda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobuo_Yoneda) (1954) and [Jean Bénabou](wikipedia://en/70081438) (1967).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

A span is a [diagram](wikipedia://en/7651625) of type $\Lambda = (-1 \leftarrow 0 \rightarrow +1),$ i.e., a diagram of the form $Y \leftarrow X \rightarrow Z$.

That is, let Λ be the category (-1 ← 0 → +1). Then a span in a category *C* is a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) *S* : Λ → *C*. This means that a span consists of three objects *X*, *Y* and *Z* of *C* and morphisms *f* : *X* → *Y* and *g* : *X* → *Z*: it is two maps with common *domain*.

The [colimit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colimit) of a span is a [pushout](wikipedia://en/582024).

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- If *R* is a relation between [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) *X* and *Y* (i.e. a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of *X* × *Y*), then *X* ← *R* → *Y* is a span, where the maps are the projection maps $X \times Y \overset{\pi_X}{\to} X$ and $X \times Y \overset{\pi_Y}{\to} Y$.
- Any object yields the trivial span *A* ← *A* → *A,* where the maps are the identity.
- More generally, let $\phi\colon A \to B$ be a morphism in some category. There is a trivial span *A* ← *A* → *B*, where the left map is the identity on *A,* and the right map is the given map *φ*.
- If *M* is a [model category](wikipedia://en/2895304), with *W* the set of [weak equivalences](wikipedia://en/34615176), then the spans of the form $X \leftarrow Y \rightarrow Z,$ where the left morphism is in *W,* can be considered a generalised morphism (i.e., where one "inverts the weak equivalences"). Note that this is not the usual point of view taken when dealing with model categories.

:::

::: {#o7 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Cospan"}

## Cospans {#Cospans}

A cospan *K* in a category **C** is a functor K : Λ<sup>op</sup> → **C**; equivalently, a *contravariant* functor from Λ to **C**. That is, a diagram of type $\Lambda^\text{op} = (-1 \rightarrow 0 \leftarrow +1),$ i.e., a diagram of the form $Y \rightarrow X \leftarrow Z$.

Thus it consists of three objects *X*, *Y* and *Z* of **C** and morphisms *f* : *Y* → *X* and *g* : *Z* → *X*: it is two maps with common *codomain.*

The [limit](wikipedia://en/39248) of a cospan is a [pullback](wikipedia://en/841689).

An example of a cospan is a [cobordism](wikipedia://en/391832) *W* between two [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) *M* and *N*, where the two maps are the inclusions into *W*. Note that while cobordisms are cospans, the category of cobordisms is not a "cospan category": it is not the category of all cospans in "the category of manifolds with inclusions on the boundary", but rather a [subcategory](wikipedia://en/310959) thereof, as the requirement that *M* and *N* form a partition of the boundary of *W* is a global constraint.

The category **nCob** of finite-dimensional cobordisms is a [dagger compact category](wikipedia://en/7155145). More generally, the category **Span**(*C*) of spans on any category *C* with finite limits is also dagger compact.
