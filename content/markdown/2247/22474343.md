# T-statistic

T-value redirects here. For the erosion value, see [Soil loss tolerance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_loss_tolerance).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="T-statistic"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the ***t*-statistic** is the ratio of the difference in a number’s estimated value from its assumed value to its [standard error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_error_%28statistics%29). It is used in [hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing) via [Student's *t*-test](wikipedia://en/536080). The *t*-statistic is used in a *t*-test to determine whether to support or reject the null hypothesis. It is very similar to the [z-score](wikipedia://en/221722) but with the difference that *t*-statistic is used when the sample size is small or the population standard deviation is unknown. For example, the *t*-statistic is used in estimating the [population mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_mean) from a [sampling distribution](wikipedia://en/520670) of [sample means](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_mean) if the population [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590) is unknown. It is also used along with [p-value](wikipedia://en/554994) when running hypothesis tests where the p-value tells us what the odds are of the results to have happened.

:::

## Definition and features {#Definition_and_features}

Let $\hat\beta$ be an [estimator](wikipedia://en/10043) of parameter *β* in some [statistical model](wikipedia://en/27576). Then a *t*-statistic for this parameter is any quantity of the form

$$t_{\hat{\beta}} = \frac{\hat\beta - \beta_0}{\operatorname{s.e.}(\hat\beta)},$$

where *β*<sub>0</sub> is a non-random, known constant, which may or may not match the actual unknown parameter value *β*, and $\operatorname{s.e.}(\hat\beta)$ is the [standard error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_error_%28statistics%29) of the estimator $\hat\beta$ for *β*.

By default, statistical packages report *t*-statistic with *β*<sub>0</sub> = 0 (these *t*-statistics are used to test the significance of corresponding regressor). However, when *t*-statistic is needed to test the hypothesis of the form *H*<sub>0</sub>: *β* = *β*<sub>0</sub>, then a non-zero *β*<sub>0</sub> may be used.

If $\hat\beta$ is an [ordinary least squares](wikipedia://en/1651906) estimator in the classical [linear regression model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_regression_model) (that is, with [normally distributed](wikipedia://en/21462) and [homoscedastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homoscedasticity) error terms), and if the true value of the parameter *β* is equal to *β*<sub>0</sub>, then the [sampling distribution](wikipedia://en/520670) of the *t*-statistic is the [Student's *t*-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375) with (*n* − *k*) degrees of freedom, where *n* is the number of observations, and *k* is the number of regressors (including the intercept).

In the majority of models, the estimator $\hat\beta$ is [consistent](wikipedia://en/1497569) for *β* and is distributed [asymptotically normally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_normality). If the true value of the parameter *β* is equal to *β*<sub>0</sub>, and the quantity $\operatorname{s.e.}(\hat\beta)$ correctly estimates the asymptotic variance of this estimator, then the *t*-statistic will asymptotically have the [standard normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal) distribution.

In some models the distribution of the *t*-statistic is different from the normal distribution, even asymptotically. For example, when a [time series](wikipedia://en/406624) with a [unit root](wikipedia://en/2398969) is regressed in the [augmented Dickey–Fuller test](wikipedia://en/3767048), the test *t*-statistic will asymptotically have one of the Dickey–Fuller distributions (depending on the test setting).

::: {#o4 .application cue=heading name="Use"}

## Use {#Use}

Most frequently, *t* statistics are used in [Student's *t*-tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student's_t_test), a form of [statistical hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing), and in the computation of certain [confidence intervals](wikipedia://en/280911).

The key property of the *t* statistic is that it is a [pivotal quantity](wikipedia://en/6081205) – while defined in terms of the sample mean, its sampling distribution does not depend on the population parameters, and thus it can be used regardless of what these may be.

### Prediction {#Prediction}

Given a normal distribution $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with unknown mean and variance, the *t*-statistic of a future observation $X_{n+1},$ after one has made *n* observations, is an [ancillary statistic](wikipedia://en/670398) – a pivotal quantity (does not depend on the values of *μ* and *σ*<sup>2</sup>) that is a statistic (computed from observations). This allows one to compute a frequentist [prediction interval](wikipedia://en/536062) (a predictive [confidence interval](wikipedia://en/280911)), via the following t-distribution:

$$\frac{X_{n+1} - \overline{X}_n}{s_n \sqrt{1 + n^{-1}}} \sim T^{n-1}.$$

Solving for $X_{n+1}$ yields the prediction distribution

$$\overline{X}_n + s_n \sqrt{1 + n^{-1}} \cdot T^{n-1},$$

from which one may compute predictive confidence intervals – given a probability *p*, one may compute intervals such that 100*p*% of the time, the next observation $X_{n+1}$ will fall in that interval.

:::

## History {#History}

The term "*t*-statistic" is abbreviated from "hypothesis test statistic".[^o7] In statistics, the t-distribution was first derived as a [posterior distribution](wikipedia://en/357672) in 1876 by [Helmert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Robert_Helmert)[^o8][^o9][^o10] and [Lüroth](wikipedia://en/33271640).[^o11][^o12][^o13] The t-distribution also appeared in a more general form as [Pearson distribution](wikipedia://en/1804023) [Type IV](wikipedia://en/1804023#The_Pearson_type_IV_distribution) in [Karl Pearson](wikipedia://en/164695)'s 1895 paper.[^o14] However, the T-Distribution, also known as [Student's T Distribution](wikipedia://en/105375) gets its name from [William Sealy Gosset](wikipedia://en/105651) who was first to publish the result in English in his 1908 paper titled "The Probable Error of a Mean" (in [Biometrika](wikipedia://en/1734178)) using his pseudonym "Student"[^o15][^o16] because his employer preferred their staff to use pen names when publishing scientific papers instead of their real name, so he used the name "Student" to hide his identity.[^o17] [Guinness Brewery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness_Brewery) in [Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin), [Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland), and was interested in the problems of small samples – for example, the chemical properties of barley where sample sizes might be as few as 3. Hence a second version of the etymology of the term Student is that Guinness did not want their competitors to know that they were using the t-test to determine the quality of raw material. Although it was William Gosset after whom the term "Student" is penned, it was actually through the work of [Ronald Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824) that the distribution became well known as "Student's distribution"[^o18][^o19] and "[Student's t-test](wikipedia://en/536080)"

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

- [*z*-score (standardization)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z-score): If the population parameters are known, then rather than computing the t-statistic, one can compute the z-score; analogously, rather than using a *t*-test, one uses a [*z*-test](wikipedia://en/332213). This is rare outside of [standardized testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standardized_testing_%28statistics%29).
- [Studentized residual](wikipedia://en/609197): In [regression analysis](wikipedia://en/826997), the standard errors of the estimators at different data points vary (compare the middle versus endpoints of a [simple linear regression](wikipedia://en/2593771)), and thus one must divide the different residuals by different estimates for the error, yielding what are called [studentized residuals](wikipedia://en/609197).
- Sample [standard score](wikipedia://en/221722): Replacing the population mean and standard deviation in a z-score by the sample mean and sample standard deviation gives: $\frac{x-\bar{X}}{s}$. This standardized observation is related to t-statistics because it uses an estimated scale parameter. It should not be confused with the one-sample t-statistics $\frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}$, which standardizes the sample mean by its estimated error.
