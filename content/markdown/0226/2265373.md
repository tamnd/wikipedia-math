# De Arte Combinatoria

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="De Arte Combinatoria"}

la

*Image: Fotothek\_df\_tg\_0005486\_Mathematik\_%5E\_Kombinatorik.jpg.* Frontispiece of the book printed in 1690

The Dissertatio **De Arte Combinatoria** (Dissertation on the Art of Combinations or Dissertation on the Combinatorial Art) is an early work by [Gottfried Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) published in 1666 in [Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig).[^n1][^n2] It is an extended version of his first [doctoral dissertation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctoral_dissertation), written before the author had seriously undertaken the study of mathematics.[^n3] The booklet was reissued without Leibniz' consent in 1690, which prompted him to publish a brief explanatory notice in the [Acta Eruditorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acta_Eruditorum).[^n4] During the following years he repeatedly expressed regrets about its being circulated as he considered it immature. Nevertheless it was a very original work and it provided the author the first glimpse of fame among the scholars of his time.

:::

The book was created as part of Leibniz's efforts to create the [Characteristica Universalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristica_Universalis), the [perfect language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_language) which would provide a direct representation of ideas along with a [calculus for the philosophical reasoning](wikipedia://en/876255).

## Summary {#Summary}

The main idea behind the text is that of an [alphabet of human thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabet_of_human_thought), which is attributed to [Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes). All concepts are nothing but [combinations](wikipedia://en/5308) of a relatively small number of simple concepts, just as words are combinations of letters. All [truths](wikipedia://en/29920) may be expressed as appropriate combinations of concepts, which can in turn be decomposed into simple ideas, rendering the analysis much easier. Therefore, this alphabet would provide a logic of invention, opposed to that of demonstration which was known so far. Since all sentences are composed of a [subject](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subject_%28grammar%29) and a [predicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_%28grammar%29), one might

- Find all the predicates which are appropriate to a given subject, or
- Find all the subjects which are convenient to a given predicate.

For this, Leibniz was inspired in the [Ars Magna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ars_Magna_%28Ramon_Llull%29) of [Ramon Llull](wikipedia://en/69677), although he criticized this author because of the arbitrariness of his categories indexing.

Leibniz discusses in this work some [combinatorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial) concepts. He had read [Clavius'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clavius) comments to [Sacrobosco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrobosco)'s [De sphaera mundi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_sphaera_mundi), and some other contemporary works. He introduced the term variationes ordinis for the [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027), combinationes for the combinations of two elements, *con3nationes* (shorthand for conternationes) for those of three elements, etc. His general term for combinations was complexions. He found the formula

$${n \choose r} = {n-1\choose r} + {n-1\choose r-1}$$

which he thought was original.

The first examples of use of his ars combinatoria are taken from law, the musical registry of an [organ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organ_%28music%29), and the [Aristotelian](wikipedia://en/308) theory of generation of elements from the four primary qualities. But philosophical applications are of greater importance. He cites the idea of [Thomas Hobbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes) that all reasoning is just a computation.

The most careful example is taken from geometry, from where we shall give some definitions. He introduces the Class I concepts, which are primitive.

**Class I**

1 point, 2 space, 3 included, \[...\] 9 parts, 10 total, \[...\] 14 number, 15 various \[...\]

Class II contains simple combinations.

**Class II.1**

Quantity is 14 των 9

Where των means "of the" (from τῶν). Thus, "Quantity" is the number of the parts. Class III contains the *con3nationes*:

**Class III.1**

Interval is 2.3.10

Thus, "Interval" is the space included in total. Of course, concepts deriving from former classes may also be defined.

**Class IV.1**

Line is 1/3 των 1

Where 1/3 means the first concept of class III. Thus, a "line" is the interval of (between) points.

Leibniz compares his system to the [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_language) and [Egyptian languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_language), although he did not really understand them at this point. For him, this is a first step towards the [Characteristica Universalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristica_Universalis), the [perfect language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_language) which would provide a direct representation of ideas along with a calculus for philosophical reasoning.

As a preface, the work begins with a [proof of the existence of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_of_the_existence_of_God), cast in geometrical form, and based on the [argument from motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument_from_motion).
