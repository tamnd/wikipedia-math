# Invariant measure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Invariant measure"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **invariant measure** is a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) that is preserved by some [function](wikipedia://en/185427). The function may be a [geometric transformation](wikipedia://en/43194879). For examples, [circular angle](wikipedia://en/1196) is invariant under rotation, [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) is invariant under [squeeze mapping](wikipedia://en/1140043), and a difference of [slopes](wikipedia://en/29368) is invariant under [shear mapping](wikipedia://en/799405).

:::

[Ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986) is the study of invariant measures in [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems). The [Krylov–Bogolyubov theorem](wikipedia://en/7635266) proves the existence of invariant measures under certain conditions on the function and space under consideration.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $(X, \Sigma)$ be a [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333) and let $f : X \to X$ be a [measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055) from $X$ to itself. A measure $\mu$ on $(X, \Sigma)$ is said to be **invariant under** $f$ if, for every measurable set $A$ in $\Sigma,$

$$\mu\left(f^{-1}(A)\right) = \mu(A).$$

In terms of the [pushforward measure](wikipedia://en/9412979), this states that $f_*(\mu) = \mu.$

The collection of measures (usually [probability measures](wikipedia://en/45437)) on $X$ that are invariant under $f$ is sometimes denoted $M_f(X).$ The collection of [ergodic measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic_%28adjective%29), $E_f(X),$ is a subset of $M_f(X).$ Moreover, any [convex combination](wikipedia://en/794534) of two invariant measures is also invariant, so $M_f(X)$ is a [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292); $E_f(X)$ consists precisely of the extreme points of $M_f(X).$

In the case of a [dynamical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_system_%28definition%29) $(X, T, \varphi),$ where $(X, \Sigma)$ is a measurable space as before, $T$ is a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) and $\varphi : T \times X \to X$ is the flow map, a measure $\mu$ on $(X, \Sigma)$ is said to be an **invariant measure** if it is an invariant measure for each map $\varphi_t : X \to X.$ Explicitly, $\mu$ is invariant [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922)

$$\mu\left(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(A)\right) = \mu(A) \qquad \text{ for all } t \in T, A \in \Sigma.$$

Put another way, $\mu$ is an invariant measure for a sequence of [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) $\left(Z_t\right)_{t \geq 0}$ (perhaps a [Markov chain](wikipedia://en/60876) or the solution to a [stochastic differential equation](wikipedia://en/1361454)) if, whenever the initial condition $Z_0$ is distributed according to $\mu,$ so is $Z_t$ for any later time $t.$

When the dynamical system can be described by a [transfer operator](wikipedia://en/1346096), then the invariant measure is an eigenvector of the operator, corresponding to an eigenvalue of $1,$ this being the largest eigenvalue as given by the [Frobenius–Perron theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius–Perron_theorem).

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Hyperbolic sector squeeze mapping.svg.* [Squeeze mapping](wikipedia://en/1140043) leaves [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) invariant as it moves a [hyperbolic sector](wikipedia://en/1139926) (purple) to one of the same area. Blue and green rectangles also keep the same area

- Consider the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) $\mathbb{R}$ with its usual [Borel σ-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_sigma_algebra); fix $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and consider the translation map $T_a : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ given by: $$T_a(x) = x + a.$$ Then one-dimensional [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) $\lambda$ is an invariant measure for $T_a.$
- More generally, on $n$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$ with its usual Borel σ-algebra, $n$-dimensional Lebesgue measure $\lambda^n$ is an invariant measure for any [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) of Euclidean space, that is, a map $T : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ that can be written as $$T(x) = A x + b$$ for some $n \times n$ [orthogonal matrix](wikipedia://en/105620) $A \in O(n)$ and a vector $b \in \mathbb{R}^n.$
- The invariant measure in the first example is unique up to trivial renormalization with a constant factor. This does not have to be necessarily the case: Consider a set consisting of just two points $\mathbf{S} = \{A,B\}$ and the identity map $T = \operatorname{Id}$ which leaves each point fixed. Then any probability measure $\mu : \mathbf{S} \to \mathbb{R}$ is invariant. Note that $\mathbf{S}$ trivially has a decomposition into $T$-invariant components $\{A\}$ and $\{B\}.$
- [Area](wikipedia://en/1209) measure in the Euclidean plane is invariant under the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) $\operatorname{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$ of the $2 \times 2$ [real matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_matrix) of [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) $1.$
- Every [locally compact group](wikipedia://en/1651204) has a [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645) that is invariant under the group action ([translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_%28group_theory%29)).
