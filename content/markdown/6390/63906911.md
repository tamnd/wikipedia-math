# Bottema's theorem

*Image: Bottema's\_theorem.png.* Bottema's theorem construction; changing the location of vertex $C$ changes the locations of vertices $E$ and $F$ but does not change the location of their midpoint $M$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bottema's theorem"}

**Bottema's theorem** is a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) in [plane geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_geometry) that is linked to the Dutch mathematician [Oene Bottema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oene_Bottema) ([Groningen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groningen), 1901–1992).[^o2] As Bottema points out himself, the theorem was known before he published the simple proof.[^o3] The theorem is, for example, stated in the popular science book "[One, Two, Three... Infinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Two_Three..._Infinity)" by theoretical physicist [George Gamow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Gamow).

:::

The theorem can be stated as follows: in any given [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) $ABC$, construct squares on any two adjacent sides, for example $AC$ and $BC$. The [midpoint](wikipedia://en/437979) of the [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) that connects the [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) of the squares opposite the common vertex, *$C$*, of the two sides of the triangle is independent of the location of $C$.[^o4][^o5]

The theorem is true when the squares are constructed in one of the following ways:

- Looking at the figure, starting from the lower left vertex, $A$, follow the triangle vertices clockwise and construct the squares to the left of the sides of the triangle.
- Follow the triangle in the same way and construct the squares to the right of the sides of the triangle.

If $S$ is the [projection](wikipedia://en/1508507) of $M$ onto $AB$, Then $AS=BS=MS$.

If the squares are replaced by regular polygons of the same type, then a generalized Bottema theorem is obtained: [^o6]

In any given triangle $ABC$ construct two regular polygons on two sides $AC$ and $BC$. Take the points $D_1$ and $D_2$ on the circumcircles of the polygons, which are diametrically opposed of the common vertex $C$. Then, the midpoint of the line segment $D_1D_2$ is independent of the location of $C$.
