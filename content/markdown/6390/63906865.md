# Jan Saxl

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Jan Saxl"}

**Jan Saxl** (5 June 1948 – 2 May 2020) was a Czech-British [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902), and a professor at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge). He was known for his work in [finite group theory](wikipedia://en/41890#Finite_group_theory), particularly on consequences of the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445).

:::

## Education and career {#Education_and_career}

Saxl was born in [Brno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brno), in what was at the time [Czechoslovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechoslovakia). He came to the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom) in 1968, during the [Prague Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prague_Spring).[^o4] After undergraduate studies at the [University of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bristol),[^o4] he completed his DPhil in 1973 at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford) under the direction of [Peter M. Neumann](wikipedia://en/7151472), with the title of *Multiply Transitive Permutation Groups*.

Saxl held postdoctoral positions at Oxford and the [University of Illinois at Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Chicago), and a lecturer position at the [University of Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Glasgow). He moved to the University of Cambridge in 1976, and spent the rest of his career there.[^o4][^o6] He was elected as a fellow of [Gonville and Caius College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonville_and_Caius_College) in 1986,[^o4] and he retired in 2015.[^o6]

Saxl published around 100 papers, and according to MathSciNet, these have been cited over 1900 times.[^o7] He is noted for his work in finite group theory, particularly on [permutation groups](wikipedia://en/24634), and often coauthored with [Robert Guralnick](wikipedia://en/57893712), [Martin Liebeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Liebeck), and [Cheryl Praeger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheryl_Praeger). Some notable and highly-cited[^o7] examples of this work are as follows. Liebeck, Saxl and Praeger gave a relatively simple and self-contained [proof](wikipedia://en/82285) of the [O'Nan–Scott theorem](wikipedia://en/40086598).[^o8] It had long been known that every [maximal subgroup](wikipedia://en/1472649) of a [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) or [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) was intransitive, imprimitive, or primitive, and the same authors in 1988 gave a partial description of which primitive subgroups could occur.[^o9][^o10]

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Saxl was married to Cambridge mathematician [Ruth M. Williams](wikipedia://en/64784708) and they had one daughter, Miriam.[^o4]

## Death {#Death}

Saxl died on 2 May 2020, after a long period of poor health.[^o4]

## Awards and honors {#Awards_and_honors}

A three-day conference in the joint honor of Saxl and Martin Liebeck was held at the University of Cambridge in July 2015.[^o14]
