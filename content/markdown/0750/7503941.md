# Jung's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Jung's theorem"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), **Jung's theorem** is an [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489) between the [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) of a set of points in any [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) and the [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) of the [minimum enclosing ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) of that set. It is named after [Heinrich Jung](wikipedia://en/37050659), who first studied this inequality in 1901. Algorithms also exist to solve the [smallest-circle problem](wikipedia://en/14355284) explicitly.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Consider a [compact set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set)

$$K \subset \mathbb{R}^n$$

and let

$$d = \max_{p,q\,\in\, K} \| p - q \|_2$$

be the [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) of *K*, that is, the largest [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/39378) between any two of its points. Jung's theorem states that there exists a [closed ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_ball) with radius

$$r \leq d \sqrt{\frac{n}{2(n+1)}}$$

that contains *K*. The boundary case of equality is attained by the regular *n*-[simplex](wikipedia://en/39781).

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Jung's theorem in the plane"}

## Jung's theorem in the plane {#Jung's_theorem_in_the_plane}

The most common case of Jung's theorem is in the [plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), that is, when *n* = 2. In this case the theorem states that there exists a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) enclosing all points whose radius satisfies

$$r \leq \frac{d}{\sqrt{3}},$$

and this bound is as tight as possible since when *K* is an [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285) (or its three vertices) one has $r = \frac{d}{\sqrt{3}}.$

:::

## General metric spaces {#General_metric_spaces}

For any [bounded set](wikipedia://en/48258#Definition_in_a_metric_space) $S$ in any [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018), $d/2\le r\le d$. The first inequality is implied by the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941) for the center of the ball and the two diametral points, and the second inequality follows since a ball of radius $d$ centered at any point of $S$ will contain all of $S$. Both these inequalities are tight:

- In a *uniform metric space*, that is, a space in which all distances are equal, $r=d$.
- At the other end of the spectrum, in an [injective metric space](wikipedia://en/7840768) such as the [Manhattan distance](wikipedia://en/408354) in the plane, $r=d/2$: any two closed balls of radius $d/2$ centered at points of $S$ have a non-[empty](wikipedia://en/9566) [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429), therefore all such balls have a common intersection, and a radius $d/2$ ball centered at a point of this intersection contains all of $S$.

Versions of Jung's theorem for various [non-Euclidean geometries](wikipedia://en/58610) are also known (see e.g. Dekster 1995, 1997).
