# M6 (cipher)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="M6"}

In [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), **M6** is a [block cipher](wikipedia://en/4594) proposed by [Hitachi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitachi,_Ltd.) in 1997 for use in the IEEE 1394 [FireWire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FireWire) standard. The design allows some freedom in choosing a few of the cipher's operations, so M6 is considered a family of ciphers. Due to export controls, M6 has not been fully published; nevertheless, a partial description of the algorithm based on a draft standard is given by Kelsey, et al. in their cryptanalysis of this family of ciphers.[^o3]

:::

The algorithm operates on blocks of 64 bits using a 10-round [Feistel network](wikipedia://en/352709) structure. The [key size](wikipedia://en/5749) is 40 bits by default, but can be up to 64 bits. The [key schedule](wikipedia://en/609603) is very simple, producing two 32-bit subkeys: the high 32 bits of the [key](wikipedia://en/53039), and the sum mod 2<sup>32</sup> of this and the low 32 bits.

Because its round function is based on rotation and addition, M6 was one of the first ciphers attacked by [mod n cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/709367).[^o3] Mod 5, about 100 known plaintexts suffice to [distinguish](wikipedia://en/11563117) the output from a [pseudorandom permutation](wikipedia://en/4681949). Mod 257, information about the secret key itself is revealed. One known plaintext reduces the complexity of a [brute force attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brute_force_attack) to about 2<sup>35</sup> trial encryptions; "a few dozen" known plaintexts lowers this number to about 2<sup>31</sup>. Due to its simple key schedule, M6 is also vulnerable to a [slide attack](wikipedia://en/1821327), which requires more known plaintext but less computation.
