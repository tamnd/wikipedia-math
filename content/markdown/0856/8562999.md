# Rippling

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rippling"}

In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), more particularly in [automated theorem proving](wikipedia://en/2546), **rippling**[^o2] is a group of meta-level [heuristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristics), developed primarily in the Mathematical Reasoning Group in the School of Informatics at the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh), and most commonly used to guide [inductive proofs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Induction) in [automated theorem proving systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automated_theorem_prover). Rippling may be viewed as a restricted form of [rewrite system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rewrite_system), where special object level annotations are used to ensure fertilization upon the completion of rewriting, with a measure decreasing requirement ensuring termination for any set of rewrite rules and expression.

:::

## History {#History}

Raymond Aubin was the first person to use the term "rippling out" whilst working on his 1976 PhD thesis[^o4] at the University of Edinburgh. He recognised a common pattern of movement during the rewriting stage of inductive proofs. [Alan Bundy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Bundy) later turned this concept on its head by defining rippling to be this pattern of movement, rather than a side effect.

Since then, "rippling sideways", "rippling in" and "rippling past" were coined, so the term was generalised to rippling. Rippling continues to be developed at Edinburgh, and elsewhere, as of 2007.

Rippling has been applied to many problems traditionally viewed as being hard in the inductive theorem proving community, including [Bledsoe](wikipedia://en/3777527)'s limit theorems and a proof of the Gordon microprocessor, a miniature computer developed by [Michael J. C. Gordon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_J._C._Gordon) and his team at Cambridge.

## Overview {#Overview}

Very often, when attempting to prove a proposition, we are given a source expression and a target expression, which differ only by the inclusion of a few extra syntactic elements.

This is especially true in inductive [proofs](wikipedia://en/82285), where the given expression is taken to be the [inductive hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_hypothesis), and the target expression the inductive conclusion. Usually, the differences between the hypothesis and conclusion are only minor, perhaps the inclusion of a successor function (e.g., +1) around the induction variable.

At the start of rippling the differences between the two expressions, known as wave-fronts in rippling parlance, are identified. Typically these differences prevent the completion of the proof and need to be "moved away". The target expression is annotated to distinguish the wavefronts (differences) and skeleton (common structure) between the two expressions. Special rules, called wave rules, can then be used in a [terminating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Termination_proof) fashion to manipulate the target expression until the source expression can be used to complete the proof.
