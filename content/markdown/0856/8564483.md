# Bogdanov–Takens bifurcation

*Image: Bogdanov takens bifurcation.svg.* Bifurcation diagrams with parameters *β*<sub>1</sub>, *β*<sub>2</sub> = (from top-left to bottom-right): (−1,1), (1/4,−1), (1,0), (0,0), (−6/25,−1), (0,1).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bogdanov–Takens bifurcation"}

In [bifurcation theory](wikipedia://en/2326042), a field within [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Bogdanov–Takens bifurcation** is a well-studied example of a bifurcation with [co-dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-dimension) two, meaning that two parameters must be varied for the bifurcation to occur. It is named after [Rifkat Bogdanov](wikipedia://en/13493122) and [Floris Takens](wikipedia://en/1790146), who independently and simultaneously described this bifurcation.

:::

A system *y**\=*****f*(*y*) undergoes a Bogdanov–Takens bifurcation if it has a fixed point and the linearization of*f*around that point has a double [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) at zero (assuming that some technical nondegeneracy conditions are satisfied).***

Three codimension-one bifurcations occur nearby: a [saddle-node bifurcation](wikipedia://en/3201787), an [Andronov–Hopf bifurcation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andronov–Hopf_bifurcation) and a [homoclinic bifurcation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homoclinic_bifurcation). All associated bifurcation curves meet at the Bogdanov–Takens bifurcation.

The [normal form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_form_%28bifurcation_theory%29) of the Bogdanov–Takens bifurcation is

$$\begin{aligned} y_1' &= y_2, \\ y_2' &= \beta_1 + \beta_2 y_1 + y_1^2 \pm y_1 y_2. \end{aligned}$$

There exist two codimension-three degenerate Takens–Bogdanov bifurcations, also known as [Dumortier–Roussarie–Sotomayor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumortier–Roussarie–Sotomayor) bifurcations.
