# Card catalog (cryptology)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Card catalog"}

The **card catalog**, or "catalog of characteristics," in [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), was a system designed by [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland) [Cipher Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biuro_Szyfrów) mathematician-cryptologist [Marian Rejewski](wikipedia://en/4349420), and first completed about 1935 or 1936, to facilitate [decrypting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decryption) German [Enigma](wikipedia://en/9256) [ciphers](wikipedia://en/5244).[^o2]

:::

## History {#History}

The Polish Cipher Bureau used the theory of [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) to start breaking the Enigma cipher in late 1932. The Bureau recognized that the Enigma machine's doubled-key (see [Grill (cryptology)](wikipedia://en/3155420)) permutations formed cycles, and those cycles could be used to break the cipher. With German cipher keys provided by a French spy, the Bureau was able to reverse engineer the Enigma and start reading German messages. At the time, the Germans were using only 6 *steckers*, and the Polish [grill method](wikipedia://en/3155420) was feasible. On 1 August 1936, the Germans started using 8 *steckers*, and that change made the grill method less feasible. The Bureau needed an improved method to break the German cipher.

Although the *steckers* would change which letters were in a doubled-key's cycle, the *steckers* would not change the number of cycles or the length of those cycles. Steckers could be ignored. Ignoring the mid-key turnovers, the Enigma machine had only $26^{3}$ distinct settings of the three rotors, and the three rotors could only be arranged in the machine $3!=6$ ways. That meant there were only likely doubled-key permutations. The Bureau set about determining and cataloging the characteristic of each of those likely permutations. Each letter of the key could be one of [partition number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_number) 13 = 101 possible values, and the 3 letters of the key meant there were possible keys. On average, a key would find one setting of the rotors, but it might find several possible settings.

The Polish cryptanalyst could then collect enough traffic to determine all the cycles in a daily key. That usually took about 60 messages. The result might be:

$$\begin{aligned} AD &= \texttt{(pjxroquctwzsy)(kvgledmanhfib)} \\ BE &= \texttt{(kxtcoigweh)(zvfbsylrnp)(ujd)(mqa)} \\ CF &= \texttt{(yvxqtdhpim)(skgrjbcolw)(un)(fa)(e)(z)} \\ \end{aligned}$$

He would use the lengths of the cycles (13<sup>2</sup>;10<sup>2</sup>-3<sup>2</sup>;10<sup>2</sup>-2<sup>2</sup>-1<sup>2</sup>) to look up the wheel order (II I III) and starting rotor positions in the card catalog. He would then use an Enigma to compute the un-*steckered* cycles:

$$\begin{aligned} AD &= \texttt{(sjxroqtcuzwpy)(kngledamvhifb)} \\ BE &= \texttt{(kxucofgzeh)(wnibpylrvs)(tjd)(aqm)} \\ CF &= \texttt{(ynxqudhsfa)(pkgrjbcolz)(tv)(im)(e)(w)} \\ \end{aligned}$$

By comparing the *steckered* cycles from the German traffic and the un-*steckered* cycles, the cryptanalyst can determine the *steckers*. In the example, the *CF* permutation has `(e)(z)` and `(e)(w)`. That requires that `e` is un*stecker*ed and a W-Z *stecker*. The cycles can then be aligned on `e` and W-Z to determine other *steckered* and un-*steckered* letters.

`(pjxroquctwzsy)(kvgledmanhfib)/(kxtcoigweh)...`

`(sjxroqtcuzwpy)(kngledamvhifb)/(kxucofgzeh)...`

`(!_____!_!**!_)(_!__=_!!!_!!_)/(__!__!_*=_)...`

Where `=` is a known un-*steckered* letter, `*` is a known *steckered* letter, and `!` is a newly discovered *stecker*. Repetition produces the *steckers* A-M, F-I, N-V, P-S, T-U, W-Z.

Preparation of the card catalog, using the [cyclometer](wikipedia://en/2774229) that Rejewski had invented about 1934 or 1935, was a laborious task that took over a year's time. But once the catalog was complete, obtaining Enigma daily keys was a matter of some fifteen minutes.[^o4][^o5]

When the Germans changed the Enigma machine's "[reflector](wikipedia://en/3153312)," or "reversing drum," on 1 November 1937, the Cipher Bureau was forced to start over again with a new card catalog: "a task," writes Rejewski, "which consumed, on account of our greater experience, probably somewhat less than a year's time."[^o4]

On 15 September 1938 the Germans completely changed the procedure for enciphering message keys, rendering the card-catalog method useless. This spurred the invention of Rejewski's [cryptologic bomb](wikipedia://en/716701) and [Henryk Zygalski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henryk_Zygalski)'s "[perforated sheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perforated_sheets)."[^o6]
