# Geodesic polyhedron

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Geodesic polyhedron"}

A **geodesic polyhedron** is a convex [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) made from [triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangle_%28geometry%29) which approximates a sphere. They usually have [icosahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2877844), such that they have 6 triangles at a [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417), except 12 vertices which have 5 triangles. They are the [dual](wikipedia://en/8815) of corresponding [Goldberg polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldberg_polyhedra), of which all but the smallest one (which is a [regular dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/7149361)) have mostly hexagonal faces. The [Goldberg–Coxeter construction](wikipedia://en/56092947) is an expansion of the concepts underlying geodesic polyhedra.

:::

Geodesic polyhedra are a good approximation to a sphere for many purposes, and appear in many different contexts. The most well-known may be the *[geodesic domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_dome)*, hemispherical architectural structures designed by [Buckminster Fuller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckminster_Fuller), which geodesic polyhedra are named after. [Geodesic grids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_grid) used in [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608) also have the geometry of geodesic polyhedra. The [capsids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capsids) of some [viruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus) have the shape of geodesic polyhedra,[^o2][^o3] and some [pollen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollen) grains are based on geodesic polyhedra.[^o4] [Fullerene](wikipedia://en/10628) molecules have the shape of [Goldberg polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldberg_polyhedra). Geodesic polyhedra are available as [geometric primitives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_primitive) in the [Blender 3D modeling software package](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blender_%28software%29), which calls them *icospheres*: they are an alternative to the [UV sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UV_sphere), having a more regular distribution.[^o5][^o6]

::: {#o8 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

Geodesic polyhedra are constructed by subdividing faces of simpler polyhedra, and then projecting the new vertices onto the surface of a sphere. A geodesic polyhedron has straight edges and flat faces that approximate a [spherical polyhedron](wikipedia://en/10393861) constructed from [geodesic triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_triangle).

:::

::: {#o10 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

## Notation {#Notation}

In [Magnus Wenninger](wikipedia://en/8864685)'s *Spherical Models*, polyhedra are given **geodesic notation** in the form $\{3,q+\}_{b,c}$, where $\{3,q\}$ is the [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) for the regular polyhedron with triangular faces, and $q$-[valence](wikipedia://en/1374448) vertices. The **\+** symbol indicates the valence of the vertices being increased. $b$ and $c$ represent a subdivision description, with $1,0$ representing the base form. There are 3 symmetry classes of forms: $\{3,3+\}_{1,0}$ for a [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606), $\{3,4+\}_{1,0}$ for an [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635), and $\{3,5+\}_{1,0}$ for an [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084).

The dual notation for [Goldberg polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldberg_polyhedra) is $\{q+,3\}_{b,c}$, with valence-3 vertices, with $q$-gonal and hexagonal faces. There are 3 symmetry classes of forms: $\{3+,3\}_{1,0}$ for a [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606), $\{4+,3\}_{1,0}$ for a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285), and $\{5+,3\}_{1,0}$ for a [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407).

Values for $b$ and $c$ are divided into three classes:

1. **Class I** ($b = 0$ or $c = 0$): $\{3,q+\}_{b,0}$ or $\{3,q+\}_{0,b}$ represent a simple division with original edges being divided into $b$ sub-edges.
1. **Class II** ($b = c$): $\{3,q+\}_{b,b}$ are easier to see from the [dual polyhedron](wikipedia://en/8815) $\{q,3\}$ with $q$-gonal faces first divided into triangles with a central point, and then all edges are divided into $b$ sub-edges.
1. **Class III**: $\{3,q+\}_{b,c}$ have nonzero unequal values for $b$ and $c$, and exist in chiral pairs. For $b > c$ we can define it as a right-handed form, and $c > b$ is a left-handed form.

Subdivisions in class III here do not line up simply with the original edges. The subgrids can be extracted by looking at a [triangular tiling](wikipedia://en/2863858), positioning a large triangle on top of grid vertices and walking paths from one vertex $b$ steps in one direction, and a turn, either clockwise or counterclockwise, and then another $c$ steps to the next primary vertex.

For example, the [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084) is $\{3,5+\}_{1,0}$, and [pentakis dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/723196), $\{3,5+\}_{1,1}$ is seen as a [regular dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/7149361) with pentagonal faces divided into 5 triangles.

*Image: Geodesic\_principal\_polyhedral\_triangles\_frequency8.png.* PPTs with frequency 8

The primary face of the subdivision is called a **principal polyhedral triangle** (PPT) or the **breakdown structure**. Calculating a single PPT allows the entire figure to be created.

The **frequency** of a geodesic polyhedron is defined by the sum of $\nu = b + c$. A **harmonic** is a subfrequency and can be any whole divisor of $\nu$. Class II always have a harmonic of 2, since $\nu = 2b$.

The **triangulation number** is $T = b^{2} + bc + c^{2}$. This number times the number of original faces expresses how many triangles the new polyhedron will have.

## Elements {#Elements}

The number of elements are specified by the triangulation number $T = b^{2} + bc + c^{2}$. Two different geodesic polyhedra may have the same number of elements, for instance, $\{3,5+\}_{5,3}$ and $\{3,5+\}_{7,0}$ both have $T = 49$.

## Relation to Goldberg polyhedra {#Relation_to_Goldberg_polyhedra}

Geodesic polyhedra are the duals of [Goldberg polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldberg_polyhedra). Goldberg polyhedra are also related in that applying a [kis operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conway_kis_operator) (dividing faces into triangles with a center point) creates new geodesic polyhedra, and [truncating](wikipedia://en/4224429) vertices of a geodesic polyhedron creates a new Goldberg polyhedron. For example, Goldberg $G(2,1)$ [kised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conway_kis_operator), becomes $\{3,5+\}_{4,1}$, and truncating that becomes $G(6,3)$. And similarly $\{3,5+\}_{2,1}$ truncated becomes $G(4,1)$, and that [kised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conway_kis_operator) becomes $\{3,5+\}_{6,3}$.

[Magnus Wenninger](wikipedia://en/8864685)'s book *Spherical Models* explores these subdivisions in building [polyhedron models](wikipedia://en/2488073). After explaining the construction of these models, he explained his usage of triangular grids to mark out patterns, with triangles colored or excluded in the models.[^n1]
