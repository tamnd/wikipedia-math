# Quentin Meillassoux

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Quentin Meillassoux"}

**Quentin Meillassoux** (;[^o4] ; born 26 October 1967)[^o5] is a French philosopher. He teaches at the [Université Paris 1 Panthéon-Sorbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheon-Sorbonne_University).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Quentin Meillassoux is the son of the [anthropologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropologist) [Claude Meillassoux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Meillassoux). He is a former student of the philosophers [Bernard Bourgeois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Bourgeois) and [Alain Badiou](wikipedia://en/713688). He is married to the [novelist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novelist) and philosopher [Gwenaëlle Aubry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwenaëlle_Aubry).[^o7]

## Philosophical work {#Philosophical_work}

Meillassoux's first book is *After Finitude* (*Après la finitude*, 2006). Alain Badiou, Meillassoux's former teacher, wrote the foreword*.*[^o9] Badiou describes the work as introducing a new possibility for philosophy which is different from [Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant)'s three alternatives of [criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criticism), [skepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skepticism), and [dogmatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogmatism).[^o10] The book was translated into English by [Ray Brassier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Brassier). Meillassoux is associated with the [speculative realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speculative_realism) movement.

In this book, Meillassoux argues that [post-Kantian philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-Kantian_philosophy) is dominated by what he calls "[correlationism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlationism)", the theory that humans cannot exist without the world nor the world without humans.[^o11] In Meillassoux's view, this theory allows philosophy to avoid the problem of how to describe the world as it really is independent of human knowledge. He terms this reality independent of human knowledge as the "ancestral" realm.[^o12] Following the commitment to mathematics of his mentor Alain Badiou, Meillassoux claims that mathematics describes the primary qualities of things as opposed to their [secondary qualities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary/secondary_quality_distinction) shown by [perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perception).

Meillassoux argues that in place of the agnostic scepticism about the reality of [cause and effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cause_and_effect), there should be a radical certainty that there is no [causality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causality) at all. Following the rejection of causality, Meillassoux says that it is absolutely necessary that the laws of nature be contingent. The world is a kind of hyper-chaos in which the [principle of sufficient reason](wikipedia://en/1137736) is not necessary although Meillassoux says that the [principle of non-contradiction](wikipedia://en/17636) is necessary.

For these reasons, Meillassoux rejects Kant's [Copernican Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernican_Revolution_%28metaphor%29) in philosophy. Since Kant makes the world dependent on the conditions by which humans observe it, Meillassoux accuses Kant of a "Ptolemaic Counter-Revolution." Meillassoux clarified and revised some of the views published in *After Finitude* during his lectures at the [Free University of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_University_of_Berlin) in 2012.[^o13]

Several of Meillassoux's articles have appeared in English via the British philosophical journal *[Collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collapse_%28journal%29)*, helping to spark interest in his work in the Anglophone world.

His unpublished dissertation *L'inexistence divine* (1997) is noted in *After Finitude* to be "forthcoming" in book form;[^o14] as of 2021, it had not yet been published. In *[Parrhesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parrhesia_%28journal%29)*, in 2016, an excerpt from Meillassoux's dissertation was translated by Nathan Brown, who noted in his introduction that "what is striking about the document... is the marked difference of its rhetorical strategies, its order of reasons, and its philosophical style" from *After Finitude*, counter to the general view that the latter merely constituted "a partial précis" of *L'inexistence divine*; he notes further that the dissertation presents a "very different articulation of the Principle of Factiality" from that in *After Finitude*.[^o15] While Nathan Brown's translation uses the French text of the 1997 dissertation, in 2011 Graham Harman used a 2003 revision to offer a partial translation of Meillassoux's ongoing work of expanding the dissertation into a book.

In September 2011, Meillassoux's book on [Stéphane Mallarmé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stéphane_Mallarmé) was published in France under the title *Le nombre et la sirène. Un déchiffrage du coup de dés de Mallarmé*.[^o16] In this second book, he offers a detailed reading of Mallarmé's famous poem "[Un coup de dés jamais n'abolira le hasard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Un_coup_de_dés_jamais_n'abolira_le_hasard)" ("A Throw of the Dice Will Never Abolish Chance"), in which he finds a numerical code at work in the text.[^o17]
