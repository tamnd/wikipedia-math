# Prony's method

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Prony's method"}

*Image: Prony2.jpg.* Prony analysis of a time-domain signal

**Prony analysis** (**Prony's method**) was developed by [Gaspard Riche de Prony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaspard_Riche_de_Prony) in 1795. However, practical use of the method awaited the digital computer.[^o2] Similar to the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247), Prony's method extracts valuable information from a uniformly sampled signal and builds a series of damped complex exponentials or [damped sinusoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damped_sinusoid). This allows the estimation of frequency, amplitude, phase and damping components of a signal.

:::

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="The method"}

## The method {#The_method}

Let $f(t)$ be a signal consisting of $N$ evenly spaced samples. Prony's method fits a function

$$\hat{f}(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} A_i e^{\sigma_i t} \cos(\omega_i t + \phi_i)$$

to the observed $f(t)$. After some manipulation utilizing [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613), the following result is obtained, which allows more direct computation of terms:

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{f}(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tfrac{1}{2} A_i \left( e^{j\phi_i}e^{\lambda^+_i t} + e^{-j\phi_i}e^{\lambda^-_i t}\right), \end{aligned}$$

where

$\lambda^{\pm}_i = \sigma_i \pm j \omega_i$ are the eigenvalues of the system,

$\sigma_i = -\omega_{0,i} \xi_i$ are the damping components,

$\omega_i = \omega_{0,i} \sqrt{1-\xi_i^2}$ are the angular-frequency components,

$\phi_i$ are the phase components,

$A_i$ are the amplitude components of the series,

$j$ is the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358) ($j^2 = -1$).

:::

::: {#o6 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Representations"}

## Representations {#Representations}

Prony's method is essentially a decomposition of a signal with $M$ complex exponentials via the following process:

Regularly sample $\hat{f}(t)$ so that the $n$-th of $N$ samples may be written as

$$F_n = \hat{f}(\Delta_t n) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \mathrm{B}_m e^{\lambda_m \Delta_t n}, \quad n=0,\dots,N-1.$$

If $\hat{f}(t)$ happens to consist of damped sinusoids, then there will be pairs of complex exponentials such that

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{B}_i^{(1)} &= \tfrac{1}{2} A_i e^{ \phi_i j}, \\ \mathrm{B}_i^{(2)} &= \tfrac{1}{2} A_i e^{-\phi_i j}, \\ \lambda_i^{(1)} &= \sigma_i + j \omega_i, \\ \lambda_i^{(2)} &= \sigma_i - j \omega_i, \end{aligned}$$

where

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{B}_i^{(1)} e^{\lambda_i^{(1)} t} + \mathrm{B}_i^{(2)} e^{\lambda_i^{(2)} t} &= \tfrac{1}{2} A_i e^{\phi_i j} e^{(\sigma_i + j \omega_i) t} + \tfrac{1}{2}A_i e^{-\phi_i j} e^{(\sigma_i - j \omega_i) t} \\ &= A_i e^{\sigma_i t} \cos(\omega_i t + \phi_i). \end{aligned}$$

Because the summation of complex exponentials is the homogeneous solution to a linear [difference equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_equation), the following difference equation will exist:

$$\hat{f}(\Delta_t n) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \hat{f}[\Delta_t (n - m)] P_m, \quad n=M,\dots,N-1.$$

The key to Prony's Method is that the coefficients in the difference equation are related to the following polynomial:

$$z^M - P_1 z^{M-1} - \dots - P_M = \prod_{m=1}^{M} \left(z - e^{\lambda_m}\right).$$

These facts lead to the following three steps within Prony's method:

\1) Construct and solve the matrix equation for the $P_m$ values:

$$\begin{bmatrix} F_{M} \\ \vdots \\ F_{N-1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} F_{M-1} & \dots & F_{0} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ F_{N-2} & \dots & F_{N-M-1} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} P_1 \\ \vdots \\ P_M \end{bmatrix}.$$

Note that if $N \ne 2M$, a [generalized matrix inverse](wikipedia://en/7043631) may be needed to find the values $P_m$.

\2) After finding the $P_m$ values, find the roots (numerically if necessary) of the polynomial

$$z^M - P_1 z^{M-1} - \dots - P_M.$$

The $m$-th root of this polynomial will be equal to $e^{\lambda_m}$.

\3) With the $e^{\lambda_m}$ values, the $F_n$ values are part of a system of linear equations that may be used to solve for the $\mathrm{B}_m$ values:

$$\begin{bmatrix} F_{k_1} \\ \vdots \\ F_{k_M} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (e^{\lambda_1})^{k_1} & \dots & (e^{\lambda_M})^{k_1} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ (e^{\lambda_1})^{k_M} & \dots & (e^{\lambda_M})^{k_M} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \mathrm{B}_1 \\ \vdots \\ \mathrm{B}_M \end{bmatrix},$$

where $M$ unique values $k_i$ are used. It is possible to use a generalized matrix inverse if more than $M$ samples are used.

Note that solving for $\lambda_m$ will yield ambiguities, since only $e^{\lambda_m}$ was solved for, and $e^{\lambda_m} = e^{\lambda_m \,+\, q 2 \pi j}$ for an integer $q$. This leads to the same Nyquist sampling criterion to which discrete Fourier transforms are subject

$$\left|\operatorname{Im}(\lambda_m)\right| = \left|\omega_m\right| < \frac{\pi}{ \Delta_t}.$$
