# Persistent homology group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Persistent homology group"}

In [persistent homology](wikipedia://en/31069689), a **persistent homology group** is a multiscale analog of a [homology group](wikipedia://en/142432) that captures information about the evolution of [topological features](wikipedia://en/29954) across a [filtration](wikipedia://en/726014) of spaces. While the ordinary homology group represents nontrivial homology classes of an individual [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), the persistent homology group tracks only those classes that remain nontrivial across multiple parameters in the underlying filtration. Analogous to the ordinary [Betti number](wikipedia://en/346262), the ranks of the persistent homology groups are known as the [persistent Betti numbers](wikipedia://en/73492903). Persistent homology groups were first introduced by [Herbert Edelsbrunner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Edelsbrunner), David Letscher, and Afra Zomorodian in a 2002 paper *Topological Persistence and Simplification*, one of the foundational papers in the fields of persistent homology and [topological data analysis](wikipedia://en/17740009),[^o2] based largely on the [persistence barcodes](wikipedia://en/73543019) and the persistence algorithm, that were first described by Serguei Barannikov in the 1994 paper.[^o3] Since then, the study of persistent homology groups has led to applications in [data science](wikipedia://en/35458904),[^o4] [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488),[^o5] [materials science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materials_science),[^o6] [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology),[^o7][^o8] and [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics).[^o9]

:::

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $K$ be a [simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/252686), and let $f: K \to \mathbb R$ be a [real-valued](wikipedia://en/22284647) [monotonic function](wikipedia://en/48260). Then for some values $a_0 < a_1 < \cdots < a_n \in \mathbb R$ the [sublevel-sets](wikipedia://en/559622) $K(a) := f^{-1}(-\infty, a]$ yield a sequence of nested subcomplexes $\emptyset = K_0 \subseteq K_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq K_n = K$ known as a *filtration* of $K$.

Applying $p^{th}$ homology to each complex yields a sequence of homology groups $0 = H_p (K_0) \to H_p (K_1) \to \cdots \to H_p (K_n) = H_p (K)$ connected by [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13564) induced by the [inclusion maps](wikipedia://en/492516) of the underlying filtration. When homology is taken over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), we get a sequence of [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) and [linear maps](wikipedia://en/18102) known as a [persistence module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistence_module).

Let $f_p^{i,j}: H_p (K_i) \to H_p (K_j)$ be the homomorphism induced by the inclusion $K_i \hookrightarrow K_j$. Then the $p^{th}$ persistent homology groups are defined as the [images](wikipedia://en/579311) $H_p^{i,j} := \operatorname{im} f_p^{i,j}$ for all $1 \leq i \leq j \leq n$. In particular, the persistent homology group $H_p^{i,i} = H_p (K_i)$.

More precisely, the $p^{th}$ persistent homology group can be defined as $H_p^{i,j} = Z_p (K_i) / \left( B_p (K_j) \cap Z_p(K_i) \right)$, where $Z_p(K_\bullet)$ and $B_p(K_\bullet)$ are the standard p-cycle and p-boundary groups, respectively.[^o12]

:::

## Birth and death of homology classes {#Birth_and_death_of_homology_classes}

Sometimes the elements of $H_p^{i,j}$ are described as the homology classes that are "born" at or before $K_i$ and that have not yet "died" entering $K_j$. These notions can be made precise as follows. A homology class $\gamma \in H_p (K_i)$ is said to be *born* at $K_i$ if it is not contained in the image of the previous persistent homology group, i.e., $\gamma \notin H_p^{i-1, i}$. Conversely, $\gamma$ is said to *die entering* $K_j$ if $\gamma$ is subsumed (i.e., merges with) another older class as the sequence proceeds from $K_{j-1} \to K_j$. That is to say, $f_p^{i,j-1} (\gamma) \notin H_p^{i-1,j-1}$ but $f_p^{i,j} (\gamma) \in H_p^{i-1,j}$. The determination that an older class persists if it merges with a younger class, instead of the other way around, is sometimes known as the *Elder Rule*.[^o14][^o15]

The indices $i,j$ at which a homology class $\gamma$ is born and dies entering are known as the *birth* and *death* indices of $\gamma$. The difference $j-i$ is known as the *index persistence* of $\gamma$, while the corresponding difference $a_j - a_i$ in function values corresponding to those indices is known as the *persistence* of $\gamma$ . If there exists no index at which $\gamma$ dies, it is assigned an infinite death index. Thus, the persistence of each class can be represented as an [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) in the [extended real line](wikipedia://en/51698) $\mathbb R \cup \{\pm \infty\}$ of either the form $[a_i, a_j)$ or $[a_i', \infty)$. Since, in the case of an infinite field, the infinite number of classes always have the same persistence, the collection over *all* classes of such intervals does not give meaningful multiplicities for a [multiset](wikipedia://en/305303) of intervals. Instead, such multiplicities and a multiset of intervals in the extended real line are given by the structure theorem of [persistence homology](wikipedia://en/31069689).[^o3] This multiset is known as the *[persistence barcode](wikipedia://en/73543019)*.[^o16]

## Canonical form {#Canonical_form}

Concretely, the structure theorem states that for any filtered complex over a field $F$, there exists a linear transformation that preserves the filtration and converts the filtered complex into so called **canonical form**, a canonically defined direct sum of filtered complexes of two types: two-dimensional complexes with trivial homology $d(e_{a_j})=e_{a_i}$ and one-dimensional complexes with trivial differential $d(e_{a'_i})=0$.[^o3]

## Persistence diagram {#Persistence_diagram}

*Image: Persistence Diagram.png.* An example of a persistence diagram

Geometrically, a barcode can be plotted as a multiset of points (with possibly infinite coordinates) $(a_i, a_j)$ in the extended [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852) $\left( \mathbb R \cup \{\pm \infty \} \right)^2$. By the above definitions, each point will lie above the diagonal, and the distance to the diagonal is exactly equal to the persistence of the corresponding class times $\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}$. This construction is known as the *persistence diagram*, and it provides a way of visualizing the structure of the persistence of homology classes in the sequence of persistent homology groups.[^o2]
