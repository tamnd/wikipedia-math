# Minimum total potential energy principle

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Minimum total potential energy principle"}

The **minimum total potential energy principle** is a fundamental concept used in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering). It dictates that at low temperatures a structure or body shall deform or displace to a position that (locally) minimizes the total [potential energy](wikipedia://en/23703), with the lost potential energy being converted into [kinetic energy](wikipedia://en/17327) (specifically heat).

:::

## Some examples {#Some_examples}

- A free [proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton) and free [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) will tend to combine to form the lowest energy state (the [ground state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_state)) of a [hydrogen atom](wikipedia://en/14225), the most stable [configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_configuration). This is because that state's energy is 13.6 [electron volts (eV)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronvolt) lower than when the two [particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle) separated by an [infinite](wikipedia://en/20110824) [distance](wikipedia://en/39378). The dissipation in this system takes the form of [spontaneous emission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_emission) of [electromagnetic radiation](wikipedia://en/9426), which increases the [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) of the surroundings.
- A rolling ball will end up stationary at the bottom of a hill, the point of minimum potential energy. The reason is that as it rolls downward under the influence of [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity), [friction](wikipedia://en/11062) produced by its motion transfers energy in the form of heat to the [surroundings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surroundings_%28thermodynamics%29) with an attendant increase in entropy.
- A [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein) folds into the state of lowest [potential energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_energy_of_protein). In this case, the dissipation takes the form of vibration of atoms within or adjacent to the protein.

## Structural mechanics {#Structural_mechanics}

The total potential energy, $\boldsymbol{\Pi}$, is the sum of the elastic strain energy, $\mathbf{U}$, stored in the deformed body and the potential energy, $\mathbf{V}$, associated to the applied forces:[^o4]

::: {#o5 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\boldsymbol{\Pi} = \mathbf{U} + \mathbf{V} \tag{1}$$

:::

This energy is at a [stationary position](wikipedia://en/885651) when an [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) variation from such position involves no change in energy:[^o4]

::: {#o6 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\delta\boldsymbol{\Pi} = \delta(\mathbf{U} + \mathbf{V}) = 0 \tag{2}$$

:::

The principle of minimum total potential energy may be derived as a special case of the [virtual work](wikipedia://en/1211986) principle for elastic systems subject to [conservative forces](wikipedia://en/44158).

The equality between external and internal virtual work (due to virtual displacements) is:

::: {#o7 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$\int_{S_t} \delta\ \mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{T} dS + \int_{V} \delta\ \mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{f} dV = \int_{V}\delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^T \boldsymbol{\sigma} dV \tag{3}$$

:::

where

- $\mathbf{u}$ = vector of displacements
- $\mathbf{T}$ = vector of distributed forces acting on the part $S_t$ of the surface
- $\mathbf{f}$ = vector of body forces

In the special case of elastic bodies, the right-hand-side of ((3)) can be taken to be the change, $\delta \mathbf{U}$, of elastic strain energy $\mathbf{U}$ due to infinitesimal variations of real displacements. In addition, when the external forces are [conservative forces](wikipedia://en/44158), the left-hand-side of ((3)) can be seen as the change in the [potential energy](wikipedia://en/23703) function $\mathbf{V}$ of the forces. The function $\mathbf{V}$ is defined as:[^o8]

$$\mathbf{V} = -\int_{S_t} \mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{T} dS - \int_{V} \mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{f} dV$$

where the minus sign implies a loss of potential energy as the force is displaced in its direction. With these two subsidiary conditions, (Equation 3) becomes:

$$-\delta\ \mathbf{V} = \delta\ \mathbf{U}$$

This leads to ((2)) as desired. The variational form of ((2)) is often used as the basis for developing the [finite element method in structural mechanics](wikipedia://en/3587096).
