# Three-dimensional space

Three-dimensional

*Image: Coord planes color.svg.* A representation of a three-dimensional [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Three-dimensional space"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **three-dimensional (3D) space** is a [mathematical space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_space) in which three values (termed *[coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinates)*) are required to determine the [position](wikipedia://en/1345771) of a [point](wikipedia://en/593693). Alternatively, it can be referred to as **3-space** or, rarely, **tri-dimensional space**. Most commonly, it means the **three-dimensional Euclidean space**, that is, the [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) of [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) three, which models [physical space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_space). More general three-dimensional spaces are called *[3-manifolds](wikipedia://en/1018257)*. The term may refer colloquially to a subset of space, a *three-dimensional region* (or 3D [domain](wikipedia://en/16796643)),[^o81] a *[solid figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_figure)*.

:::

Technically, a [tuple](wikipedia://en/132729) of $n$ [numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) can be understood as the [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) of a location in a $n$-dimensional Euclidean space. The set of these $n$-tuples is commonly denoted $\mathbb{R}^n,$ and can be identified to the pair formed by a $n$-dimensional Euclidean space and a [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706). When $n = 3$, this space is called the three-dimensional Euclidean space (or simply "Euclidean space" when the context is clear).[^o2] In [classical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_physics), it serves as a model of the physical [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe), in which all known [matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter) exists. When [relativity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativity_theory) is considered, it can be considered a local subspace of [space-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space-time).[^o3] While this space remains the most widely used way to model the world as it is experienced,[^o4] it is only one example of a 3-manifold. In this classical example, when the three values refer to measurements in different directions ([coordinates](wikipedia://en/81931)), any three directions can be chosen, provided that these directions do not lie in the same [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29). Furthermore, if these directions are pairwise [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944), the three values are often labeled by the terms *[width](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Width)/breadth*, *[height](wikipedia://en/239972)/depth*, and *[length](wikipedia://en/18542)*.

## History {#History}

The philosopher [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) recognised the existence of three dimensions:A magnitude if divisible one way is a line, if two ways a surface, and if three a body. Beyond these there is no other magnitude, because the three dimensions are all that there are, and that which is divisible in three directions is divisible in all.[^o6]

Books XI to XIII of [Euclid's Elements](wikipedia://en/244107) dealt with [three-dimensional geometry](wikipedia://en/507960). Book XI develops notions of perpendicularity, parallelism, and orthogonality of lines and planes, the construction and properties of angles, and [parallelepiped](wikipedia://en/23975) solids. Book XII discusses infinitesimals and the [method of exhaustion](wikipedia://en/1721284) for finding the area of a circle or the volume of a [pyramid](wikipedia://en/3307045),[^o8] cone, cylinder, or sphere.[^o7] Book XIII describes the construction of the five regular [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) in a sphere, covering the cube, [octahedra](wikipedia://en/22458), [icosahedra](wikipedia://en/14968) and [dodecahedra](wikipedia://en/7149361).[^o8]

In the 17th century, three-dimensional space was described with [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates), with the advent of [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202) developed by [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) in his work *[La Géométrie](wikipedia://en/239981)*.[^o9] [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966) independently developed similar ideas in the manuscript *Ad locos planos et solidos isagoge* (Introduction to Plane and Solid Loci), which was unpublished during Fermat's lifetime.[^o10] Fermat's work on seeking [extrema](wikipedia://en/298420) of a curve would lay the groundwork for [differential calculus](wikipedia://en/50416).[^o11] [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) introduced the [polar coordinate system](wikipedia://en/25120) as an alternative non-Cartesian system that is useful for certain geometries.[^o12]

The 18th century, [Alexis Clairaut](wikipedia://en/196273) studied algebraic curves in space, the concept of [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) and curvature, and the use of calculus for this purpose.[^o13][^o14] [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) studied the notion of a [geodesic](wikipedia://en/91096) on a surface deriving the first analytical [geodesic equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_equation),[^o15] and later introduced the first set of intrinsic coordinate systems on a surface,[^o14] beginning the theory of *intrinsic geometry* upon which modern geometric ideas are based. In 1760, Euler proved a theorem expressing the curvature of a space curve on a surface in terms of the principal curvatures,[^o16] known as [Euler's theorem](wikipedia://en/18753256). Later in the century, [Gaspard Monge](wikipedia://en/77840) made important contributions to the study of curves and surfaces in space.[^o14] The work of Euler and Monge laid the foundations for [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625).

In the 19th century, developments of the geometry of three-dimensional space came with [William Rowan Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817)'s development of the [quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternions), a [hypercomplex number](wikipedia://en/51438) system. For this purpose, Hamilton coined the terms [scalar](wikipedia://en/3588331) and [vector](wikipedia://en/32533), and they were first defined in the three dimensional sense within [his geometric framework for quaternions](wikipedia://en/15433374).[^o17] Three dimensional space could then be described by quaternions $q = a + ui + vj + wk$ which had a vanishing scalar component, that is, $a = 0$.[^o18]

While not explicitly studied by Hamilton, this work indirectly introduced notions of basis, here given by the quaternion elements $i,j,k$, as well as the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) and [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092), which correspond to (the negative of) the scalar part and the vector part of the product of two vector quaternions. It was not until [Josiah Willard Gibbs](wikipedia://en/37332) that these two products were identified in their own right,[^o18] and the modern notation for the dot and cross product were introduced in his classroom teaching notes, found also in the 1901 textbook *[Vector Analysis](wikipedia://en/12125059)* written by [Edwin Bidwell Wilson](wikipedia://en/11692574) based on Gibbs' lectures.[^o19]

Further development came in the abstract formalism of vector spaces, with the work of [Hermann Grassmann](wikipedia://en/99242) and [Giuseppe Peano](wikipedia://en/13043), the latter of whom first gave the modern definition of vector spaces as an [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364).[^o20] The development of [matrix mathematics](wikipedia://en/20556859) and its application to n-dimensional geometry was made by [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034).[^o21]

## In Euclidean geometry {#In_Euclidean_geometry}

### Coordinate systems {#Coordinate_systems}

In mathematics, [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202) (also called Cartesian geometry) describes every point in three-dimensional space by means of three coordinates. Three [coordinate axes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_axis) are given, each perpendicular to the other two at the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432), the point at which they cross. They are usually labeled $x, y$, and $z$. Relative to these axes, the position of any point in three-dimensional space is given by an ordered triple of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), each number giving the distance of that point from the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) measured along the given axis, which is equal to the distance of that point from the plane determined by the other two axes.[^o24]

Other popular methods of describing the location of a point in three-dimensional space include [cylindrical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylindrical_coordinates) and [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates), though there are an infinite number of possible methods.[^o25][^o26] For more, see [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697).

Below are images of the above-mentioned systems.

### Lines and planes {#Lines_and_planes}

Two distinct points always determine a (straight) [line](wikipedia://en/946975). Three distinct points are either [collinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear) or determine a unique [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29). On the other hand, four distinct points can either be collinear, [coplanar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coplanar), or determine the entire space.[^o28]

Two distinct lines can either intersect, be [parallel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_line) or be [skew](wikipedia://en/1975821). Two parallel lines, or [two intersecting lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_intersecting_lines), lie in a unique plane, so skew lines are lines that do not meet and do not lie in a common plane.[^o29]

*Image: Relations between planes.png.* Relations between up to three planes; only in example 12 do three planes meet to form a point

Two distinct planes can either meet in a common line or are parallel (i.e., do not meet).[^o29] Three distinct planes, no pair of which are parallel, can either meet in a common line, meet in a unique common point, or have no point in common. In the last case, the three lines of intersection of each pair of planes are mutually parallel.[^o30]

A line can lie in a given plane, intersect that plane in a unique point, or be parallel to the plane.[^o29] In the last case, lines can be formed in the plane that are parallel to the given line.

A [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) is a subspace of one dimension less than the dimension of the full space. The hyperplanes of a three-dimensional space are the two-dimensional subspaces, that is, the planes. In terms of Cartesian coordinates, the points of a hyperplane satisfy a single [linear equation](wikipedia://en/17570), so planes in this 3-space are described by linear equations. A line can be described by a pair of independent linear equations—each representing a plane having this line as a common intersection.[^o31]

[Varignon's theorem](wikipedia://en/17006564) states that the midpoints of any quadrilateral in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ form a [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496), and hence are coplanar.[^o32]

### Spheres and balls {#Spheres_and_balls}

*Image: Sphere wireframe 10deg 6r.svg.* A [perspective projection](wikipedia://en/313741#Perspective_projection) of a sphere onto two dimensions

A sphere in 3-space (also called a *2-sphere* because, like all [surfaces](wikipedia://en/4342970), it is intrinsically two-dimensional) consists of the set of all points in 3-space at a fixed distance $r$ from a central point $P$. The solid enclosed by the sphere is called a [*ball*](wikipedia://en/160556) (or *3-ball*).[^o34]

The volume of the ball is given by[^o35]

$$V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3},$$

and the surface area of the sphere is[^o35]

$$A = 4\pi r^2,$$

Another type of sphere arises from a 4-ball, whose three-dimensional surface is the **3-sphere**: points equidistant to the origin of the euclidean space $\mathbf{R}^{4}$. If a point has coordinates, $P(x, y, z, w)$, then $x^{2} + y^{2} + z^{2} + w^{2} = 1$ characterizes those points on the unit 3-sphere centered at the origin.[^o36]

This 3-sphere is an example of a [3-manifold](wikipedia://en/1018257): a [space](wikipedia://en/30450) which is 'looks locally' like 3-D space.[^o37] In precise topological terms, each point of the 3-sphere has a neighborhood which is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to an open [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of 3-D space.

### Surfaces of revolution {#Surfaces_of_revolution}

A [surface](wikipedia://en/27865) generated by revolving a plane [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) about a fixed line in its plane as an axis is called a surface of revolution. The plane curve is called the *[generatrix](wikipedia://en/24801595)* of the surface. A section of the surface, made by intersecting the surface with a plane that is perpendicular (orthogonal) to the axis, is a circle.[^o44][^o45]

Simple examples occur when the generatrix is a line. If the generatrix line intersects the axis line, the surface of revolution is a right circular [cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_%28mathematics%29) with vertex (apex) the point of intersection. However, if the generatrix and axis are parallel, then the surface of revolution is a circular [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29).[^o44][^o45]

### Quadric surfaces {#Quadric_surfaces}

In analogy with the [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673), the set of points whose Cartesian coordinates satisfy the general equation of the second degree, namely,

$$Ax^2 + By^2 + Cz^2 + Fxy + Gyz + Hxz + Jx + Ky + Lz + M = 0,$$

where $A, B, C, F, G, H, J, K, L$ and $M$ are real numbers and not all of $A, B, C, F, G$ and $H$ are zero, is called a **quadric surface**.[^o47]

There are six types of [non-degenerate](wikipedia://en/232952) quadric surfaces:[^o47]

1. [Ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/145381)
1. [Hyperboloid of one sheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperboloid_of_one_sheet)
1. [Hyperboloid of two sheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperboloid_of_two_sheets)
1. [Elliptic cone](wikipedia://en/66221593)
1. [Elliptic paraboloid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_paraboloid)
1. [Hyperbolic paraboloid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_paraboloid)

The degenerate quadric surfaces are the empty set, a single point, a single line, a single plane, a pair of planes or a quadratic cylinder (a surface consisting of a non-degenerate conic section in a plane $\pi$ and all the lines of $\mathbf{R}^{3}$ through that conic that are normal to $\pi$).[^o47] Elliptic cones are sometimes considered to be degenerate quadric surfaces as well.

Both the hyperboloid of one sheet and the hyperbolic paraboloid are [ruled surfaces](wikipedia://en/705158), meaning that they can be made up from a family of straight lines. In fact, each has two families of generating lines, the members of each family are disjoint and each member of one family intersects, with just one exception, every member of the other family.[^o47] Each family is called a [regulus](wikipedia://en/52079701).[^o48]

## In linear algebra {#In_linear_algebra}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), the perspective of three-dimensional space is crucially dependent on the concept of independence. Space has three dimensions because the length of a [box](wikipedia://en/156802) is independent of its width or breadth. In the technical language of linear algebra, space is three-dimensional because every point in space can be described by a linear combination of three independent [vectors](wikipedia://en/879358).[^o50]

### Dot product, angle, and length {#Dot_product,_angle,_and_length}

A vector can be pictured as an arrow. The vector's magnitude is its length, and its direction is the direction the arrow points. A vector in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ can be represented by an ordered triple of real numbers. These numbers are called the **components** of the vector.

The dot product of two vectors $\mathbf{A} = [A_{1}, A_{2}, A_{3}]$ and $\mathbf{B} = [B_{1}, B_{2}, B_{3}]$ is defined as:[^o52]

$$\mathbf{A}\cdot \mathbf{B} = A_1B_1 + A_2B_2 + A_3B_3 = \sum_{i=1}^3 A_i B_i.$$

The magnitude of a vector $\mathbf{A}$ is denoted by $||\mathbf{A}||$. The dot product of a vector $\mathbf{A} = [A_{1}, A_{2}, A_{3}]$ with itself is

$$\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf A = \|\mathbf A\|^2 = A_1^2 + A_2^2 + A_3^2,$$

which gives[^o52]

$$\|\mathbf A\| = \sqrt{\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf A} = \sqrt{A_1^2 + A_2^2 + A_3^2},$$

the formula for the [Euclidean length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_length) of the vector.

Without reference to the components of the vectors, the dot product of two non-zero Euclidean vectors $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ is given by[^o52]

$$\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B = \|\mathbf A\|\,\|\mathbf B\|\cos\theta,$$

where $\theta$ is the [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) between $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$.

For a physical example, consider a block on an [inclined plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclined_plane) that is being pulled downward by a [gravitational force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_force). The dot product can be used to compute the [work](wikipedia://en/149861) $W$ performed by the constant [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) vector $\mathbf g$ that is applied at an angle $\theta$ to the downslope direction of motion $\mathbf d$. That is:[^o53]

$$W = \mathbf g\cdot\mathbf d = \|\mathbf g\|\,\|\mathbf d\|\cos\theta$$

### Cross product {#Cross_product}

The cross product or **vector product** is a [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) on two [vectors](wikipedia://en/32533) in three-dimensional [space](wikipedia://en/9697) and is denoted by the symbol ×. The cross product **A** × **B** of the vectors **A** and **B** is a vector that is [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to both and therefore [normal](wikipedia://en/173224) to the plane containing them. It has many applications in mathematics, [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering).[^o55] For example, it can be used to compute the amount of [torque](wikipedia://en/30400) on a bolt being turned by a wrench, or the [Lorentz force](wikipedia://en/18631) on an [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) travelling through a [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563).[^o56]

In function language, the cross product is a function $\times: \mathbb{R}^3 \times \mathbb{R}^3 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^3$.[^o58]

*Image: Cross product vector.svg.* The cross-product in respect to a right-handed coordinate system

The components of the cross product are $\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B} = [A_2 B_3 - B_2 A_3, A_3 B_1 - B_3 A_1, A_1 B_2 - B_1 A_2]$, and can also be written in components, using Einstein summation convention as $(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})_i = \varepsilon_{ijk} A_j B_k$ where $\varepsilon_{ijk}$ is the [Levi-Civita symbol](wikipedia://en/195982).[^o57] It has the property that $\mathbf{A}\times \mathbf{B} = -\mathbf{B}\times \mathbf{A}$.[^o55]

Its magnitude is related to the angle $\theta$ between $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ by the identity[^o55]

$$\left\|\mathbf{A}\times \mathbf{B}\right\| = \left\|\mathbf{A}\right\| \cdot \left\|\mathbf{B}\right\| \cdot \left|\sin\theta\right|.$$

The space and product form an [algebra over a field](wikipedia://en/191788), which is not [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) nor [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative), but is a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) with the cross product being the Lie bracket.[^o59] Specifically, the space together with the product, $(\mathbb{R}^3,\times)$ is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the [Lie algebra of three-dimensional rotations](wikipedia://en/173965), denoted $\mathfrak{so}(3)$.[^o58] In order to satisfy the axioms of a Lie algebra, instead of associativity the cross product satisfies the [Jacobi identity](wikipedia://en/294370). For any three vectors $\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{C}$[^o59]

$$\mathbf{A}\times(\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{C}) + \mathbf{B}\times(\mathbf{C}\times\mathbf{A}) + \mathbf{C}\times(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}) = 0$$

One can in *n* dimensions take the product of *n* − 1 vectors to produce a vector perpendicular to all of them. But if the product is limited to non-trivial binary products with vector results, it exists only in three and [seven dimensions](wikipedia://en/2368531).[^o60]

### Abstract description {#Abstract_description}

It can be useful to describe three-dimensional space as a three-dimensional vector space $V$ over the real numbers. This differs from $\mathbb{R}^3$ in a subtle way. By definition, there exists a basis $\mathcal{B} = \{e_1,e_2,e_3\}$ for $V$. This corresponds to an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) between $V$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$:[^o50] the construction for the isomorphism is found [here](wikipedia://en/18420#Coordinates). However, there is no 'preferred' or 'canonical basis' for $V$.

On the other hand, there is a preferred basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$, which is due to its description as a [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of copies of $\mathbb{R}$, that is, $\mathbb{R}^3 = \mathbb{R}\times \mathbb{R}\times \mathbb{R}$, the three-dimensional Euclidean space.[^o62] This allows the definition of canonical projections, $\pi_i:\mathbb{R}^3 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, where $1 \leq i \leq 3$. For example, $\pi_1(x_1,x_2,x_3) = x$. This then allows the definition of the [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005) $\mathcal{B}_{\text{Standard}} = \{E_1, E_2, E_3\}$ defined by

$$\pi_i(E_j) = \delta_{ij}$$

where $\delta_{ij}$ is the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890). Written out in full, the standard basis is[^o63]

$$E_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0\\ 0\end{pmatrix}, E_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 1\\ 0\end{pmatrix}, E_3 = \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 0\\ 1\end{pmatrix}.$$

Therefore $\mathbb{R}^3$ can be viewed as the abstract vector space, together with the additional structure of a choice of basis. Conversely, $V$ can be obtained by starting with $\mathbb{R}^3$ and 'forgetting' the Cartesian product structure, or equivalently the standard choice of basis.

As opposed to a general vector space $V$, the space $\mathbb{R}^3$ is sometimes referred to as a coordinate space.[^o64]

Physically, it is conceptually desirable to use the abstract formalism in order to assume as little structure as possible if it is not given by the parameters of a particular problem. For example, in a problem with rotational symmetry, working with the more concrete description of three-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3$ assumes a choice of basis, corresponding to a set of axes. But in rotational symmetry, there is no reason why one set of axes is preferred to say, the same set of axes which has been rotated arbitrarily. Stated another way, a preferred choice of axes breaks the rotational symmetry of physical space.

Computationally, it is necessary to work with the more concrete description $\mathbb{R}^3$ in order to do concrete computations.

#### Affine description {#Affine_description}

A more abstract description still is to model physical space as a three-dimensional affine space $E(3)$ over the real numbers. This is unique up to affine isomorphism. It is sometimes referred to as three-dimensional Euclidean space.[^o67] Just as the vector space description came from 'forgetting the preferred basis' of $\mathbb{R}^3$, the affine space description comes from 'forgetting the origin' of the vector space. Euclidean spaces are sometimes called *Euclidean affine spaces* for distinguishing them from Euclidean vector spaces.[^o66]

This is physically appealing as it makes the translation invariance of physical space manifest. A preferred origin breaks the translational invariance.[^o67]

#### Inner product space {#Inner_product_space}

The above discussion does not involve the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093). The dot product is an example of an [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product). Physical space can be modelled as a vector space which additionally has the structure of an inner product. The inner product defines notions of length and angle (and therefore in particular the notion of orthogonality).[^o69] For any inner product, there exist bases under which the inner product agrees with the dot product, but again, there are many different possible bases, none of which are preferred. They differ from one another by a rotation, an element of the group of rotations [SO(3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SO%283%29).

## In calculus {#In_calculus}

Vector calculus is concerned with [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) and cumulative changes to [vector fields](wikipedia://en/62641), primarily in three-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), $\mathbb{R}^3$. For [differentiation](wikipedia://en/7921), the [del](wikipedia://en/151925) ($\nabla$), or nabla, operator is used.

### Gradient, divergence and curl {#Gradient,_divergence_and_curl}

The [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) indicates the direction of greatest increase of a function, and its magnitude. An example is a flow of particles, with the gradient being the magnitude and direction of the flow at a location.[^o72] In a rectangular coordinate system, the gradient of a differentiable function $f: \mathbb{R}^3 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is given by[^o73]

$$\nabla f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \mathbf{i} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \mathbf{j} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} \mathbf{k}$$

where **i**, **j**, and **k** are the [unit vectors](wikipedia://en/167053) for the *x*-, *y*-, and *z*-axes, respectively. In [index notation](wikipedia://en/320026) it is written[^o74]

$$(\nabla f)_i = \partial_i f.$$

The [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) indicates the net [flux](wikipedia://en/43590) of a vector field around a point, such as an increase or decrease of particle density. That is, whether the location is a [source or sink](wikipedia://en/78460696).[^o75] The divergence of a (differentiable) [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) **F** = *U* **i** + *V* **j** + *W* **k**, that is, a function $\mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}^3 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^3$, is equal to the [scalar](wikipedia://en/3588331)-valued function:[^o73]

$$\operatorname{div}\,\mathbf{F} = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} =\frac{\partial U}{\partial x} +\frac{\partial V}{\partial y} +\frac{\partial W}{\partial z }.$$

In index notation, with [Einstein summation convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_summation_convention) this is[^o74]

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \partial_i F_i.$$

The [curl](wikipedia://en/6123) (or rotor) is a vector indicating the rotational circulation of a vector field. Expanded in [Cartesian coordinates](wikipedia://en/7706) (see [Del in cylindrical and spherical coordinates](wikipedia://en/753145) for [spherical](wikipedia://en/29181) and [cylindrical](wikipedia://en/176733) coordinate representations), the curl ∇ × **F** is, for **F** composed of \[*F*<sub>x</sub>, *F*<sub>y</sub>, *F*<sub>z</sub>\]:[^o76]

$$\begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ \\ {\frac{\partial}{\partial x}} & {\frac{\partial}{\partial y}} & {\frac{\partial}{\partial z}} \\ \\ F_x & F_y & F_z \end{vmatrix}$$

This expands as follows:[^o73]

$$\operatorname{curl}\,\mathbf{F} = \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \left(\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial z}\right) \mathbf{i} + \left(\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial F_z}{\partial x}\right) \mathbf{j} + \left(\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}\right) \mathbf{k}.$$

In index notation, with Einstein summation convention this is[^o74]

$$(\nabla \times \mathbf{F})_i = \epsilon_{ijk}\partial_j F_k,$$

where $\epsilon_{ijk}$ is the totally antisymmetric symbol, the [Levi-Civita symbol](wikipedia://en/195982).

### Line, surface, and volume integrals {#Line,_surface,_and_volume_integrals}

*Image: Line integral of vector field.gif.* Illustration of a line integral along curve C in a vector field F

A [line integral](wikipedia://en/21245414) of a function along a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) can be thought of as a continuous summation of the function value along every infinitesimal increment of that curve. For some [scalar field](wikipedia://en/161879) *f* : *U* ⊆ **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R**, the *line integral* along a [piecewise smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise_smooth) curve *C* ⊂ *U* is defined as[^o78]

$$\int\limits_C f\, ds = \int_a^b f(\mathbf{r}(t)) |\mathbf{r}'(t)|\, dt.$$

where **r**: \[a, b\] → *C* is an arbitrary [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) (one-to-one correspondence) parametrization of the curve *C* such that **r**(*a*) and **r**(*b*) give the endpoints of *C* and $a < b$.

For a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) **F** : *U* ⊆ **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, the line integral along a piecewise smooth [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) *C* ⊂ *U*, in the direction of **r**, is defined as[^o78]

$$\int\limits_C \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})\cdot\,d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t))\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt,$$

where $\cdot$ is the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) and **r**: \[a, b\] → *C* is a bijective [parametrization](wikipedia://en/576108) of the curve *C* such that **r**(*a*) and **r**(*b*) give the endpoints of *C*. A subtype of line integral found in physics is the plane closed loop, which determines the circulation of the function around the loop[^o79]

$$\oint_C \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})\cdot\,d\mathbf{r}.$$

A *[surface integral](wikipedia://en/528867)* is a generalization of [multiple integrals](wikipedia://en/2757224) to integration over [surfaces](wikipedia://en/27865). It can be thought of as the [double integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_integral) analog of the line integral. To find an explicit formula for the surface integral, we need to [parameterize](wikipedia://en/81931) the surface of interest, *S*, by considering a system of [curvilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/755300) on *S*, like the [latitude and longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_coordinate_system) on a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859). Let such a parameterization be **x**(*s*, *t*), where (*s*, *t*) varies in some region *T* in the [plane](wikipedia://en/7706#Cartesian_coordinates_in_two_dimensions). Then, the surface integral is given by

$$\iint_{S} f \,\mathrm dS = \iint_{T} f(\mathbf{x}(s, t)) \left\|{\partial \mathbf{x} \over \partial s}\times {\partial \mathbf{x} \over \partial t}\right\| \mathrm ds\, \mathrm dt$$

where the expression between bars on the right-hand side is the [magnitude](wikipedia://en/577301) of the [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) of the [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565) of **x**(*s*, *t*), and is known as the surface [element](wikipedia://en/1920694). Given a vector field **v** on *S*, that is a function that assigns to each **x** in *S* a vector **v**(**x**), the surface integral can be defined component-wise according to the definition of the surface integral of a scalar field; the result is a vector.

A *[volume integral](wikipedia://en/1247265)* is an [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) over a *three-dimensional [domain](wikipedia://en/16796643)* or region. When the [integrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrand) is trivial (unity), the volume integral is simply the region's *[volume](wikipedia://en/32498)*.[^o80][^o81] It can also mean a [triple integral](wikipedia://en/2757224) within a region *D* in **R**<sup>3</sup> of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f(x,y,z),$ and is usually written as:

$$\iiint\limits_D f(x,y,z)\,dx\,dy\,dz.$$

::: {#o83 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Fundamental theorem of line integrals"}

### Fundamental theorem of line integrals {#Fundamental_theorem_of_line_integrals}

The fundamental theorem of line integrals, says that a [line integral](wikipedia://en/21245414) through a [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) field can be evaluated by evaluating the original scalar field at the endpoints of the curve.[^o84]

Let $\varphi : U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$. Then

$$\varphi\left(\mathbf{q}\right)-\varphi\left(\mathbf{p}\right) = \int_{\gamma[\mathbf{p},\,\mathbf{q}]} \nabla\varphi(\mathbf{r})\cdot d\mathbf{r}.$$

:::

::: {#o86 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Stokes' theorem"}

### Stokes' theorem {#Stokes'_theorem}

Stokes' theorem relates the [surface integral](wikipedia://en/528867) of the [curl](wikipedia://en/6123) of a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) F over a surface Σ in Euclidean three-space to the [line integral](wikipedia://en/21245414) of the vector field over its boundary ∂Σ:[^o87]

$$\iint_{\Sigma} \nabla \times \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{\Sigma} = \oint_{\partial\Sigma} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathrm{d} \mathbf{r}.$$

:::

The closed manifold $\partial V$ is quite generally the boundary of $V$ oriented by outward-pointing [normals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_normal), and $\mathbf{n}$ is the outward pointing unit normal field of the boundary $\partial V$. ## In topology {#In_topology}

*Image: WikipediaGlobeOnePiece.stl.* [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia)'s globe logo in 3-D

Three-dimensional space has a number of topological properties that distinguish it from spaces of other dimension numbers. For example, at least three dimensions are required to tie a [knot](wikipedia://en/153008) in a piece of string.[^o92]

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) the generic three-dimensional spaces are [3-manifolds](wikipedia://en/1018257), which locally resemble ${\mathbb{R}}^3$. Globally, the same 3-manifold can curve in a variety of manners, as long as it remains continuous.[^o93] An example of this is the [curved spacetime](wikipedia://en/1016422) found in [General Relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Relativity).

## In finite geometry {#In_finite_geometry}

Many ideas of dimension can be tested with [finite geometry](wikipedia://en/267061). The simplest instance is [PG(3,2)](wikipedia://en/59836267), which has [Fano planes](wikipedia://en/390404) as its 2-dimensional subspaces.[^o95] It is an instance of [Galois geometry](wikipedia://en/25197704), a study of [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) using [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615). Thus, for any Galois field GF(*q*), there is a [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) PG(3,*q*) of three dimensions.[^o96] For example, any three [skew lines](wikipedia://en/1975821) in PG(3,*q*) are contained in exactly one [regulus](wikipedia://en/52079701).[^o97]
