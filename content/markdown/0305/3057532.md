# Complex measure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complex measure"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory), a **complex measure** generalizes the concept of [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) by letting it have [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) values.[^o2] In other words, one allows for [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) whose size (length, area, volume) is a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826).

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Formally, a *complex measure* $\mu$ on a [measurable space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-algebra) $(X,\Sigma)$ is a complex-valued [function](wikipedia://en/185427)

$$\mu: \Sigma \to \mathbb{C}$$

that is [sigma-additive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_additivity). In other words, for any [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $(A_{n})_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ of [disjoint sets](wikipedia://en/39359) belonging to $\Sigma$, one has

$$\sum_{n = 1}^{\infty} \mu(A_{n}) = \mu \left( \bigcup_{n = 1}^{\infty} A_{n} \right) \in \mathbb{C}.$$

As $\displaystyle \bigcup_{n = 1}^{\infty} A_{n} = \bigcup_{n = 1}^{\infty} A_{\sigma(n)}$ for any [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) ([bijection](wikipedia://en/3942)) $\sigma: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$, it follows that $\displaystyle \sum_{n = 1}^{\infty} \mu(A_{n})$ [converges unconditionally](wikipedia://en/2620508) (hence, since $\mathbb{C}$ is finite dimensional, $\mu$ [converges absolutely](wikipedia://en/61532)).

:::

## Integration with respect to a complex measure {#Integration_with_respect_to_a_complex_measure}

One can define the *integral* of a complex-valued [measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055) with respect to a complex measure in the same way as the [Lebesgue integral](wikipedia://en/26064288) of a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued measurable function with respect to a [non-negative measure](wikipedia://en/19873), by approximating a measurable function with [simple functions](wikipedia://en/1890594).[^o6] Just as in the case of ordinary integration, this more general integral might fail to exist, or its value might be infinite (the [complex infinity](wikipedia://en/30876799)).

Another approach is to not develop a theory of integration from scratch, but rather use the already available concept of integral of a [real-valued function](wikipedia://en/22284647) with respect to a non-negative measure.[^o7] To that end, it is a quick check that the real and imaginary parts μ<sub>1</sub> and μ<sub>2</sub> of a complex measure μ are finite-valued [signed measures](wikipedia://en/2985811). One can apply the [Hahn-Jordan decomposition](wikipedia://en/2985858) to these measures to split them as

$$\mu_1=\mu_1^+-\mu_1^-$$

and

$$\mu_2=\mu_2^+-\mu_2^-$$

where μ<sub>1</sub><sup>\+</sup>, μ<sub>1</sub><sup>−</sup>, μ<sub>2</sub><sup>\+</sup>, μ<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup> are finite-valued non-negative measures (which are unique in some sense). Then, for a measurable function *f* which is *real-valued* for the moment, one can define

$$\int_X \! f \, d\mu = \left(\int_X \! f \, d\mu_1^+ - \int_X \! f \, d\mu_1^-\right) + i \left(\int_X \! f \, d\mu_2^+ - \int_X \! f \, d\mu_2^-\right)$$

as long as the expression on the right-hand side is defined, that is, all four integrals exist and when adding them up one does not encounter the [indeterminate](wikipedia://en/366723) ∞−∞.[^o7]

Given now a *complex-valued* measurable function, one can integrate its real and imaginary components separately as illustrated above and define, as expected,

$$\int_X \! f \, d\mu = \int_X \! \Re(f) \, d\mu + i \int_X \! \Im(f) \, d\mu.$$

## Variation of a complex measure and polar decomposition {#Variation_of_a_complex_measure_and_polar_decomposition}

For a complex measure μ, one defines its *variation*, or *[absolute value](wikipedia://en/991)*, |μ| by the formula

$$|\mu|(A)= \sup\sum_{n=1}^\infty |\mu(A_n)|$$

where *A* is in Σ and the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) runs over all sequences of [disjoint sets](wikipedia://en/39359) (*A*<sub>*n*</sub>)<sub>*n*</sub> whose [union](wikipedia://en/47949) is *A*. Taking only finite partitions of the set *A* into [measurable subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurable_set), one obtains an equivalent definition.

It turns out that |μ| is a non-negative finite measure. In the same way as a complex number can be represented in a [polar form](wikipedia://en/5826), one has a *polar decomposition* for a complex measure: There exists a measurable function θ with real values such that

$$d\mu = e ^{i \theta}d |\mu|,$$

meaning

$$\int_X f\, d\mu = \int_X f e ^{i \theta} \, d |\mu|$$

for any [absolutely integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_integrable) measurable function *f*, i.e., *f* satisfying

$$\int_X |f|\, d|\mu|<\infty.$$

One can use the [Radon–Nikodym theorem](wikipedia://en/338746) to prove that the variation is a measure and the existence of the [polar decomposition](wikipedia://en/1369241).

## The space of complex measures {#The_space_of_complex_measures}

The sum of two complex measures is a complex measure, as is the product of a complex measure by a complex number. That is to say, the set of all complex measures on a measure space (*X*, Σ) forms a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over the complex numbers. Moreover, the *[total variation](wikipedia://en/683561)* $\|\cdot\|$ defined as

$$\|\mu\| = |\mu| (X)\,$$

is a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534), with respect to which the space of complex measures is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989).
