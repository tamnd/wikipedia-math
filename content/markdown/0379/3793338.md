# James Gillogly

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="James Gillogly"}

**James J. Gillogly** (born 5 March 1946) is an American [computer scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_scientist) and [cryptographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptographer).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

### Early life {#Early_life}

His interest in cryptography stems from his boyhood, as did his interest in mathematics.[^o4] By junior high he was inventing his own ciphers and challenging his father, entomologist Lorin Gillogly, to solve them.[^o4]

Gillogly wrote a chess-playing program in the [Fortran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortran) programming language in 1970, and in 1977 he ported the code for "[Colossal Cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colossal_Cave_Adventure)" from Fortran to [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28programming_language%29).

### Education {#Education}

He graduated from [Carnegie Mellon University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Mellon_University) in 1978, receiving a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D.) in computer science. He was advised by [Allen Newell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_Newell), with his dissertation titled "Performance Analysis of the Technology Chess Program".[^o6]

### Career {#Career}

Gillogly worked as a computer scientist at [RAND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAND), specializing in system design and development, and [computer security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_security). He has written several articles about technology and cryptography, is currently the editor of the "Cipher Exchange" column for *[The Cryptogram](wikipedia://en/3793866)*, and was president of the [American Cryptogram Association](wikipedia://en/3793866).

Gillogly was one of the earliest authors of personal computer software, writing utility programs,[^o8] games and a computerized cookbook published by the [Software Toolworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_Toolworks) beginning in 1980.

### Cryptanalysis {#Cryptanalysis}

He is best known for his work solving or debunking some of the world's most famous unsolved codes. In 1980 he wrote a paper on unusual strings in the [Beale ciphers](wikipedia://en/449424), and he received international media attention for being the first person to publicly solve parts 1-3 on the [CIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIA)'s [Kryptos](wikipedia://en/456619) sculpture in 1999. He also coordinates a large mailing list about the ciphers in the [Voynich Manuscript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_Manuscript). On the [PBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Broadcasting_Service) website, they report that he has been called "arguably the best non-government cryptanalyst in the U.S." in the field of classical (historical) cryptosystems.[^o10]

In 1995 he deciphered a text enciphered by [Robert H. Thouless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_H._Thouless) who had hoped the message could prove that the dead could communicate with the living.[^o4] Gillogly wrote his own software to decipher the text, which was in a variant of the [playfair cipher](wikipedia://en/173586).[^o4]
