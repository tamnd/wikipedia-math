# Klee–Minty cube

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Klee–Minty cube"}

*Image: Klee-Minty-cube-for-shadow-vertex-pivot-rule.png.* Klee Minty cube for shadow vertex simplex method.

The **Klee–Minty cube** or **Klee–Minty polytope** (named after [Victor Klee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Klee) and [George J. Minty](wikipedia://en/67693763)) is a [unit](wikipedia://en/407763) [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783) of variable [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) whose corners have been perturbed. Klee and Minty demonstrated that [George Dantzig](wikipedia://en/13111)'s [simplex algorithm](wikipedia://en/349458) has poor worst-case performance when initialized at one corner of their "squashed cube". On the three-dimensional version, the [simplex algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplex_method) and the [criss-cross algorithm](wikipedia://en/31255067) visit all 8 corners in the worst case.

:::

In particular, many optimization [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) for [linear optimization](wikipedia://en/43730) exhibit poor performance when applied to the Klee–Minty cube. In 1973 Klee and Minty showed that Dantzig's [simplex algorithm](wikipedia://en/349458) was not a [polynomial-time algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial-time_algorithm) when applied to their cube.[^n1] Later, modifications of the Klee–Minty cube have shown poor behavior both for other [basis](wikipedia://en/244321)-[exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_algorithm) pivoting algorithms and also for interior-point algorithms.[^n2]

## Description {#Description}

The Klee–Minty cube was originally specified with a parameterized system of linear inequalities, with the dimension as the parameter. The cube in two-dimensional space is a *squashed square*, and the "cube" in three-dimensional space is a *squashed cube*. Illustrations of the "cube" have appeared besides algebraic descriptions.[^o5] The Klee–Minty polytope is given by:[^n3]

$$\begin{aligned} x_1 &\le 5 \\ 4x_1 + x_2 &\le 25 \\ 8x_1 + 4x_2 + x_3 &\le 125 \\ &\vdots \\ 2^Dx_1 + 2^{D-1}x_2 + \dots + 4x_{D-1} + x_D &\le 5^D \\ x_1 \ge 0, \, \, \dots , \, \, x_D &\ge 0. \end{aligned}$$

This has $D$ variables, $D$ constraints other than the $D$ non-negativity constraints, and $2^D$ vertices, just as a $D$-dimensional [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783) does. If the objective function to be maximized is

$$2^{D-1}x_1+2^{D-2}x_2+ \dots + 2x_{D-1}+x_D,$$

and if the initial vertex for the simplex algorithm is the origin, then the algorithm as formulated by Dantzig visits all $2^D$ vertices, finally reaching the optimal vertex $(0, 0, \dots, 5^D)$.

## Computational complexity {#Computational_complexity}

The Klee–Minty cube has been used to analyze the performance of many algorithms, both in the worst case and on average. The [time complexity](wikipedia://en/405944) of an [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) counts the number of [arithmetic operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_operation) sufficient for the algorithm to solve the problem. For example, [Gaussian elimination](wikipedia://en/13035) requires the [order of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_oh) $D^3$ operations, and so it is said to have polynomial time-complexity because its complexity is bounded by a [cubic polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_polynomial). There are examples of algorithms that do not have polynomial-time complexity. For example, a generalization of Gaussian elimination called [Buchberger's algorithm](wikipedia://en/696317) has for its complexity an exponential function of the problem data (the [degree of the polynomials](wikipedia://en/5930652) and the number of variables of the [multivariate polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_polynomial)). Because exponential functions eventually grow much faster than polynomial functions, an exponential complexity implies that an algorithm has slow performance on large problems.

### Worst case {#Worst_case}

*Image: Simplex description.png.* An illustration of a three-dimensional [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) which is the feasible region for a linear programming problem. The simplex algorithm traverses the edges between [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) until it reaches an optimal vertex. In the case shown, the simplex algorithm takes five steps. However, the simplex algorithm visits every vertex in the worst case of a problem whose feasible region is the Klee–Minty cube, so the number of steps rises exponentially with the dimension of the problem.

In mathematical optimization, the Klee–Minty cube is an example that shows the [worst-case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worst-case_complexity) [computational](wikipedia://en/2230) [complexity](wikipedia://en/405944) of many [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) of [linear optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_optimization). It is a deformed [cube](wikipedia://en/407763) with exactly 2<sup>*D*</sup> corners in [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) $D$. Klee and Minty showed that [Dantzig's](wikipedia://en/13111) [simplex algorithm](wikipedia://en/349458) visits all corners of a (perturbed) [cube](wikipedia://en/407763) in dimension $D$ in the [worst case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worst-case_complexity).[^o9]

Modifications of the Klee–Minty construction showed similar exponential [time complexity](wikipedia://en/405944) for other pivoting rules of simplex type, which maintain primal feasibility, such as [Bland's rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bland's_rule).[^o10] Another modification showed that the [criss-cross algorithm](wikipedia://en/31255067), which does not maintain primal feasibility, also visits all the corners of a modified Klee–Minty cube.[^o11] Like the simplex algorithm, the criss-cross algorithm visits all 8 corners of the three-dimensional cube in the worst case.

#### Path-following algorithms {#Path-following_algorithms}

Further modifications of the Klee–Minty cube have shown the poor performance of central-path[–following algorithms](wikipedia://en/1622862) for linear optimization, in that the central path comes arbitrarily close to each of the corners of a cube. This "vertex-stalking" performance is surprising because such path-following algorithms have polynomial-time complexity for linear optimization.[^o13]

### Average case {#Average_case}

The Klee–Minty cube has also inspired research on [average-case complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Average-case_complexity). When eligible pivots are made randomly (and not by the rule of steepest descent), Dantzig's simplex algorithm needs [on average](wikipedia://en/9653) [quadratically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_polynomial) many steps ([on the order of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Oh) $O(D^2)$.[^n4] Standard variants of the simplex algorithm take on average $D$ steps for a cube. However according to (Fukuda Namiki 1994), when it is initialized at a random corner of the cube, the criss-cross algorithm visits only $D$ additional corners.[^o17] Both the simplex algorithm and the criss-cross algorithm visit exactly 3 additional corners of the three-dimensional cube on average.
