# Coxeter–Dynkin diagram

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Coxeter–Dynkin diagram"}

*Image: Finite coxeter.svg.* Coxeter–Dynkin diagrams for the fundamental finite Coxeter groups

*Image: Affine coxeter.svg.* Coxeter–Dynkin diagrams for the fundamental affine Coxeter groups

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **Coxeter–Dynkin diagram** (or **Coxeter diagram**, **Coxeter graph**) is a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) with numerically labeled edges (called **branches**) representing a [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004) or sometimes a [uniform polytope](wikipedia://en/6842392) or [uniform tiling](wikipedia://en/12126635) constructed from the group.

:::

A class of closely related objects is the [Dynkin diagrams](wikipedia://en/277109), which differ from Coxeter diagrams in two respects: firstly, branches labeled "$4$" or greater are [directed](wikipedia://en/19721986), while Coxeter diagrams are [undirected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph); secondly, Dynkin diagrams must satisfy an additional ([crystallographic](wikipedia://en/2218040)) restriction, namely that the only allowed branch labels are $2, 3, 4,$ and $6.$ Dynkin diagrams correspond to and are used to classify [root systems](wikipedia://en/277087) and therefore [semisimple Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/1337587).[^o2]

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Cycloscheme"}

## Description {#Description}

A Coxeter group is a group that admits a presentation:

$$\langle r_0,r_1,\dots,r_n \mid (r_i r_j)^{m_{i,j}} = 1 \rangle$$

where the $m_{i,j}$ are integers that are elements of some [symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/126474) $M$ which has $1$s on its [diagonal](wikipedia://en/349251). (Thus each generator $r_i$ has order 2.) This matrix $M$, the **Coxeter matrix**, completely determines the Coxeter group.

Since the Coxeter matrix is symmetric, it can be viewed as the [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) of an [edge-labeled graph](wikipedia://en/1609861) that has vertices corresponding to the generators $r_{i}$, and edges labeled with $m_{i,j}$ between the vertices corresponding to $r_{i}$ and $r_{j}$. In order to simplify these diagrams, two changes can be made:

- Edges that are labeled with $2$ can be omitted, with the missing edges being implied to be $2$s. A label $2$ indicates that the corresponding two generators commute; $2$ is the smallest number that can be used to label an edge.
- Edges labeled $3$ can be left unlabeled, with the implication that an unlabeled edge acts as a $3$.

The resulting graph is a Coxeter-Dynkin diagram that describes the considered Coxeter group.

:::

## Schläfli matrix {#Schläfli_matrix}

Every Coxeter diagram has a corresponding **Schläfli matrix** (so named after [Ludwig Schläfli](wikipedia://en/1452408)), $A,$ with matrix elements $a_{i,j} = a_{j,i} = -2 \cos(\pi/p_{i,j})$ where $p_{i,j}$ is the branch order between mirrors $i$ and $j;$ that is, $\pi/p_{i,j}$ is the [dihedral angle](wikipedia://en/338046) between mirrors $i$ and $j.$ As a *matrix of cosines*, $A$ is also called a [Gramian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gramian_matrix). All [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004) Schläfli matrices are symmetric because their root vectors are normalized. $A$ is closely related to the [Cartan matrix](wikipedia://en/1146433), used in the similar but directed graph: the [Dynkin diagram](wikipedia://en/277109), in the limited cases of $p = 2,3,4,$ and $6,$ which are generally *not* symmetric.

The determinant of the Schläfli matrix is called the **Schläflian**; the Schläflian and its sign determine whether the group is finite (positive), affine (zero), or indefinite (negative).[^o6] This rule is called **Schläfli's Criterion**.[^o7] Schläfli's Criterion as described in section 7.7 applies only to linear Coxeter diagrams.

The [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) of the Schläfli matrix determine whether a Coxeter group is of *finite type* (all positive), *affine type* (all non-negative, at least one is zero), or *indefinite type* (otherwise). The indefinite type is sometimes further subdivided, e.g. into hyperbolic and other Coxeter groups. However, there are multiple non-equivalent definitions for hyperbolic Coxeter groups. We use the following definitions:

- A Coxeter group with connected diagram is *hyperbolic* if it is neither of finite nor affine type, but every proper connected subdiagram is of finite or affine type.
- A hyperbolic Coxeter group is **compact** if all its subgroups are finite (i.e. have positive determinants), and **paracompact** if all its subgroups are finite or affine (i.e. have nonnegative determinants).

Finite and affine groups are also called *elliptical* and *parabolic* respectively. Hyperbolic groups are also called Lannér, after F. Lannér who enumerated the compact hyperbolic groups in 1950,[^o8] and Koszul (or quasi-Lannér) for the paracompact groups.

### Rank 2 Coxeter groups {#Rank_2_Coxeter_groups}

The type of a rank $2$ Coxeter group, i.e. generated by two different mirrors, is fully determined by the determinant of the Schläfli matrix, as this determinant is simply the product of the eigenvalues: finite (positive determinant), affine (zero determinant), or hyperbolic (negative determinant) type. Coxeter uses an equivalent [bracket notation](wikipedia://en/32088502) which lists sequences of branch orders as a substitute for the node-branch graphic diagrams. Rational solutions $[p/q],$ , also exist, with [$\gcd$](wikipedia://en/12354)$(p,q) = 1;$ these define overlapping fundamental domains. For example, $3/2, 4/3, 5/2, 5/3, 5/4,$ and $6/5.$

The Coxeter–Dynkin diagram can be seen as a graphic description of the [fundamental domain](wikipedia://en/469365) of mirrors. A mirror represents a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) within a spherical, Euclidean, or hyperbolic space of given dimension. (In 2D spaces, a mirror is a line; in 3D, a mirror is a plane.)

These visualizations show the fundamental domains for 2D and 3D Euclidean groups, and for 2D spherical groups. For each, the Coxeter diagram can be deduced by identifying the hyperplane mirrors and labelling their connectivity, ignoring $90$-degree dihedral angles (order $2;$ see footnote \[a\] below).

Coxeter–Dynkin diagrams can explicitly enumerate nearly all classes of [uniform polytope](wikipedia://en/6842392) and [uniform tessellations](wikipedia://en/12126635). Every uniform polytope with pure reflective symmetry (all but a few special cases have pure reflectional symmetry) can be represented by a Coxeter–Dynkin diagram with permutations of *markups*. Each uniform polytope can be generated using such mirrors and a single generator point: mirror images create new points as reflections, then polytope [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034) can be defined between points and a mirror image point. [Faces](wikipedia://en/149926) are generated by the repeated reflection of an edge eventually wrapping around to the original generator; the final shape, as well as any higher-dimensional facets, are likewise created by the face being reflected to enclose an area.

To specify the generating vertex, one or more nodes are marked with rings, meaning that the vertex is *not* on the mirror(s) represented by the ringed node(s). (If two or more mirrors are marked, the vertex is equidistant from them.) A mirror is *active* (creates reflections) only with respect to points not on it. A diagram needs at least one active node to represent a polytope. An unconnected diagram (subgroups separated by order-2 branches, or orthogonal mirrors) requires at least one active node in each subgraph.

All [regular polytopes](wikipedia://en/518693), represented by [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) $\{p, q, r, ...\}$, can have their [fundamental domains](wikipedia://en/469365) represented by a set of *n* mirrors with a related Coxeter–Dynkin diagram of a line of nodes and branches labeled by $p, q, r, ...,$ with the first node ringed.

Uniform polytopes with one ring correspond to generator points at the corners of the fundamental domain simplex. Two rings correspond to the edges of simplex and have a degree of freedom, with only the midpoint as the uniform solution for equal edge lengths. In general *k*-ring generator points are on *(k-1)*-faces of the simplex, and if all the nodes are ringed, the generator point is in the interior of the simplex.

The special case of uniform polytopes with non-reflectional symmetry is represented by a secondary markup where the central dot of a ringed node is removed (called a *hole*). These shapes are [alternations](wikipedia://en/4349117) of polytopes with reflective symmetry, implying that every other vertex is deleted. The resulting polytope will have a subsymmetry of the original [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004). A truncated alternation is called a [*snub*](wikipedia://en/9428917).

- A single node represents a single mirror. This is called group A<sub>1</sub>. If ringed this creates a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) perpendicular to the mirror, represented as {}.
- Two unattached nodes represent two [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) mirrors. If both nodes are ringed, a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048) can be created, or a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) if the point is at equal distance from both mirrors.
- Two nodes attached by an order-*n* branch can create an [*n*-gon](wikipedia://en/23621) if the point is on one mirror, and a 2*n*-gon if the point is off both mirrors. This forms the $I_{1}(n)$ group.
- Two parallel mirrors can represent an infinite polygon $I_{1}(\infty)$ group, also called $\text{Ĩ}_{1}$.
- Three mirrors in a triangle form images seen in a traditional [kaleidoscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaleidoscope) and can be represented by three nodes connected in a triangle. Repeating examples will have branches labeled as (3 3 3), (2 4 4), (2 3 6), although the last two can be drawn as a line (with the *2* branches ignored). These will generate [uniform tilings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiling_by_regular_polygons).
- Three mirrors can generate [uniform polyhedra](wikipedia://en/2537223#%284_3_2%29_Oh_Octahedral_symmetry); including rational numbers gives the set of [Schwarz triangles](wikipedia://en/2069325).
- Three mirrors with one perpendicular to the other two can form the [uniform prisms](wikipedia://en/283007).

The duals of the uniform polytopes are sometimes marked up with a perpendicular slash replacing ringed nodes, and a slash-hole for hole nodes of the snubs. For example, represents a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048) (as two active orthogonal mirrors), and represents its [dual polygon](wikipedia://en/19865549), the [rhombus](wikipedia://en/183884).

## Very-extended Coxeter diagrams {#Very-extended_Coxeter_diagrams}

One usage includes a [**very-extended**](wikipedia://en/277109#Noncompact_%28Over-extended_forms%29) definition from the direct [Dynkin diagram](wikipedia://en/277109) usage which considers affine groups as **extended**, hyperbolic groups **over-extended**, and a third node as **very-extended** simple groups. These extensions are usually marked by an exponent of 1,2, or 3 + symbols for the number of extended nodes. This extending series can be extended backwards, by sequentially removing the nodes from the same position in the graph, although the process stops after removing branching node. The [E<sub>8</sub>](wikipedia://en/5351858) extended family is the most commonly shown example extending backwards from E<sub>3</sub> and forwards to E<sub>11</sub>.

The extending process can define a limited series of Coxeter graphs that progress from finite to affine to hyperbolic to Lorentzian. The determinant of the Cartan matrices determine where the series changes from finite (positive) to affine (zero) to hyperbolic (negative), and ending as a Lorentzian group, containing at least one hyperbolic subgroup.[^o19] The noncrystallographic H<sub>n</sub> groups forms an extended series where H<sub>4</sub> is extended as a compact hyperbolic and over-extended into a Lorentzian group.

The determinant of the Schläfli matrix by rank are:[^o20]

- $\det(A_{1}^{n} = [2^{n-1}]) = 2^{n}$ (finite for all $n$.)
- $\det(A_{n} = [3^{n-1}]) = n + 1$ (finite for all $n$.)
- $\det(B_{n} = [4,3^{n-2}]) = 2$ (finite for all $n$.)
- $\det(D_{n} = [3^{n-3,1,1}]) = 4$ (finite for all $n$.)

Determinants of the Schläfli matrix in exceptional series are:

- $\det(E_{n} = [3^{n-3,2,1}]) = 9 - n$ (finite for $E_{3} = A_{2}A_{1}$, $E_{4} = A_{4}$, $E_{5} = D_{5}$, [$E_{6}$](wikipedia://en/293725), [$E_{7}$](wikipedia://en/649115) and [$E_{8}$](wikipedia://en/647994), affine at [$E_{9}$](wikipedia://en/1800359) ($\tilde{E}_8$), hyperbolic at $E_{10}.)$
- $\det([3^{n-4,3,1}]) = 2(8 - n)$ (finite for $n = 4$ to $7$, affine ($\tilde{E}_7$), and hyperbolic at $n = 8$.)
- $\det([3^{n-4,2,2}]) = 3(7 - n)$ (finite for $n = 4$ to $6$, affine ($\tilde{E}_6$), and hyperbolic at $n = 7$.)
- $\det(F_{n} = [3,4,3^{n-3}]) = 5 - n$ (finite for $F_{3} = B_{3}$ to [$F_{4}$](wikipedia://en/292877), affine at $F_{5}$ ($\tilde{F}_4$), hyperbolic at $F_{6}$.)
- $\det(G_{n} = [6,3^{n-2}]) = 3 - n$ (finite for [$G_{2}$](wikipedia://en/292864), affine at $G_{3}$ ($\tilde{G}_2$), hyperbolic at $G_{4}$.)

A (simply-laced) Coxeter–Dynkin diagram (finite, affine, or hyperbolic) that has a symmetry (satisfying one condition, below) can be quotiented by the symmetry, yielding a new, generally multiply laced diagram, with the process called "folding".[^o28][^o29]

For example, in D<sub>4</sub> folding to G<sub>2</sub>, the edge in G<sub>2</sub> points from the class of the 3 outer nodes (valence 1), to the class of the central node (valence 3). And E<sub>8</sub> folds into 2 copies of H<sub>4</sub>, the second copy scaled by [τ](wikipedia://en/12386).[^o30]

Geometrically this corresponds to [orthogonal projections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_projection) of [uniform polytopes](wikipedia://en/6842392) and tessellations. Notably, any finite simply-laced Coxeter–Dynkin diagram can be folded to I<sub>2</sub>(*h*), where *h* is the [Coxeter number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_number), which corresponds geometrically to a projection to the [Coxeter plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_plane).

## Complex reflections {#Complex_reflections}

Coxeter–Dynkin diagrams have been extended to [complex space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_vector_space), C<sup>n</sup> where nodes are [unitary reflections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_reflection) of period greater than 2. Nodes are labeled by an index, assumed to be 2 for ordinary real reflection if suppressed. Coxeter writes the complex group, p\[q\]r, as diagram .[^o33]

A 1-dimensional *regular [complex polytope](wikipedia://en/8581934)* in $\mathbb{C}^1$ is represented as , having *p* vertices. Its real representation is a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306), {*p*}. Its symmetry is <sub>*p*</sub>\[\] or , order *p*. A [unitary operator](wikipedia://en/225999) generator for is seen as a rotation in $\mathbb{R}^2$ by 2$\pi$/*p* radians [counter clockwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter_clockwise), and a edge is created by sequential applications of a single unitary reflection. A unitary reflection generator for a 1-polytope with *p* vertices is $e^{2\pi i/p} = \cos(2\pi/p) + i \sin(2\pi/p)$. When *p* = 2, the generator is *e*<sup>$\pi$*i*</sup> = −1, the same as a [point reflection](wikipedia://en/12487536) in the real plane.

In a higher polytope, <sub>*p*</sub>{} or represents a *p*-edge element, with a 2-edge, {} or , representing an ordinary real edge between two vertices.

A [regular complex polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_complex_polytope) in $\mathbb{C}^2$, has the form <sub>*p*</sub>{*q*}<sub>*r*</sub> or Coxeter diagram . The symmetry group of a regular complex polygon is not called a [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004), but instead a [Shephard group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shephard_group), a type of [Complex reflection group](wikipedia://en/5381096). The order of <sub>*p*</sub>\[*q*\]<sub>*r*</sub> is $8/q \cdot (1/p+2/q+1/r-1)^{-2}$.[^o38]

The rank 2 Shephard groups are: <sub>2</sub>\[*q*\]<sub>2</sub>, <sub>*p*</sub>\[4\]<sub>2</sub>, <sub>3</sub>\[3\]<sub>3</sub>, <sub>3</sub>\[6\]<sub>2</sub>, <sub>3</sub>\[4\]<sub>3</sub>, <sub>4</sub>\[3\]<sub>4</sub>, <sub>3</sub>\[8\]<sub>2</sub>, <sub>4</sub>\[6\]<sub>2</sub>, <sub>4</sub>\[4\]<sub>3</sub>, <sub>3</sub>\[5\]<sub>3</sub>, <sub>5</sub>\[3\]<sub>5</sub>, <sub>3</sub>\[10\]<sub>2</sub>, <sub>5</sub>\[6\]<sub>2</sub>, and <sub>5</sub>\[4\]<sub>3</sub> or , , , , , , , , , , , , , of order 2*q*, 2*p*<sup>2</sup>, 24, 48, 72, 96, 144, 192, 288, 360, 600, 1200, and 1800 respectively.

The symmetry group <sub>*p*<sub>1</sub></sub>\[*q*\]<sub>*p*<sub>2</sub></sub> is represented by 2 generators R<sub>1</sub>, R<sub>2</sub>, where:

$$R_{1}^{p_{1}} = R_{2}^{p_{2}} = I.$$

If *q* is even, (R<sub>2</sub>R<sub>1</sub>)<sup>*q*/2</sup> = (R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>)<sup>*q*/2</sup>. If *q* is odd, (R<sub>2</sub>R<sub>1</sub>)<sup>(q-1)/2</sup>R<sub>2</sub> = (R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>)<sup>(*q*-1)/2</sup>R<sub>1</sub>. When *q* is odd, *p*<sub>1</sub>=*p*<sub>2</sub>.

The $\mathbb{C}^3$ group or \[1 1 1\]<sup>p</sup> is defined by 3 period 2 unitary reflections {R<sub>1</sub>, R<sub>2</sub>, R<sub>3</sub>}:

R<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup> = R<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup> = R<sub>3</sub><sup>2</sup> = (R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>)<sup>3</sup> = (R<sub>2</sub>R<sub>3</sub>)<sup>3</sup> = (R<sub>3</sub>R<sub>1</sub>)<sup>3</sup> = (R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>R<sub>3</sub>R<sub>1</sub>)<sup>*p*</sup> = 1.

The period *p* can be seen as a [double rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_rotation) in real $\mathbb{R}^4$.

A similar $\mathbb{C}^3$ group or \[1 1 1\]<sup>(p)</sup> is defined by 3 period 2 unitary reflections {R<sub>1</sub>, R<sub>2</sub>, R<sub>3</sub>}:

R<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup> = R<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup> = R<sub>3</sub><sup>2</sup> = (R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>)<sup>3</sup> = (R<sub>2</sub>R<sub>3</sub>)<sup>3</sup> = (R<sub>3</sub>R<sub>1</sub>)<sup>3</sup> = (R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>R<sub>3</sub>R<sub>2</sub>)<sup>*p*</sup> = 1.
