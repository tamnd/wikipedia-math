# Toric section

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Toric section"}

A **toric section** is an intersection of a [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852) with a [torus](wikipedia://en/74800), just as a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673) is the intersection of a [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852) with a [cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_%28geometry%29). Special cases have been known since antiquity, and the general case was studied by [Jean Gaston Darboux](wikipedia://en/1142530).[^o2]

:::

## Mathematical formulae {#Mathematical_formulae}

In general, toric sections are fourth-order ([quartic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartic_curve)) [plane curves](wikipedia://en/971691)[^o2] of the form

$$\left( x^2 + y^2 \right)^2 + a x^2 + b y^2 + cx + dy + e = 0.$$

## Spiric sections {#Spiric_sections}

A special case of a toric section is the [spiric section](wikipedia://en/5300614), in which the intersecting plane is parallel to the rotational symmetry axis of the [torus](wikipedia://en/74800). They were discovered by the ancient Greek geometer [Perseus](wikipedia://en/5300446) in roughly 150 BC.[^o5] Well-known examples include the [hippopede](wikipedia://en/2737959) and the [Cassini oval](wikipedia://en/2040906) and their relatives, such as the [lemniscate of Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/250908).

## Villarceau circles {#Villarceau_circles}

Another special case is the [Villarceau circles](wikipedia://en/1547360), in which the intersection is a circle despite the lack of any of the obvious sorts of symmetry that would entail a circular cross-section.[^o7]

## General toric sections {#General_toric_sections}

More complicated figures such as an [annulus](wikipedia://en/356158) can be created when the intersecting plane is [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) or oblique to the rotational symmetry axis.
