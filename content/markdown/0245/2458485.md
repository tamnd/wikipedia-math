# Conserved quantity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Conserved quantity"}

A **conserved quantity** is a [property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property_%28mathematics%29) or [value](wikipedia://en/4095147) that remains [constant](wikipedia://en/24758132) over time in a [system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System) even when changes occur in the system. In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a conserved quantity of a [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087) is formally defined as a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) of the [dependent variables](wikipedia://en/437701), the value of which remains constant along each [trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectory) of the system.[^o2]

:::

Not all systems have conserved quantities, and conserved quantities are not unique, since one can always produce another such quantity by applying a [suitable function](wikipedia://en/39712387), such as adding a constant, to a conserved quantity.

Since many [laws of physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_of_physics) express some kind of [conservation](wikipedia://en/6956), conserved quantities commonly exist in [mathematical models](wikipedia://en/20590) of [physical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_system). For example, any [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586) model will have [mechanical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_energy#Conservation_of_mechanical_energy) as a conserved quantity as long as the forces involved are [conservative](wikipedia://en/44158).

## Differential equations {#Differential_equations}

Differential equation For a first order system of [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309)

$$\frac{d \mathbf r}{d t} = \mathbf f(\mathbf r, t)$$

where bold indicates [vector](wikipedia://en/32533) quantities, a scalar-valued function *H*(**r**) is a conserved quantity of the system if, for all time and [initial conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_conditions) in some specific domain,

$$\frac{d H}{d t} = 0$$

Note that by using the [multivariate chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113#Multivariable_case),

$$\frac{d H}{d t} = \nabla H \cdot \frac{d \mathbf r}{d t} = \nabla H \cdot \mathbf f(\mathbf r, t)$$

so that the definition may be written as

$$\nabla H \cdot \mathbf f(\mathbf r, t) = 0$$

which contains information specific to the system and can be helpful in finding conserved quantities, or establishing whether or not a conserved quantity exists.

## Hamiltonian mechanics {#Hamiltonian_mechanics}

Hamiltonian mechanics For a system defined by the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319) $\mathcal{H}$, a function *f* of the generalized coordinates *q* and generalized momenta *p* has time evolution

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}t} = \{f, \mathcal{H}\} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}$$

and hence is conserved if and only if $\{f, \mathcal{H}\} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} = 0$. Here $\{f, \mathcal{H}\}$ denotes the [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340).

## Lagrangian mechanics {#Lagrangian_mechanics}

Lagrangian mechanics Suppose a system is defined by the [Lagrangian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_Mechanics) *L* with generalized coordinates *q*. If *L* has no explicit time dependence (so $\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}=0$), then the energy *E* defined by

$$E = \sum_i \left[ \dot q_i \frac{ \partial L}{ \partial \dot q_i} \right] - L$$

is conserved.

Furthermore, if $\frac{\partial L}{\partial q} = 0$, then *q* is said to be a cyclic coordinate and the generalized momentum *p* defined by

$$p = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q}$$

is conserved. This may be derived by using the [Euler–Lagrange equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Lagrange_equations).
