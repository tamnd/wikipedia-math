# List of fallacies

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="List of fallacies"}

A [fallacy](wikipedia://en/53986) is an error in reasoning that undermines an [argument](wikipedia://en/13018310)'s support for its conclusion.[^o2] In academic usage, the term usually applies to arguments, although it is sometimes used more broadly for errors in reasoning in explanations, definitions, questions, or other forms of discourse.[^o3] A fallacious argument may nevertheless have a true conclusion; the defect lies in the inadequate reasoning offered in support of it.[^o3]

:::

Fallacies are commonly divided into [formal](wikipedia://en/3637937) and [informal](wikipedia://en/1655450) fallacies. A formal fallacy is a defect in an argument's logical form that makes a [deductive](wikipedia://en/61093) argument [invalid](wikipedia://en/21304742).[^o2][^o3] Informal fallacies cannot ordinarily be identified from form alone, since their assessment depends on such factors as content, evidence, context, and the purpose of the argument.[^o2][^o3] They are often grouped under headings such as relevance, ambiguity, presumption, faulty generalization, and faulty causal reasoning, although the classification and boundaries of individual fallacies vary among authors.[^o2][^o3]

Fallacies may be used deliberately to persuade, but they can also arise unintentionally through [cognitive bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_bias), ambiguity, or faulty [inference](wikipedia://en/317465).[^o2][^o3] In [deductive reasoning](wikipedia://en/61093), a formal fallacy renders an argument invalid. In non-deductive reasoning, fallacious reasoning may make an argument weak, irrelevant, insufficiently supported, or otherwise inadequate without making its conclusion false.[^o3]

## Formal fallacies {#Formal_fallacies}

A formal fallacy, or *non sequitur*, is an error in the [argument's form](wikipedia://en/3331706).[^n1]

::: {#o6 .entry cue=list-entry name="Appeal to probability"}

- [Appeal to probability](wikipedia://en/915121) – taking something for granted because it would probably be the case (or might possibly be the case).[^o7][^o8]

:::

::: {#o9 .entry cue=list-entry name="Argument from fallacy"}

- [Argument from fallacy](wikipedia://en/234892) (also known as the fallacy fallacy) – the assumption that, if a particular *argument* for a "conclusion" is fallacious, then the *conclusion* by itself is false.[^n2]

:::

::: {#o11 .entry cue=list-entry name="Base rate fallacy"}

- [Base rate fallacy](wikipedia://en/732873) – making a probability judgement based on [conditional probabilities](wikipedia://en/24104134), without taking into account the effect of [prior probabilities](wikipedia://en/472877).[^o12]

:::

::: {#o13 .entry cue=list-entry name="Conjunction fallacy"}

- [Conjunction fallacy](wikipedia://en/732729) – the assumption that an outcome simultaneously satisfying multiple conditions is more probable than an outcome satisfying a single one of them.[^o14]

:::

::: {#o15 .entry cue=list-entry name="Masked-man fallacy"}

- [Masked-man fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masked-man_fallacy) (illicit substitution of identicals) – the substitution of identical designators in a true statement can lead to a false one.[^n3]

:::

### Propositional fallacies {#Propositional_fallacies}

A propositional fallacy is an error that concerns compound propositions. For a compound proposition to be true, the truth values of its constituent parts must satisfy the relevant logical connectives that occur in it (most commonly: \[and\], \[or\], \[not\], \[only if\], \[if and only if\]). The following fallacies involve relations whose truth values are not guaranteed and therefore not guaranteed to yield true conclusions. Types of [propositional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus) fallacies:

::: {#o18 .entry cue=list-entry name="Affirming a disjunct"}

- [Affirming a disjunct](wikipedia://en/708544) – concluding that one disjunct of a [logical disjunction](wikipedia://en/7962) must be false because the other disjunct is true; *A or B; A, therefore not B*.[^n4]

:::

::: {#o20 .entry cue=list-entry name="Affirming the consequent"}

- [Affirming the consequent](wikipedia://en/675) – the [antecedent](wikipedia://en/804755) in an [indicative conditional](wikipedia://en/228776) is claimed to be true because the [consequent](wikipedia://en/804778) is true; *if A, then B; B, therefore A*.[^n4]

:::

::: {#o22 .entry cue=list-entry name="Denying the antecedent"}

- [Denying the antecedent](wikipedia://en/144793) – the [consequent](wikipedia://en/804778) in an [indicative conditional](wikipedia://en/228776) is claimed to be false because the [antecedent](wikipedia://en/804755) is false; *if A, then B; not A, therefore not B*.[^n4]

:::

### Quantification fallacies {#Quantification_fallacies}

A quantification fallacy is an error in logic where the quantifiers of the premises are in contradiction to the quantifier of the conclusion. Types of [quantification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantification_%28logic%29) fallacies:

::: {#o25 .entry cue=list-entry name="Existential fallacy"}

- [Existential fallacy](wikipedia://en/705934) – an argument that has a universal premise and a particular conclusion.[^n5]

:::

### Syllogistic fallacies {#Syllogistic_fallacies}

Logical fallacies that occur in categorical [syllogisms](wikipedia://en/48046).

::: {#o28 .entry cue=list-entry name="Affirmative conclusion from a negative premise"}

- [Affirmative conclusion from a negative premise](wikipedia://en/478094) (illicit negative) – a categorical syllogism has a positive conclusion, but at least one negative premise.[^n5]

:::

::: {#o30 .entry cue=list-entry name="Fallacy of exclusive premises"}

- [Fallacy of exclusive premises](wikipedia://en/733365) – a categorical syllogism that is invalid because both of its premises are negative.[^n5]

:::

::: {#o32 .entry cue=list-entry name="Fallacy of four terms"}

- [Fallacy of four terms](wikipedia://en/698149) (*quaternio terminorum*) – a categorical syllogism that has four terms.[^n6]

:::

::: {#o34 .entry cue=list-entry name="Illicit major"}

- [Illicit major](wikipedia://en/705983) – a categorical syllogism that is invalid because its major term is not [distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_of_terms) in the major premise but distributed in the conclusion.[^n5]

:::

::: {#o36 .entry cue=list-entry name="Illicit minor"}

- [Illicit minor](wikipedia://en/706018) – a categorical syllogism that is invalid because its minor term is not distributed in the minor premise but distributed in the conclusion.[^n5]

:::

::: {#o38 .entry cue=list-entry name="Negative conclusion from affirmative premises"}

- [Negative conclusion from affirmative premises](wikipedia://en/30128635) (illicit affirmative) – a categorical syllogism has a negative conclusion but affirmative premises.[^n5]

:::

::: {#o40 .entry cue=list-entry name="Fallacy of the undistributed middle"}

- [Fallacy of the undistributed middle](wikipedia://en/478089) – the middle term in a categorical syllogism is not distributed.[^n7]

:::

  - [Politician's syllogism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politician's_syllogism)

::: {#o42 .entry cue=list-entry name="Modal fallacy"}

- [Modal fallacy](wikipedia://en/55033847) – confusing necessity with sufficiency. A condition X is necessary for Y if X is required for even the possibility of Y. X does not bring about Y by itself, but if there is no X, there will be no Y. For example, oxygen is necessary for fire. But one cannot assume that everywhere there is oxygen, there is fire. A condition X is sufficient for Y if X, by itself, is enough to bring about Y. For example, riding the bus is a sufficient mode of transportation to get to work. But there are other modes of transportation – car, taxi, bicycle, walking – that can be used.

:::

::: {#o43 .entry cue=list-entry name="Modal scope fallacy"}

- [Modal scope fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_scope_fallacy) – a degree of unwarranted necessity is placed in the conclusion.

:::

## Informal fallacies {#Informal_fallacies}

Informal fallacies – arguments that are logically unsound for lack of well-grounded premises.[^n8]

::: {#o46 .entry cue=list-entry name="Argument from incredulity"}

- [Argument from incredulity](wikipedia://en/4378433) – when someone can't imagine something to be true, and therefore deems it false, or conversely, holds that it must be true because they can't see how it could be false.[^o47]

:::

::: {#o48 .entry cue=list-entry name="Argument to moderation"}

- [Argument to moderation](wikipedia://en/226773) (false compromise, middle ground, fallacy of the mean, argumentum ad temperantiam) – assuming that a compromise between two positions is always correct.[^n9]

:::

::: {#o50 .entry cue=list-entry name="Continuum fallacy"}

- [Continuum fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuum_fallacy) (fallacy of the beard, line-drawing fallacy, sorites fallacy, fallacy of the heap, bald man fallacy, decision-point fallacy) – improperly rejecting a claim for being imprecise.[^n10]

:::

::: {#o52 .entry cue=list-entry name="Correlative-based fallacies"}

- [Correlative-based fallacies](wikipedia://en/238813)

:::

  - [Suppressed correlative](wikipedia://en/238800) – a correlative is redefined so that one alternative is made impossible (e.g., "I am not fat because I am thinner than John.").[^o53]

::: {#o54 .entry cue=list-entry name="Divine fallacy"}

- [Divine fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_fallacy) (argument from incredulity) – arguing that, because something is so phenomenal or amazing, it must be the result of superior, divine, alien or paranormal agency.[^o55]

:::

::: {#o56 .entry cue=list-entry name="Doorman fallacy"}

- [Doorman fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doorman_fallacy) – Reducing a role to its most basic visible/on-paper task without considering the full scope of tangible and intangible value that exists just under the surface, and use that to determine efficiency, cost, and redundancy. Coined by behavioral economist [Rory Sutherland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rory_Sutherland_%28advertising_executive%29) in his 2019 book *Alchemy*. A variant of [Chesterton's fence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chesterton's_fence).

:::

::: {#o57 .entry cue=list-entry name="Double counting"}

- [Double counting](wikipedia://en/6233627) – counting events or occurrences more than once in probabilistic reasoning, which leads to the sum of the probabilities of all cases exceeding [unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unity_%28number%29).

:::

::: {#o58 .entry cue=list-entry name="Ecological fallacy"}

- [Ecological fallacy](wikipedia://en/475598) – inferring about the nature of an entity based solely upon aggregate statistics collected for the group to which that entity belongs.[^n11]

:::

::: {#o60 .entry cue=list-entry name="Equivocation"}

- [Equivocation](wikipedia://en/17690) – using a term with more than one meaning in a statement without specifying which meaning is intended.[^n12]

:::

  - [Ambiguous middle term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambiguous_middle_term) – using a [middle term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_term) with multiple meanings.[^n13]
  - Definitional retreat – changing the meaning of a word when an objection is raised.[^n14] Often paired with moving the goalposts (see below), as when an argument is challenged using a common definition of a term in the argument, and the arguer presents a different definition of the term and thereby demands different evidence to debunk the argument.
  - [Motte-and-bailey fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motte-and-bailey_fallacy) – conflating two positions with similar properties, one modest and easy to defend (the "motte") and one more controversial (the "bailey").[^o64] The arguer first states the controversial position, but when challenged, states that they are advancing the modest position.[^o65][^o66]
  - [Fallacy of accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accent_%28fallacy%29) – changing the meaning of a statement by not specifying on which word emphasis falls.
  - [Persuasive definition](wikipedia://en/958627) – purporting to use the "true" or "commonly accepted" meaning of a term while, in reality, using an uncommon or altered definition.
  - (compare the if-by-whiskey fallacy)

::: {#o67 .entry cue=list-entry name="Etymological fallacy"}

- [Etymological fallacy](wikipedia://en/1971854) – assuming that the original or historical meaning of a word or phrase is necessarily similar to its actual present-day usage.[^n15]

:::

::: {#o69 .entry cue=list-entry name="Fallacy of composition"}

- [Fallacy of composition](wikipedia://en/523043) – assuming that something true of part of a whole must also be true of the whole.[^n16]

:::

::: {#o71 .entry cue=list-entry name="Fallacy of division"}

- [Fallacy of division](wikipedia://en/522936) – assuming that something true of a composite thing must also be true of all or some of its parts.[^n17]

:::

::: {#o73 .entry cue=list-entry name="False attribution"}

- [False attribution](wikipedia://en/7286618) – appealing to an irrelevant, unqualified, unidentified, biased or fabricated source in support of an argument.

:::

  - [Fallacy of quoting out of context](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallacy_of_quoting_out_of_context) (contextotomy, contextomy; quotation mining) – selective excerpting of words from their original context to distort the intended meaning.[^n18]

::: {#o75 .entry cue=list-entry name="False authority"}

- [False authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_authority) (single authority) – using an expert of dubious credentials or using only one opinion to promote a product or idea. Related to the [appeal to authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_authority).

:::

::: {#o76 .entry cue=list-entry name="False dilemma"}

- [False dilemma](wikipedia://en/17687) (false dichotomy, fallacy of bifurcation, black-or-white fallacy) – two alternative statements are given as the only possible options when, in reality, there are more.[^o77]

:::

::: {#o78 .entry cue=list-entry name="False equivalence"}

- [False equivalence](wikipedia://en/33952538) – describing two or more statements as virtually equal when they are not.

:::

::: {#o79 .entry cue=list-entry name="Feedback fallacy"}

- [Feedback fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feedback_fallacy) – believing in the objectivity of an evaluation to be used as the basis for improvement without verifying that the source of the evaluation is a disinterested party.[^o80]

:::

::: {#o81 .entry cue=list-entry name="Association fallacy"}

- Goomba fallacy - treating separate statements of opinion made by different members of a group as if they were collectively held by that group, especially if the statements are contradictory. Often used with reference to fallacious collective attribution of hypocrisy, inconsistency, or flip-flopping to groups, especially online communities. The Goomba fallacy is a subset of the [association fallacy](wikipedia://en/235596).[^o82]

:::

::: {#o83 .entry cue=list-entry name="Historian's fallacy"}

- [Historian's fallacy](wikipedia://en/848901) – assuming that decision-makers of the past had identical information as those subsequently analyzing the decision.[^n19] This is not to be confused with [presentism](wikipedia://en/2687740), in which present-day ideas and perspectives are anachronistically projected into the past.

:::

::: {#o85 .entry cue=list-entry name="Historical fallacy"}

- [Historical fallacy](wikipedia://en/4567185) – believing that certain results occurred only because a specific process was performed, though said process may actually be unrelated to the results.[^o86]

:::

  - [Baconian fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baconian_fallacy) – supposing that historians can obtain the "whole truth" via induction from individual pieces of historical evidence. The "whole truth" is defined as learning "something about everything", "everything about something", or "everything about everything". In reality, a historian "can only hope to know something about something".[^n20]

::: {#o88 .entry cue=list-entry name="Homunculus fallacy"}

- [Homunculus fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homunculus_fallacy) – using a "middle-man" for explanation; this sometimes leads to regressive middle-men. It explains a concept in terms of the concept itself without explaining its real nature (e.g.: explaining thought as something produced by a little thinker – a [homunculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homunculus) – inside the head simply identifies an intermediary actor and does not explain the product or process of thinking).[^n21]

:::

- Inflation of conflict – arguing that, if experts in a field of knowledge disagree on a certain point within that field, no conclusion can be reached or that the legitimacy of that field of knowledge is questionable.[^o90]

::: {#o91 .entry cue=list-entry name="If-by-whiskey"}

- [If-by-whiskey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/If-by-whiskey) – an argument that supports both sides of an issue by using terms that are emotionally sensitive and ambiguous.

:::

::: {#o92 .entry cue=list-entry name="Incomplete comparison"}

- [Incomplete comparison](wikipedia://en/2859475) – insufficient information is provided to make a complete comparison.

:::

::: {#o93 .entry cue=list-entry name="Intentionality fallacy"}

- [Intentionality fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intentional_Fallacy) – the insistence that the ultimate meaning of an expression must be consistent with the intention of the person from whom the communication originated (e.g. a work of fiction that is widely received as a blatant allegory must necessarily not be regarded as such if the author intended it not to be so).[^o94]

:::

::: {#o95 .entry cue=list-entry name="Kettle logic"}

- [Kettle logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kettle_logic) – using multiple, jointly inconsistent arguments to defend a position.

:::

::: {#o96 .entry cue=list-entry name="Ludic fallacy"}

- [Ludic fallacy](wikipedia://en/8810311) – failing to take into account that non-regulated random occurrences [unknown unknowns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unknown_unknowns) can affect the probability of an event taking place.[^o97]

:::

::: {#o98 .entry cue=list-entry name="Lump of labour fallacy"}

- [Lump of labour fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lump_of_labour_fallacy) – the misconception that there is a fixed amount of work to be done within an economy, which can be distributed to create more or fewer jobs.[^o99]

:::

::: {#o100 .entry cue=list-entry name="McNamara fallacy"}

- [McNamara fallacy](wikipedia://en/24290703) (quantitative fallacy) – making an argument using only quantitative observations (measurements, statistical or numerical values) and discounting subjective information that focuses on quality (traits, features, or relationships).

:::

::: {#o101 .entry cue=list-entry name="Mind projection fallacy"}

- [Mind projection fallacy](wikipedia://en/31828038) – assuming that a statement about an object describes an inherent property of the object, rather than a personal perception.

:::

::: {#o102 .entry cue=list-entry name="Moralistic fallacy"}

- [Moralistic fallacy](wikipedia://en/3478014) – inferring factual conclusions from evaluative premises in violation of [fact–value distinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fact–value_distinction) (e.g.: inferring *is* from *ought*). Moralistic fallacy is the inverse of [naturalistic fallacy](wikipedia://en/21826).

:::

::: {#o103 .entry cue=list-entry name="Moving the goalposts"}

- [Moving the goalposts](wikipedia://en/13420314) (raising the bar) – argument in which evidence presented in response to a specific claim is dismissed and some other (often greater) evidence is demanded.

:::

::: {#o104 .entry cue=list-entry name="Nirvana fallacy"}

- [Nirvana fallacy](wikipedia://en/1262979) (perfect-solution fallacy) – solutions to problems are rejected because they are not perfect.

:::

::: {#o105 .entry cue=list-entry name="Package deal"}

- [Package deal](wikipedia://en/227120) – treating essentially dissimilar concepts as though they were essentially similar.

:::

::: {#o106 .entry cue=list-entry name="Proof by assertion"}

- [Proof by assertion](wikipedia://en/2904613) – a proposition is repeatedly restated regardless of contradiction; sometimes confused with [argument from repetition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument_from_repetition) (argumentum ad infinitum, argumentum ad nauseam).

:::

::: {#o107 .entry cue=list-entry name="Prosecutor's fallacy"}

- [Prosecutor's fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosecutor's_fallacy) – a low probability of false matches does not mean a low probability of some false match being found.[^o108][^o109]

:::

::: {#o110 .entry cue=list-entry name="Proving too much"}

- [Proving too much](wikipedia://en/27881045) – an argument that results in an overly generalized conclusion

:::

::: {#o111 .entry cue=list-entry name="Psychologist's fallacy"}

- [Psychologist's fallacy](wikipedia://en/4580818) – an observer presupposes the objectivity of their own perspective when analyzing a behavioral event.

:::

::: {#o112 .entry cue=list-entry name="Referential fallacy"}

- [Referential fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_reference_theory)[^o113] – assuming that all words refer to existing things and that the meaning of words reside within the things they refer to, as opposed to words possibly referring to no real object (e.g.: Pegasus) or that the meaning comes from how they are used (e.g.: "nobody" was in the room).

:::

::: {#o114 .entry cue=list-entry name="Reification"}

- [Reification](wikipedia://en/10822899) (concretism, hypostatization, or the fallacy of misplaced concreteness) – treating an abstract belief or hypothetical construct as if it were a concrete, real event or physical entity (e.g.: saying that evolution selects which traits are passed on to future generations; evolution is not a conscious entity with agency).

:::

::: {#o115 .entry cue=list-entry name="Retrospective determinism"}

- [Retrospective determinism](wikipedia://en/1316670) – believing that, because an event has occurred under some circumstance, the circumstance must have made the event inevitable (e.g.: because someone won the lottery while wearing their lucky socks, wearing those socks made winning the lottery inevitable).

:::

::: {#o116 .entry cue=list-entry name="Slippery slope"}

- [Slippery slope](wikipedia://en/17696) (thin edge of the wedge, [camel's nose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camel's_nose)) – asserting that a proposed, relatively small, first action will inevitably lead to a chain of related events resulting in a significant and negative event and, therefore, should not be permitted.[^n22]

:::

::: {#o118 .entry cue=list-entry name="Special pleading"}

- [Special pleading](wikipedia://en/106203) – the arguer attempts to cite something as an exemption to a generally accepted rule or principle without justifying the exemption (e.g.: an orphaned defendant who murdered their parents asking for leniency).

:::

### Improper premise {#Improper_premise}

::: {#o120 .entry cue=list-entry name="Begging the question"}

- [Begging the question](wikipedia://en/43582) (petitio principii) – using the conclusion of the argument in support of itself in a premise (e.g.: saying that smoking cigarettes is deadly because cigarettes can kill you; something that kills is deadly).[^o121][^o122]

:::

  - [Circular reasoning](wikipedia://en/313944) (circulus in demonstrando) – the reasoner begins with what they are trying to end up with (e.g.: The Bible is the word of God because it says so in the Bible.' or 'The Quran is true because it says so in the Quran').[^o123][^o124]
  - Loaded label – while not inherently fallacious, the use of evocative terms to support a conclusion is a type of begging the question fallacy. When fallaciously used, the term's connotations are relied on to sway the argument towards a particular conclusion. For example, in an organic foods advertisement that says "Organic foods are safe and healthy foods grown without any pesticides, herbicides, or other unhealthy additives", the terms "safe" and "healthy" are used to fallaciously imply that non-organic foods are neither safe nor healthy.[^o125]

::: {#o126 .entry cue=list-entry name="Fallacy of many questions"}

- [Fallacy of many questions](wikipedia://en/17688) (complex question, fallacy of presuppositions, loaded question, plurium interrogationum) – someone asks a question that presupposes something that has not been proven or accepted by all the people involved. This fallacy is often used rhetorically so that the question limits direct replies to those that serve the questioner's agenda. (E.g., "Have you or have you not stopped beating your wife?".)

:::

### Faulty Generalizations {#Faulty_Generalizations}

[Faulty generalization](wikipedia://en/228851) – reaching a conclusion from weak premises.

::: {#o128 .entry cue=list-entry name="Accident"}

- [Accident](wikipedia://en/735233) – an exception to a generalization is ignored.[^n23]

:::

  - [No true Scotsman](wikipedia://en/17694) (aka appeal to purity) – makes a generalization true by changing the generalization to exclude a counterexample.[^n24]

::: {#o131 .entry cue=list-entry name="Cherry picking"}

- [Cherry picking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherry_picking_%28fallacy%29) (suppressed evidence, incomplete evidence, argument by half-truth, fallacy of exclusion, card stacking, slanting) – using individual cases or data that confirm a particular position, while ignoring related cases or data that may contradict that position.[^n25][^o133]

:::

  - [Nut-picking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nut-picking) (suppressed evidence, incomplete evidence) – intentionally seeking out extremely fringe, non-representative statements from members of an opposing group and parading these as evidence of that entire group's incompetence or irrationality.
  - [Survivorship bias](wikipedia://en/1745325) – a small number of successes of a given process are actively promoted while completely ignoring a large number of failures.

::: {#o134 .entry cue=list-entry name="False analogy"}

- [False analogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_analogy) – an argument by analogy in which the analogy is poorly suited.[^n26]

:::

::: {#o136 .entry cue=list-entry name="Hasty generalization"}

- [Hasty generalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasty_generalization) (fallacy of insufficient statistics, fallacy of insufficient sample, fallacy of the lonely fact, hasty induction, *secundum quid*, converse accident, [jumping to conclusions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jumping_to_conclusions)) – basing a broad conclusion on a small or unrepresentative sample.[^n27]

:::

::: {#o138 .entry cue=list-entry name="Anecdotal evidence"}

- Argument from anecdote or anecdotal fallacy – a fallacy where [anecdotal evidence](wikipedia://en/481925) is presented as an argument; without any other contributory evidence or reasoning.

:::

::: {#o139 .entry cue=list-entry name="Inductive fallacy"}

- [Inductive fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_fallacy) – a more general name for a class of fallacies, including hasty generalization and its relatives. A fallacy of induction happens when a conclusion is drawn from premises that only lightly support it.

:::

::: {#o140 .entry cue=list-entry name="Overwhelming exception"}

- [Overwhelming exception](wikipedia://en/228860) – an accurate generalization that comes with qualifications that eliminate so many cases that what remains is much less impressive than the initial statement might have led one to assume.[^n28]

:::

::: {#o142 .entry cue=list-entry name="Fallacy of exhaustive effort"}

- [Fallacy of exhaustive effort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallacy_of_exhaustive_effort) – assuming that, when one or more conditions for an optimal outcome cannot be met, satisfying as many of the remaining conditions as possible must yield the second-best outcome. In reality, because the conditions are typically interdependent, the second-best optimum may require violating all remaining conditions, and piecemeal satisfaction of individual conditions can worsen the outcome relative to doing nothing.[^o143]

:::

::: {#o144 .entry cue=list-entry name="Thought-terminating cliché"}

- [Thought-terminating cliché](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought-terminating_cliché) – a commonly used phrase, sometimes passing as folk wisdom, used to quell [cognitive dissonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_dissonance), conceal lack of forethought, move on to other topics, etc. – but in any case, to end the debate with a cliché rather than a point.

:::

### Questionable cause {#Questionable_cause}

[Questionable cause](wikipedia://en/235588) is a general type of error with many variants. Its primary basis is the confusion of association with causation, either by inappropriately deducing (or rejecting) causation or a broader failure to properly investigate the cause of an observed effect.

- [Cum hoc ergo propter hoc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cum_hoc_ergo_propter_hoc) (Latin for 'with this, therefore because of this'; correlation implies causation; faulty cause/effect, coincidental correlation, correlation without causation) – a faulty assumption that, because there is a correlation between two variables, one caused the other.[^n29]
  - [Post hoc ergo propter hoc](wikipedia://en/174725) (Latin for 'after this, therefore because of this'; temporal sequence implies causation) – X happened, then Y happened; therefore X caused Y.[^n30]
  - [Wrong direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrong_direction) (reverse causation) – cause and effect are reversed. The cause is said to be the effect and vice versa.[^n31] The consequence of the phenomenon is claimed to be its root cause.
  - [Ignoring a common cause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignoring_a_common_cause)

::: {#o149 .entry cue=list-entry name="Fallacy of the single cause"}

- [Fallacy of the single cause](wikipedia://en/599252) (causal oversimplification[^n32]) – it is assumed that there is one, simple cause of an outcome when in reality it may have been caused by a number of only jointly sufficient causes.

:::

::: {#o151 .entry cue=list-entry name="Furtive fallacy"}

- [Furtive fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Furtive_fallacy) – outcomes are asserted to have been caused by the malfeasance of decision makers.

:::

::: {#o152 .entry cue=list-entry name="Magical thinking"}

- [Magical thinking](wikipedia://en/185307) – fallacious attribution of causal relationships between actions and events. In [anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology), it refers primarily to cultural beliefs that ritual, prayer, sacrifice, and taboos will produce specific supernatural consequences. In [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology), it refers to an irrational belief that thoughts by themselves can affect the world or that thinking something corresponds with doing it.

:::

#### Statistical fallacies {#Statistical_fallacies}

::: {#o154 .entry cue=list-entry name="Observational interpretation fallacy"}

- [Observational interpretation fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_interpretation_fallacy) – when associations identified in observational studies are misinterpreted as causal relationships.

:::

::: {#o155 .entry cue=list-entry name="Regression fallacy"}

- [Regression fallacy](wikipedia://en/476228) – ascribes cause where none exists. The flaw is failing to account for natural fluctuations. It is frequently a special kind of *post hoc* fallacy.

:::

::: {#o156 .entry cue=list-entry name="Gambler's fallacy"}

- [Gambler's fallacy](wikipedia://en/12970) – the incorrect belief that separate, independent events can affect the likelihood of another random event. If a fair coin lands on heads 10 times in a row, the belief that it is "due to the number of times it had previously landed on tails" is incorrect.[^n33]

:::

  - [Inverse gambler's fallacy](wikipedia://en/91388) – the inverse of the gambler's fallacy. It is the incorrect belief that on the basis of an unlikely outcome, the process must have happened many times before.

::: {#o158 .entry cue=list-entry name="P-hacking"}

- [p-hacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-hacking) – belief in the significance of a result, not realizing that multiple comparisons or experiments have been run and only the most significant were published.

:::

::: {#o159 .entry cue=list-entry name="Garden of forking paths fallacy"}

- [Garden of forking paths fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_forking_paths_fallacy) – incorrect belief that a single experiment can not be subject to the multiple comparisons effect.

:::

::: {#o160 .entry cue=list-entry name="Sunk costs fallacy"}

- [Sunk costs fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunk_costs_fallacy) - refusal to leave a situation because you have already put large amounts of time or effort into it- for example trying to jump over a wall you physically cannot jump over because you have already spent an hour trying to jump.

:::

### Relevance fallacies {#Relevance_fallacies}

::: {#o162 .entry cue=list-entry name="Appeal to the stone"}

- [Appeal to the stone](wikipedia://en/7972835) (argumentum ad lapidem) – dismissing a claim as absurd without demonstrating proof for its absurdity.[^o163]

:::

::: {#o164 .entry cue=list-entry name="Invincible ignorance"}

- [Invincible ignorance](wikipedia://en/22097838) (argument by pigheadedness) – where a person simply refuses to believe the argument, ignoring any evidence given.[^o165]

:::

::: {#o166 .entry cue=list-entry name="Argument from ignorance"}

- [Argument from ignorance](wikipedia://en/17699) (appeal to ignorance, *argumentum ad ignorantiam*) – assuming that a claim is true because it has not been or cannot be proven false, or vice versa.[^n34]

:::

::: {#o168 .entry cue=list-entry name="Argument from incredulity"}

- [Argument from incredulity](wikipedia://en/4378433) (appeal to common sense) – "I cannot imagine how this could be true; therefore, it must be false."[^o169]

:::

::: {#o170 .entry cue=list-entry name="Argument from repetition"}

- [Argument from repetition](wikipedia://en/224553) (argumentum ad nauseam or argumentum ad infinitum) – repeating an argument until nobody cares to discuss it any more and referencing that lack of objection as evidence of support for the truth of the conclusion;[^o171][^o172] sometimes confused with [proof by assertion](wikipedia://en/2904613).

:::

::: {#o173 .entry cue=list-entry name="Argument from silence"}

- [Argument from silence](wikipedia://en/218567) (argumentum ex silentio) – assuming that a claim is true based on the absence of textual or spoken evidence from an authoritative source, or vice versa.[^o174]

:::

- [Ignoratio elenchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignoratio_elenchi) (irrelevant conclusion, missing the point) – an argument that may in itself be valid, but does not address the issue in question.[^n35]

#### Red herring fallacies {#Red_herring_fallacies}

A red herring fallacy, one of the main subtypes of fallacies of relevance, is an error in logic where a proposition is, or is intended to be, misleading in order to make irrelevant or false inferences. This includes any logical inference based on fake arguments, intended to replace the lack of real arguments or to replace implicitly the subject of the discussion.[^n36][^o178]

[Red herring](wikipedia://en/3197900) – introducing a second argument in response to the first argument that is irrelevant and draws attention away from the original topic (e.g.: saying "If you want to complain about the dishes I leave in the sink, what about the dirty clothes you leave in the bathroom?").[^n37] In [jury trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jury_trial), it is known as a [Chewbacca defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chewbacca_defense). In political strategy, it is called a [dead cat strategy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_cat_strategy). See also [irrelevant conclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignoratio_elenchi).

- [Ad hominem](wikipedia://en/2226) – attacking the arguer instead of the argument. (Note that "ad hominem" can also refer to the dialectical strategy of arguing on the basis of the opponent's own commitments. This type of ad hominem is not a fallacy.)
  - [Circumstantial ad hominem](wikipedia://en/2226#Circumstantial) – stating that the arguer's personal situation or perceived benefit from advancing a conclusion means that their conclusion is wrong.[^o180]
  - [Poisoning the well](wikipedia://en/195215) – a subtype of ad hominem presenting adverse information about a target person with the intention of discrediting everything that the target person says.[^n38]
  - [Appeal to motive](wikipedia://en/985983) – dismissing an idea by questioning the motives of its proposer.
  - [Tone policing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tone_policing) – focusing on emotion behind (or resulting from) a message rather than the message itself as a discrediting tactic.
  - [Traitorous critic fallacy](wikipedia://en/46351393) (ergo decedo, 'therefore I leave') – a critic's perceived affiliation is portrayed as the underlying reason for the criticism and the critic is asked to stay away from the issue altogether. Easily confused with the [association fallacy](wikipedia://en/235596) (guilt by association) below.
  - [Bulverism](wikipedia://en/8257136) (also known as a psychogenetic or "bias" fallacy) – inferring from the *psychological source* or *motivation* an argument or claim that it is invalid. For example, it is fallacious to infer that an idea is false solely because it comes from a biased mind or process.[^o182]

::: {#o183 .entry cue=list-entry name="Appeal to authority"}

- [Appeal to authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_authority) (argument from authority, argumentum ad verecundiam) – an assertion is deemed true because of the position or authority of the person asserting it.[^n39][^n40]

:::

  - [Appeal to accomplishment](wikipedia://en/27388833) – an assertion is deemed true or false based on the accomplishments of the proposer. This may often also have elements of appeal to emotion see below.
  - [Courtier's reply](wikipedia://en/28138716) – a criticism is dismissed by claiming that the critic lacks sufficient knowledge, credentials, or training to credibly comment on the subject matter.

::: {#o186 .entry cue=list-entry name="Appeal to consequences"}

- [Appeal to consequences](wikipedia://en/224012) (argumentum ad consequentiam) – the conclusion is supported by a premise that asserts positive or negative consequences from some course of action in an attempt to distract from the initial discussion.[^n41]

:::

::: {#o188 .entry cue=list-entry name="Appeal to emotion"}

- [Appeal to emotion](wikipedia://en/219718) – manipulating the emotions of the listener rather than using valid reasoning to obtain common agreement.[^n42]

:::

  - [Appeal to fear](wikipedia://en/219428) – generating distress, anxiety, cynicism, or prejudice towards the opponent in an argument.[^o190]
  - [Appeal to flattery](wikipedia://en/219448) – using excessive or insincere praise to obtain common agreement.[^n43]
  - [Appeal to pity](wikipedia://en/219684) (argumentum ad misericordiam) – generating feelings of sympathy or mercy in the listener to obtain common agreement.[^n44]
  - [Appeal to ridicule](wikipedia://en/235543) – mocking or stating that the opponent's position is laughable to deflect from the merits of the opponent's argument.[^o193]
  - [Appeal to spite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_spite) – generating bitterness or hostility in the listener toward an opponent in an argument.[^o194]
  - Judgmental language – using insulting or pejorative language in an argument.
  - [Pooh-pooh](wikipedia://en/48539247) – stating that an opponent's argument is unworthy of consideration.[^o195]
  - Style over substance – embellishing an argument with compelling language, exploiting a bias towards the esthetic qualities of an argument, e.g. the [rhyme-as-reason effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhyme-as-reason_effect)[^o196]
  - [Wishful thinking](wikipedia://en/386062) – arguing for a course of action by the listener according to what might be pleasing to imagine rather than according to evidence or reason.[^n45]

::: {#o198 .entry cue=list-entry name="Appeal to nature"}

- [Appeal to nature](wikipedia://en/4791872) – judgment is based solely on whether the subject of judgment is 'natural' or 'unnatural'.[^n46] (Sometimes also called the "naturalistic fallacy", but is not to be confused with the other fallacies by that name.)

:::

::: {#o200 .entry cue=list-entry name="Appeal to novelty"}

- [Appeal to novelty](wikipedia://en/219435) (argumentum novitatis, argumentum ad antiquitatis) – a proposal is claimed to be superior or better solely because it is new or modern.[^n47] (opposite of [appeal to tradition](wikipedia://en/173387))

:::

::: {#o202 .entry cue=list-entry name="Appeal to poverty"}

- [Appeal to poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_poverty) (argumentum ad Lazarum) – supporting a conclusion because the arguer is poor (or refuting because the arguer is wealthy). (Opposite of [appeal to wealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_wealth).)[^n48]

:::

::: {#o204 .entry cue=list-entry name="Appeal to tradition"}

- [Appeal to tradition](wikipedia://en/173387) (argumentum ad antiquitatem) – a conclusion supported solely because it has long been held to be true.[^n49]

:::

::: {#o206 .entry cue=list-entry name="Appeal to wealth"}

- [Appeal to wealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_wealth) (argumentum ad crumenam) – supporting a conclusion because the arguer is wealthy (or refuting because the arguer is poor).[^n50] (Sometimes taken together with the [appeal to poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_poverty) as a general appeal to the arguer's financial situation.)

:::

- [Argumentum ad baculum](wikipedia://en/39061) (appeal to the stick, appeal to force, appeal to threat) – an argument made through coercion or threats of force to support position.[^n51]
- [Argumentum ad populum](wikipedia://en/30874633) (appeal to widespread belief, bandwagon argument, appeal to the majority, appeal to the people) – a proposition is claimed to be true or good solely because a majority or many people believe it to be so.[^o209]

::: {#o210 .entry cue=list-entry name="Association fallacy"}

- [Association fallacy](wikipedia://en/235596) (guilt by association and honor by association) – arguing that because two things share (or are implied to share) some property, they are the same.[^n52]

:::

::: {#o212 .entry cue=list-entry name="nit-picking"}

- Logic chopping fallacy ([nit-picking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitpicking), [trivial objections](wikipedia://en/1200277)) – Focusing on trivial details of an argument, rather than the main point of the argumentation.[^o213][^o214]

:::

- [Ipse dixit](wikipedia://en/18152714) (bare assertion fallacy) – a claim that is presented as true without support, as self-evidently true, or as dogmatically true. This fallacy relies on the implied expertise of the speaker or on an unstated truism.[^o215][^o216][^o217]

::: {#o218 .entry cue=list-entry name="Chronological snobbery"}

- [Chronological snobbery](wikipedia://en/2432024) – a thesis is deemed incorrect because it was commonly held when something else, known to be false, was also commonly held.[^o219][^o220]

:::

::: {#o221 .entry cue=list-entry name="First World problem"}

- Fallacy of relative privation (also known as "appeal to worse problems" or "not as bad as") – dismissing an argument or complaint due to what are perceived to be more important problems. [First World problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_problem) are a subset of this fallacy.[^o222][^o223]

:::

::: {#o224 .entry cue=list-entry name="Genetic fallacy"}

- [Genetic fallacy](wikipedia://en/157941) – a conclusion is suggested based solely on something or someone's origin rather than its current meaning or context.[^n53]

:::

::: {#o226 .entry cue=list-entry name="I'm entitled to my opinion"}

- [I'm entitled to my opinion](wikipedia://en/9540357) – a person discredits any opposition by claiming that they are entitled to their opinion.

:::

::: {#o227 .entry cue=list-entry name="Moralistic fallacy"}

- [Moralistic fallacy](wikipedia://en/3478014) – inferring factual conclusions from evaluative premises, in violation of [fact–value distinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fact–value_distinction); e.g. making statements about what is, on the basis of claims about what ought to be. This is the inverse of the naturalistic fallacy.

:::

::: {#o228 .entry cue=list-entry name="Naturalistic fallacy"}

- [Naturalistic fallacy](wikipedia://en/21826) – inferring evaluative conclusions from purely factual premises[^n54][^o230] in violation of [fact–value distinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fact–value_distinction). Naturalistic fallacy (sometimes confused with [appeal to nature](wikipedia://en/4791872)) is the inverse of [moralistic fallacy](wikipedia://en/3478014).

:::

  - [Is–ought fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Is–ought_problem)[^n55] – deduce a conclusion about what ought to be, on the basis of what is.

::: {#o232 .entry cue=list-entry name="Argument from fallacy"}

- *Naturalistic fallacy* fallacy[^o233] (anti-naturalistic fallacy)[^o234] – inferring an impossibility to infer any instance of *ought* from *is* from the general invalidity of *is-ought fallacy*, mentioned above. For instance, *is* $P \lor \neg P$ does imply *ought* $P \lor \neg P$ for any proposition $P$, although the *naturalistic fallacy* fallacy would falsely declare such an inference invalid. *Naturalistic fallacy* fallacy is a type of [argument from fallacy](wikipedia://en/234892).

:::

::: {#o235 .entry cue=list-entry name="Straw man"}

- [Straw man](wikipedia://en/39057) fallacy – refuting an argument different from the one actually under discussion, while not recognizing or acknowledging the distinction.[^o236]

:::

::: {#o237 .entry cue=list-entry name="Texas sharpshooter fallacy"}

- [Texas sharpshooter fallacy](wikipedia://en/476197) – improperly asserting a cause to explain a cluster of data.[^n56]

:::

::: {#o239 .entry cue=list-entry name="Whataboutism"}

- [Tu quoque](wikipedia://en/1090534) ('you too' – appeal to hypocrisy, [whataboutism](wikipedia://en/35842043)) – stating that a position is false, wrong, or should be disregarded because its proponent fails to act consistently in accordance with it.[^n57]

:::

::: {#o241 .entry cue=list-entry name="Two wrongs make a right"}

- [Two wrongs make a right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_wrongs_make_a_right) – assuming that, if one wrong is committed, another wrong will rectify it.[^n58]

:::

::: {#o243 .entry cue=list-entry name="Vacuous truth"}

- [Vacuous truth](wikipedia://en/51672) – a claim that is technically true but meaningless, in the form no *A* in *B* has *C*, when there is no *A* in *B*. For example, claiming that no mobile phones in the room are on when there are no mobile phones in the room.

:::
