# Cartesian oval

*Image: Exemplo-de-ovais-de-descartes.png.* Example of Cartesian ovals.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cartesian oval"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **Cartesian [oval](wikipedia://en/2027249)** is a [plane curve](wikipedia://en/971691) consisting of points that have the same [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of distances from two fixed points ([foci](wikipedia://en/1032607)). These curves are named after French mathematician [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525), who used them in [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $P$ and $Q$ be fixed points in the plane, and let $d(P, S)$ and $d(Q, S)$ denote the [Euclidean distances](wikipedia://en/53932) from these points to a third variable point $S$. Let $m$ and $a$ be arbitrary [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438). Then the Cartesian oval is the [locus](wikipedia://en/542465) of points *S* satisfying $d(P, S) + m\,d(Q, S) = a$. The two ovals formed by the four equations $d(P, S) + m\,d(Q, S) = \pm\,a$ and $d(P, S) - m\,d(Q, S) = \pm\,a$ are closely related; together they form a [quartic plane curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartic_plane_curve) called the **ovals of Descartes**.

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Special cases"}

## Special cases {#Special_cases}

In the equation $d(P, S) + m\,d(Q, S) = a$, when $m = 1$ and $a > d(P, Q)$ the resulting shape is an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277). In the [limiting case](wikipedia://en/40400729) in which *P* and *Q* coincide, the ellipse becomes a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220). When $m = a/\!\operatorname{d}(P, Q)$ it is a [limaçon](wikipedia://en/641715) of Pascal. If $m = -1$ and $0 < a < \operatorname{d}(P, Q)$ the equation gives a branch of a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) and thus is not a closed oval.

:::

## Polynomial equation {#Polynomial_equation}

The [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of points $(x, y)$ satisfying the [quartic polynomial equation](wikipedia://en/251591)[^o4][^o8]

$$\left[(1-m^2)(x^2 + y^2) + 2m^2 cx + a^2 - m^2 c^2\right]^2 = 4a^2 (x^2+y^2)$$

where $c$ is the distance $\text{d}(P,Q)$ between the two fixed [foci](wikipedia://en/1032607) $P = (0, 0)$ and $Q = (c, 0)$, forms two ovals, the sets of points satisfying two of the following four equations

$$\operatorname{d}(P, S) \pm m \operatorname{d}(Q, S) = a \,$$

$\operatorname{d}(P, S) \pm m \operatorname{d}(Q, S) = -a \,$[^o8]

that have real solutions. The two ovals are generally [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359), except in the case that $P$ or $Q$ belongs to them. At least one of the two perpendiculars to $PQ$ through points $P$ and $Q$ cuts this quartic curve in four real points; it follows from this that they are necessarily nested, with at least one of the two points $P$ and $Q$ contained in the interiors of both of them.[^o8] For a different parametrization and resulting quartic, see Lawrence.[^o9]

## Applications in optics {#Applications_in_optics}

As Descartes discovered, Cartesian ovals may be used in [lens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_%28optics%29) design. By choosing the ratio of distances from $P$ and $Q$ to match the ratio of [sines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) in [Snell's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snell's_law), and using the [surface of revolution](wikipedia://en/329549) of one of these ovals, it is possible to design a so-called [aplanatic lens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aplanatic_lens), that has no [spherical aberration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_aberration).[^o11]

Additionally, if a spherical wavefront is refracted through a spherical lens, or reflected from a concave spherical surface, the refracted or reflected wavefront takes on the shape of a Cartesian oval. The [caustic](wikipedia://en/3498698) formed by spherical aberration in this case may therefore be described as the [evolute](wikipedia://en/842387) of a Cartesian oval.[^o12]

## History {#History}

The ovals of Descartes were first studied by René Descartes in 1637, in connection with their applications in optics.

These curves were also studied by [Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) beginning in 1664. One method of drawing certain specific Cartesian ovals, already used by Descartes, is analogous to a standard construction of an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) by a pinned thread. If one stretches a thread from a pin at one [focus](wikipedia://en/1032607) to wrap around a pin at a second focus, and ties the free end of the thread to a pen, the path taken by the pen, when the thread is stretched tight, forms a Cartesian oval with a 2:1 ratio between the distances from the two foci.[^o16] However, Newton rejected such constructions as insufficiently [rigorous](wikipedia://en/655974#In_specific_disciplines).[^o14] He defined the oval as the solution to a [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309), constructed its [subnormals](wikipedia://en/472551), and again investigated its optical properties.[^o15]

The French mathematician [Michel Chasles](wikipedia://en/2252716) discovered in the 19th century that, if a Cartesian oval is defined by two points $P$ and $Q$, then there is in general a third point $R$ on the same line such that the same oval is also defined by any pair of these three points.[^o8]

[James Clerk Maxwell](wikipedia://en/28989696) rediscovered these curves, generalized them to curves defined by keeping constant the weighted sum of distances from three or more foci, and wrote a paper titled *Observations on Circumscribed Figures Having a Plurality of Foci, and Radii of Various Proportions*. An account of his results, titled *On the description of oval curves, and those having a plurality of foci*, was written by [J.D. Forbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_David_Forbes) and presented to the [Royal Society of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Edinburgh) in 1846, when Maxwell was at the young age of 14 (almost 15).[^o16][^o17][^o18]
