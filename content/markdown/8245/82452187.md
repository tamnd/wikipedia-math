# Absorbing boundary condition

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Absorbing boundary condition"}

In [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506) of [wave](wikipedia://en/33691) problems, **absorbing boundary conditions**, **non-reflecting boundary conditions**[^o9] or **transmitting boundaries**[^o15] are artificial [boundary conditions](wikipedia://en/436779) applied at the edges of a finite computational domain to allow outgoing [waves](wikipedia://en/33516) to pass out of the grid without generating reflections.

:::

In many physical problems, such as [acoustics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustics), [electromagnetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetics), and [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034), waves naturally propagate into an infinite or semi-infinite space. However, numerical methods like [finite difference](wikipedia://en/6054681) or [finite element methods](wikipedia://en/18233581) require a finite, truncated grid to remain computationally feasible. Without an effective absorbing boundary condition, waves reaching the artificial boundary of the simulation would reflect back into the interior, causing non-physical interference and spurious echoes that contaminate the results.

## Background and history {#Background_and_history}

A perfect absorbing boundary condition would be [nonlocal](wikipedia://en/61563184), meaning that the behavior at one point on the boundary depends on the history of the wave field at all other points on the boundary. While mathematically exact, these non-local conditions are often too computationally expensive for large-scale simulations. Consequently, most practical absorbing boundary conditions utilize local approximations based on the differential properties of the wave field.[^o9][^o15] Beyond purely mathematical boundary operators, many implementations utilize material absorbers such as the [perfectly matched layer](wikipedia://en/4262792), which simulate an artificial physical region where waves are gradually attenuated through absorptive properties before they reach the simulation's edge.[^o20][^o12][^o14]

Early absorbing boundary conditions, adopted until the 1970s, were based on the application of [Sommerfeld radiation condition](wikipedia://en/6226425); these are denoted as the zeroth-order boundary conditions. From the late 1970s to the mid-1980s, low-order absorbing boundary conditions, such as Bayliss–Turkel and [Engquist–Majda absorbing boundary conditions](wikipedia://en/82425322) were introduced.[^o10] The 1990s saw the introduction of perfectly matched layers,[^o7] as well as higher-order local boundary conditions.[^o10]
