# Transposable integer

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Transposable integer"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **transposable integers** are integers that [permute](wikipedia://en/449166) or [shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_shift) cyclically when they are multiplied by another integer $n$. Examples are:

:::

- 142857 × 3 = 428571 (shifts cyclically one place left)
- 142857 × 5 = 714285 (shifts cyclically one place right)
- 128205 × 4 = 512820 (shifts cyclically one place right)
- 076923 × 9 = 692307 (shifts cyclically two places left)

These transposable integers can be but are not always [cyclic numbers](wikipedia://en/1672489). The characterization of such numbers can be done using [repeating decimals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repeating_decimals) (and thus the related fractions), or directly.

## General {#General}

For any [integer coprime](wikipedia://en/6556) to 10, its reciprocal is a repeating decimal without any non-recurring digits. E.g. $\tfrac{1}{143}$ = 0.006993006993006993...

While the expression of a single series with [vinculum](wikipedia://en/673457) on top is adequate, the intention of the above expression is to show that the six [cyclic permutations](wikipedia://en/449166) of 006993 can be obtained from this repeating decimal if we select six consecutive digits from the repeating decimal starting from different digits.

This illustrates that cyclic permutations are somehow related to repeating decimals and the corresponding fractions.

The [greatest common divisor (gcd)](wikipedia://en/12354) between any cyclic permutation of an *m*-digit integer and 10<sup>*m*</sup> − 1 is constant. Expressed as a formula,

$$\gcd\left(N,10^m-1\right)=\gcd\left(N_c,10^m-1\right),$$

where *N* is an *m*-digit integer; and *N<sub>c</sub>* is any cyclic permutation of *N*.

For example,

gcd(091575, 999999) = gcd(3<sup>2</sup>×5<sup>2</sup>×11×37, 3<sup>3</sup>×7×11×13×37) = 3663 = gcd(915750, 999999) = gcd(157509, 999999) = gcd(575091, 999999) = gcd(750915, 999999) = gcd(509157, 999999)

If *N* is an *m*-digit integer, the number *N<sub>c</sub>*, obtained by shifting *N* to the left cyclically, can be obtained from:

$$N_c = 10 N - d\left(10^m-1\right), \,$$

where *d* is the first digit of *N* and *m* is the number of digits.

This explains the above common gcd and the phenomenon is true in any [base](wikipedia://en/701207) if 10 is replaced by *b*, the base.

The cyclic permutations are thus related to repeating decimals, the corresponding fractions, and divisors of 10<sup>*m*</sup>−1. For examples the related fractions to the above cyclic permutations are thus:

- $\tfrac{091575}{999999}$, $\tfrac{915750}{999999}$, $\tfrac{157509}{999999}$, $\tfrac{575091}{999999}$, $\tfrac{750915}{999999}$, and $\tfrac{509157}{999999}$.

Reduced to their lowest terms using the common gcd, they are:

- $\tfrac{25}{273}$, $\tfrac{250}{273}$, $\tfrac{43}{273}$, $\tfrac{157}{273}$, $\tfrac{205}{273}$, and $\tfrac{139}{273}$.

That is, these fractions when expressed [in lowest terms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_lowest_terms), have the same denominator. This is true for cyclic permutations of any integer.

## Fraction method {#Fraction_method}

### Integral multiplier {#Integral_multiplier}

An integral multiplier refers to the multiplier *n* being an integer:

1. An integer *X* shift **right** cyclically by *k* positions when it is multiplied by **an integer *n***. *X* is then the repeating digits of $\tfrac{1}{F}$, whereby *F* is *F*<sub>0</sub> = *n* 10<sup>*k*</sup> − 1 (*F*<sub>0</sub> is [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) to 10), or a factor of *F*<sub>0</sub>; excluding any values of *F* which are not more than *n*.
1. An integer *X* shift **left** cyclically by *k* positions when it is multiplied by **an integer *n***. *X* is then the repeating digits of $\tfrac{1}{F}$, whereby *F* is *F*<sub>0</sub> = 10<sup>*k*</sup> - *n*, or a factor of *F*<sub>0</sub>; excluding any values of *F* which are not more than *n* and which are not [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) to 10.

It is necessary for F to be coprime to 10 in order that $\tfrac{1}{F}$ is a repeating decimal without any preceding non-repeating digits (see multiple sections of [Repeating decimal](wikipedia://en/13612447)). If there are digits not in a period, then there is no corresponding solution.

For these two cases, multiples of *X*, i.e. (*j X*) are also solutions provided that the integer *i* satisfies the condition $\tfrac{n j}{F}$ \< 1. Most often it is convenient to choose the smallest *F* that fits the above. The solutions can be expressed by the formula:

$$X = j \frac {10^p-1}{F}$$

where *p* is a period length of $\tfrac{1}{F}$; and *F* is a factor of *F*<sub>0</sub> coprime to 10.

E.g, *F*<sub>0</sub> = 1260 = 2<sup>2</sup> × 3<sup>2</sup> × 5 × 7. The factors excluding 2 and 5 recompose to *F* = 3<sup>2</sup> × 7 = 63. Alternatively, strike off all the **ending zeros** from 1260 to become 126, then divide it by 2 (or 5) iteratively until the quotient is no more divisible by 2 (or 5). The result is also *F* = 63.

To exclude integers that begin with zeros from the solutions, select an integer *j* such that $\tfrac{j}{F}$ > $\tfrac{1}{10}$, i.e. *j* > $\tfrac{F}{10}$.

There is no solution when *n* > *F*.

### Fractional multiplier {#Fractional_multiplier}

An integer *X* shift **left** cyclically by *k* positions when it is multiplied by **a fraction $\tfrac{n}{s}$**. *X* is then the repeating digits of $\tfrac{s}{F}$, whereby *F* is *F*<sub>0</sub> = s 10<sup>*k*</sup> - *n*, or a factor of *F*<sub>0</sub>; and *F* must be coprime to 10.

For this third case, multiples of *X*, i.e. (*j X*) are again solutions but the condition to be satisfied for integer *j* is that $\tfrac{n j}{F}$ \< 1. Again it is convenient to choose the smallest *F* that fits the above.

The solutions can be expressed by the formula:

$$X = j s \frac {10^p-1}{F}$$

where *p* is defined likewise; and *F* is made coprime to 10 by the same process as before.

To exclude integers that begin with zeros from the solutions, select an integer *j* such that $\tfrac{j s}{F}$ > $\tfrac{1}{10}$, i.e. *j* > $\tfrac{F}{10s}$.

Again if $\tfrac{j s}{F}$ > 1, there is no solution.

## Direct representation {#Direct_representation}

The direct algebra approach to the above cases integral multiplier lead to the following formula:

1. $$X=D \frac {10^m-1}{n10^k-1},$$

where *m* is the number of digits of *X*, and *D*, the *k*-digit number shifted from the low end of *X* to the high end of *n* *X*, satisfies *D* \< 10<sup>*k*</sup>.

If the numbers are not to have leading zeros, then *n* 10<sup>*k* − 1</sup> ≤ *D*.

1. $$X = D \frac {10^m-1}{10^k-n},$$

where *m* is the number of digits of *X*, and *D*, the *k*-digit number shifted from the high end of *X* to the low end of *n* *X*, satisfies:

   1. $$D<\frac {10^k} n - 1,$$
   1. and the 10-part (the product of the terms corresponding to the primes 2 and 5 of the [factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor)) of 10<sup>*k*</sup> − *n* divides *D*.

The 10-part of an integer *t* is often abbreviated $\operatorname{gcd}\left(10^\infty,t\right).$

If the numbers are not to have leading zeros, then 10<sup>*k* − 1</sup> ≤ *D*.

## Cyclic permutation by multiplication {#Cyclic_permutation_by_multiplication}

A long division of 1 by 7 gives:

\0.142857...

At the last step, 1 reappears as the remainder. The cyclic remainders are {1, 3, 2, 6, 4, 5}. We rewrite the quotients with the corresponding dividend/remainders above them at all the steps:

Dividend/Remainders 1 3 2 6 4 5 Quotients 1 4 2 8 5 7

and also note that:

- $\tfrac{1}{7}$ = 0.142857...
- $\tfrac{3}{7}$ = 0.428571...
- $\tfrac{2}{7}$ = 0.285714...
- $\tfrac{6}{7}$ = 0.857142...
- $\tfrac{4}{7}$ = 0.571428...
- $\tfrac{5}{7}$ = 0.714285...

By observing the remainders at each step, we can thus perform a desired [cyclic permutation](wikipedia://en/449166) by multiplication. E.g.,

- The integer 142857, corresponding to remainder 1, permutes to 428571 when multiplied by 3, the corresponding remainder of the latter.
- The integer 142857, corresponding to remainder 1, permutes to 857142 when multiplied by 6, the corresponding remainder of the latter.
- The integer 857142, corresponding to remainder 6, permutes to 571428 when multiplied by $\tfrac{5}{6}$; i.e. divided by 6 and multiplied by 5, the corresponding remainder of the latter.

In this manner, cyclical left or right shift of any number of positions can be performed.

Less importantly, this technique can be applied to any integer to [shift cyclically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_shift) right or left by any given number of places for the following reason:

- Every repeating decimal can be expressed as a rational number (fraction).
- Every integer, when added with a decimal point in front and concatenated with itself infinite times, can be converted to a fraction, e.g. we can transform 123456 in this manner to 0.123456123456..., which can thus be converted to fraction $\tfrac{123456}{999999}$. This fraction can be further simplified but it will not be done here.
- To permute the integer 123456 to 234561, all one needs to do is to multiply 123456 by $\tfrac{234561}{123456}$. This looks like cheating but if $\tfrac{234561}{123456}$ is a whole number (in this case it is not), the mission is completed.

## Proof of formula for cyclical right shift operation {#Proof_of_formula_for_cyclical_right_shift_operation}

An integer *X* shift cyclically right by *k* positions when it is multiplied by an integer *n*. Prove its formula.

::: {#o10 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof**

First recognize that *X* is the repeating digits of a [repeating decimal](wikipedia://en/13612447), which always possesses cyclic behavior in multiplication. The integer *X* and its multiple *n X* then will have the following relationship:

1. The integer *X* is the repeating digits of the fraction $\tfrac{1}{F}$, say *d<sub>p</sub>d<sub>p-1</sub>...d<sub>3</sub>d<sub>2</sub>d<sub>1</sub>*, where *d<sub>p</sub>*, *d<sub>p-1</sub>*, ..., *d<sub>3</sub>*, *d<sub>2</sub>* and *d<sub>1</sub>* each represents a digit and *p* is the number of digits.
1. The multiple *n X* is thus the repeating digits of the fraction $\tfrac{n}{F}$, say *d<sub>k</sub>d<sub>k-1</sub>...d<sub>3</sub>d<sub>2</sub>d<sub>1</sub>d<sub>p</sub>d<sub>p-1</sub>...d<sub>k+2</sub>d<sub>k+1</sub>*, representing the results after right cyclical shift of *k* positions.
1. *F* must be coprime to 10 so that when $\tfrac{1}{F}$ is expressed in decimal there is no preceding non-repeating digits otherwise the repeating decimal does not possess cyclic behavior in multiplication.
1. If the first remainder is taken to be *n* then *1* shall be the (*k* + 1)st remainder in the long division for $\tfrac{n}{F}$ in order for this cyclic permutation to take place.
1. In order that *n* × 10<sup>*k*</sup> = 1 (mod *F*) then *F* shall be either *F*<sub>0</sub> = (*n* × 10<sup>k</sup> - 1), or a factor of *F*<sub>0</sub>; but excluding any values not more than *n* and any value having a nontrivial common factor with 10, as deduced above.

This completes the proof.

:::

:::

## Proof of formula for cyclical left shift operation {#Proof_of_formula_for_cyclical_left_shift_operation}

An integer *X* shift cyclically left by *k* positions when it is multiplied by **an integer *n***. Prove its formula.

::: {#o13 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof**

First recognize that *X* is the repeating digits of a [repeating decimal](wikipedia://en/13612447), which always possesses a cyclic behavior in multiplication. The integer *X* and its multiple *n X* then will have the following relationship:

1. The integer *X* is the repeating digits of the fraction $\tfrac{1}{F}$, say *d<sub>p</sub>d<sub>p-1</sub>...d<sub>3</sub>d<sub>2</sub>d<sub>1</sub>* .
1. The multiple *n X* is thus the repeating digits of the fraction $\tfrac{n}{F}$, say *d<sub>p-k</sub>d<sub>p-k-1</sub>...d<sub>3</sub>d<sub>2</sub>d<sub>1</sub>d<sub>p</sub>d<sub>p-1</sub>...d<sub>p-k+1</sub>*,

which represents the results after left cyclical shift of *k* positions.

1. *F* must be coprime to 10 so that $\tfrac{1}{F}$ has no preceding non-repeating digits otherwise the repeating decimal does not possesses cyclic behavior in multiplication.
1. If the first remainder is taken to be 1 then *n* shall be the (*k* + 1)st remainder in the long division for $\tfrac{1}{F}$ in order for this cyclic permutation to take place.
1. In order that 1 × 10<sup>k</sup> = *n* (mode *F*) then *F* shall be either *F*<sub>0</sub> = (10<sup>k</sup> -*n*), or a factor of *F*<sub>0</sub>; but excluding any value not more than *n*, and any value having a nontrivial common factor with 10, as deduced above.

This completes the proof. The proof for non-integral multiplier such as $\tfrac{n}{s}$ can be derived in a similar way and is not documented here.

:::

## Shifting an integer cyclically {#Shifting_an_integer_cyclically}

The permutations can be:

- Shifting right cyclically by single position ([parasitic numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitic_numbers));
- Shifting right cyclically by double positions;
- Shifting right cyclically by any number of positions;
- Shifting left cyclically by single position;
- Shifting left cyclically by double positions; and
- Shifting left cyclically by any number of positions

## Parasitic numbers {#Parasitic_numbers}

When a parasitic number is multiplied by n, not only it exhibits the cyclic behavior but the permutation is such that the last digit of the parasitic number now becomes the first digit of the multiple. For example, 102564 x 4 = 410256. Note that 102564 is the repeating digits of $\tfrac{4}{39}$ and 410256 the repeating digits of $\tfrac{16}{39}$.

## Shifting right cyclically by double positions {#Shifting_right_cyclically_by_double_positions}

An integer *X* shift right cyclically by double positions when it is multiplied by an integer *n*. *X* is then the repeating digits of $\tfrac{1}{F}$, whereby $F$ = *n* × 10<sup>2</sup> - 1; or a factor of it; but excluding values for which $\tfrac{1}{F}$ has a period length dividing 2 (or, equivalently, less than 3); and $F$ must be coprime to 10.

Most often it is convenient to choose the smallest $F$ that fits the above.

## Shifting left cyclically by single position {#Shifting_left_cyclically_by_single_position}

Problem: An integer *X* shift left cyclically by single position when it is multiplied by 3. Find *X*.

Solution: First recognize that *X* is the repeating digits of a [repeating decimal](wikipedia://en/13612447), which always possesses some interesting cyclic behavior in multiplications. The integer *X* and its multiple then will have the following relationship:

- The integer *X* is the repeating digits of the fraction $\tfrac{1}{F}$, say *ab\*\*\**.
- The multiple is thus the repeating digits of the fraction $\tfrac{3}{F}$, say *b\*\*\*a*.
- In order for this cyclic permutation to take place, then 3 shall be the next remainder in the long division for $\tfrac{1}{F}$. Thus $F$ shall be 7 as 1 × 10 ÷ 7 gives remainder 3.

This yields the results that:

*X* = the repeating digits of $\tfrac{1}{7}$

\=142857, and

the multiple = 142857 × 3 = 428571, the repeating digits of $\tfrac{3}{7}$

The other solution is represented by $\tfrac{2}{7}$ x 3 = $\tfrac{6}{7}$:

- 285714 x 3 = 857142

There are no other solutions [^o20] because:

- Integer *n* must be the subsequent remainder in a long division of a fraction $\tfrac{1}{F}$. Given that n = 10 - F, and F is coprime to 10 in order for $\tfrac{1}{F}$ to be a repeating decimal, then *n* shall be less than 10.
- For *n* = 2, F must be 10 - 2 = 8. However $\tfrac{1}{8}$ does not generate a repeating decimal, similarly for *n* = 5.
- For *n* = 7, F must be 10 - 7 = 3. However 7 > 3 and $\tfrac{7}{3}$ = 2.333 > 1 and does not fit the purpose.
- Similarly there is no solution for any other integer of *n* less than 10 except *n* = 3.

However, if the multiplier is not restricted to be an integer (though ugly), there are many other solutions from this method. E.g., if an integer *X* shift right cyclically by single position when it is multiplied by $\tfrac{3}{2}$, then 3 shall be the next remainder after 2 in a long division of a fraction $\tfrac{2}{F}$. This deduces that F = 2 x 10 - 3 = 17, giving *X* as the repeating digits of $\tfrac{2}{17}$, i.e. 1176470588235294, and its multiple is 1764705882352941.
