# Squared deviations from the mean

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Squared deviations from the mean"}

**Squared deviations from the mean** (**SDM**) result from [squaring](wikipedia://en/659942) [deviations](wikipedia://en/9252911). In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the definition of *[variance](wikipedia://en/32344)* is either the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) of the SDM (when considering a theoretical [distribution](wikipedia://en/23543)) or its average value (for actual experimental data). Computations for *[analysis of variance](wikipedia://en/634)* involve the partitioning of a sum of SDM.

:::

## Background {#Background}

An understanding of the computations involved is greatly enhanced by a study of the statistical value

$\operatorname{E}( X ^ 2 )$, where $\operatorname{E}$ is the expected value operator.

For a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) $X$ with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$,

$\sigma^2 = \operatorname{E}( X ^ 2 ) - \mu^2.$[^o3]

(Its derivation is shown [here](wikipedia://en/32344#Population_variance).) Therefore,

$$\operatorname{E}( X ^ 2 ) = \sigma^2 + \mu^2.$$

From the above, the following can be derived:

$$\operatorname{E}\left( \sum\left( X ^ 2\right) \right) = n\sigma^2 + n\mu^2,$$

$$\operatorname{E}\left( \left(\sum X \right)^ 2 \right) = n\sigma^2 + n^2\mu^2.$$

## Sample variance {#Sample_variance}

The sum of squared deviations needed to calculate [sample variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_variance) (before deciding whether to divide by *n* or *n* − 1) is most easily calculated as

$$S = \sum x ^ 2 - \frac{\left(\sum x\right)^2}{n}.$$

From the two derived expectations above the expected value of this sum is

$$\operatorname{E}(S) = n\sigma^2 + n\mu^2 - \frac{n\sigma^2 + n^2\mu^2}{n},$$

which implies

$$\operatorname{E}(S) = (n - 1)\sigma^2.$$

This effectively proves the use of the divisor *n* − 1 in the calculation of an **unbiased** sample estimate of *σ*<sup>2</sup>.

## Partition — analysis of variance {#Partition_—_analysis_of_variance}

In the situation where data is available for *k* different treatment groups having size *n*<sub>*i*</sub> where *i* varies from 1 to *k*, then it is assumed that the expected mean of each group is

$$\operatorname{E}(\mu_i) = \mu + T_i$$

and the variance of each treatment group is unchanged from the population variance $\sigma^2$.

Under the Null Hypothesis that the treatments have no effect, then each of the $T_i$ will be zero.

It is now possible to calculate three sums of squares:

**Individual**

$$I = \sum x^2$$

$$\operatorname{E}(I) = n\sigma^2 + n\mu^2$$

**Treatments**

$$T = \sum_{i=1}^k \left(\left(\sum x\right)^2/n_i\right)$$

$$\operatorname{E}(T) = k\sigma^2 + \sum_{i=1}^k n_i(\mu + T_i)^2$$

$$\operatorname{E}(T) = k\sigma^2 + n\mu^2 + 2\mu \sum_{i=1}^k (n_iT_i) + \sum_{i=1}^k n_i(T_i)^2$$

Under the null hypothesis that the treatments cause no differences and all the $T_i$ are zero, the expectation simplifies to

$$\operatorname{E}(T) = k\sigma^2 + n\mu^2.$$

**Combination**

$$C = \left(\sum x\right)^2/n$$

$$\operatorname{E}(C) = \sigma^2 + n\mu^2$$

### Sums of squared deviations {#Sums_of_squared_deviations}

Under the null hypothesis, the difference of any pair of *I*, *T*, and *C* does not contain any dependency on $\mu$, only $\sigma^2$.

$\operatorname{E}(I - C) = (n - 1)\sigma^2$ total squared deviations aka *[total sum of squares](wikipedia://en/2473449)*

$\operatorname{E}(T - C) = (k - 1)\sigma^2$ treatment squared deviations aka *[explained sum of squares](wikipedia://en/2473431)*

$\operatorname{E}(I - T) = (n - k)\sigma^2$ residual squared deviations aka *[residual sum of squares](wikipedia://en/2473303)*

The constants (*n* − 1), (*k* − 1), and (*n* − *k*) are normally referred to as the number of [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321606).

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

In a very simple example, 5 observations arise from two treatments. The first treatment gives three values 1, 2, and 3, and the second treatment gives two values 4, and 6.

$$I = \frac{1^2}{1} + \frac{2^2}{1} + \frac{3^2}{1} + \frac{4^2}{1} + \frac{6^2}{1} = 66$$

$$T = \frac{(1 + 2 + 3)^2}{3} + \frac{(4 + 6)^2}{2} = 12 + 50 = 62$$

$$C = \frac{(1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 6)^2}{5} = 256/5 = 51.2$$

Giving

Total squared deviations = 66 − 51.2 = 14.8 with 4 degrees of freedom.

Treatment squared deviations = 62 − 51.2 = 10.8 with 1 degree of freedom.

Residual squared deviations = 66 − 62 = 4 with 3 degrees of freedom.

:::
