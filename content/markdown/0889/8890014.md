# Perfect totient number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Perfect totient number"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), a **perfect totient number** is an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) that is equal to the sum of its iterated [totients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totient). That is, one applies the [totient function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totient_function) to a number *n*, apply it again to the resulting totient, and so on, until the number 1 is reached, and adds together the resulting sequence of numbers; if the sum equals *n*, then *n* is a perfect totient number.

:::

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

For example, there are six positive integers less than 9 and [relatively prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_prime) to it, so the totient of 9 is 6; there are two numbers less than 6 and relatively prime to it, so the totient of 6 is 2; and there is one number less than 2 and relatively prime to it, so the totient of 2 is 1; and $9 = 6 + 2 + 1$, so 9 is a perfect totient number.

The first few perfect totient numbers are

[3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3_%28number%29), [9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/9_%28number%29), [15](wikipedia://en/208252), [27](wikipedia://en/369235), [39](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/39_%28number%29), [81](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/81_%28number%29), [111](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/111_%28number%29), [183](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/183_%28number%29), [243](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/243_%28number%29), [255](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/255_%28number%29), [327](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/327_%28number%29), [363](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/363_%28number%29), 471, [729](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/729_%28number%29), 2187, 2199, 3063, 4359, 4375, ... .

:::

::: {#o5 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

## Notation {#Notation}

In symbols, one writes

$$\varphi^i(n) = \begin{cases} \varphi(n), &\text{ if } i = 1 \\ \varphi(\varphi^{i-1}(n)), &\text{ if } i \geq 2 \end{cases}$$

for the iterated totient function. Then if *c* is the integer such that

$$\displaystyle\varphi^c(n)=2,$$

one has that *n* is a perfect totient number if

$$n = \sum_{i = 1}^{c + 1} \varphi^i(n).$$

:::

## Multiples and powers of three {#Multiples_and_powers_of_three}

It can be observed that many perfect totient are [multiples](wikipedia://en/3912709) of 3; in fact, 4375 is the smallest perfect totient number that is not [divisible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisible) by 3. All [powers of 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_of_3) are perfect totient numbers, as may be seen by [induction](wikipedia://en/18881) using the fact that

$$\displaystyle\varphi(3^k) = \varphi(2\times 3^k) = 2 \times 3^{k-1}.$$

Venkataraman (1975) found another family of perfect totient numbers: if $p = 4 \times 3^{k} + 1$ is [prime](wikipedia://en/23666), then 3*p* is a perfect totient number. The values of *k* leading to perfect totient numbers in this way are

0, 1, 2, 3, 6, 14, 15, 39, 201, 249, 1005, 1254, 1635, ... .

More generally if *p* is a prime number greater than 3, and 3*p* is a perfect totient number, then *p* ≡ 1 ([mod](wikipedia://en/20087) 4) (Mohan and Suryanarayana 1982). Not all *p* of this form lead to perfect totient numbers; for instance, 51 is not a perfect totient number. Iannucci et al. (2003) showed that if 9*p* is a perfect totient number then *p* is a prime of one of three specific forms listed in their paper. It is not known whether there are any perfect totient numbers of the form 3<sup>*k*</sup>*p* where *p* is prime and *k* > 3.
