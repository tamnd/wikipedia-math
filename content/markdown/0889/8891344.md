# Gibbs–Duhem equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gibbs–Duhem equation"}

*Image: Josiah\_Willard\_Gibbs\_-from\_MMS-.jpg.* Josiah Willard Gibbs

In [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952), the **Gibbs–Duhem equation** describes the relationship between changes in [chemical potential](wikipedia://en/218628) for components in a [thermodynamic system](wikipedia://en/425850):[^o2]

:::

$$\sum_{i=1}^I N_i \mathrm{d}\mu_i = - S \mathrm{d}T + V \mathrm{d}p$$

where $N_i$ is the number of [moles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_%28unit%29) of component $i, \mathrm{d}\mu_i$ the [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) increase in chemical potential for this component, $S$ the [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891), $T$ the [absolute temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_temperature), $V$ [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) and $p$ the [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure). $I$ is the number of different components in the system. This equation shows that in thermodynamics [intensive properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensive_property) are not independent but related, making it a mathematical statement of the [state postulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_postulate). When pressure and temperature are variable, only $I-1$ of $I$ components have independent values for chemical potential and [Gibbs' phase rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs'_phase_rule) follows.

The Gibbs−Duhem equation applies to homogeneous thermodynamic systems. It does not apply to inhomogeneous systems such as small thermodynamic systems,[^o3] systems subject to long-range forces like electricity and gravity,[^o4][^o5] or to fluids in porous media.[^o6]

The equation is named after [Josiah Willard Gibbs](wikipedia://en/37332) and [Pierre Duhem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Duhem).

## Derivation {#Derivation}

The Gibbs–Duhem equation follows from assuming the system can be scaled in amount perfectly. Gibbs derived the relationship based on the [thought experiment](wikipedia://en/49535) of varying the [amount of substance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amount_of_substance) starting from zero, keeping its nature and state the same.[^o4]

Mathematically, this means the internal energy $U$ scales with its extensive variables as follows:[^o8]

$$U(\lambda S, \lambda V, \lambda N_1, \lambda N_2, \ldots) = \lambda U (S, V, N_1, N_2, \ldots)$$

where $S, V, N_1, N_2, \ldots$ are all of the extensive variables of system: entropy, volume, and particle numbers. The [internal energy](wikipedia://en/340757) is thus a [first-order homogenous function](wikipedia://en/622844). Applying [Euler's homogeneous function theorem](wikipedia://en/622844#Positive_homogeneity), one finds the following relation:

$$U = TS - pV + \sum_{i=1}^I \mu_i N_i$$

Taking the total differential, one finds

$$\mathrm{d}U = T\mathrm{d}S + S\mathrm{d}T - p\mathrm{d}V - V \mathrm{d}p + \sum_{i=1}^I \mu_i \mathrm{d} N_i + \sum_{i=1}^I N_i \mathrm{d} \mu_i$$

From both sides one can subtract the [fundamental thermodynamic relation](wikipedia://en/5529757),

$$\mathrm{d}U = T\mathrm{d}S - p\mathrm{d}V + \sum_{i=1}^I \mu_i \mathrm{d} N_i$$

yielding the Gibbs–Duhem equation[^o8]

$$0 =S\mathrm{d}T - V \mathrm{d}p + \sum_{i=1}^I N_i \mathrm{d} \mu_i .$$

::: {#o10 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

By normalizing the above equation by the extent of a system, such as the total number of moles, the Gibbs–Duhem equation provides a relationship between the intensive variables of the system. For a simple system with $I$ different components, there will be $I+1$ independent parameters or "degrees of freedom". For example, if we know a gas cylinder filled with pure nitrogen is at room temperature (298 K) and 25 MPa, we can determine the fluid density (258 kg/m<sup>3</sup>), enthalpy (272 kJ/kg), entropy (5.07 kJ/kg⋅K) or any other intensive thermodynamic variable.[^o11] If instead the cylinder contains a nitrogen/oxygen mixture, we require an additional piece of information, usually the ratio of oxygen-to-nitrogen.

If multiple phases of matter are present, the chemical potentials across a phase boundary are equal.[^o12] Combining expressions for the Gibbs–Duhem equation in each phase and assuming systematic equilibrium (i.e. that the temperature and pressure is constant throughout the system), we recover the [Gibbs' phase rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs'_phase_rule).

One particularly useful expression arises when considering binary solutions.[^o13] At constant P ([isobaric](wikipedia://en/414765)) and T ([isothermal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isothermal)) it becomes:

$$0= N_1 \mathrm{d}\mu_1 + N_2 \mathrm{d}\mu_2$$

or, normalizing by total number of moles in the system $N_1 + N_2,$ substituting in the definition of [activity coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activity_coefficient) $\gamma$ and using the identity $x_1 + x_2 = 1$:[^o14]

$$0 = x_1 \mathrm{d}\ln(\gamma_1) + x_2 \mathrm{d}\ln(\gamma_2)$$

This equation is instrumental in the calculation of thermodynamically consistent and thus more accurate expressions for the [vapor pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vapor_pressure) of a binary mixture from limited experimental data. One can develop this further to the [Duhem–Margules equation](wikipedia://en/23556803) which relates to vapor pressures directly.

:::

## Ternary and multicomponent solutions and mixtures {#Ternary_and_multicomponent_solutions_and_mixtures}

[Lawrence Stamper Darken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Stamper_Darken) has shown that the Gibbs–Duhem equation can be applied to the determination of chemical potentials of components from a multicomponent system from experimental data regarding the chemical potential $\bar {G_2}$ of only one component (here component 2) at all compositions. He has deduced the following relation[^o16]

$$\bar{G_2} = G + (1-x_2) \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial x_2}\right)_{\frac{x_1}{x_3}}$$

x<sub>i</sub>, amount (mole) fractions of components.

Making some rearrangements and dividing by (1 – x<sub>2</sub>)<sup>2</sup> gives:

$$\frac{G}{(1-x_2)^2} + \frac{1}{1-x_2} \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial x_2}\right)_{\frac{x_1}{x_3}} = \frac{\bar{G_2}}{(1-x_2)^2}$$

or

$$\left(\mathfrak{d} \frac{G}{\frac{1 - x_2}{\mathfrak{d} x_2}}\right)_{\frac{x_1}{x_3}} = \frac{\bar{G_2}}{(1 - x_2)^2}$$

or

$$\left(\frac {\frac{\partial G}{1-x_2}}{\partial x_2}\right)_{\frac{x_1}{x_3}} = \frac{\bar{G_2}}{(1 - x_2)^2}$$

as formatting variant

The derivative with respect to one [mole fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_fraction) x<sub>2</sub> is taken at constant ratios of amounts (and therefore of mole fractions) of the other components of the solution representable in a diagram like [ternary plot](wikipedia://en/1861608).

The last equality can be integrated from $x_2 = 1$ to $x_2$ gives:

$$G - (1 - x_2) \lim_{x_2\to 1} \frac{G}{1 - x_2} = (1 - x_2) \int_{1}^{x_2}\frac{\bar{G_2}}{(1 - x_2)^2} dx_2$$

Applying [l'Hôpital's rule](wikipedia://en/18531) gives:

$$\lim_{x_2\to 1} \frac{G}{1 - x_2} = \lim_{x_2\to 1} \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial x_2}\right)_{\frac{x_1}{x_3}} .$$

This becomes further:

$$\lim_{x_2\to 1} \frac{G}{1 - x_2} = -\lim_{x_2\to 1} \frac {\bar{G_2} - G}{1 - x_2}.$$

Express the mole fractions of component 1 and 3 as functions of component 2 mole fraction and binary mole ratios:

$$x_1 = \frac{1-x_2}{1+\frac{x_3}{x_1}}$$

$$x_3 = \frac{1-x_2}{1+\frac{x_1}{x_3}}$$

and the sum of partial molar quantities

$$G=\sum _{i=1}^3 x_i \bar{G_i},$$

gives

$$G= x_1 (\bar {G_1})_{x_2 =1} + x_3 (\bar {G_3})_{x_2 =1} + (1 - x_2) \int_{1}^{x_2}\frac{\bar{G_2}}{(1 - x_2)^2} dx_2$$

$(\bar{G_1})_{x_2 =1}$ and $(\bar{G_3})_{x_2 =1}$ are constants which can be determined from the binary systems 1\_2 and 2\_3. These constants can be obtained from the previous equality by putting the complementary mole fraction x<sub>3</sub> = 0 for x<sub>1</sub> and vice versa.

Thus

$$(\bar {G_1})_{x_2 =1} = - \left(\int_{1}^{0}\frac{\bar{G_2}}{(1 - x_2)^2} dx_2 \right)_{x_3=0}$$

and

$$(\bar {G_3})_{x_2 =1} = - \left(\int_{1}^{0}\frac{\bar{G_2}}{(1 - x_2)^2} dx_2 \right)_{x_1=0}$$

The final expression is given by substitution of these constants into the previous equation:

$$G= (1 - x_2) \left(\int_{1}^{x_2}\frac{\bar{G_2}}{(1 - x_2)^2} dx_2 \right)_{\frac{x_1}{x_3}} - x_1 \left(\int_{1}^{0}\frac{\bar{G_2}}{(1 - x_2)^2} dx_2 \right)_{x_3=0} - x_3 \left(\int_{1}^{0}\frac{\bar{G_2}}{(1 - x_2)^2} dx_2 \right)_{x_1=0}$$
