# Discrete fixed-point theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Discrete fixed-point theorem"}

In [discrete mathematics](wikipedia://en/8492), a **discrete fixed-point** is a [fixed-point](wikipedia://en/449738) for functions defined on finite sets, typically subsets of the integer grid $\mathbb{Z}^n$.

:::

Discrete fixed-point theorems were developed by Iimura,[^o2] Murota and Tamura,[^o3] Chen and Deng[^o4] and others. Yang[^o5] provides a survey.

## Basic concepts {#Basic_concepts}

Continuous fixed-point theorems often require a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122). Since continuity is not meaningful for functions on discrete sets, it is replaced by conditions such as a **[direction-preserving function](wikipedia://en/63270016)**. Such conditions imply that the function does not change too drastically when moving between neighboring points of the integer grid. There are various direction-preservation conditions, depending on whether neighboring points are considered points of a hypercube (HGDP), of a simplex (SGDP) etc. See the page on [direction-preserving function](wikipedia://en/63270016) for definitions.

Continuous fixed-point theorems often require a [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292). The analogue of this property for discrete sets is an **[integrally-convex set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrally-convex_set)**.

A **fixed point** of a discrete function *f* is defined exactly as for continuous functions: it is a point *x* for which *f*(*x*)=*x*.

## For functions on discrete sets {#For_functions_on_discrete_sets}

We focus on functions $f: X\to \mathbb{R}^n$, where the domain X is a nonempty subset of the Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$. ch(*X*) denotes the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of *X*.

**Iimura-Murota-Tamura theorem**:[^o3] If *X* is a finite [integrally-convex subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrally-convex_set) of $\mathbb{Z}^n$, and $f: X\to \text{ch}(X)$ is a *hypercubic direction-preserving (HDP)* function, then *f* has a fixed-point.

**Chen-Deng theorem**:[^o4] If *X* is a finite subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and $f: X\to \text{ch}(X)$ is *simplicially direction-preserving* *(SDP)*, then *f* has a fixed-point.

**Yang's theorems**:[^o5]

- \[3.6\] If *X* is a finite [integrally-convex subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrally-convex_set) of $\mathbb{Z}^n$, $f: X\to \mathbb{R}^n$ is *simplicially gross direction preserving (SGDP)*, and for all *x* in *X* there exists some *g*(*x*)>0 such that $x+g(x)f(x)\in \text{ch}(X)$, then *f* has a zero point.
- \[3.7\] If *X* is a finite hypercubic subset of $\mathbb{Z}^n$, with minimum point *a* and maximum point *b*, $f: X\to \mathbb{R}^n$ is SGDP, and for any *x* in *X*: $f_i(x_1,\ldots,x_{i-1},a_i,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_n) \leq 0$ and $f_i(x_1,\ldots,x_{i-1},b_i,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_n) \geq 0$, then *f* has a zero point. This is a discrete analogue of the [Poincaré–Miranda theorem](wikipedia://en/46736435). It is a consequence of the previous theorem.
- \[3.8\] If *X* is a finite [integrally-convex subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrally-convex_set) of $\mathbb{Z}^n$, and $f: X\to \text{ch}(X)$ is such that $f(x)-x$ is *SGDP*, then *f* has a fixed-point.[^o8] This is a discrete analogue of the [Brouwer fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/4101).
- \[3.9\] If X = $\mathbb{Z}^n$, $f: X\to \mathbb{R}^n$ is bounded and $f(x)-x$ is SGDP, then *f* has a fixed-point (this follows easily from the previous theorem by taking *X* to be a subset of $\mathbb{Z}^n$ that bounds *f*).
- \[3.10\] If *X* is a finite [integrally-convex subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrally-convex_set) of $\mathbb{Z}^n$, $F: X\to 2^X$ a [point-to-set mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point-to-set_mapping), and for all *x* in *X*: $F(x) = \text{ch}(F(x))\cap \mathbb{Z}^n$ , and there is a function *f* such that $f(x)\in \text{ch}(F(x))$ and $f(x)-x$ is SGDP, then there is a point *y* in *X* such that $y \in F(y)$. This is a discrete analogue of the [Kakutani fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/2658571), and the function *f* is an analogue of a continuous [selection function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selection_function).
- \[3.12\] Suppose *X* is a finite [integrally-convex subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrally-convex_set) of $\mathbb{Z}^n$, and it is also *symmetric* in the sense that *x* is in *X* iff -*x* is in *X*. If $f: X\to \mathbb{R}^n$ is SGDP w.r.t. a *weakly-symmetric* triangulation of ch(*X*) (in the sense that if *s* is a simplex on the boundary of the triangulation iff -*s* is), and $f(x)\cdot f(-y) \leq 0$ for every pair of simplicially-connected points *x*, *y* in the boundary of ch(*X*), then *f* has a zero point.
- See the survey[^o5] for more theorems.

## For discontinuous functions on continuous sets {#For_discontinuous_functions_on_continuous_sets}

Discrete fixed-point theorems are closely related to fixed-point theorems on discontinuous functions. These, too, use the direction-preservation condition instead of continuity.

**Herings-Laan-Talman-Yang fixed-point theorem**:[^o10]

Let *X* be a non-empty convex compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Let *f*: *X* → *X* be a *locally gross direction preserving (LGDP)* function: at any point *x* that is not a fixed point of *f*, the direction of $f(x)-x$ is grossly preserved in some [neighborhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of *x*, in the sense that for any two points *y*, *z* in this neighborhood, its inner product is non-negative, i.e.: $(f(y)-y)\cdot (f(z)-z) \geq 0$. Then *f* has a fixed point in *X*.

The theorem is originally stated for polytopes, but Philippe Bich extends it to convex compact sets.[^o11]Note that every [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) is LGDP, but an LGDP function may be discontinuous. An LGDP function may even be neither upper nor lower [semi-continuous](wikipedia://en/57824). Moreover, there is a constructive algorithm for approximating this fixed point.

::: {#o13 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Discrete fixed-point theorems have been used to prove the existence of a [Nash equilibrium](wikipedia://en/45337) in a discrete game, and the existence of a [Walrasian equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walrasian_equilibrium) in a discrete market.[^o14]
