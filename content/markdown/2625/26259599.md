# Frucht's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Frucht's theorem"}

**Frucht's theorem** is a result in [algebraic graph theory](wikipedia://en/1620216), conjectured by [Dénes Kőnig](wikipedia://en/1327120) in 1936[^o2] and proved by [Robert Frucht](wikipedia://en/27069553) in 1939.[^o3] It states that every [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) is the [group of symmetries](wikipedia://en/56661772) of a finite [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph). More strongly, for any finite group $G$, there exist infinitely many [non-isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_graph) [connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_graph) graphs such that the [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of each of them is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to $G$.

:::

::: {#o5 .proof cue=heading name="Proof idea"}

## Proof idea {#Proof_idea}

The main idea of the proof is to observe that the [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of *G*, with the addition of colors and orientations on its edges to distinguish the generators of *G* from each other, has the desired automorphism group. Therefore, if each of these edges is replaced by an appropriate subgraph, such that each replacement subgraph is itself asymmetric and two replacements are isomorphic if and only if they replace edges of the same color, then the undirected graph created by performing these replacements will also have *G* as its symmetry group.[^o6]

:::

## Graph size {#Graph_size}

With three exceptions – the cyclic groups of orders 3, 4, and 5 – every group can be represented as the symmetries of a graph whose vertices have only two [orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29). Therefore, the number of vertices in the graph is at most twice the order of the group. With a larger set of exceptions, most finite groups can be represented as the symmetries of a [vertex-transitive graph](wikipedia://en/581175), with a number of vertices equal to the order of the group.[^o8]

## Special families of graphs {#Special_families_of_graphs}

*Image: Frucht graph.dot.svg.* The [Frucht graph](wikipedia://en/23784721), a 3-[regular](wikipedia://en/85821) graph whose automorphism group realizes the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059).

There are stronger versions of Frucht's theorem that show that certain restricted families of graphs still contain enough graphs to realize any symmetry group. Frucht[^o10] proved that in fact countably many [3-regular graphs](wikipedia://en/980508) with the desired property exist; for instance, the [Frucht graph](wikipedia://en/23784721), a 3-regular graph with 12 vertices and 18 edges, has no nontrivial symmetries, providing a realization of this type for the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059). [Gert Sabidussi](wikipedia://en/31554519) showed that any group can be realized as the symmetry groups of countably many distinct *k*-[regular graphs](wikipedia://en/85821), [*k*-vertex-connected graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph), or [*k*-chromatic graphs](wikipedia://en/426743), for all positive integer values *k* (with $k\ge 3$ for regular graphs and $k\ge 2$ for *k*-chromatic graphs).[^o11] From the facts that every graph can be reconstructed from the containment [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) of its edges and vertices, that every finite partial order is equivalent by [Birkhoff's representation theorem](wikipedia://en/20489385) to a finite [distributive lattice](wikipedia://en/310883), it follows that every finite group can be realized as the symmetries of a distributive lattice, and of the graph of the lattice, a [median graph](wikipedia://en/16970848).[^o6] It is possible to realize every finite group as the group of symmetries of a [strongly regular graph](wikipedia://en/1399856).[^o12] Every finite group can also be realized as the symmetries of a graph with [distinguishing number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distinguishing_number) two: one can (improperly) color the graph with two colors so that none of the symmetries of the graph preserve the coloring.[^o13]

However, some important classes of graphs are incapable of realizing all groups as their symmetries. [Camille Jordan](wikipedia://en/355931) characterized the symmetry groups of [trees](wikipedia://en/48560) as being the smallest set of finite groups containing the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059) and closed under [direct products](wikipedia://en/3706015) with each other and [wreath products](wikipedia://en/146625) with [symmetric groups](wikipedia://en/28901);[^o14] in particular, the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order three is not the symmetry group of a tree. [Planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314) are also not capable of realizing all groups as their symmetries; for instance, the only [finite simple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_simple_group) that are symmetries of planar graphs are the [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) and the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) $A_5$.[^o15] More generally, every [minor-closed graph family](wikipedia://en/351769) is incapable of representing all finite groups by the symmetries of its graphs.[^o16] [László Babai](wikipedia://en/3461604) conjectures, more strongly, that each minor-closed family can represent only finitely many non-cyclic finite simple groups.[^o17]

## Infinite graphs and groups {#Infinite_graphs_and_groups}

Izbicki extended these results in 1959 and showed that there were [uncountably many](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountably_many) *infinite* graphs realizing any finite symmetry group.[^o19] Finally, [Johannes de Groot](wikipedia://en/6022862) and [Gert Sabidussi](wikipedia://en/31554519) in 1959/1960 independently proved that any group (dropping the assumption that the group be finite, but with the assumption of [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840)) could be realized as the group of symmetries of an infinite graph; alternatively, the [axiom of regularity](wikipedia://en/2113) eliminates the need for choice.[^o20][^o21] Furthermore, this implication is strict, as there are models of ZF minus regularity in which Frucht's theorem for infinite groups fails.[^o22]
