# Minimal counterexample

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Minimal counterexample"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **minimal counterexample** is the smallest example which falsifies a claim. It is also sometimes called a **minimal criminal**,[^o2] **smallest criminal**,[^o3] or **least criminal**,[^o4][^o5] especially (but not exclusively) in the context of the [four-color theorem](wikipedia://en/10949).[^o6][^o7][^o8][^o9][^o10] A **proof by minimal counterexample** (or by *minimal/smallest/least criminal*) is a method of [proof](wikipedia://en/82285) which combines the use of a minimal counterexample with the methods of [proof by induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_By_Induction) and [proof by contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_contradiction).[^o11][^o12] More specifically, in trying to prove a proposition *P*, one first assumes by contradiction that it is false, and that therefore there must be at least one [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151). With respect to some idea of size (which may need to be chosen carefully), one then concludes that there is such a counterexample *C* that is *minimal*. In regard to the argument, *C* is generally something quite hypothetical (since the truth of *P* excludes the possibility of *C*), but it may be possible to argue that if *C* existed, then it would have some definite properties which, after applying some reasoning similar to that in an inductive proof, would lead to a contradiction, thereby showing that the proposition *P* is indeed true.[^o13]

:::

If the form of the contradiction is that we can derive a further counterexample *D*, that is smaller than *C* in the sense of the working hypothesis of minimality, then this technique is traditionally called [proof by infinite descent](wikipedia://en/358069). In which case, there may be multiple and more complex ways to structure the argument of the proof.

The assumption that if there is a counterexample, there is a minimal counterexample, is based on a [well-ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-ordering) of some kind. The usual ordering on the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) is clearly possible, by the most usual formulation of [mathematical induction](wikipedia://en/18881); but the scope of the method can include [well-ordered induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-ordered_induction) of any kind.

::: {#o15 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The minimal counterexample method has been much used in the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445). The [Feit–Thompson theorem](wikipedia://en/1656473), that finite simple groups that are not cyclic groups have even order, was proved based on the hypothesis of some, and therefore some minimal, simple group *G* of odd order. Every proper subgroup of *G* can be assumed a solvable group, meaning that much theory of such subgroups could be applied.[^o16]

[Euclid's proof of the fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556#Proof) is a simple proof which uses a minimal counterexample.[^o17][^o18]

A minimal counterexample has often been used in proofs of the [four-color theorem](wikipedia://en/10949), where it is usually called a *minimal criminal*.[^o6][^o7][^o8][^o9][^o10]
