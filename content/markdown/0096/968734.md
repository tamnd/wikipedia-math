# Integrability conditions for differential systems

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Integrability conditions for differential systems"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), certain systems of [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564) are usefully formulated, from the point of view of their underlying geometric and algebraic structure, in terms of a system of [differential forms](wikipedia://en/221519). The idea is to take advantage of the way a differential form *restricts* to a [submanifold](wikipedia://en/525095), and the fact that this restriction is compatible with the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782). This is one possible approach to certain [over-determined systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Over-determined_system), for example, including [Lax pairs](wikipedia://en/5527029) of [integrable systems](wikipedia://en/2573213).

:::

## Mathematical formulation {#Mathematical_formulation}

A **Pfaffian system** is specified by [1-forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1-form) alone, but the theory includes other types of example of **differential system**. To elaborate, a Pfaffian system is a set of 1-forms on a smooth manifold (which one sets equal to 0 to find *solutions* to the system).

Given a collection of differential 1-forms $\textstyle\alpha_i, i=1,2,\dots, k$ on an $\textstyle n$-dimensional manifold $M$, an **integral manifold** is an immersed (not necessarily embedded) submanifold whose tangent space at every point $\textstyle p\in N$ is annihilated by (the pullback of) each $\textstyle \alpha_i$.

A **maximal integral manifold** is an immersed (not necessarily embedded) submanifold

$$i:N\subset M$$

such that the kernel of the restriction map on forms

$$i^*:\Omega_p^1(M)\rightarrow \Omega_p^1(N)$$

is spanned by the $\textstyle \alpha_i$ at every point $p$ of $N$. If in addition the $\textstyle \alpha_i$ are linearly independent, then $N$ is ($n-k$)-dimensional.

A Pfaffian system is said to be **completely integrable** if $M$ admits a [foliation](wikipedia://en/371227) by maximal integral manifolds. (Note that the foliation need not be **regular**; i.e. the leaves of the foliation might not be embedded submanifolds.)

An **integrability condition** is a condition on the $\alpha_i$ to guarantee that there will be integral submanifolds of sufficiently high dimension.

### Intuition {#Intuition}

*Image: Standard\_contact\_structure.svg.* The standard contact structure on $\mathbb{R}^3$, defined by the 1-form $dz - ydx$. Due to the twisting, it is completely non-integrable anywhere.

A Pfaffian system is specified by 1-forms. At each point $x \in M$, the set of 1-forms can be visualized as a set of hyperplanes, or [contact elements](wikipedia://en/476398), centered on the point. The hyperplanes intersect, producing a [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of the local [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) $T_x M$. This field of linear subspaces locally look like [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) pieces of a maximal integral manifold, but it might be impossible to put together these infinitesimal pieces into a maximal integral manifold. The pieces might twist against each other, breaking any attempt to piece them together.

For example, if $M$ has 3 dimensions, then a single 1-form produces a field of planes, while two 1-forms that are linearly independent at every point produces a field of lines. Integrating a field of lines is always possible, but integrating a field of planes may be impossible, due to "twisting". Locally, such non-integrable field of planes look like the standard contact structure on $\mathbb{R}^3$, defined by the 1-form $dz - ydx$.

## Necessary and sufficient conditions {#Necessary_and_sufficient_conditions}

The necessary and sufficient conditions for **complete integrability** of a Pfaffian system are given by the [Frobenius theorem](wikipedia://en/602480). One version states that if the ideal $\mathcal I$ algebraically generated by the collection of *α*<sub>*i*</sub> inside the ring Ω(*M*) is differentially closed, in other words

$$d{\mathcal I}\subset {\mathcal I},$$

then the system admits a [foliation](wikipedia://en/371227) by maximal integral manifolds. (The converse is obvious from the definitions.)

## Examples {#Examples}

### Integrable regular systems {#Integrable_regular_systems}

Given any regular foliation, we can simply take its differentials to obtain an integrable regular system. The [rank](wikipedia://en/26561) of the system is the [codimension](wikipedia://en/525149) of the foliation.

The [Hopf fibration](wikipedia://en/580384) is a foliation of the 3-sphere into circles, which is a regular foliation of codimension 2.

### Integrable singular systems {#Integrable_singular_systems}

Similar to integrable regular systems, a singular foliation produces an integrable singular system. For example, $\mathbb{R}^3$ can be foliated into concentric circles with a singular point at the origin. This corresponds to an integrable singular system

$$d(x^2+y^2+z^2) = 0 \implies xdx + ydy + zdz = 0$$

### Completely non-integrable systems {#Completely_non-integrable_systems}

Not every Pfaffian system is completely integrable in the Frobenius sense. For example, consider the following one-form on **R**<sup>3</sup> ∖ (0,0,0):

$$\theta=z\,dx +x\,dy+y\,dz.$$

If *dθ* were in the ideal generated by *θ* we would have, by the skewness of the wedge product

$$\theta\wedge d\theta=0.$$

But a direct calculation gives

$$\theta\wedge d\theta=(x+y+z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz ,$$

which is a nonzero multiple of the standard [volume form](wikipedia://en/1855811) on **R**<sup>3</sup>. Therefore, there are no two-dimensional leaves, and the system is not completely integrable.

On the other hand, for the curve defined by

$$x = t, \quad y = c, \quad z = e^{-t/c}, \qquad t > 0$$

then *θ* defined as above is 0, and hence the curve is easily verified to be a solution (i.e. an [integral curve](wikipedia://en/1322061)) for the above Pfaffian system for any nonzero constant *c*.

In general, a 1-form $\theta$ in a manifold of dimension $2n+1$ is completely non-integrable iff $\theta \wedge d\theta^n \neq 0$ everywhere. By a theorem of Pfaff, generalized by [Darboux's theorem](wikipedia://en/1543358), there exists local coordinates in which it is of the form $dz - \sum_{i=1}^n y_i dx_i$. Such structures are [contact structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_structure).

Analogously, in an even-dimensional manifold, a 1-form $\theta$ in a manifold of dimension $2n+2$ is completely non-integrable iff $\theta \wedge d\theta^n \neq 0$ everywhere. Such structures are even-contact structures.

The [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792) $\mathbb{S}^3$ can be given a contact structure by considering it as the [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090) in $\mathbb{C}^2$. The standard contact form on $\mathbb{S}^3$ is:

$$\alpha=\frac{1}{2}\left(x_1 d y_1-y_1 d x_1+x_2 d y_2-y_2 d x_2\right)$$

where $\left(x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2\right)$ are coordinates on $\mathbb{R}^4$.

### Partially integrable system {#Partially_integrable_system}

Some Pfaffian systems do not have a maximal integrable foliation, but are also not completely non-integrable.

For example, the standard contact structure on $\mathbb{R}^3$, defined by the 1-form $dx_0 - x_2dx_1$, is completely non-integrable, in the sense that any integral manifold of it can have only 1 dimension (these are called the [Legendrian submanifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legendrian_submanifold)). However, if we were to extend to $\mathbb{R}^5$, then $dx_0 - x_2dx_1$ can have an integral manifold of 3 dimensions. This is not the lowest dimension achievable, so it is neither completely integrable nor completely non-integrable, making it partially integrable.

In $\mathbb{R}^5$, a completely integrable 1-form would have integral manifolds of 4 dimensions, and the standard contact structure $dx_0 - x_2dx_1 - x_4dx_3$ can only have integral manifolds of 2 dimensions, which makes it completely non-integrable.

## Applications {#Applications}

In [pseudo-Riemannian geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Riemannian_geometry), we may consider the problem of finding an orthogonal [coframe](wikipedia://en/3131766) *θ*<sup>*i*</sup>, i.e., a collection of 1-forms that form a basis of the [cotangent space](wikipedia://en/6620) at every point with $\langle\theta^i,\theta^j\rangle=\delta^{ij}$ that are closed (*dθ*<sup>*i*</sup> = 0, *i* = 1, 2, ..., *n*). By the [Poincaré lemma](wikipedia://en/600030), the *θ*<sup>*i*</sup> locally will have the form *dx<sup>i</sup>* for some functions *x<sup>i</sup>* on the manifold, and thus provide an isometry of an open subset of *M* with an open subset of **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. Such a manifold is called **locally flat**.

This problem reduces to a question on the [coframe bundle](wikipedia://en/366550) of *M*. Suppose we had such a closed coframe

$$\Theta=(\theta^1,\dots,\theta^n).$$

If we had another coframe $\Phi=(\phi^1,\dots,\phi^n)$, then the two coframes would be related by an orthogonal transformation

$$\Phi=M\Theta$$

If the connection 1-form is *ω*, then we have

$$d\Phi=\omega\wedge\Phi$$

On the other hand,

$$\begin{aligned} d\Phi & = (dM)\wedge\Theta+M\wedge d\Theta \\ & =(dM)\wedge\Theta \\ & =(dM)M^{-1}\wedge\Phi. \end{aligned}$$

But $\omega=(dM)M^{-1}$ is the [Maurer–Cartan form](wikipedia://en/964177) for the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954). Therefore, it obeys the structural equation $d\omega+\omega\wedge\omega=0$, and this is just the [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) of *M*: $\Omega=d\omega+\omega\wedge\omega=0.$ After an application of the Frobenius theorem, one concludes that a manifold *M* is locally flat [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its curvature vanishes.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Many generalizations exist to integrability conditions on differential systems that are not necessarily generated by one-forms. The most famous of these are the [Cartan–Kähler theorem](wikipedia://en/1968544), which only works for [real analytic](wikipedia://en/26478) differential systems, and the [Cartan–Kuranishi prolongation theorem](wikipedia://en/5710861). See for details. The [Newlander–Nirenberg theorem](wikipedia://en/514258#Integrable_almost_complex_structures) gives integrability conditions for an almost-complex structure.

:::
