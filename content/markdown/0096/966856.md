# Valuation ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Valuation ring"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **valuation ring** is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) *D* such that for every non-zero element *x* of its [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) *F*, at least one of *x* or *x*<sup>−1</sup> belongs to *D*.

:::

Given a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *F*, if *D* is a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of *F* such that either *x* or *x*<sup>−1</sup> belongs to *D* for every nonzero *x* in *F*, then *D* is said to be **a valuation ring for the field *F*** or a **place** of *F*. Since *F* in this case is indeed the field of fractions of *D*, a valuation ring for a field is a valuation ring. Another way to characterize the valuation rings of a field *F* is that valuation rings *D* of *F* have *F* as their field of fractions, and their [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) are [totally ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_ordered) by [inclusion](wikipedia://en/27631); or equivalently their [principal ideals](wikipedia://en/145126) are totally ordered by inclusion. In particular, every valuation ring is a [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391).

The valuation rings of a field are the maximal elements of the set of the local subrings in the field [partially ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) by **dominance** or **refinement**,[^n1] where

$(A,\mathfrak{m}_A)$ dominates $(B,\mathfrak{m}_B)$ if $A \supseteq B$ and $\mathfrak{m}_A \cap B = \mathfrak{m}_B$.[^n2]

Every local ring in a field *K* is dominated by some valuation ring of *K*.

An integral domain whose [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) at any [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) is a valuation ring is called a [Prüfer domain](wikipedia://en/12367835).

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

There are several equivalent definitions of valuation ring (see below for the characterization in terms of dominance). For an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) *D* and its [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) *K*, the following are equivalent:

1. For every non-zero *x* in *K*, at least one of *x* or *x*<sup>−1</sup> is in *D*.
1. The ideals of *D* are [totally ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_ordered) by inclusion.
1. The principal ideals of *D* are [totally ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_ordered) by inclusion (i.e. the elements in *D* are, [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [units](wikipedia://en/289666), totally ordered by [divisibility](wikipedia://en/32981716)).
1. There is a [totally ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_ordered_group) [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) Γ (called the **value group**) and a **[valuation](wikipedia://en/347049)** ν: *K* → Γ ∪ {∞} with *D* = { *x* ∈ *K* | ν(*x*) ≥ 0 }.

The equivalence of the first three definitions follows easily. A theorem of (Krull 1939) states that any [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) satisfying the first three conditions satisfies the fourth: take Γ to be the [quotient](wikipedia://en/11526) *K*<sup>×</sup>/*D*<sup>×</sup> of the [unit group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_group) of *K* by the unit group of *D*, and take ν to be the natural projection. We can turn Γ into a [totally ordered group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_ordered_group) by declaring the residue classes of elements of *D* as "positive".

Even further, given any totally ordered abelian group Γ, there is a valuation ring *D* with value group Γ (see [Hahn series](wikipedia://en/21368973)).

From the fact that the ideals of a valuation ring are totally ordered, one can conclude that a valuation ring is a local domain, and that every finitely generated ideal of a valuation ring is principal (i.e., a valuation ring is a [Bézout domain](wikipedia://en/3131407)). In fact, it is a theorem of Krull that an integral domain is a valuation ring if and only if it is a local Bézout domain.[^n3] It also follows from this that a valuation ring is [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) if and only if it is a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039). In this case, it is either a field or it has exactly one non-zero prime ideal; in the latter case it is called a [discrete valuation ring](wikipedia://en/848633). (By convention, a field is not a discrete valuation ring.)

A value group is called *discrete* if it is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to the additive group of the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563#Algebraic_properties), and a valuation ring has a discrete valuation group if and only if it is a [discrete valuation ring](wikipedia://en/848633).[^n4]

Very rarely, *valuation ring* may refer to a ring that satisfies the second or third condition but is not necessarily a domain. A more common term for this type of ring is *[uniserial ring](wikipedia://en/31157948)*.

## Examples {#Examples}

- Any field $\mathbb{F}$ is a valuation ring. For example, the field of rational functions $\mathbb{F}(X)$ on an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) $X$.[^o11][^o12]
- A simple non-example is the integral domain $\mathbb{C}[X]$ since the inverse of a generic $f/g \in \mathbb{C}(X)$ is $g/f \not\in \mathbb{C}[X]$.
- The field of [power series](wikipedia://en/59958):

$$\mathbb{F}((X)) =\left\{ f(X) =\! \sum_{i>-\infty}^\infty a_iX^i \, :\ a_i \in \mathbb{F} \right\}$$

has the [valuation](wikipedia://en/347049) $v(f) = \inf\nolimits_{a_n \neq 0} n$. The subring $\mathbb{F}[[X]]$ is a valuation ring as well.

- $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)},$ the [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) of the integers $\mathbb{Z}$ at the prime ideal (*p*), consisting of ratios where the numerator is any integer and the denominator is not divisible by *p*. The field of fractions is the field of rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}.$
- The ring of [meromorphic functions](wikipedia://en/80857) on the entire [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) which have a [Maclaurin series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maclaurin_series) ([Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) expansion at zero) is a valuation ring. The field of fractions are the functions meromorphic on the whole plane. If *f* does not have a Maclaurin series then 1/*f* does.
- Any ring of [*p*-adic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_integer) $\mathbb{Z}_p$ for a given [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) *p* is a [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391), with field of fractions the [*p*-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423) $\mathbb{Q}_p$. The [integral closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_closure) $\mathbb{Z}_p^{\text{cl}}$ of the *p*-adic integers is also a local ring, with field of fractions $\mathbb{Q}_p^{\text{cl}}$ (the [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129) of the *p*-adic numbers). Both $\mathbb{Z}_p$ and $\mathbb{Z}_p^{\text{cl}}$ are valuation rings.
- Let **k** be an [ordered field](wikipedia://en/22430). An element of **k** is called finite if it lies between two integers *n* \< *x* \< *m*; otherwise it is called infinite. The set *D* of finite elements of **k** is a valuation ring. The set of elements *x* such that *x* ∈ *D* and *x*<sup>−1</sup> ∉ *D* is the set of [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) elements; and an element *x* such that *x* ∉ *D* and *x*<sup>−1</sup> ∈ *D* is called infinite.
- The ring **F** of finite elements of a [hyperreal field](wikipedia://en/51429) \***R** (an ordered field containing the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438)) is a valuation ring of \***R**. **F** consists of all hyperreal numbers differing from a standard real by an infinitesimal amount, which is equivalent to saying a hyperreal number *x* such that −*n* \< *x* \< *n* for some standard integer *n*. The [residue field](wikipedia://en/10789680), finite hyperreal numbers modulo the ideal of infinitesimal hyperreal numbers, is isomorphic to the real numbers.
- A common geometric example comes from [algebraic plane curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_plane_curve). Consider the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $\mathbb{C}[x, y]$ and an [irreducible polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) $f$ in that ring. Then the ring $\mathbb{C}[x, y] / (f)$ is the ring of polynomial functions on the curve $\{(x, y) : f(x, y) = 0\}$. Choose a point $P = (P_x, P_y) \in \mathbb{C} ^2$ such that $f(P) = 0$ and it is a [regular point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_point) on the curve; i.e., the local ring *R* at the point is a [regular local ring](wikipedia://en/506959) of [Krull dimension](wikipedia://en/56079) one or a [discrete valuation ring](wikipedia://en/848633).
- For example, consider the inclusion $(\mathbb{C}[[X^2]],(X^2)) \hookrightarrow (\mathbb{C}[[X]],(X))$. These are all subrings in the field of bounded-below power series $\mathbb{C}((X))$.

:::

## Dominance and integral closure {#Dominance_and_integral_closure}

The [units](wikipedia://en/289666), or invertible elements, of a valuation ring are the elements *x* in *D* such that *x*<sup>−1</sup> is also a member of *D*. The other elements of *D* – called nonunits – do not have an inverse in *D*, and they form an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) *M*. This ideal is maximal among the (totally ordered) ideals of D. Since *M* is a [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164), the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) *D*/*M* is a field, called the **residue field** of *D*.

In general, we say a [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) $(S,\mathfrak{m}_S)$ dominates a local ring $(R,\mathfrak{m}_R)$ if $S \supseteq R$ and $\mathfrak{m}_S \cap R = \mathfrak{m}_R$; in other words, the inclusion $R \subseteq S$ is a [local ring homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_ring_homomorphism). Every local ring $(A, \mathfrak{p})$ in a field *K* is dominated by some valuation ring of *K*. Indeed, the set consisting of all subrings *R* of *K* containing *A* and $1 \not\in \mathfrak{p}R$ is [nonempty](wikipedia://en/9566) and is inductive; thus, has a maximal element $R$ by [Zorn's lemma](wikipedia://en/51442). We claim *R* is a valuation ring. *R* is a local ring with maximal ideal containing $\mathfrak{p}R$ by maximality. Again by maximality it is also integrally closed. Now, if $x \not\in R$, then, by maximality, $\mathfrak{p}R[x] = R[x]$ and thus we can write:

$$1 = r_0 + r_1 x + \cdots + r_n x^n, \quad r_i \in \mathfrak{p}R.$$

Since $1 - r_0$ is a unit element, this implies that $x^{-1}$ is integral over *R*; thus is in *R*. This proves *R* is a valuation ring. (*R* dominates *A* since its maximal ideal contains $\mathfrak{p}$ by construction.)

A local ring *R* in a field *K* is a valuation ring if and only if it is a maximal element of the set of all local rings contained in *K* partially ordered by dominance. This easily follows from the above.

Let *A* be a subring of a field *K* and $f: A \to k$ a [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) into an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) *k*. Then *f* extends to a ring homomorphism $g: D \to k$, *D* some valuation ring of *K* containing *A*. (Proof: Let $g: R \to k$ be a maximal extension, which clearly exists by Zorn's lemma. By maximality, *R* is a local ring with maximal ideal containing the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of *f*. If *S* is a local ring dominating *R*, then *S* is algebraic over *R*; if not, $S$ contains a polynomial ring $R[x]$ to which *g* extends, a contradiction to maximality. It follows $S/\mathfrak{m}_S$ is an algebraic field extension of $R/\mathfrak{m}_R$. Thus, $S \to S/\mathfrak{m}_S \hookrightarrow k$ extends *g*; hence, *S* = *R*.)

If a subring *R* of a field *K* contains a valuation ring *D* of *K*, then, by checking Definition 1, *R* is also a valuation ring of *K*. In particular, *R* is local and its maximal ideal contracts to some prime ideal of *D*, say, $\mathfrak{p}$. Then $R = D_\mathfrak{p}$ since $R$ dominates $D_\mathfrak{p}$, which is a valuation ring since the ideals are totally ordered. This observation is subsumed to the following:[^n5] there is a [bijective](wikipedia://en/3942) correspondence $\mathfrak{p} \mapsto D_\mathfrak{p}, \operatorname{Spec}(D) \to$ the set of all subrings of *K* containing *D*. In particular, *D* is integrally closed,[^n6] and the [Krull dimension](wikipedia://en/56079) of *D* is the number of proper subrings of *K* containing *D*.

In fact, the [integral closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_closure) of an integral domain *A* in the field of fractions *K* of *A* is the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of all valuation rings of *K* containing *A*.[^n7] Indeed, the integral closure is contained in the intersection since the valuation rings are integrally closed. Conversely, let *x* be in *K* but not integral over *A*. Since the ideal $x^{-1} A[x^{-1}]$ is not $A[x^{-1}]$, it is contained in a maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p}$. Then there is a valuation ring *R* that dominates the localization of $A[x^{-1}]$ at $\mathfrak{p}$. Since $x^{-1} \in \mathfrak{m}_R$, $x \not\in R$.

The dominance is used in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997). Let *X* be an algebraic variety over a field *k*. Then we say a valuation ring *R* in $k(X)$ has "center *x* on *X*" if $R$ dominates the local ring $\mathcal{O}_{x, X}$ of the structure sheaf at *x*.[^n8]

## Ideals in valuation rings {#Ideals_in_valuation_rings}

We may describe the ideals in the valuation ring by means of its value group.

Let Γ be a [totally ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_ordered_group) [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974). A subset Δ of Γ is called a *segment* if it is nonempty and, for any α in Δ, any element between −α and α is also in Δ (end points included). A subgroup of Γ is called an *isolated subgroup* if it is a segment and is a proper subgroup.

Let *D* be a valuation ring with valuation *v* and value group Γ. For any subset *A* of *D*, we let $\Gamma_A$ be the complement of the union of $v(A - 0)$ and $-v(A - 0)$ in $\Gamma$. If *I* is a proper ideal, then $\Gamma_I$ is a segment of $\Gamma$. In fact, the mapping $I \mapsto \Gamma_I$ defines an inclusion-reversing bijection between the set of proper ideals of *D* and the set of segments of $\Gamma$.[^n9] Under this correspondence, the nonzero prime ideals of *D* correspond bijectively to the isolated subgroups of Γ.

Example: The ring of *p*-adic integers $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is a valuation ring with value group $\mathbb{Z}$. The zero subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ corresponds to the unique maximal ideal $(p) \subseteq \mathbb{Z}_p$ and the whole group to the [zero ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_ideal). The maximal ideal is the only isolated subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$.

The set of isolated subgroups is totally ordered by inclusion. The **height** or **rank** *r*(Γ) of Γ is defined to be the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of the set of isolated subgroups of Γ. Since the nonzero prime ideals are totally ordered and they correspond to isolated subgroups of Γ, the height of Γ is equal to the [Krull dimension](wikipedia://en/56079) of the valuation ring *D* associated with Γ.

The most important special case is height one, which is equivalent to Γ being a subgroup of the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\mathbb{R}$ under addition (or equivalently, of the [positive real numbers](wikipedia://en/47172571) $\mathbb{R}^{+}$ under multiplication.) A valuation ring with a valuation of height one has a corresponding [absolute value](wikipedia://en/5042667) defining an [ultrametric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrametric) [place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place_%28mathematics%29). A special case of this are the [discrete valuation rings](wikipedia://en/848633) mentioned earlier.

The **rational rank** *rr*(Γ) is defined as the rank of the value group as an abelian group,

$$\mathrm{dim}_\mathbb{Q}(\Gamma \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \mathbb{Q}).$$

## Places {#Places}

::: {#o23 .definition cue=heading name="General definition"}

### General definition {#General_definition}

A *[place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place_%28mathematics%29)* of a field *K* is a ring homomorphism *p* from a valuation ring *D* of *K* to some field such that, for any $x \not\in D$, $p(1/x) = 0$. The image of a place is a field called the **residue field** of *p*. For example, the canonical map $D \to D/\mathfrak{m}_D$ is a place.

::: {#o25 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

#### Example {#Example}

Let *A* be a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132) and $\mathfrak{p}$ a prime ideal. Then the canonical map $A_{\mathfrak{p}} \to k(\mathfrak{p})$ is a place.

:::

:::

### Specialization of places {#Specialization_of_places}

We say a **place *p* *specializes to* a place *p*,** denoted by $p \rightsquigarrow p'$, if the valuation ring of *p* contains the valuation ring of *p'*. In algebraic geometry, we say a prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ [specializes](wikipedia://en/542347) to $\mathfrak{p}'$ if $\mathfrak{p} \subseteq \mathfrak{p}'$. The two notions coincide: $p \rightsquigarrow p'$ if and only if a prime ideal corresponding to *p* specializes to a prime ideal corresponding to *p* in some valuation ring (recall that if $D \supseteq D'$ are valuation rings of the same field, then *D* corresponds to a prime ideal of $D'$.)

::: {#o28 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

#### Example {#Example}

For example, in the function field $\mathbb{F}(X)$ of some algebraic variety $X$ every prime ideal $\mathfrak{p} \in \text{Spec}(R)$ contained in a maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ gives a specialization $\mathfrak{p} \rightsquigarrow \mathfrak{m}$.

:::

#### Remarks {#Remarks}

It can be shown: if $p \rightsquigarrow p'$, then $p' = q \circ p|_{D'}$ for some place *q* of the residue field $k(p)$ of *p*. (Observe $p(D')$ is a valuation ring of $k(p)$ and let *q* be the corresponding place; the rest is mechanical.) If *D* is a valuation ring of *p*, then its Krull dimension is the cardinarity of the specializations other than *p* to *p*. Thus, for any place *p* with valuation ring *D* of a field *K* over a field *k*, we have:

$$\operatorname{tr.deg}_k k(p) + \dim D \le \operatorname{tr.deg}_k K.$$

If *p* is a place and *A* is a subring of the valuation ring of *p*, then $\operatorname{ker}(p) \cap A$ is called the *center* of *p* in *A*.

### Places at infinity {#Places_at_infinity}

For the function field on an affine variety $X$ there are valuations which are not associated to any of the primes of $X$. These valuations are called **the places at infinity**.[^o31] For example, the affine line $\mathbb{A}^1_k$ has function field $k(x)$. The place associated to the localization of

$$k\left[\frac{1}{x}\right]$$

at the maximal ideal

$$\mathfrak{m} = \left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$$

is a place at infinity.
