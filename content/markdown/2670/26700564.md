# Harmonic differential

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Harmonic differential"}

In mathematics, a real [differential one-form](wikipedia://en/221519) *ω* on a surface is called a **harmonic differential** if *ω* and its conjugate one-form, written as *ω*<sup>∗</sup>, are both [closed](wikipedia://en/404181).

:::

## Explanation {#Explanation}

Consider the case of real one-forms defined on a two dimensional [real manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_manifold). Moreover, consider real one-forms that are the real parts of [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) differentials. Let *ω* = *A* d*x* + *B* d*y*, and formally define the **conjugate** one-form to be *ω*<sup>∗</sup> = *A* d*y* − *B* d*x*.

## Motivation {#Motivation}

There is a clear connection with [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759). Let us write a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) *z* in terms of its [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part) and [imaginary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_part) parts, say *x* and *y* respectively, i.e. *z* = *x* + *iy*. Since *ω* + *iω*<sup>∗</sup> = (*A* − *iB*)(d*x* + *i* d*y*), from the point of view of [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), the [quotient](wikipedia://en/332640) (*ω* + *iω*<sup>∗</sup>)/d*z* tends to a [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602) as d*z* tends to 0. In other words, the definition of *ω*<sup>∗</sup> was chosen for its connection with the concept of a derivative ([analyticity](wikipedia://en/61478)). Another connection with the [complex unit](wikipedia://en/52358) is that (*ω*<sup>∗</sup>)<sup>∗</sup> = −*ω* (just as *i*<sup>2</sup> = −1).

For a given [function](wikipedia://en/185427) *f*, let us write *ω* = d*f*, i.e. *ω* = $\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ d*x* + $\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ d*y*, where ∂ denotes the [partial derivative](wikipedia://en/52565). Then (d*f*)<sup>∗</sup> = $\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ d*y* − $\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ d*x*. Now d((d*f*)<sup>∗</sup>) is not always zero, indeed d((d*f*)<sup>∗</sup>) = Δ*f* d*x* d*y*, where Δ*f* = $\tfrac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x^{2}}$ + $\tfrac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial y^{2}}$.

## Cauchy–Riemann equations {#Cauchy–Riemann_equations}

As we have seen above: we call the one-form *ω* **harmonic** if both *ω* and *ω*<sup>∗</sup> are closed. This means that $\tfrac{\partial A}{\partial y}$ = $\tfrac{\partial B}{\partial x}$ (*ω* is closed) and $\tfrac{\partial B}{\partial y}$ = −$\tfrac{\partial A}{\partial x}$ (*ω*<sup>∗</sup> is closed). These are called the [Cauchy&ndash;Riemann equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy&ndash;Riemann_equations) on *A* − *iB*. Usually they are expressed in terms of *u*(*x*, *y*) + *iv*(*x*, *y*) as $\tfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}$ = $\tfrac{\partial v}{\partial y}$ and $\tfrac{\partial v}{\partial x}$ = −$\tfrac{\partial u}{\partial y}$.

## Notable results {#Notable_results}

- A harmonic differential (one-form) is precisely the real part of an (analytic) complex differential.[^o6] To prove this one shows that *u* + *iv* satisfies the Cauchy–Riemann equations exactly when *u* + *iv* is *[locally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighbourhood_%28topology%29)* an analytic function of *x* + *iy*. Of course an analytic function *w*(*z*) = *u* + *iv* is the local derivative of something (namely ∫*w*(*z*) d*z*).
- The harmonic differentials *ω* are (locally) precisely the differentials d*f* of solutions *f* to [Laplace's equation](wikipedia://en/36941) Δ*f* = 0.[^o6]
- If *ω* is a harmonic differential, so is *ω*<sup>∗</sup>.[^o6]
