# Association scheme

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Association scheme"}

The theory of **association schemes** arose in [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), in the theory of [experimental design](wikipedia://en/9541) for the [analysis of variance](wikipedia://en/634).[^o2][^o3][^o4] In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), association schemes belong to both [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) and [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170). In [algebraic combinatorics](wikipedia://en/14835582), association schemes provide a unified approach to many topics, for example [combinatorial designs](wikipedia://en/2761968) and [the theory of error-correcting codes](wikipedia://en/321869).[^o5][^o6] In algebra, the theory of association schemes generalizes the [character theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_character) of [linear representations of groups](wikipedia://en/12695).[^o7][^o8][^o9]

:::

::: {#o11 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

An *n*-class association scheme consists of a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) *X* together with a [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) *S* of *X* × *X* into *n* + 1 [binary relations](wikipedia://en/3931), *R*<sub>0</sub>, *R*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *R*<sub>*n*</sub> which satisfy:

- $R_{0} = \{(x,x) : x \in X\}$; it is called the [identity relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_relation).
- Defining $R^* := \{(x,y) : (y,x) \in R\}$, if *R* in *S*, then *R\** in *S*.
- If $(x,y) \in R_{k}$, the number of $z \in X$ such that $(x,z) \in R_{i}$ and $(z,y) \in R_{j}$ is a constant $p^k_{ij}$ depending on $i$, $j$, $k$ but not on the particular choice of $x$ and $y$.

An association scheme is *commutative* if $p_{ij}^k = p_{ji}^k$ for all $i$, $j$ and $k$. Most authors assume this property. Note, however, that while the notion of an association scheme generalizes the notion of a group, the notion of a commutative association scheme only generalizes the notion of a [commutative group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_group).

A *symmetric* association scheme is one in which each $R_i$ is a [symmetric relation](wikipedia://en/200459). That is:

- if (*x*, *y*) ∈ *R*<sub>*i*</sub>, then (*y*, *x*) ∈ *R*<sub>*i*</sub>. (Or equivalently, *R*\* = *R*.)

Every symmetric association scheme is commutative.

Two points *x* and *y* are called *i*th associates if $(x,y) \in R_i$. The definition states that if *x* and *y* are *i*th associates then so are *y* and *x*. Every pair of points are *i*th associates for exactly one $i$. Each point is its own zeroth associate while distinct points are never zeroth associates. If *x* and *y* are *k*th associates then the number of points $z$ which are both *i*th associates of $x$ and *j*th associates of $y$ is a constant $p^k_{ij}$.

### Graph interpretation and adjacency matrices {#Graph_interpretation_and_adjacency_matrices}

A symmetric association scheme can be visualized as a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) with labeled edges. The graph has $v$ vertices, one for each point of $X$, and the edge joining vertices $x$ and $y$ is labeled $i$ if $x$ and $y$ are $i$th associates. Each edge has a unique label, and the number of triangles with a fixed base labeled $k$ having the other edges labeled $i$ and $j$ is a constant $p^k_{ij}$, depending on $i,j,k$ but not on the choice of the base. In particular, each vertex is incident with exactly $p^0_{ii}=v_{i}$ edges labeled $i$; $v_{i}$ is the [valency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjacency_relation) of the [relation](wikipedia://en/22517558) $R_{i}$. There are also loops labeled $0$ at each vertex $x$, corresponding to $R_{0}$.

The [relations](wikipedia://en/22517558) are described by their [adjacency matrices](wikipedia://en/244463). $A_i$ is the adjacency matrix of $R_{i}$ for $i=0,\ldots,n$ and is a *v* × *v* [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) with rows and columns labeled by the points of $X$.

$$\left( A_i \right)_{x,y} = \begin{cases} 1, & \mbox{if } (x,y) \in R_{i},\\ 0, & \mbox{otherwise.} \end{cases}\qquad (1)$$

The definition of a symmetric association scheme is equivalent to saying that the $A_i$ are *v* × *v* [(0,1)-matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%280,1%29-matrix) which satisfy

I. $A_i$ is symmetric,

II. $\sum_{i=0}^n A_i = J$ (the all-ones matrix),

III. $A_0 = I$,

IV. $A_i A_j = \sum_{k=0}^n p^k_{ij}A_k = A_j A_i, i,j=0,\ldots,n$.

The (*x*, *y*)-th entry of the left side of (IV) is the number of paths of length two between *x* and *y* with labels *i* and *j* in the graph. Note that the rows and columns of $A_{i}$ contain $v_{i}$ $1$'s:

$$A_{i} J=J A_{i}=v_{i} J. \qquad(2)$$

### Terminology {#Terminology}

- The numbers $p_{ij}^k$ are called the *parameters* of the scheme. They are also referred to as the *structural constants*.

## History {#History}

The term *association scheme* is due to (Bose Shimamoto 1952) but the concept is already inherent in (Bose Nair 1939).[^o19] These authors were studying what statisticians have called *partially balanced incomplete block designs* (PBIBDs). The subject became an object of algebraic interest with the publication of (Bose Mesner 1959) and the introduction of the [Bose–Mesner algebra](wikipedia://en/22649391). The most important contribution to the theory was the thesis of [Ph. Delsarte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippe_Delsarte) (Delsarte 1973) who recognized and fully used the connections with coding theory and design theory.[^o22]

A generalization called coherent configurations has been studied by D. G. Higman.

:::

## Basic facts {#Basic_facts}

- $p_{00}^0 = 1$, i.e., if $(x,y) \in R_0$ then $x = y$ and the only $z$ such that $(x,z) \in R_0$ is $z=x$.
- $\sum_{i=0}^{k} p_{ii}^0 = |X|$; this is because the $R_i$ partition $X\times X$.

## The Bose–Mesner algebra {#The_Bose–Mesner_algebra}

The [adjacency matrices](wikipedia://en/244463) $A_i$ of the [graphs](wikipedia://en/325806) $\left(X,R_{i}\right)$ generate a [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_algebra_%28structure%29) and [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) $\mathcal{A}$ (over the [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826)) both for the [matrix product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_product) and the [Hadamard (entrywise) product](wikipedia://en/34260496). The algebra formed with the matrix product is called the [Bose–Mesner algebra](wikipedia://en/22649391) of the association scheme.

Since the matrices in $\mathcal{A}$ are [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474) and [commute](wikipedia://en/25264990) with each other, they can be [diagonalized](wikipedia://en/174080) simultaneously. Therefore, $\mathcal{A}$ is [semi-simple](wikipedia://en/7252030) and has a unique basis of primitive [idempotents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent) $J_{0},\ldots,J_{n}$.

There is another algebra of $(n+1)\times(n+1)$ matrices which is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to $\mathcal{A}$, and is often easier to work with.

## Examples {#Examples}

- The [Johnson scheme](wikipedia://en/22649423), denoted by *J*(*v*, *k*), is defined as follows. Let *S* be a set with *v* elements. The points of the scheme *J*(*v*, *k*) are the ${v \choose k}$ [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of S with *k* elements. Two *k*-element subsets *A*, *B* of *S* are *i*th associates when their intersection has size *k* − *i*.
- The [Hamming scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamming_scheme), denoted by *H*(*n*, *q*), is defined as follows. The points of *H*(*n*, *q*) are the *q<sup>n</sup>* ordered *n*-[tuples](wikipedia://en/132729) over a set of size *q*. Two *n*-tuples *x*, *y* are said to be *i*th associates if they disagree in exactly *i* coordinates. E.g., if *x* = (1,0,1,1), *y* = (1,1,1,1), *z* = (0,0,1,1), then *x* and *y* are 1st associates, *x* and *z* are 1st associates and *y* and *z* are 2nd associates in *H*(4,2).
- A [distance-regular graph](wikipedia://en/7768943), *G*, forms an association scheme by defining two vertices to be *i*th associates if their distance is *i*.
- A [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) *G* yields an association scheme on $X=G$, with a class *R*<sub>*g*</sub> for each group element, as follows: for each $g \in G$ let $R_g = \{(x,y) \mid x=g*y\}$ where $*$ is the group [operation](wikipedia://en/4140245). The class of the [group identity](wikipedia://en/14962) is *R*<sub>0</sub>. This association scheme is commutative [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) *G* is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974).
- A specific 3-class association scheme:[^o27]

Let *A*(3) be the following association scheme with three associate classes on the set *X* = {1,2,3,4,5,6}. The (*i*, *j*) entry is *s* if elements *i* and *j* are in relation *R*<sub>*s*</sub>.

|  | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | 0 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 3 |
| **2** | 1 | 0 | 1 | 3 | 2 | 3 |
| **3** | 1 | 1 | 0 | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| **4** | 2 | 3 | 3 | 0 | 1 | 1 |
| **5** | 3 | 2 | 3 | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| **6** | 3 | 3 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 0 |

## Coding theory {#Coding_theory}

The [Hamming scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamming_scheme) and the [Johnson scheme](wikipedia://en/22649423) are of major significance in classical [coding theory](wikipedia://en/321869).

In [coding theory](wikipedia://en/321869), association scheme theory is mainly concerned with the [distance](wikipedia://en/41227) of a [code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code). The [linear programming](wikipedia://en/43730) method produces upper bounds for the size of a [code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code) with given minimum [distance](wikipedia://en/41227), and lower bounds for the size of a [design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-design) with a given strength. The most specific results are obtained in the case where the underlying association scheme satisfies certain [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) properties; this leads one into the realm of [orthogonal polynomials](wikipedia://en/32811718). In particular, some universal bounds are derived for [codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code) and [designs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-design) in polynomial-type association schemes.

In classical [coding theory](wikipedia://en/321869), dealing with [codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code) in a [Hamming scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamming_scheme), the MacWilliams transform involves a family of orthogonal polynomials known as the [Krawtchouk polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krawtchouk_polynomials). These polynomials give the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of the distance relation matrices of the [Hamming scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamming_scheme).
