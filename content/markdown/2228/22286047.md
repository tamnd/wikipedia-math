# Extremum estimator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Extremum estimator"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) and [econometrics](wikipedia://en/10390), **extremum estimators** are a wide [class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_%28mathematics%29) of [estimators](wikipedia://en/10043) for [parametric models](wikipedia://en/2883137) that are calculated through maximization (or minimization) of a certain [objective function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objective_function), which depends on the data. The general theory of extremum estimators was developed by (Amemiya 1985).

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

An estimator $\scriptstyle\hat\theta$ is called an **extremum estimator**, if there is an *objective function* $\scriptstyle\hat{Q}_n$ such that

$$\hat\theta = \underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\operatorname{arg\;max}}\ \widehat{Q}_n(\theta),$$

where Θ is the [parameter space](wikipedia://en/2882813). Sometimes a slightly weaker definition is given:

$$\widehat Q_n(\hat\theta) \geq \max_{\theta\in\Theta}\,\widehat Q_n(\theta) - o_p(1),$$

where *o*<sub>*p*</sub>(1) is the variable [converging in probability to zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_o_in_probability_notation). With this modification $\scriptstyle\hat\theta$ doesn't have to be the exact maximizer of the objective function, just be sufficiently close to it.

The theory of extremum estimators does not specify what the objective function should be. There are various types of objective functions suitable for different models, and this framework allows us to analyse the theoretical properties of such estimators from a unified perspective. The theory only specifies the properties that the objective function has to possess, and so selecting a particular objective function only requires verifying that those properties are satisfied.

:::

## Consistency {#Consistency}

*Image: Ee noncompactness.svg.* When the parameter space Θ is not compact (Θ = [**R**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) in this example), then even if the objective function is uniquely maximized at *θ*<sub>0</sub>, this maximum may be not well-separated, in which case the estimator $\scriptscriptstyle\hat\theta$ will fail to be consistent.

If the parameter space Θ is [compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set) and there is a *limiting function* *Q*<sub>0</sub>(*θ*) such that: $\scriptstyle\hat{Q}_n(\theta)$ converges to *Q*<sub>0</sub>(*θ*) [in probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergence_in_probability) uniformly over Θ, and the function *Q*<sub>0</sub>(*θ*) is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) and has a unique maximum at *θ* = *θ*<sub>0</sub> then $\scriptstyle\hat\theta$ is **[consistent](wikipedia://en/1497569)** for *θ*<sub>0</sub>.[^o6]

The [uniform convergence in probability](wikipedia://en/22999791) of $\scriptstyle\hat{Q}_n(\theta)$ means that

$$\sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \big| \hat{Q}_n(\theta) - Q_0(\theta) \big| \ \xrightarrow{p}\ 0.$$

The requirement for Θ to be compact can be replaced with a weaker assumption that the maximum of *Q*<sub>0</sub> was well-separated, that is there should not exist any points *θ* that are distant from *θ*<sub>0</sub> but such that *Q*<sub>0</sub>(*θ*) were close to *Q*<sub>0</sub>(*θ*<sub>0</sub>). Formally, it means that for any sequence {*θ<sub>i</sub>*} such that *Q*<sub>0</sub>(*θ<sub>i</sub>*) → *Q*<sub>0</sub>(*θ*<sub>0</sub>), it should be true that *θ<sub>i</sub>* → *θ*<sub>0</sub>.

## Asymptotic normality {#Asymptotic_normality}

Assuming that consistency has been established and the derivatives of the sample $Q_{n}$ satisfy some other conditions,[^o8] the extremum estimator converges to an asymptotically Normal distribution.
