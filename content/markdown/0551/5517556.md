# Extension topology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Extension topology"}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), a branch of mathematics, an **extension topology** is a [topology](wikipedia://en/30450) placed on the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) and another [set](wikipedia://en/26691). There are various types of extension topology, described in the sections below.

:::

## Extension topology {#Extension_topology}

Let *X* be a topological space and *P* a set disjoint from *X*. Consider in *X* ∪ *P* the topology whose open sets are of the form *A* ∪ *Q*, where *A* is an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) of *X* and *Q* is a subset of *P*.

The closed sets of *X* ∪ *P* are of the form *B* ∪ *Q*, where *B* is a [closed set](wikipedia://en/47279) of *X* and *Q* is a subset of *P*.

For these reasons this topology is called the **extension topology** of *X* plus *P*, with which one extends to *X* ∪ *P* the open and the closed sets of *X*. As subsets of *X* ∪ *P* the [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926) of *X* is the original topology of *X*, while the subspace topology of *P* is the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology). As a topological space, *X* ∪ *P* is homeomorphic to the [topological sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_sum) of *X* and *P*, and *X* is a [clopen subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clopen_subset) of *X* ∪ *P*.

If *Y* is a topological space and *R* is a subset of *Y*, one might ask whether the extension topology of *Y* – *R* plus *R* is the same as the original topology of *Y*, and the answer is in general no.

Note the similarity of this extension topology construction and the [Alexandroff one-point compactification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-point_compactification#Alexandroff_one-point_compactification), in which case, having a topological space *X* which one wishes to compactify by adding a point ∞ at infinity, one considers the closed sets of *X* ∪ {∞} to be the sets of the form *K*, where *K* is a closed compact set of *X*, or *B* ∪ {∞}, where *B* is a closed set of *X*.

## Open extension topology {#Open_extension_topology}

Let $(X, \mathcal{T})$ be a topological space and $P$ a set disjoint from $X$. The **open extension topology** of $\mathcal{T}$ plus $P$ is

$$\mathcal{T}^* = \mathcal{T} \cup \{X \cup A : A \subset P\}.$$

Let $X^* = X \cup P$. Then $\mathcal{T}^*$ is a topology in $X^*$. The subspace topology of *$X$* is the original topology of *$X$*, i.e. $\mathcal{T}^*|X = \mathcal{T}$, while the subspace topology of *$P$* is the discrete topology, i.e. $\mathcal{T}^*|P = \mathcal{P}(P)$.

The closed sets in $X^*$ are $\{B \cup P : X \subset B \land X \setminus B \in \mathcal{T}\}$. Note that *$P$* is closed in $X^*$ and *$X$* is open and dense in $X^*$.

If *Y* a topological space and *R* is a subset of *Y*, one might ask whether the open extension topology of *Y* – *R* plus *R* is the same as the original topology of *Y*, and the answer is in general no.

Note that the open extension topology of $X^*$ is [smaller](wikipedia://en/393069) than the extension topology of $X^*$.

Assuming *$X$* and *$P$* are not empty to avoid trivialities, here are a few general properties of the open extension topology:[^n1]

- *$X$* is dense in $X^*$.
- If *$P$* is finite, $X^*$ is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042). So $X^*$ is a [compactification](wikipedia://en/6617) of *$X$* in that case.
- $X^*$ is [connected](wikipedia://en/6233).
- If *$P$* has a single point, $X^*$ is [ultraconnected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraconnected).

For a set *Z* and a point *p* in *Z*, one obtains the [excluded point topology](wikipedia://en/5539197) construction by considering in *Z* the discrete topology and applying the open extension topology construction to *Z* – {*p*} plus *p*.

## Closed extension topology {#Closed_extension_topology}

Let *X* be a topological space and *P* a set disjoint from *X*. Consider in *X* ∪ *P* the topology whose closed sets are of the form *X* ∪ *Q*, where *Q* is a subset of *P*, or *B*, where *B* is a closed set of *X*.

For this reason this topology is called the **closed extension topology** of *X* plus *P*, with which one extends to *X* ∪ *P* the closed sets of *X*. As subsets of *X* ∪ *P* the subspace topology of *X* is the original topology of *X*, while the subspace topology of *P* is the discrete topology.

The open sets of *X* ∪ *P* are of the form *Q*, where *Q* is a subset of *P*, or *A* ∪ *P*, where *A* is an open set of *X*. Note that *P* is open in *X* ∪ *P* and *X* is closed in *X* ∪ *P*.

If *Y* is a topological space and *R* is a subset of *Y*, one might ask whether the closed extension topology of *Y* – *R* plus *R* is the same as the original topology of *Y*, and the answer is in general no.

Note that the closed extension topology of *X* ∪ *P* is [smaller](wikipedia://en/393069) than the extension topology of *X* ∪ *P*.

For a set *Z* and a point *p* in *Z*, one obtains the [particular point topology](wikipedia://en/5336200) construction by considering in *Z* the discrete topology and applying the closed extension topology construction to *Z* – {*p*} plus *p*.
