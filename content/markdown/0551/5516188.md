# Moore plane

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Moore plane"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Moore plane**, also sometimes called **Niemytzki plane** (or **Nemytskii plane**, **Nemytskii's tangent disk topology**), is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450). It is a [completely regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_regular_space) [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637) (that is, a [Tychonoff space](wikipedia://en/30554)) that is not [normal](wikipedia://en/48629). It is an example of a [Moore space](wikipedia://en/8016525) that is not [metrizable](wikipedia://en/19738). It is named after [Robert Lee Moore](wikipedia://en/1077133) and [Viktor Vladimirovich Nemytskii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Vladimirovich_Nemytskii).

:::

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: Niemytzki disk.png.* Open neighborhood of the Niemytzki plane, tangent to the x-axis

If $\Gamma$ is the (closed) upper half-plane $\Gamma = \{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2 | y \geq 0 \}$, then a [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) may be defined on $\Gamma$ by taking a [local basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_basis) $\mathcal{B}(p,q)$ as follows:

- Elements of the local basis at points $(x,y)$ with $y>0$ are the open discs in the plane which are small enough to lie within $\Gamma$.
- Elements of the local basis at points $p = (x,0)$ are sets $\{p\}\cup A$ where *A* is an open disc in the upper half-plane which is tangent to the *x* axis at *p*.

That is, the local basis is given by

$$\mathcal{B}(p,q) = \begin{cases} \{ U_{\epsilon}(p,q):= \{(x,y): (x-p)^2+(y-q)^2 < \epsilon^2 \} \mid \epsilon > 0\}, & \mbox{if } q > 0; \\ \{ V_{\epsilon}(p):= \{(p,0)\} \cup \{(x,y): (x-p)^2+(y-\epsilon)^2 < \epsilon^2 \} \mid \epsilon > 0\}, & \mbox{if } q = 0. \end{cases}$$

Thus the [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926) inherited by $\Gamma\backslash \{(x,0) | x \in \mathbb{R}\}$ is the same as the subspace topology inherited from the standard topology of the Euclidean plane.

## Properties {#Properties}

- The Moore plane $\Gamma$ is [separable](wikipedia://en/27855), that is, it has a countable dense subset.
- The Moore plane is a [completely regular Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/30554) (i.e. [Tychonoff space](wikipedia://en/30554)), which is not [normal](wikipedia://en/48629).
- The subspace $\{(x,0)\in \Gamma | x\in R \}$ of $\Gamma$ has, as its [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926), the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology). Thus, the Moore plane shows that a subspace of a separable space need not be separable.
- The Moore plane is [first countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_countable), but not [second countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_countable) or [Lindelöf](wikipedia://en/310480).
- The Moore plane is not [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact).
- The Moore plane is [countably metacompact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_metacompact) but not [metacompact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metacompact).

## Proof that the Moore plane is not normal {#Proof_that_the_Moore_plane_is_not_normal}

The fact that this space $\Gamma$ is not [normal](wikipedia://en/48629) can be established by the following counting argument (which is very similar to the argument that the [Sorgenfrey plane](wikipedia://en/682853) is not normal):

1. On the one hand, the countable set $S:=\{(p,q) \in \mathbb Q\times \mathbb Q: q>0\}$ of points with rational coordinates is dense in $\Gamma$; hence every continuous function $f:\Gamma \to \mathbb R$ is determined by its restriction to $S$, so there can be at most $|\mathbb R|^{|S|} = 2^{\aleph_0}$ many continuous real-valued functions on $\Gamma$.
1. On the other hand, the real line $L:=\{(p,0): p\in \mathbb R\}$ is a closed discrete subspace of $\Gamma$ with $2^{\aleph_0}$ many points. So there are $2^{2^{\aleph_0}} > 2^{\aleph_0}$ many continuous functions from *L* to $\mathbb R$. Not all these functions can be extended to continuous functions on $\Gamma$.
1. Hence $\Gamma$ is not normal, because by the [Tietze extension theorem](wikipedia://en/31404) all continuous functions defined on a closed subspace of a normal space can be extended to a continuous function on the whole space.

In fact, if *X* is a [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) topological space having an uncountable closed discrete subspace, *X* cannot be normal.
