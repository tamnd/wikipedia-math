# Complex conjugate representation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complex conjugate representation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), if $G$ is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) and $\Pi$ is a [representation](wikipedia://en/19378200) of it over the [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $V$, then the **complex conjugate representation** $\overline{\Pi}$ is defined over the [complex conjugate vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugate_vector_space) $\overline{V}$ as follows:

:::

$\overline{\Pi}(g)$ is the [conjugate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_linear_map) of $\Pi(g)$ for all $g$ in $G$.

$\overline{\Pi}$ is also a representation, as one may check explicitly.

If $\mathbf{g}$ is a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) and $\pi$ is a representation of it over the vector space $V$, then the conjugate representation $\overline{\pi}$ is defined over the conjugate vector space $\overline{V}$ as follows:

$\overline{\pi}(X)$ is the conjugate of $\pi(X)$ for all $X$ in $\mathbf{g}$.[^o2]

$\overline{\pi}$ is also a representation, as one may check explicitly.

If two real Lie algebras have the same [complexification](wikipedia://en/755604), and we have a complex representation of the complexified Lie algebra, their conjugate representations are still going to be different. See [spinor](wikipedia://en/29276) for some examples associated with spinor representations of the [spin groups](wikipedia://en/411231) $\operatorname{Spin}(p + q)$ and $\operatorname{Spin}(p, q)$.

If $\mathfrak{g}$ is a \*-Lie algebra (a complex Lie algebra with a \* operation which is compatible with the Lie bracket),

$\overline{\pi}(X)$ is the conjugate of $-\pi(X*)$ for all $X$ in $\mathbf{g}$

For a finite-dimensional [unitary representation](wikipedia://en/292788), the dual representation and the conjugate representation coincide. This also holds for pseudounitary representations.

## Notes {#Notes}

[^o2]: This is the mathematicians' convention. Physicists use a different convention where the [Lie bracket](wikipedia://en/10282799) of two real vectors is an imaginary vector. In the physicist's convention, insert a minus in the definition.
