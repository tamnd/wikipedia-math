# Iterated function

article

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Iterated function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **iterated function** is a function that is obtained by [composing](wikipedia://en/195947) another function with itself two or several times. The process of repeatedly applying the same function is called [iteration](wikipedia://en/68833). In this process, starting from some initial object, the result of applying a given function is fed again into the function as input, and this process is repeated.

:::

For example, on the image on the right:

$$L = F(K), \ M = F \circ F (K) = F^2(K).$$

Iterated functions are studied in [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), [fractals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractals), [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087), mathematics and [renormalization group](wikipedia://en/291462) physics.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The formal definition of an iterated function on a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) *X* follows.

Let $X$ be a set and $f: X \to X$ be a [function](wikipedia://en/185427).

Defining $f^{n}$ as the *n*-th iterate of $f$, where *n* is a non-negative integer, by:

$$f^0 ~ \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} ~ \operatorname{id}_X$$

and

$$f^{n+1} ~ \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} ~ f \circ f^{n},$$

where $\operatorname{id}_{X}$ is the [identity function](wikipedia://en/15069) on $X$ and $(f \circ g)(x) = f (g(x))$ denotes [function composition](wikipedia://en/195947). This notation has been traced to [John Frederick William Herschel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Frederick_William_Herschel) in 1813.[^o50][^o51][^o53][^o49] Herschel credited [Hans Heinrich Bürmann](wikipedia://en/19714107) for it, but without giving a specific reference to the work of Bürmann, which remains undiscovered.[^o4]

Because the notation $f^{n}$ may refer to both iteration (composition) of the function $f$ or [exponentiation of the function](wikipedia://en/99491#Iterated_functions) $f$ (the latter is commonly used in [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/30367)), some mathematicians choose to use $\circ$ to denote the compositional meaning, writing $f^{\circ n}(x)$ for the $n$-th iterate of the function $f(x)$, as in, for example, $f^{\circ3}(x)$ meaning $f(f(f(x)))$. For the same purpose, $f^{[n]}(x)$ was used by [Benjamin Peirce](wikipedia://en/364270)[^o52][^o49][^o5] whereas [Alfred Pringsheim](wikipedia://en/1068126) and [Jules Molk](wikipedia://en/35227735) suggested ${}^{n}f(x)$ instead.[^o54][^o49][^o47]

:::

## Abelian property and iteration sequences {#Abelian_property_and_iteration_sequences}

In general, the following identity holds for all non-negative integers $m$ and $n$,

$$f^m \circ f^n = f^n \circ f^m = f^{m+n}~.$$

This is structurally identical to the property of [exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491) that $a^{m}a^{n} = a^{m + n}$.

In general, for arbitrary general (negative, non-integer, etc.) indices $m$ and $n$, this relation is called the **translation functional equation**, cf. [Schröder's equation](wikipedia://en/5834541) and [Abel equation](wikipedia://en/5834526). On a logarithmic scale, this reduces to the **nesting property** of [Chebyshev polynomials](wikipedia://en/184539), $T_{m}(T_{n}(x)) = T_{m n}(x)$, since $T_{n}(x) = \cos(n \arccos(x))$.

The relation $(f^{m})^{n}(x) = (f^{n})^{m}(x) = f^{mn}(x)$ also holds, analogous to the property of exponentiation that $(a^{m})^{n} = (a^{n})^{m} = a^{mn}$.

The sequence of functions $f^{n}$ is called a **Picard sequence**,[^o7][^o8] named after [Charles Émile Picard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Émile_Picard).

For a given $x$ in $X$, the [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of values $f^{n}(x)$ is called the **[orbit](wikipedia://en/252250)** of $x$.

If $f^{n} (x) = f^{n+m} (x)$ for some integer $m > 0$, the orbit is called a **periodic orbit**. The smallest such value of $m$ for a given $x$ is called the **period of the orbit**. The point $x$ itself is called a [periodic point](wikipedia://en/4941621). The [cycle detection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycle_detection) problem in computer science is the [algorithmic](wikipedia://en/775) problem of finding the first periodic point in an orbit, and the period of the orbit.

## Fixed points {#Fixed_points}

If $x = f(x)$ for some $x$ in $X$ (that is, the period of the orbit of $x$ is $1$), then $x$ is called a **[fixed point](wikipedia://en/449738)** of the iterated sequence. The set of fixed points is often denoted as $\mathbf{Fix}(f)$. There exist a number of [fixed-point theorems](wikipedia://en/1011848) that guarantee the existence of fixed points in various situations, including the [Banach fixed point theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banach_fixed_point_theorem) and the [Brouwer fixed point theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brouwer_fixed_point_theorem).

There are several techniques for [convergence acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergence_acceleration) of the sequences produced by [fixed point iteration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_point_iteration).[^o10] For example, the [Aitken method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aitken_method) applied to an iterated fixed point is known as [Steffensen's method](wikipedia://en/12180274), and produces quadratic convergence.

## Limiting behaviour {#Limiting_behaviour}

Upon iteration, one may find that there are sets that shrink and converge towards a single point. In such a case, the point that is converged to is known as an [attractive fixed point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attractive_fixed_point). Conversely, iteration may give the appearance of points diverging away from a single point; this would be the case for an [unstable fixed point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unstable_fixed_point).[^o12]

When the points of the orbit converge to one or more limits, the set of [accumulation points](wikipedia://en/226505) of the orbit is known as the **[limit set](wikipedia://en/5492198)** or the **ω-limit set**.

The ideas of attraction and repulsion generalize similarly; one may categorize iterates into [stable sets](wikipedia://en/5466649) and [unstable sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unstable_set), according to the behavior of small [neighborhoods](wikipedia://en/1529485) under iteration. Also see [infinite compositions of analytic functions](wikipedia://en/34796035).

Other limiting behaviors are possible; for example, [wandering points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wandering_point) are points that move away, and never come back even close to where they started.

## Invariant measure {#Invariant_measure}

If one considers the evolution of a density distribution, rather than that of individual point dynamics, then the limiting behavior is given by the [invariant measure](wikipedia://en/7635201). It can be visualized as the behavior of a point-cloud or dust-cloud under repeated iteration. The invariant measure is an eigenstate of the Ruelle-Frobenius-Perron operator or [transfer operator](wikipedia://en/1346096), corresponding to an eigenvalue of 1. Smaller eigenvalues correspond to unstable, decaying states.

In general, because repeated iteration corresponds to a shift, the transfer operator, and its adjoint, the [Koopman operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koopman_operator) can both be interpreted as [shift operators](wikipedia://en/467384) action on a [shift space](wikipedia://en/15470598). The theory of [subshifts of finite type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subshifts_of_finite_type) provides general insight into many iterated functions, especially those leading to chaos.

## Fractional iterates and flows, and negative iterates {#Fractional_iterates_and_flows,_and_negative_iterates}

*Image: TrivFctRootExm svg.svg.* \#20b080 is a trivial functional 5th root of \#901070. The computation of *f*($\tfrac{\pi}{6}$) = $\tfrac{1}{2}$ = *g*<sup>5</sup>($\tfrac{\pi}{6}$) is shown.

The notion $f^{1/n}$ must be used with care when the equation $g^{n}(x) = f(x)$ has multiple solutions, which is normally the case, as in [Babbage's equation](wikipedia://en/7207519) of the functional roots of the identity map. For example, for $n = 2$ and $f(x) = 4x - 6$, both $g(x) = 6 - 2x$ and $g(x) = 2x - 2$ are solutions; so the expression $f^{1/2}(x)$ does not denote a unique function, just as numbers have multiple algebraic roots. A trivial root of *f* can always be obtained if *f*'s domain can be extended sufficiently, cf. picture. The roots chosen are normally the ones belonging to the orbit under study.

Fractional iteration of a function can be defined: for instance, a [half iterate](wikipedia://en/7207519) of a function $f$ is a function $g$ such that $g(g(x)) = f(x)$.[^o15] This function $g(x)$ can be written using the index notation as $f^{1/2}(x)$ . Similarly, $f^{1/3}(x)$ is the function defined such that $f^{1/3}(f^{1/3}(f^{1/3}(x))) = f(x)$, while $f^{2/3}(x)$ may be defined as equal to $f^{1/3}(f^{1/3}(x))$, and so forth, all based on the principle, mentioned earlier, that $f^{m} \circ f^{n} = f^{m + n}$. This idea can be generalized so that the iteration count $n$ becomes a **continuous parameter**, a sort of continuous "time" of a continuous [orbit](wikipedia://en/252250).[^o16][^o17]

In such cases, one refers to the system as a [flow](wikipedia://en/2619735) (cf. section on conjugacy below.)

If a function is bijective (and so possesses an inverse function), then negative iterates correspond to function inverses and their compositions. For example, $f^{-1}(x)$ is the normal inverse of $f$, while $f^{-2}(x)$ is the inverse composed with itself, i.e. $f^{-2}(x) = f^{-1}(f^{-1}(x))$. Fractional negative iterates are defined analogously to fractional positive ones; for example, $f^{-1/2}(x)$ is defined such that $f^{-1/2}(f^{-1/2}(x)) = f^{-1}(x)$, or, equivalently, such that $f^{-1/2}(f^{1/2}(x)) = f^{0}(x) = x$.

::: {#o19 .statement cue=named-heading name="Some formulas for fractional iteration"}

### Some formulas for fractional iteration {#Some_formulas_for_fractional_iteration}

One of several methods of finding a series formula for fractional iteration, making use of a fixed point, is as follows.[^o20]

1. First determine a fixed point for the function such that $f(a) = a$.
1. Define $f^{n}(a) = a$ for all *n* belonging to the reals. This, in some ways, is the most natural extra condition to place upon the fractional iterates.
1. Expand $f^{n}(x)$ around the fixed point *a* as a [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448), $$f^n(x) = f^n(a) + (x-a)\left.\frac{d}{ds}f^n(s)\right|_{s=a} + \frac{(x-a)^2}2\left.\frac{d^2}{ds^2}f^n(s)\right|_{s=a} +\cdots$$
1. Expand out $$f^n(x) = f^n(a) + (x-a) f'(a)f'(f(a))f'(f^2(a))\cdots f'(f^{n-1}(a)) + \cdots$$
1. Substitute in for $f^{k}(a) = a$, for any *k*, $$f^n(x) = a + (x-a) f'(a)^n + \frac{(x-a)^2}2(f''(a)f'(a)^{n-1})\left(1+f'(a)+\cdots+f'(a)^{n-1} \right)+\cdots$$
1. Make use of the [geometric progression](wikipedia://en/23474670) to simplify terms, $$f^n(x) = a + (x-a) f'(a)^n + \frac{(x-a)^2}2(f''(a)f'(a)^{n-1})\frac{f'(a)^n-1}{f'(a)-1}+\cdots$$ There is a special case when $f'(a) = 1$, $$f^n(x) = x + \frac{(x-a)^2}2(n f''(a))+ \frac{(x-a)^3}6\left(\frac{3}{2}n(n-1) f''(a)^2 + n f'''(a)\right)+\cdots$$

This can be carried on indefinitely, although inefficiently, as the latter terms become increasingly complicated. A more systematic procedure is outlined in the following section on **Conjugacy**.

#### Example 1 {#Example_1}

For example, setting $f(x) = Cx + D$ gives the fixed point $a = D/(1 - C)$, so the above formula terminates to just

$$f^n(x)=\frac{D}{1-C} + \left(x-\frac{D}{1-C}\right)C^n=C^nx+\frac{1-C^n}{1-C}D ~,$$

which is trivial to check.

#### Example 2 {#Example_2}

Find the value of $\sqrt{2}^{ \sqrt{2}^{\sqrt{2}^{\cdots}} }$ where this is done *n* times (and possibly the interpolated values when *n* is not an integer). We have $f(x) = \sqrt{2}^{x}$. A fixed point is $a = f(2) = 2$.

So set $x = 1$ and $f^{n} (1)$ expanded around the fixed point value of 2 is then an infinite series,

$$\sqrt{2}^{ \sqrt{2}^{\sqrt{2}^{\cdots}} } = f^n(1) = 2 - (\ln 2)^n + \frac{(\ln 2)^{n+1}((\ln 2)^n-1)}{4(\ln 2-1)} - \cdots$$

which, taking just the first three terms, is correct to the first decimal place when *n* is positive. Also see [Tetration](wikipedia://en/482379): $f^{n}(1) =^{n}\sqrt{2}$. Using the other fixed point $a = f(4) = 4$ causes the series to diverge.

For $n = -1$, the series computes the inverse function $2\tfrac{\ln x}{\ln 2}$.

#### Example 3 {#Example_3}

With the function $f(x) = x^{b}$, expand around the fixed point 1 to get the series

$$f^n(x) = 1 + b^n(x-1) + \frac{1}2b^{n}(b^n-1)(x-1)^2 + \frac{1}{3!}b^n (b^n-1)(b^n-2)(x-1)^3 + \cdots ~,$$

which is simply the Taylor series of *x*<sup>(*b*<sup>*n*</sup> )</sup> expanded around 1.

:::

## Conjugacy {#Conjugacy}

If $f$ and $g$ are two iterated functions, and there exists a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) $h$ such that $g = h^{-1} \circ f \circ h$, then $f$ and $g$ are said to be [topologically conjugate](wikipedia://en/5506149).

Clearly, topological conjugacy is preserved under iteration, as $g^{n} = h^{-1} \circ f^{n} \circ h$. Thus, if one can solve for one iterated function system, one also has solutions for all topologically conjugate systems. For example, the [tent map](wikipedia://en/2068153) is topologically conjugate to the [logistic map](wikipedia://en/18137). As a special case, taking $f(x) = x + 1$, one has the iteration of $g(x) = h^{-1}(h(x) + 1)$ as

$g^{n}(x) = h^{-1}(h(x) + n)$, for any function $h$.

Making the substitution $x = h^{-1}(y) = \phi(y)$ yields

$g(\phi(y)) = \phi(y+1)$, a form known as the [Abel equation](wikipedia://en/5834526).

Even in the absence of a strict homeomorphism, near a fixed point, here taken to be at $x$ = 0, $f$(0) = 0, one may often solve[^o25] [Schröder's equation](wikipedia://en/5834541) for a function Ψ, which makes $f(x)$ locally conjugate to a mere dilation, $g(x) = f'(0) x$, that is

$$f(x) = \Psi^{-1}(f'(0) \Psi(x)).$$

Thus, its iteration orbit, or flow, under suitable provisions (e.g., $f'(0) \ne 1$), amounts to the conjugate of the orbit of the monomial,

$$\Psi^{-1}(f'(0)^{n} \Psi(x)),$$

where $n$ in this expression serves as a plain exponent: *functional iteration has been reduced to multiplication!* Here, however, the exponent $n$ no longer needs be integer or positive, and is a continuous "time" of evolution for the full orbit:[^o26] the [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) of the Picard sequence (cf. [transformation semigroup](wikipedia://en/19313303)) has generalized to a full [continuous group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_group).[^o27]

Half-iterate (orange), i.e., the sine's [functional square root](wikipedia://en/7207519); the functional square root of that, the quarter-iterate (black) above it; and further fractional iterates up to the 1/64th. The functions below the (blue) sine are six integral iterates below it, starting with the second iterate (red) and ending with the 64th iterate. (From the general pedagogy web-site.[^o28] For the notation, see <http://www.physics.miami.edu/~curtright/TheRootsOfSin.pdf>.)

This method (perturbative determination of the principal [eigenfunction](wikipedia://en/228601) Ψ, cf. [Jabotinsky matrix](wikipedia://en/14320253)) is equivalent to the algorithm of the preceding section, albeit, in practice, more powerful and systematic.

## Markov chains {#Markov_chains}

If the function is linear and can be described by a [stochastic matrix](wikipedia://en/217313), that is, a matrix whose rows or columns sum to one, then the iterated system is known as a [Markov chain](wikipedia://en/60876).

## Examples {#Examples}

There are [many chaotic maps](wikipedia://en/2067823). Well-known iterated functions include the [Mandelbrot set](wikipedia://en/19562) and [iterated function systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iterated_function_systems).

[Ernst Schröder](wikipedia://en/448015),[^o32] in 1870, worked out special cases of the [logistic map](wikipedia://en/18137), such as the chaotic case $f(x) = 4x(1 - x)$, so that $\Psi(x) = \arcsin(\sqrt{x})^{2}$, hence $f^{n}(x) = \sin(2^{n} \arcsin(\sqrt{x}))^{2}$.

A nonchaotic case Schröder also illustrated with his method, $f(x) = 2x(1 - x)$, yielded $\Psi(x) = -\tfrac{1}{2} \ln(1 - 2x)$, and hence $f^{n}(x) = -\tfrac{1}{2}((1 - 2x)^{2^{n}} - 1)$.

If $f$ is the [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of a group element on a set, then the iterated function corresponds to a [free group](wikipedia://en/59735).

Most functions do not have explicit general [closed-form expressions](wikipedia://en/585143) for the *n*-th iterate. The table below lists some[^o32] that do. Note that all these expressions are valid even for non-integer and negative *n*, as well as non-negative integer *n*.

::: {#o33 .table cue=table}

| $f(x)$ | $f^n(x)$ |
|---|---|
| $x+b$ | $x+nb$ |
| $ax+b \ (a\ne 1)$ | $a^nx+\frac{a^n-1}{a-1}b$ |
| $ax^b \ (b\ne 1)$ | $a^{\frac{b^n-1}{b-1}}x^{b^n}$ |
| $ax^2 + bx + \frac{b^2 - 2b}{4a}$ (see note) | $\frac{2\alpha^{2^n} - b}{2a}$ where: $\alpha = \frac{2ax + b}{2}$ |
| $ax^2 + bx + \frac{b^2 - 2b - 8}{4a}$ (see note) | $\frac{2\alpha^{2^n} + 2\alpha^{-2^n} - b}{2a}$ where: $\alpha = \frac{2ax + b \pm \sqrt{(2ax + b)^2 - 16}}{4}$ |
| $\frac{ax + b}{cx + d}$ ([fractional linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_linear_transformation))[^o35] | $\frac{a}{c} + \frac{bc - ad}{c} \left [ \frac{(cx - a + \alpha)\alpha^{n - 1} - (cx - a + \beta)\beta^{n - 1}}{(cx - a + \alpha)\alpha^{n} - (cx - a + \beta)\beta^{n}} \right ]$ where: $\alpha = \frac{a + d + \sqrt{(a - d)^2 + 4bc}}{2}$ $\beta = \frac{a + d - \sqrt{(a - d)^2 + 4bc}}{2}$ |
| $g^{-1}\Big(h\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\Big)$ | $g^{-1}\Bigl(h^n\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\Bigr)$ |
| $g^{-1}\bigl(g(x)+b\bigr)$ (generic [Abel equation](wikipedia://en/5834526)) | $g^{-1}\bigl(g(x)+nb\bigr)$ |
| $\sqrt{x^2 + b}$ | $\sqrt{x^2 + bn}$ |
| $g^{-1}\Bigl(a\ g(x)+b\Bigr) \ (a\ne 1 \vee b=0)$ | $g^{-1}\Bigl(a^ng(x)+\frac{a^n-1}{a-1}b\Bigr)$ |
| $\sqrt{ax^2 + b}$ | $\sqrt{a^nx^2 + \frac{a^n - 1}{a - 1}b}$ |
| $T_m (x)=\cos (m \arccos x)$ ([Chebyshev polynomial](wikipedia://en/184539#Trigonometric_definition) for integer *m*) | $T_{mn}=\cos(m^n \arccos x)$ |

:::

Note: these two special cases of $ax^{2} + bx + c$ are the only cases that have a closed-form solution. Choosing *b* = 2 = –*a* and *b* = 4 = –*a*, respectively, further reduces them to the nonchaotic and chaotic logistic cases discussed prior to the table.

Some of these examples are related among themselves by simple conjugacies.

:::

## Means of study {#Means_of_study}

Iterated functions can be studied with the [Artin–Mazur zeta function](wikipedia://en/1342156) and with [transfer operators](wikipedia://en/1346096).

## In computer science {#In_computer_science}

In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), iterated functions occur as a special case of [recursive functions](wikipedia://en/4044867), which in turn anchor the study of such broad topics as [lambda calculus](wikipedia://en/18203), or narrower ones, such as the [denotational semantics](wikipedia://en/55275) of computer programs.

## Definitions in terms of iterated functions {#Definitions_in_terms_of_iterated_functions}

Two important [functionals](wikipedia://en/1374948) can be defined in terms of iterated functions. These are [summation](wikipedia://en/246160):

$$\left\{b+1,\sum_{i=a}^b g(i)\right\} \equiv \left( \{i,x\} \rightarrow \{ i+1 ,x+g(i) \}\right)^{b-a+1} \{a,0\}$$

and the equivalent product:

$$\left\{b+1,\prod_{i=a}^b g(i)\right\} \equiv \left( \{i,x\} \rightarrow \{ i+1 ,x g(i) \}\right)^{b-a+1} \{a,1\}$$

## Functional derivative {#Functional_derivative}

The [functional derivative](wikipedia://en/301504) of an iterated function is given by the recursive formula:

$$\frac{ \delta f^N(x)}{\delta f(y)} = f'( f^{N-1}(x) ) \frac{ \delta f^{N-1}(x)}{\delta f(y)} + \delta( f^{N-1}(x) - y )$$

## Lie's data transport equation {#Lie's_data_transport_equation}

Iterated functions crop up in the series expansion of combined functions, such as $g(f(x))$.

Given the [iteration velocity](wikipedia://en/33973912#Structure_of_univalent_semigroups), or [beta function (physics)](wikipedia://en/1037781),

$$v(x) = \left. \frac{\partial f^n(x)}{\partial n} \right|_{n=0}$$

for the $n$<sup>th</sup> iterate of the function $f$, we have[^o43]

$$g(f(x)) = \exp\left[ v(x) \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \right] g(x).$$

For example, for rigid advection, if $f(x) = x + t$, then $v(x) = t$. Consequently, $g(x + t) = \exp(t \partial/\partial x) g(x)$, action by a plain [shift operator](wikipedia://en/467384).

Conversely, one may specify $f(x)$ given an arbitrary $v(x)$, through the generic [Abel equation](wikipedia://en/5834526) discussed above,

$$f(x) = h^{-1}(h(x)+1) ,$$

where

$$h(x) = \int \frac{1}{v(x)} \, dx .$$

This is evident by noting that

$$f^n(x)=h^{-1}(h(x)+n)~.$$

For continuous iteration index $t$, then, now written as a subscript, this amounts to Lie's celebrated exponential realization of a continuous group,

$$e^{t~\frac{\partial ~~}{\partial h(x)}} g(x)= g(h^{-1}(h(x )+t))= g(f_t(x)).$$

The initial flow velocity $v$ suffices to determine the entire flow, given this exponential realization which automatically provides the general solution to the *translation functional equation*,[^o44]

$$f_t(f_\tau (x))=f_{t+\tau} (x) ~.$$
