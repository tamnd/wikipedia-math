# Complex conjugate of a vector space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complex conjugate of a vector space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **complex conjugate** of a [complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $V\,$ is a complex vector space $\overline V$ that has the same elements and additive group structure as $V,$ but whose [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373) involves [conjugation](wikipedia://en/173918) of the scalars. In other words, the scalar multiplication of $\overline V$ satisfies

$$\alpha\,*\, v = {\,\overline{\alpha} \cdot \,v\,}$$

where $*$ is the scalar multiplication of $\overline{V}$ and $\cdot$ is the scalar multiplication of $V.$ The letter $v$ stands for a vector in $V,$ $\alpha$ is a complex number, and $\overline{\alpha}$ denotes the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) of $\alpha.$[^o2]

:::

More concretely, the complex conjugate vector space is the same underlying real vector space (same set of points, same vector addition and real scalar multiplication) with the conjugate [linear complex structure](wikipedia://en/1549805) $J$ (different multiplication by $i$).

## Motivation {#Motivation}

If $V$ and $W$ are complex vector spaces, a function $f : V \to W$ is [antilinear](wikipedia://en/293432) if

$$f(v + w) = f(v) + f(w) \quad \text{ and } \quad f(\alpha v) = \overline{\alpha} \, f(v)$$

With the use of the conjugate vector space $\overline V$, an antilinear map $f : V \to W$ can be regarded as an ordinary [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) of type $\overline{V} \to W.$ The linearity is checked by noting:

$$f(\alpha * v) = f(\overline{\alpha} \cdot v) = \overline{\overline{\alpha}} \cdot f(v) = \alpha \cdot f(v)$$

Conversely, any linear map defined on $\overline{V}$ gives rise to an antilinear map on $V.$

This is the same underlying principle as in defining the [opposite ring](wikipedia://en/3129323) so that a right $R$-[module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_module) can be regarded as a left $R^{op}$-module, or that of an [opposite category](wikipedia://en/372399) so that a [contravariant functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contravariant_functor) $C \to D$ can be regarded as an ordinary functor of type $C^{op} \to D.$

## Complex conjugation functor {#Complex_conjugation_functor}

A linear map $f : V \to W\,$ gives rise to a corresponding linear map $\overline{f} : \overline{V} \to \overline{W}$ that has the same action as $f.$ Note that $\overline f$ preserves scalar multiplication because

$$\overline{f}(\alpha * v) = f(\overline{\alpha} \cdot v) = \overline{\alpha} \cdot f(v) = \alpha * \overline{f}(v)$$

Thus, complex conjugation $V \mapsto \overline{V}$ and $f \mapsto\overline f$ define a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from the [category](wikipedia://en/5869) of complex vector spaces to itself.

If $V$ and $W$ are finite-dimensional and the map $f$ is described by the complex [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $A$ with respect to the [bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_of_a_vector_space) $\mathcal{B}$ of $V$ and $\mathcal{C}$ of $W,$ then the map $\overline{f}$ is described by the complex conjugate of $A$ with respect to the bases $\overline{\mathcal{B}}$ of $\overline{V}$ and $\overline{\mathcal{C}}$ of $\overline{W}.$

## Structure of the conjugate {#Structure_of_the_conjugate}

The vector spaces $V$ and $\overline{V}$ have the same [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_of_a_vector_space) over the complex numbers and are therefore [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) as complex vector spaces. However, there is no [natural isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_isomorphism) from $V$ to $\overline{V}.$

The double conjugate $\overline{\overline{V}}$ is identical to $V.$

## Complex conjugate of a Hilbert space {#Complex_conjugate_of_a_Hilbert_space}

Given a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) $\mathcal{H}$ (either finite or infinite dimensional), its complex conjugate $\overline{\mathcal{H}}$ is the same vector space as its [continuous dual space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_dual_space) $\mathcal{H}^{\prime}.$ There is one-to-one antilinear correspondence between continuous linear functionals and vectors. In other words, any continuous [linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) on $\mathcal{H}$ is an inner multiplication to some fixed vector, and vice versa.

Thus, the complex conjugate to a vector $v,$ particularly in finite dimension case, may be denoted as $v^\dagger$ (v-dagger, a [row vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Row_vector) that is the [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) to a column vector $v$). In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), the conjugate to a *ket vector* $\,|\psi\rangle$ is denoted as $\langle\psi|\,$ – a *bra vector* (see [bra–ket notation](wikipedia://en/4542)).
