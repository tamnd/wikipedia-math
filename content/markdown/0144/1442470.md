# Transpose of a linear map

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Transpose of a linear map"}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) and [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), the **transpose** or **algebraic adjoint** of a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) between two vector spaces, defined over the same [field](wikipedia://en/10603), is an induced map between the [dual spaces](wikipedia://en/7988) of the two vector spaces. The transpose is often used to study the original linear map. This concept is generalised by [adjoint functors](wikipedia://en/53991).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $X^{\#}$ denote the [algebraic dual space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_dual_space) of a vector space $X$. Let $X$ and $Y$ be vector spaces over the same field $\mathcal{K}$. If $u : X \to Y$ is a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102), then its **algebraic adjoint**, or **dual**,[^n1] is the map ${}^{\#\!} u : Y^{\#} \to X^{\#}$ defined by $f \mapsto f \circ u$. The resulting functional ${}^{\#\!} u(f) := f \circ u$ is called the **[pullback](wikipedia://en/9413508)** of $f$ by $u$.

The [continuous dual space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_dual_space) of a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVS) $X$ is denoted by $X^{\prime}$. If $X$ and $Y$ are TVSs then a linear map $u : X \to Y$ is **weakly continuous** if and only if ${}^{\#\!} u\left(Y^{\prime}\right) \subseteq X^{\prime}$, in which case we let ${}^\text{t}\! u : Y^{\prime} \to X^{\prime}$ denote the restriction of ${}^{\#\!} u$ to $Y^{\prime}$. The map ${}^\text{t}\! u$ is called the **transpose**[^n2] or **algebraic adjoint** of $u$. The following identity characterizes the transpose of $u$:[^o6]

$$\left\langle {}^\text{t}\! u(f), x \right\rangle = \left\langle f, u(x) \right\rangle \quad \text{ for all } f \in Y ^{\prime} \text{ and } x \in X,$$

where $\left\langle \cdot, \cdot \right\rangle$ is the [natural pairing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_pairing) defined by $\left\langle z, h \right\rangle := z(h)$.

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The assignment $u \mapsto {}^\text{t}\! u$ produces an [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) linear map between the space of linear operators from $X$ to $Y$ and the space of linear operators from $Y^{\#}$ to $X^{\#}$. If $X = Y$ then the space of linear maps is an [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) under [composition of maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composition_of_maps), and the assignment is then an [antihomomorphism](wikipedia://en/706374) of algebras, meaning that ${}^\text{t}\! (u v) = {}^\text{t}\! v \ {}^\text{t}\! u$. In the language of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), taking the dual of vector spaces and the transpose of linear maps is therefore a [contravariant functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contravariant_functor) from the category of vector spaces over $\mathcal{K}$ to itself. One can identify ${}^\text{t}\!\! \left({}^\text{t}\! u\right)$ with $u$ using the natural injection into the double dual.

- If $u : X \to Y$ and $v : Y \to Z$ are linear maps then ${}^\text{t}\! (v \circ u) = {}^\text{t}\! u \circ {}^\text{t}\! v$[^o9]
- If $u : X \to Y$ is a ([surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective)) vector space isomorphism then so is the transpose ${}^\text{t}\! u : Y^{\prime} \to X^{\prime}$.
- If $X$ and $Y$ are [normed spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space) then

$$\|x\| = \sup_{\|x^{\prime}\| \leq 1} \left|x^{\prime}(x) \right| \quad \text{ for each } x \in X$$

and if the linear operator $u : X \to Y$ is bounded then the [operator norm](wikipedia://en/246722) of ${}^\text{t}\! u$ is equal to the norm of $u$; that is[^n3][^n4]

$$\|u\| = \left\|{}^\text{t}\! u\right\|,$$

and moreover,

$$\|u\| = \sup \left\{\left| y^{\prime}(u x) \right| : \|x\| \leq 1, \left\|y^\prime\right\| \leq 1 \text{ where } x \in X, y^{\prime} \in Y^{\prime} \right\}.$$

- A bounded linear operator between [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989) is [compact](wikipedia://en/577441) if and only if its adjoint is compact (*Schauder's theorem*).[^o12]

### Polars {#Polars}

Suppose now that $u : X \to Y$ is a weakly continuous linear operator between [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) $X$ and $Y$ with continuous dual spaces $X^{\prime}$ and $Y^{\prime}$, respectively. Let $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : X \times X^{\prime} \to \mathbb{C}$ denote the canonical [dual system](wikipedia://en/63735167), defined by $\left\langle x, x^{\prime} \right\rangle = x^{\prime} x$ where $x$ and $x^{\prime}$ are said to be [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_vectors) if $\left\langle x, x^{\prime} \right\rangle = x^{\prime} x = 0$. For any subsets $A \subseteq X$ and $S^{\prime} \subseteq X^{\prime}$, let

$$A^{\circ} = \left\{ x^{\prime} \in X^{\prime} : \sup_{a \in A} \left|x^{\prime}(a)\right| \leq 1 \right\} \qquad \text{ and } \qquad S^{\circ} = \left\{ x \in X : \sup_{s^{\prime} \in S^{\prime}} \left|s^{\prime}(x)\right| \leq 1 \right\}$$

denote the (absolute) [polar](wikipedia://en/1764022) of $A$ in $X^{\prime}$ (resp. of $S^{\prime}$ in $X$).

- If $A \subseteq X$ and $B \subseteq Y$ are convex, weakly closed sets containing the origin then ${}^\text{t}\! u\left(B^{\circ}\right) \subseteq A^{\circ}$ implies $u(A) \subseteq B$.[^n5]
- If $A \subseteq X$ and $B \subseteq Y$ then[^o9]

$$[u(A)]^{\circ} = \left({}^\text{t}\! u\right)^{-1}\left(A^{\circ}\right)$$

and

$$u(A) \subseteq B \quad \text{ implies } \quad {}^\text{t}\! u\left(B^{\circ}\right) \subseteq A^{\circ}.$$

- If $X$ and $Y$ are [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) then[^n3]

$$\operatorname{ker} {}^\text{t}\! u = \left(\operatorname{Im} u\right)^{\circ}.$$

### Annihilators {#Annihilators}

Suppose $X$ and $Y$ are [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) and $u : X \to Y$ is a weakly continuous linear operator (so $\left({}^\text{t}\! u\right)\left(Y^{\prime}\right) \subseteq X^{\prime}$). Given subsets $M \subseteq X$ and $N \subseteq X^{\prime}$, define their [annihilators](wikipedia://en/7988#Quotient_spaces_and_annihilators) (with respect to the canonical dual system) by[^n4]

$$\begin{alignedat}{4} M^{\bot} :&= \left\{ x^{\prime} \in X^{\prime} : \left\langle m, x^{\prime} \right\rangle = 0 \text{ for all } m \in M \right\} \\ &= \left\{ x^{\prime} \in X^{\prime} : x^{\prime}(M) = \{0\} \right\} \qquad \text{ where } x^{\prime}(M) := \left\{ x^{\prime}(m) : m \in M \right\} \end{alignedat}$$

and

$$\begin{alignedat}{4} {}^{\bot} N :&= \left\{ x \in X : \left\langle x, n^{\prime} \right\rangle = 0 \text{ for all } n^{\prime} \in N \right\} \\ &= \left\{ x \in X : N(x) = \{ 0 \} \right\} \qquad \text{ where } N(x) := \left\{ n^{\prime}(x) : n^{\prime} \in N \right\} \\ \end{alignedat}$$

- The [kernel](wikipedia://en/1072915) of ${}^\text{t}\! u$ is the subspace of $Y^{\prime}$ orthogonal to the image of $u$:[^n5]

$$\ker {}^\text{t}\! u = (\operatorname{Im} u)^{\bot}$$

- The linear map $u$ is [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) if and only if its image is a weakly dense subset of $Y$ (that is, the image of $u$ is dense in $Y$ when $Y$ is given the weak topology induced by $\operatorname{ker} {}^\text{t}\! u$).[^n5]
- The transpose ${}^\text{t}\! u : Y^{\prime} \to X^{\prime}$ is continuous when both $X^{\prime}$ and $Y^{\prime}$ are endowed with the [weak-\* topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak-*_topology) (resp. both endowed with the [strong dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_dual) topology, both endowed with the topology of uniform convergence on compact convex subsets, both endowed with the topology of [uniform convergence on compact subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_convergence_on_compact_subsets)).[^n6]
- ([Surjection of Fréchet spaces](wikipedia://en/63562105)): If $X$ and $Y$ are [Fréchet spaces](wikipedia://en/233801) then the continuous linear operator $u : X \to Y$ is [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) if and only if (1) the transpose ${}^\text{t}\! u : Y^{\prime} \to X^{\prime}$ is [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective), and (2) the image of the transpose of $u$ is a weakly closed (i.e. [weak-\*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak-*_topology) closed) subset of $X^{\prime}$.[^n7]

### Duals of quotient spaces {#Duals_of_quotient_spaces}

Let $M$ be a closed vector subspace of a Hausdorff locally convex space $X$ and denote the canonical quotient map by

$$\pi : X \to X / M \quad \text{ where } \quad \pi(x) := x + M.$$

Assume $X / M$ is endowed with the [quotient topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_topology) induced by the quotient map $\pi : X \to X / M$. Then the transpose of the quotient map is valued in $M^{\bot}$ and

$${}^\text{t}\! \pi : (X / M)^{\prime} \to M^{\bot} \subseteq X^{\prime}$$

is a TVS-isomorphism onto $M^{\bot}$. If $X$ is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) then ${}^\text{t}\! \pi : (X / M)^{\prime} \to M^{\bot}$ is also an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777).[^n4] Using this transpose, every continuous linear functional on the quotient space $X / M$ is canonically identified with a continuous linear functional in the annihilator $M^{\bot}$ of $M$.

### Duals of vector subspaces {#Duals_of_vector_subspaces}

Let $M$ be a closed vector subspace of a Hausdorff locally convex space $X$. If $m^{\prime} \in M^{\prime}$ and if $x^{\prime} \in X^{\prime}$ is a continuous linear extension of $m^{\prime}$ to $X$ then the assignment $m^{\prime} \mapsto x^{\prime} + M^{\bot}$ induces a vector space isomorphism

$$M^{\prime} \to X^{\prime} / \left(M^{\bot}\right),$$

which is an isometry if $X$ is a Banach space.[^n4]

Denote the [inclusion map](wikipedia://en/492516) by

$$\operatorname{In} : M \to X \quad \text{ where } \quad \operatorname{In}(m) := m \quad \text{ for all } m \in M.$$

The transpose of the inclusion map is

$${}^\text{t}\! \operatorname{In} : X^{\prime} \to M^{\prime}$$

whose kernel is the annihilator $M^{\bot} = \left\{ x^{\prime} \in X^{\prime} : \left\langle m, x^{\prime} \right\rangle = 0 \text{ for all } m \in M \right\}$ and which is surjective by the [Hahn–Banach theorem](wikipedia://en/13860). This map induces an isomorphism of vector spaces

$$X^{\prime} / \left(M^{\bot}\right) \to M^{\prime}.$$

:::

## Representation as a matrix {#Representation_as_a_matrix}

If the linear map $u$ is represented by the [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $A$ with respect to two bases of $X$ and $Y$, then ${}^\text{t}\! u$ is represented by the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) matrix $A^\text{T}$ with respect to the dual bases of $Y^{\prime}$ and $X^{\prime}$, hence the name. Alternatively, as $u$ is represented by $A$ acting to the right on column vectors, ${}^\text{t}\! u$ is represented by the same matrix acting to the left on row vectors. These points of view are related by the canonical inner product on $\mathbb{R}^n$, which identifies the space of column vectors with the dual space of row vectors.

## Relation to the Hermitian adjoint {#Relation_to_the_Hermitian_adjoint}

The identity that characterizes the transpose, that is, $\left[u^{*}(f), x\right] = [f, u(x)]$, is formally similar to the definition of the [Hermitian adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_of_an_operator), however, the transpose and the Hermitian adjoint are not the same map. The transpose is a map $Y^{\prime} \to X^{\prime}$ and is defined for linear maps between any vector spaces $X$ and $Y$, without requiring any additional structure. The Hermitian adjoint maps $Y \to X$ and is only defined for linear maps between Hilbert spaces, as it is defined in terms of the [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) on the Hilbert space. The Hermitian adjoint therefore requires more mathematical structure than the transpose.

However, the transpose is often used in contexts where the vector spaces are both equipped with a [nondegenerate bilinear form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondegenerate_form) such as the Euclidean [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) or another real [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product). In this case, the nondegenerate bilinear form is often [used](wikipedia://en/7988#Bilinear_products_and_dual_spaces) implicitly to map between the vector spaces and their duals, to express the transposed map as a map $Y \to X$. For a complex Hilbert space, the inner product is sesquilinear and not bilinear, and these conversions change the transpose into the adjoint map.

More precisely: if $X$ and $Y$ are Hilbert spaces and $u : X \to Y$ is a linear map then the transpose of $u$ and the Hermitian adjoint of $u$, which we will denote respectively by ${}^\text{t}\! u$ and $u^{*}$, are related. Denote by $I : X \to X^{*}$ and $J : Y \to Y^{*}$ the canonical antilinear isometries of the Hilbert spaces $X$ and $Y$ onto their duals. Then $u^{*}$ is the following composition of maps:[^n8]

$$Y \overset{J}{\longrightarrow} Y^* \overset{{}^{\text{t}\!}u}{\longrightarrow} X^* \overset{I^{-1}}{\longrightarrow} X$$

## Applications to functional analysis {#Applications_to_functional_analysis}

Suppose that $X$ and $Y$ are [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) and that $u : X \to Y$ is a linear map, then many of $u$'s properties are reflected in ${}^\text{t}\! u$.

- If $A \subseteq X$ and $B \subseteq Y$ are weakly closed, convex sets containing the origin, then ${}^\text{t}\! u(B^{\circ}) \subseteq A^{\circ}$ implies $u(A) \subseteq B$.[^o9]
- The null space of ${}^\text{t}\! u$ is the subspace of $Y^{\prime}$ orthogonal to the range $u(X)$ of $u$.[^o9]
- ${}^\text{t}\! u$ is injective if and only if the range $u(X)$ of $u$ is weakly closed.[^o9]
