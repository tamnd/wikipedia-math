# Cannon–Thurston map

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cannon–Thurston map"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Cannon–Thurston map** is any of a number of continuous group-equivariant maps between the boundaries of two [hyperbolic metric spaces](wikipedia://en/1285375) extending a discrete isometric actions of the group on those spaces.

:::

The notion originated from a seminal 1980s preprint of [James Cannon](wikipedia://en/19404886) and [William Thurston](wikipedia://en/39190) "Group-invariant Peano curves" (eventually published in 2007) about fibered hyperbolic 3-manifolds.[^o2]

Cannon–Thurston maps provide many natural geometric examples of [space-filling curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space-filling_curves).

## History {#History}

The Cannon–Thurston map first appeared in a mid-1980s preprint of [James W. Cannon](wikipedia://en/19404886) and [William Thurston](wikipedia://en/39190) called "Group-invariant Peano curves". The preprint remained unpublished until 2007,[^o2] but, in the meantime, had generated numerous follow-up works by other researchers.[^o4]

In their paper, Cannon and Thurston considered the following situation. Let *M* be a closed [hyperbolic 3-manifold](wikipedia://en/1237700) that fibers over the circle with fiber *S*. Then *S* itself is a closed hyperbolic surface, and its [universal cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_cover) $\tilde S$ can be identified with the [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane) $\mathbb H^2$. Similarly, the universal cover of *M* can be identified with the [hyperbolic 3-space](wikipedia://en/318742) $\mathbb H^3$. The inclusion $S\subseteq M$ lifts to a $\pi_1(S)$-invariant inclusion $\tilde S=\mathbb H^2\subseteq \mathbb H^3=\tilde M$. This inclusion is highly distorted because the action of $\pi_1(S)$ on $\mathbb H^3$ is not [geometrically finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometrically_finite_group).

Nevertheless, Cannon and Thurston proved that this distorted inclusion $\mathbb H^2\subseteq \mathbb H^3$ extends to a continuous $\pi_1(S)$-equivariant map

$$j:\mathbb S^1\to \mathbb S^2,$$

where $\mathbb S^1=\partial \mathbb H^2$ and $\mathbb S^2=\partial \mathbb H^3$. Moreover, in this case, the map *j* is [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective), so that it provides a continuous onto function from the circle onto the 2-sphere, that is, a [space-filling curve](wikipedia://en/352398).

Cannon and Thurston also explicitly described the map $j:\mathbb S^1\to \mathbb S^2$, via collapsing stable and unstable laminations of the monodromy [pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/2327355) of *S* for this fibration of *M*. In particular, this description implies that the map *j* is uniformly finite-to-one, with the pre-image of every point of $\mathbb S^2$ having cardinality at most 2*g*, where *g* is the genus of *S*.

After the paper of Cannon and Thurston generated a large amount of follow-up work, with other researchers analyzing the existence or non-existence of analogs of the map *j* in various other set-ups motivated by the Cannon–Thurston result.

## Cannon–Thurston maps and [Kleinian groups](wikipedia://en/1362652) {#Cannon–Thurston_maps_and_Kleinian_groups}

### Kleinian representations of surface groups {#Kleinian_representations_of_surface_groups}

The original example of Cannon and Thurston can be thought of in terms of [Kleinian](wikipedia://en/1362652) representations of the [surface group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_group) $H=\pi_1(S)$. As a subgroup of $G=\pi_1(M)$, the group *H* acts on $\mathbb H^3=\tilde M$ by isometries, and this action is [properly discontinuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Properly_discontinuous). Thus one gets a discrete representation $\rho:H\to \mathbb PSL(2,\mathbb C)=\operatorname{Isom}_+(\mathbb H^3)$.

The group $H=\pi_1(S)$ also acts by isometries, properly discontinuously and co-compactly, on the universal cover $\mathbb H^2=\tilde S$, with the [limit set](wikipedia://en/5492198) $\Lambda H\subseteq \partial H^2=\mathbb S^1$ being equal to $\mathbb S^1$. The Cannon–Thurston result can be interpreted as saying that these actions of *H* on $\mathbb H^2$ and $\mathbb H^3$ induce a continuous *H*-equivariant map $j:\mathbb S^1\to \mathbb S^2$.

One can ask, given a hyperbolic surface *S* and a discrete representation $\rho:\pi_1(S)\to \mathbb PSL(2,\mathbb C)$, if there exists an induced continuous map $j:\Lambda H\to \mathbb S^2$.

For Kleinian representations of surface groups, the most general result in this direction is due to [Mahan Mj](wikipedia://en/33274079) (2014).[^o7] Let *S* be a complete connected finite volume hyperbolic surface. Thus *S* is a surface without boundary, with a finite (possibly empty) set of cusps. Then one still has $\mathbb H^2=\tilde S$ and $\Lambda \pi_1(S)=\mathbb S^1$ (even if *S* has some cusps). In this setting Mj[^o7] proved the following theorem:

Let *S* be a complete connected finite volume hyperbolic surface and let $H=\pi_1(S)$. Let $\rho: H\to \mathbb PSL(2,\mathbb C)$ be a discrete [faithful representation](wikipedia://en/2617872) without accidental parabolics. Then $\rho$ induces a continuous *H*-equivariant map $j:\mathbb S^1\to \mathbb S^2$.

Here the "without accidental parabolics" assumption means that for $1\ne h\in H$, the element $\rho(h)$ is a parabolic isometry of $\mathbb H^3$ if and only if $h$ is a parabolic isometry of $\mathbb H^2$. One of important applications of this result is that in the above situation the limit set $\Lambda \rho(\pi_1(S))\subseteq \mathbb S^2$ is locally connected.

This result of Mj was preceded by numerous other results in the same direction, such as [Minsky](wikipedia://en/46905726) (1994),[^o8] Alperin, Dicks and Porti (1999),[^o9] [McMullen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtis_McMullen) (2001),[^o10] [Bowditch](wikipedia://en/13677348) (2007)[^o11] and (2013),[^o12] Miyachi (2002),[^o13] Souto (2006),[^o14] [Mj](wikipedia://en/33274079) (2009),[^o15] (2011),[^o16] and others. In particular, Bowditch's 2013 paper[^o12] introduced the notion of a "stack" of Gromov-hyperbolic metric spaces and developed an alternative framework to that of Mj for proving various results about Cannon–Thurston maps.

### General Kleinian groups {#General_Kleinian_groups}

In a 2017 paper,[^o18] Mj proved the existence of the Cannon–Thurston map in the following setting:

Let $\rho:G\to \mathbb PSL(2,\mathbb C)$ be a discrete faithful representation where *G* is a [word-hyperbolic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word-hyperbolic_group), and where $\rho(G)$ contains no parabolic isometries of $\mathbb H^3$. Then $\rho$ induces a continuous *G*-equivariant map $j:\partial G\to \mathbb S^2$, where $\partial G$ is the [Gromov boundary](wikipedia://en/41160090) of *G*, and where the image of *j* is the [limit set](wikipedia://en/5492198) of *G* in $\mathbb S^2$.

Here "induces" means that the map $J: G\cup \partial G\to \mathbb H^3 \cup \mathbb S^2$ is continuous, where $J|_{\partial G}=j$ and $J(g)=gx_0, g\in G$ (for some basepoint $x_0\in \mathbb H^3$). In the same paper Mj obtains a more general version of this result, allowing *G* to contain parabolics, under some extra technical assumptions on *G*. He also provided a description of the fibers of *j* in terms of [ending laminations](wikipedia://en/30997240) of $\mathbb H^3/G$.

## Cannon–Thurston maps and [word-hyperbolic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word-hyperbolic_group) {#Cannon–Thurston_maps_and_word-hyperbolic_groups}

### Existence and non-existence results {#Existence_and_non-existence_results}

Let *G* be a [word-hyperbolic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word-hyperbolic_group) and let *H* ≤ *G* be a subgroup such that *H* is also word-hyperbolic. If the inclusion *i*:*H* → *G* extends to a continuous map *∂i*: *∂H* → *∂G* between their hyperbolic boundaries, the map *∂i* is called a **Cannon–Thurston map**. Here "extends" means that the map between hyperbolic compactifications $\hat i: H\cup \partial H\to G\cup \partial G$, given by $\hat i|_H=i, \hat i|_{\partial H}=\partial i$, is continuous. In this setting, if the map *∂i* exists, it is unique and *H*-equivariant, and the image *∂i*(*∂H*) is equal to the [limit set](wikipedia://en/5492198) $\Lambda_{\partial G}(H)$.

If *H* ≤ *G* is [quasi-isometrically embedded](wikipedia://en/12747259#Definition) (i.e. quasiconvex) subgroup, then the Cannon–Thurston map *∂i*: *∂H* → *∂G* exists and is a [topological embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_embedding). However, it turns out that the Cannon–Thurston map exists in many other situations as well.

Mitra proved [^o21] that if *G* is word-hyperbolic and *H* ≤ *G* is a [normal](wikipedia://en/21918) word-hyperbolic subgroup, then the Cannon–Thurston map exists. (In this case if *H* and *Q* = *G*/*H* are infinite then *H* is not quasiconvex in *G*.) The original Cannon–Thurston theorem about fibered hyperbolic 3-manifolds is a special case of this result.

If *H* ≤ *G* are two word-hyperbolic groups and *H* is normal in *G* then, by a result of Mosher,[^o22] the quotient group *Q* = *G*/*H* is also word-hyperbolic. In this setting Mitra also described the fibers of the map *∂i*: *∂H* → *∂G* in terms of "algebraic ending laminations" on *H*, parameterized by the boundary points *z* ∈ *∂Q*.

In another paper,[^o23] Mitra considered the case where a word-hyperbolic group *G* splits as the [fundamental group of a graph of groups](wikipedia://en/12281773), where all vertex and edge groups are word-hyperbolic, and the edge-monomorphisms are [quasi-isometric embeddings](wikipedia://en/12747259#Definition). In this setting Mitra proved that for every vertex group $A_v$, for the inclusion map $i:A_v\to G$ the Cannon–Thurston map $\partial i:\partial A_v\to \partial G$ does exist.

By combining and iterating these constructions, Mitra produced[^o23] examples of hyperbolic subgroups of hyperbolic groups *H* ≤ *G* where the [subgroup distortion](wikipedia://en/71590696) of *H* in *G* is an arbitrarily high tower of exponentials, and the Cannon–Thurston map $\partial i:\partial H\to \partial G$ exists. Later Barker and Riley showed that one can arrange for *H* to have arbitrarily high [primitive recursive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_recursive) distortion in *G*.[^o24]

In a 2013 paper,[^o25] Baker and Riley constructed the first example of a word-hyperbolic group *G* and a word-hyperbolic (in fact [free](wikipedia://en/59735)) subgroup *H* ≤ *G* such that the Cannon–Thurston map $\partial i:\partial H\to \partial G$ does not exist. Later Matsuda and Oguni generalized the Baker–Riley approach and showed that every non-elementary word-hyperbolic group *H* can be embedded in some word-hyperbolic group *G* in such a way that the Cannon–Thurston map $\partial i:\partial H\to \partial G$ does not exist.[^o26]

### Multiplicity of the Cannon–Thurston map {#Multiplicity_of_the_Cannon–Thurston_map}

As noted above, if *H* is a quasi-isometrically embedded subgroup of a word-hyperbolic group *G*, then *H* is word-hyperbolic, and the Cannon–Thurston map $\partial i:\partial H\to\partial G$ exists and is injective. Moreover, it is known that the converse is also true: If *H* is a word-hyperbolic subgroup of a word-hyperbolic group *G* such that the Cannon–Thurston map $\partial i:\partial H\to\partial G$ exists and is injective, then *H* is uasi-isometrically embedded in *G*.[^o28]

It is known, for more general [convergence groups](wikipedia://en/52297304) reasons, that if *H* is a word-hyperbolic subgroup of a word-hyperbolic group *G* such that the Cannon–Thurston map $\partial i:\partial H\to\partial G$ exists then for every [conical limit point](wikipedia://en/52297304#Conical_limit_points) for *H* in $\partial G$ has exactly one pre-image under $\partial i$.[^o29] However, the converse fails: If $\partial i:\partial H\to\partial G$ exists and is non-injective, then there always exists a non-conical limit point of *H* in *∂G* with exactly one preimage under *∂i*.[^o28]

It the context of the original Cannon–Thurston paper, and for many generalizations for the Kleinin representations $\rho:\pi_1(S)\to \mathbb PSL(2,\mathbb C),$ the Cannon–Thurston map $j:\mathbb S^1\to \mathbb S^2$ is known to be uniformly finite-to-one.[^o18] That means that for every point $p\in \mathbb S^2$, the full pre-image $j^{-1}(p)$ is a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) with cardinality bounded by a constant depending only on *S*.[^o30]

In general, it is known, as a consequence of the JSJ-decomposition theory for [word-hyperbolic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word-hyperbolic_group), that if $1\to H\to G\to Q\to 1$ is a short [exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215) of three infinite torsion-free word-hyperbolic groups, then *H* is isomorphic to a free product of some closed [surface groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_group) and of a [free group](wikipedia://en/59735).

If $H=\pi_1(S)$ is the fundamental group of a closed hyperbolic surface *S*, such hyperbolic extensions of *H* are described by the theory of "convex cocompact" subgroups of the [mapping class group](wikipedia://en/31060586) Mod(*S*). Every subgroup Γ ≤ Mod(*S*) determines, via the Birman short exact sequence, an extension

$$1\to H\to E_\Gamma \to \Gamma\to 1$$

Moreover, the group $E_\Gamma$ is word-hyperbolic if and only if Γ ≤ Mod(*S*) is convex-cocompact. In this case, by Mitra's general result, the Cannon–Thurston map *∂i*:*∂H* → *∂E*<sub>Γ</sub> does exist. The fibers of the map *∂i* are described by a collection of ending laminations on *S* determined by Γ. This description implies that map *∂i* is uniformly finite-to-one.

If $\Gamma$ is a convex-cocompact purely atoroidal subgroup of [$\operatorname{Out}(F_n)$](wikipedia://en/12747184) (where $n\ge 3$) then for the corresponding extension $1\to F_n\to E_\Gamma\to \Gamma\to 1$ the group $E_\Gamma$ is word-hyperbolic. In this setting Dowdall, Kapovich and Taylor proved[^o31] that the Cannon–Thurston map $\partial i: \partial F_n\to\partial E_\Gamma$ is uniformly finite-to-one, with point preimages having cardinality $\le 2n$. This result was first proved by Kapovich and Lustig[^o32] under the extra assumption that $\Gamma$ is infinite cyclic, that is, that $\Gamma$ is generated by an atoroidal [fully irreducible](wikipedia://en/52244947) element of $\operatorname{Out}(F_n)$.

Ghosh proved that for an arbitrary atoroidal $\phi\in\operatorname{Out}(F_n)$ (without requiring $\Gamma=\langle \phi\rangle$ to be convex cocompact) the Cannon–Thurston map $\partial i: \partial F_n\to\partial E_\Gamma$ is uniformly finite-to-one, with a bound on the cardinality of point preimages depending only on *n*.[^o33] (However, Ghosh's result does not provide an explicit bound in terms of *n*, and it is still unknown if the 2*n* bound always holds in this case.)

It remains unknown, whenever *H* is a word-hyperbolic subgroup of a word-hyperbolic group *G* such that the Cannon–Thurston map $\partial i: \partial H\to\partial G$ exists, if the map $\partial i$ is finite-to-one. However, it is known that in this setting for every $p\in \Lambda_{\partial G} H$ such that *p* is a [conical limit point](wikipedia://en/52297304), the set $(\partial i)^{-1}(p)$ has cardinality 1.

## Generalizations, applications and related results {#Generalizations,_applications_and_related_results}

- As an application of the result about the existence of Cannon–Thurston maps for Kleinian surface group representations, Mj proved[^o7] that if $\Gamma\le \mathbb PSL(2,\mathbb C)$ is a finitely generated Kleinian group such that the limit set $\Lambda\subseteq \partial \mathbb H^3$ is connected, then $\Lambda$ is locally connected.
- Leininger, Mj and Schleimer,[^o35] given a closed hyperbolic surface *S*, constructed a 'universal' Cannon–Thurston map from a subset of $\partial \pi_1(S)=\mathbb S^1$ to the boundary $\partial \mathcal C(S,z)$ of the [curve complex](wikipedia://en/51024643) of *S* with one puncture, such that this map, in a precise sense, encodes all the Cannon–Thurston maps corresponding to arbitrary ending laminations on *S*. As an application, they prove that $\partial \mathcal C(S,z)$ is [path-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path-connected) and locally path-connected.
- Leininger, Long and Reid[^o36] used Cannon–Thurston maps to show that any finitely generated torsion-free nonfree Kleinian group with limit set equal to $\mathbb S^2$, which is not a lattice and contains no parabolic elements, has discrete commensurator in $\mathbb PSL(2,\mathbb C)$.
- Jeon and Ohshika[^o37] used Cannon–Thurston maps to establish measurable rigidity for Kleinian groups.
- Inclusions of [relatively hyperbolic groups](wikipedia://en/21993809) as subgroups of other relatively hyperbolic groups in many instances also induce equivariant continuous maps between their Bowditch boundaries; such maps are also referred to as Cannon–Thurston maps.[^o7][^o38][^o39][^o26]
- More generally, if *G* is a group acting as a discrete [convergence group](wikipedia://en/52297304) on two metrizable compacta *M* and *Z*, a continuous *G*-equivariant map *M* → *Z* (if such a map exists) is also referred to as a Cannon–Thurston map. Of particular interest in this setting is the case where *G* is word-hyperbolic and *M* = *∂G* is the hyperbolic boundary of *G*, or where *G* is relatively hyperbolic and *M* = *∂G* is the Bowditch boundary of *G*.[^o28]
- Mj and Pal[^o38] obtained a generalization of Mitra's earlier result for graphs of groups to the [relatively hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/21993809) context.
- Pal [^o39] obtained a generalization of Mitra's earlier result, about the existence of the Cannon–Thurston map for short exact sequences of word-hyperbolic groups, to relatively hyperbolic contex.
- Mj and Rafi [^o40] used the Cannon–Thurston map to study which subgroups are quasiconvex in extensions of free groups and surface groups by convex cocompact subgroups of $\operatorname{Out}t(F_n)$ and of mapping class groups.
