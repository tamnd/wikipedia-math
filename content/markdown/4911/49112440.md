# Orientation sheaf

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Orientation sheaf"}

In the mathematical field of [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), the **orientation sheaf** on a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) *X* of dimension *n* is a [locally constant sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_constant_sheaf) *o*<sub>*X*</sub> on *X* such that the stalk of *o*<sub>*X*</sub> at a point *x* is the [local homology group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_homology_group)

:::

$$o_{X, x} = \operatorname{H}_n(X, X - \{x\})$$

(in the integer coefficients or some other coefficients).

Let $\Omega^k_M$ be the sheaf of [differential *k*-forms](wikipedia://en/221519) on a manifold *M*. If *n* is the dimension of *M*, then the sheaf

$$\mathcal{V}_M = \Omega^n_M \otimes \mathcal{o}_M$$

is called the sheaf of (smooth) densities on *M*. The point of this is that, while one can [integrate a differential form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integration_of_a_differential_form) only if the manifold is [oriented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented_manifold), one can always integrate a density, regardless of orientation or orientability; there is the integration map:

$$\textstyle \int_M: \Gamma_c(M, \mathcal{V}_M) \to \mathbb{R}.$$

If *M* is oriented; i.e., the orientation sheaf of the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) of *M* is literally trivial, then the above reduces to the usual integration of a differential form.

## See also {#See_also}

- There is also a definition in terms of dualizing complex in [Verdier duality](wikipedia://en/7190735); in particular, one can define a relative orientation sheaf using a relative dualizing complex.
