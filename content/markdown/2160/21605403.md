# William Neile

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="William Neile"}

**William Neile** (7 December 1637 – 24 August 1670) was an [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and founder member of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society). His major mathematical work, the [rectification](wikipedia://en/1898401) of the [semicubical parabola](wikipedia://en/2669305), was carried out when he was aged nineteen, and was published by [John Wallis](wikipedia://en/239290). By carrying out the determination of [arc lengths](wikipedia://en/1898401) on a curve given algebraically, in other words by extending to [algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/253260) generally with [Cartesian geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_geometry) a basic concept from [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), it represented a major advance in what would become [infinitesimal calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal_calculus). His name also appears as Neil.

:::

## Life {#Life}

Neile was born at [Bishopsthorpe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishopsthorpe), the eldest son of [Sir Paul Neile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Neile) MP for Ripon and Newark. His grandfather was [Richard Neile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Neile), the [Archbishop of York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_York).[^o3] He entered [Wadham College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadham_College,_Oxford) as a [gentleman-commoner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentleman-commoner) in 1652, matriculating in 1655. He was taught by [John Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wilkins) and [Seth Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seth_Ward_%28bishop%29).

In 1657, he became a student at the [Middle Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Temple). In the same year he gave his exact rectification of the semicubical parabola and communicated his discovery to [William Brouncker](wikipedia://en/1426858), [Christopher Wren](wikipedia://en/52946) and others connected with [Gresham College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gresham_College). His demonstration was published by Wallis in *De Cycloide* (1659). The general formula for rectification by [definite integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_integral) was in effect discovered by [Hendrik van Heuraet](wikipedia://en/6022646) in 1659. In 1673 Wallis asserted that [Christiaan Huyghens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christiaan_Huyghens), who was advancing his own claim to have influenced Heuraet, was also slighting the priority of Neile.[^o3][^o4]

Neile was elected a fellow of the Royal Society on 7 January 1663 and a member of the council on 11 April 1666. He entered the debate on the theory of [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_%28physics%29), as a critic of the [empiricist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricist) stance of other members. His own theory of motion was held up from publication by unfavourable peer review by Wallis, in 1667; a revision was communicated to the society on 29 April 1669. Neile objected to Wren's 1668 work on [collision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collision) as lacking discussion of causality: he asked for discussion of the nature of [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431). His own work was much influenced by ideas drawn from the *[De Corpore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Corpore)* of [Thomas Hobbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes).[^o3][^o5][^o6]

He made astronomical observations with instruments erected on the roof of his father's residence, the “Hill House” (later called Waltham Place) at [White Waltham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Waltham) in [Berkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkshire), where he died at the age of 32. A white marble monument in the parish church of White Waltham commemorates him and an inscribed slab in the floor marks his burial-place. He belonged to the [privy council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privy_Council_of_the_United_Kingdom) of [King Charles II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_II_of_England).[^o3]
