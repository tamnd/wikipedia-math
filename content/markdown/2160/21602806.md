# Sample maximum and minimum

*Image: Michelsonmorley-boxplot.svg.* [Box plots](wikipedia://en/160960) of the [Michelson–Morley experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelson–Morley_experiment), showing sample maxima and minima

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sample maximum and minimum"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **sample maximum** and **sample minimum,** also called the **largest observation** and **smallest observation,** are the values of the greatest and least elements of a [sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_%28statistics%29).[^o2] They are basic [summary statistics](wikipedia://en/27587), used in [descriptive statistics](wikipedia://en/8187) such as the [five-number summary](wikipedia://en/160963) and [Bowley's seven-figure summary](wikipedia://en/18083379#Bowley’s_seven-figure_summary) and the associated [box plot](wikipedia://en/160960).

:::

The minimum and the maximum value are the first and last [order statistics](wikipedia://en/160986) (often denoted *X*<sub>(1)</sub> and *X*<sub>(*n*)</sub> respectively, for a sample size of *n*).

If the sample has [outliers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outliers), they necessarily include the sample maximum or sample minimum, or both, depending on whether they are extremely high or low. However, the sample maximum and minimum need not be outliers, if they are not unusually far from other observations.

## Robustness {#Robustness}

The sample maximum and minimum are the *least* [robust statistics](wikipedia://en/2885691): they are maximally sensitive to outliers.

This can either be an advantage or a drawback: if extreme values are real (not measurement errors), and of real consequence, as in applications of [extreme value theory](wikipedia://en/50732) such as building dikes or financial loss, then outliers (as reflected in sample extrema) are important. On the other hand, if outliers have little or no impact on actual outcomes, then using non-robust statistics such as the sample extrema simply clouds the statistics, and robust alternatives should be used, such as other [quantiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantiles): the 10th and 90th [percentiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percentiles) (first and last [decile](wikipedia://en/1577298)) are more robust alternatives.

## Derived statistics {#Derived_statistics}

In addition to being a component of every statistic that uses all elements of the sample, the sample extrema are important parts of the [range](wikipedia://en/27588), a measure of dispersion, and [mid-range](wikipedia://en/2814021), a measure of location. They also realize the [maximum absolute deviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_absolute_deviation): one of them is the *furthest* point from any given point, particularly a measure of center such as the median or mean.

::: {#o6 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

### Smooth maximum {#Smooth_maximum}

For a sample set, the maximum function is non-smooth and thus non-differentiable. For optimization problems that occur in statistics it often needs to be approximated by a smooth function that is close to the maximum of the set.

A [smooth maximum](wikipedia://en/45456706), for example,

*g*(*x*<sub>1</sub>, *x*<sub>2</sub>, …, *x*<sub>*n*</sub>) = log( exp(*x*<sub>1</sub>) + exp(*x*<sub>2</sub>) + … + exp(*x*<sub>*n*</sub>) )

is a good approximation of the sample maximum.

### Summary statistics {#Summary_statistics}

The sample maximum and minimum are basic [summary statistics](wikipedia://en/27587), showing the most extreme observations, and are used in the [five-number summary](wikipedia://en/160963) and a version of the [seven-number summary](wikipedia://en/18083379) and the associated [box plot](wikipedia://en/160960).

### Prediction interval {#Prediction_interval}

The sample maximum and minimum provide a non-parametric [prediction interval](wikipedia://en/536062): in a sample from a population, or more generally an [exchangeable sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchangeable_sequence) of random variables, each observation is equally likely to be the maximum or minimum.

Thus if one has a sample $\{X_1,\dots,X_n\},$ and one picks another observation $X_{n+1},$ then this has $1/(n+1)$ probability of being the largest value seen so far, $1/(n+1)$ probability of being the smallest value seen so far, and thus the other $(n-1)/(n+1)$ of the time, $X_{n+1}$ falls between the sample maximum and sample minimum of $\{X_1,\dots,X_n\}.$ Thus, denoting the sample maximum and minimum by *M* and *m,* this yields an $(n-1)/(n+1)$ prediction interval of \[*m*,*M*\].

For example, if *n* = 19, then \[*m*,*M*\] gives an 18/20 = 90% prediction interval – 90% of the time, the 20th observation falls between the smallest and largest observation seen heretofore. Likewise, *n* = 39 gives a 95% prediction interval, and *n* = 199 gives a 99% prediction interval.

### Estimation {#Estimation}

Due to their sensitivity to outliers, the sample extrema cannot reliably be used as [estimators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estimators) unless data is clean – robust alternatives include the first and last [deciles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deciles).

However, with clean data or in theoretical settings, they can sometimes prove very good estimators, particularly for [platykurtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platykurtic) distributions, where for small data sets the [mid-range](wikipedia://en/2814021) is the most [efficient](wikipedia://en/27250866) estimator.

They are inefficient estimators of location for mesokurtic distributions, such as the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462), and leptokurtic distributions, however.

#### Uniform distribution {#Uniform_distribution}

For sampling without replacement from a [uniform distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28discrete%29) with one or two unknown endpoints (so $1,2,\dots,N$ with *N* unknown, or $M,M+1,\dots,N$ with both *M* and *N* unknown), the sample maximum, or respectively the sample maximum and sample minimum, are [sufficient](wikipedia://en/140841) and [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_statistic) statistics for the unknown endpoints; thus an unbiased estimator derived from these will be [UMVU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UMVU) estimator.

If only the top endpoint is unknown, the sample maximum is a biased estimator for the population maximum, but the unbiased estimator $\frac{k+1}{k}m - 1$ (where *m* is the sample maximum and *k* is the sample size) is the UMVU estimator; see [German tank problem](wikipedia://en/16547646) for details.

If both endpoints are unknown, then the sample range is a biased estimator for the population range, but correcting as for maximum above yields the UMVU estimator.

If both endpoints are unknown, then the [mid-range](wikipedia://en/2814021) is an unbiased (and hence UMVU) estimator of the midpoint of the interval (here equivalently the population median, average, or mid-range).

The reason the sample extrema are sufficient statistics is that the conditional distribution of the non-extreme samples is just the distribution for the uniform interval between the sample maximum and minimum – once the endpoints are fixed, the values of the interior points add no additional information.

### Normality testing {#Normality_testing}

*Image: Standard deviation diagram.svg.* Sample extrema can be used for [normality testing](wikipedia://en/8617378), as events beyond the 3σ range are very rare.

The sample extrema can be used for a simple [normality test](wikipedia://en/8617378), specifically of kurtosis: one computes the [t-statistic](wikipedia://en/22474343) of the sample maximum and minimum (subtracts [sample mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_mean) and divides by the [sample standard deviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_standard_deviation)), and if they are unusually large for the sample size (as per the [three sigma rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_sigma_rule) and table therein, or more precisely a [Student's t-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375)), then the kurtosis of the sample distribution deviates significantly from that of the normal distribution.

For instance, a daily process should expect a 3σ event once per year (of calendar days; once every year and a half of business days), while a 4σ event happens on average every 40 years of calendar days, 60 years of business days (once in a lifetime), 5σ events happen every 5,000 years (once in recorded history), and 6σ events happen every 1.5 million years (essentially never). Thus if the sample extrema are 6 sigmas from the mean, one has a significant failure of normality.

Further, this test is very easy to communicate without involved statistics.

These tests of normality can be applied if one faces [kurtosis risk](wikipedia://en/7069430), for instance.

### Extreme value theory {#Extreme_value_theory}

*Image: 1755 Lisbon earthquake.jpg.* Events can be beyond the extreme of any previously observed, as in the [1755 Lisbon earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1755_Lisbon_earthquake).

Sample extrema play two main roles in [extreme value theory](wikipedia://en/50732):

- first, they give a lower bound on extreme events – events can be at least this extreme, and for this size sample;
- second, they can sometimes be used in estimators of probability of more extreme events.

However, caution must be used in using sample extrema as guidelines: in [heavy-tailed distributions](wikipedia://en/8092200) or for [non-stationary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-stationary) processes, extreme events can be significantly more extreme than any previously observed event. This is elaborated in [black swan theory](wikipedia://en/7361848).
