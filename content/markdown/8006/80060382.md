# Michael D. Atkinson

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Michael D. Atkinson"}

**Michael D. Atkinson** is a mathematician and computer scientist known for his work in the theory of [permutation patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutation_patterns) and for contributions to algorithm design, data structures, and algebra. He is an emeritus professor at the [University of Otago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Otago).

:::

## Education and career {#Education_and_career}

Atkinson earned his B.A. (1967) and D.Phil. (1970) in mathematics from the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford), where he was a member of [The Queen's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Queen's_College,_Oxford) and a student of [Peter M. Neumann](wikipedia://en/7151472). His doctoral work focused on varieties of groups, within the area of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890).

He taught at [University College, Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_University) from 1970 to 1982, then joined the [Carleton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carleton_University) School of Computer Science in Canada, where he became a full professor in 1983. In 1992, Atkinson moved to the [University of St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews) as Professor of Algorithms and head of the School of Mathematical and Computational Sciences (1994–1997). He joined the University of Otago in 2000 and retired in 2012.[^o5]

## Research {#Research}

Atkinson's early research spanned algebra, permutation groups, bilinear complexity, and algorithmic linear algebra. His 1975 paper on block-finding algorithms for permutation groups[^o7] gave the first polynomial-time algorithm for the problem.[^o8] He later made contributions to data structures and computational geometry, notably on [min-max heaps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Min-max_heap),[^o9] geometric congruence testing,[^o10] the cyclic [Towers of Hanoi](wikipedia://en/56990),[^o11] and frequency assignment problems in communication networks.[^o12]

In the late 1990s, Atkinson shifted his research to permutation patterns.[^o13] His 1999 paper *Restricted permutations*[^o14] has been described as "foundational" in the field.[^o15] In 2003, he co-founded the [Permutation Patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutation_Patterns_%28conference%29) conference with [Michael H. Albert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_H._Albert), which has played a central role in the development of the field.[^o16] Their 2005 joint paper *Simple permutations and pattern restricted permutations*[^o17] introduced structural decomposition techniques, now known as the substitution decomposition. This work, described as "formative",[^o18] refined the analysis begun in his earlier work with Tim Stitt on the wreath product.[^o19] Together with Albert and [Martin Klazar](wikipedia://en/80246874), Atkinson also enumerated the simple permutations that arise in this decomposition.[^o20] In later work, he and co-authors introduced the notion of geometric grid classes,[^o21] another tool in the study of the structure of permutation classes.
