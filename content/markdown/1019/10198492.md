# Nilpotent orbit

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nilpotent orbit"}

In mathematics, **nilpotent orbits** are generalizations of [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) that play an important role in [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200) of real and complex [semisimple Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_Lie_group) and [semisimple Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/1337587).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

An element *X* of a [semisimple Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/1337587) *g* is called **nilpotent** if its [adjoint endomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_endomorphism)

*ad X*: *g* → *g*, *ad X*(*Y*) = \[*X*,*Y*\]

is nilpotent, that is, (*ad X*)<sup>*n*</sup> = 0 for large enough *n*. Equivalently, *X* is nilpotent if its [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) *p*<sub>*ad X*</sub>(*t*) is equal to *t*<sup>dim *g*</sup>.

A semisimple [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) or [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924) *G* acts on its Lie algebra via the [adjoint representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_endomorphism), and the property of being nilpotent is invariant under this action. A **nilpotent orbit** is an orbit of the adjoint action such that any (equivalently, all) of its elements is (are) nilpotent.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Nilpotent $n\times n$ matrices with complex entries form the main motivating case for the general theory, corresponding to the complex [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564). From the [Jordan normal form](wikipedia://en/312877) of matrices we know that each nilpotent matrix is conjugate to a unique matrix with Jordan blocks of sizes $\lambda_1\geq \lambda_2\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_r,$ where $\lambda$ is a [partition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_%28number_theory%29) of *n*. Thus in the case *n*=2 there are two nilpotent orbits, the *zero orbit* consisting of the [zero matrix](wikipedia://en/1168888) and corresponding to the partition (*1*,*1*) and the *principal orbit* consisting of all non-zero matrices *A* with zero trace and determinant,

$A=\begin{bmatrix}x & y\\ z & -x \end{bmatrix}, \quad (x,y,z)\ne (0,0,0)\quad{\;}$ with $x^2+yz=0,$

corresponding to the partition (*2*). Geometrically, this orbit is a two-dimensional complex quadratic [cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_%28geometry%29) in four-dimensional vector space of $2\times 2$ matrices minus its apex.

The complex [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) is a subgroup of the general linear group with the same nilpotent orbits. However, if we replace the *complex* special linear group with the *real* special linear group, new nilpotent orbits may arise. In particular, for *n*=2 there are now 3 nilpotent orbits: the zero orbit and two real half-cones (without the apex), corresponding to positive and negative values of $y-z$ in the parametrization above.

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- Nilpotent orbits can be characterized as those orbits of the adjoint action whose [Zariski closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski_closure) contains 0.
- Nilpotent orbits are finite in number.
- The Zariski closure of a nilpotent orbit is a union of nilpotent orbits.
- **Jacobson–Morozov theorem**: over a field of [characteristic zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_zero), any nilpotent element *e* can be included into an [*sl*<sub>2</sub>-triple](wikipedia://en/14994282) {*e*,*h*,*f*} and all such triples are conjugate by *Z*<sub>*G*</sub>(*e*), the [centralizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centralizer) of *e* in *G*. Together with the representation theory of *sl*<sub>2</sub>, this allows one to label nilpotent orbits by finite combinatorial data, giving rise to the *Dynkin–Kostant classification* of nilpotent orbits.

:::

## Poset structure {#Poset_structure}

Nilpotent orbits form a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572): given two nilpotent orbits, *O*<sub>1</sub> is less than or equal to *O*<sub>2</sub> if *O*<sub>1</sub> is contained in the Zariski closure of *O*<sub>2</sub>. This poset has a unique minimal element, zero orbit, and unique maximal element, the *regular nilpotent orbit*, but in general, it is not a [graded poset](wikipedia://en/2514970). If the ground field is [algebraically closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_closed) then the zero orbit is [covered](wikipedia://en/14835049) by a unique orbit, called the *minimal orbit*, and the regular orbit covers a unique orbit, called the *subregular orbit*.

In the case of the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) *SL*<sub>*n*</sub>, the nilpotent orbits are parametrized by the [partitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_%28number_theory%29) of *n*. By a theorem of [Gerstenhaber](wikipedia://en/30237327), the ordering of the orbits corresponds to the [dominance order](wikipedia://en/14834796) on the partitions of *n*. Moreover, if *G* is an [isometry group of a bilinear form](wikipedia://en/10172878), i.e. an orthogonal or symplectic subgroup of *SL*<sub>*n*</sub>, then its nilpotent orbits are parametrized by partitions of *n* satisfying a certain parity condition and the corresponding poset structure is induced by the dominance order on all partitions (this is a nontrivial theorem, due to Gerstenhaber and Hesselink).
