# Characteristic equation (calculus)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Characteristic equation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **characteristic equation** (or **auxiliary equation**[^o5]) is an [algebraic equation](wikipedia://en/404001) of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) $n$ upon which depends the solution of a given $n$th-[order](wikipedia://en/1424309#Equation_order) [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309)[^o2] or [difference equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_difference_equation).[^o3][^o4] The characteristic equation can only be formed when the differential equation is [linear](wikipedia://en/379868) and [homogeneous](wikipedia://en/6954092#Homogeneous_linear_differential_equations), and has constant [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203).[^o5] Such a differential equation, with $y$ as the [dependent variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependent_variable), superscript $(n)$ denoting *n*<sup>th</sup>-[derivative](wikipedia://en/7921), and $a_{n}, a_{n - 1}, ..., a_{1}, a_{0}$ as constants,

:::

$$a_{n}y^{(n)} + a_{n-1}y^{(n-1)} + \cdots + a_{1}y' + a_{0}y = 0,$$

will have a characteristic equation of the form

$$a_{n}r^{n} + a_{n-1}r^{n-1} + \cdots + a_{1}r + a_{0} = 0$$

whose solutions $r_{1}, r_{2}, ..., r_{n}$ are the roots from which the [general solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_solution) can be formed.[^o5][^o6][^o7] Analogously, a linear difference equation of the form

$$y_{t+n} = b_1y_{t+n-1} + \cdots + b_ny_{t}$$

has characteristic equation

$$r^n - b_1r^{n-1} - \cdots - b_n =0,$$

discussed in more detail at [Linear recurrence with constant coefficients](wikipedia://en/50700194).

The characteristic roots ([roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) of the characteristic equation) also provide qualitative information about the behavior of the variable whose evolution is described by the dynamic equation. For a differential equation parameterized on time, the variable's evolution is [stable](wikipedia://en/363360) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the [real part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part) of each root is negative. For difference equations, there is stability if and only if the [modulus](wikipedia://en/5826#Polar_complex_plane) of each root is less than 1. For both types of equation, persistent fluctuations occur if there is at least one pair of [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) roots.

The method of [integrating](wikipedia://en/15532) linear [ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/32742753) with constant coefficients was discovered by [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902), who found that the solutions depended on an algebraic 'characteristic' equation.[^o2] The qualities of the Euler's characteristic equation were later considered in greater detail by French mathematicians [Augustin-Louis Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842) and [Gaspard Monge](wikipedia://en/77840).[^o2][^o7]

## Derivation {#Derivation}

Starting with a linear homogeneous differential equation with constant coefficients $a_{n}, a_{n - 1}, ..., a_{1}, a_{0}$,

$$a_n y^{(n)} + a_{n-1}y^{(n-1)} + \cdots + a_1 y^\prime + a_0 y = 0,$$

it can be seen that if $y(x) = e^{rx}$, each term would be a constant multiple of $e^{rx}$. This results from the fact that the derivative of the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) $e^{rx}$ is a multiple of itself. Therefore, $y' = re^{rx}$, $y'' = r^{2}e^{rx}$, and $y^{(n)} = r^{n}e^{rx}$ are all multiples. This suggests that certain values of $r$ will allow multiples of $e^{rx}$ to sum to zero, thus solving the homogeneous differential equation.[^o6] In order to solve for $r$, one can substitute $y = e^{rx}$ and its derivatives into the differential equation to get

$$a_n r^n e^{rx} + a_{n-1}r^{n-1}e^{rx} + \cdots + a_1 re^{rx} + a_0 e^{rx} = 0$$

Since $e^{rx}$ can never equal zero, it can be divided out, giving the characteristic equation

$$a_n r^n + a_{n-1}r^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1 r + a_0 = 0.$$

By solving for the roots, $r$, in this characteristic equation, one can find the general solution to the differential equation.[^o5][^o7] For example, if $r$ has roots equal to 3, 11, and 40, then the general solution will be $y(x) = c_1 e^{3 x} + c_2 e^{11 x} + c_3 e^{40 x}$, where $c_1$, $c_2$, and $c_3$ are [arbitrary constants](wikipedia://en/147939) which need to be determined by the boundary and/or initial conditions.

## Formation of the general solution {#Formation_of_the_general_solution}

Solving the characteristic equation for its roots, $r_{1}, ..., r_{n}$, allows one to find the general solution of the differential equation. The roots may be [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex](wikipedia://en/5826), as well as distinct or repeated. If a characteristic equation has parts with distinct real roots, $h$ repeated roots, or $k$ complex roots corresponding to general solutions of $y_{D}(x)$, $y_{R_{1}}(x), ..., y_{R_{h}}(x)$, and $y_{C_{1}}(x), ..., y_{C_{k}}(x)$, respectively, then the general solution to the differential equation is

$$y(x) = y_\mathrm{D}(x) + y_{\mathrm{R}_1}(x) + \cdots + y_{\mathrm{R}_h}(x) + y_{\mathrm{C}_1}(x) + \cdots + y_{\mathrm{C}_k}(x)$$

::: {#o11 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

The linear homogeneous differential equation with constant coefficients

$$y^{(5)} + y^{(4)} - 4y^{(3)} - 16y'' -20y' - 12y = 0$$

has the characteristic equation

$$r^5 + r^4 - 4r^3 - 16r^2 -20r - 12 = 0$$

By [factoring](wikipedia://en/82341) the characteristic equation into

$$(r - 3)(r^2 + 2r + 2)^2 = 0$$

one can see that the solutions for $r$ are the distinct single root $r_{1} = 3$ and the double complex roots $r_{2,3,4,5} = 1 \pm i$. This corresponds to the real-valued general solution

$$y(x) = c_1 e^{3x} + e^x(c_2 \cos x + c_3 \sin x) + xe^x(c_4 \cos x + c_5 \sin x)$$

with constants $c_{1}, ..., c_{5}$.

:::

### Distinct real roots {#Distinct_real_roots}

The *[superposition principle](wikipedia://en/1201321) for linear homogeneous* says that if $u_{1}, ..., u_{n}$ are $n$ [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) solutions to a particular differential equation, then $c_{1}u_{1} + \cdots + c_{n}u_{n}$ is also a solution for all values $c_{1}, ..., c_{n}$.[^o5][^o13] Therefore, if the characteristic equation has distinct real roots $r_{1}, ..., r_{n}$, then a general solution will be of the form

$$y_\mathrm{D}(x) = c_1 e^{r_1 x} + c_2 e^{r_2 x} + \cdots + c_n e^{r_n x}$$

### Repeated real roots {#Repeated_real_roots}

If the characteristic equation has a root $r_{1}$ that is repeated $k$ times, then it is clear that $y_{p}(x) = c_{1}e^{r_{1}x}$ is at least one solution.[^o5] However, this solution lacks linearly independent solutions from the other $k - 1$ roots. Since $r_{1}$ has [multiplicity](wikipedia://en/19211790) $k$, the differential equation can be factored into[^o5]

$$\left ( \frac{d}{dx} - r_1 \right )^k y = 0 .$$

The fact that $y_{p}(x) = c_{1}e^{r_{1}x}$ is one solution allows one to presume that the general solution may be of the form $y(x) = u(x)e^{r_{1}x}$, where $u(x)$ is a function to be determined. Substituting $ue^{r_{1}x}$ gives

$$\left( \frac{d}{dx} - r_1 \right)\! ue^{r_1 x} = \frac{d}{dx}\left(ue^{r_1 x}\right) - r_1 ue^{r_1 x} = \frac{d}{dx}(u)e^{r_1 x} + r_1 ue^{r_1 x}- r_1 ue^{r_1 x} = \frac{d}{dx}(u)e^{r_1 x}$$

when $k = 1$. By applying this fact $k$ times, it follows that

$$\left( \frac{d}{dx} - r_1 \right)^k ue^{r_1 x} = \frac{d^k}{dx^k}(u)e^{r_1 x} = 0.$$

By dividing out $e^{r_{1}x}$, it can be seen that

$$\frac{d^k}{dx^k}(u) = u^{(k)} = 0.$$

Therefore, the general case for $u(x)$ is a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) of degree $k - 1$, so that $u(x) = c_{1} + c_{2}x + c_{3}x^{2} + \cdots + c_{k}x^{k-1}$.[^o7] Since $y(x) = ue^{r_{1}x}$, the part of the general solution corresponding to $r_{1}$ is

$$y_\mathrm{R}(x) = e^{r_1 x}\!\left(c_1 + c_2 x + \cdots + c_k x^{k-1}\right).$$

### Complex roots {#Complex_roots}

If a second-order differential equation has a characteristic equation with [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) roots of the form $r_{1} = a + bi$ and $r_{2} = a - bi$, then the general solution is accordingly $y(x) = c_{1}e^{(a + bi)x} + c_{2}e^{(a - bi)x}$. By [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613), which states that $e^{i\theta} = \cos(\theta) + i \sin(\theta)$, this solution can be rewritten as follows:

$$\begin{aligned} y(x) &= c_{1}e^{(a + bi)x} + c_{2}e^{(a - bi)x}\\ &= c_{1}e^{ax}(\cos bx + i \sin bx) + c_{2}e^{ax}( \cos bx - i \sin bx ) \\ &= \left(c_{1} + c_{2}\right)e^{ax} \cos bx + i(c_{1} - c_{2})e^{ax} \sin bx \end{aligned}$$

where $c_{1}$ and $c_{2}$ are constants that can be non-real and which depend on the initial conditions.[^o7] (Indeed, since $y(x)$ is real, $c_{1} - c_{2}$ must be [imaginary](wikipedia://en/14554) or zero and $c_{1} + c_{2}$ must be real, in order for both terms after the last [equals sign](wikipedia://en/501828) to be real.)

For example, if $c_{1} = c_{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}$, then the particular solution $y_{1}(x) = e^{ax} \cos(bx)$ is formed. Similarly, if $c_{1} = \tfrac{1}{2i}$ and $c_{2} = -\tfrac{1}{2i}$, then the independent solution formed is $y_{2}(x) = e^{ax} \sin(bx)$. Thus by the *superposition principle for linear homogeneous differential equations*, a second-order differential equation having complex roots $r = a \pm bi$ will result in the following general solution:

$$y_\mathrm{C}(x) = e^{ax}(C_1 \cos bx + C_2 \sin bx)$$

This analysis also applies to the parts of the solutions of a higher-order differential equation whose characteristic equation involves non-real complex conjugate roots.
