# Objection (argument)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Objection"}

In [argumentation](wikipedia://en/1317383), an **objection** is a reason [arguing](wikipedia://en/13018310) against a [premise](wikipedia://en/7993337), [argument](wikipedia://en/13018310), or [conclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conclusion_of_law). Definitions of objection vary in whether an objection is always an argument (or [counterargument](wikipedia://en/1684615)) or may include other moves such as questioning.[^o2]

:::

An objection to an objection is sometimes known as a [rebuttal](wikipedia://en/1684615).[^o3]

An objection can be issued against an argument retroactively from the point of reference of that argument. This form of objection – invented by the presocratic philosopher [Parmenides](wikipedia://en/23575) – is commonly referred to as a **retroactive refutation**.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Assumed co premise"}

## Inference objection {#Inference_objection}

An **inference objection** is an objection to an argument based not on any of its stated premises, but rather on the *relationship* between a [premise](wikipedia://en/7993337) (or set of premises) and main contention.[^o7][^o8] For a given simple argument, if the assumption is made that its premises are correct, fault may be found in the progression from these to the conclusion of the argument. This can often take the form of an unstated [co-premise](wikipedia://en/7946278),[^o9] as in [begging the question](wikipedia://en/43582). In other words, it may be necessary to make an assumption in order to conclude anything from a set of true statements. This assumption must also be true in order that the conclusion follow logically from the initial statements.

In the first example [argument map](wikipedia://en/6190251) shown below, the objector cannot find anything contentious in the stated premises of the argument, but still disagrees with the conclusion; the objection is therefore placed beside the main premise and, in this case, exactly corresponds to an unstated or 'hidden' co-premise. This is demonstrated by the second example argument map shown, in which the full pattern of reasoning relating to the contention is set out.
