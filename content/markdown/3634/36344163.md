# Linear arboricity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Linear arboricity"}

*Image: Rhombic dodecahedron linear arboricity.svg.* Partition of the graph of a [rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631) into two [linear forests](wikipedia://en/36344169), showing that its linear arboricity is two

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **linear arboricity** of an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) is the smallest number of [linear forests](wikipedia://en/36344169) its edges can be partitioned into. Here, a linear forest is an [acyclic](wikipedia://en/168609) graph with maximum [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) two; that is, it is a [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/9938459) of [path graphs](wikipedia://en/4587190). Linear arboricity is a variant of [arboricity](wikipedia://en/2476311), the minimum number of [forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_%28graph_theory%29) into which the edges can be partitioned.

:::

The linear arboricity of any graph of maximum degree $\Delta$ is known to be at least $\lceil\Delta/2\rceil$ and is [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) to be at most $\lceil(\Delta+1)/2\rceil$. This conjecture would determine the linear arboricity exactly for graphs of [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) degree, as in that case both expressions are equal. For graphs of [even](wikipedia://en/143135) degree it would imply that the linear arboricity must be one of only two possible values, but determining the exact value among these two choices is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete).

## Relation to degree {#Relation_to_degree}

::: {#o3 .problem cue=unsolved}

Does every graph of maximum degree $\Delta$ have linear arboricity at most $\lceil(\Delta+1)/2\rceil$?

:::

The linear arboricity of a graph $G$ with maximum degree $\Delta$ is always at least $\lceil\Delta/2\rceil$, because each linear forest can use only two of the edges at a maximum-degree vertex. The **linear arboricity conjecture** of (Akiyama Exoo Harary 1981) is that this [lower bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_bound) is also tight: according to their conjecture, every graph has linear arboricity at most $\lceil(\Delta+1)/2\rceil$. However, this remains [unproven](wikipedia://en/82285), with the best proven [upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound) on the linear arboricity being somewhat larger, $\Delta/2 + O(\Delta^{2/3 - c})$ for some constant $c > 0$ due to Ferber, Fox and Jain.

In order for the linear arboricity of a graph to equal $\Delta/2$, $\Delta$ must be even and each linear forest must have two edges incident to each vertex of degree $\Delta$. But at a vertex that is at the end of a path, the forest containing that path has only one incident edge, so the degree at that vertex cannot equal $\Delta$. Thus, a graph whose linear arboricity equals $\Delta/2$ must have some vertices whose degree is less than maximum. In a [regular graph](wikipedia://en/85821), there are no such vertices, and the linear arboricity cannot equal $\Delta/2$. Therefore, for regular graphs, the linear arboricity conjecture implies that the linear arboricity is exactly $\lceil(\Delta+1)/2\rceil$.

## Related problems {#Related_problems}

Linear arboricity is a variation of [arboricity](wikipedia://en/2476311), the minimum number of forests that the edges of a graph can be partitioned into. Researchers have also studied linear $k$-arboricity, a variant of linear arboricity in which each path in the linear forest can have at most $k$ edges.

Another related problem is [Hamiltonian decomposition](wikipedia://en/24004195), the problem of decomposing a [regular graph](wikipedia://en/85821) of even degree $\Delta$ into exactly $\Delta/2$ [Hamiltonian cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle). A given graph has a Hamiltonian decomposition [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the subgraph formed by removing an arbitrary vertex from the graph has linear arboricity $\Delta/2$.

## Computational complexity {#Computational_complexity}

Unlike arboricity, which can be determined in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time), linear arboricity is [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard). Even recognizing the graphs of linear arboricity two is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete). However, for [cubic graphs](wikipedia://en/980508) and other graphs of maximum degree three, the linear arboricity is always two, and a decomposition into two linear forests can be found in [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time) using an algorithm based on [depth-first search](wikipedia://en/97034).
