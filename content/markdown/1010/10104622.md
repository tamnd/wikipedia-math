# Kuratowski's closure-complement problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kuratowski's closure-complement problem"}

In [point-set topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point-set_topology), **Kuratowski's closure-complement problem** asks for the largest number of distinct sets obtainable by repeatedly applying the set operations of [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) and [complement](wikipedia://en/54347) to a given starting subset of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450). The answer is 14. This result was first published by [Kazimierz Kuratowski](wikipedia://en/46075) in 1922.[^o2] It gained additional exposure in Kuratowski's fundamental monograph *Topologie* (first published in French in 1933; the first English translation appeared in 1966) before achieving fame as a textbook exercise in [John L. Kelley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_L._Kelley)'s 1955 classic, *General Topology*.[^o3]

:::

::: {#o5 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

Letting $S$ denote an arbitrary subset of a topological space, write $kS$ for the closure of $S$, and $cS$ for the complement of $S$. The following three identities imply that no more than 14 distinct sets are obtainable:

1. $kkS=kS$. (The closure operation is [idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotency).)
1. $ccS=S$. (The complement operation is an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450).)
1. $kckckckcS=kckcS$. (Or equivalently $kckckckS=kckckckccS=kckS$, using identity (2)).

The first two are trivial. The third follows from the identity $kikiS=kiS$ where $iS$ is the [interior](wikipedia://en/55610) of $S$ which is equal to the complement of the closure of the complement of $S$, $iS=ckcS$. (The operation $ki=kckc$ is idempotent.)

A subset realizing the maximum of 14 is called a **14-set**. The space of [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) under the usual topology contains 14-sets. Here is one example:

$$(0,1)\cup(1,2)\cup\{3\}\cup\bigl([4,5]\cap\mathbb{Q}\bigr),$$

where $(1,2)$ denotes an [open interval](wikipedia://en/49172#Definitions) and $[4,5]$ denotes a closed interval. Let $X$ denote this set. Then the following 14 sets are accessible:

1. $X$, the set shown above.
1. $$cX=(-\infty,0]\cup\{1\}\cup[2,3)\cup(3,4)\cup\bigl((4,5)\setminus\mathbb{Q}\bigr)\cup(5,\infty)$$
1. $$kcX=(-\infty,0]\cup\{1\}\cup[2,\infty)$$
1. $$ckcX=(0,1)\cup(1,2)$$
1. $$kckcX=[0,2]$$
1. $$ckckcX=(-\infty,0)\cup(2,\infty)$$
1. $$kckckcX=(-\infty,0]\cup[2,\infty)$$
1. $$ckckckcX=(0,2)$$
1. $$kX=[0,2]\cup\{3\}\cup[4,5]$$
1. $$ckX=(-\infty,0)\cup(2,3)\cup(3,4)\cup(5,\infty)$$
1. $$kckX=(-\infty,0]\cup[2,4]\cup[5,\infty)$$
1. $$ckckX=(0,2)\cup(4,5)$$
1. $$kckckX=[0,2]\cup[4,5]$$
1. $$ckckckX=(-\infty,0)\cup(2,4)\cup(5,\infty)$$

:::

## Further results {#Further_results}

Despite its origin within the context of a topological space, Kuratowski's closure-complement problem is actually more [algebraic](wikipedia://en/18716923) than topological. A surprising abundance of closely related problems and results have appeared since 1960, many of which have little or nothing to do with point-set topology.[^o7]

Regarded as set-valued set functions, the 14 closure-complement operations comprise an [operator monoid](wikipedia://en/19652#Acts_and_operator_monoids) called the **Kuratowski monoid** where the monoid product is [function composition](wikipedia://en/195947).[^o8][^o9] This monoid, which can be used to classify topological spaces based on the [quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_monoid) they satisfy, has inspired similar classifications in other settings as well.[^o10]
