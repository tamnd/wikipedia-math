# Reynolds analogy

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Reynolds analogy"}

The **Reynolds Analogy** is popularly known to relate turbulent momentum and heat transfer.[^o2] That is because in a turbulent flow (in a pipe or in a [boundary layer](wikipedia://en/224301)) the transport of momentum and the transport of heat largely depends on the same turbulent [eddies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eddy_%28fluid_dynamics%29): the velocity and the temperature profiles have the same shape.

:::

The main assumption is that wall [heat flux](wikipedia://en/14581057) $\dot{q}_w$ in a turbulent system is analogous to momentum flux, or wall [shear stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_stress), $\tau_w$, which suggests that the ratio $\frac{\tau_w}{\dot{q}_w}$ must be constant for similar geometry. A common example for "similar geometry" is every radial position around a pipe at some [cross-section](wikipedia://en/1337370) with circular shape.

If the wall shear stress ($\tau_w$) is known, a relation for the turbulent heat flux ($\dot{q}_w$) can be found (or vice versa), making it a useful analogy for pipe flows and [hypersonic](wikipedia://en/55244) applications where $\tau_w$ may be more easily estimated than $\dot{q}_w$.

This is often used to estimate wall heat flux on a body in a flow[^o3]:

$$\dot{q}_w \approx \frac{\tau_wc_p(T_w-T_{\infty})}{u_{\infty}}$$

where:

- **$T_w,T_{\infty}$** are the temperatures of the wall and [freestream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freestream) (or bulk, depending on application), respectively;
- **$c_p$** is the [specific heat capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_heat_capacity) at constant pressure for the fluid;
- **$u_{\infty}$** is the [freestream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freestream) (or bulk, depending on application) fluid velocity.

The complete Reynolds analogy (in a form more often seen in relation to pipe flows) is:

$$\frac{f}{2} = \frac{h}{c_p G} = \frac{k'_c}{v_\text{avg}}$$

where:

- **$f$** is the [Fanning friction factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fanning_friction_factor);
- **$h$** is the [heat transfer coefficient](wikipedia://en/1464363);
- **$c_p$** is the [specific heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_heat) at constant pressure;
- **$G$** is the [mass velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_velocity) (mass flow rate per unit area).

Experimental data for gas streams agree approximately with above equation if the [Schmidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schmidt_number) and [Prandtl ($Pr$)](wikipedia://en/58283) numbers are near 1.0 and only [skin friction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin_friction) is present in flow past a flat plate or inside a pipe. When liquids are present and/or [form drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Form_drag) is present, the analogy is conventionally known to be invalid.[^o2]

In 2008, the qualitative validity of Reynolds' analogy was re-visited for [laminar flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laminar_flow) of an [incompressible fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incompressible_fluid) with variable [dynamic viscosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_viscosity) ($\mu$)[^o4]. It was shown that the inverse dependence of [Reynolds number](wikipedia://en/23868856) ($Re$) and [skin friction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin_friction) coefficient ($C_f$) is the basis for the validity of the analogy in both constant and variable $\mu$ flows.

For $\mu = \text{const.}$, the analogy reduces to the popular form where the [Stanton number](wikipedia://en/3739824) ($C_h\text{ or }St$) increases with increasing $Re$. For variable $\mu$, it reduces to $C_h$ increasing with decreasing $Re$.

This is represented by[^o5]:

$$s=\frac{2C_h}{C_f}\approx \text{constant}$$

where:

- **$s$** is the Reynolds analogy factor which is coupled to the [Prandtl number](wikipedia://en/58283) via $sPr\approx 0.8$;
- **$C_f$** is the skin friction coefficient given by $C_f=2\tau_w/(\rho_{\infty}u^2_{\infty})$;
  - **$\rho_{\infty}$** is the [freestream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freestream) fluid density;
- **$C_h$** is the [Stanton number](wikipedia://en/3739824) (also often denoted $St$). In [supersonic](wikipedia://en/61273)/[hypersonic](wikipedia://en/55244) applications, the Stanton Number is $C_h=\dot{q}_w/ (\rho_{\infty}u_{\infty}c_p(T_w-T_r))$;
  - **$T_r$** is the recovery temperature, $T_r=T_{\infty}+Pr^{1/3}(\gamma-1)M^2_{\infty}/2$;
    - **$\gamma$** is the [ratio of specific heats](wikipedia://en/1949009), $\gamma=c_p/c_v$;
    - **$M_{\infty}$** is the freestream [Mach number](wikipedia://en/20051).

Consequently, the [Chilton–Colburn analogy](wikipedia://en/10109665) is qualitatively valid whenever the Reynolds' analogy is valid. Furthermore, the validity of the analogy is linked to the applicability of [Prigogine's Theorem of Minimum Entropy Production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prigogine's_theorem)[^o6]. Thus, Reynolds' analogy is valid for flows that are close to being [fully developed](wikipedia://en/11034#Developed_flow), where changes in the gradients of velocity and temperature along the flow are small[^o4].
