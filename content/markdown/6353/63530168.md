# Obelus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Obelus"}

*Image: Obelus variants.svg.* Three variants of obelus glyphs

An **obelus** (plural: **obeluses** or **obeli**) is a historical annotation mark in [codicology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codicology) that has come to have three distinct modern forms, meanings and [typographical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typography) uses:

:::

- [Division sign](wikipedia://en/276918) ÷
- [Dagger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagger_%28typography%29) †
- [Commercial minus sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_minus_sign) ⁒ (limited geographical area of use)

The word "obelus" comes from grc (grc), the [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) word for a sharpened stick, spit, or pointed pillar.[^o3] This is the same root as that of the word '[obelisk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obelisk)'.[^o4] In codicology, a (usually horizontal) obelus in the form of a dagger was used to indicate erroneous or dubious content.[^o5][^o6] The third symbol is an obsolescent mark used in some European countries.

In mathematics, the ÷ symbol is mainly used in [Anglophone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglophone) countries to represent the mathematical operation of [division](wikipedia://en/53696) (but has other meanings elsewhere) and is called an obelus in older textbooks.[^o7] In modern typography, the second symbol, called a [dagger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagger_%28mark%29) mark † is used as a reference mark or [footnote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Footnote) indicator.[^o8] It also has [other uses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagger_%28mark%29#Modern_usage) in a variety of specialist contexts.

## Use in text annotation {#Use_in_text_annotation}

The modern dagger symbol originated from a variant of the obelus, originally depicted by a plain line −, or a line with one or two dots ⨪ ÷.[^o10] It represented an iron roasting spit, a dart, or the sharp end of a [javelin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Javelin),[^o11] symbolizing the skewering or cutting out of dubious matter.[^o13]

Originally, one of these marks (or a plain line) was used in ancient manuscripts to mark passages that were suspected of being corrupted or spurious; the practice of adding such marginal notes became known as [obelism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obelism). The [dagger symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagger_%28typography%29) †, also called an *obelisk*,[^o12] is derived from the obelus, and continues to be used for this purpose.

The obelus is believed to have been invented by the [Homeric scholar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeric_scholarship) [Zenodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenodotus), as one of a system of editorial symbols. They marked questionable or corrupt words or passages in manuscripts of the [Homeric epics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeric_epics).[^o13] The system was further refined by his student [Aristophanes of Byzantium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristophanes_of_Byzantium), who first introduced the [asterisk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asterisk) and used a symbol resembling a ⊤ for an obelus; and finally by Aristophanes' student, in turn, [Aristarchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristarchus_of_Samothrace), from whom they earned the name of "[Aristarchian symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristarchian_symbols)".[^o14][^o15]

In some commercial and financial documents, especially in Germany and Scandinavia, a variant (2052) is used in the margins of letters to indicate an enclosure, where the upper point is sometimes replaced with the corresponding number.[^o16] In Finland, the obelus (or a slight variant, $\cdot \! / \! \cdot$) is used as a symbol for a correct response (alongside the [check mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Check_mark), ✓, which is used for an *incorrect* response).[^o26][^o17]

In the 7.0 release of Unicode, 2E13 was one of [a group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obelism#Modern_encoding) of "Ancient Greek textual symbols" that were added to the specification (in the block [Supplemental Punctuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supplemental_Punctuation)).[^o18]

## In mathematics {#In_mathematics}

*Image: Skjermbilete 2012-11-03 kl. 02.48.36.png.* Plus and minuses. The obelus– or division sign– used as a variant of the minus sign in an excerpt from an official Norwegian trading statement form called «Næringsoppgave 1» for the taxation year 2010.

The form of the obelus as a horizontal line with a dot above and a dot below, ÷, was first used as a symbol for [division](wikipedia://en/53696) by the [Swiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_people) mathematician [Johann Rahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Rahn) in his book *Teutsche Algebra* in 1659. This gave rise to the modern mathematical symbol ÷, used in anglophone countries as a [division sign](wikipedia://en/276918).[^o20][^o21] This usage, though widespread in Anglophone countries, is neither universal nor recommended: the [ISO 80000-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_80000-2) standard for [mathematical notation](wikipedia://en/277184) recommends only the [solidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash_%28punctuation%29) / or [fraction bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29#Vocabulary) for division, or the [colon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colon_%28punctuation%29) : for [ratios](wikipedia://en/87837); it says that ÷ "should not be used" for division.[^o22] The ambiguity of mathematical expressions that involve the [obelus and implicit multiplication](wikipedia://en/212980#Mixed_division_and_multiplication) has become a subject of Internet memes.

This form of the obelus was also occasionally used as a mathematical symbol for [subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141) in Northern Europe; such usage continued in some parts of Europe (including [Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway) and, until fairly recently, [Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denmark)).[^o23] In [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy), [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland) and [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia), this notation is sometimes used in [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) to denote a [range of values](wikipedia://en/49172) (for example, "24.1÷25.6" means the range of values between 24.1 and 25.6).[^o24]

In some commercial and financial documents, especially in Germany and Scandinavia, the symbol ÷ was used to signify a negative remainder of a division operation.[^o25][^o26] (The Unicode Consortium has created a separate codepoint, ⁒, to represent that meaning. The [representative glyph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_glyph) shown here is not significant: its precise form depends on the [computer font](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_font) used.)
