# Derrick's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Derrick's theorem"}

**Derrick's theorem** is an argument by physicist G. H. Derrick which shows that stationary [localized solutions](wikipedia://en/51654) to a [nonlinear wave equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_wave_equation) or [nonlinear Klein&ndash;Gordon equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_Klein&ndash;Gordon_equation) in spatial dimensions three and higher are [unstable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instability).

:::

## Original argument {#Original_argument}

Derrick's paper,[^o3] which was considered an obstacle to interpreting soliton-like solutions as particles, contained the following physical argument about non-existence of stable [localized stationary solutions](wikipedia://en/51654) to the nonlinear wave equation

$$\nabla^2 \theta-\frac{\partial^2\theta}{\partial t^2}=\frac 1 2 f'(\theta), \qquad \theta(x,t)\in\mathbb{R},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^3,$$

now known under the name of Derrick's Theorem. (Above, $f(s)$ is a [differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206) with $f'(0)=0$.)

The energy of the time-independent solution $\theta(x)\,$ is given by

$$E=\int\left[(\nabla\theta)^2+f(\theta)\right] \, d^3 x.$$

A [necessary condition](wikipedia://en/169319) for the solution to be stable is $\delta^2 E\ge 0\,$. Suppose $\theta(x)\,$ is a localized solution of $\delta E=0\,$. Define $\theta_\lambda(x)=\theta(\lambda x)\,$ where $\lambda$ is an arbitrary constant, and write $I_1=\int(\nabla\theta)^2 d^3 x$, $I_2=\int f(\theta) d^3 x$. Then

$$E_\lambda =\int\left[(\nabla\theta_\lambda)^2+f(\theta_\lambda)\right] \, d^3 x =I_1/\lambda +I_2/\lambda^3.$$

Whence $dE_\lambda/d\lambda\vert_{\lambda=1}=-I_1-3 I_2=0.\,$ and since $I_1>0\,$,

$$\left.\frac{d^2E_\lambda}{d\lambda^2}\right|_{\lambda=1}=2 I_1+12 I_2=-2 I_1\,<0.$$

That is, $\delta^2 E<0\,$ for a variation corresponding to a uniform stretching of the *particle*. Hence the solution $\theta(x)\,$ is unstable.

Derrick's argument works for $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$, $n\ge 3\,$.

::: {#o5 .statement cue=named-heading name="Pokhozhaev's identity"}

## Pokhozhaev's identity {#Pokhozhaev's_identity}

More generally,[^o6] let $g$ be continuous, with $g(0)=0$. Denote $G(s)=\int_0^s g(t)\,dt$. Let

$$u\in L^\infty_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^n), \qquad \nabla u\in L^2(\mathbb{R}^n), \qquad G(u(\cdot))\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^n), \qquad n\in\mathbb{N},$$

be a solution to the equation

$$-\nabla^2 u=g(u),$$

[in the sense of distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29). Then $u$ satisfies the relation

$$(n-2)\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}|\nabla u(x)|^2\,dx=n\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}G(u(x))\,dx,$$

known as [Pokhozhaev's identity](wikipedia://en/63029619) (sometimes spelled as *Pohozaev's identity*).[^o7] This result is similar to the [virial theorem](wikipedia://en/32664).

:::

## Interpretation in the Hamiltonian form {#Interpretation_in_the_Hamiltonian_form}

We may write the equation $\partial_t^2 u=\nabla^2 u-\frac{1}{2}f'(u)$ in the [Hamiltonian form](wikipedia://en/1197531) $\partial_t u=\delta_v H(u,v)$, $\partial_t v=-\delta_u H(u,v)$, where $u,\,v$ are functions of $x\in\mathbb{R}^n,\,t\in\mathbb{R}$, the [Hamilton function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton_function) is given by

$$H(u,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\left( \frac{1}{2}|v|^2+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^2+\frac{1}{2}f(u) \right)\,dx,$$

and $\delta_u H\,$, $\delta_v H\,$ are the [variational derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variational_derivative) of $H(u,v)\,$.

Then the stationary solution $u(x,t)=\theta(x)\,$ has the energy $H(\theta,0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\left( \frac{1}{2}|\nabla\theta|^2+\frac{1}{2}f(\theta) \right)\,d^n x$ and satisfies the equation

$$0=\partial_t \theta(x)=-\partial_u H(\theta,0)=\frac{1}{2}E'(\theta),$$

with $E'\,$ denoting a variational derivative of the [functional](wikipedia://en/1374948) $E=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}[\vert\nabla\theta\vert^2+f(\theta)]\,d^n x$. Although the solution $\theta(x)\,$ is a critical point of $E\,$ (since $E'(\theta)=0\,$), Derrick's argument shows that $\frac{d^2}{d\lambda\,^2}E(\theta(\lambda x))<0$ at $\lambda=1\,$, hence $u(x,t)=\theta(x)\,$ is not a point of the local minimum of the energy functional $H\,$. Therefore, physically, the solution $\theta(x)\,$ is expected to be unstable. A related result, showing non-minimization of the energy of localized stationary states (with the argument also written for $n=3$, although the derivation being valid in dimensions $n\ge 2$) was obtained by R. H. Hobart in 1963.[^o9]

## Relation to linear instability {#Relation_to_linear_instability}

A stronger statement, [linear (or exponential) instability](wikipedia://en/3652079) of localized stationary solutions to the nonlinear [wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691) (in any spatial dimension) is proved by P. Karageorgis and W. A. Strauss in 2007.[^o11]

## Stability of localized time-periodic solutions {#Stability_of_localized_time-periodic_solutions}

Derrick describes some possible ways out of this difficulty, including the conjecture that *Elementary particles might correspond to stable, localized solutions which are periodic in time, rather than time-independent.* Indeed, it was later shown[^o13] that a [time-periodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-periodic) [solitary wave](wikipedia://en/51654) $u(x,t)=\phi_\omega(x)e^{-i\omega t}\,$ with frequency $\omega\,$ may be [orbitally stable](wikipedia://en/28842205) if the [Vakhitov&ndash;Kolokolov stability criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vakhitov&ndash;Kolokolov_stability_criterion) is satisfied.
