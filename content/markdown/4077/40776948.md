# Derived scheme

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Derived scheme"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a **derived scheme** is a [homotopy](wikipedia://en/212250)-theoretic generalization of a [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) in which classical [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346) are replaced with derived versions such as [differential graded algebras](wikipedia://en/2415863), commutative [simplicial rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicial_ring), or [commutative ring spectra](wikipedia://en/40777156).

:::

From the [functor of points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functor_of_points) point-of-view, a derived scheme is a [sheaf](wikipedia://en/12910) *X* on the category of simplicial commutative rings which admits an open affine covering $\{Spec(A_i) \to X\}$.

From the locally [ringed space](wikipedia://en/248014) point-of-view, a derived scheme is a pair $(X, \mathcal{O})$ consisting of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X* and a [sheaf](wikipedia://en/40784278) $\mathcal{O}$ either of simplicial commutative rings or of [commutative ring spectra](wikipedia://en/40777156)[^o2] on *X* such that (1) the pair $(X, \pi_0 \mathcal{O})$ is a [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) and (2) $\pi_k \mathcal{O}$ is a [quasi-coherent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-coherent_sheaf) $\pi_0 \mathcal{O}$-[module](wikipedia://en/276410).

A [derived stack](wikipedia://en/42074131) is a stacky generalization of a derived scheme.

## Differential graded scheme {#Differential_graded_scheme}

Over a field of characteristic zero, the theory is closely related to that of a differential graded scheme.[^o4] By definition, a **differential graded scheme** is obtained by gluing affine differential graded schemes, with respect to [étale topology](wikipedia://en/5707590).[^o5] It was introduced by [Maxim Kontsevich](wikipedia://en/104509)[^o6] "as the first approach to derived algebraic geometry"[^o7] and was developed further by [Mikhail Kapranov](wikipedia://en/62272076) and Ionut Ciocan-Fontanine.

### Connection with differential graded rings and examples {#Connection_with_differential_graded_rings_and_examples}

Just as [affine](wikipedia://en/298834) algebraic geometry is equivalent (in [categorical sense](wikipedia://en/5869)) to the theory of [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346) (commonly called [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990)), affine [derived algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/40778281) over characteristic zero is equivalent to the theory of [commutative differential graded rings](wikipedia://en/2415863). One of the main example of derived schemes comes from the derived intersection of subschemes of a scheme, giving the [Koszul complex](wikipedia://en/519992). For example, let $f_1, \ldots, f_k \in \mathbb{C}[x_1,\ldots, x_n] = R$, then we can get a derived scheme

$$(X,\mathcal{O}_\bullet) = \mathbf{RSpec} \left (R/(f_1) \otimes_R^\mathbf{L} \cdots \otimes_R^\mathbf{L} R/(f_k) \right )$$

where

$$\textbf{RSpec}:(\textbf{dga}_\mathbb{C})^{op} \to \textbf{DerSch}$$

is the [étale spectrum](wikipedia://en/54321373). Since we can construct a resolution

$$\begin{matrix} 0 \to & R & \xrightarrow{\cdot f_i} & R &\to 0 \\ &\downarrow&&\downarrow& \\ 0\to &0& \to&R/(f_i) & \to 0 \end{matrix}$$

the derived ring $R/(f_1) \otimes_R^\mathbf{L} \cdots \otimes_R^\mathbf{L} R/(f_k)$, a [derived tensor product](wikipedia://en/54501194), is the Koszul complex $K_R(f_1,\ldots, f_k)$. The truncation of this derived scheme to amplitude $[-1,0]$ provides a classical model motivating derived algebraic geometry. Notice that if we have a projective scheme

$$\operatorname{Proj}\left( \frac{\mathbb{Z}[x_0,\ldots,x_n]}{(f_1,\ldots, f_k)} \right)$$

where $\deg(f_i) = d_i$ we can construct the derived scheme $(\mathbb{P}^n, \mathcal{E}^\bullet,(f_1,\ldots, f_k))$ where

$$\mathcal{E}^\bullet = [\mathcal{O}(-d_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{O}(-d_k) \xrightarrow{(\cdot f_1,\ldots,\cdot f_k)} \mathcal{O}]$$

with amplitude $[-1,0]$.

## Cotangent complex {#Cotangent_complex}

::: {#o11 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

### Construction {#Construction}

Let $(A_\bullet,d)$ be a fixed differential graded algebra defined over a field of characteristic $0$. Then a $A_\bullet$-differential graded algebra $(R_\bullet,d_R)$ is called **semi-free** if the following conditions hold:

1. The underlying graded algebra $R_\bullet$ is a polynomial algebra over $A_\bullet$, meaning it is isomorphic to $A_\bullet[\{x_i \}_{i \in I}]$
1. There exists a filtration $\varnothing = I_0 \subseteq I_1 \subseteq \cdots$ on the indexing set $I$ where $\textstyle\bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{N}} I_n = I$ and $d_R(x_i) \in A_\bullet[\{x_j\}_{j \in I_n}]$ for any $x_i \in I_{n+1}$.

It turns out that every $A_\bullet$-differential graded algebra admits a surjective quasi-isomorphism from a semi-free $(A_\bullet,d)$ differential graded algebra, called a semi-free resolution. These are unique up to [homotopy equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_homotopy_equivalence) in a suitable [model category](wikipedia://en/2895304). The (relative) **cotangent complex** of an $(A_\bullet,d)$-differential graded algebra $(B_\bullet, d_B)$ can be constructed using a semi-free resolution $(R_\bullet,d_R) \to (B_\bullet, d_B)$: it is defined as

$$\mathbb{L}_{B_\bullet/A_\bullet} := \Omega_{R_\bullet/A_\bullet}\otimes_{R_\bullet} B_\bullet.$$

Many examples can be constructed by taking the algebra $B$ representing a variety over a field of characteristic 0, finding a presentation of $R$ as a quotient of a polynomial algebra and taking the Koszul complex associated to this presentation. The Koszul complex acts as a semi-free resolution of the differential graded algebra $(B_\bullet,0)$ where $B_\bullet$ is the graded algebra with the non-trivial graded piece in degree 0.

:::

::: {#o13 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

The [cotangent complex](wikipedia://en/17346860) of a [hypersurface](wikipedia://en/525101) $X = \mathbb{V}(f) \subset \mathbb{A}^n_\mathbb{C}$ can easily be computed: since we have the dga $K_R(f)$ representing the **derived enhancement** of $X$, we can compute the cotangent complex as

$$0 \to R\cdot ds \xrightarrow{\Phi} \bigoplus_i R \cdot dx_i \to 0$$

where $\Phi(gds) = g\cdot df$ and $d$ is the usual universal derivation. If we take a complete intersection, then the Koszul complex

$$R^\bullet = \frac{\mathbb{C}[x_1,\ldots,x_n]}{(f_1)} \otimes^\mathbf{L}_{\mathbb{C}[x_1,\ldots,x_n]} \cdots \otimes^\mathbf{L}_{\mathbb{C}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]} \frac{\mathbb{C}[x_1,\ldots,x_n]}{(f_k)}$$

is quasi-isomorphic to the complex

$$\frac{\mathbb{C}[x_1,\ldots,x_n]}{(f_1,\ldots,f_k)}[+0].$$

This implies we can construct the cotangent complex of the derived ring $R^\bullet$ as the tensor product of the cotangent complex above for each $f_i$.

:::

### Remarks {#Remarks}

Please note that the cotangent complex in the context of derived geometry differs from the cotangent complex of classical schemes. Namely, if there was a singularity in the hypersurface defined by $f$ then the cotangent complex would have infinite amplitude. These observations provide motivation for the **hidden smoothness** philosophy of derived geometry since we are now working with a complex of finite length.

## Tangent complexes {#Tangent_complexes}

### Polynomial functions {#Polynomial_functions}

Given a polynomial function $f:\mathbb{A}^n \to \mathbb{A}^m,$ then consider the (homotopy) pullback diagram

$$\begin{matrix} Z & \to & \mathbb{A}^n \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow f \\ \{ pt \} & \xrightarrow{0} & \mathbb{A}^m \end{matrix}$$

where the bottom arrow is the inclusion of a point at the origin. Then, the derived scheme $Z$ has tangent complex at $x\in Z$ is given by the morphism

$$\mathbf{T}_x = T_x\mathbb{A}^n \xrightarrow{df_x} T_0\mathbb{A}^m$$

where the complex is of amplitude $[-1,0]$. Notice that the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) can be recovered using $H^0$ and the $H^{-1}$ measures how far away $x \in Z$ is from being a smooth point.

### Stack quotients {#Stack_quotients}

Given a stack $[X/G]$ there is a nice description for the tangent complex:

$$\mathbf{T}_x = \mathfrak{g}_x \to T_xX .$$

If the morphism is not injective, the $H^{-1}$ measures again how singular the space is. In addition, the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) of this complex yields the correct (virtual) dimension of the [quotient stack](wikipedia://en/40579087). In particular, if we look at the [moduli stack of principal $G$-bundles](wikipedia://en/41790574), then the tangent complex is just $\mathfrak{g}[+1]$.

## Derived schemes in complex Morse theory {#Derived_schemes_in_complex_Morse_theory}

Derived schemes can be used for analyzing topological properties of affine varieties. For example, consider a smooth affine variety $M \subset \mathbb{A}^n$. If we take a regular function $f:M \to \mathbb{C}$ and consider the section of $\Omega_M$

$$\begin{cases} \Gamma_{df}: M \to \Omega_M \\ x \mapsto (x,df(x)) \end{cases}$$

Then, we can take the derived pullback diagram

$$\begin{matrix} X & \to & M \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow 0 \\ M & \xrightarrow{\Gamma_{df}} & \Omega_M \end{matrix}$$

where $0$ is the zero section, constructing a **derived critical locus** of the regular function $f$.

::: {#o20 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

Consider the affine variety

$$M = \operatorname{Spec} (\mathbb{C}[x,y])$$

and the regular function given by $f(x,y) = x^2 + y^3$. Then,

$$\Gamma_{df}(a,b) = (a,b,2a,3b^2)$$

where we treat the last two coordinates as $dx, dy$. The derived critical locus is then the derived scheme

$$\textbf{RSpec}\left( \frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y,dx,dy]}{(dx,dy)} \otimes_{\mathbb{C} [x,y,dx,dy]}^{\mathbf{L}} \frac{\mathbb{C} [x,y,dx,dy]}{(2x - dx, 3y^2 - dy)} \right).$$

Note that since the left term in the derived intersection is a complete intersection, we can compute a complex representing the derived ring as

$$K_{dx,dy}^\bullet(\mathbb{C} [x,y,dx,dy])\otimes_{\mathbb{C} [x,y,dx,dy]} \frac{\mathbb{C} [x,y,dx,dy]}{(2 - dx, 3y^2 - dy)}$$

where $K_{dx,dy}^\bullet(\mathbb{C} [x,y,dx,dy])$ is the Koszul complex.

:::

## Derived critical locus {#Derived_critical_locus}

Consider a [smooth function](wikipedia://en/1408000) $f:M \to \mathbb{C}$ where $M$ is smooth. The derived enhancement of $\operatorname{Crit}(f)$, the **derived critical locus**, is given by the differential graded scheme $(M,\mathcal{A}^\bullet, Q)$ where the underlying graded ring are the polyvector fields

$$\mathcal{A}^{-i} = \wedge^i T_M$$

and the differential $Q$ is defined by contraction by $df$.

::: {#o23 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

For example, if

$$\begin{cases} f:\mathbb{C}^2 \to \mathbb{C} \\ f(x,y) = x^2 + y^3 \end{cases}$$

we have the complex

$$R\cdot \partial x\wedge \partial y \xrightarrow{2xdx + 3y^2dy} R \cdot \partial x \oplus R \cdot \partial y \xrightarrow{2xdx + 3y^2dy} R$$

representing the derived enhancement of $\operatorname{Crit}(f)$.

:::
