# Thomas W. Hungerford

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Thomas W. Hungerford"}

**Thomas William Hungerford** (March 21, 1936 – November 28, 2014)[^o3] was an American mathematician who worked in [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) and [mathematics education](wikipedia://en/326471). He is the author or coauthor of several widely used[^o4] and widely cited[^o5] textbooks covering high-school to graduate-level mathematics. From 1963 until 1980, he taught at the [University of Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Washington) and then at [Cleveland State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleveland_State_University) until 2003. From 2003–2014 he was at [Saint Louis University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Louis_University_%28United_States%29). Hungerford had a special interest in promoting the use of technology to teach mathematics.

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Hungerford was born in [Oak Park, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oak_Park,_Illinois), in 1936. At age 16, he moved to [St. Louis, Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Louis), where he attended [St. Louis University High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Louis_University_High_School) and graduated as the valedictorian of his high school class.[^o3]

After high school, Hungerford enrolled at the [College of the Holy Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_of_the_Holy_Cross) in [Worcester, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worcester,_Massachusetts), graduating with a [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts), *[summa cum laude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_cum_laude)*, in mathematics in 1958.[^o7] As an undergraduate at Holy Cross, he wrote a senior thesis on [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450).[^o7] He then pursued graduate studies at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago), where he earned his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D.) in mathematics in 1963 under the supervision of mathematician [Saunders Mac Lane](wikipedia://en/336574).[^o3] His doctoral dissertation was titled, "Bockstein spectra".[^o8]

## Academic career {#Academic_career}

After receiving his doctorate in 1963, Hungerford began teaching mathematics at the [University of Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Washington) in Seattle, where he remained until 1980.[^o10] During his years at Washington, he published research on subjects including Bockstein spectra, hyperhomology, multilinear maps, and the structure of principal ideal rings.[^o11][^o12] In a 1968 paper in the *Pacific Journal of Mathematics*, he proved that every principal ideal ring is a finite direct sum of rings, each of which is a homomorphic image of a principal ideal domain.[^o13]

Hungerford's graduate-level textbook *Algebra* was originally published by [Holt, Rinehart and Winston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holt,_Rinehart_and_Winston) in 1974 and was subsequently issued by [Springer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springer_Science+Business_Media) as volume 73 of its *[Graduate Texts in Mathematics](wikipedia://en/7392980)* series.[^o14] The book covers groups, rings, modules, fields and Galois theory, linear algebra, commutative rings and modules, ring structure, and category theory.[^o14] In 1980, Hungerford moved from Washington to [Cleveland State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleveland_State_University), where he taught mathematics until 2001.[^o10]

In addition to his research in algebra, Hungerford published work in mathematics education and authored or coauthored more than a dozen mathematics textbooks from the high-school level to graduate level.[^o15] He promoted the use of technology in mathematics instruction, served as a referee and reviewer for mathematical journals, and participated on [National Science Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation) panels that evaluated grant applications.[^o15] After returning to St. Louis in 2003, Hungerford maintained an office at [Saint Louis University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Louis_University) until 2013, when he retired from writing and revising textbooks.[^o10] His later textbooks included works in abstract algebra, precalculus, college algebra, trigonometry, finite mathematics, and applied mathematics.[^o15]
