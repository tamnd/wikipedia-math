# Sebastian Finsterwalder

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sebastian Finsterwalder"}

**Sebastian Finsterwalder** (4 October 1862 – 4 December 1951) was a German mathematician and glaciologist. Acknowledged as the "father of glacier photogrammetry";[^o3][^o4] he pioneered the use of [repeat photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rephotography) as a temporal surveying instrument in measurement of the geology and structure of the Alps and their glacier flows.[^o5] The measurement techniques he developed and the data he produced are still in use to discover evidence for [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change).[^o6][^o7][^o8][^o9]

:::

## Life {#Life}

Sebastian Finsterwalder was born 4 October 1862 in [Rosenheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosenheim), son of Johann Nepomuk Finsterwalder, a master baker from [Antdorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antdorf) near [Weilheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weilheim-Schongau), [Upper Bavaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Bavaria), and Anna Amman of Rosenheim.[^o11] He died 4 December 1951 in [Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich)[^o12]).[^o13] He was a Bavarian [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and [surveyor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying).[^o14] In 1892 he married Franziska Mallepell (d. 1953) from [Brixen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brixen), [South Tyrol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Tyrol). Their two sons worked in similar fields; Richard Finsterwalder (1899-1963), Professor at the Technical University in Hanover and Munich, and Ulrich Finsterwalder (1897-1988), a [civil engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_engineer).

A keen mountaineer, Finsterwalder became interested, through the influence of his friend E. Richter, in alpine fossils as indicators of the geology and structure of the Alps and their glaciers. His desire for accurate, but also less costly, motion measurements on glaciers led him to glaciological applications of [photogrammetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photogrammetry) in [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608).[^o15]

In 1886, aged 24, he received his doctorate from the University of Tübingen, under the guidance of the algebraic geometer [Alexander von Brill](wikipedia://en/28565454). Finsterwalder observed that [Rudolf Sturm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Sturm)'s analysis of the "[homography](wikipedia://en/3051962) problem" (1869) can be used to solve the problem of 3D-reconstruction using point matches in two images; which is the mathematical foundation of photogrammetry.

Finsterwalder pioneered geodetic surveys in the high mountains. At the age of 27 years he conducted a first glacier mapping project at [Vernagtferner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernagtferner) in the [Ötztal Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ötztal_Alps), Austria.

## Research and applications of photogrammetry {#Research_and_applications_of_photogrammetry}

Following the 1878 work of Italian engineer [Pio Paganini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pio_Paganini) of the [Istituto Geografico Militare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istituto_Geografico_Militare) and others, Finstenwalder advanced methods for reconstruction and measurements of three-dimensional objects from photographic images.

He was appointed professor at the [Technical University of Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_University_of_Munich) in 1891, succeeding his teacher, A. Voss, at the Department of Analytical Geometry, Differential and Integral Calculus. He stayed at the university for forty years, until 1931. In 1892, he married, and completed the first recording of the Bavarian glacier in [Wettersteingebirge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wettersteingebirge) and the [Berchtesgaden Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berchtesgaden_Alps).

He applied the technique of [plane table](wikipedia://en/12978962) photogrammetry in addition to a conventional geodetic survey, assisted by the novel lightweight, accurate [phototheodolite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phototheodolite) that he had developed for high-mountain applications. The device was based on the prototype phototheodolite developed by Albrecht Meydenbauer (1834-1921) for architectural applications. From 1890 Finsterwalder also employed [aerial photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerial_photography),[^o17] reconstituting the topography of the area of [Gars am Inn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gars_am_Inn) in 1899 from a pair of balloon photographs using mathematical calculations of many points in the images.[^o18]

In 1897 Finsterwalder addressed the [German Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/30864973), and he described some of the results of [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) he was applying to photogrammetry.[^o19] His theory of large triangle meshes became known as the "Finsterwaldersche fields method" (1915). His analytical approach was laborious however, prompting development of analogue instrumentation with stereo measurement permitting faster optical/mechanical reconstruction of the photographic data arrays to determine object points.[^o20] This was assisted by new technology; [Carl Pulfrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Pulfrich)'s stereocomparator (1901) and Eduard Ritter von Orel's stereoautograph (1907), both instruments built by the company Carl Zeiss.[^o21]

In 1911 he took over the chair of descriptive geometry, turning down offers of appointment from Vienna, Berlin and Potsdam.

## Aerodynamics {#Aerodynamics}

[Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) commissioned Finsterwalder while the latter was professor of mathematics at the Munich polytechnic, to write on aerodynamics for his *[Enzyklopädie der mathematischen Wissenschaften mit Einschluss ihrer Anwendungen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein's_encyclopedia)* (EMW) (tr. 'Encyclopedia of mathematical sciences including their applications'). The article, which he submitted in August 1902, more than a year before the [Wrights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wright_brothers) achieved [powered flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powered_aircraft) was prescient in its insights into the mathematics behind this new field of engineering. Finsterwalder also worked with [Martin Kutta](wikipedia://en/551234) (1867–1944) at the Institute in Munich to devise formulas relating to the lift on an [aerofoil](wikipedia://en/290053) in terms of the circulation round it. Finsterwalder assisted with Kutta's 1902 [habilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation) thesis, which contains the [Kutta-Joukowski theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kutta-Joukowski_theorem) giving estimates of the [lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lift_%28force%29) of aerofoils.

## Glacier flow in the Ötztal Alps {#Glacier_flow_in_the_Ötztal_Alps}

In 1922 Finsterwalder mapped the topography of the Ötztal Alps focusing on two glaciers, i.e. Gepatschferner and Weißseeferner, using [stereophotogrammetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereophotogrammetry).[^o24] During this work he discovered Ölgruben rock glacier and the rock glacier north of Krummgampenspitze.[^o25] In 1923 and 1924 Finsterwalder measured a flow velocity profile across Ölgruben rock glacier.[^o26][^o27] Because of Finsterwalder's efforts, Ölgruben rock glacier became the subject of a notably extended, [longitudinal study](wikipedia://en/1557623) of flow velocity with high value in climate research,[^o28] with repeat surveys undertaken by Wolfgang Pillewizer in 1938, 1939, and 1953 using photogrammetry,[^o29] and which is still ongoing, employing modern satellite-based positioning techniques.[^o30] His son Richard assisted in the mapping project in the Ötztal Alps and went on to advance his father's studies.

## Other contributions {#Other_contributions}

Under his leadership the [Bavarian International Commission for Geodesy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bavarian_International_Commission_for_Geodesy) undertook precise gravity measurements with relative [gravimeters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravimeters) throughout [Bavaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bavaria).

## Honours {#Honours}

- 1965 Finsterwalder High School in his birthplace Rosenheim was named after him.
- [Finsterwalder Glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finsterwalder_Glacier) is named after him.
- 1915 President of the German Mathematical Society.
- 1943 awarded Helmert commemorative medallion for excellence by the German Association of Surveying.
- 1938 Asteroid [1482 (Sebastiana)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1482_Sebastiana) was named after him.
