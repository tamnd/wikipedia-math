# Dirichlet kernel

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dirichlet kernel"}

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), the **Dirichlet kernel**, is the collection of periodic functions defined as

:::

$$D_n(x)= \sum_{k=-n}^n e^{ikx} = 1+2\sum_{k=1}^n\cos(kx)=\frac{\sin\left(\left(n +1/2\right) x \right)}{\sin(x/2)},$$

where $n$ is any [nonnegative integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonnegative_integer). The kernel functions are periodic with period $2\pi$.

*Image: Dirichlet kernel anime.gif.* Plot restricted to one period $[-L,L],~L=\pi,~$ of the first few Dirichlet kernels showing their convergence to one of the [Dirac delta](wikipedia://en/37021) distributions of the [Dirac comb](wikipedia://en/1314272).

The importance of the Dirichlet kernel comes from its relation to [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038). The [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) of $D_n(x)$ with any function $f$ of period $2\pi$ is the $n$th-degree Fourier series approximation to $f$, i.e., we have

$$(D_n*f)(x)=\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(y)D_n(x-y)\,dy=2\pi\sum_{k=-n}^n \hat{f}(k)e^{ikx},$$

where

$$\widehat{f}(k)=\frac 1 {2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(y)e^{-iky}\,dy$$

is the $k$th Fourier coefficient of $f$. This implies that in order to study convergence of Fourier series it is enough to study properties of the Dirichlet kernel.

The Dirichlet kernel was named for [Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet](wikipedia://en/240002).

::: {#o3 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

In [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324), the Dirichlet kernel is often called the **periodic sinc function**:

$$P(\omega) = D_n(x)|_{x = 2 \pi \omega/\omega_0} = { \sin(\pi M \omega / \omega_0 ) \over \sin(\pi \omega / \omega_0 ) }$$

where $M = 2n + 1 \ge 3$ is an odd integer. In this form, $\omega$ is the [angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency), and $\omega_0$ is half of the periodicity in frequency. In this case, the periodic [sinc function](wikipedia://en/610583) in the [frequency domain](wikipedia://en/370346) can be thought of as the Fourier transform of a time bounded impulse train in the [time domain](wikipedia://en/173419):

$$p(t) = \sum_{k=-n}^n \delta(t - kT)$$

where $T = \frac{\pi}{\omega_0}$ is the time increment between each impulse and $M = 2n+1$ represents the number of impulses in the impulse train.

This kernel is also the Fourier Transform of a discrete time [moving average](wikipedia://en/990809) filter.

In [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics), the Dirichlet kernel is part of the mathematical description of the [diffraction](wikipedia://en/8603) pattern formed when monochromatic light passes through an aperture with [multiple narrow slits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_from_slits) of equal width and equally spaced along an axis perpendicular to the optical axis. In this case, $M$ is the number of slits.

:::

## $L^{1}$ norm of the kernel function {#norm_of_the_kernel_function}

Of particular importance is the fact that the [$L^1$](wikipedia://en/45194) norm of $D_n$ on $[0, 2\pi]$ diverges to infinity as $n\to\infty$. One can estimate that (using the [big omega notation](wikipedia://en/44578#The_Hardy–Littlewood_definition))

$$\| D_n \| _{L^1} = \Omega(\log n).$$

By using a Riemann-sum argument to estimate the contribution in the largest neighbourhood of zero in which $D_n$ is positive, and [Jensen's inequality](wikipedia://en/297811) for the remaining part, it is also possible to show that:

$$\| D_n \|_{L^1} \geq 4\operatorname{Si}(\pi)+\frac 8 \pi \log n$$

where $\operatorname{Si}(x)$ is the [sine integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_integral) $\int_0^x (\sin t)/t\, dt.$

This lack of uniform integrability is behind many divergence phenomena for the Fourier series. For example, together with the [uniform boundedness principle](wikipedia://en/247392), it can be used to show that the Fourier series of a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) may fail to converge pointwise, in rather dramatic fashion. See [convergence of Fourier series](wikipedia://en/927236) for further details.

A precise proof of the first result that $\| D_n \| _{L^1[0,2\pi]} = \Omega(\log n)$ is given by

$$\begin{aligned} \int_0^{2\pi} |D_n(x)| \, dx & \geq \int_0^\pi \frac{\left|\sin[(2n+1)x]\right|}{x} \, dx \\[5pt] & \geq \sum_{k=0}^{2n} \int_{k\pi}^{(k+1)\pi} \frac{\left|\sin s\right|}{s} \, ds \\[5pt] & \geq \left|\sum_{k=0}^{2n} \int_0^\pi \frac{\sin s}{(k+1)\pi} \, ds\right| \\[5pt] & = \frac{2}{\pi} H_{2n+1} \\[5pt] & \geq \frac{2}{\pi} \log(2n+1), \end{aligned}$$

where we have used the [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) identity that $2/x \leq 1 / \left|\sin(x/2)\right|$ and where $H_n$ are the first-order [harmonic numbers](wikipedia://en/214729).

## Relation to the periodic delta function {#Relation_to_the_periodic_delta_function}

The Dirichlet kernel is a [periodic function](wikipedia://en/76848) which becomes the [Dirac comb](wikipedia://en/1314272), i.e. the periodic delta function, in the limit

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} D_n(\omega) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=-n}^{n} e^{\pm i \omega k} = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} e^{\pm i 2 \pi k x} = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(x- k)=2\pi \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(\omega - 2\pi k) ~,$$

with the angular frequency $\omega=2 \pi x$.

This can be inferred from the autoconjugation property of the Dirichlet kernel under forward and inverse [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247):

$$\mathcal{F}\left[ D_n(2 \pi x) \right](\xi) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[ D_n(2 \pi x) \right](\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} D_n(2 \pi x) e^{\pm i 2\pi \xi x} \, dx = \sum_{k=-n}^{+n} \delta(\xi-k) \equiv \operatorname{comb}_n(\xi)$$

$$\mathcal{F}\left[ \operatorname{comb}_n \right](x) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[ \operatorname{comb}_n \right](x) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \operatorname{comb}_n(\xi) e^{ \pm i 2 \pi \xi x } \, d\xi = D_n(2 \pi x),$$

and $\operatorname{comb}_n(x)$ goes to the [Dirac comb](wikipedia://en/1314272) $\operatorname{\text{Ш}}$ of period $T=1$ as $n \rightarrow \infty$, which remains invariant under [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/1314272#Fourier_transform): $\mathcal{F}[\operatorname{\text{Ш}}]= \operatorname{\text{Ш}}$. Thus $D_n(2 \pi x)$ must also have converged to $\operatorname{\text{Ш}}$ as $n \rightarrow \infty$.

In a different vein, consider $\Delta(x)$ as the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) for convolution on functions of period $2\pi$. In other words, we have

$$f*( \Delta)=f$$

for every function $f$ of period 2$\pi$. The Fourier series representation of this "function" is

$$\Delta(x)\sim\sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty e^{ikx}= \left(1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^\infty \cos(kx)\right).$$

(This Fourier series converges to the function almost nowhere.) Therefore, the Dirichlet kernel, which is just the sequence of partial sums of this series, can be thought of as an *[approximate identity](wikipedia://en/949628)*. Abstractly speaking it is not however an approximate identity of *positive* elements (hence the failures in [pointwise convergence](wikipedia://en/340198) mentioned above).

## Proof of the trigonometric identity {#Proof_of_the_trigonometric_identity}

The [trigonometric identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_identity)

$$\sum_{k=-n}^n e^{ikx} = \frac{\sin((n+1/2)x)}{\sin(x/2)}$$

displayed at the top of this article may be established as follows. First recall that the sum of a finite [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630) is

$$\sum_{k=0}^n a r^k=a\frac{1-r^{n+1}}{1-r}.$$

In particular, we have

$$\sum_{k=-n}^n r^k=r^{-n}\cdot\frac{1-r^{2n+1}}{1-r}.$$

Multiply both the numerator and the denominator by $r^{-1/2}$, getting

$$\frac{r^{-n-1/2}}{r^{-1/2}}\cdot\frac{1-r^{2n+1}}{1-r} =\frac{r^{-n-1/2}-r^{n+1/2}}{r^{-1/2}-r^{1/2}}.$$

In the case $r = e^{ix}$ we have

$$\sum_{k=-n}^n e^{ikx} = \frac{e^{-(n+1/2)ix}-e^{(n+1/2)ix}}{e^{-ix/2}-e^{ix/2}} = \frac{-2i\sin((n+1/2)x)}{-2i\sin(x/2)} = \frac{\sin((n+1/2)x)}{\sin(x/2)}$$

as required.

::: {#o9 .proof cue=named-heading name="Alternative proof of the trigonometric identity"}

### Alternative proof of the trigonometric identity {#Alternative_proof_of_the_trigonometric_identity}

Start with the series

$$f(x) = 1 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^n\cos(kx).$$

Multiply both sides by $\sin(x/2)$ and use the trigonometric identity

$$\cos(a)\sin(b) = \frac{\sin(a + b) - \sin(a - b)} 2$$

to reduce the terms in the sum.

$$\sin(x/2)f(x) = \sin(x/2)+ \sum_{k=1}^n \left( \sin((k + \tfrac 1 2 )x)- \sin((k-\tfrac 1 2 )x) \right)$$

which [telescopes](wikipedia://en/508070) down to the result.

:::

## Variant of identity {#Variant_of_identity}

If the sum is only over non-negative integers (which may arise when computing a [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811) that is not centered), then using similar techniques we can show the following identity:

$$\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} e^{ikx} = e^{i(N-1)x/2}\frac{\sin(N \, x/2)}{\sin(x/2)}$$

Another variant is

$$D_n(x) - \cos (nx) = \frac{\sin\left(nx \right)}{\tan(\frac{x}{2})}$$

and this can be easily proved by using an identity $\sin (\alpha + \beta) = \sin (\alpha) \cos (\beta) + \cos (\alpha) \sin(\beta)$.[^o12]

:::
