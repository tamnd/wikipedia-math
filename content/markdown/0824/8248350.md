# Dendroid (topology)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dendroid"}

*Image: Dendrite\_julia.png.* A [dendrite](wikipedia://en/8248391) such as this [Julia set](wikipedia://en/56434) is a (locally connected) dendroid.

In mathematics, a **dendroid** is a type of [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), satisfying the properties that it is hereditarily [unicoherent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicoherent) (meaning that every [subcontinuum](wikipedia://en/24758520) of *X* is unicoherent), [arcwise connected](wikipedia://en/6233#Path_connectedness), and forms a [continuum](wikipedia://en/24758520).[^o2] The term dendroid was introduced by [Bronisław Knaster](wikipedia://en/1003125) lecturing at the [University of Wrocław](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wrocław),[^o3] although these spaces were studied earlier by [Karol Borsuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karol_Borsuk) and others.[^o4][^o5]

:::

(Borsuk 1954) proved that dendroids have the [fixed-point property](wikipedia://en/1011848): Every continuous function from a dendroid to itself has a fixed point.[^o4] (Cook 1970) proved that every dendroid is *tree-like*, meaning that it has arbitrarily fine open covers whose [nerve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nerve_of_a_covering) is a tree.[^o2][^o8] The more general question of whether every tree-like continuum has the fixed-point property, posed by (Bing 1951),[^o10] was solved in the negative by David P. Bellamy, who gave an example of a tree-like continuum without the fixed-point property. [^o11]

In Knaster's original publication on dendroids, in 1961, he posed the problem of characterizing the dendroids which can be embedded into the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410). This problem remains open.[^o3][^o12] Another problem posed in the same year by Knaster, on the existence of an uncountable collection of dendroids with the property that no dendroid in the collection has a continuous [surjection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjection) onto any other dendroid in the collection, was solved by (Minc 2010) and (Islas 2007), who gave an example of such a family.[^o15][^o16]

A locally connected dendroid is called a [dendrite](wikipedia://en/8248391). A cone over the [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172) (called a Cantor fan) is an example of a dendroid that is not a dendrite.[^o17]
