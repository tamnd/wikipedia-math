# Block graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Block graph"}

*Image: Block graph.svg.* A block graph

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a branch of combinatorial mathematics, a **block graph** or **clique tree**[^o2] is a type of [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) in which every [biconnected component](wikipedia://en/4842394) (block) is a [clique](wikipedia://en/524466).

:::

Block graphs are sometimes erroneously called Husimi trees (after [Kôdi Husimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kôdi_Husimi)),[^o7] but that name more properly refers to [cactus graphs](wikipedia://en/9944209), graphs in which every nontrivial biconnected component is a cycle.[^o3]

Block graphs may be characterized as the [intersection graphs](wikipedia://en/7726759) of the blocks of arbitrary undirected graphs.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Characterization"}

## Characterization {#Characterization}

Block graphs are exactly the graphs for which, for every four vertices $u$, $v$, $x$, and $y$, the largest two of the three distances $d(u,v) + d(x,y)$, $d(u,x) + d(v,y)$, and $d(u,y) + d(v,x)$ are always equal.[^o7][^o8]

They also have a [forbidden graph characterization](wikipedia://en/13547826) as the graphs that do not have the [diamond graph](wikipedia://en/20796238) or a cycle of four or more vertices as an [induced subgraph](wikipedia://en/1655188); that is, they are the diamond-free chordal graphs.[^o8] They are also the [Ptolemaic graphs](wikipedia://en/50730814) ([chordal](wikipedia://en/744165) [distance-hereditary graphs](wikipedia://en/17315337)) in which every two nodes at distance two from each other are connected by a unique [shortest path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortest_path),[^o7] and the chordal graphs in which every two maximal cliques have at most one vertex in common.[^o7]

A graph $G$ is a block graph [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the intersection of every two [connected](wikipedia://en/1614492) subsets of vertices of $G$ is empty or connected. Therefore, the connected subsets of vertices in a connected block graph form a [convex geometry](wikipedia://en/667063), a property that is not true of any graphs that are not block graphs.[^o9] Because of this property, in a connected block graph, every set of vertices has a unique minimal connected superset, its closure in the convex geometry. The connected block graphs are exactly the graphs in which there is a unique [induced path](wikipedia://en/7714430) connecting every pair of vertices.[^o2]

:::

## Related graph classes {#Related_graph_classes}

Block graphs are [chordal](wikipedia://en/744165), [distance-hereditary](wikipedia://en/17315337), and [geodetic](wikipedia://en/65318914). The distance-hereditary graphs are the graphs in which every two induced paths between the same two vertices have the same length, a weakening of the characterization of block graphs as having at most one induced path between every two vertices. Because both the chordal graphs and the distance-hereditary graphs are subclasses of the [perfect graphs](wikipedia://en/670531), block graphs are perfect.

Every [tree](wikipedia://en/48560), [cluster graph](wikipedia://en/50931136), or [windmill graph](wikipedia://en/24373456) is a block graph.

Every block graph has [boxicity](wikipedia://en/11167471) at most two.[^o11]

Block graphs are examples of pseudo-[median graphs](wikipedia://en/16970848): for every three vertices, either there exists a unique vertex that belongs to shortest paths between all three vertices, or there exists a unique triangle whose edges lie on these three shortest paths.[^o11]

The [line graphs](wikipedia://en/675231) of trees are exactly the block graphs in which every cut vertex is incident to at most two blocks, or equivalently the [claw-free](wikipedia://en/12415907) block graphs. Line graphs of trees have been used to find graphs with a given number of edges and vertices in which the largest induced subgraph that is a tree is as small as possible.[^o12]

The block graphs in which every block has size at most three are a special type of [cactus graph](wikipedia://en/9944209), a triangular cactus. The largest triangular cactus in any graph may be found in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time) using an algorithm for the [matroid parity problem](wikipedia://en/56069479). Since triangular cactus graphs are [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314), the largest triangular cactus can be used as an approximation to the largest planar subgraph, an important subproblem in [planarization](wikipedia://en/43170363). As an [approximation algorithm](wikipedia://en/563105), this method has [approximation ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approximation_ratio) 4/9, the best known for the maximum planar subgraph problem.[^o13]

## Block graphs of undirected graphs {#Block_graphs_of_undirected_graphs}

If *G* is any undirected graph, the block graph of *G*, denoted *B*(*G*), is the [intersection graph](wikipedia://en/7726759) of the blocks of *G*: *B*(*G*) has a vertex for every biconnected component of *G*, and two vertices of *B*(*G*) are adjacent if the corresponding two blocks meet at an articulation vertex. If *K*<sub>1</sub> denotes the graph with one vertex, then *B*(*K*<sub>1</sub>) is defined to be the [empty graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empty_graph). *B*(*G*) is necessarily a block graph: it has one biconnected component for each articulation vertex of *G*, and each biconnected component formed in this way must be a clique. Conversely, every block graph is the graph *B*(*G*) for some graph *G*.[^o4] If *G* is a tree, then *B*(*G*) coincides with the [line graph](wikipedia://en/675231) of *G*.

The graph *B*(*B*(*G*)) has one vertex for each articulation vertex of *G*; two vertices are adjacent in *B*(*B*(*G*)) if they belong to the same block in *G*.[^o4]
