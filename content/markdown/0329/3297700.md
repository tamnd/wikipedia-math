# Cross-sectional data

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cross-sectional data"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), **cross-sectional data** is a type of [data](wikipedia://en/8495) collected by observing many subjects (such as individuals, firms, countries, or regions) at a single point or period of time. [Analysis of cross-sectional data](wikipedia://en/1557627) usually consists of comparing the differences among selected subjects, typically with no regard to differences in time.

:::

For example, if we want to measure current obesity levels in a population, we could draw a sample of 1,000 people randomly from that population (also known as a cross section of that population), measure their weight and height, and calculate what percentage of that sample is categorized as obese. This cross-sectional sample provides us with a snapshot of that population, at that one point in time. Note that we do not know based on one cross-sectional sample if obesity is increasing or decreasing; we can only describe the current proportion.

Cross-sectional data differs from *[time series](wikipedia://en/406624)* data, in which the same small-scale or [aggregate](wikipedia://en/6937928) entity is observed at various points in time. Another type of data, *[panel data](wikipedia://en/2499813)* (or *longitudinal data*), combines both cross-sectional and time series data aspects and looks at how the subjects (firms, individuals, etc.) change over a time series. Panel data deals with the observations on the same subjects in different times. [Panel analysis](wikipedia://en/2369609) uses panel data to examine changes in variables over time and its differences in variables between selected subjects.

Variants include *pooled cross-sectional data*, which deals with the observations on the same subjects in different times. In a *rolling cross-section*, both the presence of an individual in the sample and the time at which the individual is included in the sample are determined randomly. For example, a political poll may decide to interview 1000 individuals. It first selects these individuals randomly from the entire population. It then assigns a random date to each individual. This is the random date that the individual will be interviewed, and thus included in the survey.[^o2]

Cross-sectional data can be used in [cross-sectional regression](wikipedia://en/2542373), which is [regression analysis](wikipedia://en/826997) of cross-sectional data. For example, the [consumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumption_%28economics%29) expenditures of various individuals in a fixed month could be regressed on their incomes, accumulated wealth levels, and their various [demographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics) features to determine how differences in those features lead to differences in consumers’ behavior.
