# Dehn plane

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dehn plane"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), [Max Dehn](wikipedia://en/570602) introduced two examples of planes, a **semi-Euclidean geometry** and a **non-Legendrian geometry**, that have infinitely many lines parallel to a given one that pass through a given point, but where the sum of the angles of a triangle is at least $\pi$. A similar phenomenon occurs in [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), except that the sum of the angles of a triangle is less than $\pi$. Dehn's examples use a non-Archimedean field, so that the [Archimedean axiom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedean_axiom) is violated. They were introduced by (txt) and discussed by (Hilbert 1902, pp. 127–130, or pp. 42–43 in some later editions).

:::

## Dehn's non-archimedean field Ω(*t*) {#Dehn's_non-archimedean_field_Ω(t)}

To construct his geometries, Dehn used a [non-Archimedean](wikipedia://en/264158) ordered [Pythagorean field](wikipedia://en/31032774) Ω(*t*), a [Pythagorean closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_closure) of the field of rational functions **R**(*t*), consisting of the smallest field of real-valued functions on the real line containing the real constants, the identity function *t* (taking any real number to itself) and closed under the operation $\omega \mapsto \sqrt{1+\omega^2}$. The field Ω(*t*) is ordered by putting *x* > *y* if the function *x* is larger than *y* for sufficiently large reals. An element *x* of Ω(*t*) is called **finite** if *m* \< *x* \< *n* for some integers *m*, *n*, and is called **infinite** otherwise.

## Dehn's semi-Euclidean geometry {#Dehn's_semi-Euclidean_geometry}

The set of all pairs (*x*, *y*), where *x* and *y* are any (possibly infinite) elements of the field Ω(*t*), and with the usual [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29)

$$\|(x,y)\| = \sqrt{x^2+y^2},$$

which takes values in Ω(*t*), gives a model of [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417). The [parallel postulate](wikipedia://en/33731493) is true in this model, but if the deviation from the perpendicular is infinitesimal (meaning smaller than any positive rational number), the intersecting lines intersect at a point that is not in the finite part of the plane. Hence, if the model is restricted to the finite part of the plane (points (*x*,*y*) with *x* and *y* finite), a geometry is obtained in which the parallel postulate fails but the sum of the angles of a triangle is $\pi$. This is Dehn's semi-Euclidean geometry. It is discussed in (Rucker 1982, pp. 91–2).

## Dehn's non-Legendrian geometry {#Dehn's_non-Legendrian_geometry}

In the same paper, Dehn also constructed an example of a non-Legendrian geometry where there are infinitely many lines through a point not meeting another line, but the sum of the angles in a triangle exceeds $\pi$. Riemann's [elliptic geometry](wikipedia://en/236020) over Ω(*t*) consists of the projective plane over Ω(*t*), which can be identified with the affine plane of points (*x*:*y*:1) together with the "line at infinity", and has the property that the sum of the angles of any triangle is greater than $\pi$ The non-Legendrian geometry consists of the points (*x*:*y*:1) of this affine subspace such that *tx* and *ty* are finite (where as above *t* is the element of Ω(*t*) represented by the [identity function](wikipedia://en/15069)). [Legendre's theorem](wikipedia://en/23413137) states that the sum of the angles of a triangle is at most $\pi$, but assumes Archimedes's axiom, and Dehn's example shows that Legendre's theorem need not hold if Archimedes' axiom is dropped.
