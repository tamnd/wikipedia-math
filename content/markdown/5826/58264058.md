# Measurable acting group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Measurable acting group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **measurable acting group** is a special group that [acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) on some space in a way that is compatible with structures of [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory). Measurable acting groups are found in the intersection of measure theory and [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), two sub-disciplines of mathematics. Measurable acting groups are the basis for the study of invariant measures in abstract settings, most famously the [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645), and the study of [stationary random measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary_random_measure).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $(G, \mathcal G, \circ)$ be a [measurable group](wikipedia://en/57900656), where $\mathcal G$ denotes the [$\sigma$-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-algebra) on $G$ and $\circ$ the [group law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_law). Let further $(S, \mathcal S)$ be a [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333) and let $\mathcal A \otimes \mathcal B$ be the [product $\sigma$-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_sigma-algebra) of the $\sigma$-algebras $\mathcal A$ and $\mathcal B$.

Let $G$ [act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) on $S$ with group action

$$\Phi \colon G \times S \to S$$

If $\Phi$ is a [measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055) from $\mathcal G \otimes \mathcal S$ to $\mathcal S$, then it is called a **measurable group action**. In this case, the group $G$ is said to act measurably on $S$.

:::

## Example: Measurable groups as measurable acting groups {#Example:_Measurable_groups_as_measurable_acting_groups}

One special case of measurable acting groups are measurable groups themselves. If $S=G$, and the group action is the group law, then a measurable group is a group $G$, acting measurably on $G$.
