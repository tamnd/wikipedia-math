# Subring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Subring"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **subring** of a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$ is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $R$ that is itself a ring when [binary operations](wikipedia://en/3948) of addition and multiplication on *R* are restricted to the subset, and that shares the same [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity) as $R$.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A subring of a ring $(R, +, *, 0, 1)$ is a subset $S$ of $R$ that preserves the structure of the ring, i.e. a ring $(S, +, *, 0, 1)$ with $S \subseteq R$. Equivalently, it is both a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of $(R, +, 0)$ and a [submonoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submonoid) of $(R, *, 1)$.

Equivalently, $S$ is a subring [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it contains the multiplicative identity of $R$, and is [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under multiplication and subtraction. This is sometimes known as the *subring test*.[^o5]

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Variations"}

### Variations {#Variations}

Some mathematicians define rings without requiring the existence of a multiplicative identity (see *Ring (mathematics)*). In this case, a subring of $R$ is a subset of $R$ that is a ring for the operations of $R$ (this does imply it contains the additive identity of $R$). This alternate definition gives a strictly weaker condition, even for rings that do have a multiplicative identity, in that all [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) become subrings, and they may have a multiplicative identity that differs from the one of $R$. With the definition requiring a multiplicative identity, which is used in the rest of this article, the only ideal of $R$ that is a subring of $R$ is $R$ itself.

:::

:::

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) $\mathbb{Z}$ is a subring of both the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) and the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $\mathbb{Z}[X]$.[^o5]
- $\mathbb{Z}$ and its quotients $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ have no subrings (with multiplicative identity) other than the full ring.[^o5]
- Every ring has a unique smallest subring, isomorphic to some ring $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ with *n* a nonnegative integer (see *[Characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621)*). The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ correspond to *n* = 0 in this statement, since $\mathbb{Z}$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/0\mathbb{Z}$.[^o10]
- The [center of a ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_a_ring) $R$ is a subring of $R$, and $R$ is an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) over its center.

:::

::: {#o12 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Generating set of a ring"}

## Subring generated by a set {#Subring_generated_by_a_set}

A special kind of subring of a ring $R$ is the subring **generated by** a subset $X$, which is defined as the intersection of all subrings of $R$ containing $X$.[^o13] The subring generated by $X$ is also the set of all [linear combinations](wikipedia://en/55632) with integer coefficients of products of elements of $X$, including the additive identity ("empty combination") and multiplicative identity ("empty product").[^o14]

Any intersection of subrings of $R$ is itself a subring of $R$; therefore, the subring generated by $X$ (denoted here as $S$) is indeed a subring of $R$. This subring $S$ is the smallest subring of $R$ containing $X$; that is, if $T$ is any other subring of $R$ containing $X$, then $S \subseteq T$.

Since $R$ itself is a subring of $R$, if $R$ is generated by $X$, it is said that the ring $R$ is *generated by* $X$.

:::

::: {#o16 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Ring extensions"}

## Ring extension {#Ring_extension}

Subrings generalize some aspects of [field extensions](wikipedia://en/11634). If $S$ is a subring of a ring $R$, then equivalently $R$ is said to be a **ring extension**[^o17] of $S$.

### Adjoining {#Adjoining}

If $A$ is a ring and $T$ is a subring of $A$ generated by $R \cup S$, where $R$ is a subring, then $T$ is a ring extension and is said to be $S$ *adjoined to* $R$, denoted $R[S]$. Individual elements can also be adjoined to a subring, denoted $R[a_{1}, a_{2}, ..., a_{n}]$.[^o19][^o13]

For example, the ring of [Gaussian integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integers) $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a subring of $\mathbb{C}$ generated by $\mathbb{Z} \cup \{i\}$, and thus is the adjunction of the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358) $i$ to $\mathbb{Z}$.[^o13]

### Prime subring {#Prime_subring}

The intersection of all subrings of a ring $R$ is a subring that may be called the *prime subring* of $R$ by analogy with [prime fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_field).

The prime subring of a ring $R$ is a subring of the center of $R$, which is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_isomorphism) either to the ring $\mathbb{Z}$ of the [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers) or to the ring of the [integers modulo $n$](wikipedia://en/20087), where $n$ is the smallest positive integer such that the sum of $n$ copies of $1$ equals $0$.

:::
