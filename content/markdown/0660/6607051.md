# Strictly positive measure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Strictly positive measure"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), strict positivity is a concept in [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory). Intuitively, a **strictly positive measure** is one that is "nowhere zero", or that is zero "only on points".[^o2][^o3]

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $(X, T)$ be a [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) and let $\Sigma$ be a [$\sigma$-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_algebra) on $X$ that contains the topology $T$ (so that every [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) is a [measurable set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurable_set), and $\Sigma$ is at least as fine as the [Borel $\sigma$-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_sigma_algebra) on $X$). Then a measure $\mu$ on $(X, \Sigma)$ is called **strictly positive** if every non-empty open subset of $X$ has strictly positive measure.

More concisely, $\mu$ is strictly positive [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) for all $U \in T$ such that $U \neq \varnothing, \mu (U) > 0.$

:::

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- [Counting measure](wikipedia://en/191786) on any set $X$ (with any topology) is strictly positive.
- [Dirac measure](wikipedia://en/1019002) is usually not strictly positive unless the topology $T$ is particularly "coarse" (contains "few" sets). For example, $\delta_0$ on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) $\mathbb{R}$ with its usual Borel topology and $\sigma$-algebra is not strictly positive; however, if $\mathbb{R}$ is equipped with the trivial topology $T = \{\varnothing, \mathbb{R}\},$ then $\delta_0$ is strictly positive. This example illustrates the importance of the topology in determining strict positivity.
- [Gaussian measure](wikipedia://en/6572909) on [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$ (with its Borel topology and $\sigma$-algebra) is strictly positive.
  - [Wiener measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiener_measure) on the space of continuous paths in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a strictly positive measure — Wiener measure is an example of a Gaussian measure on an infinite-dimensional space.
- [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on $\mathbb{R}^n$ (with its Borel topology and $\sigma$-algebra) is strictly positive.
- The [trivial measure](wikipedia://en/10122069) is never strictly positive, regardless of the space $X$ or the topology used, except when $X$ is empty.

:::

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- If $\mu$ and $\nu$ are two measures on a measurable topological space $(X, \Sigma),$ with $\mu$ strictly positive and also [absolutely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_continuous) with respect to $\nu,$ then $\nu$ is strictly positive as well. The proof is simple: let $U \subseteq X$ be an arbitrary open set; since $\mu$ is strictly positive, $\mu(U) > 0;$ by absolute continuity, $\nu(U) > 0$ as well.
- Hence, strict positivity is an [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) with respect to [equivalence of measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalence_of_measures).
- Any [uniformly distributed measure](wikipedia://en/22906120) on a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) is strictly positive. Because if there is an nonempty open set with zero measure, then the measure of some open balls will be zero, which contradicts the definition of uniformly distributed**.**

:::
