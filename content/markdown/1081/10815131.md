# Trace theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Trace theory"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), **trace theory** aims to provide a concrete mathematical underpinning for the study of [concurrent computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concurrent_computation) and [process calculi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process_calculi). The underpinning is provided by an [algebraic](wikipedia://en/19616384) definition of the [free partially commutative monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_partially_commutative_monoid) or [trace monoid](wikipedia://en/10739141), or equivalently, the [history monoid](wikipedia://en/10833335), which provides a concrete algebraic foundation, analogous to the way that the [free monoid](wikipedia://en/523166) provides the underpinning for [formal languages](wikipedia://en/10939).

:::

The power of trace theory stems from the fact that the algebra of [dependency graphs](wikipedia://en/5021549) (such as [Petri nets](wikipedia://en/225192)) is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to that of trace monoids, and thus, one can apply both algebraic [formal language](wikipedia://en/10939) tools, as well as tools from [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401).

While the trace monoid had been studied by [Pierre Cartier](wikipedia://en/1470549) and [Dominique Foata](wikipedia://en/26068475) for its [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) in the 1960s, trace theory was first formulated by Antoni Mazurkiewicz in the 1970s, in an attempt to evade some of the problems in the theory of concurrent computation, including the problems of interleaving and non-deterministic choice with regard to refinement in process calculi.
