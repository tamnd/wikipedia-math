# Levi's lemma

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Levi's lemma"}

*Image: Levi's lemma.svg.* The *uw* = *x* and v = *wy* case of Levi's lemma

In [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392) and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially in the area of [combinatorics on words](wikipedia://en/20913490), the **Levi lemma** states that, for all [strings](wikipedia://en/27701) *u*, *v*, *x* and *y*, if *uv* = *xy*, then there exists a string *w* such that either

:::

*uw = x* and *v* = *wy* (if |*u*| ≤ |*x*|)

or

*u* = *xw* and *wv* = *y* (if |*u*| ≥ |*x*|)

That is, there is a string *w* that is "in the middle", and can be grouped to one side or the other. Levi's lemma is named after [Friedrich Wilhelm Levi](wikipedia://en/3752306), who published it in 1944.[^o2]

::: {#o4 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Levi's lemma can be applied repeatedly in order to solve [word equations](wikipedia://en/78148249); in this context it is sometimes called the **Nielsen transformation** by analogy with the [Nielsen transformation for groups](wikipedia://en/18400182). For example, starting with an equation *xα* = *yβ* where *x* and *y* are the unknowns, we can transform it (assuming *\|x| ≥ |y|*, so there exists *t* such that *x*=*yt*) to *ytα* = *yβ*, thus to *tα* = *β*. This approach results in a graph of substitutions generated by repeatedly applying Levi's lemma. If each unknown appears at most twice, then a word equation is called quadratic; in a quadratic word equation the graph obtained by repeatedly applying Levi's lemma is finite, so it is [decidable](wikipedia://en/8336) if a quadratic word equation [has a solution](wikipedia://en/23401166).[^o5] A more general method for solving word equations is [Makanin's algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makanin's_algorithm).[^o6][^o7]

:::

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The above is known as the **Levi lemma for strings**; the lemma can occur in a more general form in [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401) and in [monoid theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoid_theory); for example, there is a more general Levi lemma for [traces](wikipedia://en/10739141) originally due to Christine Duboc.[^o10] Several proofs of Levi's Lemma for traces can be found in *The Book of Traces*.[^o11]

A monoid in which Levi's lemma holds is said to have the **equidivisibility property**.[^o12] The [free monoid](wikipedia://en/523166) of strings and string concatenation has this property (by Levi's lemma for strings), but by itself equidivisibility is not enough to guarantee that a monoid is free. However an equidivisible monoid *M* is free if additionally there exists a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) *f* from *M* to the [monoid of natural numbers](wikipedia://en/18071162) (free monoid on one generator) with the property that the [preimage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preimage) of 0 contains only the identity element of *M*, i.e. $f^{-1}(0) = \{1_M\}$. (Note that *f* simply being a homomorphism does not guarantee this latter property, as there could be multiple elements of *M* mapped to 0.)[^o13] A monoid for which such a homomorphism exists is also called *graded* (and the *f* is called a gradation).[^o14]
