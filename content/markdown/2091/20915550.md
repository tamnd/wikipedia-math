# Brian Snow

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Brian Snow"}

**Brian Snow** (September 5, 1943 – December 4, 2022) served in the U.S. [National Security Agency](wikipedia://en/21939) from 1971 to 2006, including a six-year term as Technical Director of the Information Assurance Directorate (IAD), which is the defensive arm of the NSA, charged with protecting U.S. [information security](wikipedia://en/15036). During his time in research management positions in the NSA, he played a key role in promoting a more open and collaborative relationship between the Agency and the broader community of security professionals.

:::

## Career {#Career}

Snow was born in Houston Texas, and received a master's degree in mathematics from the [University of Colorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Colorado) before beginning his teaching career. [^o4]

In his early career Snow was a mathematics professor at [Ohio University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_University), where he helped develop the college's [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) department. In 1971 he started working at the NSA. During the 1970s and 1980s he designed cryptographic components and secure systems. Several cryptographic systems employed by the U.S. government and military have used his algorithms for such purposes as nuclear command and control, tactical voice communications, and [network security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_security).[^o5] As a technical person with high managerial responsibilities,

...he advised senior management on the strengths and shortfalls of the technology they were using, as well as identifying emerging technologies, spotting technical gaps or risks in new systems and services, and identifying old products for withdrawal from service.[^o9]

## ECC and AES {#ECC_and_AES}

[Neal Koblitz](wikipedia://en/1587628) and [Alfred Menezes](wikipedia://en/4356319) have written that Snow was a strong supporter of the transition from [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28cryptosystem%29) to [Elliptic Curve Cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_Curve_Cryptography) (ECC) as a public key cryptographic technology:

...ECC started to get strong support from the NSA's Information Assurance Directorate (IAD) during the time when Snow was the technical director and Mike Jacobs was the head of IAD. There has never been any evidence -- in the reports on the Snowden documents or anywhere else -- of any actions by Snow and Jacobs or their researchers that would weaken or undermine cryptographic standards. On the contrary, during that period IAD cooperated with other sectors in pushing for strong security. This was consistent with IAD's mission as the defensive arm of the NSA. (The offensive arm, called SIGINT, is another matter.)[^o7]

Cybersecurity policy expert [Susan Landau](wikipedia://en/19313149) attributes the NSA's harmonious collaboration with industry and academia in the selection of the [Advanced Encryption Standard](wikipedia://en/1260) (AES) in 2000 — and the Agency's support for the choice of a strong encryption algorithm designed by Europeans rather than by Americans — in part to Snow, who represented the NSA as cochairman of the Technical Working Group for the AES competition.[^o8]

After the [terrorist attacks of 11 September 2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_11_attacks), the NSA believed that it had public support for a dramatic expansion of its surveillance activities.[^o9] According to Koblitz and Menezes, the period when the NSA was a trusted partner with academia and industry in the development of cryptographic standards came to an end when, as part of the change in the NSA in the post-September 11 era, Snow was replaced as Technical Director and could no longer effectively oppose proposed actions by the offensive arm of the NSA:

In 2002 Brian Snow was moved from the technical directorship of IAD to a different position within the NSA that had high status but little influence, particularly with regard to actions that were being proposed by SIGINT; Mike Jacobs retired from the NSA the same year.[^o7]p. ## Code of ethics {#Code_of_ethics}

In Snow's last years at the NSA before his retirement in 2006, along with Clinton Brooks he led a group that drew up a draft ethics code for intelligence officers.[^o11]

After retiring from the NSA, Snow worked as a security and ethics consultant. He was a member of the U.S. [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences) Committee on Future Research Goals and Directions for Foundational Science in Cybersecurity. He also served on the advisory board of The [Calyx Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calyx_Institute)[^o5]

## Recognition {#Recognition}

In 2019 Brian Snow was inducted into the [National Cyber Security Hall of Fame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Cyber_Security_Hall_of_Fame).[^o13]

Snow died on December 4, 2022.[^o4] He was survived by his wife of 55 years, Myra, three daughters, and eight grandchildren.[^o4]
