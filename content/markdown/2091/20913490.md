# Combinatorics on words

*Image: Morse-Thue sequence.gif.* Construction of a [Thue–Morse infinite word](wikipedia://en/491903)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Combinatorics on words"}

**Combinatorics on words** is a subfield of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) focused on the study of [words](wikipedia://en/12792326) and [formal languages](wikipedia://en/10939), which are (usually finite) strings of symbols from an arbitrary set (an "alphabet") and sets of words, respectively. The field has implications in various areas of mathematical study, notably [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) and [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392). Combinatorics on words became useful in the study of [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) and [coding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_programming).

:::

## History {#History}

Combinatorics on words is considered a relatively young area of mathematics. The subject originated with foundational work published by [Axel Thue](wikipedia://en/635533) on [square-free words](wikipedia://en/4088382), infinite words, and repetitions in the early 1900s and enjoyed some major results by [Emil Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Post) in the middle of the century. However, most research in the field has occurred since the 1980s.[^o3]

The international conference WORDS, which has occurred biannually since 1997, focuses on combinatorics on words.[^o3]

## Definition {#Definition}

Combinatorics is an area of [discrete mathematics](wikipedia://en/8492). Discrete mathematics is the study of countable structures. These objects have a definite beginning and end. The study of enumerable objects is the opposite of disciplines such as [analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), where [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) and [infinite](wikipedia://en/142679) structures are studied. Combinatorics studies how to count these objects using various representations. Combinatorics on words is a recent development in this field that focuses on the study of words and formal languages. A formal language is any set of symbols and combinations of symbols that people use to communicate information.[^o6]

Some terminology relevant to the study of words should first be explained. First and foremost, a word is basically a sequence of symbols, or letters, in a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742).[^o6] One of these sets is known by the general public as the [alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabet). For example, the word "encyclopedia" is a sequence of symbols in the [English alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_alphabet), a finite set of twenty-six letters. Since a word can be described as a sequence, other basic mathematical descriptions can be applied. The alphabet is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691), so as one would expect, the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631). In other words, there exists a [unique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unique_%28mathematics%29) word of length zero. The length of the word $w$ is defined by the number of symbols that make up the sequence, and is denoted by $|w|$.[^o6] Again looking at the example "encyclopedia", $|w| = 12$, since encyclopedia has twelve letters. The idea of [factoring](wikipedia://en/82341) of large numbers can be applied to words, where a factor of a word is a block of consecutive symbols.[^o6] Thus, "cyclop" is a factor of "encyclopedia".

In addition to examining sequences in themselves, another area to consider of combinatorics on words is how they can be represented visually. In mathematics various structures are used to encode data. A common structure used in combinatorics is the [tree structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_structure). A tree structure is a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) where the [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) are connected by one line, called a path or [edge](wikipedia://en/325802#Basics). Trees may not contain [cycles](wikipedia://en/168609), and may or may not be complete. It is possible to [encode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Character_encoding) a word, since a word is constructed by symbols, and encode the data by using a tree.[^o6] This gives a visual representation of the object.

## Major contributions {#Major_contributions}

The first books on combinatorics on words that summarize the origins of the subject were written by a group of mathematicians that collectively went by the name of [M. Lothaire](wikipedia://en/32232474). Their first book was published in 1983, when combinatorics on words became more widespread.[^o6]

## Patterns {#Patterns}

### Patterns within words {#Patterns_within_words}

A main contributor to the development of combinatorics on words was [Axel Thue](wikipedia://en/635533) (1863–1922); he researched repetition. Thue's main contribution was the proof of the existence of infinite [square-free words](wikipedia://en/4088382). Square-free words do not have adjacent repeated factors.[^o6] To clarify, "dining" is not square-free since "in" is repeated consecutively, while "servers" is square-free, its two "er" factors not being adjacent. Thue proves his conjecture on the existence of infinite square-free words by using [substitutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_substitution). A substitution is a way to take a symbol and replace it with a word. He uses this technique to describe his other contribution, the [Thue–Morse sequence](wikipedia://en/491903), or Thue–Morse word.[^o6]

Thue wrote two papers on square-free words, the second of which was on the Thue–Morse word. [Marston Morse](wikipedia://en/856523) is included in the name because he discovered the same result as Thue did, yet they worked independently. Thue also proved the existence of an overlap-free word. An overlap-free word is when, for two symbols $x$ and $y$, the pattern $xyxyx$ does not exist within the word. He continues in his second paper to prove a relationship between infinite overlap-free words and square-free words. He takes overlap-free words that are created using two different letters, and demonstrates how they can be transformed into square-free words of three letters using substitution.[^o6]

As was previously described, words are studied by examining the sequences made by the symbols. Patterns are found, and they can be described mathematically. Patterns can be either avoidable patterns, or unavoidable. A significant contributor to the work of [unavoidable patterns](wikipedia://en/37477193), or regularities, was [Frank Ramsey](wikipedia://en/238285) in 1930. His important theorem states that for integers $k$, $m \geq 2$, there exists a least positive integer $R(k,m)$ such that despite how a complete graph is colored with two colors, there will always exist a solid color subgraph of each color.[^o6]

Other contributors to the study of unavoidable patterns include [van der Waerden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_der_Waerden). His theorem states that if the positive integers are partitioned into $k$ classes, then there exists a class $c$ such that $c$ contains an arithmetic progression of some unknown length. An [arithmetic progression](wikipedia://en/168389) is a sequence of numbers in which the difference between adjacent numbers remains constant.[^o6]

When examining unavoidable patterns [sesquipowers](wikipedia://en/37476702) are also studied. For some patterns $x$,$y$,$z$, a sesquipower is of the form $x$, $xyx$, $xyxzxyx$, $\ldots~$. This is another pattern such as square-free, or unavoidable patterns. Coudrain and [Schützenberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schützenberger) mainly studied these sesquipowers for [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) applications. In addition, [Zimin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A.I._Zimin) proved that sesquipowers are all unavoidable. Whether the entire pattern shows up, or only some piece of the sesquipower shows up repetitively, it is not possible to avoid it.[^o6]

### Patterns within alphabets {#Patterns_within_alphabets}

[Necklaces](wikipedia://en/6226587) are constructed from words of circular sequences. They are most frequently used in [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music) and [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy). Flye Sainte-Marie in 1894 proved there are $2^{2^{n-1}-n}$ [binary](wikipedia://en/238686) [de Bruijn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Bruijn) necklaces of length $2^n$. A de Bruijn necklace contains factors made of words of length n over a certain number of letters. The words appear only once in the necklace.[^o6]

In 1874, [Baudot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Émile_Baudot) developed the code that would eventually take the place of [Morse code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_code) by applying the theory of binary de Bruijn necklaces. The problem continued from Sainte-Marie to [Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._H._Martin) in 1934, who began looking at algorithms to make words of the de Bruijn structure. It was then worked on by [Klaas Posthumus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaas_Posthumus) in 1943.[^o6]

## Language hierarchy {#Language_hierarchy}

Possibly the most applied result in combinatorics on words is the [Chomsky hierarchy](wikipedia://en/6011), developed by [Noam Chomsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noam_Chomsky). He studied formal language in the 1950s.[^o12] His way of looking at language simplified the subject. He disregards the actual meaning of the word, does not consider certain factors such as frequency and context, and applies patterns of short terms to all length terms. The basic idea of Chomsky's work is to divide language into four levels, or the language [hierarchy](wikipedia://en/13998). The four levels are: [regular](wikipedia://en/25723), [context-free](wikipedia://en/6867), [context-sensitive](wikipedia://en/6212), and [computably enumerable](wikipedia://en/54789) or unrestricted.[^o12] Regular is the least complex while computably enumerable is the most complex. While his work grew out of combinatorics on words, it drastically affected other disciplines, especially [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science).[^o13]

## Word types {#Word_types}

### Sturmian words {#Sturmian_words}

[Sturmian words](wikipedia://en/1701956), created by François Sturm, have roots in combinatorics on words. There exist several equivalent definitions of Sturmian words. For example, an infinite word is Sturmian if and only if it has $n+1$ distinct factors of length $n$ , for every non-negative integer $n$.[^o6]

### Lyndon word {#Lyndon_word}

A [Lyndon word](wikipedia://en/3919355) is a word over a given alphabet that is written in its simplest and most ordered form out of its respective [conjugacy class](wikipedia://en/49176). Lyndon words are important because for any given Lyndon word $x$, there exists Lyndon words $y$ and $z$, with $y<z$, $x=yz$. Further, there exists a [theorem by Chen, Fox, and Lyndon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen–Fox–Lyndon_theorem), that states any word has a unique factorization of Lyndon words, where the factorization words are [non-increasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-increasing). Due to this property, Lyndon words are used to study [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), specifically [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890). They form the basis for the idea of [commutators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutators).[^o6]

## Visual representation {#Visual_representation}

[Cobham](wikipedia://en/68162815) contributed work relating [Eugène Prouhet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugène_Prouhet)'s work with [finite automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_automata). A mathematical graph is made of edges and [nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Node_%28graph_theory%29). With finite automata, the edges are labeled with a letter in an alphabet. To use the graph, one starts at a node and travels along the edges to reach a final node. The path taken along the graph forms the word. It is a finite graph because there are a countable number of nodes and edges, and only one path connects two [distinct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distinct_%28mathematics%29) nodes.[^o6]

[Gauss codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss_code), created by [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) in 1838, are developed from graphs. Specifically, a [closed curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_curve) on a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) is needed. If the curve only crosses over itself a finite number of times, then one labels the intersections with a letter from the alphabet used. Traveling along the curve, the word is determined by recording each letter as an intersection is passed. Gauss noticed that the distance between when the same symbol shows up in a word is an [even integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_integer).[^o6]

## Group theory {#Group_theory}

[Walther Franz Anton von Dyck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walther_Franz_Anton_von_Dyck) began the work of combinatorics on words in group theory by his published work in 1882 and 1883. He began by using words as group elements. [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange) also contributed in 1771 with his work on [permutation groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutation_groups).[^o6]

One aspect of combinatorics on words studied in group theory is reduced words. A group is constructed with words on some alphabet including [generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generator_%28Mathematics%29) and [inverse elements](wikipedia://en/45199), excluding factors that appear of the form aā or āa, for some a in the alphabet. [Reduced words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_words) are formed when the factors aā, āa are used to cancel out elements until a unique word is reached.[^o6]

[Nielsen transformations](wikipedia://en/18400182) were also developed. For a set of elements of a [free group](wikipedia://en/59735), a Nielsen transformation is achieved by three transformations; replacing an element with its inverse, replacing an element with the [product](wikipedia://en/24977) of itself and another element, and eliminating any element equal to 1. By applying these transformations Nielsen reduced sets are formed. A reduced set means no element can be multiplied by other elements to cancel out completely. There are also connections with Nielsen transformations with Sturmian words.[^o6]

### Considered problems {#Considered_problems}

One problem considered in the study of combinatorics on words in group theory is the following: for two elements $x$, $y$ of a [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799), does $x = y$ [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) the defining [relations](wikipedia://en/22517558) of $x$ and $y$. [Post](wikipedia://en/362382) and [Markov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Markov,_Jr.) studied this problem and determined it [undecidable](wikipedia://en/15631055), meaning that there is no possible algorithm that can answer the question in all cases (because any such algorithm could be encoded into a word problem which that algorithm could not solve).[^o6]

The [Burnside](wikipedia://en/251883) question was proved using the existence of an infinite [cube-free word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube-free_word). This question asks if a group is finite if the group has a definite number of generators and meets the criteria $x^n=1$, for $x$ in the group.[^o6]

Many word problems are undecidable based on the [Post correspondence problem](wikipedia://en/64685). Any two [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13564#Formal_language_theory) $g,h$ with a common domain and a common codomain form an instance of the Post correspondence problem, which asks whether there exists a word $w$ in the domain such that $g(w)=h(w)$. Post proved that this problem is undecidable; consequently, any word problem that has a reduction from this basic problem is likewise undecidable.[^o6]

## Other applications {#Other_applications}

Combinatorics on words have applications on [equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations). Makanin proved that it is possible to find a solution for a finite system of equations, when the equations are constructed from words.[^o6]
