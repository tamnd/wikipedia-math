# Word sense

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Word sense"}

In [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics), a **word sense** is one of the [meanings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meaning_%28linguistics%29) of a [word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word). For example, the word "play" may have over 50 senses in a [dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictionary), each with a distinct meaning depending on the [context](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Context_%28language_use%29) of the word's [usage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usage) in a [sentence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence_%28linguistics%29), as follows:

:::

We went to see the **play** *Romeo and Juliet* at the theater. The coach devised a great **play** that put the visiting team on the defensive. The children went out to **play** in the park.

In each sentence, [different collocates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Hoey_%28linguist%29#The_Lexical_Priming_theory) of "play" indicates its different meaning.

[Word-sense disambiguation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word-sense_disambiguation) is a process used by people and [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer) to determine[^o2][^o3] the intended [meaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meaning_%28linguistics%29) of a word. It relies on context, such as adjacent words, phrases, purpose, the known or probable purpose, and [register](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Register_%28sociolinguistics%29) of the conversation, or document, and orientation, to narrow down the possible senses to the most likely one. This process is [context-sensitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Context_%28language_use%29).

Advanced [semantic analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_analysis_%28linguistics%29) has led to a distinction between word senses that correspond to either a [seme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seme_%28semantics%29) (the smallest unit of [meaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meaning_%28linguistics%29)) or a [sememe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sememe) (larger unit of meaning). [Polysemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysemy) refers to the property of a word or phrase having multiple semes or sememes, resulting in multiple senses.

## Relations between senses {#Relations_between_senses}

In language, words often have related senses within a [semantic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_field), with one sense being broader and another narrower. This pattern is common in technical [jargon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jargon), where a word may have a narrower sense for a [specialized audience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Target_audience) compared to a [general audience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public). For example, in linguistics, "[orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthography)" will often be [glossed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloss_%28annotation%29) for [spelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spelling), [casing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_case), [spacing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_%28punctuation%29), [hyphenation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyphen), and [punctuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punctuation), making it a [hypernym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypernym) of "spelling". This pattern is not limited to jargon and can be found in general vocabulary as well. [Natural language](wikipedia://en/21173) relies on context for implicit meaning, unlike programming languages that prioritize [explicitness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explicit_knowledge) about [hyponymy and hypernymy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyponymy_and_hypernymy), way more than [programming languages](wikipedia://en/23015) do, meaning is implicit within a context. Examples include variations in the senses of ["wood wool"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_wool#Terminology) and ["bean"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bean#Terminology) and the following:

- The word "diabetes" without further specification usually refers to [diabetes mellitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diabetes_mellitus).
- The word "tinnitus" without further specification usually refers to [subjective tinnitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subjective_tinnitus).
- The word "angina" without further specification usually refers to [angina pectoris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angina_pectoris).
- The word "tuberculosis" without further specification usually refers to [pulmonary tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis#Pulmonary).
- The word "emphysema" without further specification usually refers to [pulmonary emphysema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronic_obstructive_pulmonary_disease).
- The word "cervix" without further specification usually refers to the [uterine cervix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cervix).

Usage labels of "*[sensu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensu)*" plus a [qualifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensu#Common_qualifiers), such as "*sensu stricto*" ("in the strict sense") or "*sensu lato*" ("in the broad sense"), are used to specify the intended meaning in a text.

## Relation to etymology {#Relation_to_etymology}

Polysemy refers to the phenomenon where a word or phrase has multiple related meanings stemming from a common historical origin. In the medical field, broad terms are often followed by [qualifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_modifier) to specify certain conditions or anatomical locations, making them polysemic. Older conceptual words are typically highly polysemic, often extending beyond shades of similar meanings into [ambiguity](wikipedia://en/677).

[Homonymy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homonym) refers to the phenomenon where two distinct words ([lexemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexeme)) share the same [spelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spelling) and [pronunciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pronunciation).
