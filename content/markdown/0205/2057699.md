# Stable polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Stable polynomial"}

A [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) is defined to be **stable** in two different ways.

:::

- [Hurwitz-stable](wikipedia://en/45857): In the context of a [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309) ([continuous-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous-time)) the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/31050469) is stable if all its roots lie in the [open](wikipedia://en/39358) left [complex half-plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-plane).
- Schur-stable: In the context of a [difference equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_equation) ([discrete-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete-time)), its characteristic polynomial is stable if all its roots lie in the [open](wikipedia://en/39358) [complex unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738).

Also, in [control theory](wikipedia://en/7039), a linear, [time-invariant system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-invariant_system) (see [LTI system theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LTI_system_theory)) is said to be [BIBO stable](wikipedia://en/1145733) if every bounded input produces bounded output. A linear system is BIBO stable if its denominator characteristic polynomial is stable (Hurwitz stable for a continuous-time system, Schur stable for discrete-time). In practice, stability is tested via several [stability criteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stability_criterion).

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- The [Routh–Hurwitz theorem](wikipedia://en/2067581) provides an algorithm for determining if a given polynomial is Hurwitz stable, which is implemented in the [Routh–Hurwitz](wikipedia://en/1705432) and [Liénard–Chipart](wikipedia://en/40735675) tests.
- To test if a given polynomial *P* (of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) *d*) is Schur stable, it suffices to apply this theorem to the transformed polynomial

$$Q(z)=(z-1)^d P\left({{z+1}\over{z-1}}\right)$$

obtained after the [Möbius transformation](wikipedia://en/314493) $z \mapsto {{z+1}\over{z-1}}$ which maps the left half-plane to the open unit disc: *P* is Schur stable if and only if *Q* is Hurwitz stable and $P(1)\neq 0$. For higher degree polynomials the extra computation involved in this mapping can be avoided by testing the Schur stability by the Schur-Cohn test, the [Jury test](wikipedia://en/13119919) or the [Bistritz test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bistritz_stability_criterion).

- Necessary condition: a Hurwitz stable polynomial (with [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203)) has coefficients of the same sign (either all positive or all negative).
- Sufficient condition: a polynomial $f(z) = a_0+a_1 z+\cdots+a_n z^n$ with (real) coefficients such that

$$a_n>a_{n-1}>\cdots>a_0 > 0,$$

is Schur stable.

- Product rule: Two polynomials *f* and *g* are stable (of the same type) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the product *fg* is stable.
- Hadamard product: The Hadamard (coefficient-wise) product of two Hurwitz stable polynomials is again Hurwitz stable.[^o4]

:::

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- $4z^3+3z^2+2z+1$ is Schur stable because it satisfies the sufficient condition;
- $z^{10}$ is Schur stable (because all its roots equal 0) but it does not satisfy the sufficient condition;
- $z^2-z-2$ is not Hurwitz stable (its roots are −1 and 2) because it violates the necessary condition;
- $z^2+3z+2$ is Hurwitz stable (its roots are −1 and −2).
- The polynomial $z^4+z^3+z^2+z+1$ (with positive coefficients) is neither Hurwitz stable nor Schur stable. Its roots are the four primitive fifth [roots of unity](wikipedia://en/171950)

$$z_k=\cos\left({{2\pi k}\over 5}\right)+i \sin\left({{2\pi k}\over 5}\right), \, k=1, \ldots, 4\, .$$

Note here that

$$\cos({{2\pi}/5})={{\sqrt{5}-1}\over 4}>0.$$

It is a "boundary case" for Schur stability because its roots lie on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071). The example also shows that the necessary (positivity) conditions stated above for Hurwitz stability are not sufficient.

:::

## Stable matrices {#Stable_matrices}

Just as stable polynomials are crucial for assessing the stability of systems described by polynomials, stability matrices play a vital role in evaluating the stability of [systems represented by matrices](wikipedia://en/548156).

### Hurwitz matrix {#Hurwitz_matrix}

A [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) *A* is called a **Hurwitz matrix** if every [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of *A* has strictly negative [real part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part).

### Schur matrix {#Schur_matrix}

**Schur matrices** is an analogue of the Hurwitz matrices for discrete-time systems. A matrix *A* is a Schur (stable) matrix if its eigenvalues are located in the [open unit disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_unit_disk) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628).
