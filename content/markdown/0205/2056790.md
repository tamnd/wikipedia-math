# Craig interpolation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Craig interpolation"}

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), **Craig's interpolation theorem** is a result about the relationship between different logical [theories](wikipedia://en/3512103). Roughly stated, the theorem says that if a [formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well_formed_formula) φ implies a formula ψ, and the two have at least one atomic variable symbol in common, then there is a formula ρ, called an interpolant, such that every [non-logical symbol](wikipedia://en/7791994) in ρ occurs both in φ and ψ, φ implies ρ, and ρ implies ψ. The theorem was first proved for [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) by [William Craig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Craig_%28logician%29) in 1957. Variants of the theorem hold for other logics, such as [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154). A stronger form of Craig's interpolation theorem for first-order logic was proved by [Roger Lyndon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Lyndon) in 1959;[^o2][^o3] the overall result is sometimes called the **Craig–Lyndon theorem**.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

In [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154), let

$$\varphi = \lnot(P \land Q) \to (\lnot R \land Q)$$

$$\psi = (S \to P) \lor (S \to \lnot R).$$

Then $\varphi$ [tautologically implies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tautological_implication) $\psi$. This can be verified by writing $\varphi$ in [conjunctive normal form](wikipedia://en/73342):

$$\varphi \equiv (P \lor \lnot R) \land Q.$$

Thus, if $\varphi$ holds, then $P \lor \lnot R$ holds.

$$\rho = (P \lor \lnot R).$$

In turn, $P \lor \lnot R$ tautologically implies $\psi$. Because the two propositional variables occurring in $P \lor \lnot R$ occur in both $\varphi$ and $\psi$, this means that $P \lor \lnot R$ is an interpolant for the implication $\varphi \to \psi$.

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Lyndon's interpolation theorem"}

## Lyndon's interpolation theorem {#Lyndon's_interpolation_theorem}

Suppose that *S* and *T* are two first-order theories. As notation, let *S* ∪ *T* denote the smallest theory including both *S* and *T*; the [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_%28mathematical_logic%29) of *S* ∪ *T* is the smallest one containing the signatures of *S* and *T*. Also let *S* ∩ *T* be the intersection of the languages of the two theories; the signature of *S* ∩ *T* is the intersection of the signatures of the two languages.

Lyndon's theorem says that if *S* ∪ *T* is unsatisfiable, then there is an interpolating sentence ρ in the language of *S* ∩ *T* that is true in all models of *S* and false in all models of *T*. Moreover, ρ has the stronger property that every relation symbol that has a [positive occurrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_occurrence) in ρ has a positive occurrence in some formula of *S* and a negative occurrence in some formula of *T*, and every relation symbol with a negative occurrence in ρ has a negative occurrence in some formula of *S* and a positive occurrence in some formula of *T*.

:::

## Proof of Craig's interpolation theorem {#Proof_of_Craig's_interpolation_theorem}

We present here a [constructive proof](wikipedia://en/360113) of the Craig interpolation theorem for [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154).[^o10]

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

If ⊨φ → ψ then there is a ρ (the *interpolant*) such that ⊨φ → ρ and ⊨ρ → ψ, where *atoms*(ρ) ⊆ *atoms*(φ) ∩ *atoms*(ψ). Here *atoms*(φ) is the set of [propositional variables](wikipedia://en/1557562) occurring in φ, and ⊨ is the [semantic entailment relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entailment) for propositional logic.

Since the above proof is [constructive](wikipedia://en/169262), one may extract an [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) for computing interpolants. Using this algorithm, if *n* = |*atoms*(φ') − *atoms*(ψ)|, then the interpolant ρ has *O*(exp(*n*)) more [logical connectives](wikipedia://en/18153) than φ (see [Big O Notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_O_Notation) for details regarding this assertion). Similar constructive proofs may be provided for the basic [modal logic](wikipedia://en/333365) K, [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262) and [μ-calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu_calculus), with similar complexity measures.

Craig interpolation can be proved by other methods as well. However, these proofs are generally [non-constructive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-constructive):

- [model-theoretically](wikipedia://en/19858), via [Robinson's joint consistency theorem](wikipedia://en/12761741): in the presence of [compactness](wikipedia://en/152207), negation and conjunction, Robinson's joint consistency theorem and Craig interpolation are equivalent.
- [proof-theoretically](wikipedia://en/183478), via a [sequent calculus](wikipedia://en/252329). If [cut elimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cut_elimination) is possible and as a result the [subformula property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subformula_property) holds, then Craig interpolation is provable via induction over the derivations.
- [algebraically](wikipedia://en/4613861), using [amalgamation](wikipedia://en/11092492) theorems for the [variety](wikipedia://en/1021753) of algebras representing the logic.
- via translation to other logics enjoying Craig interpolation.

::: {#o16 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Craig interpolation has many applications, among them [consistency proofs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consistency_proof), [model checking](wikipedia://en/321157),[^o17] proofs in [modular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modularity_%28programming%29) [specifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specification_language), modular [ontologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology_%28computer_science%29).

:::
