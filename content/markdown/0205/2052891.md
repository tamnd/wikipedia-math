# Verisimilitude

verisimilitude in stories

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Verisimilitude"}

In [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), **verisimilitude** (or **truthlikeness**) is the [notion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notion_%28philosophy%29) that some [propositions](wikipedia://en/81094) are closer to being [true](wikipedia://en/29920) than other propositions. The problem of verisimilitude is the problem of articulating what it takes for one false [theory](wikipedia://en/17645570) to be closer to the truth than another false theory.[^o2]

:::

This problem was central to the [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science) of [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper), largely because Popper was among the first to affirm that truth is the aim of [scientific inquiry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Models_of_scientific_inquiry) while acknowledging that most of the greatest scientific theories in the [history of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science) are, strictly speaking, false. If this long string of purportedly false theories is to constitute progress with respect to the goal of truth, then it must be at least possible for one false theory to be closer to the truth than others.

## Karl Popper {#Karl_Popper}

Popper's formal definition of verisimilitude was challenged since 1974 by [Pavel Tichý](wikipedia://en/4565688),[^o4][^o5] [John Henry Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Henry_Harris_%28philosopher%29),[^o6] and [David Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Miller_%28philosopher%29),[^o7] who argued that Popper's definition has an [unintended consequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unintended_consequences): that no false theory can be closer to the truth than another. Popper himself stated: "I accepted the criticism of my definition within minutes of its presentation, wondering why I had not seen the mistake before."[^o8] This result gave rise to a search for an account of verisimilitude that did not deem progress towards the truth an impossibility.

## Post-Popperian theories {#Post-Popperian_theories}

Theories proposed by David Miller and by [Theo Kuipers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theo_Kuipers) build on Popper's approach, guided by the notion that truthlikeness is a function of a truth factor and a content factor. Others (e.g. those advanced by Gerhard Schurz in collaboration with Paul Weingartner, by Mortensen, and by [Ken Gemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Gemes)) are also inspired by Popper's approach but locate what they believe to be the error of Popper's proposal in his overly generous notion of content, or consequence, proposing instead that the consequences that contribute to closeness to truth must be, in a technical sense, "relevant". A different approach (already proposed by [Tichý](wikipedia://en/4565688) and Risto Hilpinen and developed especially by [Ilkka Niiniluoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilkka_Niiniluoto) and [Graham Oddie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Oddie)) takes the "likeness" in truthlikeness literally, holding that a proposition's likeness to the truth is a function of the overall likeness to the actual world of the possible worlds in which the proposition would be true. An attempt to use the notion of point-free metric space is proposed by Giangiacomo Gerla.[^o10] There is currently a debate about whether or to what extent these different approaches to the concept are compatible.[^o11][^o12][^o13]
