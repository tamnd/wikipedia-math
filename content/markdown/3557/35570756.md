# Ibn Adlan

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ibn Adlan"}

**ʻAfīf al-Dīn ʻAlī ibn ʻAdlān al-Mawsilī** (عفيف لدين علي بن عدلان الموصلي; 1187–1268 CE), born in [Mosul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosul), was an [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab) [cryptologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptologist), linguist and poet who is known for his early contributions to [cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715), to which he dedicated at least two books. He was also involved in [literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature) and [poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry), and taught on the Arabic language at the [Al-Salihiyya Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salihiyya_Madrasa) of Cairo.

:::

He was in contact with various rulers of his time, and in this capacity he gained practical experience in cryptanalysis or the science of breaking encoded messages. He dedicated *On Cryptanalysis,* his only surviving work on the topic*,* to [Al-Ashraf Musa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ashraf_Musa,_Emir_of_Damascus) (1229), the [Ayyubid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayyubid_dynasty) Emir of Damascus. He wrote three other books, including *Al-Mu'lam* (*The Told \[Book\]*), also on cryptanalysis, but it is now lost. *On Cryptanalysis* is a sort of guidebook for cryptanalysts, containing twenty sets of techniques he calls "rules". The methods contains more practical details than [Al-Kindi](wikipedia://en/272065)'s [8th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8th_century) *Treatise on Deciphering Cryptographic Messages*—the earliest surviving work on cryptoanalysis—but lack its predecessor's theoretical background on cryptography. Among Ibn 'Adlan's original contributions were methods for breaking no-space [monoalphabetic](wikipedia://en/48356) cryptograms, a type of ciphers which were developed to evade analysis techniques described earlier by Al-Kindi. In this treatise Ibn 'Adlan also includes a real-life example of a cryptogram that he deciphered and his full process in breaking it, which, in the words of the cryptographer [James Massey](wikipedia://en/9543907), provides "the authentic experience of a highly skilled cryptanalyst."

## Biography {#Biography}

*Image: Cairo, madrasa al-zazer 01.JPG.* Ibn 'Adlan worked as a teacher at the [Al-Salihiyya Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salihiyya_Madrasa) complex (remaining parts pictured in 2015).

'Afif al-Din 'Ali ibn 'Adlan was born in Mosul in 583 [AH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hijri_year) (c. 1187 CE).[^n1] He was of an Arab origin[^n2] and received education in Baghdad, including lessons on [syntax](wikipedia://en/26860) by the grammarian [Abu al-Baqa al-Ukbari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_al-Baqa_al-Ukbari).[^n1] Subsequently, he lived in Damascus for a time,[^n3] before became a teacher of the Arabic language at the [Al-Salihiyya Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salihiyya_Madrasa) of Cairo until his death in 666 AH (c. 1268 CE).[^n1] In addition to writing treatises on linguistics and [cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715), he was considered an authority in literature and wrote poems himself.[^n1][^n4] He was in contact with various rulers, and in this capacity he gained practical experience in cryptanalysis, which he calls *hall al-mutarjam*. One of these rulers was [Al-Ashraf Musa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ashraf_Musa,_Emir_of_Damascus) (1229), the [Ayyubid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayyubid_dynasty) Emir of Damascus, for whom he dedicated his treatise *On Cryptanalysis*.[^n5] He was also known by his multiple [*nisbas*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nisba_%28onomastics%29) (descriptive epithets): al-Mawsili (of Mosul), al-Nahwi (the Grammarian) and al-Mutarjim (the Cryptoanalyst).[^n3]

## Works {#Works}

*Image: Ibn 'Adlan - On Cryptanalysis, Al-mu'allaf lil-malik al-'Ashraf, 13th century.jpg.* The cover (*right*) and the first page (*left*) of Ibn Adlan's *On Cryptanalysis*

Early Arabic bibliographies attributed three titles to him, including one on cryptanalysis, *Fi hall al-mutarjam* (*On Cryptanalysis*), also known as *Al-mu'allaf lil-malik al-'Ashraf* (*The \[Book\] Written for King al-Ashraf*). In addition, a reference in *On Cryptanalysis* points to another book, *Al-Mu'lam* (*The Told \[Book\]*), which is now lost, in which he describes algorithms for analysing cryptograms. His other two works were titled *Al-Intihab li-kashf al-'abyat al-mushkilat al-i'rab* and *'Uqlat al-mujtaz fi hall al-aljaz*.[^n1]

### Background {#Background}

The practice and study of encrypting messages into ciphers, called [cryptography](wikipedia://en/520066), had existed since [ancient times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_history), practised by the Egyptian, Chinese, Indian, Mesopotamian, Greek, and Roman civilisations.[^n6] In contrast, [cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715), the science of breaking ciphers—in other words, recovering the plain message from an encrypted one—was founded in the [early Arab-Muslim civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spread_of_Islam).[^n7][^n8] The earliest surviving work found on the topic of cryptanalysis is the *Risalah fi Istikhraj al-Mu'amma* ("Treatise on Deciphering Cryptographic Messages") written by [Al-Kindi](wikipedia://en/272065) (c. 801–873), an Arab scholar who also wrote on other topics including philosophy, astronomy, and medicine.[^n9][^n8][^n10] Reports are also found on other works before al-Kindi, among the earliest of which is *al-Mu'amma* ("The Book of Cryptographic Messages"), written by al-Khalil ibn Ahmad in the 8th century, but they are now lost.[^n2][^n8] Al-Kindi's book presents cryptanalysis techniques such as [frequency analysis](wikipedia://en/157934), which is to also be covered by Ibn 'Adlan's works.[^n9]

### *On Cryptanalysis* {#On_Cryptanalysis}

*Image: Cour mosquee Suleymaniye Istanbul.jpg.* *On Cryptanalysis* is preserved in the library of the [Süleymaniye Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Süleymaniye_Mosque) of Istanbul (*mosque pictured in 2011*).

*On Cryptanalysis* was written in a style of a handbook or a manual, describing Ibn 'Adlan's twenty "rules" or techniques of cryptanalysis, grouped into nine themes.[^n4][^n3] Unlike the cryptological treatises of Al-Kindi before him and later [Ibn al-Durayhim](wikipedia://en/35575543) (c. 1312–1361), which provide theoretical background on cryptography including systematic explanations on types of ciphers, Ibn 'Adlan's *On Cryptanalysis* focuses on the practical matters and specific methods in breaking encrypted texts of his time, often in a more detailed manner than Al-Kindi.[^n4] The work's introduction section does include a brief description of the [simple substitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_substitution) encipherment method, and encourages its readers to read other sources to learn about other methods.[^n11]

One of Ibn 'Adlan's most original contribution in this treatise is the cryptanalysis of no-space [monoalphabetic](wikipedia://en/48356) cryptograms (*al-mudmaj*)—encrypted texts that do not include [a space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_%28punctuation%29) to denote separation between words.[^n4][^n12] This type of cryptograms was not mentioned by al-Kindi: it was developed by subsequent cryptographers (code makers) in order to counteract the cryptographic attacks described in his works, part of a kind of [arms race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arms_race) between the code makers and code breakers. In the west, this type of cryptanalysis was only attested in the sixteenth century in the works of the Italian [Giambattista della Porta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giambattista_della_Porta). According to ibn 'Adlan, the cryptographers of his time "allege that their ciphers can defy detection and analysis" by using the no-space method.[^n4] Ibn 'Adlan recommended the use of frequency analysis,[^n13] as well as the analysis of consecutive letters based on knowing how many times each letter can possibly occur consecutively in Arabic sentences and the specific ways they can do so.[^n14] He also wrote on the analysis of ciphers in which the space is represented by variable symbols.[^n15][^n4]

*On Cryptanalysis* also deals with frequency analysis: Ibn 'Adlan follows al-Kindi's data on the [frequency of Arabic letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_letter_frequency)—although Al-Kindi does not receive any direct attribution, the numbers provided by the two authors are identical—[^n16]and divided the Arabic alphabet into seven common (frequently-occurring), eleven medium, and ten rare letters.[^n3] Ibn 'Adlan presents a table of the most common two or three letter words, and offers a minimum sample size, a lower limit of text length that can be cryptoanalysed using its frequency of letters: about 90 characters (approximately three times the length of the Arabic alphabet).[^n3][^n17] Below this limit, according to Ibn 'Adlan the occurrence of letters will not follow the provided frequency distribution.[^n15]

The treatise includes the cryptanalysis of common adjacent letters, the [Arabic definite article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_definite_article) ال (al-, 'the'), and letters frequently occurring at the beginning or the end of a word. Ibn 'Adlan also writes on the probable words in the opening and closing section of a text (such as the Arabic formula [Bismillah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basmala), "In the name of God").[^n18] He adds special principles for analysing encrypted poetry, including the knowledge of prosody, rhymes and meters.[^n19] He then explains his cryptanalysis steps, moving from the ciphertext to possible solutions, then to the suspected, the probable, and eventually the confirmed solution.[^n20] In the closing section of the book, Ibn 'Adlan includes a real-life example of a cryptogram that he broke and his full process in deciphering it, including his false starts, thought process, and eventual solution. The cryptographer [James Massey](wikipedia://en/9543907) describes this section as "intriguing" and providing "the authentic experience of a highly skilled cryptanalyst."[^n4]

A copy of *On Cryptanalysis* is preserved in the library of the [Süleymaniye Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Süleymaniye_Mosque) of Istanbul (manuscript number 5359).[^n1] A modern edition was prepared by editors Muhammad Mrayati, Yahya Meer Alam and Hassan al-Tayyan and published by the [Arab Academy of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Academy_of_Damascus) in 1987, including introductions and explanatory materials from the editors. It was translated into English in 2004.[^n10][^n21]
