# Smooth projective plane

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Smooth projective plane"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), **smooth projective planes** are special [projective planes](wikipedia://en/24350). The most prominent example of a smooth projective plane is the [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325) ${\mathcal E}$. Its geometric operations of joining two distinct points by a line and of intersecting two lines in a point are not only [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_map) but even *[smooth](wikipedia://en/1408000)* (infinitely differentiable $= C^\infty$). Similarly, the classical planes over the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440), and the [octonions](wikipedia://en/51436) are smooth planes. However, these are not the only such planes.

:::

## Definition and basic properties {#Definition_and_basic_properties}

A smooth projective plane ${\mathcal P} = (P,\mathfrak{L})$ consists of a point space $P$ and a line space $\mathfrak{L}$ that are smooth [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) and where both geometric operations of joining and intersecting are smooth.

The geometric operations of smooth planes are continuous; hence, each smooth plane is a *[compact](wikipedia://en/6042)* topological plane.[^o3] Smooth planes exist only with point spaces of dimension 2<sup>*m*</sup> where $1\le m\le 4$, because this is true for compact [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) projective topological planes.[^o4][^o5] These four cases will be treated separately below.

Theorem. *The point manifold of a smooth projective plane is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to its classical counterpart, and so is the line manifold*.[^o6]

## Automorphisms {#Automorphisms}

[Automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) play a crucial role in the study of smooth planes. A [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) of the point set of a projective plane is called a [collineation](wikipedia://en/4072976), if it maps lines onto lines. The continuous collineations of a compact projective plane ${\mathcal P}$ form the group $\operatorname{Aut}{\mathcal P}$. This group is taken with the topology of [uniform convergence](wikipedia://en/50652). We have:[^o8]

Theorem. *If ${\mathcal P} = (P,\mathfrak{L})$ is a smooth plane, then each continuous collineation of ${\mathcal P}$ is smooth*; *in other words, the group of automorphisms of a smooth plane ${\mathcal P}$ coincides with $\operatorname{Aut}{\mathcal P}$. Moreover, $\operatorname{Aut}{\mathcal P}$ is a smooth Lie transformation group of $P$ and of $\mathfrak{L}$*.

The automorphism groups of the four classical planes are [simple Lie groups](wikipedia://en/292831) of dimension 8, 16, 35, or 78, respectively. All other smooth planes have much smaller groups. See below.

## Translation planes {#Translation_planes}

A projective plane is called a [translation plane](wikipedia://en/2628762) if its automorphism group has a subgroup that fixes each point on some line $W$ and [acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) [sharply transitively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharply_multiply_transitive) on the set of points not on $W$.

Theorem. *Every smooth projective translation plane ${\mathcal P}$ is isomorphic to one of the four classical planes*.[^o10]

This shows that there are many compact connected topological projective planes that are not smooth. On the other hand, the following construction yields [real analytic](wikipedia://en/61478) [non-Desarguesian planes](wikipedia://en/21092112) of dimension 2, 4, and 8, with a [compact group](wikipedia://en/750326) of automorphisms of dimension 1, 4, and 13, respectively:[^o11] represent points and lines in the usual way by [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316) over the real or complex numbers or the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440), say, by vectors of length $1$. Then the incidence of the point $(x,y,z)$ and the line $(a,b,c)$ is defined by $ax+by+cz=t |c|^2|z|^2cz$, where $t$ is a fixed real parameter such that $|t|<1/9$. These planes are self-dual.

## 2-dimensional planes {#2-dimensional_planes}

Compact 2-dimensional projective planes can be described in the following way: the point space is a compact [surface](wikipedia://en/27865) $S$, each line is a [Jordan curve](wikipedia://en/267444) in $S$ (a closed subset homeomorphic to the circle), and any two distinct points are joined by a unique line. Then $S$ is homeomorphic to the point space of the real plane ${\mathcal E}$, any two distinct lines intersect in a unique point, and the geometric operations are continuous (apply (Salzmann Betten Grundhöfer Hähl 1995, §31) to the complement of a line). A familiar family of examples was given by [Moulton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_Ray_Moulton) in 1902.[^o14][^o15] These planes are characterized by the fact that they have a 4-dimensional automorphism group. They are not isomorphic to a smooth plane.[^o16] More generally, all non-classical compact 2-dimensional planes ${\mathcal P}$ such that $\dim\operatorname{Aut}{\mathcal P} \ge 3$ are known explicitly; none of these is smooth:

Theorem. *If ${\mathcal P}$ is a smooth 2-dimensional plane and if $\dim\operatorname{Aut}{\mathcal P} \ge 3$, then ${\mathcal P}$ is the classical real plane ${\mathcal E}$*.[^o17]

## 4-dimensional planes {#4-dimensional_planes}

All compact planes ${\mathcal P}$ with a 4-dimensional point space and $\operatorname{Aut}{\mathcal P} \ge 7$ have been classified.[^o19] Up to duality, they are either translation planes or they are isomorphic to a unique so-called shift plane.[^o20] According to (Bödi 1996, Chap. 10), this shift plane is not smooth. Hence, the result on translation planes implies:

Theorem. *A smooth 4-dimensional plane is isomorphic to the classical complex plane, or $\dim\operatorname{Aut}{\mathcal P}\le 6$*.[^o22]

## 8-dimensional planes {#8-dimensional_planes}

Compact 8-dimensional *topological* planes ${\mathcal P}$ have been discussed in (Salzmann Betten Grundhöfer Hähl 1995, Chapter 8) and, more recently, in (Salzmann 2014). Put $\Sigma = \operatorname{Aut}{\mathcal P}$. Either ${\mathcal P}$ is the classical quaternion plane or $\dim\Sigma \le 18$. If $\dim\Sigma \ge 17$, then ${\mathcal P}$ is a translation plane, or a dual translation plane, or a [Hughes plane](wikipedia://en/21116300).[^o26] The latter can be characterized as follows: $\Sigma$ leaves some classical complex subplane ${\mathcal C}$ invariant and induces on ${\mathcal C}$ the connected component of its full automorphism group.[^o27][^o28] The Hughes planes are not smooth.[^o29][^o30] This yields a result similar to the case of 4-dimensional planes:

Theorem. *If ${\mathcal P}$ is a smooth 8-dimensional plane, then ${\mathcal P}$ is the classical quaternion plane or $\dim\Sigma \le 16$*.

## 16-dimensional planes {#16-dimensional_planes}

Let $\Sigma$ denote the automorphism group of a compact 16-dimensional topological projective plane ${\mathcal P}$. Either ${\mathcal P}$ is the smooth classical octonion plane or $\dim\Sigma\le 40$. If $\dim\Sigma = 40$, then $\Sigma$ fixes a line $W$ and a point $v\in W$, and the affine plane ${\mathcal P}\smallsetminus W$ and its dual are translation planes.[^o32] If $\dim\Sigma = 39$, then $\Sigma$ also fixes an incident point-line pair, but neither ${\mathcal P}$ nor $\Sigma$ are known explicitly. Nevertheless, none of these planes can be smooth:[^o33][^o34][^o35]

Theorem. *If ${\mathcal P}$ is a 16-dimensional smooth projective plane, then ${\mathcal P}$ is the classical octonion plane or $\dim\Sigma \le 38$*.

::: {#o37 .statement cue=heading name="Main theorem"}

## Main theorem {#Main_theorem}

The last four results combine to give the following theorem:

If $c_m$ is the largest value of $\dim\operatorname{Aut}{\mathcal P}$, where ${\mathcal P}$ is a non-classical compact 2<sup>*m*</sup>-dimensional *topological* projective plane, then $\dim\operatorname{Aut}{\mathcal P} \le c_m-2$ whenever ${\mathcal P}$ is even smooth.

:::

## Complex analytic planes {#Complex_analytic_planes}

The condition, that the geometric operations of a projective plane are complex analytic, is very restrictive. In fact, it is satisfied only in the classical complex plane.[^o39][^o40]

Theorem. *Every complex analytic projective plane is isomorphic as an analytic plane to the complex plane with its standard analytic structure*.
