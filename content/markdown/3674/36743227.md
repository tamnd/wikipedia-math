# Matrix splitting

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Matrix splitting"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) discipline of [numerical linear algebra](wikipedia://en/7330660), a **matrix splitting** is an expression which represents a given [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) as a sum or difference of matrices. Many [iterative methods](wikipedia://en/15237) (for example, for systems of [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309)) depend upon the direct solution of matrix equations involving matrices more general than [tridiagonal matrices](wikipedia://en/519218). These matrix equations can often be solved directly and efficiently when written as a matrix splitting. The technique was devised by [Richard S. Varga](wikipedia://en/25492358) in 1960.[^o2]

:::

## Regular splittings {#Regular_splittings}

We seek to solve the [matrix equation](wikipedia://en/20556859#Linear_equations)

::: {#o4 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\mathbf A \mathbf x = \mathbf k, \tag{1}$$

:::

where **A** is a given *n* × *n* [non-singular](wikipedia://en/217122) matrix, and **k** is a given [column vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_vector) with *n* components. We split the matrix **A** into

::: {#o5 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\mathbf A = \mathbf B - \mathbf C, \tag{2}$$

:::

where **B** and **C** are *n* × *n* matrices. If, for an arbitrary *n* × *n* matrix **M**, **M** has nonnegative entries, we write **M** ≥ **0**. If **M** has only positive entries, we write **M** > **0**. Similarly, if the matrix **M**<sub>1</sub> − **M**<sub>2</sub> has nonnegative entries, we write **M**<sub>1</sub> ≥ **M**<sub>2</sub>.

Definition: **A** = **B** − **C** is a **regular splitting of A** if **B**<sup>−1</sup> ≥ **0** and **C** ≥ **0**.

We assume that matrix equations of the form

::: {#o6 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$\mathbf B \mathbf x = \mathbf g, \tag{3}$$

:::

where **g** is a given column vector, can be solved directly for the vector **x**. If ((2)) represents a regular splitting of **A**, then the iterative method

::: {#o7 .equation cue=numblk name="4"}

$$\mathbf B \mathbf x^{(m+1)} = \mathbf C \mathbf x^{(m)} + \mathbf k, \quad m = 0, 1, 2, \ldots , \tag{4}$$

:::

where **x**<sup>(0)</sup> is an arbitrary vector, can be carried out. Equivalently, we write ((4)) in the form

::: {#o8 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$$\mathbf x^{(m+1)} = \mathbf B^{-1} \mathbf C \mathbf x^{(m)} + \mathbf B^{-1} \mathbf k, \quad m = 0, 1, 2, \ldots \tag{5}$$

:::

The matrix **D** = **B**<sup>−1</sup>**C** has nonnegative entries if ((2)) represents a regular splitting of **A**.[^o9]

It can be shown that if **A**<sup>−1</sup> > **0**, then $\rho (\mathbf D)$ \< 1, where $\rho (\mathbf D)$ represents the [spectral radius](wikipedia://en/386169) of **D**, and thus **D** is a [convergent matrix](wikipedia://en/37709899). As a consequence, the iterative method ((5)) is necessarily [convergent](wikipedia://en/4047104#Convergence).[^o10][^o11]

If, in addition, the splitting ((2)) is chosen so that the matrix **B** is a [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080) (with the diagonal entries all non-zero, since **B** must be [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122)), then **B** can be inverted in linear time (see [Time complexity](wikipedia://en/405944)).

## Matrix iterative methods {#Matrix_iterative_methods}

Many iterative methods can be described as a matrix splitting. If the diagonal entries of the matrix **A** are all nonzero, and we express the matrix **A** as the matrix sum

::: {#o13 .equation cue=numblk name="6"}

$$\mathbf A = \mathbf D - \mathbf U - \mathbf L, \tag{6}$$

:::

where **D** is the diagonal part of **A**, and **U** and **L** are respectively strictly upper and lower [triangular](wikipedia://en/374222) *n* × *n* matrices, then we have the following.

The [Jacobi method](wikipedia://en/4047104) can be represented in matrix form as a splitting

::: {#o14 .equation cue=numblk name="7"}

\mathbf x^{(m+1)} = \mathbf D^{-1}(\mathbf U + \mathbf L)\mathbf x^{(m)} + \mathbf D^{-1}\mathbf k.$[^o15][^o16] (7)

:::

The [Gauss–Seidel method](wikipedia://en/4046824) can be represented in matrix form as a splitting

::: {#o17 .equation cue=numblk name="8"}

\mathbf x^{(m+1)} = (\mathbf D - \mathbf L)^{-1}\mathbf U \mathbf x^{(m)} + (\mathbf D - \mathbf L)^{-1}\mathbf k.$[^o18][^o19] (8)

:::

The method of [successive over-relaxation](wikipedia://en/4068447) can be represented in matrix form as a splitting

::: {#o20 .equation cue=numblk name="9"}

\mathbf x^{(m+1)} = (\mathbf D - \omega \mathbf L)^{-1}[(1 - \omega) \mathbf D + \omega \mathbf U] \mathbf x^{(m)} + \omega (\mathbf D - \omega \mathbf L)^{-1}\mathbf k.$[^o21][^o22] (9)

:::

## Example {#Example}

### Regular splitting {#Regular_splitting}

In equation ((1)), let

::: {#o26 .equation cue=numblk name="10"}

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & -2 & -3 \\ -1 & 4 & -2 \\ -3 & -1 & 5 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{k} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ -12 \\ 10 \end{pmatrix}. \tag{10}$$

:::

Let us apply the splitting ((7)) which is used in the Jacobi method: we split **A** in such a way that **B** consists of *all* of the diagonal elements of **A**, and **C** consists of *all* of the off-diagonal elements of **A**, negated. (Of course this is not the only useful way to split a matrix into two matrices.) We have

::: {#o27 .equation cue=numblk name="11"}

$$\begin{aligned} & \mathbf{B} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{C} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \end{aligned} \tag{11}$$

:::

$$\begin{aligned} & \mathbf{A^{-1}} = \frac{1}{47} \begin{pmatrix} 18 & 13 & 16 \\ 11 & 21 & 15 \\ 13 & 12 & 22 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B^{-1}} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{6} & 0 & 0 \\[4pt] 0 & \frac{1}{4} & 0 \\[4pt] 0 & 0 & \frac{1}{5} \end{pmatrix}, \end{aligned}$$

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{D} = \mathbf{B^{-1}C} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{2} \\[4pt] \frac{1}{4} & 0 & \frac{1}{2} \\[4pt] \frac{3}{5} & \frac{1}{5} & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B^{-1}k} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{5}{6} \\[4pt] -3 \\[4pt] 2 \end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned}$$

Since **B**<sup>−1</sup> ≥ **0** and **C** ≥ **0**, the splitting ((11)) is a regular splitting. Since **A**<sup>−1</sup> > **0**, the spectral radius $\rho (\mathbf D)$ \< 1. (The approximate [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) of **D** are $\lambda_i \approx -0.4599820, -0.3397859, 0.7997679.$) Hence, the matrix **D** is convergent and the method ((5)) necessarily converges for the problem ((10)). Note that the diagonal elements of **A** are all greater than zero, the off-diagonal elements of **A** are all less than zero and **A** is [strictly diagonally dominant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strictly_diagonally_dominant).[^o28]

The method ((5)) applied to the problem ((10)) then takes the form

::: {#o29 .equation cue=numblk name="12"}

$$\mathbf x^{(m+1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{2} \\[4pt] \frac{1}{4} & 0 & \frac{1}{2} \\[4pt] \frac{3}{5} & \frac{1}{5} & 0 \end{pmatrix} \mathbf x^{(m)} + \begin{pmatrix} \frac{5}{6} \\[4pt] -3 \\[4pt] 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad m = 0, 1, 2, \ldots \tag{12}$$

:::

The exact solution to equation ((12)) is

::: {#o30 .equation cue=numblk name="13"}

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}. \tag{13}$$

:::

The first few iterates for equation ((12)) are listed in the table below, beginning with $\mathbf{x}^{(0)} = (0.0, 0.0, 0.0)^{T}$. From the table one can see that the method is evidently converging to the solution ((13)), albeit rather slowly.

### Jacobi method {#Jacobi_method}

As stated above, the Jacobi method ((7)) is the same as the specific regular splitting ((11)) demonstrated above.

### Gauss–Seidel method {#Gauss–Seidel_method}

Since the diagonal entries of the matrix **A** in problem ((10)) are all nonzero, we can express the matrix **A** as the splitting ((6)), where

::: {#o34 .equation cue=numblk name="14"}

$$\mathbf{D} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{U} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{L} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \tag{14}$$

:::

We then have

$$\begin{aligned} & \mathbf{(D-L)^{-1}} = \frac{1}{120} \begin{pmatrix} 20 & 0 & 0 \\ 5 & 30 & 0 \\ 13 & 6 & 24 \end{pmatrix}, \end{aligned}$$

$$\begin{aligned} & \mathbf{(D-L)^{-1}U} = \frac{1}{120} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 40 & 60 \\ 0 & 10 & 75 \\ 0 & 26 & 51 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{(D-L)^{-1}k} = \frac{1}{120} \begin{pmatrix} 100 \\ -335 \\ 233 \end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned}$$

The Gauss–Seidel method ((8)) applied to the problem ((10)) takes the form

::: {#o35 .equation cue=numblk name="15"}

$$\mathbf x^{(m+1)} = \frac{1}{120} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 40 & 60 \\ 0 & 10 & 75 \\ 0 & 26 & 51 \end{pmatrix} \mathbf x^{(m)} + \frac{1}{120} \begin{pmatrix} 100 \\ -335 \\ 233 \end{pmatrix}, \quad m = 0, 1, 2, \ldots \tag{15}$$

:::

The first few iterates for equation ((15)) are listed in the table below, beginning with $\mathbf{x}^{(0)} = (0.0, 0.0, 0.0)^{T}$. From the table one can see that the method is evidently converging to the solution ((13)), somewhat faster than the Jacobi method described above.

### Successive over-relaxation method {#Successive_over-relaxation_method}

Let *ω* = 1.1. Using the splitting ((14)) of the matrix **A** in problem ((10)) for the successive over-relaxation method, we have

$$\begin{aligned} & \mathbf{(D-\omega L)^{-1}} = \frac{1}{12} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0.55 & 3 & 0 \\ 1.441 & 0.66 & 2.4 \end{pmatrix}, \end{aligned}$$

$$\begin{aligned} & \mathbf{(D-\omega L)^{-1}[(1-\omega )D+\omega U]} = \frac{1}{12} \begin{pmatrix} -1.2 & 4.4 & 6.6 \\ -0.33 & 0.01 & 8.415 \\ -0.8646 & 2.9062 & 5.0073 \end{pmatrix}, \end{aligned}$$

$$\begin{aligned} & \mathbf{\omega (D-\omega L)^{-1}k} = \frac{1}{12} \begin{pmatrix} 11 \\ -36.575 \\ 25.6135 \end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned}$$

The successive over-relaxation method ((9)) applied to the problem ((10)) takes the form

::: {#o38 .equation cue=numblk name="16"}

$$\mathbf x^{(m+1)} = \frac{1}{12} \begin{pmatrix} -1.2 & 4.4 & 6.6 \\ -0.33 & 0.01 & 8.415 \\ -0.8646 & 2.9062 & 5.0073 \end{pmatrix} \mathbf x^{(m)} + \frac{1}{12} \begin{pmatrix} 11 \\ -36.575 \\ 25.6135 \end{pmatrix}, \quad m = 0, 1, 2, \ldots \tag{16}$$

:::

The first few iterates for equation ((16)) are listed in the table below, beginning with $\mathbf{x}^{(0)} = (0.0, 0.0, 0.0)^{T}$. From the table one can see that the method is evidently converging to the solution ((13)), slightly faster than the Gauss–Seidel method described above.
