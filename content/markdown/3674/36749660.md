# Matroid minor

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Matroid minor"}

In the mathematical theory of [matroids](wikipedia://en/244321), a **minor** of a matroid *M* is another matroid *N* that is obtained from *M* by a sequence of restriction and contraction operations. Matroid minors are closely related to [graph minors](wikipedia://en/353042), and the restriction and contraction operations by which they are formed correspond to edge deletion and [edge contraction](wikipedia://en/3758115) operations in graphs. The theory of matroid minors leads to structural decompositions of matroids, and characterizations of matroid families by forbidden minors, analogous to the corresponding theory in graphs.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

If *M* is a matroid on the set *E* and *S* is a subset of *E*, then the restriction of *M* to *S*, written *M* |*S*, is the matroid on the set *S* whose independent sets are the independent sets of *M* that are contained in *S*. Its circuits are the circuits of *M* that are contained in *S* and its [rank function](wikipedia://en/37123010) is that of *M* restricted to subsets of *S*.

If *T* is an independent subset of *E*, the contraction of *M* by *T*, written *M*/*T*, is the matroid on the underlying set *E − T* whose independent sets are the sets whose union with *T* is independent in *M*. This definition may be extended to arbitrary *T* by choosing a basis for *T* and defining a set to be independent in the contraction if its union with this basis remains independent in *M*. The rank function of the contraction is $r'(A) = r(A \cup T) - r(T).$

A matroid *N* is a minor of a matroid *M* if it can be constructed from *M* by restriction and contraction operations.

In terms of the [geometric lattice](wikipedia://en/17243191) formed by the flats of a matroid, taking a minor of a matroid corresponds to taking an interval of the lattice, the part of the lattice lying between a given lower bound and upper bound element.[^n1]

:::

## Forbidden matroid characterizations {#Forbidden_matroid_characterizations}

Many important families of matroids are closed under the operation of taking minors: if a matroid *M* belongs to the family, then every minor of *M* also belongs to the family. In this case, the family may be characterized by its set of "forbidden matroids", the minor-minimal matroids that do not belong to the family. A matroid belongs to the family if and only if it does not have a forbidden matroid as a minor. Often, but not always, the set of forbidden matroids is finite, paralleling the [Robertson–Seymour theorem](wikipedia://en/351769) which states that the set of forbidden minors of a minor-closed graph family is always finite.

An example of this phenomenon is given by the [regular matroids](wikipedia://en/13415343), matroids that are representable over all fields. Equivalently a matroid is regular if it can be represented by a [totally unimodular matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_unimodular_matrix) (a matrix whose square submatrices all have determinants equal to 0, 1, or −1). (Tutte 1958) proved that a matroid is regular if and only if it does not have one of three forbidden minors: the [uniform matroid](wikipedia://en/27967069) $U{}^2_4$ (the four-point line), the [Fano plane](wikipedia://en/390404), or the [dual matroid](wikipedia://en/23515837) of the Fano plane. For this he used his difficult [homotopy theorem](wikipedia://en/37921130). Simpler proofs have since been found.

The [graphic matroids](wikipedia://en/745714), matroids whose independent sets are the forest subgraphs of a graph, have five forbidden minors: the three for the regular matroids, and the two duals of the graphic matroids for the graphs *K*<sub>5</sub> and *K*<sub>3,3</sub> that by [Wagner's theorem](wikipedia://en/3126130) are forbidden minors for the [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314).

The [binary matroids](wikipedia://en/36848165), matroids representable over the two-element [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615), include both graphic and regular matroids. Tutte again showed that these matroids have a forbidden minor characterization: they are the matroids that do not have the four-point line as a minor. [Rota conjectured](wikipedia://en/36760081) that, for any finite field, the matroids representable over that field have finitely many forbidden minors.[^o7] A proof of this conjecture was announced, but not published, by Geelen, Gerards, and Whittle in 2014.[^n2] The matroids that can be represented over the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) have infinitely many forbidden minors.[^o9]

## Branchwidth {#Branchwidth}

[Branch-decompositions](wikipedia://en/16823137) of matroids may be defined analogously to their definition for graphs. A branch-decomposition of a matroid is a [hierarchical clustering](wikipedia://en/477573) of the matroid elements, represented as an [unrooted binary tree](wikipedia://en/27950476) with the elements of the matroid at its leaves. Removing any edge of this tree partitions the matroids into two disjoint subsets; such a partition is called an e-separation. If *r* denotes the rank function of the matroid, then the width of an e-separation is defined as *r*(*A*) + *r*(*B*) − *r*(*M*) + 1. The width of a decomposition is the maximum width of any of its e-separations, and the branchwidth of a matroid is the minimum width of any of its branch-decompositions.

The branchwidth of a graph and the branchwidth of the corresponding [graphic matroid](wikipedia://en/745714) may differ: for instance, the three-edge [path graph](wikipedia://en/4587190) and the three-edge [star](wikipedia://en/19310129) have different branchwidths, 2 and 1 respectively, but they both induce the same graphic matroid with branchwidth 1.[^o12] However, for graphs that are not trees, the branchwidth of the graph is equal to the branchwidth of its associated graphic matroid.[^o11] The branchwidth of a matroid always equals the branchwidth of its dual.[^o12]

Branchwidth is an important component of attempts to extend the theory of graph minors to matroids: although [treewidth](wikipedia://en/3987086) can also be generalized to matroids,[^o13] and plays a bigger role than branchwidth in the theory of graph minors, branchwidth has more convenient properties in the matroid setting.[^o14] If a minor-closed family of matroids representable over a finite field does not include the graphic matroids of all planar graphs, then there is a constant bound on the branchwidth of the matroids in the family, generalizing similar results for minor-closed graph families.[^o15]

## Well-quasi-ordering {#Well-quasi-ordering}

The [Robertson–Seymour theorem](wikipedia://en/351769) implies that every matroid property of *graphic* matroids characterized by a list of forbidden minors can be characterized by a finite list. Another way of saying the same thing is that the [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) on graphic matroids formed by the minor operation is a [well-quasi-ordering](wikipedia://en/739775). However, the example of the real-representable matroids, which have infinitely many forbidden minors, shows that the minor ordering is not a well-quasi-ordering on all matroids.

Robertson and Seymour conjectured that the matroids representable over any particular [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) are well-quasi-ordered. So far this has been proven only for the matroids of bounded branchwidth.[^o17]

## Matroid decompositions {#Matroid_decompositions}

The [graph structure theorem](wikipedia://en/21318521) is an important tool in the theory of graph minors, according to which the graphs in any minor-closed family can be built up from simpler graphs by [clique-sum](wikipedia://en/17598460) operations. Some analogous results are also known in matroid theory. In particular, [Seymour's decomposition theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seymour's_decomposition_theorem) states that all regular matroids can be built up in a simple way as the clique-sum of graphic matroids, their duals, and one special 10-element matroid.[^o19] As a consequence, [linear programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_program) defined by totally unimodular matrices may be solved combinatorially by combining the solutions to a set of [minimum spanning tree](wikipedia://en/41795) problems corresponding to the graphic and co-graphic parts of this decomposition.

## Algorithms and complexity {#Algorithms_and_complexity}

One of the important components of graph minor theory is the existence of an algorithm for testing whether a graph *H* is a minor of another graph *G*, taking an amount of time that is polynomial in *G* for any fixed choice of *H* (and more strongly [fixed-parameter tractable](wikipedia://en/603026#FPT) if the size of *H* is allowed to vary). By combining this result with the Robertson–Seymour theorem, it is possible to recognize the members of any minor-closed graph family in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time). Correspondingly, in matroid theory, it would be desirable to develop efficient algorithms for recognizing whether a given fixed matroid is a minor of an input matroid. Unfortunately, such a strong result is not possible: in the [matroid oracle](wikipedia://en/36878315) model, the only minors that can be recognized in polynomial time are the [uniform matroids](wikipedia://en/27967069) with rank or corank one.[^n3] However, if the problem is restricted to the matroids that are representable over some fixed finite field (and represented as a matrix over that field) then, as in the graph case, it is conjectured to be possible to recognize the matroids that contain any fixed minor in polynomial time.[^o14]
