# Gibbs measure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gibbs measure"}

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Gibbs measure**, named after [Josiah Willard Gibbs](wikipedia://en/37332), is a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) frequently seen in many problems of [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481), and [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/990632).[^o2] It is a generalization of the [canonical ensemble](wikipedia://en/1137672) to infinite systems. The canonical ensemble gives the probability of the system *X* being in state *x* (equivalently, of the [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) *X* having value *x*) as

:::

$$P(X=x) = \frac{1}{Z(\beta)} \exp ( - \beta E(x)).$$

Here, $E$ is a function from the space of states to the real numbers; in physics applications, $E(x)$ is interpreted as the energy of the configuration *x*. The parameter $\beta$ is a free parameter; in physics, it is the [inverse temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_temperature). The [normalizing constant](wikipedia://en/398189) $Z(\beta)$ is the [partition function](wikipedia://en/16846849). However, in infinite systems, the total energy is no longer a finite number and cannot be used in the traditional construction of the probability distribution of a canonical ensemble. Traditional approaches in statistical physics studied the limit of [intensive properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensive_property) as the size of a finite system approaches infinity (the [thermodynamic limit](wikipedia://en/2262550)). When the energy function can be written as a sum of terms that each involve only variables from a finite subsystem, the notion of a Gibbs measure provides an alternative approach. Gibbs measures were proposed by probability theorists such as [Dobrushin](wikipedia://en/6368767), [Lanford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Lanford), and [Ruelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Ruelle) and provided a framework to directly study infinite systems, instead of taking the limit of finite systems.

A measure is a Gibbs measure if the conditional probabilities it induces on each finite subsystem satisfy a consistency condition: if all degrees of freedom outside the finite subsystem are frozen, the canonical ensemble for the subsystem subject to these [boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_conditions) matches the probabilities in the Gibbs measure [conditional](wikipedia://en/24104134) on the frozen degrees of freedom.

The [Hammersley–Clifford theorem](wikipedia://en/21963702) implies that any probability measure that satisfies a [Markov property](wikipedia://en/306422) is a Gibbs measure for an appropriate choice of (locally defined) energy function. Therefore, the Gibbs measure applies to widespread problems outside of [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), such as [Hopfield networks](wikipedia://en/1170097), [Markov networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_network), [Markov logic networks](wikipedia://en/3670357), and [boundedly rational potential games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econophysics#Basic_tools) in game theory and economics. A Gibbs measure in a system with local (finite-range) interactions maximizes the [entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_%28general_concept%29) density for a given expected [energy density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_density); or, equivalently, it minimizes the [free energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_free_energy) density.

The Gibbs measure of an infinite system is not necessarily unique, in contrast to the canonical ensemble of a finite system, which is unique. The existence of more than one Gibbs measure is associated with statistical phenomena such as [symmetry breaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_breaking) and [phase coexistence](wikipedia://en/54423#Phase_coexistence).

## Statistical physics {#Statistical_physics}

The set of Gibbs measures on a system is always convex,[^o4][^o5] so there is either a unique Gibbs measure (in which case the system is said to be "[ergodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic)"), or there are infinitely many (and the system is called "nonergodic"). In the nonergodic case, the Gibbs measures can be expressed as the set of [convex combinations](wikipedia://en/794534) of a much smaller number of special Gibbs measures known as "pure states" (not to be confused with the related but distinct notion of [pure states in quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_states)). In physical applications, the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381) (the energy function) usually has some sense of [*locality*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_locality), and the pure states have the [cluster decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cluster_decomposition) property that "far-separated subsystems" are independent. In practice, physically realistic systems are found in one of these pure states.

If the Hamiltonian possesses a symmetry, then a unique (i.e. ergodic) Gibbs measure will necessarily be invariant under the symmetry. But in the case of multiple (i.e. nonergodic) Gibbs measures, the pure states are typically *not* invariant under the Hamiltonian's symmetry. For example, in the infinite ferromagnetic [Ising model](wikipedia://en/292744) below the critical temperature, there are two pure states, the "mostly-up" and "mostly-down" states, which are interchanged under the model's $\mathbb{Z}_2$ symmetry.

## Markov property {#Markov_property}

An example of the [Markov property](wikipedia://en/306422) can be seen in the Gibbs measure of the [Ising model](wikipedia://en/292744). The probability for a given spin $\sigma_{k}$ to be in state *s* could, in principle, depend on the states of all other spins in the system. Thus, we may write the probability as

$$P(\sigma_k = s\mid\sigma_j,\, j\ne k).$$

However, in an Ising model with only finite-range interactions (for example, nearest-neighbor interactions), we actually have

$$P(\sigma_k = s\mid\sigma_j,\, j\ne k) = P(\sigma_k = s\mid\sigma_j,\, j\in N_k),$$

where $N_{k}$ is a neighborhood of the site $k$. That is, the probability at site $k$ depends *only* on the spins in a finite neighborhood. This last equation is in the form of a local [Markov property](wikipedia://en/306422). Measures with this property are sometimes called [Markov random fields](wikipedia://en/1323985). More strongly, the converse is also true: *any* positive probability distribution (nonzero density everywhere) having the Markov property can be represented as a Gibbs measure for an appropriate energy function.[^o7] This is the [Hammersley–Clifford theorem](wikipedia://en/21963702).

## Formal definition on lattices {#Formal_definition_on_lattices}

What follows is a formal definition for the special case of a random field on a lattice. The idea of a Gibbs measure is, however, much more general than this.

The definition of a **Gibbs random field** on a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) requires some terminology:

- The **lattice**: A countable set $\mathbb{L}$.
- The **single-spin space**: A [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325) $(S,\mathcal{S},\lambda)$.
- The **[configuration space](wikipedia://en/473596)**: $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$, where $\Omega = S^{\mathbb{L}}$ and $\mathcal{F} = \mathcal{S}^{\mathbb{L}}$.
- Given a configuration $\omega \in \Omega$ and a subset $\Lambda \subset \mathbb{L}$, the restriction of $\omega$ to $\Lambda$ is $\omega_\Lambda = (\omega(t))_{t\in\Lambda}$. If $\Lambda_1\cap\Lambda_2=\emptyset$ and $\Lambda_1\cup\Lambda_2=\mathbb{L}$, then the configuration $\omega_{\Lambda_1}\omega_{\Lambda_2}$ is the configuration whose restrictions to $\Lambda_{1}$ and $\Lambda_{2}$ are $\omega_{\Lambda_1}$ and $\omega_{\Lambda_2}$, respectively.
- The set $\mathcal{L}$ of all finite subsets of $\mathbb{L}$.
- For each subset $\Lambda\subset\mathbb{L}$, $\mathcal{F}_\Lambda$ is the [$\sigma$-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_algebra) generated by the family of functions $(\sigma(t))_{t\in\Lambda}$, where $\sigma(t)(\omega)=\omega(t)$. The union of these $\sigma$-algebras as $\Lambda$ varies over $\mathcal{L}$ is the algebra of [cylinder sets](wikipedia://en/2613658) on the lattice.
- The **[potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential)**: A family $\Phi=(\Phi_A)_{A\in\mathcal{L}}$ of functions $\Phi_{A} : \Omega \to \mathbf{R}$ such that
  1. For each $A\in\mathcal{L}, \Phi_A$ is $\mathcal{F}_A$-[measurable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurable), meaning it depends only on the restriction $\omega_A$ (and does so measurably).
  1. For all $\Lambda\in\mathcal{L}$ and $\omega \in \Omega$, the following series exists:

$$H_\Lambda^\Phi(\omega) = \sum_{A\in\mathcal{L}, A\cap\Lambda\neq\emptyset} \Phi_A(\omega).$$

We interpret $\Phi_{A}$ as the contribution to the total energy (the Hamiltonian) associated to the interaction among all the points of finite set *A*. Then $H_\Lambda^\Phi(\omega)$ as the contribution to the total energy of all the finite sets *A* that meet $\Lambda$. Note that the total energy is typically infinite, but when we "localize" to each $\Lambda$ it may be finite, we hope.

- The **[Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319#Mathematical_formalism)** in $\Lambda\in\mathcal{L}$ with **boundary conditions** $\bar\omega$, for the potential $\Phi$, is defined by

$$H_\Lambda^\Phi(\omega \mid \bar\omega) = H_\Lambda^\Phi \left(\omega_\Lambda\bar\omega_{\Lambda^c} \right )$$

where $\omega_\Lambda\bar\omega_{\Lambda^c}$ denotes the configuration taking the values of $\omega$ in $\Lambda$, and those of $\bar\omega$ in $\Lambda^c := \mathbb{L}\setminus\Lambda$.

- The **[partition function](wikipedia://en/16846849)** in $\Lambda\in\mathcal{L}$ with **boundary conditions** $\bar\omega$ and inverse temperature $\beta > 0$ (for the potential $\Phi$ and $\lambda$) is defined by

$$Z_\Lambda^\Phi(\bar\omega) = \int \lambda^\Lambda(\mathrm{d}\omega) \exp(-\beta H_\Lambda^\Phi(\omega \mid \bar\omega)),$$

where

$$\lambda^\Lambda(\mathrm{d}\omega) = \prod_{t\in\Lambda}\lambda(\mathrm{d}\omega(t)),$$

is the product measure

A potential $\Phi$ is $\lambda$-admissible if $Z_\Lambda^\Phi(\bar\omega)$ is finite for all $\Lambda\in\mathcal{L}, \bar\omega\in\Omega$ and $\beta > 0$.

A [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) $\mu$ on $(\Omega,\mathcal{F})$ is a **Gibbs measure** for a $\lambda$-admissible potential $\Phi$ if it satisfies the **Dobrushin–Lanford–Ruelle (DLR) equation**

$$\int \mu(\mathrm{d}\bar\omega)Z_\Lambda^\Phi(\bar\omega)^{-1} \int\lambda^\Lambda(\mathrm{d}\omega) \exp(-\beta H_\Lambda^\Phi(\omega \mid \bar\omega))\, 1_A(\omega_\Lambda\bar\omega_{\Lambda^c}) = \mu(A),$$

for all $A\in\mathcal{F}$ and $\Lambda\in\mathcal{L}$.

::: {#o10 .example cue=heading name="An example"}

### An example {#An_example}

To help understand the above definitions, here are the corresponding quantities in the important example of the [Ising model](wikipedia://en/292744) with nearest-neighbor interactions (coupling constant $J$) and a magnetic field ($h$), on $\mathbf{Z}^{d}$:

- The lattice is simply $\mathbb{L} = \mathbf{Z}^d$.
- The single-spin space is $S = \{-1, 1\}.$
- The potential is given by

$$\Phi_A(\omega) = \begin{cases} -J\,\omega(t_1)\omega(t_2) & \text{if } A=\{t_1,t_2\} \text{ with } \|t_2-t_1\|_1 = 1 \\ -h\,\omega(t) & \text{if } A=\{t\}\\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

:::
