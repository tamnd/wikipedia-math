# Semi-differentiability

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Semi-differentiability"}

In [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), the notions of **one-sided differentiability** and **semi-differentiability** of a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued [function](wikipedia://en/185427) *f* of a real variable are weaker than [differentiability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiability). Specifically, the function *f* is said to be **right differentiable** at a point *a* if, roughly speaking, a [derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative_%28mathematics%29) can be defined as the function's argument *x* moves to *a* from the right, and **left differentiable** at *a* if the derivative can be defined as *x* moves to *a* from the left.

:::

## One-dimensional case {#One-dimensional_case}

*Image: Right-continuous.svg.* This function does not have a derivative at the marked point, as the function is not [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) there. However, it has a right derivative at all points, with $\partial_+f(a)$ constantly equal to 0.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **left derivative** and a **right derivative** are [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) (rates of change of a function) defined for movement in one direction only (left or right; that is, to lower or higher values) by the argument of a function.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

### Definitions {#Definitions}

Let *f* denote a real-valued function defined on a subset *I* of the real numbers.

If $a \in I$ is a [limit point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point) of $I \cap$ *a*,∞ and the [one-sided limit](wikipedia://en/379840)

$$\partial_+f(a):=\lim_{{\scriptstyle x\to a^+\atop\scriptstyle x\in I}}\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}$$

exists as a real number, then *f* is called **right differentiable** at *a* and the limit *∂*<sub>\+</sub>*f*(*a*) is called the **right derivative** of *f* at *a*.

If $a \in I$ is a limit point of $I \cap$ –∞,*a* and the one-sided limit

$$\partial_-f(a):=\lim_{{\scriptstyle x\to a^-\atop\scriptstyle x\in I}}\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}$$

exists as a real number, then *f* is called **left differentiable** at *a* and the limit *∂*<sub>–</sub>*f*(*a*) is called the **left derivative** of *f* at *a*.

If $a \in I$ is a limit point of $I \cap$ *a*,∞ and $I \cap (-\infty,a]$ and if *f* is left and right differentiable at *a*, then *f* is called **semi-differentiable** at *a*.

If the left and right derivatives are equal, then they have the same value as the usual ("bidirectional") derivative. One can also define a [symmetric derivative](wikipedia://en/3219921), which equals the [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612) of the left and right derivatives (when they both exist), so the symmetric derivative may exist when the usual derivative does not.[^o5]

:::

### Remarks and examples {#Remarks_and_examples}

- A function is [differentiable](wikipedia://en/7921) at an [interior point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interior_point) *a* of its [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) if and only if it is semi-differentiable at *a* and the left derivative is equal to the right derivative.
- An example of a semi-differentiable function, which is not differentiable, is the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) function $f(x)=|x|$, at *a* = 0. We find easily $\partial_-f(0)=-1, \partial_+f(0)=1.$
- If a function is semi-differentiable at a point *a*, it implies that it is continuous at *a*.
- The [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) 1<sub>\[0,∞)</sub> is right differentiable at every real *a*, but discontinuous at zero (note that this indicator function is not left differentiable at zero).

::: {#o8 .application cue=heading name="Application"}

### Application {#Application}

If a real-valued, differentiable function *f*, defined on an interval *I* of the real line, has zero derivative everywhere, then it is constant, as an application of the [mean value theorem](wikipedia://en/19662) shows. The assumption of differentiability can be weakened to continuity and one-sided differentiability of *f*. The version for right differentiable functions is given below, the version for left differentiable functions is analogous.

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

Let *f* be a real-valued, [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122), defined on an arbitrary [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) *I* of the real line. If *f* is right differentiable at every point $a \in I$, which is not the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) of the interval, and if this right derivative is always zero, then *f* is [constant](wikipedia://en/320861).

:::

::: {#o10 .proof cue=math-proof}

For a [proof by contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_contradiction), assume there exist $a < b$ in *I* such that $f(a) \ne f(b)$. Then

$$\varepsilon:=\frac{|f(b)-f(a)|}{2(b-a)}>0.$$

Define *c* as the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) of all those *x* in the interval *a*,*b* for which the [difference quotient](wikipedia://en/241863) of *f* exceeds *ε* in absolute value, i.e.

$$c=\inf\{\,x\in(a,b]\mid |f(x)-f(a)|>\varepsilon(x-a)\,\}.$$

Due to the continuity of *f*, it follows that $c < b$ and $\lvert f(c) - f(a)\rvert = \varepsilon(c - a)$. At *c* the right derivative of *f* is zero by assumption, hence there exists *d* in the interval *c*,*b* with $\lvert f(x) - f(c)\rvert \le \varepsilon(x - c)$ for all *x* in *c*,*d*. Hence, by the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941),

$$|f(x)-f(a)|\le|f(x)-f(c)|+|f(c)-f(a)|\le\varepsilon(x-a)$$

for all *x* in *c*,*d*, which contradicts the definition of *c*.

:::

:::

### Differential operators acting to the left or the right {#Differential_operators_acting_to_the_left_or_the_right}

Another common use is to describe derivatives treated as [binary operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_operator) in [infix notation](wikipedia://en/199014), in which the derivatives is to be applied either to the left or right [operands](wikipedia://en/22656). This is useful, for example, when defining generalizations of the [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340). For a pair of functions f and g, the left and right derivatives are respectively defined as

$$f \stackrel{\leftarrow }{\partial_x} g = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \cdot g$$

$$f \stackrel{\rightarrow }{\partial_x} g = f \cdot \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}.$$

In [bra–ket notation](wikipedia://en/4542), the derivative operator can act on the right operand as the regular derivative or on the left as the negative derivative.[^o12]

## Higher-dimensional case {#Higher-dimensional_case}

This above definition can be generalized to real-valued functions *f* defined on subsets of **R**<sup>*n*</sup> using a weaker version of the [directional derivative](wikipedia://en/487859). Let **a** be an interior point of the domain of *f*. Then *f* is called *semi-differentiable* at the point **a** if for every direction **u** ∈ **R**<sup>*n*</sup> the limit

$$\partial_{\mathbf{u}}f(\mathbf{a})=\lim_{h\to 0^+}\frac{f(\mathbf{a}+h\mathbf{u})-f(\mathbf{a})}{h}$$

exists as a real number, with *h* ∈ **R**.

Semi-differentiability is thus weaker than [Gateaux differentiability](wikipedia://en/2253139), for which one takes in the limit above *h* → 0 without restricting *h* to only positive values.

For example, the function $f(x, y) = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ is semi-differentiable at $(0, 0)$, but not Gateaux differentiable there. Indeed, $f(hx,hy)=|h|f(x,y) \text{ and for } h \geq 0, f(hx,hy)=h f(x,y), f(hx,hy)/h=f(x,y),$ with $\mathbf{a}=(0,0), \mathbf{u}=(x,y) \text{ and } \partial_{\mathbf{u}}f(0,0)=f(x,y).$

(Note that this generalization is not equivalent to the original definition for *n = 1* since the concept of one-sided limit points is replaced with the stronger concept of interior points.)

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- Any [convex function](wikipedia://en/245568) on a convex [open subset](wikipedia://en/39358) of **R**<sup>*n*</sup> is semi-differentiable.
- While every semi-differentiable function of one variable is continuous; this is no longer true for several variables.

:::

::: {#o17 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

Instead of real-valued functions, one can consider functions taking values in **R**<sup>*n*</sup> or in a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989).
