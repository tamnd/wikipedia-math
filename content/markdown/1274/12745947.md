# Permutohedron

*Image: Symmetric group 4; permutohedron 3D; transpositions (1-based).png.* The permutohedron of order 4

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Permutohedron"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **permutohedron** (also spelled **permutahedron**) of order $n$ is an $(n - 1)$-dimensional [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) embedded in an $n$-dimensional space. Its [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) coordinates (labels) are the [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) of the first $n$ [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474). Two permutations connected by an edge differ in only two places (one [transposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposition_%28mathematics%29)), and the numbers on these places are neighbors (differ in value by 1).

:::

The image on the right shows the permutohedron of order 4, which is the [truncated octahedron](wikipedia://en/279654). Its vertices are the 24 permutations of $(1, 2, 3, 4)$. Parallel edges have the same edge color. The 6 edge colors correspond to the 6 possible [transpositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposition_%28mathematics%29) of 4 elements, i.e. they indicate in which two places the connected permutations differ. (E.g. red edges connect permutations that differ in the last two places.)

## History {#History}

According to (txt), permutohedra were first studied by (txt). The name *permutoèdre* was coined by (txt). They describe the word as barbaric, but easy to remember, and submit it to the criticism of their readers.[^o6]

The alternative spelling *permut**a**hedron* is sometimes also used.[^o7] Permutohedra are sometimes called *permutation polytopes*, but this terminology is also used for the related [Birkhoff polytope](wikipedia://en/7771277), defined as the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of [permutation matrices](wikipedia://en/238849). More generally, (txt) uses that term for any polytope whose vertices have a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) with the permutations of some set.

The permutohedron of order $n$ has $n!$ vertices, each of which is adjacent to $n - 1$ others. The number of edges is $\tfrac{(n - 1) n!}{2}$, and their length is $\sqrt{2}$.

Two vertices that are connected by an edge differ by a swap of two coordinates whose values differ by 1.[^o10] The pair of swapped places corresponds to the direction of the edge. (In the example image the vertices $(3, 2, 1, 4)$ and $(2, 3, 1, 4)$ are connected by a blue edge and differ by swapping 2 and 3 on the first two places. The values 2 and 3 differ by 1. All blue edges correspond to swaps of coordinates on the first two places.)

The number of [facets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facet_%28mathematics%29) is $2^{n} - 2$, because they correspond to non-empty proper [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) $S$ of $\{1 ... n\}$. The vertices of a facet corresponding to subset $S$ have in common, that their coordinates on places in $S$ are smaller than the rest.[^o11]

More generally, the [faces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-face) of dimensions 0 (vertices) to $n - 1$ (the permutohedron itself) correspond to the [strict weak orderings](wikipedia://en/828131#Strict_weak_orderings) of the set $\{1 ... n\}$. So the number of all faces is the $n$-th [ordered Bell number](wikipedia://en/6992164).[^o13] A face of dimension $d$ corresponds to an ordering with $k = n - d$ equivalence classes.

The number of faces of dimension $d = n - k$ in the permutohedron of order $n$ is given by the triangle $T$ :

$$T(n,k) = k! \cdot \left\{{n\atop k}\right\}$$

with $\textstyle \left\{{n\atop k}\right\}$ representing the [Stirling numbers of the second kind](wikipedia://en/2229292).

It is shown on the right together with its row sums, the [ordered Bell numbers](wikipedia://en/6992164).

## Other properties {#Other_properties}

*Image: Symmetric group 4; Cayley graph 1,2,6 (1-based).png.* The permutohedron-like Cayley graph of $S_{4}$ (see here for a comparison with the permutohedron)

The permutohedron is [vertex-transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex-transitive): the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_{n}$ [acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) on the permutohedron by permutation of coordinates.

The permutohedron is a [zonotope](wikipedia://en/669406); a translated copy of the permutohedron can be generated as the [Minkowski sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_sum) of the $n(n - 1)/2$ line segments that connect the pairs of the [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005) vectors.[^n1]

The [vertex-edge graph](wikipedia://en/74718534) of the permutohedron is the [Bruhat graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruhat_graph), which is a [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) generated by the [transpositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposition_%28mathematics%29) that swap consecutive elements. The vertices of the Cayley graph are the [inverse](wikipedia://en/14907) permutations of those in the permutohedron.[^o19] The image on the right shows the Cayley graph of $S4$. Its edge colors represent the 3 generating transpositions: blue, green, red.

This Cayley graph is [Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle); a Hamiltonian cycle may be found by the [Steinhaus–Johnson–Trotter algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steinhaus–Johnson–Trotter_algorithm).

## Tessellation of the space {#Tessellation_of_the_space}

The permutohedron of order $n$ lies entirely in the $(n - 1)$-dimensional [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) consisting of all points whose coordinates sum to the number:

$$1 + 2 + \ldots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$$

Moreover, this hyperplane can be [tiled](wikipedia://en/321671) by infinitely many [translated](wikipedia://en/204682) copies of the permutohedron. Each of them differs from the basic permutohedron by an element of a certain $(n - 1)$-dimensional [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246), which consists of the $n$-tuples of integers that sum to zero and whose [residues](wikipedia://en/20087) (modulo $n$) are all equal:

$$\begin{aligned} & x_1 + x_2 + \ldots + x_n = 0 \\ & x_1 \equiv x_2 \equiv \ldots \equiv x_n \pmod n. \end{aligned}$$

This is the lattice $A_{n-1}^*$, the [dual lattice](wikipedia://en/1395200) of the [root lattice $A_{n-1}$](wikipedia://en/277087#An). In other words, the permutohedron is the [Voronoi cell](wikipedia://en/177668) for $A_{n-1}^*$. Accordingly, this lattice is sometimes called the permutohedral lattice.[^n2]

Thus, the permutohedron of order 4 shown above tiles the 3-dimensional space by translation. Here the 3-dimensional space is the [affine subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_subspace) of the 4-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^4$ with coordinates $x, y, z, w$ that consists of the 4-tuples of real numbers whose sum is 10,

$$x + y + z + w = 10.$$

One easily checks that for each of the following four vectors,

$$(1,1,1,-3),\ (1,1,-3,1),\ (1,-3,1,1),\ (-3,1,1,1),$$

the sum of the coordinates is zero and all coordinates are congruent to 1 (mod 4). Any three of these vectors [generate](wikipedia://en/99945) the translation lattice.

The tessellations formed in this way from the order-2, order-3, and order-4 permutohedra, respectively, are the [apeirogon](wikipedia://en/5658261), the regular [hexagonal tiling](wikipedia://en/2863719), and the [bitruncated cubic honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3736299). The dual tessellations contain all [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) facets, although they are not regular polytopes beyond order-3.
