# Quasi-isometry

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quasi-isometry"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **quasi-isometry** is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) between two [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018) that respects large-scale geometry of these spaces and ignores their small-scale details. Two metric spaces are **quasi-isometric** if there exists a quasi-isometry between them. The property of being quasi-isometric behaves like an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) on the [class](wikipedia://en/20696) of metric spaces.

:::

The concept of quasi-isometry is especially important in [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164), following the work of [Gromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Leonidovich_Gromov).[^o2]

*Image: Equilateral\_Triangle\_Lattice.svg.* This [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) is quasi-isometric to the plane.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Suppose that $f$ is a (not necessarily continuous) function from one metric space $(M_1,d_1)$ to a second metric space $(M_2,d_2)$. Then $f$ is called a *quasi-isometry* from $(M_1,d_1)$ to $(M_2,d_2)$ if there exist constants $A\ge 1$, $B\ge 0$, and $C\ge 0$ such that the following two properties both hold:[^o5]

1. For every two points $x$ and $y$ in $M_1$, the distance between their images is up to the additive constant $B$ within a factor of $A$ of their original distance. More formally:

$$\forall x,y\in M_1: \frac{1}{A}\; d_1(x,y)-B\leq d_2(f(x),f(y))\leq A\; d_1(x,y)+B.$$

1. Every point of $M_2$ is within the constant distance $C$ of an image point. More formally:

$$\forall z\in M_2:\exists x\in M_1: d_2(z,f(x))\le C.$$

The two metric spaces $(M_1,d_1)$ and $(M_2,d_2)$ are called **quasi-isometric** if there exists a quasi-isometry $f$ from $(M_1,d_1)$ to $(M_2,d_2)$.

A map is called a **quasi-isometric embedding** if it satisfies the first condition but not necessarily the second (i.e. it is coarsely [Lipschitz](wikipedia://en/51731) but may fail to be coarsely surjective). In other words, if through the map, $(M_1,d_1)$ is quasi-isometric to a subspace of $(M_2,d_2)$.

Two metric spaces *M<sub>1</sub>* and *M<sub>2</sub>* are said to be **quasi-isometric**, denoted $M_1\underset{q.i.}{\sim} M_2$, if there exists a quasi-isometry $f:M_1\to M_2$.

:::

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The map between the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) and the plane with the [Manhattan distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_distance) that sends every point to itself is a quasi-isometry: in it, distances are multiplied by a factor of at most $\sqrt 2$. Note that there can be no isometry, since, for example, the points $(1, 0), (-1, 0), (0, 1), (0, -1)$ are of equal distance to each other in Manhattan distance, but in the Euclidean plane, there are no 4 points that are of equal distance to each other.

The map $f:\mathbb{Z}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$ (both with the [Euclidean metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_metric)) that sends every $n$-tuple of integers to itself is a quasi-isometry: distances are preserved exactly, and every real tuple is within distance $\sqrt{n/4}$ of an integer tuple. In the other direction, the discontinuous function that [rounds](wikipedia://en/169945) every tuple of real numbers to the nearest integer tuple is also a quasi-isometry: each point is taken by this map to a point within distance $\sqrt{n/4}$ of it, so rounding changes the distance between pairs of points by adding or subtracting at most $2\sqrt{n/4}$.

Every pair of finite or bounded metric spaces is quasi-isometric. In this case, every function from one space to the other is a quasi-isometry.

:::

## Equivalence relation {#Equivalence_relation}

If $f:M_1\mapsto M_2$ is a quasi-isometry, then there exists a quasi-isometry $g:M_2\mapsto M_1$. Indeed, $g(x)$ may be defined by letting $y$ be any point in the image of $f$ that is within distance $C$ of $x$, and letting $g(x)$ be any point in $f^{-1}(y)$.

Since the [identity map](wikipedia://en/15069) is a quasi-isometry, and the [composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_composition) of two quasi-isometries is a quasi-isometry, it follows that the property of being quasi-isometric behaves like an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) on the class of metric spaces.

## Use in geometric group theory {#Use_in_geometric_group_theory}

Given a finite [generating set](wikipedia://en/99945) *S* of a finitely generated [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G*, we can form the corresponding [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of *S* and *G*. This graph becomes a metric space if we declare the length of each edge to be 1. Taking a different finite generating set *T* results in a different graph and a different metric space, however the two spaces are quasi-isometric.[^o10] This quasi-isometry class is thus an [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) of the group *G*. Any property of metric spaces that only depends on a space's quasi-isometry class immediately yields another invariant of groups, opening the field of group theory to geometric methods.

More generally, the **[Švarc–Milnor lemma](wikipedia://en/51457085)** states that if a group *G* acts [properly discontinuously](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Properly_discontinuous_action) with compact quotient on a proper geodesic space *X* then *G* is quasi-isometric to *X* (meaning that any Cayley graph for *G* is). This gives new examples of groups quasi-isometric to each other:

- If *G'* is a subgroup of finite [index](wikipedia://en/230581) in *G* then *G'* is quasi-isometric to *G*;
- If *G* and *H* are the fundamental groups of two compact [hyperbolic manifolds](wikipedia://en/1589135) of the same dimension *d* then they are both quasi-isometric to the hyperbolic space **H**<sup>*d*</sup> and hence to each other; on the other hand there are infinitely many quasi-isometry classes of fundamental groups of finite-volume.[^o11]

## Quasigeodesics and the Morse lemma {#Quasigeodesics_and_the_Morse_lemma}

A *quasi-geodesic* in a metric space $(X, d)$ is a quasi-isometric embedding of $\mathbb R$ into $X$. More precisely a map $\phi: \mathbb R \to X$ such that there exists $C,K > 0$ so that

$$\forall s, t \in \mathbb R : C^{-1} |s - t| - K \le d(\phi(t), \phi(s)) \le C|s - t| + K$$

is called a $(C,K)$-quasi-geodesic. Obviously geodesics (parametrised by arclength) are quasi-geodesics. The fact that in some spaces the converse is coarsely true, i.e. that every quasi-geodesic stays within bounded distance of a true geodesic, is called the *Morse Lemma* (not to be confused with the [Morse lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_lemma) in differential topology). Formally the statement is:

*Let $\delta, C, K > 0$ and $X$ a proper [δ-hyperbolic space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Δ-hyperbolic_space). There exists $M$ such that for any $(C, K)$-quasi-geodesic $\phi$ there exists a geodesic $L$ in $X$ such that $d(\phi(t), L) \le M$ for all $t \in \mathbb R$.*

It is an important tool in geometric group theory. An immediate application is that any quasi-isometry between proper hyperbolic spaces induces a homeomorphism between their boundaries. This result is the first step in the proof of the [Mostow rigidity theorem](wikipedia://en/2782164).

Furthermore, this result has found utility in analyzing user interaction design in applications similar to [Google Maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Maps).[^o14]

:::

## Examples of quasi-isometry invariants of groups {#Examples_of_quasi-isometry_invariants_of_groups}

The following are some examples of properties of group Cayley graphs that are invariant under quasi-isometry:[^o5]

### Hyperbolicity {#Hyperbolicity}

A group is called *hyperbolic* if one of its Cayley graphs is a δ-hyperbolic space for some δ. When translating between different definitions of hyperbolicity, the particular value of δ may change, but the resulting notions of a hyperbolic group turn out to be equivalent.

Hyperbolic groups have a solvable [word problem](wikipedia://en/33563). They are [biautomatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biautomatic_group) and [automatic](wikipedia://en/4224977).:[^o17] indeed, they are [strongly geodesically automatic](wikipedia://en/4224977), that is, there is an automatic structure on the group, where the language accepted by the word acceptor is the set of all geodesic words.

### Growth {#Growth}

The **growth rate** of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) with respect to a symmetric [generating set](wikipedia://en/99945) describes the size of balls in the group. Every element in the group can be written as a product of generators, and the growth rate counts the number of elements that can be written as a product of length *n*.

According to [Gromov's theorem](wikipedia://en/635483), a group of polynomial growth is [virtually nilpotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtually_nilpotent), i.e. it has a [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569) [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of finite [index](wikipedia://en/230581). In particular, the order of polynomial growth $k_0$ has to be a [natural number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers) and in fact $\#(n)\sim n^{k_0}$.

If $\#(n)$ grows more slowly than any exponential function, *G* has a **subexponential growth rate**. Any such group is [amenable](wikipedia://en/657430).

### Ends {#Ends}

The **ends** of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) are, roughly speaking, the [connected components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28topology%29) of the “ideal boundary” of the space. That is, each end represents a topologically distinct way to move to [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824) within the space. Adding a point at each end yields a [compactification](wikipedia://en/6617) of the original space, known as the **end compactification**.

The ends of a [finitely generated group](wikipedia://en/963042) are defined to be the ends of the corresponding [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277); this definition is independent of the choice of a finite generating set. Every finitely-generated infinite group has either 1, 2, or infinitely many ends, and [Stallings theorem about ends of groups](wikipedia://en/19196631) provides a decomposition for groups with more than one end.

If two connected locally finite graphs are quasi-isometric then they have the same number of ends.[^o20] In particular, two quasi-isometric finitely generated groups have the same number of ends.

### Amenability {#Amenability}

An **amenable group** is a [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) *G* carrying a kind of averaging operation on bounded functions that is [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under translation by group elements. The original definition, in terms of a finitely additive invariant measure (or mean) on subsets of *G*, was introduced by [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) in 1929 under the [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language) name "messbar" ("measurable" in English) in response to the [Banach–Tarski paradox](wikipedia://en/19759220). In 1949 Mahlon M. Day introduced the English translation "amenable", apparently as a pun.[^o22]

In [discrete group theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_group_theory), where *G* has the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology), a simpler definition is used. In this setting, a group is amenable if one can say what proportion of *G* any given subset takes up.

If a group has a [Følner sequence](wikipedia://en/1424857) then it is automatically amenable.

### Asymptotic cone {#Asymptotic_cone}

An **ultralimit** is a geometric construction that assigns to a sequence of [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018) *X<sub>n</sub>* a limiting metric space. An important class of ultralimits are the so-called *asymptotic cones* of metric spaces. Let (*X*,*d*) be a metric space, let *ω* be a non-principal ultrafilter on $\mathbb N$ and let *p<sub>n</sub>* ∈ *X* be a sequence of base-points. Then the *ω*–ultralimit of the sequence $(X, \frac{d}{n}, p_n)$ is called the asymptotic cone of *X* with respect to *ω* and $(p_n)_n\,$ and is denoted $Cone_\omega(X,d, (p_n)_n)\,$. One often takes the base-point sequence to be constant, *p<sub>n</sub>* = *p* for some *p ∈ X*; in this case the asymptotic cone does not depend on the choice of *p ∈ X* and is denoted by $Cone_\omega(X,d)\,$ or just $Cone_\omega(X)\,$.

The notion of an asymptotic cone plays an important role in [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164) since asymptotic cones (or, more precisely, their [topological types](wikipedia://en/13660) and [bi-Lipschitz types](wikipedia://en/51731)) provide quasi-isometry invariants of metric spaces in general and of finitely generated groups in particular.[^o24] Asymptotic cones also turn out to be a useful tool in the study of [relatively hyperbolic groups](wikipedia://en/21993809) and their generalizations.[^o25]
