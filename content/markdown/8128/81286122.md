# Albertson index

*Image: Albertson index.svg.* The vertices are labeled by their [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448), and the edges by their **imbalance**, the difference of the degrees of the vertices [incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incidence_%28graph%29) to them. The **Albertson index** $\operatorname{Alb}(G)$ of a graph $G$ is the sum of the imbalances of all edges.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Albertson index"}

The **Albertson index** (also known as **irregularity** or **Albertson irregularity index**) is a [graph invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_invariant) that measures the irregularity of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) by summing the imbalances of all its edges. It was introduced by [Michael O. Albertson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_O._Albertson) in 1997 as a single parameter to capture how "non-regular" a graph is.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

For a [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_graph) [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) $G = (V, E)$, the **imbalance** of an edge $e = {u,v}$ is defined as:

$$\operatorname{imb}(e) = |\deg(u) - \deg(v)|$$

where $\deg(u)$ and $\deg(v)$ denote the [degrees](wikipedia://en/1374448) of vertices $u$ and $v$.

The **Albertson index** of $G$, denoted $\operatorname{Alb}(G)$ (sometimes $\operatorname{I}(G)$ or $\operatorname{irr}(G)$ in the literature), is the sum of the imbalances of all edges:

$$\operatorname{Alb}(G) = \sum_{uv \in E(G)} |\deg(u) - \deg(v)|$$

A graph is [regular](wikipedia://en/85821) if and only if its Albertson index is zero.

:::

## Properties and bounds {#Properties_and_bounds}

The Albertson index is always an [even](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_and_odd) integer. For a graph with $n$ vertices, the index is bounded by $O(n^3)$.[^o2]

### Maximum irregularity {#Maximum_irregularity}

The maximum Albertson index over all graphs with $n$ vertices is less than $\frac{4n^3}{27}$, and this bound is tight. The extremal graphs that approach this bound are of the form $H_{r,n} = K_r \vee \overline{K}{n-r}$, the [join](wikipedia://en/81278692) of a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_r$ and an [independent set](wikipedia://en/524501) $\overline{K}{n-r}$, where $r$ is approximately $n/3$.[^o2]

A graph $G$ is called **critical** if adding or removing any single edge does not increase its Albertson index. Albertson proved that a graph is critical if and only if it is of the form $H_{r,n}$ for some $r \leq \frac{n}{3}$, a result used to establish the general upper bound.[^o2]

For specific classes of graphs, tighter bounds exist:

If $G$ is [bipartite](wikipedia://en/244431), then

$$\operatorname{Alb}(G) \leq \frac{2n^3}{27}.$$

The irregularity of a [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) $K_{t,n-t}$ is $t(n-t)(n-2t)$.

If $G$ is [triangle-free](wikipedia://en/7667996), then

$$\operatorname{Alb}(G) < \frac{4n^3}{27}.$$

### Extremal trees {#Extremal_trees}

Among all [trees](wikipedia://en/48560) with $n$ vertices, the Albertson index is bounded by:

$$2 \leq \operatorname{Alb}(T) \leq (n-1)(n-2)$$

The lower bound of 2 is achieved exclusively by the [path graph](wikipedia://en/4587190) $P_n$, while the upper bound is achieved exclusively by the [star graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_graph) $K_{1,n-1}$.[^o2]

### Minimal irregularity {#Minimal_irregularity}

While the star graph maximizes irregularity for trees, the minimal irregularity for certain graph classes has also been studied. For a connected graph $G$ with $k$ [pendant vertices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendant_vertex), a general lower bound is $\operatorname{Alb}(G) \geq 2k$.[^o9]

## Related concepts and generalizations {#Related_concepts_and_generalizations}

### Total irregularity {#Total_irregularity}

A related measure, the **total irregularity**, was introduced by Abdo, Brandt, and Dimitrov. It is defined as the sum of absolute degree differences over all pairs of vertices, not just adjacent ones:[^o12]

$$\operatorname{irr}t(G) = \frac{1}{2}\sum{u,v \in V(G)} |\deg(u) - \deg(v)|$$

Unlike the Albertson index, the total irregularity depends only on the [degree sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_sequence) of a graph, not its specific edge structure.[^o12]

### Generalized Albertson index {#Generalized_Albertson_index}

A generalization was introduced by Lin, Zhou, Wang, and Miao in 2022. The **general Albertson index** (or **$p$-Albertson index**) is defined as:[^o14]

$$A_p(G) = \left(\sum_{uv \in E(G)} |\deg(u) - \deg(v)|^p\right)^{1/p}$$

where $p$ is a positive real number. This reduces to the classical Albertson index for $p = 1$. For $p = 2$, its square $A_2^2(G)$ is known as the $\sigma$-index and is related to the [Zagreb indices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zagreb_indices) and the *forgotten topological index*. The extremal trees for the $p$-Albertson index remain the path graph (minimum) and the star graph (maximum).[^o14]

## Applications and connections {#Applications_and_connections}

The Albertson index has applications in [mathematical chemistry](wikipedia://en/1570072) for predicting physicochemical properties of molecules in [QSAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QSAR)/[QSPR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QSPR) modeling. In [network science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_science), it provides a quantitative measure of network heterogeneity.

The concept is also connected to other graph theoretical topics, such as [stepwise irregular graphs](wikipedia://en/81259284), where every edge has an imbalance of exactly 1,[^o16] and the [imbalance conjecture](wikipedia://en/81259798), which studies whether sequences of edge imbalances can form a valid [degree sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_sequence) of some graph.[^o17]
