# Hamming space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hamming space"}

*Image: Hamming distance 3 bit binary.svg.* The Hamming space of binary strings of length 3. The distance between vertices in the [cube graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube_graph) equals the [Hamming distance](wikipedia://en/41227) between the strings.

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) and [coding theory](wikipedia://en/321869), a **Hamming space** is usually the set of all $2^N$ [binary strings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_string) of length *N*, where different binary strings are considered to be *adjacent* when they differ only in one position. The total distance between any two binary strings is then the total number of positions at which the corresponding bits are different, called the [Hamming distance](wikipedia://en/41227).[^o2][^o3] Hamming spaces are named after American mathematician [Richard Hamming](wikipedia://en/99326), who introduced the concept in 1950.[^o4] They are used in the theory of coding signals and transmission.

:::

More generally, a Hamming space can be defined over any [alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabet_%28computer_science%29) (set) *Q* as the set of [words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_%28formal_language_theory%29) of a fixed length *N* with letters from *Q*.[^o6][^o5] If *Q* is a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615), then a Hamming space over *Q* is an *N*-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over *Q*. In the typical, binary case, the field is thus [GF(2)](wikipedia://en/11137263) (also denoted by **Z**<sub>2</sub>).[^o6]

In coding theory, if *Q* has *q* elements, then any [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) *C* (usually assumed of [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) at least two) of the *N*-dimensional Hamming space over *Q* is called a **q-ary [code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code) of length N**; the elements of *C* are called **codewords**.[^o6][^o5] In the case where *C* is a [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of its Hamming space, it is called a [linear code](wikipedia://en/1866743).[^o6] A typical example of linear code is the [Hamming code](wikipedia://en/41226). Codes defined via a Hamming space necessarily have the same length for every codeword, so they are called [block codes](wikipedia://en/1134659) when it is necessary to distinguish them from [variable-length codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable-length_code) that are defined by unique factorization on a monoid.

The [Hamming distance](wikipedia://en/41227) endows a Hamming space with a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29), which is essential in defining basic notions of coding theory such as [error detecting and error correcting codes](wikipedia://en/10375).[^o6]

Hamming spaces over non-field alphabets have also been considered, especially over [finite rings](wikipedia://en/23706953) (most notably over [**Z**<sub>4</sub>](wikipedia://en/20087)) giving rise to [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) instead of vector spaces and [ring-linear codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring-linear_code) (identified with [submodules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule)) instead of linear codes. The typical metric used in this case the [Lee distance](wikipedia://en/17766039). There exist a [Gray isometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gray_isometry) between $\mathbb{Z}_2^{2m}$ (i.e. GF(2<sup>2m</sup>)) with the Hamming distance and $\mathbb{Z}_4^m$ (also denoted as GR(4,m)) with the Lee distance.[^o7][^o8][^o9]
