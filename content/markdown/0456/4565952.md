# Rabbit (cipher)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rabbit"}

**Rabbit** is a high-speed [stream cipher](wikipedia://en/49007) from 2003. The algorithm and [source code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source_code) was released in 2008 as [public domain software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_domain_software).

:::

## History {#History}

*Rabbit* was first presented[^o3] in February 2003 at the 10th FSE workshop. In May 2005, it was submitted to the [eSTREAM](wikipedia://en/2287686) project of the [ECRYPT](wikipedia://en/1635837) network.

Rabbit was designed by Martin Boesgaard, Mette Vesterager, Thomas Pedersen, Jesper Christiansen and Ove Scavenius.

The authors of the cipher have provided a full set of cryptanalytic white papers on the Cryptico home page.[^o4] It is also described in RFC 4503. Cryptico had [patents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patent) pending for the algorithm and for many years required a license fee for commercial use of the cipher which was waived for non-commercial uses. However, the algorithm was made free for any use on October 6, 2008.[^o5] Also the website states that the algorithm and implementation is [public domain software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_domain_software) and offers the [source code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source_code) free for download.[^o6]

## Functionality {#Functionality}

Rabbit uses a 128-bit key and a 64-bit initialization vector. The cipher was designed with high performance in software in mind, where fully optimized implementations achieve an encryption cost of up to 3.7 [cpb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycles_per_byte) on a Pentium 3, and of 9.7 cpb on an ARM7. However, the cipher also turns out to be very fast and compact in hardware.

The core component of the cipher is a bitstream generator which encrypts 128 message bits per iteration. The cipher's strength rests on a strong mixing of its inner state between two consecutive iterations. The mixing function is entirely based on arithmetical operations that are available on a modern processor, i.e., no [S-boxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substitution_box) or lookup tables are required to implement the cipher. The mixing function uses a g-function based on arithmetical squaring, and the [ARX operations](wikipedia://en/4594#Operations) – logical XOR, bit-wise rotation with fixed rotation amounts, and addition modulo 2<sup>32</sup>.

The g-function used in Rabbit – squaring a 32-bit number to produce a 64-bit number, and then combining the left half and the right half of that square number with xor, to produce a 32-bit result – provides much better results than using the 32 middle bits of that squared number (the [middle-square method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle-square_method)).[^o8]

## Security {#Security}

Rabbit claims 128-bit security against attackers whose target is one specific key. If, however, the attacker targets a large number of keys at once and does not really care which one he breaks, then the small IV size results in a reduced [security level](wikipedia://en/52729212) of 96 bit. This is due to generic TMD trade-off attacks.[^o10]

A small bias in the output of Rabbit exists,[^o11] resulting in a distinguisher with 2<sup>247</sup> complexity discovered by Jean-Philippe Aumasson in December 2006. Even though this distinguisher was improved to 2<sup>158</sup> in 2008,[^o12] it is not a threat to Rabbit's security because its complexity is significantly higher than the brute-force of the key space (2<sup>128</sup>).
