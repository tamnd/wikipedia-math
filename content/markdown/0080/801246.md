# Cocountable topology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cocountable topology"}

The **cocountable topology**, also known as the **countable complement topology**, is a [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) that can be defined on any [infinite set](wikipedia://en/142679) $X$. In this topology, a set is [open](wikipedia://en/39358) if its [complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_complement) in $X$ is either countable or equal to the entire set. Equivalently, the open sets consist of the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) and all subsets of $X$ whose complements are countable, a property known as [cocountability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocountable). The only [closed sets](wikipedia://en/47279) in this topology are $X$ itself and the countable subsets of $X$.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Let $X$ be an [infinite set](wikipedia://en/142679) and let $\mathcal{T}$ be the set of [subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsets) of $X$ such that

$$H \in \mathcal{T} \iff X \setminus H \mbox{ is countable, or}\, H = \varnothing$$

then $\mathcal{T}$ is the countable complement topology on $X$, and the [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $T = ( X , \mathcal{T} )$ is a countable complement space.[^o4]

Symbolically, the topology is typically written as

$$\mathcal{T} = \{H \subseteq X : H = \varnothing \mbox{ or } X \setminus H \mbox{ is countable} \}.$$

### Double pointed cocountable topology {#Double_pointed_cocountable_topology}

Let $X$ be an [uncountable set](wikipedia://en/32248). We define the topology $\mathcal{T}$ as all open sets whose complements are countable, along with $\varnothing$ and $X$ itself.[^o6]

### Cocountable extension topology {#Cocountable_extension_topology}

Let $X$ be the real line. Now let $\mathcal{T}_1$ be the [Euclidean topology](wikipedia://en/1482085) and $\mathcal{T}_2$ be the cocountable topology on $X$. The *cocountable extension topology* is the smallest topology generated by $\mathcal{T}_1 \cup \mathcal{T}_2$.[^o8]

:::

::: {#o10 .proof cue=heading name="Proof that cocountable topology is a topology"}

## Proof that cocountable topology is a topology {#Proof_that_cocountable_topology_is_a_topology}

By definition, the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) $\varnothing$ is an element of $\mathcal{T}$. Similarly, the entire set $X \in \mathcal{T}$, since the [complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_complement) of $X$ relative to itself is the empty set, which is [vacuously](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuously) countable.

Suppose $A, B \in \mathcal{T}$. Let $H = A \cap B$. Then

$$X \setminus H = X \setminus (A \cap B) = (X \setminus A) \cup (X \setminus B)$$

by [De Morgan's laws](wikipedia://en/64669#Set_theory). Since $A, B \in \mathcal{T}$, it follows that $X \setminus A$ and $X \setminus B$ are both countable. Because the countable union of countable sets is countable, $X \setminus H$ is also countable. Therefore, $H = A \cap B \in \mathcal{T}$, as its complement is countable.

Now let $\mathcal{U} \subseteq \mathcal{T}$. Then

$$X \setminus \left( \bigcup \mathcal{U} \right) = \bigcap_{U \in \mathcal{U}} (X \setminus U)$$

again by De Morgan's laws. For each $U \in \mathcal{U}$, $X \setminus U$ is countable. The countable intersection of countable sets is also countable (assuming $\mathcal{U}$ is countable), so $S \setminus \left( \bigcup \mathcal{U} \right)$ is countable. Thus, $\bigcup \mathcal{U} \in \mathcal{T}$.

Since all three [open set axioms](wikipedia://en/30450#Definition_via_open_sets) are met, $\mathcal{T}$ is a topology on $X$.[^o11]

:::

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Every set $X$ with the cocountable topology is [Lindelöf](wikipedia://en/310480), since every nonempty [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) omits only countably many points of $X$. It is also [T<sub>1</sub>](wikipedia://en/148721), as all singletons are closed.

If $X$ is an uncountable set, then any two nonempty open sets [intersect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_intersection), hence, the space is not [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637). However, in the cocountable topology all convergent sequences are eventually constant, so limits are unique. Since [compact sets](wikipedia://en/6042) in $X$ are finite subsets, all compact subsets are closed, another condition usually related to the Hausdorff separation axiom.

The cocountable topology on a countable set is the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology). The cocountable topology on an uncountable set is [hyperconnected](wikipedia://en/7556651), thus [connected](wikipedia://en/6233), [locally connected](wikipedia://en/6959754) and [pseudocompact](wikipedia://en/5075659), but neither [weakly countably compact](wikipedia://en/9861462) nor [countably metacompact](wikipedia://en/5074872), hence not compact.

:::

::: {#o15 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- Uncountable set: On any uncountable set, such as the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$, the cocountable topology is a proper subset of the [standard topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_topology). In this case, the topology is T<sub>1</sub> but not Hausdorff, first-countable, nor [metrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrizable).
- Countable set (possibly finite): If $X$ is countable, then every subset of $X$ has a countable complement. In this case, the cocountable topology is just the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology).
- Subspace topology: If $Y \subseteq X$ and $X$ carries the cocountable topology, then $Y$ inherits the [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926). This topology on $Y$ consists of the empty set, all of $Y$, and all subsets $U \subseteq Y$ such that $Y \setminus U$ is countable.
