# Motivic cohomology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Motivic cohomology"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), **motivic cohomology** is an invariant of [algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/248808) and of more general [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754). It is a type of [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060) related to [motives](wikipedia://en/430680) and includes the [Chow ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chow_ring) of algebraic cycles as a special case. Some of the deepest problems in algebraic geometry and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) are attempts to understand motivic cohomology.

:::

::: {#o3 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Motivic homology"}

## Motivic homology and cohomology {#Motivic_homology_and_cohomology}

Let *X* be a scheme of [finite type](wikipedia://en/35457783#finite) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *k*. A key goal of algebraic geometry is to compute the [Chow groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chow_ring) of *X*, because they give strong information about all subvarieties of *X*. The Chow groups of *X* have some of the formal properties of [Borel–Moore homology](wikipedia://en/6495737) in topology, but some things are missing. For example, for a closed subscheme *Z* of *X*, there is an [exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215) of Chow groups, the **localization sequence**

$$CH_i(Z) \to CH_i(X) \to CH_i(X-Z) \to 0,$$

whereas in topology this would be part of a [long exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_exact_sequence).

This problem was resolved by generalizing Chow groups to a bigraded family of groups, **(Borel–Moore) motivic homology groups** (which were first called **[higher Chow groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloch's_higher_Chow_groups)** by [Bloch](wikipedia://en/25794633)).[^n1][^n2] Namely, for every scheme *X* of finite type over a field *k* and integers *i* and *j*, we have an abelian group $H_{i}(X,\mathbb{Z}(j))$, with the usual Chow group being the special case

$$CH_i(X) \cong H_{2i}(X,\mathbb{Z}(i)).$$

For a closed subscheme *Z* of a scheme *X*, there is a long exact localization sequence for motivic homology groups, ending with the localization sequence for Chow groups:

$$\cdots\to H_{2i+1}(X-Z,\mathbb{Z}(i))\to H_{2i}(Z,\mathbb{Z}(i))\to H_{2i}(X,\mathbb{Z}(i))\to H_{2i}(X-Z,\mathbb{Z}(i))\to 0.$$

In fact, this is one of a family of four theories constructed by [Voevodsky](wikipedia://en/104482): motivic cohomology, motivic cohomology with compact support, Borel-Moore motivic homology (as above), and motivic homology with compact support.[^o6] These theories have many of the formal properties of the corresponding theories in topology. For example, the **motivic cohomology** groups $H^{i}(X,\mathbb{Z}(j))$ form a bigraded [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) for every scheme *X* of finite type over a field. When *X* is [smooth](wikipedia://en/35033819) of dimension *n* over *k*, there is a [Poincaré duality](wikipedia://en/540732) isomorphism

$$H^i(X,\mathbb{Z}(j))\cong H_{2n-i}(X,\mathbb{Z}(n-j)).$$

In particular, the Chow group *CH*<sup>*i*</sup>(*X*) of codimension-*i* cycles is isomorphic to $H^{2i}(X,\mathbb{Z}(i))$ when *X* is smooth over *k*.

The motivic cohomology $H^{i}(X,\mathbb{Z}(j))$ of a smooth scheme *X* over *k* is the [cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercohomology) of *X* in the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744) with coefficients in a certain [complex](wikipedia://en/138404) of [sheaves](wikipedia://en/245466) **Z**(j) on *X* (some properties are easier to prove using the [Nisnevich topology](wikipedia://en/14627005), but this gives the same motivic cohomology groups).[^n3] For example, **Z**(j) is zero for *j* \< 0, **Z**(0) is the constant sheaf **Z**, and **Z**(1) is isomorphic in the [derived category](wikipedia://en/985897) of *X* to *G*<sub>m</sub>\[−1\].[^n4] Here *G*<sub>m</sub> (the [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742)) denotes the sheaf of invertible [regular functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_function), and the shift \[−1\] means that this sheaf is viewed as a complex in degree 1.

The four versions of motivic homology and cohomology can be defined with coefficients in any abelian group. The theories with different coefficients are related by the [universal coefficient theorem](wikipedia://en/996107), as in topology.

## Relations to other cohomology theories {#Relations_to_other_cohomology_theories}

### Relation to K-theory {#Relation_to_K-theory}

By Bloch, [Lichtenbaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Lichtenbaum), [Friedlander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Friedlander), [Suslin](wikipedia://en/7111820), and Levine, there is a [spectral sequence](wikipedia://en/803894) from motivic cohomology to [algebraic K-theory](wikipedia://en/598500) for every smooth scheme *X* over a field, analogous to the [Atiyah-Hirzebruch spectral sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atiyah-Hirzebruch_spectral_sequence) in topology:

$$E_2^{pq}=H^p(X,\mathbb{Z}(-q/2)) \implies K_{-p-q}(X).$$

As in topology, the spectral sequence degenerates after [tensoring](wikipedia://en/30844) with the rationals.[^o11] For arbitrary schemes of finite type over a field (not necessarily smooth), there is an analogous spectral sequence from motivic homology to G-theory (the K-theory of [coherent sheaves](wikipedia://en/603780), rather than [vector bundles](wikipedia://en/276524)).

### Relation to Milnor K-theory {#Relation_to_Milnor_K-theory}

Motivic cohomology provides a rich invariant already for fields. (Note that a field *k* determines a scheme Spec(*k*), for which motivic cohomology is defined.) Although motivic cohomology $H^i(k, \mathbb{Z}(j))$ for fields *k* is far from understood in general, there is a description when *i* = *j*:

$$K_j^M(k) \cong H^j(k, \mathbb{Z}(j)),$$

where *K*<sub>*j*</sub><sup>M</sup>(*k*) is the *j*th [Milnor K-group](wikipedia://en/2914128) of *k*.[^n5] Since Milnor K-theory of a field is defined explicitly by generators and relations, this is a useful description of one piece of the motivic cohomology of *k*.

### Map to étale cohomology {#Map_to_étale_cohomology}

Let *X* be a smooth scheme over a field *k*, and let *m* be a positive integer which is invertible in *k*. Then there is a natural homomorphism (the **cycle map**) from motivic cohomology to [étale cohomology](wikipedia://en/396231):

$$H^i(X,\mathbb{Z}/m(j))\to H^i_{et}(X,\mathbb{Z}/m(j)),$$

where **Z**/*m*(*j*) on the right means the étale sheaf (μ<sub>*m*</sub>)<sup>⊗*j*</sup>, with μ<sub>*m*</sub> being the *m*th roots of unity. This generalizes the [cycle map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chow_ring#Cycle_maps) from the Chow ring of a smooth variety to étale cohomology.

A frequent goal in algebraic geometry or number theory is to compute motivic cohomology, whereas étale cohomology is often easier to understand. For example, if the base field *k* is the complex numbers, then étale cohomology coincides with [singular cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_cohomology) (with finite coefficients). A powerful result proved by Voevodsky, known as the **Beilinson-Lichtenbaum conjecture**, says that many motivic cohomology groups are in fact isomorphic to étale cohomology groups. This is a consequence of the [norm residue isomorphism theorem](wikipedia://en/27126857). Namely, the Beilinson-Lichtenbaum conjecture (Voevodsky's theorem) says that for a smooth scheme *X* over a field *k* and *m* a positive integer invertible in *k*, the cycle map

$$H^i(X,\mathbb{Z}/m(j))\to H^i_{et}(X,\mathbb{Z}/m(j))$$

is an isomorphism for all *j* ≥ *i* and is injective for all *j* ≥ *i* − 1.[^n6]

### Relation to motives {#Relation_to_motives}

For any field *k* and commutative ring *R*, Voevodsky defined an *R*-linear [triangulated category](wikipedia://en/1679700) called the **derived category of motives** over *k* with coefficients in *R*, DM(*k*; *R*). Each scheme *X* over *k* determines two objects in DM called the [motive](wikipedia://en/430680) of *X*, M(*X*), and the **compactly supported motive** of *X*, M<sup>c</sup>(*X*); the two are isomorphic if *X* is [proper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_scheme) over *k*.

One basic point of the derived category of motives is that the four types of motivic homology and motivic cohomology all arise as sets of morphisms in this category. To describe this, first note that there are **Tate motives** *R*(*j*) in DM(*k*; *R*) for all integers *j*, such that the motive of projective space is a direct sum of Tate motives:

$$M(\mathbf{P}^n_k)\cong \oplus_{j=0}^n R(j)[2j],$$

where *M* ↦ *M*\[1\] denotes the shift or "translation functor" in the triangulated category DM(*k*; *R*). In these terms, motivic cohomology (for example) is given by

$$H^i(X,R(j))\cong \text{Hom}_{DM(k; R)}(M(X),R(j)[i])$$

for every scheme *X* of finite type over *k*.

When the coefficients *R* are the rational numbers, a modern version of a conjecture by [Beilinson](wikipedia://en/10230613) predicts that the subcategory of compact objects in DM(k; **Q**) is equivalent to the bounded derived category of an [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063) MM(*k*), the category of **mixed motives** over *k*. In particular, the conjecture would imply that motivic cohomology groups can be identified with [Ext groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ext_group) in the category of mixed motives.[^n7] This is far from known. Concretely, Beilinson's conjecture would imply the **Beilinson-[Soulé](wikipedia://en/32705286) conjecture** that *H*<sup>*i*</sup>(X,**Q**(*j*)) is zero for *i* \< 0, which is known only in a few cases.

Conversely, a variant of the Beilinson-Soulé conjecture, together with Grothendieck's [standard conjectures](wikipedia://en/10470079) and Murre's conjectures on Chow motives, would imply the existence of an abelian category *MM*(*k*) as the heart of a [t-structure](wikipedia://en/1798923) on *DM*(*k*; **Q**).[^n8] More would be needed in order to identify Ext groups in *MM*(*k*) with motivic cohomology.

For *k* a subfield of the complex numbers, a candidate for the abelian category of mixed motives has been defined by Nori.[^o19] If a category *MM*(*k*) with the expected properties exists (notably that the Betti realization functor from *MM*(*k*) to **Q**-vector spaces is [faithful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faithful_functor)), then it must be equivalent to Nori's category.

## Applications to arithmetic geometry {#Applications_to_arithmetic_geometry}

Arithmetic geometry

### Values of L-functions {#Values_of_L-functions}

Let *X* be a smooth projective variety over a number field. The Bloch-Kato conjecture on [values of L-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Values_of_L-functions) predicts that the order of vanishing of an L-function of *X* at an integer point is equal to the rank of a suitable motivic cohomology group. This is one of the central problems of number theory, incorporating earlier conjectures by Deligne and Beilinson. The [Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer_conjecture) is a special case. More precisely, the conjecture predicts the leading coefficient of the L-function at an integer point in terms of [regulators](wikipedia://en/460227#Higher_regulators) and a [height pairing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Height_pairing) on motivic cohomology.

## History {#History}

The first clear sign of a possible generalization from Chow groups to a more general motivic cohomology theory for algebraic varieties was [Quillen](wikipedia://en/435519)'s definition and development of [algebraic K-theory](wikipedia://en/598500) (1973), generalizing the [Grothendieck group](wikipedia://en/1634790) *K*<sub>0</sub> of vector bundles. In the early 1980s, Beilinson and Soulé observed that [Adams operations](wikipedia://en/1226666) gave a splitting of algebraic K-theory tensored with the rationals; the summands are now called motivic cohomology (with rational coefficients). Beilinson and Lichtenbaum made influential conjectures predicting the existence and properties of motivic cohomology. Most but not all of their conjectures have now been proved.

Bloch's definition of higher Chow groups (1986) was the first integral (as opposed to rational) definition of [Borel-Moore](wikipedia://en/6495737) motivic homology for quasi-projective varieties over a field *k* (and hence motivic cohomology, in the case of smooth varieties). The definition of higher Chow groups of *X* is a natural generalization of the definition of Chow groups, involving algebraic cycles on the product of *X* with affine space which meet a set of hyperplanes (viewed as the faces of a [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781)) in the expected dimension.

In the 1990s, Voevodsky (building on his work with Suslin) defined the four types[^o23] of motivic homology and motivic cohomology for smooth schemes over a perfect field, along with a triangulated category of motives inside a very robust framework of [$\mathbb{A}^1$-homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/17346948).[^n9] Different constructions were also given by Hanamura and Levine. These three triangulated categories of motives are now known to be equivalent, by work of Levine, Ivorra, and Bondarko.

Voevodsky also defined a motivic cohomology for singular varieties[^n10] and used it in the proof of the [Bloch-Kato conjecture](wikipedia://en/27126857).[^o26] This has become known as [cdh](wikipedia://en/28299952#cdh_topology) motivic cohomology, as it sits in an Atiyah–Hirzebruch style spectral sequence calculating homotopy-invariant algebraic *K*-theory (the cdh-localization of algebraic *K*-theory), rather than algebraic *K*-theory itself.
