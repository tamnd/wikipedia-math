# Pairwise independence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pairwise independence"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), a **pairwise independent** collection of [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) is a set of random variables any two of which are [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence).[^o2] Any collection of [mutually independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_independence) random variables is pairwise independent, but some pairwise independent collections are not mutually independent. Pairwise independent random variables with finite [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) are [uncorrelated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncorrelated).

:::

A pair of random variables *X* and *Y* are **independent** [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the random vector (*X*, *Y*) with [joint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_distribution) [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) (CDF) $F_{X,Y}(x,y)$ satisfies

$$F_{X,Y}(x,y) = F_X(x) F_Y(y),$$

or equivalently, their joint density $f_{X,Y}(x,y)$ satisfies

$$f_{X,Y}(x,y) = f_X(x) f_Y(y).$$

That is, the joint distribution is equal to the product of the marginal distributions.[^o3]

Unless it is not clear in context, in practice the modifier "mutual" is usually dropped so that **independence** means **mutual independence**. A statement such as " *X*, *Y*, *Z* are independent random variables" means that *X*, *Y*, *Z* are mutually independent.

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence, as shown by the following example attributed to S. Bernstein.[^o6]

Suppose *X* and *Y* are two independent tosses of a [fair coin](wikipedia://en/9597100), where we designate 1 for heads and 0 for tails. Let the third random variable *Z* be equal to 1 if exactly one of those coin tosses resulted in "heads", and 0 otherwise (i.e., $Z = X \oplus Y$). Then jointly the triple (*X*, *Y*, *Z*) has the following [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/879637):

$$(X,Y,Z) = \begin{cases} (0,0,0) & \text{with probability}\ 1/4, \\ (0,1,1) & \text{with probability}\ 1/4, \\ (1,0,1) & \text{with probability}\ 1/4, \\ (1,1,0) & \text{with probability}\ 1/4. \end{cases}$$

Here the [marginal probability distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_probability_distribution) are identical: $f_X(0) = f_Y(0) = f_Z(0) = 1/2,$ and $f_X(1) = f_Y(1) = f_Z(1) = 1/2.$ The [bivariate distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bivariate_distribution) also agree: $f_{X,Y} = f_{X,Z} = f_{Y,Z},$ where $f_{X,Y}(0,0)=f_{X,Y}(0,1)=f_{X,Y}(1,0)=f_{X,Y}(1,1)=1/4.$

Since each of the pairwise joint distributions equals the product of their respective marginal distributions, the variables are pairwise independent:

- *X* and *Y* are independent, and
- *X* and *Z* are independent, and
- *Y* and *Z* are independent.

However, *X*, *Y*, and *Z* are **not** [mutually independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutually_independent#More_than_two_random_variables), since $f_{X,Y,Z}(x,y,z) \neq f_X(x)f_Y(y)f_Z(z),$ the left side equalling for example 1/4 for (*x*, *y*, *z*) = (0, 0, 0) while the right side equals 1/8 for (*x*, *y*, *z*) = (0, 0, 0). In fact, any of $\{X,Y,Z\}$ is completely determined by the other two (any of *X*, *Y*, *Z* is the [sum (modulo 2)](wikipedia://en/20087) of the others). That is as far from independence as random variables can get.

:::

## Probability of the union of pairwise independent events {#Probability_of_the_union_of_pairwise_independent_events}

Bounds on the [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) that the sum of [Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/199189) [random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variables) is at least one, commonly known as the [union bound](wikipedia://en/344887), are provided by the [Boole–Fréchet](wikipedia://en/35736414)[^o8][^o9] inequalities. While these bounds assume only [univariate](wikipedia://en/2398982) information, several bounds with knowledge of general [bivariate](wikipedia://en/879637) probabilities, have been proposed too. Denote by $\{{A}_i, i \in \{1,2,...,n\}\}$ a set of $n$ [Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/199189) events with [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) of occurrence $\mathbb{P}(A_{i})=p_i$ for each $i$. Suppose the [bivariate](wikipedia://en/879637) probabilities are given by $\mathbb{P}(A_{i} \cap A_{j})=p_{ij}$ for every pair of indices $(i,j)$. Kounias [^o10] derived the following [upper bound](wikipedia://en/42693):

$$\mathbb{P}(\displaystyle {\cup}_iA_{i}) \leq \displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^n p_{i}-\underset {j\in \{1,2,..,n\}}{\max} \sum_{i\neq j} p_{ij},$$

which subtracts the maximum weight of a [star](wikipedia://en/19310129) [spanning tree](wikipedia://en/455770) on a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) with $n$ nodes (where the edge weights are given by $p_{ij}$) from the sum of the [marginal](wikipedia://en/506077) probabilities $\sum_i p_i$. Hunter-Worsley[^o11][^o12] tightened this [upper bound](wikipedia://en/42693) by optimizing over $\tau \in T$ as follows:

$$\mathbb{P}(\displaystyle {\cup}_i A_{i}) \leq \displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^n p_{i}-\underset {\tau \in T}{\max}\sum_{(i,j) \in \tau} p_{ij},$$

where $T$ is the set of all [spanning trees](wikipedia://en/455770) on the graph. These bounds are not the [tightest](wikipedia://en/42693#Tight_bounds) possible with general [bivariates](wikipedia://en/879637) $p_{ij}$ even when [feasibility](wikipedia://en/9157119) is guaranteed as shown in Boros et.al.[^o13] However, when the variables are pairwise independent ($p_{ij}=p_ip_j$), Ramachandra—Natarajan [^o14] showed that the Kounias-Hunter-Worsley [^o15][^o16][^o17] bound is [tight](wikipedia://en/42693#Tight_bounds) by proving that the maximum probability of the union of events admits a [closed-form expression](wikipedia://en/585143) given as:

::: {#o18 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\max \mathbb{P}(\displaystyle {\cup}_i A_{i}) = \displaystyle \min\left(\sum_{i=1}^n p_i - p_n \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1} p_{i}\right),1\right) \tag{1}$$

:::

where the [probabilities](wikipedia://en/22934) are sorted in increasing order as $0 \leq p_1 \leq p_2 \leq \ldots \leq p_n \leq 1$. The [tight](wikipedia://en/42693#Tight_bounds) bound in (Eq. 1) depends only on the sum of the smallest $n-1$ [probabilities](wikipedia://en/22934) $\sum_{i=1}^{n-1} p_{i}$ and the largest probability $p_n$. Thus, while [ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorting) of the [probabilities](wikipedia://en/22934) plays a role in the derivation of the bound, the [ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorting) among the smallest $n-1$ [probabilities](wikipedia://en/22934) $\{p_1,p_2,\dots,p_{n-1}\}$ is inconsequential since only their sum is used.

### Comparison with the [Boole–Fréchet](wikipedia://en/35736414) [union bound](wikipedia://en/344887) {#Comparison_with_the_Boole–Fréchet_union_bound}

It is useful to compare the smallest bounds on the probability of the union with arbitrary [dependence](wikipedia://en/437701) and pairwise independence respectively. The [tightest](wikipedia://en/42693#Tight_bounds) [Boole–Fréchet](wikipedia://en/35736414) [upper](wikipedia://en/42693) [union bound](wikipedia://en/344887) (assuming only [univariate](wikipedia://en/2398982) information) is given as:

::: {#o20 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\displaystyle \max \mathbb{P}(\displaystyle {\cup}_i A_{i}) = \displaystyle \min\left(\sum_{i=1}^n p_{i},1\right) \tag{2}$$

:::

As shown in Ramachandra-Natarajan,[^o14] it can be easily verified that the ratio of the two [tight](wikipedia://en/42693#Tight_bounds) bounds in (Eq. 2) and (Eq. 1) is [upper bounded](wikipedia://en/42693) by $4/3$ where the maximum value of $4/3$ is attained when

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n-1} p_i = 1/2 \, , \quad p_n=1/2$$

where the [probabilities](wikipedia://en/22934) are sorted in increasing order as $0 \leq p_1 \leq p_2 \leq \dots \leq p_n \leq 1$. In other words, in the best-case scenario, the pairwise independence bound in (Eq. 1) provides an improvement of $25\%$ over the [univariate](wikipedia://en/2398982) bound in (Eq. 2).

::: {#o22 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

More generally, we can talk about *k*-wise independence, for any *k* ≥ 2. The idea is similar: a set of [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) is *k*-wise independent if every subset of size *k* of those variables is independent. *k*-wise independence has been used in theoretical computer science, where it was used to prove a theorem about the problem [MAXEkSAT](wikipedia://en/22762876).

*k*-wise independence is used in the proof that [k-independent hashing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-independent_hashing) functions are secure unforgeable [message authentication codes](wikipedia://en/567523).

:::
