# Star-free language

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Star-free language"}

In [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392) and [formal language theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_language_theory), a [regular language](wikipedia://en/25723) is said to be **star-free** if it can be described by a [regular expression](wikipedia://en/25717) constructed from the letters of the [alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabet_%28computer_science%29), the [empty word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empty_word), the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) symbol, all [boolean operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_operations_%28Boolean%29) – including [complementation](wikipedia://en/54347) – and [concatenation](wikipedia://en/64474) but no [Kleene star](wikipedia://en/16750).[^o2] The condition is equivalent to having [generalized star height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_star_height) zero.

:::

It turns out some class of [transformers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer_%28deep_learning%29) exactly corresponds to star-free languages[^o3].

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

All finite languages are star-free. But no having stars does not mean that we are stuck to finite languages. Indeed, we can use the complementation to build infinite languages. Actually, the language $\Sigma^*$ of *all* finite words over an alphabet $\Sigma$ is star-free because it is the complement of the empty set, $\Sigma^*=\bar{\emptyset}$.

Then, the language of words over the alphabet $\{a,\,b\}$ that do not have consecutive a's can be defined as $\overline{\Sigma^* aa \Sigma^*}$, first constructing the language of words consisting of $aa$ with an arbitrary prefix and suffix, and then taking its complement, which must be all words which do not contain the substring $aa$.

An example of a regular language which is not star-free is $(aa)^*$,[^o6] i.e. the language of strings consisting of an even number of "a". However, for $(ab)^*$ where $a \neq b$, the language can be defined as $\Sigma^* \setminus (b\Sigma^* \cup \Sigma^*a \cup \Sigma^*aa\Sigma^* \cup \Sigma^*bb\Sigma^* )$, taking the set of all words and removing from it words starting with $b$, ending in $a$ or containing $aa$ or $bb$. However, when $a = b$, this definition does not create $(aa)^*$.

:::

## Characterisations {#Characterisations}

### Via aperiod syntact monoids {#Via_aperiod_syntact_monoids}

[Marcel-Paul Schützenberger](wikipedia://en/2817337) characterized star-free languages as those with [aperiodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aperiodic_monoid) [syntactic monoids](wikipedia://en/670026).[^o9][^o10] Said differently, they are characterised as languages accepted by some [aperiodic finite-state automaton](wikipedia://en/17995149) (known as counter-free languages)[^o11].

### Logical characterisation {#Logical_characterisation}

They can also be characterized logically as languages definable in FO\[\<\], the [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) over the natural numbers with the less-than relation[^o13]. First-order variables denote positions in words and \< means that a position is strictly before another one. Here are some examples.

- The language $\Sigma^*$(all words) is captured by the tautology $\top$ (no constraints on the words)
- The language of that have no consecutive a's is defined by the FO\[\<\] formula $\forall x \forall y \, \Big( \big( a(x) \land x < y \land \neg \exists z (x < z \land z < y) \big) \implies \neg a(y) \Big)$ meaning for all positions $x$ and $y$, if $x$ contains letter $a$ and if $y$ is the position after $x$ ($y$ is after $x$ but there is no positions in between $x$ and $y$), then there is $y$ does not contain letter $a$.

They can also be characterized by formulas in [linear temporal logic](wikipedia://en/710307).[^o14] Here are some examples.

- Again, The language $\Sigma^*$ is captured by the tautology $\top$
- The language of that have no consecutive a's is defined by the formula in linear temporal logic $G(a \rightarrow X \lnot a)$ where is the meaning is as follows: always (operator $G$), if there is letter $a$ then at the next step (operator $X$) there is no letter $a$.

## Algorithms {#Algorithms}

### Membership {#Membership}

All star-free languages are in uniform [AC<sup>0</sup>](wikipedia://en/7400698). More precisely, given a star-free language $L$, deciding whether a word belongs to $L$ is in uniform AC<sup>0</sup>.

### Non-emptiness {#Non-emptiness}

It takes non-elementary time to decide whether a star-free language over two letters is empty. More precisely, the non-emptiness problem of starfree language is defined as follows.

- Input: a string in the symbols of $\{a,\,b\}$, empty set, concatenation, union, intersection, and complement.
- Output: Whether this language contains any element.

This problem is [decidable](wikipedia://en/913118), but only in [nonelementary time](wikipedia://en/2284654). As immediate corollaries, it is decidable but nonelementary to decide whether two starfree languages are equal, are disjoint, or contain one another.[^o19]
