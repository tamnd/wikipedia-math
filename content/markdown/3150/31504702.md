# Cellular algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cellular algebra"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **cellular algebra** is a [finite-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite-dimensional) [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) *A* with a distinguished **cellular basis** which is particularly well-adapted to studying the [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200) of *A*.

:::

## History {#History}

The cellular algebras discussed in this article were introduced in a 1996 paper of Graham and [Lehrer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Lehrer).[^o3] However, the terminology had previously been used by [Weisfeiler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Weisfeiler) and Lehman in the Soviet Union in the 1960s, to describe what are also known as [coherent algebras](wikipedia://en/55268440). [^o4][^o5][^o6]

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Let $R$ be a fixed [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) with unit. In most applications this is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), but this is not needed for the definitions. Let also $A$ be an $R$-[algebra](wikipedia://en/191788).

### The concrete definition {#The_concrete_definition}

A *cell datum* for $A$ is a tuple $(\Lambda,i,M,C)$ consisting of

- A finite [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) $\Lambda$.
- A $R$-[linear](wikipedia://en/474118) [anti-automorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-automorphism) $i:A\to A$ with $i^2 = \operatorname{id}_A$.
- For every $\lambda\in\Lambda$ a [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566) finite set $M(\lambda)$ of indices.
- An [injective](wikipedia://en/45196) map

$$C: \dot{\bigcup}_{\lambda\in\Lambda} M(\lambda)\times M(\lambda) \to A$$

The images under this map are notated with an upper index $\lambda\in\Lambda$ and two lower indices $\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t}\in M(\lambda)$ so that the typical element of the image is written as $C_\mathfrak{st}^\lambda$.

and satisfying the following conditions:

1. The image of $C$ is a $R$-[basis](wikipedia://en/326454) of $A$.
1. $i(C_\mathfrak{st}^\lambda)=C_\mathfrak{ts}^\lambda$ for all elements of the basis.
1. For every $\lambda\in\Lambda$, $\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t}\in M(\lambda)$ and every $a\in A$ the equation

$$aC_\mathfrak{st}^\lambda \equiv \sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in M(\lambda)} r_a(\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{s}) C_\mathfrak{ut}^\lambda \mod A(<\lambda)$$

with coefficients $r_a(\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{s})\in R$ depending only on $a$, $\mathfrak{u}$ and $\mathfrak{s}$ but not on $\mathfrak{t}$. Here $A(<\lambda)$ denotes the $R$-span of all basis elements with upper index strictly smaller than $\lambda$.

This definition was originally given by Graham and Lehrer who invented cellular algebras.[^o3]

### The more abstract definition {#The_more_abstract_definition}

Let $i:A\to A$ be an anti-automorphism of $R$-algebras with $i^2 = \operatorname{id}$ (just called "[involution](wikipedia://en/293450)" from now on).

A *cell ideal* of $A$ with respect to $i$ is a two-sided [ideal](wikipedia://en/191788#Subalgebras_and_ideals) $J\subseteq A$ that satisfies the following.

1. $$i(J)=J.$$
1. There is a left ideal $\Delta\subseteq J$ that is [free](wikipedia://en/326454) as a $R$-[module](wikipedia://en/276410) and an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828)

$$\alpha: \Delta\otimes_R i(\Delta) \to J$$

of $A$-$A$-[bimodules](wikipedia://en/492612) such that $\alpha$ and $i$ are compatible in the sense that

$$\forall x,y\in\Delta: i(\alpha(x\otimes i(y))) = \alpha(y\otimes i(x))$$

A *cell chain* for $A$ with respect to $i$ is defined as a [direct decomposition](wikipedia://en/2084687)

$$A=\bigoplus_{k=1}^m U_k$$

into free $R$-submodules such that

1. $$i(U_k)=U_k$$
1. $J_k:=\bigoplus_{j=1}^k U_j$ is a two-sided ideal of $A$
1. $J_k/J_{k-1}$ is a cell ideal of $A/J_{k-1}$ w.r.t. the induced involution.

Now $(A,i)$ is called a cellular algebra if it has a cell chain. One can show that the two definitions are equivalent.[^o11] Every basis gives rise to cell chains (one for each [topological ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_ordering) of $\Lambda$) and choosing a basis of every left ideal $\Delta/J_{k-1}\subseteq J_k/J_{k-1}$ one can construct a corresponding cell basis for $A$.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Polynomial examples {#Polynomial_examples}

$R[x]/(x^n)$ is cellular. A cell datum is given by $i = \operatorname{id}$ and

- $\Lambda := \lbrace 0,\ldots,n-1\rbrace$ with the reverse of the natural ordering.
- $$M(\lambda) := \lbrace 1\rbrace$$
- $$C_{11}^\lambda := x^\lambda$$

A cell-chain in the sense of the second, abstract definition is given by

$$0 \subseteq (x^{n-1}) \subseteq (x^{n-2}) \subseteq \ldots \subseteq (x^1) \subseteq (x^0)=R[x]/(x^n)$$

### Matrix examples {#Matrix_examples}

$R^{\,d \times d}$ is cellular. A cell datum is given by $i(A)=A^T$ and

- $$\Lambda := \lbrace 1 \rbrace$$
- $$M(1) := \lbrace 1,\dots,d\rbrace$$
- For the basis one chooses $C_{st}^1 := E_{st}$ the standard [matrix units](wikipedia://en/1239237), i.e. $C_{st}^1$ is the matrix with all entries equal to zero except the (*s*,*t*)-th entry which is equal to 1.

A cell-chain (and in fact the only cell chain) is given by

$$0 \subseteq R^{\!d \times d}$$

In some sense all cellular algebras "interpolate" between these two extremes by arranging matrix-algebra-like pieces according to the [poset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poset) $\Lambda$.

::: {#o17 .example cue=heading name="Further examples"}

### Further examples {#Further_examples}

Modulo minor technicalities all [Iwahori–Hecke algebras](wikipedia://en/521808) of finite type are cellular with respect to the involution that maps the [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005) as $T_w\mapsto T_{w^{-1}}$.[^o18] This includes for example the [integral](wikipedia://en/14563) [group algebra](wikipedia://en/349223) of the [symmetric groups](wikipedia://en/28901) as well as all other [finite](wikipedia://en/323707) [Weyl groups](wikipedia://en/296332).

A basic Brauer tree algebra over a field is cellular [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the Brauer tree is a straight line (with arbitrary number of exceptional vertices).[^o11]

Further examples include q-[Schur algebras](wikipedia://en/26339669), the [Brauer algebra](wikipedia://en/3875858), the [Temperley–Lieb algebra](wikipedia://en/2802848), the [Birman–Murakami–Wenzl algebra](wikipedia://en/22180498), the blocks of the Bernstein–Gelfand–Gelfand category $\mathcal{O}$ of a [semisimple Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/1337587).[^o11]

## Representations {#Representations}

### Cell modules and the invariant bilinear form {#Cell_modules_and_the_invariant_bilinear_form}

Assume $A$ is cellular and $(\Lambda,i,M,C)$ is a cell datum for $A$. Then one defines the *cell module* $W(\lambda)$ as the free $R$-module with basis $\lbrace C_\mathfrak{s} \mid \mathfrak{s} \in M(\lambda)\rbrace$ and multiplication

$$aC_\mathfrak{s} := \sum_{\mathfrak{u}} r_a(\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{s}) C_\mathfrak{u}$$

where the coefficients $r_a(\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{s})$ are the same as above. Then $W(\lambda)$ becomes an $A$-left module.

These modules generalize the [Specht modules](wikipedia://en/5122533) for the symmetric group and the Hecke-algebras of type A.

There is a canonical bilinear form $\phi_\lambda: W(\lambda)\times W(\lambda)\to R$ which satisfies

$$C_\mathfrak{st}^\lambda C_\mathfrak{uv}^\lambda \equiv \phi_\lambda(C_\mathfrak{t},C_\mathfrak{u}) C_\mathfrak{sv}^\lambda \mod A(<\lambda)$$

for all indices $s,t,u,v\in M(\lambda)$.

One can check that $\phi_\lambda$ is symmetric in the sense that

$$\phi_\lambda(x,y) = \phi_\lambda(y,x)$$

for all $x,y\in W(\lambda)$ and also $A$-invariant in the sense that

$$\phi_\lambda(i(a)x,y)=\phi_\lambda(x,ay)$$

for all $a\in A$,$x,y\in W(\lambda)$.

### Simple modules {#Simple_modules}

Assume for the rest of this section that the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$ is a field. With the information contained in the invariant bilinear forms one can easily list all [simple](wikipedia://en/29430) $A$-modules:

Let $\Lambda_0:=\lbrace \lambda\in\Lambda \mid \phi_\lambda\neq 0\rbrace$ and define $L(\lambda):=W(\lambda)/\operatorname{rad}(\phi_\lambda)$ for all $\lambda\in\Lambda_0$. Then all $L(\lambda)$ are [absolute simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_irreducible) $A$-modules and every simple $A$-module is one of these.

These theorems appear already in the original paper by Graham and Lehrer.[^o3]

## Properties of cellular algebras {#Properties_of_cellular_algebras}

### Persistence properties {#Persistence_properties}

- [Tensor products](wikipedia://en/372395) of finitely many cellular $R$-algebras are cellular.
- A $R$-algebra $A$ is cellular if and only if its [opposite algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposite_algebra) $A^{\text{op}}$ is.
- If $A$ is cellular with cell-datum $(\Lambda,i,M,C)$ and $\Phi\subseteq\Lambda$ is an [ideal](wikipedia://en/530559) (a downward [closed subset](wikipedia://en/47279)) of the poset $\Lambda$ then $A(\Phi):=\sum RC_\mathfrak{st}^\lambda$ (where the sum runs over $\lambda\in\Lambda$ and $s,t\in M(\lambda)$) is a two-sided, $i$-invariant ideal of $A$ and the [quotient](wikipedia://en/184635#For_algebras_over_a_ring) $A/A(\Phi)$ is cellular with cell datum $(\Lambda\setminus\Phi,i,M,C)$ (where *i* denotes the induced involution and *M*, *C* denote the restricted mappings).
- If $A$ is a cellular $R$-algebra and $R\to S$ is a unitary [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) of commutative rings, then the [extension of scalars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_of_scalars) $S\otimes_R A$ is a cellular $S$-algebra.
- [Direct products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_product_of_rings) of finitely many cellular $R$-algebras are cellular.

If $R$ is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) then there is a [converse](wikipedia://en/155941) to this last point:

- If $(A,i)$ is a finite-dimensional $R$-algebra with an involution and $A=A_1\oplus A_2$ a decomposition in two-sided, $i$-invariant ideals, then the following are equivalent:

<!-- -->

1. $(A,i)$ is cellular.
1. $(A_1,i)$ and $(A_2,i)$ are cellular.

<!-- -->

- Since in particular all [blocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_of_a_ring) of $A$ are $i$-invariant if $(A,i)$ is cellular, an immediate [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865) is that a finite-dimensional $R$-algebra is cellular w.r.t. $i$ if and only if all blocks are $i$-invariant and cellular w.r.t. $i$.
- [Tits' deformation theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tits'_deformation_theorem) for cellular algebras: Let $A$ be a cellular $R$-algebra. Also let $R\to k$ be a unitary homomorphism into a field $k$ and $K:=\operatorname{Quot}(R)$ the [quotient field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_field) of $R$. Then the following holds: If $kA$ is semisimple, then $KA$ is also semisimple.

If one further assumes $R$ to be a [local domain](wikipedia://en/153391), then additionally the following holds:

- If $A$ is cellular w.r.t. $i$ and $e\in A$ is an [idempotent](wikipedia://en/361940) such that $i(e)=e$, then the algebra $eAe$ is cellular.

### Other properties {#Other_properties}

Assuming that $R$ is a field (though a lot of this can be generalized to arbitrary rings, [integral domains](wikipedia://en/15462), [local rings](wikipedia://en/153391) or at least [discrete valuation rings](wikipedia://en/848633)) and $A$ is cellular w.r.t. the involution $i$. Then the following hold

- $A$ is split, i.e. all simple modules are [absolutely irreducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_irreducible).
- The following are equivalent:[^o3]

<!-- -->

1. $A$ is [semisimple](wikipedia://en/3876151).
1. $A$ is split semisimple.
1. $\forall\lambda\in\Lambda: W(\lambda)$ is simple.
1. $\forall\lambda\in\Lambda: \phi_\lambda$ is [nondegenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondegenerate).

<!-- -->

- The [Cartan matrix](wikipedia://en/1146433) $C_A$ of $A$ is [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474) and [positive definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix).
- The following are equivalent:[^o26]

<!-- -->

1. $A$ is [quasi-hereditary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-hereditary_algebra) (i.e. its [module category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Module_category) is a [highest-weight category](wikipedia://en/30001045)).
1. $$\Lambda=\Lambda_0.$$
1. All cell chains of $(A,i)$ have the same length.
1. All cell chains of $(A,j)$ have the same length where $j:A\to A$ is an arbitrary involution w.r.t. which $A$ is cellular.
1. $$\det(C_A)=1.$$

<!-- -->

- If $A$ is [Morita equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morita_equivalent) to $B$ and the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) of $R$ is not two, then $B$ is also cellular w.r.t. a suitable involution. In particular $A$ is cellular (to some involution) if and only if its basic algebra is.[^o27]
- Every idempotent $e\in A$ is equivalent to $i(e)$, i.e. $Ae\cong Ai(e)$. If $\operatorname{char}(R) \neq 2$ then in fact every [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) contains an $i$-invariant idempotent.[^o11]
