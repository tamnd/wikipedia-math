# Higher-order statistics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Higher-order statistics"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the term **higher-order statistics** (**HOS**) refers to functions which use the third or higher power of a [sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_%28statistics%29), as opposed to more conventional techniques of lower-order statistics, which use constant, linear, and quadratic terms (zeroth, first, and second powers). The third and higher [moments](wikipedia://en/368684), as used in the [skewness](wikipedia://en/28212) and [kurtosis](wikipedia://en/16848), are examples of HOS, whereas the first and second moments, as used in the [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612) (first), and [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) (second) are examples of low-order statistics. HOS are particularly used in the estimation of [shape parameters](wikipedia://en/9210865), such as skewness and kurtosis, as when measuring the deviation of a distribution from the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462).

:::

In [statistical theory](wikipedia://en/27579), one long-established approach to higher-order statistics, for univariate and multivariate distributions is through the use of [cumulants](wikipedia://en/359684) and joint cumulants.[^o2] In [time series analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_series_analysis), the extension of these is to higher order spectra, for example the [bispectrum](wikipedia://en/2496000) and [trispectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trispectrum).

An alternative to the use of HOS and higher moments is to instead use [L-moments](wikipedia://en/24350828), which are linear statistics (linear combinations of [order statistics](wikipedia://en/160986)), and thus more robust than HOS.
