# Peter Schönemann

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Peter Schönemann"}

**Peter Hans Schönemann** (July 15, 1929 – April 7, 2010) was a German-born [psychometrician](wikipedia://en/24982) and [statistical](wikipedia://en/26685) expert. He was [professor emeritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor_emeritus) in the Department of [Psychological Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology) at [Purdue University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_University). His research interests included [multivariate statistics](wikipedia://en/19384), [multidimensional scaling](wikipedia://en/398786) and measurement, [quantitative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantitative_property) [behavior genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavior_genetics), [test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_%28assessment%29) [theory](wikipedia://en/30746) and mathematical tools for [social scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_scientists). He published around 90 papers dealing mainly with the subjects of [psychometrics](wikipedia://en/24982) and mathematical scaling. Schönemann's influences included [Louis Guttman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Guttman), [Lee Cronbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Cronbach), [Oscar Kempthorne](wikipedia://en/23146939) and [Henry Kaiser](wikipedia://en/15714607).

:::

Schönemann was a persistent critic of what he considered to be scientifically sanctioned [racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racism) in [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology). In particular, he claimed that (1) [Arthur Jensen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Jensen) and others routinely confuse the first [principal component](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_component) (PC1) with *[g](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_intelligence_factor)* as [Charles Spearman](wikipedia://en/1290799) defined it, and that (2) the high IQ heritability estimates reported in the literature derive from restrictive formal models whose underlying assumptions are rarely tested and usually violated by the data.[^o3][^o4][^o5]

Schönemann died on April 7, 2010.[^o6]

## Notable work {#Notable_work}

Schönemann's PhD thesis "A solution of the orthogonal Procrustes problem with applications to orthogonal and oblique rotation," proposed a solution to the [orthogonal Procrustes problem](wikipedia://en/13548016). Other Schönemann papers include "A generalized solution of the orthogonal Procrustes problem",[^o9] "The minimum average correlation between equivalent sets of uncorrelated factors",[^o10] and "Some new results on [factor](wikipedia://en/253492) [indeterminacy](wikipedia://en/15890311)"[^o11] co-authored with M.M. Wang. Schönemann wrote also numerous book chapters, including the "Psychometrics of [Intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence)" chapter in *Encyclopedia of Social Measurement* and the "Heritability" chapter in *Encyclopedia of Human Intelligence*.[^o12]

## *g* theory {#g_theory}

[Spearman's hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spearman's_hypothesis) asserts a correlation between the g-loadedness of IQ tests and measures of their heritability, a concept put to work in [Arthur Jensen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Jensen)'s discussion of black—white race differences from the 1980s. Schönemann regarded this work as resting on a conceptual confusion.

Schönemann argued for the non-existence of [psychometric *g*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_intelligence_factor). He wrote that there is a fundamental difference between *g*, first defined by [Charles Spearman](wikipedia://en/1290799) as a latent one-dimensional variable that accounts for all correlations among any [intelligence tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_tests), and a first principal component (PC1) of a positive [correlation matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_matrix). Spearman's tetrad difference equation states a necessary condition for such a *g* to exist.[^o14] The important proviso for Spearman's claim that such a *g* qualifies as an "objective definition" of "intelligence", is that all correlation matrices of "intelligence tests" must satisfy this necessary condition, not just one or two, because they are all samples of a universe of tests that are subject to the same *g*. Schönemann argued that this condition is routinely violated by all correlation matrices of reasonable size, and thus, such a *g* does not exist.[^o15]

## Twin studies {#Twin_studies}

In a number of publications, Schönemann argued that the [statistical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical) [heritability](wikipedia://en/155624) estimates used in most [twin studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_studies) rest on restrictive assumptions which are usually not tested, and if they are, often are found to be violated by the data.[^o17] He argued that this was true for the [monozygotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monozygotic) [twins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twins) raised apart vs. together (MZT) studies (Burt, Shields, Jinks and Fulker, Bouchard) as well as for the more widely used MZT vs [dizygotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dizygotic) twins raised together studies.[^o18] For example, the narrow heritability of responses to the question "did you have your back rubbed" work out to 0.92 heritable for males and 0.21 heritable for females. Using the statistical models published in Loehlin and Nichols (1976) the question "Did you wear sunglasses after dark?" is 130% heritable for males and 103% for females.[^o3][^o19]
