# A. A. Albert

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="A. A. Albert"}

**Abraham Adrian Albert** (November 9, 1905 – June 6, 1972) was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902).[^o3] In 1939, he received the American Mathematical Society's [Cole Prize](wikipedia://en/1372177) in Algebra for his work on [Riemann matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_matrices).[^o4] He is best known for his work on the [Albert–Brauer–Hasse–Noether theorem](wikipedia://en/23502488) on [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [division algebras](wikipedia://en/26549625) over [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) and as the developer of [Albert algebras](wikipedia://en/9153180), which are also known as [exceptional](wikipedia://en/5280515) [Jordan algebras](wikipedia://en/936371).

:::

## Professional overview {#Professional_overview}

A [first generation American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigrant_generations#First_generation), he was born in [Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago) and most associated with that city. He received his [Bachelor of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science) in 1926, [Masters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master's_degree) in 1927, and [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) in 1928, at the age of 22. All degrees were obtained from the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago). He married around the same time as his graduation. He spent his postdoctoral year at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) and then from 1929 to 1931 he was an instructor at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University). During this period he worked on [Abelian varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_varieties) and their [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569) algebras. He returned to Princeton for the opening year of the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) in 1933-34 and spent another year in Princeton in 1961–62 as the first Director of the [Communications Research Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Defense_Analyses#Center_for_Communications_and_Computing) of the [Institute for Defense Analyses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Defense_Analyses) (IDA). He later served on the Board of Trustees of IDA 1969–1972.[^o6]

From 1931 to 1972, he served on the mathematics faculty at the University of Chicago, where he became chair of the Mathematics Department in 1958 and Dean of the Physical Sciences Division in 1961.

As a research mathematician, he is primarily known for his work as one of the principal developers of the theory of [linear associative algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_associative_algebra) and as a pioneer in the development of linear non-[associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) [algebras](wikipedia://en/191788), although all of this grew out of his work on endomorphism algebras of Abelian varieties.

As an [applied mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_mathematician), he also did work for the military during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) and thereafter. One of his most notable achievements was his groundbreaking work on [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432). He prepared a manuscript, "Some Mathematical Aspects of Cryptography", for his invited address at a meeting of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822) in November 1941. The theory that developed from this work can be seen in [digital communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_communications) technologies.

After WWII, he became a forceful advocate favoring government support for research in mathematics on a par with physical sciences. He served on policy-making bodies at the [Office of Naval Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Naval_Research), the [United States National Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Research_Council), and the [National Science Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation) that funneled research grants into mathematics, giving many young mathematicians career opportunities previously unavailable. Due to his success in helping to give mathematical research a sound financial footing, he earned the title of "statesman for mathematics". Albert was elected a Fellow of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences) in 1968.[^o7]
