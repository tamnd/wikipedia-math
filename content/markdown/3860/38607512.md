# Delone set

*Image: Metric epsilon-net.svg.* The red points form part of an $\varepsilon$-net for the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), where $\varepsilon$ is the radius of the large yellow disks. The blue disks of half the radius are [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359), and the yellow disks together cover the whole plane, satisfying the two definitional requirements on an $\varepsilon$-net.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Delone set"}

In the mathematical theory of [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018), **$\varepsilon$-nets**, **$\varepsilon$-packings**, **$\varepsilon$-coverings**, **uniformly discrete sets**, **relatively dense sets**, and **Delone sets** (named after [Boris Delone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Delone)) are several closely related definitions of well-spaced [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) of points, and the **packing radius** and **covering radius** of these sets measure how well-spaced they are. These sets have applications in [coding theory](wikipedia://en/321869), [approximation algorithms](wikipedia://en/563105), and the theory of [quasicrystals](wikipedia://en/25350).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

If $(M, d)$ is a metric space, and $X$ is a subset of $M$, then the **packing radius**, $r$, of $X$ is half of the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) of distances between distinct members of $X$. [Open balls](wikipedia://en/160556) of radius $r$ centered at the points of $X$ will all be disjoint from each other. The **covering radius**, $R$, of $X$ is the smallest distance such that every point of $M$ is within distance $R$ of at least one point in $X$; that is, $R$ is the smallest radius such that [closed balls](wikipedia://en/160556) of that radius centered at the points of $X$ have all of $M$ as their union.

An **$\varepsilon$-packing** is a set $X$ of packing radius $r \ge \varepsilon/2$ (equivalently, minimum distance $\ge \varepsilon$), an **$\varepsilon$-covering** is a set $X$ of covering radius $R \le \varepsilon$, and an **$\varepsilon$-net** is a set that is both an $\varepsilon$-packing and an $\varepsilon$-covering ($\varepsilon/2 \le r \le R \le \varepsilon$).

A set is **uniformly discrete** if it has a nonzero packing radius ($0 < r$), and **relatively dense** if it has a finite covering radius ($R < \infty$).

A **Delone set** is a set that is both uniformly discrete and relatively dense ($0 < r \le R < \infty$). Thus every $\varepsilon$-net is Delone, but not vice versa.[^o4][^o5]

:::

## Construction of *ε*-nets {#Construction_of_ε-nets}

As the most restrictive of the definitions above, $\varepsilon$-nets are at least as difficult to construct as $\varepsilon$-packings, $\varepsilon$-coverings, and Delone sets. However, whenever the points of $M$ have a [well-ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-ordering), [transfinite induction](wikipedia://en/30461) shows that it is possible to construct an $\varepsilon$-net $N$, by including in $N$ every point for which the infimum of distances to the set of earlier points in the ordering is at least $\varepsilon$. For finite sets of points in a Euclidean space of bounded dimension, each point may be tested in constant time by mapping it to a grid of cells of diameter $\varepsilon$, and using a [hash table](wikipedia://en/13833) to test which nearby cells already contain points of $N$; thus, in this case, an $\varepsilon$-net can be constructed in [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time).[^o7][^o8]

For more general finite or [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) metric spaces, an alternative algorithm of [Teo Gonzalez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teofilo_F._Gonzalez) based on the [farthest-first traversal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farthest-first_traversal) can be used to construct a finite $\varepsilon$-net. This algorithm initializes the net $N$ to be empty, and then repeatedly adds to $N$ the farthest point in $M$ from $N$, breaking ties arbitrarily and stopping when all points of $M$ are within distance $\varepsilon$ of $N$.[^o9] In [spaces of bounded doubling dimension](wikipedia://en/30152339), Gonzalez' algorithm can be implemented in $O(n \log n)$ time for point sets with a polynomial ratio between their farthest and closest distances, and approximated in the same time bound for arbitrary point sets.[^o10]

## Applications {#Applications}

### Coding theory {#Coding_theory}

In the theory of [error-correcting codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error-correcting_code), the metric space containing a [block code](wikipedia://en/1134659) $C$ consists of strings of a fixed length, say $n$, taken over an alphabet of size $q$ (can be thought of as [vectors](wikipedia://en/879358)), with the [Hamming metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamming_metric). This space is denoted by $\mathcal{A}_q^n.$ The covering radius and packing radius of this metric space are related to the code's ability to correct errors. An example is provided by the [Berlekamp switching game](wikipedia://en/64510569).

### Approximation algorithms {#Approximation_algorithms}

(Har-Peled Raichel 2013) describe an algorithmic paradigm that they call "net and prune" for designing [approximation algorithms](wikipedia://en/563105) for certain types of geometric optimization problems defined on sets of points in [Euclidean spaces](wikipedia://en/9697). An algorithm of this type works by performing the following steps:

1. Choose a random point $p$ from the point set, find its nearest neighbor $q$, and set $\varepsilon$ to the distance between $p$ and $q$.
1. Test whether $\varepsilon$ is (approximately) bigger than or smaller than the optimal solution value (using a technique specific to the particular optimization problem being solved)
   - If it is bigger, remove from the input the points whose closest neighbor is farther than $\varepsilon$
   - If it is smaller, construct an $\varepsilon$-net $N$, and remove from the input the points that are not in $N$.

In both cases, the expected number of remaining points decreases by a constant factor, so the time is dominated by the testing step. As they show, this paradigm can be used to construct fast approximation algorithms for [k-center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-center) clustering, finding a pair of points with median distance, and several related problems.

A hierarchical system of nets, called a *net-tree*, may be used in [spaces of bounded doubling dimension](wikipedia://en/30152339) to construct [well-separated pair decompositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-separated_pair_decomposition), [geometric spanners](wikipedia://en/11844294), and approximate [nearest neighbors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nearest_neighbor_search).[^o10][^o16]

### Crystallography {#Crystallography}

For points in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), a set $X$ is a [Meyer set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meyer_set) if it is relatively dense and its [difference set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_difference) $X - X$ is uniformly discrete. Equivalently, $X$ is a Meyer set if both $X$ and $X - X$ are Delone sets. Meyer sets are named after [Yves Meyer](wikipedia://en/2096465), who introduced them (with a different but equivalent definition based on [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147)) as a mathematical model for [quasicrystals](wikipedia://en/25350). They include the point sets of [lattices](wikipedia://en/469246), [Penrose tilings](wikipedia://en/26611936), and the [Minkowski sums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_sum) of these sets with finite sets.[^o18]

The [Voronoi cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voronoi_cell) of symmetric Delone sets form [space-filling polyhedra](wikipedia://en/58607583) called [plesiohedra](wikipedia://en/53396858).[^o19]
