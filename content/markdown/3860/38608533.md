# Ring of polynomial functions

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ring of polynomial functions"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **ring of polynomial functions** on a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V* over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *k* gives a coordinate-free analog of a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065). It is denoted by *k*\[*V*\]. If *V* is [finite dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) and is viewed as an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808), then *k*\[*V*\] is precisely the [coordinate ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_ring) of *V*.

:::

The explicit definition of the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) can be given as follows. Given a polynomial ring $k[t_1, \dots, t_n]$, we can view $t_i$ as a coordinate function on $k^n$; i.e., $t_i(x) = x_i$ where $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n).$ This suggests the following: given a vector space *V*, let *k*\[*V*\] be the [commutative *k*-algebra](wikipedia://en/2112#Definition) generated by the [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) $V^*$, which is a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of the ring of all [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) $V \to k$. If we fix a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for *V* and write $t_i$ for its dual basis, then *k*\[*V*\] consists of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) in $t_i$.

If *k* is infinite, then *k*\[*V*\] is the [symmetric algebra](wikipedia://en/654098) of the dual space $V^*$.

In applications, one also defines *k*\[*V*\] when *V* is defined over some [subfield](wikipedia://en/11634) of *k* (e.g., *k* is the [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) field and *V* is a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) vector space.) The same definition still applies.

Throughout the article, for simplicity, the base field *k* is assumed to be infinite.

## Relation with polynomial ring {#Relation_with_polynomial_ring}

Let $A=K[x]$ be the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all polynomials over a field *K* and *B* be the set of all polynomial functions in one variable over *K*. Both *A* and *B* are algebras over *K* given by the standard multiplication and addition of polynomials and functions. We can map each $f$ in *A* to $\hat{f}$ in *B* by the rule $\hat{f}(t) = f(t)$. A routine check shows that the mapping $f \mapsto \hat{f}$ is a [homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_homomorphism) of the algebras *A* and *B*. This homomorphism is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) if and only if *K* is an infinite field. For example, if *K* is a finite field then let $p(x) = \prod\limits_{t \in K} (x-t)$. *p* is a nonzero polynomial in *K*\[*x*\], however $p(t) = 0$ for all *t* in *K*, so $\hat{p} = 0$ is the zero function and our homomorphism is not an isomorphism (and, actually, the algebras are not isomorphic, since the algebra of polynomials is infinite while that of polynomial functions is finite).

If *K* is infinite then choose a polynomial *f* such that $\hat{f} = 0$. We want to show this implies that $f = 0$. Let $\deg f = n$ and let $t_0,t_1,\dots,t_n$ be *n*+1 distinct elements of *K*. Then $f(t_i) = 0$ for $0 \le i \le n$ and by [Lagrange interpolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange_interpolation) we have $f = 0$. Hence the mapping $f \mapsto \hat{f}$ is [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective). Since this mapping is clearly [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective), it is [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) and thus an algebra isomorphism of *A* and *B*.

## Symmetric multilinear maps {#Symmetric_multilinear_maps}

Let *k* be an infinite field of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) zero (or at least very large) and *V* a finite-dimensional vector space.

Let $S^q(V)$ denote the vector space of multilinear functionals $\textstyle \lambda: \prod_1^q V \to k$ that are symmetric; $\lambda(v_1, \dots, v_q)$ is the same for all permutations of $v_i$'s.

Any λ in $S^q(V)$ gives rise to a [homogeneous polynomial](wikipedia://en/1698977) function *f* of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) *q*: we just let $f(v) = \lambda(v, \dots, v).$ To see that *f* is a polynomial function, choose a basis $e_i, \, 1 \le i \le n$ of *V* and $t_i$ its dual. Then

$$\lambda(v_1, \dots, v_q) = \sum_{i_1, \dots, i_q = 1}^n \lambda(e_{i_1}, \dots, e_{i_q}) t_{i_1}(v_1) \cdots t_{i_q}(v_q),$$

which implies *f* is a polynomial in the *t*<sub>*i*</sub>'s.

Thus, there is a well-defined [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102):

$$\phi: S^q(V) \to k[V]_q, \, \phi(\lambda)(v) = \lambda(v, \cdots, v).$$

We show it is an isomorphism. Choosing a basis as before, any homogeneous polynomial function *f* of degree *q* can be written as:

$$f = \sum_{i_1, \dots, i_q = 1}^n a_{i_1 \cdots i_q} t_{i_1} \cdots t_{i_q}$$

where $a_{i_1 \cdots i_q}$ are symmetric in $i_1, \dots, i_q$. Let

$$\psi(f)(v_1, \dots, v_q) = \sum_{i_1, \cdots, i_q = 1}^n a_{i_1 \cdots i_q} t_{i_1}(v_1) \cdots t_{i_q}(v_q).$$

Clearly, $\phi\circ\psi$ is the identity; in particular, φ is surjective. To see φ is injective, suppose φ(λ) = 0. Consider

$$\phi(\lambda)(t_1 v_1 + \cdots + t_q v_q) = \lambda(t_1 v_1 + \cdots + t_q v_q, ..., t_1 v_1 + \cdots + t_q v_q),$$

which is zero. The coefficient of *t*<sub>1</sub>*t*<sub>2</sub> ... *t*<sub>*q*</sub> in the above expression is *q*! times λ(*v*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *v*<sub>*q*</sub>); it follows that λ = 0.

Note: φ is independent of a choice of basis; so the above proof shows that ψ is also independent of a basis, the fact not *a priori* obvious.

Example: A bilinear functional gives rise to a [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) in a unique way and any quadratic form arises in this way.

## Taylor series expansion {#Taylor_series_expansion}

Given a [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) function, locally, one can get a [partial derivative](wikipedia://en/52565) of the function from its [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) expansion and, conversely, one can recover the function from the series expansion. This fact continues to hold for polynomials functions on a vector space. If *f* is in *k*\[*V*\], then we write: for *x*, *y* in *V*,

$$f(x + y) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} g_n(x, y)$$

where *g*<sub>*n*</sub>(x, y) are homogeneous of degree *n* in *y*, and only finitely many of them are nonzero. We then let

$$(P_y f)(x) = g_1(x, y),$$

resulting in the linear [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569) *P*<sub>*y*</sub> of *k*\[*V*\]. It is called the polarization operator. We then have, as promised:

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

For each *f* in *k*\[V\] and *x*, *y* in *V*,

$$f(x + y) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} {1 \over n!} P_y^n f(x).$$

:::

Proof: We first note that (*P*<sub>*y*</sub> *f*) (*x*) is the coefficient of *t* in *f*(*x* + *t* *y*); in other words, since *g*<sub>0</sub>(*x*, *y*) = *g*<sub>0</sub>(*x*, 0) = *f*(*x*),

$$P_y f (x) = \left . {d \over dt} \right |_{t=0} f(x + ty)$$

where the right-hand side is, by definition,

$$\left . {f(x+ty) - f(x) \over t} \right |_{t=0}.$$

The theorem follows from this. For example, for *n* = 2, we have:

$$P_y^2 f (x) = \left . {\partial \over \partial t_1} \right |_{t_1=0} P_y f(x + t_1 y) = \left . {\partial \over \partial t_1} \right |_{t_1=0} \left . {\partial \over \partial t_2} \right |_{t_2=0} f(x + (t_1 + t_2) y) = 2! g_2(x, y).$$

The general case is similar. $\square$

## Operator product algebra {#Operator_product_algebra}

When the polynomials are valued not over a field *k*, but over some algebra, then one may define additional structure. Thus, for example, one may consider the ring of functions over *[GL(n,m)](wikipedia://en/113564)*, instead of for *k = GL(1,m)*. In this case, one may impose an additional axiom.

The **operator product algebra** is an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) of the form

$$A^i(x)B^j(y) = \sum_k f^{ij}_k (x,y,z) C^k(z)$$

The [structure constants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_constant) $f^{ij}_k (x,y,z)$ are required to be single-valued functions, rather than [sections](wikipedia://en/367621) of some [vector bundle](wikipedia://en/276524). The fields (or operators) $A^i(x)$ are required to span the [ring of functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_functions). In practical calculations, it is usually required that the sums be analytic within some [radius of convergence](wikipedia://en/61476); typically with a radius of convergence of $|x-y|$. Thus, the ring of functions can be taken to be the ring of polynomial functions.

The above can be considered to be an additional requirement imposed on the ring; it is sometimes called the *bootstrap*. In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), a special case of the operator product algebra is known as the [operator product expansion](wikipedia://en/1877895).
