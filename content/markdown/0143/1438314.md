# General algebraic modeling system

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="General algebraic modeling system"}

The **general algebraic modeling system** (**GAMS**) is a high-level [modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_model) system for [mathematical optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimization_%28mathematics%29). GAMS is designed for modeling and solving [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_optimization), [nonlinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_optimization), and [mixed-integer optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_integer_programming) problems. The system is tailored for complex, large-scale modeling applications and allows the user to build large maintainable models that can be adapted to new situations. The system is available for use on various computer platforms. Models are [portable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_portability) from one platform to another.

:::

GAMS was the first [algebraic modeling language](wikipedia://en/9463527) (AML)[^o4] and is formally similar to commonly used [fourth-generation programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth-generation_programming_language). GAMS contains an [integrated development environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_development_environment) (IDE) and is connected to a group of third-party optimization [solvers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solver). Among these [solvers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solver) are [BARON](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BARON), [COIN-OR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COIN-OR) solvers, CONOPT, [COPT Cardinal Optimizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COPT_Cardinal_Optimizer), [CPLEX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CPLEX), DICOPT, [IPOPT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPOPT), [MOSEK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOSEK), [SNOPT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNOPT), and [XPRESS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FICO_Xpress).

GAMS allows the users to implement a sort of [hybrid algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_algorithm) combining different solvers. Models are described in concise, human-readable algebraic statements. GAMS is among the most popular input formats for the [NEOS Server](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NEOS_Server). Although initially designed for applications related to economics and [management science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Management_science), it has a community of users from various backgrounds of engineering and science.

## Background {#Background}

The driving forces behind the development of GAMS were the users of [mathematical programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_programming) who believed in [optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimization_%28mathematics%29) as a powerful and elegant framework for solving real life problems in science and engineering. At the same time, these users were frustrated by high costs, skill requirements, and an overall low reliability of applying optimization tools. Most of the system's initiatives and support for new development arose in response to problems in the fields of economics, finance, and [chemical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_engineering), since these disciplines view and understand the world as a mathematical program.

GAMS’s impetus for development arose from the frustrating experience of a large economic modeling group at the [World Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Bank). In hindsight, one may call it a historic accident that in the 1970s mathematical economists and statisticians were assembled to address problems of development. They used the best techniques available at that time to solve multi-sector economy-wide models and large simulation and optimization models in agriculture, steel, fertilizer, power, water use, and other sectors. Although the group produced impressive research, initial success was difficult to reproduce outside their well functioning research environment. The existing techniques to construct, manipulate, and solve such models required several manual, time-consuming, and error-prone translations into different, problem-specific representations required by each solution method. During seminar presentations, modelers had to defend the existing versions of their models, sometimes quite irrationally, because of time and money considerations. Their models just could not be moved to other environments, because special programming knowledge was needed, and data formats and solution methods were not portable.

The idea of an algebraic approach to represent, manipulate, and solve large-scale mathematical models fused old and new paradigms into a consistent and computationally tractable system. Using [generator matrices](wikipedia://en/6133005) for [linear programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_program) revealed the importance of naming rows and columns in a consistent manner. The connection to the emerging relational data model became evident. Experience using traditional [programming languages](wikipedia://en/23015) to manage those name spaces naturally lead one to think in terms of [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) and [tuples](wikipedia://en/132729), and this led to the relational data model.

Combining multi-dimensional algebraic notation with the relational data model was the obvious answer. Compiler writing techniques were by now widespread, and languages like GAMS could be implemented relatively quickly. However, translating this rigorous mathematical representation into the algorithm-specific format required the computation of [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565) on very large systems. In the 1970s, [TRW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TRW_Inc.) developed a system called [PROSE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PROSE_modeling_language) that took the ideas of chemical engineers to compute point derivatives that were exact [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) at a given point, and to embed them in a consistent, Fortran-style calculus [modeling language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modeling_language). The resulting system allowed the user to use automatically generated exact first and second order derivatives. This was a pioneering system and an important demonstration of a concept. However, [PROSE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PROSE_modeling_language) had a number of shortcomings: it could not handle large systems, problem representation was tied to an array-type data structure that required address calculations, and the system did not provide access to state-of-the art solution methods. From linear programming, GAMS learned that exploitation of sparsity was key to solving large problems. Thus, the final piece of the puzzle was the use of sparse data structures.

## A sample model {#A_sample_model}

A transportation problem from [George Dantzig](wikipedia://en/13111) is used to provide a sample GAMS model.[^o11] This model is part of the model library which contains many more complete GAMS models. This problem finds a least cost shipping schedule that meets requirements at markets and supplies at factories.

[Dantzig](wikipedia://en/13111), G B, Chapter 3.3. In Linear Programming and Extensions. Princeton University Press, Princeton, New Jersey, 1963.

## Subsystems {#Subsystems}

The Mathematical Programming System for General Equilibrium analysis (MPSGE) is a language used for formulating and solving [Arrow–Debreu](wikipedia://en/1239556) economic equilibrium models and exists as a subsystem within GAMS.[^o13]
