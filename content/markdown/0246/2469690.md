# Fyodor Shcherbatskoy

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fyodor Shcherbatskoy"}

*Image: Cherbatskoy.jpg.* Fyodor Stcherbatskoy

**Fyodor Ippolitovich Shcherbatskoy** or **Stcherbatsky** (Фёдор Ипполи́тович Щербатско́й; 11 September (N.S.) 1866 – 18 March 1942),[^o2] often referred to in the literature as **F. Th. Stcherbatsky**, was a Russian [Indologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indologist) who, in large part, was responsible for laying the foundations in the Western world for the [scholarly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholar) study of [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism) and [Buddhist philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_philosophy). He was born in [Kielce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kielce), [Congress Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_Poland), and died at the Borovoye Resort,[^o3] in what is now northern [Kazakhstan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazakhstan).

:::

Stcherbatsky studied in the famous [Tsarskoye Selo Lyceum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsarskoye_Selo_Lyceum) (graduating in 1884), and later in the Historico-[Philological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philological) Faculty of [Saint Petersburg University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Petersburg_State_University) (graduating in 1889), where [Ivan Minayeff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Minayeff) and [Serge Oldenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serge_Oldenburg) were his teachers. Subsequently, sent abroad, he studied [Indian poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_poetry) with [Georg Bühler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Bühler) in Vienna, and Buddhist philosophy with [Hermann Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Jacobi) in [Bonn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonn). In 1897, he and Oldenburg inaugurated *Bibliotheca Buddhica*, a library of rare [Buddhist texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_texts).

Returning from a trip to [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India) and [Mongolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongolia), in 1903 Stcherbatsky published (in Russian) the first volume of *Theory of [Knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge) and [Logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) of the [Doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctrine) of Later Buddhists*[^o4] (2 vols., St. Petersburg, 1903–1909). In 1928 he established the Institute of Buddhist Culture in [Leningrad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Petersburg). His *The [Conception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept) of Buddhist [Nirvana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nirvana)* (Leningrad, 1927), written in [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English-language), caused a sensation in the West. He followed suit with his main work in English, *[Buddhist Logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_Logic)* (2 vols., 1930–32), which has exerted an immense influence on [Buddhology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhology).

Although Stcherbatsky remained less well known in his own country, his extraordinary fluency in [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) and [Tibetan languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Tibetan) won him the admiration of [Jawaharlal Nehru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jawaharlal_Nehru) and [Rabindranath Tagore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabindranath_Tagore). According to [Debiprasad Chattopadhyaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debiprasad_Chattopadhyaya),[^o5] "Stcherbatsky did help us – the Indians – to discover our own past and to restore the right perspective of our own philosophical heritage." The [Encyclopædia Britannica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopædia_Britannica) (2004 edition) acclaimed Stcherbatsky as "the foremost Western authority on Buddhist philosophy".
