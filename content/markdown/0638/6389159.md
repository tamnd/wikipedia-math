# Nilpotent ideal

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nilpotent ideal"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), more specifically [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424), an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) *I* of a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *R* is said to be a **nilpotent ideal** if there exists a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) *k* such that *I*<sup>*k*</sup> = 0.[^n1] By *I*<sup>*k*</sup>, it is meant the additive [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) generated by the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all products of *k* elements in *I*.[^n1] Therefore, *I* is nilpotent if and only if there is a natural number *k* such that the product of any *k* elements of *I* is 0.

:::

The notion of a nilpotent ideal is much stronger than that of a [nil ideal](wikipedia://en/3100140) in many [classes](wikipedia://en/20696) of rings. There are, however, instances when the two notions coincide—this is exemplified by [Levitzky's theorem](wikipedia://en/24015132).[^n2][^n3] The notion of a nilpotent ideal, although interesting in the case of [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346), is most interesting in the case of [noncommutative rings](wikipedia://en/24062516).

## Relation to nil ideals {#Relation_to_nil_ideals}

The notion of a nil ideal has a deep connection with that of a nilpotent ideal, and in some classes of rings, the two notions coincide. If an ideal is nilpotent, it is of course nil, but a nil ideal need not be nilpotent for more than one reason. The first is that there need not be a global upper bound on the exponent required to annihilate various elements of the nil ideal, and secondly, each element being nilpotent does not force products of distinct elements to vanish.[^n1]

In a right [Artinian ring](wikipedia://en/489273), any nil ideal is nilpotent.[^n4] This is proven by observing that any nil ideal is contained in the [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656) of the ring, and since the Jacobson radical is a nilpotent ideal (due to the Artinian hypothesis), the result follows. In fact, this can be generalized to right [Noetherian rings](wikipedia://en/56129); this result is known as [Levitzky's theorem](wikipedia://en/24015132).[^n3]
