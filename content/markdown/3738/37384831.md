# Linearised polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Linearised polynomial"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **linearised polynomial** (or *q*-polynomial) is a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) for which the exponents of all the constituent [monomials](wikipedia://en/357416) are [powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_%28mathematics%29) of *q* and the [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) come from some [extension field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_field) of the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) of order *q*.

:::

We write a typical example as

$$L(x) = \sum_{i=0}^n a_i x^{q^i},$$

where each $a_i$ is in $F_{q^m} (= \operatorname{GF}(q^m))$ for some fixed positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $m$.

This special class of polynomials is important from both a theoretical and an applications viewpoint.[^o2] The highly structured nature of their [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) makes these roots easy to determine.

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- The map $x \mapsto L(x)$ is a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) over any [field](wikipedia://en/10603) containing **F**<sub>*q*</sub>.
- The [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of roots of *L* is an **F**<sub>*q*</sub>-[vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) and is closed under the *q*-[Frobenius map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_map).
- Conversely, if *U* is any **F**<sub>*q*</sub>-[linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of some finite field containing **F**<sub>*q*</sub>, then the polynomial that vanishes exactly on *U* is a linearised polynomial.
- The set of linearised polynomials over a given field is closed under addition and [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of polynomials.
- If *L* is a nonzero linearised polynomial over $F_{q^n}$ with all of its roots lying in the field $F_{q^s}$ an extension field of $F_{q^n}$, then each root of *L* has the same multiplicity, which is either 1, or a positive power of *q*.[^o5]

:::

## Symbolic multiplication {#Symbolic_multiplication}

In general, the product of two linearised polynomials will not be a linearized polynomial, but since the composition of two linearised polynomials results in a linearised polynomial, composition may be used as a replacement for multiplication and, for this reason, composition is often called **symbolic multiplication** in this setting. Notationally, if *L*<sub>1</sub>(*x*) and *L*<sub>2</sub>(*x*) are linearised polynomials we define

$$L_1(x) \otimes L_2(x) = L_1(L_2(x))$$

when this point of view is being taken.

## Associated polynomials {#Associated_polynomials}

The polynomials $L(x)$ and

$$l(x) = \sum_{i=0}^n a_i x^i$$

are *q-associates* (note: the exponents "*q*<sup>*i*</sup>" of *L*(*x*) have been replaced by "*i*" in *l*(*x*)). More specifically, *l*(*x*) is called the *conventional q-associate* of *L*(*x*), and *L*(*x*) is the *linearised q-associate* of *l*(*x*).

## *q*-polynomials over **F**<sub>*q*</sub> {#q-polynomials_over_Fq}

Linearised polynomials with coefficients in **F**<sub>*q*</sub> have additional properties which make it possible to define symbolic division, symbolic reducibility and symbolic factorization. Two important examples of this type of linearised polynomial are the Frobenius automorphism $x \mapsto x^q$ and the trace function $\operatorname{Tr}(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} x^{q^i}.$

In this special case it can be shown that, as an [operation](wikipedia://en/4140245), symbolic multiplication is [commutative](wikipedia://en/294390), [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) and [distributes](wikipedia://en/103118) over ordinary addition.[^o9] Also, in this special case, we can define the operation of **symbolic division**. If *L*(*x*) and *L*<sub>1</sub>(*x*) are linearised polynomials over **F**<sub>*q*</sub>, we say that *L*<sub>1</sub>(*x*) *symbolically divides* *L*(*x*) if there exists a linearised polynomial *L*<sub>2</sub>(*x*) over **F**<sub>*q*</sub> for which:

$$L(x) = L_1(x) \otimes L_2(x).$$

If *L*<sub>1</sub>(*x*) and *L*<sub>2</sub>(*x*) are linearised polynomials over **F**<sub>*q*</sub> with conventional *q*-associates *l*<sub>1</sub>(*x*) and *l*<sub>2</sub>(*x*) respectively, then *L*<sub>1</sub>(*x*) symbolically divides *L*<sub>2</sub>(*x*) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) *l*<sub>1</sub>(*x*) divides *l*<sub>2</sub>(*x*).[^o10] Furthermore, *L*<sub>1</sub>(*x*) divides *L*<sub>2</sub>(*x*) in the ordinary sense in this case.[^o11]

A linearised polynomial *L*(*x*) over **F**<sub>*q*</sub> of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) > 1 is *symbolically irreducible* over **F**<sub>*q*</sub> if the only symbolic decompositions

$$L(x) = L_1(x) \otimes L_2(x),$$

with *L*<sub>*i*</sub> over **F**<sub>*q*</sub> are those for which one of the factors has degree 1. Note that a symbolically irreducible polynomial is always [reducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reducible_polynomial) in the ordinary sense since any linearised polynomial of degree > 1 has the nontrivial factor *x*. A linearised polynomial *L*(*x*) over **F**<sub>*q*</sub> is symbolically irreducible if and only if its conventional *q*-associate *l*(*x*) is irreducible over **F**<sub>*q*</sub>.

Every *q*-polynomial *L*(*x*) over **F**<sub>*q*</sub> of degree > 1 has a *symbolic factorization* into symbolically irreducible polynomials over **F**<sub>*q*</sub> and this factorization is essentially unique (up to rearranging factors and multiplying by nonzero elements of **F**<sub>*q*</sub>.)

For example,[^o12] consider the 2-polynomial *L*(*x*) = *x*<sup>16</sup> + *x*<sup>8</sup> + *x*<sup>2</sup> + *x* over **F**<sub>2</sub> and its conventional 2-associate *l*(*x*) = *x*<sup>4</sup> + *x*<sup>3</sup> + *x* + 1. The factorization into irreducibles of *l*(*x*) = (*x*<sup>2</sup> + *x* + 1)(*x* + 1)<sup>2</sup> in **F**<sub>2</sub>\[*x*\], gives the symbolic factorization

$$L(x) = (x^4 + x^2 + x) \otimes (x^2 + x) \otimes (x^2 + x).$$

## Affine polynomials {#Affine_polynomials}

Let *L* be a linearised polynomial over $F_{q^n}$. A polynomial of the form $A(x) = L(x) - \alpha \text{ for } \alpha \in F_{q^n},$ is an *affine polynomial* over $F_{q^n}$.

Theorem: If *A* is a nonzero affine polynomial over $F_{q^n}$ with all of its roots lying in the field $F_{q^s}$ an extension field of $F_{q^n}$, then each root of *A* has the same multiplicity, which is either 1, or a positive power of *q*.[^o14]
