# Hat notation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hat notation"}

A "hat" ([circumflex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumflex) (ˆ)), placed over a symbol is a [mathematical notation](wikipedia://en/277184) with various uses.

:::

## Estimated value {#Estimated_value}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), a [circumflex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumflex) (ˆ), nicknamed a "hat", is used to denote an [estimator](wikipedia://en/10043) or an estimated value.[^o3] For example, in the context of [errors and residuals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errors_and_residuals_in_statistics), the "hat" over the letter $\hat{\varepsilon}$ indicates an observable estimate (the residuals) of an unobservable quantity called $\varepsilon$ (the statistical errors).

Another example of the hat denoting an estimator occurs in [simple linear regression](wikipedia://en/2593771). Assuming a model of $y_i = \beta_0+\beta_1 x_i+\varepsilon_i$, with observations of independent variable data $x_i$ and dependent variable data $y_i$, the estimated model is of the form $\hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0+\hat{\beta}_1 x_i$ where $\sum_i (y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2$ is commonly minimized via [least squares](wikipedia://en/82359) by finding optimal values of $\hat{\beta}_0$ and $\hat{\beta}_1$ for the observed data.

## Hat matrix {#Hat_matrix}

In statistics, the hat matrix *H* projects the observed values **y** of response variable to the predicted values **ŷ**:

$$\hat{\mathbf{y}} = H \mathbf{y}.$$

## Cross product {#Cross_product}

In [screw theory](wikipedia://en/1318037), one use of the [hat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumflex) operator is to represent the [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) operation. Since the cross product is a [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation), it can be represented as a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859). The hat operator takes a [vector](wikipedia://en/879358) and transforms it into its equivalent matrix.

$$\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{\hat{a}} \mathbf{b}$$

For example, in three dimensions,

$$\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} a_x \\ a_y \\ a_z \end{bmatrix} \times \begin{bmatrix} b_x \\ b_y \\ b_z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -a_z & a_y \\ a_z & 0 & -a_x \\ -a_y & a_x & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} b_x \\ b_y \\ b_z \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{\hat{a}} \mathbf{b}.$$

## Unit vector {#Unit_vector}

In mathematics, a unit vector in a [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538) is a vector (often a spatial vector) of length 1. A unit vector is often denoted by a lowercase letter with a circumflex, or "hat", as in $\hat {\mathbf {v} }$ (pronounced "v-hat").[^o7][^o3] This is especially common in physics context.

## Fourier transform {#Fourier_transform}

The [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of a function $f$ is traditionally denoted by $\hat{f}$.

## Operator {#Operator}

In quantum mechanics, [operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_%28physics%29) are denoted with hat notation. For instance, see the [time-independent Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874#Time-independent_equation), where the [Hamiltonian operator](wikipedia://en/14381) is denoted $\hat{H}$.

$$\hat{H}\psi = E\psi$$
