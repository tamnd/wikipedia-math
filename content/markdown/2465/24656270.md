# Polyhedral graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Polyhedral graph"}

*Image: Dodecahedron schlegel.svg.* The polyhedral graph formed as the [Schlegel diagram](wikipedia://en/7201595) of a [regular dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/7149361).

In [geometric graph theory](wikipedia://en/3313527), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **polyhedral graph** is the [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) formed from the [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) and [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034) of a [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron). Alternatively, in purely graph-theoretic terms, the polyhedral graphs are the [3-vertex-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph), [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314). The analogue concept for [polytopes](wikipedia://en/23471) of general dimension are the [polytopal graphs](wikipedia://en/74718534).

:::

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Characterization"}

## Characterization {#Characterization}

The [Schlegel diagram](wikipedia://en/7201595) of a convex polyhedron represents its vertices and edges as points and line segments in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), forming a subdivision of an outer [convex polygon](wikipedia://en/660019) into smaller convex polygons (a [convex drawing](wikipedia://en/64649083) of the graph of the polyhedron). It has no crossings, so every polyhedral graph is also a [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314). Additionally, by [Balinski's theorem](wikipedia://en/24732291), it is a [3-vertex-connected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph).

According to [Steinitz's theorem](wikipedia://en/19762817), these two graph-theoretic properties are enough to completely [characterize](wikipedia://en/780886) the polyhedral graphs: they are exactly the 3-vertex-connected planar graphs. That is, whenever a graph is both planar and 3-vertex-connected, there exists a polyhedron whose vertices and edges form an [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) graph.[^o4][^o5] Given such a graph, a representation of it as a subdivision of a convex polygon into smaller convex polygons may be found using the [Tutte embedding](wikipedia://en/39503349).[^o6]

:::

## Hamiltonicity and shortness {#Hamiltonicity_and_shortness}

[Tait conjectured](wikipedia://en/52435) that every [cubic](wikipedia://en/980508) polyhedral graph (that is, a polyhedral graph in which each vertex is incident to exactly three edges) has a [Hamiltonian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle), but this conjecture was disproved by a [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) of [W. T. Tutte](wikipedia://en/52021), the polyhedral but non-[Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_graph) [Tutte graph](wikipedia://en/24511700). If one relaxes the requirement that the graph be cubic, there are much smaller non-Hamiltonian polyhedral graphs. The graph with the fewest vertices and edges is the 11-vertex and 18-edge [Herschel graph](wikipedia://en/24670148),[^o8] and there also exists an 11-vertex non-Hamiltonian polyhedral graph in which all faces are triangles, the [Goldner–Harary graph](wikipedia://en/24674758).[^o9]

More strongly, there exists a constant $\alpha<1$ (the [shortness exponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortness_exponent)) and an infinite family of polyhedral graphs such that the length of the longest [simple path](wikipedia://en/638889) of an $n$-vertex graph in the family is $O(n^{\alpha})$.[^o10][^o11]

A problem with one additional constraint exists, [Barnette's conjecture](wikipedia://en/28059825), asking whether every cubic *[bipartite](wikipedia://en/244431)* polyhedral graph is Hamiltonian, which remains unsolved.

## Combinatorial enumeration {#Combinatorial_enumeration}

Duijvestijn provides a count of the polyhedral graphs with up to 26 edges;[^o13] The number of these graphs with 6, 7, 8, ... edges is

1, 0, 1, 2, 2, 4, 12, 22, 58, 158, 448, 1342, 4199, 13384, 43708, 144810, ... .

One may also [enumerate](wikipedia://en/9982439) the polyhedral graphs by their numbers of vertices: for graphs with 4, 5, 6, ... vertices, the number of polyhedral graphs is

1, 2, 7, 34, 257, 2606, 32300, 440564, 6384634, 96262938, 1496225352, ... .

## Special cases {#Special_cases}

240px The [vertex-edge graphs](wikipedia://en/74718534) of the [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) are also called *Platonic graphs*. As well as having all the other properties of polyhedral graphs, these are [symmetric graphs](wikipedia://en/1577896), and all of them have [Hamiltonian cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle).[^o16] There are five of these graphs:

- [Tetrahedral graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedral_graph) – 4 vertices, 6 edges
- [Octahedral graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octahedral_graph) – 6 vertices, 12 edges
- [Cubical graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubical_graph) – 8 vertices, 12 edges
- [Icosahedral graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icosahedral_graph) – 12 vertices, 30 edges
- [Dodecahedral graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodecahedral_graph) – 20 vertices, 30 edges

A polyhedral graph is the graph of a [simple polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_polyhedron) if it is [cubic](wikipedia://en/980508) (every vertex has three edges), and it is the graph of a [simplicial polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicial_polyhedron) if it is a [maximal planar graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_planar_graph). For example, the tetrahedral, cubical, and dodecahedral graphs are simple; the tetrahedral, octahedral, and icosahedral graphs are simplicial.

The [Halin graphs](wikipedia://en/19658548), graphs formed from a planar embedded [tree](wikipedia://en/48560) by adding an outer cycle connecting all of the leaves of the tree, form another important subclass of the polyhedral graphs.
