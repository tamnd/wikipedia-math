# Additively indecomposable ordinal

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Additively indecomposable ordinal"}

In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **additively indecomposable ordinal** *α* is any [ordinal number](wikipedia://en/26547932) that is not 0 such that for any $\beta,\gamma<\alpha$, we have $\beta+\gamma<\alpha.$ Additively indecomposable ordinals were named the *gamma numbers* by [Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216),[^o2]<sup>p.20</sup> and are also called *additive principal numbers*. The [class](wikipedia://en/20696) of additively indecomposable ordinals may be denoted $\mathbb H$, from the German "Hauptzahl".[^o3] The additively indecomposable ordinals are precisely those ordinals of the form $\omega^\beta$ for some ordinal $\beta$.

:::

From the [continuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28set_theory%29) of addition in its right argument, we get that if $\beta < \alpha$ and *α* is additively indecomposable, then $\beta + \alpha = \alpha.$

Obviously 1 is additively indecomposable, since $0+0<1.$ No [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) ordinal other than $1$ is additively indecomposable. Also, $\omega$ is additively indecomposable, since the sum of two finite ordinals is still finite. More generally, every [infinite](wikipedia://en/142679) [initial ordinal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_ordinal) (an ordinal corresponding to a [cardinal number](wikipedia://en/6173)) is additively indecomposable.

The class of additively indecomposable numbers is [closed](wikipedia://en/213650) and unbounded. Its enumerating function is [normal](wikipedia://en/404306), given by $\omega^\alpha$.

The derivative of $\omega^\alpha$ (which enumerates its fixed points) is written $\varepsilon_\alpha$ Ordinals of this form (that is, [fixed points](wikipedia://en/449738) of $\omega^\alpha$) are called *[epsilon numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsilon_numbers_%28mathematics%29)*. The number $\varepsilon_0 = \omega^{\omega^{\omega^{\cdots}}}$ is therefore the first fixed point of the [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $\omega,\omega^\omega\!,\omega^{\omega^\omega}\!\!,\ldots$

## Multiplicatively indecomposable {#Multiplicatively_indecomposable}

A similar notion can be defined for multiplication. If *α* is greater than the multiplicative identity, 1, and *β* \< *α* and *γ* \< *α* imply *β*·*γ* \< *α*, then *α* is multiplicatively indecomposable. The finite ordinal 2 is multiplicatively indecomposable since 1·1 = 1 \< 2. Besides 2, the multiplicatively indecomposable ordinals (named the *delta numbers* by Cantor[^o2]<sup>p.20</sup>) are those of the form $\omega^{\omega^\alpha} \,$ for any ordinal *α*. Every [epsilon number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsilon_numbers_%28mathematics%29) is multiplicatively indecomposable; and every multiplicatively indecomposable ordinal (other than 2) is additively indecomposable. The delta numbers (other than 2) are the same as the [prime ordinals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_ordinal) that are limits.

## Higher indecomposables {#Higher_indecomposables}

Exponentially indecomposable ordinals are equal to the epsilon numbers. Tetration, as usually defined, gets stuck at limit ordinals, so the notion of indecomposable beyond exponentiation is not useful.
