# J. Laurie Snell

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="J. Laurie Snell"}

*Image: Laurie Snell.jpg.* James Laurie Snell

**James Laurie Snell** (January 15, 1925 in Wheaton, [Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois) – March 19, 2011 in Hanover, [New Hampshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Hampshire)) was an American mathematician and educator.

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

J. Laurie Snell was the son of [Roy Snell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_J._Snell), an adventure author, and Lucille, a concert pianist. Lucille taught the three sons (Jud, John and Laurie) to play piano, cello, and violin. The family had a life-lease on a cabin in [Isle Royale National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_Royale_National_Park) where they would go for summer holidays.[^o3]

## Graduate work {#Graduate_work}

Snell studied mathematics at the [University of Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois) with [Joseph L. Doob](wikipedia://en/1547599) from 1948 through 1951; Doob introduced him to [martingales](wikipedia://en/274536), an aspect of [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542). Doob assigned such topics by having students attempt to solve a series of problems that he kept on file cards.[^o5] Snell earned his Ph.D. in 1951 ("Applications of Martingale System Theorems"), with Doob as his supervisor.

## Dartmouth College {#Dartmouth_College}

At [Dartmouth College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_College) Snell became involved in a mathematics department project to develop a course on modern mathematics used in biological and social sciences. He worked with [John G. Kemeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_G._Kemeny) and [Gerald L. Thompson](wikipedia://en/25301762) to write *Introduction to Finite Mathematics* (1957) which described probability theory, linear algebra, and applications in sociology, genetics, psychology, anthropology, and economics. They found "the basic ideas of [finite mathematics](wikipedia://en/226981) were easier to state and theorems about them considerably easier to prove than their infinite counterparts." A French translation was made by M. C. Loyau and published in 1960 by Donod.[^o7]

Another colleague at Dartmouth, Hazleton Mirkil, joined the team to write *Finite Mathematical Structures* (1959) for sophomores at Dartmouth studying science. Infinite problems are considered after their finite counterparts are fully developed in the text. In 1962 the publisher [Prentice-Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prentice-Hall) issued a third book from a Dartmouth team: Kemeny, Snell, Thompson, and Arthur Schleifer Jr. wrote *Finite Mathematics with Business Applications* which included applications: computer circuits, critical path analysis, flow diagrams for computing and accounting procedures, Monte Carlo simulation of decision processes, reliability, decision theory, waiting line theory, a simple approach to mathematics of finance, matrix games, and the simplex method for solving linear programming problems. A second edition of the first text came out in 1966.

## Writings {#Writings}

In 1959 Snell published a survey article on [Markov chains](wikipedia://en/60876).[^o9] He worked the material into a book *Finite Markov Chains* with Kemeny. As the "first self-contained account in English",[^o10] it attracted wide interest. While one reviewer said "the exposition is of high quality",[^o11] other reviewers found fault: Too little attention paid to assumptions inherent in a model.[^o12] "Interest builds steadily as one peruses the book." But "little attention to historical development."[^o13] "From the point of view of an undergraduate ... the opening chapter on mathematical prerequisites is rather frightening."[^o14] "Does not supersede the corresponding chapters in Feller's classic *Introduction to Probability*; "No index and not even the sketchiest bibliography."[^o15]

Snell started *Chance News* in 1992 to "review news and journal articles pertaining to probability and statistics in the real world." One feature is *Forsooth* for statistical gaffes in media reports, a column originally found in the newsletter of the [Royal Statistical Society](wikipedia://en/552664). In 2005 *Chance News* was moved to [Chance Wiki](https://www.causeweb.org/wiki/chance/index.php/Main_Page) where there is an archive of *Forsooths* and previous *News*. Out of collaborations in *Chance News* with Charles M. Grinstead and William P. Peterson, a book *Probability Tales* (2011) was published by [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822) in the Student Mathematical Library. The book covers four topics: streaks in sports as streaks of successful [Bernoulli trials](wikipedia://en/102653) (like [hitting streaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitting_streak)), constructing [stock market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_market) models, estimating expected value of a [lottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lottery) ticket, and reliability of [fingerprint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fingerprint) identification.

## Legacy {#Legacy}

Snell retired in 1995 and was elected as a fellow of the [American Statistical Association](wikipedia://en/341988) in 1996.

The [Snell envelope](wikipedia://en/35652136), used in [stochastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastics) and [mathematical finance](wikipedia://en/33589680), is the smallest [supermartingale](wikipedia://en/274536) dominating the price process. The Snell envelope refers to results in a 1952 paper *Applications of martingale system theorems*.[^o17]
