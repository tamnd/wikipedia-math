# Self-similar solution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Self-similar solution"}

In the study of [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564), particularly in [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034), a **self-similar solution** is a form of solution which is similar to itself if the independent and dependent variables are appropriately scaled. Self-similar solutions appear whenever the problem lacks a [characteristic length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_length) or time scale (for example, the [Blasius boundary layer](wikipedia://en/4377661) of an infinite plate, but not of a finite-length plate). These include, for example, the Blasius boundary layer or the [Sedov–Taylor shell](wikipedia://en/65783919).[^o2][^o3]

:::

## Concept {#Concept}

A powerful tool in physics is the concept of [dimensional analysis](wikipedia://en/8267) and scaling laws. By examining the physical effects present in a system, we may estimate their size and hence which, for example, might be neglected. In some cases, the system may not have a fixed natural length or time scale, while the solution depends on space or time. It is then necessary to construct a scale using space or time and the other dimensional quantities present—such as the viscosity $\nu$. These constructs are not 'guessed' but are derived immediately from the scaling of the governing equations.

## Classification {#Classification}

The normal self-similar solution is also referred to as a **self-similar solution of the first kind**, since another type of self-similar exists for finite-sized problems, which cannot be derived from [dimensional analysis](wikipedia://en/8267), known as a **self-similar solution of the second kind**.

### Self-similar solution of the second kind {#Self-similar_solution_of_the_second_kind}

The early identification of self-similar solutions of the second kind can be found in problems of imploding shock waves ([Guderley–Landau–Stanyukovich problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guderley–Landau–Stanyukovich_problem)), analyzed by G. Guderley (1942) and [Lev Landau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lev_Landau) and K. P. Stanyukovich (1944),[^o7] and propagation of shock waves by a short impulse, analysed by [Carl Friedrich von Weizsäcker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_von_Weizsäcker)[^o8] and [Yakov Borisovich Zel'dovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakov_Borisovich_Zel'dovich) (1956), who also classified it as the second kind for the first time.[^o9] An independent study about the same field was published by [Leonid Ivanovich Sedov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Sedov) in 1959.[^o10] A complete description was made in 1972 by [Grigory Barenblatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grigory_Barenblatt) and [Yakov Borisovich Zel'dovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakov_Borisovich_Zel'dovich).[^o11] The self-similar solution of the second kind also appears in different contexts such as in boundary-layer problems subjected to small perturbations,[^o12] as was identified by [Keith Stewartson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Stewartson),[^o13] [Paul A. Libby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_A._Libby) and Herbert Fox.[^o14] [Moffatt eddies](wikipedia://en/61551787) are also a self-similar solution of the second kind.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Rayleigh problem {#Rayleigh_problem}

A simple example is a semi-infinite domain bounded by a rigid wall and filled with viscous fluid.[^o18] At time $t=0$ the wall is made to move with constant speed $U$ in a fixed direction (for definiteness, say the $x$ direction and consider only the $x-y$ plane), one can see that there is no distinguished [length scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Length_scale) given in the problem. This is known as the [Rayleigh problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayleigh_problem). The boundary conditions of no-slip is

$$u{(y\!=\!0)} = U$$

Also, the condition that the plate has no effect on the fluid at infinity is enforced as

$$u{(y\!\to\!\infty)} = 0.$$

Now, from the Navier–Stokes equations

$$\rho \left( \dfrac{\partial \vec{u}}{\partial t} + \vec{u} \cdot \nabla \vec{u} \right) =- \nabla p + \mu \nabla^{2} \vec{u}$$

one can observe that this flow will be [rectilinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectilinear_motion), with gradients in the $y$ direction and flow in the $x$ direction, and that the pressure term will have no tangential component so that $\dfrac{\partial p}{\partial y} = 0$. The $x$ component of the Navier–Stokes equations then becomes

$$\dfrac{\partial \vec{u}}{\partial t} = \nu \frac{\partial^2 \vec{u}}{\partial y^2}$$

and the scaling arguments can be applied to show that

$$\frac{U}{t} \sim \nu \frac{U}{y^{2}}$$

which gives the scaling of the $y$ co-ordinate as

$$y \sim (\nu t)^{1/2}.$$

This allows one to pose a self-similar [ansatz](wikipedia://en/1359360) such that, with $f$ and $\eta$ dimensionless,

$$u = U f{\left(\eta \equiv \dfrac{y}{(\nu t)^{1/2}}\right)}$$

The above contains all the relevant physics and the next step is to solve the equations, which for many cases will include numerical methods. This equation is

$$- \eta f'/2 = f''$$

with solution satisfying the boundary conditions that

$$f = 1 - \operatorname{erf} (\eta / 2) \quad \text{ or } \quad u = U \left(1 - \operatorname{erf} \left(y / (4 \nu t)^{1/2} \right)\right)$$

which is a self-similar solution of the first kind.

### Semi-infinite solid approximation {#Semi-infinite_solid_approximation}

In transient [heat transfer](wikipedia://en/184726) applications, such as impingement heating on a ship deck during missile launches and the sizing of [thermal protection systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_protection_system), self-similar solutions can be found for semi-infinite solids.[^o20][^o21] The governing equation when [heat conduction](wikipedia://en/72536) is the primary heat transfer mechanism is the one-dimensional energy equation:

$$\rho c_{p} \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left( k \frac{\partial T}{\partial x} \right)$$

where $\rho$ is the material's [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density), $c_{p}$ is the material's [specific heat capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_heat_capacity), $k$ is the material's [thermal conductivity](wikipedia://en/59438). In the case when the material is assumed to be homogeneous and its properties constant, the energy equation is reduced to the [heat equation](wikipedia://en/166415):

$$\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \alpha \frac{\partial^{2}T}{\partial x^{2}}, \quad \alpha = \frac{k}{\rho c_{p}}$$

with $\alpha$ being the [thermal diffusivity](wikipedia://en/58282). By introducing the similarity variable $\eta = x/\sqrt{t}$ and assuming that $T(t,x) = f(\eta)$, the PDE can be transformed into the ODE:

$$f''(\eta) + \frac{1}{2\alpha}\eta f'(\eta) = 0$$

If a simple model of thermal protection system sizing is assumed, where decomposition, [pyrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrolysis) gas flow, and surface recession are ignored, with the initial temperature $T(0,x) = f(\infty) = T_{i}$ and a constant surface temperature $T(t,0) = f(0) = T_{s}$, then the ODE can be solved for the temperature at a depth $x$ and time $t$:[^o21]

$$T(t,x) = \text{erf}\left( \frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}} \right) \left( T_{i} - T_{s} \right) + T_{s}$$

where $\text{erf}(\cdot)$ is the [error function](wikipedia://en/177111).
