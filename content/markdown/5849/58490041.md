# Bruun rule

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bruun rule"}

*Image: Norfolk Beach, Coochiemudlo Island, coast erosion 02.jpg.* Shoreline recession of a sandy shore at Norfolk Beach, Coochiemuldo Island, Australia

The **Bruun rule** is a [formula](wikipedia://en/164040) for estimating the magnitude of the retreat of the [shoreline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shore) of a sandy shore in response to [changes in sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level#Change). Originally published in 1962 by [Per Moller Bruun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_Moller_Bruun),[^o2] the Bruun rule was the first to give a relationship between [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise) and [shoreline recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastal_erosion).[^o3] The rule is a simple, [two dimensional](wikipedia://en/70691232) [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) conversion, and remains in common use to estimate shoreline recession in response to sea level rise, despite criticism and modification, and the availability of more complex alternate models.[^o4]

:::

## The calculation {#The_calculation}

The Bruun rule gives a [linear relationship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_and_dependence) between [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise) and [shoreline recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastal_erosion) based on equilibrium profile theory, which asserts that [shore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shore) face profile maintains an equilibrium shape, and as sea level rises the increasing [accommodation space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accommodation_%28geology%29) forces this equilibrium profile landward and upward to preserve its shape relative to the new sea level.[^o6] As such, the Bruun rule analysis assumes that the upper [beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beach) is eroded as the shore profile moves landward, and that the volume of [eroded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erosion) material is deposited offshore, resulting in a rise of the nearshore [bottom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seabed) which maintains a constant water depth.[^o7] The Bruun rule predicts [coastal recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastal_erosion) to be as much as 10 to 50 times [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise), depending on the [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) of the [beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beach).[^o8]

*Image: The Bruun Rule SCOR 1991.JPG.* Illustration of the Bruun rule, by the Scientific Committee on Ocean Research (1991)

The mathematical notation of the Bruun rule is:

$R = \frac{SL}{h+B} = \frac{S}{\tan\beta}$[^o9][^o4]

Where,

- *R* is [shoreline recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastal_erosion), in metres
- *S* is [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise), in metres
- *L* is the horizontal length of the bottom affected by the [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise) (from the [dune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dune) peak to depth of closure), in metres
- *h* is the depth of closure (the water depth beyond which significant [sediment transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sediment_transport) does not occur[^o10][^o11]), in metres
- *B* is the dune height above sea level, in metres
- *β* is the average [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) of the active profile[^o9]

## History {#History}

In 1954, Per Moller Bruun published a paper[^o13] describing beach profiles and cross shore equilibrium.[^o32] Developing on these ideas, in 1962 he published his paper "Sea-Level Rise as a Cause of Shore Erosion",*[^o2]* which first gave a relationship between [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise) and the [shoreline recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastal_erosion) of equilibrium beach profiles.[^o3] His rule wasn't given a name until 1967, when Schwartz published "The Bruun Theory of Sea-Level Rise as a Cause of Shore Erosion" which detailed [laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratory) and [field](wikipedia://en/1305037) tests of the theory and concluded that "the concept henceforth be known as 'Bruun's Rule'."[^o14]

Per Bruun has since published works to clarify the limitations and practicality of his model, including "Review of Conditions for Uses of the Bruun Rule of Erosion" in 1983,[^o34] and "The Bruun Rule of Erosion by Sea-Level Rise: A Discussion on Large-Scale Two- and Three- Dimensional Usages" in 1988.[^o35] These publications outline the rule's limitations and practicality and stress that, though this model is intentionally [two dimensional](wikipedia://en/70691232), it is applied to [three dimensional](wikipedia://en/3054853) environments in practice.[^o34][^o35] Bruun states that the rule "must be subjected to realistic adjustments"[^o34] for practical use.

As Bruun acknowledges, the Bruun rule is a characteristically simple rule and has therefore been modified many times over, to account for more factors and in order to be accurately applicable in more cases.[^o15] bThe Bruun rule has been most often modified to account for [longshore transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longshore_drift), [overwash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overwash), and [Aeolian sediment transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeolian_processes).*[^o15]* For example, Dean and Maurmeier in 1983 modified the rule to be applicable to barrier shores and islands, some of which experience accretion instead of erosion.[^o6][^o36] Rosati et al. in 2013[^o15] and Dean and Houston in 2016[^o16] have modified the rule to account for onshore transport, and cross shore movement over the supposed depth of closure.[^o15][^o16] Further, Ashton et al. in 2011 modified the Bruun rule for use on [cliffed coasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliffed_coast),[^o17] and Hinkel et al. in 2013 used the rule as part of a wider methodology to describe the effects on [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise) on and around [tidal inlets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inlet).[^o18] The biggest challenge that remains seems to be isolating the effect of [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise) on beach morphology from the coupled effects of wave energy, tidal currents, wind action, sediment supplies, sediment types and grain size, among others.[^o19][^o32]

In various forms and coupled with other models, the Bruun rule has now been used to estimate shoreline responses to [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise) worldwide.[^o8] The Bruun rule has been applied to [coasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coast) including of the [Caspian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caspian_Sea),[^o20] [Korean Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_Peninsula),[^o21] Shuidong Bay,[^o22] [Norfolk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norfolk),[^o23] [Rhode Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhode_Island),[^o24] [Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida),[^o25] [Accra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accra),[^o26] and [Hawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii).[^o9]

## Criticisms {#Criticisms}

The effect of [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change) on beaches is challenging to accurately model, as it is an interdisciplinary subject that involves [ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_science), [earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_science), and [atmospheric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_sciences) science as well as [civil engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_engineering) and [policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policy_analysis).[^o28] Reliable coastal climate change impact assessments are needed to underpin effective strategies of adaptation in order to prepare growing coastal communities and high value coastal assets.[^o29] As a result, models for estimating coastal erosion as a result of sea level rise - including the Bruun rule and models based on the Bruun rule - are constantly being reviewed and updated.[^o9][^o10][^o11] The Bruun rule has become the centre of much academic debate.[^o18]

In response to its inherent assumptions, the Bruun rule has been widely criticised.[^o6][^o9]*[^o11][^o29][^o30][^o31]*[^o32] Some of the rule's most criticised assumptions include the nonexistence of [gradients](wikipedia://en/12461) in [longshore sediment transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longshore_drift), the existence of a depth of closure, a closed [sediment budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary_budget), and the availability of sufficient [sand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sand) sources.[^o6] In 2015, Andersen et al. labelled the Bruun rule as " on its own...virtually unusable in open-ocean coastal environments"[^o9] due to its assumptions of [physical environmental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biophysical_environment) setting.[^o9]

One prominently criticised assumption of the Bruun rule is its postulation of the net effects of longshore transport as negligible,[^o6][^o9][^o32] as the rule is by definition a two dimensional cross shore model that does not account for the longshore third dimension.[^o4] These longshore effects can, however, be the major cause of [sediment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sediment) erosion or [deposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deposition_%28geology%29) along beaches, dominating shoreline morphology and even masking the impacts of sea level rise as described by the Bruun Model.[^o6][^o9]

Another criticised assumption is the existence of a 'depth of closure'. The depth of closure is considered to be the water depth beyond which there are no significant changes in bed level, and is usually taken as the boundary between the [upper shoreface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_shoreface), characterised by breaking [waves](wikipedia://en/710251) and [bars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoal), and the [lower shoreface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_shoreface), characterised by nonbreaking waves and a lack of bars.[^o6] The Bruun rule stipulates that there is no significant [sediment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sediment) transfer across this boundary, however the strength of this concept in practice is debated.[^o6][^o32] The alternative R-DA model, proposed by Davidson-Arnott,[^o19] is based on the same assumptions as the Bruun rule, except it recognises significant sediment transfer between the upper and lower shoreface, hypothesising that sediment is eroded from the lower shoreface and transported to the upper shoreface to maintain an equilibrium profile, and that as a result there is an upward and landward migration of the depth of closure with sea level rise.[^o6][^o19]

Cooper and Pilkey have been direct in their criticism of the Bruun rule, publishing a paper in 2004 titled "Sea-level rise and shoreline retreat: time to abandon the Bruun Rule",[^o32] which is regularly cited in ensuing literature.**[^o32]** They argue that despite widespread criticism, the original Bruun rule continues to be applied in inappropriate contexts, as outlined by Bruun[^o35] and experimental history, and that the Bruun rule and its accompanying controversial assumptions are embedded in later models claiming to offer more sophisticated insights into coastal behaviour.[^o33] Cooper and Pilkey describe the use of the Bruun rule as "a "one model fits all" approach"[^o32] to a range of complex coastal environments for which it is unfit to describe.[^o32] They list three main reasons that the rule "does not work":[^o32] its restrictive assumptions, the omission of important [variables](wikipedia://en/30423282), and its reliance on outdated and erroneous concepts.[^o32] They describe the rule's restrictive assumptions as a closed materials balance, which ignores net [longshore transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longshore_drift), and the lack of an accretionary component, that assumes [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise) always results in [beach recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastal_erosion), which together limit the rule's use to a small number of coasts.[^o32] Cooper and Pilkey list the omitted [variables](wikipedia://en/30423282) as the presence of [outcrops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outcrop) or bottom [currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_current), the effect of [continental shelf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_shelf) [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) on retreat rate, site specific feedback relationships, and the highly variable patterns of coastal evolution at millennial [time scales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologic_time_scale), and the outdated and erroneous concepts relied upon as a universal equilibrium profile theory, a closure depth, and that unsupported idea that shoreface steepness has an effect on the rate of shore retreat.[^o32] They "conclude that \[the Bruun rule\] has outlived its usefulness and should be abandoned",[^o32] however they have been criticised for providing no "meaningful alternative"[^o15] to the rule.

In his papers,[^o2][^o34][^o35] Bruun did not present a rigorous mathematical derivation for his rule, which has caused confusion in the [research community](wikipedia://en/338705).[^o36] For example, Rosen in 1978, Allison and Schwartz in 1981, Dean and Maurmeyer in 1983, and Zhang, Douglas and Leatherman in 2004 have all mathematically derived the Bruun rule differently, with disagreement on the assumptions and limitations of the Bruun rule unique to their own derivations.[^o36] However, the latter revision by Zhang et al.[^o36] presents an alternative derivation showing that "even though very simple, the Bruun model has considerable generality".[^o36]

Some field and laboratory tests have supported the Bruun rule, although claimed [experimental](wikipedia://en/59861) flaws in these publications have been criticised.[^o9] Amongst others, Ranasinghe and Stive in 2009,[^o4] and later Andersen et al. in 2015,[^o9] have concluded that "no study has produced comprehensive, well-accepted verification of the Brunn model".[^o9] However, there is a near consensus that the basic [qualitative](wikipedia://en/371299) model of shoreline recession is valid,[^o4][^o9][^o7] despite [quantitative](wikipedia://en/389564) data gleaned from the Bruun rule being dubbed as "very coarse approximations"[^o9] or "broadly indicative estimates".[^o4] Despite these criticisms, the Bruun rule is credited for its simplicity, and there remains "no simple, viable alternative".[^o15]
