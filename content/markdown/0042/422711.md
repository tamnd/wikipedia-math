# Equivariant map

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Equivariant map"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **equivariance** is a form of [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) for [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) from one space with symmetry to another (such as [symmetric spaces](wikipedia://en/1642877)). A function is said to be an **equivariant map** when its domain and codomain are [acted on](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) by the same [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186), and when the function [commutes](wikipedia://en/294390) with the action of the group. That is, applying a symmetry transformation and then computing the function produces the same result as computing the function and then applying the transformation.

:::

Equivariant maps generalize the concept of [invariants](wikipedia://en/1126638), functions whose value is unchanged by a symmetry transformation of their argument. The value of an equivariant map is often (imprecisely) called an invariant.

In [statistical inference](wikipedia://en/27577), equivariance under statistical transformations of data is an important property of various estimation methods; see [invariant estimator](wikipedia://en/16959378) for details. In pure mathematics, equivariance is a central object of study in [equivariant topology](wikipedia://en/41144410) and its subtopics [equivariant cohomology](wikipedia://en/2976342) and [equivariant stable homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/42486328).

## Examples {#Examples}

### Elementary geometry {#Elementary_geometry}

*Image: Triangle.Centroid.svg.* The centroid of a triangle (where the three red segments meet) is equivariant under [affine transformations](wikipedia://en/38449): the centroid of a transformed triangle is the same point as the transformation of the centroid of the triangle.

In the geometry of [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654), the [area](wikipedia://en/1209) and [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636) of a triangle are invariants under [Euclidean transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_transformation): translating, rotating, or reflecting a triangle does not change its area or perimeter. However, [triangle centers](wikipedia://en/12401488) such as the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926), [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter), [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) and [orthocenter](wikipedia://en/161244) are not invariant, because moving a triangle will also cause its centers to move. Instead, these centers are equivariant: applying any Euclidean [congruence](wikipedia://en/39330) (a combination of a translation and rotation) to a triangle, and then constructing its center, produces the same point as constructing the center first, and then applying the same congruence to the center. More generally, all triangle centers are also equivariant under [similarity transformations](wikipedia://en/39377) (combinations of translation, rotation, reflection, and scaling),[^o5] and the centroid is equivariant under [affine transformations](wikipedia://en/38449).[^o6]

The same function may be an invariant for one group of symmetries and equivariant for a different group of symmetries. For instance, under similarity transformations instead of congruences the area and perimeter are no longer invariant: scaling a triangle also changes its area and perimeter. However, these changes happen in a predictable way: if a triangle is scaled by a factor of $s$, the perimeter also scales by $s$ and the area scales by $s^{2}$. In this way, the function mapping each triangle to its area or perimeter can be seen as equivariant for a multiplicative group action of the scaling transformations on the positive real numbers.

### Statistics {#Statistics}

Another class of simple examples comes from [statistical estimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_estimation). The [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) of a sample (a set of real numbers) is commonly used as a [central tendency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_tendency) of the sample. It is equivariant under [linear transformations](wikipedia://en/39712387) of the real numbers, so for instance it is unaffected by the choice of units used to represent the numbers. By contrast, the mean is not equivariant with respect to nonlinear transformations such as exponentials.

The [median](wikipedia://en/18837) of a sample is equivariant for a much larger group of transformations, the (strictly) [monotonic functions](wikipedia://en/48260) of the real numbers. This analysis indicates that the median is more [robust](wikipedia://en/2885691) against certain kinds of changes to a data set, and that (unlike the mean) it is meaningful for [ordinal data](wikipedia://en/52904244).[^o8]

The concepts of an [invariant estimator](wikipedia://en/16959378) and equivariant estimator have been used to formalize this style of analysis.

### Representation theory {#Representation_theory}

In the [representation theory of finite groups](wikipedia://en/874400), a vector space equipped with a group that acts by linear transformations of the space is called a [linear representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_representation) of the group. A [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) that commutes with the action is called an **intertwiner**. That is, an intertwiner is just an equivariant linear map between two representations. Alternatively, an intertwiner for representations of a group $G$ over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $K$ is the same thing as a [module homomorphism](wikipedia://en/276410) of $K[G]$-[modules](wikipedia://en/276410), where $K[G]$ is the [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) of *G*.[^o11]

Under some conditions, if *X* and *Y* are both [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982), then an intertwiner (other than the [zero map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_map)) only exists if the two representations are equivalent (that is, are [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) as [modules](wikipedia://en/276410)). That intertwiner is then unique [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) a multiplicative factor (a non-zero [scalar](wikipedia://en/3588331) from $K$). These properties hold when the image of $K[G]$ is a simple algebra, with centre $K$ (by what is called [Schur's lemma](wikipedia://en/521696): see [simple module](wikipedia://en/29430)). As a consequence, in important cases the construction of an intertwiner is enough to show the representations are effectively the same.[^o12]

## Formalization {#Formalization}

Equivariance can be formalized using the concept of a [$G$-set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) for a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$. This is a mathematical object consisting of a [mathematical set](wikipedia://en/26691) $S$ and a [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) (on the left) of $G$ on $S$. If $X$ and $Y$ are both $G$-sets for the same group $G$, then a function $f : X \to Y$ is said to be equivariant if

$$f(g\cdot x) = g\cdot f(x)$$

for all $g \in G$ and all $x\text{ in } X$.[^o14]

If one or both of the actions are right actions the equivariance condition may be suitably modified:

$f(x\cdot g) = f(x)\cdot g$; (right-right)

$f(x\cdot g) = g^{-1}\cdot f(x)$; (right-left)

$f(g\cdot x) = f(x)\cdot g^{-1}$; (left-right)

Equivariant maps are [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) in the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of *G*-sets (for a fixed *G*).[^o15] Hence they are also known as ***G*-morphisms**,[^o15] ***G*-maps**,[^o16] or ***G*-homomorphisms**.[^o17] [Isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/14828) of *G*-sets are simply [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) equivariant maps.[^o15]

## Generalization {#Generalization}

Equivariant maps can be generalized to arbitrary [categories](wikipedia://en/53759) in a straightforward manner. Every group *G* can be viewed as a category with a single object ([morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) in this category are just the elements of *G*). Given an arbitrary category *C*, a *representation* of *G* in the category *C* is a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from *G* to *C*.[^o20] Such a functor selects an object of *C* and a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of that object. For example, a *G*-set is equivalent to a functor from *G* to the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829), **Set**, and a linear representation is equivalent to a functor to the [category of vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_vector_spaces) over a field, **Vect**<sub>*K*</sub>.

Given two representations, ρ and σ, of *G* in *C*, an equivariant map between those representations is simply a [natural transformation](wikipedia://en/45022) from ρ to σ. Using natural transformations as morphisms, one can form the category of all representations of *G* in *C*. This is just the [functor category](wikipedia://en/407039) *C*<sup>*G*</sup>.

For another example, take *C* = **Top**, the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051). A representation of *G* in **Top** is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) on which *G* acts [continuously](wikipedia://en/6122). An equivariant map is then a continuous map *f*: *X* → *Y* between representations which commutes with the action of *G*.
