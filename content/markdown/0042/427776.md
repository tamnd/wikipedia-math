# Whitfield Diffie

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Whitfield Diffie"}

**Bailey Whitfield 'Whit' Diffie** [ForMemRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ForMemRS) (born June 5, 1944) is an American [cryptographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptographer) and mathematician and one of the pioneers of [public-key cryptography](wikipedia://en/24222) along with [Martin Hellman](wikipedia://en/422387) and [Ralph Merkle](wikipedia://en/25849). Diffie and Hellman's 1976 paper *New Directions in Cryptography*[^o25] introduced a radically new method of distributing [cryptographic](wikipedia://en/18934432) keys, that helped solve [key distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_distribution)—a fundamental problem in cryptography. Their technique became known as [Diffie–Hellman key exchange](wikipedia://en/7903). The article stimulated the almost immediate public development of a new class of encryption algorithms, the [asymmetric key algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymmetric_key_algorithm).[^o6]

:::

After his career at [Sun Microsystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Microsystems), where he became a Sun [Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow), Diffie served for two and a half years as Vice President for Information Security and Cryptography at the [Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ICANN) (2010–2012). He has also served as a visiting scholar (2009–2010) and affiliate (2010–2012) at the Freeman Spogli Institute's [Center for International Security and Cooperation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_International_Security_and_Cooperation) at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University), where he is currently a consulting scholar.[^o7]

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Diffie was born in [Washington, D.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_D.C.) His mother is Justine Louise (Whitfield), a writer and scholar. His father is [Bailey Wallys Diffie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bailey_W._Diffie), who taught [Iberian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_Peninsula) history and culture at the [City College of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_College_of_New_York).[^o22] His interest in cryptography began at "age 10 when his father, a professor, brought home the entire crypto shelf of the City College Library in New York".[^o22]

At [Jamaica High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaica_High_School) in [Queens, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queens,_New_York), Diffie "performed competently" but "never did apply himself to the degree his father hoped". Although he graduated with a local diploma, he did not take the statewide Regents examinations that would have awarded him an academic diploma because he had previously secured admission to the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology) on the basis of "stratospheric scores on standardized tests".[^o9] During the first two years of his undergraduate studies at MIT, he felt unengaged and seriously considered transferring to the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley), which he perceived as a more hospitable academic environment. At MIT, he began to program computers (in an effort to cultivate a practical skill set) while continuing to perceive the devices "as very low class... I thought of myself as a pure mathematician and was very interested in [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations) and [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) and things like that."[^o9]

Diffie received a [Bachelor of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science) with a major in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) from the MIT in 1965.[^o9]

## Career and research {#Career_and_research}

*Image: Whit Diffie at CFP 2007.jpg.* Diffie at Computers, Freedom, and Privacy conference (CFP) in 2007

From 1965 to 1969, he remained in [Greater Boston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Boston) as a research assistant for the [MITRE Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MITRE_Corporation) in [Bedford, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedford,_Massachusetts). As MITRE was a defense contractor, this position enabled Diffie (a pacifist who opposed the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War)) to avoid [the draft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_draft). During this period, he helped to develop [MATHLAB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MATHLAB) (an early symbolic manipulation system that served as the basis for [Macsyma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macsyma)) and other non-military applications.

In November 1969, Diffie became a research programmer at the [Stanford Artificial Intelligence Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_Artificial_Intelligence_Laboratory), where he worked on [LISP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisp_%28programming_language%29) 1.6 (widely distributed to [PDP-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDP-10) systems running the [TOPS-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TOPS-10) operating system) and [correctness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correctness_%28computer_science%29) problems while cultivating interests in cryptography and [computer security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_security) under the aegis of [John McCarthy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_McCarthy_%28computer_scientist%29).

Diffie left SAIL to pursue independent research in cryptography in May 1973. As the most current research in the field during the epoch fell under the classified oversight of the [National Security Agency](wikipedia://en/21939), Diffie "went around doing one of the things I am good at, which is digging up rare manuscripts in libraries, driving around, visiting friends at universities." He was assisted by his new girlfriend and future wife, Mary Fischer.[^o11]

In the summer of 1974, Diffie and Fischer met with a friend at the [Thomas J. Watson Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_J._Watson_Research_Center) (headquarters of [IBM Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Research)) in [Yorktown Heights, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorktown_Heights,_New_York), which housed one of the only nongovernmental cryptographic research groups in the United States. While group director Alan Konheim "couldn't tell \[Diffie\] very much because of a secrecy order," he advised him to meet with [Martin Hellman](wikipedia://en/422387), a young [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering) professor at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University) who was also pursuing a cryptographic research program.[^o12] A planned half-hour meeting between Diffie and Hellman extended over many hours as they shared ideas and information.[^o12]

Hellman then hired Diffie as a grant-funded part-time research programmer for the 1975 spring term. Under his sponsorship, he also enrolled as a doctoral student in electrical engineering at Stanford in June 1975; however, Diffie was once again unable to acclimate to "homework assignments \[and\] the structure" and eventually dropped out after failing to complete a required physical examination: "I didn't feel like doing it, I didn't get around to it."[^o9] Although it is unclear when he dropped out, Diffie remained employed in Hellman's lab as a research assistant through June 1978.[^o13]

In 1975–76, Diffie and Hellman criticized the [NBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Standards) proposed [Data Encryption Standard](wikipedia://en/7978), largely because its 56-bit key length was too short to prevent [brute-force attack](wikipedia://en/53784). An audio recording survives of their review of DES at Stanford in 1976 with Dennis Branstad of [NBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology) and representatives of the [National Security Agency](wikipedia://en/21939).[^o14] Their concern was well-founded: subsequent history has shown not only that NSA actively intervened with IBM and NBS to shorten the key size, but also that the short key size enabled exactly the kind of massively parallel key crackers that Hellman and Diffie sketched out. When these were ultimately built outside the classified world ([EFF DES cracker](wikipedia://en/839358)), they made it clear that DES was insecure and obsolete.

From 1978 to 1991, Diffie was Manager of Secure Systems Research for [Northern Telecom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nortel_Networks) in [Mountain View, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_View,_California), where he designed the key management architecture for the PDSO security system for [X.25](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X.25) networks.[^o15] He was named an [IEEE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE) fellow in 2025 for the development of public key cryptography and its applications.[^o16]

In 1991, he joined [Sun Microsystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Microsystems) Laboratories in [Menlo Park, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menlo_Park,_California), as a distinguished [engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineer), working primarily on public policy aspects of cryptography. Diffie remained with Sun, serving as its chief security officer and as a vice president until November 2009. He was also a Sun Fellow.[^o17]

, Diffie was a visiting professor at the [Information Security Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_Security_Group) based at [Royal Holloway, University of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Holloway,_University_of_London).[^o18]

In May 2010, Diffie joined the Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers ([ICANN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ICANN)) as vice president for information security and cryptography, a position he left in October 2012.[^o19]

Diffie is a member of the technical advisory boards of BlackRidge Technology, and [Cryptomathic](wikipedia://en/171385) where he collaborates with researchers such as [Vincent Rijmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_Rijmen), [Ivan Damgård](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Damgård) and [Peter Landrock](wikipedia://en/46710302).[^o20]

In 2018, he joined [Zhejiang University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhejiang_University), China, as a visiting professor, Cryptic Labs generated 2 months course in Zhejiang University.

### Public key cryptography {#Public_key_cryptography}

In the early 1970s, Diffie worked with [Martin Hellman](wikipedia://en/422387) to develop the fundamental ideas of dual-key, or [public key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_key), [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432). They published their results in 1976—solving one of the fundamental problems of cryptography, [key distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_distribution)—and essentially broke the [monopoly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopoly) that had previously existed where [government entities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_%28polity%29) controlled cryptographic technology and the terms on which other individuals could have access to it. "From the moment Diffie and Hellman published their findings..., the National Security Agency's crypto monopoly was effectively terminated. ... Every company, every citizen now had routine access to the sorts of cryptographic technology that not many years ago ranked alongside the atom bomb as a source of power."[^o22] The solution has become known as [Diffie–Hellman key exchange](wikipedia://en/7903).

### Awards and honors {#Awards_and_honors}

Together with [Martin Hellman](wikipedia://en/422387), Diffie won the 2015 [Turing Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_Award), widely considered the [most prestigious award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_prizes_known_as_the_Nobel_of_a_field) in the field of computer science. The citation for the award was: "For fundamental contributions to modern cryptography. Diffie and Hellman's groundbreaking 1976 paper, 'New Directions in Cryptography', introduced the ideas of [public-key cryptography](wikipedia://en/24222) and [digital signatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signatures), which are the foundation for most regularly-used security protocols on the internet today."[^o27]

Diffie received an [honorary doctorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorary_doctorate) from the [Swiss Federal Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_Federal_Institute_of_Technology_Zürich) in 1992.[^o15] He is also a fellow of the [Marconi Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marconi_Foundation) and visiting fellow of the [Isaac Newton Institute](wikipedia://en/736837). He has received various awards from other organisations. In July 2008, he was also awarded a Degree of Doctor of Science (Honoris Causa) by [Royal Holloway, University of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Holloway,_University_of_London).[^o28]

He was also awarded the [IEEE Donald G. Fink Prize Paper Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_Donald_G._Fink_Prize_Paper_Award) in 1981 (together with [Martin E. Hellman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_E._Hellman)),[^o29] [The Franklin Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Franklin_Institute)'s [Louis E. Levy Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_E._Levy_Medal) in 1997[^o30] a Golden Jubilee Award for Technological Innovation from the [IEEE Information Theory Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_Information_Theory_Society) in 1998,[^o31] and the [IEEE Richard W. Hamming Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_Richard_W._Hamming_Medal) in 2010.[^o32] In 2011, Diffie was inducted into the [National Inventors Hall of Fame](http://www.invent.org/honor/inductees/inductee-detail/?IID=428) and named a [Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow) of the [Computer History Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_History_Museum) "for his work, with Martin Hellman and Ralph Merkle, on public key cryptography."[^o33] Diffie was elected a [Foreign Member of the Royal Society (ForMemRS) in 2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_2017).[^o34] Diffie was also elected a member of the [National Academy of Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Engineering) in 2017 for the invention of public key cryptography and for broader contributions to privacy.

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Diffie self-identifies as an [iconoclast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iconoclasm). He has stated that he "was always concerned about [individuals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Individualism), an [individual's privacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privacy#An_individual_right) as opposed to [government secrecy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secrecy#Government)."[^o22]
