# Common graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Common graph"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), an area of mathematics, **common graphs** belong to a branch of [extremal graph theory](wikipedia://en/529568) concerning [inequalities in homomorphism densities](wikipedia://en/62469111). Roughly speaking, $F$ is a common [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) if it "commonly" appears as a subgraph, in a sense that the total number of copies of $F$ in any graph $G$ and its [complement](wikipedia://en/1175649) $\overline{G}$ is a large fraction of all possible copies of $F$ on the same vertices. Intuitively, if $G$ contains few copies of $F$, then its complement $\overline{G}$ must contain lots of copies of $F$ in order to compensate for it.

:::

Common graphs are closely related to other graph notions dealing with homomorphism density inequalities. For example, common graphs are a more general case of [Sidorenko graphs](wikipedia://en/62480458#Statement).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A graph $F$ is common if the inequality:

$$t(F, W) + t(F, 1 - W) \ge 2^{-e(F)+1}$$

holds for any [graphon](wikipedia://en/26987628) $W$, where $e(F)$ is the number of edges of $F$ and $t(F, W)$ is the [homomorphism density](wikipedia://en/62469111).[^o4]

The inequality is tight because the lower bound is always reached when $W$ is the constant graphon $W \equiv 1/2$.

:::

## Interpretations of definition {#Interpretations_of_definition}

For a graph $G$, we have $t(F, G) = t(F, W_{G})$ and $t(F, \overline{G})=t(F, 1 - W_G)$ for the [associated graphon](wikipedia://en/26987628#Analytic_Formulation) $W_G$, since graphon associated to the complement $\overline{G}$ is $W_{\overline{G}}=1 - W_G$. Hence, this formula provides us with the very informal intuition to take a close enough approximation, whatever that means,[^o6] $W$ to $W_G$, and see $t(F, W)$ as roughly the fraction of labeled copies of graph $F$ in "approximate" graph $G$. Then, we can assume the quantity $t(F, W) + t(F, 1 - W)$ is roughly $t(F, G) + t(F, \overline{G})$ and interpret the latter as the combined number of copies of $F$ in $G$ and $\overline{G}$. Hence, we see that $t(F, G) + t(F, \overline{G}) \gtrsim 2^{-e(F)+1}$ holds. This, in turn, means that common graph $F$ commonly appears as subgraph.

In other words, if we think of edges and non-edges as [2-coloring of edges](wikipedia://en/690647) of complete graph on the same vertices, then at least $2^{-e(F)+1}$ fraction of all possible copies of $F$ are monochromatic. Note that in a [Erdős–Rényi random graph](wikipedia://en/10477221) $G = G(n, p)$ with each edge drawn with probability $p=1/2$, each [graph homomorphism](wikipedia://en/676328) from $F$ to $G$ have probability $2 \cdot 2^{-e(F)} = 2^ {-e(F) +1}$of being monochromatic. So, common graph $F$ is a graph where it attains its minimum number of appearance as a monochromatic subgraph of graph $G$ at the graph $G=G(n, p)$ with $p=1/2$

$p=1/2$. The above definition using the generalized homomorphism density can be understood in this way.

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- As stated above, all Sidorenko graphs are common graphs.[^o9] Hence, any [known Sidorenko graph](wikipedia://en/62480458#Partial_results) is an example of a common graph, and, most notably, [cycles of even length](wikipedia://en/168609) are common.[^o10] However, these are limited examples since all Sidorenko graphs are [bipartite graphs](wikipedia://en/244431) while there exist non-bipartite common graphs, as demonstrated below.
- The [triangle graph](wikipedia://en/27071812) $K_{3}$ is one simple example of non-bipartite common graph.[^o11]
- $K_4 ^{-}$, the graph obtained by removing an edge of the [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) on 4 vertices $K_4$, is common.[^o12]
- Non-example: It was believed for a time that all graphs are common. However, it turns out that $K_{t}$ is not common for $t \ge 4$.[^o13] In particular, $K_4$ is not common even though $K_{4} ^{-}$ is common.

:::

## Proofs {#Proofs}

### Sidorenko graphs are common {#Sidorenko_graphs_are_common}

A graph $F$ is a Sidorenko graph if it satisfies $t(F, W) \ge t(K_2, W)^{e(F)}$ for all graphons $W$.

In that case, $t(F, 1 - W) \ge t(K_2, 1 - W)^{e(F)}$. Furthermore, $t(K_2, W) + t(K_2, 1 - W) = 1$, which follows from the definition of homomorphism density. Combining this with [Jensen's inequality](wikipedia://en/297811) for the function $f(x) = x^{e(F)}$:

$$t(F, W) + t(F, 1 - W) \ge t(K_2, W)^{e(F)} + t(K_2, 1 - W)^{e(F)} \ge 2 \bigg( \frac{t(K_2, W) + t(K_2, 1 - W)}{2} \bigg)^{e(F)} = 2^{-e(F) + 1}$$

Thus, the conditions for common graph is met.[^o17]

### The triangle graph is common {#The_triangle_graph_is_common}

Expand the integral expression for $t(K_3, 1 - W)$ and take into account the symmetry between the variables:

$$\int_{[0, 1]^3} (1 - W(x, y))(1 - W(y, z))(1 - W(z, x)) dx dy dz = 1 - 3 \int_{[0, 1]^2} W(x, y) + 3 \int_{[0, 1]^3} W(x, y) W(x, z) dx dy dz - \int_{[0, 1]^3} W(x, y) W(y, z) W(z, x) dx dy dz$$

Each term in the expression can be written in terms of homomorphism densities of smaller graphs. By the definition of homomorphism densities:

$$\int_{[0, 1]^2} W(x, y) dx dy = t(K_2, W)$$

$$\int{[0, 1]^3} W(x, y) W(x, z) dx dy dz = t(K_{1, 2}, W)$$

$$\int_{[0, 1]^3} W(x, y) W(y, z) W(z, x) dx dy dz = t(K_3, W)$$

where $K_{1, 2}$ denotes the [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) on $1$ vertex on one part and $2$ vertices on the other. It follows:

$$t(K_3, W) + t(K_3, 1 - W) = 1 - 3 t(K_2, W) + 3 t(K_{1, 2}, W).$$

$t(K_{1, 2}, W)$ can be related to $t(K_2, W)$ thanks to the symmetry between the variables $y$ and $z$:

$$\begin{alignedat}{4} t(K_{1, 2}, W) &= \int_{[0, 1]^3} W(x, y) W(x, z) dx dy dz && \\ &= \int_{x \in [0, 1]} \bigg( \int_{y \in [0, 1]} W(x, y) \bigg) \bigg( \int_{z \in [0, 1]} W(x, z) \bigg) && \\ &= \int_{x \in [0, 1]} \bigg( \int_{y \in [0, 1]} W(x, y) \bigg)^2 && \\ &\ge \bigg( \int_{x \in [0, 1]} \int_{y \in [0, 1]} W(x, y) \bigg)^2 = t(K_2, W)^2 \end{alignedat}$$

where the last step follows from the integral [Cauchy–Schwarz inequality](wikipedia://en/38128). Finally:

$$t(K_3, W) + t(K_3, 1 - W) \ge 1 - 3 t(K_2, W) + 3 t(K_{2}, W)^2 = 1/4 + 3 \big( t(K_2, W) - 1/2 \big)^2 \ge 1/4.$$

This proof can be obtained from taking the continuous analog of Theorem 1 in "On Sets Of Acquaintances And Strangers At Any Party"[^o19]
