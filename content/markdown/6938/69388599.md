# Polyhedral map projection

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Polyhedral map projection"}

*Image: Dymaxion projection.png.* Buckminster Fuller's Dymaxion map

A **polyhedral map projection** is a [map projection](wikipedia://en/51784) based on a [spherical polyhedron](wikipedia://en/10393861). Typically, the polyhedron is overlaid on the globe, and each face of the polyhedron is transformed to a polygon or other shape in the plane. The best-known polyhedral map projection is [Buckminster Fuller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckminster_Fuller)'s [Dymaxion map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dymaxion_map). When the spherical polyhedron faces are transformed to the faces of an ordinary [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) instead of laid flat in a plane, the result is a **polyhedral globe**.[^o10]

:::

Often the polyhedron used is a [Platonic solid](wikipedia://en/23905) or [Archimedean solid](wikipedia://en/1847). However, other polyhedra can be used: the [AuthaGraph projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AuthaGraph_projection) makes use of a polyhedron with 96 faces, and the myriahedral projection allows for an arbitrary large number of faces.[^o2] Although [interruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interruption_%28map_projection%29) between faces are common, and more common with an increasing number of faces, some maps avoid them: the [Lee conformal projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_conformal_projection) only has interruptions at its border, and the [AuthaGraph projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AuthaGraph_projection) scales its faces so that the map fills a rectangle without internal interruptions. Some projections can be [tesselated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesselate) to fill the plane, the Lee conformal projection among them.

To a degree, the polyhedron and the projection used to transform each face of the polyhedron can be considered separately, and some projections can be applied to differently shaped faces. The [gnomonic projection](wikipedia://en/1331480) transforms the edges of spherical polyhedra to straight lines, preserving all polyhedra contained within a hemisphere, so it is a common choice. The [Snyder equal-area projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snyder_equal-area_projection) can be applied to any polyhedron with regular faces.[^o3] The projection used in later versions of the Dymaxion map can be generalized to other equilateral triangular faces,[^o4] and even to certain quadrilaterals.[^o5]

Polyhedral map projections are useful for creating [discrete global grids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_global_grid), as with the [quadrilateralized spherical cube](wikipedia://en/18432868) and Icosahedral Snyder Equal Area (ISEA) grids.[^o6]

## History {#History}

The earliest known polyhedral projection is the [octant projection](wikipedia://en/47754920) developed by [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci) or his associate around 1514, which transforms the faces of an [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635) to [Reuleaux triangles](wikipedia://en/580252).[^o10]

[Christian Gottlieb Reichard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Gottlieb_Reichard) created a polyhedral globe based on the cube in 1803. An icosahedral globe appeared in 1851. Polyhedral globes cheaply constructed from cardboard were popular for a time in Europe.[^o10]

Projections based on [dihedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihedra) begin appearing with the [Peirce quincuncial projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peirce_quincuncial_projection) in 1879, [Guyou hemisphere-in-a-square projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guyou_hemisphere-in-a-square_projection) in 1887, and [Adams hemisphere-in-a-square projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adams_hemisphere-in-a-square_projection) in 1925. Although the dihedra are not traditional [polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedra) they are spherical polyhedra, and the methods used in these projections are also used in other polyhedral projections. In the same work as the hemisphere-in-a-square projection, [Adams](wikipedia://en/77543256) created maps depicting the entire globe in a [rhombus](wikipedia://en/183884), [hexagon](wikipedia://en/59733), and [hexagram](wikipedia://en/302441).[^o8][^o9]

[Bernard J. S. Cahill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_J._S._Cahill) invented the "butterfly map", based on the octahedron, in 1909. This was generalized into the [Cahill–Keyes projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahill–Keyes_projection) in 1975 and the [Waterman butterfly projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterman_butterfly_projection) in 1996. Cahill's work was also influential on Fuller's Dymaxion maps: Fuller's first version, based on a [cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/6280), was published in 1943, and his second, based on an icosahedron, was published in 1954.[^o10]

In 1965, Wellman Chamberlin (also known for his [Chamberlin trimetric projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamberlin_trimetric_projection)) and Howard E. Paine of the [National Geographic Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Geographic_Society) designed a polyhedral map based on the 12 equal pentagon faces of a [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407). 20 years later, Chamberlin and Paine used that polyhedral map in "Global Pursuit", a [board game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_game) intended to teach geography to children.[^o11][^o12]

The [quadrilateralized spherical cube](wikipedia://en/18432868) was devised in 1975 for the [Cosmic Background Explorer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_Background_Explorer) project.[^o13][^o14]

The [Traveller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traveller_%28role-playing_game%29) (1977) and [GURPS Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GURPS_Space) (1987) [role-playing games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Role-playing_game) use an icosahedral projection for planetary maps with a [hexagon grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagon_grid).
