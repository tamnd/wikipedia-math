# Prime gap

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Prime gap"}

A **prime gap** is the difference between two successive [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666). The $n$-th prime gap, denoted $g_n$ or $g(p_n)$ is the difference between the $(n+1)$th and the $n$-th prime numbers, i.e.,

:::

$$g_n = p_{n+1} - p_n$$

For example, since the first few primes are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11..., we have $g_1 = 1$, $g_2 = g_3 = 2$, $g_4 = 4$. The [sequence](wikipedia://en/51421) $g_n$ of prime gaps has been extensively studied; however, many questions and [conjectures](wikipedia://en/6138) remain unanswered.

The first 60 prime gaps are:

1, 2, 2, 4, 2, 4, 2, 4, 6, 2, 6, 4, 2, 4, 6, 6, 2, 6, 4, 2, 6, 4, 6, 8, 4, 2, 4, 2, 4, 14, 4, 6, 2, 10, 2, 6, 6, 4, 6, 6, 2, 10, 2, 4, 2, 12, 12, 4, 2, 4, 6, 2, 10, 6, 6, 6, 2, 6, 4, 2, ... .

By the definition of $g_n$ every prime can be written as

$$p_{n+1} = 2 + \sum_{i=1}^n g_i.$$

## Simple observations {#Simple_observations}

The first, smallest, and only [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) prime gap is the gap of size 1 between 2, the only [even](wikipedia://en/143135) prime number, and 3, the first odd prime. All other prime gaps are even. There is only one pair of consecutive gaps having length 2: the gaps $g_2$ and $g_3$ between the primes 3, 5, and 7.

For any [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $n$, the [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606) $n!$ is the product of all positive integers up to and including $n$. Then in the sequence

$$n!+2,\; n!+3,\; \ldots,\; n!+n,$$

the first term is [divisible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisible) by 2, the second term is divisible by 3, and so on. Thus, this is a sequence of *n* − 1 consecutive [composite](wikipedia://en/82289) integers, and it must belong to a gap between primes having length at least $n$. It follows that there are gaps between primes that are arbitrarily large, that is, for any integer $N$, there is an integer $m$ with $g_m \geq N$.

However, prime gaps of $n$ numbers can occur at numbers much smaller than $n!$. For instance, the first prime gap of size larger than 14 occurs between the primes 523 and 541, while 15! is the vastly larger number 1 307 674 368 000.

The average gap between primes increases as the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476) of these primes, and therefore the [ratio](wikipedia://en/87837) of the prime gap to the primes involved decreases (and is asymptotically zero). This is a consequence of the [prime number theorem](wikipedia://en/23692). From a heuristic view, we expect the probability that the ratio of the length of the gap to the natural logarithm is greater than or equal to a fixed positive number $k$ to be $e^{-k}$; consequently the ratio can be arbitrarily large. Indeed, the ratio of the gap to the number of digits of the integers involved does increase without bound. This is a consequence of a result by Westzynthius.[^o3]

In the opposite direction, the [twin prime conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_prime_conjecture) posits that $g_n=2$ for infinitely many integers $n$.

## Numerical results {#Numerical_results}

Usually the ratio $g_n / (\ln p_n)$ is called the *merit* of the gap $g_n$. Informally, the merit of a gap $g_n$ can be thought of as the ratio of the size of the gap compared to the average prime gap sizes in the vicinity of $p_n$.

## Further results {#Further_results}

### Upper bounds {#Upper_bounds}

[Bertrand's postulate](wikipedia://en/232526), [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) in 1852, states that there is always a prime number between $k$ and $2k$ (for all *k* ≥ 2), so in particular $p_{n+1} < 2 p_n$, which means $g_n < p_n$.

The [prime number theorem](wikipedia://en/23692), proven in 1896, says that the average length of the gap between a prime $p$ and the next prime will asymptotically approach $\ln p$, the natural logarithm of $p$, for sufficiently large primes. The actual length of the gap might be much more or less than this. However, one can deduce from the prime number theorem that the gaps get arbitrarily smaller in proportion to the primes: the quotient

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{g_n}{p_n}=0.$$

In other words (by [definition of a limit](wikipedia://en/26551602#Infinity_in_limits_of_functions)), for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there is a number $N$ such that for all $n > N$,

$$g_n < p_n\varepsilon$$

[Hoheisel](wikipedia://en/5384175) (1930) was the first to show[^o20] a sublinear dependence; that there exists a constant $\theta < 1$ such that

$$\pi(x + x^\theta) - \pi(x) \sim \frac{x^\theta}{\log x} \text{ as } x \to \infty,$$

hence showing that for [sufficiently large](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufficiently_large) $n$,

$$g_n < {p_n}^\theta$$

Hoheisel obtained the possible value 32999/33000 for $\theta$. This was improved to 249/250 by [Heilbronn](wikipedia://en/4388848),[^o21] and to $\theta = 3/4 + \varepsilon$, for any $\varepsilon > 0$, by [Chudakov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Chudakov).[^o22]

A major improvement is due to [Ingham](wikipedia://en/5380138),[^o23] who showed that for some positive constant *c*,

if $\zeta(1/2 + it) = O(t^c)$ then $\pi(x + x^\theta) - \pi(x) \sim \frac{x^\theta}{\log x}$ for any $\theta > (1 + 4c)/(2 + 4c).$

Here, *O* refers to the [big O notation](wikipedia://en/44578), *ζ* denotes the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809) and *π* the [prime-counting function](wikipedia://en/331826). Knowing that any *c* > 1/6 is admissible, one obtains that *θ* may be any number greater than 5/8.

Since 5/8+*ε* \< 2/3, and the gap between consecutive cubes is of the order of $n^{2/3}$, it follows that there is always a prime number between *n*<sup>3</sup> and (*n* + 1)<sup>3</sup>, if *n* is sufficiently large.[^o24] In 2016, Dudek gave an explicit version of Ingham's result: there are primes between consecutive cubes for all $n > e^{e^{33.217}}\approx1.01\cdot10^{115809466034000}$.[^o25]

The [Lindelöf hypothesis](wikipedia://en/1465085) would imply that Ingham's formula holds for *c* any positive number: but even this would not be enough to imply that there is a prime number between *n*<sup>2</sup> and (*n* + 1)<sup>2</sup> for *n* sufficiently large (see [Legendre's conjecture](wikipedia://en/2620450)). To verify this, a stronger result such as [Cramér's conjecture](wikipedia://en/290441) would be needed.

[Huxley](wikipedia://en/5385232) in 1972 showed that one may choose *θ* = 7/12 = $\overline{0.58}$.[^o26]

A result, due to Baker, [Harman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glyn_Harman) and [Pintz](wikipedia://en/22191053) in 2001, shows that *θ* may be taken to be 0.525.[^o27]

The above describes limits on *all* gaps; another area of interest is the *minimum* gap size. The [twin prime conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_prime_conjecture) asserts that there are always more gaps of size 2, but remains unproven. In 2005, [Daniel Goldston](wikipedia://en/2393553), [János Pintz](wikipedia://en/22191053) and [Cem Yıldırım](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cem_Yıldırım) proved that

$$\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{g_n}{\log p_n} = 0$$

and 2 years later improved this[^o28] to

$$\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{g_n}{\sqrt{\log p_n}(\log\log p_n)^2}<\infty.$$

In 2013, [Yitang Zhang](wikipedia://en/39441844) proved that

$$\liminf_{n\to\infty} g_n < 7\cdot 10^7,$$

meaning that there are infinitely many gaps that do not exceed 70 million.[^o29] A [Polymath Project](wikipedia://en/34858344) collaborative effort to optimize Zhang's bound managed to lower the bound to 4680 on July 20, 2013.[^o30] In November 2013, [James Maynard](wikipedia://en/42512102) introduced a new refinement of the [GPY sieve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPY_sieve), allowing him to reduce the bound to 600 and also show that the gaps between primes *m* apart are bounded for all *m*. That is, for any *m* there exists a bound *Δ<sub>m</sub>* such that *p*<sub>*n*+*m*</sub> − *p<sub>n</sub>* ≤ *Δ<sub>m</sub>* for infinitely many *n*.[^o31] Using Maynard's ideas, the Polymath project improved the bound to 246.[^o30][^o32]

On August 28, 2026, an argument discovered by GPT-5.6 Sol and presented by Hanxin Zhang claimed an improvement on the Polymath8b bound from 246 to 240.[^o33][^o34] 3 days later [Julia Stadlmann](wikipedia://en/84152308) submitted a preprint developing new techniques and also claiming to reduce the bound to 240.[^o35] In work dating 1 September, 2026 Shiva Kintali with unspecified AI agents claimed an improvement of the bound to 236.[^o36][^o37] On September 3 Axiom Math with AxiomProver claimed an improvement of the bound to 212[^o38] and a few hours before (on September 2 in USA) [OpenAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI) using [GPT-6 Astra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPT-6_Astra) produced a further claim to the bound to 186.[^o39]

As of September 2026, none of those claims has been peer-reviewed, leaving the established bound at 246.

Assuming the [Elliott–Halberstam conjecture](wikipedia://en/1898427) and [its generalized form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Elliott–Halberstam_conjecture), the bound has been reduced to 12 and 6, respectively.[^o30]

### Lower bounds {#Lower_bounds}

In 1931, Erik Westzynthius proved that maximal prime gaps grow more than logarithmically. That is,[^o3]

$$\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{g_n}{\log p_n}=\infty.$$

In 1938, [Robert Rankin](wikipedia://en/5884314) proved the existence of a constant *c* > 0 such that the inequality

$$g_n > \frac{c\ \log n\ \log\log n\ \log\log\log\log n}{(\log\log\log n)^2}$$

holds for infinitely many values of *n*, improving the results of Westzynthius and [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284). He later showed that one can take any constant *c* \< *e*<sup>*γ*</sup>, where *γ* is the [Euler–Mascheroni constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Mascheroni_constant). The value of the constant *c* was improved in 1997 to any value less than 2*e*<sup>*γ*</sup>.[^o41]

Paul Erdős offered a \$10,000 prize for a proof or disproof that the constant *c* in the above inequality may be taken arbitrarily large.[^o42] This was proved to be correct in 2014 by Ford–Green–Konyagin–Tao and, independently, James Maynard (the two works were submitted to arXiv within one day of each other).[^o43][^o44]

The result was further improved to

$$g_n > \frac{c\ \log n\ \log\log n\ \log\log\log\log n}{\log\log\log n}$$

for infinitely many values of *n* by Ford–Green–Konyagin–Maynard–Tao.[^o45]

In the spirit of Erdős' original prize, [Terence Tao](wikipedia://en/1118498) offered US\$10,000 for a proof that *c* may be taken arbitrarily large in this inequality.[^o46]

In 2026, a proof and formalization in [Lean](wikipedia://en/62889984) claiming authorship by AI model [GPT-5.6 Sol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPT-5.6_Sol) was uploaded to [Github](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Github), positing an improvement on the bound to:

$$g_n > \frac{c\ \log n\ \log\log n}{\log\log\log\log n}$$

The claimed proof has not yet been fully verified.[^o47][^o48]

Later that year, [OpenAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI) released a proof and formalization in [Lean](wikipedia://en/62889984) improving the bound to:[^o49][^o50]

$$g_n > \frac{c\ \log n\ (\log\log n)^2\ \log\log\log\log n}{(\log\log\log n)^2}$$

The claimed proof has not yet been fully verified.

Lower bounds for chains of primes have also been determined.[^o51]

## Conjectures about gaps between primes {#Conjectures_about_gaps_between_primes}

As described above, the best proven bound on gap sizes is *g*<sub>*n*</sub> \< *p*<sub>*n*</sub><sup>\0.525</sup> (for *n* sufficiently large; we do not worry about 5 − 3 > 3<sup>\0.525</sup> or 29 − 23 > 23<sup>\0.525</sup>), but it is observed that even maximal gaps are significantly smaller than that, leading to a plethora of unproven conjectures.

The first group hypothesize that the exponent can be reduced to *θ* = 0.5.

Both [Legendre's conjecture](wikipedia://en/2620450) that there always exists a prime between consecutive [perfect squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_numbers) and [Andrica's conjecture](wikipedia://en/7878893) that the difference of square roots of consecutive primes is bounded by 1[^o54] imply that

$$g_n < 2\sqrt{p_n}.$$

[Oppermann's conjecture](wikipedia://en/29543748) makes the stronger claim that, for sufficiently large *n* (probably *n* ≥ 31),

$$g_n < \sqrt{p_n}.$$

All of these remain unproved. [Harald Cramér](wikipedia://en/223129) came close, proving[^o55] that the [Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/19344125) implies the gap *g<sub>n</sub>* satisfies

$$g_n = O(\sqrt{p_n} \log p_n),$$

using the [big O notation](wikipedia://en/44578). (In fact this result needs only the weaker [Lindelöf hypothesis](wikipedia://en/1465085), if one can tolerate an exponent infinitesimally larger than 1/2.[^o56])

Dudek also proved an explicit version of Cramer's result (also assuming Riemann hypothesis) that is, for all *n* ≥ 2,

$$g_n < \frac{4}{\pi}\sqrt{p_n}\log p_n$$

*Image: Wikipedia primegaps.png.* Prime gap function

As noted by Dudek, for sufficiently large *n* one may reduce the constant $4/\pi$ to be any constant greater than 1.

In the same article, Cramér conjectured that the gaps are far smaller. Roughly speaking, [Cramér's conjecture](wikipedia://en/290441) states that

$$g_n = O\!\left((\log p_n)^2\right)\!,$$

a [polylogarithmic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polylogarithmic) growth rate slower than any exponent *θ* > 0.

Cramér's model, under which he made the conjecture, was oversimplified (assuming some events are statistically independent when they are dependent) and thus not very accurate (see [Cramér's conjecture](wikipedia://en/290441)), but after further investigations, new heuristics were found which became strong evidence that the conjecture is true.

As this matches the observed growth rate of prime gaps, there are a number of similar conjectures. [Firoozbakht's conjecture](wikipedia://en/36793030) is slightly stronger, stating that *p*<sub>*n*</sub><sup>1/*n*</sup> is a [strictly decreasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strictly_decreasing) function of *n*, i.e., for all n ≥ 1,

$$(p_{n+1})^{1/(n+1)} < (p_n)^{1/n}$$

If this conjecture is true, then *g*<sub>*n*</sub> \< (log *p*<sub>*n*</sub>)<sup>2</sup> − log *p*<sub>*n*</sub> − 1 for all *n* ≥ 10.[^o57][^o58] It implies a strong form of Cramér's conjecture but is inconsistent with the heuristics of [Granville](wikipedia://en/2910434) and [Pintz](wikipedia://en/22191053),[^o59][^o60][^o61] which suggest that *g*<sub>*n*</sub> > (2 − *ϵ*)*e*<sup>−*γ*</sup>(log *p*<sub>*n*</sub>)<sup>2</sup> > (1.1229 − *ϵ*)(log *p*<sub>*n*</sub>)<sup>2</sup> infinitely often for any *ϵ* > 0, where *γ* denotes the [Euler–Mascheroni constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Mascheroni_constant).

[Polignac's conjecture](wikipedia://en/3348772) states that every positive even number *k* occurs as a prime gap infinitely often. The case *k* = 2 is the [twin prime conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_prime_conjecture).

## As an arithmetic function {#As_an_arithmetic_function}

The gap *g*<sub>*n*</sub> between the *n*th and (*n* + 1)th prime numbers is an example of an [arithmetic function](wikipedia://en/3170). In this context it is usually denoted *d*<sub>*n*</sub> and called the prime difference function.[^o54] The function is neither [multiplicative](wikipedia://en/20059) nor [additive](wikipedia://en/87372).
