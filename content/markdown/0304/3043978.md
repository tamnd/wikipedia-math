# Generic point

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Generic point"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a **generic point** *P* of an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) *X* is a point in a *[general position](wikipedia://en/383736)*, at which all [generic properties](wikipedia://en/12886758) are true, a generic property being a property which is true for [almost every](wikipedia://en/44688) point.

:::

In classical algebraic geometry, a generic point of an [affine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_algebraic_variety) or [projective algebraic variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_algebraic_variety) of dimension *d* is a point such that the field generated by its coordinates has [transcendence degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_degree) *d* over the field generated by the coefficients of the equations of the variety.

In [scheme theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_theory), the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/37291) of an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) has a unique generic point, which is the zero ideal. As the closure of this point for the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744) is the whole spectrum, the definition has been extended to [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649), where a **generic point** of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X* is a point whose closure is *X*.

## Definition and motivation {#Definition_and_motivation}

A generic point of the topological space *X* is a point *P* whose [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) is all of *X*, that is, a point that is [dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_%28topology%29) in *X*.[^o3]

The terminology arises from the case of the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744) on the set of [subvarieties](wikipedia://en/248808) of an [algebraic set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_set): the algebraic set is [irreducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreducible_algebraic_set) (that is, it is not the union of two proper algebraic subsets) if and only if the topological space of the subvarieties has a generic point.

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The only [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637) that has a generic point is the [singleton set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singleton_set).
- Any [integral scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_scheme_theory) has a (unique) generic point; in the case of an affine integral scheme (i.e., the [prime spectrum](wikipedia://en/37291) of an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462)) the generic point is the point associated to the prime ideal (0).

:::

## History {#History}

In the foundational approach of [André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019), developed in his *Foundations of Algebraic Geometry*, generic points played an important role, but were handled in a different manner. For an algebraic variety *V* over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K*, *generic points* of *V* were a whole class of points of *V* taking values in a [universal domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_domain) Ω, an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) containing *K* but also an infinite supply of fresh indeterminates. This approach worked, without any need to deal directly with the topology of *V* (*K*-Zariski topology, that is), because the specializations could all be discussed at the field level (as in the [valuation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valuation_theory) approach to algebraic geometry, popular in the 1930s).

This was at a cost of there being a huge collection of equally generic points. [Oscar Zariski](wikipedia://en/359352), a colleague of Weil's at [São Paulo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/São_Paulo) just after [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), always insisted that generic points should be unique. (This can be put back into topologists' terms: Weil's idea fails to give a [Kolmogorov space](wikipedia://en/68121) and Zariski thinks in terms of the [Kolmogorov quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov_quotient).)

In the rapid foundational changes of the 1950s Weil's approach became obsolete. In [scheme theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_theory), though, from 1957, generic points returned: this time *à la Zariski*. For example for *R* a [discrete valuation ring](wikipedia://en/848633), *Spec*(*R*) consists of two points, a generic point (coming from the [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) {0}) and a **[closed point](wikipedia://en/33086408)** or **special point** coming from the unique [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164). For morphisms to *Spec*(*R*), the [fiber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber_%28algebraic_geometry%29) above the special point is the **special fiber**, an important concept for example in [reduction modulo p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduction_modulo_p), [monodromy theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monodromy_theory) and other theories about degeneration. The **generic fiber**, equally, is the fiber above the generic point. Geometry of degeneration is largely then about the passage from generic to special fibers, or in other words how specialization of parameters affects matters. (For a discrete valuation ring the topological space in question is the [Sierpinski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpinski_space) of topologists. Other [local rings](wikipedia://en/153391) have unique generic and special points, but a more complicated spectrum, since they represent general dimensions. The discrete valuation case is much like the complex [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738), for these purposes.)
