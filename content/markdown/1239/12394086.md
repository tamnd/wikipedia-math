# Self-similar process

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Self-similar process"}

**Self-similar processes** are [stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/47895) satisfying a mathematically precise version of the [self-similarity](wikipedia://en/28782) property. Several related properties have this name, and some are defined here.

:::

A self-similar phenomenon behaves the same when viewed at different degrees of magnification, or different scales on a dimension. Because stochastic processes are [random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variables) with a time and a space component, their self-similarity properties are defined in terms of how a scaling in time relates to a scaling in space.

## Distributional self-similarity {#Distributional_self-similarity}

*Image: Wiener process animated.gif.* A plot of $(1/\sqrt c) W_{ct}$ for $W$ a Brownian motion and *c* decreasing, demonstrating the self-similarity with parameter $H=1/2$.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

A [continuous-time stochastic process](wikipedia://en/12466697) $(X_t)_{t\ge0}$ is called *self-similar* with parameter $H>0$ if for all $a>0$, the processes $(X_{at})_{t\ge0}$ and $(a^HX_t)_{t\ge0}$ have the same [law](wikipedia://en/47895#Law).

:::

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

- The [Wiener process](wikipedia://en/149984) (or [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436)) is self-similar with $H=1/2$.
- The [fractional Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/1607785) is a generalisation of Brownian motion that preserves self-similarity; it can be self-similar for any $H\in(0,1)$.
- The class of self-similar [Lévy processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lévy_processes) are called [stable processes](wikipedia://en/38455452). They can be self-similar for any $H\in[1/2,\infty)$.

:::

## Second-order self-similarity {#Second-order_self-similarity}

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

A [wide-sense stationary process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wide-sense_stationary_process) $(X_n)_{n\ge0}$ is called *exactly second-order self-similar* with parameter $H>0$ if the following hold:

(i) $\mathrm{Var}(X^{(m)})=\mathrm{Var}(X)m^{2(H-1)}$, where for each $k\in\mathbb N_0$, $X^{(m)}_k = \frac 1 m \sum_{i=1}^m X_{(k-1)m + i},$

(ii) for all $m\in\mathbb N^+$, the [autocorrelation functions](wikipedia://en/2724#Definition_for_wide-sense_stationary_stochastic_process) $r$ and $r^{(m)}$ of $X$ and $X^{(m)}$ are equal.

If instead of (ii), the weaker condition

(iii) $r^{(m)} \to r$ pointwise as $m\to\infty$

holds, then $X$ is called *asymptotically second-order self-similar*.

:::

### Connection to long-range dependence {#Connection_to_long-range_dependence}

In the case $1/2<H<1$, asymptotic self-similarity is equivalent to [long-range dependence](wikipedia://en/1516694). Self-similar and long-range dependent characteristics in computer networks present a fundamentally different set of problems to people doing analysis and/or design of networks, and many of the previous assumptions upon which systems have been built are no longer valid in the presence of self-similarity.

Long-range dependence is closely connected to the theory of [heavy-tailed distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy-tailed_distributions).[^o11] A distribution is said to have a heavy tail if

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} e^{\lambda x}\Pr[X>x] = \infty \quad \mbox{for all } \lambda>0.\,$$

One example of a heavy-tailed distribution is the [Pareto distribution](wikipedia://en/53057). Examples of processes that can be described using heavy-tailed distributions include traffic processes, such as packet inter-arrival times and burst lengths.

::: {#o13 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

- The [Tweedie convergence theorem](wikipedia://en/15204099#Tweedie_convergence_and_1/f_noise) can be used to explain the origin of the [variance to mean power law](wikipedia://en/34983797), [*1/f* noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pink_noise) and [multifractality](wikipedia://en/6498864), features associated with self-similar processes.
- [Ethernet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethernet) traffic data is often self-similar. Empirical studies of measured traffic traces have led to the wide recognition of self-similarity in network traffic.

:::
