# Maximum-entropy random graph model

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Maximum-entropy random graph model"}

**Maximum-entropy random graph models** are [random graph](wikipedia://en/358477) models used to study [complex networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_networks) subject to the [principle of maximum entropy](wikipedia://en/201718) under a set of structural constraints,[^o2] which may be global, distributional, or local.

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

Any random graph model (at a fixed set of parameter values) results in a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) on [graphs](wikipedia://en/325806), and those that are maximum [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) within the considered class of distributions have the special property of being maximally [unbiased](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unbiased) [null models](wikipedia://en/37658021) for network inference[^o4] (e.g. [biological network inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_network_inference)). Each model defines a family of probability distributions on the set of graphs of size $n$ (for each $n>n_0$ for some finite $n_0$), parameterized by a collection of constraints on $J$ observables $\{Q_j(G)\}_{j=1}^J$ defined for each graph $G$ (such as fixed expected average [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448), [degree distribution](wikipedia://en/2129782) of a particular form, or specific [degree sequence](wikipedia://en/1374448#Degree_sequence)), enforced in the graph distribution alongside entropy maximization by the [method of Lagrange multipliers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_Lagrange_multipliers). Note that in this context "maximum entropy" refers not to the [entropy of a single graph](wikipedia://en/52204420), but rather the entropy of the whole probabilistic ensemble of random graphs.

Several commonly studied random network models are in fact maximum entropy, for example the [ER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erdos-Renyi_model) graphs $G(n,m)$ and $G(n,p)$ (which each have one global constraint on the number of edges), as well as the [configuration model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Configuration_model) (CM).[^o5] and [soft configuration model](wikipedia://en/58486357) (SCM) (which each have $n$ local constraints, one for each nodewise degree-value). In the two pairs of models mentioned above, an important distinction[^o6][^o7] is in whether the constraint is sharp (i.e. satisfied by every element of the set of size-$n$ graphs with nonzero probability in the ensemble), or soft (i.e. satisfied on average across the whole ensemble). The former (sharp) case corresponds to a [microcanonical ensemble](wikipedia://en/1136447),[^o8] the condition of maximum entropy yielding all graphs $G$ satisfying $Q_j(G)=q_j\forall j$ as equiprobable; the latter (soft) case is [canonical](wikipedia://en/1137672),[^o9] producing an [exponential random graph model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_random_graph_model) (ERGM).

| [ER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erdos-Renyi_model), $G(n,p)$ | Soft, global | Expected total edge-count $\|E(G)\|$ | $p^{\|E(G)\|}(1-p)^{\binom{n}{2}-\|E(G)\|}$ |

## Canonical ensemble of graphs (general framework) {#Canonical_ensemble_of_graphs_(general_framework)}

Suppose we are building a random graph model consisting of a probability distribution $\mathbb{P}(G)$ on the set $\mathcal{G}_n$ of [simple graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_graph) with $n$ vertices. The [Gibbs entropy](wikipedia://en/4701125#Gibbs_entropy_formula) $S[G]$ of this ensemble will be given by

$$S[G]=-\sum_{G\in \mathcal{G}_n}\mathbb{P}(G)\log\mathbb{P}(G).$$

We would like the ensemble-averaged values $\{\langle Q_j \rangle\}_{j=1}^J$ of observables $\{Q_j(G)\}_{j=1}^J$ (such as average [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448), average [clustering](wikipedia://en/1457636), or [average shortest path length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Average_path_length)) to be tunable, so we impose $J$ "soft" constraints on the graph distribution:

$$\langle Q_j \rangle = \sum_{G\in \mathcal{G}_n}\mathbb{P}(G)Q_j(G) = q_j,$$

where $j=1,...,J$ label the constraints. Application of the method of Lagrange multipliers to determine the distribution $\mathbb{P}(G)$ that maximizes $S[G]$ while satisfying $\langle Q_j \rangle=q_j$, and the normalization condition $\sum_{G\in \mathcal{G}_n}\mathbb{P}(G)=1$ results in the following:[^o2]

$$\mathbb{P}(G) = \frac{1}{Z}\exp\left[-\sum_{j=1}^J\psi_j Q_j(G)\right],$$

where $Z$ is a normalizing constant (the [partition function](wikipedia://en/16846849)) and $\{\psi_j\}_{j=1}^J$ are parameters (Lagrange multipliers) coupled to the correspondingly indexed graph observables, which may be tuned to yield graph samples with desired values of those properties, on average; the result is an exponential family and [canonical ensemble](wikipedia://en/1137672); specifically yielding an [ERGM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_random_graph_model).

## The Erdős–Rényi model $G(n,m)$ {#The_Erdős–Rényi_model_G(n,m)}

In the canonical framework above, constraints were imposed on ensemble-averaged quantities $\langle Q_j \rangle$. Although these properties will on average take on values specifiable by appropriate setting of $\{\psi_j\}_{j=1}^J$, each specific instance $G$ may have $Q_j(G)\ne q_j$, which may be undesirable. Instead, we may impose a much stricter condition: every graph with nonzero probability must satisfy $Q_j(G)= q_j$ exactly. Under these "sharp" constraints, the maximum-entropy distribution is determined. We exemplify this with the Erdős–Rényi model $G(n,m)$.

The sharp constraint in $G(n,m)$ is that of a fixed number of [edges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_graph_theory_terms#edge) $m$,[^o17] that is $|\operatorname E(G)|=m$, for all graphs $G$ drawn from the ensemble (instantiated with a probability denoted $\mathbb{P}_{n,m}(G)$). This restricts the sample space from $\mathcal{G}_n$ (all graphs on $n$ vertices) to the subset $\mathcal{G}_{n,m}=\{g\in\mathcal{G}_n;|\operatorname E(g)|=m\}\subset \mathcal{G}_n$. This is in direct analogy to the [microcanonical ensemble](wikipedia://en/1136447) in classical [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481), wherein the system is restricted to a thin manifold in the [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) of all states of a particular [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) value.

Upon restricting our sample space to $\mathcal{G}_{n,m}$, we have no external constraints (besides normalization) to satisfy, and thus we'll select $\mathbb{P}_{n,m}(G)$ to maximize $S[G]$ without making use of Lagrange multipliers. It is well known that the entropy-maximizing distribution in the absence of external constraints is the [uniform distribution](wikipedia://en/1699214) over the sample space (see [maximum entropy probability distribution](wikipedia://en/1813193)), from which we obtain:

$$\mathbb{P}_{n,m}(G)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_{n,m}|}=\binom{\binom{n}{2}}{m}^{-1},$$

where the last expression in terms of [binomial coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_coefficients) is the number of ways to place $m$ edges among $\binom{n}{2}$ possible [edges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_%28graph_theory%29), and thus is the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of $\mathcal{G}_{n,m}$.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

A variety of maximum-entropy ensembles have been studied on generalizations of simple graphs. These include, for example, ensembles of simplicial complexes,[^o20] and weighted random graphs with a given expected degree sequence [^o21]
