# Great dirhombicosidodecahedron

Uniform polyhedron stat table

*Image: Great dirhombicosidodecahedron.stl.* 3D model of a great dirhombicosidodecahedron.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Great dirhombicosidodecahedron"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **great dirhombicosidodecahedron** (or **great snub disicosidisdodecahedron**) is a degenerate [nonconvex uniform polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonconvex_uniform_polyhedron), indexed last as $U_{75}$. It has 124 faces (40 [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654), 60 [squares](wikipedia://en/659939), and 24 [pentagrams](wikipedia://en/197544)), 240 [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034), and 60 [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417).[^o2]

:::

It has been nicknamed "Miller's monster"[^n1] (after [J. C. P. Miller](wikipedia://en/20706909), who with [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328) and [M. S. Longuet-Higgins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._S._Longuet-Higgins) enumerated the [uniform polyhedra](wikipedia://en/2537223) in 1954).

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

This is the only [non-degenerate](wikipedia://en/232952) uniform polyhedron with more than six faces meeting at a vertex. Each vertex has 4 squares which pass through the vertex central axis (and thus through the centre of the figure), alternating with two triangles and two pentagrams. Since all its 60 double-square facial planes pass through its center, the great dirhombicosidodecahedron classifies as a [hemipolyhedron](wikipedia://en/24084383).[^n2] Another unusual feature is that the faces all occur in coplanar pairs.

This is also the only uniform polyhedron that cannot be made by the [Wythoff construction](wikipedia://en/2950777) from a spherical triangle. It has a special [Wythoff symbol](wikipedia://en/3691548) $| \tfrac{3}{2} \tfrac{5}{3} 3 \tfrac{5}{2},$ relating it to a spherical [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278). This symbol suggests that it is a sort of [snub polyhedron](wikipedia://en/3752558), except that instead of the non-snub faces being surrounded by snub triangles as in most snub polyhedra, they are surrounded by snub squares.

### Related polyhedra {#Related_polyhedra}

If the definition of a uniform polyhedron is relaxed to allow any [even number](wikipedia://en/143135) of faces adjacent to an edge, then this definition gives rise to one further polyhedron: the [great disnub dirhombidodecahedron](wikipedia://en/4145225) which has the same vertices and edges but with a different arrangement of triangular faces.

The vertices and edges are also shared with the uniform compounds of [20 octahedra](wikipedia://en/14737027) or [20 tetrahemihexahedra](wikipedia://en/14736821). 180 of the 240 edges are shared with the [great snub dodecicosidodecahedron](wikipedia://en/4157674).

This polyhedron is related to the [nonconvex great rhombicosidodecahedron](wikipedia://en/4157639) (quasirhombicosidodecahedron) by a branched cover: there is a function from the great dirhombicosidodecahedron to the quasirhombicosidodecahedron that is 2-to-1 everywhere, except for the vertices.[^o11]

## Cartesian coordinates {#Cartesian_coordinates}

Let the point $p$ be given by

$$p= \begin{pmatrix} \phi^{-\frac12} \\ 0 \\ \phi^{-1} \end{pmatrix},$$

where $\phi$ is the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386). Let the matrix $M$ be given by

$$M= \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & -\phi/2 & 1/(2\phi) \\ \phi/2 & 1/(2\phi) & -1/2 \\ 1/(2\phi) & 1/2 & \phi/2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

$M$ is the rotation around the axis $(1, 0, \phi)$ by an angle of $2\pi/5$, counterclockwise. Let the linear transformations $T_0, \ldots, T_{11}$ be the transformations which send a point $(x, y, z)$ to the [even permutations](wikipedia://en/49727) of $(\pm x, \pm y, \pm z)$ with an even number of minus signs. The transformations $T_i$ constitute the group of rotational symmetries of a [regular tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/5242521). The transformations $T_i M^j$ $(i = 0,\ldots, 11$, $j = 0,\ldots, 4)$ constitute the group of rotational symmetries of a [regular icosahedron](wikipedia://en/14968). Then the 60 points $T_i M^j p$ are the vertices of a great dirhombicosidodecahedron. The edge length equals $\sqrt2$, the circumradius equals $1$, and the midradius equals $\frac12\sqrt2$.

For a great dirhombicosidodecahedron whose edge length is 1, the circumradius is

$$R = \frac12\sqrt2.$$

Its midradius is

$$r=\frac12.$$
