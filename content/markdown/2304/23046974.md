# Omega and agemo subgroup

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Omega and agemo subgroup"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), or more specifically [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), the **omega** and **agemo** [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) described the so-called "power structure" of a [finite](wikipedia://en/323707) [*p*-group](wikipedia://en/24664). They were introduced in (Hall 1933) where they were used to describe a class of finite *p*-groups whose structure was sufficiently similar to that of finite [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) *p*-groups, the so-called, [regular p-groups](wikipedia://en/15442661). The relationship between power and [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193) structure forms a central theme in the modern study of *p*-groups, as exemplified in the work on uniformly [powerful p-groups](wikipedia://en/1841291).

:::

The word "agemo" is just "omega" spelled backwards, and the agemo subgroup is denoted by an upside-down omega (℧).

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The omega subgroups are the series of subgroups of a finite p-group, *G*, indexed by the natural numbers:

$$\Omega_i(G) = \langle \{g\in G : g^{p^i} = 1 \} \rangle.$$

The agemo subgroups are the series of subgroups:

$$\mho^i(G) = \langle \{ g^{p^i} : g \in G \} \rangle.$$

When *i* = 1 and *p* is odd, then *i* is normally omitted from the definition. When *p* is even, an omitted *i* may mean either *i* = 1 or *i* = 2 depending on local convention. In this article, we use the convention that an omitted *i* always indicates *i* = 1.

:::

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The [dihedral group of order 8](wikipedia://en/78749515), *G*, satisfies: ℧(*G*) = Z(*G*) = \[ *G*, *G* \] = Φ(*G*) = Soc(*G*) is the unique normal subgroup of order 2, typically realized as the subgroup containing the identity and a 180° rotation. However Ω(*G*) = *G* is the entire group, since *G* is generated by reflections. This shows that Ω(*G*) need not be the set of elements of order *p*.

The [quaternion group of order 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternion_group_of_order_8), *H*, satisfies Ω(*H*) = ℧(*H*) = Z(*H*) = \[ *H*, *H* \] = Φ(*H*) = Soc(*H*) is the unique subgroup of order 2, normally realized as the subgroup containing only 1 and −1.

The [Sylow *p*-subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylow_subgroup), *P*, of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) on *p*<sup>2</sup> points is the [wreath product](wikipedia://en/146625) of two [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) of prime order. When *p* = 2, this is just the dihedral group of order 8. It too satisfies Ω(*P*) = *P*. Again ℧(*P*) = Z(*P*) = Soc(*P*) is cyclic of order *p*, but \[ *P*, *P* \] = Φ(*G*) is [elementary abelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_abelian) of order *p*<sup>*p*−1</sup>.

The [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) of a cyclic group of order 4 acting non-trivially on a cyclic group of order 4,

$$K = \langle a,b : a^4 = b^4 = 1, ba=ab^3 \rangle,$$

has ℧(*K*) elementary abelian of order 4, but the set of squares is simply { 1, *aa*, *bb* }. Here the element *aabb* of ℧(*K*) is not a square, showing that ℧ is not simply the set of squares.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

In this section, let *G* be a finite *p*-group of [order](wikipedia://en/493527) |*G*| = *p*<sup>*n*</sup> and [exponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponent_%28group_theory%29) exp(*G*) = *p*<sup>*k*</sup>. Then the omega and agemo families satisfy a number of useful properties.

**General properties**

- Both Ω<sub>*i*</sub>(*G*) and ℧<sup>*i*</sup>(*G*) are [characteristic subgroups](wikipedia://en/7198) of *G* for all natural numbers, *i*.
- The omega and agemo subgroups form two [normal series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_series):

*G* = ℧<sup>0</sup>(*G*) ≥ ℧<sup>1</sup>(*G*) ≥ ℧<sup>2</sup>(*G*) ≥ ... ≥ ℧<sup>*k*−2</sup>(*G*) ≥ ℧<sup>*k*−1</sup>(*G*) > ℧<sup>*k*</sup>(*G*) = 1

*G* = Ω<sub>*k*</sub>(*G*) ≥ Ω<sub>*k*−1</sub>(*G*) ≥ Ω<sub>*k*−2</sub>(*G*) ≥ ... ≥ Ω<sub>2</sub>(*G*) ≥ Ω<sub>1</sub>(*G*) > Ω<sub>0</sub>(*G*) = 1

and the series are loosely intertwined: For all *i* between 1 and *k*:

℧<sup>*i*</sup>(*G*) ≤ Ω<sub>*k*−*i*</sub>(*G*), but

℧<sup>*i*−1</sup>(*G*) is not contained in Ω<sub>*k*−*i*</sub>(*G*).

**Behavior under quotients and subgroups**

If *H* ≤ *G* is a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of *G* and *N* ⊲ *G* is a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of *G*, then:

- ℧<sup>*i*</sup>(*H*) ≤ *H* ∩ ℧<sup>*i*</sup>(*G*)
- ℧<sup>*i*</sup>(*N*) ⊲ *G*
- Ω<sub>*i*</sub>(*N*) ⊲ *G*
- ℧<sup>*i*</sup>(*G*/*N*) = ℧<sup>*i*</sup>(*G*)*N*/*N*
- Ω<sub>*i*</sub>(*G*/*N*) ≥ Ω<sub>*i*</sub>(*G*)*N*/*N*

**Relation to other important subgroups**

- Soc(*G*) = Ω(Z(*G*)), the subgroup consisting of central elements of order *p* is the [socle](wikipedia://en/3571350), Soc(*G*), of *G*
- *Φ*(*G*) = ℧(*G*)\[*G*,*G*\], the subgroup generated by all *p*th powers and [commutators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutators) is the [Frattini subgroup](wikipedia://en/540707), Φ(*G*), of *G*.

**Relations in special classes of groups**

- In an abelian *p*-group, or more generally in a regular *p*-group:

\|℧<sup>*i*</sup>(*G*)|⋅|Ω<sub>*i*</sub>(*G*)| = |*G*|

\[℧<sup>*i*</sup>(*G*):℧<sup>*i*+1</sup>(*G*)\] = \[Ω<sub>*i*</sub>(*G*):Ω<sub>*i*+1</sub>(*G*)\],

where |*H*| is the [order](wikipedia://en/493527) of *H* and \[*H*:*K*\] = |*H*|/|*K*| denotes the [index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_%28group_theory%29) of the subgroups *K* ≤ *H*.

:::

::: {#o10 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The first application of the omega and agemo subgroups was to draw out the analogy of [regular](wikipedia://en/15442661) *p*-groups with [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) *p*-groups in (Hall 1933).

Groups in which Ω(*G*) ≤ Z(*G*) were studied by [John G. Thompson](wikipedia://en/862189) and have seen several more recent applications.

The dual notion, groups with \[*G*,*G*\] ≤ ℧(*G*) are called [powerful p-groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powerful_p-groups) and were introduced by [Avinoam Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avinoam_Mann). These groups were critical for the proof of the **coclass conjectures** which introduced an important way to understand the structure and classification of finite *p*-groups.

:::
