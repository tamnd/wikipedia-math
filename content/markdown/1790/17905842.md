# Linear flow on the torus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Linear flow on the torus"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially in the area of [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) known as [dynamical systems theory](wikipedia://en/990632), a **linear flow on the torus** is a [flow](wikipedia://en/2619735) on the *n*-dimensional [torus](wikipedia://en/74800)

$$\mathbb{T}^n = \underbrace{S^1 \times S^1 \times \cdots \times S^1}_n,$$

which is represented by the following differential equations with respect to the standard angular coordinates $\left(\theta_1, \theta_2, \ldots, \theta_n\right):$

$$\frac{d\theta_1}{dt} = \omega_1, \quad \frac{d\theta_2}{dt} = \omega_2,\quad \ldots, \quad \frac{d\theta_n}{dt} = \omega_n.$$

The solution of these equations can explicitly be expressed as

$$\Phi_\omega^t(\theta_1, \theta_2, \dots, \theta_n) = (\theta_1 + \omega_1 t, \theta_2 + \omega_2 t, \dots, \theta_n + \omega_n t) \bmod 2 \pi.$$

If we represent the torus as $\mathbb{T}^n = \mathbb{R}^n / \mathbb{Z}^n$ we see that a starting point is moved by the flow in the direction $\omega = \left(\omega_1, \omega_2, \ldots, \omega_n\right)$ at constant speed and when it reaches the border of the unitary $n$-cube it jumps to the opposite face of the cube.

:::

*Image: Irrational Rotation on a 2 Torus.png.* Irrational rotation on a 2-torus

For a linear flow on the torus, all orbits are either [periodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_orbit) or [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) on a subset of the $n$-torus, which is a $k$-torus. When the components of $\omega$ are [rationally independent](wikipedia://en/17904953) all the orbits are dense on the whole space. This can be easily seen in the two-dimensional case: if the two components of $\omega$ are rationally independent, the [Poincaré section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_section) of the flow on an edge of the unit square is an [irrational rotation](wikipedia://en/9445837) on a circle and therefore its orbits are dense on the circle, as a consequence the orbits of the flow must be dense on the torus.

## Irrational winding of a torus {#Irrational_winding_of_a_torus}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), an **irrational winding of a torus** is a continuous [injection](wikipedia://en/45196) of a [line](wikipedia://en/946975) into a two-dimensional [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) that is used to set up several counterexamples.[^o3] A related notion is the [Kronecker foliation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kronecker_foliation) of a torus, a foliation formed by the set of all translates of a given irrational winding.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

One way of constructing a torus is as the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) $\mathbb{T^2} = \mathbb{R}^2 / \mathbb{Z}^2$ of a two-dimensional real vector space by the additive subgroup of integer vectors, with the corresponding [projection](wikipedia://en/237213) $\pi : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{T^2}.$ Each point in the torus has as its preimage one of the translates of the square lattice $\mathbb{Z}^2$ in $\mathbb{R}^2,$ and $\pi$ factors through a map that takes any point in the plane to a point in the [unit square](wikipedia://en/285301) $[0, 1)^2$ given by the fractional parts of the original point's Cartesian coordinates.

Now consider a line in $\mathbb{R}^2$ given by the equation $y = k x.$ If the slope $k$ of the line is [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024), it can be represented by a fraction and a corresponding lattice point of $\mathbb{Z}^2.$ It can be shown that then the projection of this line is a [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_curve) [closed curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_curve) on a torus.

If, however, $k$ is [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689), it will not cross any lattice points except 0, which means that its projection on the torus will not be a closed curve, and the restriction of $\pi$ on this line is [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective_mapping). Moreover, it can be shown that the image of this restricted projection as a subspace, called the irrational winding of a torus, is [dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_subspace) in the torus.

:::

::: {#o7 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

### Applications {#Applications}

Irrational windings of a torus may be used to set up counter-examples related to [monomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monomorphisms). An irrational winding is an [immersed submanifold](wikipedia://en/525095#Immersed_submanifolds) but not a [regular submanifold](wikipedia://en/525095#Embedded_submanifolds) of the torus, which shows that the image of a manifold under a [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) injection to another manifold is not necessarily a (regular) submanifold.[^o8] Irrational windings are also examples of the fact that the topology of the submanifold does not have to coincide with the [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926) of the submanifold.[^o8]

Secondly, the torus can be considered as a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) $U(1) \times U(1)$, and the line can be considered as $\mathbb{R}$. It is then easy to show that the image of the continuous and analytic [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) $x \mapsto \left(e^{ix}, e^{ikx}\right)$ is not a regular submanifold for irrational $k,$[^o8][^o9] although it is an immersed submanifold, and therefore a Lie subgroup. It may also be used to show that if a subgroup $H$ of the Lie group $G$ is not closed, the quotient $G / H$ does not need to be a manifold[^o10] and might even fail to be a [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637).

:::
