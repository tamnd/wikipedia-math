# Trigonometric series

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Trigonometric series"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **trigonometric series** are a special class of [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/71675950) [series](wikipedia://en/15287) of the form[^n1][^n2]

:::

$$A_0 + \sum_{n=1}^\infty A_n \cos{(nx)} + B_n \sin{(nx)},$$

where $x$ is the variable and $\{A_n\}$ and $\{B_n\}$ are [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203). It is an infinite version of a [trigonometric polynomial](wikipedia://en/591568).

A trigonometric series is called the [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) of the [integrable function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_function) $f$ if the coefficients have the form:

$$A_n=\frac1\pi \int^{2 \pi}_0\! f(x) \cos{(nx)} \,dx$$

$$B_n=\frac{1}{\pi}\displaystyle\int^{2 \pi}_0\! f(x) \sin{(nx)} \, dx$$

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Fourier series for the identity function.png.* The Fourier series for the identity function suffers from the [Gibbs phenomenon](wikipedia://en/373986) near the ends of the periodic interval.

Every Fourier series gives an example of a trigonometric series. Let the function $f(x) = x$ on $[-\pi,\pi]$ be extended periodically (see [sawtooth wave](wikipedia://en/52147)). Then its Fourier coefficients are:

$$\begin{aligned} A_n &= \frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x \cos{(nx)} \,dx = 0, \quad n \ge 0. \\[4pt] B_n &= \frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x \sin{(nx)} \, dx \\[4pt] &= -\frac{x}{n\pi} \cos{(nx)} + \frac1{n^2\pi}\sin{(nx)} \Bigg\vert_{x=-\pi}^\pi \\[5mu] &= \frac{2\,(-1)^{n+1}}{n}, \quad n \ge 1.\end{aligned}$$

Which gives an example of a trigonometric series:

$$2\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} \sin{(nx)} = 2\sin{(x)} - \frac22\sin{(2x)} + \frac23\sin{(3x)} - \frac24\sin{(4x) } + \cdots$$

*Image: Trigonometric series with wiggles and spike.png.* The trigonometric series $\sin 2x\,/\,\log 2$ $+ \sin 3x\,/\,\log 3$ $+ \sin 4x\,/\,\log 4 + ...$ is not a Fourier series.

However, the converse is false. For example,

$$\sum_{n=2}^\infty \frac{\sin{(nx)}}{\log{n}} = \frac{\sin{(2x)}}{\log{2}} + \frac{\sin{(3x)}}{\log{3}} + \frac{\sin{( 4x)}}{\log{4}}+\cdots$$

is a trigonometric series which converges for all $x$ but is not a [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038).[^n3][^n4]

:::

## Uniqueness of trigonometric series {#Uniqueness_of_trigonometric_series}

The uniqueness and the zeros of trigonometric series was an active area of research in 19th century Europe. First, [Georg Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216) proved that if a trigonometric series is convergent to a function $f$ on the interval $[0, 2\pi]$, which is identically zero, or more generally, is nonzero on at most finitely many points, then the coefficients of the series are all zero.[^o9]

Later Cantor proved that even if the set *S* on which $f$ is nonzero is infinite, but the [derived set](wikipedia://en/1567335) *S**of*****S*is finite, then the coefficients are all zero. In fact, he proved a more general result. Let*S*<sub>*0*</sub> =*S*and let*S*<sub>*k+1*</sub> be the [derived set](wikipedia://en/1567335) of*S*<sub>*k*</sub>. If there is a finite number*n*for which*S*<sub>*n*</sub> is finite, then all the coefficients are zero. Later, Lebesgue proved that if there is a countably infinite [ordinal](wikipedia://en/26547932)*α*such that*S*<sub>*α*</sub> is finite, then the coefficients of the series are all zero. Cantor's work on the uniqueness problem famously led him to invent [transfinite](wikipedia://en/189734) [ordinal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinal_numbers), which appeared as the subscripts*α*in*S*<sub>*α*</sub> .[^o10]***
