# Equational logic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Equational logic"}

First-order **equational [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065)** consists of [quantifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantification_%28logic%29)-free terms of ordinary [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983), with equality as the only [predicate symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_symbol). The [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858) of this logic was developed into [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200) by [Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/1180916), [Grätzer](wikipedia://en/42456445), and [Cohn](wikipedia://en/18326967). It was later made into a branch of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869) by [Lawvere](wikipedia://en/872760) ("algebraic theories").[^o2]

:::

The terms of equational logic are built up from variables and constants using function symbols (or operations).

## Syllogism {#Syllogism}

Here are the four [inference rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inference_rule) of logic. $P[x := E]$ denotes textual substitution of expression $E$ for variable $x$ in expression $P$. Next, $b = c$ denotes equality, for $b$ and $c$ of the same type, while $b \equiv c$, or equivalence, is defined only for $b$ and $c$ of type [boolean](wikipedia://en/54476844). For $b$ and $c$ of type boolean, $b = c$ and $b \equiv c$ have the same meaning.

::: {#o4 .table cue=table}

| [Substitution](wikipedia://en/5438948) | If $P$ is a theorem, then so is $P[x := E]$. | $\vdash P \qquad \rightarrow \qquad \vdash P[x := E]$ |
|---|---|---|
| [Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) | If $P = Q$ is a theorem, then so is $E[x:= P] = E[x:= Q]$. | $\vdash P = Q \qquad \rightarrow \qquad \vdash E[x := P] = E[x := Q]$ |
| [Transitivity](wikipedia://en/200463) | If $P = Q$ and $Q = R$ are theorems, then so is $P = R$. | $\vdash P = Q, \; \vdash Q = R \qquad \rightarrow \qquad \vdash P = R$ |
| Equanimity | If $P$ and $P \equiv Q$ are theorems, then so is $Q$. | $\vdash P, \; \vdash P \equiv Q \qquad \rightarrow \qquad \vdash Q$ |

:::

## Proof {#Proof}

We explain how the four inference rules are used in proofs, using the proof of $\lnot p \equiv p \equiv \bot$. The [logic symbols](wikipedia://en/2498388) $\top$ and $\bot$ indicate "true" and "false," respectively, and $\lnot$ indicates "[not](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_negation)." The theorem numbers refer to theorems of *A Logical Approach to Discrete Math*.[^o8]

$$\begin{array}{lcl} (0) & & \lnot p \equiv p \equiv \bot \\ (1) & = & \quad \left\langle\; (3.9),\; \lnot(p \equiv q) \equiv \lnot p \equiv q,\; \text{with}\ q := p \;\right\rangle \\ (2) & & \lnot (p \equiv p) \equiv \bot \\ (3) & = & \quad \left\langle\; \text{Identity of}\ \equiv (3.9),\; \text{with}\ q := p \;\right\rangle \\ (4) & & \lnot \top \equiv \bot & (3.8) \end{array}$$

First, lines $(0)$–$(2)$ show a use of inference rule Leibniz:

$$(0) = (2)$$

is the conclusion of Leibniz, and its premise $\lnot(p \equiv p) \equiv \lnot p \equiv p$ is given on line $(1)$. In the same way, the equality on lines $(2)$–$(4)$ are substantiated using Leibniz.

The "hint" on line $(1)$ is supposed to give a premise of Leibniz, showing what substitution of equals for equals is being used. This premise is theorem $(3.9)$ with the substitution $p := q$, i.e.

$$(\lnot(p \equiv q) \equiv \lnot p \equiv q)[p := q]$$

This shows how inference rule Substitution is used within hints.

From $(0) = (2)$ and $(2) = (4)$, we conclude by inference rule Transitivity that $(0) = (4)$. This shows how Transitivity is used.

Finally, note that line $(4)$, $\lnot \top \equiv \bot$, is a theorem, as indicated by the hint to its right. Hence, by inference rule Equanimity, we conclude that line $(0)$ is also a theorem. And $(0)$ is what we wanted to prove.[^o8]
