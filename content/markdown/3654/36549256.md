# Sumario Compendioso

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sumario Compendioso"}

The ***Sumario Compendioso*** was the first mathematics book published in the [New World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_World). The book was published in [Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico_City) in 1556 by a clergyman [Juan Diez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Diez).

:::

## Availability {#Availability}

The book has been digitized and is available on the [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet).

Before the [Digital Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_Age) the only four known surviving copies were preserved at the [Huntington Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huntington_Library), San Marino, California, the [British Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Library), London, [Duke University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_University) Library, and the [University of Salamanca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Salamanca) in Spain.[^o3]

## Excerpts {#Excerpts}

In his book *[The Math Book](wikipedia://en/36549055)*, [Clifford A. Pickover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifford_A._Pickover) provided the following information about *Sumario Compendioso*: The *Sumario Compendioso*, published in Mexico City in 1556, is the first work on mathematics printed in the Americas. The publication of *Sumario Compendioso* in the New World preceded by many decades the emigration of the Puritans to North America and the settlement in [Jamestown, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamestown,_Virginia). The author, Brother Juan Diez, was a companion of [Hernando Cortes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hernando_Cortes), the Spanish conquistador, during Cortes's conquests of the [Aztec Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec_Empire).[^o5]
