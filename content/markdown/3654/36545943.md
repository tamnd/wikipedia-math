# Slope number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Slope number"}

*Image: Petersen graph with slope number 3.svg.* A drawing of the [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926) with slope number 3

In [graph drawing](wikipedia://en/325813) and [geometric graph theory](wikipedia://en/3313527), the **slope number** of a graph is the minimum possible number of distinct [slopes](wikipedia://en/29368) of edges in a drawing of the graph in which vertices are represented as points in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) and edges are represented as [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860) that do not pass through any non-incident vertex.

:::

## Complete graphs {#Complete_graphs}

Although closely related problems in [discrete geometry](wikipedia://en/386468) had been studied earlier, e.g. by (Scott 1970) and (Jamison 1984), the problem of determining the slope number of a graph was introduced by (Wade Chu 1994), who showed that the slope number of an $n$-vertex [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_{n}$ is exactly $n$. A drawing with this slope number may be formed by placing the vertices of the graph on a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306).

## Relation to degree {#Relation_to_degree}

The slope number of a graph of maximum degree $d$ is clearly at least $\lceil d/2\rceil$, because at most two of the incident edges at a degree-$d$ vertex can share a slope. More precisely, the slope number is at least equal to the [linear arboricity](wikipedia://en/36344163) of the graph, since the edges of a single slope must form a [linear forest](wikipedia://en/36344169), and the linear arboricity in turn is at least $\lceil d/2\rceil$.

::: {#o7 .problem cue=unsolved}

Do the graphs of maximum degree four have bounded slope number?

:::

There exist graphs with maximum [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) five that have arbitrarily large slope number.[^o8] However, every graph of maximum degree three has slope number at most four;[^o9] the result of (Wade Chu 1994) for the complete graph $K_{4}$ shows that this is tight. Not every set of four slopes is suitable for drawing all degree-3 graphs: a set of slopes is suitable for this purpose if and only if it forms the slopes of the sides and diagonals of a [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496). In particular, any degree 3 graph can be drawn so that its edges are either axis-parallel or parallel to the main diagonals of the [integer lattice](wikipedia://en/3333402).[^o11] It is not known whether graphs of maximum degree four have bounded or unbounded slope number.[^o12]

*Image: KesPacPal-GD-10.svg.* The method of (Keszegh Pach Pálvölgyi 2011) for combining circle packings and quadtrees to achieve bounded slope number for planar graphs with bounded degree

## Planar graphs {#Planar_graphs}

As (Keszegh Pach Pálvölgyi 2011) showed, every [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314) has a [planar straight-line drawing](wikipedia://en/6805386) in which the number of distinct slopes is a function of the degree of the graph. Their proof follows a construction of (Malitz Papakostas 1994) for bounding the [angular resolution](wikipedia://en/33245450) of planar graphs as a function of degree, by completing the graph to a [maximal planar graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_planar_graph) without increasing its degree by more than a constant factor, and applying the [circle packing theorem](wikipedia://en/16648043) to represent this augmented graph as a collection of tangent circles. If the degree of the initial graph is bounded, the ratio between the radii of adjacent circles in the packing will also be bounded by the [ring lemma](wikipedia://en/63579202),[^o17] which in turn implies that using a [quadtree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadtree) to place each graph vertex on a point within its circle will produce slopes that are ratios of small integers. The number of distinct slopes produced by this construction is exponential in the degree of the graph.

## Complexity {#Complexity}

It is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete) to determine whether a graph has slope number two.[^o19] From this, it follows that it is NP-hard to determine the slope number of an arbitrary graph, or to approximate it with an [approximation ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approximation_ratio) better than 3/2.

It is also NP-complete to determine whether a planar graph has a planar drawing with slope number two,[^o20] and hard for the [existential theory of the reals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_theory_of_the_reals) to determine the minimum slope number of a planar drawing.[^o21]
