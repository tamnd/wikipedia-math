# Reflection group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Reflection group"}

In [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) and [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **reflection group** is a [discrete group](wikipedia://en/468536) which is generated by a set of [reflections](wikipedia://en/295829) of a finite-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). The symmetry group of a [regular polytope](wikipedia://en/518693) or of a [tiling](wikipedia://en/321671) of the Euclidean space by congruent copies of a regular polytope is necessarily a reflection group. Reflection groups also include [Weyl groups](wikipedia://en/296332) and crystallographic [Coxeter groups](wikipedia://en/297004). While the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) is generated by reflections (by the [Cartan–Dieudonné theorem](wikipedia://en/4048180)), it is a continuous group (indeed, [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945)), not a discrete group, and is generally considered separately.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let *E* be a finite-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). A **finite reflection group** is a subgroup of the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) of *E* which is generated by a set of orthogonal [reflections](wikipedia://en/295829) across hyperplanes passing through the origin. An **affine reflection group** is a discrete subgroup of the [affine group](wikipedia://en/298828) of *E* that is generated by a set of *affine reflections* of *E* (without the requirement that the reflection hyperplanes pass through the origin).

The corresponding notions can be defined over other [fields](wikipedia://en/10603), leading to **[complex reflection groups](wikipedia://en/5381096)** and analogues of reflection groups over a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615).

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### Two dimensions {#Two_dimensions}

In two dimensions, the finite reflection groups are the [dihedral groups](wikipedia://en/100267), which are generated by reflection in two lines that form an angle of $2\pi/n$ and correspond to the [Coxeter diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram) $I_2(n).$ Conversely, the cyclic [point groups in two dimensions](wikipedia://en/3037988) are not generated by reflections, nor contain any – they are subgroups of index 2 of a dihedral group.

Infinite reflection groups include the [frieze groups](wikipedia://en/375503) $*\infty\infty$ and $*22\infty$ and the [wallpaper groups](wikipedia://en/386214) $**$, $*2222$, $*333$, $*442$ and $*632$. If the angle between two lines is an irrational multiple of pi, the group generated by reflections in these lines is infinite and non-discrete, hence, it is not a reflection group.

### Three dimensions {#Three_dimensions}

Finite reflection groups are the [point groups](wikipedia://en/2694525) *C<sub>nv</sub>*, *D<sub>nh</sub>*, and the [symmetry groups](wikipedia://en/28186) of the five [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905). Dual regular polyhedra (cube and octahedron, as well as dodecahedron and icosahedron) give rise to isomorphic symmetry groups. The classification of finite reflection groups of **R**<sup>3</sup> is an instance of the [ADE classification](wikipedia://en/648042).

:::

## Relation with Coxeter groups {#Relation_with_Coxeter_groups}

A reflection group *W* admits a [presentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_presentation) of a special kind discovered and studied by [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328).[^o9] The reflections in the faces of a fixed [fundamental](wikipedia://en/469365) "chamber" are generators *r*<sub>*i*</sub> of *W* of order 2. All relations between them formally follow from the relations

$$(r_i r_j)^{c_{ij}} = 1,$$

expressing the fact that the product of the reflections *r*<sub>*i*</sub> and *r*<sub>*j*</sub> in two hyperplanes *H*<sub>*i*</sub> and *H*<sub>*j*</sub> meeting at an angle $\pi/c_{ij}$ is a [rotation](wikipedia://en/39789) by the angle $2\pi/c_{ij}$ fixing the subspace *H*<sub>*i*</sub> ∩ *H*<sub>*j*</sub> of codimension 2. Thus, viewed as an abstract group, every reflection group is a [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004).

## Finite fields {#Finite_fields}

When working over finite fields, one defines a "reflection" as a map that fixes a hyperplane. Geometrically, this amounts to including [shears](wikipedia://en/799405) in a hyperplane. Reflection groups over finite fields of characteristic not 2 were classified by (Zalesskiĭ Serežkin 1981).

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Discrete [isometry groups](wikipedia://en/1101694) of more general [Riemannian manifolds](wikipedia://en/144652) generated by reflections have also been considered. The most important class arises from [Riemannian symmetric spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_symmetric_space) of rank 1: the [n-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) *S*<sup>*n*</sup>, corresponding to finite reflection groups, the Euclidean space **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, corresponding to affine reflection groups, and the [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742) *H*<sup>*n*</sup>, where the corresponding groups are called **hyperbolic reflection groups**. In two dimensions, [triangle groups](wikipedia://en/2069342) include reflection groups of all three kinds.

:::
