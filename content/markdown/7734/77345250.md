# Squigonometry

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Squigonometry"}

**Squigonometry** or **$p$-trigonometry** is a generalization of traditional [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261) which replaces the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) and [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) function with the [squircle](wikipedia://en/5565333) (shape intermediate between a [square](wikipedia://en/659939) and circle) and [$p$-norm](wikipedia://en/990534#p-norm). While trigonometry deals with the relationships between [angles](wikipedia://en/1196) and lengths in the plane using [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367) defined relative to a [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071), squigonometry focuses on analogous relationships and functions within the context of a **unit squircle**.

:::

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The term squigonometry is a [portmanteau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portmanteau) of *square* or *squircle* and *trigonometry*. It was used by Derek Holton to refer to an analog of trigonometry using a square as a basic shape (instead of a circle) in his 1990 pamphlet *Creating Problems*.[^o3] In 2011 it was used by William Wood to refer to trigonometry with a squircle as its base shape in a recreational mathematics article in *[Mathematics Magazine](wikipedia://en/4230329)*. In 2016 Robert Poodiack extended Wood's work in another *Mathematics Magazine* article. Wood and Poodiack published a book about the topic in 2022.

However, the idea of generalizing trigonometry to curves other than circles is centuries older.[^o4]

## Squigonometric functions {#Squigonometric_functions}

### Cosquine and squine {#Cosquine_and_squine}

#### Definition through unit squircle {#Definition_through_unit_squircle}

*Image: Unit-squircle-graph-p-1-2-3-4.png.* Unit squircle for different values of p

The cosquine and squine functions, denoted as $\operatorname{cq}_p(t)$ and $\operatorname{sq}_p(t),$ can be defined analogously to trigonometric functions on a [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071), but instead using the [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinates) of points on a *unit squircle*, described by the [equation](wikipedia://en/9284):

$$|x|^p + |y|^p = 1$$

where $p$ is a [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) greater than or equal to 1. Here $x$ corresponds to $\operatorname{cq}_p(t)$ and $y$ corresponds to $\operatorname{sq}_p(t)$.

Notably, when $p=2$, the squigonometric functions coincide with the trigonometric functions.

#### Definition through [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations) {#Definition_through_differential_equations}

Similarly to how trigonometric functions are defined through differential equations, the cosquine and squine functions are also uniquely determined[^o9] by solving the coupled [initial value problem](wikipedia://en/498383)[^o10][^o11]

$$\begin{cases} x'(t)=-|y(t)|^{p-1}\\ y'(t)=|x(t)|^{p-1}\\ x(0)=1\\ y(0)=0 \end{cases}$$

Where $x$ corresponds to $\operatorname{cq}_p(t)$ and $y$ corresponds to $\operatorname{sq}_p(t)$.[^o12]

#### Definition through [analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) {#Definition_through_analysis}

The definition of sine and cosine through integrals can be extended to define the squigonometric functions. Let $1<p<\infty$ and define a [differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable) function $F_p:[0,1]\rightarrow{{\mathbb{R}}}$ by:

$$F_p (x)=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{1}{{(1-t^p)}^\tfrac{p-1}{p}}\,dt$$

Since $F_p$ is [strictly increasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strictly_increasing) it is a [one-to-one function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-to-one_function) on $[0,1]$ with range $[0,\pi_p/2]$, where $\pi_p$ is defined as follows:

$$\pi_p=2\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{{(1-t^p)}^\tfrac{p-1}{p}}\,dt$$

Let $\operatorname{sq}_p$ be the [inverse](wikipedia://en/14907) of $F_p$ on $[0,\pi_p/2]$. This function can be extended to $[0,\pi_p]$ by defining the following relationship:

$$\operatorname{sq}_p (x)=\operatorname{sq}_p (\pi_p-x)$$

By this means $\operatorname{sq}_p$ is differentiable in ${{\mathbb{R}}}$ and, corresponding to this, the function $\operatorname{cq}_p$ is defined by:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\operatorname{sq}_p (x) = \operatorname{cq}_p(x)^{p-1}.$$

### Tanquent, cotanquent, sequent and cosequent {#Tanquent,_cotanquent,_sequent_and_cosequent}

The tanquent, cotanquent, sequent and cosequent functions can be defined as follows:[^o15]

$$\operatorname{tq}_p(t)=\frac{\operatorname{sq}_p(t)}{\operatorname{cq}_p(t)}$$

$$\operatorname{ctq}_p(t)=\frac{\operatorname{cq}_p(t)}{\operatorname{sq}_p(t)}$$

$$\operatorname{seq}_p(t)=\frac{1}{\operatorname{cq}_p(t)}$$

$$\operatorname{csq}_p(t)=\frac{1}{\operatorname{sq}_p(t)}$$

### Inverse squigonometric functions {#Inverse_squigonometric_functions}

General versions of the inverse squine and cosquine can be derived from the initial value problem above. Let $x=\operatorname{cq}_p (y)$; by the [inverse function rule](wikipedia://en/148064), $\frac{dx}{dy} =-[\operatorname{sq}_p (y)]^{p-1}=(1-x^p)^{(p-1)/p}$. Solving for $y$ gives the definition of the inverse cosquine:

$$y=\operatorname{cq}_{p}^{-1}(x) = \int_{x}^{1}\frac{1}{(1-t^p)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}}\,dt$$

Similarly, the inverse squine is defined as:

$$\operatorname{sq}_{p}^{-1}(x) = \int_{0}^{x}\frac{1}{(1-t^p)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}}\,dt$$

### Multiple ways to approach squigonometry {#Multiple_ways_to_approach_squigonometry}

Other parameterizations of squircles give rise to alternate definitions of these functions. For example, Edmunds, Lang, and Gurka [^o18] define $\tilde F_ p(x)$ as:

$$\tilde F_p (x)= \int_{0}^{x}(1-t^p)^{-(1/p)}\,dt.$$

Since $F_p$ is [strictly increasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strictly_increasing) it has an inverse which, by analogy with the case $p=2$, we denote by $\sin_p$. This is defined on the interval $[0,\pi_p/2]$, where $\tilde \pi_p$ is defined as follows:

$$\tilde \pi_p=2 \int_{0}^{1}(1-t^p)^{-(1/p)}\,dt.$$

Because of this, we know that $\sin_p$ is [strictly increasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strictly_increasing) on $[0,\tilde \pi_p/2]$, $\sin_p(0)=0$ and $\sin_p(\tilde \pi_p/2)=1$. We extend $\sin_p$ to $[0,\tilde \pi_p]$ by defining:

$\sin_p(x)=\sin_p(\tilde \pi_p-x)$ for $x \in[\tilde \pi_p/2,\tilde \pi_p ]$ Similarly $\cos_p(x)=(1-(\sin_p(x))^p)^\frac{1}{p}$.

Thus $\cos_p$ is [strictly decreasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strictly_decreasing) on $[0,\tilde \pi_p/2]$, $\cos_p(0)=1$ and $\cos_p(\tilde \pi_2/2)=0$. Also:

$|\sin_px|^p+|\cos_px|^p=1$ .

This is immediate if $x \in [0,\tilde \pi/2 ]$, but it holds for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$ in view of symmetry and periodicity.

## Applications {#Applications}

Squigonometric substitution can be used to solve [indefinite integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_integrals) using a method akin to [trigonometric substitution](wikipedia://en/375033), such as integrals in the generic form[^o21]

$$I = \int ({1-t^p})^\frac{1}{p}\,dt$$

that are otherwise computationally difficult to handle.

Squigonometry has been applied to find expressions for the volume of [superellipsoids](wikipedia://en/19681409), such as the [superegg](wikipedia://en/10405346).
