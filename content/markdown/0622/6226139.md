# Transferable belief model

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Transferable belief model"}

The **transferable belief model (TBM)** is an elaboration on the [Dempster–Shafer theory](wikipedia://en/226660) (DST), which is a mathematical model used to evaluate the [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) that a given [proposition](wikipedia://en/81094) is true from other propositions that are assigned probabilities. It was developed by [Philippe Smets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippe_Smets) who proposed his approach as a response to [Zadeh’s example](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zadeh’s_example) against [Dempster's rule of combination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dempster's_rule_of_combination). In contrast to the original DST the TBM propagates the [open-world assumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-world_assumption) that relaxes the assumption that all possible outcomes are known. Under the open world assumption Dempster's rule of combination is adapted such that there is no [normalization](wikipedia://en/2978513). The underlying idea is that the [probability mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_mass) pertaining to the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) is taken to indicate an unexpected outcome, e.g. the [belief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief) in a [hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis) outside the [frame of discernment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_discernment). This adaptation violates the probabilistic character of the original DST and also [Bayesian inference](wikipedia://en/49571). Therefore, the authors substituted notation such as *probability masses* and *probability update* with terms such as *degrees of belief* and *transfer* giving rise to the name of the method: The *transferable belief model*.[^n1][^n2]

:::

## Zadeh’s example in TBM context {#Zadeh’s_example_in_TBM_context}

[Lotfi Zadeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotfi_Zadeh) describes an [information fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_fusion) problem.[^o5] A patient has an illness that can be caused by three different factors *A*, *B* or *C*. Doctor 1 says that the patient's illness is very likely to be caused by A (very likely, meaning probability *p* = 0.95), but *B* is also possible but not likely (*p* = 0.05). Doctor 2 says that the cause is very likely *C* (*p* = 0.95), but *B* is also possible but not likely (*p* = 0.05). How is one to make one's own opinion from this?

Bayesian updating the first opinion with the second (or the other way round) implies certainty that the cause is *B*. [Dempster's rule of combination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dempster's_rule_of_combination) lead to the same result. This can be seen as [paradoxical](wikipedia://en/24390), since although the two doctors point at different causes, *A* and *C*, they both agree that *B* is not likely. (For this reason the standard Bayesian approach is to adopt [Cromwell's rule](wikipedia://en/1690329) and avoid the use of 0 or 1 as probabilities.)

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

The TBM describes [beliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beliefs) at two levels:[^n3]

1. a *credal level* where *beliefs* are entertained and quantified by [belief functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief_functions),
1. a *pignistic level* where *beliefs* can be used to make [decisions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision_making) and are quantified by [probability functions](wikipedia://en/43487).

### Credal level {#Credal_level}

According to the DST, a [probability mass function](wikipedia://en/154725) $m$ is defined such that:[^n1]

$$m: 2^X \rightarrow [0,1] \,\!$$

with

$$\sum_{A \in 2^X} m(A) = 1 \,\!$$

where the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) $2^X$ contains all possible subsets of the [frame of discernment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_discernment) $X$. In contrast to the DST the mass $m$ allocated to the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) $\emptyset$ is not required to be zero, and hence generally $0 \leq m(\emptyset) \leq 1.0$ holds true. The underlying idea is that the frame of discernment is not necessarily [exhaustive](wikipedia://en/1519880), and thus belief allocated to a proposition $A \in 2^X$, is in fact allocated to $A \in 2^X\cup{e}$ where ${e}$ is the set of unknown outcomes. Consequently, the combination rule underlying the TBM corresponds to [Dempster's rule of combination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dempster's_rule_of_combination), except the normalization that grants $m(\emptyset)=0$. Hence, in the TBM any two independent functions $m_1$ and $m_2$ are combined to a single function $m_{1,2}$ by:[^n4]

$$m_{1,2}(A) = (m_1 \otimes m_2) (A) = \sum_{B \cap C = A} m_1(B) m_2(C) \, \!$$

where

$$A,B,C \in 2^X \ne \emptyset. \, \!$$

In the TBM the *degree of belief* in a hypothesis $H \in 2^X \ne \emptyset$ is defined by a function:[^n1]

$$\operatorname{bel}: 2^X \rightarrow [0,1] \, \!$$

with

$$\operatorname{bel}(H)= \sum_{\emptyset \ne A \subseteq H} m(A)$$

$$\operatorname{bel}(\emptyset)=0. \, \!$$

### Pignistic level {#Pignistic_level}

When a decision must be made the *credal beliefs* are transferred to [pignistic probabilities](wikipedia://en/16070384) by:[^n3]

$$P_\text{Bet}(x)= \sum_{x \in A \subseteq X} \frac {m(A)} {|A|} \, \!$$

where $x \in X$ denote the atoms (also denoted as [singletons](wikipedia://en/381798))[^o15] and $|A|$ the number of atoms $x$ that appear in $A$. Hence, probability masses $m(A)$ are equally distributed among the atoms of A. This strategy corresponds to the [principle of insufficient reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_insufficient_reason) (also denoted as [principle of maximum entropy](wikipedia://en/201718)) according to which an *unknown* [distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) most probably corresponds to a [uniform distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29).

In the TBM *pignistic probability functions* are described by functions $P_\text{Bet}$. Such a function satisfies the [probability axioms](wikipedia://en/23539):[^n3]

$$P_\text{Bet}: X \rightarrow [0,1] \,\!$$

with

$$\sum_{x \in X} P_\text{Bet}(x) = 1 \,\!$$

$$P_\text{Bet}(\emptyset)=0 \,\!$$

[Philip Smets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Smets) introduced them as *pignistic* to stress the fact that those probability functions are based on incomplete data, whose only purpose is a forced decision, e.g. to place a bet. This is in contrast to the *credal beliefs* described above, whose purpose is representing the actual *belief*.[^n1]

:::

## Open world example {#Open_world_example}

When tossing a coin one usually assumes that Head or Tail will occur, so that $\Pr(\text{Head}) + \Pr(\text{Tail}) = 1$. The open-world assumption is that the coin can be stolen in mid-air, disappear, break apart or otherwise fall sideways so that neither Head nor Tail occurs, so that the power set of {Head,Tail} is considered and there is a decomposition of the overall probability (i.e. 1) of the following form:

$$\Pr(\emptyset) + \Pr(\text{Head}) + \Pr(\text{Tail}) + \Pr(\text{Head,Tail}) = 1.$$
