# Coulomb's law

*Image: CoulombsLaw scal.svg.* The magnitude of the electrostatic [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) $\boldsymbol{F}$ between two [point charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_charge) $q_{1}$ and $q_{2}$ is directly proportional to the product of the magnitudes of charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. Like charges repel each other, and unlike charges attract each other.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Coulomb's law"}

**Coulomb's inverse-square law**, or simply **Coulomb's law**, is a [scientific law](wikipedia://en/244629)[^o2] of [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) that describes the amount of [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_%28physics%29) between two [electrically charged](wikipedia://en/9804) particles at rest. This electric force is conventionally called the *electrostatic force* or **Coulomb force**.[^o3] Although the law was known earlier, it was first published in 1785 by French physicist [Charles-Augustin de Coulomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles-Augustin_de_Coulomb). Coulomb's law was essential to the development of the [theory of electromagnetism](wikipedia://en/426219) and may even be its starting point,[^o2] as it allowed meaningful discussions of the amount of electric charge in a particle.[^o4]

:::

The law states that the magnitude, or absolute value, of the attractive or repulsive electrostatic [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) between two [point charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_charge) is directly proportional to the product of the magnitudes of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.[^o5] Coulomb discovered that bodies with like electrical charges repel:

It follows therefore from these three tests, that the repulsive force that the two balls – \[that were\] electrified with the same kind of electricity – exert on each other, follows the inverse proportion of the square of the distance.[^o6]

Coulomb also showed that oppositely charged bodies attract according to an inverse-square law:

$$|F| \propto \frac{|q_1||q_2|}{r^2}$$

Here $q_{1}$ and $q_{2}$ are the quantities of each charge, and the scalar *r* is the distance between the charges. The proportionality constant depends upon the unit of charge used[^o7] and may be called the **Coulomb constant**.[^o2]

The force is along the straight line joining the two bodies. If the charges have the same sign, the electrostatic force between them makes them repel; if they have different signs, the force between them makes them attract.

Being an [inverse-square law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse-square_law), the law is similar to [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627)'s inverse-square [law of universal gravitation](wikipedia://en/244611), but gravitational forces always make things attract, while electrostatic forces make charges attract or repel. Also, gravitational forces are much weaker than electrostatic forces.[^o3] Coulomb's law can be used to derive [Gauss's law](wikipedia://en/74964), and vice versa. In the case of a single point charge at rest, the two laws are equivalent, expressing the same physical law in different ways.[^o8] The law has been [tested extensively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tests_of_electromagnetism), and observations have upheld the law on the scale from 10<sup>−16</sup> m to 10<sup>8</sup> m.[^o8]

The time-dependent generalization of Coulomb's law is given by [Jefimenko's equations](wikipedia://en/3888215), which describe the electric field and magnetic fields generated by time-dependent distributions of electric charge and current.

## History {#History}

*Image: Coulomb.jpg.* [Charles-Augustin de Coulomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles-Augustin_de_Coulomb)

Ancient cultures around the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea) knew that certain objects, such as rods of [amber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amber), could be rubbed with cat's fur to attract light objects like feathers and pieces of paper. [Thales of Miletus](wikipedia://en/30072) made the first recorded description of [static electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_electricity) around 600 BC,[^o10] when he noticed that [friction](wikipedia://en/11062) could make a piece of amber attract small objects.[^o11][^o12]

In 1600, English scientist [William Gilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Gilbert_%28astronomer%29) made a careful study of electricity and magnetism, distinguishing the [lodestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lodestone) effect from static electricity produced by rubbing amber.[^o11] He coined the [Neo-Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Latin) word *electricus* ("of amber" or "like amber", from ἤλεκτρον \[*elektron*\], the Greek word for "amber") to refer to the property of attracting small objects after being rubbed.[^o13] This association gave rise to the English words "electric" and "electricity", which made their first appearance in print in [Thomas Browne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Browne)'s *[Pseudodoxia Epidemica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudodoxia_Epidemica)* of 1646.[^o14]

Early investigators of the 18th century who suspected that the electrical [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) diminished with distance as the force of [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) did (i.e., as the inverse square of the distance) included [Daniel Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/46784)[^o15] and [Alessandro Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alessandro_Volta), both of whom measured the force between plates of a [capacitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacitor), and [Franz Aepinus](wikipedia://en/604168) who supposed the inverse-square law in 1758.[^o16]

Based on experiments with [electrically charged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrically_charged) spheres, [Joseph Priestley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Priestley) of England was among the first to propose that electrical force followed an [inverse-square law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse-square_law), similar to [Newton's law of universal gravitation](wikipedia://en/244611). However, he did not generalize or elaborate on this.[^o17] In 1767, he conjectured that the force between charges varied as the inverse square of the distance.[^o18][^o19]

*Image: Bcoulomb.png.* Coulomb's [torsion balance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion_balance)

In 1769, Scottish physicist [John Robison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Robison_%28physicist%29) announced that, according to his measurements, the force of repulsion between two spheres with charges of the same sign varied as $x^{-2.06}$.[^o20]

In the early 1770s, the dependence of the force between charged bodies upon both distance and charge had already been discovered, but not published, by [Henry Cavendish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Cavendish) of England.[^o21] In his notes, Cavendish wrote, "We may therefore conclude that the electric attraction and repulsion must be inversely as some power of the distance between that of the 2 + $\tfrac{1}{50}$th and that of the 2 − $\tfrac{1}{50}$th, and there is no reason to think that it differs at all from the inverse duplicate ratio".

Finally, in 1785, the French physicist [Charles-Augustin de Coulomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles-Augustin_de_Coulomb) published[^o5] his application of a [torsion balance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion_balance) to determine that the magnitude of the electric force between two [point charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_charge). He verified Priestley's earlier claim concerning like charges that the force is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. Coulomb then showed the same law applied to the attraction of unlike charges.[^o22]

The torsion balance consists of a bar suspended from its middle by a thin fiber. The fiber acts as a very weak [torsion spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion_spring). In Coulomb's experiment, the torsion balance was an [insulating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insulator_%28electricity%29) rod with a [metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal)-coated ball attached to one end, suspended by a [silk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk) thread. The ball was charged with a known charge of [static electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_electricity), and a second charged ball of the same polarity was brought near it. The two charged balls repelled one another, twisting the fiber through a certain angle, which could be read from a scale on the [instrument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_instrument). By knowing how much force it took to twist the fiber through a given angle, Coulomb was able to calculate the force between the balls and derive his inverse-square proportionality law.

## Mathematical form {#Mathematical_form}

*Image: Coulombslawgraph.svg.* In the image, the vector $\mathbf{F}_{1}$ is the force experienced by $q_{1}$, and the vector $\mathbf{F}_{2}$ is the force experienced by $q_{2}$. When $q_{1}q_{2} > 0$ the forces are repulsive (as in the image) and when $q_{1}q_{2} < 0$ the forces are attractive (opposite to the image). The magnitude of the forces will always be equal.

Coulomb's law states that the electrostatic force $\mathbf{F}_1$ experienced by a charge, $q_1$ at position $\mathbf{r}_1$, in the vicinity of another charge, $q_2$ at position $\mathbf{r}_2$, in a vacuum is equal to[^o40]

$$\mathbf{F}_1 = \frac{q_1q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} {\hat\mathbf r_{12}\over {|\mathbf r_{12}|}^2}$$

where [MKS units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MKS_units) are used and $\mathbf{r_{12} = r_1 - r_2}$ is the [displacement vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_vector) between the charges, $\hat\mathbf r_{12}$ a [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) pointing from $q_2$ to $q_1$, and $\varepsilon_0$ the [electric constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_constant). Here, $\mathbf{\hat{r}}_{12}$ is used for the vector notation. The electrostatic force $\mathbf{F}_2$ experienced by $q_2$, according to [Newton's third law](wikipedia://en/55212), is $\mathbf{F}_2=-\mathbf{F}_1$.

If both charges have the same [sign](wikipedia://en/245206) (like charges) then the [product](wikipedia://en/244373) $q_1q_2$ is positive and the direction of the force on $q_1$ is given by $\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{12}$; the charges repel each other. If the charges have opposite signs then the product $q_1q_2$ is negative and the direction of the force on $q_1$ is $-\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{12}$; the charges attract each other.[^o24]

### System of discrete charges {#System_of_discrete_charges}

The [law of superposition](wikipedia://en/1201321) allows Coulomb's law to be extended to include any number of point charges. The force acting on a point charge due to a system of point charges is simply the [vector addition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_addition) of the individual forces acting alone on that point charge due to each one of the charges. The resulting force vector is parallel to the [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) vector at that point, with that point charge removed.

Force $\mathbf{F}$ on a small charge $q$ at position $\mathbf{r}$, due to a system of $n$ discrete charges in vacuum is[^o40]

$$\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}) = {q\over4\pi\varepsilon_0} \sum_{i=1}^n q_i {\hat\mathbf r_i\over {|\mathbf r_i|}^2} ,$$

where $q_i$ is the magnitude of the $i$ th charge, $\mathbf{r}_i$ is the vector from its position to $\mathbf{r}$ and $\hat\mathbf r_i$ is the unit vector in the direction of $\mathbf r_i$.

### Continuous charge distribution {#Continuous_charge_distribution}

In this case, the principle of [linear superposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_superposition) is also used. For a continuous charge distribution, an [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) over the region containing the charge is equivalent to an infinite summation, treating each [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) element of space as a point charge $dq$. The distribution of charge is usually linear, surface or volumetric.

For a linear charge distribution (a good approximation for charge in a wire) where $\lambda(\mathbf{r}')$ gives the charge per unit length at position $\mathbf{r}'$, and $d\ell'$ is an infinitesimal element of length,[^o27]

$$dq' = \lambda(\mathbf{r'}) \, d\ell'.$$

For a surface charge distribution (a good approximation for charge on a plate in a parallel plate [capacitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacitor)) where $\sigma(\mathbf{r}')$ gives the charge per unit area at position $\mathbf{r}'$, and $dA'$ is an infinitesimal element of area,

$$dq' = \sigma(\mathbf{r'})\,dA'.$$

For a volume charge distribution (such as charge within a bulk metal) where $\rho(\mathbf{r}')$ gives the charge per unit volume at position $\mathbf{r}'$, and $dV'$ is an infinitesimal element of volume,[^o24]

$$dq' = \rho(\boldsymbol{r'})\,dV'.$$

The force on a small test charge $q$ at position $\boldsymbol{r}$ in vacuum is given by the integral over the distribution of charge

$$\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int dq' \frac{\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r'}}{|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r'}|^3}.$$

The "continuous charge" version of Coulomb's law is never supposed to be applied to locations for which $|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r'}| = 0$ because that location would directly overlap with the location of a charged particle (e.g. electron or proton) which is not a valid location to analyze the electric field or potential classically. Charge is always discrete in reality, and the "continuous charge" assumption is just an approximation that is not supposed to allow $|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r'}| = 0$ to be analyzed.

## Coulomb constant {#Coulomb_constant}

The constant of proportionality $k_e$ in Coulomb's law depends upon the unit of charge used. In [Gaussian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_units) and [Heaviside–Lorentz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaviside–Lorentz_units) units the unit of charge is determined by choosing a value for the Coulomb constant and using the force between charges to define the unit of charge. The constant $k_e$ is [chosen to be 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_units#Unit_of_charge) in Gaussian units and [1/4$\pi$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaviside–Lorentz_units#Length%E2%80%93mass%E2%80%93time_framework) in Heaviside-Lorentz units.[^o30]

In the [SI units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) (which grew out of the [MKS units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MKS_units) and [MKSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MKS_units#Electromagnetic_units) units[^o31]), the unit of charge, the [Coulomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb), has been defined since [the SI units were revised in 2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_revision_of_the_SI) as a multiple of the fundamental charge (i.e. the charge on the electron or proton).. Accordingly, the Coulomb constant is something that has to be determined through measurement. The value of the proportionality factor $k_e$is not listed by standards organization such as [NIST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIST), but the value of $\mu_0/4\pi$ is listed, as is the value of $\varepsilon_0$.[^o33] From the first of these one can use the identity[^o34] $c^2 = 1/\varepsilon_0\mu_0$ to write the **Coulomb constant** as

$$k_e = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = \frac{\mu_0 c^2}{4\pi} = (0.999 999 999 87(16) \times 10^{-7} \mathrm{ N \cdot A^{-2} }) c^2$$

Since 1983 the meter has been defined so that the speed of light $c$ has an exact value, namely 299,792,458 m/s.[^o35] Using that value and one [ampere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampere) as one [coulomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb) per second gives:

$$k_e = 8.9875517862(14)\times 10^9\mathrm{N\cdot m^2\cdot C^{-2}}$$

Because the value of $c$ is very close to $3 \times 10^8$ m/s, the value of $k_e$ is very close to $9\times 10^9\ \mathrm{N{\cdot}m^2{\cdot}C^{-2}}$. Some textbooks (after warning the reader) use 3 as an abbreviation for 2.99792458 (see e.g. Jacksonp. 820) and 9 for 8.9875517862 (e.g. Feynman[^o40]).

Prior to the 2019 revision, the Ampere was defined by setting $\mu_0$ to the exact value $4 \pi \times 10^{-7}$N A$^{-2}$.[^o36]. Accordingly, in the SI units as defined between 1984 and 2018,

$$k_e = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = \frac{\mu_0 c^2}{4\pi} = (10^{-7} \mathrm{ N \cdot A^{-2} }) c^2 = 8.9875517873681764 \times 10^9 \mathrm{N\cdot m^2\cdot C^{-2}}$$

exactly. Prior to 1983, the definition of $\varepsilon_0$ meant that $k_e = 1/4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 10^{-7}c^2$ held, but the speed of light was a measured value, not a defined exact constant.

## Limitations {#Limitations}

There are three conditions to be fulfilled for the validity of Coulomb's inverse square law:[^o38]

1. The charges must have a spherically symmetric distribution (e.g. be point charges, or a charged metal sphere).
1. The charges must not overlap (e.g. they must be distinct point charges).
1. The charges must be stationary with respect to a non-accelerating frame of reference.

The last of these is known as the [electrostatic approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics#Electrostatic_approximation). When movement takes place, an extra factor is introduced, which alters the force produced on the two objects. This extra part of the force is called the [magnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic) force. For slow movement, the magnetic force is minimal and Coulomb's law can still be considered approximately correct. A more accurate approximation in this case is, however, the [Weber force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weber_electrodynamics). When the charges are moving more quickly in relation to each other or accelerations occur, [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737) and [Einstein](wikipedia://en/736)'s [theory of relativity](wikipedia://en/30001) must be taken into consideration.

## Electric field {#Electric_field}

*Image: Electric field one charge changing.gif.* If two charges have the same sign, the electrostatic force between them is repulsive; if they have different sign, the force between them is attractive.

An electric field is a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) that associates to each point in space the Coulomb force experienced by a [unit test charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_particle#Test_particles_in_plasma_physics_or_electrodynamics).[^o40] The strength and direction of the Coulomb force $\mathbf F$ on a charge $q_t$ depends on the electric field $\mathbf{E}$ established by other charges that it finds itself in, such that $\mathbf{F} = q_t \mathbf{E}$. In the simplest case, the field is considered to be generated solely by a single source [point charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_charge). More generally, the field can be generated by a distribution of charges who contribute to the overall by the [principle of superposition](wikipedia://en/1201321).

If the field is generated by a positive source point charge $q$, the direction of the electric field points along lines directed radially outwards from it, i.e. in the direction that a positive point test charge $q_t$ would move if placed in the field. For a negative point source charge, the direction is radially inwards.

The magnitude of the electric field $\mathbf{E}$ can be derived from Coulomb's law. By choosing one of the point charges to be the source, and the other to be the test charge, it follows from Coulomb's law that the magnitude of the [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) $\mathbf{E}$ created by a single source [point charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_charge) *Q* at a certain distance from it *r* in vacuum is given by

$$|\mathbf{E}| = k_\text{e} \frac{|Q|}{r^2}$$

A system of *n* discrete charges $q_i$ stationed at $\mathbf r_i = \mathbf r-\mathbf r_i$ produces an electric field whose magnitude and direction is, by superposition

$$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = {1\over4\pi\varepsilon_0} \sum_{i=1}^n q_i {\hat\mathbf r_i\over {|\mathbf r_i|}^2}$$

## Atomic forces {#Atomic_forces}

Coulomb's law holds even within [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom), correctly describing the [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) between the positively charged [atomic nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus) and each of the negatively charged [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron). This simple law also correctly accounts for the forces that bind atoms together to form [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule) and for the forces that bind atoms and molecules together to form solids and liquids. Generally, as the distance between [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion) increases, the force of attraction, and binding energy, approach zero and [ionic bonding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_bonding) is less favorable. As the magnitude of opposing charges increases, energy increases and ionic bonding is more favorable.

### Deriving Coulomb's law from Gauss's law {#Deriving_Coulomb's_law_from_Gauss's_law}

Strictly speaking, Coulomb's law cannot be derived from Gauss's law alone, since Gauss's law does not give any information regarding the [curl](wikipedia://en/6123) of $\mathbf{E}$ (see [Helmholtz decomposition](wikipedia://en/820253) and [Faraday's law](wikipedia://en/742288)). However, Coulomb's law *can* be proven from Gauss's law if it is assumed, in addition, that the electric field from a [point charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_charge) is spherically symmetric (this assumption, like Coulomb's law itself, is exactly true if the charge is stationary, and approximately true if the charge is in motion).

::: {#o48 .proof cue=math-proof name="Outline of proof"}

**Outline of proof.**

Taking $S$ in the integral form of Gauss's law to be a spherical surface of radius $r$, centered at the point charge $Q$, we have

$$\oint_S\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}$$

By the assumption of spherical symmetry, the integrand is a constant which can be taken out of the integral. The result is

$$4\pi r^2\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}$$

where $\mathbf{r\text{̂}}$ is a [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) pointing radially away from the charge. Again by spherical symmetry, $\mathbf{E}$ points in the radial direction, and so we get

$$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{Q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r^2}$$

which is essentially equivalent to Coulomb's law. Thus the [inverse-square law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse-square_law) dependence of the electric field in Coulomb's law follows from Gauss's law.

## In relativity {#In_relativity}

Coulomb's law can be used to gain insight into the form of the magnetic field generated by moving charges since by special relativity, in certain cases the [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563) can be shown to be a transformation of forces caused by the [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092). When no acceleration is involved in a particle's history, Coulomb's law can be assumed on any test particle in its own inertial frame, supported by symmetry arguments in solving [Maxwell's equation](wikipedia://en/19737), shown above. Coulomb's law can be expanded to moving test particles to be of the same form. This assumption is supported by [Lorentz force law](wikipedia://en/18631) which, unlike Coulomb's law is not limited to stationary test charges. Considering the charge to be invariant of observer, the electric and magnetic fields of a uniformly moving point charge can hence be derived by the [Lorentz transformation](wikipedia://en/18404) of the [four force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-force) on the test charge in the charge's frame of reference given by Coulomb's law and attributing magnetic and electric fields by their definitions given by the form of [Lorentz force](wikipedia://en/18631#Definition_and_properties).[^o50] The fields hence found for uniformly moving point charges are given by:[^o51]

$$\mathbf{E} = \frac q {4 \pi \varepsilon_0 r^3} \frac {1- \beta^2} {(1- \beta^2 \sin^2 \theta)^{3/2}} \mathbf{r}$$

$$\mathbf{B} = \frac q {4 \pi \varepsilon_0 r^3} \frac {1- \beta^2} {(1- \beta^2 \sin^2 \theta)^{3/2}} \frac{\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{r}}{c^2} = \frac{\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{E}}{c^2}$$

where $q$ is the charge of the point source, $\mathbf{r}$ is the position vector from the point source to the point in space, $\mathbf{v}$ is the velocity vector of the charged particle, $\beta$ is the ratio of speed of the charged particle divided by the speed of light and $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{r}$ and $\mathbf{v}$.

This form of solutions need not obey [Newton's third law](wikipedia://en/55212) as is the case in the framework of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) (yet without violating relativistic-energy momentum conservation).[^o52] Note that the expression for electric field reduces to Coulomb's law for non-relativistic speeds of the point charge and that the magnetic field in non-relativistic limit (approximating $\beta\ll 1$) can be applied to electric currents to get the [Biot–Savart law](wikipedia://en/75110). These solutions, when expressed in retarded time also correspond to the general solution of [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737) given by solutions of [Liénard–Wiechert potential](wikipedia://en/9999827), due to the validity of Coulomb's law within its specific range of application. Also note that the spherical symmetry for gauss law on stationary charges is not valid for moving charges owing to the breaking of symmetry by the specification of direction of velocity in the problem. Agreement with [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737) can also be manually verified for the above two equations.[^o53]

## Coulomb potential {#Coulomb_potential}

### Quantum field theory {#Quantum_field_theory}

*Image: Feynman diagram - Moller scattering 1.svg.* The most basic [Feynman diagram](wikipedia://en/11617) for [QED](wikipedia://en/25268) interaction between two [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion)

The **Coulomb potential** admits continuum states (with *E* > 0), describing electron-proton [scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattering_theory), as well as discrete bound states, representing the hydrogen atom.[^o56] It can also be derived within the [non-relativistic limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-relativistic_quantum_electrodynamics) between two charged particles, as follows:

Under [Born approximation](wikipedia://en/2460228), in non-relativistic quantum mechanics, the scattering amplitude $\mathcal{A}(| \mathbf{p} \rangle \to | \mathbf{p}'\rangle)$ is:

$$\mathcal{A}(| \mathbf{p} \rangle \to | \mathbf{p}'\rangle) - 1 = 2\pi \delta(E_p - E_{p'})(-i)\int d^3\mathbf r \, V(\mathbf r) e^{-i(\mathbf p - \mathbf p')\mathbf r}$$

This is to be compared to the:

$$\int \frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3} e^{i k r_0} \langle p',k |S| p,k \rangle$$

where we look at the (connected) S-matrix entry for two electrons scattering off each other, treating one with "fixed" momentum as the source of the potential, and the other scattering off that potential.

Using the Feynman rules to compute the S-matrix element, we obtain in the non-relativistic limit with $m_0 \gg | \mathbf p|$

$$\langle p',k | S | p,k \rangle |_{conn} = -i\frac{e^2}{| \mathbf p - \mathbf p'|^2 - i\varepsilon}(2m)^2\delta(E_{p,k} - E_{p',k})(2\pi)^4\delta(\mathbf p - \mathbf p')$$

Comparing with the QM scattering, we have to discard the $(2m)^2$ as they arise due to differing normalizations of momentum eigenstate in QFT compared to QM and obtain:

$$\int V(\mathbf r)e^{-i(\mathbf p - \mathbf p') \mathbf r}d^3\mathbf r = \frac{e^2}{| \mathbf p - \mathbf p'|^2 - i\varepsilon}$$

where Fourier transforming both sides, solving the integral and taking $\varepsilon \to 0$ at the end will yield

$$V(r) = \frac{e^2}{4\pi r}$$

as the Coulomb potential.[^o57]

However, the equivalent results of the classical Born derivations for the Coulomb problem are thought to be strictly accidental.[^o58][^o59]

The Coulomb potential, and its derivation, can be seen as a special case of the [Yukawa potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukawa_potential), which is the case where the exchanged boson – the photon – has no rest mass.[^o56]

## Verification {#Verification}

section

*Image: Verificacion ley coulomb.png.* Experiment to verify Coulomb's law.

It is possible to verify Coulomb's law with a simple experiment. Consider two small spheres of mass $m$ and same-sign charge $q$, hanging from two ropes of negligible mass of length $l$. The forces acting on each sphere are three: the weight $mg$, the rope tension $\mathbf T$ and the electric force $\mathbf F$. In the equilibrium state:

::: {#o61 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\mathbf T \sin \theta_1 =\mathbf F_1 \tag{1}$$

:::

and

::: {#o62 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\mathbf T \cos \theta_1 = mg \tag{2}$$

:::

Dividing ((1)) by ((2)):

::: {#o63 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$\frac {\sin \theta_1}{\cos \theta_1 }=\frac {F_1}{mg}\Rightarrow F_1= mg \tan \theta_1 \tag{3}$$

:::

Let $\mathbf L_1$ be the distance between the charged spheres; the repulsion force between them $\mathbf F_1$, assuming Coulomb's law is correct, is equal to

::: {#o64 .equation cue=numblk name="Coulomb's law"}

$$F_1 = \frac{q^2}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0 L_1^2} \tag{Coulomb's law}$$

:::

so:

::: {#o65 .equation cue=numblk name="4"}

$$\frac{q^2}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0 L_1^2}=mg \tan \theta_1 \tag{4}$$

:::

If we now discharge one of the spheres, and we put it in contact with the charged sphere, each one of them acquires a charge $\frac{q}{2}$. In the equilibrium state, the distance between the charges will be $\mathbf L_2 < \mathbf L_1$ and the repulsion force between them will be:

::: {#o66 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$$F_2 = \frac{{(\frac{q}{2})}^2}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0 L_2^2}=\frac{\frac{q^2}{4}}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0 L_2^2} \tag{5}$$

:::

We know that $\mathbf F_2 = mg \tan \theta_2$ and:

$$\frac{\frac{q^2}{4}}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0 L_2^2}=mg \tan \theta_2$$

Dividing ((4)) by ((5)), we get:

::: {#o67 .equation cue=numblk name="6"}

$$\frac{\left(\cfrac{q^2}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0 L_1^2} \right)}{\left(\cfrac{\frac{q^2}{4}}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0 L_2^2}\right)} = \frac{mg \tan \theta_1}{mg \tan \theta_2} \Rightarrow 4 {\left( \frac {L_2}{L_1} \right) }^2 = \frac{ \tan \theta_1}{ \tan \theta_2} \tag{6}$$

:::

Measuring the angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ and the distance between the charges $\mathbf L_1$ and $\mathbf L_2$ is sufficient to verify that the equality is true taking into account the experimental error. In practice, angles can be difficult to measure, so if the length of the ropes is sufficiently great, the angles will be small enough to make the following approximation:

::: {#o68 .equation cue=numblk name="7"}

$$\tan \theta \approx \sin \theta = \frac{\frac{L}{2}}{\ell} = \frac{L}{2\ell} \Rightarrow \frac{ \tan \theta_1}{ \tan \theta_2} \approx \frac{\frac{L_1}{2\ell}}{\frac{L_2}{2\ell}} \tag{7}$$

:::

Using this approximation, the relationship ((6)) becomes the much simpler expression:

::: {#o69 .equation cue=numblk name="8"}

$$\frac{\frac{L_1}{2\ell}}{\frac{L_2}{2\ell}}\approx 4 {\left ( \frac {L_2}{L_1} \right ) }^2 \Rightarrow \frac{L_1}{L_2}\approx 4 {\left ( \frac {L_2}{L_1} \right ) }^2 \Rightarrow \frac{L_1}{L_2}\approx\sqrt[3]{4} \tag{8}$$

:::

In this way, the verification is limited to measuring the distance between the charges and checking that the division approximates the theoretical value.
