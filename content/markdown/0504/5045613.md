# Pauli group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pauli group"}

*Image: Pauli group-Cayley graph.svg.* The [Möbius–Kantor graph](wikipedia://en/13396591), the [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of the Pauli group with generators $x$, $y$, and $z$

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), [quantum information](wikipedia://en/25274) and [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), the **Pauli group** is a group formed by tensor products of [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868), including the identity.[^o2] The single-qubit Pauli group is a 16-element [matrix group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_group), consisting of the 4 Pauli matrices each with 4 possible phase factors. The $n$-qubit Pauli group is a $4^{n+1}$-element group consisting of tensor products of single-qubit Paulis.[^o2][^o3]

:::

In [quantum information theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_information_science), Pauli groups are important because they are the basis for [stabilizer formalism](wikipedia://en/5045759), a widely-used framework for constructing and describing [quantum error correction](wikipedia://en/892803) codes using sets of commuting Pauli operators. [Stabilizer codes](wikipedia://en/5045759) are formed from commuting subgroups of the Pauli group.[^o4][^o5]

## Single-qubit Pauli group {#Single-qubit_Pauli_group}

The Pauli group consists of the 2 × 2 [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) $I$ and all of the [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868)

$$X = \sigma_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0&1\\ 1&0 \end{pmatrix},\quad Y = \sigma_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0&-i\\ i&0 \end{pmatrix},\quad Z = \sigma_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 1&0\\ 0&-1 \end{pmatrix},$$

together with the products of these matrices with the factors $\pm 1$ and $\pm i$:

$$G \ \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\ \{\pm I,\pm iI,\pm X,\pm iX,\pm Y,\pm iY,\pm Z,\pm iZ\} \equiv \langle X, Y, Z \rangle.$$

The Pauli group is [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by the Pauli matrices, and like them it is named after [Wolfgang Pauli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Pauli).

As an abstract group, $G \ \cong C_4 \circ D_4$ is the [central product](wikipedia://en/4842988) of a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order 4 and the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) of order 8.[^o7]

The Pauli group is a [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) of the [gamma group](wikipedia://en/25322570) in three-dimensional Euclidean space. It is *not* isomorphic to the gamma group; it is less free, in that its chiral element is $\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3=iI$ whereas there is no such relationship for the gamma group.

### Pauli algebra {#Pauli_algebra}

The [Pauli algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauli_algebra) is the [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) of 2 x 2 complex matrices M(2, **C**) with matrix addition and [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280). It has a long history beginning with the [biquaternions](wikipedia://en/1207070) introduced by [W. R. Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._R._Hamilton) in his *Lectures on Quaternions* (1853). The representation with matrices was noted by [L. E. Dickson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._E._Dickson) in 1914.[^o9] Publications by Pauli eventually led to the [eponym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eponym) now in use. [Basis](wikipedia://en/18420) elements of the algebra generate the Pauli group.

## Multi-qubit Pauli group {#Multi-qubit_Pauli_group}

The Pauli group on $n$ [qubits](wikipedia://en/25284), $G_n$, is the group generated by the operators described above applied to each of $n$ qubits in the [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) $(\mathbb{C}^2)^{\otimes n}$. That is,

$$G_n = \langle P_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes P_n : P_i \in \{I, X, Y, Z \} \rangle = \{ c \cdot P_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes P_n : c \in \{\pm 1, \pm i\}, P_i \in \{I, X, Y, Z \} \}.$$

The [order](wikipedia://en/493527) of $G_n$ is $4 \cdot 4^n$ since a scalar $\pm 1$ or $\pm i$ factor in any tensor position can be moved to any other position.

::: {#o12 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notations"}

### Notations {#Notations}

An $n$-qubit Pauli operator that only acts on a single qubit is often denoted as a single Pauli letter with an integer subscript. For example, in a system with 3 qubits,

$$X_1 \equiv X \otimes I \otimes I, \qquad Z_2 \equiv I \otimes Z \otimes I.$$

Multi-qubit Pauli operators can be written as products of single-qubit Paulis on disjoint qubits. Alternatively, when it is clear from context, the tensor product symbol $\otimes$ can be omitted, i.e. unsubscripted Pauli matrices written consecutively represents tensor product rather than matrix product. For example:

$$XZI \equiv X_1Z_2 = X \otimes Z \otimes I.$$

:::

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

Operators in $G_n$ can also be represented as $2^n \times 2^n$ matrices. An operator $P = c \cdot P_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes P_n$ always has two distinct [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue), either $\pm 1$ or $\pm i$ depending on whether the scalar factor $c$ is $\pm 1$ or $\pm i$. An operator with eigenvalues $\pm 1$ is [Hermitian](wikipedia://en/187408), and one with eigenvalues $\pm i$ is anti-Hermitian. In either case, a set of $2^n$ eigenvectors of $P$ can be constructed by taking tensor products of eigenvectors of each $P_i$, with the eigenvalue being $c$ times the product of the eigenvalues of each factor.

Two operators in $G_n$ either [commute](wikipedia://en/294390) or [anti-commute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-commute), depending on whether the number of anti-commuting pairs of single-qubit Pauli operators at the same location is even or odd. For example, $XXX$ and $ZZX$ commute with each other since there are exactly two anti-commuting pairs (on qubits 1 and 2), but $XXX$ and $ZZZ$ anti-commute since there are three such pairs.

:::

### Binary vector representation {#Binary_vector_representation}

A simple but useful mapping $N$ exists between the binary [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $(\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{2}$ and the set of Pauli matrices $\{I, X, Y, Z\}$:

$$00 \to I, \; 01 \to X, \; 11 \to Y, \; 10 \to Z.$$

This mapping allows a multi-qubit Pauli operator to be represented as a [binary vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit_vector) with a phase factor, and operations on these operators to be defined as [binary operations](wikipedia://en/3948) rather than [matrix operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_operation).

Some useful properties of this mapping becomes evident when the phaseless Pauli operators $I, X, Y, Z$ are regarded as representatives of [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) in the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) $[G] = G / \{\pm 1, \pm i\}$ (where $G$ is the single-qubit Pauli group). For $P \in G$, denote the equivalence class represented by $P$ as

$$[P] = \{\beta P \mid \beta \in \{\pm 1, \pm i\}\}.$$

Note that $[G]$ is a [commutative group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_group) since two Pauli operators either commute or anti-commute, but $[-P] = [P]$.

The map $N$ now induces an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) $[N] : (\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{2} \mapsto [G]$, i.e., addition of vectors in $(\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{2}$ is equivalent to multiplication of Pauli operators up to a global phase:

$$[N(u + v)] = [N(u)][N(v)].$$

Furthermore, let $\odot$ denote the [symplectic product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_product) between two elements $u, v \in (\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{2}$, where $u = z \vert x$ and $v = z' \vert x'$ (this notation represents binary string concatenation, e.g., $01 \equiv 0 \vert 1$), $z, x, z', x' \in \mathbb{Z}_{2}$:

$$u \odot v \equiv zx' - xz'.$$

Then the symplectic product $\odot$ gives the [commutation](wikipedia://en/294390) relations of elements of $G$:

$$N(u)N(v) = (-1)^{(u \odot v)}N(v)N(u).$$

The symplectic product and the mapping $N$ thus give a useful way to phrase Pauli relations in terms of [binary algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_algebra_%28logic%29).

The above definitions can be straightforwardly extended to multiple qubits, defining a mapping $\mathbf{N} : (\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{2n} \mapsto G_n$ such that

$$\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{z} \vert \mathbf{x}) = N(z_1 \vert x_1) \otimes \cdots \otimes N(z_n \vert x_n).$$

Similar to the single-qubit case, denoting the quotient group $G_n / \{\pm 1, \pm i\}$ as $[G_n]$, the map $[\mathbf{N}] : (\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{2n} \mapsto [G_n]$ is an isomorphism:

$$[\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v})] = [\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{u})][\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{v})].$$

Furthermore, for $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{z} \vert \mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{z}' \vert \mathbf{x}'$, where $\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}', \mathbf{x}' \in (\mathbb{Z}_{2})^n$, define the symplectic product $\odot$ as

$$\mathbf{u}\odot\mathbf{v} \equiv \sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}x_{i}'-x_{i} z_{i}' = \sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\odot v_{i},$$

where $u_{i} = z_{i} \vert x_{i}$ and $v_{i} = z_{i}' \vert x_{i}'$. Then the symplectic product captures the commutation relations of any operators $\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{u})$ and $\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{v})$:

$$\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{v}) = (-1)^{(\mathbf{u}\odot\mathbf{v}) }\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{v})\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{u}).$$

The above binary representation and [symplectic algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_algebra) are especially useful in making the relation between classical linear [error correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_correction) and quantum [stabilizer codes](wikipedia://en/5045759) more explicit. In the language of [symplectic vector spaces](wikipedia://en/292852), a [symplectic](wikipedia://en/292852#Subspaces) subspace corresponds to a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) of Pauli algebras (i.e., encoded qubits), while an [isotropic](wikipedia://en/292852#Subspaces) subspace corresponds to a set of stabilizers.
