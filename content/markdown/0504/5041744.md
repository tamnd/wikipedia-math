# Eulerian number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Eulerian number"}

In [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170), the **Eulerian number** $A(n,k)$ is the number of [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) of the numbers 1 to *$n$* in which exactly *$k$* elements are greater than the previous element (permutations with *$k$* "ascents").

:::

[Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) investigated them and associated [polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomials) in his 1755 book *[Institutiones calculi differentialis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutiones_calculi_differentialis)*.[^o2][^o3]

*Image: EulerianPolynomialsByEuler1755.png.* The polynomials presently known as Eulerian polynomials in Euler's work from 1755, Institutiones calculi differentialis, part 2, §173, p. 485/6. The coefficients of these polynomials are known as Eulerian numbers.

Other notations for $A(n,k)$ are $E(n,k)$ and $\textstyle \left\langle {n \atop k} \right\rangle$.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: EulerianNumberPlot.svg.* A plot of the Eulerian numbers with the second argument fixed to 5.

The **Eulerian polynomials** $A_n(t)$ are defined by the exponential generating function

$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} A_{n}(t) \,\frac{x^n}{n!} = \frac{t-1}{t - e^{(t-1)\,x}} = \left(1-\frac{e^{(t-1)x}-1}{t-1}\right)^{-1}.$$

The **Eulerian numbers** $A(n,k)$ may also be defined as the coefficients of the Eulerian polynomials:

$$A_{n}(t) = \sum_{k=0}^n A(n,k)\,t^k.$$

An explicit formula for $A(n,k)$ is[^n1]

$$A(n,k)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^i \binom{n+1}{i} (k+1-i)^n.$$

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Basic properties"}

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

- For fixed *$n$* there is a single permutation which has 0 ascents: $(n, n-1, n-2, \ldots, 1)$. Indeed, as ${\tbinom n 0}=1$ for all $n$, $A(n, 0) = 1$. This formally includes the empty collection of numbers, $n=0$. And so $A_0(t)=A_1(t)=1$.
- For $k=1$ the explicit formula implies $A(n,1)=2^n-(n+1)$, a sequence in $n$ that reads $0, 0, 1, 4, 11, 26, 57, \dots$.
- Fully reversing a permutation with *$k$* ascents creates another permutation in which there are *$n-k-1$* ascents. Therefore $A(n, k) = A(n, n-k-1)$. So there is also a single permutation which has *$n-1$* ascents, namely the rising permutation $(1, 2, \ldots, n)$. So also $A(n, n-1)$ equals $1$.
- Since a permutation of the numbers $1$ to $n$ which has $k$ ascents must have $n-1-k$ descents, the symmetry $A(n, k) = A(n, n-k-1)$ shows that $A(n, k)$ also counts the number of permutations with $k$ *descents*.
- For $k\ge n > 0$, the values are formally zero, meaning many sums over $k$ can be written with an upper index only up to $n-1$. It also means that the polynomials $A_{n}(t)$ are really of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) $n-1$ for $n>0$.

## Computation {#Computation}

For larger values of $n$, $A(n,k)$ can also be calculated using the [recursive](wikipedia://en/25407) formula[^n2]

$$A(n,k)=(n-k)A(n-1,k-1) + (k+1)A(n-1,k).$$

This formula can be motivated from the combinatorial definition and thus serves as a natural starting point for the theory.

For small values of $n$ and $k$, the values of $A(n,k)$ can be calculated by hand. For example

{| class="wikitable"

\|- ! *n* ! *k* ! Permutations ! *A*(*n*, *k*) |- | 1 | 0 | (1) | *A*(1,0) = 1 |- |rowspan="2" | 2 | 0 | (2, 1) | *A*(2,0) = 1 |- | 1 | (1, **2**) | *A*(2,1) = 1 |- |rowspan="3" | 3 | 0 | (3, 2, 1) | *A*(3,0) = 1 |- | 1 | (1, **3**, 2), (2, 1, **3**), (2, **3**, 1) and (3, 1, **2**) | *A*(3,1) = 4 |- | 2 | (1, **2**, **3**) | *A*(3,2) = 1 |}

Applying the recurrence to one example, we may find

$$A(4,1)=(4-1)\,A(3,0) + (1+1)\,A(3,1)=3 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 4 = 11.$$

Likewise, the Eulerian polynomials can be computed by the recurrence

$$A_{0}(t) = 1,$$

$$A_{n}(t) = A_{n-1}'(t)\cdot t\,(1-t) + A_{n-1}(t)\cdot (1+(n-1)\,t),\text{ for } n > 1.$$

The second formula can be cast into an inductive form,

$$A_{n}(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \binom{n}{k} A_{k}(t)\cdot (t-1)^{n-1-k}, \text{ for } n > 1.$$

:::

## Identities {#Identities}

For any [set partition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_partition) of a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) into [disjoint subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjoint_subsets), the sum of the cardinalities of the parts equals the cardinality of the whole set. Since there are $n!$ (the [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606) of $n$) permutations of size $n$, and the Eulerian numbers give the cardinalities of the subsets of these permutations with fixed number of descents, it follows that

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n} A(n,k) = n! \,.$$

(The summand $k = n$ is 0 for $n>0$, but is included to give the correct sum $A(0,0)=0!$ when $n = 0$.) Much more generally, for a fixed function $f\colon \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$ [integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_function) on the interval $[0, n]$, the following identity holds:[^n3]

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} A(n, k)\, f(k) = n!\int_0^1 \cdots \int_0^1 f\left(\left\lfloor x_1 + \cdots + x_n\right\rfloor\right) dx_1 \cdots dx_n .$$

**Worpitzky's identity** expresses $x^n$ as the [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of Eulerian numbers with [binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/4668):

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A(n,k)\binom{x+k}{n}=x^n.$$

From this, it follows that

$$\sum_{k=1}^{m}k^n=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} A(n,k) \binom{m+k+1}{n+1}.$$

This identity is named after Julius Worpitzky, who discovered it in the 1880s;[^o14] it had been originally discovered somewhat earlier, by [Li Shanlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_Shanlan) in his 1867 work *Duò Jī Bǐ Lèi*.[^n4][^o16]

Eulerian numbers appear as the coefficients of the [polylogarithm](wikipedia://en/482471) for negative integer inputs:

$$\operatorname{Li}_{-n}(z) = {1 \over (1-z)^{n+1}} \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} A(n, k) z^{n-k} \qquad (n=1,2,3,\ldots).$$

::: {#o18 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formulas involving alternating sums"}

### Formulas involving alternating sums {#Formulas_involving_alternating_sums}

The [alternating sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_sum) of the Eulerian numbers for a fixed value of *$n$* is related to the [Bernoulli number](wikipedia://en/4964) $B_{n+1}$

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^k A(n,k) = 2^{n+1}(2^{n+1}-1) \frac{B_{n+1}}{n+1}, \text{ for }n > 0.$$

Furthermore,

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^k \frac{A(n,k)}{\binom{n-1}{k}}=0, \text{ for }n > 1$$

and

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^k \frac{A(n,k)}{\binom{n}{k}}=(n+1)B_{n}, \text{ for } n > 1$$

:::

::: {#o20 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formulas involving the polynomials"}

### Formulas involving the polynomials {#Formulas_involving_the_polynomials}

The symmetry property implies:

$$A_n(t) = t^{n-1} A_n(t^{-1})$$

The Eulerian numbers are involved in the [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) for the sequence of *n*<sup>th</sup> powers:

$$\sum_{i=1}^\infty i^n x^i = \frac{1}{(1-x)^{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^n A(n,k)\,x^{k+1} = \frac{x}{(1-x)^{n+1}}A_n(x)$$

An explicit expression for Eulerian polynomials is[^o21]

$$A_n(t) = \sum_{k=0}^n \left\{ {n \atop k} \right\} k! (t-1)^{n-k}$$

where $\left\{ {n \atop k} \right\}$ is the [Stirling number of the second kind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirling_number_of_the_second_kind).

:::

## Geometric interpretations {#Geometric_interpretations}

The Eulerian numbers have two important geometric interpretations involving [convex polytopes](wikipedia://en/1817885).

First of all, the identity

$$\sum_{i=0}^\infty (i+1)^n x^i = \frac{1}{(1-x)^{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^n A(n,k)\,x^{k}$$

implies that the Eulerian numbers form the [$h^\ast$-vector](wikipedia://en/316904#Ehrhart_series) of the standard $n$-dimensional [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783), which is the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of all $0,1$-vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Secondly, the identity

$$A_n(t) = \sum_{k=0}^n \left\{ {n \atop k} \right\} k! (t-1)^{n-k}$$

means that the Eulerian numbers also form the [$h$-vector](wikipedia://en/11503563) of the [simple polytope](wikipedia://en/8853286) which is [dual](wikipedia://en/8815) to the $n$-dimensional [permutohedron](wikipedia://en/12745947), which is the convex hull of all permutations of the vector $(1,2,\ldots,n)$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

## Type B Eulerian numbers {#Type_B_Eulerian_numbers}

The [hyperoctahedral group](wikipedia://en/24268961) of order $n$ is the group of all *signed permutations* of the numbers $1$ to $n$, meaning bijections $\pi$ from the set $\{-n,-n+1,\ldots,-1,1,2,\ldots,n\}$ to itself with the property that $\pi(-i)=-\pi(i)$ for all $i$. Just as the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) of order $n$ (i.e., the group of all permutations of the numbers $1$ to $n$) is the [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004) of Type $A_{n-1}$, the hyperoctahedral group of order $n$ is the Coxeter group of Type $B_n$.

Given an element $\pi$ of the hyperoctahedral group of order $n$ a *Type B descent* of $\pi$ is an index $i \in \{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}$ for which $\pi(i) > \pi(i-1)$, with the convention that $\pi(0)=0$. The **Type B Eulerian number** $B(n,k)$ is the number of elements of the hyperoctahedral group of order $n$ with exactly $k$ descents.[^o24] They are given by the following formula:

$$B(n,k)=\sum_{i=1}^k(-1)^{k-i}\binom{n}{k-i}(2i-1)^{n-1}.$$

The table of $B(n,k)$ (sequence [A060187](https://oeis.org/A060187) in the [OEIS](wikipedia://en/500004)) is

The corresponding polynomials $M_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n}B(n,k)x^k$ are called **midpoint Eulerian polynomials** because of their use in interpolation and spline theory; see Schoenberg.[^o26]

The Type B Eulerian numbers and polynomials satisfy many similar identities, and have many similar properties, as the Type A, i.e., usual, Eulerian numbers and polynomials. For example, for any $n \geq 1$,

$$\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(2i+1)^nx^i = \frac{M_{n}(x)}{(1-x)^{n+1}}.$$

And the Type B Eulerian numbers give the h-vector of the simple polytope dual to the Type B permutohedron.

In fact, one can define Eulerian numbers for any [finite Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004#Finite_Coxeter_groups) with analogous properties.[^n5]

## Eulerian numbers of the second order {#Eulerian_numbers_of_the_second_order}

The permutations of the [multiset](wikipedia://en/305303) $\{1, 1, 2, 2, \ldots, n, n\}$ which have the property that for each *k*, all the numbers appearing between the two occurrences of *k* in the permutation are greater than *k* are counted by the [double factorial](wikipedia://en/507208) number $(2n-1)!!$. These are called [Stirling permutations](wikipedia://en/38851923).

The Eulerian number of the second order, denoted $\left\langle \! \left\langle {n \atop m} \right\rangle \! \right\rangle$, counts the number of all such Stirling permutations that have exactly *m* ascents. For instance, for *n* = 3 there are 15 such permutations, 1 with no ascents, 8 with a single ascent, and 6 with two ascents:

332211,

221133, 221331, 223311, 233211, 113322, 133221, 331122, 331221,

112233, 122133, 112332, 123321, 133122, 122331.

The Eulerian numbers of the second order satisfy the recurrence relation, that follows directly from the above definition:

$$\left\langle \!\! \left\langle {n \atop k} \right\rangle \!\! \right\rangle = (2n-k-1) \left\langle \!\! \left\langle {n-1 \atop k-1} \right\rangle \!\! \right\rangle + (k+1) \left\langle \!\! \left\langle {n-1 \atop k} \right\rangle \!\! \right\rangle,$$

with [initial condition](wikipedia://en/1186804) for *n* = 0, expressed in [Iverson bracket](wikipedia://en/1721092) notation:

$$\left\langle \!\! \left\langle {0 \atop k} \right\rangle \!\! \right\rangle = [k=0].$$

Correspondingly, the Eulerian polynomial of second order, here denoted *P*<sub>*n*</sub> (no standard notation exists for them) are

$$P_n(x) := \sum_{k=0}^n \left\langle \!\! \left\langle {n \atop k} \right\rangle \!\! \right\rangle x^k$$

and the above recurrence relations are translated into a recurrence relation for the sequence *P*<sub>*n*</sub>(*x*):

$$P_{n+1}(x) = (2nx+1) P_n(x) - x(x-1) P_n^\prime(x)$$

with initial condition $P_0(x) = 1$. The latter recurrence may be written in a somewhat more compact form by means of an [integrating factor](wikipedia://en/1912874):

$$(x-1)^{-2n-2} P_{n+1}(x) = \left( x\,(1-x)^{-2n-1} P_n(x) \right)^\prime$$

so that the [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210)

$$u_n(x) := (x-1)^{-2n} P_{n}(x)$$

satisfies a simple autonomous recurrence:

$$u_{n+1} = \left( \frac{x}{1-x} u_n \right)^\prime, \quad u_0=1$$

Whence one obtains the Eulerian polynomials of second order as $P_n(x) = (1-x)^{2n} u_n(x)$, and the Eulerian numbers of second order as their coefficients.

The Eulerian polynomials of the second order satisfy an identity analogous to the identity

$$\sum_{i=1}^\infty i^n x^i = \frac{xA_n(x)}{(1-x)^{n+1}}$$

satisfied by the usual Eulerian polynomials. Specifically, as proved by Gessel and Stanley,[^o30] they satisfy the identity

$$\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left\{ {n+m \atop m} \right\}x^m = \frac{xP_n(x)}{(1-x)^{2n+1}}$$

where again the $\left\{ {n \atop k} \right\}$ denote the [Stirling numbers of the second kind](wikipedia://en/2229292). (This appearance of the Stirling numbers explains the terminology "[Stirling permutations](wikipedia://en/38851923).")
