# Convexity in economics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Convexity in economics"}

[C65](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JEL_classification_codes#Mathematical_and_quantitative_methods_JEL:_C_Subcategories)

**Convexity** is a geometric property with a variety of applications in **economics**.[^o2] Informally, an economic phenomenon is convex when "intermediates (or combinations) are better than extremes". For example, an economic agent with [convex preferences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_preferences) prefers *combinations* of goods over having a lot of any *one* sort of good; this represents a kind of [diminishing marginal utility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_utility#Law_of_diminishing_marginal_utility) of having more of the same good.

:::

Convexity is a key simplifying assumption in many economic models, as it leads to market behavior that is easy to understand and which has desirable properties. For example, the [Arrow–Debreu model](wikipedia://en/1239556) of [general economic equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_equilibrium) posits that if preferences are convex and there is perfect competition, then [aggregate supplies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aggregate_supply) will equal [aggregate demands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aggregate_demand) for every commodity in the economy.

In contrast, [non-convexity](wikipedia://en/30618217) is associated with [market failures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_failure), where [supply and demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supply_and_demand) differ or where [market equilibria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_equilibrium) can be [inefficient](wikipedia://en/45468).

The branch of mathematics which supplies the tools for convex functions and their properties is called [convex analysis](wikipedia://en/3237201); non-convex phenomena are studied under [nonsmooth analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subgradient).

## Preliminaries {#Preliminaries}

convex analysis The economics depends upon the following definitions and results from [convex geometry](wikipedia://en/4165882).

### Real vector spaces {#Real_vector_spaces}

A [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of two [dimensions](wikipedia://en/38267) may be given a [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706) in which every point is identified by a list of two real numbers, called "coordinates", which are conventionally denoted by *x* and *y*. Two points in the Cartesian plane can be *[added](wikipedia://en/32533#Addition_and_subtraction)* coordinate-wise

(*x*<sub>1</sub>, *y*<sub>1</sub>) + (*x*<sub>2</sub>, *y*<sub>2</sub>) = (*x*<sub>1</sub>+*x*<sub>2</sub>, *y*<sub>1</sub>+*y*<sub>2</sub>);

further, a point can be *[multiplied](wikipedia://en/244373)* by each real number *λ* coordinate-wise

*λ* (*x*, *y*) = (*λx*, *λy*).

More generally, any real vector space of (finite) dimension *D* can be viewed as the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all possible lists of *D* real numbers { (*v*<sub>1</sub>, *v*<sub>2</sub>, . . . , *v*<sub>D</sub>) } together with two [operations](wikipedia://en/4140245): [vector addition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_addition) and [multiplication by a real number](wikipedia://en/244373). For finite-dimensional vector spaces, the operations of vector addition and real-number multiplication can each be defined coordinate-wise, following the example of the Cartesian plane.

### Convex sets {#Convex_sets}

*Image: Extreme points.svg.* In the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of the red set, each blue point is a [convex combination](wikipedia://en/794534) of some red points.

In a real vector space, a set is defined to be *[convex](wikipedia://en/6292)* if, for each pair of its points, every point on the [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) that joins them is [covered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cover_%28mathematics%29) by the set. For example, a solid [cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube_%28geometry%29) is convex; however, anything that is hollow or dented, for example, a [crescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crescent) shape, is non‑convex. [Trivially](wikipedia://en/51672), the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) is convex.

More formally, a set *Q* is convex if, for all points *v*<sub>0</sub> and *v*<sub>1</sub> in *Q* and for every real number *λ* in the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) 0,1, the point

(1 − *λ*) *v*<sub>0</sub> + *λv*<sub>1</sub>

is a [member](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29) of *Q*.

By [mathematical induction](wikipedia://en/18881), a set *Q* is convex if and only if every [convex combination](wikipedia://en/794534) of members of *Q* also belongs to *Q*. By definition, a *convex combination* of an indexed subset {*v*<sub>0</sub>, *v*<sub>1</sub>, . . . , *v*<sub>D</sub>} of a vector space is any [weighted average](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weighted_mean) *λ*<sub>0</sub>*v*<sub>0</sub> + *λ*<sub>1</sub>*v*<sub>1</sub> + . . . + *λ*<sub>D</sub>*v*<sub>D</sub>, for some indexed set of non‑negative real numbers {*λ*<sub>d</sub>} satisfying the [equation](wikipedia://en/298835) *λ*<sub>0</sub> + *λ*<sub>1</sub> + . . . + *λ*<sub>D</sub> = 1.

The definition of a convex set implies that the *[intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429)* of two convex sets is a convex set. More generally, the intersection of a family of convex sets is a convex set.

### Convex hull {#Convex_hull}

For every subset *Q* of a real vector space, its *[convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634)* Conv(*Q*) is the [minimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_element) convex set that contains *Q*. Thus Conv(*Q*) is the intersection of all the convex sets that [cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cover_%28mathematics%29) *Q*. The convex hull of a set can be equivalently defined to be the set of all convex combinations of points in *Q*.

## Duality: Intersecting half-spaces {#Duality:_Intersecting_half-spaces}

*Image: Supporting hyperplane1.svg.* A [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292) $S$ (in pink), a supporting hyperplane of $S$ (the dashed line), and the half-space delimited by the hyperplane that contains $S$ (in light blue).

*Supporting hyperplane* is a concept in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446). A [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) divides a space into two [half-spaces](wikipedia://en/647297). A hyperplane is said to **support** a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $S$ in the [real *n*-space](wikipedia://en/1482083) $\mathbb R^n$ if it meets both of the following:

- $S$ is entirely contained in one of the two [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) half-spaces determined by the hyperplane
- $S$ has at least one point on the hyperplane.

Here, a closed half-space is the half-space that includes the hyperplane.

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Supporting hyperplane theorem"}

### Supporting hyperplane theorem {#Supporting_hyperplane_theorem}

*Image: Supporting hyperplane2.svg.* A convex set can have more than one supporting hyperplane at a given point on its boundary.

This [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) states that if $S$ is a closed [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292) in $\mathbb R^n,$ and $x$ is a point on the [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) of $S,$ then there exists a supporting hyperplane containing $x.$

The hyperplane in the theorem may not be unique, as noticed in the second picture on the right. If the closed set $S$ is not convex, the statement of the theorem is not true at all points on the boundary of $S,$ as illustrated in the third picture on the right.

*Image: Supporting hyperplane3.svg.* A supporting hyperplane containing a given point on the boundary of $S$ may not exist if $S$ is not convex.

:::

### Economics {#Economics}

*Image: Indifference curves showing budget line.svg.* The consumer prefers the vector of goods (*Q*<sub>*x*</sub>, *Q*<sub>*y*</sub>) over other affordable vectors. At this optimal vector, the budget line supports the [indifference curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indifference_curve) *I*<sub>2</sub>.

An optimal basket of goods occurs where the consumer's convex [preference set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_preferences) is [supported](wikipedia://en/4165181) by the budget constraint, as shown in the diagram. If the preference set is convex, then the consumer's set of optimal decisions is a convex set, for example, a unique optimal basket (or even a line segment of optimal baskets).

For simplicity, we shall assume that the preferences of a consumer can be described by a [utility function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_function) that is a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122), which implies that the [preference sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_preferences) are [closed](wikipedia://en/47279). (The meanings of "closed set" is explained below, in the subsection on optimization applications.)

## Non-convexity {#Non-convexity}

*Image: NonConvex.gif.* When consumer preferences have concavities, then the linear budgets need not support equilibria: Consumers can jump between allocations.

If a preference set is non‑convex, then some prices produce a budget supporting two different optimal consumption decisions. For example, we can imagine that, for zoos, a lion costs as much as an eagle, and further that a zoo's budget suffices for one eagle or one lion. We can suppose also that a zoo-keeper views either animal as equally valuable. In this case, the zoo would purchase either one lion or one eagle. Of course, a contemporary zoo-keeper does not want to purchase a half an eagle and a half a lion (or a [griffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griffin))! Thus, the contemporary zoo-keeper's preferences are non‑convex: The zoo-keeper prefers having either animal to having any strictly convex combination of both.

Non‑convex sets have been incorporated in the theories of general economic equilibria,[^o12] of [market failures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_failure),[^o13] and of [public economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_economics).[^o14] These results are described in graduate-level textbooks in [microeconomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microeconomics),[^o15] general equilibrium theory,[^o16] [game theory](wikipedia://en/11924),[^o17] [mathematical economics](wikipedia://en/18967255),[^o18] and applied mathematics (for economists).[^o19] The [Shapley–Folkman lemma](wikipedia://en/29237460) results establish that non‑convexities are compatible with approximate equilibria in markets with many consumers; these results also apply to [production economies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production_%28economics%29) with many small [firms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business).[^o20]

In "[oligopolies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oligopoly)" (markets dominated by a few producers), especially in "[monopolies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopoly)" (markets dominated by one producer), non‑convexities remain important.[^o21] Concerns with large producers exploiting market power in fact initiated the literature on non‑convex sets, when [Piero Sraffa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_Sraffa) wrote about on firms with increasing [returns to scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Returns_to_scale) in 1926,[^o22] after which [Harold Hotelling](wikipedia://en/935621) wrote about [marginal cost pricing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_cost_pricing) in 1938.[^o23] Both Sraffa and Hotelling illuminated the [market power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_power) of producers without competitors, clearly stimulating a literature on the supply-side of the economy.[^o24] Non‑convex sets arise also with [environmental goods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_economics) (and other [externalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Externality)),[^o25][^o26] with [information economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_economics),[^o27] and with [stock markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_market)[^o21] (and other [incomplete markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incomplete_markets)).[^o28][^o29] Such applications continued to motivate economists to study non‑convex sets.[^o30]

### Nonsmooth analysis {#Nonsmooth_analysis}

section Economists have increasingly studied non‑convex sets with [nonsmooth analysis](wikipedia://en/3462862), which generalizes [convex analysis](wikipedia://en/3237201). "Non‑convexities in \[both\] production and consumption ... required mathematical tools that went beyond convexity, and further development had to await the invention of non‑smooth calculus" (for example, Francis Clarke's [locally Lipschitz](wikipedia://en/7764195) calculus), as described by (Rockafellar Wets 1998)[^o33] and (Mordukhovich 2006),[^o35] according to (Khan 2008).[^o37] (Brown 1991, 1967–1968) wrote that the "major methodological innovation in the general equilibrium analysis of firms with pricing rules" was "the introduction of the methods of non‑smooth analysis, as a \[synthesis\] of global analysis (differential topology) and \[of\] convex analysis." According to (Brown 1991, 1966), "Non‑smooth analysis extends the local approximation of manifolds by tangent planes \[and extends\] the analogous approximation of convex sets by tangent cones to sets" that can be non‑smooth or non‑convex.[^o40] Economists have also used [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/431041).[^o41]
