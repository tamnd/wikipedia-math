# Distance correlation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Distance correlation"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) and in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), **distance correlation** is a measure of [dependence](wikipedia://en/27593) between two paired [random vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_vector) of arbitrary, not necessarily equal, [dimension](wikipedia://en/32533). The population distance correlation coefficient is zero [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the random vectors are [independent](wikipedia://en/27593). Thus, distance correlation measures both linear and nonlinear [association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_%28statistics%29) between two random variables or random vectors. This is in contrast to [Pearson's correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearson's_correlation), which can only detect linear association between two [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685).

:::

Distance correlation can be used to perform a [statistical test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing) of dependence with a [permutation test](wikipedia://en/2468117). One first computes the distance correlation (involving the re-centering of Euclidean distance matrices) between two random vectors, and then compares this value to the distance correlations of many shuffles of the data.

## Background {#Background}

The classical measure of dependence, the [Pearson correlation coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearson_product-moment_correlation_coefficient),[^o3] is mainly sensitive to a linear relationship between two variables. Distance correlation was introduced in 2005 by [Gábor J. Székely](wikipedia://en/28171816) in several lectures to address this deficiency of Pearson's [correlation](wikipedia://en/157057), namely that it can easily be zero for [dependent variables](wikipedia://en/437701). Correlation = 0 (uncorrelatedness) does not imply independence while distance correlation = 0 does imply independence. The first results on distance correlation were published in 2007 and 2009.[^n1][^n2] It was proved that distance covariance is the same as the Brownian covariance.[^n2] These measures are examples of [energy distances](wikipedia://en/30316300).

The distance correlation is derived from a number of other quantities that are used in its specification, specifically: **distance variance**, **distance standard deviation**, and **distance covariance**. These quantities take the same roles as the ordinary [moments](wikipedia://en/368684) with corresponding names in the specification of the [Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearson_product-moment_correlation_coefficient).

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

### Distance covariance {#Distance_covariance}

Let us start with the definition of the **sample distance covariance**. Let (*X*<sub>*k*</sub>, *Y*<sub>*k*</sub>), *k* = 1, 2, ..., *n* be a [statistical sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_sample) from a pair of real valued or vector valued random variables (*X*, *Y*). First, compute the *n* by *n* [distance matrices](wikipedia://en/831350) (*a*<sub>*j*, *k*</sub>) and (*b*<sub>*j*, *k*</sub>) containing all pairwise [distances](wikipedia://en/53932)

$$\begin{aligned} a_{j, k} &= \|X_j-X_k\|, \qquad j, k =1,2,\ldots,n, \\ b_{j, k} &= \|Y_j-Y_k\|, \qquad j, k=1,2,\ldots,n, \end{aligned}$$

where ||⋅ ||denotes [Euclidean norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_norm). Then take all doubly centered distances

$$A_{j, k} := a_{j, k}-\overline{a}_{j\cdot}-\overline{a}_{\cdot k} + \overline{a}_{\cdot\cdot}, \qquad B_{j, k} := b_{j, k} - \overline{b}_{j\cdot} -\overline{b}_{\cdot k} + \overline{b}_{\cdot\cdot},$$

where $\textstyle \overline{a}_{j\cdot}$ is the $j$-th row mean, $\textstyle \overline{a}_{\cdot k}$ is the $k$-th column mean, and $\textstyle \overline{a}_{\cdot\cdot}$ is the [grand mean](wikipedia://en/8132847) of the distance matrix of the $X$ sample. The notation is similar for the $b$ values. (In the matrices of centered distances (*A*<sub>*j*, *k*</sub>) and (*B*<sub>*j*,*k*</sub>) all rows and all columns sum to zero.) The squared **sample distance covariance** (a scalar) is simply the arithmetic average of the products *A*<sub>*j*, *k*</sub>*B*<sub>*j*, *k*</sub>:

$$\operatorname{dCov}^2_n(X,Y) := \frac 1 {n^2} \sum_{j = 1}^n \sum_{k = 1}^n A_{j, k} \, B_{j, k}.$$

The statistic *T*<sub>*n*</sub> = *n* dCov<sup>2</sup><sub>*n*</sub>(*X*, *Y*) determines a consistent multivariate test of independence of random vectors in arbitrary dimensions. For an implementation see *dcov.test* function in the *energy* package for [R](wikipedia://en/376707).[^n3]

The population value of **distance covariance** can be defined along the same lines. Let *X* be a random variable that takes values in a *p*-dimensional Euclidean space with [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) $\mu$ and let *Y* be a random variable that takes values in a *q*-dimensional Euclidean space with probability distribution $\nu$, and suppose that *X* and *Y* have finite expectations. Write

$$a_\mu(x):= \operatorname{E}[\|X-x\|], \quad D(\mu) := \operatorname{E}[a_\mu(X)], \quad d_\mu(x, x') := \|x-x'\|-a_\mu(x)-a_\mu(x')+D(\mu).$$

Finally, define the population value of squared distance covariance of *X* and *Y* as

$$\operatorname{dCov}^2(X, Y) := \operatorname{E}\big[d_\mu(X,X')d_\nu(Y,Y')\big].$$

One can show that this is equivalent to the following definition:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{dCov}^2(X,Y) := {} & \operatorname{E}[\|X-X'\|\,\|Y-Y'\|] + \operatorname{E}[\|X-X'\|]\,\operatorname{E}[\|Y-Y'\|] \\ &\qquad {} - \operatorname{E}[\|X-X'\|\,\|Y-Y''\|] - \operatorname{E}[\|X-X''\|\,\|Y-Y'\|] \\ = {} & \operatorname{E}[\|X-X'\|\,\|Y-Y'\|] + \operatorname{E}[\|X-X'\|]\,\operatorname{E}[\|Y-Y'\|] \\ &\qquad {} - 2\operatorname{E}[\|X-X'\|\,\|Y-Y''\|], \end{aligned}$$

where ***E*** denotes expected value, and $\textstyle (X, Y),$ $\textstyle (X', Y'),$ and $\textstyle (X'',Y'')$ are independent and identically distributed. The primed random variables $\textstyle (X', Y')$ and $\textstyle (X'',Y'')$ denote independent and identically distributed (iid) copies of the variables $X$ and $Y$ and are similarly iid.[^n4] Distance covariance can be expressed in terms of the classical Pearson's [covariance](wikipedia://en/157059), **cov**, as follows:

$$\operatorname{dCov}^2(X,Y) = \operatorname{cov}(\|X-X'\|,\|Y-Y'\|) - 2\operatorname{cov}(\|X-X'\|,\|Y-Y''\|).$$

This identity shows that the distance covariance is not the same as the covariance of distances, cov($\lVert X - X'\rVert$, $\lVert Y - Y'\rVert$). This can be zero even if *X* and *Y* are not independent.[^o12]

Alternatively, the distance covariance can be defined as the weighted [*L*<sup>2</sup> norm](wikipedia://en/990534#Euclidean_norm) of the distance between the joint [characteristic function](wikipedia://en/3229401) of the random variables and the product of their marginal characteristic functions:[^o13]

$$\operatorname{dCov}^2(X,Y)= \frac 1 {c_p c_q} \int_{\mathbb{R}^{p+q}} \frac{\left|\varphi_{X,Y}(s, t) - \varphi_X(s)\varphi_Y(t) \right|^2}{|s|_p^{1+p} |t|_q^{1+q}} \,dt\,ds$$

where $\varphi_{X,Y}(s,t)$, $\varphi_{X}(s)$, and $\varphi_{Y}(t)$ are the [characteristic functions](wikipedia://en/3229401) of (*X*, *Y*), *X*, and *Y*, respectively, *p*, *q* denote the Euclidean dimension of *X* and *Y*, and thus of *s* and *t*, and *c*<sub>*p*</sub>, *c*<sub>*q*</sub> are constants. The [weight function](wikipedia://en/245523) $({c_p c_q}{|s|_p^{1+p} |t|_q^{1+q}})^{-1}$ is chosen to produce a scale equivariant and rotation [invariant measure](wikipedia://en/7635201) that doesn't go to zero for dependent variables.[^o13][^n5] One interpretation of the characteristic function definition is that the variables *e<sup>isX</sup>* and *e<sup>itY</sup>* are cyclic representations of *X* and *Y* with different periods given by *s* and *t*, and the expression *ϕ*<sub>*X*, *Y*</sub>(*s*, *t*) − *ϕ*<sub>*X*</sub>(*s*) *ϕ*<sub>*Y*</sub>(*t*) in the numerator of the characteristic function definition of distance covariance is simply the classical covariance of *e<sup>isX</sup>* and *e<sup>itY</sup>*. The characteristic function definition clearly shows that dCov<sup>2</sup>(*X*, *Y*) = 0 if and only if *X* and *Y* are independent.

### Distance variance and distance standard deviation {#Distance_variance_and_distance_standard_deviation}

The *distance variance* is a special case of distance covariance when the two variables are identical. The population value of distance variance is the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of

$$\operatorname{dVar}^2(X) := \operatorname{E}[\|X-X'\|^2] + \operatorname{E}^2[\|X-X'\|] - 2\operatorname{E}[\|X-X'\|\,\|X-X''\|],$$

where $X$, $X'$, and $X''$ are [independent and identically distributed random variables](wikipedia://en/30876902), $\operatorname{E}$ denotes the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653), and $f^2(\cdot)=(f(\cdot))^2$ for function $f(\cdot)$, e.g., $\operatorname{E}^2[\cdot] = (\operatorname{E}[\cdot])^2$.

The *sample distance variance* is the square root of

$$\operatorname{dVar}^2_n(X) := \operatorname{dCov}^2_n(X,X) = \tfrac{1}{n^2}\sum_{k,\ell}A_{k,\ell}^2,$$

which is a relative of [Corrado Gini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrado_Gini)'s [mean difference](wikipedia://en/5730990) introduced in 1912 (but Gini did not work with centered distances).[^n6]

The *distance standard deviation* is the square root of the *distance variance*.

### Distance correlation {#Distance_correlation}

The *distance correlation* [^n1][^n2] of two random variables is obtained by dividing their *distance covariance* by the product of their *distance standard deviations*. The distance correlation is the square root of

$$\operatorname{dCor}^2(X,Y) = \frac{\operatorname{dCov}^2(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{dVar}^2(X)\,\operatorname{dVar}^2(Y)}},$$

and the *sample distance correlation* is defined by substituting the sample distance covariance and distance variances for the population coefficients above.

For easy computation of sample distance correlation see the *dcor* function in the *energy* package for [R](wikipedia://en/376707).[^n3]

## Properties {#Properties}

### Distance correlation {#Distance_correlation}

$0\leq\operatorname{dCor}_n(X,Y)\leq1$ and $0\leq\operatorname{dCor}(X,Y)\leq1$; this is in contrast to Pearson's correlation, which can be negative.; $\operatorname{dCor}(X,Y) = 0$ if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are independent.; $\operatorname{dCor}_n(X,Y) = 1$ implies that dimensions of the linear subspaces spanned by $X$ and $Y$ samples respectively are almost surely equal and if we assume that these subspaces are equal, then in this subspace $Y = A + b\,\mathbf{C}X$ for some vector $A$, scalar $b$, and [orthonormal matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthonormal_matrix) $\mathbf{C}$.

### Distance covariance {#Distance_covariance}

$\operatorname{dCov}(X,Y)\geq0$ and $\operatorname{dCov}_n(X,Y)\geq0$;; $\operatorname{dCov}^2(a_1 + b_1\,\mathbf{C}_1\,X, a_2 + b_2\,\mathbf{C}_2\,Y) = |b_1\,b_2|\operatorname{dCov}^2(X,Y)$ for all constant vectors $a_1, a_2$, scalars $b_1, b_2$, and orthonormal matrices $\mathbf{C}_1, \mathbf{C}_2$.; If the random vectors $(X_1, Y_1)$ and $(X_2, Y_2)$ are independent then $\operatorname{dCov}(X_1 + X_2, Y_1 + Y_2) \leq \operatorname{dCov}(X_1, Y_1) + \operatorname{dCov}(X_2, Y_2).$ Equality holds if and only if $X_1$ and $Y_1$ are both constants, or $X_2$ and $Y_2$ are both constants, or $X_1, X_2, Y_1, Y_2$ are mutually independent.; $\operatorname{dCov}(X,Y) = 0$ if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are independent. This last property is the most important effect of working with centered distances.

The statistic $\operatorname{dCov}^2_n(X,Y)$ is a biased estimator of $\operatorname{dCov}^2(X,Y)$. Under independence of X and Y [^n7]

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}[\operatorname{dCov}^2_n(X,Y)] & = \frac{n-1}{n^2} \left\{(n-2) \operatorname{dCov}^2(X,Y)+ \operatorname{E}[\|X-X'\|]\,\operatorname{E}[\|Y-Y'\|] \right\} \\[6pt] & = \frac{n-1}{n^2}\operatorname {E}[\|X-X'\|]\,\operatorname{E}[\|Y-Y'\|]. \end{aligned}$$

An [unbiased estimator](wikipedia://en/8450479) of $\operatorname{dCov}^2(X,Y)$ is given by Székely and Rizzo.[^n8]

### Distance variance {#Distance_variance}

$\operatorname{dVar}(X) = 0$ if and only if $X = \operatorname{E}[X]$ almost surely.; $\operatorname{dVar}_n(X) = 0$ if and only if every sample observation is identical.; $\operatorname{dVar}(A + b\,\mathbf{C}\,X) = |b|\operatorname{dVar}(X)$ for all constant vectors $A$, scalars $b$, and orthonormal matrices $\mathbf{C}$.; If $X$ and $Y$ are independent then $\operatorname{dVar}(X + Y) \leq\operatorname{dVar}(X) + \operatorname{dVar}(Y)$. Equality holds in (iv) if and only if one of the random variables $X$ or $Y$ is a constant.

:::

## Generalization {#Generalization}

Distance covariance can be generalized to include powers of Euclidean distance. Define

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{dCov}^2(X, Y; \alpha) := {} & \operatorname{E}[\|X-X'\|^\alpha\,\|Y-Y'\|^\alpha] + \operatorname{E}[\|X-X'\|^\alpha]\,\operatorname{E}[\|Y-Y'\|^\alpha]\\ &\qquad {} - 2\operatorname{E}[\|X-X'\|^\alpha\,\|Y-Y''\|^\alpha]. \end{aligned}$$

Then for every $0<\alpha<2$, $X$ and $Y$ are independent if and only if $\operatorname{dCov}^2(X, Y; \alpha) = 0$. This characterization does not hold for exponent $\alpha=2$; in this case for bivariate $(X, Y)$, $\operatorname{dCor}(X, Y; \alpha=2)$ is a deterministic function of the Pearson correlation.[^n1] If $a_{k,\ell}$ and $b_{k,\ell}$ are $\alpha$ powers of the corresponding distances, $0<\alpha\leq2$, then $\alpha$ sample distance covariance can be defined as the nonnegative number for which

$$\operatorname{dCov}^2_n(X, Y; \alpha):= \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{k,\ell}A_{k,\ell}\,B_{k,\ell}.$$

One can extend $\operatorname{dCov}$ to [metric-space](wikipedia://en/20018)-valued [random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variables) $X$ and $Y$: If $X$ has law $\mu$ in a metric space with metric $d$, then define $a_\mu(x):= \operatorname{E}[d(X, x)]$, $D(\mu) := \operatorname{E}[a_\mu(X)]$, and (provided $a_\mu$ is finite, i.e., $X$ has finite first moment), $d_\mu(x, x') := d(x, x')-a_\mu(x)-a_\mu(x')+D(\mu)$. Then if $Y$ has law $\nu$ (in a possibly different metric space with finite first moment), define

$$\operatorname{dCov}^2(X, Y) := \operatorname{E}\big[d_\mu(X,X')d_\nu(Y,Y')\big].$$

This is non-negative for all such $X, Y$ iff both metric spaces have negative type.[^n9] Here, a metric space $(M, d)$ has negative type if $(M, d^{1/2})$ is [isometric](wikipedia://en/254777) to a subset of a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932).[^n10] If both metric spaces have strong negative type, then $\operatorname{dCov}^2(X, Y)= 0$ iff $X, Y$ are independent.[^n9]

:::

## Alternative definition of distance covariance {#Alternative_definition_of_distance_covariance}

The original [distance covariance](wikipedia://en/28217722#Distance_covariance) has been defined as the square root of $\operatorname{dCov}^2(X,Y)$, rather than the squared coefficient itself. $\operatorname{dCov}(X,Y)$ has the property that it is the [energy distance](wikipedia://en/30316300) between the joint distribution of $\operatorname X, Y$ and the product of its marginals. Under this definition, however, the distance variance, rather than the distance [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590), is measured in the same units as the $\operatorname X$ distances.

Alternately, one could define ***distance covariance*** to be the square of the energy distance: $\operatorname{dCov}^2(X,Y).$ In this case, the distance standard deviation of $X$ is measured in the same units as $X$ distance, and there exists an unbiased estimator for the population distance covariance.[^n8]

Under these alternate definitions, the distance correlation is also defined as the square $\operatorname{dCor}^2(X,Y)$, rather than the square root.

## Alternative formulation: Brownian covariance {#Alternative_formulation:_Brownian_covariance}

Brownian covariance is motivated by generalization of the notion of covariance to stochastic processes. The square of the covariance of random variables X and Y can be written in the following form:

$$\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)^2 = \operatorname{E}\left[ \big(X - \operatorname{E}(X)\big) \big(X^\mathrm{'} - \operatorname{E}(X^\mathrm{'})\big) \big(Y - \operatorname{E}(Y)\big) \big(Y^\mathrm{'} - \operatorname{E}(Y^\mathrm{'})\big) \right]$$

where E denotes the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) and the prime denotes independent and identically distributed copies. We need the following generalization of this formula. If U(s), V(t) are arbitrary random processes defined for all real s and t then define the U-centered version of X by

$$X_U := U(X) - \operatorname{E}_X\left[ U(X) \mid \left \{ U(t) \right \} \right]$$

whenever the subtracted conditional expected value exists and denote by Y<sub>V</sub> the V-centered version of Y.[^n2][^n11][^n12] The (U,V) covariance of (X,Y) is defined as the nonnegative number whose square is

$$\operatorname{cov}_{U,V}^2(X,Y) := \operatorname{E}\left[X_U X_U^\mathrm{'} Y_V Y_V^\mathrm{'}\right]$$

whenever the right-hand side is nonnegative and finite. The most important example is when U and V are two-sided independent [Brownian motions](wikipedia://en/4436) /[Wiener processes](wikipedia://en/149984) with expectation zero and covariance $\lvert s\rvert$ + $\lvert t\rvert$ − $\lvert s - t\rvert$ = 2 min(*s*,*t*) (for nonnegative s, t only). (This is twice the covariance of the standard Wiener process; here the factor 2 simplifies the computations.) In this case the (*U*,*V*) covariance is called **Brownian covariance** and is denoted by

$$\operatorname{cov}_W(X,Y).$$

There is a surprising coincidence: The Brownian covariance is the same as the distance covariance:

$$\operatorname{cov}_{\mathrm{W}}(X, Y) = \operatorname{dCov}(X, Y),$$

and thus **Brownian correlation** is the same as distance correlation.

On the other hand, if we replace the Brownian motion with the deterministic [identity function](wikipedia://en/15069) *id* then Cov<sub>id</sub>(*X*,*Y*) is simply the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of the classical Pearson [covariance](wikipedia://en/157059),

$$\operatorname{cov}_{\mathrm{id}}(X,Y) = \left\vert\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)\right\vert.$$

## Related metrics {#Related_metrics}

Other correlational metrics, including kernel-based correlational metrics (such as the Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion or HSIC) can also detect linear and nonlinear interactions. Both distance correlation and kernel-based metrics can be used in methods such as [canonical correlation analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_correlation_analysis) and [independent component analysis](wikipedia://en/598031) to yield stronger [statistical power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_power).
