# John Major (philosopher)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="John Major"}

*Image: John Mair.jpg.* Major in a contemporary [woodcut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodcut)

**John Major** (or **Mair**; also known in [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) as *Joannes Majoris* and *Haddingtonus Scotus*; 1467–1550) was a Scottish philosopher, theologian, and historian who was admired and influential. As a renowned teacher, his works were collected and republished across Europe. His "sane conservatism" and his [sceptical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scepticism), [logical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_logic) approach to the study of texts such as [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) or the [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible) went out of fashion in the subsequent age of [humanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_humanism).

:::

Mair's influence extended to [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) (especially the analysis of [terms](wikipedia://en/5395098)), natural philosophy ([impetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_impetus) and [infinitesimals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimals)), politics (placing the people over kings), Church (councils over Popes), and [international law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_law) (human rights of indigenous peoples conquered by the [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conquistador)). His [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) style lacked elegance; but he was observant, and used his experiences – of earthquakes in [Paisley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paisley,_Renfrewshire), thunder in [Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow), storms at sea, eating oatcakes in northern England – to illustrate his logical writings.

## Life {#Life}

### School {#School}

John Major (or 'Mair') was born about 1467 at Gleghornie, East Lothian near [North Berwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Berwick) where he received his early education. It was at nearby [Haddington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haddington,_East_Lothian), East Lothian, Scotland, where he attended [grammar school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammar_school). He was probably taught by the town schoolmaster, who was, according to Major "although a circumspect man in other ways, more severe than was just in beating boys". If it had not been for the influence of his mother, Major says he would have left, but he and his brother stayed on and were successful. According to him, Haddington was "the town which fostered the beginning of my studies, and in whose kindly embrace I was nourished as a novice with the sweetest milk of the art of grammar". He says he stayed in Haddington "to a pretty advanced age" and he remembers the sound of the [King James III's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_III_of_Scotland) bombardment of the nearby [castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunbar_Castle) of [Dunbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunbar), which was in 1479. He also remembers the comet which was supposed to have foretold the King's defeat at [Sauchieburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauchieburn) which was in 1488.

However, it was in 1490, he reports, that he "first left the paternal hearth". In 1490, probably under the influence of [Robert Cockburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Cockburn_%28diplomat%29), another Haddington man, destined to be an influential bishop (of [Ross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Ross_%28Scotland%29) and later of [Dunkeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Dunkeld)), he decided to go to [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris) to study among the great numbers of Scots there at the time.

### University {#University}

It is not known whether he attended university in Scotland as a student – there are no matriculation records of him and he claimed never to have seen the university town of [St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Andrews_University), Fife as a young man (though he did complain later of its bad beer). He seems to have decided to prepare for Paris at [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge) in England. He says that in 1492 he attended "Gods House", which later became [Christ's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ's_College,_Cambridge). He remembers the bells – "on great feast days, I spent half the night listening to them" – but was obviously well-prepared, as he left for Paris after three terms.

In 1493 he matriculated in the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris), France, then the foremost university in Europe. He studied at the [Collège Sainte-Barbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collège_Sainte-Barbe) and took his [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts) degree there in 1495 followed by his master's degree in 1496. There were many currents of thought in Paris but he was heavily influenced, as were fellow Scots such as [Lawrence of Lindores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_of_Lindores) by the [nominalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nominalist) and [empiricist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricist) approach of [John Buridan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Buridan). (The latter's influence on [Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicus) and [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo) can be traced through Major's published works). He became a student master ('regent') in Arts in the [Collège de Montaigu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collège_de_Montaigu) in 1496 and began the study of theology under the formidable [Jan Standonck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Standonck). He consorted with scholars of later renown, some from his hometown, [Robert Walterston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Walterston), and his home country ([David Cranston](wikipedia://en/63437817) of [Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow), who died in 1512), but mostly they were the luminaries of the age, including [Erasmus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erasmus), whose reforming enthusiasms he shared, [Rabelais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabelais) and [Reginald Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reginald_Pole). In the winter of 1497 he had a serious illness, from which he never completely recovered. He had never had dreams before, but ever afterwards he was troubled by dreams, [migraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migraine), [colic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colic) and "excessive sleepiness" (he was always hard to awaken). In 1499, he moved to the [College of Navarre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_of_Navarre). In 1501, he received his degree of [Bachelor of Sacred Theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Sacred_Theology) and in 1505 his logical writings were collected and published for the first time. In 1506 he was licensed to teach theology and was awarded the degree of [Doctor of Sacred Theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Sacred_Theology) on 11 November that year (coming 3rd in the listings). He taught at the Collège de Montaigu (where he was, temporarily joint Director) and also the prestigious [Sorbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_of_Sorbonne), where he served on many commissions.

### Later career {#Later_career}

In 1510 he discussed the moral and legal questions arising from the [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Conquest) discovery of [America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americas). He claimed that the natives had political and property rights that could not be invaded, at least not without compensation. He also uses the new discoveries to argue for the possibility of innovation in all knowledge saying "Has not [Amerigo Vespucci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amerigo_Vespucci) discovered lands unknown to [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979), [Pliny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder) and other geographers up to the present? Why cannot the same happen in other spheres?" At the same time, he was impatient of [humanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanism) criticism of the logical analysis of texts (including the Bible). "...these questions which the [humanists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanists) think futile, are like a ladder for the intelligence to rise towards the Bible" (which he elsewhere, perhaps unwisely called "the easier parts of theology"). Nevertheless, in 1512, like a good humanist, he learned Greek from [Girolamo Aleandro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girolamo_Aleandro) (who re-introduced the study of Greek to Paris) who wrote "Many scholastics are to be found in France who are keen students in different kinds of knowledge and several of these are among my faithful hearers, such as John Mair, Doctor of Philosophy..."

In 1518 he returned to Scotland to become [Principal of the University of Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_of_the_University_of_Glasgow) (and also [canon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_%28priest%29) of [the cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow_Cathedral), [vicar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vicar#Roman_Catholic) of [Dunlop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corsehill,_Lainshaw,_Robertland_and_Dunlop#The_Lands_of_Dunlop) and Treasurer of the Chapel Royal). He returned to Paris several times – by sea one time, getting delayed in Dieppe for three weeks by a storm; and by land another time, having dinner en route through England with his friend, [Cardinal Wolsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_Wolsey). He offered Major a post, which he declined, in his new college at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford), to be called Cardinal's College, (later [Christ Church, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_Church,_Oxford)). In 1528, [King Francis I of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_I_of_France) issued Major with a patent of naturalisation, making him a naturalised subject of France.

In 1533 he was made Provost of [St Salvator's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Salvator's_College) in the [University of St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews) – to which thronged many of the most significant men in Scotland, including [John Knox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Knox) and [George Buchanan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Buchanan). He missed Paris – "When I was in Scotland, I often thought how I would go back to Paris and give lectures as I used to and hear disputations". He died in 1550 (perhaps on 1 May), his works read throughout Europe, his name honoured everywhere, just as the storms of the [Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Reformation) were about to sweep away, at least in his own country, any respect for his centuries-old methodology.

## Influence {#Influence}

### Historians {#Historians}

His *De Gestis Scotorum* (Paris, 1521) was partly a patriotic attempt to raise the profile of his native country, but was also an attempt to clear away myth and fable, basing his history on evidence. In this, he was following in the footsteps of his predecessor, the [Chronicler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronicler) [Andrew of Wyntoun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_of_Wyntoun), though writing in [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) for a European audience as opposed to the [Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scots_Language) Andrew wrote for his aristocratic Scots patrons. Although the documentary evidence available to Major was limited, his scholarly approach was adopted and improved by later historians of [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland), including his pupil [Hector Boece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hector_Boece), and [John Lesley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lesley).

### Calvin and Loyola {#Calvin_and_Loyola}

In 1506 he was awarded a doctorate in theology by Paris where he began to teach and progress through the hierarchy, becoming for a brief period [Rector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rector_%28academia%29). (Some 18 of his fellow Scots had held or were to hold this prestigious position). He was a renowned [logician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logician) and philosopher. He is reported to have been a very clear and forceful lecturer, attracting students from all over Europe. In contrast, he had a rather dry, some said 'barbaric', written [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) style. He was referred to by [Pierre Bayle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Bayle) as writing "*in stylo Sorbonico*", not meaning this as a compliment. His interests ranged across the burning issues of the day. His approach largely followed [Nominalism](wikipedia://en/21176) which was in tune with the growing emphasis on the absolutely unconstrained nature of God, which in turn emphasised his grace and the importance of individual belief and submission. His [humanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanism) approach was in tune with the return to the texts in the original languages of the Scriptures and classical authors. He emphasised that authority lay with the whole church and not with the Pope. Similarly, he asserted that authority in a kingdom lay not with the king but with the people, who could retake their power from a delinquent king (a striking echo of the ringing [Declaration of Arbroath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_Arbroath) 1320 confirming to the Pope the independence of the Scottish crown from that of England). It is not surprising that he emphasised the natural freedom of human beings.

His influence extended through enthusiastic pupils to the leading thinkers of the day but most obviously to a group of Spanish thinkers, including Antonio Coronel, who taught [John Calvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Calvin) and very probably [Ignatius of Loyola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignatius_of_Loyola).

In 1522, at [Salamanca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_of_Salamanca), [Domingo de San Juan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domingo_de_San_Juan) referred to him as "*the revered master, John Mair, a man celebrated the world over*". The Salamanca school of (largely [Thomist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomist)) philosophers was a brilliant flowering of thought until the early parts of the seventeenth century. It included [Francisco de Vitoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_de_Vitoria), **Cano**, de [Domingo de Soto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domingo_de_Soto) and [Bartolomé de Medina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartolomé_de_Medina_%28theologian%29), each one thorough soaked in Mairian enthusiasms.

### Knox {#Knox}

Major wrote in his *Commentary on the Sentences of [Peter Lombard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Lombard)* *"Our native soil attracts us with a secret and inexpressible sweetness and does not permit us to forget it".* He returned to Scotland in 1518. Given his success and experience in Paris, it is no surprise that he became the Principal of the [University of Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Glasgow). In 1523 left for the [University of St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews) where he was assessor to the Dean of Arts. In 1525 he went again to Paris from where he returned in 1531 eventually to become [Provost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provost_%28education%29) of [St Salvator's College, St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Salvator's_College,_St_Andrews) until his death in 1550, aged about eighty three.

One of his most notable students was [John Knox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Knox) (coincidentally, another native of Haddington) who said of Major that he was such as "*whose work was then held as an oracle on the matters of religion*" If this is not exactly a ringing endorsement, it is not hard to see in Knox's preaching an intense version of Major's enthusiasms – the utter freedom of God, the importance of the Bible, scepticism of earthly authority. It might be more surprising that Major preferred to follow his friend [Erasmus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erasmus)'s example and remain within the Roman Catholic Church (though he did envisage a national church for Scotland). Major also filled with enthusiasm other Scottish Reformers including the Protestant martyr [Patrick Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Hamilton_%28martyr%29) and the Latin stylist [George Buchanan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Buchanan), whose enthusiasm for witty Latinisms had him waspishly suggesting that the only thing major about his ex-teacher was his surname – typical Renaissance disdain for the [Schoolmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schoolmen).

### Empiricism {#Empiricism}

Major and his circle were interested in the structures of [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language) – spoken, written and 'mental'. This latter was the language which underlies the thoughts that are expressed in natural languages, like [Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scots_language), English or [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin). He attacks a whole range of questions from a generally 'nominalist' perspective – a form of philosophical discourse whose tradition derives from the high [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) and was to continue into that of the Scottish and other European [empiricists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricists). According to [Alexander Broadie](wikipedia://en/30277296), Major's influence on this latter tradition reached as far as the 18th and 19th century [Scottish School of Common Sense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_School_of_Common_Sense) initiated by [Thomas Reid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Reid). The highly logical and technical approach of [Medieval philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_philosophy) – perhaps added to by Major's poor written style as well as his adherence to the Catholic party at the time of the [Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformation) – explain in some part why this influence is still somewhat occluded.

### Human rights {#Human_rights}

More obviously influential was his [moral philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_philosophy), not primarily because of his [casuistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casuistry) – an approach acknowledging the complexity of individual cases. This was later so strong in [Jesuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuit) teaching, possibly related to the Major's renown in Spain mentioned above. His legal views were also influential. His Commentaries on the [Sentences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentences) of [Peter Lombard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Lombard) was most certainly studied and quoted in the debates at [Burgos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burgos) in 1512, by [Frày Anton Montesino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_de_Montesinos_%28Dominican_friar%29), a graduate of [Salamanca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salamanca). This "*debate unique in the history of empires*", as [Hugh Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Thomas_%28historian%29) calls it, resulted in the recognition in Spanish law of the [indigenous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas) populations of [America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americas) as being free [human beings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_beings) with all the rights (to liberty and property, for example) attached to them. This pronouncement was hedged in with many subtle qualifications, and the [Spanish crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_monarchy) was never efficient at enforcing it, but it can be regarded as the fount of [human rights law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_law).[^o14]
