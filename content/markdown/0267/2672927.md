# Philip Kitcher

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Philip Kitcher"}

**Philip Stuart Kitcher** (born 20 February 1947) is a British [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher) who is the John Dewey professor emeritus of philosophy at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University).[^o6] He specialises in the [philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science), the [philosophy of biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_biology), the [philosophy of mathematics](wikipedia://en/46439), and more recently [pragmatism](wikipedia://en/95170).

:::

## Life and career {#Life_and_career}

Born in London, Kitcher spent his early life in [Eastbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastbourne), [East Sussex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Sussex), on the south coast of the United Kingdom, where another distinguished philosopher of an earlier generation ([A.&nbsp;J. Ayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A.&nbsp;J._Ayer)) was also at school. Kitcher himself went to school at [Christ's Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ's_Hospital), Horsham, [West Sussex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Sussex).[^o8][^o9] He earned his BA in mathematics/history and philosophy of science from [Christ's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ's_College,_Cambridge), in 1969, and his PhD in history and philosophy of science from [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) in 1974, where he worked closely with [Carl Hempel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Hempel) and [Thomas Kuhn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Kuhn).

Kitcher is currently [John Dewey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dewey) Professor of Philosophy Emeritus at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University). As chair of Columbia's [Contemporary Civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core_Curriculum_%28Columbia_College%29#Table_of_Core_Curriculum_Requirements) program (part of its undergraduate [Core Curriculum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core_Curriculum_%28Columbia_College%29)), he also held the [James R. Barker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_R._Barker_%28businessman%29) Professorship of Contemporary Civilization. Before moving to Columbia, Kitcher held tenure-track positions at the [University of Vermont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Vermont), the [University of Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Minnesota), and [University of California, San Diego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_San_Diego), where he held the position of Presidential Professor of Philosophy.

Kitcher is past president of the [American Philosophical Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Association). In 2002, Kitcher was named a fellow of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences), and he was awarded the inaugural Prometheus Prize from the [American Philosophical Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Association) in 2006 in honour of extended achievement in the [philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science). He was elected to the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society) in 2018.[^o10] Kitcher was Editor-in-Chief of the journal *Philosophy of Science* from 1994 to 1999, was also a member of the [NIH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institutes_of_Health)/[DOE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Energy) Working Group on the Ethical, Legal, and Social Implications of the [Human Genome Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Genome_Project) from 1995 to 1997.

He has trained a number of philosophers of science, including [Peter Godfrey-Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Godfrey-Smith) ([University of Sydney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Sydney)), [Kyle Stanford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyle_Stanford) ([University of California, Irvine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Irvine)), and [Michael R. Dietrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_R._Dietrich) ([University of Pittsburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pittsburgh)). He also taught [C. Kenneth Waters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._Kenneth_Waters) (University of Calgary) and [Michael Weisberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Weisberg) (University of Pennsylvania) as undergraduates.

Kitcher is married to [Patricia Kitcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patricia_Kitcher), a [Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant) scholar, [philosopher of mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher_of_mind), and the [Mark Van Doren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Van_Doren) Professor of Humanities at Columbia. Their son, Charles Kitcher, is the associate general counsel for the [Federal Election Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Election_Commission).[^o11][^o12]

## Philosophical work {#Philosophical_work}

Within philosophy, Kitcher is best known for his work in philosophy of [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology), [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science), and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and outside academia for his work examining [creationism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creationism) and [sociobiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociobiology). His works attempt to connect the questions raised in [philosophy of biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_biology) and [philosophy of mathematics](wikipedia://en/46439) with the central philosophical issues of [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology), [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics), and [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics). He has also published papers on [John Stuart Mill](wikipedia://en/15626), [Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant) and other figures in the [history of philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_philosophy). His 2012 book[^o14] documented his developing interest in [John Dewey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dewey) and a pragmatic approach to philosophical issues. He sees [pragmatism](wikipedia://en/95170) as providing a unifying and reconstructive approach to traditional philosophy issues. He had, a year earlier, published a book outlining a naturalistic approach to ethics, *The Ethical Project* (Harvard University Press, 2011).[^o15] He has also done work on the philosophy of [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change).[^o16][^o17]

### Criteria for what constitutes "good science" {#Criteria_for_what_constitutes_"good_science"}

Kitcher's three criteria for good science are:[^o19]

\1. Independent testability of auxiliary hypotheses

"An auxiliary hypothesis ought to be testable independently of the particular problem it is introduced to solve, independently of the theory it is designed to save" (e.g. the evidence for the existence of [Neptune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptune) is independent of the anomalies in Uranus's orbit).

\2. Unification

"A science should be unified .... Good theories consist of just one problem-solving strategy, or a small family of problem-solving strategies, that can be applied to a wide range of problems".

\3. Fecundity

"A great scientific theory, like Newton's, opens up new areas of research... Because a theory presents a new way of looking at the world, it can lead us to ask new questions, and so to embark on new and fruitful lines of inquiry... Typically, a flourishing science is incomplete. At any time, it raises more questions than it can currently answer. But incompleteness is no vice. On the contrary, incompleteness is the mother of fecundity... A good theory should be productive; it should raise new questions and presume that those questions can be answered without giving up its problem-solving strategies".

He increasingly recognised the role of values in practical decisions about scientific research.[^o20]

### Kuhn and creationism {#Kuhn_and_creationism}

Kitcher is the author of *Abusing Science: The Case Against Creationism*. He has commented on the way [creationists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creationism) have misinterpreted Kuhn:

[Thomas Kuhn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Kuhn)'s book *[The Structure of Scientific Revolutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Structure_of_Scientific_Revolutions)* has probably been more widely read—and more widely misinterpreted—than any other book in the recent philosophy of science. The broad circulation of his views has generated a popular caricature of Kuhn's position. According to this popular caricature, scientists working in a field belong to a club. All club members are required to agree on main points of doctrine. Indeed, the price of admission is several years of [graduate education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graduate_education), during which the chief [dogmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogma) are inculcated. The views of outsiders are ignored. Now I want to emphasize that this is a hopeless caricature, both of the practice of scientists and of Kuhn's analysis of the practice. Nevertheless, the caricature has become commonly accepted as a faithful representation, thereby lending support to the Creationists' claims that their views are arrogantly disregarded.[^o22]
