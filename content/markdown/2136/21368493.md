# 2-group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="2-group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), particularly [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a **2-group** is a [groupoid](wikipedia://en/12543) with a way to multiply [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) and [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891), making it resemble a [group](wikipedia://en/19447). They are part of a larger hierarchy of [*n*-groups](wikipedia://en/21368075). They were introduced by [Hoàng Xuân Sính](wikipedia://en/47277239) in the late 1960s under the name **gr-categories**,[^o2][^o3] and they are also known as **categorical groups**.

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A 2-group is a [monoidal category](wikipedia://en/330604) *G* in which every [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) is [invertible](wikipedia://en/14828) and every object has a weak inverse. (Here, a *weak inverse* of an object *x* is an object *y* such that *xy* and *yx* are both [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the unit object.)

:::

::: {#o7 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Strict 2-group"}

## Strict 2-groups {#Strict_2-groups}

Much of the literature focuses on *strict 2-groups*. A strict 2-group is a [*strict* monoidal category](wikipedia://en/330604#Formal_definition) in which every morphism is invertible and every object has a strict inverse (so that *xy* and *yx* are actually equal to the unit object).

A strict 2-group is a [group object](wikipedia://en/53507) in a [category of (small) categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_categories); as such, they could be called *groupal categories*. Conversely, a strict 2-group is a [category object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_object) in the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092); as such, they are also called *categorical groups*. They can also be identified with [crossed modules](wikipedia://en/1977281), and are most often studied in that form. Thus, 2-groups in general can be seen as a weakening of crossed modules.

Every 2-group is [equivalent](wikipedia://en/379051) to a strict 2-group, although this can't be done coherently: it doesn't extend to 2-group homomorphisms.

:::

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Given a ([small](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_category)) category *C*, we can consider the 2-group Aut *C*. This is the monoidal category whose objects are the autoequivalences of *C* (i.e. [equivalences](wikipedia://en/379051) *F*: *C*→*C*), whose morphisms are [natural isomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_isomorphism) between such autoequivalences, and the multiplication of autoequivalences is given by their composition.

Given a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X* and a point *x* in that space, there is a **fundamental 2-group** of *X* at *x*, written Π<sub>2</sub>(*X*,*x*). As a monoidal category, the objects are [loops](wikipedia://en/1189560) at *x*, with multiplication given by concatenation, and the morphisms are basepoint-preserving [homotopies](wikipedia://en/212250) between loops, with these morphisms identified if they are themselves homotopic.

:::

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Weak inverses can always be assigned coherently:[^n1] one can define a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) on any 2-group *G* that assigns a weak inverse to each object, so that each object is related to its designated weak inverse by an [adjoint equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_equivalence) in the monoidal category *G*.

Given a [bicategory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicategory) *B* and an object *x* of *B*, there is an *automorphism 2-group* of *x* in *B*, written Aut<sub>*B*</sub>*x*. The objects are the [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of *x*, with multiplication given by composition, and the morphisms are the invertible 2-morphisms between these. If *B* is a [2-groupoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-groupoid) (so all objects and morphisms are weakly invertible) and *x* is its only object, then Aut<sub>*B*</sub>*x* is the only data left in *B*. Thus, 2-groups may be identified with one-object 2-groupoids, much as groups may be identified with one-object groupoids and monoidal categories may be identified with one-object bicategories.

If *G* is a strict 2-group, then the objects of *G* form a group, called the *underlying group* of *G* and written *G*<sub>0</sub>. This will not work for arbitrary 2-groups; however, if one identifies isomorphic objects, then the [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) form a group, called the *fundamental group* of *G* and written π<sub>1</sub>*G*. (Note that even for a strict 2-group, the fundamental group will only be a [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) of the underlying group.)

As a monoidal category, any 2-group *G* has a unit object *I*<sub>*G*</sub>. The [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of *I*<sub>*G*</sub> is an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) by the [Eckmann–Hilton argument](wikipedia://en/488809), written Aut(*I*<sub>*G*</sub>) or π<sub>2</sub>*G*.

The fundamental group of *G* [acts](wikipedia://en/12781) on either side of π<sub>2</sub>*G*, and the [associator](wikipedia://en/330604#Formal_definition) of *G* defines an element of the [cohomology group](wikipedia://en/248635) H<sup>3</sup>(π<sub>1</sub>*G*, π<sub>2</sub>*G*). In fact, 2-groups are [classified](wikipedia://en/4576710) in this way: given a group π<sub>1</sub>, an abelian group π<sub>2</sub>, a group action of π<sub>1</sub> on π<sub>2</sub>, and an element of H<sup>3</sup>(π<sub>1</sub>, π<sub>2</sub>), there is a unique ([up to](wikipedia://en/44787) equivalence) 2-group *G* with π<sub>1</sub>*G* isomorphic to π<sub>1</sub>, π<sub>2</sub>*G* isomorphic to π<sub>2</sub>, and the other data corresponding.

The element of H<sup>3</sup>(π<sub>1</sub>, π<sub>2</sub>) associated to a 2-group is sometimes called its **Sinh invariant**, as it was developed by [Grothendieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck)'s student [Hoàng Xuân Sính](wikipedia://en/47277239).

:::

## Fundamental 2-group {#Fundamental_2-group}

As mentioned above, the fundamental 2-group of a topological space *X* and a point *x* is the 2-group Π<sub>2</sub>(*X*,*x*), whose objects are [loops](wikipedia://en/1189560) at *x*, with multiplication given by concatenation, and the morphisms are basepoint-preserving [homotopies](wikipedia://en/212250) between loops, with these morphisms identified if they are themselves homotopic.

Conversely, given any 2-group *G*, one can find a unique ([up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [weak homotopy equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_homotopy_equivalence)) [pointed](wikipedia://en/1236666) [connected space](wikipedia://en/6233) (*X*,*x*) whose fundamental 2-group is *G* and whose [homotopy groups](wikipedia://en/297506) π<sub>*n*</sub> are trivial for *n* > 2. In this way, 2-groups [classify](wikipedia://en/4576710) pointed connected weak homotopy 2-types. This is a generalisation of the construction of [Eilenberg–Mac Lane spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eilenberg–Mac_Lane_space).

If *X* is a topological space with basepoint *x*, then the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of *X* at *x* is the same as the fundamental group of the fundamental 2-group of *X* at *x*; that is,

$$\pi_1(X,x) = \pi_1(\Pi_2(X,x)) .\!$$

This fact is the origin of the term "fundamental" in both of its 2-group instances.

Similarly,

$$\pi_2(X,x) = \pi_2(\Pi_2(X,x)) .\!$$

Thus, both the first and second [homotopy groups](wikipedia://en/297506) of a space are contained within its fundamental 2-group. As this 2-group also defines an action of π<sub>1</sub>(*X*,*x*) on π<sub>2</sub>(*X*,*x*) and an element of the cohomology group H<sup>3</sup>(π<sub>1</sub>(*X*,*x*), π<sub>2</sub>(*X*,*x*)), this is precisely the data needed to form the [Postnikov tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postnikov_tower) of *X* if *X* is a pointed connected homotopy 2-type.
