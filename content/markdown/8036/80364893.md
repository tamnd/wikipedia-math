# Principal U(1)-bundle

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Principal U(1)-bundle"}

*Image: Circle\_as\_Lie\_group.svg.* Composition in the first unitary group

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), **principal $\operatorname{U}(1)$-bundles** (or **principal $\operatorname{SO}(2)$-bundles**) are special [principal bundles](wikipedia://en/310950) with the first [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) $\operatorname{U}(1)$ ([isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to the second [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) **$\operatorname{SO}(2)$**) as [structure group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_group). Topologically, it has the structure of the one-dimensional [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859), hence principal **$\operatorname{U}(1)$**-bundles without their group action are in particular [circle bundles](wikipedia://en/2794417). These are basically [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) with a circle glued to every point, so that all of them are connected with each other, but globally aren't necessarily a [product](wikipedia://en/24910) and can instead be twisted like a [Möbius strip](wikipedia://en/37817).

:::

Principal $\operatorname{U}(1)$-bundles are used in many areas of mathematics, for example for the formulation of the [Seiberg–Witten equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seiberg–Witten_equations) or [monopole Floer homology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopole_Floer_homology). Since $\operatorname{U}(1)$ is the [gauge group](wikipedia://en/39240530) of the [electromagnetic interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_interaction), principal $\operatorname{U}(1)$-bundles are also of interest in [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028). Concretely, the $\operatorname{U}(1)$-Yang–Mills equations are exactly [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737). In particular, principal $\operatorname{U}(1)$-bundles over the two-dimensional [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) $S^2$, which include the complex [Hopf fibration](wikipedia://en/580384), can be used to describe hypothetical [magnetic monopoles](wikipedia://en/175470) in three dimensions, known as [Dirac monopoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_monopole), see also [two-dimensional Yang–Mills theory](wikipedia://en/66319632).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Principal $\operatorname{U}(1)$-bundles are generalizations of [canonical projections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_projection) $B\times\operatorname{U}(1)\twoheadrightarrow B$ for [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) $B$, so that the source is not globally a product but only locally. More concretely, a continuous map $p\colon E\twoheadrightarrow B$ with a continuous [right group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_group_action) $E\times\operatorname{U}(1)\rightarrow E$, which preserves all [preimages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preimage) of points, hence $p(eg)=p(e)$ for all $e\in E$ and $g\in G$, and also acts [free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_group_action) and [transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_group) on all preimages of points, which makes all of them [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) to $\operatorname{U}(1)$, is a principal $\operatorname{U}(1)$-bundle.[^o4][^o5]

Since principal bundles are in particular fiber bundles with the group action missing, their nomenclature can be transferred. $E$ is also called the *total space* and $B$ is also called the *base space*. Preimages of points are then the fibers. Since $\operatorname{U}(1)$ is a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945), hence in particular a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold), the base space $B$ is often chosen to be a smooth manifold as well since this automatically makes the total space $E$ into a smooth manifold as well.

:::

## Classification {#Classification}

Principal $\operatorname{U}(1)$-bundles can be fully classified using the [classifying space](wikipedia://en/2819900) $\operatorname{BU}(1)$ of the first unitary group $\operatorname{U}(1)$, which is exactly the infinite [complex projective space](wikipedia://en/762977) $\mathbb{C}P^\infty\!$. For a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $B$, let $\operatorname{Prin}_{\operatorname{U}(1)}(B)$ denote the set of [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of principal $\operatorname{U}(1)$-bundles over it, then there is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) with [homotopy classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_classes):[^o7]

$$\operatorname{Prin}_{\operatorname{U}(1)}(B) \cong[B,\operatorname{BU}(1)] \cong[B,\mathbb{C}P^\infty].$$

$\mathbb{C}P^\infty$ is a [CW complex](wikipedia://en/353022) with its $n$-skeleton being $\mathbb{C}P^k$ for the largest [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $k\in\mathbb{N}$ with $2k\leq n$. For a $n$-dimensional CW complex $B$, the [cellular approximation theorem](wikipedia://en/21537120)[^o8] states that every continuous map $B\rightarrow\mathbb{C}P^\infty$ is homotopic to a cellular map factoring over the canonical inclusion $\mathbb{C}P^k\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}P^\infty\!$. As a result, the induced map $[B,\mathbb{C}P^k]\hookrightarrow[B,\mathbb{C}P^\infty]$ is surjective, but not necessarily injective as higher cells of $\mathbb{C}P^\infty$ allow additional homotopies. In particular if $B$ is a CW complex of three or less dimensions, then $k=1$ and with $\mathbb{C}P^1 \cong S^2$, there is a connection to [cohomotopy sets](wikipedia://en/682693) with a surjective map:

$$\pi^2(B)\rightarrow\operatorname{Prin}_{\operatorname{U}(1)}(B).$$

$\mathbb{C}P^\infty$ is also the [Eilenberg–MacLane space](wikipedia://en/1073230) $K(\mathbb{Z},2)$,[^o9] which [represents](wikipedia://en/844461) [singular cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_cohomology),[^o10] compare to [Brown's representability theorem](wikipedia://en/1073746):

$$\operatorname{Prin}_{\operatorname{U}(1)}(B) \cong H^2(B,\mathbb{Z}).$$

(The composition $\pi^2(B)\rightarrow H^2(B,\mathbb{Z})$ is the [Hurewicz map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurewicz_map).) A corresponding isomorphism is given by the first [Chern class](wikipedia://en/294349). Although [characteristic classes](wikipedia://en/363530) are defined for [vector bundles](wikipedia://en/276524), it is possible to also define them for certain principal bundles.

## Associated vector bundle {#Associated_vector_bundle}

Given a principal $\operatorname{U}(1)$-bundle $E\twoheadrightarrow B$, there is an associated [vector bundle](wikipedia://en/276524) $E\times_{\operatorname{U}(1)}\mathbb{C}\twoheadrightarrow B$. Intuitively, the spheres at every point are filled over the canonical inclusions $\operatorname{U}(1)\subset\mathbb{C}$. Due to the single rank, the vector bundle is only described by the first [Chern class](wikipedia://en/294349) $c_1\colon\operatorname{Vect}_\mathbb{C}(B)\rightarrow H^2(B,\mathbb{Z})$, which is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) over CW complexes.[^o12]

Principal bundles also have an adjoint vector bundle, which is trivial for principal $\operatorname{U}(1)$-bundles.

::: {#o14 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- By definition of [complex projective space](wikipedia://en/762977), the canonical projection $S^{2n+1}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{C}P^n$ is a principal $\operatorname{U}(1)$-bundle. With $\mathbb{C}P^1\cong S^2$, known as the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799), the complex [Hopf fibration](wikipedia://en/580384) $h_\mathbb{C}\colon S^3\twoheadrightarrow S^2$ is a special case. For the general case, the classifying map is the canonical inclusion:

$$\mathbb{C}P^n\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}P^\infty \cong\operatorname{BU}(1).$$

- One has $S^{2n+1} \cong\operatorname{U}(n+1)/\operatorname{U}(n)$, which means that there is a principal $\operatorname{U}(1)$-bundle $\operatorname{U}(2)\twoheadrightarrow S^3\!$. Such bundles are classified by:[^o15]

$$\pi_3\operatorname{BU}(1) \cong\pi_2\operatorname{U}(1) \cong\pi_2S^1 \cong 1.$$

Hence the bundle is trivial, which fits that $\operatorname{SU}(2)\cong S^3$ and $\operatorname{U}(n)\cong\operatorname{SU}(n)\times\operatorname{U}(1)$.

- One has $S^n \cong\operatorname{SO}(n+1)/\operatorname{SO}(n)$, which means that (using $\operatorname{U}(1) \cong\operatorname{SO}(2)$) there is a principal $\operatorname{U}(1)$-bundle $\operatorname{SO}(3)\twoheadrightarrow S^2$. Such bundles are classified by:[^o15]

$$\pi_2\operatorname{BU}(1) \cong\pi_1\operatorname{U}(1) \cong\pi_1S^1 \cong\mathbb{Z}.$$

One has $\operatorname{SO}(3)\cong\mathbb{R}P^3$ and the composition of the canonical double cover $S^3\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{R}P^3$ with the principal bundle $\mathbb{R}P^3\twoheadrightarrow S^2$ is exactly the complex Hopf fibration $S^3\twoheadrightarrow S^2$. Since the first Chern class of the complex Hopf fibration is $-1$, the first Chern class of the principal bundle $\mathbb{R}P^3\twoheadrightarrow S^2$ is $-2$.

:::
