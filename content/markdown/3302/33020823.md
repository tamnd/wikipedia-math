# 56-bit encryption

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="56-bit encryption"}

In computing, **56-bit encryption** refers to a [key size](wikipedia://en/5749) of fifty-six [bits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit), or seven [bytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byte), for [symmetric encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_encryption). While stronger than [40-bit encryption](wikipedia://en/487403), this still represents a relatively low [level of security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Level_of_security) in the context of a [brute force attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brute_force_attack).

:::

## Description {#Description}

The US government traditionally regulated encryption for reasons of national security, law enforcement and foreign policy. Encryption was regulated from 1976 by the [Arms Export Control Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arms_Export_Control_Act) until control was transferred to the [Department of Commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Commerce) in 1996.

56-bit refers to the size of a symmetric key used to encrypt data, with the number of unique possible permutations being $2^{56}$ (72,057,594,037,927,936). 56-bit encryption has its roots in [DES](wikipedia://en/7978), which was the official standard of the US [National Bureau of Standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Standards) from 1976, and later also the [RC5](wikipedia://en/102600) algorithm. US government regulations required any users of stronger 56-bit symmetric keys to submit to key recovery through algorithms like [CDMF](wikipedia://en/10605858) or key escrow,[^o3] effectively reducing the key strength to 40-bit, and thereby allowing organisations such as the [NSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NSA) to brute-force this encryption. Furthermore, from 1996 software products [exported](wikipedia://en/25872370) from the United States were not permitted to use stronger than 56-bit encryption, requiring different software editions for the US and export markets.[^o4] In 1999, US allowed 56-bit encryption to be exported without key escrow or any other key recovery requirements.

The advent of [commerce on the Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-commerce) and faster computers raised concerns about the security of electronic transactions initially with 40-bit, and subsequently also with 56-bit encryption. In February 1997, [RSA Security](wikipedia://en/301240) ran a brute force competition with a \$10,000 prize to demonstrate the weakness of 56-bit encryption; the contest was won four months later.[^o5] In July 1998, a successful brute-force attack was demonstrated against 56-bit encryption with [Deep Crack](wikipedia://en/839358) in just 56 hours.[^o6]

In 2000, all restrictions on key length were lifted, except for exports to embargoed countries.[^o7]

56-bit DES encryption is now obsolete, having been replaced as a standard in 2002 by the 128-bit (and stronger) [Advanced Encryption Standard](wikipedia://en/1260). DES continues to be used as a symmetric cipher in combination with [Kerberos](wikipedia://en/16947) because older products do not support newer ciphers like [AES](wikipedia://en/1260).[^o8]
