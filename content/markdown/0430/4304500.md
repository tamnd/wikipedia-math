# Singular measure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Singular measure"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), two positive (or [signed](wikipedia://en/2985811) or [complex](wikipedia://en/3057532)) measures $\mu$ and $\nu$ defined on a [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333) $(\Omega, \Sigma)$ are called **singular** if there exist two disjoint measurable sets $A, B \in \Sigma$ whose [union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_union) is $\Omega$ such that $\mu$ is zero on all measurable subsets of $B$ while $\nu$ is zero on all measurable subsets of $A.$ This is denoted by $\mu \perp \nu.$

:::

A refined form of [Lebesgue's decomposition theorem](wikipedia://en/4316238) decomposes a singular measure into a singular continuous measure and a [discrete measure](wikipedia://en/8912350).

## Examples on **R**<sup>*n*</sup> {#Examples_on_Rn}

As a particular case, a measure defined on the [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$ is called *singular*, if it is singular with respect to the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on this space. For example, the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021) is a singular measure.

::: {#o3 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example.** A [discrete measure](wikipedia://en/8912350).

The [Heaviside step function](wikipedia://en/87299) on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line),

$$H(x) \ \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} \begin{cases} 0, & x < 0; \\ 1, & x \geq 0; \end{cases}$$

has the [Dirac delta distribution](wikipedia://en/37021) $\delta_0$ as its [distributional derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributional_derivative). This is a measure on the real line, a "[point mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_mass)" at $0.$ However, the [Dirac measure](wikipedia://en/1019002) $\delta_0$ is not absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure $\lambda,$ nor is $\lambda$ absolutely continuous with respect to $\delta_0:$ $\lambda(\{0\}) = 0$ but $\delta_0(\{0\}) = 1;$ if $U$ is any non-empty [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) not containing 0, then $\lambda(U) > 0$ but $\delta_0(U) = 0.$

:::

::: {#o4 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example.** A singular continuous measure.

The [Cantor distribution](wikipedia://en/387941) has a [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) that is continuous but not [absolutely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_continuous), and indeed its absolutely continuous part is zero: it is singular continuous.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example.** A singular continuous measure on $\mathbb{R}^2.$

The upper and lower [Fréchet–Hoeffding bounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copula_%28probability_theory%29#Fréchet–Hoeffding_copula_bounds) are singular distributions in two dimensions.

:::
