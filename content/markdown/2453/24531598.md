# Conjugate diameters

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Conjugate diameters"}

*Image: Diametri-coniugati.jpg.* Conjugated diameters of an ellipse

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), two [diameters](wikipedia://en/8007) of a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673) are said to be **conjugate** if each [chord](wikipedia://en/297947) [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) to one diameter is [bisected](wikipedia://en/152547) by the other diameter. For example, two diameters of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) are conjugate [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) they are [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944).

:::

::: {#o3 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Tangent parallelogram"}

## Of ellipse {#Of_ellipse}

*Image: Conjugate Diameters.svg.* Two conjugate diameters of an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277). Each edge of the bounding [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496) is [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) to one of the diameters.

For an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277), two diameters are conjugate if and only if the [tangent line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_line) to the ellipse at an endpoint of one diameter is parallel to the other diameter. Each pair of conjugate diameters of an ellipse has a corresponding **tangent [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496)**, sometimes called a **bounding parallelogram** (skewed compared to a [bounding rectangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounding_rectangle)). In his manuscript [De motu corporum in gyrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_motu_corporum_in_gyrum), and in the '[Principia](wikipedia://en/48781)', [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) cites as a [lemma](wikipedia://en/18634) proved by previous authors that all (bounding) parallelograms for a given ellipse have the same [area](wikipedia://en/1209).

It is possible to [reconstruct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge_constructions) an ellipse from any pair of conjugate diameters, or from any bounding parallelogram. For example, in [proposition](wikipedia://en/81094) 14 of Book VIII of his *Collection*, [Pappus of Alexandria](wikipedia://en/509300) gives a method for constructing the axes of an ellipse from a given pair of conjugate diameters. Another method is using [Rytz's construction](wikipedia://en/40780131), which takes advantage of the [Thales' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales'_theorem) for finding the directions and lengths of the major and minor axes of an ellipse regardless of its [rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_%28geometry%29) or [shearing](wikipedia://en/799405).

In [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202), if we let the vectors of the two conjugate half-diameters be $\vec a, \vec b$, then the ellipse is parameterized by $\vec a \ \cos\theta + \vec b \ \sin\theta$ as $\theta$ varies over $[0, 2\pi]$.

:::

## Of hyperbola {#Of_hyperbola}

*Image: Orthogonality and rotation.svg.* For any φ, the indicated diameters of the circles and hyperbolas are conjugate.

Similar to the elliptic case, diameters of a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) are conjugate when each bisects all chords parallel to the other.[^o5] In this case both the hyperbola and its conjugate are sources for the chords and diameters.

[Apollonius of Perga](wikipedia://en/257242) gave the following construction of conjugate diameters, given the [conjugate hyperbola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_hyperbola): "If Q be any point on a hyperbola and CE be drawn from the centre parallel to the tangent at Q to meet the conjugate hyperbola in E, then (1) the tangent at E will be parallel to CQ and (2) CQ and CE will be conjugate diameters."[^o6]

In [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202), if $\vec a,\ \vec b$ are vectors of the two conjugate half-diameters, then the hyperbola is parameterized by $\vec a \ \cosh \theta + \vec b \ \sinh \theta$ as $\theta$ varies over $\mathbb{R}$.

In the case of a rectangular hyperbola, its conjugate is the [reflection](wikipedia://en/295829) across an [asymptote](wikipedia://en/3107). A diameter of one hyperbola is conjugate to its reflection in the asymptote, which is a diameter of the other hyperbola. As perpendicularity is the relation of conjugate diameters of a circle, so [hyperbolic orthogonality](wikipedia://en/958449) is the relation of conjugate diameters of rectangular hyperbolas.

The placement of [tie rods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tie_rod) reinforcing a square assembly of [girders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girder) is guided by the relation of conjugate diameters in a book on [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202).[^o7]

Conjugate diameters of hyperbolas are also useful for stating the [principle of relativity](wikipedia://en/74327) in the modern physics of [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758). The concept of relativity is first introduced in a plane consisting of a single dimension in [space](wikipedia://en/27667), the second dimension being [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time). In such a plane, [one hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122) corresponds to events a constant space-like interval from the origin event, the other hyperbola corresponds to events a constant time-like interval from it. The principle of relativity can be formulated "Any pair of conjugate diameters of conjugate hyperbolas can be taken for the axes of space and time". This interpretation of relativity was enunciated by [E. T. Whittaker](wikipedia://en/1077330) in 1910.[^o8]

## In projective geometry {#In_projective_geometry}

Every line in [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) contains a [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139), also called a *figurative point*. The ellipse, parabola, and hyperbola are viewed as *conics* in projective geometry, and each conic determines a relation of [pole and polar](wikipedia://en/19451201) between points and lines. Using these concepts, "two diameters are conjugate when each is the polar of the figurative point of the other."[^o10]

Only one of the conjugate diameters of a hyperbola cuts the curve.

The notion of [point-pair separation](wikipedia://en/68571139) distinguishes an ellipse from a hyperbola: In the ellipse every pair of conjugate diameters separates every other pair. In a hyperbola, one pair of conjugate diameters never separates another such pair.
