# Unit hyperbola

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unit hyperbola"}

*Image: Conjugate-unit-hyperbolas.svg.* The unit hyperbola is blue, its conjugate is green, and the asymptotes are red.

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **unit hyperbola** is the set of points $(x, y)$ in the [Cartesian plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_plane) that satisfy the [implicit equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicit_equation) $x^2 - y^2 = 1$. In the study of [indefinite orthogonal groups](wikipedia://en/293609), the unit hyperbola forms the basis for an *alternative radial length* $\textstyle r = \sqrt {x^2 - y^2}$.

:::

Whereas the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) surrounds its center, the unit hyperbola requires the [conjugate hyperbola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_hyperbola) $y^2 - x^2 = 1$ to complement it in the plane. This pair of [hyperbolas](wikipedia://en/14052) share the [asymptotes](wikipedia://en/3107) *y* = *x* and *y* = −*x*. When the conjugate of the unit hyperbola is in use, the alternative radial length is $\textstyle r = \sqrt{y^2 - x^2} .$

The unit hyperbola is a special case of the [rectangular hyperbola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_hyperbola), with a particular [orientation](wikipedia://en/2544098), [location](wikipedia://en/204682), and [scale](wikipedia://en/651752). As such, its [eccentricity](wikipedia://en/1239472) equals $\textstyle \sqrt{2}.$[^o2]

The unit hyperbola finds applications where the circle must be replaced with the hyperbola for purposes of analytic geometry. A prominent instance is the depiction of [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) as a [pseudo-Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/10962546). There the asymptotes of the unit hyperbola form a [light cone](wikipedia://en/310474). Further, the attention to areas of [hyperbolic sectors](wikipedia://en/1139926) by [Gregoire de Saint-Vincent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregoire_de_Saint-Vincent) led to the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) function and the modern parametrization of the hyperbola by sector areas. When the notions of conjugate hyperbolas and hyperbolic angles are understood, then the classical [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), which are built around the unit circle, can be replaced with numbers built around the unit hyperbola.

## Asymptotes {#Asymptotes}

Generally asymptotic lines to a curve are said to converge toward the curve. In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) and the theory of [algebraic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_curves) there is a different approach to asymptotes. The curve is first interpreted in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) using [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316). Then the asymptotes are lines that are tangent to the projective curve at a [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139), thus circumventing any need for a distance concept and convergence. In a common framework (*x, y, z*) are homogeneous coordinates with the [line at infinity](wikipedia://en/403142) determined by the equation *z* = 0. For instance, C. G. Gibson wrote:[^o4]

For the standard rectangular hyperbola $f = x^2 - y^2 -1$ in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$, the corresponding projective curve is $F = x^2 - y^2 - z^2,$ which meets *z* = 0 at the points *P* = (1 : 1 : 0) and *Q* = (1 : −1 : 0). Both *P* and *Q* are [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_%28complex_analysis%29#Multiplicity_of_a_zero) on *F*, with tangents *x* + *y* = 0, *x* − *y* = 0; thus we recover the familiar 'asymptotes' of elementary geometry.

## Minkowski diagram {#Minkowski_diagram}

The [Minkowski diagram](wikipedia://en/11647860), or spacetime diagram, is drawn in a spacetime plane where the spatial aspect has been restricted to a single dimension. Spacetime diagrams show the geometry underlying phenomena like time dilation. The units of distance and time on such a plane are:

- units of 30 centimetres length and [nanoseconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanosecond), or
- [astronomical units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_unit) and intervals of 8 minutes and 20 seconds, or
- [light years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_year) and [years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year).

Each of these scales of coordinates results in [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) connections of events along diagonal lines of [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) plus or minus one. Five elements constitute the diagram [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537) used to describe the relativity transformations: the unit hyperbola, its [conjugate hyperbola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_hyperbola), the axes of the hyperbola, a diameter of the unit hyperbola, and the [conjugate diameter](wikipedia://en/24531598). The plane with the axes refers to a resting [frame of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference). The diameter of the unit hyperbola represents a frame of reference in motion with [rapidity](wikipedia://en/4520673) *a* where [tanh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanh) *a* = *y*/*x* and (*x*,*y*) is the endpoint of the diameter on the unit hyperbola. The conjugate diameter represents the *spatial hyperplane of simultaneity* corresponding to rapidity *a*. In this context the unit hyperbola is a *calibration hyperbola*[^o6][^o7] Commonly in relativity study the hyperbola with vertical axis is taken as primary:

The arrow of time goes from the bottom to top of the figure — a convention adopted by [Richard Feynman](wikipedia://en/25523) in his famous diagrams. Space is represented by planes perpendicular to the time axis. The here and now is a singularity in the middle.[^o8]

The vertical time axis convention stems from Minkowski in 1908, and is also illustrated on page 48 of Eddington's *The Nature of the Physical World* (1928).

## Parametrization {#Parametrization}

*Image: Hyperbolic functions-2.svg.* The branches of the unit hyperbola evolve as the points $(\cosh a, \sinh a)$ and $(-\cosh a, -\sinh a)$ depending on the hyperbolic angle parameter $a$.

A direct way to parameterizing the unit hyperbola starts with the hyperbola *xy* = 1 parameterized with the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678): $( e^t, e^{-t}).$

This hyperbola is transformed into the unit hyperbola by a [linear mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_mapping) having the matrix $A = \tfrac {1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\ :$

$$(e^t, e^{-t}) \ A = \left(\frac{e^t + e^{-t}}{2}, \frac{e^t - e^{-t}}{2}\right) = (\cosh t, \sinh t).$$

This parameter *t* is the *hyperbolic angle*, which is the [argument](wikipedia://en/32842052) of the [hyperbolic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function).

One finds an early expression of the parametrized unit hyperbola in [Elements of Dynamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elements_of_Dynamic) (1878) by [W. K. Clifford](wikipedia://en/250820). He describes quasi-harmonic motion in a hyperbola as follows:

The motion $\rho = \alpha \cosh(nt + \epsilon) + \beta \sinh(nt + \epsilon)$ has some curious analogies to elliptic harmonic motion. ... The acceleration $\ddot{\rho} = n^2 \rho \ ;$ thus it is always proportional to the distance from the centre, as in elliptic harmonic motion, but directed *away* from the centre.[^o11]

As a particular [conic](wikipedia://en/19008673), the hyperbola can be parametrized by the process of addition of points on a conic. The following description was given by Russian analysts:

Fix a point *E* on the conic. Consider the points at which the straight line drawn through *E* parallel to *AB* intersects the conic a second time to be the *sum of the points A and B*.

For the hyperbola $x^2 - y^2 = 1$ with the fixed point *E* = (1,0) the sum of the points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2,\ y_2)$ is the point $(x_1 x_2 + y_1 y_2, y_ 1 x_2 + y_2 x_1 )$ under the parametrization $x = \cosh \ t$ and $y = \sinh \ t$ this addition corresponds to the addition of the parameter *t*.[^o12]

## Complex plane algebra {#Complex_plane_algebra}

Whereas the unit circle is associated with [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), the unit hyperbola is key to the *split-complex number plane* consisting of *z* = *x* + *yj*, where *j* <sup>2</sup> = +1. Then *jz = y + xj*, so the action of *j* on the plane is to swap the coordinates. In particular, this action swaps the unit hyperbola with its conjugate and swaps pairs of [conjugate diameters](wikipedia://en/24531598) of the hyperbolas.

In terms of the [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) parameter *a*, the unit hyperbola consists of points

$\pm(\cosh a + j \sinh a)$, where *j* = (0,1).

The right branch of the unit hyperbola corresponds to the positive coefficient. In fact, this branch is the image of the [exponential map](wikipedia://en/44612290) acting on the *j*-axis. Thus this branch is the curve $f(a) = \exp(aj).$ The [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) of the curve at *a* is given by the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921)

$f^\prime(a) = \sinh a + j \cosh a = j f(a).$ For any *a*, $f^\prime(a$) is [hyperbolic-orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic-orthogonal) to $f(a)$. This relation is analogous to the perpendicularity of exp(*a* i) and i exp(*a* i) when i<sup>2</sup> = − 1.

Since $\exp(aj) \exp(bj) = \exp((a+b)j)$, the branch is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) under multiplication.

Unlike the [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177), this unit hyperbola group is *not* [compact](wikipedia://en/6042). Similar to the ordinary complex plane, a point not on the diagonals has a [polar decomposition](wikipedia://en/1369241#Alternative_planar_decompositions) using the parametrization of the unit hyperbola and the alternative radial length.
