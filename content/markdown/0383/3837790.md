# Trilinear coordinates

*Image: Trilinear coordinates.svg.* In $\triangle ABC$, $a'$, $b'$, $c'$ are the actual distances from an interior point to the sidelines $BC$, $CA$, $AB$ respectively.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Trilinear coordinates"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **trilinear coordinates** $x : y : z$ of a point relative to a given [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) describe the relative [directed distances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_distance) from the three [sidelines](wikipedia://en/43958459) of the triangle. Trilinear coordinates are an example of [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316). The ratio $x : y$ is the ratio of the [perpendicular distances](wikipedia://en/13556959) from the point to the sides (extended if necessary) opposite [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) $A$ and $B$ respectively; the ratio $y : z$ is the ratio of the perpendicular distances from the point to the sidelines opposite vertices $B$ and $C$ respectively; and likewise for $z : x$ and vertices $C$ and $A$.

:::

In the diagram at right, the trilinear coordinates of the indicated interior point are the actual distances ($a'$, $b'$, $c'$), or equivalently in ratio form, $ka' : kb' : kc'$ for any positive constant $k$. If a point is on a sideline of the reference triangle, its corresponding trilinear coordinate is 0. If an exterior point is on the opposite side of a sideline from the interior of the triangle, its trilinear coordinate associated with that sideline is negative. It is impossible for all three trilinear coordinates to be non-positive.

::: {#o3 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

## Notation {#Notation}

The ratio notation $x : y : z$ for trilinear coordinates is often used in preference to the ordered [triple](wikipedia://en/132729) notation $(x, y, z),$ with the latter reserved for triples of directed distances $(a', b', c')$ relative to a specific triangle. The trilinear coordinates $x : y : z,$ can be rescaled by any arbitrary value without affecting their ratio. The bracketed, comma-separated triple notation $(x, y, z)$ can cause confusion because conventionally this represents a different triple than e.g. $(2x, 2y, 2z),$ but these equivalent ratios $x : y : z = {}\!$$2x : 2y : 2z$ represent the same point.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

The trilinear coordinates of the [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) of a triangle $\triangle ABC$ are $1 : 1 : 1$; that is, the (directed) distances from the incenter to the sidelines $BC, CA, AB$ are proportional to the actual distances denoted by $(r, r, r)$, where $r$ is the inradius of $\triangle ABC$. Given side lengths $a, b, c$ we have:

::: {#o6 .table cue=table}

| Name; Symbol |  | Trilinear coordinates | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vertices | $A$ | $1 : 0 : 0$ | Points at the corners of the triangle |
|  | $B$ | $0 : 1 : 0$ |  |
|  | $C$ | $0 : 0 : 1$ |  |
| [Incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) | $I$ | $1 : 1 : 1$ | Intersection of the internal [angle bisectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_bisectors); Center of the triangle's [inscribed circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle) |
| [Excenters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excenter) | $I_{A}$ | $-1 : 1 : 1$ | Intersections of the angle bisectors (two external, one internal); Centers of the triangle's three [escribed circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excircle) |
|  | $I_{B}$ | $1 : -1 : 1$ |  |
|  | $I_{C}$ | $1 : 1 : -1$ |  |
| [Centroid](wikipedia://en/187926#Of_triangle_and_tetrahedron) | $G$ | $\frac1a : \frac1b : \frac1c$ | Intersection of the [medians](wikipedia://en/1072006); [Center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961) of a uniform triangular [lamina](wikipedia://en/3121604) |
| [Circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) | $O$ | $\cos A : \cos B : \cos C$ | Intersection of the [perpendicular bisectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular_bisector) of the sides; Center of the triangle's [circumscribed circle](wikipedia://en/1926432) |
| [Orthocenter](wikipedia://en/161244) | $H$ | $\sec A : \sec B : \sec C$ | Intersection of the [altitudes](wikipedia://en/161241) |
| [Nine-point center](wikipedia://en/4517570) | $N$ | $\begin{aligned}&\cos(B-C) : \cos(C-A) \\ &\qquad : \cos(A-B)\end{aligned}$ | Center of the circle passing through the midpoint of each side, the foot of each altitude, and the midpoint between the orthocenter and each vertex |
| [Symmedian point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmedian_point) | $K$ | $a : b : c$ | Intersection of the symmedians – the reflection of each median about the corresponding angle bisector |

:::

Note that, in general, the incenter is not the same as the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926); the centroid has [barycentric coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycentric_coordinates_%28mathematics%29) $1 : 1 : 1$ (these being proportional to actual signed areas of the triangles $\triangle BGC$, $\triangle CGA$, $\triangle AGB$, where $G$ = centroid.)

The midpoint of, for example, side $BC$ has trilinear coordinates in actual sideline distances $(0 , \tfrac{\Delta}{b} , \tfrac{\Delta}{c})$ for triangle area $\Delta$, which in arbitrarily specified relative distances simplifies to $0 : ca : ab$. The coordinates in actual sideline distances of the foot of the altitude from $A$ to $BC$ are $(0, \tfrac{2\Delta}{a}\cos C, \tfrac{2\Delta}{a}\cos B),$ which in purely relative distances simplifies to $0 : \cos C : \cos B$.[^o17]

:::

## Formulas {#Formulas}

### Collinearities and concurrencies {#Collinearities_and_concurrencies}

Trilinear coordinates enable many algebraic methods in triangle geometry. For example, three points

$$\begin{aligned} P &= p:q:r \\ U &= u:v:w \\ X &= x:y:z \\ \end{aligned}$$

are [collinear](wikipedia://en/3189581) if and only if the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468)

$$D = \begin{vmatrix} p & q & r \\ u & v & w \\ x & y & z \end{vmatrix}$$

equals zero. Thus if $x : y : z$ is a variable point, the equation of a line through the points $P$ and $U$ is $D = 0$.[^o17] From this, every straight line has a linear equation homogeneous in $x, y, z$. Every equation of the form $lx+my+nz=0$ in real coefficients is a real straight line of finite points unless $l : m : n$ is proportional to $a : b : c$, the side lengths, in which case we have the locus of points at infinity.[^o17]

The dual of this proposition is that the lines

$$\begin{aligned} p\alpha + q\beta + r\gamma &= 0 \\ u\alpha + v\beta + w\gamma &= 0 \\ x\alpha + y\beta + z\gamma &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

[concur](wikipedia://en/2206157) in a point $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)$ if and only if $D = 0$.[^o17]

Also, if the actual directed distances are used when evaluating the determinant of $D$, then the area of triangle $\triangle PUX$ is $KD$, where $K = \tfrac{-abc}{8\Delta^2}$ (and where $\Delta$ is the area of triangle $\triangle ABC$, as above) if triangle $\triangle PUX$ has the same orientation (clockwise or counterclockwise) as $\triangle ABC$, and $K = \tfrac{-abc}{8\Delta^2}$ otherwise.

### Parallel lines {#Parallel_lines}

Two lines with trilinear equations $lx+my+nz=0$ and $l'x+m'y+n'z=0$ are parallel if and only if[^o17]

$$\begin{vmatrix} l & m & n \\ l' & m' & n' \\ a & b & c \end{vmatrix}=0,$$

where $a, b, c$ are the side lengths.

### Angle between two lines {#Angle_between_two_lines}

The [tangents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_%28trigonometry%29) of the angles between two lines with trilinear equations $lx+my+nz=0$ and $l'x+m'y+n'z=0$ are given by[^o17]

$$\pm \frac{(mn'-m'n)\sin A + (nl'-n'l)\sin B + (lm'-l'm)\sin C}{ll' + mm' + nn' - (mn'+m'n)\cos A -(nl'+n'l)\cos B - (lm'+l'm)\cos C}.$$

Thus they are perpendicular if[^o17]

$$ll'+mm'+nn'-(mn'+m'n)\cos A-(nl'+n'l)\cos B-(lm'+l'm)\cos C=0.$$

### Altitude {#Altitude}

The equation of the [altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude_%28geometry%29) from vertex $A$ to side $BC$ is[^o17]

$$y\cos B-z\cos C=0.$$

### Line in terms of distances from vertices {#Line_in_terms_of_distances_from_vertices}

The equation of a line with variable distances $p, q, r$ from the vertices $A, B, C$ whose opposite sides are $a, b, c$ is[^o17]

$$apx+bqy+crz=0.$$

### Actual-distance trilinear coordinates {#Actual-distance_trilinear_coordinates}

The trilinears with the coordinate values $a', b', c'$ being the actual perpendicular distances to the sides satisfy[^o17]

$$aa' +bb' + cc' =2\Delta$$

for triangle sides $a, b, c$ and area $\Delta$. This can be seen in the figure at the top of this article, with interior point $P$ partitioning triangle $\triangle ABC$ into three triangles $\triangle PBC$, $\triangle PCA$, $\triangle PAB$ with respective areas $\tfrac{1}{2}aa' , \tfrac{1}{2}bb', \tfrac{1}{2}cc'.$

### Distance between two points {#Distance_between_two_points}

The distance $d$ between two points with actual-distance trilinears $a_{i} : b_{i} : c_{i}$ is given by[^o17]

$$d^2\sin ^2 C=(a_1-a_2)^2+(b_1-b_2)^2+2(a_1-a_2)(b_1-b_2)\cos C$$

or in a more symmetric way

$$d^2 = \frac{a b c}{4\Delta^2}\left(a(b_1-b_2)(c_2-c_1)+b(c_1-c_2)(a_2-a_1)+c(a_1-a_2)(b_2-b_1)\right).$$

### Distance from a point to a line {#Distance_from_a_point_to_a_line}

The distance $d$ from a point $a' : b' : c'$, in trilinear coordinates of actual distances, to a straight line $lx+my+nz=0$ is[^o17]

$$d=\frac{la'+mb'+nc'}{\sqrt{l^2+m^2+n^2-2mn\cos A -2nl\cos B -2lm\cos C}}.$$

### Quadratic curves {#Quadratic_curves}

The equation of a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673) in the variable trilinear point $x : y : z$ is[^o17]

$$rx^2+sy^2+tz^2+2uyz+2vzx+2wxy=0.$$

It has no linear terms and no [constant term](wikipedia://en/79861).

The equation of a circle of radius $r$ having center at actual-distance coordinates $(a', b', c' )$ is[^o17]

$$(x-a')^2\sin 2A+(y-b')^2\sin 2B+(z-c')^2\sin 2C=2r^2\sin A\sin B\sin C.$$

#### Circumconics {#Circumconics}

The equation in trilinear coordinates $x, y, z$ of any [circumconic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumconic) of a triangle is[^o17]

$$lyz+mzx+nxy=0.$$

If the parameters $l, m, n$ respectively equal the side lengths $a, b, c$ (or the sines of the angles opposite them) then the equation gives the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432).[^o17]

Each distinct circumconic has a center unique to itself. The equation in trilinear coordinates of the circumconic with center $x' : y' : z'$ is[^o17]

$$yz(x'-y'-z')+zx(y'-z'-x')+xy(z'-x'-y')=0.$$

#### Inconics {#Inconics}

Every conic section [inscribed](wikipedia://en/4470203) in a triangle has an equation in trilinear coordinates:[^o17]

$$l^2x^2+m^2y^2+n^2z^2 \pm 2mnyz \pm 2nlzx\pm 2lmxy =0,$$

with exactly one or three of the unspecified signs being negative.

The equation of the [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle) can be simplified to[^o17]

$$\pm \sqrt{x}\cos \frac{A}{2}\pm \sqrt{y}\cos \frac{B}{2}\pm\sqrt{z}\cos \frac{C}{2}=0,$$

while the equation for, for example, the [excircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excircle) adjacent to the side segment opposite vertex $A$ can be written as[^o17]

$$\pm \sqrt{-x}\cos \frac{A}{2}\pm \sqrt{y}\cos \frac{B}{2}\pm\sqrt{z}\cos \frac{C}{2}=0.$$

### Cubic curves {#Cubic_curves}

Many cubic curves are easily represented using trilinear coordinates. For example, the pivotal self-isoconjugate cubic $Z(U, P)$, as the locus of a point $X$ such that the $P$-isoconjugate of $X$ is on the line $UX$ is given by the determinant equation

$$\begin{vmatrix}x&y&z\\ qryz&rpzx&pqxy\\ u&v&w\end{vmatrix} = 0.$$

Among named cubics $Z(U, P)$ are the following:

- [Thomson cubic](wikipedia://en/12267919): $Z(X(2),X(1))$, where $X(2)$ is *[centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) and $X(1)$ is*[incenter](wikipedia://en/316837)
- [Feuerbach cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_plane_curve#Napoleon–Feuerbach_cubic): $Z(X(5),X(1))$, where $X(5)$ is *[Feuerbach point](wikipedia://en/547762)*
- [Darboux cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_plane_curve#Darboux_cubic): $Z(X(20),X(1))$, where $X(20)$ is *[De Longchamps point](wikipedia://en/14593776)*
- [Neuberg cubic](wikipedia://en/69415319): $Z(X(30),X(1))$, where $X(30)$ is *[Euler infinity point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_infinity_point)*.

:::

## Conversions {#Conversions}

### Between trilinear coordinates and distances from sidelines {#Between_trilinear_coordinates_and_distances_from_sidelines}

For any choice of trilinear coordinates $x : y : z$ to locate a point, the actual distances of the point from the sidelines are given by $a' = kx$, $b' = ky$, $c' = kz$ where $k$ can be determined by the formula $k = \tfrac{2\Delta}{ax + by + cz}$ in which $a, b, c$ are the respective sidelengths $BC, CA, AB$, and $\text{∆}$ is the area of $\triangle ABC$.

### Between barycentric and trilinear coordinates {#Between_barycentric_and_trilinear_coordinates}

A point with trilinear coordinates $x : y : z$ has [barycentric coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycentric_coordinates_%28mathematics%29) $ax : by : cz$ where $a, b, c$ are the sidelengths of the triangle. Conversely, a point with barycentrics $\alpha : \beta : \gamma$ has trilinear coordinates $\tfrac{\alpha}{a} : \tfrac{\beta}{b} : \tfrac{\gamma}{c}.$

### Between Cartesian and trilinear coordinates {#Between_Cartesian_and_trilinear_coordinates}

Given a reference triangle $\triangle ABC$, express the position of the vertex $B$ in terms of an ordered pair of [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) and represent this algebraically as a [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29) $\vec B,$ using vertex $C$ as the origin. Similarly define the position vector of vertex $A$ as $\vec A.$ Then any point $P$ associated with the reference triangle $\triangle ABC$ can be defined in a Cartesian system as a vector $\vec P = k_1\vec A + k_2\vec B.$ If this point $P$ has trilinear coordinates $x : y : z$ then the conversion formula from the coefficients $k_{1}$ and $k_{2}$ in the Cartesian representation to the trilinear coordinates is, for side lengths $a, b, c$ opposite vertices $A, B, C$,

$$x:y:z = \frac{k_1}{a} : \frac{k_2}{b} : \frac{1 - k_1 - k_2}{c},$$

and the conversion formula from the trilinear coordinates to the coefficients in the Cartesian representation is

$$k_1 = \frac{ax}{ax + by + cz}, \quad k_2 = \frac{by}{ax + by + cz}.$$

More generally, if an arbitrary origin is chosen where the Cartesian coordinates of the vertices are known and represented by the vectors $\vec A, \vec B, \vec C$ and if the point $P$ has trilinear coordinates $x : y : z$, then the Cartesian coordinates of $\vec P$ are the weighted average of the Cartesian coordinates of these vertices using the barycentric coordinates $ax, by, cz$ as the weights. Hence the conversion formula from the trilinear coordinates $x, y, z$ to the vector of Cartesian coordinates $\vec P$ of the point is given by

$$\vec{P}=\frac{ax}{ax+by+cz}\vec{A}+\frac{by}{ax+by+cz}\vec{B}+\frac{cz}{ax+by+cz}\vec{C},$$

where the side lengths are

$$\begin{aligned} & \left|\vec C - \vec B\right| = a, \\ & \left|\vec A - \vec C\right| = b, \\ & \left|\vec B - \vec A\right| = c. \end{aligned}$$
