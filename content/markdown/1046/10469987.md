# Schubert variety

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Schubert variety"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a **Schubert variety** is a certain [subvariety](wikipedia://en/248808) of a [Grassmannian](wikipedia://en/373810), $\mathbf{Gr}_k(V)$ of $k$-dimensional subspaces of a vector space $V$, usually with [singular points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity). Like the Grassmannian, it is a kind of [moduli space](wikipedia://en/361609), whose elements satisfy conditions giving lower bounds to the dimensions of the intersections of its elements $w\subset V$, with the elements of a specified [complete flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_flag). Here $V$ may be a vector space over an arbitrary [field](wikipedia://en/10603), but most commonly this taken to be either the real or the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826).

:::

A typical example is the set $X$ of $2$-dimensional subspaces $w \subset V$ of a 4-dimensional space $V$ that intersect a fixed (reference) 2-dimensional subspace $V_2$ nontrivially.

$$X \ =\ \{w\subset V \mid \dim(w)=2,\, \dim(w \cap V_2)\ge 1\}.$$

Over the [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) field, this can be pictured in usual *xyz*-space as follows. Replacing subspaces with their corresponding projective spaces, and intersecting with an affine coordinate patch of $\mathbb{P}(V)$, we obtain an open subset *X*° ⊂ *X*. This is isomorphic to the set of all lines *L* (not necessarily through the origin) which meet the *x*-axis. Each such line *L* corresponds to a point of *X*°, and continuously moving *L* in space (while keeping contact with the *x*-axis) corresponds to a curve in *X*°. Since there are three degrees of freedom in moving *L* (moving the point on the *x*-axis, rotating, and tilting), *X* is a three-dimensional real [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808). However, when *L* is equal to the *x*-axis, it can be rotated or tilted around any point on the axis, and this excess of possible motions makes *L* a singular point of *X*.

More generally, a Schubert variety in $\mathbf{Gr}_k(V)$ is defined by specifying the minimal dimension of intersection of a $k$-dimensional subspace $w\subset V$ with each of the spaces in a fixed reference complete flag $V_1\subset V_2\subset \cdots \subset V_n=V$, where $\dim V_j=j$. (In the example above, this would mean requiring certain intersections of the line *L* with the *x*-axis and the *xy*-plane.)

In even greater generality, given a [semisimple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_algebraic_group) [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924) $G$ with a [Borel subgroup](wikipedia://en/588356) $B$ and a standard parabolic subgroup $P$, it is known that the [homogeneous space](wikipedia://en/363325) $G/P$, which is an example of a [flag variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_variety), consists of finitely many $B$-orbits, which may be parametrized by certain elements $w\in W$ of the [Weyl group](wikipedia://en/296332) $W$. The closure of the $B$-orbit associated to an element $w\in W$ is denoted $X_{w}$ and is called a Schubert variety in $G/P$. The classical case corresponds to $G=SL_n$, with $P=P_k$, the $k$th maximal parabolic subgroup of $SL_n$, so that $G/P = \mathbf{Gr}_k(\mathbf{C}^n)$ is the Grassmannian of $k$-planes in $\mathbf{C}^n$.

## Significance {#Significance}

Schubert varieties form one of the most important and best studied classes of [singular algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/35033819). A certain measure of singularity of Schubert varieties is provided by [Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials](wikipedia://en/10161645), which encode their local Goresky–MacPherson [intersection cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersection_cohomology).

The algebras of regular functions on Schubert varieties have deep significance in [algebraic combinatorics](wikipedia://en/14835582) and are examples of [algebras with a straightening law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_with_a_straightening_law). (Co)homology of the Grassmannian, and more generally, of more general flag varieties, has a basis consisting of the (co)homology classes of Schubert varieties, or **Schubert cycles**. The study of the intersection theory on the Grassmannian was initiated by [Hermann Schubert](wikipedia://en/7998163) and continued by [Zeuthen](wikipedia://en/1732797) in the 19th century under the heading of [enumerative geometry](wikipedia://en/3069092). This area was deemed by [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) important enough to be included as the [fifteenth](wikipedia://en/2336205) of his celebrated [23 problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_problems). The study continued in the 20th century as part of the general development of [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) and [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200), but accelerated in the 1990s beginning with the work of [William Fulton](wikipedia://en/9642149) on the [degeneracy loci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degeneracy_loci) and [Schubert polynomials](wikipedia://en/31925388), following up on earlier investigations of [Bernstein](wikipedia://en/5815383)–[Gelfand](wikipedia://en/364643)–Gelfand and [Demazure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demazure) in representation theory in the 1970s, [Lascoux](wikipedia://en/32555229) and [Schützenberger](wikipedia://en/2817337) in combinatorics in the 1980s, and Fulton and MacPherson in [intersection theory](wikipedia://en/3148602) of singular algebraic varieties, also in the 1980s.
