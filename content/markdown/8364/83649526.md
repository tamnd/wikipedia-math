# Multiplicative graph

*Image: Category\_SVG.svg.* For comparison, this diagram shows a typical [arrow](wikipedia://en/27716891) composition in an ordinary category. (Without arrow composition, it is simply a directed graph.) The arrows $f$ and $g$ in the diagram are consecutive; they connect in $B = \text{cod} (f) = \text{dom} (g).$ In an ordinary category, the composition of any pair of consecutive arrows exists, whereas in a multiplicative graph, a pair of consecutive arrows is not necessarily composable.[^o1]

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Multiplicative graph"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **multiplicative graph** (in French: **graphe multiplicatif** or **neocategory** in some English-language papers) is an [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) in [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869). It is a generalization of an [ordinary category](wikipedia://en/53759) in the sense that neither the [associativity](wikipedia://en/53759#Definition) of arrow composition nor the [composibility](wikipedia://en/3948) of a pair of connected arrows are assumed. While an ordinary category is a notion combining a [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) and a [monoidal structure](wikipedia://en/19652), a multiplicative graph is a [partial magma-like structure](wikipedia://en/43662522). Namely, it is a structure in one‑to‑one correspondence with the vertices of a directed graph, and each object has [left and right identity morphisms](wikipedia://en/53759#Definition), but composability is partial, and, moreover, associativity is not required.[^o3]

:::

Arrow composition in an ordinary category satisfies the following property: if arrows $f$ and $g$ connect in the sense that $\text{dom} (g) = \text{cod} (f),$ their composition $g \circ f$ is defined, and, moreover, $\text{dom} (g \circ f) = \text{dom} (f)$ and $\text{cod} (g \circ f) = \text{cod} (g).$ In a multiplicative graph, however, condition $\text{dom} (g) = \text{cod} (f)$ does not guarantee the existence of $g \circ f$ within that structure without further assumptions – but *if* this composition exists, it also satisfies $\text{dom} (g \circ f) = \text{dom} (f)$ and $\text{cod} (g \circ f) = \text{cod} (g).$[^o4] When drawing a diagram for a multiplicative graph, it is almost always necessary to explicitly draw all existing arrows that play a role in the argument. For example, as shown in Coppey (1980), square diagrams in a multiplicative graph can take one of five types depending on which potential compositions in the diagram are actually defined.[^o5]

This notion first appears in Ehresmann's book *Catégories et structures*.[^o6] The French school bases its definition of [sketch](wikipedia://en/20494787) on the notion of a multiplicative graph, because this definition required a category-like structure that avoided redundant axioms as much as possible.[^o7] This structure is the multiplicative graph, and this is a type of relaxed notion of category, such as a [semicategory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semicategory).

Cury studied [enriched](wikipedia://en/142622) multiplicative graph.[^o8] As a more general notion, there is the compositional graph, and multiplicative graphs can be seen as [strongly identitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-sided_identity) compositional graphs.[^o9]

::: {#o11 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A multiplicative graph $\Sigma$ is couple formed by a set denoted by $\underline{\Sigma}$, and a [partial law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_operation) of composition $\kappa$ on $\underline{\Sigma}$ satisfying the following axioms:[^o12]

1. $\kappa$ is a mapping from a subset of $\underline{\Sigma} \times \underline{\Sigma}$ (denoted by $\Sigma * \Sigma$ and called the set of composable couples) into $\underline{\Sigma}$; instead of $\kappa(y, x)$, we write $y \circ x$ and we call $y \circ x$ the composite of $(y, x)$.
1. There exists a [reflexive graph](wikipedia://en/355100#Reflexive_quiver)[^o13] $(\underline{\Sigma}, \beta, \alpha)$ (i.e. $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are [retractions](wikipedia://en/10630303) from $\underline{\Sigma}$ onto a subset of $\underline{\Sigma}$, denoted by $\Sigma_0$), such that:

(existence of units[^o14][^o15] existence of composition for [loops](wikipedia://en/1449083)): For each element $x$ of $\underline{\Sigma}$, the composites $x \circ \alpha (x)$ and $\beta (x) \circ x$ are defined, and we have

$$x \circ \alpha(x) = x = \beta (x) \circ x.$$

Here, $\alpha(x)$ is the [right identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_identity) of $x$ and is called the source of $x$, while $\beta (x)$ is the [left identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_identity) of $x$ and is called the target of $x$;

(coherence of dom/cod[^o16]): If the composite $y \circ x$ is defined, then:

$$\alpha(y \circ x) = \alpha (x), \ \beta(y \circ x) = \beta (y), \ \text{and} \ \alpha (y) = \beta (x).$$

From the condition 2, the reflexive graph $(\Sigma, \beta, \alpha)$ is uniquely defined.

:::

## Inverse morphisms are not unique {#Inverse_morphisms_are_not_unique}

Let $C^*$ be a multiplicative graph, if one has a $(f, f') \in C^{*} * C^{*}$ and $f \circ f' = \beta(f)$ (resp. $(f', f) \in C^{*} * C^{*}$ and $f' \circ f = \alpha(f)$), then we say that $f \in C$ admit a right (resp. left) inverse of a morphism $f'$ in $C^*$. If there exists an $f' \in C$, we say that $f$ in $C^*$ is invertible such that $f'$ is the right and left inverse of $f$ in $C^*$, then we called $f'$ an inverse of $f$ in $C^*$. If $C^*$ is a multiplicative graph and if $f \in C$ admit f' for right (resp. left) inverse in $C^*$, one has:

$\alpha(f') = \beta(f)$ and $\beta(f') = \alpha(f)$

While inverse morphisms in a ordinary category are unique, a morphisme of multiplicative graph $C^*$ can have several inverse morphisms.[^o18] The law of composition are shown in the table below:

*Image: Inverse morphisms in a multiplicative graph.svg.*

::: {#o19 .table cue=table}

|  | f | f' | f'' | e | e' |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| f |  | e' | e' | f |  |
| f' | e |  |  |  | f' |
| f'' | e |  |  |  | f'' |
| e |  | f' | f'' | e |  |
| e' | f |  |  |  | e' |

:::

## Example {#Example}

- An ordinary category is a multiplicative graph if it satisfies the following two axioms:[^o22]

(composibility):all the couples $(y, x)$ where $\alpha (y) = \beta (x)$ are composable (so that $\Sigma * \Sigma$ is the [pullback](wikipedia://en/841689) of $(\alpha, \beta)$);

(strong associativity):the law of composition being furthermore associative.

- For the two axioms above, a notion got by adding only the associativity axiom (that is, associativity is not strong) to a multiplicative graph, that is, a notion that an ordinary category without the composibility axiom, this is called a precategory. But, this is not standard terminology, a precategory is usually synonymous with a [semigroupoid](wikipedia://en/528602) and does not require each object to have an identity morphism.

:::
