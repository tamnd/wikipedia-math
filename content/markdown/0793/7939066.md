# M-matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="M-matrix"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), an ***M*-matrix** is a matrix whose off-diagonal entries are less than or equal to zero (i.e., it is a [*Z*-matrix](wikipedia://en/7838302)) and whose [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) have nonnegative [real parts](wikipedia://en/5826#Notation). The set of non-singular *M*-matrices are a subset of the class of [*P*-matrices](wikipedia://en/7632450), and also of the class of [inverse-positive matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse-positive_matrix) (i.e. matrices with inverses belonging to the class of [positive matrices](wikipedia://en/3945884)).[^o2] The name *M*-matrix was seemingly originally chosen by [Alexander Ostrowski](wikipedia://en/5827091) in reference to [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537), who proved that if a Z-matrix has all of its row sums positive, then the determinant of that matrix is positive.[^o3]

:::

## Characterizations {#Characterizations}

An M-matrix is commonly defined as follows:

::: {#o6 .definition cue=inline-head name="Definition"}

**Definition:** Let $A$ be a $n \times n$ real [Z-matrix](wikipedia://en/7838302). That is, $A = (a_{ij})$ where $a_{ij} \le 0$ for all $i \ne j, 1 \le i,j \le n$. Then matrix *A* is also an *M-matrix* if it can be expressed in the form $A = sI - B$, where $B = (b_{ij})$ with $b_{ij} \ge 0$, for all $1 \le i,j \le n$, where $s$ is at least as large as the maximum of the moduli of the eigenvalues of $B$, and $I$ is an identity matrix.

For the [non-singularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-singularity) of $A$, according to the [Perron–Frobenius theorem](wikipedia://en/1540333), it must be the case that $s > \rho(B)$. Also, for a non-singular M-matrix, the diagonal elements $a_{ii}$ of *A* must be positive. Here we will further characterize only the class of non-singular M-matrices.

Many statements that are equivalent to this definition of non-singular M-matrices are known, and any one of these statements can serve as a starting definition of a non-singular M-matrix.[^o7] For example, Plemmons lists 40 such equivalences.[^o8] These characterizations has been categorized by Plemmons in terms of their relations to the properties of: (1) positivity of principal minors, (2) inverse-positivity and splittings, (3) stability, and (4) semipositivity and diagonal dominance. It makes sense to categorize the properties in this way because the statements within a particular group are related to each other even when matrix $A$ is an arbitrary matrix, and not necessarily a Z-matrix. Here we mention a few characterizations from each category.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

Below, $\ge$ denotes the element-wise order (not the usual [positive semidefinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_semidefinite_matrix) order on matrices). That is, for any real matrices *A*, *B* of size $m \times n$, we write $A \ge B (\text{or } A > B)$ if $a_{ij} \ge b_{ij} (\text{or } a_{ij} > b_{ij})$ for all $i, j$.

Let *A* be a $n \times n$ real [Z-matrix](wikipedia://en/7838302), then the following statements are equivalent to *A* being a [non-singular](wikipedia://en/253260#Singularities) M-matrix:

*Positivity of principal minors*

- All the [principal minors](wikipedia://en/262107) of *A* are positive. That is, the determinant of each submatrix of *A* obtained by deleting a set, possibly empty, of corresponding rows and columns of *A* is positive.
- $A + D$ is non-singular for each nonnegative diagonal matrix *D*.
- Every real eigenvalue of *A* is positive.
- All the leading principal minors of *A* are positive.
- There exist lower and [upper triangular](wikipedia://en/374222) matrices *L* and *U* respectively, with positive diagonals, such that $A = LU$.

*Inverse-positivity and splittings*

- *A* is *inverse-positive*. That is, $A^{-1}$ exists and $A^{-1} \ge 0$.
- *A* is *monotone*. That is, $Ax \ge 0$ implies $x \ge 0$.
- *A* has a *convergent regular splitting*. That is, *A* has a representation $A = M - N$, where $M^{-1} \ge 0, N \ge 0$ with $M^{-1}N$ *convergent*. That is, $\rho(M^{-1}N) < 1$.
- There exist inverse-positive matrices $M_{1}$ and $M_{2}$ with $M_{1} \le A \le M_{2}$.
- Every regular splitting of *A* is convergent.

*Stability*

- There exists a positive diagonal matrix *D* such that $AD + DA^{T}$ is positive definite.
- *A* is *positive stable*. That is, the real part of each eigenvalue of *A* is positive.
- There exists a symmetric [positive definite matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_definite_matrix) *W* such that $AW + WA^{T}$ is positive definite.
- $A + I$ is non-singular, and $G = (A + I)^{-1}(A - I)$ is convergent.
- $A + I$ is non-singular, and for $G = (A + I)^{-1}(A - I)$, there exists a positive definite symmetric matrix *W* such that $W - G^{T}WG$ is positive definite.

*Semipositivity and diagonal dominance*

- *A* is *semi-positive*. That is, there exists $x > 0$ with $Ax > 0$.
- There exists $x \ge 0$ with $Ax > 0$.
- There exists a positive diagonal matrix *D* such that $AD$ has all positive row sums.
- *A* has all positive diagonal elements, and there exists a positive diagonal matrix *D* such that $AD$ is *strictly [diagonally dominant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagonally_dominant)*.
- *A* has all positive diagonal elements, and there exists a positive diagonal matrix *D* such that $D^{-1}AD$ is strictly diagonally dominant.

## Applications {#Applications}

The primary contributions to M-matrix theory has mainly come from mathematicians and economists. M-matrices are used in mathematics to establish bounds on eigenvalues and on the establishment of convergence criteria for [iterative methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iterative_methods) for the solution of large [sparse](wikipedia://en/341015) [systems of linear equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_of_linear_equations). M-matrices arise naturally in some discretizations of [differential operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_operators), such as the [Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian), and as such are well-studied in scientific computing. M-matrices also occur in the study of solutions to [linear complementarity problem](wikipedia://en/1470767). Linear complementarity problems arise in [linear](wikipedia://en/43730) and [quadratic programming](wikipedia://en/40324), [computational mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_mechanics), and in the problem of finding [equilibrium point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_point) of a [bimatrix game](wikipedia://en/48843663). Lastly, M-matrices occur in the study of finite [Markov chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chains) in the field of [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [operations research](wikipedia://en/43476) like [queuing theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queuing_theory). Meanwhile, economists have studied M-matrices in connection with gross substitutability, stability of a [general equilibrium](wikipedia://en/45938) and [Leontief's input–output analysis](wikipedia://en/1036651) in economic systems. The condition of positivity of all principal minors is also known as the Hawkins–Simon condition in economic literature.[^o13] In engineering, M-matrices also occur in the problems of [Lyapunov stability](wikipedia://en/363360) and [feedback control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feedback_control) in [control theory](wikipedia://en/7039) and are related to [Hurwitz matrices](wikipedia://en/78291990). In [computational biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_biology), M-matrices occur in the study of [population dynamics](wikipedia://en/501466).
