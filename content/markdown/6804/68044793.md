# Cobham's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cobham's theorem"}

**Cobham's theorem** is a theorem in [combinatorics on words](wikipedia://en/20913490) that has important connections with [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), notably [transcendental numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_numbers), and [automata theory](wikipedia://en/103356). Informally, the theorem gives the condition for the members of a set *S* of natural numbers written in [bases](wikipedia://en/701207) *b<sub>1</sub>* and base *b<sub>2</sub>* to be recognised by [finite automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_automaton). Specifically, consider bases *b<sub>1</sub>* and *b<sub>2</sub>* such that they are not powers of the same [integer](wikipedia://en/14563). Cobham's theorem states that *S* written in bases *b<sub>1</sub>* and *b<sub>2</sub>* is recognised by finite automata if and only if *S* differs by a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) from a finite union of [arithmetic progressions](wikipedia://en/168389). The theorem was proved by [Alan Cobham](wikipedia://en/68162815) in 1969[^o2] and has since given rise to many extensions and generalisations.[^o3][^o4]

:::

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Let $n>0$ be an integer. The representation of a natural number $n$ in [base](wikipedia://en/701207) $b$ is the sequence of digits $n_0n_1\cdots n_h$ such that

$$n=n_0+n_1b+\cdots+n_hb^h$$

where $0\le n_0,n_1,\ldots,n_h < b$ and $n_h>0$. The word $n_0n_1\cdots n_h$ is often denoted $\langle n\rangle_b$, or more simply, $n_b$.

A set of natural numbers *S* is *recognisable in base* $b$ or more simply [*$b$-recognisable*](wikipedia://en/7740302) or *$b$-automatic* if the set $\{n_b\mid n\in S\}$ of the representations of its elements in base $b$ is a language recognisable by a [finite automaton](wikipedia://en/653404) on the alphabet $\{0,1,\ldots,b-1\}$.

Two positive integers $k$ and $\ell$ are [multiplicatively independent](wikipedia://en/50027042) if there are no non-negative integers $p$ and $q$ such that $k^p=\ell^q$. For example, 2 and 3 are multiplicatively independent, but 8 and 16 are not since $8^4=16^3$. Two integers are multiplicatively dependent [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) they are powers of a same third integer.

:::

## Problem statements {#Problem_statements}

### Original problem statement {#Original_problem_statement}

More equivalent statements of the theorem have been given. The original version by Cobham is the following:[^o2]

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem (Cobham 1969)"}

**Theorem (Cobham 1969).**

Let $S$ be a set of non-negative integers and let $m$ and $n$ be multiplicatively independent positive integers. Then $S$ is recognizable by finite automata in both $m$-ary and $n$-ary notation if and only if it is ultimately periodic.

:::

Another way to state the theorem is by using [automatic sequences](wikipedia://en/7740302). Cobham himself calls them "uniform tag sequences."[^o10] The following form is found in Allouche and Shallit's book:[^o11]

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

Let $k$ and $\ell$ be two multiplicatively independent integers. A sequence is both $k$-automatic and $\ell$-automatic only if it is $1$-automatic[^o13]

:::

We can show that the characteristic sequence of a set of natural numbers *S* recognisable by finite automata in base *k* is a *k*-automatic sequence and that conversely, for all *k*-automatic sequences $u$ and all integers $0\le i <k$, the set $S_i$ of natural numbers $s$ such that $u_s=i$ is recognisable in base $k$.

### Formulation in logic {#Formulation_in_logic}

Cobham's theorem can be formulated in [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) using a theorem proven by Büchi in 1960.[^o15] This formulation in logic allows for extensions and generalisations. The logical expression uses the theory[^o16]

$$\langle N, +, V_r\rangle$$

of natural integers equipped with addition and the function $V_r$ defined by $V_r(0)=1$ and for any positive integer $n$, $V_r(n)=r^m$ if $r^m$ is the largest power of $r$ that divides $n$. For example, $V_2(20)=4$, and $V_3(20)=1$.

A set of integers $S$ is *definable in first-order logic in* $\langle N, +, V_r\rangle$ if it can be described by a first-order formula with equality, addition, and $V_r$.

Examples:

- The set of odd numbers is definable (without $V_r$) by the formula $(\exists y)(x=y+y+1)$
- The set $\{2^n\mid n\ge0\}$ of the powers of 2 is definable by the simple formula $V_2(x)=x$.

::: {#o17 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Cobham's theorem reformulated"}

**Cobham's theorem reformulated.**

Let *S* be a set of natural numbers, and let $k$ and $\ell$ be two multiplicatively independent positive integers. Then *S* is first-order definable in $\langle N, +, V_k\rangle$ and in $\langle N, +, V_\ell\rangle$ if and only if *S* is ultimately periodic.

:::

We can push the analogy with logic further by noting that *S* is first-order definable in [Presburger arithmetic](wikipedia://en/23756) if and only if it is ultimately periodic. So, a set *S* is definable in the logics $\langle N, +, V_k\rangle$ and $\langle N, +, V_\ell\rangle$ if and only if it is definable in [Presburger arithmetic](wikipedia://en/23756).

## Generalisations {#Generalisations}

### Approach by morphisms {#Approach_by_morphisms}

An automatic sequence is a particular [morphic word](wikipedia://en/37432570), whose [morphism](wikipedia://en/37432570) is [uniform](wikipedia://en/523166#Morphisms), meaning that the length of the images generated by the morphism for each letter of its input alphabet is the same. A set of integers is hence *k*-recognisable if and only if its characteristic sequence is generated by a uniform morphism followed by a [coding](wikipedia://en/37432570), where a coding is a morphism that maps each letter of the input alphabet to a letter of the output alphabet. For example, the [characteristic sequence](wikipedia://en/240790) of the powers of 2 is produced by the 2-uniform morphism (meaning each letter is mapped to a word of length 2) over the alphabet $B=\{a,0,1\}$ defined by

$$a \mapsto a1\ ,\quad 1\mapsto 10\ ,\quad 0\mapsto 00$$

which generates the infinite word

$$a11010001\cdots,$$

followed by the coding (that is, letter to letter) that maps $a$ to $0$ and leaves $0$ and $1$ unchanged, giving

$$011010001\cdots.$$

The notion has been extended as follows:[^o20] a morphic word $s$ is $\alpha$-*substitutive* for a certain number $\alpha$ if when written in the form

$$s=\pi(f^\omega(b))$$

where the morphism $f:B^*\to B^*$, [prolongable](wikipedia://en/37432570) in $b$, has the following properties:

- all letters of $B$ occur in $f^\omega(b)$, and
- $\alpha>1$ is the dominant [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of the [matrix of morphism](wikipedia://en/37432570) $f$, namely, the matrix $M(f)=(m_{x,y})_{x\in B,y\in A}$, where $m_{x,y}$ is the number of occurrences of the letter $x$ in the word $f(y)$.

A set *S* of natural numbers is $\alpha$-*recognisable* if its characteristic sequence $s$ is $\alpha$-substitutive.

A last definition: a *[Perron number](wikipedia://en/5362893)* is an [algebraic number](wikipedia://en/1158) $z > 1$ such that all its conjugates belong to the disc $\{z' \in \mathbb{C},|z'|< z\}$. These are exactly the dominant eigenvalues of the primitive matrices of positive integers.

We then have the following statement:[^o20]

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Cobham's theorem for substitutions"}

**Cobham's theorem for substitutions.**

Let *α* et *β* be two multiplicatively independent Perron numbers. Then a sequence *x* with elements belonging to a finite set is both *α*-substitutive and *β*-substitutive if and only if *x* is ultimately periodic.

:::

### Logic approach {#Logic_approach}

The logic equivalent permits to consider more general situations: the automatic sequences over the natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$ or recognisable sets have been extended to the integers $\mathbb{Z}$, to the Cartesian products $\mathbb{N}^m$, to the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ and to the Cartesian products $\mathbb{R}^m$.[^o16]

**Extension to $\mathbb{Z}$**

We code the base $k$ integers by prepending to the representation of a positive integer the digit $0$, and by representing negative integers by $k-1$ followed by the number's [$k$-complement](wikipedia://en/358754). For example, in base 2, the integer $-6=-8+2$ is represented as $1010$. The powers of 2 are written as $010^*$, and their negatives $110^*$ (since $11000$ is the representation of $-16+8=-8$).

**Extension to $\mathbb{N}^m$**

A subset $X$ of $N^m$ is recognisable in base $k$ if the elements of $X$, written as vectors with $m$ components, are recognisable over the resulting alphabet.

For example, in base 2, we have $3=11_2$ and $9=1001_2$; the vector $\begin{pmatrix}3\\9\end{pmatrix}$ is written as $\begin{pmatrix}0011\\1001\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}$.

::: {#o23 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Semenov's theorem (1977)"}

**Semenov's theorem (1977)[^o24].**

Let $r$ and $s$ be two multiplicatively independent positive integers. A subset $S$ of $N^m$ is $r$-recognisable and $s$-recognisable if and only if $S$ is describable in Presburger arithmetic.

:::

An elegant proof of this theorem is given by Muchnik in 1991 by induction on $m$.[^o25]

Other extensions have been given to the real numbers and vectors of real numbers.[^o16]

::: {#o27 .proof cue=heading name="Proofs"}

## Proofs {#Proofs}

[Samuel Eilenberg](wikipedia://en/362501) announced the theorem without proof in his book;[^o28] he says "The proof is correct, long, and hard. It is a challenge to find a more reasonable proof of this fine theorem." Georges Hansel proposed a more simple proof, published in the not-easily accessible proceedings of a conference.[^o29] The proof of Dominique Perrin[^o30] and that of Allouche and Shallit's book[^o31] contain the same error in one of the lemmas, mentioned in the list of errata of the book.[^o32] This error was uncovered in a note by Tomi Kärki,[^o33] and corrected by Michel Rigo and Laurent Waxweiler.[^o34] This part of the proof has been recently written.[^o35]

In January 2018, Thijmen J. P. Krebs announced, on [Arxiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ArXiv), a simplified proof of the original theorem, based on [Dirichlet's approximation criterion](wikipedia://en/2770230) instead of that of Kronecker; the article appeared in 2021.[^o36] The employed method has been refined and used by Mol, Rampersad, Shallit and Stipulanti.[^o37]

:::
