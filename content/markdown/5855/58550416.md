# Fischer's inequality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fischer's inequality"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Fischer's inequality** gives an upper bound for the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of a [positive-semidefinite matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix) whose entries are complex numbers in terms of the determinants of its principal diagonal blocks. Suppose *A*, *C* are respectively *p*×*p*, *q*×*q* positive-semidefinite complex matrices and *B* is a *p*×*q* complex matrix. Let

:::

$$M := \left[\begin{matrix} A & B \\ B^* & C \end{matrix}\right]$$

so that *M* is a (*p*+*q*)×(*p*+*q*) matrix.

Then Fischer's inequality states that

$$\det (M) \le \det(A) \det(C).$$

If *M* is positive-definite, equality is achieved in Fischer's inequality if and only if all the entries of *B* are 0. Inductively one may conclude that a similar inequality holds for a block decomposition of *M* with multiple principal diagonal blocks. Considering 1×1 blocks, a corollary is [Hadamard's inequality](wikipedia://en/612000). On the other hand, Fischer's inequality can also be proved by using Hadamard's inequality, see the proof of Theorem 7.8.5 in Horn and Johnson's [Matrix Analysis](wikipedia://en/40956141).

::: {#o3 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

Assume that *A* and *C* are positive-definite. We have $A^{-1}$ and $C^{-1}$ are positive-definite. Let

$$D := \left[\begin{matrix} A & 0 \\ 0 & C \end{matrix}\right].$$

We note that

$$D^{-\frac{1}{2}} M D^{-\frac{1}{2} } = \left[\begin{matrix} A^{-\frac{1}{2}} & 0 \\ 0 & C^{-\frac{1}{2}} \end{matrix}\right] \left[\begin{matrix} A & B \\ B^* & C \end{matrix}\right] \left[\begin{matrix} A^{-\frac{1}{2}} & 0 \\ 0 & C^{-\frac{1}{2}} \end{matrix}\right] = \left[\begin{matrix} I_{p} & A^{-\frac{1}{2}} BC^{-\frac{1}{2}} \\ C^{-\frac{1}{2}}B^*A^{-\frac{1}{2}} & I_{q}\end{matrix}\right]$$

Applying the [AM-GM inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inequality_of_arithmetic_and_geometric_means) to the eigenvalues of $D^{-\frac{1}{2}} M D^{-\frac{1}{2} }$, we see

$$\det (D^{-\frac{1}{2}} M D^{-\frac{1}{2}}) \le \left({1 \over p + q} \mathrm{tr} (D^{-\frac{1}{2}} M D^{-\frac{1}{2}}) \right)^{p+q} = 1^{p+q} = 1.$$

By multiplicativity of [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468), we have

$$\begin{aligned} \det(D^{-\frac{1}{2}} ) \det(M) \det(D^{-\frac{1}{2}} ) \le 1 \\ \Longrightarrow \det(M) \le \det(D) = \det(A) \det(C). \end{aligned}$$

In this case, equality holds if and only if *M* = *D* that is, all entries of *B* are 0.

For $\varepsilon > 0$, as $A + \varepsilon I_p$ and $C + \varepsilon I_q$ are positive-definite, we have

$$\det(M+ \varepsilon I_{p+q}) \le \det(A + \varepsilon I_p) \det(C + \varepsilon I_q).$$

Taking the limit as $\varepsilon \rightarrow 0$ proves the inequality. From the inequality we note that if *M* is invertible, then both *A* and *C* are invertible and we get the desired equality condition.

:::

## Improvements {#Improvements}

If *M* can be partitioned in square blocks *M<sub>ij</sub>*, then the following inequality by Thompson is valid:[^o5]

$$\det(M) \leq \det([\det(M_{ij})])$$

where \[det(*M<sub>ij</sub>*)\] is the matrix whose (*i*,*j*) entry is det(*M<sub>ij</sub>*).

In particular, if the block matrices *B* and *C* are also square matrices, then the following inequality by Everett is valid:[^o6]

$$\det(M) \le \det \begin{bmatrix} \det(A) && \det(B) \\ \det(B^*) && \det(C) \end{bmatrix}$$

Thompson's inequality can also be generalized by an inequality in terms of the coefficients of the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) of the block matrices. Expressing the characteristic polynomial of the matrix *A* as

$$p_A (t) = \sum_{k=0}^n t^{n-k} (-1)^k \operatorname{tr}(\Lambda^k A)$$

and supposing that the blocks *M<sub>ij</sub>* are *m* x *m* matrices, the following inequality by Lin and Zhang is valid:[^o7]

$$\det(M) \le \left(\frac{\det([\operatorname{tr}(\Lambda^r M_{ij}]))}{ \binom{m}r} \right)^{\frac{m}{r}},\quad r=1, \ldots, m$$

Note that if *r* = *m*, then this inequality is identical to Thompson's inequality.
