# Minimum-variance unbiased estimator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Minimum-variance unbiased estimator"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) a **minimum-variance unbiased estimator (MVUE)** or **uniformly minimum-variance unbiased estimator (UMVUE)** is an [unbiased estimator](wikipedia://en/8450479) that has lower variance than any other unbiased estimator for all possible values of the parameter.

:::

For practical statistics problems, it is important to determine the MVUE if one exists, since less-than-optimal procedures would naturally be avoided, other things being equal. This has led to substantial development of statistical theory related to the problem of optimal estimation.

While combining the constraint of [unbiasedness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unbiasedness) with the desirability metric of least [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) leads to good results in most practical settings—making MVUE a natural starting point for a broad range of analyses—a targeted specification may perform better for a given problem; thus, MVUE is not always the best stopping point.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Consider estimation of $g(\theta)$ based on data $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ [i.i.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I.i.d.) from some member of a family of densities $p_\theta, \theta \in \Omega$, where $\Omega$ is the parameter space. An estimator $\delta(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n)$ of $g(\theta)$ is unbiased if $\operatorname{E}(\delta(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n))=\operatorname{E}(g(\theta))$. Furthermore, an unbiased estimator is *UMVUE* if $\forall \theta \in \Omega$,

$$\operatorname{var}(\delta(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n)) \leq \operatorname{var}(\tilde{\delta}(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n))$$

for any other unbiased estimator $\tilde{\delta}.$

Using the [Rao–Blackwell theorem](wikipedia://en/400010) one can prove that determining the MVUE is simply a matter of finding a [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_statistic) [sufficient](wikipedia://en/140841) statistic for the family $p_\theta, \theta \in \Omega$ and conditioning *any* unbiased estimator on it.

Further, by the [Lehmann–Scheffé theorem](wikipedia://en/342602), an unbiased estimator that is a function of a complete, sufficient statistic is the UMVUE.

Put formally, suppose $\delta(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n)$ is unbiased for $g(\theta)$, and that $T$ is a complete sufficient statistic for the family of densities. Then

$$\eta(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n) = \operatorname{E}(\delta(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n)\mid T)\,$$

is the MVUE for $g(\theta).$

A [Bayesian](wikipedia://en/404412) analog is a [Bayes estimator](wikipedia://en/6978815), particularly with [minimum mean square error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_mean_square_error) (MMSE).

:::

## Estimator selection {#Estimator_selection}

An [efficient estimator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efficient_estimator) need not exist, but if it does and if it is unbiased, it is the MVUE. Since the [mean squared error](wikipedia://en/201816) (MSE) of an estimator *δ* is

$$\operatorname{MSE}(\delta) = \operatorname{var}(\delta) +[ \operatorname{bias}(\delta)]^2 \$$

the MVUE minimizes MSE *among unbiased estimators*. In some cases biased estimators have lower MSE because they have a smaller variance than does any unbiased estimator; see [estimator bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estimator_bias).

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Consider the data to be a single observation from an [absolutely continuous distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_continuous_random_variable) on $\mathbb{R}$ with density

$$p_\theta(x) = \frac{ \theta e^{-x} }{\left(1 + e^{-x}\right)^{\theta + 1} },$$

where *θ* > 0, and we wish to find the UMVU estimator of

$$g(\theta) = \frac 1 {\theta^2}$$

First we recognize that the density can be written as

$$\frac{ e^{-x} } { 1 + e^{-x} } \exp\left( -\theta \log(1 + e^{-x}) + \log(\theta)\right)$$

which is an exponential family with [sufficient statistic](wikipedia://en/140841) $T = \log(1 + e^{-X})$. In fact this is a full rank exponential family, and therefore $T$ is complete sufficient. See [exponential family](wikipedia://en/339174) for a derivation which shows

$$\operatorname{E}(T) = \frac 1 \theta,\quad \operatorname{var}(T) = \frac 1 {\theta^2}$$

Therefore,

$$\operatorname{E}(T^2) = \frac 2 {\theta^2}$$

Here we use Lehmann–Scheffé theorem to get the MVUE. Clearly, $\delta(X) = T^2/2$ is unbiased and $T = \log(1 + e^{-X})$ is complete sufficient, thus the UMVU estimator is

$$\eta(X) = \operatorname{E}(\delta(X) \mid T) = \operatorname{E} \left( \left. \frac{T^2} 2 \,\right|\, T \right) = \frac{T^2} 2 = \frac{\log\left(1 + e^{-X}\right)^2}{2}$$

This example illustrates that an unbiased function of the complete sufficient statistic will be UMVU, as [Lehmann–Scheffé theorem](wikipedia://en/342602) states.

:::

## Other examples {#Other_examples}

- For a normal distribution with unknown mean and variance, the [sample mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_mean) and (unbiased) [sample variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_variance) are the MVUEs for the population mean and population variance.

However, the [sample standard deviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_standard_deviation) is not unbiased for the population standard deviation – see [unbiased estimation of standard deviation](wikipedia://en/10240807).

Further, for other distributions the sample mean and sample variance are not in general MVUEs – for a [uniform distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29) with unknown upper and lower bounds, the [mid-range](wikipedia://en/2814021) is the MVUE for the population mean.

- If *k* exemplars are chosen (without replacement) from a [discrete uniform distribution](wikipedia://en/1699214) over the set {1, 2, ..., *N*} with unknown upper bound *N*, the MVUE for *N* is: $$\frac{k+1}{k} m - 1,$$ where *m* is the [sample maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_maximum). This is a scaled and shifted (so unbiased) transform of the sample maximum, which is a sufficient and complete statistic. See [German tank problem](wikipedia://en/16547646) for details.
