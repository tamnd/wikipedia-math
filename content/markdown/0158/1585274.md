# Heawood conjecture

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Heawood conjecture"}

*Image: 7\_colour\_torus.svg.* A radially symmetric 7-colored [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) – regions of the same colour wrap around along dotted lines

*Image: 8 mutually adjacent regions on double torus.svg.* An 8-coloured double torus (genus-two surface) – opposite circles are linked by handles

*Image: Klein\_bottle\_colouring.svg.* A 6-colored Klein bottle, the only exception to the Heawood conjecture

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **Heawood conjecture** or **Ringel–Youngs theorem** gives a [lower bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_bound) for the number of colors that are necessary for [graph coloring](wikipedia://en/426743) on a [surface](wikipedia://en/27865) of a given [genus](wikipedia://en/61891). For surfaces of genus 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, ..., the required number of colors is 4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 12, .... , the *chromatic number* or *Heawood number*.

:::

The [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) was formulated in 1890 by [P.J. Heawood](wikipedia://en/3605843) and [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) in 1968 by [Gerhard Ringel](wikipedia://en/1571968) and [J.W.T. Youngs](wikipedia://en/38288859). One case, the [non-orientable](wikipedia://en/187446) [Klein bottle](wikipedia://en/17412), proved an exception to the general formula. An entirely different approach was needed for the much older problem of finding the number of colors needed for the [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) or [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859), solved in 1976 as the [four color theorem](wikipedia://en/10949) by [Haken](wikipedia://en/311546) and [Appel](wikipedia://en/311544). On the sphere the lower bound is easy, whereas for higher genera the upper bound is easy and was proved in Heawood's original short paper that contained the conjecture. In other words, Ringel, Youngs, and others had to construct extreme examples for every genus *g* = 1, 2, 3, …. If *g* = 12*s* + *k*, then the genera fall into the 12 cases as *k* = 0, 1, 2, 3, …., 11. To simplify, suppose that case *k* has been established if only a finite number of *g*s of the form 12*s* + *k* are in doubt. Then the years in which the twelve cases were settled, and by whom, are the following:

- 1954, Ringel: case 5
- 1961, Ringel: cases 3, 7, 10
- 1963, Terry, Welch, Youngs: cases 0, 4
- 1964, Gustin, Youngs: case 1
- 1965, Gustin: case 9
- 1966, Youngs: case 6
- 1967, Ringel, Youngs: cases 2, 8, 11

The last seven sporadic exceptions were settled as follows:

- 1967, Mayer: cases 18, 20, 23
- 1968, Ringel, Youngs: cases 30, 35, 47, 59, and the conjecture was proved.

## Formal statement {#Formal_statement}

*Image: franklin graph.svg.* The [Franklin graph](wikipedia://en/23717056).

[Percy John Heawood](wikipedia://en/3605843) conjectured in 1890 that for a given genus *g* > 0, the minimum number of colors necessary to color all graphs drawn on an orientable surface of that genus (or equivalently, to color the regions of any partition of the surface into [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) regions) is given by

$$\gamma(g) = \left\lfloor \frac{7 + \sqrt{1 + 48g}}{2} \right\rfloor,$$

where $\left \lfloor x \right \rfloor$ is the [floor function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_function).

Replacing the genus by the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420), we obtain a formula that covers both the orientable and non-orientable cases,

$$\gamma(\chi) = \left\lfloor \frac{7 + \sqrt{49 - 24\chi}}2 \right\rfloor.$$

This relation holds, as Ringel and Youngs showed, for all surfaces except for the [Klein bottle](wikipedia://en/17412). [Philip Franklin](wikipedia://en/11458078) (1930) proved that the Klein bottle requires at most 6 colors, rather than 7 as predicted by the formula. The [Franklin graph](wikipedia://en/23717056) can be drawn on the Klein bottle in a way that forms six mutually-adjacent regions, showing that this bound is tight.

The upper bound, proved in Heawood's original short paper, is based on a [greedy coloring](wikipedia://en/21051195) algorithm. By manipulating the Euler characteristic, one can show that every graph embedded in the given surface must have at least one vertex of [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) less than the given bound. If one removes this vertex, and colors the rest of the graph, the small number of edges incident to the removed vertex ensures that it can be added back to the graph and colored without increasing the needed number of colors beyond the bound. In the other direction, the proof is more difficult, and involves showing that in each case (except the Klein bottle) a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) with a number of vertices equal to the given number of colors can be embedded on the surface.

## Example {#Example}

*Image: 7x-torus.svg.* A partition of the torus into seven mutually adjacent regions, requiring seven colors.

The [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) has *g* = 1, so χ = 0. Therefore, as the formula states, any subdivision of the torus into regions can be colored using at most seven colors. The illustration shows a subdivision of the torus in which each of seven regions are adjacent to each other region; this subdivision shows that the bound of seven on the number of colors is tight for this case. The boundary of this subdivision forms an embedding of the [Heawood graph](wikipedia://en/1396870) onto the torus.

*Image: Szilassi polyhedron 3D model.svg.* Horizontally rotatable [Szilassi polyhedron](wikipedia://en/7652409) model with each of 7 faces adjacent to every other. In \[ the SVG image,\] move the mouse to rotate it.[^o6]
