# Demographics of Istanbul

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Demographics of Istanbul"}

*Image: Istanbul 1930s.jpg.* People at the [commuter ferry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferries_in_Istanbul) [quay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferry_quays_in_İstanbul) of [Karaköy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaköy) in Istanbul in the 1930s

*Image: IstanbulGrowth1975and2011.png.* Two maps comparing the size of urban areas in Istanbul (indicated as the grey zones) in 1975 and 2011

Throughout most of its history, [Istanbul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul) has ranked among the largest cities in the world. By 500 [CE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era), [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople) had somewhere between 400,000 and 500,000 people, edging out its predecessor, [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome), for [world's largest city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_cities_throughout_history).[^o4] Constantinople jostled with other major historical cities, such as [Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad), [Chang'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang'an), [Kaifeng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaifeng) and [Merv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merv) for the position of [world's most populous city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_cities_throughout_history) until the 12th century. It never returned to being the world's largest, but remained [Europe's largest city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_European_cities_in_history) from 1500 to 1750, when it was surpassed by [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London).[^o5]

:::

The [Turkish Statistical Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_Statistical_Institute) estimates that the population of [Istanbul Metropolitan Municipality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul_Metropolitan_Municipality) was 15,701,602 at the end of 2024, hosting 18 percent of the country's population.[^o6] Then about 97–98% of the inhabitants of the metropolitan municipality were within city limits, up from 89% in 2007[^o7] and 61% in 1980.[^o8] 64.9% of the residents live on the [European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe) side and 35.1% on the [Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia) side.[^o9] While the city ranks as the world's [5th](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_proper_by_population)-largest city proper, it drops to the [24th](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_urban_areas_by_population) place as an urban area and to the [18th](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_metropolitan_areas_by_population) place as a metro area because the city limits are roughly equivalent to the agglomeration. Today, it forms one of the [largest urban agglomerations in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_metropolitan_areas_in_Europe), alongside [Moscow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow). The city's annual population growth of 3.45 percent ranks as the highest among the seventy-eight largest metropolises in the [Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organisation_for_Economic_Co-operation_and_Development). The high population growth mirrors an urbanization trend across the country, as the second and third fastest-growing OECD metropolises are the Turkish cities of [İzmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/İzmir) and Ankara.[^o10]

*Image: Istiklal busy afternoon.JPG.* [İstiklal Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/İstiklal_Avenue) is visited by nearly three million people on weekend days.

Istanbul experienced especially rapid growth during the second half of the 20th century, with its population increasing tenfold between 1950 and 2000.[^o11][^o12] This growth in population comes, in part, from an expansion of city limits—particularly between 1980 and 1985, when the number of Istanbulites nearly doubled.[^o13] The remarkable growth was, and still is, largely fueled by migrants from eastern Turkey seeking employment and improved living conditions. The number of residents of Istanbul originating from seven northern and eastern provinces is greater than the populations of their entire respective provinces; [Sivas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sivas_Province) and [Kastamonu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kastamonu_Province) each account for more than half a million residents of Istanbul.[^o12] Istanbul's foreign population, by comparison, was very small, 42,228 residents in 2007.[^o14] Only 28 percent of the city's residents are originally from Istanbul.[^o15] The most densely populated areas tend to lie to the northwest, west, and southwest of the city center, on the European side; the most densely populated district on the Asian side is [Üsküdar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Üsküdar).[^o12] As of 2023, Istanbul has Turkey’s biggest foreign migrant population, with 34.5% of foreign nationals in Turkey living there.[^o16]

## Religious groups {#Religious_groups}

*Image: Greeks in Istanbul 1844 1997.png.* Greek population in Istanbul and percentages of the city population (1844–1997). The [1923 population exchange between Greece and Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_exchange_between_Greece_and_Turkey), the [1942 wealth tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varlık_Vergisi), and the [Istanbul pogrom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul_pogrom) in 1955 contributed to the sharp decrease of the Greek community.

Istanbul has been a cosmopolitan city throughout much of its history, but it has become more homogenized since the end of the Ottoman Empire. The vast majority of people across Turkey, and in Istanbul, are [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim), and more specifically members of the [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) branch of [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam). Most Sunni Turks follow the [Hanafi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanafi) school of Islamic thought, while Sunni Kurds tend to follow the [Shafi'i](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shafi'i) school. The largest non-Sunni Muslim group, accounting 10-20% of Turkey's population,[^o18] are the [Alevis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alevi); a third of all Alevis in the country live in Istanbul.[^o15] Mystic movements, like [Sufism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism), were officially banned after the establishment of the Turkish Republic, but they still boast numerous followers.[^o19] Istanbul is a migrant city. Since the 1950s, Istanbul's population has increased from 1 million to about 10 million residents. Almost 200,000 new immigrants, many of them from Turkey's own villages, continue to arrive each year. As a result, the city is constantly changing and being reshaped to meet the needs of these new arrivals.[^o20]

The [Patriarch of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarch_of_Constantinople) has been designated Ecumenical Patriarch since the sixth century, and has come to be regarded as the leader of the world's 300 million [Orthodox Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church).[^o21] Since 1601, the Patriarchate has been based in Istanbul's [Church of St. George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_St._George,_Istanbul).[^o22] Into the 19th century, the Christians of Istanbul tended to be either [Greek Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Church), members of the [Armenian Apostolic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Apostolic_Church) or Catholic [Levantines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levantines_%28Latin_Christians%29).[^o23] Today, most of Turkey's remaining [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greeks_in_Turkey), [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians_in_Turkey) and [Assyrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrians_in_Turkey) minorities live in or near Istanbul.[^o24]

Eldem Edhem, in his entry on "Istanbul" in the *Encyclopedia of the Ottoman Empire*, wrote that about 50% of the residents of the city were Muslim at the turn of the 20th century.[^o25]

### Arabs {#Arabs}

The Arabic newspaper *[Al Jawaib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Jawaib)* began in Ottoman Constantinople, established by [Ahmad Faris al-Shidyaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmad_Faris_al-Shidyaq) a.k.a. Ahmed Faris Efendi (1804–1887), after 1860. It published Ottoman laws in Arabic,[^o28] including the [Ottoman Constitution of 1876](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Constitution_of_1876).[^o28]

Besides the large communities of both foreign and [Turkish Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabs_in_Turkey) in Istanbul and other large cities, most live in the south and southeast.[^o29] Most [Turkish Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabs_in_Turkey) in Istanbul are Sunni Muslim, while the remaining consists mainly [Arab Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Christians) ([Antiochian Greek Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiochian_Greek_Christians)) and [Alawites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alawites).[^o30]

Istanbul, the most populous city in Turkey, hosts the highest number of [Syrian refugees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrians_in_Turkey), with approximately 550,000 registered people.[^o31]

### Armenians {#Armenians}

2015 there are between 50,000 and 70,000 [Armenians in Istanbul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians_in_Istanbul) (0.3-0.5%), down from about 164,000 according to the Ottoman Census of 1913 (14.5%).[^o33] In late 1918, [Celal Nuri İleri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celal_Nuri_İleri) reported that there were around 200,000 Armenians in Istanbul (~17%).[^o34]

::: {#o36 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Bulgarians in Istanbul"}

### Bulgarians {#Bulgarians}

Bulgarian newspapers in the late Ottoman period published in Constantinople were *Makedoniya*, *Napredŭk* or *Napredǎk* ("Progress"), *Pravo*,[^o37] and *Turtsiya*; Johann Strauss, author of "A Constitution for a Multilingual Empire," described the last one as "probably a Bulgarian version of \[the French-language paper\] *La Turquie*."[^o38] By the end of the 19th century, the Bulgarian population of Istanbul numbered between 40,000 and 50,000 people.[^o39]

### Greeks {#Greeks}

*Image: Fener\_Rum\_Lisesi.jpg.* [Phanar Greek Orthodox College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phanar_Greek_Orthodox_College) is a Greek minority school was founded in [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire) in 1454.

[Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople) had a majority Greek population from the 8th century BCE until the Ottoman conquest in 1453.

After 1453, there remained a group of prominent ethnic Greeks and/or people adopting Greek culture, the [Phanariotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phanariotes), based in the neighbourhood of Phanar, now [Fener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fener), in [Fatih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatih). About eleven families were a part of the Phanariotes.[^o42]

The city remained a centre of Greek cultural and political life, and Greeks were a visible presence in the city. According to the Ottoman census of 1893, Greeks made up almost 30% of the city's population, while accounting for 43% of the population in the suburbs.[^o43] As the city was also home to significant Armenian, Catholic and Jewish minorities, there were more non-Muslims than Muslims in Istanbul, with Muslims making up 44% of the city's population in 1893.[^o43] The Greek community also dominated the city's economy, owning 50% of the city's total production and distribution capital in 1915.[^o44] In 1919, of the city's 1,173,670 inhabitants, 364,459 were Greek (31%) and 449,114 were Turk (38%).[^o45][^o46] Because of considerable presence of other non-Muslim ethnic groups like Armenians (17%), Bulgarians (3%) and the Jews (4%) at the time, Muslims were a minority in the city.[^o34][^o47]

Because of events during the 20th century, the [1923 population exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_exchange_between_Greece_and_Turkey) between Greece and Turkey, a [1942 wealth tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varlık_Vergisi), and the [1955 Istanbul riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul_pogrom)—the Greek population, originally centered in [Fener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fener) and [Samatya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samatya), has decreased substantially.

At the start of the 21st century, Istanbul's Greek population numbered 3,000 (down from 260,000 out of 850,000 according to the Ottoman Census of 1910, and a peak of 350,000 in 1919).[^o48][^o49]

Even with these reduced numbers, there remains a Greek-language newspaper, *[Apoyevmatini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apoyevmatini),* in active circulation.[^o50]

### Jews {#Jews}

*Image: Şınayder\_Sinagogu\_(16231012698).jpg.* [Ashkenazi Synagogue of Istanbul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashkenazi_Synagogue_of_Istanbul)

The neighbourhood of [Balat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balat_%28Istanbul%29) used to be home to a sizable [Sephardi Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sephardi_Jews) community, first formed after their expulsion from Spain in 1492.[^o52] At the start of the World War I, there were 52,153 Jews (4%) in the city, which fell to 47,035 (7%) by 1927.[^o47] [Romaniotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romaniotes) and [Ashkenazi Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashkenazi_Jews) resided in Istanbul even before the Sephardim, but their proportion has since dwindled; today, 1 percent of Istanbul's Jews are Ashkenazi.[^o53][^o54] In large part due to emigration to [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel), the Jewish population nationwide dropped from 100,000 in 1950 to 18,000 in 2005, with the majority living in either Istanbul or İzmir.[^o55] As of 2022, the Jewish population in Turkey is around 14,500.[^o56]

### Kurds {#Kurds}

The largest ethnic minority in Istanbul is the [Kurdish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdish_people) community, originating from eastern and southeastern Turkey. Although the Kurdish presence in the city dates back to the early Ottoman period,[^o58] the influx of Kurds into the city has accelerated since the beginning of the [Kurdish–Turkish conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdish–Turkish_conflict) with the [PKK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdistan_Workers'_Party) (i.e. since the late 1970s).[^o59] About two to four million residents of Istanbul are Kurdish, meaning there are more Kurds in Istanbul than in any other city in the world.[^o60][^o61][^o62][^o63][^o64][^o65]

### Levantines {#Levantines}

The [Levantines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levantines_%28Latin_Christians%29), Latin Christians who settled in [Galata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galata) during the Ottoman period, played a seminal role in shaping the culture and architecture of then-Constantinople during the 19th and early 20th centuries; their population in Istanbul has dwindled, but they remain in the city in small numbers.[^o67]

### Romani {#Romani}

There is a [Roma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romani_people) community Istanbul.[^o69]

### Turks {#Turks}

Parallel to the overall demographics of Turkey, Turks are the largest group in Istanbul. Although the presence of Turks in Istanbul goes back to the early Ottoman times, the bulk of this population is composed of recent migrants from the Balkans and Anatolia. [^o71]

### Other ethnicities {#Other_ethnicities}

There are other significant ethnic minorities as well, the [Bosniaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosniaks) are the main people of an entire district—[Bayrampaşa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayrampaşa).[^o73]

From the increase in mutual cooperation between Turkey and several African States like Somalia and Djibouti, several young students and workers have been migrating to Istanbul in search of better education and employment opportunities. c. 2015 the major areas of African settlement are [Eminönü](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eminönü) and [Yenikapi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yenikapi) in [Fatih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatih), and [Kurtulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurtulus) and [Osmanbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osmanbey) in [Şişli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Şişli). The largest groups of Africans that year were from Nigeria and Somalia, with the latter often working in business and the manufacturing of clothing. There are also Cameroonian, Congolese, and Senegalese communities present, with the first group directly involved in the vending of clothing and the last involved in the sale of goods streetside.[^o74]

As of 2011 about 900 Japanese persons resided in Istanbul; 768 were officially registered with the Japanese Consulate of Istanbul as of October 2010. Of those living in Istanbul, about 450–500 are employees of Japanese companies and their family members, making up around half of the total Japanese population. Others include students of Turkish language and culture, business owners, and Japanese women married to Turkish men. Istanbul has several Japanese restaurants, a Japanese newspaper, and a 32-page Japanese magazine.[^o75] According to the [Istanbul Japanese School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul_Japanese_School), circa 2019 there were about 2,000 Japanese citizens in the Istanbul area, with about 100 of them being children of the ages in which, in Japan, they would be legally required to attend school. At the same period there were about 110 Japanese companies in operation in the city.[^o76] Istanbul also has a [weekend Japanese education programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoshū_jugyō_kō), The Japanese Saturday School in Istanbul.[^o77]

After the [Romanian revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_Revolution_of_1989), a significant number of Romanian entrepreneurs started investing and establishing business ventures in Turkey, and a certain proportion chose to take up residence there in Istanbul. There are also Romanian migrant workers, as well as students and artists living in the city.[^o78][^o79] Some sources claim that there are 14,000 [Romanians living in Istanbul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanians_in_Turkey).[^o80] There is also a Romanian Orthodox Church in the city.[^o81][^o82]

Russians began migrating to Turkey during the first half of the 1990s. Most had fled the economic problems prevalent after the [dissolution of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union). During this period, many intermarried and assimilated with locals, bringing a rapid increase in mixed marriages. Following the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine), many Russians have fled to Turkey, especially Istanbul, after [Vladimir Putin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Putin) announced a "[partial mobilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Russian_mobilization)" of military reservists.[^o83]
