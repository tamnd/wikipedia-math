# Inverse consequences

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Inverse consequences"}

The term "**inverse consequences**"[^o4] or the "**Law of Inverse Consequences**"[^o2] refers to results that are the opposite of the expected results as initially intended or planned.[^o2] One consequence is in the "reverse predicament" of the other.[^o4]

:::

## History {#History}

The term "inverse consequences" has been in use for over 175 years (since at least 1835).[^o4] The term was also used by [Auguste Comte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Comte) (1798–1857) in his book *System of Positive Polity* (published 1875), stating, "Inevitable increase in Complication, in proportion with the decrease of Generality, gives rise to two inverse consequences."[^o5]

## Documented examples {#Documented_examples}

The term "inverse consequences" has been applied in numerous situations, for example:

- In treatment of drug addiction, medications intended to reduce one type of addiction might trigger another addiction: long-term treatment with [opiate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opiate) medications (such as [morphine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphine)) has *inverse consequences*.[^o7]
- In management of work tasks, a total sequential execution, of work tasks, has inverse consequences, such as a decrease of the workload with an increase of the lead time.[^o8]
- In [asset management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asset_management), plans for [portfolio management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Investment_management) might have inverse consequences to the potential benefits.[^o9]

## Related phrases {#Related_phrases}

The concept of "inverse consequences" has a [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865) in other phrases, as well:

- "the plan [backfired](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Back-fire)" - meaning the opposite result occurred, as in a gun firing backward, rather than forward.
