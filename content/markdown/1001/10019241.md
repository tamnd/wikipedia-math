# Anscombe's quartet

*Image: Anscombe's quartet 3.svg.* The four [datasets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dataset) composing Anscombe's quartet. All four sets have almost identical statistical parameters, but the graphs show them to be considerably different.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Anscombe's quartet"}

**Anscombe's quartet** comprises four [datasets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dataset) that have nearly identical simple [descriptive statistics](wikipedia://en/8187), yet have very different [distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) and appear very different when [graphed](wikipedia://en/19774918). Each dataset consists of eleven [(*x*, *y*) points](wikipedia://en/7706). They were constructed in 1973 by the [statistician](wikipedia://en/48661) [Francis Anscombe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Anscombe) to demonstrate both the importance of graphing data when analyzing it, and the effect of [outliers](wikipedia://en/160951) and other [influential observations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Influential_observations) on statistical properties. He described the article as being intended to counter the impression among statisticians that "numerical calculations are exact, but graphs are rough".[^o2]

:::

## Data {#Data}

For all four datasets:

::: {#o4 .table cue=table}

| Property | Value | Accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| [Mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) of *x* | 9 | exact |
| Sample [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) of *x*: *s*2 | 11 | exact |
| Mean of *y* | \7.50 | to 2 decimal places |
| Sample variance of *y*: *s*2 | \4.125 | ±0.003 |
| [Correlation](wikipedia://en/157057) between *x* and *y* | \0.816 | to 3 decimal places |
| [Linear regression](wikipedia://en/48758386) line | *y* = 3.00 + 0.500*x* | to 2 and 3 decimal places, respectively |
| [Coefficient of determination](wikipedia://en/1500869) of the linear regression: $R^2$ | \0.67 | to 2 decimal places |

:::

- The first [scatter plot](wikipedia://en/412544) (top left) appears to be a simple [linear relationship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_relationship), corresponding to two correlated [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109), where *y* could be modelled as [Gaussian](wikipedia://en/21462) with mean linearly dependent on *x*.
- For the second graph (top right), while a relationship between the two variables is obvious, it is not linear, and the [Pearson correlation coefficient](wikipedia://en/221708) is not relevant. A more general regression and the corresponding [coefficient of determination](wikipedia://en/1500869) would be more appropriate.
- In the third graph (bottom left), the modelled relationship is linear, but should have a different [regression line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regression_line) (a [robust regression](wikipedia://en/2713327) would have been called for). The calculated regression is offset by the one [outlier](wikipedia://en/160951), which exerts enough influence to lower the correlation coefficient from 1 to 0.816.
- Finally, the fourth graph (bottom right) shows an example when one [high-leverage point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-leverage_point) is enough to produce a high correlation coefficient, even though the other data points do not indicate any relationship between the variables.

The quartet is still often used to illustrate the importance of looking at a set of data graphically before starting to analyze according to a particular type of relationship, and the inadequacy of basic statistic properties for describing realistic datasets.[^o10][^o11][^o12][^o13][^o14]

It is not known how Anscombe created his datasets.[^o15] Since its publication, several methods to generate similar datasets with identical statistics and dissimilar graphics have been developed.[^o15][^o16] One of these, the *[Datasaurus dozen](wikipedia://en/75971902)*, consists of points tracing out the outline of a dinosaur, plus twelve other datasets that have the same summary statistics.[^o17][^o18][^o19]
