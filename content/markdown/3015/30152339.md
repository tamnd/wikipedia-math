# Doubling space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Doubling space"}

*Image: 7 disks cover 2x disk.svg.* In the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), seven disks of radius $r/2$ can cover any disk of radius $r$, so the plane is a doubling space with doubling constant 7 and doubling dimension log<sub>2</sub> 7.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) $X$ with metric $d$ is said to be **doubling** if there is some doubling constant $M > 0$ such that for any $x \in X$ and $r > 0$, it is possible to cover the ball $B(x, r) = \{y | d(x, y) < r\}$ with the union of at most $M$ balls of radius $\tfrac{r}{2}$.[^o2] The base-2 [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) of $M$ is called the **doubling dimension** of $X$.[^o3] [Euclidean spaces](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^d$ equipped with the usual Euclidean metric are examples of doubling spaces where the doubling constant $M$ depends on the dimension $d$. For example, in one dimension, $M = 3$; and in two dimensions, $M = 7$.[^o4] In general, Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^d$ has doubling dimension $\Theta(d)$.[^o3][^o5]

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Assouad's embedding theorem"}

## Assouad's embedding theorem {#Assouad's_embedding_theorem}

An important question in metric space geometry is to characterize those metric spaces that can be embedded in some Euclidean space by a [bi-Lipschitz](wikipedia://en/51731) function. This means that one can essentially think of the metric space as a subset of Euclidean space. Not all metric spaces may be embedded in Euclidean space. Doubling metric spaces, on the other hand, would seem like they have more of a chance, since the doubling condition says, in a way, that the metric space is not infinite dimensional. However, this is still not the case in general. The [Heisenberg group](wikipedia://en/448518) with its [Carnot-Carathéodory metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnot-Carathéodory_metric) is an example of a doubling metric space which cannot be embedded in any Euclidean space.[^o8]

**Assouad's Theorem** states that, for a $M$-doubling metric space $X$, if we give it the metric $d(x, y)^{\varepsilon}$ for some $0 < \varepsilon < 1$, then there is a $L$-bi-Lipschitz map $f:X \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d$, where $d$ and $L$ depend on $M$ and $\varepsilon$.

:::

## Doubling Measures {#Doubling_Measures}

::: {#o11 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

A nontrivial [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) on a metric space *X* is said to be **doubling** if the measure of any ball is finite and approximately the measure of its double, or more precisely, if there is a constant *C* > 0 such that

$$0<\mu(B(x,2r))\leq C\mu(B(x,r))<\infty \,$$

for all *x* in *X* and *r* > 0. In this case, we say *μ* is **C-doubling**. In fact, it can be proved that, necessarily, *C* $\geq$ 2.[^o12]

A metric measure space that supports a doubling measure is necessarily a doubling metric space, where the doubling constant depends on the constant *C*. Conversely, every [complete](wikipedia://en/7832) doubling metric space supports a doubling measure.[^o13][^o14]

:::

::: {#o16 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

A simple example of a doubling measure is [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on a Euclidean space. One can, however, have doubling measures on Euclidean space that are [singular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_measures) with respect to Lebesgue measure. One example on the real line is the [weak limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_limit) of the following sequence of measures:[^o17]

$$d\mu_n = \prod_{i=1}^n (1+a\cos (3^i 2\pi x))\,dx,\;\;\; |a|<1.$$

One can construct another singular doubling measure *μ* on the interval \[0, 1\] as follows: for each *k* ≥ 0, partition the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) \[0,1\] into 3<sup>*k*</sup> intervals of length 3<sup>−*k*</sup>. Let Δ be the collection of all such intervals in \[0,1\] obtained for each *k* (these are the *triadic intervals*), and for each such interval *I*, let *m*(*I*) denote its "middle third" interval. Fix 0 \< *δ* \< 1 and let *μ* be the measure such that *μ*(\[0, 1\]) = 1 and for each triadic interval *I*, *μ*(*m*(*I*)) = *δμ*(*I*). Then this gives a doubling measure on \[0, 1\] singular to Lebesgue measure.[^o18]

:::

::: {#o20 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The definition of a doubling measure may seem arbitrary, or purely of geometric interest. However, many results from classical [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147) and [computational geometry](wikipedia://en/176927) extend to the setting of metric spaces with doubling measures.

:::
