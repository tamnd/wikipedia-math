# Lieb–Oxford inequality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lieb–Oxford inequality"}

In [quantum chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chemistry) and [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), the **Lieb–Oxford inequality** provides a lower bound for the indirect part of the [Coulomb energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb_energy) of a [quantum mechanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_Mechanics) system. It is named after [Elliott H. Lieb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliott_H._Lieb) and [Stephen Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Oxford).

:::

The inequality is of importance for [density functional theory](wikipedia://en/209874) and plays a role in the proof of [stability of matter](wikipedia://en/44172803#The_stability_of_matter).

## Introduction {#Introduction}

In classical physics, one can calculate the [Coulomb energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb_energy) of a configuration of charged particles in the following way. First, calculate the [charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_density) $\rho$, where $\rho$ is a function of the coordinates $x\in\mathbb{R}^3$. Second, calculate the Coulomb energy by integrating:

$$\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^3}\frac{\rho(x)\rho(y)}{|x-y|} \, \mathrm{d}^3 x \, \mathrm{d}^3 y.$$

In other words, for each pair of points $x$ and $y$, this expression calculates the energy related to the fact that the charge at $x$ is attracted to or repelled from the charge at $y$. The factor of $\tfrac{1}{2}$ corrects for double-counting the pairs of points.

In quantum mechanics, it is *also* possible to calculate a charge density $\rho$, which is a function of $x\in\mathbb{R}^3$. More specifically, $\rho$ is defined as the [expectation value](wikipedia://en/11371560) of charge density at each point. But in this case, the above formula for Coulomb energy is not correct, due to [exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_interaction) and [correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_correlation) effects. The above, classical formula for Coulomb energy is then called the "direct" part of Coulomb energy. To get the *actual* Coulomb energy, it is necessary to add a correction term, called the "indirect" part of Coulomb energy. The Lieb–Oxford inequality concerns this indirect part. It is relevant in [density functional theory](wikipedia://en/209874), where the expectation value ρ plays a central role.

::: {#o4 .statement cue=named-heading name="Statement of the inequality"}

## Statement of the inequality {#Statement_of_the_inequality}

For a [quantum mechanical](wikipedia://en/25202) system of $N$ particles, each with charge $e$, the $N$-particle density is denoted by

$$P(x_1,\dots,x_N).$$

The function $P$ is only assumed to be non-negative and [normalized](wikipedia://en/145343#Normalized_components_and_probabilities). Thus the following applies to particles with any "statistics". For example, if the system is described by a normalised [square integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_integrable) $N$-particle [wave function](wikipedia://en/145343)

$$\psi\in L^2(\mathbb{R}^{3N}),$$

then

$$P(x_1,\dots,x_N)=|\psi(x_1,\dots,x_N)|^2.$$

More generally, in the case of particles with [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829) having $q$ spin states per particle and with corresponding wave function

$$\psi(x_1,\sigma_1,\dots,x_N,\sigma_N)$$

the $N$-particle density is given by

$$P(x_1,\dots,x_N)=\sum_{\sigma_1=1}^q\cdots\sum_{\sigma_N=1}^q|\psi(x_1,\sigma_1,\dots,x_N,\sigma_N)|^2.$$

Alternatively, if the system is described by a density matrix $\gamma$, then $P$ is the diagonal

$$\gamma(x_1, ... , x_N; x_1, ..., x_N ).$$

The electrostatic energy of the system is defined as

$$I_P=e^2\sum_{1\le i<j\le N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{P(x_1,\dots,x_i,\dots,x_j,\dots,x_N)}{|x_i-x_j|} \, \mathrm{d}^3 x_1\cdots\mathrm{d}^3 x_N.$$

For $x\in\mathbb{R}^3$, the single particle charge density is given by

$$\rho(x)=|e|\sum_{i=1}^N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3(N-1)}}P(x_1,\dots,x_{i-1},x,x_{i+1},\dots,x_N) \, \mathrm{d}^3 x_1\cdots\mathrm{d}^3 x_{i-1} \, \mathrm{d}^3 x_{i+1}\cdots\mathrm{d}^3 x_N$$

and the direct part of the Coulomb energy of the system of $N$ particles is defined as the electrostatic energy associated with the charge density $\rho$, i.e.

$$D(\rho)=\frac12\int_{\mathbb{R}^3} \int_{\mathbb{R}^3} \frac{\rho(x)\rho(y)}{|x-y|} \, \mathrm{d}^3 x \, \mathrm{d}^3 y.$$

The **Lieb–Oxford inequality** states that the difference between the true energy $I_{P}$ and its semiclassical approximation $D(\rho)$ is bounded from below as

::: {#o5 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$E_P=I_P-D(\rho)\ge -C|e|^\frac23\int_{\mathbb{R}^3}|\rho(x)|^\frac43 \, \mathrm{d}^3 x, \tag{1}$$

:::

where $C \le 1.58$ is a constant independent of the particle number $N$. $E_{P}$ is referred to as the indirect part of the Coulomb energy and in density functional theory more commonly as the [exchange plus correlation energy](wikipedia://en/209874#Derivation_and_formalism). A similar bound exists if the particles have different charges $e_{1}, ... , e_{N}$. No upper bound is possible for $E_{P}$.

:::

## Optimal constant {#Optimal_constant}

While the original proof yielded the constant $C = 8.52$,[^o7] Lieb and Oxford managed to refine this result to $C = 1.68$.[^o8] Later, the same method of proof was used to further improve the constant to $C = 1.64$.[^o9] It is only recently that the constant was decreased to $C = 1.58$.[^o10] With these constants the inequality holds for any particle number $N$.

The constant can be further improved if the particle number $N$ is restricted. In the case of a single particle $N = 1$ the Coulomb energy vanishes, $I_{P} = 0$, and the smallest possible constant can be computed explicitly as $C_{1} = 1.092$.[^o8] The corresponding [variational equation](wikipedia://en/171882) for the optimal $\rho$ is the [Lane–Emden equation](wikipedia://en/341818) of order 3. For two particles ($N = 2$) it is known that the smallest possible constant satisfies $C_{2} \ge 1.234$.[^o8] In general it can be proved that the optimal constants $C_{N}$ increase with the number of particles, i.e. $C_{N} \le C_{N + 1}$,[^o8] and converge in the limit of large $N$ to the best constant $C_{LO}$ in the inequality ((1)). Any lower bound on the optimal constant for fixed particle number $N$ is also a lower bound on the optimal constant $C_{LO}$. The best numerical lower bound was obtained for $N = 60$ where $C_{60} \ge 1.41$.[^o11] This bound has been obtained by considering an exponential density. For the same [particle number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_number) a uniform density gives $C_{60} \ge 1.34$.

The largest proved lower bound on the best constant is $C_{LO} \ge 1.4442$, which was first proven by Cotar and Petrache.[^o12] The same lower bound was later obtained in using a uniform [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) gas, melted in the neighborhood of its surface, by Lewin, Lieb & Seiringer.[^o13] Hence, to summarise, the best known bounds for $C$ are $1.44 \le C \le 1.58$.

## Dirac constant {#Dirac_constant}

Historically, the first approximation of the indirect part $E_{P}$ of the Coulomb energy in terms of the single particle charge density was given by [Paul Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742) in 1930 for [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermions).[^o15] The wave function under consideration is

$$\psi(x_1,\sigma_1,\dots,x_N,\sigma_N)= \frac{\det(\varphi_i(x_j,\sigma_j))}{\sqrt{N!}}.$$

With the aim of evoking perturbation theory, one considers the eigenfunctions of the [Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian) in a large cubic box of volume $|\Lambda|$ and sets

$$\varphi_{\alpha,k}(x,\sigma) = \frac{\chi_\alpha(\sigma)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i} k\cdot x}}{\sqrt{|\Lambda|}},$$

where $\chi_{1}, ..., \chi_{q}$ forms an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) of $\mathbb{C}^q$. The allowed values of $k\in\mathbb{R}^3$ are $n/|\Lambda|^{\tfrac{1}{3}}$ with $n\in\mathbb{Z}_+^3$. For large $N$, $|\Lambda|$, and fixed $\rho = N |e|/|\Lambda|$, the indirect part of the Coulomb energy can be computed to be

$$E_P(\mathrm{Dirac})=-C |e|^{2/3} q^{-1/3}\rho^{4/3}|\Lambda|,$$

with $C = 0.93$.

This result can be compared to the lower bound ((1)). In contrast to Dirac's approximation the Lieb–Oxford inequality does not include the number $q$ of spin states on the right-hand side. The dependence on $q$ in Dirac's formula is a consequence of his specific choice of wave functions and not a general feature.

## Generalisations {#Generalisations}

The constant $C$ in ((1)) can be made smaller at the price of adding another term to the right-hand side. By including a term that involves the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) of a power of the single particle charge density $\rho$, the constant $C$ can be improved to $1.45$.[^o17][^o18] Thus, for a uniform density system $C \le 1.45$.
