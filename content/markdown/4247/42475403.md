# Spherical wave transformation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Spherical wave transformation"}

**Spherical wave transformations** leave the form of [spherical waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_wave) as well as the laws of [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics) and [electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrodynamics) invariant in all [inertial frames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_frames). They were defined between 1908 and 1909 by [Harry Bateman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Bateman) and [Ebenezer Cunningham](wikipedia://en/11767545), with Bateman giving the transformation its name.[^o2] They correspond to the [conformal group](wikipedia://en/364775) of "transformations by reciprocal radii" in relation to the framework of [Lie sphere geometry](wikipedia://en/16646944), which were already known in the 19th century.

:::

Time is used as [fourth dimension](wikipedia://en/28758) as in [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space), so spherical wave transformations are connected to the [Lorentz transformation](wikipedia://en/18404) of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962), and it turns out that the [conformal group of spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_group_of_spacetime) includes the [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489) and the [Poincaré group](wikipedia://en/230487) as subgroups. However, only the Lorentz/Poincaré groups represent symmetries of all laws of nature including mechanics, whereas the conformal group is related to certain areas such as electrodynamics.[^o3][^o4][^o5] In addition, it can be shown that the conformal group of the plane (corresponding to the [Möbius group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Möbius_group) of the [extended complex plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_complex_plane)) is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to the Lorentz group.[^o21]

A special case of Lie sphere geometry is the transformation by reciprocal directions or Laguerre inversion, being a generator of the Laguerre group. It transforms not only spheres into spheres but also planes into planes.[^o77][^o6][^o7] If time is used as fourth dimension, a close analogy to the Lorentz transformation as well as isomorphism to the Lorentz group was pointed out by several authors such as Bateman, [Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) or [Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740).[^o67][^o8][^o108][^o102][^o9][^o10][^o11][^o12]

## Transformation by reciprocal radii {#Transformation_by_reciprocal_radii}

### Development in the 19th century {#Development_in_the_19th_century}

[Inversions](wikipedia://en/295844) preserving angles between circles were first discussed by [Durrande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Durrande) (1820), with [Quetelet](wikipedia://en/184215) (1827) and [Plücker](wikipedia://en/52146) (1828) writing down the corresponding transformation formula, $k$ being the radius of inversion:[^o15]

$$x^{\prime}=\frac{k^{2}x}{x^{2}+y^{2}},\quad y^{\prime}=\frac{k^{2}y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}.$$

These inversions were later called "transformations by reciprocal radii", and became better known when [Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thomson,_1st_Baron_Kelvin) (1845, 1847) applied them on spheres with coordinates $x, y, z$ in the course of developing the [method of inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_inversion) in [electrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics).[^o16] [Joseph Liouville](wikipedia://en/341810) (1847) demonstrated its mathematical meaning by showing that it belongs to the [conformal transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_transformation) producing the following [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478):[^o17]

$$\delta x^{\prime2}+\delta y^{\prime2}+\delta z^{\prime2}=\lambda\left(\delta x^{2}+\delta y^{2}+\delta z^{2}\right).$$

Liouville himself[^o18] and more extensively [Sophus Lie](wikipedia://en/54010) (1871)[^o19] showed that the related [conformal group](wikipedia://en/364775) can be differentiated ([Liouville's theorem](wikipedia://en/12458499)): For instance, $\lambda=1$ includes the [Euclidean group](wikipedia://en/652164) of ordinary motions; $\lambda\ne 1$ [scale or similarity transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similarity_transformation_%28geometry%29) in which the coordinates of the previous transformations are multiplied by $\sqrt{\lambda}$; and $\lambda=k^{4}/\left(x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}\right)^{2}$ gives Thomson's transformation by reciprocal radii (inversions):[^o18]

$$x^{\prime}=\frac{k^{2}x}{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}},\quad y^{\prime}=\frac{k^{2}y}{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}},\quad z^{\prime}=\frac{k^{2}z}{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}}.$$

Subsequently, Liouville's theorem was extended to $n$ dimensions by Lie (1871)[^o19] and others such as [Darboux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaston_Darboux) (1878):[^o20]

$$\delta x_{1}^{\prime2}+\dots+\delta x_{n}^{\prime2}=\lambda\left(\delta x_{1}^{2}+\dots+\delta x_{n}^{2}\right).$$

This group of conformal transformations by reciprocal radii preserves angles and transforms spheres into spheres or [hyperspheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere) (see [Möbius transformation](wikipedia://en/314493), [conformal symmetry](wikipedia://en/671875), [special conformal transformation](wikipedia://en/24675202)). It is a 6-parameter group in the plane **R**<sup>2</sup> which corresponds to the Möbius group of the [extended complex plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_complex_plane),[^o22][^o21] a 10-parameter group in space **R**<sup>3</sup>, and a 15-parameter group in **R**<sup>4</sup>. In **R**<sup>2</sup> it represents only a small subset of all conformal transformations therein, whereas in **R**<sup>2+n</sup> it is identical to the group of all conformal transformations (corresponding to the Möbius transformations in higher dimensions) therein, in accordance with Liouville's theorem.[^o22] Conformal transformations in **R**<sup>3</sup> were often applied to what Darboux (1873) called "pentaspherical coordinates" by relating the points to [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316) based on five spheres.[^o23][^o24]

### Oriented spheres {#Oriented_spheres}

Another method for solving such sphere problems was to write down the coordinates together with the sphere's radius.[^o26] This was employed by Lie (1871) in the context of [Lie sphere geometry](wikipedia://en/16646944) which represents a general framework of sphere-transformations (being a special case of [contact transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_transformation)) conserving [lines of curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lines_of_curvature) and transforming spheres into spheres.[^o27] The previously mentioned 10-parameter group in **R**<sup>3</sup> related to pentaspherical coordinates is extended to the 15-parameter group of Lie sphere transformations related to "hexaspherical coordinates" (named by [Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) in 1893) by adding a sixth homogeneous coordinate related to the radius.[^o28][^o23][^o29] Since the radius of a sphere can have a positive or negative sign, one sphere always corresponds to two transformed spheres. It is advantageous to remove this ambiguity by attributing a definite sign to the radius, consequently giving the spheres a definite orientation too, so that one oriented sphere corresponds to one transformed oriented sphere.[^o30] This method was occasionally and implicitly employed by Lie (1871)[^o19] himself and explicitly introduced by [Laguerre](wikipedia://en/1011080) (1880).[^o65] In addition, Darboux (1887) brought the transformations by reciprocal radii into a form by which the radius *r* of a sphere can be determined if the radius of the other one is known:[^o31]

$$\begin{aligned} x^{\prime} & =\frac{k^{2}x}{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-r^{2}},\quad & z^{\prime} & =\frac{k^{2}z}{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-r^{2}},\\ y' & =\frac{k^{2}y}{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-r^{2}}, & r^{\prime} & =\frac{\pm k^{2}r}{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-r^{2}}. \end{aligned}$$

Using coordinates together with the radius was often connected to a method called "minimal projection" by Klein (1893),[^o32] which was later called "isotropy projection" by [Blaschke](wikipedia://en/728998) (1926) emphasizing the relation to oriented circles and spheres.[^o33] For instance, a circle with rectangular coordinates $x, y$ and radius $r$ in **R**<sup>2</sup> corresponds to a point in **R**<sup>3</sup> with coordinates $x, y, z$. This method was known for some time in circle geometry (though without using the concept of orientation) and can be further differentiated depending on whether the additional coordinate is treated as [imaginary](wikipedia://en/14554) or real: $z=ir$ was used by [Chasles](wikipedia://en/2252716) (1852), [Möbius](wikipedia://en/37875) (1857), [Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034) (1867), and Darboux (1872);[^o34] $z=r$ was used by [Cousinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barthélémy-Édouard_Cousinery) (1826), [Druckenmüller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Druckenmüller) (1842), and in the "cyclography" of [Fiedler](wikipedia://en/42762029) (1882), therefore the latter method was also called "cyclographic projection" – see [E. Müller](wikipedia://en/29039777) (1910) for a summary.[^o35] This method was also applied to spheres[^o36] by Darboux (1872),[^o37] Lie (1871),[^o19] or Klein (1893).[^o32] Let $x, y, z, r$ and $x', y', z', r'$ be the center coordinates and radii of two spheres in three-dimensional space **R**<sup>3</sup>. If the spheres are touching each other with same orientation, their equation is given

$$(x-x')^{2}+(y-y')^{2}+(z-z')^{2}-(r-r')^{2}=0.$$

Setting $t=ir$, these coordinates correspond to rectangular coordinates in four-dimensional space **R**<sup>4</sup>:[^o37][^o32]

$$(x-x')^{2}+(y-y')^{2}+(z-z')^{2}+(t-t')^{2}=0.$$

In general, Lie (1871) showed that the conformal point transformations in **R**<sup>n</sup> (composed of motions, similarities, and transformations by reciprocal radii) correspond in **R**<sup>n-1</sup> to those sphere transformations which are contact transformations.[^o38][^o39] Klein (1893) pointed out that by using minimal projection on hexaspherical coordinates, the 15-parameter Lie sphere transformations in **R**<sup>3</sup> are simply the projections of the 15-parameter conformal point transformations in **R**<sup>4</sup>, whereas the points in **R**<sup>4</sup> can be seen as the [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431) of the points of a sphere in **R**<sup>5</sup>.[^o28][^o40]

### Relation to electrodynamics {#Relation_to_electrodynamics}

[Harry Bateman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Bateman) and [Ebenezer Cunningham](wikipedia://en/11767545) (1909)[^o2] showed that the electromagnetic equations are not only Lorentz invariant, but also [scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance) and conformal invariant.[^o42] They are invariant under the 15-parameter group of conformal transformations $G_{15}$ (transformations by reciprocal radii) in **R**<sup>4</sup> producing the relation

$$\delta x^{\prime2}+\delta y^{\prime2}+\delta z^{\prime2}+\delta u^{\prime2}=\lambda\left(\delta x^{2}+\delta y^{2}+\delta z^{2}+\delta u^{2}\right),$$

where $u=ict$ includes $t$ as time component and $c$ as the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light). Bateman (1909) also noticed the equivalence to the previously mentioned Lie sphere transformations in **R**<sup>3</sup>, because the radius $r$ used in them can be interpreted as the radius $ct$ of a spherical wave contracting or expanding with $c$, therefore he called them "spherical wave transformations".[^o43] He wrote:[^o44]

When we use Darboux's representation of a point in $S_4$ by a spherical wave in $S_3$, the group $G_{15}$ becomes the group of spherical wave transformations which transform a spherical wave into a spherical wave. This group of transformations has been discussed by S. Lie; it is the group of transformations which transform lines of curvature on a surface enveloped by spherical waves into lines of curvature on the surface enveloped by the corresponding spherical waves.

Depending on $\lambda$ they can be differentiated into subgroups:[^o45]

(a) $\lambda=1$ correspond to mappings which transform not only spheres into spheres but also planes into planes. These are called Laguerre transformations/inversions forming the Laguerre group, which in physics correspond to the Lorentz transformations forming the 6-parameter [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489) or 10-parameter [Poincaré group](wikipedia://en/230487) with translations.[^o46]

(b) $\lambda\ne1$ represents [scale or similarity transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similarity_transformation_%28geometry%29) by multiplication of the space-time variables of the Lorentz transformations by a constant factor depending on $\lambda$.[^o47] For instance, if $l=\sqrt{\lambda}$ is used, then the transformation given by [Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) in 1905 follows:[^o48]

$$x^{\prime}=\gamma l\left(x-vt\right),\quad y^{\prime}=ly,\quad z^{\prime}=lz,\quad t^{\prime}=\gamma l\left(t-x\frac{v}{c^{2}}\right).$$

However, it was shown by Poincaré and [Einstein](wikipedia://en/736) that only $l=1$ produces a group that is a symmetry of all laws of nature as required by the [principle of relativity](wikipedia://en/74327) (the Lorentz group), while the group of scale transformations is only a symmetry of optics and electrodynamics.

(c) Setting $\lambda=r^{4}/\left(x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+u^{2}\right)^{2}$ particularly relates to the wide conformal group of transformations by reciprocal radii. It consists of elementary transformations that represent a generalized inversion into a four-dimensional [hypersphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere):[^o49]

$$\begin{aligned} x' & =\frac{k^{2}x}{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+u^{2}},\quad & z' & =\frac{k^{2}z}{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+u^{2}},\\ y' & =\frac{k^{2}y}{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+u^{2}}, & u' & =\frac{k^{2}u}{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+u^{2}}, \end{aligned}$$

which become real spherical wave transformations in terms of Lie sphere geometry if the real radius $ct$ is used instead of $u=ict$, thus $x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-c^{2}t^{2}$ is given in the denominator.[^o2]

[Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) (1921) pointed out the similarity of these relations to Lie's and his own researches of 1871, adding that the conformal group doesn't have the same meaning as the Lorentz group, because the former applies to electrodynamics whereas the latter is a symmetry of all laws of nature including mechanics.[^o50] The possibility was discussed for some time, whether conformal transformations allow for the transformation into uniformly accelerated frames.[^o51] Later, conformal invariance became important again in certain areas such as [conformal field theory](wikipedia://en/364774).[^o52]

### Lorentz group isomorphic to Möbius group {#Lorentz_group_isomorphic_to_Möbius_group}

It turns out that also the 6-parameter conformal group of **R**<sup>2</sup> (i.e. the [Möbius group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Möbius_group) composed of [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799)),[^o21] which in turn is isomorphic to the 6-parameter group of [hyperbolic motions](wikipedia://en/1229416) (i.e. [isometric](wikipedia://en/254777) automorphisms of a [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742)) in **R**<sup>3</sup>,[^o54] can be physically interpreted: It is isomorphic to the Lorentz group.

For instance, [Fricke](wikipedia://en/10291191) and Klein (1897) started by defining an "absolute" [Cayley metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cayley_metric) in terms of a one-part curvilinear surface of second degree, which can be represented by a sphere whose interior represents hyperbolic space with the equation[^o55]

$$z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}-z_{4}^{2}=0,$$

where $z_{1},\ z_{2},\ z_{3},\ z_{4}$ are homogeneous coordinates. They pointed out that motions of hyperbolic space into itself also transform this sphere into itself. They developed the corresponding transformation by defining a complex parameter $\xi$ of the sphere[^o56]

$$\xi=\frac{z_{1}+iz_{2}}{z_{4}-z_{3}}$$

which is connected to another parameter $\xi'$ by the substitution

$$\xi'=\frac{\alpha\xi+\beta}{\gamma\xi+\delta}$$

where $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$ are complex coefficients. They furthermore showed that by setting $z_{1}:z_{2}:z_{3}:z_{4}=X:Y:Z:1$, the above relations assume the form in terms of the unit sphere in **R**<sup>3</sup>:[^o57]

$$X^{2}+Y^{2}+Z^{2}=1,\quad\xi=\frac{X+iY}{1-Z}.$$

which is identical to the stereographic projection of the $\xi$-plane on a spherical surface already given by Klein in 1884.[^o58] Since the substitutions $\xi,\xi'$ are [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493) (Kreisverwandtschaften) in the $\xi$-plane or upon the $\xi$-sphere, they concluded that by carrying out an arbitrary motion of hyperbolic space in itself, the $\xi$-sphere undergoes a Möbius transformation, that the entire group of hyperbolic motions gives all direct Möbius transformations, and finally that *any* direct Möbius transformation corresponds to a motion of hyperbolic space.[^o59]

Based on the work of Fricke & Klein, the isomorphism of that group of hyperbolic motions (and consequently of the Möbius group) to the Lorentz group was demonstrated by [Gustav Herglotz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Herglotz) (1909).[^o60] Namely, the Minkowski metric corresponds to the above Cayley metric (based on a real [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673)), if the spacetime coordinates are identified with the above homogeneous coordinates

$$z_{1}=x,\quad z_{2}=y,\quad z_{3}=z,\quad z_{4}=t,$$

by which the above parameter become

$\mathsf{\xi}=\frac{x+iy}{t-z},\quad\xi'=\frac{x'+iy'}{t'-z'},$ again connected by the substitution $\xi'=\frac{\alpha\xi+\beta}{\gamma\xi+\delta}$.

Herglotz concluded, that any such substitution corresponds to a Lorentz transformation, establishing a [one-to-one correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) to hyperbolic motions in **R**<sup>3</sup>. The relation between the Lorentz group and the Cayley metric in hyperbolic space was also pointed out by Klein (1910)[^o61] as well as Pauli (1921).[^o62] The corresponding isomorphism of the Möbius group to the Lorentz group was employed, among others, by [Roger Penrose](wikipedia://en/26193).

## Transformation by reciprocal directions {#Transformation_by_reciprocal_directions}

### Development in the 19th century {#Development_in_the_19th_century}

Above, the connection of conformal transformations with coordinates including the radius of spheres within Lie sphere geometry was mentioned. The special case $\lambda=1$ corresponds to a sphere transformation given by [Edmond Laguerre](wikipedia://en/1011080) (1880–1885), who called it the "transformation by reciprocal directions" and who laid down the foundation of a geometry of oriented spheres *and planes*.[^o65][^o77][^o6] According to Darboux[^o66] and Bateman,[^o67] similar relations were discussed before by [Albert Ribaucour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Ribaucour) (1870)[^o68] and by Lie himself (1871).[^o19] [Stephanos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyparissos_Stephanos) (1881) pointed out that Laguerre's geometry is indeed a special case of Lie's sphere geometry.[^o69] He also represented Laguerre's oriented spheres by [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) (1883).[^o70]

Lines, circles, planes, or spheres with radii of certain orientation are called by Laguerre half-lines, half-circles (cycles), half-planes, half-spheres, etc. A tangent is a half-line cutting a cycle at a point where both have the same direction. The transformation by reciprocal directions transforms oriented spheres into oriented spheres and oriented planes into oriented planes, leaving invariant the "tangential distance" of two cycles (the distance between the points of each one of their common tangents), and also conserves the [lines of curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lines_of_curvature).[^o71] Laguerre (1882) applied the transformation to two cycles under the following conditions: Their [radical axis](wikipedia://en/2093844) is the axis of transformation, and their common tangents are parallel to two fixed directions of the half-lines that are transformed into themselves (Laguerre called this specific method the "transformation by reciprocal half-lines", which was later called "Laguerre inversion"[^o72][^o73]). Setting $R$ and $R'$ as the radii of the cycles, and $D$ and $D'$ as the distances of their centers to the axis, he obtained:[^o74]

$$D^{2}-D^{\prime2}=R^{2}-R^{\prime2},\quad D-D'=\alpha(R-R'),\quad D+D'=\frac{1}{\alpha}(R+R'),$$

with the transformation:[^o75]

$$D'=\frac{D\left(1+\alpha^{2}\right)-2\alpha R}{1-\alpha^{2}},\quad R'=\frac{2\alpha D-R\left(1+\alpha^{2}\right)}{1-\alpha^{2}}.$$

Darboux (1887) obtained the same formulas in different notation (with $z=D$ and $k=\alpha$) in his treatment of the "transformation by reciprocal directions", though he included the $x$ and $y$ coordinates as well:[^o76]

$$\begin{aligned} x' & =x,\quad & z' & =\frac{1+k^{2}}{1-k^{2}}z-\frac{2kR}{1-k^{2}},\\ y' & =y, & R' & =\frac{2kz}{1-k^{2}}-\frac{1+k^{2}}{1-k^{2}}R, \end{aligned}$$

with

$$z'+R' =\frac{1+k}{1-k}(z-R),\quad z'-R' =\frac{1-k}{1+k}(z+R),$$

consequently he obtained the relation

$$x^{\prime2}+y^{\prime2}+z^{\prime2}-R^{\prime2}=x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-R^{2}.$$

As mentioned above, oriented spheres in **R**<sup>3</sup> can be represented by points of four-dimensional space **R**<sup>4</sup> using minimal (isotropy) projection, which became particularly important in Laguerre's geometry.[^o77] For instance, [E. Müller](wikipedia://en/29039777) (1898) based his discussion of oriented spheres on the fact that they can be mapped upon the points of a plane manifold of four dimensions (which he likened to Fiedler's "cyclography" from 1882). He systematically compared the transformations by reciprocal radii (calling it "inversion at a sphere") with the transformations by reciprocal directions (calling it "inversion at a plane sphere complex").[^o78] Following Müller's paper, [Smith](wikipedia://en/42762166) (1900) discussed Laguerre's transformation and the related "group of the geometry of reciprocal directions". Alluding to Klein's (1893) treatment of minimal projection, he pointed out that this group "is simply isomorphic with the group of all displacements and symmetry transformations in space of four dimensions".[^o79] Smith obtained the same transformation as Laguerre and Darboux in different notation, calling it "inversion into a spherical complex":[^o80]

$$p'=\frac{\kappa^{2}+1}{\kappa^{2}-1}p-\frac{2\kappa}{\kappa^{2}-1}R,\quad R'=\frac{2\kappa}{\kappa^{2}-1}p-\frac{\kappa^{2}+1}{\kappa^{2}-1}R$$

with the relations

$$\kappa=\frac{R'-R}{p'-p},\quad p^{\prime2}-p^{2}=R^{\prime2}-R^{2}.$$

### Laguerre inversion and Lorentz transformation {#Laguerre_inversion_and_Lorentz_transformation}

In 1905 both Poincaré and Einstein pointed out that the [Lorentz transformation](wikipedia://en/18404) of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) (setting $c=1$)

$$x'=\frac{x-vt}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}},\quad y'=y,\quad z'=z,\quad t'=\frac{t-vx}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}$$

leaves the relation $x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-t^{2}$ invariant.[^o4] Einstein stressed the point that by this transformation a spherical light wave in one frame is transformed into a spherical light wave in another one.[^o82] Poincaré showed that the Lorentz transformation can be seen as a rotation in four-dimensional space with time as fourth coordinate, with [Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537) deepening this insight much further (see [History of special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_special_relativity)).

As shown above, also Laguerre's transformation by reciprocal directions or half-lines – later called Laguerre inversion[^o72][^o73] – in the form given by Darboux (1887) leaves the expression $x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-R^{2}$ invariant. Subsequently, the relation to the Lorentz transformation was noted by several authors. For instance, Bateman (1910) argued that this transformation (which he attributed to Ribaucour) is "identical" to the Lorentz transformation.[^o67] In particular, he argued (1912) that the variant given by Darboux (1887) corresponds to the Lorentz transformation in $z$ direction, if $R=ct$, $R'=ct'$, and the $k$ terms are replaced by velocities.[^o83] Bateman (1910) also sketched geometric representations of relativistic light spheres using such spherical systems.[^o84][^o85] However, [Kubota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tadahiko_Kubota) (1925) responded to Bateman by arguing that the Laguerre inversion is [involutory](wikipedia://en/293450) whereas the Lorentz transformation is not. He concluded that in order to make them equivalent, the Laguerre inversion has to be combined with a reversal of direction of the cycles.[^o86]

The specific relation between the Lorentz transformation and the Laguerre inversion can also be demonstrated as follows (see [H.R. Müller](wikipedia://en/42962760) (1948)[^o87] for analogous formulas in different notation). Laguerre's inversion formulas from 1882 (equivalent to those of Darboux in 1887) read:

$$D'=\frac{D\left(1+\alpha^{2}\right)-2\alpha R}{1-\alpha^{2}},\quad R'=\frac{2\alpha D-R\left(1+\alpha^{2}\right)}{1-\alpha^{2}}.$$

by setting

$$\frac{2\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}=w$$

it follows

$$\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}}=\sqrt{1-w^{2}},\quad\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha^{2}}=\frac{w}{\sqrt{1-w^{2}}},$$

finally by setting $D=x,D'=x',R=t,R'=t'$ the Laguerre inversion becomes very similar to the Lorentz transformation except that the expression $t-vx$ is reversed into $wx-t$:

$$x'=\frac{x-wt}{\sqrt{1-w^{2}}},\quad t'=\frac{wx-t}{\sqrt{1-w^{2}}}.$$

According to Müller, the Lorentz transformation can be seen as the product of an even number of such Laguerre inversions that change the sign. First an inversion is conducted into plane $\pi_1$ which is inclined with respect to plane $\pi$ under a certain angle, followed by another inversion back to $\pi$.[^o87] See section #Laguerre group isomorphic to Lorentz group for more details of the connection between the Laguerre inversion to other variants of Laguerre transformations.

### Lorentz transformation within Laguerre geometry {#Lorentz_transformation_within_Laguerre_geometry}

[Timerding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Emil_Timerding) (1911)[^o89] used Laguerre's concept of oriented spheres in order to represent and derive the Lorentz transformation. Given a sphere of radius $r$, with $x$ as the distance between its center and the central plane, he obtained the relations to a corresponding sphere

$$x'+r'=\sqrt{\frac{1+\lambda^{2}}{1-\lambda^{2}}}(x+r),\quad\frac{x'-r'}{x'+r'}=\frac{1-\lambda}{1+\lambda}\cdot\frac{x-r}{x+r},$$

resulting in the transformation

$$\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\cdot x'=x-\lambda r,\quad\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\cdot r'=r-\lambda x.$$

By setting $\lambda=v/c$ and $r=ct$, it becomes the Lorentz transformation.

Following Timerding and Bateman, [Ogura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinnosuke_Ogura) (1913) analyzed a Laguerre transformation of the form[^o90]

$$\alpha'=\alpha\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}}-R\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}},\quad\beta'=\beta,\quad\gamma'=\gamma,\quad R'=\alpha\frac{-\lambda}{\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}}+R\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}},$$

which become the Lorentz transformation with

$\begin{aligned} x & =\alpha, & y & =\beta, & z & =\gamma, & R & =ct,\\ x' & =\alpha', & y' & =\beta', & z' & =\gamma', & R' & =ct', \end{aligned}$ $\lambda=\frac{v}{c}$.

He stated that "the Laguerre transformation in sphere manifoldness is equivalent to the Lorentz transformation in spacetime manifoldness".

### Laguerre group isomorphic to Lorentz group {#Laguerre_group_isomorphic_to_Lorentz_group}

As shown above, the group of conformal point transformations in **R**<sup>n</sup> (composed of motions, similarities, and inversions) can be related by minimal projection to the group of contact transformations in **R**<sup>n-1</sup> transforming circles or spheres into other circles or spheres. In addition, Lie (1871, 1896) pointed out that in **R**<sup>3</sup> there is a 7-parameter subgroup of point transformations composed of motions and similarities, which by using minimal projection corresponds to a 7-parameter subgroup of [contact transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_transformation) in **R**<sup>2</sup> transforming circles into circles.[^o92] These relations were further studied by [Smith](wikipedia://en/42762166) (1900),[^o79] [Blaschke](wikipedia://en/728998) (1910),[^o93] [Coolidge](wikipedia://en/4379392) (1916)[^o94] and others, who pointed out the connection to Laguerre's geometry of reciprocal directions related to oriented lines, circles, planes and spheres. Therefore, Smith (1900) called it the "group of the geometry of reciprocal directions",[^o79] and Blaschke (1910) used the expression "Laguerre group".[^o93] The "extended Laguerre group" consists of motions and similarities, having 7 parameters in **R**<sup>2</sup> transforming oriented lines and circles, or 11 parameters in **R**<sup>3</sup> transforming oriented planes and spheres. If similarities are excluded, it becomes the "restricted Laguerre group" having 6 parameters in **R**<sup>2</sup> and 10 parameters in **R**<sup>3</sup>, consisting of orientation-preserving or orientation-reversing motions, and preserving the tangential distance between oriented circles or spheres.[^o95][^o96] Subsequently, it became common that the term Laguerre group only refers to the restricted Laguerre group.[^o96][^o97] It was also noted that the Laguerre group is part of a wider group conserving tangential distances, called the "equilong group" by [Scheffers](wikipedia://en/31778680) (1905).[^o98][^o99]

In **R**<sup>2</sup> the Laguerre group leaves invariant the relation $dx^{2}+dy^{2}-dr^{2}$, which can be extended to arbitrary **R**<sup>n</sup> as well.[^o100] For instance, in **R**<sup>3</sup> it leaves invariant the relation $dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2}-dr^{2}$.[^o101] This is equivalent to relation $dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2}+dr^{2}$ in **R**<sup>4</sup> by using minimal (isotropy) projection with [imaginary](wikipedia://en/14554) radius coordinate, or cyclographic projection (in [descriptive geometry](wikipedia://en/484891)) with real radius coordinate.[^o102] The transformations forming the Laguerre group can be further differentiated into "direct Laguerre transformations" which are related to motions preserving both the tangential distance as well as the sign; or "indirect Laguerre transformations" which are related to orientation-reversing motions, preserving the tangential distance with the sign reversed.[^o98][^o103] The Laguerre inversion first given by Laguerre in 1882 is [involutory](wikipedia://en/293450), thus it belongs to the indirect Laguerre transformations. Laguerre himself did not discuss the group related to his inversion, but it turned out that every Laguerre transformation can be generated by at most four Laguerre inversions and every direct Laguerre transformation is the product of two involutory transformations, thus Laguerre inversions are of special importance because they are generating operators of the entire Laguerre group.[^o104][^o105]

It was noted that the Laguerre group is indeed [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to the Lorentz group (or the [Poincaré group](wikipedia://en/230487) if translations are included), as both groups leave invariant the form $dx_{1}^{2}+dx_{2}^{2}+dx_{3}^{2}-dx_{4}^{2}$. After the first comparison of the Lorentz transformation and the Laguerre inversion by Bateman (1910) as mentioned above, the equivalence of both groups was pointed out by [Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) in 1912[^o106] and 1914,[^o107] and he expanded upon it in 1915 (published 1955) in the French version of [Klein's encyclopedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein's_encyclopedia).[^o8] Also Poincaré (1912, published 1921) wrote:[^o108][^o109]

Mr. Cartan has recently given a curious example. We know the importance in mathematical physics of what has been called the Lorentz group; it is this group upon which our new ideas on the principle of relativity and the dynamics of the electron are based. On the other hand, Laguerre once introduced into geometry a group of transformations that change the spheres into spheres. These two groups are isomorphic, so that mathematically these two theories, one physical, the other one geometric, show no essential difference.[^o110]

Others who noticed this connection include [Coolidge](wikipedia://en/4379392) (1916),[^o102] [Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) & [Blaschke](wikipedia://en/728998) (1926),[^o9] Blaschke (1929),[^o93] [H.R.
