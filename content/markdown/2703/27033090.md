# Unit sphere

*Image: Unit disc 2-norm qtl1.svg.* Unit ball (red) and unit sphere (blue) for the Euclidean norm in two dimensions.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unit sphere"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **unit sphere** is a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) of unit [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815): the [set of points](wikipedia://en/542465) at [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) [1](wikipedia://en/77591639) from some [center point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_%28geometry%29) in [three-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/3054853). More generally, the *unit $n$-sphere* is an [$n$-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) of unit radius in $(n+1)$-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697); the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) is a special case, the unit $1$-sphere in the [plane](wikipedia://en/15575410). An ([open](wikipedia://en/39358)) **unit ball** is the region inside of a unit sphere, the set of points of distance less than 1 from the center.

:::

A unit sphere or unit ball with center at the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) of the space is called *the* (canonical) unit sphere or *the* (canonical) unit ball. Any arbitrary sphere can be transformed to the unit sphere by a combination of [translation](wikipedia://en/204682) and [scaling](wikipedia://en/651752), so the study of spheres in general can often be reduced to the study of the unit sphere.

The unit sphere is often used as a model for [spherical geometry](wikipedia://en/174026) because it has constant [sectional curvature](wikipedia://en/285618) of 1, which simplifies calculations. In [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261), circular [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) on the unit circle is called [radians](wikipedia://en/26003) and used for measuring [angular distance](wikipedia://en/3720362); in [spherical trigonometry](wikipedia://en/650405) surface area on the unit sphere is called [steradians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steradian) and used for measuring [solid angle](wikipedia://en/94102).

In more general contexts, a *unit sphere* is the set of points of [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) 1 from a fixed central point, where different [norms](wikipedia://en/990534) can be used as general notions of "distance", and an (open) *unit ball* is the region inside.

*Image: Vector norms.svg.* Some 1-spheres: $\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}$ is the norm for Euclidean space.

## Unit spheres and balls in Euclidean space {#Unit_spheres_and_balls_in_Euclidean_space}

In [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) of $n$ dimensions, the $(n-1)$-dimensional unit sphere is the set of all points $(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ which satisfy the equation

$$x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots + x_n ^2 = 1.$$

The open unit $n$-ball is the set of all points satisfying the [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489)

$$x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots + x_n ^2 < 1,$$

and closed unit $n$-ball is the set of all points satisfying the inequality

$$x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots + x_n ^2 \le 1.$$

### Volume and area {#Volume_and_area}

*Image: Graphs of volumes (V) and surface areas (S) of n-balls of radius 1.png.* Graphs of volumes ($V$) and surface areas ($S$) of unit $n$-balls

The classical equation of a unit sphere is that of the ellipsoid with a radius of 1 and no alterations to the $x$-, $y$-, or $z$- axes:

$$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1$$

The volume of the unit ball in Euclidean $n$-space, and the surface area of the unit sphere, appear in many important formulas of [analysis](wikipedia://en/48396). The volume of the unit $n$-ball, which we denote $V_n,$ can be expressed by making use of the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316). It is

$$V_n = \frac{\pi ^ {n/2}}{\Gamma(1+n/2)} = \begin{cases} {\pi^{n/2}}/{(n/2)!} & \mathrm{if~}n \ge 0\mathrm{~is~even} \\[6mu] {2(2\pi)^{(n-1)/2}}/{n!!} & \mathrm{if~}n \ge 0\mathrm{~is~odd,} \end{cases}$$

where $n!!$ is the [double factorial](wikipedia://en/507208).

The hypervolume of the $(n-1)$-dimensional unit sphere (*i.e.*, the "area" of the boundary of the $n$-dimensional unit ball), which we denote $A_{n-1},$ can be expressed as

$$A_{n-1} = n V_n = \frac{n \pi ^ {n/2}}{\Gamma(1+n/2)} = \frac{2 \pi ^ {n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2)} = \begin{cases} {2 \pi^{n/2}}/{(n/2 - 1)!} & \mathrm{if~}n \ge 1\mathrm{~is~even} \\[6mu] {2(2\pi)^{(n-1)/2}}/{(n-2)!!} & \mathrm{if~}n \ge 1\mathrm{~is~odd.} \end{cases}$$

For example, $A_0 = 2$ is the "area" of the boundary of the unit ball $[-1,1] \subset \mathbb{R}$, which simply counts the two points. Then $A_1 = 2\pi$ is the "area" of the boundary of the unit disc, which is the circumference of the unit circle. $A_2 = 4\pi$ is the area of the boundary of the unit ball $\{ x \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 \leq 1 \}$, which is the surface area of the unit sphere $\{ x \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 = 1 \}$.

The surface areas and the volumes for some values of $n$ are as follows:

#### Recursion {#Recursion}

The $A_n$ values satisfy the recursion:

$$A_0 = 2$$

$$A_1 = 2\pi$$

$$A_n = \frac{2 \pi}{n-1} A_{n-2}$$

for $n > 1$.

The $V_n$ values satisfy the recursion:

$$V_0 = 1$$

$$V_1 = 2$$

$$V_n = \frac{2 \pi}{n} V_{n-2}$$

for $n > 1$.

#### Non-negative real-valued dimensions {#Non-negative_real-valued_dimensions}

The value $2^{-n} V_n = \pi ^ {n/2} \big/\, 2^n \Gamma\bigl(1+\tfrac12n\bigr)$ at non-negative real values of $n$ is sometimes used for normalization of Hausdorff measure.[^o7][^o8]

#### Other radii {#Other_radii}

The surface area of an $(n-1)$-sphere with radius $r$ is $A_{n-1} r^{n-1}$ and the volume of an $n$-ball with radius $r$ is $V_{n} r^{n}.$ For instance, the area is $A_2 = 4\pi r^2$ for the two-dimensional surface of the three-dimensional ball of radius $r.$ The volume is $V_3 = \tfrac43\pi r^3$ for the three-dimensional ball of radius $r$.

## Unit balls in normed vector spaces {#Unit_balls_in_normed_vector_spaces}

The **open unit ball** of a [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538) $V$ with the [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) $\|\cdot\|$ is given by

$$\{ x\in V: \|x\|<1 \}$$

It is the [topological interior](wikipedia://en/55610) of the **closed unit ball** of $(V, \|\cdot\|)\colon$

$$\{ x\in V: \|x\|\le 1\}$$

The latter is the disjoint union of the former and their common border, the **unit sphere** of $(V, \|\cdot\|)\colon$

$$\{ x\in V: \|x\| = 1 \}$$

The "shape" of the *unit ball* is entirely dependent on the chosen norm; it may well have "corners", and for example may look like $[-1,1]^n$ in the case of the max-norm in $\mathbb{R}^n$. One obtains a naturally *round ball* as the unit ball pertaining to the usual [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) norm, based in the finite-dimensional case on the [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932); its boundary is what is usually meant by the *unit sphere*.

Let $x=(x_1,...x_n)\in \mathbb{R}^n.$ Define the usual $\ell_p$-norm for $p \ge 1$ as:

$$\|x\|_p = \biggl(\sum_{k=1}^n |x_k|^p \biggr)^{1/p}$$

Then $\|x\|_2$ is the usual [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) norm. $\|x\|_1$ is called the Hamming norm, or $\ell_1$-norm. The condition $p \geq 1$ is necessary in the definition of the $\ell_p$ norm, as the unit ball in any normed space must be [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) as a consequence of the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941). Let $\|x\|_\infty$ denote the max-norm or $\ell_\infty$-norm of $x$.

Note that for the one-dimensional circumferences $C_p$ of the two-dimensional unit balls, we have:

$$C_{1} = 4 \sqrt{2}$$

is the minimum value.

$$C_{2} = 2 \pi$$

$$C_{\infty} = 8$$

is the maximum value.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### Metric spaces {#Metric_spaces}

All three of the above definitions can be straightforwardly generalized to a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018), with respect to a chosen origin. However, topological considerations (interior, closure, border) need not apply in the same way (e.g., in [ultrametric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrametric) spaces, all of the three are simultaneously open and closed sets), and the unit sphere may even be empty in some metric spaces.

### Quadratic forms {#Quadratic_forms}

If $V$ is a linear space with a real [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) $F : V \to \mathbb{R},$ then $\{ p \in V : F(p) = 1\}$ may be called the unit sphere[^o15][^o16] or [unit quasi-sphere](wikipedia://en/145844#Relation_to_the_sphere) of $V.$ For example, the quadratic form $x^2 - y^2$, when set equal to one, produces the [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122), which plays the role of the "unit circle" in the plane of [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559). Similarly, the quadratic form $x^2$ yields a pair of lines for the unit sphere in the [dual number](wikipedia://en/42169) plane.

:::
