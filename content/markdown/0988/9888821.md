# Trimean

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Trimean"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) the **trimean** (**TM**), or **Tukey's trimean**, is a measure of a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543)'s [central tendency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_tendency) defined as a [weighted average](wikipedia://en/189822) of the distribution's [quartiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartiles):

:::

$$TM= \frac{Q_1 + 2Q_2 + Q_3}{4}$$

It gives twice as much weight to the [median](wikipedia://en/18837) or second quartile $Q_2$ than the first and third quartiles.

This is equivalent to the [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612) of the [median](wikipedia://en/18837) and the [midhinge](wikipedia://en/12559595):

$$TM= \frac{1}{2}\left(Q_2 + \frac{Q_1 + Q_3}{2}\right)$$

The foundations of the trimean were part of [Arthur Bowley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Bowley)'s teachings, and later popularized by statistician [John Tukey](wikipedia://en/415974) in his 1977 book[^o2] which has given its name to a set of techniques called [exploratory data analysis](wikipedia://en/416589).

Like the median and the midhinge, but unlike the [sample mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_mean), it is a [statistically resistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistically_resistant) [L-estimator](wikipedia://en/17398955) with a [breakdown point](wikipedia://en/2885691#Breakdown_point) of 25%. This beneficial property has been described as follows: An advantage of the trimean as a measure of the center (of a distribution) is that it combines the [median](wikipedia://en/18837)'s emphasis on center values with the [midhinge](wikipedia://en/12559595)'s attention to the extremes.

## Efficiency {#Efficiency}

Despite its simplicity, the trimean is a remarkably [efficient](wikipedia://en/27250866) estimator of population mean. More precisely, for a large data set (over 100 points[^n1]) from a symmetric population, the average of the 18th, 50th, and 82nd percentile is the most efficient 3-point L-estimator, with 88% efficiency.[^o6] For context, the best single point estimate by L-estimators is the median, with an efficiency of 64% or better (for all *n*), while using two points (for a large data set of over 100 points from a symmetric population), the most efficient estimate is the 27% [midsummary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midsummary) (mean of 27th and 73rd percentiles), which has an efficiency of about 81%. Using quartiles, these optimal estimators can be approximated by the midhinge and the trimean. Using further points yield higher efficiency, though it is notable that only three points are needed for very high efficiency.
