# Lisa Piccirillo

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Lisa Piccirillo"}

**Lisa Marie Piccirillo** (born 1990 or 1991)[^o3] is an American mathematician specializing in [low-dimensional topology](wikipedia://en/621774). She is a Professor and holds the Sid W. Richardson Foundation Regents Chair in Mathematics at the [University of Texas at Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_Austin).[^o4] She is known for solving a long-standing problem in [knot theory](wikipedia://en/153008) by proving that the [Conway knot](wikipedia://en/3727038) is not [smoothly slice](wikipedia://en/4158126#Slice_knots_and_the_knot_concordance_group).[^o5][^o6]

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Piccirillo was raised in [Greenwood, Maine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenwood,_Maine), and attended [Telstar Regional High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telstar_Regional_High_School) in [Bethel, Maine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bethel,_Maine).[^o8] Her mother was a middle school math teacher. As a child, her hobbies included riding [dressage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dressage), church youth group activities, drama, and band.[^o3]

She earned a B.S. in mathematics from [Boston College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_College) in 2013 and a PhD in low-dimensional topology at the [University of Texas at Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_Austin) under the supervision of [John Edwin Luecke](wikipedia://en/29631637) in 2019.[^o9] Boston College professor [Elisenda Grigsby](wikipedia://en/54248237) noted Piccirillo's creativity as a key to her success, adding that Piccirillo did not fit the mold of a "standard golden child math prodigy" during her undergraduate studies.[^o6]

## Career {#Career}

After earning her doctorate, Piccirillo was a postdoctoral researcher at [Brandeis University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandeis_University).[^o11] Following the publication of her proof on the Conway knot, she was offered a tenure-track position at the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology), where she served as an assistant professor of mathematics from 2020 to 2024.[^o12] In 2024, she returned to the University of Texas at Austin as a full professor.[^o4]

### The Conway knot problem {#The_Conway_knot_problem}

*Image: Conway knot.png.* The Conway Knot

The Conway knot is named after its discoverer, English mathematician [John Horton Conway](wikipedia://en/15807), who first described it in 1970. While the knot was determined to be [topologically slice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologically_slice) in the 1980s, the question of whether it was [smoothly slice](wikipedia://en/4158126#Slice_knots_and_the_knot_concordance_group) remained unanswered for fifty years, becoming a famous unsolved problem in knot theory.[^o6][^o14]

Piccirillo's proof, developed while she was a graduate student, completed the classification of slice knots with fewer than 13 crossings.[^o5] Her work was hailed for its creative methodology; instead of attacking the problem directly, she constructed a "trace" of the Conway knot and found a different knot that shared this trace but was already known not to be smoothly slice. This novel approach demonstrated that the Conway knot was also not slice and, according to *The Washington Post*, "could point to new ways to understand knots."[^o14]

Piccirillo learned of the problem at a [2018 conference](https://web.ma.utexas.edu/conferences/gompf/?) and reportedly found the solution in less than a week.[^o6] In an interview, she described her initial approach to the problem:

> I think the next day, which was a Sunday, I just started trying to run the approach for fun and I worked on it a bit in the evenings just to try to see what's supposed to be hard about this problem.[^o14]

A few days later, she met with [Cameron Gordon](wikipedia://en/4695115), a professor at UT Austin (a senior topologist), and casually mentioned her solution.[^o15] Reflecting on the nature of her discovery in an interview with *[Quanta Magazine](wikipedia://en/51804976)*, Piccirillo noted the specificity of her proof in a field that often favors broad generalizations:

> It was quite surprising to me. I mean, it's just one knot. In general, when mathematicians prove things, we like to prove really broad, general statements: All objects like this have some property. And I proved like, one knot has a thing. I don't care about knots. So, I do care about three and four dimensional spaces, though. And it turns out that, when you want to study three and four dimensional spaces, you find yourself studying knots anyway...[^o15]

## Recognition {#Recognition}

For her work on the Conway knot, Piccirillo has received several prestigious awards:

- In 2021, she was one of the inaugural recipients of the [Maryam Mirzakhani New Frontiers Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakthrough_Prize#New_Horizons_in_Mathematics_Prize), which recognizes outstanding early-career women mathematicians.[^o17]
- In 2021, she was awarded a [Clay Research Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clay_Research_Fellowship), given to recent PhDs of unusual promise,[^o18] and a [Sloan Research Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloan_Research_Fellowship), which honors "early-career researchers of outstanding promise."[^o19]

In 2020, the UK magazine *[Prospect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prospect_%28magazine%29)* included her on its list of "The world's top 50 thinkers for the Covid-19 age."[^o20]
