# List of spherical symmetry groups

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="List of spherical symmetry groups"}

Finite spherical symmetry groups are also called [point groups in three dimensions](wikipedia://en/2694525). There are five fundamental symmetry classes which have triangular fundamental domains: [dihedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihedral_symmetry), [cyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_symmetry), [tetrahedral](wikipedia://en/2904472), [octahedral](wikipedia://en/2900805), and [icosahedral](wikipedia://en/2877844) symmetry.

:::

This article lists the groups by [Schoenflies notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schoenflies_notation), [Coxeter notation](wikipedia://en/32088502),[^o2] [orbifold notation](wikipedia://en/2915678),[^o3] and order. [John Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) used a variation of the Schoenflies notation, based on the groups' [quaternion](wikipedia://en/51440) algebraic structure, labeled by one or two upper case letters, and whole number subscripts. The group order is defined as the subscript, unless the order is doubled for symbols with a plus or minus, "±", prefix, which implies a [central inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_inversion).[^o4]

[Hermann–Mauguin notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann–Mauguin_notation) (International notation) is also given. The [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography) groups, 32 in total, are a subset with element orders 2, 3, 4 and 6.[^o5]

## Involutional symmetry {#Involutional_symmetry}

There are four [involutional](wikipedia://en/293450) groups: no symmetry (C<sub>1</sub>), [reflection symmetry](wikipedia://en/1390873) (C<sub>s</sub>), 2-fold rotational symmetry (C<sub>2</sub>), and central [point symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_symmetry) (C<sub>i</sub>).

## Continuous symmetries {#Continuous_symmetries}

All of the discrete point symmetries are subgroups of certain continuous symmetries. They can be classified as products of [orthogonal groups](wikipedia://en/173954) O(*n*) or [special orthogonal groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) SO(*n*). O(1) is a single orthogonal reflection, [dihedral symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihedral_symmetry) order 2, Dih<sub>1</sub>. SO(1) is just the identity. Half turns, C<sub>2</sub>, are needed to complete.
