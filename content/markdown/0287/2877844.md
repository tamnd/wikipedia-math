# Icosahedral symmetry

*Image: Icosahedral reflection domains.png.* Icosahedral symmetry fundamental domains

*Image: Soccer ball.svg.* A [soccer ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_%28association_football%29), a common example of a [spherical](wikipedia://en/10393861) [truncated icosahedron](wikipedia://en/31282), has full icosahedral symmetry.

*Image: Sixteenth stellation of icosahedron.png.* Rotations and reflections form the symmetry group of a [great icosahedron](wikipedia://en/3029898).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Icosahedral symmetry"}

In mathematics, and especially in geometry, an object has **icosahedral symmetry** if it has the same [symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetries) as a [regular icosahedron](wikipedia://en/14968). Examples of other [polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedra) with icosahedral symmetry include the [regular dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/7149361) (the [dual](wikipedia://en/8815) of the icosahedron) and the [rhombic triacontahedron](wikipedia://en/665025).

:::

Every polyhedron with icosahedral symmetry has 60 [rotational](wikipedia://en/701096) (or orientation-preserving) symmetries and 60 orientation-reversing symmetries (that combine a rotation and a [reflection](wikipedia://en/1390873)), for a total [symmetry order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_order) of 120. The full [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) is the [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004) of type $H_{3}$. It may be represented by [Coxeter notation](wikipedia://en/32088502) $[5,3]$ and [Coxeter diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram) . The set of rotational symmetries forms a subgroup that is isomorphic to the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) $A_{5}$ on 5 letters.

## As point group {#As_point_group}

Apart from the two infinite series of prismatic and antiprismatic symmetry, *rotational icosahedral symmetry* or *chiral icosahedral symmetry* of [chiral](wikipedia://en/32703814) objects and *full icosahedral symmetry* or *achiral icosahedral symmetry* are the [discrete point symmetries](wikipedia://en/2694525) (or equivalently, [symmetries on the sphere](wikipedia://en/2871265)) with the largest [symmetry groups](wikipedia://en/28186).

Icosahedral symmetry is not compatible with [translational symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translational_symmetry), so there are no associated [crystallographic point groups](wikipedia://en/456410#Overview_of_point_groups_by_crystal_system) or [space groups](wikipedia://en/463721).

[Presentations](wikipedia://en/99494) corresponding to the above are:

$$I: \langle s,t \mid s^2, t^3, (st)^5 \rangle\$$

$$I_h: \langle s,t\mid s^3(st)^{-2}, t^5(st)^{-2}\rangle.\$$

These correspond to the icosahedral groups (rotational and full) being the (2,3,5) [triangle groups](wikipedia://en/2069342).

The first presentation was given by [William Rowan Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817) in 1856, in his paper on [icosian calculus](wikipedia://en/316081).[^o4]

Note that other presentations are possible, for instance as an [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) (for *I*).

## Group structure {#Group_structure}

Every [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) with icosahedral symmetry has 60 [rotational](wikipedia://en/701096) (or orientation-preserving) symmetries and 60 orientation-reversing symmetries (that combine a rotation and a [reflection](wikipedia://en/1390873)), for a total [symmetry order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_order) of 120.

:::

The **icosahedral rotation group** ***I*** is of order 60. The group *I* is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to *A*<sub>5</sub>, the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) of even permutations of five objects. This isomorphism can be realized by *I* acting on various compounds, notably the [compound of five cubes](wikipedia://en/8010502) (which inscribe in the [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407)), the [compound of five octahedra](wikipedia://en/8010546), or either of the two [compounds of five tetrahedra](wikipedia://en/3645795) (which are [enantiomorphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enantiomorphs), and inscribe in the dodecahedron). The group contains 5 versions of *T*<sub>h</sub> with 20 versions of *D<sub>3</sub>* (10 axes, 2 per axis), and 6 versions of *D<sub>5</sub>*.

The **full icosahedral group** ***I<sub>h</sub>*** has order 120. It has *I* as [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of [index](wikipedia://en/230581) 2. The group *I<sub>h</sub>* is isomorphic to *I* × *Z*<sub>2</sub>, or *A*<sub>5</sub> × *Z*<sub>2</sub>, with the [inversion in the center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inversion_in_a_point) corresponding to element (identity,-1), where *Z*<sub>2</sub> is written multiplicatively.

*I<sub>h</sub>* acts on the [compound of five cubes](wikipedia://en/8010502) and the [compound of five octahedra](wikipedia://en/8010546), but −1 acts as the identity (as cubes and octahedra are centrally symmetric). It acts on the [compound of ten tetrahedra](wikipedia://en/8010465): *I* acts on the two chiral halves ([compounds of five tetrahedra](wikipedia://en/3645795)), and −1 interchanges the two halves. Notably, it does *not* act as S<sub>5</sub>, and these groups are not isomorphic; see below for details.

The group contains 10 versions of *D<sub>3d</sub>* and 6 versions of *D<sub>5d</sub>* (symmetries like antiprisms).

*I* is also isomorphic to PSL<sub>2</sub>(5), but *I<sub>h</sub>* is not isomorphic to SL<sub>2</sub>(5).

### Isomorphism of *I* with A<sub>5</sub> {#Isomorphism_of_I_with_A5}

It is useful to describe explicitly what the isomorphism between *I* and A<sub>5</sub> looks like. In the following table, permutations P<sub>i</sub> and Q<sub>i</sub> act on 5 and 12 elements respectively, while the rotation matrices M<sub>i</sub> are the elements of *I*. If P<sub>k</sub> is the product of taking the permutation P<sub>i</sub> and applying P<sub>j</sub> to it, then for the same values of *i*, *j* and *k*, it is also true that Q<sub>k</sub> is the product of taking Q<sub>i</sub> and applying Q<sub>j</sub>, and also that premultiplying a vector by M<sub>k</sub> is the same as premultiplying that vector by M<sub>i</sub> and then premultiplying that result with M<sub>j</sub>, that is M<sub>k</sub> = M<sub>j</sub> × M<sub>i</sub>. Since the permutations P<sub>i</sub> are all the 60 even permutations of 12345, the [one-to-one correspondence](wikipedia://en/3942) is made explicit, therefore the isomorphism too.

The proof of the [Abel–Ruffini theorem](wikipedia://en/152518) uses this simple fact,[^n1] and [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) wrote a book that made use of the theory of icosahedral symmetries to derive an analytical solution to the general quintic equation.[^o16] A modern exposition is given in (Tóth 2002).[^o18]

### Commonly confused groups {#Commonly_confused_groups}

The following groups all have order 120, but are not isomorphic:

- *S*<sub>5</sub>, the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) on 5 elements
- *I<sub>h</sub>*, the full icosahedral group (subject of this article, also known as *H*<sub>3</sub>)
- 2*I*, the [binary icosahedral group](wikipedia://en/3195145)

They correspond to the following [short exact sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) (the latter of which does not split) and product

$$1\to A_5 \to S_5 \to Z_2 \to 1$$

$$I_h = A_5 \times Z_2$$

$$1\to Z_2 \to 2I\to A_5 \to 1$$

In words,

- $A_5$ is a *[normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918)* of $S_5$
- $A_5$ is a *factor* of $I_h$, which is a *[direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015)*
- $A_5$ is a *[quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526)* of $2I$

Note that $A_5$ has an [exceptional](wikipedia://en/5280515) irreducible 3-dimensional [representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_representation) (as the icosahedral rotation group), but $S_5$ does not have an irreducible 3-dimensional representation, corresponding to the full icosahedral group not being the symmetric group.

These can also be related to linear groups over the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) with five elements, which exhibit the subgroups and covering groups directly; none of these are the full icosahedral group:

- $A_5 \cong \operatorname{PSL}(2,5),$ the [projective special linear group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_special_linear_group), see [here](wikipedia://en/382667#Action_on_p_points) for a proof;
- $S_5 \cong \operatorname{PGL}(2,5),$ the [projective general linear group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_general_linear_group);
- $2I \cong \operatorname{SL}(2,5),$ the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823).

#### Vertex stabilizers {#Vertex_stabilizers}

Stabilizers of an opposite pair of vertices can be interpreted as stabilizers of the axis they generate.

- vertex stabilizers in *I* give [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) *C*<sub>3</sub>
- vertex stabilizers in *I<sub>h</sub>* give [dihedral groups](wikipedia://en/2922089) *D*<sub>3</sub>
- stabilizers of an opposite pair of vertices in *I* give dihedral groups *D*<sub>3</sub>
- stabilizers of an opposite pair of vertices in *I<sub>h</sub>* give $D_3 \times \pm 1$

#### Edge stabilizers {#Edge_stabilizers}

Stabilizers of an opposite pair of edges can be interpreted as stabilizers of the rectangle they generate.

- edges stabilizers in *I* give cyclic groups *Z*<sub>2</sub>
- edges stabilizers in *I<sub>h</sub>* give [Klein four-groups](wikipedia://en/41928) $Z_2 \times Z_2$
- stabilizers of a pair of edges in *I* give [Klein four-groups](wikipedia://en/41928) $Z_2 \times Z_2$; there are 5 of these, given by rotation by 180° in 3 perpendicular axes.
- stabilizers of a pair of edges in *I<sub>h</sub>* give $Z_2 \times Z_2 \times Z_2$; there are 5 of these, given by reflections in 3 perpendicular axes.

#### Face stabilizers {#Face_stabilizers}

Stabilizers of an opposite pair of faces can be interpreted as stabilizers of the [antiprism](wikipedia://en/1851) they generate.

- face stabilizers in *I* give cyclic groups *C*<sub>5</sub>
- face stabilizers in *I<sub>h</sub>* give dihedral groups *D*<sub>5</sub>
- stabilizers of an opposite pair of faces in *I* give dihedral groups *D*<sub>5</sub>
- stabilizers of an opposite pair of faces in *I<sub>h</sub>* give $D_5 \times \pm 1$

#### Polyhedron stabilizers {#Polyhedron_stabilizers}

For each of these, there are 5 conjugate copies, and the conjugation action gives a map, indeed an isomorphism, $I \stackrel{\sim}\to A_5 < S_5$.

- stabilizers of the inscribed tetrahedra in *I* are a copy of *T*
- stabilizers of the inscribed tetrahedra in *I<sub>h</sub>* are a copy of *T*
- stabilizers of the inscribed cubes (or opposite pair of tetrahedra, or octahedra) in *I* are a copy of *T*
- stabilizers of the inscribed cubes (or opposite pair of tetrahedra, or octahedra) in *I<sub>h</sub>* are a copy of *T<sub>h</sub>*

### Liquid crystals with icosahedral symmetry {#Liquid_crystals_with_icosahedral_symmetry}

For the intermediate material phase called [liquid crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_crystals) the existence of icosahedral symmetry was proposed by [H. Kleinert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hagen_Kleinert) and K. Maki[^o43] and its structure was first analyzed in detail in that paper. See the review article [here](http://chemgroups.northwestern.edu/seideman/Publications/The%20liquid-crystalline%20blue%20phases.pdf). In aluminum, the icosahedral structure was discovered experimentally three years after this by [Dan Shechtman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Shechtman), which earned him the Nobel Prize in 2011.

### Icosahedral nanoparticles {#Icosahedral_nanoparticles}

At small sizes, many elements form [icosahedral nanoparticles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icosahedral_nanoparticle), which are often lower in energy than [single crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_crystal).[^o45][^o46]

## Related geometries {#Related_geometries}

Icosahedral symmetry is equivalently the [projective special linear group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_special_linear_group) PSL(2,5), and is the symmetry group of the [modular curve](wikipedia://en/669552) X(5), and more generally PSL(2,*p*) is the symmetry group of the modular curve X(*p*). The modular curve X(5) is geometrically a dodecahedron with a cusp at the center of each polygonal face, which demonstrates the symmetry group.

This geometry, and associated symmetry group, was studied by [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) as the [monodromy groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monodromy_group) of a Belyi surface – a Riemann surface with a holomorphic map to the Riemann sphere, ramified only at 0, 1, and infinity (a [Belyi function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belyi_function)) – the cusps are the points lying over infinity, while the vertices and the centers of each edge lie over 0 and 1; the degree of the covering (number of sheets) equals 5.

Klein's investigations continued with his discovery of order 7 and order 11 symmetries in (Klein 1878) and (Klein 1879) (and associated coverings of degree 7 and 11) and [dessins d'enfants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dessins_d'enfants), the first yielding the [Klein quartic](wikipedia://en/390976), whose associated geometry has a tiling by 24 heptagons (with a cusp at the center of each).

Similar geometries occur for PSL(2,*n*) and more general groups for other modular curves.

More exotically, there are special connections between the groups PSL(2,5) (order 60), [PSL(2,7)](wikipedia://en/390426) (order 168) and PSL(2,11) (order 660), which also admit geometric interpretations – PSL(2,5) is the symmetries of the icosahedron (genus 0), PSL(2,7) of the [Klein quartic](wikipedia://en/390976) (genus 3), and PSL(2,11) the [buckyball surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckyball_surface) (genus 70). These groups form a "[trinity](wikipedia://en/648042#Trinities)" in the sense of [Vladimir Arnold](wikipedia://en/32490), which gives a framework for the various relationships; see *[trinities](wikipedia://en/648042#Trinities)* for details.

There is a close relationship to other [Platonic solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_solids).

Poincaré used the rotational symmetry group $I \subset \mathrm{SO}_3$ to construct the [Poincaré homology sphere](wikipedia://en/585388#Poincaré_homology_sphere) as the quotient manifold $X = \mathrm{SO}_3/I$, an important example of a space whose homology is the same as that of the sphere $S^3$, but which is not homotopic to it.
