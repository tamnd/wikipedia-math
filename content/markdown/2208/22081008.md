# Yvonne Choquet-Bruhat

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Yvonne Choquet-Bruhat"}

**Yvonne Choquet-Bruhat** (; 29 December 1923 – 11 February 2025)[^o3] was a French mathematical physicist now best remembered for her investigation of the mathematics of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024).[^o9] Her proof that the [Einstein field equations](wikipedia://en/424540) can be expressed as a [well-posed](wikipedia://en/176673) [initial-value problem](wikipedia://en/498383) was listed by the journal *[Classical and Quantum Gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_and_Quantum_Gravity)* as one of thirteen "milestone" results in general relativity in an issue published in the centennial anniversary of its birth in 2015.[^o4] She also studied [non-Abelian gauge theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-abelian_gauge_transformation), relativistic hydrodynamics, and [supergravity](wikipedia://en/432632).[^o5]

:::

Choquet-Bruhat was the first woman to be elected to the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences)[^o5] and was a Grand Officer of the [Legion of Honour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legion_of_Honour).[^o6]

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Yvonne Bruhat was born in [Lille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lille) on 29 December 1923[^o8] in an intellectual family.[^o9] Her mother was philosophy professor Berthe Hubert and her father was physicist [Georges Bruhat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Bruhat),[^o5] who died in 1945 in the [Sachsenhausen concentration camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sachsenhausen_concentration_camp).[^o9] He was sent there after refusing to divulge information on the [French Resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Resistance) to Nazi occupation.[^o9] Her brother [François Bruhat](wikipedia://en/9169813) also became a mathematician.[^o5]

Bruhat attended secondary school in Paris, earning her [Baccalauréat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baccalauréat) in 1941, at the top of her class.[^o3] She received the silver medal for physics in the prestigious [Concours Général](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concours_général) national competition.[^o5] In her view, mathematics and the natural sciences were the best ways to understand the Universe.[^o10] Uncertain whether to study medicine, physics, or mathematics, she decided on the last.[^o9] She wanted to research rather than teach, as was more usual for women at the time.[^o9] From 1943 to 1946 she studied at the [École normale supérieure de jeunes filles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_normale_supérieure_de_jeunes_filles) (a [girls' school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-sex_education), also known as the École normale de [Sèvres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sèvres)) near Paris.[^o5]

## Career {#Career}

From 1946 to 1949, she was an assistant at the [École normale supérieure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_normale_supérieure_%28Paris%29).[^o5] In 1947, Bruhat undertook research under the supervision of mathematical physicist [André Lichnerowicz](wikipedia://en/2389188),[^o9] who invited her to be his doctoral student after seeing her excellent marks on an examination for future high-school teachers.[^o10] But unlike most mathematicians at the time, she did not want to follow the trend towards increasing [abstraction](wikipedia://en/454446) and preferred to remain close to physics, motivated by her desire to better understand nature.[^o9] [Jean Leray](wikipedia://en/245430), an expert on the theory of [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564), suggested that she investigate whether or not the Einstein field equations of general relativity were well-posed.[^o9] This became the topic of her dissertation,[^o12][^o9] for which she earned her doctorate in 1951. While it was known that the equations of motion derived from [Newton's laws of motion](wikipedia://en/55212) and [gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation) yielded unique solutions with initial conditions, whether this was true for Einstein's gravitational field equations remained unproven until her landmark thesis.[^o9]

Her work guaranteed the predictability of the universe as described by general relativity.[^o9] Her work also lent credence to [gravitational waves](wikipedia://en/8111079), predicted by general relativity, but initially doubted by Einstein, who thought that they were merely a mathematical artefact of [coordinate](wikipedia://en/81931) choices.[^o9] In two papers published in 1916 and 1918, Einstein had only shown that waves were solutions to the [linearized gravitational field equations](wikipedia://en/2865024). Bruhat demonstrated that this was also true for the fully non-linear equations.[^o14] She expressed the Einstein field equations in vacuum using [harmonic coordinates](wikipedia://en/7203375), previously introduced by [Théophile De Donder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Théophile_De_Donder) and [Cornelius Lanczos](wikipedia://en/1657748), in which case they become non-linear [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/1575825) partial differential equations,[^o10] and as such could describe the propagation of waves.[^o15] While [Georges Darmois](wikipedia://en/28719892) had already tackled the problem for [analytic](wikipedia://en/61478) solutions, Bruhat's proof applied to non-analytic cases as well.[^o10]

She also showed that gravitational waves could not propagate at an arbitrary speed; they must travel at the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) in vacuum.[^o9] [Causality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causality_%28physics%29) was therefore not violated.[^o10] With the 2017 detection of both gravitational and [electromagnetic waves](wikipedia://en/9426) from [GW170817](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GW170817), the merger of two [neutron stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star) in the galaxy [NGC 4993](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NGC_4993),[^o16] Bruhat's faith in her mathematical work was vindicated by [multi-messenger astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-messenger_astronomy).[^o9]

With some help from Leray, Bruhat and her first husband, Léonce Fourès, were offered fellowships at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) (IAS) in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey), in the academic year 1951–51.[^o10] During her time there, she met a number of scientific luminaries, including the Institute director, [J. Robert Oppenheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Robert_Oppenheimer), former scientific director of the Manhattan Project,[^o3] [John Archibald Wheeler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Archibald_Wheeler), who became her friend,[^o9] and [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942), who showed her an early electronic computer he built called the [IAS machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IAS_machine).[^o10] She also met [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736), father of general relativity, whom she remembered as a kind and friendly man.[^o9] She presented her work to him on his [blackboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackboard).[^o9] She described this as a happy time in her life.[^o10] After their time at the IAS, she and her husband accepted positions at the [University of Marseille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Marseille).[^o10]

While a number of [solutions to the Einstein field equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solutions_of_the_Einstein_field_equations) had already been obtained, her contributions to the mathematics of general relativity stimulated interest in the topic among mathematicians.[^o17][^o5] Her dissertation showed that general relativity could be handled with mathematical [rigour](wikipedia://en/655974#Mathematics) and became the basis for future studies on the mathematics of general relativity.[^o15] In the long run, it also facilitated the development of [numerical relativity](wikipedia://en/1797708),[^o5][^o22] making it possible to run [computer simulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_physics) of the [motion of two bodies](wikipedia://en/11694610) (such as black holes and other [compact objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_object) in a [binary system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_system)), their merger, and the gravitational waves they emit during the process.[^o10] In 2005, [Frans Pretorius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frans_Pretorius) successfully wrote the first computer simulation of two merging black holes based on Choquet-Bruhat's work, in particular, her use of harmonic coordinates.[^o15] Simulations of this kind were used in analysing the [first detection of gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_observation_of_gravitational_waves) in 2015.[^o3] Along with other key figures such as Wheeler, [Peter Bergmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Bergmann), and [Dennis Sciama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_W._Sciama), her studies and collaborations spurred the revival and growth of research in general relativity during the second half of the 1950s.[^o18] She attended the [Conference on the Role of Gravitation in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapel_Hill_Conference) in [Chapel Hill, North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapel_Hill,_North_Carolina), in 1957.[^o10] This conference marked the beginning of the renaissance of general relativity after the Second World War.[^o19] The [International Society on General Relativity and Gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Society_on_General_Relativity_and_Gravitation) (ISGRG) was formed 14 years later.[^o10] Bruhat served its president from 1980 to 1983.[^o9]

In 1958, she was awarded the [CNRS Silver Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNRS_Silver_Medal).[^o20] From 1958 to 1959 she taught at the [University of Reims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Reims). In 1960 relocated to [the Sorbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorbonne_%28building%29) in Paris.[^o10] When this was later reorganized, she moved to Paris VI (later the [Pierre and Marie Curie University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_and_Marie_Curie_University) or UPMC),[^o10] and remained there until her retirement in 1990.[^o10] Afterward, she occupied a position to the Institute for Higher Scientific Studies ([IHÉS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institut_des_Hautes_Études_Scientifiques)).[^o9] At the UPMC she continued to make significant contributions to mathematical physics, notably in general relativity, supergravity, and the non-Abelian [gauge theories](wikipedia://en/23912155) of the [Standard Model](wikipedia://en/47641) of particle physics.[^o5]

During the 1950s and 1960s, she generalized the main result of her doctoral dissertation to cases of the Einstein field equations in the presence of various types of matter.[^o14] In 1969 she and [Robert Geroch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Geroch) introduced the notion of maximal Cauchy development,[^o21] important in the study of global aspects of the [Cauchy problem](wikipedia://en/1725027) in general relativity and the [theorems on gravitational singularities](wikipedia://en/294408) of [Roger Penrose](wikipedia://en/26193) and [Stephen Hawking](wikipedia://en/19376148).[^o9] In 1976, she and [Jerrold Marsden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerrold_E._Marsden) proved the [positive-mass theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_mass_theorem) for the case of a vacuum flat spacetime near [Minkowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space).[^o22] With [Cécile DeWitt-Morette](wikipedia://en/26556512) and Margaret Dillard-Bleick, she published monograph titled *Analysis, Manifolds and Physics*, detailing the relevance of [global analysis](wikipedia://en/42027498) for physics.[^o23] Her work in 1981 with [Demetrios Christodoulou](wikipedia://en/2407999) demonstrated the existence of global solutions of the [Yang–Mills](wikipedia://en/35544664), [Higgs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_field), and [spinor field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinor_field) equations in 3+1 dimensions.[^o24] In 1984 she examined results in supergravity that can be extended to 11 dimensions.[^o25]

In 1978 Yvonne Choquet-Bruhat was elected a correspondent to the Academy of Sciences and on 14 May 1979 became the first woman to be elected a full member.[^o9] In 1985 she was elected to the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences).[^o5] In 1986 she was chosen to deliver the prestigious [Noether Lecture](wikipedia://en/11566873) by the [Association for Women in Mathematics](wikipedia://en/9858698).[^o5] In 2006, she delivered a talk titled "Results and Open Problems in [Mathematical General Relativity](wikipedia://en/1789646)" at the [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) in Madrid.[^o23] In 2011, she exhibited a simplified version of ([spinorial](wikipedia://en/29276)) proof of the positive-energy theorem by [Edward Witten](wikipedia://en/54894), valid for any number of dimensions.[^o22] She completed her last technical book, *Introduction to General Relativity, Black Holes & Cosmology*, in 2014.[^o23]

## Technical research contributions {#Technical_research_contributions}

Choquet-Bruhat's best-known research deals with the mathematical nature of the initial data formulation of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024). A summary of results can be phrased purely in terms of standard [differential geometric](wikipedia://en/8625) objects.

- An **initial data set** is a triplet $(M, g, k)$ in which $M$ is a three-dimensional [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold), $g$ is a smooth Riemannian metric on $M$, and $k$ is a smooth (0,2)-tensor field on $M$.
- Given an initial data set $(M, g, k)$, a **development** of $(M, g, k)$ is a four-dimensional [Lorentzian manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentzian_manifold) $(\overline{M}, \overline{g})$ together with a smooth [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119) $f : M \to \overline{M}$ and a smooth unit normal vector field along $f$ such that $f^{*}\overline{g} = g$ and such that the [second fundamental form](wikipedia://en/652078) of $f$, relative to the given normal vector field, is $k$.

In this sense, an initial data set can be viewed as the prescription of the submanifold geometry of an embedded spacelike hypersurface in a Lorentzian manifold.

- An initial data set $(M, g, k)$ satisfies the **vacuum constraint equations**, or is said to be a **vacuum initial data set**, if the following two equations are satisfied:

$$\begin{aligned} R_g-|k|_g^2+(\operatorname{tr}_gk)^2&=0\\ \operatorname{div}_gk-d(\operatorname{tr}_gk)&=0. \end{aligned}$$

Here $R_{g}$ denotes the [scalar curvature](wikipedia://en/285622) of $g$.

One of Choquet-Bruhat's seminal 1952 results states the following: Every vacuum initial data set $(M, g, k)$ has a development $f : M \to (\overline{M}, \overline{g})$ such that $\overline{g}$ has zero [Ricci curvature](wikipedia://en/276582), and such that every inextendible timelike curve in the Lorentzian manifold $(\overline{M}, \overline{g})$ intersects $f(M)$ exactly once. Briefly, this can be summarized as saying that there exists a [vacuum spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_solution_%28general_relativity%29) $(\overline{M}, \overline{g})$ for which $f(M)$ is a [Cauchy surface](wikipedia://en/2534823). Such a development is called a **globally hyperbolic vacuum development**. Choquet-Bruhat also proved a uniqueness theorem:

Given any two globally hyperbolic vacuum developments $f_{1} : M \to (\overline{M}_{1}, \overline{g}_{1})$ and $f_{2} : M \to (\overline{M}_{2}, \overline{g}_{2})$ of the same vacuum initial data set, there is an open subset $U_{1}$ of $\overline{M}_{1}$ containing $f_{1}(M)$ and an open subset $U_{2}$ of $\overline{M}_{2}$ containing $f_{2}(M)$, together with an isometry $i : (U_{1}, \overline{g}_{1}) \to (U_{2}, \overline{g}_{2})$ such that $i(f_{1}(p)) = f_{2}(p)$ for all $p$ in $M$.

In a slightly imprecise form, this says: given any embedded spacelike hypersurface $M$ of a Ricci-flat Lorentzian manifold $\overline{M}$, the geometry of $\overline{M}$ near $M$ is fully determined by the submanifold geometry of $M$.

In an article written with [Robert Geroch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Geroch) in 1969, Choquet-Bruhat fully clarified the nature of uniqueness. With a two-page argument in [point-set topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point-set_topology) using [Zorn's lemma](wikipedia://en/51442), they showed that Choquet-Bruhat's above existence and uniqueness theorems automatically imply a global uniqueness theorem:[^o27] Any vacuum initial data set $(M, g, k)$ has a **maximal globally hyperbolic vacuum development**, meaning a globally hyperbolic vacuum development $f : M \to (\overline{M}, \overline{g})$ such that, for any other globally hyperbolic vacuum development $f_{1} : M \to (\overline{M}_{1}, \overline{g}_{1})$, there is an open subset $U$ of $\overline{M}$ containing $f(M)$ and an isometry $i : \overline{M}_{1} \to U$ such that $i(f_{1}(p)) = f(p)$ for all $p$ in $M$. Any two maximal globally hyperbolic vacuum developments of the same vacuum initial data are isometric to one another. It is now common to study such developments. For instance, the well-known theorem of [Demetrios Christodoulou](wikipedia://en/2407999) and [Sergiu Klainerman](wikipedia://en/25808826) on stability of Minkowski space asserts that if $(\mathbb{R}^{3}, g, k)$ is a vacuum initial data set with $g$ and $k$ sufficiently close to zero (in a certain precise form), then its maximal globally hyperbolic vacuum development is [geodesically complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesically_complete) and geometrically close to [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space).

Choquet-Bruhat's proof makes use of a clever choice of coordinates, the [wave coordinates](wikipedia://en/7203375) (which are the Lorentzian equivalent to the [harmonic coordinates](wikipedia://en/7217102)), in which the Einstein equations become a system of [hyperbolic partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_partial_differential_equations), for which well-posedness results can be applied.

## Personal life and death {#Personal_life_and_death}

*Image: Yvonne Choquet-Bruhat 2.jpeg.* Choquet-Bruhat in 2006

In 1947, she married fellow mathematician Léonce Fourès.[^o10] Their daughter Michelle is an [ecologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecologist). Her doctoral work and early research was under the name Yvonne Fourès-Bruhat.[^o3] In 1960, Bruhat and Fourès divorced.[^o10]

The following year she married the mathematician [Gustave Choquet](wikipedia://en/17301193)[^o10] and [changed her last name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiden_and_married_names) to Choquet-Bruhat. She and Choquet had two children: a son, neuroscientist [Daniel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Choquet), and a daughter, medical doctor Geneviève.

Her memoir, from her academic and personal life, was translated into English in 2018.[^o10][^o29]

Choquet-Bruhat died on 11 February 2025 in Merignac, France (33700), at the age of 101.[^o30][^o9]
