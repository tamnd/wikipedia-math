# Projection pursuit

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Projection pursuit"}

**Projection pursuit (PP)** is a type of statistical technique that involves finding the most "interesting" possible [projections](wikipedia://en/519182) in multidimensional data. Often, projections that deviate more from a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) are considered to be more interesting. As each projection is found, the data are reduced by removing the component along that projection, and the process is repeated to find new projections; this is the "pursuit" aspect that motivated the technique known as [matching pursuit](wikipedia://en/5534001).[^o2][^o3]

:::

The idea of projection pursuit is to locate the projection or projections from [high-dimensional space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-dimensional_space) to low-dimensional space that reveal the most details about the structure of the data set. Once an interesting set of projections has been found, existing structures (clusters, surfaces, etc.) can be extracted and analyzed separately.

Projection pursuit has been widely used for [blind source separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_source_separation), so it is very important in [independent component analysis](wikipedia://en/598031). Projection pursuit seeks one projection at a time such that the extracted signal is as non-Gaussian as possible.[^o4]

## History {#History}

Projection pursuit technique were originally proposed and experimented by [Kruskal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Kruskal).[^o6] Related ideas occur in Switzer (1970) "Numerical classification" pp31–43 in "Computer Applications in the Earth Sciences: Geostatistics, and Switzer and Wright (1971) "Numerical classification of eocene nummulitids," Mathematical Geology pp 297–311. The first successful implementation is due to [Jerome H. Friedman](wikipedia://en/40671069) and [John Tukey](wikipedia://en/415974) (1974), who named projection pursuit.

The original purpose of projection pursuit was to machine-pick "interesting" low-dimensional projections of a high-dimensional point cloud by numerically maximizing a certain objective function or projection index.[^o7]

Several years later, Friedman and Stuetzle extended the idea behind projection pursuit and added [projection pursuit regression](wikipedia://en/25092894) (PPR), projection pursuit classification (PPC), and projection pursuit density estimation (PPDE).

## Feature {#Feature}

The most exciting feature of projection pursuit is that it is one of the very few multivariate methods able to bypass the "[curse of dimensionality](wikipedia://en/787776)" caused by the fact that high-dimensional space is mostly empty. In addition, projection pursuit is able to ignore irrelevant (i.e. noisy and information-poor) variables. This is a distinct advantage over methods based on interpoint distances like minimal spanning trees, multidimensional scaling and most clustering techniques.

Many of the methods of classical multivariate analysis turn out to be special cases of projection pursuit. Examples are [principal component analysis](wikipedia://en/76340) and [discriminant analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discriminant_analysis), and the quartimax and oblimax methods in [factor analysis](wikipedia://en/253492).

One serious drawback of projection pursuit methods is their high demand on computer time.
