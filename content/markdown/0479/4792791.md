# Beltrami–Klein model

*Image: Klein model.svg.* Many hyperbolic lines through point *P* not intersecting line *a* in the Beltrami–Klein model

*Image: Uniform tiling 73-t1 klein.png.* A hyperbolic [triheptagonal tiling](wikipedia://en/8834492) in a Beltrami–Klein model projection

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Beltrami–Klein model"}

In geometry, the **Beltrami–Klein model**, also called the **projective model**, **Klein disk model**, and the **Cayley–Klein model**, is a model of [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291) in which points are represented by the points in the interior of the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738) (or *n*-dimensional [unit ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_ball)) and lines are represented by the [chords](wikipedia://en/297947), straight line segments with [ideal endpoints](wikipedia://en/4995922) on the boundary [sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090).

:::

It is analogous to the [gnomonic projection](wikipedia://en/1331480) of [spherical geometry](wikipedia://en/174026), in that [geodesics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesics) ([great circles](wikipedia://en/48082) in spherical geometry) are mapped to straight lines.

This model is not [conformal](wikipedia://en/50627): angles are not faithfully represented, and [circles](wikipedia://en/6220) become [ellipses](wikipedia://en/9277), increasingly flattened near the edge. This is in contrast to the [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836), which is conformal. However, lines in the Poincaré model are not represented by straight line segments, but by [arcs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_%28geometry%29) that meet the boundary [orthogonally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal).

The **Beltrami–Klein model** is named after the Italian geometer [Eugenio Beltrami](wikipedia://en/543760) and the German [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) while "Cayley" in **Cayley–Klein model** refers to the English geometer [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034).

## History {#History}

This model made its first appearance for [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291) in two memoirs of [Eugenio Beltrami](wikipedia://en/543760) published in 1868, first for dimension *n* = 2 and then for general *n*, and these essays proved the [equiconsistency](wikipedia://en/3435716) of hyperbolic geometry with ordinary [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417).[^o3][^o4][^o5]

The papers of Beltrami remained little noticed until recently and the model was named after Klein ("The Klein disk model"). In 1859 [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034) used the [cross-ratio](wikipedia://en/401767) definition of angle due to [Laguerre](wikipedia://en/1011080) to show how Euclidean geometry could be defined using [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849).[^o6] His definition of distance later became known as the [Cayley metric](wikipedia://en/30794512).

In 1869, the young (twenty-year-old) [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) became acquainted with Cayley's work. He recalled that in 1870 he gave a talk on the work of Cayley at the seminar of [Weierstrass](wikipedia://en/93459) and he wrote:

"I finished with a question whether there might exist a connection between the ideas of Cayley and [Lobachevsky](wikipedia://en/648527). I was given the answer that these two systems were conceptually widely separated."[^o7]

Later, [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) realized that Cayley's ideas give rise to a projective model of the non-Euclidean plane.[^o8]

As Klein puts it, "I allowed myself to be convinced by these objections and put aside this already mature idea." However, in 1871, he returned to this idea, formulated it mathematically, and published it.[^o9]

## Distance formula {#Distance_formula}

The distance function for the Beltrami–Klein model is a [Cayley–Klein metric](wikipedia://en/30794512). Given two distinct points *p* and *q* in the open unit ball, the unique straight line connecting them intersects the boundary at two [ideal points](wikipedia://en/4995922), *a* and *b*, label them so that the points are, in order, *a*, *p*, *q*, *b*, so that $\lvert aq\rvert$ > $\lvert ap\rvert$ and $\lvert pb\rvert$ > $\lvert qb\rvert$.

The hyperbolic distance between *p* and *q* is then: $d(p, q)=\frac{1}{2} \ln \frac{ \left| aq \right| \, \left| pb \right| }{ \left| ap \right| \, \left| qb \right| }$

The vertical bars indicate Euclidean distances between the points in the model, where ln is the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476) and the factor of one half is needed to give the model the standard [curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) of −1.

When one of the points is the origin and [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) between the points is *r* then the hyperbolic distance is:

$$\frac12\ln\left(\frac{1+r}{1-r}\right)= \operatorname{artanh}r,$$

where $\operatorname{artanh}$ is the [inverse hyperbolic function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_hyperbolic_function) of the [hyperbolic tangent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function).

:::

## The Klein disk model {#The_Klein_disk_model}

*Image: Klein model.png.* Lines in the projective model of the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/241291)

In two dimensions the **Beltrami–Klein model** is called the **Klein disk model**. It is a [disk](wikipedia://en/329542) and the inside of the disk is a model of the entire [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane). Lines in this model are represented by [chords](wikipedia://en/297947) of the boundary circle (also called the *[absolute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cayley_absolute)*). The points on the boundary circle are called **[ideal points](wikipedia://en/4995922)**; although [well defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well_defined), they do not belong to the hyperbolic plane. Points outside the disk do not belong to the hyperbolic plane either, and they are sometimes called **ultra ideal points**.

The model is not [conformal](wikipedia://en/50627), meaning that angles are distorted, and circles on the [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane) are in general not circular in the model. Only circles that have their centre at the centre of the boundary circle are not distorted. All other circles are distorted, as are [horocycles](wikipedia://en/11084460) and [hypercycles](wikipedia://en/7516582).

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

Chords that meet on the boundary circle are [limiting parallel](wikipedia://en/31547181) lines.

Two chords are perpendicular if, when extended outside the disk, each goes through the [pole](wikipedia://en/19451201) of the other. (The pole of a chord is an ultra ideal point: the point outside the disk where the tangents to the disk at the endpoints of the chord meet.) Chords that go through the centre of the disk have their pole at infinity, orthogonal to the direction of the chord (this implies that right angles on diameters are not distorted).

:::

### Compass and straightedge constructions {#Compass_and_straightedge_constructions}

Here is how one can use [compass and straightedge constructions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge_constructions) in the model to achieve the effect of the basic constructions in the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/241291).

- The **[pole of a line](wikipedia://en/19451201)**. While the pole is not a point in the hyperbolic plane (it is an **ultra ideal point**) most constructions will use the pole of a line in one or more ways.

For a line: construct the tangents to the boundary circle through the [ideal (end) points](wikipedia://en/4995922) of the line. the point where these tangents intersect is the pole.

For [diameters](wikipedia://en/8007) of the disk: the pole is at infinity perpendicular to the diameter.

- To **[construct a perpendicular to a given line through a given point](wikipedia://en/76944),** draw the [ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28geometry%29) from the **pole** of the line through the given point. The part of the ray that is inside the disk is the perpendicular.

When the line is a diameter of the disk then the perpendicular is the chord that is (Euclidean) perpendicular to that diameter and going through the given point.

- To **[find the midpoint of given segment](wikipedia://en/152547#Line_segmentbisector)** $AB$: Draw the [lines](wikipedia://en/946975) through A and B that are perpendicular to $AB$. (see above) Draw the lines connecting the **ideal points** of these lines, two of these lines will intersect the segment $AB$ and will do this at the same point. This point is the (hyperbolic) [midpoint](wikipedia://en/437979) of$AB$.[^o16]
- To **[bisect a given angle](wikipedia://en/152547#Angle_bisector)** $\angle BAC$: Draw the [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28geometry%29) AB and AC. Draw tangents to the circle where the rays intersect the boundary circle. Draw a line from *A* to the point where the tangents intersect. The part of this line between *A* and the boundary circle is the bisector.[^o17]
- The **[common perpendicular of two lines](wikipedia://en/1403889)** is the chord that when extended goes through both [poles](wikipedia://en/19451201) of the chords.

When one of the chords is a diameter of the boundary circle then the common perpendicular is the chord that is perpendicular to the diameter and that when lengthened goes through the pole of the other chord.

- To **[reflect a point P in a line l](wikipedia://en/295829)**: From a point R on the line l draw the ray through P. Let X be the ideal point where the ray intersects the absolute. Draw the ray from the pole of line l through X, let Y be another ideal point that intersects the ray. Draw the segment RY. The reflection of point P is the point where the ray from the pole of line l through P intersects RY.[^o18]

### Circles, hypercycles and horocycles {#Circles,_hypercycles_and_horocycles}

*Image: Klein model Circles.svg.* Circles in the Klein-Beltrami model of hyperbolic geometry.

While lines in the hyperbolic plane are straightforward to project into the Klein disk model, circles, hypercycles and horocycles are not.

Circles in the model that are not [concentric](wikipedia://en/19049087) with the model become ellipses, increasing in [eccentricity](wikipedia://en/1239472) near the edge. Angles, hypercycles, and horocycles in the Klein disk model are also deformed.

For constructions in the hyperbolic plane that contain circles, hypercycles, horocycles or non [right angles](wikipedia://en/76956) it is perhaps more convenient to use the [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836) or the [Poincaré half-plane model](wikipedia://en/461942).

### Relation to the Poincaré disk model {#Relation_to_the_Poincaré_disk_model}

*Image: KleinToPoincare.png.* Combined projections from the Klein disk model (yellow) to the [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836) (red) via the hemisphere model (blue)

*Image: Relation5models.png.* The Beltrami–Klein model (K in the picture) is an [orthographic projection](wikipedia://en/203052) from the hemispherical model and a [gnomonic projection](wikipedia://en/1331480) of the hyperboloid model (Hy) with the center of the hyperboloid (O) as its center.

Both the [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836) and the Klein disk model are models of the hyperbolic plane. An advantage of the Poincaré disk model is that it is conformal (circles and angles are not distorted); a disadvantage is that straight lines project to [circular arcs](wikipedia://en/5578920) orthogonal to the boundary circle of the disk.

[The two models are related](wikipedia://en/241291#Connection_between_the_models) through a projection on or from the [hemisphere model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemisphere_model). The Klein model is an [orthographic projection](wikipedia://en/203052) to the hemisphere model, while the Poincaré disk model is a [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431).

Straight lines always intersect the circular boundary of the two models in the same place, regardless of which model is used. Also, the [pole](wikipedia://en/19451201) of the chord is the centre of the circle that contains the [arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_%28geometry%29).

If P is a point a distance $s$ from the centre of the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) in the Beltrami–Klein model, then the corresponding point on the Poincaré disk model a distance of u on the same radius:

$$u = \frac{s}{1+\sqrt{1-s^2}} = \frac{\left(1-\sqrt{1-s^2}\right)}{s}.$$

Conversely, If P is a point a distance $u$ from the centre of the unit circle in the Poincaré disk model, then the corresponding point of the Beltrami–Klein model is a distance of s on the same radius:

$$s = \frac{2u}{1+u^2}.$$

### Relation of the disk model to the hyperboloid model and the gnomonic projection of the sphere {#Relation_of_the_disk_model_to_the_hyperboloid_model_and_the_gnomonic_projection_of_the_sphere}

The [gnomonic projection](wikipedia://en/1331480) of the sphere projects from the sphere's center onto a tangent plane. Every great circle on the sphere is projected to a straight line, but it is not conformal. Angles are not faithfully represented, and circles become ellipses, increasingly stretched as they get further from the tangent point.

Similarly the Klein disk (K, in the picture) is a gnomonic projection of the hyperboloid model (Hy) with as center the center of the hyperboloid (O) and the [projection plane](wikipedia://en/39680127) tangent to the hyperboloid.[^o22]

## Distance and metric tensor {#Distance_and_metric_tensor}

*Image: Hyperbolic orthogonal dodecahedral honeycomb.png.* The regular hyperbolic [dodecahedral honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3754285), {5,3,4}

Given two distinct points *U* and *V* in the open unit ball of the model in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), the unique straight line connecting them intersects the unit sphere at two [ideal points](wikipedia://en/4995922) *A* and *B*, labeled so that the points are, in order along the line, *A*, *U*, *V*, *B*. Taking the centre of the unit ball of the model as the origin, and assigning position vectors **u**, **v**, **a**, **b** respectively to the points *U*, *V*, *A*, *B*, we have that that ‖**a** − **v**‖ > ‖**a** − **u**‖ and ‖**u** − **b**‖ > ‖**v** − **b**‖, where ‖ · ‖ denotes the [Euclidean norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_norm). Then the distance between *U* and *V* in the modelled hyperbolic space is expressed as

$$d(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}) = \frac{1}{2} \ln \frac{ \left\| \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{a} \right\| \, \left\| \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{u} \right\| }{ \left\| \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{a} \right\| \, \left\| \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{v} \right\| } ,$$

where the factor of one half is needed to make the [curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) −1.

The associated [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) is given by[^o24][^o25]

$$ds^2 = g (\mathbf{x}, d\mathbf{x}) = \frac{ \left\| d \mathbf{x} \right\| ^2 }{ 1 - \left\| \mathbf{x} \right\| ^2 } + \frac{ ( \mathbf{x} \cdot d \mathbf{x} ) ^2 }{\bigl( 1 - \left\| \mathbf{x} \right\| ^2 \bigr) ^2 } = \frac{(1 - \left\| \mathbf{x} \right\| ^2) \left\| d \mathbf{x} \right\| ^2 + ( \mathbf{x} \cdot d \mathbf{x} ) ^2 }{\bigl( 1 - \left\| \mathbf{x} \right\| ^2 \bigr) ^2 }$$

## Relation to the hyperboloid model {#Relation_to_the_hyperboloid_model}

*Image: Hyperboloid-tiling-klein.png.* Partial {7,3} hyperbolic tiling of the hyperboloid as seen in Beltrami-Klein perspective.

*Image: Hyperboloid-tiling-klein-animation.webm.* Animation of partial {7,3} hyperbolic tiling of the hyperboloid rotating into the Beltrami-Klein perspective.

The [hyperboloid model](wikipedia://en/4771189) is a model of hyperbolic geometry within (*n* + 1)-dimensional [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space). The Minkowski inner product is given by

$$\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y} = x_0 y_0 - x_1 y_1 - \cdots - x_n y_n \,$$

and the norm by $\left\| \mathbf{x} \right\| = \sqrt{ \mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{x} }$. The hyperbolic plane is embedded in this space as the vectors **x** with ‖**x**‖ = 1 and *x*<sub>0</sub> (the "timelike component") positive. The intrinsic distance (in the embedding) between points **u** and **v** is then given by

$$d ( \mathbf{u} , \mathbf{v} ) = \operatorname{arcosh} ( \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}) .$$

This may also be written in the homogeneous form

$$d ( \mathbf{u} , \mathbf{v} ) = \operatorname{arcosh} \left( \frac{ \mathbf{u} }{ \left\| \mathbf{u} \right\| } \cdot \frac{ \mathbf{v} }{ \left\| \mathbf{v} \right\| } \right) ,$$

which allows the vectors to be rescaled for convenience.

The Beltrami–Klein model is obtained from the hyperboloid model by rescaling all vectors so that the timelike component is 1, that is, by projecting the hyperboloid embedding through the origin onto the plane *x*<sub>0</sub> = 1. The distance function, in its homogeneous form, is unchanged. Since the intrinsic lines (geodesics) of the hyperboloid model are the intersection of the embedding with planes through the Minkowski origin, the intrinsic lines of the Beltrami–Klein model are the chords of the sphere.

## Relation to the Poincaré ball model {#Relation_to_the_Poincaré_ball_model}

Both the [Poincaré ball model](wikipedia://en/30871836) and the Beltrami–Klein model are models of the *n*-dimensional hyperbolic space in the *n*-dimensional unit ball in **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. If $u$ is a vector of norm less than one representing a point of the Poincaré disk model, then the corresponding point of the Beltrami–Klein model is given by

$$s = \frac{2u}{1+u \cdot u}.$$

Conversely, from a vector $s$ of norm less than one representing a point of the Beltrami–Klein model, the corresponding point of the Poincaré disk model is given by

$$u = \frac{s}{1+\sqrt{1-s \cdot s}} = \frac{\left(1-\sqrt{1-s \cdot s}\right)s}{s \cdot s}.$$

Given two points on the boundary of the unit disk, which are called *ideal points*, the straight line connecting them in the Beltrami–Klein model is the chord between them, while in the corresponding Poincaré model the line is a [circular arc](wikipedia://en/5578920) on the two-dimensional subspace generated by the two boundary point vectors, meeting the boundary of the ball at right angles. The two models are related through a projection from the center of the disk; a ray from the center passing through a point of one model line passes through the corresponding point of the line in the other model.
