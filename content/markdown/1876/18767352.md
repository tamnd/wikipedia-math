# Shape theory (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Shape theory"}

the theory itself (concepts, methods, results) **Shape theory** is a branch of [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) that provides a more global view of the topological spaces than [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831). The two coincide on [compacta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set) dominated homotopically by finite [polyhedra](wikipedia://en/252686). Shape theory associates with the [Čech homology](wikipedia://en/856614) theory while homotopy theory associates with the [singular homology](wikipedia://en/431041) theory.

:::

## Background {#Background}

Shape theory was invented and published by [D. E. Christie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D._E._Christie) in 1944; it was reinvented, further developed and promoted by the Polish mathematician [Karol Borsuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karol_Borsuk) in 1968. Actually, the name *shape theory* was coined by Borsuk.

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Warsaw circle"}

### Warsaw circle {#Warsaw_circle}

*Image: Warsaw Circle.png.* The Warsaw circle

Borsuk lived and worked in [Warsaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw), hence the name of one of the fundamental examples of the area, the **Warsaw circle**.[^o5] It is a compact subset of the plane produced by "closing up" a [topologist's sine curve](wikipedia://en/292224) (also called a *Warsaw sine curve*) with an arc. The [homotopy groups](wikipedia://en/297506) of the Warsaw circle are all [trivial](wikipedia://en/193059), just like those of a point, and so any map between the Warsaw circle and a point induces a [weak homotopy equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_homotopy_equivalence). However these two spaces are not [homotopy equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_equivalence). So by the [Whitehead theorem](wikipedia://en/976793), the Warsaw circle does not have the homotopy type of a [CW complex](wikipedia://en/353022).

:::

## Historical development {#Historical_development}

Borsuk's shape theory was generalized onto arbitrary (non-[metric](wikipedia://en/20018)) compact spaces, and even onto general categories, by Włodzimierz Holsztyński in year 1968/1969, and published in [Fund. Math.](wikipedia://en/1576701) **70**, 157–168, y. 1971 (see Jean-Marc Cordier, Tim Porter, (1989) below). This was done in a *continuous style*, characteristic for the Čech homology rendered by [Samuel Eilenberg](wikipedia://en/362501) and [Norman Steenrod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Steenrod) in their monograph *Foundations of Algebraic Topology*. Due to the circumstance, Holsztyński's paper was hardly noticed, and instead a great popularity in the field was gained by a later paper by [Sibe Mardešić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sibe_Mardešić) and Jack Segal, Fund. Math. **72**, 61–68, y.1971. Further developments are reflected by the references below, and by their contents.

For some purposes, like [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087), more sophisticated invariants were developed under the name **strong shape**. Generalizations to [noncommutative geometry](wikipedia://en/295917), e.g. the shape theory for [operator algebras](wikipedia://en/455987) have been found.
