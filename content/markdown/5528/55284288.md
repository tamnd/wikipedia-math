# Kolmogorov's normability criterion

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kolmogorov's normability criterion"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Kolmogorov's normability criterion** is a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) that provides a [necessary and sufficient condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_condition) for a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) to be [normable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normable_space); that is, for the existence of a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) on the space that generates the given [topology](wikipedia://en/29954).[^o2][^o3] The normability criterion can be seen as a result in same vein as the [Nagata–Smirnov metrization theorem](wikipedia://en/1236569) and [Bing metrization theorem](wikipedia://en/9712648), which gives a necessary and sufficient condition for a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) to be [metrizable](wikipedia://en/19738). The result was proved by the Russian mathematician [Andrey Nikolayevich Kolmogorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Nikolayevich_Kolmogorov) in 1934.[^o4][^o5][^o6]

:::

::: {#o8 .statement cue=heading name="Statement of the theorem"}

## Statement of the theorem {#Statement_of_the_theorem}

::: {#o9 .statement cue=math-theorem name="Kolmogorov's normability criterion"}

**Kolmogorov's normability criterion.**

A [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) is normable if and only if it is a [T<sub>1</sub> space](wikipedia://en/148721) and admits a [bounded](wikipedia://en/1789812) [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of the origin.

:::

Because translation (that is, vector addition) by a constant preserves the convexity, boundedness, and [openness of sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_invariant_topology), the words "of the origin" can be replaced with "of some point" or even with "of every point".

::: {#o11 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

### Definitions {#Definitions}

It may be helpful to first recall the following terms:

- A [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVS) is a vector space $X$ equipped with a topology $\tau$ such that the vector space operations of scalar multiplication and vector addition are continuous.
- A topological vector space $(X, \tau)$ is called [normable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normable_space) if there is a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) $\|\cdot\|: X \to \mathbb{R}$ on $X$ such that the open balls of the norm $\|\cdot\|$ generate the given topology $\tau.$ (Note well that a given normable topological vector space might admit multiple such norms.)
- A [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$ is called a [T<sub>1</sub> space](wikipedia://en/148721) if, for every two distinct points $x, y \in X,$ there is an open [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) $U_x$ of $x$ that does not contain $y.$ In a topological vector space, this is equivalent to requiring that, for every $x \neq 0,$ there is an open neighbourhood of the origin not containing $x.$ Note that being T<sub>1</sub> is weaker than being a [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637), in which every two distinct points $x, y \in X$ admit open neighbourhoods $U_x$ of $x$ and $U_y$ of $y$ with $U_x \cap U_y = \varnothing$; since normed and normable spaces are always Hausdorff, it is a "surprise" that the theorem only requires T<sub>1</sub>.
- A subset $A$ of a vector space $X$ is a [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292) if, for any two points $x, y \in A,$ the line segment joining them lies wholly within $A,$ that is, for all $0 \leq t \leq 1,$ $(1 - t) x + t y \in A.$
- A subset $A$ of a topological vector space $(X, \tau)$ is a [bounded set](wikipedia://en/1789812) if, for every open neighbourhood $U$ of the origin, there exists a scalar $\lambda$ so that $A \subseteq \lambda U.$ (One can think of $U$ as being "small" and $\lambda$ as being "big enough" to inflate $U$ to cover $A.$)
