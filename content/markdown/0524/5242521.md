# Regular tetrahedron

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Regular tetrahedron"}

A **regular tetrahedron** is a polyhedron with four [equilateral triangular](wikipedia://en/173285) faces.

:::

## Description {#Description}

### Classifications {#Classifications}

A regular tetrahedron is a [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606) (that is, a four-sided [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470)) in which all four faces are [equilateral triangles](wikipedia://en/173285). In other words, all of its faces are the same size and shape ([congruent](wikipedia://en/39330)), and all edges are the same length. As a [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron), the regular tetrahedron is the [deltahedron](wikipedia://en/493995) with the smallest number of vertices and faces out of eight convex deltahedra. Being a deltahedron means that all faces of a polyhedron are equilateral triangles.[^n1] Like the other [pyramids](wikipedia://en/3307045) and all tetrahedra in general, the regular tetrahedron is [self-dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-dual), meaning its [dual](wikipedia://en/8815) is a regular tetrahedron itself.

The regular tetrahedron is also one of the five regular [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905)—polyhedra in which all of their faces are [regular polygons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_polygons).[^n2] Known since antiquity, Platonic solids are named after the Greek philosopher [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954), who associated four of those solids with fundamental natural elements. He assigned the regular tetrahedron to the classical element of [fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_%28classical_element%29), because its corner is sharpest and most penetrating. Following its attribution with nature by Plato, [Johannes Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736) in his *[Harmonices Mundi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonices_Mundi)* sketched each of the Platonic solids.[^n3] In his *[Mysterium Cosmographicum](wikipedia://en/3194945)*, Kepler also proposed the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System) by using the Platonic solids, setting one inside another and separating them with six spheres resembling the six planets. The ordered solids started from the innermost to the outermost: [regular octahedron](wikipedia://en/22458), [regular icosahedron](wikipedia://en/14968), [regular dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/7149361), regular tetrahedron, and [cube](wikipedia://en/6285).[^n4] Plato's student [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) incorrectly claimed that the regular tetrahedron [can fill space](wikipedia://en/58607583),[^o9] but the cube is the only Platonic solid with this property.

Regular tetrahedron is the [degenerated](wikipedia://en/232952) [antiprism](wikipedia://en/1851).[^n5]

### Cartesian coordinates {#Cartesian_coordinates}

One way to construct a regular tetrahedron is by using the following [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates), defining the four vertices of a tetrahedron with edge length 2, centered at the origin, and two-level edges:

$$\left(\pm 1, 0, -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) \qquad \left(0, \pm 1, \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)$$

A regular tetrahedron can be embedded inside a [cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube_%28geometry%29) in two ways such that each vertex is a vertex of the cube, and each edge is a diagonal of one of the cube's faces. For one such embedding, the [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) of the vertices are

$$\begin{aligned} (1,1,1), &\quad (1,-1,-1), \\ (-1,1,-1), &\quad (-1,-1,1). \end{aligned}$$

This yields a tetrahedron with edge-length $2 \sqrt{2}$, centered at the origin. For the other tetrahedron (which is [dual](wikipedia://en/8815) to the first), reverse all the signs. These two tetrahedra's vertices combined are the vertices of a cube, demonstrating that the regular tetrahedron is the 3-[demicube](wikipedia://en/6833695), a polyhedron that is by [alternating](wikipedia://en/4349117) a cube. This form has [Coxeter diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram) and [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) $\mathrm{h}\{4,3\}$.

## Properties {#Properties}

### Measurement {#Measurement}

*Image: Tetrahedron.stl.* 3D model of a regular tetrahedron

Let $a$ be the edge length of a regular tetrahedron. The height of an equilateral triangle, the segment line is drawn from any vertex to the midpoint of an edge, and by the calculation of [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034), the height of any equilateral triangle is $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a$. The distance between the centroid and a vertex has two different lengths; the longest is two-thirds of the height of an equilateral triangle. By the Pythagorean theorem again, one can find the height of a regular tetrahedron:[^n6]

$$h = \frac{\sqrt{6}}{3}a \approx 0.816a.$$

Since the shortest distance between the centroid and a vertex is one-third of the height of an equilateral triangle, the [dihedral angle](wikipedia://en/338046) (i.e., an angle between two triangular faces) of a regular tetrahedron is $\arccos \left(1/3 \right) = \arctan\left(2\sqrt{2}\right) \approx 70.529^\circ$.[^o16]

The surface area of a regular tetrahedron is four times area of an equilateral triangle:[^n7]

$$A = 4 \cdot \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a^2\right) = a^2 \sqrt{3} \approx 1.732a^2.$$

The volume is one-third of the base times the height, the general formula for a pyramid.[^n7] This can also be found by dissecting a cube into a tetrahedron and four triangular pyramids.[^n8]

$$V = \frac{1}{3} \cdot \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a^2\right) \cdot \frac{\sqrt{6}}{3}a = \frac{a^3}{6\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.118a^3.$$

*Image: Regular\_tetrahedron\_square\_cross\_section.png.* A central cross section of a regular tetrahedron is a [square](wikipedia://en/659939).[^o20]

The radii of its [circumsphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumsphere) $r_\mathrm{ci}$ (a sphere that contains the regular tetrahedron and touches its vertices), [insphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insphere) $r_\text{in}$ (a sphere within a regular tetrahedron and touches to its faces), [midsphere](wikipedia://en/4709281) $r_\mathrm{mi}$ (a sphere that touches its edges), and [exsphere](wikipedia://en/34873594) (a sphere outside, touches the face of a regular tetrahedron and the planes defined by extending the adjacent faces outwards) $r_\mathrm{ex}$ are:[^n7]

$$\begin{aligned} r_\mathrm{ci} = \frac{a\sqrt{3}}{2\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.612a, &\qquad r_\mathrm{in} = \frac{1}{3}r_\mathrm{ci} \approx 0.204a, \\ r_\mathrm{mi} = \sqrt{r_\mathrm{in} r_\mathrm{ci}} \approx 0.354a, &\qquad r_\mathrm{ex} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{6}} \approx 0.408a. \end{aligned}$$

For a regular tetrahedron with side length $a$ and circumsphere radius $R$, the distances $d_i$ from an arbitrary point in 3-space to its four vertices satisfy the equations:[^n9]

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{d_1^4 + d_2^4 + d_3^4 + d_4^4}{4} + \frac{16R^4}{9}&= \left(\frac{d_1^2 + d_2^2 + d_3^2 + d_4^2}{4} + \frac{2R^2}{3}\right)^2, \\ 4\left(a^4 + d_1^4 + d_2^4 + d_3^4 + d_4^4\right) &= \left(a^2 + d_1^2 + d_2^2 + d_3^2 + d_4^2\right)^2.\end{aligned}$$

With respect to the base plane the [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) of a face (2$\sqrt{2}$) is twice that of an edge ($\sqrt{2}$), corresponding to the fact that the *horizontal* distance covered from the base to the [apex](wikipedia://en/6022079) along an edge is twice that along the [median](wikipedia://en/1072006) of a face. In other words, if *C* is the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) of the base, the distance from *C* to a vertex of the base is twice that from *C* to the midpoint of an edge of the base. This follows from the fact that the medians of a triangle intersect at its centroid, and this point divides each of them in two segments, one of which is twice as long as the other (see [proof](wikipedia://en/187926#Proof_that_the_centroid_of_a_triangle_divides_each_median_in_the_ratio_2:1)).

Its [solid angle](wikipedia://en/94102) at a vertex subtended by a face is $\arccos\left(\frac{23}{27}\right) = \frac{\pi}{2} - 3\arcsin\left(\frac{1}{3}\right) = 3\arccos \left(\frac{1}{3}\right)-\pi,$ or approximately 0.55129 [steradians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steradian) or 1809.8 [square degrees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_degree).[^o23]

### Symmetry {#Symmetry}

*Image: Symmetries of the tetrahedron.svg.* Illustration of the rotational axes (order-3 rotation on a vertex and face, and order-2 on two edges) and reflection planes (through two faces and one edge) in the symmetry group of the regular tetrahedron

The regular tetrahedron has a [three-dimensional symmetry group](wikipedia://en/2694525) known as [full tetrahedral symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_tetrahedral_symmetry) $\mathrm{T}_\mathrm{d}$ or as the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_4$. This symmetry group has 24 isometries, containing seven rotational axes and six reflectional planes. The seven rotational axes are the four axes of three-fold rotational symmetry (0°, 120°, and 240°) passing through a vertex to the centroid of an equilateral triangular face, and the three axes of two-fold rotational symmetry (0° and 180°) passing through the midpoint of two edges. This point group has rotational tetrahedral symmetry $\mathrm{T}$.[^n10]

The vertices of a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) can be grouped into two groups of four, each forming a regular tetrahedron, showing one of the two tetrahedra in the cube. The [symmetries](wikipedia://en/2714149) of a regular tetrahedron correspond to half of those of a cube: those that map the tetrahedra to themselves, and not to each other. The tetrahedron is the only Platonic solid not mapped to itself by [point inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_inversion).

#### Spherical tetrahedron {#Spherical_tetrahedron}

*Image: Uniform tiling 332-t2.svg.* Spherical tetrahedron

The tetrahedron can also be represented as a [spherical tiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_tiling) (of [spherical triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_triangle)), and projected onto the plane via a [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431). This projection is [conformal](wikipedia://en/50627), preserving angles but not areas or lengths. Straight lines on the sphere are projected as circular arcs on the plane.

The first few members in the infinite families of regular [hosohedra](wikipedia://en/2316192) and [dihedra](wikipedia://en/6109457) are geometrically simpler than the spherical tetrahedron, where the [convex](wikipedia://en/4165882) regular tetrahedron instead classifies as the simplest regular polyhedron of its [class](wikipedia://en/20696). For example, the hexagonal hosohedron or dihedron and the spherical tetrahedron are regular spherical polyhedra of equal order and total [dimensional elements](wikipedia://en/23471#Elements), though they are non-[isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) (as they have distinct [point group](wikipedia://en/1138942) symmetries and associated fundamental domains).

#### Orthogonal projections {#Orthogonal_projections}

The regular tetrahedron has two special [orthogonal projections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_projection), one centered on a vertex or equivalently on a face, and one centered on an edge. The first corresponds to the A<sub>2</sub> [Coxeter plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_plane).

### Projective spaces {#Projective_spaces}

*Image: PG(3,2) model.png.* Extending the [Fano plane](wikipedia://en/390404) in three dimension yields the smallest [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135), $\operatorname{PG}(3,2)$, which can be represented in a regular tetrahedron.

The smallest three-dimensional [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) $\operatorname{PG}(3,2)$ shares symmetry with the regular tetrahedron, when extending the two-dimensional [Fano plane](wikipedia://en/390404) $\operatorname{PG}(2,2)$ in three dimensions. $\operatorname{PG}(3,2)$ has four points lying on the four vertices of a regular tetrahedron, six points on the midpoints of its six edges, four on the center (centroids) of its four faces, and a point at the interior core of the tetrahedron.[^n11] Every single plane is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to a Fano plane as the smallest projective plane. In the geometry over the field with one element $\operatorname{F}_1$, vector spaces behave like finite sets, and projective subspaces correspond to ordinary subsets, therefore, $\operatorname{PG}(3,1)$ forms a standard 3-simplex, which is abstractly a regular tetrahedron. $\operatorname{PG}(3,2)$ serves as the three-dimensional projective realization of the sixteen-dimensional [sedenions](wikipedia://en/51434) and their algebraic multiplication.

## Related figures {#Related_figures}

### Construction of polyhedra {#Construction_of_polyhedra}

Many polyhedra are constructed involving regular tetrahedra. By [truncating](wikipedia://en/4224429) the vertices of a regular tetrahedron, it becomes a [truncated tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/279651).[^n12] The dual of this solid is the [triakis tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/722548), a regular tetrahedron with four triangular pyramids attached to each of its faces. i.e., its [Kleetope](wikipedia://en/31165912).[^o36] Some [Johnson solid](wikipedia://en/16034) such as [elongated triangular pyramid](wikipedia://en/1177937) and [elongated triangular bipyramid](wikipedia://en/1177950) are constructed by attaching one and two regular tetrahedra onto the bases of a triangular prism; the [triangular bipyramid](wikipedia://en/646933) is constructed by attaching two regular tetrahedra face-to-face.[^n13]

Regular tetrahedra can be stacked face-to-face in a [chiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiral) aperiodic chain called the [Boerdijk–Coxeter helix](wikipedia://en/19172682).

#### Polyhedral compounds {#Polyhedral_compounds}

The [stellated octahedron](wikipedia://en/2601866) is constructed by [extending its faces](wikipedia://en/180253) to form equilateral triangles on each regular octahedron's face. In the perspective of [polyhedral compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedral_compound), this figure comprises two such dual tetrahedra,[^n14] a self-dual because of sharing a common intersphere in the center.[^n15] Another interesting polyhedral compound involves [five intersecting tetrahedra](wikipedia://en/3645795), which has been known for hundreds of years. It comes up regularly in the world of [origami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origami). Joining the twenty vertices would form a regular [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407). There are both [left-handed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-handed) and [right-handed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-handed) forms, which are [mirror images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror_image) of each other. Superimposing both forms gives a [compound of ten tetrahedra](wikipedia://en/8010465), in which the ten tetrahedra are arranged as five pairs of [stellae octangulae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stella_octangula).

### Dehn invariant and honeycombs {#Dehn_invariant_and_honeycombs}

The [Dehn invariant](wikipedia://en/338051) of a regular tetrahedron can be defined as a [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) of the edge length and the dihedral angle of a regular tetrahedron $6a \otimes \arccos \frac{1}{3}$, which is non-zero, where $a$ is the edge length of a regular tetrahedron. The Dehn invariant was originally dated from [Hilbert's third problem](wikipedia://en/152703), [a set of 23 problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_problems) by [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302), asking whether, given any two polyhedra with the same volume, the first polyhedron may be dissected into pieces, which then reassembled into the second polyhedron. His student, [Max Dehn](wikipedia://en/570602), provided the negative answer by showing his invariant.[^n16]

Every polyhedron with a Dehn invariant of zero can tile a space with its copy by attaching its faces to another, forming a [honeycomb](wikipedia://en/6008654). Since the regular tetrahedron has a non-zero Dehn invariant, it cannot alone do such. A related result that two different polyhedra that join to tile the space can have the Dehn invariant of zero.[^n17] In the case of a regular tetrahedron, it can be alternated with [regular octahedra](wikipedia://en/22458) in the ratio of two tetrahedra to one octahedron, forming the [alternated cubic honeycomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternated_cubic_honeycomb).[^n18]

### Four-dimensional figures {#Four-dimensional_figures}

The [pentachoron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentachoron) is a [four-dimensional polytope](wikipedia://en/23471), a generalization of a tetrahedron in four-dimensional space. It is bounded by five regular tetrahedra, known as [cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_%28mathematics%29).[^n19]

In [four dimensions](wikipedia://en/1364622), all the convex [regular 4-polytopes](wikipedia://en/18801624) with tetrahedral cells (the [5-cell](wikipedia://en/513821#Boerdijk–Coxeter_helix), [16-cell](wikipedia://en/716422#Helical_construction) and [600-cell](wikipedia://en/501447#Union_of_two_tori)) can be constructed as tilings of the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792) by these chains, which become periodic in the three-dimensional space of the 4-polytope's boundary surface.
