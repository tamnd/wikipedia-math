# Smooth infinitesimal analysis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Smooth infinitesimal analysis"}

**Smooth infinitesimal analysis** is a modern reformulation of the [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) in terms of [infinitesimals](wikipedia://en/160990). Based on the ideas of [F. W. Lawvere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._W._Lawvere) and employing the methods of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), it views all [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) as being [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) and incapable of being expressed in terms of [discrete](wikipedia://en/8492) entities. As a theory, it is a subset of [synthetic differential geometry](wikipedia://en/3869427).

:::

The *nilsquare* or *[nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235)* infinitesimals are numbers *ε* where *ε*² = 0 is true, but *ε* = 0 need not be true at the same time. *[Calculus Made Easy](wikipedia://en/14020714)* notably uses nilpotent infinitesimals.

## Overview {#Overview}

This approach departs from the [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868) used in conventional mathematics by denying the [law of the excluded middle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_excluded_middle), e.g., *NOT* (*a* ≠ *b*) does not imply *a* = *b*. In particular, in a theory of smooth infinitesimal analysis one can prove for all infinitesimals *ε*, *NOT* (*ε* ≠ 0); yet it is provably false that all infinitesimals are equal to zero.[^o3] One can see that the law of excluded middle cannot hold from the following basic theorem (again, understood in the context of a theory of smooth infinitesimal analysis):

*Every function whose [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) is* **R**, *the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), is continuous and [infinitely differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function).*

Despite this fact, one could attempt to define a discontinuous function *f*(*x*) by specifying that *f*(*x*) = 1 for *x* = 0, and *f*(*x*) = 0 for *x* ≠ 0. If the law of the excluded middle held, then this would be a fully defined, discontinuous function. However, there are plenty of *x*, namely the infinitesimals, such that neither *x* = 0 nor *x* ≠ 0 holds, so the function is not defined on the real numbers.

In typical [models](wikipedia://en/19858) of smooth infinitesimal analysis, the infinitesimals are not invertible, and therefore the theory does not contain infinite numbers. However, there are also models that include invertible infinitesimals.

Other mathematical systems exist which include infinitesimals, including [nonstandard analysis](wikipedia://en/21708) and the [surreal numbers](wikipedia://en/51432). Smooth infinitesimal analysis is like nonstandard analysis in that (1) it is meant to serve as a foundation for [analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), and (2) the infinitesimal quantities do not have concrete sizes (as opposed to the surreals, in which a typical infinitesimal is 1/ω, where ω is a [von Neumann ordinal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Neumann_ordinal)). However, smooth infinitesimal analysis differs from nonstandard analysis in its use of [nonclassical logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonclassical_logic), and in lacking the [transfer principle](wikipedia://en/637138). Some theorems of standard and nonstandard analysis fail in smooth infinitesimal analysis, including the [intermediate value theorem](wikipedia://en/14884) (without modification), and the [Banach–Tarski paradox](wikipedia://en/19759220). Statements in [nonstandard analysis](wikipedia://en/21708) can be translated into statements about [limits](wikipedia://en/26551602), but the same is not always true in smooth infinitesimal analysis.

Intuitively, smooth infinitesimal analysis can be interpreted as describing a world in which lines are made out of infinitesimally small segments, not out of points. These segments can be thought of as being long enough to have a definite direction, but not long enough to be curved. The construction of discontinuous functions fails because a function is identified with a curve, and the curve cannot be constructed pointwise. We can imagine the intermediate value theorem's failure as resulting from the ability of an infinitesimal segment to straddle a line. Similarly, the Banach–Tarski paradox fails because a volume cannot be taken apart into points.
