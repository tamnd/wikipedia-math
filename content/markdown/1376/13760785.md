# Prehomogeneous vector space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Prehomogeneous vector space"}

In mathematics, a **prehomogeneous vector space (PVS)** is a finite-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V* together with a subgroup *G* of the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) GL(*V*) such that *G* has an open dense [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) in *V*. The term **prehomogeneous vector space** was introduced by [Mikio Sato](wikipedia://en/373336) in 1970. These spaces have many applications in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) and [analysis](wikipedia://en/1134), as well as [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200). The irreducible PVS were classified first by Vinberg in his 1960 thesis in the special case when G is simple and later by Sato and Tatsuo Kimura in 1977 in the general case by means of a transformation known as "castling". They are subdivided into two types, according to whether the semisimple part of *G* acts prehomogeneously or not. If it doesn't then there is a homogeneous polynomial on *V* which is invariant under the semisimple part of *G*.

:::

## Setting {#Setting}

In the setting of Sato, *G* is an [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924) and *V* is a rational representation of *G* which has a (nonempty) open orbit in the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744). However, PVS can also be studied from the point of view of Lie theory: for instance, in Knapp (2002), *G* is a complex [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) and *V* is a holomorphic representation of *G* with an open dense orbit. The two approaches are essentially the same, and the theory has validity over the real numbers. We assume, for simplicity of notation, that the action of *G* on *V* is a [faithful representation](wikipedia://en/2617872). We can then identify *G* with its image in GL(*V*), although in practice it is sometimes convenient to let *G* be a [covering group](wikipedia://en/1378699).

Although prehomogeneous vector spaces do not necessarily decompose into direct sums of irreducibles, it is natural to study the irreducible PVS (i.e., when *V* is an irreducible representation of *G*). In this case, a theorem of [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) shows that

*G* ≤ GL(*V*)

is a [reductive group](wikipedia://en/1088386), with a [centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_%28group_theory%29) that is at most one-dimensional. This, together with the obvious dimensional restriction

dim *G* ≥ dim *V*,

is the key ingredient in the Sato–Kimura classification.

## Castling {#Castling}

The classification of PVS is complicated by the following fact. Suppose *m* > *n* > 0 and *V* is an *m*-dimensional representation of *G* over a field F. Then:

(*G* × SL(*n*), *V* ⊗ **F**<sup>*n*</sup>) is a PVS if and only if (*G* × SL(*m* − *n*), *V*<sup>\*</sup> ⊗ **F**<sup>*m*−*n*</sup>) is a PVS.

The proof is to observe that both conditions are equivalent to there being an open dense orbit of the action of *G* on the [Grassmannian](wikipedia://en/373810) of *n*-planes in *V*, because this is isomorphic to the [Grassmannian](wikipedia://en/373810) of (*m* − *n*)-planes in *V*<sup>\*</sup>.

(In the case that *G* is reductive, the pair (*G*, *V*) is equivalent to the pair (*G*, *V*<sup>\*</sup>) by an automorphism of *G*.)

This transformation of PVS is called [castling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castling). Given a PVS *V*, a new PVS can be obtained by tensoring *V* with F and castling. By repeating this process, and regrouping tensor products, many new examples can be obtained, which are said to be "castling-equivalent". Thus PVS can be grouped into castling equivalence classes. Sato and Kimura show that in each such class, there is essentially one PVS of minimal dimension, which they call "reduced", and they classify the reduced irreducible PVS.

## Classification {#Classification}

The classification of irreducible reduced PVS (*G*, *V*) splits into two cases: those for which *G* is semisimple, and those for which it is reductive with one-dimensional centre. If *G* is semisimple, it is (perhaps a covering of) a subgroup of SL(*V*), and hence *G* × GL(1) acts prehomogenously on *V*, with one-dimensional centre. We exclude such trivial extensions of semisimple PVS from the PVS with one-dimensional center. In other words, in the case that *G* has one-dimensional center, we assume that the semisimple part does *not* act prehomogeneously; it follows that there is a *relative invariant*, i.e., a function invariant under the semisimple part of *G*, which is homogeneous of a certain degree *d*.

This makes it possible to restrict attention to semisimple *G* ≤ SL(*V*) and split the classification as follows:

1. (*G*, *V*) is a PVS;
1. (*G*, *V*) is not a PVS, but (*G* × GL(1), *V*) is.

However, it turns out that the classification is much shorter, if one allows not just products with GL(1), but also with SL(*n*) and GL(*n*). This is quite natural in terms of the castling transformation discussed previously. Thus we wish to classify irreducible reduced PVS in terms of semisimple *G* ≤ SL(*V*) and *n* ≥ 1 such that either:

1. (*G* × SL(*n*), *V* ⊗ **F**<sup>*n*</sup>) is a PVS;
1. (*G* × SL(*n*), *V* ⊗ **F**<sup>*n*</sup>) is not a PVS, but (*G* × GL(*n*), *V* ⊗ **F**<sup>*n*</sup>) is.

In the latter case, there is a [homogeneous polynomial](wikipedia://en/1698977) which separates the *G* × GL(*n*) orbits into *G* × SL(*n*) orbits.

This has an interpretation in terms of the grassmannian Gr<sub>*n*</sub>(*V*) of *n*-planes in *V* (at least for *n* ≤ dim *V*). In both cases *G* acts on Gr<sub>*n*</sub>(*V*) with a dense open orbit *U*. In the first case the complement Gr<sub>*n*</sub>(*V*) ∖ *U* has [codimension](wikipedia://en/525149) ≥ 2; in the second case it is a [divisor](wikipedia://en/1693055) of some degree *d*, and the relative invariant is a homogeneous polynomial of degree *nd*.

In the following, the classification list will be presented over the complex numbers.

## Proofs {#Proofs}

Sato and Kimura establish this classification by producing a list of possible irreducible prehomogeneous (*G*, *V*), using the fact that *G* is reductive and the dimensional restriction. They then check whether each member of this list is prehomogeneous or not.

However, there is a general explanation why most of the pairs (*G*, *V*) in the classification are prehomogeneous, in terms of isotropy representations of [generalized flag varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_variety). Indeed, in 1974, [Richardson](wikipedia://en/19186114) observed that if *H* is a semisimple Lie group with a [parabolic subgroup](wikipedia://en/588356) *P*, then the action of *P* on the [nilradical](wikipedia://en/22468313) $\mathfrak{p}$<sup>⊥</sup> of its Lie algebra has a dense open orbit. This shows in particular (and was noted independently by [Vinberg](wikipedia://en/19177826) in 1975) that the [Levi factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi_factor) *G* of *P* acts prehomogeneously on *V* := $\mathfrak{p}$<sup>⊥</sup>/\[$\mathfrak{p}$<sup>⊥</sup>, $\mathfrak{p}$<sup>⊥</sup>\]. Almost all of the examples in the classification can be obtained by applying this construction with *P* a maximal parabolic subgroup of a simple Lie group *H*: these are classified by connected [Dynkin diagrams](wikipedia://en/277109) with one distinguished node.

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

One reason that PVS are interesting is that they classify generic objects that arise in *G*-invariant situations. For example, if *G* = GL(7), then the above tables show that there are generic 3-forms under the action of *G*, and the stabilizer of such a 3-form is isomorphic to the exceptional Lie group [G<sub>2</sub>](wikipedia://en/292864).

Another example concerns the prehomogeneous vector spaces with a cubic relative invariant. By the Sato-Kimura classification, there are essentially four such examples, and they all come from complexified isotropy representations of [hermitian symmetric spaces](wikipedia://en/4127357) for a larger group *H* (i.e., *G* is the semisimple part of the stabilizer of a point, and *V* is the corresponding [tangent](wikipedia://en/30807) representation).

In each case a generic point in *V* identifies it with the complexification of a [Jordan algebra](wikipedia://en/936371) of 3 × 3 hermitian matrices (over the [division algebras](wikipedia://en/26549625) **R**, **C**, **H** and **O** respectively) and the cubic relative invariant is identified with a suitable determinant. The isotropy algebra of such a generic point, the Lie algebra of *G* and the Lie algebra of *H* give the complexifications of the first three rows of the [Freudenthal magic square](wikipedia://en/13796065).

::: {#o14 .table cue=table}

| *H* | *G* | *V* | Isotropy algebra | Jordan algebra |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sp(6, **C**) | SL(3, **C**) | *S*<sup>2</sup>**C**<sup>3</sup> | $\mathfrak{so}$(3, **C**) | *J*<sub>3</sub>(**R**) |
| SL(6, **C**) | SL(3, **C**) × SL(3, **C**) | **C**<sup>3</sup> ⊗ **C**<sup>3</sup> | $\mathfrak{sl}$(3, **C**) | *J*<sub>3</sub>(**C**) |
| SO(12, **C**) | SL(6, **C**) | Λ<sup>2</sup>**C**<sup>6</sup> | $\mathfrak{sp}$(6, **C**) | *J*<sub>3</sub>(**H**) |
| *E*${}^{\mathbf{C}}_{7}$ | *E*${}^{\mathbf{C}}_{6}$ | **C**<sup>27</sup> | $\mathfrak{f}$${}^{\mathbf{C}}_{4}$ | *J*<sub>3</sub>(**O**) |

:::

Other Hermitian symmetric spaces yields prehomogeneous vector spaces whose generic points define Jordan algebras in a similar way.

::: {#o15 .table cue=table}

| *H* | *G* | *V* | Isotropy algebra | Jordan algebra |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sp(2*n*, **C**) | SL(*n*, **C**) | *S*<sup>2</sup>**C**<sup>n</sup> | $\mathfrak{so}$(*n*, **C**) | *J*<sub>*n*</sub>(**R**) |
| SL(2*n*, **C**) | SL(*n*, **C**) × SL(*n*, **C**) | **C**<sup>*n*</sup> ⊗ **C**<sup>*n*</sup> | $\mathfrak{sl}$(*n*, **C**) | *J*<sub>*n*</sub>(**C**) |
| SO(4*n*, **C**) | SL(2*n*, **C**) | Λ<sup>2</sup>**C**<sup>2*n*</sup> | $\mathfrak{sp}$(2*n*, **C**) | *J*<sub>*n*</sub>(**H**) |
| SO(*m* + 2, **C**) | SO(*m*, **C**) | **C**<sup>*m*</sup> | $\mathfrak{so}$(*m* − 1, **C**) | *J*(*m* − 1) |

:::

The Jordan algebra *J*(*m* − 1) in the last row is the spin factor (which is the vector space **R**<sup>*m*−1</sup> ⊕ **R**, with a Jordan algebra structure defined using the inner product on **R**<sup>*m*−1</sup>). It reduces to *J*<sub>2</sub>(**R**), *J*<sub>2</sub>(**C**), *J*<sub>2</sub>(**H**), *J*<sub>2</sub>(**O**) for *m* = 3, 4, 6 and 10 respectively.

The relation between hermitian symmetric spaces and Jordan algebras can be explained using [Jordan triple systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_triple_system).

:::
