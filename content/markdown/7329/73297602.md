# Round (cryptography)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Round"}

In [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), a **round** or **round function** is a basic transformation that is repeated ([iterated](wikipedia://en/68833)) multiple times inside the algorithm. Splitting a large algorithmic function into rounds simplifies both implementation and [cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715).[^n1]

:::

For example, encryption using an oversimplified three-round cipher can be written as $C = R_3(R_2(R_1(P)))$, where $C$ is the [ciphertext](wikipedia://en/175560) and $P$ is the [plaintext](wikipedia://en/157935). Typically, rounds $R_1, R_2, ...$ are implemented using the same function, parameterized by the **round constant** and, for [block ciphers](wikipedia://en/4594), the *round key* from the [key schedule](wikipedia://en/609603). Parameterization is essential to reduce the **self-similarity** of the cipher, which could lead to [slide attacks](wikipedia://en/1821327).[^n1]

Increasing the number of rounds "almost always"[^n2] protects against [differential](wikipedia://en/8536) and [linear cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/59469), as for these tools the effort grows exponentially with the number of rounds. However, increasing the number of rounds does not *always* make weak ciphers into strong ones, as some attacks do not depend on the number of rounds.[^n3]

The idea of an [iterative cipher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iterative_cipher) using repeated application of simple [non-commutating](wikipedia://en/294390) operations producing [diffusion and confusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_and_confusion) goes as far back as 1945, to the then-secret version of [C. E. Shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._E._Shannon)'s work "[Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems](wikipedia://en/452085)";[^o6] Shannon was inspired by mixing transformations used in the field of [dynamical systems theory](wikipedia://en/990632) (cf. [horseshoe map](wikipedia://en/449364)). Most of the modern ciphers use [iterative design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iterative_design) with number of rounds usually chosen between 8 and 32 (with 64 and even 80 used in [cryptographic hashes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptographic_hash)).[^n4]

For some [Feistel-like cipher](wikipedia://en/352709) descriptions, notably that of the [RC5](wikipedia://en/102600), a term "**half-round**" is used to define the transformation of part of the data (a distinguishing feature of the Feistel design). This operation corresponds to a full round in traditional descriptions of Feistel ciphers (like [DES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_encryption_standard)).[^n5]

::: {#o10 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Round constant"}

## Round constants {#Round_constants}

Inserting round-dependent constants into the encryption process breaks the symmetry between rounds and thus thwarts the most obvious slide attacks.[^n3] The technique is a standard feature of most modern block ciphers. However, a poor choice of round constants or unintended interrelations between the constants and other cipher components could still allow slide attacks (e.g., attacking the initial version of the [format-preserving encryption](wikipedia://en/23271188) mode FF3).[^n6]

Many [lightweight ciphers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lightweight_cipher) utilize very simple key scheduling: the round keys come from adding the [round constants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round_constant) to the [encryption key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encryption_key). A poor choice of round constants in this case might make the cipher vulnerable to [invariant attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_attack); ciphers broken this way include [SCREAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SCREAM_%28cipher%29) and [Midori64](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midori64).[^n7]

:::

## Optimization {#Optimization}

[Daemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Daemen) and [Rijmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_Rijmen) assert that one of the goals of optimizing the cipher is reducing the overall workload, the product of the round complexity and the number of rounds. There are two approaches to address this goal:[^n2]

- local optimization improves the worst-case behavior of a single round (two rounds for Feistel ciphers);
- [global optimization](wikipedia://en/563854) optimizes the worst-case behavior of more than one round, allowing the use of less sophisticated components.

::: {#o17 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Reduced-round"}

## Reduced-round ciphers {#Reduced-round_ciphers}

[Cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715) techniques include the use of versions of ciphers with fewer rounds than specified by their designers. Since a single round is usually cryptographically weak, many attacks that fail to work against the full version of ciphers will work on such *reduced-round* variants. The result of such attack provides valuable information about the strength of the algorithm,[^n8] a typical break of the full cipher starts out as a success against a reduced-round one.[^n9]

Sateesan et al. propose using the reduced-round versions of lightweight hashes and ciphers as [non-cryptographic hash functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-cryptographic_hash_function).[^n10]

:::
