# Skew lines

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Skew lines"}

*Image: Rectangular parallelepiped.png.* [Rectangular parallelepiped](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_parallelepiped). The line through segment AD and the line through segment B<sub>1</sub>B are skew lines because they are not in the same plane.

In [three-dimensional geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-dimensional_geometry), **skew lines** are two [lines](wikipedia://en/946975) that do not [intersect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line-line_intersection) and are not [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497). A simple example of a pair of skew lines is the pair of lines through opposite edges of a [regular tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/5242521). Two lines that both lie in the same plane must either cross each other or be parallel, so skew lines can exist only in three or more [dimensions](wikipedia://en/8398). Two lines are skew if and only if they are not [coplanar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coplanar).

:::

## General position {#General_position}

If four points are chosen at random [uniformly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29) within a unit [cube](wikipedia://en/6285), they will [almost surely](wikipedia://en/351908) define a pair of skew lines. After the first three points have been chosen, the fourth point will define a non-skew line if, and only if, it is coplanar with the first three points. However, the plane through the first three points forms a subset of measure zero of the cube, and the probability that the fourth point lies on this plane is zero. If it does not, the lines defined by the points will be skew.

Similarly, in three-dimensional space a very small perturbation of any two parallel or intersecting lines will almost certainly turn them into skew lines. Therefore, any four points in [general position](wikipedia://en/383736) always form skew lines.

In this sense, skew lines are the "usual" case, and parallel or intersecting lines are special cases.

### Testing for skewness {#Testing_for_skewness}

If each line in a pair of skew lines is defined by two [points](wikipedia://en/593693) that it passes through, then these four points must not be coplanar, so they must be the [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) of a [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606) of nonzero [volume](wikipedia://en/32498). Conversely, any two pairs of points defining a tetrahedron of nonzero volume also define a pair of skew lines. Therefore, a test of whether two pairs of points define skew lines is to apply the formula for the volume of a tetrahedron in terms of its four vertices. Denoting one point as the 1×3 vector $\mathbf{a}$ whose three elements are the point's three coordinate values, and likewise denoting $\mathbf{b}$, $\mathbf{c}$, and $\mathbf{d}$ for the other points, we can check if the line through $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ is skew to the line through $\mathbf{c}$ and $\mathbf{d}$ by seeing if the tetrahedron volume formula gives a non-zero result:

$$V=\frac{1}{6}\left|\det\left[\begin{matrix}\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b} \\ \mathbf{b}-\mathbf{c} \\ \mathbf{c}-\mathbf{d} \end{matrix}\right]\right|.$$

### Nearest points {#Nearest_points}

Expressing the two lines as vectors:

$$\text{Line 1:} \; \mathbf{v_1}=\mathbf{p_1}+t_1\mathbf{d_1}$$

$$\text{Line 2:} \; \mathbf{v_2}=\mathbf{p_2}+t_2\mathbf{d_2}$$

The [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) of $\mathbf{d_1}$ and $\mathbf{d_2}$ is perpendicular to the lines.

$$\mathbf{n}= \mathbf{d_1} \times \mathbf{d_2}$$

The plane formed by the translations of Line 2 along $\mathbf{n}$ contains the point $\mathbf{p_2}$ and is perpendicular to $\mathbf{n_2}= \mathbf{d_2} \times \mathbf{n}$.

Therefore, the intersecting point of Line 1 with the above-mentioned plane, which is also the point on Line 1 that is nearest to Line 2 is given by

$$\mathbf{c_1}=\mathbf{p_1}+ \frac{(\mathbf{p_2}-\mathbf{p_1})\cdot\mathbf{n_2}}{\mathbf{d_1}\cdot\mathbf{n_2}} \mathbf{d_1}$$

Similarly, the point on Line 2 nearest to Line 1 is given by (where $\mathbf{n_1}= \mathbf{d_1} \times \mathbf{n}$ )

$$\mathbf{c_2}=\mathbf{p_2}+ \frac{(\mathbf{p_1}-\mathbf{p_2})\cdot\mathbf{n_1}}{\mathbf{d_2}\cdot\mathbf{n_1}} \mathbf{d_2}$$

::: {#o8 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Distance between two skew lines"}

#### Distance {#Distance}

The nearest points $\mathbf{c_1}$ and $\mathbf{c_2}$ form the shortest line segment joining Line 1 and Line 2:

$$d = \Vert \mathbf{c_1} - \mathbf{c_2} \Vert.$$

The distance between nearest points in two skew lines may also be expressed using other vectors:

$$\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{a} + \lambda \mathbf{b};$$

$$\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{c} + \mu \mathbf{d}.$$

Here the 1×3 vector $\mathbf{x}$ represents an arbitrary point on the line through particular point $\mathbf{a}$ with $\mathbf{b}$ representing the direction of the line and with the value of the real number $\lambda$ determining where the point is on the line, and similarly for arbitrary point $\mathbf{y}$ on the line through particular point $\mathbf{c}$ in direction $\mathbf{d}$.

The [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) of **b** and **d** is perpendicular to the lines, as is the [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053)

$$\mathbf{n} = \frac{\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{d}}{||\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{d}||}$$

The [perpendicular distance](wikipedia://en/13556959) between the lines is then

$$d = |\mathbf{n} \cdot (\mathbf{c} - \mathbf{a})|.$$

(if |**b** × **d**| is zero the lines are parallel and this method cannot be used).

:::

## More than two lines {#More_than_two_lines}

### Configurations {#Configurations}

A *configuration* of skew lines is a set of lines in which all pairs are skew. Two configurations are said to be *isotopic* if it is possible to continuously transform one configuration into the other, maintaining throughout the transformation the invariant that all pairs of lines remain skew. Any two configurations of two lines are easily seen to be isotopic, and configurations of the same number of lines in dimensions higher than three are always isotopic, but there exist multiple non-isotopic configurations of three or more lines in three dimensions. The number of nonisotopic configurations of *n* lines in **R**<sup>3</sup>, starting at *n* = 1, is

1, 1, 2, 3, 7, 19, 74, ... .

### Ruled surfaces {#Ruled_surfaces}

*Image: Nested hyperboloids.png.* A [fibration](wikipedia://en/276526) of [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) by skew lines on nested [hyperboloids](wikipedia://en/145844).

If one rotates a line *L* around another line *M* skew but not perpendicular to it, the [surface of revolution](wikipedia://en/329549) swept out by *L* is a [hyperboloid of one sheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperboloid_of_one_sheet). For instance, the three hyperboloids visible in the illustration can be formed in this way by rotating a line *L* around the central white vertical line *M*. The copies of *L* within this surface form a [regulus](wikipedia://en/52079701); the hyperboloid also contains a second family of lines that are also skew to *M* at the same distance as *L* from it but with the opposite angle that form the opposite regulus. The two reguli display the hyperboloid as a [ruled surface](wikipedia://en/705158).

An [affine transformation](wikipedia://en/38449) of this [ruled surface](wikipedia://en/705158) produces a surface which in general has an elliptical cross-section rather than the circular cross-section produced by rotating L around L'; such surfaces are also called hyperboloids of one sheet, and again are ruled by two families of mutually skew lines. A third type of ruled surface is the [hyperbolic paraboloid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_paraboloid). Like the hyperboloid of one sheet, the hyperbolic paraboloid has two families of skew lines; in each of the two families the lines are parallel to a common plane although not to each other. Any three skew lines in **R**<sup>3</sup> lie on exactly one ruled surface of one of these types.

### Gallucci's theorem {#Gallucci's_theorem}

If three skew lines all meet three other skew lines, any transversal of the first set of three meets any transversal of the second set.

## Skew flats in higher dimensions {#Skew_flats_in_higher_dimensions}

In higher-dimensional space, a [flat](wikipedia://en/13005617) of dimension *k* is referred to as a *k*-flat. Thus, a line may also be called a 1-flat.

Generalizing the concept of *skew lines* to *d*-dimensional space, an *i*-flat and a *j*-flat may be **skew** if $i + j < d$.[^o16] As with lines in 3-space, skew flats are those that are neither parallel nor intersect.

In [affine *d*-space](wikipedia://en/243890), two flats of any dimension may be parallel. However, in [projective space](wikipedia://en/243849), parallelism does not exist; two flats must either intersect or be skew. Let $I$ be the set of points on an *i*-flat, and let $J$ be the set of points on a *j*-flat. In projective *d*-space, if $i + j \ge d$ then the intersection of $I$ and $J$ must contain a (*i*+*j*−*d*)-flat. (A *0*-flat is a point.)

In either geometry, if $I$ and $J$ intersect at a *k*-flat, for $k \ge 0$, then the points of $I \cup J$ determine a (*i*+*j*−*k*)-flat.

:::
