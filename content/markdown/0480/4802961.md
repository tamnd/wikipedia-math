# Bicomplex number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bicomplex number"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **bicomplex number** is a pair (*w*, *z*) of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) constructed by the [Cayley–Dickson process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cayley–Dickson_process) that defines the bicomplex conjugate $(w,z)^* = (w, -z)$, and the product of two bicomplex numbers as

:::

$$(u,v)(w,z) = (u w - v z, u z + v w).$$

Then the **bicomplex norm** is given by

$(w,z)^* (w,z) = (w, -z)(w,z) = (w^2 + z^2, 0),$ a [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) in the first component.

The bicomplex numbers form a commutative [algebra over **C**](wikipedia://en/191788) of dimension two that is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the [direct sum of algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_sum_of_algebras) **C** ⊕ **C**.

The product of two bicomplex numbers yields a quadratic form value that is the product of the individual quadratic forms of the numbers: a verification of this property of the quadratic form of a product refers to the [Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity](wikipedia://en/326483). This property of the quadratic form of a bicomplex number indicates that these numbers form a [composition algebra](wikipedia://en/3861635). In fact, bicomplex numbers arise at the binarion level of the Cayley–Dickson construction based on $\mathbb{C}$ with norm z<sup>2</sup>.

The general bicomplex number can be represented by the matrix $\begin{pmatrix}w & iz \\ iz & w \end{pmatrix}$, which has [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) $w^2 + z^2$. Thus, the composing property of the quadratic form concurs with the composing property of the determinant.

Bicomplex numbers feature two distinct [imaginary units](wikipedia://en/52358). Multiplication being associative and commutative, the product of these imaginary units must have positive one for its square. Such an element as this product has been called a [hyperbolic unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_unit).[^o2]

## As a real algebra {#As_a_real_algebra}

*Tessarine multiplication*

::: {#o4 .table cue=table name="Tessarine multiplication"}

| × | 1 | *i* | *j* | *k* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | *i* | *j* | *k* |
| *i* | *i* | −1 | *k* | −*j* |
| *j* | *j* | *k* | 1 | *i* |
| *k* | *k* | −*j* | *i* | −1 |

:::

Bicomplex numbers form an algebra over **C** of dimension two, and since **C** is of dimension two over **R**, the bicomplex numbers are an algebra over **R** of dimension four. In fact the real algebra is older than the complex one; it was labelled *tessarines* in 1848 while the complex algebra was not introduced until 1892.

A [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for the tessarine 4-algebra over **R** uses the following [units](wikipedia://en/289666) (with matrix representations given): the [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity) $1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, the same [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358) $i = \begin{pmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & i \end{pmatrix}$ as in the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), the same [hyperbolic unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_unit) $j = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ as in the [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559) and a second imaginary unit $k = i j = j i = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which multiply according to the table given.

## History {#History}

The subject of multiple [imaginary units](wikipedia://en/52358) was examined in the 1840s. In a long series "On quaternions, or on a new system of imaginaries in algebra" beginning in 1844 in [Philosophical Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Magazine), [William Rowan Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817) communicated a system multiplying according to the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130). In 1848 [Thomas Kirkman](wikipedia://en/7803789) reported on his correspondence with [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034) regarding equations on the units determining a system of hypercomplex numbers.[^o6]

### Tessarines {#Tessarines}

In 1848 [James Cockle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cockle_%28lawyer%29) introduced the tessarines in a series of articles in *Philosophical Magazine*.[^o9]

A **tessarine** is a hypercomplex number of the form

$$t = w + x i + y j + z k, \quad w, x, y, z \in \mathbb{R}$$

where $i j = j i = k, \quad i^2 = -1, \quad j^2 = +1 .$ Cockle used tessarines to isolate the hyperbolic cosine series and the hyperbolic sine series in the exponential series. He also showed how [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441) arise in tessarines, inspiring him to use the term "impossibles". The tessarines are now best known for their subalgebra of **real tessarines** $t = w + y j \$, also called [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559), which express the parametrization of the [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122).

:::

### Bicomplex numbers {#Bicomplex_numbers}

*Bicomplex number multiplication*

::: {#o11 .table cue=table name="Bicomplex number multiplication"}

| × | 1 | *h* | *i* | *hi* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | *h* | *i* | *hi* |
| *h* | *h* | −1 | *hi* | −*i* |
| *i* | *i* | *hi* | −1 | −*h* |
| *hi* | *hi* | −*i* | −*h* | 1 |

:::

In an 1892 *[Mathematische Annalen](wikipedia://en/1423381)* paper, [Corrado Segre](wikipedia://en/2422061) introduced **bicomplex numbers**,[^o12] which form an algebra isomorphic to the tessarines.

Segre read [W. R. Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._R._Hamilton)'s *Lectures on Quaternions* (1853) and the works of [W. K. Clifford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._K._Clifford). Segre used some of Hamilton's notation to develop his system of **bicomplex numbers**: Let *h* and *i* be elements that square to −1 and that commute. Then, presuming [associativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity) of multiplication, the product *hi* must square to +1. The algebra constructed on the basis { 1, *h*, *i*, *hi* } is then the same as James Cockle's tessarines, represented using a different basis. Segre noted that elements

$g = (1 - hi)/2, \quad g' = (1 + hi)/2$ are [idempotents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent).

When bicomplex numbers are expressed in terms of the basis { 1, *h*, *i*, −*hi* }, their equivalence with tessarines is apparent, particularly if the vectors in this basis are reordered as { 1, *i*, −*hi*, *h* }. Looking at the linear representation of these [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_isomorphism) algebras shows agreement in the fourth dimension when the negative sign is used; consider the sample product given above under linear representation.

### Bibinarions {#Bibinarions}

The modern theory of [composition algebras](wikipedia://en/3861635) positions the algebra as a binarion construction based on another binarion construction, hence the **bibinarions**. The unarion level in the Cayley-Dickson process must be a field, and starting with the real field, the usual complex numbers arises as division binarions, another field. Thus the process can begin again to form bibinarions. [Kevin McCrimmon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_McCrimmon) noted the simplification of nomenclature provided by the term *binarion* in his text *A Taste of Jordan Algebras* (2004).

## Polynomial roots {#Polynomial_roots}

Write <sup>2</sup>**C** = **C** ⊕ **C** and represent elements of it by ordered pairs (*u*,*v*) of complex numbers. Since the algebra of tessarines **T** is isomorphic to <sup>2</sup>**C**, the [rings of polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_polynomials) **T**\[X\] and <sup>2</sup>**C**\[*X*\] are also isomorphic, however polynomials in the latter algebra split:

$$\sum_{k=1}^n (a_k, b_k ) (u, v)^k \quad = \quad \left({\sum_{k=1}^n a_i u^k},\quad \sum_{k=1}^n b_k v^k \right).$$

In consequence, when a polynomial equation $f(u,v) = (0,0)$ in this algebra is set, it reduces to two polynomial equations on **C**. If the degree is *n*, then there are *n* [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) for each equation: $u_1, u_2, \dots, u_n,\ v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n .$ Any ordered pair $( u_i, v_j ) \!$ from this set of roots will satisfy the original equation in <sup>2</sup>**C**\[*X*\], so it has *n*<sup>2</sup> roots.[^o17]

Due to the isomorphism with **T**\[*X*\], there is a correspondence of polynomials and a correspondence of their roots. Hence the tessarine polynomials of degree *n* also have *n*<sup>2</sup> roots, counting [multiplicity of roots](wikipedia://en/19211790).

## Applications {#Applications}

Bicomplex number appears as the center of CAPS (complexified [algebra of physical space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_of_physical_space)), which is Clifford algebra $Cl(3,\mathbb{C})$.[^o20] Since the linear space of CAPS can be viewed as the four dimensional space span {$1, e_1, e_2, e_3$} over {$1,i,k,j$}.

Tessarines have been applied in [digital signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_processing).[^o21][^o22][^o23]

Bicomplex numbers are employed in fluid mechanics. The use of bicomplex algebra reconciles two distinct applications of complex numbers: the representation of [two-dimensional potential flows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_flow_in_two_dimensions) in the complex plane and the [complex exponential function](wikipedia://en/9613).[^o24]
