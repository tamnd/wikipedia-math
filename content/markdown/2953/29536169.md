# Key disclosure law

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Key disclosure law"}

**Key disclosure laws**, also known as **mandatory key disclosure**, is legislation that requires individuals to surrender [cryptographic keys](wikipedia://en/53039) to law enforcement. The purpose is to allow access to material for confiscation or [digital forensics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_forensics) purposes and use it either as evidence in a court of law or to enforce [national security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_security) interests. Similarly, **mandatory decryption** laws force owners of [encrypted data](wikipedia://en/10294) to supply decrypted data to law enforcement.[^o2]

:::

Nations vary widely in the specifics of how they implement key disclosure laws. Some, such as Australia, give law enforcement wide-ranging power to compel assistance in decrypting data from any party. Some, such as Belgium, concerned with [self-incrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-incrimination), only allow law enforcement to compel assistance from non-suspects. Some require only specific third parties such as telecommunications carriers, certification providers, or maintainers of encryption services to provide assistance with decryption. In all cases, a warrant is generally required.

## Theory and countermeasures {#Theory_and_countermeasures}

Mandatory decryption is technically a weaker requirement than key disclosure, since it is possible in some cryptosystems to prove that a message has been decrypted correctly without revealing the key. For example, using [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29) public-key encryption, one can verify given the message ([plaintext](wikipedia://en/157935)), the encrypted message (ciphertext), and the public key of the recipient that the message is correct by merely re-encrypting it and comparing the result to the encrypted message. Such a scheme is called *undeniable*, since once the government has validated the message they cannot deny that it is the correct decrypted message.[^o4]

As a countermeasure to key disclosure laws, some personal privacy products such as [BestCrypt](wikipedia://en/7748321), [FreeOTFE](wikipedia://en/4954583), and [TrueCrypt](wikipedia://en/905962) have begun incorporating [deniable encryption](wikipedia://en/1055437) technology, which enable a single piece of encrypted data to be decrypted in two or more different ways, creating [plausible deniability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plausible_deniability).[^o5] Another alternative is [steganography](wikipedia://en/28733), which hides encrypted data inside of benign data so that it is more difficult to identify in the first place.

A problematic aspect of key disclosure is that it leads to a total compromise of all data encrypted using that key in the past or future; time-limited encryption schemes such as those of Desmedt et al.[^o4] allow decryption only for a limited time period.

## Criticism and alternatives {#Criticism_and_alternatives}

Critics of key disclosure laws view them as compromising [information privacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_privacy),[^o2] by revealing personal information that may not be pertinent to the crime under investigation, as well as violating the right against [self-incrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-incrimination) and more generally the [right to silence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_silence), in nations which respect these rights. In some cases, it may be impossible to decrypt the data because the key has been lost, forgotten or revoked, or because the data is actually random data which cannot be effectively distinguished from encrypted data.

A proactive alternative to key disclosure law is [key escrow](wikipedia://en/17114) law, where the government holds in escrow a copy of all cryptographic keys in use, but is only permitted to use them if an appropriate warrant is issued. Key escrow systems face difficult technical issues and are subject to many of the same criticisms as key disclosure law; they avoid some issues like lost keys, while introducing new issues such as the risk of accidental disclosure of large numbers of keys, theft of the keys by hackers or abuse of power by government employees with access to the keys. It would also be nearly impossible to prevent the government from secretly using the key database to aid mass surveillance efforts such as those exposed by [Edward Snowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Snowden).[^o2] The ambiguous term *key recovery* is applied to both types of systems.

## Legislation by nation {#Legislation_by_nation}

This list shows only nations where laws or cases are known about this topic.

### Antigua and Barbuda {#Antigua_and_Barbuda}

The Computer Misuse Bill, 2006, Article 21(5)(c), if enacted, would allow police with a warrant to demand and use decryption keys. Failure to comply may incur "a fine of fifteen thousand \[East Caribbean\] dollars" and/or "imprisonment for two years."[^o10]

### Australia {#Australia}

The Cybercrime Act 2001 No. 161, Items 12 and 28 grant police with a magistrate's order the wide-ranging power to require "a specified person to provide any information or assistance that is reasonable and necessary to allow the officer to "access computer data that is "evidential material"; this is understood to include mandatory decryption. Failing to comply carries a penalty of 6 months' imprisonment.[^o12] [Electronic Frontiers Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontiers_Australia) calls the provision "alarming" and "contrary to the [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law) privilege against self-incrimination."[^o13]

The Crimes Act 1914, 3LA(5) "A person commits an offence if the person fails to comply with the order. Penalty for contravention of this subsection: Imprisonment for 2 years."[^o14]

### Belgium {#Belgium}

The *Loi du 28 novembre 2000 relative à la criminalité informatique* (Law on computer crime of 28 November 2000), Article 9 allows a judge to order the authorities to search the computer systems and telecommunications providers to provide assistance to law enforcement, including mandatory decryption, and to keep their assistance secret; but this action cannot be taken against suspects or their families.[^o16][^o17] Failure to comply is punishable by 6 months to 1 year in jail and/or a fine of 130 to 100,000 euros.

### Cambodia {#Cambodia}

Cambodia promulgated its Law on Electronic Commerce on 2 November 2019, after passage through legislature and receiving consent from the monarch, becoming the last among ASEAN states to adopt a domestic law governing electronic commerce.[^o19] Article 43 of the statute prohibits any encryption of evidence in the form of data that could lead to an indictment, or any evidence in an electronic system that relates to an offense.[^o20] This statutory obligation may imply that authorities could order decryption of any data implicated in an investigation.[^o21] While remaining untested in courts, this obligation actively contradicts an accused person's procedural right against self-incrimination as provided under Article 143 of the Code of Criminal Procedure.[^o22]

### Canada {#Canada}

In Canada key disclosure is covered under the *[Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Charter_of_Rights_and_Freedoms)* section 11(c) which states "any person charged with an offence has the right not to be compelled to be a witness in proceedings against that person in respect of the offence;"[^o24] and protects the rights of individuals that are both citizens and non-citizens of Canada as long as they are physically present in Canada.[^o25]

In a 2010 [Quebec Court of Appeal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_Court_of_Appeal) case the court stated that a password compelled from an individual by law enforcement "is inadmissible and that renders the subsequent seizure of the data unreasonable. In short, even had the seizure been preceded by judicial authorization, the law will not allow an order to be joined compelling the respondent to self-incriminate."[^o26]

In a 2019 Ontario court case ([R v. Shergill](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/oncj/doc/2019/2019oncj54/2019oncj54.html)), the defendant was initially ordered to provide the password to unlock his phone. However, the judge concluded that providing a password would be tantamount to self-incrimination by testifying against oneself. As a result, the defendant was not compelled to provide his password.[^o27]

### Czech Republic {#Czech_Republic}

In the Czech Republic there is no law specifying obligation to issue keys or passwords.[^o29] Law provides protecting against self-incrimination, including lack of penalization for refusing to answer any question which would enable law enforcement agencies to obtain access to potential evidence, which could be used against testifying person.[^o30]

### Finland {#Finland}

The Coercive Measures Act (*Pakkokeinolaki*) 2011/806 section 8 paragraph 23[^o32] requires the system owner, its administrator, or a specified person to surrender the necessary "passwords and other such information" in order to provide access to information stored on an information system. The suspect and some other persons specified in section 7 paragraph 3 that cannot otherwise be called as witnesses are exempt from this requirement.

### France {#France}

(Law #2001-1062 of 15 November 2001 on Community Safety) allows a judge or prosecutor to compel any qualified person to decrypt or surrender keys to make available any information encountered in the course of an investigation. Failure to comply incurs three years of jail time and a fine of €45,000; if the compliance would have prevented or mitigated a crime, the penalty increases to five years of jail time and €75,000.[^o35]

### Germany {#Germany}

The German Code of Criminal Procedure grants a suspect the right to deny cooperation in an investigation that may lead to incriminating information to be revealed about themselves. For private usage is no legal basis that would compel a suspect to hand over any kind of cryptographic key due to this *[nemo tenetur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nemo_tenetur)* principle.[^o37]

### Hong Kong {#Hong_Kong}

In [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong), police can demand passwords under the amended [2020 Hong Kong national security law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Hong_Kong_national_security_law). Failure to comply could be punished by up to one year in jail and a fine of up to HK\$100,000.[^o39]

### Iceland {#Iceland}

In [Iceland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iceland) there is no law specifying obligation to issue keys or passwords.

### India {#India}

Section 69 of the [Information Technology Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_Technology_Act), as amended by the Information Technology (Amendment) Act, 2008, empowers the central and state governments to compel assistance from any "subscriber or intermediary or any person in charge of the computer resource" in decrypting information.[^o42][^o43] Failure to comply is punishable by up to seven years' imprisonment and/or a fine.

### Ireland {#Ireland}

Section 7(4)(b) of the Criminal Justice (Offences Relating to Information Systems) Act 2017 allows a member of the [Garda Síochána](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garda_Síochána) or other persons as deemed necessary (via a search warrant issued by a judge of the District Court (Section 7(1))) to demand the disclosure of a password to operate a computer and any decryption keys required to access the information contained therein.[^o45]

> 7(4) A member acting under the authority of a search warrant under this section may—
>
> (a) operate any computer at the place that is being searched or cause any such computer to be operated by a person accompanying the member for that purpose, and
>
> (b) require any person at that place who appears to the member to have lawful access to the information in any such computer—
>
> (i) to give to the member any password necessary to operate it and any encryption key or code necessary to unencrypt the information accessible by the computer, immediate data destruction
>
> (ii) otherwise to enable the member to examine the information accessible by the computer in a form in which the information is visible and legible, or
>
> (iii) to produce the information in a form in which it can be removed and in which it is, or can be made, visible and legible.

### New Zealand {#New_Zealand}

2016 New Zealand Customs was seeking power to compel key disclosure.[^o47] Although New Zealand may not have a key disclosure law, they have since enforced penalties against travelers unwilling to unlock mobile devices when compelled to do so by officials.[^o48]

### Poland {#Poland}

In relatively few known cases in which police or prosecutor requested cryptographic keys from those formally accused and these requests were not fulfilled, no further consequences were imposed on the accused. There's no specific law in this matter, as e.g. in the UK. It is generally assumed that the Polish Criminal Procedure Code (Kodeks Postępowania Karnego Dz.U. 1997 nr 89 poz. 555.) provides means of protecting against self-incrimination, including lack of penalization for refusing to answer any question which would enable law enforcement agencies to obtain access to potential evidence, which could be used against testifying person.

### South Africa {#South_Africa}

Under the [RICA Act of 2002](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation_of_Interception_of_Communications_and_Provision_of_Communication-related_Information_Act,_2002), refusal to disclose a cryptographic key in one's possession could result in a fine up to ZAR 2 million or up to 10 years' imprisonment. This requires a judge to issue a decryption direction to a person believed to hold the key. The constitutional court deemed the RICA Act to be unconstitutional in 2021

### Spain {#Spain}

Spain's Criminal Procedure Law grants suspects rights against self-incrimination,[^o53] and this would prevent the suspect from being compelled to reveal passwords.[^o54] However, a judge may order third parties to collaborate with any criminal investigation, including revealing decryption keys, where possible.[^o55]

### Sweden {#Sweden}

There are currently no laws that force the disclosure of cryptographic keys. However, there is legislation proposed on the basis that the [Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Europe) has already adopted a convention on cyber-crime related to this issue. The proposed legislation would allow police to require an individual to disclose information, such as passwords and cryptographic keys, during searches. The proposal has been introduced to make it easier for police and prosecutors. The proposal has been criticized by the [Swedish Data Protection Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Data_Protection_Authority).[^o57][^o58]

### Switzerland {#Switzerland}

In [Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switzerland) there is no law specifying obligation to issue keys or passwords.[^o60]

### The Netherlands {#The_Netherlands}

Article 125k of the Wetboek van Strafvordering allows investigators with a warrant to access information carriers and networked systems. The same article allows the district attorney and similar officers of the court to order persons who know how to access those systems to share their knowledge in the investigation, including any knowledge of encryption of data on information carriers. However, such an order may not be given to the suspect under investigation.[^o62]

### United Kingdom {#United_Kingdom}

The [Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation_of_Investigatory_Powers_Act_2000) (RIPA), Part III, activated by [ministerial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministerial_order_%28United_Kingdom%29) in October 2007,[^o64] requires persons to decrypt information and/or supply keys to government representatives to decrypt information without a court order. Failure to disclose carries a maximum penalty of two years in jail, or five years in the cases of national security or child indecency. The provision was first used against [animal rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_rights) activists in November 2007,[^o65] and at least three people have been prosecuted and convicted for refusing to surrender their encryption keys,[^o66] one of whom was sentenced to 13 months' imprisonment.[^o67] Even politicians responsible for the law have voiced concerns that its broad application may be problematic.[^o68]

In 2017, schedule 7 of the [Terrorism Act 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrorism_Act_2000) was used to charge Muhammad Rabbani with "wilfully obstructing or seeking to frustrate a search examination" after allegedly refusing to disclose passwords.[^o69] He was later convicted.[^o70]

In 2018, Stephen-Alan Nicholson, the prime suspect in a murder case, was charged with refusing to provide his [Facebook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facebook) password to police.[^o71]

### United States {#United_States}

The [Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privilege_against_self_incrimination) protects witnesses from being forced to incriminate themselves, and there is currently no law regarding key disclosure in the United States.[^o73] However, the federal case *[In re Boucher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_re_Boucher)* may be influential as [case law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_law). In this case, a man's laptop was inspected by customs agents and child pornography was discovered. The device was seized and powered-down, at which point [disk encryption](wikipedia://en/7066791) technology made the evidence unavailable. The judge held that it was a foregone conclusion that the content exists since it had already been seen by the customs agents, Boucher's encryption password "adds little or nothing to the sum total of the Government's information about the existence and location of files that may contain incriminating information."[^o74][^o75]

[In another case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_v._Fricosu), a district court judge ordered a Colorado woman to decrypt her laptop so prosecutors can use the files against her in a criminal case: "I conclude that the Fifth Amendment is not implicated by requiring production of the unencrypted contents of the Toshiba Satellite M305 laptop computer," Colorado U.S. District Judge Robert Blackburn ruled on January 23, 2012.[^o76] In *Commonwealth v. Gelfgatt*,[^o77] the court ordered a suspect to decrypt his computer, citing exception to Fifth Amendment can be invoked because "*an act of production does not involve testimonial communication where the facts conveyed already are known to the government...*".[^o78]

However, in *United States v. Doe*, the [United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Eleventh_Circuit) ruled on 24 February 2012 that forcing the decryption of one's laptop violates the Fifth Amendment.[^o79][^o80]

The [Federal Bureau of Investigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Bureau_of_Investigation) may also issue [national security letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_security_letter) that require the disclosure of keys for investigative purposes.[^o81] One company, [Lavabit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lavabit), chose to shut down rather than surrender its master private keys due to the government wanting to spy on [Edward Snowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Snowden)'s emails.

Since the summer of 2015, cases have been fought between major tech companies such as Apple over the regulation of encryption with government agencies asking for access to private encrypted information for law enforcement purposes. A technical report was written and published by [MIT Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT_Computer_Science_and_Artificial_Intelligence_Laboratory), where [Ronald Rivest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Rivest), an inventor of [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28cryptosystem%29), and [Harold Abelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Abelson), a computer science professor at MIT with others, explain the technical difficulties, including security issues that arise from the regulation of encryption or by making a key available to a third party for purposes of decrypting any possible encrypted information. The report lists scenarios and raises questions for policy makers. It also asks for more technical details if the request for regulating encryption is to be pursued further.[^o82]

In 2019, the [Pennsylvania Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania_Supreme_Court), in a ruling that only controls for that state's law, held that a suspect in a child pornography case could not be compelled to reveal his password, despite telling the police "We both know what's on there."[^o83]
