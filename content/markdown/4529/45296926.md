# Polygram (geometry)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Polygram"}

*Image: Regular Star Polygons-en.svg.* Regular polygrams {*n*/*d*}, with red lines showing constant *d*, and blue lines showing compound sequences *k*{*n*/*d*}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a generalized polygon can be called a **polygram**, and named specifically by its number of sides. All [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621) are polygrams, but they can also include disconnected sets of edges, called a [compound polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_polygon). For example, a regular [pentagram](wikipedia://en/197544), {5/2}, has 5 sides, and the regular [hexagram](wikipedia://en/302441), {6/2} or 2{3}, has 6 sides divided into two triangles.

:::

A **regular polygram** {*p*/*q*} can either be in a set of **[regular star polygons](wikipedia://en/313303)** (for [gcd](wikipedia://en/12354)(*p*,*q*) = 1, *q* > 1) or in a set of **regular polygon compounds** (if gcd(*p*,*q*) > 1).

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The polygram names combine a [numeral prefix](wikipedia://en/1705121), such as *penta-*, with the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) suffix *\-gram* (in this case generating the word *[pentagram](wikipedia://en/197544)*). The prefix is normally a Greek [cardinal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_number_%28linguistics%29), but synonyms using other prefixes exist. The *\-gram* suffix derives from *γραμμή* (*grammē*) meaning a line.[^o4]

## Generalized regular polygons {#Generalized_regular_polygons}

Regular polygon#Regular star polygons A regular polygram, as a general [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306), is denoted by its [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) {*p*/*q*}, where *p* and *q* are [relatively prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) (they share no factors) and *q* ≥ 2. For [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) *p* and *q*, it can be considered as being constructed by connecting every *q*th point out of *p* points regularly spaced in a circular placement.[^o6][^o2]

## Regular compound polygons {#Regular_compound_polygons}

List of regular polytopes and compounds#Two dimensions In other cases where *n* and *m* have a common factor, a *polygram* is interpreted as a lower polygon, {*n*/*k*, *m*/*k*}, with *k* = gcd(*n*,*m*), and rotated copies are combined as a compound polygon. These figures are called **regular compound polygons**.
