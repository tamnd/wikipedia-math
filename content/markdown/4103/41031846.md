# Truncated triangular trapezohedron

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Truncated triangular trapezohedron"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **truncated triangular trapezohedron** is the first in an infinite series of [truncated trapezohedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truncated_trapezohedra). It has 6 [pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014) and 2 [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) faces.

:::

## Geometry {#Geometry}

This [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) can be constructed by [truncating](wikipedia://en/4224429) two opposite [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) of a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285), of a [trigonal trapezohedron](wikipedia://en/3504388) (a convex polyhedron with six congruent [rhombus](wikipedia://en/183884) sides, formed by stretching or shrinking a cube along one of its long diagonals), or of a [rhombohedron](wikipedia://en/2706406) or [parallelepiped](wikipedia://en/23975) (less symmetric polyhedra that still have the same combinatorial structure as a cube). In the case of a cube, or of a trigonal trapezohedron where the two truncated vertices are the ones on the stretching axes, the resulting shape has three-fold [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096).

## Dürer's solid {#Dürer's_solid}

*Image: Dürer Melancholia I.jpg.* *[Melencolia I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melencolia_I)* by Albrecht Dürer, the first appearance of Dürer's solid in 1514

This polyhedron is sometimes called **Dürer's solid**, from its appearance in [Albrecht Dürer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albrecht_Dürer)'s 1514 engraving *[Melencolia I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melencolia_I)*. The graph formed by its edges and vertices is called the [Dürer graph](wikipedia://en/23453065).

The shape of the solid depicted by Dürer is a subject of some academic debate.[^o6] According to (Lynch 1982), the hypothesis that the shape is a misdrawn [truncated cube](wikipedia://en/280529) was promoted by (Strauss 1972); however most sources agree that it is the truncation of a [rhombohedron](wikipedia://en/2706406).[^o9] Despite this agreement, the exact geometry of this rhombohedron is the subject of several contradictory theories:

- (Richter 1957) claims that the [rhombi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhombi) of the rhombohedron from which this shape is formed have 5:6 as the ratio between their short and long diagonals, from which the acute angles of the rhombi would be approximately 80°.[^n1]
- (Schröder 1980) and (Lynch 1982) instead conclude that the ratio is $\sqrt3:2$ and that the angle is approximately 82°.[^o14]
- (MacGillavry 1981) measures features of the drawing and finds that the angle is approximately 79°. She and a later author, [Wolf von Engelhardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_von_Engelhardt), argue that this choice of angle comes from its physical occurrence in [calcite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcite) [crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal).[^o16]
- (Schreiber 1999) argues based on the writings of Dürer that all vertices of Dürer's solid lie on a common sphere, and further claims that the rhombus angles are 72°.[^n2] (Hideko 2009) lists several other scholars who also favor the 72° theory, beginning with Paul Grodzinski in 1955. He argues that this theory is motivated less by analysis of the actual drawing, and more by aesthetic principles relating to [regular pentagons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_pentagon) and the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386).[^n3]
- (Weitzel 2004) analyzes a 1510 sketch by Dürer of the same solid, from which he confirms Schreiber's hypothesis that the shape has a [circumsphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumsphere) but with rhombus angles of approximately 79.5°.[^n4]
- (Hideko 2009) argues that the shape is intended to depict a solution to the famous geometric problem of [doubling the cube](wikipedia://en/91110), which Dürer also wrote about in 1525. He therefore concludes that (before the corners are cut off) the shape is a cube stretched along its long diagonal. More specifically, he argues that Dürer drew an actual cube, with the long diagonal parallel to the [perspective plane](wikipedia://en/937408), and then enlarged his drawing by some factor in the direction of the long diagonal; the result would be the same as if he had drawn the elongated solid. The enlargement factor that is relevant for doubling the cube is 2<sup>1/3</sup> ≈ 1.253, but Hideko derives a different enlargement factor that fits the drawing better, 1.277, in a more complicated way.[^n3]
- (Futamura Frantz Crannell 2014) classify the proposed solutions to this problem by two parameters: the acute angle and the level of cutting, called the cross ratio. Their estimate of the cross ratio is close to MacGillavry's, and has a numerical value close to the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386). Based on this, they posit that the acute angle is $2\arctan(\varphi/2)\approx 78^\circ$ and that the cross ratio is exactly $\varphi$.[^n5]
