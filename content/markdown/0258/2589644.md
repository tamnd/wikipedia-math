# Willem Jacob van Stockum

Van Stockum

*Image: Willem Jacob van Stockum.jpg.* Van Stockum, Toronto, c. 1935.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Willem Jacob van Stockum"}

**Willem Jacob van Stockum** (20 November 1910 – 10 June 1944) was a Dutch mathematician who made an important contribution to the early development of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Van Stockum was born in [Hattem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hattem) in the [Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands). His father was a mechanically talented officer in the [Dutch Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Navy). After the family (less the father) relocated to Ireland in the late 1920s, Willem studied mathematics at [Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Dublin), [Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Dublin), where he earned a gold medal. He went on to earn an M.A. from the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto) and his Ph.D. from the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh) in 1937.

In the mid-1930s, van Stockum became an early enthusiast of the then new theory of [gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation), general relativity. In 1938, he published a paper which contains one of the first exact solutions in general relativity which modeled the [gravitational field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_field) produced by a configuration of *rotating* matter, the [van Stockum dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Stockum_dust), which remains an important example noted for its unusual simplicity. In this paper, van Stockum was apparently the first to notice the possibility of [closed timelike curves](wikipedia://en/287091), one of the strangest and most disconcerting phenomena in general relativity.

Van Stockum left for the United States in hope of studying under [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736), eventually in the spring of 1939 gaining a temporary position under Professor [Oswald Veblen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Veblen) at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) in [Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey). The outbreak of the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War) occurred while he was teaching at the [University of Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Maryland). Anxious to join the fight against Hitler, he enlisted in the Royal Canadian Air Force, eventually earning his pilots wings in July 1942. Because of his advanced knowledge of [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), he spent much of the next year as a test pilot in [Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada). After the Netherlands was invaded by Hitler, van Stockum sought to join the war as a pilot. Finally, he was able to transfer to the [Dutch Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Air_Force) (in exile), and in 1944 became the only Dutch officer posted to No. 10 Sq­ron of the [RAF Bomber Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAF_Bomber_Command), which was stationed in [Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorkshire) and flew combat missions in the *Halifax* heavy bomber over Europe before and after the [Normandy invasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normandy_invasion). On 10 June 1944, van Stockum and his crew of six took off on their sixth combat mission, as part of another 400-plane raid. Near their target, the plane was hit by [flak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flak), and all seven crew members were lost, along with seven from another bomber on the same mission. The fourteen airmen are buried in [Laval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laval,_Mayenne), near the place where the planes went down.
