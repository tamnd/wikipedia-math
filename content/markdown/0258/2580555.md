# Bernoulli scheme

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bernoulli scheme"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Bernoulli scheme** or **Bernoulli shift** is a generalization of the [Bernoulli process](wikipedia://en/102651) to more than two possible outcomes.[^o2][^o3] Bernoulli schemes appear naturally in [symbolic dynamics](wikipedia://en/1986263), and are thus important in the study of [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087). Many important dynamical systems (such as [Axiom A systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_A_system)) exhibit a [repellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repellor) that is the product of the [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172) and a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold), and the dynamics on the Cantor set are isomorphic to that of the Bernoulli shift.[^o4] This is essentially the [Markov partition](wikipedia://en/11491735). The term *shift* is in reference to the [shift operator](wikipedia://en/467384), which may be used to study Bernoulli schemes. The [Ornstein isomorphism theorem](wikipedia://en/5443884)[^o5] shows that Bernoulli shifts are isomorphic when their [entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov_entropy) is equal.

:::

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A Bernoulli scheme is a [discrete-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete-time) [stochastic process](wikipedia://en/47895) where each [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence) [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) may take on one of *N* distinct possible values, with the outcome *i* occurring with probability $p_i$, with *i* = 1, ..., *N*, and

$$\sum_{i=1}^N p_i = 1.$$

The [sample space](wikipedia://en/22958) is usually denoted as

$$X=\{1,\ldots,N \}^\mathbb{Z}$$

as a shorthand for

$$X=\{ x=(\ldots,x_{-1},x_0,x_1,\ldots) : x_k \in \{1,\ldots,N\} \; \forall k \in \mathbb{Z} \}.$$

The associated [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) is called the **Bernoulli measure**[^o9]

$$\mu = \{p_1,\ldots,p_N\}^\mathbb{Z}$$

The [σ-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-algebra) $\mathcal{A}$ on *X* is the product sigma algebra; that is, it is the (countable) [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887) of the σ-algebras of the finite set {1, ..., *N*}. Thus, the triplet

$$(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)$$

is a [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270). A basis of $\mathcal{A}$ is the [cylinder sets](wikipedia://en/2613658). Given a cylinder set $[x_0, x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, its measure is

$$\mu\left([x_0, x_1,\ldots,x_n]\right)= \prod_{i=0}^n p_{x_i}$$

The equivalent expression, using the notation of probability theory, is

$$\mu\left([x_0, x_1,\ldots,x_n]\right)= \mathrm{Pr}(X_0=x_0, X_1=x_1, \ldots, X_n=x_n)$$

for the random variables $\{X_k\}$

The Bernoulli scheme, as any stochastic process, may be viewed as a [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087) by endowing it with the [shift operator](wikipedia://en/467384) *T* where

$$T(x_k) = x_{k+1}.$$

Since the outcomes are independent, the shift preserves the measure, and thus *T* is a [measure-preserving transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure-preserving_transformation). The quadruplet

$$(X,\mathcal{A},\mu, T)$$

is a [measure-preserving dynamical system](wikipedia://en/398931), and is called a **Bernoulli scheme** or a **Bernoulli shift**. It is often denoted by

$$BS(p)=BS(p_1,\ldots,p_N).$$

The *N* = 2 Bernoulli scheme is called a [Bernoulli process](wikipedia://en/102651). The Bernoulli shift can be understood as a special case of the [Markov shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_shift), where all entries in the [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) are one, the corresponding graph thus being a [clique](wikipedia://en/524466).

:::

## Matches and metrics {#Matches_and_metrics}

The [Hamming distance](wikipedia://en/41227) provides a natural metric on a Bernoulli scheme. Another important metric is the so-called $\overline f$ metric, defined via a supremum over *string matches*.[^o11]

Let $A = a_1a_2\cdots a_m$ and $B = b_1b_2\cdots b_n$ be two strings of symbols. A **match** is a sequence *M* of pairs $(i_k, j_k)$ of indexes into the string, i.e. pairs such that $a_{i_k}=b_{j_k},$ understood to be totally ordered. That is, each individual subsequence $(i_k)$ and $(j_k)$ are ordered: $1\le i_1 < i_2<\cdots <i_r\le m$ and likewise $1\le j_1 < j_2<\cdots <j_r\le n.$

The $\overline f$-*distance* between $A$ and $B$ is

$$\overline f(A,B) = 1-\frac{2\sup |M|}{m+n}$$

where the supremum is being taken over all matches $M$ between $A$ and $B$. This satisfies the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941) only when $m=n,$ and so is not quite a true metric; despite this, it is commonly called a "distance" in the literature.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Most of the properties of the Bernoulli scheme follow from the countable [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887), rather than from the finite base space. Thus, one may take the base space to be any [standard probability space](wikipedia://en/10406190) $(Y,\mathcal{B},\nu)$, and define the Bernoulli scheme as

$$(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)=(Y,\mathcal{B},\nu)^\mathbb{Z}$$

This works because the countable direct product of a standard probability space is again a standard probability space.

As a further generalization, one may replace the integers $\mathbb{Z}$ by a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) [discrete group](wikipedia://en/468536) $G$, so that

$$(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)=(Y,\mathcal{B},\nu)^G$$

For this last case, the shift operator is replaced by the [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29)

$$gx(f)=x(g^{-1}f)$$

for group elements $f,g\in G$ and $x\in Y^G$ understood as a function $x:G\to Y$ (any direct product $Y^G$ can be understood to be the set of functions $[G\to Y]$, as this is the [exponential object](wikipedia://en/2237858)). The measure $\mu$ is taken as the [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645), which is invariant under the group action:

$$\mu(gx)=\mu(x). \,$$

These generalizations are also commonly called Bernoulli schemes, as they still share most properties with the finite case.

## Properties {#Properties}

[Ya. Sinai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ya._Sinai) demonstrated that the [Kolmogorov entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov_entropy) of a Bernoulli scheme is given by[^o16][^o17]

$$H = -\sum_{i=1}^N p_i \log p_i .$$

This may be seen as resulting from the general definition of the entropy of a [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of probability spaces, which follows from the [asymptotic equipartition property](wikipedia://en/248710). For the case of a general base space $(Y, \mathcal{B}, \nu)$ (*i.e.* a base space which is not countable), one typically considers the [relative entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_entropy). So, for example, if one has a countable [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) $Y'\subset Y$ of the base *Y*, such that $\nu(Y')=1$, one may define the entropy as

$$H_{Y'} = -\sum_{y'\in Y'} \nu(y') \log \nu(y') .$$

In general, this entropy will depend on the partition; however, for many [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087), it is the case that the [symbolic dynamics](wikipedia://en/1986263) is independent of the partition (or rather, there are isomorphisms connecting the symbolic dynamics of different partitions, leaving the measure invariant), and so such systems can have a well-defined entropy independent of the partition.

:::

## Ornstein isomorphism theorem {#Ornstein_isomorphism_theorem}

The [Ornstein isomorphism theorem](wikipedia://en/5443884) states that two Bernoulli schemes with the same entropy are [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_of_dynamical_systems).[^o5] The result is sharp,[^o20] in that very similar, non-scheme systems, such as [Kolmogorov automorphisms](wikipedia://en/36563911), do not have this property.

The Ornstein isomorphism theorem is in fact considerably deeper: it provides a simple criterion by which many different [measure-preserving dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/398931) can be judged to be isomorphic to Bernoulli schemes. The result was surprising, as many systems previously believed to be unrelated proved to be isomorphic. These include all finite [stationary stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary_stochastic_process), [subshifts of finite type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subshifts_of_finite_type), finite [Markov chains](wikipedia://en/60876), [Anosov flows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anosov_flow), and [Sinai's billiards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinai's_billiards): these are all isomorphic to Bernoulli schemes.

For the generalized case, the Ornstein isomorphism theorem still holds if the group *G* is a countably infinite [amenable group](wikipedia://en/657430). [^o21][^o22]

:::

## Bernoulli automorphism {#Bernoulli_automorphism}

An invertible, [measure-preserving transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure-preserving_transformation) of a [standard probability space](wikipedia://en/10406190) (Lebesgue space) is called a **Bernoulli automorphism** if it is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_of_dynamical_systems) to a [Bernoulli shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_shift).[^o25]

:::

## Loosely Bernoulli {#Loosely_Bernoulli}

A system is termed "loosely Bernoulli" if it is [Kakutani-equivalent](wikipedia://en/58350478) to a Bernoulli shift; in the case of zero entropy, if it is Kakutani-equivalent to an irrational rotation of a circle.
