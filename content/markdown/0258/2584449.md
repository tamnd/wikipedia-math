# Two-element Boolean algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Two-element Boolean algebra"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), the **two-element Boolean algebra** is the [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959) whose *underlying set* (or [universe](wikipedia://en/296838) or *carrier*) *B* is the [Boolean domain](wikipedia://en/3954077). The elements of the Boolean domain are 1 and 0 by convention, so that *B* = {0, 1}. [Paul Halmos](wikipedia://en/318598)'s name for this algebra "**2**" has some following in the literature, and will be employed here.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

*B* is a [partially ordered set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) and the elements of *B* are also its [bounds](wikipedia://en/48258#Boundedness_in_order_theory).

An [operation](wikipedia://en/4140245) of [arity](wikipedia://en/42301) *n* is a [mapping](wikipedia://en/516931) from *B*<sup>n</sup> to *B*. Boolean algebra consists of two [binary operations](wikipedia://en/3948) and [unary](wikipedia://en/161006) [complementation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complement_%28order_theory%29). The binary operations have been named and notated in various ways. Here they are called 'sum' and 'product', and notated by infix '+' and '∙', respectively. Sum and product [commute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity) and [associate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity), as in the usual [algebra of real numbers](wikipedia://en/9710). As for the [order of operations](wikipedia://en/212980), brackets are decisive if present. Otherwise '∙' precedes '+'. Hence $A \bullet B + C$ is parsed as $(A \bullet B) + C$ and not as $A \bullet (B + C)$. [Complementation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_algebra_%28logic%29) is denoted by writing an overbar over its argument. The numerical analog of the complement of $X$ is $1 - X$. In the language of [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200), a Boolean algebra is a $\langle B,+,$∙$,\overline{..},1,0\rangle$ [algebra](wikipedia://en/106364) of [type](wikipedia://en/42301) $\langle 2,2,1,0,0\rangle$.

Either [one-to-one correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-to-one_correspondence) between {0,1} and {*True*,*False*} yields classical [bivalent logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bivalent_logic) in equational form, with complementation read as [NOT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_NOT). If 1 is read as *True*, '+' is read as [OR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_OR), and '∙' as [AND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_AND), and vice versa if 1 is read as *False*. These two operations define a commutative [semiring](wikipedia://en/371468), known as the [Boolean semiring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_semiring).

:::

## Some basic identities {#Some_basic_identities}

**2** can be seen as grounded in the following trivial "Boolean" arithmetic:

$$\begin{aligned} &1 + 1 = 1 + 0 = 0 + 1 = 1 \\ &0 + 0 = 0 \\ &0\cdot0 = 0\cdot1 = 1\cdot0 = 0 \\ &1\cdot1 = 1 \\ &\overline{1} = 0 \\ &\overline{0} = 1 \end{aligned}$$

Note that:

- '+' and '∙' work exactly as in numerical arithmetic, except that 1+1=1. '+' and '∙' are derived by analogy from numerical arithmetic; simply set any nonzero number to 1.
- Swapping 0 and 1, and '+' and '∙' preserves truth; this is the essence of the [duality](wikipedia://en/600618) pervading all Boolean algebras.

This Boolean arithmetic suffices to verify any equation of **2**, including the axioms, by examining every possible assignment of 0s and 1s to each variable (see [decision procedure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision_procedure)).

The following equations may now be verified:

$$\begin{aligned} &A + A = A \\ &A \cdot A = A \\ &A + 0 = A \\ &A + 1 = 1 \\ &A \cdot 0 = 0 \\ &\overline{\overline{A}} = A \end{aligned}$$

Each of '+' and '∙' [distributes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributivity) over the other:

- $$\ A \cdot (B+C) = A \cdot B + A \cdot C;$$
- $$\ A+(B \cdot C) = (A+B) \cdot (A+C).$$

That '∙' distributes over '+' agrees with [elementary algebra](wikipedia://en/9710), but not '+' over '∙'. For this and other reasons, a sum of products (leading to a [NAND](wikipedia://en/29388) synthesis) is more commonly employed than a product of sums (leading to a [NOR](wikipedia://en/209154) synthesis).

Each of '+' and '∙' can be defined in terms of the other and complementation:

- $$A \cdot B=\overline{\overline{A}+\overline{B}}$$
- $$A+B=\overline{\overline{A} \cdot \overline{B}}.$$

We only need one binary operation, and [concatenation](wikipedia://en/64474) suffices to denote it. Hence concatenation and overbar suffice to notate **2**. This notation is also that of [Quine](wikipedia://en/33833)'s [Boolean term schemata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_term_schemata). Letting (*X*) denote the complement of *X* and "()" denote either 0 or 1 yields the [syntax](wikipedia://en/26860) of the primary algebra of [G. Spencer-Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._Spencer-Brown)'s *[Laws of Form](wikipedia://en/660651)*.

A *basis* for **2** is a set of equations, called [axioms](wikipedia://en/928), from which all of the above equations (and more) can be derived. There are many known bases for all Boolean algebras and hence for **2**. An elegant basis notated using only concatenation and overbar is:

1. $\ ABC = BCA$ (Concatenation commutes, associates)
1. $\overline{A}A = 1$ (**2** is a [complemented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complement_%28order_theory%29) lattice, with an [upper bound](wikipedia://en/48258) of 1)
1. $\ A0 = A$ (0 is the [lower bound](wikipedia://en/48258)).
1. $A\overline{AB} = A\overline{B}$ (**2** is a [distributive lattice](wikipedia://en/310883))

Where concatenation = OR, 1 = true, and 0 = false, or concatenation = AND, 1 = false, and 0 = true. (overbar is negation in both cases.)

If 0=1, (1)–(3) are the axioms for an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974).

(1) only serves to prove that concatenation commutes and associates. First assume that (1) associates from either the left or the right, then prove commutativity. Then prove association from the other direction. Associativity is simply association from the left and right combined.

This basis makes for an easy approach to proof, called "calculation" in *[Laws of Form](wikipedia://en/660651)*, that proceeds by simplifying expressions to 0 or 1, by invoking axioms (2)–(4), and the elementary identities $AA=A, \overline{\overline{A}}=A, 1+A = 1$, and the distributive law.

## Metatheory {#Metatheory}

[De Morgan's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Morgan's_theorem) states that if one does the following, in the given order, to any [Boolean function](wikipedia://en/753349):

- Complement every variable;
- Swap '+' and '∙' operators (taking care to add brackets to ensure the order of operations remains the same);
- Complement the result,

the result is [logically equivalent](wikipedia://en/169324) to what you started with. Repeated application of De Morgan's theorem to parts of a function can be used to drive all complements down to the individual variables.

A powerful and nontrivial [metatheorem](wikipedia://en/5106151) states that any identity of **2** holds for all Boolean algebras.[^o6] Conversely, an identity that holds for an arbitrary nontrivial Boolean algebra also holds in **2**. Hence all identities of Boolean algebra are captured by **2**. This theorem is useful because any equation in **2** can be verified by a [decision procedure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision_procedure). Logicians refer to this fact as "**2** is [decidable](wikipedia://en/913118)". All known [decision procedures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision_procedure) require a number of steps that is an [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) of the number of variables *N* appearing in the equation to be verified. Whether there exists a decision procedure whose steps are a [polynomial function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_function) of *N* falls under the [P&nbsp;=&nbsp;NP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P&nbsp;=&nbsp;NP) conjecture.

The above metatheorem does not hold if we consider the validity of more general [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) formulas instead of only atomic positive equalities. As an example consider the formula $(x = 0) \lor (x = 1)$. This formula is always true in a two-element Boolean algebra. In a four-element Boolean algebra whose domain is the powerset of $\{0,1\}$, this formula corresponds to the statement $(x = \emptyset) \lor (x = \{0,1\})$ and is false when *x* is $\{1\}$. The decidability for the [first-order theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-order_theory) of many classes of [Boolean algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_algebras) can still be shown, using [quantifier elimination](wikipedia://en/2150441) or small model property (with the domain size computed as a function of the formula and generally larger than 2).
