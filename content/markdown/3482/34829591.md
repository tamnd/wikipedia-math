# John Edmund Kerrich

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="John Edmund Kerrich"}

**John Edmund Kerrich** (1903–1985) was a [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) noted for a series of experiments in [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) which he conducted while interned in [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi)-occupied Denmark in the 1940s.[^o2]

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

John Kerrich was born in [Norfolk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norfolk), England,[^o4] and grew up in [South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Africa). He was educated there and in the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom) (First class Honours in Mathematics & MSc Astronomy, [University of the Witwatersrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_the_Witwatersrand); Diploma in Actuarial Mathematics, [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh)).

He was appointed lecturer in mathematics in 1929, and senior lecturer six years later. In April 1940, while visiting in-laws in Copenhagen, Kerrich was caught up in the [Nazi invasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_invasion_of_Denmark_%281940%29)[^o5] and interned in [Hald Ege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hald_Ege), [Viborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viborg_Municipality), [Midtjylland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midtjylland). While there he conducted simple experiments using coins and [ping-pong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ping-pong) balls to demonstrate the empirical validity of a number of fundamental laws of probability.

On his release after the end of the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War), Kerrich published an account of his experiments in a short book entitled *An Experimental Introduction to the Theory of Probability*.[^o6] Originally published in Denmark, the book was later reprinted by the [University of Witwatersrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Witwatersrand) Press.

In 1957, Kerrich was appointed Foundation Professor of Statistics at the University of Witwatersrand and retired in 1971. He was married with two sons.[^o4]

## Experiments in empirical probability {#Experiments_in_empirical_probability}

During his [internment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internment), Kerrich worked with fellow internee Eric Christensen. The most famous was a demonstration of [Jacob Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/64740)'s famous [law of large numbers](wikipedia://en/157055) using a coin which they tossed 10,000 times. By recording the number of heads obtained as the trials continued, Kerrich was able to demonstrate that the proportion of heads obtained asymptotically approached the theoretical value of 50 percent (the precise number obtained was 5,067, which is 1.34 [standard deviations](wikipedia://en/27590) above the mean for a "fair" coin thrown that many times).[^o8]

Kerrich and Christensen also performed experiments using a "biased coin", made from a wooden disk partly coated in lead, to show that it too tended towards a stable asymptotic state with probability of approximately 70 percent.

In addition, the pair used [urn problems](wikipedia://en/435084) involving drawing colored ping-pong balls from a box to demonstrate [Bayes's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayes's_theorem).[^o9]

Until the advent of computer simulations, Kerrich's study, published in 1946, was widely cited as evidence of the asymptotic nature of probability.
