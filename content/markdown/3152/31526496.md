# Orthoptic (geometry)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Orthoptic"}

In the [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) of [curves](wikipedia://en/89246), an **orthoptic** is the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of points for which two [tangents](wikipedia://en/31482) of a given curve meet at a right angle.

:::

*Image: Parabel-orthop.svg.* solid red solid purple

*Image: Orthoptic-ellipse-s.svg.* solid red solid purple solid green dashed black

*Image: Orthoptic-hyperbola-s.svg.* solid red solid purple dashed black

Examples:

1. The orthoptic of a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) is its directrix (proof: see below),
1. The orthoptic of an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) $\tfrac{x^2}{a^2} + \tfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ is the [director circle](wikipedia://en/25043024) $x^2 + y^2 = a^2 + b^2$ (see below),
1. The orthoptic of a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) $\tfrac{x^2}{a^2} - \tfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1,\ a > b$ is the director circle $x^2 + y^2 = a^2 - b^2$ (in case of $a \le b$ there are no orthogonal tangents, see below),
1. The orthoptic of an [astroid](wikipedia://en/769163) $x^{2/3} + y^{2/3} = 1$ is a [quadrifolium](wikipedia://en/2748979) with the polar equation $r=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\cos(2\varphi), \ 0\le \varphi < 2\pi$ (see below).

Generalizations:

1. An **isoptic** is the set of points for which two tangents of a given curve meet at a *fixed angle* (see below).
1. An **isoptic** of *two* plane curves is the set of points for which two tangents meet at a *fixed angle*.
1. [Thales' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales'_theorem) on a [chord](wikipedia://en/297947) $PQ$ can be considered as the orthoptic of two circles which are degenerated to the two points $P$ and $Q$.

## Orthoptic of a parabola {#Orthoptic_of_a_parabola}

Any parabola can be transformed by a [rigid motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigid_motion) (angles are not changed) into a parabola with equation $y = ax^2$. The slope at a point of the parabola is $m = 2ax$. Replacing $x$ gives the parametric representation of the parabola with the tangent slope as parameter: $\left(\tfrac{m}{2a},\tfrac{m^2}{4a} \right) \! .$ The tangent has the equation $y=mx+n$ with the still unknown $n$, which can be determined by inserting the coordinates of the parabola point. One gets $y=mx-\tfrac{m^2}{4a}\; .$

If a tangent contains the point $(x_{0}, y_{0})$, off the parabola, then the equation

$$y_0 = m x_0 -\frac{m^2}{4a} \quad \rightarrow \quad m^2 - 4ax_0\,m + 4ay_0 = 0$$

holds, which has two solutions $m_{1}$ and $m_{2}$ corresponding to the two tangents passing $(x_{0}, y_{0})$. The free term of a reduced quadratic equation is always the product of its solutions. Hence, if the tangents meet at $(x_{0}, y_{0})$ orthogonally, the following equations hold:

$$m_1 m_2 = -1 = 4 a y_0$$

The last equation is equivalent to

$$y_0 = -\frac{1}{4a}\, ,$$

which is the equation of the [directrix](wikipedia://en/23231#Parabola_as_graph_of_a_function).

## Orthoptic of an ellipse and hyperbola {#Orthoptic_of_an_ellipse_and_hyperbola}

### Ellipse {#Ellipse}

Let $E:\; \tfrac{x^2}{a^2} + \tfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ be the ellipse of consideration.

1. The tangents to the ellipse $E$ at the vertices and co-vertices intersect at the 4 points $(\pm a, \pm b)$, which lie on the desired orthoptic curve (the circle $x^2+y^2 = a^2 + b^2$).
1. The tangent at a point $(u,v)$ of the ellipse $E$ has the equation $\tfrac{u}{a^2} x + \tfrac{v}{b^2} y = 1$ (see [tangent to an ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277#Tangent)). If the point is not a vertex this equation can be solved for $y$: $y = -\tfrac{b^2u}{a^2v}\;x\; + \;\tfrac{b^2}{v}\, .$

Using the abbreviations

::: {#o5 .equation cue=numblk name="I"}

$$\begin{aligned} m &= -\tfrac{b^2u}{a^2v},\\ \color{red}n &= \color{red}\tfrac{b^2}{v} \end{aligned} \tag{I}$$

:::

and the equation ${\color{blue}\tfrac{u^2}{a^2} = 1 - \tfrac{v^2}{b^2} = 1-\tfrac{b^2}{n^2}}$ one gets:

$$m^2 = \frac{b^4 u^2}{a^4 v^2} = \frac{1}{a^2} {\color{red}\frac{b^4}{v^2}} {\color{blue}\frac{u^2}{a^2}} = \frac{1}{a^2} {\color{red}n^2} {\color{blue}\left(1-\frac{b^2}{n^2}\right)} = \frac{n^2-b^2}{a^2}\, .$$

Hence

::: {#o6 .equation cue=numblk name="II"}

$$n = \pm\sqrt{m^2 a^2 + b^2} \tag{II}$$

:::

and the equation of a non vertical tangent is

$$y = m x \pm \sqrt{m^2 a^2 + b^2}.$$

Solving relations ((I)) for $u,v$ and respecting ((II)) leads to the slope depending parametric representation of the ellipse:

$$(u,v) = \left(-\tfrac{ma^2}{\pm\sqrt{m^2a^2+b^2}}\;,\;\tfrac{b^2}{\pm\sqrt{m^2a^2+b^2}}\right)\, .$$

(For another proof: see Ellipse#Parametric representation.)

If a tangent contains the point $(x_0,y_0)$, off the ellipse, then the equation

$$y_0 = m x_0 \pm \sqrt{m^2a^2+b^2}$$

holds. Eliminating the square root leads to

$$m^2 - \frac{2x_0y_0}{x_0^2-a^2}m + \frac{y_0^2-b^2}{x_0^2-a^2} = 0,$$

which has two solutions $m_1,m_2$ corresponding to the two tangents passing through $(x_0,y_0)$. The constant term of a monic quadratic equation is always the product of its solutions. Hence, if the tangents meet at $(x_0,y_0)$ orthogonally, the following equations hold:

*Image: Orthoptic locus of a circle, ellipses and hyperbolas.gif.* Orthoptics (red circles) of a circle, ellipses and hyperbolas

$$m_1 m_2 = -1 = \frac{y_0^2 - b^2}{x_0^2 - a^2}$$

The last equation is equivalent to

$$x_0^2+y_0^2 = a^2+b^2\, .$$

## Orthoptic of an astroid {#Orthoptic_of_an_astroid}

*Image: Orthoptic-astroid.svg.* Orthoptic (purple) of an astroid

An astroid can be described by the parametric representation

$$\mathbf c(t) = \left(\cos^3t, \sin^3t\right), \quad 0 \le t < 2\pi.$$

From the condition

$$\mathbf \dot c(t) \cdot \mathbf \dot c(t+\alpha) = 0$$

one recognizes the distance $\alpha$ in [parameter space](wikipedia://en/2882813) at which an orthogonal tangent to $\mathbf{\text{ċ}}(t)$ appears. It turns out that the distance is independent of parameter $t$, namely $\alpha = \pm \tfrac{\pi}{2}$. The equations of the (orthogonal) tangents at the points $\mathbf{c}(t)$ and $\mathbf{c}(t + \tfrac{\pi}{2})$ are respectively:

$$\begin{aligned} y &= -\tan t \left(x-\cos^3 t\right) + \sin^3 t, \\ y &= \frac{1}{\tan t} \left(x+\sin^3 t\right) + \cos^3 t. \end{aligned}$$

Their common point has coordinates:

$$\begin{aligned} x &= \sin t \cos t \left(\sin t - \cos t\right), \\ y &= \sin t \cos t \left(\sin t + \cos t\right). \end{aligned}$$

This is simultaneously a parametric representation of the orthoptic.

Elimination of the parameter $t$ yields the implicit representation

$$2\left(x^2+y^2\right)^3 - \left(x^2-y^2\right)^2 = 0.$$

Introducing the new parameter $\varphi = t - \tfrac{5\pi}{4}$ one gets

$$\begin{aligned} x &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cos(2\varphi)\cos\varphi, \\ y &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cos(2\varphi)\sin\varphi. \end{aligned}$$

(The proof uses the [angle sum and difference identities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_sum_and_difference_identities).) Hence we get the polar representation

$$r = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cos(2\varphi), \quad 0 \le \varphi < 2\pi$$

of the orthoptic. Hence:

## Isoptic of a parabola, an ellipse and a hyperbola {#Isoptic_of_a_parabola,_an_ellipse_and_a_hyperbola}

*Image: Isoptic-80-parabola-s.svg.* Isoptics (purple) of a parabola for angles 80° and 100°

*Image: Isoptic-ellipse-s.svg.* Isoptics (purple) of an ellipse for angles 80° and 100°

*Image: Isoptic-hyperbola-s.svg.* Isoptics (purple) of a hyperbola for angles 80° and 100°

Below the isotopics for angles $\alpha \ne 90^{\circ}$ are listed. They are called $\alpha$-isoptics. For the proofs see below.

### Equations of the isoptics {#Equations_of_the_isoptics}

**Parabola:**

The $\alpha$-isoptics of the parabola with equation $y = ax^{2}$ are the branches of the hyperbola

$$x^2-\tan^2\alpha\left(y+\frac{1}{4a}\right)^2-\frac{y}{a}=0.$$

The branches of the hyperbola provide the isoptics for the two angles $\alpha$ and $180^{\circ} - \alpha$ (see picture).

**Ellipse:**

The $\alpha$-isoptics of the ellipse with equation $\tfrac{x^{2}}{a^{2}} + \tfrac{y^{2}}{b^{2}} = 1$ are the two parts of the degree-4 curve

$$\left(x^2+y^2-a^2-b^2\right)^2 \tan^2\alpha = 4\left(a^2y^2 + b^2x^2 - a^2b^2\right)$$

(see picture).

**Hyperbola:**

The $\alpha$-isoptics of the hyperbola with the equation $\tfrac{x^{2}}{a^{2}} - \tfrac{y^{2}}{b^{2}} = 1$ are the two parts of the degree-4 curve

$$\left(x^2 + y^2 - a^2 + b^2\right)^2 \tan^2\alpha = 4 \left(a^2y^2 - b^2x^2 + a^2b^2\right).$$

::: {#o12 .proof cue=heading name="Proofs"}

### Proofs {#Proofs}

**Parabola:**

A parabola $y = ax^{2}$ can be parametrized by the slope of its tangents $m = 2ax$:

$$\mathbf c(m) = \left(\frac{m}{2a},\frac{m^2}{4a}\right), \quad m \in \mathbb{R}.$$

The tangent with slope $m$ has the equation

$$y=mx-\frac{m^2}{4a}.$$

The point $(x_{0}, y_{0})$ is on the tangent if and only if

$$y_0 = m x_0 - \frac{m^2}{4a}.$$

This means the slopes $m_{1}$, $m_{2}$ of the two tangents containing $(x_{0}, y_{0})$ fulfil the quadratic equation

$$m^2 - 4ax_0m + 4ay_0 = 0.$$

If the tangents meet at angle $\alpha$ or $180^{\circ} - \alpha$, the equation

$$\tan^2\alpha = \left(\frac{m_1-m_2}{1+m_1 m_2}\right)^2$$

must be fulfilled. Solving the quadratic equation for $m$, and inserting $m_{1}$, $m_{2}$ into the last equation, one gets

$$x_0^2-\tan^2\alpha\left(y_0+\frac{1}{4a}\right)^2-\frac{y_0}{a} = 0.$$

This is the equation of the hyperbola above. Its branches bear the two isoptics of the parabola for the two angles $\alpha$ and $180^{\circ} - \alpha$.

**Ellipse:**

In the case of an ellipse $\tfrac{x^{2}}{a^{2}} + \tfrac{y^{2}}{b^{2}} = 1$ one can adopt the idea for the orthoptic for the quadratic equation

$$m^2-\frac{2x_0y_0}{x_0^2-a^2}m + \frac{y_0^2-b^2}{x_0^2-a^2} = 0.$$

Now, as in the case of a parabola, the quadratic equation has to be solved and the two solutions $m_{1}$, $m_{2}$ must be inserted into the equation

$$\tan^2\alpha=\left(\frac{m_1-m_2}{1+m_1m_2}\right)^2.$$

Rearranging shows that the isoptics are parts of the degree-4 curve:

$$\left(x_0^2+y_0^2-a^2-b^2\right)^2 \tan^2\alpha = 4\left(a^2y_0^2+b^2x_0^2-a^2b^2\right).$$

**Hyperbola:**

The solution for the case of a hyperbola can be adopted from the ellipse case by replacing $b^{2}$ with $-b^{2}$ (as in the case of the orthoptics, see above).

To visualize the isoptics, see [implicit curve](wikipedia://en/11286809).
