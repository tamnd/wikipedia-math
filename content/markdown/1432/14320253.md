# Jabotinsky matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Jabotinsky matrix"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Jabotinsky matrix** (sometimes called **iteration matrix** or **power matrix**) is an [infinite matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_matrix) used to convert [function composition](wikipedia://en/195947) into [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280). It is often used in [iteration theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iteration_theory) to find the continuous [iteration of functions](wikipedia://en/1448702). The matrix is named after mathematician [Eri Jabotinsky](wikipedia://en/22931897).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $f$ be a [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012). There exists coefficients $(B_{n,k})_{n, k\geq 0}$ such that

$$f(x)^k = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} B_{n,k} x^n.$$

The **Jabotinsky matrix** of $f(x)$ is defined as the infinite matrix[^o4][^o5]

$$\mathbf B(f) = \left(\begin{array}{cccc} B_{0,0} & B_{0,1} & B_{0,2} & \cdots \\ B_{1,0} & B_{1,1} & B_{1,2} & \cdots \\ B_{2,0} & B_{2,1} & B_{2,2} & \cdots \\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots \end{array}\right).$$

When $f(0) = 0$, $\mathbf B(f)$ becomes an infinite lower [triangular matrix](wikipedia://en/374222) whose entries are given by [ordinary Bell polynomials](wikipedia://en/572498#Ordinary_Bell_polynomials) evaluated at the coefficients of $f$. This is why $\mathbf B(f)$ is sometimes referred to as a *Bell matrix*.[^o6][^o7]

:::

## History {#History}

Jabotinsky matrices have a long history, and were perhaps used for the first time in the context of [iteration theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iteration_theory) by Albert A. Bennett[^o9] in 1915. Jabotinsky later pursued Bennett's research[^o10][^o11][^o12][^o13] and applied them to [Faber polynomials](wikipedia://en/33026245)[^o14]. Jabotinsky matrices were popularized during the 70s by Louis Comtet's book *Advanced Combinatorics*, where he referred to them as *iteration matrices* (which is a denomination also sometimes used nowadays[^o15]). This article's denomination appeared later[^o16][^o17]**[^o18]**[^o19][^o20]. [Donald Knuth](wikipedia://en/8095) uses the name *convolution matrix*.[^o5]

::: {#o22 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Jabotinsky matrices satisfy the fundamental relationship

$$\textbf B(f \circ g) = \textbf B(g)\textbf B(f)$$

which makes the Jabotinsky matrix $\mathbf B(f)$ a (direct) representation of $f(x)$. Here the term $f \circ g$ denotes the [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of functions $f(g(x))$.

The fundamental property implies

- $\textbf B(f^n) = \textbf B(f)^n$, where $f^n$ is an [iterated function](wikipedia://en/1448702) and $n$ is a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474).
- $\textbf B(f^{-1}) = \textbf B(f)^{-1}$, where $f^{-1}$ is the [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907), if $f$ has a [compositional inverse](wikipedia://en/60012#Composition_inverse).
- $$\begin{bmatrix}1,x,x^2,...\end{bmatrix} \textbf B(f) = \begin{bmatrix}1,f(x),f(x)^2,...\end{bmatrix}.$$

:::

::: {#o24 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

Given a sequence $(\Omega_n)_{n\ge0}$, we can instead define the matrix with the coefficient $(B_{n,k}^\Omega)_{n, k\geq 0}$ by[^o4]

$$\Omega_k f(x)^k = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} B^\Omega_{n,k} \Omega_n x^n.$$

If $(\Omega_n)_{n\ge0}$ is the constant sequence equal to $1$, we recover Jabotinsky matrices. In some contexts, the sequence is chosen to be $\Omega_n = 1/n!$, so that the entry are given by regular [Bell polynomials](wikipedia://en/572498). This is a more convenient form for functions such as $f(x) = -\log(1-x)$ and $f(x) = e^x - 1$ where [Stirling numbers of the first and second kind](wikipedia://en/95465) appear in the matrices (see the examples).

This generalization gives a completely equivalent matrix since $B_{n,k}^\Omega \frac{\Omega_n}{\Omega_k} = B_{n,k}$.

:::

::: {#o26 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The Jabotinsky matrix of a constant is:

$$\mathbf B(a) = \left(\begin{array}{cccc} 1&a&a^2& \cdots \\ 0&0&0& \cdots \\ 0&0&0& \cdots \\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots \end{array}\right)$$

- The Jabotinsky matrix of a constant multiple is:

$$\textbf B(cx) = \left(\begin{array}{cccc} 1&0&0& \cdots \\ 0&c&0& \cdots \\ 0&0&c^2& \cdots \\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots \end{array}\right)$$

- The Jabotinsky matrix of the [successor function](wikipedia://en/325637):

$$\textbf B(1+x) = \left(\begin{array}{ccccc} 1&1&1&1& \cdots \\ 0&1&2&3& \cdots \\ 0&0&1&3& \cdots \\ 0&0&0&1& \cdots \\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots \end{array}\right)$$

The matrix displays [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497).

- The Jabotinsky matrix of the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) is given by $\textbf B(\exp)_{n,k} = \frac{k^n}{n!}$.
- The Jabotinsky matrix of the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) is related to the (unsigned) [Stirling numbers of the first kind](wikipedia://en/2229296) scaled by [factorials](wikipedia://en/10606):

$$\textbf B(-\log(1 - x)) = \left(\begin{array}{cccccc} 1&0&0&0&0& \cdots \\ 0&1&0&0&0& \cdots \\ 0&\frac{1}{2}&1&0&0& \cdots \\ 0&\frac{1}{3}&1&1&0& \cdots \\ 0&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{11}{12}&\frac{3}{2}&1& \cdots \\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots \end{array}\right)$$

$$\textbf B(-\log(1 - x))_{n, k} = \left[{n \atop k}\right] \frac{k!}{n!}$$

- The Jabotinsky matrix of the exponential function minus 1 is related to the [Stirling numbers of the second kind](wikipedia://en/2229292) scaled by factorials:

$$\textbf B(\exp(x) - 1) = \left(\begin{array}{cccccc} 1&0&0&0&0& \cdots \\ 0&1&0&0&0& \cdots \\ 0&\frac{1}{2}&1&0&0& \cdots \\ 0&\frac{1}{6}&1&1&0& \cdots \\ 0&\frac{1}{24}&\frac{7}{12}&\frac{3}{2}&1& \cdots \\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots \end{array}\right)$$

$$\textbf B(\exp(x) - 1)_{n,k} = \left\{{n \atop k}\right\} \frac{k!}{n!}$$

:::

## Alternative convention {#Alternative_convention}

Some authors,[^o33][^o28][^o15] which include Bennett and Jabotinsky in their original work, use the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) convention $\mathbf B(f)^{\text{T}}$ for the matrix, which is an infinite upper [triangular matrix](wikipedia://en/374222) when $f(0) = 0$. The fundamental relationship becomes

$$\textbf B(f \circ g)^{\text{T}} = \textbf B(f)^{\text{T}}\textbf B(g)^{\text{T}},$$

which preserves the order of the operations.

::: {#o30 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Bell matrix"}

## Related matrices {#Related_matrices}

- Jabotinsky matrices are a special case of the later introduced [Riordan arrays](wikipedia://en/38457493).
- They are also related to [Carleman linearization](wikipedia://en/62503788) and *Carleman (embedding) matrices.*[^o31][^o32][^o33]
- [Grunsky matrices](wikipedia://en/33994467) can be expressed with Jabotinsky matrices.[^o14]
