# Semimartingale

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Semimartingale"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), a real-valued [stochastic process](wikipedia://en/47895) *X* is called a **semimartingale** if it can be decomposed as the sum of a [local martingale](wikipedia://en/7302795) and an adapted finite-variation process whose paths are right-continuous with left limits ([càdlàg](wikipedia://en/30862839)). Semimartingales are "good integrators", forming the largest class of processes with respect to which the [Itô integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itô_integral) and the [Stratonovich integral](wikipedia://en/2314574) can be defined.

:::

The class of semimartingales is quite large (including, for example, all continuously differentiable processes, [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/149984) and [Poisson processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_process)). [Submartingales](wikipedia://en/274536#Submartingales_and_supermartingales) and [supermartingales](wikipedia://en/274536#Submartingales_and_supermartingales) together represent a subset of the semimartingales.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A real-valued process *X* defined on the [filtered probability space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filtered_probability_space) (Ω,*F*,(*F*<sub>*t*</sub>)<sub>*t* ≥ 0</sub>,P) is called a **semimartingale** if it can be decomposed as

$$X_t = M_t + A_t$$

where *M* is a [local martingale](wikipedia://en/7302795) and *A* is a [càdlàg](wikipedia://en/30862839) [adapted process](wikipedia://en/6583420) of locally [bounded variation](wikipedia://en/330673). This means that for almost all $\omega \in \Omega$ and all compact intervals $I \subset [0,\infty)$, the sample path $I \ni s \mapsto A_s(\omega)$ is of bounded variation. Because every martingale in a right-continuous filtration has a [càdlàg](wikipedia://en/30862839) version, one commonly considers semimartingale $X$ to have right-continuous paths with left limits denoted by $X_-$. The [jumps](wikipedia://en/2180750) of a semimartingale are then given by $\Delta X = X-X_-$.

An **R**<sup>*n*</sup>-valued process *X* = (*X*<sup>1</sup>,...,*X*<sup>*n*</sup>) is a semimartingale if each of its components *X*<sup>*i*</sup> is a semimartingale.

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Alternative definition"}

## Alternative definition {#Alternative_definition}

First, the simple [predictable processes](wikipedia://en/33414015) are defined to be linear combinations of processes of the form *H*<sub>*t*</sub> = *A*1<sub>{*t* > *T*}</sub> for stopping times *T* and *F*<sub>*T*</sub> -measurable random variables *A*. The integral *H* ⋅ *X* for any such simple predictable process *H* and real-valued process *X* is

$$H\cdot X_t := 1_{\{t>T\}}A(X_t-X_T).$$

This is extended to all simple predictable processes by the linearity of *H* ⋅ *X* in *H*.

A real-valued process *X* is a semimartingale if it is càdlàg, adapted, and for every *t* ≥ 0,

$$\left\{H\cdot X_t:H{\rm\ is\ simple\ predictable\ and\ }|H|\le 1\right\}$$

is bounded in probability. The Bichteler–Dellacherie Theorem states that these two definitions are equivalent (Protter 2004, 144).

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- Adapted and continuously differentiable processes are continuous, locally finite-variation processes, and hence semimartingales.
- [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/149984) is a semimartingale.
- All càdlàg [martingales](wikipedia://en/274536), submartingales and supermartingales are semimartingales.
- [Itō processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itō_calculus#Itō_processes), which satisfy a stochastic differential equation of the form *dX* = *σdW* + *μdt* are semimartingales. Here, *W* is a Brownian motion and *σ, μ* are adapted processes.
- Every [Lévy process](wikipedia://en/1025748) is a semimartingale.

Although most continuous and adapted processes studied in the literature are semimartingales, this is not always the case.

- [Fractional Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/1607785) with Hurst parameter *H* ≠ 1/2 is not a semimartingale.

:::

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- The semimartingales form the largest class of processes for which the [Itō integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itō_calculus) can be defined.
- Linear combinations of semimartingales are semimartingales.
- Products of semimartingales are semimartingales, which is a consequence of the integration by parts formula for the [Itō integral](wikipedia://en/228001).
- The [quadratic variation](wikipedia://en/2143889) exists for every semimartingale.
- The class of semimartingales is closed under [optional stopping](wikipedia://en/7308284), [localization](wikipedia://en/1202193#Localization), [change of time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Change_of_time) and absolutely continuous [change of probability measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_continuous#Absolute_continuity_of_measures) (see [Girsanov's Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girsanov's_Theorem)).
- If *X* is an **R**<sup>*m*</sup>-valued semimartingale and *f* is a twice continuously differentiable function from **R**<sup>*m*</sup> to **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, then *f*(*X*) is a semimartingale. This is a consequence of [Itō's lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itō's_lemma).
- The property of being a semimartingale is preserved under shrinking the filtration. More precisely, if *X* is a semimartingale with respect to the filtration *F*<sub>t</sub>, and is adapted with respect to the subfiltration *G*<sub>t</sub>, then *X* is a *G*<sub>t</sub>-semimartingale.
- (Jacod's Countable Expansion) The property of being a semimartingale is preserved under enlarging the filtration by a countable set of disjoint sets. Suppose that *F*<sub>t</sub> is a filtration, and *G*<sub>t</sub> is the filtration generated by *F*<sub>t</sub> and a countable set of disjoint measurable sets. Then, every *F*<sub>t</sub>-semimartingale is also a *G*<sub>t</sub>-semimartingale. (Protter 2004, 53)

:::

## Semimartingale decompositions {#Semimartingale_decompositions}

By definition, every semimartingale is a sum of a local martingale and a finite-variation process. However, this decomposition is not unique.

### Continuous semimartingales {#Continuous_semimartingales}

A continuous semimartingale uniquely decomposes as *X* = *M* + *A* where *M* is a continuous local martingale and *A* is a continuous finite-variation process starting at zero. (Rogers Williams 1987, 358)

For example, if *X* is an Itō process satisfying the stochastic differential equation d*X*<sub>t</sub> = σ<sub>t</sub> d*W*<sub>t</sub> + *b*<sub>t</sub> dt, then

$$M_t=X_0+\int_0^t\sigma_s\,dW_s,\ A_t=\int_0^t b_s\,ds.$$

::: {#o16 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Special semimartingale"}

### Special semimartingales {#Special_semimartingales}

A special semimartingale is a real-valued process *$X$* with the decomposition $X = M^X +B^X$, where $M^X$ is a local martingale and $B^X$ is a predictable finite-variation process starting at zero. If this decomposition exists, then it is unique up to a P-null set.

Every special semimartingale is a semimartingale. Conversely, a semimartingale is a special semimartingale if and only if the process *X*<sub>t</sub><sup>\*</sup> ≡ sup<sub>*s* ≤ *t*</sub> |X<sub>*s*</sub>| is [locally integrable](wikipedia://en/1202193#Localization) (Protter 2004, 130).

For example, every continuous semimartingale is a special semimartingale, in which case *M* and *A* are both continuous processes.

:::

### Multiplicative decompositions {#Multiplicative_decompositions}

Recall that $\mathcal{E}(X)$ denotes the [stochastic exponential](wikipedia://en/16986027) of semimartingale $X$. If $X$ is a [special semimartingale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_semimartingale) such that $\Delta B^X \neq -1$, then $\mathcal{E}(B^X)\neq 0$ and $\mathcal{E}(X)/\mathcal{E}(B^X)=\mathcal{E}\left(\int_0^\cdot \frac{M^X_u}{1+\Delta B^X_u}\right)$ is a local martingale.[^o19] Process $\mathcal{E}(B^X)$ is called the *multiplicative compensator* of $\mathcal{E}(X)$ and the identity $\mathcal{E}(X)=\mathcal{E}\left(\int_0^\cdot \frac{M^X_u}{1+\Delta B^X_u}\right)\mathcal{E}(B^X)$ the *multiplicative decomposition* of $\mathcal{E}(X)$.

### Purely discontinuous semimartingales / quadratic pure-jump semimartingales {#Purely_discontinuous_semimartingales_/_quadratic_pure-jump_semimartingales}

A semimartingale is called *purely discontinuous* ([Kallenberg 2002](wikipedia://en/9653775#CITEREFKallenberg2002)) if its quadratic variation \[*X*\] is a finite-variation pure-jump process, i.e.,

$$[X]_t=\sum_{s\le t}(\Delta X_s)^2.$$

By this definition, *time* is a purely discontinuous semimartingale even though it exhibits no jumps at all. The alternative (and preferred) terminology *quadratic pure-jump* semimartingale for a purely discontinuous semimartingale (Protter 2004, 71) is motivated by the fact that the quadratic variation of a purely discontinuous semimartingale is a pure jump process. Every finite-variation semimartingale is a quadratic pure-jump semimartingale. An adapted continuous process is a quadratic pure-jump semimartingale if and only if it is of finite variation.

For every semimartingale X there is a unique continuous local martingale $X^c$ starting at zero such that $X-X^c$ is a quadratic pure-jump semimartingale ((He Wang Yan 1992, 209); (Kallenberg 2002, 527)). The local martingale $X^c$ is called the *continuous martingale part of* *X*.

Observe that $X^c$ is measure-specific. If *$P$* and *$Q$* are two equivalent measures then $X^c(P)$ is typically different from $X^c(Q)$, while both $X-X^c(P)$ and $X-X^c(Q)$ are quadratic pure-jump semimartingales. By [Girsanov's theorem](wikipedia://en/336940) $X^c(P)-X^c(Q)$ is a continuous finite-variation process, yielding $[X^c(P)]=[X^c(Q)] = [X]-\sum_{s\leq\cdot}(\Delta X_s)^2$.

### Continuous-time and discrete-time components of a semimartingale {#Continuous-time_and_discrete-time_components_of_a_semimartingale}

Every semimartingale $X$ has a unique decomposition

$$X = X_0 + X^{\mathrm{qc}} +X^{\mathrm{dp}},$$

where $X^{\mathrm{qc}}_0=X^{\mathrm{dp}}_0=0$, the $X^{\mathrm{qc}}$ component does not jump at predictable times, and the $X^{\mathrm{dp}}$ component is equal to the sum of its jumps at predictable times in the semimartingale topology. One then has $[X^{\mathrm{qc}},X^{\mathrm{dp}}]=0$.[^o25] Typical examples of the "qc" component are [Itô process](wikipedia://en/1498680) and [Lévy process](wikipedia://en/1025748). The "dp" component is often taken to be a [Markov chain](wikipedia://en/60876) but in general the predictable jump times may not be isolated points; for example, in principle $X^{\mathrm{dp}}$ may jump at every rational time. Observe also that $X^{\mathrm{dp}}$ is not necessarily of finite variation, even though it is equal to the sum of its jumps (in the [semimartingale topology](wikipedia://en/73110666)). For example, on the time interval $[0,\infty)$ take $X^{\mathrm{dp}}$ to have independent increments, with jumps at times $\{\tau_n = 2-1/n\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ taking values $\pm 1/n$ with equal probability.

### Canonical Decomposition {#Canonical_Decomposition}

Although the decomposition of a semimartingale into the sum of a local martingale and a finite-variation process is not unique, every semimartingale has a unique *canonical decomposition* as

$$X = N^{X}+A^{X}+\sum_{s\leq\cdot}\mathbf{1}_{\{\vert{\Delta X}_{s}\vert>1\}}\Delta X_{s},$$

for a unique local martingale $N^{X}$ and a unique predictable finite variation process $A^{X}$ starting at zero.

## Semimartingales on a manifold {#Semimartingales_on_a_manifold}

The concept of semimartingales, and the associated theory of stochastic calculus, extends to processes taking values in a [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219). A process *X* on the manifold *M* is a semimartingale if *f*(*X*) is a semimartingale for every smooth function *f* from *M* to **R**. (Rogers Williams 1987, 24) Stochastic calculus for semimartingales on general manifolds requires the use of the [Stratonovich integral](wikipedia://en/2314574).
