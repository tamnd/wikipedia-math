# Geometric measure theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Geometric measure theory"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **geometric measure theory** (**GMT**) is the study of [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) properties of [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) (typically in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697)) through [measure theory](wikipedia://en/19873). It allows mathematicians to extend tools from [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) to a much larger class of [surfaces](wikipedia://en/27865) that are not necessarily [smooth](wikipedia://en/2119219).

:::

## History {#History}

Geometric measure theory was born out of the desire to solve [Plateau's problem](wikipedia://en/338129) (named after [Joseph Plateau](wikipedia://en/331154)) which asks if for every smooth closed curve in $\mathbb{R}^3$ there exists a [surface](wikipedia://en/27865) of least [area](wikipedia://en/1209) among all surfaces whose [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) equals the given curve. Such surfaces mimic [soap films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap_film).

The problem had remained open since it was posed in 1760 by [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Louis_Lagrange). It was solved independently in the 1930s by [Jesse Douglas](wikipedia://en/387571) and [Tibor Radó](wikipedia://en/1490393) under certain [topological](wikipedia://en/29954) restrictions. In 1960 [Herbert Federer](wikipedia://en/12185345) and [Wendell Fleming](wikipedia://en/35262774) used the theory of [currents](wikipedia://en/3021207) with which they were able to solve the orientable Plateau's problem [analytically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Analysis) without topological restrictions, thus sparking geometric measure theory. Later [Jean Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Taylor) after [Fred Almgren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_J._Almgren,_Jr.) proved [Plateau's laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plateau's_laws) for the kind of singularities that can occur in these more general soap films and soap bubbles clusters.

## Important notions {#Important_notions}

The following objects are central in geometric measure theory:

- [Hausdorff measure](wikipedia://en/504109) and [Hausdorff dimension](wikipedia://en/14294)
- [Rectifiable](wikipedia://en/2644238) sets (or [Radon measures](wikipedia://en/521933)), which are [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) with the least possible regularity required to admit approximate [tangent spaces](wikipedia://en/30807).
- Characterization of rectifiability through existence of approximate tangents, densities, projections, etc.
- Orthogonal projections, [Kakeya sets](wikipedia://en/588260), [Besicovitch sets](wikipedia://en/588260#Besicovitch_needle_sets)
- Uniform rectifiability
- Rectifiability and uniform rectifiability of (subsets of) [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018), e.g. SubRiemannian manifolds, Carnot groups, Heisenberg groups, etc.
- Connections to singular integrals, Fourier transform, Frostman measures, harmonic measures, etc
- [Currents](wikipedia://en/36180950#Integral_currents), a generalization of the concept of [oriented](wikipedia://en/187446) [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470), possibly with [boundary](wikipedia://en/2073470#Manifold_with_boundary).
- Flat chains, an alternative generalization of the concept of [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470), possibly with [boundary](wikipedia://en/2073470#Manifold_with_boundary).
- [Caccioppoli sets](wikipedia://en/8907443) (also known as sets of locally finite perimeter), a generalization of the concept of [manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifolds) on which the [divergence theorem](wikipedia://en/151864) applies.
- Plateau type minimization problems from calculus of variations

The following theorems and concepts are also central:

- The [area formula](wikipedia://en/69563183), which generalizes the concept of [change of variables](wikipedia://en/193748) in integration.
- The [coarea formula](wikipedia://en/17915846), which generalizes and adapts [Fubini's theorem](wikipedia://en/255245) to geometric measure theory.
- The [isoperimetric inequality](wikipedia://en/326182), which states that the smallest possible [circumference](wikipedia://en/5916) for a given [area](wikipedia://en/1209) is that of a round [circle](wikipedia://en/6220).
- [Flat convergence](wikipedia://en/36180950), which generalizes the concept of manifold convergence.

## Examples {#Examples}

The [Brunn–Minkowski inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunn–Minkowski_inequality) for the *n*-dimensional volumes of [convex bodies](wikipedia://en/8652020) *K* and *L*,

$$\mathrm{vol} \big( (1 - \lambda) K + \lambda L \big)^{1/n} \geq (1 - \lambda) \mathrm{vol} (K)^{1/n} + \lambda \, \mathrm{vol} (L)^{1/n},$$

can be proved on a single page and quickly yields the classical [isoperimetric inequality](wikipedia://en/326182). The Brunn–Minkowski inequality also leads to [Anderson's theorem](wikipedia://en/10614125) in statistics. The proof of the Brunn–Minkowski inequality predates modern measure theory; the development of measure theory and [Lebesgue integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_integration) allowed connections to be made between geometry and analysis, to the extent that in an integral form of the Brunn–Minkowski inequality known as the [Prékopa–Leindler inequality](wikipedia://en/10570298) the geometry seems almost entirely absent.

### Gauss curvature measure {#Gauss_curvature_measure}

*Image: Gauss\_map.svg.* The Gauss map provides a mapping from every point on a curve or a surface to a corresponding point on a unit sphere. In this example, the curvature of a 2D-surface is mapped onto a 1D unit circle.

Consider the **[Gauss map](wikipedia://en/378881)**, which sends each point on a smooth surface to the unit vector orthogonal to its local tangent plane. This can be generalized. Let $K$ be a closed surface in $\mathbb{R}^n$ containing the origin, and enclosing a convex region, then each point on $K$ has one or more local [supporting planes](wikipedia://en/28752383). For any $z \in K$, define $N_K(z)$ to be the set of orthogonal directions to all local supporting planes to $K$ at $z$.

Now we define the [multivalued](wikipedia://en/222367) Gauss map $G_K: \mathbb S^{n-1} \to \mathbb S^{n-1}$ defined by

$$G_K(x) = N_K(\rho(x) x)$$

where $\rho(x) x$ is the intersection of the ray $0 \to x$ with $K$. This map then maps the [uniform measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_measure) $\mu$ on $\mathbb S^{n-1}$ to another measure $\mathbb S^{n-1}$ by

$$\mu_K (E) := \mu(G_K(E))$$

for any [Borel set](wikipedia://en/43327). This is the **Gaussian curvature measure** associated with $K$. It is a [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322) for any $K$.

This then induces the inverse problem: Given a Borel measure on the sphere, is it a Gaussian curvature measure? Alexandrov showed the following:[^o7]

- Given a Borel measure $\nu$, there exists some such $K$ such that $\nu = \mu_K$ iff $$\begin{cases} v\left(\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\right)=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\right) \\ v\left(\mathbb{S}^{n-1} \backslash \omega\right)>\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\left(\omega^*\right),\;\; \forall \omega \subseteq \mathbb{S}^{n-1} \text { compact and convex. }\end{cases}$$
- If such a $K$ exists, then it is unique up to [dilation](wikipedia://en/1591770).

Such $K$ can be constructed as follows: Approximate $\nu$ as the [weak limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_limit) of a sequence of point-mass measures. Each is the Gaussian curvature measure of a [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471), and the sequence of polytopes converge to $K$.

If $\mu_K$ has a bounded probability density function (in the sense of a [Radon–Nikodym derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon–Nikodym_derivative)):

$$\frac{d\mu_K}{d\mu}(y) = \rho(y), \quad\rho: \mathbb S^{n-1} \to [0, c]$$

then $K$ is [continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuously_differentiable) (that is, its tangent planes are unique and varies continuously over its surface).[^o7]

### Surface area measure {#Surface_area_measure}

Similarly, we define the **surface area measure** of $K$ by

$$\nu_K (E) := \mu(N_K^{-1}(E))$$

for any Borel set. It is a Borel measure for any $K$. This has an intuitive picture in as follows: Consider an open set $U \subset \mathbb S^{n-1}$ with a single connected continuous boundary $\partial U$. For each $\hat n \in \partial U$, produce the supporting plane of $K$ perpendicular to $\hat n$. As $\hat n$ runs over $\partial U$, the supporting planes rub all over $K$ and the contact points produces a $(n-2)$-dimensional closed subset of $K$ that encloses a $(n-1)$-dimensional region of $K$. Then $\nu_K(U)$ is its measure.

For example, if $K$ is a rectangle of side lengths $2a, 2b$ in the plane, then $\mu_K$ is the sum of four equal point mass measures

$$\frac 14 (\delta_{(a, b)/\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}} + \delta_{(-a, b)/\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}} + \delta_{(a, -b)/\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}} + \delta_{(-a, -b)/\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}})$$

but $\nu_K$ is the sum of four point mass measures $2b \delta_{(1, 0)} + 2b \delta_{(-1, 0)} + 2a \delta_{(0, 1)} + 2a \delta_{(0, -1)}$.

### Others {#Others}

Given a [convex function](wikipedia://en/245568) $u: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ where $\Omega$ is an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$, its associated **Monge–Ampère measure** $\mu_u$ on $\Omega$ is defined as $\mu_u(E) := \mu(\cap_{x \in \Omega} \partial u(x))$, where $\partial$ is the [subdifferential](wikipedia://en/3462862) function, and $\mu$ is the Lebesque measure on $\mathbb{R}^n$. This has applications in the study of [weak solutions](wikipedia://en/3555416) to the [Monge–Ampère equation](wikipedia://en/4914066). It is a nonnegative, [locally finite](wikipedia://en/6621919), Borel measure.[^o7]
