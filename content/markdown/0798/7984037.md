# Correlation (projective geometry)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Correlation"}

In [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849), a **correlation** is a transformation of a *d*-dimensional [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) that maps [subspaces](wikipedia://en/242135#Projective_subspace) of dimension *k* to subspaces of dimension *d* − *k* − 1, reversing [inclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusion_%28set_theory%29) and preserving [incidence](wikipedia://en/387224). Correlations are also called **reciprocities** or **reciprocal transformations**.

:::

## In two dimensions {#In_two_dimensions}

In the [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325), points and lines are [dual](wikipedia://en/539925) to each other. As expressed by Coxeter,

A correlation is a point-to-line and a line-to-point transformation that preserves the relation of incidence in accordance with the principle of duality. Thus it transforms [ranges](wikipedia://en/34644484) into [pencils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pencil_%28mathematics%29), pencils into ranges, [\[complete\] quadrangles](wikipedia://en/2605272) into \[complete\] quadrilaterals, and so on.[^o3]

Given a line *m* and *P* a point not on *m*, an elementary correlation is obtained as follows: for every *Q* on *m* form the line *PQ*. The [inverse](wikipedia://en/14907) correlation starts with the pencil on *P*: for any line *q* in this pencil take the point *m* ∩ *q*. The [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of two correlations that share the same pencil is a [perspectivity](wikipedia://en/23912960).

## In three dimensions {#In_three_dimensions}

In a 3-dimensional projective space a correlation maps a point to a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29). As stated in one textbook:[^o5]

If *κ* is such a correlation, every point *P* is transformed by it into a plane *π*′ = *κP*, and conversely, every point *P* arises from a unique plane *π*′ by the inverse transformation *κ*<sup>−1</sup>.

Three-dimensional correlations also transform lines into lines, so they may be considered to be [collineations](wikipedia://en/4072976) of the two spaces.

## In higher dimensions {#In_higher_dimensions}

In general *n*-dimensional projective space, a correlation takes a point to a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862). This context was described by Paul Yale:

A correlation of the projective space **P**(*V*) is an inclusion-reversing permutation of the proper subspaces of **P**(*V*).[^o7]

He proves a theorem stating that a correlation *φ* interchanges joins and intersections, and for any projective subspace *W* of **P**(*V*), the dimension of the image of *W* under *φ* is (*n* − 1) − dim *W*, where *n* is the dimension of the [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V* used to produce the projective space **P**(*V*).

## Existence of correlations {#Existence_of_correlations}

Correlations can exist only if the space is self-dual. For dimensions 3 and higher, self-duality is easy to test: A coordinatizing [skewfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skewfield) exists and self-duality fails if and only if the skewfield is not isomorphic to its opposite.

## Special types of correlations {#Special_types_of_correlations}

### Polarity {#Polarity}

If a correlation *φ* is an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) (that is, two applications of the correlation equals the identity: *φ*<sup>2</sup>(*P*) = *P* for all points *P*) then it is called a [polarity](wikipedia://en/19451201). Polarities of projective spaces lead to [polar spaces](wikipedia://en/3948557), which are defined by taking the collection of all subspace which are contained in their image under the polarity.

### Natural correlation {#Natural_correlation}

There is a natural correlation induced between a projective space **P**(*V*) and its dual **P**(*V*<sup>∗</sup>) by the [natural pairing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_pairing) $\langle$⋅,⋅$\rangle$ between the underlying vector spaces *V* and its [dual](wikipedia://en/7988) *V*<sup>∗</sup>, where every subspace *W* of *V*<sup>∗</sup> is mapped to its [orthogonal complement](wikipedia://en/1058833) *W*<sup>⊥</sup> in *V*, defined as *W*<sup>⊥</sup> = {*v* ∈ *V* | $\langle$*w*, *v*$\rangle$ = 0, ∀*w* ∈ *W*}.

Composing this natural correlation with an isomorphism of projective spaces induced by a semilinear map produces a correlation of **P**(*V*) to itself. In this way, every nondegenerate semilinear map *V* → *V*<sup>∗</sup> induces a correlation of a projective space to itself.
