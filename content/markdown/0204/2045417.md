# Petrov classification

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Petrov classification"}

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) and [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028), the **Petrov classification** (also known as Petrov–Pirani–Penrose classification) describes the possible algebraic [symmetries](wikipedia://en/53741) of the [Weyl tensor](wikipedia://en/1026848) at each [event](wikipedia://en/28758#Basic_concepts) in a [Lorentzian manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentzian_manifold).

:::

It is most often applied in studying [exact solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exact_solutions) of [Einstein's field equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein's_field_equations), but strictly speaking the classification is a theorem in pure mathematics applying to any Lorentzian manifold, independent of any physical interpretation. The classification was found in 1954 by [A. Z. Petrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._Z._Petrov) and independently by [Felix Pirani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Pirani) in 1957.

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Classification theorem"}

## Classification theorem {#Classification_theorem}

We can think of a fourth [rank](wikipedia://en/29965#Tensor_rank) [tensor](wikipedia://en/29965) such as the [Weyl tensor](wikipedia://en/1026848), *evaluated at some event*, as acting on the space of [bivectors](wikipedia://en/884040) at that event like a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) acting on a vector space:

$$X^{ab} \rightarrow \frac{1}{2} \, {C^{ab}}_{mn} X^{mn}$$

Then, it is natural to consider the problem of finding [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) $\lambda$ and [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvectors) (which are now referred to as eigenbivectors) $X^{ab}$ such that

$$\frac{1}{2} \, {C^{ab}}_{mn} \, X^{mn} = \lambda \, X^{ab}$$

In (four-dimensional) Lorentzian spacetimes, there is a six-dimensional space of antisymmetric bivectors at each event. However, the symmetries of the Weyl tensor imply that any eigenbivectors must belong to a four-dimensional subset. Thus, the Weyl tensor (at a given event) can in fact have *at most four* linearly independent eigenbivectors.

The eigenbivectors of the Weyl tensor can occur with various [multiplicities](wikipedia://en/19211790) and any multiplicities among the eigenbivectors indicates a kind of *algebraic symmetry* of the Weyl tensor at the given event. The different types of Weyl tensor (at a given event) can be determined by solving a [characteristic equation](wikipedia://en/218268#Characteristic_equation), in this case a [quartic equation](wikipedia://en/251591). All the above happens similarly to the theory of the eigenvectors of an ordinary linear operator.

These eigenbivectors are associated with certain [null vectors](wikipedia://en/1453977) in the original spacetime, which are called the **principal null directions** (at a given event). The relevant [multilinear algebra](wikipedia://en/243946) is somewhat involved (see the citations below), but the resulting classification theorem states that there are precisely six possible types of algebraic symmetry. These are known as the **Petrov types**:

*Image: Petrov.png.* The *Penrose diagram* showing the possible degenerations of the Petrov type of the Weyl tensor

- **Type I**: four simple principal null directions,
- **Type II**: one double and two simple principal null directions,
- **Type D**: two double principal null directions,
- **Type III**: one triple and one simple principal null direction,
- **Type N**: one quadruple principal null direction,
- **Type O**: the Weyl tensor vanishes.

The possible transitions between Petrov types are shown in the figure, which can also be interpreted as stating that some of the Petrov types are "more special" than others. For example, type **I**, the most general type, can *degenerate* to types **II** or **D**, while type **II** can degenerate to types **III**, **N**, or **D**.

Different events in a given spacetime can have different Petrov types. A Weyl tensor that has type **I** (at some event) is called **algebraically general**; otherwise, it is called **algebraically special** (at that event). In General Relativity, type **O** spacetimes are **[conformally flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformally_flat)**.

:::

## Newman–Penrose formalism {#Newman–Penrose_formalism}

The [Newman–Penrose formalism](wikipedia://en/6176811) is often used in practice for the classification. Consider the following set of bivectors, constructed out of [tetrads](wikipedia://en/11141222) of [null vectors](wikipedia://en/1453977) (note that in some notations, symbols l and n are interchanged):

$$U_{ab}=-2l_{[a}\bar{m}_{b]}$$

$$V_{ab}=2n_{[a}m_{b]}$$

$$W_{ab}=2m_{[a}\bar{m}_{b]}-2n_{[a}l_{b]}.$$

The Weyl tensor can be expressed as a combination of these bivectors through

$$\begin{aligned}C_{abcd}&= \Psi_0U_{ab}U_{cd} \\ &\, \, \, +\Psi_1(U_{ab}W_{cd}+W_{ab}U_{cd}) \\ &\, \, \, +\Psi_2(V_{ab}U_{cd}+U_{ab}V_{cd}+W_{ab}W_{cd}) \\ &\, \, \, +\Psi_3(V_{ab}W_{cd}+W_{ab}V_{cd}) \\ &\, \, \, +\Psi_4V_{ab}V_{cd}+c.c.\end{aligned}$$

where the $\{\Psi_j\}$ are the [Weyl scalars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_scalar) and c.c. is the complex conjugate. The six different Petrov types are distinguished by which of the Weyl scalars vanish. The conditions are

- **Type I** : $\Psi_0=0$,
- **Type II** : $\Psi_0=\Psi_1=0$,
- **Type D** : $\Psi_0=\Psi_1=\Psi_3=\Psi_4=0$,
- **Type III** : $\Psi_0=\Psi_1=\Psi_2=0$,
- **Type N** : $\Psi_0=\Psi_1=\Psi_2=\Psi_3=0$,
- **Type O** : $\Psi_0=\Psi_1=\Psi_2=\Psi_3=\Psi_4=0$.

## Bel criteria {#Bel_criteria}

Given a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28general_relativity%29) on a Lorentzian manifold $M$, the Weyl tensor $C$ for this metric may be computed. If the Weyl tensor is *algebraically special* at some $p \in M$, there is a useful set of conditions, found by Lluis (or Louis) Bel and Robert Debever,[^o6] for determining precisely the Petrov type at $p$. Denoting the Weyl tensor components at $p$ by $C_{abcd}$ (assumed non-zero, i.e., not of type **O**), the **Bel criteria** may be stated as:

- $C_{abcd}$ is type **N** if and only if there exists a vector $k(p)$ satisfying

$$C_{abcd} \, k^d =0$$

where $k$ is necessarily null and unique (up to scaling).

- If $C_{abcd}$ is **not type N**, then $C_{abcd}$ is of type **III** if and only if there exists a vector $k(p)$ satisfying

$$C_{abcd}\, k^bk^d=0= {^*C}_{abcd}\, k^bk^d$$

where $k$ is necessarily null and unique (up to scaling).

- $C_{abcd}$ is of type **II** if and only if there exists a vector $k$ satisfying

$C_{abcd}\, k^bk^d=\alpha k_ak_c$ and ${}^*C_{abcd}\, k^bk^d=\beta k_ak_c$ ($\alpha \beta \neq 0$)

where $k$ is necessarily null and unique (up to scaling).

- $C_{abcd}$ is of type **D** if and only if there exists *two linearly independent vectors* $k$, $k'$ satisfying the conditions

$C_{abcd}\, k^bk^d=\alpha k_ak_c$, ${}^*C_{abcd}\, k^bk^d=\beta k_ak_c$ ($\alpha \beta \neq 0$)

and

$C_{abcd}\, k'^bk'^d=\gamma k'_ak'_c$, ${}^*C_{abcd}\, k'^bk'^d=\delta k'_ak'_c$ ($\gamma \delta \neq 0$).

where ${{}^*C}_{abcd}$ is the dual of the Weyl tensor at $p$.

In fact, for each criterion above, there are equivalent conditions for the Weyl tensor to have that type. These equivalent conditions are stated in terms of the dual and self-dual of the Weyl tensor and certain bivectors and are collected together in Hall (2004).

The Bel criteria find application in general relativity where determining the Petrov type of algebraically special Weyl tensors is accomplished by searching for null vectors.

## Physical interpretation {#Physical_interpretation}

According to [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), the various algebraically special Petrov types have some interesting physical interpretations, the classification then sometimes being called the **classification of gravitational fields**.

**Type D** regions are associated with the gravitational fields of isolated massive objects, such as stars. More precisely, type **D** fields occur as the exterior field of a gravitating object which is completely characterized by its mass and angular momentum. (A more general object might have nonzero higher [multipole moments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multipole_moments).) The two double principal null directions define "radially" ingoing and outgoing [null congruences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_congruence) near the object which is the source of the field.

The [electrogravitic tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrogravitic_tensor) (or *tidal tensor*) in a type **D** region is very closely analogous to the gravitational fields which are described in [Newtonian gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_gravity) by a [Coulomb](wikipedia://en/47719) type [gravitational potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_potential). Such a tidal field is characterized by *tension* in one direction and *compression* in the orthogonal directions; the eigenvalues have the pattern (-2,1,1). For example, a spacecraft orbiting the Earth experiences a tiny tension along a radius from the center of the Earth, and a tiny compression in the orthogonal directions. Just as in Newtonian gravitation, this tidal field typically decays like $O(r^{-3})$, where $r$ is the distance from the object.

If the object is rotating about some [axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_of_rotation), in addition to the tidal effects, there will be various [gravitomagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitomagnetism) effects, such as [spin-spin forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin-spin_forces) on [gyroscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyroscopes) carried by an observer. In the [Kerr vacuum](wikipedia://en/456715), which is the best known example of type **D** vacuum solution, this part of the field decays like $O(r^{-4})$.

**Type III** regions are associated with a kind of [longitudinal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitudinal_wave) gravitational radiation. In such regions, the tidal forces have a [shearing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_%28fluid%29) effect. This possibility is often neglected, in part because the gravitational radiation which arises in [weak-field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak-field_approximation) is type **N**, and in part because type **III** radiation decays like $O(r^{-2})$, which is faster than type **N** radiation.

**Type N** regions are associated with [transverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transversality_%28mathematics%29) gravitational radiation, which is the type astronomers have detected with [LIGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIGO). The quadruple principal null direction corresponds to the [wave vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_vector) describing the direction of propagation of this radiation. It typically decays like $O(r^{-1})$, so the long-range radiation field is type **N**.

**Type II** regions combine the effects noted above for types **D**, **III**, and **N**, in a rather complicated nonlinear way.

**Type O** regions, or [conformally flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformally_flat) regions, are associated with places where the Weyl tensor vanishes identically. In this case, the curvature is said to be *pure [Ricci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_tensor)*. In a conformally flat region, any gravitational effects must be due to the immediate presence of matter or the [field](wikipedia://en/1293340) [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) of some nongravitational field (such as an [electromagnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field)). In a sense, this means that any distant objects are not exerting any [long range influence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_range_influence) on events in our region. More precisely, if there are any time varying gravitational fields in distant regions, the [news](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_function) has not yet reached our conformally flat region.

[Gravitational radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_radiation) emitted from an isolated system will usually not be algebraically special. The [peeling theorem](wikipedia://en/34253455) describes the way in which, as one moves farther way from the source of the radiation, the various components of the [radiation field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_field) "peel" off, until finally only type **N** radiation is noticeable at large distances. This is similar to the [electromagnetic peeling theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_peeling_theorem).

## Examples {#Examples}

In some (more or less) familiar solutions, the Weyl tensor has the same Petrov type at each event:

- the [Kerr vacuum](wikipedia://en/456715) is everywhere type **D**,
- certain [Robinson/Trautman vacuums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robinson/Trautman_spacetimes) are everywhere type **III**,
- the [pp-wave spacetimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pp-wave_spacetimes) are everywhere type **N**,
- the [FLRW models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedmann-Lemaître_metric) are everywhere type **O**.

More generally, any [spherically symmetric spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherically_symmetric_spacetime) must be of type **D** (or **O**). All algebraically special spacetimes having various types of [stress–energy tensor](wikipedia://en/70671) are known, for example, all the type **D** vacuum solutions.

Some classes of solutions can be invariantly characterized using algebraic symmetries of the Weyl tensor: for example, the class of non-conformally flat null [electrovacuum](wikipedia://en/2424431) or [null dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_dust_solution) solutions admitting an expanding but nontwisting null congruence is precisely the class of *Robinson/Trautmann spacetimes*. These are usually type **II**, but include type **III** and type **N** examples.

:::

## Generalization to higher dimensions {#Generalization_to_higher_dimensions}

A. Coley, R. Milson, V. Pravda and A. Pravdová (2004) developed a generalization of algebraic classification to arbitrary spacetime dimension $d$. Their approach uses a null [frame basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vielbein) approach, that is a frame basis containing two null vectors $l$ and $n$, along with $d-2$ spacelike vectors. Frame basis components of the [Weyl tensor](wikipedia://en/1026848) are classified by their transformation properties under local [Lorentz boosts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_transformations). If particular Weyl components vanish, then $l$ and/or $n$ are said to be **Weyl-Aligned Null Directions** (WANDs). In four dimensions, $l$ is a WAND if and only if it is a principal null direction in the sense defined above. This approach gives a natural higher-dimensional extension of each of the various algebraic types **II**,**D** etc. defined above.

An alternative, but inequivalent, generalization was previously defined by de Smet (2002), based on a [spinorial approach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinors). However, de Smet's approach is restricted to 5 dimensions only.
