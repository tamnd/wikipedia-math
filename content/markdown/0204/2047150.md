# Coincidence point

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Coincidence point"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **coincidence point** (or simply **coincidence**) of two [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) is a point in their common [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) having the same image.

:::

Formally, given two functions

$$f,g \colon X \rightarrow Y$$

we say that a point *x* in *X* is a *coincidence point* of *f* and *g* if *f*(*x*) = *g*(*x*).[^o2]

Coincidence theory (the study of coincidence points) is, in most settings, a generalization of [fixed point](wikipedia://en/449738) theory, the study of points *x* with *f*(*x*) = *x*. Fixed point theory is the special case obtained from the above by letting *X = Y* and taking *g* to be the [identity function](wikipedia://en/15069).

Just as fixed point theory has its [fixed-point theorems](wikipedia://en/1011848), there are [theorems](wikipedia://en/30977) that guarantee the existence of coincidence points for pairs of functions. Notable among them, in the setting of [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470), is the [Lefschetz coincidence theorem](wikipedia://en/582530), which is typically known only in its [special case](wikipedia://en/31308010) formulation for fixed points.[^o3]

Coincidence points, like fixed points, are today studied using many tools from [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) and [topology](wikipedia://en/29954). An [equaliser](wikipedia://en/310767) is a generalization of the coincidence set.[^o4]
