# Brown–Forsythe test

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Brown–Forsythe test"}

The **Brown–Forsythe test** is a [statistical test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_test) for the equality of group variances based on performing an [Analysis of Variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_of_Variance) (ANOVA) on a [transformation](wikipedia://en/10056274) of the [response variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Response_variable). When a [one-way ANOVA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-way_ANOVA) is performed, samples are assumed to have been drawn from distributions with equal [variance](wikipedia://en/32344). If this assumption is not valid, the resulting [*F*-test](wikipedia://en/318976) is invalid. The Brown–Forsythe [test statistic](wikipedia://en/1416975) is the F statistic resulting from an ordinary one-way analysis of variance on the absolute deviations of the groups or treatments data from their individual medians.[^o2]

:::

## Transformation {#Transformation}

The transformed response variable is constructed to measure the [spread](wikipedia://en/23773247) in each group. Let

$$z_{ij}=\left\vert y_{ij} - \tilde{y}_j \right\vert$$

where $\tilde{y}_j$ is the [median](wikipedia://en/18837) of group *j*. The Brown–Forsythe test statistic is the model *F* statistic from a one way ANOVA on *z<sub>ij</sub>*:

$$F = \frac{(N-p)}{(p-1)} \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{p} n_j (\tilde{z}_{\cdot j}-\tilde{z}_{\cdot\cdot})^2} {\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{n_j} (z_{ij}-\tilde{z}_{\cdot j})^2}$$

where *p* is the number of groups, *n<sub>j</sub>* is the number of observations in group *j*, and *N* is the total number of observations. Also $\tilde{z}_{\cdot j}$ are the group means of the $z_{ij}$ and $\tilde{z}_{\cdot\cdot}$ is the overall mean of the $z_{ij}$. This *F*-statistic follows the [*F*-distribution](wikipedia://en/332116) with degrees of freedom $d_1=p-1$ and $d_2=N-p$ under the null hypothesis.

If the variances are indeed heterogeneous, techniques that allow for this (such as the [Welch one-way ANOVA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welch_one-way_ANOVA)) may be used instead of the usual ANOVA.

Good, noting that the deviations are linearly dependent, has modified the test so as to drop the redundant deviations.[^o4]

## Comparison with Levene's test {#Comparison_with_Levene's_test}

[Levene's test](wikipedia://en/4833235) uses the mean instead of the median. Although the optimal choice depends on the underlying distribution, the definition based on the median is recommended as the choice that provides good [robustness](wikipedia://en/2885691) against many types of non-normal data while retaining good [statistical power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_power).[^o6] If one has knowledge of the underlying distribution of the data, this may indicate using one of the other choices. Brown and Forsythe[^o7] performed [Monte Carlo](wikipedia://en/56098) studies that indicated that using the [trimmed mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trimmed_mean) performed best when the underlying data followed a [Cauchy distribution](wikipedia://en/7003) (a [heavy-tailed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy-tailed) distribution) and the median performed best when the underlying data followed a [χ<sup>2</sup> distribution](wikipedia://en/113424) with four degrees of freedom (a sharply [skewed distribution](wikipedia://en/28212)). Using the mean provided the best power for symmetric, moderate-tailed, distributions. O'Brien tested several ways of using the traditional analysis of variance to test heterogeneity of spread in [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606) designs with equal or unequal sample sizes. The jackknife pseudovalues of s<sup>2</sup> and the absolute deviations from the cell median are shown to be robust and relatively powerful.[^o8]
