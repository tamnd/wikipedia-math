# Names of large numbers

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Names of large numbers"}

Depending on context (e.g., language, culture, region), some [large numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_number) have [names](wikipedia://en/164226) that allow for describing large quantities in a textual, not [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831), form. For very large values, the text is generally shorter than a [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214) numeric representation, although longer than [scientific notation](wikipedia://en/59715).

:::

Two naming scales for [large numbers](wikipedia://en/187750) have been used in English and other European languages since the early modern era: the [long and short scales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_and_short_scale). Most English variants use the short scale today, but the long scale remains dominant in many non-English-speaking areas, including [continental Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Europe) and [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_language)-speaking countries in [the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Americas). These naming procedures are based on taking the number *n* occurring in 10<sup>3*n*+3</sup> (short scale) or 10<sup>6*n*</sup> (long scale) and concatenating Latin roots for its units, tens, and hundreds place, together with the suffix *\-illion*.

Names of numbers above a trillion are rarely used in practice; such large numbers have practical usage primarily in the scientific domain, where powers of ten are expressed as *10* with a numeric superscript, and in the case of [hyperinflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation). However, these somewhat rare names are considered acceptable for approximate statements. For example, the statement "There are approximately 7.1 octillion atoms in an adult human body" is understood to be in short scale of the table below (and is only accurate if referring to short scale rather than long scale).

The [Indian numbering system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_numbering_system) uses the named numbers common between the long and short scales up to ten thousand. For larger values, it includes named numbers at each multiple of 100, including [lakh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakh) (10<sup>5</sup>) and [crore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crore) (10<sup>7</sup>).[^o28]

English also has words, such as *zillion*, that are used informally to mean [large but unspecified amounts](wikipedia://en/538913).

## Usage of names of large numbers {#Usage_of_names_of_large_numbers}

Some names of large numbers, such as *million*, *billion*, and *trillion*, have real referents in human experience, and are encountered in many contexts, particularly in finance and economics. At times, the names of large numbers have been forced into common usage as a result of [hyperinflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation). The highest numerical value banknote ever printed was a note for 1 sextillion [pengő](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pengő) (10<sup>21</sup> or 1 milliárd bilpengő as printed) printed in [Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungary) in 1946. In 2009, [Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwe) printed a 100 trillion (10<sup>14</sup>) [Zimbabwean dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwean_dollar_%281980–2009%29) note, which at the time of printing was worth about US\$30.[^o26] In global economics, the name of a significantly larger number was used in 2024, when the Russian news outlet [RBK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RBK_Group) stated that the sum of legal claims against [Google](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google) in [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia) totalled 2 undecillion (2) [rubles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Ruble), or US\$20 decillion (US \$2), a value worth more than all financial assets in the world combined.[^o8] A [Kremlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kremlin) spokesperson, [Dmitry Peskov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitry_Peskov), stated that this value was symbolic.[^o9]

Names of larger numbers, however, have a tenuous, artificial existence, rarely found outside definitions, lists, and discussions of how large numbers are named. Even well-established names like *sextillion* are rarely used, since in the context of science, including astronomy, where such large numbers often occur, they are nearly always written using [scientific notation](wikipedia://en/59715). In this notation, powers of ten are expressed as *10* with a numeric superscript, e.g. "The X-ray emission of the radio galaxy is \1.3 × 10<sup>45</sup> joules." When a number such as 10<sup>45</sup> needs to be referred to in words, it is simply read out as "ten to the forty-fifth" or "ten to the forty-five". This is easier to say and less ambiguous than "quattuordecillion", which means something different in the long scale and the short scale.

When a number represents a quantity rather than a count, [SI prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_prefix) can be used—thus "[femtosecond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Femtosecond)", not "one quadrillionth of a second"—although often powers of ten are used instead of some of the very high and very low prefixes. In some cases, specialized units are used, such as the astronomer's [parsec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsec) and [light year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_year) or the particle physicist's [barn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barn_%28unit%29).

Large numbers have an intellectual fascination and are of mathematical interest, and giving them names is a way to conceptualize and understand them.

One of the earliest examples of this is *[The Sand Reckoner](wikipedia://en/276396)*, in which [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) gave a system for naming large numbers. To do this, he called the numbers up to a [myriad](wikipedia://en/20088) myriad (10<sup>8</sup>) "first numbers" and called 10<sup>8</sup> itself the "unit of the second numbers". Multiples of this unit then became the second numbers, up to this unit taken a myriad myriad times, 10<sup>8</sup>·10<sup>8</sup>=10<sup>16</sup>. This became the "unit of the third numbers", whose multiples were the third numbers, and so on. Archimedes continued naming numbers in this way up to a myriad myriad times the unit of the 10<sup>8</sup>-th numbers, i.e. $(10^8)^{(10^8)}=10^{8\cdot 10^8},$ and embedded this construction within another copy of itself to produce names for numbers up to $((10^8)^{(10^8)})^{(10^8)}=10^{8\cdot 10^{16}}.$ Archimedes then estimated the number of grains of sand that would be required to fill the known universe, and found that it was no more than "one thousand myriad of the eighth numbers" (10<sup>63</sup>).

## Origins of the "standard dictionary numbers" {#Origins_of_the_"standard_dictionary_numbers"}

*Image: Chuquet.gif.*

The words *bymillion* and *trimillion* were first recorded in 1475 in a manuscript of [Jehan Adam](wikipedia://en/9711725). Subsequently, [Nicolas Chuquet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Chuquet) wrote a book *Triparty en la science des nombres* which was not published during Chuquet's lifetime. However, most of it was copied by [Estienne de La Roche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estienne_de_La_Roche) for a portion of his 1520 book, *[L'arismetique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L'arismetique)*. Chuquet's book contains a passage in which he shows a large number marked off into groups of six digits, with the comment:

> Ou qui veult le premier point peult signiffier million Le second point byllion Le tiers point tryllion Le quart quadrillion Le cinq<sup>e</sup> quyllion Le six<sup>e</sup> sixlion Le sept.<sup>e</sup> septyllion Le huyt<sup>e</sup> ottyllion Le neuf<sup>e</sup> nonyllion et ainsi des ault'<sup>s</sup> se plus oultre on vouloit preceder

> (Or if you prefer the first mark can signify million, the second mark byllion, the third mark tryllion, the fourth quadrillion, the fifth quyillion, the sixth sixlion, the seventh septyllion, the eighth ottyllion, the ninth nonyllion and so on with others as far as you wish to go).

Adam and Chuquet used the [long scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_and_short_scale) of powers of a million; that is, Adam's *bymillion* (Chuquet's *byllion*) denoted 10<sup>12</sup>, and Adam's *trimillion* (Chuquet's *tryllion*) denoted 10<sup>18</sup>.

## Googol family {#Googol_family}

The names *[googol](wikipedia://en/18994679)* and *[googolplex](wikipedia://en/12365)* were invented by [Edward Kasner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Kasner)'s nephew Milton Sirotta and introduced in Kasner and Newman's 1940 book *[Mathematics and the Imagination](wikipedia://en/31052235)*[^o41] in the following passage:

The name "googol" was invented by a child (Dr. Kasner's nine-year-old nephew) who was asked to think up a name for a very big number, namely 1 with one hundred zeroes after it. He was very certain that this number was not infinite, and therefore equally certain that it had to have a name. At the same time that he suggested "googol" he gave a name for a still larger number: "googolplex". A googolplex is much larger than a googol, but is still finite, as the inventor of the name was quick to point out. It was first suggested that a googolplex should be 1, followed by writing zeros until you got tired. This is a description of what would happen if one tried to write a googolplex, but different people get tired at different times and it would never do to have [Carnera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primo_Carnera) a better mathematician than [Dr. Einstein](wikipedia://en/736), simply because he had more endurance. The googolplex is, then, a specific finite number, equal to 1 with a googol zeros after it.

[John Horton Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) and [Richard K. Guy](wikipedia://en/816308)[^o27] have suggested that *N-plex* be used as a name for 10<sup>*N*</sup>. This gives rise to the name *googolplexplex* for 10<sup>googolplex</sup> = 10${}^{10^{10^{100}}}$, which is unofficially known as a *googolplexian* (or *googolduplex*) by some googologists. Conway and Guy[^o27] have proposed that *N-minex* be used as a name for 10<sup>−*N*</sup>, giving rise to the name *googolminex* for the [reciprocal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_%28mathematics%29) of a googolplex, which is written as 10${}^{-(10^{100})}$. None of these names are in wide use.

A googologist from Texas coined the terms giggol and giggolplex for tetrational numbers. Giggol is equal to ${10 \uparrow \uparrow 100}$, or 10 [tetrated](wikipedia://en/482379) to 100 and Giggolplex is 10 tetrated to giggol or $10\uparrow\uparrow (10\uparrow\uparrow 100)$. If they are written in [exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491), then they would be power towers of 100 and "a power tower of 100 tens" tens, respectively. They were coined by altering the vowel sound of "googol" and a different definition of "-plex": If $N = f(10, 10)$ or $f(10, 100)$, where $f$ is any googolocial function, then $N$-plex $= f(10, N)$[^o14]

The names *googol* and *googolplex* inspired the name of the [Internet company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot-com_company) [Google](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google) and its [corporate headquarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_headquarters), the [Googleplex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Googleplex), respectively.[^o42]

## Extensions of the standard dictionary numbers {#Extensions_of_the_standard_dictionary_numbers}

This section illustrates several systems for naming large numbers, and shows how they can be extended past *vigintillion*.

Traditional British usage assigned new names for each power of one million (the [long scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_and_short_scale)): 1,000,000 = 1 million; 1,000,000<sup>2</sup> = 1 billion; 1,000,000<sup>3</sup> = 1 trillion; and so on. It was adapted from French usage, and is similar to the system that was documented or invented by [Chuquet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Chuquet).

Traditional American usage (which was also adapted from French usage but at a later date), Canadian, and modern British usage assign new names for each power of one thousand (the [short scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_and_short_scale)). Thus, a *billion* is 1000 × 1000<sup>2</sup> = 10<sup>9</sup>; a *trillion* is 1000 × 1000<sup>3</sup> = 10<sup>12</sup>; and so forth. Due to its dominance in the financial world (along with the [US dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_dollar)), this was adopted for official [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations) documents.

Traditional French usage has varied; in 1948, France, which had originally popularized the short scale worldwide, reverted to the long scale.

The term *milliard* is unambiguous and always means 10<sup>9</sup>. It is seldom seen in American usage and rarely in British usage, but frequently in continental European usage. The term is sometimes attributed to French mathematician [Jacques Peletier du Mans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Peletier_du_Mans) c. 1550 (for this reason, the long scale is also known as the *Chuquet-Peletier* system), but the [Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary) states that the term derives from post-Classical [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) term *milliartum*, which became *milliare* and then *milliart* and finally our modern term.

Concerning names ending in -illiard for numbers 10<sup>6*n*+3</sup>, *milliard* is certainly in widespread use in languages other than English, but the degree of actual use of the larger terms is questionable. The terms "milliardo" in Italian, "Milliarde" in German, "miljard" in Dutch, "milyar" in Turkish, and "миллиард", *milliard* (transliterated) in Russian, are standard usage when discussing financial topics.

The naming procedure for large numbers is based on taking the number *n* occurring in 10<sup>3*n*+3</sup> (short scale) or 10<sup>6*n*</sup> (long scale) and concatenating Latin roots for its units, tens, and hundreds place, together with the suffix *\-illion*. If the final root is multisyllabic and ends in a vowel, that vowel is removed; for example, centi + illion = centillion, not centiillion. Monosyllabic final roots ending in vowels only occur when the naming system does not apply, for very small numbers (million, billion, and sextillion), so they do not have defined behavior. The number "0" is skipped, i.e. it produces the empty string - 103 results in the root for units 3 followed by the root for hundreds 1. In this way, numbers up to 10<sup>3·999+3</sup> = 10<sup>3000</sup> (short scale) or 10<sup>6·999</sup> = 10<sup>5994</sup> (long scale) may be named. The choice of roots and the concatenation procedure is that of the standard dictionary numbers if *n* is 9 or smaller. For larger *n* (between 10 and 999), prefixes can be constructed based on a system described by Conway and Guy.[^o27] Today, sexdecillion and novemdecillion are standard dictionary numbers and, using the same reasoning as Conway and Guy did for the numbers up to nonillion, could probably be used to form acceptable prefixes. The Conway–Guy system disagrees with some standard dictionary names, like "quindecillion", "sexdecillion", and "novemdecillion". Oliver Miakinen argued that since "quindecillion" is a widely accepted term, and the Latin for 15 is actually *quindecim* and not *quinquadecim*, the prefix "quinqua-" should be replaced with "quin-". This new prefix is more commonly used nowadays.[^o18]

Since the system of using Latin prefixes will become ambiguous for numbers with exponents of a size which the Romans rarely counted to, like 10<sup>6,000,258</sup>, Conway and Guy co-devised with Allan Wechsler the following set of consistent conventions that permit, in principle, the extension of this system indefinitely to provide English short-scale names for any integer whatsoever.[^o27] The name of a number 10<sup>3*n*+3</sup>, where *n* is greater than or equal to 1000, is formed by concatenating the names of the numbers of the form 10<sup>3*m*+3</sup>, where *m* represents each group of comma-separated digits of *n*, with each but the last "-illion" trimmed to "-illi-", or, in the case of *m* = 0, either "-nilli-" or "-nillion".[^o27] For example, 10<sup>3,000,012</sup>, the 1,000,003rd "-illion" number, equals one "millinillitrillion"; 10<sup>33,002,010,111</sup>, the 11,000,670,036th "-illion" number, equals one "undecillinilliseptuagintasescentillisestrigintillion"; and 10<sup>29,629,629,633</sup>, the 9,876,543,210th "-illion" number, equals one "nonilliseseptuagintaoctingentillitresquadragintaquingentillideciducentillion".[^o27]

## Other named large numbers used in mathematics, physics and chemistry {#Other_named_large_numbers_used_in_mathematics,_physics_and_chemistry}

- [Avogadro number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avogadro_number) – Number of particles in one mole, \6.02214076 × 10<sup>23</sup>
- [Eddington number](wikipedia://en/461770) – Number of protons in the observable universe, about \1.57 × 10<sup>79</sup>
- [Shannon number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_number) – Lower bound on the game-tree complexity of chess, about 10<sup>120</sup>
- [Skewes's number](wikipedia://en/250178) – Large upper bound related to the prime-counting function, about 33.94705 or exactly e<sup>e<sup>e<sup>79</sup></sup></sup>
- [Moser's number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moser's_number)
- [Graham's number](wikipedia://en/234232) – Upper bound on the answer to a problem in Ramsey theory
- [TREE(3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TREE%283%29)
- [SSCG(3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SSCG%283%29)
- [Rayo's number](wikipedia://en/42297252) – Claimed to be the largest named number
