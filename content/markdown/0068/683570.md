# Total variation distance of probability measures

*Image: Total variation distance.svg.* Total variation distance is half the absolute area between the two curves: Half the shaded area above.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Total variation distance of probability measures"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), the **total variation distance** is a [statistical distance](wikipedia://en/5187620) between [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543), and is sometimes called the **statistical distance**, **statistical difference** or **variational distance**.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Consider a [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333) $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$ and [probability measures](wikipedia://en/45437) $P$ and $Q$ defined on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$. The total variation distance between $P$ and $Q$ is defined as[^o4]

$$\delta(P,Q)=\sup_{ A\in \mathcal{F}}\left|P(A)-Q(A)\right|.$$

This is the largest absolute difference between the probabilities that the two [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) assign to the same event.

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The total variation distance is an [*f*-divergence](wikipedia://en/15224289) and an [integral probability metric](wikipedia://en/73598108).

### Relation to other distances {#Relation_to_other_distances}

The total variation distance is related to the [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052) by [Pinsker’s inequality](wikipedia://en/20064401):

$$\delta(P,Q) \le \sqrt{\frac{1}{2} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q)}.$$

One also has the following inequality, due to [Bretagnolle and Huber](wikipedia://en/71550825)[^o8] (see also [^o9]), which has the advantage of providing a non-vacuous bound even when $\textstyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q)>2\colon$

$$\delta(P,Q) \le \sqrt{1-e^{ -D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q) }}.$$

The total variation distance is half of the [L<sup>1</sup> distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L1_norm) between the probability functions: on discrete domains, this is the distance between the [probability mass functions](wikipedia://en/154725)[^o10]

$$\delta(P, Q) = \frac12 \sum_{x} |P(x) - Q(x)|,$$

and when the distributions have standard [probability density functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_density_functions) $p$ and $q$,[^o11]

$$\delta(P, Q) = \frac12 \int | p(x) - q(x) | \, \mathrm{d}x$$

(or the analogous distance between [Radon-Nikodym derivatives](wikipedia://en/338746#Radon–Nikodym_derivative) with any common [dominating measure](wikipedia://en/252670#Absolute_continuity_of_measures)). This result can be shown by noticing that the supremum in the definition is achieved exactly at the set where one distribution dominates the other.[^o12]

The total variation distance is related to the [Hellinger distance](wikipedia://en/13035709) $H(P,Q)$ as follows:[^o13]

$$H^2(P,Q) \leq \delta(P,Q) \leq \sqrt 2 H(P,Q).$$

These inequalities follow immediately from the inequalities between the [1-norm](wikipedia://en/45194#The_p-norm_in_finite_dimensions) and the [2-norm](wikipedia://en/45194#The_p-norm_in_finite_dimensions).

### Connection to transportation theory {#Connection_to_transportation_theory}

The total variation distance (or half the norm) arises as the optimal transportation cost, when the cost function is $c(x,y) = {\mathbf{1}}_{x \neq y}$, that is,

$$\frac{1}{2} \| P - Q \|_1 = \delta(P,Q) = \inf\big\{ \mathbb{P}(X\neq Y ) : \text{Law}(X) = P , \text{Law}(Y) = Q\big\} = \inf_\pi \operatorname{E}_{\pi}[{\mathbf{1}}_{x\neq y}],$$

where the expectation is taken with respect to the probability measure $\pi$ on the space where $(x,y)$ lives, and the infimum is taken over all such $\pi$ with marginals $P$ and $Q$, respectively.[^o15]
