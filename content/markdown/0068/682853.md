# Sorgenfrey plane

*Image: Sorgenfrey plane.png.* An illustration of the anti-diagonal and an open rectangle in the Sorgenfrey plane that meets the anti-diagonal at a single point.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sorgenfrey plane"}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), the **Sorgenfrey plane** is a frequently-cited [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) to many otherwise plausible-sounding conjectures. It consists of the [product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_space) of two copies of the [Sorgenfrey line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorgenfrey_line), which is the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) $\mathbb{R}$ under the [half-open interval topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-open_interval_topology). The Sorgenfrey line and plane are named for the American mathematician [Robert Sorgenfrey](wikipedia://en/24960468).

:::

A [basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_%28topology%29) for the Sorgenfrey plane, denoted $\mathbb{S}$ from now on, is therefore the set of [rectangles](wikipedia://en/70048) that include the west edge, southwest corner, and south edge, and omit the southeast corner, east edge, northeast corner, north edge, and northwest corner. [Open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) in $\mathbb{S}$ are unions of such rectangles.

$\mathbb{S}$ is an example of a space that is a product of [Lindelöf spaces](wikipedia://en/310480) that is not itself a Lindelöf space. The so-called **anti-diagonal** $\Delta = \{(x, -x) \mid x \in \mathbb{R}\}$ is an [uncountable](wikipedia://en/32248) [discrete](wikipedia://en/56061) subset of this space, and this is a non-[separable](wikipedia://en/27855) subset of the [separable space](wikipedia://en/27855) $\mathbb{S}$. It shows that separability does not inherit to closed [subspaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_subspace). Note that $K = \{(x, -x) \mid x \in \mathbb{Q}\}$ and $\Delta \setminus K$ are closed sets; it can be proved that they cannot be separated by open sets, showing that $\mathbb{S}$ is not [normal](wikipedia://en/48629). Thus it serves as a counterexample to the notion that the product of normal spaces is normal; in fact, it shows that even the finite product of [perfectly normal spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfectly_normal_space) need not be normal.
