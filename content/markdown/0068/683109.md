# Triality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Triality"}

*Image: Dynkin diagram D4.png.* The automorphisms of the Dynkin diagram D<sub>4</sub> give rise to triality in Spin(8).

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **triality** is a relationship among three [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370), analogous to the [duality](wikipedia://en/609737) relation between [dual vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_vector_space). Most commonly, it describes those special features of the [Dynkin diagram](wikipedia://en/277109) D<sub>4</sub> and the associated [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) [Spin(8)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin%288%29), the [double cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_covering_group) of 8-dimensional rotation group [SO(8)](wikipedia://en/683116), arising because the group has an [outer automorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_automorphism) of order three. There is a geometrical version of triality, analogous to [duality in projective geometry](wikipedia://en/539925).

:::

Of all [simple Lie groups](wikipedia://en/292831), Spin(8) has the most symmetrical Dynkin diagram, D<sub>4</sub>. The diagram has four nodes with one node located at the center, and the other three attached symmetrically. The [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) of the diagram is the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) *S*<sub>3</sub> which acts by permuting the three legs. This gives rise to an *S*<sub>3</sub> group of outer automorphisms of Spin(8). This [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) permutes the three 8-dimensional [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982) of Spin(8); these being the *vector* representation and two [chiral](wikipedia://en/1170160) *spin* representations. These automorphisms do not project to automorphisms of SO(8). The vector representation—the natural action of SO(8) (hence Spin(8)) on $F^{8}$—consists over the real numbers of [Euclidean 8-vectors](wikipedia://en/9697) and is generally known as the "defining module", while the chiral spin representations are also known as ["half-spin representations"](wikipedia://en/29276), and all three of these are [fundamental representations](wikipedia://en/649743).

No other connected Dynkin diagram has an automorphism group of order greater than 2; for other D<sub>*n*</sub> (corresponding to other even Spin groups, Spin(2*n*)), there is still the automorphism corresponding to switching the two half-spin representations, but these are not isomorphic to the vector representation.

Roughly speaking, symmetries of the Dynkin diagram lead to automorphisms of the [Tits building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tits_building) associated with the group. For [special linear groups](wikipedia://en/144823), one obtains projective duality. For Spin(8), one finds a curious phenomenon involving 1-, 2-, and 4-dimensional subspaces of 8-dimensional space, historically known as "geometric triality".

The exceptional 3-fold symmetry of the D<sub>4</sub> diagram also gives rise to the [Steinberg group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steinberg_group_%28Lie_theory%29) [<sup>3</sup>D<sub>4</sub>](wikipedia://en/36177249).

## General formulation {#General_formulation}

A duality between two vector spaces over a field $F$ is a non-degenerate [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477)

$$V_1\times V_2\to F,$$

i.e., for each non-zero vector $v$ in one of the two vector spaces, the pairing with $v$ is a non-zero [linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) on the other.

Similarly, a triality between three vector spaces over a field $F$ is a non-degenerate [trilinear form](wikipedia://en/832212)

$$V_1\times V_2\times V_3\to F,$$

i.e., each non-zero vector in one of the three vector spaces induces a duality between the other two.

By choosing vectors $e_{i}$ in each $V_{i}$ on which the trilinear form evaluates to 1, we find that the three vector spaces are all [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to each other, and to their duals. Denoting this common vector space by $V$, the triality may be re-expressed as a [bilinear multiplication](wikipedia://en/191788)

$$V \times V \to V$$

where each $e_{i}$ corresponds to the identity element in $V$. The non-degeneracy condition now implies that $V$ is a [composition algebra](wikipedia://en/3861635). It follows that $V$ has dimension 1, 2, 4 or 8. If further $F = \mathbf{R}$ and the form used to identify $V$ with its dual is [positive definite](wikipedia://en/20437320), then $V$ is a [Euclidean Hurwitz algebra](wikipedia://en/25167317), and is therefore isomorphic to [**R**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers), [**C**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers), [**H**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternions) or [**O**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octonions).

Conversely, composition algebras immediately give rise to trialities by taking each $V_{i}$ equal to the algebra, and [contracting](wikipedia://en/202722) the multiplication with the inner product on the algebra to make a trilinear form.

An alternative construction of trialities uses spinors in dimensions 1, 2, 4 and 8. The eight-dimensional case corresponds to the triality property of Spin(8).
