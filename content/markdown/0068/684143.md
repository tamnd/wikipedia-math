# Wedge sum

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Wedge sum"}

*Image: Marked 2-rose.svg.* A wedge sum of two circles

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), the **wedge sum** is a "one-point union" of a family of [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450). Specifically, if *X* and *Y* are [pointed spaces](wikipedia://en/1236666) (i.e. topological spaces with distinguished basepoints $x_0$ and $y_0$) the wedge sum of *X* and *Y* is the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) of the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/1564380) of *X* and *Y* by the identification $x_0 \sim y_0:$

$$X \vee Y = (X \sqcup Y)\;/{\sim},$$

:::

where $\,\sim\,$ is the [equivalence closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalence_closure) of the relation $\left\{ \left(x_0, y_0\right) \right\}.$ More generally, suppose $\left(X_i\right)_{i \in I}$ is an [indexed family](wikipedia://en/23624339) of pointed spaces with basepoints $\left(p_i\right)_{i \in I}.$ The wedge sum of the family is given by:

$$\bigvee_{i \in I} X_i = \bigsqcup_{i \in I} X_i\;/{\sim},$$

where $\,\sim\,$ is the equivalence closure of the relation $\left\{ \left(p_i, p_j\right) : i, j \in I\right\}.$ In other words, the wedge sum is the joining of several spaces at a single point. This definition is sensitive to the choice of the basepoints $\left(p_i\right)_{i \in I},$ unless the spaces $\left(X_i\right)_{i \in I}$ are [homogeneous](wikipedia://en/363325).

The wedge sum is again a pointed space, and the binary operation is [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) and [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) (up to homeomorphism).

Sometimes the wedge sum is called the **wedge product**, but this is not the same concept as the [exterior product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_product), which is also often called the wedge product.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The wedge sum of two circles is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to a [figure-eight space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figure-eight_space). The wedge sum of $n$ circles is often called a *[bouquet of circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouquet_of_circles)*, while a wedge product of arbitrary spheres is often called a **bouquet of spheres**.

A common construction in [homotopy](wikipedia://en/212250) is to identify all of the points along the equator of an $n$-sphere $S^n$. Doing so results in two copies of the sphere, joined at the point that was the equator:

$$S^n/{\sim} = S^n \vee S^n.$$

Let $\Psi$ be the map $\Psi : S^n \to S^n \vee S^n,$ that is, of identifying the equator down to a single point. Then addition of two elements $f, g \in \pi_n(X,x_0)$ of the $n$-dimensional [homotopy group](wikipedia://en/297506) $\pi_n(X,x_0)$ of a space $X$ at the distinguished point $x_0 \in X$ can be understood as the composition of $f$ and $g$ with $\Psi$:

$$f + g = (f \vee g) \circ \Psi.$$

Here, $f, g : S^n \to X$ are maps which take a distinguished point $s_0 \in S^n$ to the point $x_0 \in X.$ Note that the above uses the wedge sum of two functions, which is possible precisely because they agree at $s_0,$ the point common to the wedge sum of the underlying spaces.

:::

## Categorical description {#Categorical_description}

The wedge sum can be understood as the [coproduct](wikipedia://en/310889) in the [category of pointed spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_pointed_spaces). Alternatively, the wedge sum can be seen as the [pushout](wikipedia://en/582024) of the diagram $X \leftarrow \{ \bullet \} \to Y$ in the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051) (where $\{ \bullet \}$ is any one-point space).

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

[Van Kampen's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Kampen's_theorem) gives certain conditions (which are usually fulfilled for [well-behaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-behaved) spaces, such as [CW complexes](wikipedia://en/353022)) under which the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of the wedge sum of two spaces $X$ and $Y$ is the [free product](wikipedia://en/360036) of the fundamental groups of $X$ and $Y.$
