# Dandelin spheres

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dandelin spheres"}

*Image: Dandelin spheres.svg.* Two Dandelin spheres touch the pale yellow plane that intersects the cone. The points of tangency F<sub>1</sub>, F<sub>2</sub> are the foci of the blue ellipse. The spheres are also tangent to the cone at circles k<sub>1</sub>, k<sub>2</sub>. For a point P on the ellipse, the tangent segments PF<sub>1</sub> and PF<sub>2</sub> can each be reflected to other tangents of equal length, PF<sub>1</sub> = PP<sub>1</sub> and PF<sub>2</sub> = PP<sub>2</sub>, with PP<sub>1</sub> and PP<sub>2</sub> colinear along the ray SP. Their combined length P<sub>1</sub>P + PP<sub>2</sub> = P<sub>1</sub>P<sub>2</sub> = L is the distance between circles k<sub>1</sub> and k<sub>2</sub>, and is independent of the choice of P; thus any point on the ellipse has PF<sub>1</sub> + PF<sub>2</sub> = L.

*Image: Cone, Dandelin circles and ellipse.svg.* This construction shows how the focal points of an ellipse can be found using the Dandelin spheres. The [angle bisector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_bisector) between the line representing the plane and a line representing the cone surface leads to the center of the respective sphere.

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **Dandelin spheres** are one or two [spheres](wikipedia://en/27859) that are [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) both to a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) and to a [cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_%28geometry%29) that intersects the plane. The intersection of the cone and the plane is a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673), and the point at which either sphere touches the plane is a [focus](wikipedia://en/1032607) of the conic section, so the Dandelin spheres are also sometimes called **focal spheres**.[^o2]

:::

The Dandelin spheres were discovered in 1822.[^o2][^o3] They are named in honor of the [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France) mathematician [Germinal Pierre Dandelin](wikipedia://en/685727), though [Adolphe Quetelet](wikipedia://en/184215) is sometimes given partial credit as well.[^o4][^o5][^o6]

The Dandelin spheres can be used to give elegant modern proofs of two [classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) theorems known to [Apollonius](wikipedia://en/257242). The first theorem is that a closed conic section (i.e. an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277)) is the [locus](wikipedia://en/542465) of points such that the sum of the distances to two fixed points (the foci) is constant. The second theorem is that for any conic section, the distance from a fixed point (the focus) is proportional to the distance from a fixed line (the [directrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directrix_%28conic_section%29)), the constant of proportionality being called the [eccentricity](wikipedia://en/1239472).[^o12]

A conic section has one Dandelin sphere for each focus. An ellipse has two Dandelin spheres touching the same [nappe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nappe_%28geometry%29) of the cone, while [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) has two Dandelin spheres touching opposite nappes. A [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) has just one Dandelin sphere.

## Proof that the intersection curve has constant sum of distances to foci {#Proof_that_the_intersection_curve_has_constant_sum_of_distances_to_foci}

Consider the illustration, depicting a cone with apex *S* at the top. A plane *e* intersects the cone in a curve *C* (with blue interior). The following proof shall show that the curve *C* is an ellipse.

The two brown Dandelin spheres, *G*<sub>1</sub> and *G*<sub>2</sub>, are placed tangent to both the plane and the cone: *G*<sub>1</sub> above the plane, *G*<sub>2</sub> below. Each sphere touches the cone along a circle (colored white), $k_1$ and $k_2$.

Denote the point of tangency of the plane with *G*<sub>1</sub> by *F*<sub>1</sub>, and similarly for *G*<sub>2</sub> and *F*<sub>2</sub> . Let *P* be a typical point on the curve *C*.

*To Prove:* The sum of distances $d(P,F_1) + d(P,F_2)$ remains constant as the point *P* moves along the intersection curve *C*. (This is one definition of *C* being an ellipse, with $F_1$ and $F_2$ being its foci.)

- A line passing through *P* and the [vertex](wikipedia://en/6022079) *S* of the cone intersects the two circles, touching *G*<sub>1</sub> and *G*<sub>2</sub> respectively at points *P*<sub>1</sub> and *P*<sub>2</sub>.
- As *P* moves around the curve, *P*<sub>1</sub> and *P*<sub>2</sub> move along the two circles, and their distance *d*(*P*<sub>1</sub>, *P*<sub>2</sub>) remains constant.
- The distance from *P* to *F*<sub>1</sub> is the same as the distance from *P* to *P*<sub>1</sub>, because the line segments *PF*<sub>1</sub> and *PP*<sub>1</sub> are both [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to the same sphere *G*<sub>1</sub>.
- By a symmetrical argument, the distance from *P* to *F*<sub>2</sub> is the same as the distance from *P* to *P*<sub>2</sub>.
- Consequently, we compute the sum of distances as $d(P,F_1) + d(P,F_2) \ =\ d(P,P_1) + d(P,P_2) \ =\ d(P_1,P_2),$ which is constant as *P* moves along the curve.

This gives a different proof of a theorem of [Apollonius of Perga](wikipedia://en/257242).[^o12]

If we define an ellipse to mean the locus of points *P* such that *d*(*F*<sub>1</sub>, *P*) + *d*(*F*<sub>2</sub>, *P*) = a constant, then the above argument proves that the intersection curve *C* is indeed an ellipse. That the intersection of the plane with the cone is symmetric about the perpendicular bisector of the line through *F*<sub>1</sub> and *F*<sub>2</sub> may be counterintuitive, but this argument makes it clear.

*Image: Zylinder-dandelin.svg.* Cylinder case

Adaptations of this argument work for [hyperbolas](wikipedia://en/14052#As_plane_section_of_a_cone) and [parabolas](wikipedia://en/23231#Alternative_proof_with_Dandelin_spheres) as intersections of a plane with a cone. Another adaptation works for an ellipse realized as the intersection of a plane with a right circular [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29).

:::

## Proof of the focus-directrix property {#Proof_of_the_focus-directrix_property}

The directrix of a conic section can be found using Dandelin's construction. Each Dandelin sphere intersects the cone at a circle; let both of these circles define their own planes. The intersections of these two parallel planes with the conic section's plane will be two parallel lines; these lines are the directrices of the conic section. However, a parabola has only one Dandelin sphere, and thus has only one directrix.

Using the Dandelin spheres, it's possible to conduct [a proof for the parabola](wikipedia://en/23231#Alternative_proof_with_Dandelin_spheres), that it is the locus of points for which the distance from a point (focus) is the same as the distance from the directrix, as well as a more general proof that any conic section is the locus of points where the distances maintain a constant proportion.[^o11] Ancient Greek mathematicians such as [Pappus of Alexandria](wikipedia://en/509300) were aware of this property, but the Dandelin spheres facilitate the proof.[^o12]

Neither Dandelin nor Quetelet used the Dandelin spheres to prove the focus-directrix property. The first to do so may have been Pierce Morton in 1829,[^o13] or perhaps [Hugh Hamilton](wikipedia://en/32401936) who remarked (in 1758) that a sphere touches the cone at a circle which defines a plane whose intersection with the plane of the conic section is a directrix.[^o2][^o14][^o15][^o16] The focus-directrix property can be used to prove that [astronomical objects move along conic sections](wikipedia://en/17553) around the Sun.[^o17]
