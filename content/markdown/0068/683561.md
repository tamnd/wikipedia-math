# Total variation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Total variation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **total variation** identifies several slightly different concepts, related to the ([local](wikipedia://en/1462457) or global) structure of the [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) or a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873). For a [real-valued](wikipedia://en/20646438) [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) *f*, defined on an [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) \[*a*, *b*\] ⊂ **R**, its total variation on the interval of definition is a measure of the one-dimensional [arclength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arclength) of the curve with [parametric equation](wikipedia://en/576108) *x* ↦ *f*(*x*), for *x* ∈ \[*a*, *b*\]. Functions whose total variation is finite are called *[functions of bounded variation](wikipedia://en/330673)*.

:::

## Historical note {#Historical_note}

The concept of total variation for functions of one real variable was first introduced by [Camille Jordan](wikipedia://en/355931) in the paper (Jordan 1881).[^o4] He used the new concept in order to prove a convergence theorem for [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) of [discontinuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discontinuous_function) [periodic functions](wikipedia://en/76848) whose variation is [bounded](wikipedia://en/330673). The extension of the concept to functions of more than one variable however is not simple for various reasons.

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

### Total variation for functions of one real variable {#Total_variation_for_functions_of_one_real_variable}

(Definition 1.1.) The **total variation** of a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued (or more generally [complex](wikipedia://en/5826)-valued) [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f$, defined on an [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $[a , b] \subset \mathbb{R}$ is the quantity

$$V_a^b(f)=\sup_{\mathcal{P}} \sum_{i=0}^{n_P-1} | f(x_{i+1})-f(x_i) |,$$

where the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) runs over the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all [partitions](wikipedia://en/471332) $\mathcal{P} = \left\{P=\{ x_0, \dots , x_{n_P}\} \mid P\text{ is a partition of } [a,b] \right\}$ of the given [interval](wikipedia://en/49172). Which means that $a = x_{0} < x_{1} < ... < x_{n_{P}} = b$.

### Total variation for functions of *n* > 1 real variables {#Total_variation_for_functions_of_n_>_1_real_variables}

(Definition 1.2.)[^o9] Let **Ω** be an [open subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) of **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. Given a function *f* belonging to *L*<sup>1</sup>(**Ω**), the **total variation** of *f* in **Ω** is defined as

$$V(f,\Omega):=\sup\left\{\int_\Omega f(x) \operatorname{div} \phi(x) \, \mathrm{d}x \colon \phi\in C_c^1(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^n),\ \Vert \phi\Vert_{L^\infty(\Omega)}\le 1\right\},$$

where

- $C_c^1(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^n)$ is the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of [continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) [vector functions](wikipedia://en/3675281) of [compact support](wikipedia://en/381013#Compact_support) contained in $\Omega$,
- $\Vert\;\Vert_{L^\infty(\Omega)}$ is the [essential supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essential_supremum) [norm](wikipedia://en/990534), and
- $\operatorname{div}$ is the [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) operator.

This definition *does not require* that the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ of the given function be a [bounded set](wikipedia://en/48258).

### Total variation in measure theory {#Total_variation_in_measure_theory}

#### Classical total variation definition {#Classical_total_variation_definition}

Following (Saks 1937, 10), consider a [signed measure](wikipedia://en/2985811) $\mu$ on a [measurable space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-algebra) $(X,\Sigma)$: then it is possible to define two [set functions](wikipedia://en/25911217) $\overline{\mathrm{W}}(\mu,\cdot)$ and $\underline{\mathrm{W}}(\mu,\cdot)$, respectively called **upper variation** and **lower variation**, as follows

$$\overline{\mathrm{W}}(\mu,E)=\sup\left\{\mu(A)\mid A\in\Sigma\text{ and }A\subset E \right\}\qquad\forall E\in\Sigma$$

$$\underline{\mathrm{W}}(\mu,E)=\inf\left\{\mu(A)\mid A\in\Sigma\text{ and }A\subset E \right\}\qquad\forall E\in\Sigma$$

clearly

$$\overline{\mathrm{W}}(\mu,E)\geq 0 \geq \underline{\mathrm{W}}(\mu,E)\qquad\forall E\in\Sigma$$

(Definition 1.3.) The **variation** (also called **absolute variation**) of the signed measure $\mu$ is the set function

$$|\mu|(E)=\overline{\mathrm{W}}(\mu,E)+\left|\underline{\mathrm{W}}(\mu,E)\right|\qquad\forall E\in\Sigma$$

and its **total variation** is defined as the value of this measure on the whole space of definition, i.e.

$$\|\mu\|=|\mu|(X)$$

#### Modern definition of total variation norm {#Modern_definition_of_total_variation_norm}

(Saks 1937, 11) uses upper and lower variations to prove the [Hahn–Jordan decomposition](wikipedia://en/2985858): according to his version of this theorem, the upper and lower variation are respectively a [non-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative) and a [non-positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-positive) [measure](wikipedia://en/19873). Using a more modern notation, define

$$\mu^+(\cdot)=\overline{\mathrm{W}}(\mu,\cdot)\,,$$

$$\mu^-(\cdot)=-\underline{\mathrm{W}}(\mu,\cdot)\,,$$

Then $\mu^+$ and $\mu^-$ are two non-negative [measures](wikipedia://en/19873) such that

$$\mu=\mu^+-\mu^-$$

$$|\mu|=\mu^++\mu^-$$

The last measure is sometimes called, by [abuse of notation](wikipedia://en/1698066), **total variation measure**.

#### Total variation norm of complex measures {#Total_variation_norm_of_complex_measures}

If the measure $\mu$ is [complex-valued](wikipedia://en/5826) i.e. is a [complex measure](wikipedia://en/3057532), its upper and lower variation cannot be defined and the Hahn–Jordan decomposition theorem can only be applied to its real and imaginary parts. However, it is possible to follow (Rudin 1966, 137–139) and define the total variation of the complex-valued measure $\mu$ as follows

(Definition 1.4.) The **variation** of the complex-valued measure $\mu$ is the [set function](wikipedia://en/25911217)

$$|\mu|(E)=\sup_\pi \sum_{A\in\pi} |\mu(A)|\qquad\forall E\in\Sigma$$

where the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) is taken over all partitions $\pi$ of a [measurable set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurable_set) $E$ into a countable number of disjoint measurable subsets.

This definition coincides with the above definition $|\mu|=\mu^++\mu^-$ for the case of real-valued signed measures.

#### Total variation norm of vector-valued measures {#Total_variation_norm_of_vector-valued_measures}

The variation so defined is a [positive measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_measure) (see (Rudin 1966, 139)) and coincides with the one defined by (1.3) when $\mu$ is a [signed measure](wikipedia://en/2985811): its total variation is defined as above. This definition works also if $\mu$ is a [vector measure](wikipedia://en/9399072): the variation is then defined by the following formula

$$|\mu|(E) = \sup_\pi \sum_{A\in\pi} \|\mu(A)\|\qquad\forall E\in\Sigma$$

where the supremum is as above. This definition is slightly more general than the one given by (Rudin 1966, 138) since it requires only to consider *finite partitions* of the space $X$: this implies that it can be used also to define the total variation on [finite-additive measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_additivity).

#### Total variation of probability measures {#Total_variation_of_probability_measures}

The total variation of any [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) is exactly one, therefore it is not interesting as a means of investigating the properties of such measures. However, when μ and ν are [probability measures](wikipedia://en/45437), the **[total variation distance of probability measures](wikipedia://en/683570)** can be defined as $\| \mu - \nu \|$ where the norm is the total variation norm of signed measures. Using the property that $(\mu-\nu)(X)=0$, we eventually arrive at the equivalent definition

$\|\mu-\nu\| = |\mu-\nu|(X)=2 \sup\left\{\,\left|\mu(A)-\nu(A)\right| : A\in \Sigma\,\right\}$[^o22]

and its values are non-trivial. The factor $2$ above is usually dropped (as is the convention in the article [total variation distance of probability measures](wikipedia://en/683570)). Informally, this is the largest possible difference between the probabilities that the two [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) can assign to the same event. For a [categorical distribution](wikipedia://en/10072717) it is possible to write the total variation distance as follows

$\delta(\mu,\nu) = \sum_x \left| \mu(x) - \nu(x) \right|\;.$[^o23]

It may also be normalized to values in $[0, 1]$ by halving the previous definition as follows

$\delta(\mu,\nu) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_x \left| \mu(x) - \nu(x) \right|.$[^o24]

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

### Total variation of differentiable functions {#Total_variation_of_differentiable_functions}

The total variation of a $C^1(\overline{\Omega})$ function $f$ can be expressed as an [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) involving the given function instead of as the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) of the [functionals](wikipedia://en/1374948) of definitions (1.1) and (1.2).

#### The form of the total variation of a differentiable function of one variable {#The_form_of_the_total_variation_of_a_differentiable_function_of_one_variable}

(Theorem 1.) The **total variation** of a [differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206) $f$, defined on an [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $[a , b] \subset \mathbb{R}$, has the following expression if $f'$ is Riemann integrable

$$V_a^b(f) = \int _a^b |f'(x)|\mathrm{d}x$$

If $f$ is differentiable and [monotonic](wikipedia://en/48260), then the above simplifies to

$$V_a^b(f) = |f(a) - f(b)|$$

For any differentiable function $f$, we can decompose the domain interval $[a,b]$, into subintervals $[a,a_1], [a_1,a_2], \dots, [a_N,b]$ (with $a<a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_N<b$) in which $f$ is locally monotonic, then the total variation of $f$ over $[a,b]$ can be written as the sum of local variations on those subintervals:

$$\begin{aligned} V_a^b(f) &= V_a^{a_1}(f) + V_{a_1}^{a_2}(f) + \, \cdots \, +V_{a_N}^b(f)\\[0.3em] &=|f(a)-f(a_1)|+|f(a_1)-f(a_2)|+ \,\cdots \, + |f(a_N)-f(b)| \end{aligned}$$

#### The form of the total variation of a differentiable function of several variables {#The_form_of_the_total_variation_of_a_differentiable_function_of_several_variables}

(Theorem 2.) Given a $C^1(\overline{\Omega})$ function $f$ defined on a [bounded](wikipedia://en/48258) [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, with $\partial \Omega$ of class $C^1$, the **total variation of $f$** has the following expression

$V(f,\Omega) = \int_\Omega \left|\nabla f(x) \right| \mathrm{d}x$ .

::: {#o31 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

##### Proof {#Proof}

The first step in the proof is to first prove an equality which follows from the [Gauss–Ostrogradsky theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss–Ostrogradsky_theorem).

:::

::: {#o33 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=named-heading name="Lemma"}

##### Lemma {#Lemma}

Under the conditions of the theorem, the following equality holds:

$$\int_\Omega f\operatorname{div}\varphi = -\int_\Omega\nabla f\cdot\varphi$$

::: {#o35 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of the lemma"}

###### Proof of the lemma {#Proof_of_the_lemma}

From the [Gauss–Ostrogradsky theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss–Ostrogradsky_theorem):

$$\int_\Omega \operatorname{div}\mathbf R = \int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf R\cdot \mathbf n$$

by substituting $\mathbf R:= f\mathbf\varphi$, we have:

$$\int_\Omega\operatorname{div}\left(f\mathbf\varphi\right) = \int_{\partial\Omega}\left(f\mathbf\varphi\right)\cdot\mathbf n$$

where $\mathbf\varphi$ is zero on the border of $\Omega$ by definition:

$$\int_\Omega\operatorname{div}\left(f\mathbf\varphi\right)=0$$

$$\int_\Omega \partial_{x_i} \left(f\mathbf\varphi_i\right)=0$$

$$\int_\Omega \mathbf\varphi_i\partial_{x_i} f + f\partial_{x_i}\mathbf\varphi_i=0$$

$$\int_\Omega f\partial_{x_i}\mathbf\varphi_i = - \int_\Omega \mathbf\varphi_i\partial_{x_i} f$$

$$\int_\Omega f\operatorname{div} \mathbf\varphi = - \int_\Omega \mathbf\varphi\cdot\nabla f$$

:::

:::

::: {#o37 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of the equality"}

##### Proof of the equality {#Proof_of_the_equality}

Under the conditions of the theorem, from the lemma we have:

$$\int_\Omega f\operatorname{div} \mathbf\varphi = - \int_\Omega \mathbf\varphi\cdot\nabla f \leq \left| \int_\Omega \mathbf\varphi\cdot\nabla f \right| \leq \int_\Omega \left|\mathbf\varphi\right|\cdot\left|\nabla f\right| \leq \int_\Omega \left|\nabla f\right|$$

in the last part $\mathbf\varphi$ could be omitted, because by definition its essential supremum is at most one.

On the other hand, we consider $\theta_N:=-\mathbb I_{\left[-N,N\right]}\mathbb I_{\{\nabla f\ne 0\}}\frac{\nabla f}{\left|\nabla f\right|}$ and $\theta^*_N$ which is the up to $\varepsilon$ approximation of $\theta_N$ in $C^1_c$ with the same integral. We can do this since $C^1_c$ is dense in $L^1$. Now again substituting into the lemma:

$$\begin{aligned} &\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_\Omega f\operatorname{div}\theta^*_N \\[4pt] &= \lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{\{\nabla f\ne 0\}}\mathbb I_{\left[-N,N\right]}\nabla f\cdot\frac{\nabla f}{\left|\nabla f\right|} \\[4pt] &= \lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{\left[-N,N\right]\cap{\{\nabla f\ne 0\}}} \nabla f\cdot\frac{\nabla f}{\left|\nabla f\right|} \\[4pt] &= \int_\Omega\left|\nabla f\right| \end{aligned}$$

This means we have a convergent sequence of $\int_\Omega f \operatorname{div} \mathbf\varphi$ that tends to $\int_\Omega\left|\nabla f\right|$ as well as we know that $\int_\Omega f\operatorname{div}\mathbf\varphi \leq \int_\Omega\left|\nabla f\right|$. [Q.E.D.](wikipedia://en/25228)

It can be seen from the proof that the supremum is attained when

$$\varphi\to \frac{-\nabla f}{\left|\nabla f\right|}.$$

The [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f$ is said to be of [bounded variation](wikipedia://en/330673) precisely if its total variation is finite.

:::

### Total variation of a measure {#Total_variation_of_a_measure}

The total variation is a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) defined on the space of measures of bounded variation. The space of measures on a σ-algebra of sets is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), called the [ca space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ca_space), relative to this norm. It is contained in the larger Banach space, called the [ba space](wikipedia://en/629005), consisting of *[finitely additive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_additive_measure)* (as opposed to countably additive) measures, also with the same norm. The [distance function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distance_function) associated to the norm gives rise to the total variation distance between two measures *μ* and *ν*.

For finite measures on **R**, the link between the total variation of a measure *μ* and the total variation of a function, as described above, goes as follows. Given *μ*, define a function $\varphi\colon \mathbb{R}\to \mathbb{R}$ by

$$\varphi(t) = \mu((-\infty,t])~.$$

Then, the total variation of the signed measure *μ* is equal to the total variation, in the above sense, of the function $\varphi$. In general, the total variation of a signed measure can be defined using [Jordan's decomposition theorem](wikipedia://en/2985858) by

$$\|\mu\|_{TV} = \mu_+(X) + \mu_-(X)~,$$

for any signed measure *μ* on a measurable space $(X,\Sigma)$.

## Applications {#Applications}

Total variation can be seen as a [non-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative) [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued [functional](wikipedia://en/1374948) defined on the space of [real-valued](wikipedia://en/20646438) [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) (for the case of functions of one variable) or on the space of [integrable functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_function) (for the case of functions of several variables). As a functional, total variation finds applications in several branches of mathematics and engineering, like [optimal control](wikipedia://en/362565), [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506), and [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882), where the solution to a certain problem has to [minimize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxima_and_minima) its value. As an example, use of the total variation functional is common in the following two kind of problems

- **Numerical analysis of differential equations**: it is the science of finding approximate solutions to [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309). Applications of total variation to these problems are detailed in the article "*[total variation diminishing](wikipedia://en/5677733)*"
- **Image denoising**:[^o41] in [image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processing), denoising is a collection of methods used to reduce the [noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_noise) in an [image](wikipedia://en/71925) reconstructed from data obtained by electronic means, for example [data transmission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_transmission) or [sensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensor). "*[Total variation denoising](wikipedia://en/26792343)*" is the name for the application of total variation to image noise reduction; further details can be found in the papers of (Rudin Osher Fatemi 1992) and (Caselles Chambolle Novaga 2007). A sensible extension of this model to colour images, called Colour TV, can be found in (Blomgren Chan 1998).
