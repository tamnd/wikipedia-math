# F-statistics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="F-statistics"}

In [population genetics](wikipedia://en/219268), ***F*-statistics** (also known as **fixation indices**) describe the statistically expected level of [heterozygosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zygosity) in a population; more specifically the expected degree of (usually) a reduction in heterozygosity when compared to [Hardy–Weinberg expectation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardy–Weinberg_law).

:::

*F*-statistics can also be thought of as a measure of the correlation between genes drawn at different levels of a (hierarchically) subdivided population. This correlation is influenced by several [evolutionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution) processes, such as [genetic drift](wikipedia://en/72016), [founder effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Founder_effect), [bottleneck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_bottleneck), [genetic hitchhiking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_hitchhiking), [meiotic drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiotic_drive), [mutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation), [gene flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_flow), [inbreeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inbreeding), [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection), or the [Wahlund effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wahlund_effect), but it was originally designed to measure the amount of allelic fixation owing to [genetic drift](wikipedia://en/72016).

The concept of *F*-statistics was developed during the 1920s by the American geneticist [Sewall Wright](wikipedia://en/1700593),[^o2][^o3] who was interested in inbreeding in [cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle). However, because [complete dominance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_dominance) causes the [phenotypes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenotype) of [homozygote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zygosity) dominants and heterozygotes to be the same, it was not until the advent of [molecular genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_genetics) from the 1960s onwards that heterozygosity in populations could be measured.

*F* can be used to define [effective population size](wikipedia://en/1543960).

## Definitions and equations {#Definitions_and_equations}

The measures F<sub>IS</sub>, [F<sub>ST</sub>](wikipedia://en/4196163), and F<sub>IT</sub> are related to the amounts of heterozygosity at various levels of population structure. Together, they are called *F*-statistics, and are derived from *F*, the [inbreeding coefficient](wikipedia://en/8543254). In a simple two-allele system with inbreeding, the genotypic frequencies are:

$$p^2(1-F) + pF\text{ for }\mathbf{AA};\ 2pq(1-F)\text{ for }\mathbf{Aa};\text{ and }q^2(1-F) + qF\text{ for }\mathbf{aa}.$$

The value for $F$ is found by solving the equation for $F$ using heterozygotes in the above inbred population. This becomes one minus the [observed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation) frequency of heterozygotes in a population divided by the [expected](wikipedia://en/9653) frequency of heterozygotes at [Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium](wikipedia://en/230319):

$$F = 1- \frac{\operatorname{O}(f(\mathbf{Aa}))} {\operatorname{E}(f(\mathbf{Aa}))} = 1- \frac{\operatorname{ObservedFrequency}(\mathbf{Aa})} {\operatorname{ExpectedFrequency}(\mathbf{Aa})}, \!$$

where the expected frequency at Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium is given by

$$\operatorname{E}(f(\mathbf{Aa})) = 2pq, \!$$

where $p$ and $q$ are the [allele frequencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allele_frequencies) of $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{a}$, respectively. It is also the probability that at any [locus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_locus), two alleles from a random individual of the population are [identical by descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_by_descent).

For example, consider the data from [E.B. Ford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E.B._Ford) (1971) on a single population of the [scarlet tiger moth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scarlet_tiger_moth):

*Table 1:*

| Genotype | White-spotted ($\mathbf{AA}$) | Intermediate ($\mathbf{Aa}$) | Little spotting ($\mathbf{aa}$) | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number | 1469 | 138 | 5 | 1612 |

From this, the [allele frequencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allele_frequencies) can be calculated, and the expectation of $f\left(\mathbf{Aa}\right)$ derived :

$$p = {2 \times \mathrm{obs}(AA) + \mathrm{obs}(Aa) \over 2 \times (\mathrm{obs}(AA) + \mathrm{obs}(Aa) + \mathrm{obs}(aa))} = 0.954$$

$$q = 1 - p = 0.046\,$$

$$F = 1- \frac{ \mathrm{obs}(Aa) / n } { 2pq } = 1- {138 / 1612 \over 2(0.954)(0.046)} = 0.023$$

The different F-statistics look at different levels of population structure. **F<sub>IT</sub>** is the inbreeding coefficient of an individual (**I**) relative to the total (**T**) population, as above; **F<sub>IS</sub>** is the inbreeding coefficient of an individual (**I**) relative to the subpopulation (**S**), using the above for subpopulations and averaging them; and **F<sub>ST</sub>** is the effect of subpopulations (**S**) compared to the total population (**T**), and is calculated by solving the equation:

$$(1-F_{IS})(1-F_{ST}) = 1-F_{IT}, \,$$

as shown in the next section.

## Partition due to population structure {#Partition_due_to_population_structure}

*Image: F-statistics.png.* $F_{IT}$ can be partitioned into $F_{ST}$ due to the [Wahlund effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wahlund_effect) and $F_{IS}$ due to [inbreeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inbreeding).

Consider a population that has a [population structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_stratification) of two levels; one from the individual (I) to the subpopulation (S) and one from the subpopulation to the total (T). Then the total $F$, known here as $F_{IT}$, can be [partitioned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_the_sum_of_squares) into $F_{IS}$ and $F_{ST}$:

$$1 - F_{IT} = (1 - F_{IS})\,(1 - F_{ST}). \!$$

This may be further partitioned for population substructure, and it expands according to the rules of [binomial expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_expansion), so that for *I* partitions:

$$1 - F = \prod_{i=0}^{i=I} (1 - F_{i,i+1}) \!$$

## Fixation index {#Fixation_index}

A reformulation of the definition of $F$ would be the ratio of the average number of differences between pairs of chromosomes sampled within diploid individuals with the average number obtained when sampling chromosomes randomly from the population (excluding the grouping per individual). One can modify this definition and consider a grouping per sub-population instead of per individual. Population geneticists have used that idea to measure the degree of structure in a population.

Unfortunately, there is a large number of definitions for [$F_{ST}$](wikipedia://en/4196163), causing some confusion in the scientific literature. A common definition is the following:

$$F_{ST} = \frac{\operatorname{var}(\mathbf{p})}{p\,(1 - p)} \!$$

where the variance of $\mathbf{p}$ is computed across sub-populations and $p\,(1 - p)$ is the expected frequency of heterozygotes.

### Fixation index in human populations {#Fixation_index_in_human_populations}

It is well established that the genetic diversity among human populations is low,[^o8] although the distribution of the genetic diversity was only roughly estimated. Early studies argued that 85–90% of the genetic variation is found within individuals residing in the same populations within continents (intra-continental populations) and only an additional 10–15% is found between populations of different continents (continental populations).[^o9][^o10][^o11][^o12][^o13] Later studies based on hundreds of thousands [single-nucleotide polymorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-nucleotide_polymorphism) (SNPs) suggested that the genetic diversity between continental populations is even smaller and accounts for 3 to 7%[^o14][^o15][^o16][^o17][^o18][^o19] A later study based on three million SNPs found that 12% of the genetic variation is found between continental populations and only 1% within them.[^o20] Most of these studies have used the [*F*<sub>*ST*</sub>](wikipedia://en/4196163) statistics [^o21] or closely related statistics.[^o22][^o23]
