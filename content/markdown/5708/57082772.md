# Martha B. Briggs

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Martha B. Briggs"}

**Martha Bailey Briggs** (March 31, 1838 – March 28, 1889) was an American educator who was born and educated in [New Bedford, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Bedford,_Massachusetts), where she taught formerly enslaved men and women to read and write.[^o3][^o4] In 1869, she moved to [Washington, D.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_D.C.), where she taught and served as a principal at Anthony Bowen Elementary School until 1873,[^o4][^o6] when she began work at [Howard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_University).[^o3] At Howard, she trained teachers and taught math.[^o3] In 1879, she was hired to serve as principal of the [Miner Normal School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miner_Normal_School).[^o3][^o4] She stepped down from Miner Normal in 1883 and returned to Howard, where she served as principal of the Howard Normal Department until her death in 1889.[^o4][^o6]

:::

## Early life and career {#Early_life_and_career}

Martha Bailey Briggs was born March 31, 1838, to John Briggs and Fannie Bassett Briggs, a black abolitionist family in [New Bedford, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Bedford,_Massachusetts).[^o15][^o6] Shortly after his emancipation, [Frederick Douglass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Douglass) worked with John Briggs in New Bedford.[^o7] She was the first African American to graduate from her high school.[^o8] She received teacher education at the [Bridgewater Normal School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridgewater_Normal_School).[^o6] Her first teaching experience was in her father's home, tutoring formerly enslaved men and women, many of whom had escaped enslavement through the [Underground Railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underground_Railroad). Her reputation as a teacher grew, and she was also hired to teach in small home-based schools in the region.[^o3] In addition to teaching at the private school she began, she taught in a private school in Christiantown, [Martha's Vineyard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martha's_Vineyard), and in public schools of Newport, Rhode Island.[^o6] In 1859, [Myrtilla Miner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myrtilla_Miner) of Washington, D.C., invited Briggs to come to her "School for Colored Girls," but Briggs declined.[^o6] Briggs briefly studied nursing at Boston Medical College but did not complete study. Briggs taught in [Easton, Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easton,_Maryland), from 1862 to 1869.[^o9]

## Career in Washington, DC {#Career_in_Washington,_DC}

In 1869, Briggs moved to Washington, D.C., where she quickly became both teacher and principal at the Anthony Bowen Elementary School, which permitted children of color to attend.[^o8][^o6] In 1873, Howard University hired her to teach in their mathematics and teacher preparation programs.[^o3][^o4] Following Myrtilla Miner's death, Briggs left Howard to serve as principal of the Miner Normal School (now known as [University of the District of Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_the_District_of_Columbia)) from 1879 until 1883.[^o11][^o6] Briggs was the first African American woman to serve as principal of [Miner Normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_School_for_Colored_Girls), and a Board of Education Report described her as: "a born teacher, and her work showed those qualities of head and heart that have made her name famous in the annals of education in the character of the graduates."[^o12][^o11] Briggs was a member of the [Monday Night Literary Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monday_Night_Literary_Club), a weekly gathering hosted by [Frederick Douglass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Douglass).

Briggs stepped down from her position with the Miner Normal School to work again for Howard University in 1883, a result of her poor health.[^o8][^o6] She remained principal of the university's normal department until her death in 1889.[^o4][^o13] A personnel roster for Howard lists her degree as [D.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Divinity)[^o13] She advocated for vocational as well as liberal arts education for people of color, and was elected president of the Industrial Institute Association of Washington, D.C., shortly before her death.[^o8]

## Legacy {#Legacy}

After her death on March 28, 1889,[^o15] a group of citizens, led by [Frederick Douglass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Douglass),[^o15] successfully petitioned that a DC school building be named the 'Martha B. Briggs Building' after her.[^o8][^o3] Two District of Columbia schools were named after her: Briggs Elementary School in 1889 and Briggs-Montgomery Elementary School; however, both schools were torn down for other construction projects.[^o6] A plaque was placed at the Howard University Chapel in her memory, with the inscription: "Her works do follow her."[^o8][^o15] In 1920, a group of educators in New Bedford created the Martha Briggs Educational Club and student aid fund in her honor.[^o15] In 1935, Briggs' former students gathered to honor her legacy as part of founders' day celebrations at Miner Teachers College.[^o16]
