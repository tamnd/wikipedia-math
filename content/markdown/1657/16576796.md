# Shape analysis (digital geometry)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Shape analysis"}

This article describes **shape analysis** to analyze and process geometric [shapes](wikipedia://en/169191).

:::

## Description {#Description}

*Shape analysis* is the (mostly) automatic analysis of geometric shapes, for example using a computer to detect similarly shaped objects in a database or parts that fit together. For a computer to automatically analyze and process geometric shapes, the objects have to be represented in a digital form. Most commonly a [boundary representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_representation) is used to describe the object with its boundary (usually the outer shell, see also [3D model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_model)). However, other volume based representations (e.g. [constructive solid geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_solid_geometry)) or point based representations ([point clouds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_cloud)) can be used to represent shape.

Once the objects are given, either by modeling ([computer-aided design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer-aided_design)), by scanning ([3D scanner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_scanner)) or by extracting shape from 2D or 3D images, they have to be simplified before a comparison can be achieved. The simplified representation is often called a *shape descriptor* (or fingerprint, signature). These simplified representations try to carry most of the important information, while being easier to handle, to store and to compare than the shapes directly. A *complete shape descriptor* is a representation that can be used to completely reconstruct the original object (for example the [medial axis](wikipedia://en/4537039) transform).

## Application fields {#Application_fields}

Shape analysis is used in many application fields:

- [archeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archeology) for example, to find similar objects or missing parts
- [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture) for example, to identify objects that spatially fit into a specific space
- [medical imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_imaging) to understand shape changes related to illness or aid [surgical planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surgical_planning)
- [virtual environments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_reality) or on the [3D model market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_models) to identify objects for copyright purposes
- security applications such as [face recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facial_recognition_system)
- entertainment industry (movies, games) to construct and process geometric models or animations
- [computer-aided design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer-aided_design) and [computer-aided manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer-aided_manufacturing) to process and to compare designs of mechanical parts or design objects.

## Shape descriptors {#Shape_descriptors}

Shape descriptors can be classified by their invariance with respect to the transformations allowed in the associated shape definition. Many descriptors are invariant with respect to *congruency*, meaning that congruent shapes (shapes that could be translated, rotated and mirrored) will have the same descriptor (for example [moment](wikipedia://en/368684) or [spherical harmonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_harmonic) based descriptors or [Procrustes analysis](wikipedia://en/3680074) operating on point clouds).

Another class of shape descriptors (called *intrinsic* shape descriptors) is invariant with respect to [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777). These descriptors do not change with different isometric embeddings of the shape. Their advantage is that they can be applied nicely to deformable objects (e.g. a person in different body postures) as these deformations do not involve much stretching but are in fact near-isometric. Such descriptors are commonly based on geodesic distance measures along the surface of an object or on other isometry invariant characteristics such as the [Laplace–Beltrami](wikipedia://en/1763356) [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137) (see also [spectral shape analysis](wikipedia://en/34878672)).

There are other shape descriptors, such as *graph-based* descriptors like the [medial axis](wikipedia://en/4537039) or the [Reeb graph](wikipedia://en/24126396) that capture geometric and/or topological information and simplify the shape representation but can not be as easily compared as descriptors that represent shape as a vector of numbers.

From this discussion it becomes clear, that different shape descriptors target different aspects of shape and can be used for a specific application. Therefore, depending on the application, it is necessary to analyze how well a descriptor captures the features of interest.
