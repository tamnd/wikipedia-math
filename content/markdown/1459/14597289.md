# Pythagorean addition

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pythagorean addition"}

*Image: Triangle Sides ABC.svg.* The [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) $c$ of a right triangle with sides $a$ and $b$ is the Pythagorean sum of $a$ and $b$.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Pythagorean addition** is a [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) on the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) that computes the length of the [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) of a [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420), given its two sides. Like the more familiar addition and multiplication operations of [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118), it is both [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) and [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative).

:::

This operation can be used in the conversion of [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) to [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates), and in the calculation of [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932). It also provides a simple notation and terminology for the [diameter](wikipedia://en/68283333) of a [cuboid](wikipedia://en/156802), the [energy-momentum relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy-momentum_relation) in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), and the overall noise from independent sources of noise. In its applications to [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324) and [propagation](wikipedia://en/593908) of [measurement uncertainty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurement_uncertainty), the same operation is also called **addition in quadrature**. A scaled version of this operation gives the [quadratic mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_mean) or *root mean square*.

It is available in many programming libraries as the **hypot** function (short for hypotenuse), implemented in a way designed to avoid errors arising due to limited-precision calculations performed on computers. [Donald Knuth](wikipedia://en/8095) has written that "Most of the square root operations in computer programs could probably be avoided if \[Pythagorean addition\] were more widely available, because people seem to want square roots primarily when they are computing distances." Although the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) is ancient, its application in computing distances began in the 18th century, and the various names for this operation came into use in the 20th century.

## Definition {#Definition}

\|}

According to the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034), for a [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420) with side lengths $a$ and $b$, the length of the [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) can be calculated as $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}.$ This formula defines the Pythagorean addition operation, denoted here as $\oplus$: for any two [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $a$ and $b$, the result of this operation is defined to be

$$a\oplus b = \sqrt{a^2+b^2 \vphantom)}.$$

For instance, the [special right triangle](wikipedia://en/2278116) based on the [Pythagorean triple](wikipedia://en/24172) $(3,4,5)$ gives $3\oplus 4=5$.[^o4] However, the [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) result of this example is unusual: for other integer arguments, Pythagorean addition can produce a [quadratic irrational number](wikipedia://en/305331) as its result.

## Properties {#Properties}

The operation $\oplus$ is [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) and [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative). Therefore, if three or more numbers are to be combined with this operation, the order of combination makes no difference to the result, which can be expressed without need of parenthesization as

$$x_1 \oplus x_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus x_n=\sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots + x_n^2}.$$

Additionally, on the non-negative real numbers, zero is an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) for Pythagorean addition. On numbers that can be negative, the Pythagorean sum with zero gives the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991):

$$x\oplus 0=|x|.$$

The three properties of associativity, commutativity, and having an identity element (on the non-negative numbers) are the defining properties of a [commutative monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_monoid).

## Applications {#Applications}

### Distance and diameter {#Distance_and_diameter}

*Image: add\_in\_quadrature.svg.* Pythagorean addition finds the length of the body diagonal of a [rectangular cuboid](wikipedia://en/38848329), or equivalently the length of the [vector sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_sum) of [orthogonal vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_vectors).

The [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) between two points in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), given by their [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$, is

$$(x_1-x_2)\oplus (y_1-y_2).$$

In the same way, the distance between three-dimensional points $(x_1,y_1,z_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2,z_2)$ can be found by repeated Pythagorean addition as

$$(x_1-x_2)\oplus (y_1-y_2)\oplus (z_1-z_2).$$

Pythagorean addition can also find the length of an interior [diagonal](wikipedia://en/349251) of a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048) or [rectangular cuboid](wikipedia://en/38848329). For a rectangle with sides $a$ and $b$, the diagonal length is $a\oplus b$. For a cuboid with side lengths $a$, $b$, and $c$, the length of a [body diagonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_diagonal) is $a\oplus b\oplus c$.

### Coordinate conversion {#Coordinate_conversion}

Pythagorean addition (and its implementation as the `hypot` function) is often used together with the `[[atan2]]` function (a two-parameter form of the [arctangent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctangent)) to convert from [Cartesian coordinates](wikipedia://en/7706) $(x,y)$ to [polar coordinates](wikipedia://en/25120) $(r,\theta)$:

$$\begin{aligned} r&=x\oplus y=\operatorname{hypot}(x,y), \\ \theta&=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x). \end{aligned}$$

### Quadratic mean and spread of deviation {#Quadratic_mean_and_spread_of_deviation}

The [root mean square](wikipedia://en/66819) or quadratic mean of a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) of $n$ numbers is $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}$ times their Pythagorean sum. This is a [generalized mean](wikipedia://en/13143) of the numbers.

The [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590) of a collection of observations is the quadratic mean of their individual deviations from the mean. When two or more independent random variables are added, the standard deviation of their sum is the Pythagorean sum of their standard deviations. Thus, the Pythagorean sum itself can be interpreted as giving the amount of overall noise when combining independent sources of noise.

If the [engineering tolerances](wikipedia://en/522062) of different parts of an assembly are treated as independent noise, they can be combined using a Pythagorean sum. In [experimental sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_science) such as [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), addition in quadrature is often used to combine different sources of [measurement uncertainty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurement_uncertainty). However, this method of [propagation of uncertainty](wikipedia://en/593908) applies only when there is no correlation between sources of uncertainty, and it has been criticized for conflating experimental noise with [systematic errors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematic_error).

### Other {#Other}

*Image: Einstein-triangle-kinetic-energy.svg.* The [energy-momentum relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy-momentum_relation), visualized as a right triangle. Here $E_{0} = mc^{2}$ is the [rest energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rest_energy) of a particle, $p$ is its [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431), $E$ is the total energy given by the energy-momentum relation, and $E_{\operatorname{KIN}} = E - E_{0}$ is its [kinetic energy](wikipedia://en/17327).

The [energy-momentum relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy-momentum_relation) in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), describing the energy of a moving particle, can be expressed as the Pythagorean sum

$$E = mc^2 \oplus pc,$$

where $m$ is the [rest mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rest_mass) of a particle, $p$ is its [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431), $c$ is the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light), and $E$ is the particle's resulting [relativistic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_energy).

When combining signals, it can be a useful design technique to arrange for the combined signals to be [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/102221) in [polarization](wikipedia://en/41564) or [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29), so that they add in quadrature. In early [radio engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_engineering), this idea was used to design [directional antennas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directional_antenna), allowing signals to be received while nullifying the interference from signals coming from other directions. When the same technique is applied in software to obtain a directional signal from a radio or [ultrasound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrasound) [phased array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phased_array), Pythagorean addition may be used to combine the signals. Other recent applications of this idea include improved efficiency in the [frequency conversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_frequency_multiplier) of [lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser).

In the [psychophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychophysics) of [haptic perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haptic_perception), Pythagorean addition has been proposed as a model for the perceived intensity of [vibration](wikipedia://en/20646772) when two kinds of vibration are combined.

In [image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processing), the [Sobel operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sobel_operator) for [edge detection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_detection) consists of a [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) step to determine the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) of an image followed by a Pythagorean sum at each pixel to determine the magnitude of the gradient.

## Implementation {#Implementation}

In a 1983 paper, [Cleve Moler](wikipedia://en/562706) and Donald Morrison described an [iterative method](wikipedia://en/15237) for computing Pythagorean sums, without taking square roots. This was soon recognized to be an instance of [Halley's method](wikipedia://en/9053035), and extended to analogous operations on [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859).

Although many modern implementations of this operation instead compute Pythagorean sums by reducing the problem to the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) function, they do so in a way that has been designed to avoid errors arising from the limited-precision calculations performed on computers. If calculated using the natural formula,

$$x \oplus y = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2},$$

the squares of very large or small values of $x$ and $y$ may exceed the range of [machine precision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_precision) when calculated on a computer. This may lead to an inaccurate result caused by [arithmetic underflow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_underflow) and [overflow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_overflow), although when overflow and underflow do not occur the output is within two [ulp](wikipedia://en/1498076) of the exact result. Common implementations of the `hypot` function rearrange this calculation in a way that avoids the problem of overflow and underflow and are even more precise.

If either input to `hypot` is infinite, the result is infinite. Because this is true for all possible values of the other input, the [IEEE 754](wikipedia://en/405039) floating-point standard requires that this remains true even when the other input is [not a number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Not_a_number) (NaN).

### Calculation order {#Calculation_order}

The difficulty with the naive implementation is that $x^2+y^2$ may overflow or underflow, unless the intermediate result is computed with [extended precision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_precision). Instead, an implementation can uniformly scale the inputs by some positive number, perform the calculation in a range which avoids overflow or underflow, and then inversely scale the result, based on the identity

$$\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = k^{-1}\sqrt{(kx)^2 + (ky)^2}.$$

A simple version of this idea first sorts the inputs so that $|x|\ge|y|$, and then scales them by $k = 1/|x|$, computing

$$x \oplus y = |x| \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{y}{x}\right)^2}.$$

The computation of $y/x$ cannot overflow unless both $x$ and $y$ are zero. If $y/x$ underflows, the final result is equal to $|x|$, which is correct within the precision of the calculation. The square root is computed of a value between 1 and 2. Finally, the multiplication by $|x|$ cannot underflow, and overflows only when the result is too large to represent.

One drawback of this rearrangement is the additional division by $x$, which increases both the time and inaccuracy of the computation. More complex implementations avoid these costs by dividing the inputs into more cases:

- When $x$ is much larger than $y$, $x\oplus y\approx|x|$, to within [machine precision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_precision).
- When $x^2$ overflows, multiply both $x$ and $y$ by a small scaling factor, use the naive algorithm which will now not overflow, and multiply the result by the (large) inverse.
- When $y^2$ underflows, scale as above but reverse the scaling factors to scale up the intermediate values.
- Otherwise, the naive algorithm is safe to use.

Additional techniques allow the result to be computed more accurately than the naive algorithm, e.g. to less than one [ulp](wikipedia://en/1498076). Researchers have also developed analogous algorithms for computing Pythagorean sums of more than two values.

### Fast approximation {#Fast_approximation}

The [alpha max plus beta min algorithm](wikipedia://en/6016645) is a high-speed approximation of Pythagorean addition using only comparison, multiplication, and addition, producing a value whose error is less than 4% of the correct result. It is computed as

$$x\oplus y\approx \alpha\cdot\max(|x|,|y|)+\beta\cdot\min(|x|,|y|)$$

for a careful choice of parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

## History {#History}

The [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) on which this operation is based was studied in ancient [Greek mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics), and may have been known earlier in [Egyptian mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_mathematics) and [Babylonian mathematics](wikipedia://en/3861353); see Pythagorean theorem. However, its use for computing distances in Cartesian coordinates could not come until after [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) invented these coordinates in 1637; the formula for distance from these coordinates was published by [Alexis Clairaut](wikipedia://en/196273) in 1731.[^n1]

The terms "Pythagorean addition" and "Pythagorean sum" for this operation have been used at least since the 1950s, and its use in signal processing as "addition in quadrature" goes back at least to 1919.

From the 1920s to the 1940s, before the widespread use of computers, multiple designers of [slide rules](wikipedia://en/28743) included square-root scales in their devices, allowing Pythagorean sums to be calculated mechanically. Researchers have also investigated [analog circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analog_circuit) for approximating the value of Pythagorean sums.
