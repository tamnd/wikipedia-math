# Isochoric process

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Isochoric process"}

In [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952), an **isochoric process**, also called a **constant-volume process**, an **isovolumetric process**, or an **isometric process**, is a [thermodynamic process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_process) during which the [volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume_%28thermodynamics%29) of the [closed system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_system) undergoing such a process remains constant. An isochoric process is exemplified by the heating or the cooling of the contents of a sealed, [inelastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_%28physics%29) container: The thermodynamic process is the addition or removal of heat; the isolation of the contents of the container establishes the closed system; and the inability of the container to [deform](wikipedia://en/19194778) imposes the constant-volume condition.

:::

## Formalism {#Formalism}

An isochoric thermodynamic [quasi-static](wikipedia://en/582702) process is characterized by constant [volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume_%28thermodynamics%29), i.e., $\Delta V = 0$.[^o3] The process does no [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure)-volume [work](wikipedia://en/3616613), since such work is defined by

$$W = P \Delta V ,$$

where $P$ is pressure. The sign convention is such that positive work is performed by the system on the environment.

If the process is not quasi-static, the work can perhaps be done in a volume constant thermodynamic process.

For a [reversible process](wikipedia://en/416681), the [first law of thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/166404) gives the change in the system's [internal energy](wikipedia://en/340757):

$$dU = dQ - dW$$

Replacing [work](wikipedia://en/149861) with a change in volume gives

$$dU = dQ - P \, dV$$

Since the process is isochoric, $dV = 0$, the previous equation now gives

$$dU = dQ$$

Using the definition of [specific heat capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_heat_capacity) at constant volume, $c_{v} = (dQ/dT)/m$, where $m$ is the mass of the gas, we get

$$dQ = m c_\mathrm{v} \, dT$$

Integrating both sides yields

$$\Delta Q\ = m \int_{T_1}^{T_2} \! c_\mathrm{v} \, dT,$$

where $c_{v}$ is the specific heat capacity at constant volume, $T_{1}$ is the initial [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050) and $T_{2}$ is the final [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050). We conclude with:

$$\Delta Q\ = m c_\mathrm{v} \Delta T$$

*Image: isochoric process SVG.svg.* Isochoric process in the [pressure volume diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_volume_diagram). In this diagram, pressure increases, but volume remains constant.

On a [pressure volume diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_volume_diagram), an isochoric process appears as a straight vertical line. Its thermodynamic conjugate, an [isobaric process](wikipedia://en/414765) would appear as a straight horizontal line.

### Ideal gas {#Ideal_gas}

If an [ideal gas](wikipedia://en/65905) is used in an isochoric process, and the quantity of [gas](wikipedia://en/65905) stays constant, then the increase in [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) is proportional to an increase in [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050) and pressure. For example a gas heated in a rigid container: the pressure and temperature of the gas will increase, but the volume will remain the same.

## Ideal Otto cycle {#Ideal_Otto_cycle}

The ideal [Otto cycle](wikipedia://en/147566) is an example of an isochoric process when it is assumed that the burning of the [gasoline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gasoline)-air mixture in an [internal combustion engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_combustion_engine) car is instantaneous. There is an increase in the temperature and the pressure of the gas inside the cylinder while the volume remains the same.

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The noun "isochor" and the adjective "isochoric" are derived from the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) words ἴσος (*isos*) meaning "equal", and χῶρος (*khôros*) meaning "space."
