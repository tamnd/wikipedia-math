# John Tukey

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="John Tukey"}

**John Wilder Tukey** (;[^o5] June 16, 1915 – July 26, 2000) was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and [statistician](wikipedia://en/48661), best known for the development of the [fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm](wikipedia://en/352702), the [box plot](wikipedia://en/160960) and for laying the foundations of the field of [exploratory data analysis](wikipedia://en/416589).[^o6] The [Tukey range test](wikipedia://en/14653762), the [Tukey lambda distribution](wikipedia://en/15780233), the [Tukey test of additivity](wikipedia://en/26035665), and the [Teichmüller–Tukey lemma](wikipedia://en/21922970) all bear his name. He is also credited with coining the term *[bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit)* and the first published use of the word *[software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software)*.

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Tukey was born in [New Bedford, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Bedford,_Massachusetts), in 1915, to a Latin teacher father and a private tutor. He was mainly taught by his mother and attended regular classes only for certain subjects like French.[^o60] Tukey obtained a [B.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts) in 1936 and [M.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M.S.) in 1937 in chemistry, from [Brown University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_University), before moving to [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University), where in 1939 he received a [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD) in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) after completing a doctoral dissertation titled "On [denumerability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denumerability) in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954)".[^o3][^o8][^o9]

During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), Tukey worked at the Fire Control Research Office and collaborated with [Samuel Wilks](wikipedia://en/8136395) and [William Cochran](wikipedia://en/2223833). After the war, he returned to Princeton, dividing his time between the university and [AT&T Bell Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs). In 1962, Tukey was elected to the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society).[^o10] He became a full professor at 35 and founding chairman of the Princeton statistics department in 1965.[^o60]

Tukey married Elizabeth Rapp, a [Temple University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_University) graduate who took graduate courses at [Radcliffe College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radcliffe_College) and worked as a personnel director for the [Educational Testing Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Educational_Testing_Service), in 1950.[^o11] Tukey's [sister-in-law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sister-in-law) Phyllis married the English statistician [Frank Anscombe](wikipedia://en/15385810).[^o12]

Tukey retired in 1985. He died in [New Brunswick, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Brunswick,_New_Jersey), on July 26, 2000.[^o60]

## Scientific contributions {#Scientific_contributions}

Early in his career Tukey worked on developing [statistical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical) methods for computers at [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs), where he coined the word *bit* in 1947.[^o57][^o58][^o59]

His statistical interests were many and varied. He is particularly remembered for his development with [James Cooley](wikipedia://en/1565795) of the [Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm](wikipedia://en/352702). In 1970, he contributed significantly to what is today known as the [jackknife](wikipedia://en/9158134)—also termed [Quenouille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Quenouille)–Tukey jackknife. He introduced the [box plot](wikipedia://en/160960) in his 1977 book, "Exploratory Data Analysis", building on the earlier work of [Mary Eleanor Spear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Eleanor_Spear).

[Tukey's range test](wikipedia://en/14653762), the [Tukey lambda distribution](wikipedia://en/15780233), [Tukey's test of additivity](wikipedia://en/26035665), [Tukey's lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tukey's_lemma), and the [Tukey window](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tukey_window) all bear his name. He is also the creator of several little-known methods such as the [trimean](wikipedia://en/9888821) and [median-median line](wikipedia://en/18837#Median.E2.80.93median_line), an easier alternative to [linear regression](wikipedia://en/48758386).

In 1974, he developed, with [Jerome H. Friedman](wikipedia://en/40671069), the concept of the [projection pursuit](wikipedia://en/2830368).[^o14]

### Accolades {#Accolades}

Tukey was awarded the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science) by President Nixon in 1973.[^o60] He was awarded the [IEEE Medal of Honor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_Medal_of_Honor) in 1982 "For his contributions to the spectral analysis of random processes and the [fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512) (FFT) [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775)".[^o16]

### Data analysis and foundations of data science {#Data_analysis_and_foundations_of_data_science}

John Tukey contributed greatly to statistical practice and data analysis in general. In fact, some regard John Tukey as the father of data science. At the very least, he pioneered many of the key foundations of what came later to be known as [data science](wikipedia://en/35458904).[^o18]

Making sense of data has a long history and has been addressed by statisticians, mathematicians, scientists, and others for many many years. During the 1960s, Tukey challenged the dominance at the time of what he called "confirmatory data analysis", statistical analyses driven by rigid mathematical configurations.[^o19] Tukey emphasized the importance of having a more flexible attitude towards data analysis and of exploring data carefully to see what structures and information might be contained therein. He called this "exploratory data analysis" (EDA). In many ways, EDA was a precursor to data science.

Tukey also realized the importance of computer science to EDA. Graphics are an integral part of EDA methodology and, while much of Tukey's work focused on static displays (such as box plots) that could be drawn by hand, he realized that computer graphics would be much more effective for studying multivariate data. PRIM-9, the first program for viewing multivariate data, was conceived by him during the early 1970s.[^o20]

This coupling of data analysis and computer science is what is now called data science.

Tukey articulated the important distinction between [exploratory data analysis](wikipedia://en/416589) and [confirmatory data analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmatory_data_analysis), believing that much statistical methodology placed too great an emphasis on the latter. Though he believed in the utility of separating the two types of analysis, he pointed out that sometimes, especially in [natural science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science), this was problematic and termed such situations [uncomfortable science](wikipedia://en/416598).

A. D. Gordon of the [University of St. Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St._Andrews) offered the following summary of Tukey's principles for statistical practice:[^o21] ... the usefulness and limitation of mathematical statistics; the importance of having methods of statistical analysis that are robust to violations of the assumptions underlying their use; the need to amass experience of the behaviour of specific methods of analysis in order to provide guidance on their use; the importance of allowing the possibility of data's influencing the choice of method by which they are analysed; the need for statisticians to reject the role of "guardian of proven truth", and to resist attempts to provide once-for-all solutions and tidy over-unifications of the subject; the iterative nature of data analysis; implications of the increasing power, availability, and cheapness of computing facilities; the training of statisticians.

Tukey's lectures were described to be unusual. McCullagh described his lecture given in London in 1977:[^o21][^o22] Tukey ambled to the podium, a great bear of a man dressed in baggy pants and a black knitted shirt. These might once have been a matching pair but the vintage was such that it was hard to tell. ... Carefully and deliberately a list of headings was chalked on the blackboard. The words came too, not many, like overweight parcels, delivered at a slow unfaltering pace. ... When it was complete, Tukey turned to face the audience and the podium ... "Comments, queries, suggestions?" he asked the audience ... As he waited for a response, he clambered onto the podium and manoeuvred until he was sitting cross-legged facing the audience. ... We in the audience sat like spectators at the zoo waiting for the great bear to move or say something. But the great bear appeared to be doing the same thing, and the feeling was not comfortable.

## Contributions to other fields {#Contributions_to_other_fields}

Tukey made wide-ranging contributions beyond statistics, once reportedly remarking, "The best thing about being a statistician is that you get to play in everyone's backyard."[^o60]

In the 1950s, Tukey served on a committee of the [National Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Research_Council_%28United_States%29) that produced a report critiquing the statistical methodology of the [Kinsey Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinsey_Reports), and he chaired a committee in the 1970s on the role of aerosol sprays in damaging the [ozone layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_layer).[^o60]

From 1960 to 1980, Tukey helped design the NBC television network polls used to predict and analyze elections. He was also a consultant to the [Educational Testing Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Educational_Testing_Service), the [Xerox Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Corporation), and [Merck & Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merck_&_Company).[^o60]

During the 1970s and early 1980s, Tukey played a key role in the design and conduct of the [National Assessment of Educational Progress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assessment_of_Educational_Progress).

### Coining neologisms {#Coining_neologisms}

While working with [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) on early computer designs, Tukey introduced the word *[bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit)* as a portmanteau of *binary digit*.[^o25] The term *bit* was first used in [an article](wikipedia://en/828061) by [Claude Shannon](wikipedia://en/5693) in 1948.

Tukey is also credited with the first use of the word *[software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software)* to describe computer programs in a 1958 article[^o26] in [American Mathematical Monthly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Monthly).[^o27]
