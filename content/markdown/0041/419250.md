# Significand

Significance (disambiguation)|Significant

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Significand"}

The **significand**[^o18] (also **coefficient**,[^o18] sometimes **argument**,[^o2] or more ambiguously **mantissa**,[^o25] **fraction**,[^o28][^o24][^o15] or **characteristic**[^o27][^o25]) is the first (left) part of a number in [scientific notation](wikipedia://en/59715) or related concepts in [floating-point](wikipedia://en/11376) representation, consisting of its [significant digits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Significant_digit). For negative numbers, it does not include the initial minus sign.

:::

Depending on the interpretation of the [exponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponent), the significand may represent an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) or a [fractional number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_number), which may cause the term "mantissa" to be misleading, since [the *mantissa* of a logarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantissa_%28logarithm%29) is always its fractional part.[^o3][^o4] Although the other names mentioned are common, *significand* is the word used by [IEEE 754](wikipedia://en/405039), an important technical standard for floating-point arithmetic.[^o5] In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the term "argument" may also be ambiguous, since "the argument of a number" sometimes refers to the length of a circular arc from [1](wikipedia://en/77591639) to a number on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628).[^o6]

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

The number 123.45 can be represented as a [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214) floating-point number with the integer 12345 as the significand and a 10<sup>−2</sup> power term, also called [characteristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_%28exponent_notation%29),[^o17][^o19][^o20] where −2 is the exponent (and 10 is the base). Its value is given by the following arithmetic:

\123.45 = 12345 × 10<sup>−2</sup>.

This same value can also be represented in [scientific notation](wikipedia://en/59715) with the significand 1.2345 as a fractional coefficient, and +2 as the exponent (and 10 as the base):

\123.45 = 1**.**2345 × 10<sup>\+2</sup>.

Computer scientist Hermann Schmid, however, called this representation with a significand ranging between 1.0 and 10 a *modified normalized form*.[^o19][^o20]

For base 2, this 1.xxxx form is also called a *normalized significand*.

Finally, the value can be represented in the format given by the [Language Independent Arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_Independent_Arithmetic) standard and several programming language standards, including [Ada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_%28programming_language%29), [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28programming_language%29), [Fortran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortran) and [Modula-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modula-2), as

\123.45 = 0**.**12345 × 10<sup>\+3</sup>.

Schmid called this representation with a significand ranging between 0.1 and 1.0 the *true normalized form*.[^o19][^o20]

:::

## The hidden bit in floating point {#The_hidden_bit_in_floating_point}

For a [normalized number](wikipedia://en/1349666), the most significant digit is always non-zero. When working in [binary](wikipedia://en/238686), this constraint uniquely determines this digit to always be 1. As such, it is not explicitly stored, being called the *[hidden bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidden_bit)*.

The significand is characterized by its width in [(binary) digits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit), and depending on the context, the hidden bit may or may not be counted toward the width. For example, the same [IEEE 754](wikipedia://en/405039) [double-precision format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-precision_floating-point_format) is commonly described as having either a 53-bit significand, including the hidden bit, or a 52-bit significand, excluding the hidden bit. IEEE 754 defines the precision *p* to be the number of digits in the significand, including any implicit leading bit (e.g., *p* = 53 for the double-precision format), thus in a way independent from the encoding, and the term to express what is encoded (that is, the significand without its leading bit) is *trailing significand field*.

## Floating-point mantissa {#Floating-point_mantissa}

In 1914, [Leonardo Torres Quevedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_Torres_Quevedo) introduced the idea of [floating-point arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11376) in his *Essays on Automatics*,[^o11] where he proposed the format *n*; *m*, showing the need for a fixed-sized significand as currently used for floating-point data.[^o12]

In 1946, [Arthur Burks](wikipedia://en/6479641) used the terms *mantissa* and *characteristic* to describe the two parts of a floating-point number ([Burks](wikipedia://en/6479641)[^o17] *et al.*) by analogy with the then-prevalent [common logarithm](wikipedia://en/174482) tables: the *characteristic* is the integer part of the logarithm (i.e. the exponent), and the *mantissa* is the fractional part. The usage remains common among [computer scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_scientist) today.

The term *significand* was introduced by [George Forsythe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Elmer_Forsythe) and [Cleve Moler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleve_Barry_Moler) in 1967[^o21][^o22][^o23][^o24] and is the word used in the IEEE standard[^o26] as the coefficient in front of a scientific notation number discussed above. The fractional part is called the *fraction*.

To understand both terms, notice that in binary, 1 + mantissa ≈ significand, and the correspondence is exact when storing a power of two. This fact allows for a fast approximation of the base-2 logarithm, leading to algorithms e.g. for computing the [fast square-root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methods_of_computing_square_roots#Approximations_that_depend_on_the_floating_point_representation) and [fast inverse-square-root](wikipedia://en/20989916). The implicit leading 1 is nothing but the hidden bit in IEEE 754 floating point, and the bitfield storing the remainder is thus the *mantissa*.

However, whether or not the implicit 1 is included is a major point of confusion with both terms—and especially so with *mantissa*. In keeping with the original usage in the context of log tables, it should not be present.

For those contexts where 1 is considered included, [William Kahan](wikipedia://en/303413),[^o18] lead creator of IEEE 754, and [Donald E. Knuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_E._Knuth), prominent computer programmer and author of *[The Art of Computer Programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Art_of_Computer_Programming)*,[^o27] condemn the use of *mantissa*. This has led to declining use of the term *mantissa* in *all* contexts. In particular, the current IEEE 754 standard does not mention it.
