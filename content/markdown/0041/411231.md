# Spin group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Spin group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) the **spin group**, denoted Spin(*n*),[^o2][^o3] is a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) whose underlying [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) is the [double cover](wikipedia://en/250323) of the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) SO(*n*) = SO(*n*, **R**), such that there exists a [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) of Lie groups (when *n* ≠ 2)

:::

$$1 \to \mathbb{Z}_2 \to \operatorname{Spin}(n) \to \operatorname{SO}(n) \to 1.$$

The group multiplication law on the double cover is given by [lifting](wikipedia://en/10504570) the multiplication on $\operatorname{SO}(n)$.

As a Lie group, Spin(*n*) therefore shares its [dimension](wikipedia://en/17944#Generators_and_dimension), $\tfrac{n(n - 1)}{2}$, and its [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) with the special orthogonal group.

For *n* > 2, Spin(*n*) is [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) and so coincides with the [universal cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_cover) of [SO(*n*)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group).

The non-trivial element of the [kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_%28group_theory%29) is denoted −1, which should not be confused with the orthogonal transform of [reflection through the origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_through_the_origin), generally denoted −$I$.

Spin(*n*) can be constructed as a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of the invertible elements in the [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305) Cl(*n*). A distinct article discusses the [spin representations](wikipedia://en/10160091).

## Use for physics models {#Use_for_physics_models}

The spin group is used in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) when describing the symmetries of (electrically neutral, uncharged) [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion). Its complexification, Spinc, is used to describe electrically charged fermions, most notably the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron). Strictly speaking, the spin group describes a fermion in a zero-dimensional space; however, space is not zero-dimensional, and so the spin group is used to define (non-existent) [spin structures](wikipedia://en/2844303) as a calculation tool on (pseudo-)[Riemannian manifolds](wikipedia://en/144652): the spin group is the [structure group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_group) of a [spinor bundle](wikipedia://en/348085). The [affine connection](wikipedia://en/607690) on a spinor bundle is the [spin connection](wikipedia://en/2915190); the spin connection can simplify calculations in [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024). The spin connection in turn enables the [Dirac equation](wikipedia://en/39407) to be written [in curved spacetime](wikipedia://en/39171284) (effectively in the [tetrad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrad_%28general_relativity%29) coordinates).

::: {#o6 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

Construction of the Spin group often starts with the construction of a [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305) over a real vector space *V* with a [definite quadratic form](wikipedia://en/20437320) *q*.[^o7] The Clifford algebra is the quotient of the [tensor algebra](wikipedia://en/363628) T*V* of *V* by a two-sided ideal. The tensor algebra (over the reals) may be written as

$$\mathrm{T}V= \mathbb {R} \oplus V \oplus (V\otimes V) \oplus \cdots$$

The Clifford algebra Cl(*V*) is then the [quotient algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_associative_algebra)

$$\operatorname{Cl}(V) = \mathrm{T}V / \left( v \otimes v - q(v) \right) ,$$

where $q(v)$ is the quadratic form applied to a vector $v\in V$. The resulting space is finite dimensional, naturally [graded](wikipedia://en/1155734) (as a vector space), and can therefore be written as

$$\operatorname{Cl}(V) = \operatorname{Cl}^0 \oplus \operatorname{Cl}^1 \oplus \operatorname{Cl}^2 \oplus \cdots \oplus \operatorname{Cl}^n$$

where $n$ is the dimension of $V$, $\operatorname{Cl}^0 = \mathbb{R}$ and $\operatorname{Cl}^1 = V$. The **spin algebra** $\mathfrak{spin}$ is defined as the [bivector](wikipedia://en/884040) subalgebra

$$\operatorname{Cl}^2 =\mathfrak{spin}(V) = \mathfrak{spin}(n) ,$$

where the last is a short-hand for *V* being a real vector space of real dimension *n*. It is a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) with the commutator as multiplication; it has a natural action on *V*, and is isomorphic to the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ of the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group): If the set $\{e_i\}$ are an orthonormal basis of the (real) vector space *V*, then the quotient above endows the Clifford algebra with a natural anti-commuting structure:

$e_i e_j = -e_j e_i$ for $i \ne j ,$

which follows by considering $v\otimes v$ for $v=e_i+e_j$. Then in $\mathfrak{spin}(n)$ we have that the Lie commutator $[e_i \otimes e_j, e_j \otimes e_k ]=2 e_i \otimes e_k$ and $[e_i \otimes e_j, e_k \otimes e_l ]=0$, so $e_i \otimes e_j \rightarrow 2e_i \otimes e_j -2e_j \otimes e_i$ gives the isomorphism to $\mathfrak{so}(n)$. On the right hand side $\otimes$ is the [outer product](wikipedia://en/103109). The multiplication by 2 explains why rotating a spinor by 360 degrees returns minus the spinor: in $e^{i \phi b}$ dividing the basis element *b* by 2 gives half a rotation for 360 degrees.

The [pin group](wikipedia://en/1794902) $\operatorname{Pin}(V)$ is a subgroup of $\operatorname{Cl}(V)$'s Clifford group of all elements of the form

$$v_1 v_2 \cdots v_k ,$$

where each $v_i\in V$ is of unit length: $q(v_i) = 1.$

The spin group is then defined as

$$\operatorname{Spin}(V) = \operatorname{Pin}(V) \cap \operatorname{Cl}^{\text{even}} ,$$

where $\operatorname{Cl}^\text{even}=\operatorname{Cl}^0 \oplus \operatorname{Cl}^2 \oplus \operatorname{Cl}^4 \oplus \cdots$ is the subspace generated by elements that are the product of an even number of vectors. That is, Spin(*V*) consists of all elements of Pin(*V*), given above, with the restriction to *k* being an even number. The restriction to the even subspace is key to the formation of two-component (Weyl) spinors, constructed below.

The anti-commutation of the Clifford algebra turns out to be of importance in physics, as it captures the spirit of the [Pauli exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/24669) for [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion). A precise formulation is out of scope here, but it involves the creation of a [spinor bundle](wikipedia://en/348085) on [Minkowski spacetime](wikipedia://en/230488); the resulting spinor fields can be seen to be anti-commuting as a by-product of the Clifford algebra construction. This anti-commutation property is also key to the formulation of [supersymmetry](wikipedia://en/224636). The Clifford algebra and the spin group have many interesting and curious properties, some of which are listed below.

### Geometric construction {#Geometric_construction}

The spin groups can be constructed less explicitly but without appealing to Clifford algebras. As a manifold, $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ is the double cover of $\operatorname{SO}(n)$. Its multiplication law can be defined by lifting as follows. Call the [covering map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covering_map) $p: \operatorname{Spin}(n) \rightarrow \operatorname{SO}(n)$. Then $p^{-1}(\{e\})$ is a set with two elements, and one can be chosen without loss of generality to be the identity. Call this $\tilde e$. Then to define multiplication in $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$, for $a, b \in \operatorname{Spin}(n)$ choose paths $\gamma_a, \gamma_b$ satisfying $\gamma_a(0) = \gamma_b(0) = \tilde e$, and $\gamma_a(1) = a, \gamma_b(1) = b$. These define a path $\gamma$ in $\operatorname{SO}(n)$ defined $\gamma(t) = p(\gamma_a(t))\cdot p(\gamma_b(t))$ satisfying $\gamma(0) = e$. Since $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ is a double cover, there is a unique lift $\tilde \gamma$ of $\gamma$ with $\tilde \gamma(0) = \tilde e$. Then define the product as $a \cdot b = \tilde \gamma (1)$.

It can then be shown that this definition is independent of the paths $\gamma_a, \gamma_b$, that the multiplication is continuous, and the group axioms are satisfied with inversion being continuous, making $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ a Lie group.

:::

## Double covering {#Double_covering}

For a quadratic space *V*, a double covering of SO(*V*) by Spin(*V*) can be given explicitly, as follows. Let $\{e_i\}$ be an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) for *V*. Define an [antiautomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiautomorphism) $t : \operatorname{Cl}(V) \to \operatorname{Cl}(V)$ by

$$\left(e_i e_j \cdots e_k\right)^t = e_k\cdots e_j e_i.$$

This can be extended to all elements of $a,b\in \operatorname{Cl}(V)$ by linearity. It is an antihomomorphism since

$$(a b)^t = b^t a^t.$$

Observe that $\operatorname{Pin}(V)$ can then be defined as all elements $a \in \operatorname{Cl}(V)$ for which

$$a a^t = 1.$$

Now define the automorphism $\alpha\colon \operatorname{Cl}(V)\to\operatorname{Cl}(V)$ which on degree 1 elements is given by

$$\alpha(v)=-v,\quad v\in V,$$

and let $a^*$ denote $\alpha(a)^t$, which is an antiautomorphism of $\operatorname{Cl}(V)$. With this notation, an explicit double covering is the homomorphism $\rho:\operatorname{Pin}(V)\to\operatorname O(V)$ given by

$$\rho(a) v = a v a^* ,$$

where $v \in V$. When $a$ has degree 1 (i.e. $a\in V$), $\rho(a)$ is the reflection across the hyperplane orthogonal to $a$; this follows from the anti-commuting property of the Clifford algebra.

This gives a double covering of both $\operatorname{O}(V)$ by $\operatorname{Pin}(V)$ and of $\operatorname{SO}(V)$ by $\operatorname{Spin}(V)$ because $a$ gives the same transformation as $-a$.

## Spinor space {#Spinor_space}

It is worth reviewing how spinor space and [Weyl spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_spinor) are constructed, given this formalism. Given a real vector space *V* of dimension *n* = 2*m* an even number, its [complexification](wikipedia://en/755604) is $V \otimes \mathbf{C}$. It can be written as the direct sum of a subspace $W$ of spinors and a subspace $\overline{W}$ of anti-spinors:

$$V \otimes \mathbf{C} = W \oplus \overline{W}$$

The space $W$ is spanned by the spinors $\eta_k = \left( e_{2k-1} - ie_{2k} \right) / \sqrt 2$ for $1\le k\le m$ and the complex conjugate spinors span $\overline{W}$. It is straightforward to see that the spinors anti-commute, and that the product of a spinor and anti-spinor is a scalar.

The **spinor space** is defined as the [exterior algebra](wikipedia://en/221537) $\textstyle{\bigwedge} W$. The (complexified) Clifford algebra acts naturally on this space; the (complexified) spin group corresponds to the length-preserving [endomorphisms](wikipedia://en/9569). There is a natural grading on the exterior algebra: the product of an odd number of copies of $W$ correspond to the physics notion of fermions; the even subspace corresponds to the bosons. The representations of the action of the spin group on the spinor space can be built in a relatively straightforward fashion.[^o7]

## Complex case {#Complex_case}

The Spin<sup>**C**</sup> group is defined by the [exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215)

$$1 \to \mathrm{Z}_2 \to \operatorname{Spin}^{\mathbf{C}}(n) \to \operatorname{SO}(n)\times \operatorname{U}(1) \to 1.$$

It is a multiplicative subgroup of the [complexification](wikipedia://en/755604) $\operatorname{Cl}(V)\otimes \mathbf{C}$ of the Clifford algebra, and specifically, it is the subgroup generated by Spin(*V*) and the unit circle in **C**. Alternately, it is the quotient

$$\operatorname{Spin}^{\mathbf{C}}(V) = \left( \operatorname{Spin}(V) \times S^1 \right) / \sim$$

where the equivalence $\sim$ identifies (*a*, *u*) with (−*a*, −*u*).

This has important applications in 4-manifold theory and [Seiberg–Witten theory](wikipedia://en/3526032). In physics, the Spin group is appropriate for describing uncharged fermions, while the Spin<sup>**C**</sup> group is used to describe electrically charged fermions. In this case, the U(1) symmetry is specifically the [gauge group](wikipedia://en/23912155) (structure group) of [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism).

## Indefinite signature {#Indefinite_signature}

In [indefinite signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_%28quadratic_form%29), the spin group $\text{Spin}(p, q)$ is constructed through [Clifford algebras](wikipedia://en/45305) in a similar way to standard spin groups. It is a [double cover](wikipedia://en/1378699) of $\text{SO}_0(p, q)$, the [connected component of the identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_of_the_identity) of the [indefinite orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/293609) $\text{SO}(p, q)$. For $p + q > 2$, $\text{Spin}(p, q)$ is connected; for $(p, q) = (1,1)$, there are two connected components.[^o17]

## Topological considerations {#Topological_considerations}

[Connected](wikipedia://en/6233) and [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) Lie groups are classified by their Lie algebra. So if *G* is a connected Lie group with a simple Lie algebra, with *G*′ the [universal cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_cover) of *G*, there is an inclusion

$$\pi_1 (G) \subset \operatorname{Z}(G'),$$

with Z(*G*′) the [center](wikipedia://en/7125) of *G*′. This inclusion and the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ of *G* determine *G* entirely (note that it is not the case that $\mathfrak{g}$ and π<sub>1</sub>(*G*) determine *G* entirely; for instance SL(2, **R**) and PSL(2, **R**) have the same Lie algebra and same fundamental group **Z**, but are not isomorphic).

The definite signature Spin(*n*) are all [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) for *n* > 2, so they are the universal coverings of SO(*n*).

In indefinite signature, Spin(*p*, *q*) is not necessarily connected, and in general the [identity component](wikipedia://en/1088787), Spin<sub>0</sub>(*p*, *q*), is not simply connected, thus it is not a universal cover. The fundamental group is most easily understood by considering the [maximal compact subgroup](wikipedia://en/1496726) of SO(*p*, *q*), which is SO(*p*) × SO(*q*), and noting that rather than being the product of the 2-fold covers (hence a 4-fold cover), Spin(*p*, *q*) is the "diagonal" 2-fold cover – it is a 2-fold quotient of the 4-fold cover. Explicitly, the maximal compact connected subgroup of Spin(*p*, *q*) is

Spin(*p*) × Spin(*q*)/{(1, 1), (−1, −1)}.

This allows us to calculate the [fundamental groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_groups) of SO(*p*, *q*), taking *p* ≥ *q*:

$$\pi_1(\mbox{SO}(p,q)) = \begin{cases} 0 & (p,q)=(1,1) \mbox{ or } (1,0) \\ \mathbb{Z}_2 & p > 2, q = 0,1 \\ \mathbb{Z} & (p,q)=(2,0) \mbox{ or } (2,1) \\ \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z} & (p,q) = (2,2) \\ \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}_2 & p > 2, q=2 \\ \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2 & p, q >2\\ \end{cases}$$

Thus once *p*, *q* > 2 the fundamental group is Z<sub>2</sub>, as it is a 2-fold quotient of a product of two universal covers.

The maps on fundamental groups are given as follows. For *p*, *q* > 2, this implies that the map π<sub>1</sub>(Spin(*p*, *q*)) → π<sub>1</sub>(SO(*p*, *q*)) is given by 1 ∈ Z<sub>2</sub> going to (1, 1) ∈ Z<sub>2</sub> × Z<sub>2</sub>. For *p* = 2, *q* > 2, this map is given by 1 ∈ **Z** → (1,1) ∈ **Z** × Z<sub>2</sub>. And finally, for *p* = *q* = 2, (1, 0) ∈ **Z** × **Z** is sent to (1,1) ∈ **Z** × **Z** and (0, 1) is sent to (1, −1).

### Fundamental groups of SO(n) {#Fundamental_groups_of_SO(n)}

The fundamental groups $\pi_1(\operatorname{\text{SO}}(n))$ can be more directly derived using results in [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831). In particular we can find $\pi_1(\operatorname{\text{SO}}(n))$ for $n > 3$ as the three smallest have familiar underlying manifolds: $\operatorname{\text{SO}}(1)$ is the point manifold, $\operatorname{\text{SO}}(2) \cong S^1$, and $\operatorname{\text{SO}}(3) \cong \mathbb{RP}^3$ (shown using the [axis-angle representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis-angle_representation)).

The proof uses known results in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801).[^o21]

::: {#o22 .table cue=table}

| Proof |
|---|
| First consider the action of $\operatorname{SO}(n)$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$, in particular on the vector $v = (1, 0, \cdots, 0)$. The orbit of this vector is $\text{Orbit}_{\text{SO}(n)}(v) = S^{n-1}$, while the stabilizer is $\text{Stab}_{\text{SO}(n)}(v) = \text{SO}(n-1)$. Thus from the [orbit-stabilizer theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit-stabilizer_theorem) one obtains an isomorphism $\text{SO}(n)/\text{SO}(n-1) \cong S^{n-1}.$ Geometrically, this provides a [fibration](wikipedia://en/316826) $\text{SO}(n-1) \rightarrow \text{SO}(n) \rightarrow S^{n-1}.$ Then Theorem 4.41 in Hatcher tells us that there is a [long exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_exact_sequence) of homotopy groups $\cdots \rightarrow \pi_k(\text{SO}(n-1)) \rightarrow \pi_k(\text{SO}(n)) \rightarrow \pi_k(S^{n-1}) \rightarrow \pi_{k-1}(\text{SO}(n-1)) \rightarrow \cdots$ and we concentrate on a section at the end of the sequence: $\pi_2(S^{n-1}) \rightarrow \pi_1(\text{SO}(n-1))\rightarrow \pi_1(\text{SO}(n)) \rightarrow \pi_1(S^{n-1}).$ Corollary 4.9 in Hatcher states $\pi_k(S^n) = 0$ for $k < n$. So for $n > 3$, the exact sequence becomes $0 \rightarrow \pi_1(\text{SO}(n-1))\rightarrow \pi_1(\text{SO}(n)) \rightarrow 0,$ hence $\pi_1(\text{SO}(n))$ and $\pi_1(\text{SO}(n-1))$ are isomorphic **as long as $n > 3$**, so for $n > 3$, we have $\pi_1(\text{SO}(n)) \cong \pi_1(\text{SO}(3))$. And since $\text{SO}(3) \cong \mathbb{RP}^3 \cong S^3 / \{\pm 1\}$, we get $\pi_1(\text{SO}(3)) \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$. |

:::

The same argument can be used to show $\pi(\text{SO}(1,n)^\uparrow) \cong \pi(\text{SO}(n))$, by considering a fibration

$$\text{SO}(n) \rightarrow \text{SO}(1,n)^\uparrow \rightarrow H^n,$$

where $H^n$ is the upper sheet of a two-sheeted [hyperboloid](wikipedia://en/145844), which is [contractible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contractible), and $\text{SO}(1,n)^\uparrow$ is the identity component of the proper [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489) (the proper orthochronous Lorentz group).

## Center {#Center}

The center of the spin groups, for *n* ≥ 3, (complex and real) are given as follows:[^o17]

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Z}(\operatorname{Spin}(n,\mathbf{C})) &= \begin{cases} \mathrm{Z}_2 & n = 2k+1\\ \mathrm{Z}_4 & n = 4k+2\\ \mathrm{Z}_2 \oplus \mathrm{Z}_2 & n = 4k\\ \end{cases} \\ \operatorname{Z}(\operatorname{Spin}(p,q)) &= \begin{cases} \mathrm{Z}_2 & p \text{ or } q \text{ odd}\\ \mathrm{Z}_4 & n = 4k+2, \text{ and } p, q \text{ even}\\ \mathrm{Z}_2 \oplus \mathrm{Z}_2 & n = 4k, \text{ and } p, q \text{ even}\\ \end{cases} \end{aligned}$$

## Quotient groups {#Quotient_groups}

[Quotient groups](wikipedia://en/11526) can be obtained from a spin group by quotienting out by a subgroup of the center, with the spin group then being a [covering group](wikipedia://en/1378699) of the resulting quotient, and both groups having the same Lie algebra.

Quotienting out by the entire center yields the minimal such group, the [projective special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_special_orthogonal_group), which is [centerless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centerless), while quotienting out by {±1} yields the special orthogonal group – if the center equals {±1} (namely in odd dimension), these two quotient groups agree. If the spin group is simply connected (as Spin(*n*) is for *n* > 2), then Spin is the *maximal* group in the sequence, and one has a sequence of three groups,

Spin(*n*) → SO(*n*) → PSO(*n*),

splitting by parity yields:

Spin(2*n*) → SO(2*n*) → PSO(2*n*),

Spin(2*n*+1) → SO(2*n*+1) = PSO(2*n*+1),

which are the three [compact real forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_real_form) (or two, if SO = PSO) of the [compact Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/25262324) $\mathfrak{so} (n, \mathbf{R}).$

The [homotopy groups](wikipedia://en/297506) of the cover and the quotient are related by the [long exact sequence of a fibration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_exact_sequence_of_a_fibration), with discrete fiber (the fiber being the kernel) – thus all homotopy groups for *k* > 1 are equal, but π<sub>0</sub> and π<sub>1</sub> may differ.

For *n* > 2, Spin(*n*) is [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) (π<sub>0</sub> = π<sub>1</sub> = Z<sub>1</sub> is trivial), so SO(*n*) is connected and has fundamental group Z<sub>2</sub> while PSO(*n*) is connected and has fundamental group equal to the center of Spin(*n*).

In indefinite signature the covers and homotopy groups are more complicated – Spin(*p*, *q*) is not simply connected, and quotienting also affects connected components. The analysis is simpler if one considers the maximal (connected) compact SO(*p*) × SO(*q*) ⊂ SO(*p*, *q*) and the [component group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Component_group) of Spin(*p*, *q*).

## Whitehead tower {#Whitehead_tower}

The spin group appears in a [Whitehead tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitehead_tower) anchored by the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954):

$$\ldots\rightarrow \text{Fivebrane}(n) \rightarrow \text{String}(n)\rightarrow \text{Spin}(n)\rightarrow \text{SO}(n) \rightarrow \text{O}(n)$$

The tower is obtained by successively removing (killing) homotopy groups of increasing order. This is done by constructing [short exact sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) starting with an [Eilenberg&ndash;MacLane space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eilenberg&ndash;MacLane_space) for the homotopy group to be removed. Killing the $\pi$<sub>3</sub> homotopy group in Spin(*n*), one obtains the infinite-dimensional [string group](wikipedia://en/28936135) String(*n*).

## Discrete subgroups {#Discrete_subgroups}

Discrete subgroups of the spin group can be understood by relating them to discrete subgroups of the special orthogonal group (rotational [point groups](wikipedia://en/1138942)).

Given the double cover Spin(*n*) → SO(*n*), by the [lattice theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_theorem), there is a [Galois connection](wikipedia://en/156411) between subgroups of Spin(*n*) and subgroups of SO(*n*) (rotational point groups): the image of a subgroup of Spin(*n*) is a rotational point group, and the preimage of a point group is a subgroup of Spin(*n*), and the [closure operator](wikipedia://en/483120) on subgroups of Spin(*n*) is multiplication by {±1}. These may be called "binary point groups"; most familiar is the 3-dimensional case, known as [binary polyhedral groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_polyhedral_group).

Concretely, every binary point group is either the preimage of a point group (hence denoted 2*G*, for the point group *G*), or is an index 2 subgroup of the preimage of a point group which maps (isomorphically) onto the point group; in the latter case the full binary group is abstractly $\mathrm{C}_2 \times G$ (since {±1} is central). As an example of these latter, given a cyclic group of odd order $\mathrm{Z}_{2k+1}$ in SO(*n*), its preimage is a cyclic group of twice the order, $\mathrm{C}_{4k+2} \cong \mathrm{Z}_{2k+1} \times \mathrm{Z}_2,$ and the subgroup Z<sub>2*k*+1</sub> \< Spin(*n*) maps isomorphically to Z<sub>2*k*+1</sub> \< SO(*n*).

Of particular note are two series:

- higher [binary tetrahedral groups](wikipedia://en/10997586), corresponding to the 2-fold cover of symmetries of the *n*-simplex; this group can also be considered as the [double cover of the symmetric group](wikipedia://en/25118486), 2⋅A<sub>*n*</sub> → A<sub>*n*</sub>, with the alternating group being the (rotational) symmetry group of the *n*-simplex.
- higher [binary octahedral groups](wikipedia://en/10997598), corresponding to the 2-fold covers of the [hyperoctahedral group](wikipedia://en/24268961) (symmetries of the [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783), or equivalently of its dual, the [cross-polytope](wikipedia://en/716401)).

For point groups that reverse orientation, the situation is more complicated, as there are two [pin groups](wikipedia://en/1794902), so there are two possible binary groups corresponding to a given point group.
