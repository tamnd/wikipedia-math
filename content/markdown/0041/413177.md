# Pincherle derivative

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pincherle derivative"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Pincherle derivative**[^o2] $T'$ of a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) $T: \mathbb{K}[x] \to \mathbb{K}[x]$ on the [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) in the variable *x* over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $\mathbb{K}$ is the [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193) of $T$ with the multiplication by *x* in the [algebra of endomorphisms](wikipedia://en/59623) $\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{K}[x])$. That is, $T'$ is another linear operator $T': \mathbb{K}[x] \to \mathbb{K}[x]$

:::

$$T' := [T,x] = Tx-xT = -\operatorname{ad}(x)T,\,$$

(for the origin of the $\operatorname{ad}$ notation, see the article on the [adjoint representation](wikipedia://en/302188#Derivative_of_Ad)) so that

$$T'\{p(x)\}=T\{xp(x)\}-xT\{p(x)\}\qquad\forall p(x)\in \mathbb{K}[x].$$

This concept is named after the Italian mathematician [Salvatore Pincherle](wikipedia://en/11950956) (1853–1936).

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The Pincherle derivative, like any [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193), is a [derivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivation_%28abstract_algebra%29), meaning it satisfies the sum and products rules: given two [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) $S$ and $T$ belonging to $\operatorname{End}\left( \mathbb{K}[x] \right),$

1. $$(T + S)^\prime = T^\prime + S^\prime;$$
1. $(TS)^\prime = T^\prime\!S + TS^\prime$ where $TS = T \circ S$ is the [composition of operators](wikipedia://en/195947#Composition_operator).

One also has $[T,S]^{\prime} = [T^{\prime}, S] + [T, S^{\prime}]$ where $[T,S] = TS - ST$ is the usual [Lie bracket](wikipedia://en/17944), which follows from the [Jacobi identity](wikipedia://en/294370).

The usual [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921), *D* = *d*/*dx*, is an operator on polynomials. By straightforward computation, its Pincherle derivative is

$$D'= \left({d \over {dx}}\right)' = \operatorname{Id}_{\mathbb K [x]} = 1.$$

This formula generalizes to

$$(D^n)'= \left({{d^n} \over {dx^n}}\right)' = nD^{n-1},$$

by [induction](wikipedia://en/18881). This proves that the Pincherle derivative of a [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886)

$$\partial = \sum a_n {{d^n} \over {dx^n} } = \sum a_n D^n$$

is also a differential operator, so that the Pincherle derivative is a derivation of $\operatorname{Diff}(\mathbb K [x])$.

When $\mathbb{K}$ has [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) zero, the shift operator

$$S_h(f)(x) = f(x+h) \,$$

can be written as

$$S_h = \sum_{n \ge 0} {{h^n} \over {n!} }D^n$$

by the [Taylor formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_formula). Its Pincherle derivative is then

$$S_h' = \sum_{n \ge 1} {{h^n} \over {(n-1)!} }D^{n-1} = h \cdot S_h.$$

In other words, the shift operators are [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) of the Pincherle derivative, whose spectrum is the whole space of scalars $\mathbb{K}$.

If *T* is [shift-equivariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shift-equivariant), that is, if *T* commutes with *S*<sub>*h*</sub> or $[T,S_h] = 0$, then we also have $[T',S_h] = 0$, so that $T'$ is also shift-equivariant and for the same shift $h$.

The "discrete-time delta operator"

$$(\delta f)(x) = {{ f(x+h) - f(x) } \over h }$$

is the operator

$$\delta = {1 \over h} (S_h - 1),$$

whose Pincherle derivative is the shift operator $\delta' = S_h$.
