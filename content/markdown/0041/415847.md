# Rewriting

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rewriting"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics), [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), and [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), **rewriting** covers a wide range of methods of replacing subterms of a [formula](wikipedia://en/404582) with other terms. Such methods may be achieved by **rewriting systems** (also known as **rewrite systems**, **rewrite engines**,[^o2][^o3] or **reduction systems**). In their most basic form, they consist of a set of objects, plus [relations](wikipedia://en/22517558) on how to transform those objects.

:::

Rewriting can be [non-deterministic](wikipedia://en/665957). One rule to rewrite a term could be applied in many different ways to that term, or more than one rule could be applicable. Rewriting systems then do not provide an [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) for changing one term to another, but a set of possible rule applications. When combined with an appropriate algorithm, however, rewrite systems can be viewed as [computer programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_program), and several [theorem provers](wikipedia://en/2546)[^o4] and [declarative programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declarative_programming_language) are based on term rewriting.[^o5][^o6]

## Example cases {#Example_cases}

### Logic {#Logic}

In [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), the procedure for obtaining the [conjunctive normal form](wikipedia://en/73342) (CNF) of a formula can be implemented as a rewriting system.[^o9] For example, the rules of such a system would be:

$\neg\neg A \to A$ ([double negation elimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_negation_elimination))

$\neg(A \land B) \to \neg A \lor \neg B$ ([De Morgan's laws](wikipedia://en/64669))

$$\neg(A \lor B) \to \neg A \land\neg B$$

$(A \land B) \lor C \to (A \lor C) \land (B \lor C)$ ([distributivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributivity))

$A \lor (B \land C) \to (A \lor B) \land (A \lor C),$[^o10]

For each rule, each [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109) denotes a subexpression, and the symbol ($\to$) indicates that an expression matching the left hand side of it can be rewritten to one matching the right hand side of it. In such a system, each rule is a [logical equivalence](wikipedia://en/169324), so performing a rewrite on an expression by these rules does not change the truth value of it. Other useful rewriting systems in logic may not preserve truth values, see e.g. [equisatisfiability](wikipedia://en/15895296).

### Arithmetic {#Arithmetic}

Term rewriting systems can be employed to compute arithmetic operations on [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474). To this end, each such number has to be encoded as a [term](wikipedia://en/24885593). The simplest encoding is the one used in the [Peano axioms](wikipedia://en/25005), based on the constant $0$ (zero) and the [successor function](wikipedia://en/325637) $S$. For example, the numbers 0, 1, 2, and 3 are represented by the terms $0$, $S(0)$, $S(S(0))$, and $S(S(S(0)))$, respectively. The following term rewriting system can then be used to compute sum and product of given natural numbers.[^o12]

$$\begin{aligned} A + 0 &\to A & \textrm{(1)}, \\ A + S(B) &\to S (A + B) & \textrm{(2)}, \\ A \cdot 0 &\to 0 & \textrm{(3)}, \\ A \cdot S(B) &\to A + (A \cdot B) & \textrm{(4)}. \end{aligned}$$

For example, the computation of $2+2$ to result in $4$ can be duplicated by term rewriting as follows:

$S(S(0)) + S(S(0))$ $\;\;\stackrel{(2)}{\to}\;\;$ $S( \; S(S(0)) + S(0) \; )$ $\;\;\stackrel{(2)}{\to}\;\;$ $S(S( \; S(S(0)) + 0 \; ))$ $\;\;\stackrel{(1)}{\to}\;\;$ $S(S( S(S(0)) )),$

where the notation above each arrow indicates the rule used for each rewrite.

As another example, the computation of $2\cdot2$ looks like:

$S(S(0)) \cdot S(S(0))$ $\;\;\stackrel{(4)}{\to}\;\;$ $S(S(0)) + S(S(0)) \cdot S(0)$ $\;\;\stackrel{(4)}{\to}\;\;$ $S(S(0)) + S(S(0)) + S(S(0)) \cdot 0$ $\;\;\stackrel{(3)}{\to}\;\;$ $S(S(0)) + S(S(0)) + 0$ $\;\;\stackrel{(1)}{\to}\;\;$ $S(S(0)) + S(S(0))$ $\;\;\stackrel{\textrm{s.a.}}{\to}\;\;$ $S(S( S(S(0)) )),$

where the last step comprises the previous example computation.

### Linguistics {#Linguistics}

In [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics), [phrase structure rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrase_structure_rule), also called **rewrite rules**, are used in some systems of [generative grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_grammar),[^o14] as a means of generating the grammatically correct sentences of a language. Such a rule typically takes the form $\rm A \rightarrow X$, where A is a [syntactic category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntactic_category) label, such as [noun phrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noun_phrase) or [sentence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence_%28linguistics%29), and X is a sequence of such labels or [morphemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morpheme), expressing the fact that A can be replaced by X in generating the constituent structure of a sentence. For example, the rule $\rm S \rightarrow NP\ VP$ means that a sentence can consist of a noun phrase (NP) followed by a [verb phrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verb_phrase) (VP); further rules will specify what sub-constituents a noun phrase and a verb phrase can consist of, and so on.

## Abstract rewriting systems {#Abstract_rewriting_systems}

From the above examples, it is clear that we can think of rewriting systems in an abstract manner. We need to specify a set of objects and the rules that can be applied to transform them. The most general (unidimensional) setting of this notion is called an *abstract reduction system*[^o16] or *abstract rewriting system* (abbreviated *ARS*).[^o17] An ARS is simply a set *A* of objects, together with a [binary relation](wikipedia://en/3931) → on *A* called the *reduction relation*, *rewrite relation*[^o18] or just *reduction*.[^o16]

Many notions and notations can be defined in the general setting of an ARS. $\overset{*}\rightarrow$ is the [reflexive transitive closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflexive_transitive_closure) of $\rightarrow$. $\leftrightarrow$ is the [symmetric closure](wikipedia://en/22603634) of $\rightarrow$. $\overset{*}{\leftrightarrow}$ is the [reflexive transitive symmetric closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflexive_transitive_symmetric_closure) of $\rightarrow$. The [word problem](wikipedia://en/3852079) for an ARS is determining, given *x* and *y*, whether $x \overset{*}{\leftrightarrow} y$. An object *x* in *A* is called *reducible* if there exists some other *y* in *A* such that $x \rightarrow y$; otherwise it is called *irreducible* or a [normal form](wikipedia://en/5085531). An object *y* is called a "normal form of *x*" if $x \stackrel{*}{\rightarrow} y$, and *y* is irreducible. If the normal form of *x* is unique, then this is usually denoted with $x{\downarrow}$. If every object has at least one normal form, the ARS is called *normalizing*. $x \downarrow y$ or *x* and *y* are said to be *joinable* if there exists some *z* with the property that $x \overset{*}{\rightarrow} z \overset{*}{\leftarrow} y$. An ARS is said to possess the [Church–Rosser property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church–Rosser_property) if $x \overset{*}{\leftrightarrow} y$ implies $x \downarrow y$. An ARS is [confluent](wikipedia://en/3755377) if for all *w*, *x*, and *y* in *A*, $x \overset{*}{\leftarrow} w \overset{*}{\rightarrow} y$ implies $x \downarrow y$. An ARS is *locally confluent* if and only if for all *w*, *x*, and *y* in *A*, $x \leftarrow w \rightarrow y$ implies $x\mathbin\downarrow y$. An ARS is said to be *terminating* or *noetherian* if there is no infinite chain $x_0 \rightarrow x_1 \rightarrow x_2 \rightarrow \cdots$. A confluent and terminating ARS is called *convergent* or *canonical*.

Important theorems for abstract rewriting systems are that an ARS is [confluent](wikipedia://en/3755377) [iff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iff) it has the Church–Rosser property, [Newman's lemma](wikipedia://en/6038529) (a terminating ARS is confluent if and only if it is locally confluent), and that the [word problem](wikipedia://en/3852079) for an ARS is [undecidable](wikipedia://en/15631055) in general.

## String rewriting systems {#String_rewriting_systems}

A *string rewriting system* (SRS), also known as *semi-Thue system*, exploits the [free monoid](wikipedia://en/523166) structure of the [strings](wikipedia://en/27701) (words) over an [alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabet_%28computer_science%29) to extend a rewriting relation, $R$, to *all* strings in the alphabet that contain left- and respectively right-hand sides of some rules as [substrings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substring). Formally a semi-Thue system is a [tuple](wikipedia://en/132729) $(\Sigma, R)$ where $\Sigma$ is a (usually finite) alphabet, and $R$ is a binary relation between some (fixed) strings in the alphabet, called the set of *rewrite rules*. The *one-step rewriting relation* $\underset{R}\rightarrow$ induced by $R$ on $\Sigma^*$ is defined as: if $s, t \in \Sigma^*$ are any strings, then $s \underset{R}\rightarrow t$ if there exist $x, y, u, v \in \Sigma^*$ such that $s = xuy$, $t = xvy$, and $u R v$. Since $\underset{R}\rightarrow$ is a relation on $\Sigma^*$, the pair $(\Sigma^*, \underset{R}\rightarrow)$ fits the definition of an abstract rewriting system. Since the empty string is in $\Sigma^*$, $R$ is a subset of $\underset{R}\rightarrow$. If the relation $R$ is [symmetric](wikipedia://en/200459), then the system is called a *Thue system*.

In a SRS, the reduction relation $\overset{*}\underset{R}\rightarrow$ is compatible with the monoid operation, meaning that $x \overset{*}\underset{R}\rightarrow y$ implies $uxv \overset{*}\underset{R}\rightarrow uyv$ for all strings $x, y, u, v \in \Sigma^*$. Similarly, the reflexive transitive symmetric closure of $\underset{R}\rightarrow$, denoted $\overset{*}{\underset R \leftrightarrow}$, is a [congruence](wikipedia://en/48167), meaning it is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) (by definition) and it is also compatible with string concatenation. The relation $\overset{*}\underset{R} \leftrightarrow$ is called the *Thue congruence* generated by $R$. In a Thue system, i.e. if $R$ is symmetric, the rewrite relation $\overset{*}\underset{R}\rightarrow$ coincides with the Thue congruence $\overset{*}{\underset R \leftrightarrow}$.

The notion of a semi-Thue system essentially coincides with the [presentation of a monoid](wikipedia://en/6730121). Since $\overset{*}{\underset R \leftrightarrow}$ is a congruence, we can define the [factor monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_monoid) $\mathcal{M}_R = \Sigma^*/\overset{*}{\underset R \leftrightarrow}$ of the free monoid $\Sigma^*$ by the Thue congruence. If a monoid $\mathcal{M}$ is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) with $\mathcal{M}_R$, then the semi-Thue system $(\Sigma, R)$ is called a [monoid presentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoid_presentation) of $\mathcal{M}$.

We immediately get some very useful connections with other areas of algebra. For example, the alphabet $\{ a,b \}$ with the rules $\{ ab \rightarrow \varepsilon, ba \rightarrow \varepsilon \}$, where $\varepsilon$ is the [empty string](wikipedia://en/835827), is a presentation of the [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) on one generator. If instead the rules are just $\{ ab \rightarrow \varepsilon \}$, then we obtain a presentation of the [bicyclic monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicyclic_monoid). Thus semi-Thue systems constitute a natural framework for solving the [word problem](wikipedia://en/3852079) for monoids and groups. In fact, every monoid has a presentation of the form $(\Sigma, R)$, i.e. it may always be presented by a semi-Thue system, possibly over an infinite alphabet.

The word problem for a semi-Thue system is undecidable in general; this result is sometimes known as the *Post–Markov theorem*.[^o20]

## Term rewriting systems {#Term_rewriting_systems}

*Image: Triangle diagram of rewrite rule application svg.svg.* **Pic.1:** Schematic triangle diagram of application of a rewrite rule $l \longrightarrow r$ at position $p$ in a term, with matching substitution $\sigma$

*Image: Example term for position, path, depth, match svg.svg.* **Pic.2:** Rule lhs term $x*(y*z)$ matching in term $\frac{a*((a+1)*(a+2))}{1*(2*3)}$

A **term rewriting system** (**TRS**) is a rewriting system whose objects are *[terms](wikipedia://en/24885593)*, which are expressions with nested sub-expressions. For example, the system shown under above is a term rewriting system. The terms in this system are composed of binary operators $(\vee)$ and $(\wedge)$ and the unary operator $(\neg)$. Also present in the rules are variables, which represent any possible term (though a single variable always represents the same term throughout a single rule).

In contrast to string rewriting systems, whose objects are sequences of symbols, the objects of a term rewriting system form a [term algebra](wikipedia://en/2216678). A term can be visualized as a tree of symbols, the set of admitted symbols being fixed by a given [signature](wikipedia://en/7788156). As a formalism, term rewriting systems have the full power of [Turing machines](wikipedia://en/30403), that is, every [computable function](wikipedia://en/1139338) can be defined by a term rewriting system.[^o22]

Some programming languages are based on term rewriting. One such example is Pure, a functional programming language for mathematical applications.[^o23][^o24]

### Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

A *rewrite rule* is a pair of [terms](wikipedia://en/24885593), commonly written as $l \rightarrow r$, to indicate that the left-hand side $l$ can be replaced by the right-hand side $r$. A *term rewriting system* is a set $R$ of such rules. A rule $l \rightarrow r$ can be *applied* to a term $s$ if the left term $l$ [matches](wikipedia://en/24885593#Operations_with_terms) some [subterm](wikipedia://en/24885593#Operations_with_terms) of $s$, that is, if there is some [substitution](wikipedia://en/5438948) $\sigma$ such that the subterm of $s$ rooted at some [position](wikipedia://en/24885593#Operations_with_terms) $p$ is the result of applying the substitution $\sigma$ to the term $l$. The subterm matching the left hand side of the rule is called a **redex** or **reducible expression**.[^o27] The result term $t$ of this rule application is then the result of [replacing the subterm](wikipedia://en/24885593#Operations_with_terms) at position $p$ in $s$ by the term $r$ with the substitution $\sigma$ applied, see picture 1. In this case, $s$ is said to be *rewritten in one step*, or *rewritten directly*, to $t$ by the system $R$, formally denoted as $s \rightarrow_R t$, $s \underset{R}\rightarrow t$, or as $s \overset{R}\rightarrow t$ by some authors.

If a term $t_1$ can be rewritten in several steps into a term $t_n$, that is, if $t_1 \underset{R}\rightarrow t_2 \underset{R}\rightarrow \cdots \underset{R}\rightarrow t_n$, the term $t_1$ is said to be *rewritten* to $t_n$, formally denoted as $t_1 \overset{+}\underset{R}\rightarrow t_n$. In other words, the relation $\overset{+}\underset{R}\rightarrow$ is the [transitive closure](wikipedia://en/292239) of the relation $\underset{R}\rightarrow$; often, also the notation $\overset{*}\underset{R}\rightarrow$ is used to denote the [reflexive-transitive closure](wikipedia://en/293802#Binary_relation_closures) of $\underset{R}\rightarrow$, that is, $s \overset{*}\underset{R}\rightarrow t$ if $s = t$ or $s \overset{+}\underset{R}\rightarrow t$.[^o28] A term rewriting given by a set $R$ of rules can be viewed as an abstract rewriting system as defined above, with terms as its objects and $\underset{R}\rightarrow$ as its rewrite relation.

For example, $x*(y*z) \rightarrow (x*y)*z$ is a rewrite rule, commonly used to establish a normal form with respect to the associativity of $*$. That rule can be applied at the numerator in the term $\frac{a*((a+1)*(a+2))}{1*(2*3)}$ with the matching substitution $\{ x \mapsto a, \; y \mapsto a+1, \; z \mapsto a+2 \}$, see picture 2.[^o29] Applying that substitution to the rule's right-hand side yields the term $(a*(a+1))*(a+2)$, and replacing the numerator by that term yields $\frac{(a*(a+1))*(a+2)}{1*(2*3)}$, which is the result term of applying the rewrite rule. Altogether, applying the rewrite rule has achieved what is called "applying the associativity law for $*$ to $\frac{a*((a+1)*(a+2))}{1*(2*3)}$" in elementary algebra. Alternately, the rule could have been applied to the denominator of the original term, yielding $\frac{a*((a+1)*(a+2))}{(1*2)*3}$.

:::

::: {#o31 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Termination (rewriting)"}

### Termination {#Termination}

Termination issues of rewrite systems in general are handled in *[Abstract rewriting system#Termination and convergence](wikipedia://en/23947591#Termination_and_convergence)*. For term rewriting systems in particular, the following additional subtleties are to be considered.

Termination even of a system consisting of one rule with a [linear](wikipedia://en/24885593#Ground_and_linear_terms) left-hand side is undecidable.[^o32][^o33] Termination is also undecidable for systems using only unary function symbols; however, it is decidable for finite [ground](wikipedia://en/24885593#Ground_and_linear_terms) systems.[^o34]

The following term rewrite system is normalizing,[^o35] but not terminating,[^o36] and not confluent:[^o37]

$$\begin{aligned} f(x,x) & \rightarrow g(x) , \\ f(x,g(x)) & \rightarrow b , \\ h(c,x) & \rightarrow f(h(x,c),h(x,x)) . \\ \end{aligned}$$

The following two examples of terminating term rewrite systems are due to Toyama:[^o38]

$$f(0,1,x) \rightarrow f(x,x,x)$$

and

$$g(x,y) \rightarrow x,$$

$$g(x,y) \rightarrow y.$$

Their union is a non-terminating system, since

$$\begin{aligned} & f(g(0,1),g(0,1),g(0,1)) \\ \rightarrow & f(0,g(0,1),g(0,1)) \\ \rightarrow & f(0,1,g(0,1)) \\ \rightarrow & f(g(0,1),g(0,1),g(0,1)) \\ \rightarrow & \cdots \end{aligned}$$

This result disproves a conjecture of [Dershowitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nachum_Dershowitz),[^o39] who claimed that the union of two terminating term rewrite systems $R_1$ and $R_2$ is again terminating if all left-hand sides of $R_1$ and right-hand sides of $R_2$ are [linear](wikipedia://en/24885593#Ground_and_linear_terms), and there are no "*overlaps*" between left-hand sides of $R_1$ and right-hand sides of $R_2$. All these properties are satisfied by Toyama's examples.

See [Rewrite order](wikipedia://en/42966879) and [Path ordering (term rewriting)](wikipedia://en/42973489) for ordering relations used in termination proofs for term rewriting systems.

:::

### Higher-order rewriting systems {#Higher-order_rewriting_systems}

Higher-order rewriting systems are a generalization of first-order term rewriting systems to [lambda terms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambda_term), allowing higher order functions and bound variables.[^o41] Various results about first-order TRSs can be reformulated for HRSs as well.[^o42]

### Graph rewriting systems {#Graph_rewriting_systems}

[Graph rewrite systems](wikipedia://en/1252846) are another generalization of term rewrite systems, operating on [graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_%28graph_theory%29) instead of ([ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_term)-) [terms](wikipedia://en/24885593) / their corresponding [tree](wikipedia://en/48560) representation.

::: {#o45 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Trace rewriting system"}

## Trace rewriting systems {#Trace_rewriting_systems}

[Trace theory](wikipedia://en/10815131) provides a means for discussing multiprocessing in more formal terms, such as via the [trace monoid](wikipedia://en/10739141) and the [history monoid](wikipedia://en/10833335). Rewriting can be performed in trace systems as well.
