# Expander walk sampling

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Expander walk sampling"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) discipline of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **expander walk sampling theorem** intuitively states that [sampling](wikipedia://en/160361) [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) in an [expander graph](wikipedia://en/9313) by doing relatively short [random walk](wikipedia://en/235451#On_graphs) can simulate sampling the vertices [independently](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence) from a [uniform distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28discrete%29). The earliest version of this theorem is due to (Ajtai Komlós Szemerédi 1987), and the more general version is typically attributed to (Gillman 1998).

:::

::: {#o5 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Let $G=(V,E)$ be an n-vertex [expander graph](wikipedia://en/9313) with positively weighted edges, and let $A\subset V$. Let $P$ denote the [stochastic matrix](wikipedia://en/217313) of the graph, and let $\lambda_2$ be the second largest [eigenvalue](wikipedia://en/2161429) of $P$. Let $y_0, y_1, \ldots, y_{k-1}$ denote the vertices encountered in a $(k-1)$-step random walk on $G$ starting at vertex $y_0$, and let $\pi (A):=$ $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty} \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i = 0}^{k-1} \mathbf{1}_A(y_i)$. Where $\mathbf{1}_A(y)\begin{cases} 1, & \text{if }y \in A \\ 0, & \text{otherwise }\end{cases}$

(It is well known[^o6] that almost all trajectories $y_0, y_1, \ldots, y_{k-1}$ converges to some limiting point, $\pi (A)$, as $k \rightarrow$$\infty$.)

The theorem states that for a weighted graph $G=(V,E)$ and a random walk $y_0, y_1, \ldots, y_{k-1}$ where $y_0$ is chosen by an initial distribution $\mathbf{q}$, for all $\gamma > 0$, we have the following bound:

$$\Pr\left[\bigg| \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} \mathbf{1}_A(y_i) - \pi(A)\bigg| \geq \gamma\right] \leq C e^{-\frac{1}{20} (\gamma^2 (1-\lambda_2) k)}.$$

Where $C$ is dependent on $\mathbf{q}, G$ and $A$.

The theorem gives a bound for the [rate of convergence](wikipedia://en/999701) to $\pi(A)$ with respect to the length of the random walk, hence giving a more efficient method to estimate $\pi(A)$ compared to independent sampling the vertices of $G$.

:::

::: {#o8 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

In order to prove the theorem, we provide a few definitions followed by three lemmas.

Let $\it{w}_{xy}$ be the weight of the edge $xy\in E(G)$ and let $\it{w}_x=\sum_{y:xy\in E(G)}\it{w}_{xy}.$ Denote by $\pi(x):=\it{w}_x/\sum_{y\in V} \it{w}_y$. Let $\frac{\mathbf{q}}{\sqrt\pi}$ be the matrix with entries$\frac{\mathbf{q}(x)}{\sqrt{\pi(x)}}$ , and let $N_{\pi,\mathbf{q}}=||\frac{\mathbf{q}}{\sqrt\pi}||_{2}$.

Let $D=\text{diag}(1/\it{w}_i )$ and $M=(\it{w}_{ij})$. Let $P(r)=PE_r$ where $P$ is the stochastic matrix, $E_r=\text{diag}(e^{r\mathbf{1}_A})$ and $r \ge 0$. Then:

$$P = \sqrt{D}S\sqrt{D^{-1}} \qquad \text{and} \qquad P(r) = \sqrt{DE_r^{-1}}S(r)\sqrt{E_rD^{-1}}$$

Where $S:=\sqrt{D}M\sqrt{D} \text{ and } S(r) := \sqrt{DE_r}M\sqrt{DE_r}$. As $S$ and $S(r)$ are symmetric, they have real eigenvalues. Therefore, as the eigenvalues of $S(r)$ and $P(r)$ are equal, the eigenvalues of $P(r)$ are real. Let $\lambda(r)$ and $\lambda_2(r)$ be the first and second largest eigenvalue of $P(r)$ respectively.

For convenience of notation, let $t_k=\frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} \mathbf{1}_A(y_i)$, $\epsilon=\lambda-\lambda_2$, $\epsilon_r=\lambda(r)-\lambda_2(r)$, and let $\mathbf{1}$ be the all-1 vector.

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=inline-head name="Lemma 1"}

**Lemma 1**

$$\Pr\left[t_k- \pi(A) \ge \gamma\right] \leq e^{-rk(\pi(A)+\gamma)+k\log\lambda(r)}(\mathbf{q}P(r)^k\mathbf{1})/\lambda(r)^k$$

:::

::: {#o10 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof:**

By [Markov's inequality](wikipedia://en/238232),

$$\begin{alignedat}{2} \Pr\left[t_k \ge \pi(A) +\gamma\right] =\Pr[e^{rt_k}\ge e^{rk(\pi(A)+\gamma)}]\leq e^{-rk(\pi(A)+\gamma)}E_\mathbf{q}e^{rt_k} \end{alignedat}$$

Where $E_\mathbf{q}$ is the expectation of $x_0$ chosen according to the probability distribution $\mathbf{q}$. As this can be interpreted by summing over all possible trajectories $x_0,x_1,.. .,x_k$, hence:

$$E_{\mathbf{q}}e^{rt}=\sum_{x_1,x_2,...,x_k}e^{rt}\mathbb{q}(x_0)\Pi_{i=1}^kp_{x_{i-1}x_i}=\mathbf{q}P(r)^k\mathbf{1}$$

Combining the two results proves the lemma.

:::

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=inline-head name="Lemma 2"}

**Lemma 2**

For $0\le r \le 1$,

$$(\mathbf{q}P(r)^k\mathbf{1})/\lambda(r)^k\le (1+r)N_{\pi,\mathbf{q}}$$

:::

::: {#o12 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof:**

As eigenvalues of $P(r)$ and $S(r)$ are equal,

$$\begin{aligned} (\mathbf{q}P(r)^k\mathbf{1})/\lambda(r)^k&= (\mathbf{q}P\sqrt{DE_r^{-1}}S(r)^k \sqrt{D^{-1}E_r}\mathbf{1})/\lambda(r)^k\\ &\le e^{r/2}||\frac{\mathbf{q}}{\sqrt{\pi}}||_2||S(r)^k||_2||\sqrt{\pi}||_2/\lambda(r)^k\\ &\le e^{r/2}N_{\pi,\mathbf{q}}\\ &\le (1+r)N_{\pi,\mathbf{q}}\qquad \square \end{aligned}$$

:::

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=inline-head name="Lemma 3"}

**Lemma 3**

If $r$ is a real number such that $0\le e^r-1\le \epsilon/4$,

$$\log\lambda(r)\le r\pi(A)+5r^2/\epsilon$$

:::

::: {#o14 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof summary"}

**Proof summary:**

We Taylor expand $\log \lambda(y)$ about point $r=z$ to get:

$$\log\lambda(r)= \log\lambda(z)+m_z(r-z)+(r-z)^2\int_0^1 (1-t)V_{z+(r-z)t}dt$$

Where $m_x \text{ and } V_x$ are first and second derivatives of $\log \lambda(r)$ at $r=x$. We show that $m_0=\lim_{k \to \infty}t_k=\pi(A).$ We then prove that (i) $\epsilon_r\ge 3\epsilon/4$ by matrix manipulation, and then prove (ii)$V_r\le 10/\epsilon$ using (i) and [Cauchy's estimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy's_estimate) from [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759).

The results combine to show that

$$\begin{aligned} \log\lambda(r)= \log\lambda(0)+m_0r+r^2\int_0^1 (1-t)V_{rt}dt \le r\pi(A)+5r^2/\epsilon \end{aligned}$$

A line to line proof can be found in Gilman (1998)<https://epubs.siam.org/doi/pdf/10.1137/S0097539794268765#page23>

:::

::: {#o15 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof of theorem"}

**Proof of theorem**

Combining lemma 2 and lemma 3, we get that

$$\Pr[t_k-\pi(A)\ge \gamma]\le(1+r)N_{\pi,\mathbf{q}}e^{-k(r\gamma-5r^2/\epsilon)}$$

Interpreting the exponent on the right hand side of the inequality as a quadratic in $r$ and minimising the expression, we see that

$$\Pr[t_k-\pi(A)\ge \gamma]\le(1+\gamma\epsilon/10)N_{\pi,\mathbf{q}}e^{-k\gamma^2\epsilon/20}$$

A similar bound

$$\Pr[t_k-\pi(A)\le - \gamma]\le (1+\gamma\epsilon/10)N_{\pi,\mathbf{q}}e^{-k\gamma^2\epsilon/20}$$

holds, hence setting $C=2(1+\gamma\epsilon/10)N_{\pi,\mathbf{q}}$ gives the desired result.

:::

::: {#o17 .application cue=heading name="Uses"}

## Uses {#Uses}

This theorem is useful in randomness reductions in the study of [derandomization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derandomization),[^o18] showing that expander random walks are good pseudorandom generators against various classes of test functions. Sampling from an expander walk is an example of a randomness-efficient [sampler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_%28statistics%29). Note that the number of [bits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit) used in sampling $k$ independent samples from $f$ is $k \log n$, whereas if we sample from an infinite family of constant-degree expanders this costs only $\log n + O(k)$. Such families exist and are efficiently constructible, e.g. the [Ramanujan graphs](wikipedia://en/849412) of [Lubotzky](wikipedia://en/22754130)-Phillips-[Sarnak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Sarnak).
