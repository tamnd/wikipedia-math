# Beltrami identity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Beltrami identity"}

The **Beltrami identity**, named after [Eugenio Beltrami](wikipedia://en/543760), is a special case of the [Euler–Lagrange equation](wikipedia://en/294995) in the [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882).

:::

The Euler–Lagrange equation serves to extremize action [functionals](wikipedia://en/1374948) of the form

$$I[u]=\int_a^b L[x,u(x),u'(x)] \, dx \, ,$$

where $a$ and $b$ are constants and $u'(x) = \frac{du}{dx}$.[^o2]

If $\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = 0$, then the Euler–Lagrange equation reduces to the Beltrami identity,

where $C$ is a constant.[^o4][^o5]

## Derivation {#Derivation}

By the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113), the derivative of $L$ is

$$\frac{dL}{dx} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} \frac{dx}{dx} + \frac{\partial L}{\partial u} \frac{du}{dx} + \frac{\partial L}{\partial u'} \frac{du'}{dx} \, .$$

Because $\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = 0$, we write

$$\frac{dL}{dx} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial u} u' + \frac{\partial L}{\partial u'} u'' \, .$$

We have an expression for $\frac{\partial L}{\partial u}$ from the Euler–Lagrange equation,

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial u} = \frac{d}{dx} \frac{\partial L}{\partial u'} \,$$

that we can substitute in the above expression for $\frac{dL}{dx}$ to obtain

$$\frac{dL}{dx} =u'\frac{d}{dx} \frac{\partial L}{\partial u'} + u''\frac{\partial L}{\partial u'} \, .$$

By the [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988), the right side is equivalent to

$$\frac{dL}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx} \left( u' \frac{\partial L}{\partial u'} \right) \, .$$

By [integrating](wikipedia://en/2823) both sides and putting both terms on one side, we get the Beltrami identity,

$$L - u'\frac{\partial L}{\partial u'} = C \, .$$

## Applications {#Applications}

### Solution to the brachistochrone problem {#Solution_to_the_brachistochrone_problem}

*Image: Brachistochrone.gif.* The solution to the brachistochrone problem is the cycloid.

An example of an application of the Beltrami identity is the [brachistochrone problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brachistochrone_problem), which involves finding the curve $y = y(x)$ that minimizes the integral

$$I[y] = \int_0^a \sqrt { {1+y'^{\, 2}} \over y } dx \, .$$

The integrand

$$L(y,y') = \sqrt{ {1+y'^{\, 2}} \over y }$$

does not depend explicitly on the variable of integration $x$, so the Beltrami identity applies,

$$L-y'\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}=C \, .$$

Substituting for $L$ and simplifying,

$$y(1+y'^{\, 2}) = 1/C^2 ~~\text {(constant)} \, ,$$

which can be solved with the result put in the form of [parametric equations](wikipedia://en/576108)

$$x = A(\phi - \sin \phi)$$

$$y = A(1 - \cos \phi)$$

with $A$ being half the above constant, $\frac{1}{2C^{2}}$, and $\phi$ being a variable. These are the parametric equations for a [cycloid](wikipedia://en/41638).[^o10]

### Solution to the catenary problem {#Solution_to_the_catenary_problem}

*Image: Kette Kettenkurve Catenary 2008 PD.JPG.* A chain hanging from points forms a [catenary](wikipedia://en/7163).

Consider a string with uniform density $\mu$ of length $l$ suspended from two points of equal height and at distance $D$. By the formula for [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401),

$$l = \int_S dS = \int_{s_1}^{s_2} \sqrt{1+y'^2}dx,$$

where $S$ is the path of the string, and $s_1$ and $s_2$ are the boundary conditions.

The curve has to minimize its potential energy

$$U = \int_S g\mu y\cdot dS = \int_{s_1}^{s_2} g\mu y\sqrt{1+y'^2} dx,$$

and is subject to the constraint

$$\int_{s_1}^{s_2} \sqrt{1+y'^2} dx = l ,$$

where $g$ is the force of gravity.

Because the independent variable $x$ does not appear in the integrand, the Beltrami identity may be used to express the path of the string as a [separable](wikipedia://en/387750) [first order differential equation](wikipedia://en/32742753)

$$L - y\prime \frac{\partial L}{\partial y\prime} = \mu gy\sqrt{1+y\prime ^2} + \lambda \sqrt{1+y\prime ^2} - \left[\mu gy\frac{y\prime ^2}{\sqrt{1+y\prime ^2}} + \lambda \frac{y\prime ^2}{\sqrt{1+y\prime ^2}}\right] = C,$$

where $\lambda$ is the [Lagrange multiplier](wikipedia://en/159974).

It is possible to simplify the differential equation as such:

$$\frac{g\rho y - \lambda }{\sqrt{1+y'^2}} = C.$$

Solving this equation gives the [hyperbolic cosine](wikipedia://en/7163), where $C_0$ is a second constant obtained from integration

$$y = \frac{C}{\mu g}\cosh \left[ \frac{\mu g}{C} (x + C_0) \right] - \frac{\lambda}{\mu g}.$$

The three unknowns $C$, $C_0$, and $\lambda$ can be solved for using the constraints for the string's endpoints and arc length $l$, though a closed-form solution is often very difficult to obtain.
