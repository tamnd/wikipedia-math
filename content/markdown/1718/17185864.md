# Steiner inellipse

*Image: Steiner Inellipse.svg.* The Steiner inellipse. According to [Marden's theorem](wikipedia://en/17185722), given the triangle with vertices $(1, 7), (7, 5), (3, 1)$, the [foci](wikipedia://en/1032607) of the inellipse are $(3, 5)$ and $(13/3, 11/3)$, since $\begin{aligned} & D_x(1+7i-x)(7+5i-x)(3+i-x) \\ & = -3\left(\tfrac{13}{3}+\tfrac{11}{3}i-x\right)(3+5i-x) \end{aligned}$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Steiner inellipse"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **Steiner inellipse**, **midpoint inellipse**, or **midpoint ellipse** of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) is the unique [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) inscribed in the triangle and [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to the sides at their [midpoints](wikipedia://en/437979). It is an example of an [inellipse](wikipedia://en/43943006). By comparison the [inscribed circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscribed_circle) and [Mandart inellipse](wikipedia://en/14974602) of a triangle are other inconics that are tangent to the sides, but not at the midpoints unless the triangle is [equilateral](wikipedia://en/173285). The Steiner inellipse is attributed by Dörrie[^o3] to [Jakob Steiner](wikipedia://en/567445), and a proof of its uniqueness is given by Dan Kalman.[^o4]

:::

The Steiner inellipse contrasts with the [Steiner circumellipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steiner_circumellipse), also called simply the Steiner ellipse, which is the unique ellipse that passes through the vertices of a given triangle and whose center is the triangle's [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926).

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Definition and properties"}

## Definition and properties {#Definition_and_properties}

::: {#o8 .definition cue=inline-head name="Definition"}

**Definition**

An ellipse that is tangent to the sides of a triangle $\triangle ABC$ at its midpoints $M_1,M_2,M_3$ is called the **Steiner inellipse** of $\triangle ABC$.

*Image: Steiner-inellipse-1.svg.* solid #333333 solid blue solid red dashed #e053bb 2px

*Image: Steiner-inellipse-0.svg.* solid #333333 solid blue solid red

**Properties:** For an arbitrary triangle $\triangle ABC$ with midpoints $M_1,M_2,M_3$ of its sides the following statements are true: a) There *exists* exactly one Steiner inellipse. b) The *center* of the Steiner inellipse is the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) $S$ of $\triangle ABC$. c1) The triangle $\triangle M_1M_2M_3$ has the same centroid $S$ and the Steiner inellipse of $\triangle ABC$ is the Steiner ellipse of the triangle $\triangle M_1M_2M_3.$ c2) The Steiner inellipse of a triangle is the *scaled* Steiner Ellipse with scaling factor 1/2 and the centroid as center. Hence both ellipses have the same [eccentricity](wikipedia://en/1239472), are *similar*. d) The *area* of the Steiner inellipse is $\tfrac{\pi}{3 \sqrt{3}}$-times the area of the triangle. e) The Steiner inellipse has the *greatest area* of all inellipses of the triangle. [^o9][^o14]

:::

::: {#o10 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof**

The proofs of properties a),b),c) are based on the following properties of an affine mapping: 1) any triangle can be considered as an affine image of an equilateral triangle. 2) Midpoints of sides are mapped onto midpoints and centroids on centroids. The center of an ellipse is mapped onto the center of its image. Hence its suffice to prove properties a),b),c) for an equilateral triangle: a) To any equilateral triangle there exists an [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle_and_excircles_of_a_triangle). It touches the sides at its midpoints. There is no other (non-degenerate) conic section with the same properties, because a conic section is determined by 5 points/tangents. b) By a simple calculation. c) The [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432) is mapped by a scaling, with factor 1/2 and the centroid as center, onto the incircle. The eccentricity is an invariant. d) The ratio of areas is invariant to affine transformations. So the ratio can be calculated for the equilateral triangle. e) See [Inellipse](wikipedia://en/43943006#Inellipse_with_the_greatest_area).

:::

:::

## Parametric representation and semi-axes {#Parametric_representation_and_semi-axes}

**Parametric representation:**

- Because a Steiner inellipse of a triangle $\triangle ABC$ is a scaled Steiner ellipse (factor 1/2, center is centroid) one gets a parametric representation derived from the trigonometric representation of the [Steiner ellipse](wikipedia://en/19106257#Parametric_representaion_and_equation) :

$$\vec x =\vec p(t)=\overrightarrow{OS}\; +\; {\color{blue}\frac 1 2 }\overrightarrow{SC}\; \cos t \;+\; \frac{1}{{\color{blue}2}\sqrt{3}}\overrightarrow{AB}\; \sin t \; , \quad 0\le t <2\pi\ .$$

- The **4 vertices** of the Steiner inellipse are

$$\vec p(t_0),\; \vec p(t_0\pm\frac{\pi}{2}),\; \vec p(t_0+\pi),$$

where $t_{0}$ is the solution of

$\cot (2t_0)= \tfrac{\vec f_1^{\, 2}-\vec f_2^{\, 2}}{2\vec f_1 \cdot \vec f_2}\quad$ with $\quad \vec f_1=\frac 1 2 \vec{SC},\quad \vec f_2=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}\vec{AB}\ .$

**Semi-axes:**

- With the abbreviations

$$\begin{aligned} M &:= {\color{blue} \frac 1 4} \left(\vec{SC}^2+\frac{1}{3}\vec{AB}^2 \right) \\ N &:= \frac{1}{{\color{blue}4}\sqrt{3}} \left|\det \left(\vec{SC},\vec{AB} \right)\right| \end{aligned}$$

one gets for the [semi-axes](wikipedia://en/23409699) $a, b$ (where $a > b$):

$$\begin{aligned} a &= \frac{1}{2} \left(\sqrt{M+2N}+\sqrt{M-2N} \right) \\ b &= \frac{1}{2} \left(\sqrt{M+2N}-\sqrt{M-2N} \right)\ . \end{aligned}$$

- The **linear eccentricity** $c$ of the Steiner inellipse is

$$c=\sqrt{a^2-b^2}=\dotsb=\sqrt{\sqrt{M^2-4N^2}}\ .$$

## Trilinear equation {#Trilinear_equation}

The equation of the Steiner inellipse in [trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790) for a triangle with side lengths $a, b, c$ (with these parameters having a different meaning than previously) is[^o2]

$$a^2x^2+b^2y^2+c^2z^2-2abxy-2bcyz-2cazx = 0$$

where $x$ is an arbitrary positive constant times the distance of a point from the side of length $a$, and similarly for $b$ and $c$ with the same multiplicative constant.

## Other properties {#Other_properties}

The lengths of the semi-major and semi-minor axes for a triangle with sides $a, b, c$ are[^o2]

$$\frac{1}{6}\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2 \pm 2Z},$$

where

$$Z=\sqrt{a^4+b^4+c^4-a^2b^2-b^2c^2-c^2a^2}.$$

According to [Marden's theorem](wikipedia://en/17185722),[^o4] if the three [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) of the triangle are the [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [zeros](wikipedia://en/23000#Solving_polynomial_equations) of a cubic [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000), then the [foci](wikipedia://en/1032607) of the Steiner inellipse are the zeros of the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of the polynomial.

The major axis of the Steiner inellipse is the [line of best orthogonal fit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_regression) for the vertices.[^o14]

Denote the centroid and the first and second [Fermat points](wikipedia://en/2666205) of a triangle as $G,F_+,F_-$ respectively. The major axis of the triangle's Steiner inellipse is the inner bisector of $\angle F_+GF_-.$ The lengths of the axes are $|GF_-| \pm |GF_+|\! ;$ that is, the sum and difference of the distances of the Fermat points from the centroid.[^o15]

The axes of the Steiner inellipse of a triangle are tangent to its [Kiepert parabola](wikipedia://en/69991093), the unique parabola that is tangent to the sides of the triangle and has the [Euler line](wikipedia://en/203622) as its [directrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directrix_%28conic_section%29).[^o15]

The foci of the Steiner inellipse of a triangle are the intersections of the inellipse's major axis and the circle with center on the minor axis and going through the Fermat points.[^o15]

As with any ellipse inscribed in a triangle $\triangle ABC$, letting the foci be $P$ and $Q$ we have[^o16]

$$\frac{\overline{PA} \cdot \overline{QA}}{\overline{CA} \cdot \overline{AB}} + \frac{\overline{PB} \cdot \overline{QB}}{\overline{AB} \cdot \overline{BC}} + \frac{\overline{PC} \cdot \overline{QC}}{\overline{BC} \cdot \overline{CA}} = 1.$$

## Graphical construction {#Graphical_construction}

*Image: ConstruccionInelipseSteiner.png.* Graphical construction of the Steiner inellipse. solid #3366ff 2px dotted #6699ff 2px dashed red 1px dotted red 2px dotted green 2px dashed red 3px

The Steiner inellipse of a triangle $\triangle ABC$ can be determined using a graphical construction based on establishing an [affine transformation](wikipedia://en/38449) between the given triangle and an [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285). Since this projection preserves the midpoints of the sides, it allows for a graphical correspondence between the [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle) of the equilateral triangle and the desired inellipse. The major and minor axes of an ellipse are always perpendicular. The construction uses a circle passing through $A$ and $A'$ (the original and transformed vertices) to find specific points $k_{1}$ and $k_{2}$ on the axis of affinity. The rays $\overline{k_{1}A}$ and $\overline{k_{2}A}$ identify the unique directions that remain perpendicular after the transformation, which must be the directions of the ellipse's semi-axes.[^o18]

To perform the construction, one side of the triangle is taken as the axis of affinity. (If the triangle is equilateral, the problem is trivial as the inellipse is its incircle; if it is isosceles, one of the two equal sides is typically chosen).

The procedure is as follows:

1. Given $\triangle ABC$, construct the equilateral triangle $\triangle A'BC$ on side $BC$.
1. Draw the segment $\overline{A'A}$ (the direction of affinity with axis $BC$) between the two triangles.
1. From the midpoint $m$ of $\overline{A'A}$, draw the [perpendicular bisector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular_bisector) until it intersects the line $BC$ (or its extension) at point $O$.
1. Draw the circle centered at $O$ that passes through $A'$ and $A$, which intersects the line $BC$ at points $k_{1}$ and $k_{2}$.
1. The direction of the ray $\overline{k_{1}A}$ coincides with the major axis of the Steiner inellipse, and the ray $\overline{k_{2}A}$ with the minor axis.
1. The endpoints of the semi-axes are obtained by projecting the points of the incircle of the equilateral triangle—specifically those where the tangents are parallel to rays $\overline{k_{1}A'}$ and $\overline{k_{2}A'}$ —it relies on the fact that affine transformations preserve parallelism between two lines in the same plane.

This same construction is applicable to the [Steiner circumellipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steiner_circumellipse), but uses the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432) of the equilateral triangle instead of the incircle.

::: {#o20 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

The Steiner inellipse of a triangle can be generalized to $n$-gons: some $n$-gons have an interior ellipse that is tangent to each side at the side's midpoint. Marden's theorem still applies: the foci of the Steiner inellipse are zeroes of the derivative of the polynomial whose zeroes are the vertices of the $n$-gon.[^o21]

:::
