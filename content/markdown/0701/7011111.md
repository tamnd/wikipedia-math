# Ramanujan prime

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ramanujan prime"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Ramanujan prime** is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) that satisfies a result proven by [Srinivasa Ramanujan](wikipedia://en/47717) relating to the [prime-counting function](wikipedia://en/331826).

:::

## Origins and definition {#Origins_and_definition}

In 1919, Ramanujan published a new proof of [Bertrand's postulate](wikipedia://en/232526) which, as he notes, was first proved by [Chebyshev](wikipedia://en/149568).[^o3] At the end of the two-page published paper, Ramanujan derived a generalized result, and that is:

$$\pi(x) - \pi\left( \frac x 2 \right) \ge 1,2,3,4,5,\ldots \text{ for all } x \ge 2, 11, 17, 29, 41, \ldots \text{ respectively}$$

where $\pi(x)$ is the [prime-counting function](wikipedia://en/331826), equal to the number of primes less than or equal to *x*.

The converse of this result is the definition of Ramanujan primes:

The *n*th Ramanujan prime is the least integer *R<sub>n</sub>* for which $\pi(x) - \pi(x/2) \ge n,$ for all *x* ≥ *R<sub>n</sub>*. In other words: Ramanujan primes are the least integers *R<sub>n</sub>* for which there are at least *n* primes between *x* and *x*/2 for all *x* ≥ *R<sub>n</sub>*.

The first five Ramanujan primes are thus 2, 11, 17, 29, and 41.

Note that the integer *R<sub>n</sub>* is necessarily a prime number: $\pi(x) - \pi(x/2)$ and, hence, $\pi(x)$ must increase by obtaining another prime at *x* = *R<sub>n</sub>*. Since $\pi(x) - \pi(x/2)$ can increase by at most 1,

$$\pi(R_n) - \pi\left( \frac{R_n} 2 \right) = n.$$

::: {#o6 .statement cue=named-heading name="Bounds and an asymptotic formula"}

## Bounds and an asymptotic formula {#Bounds_and_an_asymptotic_formula}

For all $n \geq 1$, the bounds

$$2n\ln2n < R_n < 4n\ln4n$$

hold. If $n > 1$, then also

$$p_{2n} < R_n < p_{3n}$$

where *p*<sub>*n*</sub> is the *n*th prime number.

As *n* tends to infinity, *R*<sub>*n*</sub> is [asymptotic](wikipedia://en/641995) to the 2*n*th prime, i.e.,

*R*<sub>*n*</sub> ~ *p*<sub>2*n*</sub> (*n* → ∞).

All these results were proved by Sondow (2009),[^o7] except for the upper bound *R*<sub>*n*</sub> \< *p*<sub>3*n*</sub> which was conjectured by him and proved by Laishram (2010).[^o8] The bound was improved by Sondow, Nicholson, and Noe (2011)[^o9] to

$$R_n \le \frac{41}{47} \ p_{3n}$$

which is the optimal form of *R*<sub>*n*</sub> ≤ *c·p*<sub>3*n*</sub> since it is an equality for *n* = 5.

:::
