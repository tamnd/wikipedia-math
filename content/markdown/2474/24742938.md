# Sinkhorn's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sinkhorn's theorem"}

**Sinkhorn's theorem** states that every [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) with positive entries can be written in a certain standard form.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Theorem"}

## Theorem {#Theorem}

If *A* is an [*n* × *n* matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859#Square_matrices) with strictly positive elements, then there exist [diagonal matrices](wikipedia://en/174080) *D*<sub>1</sub> and *D*<sub>2</sub> with strictly positive diagonal elements such that *D*<sub>1</sub>*AD*<sub>2</sub> is [doubly stochastic](wikipedia://en/2906180). The matrices *D*<sub>1</sub> and *D*<sub>2</sub> are unique up to multiplying the first matrix by a positive number and dividing the second one by the same number.[^o4] [^o5]

:::

## Sinkhorn–Knopp algorithm {#Sinkhorn–Knopp_algorithm}

A simple [iterative method](wikipedia://en/15237) to approach the double [stochastic matrix](wikipedia://en/217313) is to alternately rescale all rows and all columns of *A* to sum to 1. Sinkhorn and Knopp presented this algorithm and analyzed its convergence.[^o7] This is essentially the same as the [Iterative proportional fitting](wikipedia://en/23416870) algorithm, well known in survey statistics. The convergence of the Sinkhorn–Knopp algorithm has been extensively studied [^o8]. Following these foundational studies, several acceleration techniques have been proposed to improve its performance, such as Arnoldi-type methods [^o9].

## Analogues and extensions {#Analogues_and_extensions}

The following analogue for unitary matrices is also true: for every [unitary matrix](wikipedia://en/129222) *U* there exist two diagonal unitary matrices *L* and *R* such that *LUR* has each of its columns and rows summing to 1.[^o11]

The following extension to maps between matrices is also true (see Theorem 5[^o12] and also Theorem 4.7[^o13]): given a [Kraus operator](wikipedia://en/666107) that represents the quantum operation Φ mapping a [density matrix](wikipedia://en/62844) into another,

$$S \mapsto \Phi(S) = \sum_i B_i S B_i^*,$$

that is trace preserving,

$$\sum_i B_i^* B_i = I,$$

and, in addition, whose range is in the interior of the positive definite cone (strict positivity), there exist scalings *x*<sub>*j*</sub>, for *j* in {0,1}, that are positive definite so that the rescaled [Kraus operator](wikipedia://en/666107)

$$S \mapsto x_1\Phi(x_0^{-1}Sx_0^{-1})x_1 = \sum_i (x_1B_ix_0^{-1}) S (x_1B_ix_0^{-1})^*$$

is doubly stochastic. In other words, it is such that both,

$$x_1\Phi(x_0^{-1}I x_0^{-1})x_1 = I,$$

as well as for the adjoint,

$$x_0^{-1}\Phi^*(x_1I x_1)x_0^{-1} = I,$$

where I denotes the identity operator.

::: {#o15 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

In the 2010s Sinkhorn's theorem came to be used to find solutions of entropy-regularised [optimal transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimal_transport) problems.[^o16] This has been of interest in [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488) because such "Sinkhorn distances" can be used to evaluate the difference between data distributions and permutations.[^o17][^o18][^o19] This improves the training of machine learning algorithms, in situations where [maximum likelihood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_likelihood) training may not be the best method.

:::
