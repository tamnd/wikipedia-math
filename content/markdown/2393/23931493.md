# EWMA chart

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="EWMA chart"}

In [statistical quality control](wikipedia://en/593680), an **EWMA chart** (or **exponentially weighted moving average chart**) is a type of [control chart](wikipedia://en/435754) used to monitor either variables or attributes-type data using the monitored [business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_process) or [industrial process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_industrial_processes)'s entire history of output.[^o3] While other control charts treat rational subgroups of samples individually, the EWMA chart tracks the [exponentially-weighted moving average](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EWMA) of all prior sample means. EWMA weights samples in geometrically decreasing order so that the most recent samples are weighted most highly while the most distant samples contribute very little.[^o4]

:::

Although the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) is the basis of the EWMA chart, the chart is also relatively robust in the face of [non-normally distributed](wikipedia://en/21462) quality characteristics.[^o4] There is, however, an adaptation of the chart that accounts for quality characteristics that are better modeled by the [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144).[^o4] The chart monitors only the process mean; monitoring the process variability requires the use of some other technique.[^o4]

The EWMA control chart requires a knowledgeable person to select two parameters before setup:

1. The first parameter is λ, the weight given to the most recent rational subgroup mean. λ must satisfy 0 \< λ ≤ 1, but selecting the "right" value is a matter of personal preference and experience. One 2005 textbook recommends 0.05 ≤ λ ≤ 0.25,[^o4] while a 1986 journal article recommends 0.1 ≤ λ ≤ 0.3.[^o5]
1. The second parameter is L, the multiple of the rational subgroup standard deviation that establishes the control limits. L is typically set at 3 to match other control charts, but it may be necessary to reduce L slightly for small values of λ.[^o4]

Instead of plotting rational subgroup averages directly, the EWMA chart computes successive observations z<sub>i</sub> by computing the rational subgroup average, $\bar x_i$, and then combining that new subgroup average with the running average of all preceding observations, z<sub>i - 1</sub>, using the specially–chosen weight, λ, as follows:

$$z_i = \lambda \bar x_i + \left ( 1 - \lambda \right)z_{i - 1}.$$

The control limits for this chart type are $T \pm L\frac {S}{\sqrt n}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{2 - \lambda}\lbrack 1 - \left ( 1 - \lambda \right )^{2i} \rbrack}$ where T and S are the estimates of the long-term process mean and standard deviation established during control-chart setup and n is the number of samples in the rational subgroup. Note that the limits widen for each successive rational subgroup, approaching $\pm L\frac {\hat \sigma}{\sqrt n}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{2-\lambda}}$.[^o4]

The EWMA chart is sensitive to small shifts in the process mean, but does not match the ability of Shewhart-style charts (namely the [$\bar x$ and R](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xbar_and_R_chart) and [$\bar x$ and s charts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xbar_and_s_chart)) to detect larger shifts.[^o4] One author recommends superimposing the EWMA chart on top of a suitable Shewhart-style chart with widened control limits in order to detect both small and large shifts in the process mean.

Exponentially weighted moving variance (EWMVar) can be used to obtain a significance score or limits that automatically adjust to the observed data.[^o6][^o7]
