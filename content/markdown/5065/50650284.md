# Boolean Pythagorean triples problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Boolean Pythagorean triples problem"}

The **Boolean Pythagorean triples problem** is a problem from [Ramsey theory](wikipedia://en/157175) about whether the [positive integers](wikipedia://en/21474) can be colored red and blue so that no [Pythagorean triples](wikipedia://en/24172) consist of all red or all blue members. The Boolean Pythagorean triples problem was solved by [Marijn Heule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marijn_Heule), Oliver Kullmann and [Victor W. Marek](wikipedia://en/51001800) in May 2016 through a [computer-assisted proof](wikipedia://en/2840305), which showed that such a coloring is only possible up to the number 7824.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

The problem asks if it is possible to color each of the positive integers either red or blue, so that no Pythagorean triple of integers *a*, *b*, *c*, satisfying $a^2+b^2=c^2$ are all the same color.

For example, in the Pythagorean triple 3, 4, and 5 ($3^2+4^2=5^2$), if 3 and 4 are colored red, then 5 must be colored blue.

:::

## Solution {#Solution}

Marijn Heule, Oliver Kullmann and Victor W. Marek showed that such a coloring is only possible up to the number 7824. The actual statement of the theorem proved is

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

The set {1, . . . , 7824} can be partitioned into two parts, such that no part contains a Pythagorean triple, while this is impossible for {1, . . . , 7825}.[^o7]

:::

There are 2<sup>7825</sup> ≈ 3.63×10<sup>2355</sup> possible coloring combinations for the numbers up to [7825](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7825_%28number%29). These possible colorings were logically and algorithmically narrowed down to around a trillion (still highly complex) cases, and those, expressed as [Boolean satisfiability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_satisfiability) problems, were examined using a [SAT solver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAT_solver). Creating the proof took about 4 CPU-years of computation over a period of two days on the Stampede supercomputer at the [Texas Advanced Computing Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Advanced_Computing_Center) and generated a 200 terabyte propositional proof, which was compressed to 68 gigabytes.

The paper describing the proof was published in the SAT 2016 conference,[^o7] where it won the best paper award.[^o8] The figure below shows a possible family of colorings for the set {1,...,7824} with no monochromatic Pythagorean triple, and the white squares can be colored either red or blue while still satisfying this condition .

## Prize {#Prize}

In the 1980s [Ronald Graham](wikipedia://en/240694) offered a \$100 prize for the solution of the problem, which has now been awarded to [Marijn Heule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marijn_Heule).[^o2]

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

As of 2018, the problem is still open for more than 2 colors, that is, if there exists a *k*-coloring (*k* ≥ 3) of the positive integers such that no Pythagorean triples are the same color.[^o12]

:::
