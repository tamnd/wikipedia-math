# Bilinski dodecahedron

*Image: Bilinski dodecahedron.stl.* 3D model of a Bilinski dodecahedron

*Image: Bilinski dodecahedron.gif.*

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Bilinski dodecahedron"}

In geometry, the **Bilinski dodecahedron** is a [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) polyhedron with twelve [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) [golden rhombus](wikipedia://en/20825292) faces. It has the same topology as the [face-transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face-transitive) [rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631), but a different geometry. It is a [parallelohedron](wikipedia://en/25207334), a polyhedron that can [tile space](wikipedia://en/6008654) with translated copies of itself.

:::

## History {#History}

This shape appears in a 1752 book by [John Lodge Cowley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lodge_Cowley), labeled as the **dodecarhombus**.[^o4][^o5] It is named after [Stanko Bilinski](wikipedia://en/44812552), who rediscovered it in 1960.[^o6] Bilinski himself called it the **rhombic dodecahedron of the second kind**.[^o7] Bilinski's discovery corrected a 75-year-old omission in [Evgraf Fedorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evgraf_Fedorov)'s classification of convex polyhedra with congruent [rhombic](wikipedia://en/183884) faces.[^o25]

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Definition and properties"}

## Definition and properties {#Definition_and_properties}

::: {#o11 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

The Bilinski dodecahedron is formed by gluing together twelve [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) [golden rhombi](wikipedia://en/20825292). These are [rhombi](wikipedia://en/183884) whose diagonals are in the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386):

$$\varphi = { {1 + \sqrt{5} } \over 2} \approx 1.618~034 .$$

The graph of the resulting polyhedron is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) to the graph of the [rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631), but the faces are oriented differently: one pair of opposite rhombi has their long and short diagonals reversed relative to the orientation of the corresponding rhombi in the rhombic dodecahedron.

:::

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Symmetry"}

### Symmetry {#Symmetry}

Because of its reversal, the Bilinski dodecahedron has a lower order of symmetry; its symmetry group is that of a [rectangular cuboid](wikipedia://en/38848329): [$D_{2h}, [2,2], (*222),$](wikipedia://en/2922089) of order 8. This is a subgroup of [octahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2900805); its elements are three 2-fold symmetry axes, three symmetry planes (which are also the axial planes of this solid), and a center of [inversion symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inversion_symmetry). The rotation group of the Bilinski dodecahedron is $D_{2}, [2,2]^{+}, (222),$ of order 4.

:::

### Vertices {#Vertices}

Like the rhombic dodecahedron, the Bilinski dodecahedron has eight vertices of [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) 3 and six of degree 4. It has two apices on the vertical axis, and four vertices on each axial plane. But due to the reversal, its non-apical vertices form two squares (red and green) and one rectangle (blue), and its fourteen vertices in all are of four different kinds:

- two degree-4 apices surrounded by four acute face angles (vertical-axis vertices, black in first figure);
- four degree-4 vertices surrounded by three acute and one obtuse face angles (horizontal-axial-plane vertices, blue in first figure);
- four degree-3 vertices surrounded by three obtuse face angles (one vertical-axial-plane vertices, red in first figure);
- four degree-3 vertices surrounded by two obtuse and one acute face angles (other vertical-axial-plane vertices, green in first figure).

### Faces {#Faces}

The supplementary internal angles of a golden rhombus are:[^o16]

- acute angle:

$$\alpha = \arctan 2 \approx 63.434~949 ^ \circ ;$$

- obtuse angle:

$$\beta = \pi - \arctan 2 \approx 116.565~051 ^ \circ .$$

The faces of the Bilinski dodecahedron are twelve congruent golden rhombi; but due to the reversal, they are of three different kinds:

- eight apical faces with all four kinds of vertices,
- two side faces with alternate blue and red vertices (front and back in first figure),
- two side faces with alternating blue and green vertices (left and right in first figure).

(See also the figure with edges and front faces colored.)

### Edges {#Edges}

*Image: Bilinski dodecahedron.png.* Edges and front faces colored by their symmetry positions

The 24 edges of the Bilinski dodecahedron have the same length; but due to the reversal, they are of four different kinds:

- four apical edges with black and red vertices (in first figure),
- four apical edges with black and green vertices (in first figure),
- eight side edges with blue and red vertices (in first figure),
- eight side edges with blue and green vertices (in first figure).

(See also the figure with edges and front faces colored.)

### In families of polyhedra {#In_families_of_polyhedra}

The Bilinski dodecahedron is a [parallelohedron](wikipedia://en/25207334); thus it is also a [space-filling polyhedron](wikipedia://en/58607583), and a [zonohedron](wikipedia://en/669402).

:::

## Relation to rhombic dodecahedron {#Relation_to_rhombic_dodecahedron}

In a 1962 paper, [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328) claimed that the Bilinski dodecahedron could be obtained by an [affine transformation](wikipedia://en/38449) from the rhombic dodecahedron, but this is false.[^o23]

In the rhombic dodecahedron: every long body diagonal (i.e. lying on opposite degree-4 vertices) is parallel to the short diagonals of four faces.

In the Bilinski dodecahedron: the longest body diagonal (i.e. lying on opposite black degree-4 vertices) is parallel to the short diagonals of two faces, and to the long diagonals of two other faces; the shorter body diagonals (i.e. lying on opposite blue degree-4 vertices) are not parallel to the diagonal of any face.[^o25]

In any affine transformation of the rhombic dodecahedron: every long body diagonal (i.e. lying on opposite degree-4 vertices) remains parallel to four face diagonals, and these remain of the same (new) length.

## Zonohedra with golden rhombic faces {#Zonohedra_with_golden_rhombic_faces}

*Image: Bilinski\_dodecahedron\_parallelohedron.png.* A Bilinski dodecahedron is a [zonohedron](wikipedia://en/669402); it has four sets of six parallel edges. Contracting any set of six parallel edges to zero length produces a [golden rhombohedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_rhombohedron).

The Bilinski dodecahedron can be formed from the [rhombic triacontahedron](wikipedia://en/665025) (another zonohedron, with thirty [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) golden rhombic faces) by removing or collapsing two zones or belts of ten and eight golden rhombic faces with parallel edges. Removing only one zone of ten faces produces the [rhombic icosahedron](wikipedia://en/3691443). Removing three zones of ten, eight, and six faces produces a [golden rhombohedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_rhombohedron).[^o7][^o25] Thus removing a zone of six faces from the Bilinski dodecahedron produces a golden rhombohedron. The Bilinski dodecahedron can be [dissected](wikipedia://en/2915839) into four golden rhombohedra, two of each type.[^o26]

The vertices of the zonohedra with golden rhombic faces can be computed by linear combinations of two to six generating edge vectors with coefficients 0 or 1.[^o27] A [belt](wikipedia://en/25207334#Topological_types) $m_{n}$ means a belt representing $n$ directional vectors, and containing $m$ coparallel edges with same length. The Bilinski dodecahedron has four belts of six coparallel edges.

These zonohedra are projection envelopes of the [hypercubes](wikipedia://en/39783), with $n$-dimensional projection basis, with [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386) ($\varphi$). For $n = 6,$ the specific basis is:

$$x = (1, \varphi, 0,-1, \varphi, 0),$$

$$y = (\varphi, 0, 1, \varphi, 0,-1),$$

$$z = (0, 1, \varphi, 0,-1, \varphi).$$

For $n = 5,$ the basis is the same with the sixth column removed. For $n = 4,$ the fifth and sixth columns are removed.
