# DESCHALL Project

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="DESCHALL Project"}

**DESCHALL**, short for DES Challenge, was the first group to publicly break a message which used the [Data Encryption Standard](wikipedia://en/7978) (DES), becoming the \$10,000 winner of the first of the set of [DES Challenges](wikipedia://en/4426114) proposed by [RSA Security](wikipedia://en/301240) in 1997. It was established by a group of computer scientists led by [Rocke Verser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocke_Verser)[^o2] assisted by [Justin Dolske](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Dolske) and [Matt Curtin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matt_Curtin) and involved thousands of volunteers who ran software in the background on their own machines, connected by the [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet). They announced their success on June 18, only 96 days after the challenge was announced on January 28.

:::

## Background {#Background}

To search the 72 [quadrillion](wikipedia://en/682403) possible keys of a 56-bit DES key using conventional computers was considered impractical even in the 1990s. Rocke Verser already had an efficient algorithm that ran on a standard PC[^o4] and had the idea of involving the spare time on hundreds of other such machines that were connected to the internet. So they set up a [server](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communications_server) on a 486-based [PS/2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Personal_System/2) PC with 56MB of memory and announced the project via [Usenet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usenet) towards the end of March. Client software was rapidly written for a large variety of home machines and eventually some more powerful 64 bit systems.

There were two other main contenders: SoINET[^o5] (a Swedish group), and a group at [Silicon Graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_Graphics), a manufacturer of [high-performance computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-performance_computing), which was in the lead until late in the day. Other groups using [supercomputers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercomputer) withdrew after [SYN flood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SYN_flood) attacks on their networks.

## The Project {#The_Project}

With the software that was used, a single 200 MHz Pentium system was able to test approximately 1 million keys/second if it was doing nothing else. At this rate it would take around 2,285 years to search the entire key-space. The number of computers being used rose rapidly and in the end, a total of 78,000 different [IP addresses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_address) had been recorded, with a maximum of 14,000 unique hosts in a 24-hour period. By the time the key was found, they had searched about a quarter of the key-space and were searching about 7 billion keys per second, but the number of participants was still increasing rapidly.

The solution was:

`Strong cryptography makes the world a safer place.`

The owner of the computer that found the solution was awarded \$4,000 of the prize, with the rest going to the originator of the project.

The conclusion of the paper describing the project was "We have demonstrated that a brute-force search of DES keyspace is not only possible, but is also becoming practical for even modestly funded groups. RSA's prize for the find was US\$10,000; it is safe to say that DES is inadequate for protecting data of any greater value."[^o7]
