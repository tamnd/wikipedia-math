# Hertz vector

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hertz vector"}

**Hertz vectors**, or the **Hertz vector potentials**, are an alternative formulation of the electromagnetic potentials. They are most often introduced in electromagnetic theory textbooks as practice problems for students to solve.[^o2] There are multiple cases where they have a practical use, including antennas[^o3] and waveguides.[^o4] Though they are sometimes used in such practice problems, they are still rarely mentioned in most electromagnetic theory courses, and when they are, they are often not practiced in a manner that demonstrates when they may be useful or provide a simpler method to solving a problem than more commonly practiced methods.

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

Hertz vectors can be advantageous when solving for the electric and magnetic fields in certain scenarios, as they provide an alternative way to define the scalar potential $\phi$ and the vector potential $\mathbf{A}$ which are used to find the fields as is commonly done.

::: {#o6 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\mathbf{E} = -\nabla \phi - \frac{\partial \mathbf{A}}{\partial t} \tag{1}$$

:::

::: {#o7 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\mathbf{B} = \nabla \times \mathbf{A} \tag{2}$$

:::

Considering cases of electric and magnetic polarization separately for simplicity, each can be defined in terms of the scalar and vector potentials which then allows for the electric and magnetic fields to be found. For cases of just electric polarization the following relations are used.

::: {#o8 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$\phi = - \nabla\cdot\mathbf{\Pi}_e \tag{3}$$

:::

::: {#o9 .equation cue=numblk name="4"}

$$\mathbf{A}=\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial\mathbf{\Pi}_e}{\partial t} \tag{4}$$

:::

And for cases of solely magnetic polarization they are defined as:

::: {#o10 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$$\phi = 0 \tag{5}$$

:::

::: {#o11 .equation cue=numblk name="6"}

$$\mathbf{A}=\nabla\times\mathbf{\Pi}_m \tag{6}$$

:::

To apply these, the polarizations need to be defined so that the form of the Hertz vectors can be obtained. Considering the case of simple electric polarization provides the path to finding this form via the wave equation. Assuming the space is uniform and non-conducting, and the charge and current distributions are given by $\rho(\mathbf{r},t), \mathbf{J}(\mathbf{r},t)$, define a vector $\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r},t)$ such that $\rho = -\nabla \cdot \mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{J}= \frac{\partial\mathbf{P}}{\partial t}$. Using these to solve for the $\mathbf{\Pi}$ vectors is similar to how the auxiliary fields $\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ can be found, however here the Hertz vectors treat the electric and magnetic polarizations as sources. The Hertz vector potentials from these sources, $\mathbf{\Pi}_e$ for the electric Hertz potential, and $\mathbf{\Pi}_m$ for the magnetic Hertz potential can be derived using the wave equation for each, similar to the wave equations for $\phi$ and $\mathbf{A}$:

::: {#o12 .equation cue=numblk name="7"}

$$\nabla^2\mathbf{\Pi}_e - \mu \epsilon \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{\Pi}_e}{\partial t^2} = -\frac{\mathbf{P}_f}{\epsilon} \tag{7}$$

:::

::: {#o13 .equation cue=numblk name="8"}

$$\nabla^2\mathbf{\Pi}_m - \mu \epsilon \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{\Pi}_m}{\partial t^2} = -\mu \mathbf{M}_f \tag{8}$$

:::

where $\mathbf{P}_f$ and $\mathbf{M}_f$ are the free electric and magnetic polarizations. These wave equations yield the following solutions for the Hertz vectors:

::: {#o14 .equation cue=numblk name="9"}

$$\mathbf{\Pi}_e = \frac{1}{4 \pi \epsilon} \int\limits_V \frac{\mathbf{P}_f\left(\mathbf{r}',t_r\right)}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|}d^3\mathbf{r}' \tag{9}$$

:::

::: {#o15 .equation cue=numblk name="10"}

$$\mathbf{\Pi}_m = \frac{\mu}{4 \pi} \int\limits_V \frac{\mathbf{M}_f\left(\mathbf{r}',t_r\right)}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|}d^3\mathbf{r}' \tag{10}$$

:::

where $\mathbf{P}_f$ and $\mathbf{M}_f$ are evaluated at the retarded time $t_r=t-|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r'}|/c$.[^o2] The electric and magnetic fields can then be found using the Hertz vectors. In the absence of free magnetic polarization, the $\mathbf{\Pi}_e$ vector is used to find the fields as follows:

::: {#o16 .equation cue=numblk name="11"}

$$\mathbf{E}=\nabla\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\Pi}_e\right)-\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf{\Pi}_e}{\partial t^2} \tag{11}$$

:::

::: {#o17 .equation cue=numblk name="12"}

$$\mathbf{B}=\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\nabla\times\mathbf{\Pi}_e\right) \tag{12}$$

:::

To see that the Maxwell curl relations are satisfied, it can be insightful to express the electric field as a curl of a curl. We can apply the identity $\nabla \times \nabla \times\mathbf{A} = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}) - \nabla^2\mathbf{A}$. When free electric polarizations are absent, the electric field can then be expressed as

::: {#o18 .equation cue=numblk name="12a"}

$$\mathbf{E}=\nabla \times \nabla \times\mathbf{\Pi}_e \tag{12a}$$

:::

Similarly, solutions can also be determined via the magnetic Hertz potential, with its relations to the scalar and vector potentials in (5),(6):

::: {#o19 .equation cue=numblk name="13"}

$$\mathbf{E}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\nabla\times\mathbf{\Pi}_m\right) \tag{13}$$

:::

::: {#o20 .equation cue=numblk name="14"}

$$\mathbf{B}=\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{\Pi}_m \tag{14}$$

:::

In general both the electric and magnetic types can be superimposed. In the absence of free polarizations we have

::: {#o21 .equation cue=numblk name="15"}

$$\mathbf{E} = \nabla \times \nabla \times\mathbf{\Pi}_e -\nabla \times \frac{\partial\mathbf{\Pi}_m}{\partial t} \tag{15}$$

:::

::: {#o22 .equation cue=numblk name="16"}

$$\mathbf{B} = \nabla \times \nabla \times\mathbf{\Pi}_m +\mu\epsilon\nabla \times \frac{\partial\mathbf{\Pi}_e}{\partial t} \tag{16}$$

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

### Oscillating dipole {#Oscillating_dipole}

Consider a one dimensional, uniformly oscillating current. The current is aligned along the *z*-axis in some length of conducting material $\ell$ with an oscillation frequency $\omega$. We will define the polarization vector

::: {#o26 .equation cue=numblk name="17"}

$$\mathbf{P}=(-I\ell/\omega) \cos\left[\omega t\right]_{t'}\mathbf{\hat{z}} \tag{17}$$

:::

where $t$ is evaluated at the retarded time $t' = t-|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|/v$. Inserting this into the electric Hertz vector equation knowing that the length $\ell$ is small and the polarization is in one dimension it can be approximated in spherical coordinates as follows

::: {#o27 .equation cue=numblk name="18"}

$$\mathbf{\Pi}_e = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon}\frac{\left(-I\ell/\omega\right)\cos\left[\omega t\right]_{t'}}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|}\left[\cos\left(\theta\right)\mathbf{\hat{r}}-\sin\left(\theta\right)\mathbf{\hat{\theta}}\right] \tag{18}$$

:::

Continuing directly to taking the divergence quickly becomes messy due to the $|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r'}|$ denominator. This is readily resolved by using [Legendre Polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legendre_Polynomials) for expanding a $1/r$ potential:

::: {#o28 .equation cue=numblk name="19"}

$$\frac{1}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'|} = \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{r'^\ell}{r^{\ell+1}} P_\ell\left(\cos \gamma\right) \tag{19}$$

:::

In the above equation, $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{x}'$ are vectors, while $r$ and $r'$ are the lengths of those vectors. $\gamma$ is the angle between the vectors $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{x}'$. The Hertz vector is now written as follows.

::: {#o29 .equation cue=numblk name="20"}

$$\mathbf{\Pi}_e = \frac{\left(-I\ell/\omega\right)\cos\left[\omega t\right]_{t'}}{4\pi\epsilon} \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{r'^\ell}{r^{\ell+1}} P_\ell\left(\cos \gamma\right)\left[\cos\left(\theta\right)\mathbf{\hat{r}}-\sin\left(\theta\right)\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}\right] \tag{20}$$

:::

Taking the divergence

::: {#o30 .equation cue=numblk name="21"}

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\Pi}_e = \frac{\left(I\ell/\omega\right)\cos\left[\omega t\right]_{t'}\cos\left(\theta\right)}{4\pi\epsilon}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty} \frac{r'^\ell\left(\ell+1\right)}{r^{\ell+2}}P_\ell\left(\cos \gamma\right) \tag{21}$$

:::

Then the gradient of the result

::: {#o31 .equation cue=numblk name="22"}

$$\nabla\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\Pi}_e\right) = \frac{\left(-I\ell/\omega\right)\cos\left[\omega t\right]_{t'}}{4\pi\epsilon}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{r'^\ell\left(\ell+1\right)P_\ell\left(\cos\gamma\right)}{r^{\ell+3}}\left[\left(\ell+2\right)\cos\left(\theta\right)\mathbf{\hat{r}}+\sin\left(\theta\right)\mathbf{\hat{\theta}}\right] \tag{22}$$

:::

Finally finding the second partial with respect to time

::: {#o32 .equation cue=numblk name="23"}

$$\mu \epsilon \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{\Pi}_e}{\partial t^2}=\frac{\mu I \ell \omega \cos\left[\omega t\right]_{t'}}{4 \pi}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{r'^\ell}{r^{\ell+1}}P_\ell\left(\cos \gamma\right)\left[\cos\left(\theta\right)\mathbf{\hat{r}}-\sin\left(\theta\right)\mathbf{\hat{\theta}}\right] \tag{23}$$

:::

Allows for finding the electric field

::: {#o33 .equation cue=numblk name="24"}

$$\mathbf{E}=\nabla\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\Pi}_e\right)-\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf{\Pi}_e}{\partial t^2}=\frac{I\ell\cos\left[\omega t\right]_{t'}}{4\pi} \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{r'^\ell P_\ell\left(\cos\left(\gamma\right)\right)}{r^{\ell+1}}\left[\left(\frac{-\left(\ell+1\right)\left(\ell+2\right)}{r^2\epsilon\omega}-\mu\omega\right)\cos\left(\theta\right)\mathbf{\hat{r}} + \left(\frac{-\left(\ell+1\right)}{r^2\epsilon\omega}+\mu\omega\right)\sin\left(\theta\right)\mathbf{\hat{\theta}}\right] \tag{24}$$

:::

#### Simulation {#Simulation}

Using the appropriate conversions to Cartesian coordinates, this field can be simulated in a 3D grid. Viewing the X-Y plane at the origin shows the two-lobed field in one plane we expect from a dipole, and it oscillates in time. The image below shows the shape of this field and how the polarity reverses in time due to the cosine term, however it does not currently show the amplitude change due to the time varying strength of the current. Regardless, its shape alone shows the effectiveness of using the electric Hertz vector in this scenario. This approach is significantly more straightforward than finding the electric field in terms of charges within the infinitely thin wire, especially as they vary with time. This is just one of several examples of when the use of Hertz vectors is advantageous compared to more common methods.

### Current loop {#Current_loop}

Consider a small loop of area $A$ carrying a time varying current $I \sin\left(\omega t\right)$. With current flow, a magnetic field perpendicular to the direction of flow as a result of the right hand rule will be present. Due to this field being generated in a loop, it is expected that the field would look similar to that of an electric dipole. This can be proven quickly using Hertz vectors. First the magnetic polarization is determined by its relation to magnetic moment $\mathbf{M}=\frac{d\mathbf{m}}{dV}$. The magnetic moment of a current loop is defined as $\mathbf{m}=IA\mathbf{\hat{n}}$, so if the loop lies in the x-y plane and has the previously defined time-varying current, the magnetic moment is $\mathbf{m}=IA \sin\left(\omega t\right)\mathbf{\hat{z}}$. Inserting this into $\mathbf{M}$, and then into Equation ((10)), the magnetic Hertz vector is found in a simple form.

::: {#o36 .equation cue=numblk name="25"}

$$\mathbf{\Pi}_m=\frac{\mu I A \sin\left(\omega t\right)}{4 \pi|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|}\mathbf{\hat{z}} \tag{25}$$

:::

As in the electric dipole example, the Legendre polynomials can be used to simplify the derivatives necessary to obtain $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$. The electric field is then found through

::: {#o37 .equation cue=numblk name="26"}

$$\mathbf{E}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\nabla\times\frac{\mu I A \sin\left(\omega t\right)}{4\pi}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{r'^\ell}{r^{\ell+1}}P_\ell\left(\cos \gamma\right)\right)\mathbf{\hat{z}} \tag{26}$$

:::

Due to the dependence on $r$, it is significantly simpler to express the Hertz vector in spherical coordinates by transforming from the sole $\mathbf{\hat{z}}$ component vector to the $\mathbf{\hat{r}}$ and $\mathbf{\hat{\theta}}$ components.

::: {#o38 .equation cue=numblk name="27"}

$$\mathbf{E}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\nabla\times\frac{\mu I A \sin\left(\omega t\right)}{4\pi}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{r'^\ell}{r^{\ell+1}}P_\ell\left(\cos \gamma\right)\left[\cos\left(\theta\right)\mathbf{\hat{r}}-\sin\left(\theta\right)\mathbf{\hat{\theta}}\right]\right) \tag{27}$$

:::

::: {#o39 .equation cue=numblk name="28"}

$$\mathbf{E}=\frac{-\mu A I \omega \cos\left(\omega t\right)}{4 \pi}\cos\left(\theta\right)\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{r'^\ell}{r^{\ell+2}}P_\ell\left(\cos \gamma\right)\left(1-\ell\right)\mathbf{\hat{\phi}} \tag{28}$$

:::

#### Simulation {#Simulation}

This field was simulated using Python by converting the spherical component to x and y components. The result is as expected. Due to the changing current, there is a time dependent magnetic field which induces an electric field. Due to the shape, the field appears as if it were a dipole.

*Image: Current loop Exz Contour.gif.* Electric field around the current loop. It shows a dipole shape and the polarity difference can be seen above and below the loop as the current direction changes with time.

:::
