# Seneca effect

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Seneca effect"}

*Image: Seneca Effect after H. Bardi.png.* Illustration of asymmetrical growth and decline

The **Seneca effect**, or **Seneca cliff** or **Seneca collapse**, is a [mathematical model](wikipedia://en/20590) proposed by [Ugo Bardi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ugo_Bardi) to describe situations where a system's rate of decline is much sharper than its earlier rate of growth.

:::

## Description {#Description}

In 2017, Bardi published a book titled *The Seneca Effect: When Growth is Slow but Collapse is Rapid*, named after the Roman philosopher and writer [Seneca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_the_Younger), who wrote *Fortune is of sluggish growth, but ruin is rapid* (*[Letters to Lucilius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_to_Lucilius)*, 91.6):[^o3]

Bardi's book looked at cases of rapid decline across societies (including the fall of empires, financial crises, and major famines), in nature (including avalanches), and through man-made systems (including cracks in metal objects). Bardi concluded that rapid collapse is not a flaw, or "bug" as he terms it, but a "varied and ubiquitous phenomena" with multiple causes and resultant pathways. The collapse of a system can often clear the path for new, and better adapted, structures.[^o4] In a 2019 book titled *Before the Collapse: A Guide to the Other Side of Growth*, Bardi describes a "Seneca Rebound" that often takes place where new systems replace the collapsed system, and often at a rate faster than preceding growth rates as the collapse has eliminated many of impediments or constraints from the previous system.[^o4]

The "Seneca effect" model is related to the "[World3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World3)" model from the 1972 report *[The Limits to Growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Limits_to_Growth)*, issued by the [Club of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Club_of_Rome).[^o3][^o5]

::: {#o7 .application cue=heading name="Use"}

## Use {#Use}

One of the model's main practical applications has been to describe the resultant outcomes given the condition of a global shortage of [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel).[^o3] Unlike the symmetrical [Hubbert curve](wikipedia://en/84652) fossil fuel model, the Seneca cliff model shows material [asymmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymmetry), where the global rate of decline in fossil fuel production is far steeper than forecasted by the Hubbert curve.[^o8]

The term has also been used to describe rapid declines in businesses that had grown for decades, with the rapid post-2005 decline and resultant bankruptcy in [Kodak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kodak) as a quoted example.[^o9]
