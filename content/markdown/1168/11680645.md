# Interval order

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Interval order"}

*Image: Interval order, Hasse diagram and interval realization.png.* A partial order on the set {*a*, *b*, *c*, *d*, *e*, *f*} illustrated by its [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199) (left) and a collection of intervals that represents it (right).

*Image: Critical pair (order theory).svg.* The $(2+2)$ poset (black Hasse diagram) cannot be part of an interval order: if *a* is completely right of *b*, and *d* overlaps with both *a* and *b*, and *c* is completely right of *d*, then *c* must be completely right of *b* (light gray edge).

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), the **interval order** for a collection of intervals on the real line is the [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) corresponding to their left-to-right precedence relation—one interval, *I*<sub>1</sub>, being considered less than another, *I*<sub>2</sub>, if *I*<sub>1</sub> is completely to the left of *I*<sub>2</sub>. More formally, a [countable](wikipedia://en/6026) [poset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poset) $P = (X, \leq)$ is an interval order if and only if there exists a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) from $X$ to a set of real intervals, so $x_i \mapsto (\ell_i, r_i)$, such that for any $x_i, x_j \in X$ we have $x_i < x_j$ in $P$ exactly when $r_i < \ell_j$.

:::

Such posets may be equivalently characterized as those with no induced subposet [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/23916899) to the pair of two-element [chains](wikipedia://en/30330#Chains), in other words as the $(2+2)$-free posets.[^o2] Fully written out, this means that for any two pairs of elements $a > b$ and $c > d$ one must have $a > d$ or $c > b$.

The subclass of interval orders obtained by restricting the intervals to those of unit length, so they all have the form $(\ell_i, \ell_i + 1)$, is precisely the [semiorders](wikipedia://en/28771796).

The [complement](wikipedia://en/1175649) of the [comparability graph](wikipedia://en/6978672) of an interval order ($X$, ≤) is the [interval graph](wikipedia://en/363423) $(X, \cap)$.

Interval orders should not be confused with the interval-containment orders, which are the [inclusion orders](wikipedia://en/11728075) on intervals on the real line (equivalently, the orders of [dimension](wikipedia://en/3121852) ≤ 2).

Interval orders' practical applications include modelling evolution of species and archaeological histories of pottery styles.[^n1]

## Interval orders and dimension {#Interval_orders_and_dimension}

::: {#o5 .problem cue=unsolved}

What is the complexity of determining the order dimension of an interval order?

:::

An important parameter of partial orders is [order dimension](wikipedia://en/3121852): the dimension of a partial order $P$ is the least number of [linear orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_order) whose intersection is $P$. For interval orders, dimension can be arbitrarily large. And while the problem of determining the dimension of general partial orders is known to be [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard), determining the dimension of an interval order remains a problem of unknown [computational complexity](wikipedia://en/6511).[^o6]

A related parameter is **interval dimension**, which is defined analogously, but in terms of interval orders instead of linear orders. Thus, the interval dimension of a partially ordered set $P = (X, \leq)$ is the least [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $k$ for which there exist interval orders $\preceq_1, \ldots, \preceq_k$ on $X$ with $x \leq y$ exactly when $x \preceq_1 y, \ldots,$ and $x \preceq_k y$. The interval dimension of an order is never greater than its order dimension.[^n2]

## Combinatorics {#Combinatorics}

In addition to being isomorphic to $(2+2)$-free posets, unlabeled interval orders on $[n]$ are also in bijection with a subset of [fixed-point-free](wikipedia://en/449738) [involutions](wikipedia://en/293450) on ordered sets with [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) $2n$.[^o9] These are the involutions with no so-called left- or right-neighbor nestings where, for any involution $f$ on $[2n]$, a left nesting is an $i \in [2n]$ such that $i < i+1 < f(i+1) < f(i)$ and a right nesting is an $i \in [2n]$ such that $f(i) < f(i+1) < i < i+1$.

Such involutions, according to semi-length, have [ordinary generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_generating_function)[^o10]

$$F(t) = \sum_{n \geq 0} \prod_{i = 1}^n (1-(1-t)^i).$$

The coefficient of $t^n$ in the expansion of $F(t)$ gives the number of unlabeled interval orders of size $n$. The sequence of these numbers begins

1, 2, 5, 15, 53, 217, 1014, 5335, 31240, 201608, 1422074, 10886503, 89903100, 796713190, 7541889195, 75955177642, …
