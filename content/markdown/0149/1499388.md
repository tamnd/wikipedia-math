# Hadamard three-circle theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hadamard three-circle theorem"}

In [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Hadamard three-circle theorem** is a result about the behavior of [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110).

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

> **Hadamard three-circle theorem:** Let $f(z)$ be a holomorphic function on the [annulus](wikipedia://en/356158) $r_1\leq\left| z\right| \leq r_3$. Let $M(r)$ be the [maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxima_and_minima) of $|f(z)|$ on the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) $|z|=r.$ Then, $\log M(r)$ is a [convex function](wikipedia://en/245568) of the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) $\log (r).$ Moreover, if $f(z)$ is not of the form $cz^\lambda$ for some [constants](wikipedia://en/51203) $\lambda$ and $c$, then $\log M(r)$ is strictly convex as a function of $\log (r).$

The conclusion of the [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) can be restated as

$$\log\left(\frac{r_3}{r_1}\right)\log M(r_2)\leq \log\left(\frac{r_3}{r_2}\right)\log M(r_1) +\log\left(\frac{r_2}{r_1}\right)\log M(r_3)$$

for any three [concentric circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentric_circles) of radii $r_1<r_2<r_3.$

:::

::: {#o5 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

The three circles theorem follows from the fact that for any real *a*, the function Re log(*z*<sup>*a*</sup>*f*(*z*)) is harmonic between two circles, and therefore takes its maximum value on one of the circles. The theorem follows by choosing the constant *a* so that this [harmonic function](wikipedia://en/55762) has the same maximum value on both circles.

The theorem can also be deduced directly from [Hadamard's three-line theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadamard_three-line_theorem).[^o6]

:::

## History {#History}

A statement and proof for the theorem was given by [J.E. Littlewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J.E._Littlewood) in 1912, but he attributes it to no one in particular, stating it as a known theorem. [Harald Bohr](wikipedia://en/638305) and [Edmund Landau](wikipedia://en/222230) attribute the theorem to [Jacques Hadamard](wikipedia://en/312675), writing in 1896; Hadamard published no proof.[^o8]
