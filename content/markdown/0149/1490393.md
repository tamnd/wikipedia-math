# Tibor Radó

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Tibor Radó"}

Radó Tibor

**Tibor Radó** (Radó Tibor ; June 2, 1895 – December 29, 1965) was a Hungarian [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who moved to the United States after [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Radó was born in [Budapest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budapest) in 1895. He later went on to attend the [Polytechnic Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_universities_in_Hungary#State_funded), studying [civil engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_engineering). In [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I), he became a First Lieutenant in the Hungarian Army and was captured on the Russian Front. He escaped from a Siberian prisoner camp and, traveling thousands of miles across [Arctic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic) [wasteland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barren_vegetation), managed to return to Hungary.

He received a doctorate from the [Franz Joseph University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Joseph_University) in 1923. He taught briefly at the university and then became a research fellow in Germany for the [Rockefeller Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_Foundation). In 1929, he moved to the United States and lectured at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) and the [Rice Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice_Institute) before obtaining a faculty position in the Department of Mathematics at [Ohio State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_State_University) in 1930. In 1935 he was granted American citizenship. In [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) he was a science consultant to the United States government, interrupting his academic career. He became Chairman of the Department of Mathematics at Ohio State University in 1948.

In the 1920s, he proved that [surfaces](wikipedia://en/27865) have an [essentially unique triangulation](wikipedia://en/2180532). In 1933, Radó published "On the Problem of Plateau" in which he gave a solution to [Plateau's problem](wikipedia://en/338129), and in 1935, "Subharmonic Functions". His work focused on computer science in the last decade of his life and in May 1962 he published one of his most famous results in the *[Bell System Technical Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_System_Technical_Journal)*: the [busy beaver function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Busy_beaver_function) and its [non-computability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-computable_function) ("On Non-Computable Functions").

He died in [New Smyrna Beach, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Smyrna_Beach,_Florida).
