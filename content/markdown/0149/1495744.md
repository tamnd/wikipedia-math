# Intrinsic equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Intrinsic equation"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), an **intrinsic equation** of a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) is an [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) that defines the curve using a [relation](wikipedia://en/22517558) between the curve's intrinsic properties, that is, properties that do not depend on the location and possibly the orientation of the curve. Therefore an intrinsic equation defines the shape of the curve without specifying its position relative to an arbitrarily defined [coordinate system](wikipedia://en/81931).

:::

The intrinsic quantities used most often are [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) $s$, [tangential angle](wikipedia://en/15785733) $\theta$, [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) $\kappa$ or [radius of curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radius_of_curvature_%28mathematics%29), and, for 3-dimensional curves, [torsion](wikipedia://en/3039281) $\tau$. Specifically:

- The [natural equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_equation) is the curve given by its curvature and torsion.
- The [Whewell equation](wikipedia://en/11128841) is obtained as a relation between arc length and tangential angle.
- The [Cesàro equation](wikipedia://en/11129054) is obtained as a relation between arc length and curvature.

The equation of a circle (including a line) for example is given by the equation $\kappa(s) = \tfrac{1}{r}$ where $s$ is the arc length, $\kappa$ the curvature and $r$ the radius of the circle.

These coordinates greatly simplify some physical problems. For elastic rods for example, the potential energy is given by

$$E= \int_0^L B \kappa^2(s)ds$$

where $B$ is the bending modulus $EI$. Moreover, as $\kappa(s) = d\theta/ds$, elasticity of rods can be given a simple [variational](wikipedia://en/171882) form.
