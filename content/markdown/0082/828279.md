# NewDES

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="NewDES"}

In [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), **NewDES** is a [symmetric key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_key_cryptography) [block cipher](wikipedia://en/4594). It was created in 1984–1985 by Robert Scott as a potential [DES](wikipedia://en/7978) replacement.

:::

Despite its name, it is not derived from DES and has quite a different structure. Its intended niche as a DES replacement has now mostly been filled by [AES](wikipedia://en/1260). The algorithm was revised with a modified [key schedule](wikipedia://en/609603) in 1996 to counter a [related-key attack](wikipedia://en/1516144); this version is sometimes referred to as **NewDES-96**.

In 2004, Scott posted some comments on [sci.crypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sci.crypt) reflecting on the motivation behind NewDES's design and what he might have done differently so as to make the cipher more secure.[^o3]

::: {#o5 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Algorithm"}

## Algorithm {#Algorithm}

NewDES, unlike DES, has no bit-level permutations, making it easy to implement in software. All operations are performed on whole bytes. It is a [product cipher](wikipedia://en/598557), consisting of 17 rounds performed on a 64-bit data block and makes use of a 120-bit [key](wikipedia://en/53039).

In each round, subkey material is [XORed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XOR) with the 1-byte sub-blocks of data, then fed through an [S-box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substitution_box), the output of which is then XORed with another sub-block of data. In total, 8 XORs are performed in each round. The S-box is derived from the [United States Declaration of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_Independence_%28United_States%29) (used as a [nothing-up-my-sleeve number](wikipedia://en/1249296)).

Each set of two rounds uses seven 1-byte subkeys, which are derived by splitting 56 bits of the key into bytes. The key is then rotated 56 bits for use in the next two rounds.

:::

## Cryptanalysis {#Cryptanalysis}

Only a small amount of [cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715) has been published on NewDES. The designer showed that NewDES exhibits the full [avalanche effect](wikipedia://en/769439) after seven rounds: every [ciphertext](wikipedia://en/175560) bit depends on every [plaintext](wikipedia://en/157935) bit and key bit.

NewDES has the same complementation property that DES has: namely, that if

$$E_K(P)=C,$$

then

$$E_{\overline{K}}(\overline{P})=\overline{C},$$

where

$$\overline{x}$$

is the [bitwise complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitwise_complement) of *x*. This means that the work factor for a [brute force attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brute_force_attack) is reduced by a factor of 2. [Eli Biham](wikipedia://en/348920) also noticed that changing a full byte in all the key and data bytes leads to another complementation property. This reduces the work factor by 2<sup>8</sup>.

Biham's related-key attack can break NewDES with 2<sup>33</sup> chosen-key chosen plaintexts, meaning that NewDES is not as secure as DES.

[John Kelsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Kelsey_%28cryptanalyst%29), [Bruce Schneier](wikipedia://en/36732), and [David Wagner](wikipedia://en/298253) used related-key cryptanalysis to develop another attack on NewDES; it requires 2<sup>32</sup> known plaintexts and one related key.[^o7]
