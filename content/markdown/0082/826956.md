# Clausius–Mossotti relation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Clausius–Mossotti relation"}

In [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism), the **Clausius–Mossotti relation**, named for [O. F. Mossotti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottaviano-Fabrizio_Mossotti) and [Rudolf Clausius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Clausius), expresses the [dielectric constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielectric_constant) (relative [permittivity](wikipedia://en/53933) $\varepsilon_{r}$) of a material in terms of the atomic [polarizability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polarizability) $\alpha$ of the material's constituent atoms and/or molecules, or a [homogeneous mixture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_mixture) thereof. It is equivalent to the Lorentz–Lorenz equation, which relates the [refractive index](wikipedia://en/25880) (rather than the dielectric constant) of a substance to its polarizability. It may be expressed in [SI units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_units) as[^o2][^o3]

$$\frac{\varepsilon_\text{r} - 1}{\varepsilon_\text{r} + 2} = \frac{N \alpha}{3\varepsilon_0},$$

where

:::

$\varepsilon_\text{r} = \varepsilon/\varepsilon_0$ is the [dielectric constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielectric_constant) of the material, which for non-magnetic materials is equal to $n^{2}$, where $n$ is the [refractive index](wikipedia://en/25880);

$\varepsilon_{0}$ is the [permittivity of free space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permittivity_of_free_space);

$N$ is the [number density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_density) of the molecules (m<sup>−3</sup>);

$\alpha$ is the [molecular polarizability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_susceptibility#Molecular_polarizability) ([C](wikipedia://en/47719)·m<sup>2</sup>/V).

In the case that the material consists of a mixture of two or more species, the right side of the above equation would consist of the sum of the molecular polarizability contribution from each species, indexed by $i$ in the following form:[^o4]

$$\frac{\varepsilon_\text{r} - 1}{\varepsilon_\text{r} + 2} = \sum_i \frac{N_i \alpha_i}{3\varepsilon_0}.$$

In the [CGS system of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centimetre–gram–second_system_of_units) the Clausius–Mossotti relation is typically rewritten to show the [molecular polarizability *volume*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polarizability#Definition) $\alpha' = \frac{\alpha}{4\pi\varepsilon_0},$ which has units of volume (cm<sup>3</sup>).[^o3] Confusion may arise from the practice of using the shorter name "molecular polarizability" for both $\alpha$ and $\alpha'$ within literature intended for the respective unit system.

The Clausius–Mossotti relation assumes only an induced dipole relevant to its polarizability and is thus inapplicable for substances with a significant permanent [dipole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipole). It is applicable to gases such as [N2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen), [CO2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CO2), [CH4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CH4) and [H2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) at sufficiently low densities and pressures.[^o5] For example, the Clausius–Mossotti relation is accurate for N<sub>2</sub> gas up to 1000 atm between 25 °C and 125 °C.[^o6] Moreover, the Clausius–Mossotti relation may be applicable to substances if the applied electric field is at a sufficiently high frequencies such that any permanent dipole modes are inactive.[^o7]

## Lorentz–Lorenz equation {#Lorentz–Lorenz_equation}

The **Lorentz–Lorenz equation** is similar to the Clausius–Mossotti relation, except that it relates the refractive index (rather than the dielectric constant) of a substance to its [polarizability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polarizability). The Lorentz–Lorenz equation is named after the Danish mathematician and scientist [Ludvig Lorenz](wikipedia://en/3422173), who published it in 1869, and the Dutch physicist [Hendrik Lorentz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lorentz), who discovered it independently in 1878.

The most general form of the Lorentz–Lorenz equation is (in [Gaussian-CGS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_units) units)

$$\frac{n^2 - 1}{n^2 + 2} = \frac{4 \pi}{3} N \alpha_\text{m},$$

where $n$ is the refractive index, $N$ is the number of molecules per unit volume, and $\alpha_\text{m}$ is the mean polarizability. This equation is approximately valid for homogeneous solids, as well as liquids and gases.

When the square of the refractive index is $n^2 \approx 1$, as it is for many gases, the equation reduces to

$$n^2 - 1 \approx 4 \pi N \alpha_\text{m}$$

or simply

$$n - 1 \approx 2 \pi N \alpha_\text{m}.$$

This applies to gases at ordinary pressures. The refractive index $n$ of the gas can then be expressed in terms of the [molar refractivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_refractivity) $A$ as

$$n \approx \sqrt{1 + \frac{3 A p}{R T}},$$

where $p$ is the pressure of the gas, $R$ is the [universal gas constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_gas_constant), and $T$ is the (absolute) temperature, which together determine the number density $N$.
