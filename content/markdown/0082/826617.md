# Euclid's lemma

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Euclid's lemma"}

*Image: Title\_page\_of\_Sir\_Henry\_Billingsley's\_first\_English\_version\_of\_Euclid's\_Elements,\_1570\_(560x900).jpg.* Title page of Sir Henry Billingsley's first English version of Euclid's Elements, 1570.

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **Euclid's lemma** is a [lemma](wikipedia://en/18634) that captures a fundamental property of [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666):

::: {#o2 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=math-theorem name="Euclid's lemma"}

**Euclid's lemma.**

If a prime $p$ divides the product $ab$ of two integers $a$ and $b$, then $p$ must divide at least one of those integers $a$ or $b$.

:::

For example, if $p = 19$, $a = 133$, $b = 143$, then $ab = 133 \times 143 = 19019$, and since this is divisible by 19, the lemma implies that one or both of 133 or 143 must be as well. In fact, $133 = 19 \times 7$.

:::

The lemma first appeared in [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s *[Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)*, and is a fundamental result in elementary number theory.

If the premise of the lemma does not hold, that is, if $p$ is a [composite number](wikipedia://en/82289), its consequent may be either true or false. For example, in the case of $p = 10$, $a = 4$, $b = 15$, composite number 10 divides $ab = 4 \times 15 = 60$, but 10 divides neither 4 nor 15.

This property is the key in the proof of the [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556). It is used to define [prime elements](wikipedia://en/60692), a generalization of prime numbers to arbitrary [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346). Euclid's lemma shows that in the integers [irreducible elements](wikipedia://en/60693) are also prime elements. The proof uses [induction](wikipedia://en/18881) so it does not apply to all [integral domains](wikipedia://en/15462).

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Formulations"}

## Formulations {#Formulations}

Euclid's lemma is commonly used in the following equivalent form:

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

If $p$ is a prime number that divides the product $ab$ and does not divide $a,$ then it divides $b.$

:::

Euclid's lemma can be generalized as follows from prime numbers to any integers.

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

If an integer $n$ divides the product $ab$ of two integers, and is [coprime](wikipedia://en/6556) with $a$, then $n$ divides $b$.

:::

This is a generalization because a prime number $p$ is coprime with an integer $a$ if and only if $p$ does not divide $a$.

:::

## History {#History}

The lemma first appears as proposition 30 in Book VII of [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s *[Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)*. It is included in practically every book that covers elementary number theory.[^o8][^o9][^o10][^o11][^o12]

The generalization of the lemma to integers appeared in [Jean Prestet](wikipedia://en/47744766)'s textbook *Nouveaux Elémens de Mathématiques* in 1689.[^o13]

In [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125)'s treatise *[Disquisitiones Arithmeticae](wikipedia://en/736713)*, the statement of the lemma is Euclid's Proposition 14 (Section 2), which he uses to prove the uniqueness of the decomposition product of prime factors of an integer (Theorem 16), admitting the existence as "obvious". From this existence and uniqueness he then deduces the generalization of prime numbers to integers.[^o14] For this reason, the generalization of Euclid's lemma is sometimes referred to as Gauss's lemma, but some believe this usage is incorrect due to confusion with [Gauss's lemma on quadratic residues](wikipedia://en/2310978).

::: {#o17 .proof cue=heading name="Proofs"}

## Proofs {#Proofs}

The two first subsections, are proofs of the generalized version of Euclid's lemma, namely that: *if $n$ divides $ab$ and is [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) with $a$ then it divides $b$.*

The original Euclid's lemma follows immediately, since, if $n$ is prime then it divides $a$ or does not divide $a$ in which case it is coprime with $a$ so per the generalized version it divides $b$.

::: {#o19 .statement cue=named-heading name="Using Bézout's identity"}

### Using Bézout's identity {#Using_Bézout's_identity}

In modern mathematics, a common proof involves [Bézout's identity](wikipedia://en/4947), which was unknown at Euclid's time.[^o20] Bézout's identity states that if $x$ and $y$ are [coprime integers](wikipedia://en/6556) (i.e. they share no common divisors other than 1 and −1) there exist integers $r$ and $s$ such that

$$rx+sy = 1.$$

Let $a$ and $n$ be coprime, and assume that $n|ab$. By Bézout's identity, there are $r$ and $s$ such that

$$rn+sa = 1.$$

Multiply both sides by $b$:

$$rnb+sab = b.$$

The first term on the left is divisible by $n$, and the second term is divisible by $ab$, which by hypothesis is divisible by $n$. Therefore their sum, $b$, is also divisible by $n$.

:::

### By induction {#By_induction}

The following proof is inspired by Euclid's version of [Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/10377), which proceeds by using only subtractions.

Suppose that $n \mid ab$ and that $n$ and $a$ are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) (that is, their [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) is $1$). One has to prove that $n$ divides $b$. Since $n \mid ab,$ there exists an integer $q$ such that

$$nq=ab.$$

Without loss of generality, one can suppose that $n$, $q$, $a$, and $b$ are positive, since the divisibility relation is independent of the signs of the involved integers.

To prove the theorem by [strong induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_induction), we suppose that it has been proved for all smaller values of $ab$. There are three cases:

1. If $n = a$, coprimality implies $n = 1$, and $n$ divides $b$ trivially.
1. If $n < a$, then subtracting $nb$ from both sides gives $n(q-b)=(a-n)b.$ Thus, $n$ divides $(a - n) b$. Since we assumed that $n$ and $a$ are coprime, it follows that $a - n$ and $n$ must be coprime. (If not, their greatest common divisor $d$ would divide their sum $a$ as well as $n$, contradicting our assumption.) The conclusion therefore follows by induction hypothesis, since $0 < (a - n) b < ab$.
1. If $n > a$ then subtracting $aq$ from both sides gives $(n-a)q=a(b-q).$ Thus, $n - a$ divides $a (b - q)$. Since (as in the previous case), $n - a$ and $a$ are coprime, and since $0 < b - q < b$, then the induction hypothesis implies that $n - a$ divides $b - q$; that is, $b-q=r(n-a)$ for some integer $r$. So, $(n-a)q=ar(n-a),$ and, by dividing by $n - a$, one has $q=ar.$ Therefore, $ab=nq=anr,$ and by dividing by $a$, one gets $b=nr,$ the desired conclusion.

::: {#o23 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of Elements"}

### Proof of *Elements* {#Proof_of_Elements}

Euclid's lemma is proved at the Proposition 30 in Book VII of [Euclid's *Elements*](wikipedia://en/244107). The original proof is difficult to understand as is, so we quote the commentary from (Euclid 1956, 319–332).

::: {#o25 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=inline-head name="Proposition 19"}

**Proposition 19**

If four numbers be proportional, the number produced from the first and fourth is equal to the number produced from the second and third; and, if the number produced from the first and fourth be equal to that produced from the second and third, the four numbers are proportional.

:::

::: {#o26 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=inline-head name="Proposition 20"}

**Proposition 20**

The least numbers of those that have the same ratio with them measures those that have the same ratio the same number of times—the greater the greater and the less the less.

:::

::: {#o27 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=inline-head name="Proposition 21"}

**Proposition 21**

Numbers prime to one another are the least of those that have the same ratio with them.

:::

::: {#o28 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=inline-head name="Proposition 29"}

**Proposition 29**

Any prime number is prime to any number it does not measure.

:::

::: {#o29 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=inline-head name="Proposition 30"}

**Proposition 30**

If two numbers, by multiplying one another, make the same number, and any prime number measures the product, it also measures one of the original numbers.

:::

::: {#o30 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof of 30"}

**Proof of 30**

If *c*, a prime number, measure *ab*, *c* measures either *a* or *b*. Suppose *c* does not measure *a*. Therefore *c*, *a* are prime to one another. ［VII. 29］ Suppose *ab*=*mc*. Therefore *c* : *a* = *b* : *m*. ［VII. 19］ Hence ［VII. 20, 21］ *b*=*nc*, where *n* is some integer. Therefore *c* measures *b*. Similarly, if *c* does not measure *b*, *c* measures *a*. Therefore *c* measures one or other of the two numbers *a*, *b*. [Q.E.D.](wikipedia://en/25228)[^o31]

:::
