# Hyperrectangle

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hyperrectangle"}

*Image: N-wymiarowe sześciany.svg.* Projections of $k$-cells onto the plane (from $k\in\{1,\dots{},6\}$). Only the edges of the higher-dimensional cells are shown.

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **hyperrectangle** (also called a **box**, **hyperbox**, **$k$-cell** or **orthotope**[^o4]), is the generalization of a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048) (a [plane figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_figure)) and the [rectangular cuboid](wikipedia://en/38848329) (a [solid figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_figure)) to [higher dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_dimension). A [necessary and sufficient condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_condition) is that it is [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) to the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of finite [intervals](wikipedia://en/49172).[^o5] This means that a $k$-dimensional rectangular solid has each of its edges equal to one of the closed intervals used in the definition. Every $k$-cell is [compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_%28mathematics%29).[^o6][^o7]

:::

If all of the edges are equal length, it is a *[hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783)*. A hyperrectangle is a special case of a [parallelotope](wikipedia://en/25207334#Related_shapes).

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

For every integer $i$ from $1$ to $k$, let $a_i$ and $b_i$ be [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) such that $a_i < b_i$. The set of all points $x=(x_1,\dots,x_k)$ in $\mathbb{R}^k$ whose coordinates satisfy the inequalities $a_i\leq x_i\leq b_i$ is a **$k$-cell**.[^o10]

:::

## Intuition {#Intuition}

A $k$-cell of dimension $k\leq 3$ is especially simple. For example, a 1-cell is simply the interval $[a,b]$ with $a < b$. A 2-cell is the rectangle formed by the Cartesian product of two closed intervals, and a 3-cell is a rectangular solid.

The sides and edges of a $k$-cell need not be equal in (Euclidean) length; although the [unit cube](wikipedia://en/407763) (which has boundaries of equal Euclidean length) is a 3-cell, the set of all 3-cells with equal-length edges is a strict subset of the set of all 3-cells.

::: {#o13 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Hypercuboid"}

## Types {#Types}

A four-dimensional orthotope is likely a hypercuboid.[^o14]

The special case of an $n$-dimensional orthotope where all edges have equal length is the $n$-[cube](wikipedia://en/6285) or hypercube.[^o4]

By analogy, the term "hyperrectangle" can refer to Cartesian products of [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) intervals of other kinds, such as ranges of keys in [database theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database_theory) or ranges of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), rather than [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438).[^o15]

:::

## Dual polytope {#Dual_polytope}

The [dual polytope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_polytope) of an $n$-orthotope has been variously called a rectangular $n$-[orthoplex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthoplex), rhombic $n$-[fusil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fusil_%28geometry%29), or $n$-[lozenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lozenge_%28shape%29). It is constructed by $2n$ points located in the center of the orthotope rectangular faces.

An $n$-fusil's [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) can be represented by a sum of $n$ orthogonal line segments: $\{ \} + \{ \} + ... + \{ \}$ or $n\{ \}.$

A 1-fusil is a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860). A 2-fusil is a [rhombus](wikipedia://en/183884). Its plane cross selections in all pairs of axes are [rhombi](wikipedia://en/183884).
