# Equichordal point

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Equichordal point"}

*Image: Equichordal-center.svg.* Curve (red) with two chords (black), intersecting in the equichordal point.

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), an **equichordal point** is a point defined relative to a [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) plane curve such that all [chords](wikipedia://en/297947) passing through the point are equal in length. Two common figures with equichordal points are the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) and the [limaçon](wikipedia://en/641715). It is impossible for a curve to have more than one equichordal point.

:::

## Equichordal curves {#Equichordal_curves}

A curve is called equichordal when it has an equichordal point.[^o3] Such a curve may be constructed as the [pedal curve](wikipedia://en/845864) of a [curve of constant width](wikipedia://en/580238).[^o4] For instance, the pedal curve of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) is either another circle (when the center of the circle is the pedal point) or a [limaçon](wikipedia://en/641715); both are equichordal curves.

## Multiple equichordal points {#Multiple_equichordal_points}

In 1916 Fujiwara proposed the question of whether a curve could have two equichordal points (offering in the same paper a proof that three or more is impossible). Independently, a year later, Blaschke, Rothe and Weitzenböck posed the same question.[^o6] The problem remained unsolved until it was finally proven impossible in 1996 by [Marek Rychlik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marek_Rychlik).[^o7][^o8] Despite its elementary formulation, the [equichordal point problem](wikipedia://en/29744555) was difficult to solve. [Rychlik's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rychlik's_theorem) is proved by methods of advanced complex analysis and algebraic geometry and it is 72 pages long.
