# Schneider–Lang theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Schneider–Lang theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Schneider–Lang theorem** is a refinement by (Lang 1966) of a theorem of (Schneider 1949) about the [transcendence](wikipedia://en/30325) of values of [meromorphic functions](wikipedia://en/80857). The theorem implies both the [Hermite–Lindemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite–Lindemann_theorem) and [Gelfond–Schneider theorems](wikipedia://en/609487), and implies the transcendence of some values of [elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/69939) and [elliptic modular functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_modular_function).

:::

::: {#o5 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Fix a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) $K$ and [meromorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meromorphic) $f_{1}, ..., f_{N}$, of which at least two are algebraically independent and have [orders](wikipedia://en/10372) $\rho_{1}$ and $\rho_{2}$, and such that $f_{j} \in K[f_{1}, ..., f_{N}]$ for any $j$. Then there are at most

$$(\rho_1+\rho_2) [K:\mathbb{Q}] \,$$

distinct [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\omega_{1}, ..., \omega_{m}$ such that $f_{i}(\omega_{j}) \in K$ for all combinations of $i$ and $j$.

:::

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- If $f_{1}(z) = z$ and $f_{2}(z) = e^{z}$ then the theorem implies the [Hermite–Lindemann theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite–Lindemann_theorem) that $e^{\alpha}$ is transcendental for nonzero [algebraic](wikipedia://en/1158) $\alpha$: otherwise, $\alpha, 2\alpha, 3\alpha, ...$ would be an infinite number of values at which both $f_{1}$ and $f_{2}$ are algebraic.
- Similarly taking $f_{1}(z) = e^{z}$ and $f_{2}(z) = e^{\beta z}$ for $\beta$ [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689) algebraic implies the [Gelfond–Schneider theorem](wikipedia://en/609487) that if $\alpha$ and $\alpha^{\beta}$ are algebraic, then $\alpha \in \{0,1$}: otherwise, $\log(\alpha), 2\log(\alpha), 3\log(\alpha), ...$ would be an infinite number of values at which both $f_{1}$ and $f_{2}$ are algebraic.
- Recall that the [Weierstrass P function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_P_function) satisfies the [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309)

$$\wp'(z)^2 = 4\wp(z)^3-g_2\wp(z)-g_3. \,$$

Taking the three functions to be $z$, $\wp(\alpha z)$, $\wp(\alpha z)$ shows that, for any algebraic $\alpha$, if $g_{2}(\alpha)$ and $g_{3}(\alpha)$ are algebraic, then $\wp(\alpha)$ is transcendental.

- Taking the functions to be $z$ and $e^{f(z)}$ for a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) $f$ of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) $\rho$ shows that the number of points where the functions are all algebraic can grow linearly with the order $\rho = \deg f$.

:::

::: {#o9 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

To [prove](wikipedia://en/82285) the result Lang took two algebraically independent functions from $f_{1}, ..., f_{N}$, say, $f$ and $g$, and then created an auxiliary function $F \in K[ f, g]$. Using [Siegel's lemma](wikipedia://en/11816319), he then showed that one could assume $F$ vanished to a high order at the $\omega_{1}, ..., \omega_{m}$. Thus a high-order [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of $F$ takes a value of small size at one such $\omega_{i}$s, "size" here referring to [an algebraic property of a number](wikipedia://en/3141855). Using the [maximum modulus principle](wikipedia://en/880710), Lang also found a separate estimate for absolute values of derivatives of $F$. Standard results connect the size of a number and its absolute value, and the combined estimates imply the claimed bound on $m$.

:::

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Bombieri's theorem"}

## Bombieri's theorem {#Bombieri's_theorem}

(Bombieri Lang 1970) and (Bombieri 1970) generalized the result to functions of several variables. Bombieri showed that if *K* is an algebraic number field and *f*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *f*<sub>*N*</sub> are meromorphic functions of *d* complex variables of order at most ρ generating a field *K*(*f*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *f*<sub>*N*</sub>) of transcendence degree at least *d* + 1 that is closed under all [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565), then the set of points where all the functions *f*<sub>*n*</sub> have values in *K* is contained in an algebraic hypersurface in **C**<sup>*d*</sup> of degree at most

$$d((d+1)\rho[K:\mathbb{Q}]+1).$$

(Waldschmidt 1979, theorem 5.1.1) gave a simpler proof of Bombieri's theorem, with a slightly stronger bound of *d*(ρ<sub>1</sub> + ... + ρ<sub>*d*+1</sub>)\[*K*:**Q**\] for the degree, where the ρ<sub>*j*</sub> are the orders of *d* + 1 algebraically independent functions. The special case *d* = 1 gives the Schneider–Lang theorem, with a bound of (ρ<sub>1</sub> + ρ<sub>2</sub>)\[*K*:**Q**\] for the number of points.

::: {#o16 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

If $p$ is a polynomial with [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) then the functions $z_1,...,z_n,e^{p(z_1,...,z_n)}$ are all algebraic at a dense set of points of the hypersurface $p=0$.

:::
