# Jillian Beardwood

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Jillian Beardwood"}

**Jillian Beardwood** (20 December 1934 – 28 October 2019)[^o3] was a British mathematician known for the Beardwood–Halton–Hammersley theorem.[^o4] Published by the [Cambridge Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Philosophical_Society) in a 1959 article entitled "The Shortest Path Through Many Points",[^o5] the [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) provides an [asymptotic formula](wikipedia://en/641995) for the length of a [traveling salesperson tour](wikipedia://en/31248) of a given number of points in a [unit square](wikipedia://en/285301), both in the [worst case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worst_case_analysis) and for random points.[^o4][^o6]

:::

## Early life {#Early_life}

Beardwood was born in [Norwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwich), England in 1934. After attending [The Blyth School for Girls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewell_Park_Academy), she studied mathematics at [St. Hugh's College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Hugh's_College,_Oxford), earning [first-class honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_undergraduate_degree_classification#First-class_honours) and a [master's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master's_degree) in 1956.[^o8]

## Mathematics career {#Mathematics_career}

After university, Beardwood accepted a position at the newly formed [United Kingdom Atomic Energy Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_Atomic_Energy_Authority) (UKAEA), where she was one of four postgraduate students selected to study with [John Hammersley](wikipedia://en/656531), a professor at [Trinity College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Oxford). In that position, Beardwood gained access to the [Ferranti Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferranti_Mercury) computer at the UKAEA's research facility at [Harwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harwell_Science_and_Innovation_Campus), as well as to the [ILLIAC II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ILLIAC_II) computer at the [University of Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois). She was later promoted to Senior Scientific Officer at the UKAEA, where she specialised in [Monte Carlo methods](wikipedia://en/56098) and algorithms for modeling [complex geometrical situations](wikipedia://en/186101).[^o8]

## The Beardwood-Halton-Hammersley Theorem {#The_Beardwood-Halton-Hammersley_Theorem}

The problem of determining the shortest closed path through a given set of n points is often called the "travelling salesman problem". A salesman, starting at and finally returning to his base, visits (n-1) other towns by the shortest possible route. If it is large, it may be prohibitively difficult to calculate the total mileages for each of the (n-1)! orders in which the towns may be visited, and to choose the smallest total.

As a practical substitute for an exact formula to determine the length of the shortest path, the Beardwood-Halton-Hammersley Theorem derived a simple asymptotic formula for the shortest length when n is large. The travelling salesman problem can involve either fixed or random points distributed over a certain region. The Theorem established that the shortest length between random points is asymptotically equal to a non-random function of n. For large n the distinction between the random and the non-random versions of the problem effectively vanishes. [David L. Applegate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Applegate) described this in 2011 as a "famous result", and said "The remarkable theorem of Beardwood-Halton-Hammersley has received considerable attention in the research community, "with demonstrated uses in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), physics, [operations research](wikipedia://en/43476) and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science).[^o12]

:::

## Later career {#Later_career}

After leaving the UKAEA in 1968, Beardwood worked in transport modeling for the UK government's [Road Research Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_Research_Laboratory). In 1973, she joined the staff of the [Greater London Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_London_Council) (GLC) where she directed the transport studies group until the GLC was dissolved in 1987. Her team helped to plan the [M25 orbital motorway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M25_motorway) around London and early [congestion pricing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congestion_pricing) systems.

One of Beardwood's most cited studies for the GLC, "Roads Generate Traffic", found that highway construction encourages people to drive and leads to increased congestion.[^o14][^o15] "All that increases in road capacity do is allow people to abandon public transport in favour of the car".[^o16] Beardwood's research accurately predicted that the M25 would quickly exceed its maximum capacity. It has been cited in support of policies encouraging the use of bicycles and other alternatives to cars.[^o17] Similarly, her later work included a study which predicted that the proposed [East London River Crossing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_London_River_Crossing) would quickly become congested if there were no significant routes available to provide relief.[^o3]

After the GLC's dissolution, Beardwood was employed in (and was a retained consultant to) the private sector, including for the transport planning consultancy MVA, Marcial Echenique and Partners Ltd and [WSP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WSP_Global) Group. She also worked in academic posts, as a senior research fellow at the [London School of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_School_of_Economics) and as a lecturer in transport planning at the [Polytechnic of Central London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytechnic_of_Central_London) (1989–90).[^o3]
