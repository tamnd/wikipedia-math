# Mary Tsingou

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Mary Tsingou"}

**Mary Tsingou-Menzel** (October 14, 1928 – August 27, 2026) was an American physicist and mathematician of [Greek-Bulgarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_people) descent.[^o3] She was one of the first programmers on the [MANIAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MANIAC) computer at [Los Alamos National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Alamos_National_Laboratory) and is best known for having coded the [celebrated computer experiment](wikipedia://en/2864857) with [Enrico Fermi](wikipedia://en/10264), [John Pasta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Pasta), and [Stanisław Ulam](wikipedia://en/41531). This experiment became an inspiration for the fields of [chaos theory](wikipedia://en/6295) and [scientific computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_computing), and was a turning point in [soliton](wikipedia://en/51654) theory.

:::

## Background {#Background}

Mary Tsingou was born in [Milwaukee, Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milwaukee,_Wisconsin), on October 14, 1928, her Greek parents having moved to the United States from [Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgaria). In the aftermath of the Great Depression, the family left the US to spend several years in Bulgaria. In 1940, they returned to the States, where Tsingou attended high school and college. She graduated with a bachelor's degree in mathematics and education in 1951 from the [University of Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin). She then studied at the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan), receiving a master's degree in mathematics in 1955. In 1958, she married Joseph Menzel.[^o14] She had two daughters, Ann and Carol, who grew up to be medical doctors.[^o5]

Tsingou died on August 27, 2026, at the age of 97.[^o5]

## Career {#Career}

Tsingou joined the theoretical division of the [Los Alamos National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Alamos_National_Laboratory), where she became one of the first programmers on the [MANIAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MANIAC). Besides working on weapons, the group also studied fundamental physics. Following Fermi's suggestion to analyze numerically the predictions of a statistical model of solids, Tsingou came up with an algorithm to simulate the relaxation of energy in a model crystal, which she implemented on the MANIAC.[^o14] The analysis became known in the computational physics community as the [Fermi–Pasta–Ulam problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi–Pasta–Ulam_problem) (FPU). In 2008, Tsingou's contributions were recognized and the analysis was renamed as the Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou problem (FPUT).[^o7][^o8] The result was an important stepping stone for [chaos theory](wikipedia://en/6295).[^o9]

*Image: The MANIAC’s arithmetic unit nearing completion in 1952.jpg.* **The MANIAC’s arithmetic unit nearing completion in 1952.**

After Fermi's death, [James L. Tuck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_L._Tuck) and Tsingou-Menzel repeated the original FPUT results and provided strong indication that the nonlinear FPUT problem might be integrable.[^o10]

Tsingou-Menzel continued her computational career at Los Alamos. She was an early expert on [Fortran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortran). In the 1980s, she worked on calculations for the "proton storage ring" in the [Star Wars program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_Defense_Initiative) (the Strategic Defense Initiative), which was one of President Ronald Reagan's projects.[^o15][^o11] She retired in 1991.[^o14]

## Recognition {#Recognition}

The paper published by Los Alamos National Laboratory in 1955 earned recognition for Fermi, Pasta, and Ulam for its novel discoveries, with Tsingou being acknowledged in the footnote. It was not until 2008, when an article published in *[Physics Today](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_Today)* called to rename the FPU problem to the FPUT problem to give her proper credit for her contribution.[^o14] Subsequent publications referencing the FPUT problem reflect this change.[^o13][^o14] In 2020, [National Security Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Science) magazine, published by Los Alamos National Laboratory, featured an article on Tsingou that included her commentary and historical reflections on the FPUT problem. The article was titled "We thank Miss Mary Tsingou" in reference to the acknowledgement that appeared on the title page of the original FPUT technical report from 1955.[^o15]
