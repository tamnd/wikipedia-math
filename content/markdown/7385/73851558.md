# Lovász–Woodall conjecture

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lovász–Woodall conjecture"}

*Image: Lovász–Woodall conjecture.svg.* An illustration of the Lovász–Woodall conjecture in the [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926): the graph is 3-connected, and 3 edges lie on a common cycle.

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **Lovász–Woodall conjecture** is a long-standing problem on cycles in graphs. It says:

:::

If $G$ is a [$k$-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph) graph and $L$ is a set of $k$ [independent edges](wikipedia://en/581797) in $G$, then $G$ has a [cycle](wikipedia://en/168609) containing $L$, unless $k$ is odd and $L$ is an [edge cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_cut).

It was proposed independently by [László Lovász](wikipedia://en/1623938)[^o2] in 1974 and by [D. R. Woodall](wikipedia://en/70804411)[^o3] in 1977.

## Background and motivation {#Background_and_motivation}

Many results in graph theory, starting with [Menger's theorem](wikipedia://en/1155738), guarantee the existence of paths or cycles in a $k$-connected graph. For 2-connected graphs, Menger's theorem is equivalent to the statement that any two vertices lie on a common cycle. A theorem of [G. A. Dirac](wikipedia://en/1643806) generalizes this claim: if a graph is $k$-connected for $k \ge 2$, then for every set of $k$ vertices in the graph there is a cycle that passes through all the vertices in the set.[^o5]

Another corollary of Menger's theorem is that in 2-connected graphs, any two edges lie on a common cycle. The proof, however, does not generalize to the corresponding statement for $k$ edges in a $k$-connected graph; rather, Menger's theorem can be used to show that in a $k$-connected graph, given any 2 edges and $k - 2$ vertices, there is a cycle passing through all of them.[^o6]

There is one obstacle to the stronger claim that in a $k$-connected graph $G$, given any set $L$ of $k$ edges, there should be a cycle containing $L$. Suppose that the edges in $L$ form an edge cut: the vertices of $G$ can be separated into two sets $A$ and $B$ such that the edges in $L$ all join a vertex in $A$ to a vertex in $B$, and are the only edges to do so. Then any cycle in $G$ can only use an even number of edges of $L$: it must cross from $A$ to $B$ and from $B$ back to $A$ an equal number of times. If $k$ is odd, this means that no cycle can contain all of $L$.

The Lovász–Woodall conjecture states that this is the only obstacle: given any set $L$ of $k$ edges, there is a cycle containing $L$, except in the case that $k$ is odd and $L$ is an edge cut.

Woodall proposed the conjecture as one of several possible statements[^o3] that would imply a conjecture made by [Claude Berge](wikipedia://en/5893760): given a $k$-connected graph $G$ with [independence number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_number) $\alpha(G)$, and any [subgraph](wikipedia://en/325802#subgraph) $F$ of $G$ with at most $k - \alpha(G)$ edges whose [components](wikipedia://en/246223) are all paths, $G$ has a [Hamiltonian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle) containing $F$.[^o7] In 1982, Roland Häggkvist and [Carsten Thomassen](wikipedia://en/7460839) proved Berge's conjecture by proving one of the weaker statements proposed by Woodall.[^o8]

## Partial results {#Partial_results}

As mentioned above, the $k = 2$ case of the Lovász–Woodall conjecture follows from Menger's theorem. The $k = 3$ case was given as an exercise by Lovász.[^o14] After the conjecture was made, it was proven for $k = 4$ by Péter L. Erdős and E. Győri[^o10] and independently by Michael V. Lomonosov.,[^o11] and for $k = 5$ by Daniel P. Sanders.[^o12]

Other partial progress toward the conjecture has included versions of the result with a stronger assumption on connectivity. Woodall's paper[^o3] included a proof that the conclusion of the conjecture holds if $G$ is $(2k - 2)$-connected, and in 1977, Thomassen proved that the conjecture holds if $G$ is $(3k - 1)/2$-connected.[^o13] In 1982, Häggkvist and Thomassen proved that the conjecture holds if $G$ is $(k + 1)$-connected.[^o8]

In 2002, [Ken-ichi Kawarabayashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken-ichi_Kawarabayashi) proved that under the hypotheses of the conjecture, $L$ is either contained in a cycle of $G$ or in two disjoint cycles.[^o14]

## Current status {#Current_status}

In two publications in 2002[^o14] and 2008,[^o16] Kawarabayashi claimed to have a proof on the conjecture, giving an outline for the proof and leaving several steps to future publications, but the full proof has not been published since.
