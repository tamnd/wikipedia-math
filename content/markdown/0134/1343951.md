# Seifert surface

*Image: Borromean Seifert surface.png.* A Seifert surface bounded by a set of [Borromean rings](wikipedia://en/873118).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Seifert surface"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Seifert surface** (named after [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Herbert Seifert](wikipedia://en/637024)[^o2][^o3]) is an orientable [surface](wikipedia://en/27865) whose [boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_of_a_manifold) is a given [knot](wikipedia://en/366808) or [link](wikipedia://en/1161784).

:::

Such surfaces can be used to study the properties of the associated knot or link. For example, many [knot invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_invariants) are most easily calculated using a Seifert surface. Seifert surfaces are also interesting in their own right, and the subject of considerable research.

Specifically, let *L* be a [tame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tame_knot) [oriented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented) knot or link in [Euclidean 3-space](wikipedia://en/9697) (or in the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792)). A Seifert surface is a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), [connected](wikipedia://en/6233), [oriented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented) [surface](wikipedia://en/27865) *S* embedded in 3-space whose boundary is *L* such that the orientation on *L* is just the induced orientation from *S*.

Note that any compact, connected, oriented surface with nonempty boundary in [Euclidean 3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_3-space) is the Seifert surface associated to its boundary link. A single knot or link can have many different inequivalent Seifert surfaces. A Seifert surface must be [oriented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented). It is possible to associate surfaces to knots which are neither oriented nor orientable as well.

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Hopf band wikipedia.png.* A Seifert surface for the [Hopf link](wikipedia://en/1515511). This is an annulus, not a Möbius strip. It has two half-twists and is thus orientable.

The standard [Möbius strip](wikipedia://en/37817) has the [unknot](wikipedia://en/60075) for a boundary but is not a Seifert surface for the unknot because it is not orientable.

The "checkerboard" coloring of the usual minimal crossing projection of the [trefoil knot](wikipedia://en/404773) gives a Mobius strip with three half twists. As with the previous example, this is not a Seifert surface as it is not orientable. Applying Seifert's algorithm to this diagram, as expected, does produce a Seifert surface; in this case, it is a punctured torus of genus *g* = 1, and the Seifert matrix is

$$V = \begin{pmatrix}1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}.$$

:::

## Existence and Seifert matrix {#Existence_and_Seifert_matrix}

It is a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) that any link always has an associated Seifert surface. This theorem was first published by Frankl and [Pontryagin](wikipedia://en/582075) in 1930.[^o7] A different proof was published in 1934 by [Herbert Seifert](wikipedia://en/637024) and relies on what is now called the Seifert algorithm. The [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) produces a Seifert surface $S$, given a projection of the knot or link in question.

Suppose that link has *m* components (*m* = 1 for a knot), the diagram has *d* crossing points, and resolving the crossings (preserving the orientation of the knot) yields *f* circles. Then the surface $S$ is constructed from *f* disjoint disks by attaching *d* bands. The [homology group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_group) $H_1(S)$ is free abelian on 2*g* generators, where

$$g = \frac{1}{2}(2 + d - f - m)$$

is the [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) of $S$. The [intersection form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersection_form_%284-manifold%29) *Q* on $H_1(S)$ is [skew-symmetric](wikipedia://en/174055), and there is a basis of 2*g* cycles $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_{2g}$ with $Q = (Q(a_i, a_j))$ equal to a direct sum of the *g* copies of the matrix

$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

*Image: Seiferthomologypushoff.png.* An illustration of (curves isotopic to) the pushoffs of a homology generator *a* in the positive and negative directions for a Seifert surface of the figure eight knot.

The 2*g* × 2*g* integer **Seifert matrix**

$$V = (v(i, j))$$

has $v(i, j)$ the [linking number](wikipedia://en/451999) in [Euclidean 3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_3-space) (or in the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792)) of *a*<sub>*i*</sub> and the "pushoff" of *a*<sub>*j*</sub> in the positive direction of $S$. More precisely, recalling that Seifert surfaces are bicollared, meaning that we can extend the embedding of $S$ to an embedding of $S \times [-1, 1]$, given some representative loop $x$ which is homology generator in the interior of $S$, the positive pushout is $x \times \{1\}$ and the negative pushout is $x \times \{-1\}$.[^o8]

With this, we have

$$V - V^* = Q,$$

where *V*<sup>∗</sup> = (*v*(*j*, *i*)) the transpose matrix. Every integer 2*g* × 2*g* matrix $V$ with $V - V^* = Q$ arises as the Seifert matrix of a knot with genus *g* Seifert surface.

The [Alexander polynomial](wikipedia://en/1107647) is computed from the Seifert matrix by $A(t) = \det\left(V - tV^*\right),$ which is a polynomial of degree at most 2*g* in the indeterminate $t.$ The Alexander polynomial is independent of the choice of Seifert surface $S,$ and is an invariant of the knot or link.

The [signature of a knot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_of_a_knot) is the [signature](wikipedia://en/1803375#Signature_and_Sylvester's_law_of_inertia) of the symmetric Seifert matrix $V + V^\mathrm{T}.$ It is again an invariant of the knot or link.

## Genus of a knot {#Genus_of_a_knot}

Seifert surfaces are not at all unique: a Seifert surface *S* of genus *g* and Seifert matrix *V* can be modified by a [topological surgery](wikipedia://en/5961115), resulting in a Seifert surface *S*′ of genus *g* + 1 and Seifert matrix

$$V' = V \oplus \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The **genus** of a knot *K* is the [knot invariant](wikipedia://en/166898) defined by the minimal [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) *g* of a Seifert surface for *K*.

For instance:

- An [unknot](wikipedia://en/60075)—which is, by definition, the boundary of a [disc](wikipedia://en/329542)—has genus zero. Moreover, the unknot is the only knot with genus zero.
- The [trefoil knot](wikipedia://en/404773) has genus 1, as does the [figure-eight knot](wikipedia://en/59565).
- The genus of a (*p*,*q*)-[torus knot](wikipedia://en/1165182) is (*p* − 1)(*q* − 1)/2
- The degree of a knot's [Alexander polynomial](wikipedia://en/1107647) is a lower bound on twice its genus.

A fundamental property of the genus is that it is additive with respect to the [knot sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_sum):

$$g(K_1 \mathbin{\#} K_2) = g(K_1) + g(K_2)$$

In general, the genus of a knot is difficult to compute, and the Seifert algorithm usually does not produce a Seifert surface of least genus. For this reason other related invariants are sometimes useful. The **canonical genus** $g_c$ of a knot is the least genus of all Seifert surfaces that can be constructed by the Seifert algorithm, and the **free genus** $g_f$ is the least genus of all Seifert surfaces whose complement in $S^3$ is a [handlebody](wikipedia://en/864472#3-dimensional_handlebodies). (The complement of a Seifert surface generated by the Seifert algorithm is always a handlebody.) For any knot the inequality $g \leq g_f \leq g_c$ obviously holds, so in particular these invariants place upper bounds on the genus.[^o11]

The knot genus is [NP-complete](wikipedia://en/23385892) by work of [Ian Agol](wikipedia://en/32084718), [Joel Hass](wikipedia://en/36320244) and [William Thurston](wikipedia://en/39190).[^o12]

It has been shown that there are Seifert surfaces of the same genus that do not become [isotopic](wikipedia://en/212250#Isotopy) either topologically or smoothly in the 4-ball.[^o13][^o14]
