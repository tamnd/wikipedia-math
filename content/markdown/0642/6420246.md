# Sinusoidal plane-wave solutions of the electromagnetic wave equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sinusoidal plane-wave solutions of the electromagnetic wave equation"}

**Sinusoidal plane-wave solutions** are particular solutions to the [wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691).

:::

The general solution of the electromagnetic [wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691) in homogeneous, linear, time-independent media can be written as a [linear superposition](wikipedia://en/1201321) of plane-waves of different frequencies and [polarizations](wikipedia://en/41564).

The treatment in this article is [classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_physics) but, because of the generality of [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737) for electrodynamics, the treatment can be converted into the [quantum mechanical](wikipedia://en/25202) treatment with only a reinterpretation of classical quantities (aside from the quantum mechanical treatment needed for charge and current densities).

The reinterpretation is based on the theories of [Max Planck](wikipedia://en/19848) and the interpretations by [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736) of those theories and of other experiments. The quantum generalization of the classical treatment can be found in the articles on [photon polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon_polarization) and photon dynamics in the double-slit experiment.

## Explanation {#Explanation}

Experimentally, every light signal can be decomposed into a [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_spectrum) of frequencies and wavelengths associated with sinusoidal solutions of the wave equation. Polarizing filters can be used to decompose light into its various polarization components. The polarization components can be [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_polarization), [circular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_polarization) or [elliptical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptical_polarization).

## Plane waves {#Plane_waves}

The plane [sinusoidal](wikipedia://en/324749) solution for an [electromagnetic wave](wikipedia://en/2924436) traveling in the z direction is

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{E} ( \mathbf{r} , t ) &= \begin{pmatrix} E_{0,x} \cos \left ( kz-\omega t + \alpha_x \right ) \\ E_{0,y} \cos \left ( kz-\omega t + \alpha_y \right ) \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \\[1ex] &= E_{0,x} \cos \left ( kz-\omega t + \alpha_x \right ) \, \hat {\mathbf{x}} \; + \; E_{0,y} \cos \left ( kz-\omega t + \alpha_y \right ) \, \hat {\mathbf{y}} \end{aligned}$$

for the electric field and

$$\begin{aligned} c \, \mathbf{B} ( \mathbf{r} , t ) &= \hat { \mathbf{z} } \times \mathbf{E} ( \mathbf{r} , t ) \\[1ex] &= \begin{pmatrix} -E_{0,y} \cos \left ( kz-\omega t + \alpha_y \right ) \\ \hphantom{-}E_{0,x} \cos \left ( kz-\omega t + \alpha_x \right ) \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \\[1ex] &= -E_{0,y} \cos \left ( kz-\omega t + \alpha_y \right ) \hat {\mathbf{x}} \; + \; E_{0,x} \cos \left ( kz-\omega t + \alpha_x \right ) \hat {\mathbf{y}} \end{aligned}$$

for the magnetic field, where k is the [wavenumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavenumber),

$$\omega = c k$$

$\omega$ is the [angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency) of the wave, and $c$ is the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light). The hats on the [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29) indicate [unit vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_vectors) in the x, y, and z directions. $\mathbf{r} = (x, y, z)$ is the position vector (in meters).

The plane wave is parameterized by the [amplitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude)

*Image: Light-wave.svg.* Electromagnetic radiation can be imagined as a self-propagating transverse oscillating wave of electric and magnetic fields. This diagram shows a plane linearly polarized wave propagating from right to left. The magnetic field (labeled M) is in a horizontal plane, and the electric field (labeled E) is in a vertical plane.

$$\begin{aligned} E_{0,x} &= \left| \mathbf{E} \right| \cos \theta \\[1.56ex] E_{0,y} &= \left| \mathbf{E} \right| \sin \theta \end{aligned}$$

and [phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29)

$$\alpha_x , \alpha_y$$

where

$$\theta \ \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\ \tan^{-1} \left ( \frac{E_{0,y}}{E_{0, x}} \right ) .$$

and

$$\left| \mathbf{E} \right|^2 \ \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\ \left ( E_{0,x} \right )^2 + \left ( E_{0,y} \right )^2 .$$

## Polarization state vector {#Polarization_state_vector}

### Jones vector {#Jones_vector}

All the polarization information can be reduced to a single vector, called the [Jones vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jones_vector), in the x-y plane. This vector, while arising from a purely classical treatment of polarization, can be interpreted as a [quantum state](wikipedia://en/30876419) vector. The connection with quantum mechanics is made in the article on [photon polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon_polarization).

The vector emerges from the plane-wave solution. The electric field solution can be rewritten in [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) notation as

$$\mathbf{E} ( \mathbf{r} , t ) = |\mathbf{E}| \, \operatorname\mathcal{R_e}\left[ |\psi\rangle e^{ i ( kz - \omega t ) } \right]$$

where

$$|\psi\rangle \ \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\ \begin{pmatrix} \psi_x \\ \psi_y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(\theta) e^{i \alpha_x} \\ \sin(\theta) e^{i \alpha_y} \end{pmatrix}$$

is the Jones vector in the x-y plane. The notation for this vector is the [bra–ket notation](wikipedia://en/4542) of [Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742), which is normally used in a quantum context. The quantum notation is used here in anticipation of the interpretation of the Jones vector as a quantum state vector.

### Dual Jones vector {#Dual_Jones_vector}

The Jones vector has a [dual](wikipedia://en/7988) given by

$$\langle \psi | \ \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\ \begin{pmatrix} \psi_x^* & \psi_y^* \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(\theta) e^{-i \alpha_x} & \sin(\theta) e^{-i \alpha_y} \end{pmatrix}.$$

### Normalization of the Jones vector {#Normalization_of_the_Jones_vector}

*Image: Linear polarization schematic.png.* Linear polarization.

A Jones vector represents a specific wave with a specific phase, amplitude and state of polarization. When one is using a Jones vector simply to indicate a state of polarization, then it is customary for it to be [normalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalisable_wavefunction). That requires that the [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) of the vector with itself to be unity:

$$\langle \psi | \psi \rangle = \begin{pmatrix} \psi_x^* & \psi_y^* \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \psi_x \\ \psi_y \end{pmatrix} = 1 .$$

An arbitrary Jones vector can simply be scaled to achieve this property. All normalized Jones vectors represent a wave of the same intensity (within a particular isotropic medium). Even given a normalized Jones vector, multiplication by a pure phase factor will result in a different normalized Jones vector representing the same state of polarization.

## Polarization states {#Polarization_states}

*Image: Elliptical polarization schematic.png.* Elliptical polarization.

### Linear polarization {#Linear_polarization}

In general, the wave is linearly polarized when the phase angles $\alpha_x , \alpha_y$ are equal,

$$\alpha_x = \alpha_y \ \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\ \alpha .$$

This represents a wave polarized at an angle $\theta$ with respect to the x axis. In that case the Jones vector can be written

$$|\psi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta \\ \sin\theta \end{pmatrix} e^{i \alpha} .$$

### Elliptical and circular polarization {#Elliptical_and_circular_polarization}

The general case in which the electric field is not confined to one direction but rotates in the *x*-*y* plane is called [elliptical polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptical_polarization). The state vector is given by

$$|\psi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} \psi_x \\ \psi_y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(\theta) e^{i \alpha_x} \\ \sin(\theta) e^{i \alpha_y} \end{pmatrix} = e^{i \alpha} \begin{pmatrix} \cos(\theta) \\ \sin(\theta) e^{i \Delta \alpha} \end{pmatrix} .$$

In the special case of $\Delta\alpha = 0$, this reduces to linear polarization.

[Circular polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_polarization) corresponds to the special cases of $\theta=\pm\pi/4$ with $\Delta\alpha=\pi/2$. The two circular polarization states are thus given by the Jones vectors:

$$|\psi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} \psi_x \\ \psi_y \end{pmatrix} = e^{i \alpha} \frac 1 \sqrt{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \pm i \end{pmatrix}.$$
