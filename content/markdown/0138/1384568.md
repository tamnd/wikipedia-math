# Character group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Character group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **character group** is the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of [representations](wikipedia://en/12695) of an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) by [complex](wikipedia://en/5826)-valued [functions](wikipedia://en/185427). These functions can be thought of as one-dimensional [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) representations and so are special cases of the group [characters](wikipedia://en/293418) that arise in the related context of [character theory](wikipedia://en/691741). Whenever a group is represented by matrices, the function defined by the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) of the matrices is called a character; however, these traces *do not* in general form a group. Some important properties of these one-dimensional characters apply to characters in general:

:::

- Characters are invariant on [conjugacy classes](wikipedia://en/49176).
- The characters of irreducible representations are orthogonal.

The primary importance of the character group for [finite](wikipedia://en/323707) abelian groups is in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), where it is used to construct [Dirichlet characters](wikipedia://en/174515). The character group of the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) also appears in the theory of the [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811). For [locally compact abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454325), the character group (with an assumption of continuity) is central to [Fourier analysis](wikipedia://en/11659).

## Preliminaries {#Preliminaries}

Let $G$ be an abelian group. A function $f: G \to \mathbb{C}^\times$ mapping $G$ to the group of non-zero complex numbers $\mathbb{C}^\times = \mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ is called a **character** of $G$ if it is a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396)—that is, if $f(g_1 g_2) = f(g_1)f(g_2)$ for all $g_1, g_2 \in G$.

If $f$ is a character of a finite group (or more generally a [torsion group](wikipedia://en/3568695)) $G$, then each function value $f(g)$ is a [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950), since for each $g \in G$ there exists $k \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $g^k = e$, and hence $f(g)^k = f(g^k) = f(e) = 1$.

A finite abelian group of [order](wikipedia://en/493527) *n* has exactly *n* distinct characters. These are denoted by *f*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *f<sub>n</sub>*. The function *f*<sub>1</sub> is the trivial representation, which is given by $f_1(g) = 1$ for all $g \in G$. It is called the **principal character of *G***; the others are called the **non-principal characters**.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

If *G* is an abelian group, then the set of characters *f<sub>k</sub>* forms an abelian group under pointwise multiplication. That is, the product of characters $f_j$ and $f_k$ is defined by $(f_j f_k)(g) = f_j(g) f_k(g)$ for all $g \in G$. This group is the **character group of *G*** and is sometimes denoted as $\hat{G}$. The [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) of $\hat{G}$ is the principal character *f*<sub>1</sub>, and the inverse of a character *f<sub>k</sub>* is its reciprocal 1/*f<sub>k</sub>*. If $G$ is finite of order *n*, then $\hat{G}$ is also of order *n*. In this case, since $|f_k(g)| = 1$ for all $g \in G$, the inverse of a character is equal to the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918).

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Alternative definition"}

### Alternative definition {#Alternative_definition}

There is another definition of character group[^o7]<sup>pg 29</sup> which uses $U(1) = \{z \in \mathbb{C}^*: |z|=1 \}$ as the target instead of just $\mathbb{C}^*$. This is useful when studying [complex tori](wikipedia://en/3055042) because the character group of the lattice in a complex torus $V/\Lambda$ is [canonically isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_isomorphism) to the dual torus via the [Appell–Humbert theorem](wikipedia://en/23379150). That is,

> $$\text{Hom}(\Lambda, U(1)) \cong V^\vee\!/\Lambda^\vee = X^\vee$$

We can express explicit elements in the character group as follows: recall that elements in $U(1)$ can be expressed as

> $$e^{2\pi i x}$$

for $x \in \mathbb{R}$. If we consider the lattice as a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of the underlying [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of $V$, then a homomorphism

> $$\phi: \Lambda \to U(1)$$

can be factored as a map

> $$\phi : \Lambda \to \mathbb{R} \xrightarrow{\exp({2\pi i \cdot })} U(1)$$

This follows from elementary properties of homomorphisms. Note that

> $$\begin{aligned} \phi(x+y) &= \exp({2\pi i }f(x+y)) \\ &= \phi(x) + \phi(y) \\ &= \exp(2\pi i f(x))\exp(2\pi i f(y)) \end{aligned}$$

giving us the desired factorization. As the group

> $$\text{Hom}(\Lambda,\mathbb{R}) \cong \text{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}^{2n},\mathbb{R})$$

we have the isomorphism of the character group, as a group, with the group of homomorphisms of $\mathbb{Z}^{2n}$ to $\mathbb{R}$. Since $\text{Hom}(\mathbb{Z},G)\cong G$ for any abelian group $G$, we have

> $$\text{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}^{2n}, \mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R}^{2n}$$

after composing with the complex exponential, we find that

> $$\text{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}^{2n}, U(1)) \cong \mathbb{R}^{2n}/\mathbb{Z}^{2n}$$

which is the expected result.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Finitely generated abelian groups {#Finitely_generated_abelian_groups}

Since every [finitely generated abelian group](wikipedia://en/58527) is isomorphic to

> $$G \cong \mathbb{Z}^n \oplus \bigoplus_{i=1}^m \mathbb{Z}/a_i$$

the character group can be easily computed in all finitely generated cases. From universal properties, and the isomorphism between finite products and coproducts, we have the character groups of $G$ is isomorphic to

> $$\text{Hom}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^*)^{\oplus n}\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^k\text{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}/n_i,\mathbb{C}^*)$$

for the first case, this is isomorphic to $(\mathbb{C}^*)^{\oplus n}$, the second is computed by looking at the maps which send the generator $1 \in \mathbb{Z}/n_i$ to the various powers of the $n_i$-th roots of unity $\zeta_{n_i} = \exp(2\pi i/n_i)$.

:::

## Orthogonality of characters {#Orthogonality_of_characters}

Consider the $n \times n$ matrix *A* = *A*(*G*) whose matrix elements are $A_{jk} = f_j(g_k)$ where $g_k$ is the *k*th element of *G*.

The sum of the entries in the *j*th row of *A* is given by

$\sum_{k=1}^n A_{jk} = \sum_{k=1}^n f_j(g_k) = 0$ if $j \neq 1$, and

$$\sum_{k=1}^n A_{1k} = n.$$

The sum of the entries in the *k*th column of *A* is given by

$\sum_{j=1}^n A_{jk} = \sum_{j=1}^n f_j(g_k) = 0$ if $k \neq 1$, and

$$\sum_{j=1}^n A_{j1} = \sum_{j=1}^n f_j(e) = n.$$

Let $A^\ast$ denote the [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) of *A*. Then

$$AA^\ast = A^\ast A = nI.$$

This implies the desired orthogonality relationship for the characters: i.e.,

$\sum_{k=1}^n {f_k}^* (g_i) f_k (g_j) = n \delta_{ij}$ ,

where $\delta_{ij}$ is the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890) and $f^*_k (g_i)$ is the complex conjugate of $f_k (g_i)$.
