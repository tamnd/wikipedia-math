# Nine-point hyperbola

*Image: Nine point conic.svg.* blue solid #a17123 solid #188f61

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nine-point hyperbola"}

In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417) with [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) $\triangle ABC$, the **nine-point hyperbola** is an instance of the [nine-point conic](wikipedia://en/44779201) described by American mathematician [Maxime Bôcher](wikipedia://en/3180285) in 1892. The celebrated [nine-point circle](wikipedia://en/161243) is a separate instance of Bôcher's conic:

:::

Given a triangle $\triangle ABC$ and a point $P$ in its plane, a [conic](wikipedia://en/19008673) can be drawn through the following nine points:

the [midpoints](wikipedia://en/437979) of the sides of $\triangle ABC$,

the midpoints of the lines joining $P$ to the vertices, and

the points where these last named lines cut the sides of the triangle.

The conic is an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) if $P$ lies in the interior of $\triangle ABC$ or in one of the regions of the plane separated from the interior by two sides of the triangle; otherwise, the conic is a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052). Bôcher notes that when $P$ is the [orthocenter](wikipedia://en/161244), one obtains the nine-point circle, and when $P$ is on the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432) of $\triangle ABC$, then the conic is an equilateral hyperbola.

## Allen {#Allen}

An approach to the nine-point hyperbola using the [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202) of [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559) was devised by E. F. Allen in 1941.[^o3] Writing $z=a+bj$, $j^{2} = 1$, he uses split-complex arithmetic to express a hyperbola as

$$z z^* = a^2 .$$

It is used as the [circumconic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumconic) of triangle $t_1, t_2, t_3 .$ Let $s = t_1 + t_2 + t_3 .$ Then the nine-point conic is

$$\left(z - \frac{s}{2}\right)\left( z^* - \frac{s^*}{2}\right) = \frac{a^2}{4} .$$

Allen's description of the nine-point hyperbola followed a development of the [nine-point circle](wikipedia://en/161243) that [Frank Morley](wikipedia://en/589167) and his son published in 1933. They requisitioned the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) as the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432) of the given triangle.

In 1953 Allen extended his study to a nine-point conic of a triangle inscribed in any central conic.[^o4]

## Yaglom {#Yaglom}

For Yaglom, a hyperbola is a *Minkowskian circle* as in the [Minkowski plane](wikipedia://en/4500167). Yaglom's description of this geometry is found in the "Conclusion" chapter of a book that initially addresses Galilean geometry.[^o6] He considers a triangle inscribed in a "circumcircle" which is in fact a hyperbola. In the Minkowski plane the nine-point hyperbola is also described as a circle:

… the midpoints of the sides of a triangle $\triangle ABC$ and the feet of its altitudes (as well as the midpoints of the segments joining the orthocenter of $\triangle ABC$ to its vertices) lie on a \[Minkowskian\] circle $S$ whose radius is half the radius of the circumcircle of the triangle. It is natural to refer to S as the six- (nine-) point circle of the (Minkowskian) triangle $\triangle ABC$; if $\triangle ABC$ has an [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle_and_excircles_of_a_triangle) $s$, then the six- (nine-) point circle $S$ of $\triangle ABC$ touches its incircle $s$ (Fig.173).

## Others {#Others}

In 2005 J. A. Scott[^o8] used the [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122) as the [circumconic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumconic) of triangle ABC and found conditions for it to include six triangle centers: the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) X(2), the [orthocenter](wikipedia://en/161244) X(4), the [Fermat points](wikipedia://en/2666205) X(13) and X(14), and the [Napoleon points](wikipedia://en/35718816) X(17) and X(18) as listed in the [Encyclopedia of Triangle Centers](wikipedia://en/15003593). Scott’s hyperbola is a [Kiepert hyperbola](wikipedia://en/69991093) of the triangle.

Christopher Bath[^o9] describes a nine-point rectangular hyperbola passing through these centers: [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) X(1), the three [excenters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excenter), the centroid X(2), the [de Longchamps point](wikipedia://en/14593776) X(20), and the three points obtained by extending the triangle [medians](wikipedia://en/1072006) to twice their [cevian](wikipedia://en/6135790) length.
