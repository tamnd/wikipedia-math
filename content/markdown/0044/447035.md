# Dirichlet eta function

*Image: Dirichlet eta function.png.* Color representation of the Dirichlet eta function. It is generated as a [Matplotlib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matplotlib) plot using a version of the [Domain coloring](wikipedia://en/5280990) method.[^o1]

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Dirichlet eta function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the area of [analytic number theory](wikipedia://en/251513), the **Dirichlet eta function** is defined by the following [Dirichlet series](wikipedia://en/393258), which converges for any [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) having real part greater than zero:

$$\eta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{(-1)^{n-1} \over n^s} = \frac{1}{1^s} - \frac{1}{2^s} + \frac{1}{3^s} - \frac{1}{4^s} + \cdots.$$

:::

This Dirichlet series is the alternating sum corresponding to the Dirichlet series expansion of the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809), *ζ*(*s*) — and for this reason the Dirichlet eta function is also known as the **alternating zeta function**, also denoted \*(*s*). The following relation holds:

$$\eta(s) = \left(1-2^{1-s}\right) \zeta(s)$$

Both the Dirichlet eta function and the Riemann zeta function are special cases of [polylogarithms](wikipedia://en/482471#Relationship_to_other_functions).

While the Dirichlet series expansion for the eta function is convergent only for any [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) *s* with real part > 0, it is [Abel summable](wikipedia://en/876428) for any complex number. This serves to define the eta function as an [entire function](wikipedia://en/10372).

Equivalently, we may begin by defining

$$\eta(s) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_0^\infty \frac{x^{s-1}}{e^x+1}{dx}$$

which is also defined in the region of positive real part ($\Gamma(s)$ represents the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316)). This gives the eta function as a [Mellin transform](wikipedia://en/387297).

[Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599) gave a simple proof of the [functional equation](wikipedia://en/364377) for the eta function,[^o3] which is

$$\eta(-s) = 2 \frac{1-2^{-s-1}}{1-2^{-s}} \pi^{-s-1} s \sin\left({\pi s \over 2}\right) \Gamma(s)\eta(s+1).$$

From this, one immediately has the functional equation of the zeta function also, as well as another means to extend the definition of eta to the entire complex plane.

## Zeros {#Zeros}

The [zeros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_%28complex_analysis%29) of the eta function include all the zeros of the zeta function: the negative even integers (real equidistant simple zeros); the zeros along the critical line, none of which are known to be multiple and over 40% of which have been proven to be simple, and the hypothetical zeros in the critical strip but not on the critical line, which if they do exist must occur at the vertices of rectangles symmetrical around the *x*-axis and the critical line and whose multiplicity is unknown. In addition, the factor $1-2^{1-s}$ adds an infinite number of complex simple zeros, located at equidistant points on the line $\Re(s) = 1$, at $s_n=1+2n\pi i/\ln(2)$ where *n* is any nonzero [integer](wikipedia://en/14563).

The zeros of the eta function are located symmetrically with respect to the real axis and under the [Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/19344125) would be on two parallel lines $\Re(s)=1/2, \Re(s)=1$, and on the perpendicular half line formed by the negative real axis.

### Landau's problem {#Landau's_problem}

In the equation $\eta(s) = (1 - 2^{1-s}) \zeta(s)$, "the pole of $\zeta(s)$ at $s = 1$ is cancelled by the zero of the other factor" (Titchmarsh, 1986, p. 17), and as a result $\eta(1)$ is neither infinite nor zero (see ). However, in the equation

$$\zeta(s)=\frac{\eta(s)}{1-2^{1-s}},$$

*η* must be zero at all the points $s_n = 1+n\frac{2\pi}{\ln{2} }i, n\ne0, n \in \mathbb{Z}$, where the denominator is zero, if the Riemann zeta function is analytic and finite there. The problem of proving this without defining the zeta function first was signaled and left open by [E. Landau](wikipedia://en/222230) in his 1909 treatise on number theory: "Whether the eta series is different from zero or not at the points $s_n\ne1$, i.e., whether these are poles of zeta or not, is not readily apparent here."

A first solution for Landau's problem was published almost 40 years later by [D. V. Widder](wikipedia://en/14428679) in his book *The [Laplace Transform](wikipedia://en/18610)*. It uses the next prime 3 instead of 2 to define a Dirichlet series similar to the eta function, which we will call the $\lambda$ function, defined for $\Re(s)>0$ and with some zeros also on $\Re(s) = 1$, but not equal to those of eta.

::: {#o6 .proof cue=math-proof name="Indirect proof of following Widder"}

**Indirect proof of $\eta(s_{n}) = 0$ following Widder.**

$$\lambda(s) = \left(1-\frac{3}{3^s}\right)\zeta(s) = \left(1+\frac{1}{2^s}\right)-\frac{2}{3^s}+\left(\frac{1}{4^s}+\frac{1}{5^s}\right)-\frac{2}{6^s} + \cdots$$

If $s$ is real and strictly positive, the series converges since the regrouped terms alternate in sign and decrease in absolute value to zero. According to a theorem on uniform convergence of Dirichlet series first proven by Cahen in 1894, the $\lambda(s)$ function is then analytic for $\Re(s)>0$, a region which includes the line $\Re(s)=1$. Now we can define correctly, where the denominators are not zero,

$$\zeta(s) = \frac{\eta(s)}{1-\frac{2}{2^s}}$$

or

$$\zeta(s) = \frac{\lambda(s)}{1-\frac{3}{3^s}}$$

Since $\frac{\log 3}{\log 2}$ is irrational, the denominators in the two definitions are not zero at the same time except for $s=1$, and the $\zeta(s)\,$ function is thus well defined and analytic for $\Re(s)>0$ except at $s=1$. We finally get indirectly that $\eta(s_n)=0$ when $s_n\ne1$:

$$\eta(s_n) = \left(1-\frac{2}{2^{s_n}}\right)\zeta(s_n) = \frac{1-\frac{2}{2^{s_n}}}{1-\frac{3}{3^{s_n}}} \lambda(s_n) = 0.$$

:::

An elementary direct and $\zeta$-independent proof of the vanishing of the eta function at $s_n\ne1$ was published by J. Sondow in 2003. It expresses the value of the eta function as the limit of special Riemann sums associated to an integral known to be zero, using a relation between the partial sums of the Dirichlet series defining the eta and zeta functions for $\Re(s)>1$.

::: {#o7 .proof cue=math-proof name="Direct proof of by Sondow"}

**Direct proof of $\eta(s_{n}) = 0$ by Sondow.**

With some simple algebra performed on finite sums, we can write for any complex *s*

$$\begin{aligned} \eta_{2n}(s) &= \sum_{k=1}^{2n}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k^s} \\ &= 1-\frac{1}{2^s}+\frac{1}{3^s}-\frac{1}{4^s}+\dots+\frac{(-1)^{2n-1}}{{(2n)}^s} \\[2pt] &= 1+\frac{1}{2^s}+\frac{1}{3^s}+\frac{1}{4^s}+\dots+\frac{1}{{(2n)}^s} - 2\left(\frac{1}{2^s}+\frac{1}{4^s}+\dots+\frac{1}{{(2n)}^s}\right) \\[2pt] &= \left(1-\frac{2}{2^s}\right)\zeta_{2n}(s) + \frac{2}{2^s}\left(\frac{1}{{(n+1)}^s} + \dots + \frac{1}{{(2n)}^s}\right) \\[2pt] &= \left(1-\frac{2}{2^s}\right)\zeta_{2n}(s) + \frac{2n}{{(2n)}^s}\,\frac{1}{n}\,\left(\frac{1}{{(1+1/n)}^s}+ \dots + \frac{1}{{(1+n/n)}^s} \right). \end{aligned}$$

Now if $s=1+it$ and $2^s=2$, the factor multiplying $\zeta_{2n}(s)$ is zero, and

$$\eta_{2n}(s) = \frac{1}{n^{it}} R_n\left(\frac{1}{{(1+x)}^s},0,1\right),$$

where $R_{n}(f(x), a, b)$ denotes a special Riemann sum approximating the integral of $f(x)$ over *a*, *b*. For $t = 0$ i.e., $s = 1$, we get

$$\eta(1) = \lim_{n\to\infty} \eta_{2n}(1) = \lim_{n\to\infty} R_n\left(\frac{1}{1+x},0,1\right) = \int_0^1 \frac{dx}{1+x} = \log 2 \ne 0.$$

Otherwise, if $t \ne 0$, then $|n^{1-s}|=|n^{-it}|=1$, which yields

$$|\eta(s)| = \lim_{n\to\infty} |\eta_{2n}(s)| = \lim_{n\to\infty} \left|R_n\left(\frac{1}{{(1+x)}^s},0,1\right)\right| = \left|\int_0^1 \frac{dx}{{(1+x)}^s}\right| = \left|\frac{2^{1-s}-1}{1-s}\right| = \left|\frac{1-1}{-it}\right| = 0.$$

:::

Assuming $\eta(s_n)=0$, for each point $s_n\ne1$ where $2^{s_n}=2$, we can now define $\zeta(s_n)\,$ by continuity as follows,

$$\zeta(s_n) = \lim_{s\to s_n}\frac{\eta(s)}{1-\frac{2}{2^s}} = \lim_{s\to s_n}\frac{\eta(s)-\eta(s_n)}{\frac{2}{2^{s_n}}-\frac{2}{2^s}} = \lim_{s\to s_n}\frac{\eta(s)-\eta(s_n)}{s-s_n}\,\frac{s-s_n}{\frac{2}{2^{s_n}}-\frac{2}{2^s}} = \frac{\eta'(s_n)}{\log(2)}.$$

The apparent singularity of zeta at $s_n\ne1$ is now removed, and the zeta function is proven to be analytic everywhere in $\Re{s} > 0$, except at $s=1$ where

$$\lim_{s \to 1} (s-1)\zeta(s) = \lim_{s\to 1} \frac{\eta(s)}{\frac{1-2^{1-s}}{s-1}} = \frac{\eta(1)}{\log 2} = 1.$$

## Integral representations {#Integral_representations}

A number of integral formulas involving the eta function can be listed. The first one follows from a change of variable of the integral representation of the Gamma function (Abel, 1823), giving a [Mellin transform](wikipedia://en/387297) which can be expressed in different ways as a [double integral](wikipedia://en/2757224) (Sondow, 2005). This is valid for $\Re s > 0.$

$$\begin{aligned} \Gamma(s)\eta(s) &= \int_0^\infty \frac{x^{s-1}}{e^x+1} \, dx = \int_0^\infty \int_0^x \frac{x^{s-2}}{e^x+1} \, dy \, dx \\[8pt] &= \int_0^\infty \int_0^\infty \frac{(t+r)^{s-2}}{e^{t+r}+1} dr \, dt =\int_0^1 \int_0^1 \frac{\left(-\log(x y)\right)^{s-2}}{1 + x y} \, dx \, dy. \end{aligned}$$

The Cauchy–Schlömilch transformation (Amdeberhan, Moll et al., 2010) can be used to prove this other representation, valid for $\Re s > -1$. [Integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252) of the first integral above in this section yields another derivation.

$$2^{1-s}\,\Gamma(s+1)\,\eta(s) = 2 \int_0^\infty \frac{x^{2s+1}}{\cosh^2(x^2)} \, dx = \int_0^\infty \frac{t^s}{\cosh^2(t)} \, dt.$$

The next formula, due to Lindelöf (1905), is valid over the whole complex plane, when the principal value is taken for the logarithm implicit in the exponential.

$$\eta(s) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{(1/2 + i t)^{-s}}{e^{\pi t}+e^{-\pi t}} \, dt.$$

This corresponds to a [Jensen](wikipedia://en/1430666) (1895) formula for the entire function $(s-1)\,\zeta(s)$, valid over the whole complex plane and also proven by Lindelöf.

$$(s-1)\zeta(s) = 2\pi\,\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{(1/2 + i t)^{1-s}}{(e^{\pi t}+e^{-\pi t})^2} \, dt.$$

"This formula, remarquable by its simplicity, can be proven easily with the help of Cauchy's theorem, so important for the summation of series" wrote Jensen (1895). Similarly by converting the integration paths to contour integrals one can obtain other formulas for the eta function, such as this generalisation (Milgram, 2013) valid for $0 < c < 1$ and all $s$:

$$\eta(s) = \frac{1}{2} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{(c + i t)^{-s}}{\sin{(\pi(c+i t))}} \, dt.$$

The zeros on the negative real axis are factored out cleanly by making $c\to 0^+$ (Milgram, 2013) to obtain a formula valid for $\Re s < 0$:

$$\eta(s) = - \sin\left(\frac{s\pi}{2}\right) \int_{0}^\infty \frac{t^{-s}}{\sinh{(\pi t)}} \, dt.$$

## Numerical algorithms {#Numerical_algorithms}

Most of the [series acceleration](wikipedia://en/8737421) techniques developed for [alternating series](wikipedia://en/494308) can be profitably applied to the evaluation of the eta function. One particularly simple, yet reasonable method is to apply [Euler's transformation of alternating series](wikipedia://en/3877474#Euler_transform), to obtain

$$\eta(s)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{1}{2^{n+1}} \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^{k} {n \choose k} \frac {1}{(k+1)^s}.$$

Note that the second, inside summation is a [forward difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forward_difference).

### Borwein's method {#Borwein's_method}

[Peter Borwein](wikipedia://en/1339736) used approximations involving [Chebyshev polynomials](wikipedia://en/184539) to produce a method for efficient evaluation of the eta function.[^o11] If

$$d_k = n\sum_{\ell=0}^k \frac{(n+\ell-1)!4^\ell}{(n-\ell)!(2\ell)!}$$

then

$$\eta(s) = -\frac{1}{d_n} \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^k(d_k-d_n)}{(k+1)^s}+\gamma_n(s),$$

where for $\Re(s) \ge \frac{1}{2}$ the error term $\gamma_{n}$ is bounded by

$$|\gamma_n(s)| \le \frac{3}{(3+\sqrt{8})^n} (1+2|\Im(s)|)\exp\left(\frac{\pi}{2}|\Im(s)|\right).$$

The factor of $3+\sqrt{8}\approx 5.8$ in the error bound indicates that the Borwein series converges quite rapidly as *n* increases.

The result for $\eta(s)$ can also be expressed as:

$$\eta(s) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k^s}(1-\frac{d_{k-1}}{d_n})+\gamma_n(s)$$

This form appears similar to the definition of the eta function but with finitely many terms, with each term being multiplied by a scaling factor.

## Particular values {#Particular_values}

- *η*(0) = $\tfrac{1}{2}$, the [Abel sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_summation) of [Grandi's series](wikipedia://en/7830441) 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + ... .
- *η*(−1) = $\tfrac{1}{4}$, the Abel sum of [1 − 2 + 3 − 4 + ⋯](wikipedia://en/9702578) .
- For positive integer *k*, $$\eta(1-k) = \frac{2^k-1}{k} B^{+{}}_k,$$ where $B^{+}_{k}$ is the *k*th [Bernoulli number](wikipedia://en/4964).

Also:

- $\eta(1) = \ln2$, this is the [alternating harmonic series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_harmonic_series)
- $$\eta(2) = {\pi^2 \over 12}$$
- $$\eta(4) = {{7\pi^4} \over 720} \approx 0.94703283$$
- $$\eta(6) = {{31\pi^6} \over 30240} \approx 0.98555109$$
- $$\eta(8) = {{127\pi^8} \over 1209600} \approx 0.99623300$$
- $$\eta(10) = {{73\pi^{10}} \over 6842880} \approx 0.99903951$$
- $$\eta(12) = {{1414477\pi^{12}} \over {1307674368000}} \approx 0.99975769$$

The general form for even positive integers is:

$$\eta(2n) = (-1)^{n+1}{{B_{2n}\pi^{2n} \left(2^{2n-1} - 1\right)} \over {(2n)!}}.$$

Taking the limit $n \to \infty$, one obtains $\eta (\infty) = 1$.

## Derivatives {#Derivatives}

The derivative with respect to the parameter $s$ is for $s\neq 1$

$$\eta'(s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n\ln n}{n^s} = 2^{1-s}\ln(2)\,\zeta(s)+(1-2^{1-s})\,\zeta'(s).$$

$$\eta'(1) = \ln(2)\,\gamma-\ln(2)^2\,2^{-1}$$
