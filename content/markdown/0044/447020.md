# Dedekind eta function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dedekind eta function"}

*Image: Dedekind Eta.jpg.* Dedekind $\eta$-function in the upper half-plane

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Dedekind eta function**, named after [Richard Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106), is a [modular form](wikipedia://en/286000) of weight 1/2 and is a function defined on the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826) of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), where the imaginary part is positive. It also occurs in [bosonic string theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosonic_string_theory).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

For any complex number $\tau$ with $\operatorname{Im}(\tau) > 0$, let $q = e^{2\pi i\tau}$; then the eta function is defined by,

$$\eta(\tau) = e^\frac{\pi i \tau}{12} \prod_{n=1}^\infty \left(1-e^{2 n\pi i \tau}\right) = q^\frac{1}{24} \prod_{n=1}^\infty \left(1 - q^n\right) .$$

Raising the eta equation to the 24th power and multiplying by $(2\pi)^{12}$ gives

$$\Delta(\tau)=(2\pi)^{12}\eta^{24}(\tau)$$

where $\Delta$ is the [modular discriminant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_discriminant). The presence of [24](wikipedia://en/362201) can be understood by connection with other occurrences, such as in the 24-dimensional [Leech lattice](wikipedia://en/344919).

The eta function is [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110) on the upper half-plane but cannot be continued analytically beyond it.

*Image: Q-Eulero.jpeg.* Modulus of Euler phi on the unit disc, colored so that black = 0, red = 4

*Image: Discriminant real part.jpeg.* The real part of the modular discriminant as a function of $q$.

The eta function satisfies the [functional equations](wikipedia://en/364377)[^o4]

$$\begin{aligned} \eta(\tau+1) &=e^\frac{\pi i}{12}\eta(\tau),\\ \eta\left(-\frac{1}{\tau}\right) &= \sqrt{-i\tau}\, \eta(\tau).\, \end{aligned}$$

In the second equation the [branch of the square root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_square_root) is chosen such that $\sqrt{-i\tau} = 1$ when $\tau = i$.

More generally, suppose $a, b, c, d$ are integers with $ad - bc = 1$, so that

$$\tau\mapsto\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}$$

is a transformation belonging to the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019). We may assume that either $c > 0$, or $c = 0$ and $d = 1$. Then

$$\eta \left( \frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d} \right) = \epsilon (a,b,c,d) \left(c\tau+d\right)^\frac12 \eta(\tau),$$

where

$$\epsilon (a,b,c,d)= \begin{cases} e^\frac{bi \pi}{12} &c=0,\,d=1, \\ e^{i\pi \left(\frac{a+d}{12c} - s(d,c)-\frac14\right)} &c>0. \end{cases}$$

Here $s(h,k)$ is the [Dedekind sum](wikipedia://en/1122242)

$$s(h,k)=\sum_{n=1}^{k-1} \frac{n}{k} \left( \frac{hn}{k} - \left\lfloor \frac{hn}{k} \right\rfloor -\frac12 \right).$$

Because of these functional equations the eta function is a [modular form](wikipedia://en/286000) of weight $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and level 1 for a certain character of order 24 of the [metaplectic double cover](wikipedia://en/2981795) of the modular group, and can be used to define other modular forms. In particular the [modular discriminant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_discriminant) of the [Weierstrass elliptic function](wikipedia://en/447181) with

$$\omega_2=\tau\omega_1$$

can be defined as

$$\Delta(\tau) = (2 \pi\omega_1)^{12} \eta(\tau)^{24}\,$$

and is a modular form of weight 12. Some authors omit the factor of $(2\pi)^{12}$, so that the series expansion has integral coefficients.

The [Jacobi triple product](wikipedia://en/1475657) implies that the eta is (up to a factor) a Jacobi [theta function](wikipedia://en/449745) for special values of the arguments:[^o5]

$$\eta(\tau) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \chi(n) \exp\left(\frac {\pi i n^2 \tau}{12}\right),$$

where $\chi(n)$ is "the" [Dirichlet character](wikipedia://en/174515) modulo 12 with $\chi(\pm1) = 1$ and $\chi(\pm5) = -1$. Explicitly,

$$\eta(\tau) = e^\frac{\pi i \tau}{12}\vartheta\left(\frac{\tau+1}{2}; 3\tau\right).$$

The [Euler function](wikipedia://en/2319945)

$$\begin{aligned} \phi(q) &= \prod_{n=1}^\infty \left(1-q^n\right) \\ &= q^{-\frac{1}{24}} \eta(\tau), \end{aligned}$$

has a power series by the [Euler Pentagonal number theorem](wikipedia://en/340294):

$$\phi(q)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty (-1)^n q^\frac{3n^2-n}{2}.$$

Note that by using this theorem for $\mathfrak{I} (\tau )>0$, the eta function can be expressed as

$$\eta(\tau)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty e^{\pi i n}e^{3\pi i \left(n-\frac{1}{6}\right)^2 \tau}.$$

This can be proved by using $x=2\pi i \tau$ in [Euler Pentagonal number theorem](wikipedia://en/340294) with the definition of eta function.

Another way to see the Eta function is through the following limit

$$\lim_{z \to 0} \frac{\vartheta_1(z|\tau)}{z}=2\pi \eta^3(\tau)$$

Which alternatively is:

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty (-1)^n (2n+1)q^{\frac{(2n+1)^2}8}=\eta^3(\tau)$$

Where $\vartheta_1(z|\tau)$ is the [Jacobi Theta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_Theta_function) and $\vartheta_1(z|\tau)=-\vartheta_{11}(z;\tau)$

Because the eta function is easy to compute numerically from either [power series](wikipedia://en/59958), it is often helpful in computation to express other functions in terms of it when possible, and products and quotients of eta functions, called eta quotients, can be used to express a great variety of modular forms.

The picture on this page shows the modulus of the Euler function: the additional factor of $q^{\tfrac{1}{24}}$ between this and eta makes almost no visual difference whatsoever. Thus, this picture can be taken as a picture of eta as a function of $q$.

## Combinatorial identities {#Combinatorial_identities}

The theory of the [algebraic characters](wikipedia://en/10242885) of the [affine Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/1350865) gives rise to a large class of previously unknown identities for the eta function. These identities follow from the [Weyl–Kac character formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl–Kac_character_formula), and more specifically from the so-called "denominator identities". The characters themselves allow the construction of generalizations of the [Jacobi theta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_theta_function) which transform under the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019); this is what leads to the identities. An example of one such new identity[^o7] is

$$\eta(8\tau)\eta(16\tau) = \sum_{m,n\in \mathbb{Z} \atop m \le |3n|} (-1)^m q^{(2m+1)^2 - 32n^2}$$

where $q = e^{2\pi i\tau}$ is the [$q$-analog](wikipedia://en/1619396) or "deformation" of the [highest weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highest_weight) of a module.

## Special values {#Special_values}

From the above connection with the Euler function together with the special values of the latter, it can be easily deduced that

$$\begin{aligned} \eta(i)&=\frac{\Gamma \left(\frac14\right)}{2 \pi ^\frac34} \\[6pt] \eta\left(\tfrac{1}{2}i\right)&=\frac{\Gamma \left(\frac14\right)}{2^\frac78 \pi ^\frac34} \\[6pt] \eta(2i)&=\frac{\Gamma \left(\frac14\right)}{2^\frac{11}{8} \pi ^\frac34} \\[6pt] \eta(3i)&=\frac{\Gamma \left(\frac14\right)}{2\sqrt[3]{3} \left(3+2 \sqrt{3}\right)^\frac{1}{12} \pi ^\frac34} \\[6pt] \eta(4i)&=\frac{\sqrt[4]{-1+\sqrt{2}}\, \Gamma \left(\frac14\right)}{2^\frac{29}{16} \pi ^\frac34} \\[6pt] \eta\left(e^\frac{2 \pi i}{3}\right)&=e^{-\frac{\pi i}{24}} \frac{\sqrt[8]{3} \, \Gamma \left(\frac13\right)^\frac32}{2 \pi } \end{aligned}$$

## Eta quotients {#Eta_quotients}

Eta quotients are defined by quotients of the form

$$\prod_{0<d\mid N}\eta(d\tau)^{r_d}$$

where $d$ is a non-negative integer and $r_{d}$ is any integer. Linear combinations of eta quotients at imaginary quadratic arguments may be [algebraic](wikipedia://en/1158), while combinations of eta quotients may even be [integral](wikipedia://en/14563). For example, define,

$$\begin{aligned} j(\tau)&=\left(\left(\frac{\eta(\tau)}{\eta(2\tau)}\right)^{8}+2^8 \left(\frac{\eta(2\tau)}{\eta(\tau)}\right)^{16}\right)^3 \\[6pt] j_{2A}(\tau)&=\left(\left(\frac{\eta(\tau)}{\eta(2\tau)}\right)^{12}+2^6 \left(\frac{\eta(2\tau)}{\eta(\tau)}\right)^{12}\right)^2 \\[6pt] j_{3A}(\tau) &=\left(\left(\frac{\eta(\tau)}{\eta(3\tau)}\right)^{6}+3^3 \left(\frac{\eta(3\tau)}{\eta(\tau)}\right)^{6}\right)^2 \\[6pt] j_{4A}(\tau) &=\left(\left(\frac{\eta(\tau)}{\eta(4\tau)}\right)^{4} + 4^2 \left(\frac{\eta(4\tau)}{\eta(\tau)}\right)^{4}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{\eta^2(2\tau)}{\eta(\tau)\,\eta(4\tau)} \right)^{24} \end{aligned}$$

with the 24th power of the [Weber modular function](wikipedia://en/33559472) $\text{𝔣}(\tau)$. Then,

$$\begin{aligned} j\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{-163}}{2}\right) &= -640320^3, & e^{\pi\sqrt{163}} &\approx 640320^3+743.99999999999925\dots \\[6pt] j_{2A}\left(\frac{\sqrt{-58}}{2}\right) &= 396^4, & e^{\pi\sqrt{58}}&\approx 396^4-104.00000017\dots \\[6pt] j_{3A}\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{-\frac{89}{3}}}{2}\right) &= -300^3, & e^{\pi\sqrt\frac{89}{3}}&\approx 300^3+41.999971\dots \\[6pt] j_{4A}\left(\frac{\sqrt{-7}}{2}\right)&=2^{12}, & e^{\pi\sqrt{7}}&\approx 2^{12}-24.06\dots \end{aligned}$$

and so on, values which appear in [Ramanujan–Sato series](wikipedia://en/42185330).

Eta quotients may also be a useful tool for describing bases of [modular forms](wikipedia://en/286000), which are difficult to compute and express directly. In 1959 Morris Newman proved that if an eta quotient $\eta_{g}$ of the form given above, namely $\prod_{0<d\mid N}\eta(d\tau)^{r_d}$ satisfies

$$\sum_{0<d\mid N}d r_d \equiv 0 \pmod{24} \quad \text{and} \quad \sum_{0<d\mid N}\frac{N}{d}r_d \equiv 0 \pmod{24},$$

then $\eta_{g}$ is a [weight $k$ modular form](wikipedia://en/286000) for the [congruence subgroup](wikipedia://en/382694) $\Gamma_{0}(N)$ (up to [holomorphicity](wikipedia://en/14110)) where[^o10]

$$k=\frac12\sum_{0<d\mid N} r_d.$$

This result was extended in 2019 such that the converse holds for cases when $N$ is [coprime](wikipedia://en/6556) to 6, and it remains open that the original theorem is sharp for all integers $N$.[^o11] This also extends to state that any [modular eta quotient](wikipedia://en/286000) for any [level $n$ congruence subgroup](wikipedia://en/382694) must also be a modular form for the group $\Gamma(N)$. While these theorems characterize [modular](wikipedia://en/286000) eta quotients, the condition of [holomorphicity](wikipedia://en/14110) must be checked separately using a theorem that emerged from the work of Gérard Ligozat[^o12] and Yves Martin:[^o13]

If $\eta_{g}$ is an eta quotient satisfying the above conditions for the integer $N$ and $c$ and $d$ are coprime integers, then the order of vanishing at the [cusp](wikipedia://en/6851758) $\tfrac{c}{d}$ relative to $\Gamma_{0}(N)$ is

$$\frac{N}{24}\sum_{0<\delta|N} \frac{\gcd\left(d,\delta\right)^2r_\delta}{\gcd\left(d,\frac{N}{d}\right)d\delta} .$$

These theorems provide an effective means of creating holomorphic modular eta quotients, however this may not be sufficient to construct a basis for a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of modular forms and [cusp forms](wikipedia://en/610165). A useful theorem for limiting the number of modular eta quotients to consider states that a holomorphic weight $k$ modular eta quotient on $\Gamma_{0}(N)$ must satisfy

$$\sum_{0<d\mid N} |r_d|\leq \prod_{p\mid N}\left(\frac{p+1}{p-1}\right)^{\min\bigl(2,\text{ord}_p(N)\bigr)},$$

where $\operatorname{ord}_{p}(N)$ denotes the largest integer $m$ such that $p^{m}$ divides $N$.[^o14] These results lead to several characterizations of spaces of modular forms that can be spanned by modular eta quotients.[^o14] Using the [graded ring](wikipedia://en/103094) structure on the ring of modular forms, we can compute bases of vector spaces of modular forms composed of $\mathbb{C}$-linear combinations of eta-quotients. For example, if we assume $N = pq$ is a [semiprime](wikipedia://en/339542) then the following process can be used to compute an eta-quotient basis of [$M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))$](wikipedia://en/286000).[^o11]

Fix a semiprime $N = pq$ which is coprime to 6 (that is, $p, q > 3$). We know that any modular eta quotient may be found using the above theorems, therefore it is reasonable to algorithmically to compute them.; Compute the dimension $D$ of $M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))$. This tells us how many linearly-independent modular eta quotients we will need to compute to form a basis.; Reduce the number of eta quotients to consider. For semiprimes we can reduce the number of partitions using the bound on $\sum_{0<d\mid N} |r_d|$ and by noticing that the sum of the orders of vanishing at the cusps of $\Gamma_{0}(N)$ must equal $S:=\frac{(p+1)(q+1)}{6}$.[^o11]; Find all partitions of $S$ into 4-tuples (there are 4 cusps of $\Gamma_{0}(N)$), and among these consider only the partitions which satisfy Newman's conditions (we can convert orders of vanishing into exponents). Each of these partitions corresponds to a unique eta quotient.; Determine the minimum number of terms in the [$q$-expansion](wikipedia://en/286000) of each eta quotient required to identify elements uniquely (this uses a result known as [Sturm's bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturm's_bound)). Then use linear algebra to determine a maximal independent set among these eta quotients.; Assuming that we have not already found $D$ linearly independent eta quotients, find an appropriate vector space $M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))$ such that $k$ and $M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))$ is spanned by ([weakly holomorphic](wikipedia://en/42577984)) eta quotients,[^o14] and $M_{k-k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))$ contains an eta quotient $\eta_{g}$.; Take a modular form $f$ with weight $k$ that is not in the span of our computed eta quotients, and compute $f \eta_{g}$ as a linear combination of eta-quotients in $M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))$ and then divide out by $\eta_{g}$. The result will be an expression of $f$ as a linear combination of eta quotients as desired. Repeat this until a basis is formed. A collection of over 6300 product identities for the Dedekind eta function in a canonical, standardized form is available at the Wayback machine[^o15] of Michael Somos' website.
