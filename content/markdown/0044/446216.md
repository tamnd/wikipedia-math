# Decision theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Decision theory"}

*Image: Jacques-Clément Wagrez - The Judgement of Paris, 1886.jpg.* The mythological [Judgement of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judgement_of_Paris) required selecting from three incomparable alternatives (the [goddesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Goddess) shown).

**Decision theory** or the **theory of rational choice** is a branch of [probability](wikipedia://en/23542), [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), and [analytic philosophy](wikipedia://en/159211) that uses [expected utility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_utility) and [probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probabilities) to model how individuals would behave [rationally](wikipedia://en/61032) under [uncertainty](wikipedia://en/63778).[^o2][^o3] It differs from the [cognitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science) and [behavioral sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioural_sciences) in that it is mainly [prescriptive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prescriptive_economics) and concerned with identifying [optimal decisions](wikipedia://en/16414027) for a [rational agent](wikipedia://en/1187268), rather than [describing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descriptive_economics) how people actually make decisions. Despite this, the field is important to the study of real human behavior by [social scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science), as it lays the foundations to [mathematically model](wikipedia://en/20590) and analyze individuals in fields such as [sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology), [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), [criminology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminology), [cognitive science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science), [moral philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_philosophy) and [political science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science).

:::

::: {#o5 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="History of decision theory"}

## History {#History}

The roots of decision theory lie in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), developed by [Blaise Pascal](wikipedia://en/4068) and [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966) in the 17th century, which was later refined by others like [Christiaan Huygens](wikipedia://en/42127). These developments provided a framework for understanding risk and uncertainty, which are central to [decision-making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision-making).[^o6]

In the 18th century, [Daniel Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/46784) introduced the concept of "expected utility" in the context of gambling, which was later formalized by [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) and [Oskar Morgenstern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Morgenstern) in the 1940s. Their work on *Game Theory and Expected Utility Theory* helped establish a rational basis for decision-making under uncertainty.

After [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), decision theory expanded into economics, particularly with the work of economists like [Milton Friedman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Friedman) and others, who applied it to market behavior and consumer choice theory. This era also saw the development of [Bayesian decision theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_decision_theory), which incorporates [Bayesian probability](wikipedia://en/4890) into decision-making models.

By the late 20th century, scholars like [Daniel Kahneman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Kahneman) and [Amos Tversky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amos_Tversky) challenged the assumptions of rational decision-making. Their work in [behavioral economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_economics) highlighted [cognitive biases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_bias) and [heuristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristics) that influence real-world decisions, leading to the development of [prospect theory](wikipedia://en/197284), which modified expected utility theory by accounting for psychological factors.

:::

## Branches {#Branches}

Normative decision theory is concerned with identification of optimal decisions where optimality is often determined by considering an ideal decision maker who is able to calculate with perfect accuracy and is in some sense fully [rational](wikipedia://en/61032). The practical application of this prescriptive approach (how people *ought to* make decisions) is called [decision analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision_analysis) and is aimed at finding tools, methodologies, and software ([decision support systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision_support_system)) to help people make better decisions.[^o8][^o9]

In contrast, descriptive decision theory is concerned with describing observed behaviors often under the assumption that those making decisions are behaving under some consistent rules. These rules may, for instance, have a procedural framework (e.g. [Amos Tversky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amos_Tversky)'s elimination by aspects model) or an [axiomatic](wikipedia://en/928) framework (e.g. [stochastic transitivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_transitivity) axioms), reconciling the [Von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms](wikipedia://en/736803#Von_Neumann–Morgenstern_utility_theorem) with behavioral violations of the [expected utility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_utility) hypothesis, or they may explicitly give a functional form for [time-inconsistent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_inconsistency) [utility functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_function) (e.g. Laibson's [quasi-hyperbolic discounting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_discounting)).[^o8][^o9]

Prescriptive decision theory is concerned with predictions about behavior that positive decision theory produces to allow for further tests of the kind of decision-making that occurs in practice. In recent decades, there has also been increasing interest in "behavioral decision theory", contributing to a re-evaluation of what useful decision-making requires.[^o10][^o11]

## Types of decisions {#Types_of_decisions}

::: {#o14 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Choice under uncertainty"}

### Choice under uncertainty {#Choice_under_uncertainty}

The area of choice under uncertainty represents the heart of decision theory. Known from the 17th century ([Blaise Pascal](wikipedia://en/4068) invoked it in his [famous wager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal's_Wager), which is contained in his *[Pensées](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pensées)*, published in 1670), the idea of [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) is that, when faced with a number of actions, each of which could give rise to more than one possible outcome with different probabilities, the rational procedure is to identify all possible outcomes, determine their values (positive or negative) and the probabilities that will result from each course of action, and multiply the two to give an "expected value", or the average expectation for an outcome; the action to be chosen should be the one that gives rise to the highest total expected value. In 1738, [Daniel Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/46784) published an influential paper entitled *Exposition of a New Theory on the Measurement of Risk*, in which he uses the [St. Petersburg paradox](wikipedia://en/466277) to show that expected value theory must be [normatively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norm_%28philosophy%29) wrong. He gives an example in which a Dutch merchant is trying to decide whether to insure a cargo being sent from Amsterdam to St. Petersburg in winter. In his solution, he defines a [utility function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_function) and computes [expected utility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_utility) rather than expected financial value.[^o15]

In the 20th century, interest was reignited by [Abraham Wald](wikipedia://en/1239430)'s 1939 paper pointing out that the two central procedures of [sampling-distribution-based](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequentist_statistics) statistical-theory, namely [hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing) and [parameter estimation](wikipedia://en/1565926), are special cases of the general decision problem.[^o16] Wald's paper renewed and synthesized many concepts of statistical theory, including [loss functions](wikipedia://en/442137), [risk functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk_function), [admissible decision rules](wikipedia://en/1776726), [antecedent distributions](wikipedia://en/472877), [Bayesian procedures](wikipedia://en/1776726#Bayes_rules), and [minimax](wikipedia://en/19589) procedures. The phrase "decision theory" itself was used in 1950 by [E. L. Lehmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._L._Lehmann).[^o17]

The revival of [subjective probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subjective_probability) theory, from the work of [Frank Ramsey](wikipedia://en/238285), [Bruno de Finetti](wikipedia://en/180825), [Leonard Savage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._J._Savage) and others, extended the scope of expected utility theory to situations where subjective probabilities can be used. At the time, von Neumann and Morgenstern's theory of [expected utility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_utility)[^o18] proved that expected utility maximization followed from basic postulates about rational behavior.

The work of [Maurice Allais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Allais) and [Daniel Ellsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Ellsberg) showed that human behavior has systematic and sometimes important departures from expected-utility maximization ([Allais paradox](wikipedia://en/3223960) and [Ellsberg paradox](wikipedia://en/1912480)).[^o19] The [prospect theory](wikipedia://en/197284) of [Daniel Kahneman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Kahneman) and [Amos Tversky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amos_Tversky) renewed the empirical study of [economic behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_economics) with less emphasis on rationality presuppositions. It describes a way by which people make decisions when all of the outcomes carry a risk.[^o20] Kahneman and Tversky found three regularities – in actual human decision-making, "losses loom larger than gains"; people focus more on *changes* in their utility-states than they focus on absolute utilities; and the estimation of subjective probabilities is severely biased by [anchoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchoring_%28cognitive_bias%29).

:::

### Intertemporal choice {#Intertemporal_choice}

Intertemporal choice is concerned with the kind of choice where different actions lead to outcomes that are realized at different stages over time.[^o22] It is also described as [cost-benefit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost-benefit) decision making since it involves the choices between rewards that vary according to magnitude and time of arrival.[^o23] If someone received a windfall of several thousand dollars, they could spend it on an expensive holiday, giving them immediate pleasure, or they could invest it in a pension scheme, giving them an income at some time in the future. What is the optimal thing to do? The answer depends partly on factors such as the expected [rates of interest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interest_rate) and [inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation), the person's [life expectancy](wikipedia://en/18669), and their confidence in the pensions industry. However even with all those factors taken into account, human behavior again deviates greatly from the predictions of prescriptive decision theory, leading to alternative models in which, for example, objective interest rates are replaced by [subjective discount rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_discounting).

### Interaction of decision makers {#Interaction_of_decision_makers}

*Image: NWC wargame 1958.jpg.* [Military planners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_planners) often conduct extensive [simulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulation) to help predict the decision-making of relevant actors.

Some decisions are difficult because of the need to take into account how other people in the situation will respond to the decision that is taken. The analysis of such social decisions is often treated under decision theory, though it involves mathematical methods. In the emerging field of [socio-cognitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socio-cognitive) engineering, the research is especially focused on the different types of distributed decision-making in human organizations, in normal and abnormal/emergency/crisis situations.[^o25]

### Complex decisions {#Complex_decisions}

Other areas of decision theory are concerned with decisions that are difficult simply because of their complexity, or the complexity of the organization that has to make them. Individuals making decisions are limited in resources (i.e. time and intelligence) and are therefore [boundedly rational](wikipedia://en/70400); the issue is thus, more than the deviation between real and optimal behavior, the difficulty of determining the optimal behavior in the first place. Decisions are also affected by whether options are framed together or separately; this is known as the [distinction bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distinction_bias).

## Heuristics {#Heuristics}

*Image: Roulette - detail.jpg.* The [gambler's fallacy](wikipedia://en/12970): even when the [roulette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roulette) ball repeatedly lands on red, it is no more likely to land on black the next time.

[Heuristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic) are procedures for making a decision without working out the consequences of every option. Heuristics decrease the amount of evaluative thinking required for decisions, focusing on some aspects of the decision while ignoring others.[^o29] While quicker than step-by-step processing, heuristic thinking is also more likely to involve fallacies or inaccuracies.[^o30]

One example of a common and erroneous thought process that arises through heuristic thinking is the *[gambler's fallacy](wikipedia://en/12970)* — believing that an isolated random event is affected by previous isolated random events. For example, if flips of a fair coin give repeated tails, the coin still has the same probability (i.e., 0.5) of tails in future turns, though intuitively it might seems that heads becomes more likely.[^o31] In the long run, heads and tails should occur equally often; people commit the gambler's fallacy when they use this heuristic to predict that a result of heads is "due" after a run of tails.[^o32] Another example is that decision-makers may be biased towards preferring moderate alternatives to extreme ones. The *compromise effect* operates under a mindset that the most moderate option carries the most benefit. In an incomplete information scenario, as in most daily decisions, the moderate option will look more appealing than either extreme, independent of the context, based only on the fact that it has characteristics that can be found at either extreme.[^o33]

:::

## Alternatives {#Alternatives}

A highly controversial issue is whether one can replace the use of probability in decision theory with something else.

### Probability theory {#Probability_theory}

Advocates for the use of probability theory point to:

- the work of [Richard Threlkeld Cox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Threlkeld_Cox) for justification of the probability axioms,
- the [Dutch book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_book) paradoxes of [Bruno de Finetti](wikipedia://en/180825) as illustrative of the theoretical difficulties that can arise from departures from the probability axioms, and
- the complete class theorems, which show that all [admissible decision rules](wikipedia://en/1776726) are equivalent to the Bayesian decision rule for some utility function and some [prior distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prior_distribution) (or for the limit of a sequence of prior distributions). Thus, for every decision rule, either the rule may be reformulated as a [Bayesian](wikipedia://en/4890) procedure (or a limit of a sequence of such), or there is a rule that is sometimes better and never worse.

### Alternatives to probability theory {#Alternatives_to_probability_theory}

The proponents of [fuzzy logic](wikipedia://en/49180), [possibility theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Possibility_theory), [Dempster–Shafer theory](wikipedia://en/226660), and [info-gap decision theory](wikipedia://en/4839173) maintain that probability is only one of many alternatives and point to many examples where non-standard alternatives have been implemented with apparent success. Notably, probabilistic decision theory can sometimes be [sensitive](wikipedia://en/620083) to assumptions about the probabilities of various events, whereas non-probabilistic rules, such as [minimax](wikipedia://en/19589), are [robust](wikipedia://en/2885691) in that they do not make such assumptions.

### Ludic fallacy {#Ludic_fallacy}

A general criticism of decision theory based on a fixed universe of possibilities is that it considers the "known unknowns", not the "[unknown unknowns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unknown_unknowns)":[^o38] it focuses on expected variations, not on unforeseen events, which some argue have outsized impact and must be considered – significant events may be "outside model". This line of argument, called the [ludic fallacy](wikipedia://en/8810311), is that there are inevitable imperfections in modeling the real world by particular models, and that unquestioning reliance on models blinds one to their limits.
