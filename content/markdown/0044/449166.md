# Cyclic permutation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cyclic permutation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and in particular in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), a **cyclic permutation** is a [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) consisting of a single cycle.[^o2][^o3] In some cases, cyclic permutations are referred to as **cycles**;[^o4] if a cyclic permutation has *k* elements, it may be called a ***k*-cycle**. Some authors widen this definition to include permutations with fixed points in addition to at most one non-trivial cycle.[^o4][^o5] In [cycle notation](wikipedia://en/44027#Cycle_notation), cyclic permutations are denoted by the list of their elements enclosed with parentheses, in the order to which they are permuted.

:::

For example, the permutation (1 3 2 4) that sends 1 to 3, 3 to 2, 2 to 4 and 4 to 1 is a 4-cycle, and the permutation (1 3 2)(4) that sends 1 to 3, 3 to 2, 2 to 1 and 4 to 4 is considered a 3-cycle by some authors. On the other hand, the permutation (1 3)(2 4) that sends 1 to 3, 3 to 1, 2 to 4 and 4 to 2 is not a cyclic permutation because it separately permutes the pairs {1, 3} and {2, 4}.

For the wider definition of a cyclic permutation, allowing fixed points, these fixed points each constitute trivial [orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) of the permutation, and there is a single non-trivial orbit containing all the remaining points. This can be used as a definition: a cyclic permutation (allowing fixed points) is a permutation that has a single non-trivial orbit. Every permutation on finitely many elements can be decomposed into cyclic permutations whose non-trivial orbits are disjoint.[^o6]

The individual cyclic parts of a permutation are also called **[cycles](wikipedia://en/2221032)**, thus the second example is composed of a 3-cycle and a 1-cycle (or *fixed point*) and the third is composed of two 2-cycles.

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: 050712\_perm\_2.svg.* A cyclic permutation consisting of a single 8-cycle.

There is not widespread consensus about the precise definition of a cyclic permutation. Some authors define a [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) $\sigma$ of a set $X$ to be cyclic if "successive application would take each object of the permuted set successively through the positions of all the other objects",[^o2] or, equivalently, if its representation in cycle notation consists of a single cycle.[^o3] Others provide a more permissive definition which allows fixed points.[^o4][^o5]

A nonempty subset $S$ of $X$ is a *cycle* of $\sigma$ if the restriction of $\sigma$ to $S$ is a cyclic permutation of $S$. If $X$ is [finite](wikipedia://en/11742), its cycles are [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359), and their [union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_union) is $X$. That is, they form a [partition](wikipedia://en/340240), called the [cycle decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycle_decomposition_%28group_theory%29) of $\sigma.$ So, according to the more permissive definition, a permutation of $X$ is cyclic if and only if $X$ is its unique cycle.

For example, the permutation, written in [cycle notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycle_notation) and [two-line notation](wikipedia://en/44027#Two-line_notation) (in two ways) as

$$\begin{aligned} (1\ 4\ 6\ &8\ 3\ 7)(2)(5) \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 & 8 \\ 4 & 2 & 7 & 6 & 5 & 8 & 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 & 6 & 8 & 3 & 7 & 2 & 5 \\ 4 & 6 & 8 & 3 & 7 & 1 & 2 & 5 \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

has one 6-cycle and two 1-cycles its cycle diagram is shown at right. Some authors consider this permutation cyclic while others do not.

*Image: 050712\_perm\_3.svg.* A permutation that is cyclic for the enlarged definition but not for the restricted one, with two fixed points (1-cycles) and a 6-cycle

With the enlarged definition, there are cyclic permutations that do not consist of a single cycle.

More formally, for the enlarged definition, a permutation $\sigma$ of a set *X*, viewed as a [bijective function](wikipedia://en/3942) $\sigma:X\to X$, is called a cycle if the action on *X* of the subgroup generated by $\sigma$ has at most one orbit with more than a single element.[^o9] This notion is most commonly used when *X* is a finite set; then the largest orbit, *S*, is also finite. Let $s_0$ be any element of *S*, and put $s_i=\sigma^i(s_0)$ for any $i\in\mathbf{Z}$. If *S* is finite, there is a minimal number $k \geq 1$ for which $s_k=s_0$. Then $S=\{ s_0, s_1, \ldots, s_{k-1}\}$, and $\sigma$ is the permutation defined by

$\sigma(s_i) = s_{i+1}$ for 0 ≤ *i* \< *k*

and $\sigma(x)=x$ for any element of $X\setminus S$. The elements not fixed by $\sigma$ can be pictured as

$$s_0\mapsto s_1\mapsto s_2\mapsto\cdots\mapsto s_{k-1}\mapsto s_k=s_0.$$

A cyclic permutation can be written using the compact [cycle notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycle_notation) $\sigma = (s_0~s_1~\dots~s_{k-1})$ (there are no commas between elements in this notation, to avoid confusion with a *k*-[tuple](wikipedia://en/132729)). The *length* of a cycle is the number of elements of its largest orbit. A cycle of length *k* is also called a *k*-cycle.

The orbit of a 1-cycle is called a *fixed point* of the permutation, but as a permutation every 1-cycle is the [identity permutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_permutation).[^o10] When cycle notation is used, the 1-cycles are often omitted when no confusion will result.[^o11]

:::

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

One of the basic results on [symmetric groups](wikipedia://en/28901) is that any permutation can be expressed as the product of [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359) cycles (more precisely: cycles with disjoint orbits); such cycles commute with each other, and the expression of the permutation is unique up to the order of the cycles. The [multiset](wikipedia://en/305303) of lengths of the cycles in this expression (the [cycle type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycle_type)) is therefore uniquely determined by the permutation, and both the signature and the [conjugacy class](wikipedia://en/49176) of the permutation in the symmetric group are determined by it.[^o14]

The number of *k*-cycles in the symmetric group *S*<sub>*n*</sub> is given, for $1\leq k\leq n$, by the following equivalent formulas:

$$\binom nk(k-1)!=\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{k}=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!k}.$$

A *k*-cycle has [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_of_a_permutation) (−1)<sup>*k* − 1</sup>.

The [inverse](wikipedia://en/14907) of a cycle $\sigma = (s_0~s_1~\dots~s_{k-1})$ is given by reversing the order of the entries: $\sigma^{-1} = (s_{k - 1}~\dots~s_1~s_{0})$. In particular, since $(a ~ b) = (b ~ a)$, every two-cycle is its own inverse. Since disjoint cycles commute, the inverse of a product of disjoint cycles is the result of reversing each of the cycles separately.

:::

## Transpositions {#Transpositions}

*Image: 4-el perm matrix 14.svg.* [Matrix](wikipedia://en/238849) of $\pi$

A cycle with only two elements is called a **transposition**. For example, the permutation $\pi = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 1 & 4 & 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ that swaps 2 and 4. Since it is a 2-cycle, it can be written as $\pi = (2\ 4)$.

::: {#o17 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

Any permutation can be expressed as the [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) (product) of transpositions—formally, they are [generators](wikipedia://en/99945) for the [group](wikipedia://en/19447).[^o18] In fact, when the set being permuted is $\{1, 2, ..., n\}$ for some integer $n$, then any permutation can be expressed as a product of **adjacent transpositions** $(1~2), (2~3), (3~4),$ and so on. This follows because an arbitrary transposition can be expressed as the product of adjacent transpositions. Concretely, one can express the transposition $(k~~l)$ where $k < l$ by moving $k$ to $l$ one step at a time, then moving $l$ back to where $k$ was, which interchanges these two and makes no other changes:

$$(k~~l) = (k~~k+1)\cdot(k+1~~k+2)\cdots(l-1~~l)\cdot(l-2~~l-1)\cdots(k~~k+1).$$

The decomposition of a permutation into a product of transpositions is obtained for example by writing the permutation as a product of disjoint cycles, and then splitting iteratively each of the cycles of length 3 and longer into a product of a transposition and a cycle of length one less:

$$(a~b~c~d~\ldots~y~z) = (a~b)\cdot (b~c~d~\ldots~y~z).$$

This means the initial request is to move $a$ to $b,$ $b$ to $c,$ $y$ to $z,$ and finally $z$ to $a.$ Instead one may roll the elements keeping $a$ where it is by executing the right factor first (as usual in operator notation, and following the convention in the article [Permutation](wikipedia://en/44027#Product_and_inverse)). This has moved $z$ to the position of $b,$ so after the first permutation, the elements $a$ and $z$ are not yet at their final positions. The transposition $(a~b),$ executed thereafter, then addresses $z$ by the index of $b$ to swap what initially were $a$ and $z.$

In fact, the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) is a [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004), meaning that it is generated by elements of order 2 (the adjacent transpositions), and all relations are of a certain form.

One of the main results on symmetric groups states that either all of the decompositions of a given permutation into transpositions have an [even number](wikipedia://en/143135) of transpositions, or they all have an odd number of transpositions.[^o19] This permits the [parity of a permutation](wikipedia://en/49727) to be a [well-defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-defined) concept.

:::
