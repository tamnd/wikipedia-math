# Bilinear form

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bilinear form"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **bilinear form** is a [bilinear map](wikipedia://en/4365) $V \times V \to K$ on a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $V$ (the elements of which are called *[vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28mathematics%29)*) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K* (the elements of which are called *[scalars](wikipedia://en/3588331)*). In other words, a bilinear form is a function $B : V \times V \to K$ that is [linear](wikipedia://en/18102) in each argument separately:

:::

- $B(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}) = B(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{w}) + B(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w})$ 3 and 3 $B(\lambda\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \lambda B(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})$
- $B(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}) = B(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) + B(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{w})$ 3 and 3 $B(\mathbf{u}, \lambda\mathbf{v}) = \lambda B(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})$

The [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is an example of a bilinear form which is also an [inner product](wikipedia://en/14856).[^o2] An example of a bilinear form that is not an inner product would be the [four-vector](wikipedia://en/226424) product.

The definition of a bilinear form can be extended to include [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) over a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), with [linear maps](wikipedia://en/18102) replaced by [module homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/474118).

When $K$ is the field of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\mathbf{C}$, one is often more interested in [sesquilinear forms](wikipedia://en/860138), which are similar to bilinear forms but are [conjugate linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_linear) in one argument.

## Coordinate representation {#Coordinate_representation}

Let $V$ be an $n$-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) vector space with [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) $\{\mathbf{e}_{1}, \ldots, \mathbf{e}_{n}\}$.

The $n\,\times\,n$ matrix *A*, defined by $A_{ij} = B(\mathbf{e}_{i}, \mathbf{e}_{j})$ is called the *matrix of the bilinear form* on the basis $\{\mathbf{e}_{1}, \ldots, \mathbf{e}_{n}\}$.

If the $n\,\times\,1$ matrix $x$ represents a vector $\mathbf{x}$ with respect to this basis, and similarly, the $n \times 1$ matrix $y$ represents another vector $\mathbf{y}$, then:

$$B(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{x}^\textsf{T} A\mathbf{y} = \sum_{i,j=1}^n x_i A_{ij} y_j.$$

A bilinear form has different matrices on different bases. However, the matrices of a bilinear form on different bases are all [congruent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruent_matrices). More precisely, if $\{\mathbf{f}_{1}, \ldots, \mathbf{f}_{n}\}$ is another basis of $V$, then

$$\mathbf{f}_j=\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{e}_iS_{ij},$$

where the $S_{ij}$ form an [invertible matrix](wikipedia://en/217122) $S$. Then, the matrix of the bilinear form on the new basis is $S^{T}AS$.

In an orthonormal basis, the Euclidean dot product is represented by the $n\,\times\,n$ [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718).

For a finite-dimensional vector space $V$, if either of $B_{1}$ or $B_{2}$ is an isomorphism, then both are, and the bilinear form $B$ is said to be [nondegenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_form). More concretely, for a finite-dimensional vector space, non-degenerate means that every non-zero element pairs non-trivially with some other element:

$B(x,y)=0$ for all $y \in V$ implies that $x = 0$ and

$B(x,y)=0$ for all $x \in V$ implies that $y = 0$.

The corresponding notion for a module over a commutative ring is that a bilinear form is **unimodular** if $V \to V^{\ast}$ is an isomorphism. Given a finitely generated module over a commutative ring, the pairing may be injective (hence "nondegenerate" in the above sense) but not unimodular. For example, over the integers, the pairing $B(x, y) = 2xy$ is nondegenerate but not unimodular, as the induced map from $V = \mathbf{Z}$ to $V^{\ast} = \mathbf{Z}$ is multiplication by 2.

If $V$ is finite-dimensional then one can identify $V$ with its double dual $V^{\ast\ast}$. One can then show that $B_{2}$ is the [transpose](wikipedia://en/1442470) of the linear map $B_{1}$ (if $V$ is infinite-dimensional then $B_{2}$ is the transpose of $B_{1}$ restricted to the image of $V$ in $V^{\ast\ast}$). The **left radical** and **right radical** of the form $B$ are the [kernels](wikipedia://en/45240) of $B_{1}$ and $B_{2}$ respectively;[^n1] they are the vectors orthogonal to the whole space on the left and on the right.[^n2]

If $V$ is finite-dimensional then the [rank](wikipedia://en/26561) of $B_{1}$ is equal to the rank of $B_{2}$. If this number is equal to $\dim(V)$ then $B_{1}$ and $B_{2}$ are linear isomorphisms from $V$ to $V^{\ast}$. In this case $B$ is nondegenerate. By the [rank–nullity theorem](wikipedia://en/330310), this is equivalent to the condition that the left and equivalently right radicals be trivial. For finite-dimensional spaces, this is often taken as the *definition* of nondegeneracy:

If $V$ is [finite-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite-dimensional) then, relative to some [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for $V$, a bilinear form is degenerate if and only if the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the associated matrix is zero. Likewise, a nondegenerate form is one for which the determinant of the associated matrix is non-zero (the matrix is [non-singular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-singular_matrix)). These statements are independent of the chosen basis. For a module over a commutative ring, a unimodular form is one for which the determinant of the associate matrix is a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) (for example 1), hence the term; note that a form whose matrix determinant is non-zero but not a unit will be nondegenerate but not unimodular, for example $B(x, y) = 2xy$ over the integers.

### Symmetric, skew-symmetric, and alternating forms {#Symmetric,_skew-symmetric,_and_alternating_forms}

We define a bilinear form to be

- **[symmetric](wikipedia://en/1803375)** if $B(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}) = B(\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{v})$ for all $\mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{w}$ in $V$;
- **[alternating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_form)** if $B(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v}) = 0$ for all $\mathbf{v}$ in $V$;
- **skew-symmetric bilinear form** or **antisymmetric bilinear form** if $B(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}) = -B(\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{v})$ for all $\mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{w}$ in $V$;

**Proposition**

Every alternating form is skew-symmetric.

**Proof**

This can be seen by expanding $B(\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})$.

If the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) of $K$ is not 2 then the converse is also true: every skew-symmetric form is alternating. However, if $\operatorname{char}(K) = 2$ then a skew-symmetric form is the same as a symmetric form and there exist symmetric/skew-symmetric forms that are not alternating.

A bilinear form is symmetric (respectively skew-symmetric) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its coordinate matrix (relative to any basis) is [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474) (respectively [skew-symmetric](wikipedia://en/174055)). A bilinear form is alternating if and only if its coordinate matrix is skew-symmetric and the diagonal entries are all zero (which follows from skew-symmetry when $\operatorname{char}(K) \ne 2$).

A bilinear form is symmetric if and only if the maps $B_{1}, B_{2}: V \to V^{\ast}$ are equal, and skew-symmetric if and only if they are negatives of one another. If $\operatorname{char}(K) \ne 2$ then one can decompose a bilinear form into a symmetric and a skew-symmetric part as follows

$$B^{+} = \tfrac{1}{2} (B + {}^{\text{t}}B) \qquad B^{-} = \tfrac{1}{2} (B - {}^{\text{t}}B) ,$$

where ${}^{t}B$ is the transpose of $B$ (defined above).

### Reflexive bilinear forms and orthogonal vectors {#Reflexive_bilinear_forms_and_orthogonal_vectors}

**Definition:** A bilinear form $B : V \times V \to K$ is called **reflexive** if $B(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}) = 0$ implies $B(\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{v}) = 0$ for all **v**, **w** in *V*. **Definition:** Let $B : V \times V \to K$ be a reflexive bilinear form. **v**, **w** in *V* are **orthogonal with respect to *B*** if $B(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}) = 0$.

A bilinear form $B$ is reflexive if and only if it is either symmetric or alternating.[^n3] In the absence of reflexivity we have to distinguish left and right orthogonality. In a reflexive space the left and right radicals agree and are termed the *kernel* or the *radical* of the bilinear form: the subspace of all vectors orthogonal with every other vector. A vector $\mathbf{v}$, with matrix representation $x$, is in the radical of a bilinear form with matrix representation $A$, if and only if $Ax = 0 \Leftrightarrow x^{T}A = 0$. The radical is always a subspace of $V$. It is trivial if and only if the matrix $A$ is nonsingular, and thus if and only if the bilinear form is nondegenerate.

Suppose $W$ is a subspace. Define the *[orthogonal complement](wikipedia://en/1058833)*[^n4]

$$W^{\perp} = \left\{\mathbf{v} \mid B(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}) = 0 \text{ for all } \mathbf{w} \in W\right\} .$$

For a non-degenerate form on a finite-dimensional space, the map $V/W \to W^{\perp}$ is [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective), and the dimension of $W^{\perp}$ is $\dim(V) - \dim(W)$.

### Bounded and elliptic bilinear forms {#Bounded_and_elliptic_bilinear_forms}

::: {#o14 .definition cue=inline-head name="Definition"}

**Definition:** A bilinear form on a [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538) $(V, \|\cdot\|)$ is **bounded**, if there is a constant $C$ such that for all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in V$,

$$B ( \mathbf{u} , \mathbf{v}) \le C \left\| \mathbf{u} \right\| \left\|\mathbf{v} \right\| .$$

:::

::: {#o15 .definition cue=inline-head name="Definition"}

**Definition:** A bilinear form on a normed vector space $(V, \|\cdot\|)$ is **elliptic**, or [coercive](wikipedia://en/8971904#Coercive_operators_and_forms), if there is a constant $c > 0$ such that for all $\mathbf{u} \in V$,

$$B ( \mathbf{u} , \mathbf{u}) \ge c \left\| \mathbf{u} \right\| ^2 .$$

## Associated quadratic form {#Associated_quadratic_form}

For any bilinear form $B : V \times V \to K$, there exists an associated [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) $Q : V \to K$ defined by $Q : V \to K : \mathbf{v} \mapsto B(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v})$.

When $\operatorname{char}(K) \ne 2$, the quadratic form *Q* is determined by the symmetric part of the bilinear form *B* and is independent of the antisymmetric part. In this case there is a one-to-one correspondence between the symmetric part of the bilinear form and the quadratic form, and it makes sense to speak of the symmetric bilinear form associated with a quadratic form.

When $\operatorname{char}(K) = 2$ and $\dim V > 1$, this correspondence between quadratic forms and symmetric bilinear forms breaks down.

In the other direction, if $F : V \otimes V \to K$ is a linear map the corresponding bilinear form is given by composing *F* with the bilinear map $V \times V \to V \otimes V$ that sends $(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w})$ to $\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{w}$.

The set of all linear maps $V \otimes V \to K$ is the [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) of $V \otimes V$, so bilinear forms may be thought of as elements of $(V \otimes V)^{\ast}$ which (when $V$ is finite-dimensional) is canonically isomorphic to $V^{\ast} \otimes V^{\ast}$.

Likewise, symmetric bilinear forms may be thought of as elements of $(\operatorname{Sym}^{2}V)^{*}$ (dual of the second [symmetric power](wikipedia://en/57047243) of $V$) and alternating bilinear forms as elements of $(\Lambda^{2}V)^{\ast} \simeq \Lambda^{2}V^{\ast}$ (the second [exterior power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_power) of $V^{\ast}$). If $\operatorname{char}(K) \ne 2$, $(\operatorname{Sym}^{2}V)^{*} \simeq \operatorname{Sym}^{2}(V^{\ast})$.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### Pairs of distinct vector spaces {#Pairs_of_distinct_vector_spaces}

Much of the theory is available for a [bilinear mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilinear_mapping) from two vector spaces over the same base field to that field

Here we still have induced linear mappings from $V$ to $W^{\ast}$, and from $W$ to $V^{\ast}$. It may happen that these mappings are isomorphisms; assuming finite dimensions, if one is an isomorphism, the other must be. When this occurs, *B* is said to be a **perfect pairing**.

In finite dimensions, this is equivalent to the pairing being nondegenerate (the spaces necessarily having the same dimensions). For modules (instead of vector spaces), just as how a nondegenerate form is weaker than a unimodular form, a nondegenerate pairing is a weaker notion than a perfect pairing. A pairing can be nondegenerate without being a perfect pairing, for instance $\mathbf{Z} \times \mathbf{Z} \to \mathbf{Z}$ via $(x, y) \mapsto 2xy$ is nondegenerate, but induces multiplication by 2 on the map $\mathbf{Z} \to \mathbf{Z}^{\ast}$.

Terminology varies in coverage of bilinear forms. For example, [F. Reese Harvey](wikipedia://en/65653589) discusses "eight types of inner product".[^n5] To define them he uses diagonal matrices *A<sub>ij</sub>* having only +1 or −1 for non-zero elements. Some of the "inner products" are [symplectic forms](wikipedia://en/292852) and some are [sesquilinear forms](wikipedia://en/860138) or [Hermitian forms](wikipedia://en/860138#Hermitian_form). Rather than a general field $K$, the instances with real numbers $\mathbf{R}$, complex numbers $\mathbf{C}$, and [quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternions) $\mathbf{H}$ are spelled out. The bilinear form

$$\sum_{k=1}^p x_k y_k - \sum_{k=p+1}^n x_k y_k$$

is called the **real symmetric case** and labeled $\mathbf{R}(p, q)$, where $p + q = n$. Then he articulates the connection to traditional terminology:[^n6]

Some of the real symmetric cases are very important. The positive definite case **R**(*n*, 0) is called *Euclidean space*, while the case of a single minus, **R**(*n*−1, 1) is called *Lorentzian space*. If *n* = 4, then Lorentzian space is also called *[Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space)* or *Minkowski spacetime*. The special case **R**(*p*, *p*) will be referred to as the *split-case*.

### General modules {#General_modules}

Given a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$ and a right [$R$-module](wikipedia://en/276410) $M$ and its [dual module](wikipedia://en/44902322) $M^{\ast}$, a mapping $B : M^{\ast} \times M \to R$ is called a **bilinear form** if

for all $u, v \in M^{\ast}$, all $x, y \in M$ and all $\alpha, \beta \in R$.

The mapping $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle : M^{\ast} \times M \to R : (u, x) \mapsto u(x)$ is known as the *[natural pairing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_pairing)*, also called the *canonical bilinear form* on $M^{\ast} \times M$.[^n7]

A linear map $S : M^{\ast} \to M^{\ast} : u \mapsto S(u)$ induces the bilinear form $B : M^{\ast} \times M \to R : (u, x) \mapsto \langle S(u), x\rangle$, and a linear map $T : M \to M : x \mapsto T(x)$ induces the bilinear form $B : M^{\ast} \times M \to R : (u, x) \mapsto \langle u, T(x)\rangle$.

Conversely, a bilinear form $B : M^{\ast} \times M \to R$ induces the *R*-linear maps $S : M^{\ast} \to M^{\ast} : u \mapsto (x \mapsto B(u, x))$ and $T' : M \to M^{\ast\ast} : x \mapsto (u \mapsto B(u, x))$. Here, $M^{\ast\ast}$ denotes the [double dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_dual) of $M$.
