# Fundamental theorem of asset pricing

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fundamental theorem of asset pricing"}

The **fundamental theorems of asset pricing** (also: **of arbitrage**, **of finance**), in both [financial economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_economics) and [mathematical finance](wikipedia://en/33589680), provide necessary and sufficient conditions for a market to be [arbitrage-free](wikipedia://en/3162), and for a market to be [complete](wikipedia://en/2189987). An arbitrage opportunity is a way of making money with no initial investment without any possibility of loss.[^o2] Though arbitrage opportunities do exist briefly in real life, it has been said that any sensible market model must avoid this type of profit.[^o3] The first theorem is important in that it ensures a fundamental property of market models. Completeness is a common property of market models (for instance the [Black–Scholes model](wikipedia://en/113515)). A complete market is one in which every [contingent claim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contingent_claim) can be [replicated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replicating_strategy). Though this property is common in models, it is not always considered desirable or realistic.[^o3]

:::

## Discrete markets {#Discrete_markets}

In a discrete (i.e. finite state) market, the following hold:[^o3]

1. **The First Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing**: A discrete market on a discrete [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325) $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ is [arbitrage-free](wikipedia://en/3162) if, and only if, there exists at least one [risk neutral probability measure](wikipedia://en/441911) that is [equivalent](wikipedia://en/6612667) to the original probability measure, *P*.
1. **The Second Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing**: An arbitrage-free market (S,B) consisting of a collection of stocks *S* and a [risk-free bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk-free_bond) *B* is [complete](wikipedia://en/2189987) if and only if there exists a unique risk-neutral measure that is equivalent to *P* and has [numeraire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numeraire) *B*.

## In more general markets {#In_more_general_markets}

When stock price returns follow a single [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436), there is a unique risk neutral measure. When the stock price process is assumed to follow a more general [sigma-martingale](wikipedia://en/33412531) or [semimartingale](wikipedia://en/9653775), then the concept of arbitrage is too narrow, and a stronger concept such as [no free lunch with vanishing risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_free_lunch_with_vanishing_risk) (NFLVR) must be used to describe these opportunities in an infinite dimensional setting.[^o6]

In continuous time, a version of the fundamental theorems of asset pricing reads:[^o7]

Let $S=(S_t)_{t\geq 0}$ be a d-dimensional semimartingale market (a collection of stocks), $B$ the risk-free bond and $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ the underlying probability space. Furthermore, we call a measure $Q$ an [equivalent local martingale measure](wikipedia://en/441911) if $Q \approx P$ and if the processes $\left( \frac{S^i_t }{B_t} \right) _t$ are local martingales under the measure $Q$.

1. **The First Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing**: Assume $S$ is locally bounded. Then the market $S$ satisfies NFLVR if and only if there exists an equivalent local martingale measure.
1. **The Second Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing**: Assume that there exists an equivalent local martingale measure $Q$. Then $S$ is a complete market if and only if $Q$ is the unique local martingale measure.
