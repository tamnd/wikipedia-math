# Weierstrass elliptic function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Weierstrass elliptic function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Weierstrass elliptic functions** are [elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/69939) that take a particularly simple form. They are named for [Karl Weierstrass](wikipedia://en/93459). This class of functions is also referred to as **℘-functions** and they are usually denoted by the symbol ℘, a uniquely fancy [script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursive) *p*. They play an important role in the theory of elliptic functions, i.e., [meromorphic functions](wikipedia://en/80857) that are [doubly periodic](wikipedia://en/3643495). A ℘-function together with its derivative can be used to parameterize [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225) and they generate the field of elliptic functions with respect to a given period lattice.

## Motivation {#Motivation}

A [cubic](wikipedia://en/6680683) of the form $C_{g_2,g_3}^\mathbb{C}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}^2:y^2=4x^3-g_2x-g_3\}$, where $g_2,g_3\in\mathbb{C}$ are complex numbers with $g_2^3-27g_3^2\neq0$, cannot be [rationally parameterized](wikipedia://en/865939).[^o31] Yet one still wants to find a way to parameterize it.

For the [quadric](wikipedia://en/145570) $K=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2:x^2+y^2=1\right\}$; the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071), there exists a (non-rational) parameterization using the sine function and its derivative the cosine function:

$$\psi:\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\to K, \quad t\mapsto(\sin t,\cos t).$$

Because of the periodicity of the sine and cosine $\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}$ is chosen to be the domain, so the function is bijective.

In a similar way one can get a parameterization of $C_{g_2,g_3}^\mathbb{C}$ by means of the doubly periodic $\wp$-function and its derivative, namely via $(x,y)=(\wp(z),\wp'(z))$. This parameterization has the domain $\mathbb{C}/\Lambda$, which is topologically equivalent to a [torus](wikipedia://en/74800).[^o3]

There is another analogy to the [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367). Consider the integral function

$$a(x)=\int_0^x\frac{dy}{\sqrt{1-y^2}} .$$

It can be simplified by substituting $y=\sin t$ and $s=\arcsin x$:

$$a(x)=\int_0^s dt = s = \arcsin x .$$

That means $a^{-1}(x) = \sin x$. So the sine function is an [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907) of an integral function.[^o4]

Elliptic functions are the inverse functions of [elliptic integrals](wikipedia://en/9960). In particular, let:

$$u(z)=\int_z^\infty\frac{ds}{\sqrt{4s^3-g_2s-g_3}} .$$

Then the extension of $u^{-1}$ to the complex plane equals the $\wp$-function.[^o5] This invertibility is used in [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759) to provide a solution to certain [nonlinear differential equations](wikipedia://en/146103#Nonlinear_differential_equations) satisfying the [Painlevé property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Painlevé_property), i.e., those equations that admit [poles](wikipedia://en/81560) as their only [movable singularities](wikipedia://en/7664719).[^o6]

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: Weierstrass elliptic function P.png.* Visualization of the $\wp$-function with invariants $g_2=1+i$ and $g_3=2-3i$ in which white corresponds to a pole, black to a zero.

Let $\omega_1,\omega_2\in\mathbb{C}$ be two [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) that are [linearly independent](wikipedia://en/101863) over $\mathbb{R}$ and let $\Lambda:=\mathbb{Z}\omega_1+\mathbb{Z}\omega_2:=\{m\omega_1+n\omega_2: m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ be the [period lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Period_lattice) generated by those numbers. Then the $\wp$-function is defined as follows:

$$\wp(z,\omega_1,\omega_2):=\wp(z) = \frac{1}{z^2} + \sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\left(\frac 1 {(z-\lambda)^2} - \frac 1 {\lambda^2}\right).$$

This series converges locally [uniformly absolutely](wikipedia://en/19421960) in the [complex torus](wikipedia://en/3055042) $\mathbb{C} / \Lambda$.

It is common to use $1$ and $\tau$ in the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826) $\mathbb{H}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Im}(z) > 0\}$ as [generators](wikipedia://en/56353) of the [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246). Dividing by $\omega_1$ maps the lattice $\mathbb{Z}\omega_1+\mathbb{Z}\omega_2$ isomorphically onto the lattice $\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\tau$ with $\tau=\tfrac{\omega_2}{\omega_1}$. Because $-\tau$ can be substituted for $\tau$, [without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000) we can assume $\tau\in\mathbb{H}$, and then define $\wp(z,\tau) := \wp(z, 1,\tau)$. With that definition, we have $\wp(z,\omega_1,\omega_2) = \omega_1^{-2}\wp(z/\omega_1,\omega_2/\omega_1)$.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

- $\wp$ is a [meromorphic function](wikipedia://en/80857) with a pole of order 2 at each period $\lambda$ in $\Lambda$.
- $\wp$ is a [homogeneous function](wikipedia://en/622844) in that:

$$\wp(\lambda z , \lambda\omega_{1}, \lambda\omega_{2}) = \lambda^{-2} \wp (z, \omega_{1},\omega_{2}).$$

- $\wp$ is an even function. That means $\wp(z)=\wp(-z)$ for all $z \in \mathbb{C} \setminus \Lambda$, which can be seen in the following way:

$$\begin{aligned} \wp(-z) & =\frac{1}{(-z)^2} + \sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\left(\frac{1}{(-z-\lambda)^2}-\frac{1}{\lambda^2}\right) \\ & =\frac{1}{z^2}+\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\left(\frac{1}{(z+\lambda)^2}-\frac{1}{\lambda^2}\right) \\ & =\frac{1}{z^2}+\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\left(\frac{1}{(z-\lambda)^2}-\frac{1}{\lambda^2}\right)=\wp(z). \end{aligned}$$

The second last equality holds because $\{-\lambda:\lambda \in \Lambda\}=\Lambda$. Since the sum converges absolutely this rearrangement does not change the limit.

- The derivative of $\wp$ is given by:[^o11] $$\wp'(z)=-2\sum_{\lambda \in \Lambda}\frac1{(z-\lambda)^3}.$$
- $\wp$ and $\wp'$ are [doubly periodic](wikipedia://en/3643495) with the periods $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$.[^o11] This means: $$\begin{aligned} \wp(z+\omega_1) &= \wp(z) = \wp(z+\omega_2),\ \textrm{and} \\[3mu] \wp'(z+\omega_1) &= \wp'(z) = \wp'(z+\omega_2). \end{aligned}$$ It follows that $\wp(z+\lambda)=\wp(z)$ and $\wp'(z+\lambda)=\wp'(z)$ for all $\lambda \in \Lambda$.

:::

## Laurent expansion {#Laurent_expansion}

Let $r:=\min\{{|\lambda}|:0\neq\lambda\in\Lambda\}$. Then for $0<|z|<r$ the $\wp$-function has the following [Laurent expansion](wikipedia://en/61213)

$$\wp(z)=\frac1{z^2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty (2n+1)G_{2n+2}z^{2n}$$

where

$$G_n=\sum_{0\neq\lambda\in\Lambda}\lambda^{-n}$$

for $n \geq 3$ are so called [Eisenstein series](wikipedia://en/1270458).[^o11]

## Differential equation {#Differential_equation}

Set $g_2=60G_4$ and $g_3=140G_6$. Then the $\wp$-function satisfies the differential equation[^o11]

$$\wp'^2(z) = 4\wp ^3(z)-g_2\wp(z)-g_3.$$

This relation can be verified by forming a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of powers of $\wp$ and $\wp'$ to eliminate the pole at $z=0$. This yields an entire elliptic function that has to be constant by [Liouville's theorem](wikipedia://en/312293).[^o11]

### Invariants {#Invariants}

*Image: Gee three real.jpeg.* The real part of the invariant *g*<sub>3</sub> as a function of the square of the [nome](wikipedia://en/1471192) *q* on the unit disk.

*Image: Gee three imag.jpeg.* The imaginary part of the invariant *g*<sub>3</sub> as a function of the square of the nome *q* on the unit disk.

The coefficients of the above differential equation $g_2$ and $g_3$ are known as the *invariants*. Because they depend on the lattice $\Lambda$ they can be viewed as functions in $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$.

The series expansion suggests that $g_2$ and $g_3$ are [homogeneous functions](wikipedia://en/622844) of degree $-4$ and $-6$. That is[^o15]

$$g_2(\lambda \omega_1, \lambda \omega_2) = \lambda^{-4} g_2(\omega_1, \omega_2)$$

$$g_3(\lambda \omega_1, \lambda \omega_2) = \lambda^{-6} g_3(\omega_1, \omega_2)$$

for $\lambda \neq 0$.

If $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ are chosen in such a way that $\operatorname{Im}\left( \tfrac{\omega_2}{\omega_1} \right)>0$, $g_2$ and $g_3$ can be interpreted as functions on the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826) $\mathbb{H}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Im}(z)>0\}$.

Let $\tau=\tfrac{\omega_2}{\omega_1}$. One has:[^o16]

$$g_2(1,\tau)=\omega_1^4g_2(\omega_1,\omega_2),$$

$$g_3(1,\tau)=\omega_1^6 g_3(\omega_1,\omega_2).$$

That means *g*<sub>2</sub> and *g*<sub>3</sub> are only scaled by doing this. Set

$$g_2(\tau):=g_2(1,\tau)$$

and

$$g_3(\tau):=g_3(1,\tau).$$

As functions of $\tau\in\mathbb{H}$, $g_2$ and $g_3$ are so called [modular forms.](wikipedia://en/286000)

The [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) for $g_2$ and $g_3$ are given as follows:[^o17]

$$g_2(\tau)=\frac43\pi^4 \left[ 1+ 240\sum_{k=1}^\infty \sigma_3(k) q^{2k} \right]$$

$$g_3(\tau)=\frac{8}{27}\pi^6 \left[ 1- 504\sum_{k=1}^\infty \sigma_5(k) q^{2k} \right]$$

where

$$\sigma_m(k):=\sum_{d\mid{k}}d^m$$

is the [divisor function](wikipedia://en/407249) and $q=e^{\pi i\tau}$ is the [nome](wikipedia://en/1471192).

::: {#o19 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Modular discriminant"}

### Modular discriminant {#Modular_discriminant}

*Image: Discriminant real part.jpeg.* The real part of the discriminant as a function of the square of the nome *q* on the unit disk.

The *modular discriminant* $\Delta$ is defined as the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607) of the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) of the differential equation

$$\wp'^2(z) = 4\wp ^3(z)-g_2\wp(z)-g_3$$

as follows:

$$\Delta=g_2^3-27g_3^2.$$

The discriminant is a modular form of weight $12$. That is, under the action of the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019), it transforms as

$$\Delta \left( \frac {a\tau+b} {c\tau+d}\right) = \left(c\tau+d\right)^{12} \Delta(\tau)$$

where $a,b,d,c\in\mathbb{Z}$ with $ad-bc = 1$.[^o20]

Note that $\Delta=(2\pi)^{12}\eta^{24}$ where $\eta$ is the [Dedekind eta function](wikipedia://en/447020).[^o21]

For the Fourier coefficients of $\Delta$, see [Ramanujan tau function](wikipedia://en/1231708).

:::

### The constants *e*<sub>1</sub>, *e*<sub>2</sub> and *e*<sub>3</sub> {#The_constants_e1,_e2_and_e3}

$e_1$, $e_2$ and $e_3$ are usually used to denote the values of the $\wp$-function at the half-periods.

$$e_1\equiv\wp\left(\frac{\omega_1}{2}\right)$$

$$e_2\equiv\wp\left(\frac{\omega_2}{2}\right)$$

$$e_3\equiv\wp\left(\frac{\omega_1+\omega_2}{2}\right)$$

They are pairwise distinct and only depend on the lattice $\Lambda$ and not on its generators.[^o23]

$e_1$, $e_2$ and $e_3$ are the roots of the cubic polynomial $4\wp(z)^3-g_2\wp(z)-g_3$ and are related by the equation:

$$e_1+e_2+e_3=0.$$

Because those roots are distinct the discriminant $\Delta$ does not vanish on the upper half plane.[^o24] Now we can rewrite the differential equation:

$$\wp'^2(z)=4(\wp(z)-e_1)(\wp(z)-e_2)(\wp(z)-e_3).$$

That means the half-periods are zeros of $\wp'$.

The invariants $g_2$ and $g_3$ can be expressed in terms of these constants in the following way:[^o25]

$$g_2 = -4 (e_1 e_2 + e_1 e_3 + e_2 e_3)$$

$$g_3 = 4 e_1 e_2 e_3$$

$e_1$, $e_2$ and $e_3$ are related to the [modular lambda function](wikipedia://en/27903678):

$$\lambda (\tau)=\frac{e_3-e_2}{e_1-e_2},\quad \tau=\frac{\omega_2}{\omega_1}.$$

## Relation to Jacobi's elliptic functions {#Relation_to_Jacobi's_elliptic_functions}

For numerical work, it is often convenient to calculate the Weierstrass elliptic function in terms of [Jacobi's elliptic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi's_elliptic_functions).

The basic relations are:[^o27]

$$\wp(z) = e_3 + \frac{e_1 - e_3}{\operatorname{sn}^2 w} = e_2 + ( e_1 - e_3 ) \frac{\operatorname{dn}^2 w}{\operatorname{sn}^2 w} = e_1 + ( e_1 - e_3 ) \frac{\operatorname{cn}^2 w}{\operatorname{sn}^2 w}$$

where $e_1,e_2$ and $e_3$ are the three roots described above and where the modulus *k* of the Jacobi functions equals

$$k = \sqrt\frac{e_2 - e_3}{e_1 - e_3}$$

and their argument *w* equals

$$w = z \sqrt{e_1 - e_3}.$$

## Relation to Jacobi's theta functions {#Relation_to_Jacobi's_theta_functions}

The function $\wp (z,\tau)=\wp (z,1,\omega_2/\omega_1)$ can be represented by [Jacobi's theta functions](wikipedia://en/449745#Auxiliary_functions):

$$\wp (z,\tau)=\left(\pi \theta_2(0,q)\theta_3(0,q)\frac{\theta_4(\pi z,q)}{\theta_1(\pi z,q)}\right)^2-\frac{\pi^2}{3}\left(\theta_2^4(0,q)+\theta_3^4(0,q)\right)$$

where $q=e^{\pi i\tau}$ is the nome and $\tau$ is the period ratio $(\tau\in\mathbb{H})$. This also provides a very rapid algorithm for computing $\wp (z,\tau)$.

## Relation to elliptic curves {#Relation_to_elliptic_curves}

Consider the embedding of the cubic curve in the [complex projective plane](wikipedia://en/737155)

$$\bar C_{g_2,g_3}^\mathbb{C} = \{(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}^2:y^2=4x^3-g_2x-g_3\}\cup\{O\}\subset \mathbb{C}^{2} \cup \mathbb{P}_1(\mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{P}_2(\mathbb{C}).$$

where $O$ is a point lying on the [line at infinity](wikipedia://en/403142) $\mathbb{P}_1(\mathbb{C})$. For this cubic there exists no rational parameterization, if $\Delta \neq 0$.[^o31] In this case it is also called an elliptic curve. Nevertheless there is a parameterization in [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316) that uses the $\wp$-function and its derivative $\wp'$:[^o32]

$$\varphi(\wp,\wp'): \mathbb{C}/\Lambda\to\bar C_{g_2,g_3}^\mathbb{C}, \quad z \mapsto \begin{cases} \left[\wp(z):\wp'(z):1\right] & z \notin \Lambda \\ \left[0:1:0\right] \quad & z \in \Lambda \end{cases}$$

Now the map $\varphi$ is [bijective](wikipedia://en/3942) and parameterizes the elliptic curve $\bar C_{g_2,g_3}^\mathbb{C}$.

$\mathbb{C}/\Lambda$ is an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) and a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), equipped with the [quotient topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_topology).

It can be shown that every Weierstrass cubic is given in such a way. That is to say that for every pair $g_2,g_3\in\mathbb{C}$ with $\Delta = g_2^3 - 27g_3^2 \neq 0$ there exists a lattice $\mathbb{Z}\omega_1+\mathbb{Z}\omega_2$, such that

$g_2=g_2(\omega_1,\omega_2)$ and $g_3=g_3(\omega_1,\omega_2)$.[^o33]

::: {#o35 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Addition theorem"}

## Addition theorem {#Addition_theorem}

The addition theorem states[^o36] that if $z,w,$ and $z+w$ do not belong to $\Lambda$, then

$$\det\begin{bmatrix}1 & \wp(z) & \wp'(z) \\ 1 & \wp(w) & \wp'(w) \\ 1 & \wp(z+w) & -\wp'(z+w)\end{bmatrix}=0.$$

This states that the points $P=(\wp(z),\wp'(z)),$ $Q=(\wp(w),\wp'(w)),$ and $R=(\wp(z+w),-\wp'(z+w))$ are collinear, the geometric form of the [group law of an elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/10225#Group_law).

This can be proven[^o37] by considering constants $A,B$ such that

$$\wp'(z) = A\wp(z) + B, \quad \wp'(w) = A\wp(w) + B.$$

Then the elliptic function

$$\wp'(\zeta) - A\wp(\zeta) - B$$

has a pole of order three at zero, and therefore three zeros whose sum belongs to $\Lambda$. Two of the zeros are $z$ and $w$, and thus the third is congruent to $-z-w$.

### Alternative form {#Alternative_form}

The addition theorem can be put into the alternative form, for $z,w,z-w,z+w\not\in\Lambda$:[^o39]

$$\wp(z+w)=\frac14 \left[\frac{\wp'(z)-\wp'(w)}{\wp(z)-\wp(w)}\right]^2-\wp(z)-\wp(w).$$

As well as the duplication formula:[^o39]

$$\wp(2z)=\frac14\left[\frac{\wp''(z)}{\wp'(z)}\right]^2-2\wp(z).$$

::: {#o41 .proof cue=heading name="Proofs"}

#### Proofs {#Proofs}

This can be proven from the addition theorem shown above. The points $P=(\wp(u),\wp'(u)), Q=(\wp(v),\wp'(v)),$ and $R=(\wp(u+v),-\wp'(u+v))$ are collinear and lie on the curve $y^2=4x^3-g_2x-g_3$. The slope of that line is

$$m=\frac{y_P-y_Q}{x_P-x_Q}=\frac{\wp'(u)-\wp'(v)}{\wp(u)-\wp(v)}.$$

So $x=x_P=\wp(u)$, $x=x_Q=\wp(v)$, and $x=x_R=\wp(u+v)$ all satisfy a cubic

$$(mx+q)^2=4x^3-g_2x-g_3,$$

where $q$ is a constant. This becomes

$$4x^3-m^2x^2-(2mq+g_2)x-g_3-q^2=0.$$

Thus $x_P+x_Q+x_R=\frac{m^2}4$ which provides the wanted formula $\wp(u+v)+\wp(u)+\wp(v)=\frac14 \left[ \frac{\wp'(u)-\wp'(v)}{\wp(u)-\wp(v)} \right]^2.$

A direct proof is as follows.[^o42] Any elliptic function $f$ can be expressed as:

$$f(u)=c\prod_{i=1}^n \frac{\sigma(u-a_i)}{\sigma(u-b_i)} \quad c \in \mathbb{C}$$

where $\sigma$ is the [Weierstrass sigma function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_sigma_function) and $a_i , b_i$ are the respective zeros and poles in the period [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496). Considering the function $\wp(u)-\wp(v)$ as a function of $u$, we have

$$\wp(u)-\wp(v)=c\frac{\sigma(u+v)\sigma(u-v)}{\sigma(u)^2}.$$

Multiplying both sides by $u^2$ and letting $u\to 0$, we have $1 = -c\sigma(v)^2$, so $c=-\frac1{\sigma(v)^2} \implies\wp(u)-\wp(v)=-\frac{\sigma(u+v)\sigma(u-v)}{\sigma(u)^2\sigma(v)^2}.$

By definition the [Weierstrass zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_zeta_function): $\frac{d}{dz}\ln \sigma(z)=\zeta(z)$ therefore we logarithmically differentiate both sides with respect to $u$ obtaining:

$$\frac{\wp'(u)}{\wp(u)-\wp(v)}=\zeta(u+v)-2\zeta(u)-\zeta(u-v)$$

Once again by definition $\zeta'(z)=-\wp(z)$ thus by differentiating once more on both sides and rearranging the terms we obtain

$$-\wp(u+v)=-\wp(u)+\frac12 \frac{ \wp''(v)[\wp(u)-\wp(v) ]-\wp'(u)[\wp'(u)-\wp'(v)] }{ [\wp(u)-\wp(v) ]^2 }$$

Knowing that $\wp''$ has the following differential equation $2\wp''=12\wp^2-g_2$ and rearranging the terms one gets the wanted formula

$$\wp(u+v)=\frac14 \left[\frac{\wp'(u)-\wp'(v)}{\wp(u)-\wp(v)}\right]^2-\wp(u)-\wp(v).$$

## Typography {#Typography}

The Weierstrass's elliptic function is usually written with a rather special, lower case script letter ℘, which was Weierstrass's own notation introduced in his lectures of 1862–1863. It should not be confused with the normal mathematical script letters P: 𝒫 and 𝓅.

In computing, the letter ℘ is available as `\wp` in [TeX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TeX). In [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode) the code point is 2118, with the more correct alias weierstrass elliptic function. In [HTML](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTML), it can be escaped as `&weierp;` or `&wp;`. 2118
