# Fixed point (mathematics)

*Image: Fixpoint012 svg.svg.* The function $f(x)=x^3 - 3x^2 + 3x$ (shown in red) has the fixed points 0, 1, and 2.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fixed point"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **fixed point** (sometimes shortened to **fixpoint**), also known as an **invariant point**, is a value that does not change under a given [transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_%28mathematics%29). Specifically, for [functions](wikipedia://en/185427), a fixed point is an element that is mapped to itself by the function. Any set of fixed points of a transformation is also an [invariant set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_set).

:::

## Fixed point of a function {#Fixed_point_of_a_function}

Formally, $c$ is a fixed point of a function $f$ if $c$ belongs to both the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) and the [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) of $f$, and $f(c) = c$. In particular, $f$ cannot have any fixed point if its domain is disjoint from its codomain. If $f$ is defined on the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), it corresponds, in graphical terms, to a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), and each fixed-point $c$ corresponds to an intersection of the curve with the line $y\,=\,x$, cf. picture.

For example, if $f$ is defined on the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) by $f(x) = x^2 - 3 x + 4,$ then 2 is a fixed point of $f$, because $f(2) = 2$.

Not all functions have fixed points: for example, $f(x) = x + 1$ has no fixed points because $x + 1$ is never equal to $x$ for any real number.

## Fixed point iteration {#Fixed_point_iteration}

In [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506), *fixed-point iteration* is a method of computing fixed points of a function. Specifically, given a function $f$ with the same domain and codomain, a point $x_0$ in the domain of $f$, the fixed-point iteration is

$$x_{n+1}=f(x_n), \, n=0, 1, 2, \dots$$

which gives rise to the [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots$ of [iterated function](wikipedia://en/1448702) applications $x_0, f(x_0), f(f(x_0)), \dots$ which is hoped to [converge](wikipedia://en/26551602) to a point $x$. If $f$ is continuous, then one can prove that the obtained $x$ is a fixed point of $f$.

The notions of attracting fixed points, repelling fixed points, and [periodic points](wikipedia://en/4941621) are defined with respect to fixed-point iteration.

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Fixed-point theorems"}

## Fixed-point theorems {#Fixed-point_theorems}

A fixed-point theorem is a result saying that at least one fixed point exists, under some general condition.[^o6]

For example, the [Banach fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/46676) (1922) gives a general criterion guaranteeing that, if it is satisfied, [fixed-point iteration](wikipedia://en/7344320) will always converge to a fixed point.

The [Brouwer fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/4101) (1911) says that any [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) from the closed [unit ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_ball) in *n*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) to itself must have a fixed point, but it doesn't describe how to find the fixed point.

The [Lefschetz fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/582530) (and the [Nielsen fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/3603035)) from [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) give a way to count fixed points.

:::

## Fixed point of a group action {#Fixed_point_of_a_group_action}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), for a group *G* acting on a set *X* with a [group action](wikipedia://en/12781) $\cdot$, *x* in *X* is said to be a fixed point of *g* if $g \cdot x = x$.

The [fixed-point subgroup](wikipedia://en/43166060) $G^f$ of an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) *f* of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G* is the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of *G*:

$$G^f = \{ g \in G \mid f(g) = g \}.$$

Similarly, the [fixed-point subring](wikipedia://en/47933712) $R^f$ of an automorphism *f* of a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *R* is the [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of the fixed points of *f*, that is,

$$R^f = \{ r \in R \mid f(r) = r \}.$$

In [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316), the set of the fixed points of a set of automorphisms of a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) is called the [fixed field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_field) of the set of automorphisms.

## Topological fixed point property {#Topological_fixed_point_property}

A [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$ is said to have the [fixed point property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_point_property) (FPP) if for any [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122)

$$f\colon X \to X$$

there exists $x \in X$ such that $f(x)=x$.

The FPP is a [topological invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_invariant), i.e., it is preserved by any [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660). The FPP is also preserved by any [retraction](wikipedia://en/2120001).

According to the [Brouwer fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/4101), every [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) and [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) has the FPP. Compactness alone does not imply the FPP, and convexity is not even a topological property, so it makes sense to ask how to topologically characterize the FPP. In 1932 [Borsuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karol_Borsuk) asked whether compactness together with [contractibility](wikipedia://en/1752414) could be a necessary and sufficient condition for the FPP to hold. The problem was open for 20 years until the conjecture was disproved by Kinoshita, who found an example of a compact contractible space without the FPP.[^o9]

## Fixed points of partial orders {#Fixed_points_of_partial_orders}

In [domain theory](wikipedia://en/325077), the notion and terminology of fixed points is generalized to a [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order). Let ≤ be a partial order over a set *X* and let *f*: *X* → *X* be a function over *X*. Then a **prefixed point** (also spelled **pre-fixed point**, sometimes shortened to **prefixpoint** or **pre-fixpoint**) of *f* is any *p* such that *f*(*p*) ≤ *p*. Analogously, a *postfixed point* of *f* is any *p* such that *p* ≤ *f*(*p*).[^o12] The opposite usage occasionally appears.[^o13] Malkis justifies the definition presented here as follows: "since *f* is before the inequality sign in the term *f*(*x*) ≤ *x*, such *x* is called a prefix point."[^o14] A fixed point is a point that is both a prefixpoint and a postfixpoint. Prefixpoints and postfixpoints have applications in [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392).[^o15]

### Least fixed point {#Least_fixed_point}

In [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), the [least fixed point](wikipedia://en/1211913) of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) from a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) (poset) to itself is the fixed point which is less than each other fixed point, according to the order of the poset. A function need not have a least fixed point, but if it does then the least fixed point is unique.

One way to express the [Knaster–Tarski theorem](wikipedia://en/181417) is to say that a [monotone function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotone_function) on a [complete lattice](wikipedia://en/44777) has a [least fixed point](wikipedia://en/1211913) that coincides with its least prefixpoint (and similarly its greatest fixed point coincides with its greatest postfixpoint).[^o17]

:::

## Fixed-point combinator {#Fixed-point_combinator}

In [combinatory logic](wikipedia://en/149848) for [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), a fixed-point combinator is a [higher-order function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher-order_function) $\mathsf{fix}$ that returns a fixed point of its argument function, if one exists. Formally, if the function *f* has one or more fixed points, then

$$\operatorname{\mathsf{fix}}f = f(\operatorname{\mathsf{fix}}f).$$

## Fixed-point logics {#Fixed-point_logics}

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), fixed-point logics are extensions of classical predicate logic that have been introduced to express recursion. Their development has been motivated by [descriptive complexity theory](wikipedia://en/1212009) and their relationship to [database query languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database_query_language), in particular to [Datalog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Datalog).

::: {#o21 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

In many fields, equilibria or [stability](wikipedia://en/5493795) are fundamental concepts that can be described in terms of fixed points. Some examples follow.

- In [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849), a fixed point of a [projectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectivity) has been called a **double point**.[^o22][^o23]
- In [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), a [Nash equilibrium](wikipedia://en/45337) of a [game](wikipedia://en/11924) is a fixed point of the game's [best response correspondence](wikipedia://en/759831). [John Nash](wikipedia://en/102567) exploited the [Kakutani fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/2658571) for his seminal paper that won him the Nobel prize in economics.
- In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), more precisely in the [theory of phase transitions](wikipedia://en/54423), *linearization* near an *unstable* fixed point has led to [Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_G._Wilson)'s Nobel prize-winning work inventing the [renormalization group](wikipedia://en/291462), and to the mathematical explanation of the term "[critical phenomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_phenomenon)."[^o24][^o25]
- [Programming language](wikipedia://en/23015) [compilers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compilers) use fixed point computations for program analysis, for example in [data-flow analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data-flow_analysis), which is often required for code [optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimization_%28computer_science%29). They are also the core concept used by the generic program analysis method [abstract interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_interpretation).[^o26]
- In [type theory](wikipedia://en/40282), the [fixed-point combinator](wikipedia://en/150287) allows definition of recursive functions in the [untyped lambda calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Untyped_lambda_calculus).
- The vector of [PageRank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PageRank) values of all web pages is the fixed point of a [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) derived from the [World Wide Web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Web)'s link structure.
- The stationary distribution of a [Markov chain](wikipedia://en/60876) is the fixed point of the one step transition probability function.
- Fixed points are used to finding formulas for [iterated functions](wikipedia://en/1448702).
