# Primitive element theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Primitive element theorem"}

In [field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_theory_%28mathematics%29), the **primitive element theorem** states that every [finite](wikipedia://en/4382697) [separable](wikipedia://en/363340) [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) is [simple](wikipedia://en/4771726), i.e. generated by a single element. This theorem implies in particular that all [algebraic number fields](wikipedia://en/28730822) over the rational numbers, and all extensions in which both fields are finite, are simple.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Terminology"}

## Terminology {#Terminology}

Let $E/F$ be a *[field extension](wikipedia://en/11634)*. An element $\alpha\in E$ is a *primitive element* for $E/F$ if $E=F(\alpha),$ i.e. if every element of $E$ can be written as a [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210) in $\alpha$ with coefficients in $F$. If there exists such a primitive element, then $E/F$ is referred to as a *[simple extension](wikipedia://en/4771726)*.

If the field extension $E/F$ has primitive element $\alpha$ and is of finite [degree](wikipedia://en/4382697) $n = [E:F]$, then every element $\gamma\in E$ can be written in the form

$$\gamma =a_0+a_1{\alpha}+\cdots+a_{n-1}{\alpha}^{n-1},$$

for unique coefficients $a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1}\in F$. That is, the set

$$\{1,\alpha,\ldots,{\alpha}^{n-1}\}$$

is a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for *E* as a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over *F*. The degree *n* is equal to the degree of the [irreducible polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) of *α* over *F*, the unique monic $f(X)\in F[X]$ of minimal degree with *α* as a root (a linear dependency of $\{1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{n-1},\alpha^n\}$).

If *L* is a [splitting field](wikipedia://en/174440) of $f(X)$ containing its *n* distinct roots $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n$, then there are *n* [field embeddings](wikipedia://en/13564) $\sigma_i : F(\alpha)\hookrightarrow L$ defined by $\sigma_i(\alpha)=\alpha_i$ and $\sigma(a)=a$ for $a\in F$, and these extend to automorphisms of *L* in the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673), $\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_n\in \mathrm{Gal}(L/F)$. Indeed, for an extension field with $[E: F]=n$, an element $\alpha$ is a primitive element if and only if $\alpha$ has *n* distinct conjugates $\sigma_1(\alpha),\ldots,\sigma_n(\alpha)$ in some splitting field $L \supseteq E$.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

If one adjoins to the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) $F = \mathbb{Q}$ the two irrational numbers $\sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{3}$ to get the extension field $E=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3})$ of degree 4, one can show this extension is simple, meaning $E=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ for a single $\alpha\in E$. Taking $\alpha = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$, the powers 1, *α*, *α*<sup>2</sup>, *α*<sup>3</sup> can be expanded as [linear combinations](wikipedia://en/55632) of 1, $\sqrt{2}$, $\sqrt{3}$, $\sqrt{6}$ with [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) coefficients. One can solve this [system of linear equations](wikipedia://en/113087) for $\sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{3}$ over $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$, to obtain $\sqrt{2} = \tfrac12(\alpha^3-9\alpha)$ and $\sqrt{3} = -\tfrac12(\alpha^3-11\alpha)$. This shows that *α* is indeed a primitive element:

$$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3)=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2 + \sqrt3).$$

One may also use the following more general argument.[^o6] The field $E=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2,\sqrt 3)$ clearly has four field automorphisms $\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3,\sigma_4: E\to E$ defined by $\sigma_i(\sqrt 2)=\pm\sqrt 2$ and $\sigma_i(\sqrt 3)=\pm\sqrt 3$ for each choice of signs. The minimal polynomial $f(X)\in\mathbb{Q}[X]$ of $\alpha=\sqrt 2+\sqrt 3$ must have $f(\sigma_i(\alpha)) = \sigma_i(f(\alpha)) = 0$, so $f(X)$ must have at least four distinct roots $\sigma_i(\alpha)=\pm\sqrt 2 \pm\sqrt 3$. Thus $f(X)$ has degree at least four, and $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]\geq 4$, but this is the degree of the entire field, $[E:\mathbb{Q}]=4$, so $E = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha )$.

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem statement"}

## Theorem statement {#Theorem_statement}

The primitive element theorem states:

Every [separable](wikipedia://en/363340) field extension of finite degree is simple.

This theorem applies to [algebraic number fields](wikipedia://en/28730822), i.e. finite extensions of the rational numbers **Q**, since **Q** has [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) 0 and therefore every finite extension over **Q** is separable.

Using the [fundamental theorem of Galois theory](wikipedia://en/1947635), the former theorem immediately follows from [Steinitz's theorem](wikipedia://en/75484173).

:::

## Characteristic *p* {#Characteristic_p}

For a non-separable extension $E/F$ of [characteristic p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_p), there is nevertheless a primitive element provided the degree \[*E* : *F*\] is *p:* indeed, there can be no non-trivial intermediate subfields since their degrees would be factors of the prime *p*.

When \[*E* : *F*\] = *p*<sup>2</sup>, there may not be a primitive element (in which case there are infinitely many intermediate fields by [Steinitz's theorem](wikipedia://en/75484173)). The simplest example is $E=\mathbb{F}_p(T,U)$, the field of rational functions in two indeterminates *T* and *U* over the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) with *p* elements, and $F=\mathbb{F}_p(T^p,U^p)$. In fact, for any $\alpha=g(T,U)$ in $E \setminus F$, the [Frobenius endomorphism](wikipedia://en/1088270) shows that the element $\alpha^p$ lies in *F* , so *α* is a root of $f(X)=X^p-\alpha^p\in F[X]$, and *α* cannot be a primitive element (of degree *p*<sup>2</sup> over *F*), but instead *F*(*α*) is a non-trivial intermediate field.

::: {#o11 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

Suppose first that $F$ is infinite. By induction, it suffices to prove that any finite extension $E=F(\beta,\gamma)$ is simple. For $c\in F$, suppose $\alpha = \beta+ c\gamma$ fails to be a primitive element, $F(\alpha)\subsetneq F(\beta,\gamma)$. Then $\gamma\notin F(\alpha)$, since otherwise $\beta = \alpha-c\gamma\in F(\alpha)=F(\beta,\gamma)$. Consider the minimal polynomials of $\beta,\gamma$ over $F(\alpha)$, respectively $f(X), g(X) \in F(\alpha)[X]$, and take a splitting field $L$ containing all roots $\beta,\beta',\ldots$ of $f(X)$ and $\gamma,\gamma',\ldots$ of $g(X)$. Since $\gamma\notin F(\alpha)$, there is another root $\gamma'\neq \gamma$, and a field automorphism $\sigma:L\to L$ which fixes $F(\alpha)$ and takes $\sigma(\gamma)=\gamma'$. We then have $\sigma(\alpha) =\alpha$, and:

$\beta + c \gamma = \sigma(\beta + c \gamma) = \sigma(\beta) + c \, \sigma(\gamma)$, and therefore $c = \frac{\sigma(\beta) - \beta}{\gamma - \sigma(\gamma)}$.

Since there are only finitely many possibilities for $\sigma(\beta)=\beta'$ and $\sigma(\gamma)=\gamma'$, only finitely many $c\in F$ fail to give a primitive element $\alpha=\beta+c\gamma$. All other values give $F(\alpha)=F(\beta,\gamma)$.

For the case where $F$ is finite, we simply take $\alpha$ to be a [primitive root](wikipedia://en/186864) of the finite extension field $E$.

:::

## History {#History}

In his First Memoir of 1831, published in 1846,[^o13] [Évariste Galois](wikipedia://en/9815) sketched a proof of the classical primitive element theorem in the case of a [splitting field](wikipedia://en/174440) of a polynomial over the rational numbers. The gaps in his sketch could easily be filled[^o14] (as remarked by the referee [Poisson](wikipedia://en/44670)) by exploiting a theorem[^o15][^o16] of [Lagrange](wikipedia://en/87793) from 1771, which Galois certainly knew. It is likely that Lagrange had already been aware of the primitive element theorem for splitting fields.[^o16] Galois then used this theorem heavily in his development of the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673). Since then it has been used in the development of [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316) and the [fundamental theorem of Galois theory](wikipedia://en/1947635).

The primitive element theorem was proved in its modern form by [Ernst Steinitz](wikipedia://en/3966685), in an influential article on [field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_theory_%28mathematics%29) in 1910, which also contains [Steinitz's theorem](wikipedia://en/75484173);[^o17] Steinitz called the "classical" result *Theorem of the primitive elements* and his modern version *Theorem of the intermediate fields*.

[Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351) reformulated Galois theory in the 1930s without relying on primitive elements.[^o18][^o19]
