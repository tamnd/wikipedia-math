# −1

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="−1"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **−1** (**negative one** or **minus one**) is the [additive inverse](wikipedia://en/228312) of [1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_%28number%29), that is, the number that when [added](wikipedia://en/61338) to 1 gives the [additive identity](wikipedia://en/4178225) element, [0](wikipedia://en/34513). It is the [negative](wikipedia://en/154616) [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) greater than [negative two (−2)](wikipedia://en/80815039) and less than 0.

:::

## In mathematics {#In_mathematics}

### Algebraic properties {#Algebraic_properties}

Multiplying a number by −1 is equivalent to changing the [sign](wikipedia://en/7951270) of the number – that is, for any $x$ we have $(-1) \cdot x = -x$. This can be [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) using the [distributive law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributive_law) and the axiom that 1 is the [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity):

$$x + (-1) \cdot x = 1 \cdot x + (-1) \cdot x = (1 + (-1)) \cdot x = 0 \cdot x = 0.$$

Here we have used the fact that any number $x$ [times 0 equals 0](wikipedia://en/4062502), which follows by [cancellation](wikipedia://en/490646) from the equation

$$0 \cdot x = (0 + 0) \cdot x = 0 \cdot x + 0 \cdot x.$$

In other words,

$$x + (-1) \cdot x = 0,$$

so $(-1) \cdot x$ is the additive inverse of $x$, i.e. $(-1) \cdot x = -x$, as was to be shown.

The [square](wikipedia://en/659942) of −1 (that is −1 multiplied by −1) equals 1. As a consequence, a product of two negative numbers is positive. For an algebraic proof of this result, start with the equation

$$0 = -1 \cdot 0 = -1 \cdot [1 + (-1)].$$

The first equality follows from the above result, and the second follows from the definition of −1 as additive inverse of 1: it is precisely that number which when added to 1 gives 0. Now, using the distributive law, it can be seen that

$$0 = -1 \cdot [1 + (-1)] = -1 \cdot 1 + (-1) \cdot (-1) = -1 + (-1) \cdot (-1).$$

The third equality follows from the fact that 1 is a multiplicative identity. But now adding 1 to both sides of this last equation implies

$$(-1) \cdot (-1) = 1.$$

The above arguments hold in any [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), a concept of [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) generalizing integers and [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438).[^o6]

*Image: ImaginaryUnit5.svg.* 0, 1, −1, $i$, and −$i$ in the [complex](wikipedia://en/217628) or [Cartesian plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_plane)

Although there are no [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) square roots of −1, the [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $i$ satisfies $i^{2} = -1$, and as such can be considered as a [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of −1.[^o7] The only other complex number whose square is −1 is −$i$ because there are exactly two square roots of any non‐zero complex number, which follows from the [fundamental theorem of algebra](wikipedia://en/51414). In the algebra of [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) – where the fundamental theorem does not apply – which contains the complex numbers, the equation $x^{2} = -1$ has [infinitely many solutions](wikipedia://en/51440#Square_roots_of_−1).[^o8][^o9]

### Inverse and invertible elements {#Inverse_and_invertible_elements}

*Image: Geogebra f(x)=1÷x 20211118.svg.* The reciprocal function $f(x) = x^{-1}$ where for every $x$ except 0, $f(x)$ represents its [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940)

[Exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491) of a non‐zero real number can be [extended to negative integers](wikipedia://en/99491#Negative_exponents), where raising a number to the power −1 has the same effect as taking its [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940):

$$x^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{x}.$$

This definition is then applied to negative integers, preserving the exponential law $x^{a}x^{b} = x^{(a + b)}$ for real numbers $a$ and $b$.

A −1 [superscript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superscript) in $f^{-1}(x)$ takes the [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907) of $f(x)$, where $(f(x))^{-1}$ specifically denotes a [pointwise](wikipedia://en/4786797) reciprocal. Where $f$ is [bijective](wikipedia://en/3942) specifying an output [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) of every $y \in Y$ from every input [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) $x \in X$, there will be

$f^{-1}(f(x)) = x,$ and $f^{-1}(f(y)) = y$.

When a subset of the codomain is specified inside the function $f$, its inverse will yield an [inverse image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_image), or preimage, of that subset under the function.

Exponentiation to negative integers can be further extended to [invertible elements](wikipedia://en/45199) of a ring by defining $x^{-1}$ as the multiplicative inverse of $x$; in this context, these elements are considered [units](wikipedia://en/289666).[^o6]

In a [polynomial domain](wikipedia://en/23000#Polynomial_ring) $F[x]$ over any [field](wikipedia://en/10603#Constructing_fields) $F$, the polynomial $x$ has no inverse. If it did have an inverse $q(x)$, then there would be[^o11]

$$x q(x) = 1 \Rightarrow deg(x) + deg(q(x)) = deg(1)$$

$$\ \,\Rightarrow 1 + deg(q(x)) = 0$$

$$\ \,\Rightarrow deg(q(x)) = -1$$

which is not possible, and therefore, $F[x]$ is not a field. More specifically, because the polynomial is not [constant](wikipedia://en/373065#Terminology), it is not a unit in $F$.
