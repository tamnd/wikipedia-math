# Derivative of the exponential map

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Derivative of the exponential map"}

*Image: poincare.jpg.* In 1899, [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740)'s investigations into group multiplication in Lie algebraic terms led him to the formulation of the [universal enveloping algebra](wikipedia://en/363551).[^o2]

In the theory of [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945), the [exponential map](wikipedia://en/44612290) is a map from the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) $\mathbf{g}$ of a Lie group $G$ into $G$. In case $G$ is a [matrix Lie group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_Lie_group), the exponential map reduces to the [matrix exponential](wikipedia://en/672731). The exponential map, denoted $\exp:\mathbf{g} \to G$, is [analytic](wikipedia://en/61478) and has as such a [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) $\tfrac{d}{dt}\exp(X(t)):T\mathbf{g} \to TG$, where $X(t)$ is a $C^{1}$ [path](wikipedia://en/1189553) in the Lie algebra, and a closely related [differential](wikipedia://en/23997203) $d\exp:T\mathbf{g} \to TG$.[^o3]

:::

The formula for $d\exp$ was first proved by [Friedrich Schur](wikipedia://en/35183056) (1891).[^o4] It was later elaborated by [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) (1899) in the context of the problem of expressing Lie group multiplication using Lie algebraic terms.[^o5] It is also sometimes known as **Duhamel's formula**.

The formula is important both in pure and applied mathematics. It enters into proofs of theorems such as the [Baker&ndash;Campbell&ndash;Hausdorff formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baker&ndash;Campbell&ndash;Hausdorff_formula), and it is used frequently in physics[^o6] for example in [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), as in the [Magnus expansion](wikipedia://en/22061787) in [perturbation theory](wikipedia://en/102140), and in [lattice gauge theory](wikipedia://en/671804).

Throughout, the notations $\exp(X)$ and $e^{X}$ will be used interchangeably to denote the exponential given an argument, *except* when, where as noted, the notations have dedicated *distinct* meanings. The calculus-style notation is preferred here for better readability in equations. On the other hand, the $\exp$-style is sometimes more convenient for inline equations, and is necessary on the rare occasions when there is a real distinction to be made.

## Statement {#Statement}

The derivative of the exponential map is given by[^o9]

**Explanation**

::: {#o11 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$X = X(t)$ is a $C^{1}$ (continuously differentiable) path in the Lie algebra with derivative $X'(t) = \tfrac{dX(t)}{dt}$. The argument $t$ is omitted where not needed.; $\operatorname{ad}_{X}$ is the linear transformation of the Lie algebra given by $\operatorname{ad}_{X}(Y) = [X, Y]$. It is the [adjoint action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_representation_of_a_Lie_algebra) of a Lie algebra on itself.; The fraction $\tfrac{1 - \exp(-\operatorname{ad}_{X})}{\operatorname{ad}_{X}}$ is given by the power series $\frac{1 - e^{-\mathrm{ad}_{X}}}{\mathrm{ad}_{X}} = \sum_{k = 0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k}{(k + 1)!}(\mathrm{ad}_X)^k. \tag{2}$ derived from the power series of the exponential map of a linear endomorphism, as in matrix exponentiation.[^o9]; When $G$ is a matrix Lie group, all occurrences of the exponential are given by their power series expansion.; When $G$ is *not* a matrix Lie group, $\tfrac{1 - \exp(-\operatorname{ad}_{X})}{\operatorname{ad}_{X}}$ is still given by its power series ((2)), while the other two occurrences of $\exp$ in the formula, which now are the [exponential map in Lie theory](wikipedia://en/44612290), refer to the time-one [flow](wikipedia://en/2619735) of the [left invariant](wikipedia://en/17945#Basic_concepts) [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) $X$, i.e. element of the Lie algebra as defined in the general case, on the Lie group $G$ viewed as an [analytic manifold](wikipedia://en/3183798). This still amounts to exactly the same formula as in the matrix case. Left multiplication of an element of the algebra $\mathbf{g}$ by an element $\exp(X(t))$ of the Lie group is interpreted as applying the differential of the left translation $\operatorname{dL}_{\exp(X(t))}$.; The formula applies to the case where $\exp$ is considered as a map on matrix space over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbf{C}$, see [matrix exponential](wikipedia://en/672731). When $G = \operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{C})$ or $\operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$, the notions coincide precisely.

:::

To compute the **differential** $d\exp$ of $\exp$ at $X$, $d\exp_{X}: T\mathbf{g}_{X} \to TG_{\exp(X)}$, the standard recipe[^o3]

$$d\exp_XY = \left .\frac{d}{dt}e^{Z(t)}\right|_{t = 0}, Z(0) = X, Z'(0) = Y$$

is employed. With $Z(t) = X + tY$ the result[^o9]

::: {#o12 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$d\exp_XY = e^{X}\frac{1 - e^{-\mathrm{ad}_{X}}}{\mathrm{ad}_{X}}Y \tag{3}$$

:::

follows immediately from ((1)). In particular, $d\exp_{0}:T\mathbf{g}_{0} \to TG_{\exp(0)} = TG_{e}$ is the identity because $T\mathbf{g}_{X} \simeq \mathbf{g}$ (since $\mathbf{g}$ is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370)) and $TG_{e} \simeq \mathbf{g}$.

:::

## Proof {#Proof}

The proof given below assumes a matrix Lie group. This means that the exponential mapping from the Lie algebra to the matrix Lie group is given by the usual power series, i.e. matrix exponentiation. The conclusion of the proof still holds in the general case, provided each occurrence of $\exp$ is correctly interpreted. See comments on the general case below.

The outline of proof makes use of the technique of differentiation with respect to $s$ of the parametrized expression

$$\Gamma(s, t) = e^{-sX(t)}\frac{\partial}{\partial t} e^{sX(t)}$$

to obtain a first order [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309) for $\Gamma$ which can then be solved by direct integration in $s$. The solution is then $e^{X} \Gamma(1, t)$.

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=inline-head name="Lemma"}

**Lemma**

Let $\operatorname{Ad}$ denote the [adjoint action](wikipedia://en/302188) of the group on its Lie algebra. The action is given by $\operatorname{Ad}_{A}X = AXA^{-1}$ for $A \in G, X \in \mathbf{g}$. A frequently useful relationship between $\operatorname{Ad}$ and $\operatorname{ad}$ is given by[^o16][^o17]

:::

::: {#o19 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof**

Using the [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988) twice one finds,

$$\frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial s} = e^{-sX}(-X)\frac{\partial}{\partial t}e^{sX(t)} + e^{-sX}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[X(t)e^{sX(t)}\right] = e^{-sX}\frac{dX}{dt}e^{sX}.$$

Then one observes that

$$\frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial s} = \mathrm{Ad}_{e^{-sX}}X' = e^{-\mathrm{ad}_{sX}}X',$$

by ((4)) above. Integration yields

$$\Gamma(1, t) = e^{-X(t)}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}e^{X(t)} = \int_0^1 \frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial s}ds = \int_0^1 e^{-\mathrm{ad}_{sX}}X'ds.$$

Using the [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012) to expand the exponential, integrating term by term, and finally recognizing ((2)),

$$\Gamma(1, t) = \int_0^1 \sum_{k = 0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^ks^k}{k!} (\mathrm{ad}_X)^k\frac{dX}{dt}ds = \sum_{k = 0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k}{(k + 1)!}(\mathrm{ad}_X)^k \frac{dX}{dt} = \frac{1-e^{-\mathrm{ad}_X}}{\mathrm{ad}_X}\frac{dX}{dt},$$

and the result follows. The proof, as presented here, is essentially the one given in (Rossmann 2002). A proof with a more algebraic touch can be found in (Hall 2015).[^o22]

:::

### Comments on the general case {#Comments_on_the_general_case}

The formula in the general case is given by[^o24]

$$\frac{d}{dt}\exp(C(t)) = \exp(C)\phi(-\mathrm{ad}(C))C~ ',$$

where[^o25]

$$\phi(z) = \frac{e^z - 1}{z} = 1 + \frac{1}{2!}z + \frac{1}{3!}z^2 + \cdots,$$

which formally reduces to

$$\frac{d}{dt}\exp(C(t)) = \exp(C)\frac{1 - e^{-\mathrm{ad}_{C}}}{\mathrm{ad}_{C}}\frac{dC(t)}{dt}.$$

Here the $\exp$-notation is used for the exponential mapping of the Lie algebra and the calculus-style notation in the fraction indicates the usual formal series expansion. For more information and two full proofs in the general case, see the freely available (Sternberg 2004) reference.

### A direct formal argument {#A_direct_formal_argument}

An immediate way to see what the answer *must* be, provided it exists is the following. Existence needs to be proved separately in each case. By direct differentiation of the standard limit definition of the exponential, and exchanging the order of differentiation and limit,

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}e^{X(t)} &= \lim_{N \to \infty}\frac{d}{dt}\left(1 + \frac{X(t)}{N}\right)^N\\ &= \lim_{N \to \infty}\sum_{k=1}^N\left(1 + \frac{X(t)}{N}\right)^{N-k}\frac{1}{N}\frac{dX(t)}{dt}\left(1 + \frac{X(t)}{N}\right)^{k-1}~, \end{aligned}$$

where each factor owes its place to the non-commutativity of $X(t)$ and $X\text{´}(t)$.

Dividing the unit interval into $N$ sections $\Delta s = \tfrac{\Delta k}{N}$ ($\Delta k = 1$ since the sum indices are integers) and letting $N$ → ∞, $\Delta k \to dk, \tfrac{k}{N} \to s$, $\Sigma \to \int$ yields

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}e^{X(t)} &= \int_{0}^1e^{(1-s)X}X'e^{sX}ds = e^X \int_{0}^1 \mathrm{Ad}_{e^{-sX}} X' ds \\ &= e^X \int_{0}^1 e^{-\mathrm{ad}_{sX}} dsX' = e^X \frac{1-e^{-\mathrm{ad}_X}}{\mathrm{ad}_X}\frac{dX}{dt}~. \end{aligned}$$

## Applications {#Applications}

### Local behavior of the exponential map {#Local_behavior_of_the_exponential_map}

The [inverse function theorem](wikipedia://en/287229) together with the derivative of the exponential map provides information about the local behavior of $\exp$. Any $C^{k}, 0 \le k \le \infty, \omega$ map $f$ between vector spaces (here first considering matrix Lie groups) has a $C^{k}$ inverse such that $f$ is a $C^{k}$ bijection in an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) around a point $x$ in the domain provided $df_{x}$ is invertible. From ((3)) it follows that this will happen precisely when

$$\frac{1 - e^{\mathrm{ad_X}}}{\mathrm{ad}_X}$$

is invertible. This, in turn, happens when the eigenvalues of this operator are all nonzero. The eigenvalues of $\tfrac{1 - \exp(-\operatorname{ad}_{X})}{\operatorname{ad}_{X}}$ are related to those of $\operatorname{ad}_{X}$ as follows. If $g$ is an analytic function of a complex variable expressed in a power series such that $g(U)$ for a matrix $U$ converges, then the eigenvalues of $g(U)$ will be $g(\lambda_{ij})$, where $\lambda_{ij}$ are the eigenvalues of $U$, the double subscript is made clear below.[^o31] In the present case with $g(U) = \tfrac{1 - \exp(-U)}{U}$ and $U = \operatorname{ad}_{X}$, the eigenvalues of $\tfrac{1 - \exp(-\operatorname{ad}_{X})}{\operatorname{ad}_{X}}$ are

$$\frac{1 - e^{-\lambda_{ij}}}{\lambda_{ij}},$$

where the $\lambda_{ij}$ are the eigenvalues of $\operatorname{ad}_{X}$. Putting $\tfrac{1 - \exp(-\lambda_{ij})}{\lambda_{ij}} = 0$ one sees that $d\exp$ is invertible precisely when

$$\lambda_{ij} \ne k2\pi i, k = \pm1, \pm2, \ldots.$$

The eigenvalues of $\operatorname{ad}_{X}$ are, in turn, related to those of $X$. Let the eigenvalues of $X$ be $\lambda_{i}$. Fix an ordered basis $e_{i}$ of the underlying vector space $V$ such that $X$ is lower triangular. Then

$$Xe_i = \lambda_ie_i + \cdots,$$

with the remaining terms multiples of $e_{n}$ with $n > i$. Let $E_{ij}$ be the corresponding basis for matrix space, i.e. $(E_{ij})_{kl} = \delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}$. Order this basis such that $E_{ij} < E_{nm}$ if $i - j < n - m$. One checks that the action of $\operatorname{ad}_{X}$ is given by

$$\mathrm{ad}_XE_{ij} = (\lambda_i - \lambda_j)E_{ij} + \cdots \equiv \lambda_{ij}E_{ij} + \cdots,$$

with the remaining terms multiples of $E_{mn} > E_{ij}$. This means that $\operatorname{ad}_{X}$ is lower triangular with its eigenvalues $\lambda_{ij} = \lambda_{i} - \lambda_{j}$ on the diagonal. The conclusion is that $d\exp_{X}$ is invertible, hence $\exp$ is a local bianalytical bijection around $X$, when the eigenvalues of $X$ satisfy[^o32][^o33]

$$\lambda_i - \lambda_j \ne k2\pi i, \quad k = \pm1, \pm2, \ldots, \quad 1 \le i, j \le n = \dim V.$$

In particular, in the case of matrix Lie groups, it follows, since $d\exp_{0}$ is invertible, by the [inverse function theorem](wikipedia://en/287229) that $\exp$ is a bi-analytic bijection in a neighborhood of $0 \in \mathbf{g}$ in matrix space. Furthermore, $\exp$, is a bi-analytic bijection from a neighborhood of $0 \in \mathbf{g}$ in $\mathbf{g}$ to a neighborhood of $e \in G$.[^o34] The same conclusion holds for general Lie groups using the manifold version of the inverse function theorem.

It also follows from the [implicit function theorem](wikipedia://en/581005) that $d\exp_{\xi}$ itself is invertible for $\xi$ sufficiently small.[^o35]

### Derivation of a Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula {#Derivation_of_a_Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff_formula}

If $Z(t)$ is defined such that

$$e^{Z(t)} = e^{X} e^{tY},$$

an expression for $Z(1) = \log( \exp X \exp Y )$, the [Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula](wikipedia://en/285422), can be derived from the above formula,

$$\exp(-Z(t))\frac{d}{dt}\exp(Z(t)) = \frac{1 - e^{-\mathrm{ad}_{Z}}}{\mathrm{ad}_{Z}}Z'(t).$$

Its left-hand side is easy to see to equal *Y*. Thus,

$$Y = \frac{1 - e^{-\mathrm{ad}_{Z}}}{\mathrm{ad}_{Z}}Z'(t),$$

and hence, formally,[^o38][^o39]

$$Z'(t) = \frac{\mathrm{ad}_{Z}}{1 - e^{-\mathrm{ad}_{Z}}} Y \equiv \psi\left(e^{\mathrm{ad}_{Z}}\right)Y, \quad \psi(w) = \frac{w\log w}{w - 1} = 1 + \sum_{m=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{m + 1}}{m(m + 1)}(w - 1)^m, \|w\| < 1.$$

However, using the relationship between $\operatorname{Ad}$ and $\operatorname{ad}$ given by ((4)), it is straightforward to further see that

$$e^{\mathrm{ad}_{Z}} = e^{\mathrm{ad}_{X}} e^{t\mathrm{ad}_{Y}}$$

and hence

$$Z'(t) = \psi\left(e^{\mathrm{ad}_{X}} e^{t\mathrm{ad}_{Y}}\right)Y.$$

Putting this into the form of an integral in *t* from 0 to 1 yields,

$$Z(1) = \log(\exp X\exp Y) = X + \left( \int^1_0 \psi \left(e^{\operatorname{ad}_X} ~ e^{t \,\text{ad}_Y}\right) \, dt \right) \, Y,$$

an [integral formula](wikipedia://en/285422#Selected_tractable_cases) for $Z(1)$ that is more tractable in practice than the explicit [Dynkin's series formula](wikipedia://en/285422#An_explicit_Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff_formula) due to the simplicity of the series expansion of $\psi$. Note this expression consists of $X+Y$ and nested commutators thereof with $X$ or $Y$. A textbook proof along these lines can be found in (Hall 2015) and (Miller 1972).

:::

### Derivation of Dynkin's series formula {#Derivation_of_Dynkin's_series_formula}

*Image: Eugene Dynkin 2003.jpg.* [Eugene Dynkin](wikipedia://en/1351540) at home in 2003. In 1947 Dynkin proved the explicit BCH series formula.[^o44] [Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740), [Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Frederick_Baker), [Campbell](wikipedia://en/8963216) and [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/10989) were mostly concerned with the *existence* of a bracket series, which suffices in many applications, for instance, in proving central results in the [Lie correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_correspondence).[^o45][^o46]

[Dynkin's formula](wikipedia://en/12130843) mentioned may also be derived analogously, starting from the parametric extension

$$e^{Z(t)} = e^{tX} e^{tY},$$

whence

$$e^{-Z(t)} \frac{de^{Z(t)}}{dt} = e^{-t \, \mathrm{ad}_{Y}}X + Y ~,$$

so that, using the above general formula,

$$Z' = \frac{\mathrm{ad}_{Z}}{1 - e^{-\mathrm{ad}_{Z}}} ~ \left(e^{-t \, \mathrm{ad}_{Y}}X + Y\right) = \frac{\mathrm{ad}_{Z}}{e^{\mathrm{ad}_{Z}} - 1} ~ \left(X + e^{t \, \mathrm{ad}_{X}}Y\right) .$$

Since, however,

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{ad_Z} &= \log\left(\exp\left(\mathrm{ad}_Z\right)\right) = \log\left(1 + \left(\exp\left(\mathrm{ad}_Z\right) - 1\right)\right) \\ &= \sum\limits^{\infty}_{n=1} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} (\exp(\mathrm{ad}_Z) - 1)^n ~, \quad \|\mathrm{ad}_Z\| < \log 2 ~~, \end{aligned}$$

the last step by virtue of the [Mercator series](wikipedia://en/3995885) expansion, it follows that

::: {#o47 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$$Z' = \sum\limits^{\infty}_{n=1} \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n} \left(e^{\mathrm{ad}_Z} - 1\right)^{n-1} ~ \left(X + e^{t \, \mathrm{ad}_{X}}Y\right)~, \tag{5}$$

:::

and, thus, integrating,

$$Z(1) = \int^1 _0 dt ~\frac{dZ(t)}{dt} = \sum^{\infty}_{n=1} \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n} \int^1 _0 dt ~\left(e^{t \, \mathrm{ad}_{X}} e^{t\mathrm{ad}_{Y}} - 1\right)^{n-1} ~ \left(X + e^{t \, \mathrm{ad}_{X}}Y\right) .$$

It is at this point evident that the qualitative statement of the BCH formula holds, namely $Z$ lies in the Lie algebra generated by $X, Y$ and is expressible as a series in repeated brackets ((A)). is expressible as a bracket series, then necessarily[^o56]

::: {#o57 .equation cue=numblk name="B"}

$$X^{i_1}Y^{j_1} \cdots X^{i_k}Y^{j_k} = \frac{\left[X^{(i_1)}Y^{(j_1)}\cdots X^{(i_k)}Y^{(j_k)}\right]}{i_1 + j_1 + \cdots + i_k + j_k}. \tag{B}$$

:::

Putting observation ((A)) and theorem ((B)) together yields a concise proof of the explicit BCH formula.

:::
