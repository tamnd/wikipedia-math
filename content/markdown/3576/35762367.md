# Petr–Douglas–Neumann theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Petr–Douglas–Neumann theorem"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **Petr–Douglas–Neumann theorem** (or the **PDN-theorem**) is a result concerning arbitrary [planar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621). The theorem asserts that a certain [procedure](wikipedia://en/775) when applied to an arbitrary polygon always yields a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) having the same number of sides as the initial polygon. The theorem was first published by [Karel Petr](wikipedia://en/4436275) (1868–1950) of [Prague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prague) in 1905 (in Czech)[^o2] and in 1908 (in German).[^o3][^o23] It was independently rediscovered by [Jesse Douglas](wikipedia://en/387571) (1897–1965) in 1940[^o4] and also by [B H Neumann](wikipedia://en/5032588) (1909–2002) in 1941.[^o23][^o5] The naming of the theorem as *Petr–Douglas–Neumann theorem*, or as the *PDN-theorem* for short, is due to Stephen B Gray.[^o23] It has also been called **Douglas's theorem**, the **Douglas–Neumann theorem**, the **Napoleon–Douglas–Neumann theorem** and **Petr's theorem**.[^o23]

:::

The PDN-theorem is a generalisation of [Napoleon's theorem](wikipedia://en/2728914), which corresponds to the case of [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654), and [van Aubel's theorem](wikipedia://en/3021425) which corresponds to the case of [quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/25278).

::: {#o7 .statement cue=heading name="Statement of the theorem"}

## Statement of the theorem {#Statement_of_the_theorem}

The Petr–Douglas–Neumann theorem asserts the following.[^o4][^o8]

*If isosceles triangles with apex angles 2kπ/n, for an integer k with 1 ≤ k ≤ n − 2 are erected on the sides of an arbitrary n-gon A<sub>0</sub>, whose apices are the vertices of a new n-gon A<sub>1</sub>, and if this process is repeated n-2 times, but with a different value of k for the n-gon formed from the free apices of these triangles at each step, until all values 1 ≤ k ≤ n − 2 have been used (in arbitrary order), to form a sequence A<sub>1</sub>, A<sub>2</sub>, ... A<sub>n-2</sub>, of n-gons, their centroids all coincide with the centroid of A<sub>0</sub>, and the last one, A<sub>n−2</sub> is a regular n-gon*.

:::

## Specialisation to triangles {#Specialisation_to_triangles}

*Image: PDNTheoremForTriangle.svg.* Diagram illustrating the fact that Napoleon's theorem is a special case of Petr–Douglas–Neumann theorem.

In the case of triangles, the value of *n* is 3 and that of *n* − 2 is 1. Hence there is only one possible value for *k*, namely 1. The specialisation of the theorem to triangles asserts that the triangle A<sub>1</sub> is a regular 3-gon, that is, an equilateral triangle.

A<sub>1</sub> is formed by the apices of the isosceles triangles with apex angle 2π/3 erected over the sides of the triangle A<sub>0</sub>. The vertices of A<sub>1</sub> are the centers of equilateral triangles erected over the sides of triangle A<sub>0</sub>. Thus the specialisation of the PDN theorem to a triangle can be formulated as follows:

*If equilateral triangles are erected over the sides of any triangle, then the triangle formed by the centers of the three equilateral triangles is equilateral.*

The last statement is the assertion of the [Napoleon's theorem](wikipedia://en/2728914).

## Specialisation to quadrilaterals {#Specialisation_to_quadrilaterals}

In the case of [quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/25278), the value of *n* is 4 and that of *n* − 2 is 2. There are two possible values for *k*, namely 1 and 2, and so two possible apex angles, namely:

(2×1×π)/4 = π/2 = 90° ( corresponding to *k* = 1 )

(2×2×π)/4 = π = 180° ( corresponding to *k* = 2 ).

According to the PDN-theorem the quadrilateral A<sub>2</sub> is a regular 4-gon, that is, a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29). The two-stage process yielding the square A<sub>2</sub> can be carried out in two different ways. (The apex *Z* of an [isosceles triangle](wikipedia://en/315428) with apex angle π erected over a line segment *XY* is the [midpoint](wikipedia://en/437979) of the line segment *XY*.)

### Construct A<sub>1</sub> using apex angle π/2 and then A<sub>2</sub> with apex angle π. {#Construct_A1_using_apex_angle_π/2_and_then_A2_with_apex_angle_π.}

In this case the [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) of A<sub>1</sub> are the free apices of [isosceles triangles](wikipedia://en/315428) with apex angles π/2 erected over the sides of the quadrilateral A<sub>0</sub>. The vertices of the quadrilateral A<sub>2</sub> are the [midpoints](wikipedia://en/437979) of the sides of the quadrilateral A<sub>1</sub>. By the PDN theorem, A<sub>2</sub> is a square.

The vertices of the quadrilateral A<sub>1</sub> are the centers of squares erected over the sides of the quadrilateral A<sub>0</sub>. The assertion that quadrilateral A<sub>2</sub> is a square is equivalent to the assertion that the [diagonals](wikipedia://en/349251) of A<sub>1</sub> are equal and [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to each other. The latter assertion is the content of [van Aubel's theorem](wikipedia://en/3021425).

Thus [van Aubel's theorem](wikipedia://en/3021425) is a special case of the PDN-theorem.

### Construct A<sub>1</sub> using apex angle π and then A<sub>2</sub> with apex angle π/2. {#Construct_A1_using_apex_angle_π_and_then_A2_with_apex_angle_π/2.}

In this case the vertices of A<sub>1</sub> are the [midpoints](wikipedia://en/437979) of the sides of the quadrilateral A<sub>0</sub> and those of A<sub>2</sub> are the apices of the triangles with apex angles π/2 erected over the sides of A<sub>1</sub>. The PDN-theorem asserts that A<sub>2</sub> is a square in this case also.

## Specialisation to pentagons {#Specialisation_to_pentagons}

*Image: PDN Theorem For Pentagon.svg.* Diagram illustrating Petr–Douglas–Neumann theorem as applied to a pentagon. The pentagon *A*<sub>0</sub> is *ABCDE*. *A*<sub>1</sub> ( = *FGHIJ* ) is constructed with apex angle 72°, *A*<sub>2</sub> ( = *KLMNO* ) with apex angle 144° and *A*<sub>3</sub> ( = *PQRST* ) with apex angle 216°.

In the case of [pentagons](wikipedia://en/20741014), we have *n* = 5 and *n* − 2 = 3. So there are three possible values for *k*, namely 1, 2 and 3, and hence three possible apex angles for isosceles triangles:

(2×1×π)/5 = 2π/5 = 72°

(2×2×π)/5 = 4π/5 = 144°

(2×3×π)/5 = 6π/5 = 216°

According to the PDN-theorem, A<sub>3</sub> is a [regular pentagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_pentagon). The three-stage process leading to the construction of the regular pentagon A<sub>3</sub> can be performed in six different ways depending on the order in which the apex angles are selected for the construction of the isosceles triangles.

## Proof of the theorem {#Proof_of_the_theorem}

The theorem can be proved using some elementary concepts from [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422).[^o23][^o24]

The proof begins by encoding an *n*-gon by a list of complex numbers representing the vertices of the *n*-gon. This list can be thought of as a vector in the *n*-dimensional complex linear space C<sup>n</sup>. Take an *n*-gon *A* and let it be represented by the complex vector

*A* = ( *a*<sub>1</sub>, *a*<sub>2</sub>, ... , *a*<sub>*n*</sub> ).

Let the polygon *B* be formed by the free vertices of similar triangles built on the sides of *A* and let it be represented by the complex vector

*B* = ( *b*<sub>1</sub>, *b*<sub>2</sub>, ... , *b*<sub>*n*</sub> ).

Then we have

α( *a*<sub>*r*</sub> − *b*<sub>*r*</sub> ) = *a*<sub>*r*+1</sub> − *b*<sub>*r*</sub>, where α = exp( *i* θ ) for some θ (here *i* is the square root of −1).

This yields the following expression to compute the *b*<sub>r</sub> ' s:

*b*<sub>*r*</sub> = (1−α)<sup>−1</sup> ( *a*<sub>*r*+1</sub> − α*a*<sub>*r*</sub> ).

In terms of the linear operator *S* : C<sup>n</sup> → C<sup>n</sup> that cyclically permutes the coordinates one place, we have

*B* = (1−α)<sup>−1</sup>( *S* − α*I* )*A*, where *I* is the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718).

This means that the polygon *A*<sub>j+1</sub> obtained at the *j*the step is related to the preceding one *A*<sub>j</sub> by

*A*<sub>j+1</sub> *\= ( 1 − ω<sup>σ<sub>j</sub></sup> )<sup>−1</sup>(*S*− ω<sup>σ<sub>j</sub></sup>*I*)*A*<sub>j</sub>*,

where ω = exp( 2π*i*/*n* ) is the *n*th primitive [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950) and *σ<sub>j</sub>* is the *j*th term of a permutation *σ* of the integer sequence *(1,..., n-2)*.

The last polygon in the sequence, *A*<sub>*n*−2</sub>, which we need to show is regular, is thus obtained from *A*<sub>0</sub> by applying the composition of all the following operators:

( 1 − ω<sup>*j*</sup> )<sup>−1</sup>( *S* − ω<sup>j</sup> *I* ) for *j* = 1, 2, ... , *n* − 2 .

(These factors commute, since they are all polynomials in the same operator *S*, so the ordering of the product does not depend on the choice of the permutation *σ*.)

A polygon *P* = ( *p*<sub>1</sub>, *p*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *p*<sub>*n*</sub> ) is a regular *n*-gon if each side of *P* is obtained from the next by rotating through an angle of 2π/*n*, that is, if

*p*<sub>*r* + 1</sub> − *p*<sub>*r*</sub> = ω( *p*<sub>*r* + 2</sub> − *p*<sub>*r* + 1</sub> ).

This condition can be formulated in terms of S as follows:

( *S* − *I* )( *I* − ω*S* ) *P* = 0.

Or equivalently as

( *S* − *I* )( *S* − ω<sup>*n* − 1</sup> *I* ) *P* = 0, since ω<sup>*n*</sup> = 1.

The main result of the Petr–Douglas–Neumann theorem now follows from the following computations.

( *S* − *I* )( *S* − ω<sup>*n* − 1</sup> *I* ) *A*<sub>*n* − 2</sub>

\= ( *S* − *I* )( *S* − ω<sup>*n* − 1</sup> *I* ) ( 1 − ω )<sup>−1</sup> ( *S* − ω *I* ) ( 1 − ω<sup>2</sup> )<sup>−1</sup> ( *S* − ω<sup>2</sup> *I* ) ... ( 1 − ω<sup>*n* − 2</sup> )<sup>−1</sup> ( *S* − ω<sup>*n* − 2</sup> *I* ) *A*<sub>0</sub>

\= ( 1 − ω )<sup>−1</sup>( 1 − ω<sup>2</sup> )<sup>−1</sup> ... ( 1 − ω<sup>*n* − 2</sup> )<sup>−1</sup> ( *S* − *I* ) ( *S* − ω *I* ) ( *S* − ω<sup>2</sup> *I* ) ... ( *S* − ω<sup>*n* − 1</sup> *I*)*A*<sub>0</sub>

\= ( 1 − ω )<sup>−1</sup>( 1 − ω<sup>2</sup> )<sup>−1</sup> ... ( 1 − ω<sup>*n* − 2</sup> )<sup>−1</sup> ( *S*<sup>*n*</sup> − *I* ) *A*<sub>0</sub>

\= 0, since *S*<sup>*n*</sup> = *I*.

To show that all the centroids are equal, we note that the centroid *c<sub>A</sub>* of any n-gon is obtained by averagining all the vertices. This means that, viewing *A* as an *n*-component vector, its centroid is given by taking its scalar product

*c<sub>A</sub>*= *(E , A)*

with the vector *E:=(1/n) (1, 1, ..., 1)* . Taking the scalar product of both sides of the equation

*A*<sub>j+1</sub> *\= ( 1 − ω<sup>σ<sub>j</sub></sup> )<sup>−1</sup>(*S*− ω<sup>σ<sub>j</sub></sup>*I*)*A*<sub>j</sub>*,

with *E*, and noting that *E* is invariant under the [cyclic permutation](wikipedia://en/449166) operator *S*, we obtain

*c<sub>A<sub>j+1</sub></sub>*= (E, *A<sub>j+1</sub>*) = ( 1 − ω<sup>σ<sub>j</sub></sup> )<sup>−1</sup> ( 1 − ω<sup>σ<sub>j</sub></sup> )(E, *A<sub>j</sub>*) = (E, *A<sub>j</sub>*) = *c<sub>A<sub>j</sub></sub>*,

so all the centroids are equal.

:::
