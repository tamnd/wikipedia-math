# Erdős–Woods number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Erdős–Woods number"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), a [positive integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_integer) $k$ is said to be an **Erdős–Woods number** if it has the following property: there exists a positive integer $a$ such that in the [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $(a, a + 1, \ldots, a + k)$ of consecutive integers, each of the elements has a non-trivial [common factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_factor) with one of the endpoints. In other words, $k$ is an Erdős–Woods number if there exists a positive integer $a$ such that for each integer $i$ between $0$ and $k$, at least one of the [greatest common divisors](wikipedia://en/12354) $\gcd(a, a + i)$ or $\gcd(a + i, a + k)$ is greater than $1$.

## Prime partitions {#Prime_partitions}

The Erdős–Woods numbers can be characterized in terms of certain partitions of the [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666). A number $k$ is an Erdős–Woods number if and only if the prime numbers less than $k$ can be partitioned into two subsets $X$ and $Y$ with the following property: for every pair of positive integers $x$ and $y$ with $x + y = k$, either $x$ is divisible by a prime in $X$, or $y$ is divisible by a prime in $Y$. For this reason, these numbers are also called *prime-partitionable numbers*.

## History {#History}

In a 1971 paper, [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) and [John Selfridge](wikipedia://en/995084) considered intervals of integers containing an element coprime to both endpoints. They observed that earlier results of [S. S. Pillai](wikipedia://en/681150) and [George Szekeres](wikipedia://en/2563194) implied that such an element exists for every interval of at most 16 integers; thus, no Erdős–Woods number can be less than 16. In his 1981 thesis, Alan R. Woods independently conjectured that whenever $k > 1$, the interval $[a, a + k]$ always includes a number [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) to both endpoints. It was only later that he found the first counterexample, $[2184, 2185, \ldots, 2200]$, with $k = 16$. The existence of this counterexample shows that 16 is an Erdős–Woods number. (Dowe 1989) proved that there are infinitely many Erdős–Woods numbers, and (Cégielski Heroult Richard 2003) showed that the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of Erdős–Woods numbers is [recursive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursive_set).

Meanwhile, the prime-partitionable numbers had been defined by Holsztyński and Strube in 1978, following which Erdős and [William T. Trotter](wikipedia://en/41414002) proved in 1978 that they form an infinite sequence. Erdős and Trotter applied these results to generate pairs of [directed cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_cycle) whose [Cartesian product of graphs](wikipedia://en/2856852) does not contain a [Hamiltonian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle), and they used a computer search to find several odd prime-partitionable numbers, including 15395 and 397197. In 2014, M. F. Hasler observed on the [On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences](wikipedia://en/500004) that the prime-partitionable numbers appeared to be the same as the Erdős–Woods numbers, and this was proven in the same year by Christopher Hunt Gribble. The same equivalence was also shown by Hasler and Mathar in 2015, together with an equivalence between two definitions of the prime-partitionable numbers from the two earlier works on the subject.
