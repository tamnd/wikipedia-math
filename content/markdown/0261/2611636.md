# John Myhill

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="John Myhill"}

**John R. Myhill Sr.** (11 August 1923 – 15 February 1987)[^o3] was a British [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902).

:::

## Education {#Education}

Myhill received his Ph.D. from [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) under the supervision of [Willard Van Orman Quine](wikipedia://en/33833) in 1949.[^o5] He was a professor at [SUNY Buffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_University_of_New_York) from 1966 until his death in 1987. He also taught at several other universities during his career.

His son, also called John Myhill, is a professor of linguistics in the English department of the [University of Haifa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Haifa) in Israel.[^o6]

## Contributions {#Contributions}

In the theory of [formal languages](wikipedia://en/10939), the [Myhill–Nerode theorem](wikipedia://en/339992), proven by Myhill[^o9] and [Anil Nerode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anil_Nerode),[^o10] characterizes the [regular languages](wikipedia://en/25723) as the languages that have only finitely many inequivalent prefixes.

In [computability theory](wikipedia://en/155414), the [Rice–Myhill–Shapiro theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice–Myhill–Shapiro_theorem),[^o11] more commonly known as Rice's theorem, states that, for any nontrivial property *P* of partial functions, it is [undecidable](wikipedia://en/15631055) whether a given Turing machine computes a function with property *P*. The [Myhill isomorphism theorem](wikipedia://en/2611629) is a computability-theoretic analogue of the [Cantor–Bernstein–Schroeder theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantor–Bernstein–Schroeder_theorem) that characterizes the recursive isomorphisms of pairs of sets.

In the theory of [cellular automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_automata), Myhill is known for proving (along with [E. F. Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_F._Moore)) the [Garden of Eden theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_Eden_theorem), which states that a cellular automaton has a configuration with no predecessor if and only if it has two different asymptotic configurations which evolve to the same configuration. He is also known for posing the [firing squad synchronization problem](wikipedia://en/7247677) of designing an automaton that, starting from a single non-quiescent cell, evolves to a configuration in which all cells reach the same non-quiescent state at the same time; this problem was again solved by Moore.

In [constructive set theory](wikipedia://en/5042360), Myhill proposed an axiom system that avoids the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) and the [law of the excluded middle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_excluded_middle), known as [intuitionistic Zermelo–Fraenkel](wikipedia://en/5042360#Intuitionistic_Zermelo–Fraenkel). He also developed a constructive set theory based on natural numbers, functions, and sets, rather than (as in many other foundational theories) basing it purely on sets.

The **Russell–Myhill paradox** or **Russell–Myhill antinomy**, discovered by [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) in 1902 (and discussed in his *[The Principles of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Principles_of_Mathematics)*, 1903)[^o12][^o13] and rediscovered by Myhill in 1958,[^o14] concerns systems of logic in which logical propositions can be members of classes, and can also be about classes; for instance, a proposition *P* can "state the product" of a class *C*, meaning that proposition *P* asserts that all propositions contained in class *C* are true. In such a system, the class of propositions that state the product of classes that do not include them is paradoxical. For, if proposition *P* states the product of this class, an inconsistency arises regardless of whether *P* does or does not belong to the class it describes.[^o12]

In [music theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_theory), [Myhill's property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myhill's_property) is a mathematical property of [musical scales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_scale) described by John Clough and Gerald Myerson and named by them after Myhill.
