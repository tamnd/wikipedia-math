# Cylinder set

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cylinder set"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **cylinder sets** form a [basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_%28topology%29) of the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910) on a product of sets; they are also a generating family of the [cylinder σ-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_σ-algebra).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="General definition"}

## General definition {#General_definition}

Given a collection $S$ of sets, consider the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) $X = \prod_{Y\in S} Y$ of all sets in the collection. The **canonical projection** corresponding to some $Y\in S$ is the [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $p_{Y} : X \to Y$ that maps every element of the product to its $Y$ component. A cylinder set is a [preimage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preimage) of a canonical projection or finite [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of such preimages. Explicitly, it is a set of the form,

$$\bigcap_{i=1}^n p_{Y_i}^{-1} \left(A_i\right) = \left\{ \left(x\right) \in X \mid p_{Y_1}(x) \in A_1, \dots, p_{Y_n}(x) \in A_n\right\}$$

for any choice of $n$, finite sequence of sets $Y_1,...Y_n\in S$ and [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) $A_{i} \subseteq Y_i$ for $1 \leq i \leq n$.

Then, when all sets in $S$ are [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450), the product topology is [generated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_%28topology%29) by cylinder sets corresponding to the components' open sets. That is cylinders of the form $\bigcap_{i=1}^n p_{Y_i}^{-1} \left(U_i\right)$ where for each $i$, $U_i$ is open in $Y_i$. In the same manner, in case of measurable spaces, the [cylinder σ-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_σ-algebra) is the one which is [generated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-algebra#σ-algebra_generated_by_an_arbitrary_family) by cylinder sets corresponding to the components' measurable sets.

The restriction that the cylinder set be the intersection of a *finite* number of open cylinders is important; allowing infinite intersections generally results in a [finer](wikipedia://en/393069) topology. In the latter case, the resulting topology is the [box topology](wikipedia://en/954333); cylinder sets are never [Hilbert cubes](wikipedia://en/216389).

:::

## Cylinder sets in products of discrete sets {#Cylinder_sets_in_products_of_discrete_sets}

Let $S = \{1,2,\ldots,n\}$ be a finite set, containing *n* objects or **letters**. The collection of all [bi-infinite strings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi-infinite_string) in these letters is denoted by

$$S^\mathbb{Z} = \{ x = (\ldots, x_{-1}, x_0, x_1, \ldots) : x_k \in S \; \forall k \in \mathbb{Z}\}.$$

The natural topology on $S$ is the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology). Basic open sets in the discrete topology consist of individual letters; thus, the open cylinders of the product topology on $S^\mathbb{Z}$ are

$$C_t[a]= \{x \in S^\mathbb{Z} : x_t = a \}.$$

The intersections of a finite number of open cylinders are the **cylinder sets**

$$\begin{aligned} C_t[a_0, \ldots, a_m] & = C_t[a_0] \,\cap\, C_{t+1}[a_1] \,\cap \cdots \cap\, C_{t+m}[a_m] \\ & = \{x \in S^\mathbb{Z} : x_t = a_0, \ldots ,x_{t+m} = a_m \} \end{aligned}.$$

Cylinder sets are [clopen sets](wikipedia://en/233398). As elements of the topology, cylinder sets are by definition open sets. The complement of an open set is a closed set, but the complement of a cylinder set is a [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of cylinders, and so cylinder sets are also closed, and are thus clopen.

## Definition for vector spaces {#Definition_for_vector_spaces}

Given a finite or infinite-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $V$ over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K* (such as the [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826)), the cylinder sets may be defined as

$$C_A[f_1, \ldots, f_n] = \{x \in V : (f_1(x),f_2(x),\ldots,f_n(x)) \in A \}$$

where $A \subset K^n$ is a [Borel set](wikipedia://en/43327) in $K^n$, and each $f_j$ is a [linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) on $V$; that is, $f_j\in (V^*)^{\otimes n}$, the [algebraic dual space](wikipedia://en/7988#Algebraic_dual_space) to $V$. When dealing with [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752), the definition is made instead for elements $f_j \in (V')^{\otimes n}$, the [continuous dual space](wikipedia://en/7988#Continuous_dual_space). That is, the functionals $f_j$ are taken to be continuous linear functionals.

::: {#o7 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Cylinder sets are often used to define a topology on sets that are subsets of $S^\mathbb{Z}$ and occur frequently in the study of [symbolic dynamics](wikipedia://en/1986263); see, for example, [subshift of finite type](wikipedia://en/1986265). Cylinder sets are often used to define a [measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory), using the [Kolmogorov extension theorem](wikipedia://en/6674542); for example, the measure of a cylinder set of length *m* might be given by $1/m$ or by $1/2^{m}$.

Cylinder sets may be used to define a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) on the space: for example, one says that two strings are **ε-close** if a fraction 1−ε of the letters in the strings match.

Since strings in $S^\mathbb{Z}$ can be considered to be [*p*-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423), some of the theory of *p*-adic numbers can be applied to cylinder sets, and in particular, the definition of [*p*-adic measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_measure) and [*p*-adic metrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_metric) apply to cylinder sets. These types of measure spaces appear in the theory of [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems) and are called [nonsingular odometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonsingular_odometer). A generalization of these systems is the [Markov odometer](wikipedia://en/56880139).

Cylinder sets over topological vector spaces are the core ingredient in the definition of [abstract Wiener spaces](wikipedia://en/6592746), which provide the formal definition of the [Feynman path integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feynman_path_integral) or [functional integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_integral) of [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), and the [partition function](wikipedia://en/16846849) of [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481).
