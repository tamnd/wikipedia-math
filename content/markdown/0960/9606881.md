# 68–95–99.7 rule

*Image: Empirical rule histogram.svg.* For an approximately [normal data set](wikipedia://en/21462), the values within one standard deviation of the mean account for about 68% of the set; while within two standard deviations account for about 95%; and within three standard deviations account for about 99.7%. Shown percentages are rounded theoretical probabilities intended only to approximate the empirical data derived from a normal population.

*Image: Standard score and prediction interval.svg.* Prediction interval (on the [$y$-axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y-axis)) given from the [standard score](wikipedia://en/221722) (on the [$x$-axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-axis)). The $y$-axis is scaled as the negative logarithm of the complement of the probability to 1, i.e. $-\log (1 - p) ,$ and labeled with the values of $p$.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="68–95–99.7 rule"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **68–95–99.7 rule**, also known as the **empirical rule** or **68–95–99.7 rule for a normal distribution**[^o2] and sometimes abbreviated **3sr** or **$3 \sigma$**, is a shorthand used to remember the percentage of values that lie within an [interval estimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_estimate) in a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462): approximately 68%, 95%, and 99.7% of the values lie within one, two, and three [standard deviations](wikipedia://en/27590) of the [mean](wikipedia://en/612), respectively.

:::

In mathematical notation, these facts can be expressed as follows, where $\Pr()$ is the [probability function](wikipedia://en/23543),[^o3] $\mathrm{X}$ is an observation from a normally distributed [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685), $\mu$ (mu) is the mean of the distribution, and $\sigma$ (sigma) is its standard deviation:

$$\begin{aligned} \Pr(\mu-1\sigma \le X \le \mu+1\sigma) & \approx 68.27\% \\ \Pr(\mu-2\sigma \le X \le \mu+2\sigma) & \approx 95.45\% \\ \Pr(\mu-3\sigma \le X \le \mu+3\sigma) & \approx 99.73\% \end{aligned}$$

The usefulness of this heuristic depends especially on the question under consideration and the manner in which the data have been collected; most particularly the heuristic depends on the data genuinely being [normally distributed](wikipedia://en/21462): Among the many bell-shaped distributions often seen in real-life data, the normal distribution has notoriously "[thin tails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin-tailed_distribution)" – an unusual concentration of probability near its center. If the datum $X$ is instead governed by one of the many similar-appearing and [commonly encountered distributions](wikipedia://en/105375) that have "[fatter tails](wikipedia://en/4357461)" – with probability more spread-out – the significance would be lower for all three deviations from the mean.

In the [empirical sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_science), the so-called **three-sigma rule of thumb** (or **3 $\sigma$ rule**) expresses a conventional [heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic) that nearly all values are taken to lie within three standard deviations of the mean, and thus it is empirically useful to treat 99.7% [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) as near certainty.[^o4]

In the [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_sciences), a result may be considered [statistically significant](wikipedia://en/160995) (clear enough to warrant closer examination) if its [confidence level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_level) is of the order of a two-sigma effect (95%), while in [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics), there is a convention of requiring statistical significance of a five-sigma effect (99.99994% confidence) to qualify as a [discovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_%28observation%29).[^o5]

A weaker three-sigma rule can be derived from [Chebyshev's inequality](wikipedia://en/156533), stating that even for non-normally distributed variables, at least 88.8% of cases should fall within properly calculated three-sigma intervals. For [unimodal distributions](wikipedia://en/1588509#Unimodal_probability_distribution), the probability of being within three-sigma is at least 95% by the [Vysochanskij–Petunin inequality](wikipedia://en/435813). There may be certain assumptions for a distribution that force this probability to be at least 98%.[^o6]

::: {#o8 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

We have that

$$\begin{aligned}\Pr(\mu -n\sigma \leq X \leq \mu + n\sigma) = \int_{\mu-n\sigma}^{\mu + n\sigma} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi} \sigma} e^{-\frac{1}{2} \left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^2} dx, \end{aligned}$$

doing the [change of variable](wikipedia://en/193748) in terms of the [standard score](wikipedia://en/221722) $z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$, we have

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-n}^{n} e^{-\frac{z^2}{2}}dz\end{aligned},$$

and this integral is independent of $\mu$ and $\sigma$. We only need to calculate each integral for the cases $n = 1,2,3$.

$$\begin{aligned} \Pr(\mu -1\sigma \leq X \leq \mu + 1\sigma) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-1}^{1} e^{-\frac{z^2}{2}}dz \approx 0.6826894921 \\ \Pr(\mu -2\sigma \leq X \leq \mu + 2\sigma) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-2}^{2} e^{-\frac{z^2}{2}}dz \approx 0.9544997361 \\ \Pr(\mu -3\sigma \leq X \leq \mu + 3\sigma) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-3}^{3} e^{-\frac{z^2}{2}}dz \approx 0.9973002039. \end{aligned}$$

:::

## Cumulative distribution function {#Cumulative_distribution_function}

*Image: Cumulative distribution function for normal distribution, mean 0 and sd 1.png.* Diagram showing the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) for the normal distribution with mean ($\mu$) 0 and variance ($\sigma^{2}$) 1

These numerical values "68%, 95%, 99.7%" come from the [cumulative distribution function of the normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462#Cumulative_distribution_function).

The [prediction interval](wikipedia://en/536062) for any [standard score](wikipedia://en/221722) *z* corresponds numerically to $(1 - (1 - \Phi_{\mu,\sigma^{2}}(z)) \cdot 2)$.

For example, $\Phi(2) \approx 0.9772$, or $\Pr(X \le \mu + 2\sigma) \approx 0.9772$, corresponding to a prediction interval of $(1 - (1 - 0.97725)\cdot2) = 0.9545 = 95.45\%$. This is not a symmetrical interval – this is merely the probability that an observation is less than $\mu + 2\sigma$. To compute the probability that an observation is within two standard deviations of the mean (small differences due to rounding):

$$\Pr(\mu-2\sigma \le X \le \mu+2\sigma) = \Phi(2) - \Phi(-2) \approx 0.9772 - (1 - 0.9772) \approx 0.9545$$

This is related to [confidence interval](wikipedia://en/280911) as used in statistics: $\bar{X} \pm 2\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$ is approximately a 95% confidence interval when $\bar{X}$ is the average of a sample of size $n$.

## Normality tests {#Normality_tests}

The "68–95–99.7 rule" is often used to quickly get a rough probability estimate of something, given its standard deviation, if the population is assumed to be normal. It is also used as a simple test for [outliers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outliers) if the population is assumed normal, and as a [normality test](wikipedia://en/8617378) if the population is potentially not normal.

To pass from a sample to a number of standard deviations, one first computes the [deviation](wikipedia://en/9252911), either the [error or residual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errors_and_residuals_in_statistics) depending on whether one knows the population mean or only estimates it. The next step is [standardizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standardizing) (dividing by the population standard deviation), if the population parameters are known, or [studentizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Studentizing) (dividing by an estimate of the standard deviation), if the parameters are unknown and only estimated.

To use as a test for outliers or a normality test, one computes the size of deviations in terms of standard deviations, and compares this to expected frequency. Given a sample set, one can compute the [studentized residuals](wikipedia://en/609197) and compare these to the expected frequency: points that fall more than 3 standard deviations from the norm are likely outliers (unless the [sample size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_size) is significantly large, by which point one expects a sample this extreme), and if there are many points more than 3 standard deviations from the norm, one likely has reason to question the assumed normality of the distribution. This holds ever more strongly for moves of 4 or more standard deviations.

One can compute more precisely, approximating the number of extreme moves of a given magnitude or greater by a [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144), but simply, if one has multiple 4 standard deviation moves in a sample of size 1,000, one has strong reason to consider these outliers or question the assumed normality of the distribution.

For example, a 6*σ* event corresponds to a chance of about two [parts per billion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts_per_billion). For illustration, if events are taken to occur daily, this would correspond to an event expected every 1.4 million years. This gives a [simple normality test](wikipedia://en/8617378#Back_of_the_envelope_test): if one witnesses a 6*σ* in daily data and significantly fewer than 1 million years have passed, then a normal distribution most likely does not provide a good model for the magnitude or frequency of large deviations in this respect.
