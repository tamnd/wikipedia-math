# Flow plasticity theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Flow plasticity theory"}

*Image: Biegeanimation 2D.gif.* Plastic deformation of a thin metal sheet.

**Flow plasticity** is a [solid mechanics](wikipedia://en/458866) theory that is used to describe the [plastic](wikipedia://en/240123) behavior of materials.[^o2] Flow plasticity theories are characterized by the assumption that a [flow rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_rule_%28plasticity%29) exists that can be used to determine the amount of plastic deformation in the material.

:::

In flow plasticity theories it is assumed that the total [strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_%28mechanics%29) in a body can be decomposed additively (or multiplicatively) into an elastic part and a plastic part. The elastic part of the strain can be computed from a [linear elastic](wikipedia://en/301429) or [hyperelastic](wikipedia://en/4472938) constitutive model. However, determination of the plastic part of the strain requires a [flow rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_rule_%28plasticity%29) and a [hardening model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardening_model_%28plasticity%29).

## Small deformation theory {#Small_deformation_theory}

*Image: Rock plasticity compression plain.svg.* Stress-strain curve showing typical plastic behavior of materials in uniaxial compression. The strain can be decomposed into a recoverable elastic strain ($\varepsilon_e$) and an inelastic strain ($\varepsilon_p$). The stress at initial yield is $\sigma_0$. For strain hardening materials (as shown in the figure) the yield stress increases with increasing plastic deformation to a value of $\sigma_y$.

Typical flow plasticity theories for unidirectional loading (for small deformation perfect plasticity or hardening plasticity) are developed on the basis of the following requirements:

1. The material has a linear elastic range.
1. The material has an elastic limit defined as the stress at which plastic deformation first takes place, i.e., $\sigma = \sigma_0$.
1. Beyond the elastic limit the stress state always remains on the yield surface, i.e., $\sigma = \sigma_y$.
1. Loading is defined as the situation under which increments of stress are greater than zero, i.e., $d\sigma > 0$. If loading takes the stress state to the plastic domain then the increment of plastic strain is always greater than zero, i.e., $d\varepsilon_p > 0$.
1. Unloading is defined as the situation under which increments of stress are less than zero, i.e., $d\sigma < 0$. The material is elastic during unloading and no additional plastic strain is accumulated.
1. The total strain is a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of the elastic and plastic parts, i.e., $d\varepsilon = d\varepsilon_e + d\varepsilon_p$. The plastic part cannot be recovered while the elastic part is fully recoverable.
1. The work done of a loading-unloading cycle is positive or zero, i.e., $d\sigma\,d\varepsilon = d\sigma\,(d\varepsilon_e + d\varepsilon_p) \ge 0$. This is also called the [Drucker stability](wikipedia://en/39504221) postulate and eliminates the possibility of strain softening behavior.

The above requirements can be expressed in three dimensional states of stress and multidirectional loading as follows.

- **Elasticity** ([Hooke's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooke's_law)). In the linear elastic regime the stresses and strains in the material are related by

$$\boldsymbol{\sigma} = \mathsf{D}:\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$$

where the stiffness matrix $\mathsf{D}$ is constant.

- **Elastic limit** ([Yield surface](wikipedia://en/8001517)). The elastic limit is defined by a yield surface that does not depend on the plastic strain and has the form

$$f(\boldsymbol{\sigma}) = 0 \,.$$

- **Beyond the elastic limit**. For strain hardening materials, the yield surface evolves with increasing plastic strain and the elastic limit changes. The evolving yield surface has the form

$$f(\boldsymbol{\sigma}, \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_p) = 0 \,.$$

- **Loading**. For general states of stress, plastic **loading** is indicated if the state of stress is on the yield surface and the stress increment is directed toward the outside of the yield surface; this occurs if the inner product of the stress increment and the outward normal of the yield surface is positive, i.e.,

$$d\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\frac{\partial f}{\partial \boldsymbol{\sigma}} \ge 0 \,.$$

The above equation, when it is equal to zero, indicates a state of **neutral loading** where the stress state moves along the yield surface.

- **Unloading**: A similar argument is made for unloading for which situation $f < 0$, the material is in the elastic domain, and

$$d\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\frac{\partial f}{\partial \boldsymbol{\sigma}} < 0 \,.$$

- **Strain decomposition**: The additive decomposition of the strain into elastic and plastic parts can be written as

$$d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_e + d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_p \,.$$

- **Stability postulate**: The stability postulate is expressed as

$$d\boldsymbol{\sigma}:d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \ge 0 \,.$$

### Flow rule {#Flow_rule}

In metal plasticity, the assumption that the plastic strain increment and deviatoric stress tensor have the same principal directions is encapsulated in a relation called the [flow rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_rule). Rock plasticity theories also use a similar concept except that the requirement of pressure-dependence of the yield surface requires a relaxation of the above assumption. Instead, it is typically assumed that the plastic strain increment and the normal to the pressure-dependent yield surface have the same direction, i.e.,

$$d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_p = d\lambda\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial \boldsymbol{\sigma}}$$

where $d\lambda > 0$ is a hardening parameter. This form of the flow rule is called an [associated flow rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associated_flow_rule) and the assumption of co-directionality is called the [normality condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normality_condition_%28plasticity%29). The function $f$ is also called a [plastic potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_potential).

The above flow rule is easily justified for perfectly plastic deformations for which $d\boldsymbol{\sigma} = 0$ when $d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_p > 0$, i.e., the yield surface remains constant under increasing plastic deformation. This implies that the increment of elastic strain is also zero, $d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_e = 0$, because of Hooke's law. Therefore,

$$d\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\frac{\partial f}{\partial \boldsymbol{\sigma}} = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad d\boldsymbol{\sigma}:d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_p = 0 \,.$$

Hence, both the normal to the yield surface and the plastic strain tensor are perpendicular to the stress tensor and must have the same direction.

For a [work hardening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_hardening) material, the yield surface can expand with increasing stress. We assume Drucker's second stability postulate which states that for an [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) stress cycle this plastic work is positive, i.e.,

$$d\boldsymbol{\sigma}: d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_p \ge 0 \,.$$

The above quantity is equal to zero for purely elastic cycles. Examination of the work done over a cycle of plastic loading-unloading can be used to justify the validity of the associated flow rule.[^o5]

### Consistency condition {#Consistency_condition}

The [Prager consistency condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prager_consistency_condition) is needed to close the set of [constitutive equations](wikipedia://en/1034699) and to eliminate the unknown parameter $d\lambda$ from the system of equations. The consistency condition states that $df = 0$ at yield because $f(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_p) = 0$, and hence

$$df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \boldsymbol{\sigma}}:d\boldsymbol{\sigma} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_p}:d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_p = 0 \,.$$

## Large deformation theory {#Large_deformation_theory}

Large deformation flow theories of plasticity typically start with one of the following assumptions:

- the [rate of deformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_of_deformation) tensor can be additively decomposed into an elastic part and a plastic part, or
- the [deformation gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_gradient) tensor can be multiplicatively decomposed in an elastic part and a plastic part.

The first assumption was widely used for numerical simulations of metals but has gradually been superseded by the multiplicative theory.

### Kinematics of multiplicative plasticity {#Kinematics_of_multiplicative_plasticity}

The concept of multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient into elastic and plastic parts was first proposed independently by [B. A. Bilby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Bilby),[^o9] E. Kröner,[^o10] in the context of [crystal plasticity](wikipedia://en/67665947) and extended to continuum plasticity by Erasmus Lee.[^o11] The decomposition assumes that the total deformation gradient (***F***) can be decomposed as:

$$\boldsymbol{F} = \boldsymbol{F}^e\cdot\boldsymbol{F}^p$$

where ***F***<sup>e</sup> is the elastic (recoverable) part and ***F***<sup>p</sup> is the plastic (unrecoverable) part of the deformation. The [spatial velocity gradient](wikipedia://en/2210759#Time-derivative_of_the_deformation_gradient) is given by

$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{l} & = \dot{\boldsymbol{F}}\cdot\boldsymbol{F}^{-1} = \left(\dot{\boldsymbol{F}}^e\cdot\boldsymbol{F}^p + \boldsymbol{F}^e\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{F}}^p\right)\cdot \left[(\boldsymbol{F}^p)^{-1}\cdot(\boldsymbol{F}^e)^{-1}\right] \\ & = \dot{\boldsymbol{F}}^e\cdot(\boldsymbol{F}^e)^{-1} + \boldsymbol{F}^e\cdot[\dot{\boldsymbol{F}}^p\cdot (\boldsymbol{F}^p)^{-1}]\cdot(\boldsymbol{F}^e)^{-1} \,. \end{aligned}$$

where a superposed dot indicates a [time derivative](wikipedia://en/1783069). We can write the above as

$$\boldsymbol{l} = \boldsymbol{l}^e + \boldsymbol{F}^e\cdot\boldsymbol{L}^p\cdot(\boldsymbol{F}^e)^{-1} \,.$$

The quantity

$$\boldsymbol{L}^p := \dot{\boldsymbol{F}}^p\cdot (\boldsymbol{F}^p)^{-1}$$

is called a **plastic velocity gradient** and is defined in an intermediate ([incompatible](wikipedia://en/25800675)) stress-free configuration. The symmetric part (***D***<sup>p</sup>) of ***L***<sup>p</sup> is called the **plastic rate of deformation** while the skew-symmetric part (***W***<sup>p</sup>) is called the **plastic spin**:

$$\boldsymbol{D}^p = \tfrac{1}{2}[\boldsymbol{L}^p +(\boldsymbol{L}^p)^T] ~,~~ \boldsymbol{W}^p = \tfrac{1}{2}[\boldsymbol{L}^p -(\boldsymbol{L}^p)^T] \,.$$

Typically, the plastic spin is ignored in most descriptions of finite plasticity.

### Elastic regime {#Elastic_regime}

The elastic behavior in the finite strain regime is typically described by a [hyperelastic material](wikipedia://en/4472938) model. The elastic strain can be measured using an elastic right [Cauchy-Green deformation tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy-Green_deformation_tensor) defined as:

$$\boldsymbol{C}^e := (\boldsymbol{F}^e)^T\cdot\boldsymbol{F}^e \,.$$

The [logarithmic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_strain) or [Hencky strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_strain) tensor may then be defined as

$$\boldsymbol{E}^e := \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\boldsymbol{C}^e \,.$$

The symmetrized [Mandel stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandel_stress) tensor is a convenient stress measure for finite plasticity and is defined as

$$\boldsymbol{M} := \tfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{C}^e\cdot\boldsymbol{S} + \boldsymbol{S}\cdot\boldsymbol{C}^e)$$

where ***S*** is the [second Piola-Kirchhoff stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_measures). A possible hyperelastic model in terms of the logarithmic strain is [^o13]

$$\boldsymbol{M} = \frac{\partial W}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}^e} = J\,\frac{dU}{dJ} + 2\mu\,\text{dev}(\boldsymbol{E}^e)$$

where *W* is a strain [energy density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_density) function, *J* = det(***F***), *μ* is a modulus, and "dev" indicates the deviatoric part of a tensor.

### Flow rule {#Flow_rule}

Application of the [Clausius-Duhem inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clausius-Duhem_inequality) leads, in the absence of a plastic spin, to the finite strain flow rule

$$\boldsymbol{D}^p = \dot{\lambda}\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial \boldsymbol{M}} \,.$$

### Loading-unloading conditions {#Loading-unloading_conditions}

The loading-unloading conditions can be shown to be equivalent to the [Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karush-Kuhn-Tucker_conditions)

$$\dot{\lambda} \ge 0 ~,~~ f \le 0~,~~ \dot{\lambda}\,f = 0 \,.$$

### Consistency condition {#Consistency_condition}

The consistency condition is identical to that for the small strain case,

$$\dot{\lambda}\,\dot{f} = 0 \,.$$
