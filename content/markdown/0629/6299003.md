# Cubic reciprocity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cubic reciprocity"}

**Cubic reciprocity** is a collection of theorems in [elementary](wikipedia://en/21527#Elementary_number_theory) and [algebraic](wikipedia://en/174705) [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) that state conditions under which the [congruence](wikipedia://en/20087) *x*<sup>3</sup> ≡ *p* (mod *q*) is solvable; the word "reciprocity" comes from the form of the main theorem, which states that if *p* and *q* are primary numbers in the ring of [Eisenstein integers](wikipedia://en/2353035), both coprime to 3, the congruence *x*<sup>3</sup> ≡ *p* (mod *q*) is solvable if and only if *x*<sup>3</sup> ≡ *q* (mod *p*) is solvable.

:::

## History {#History}

Sometime before 1748 [Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) made the first conjectures about the cubic residuacity of small integers, but they were not published until 1849, 62 years after his death.[^o3]

Gauss's published works mention cubic residues and reciprocity three times: there is one result pertaining to cubic residues in the [Disquisitiones Arithmeticae](wikipedia://en/736713) (1801).[^o4] In the introduction to the fifth and sixth [proofs of quadratic reciprocity](wikipedia://en/3090886) (1818)[^o5] he said that he was publishing these proofs because their techniques ([Gauss's lemma](wikipedia://en/2310978) and [Gaussian sums](wikipedia://en/595898), respectively) can be applied to cubic and biquadratic reciprocity. Finally, a footnote in the second (of two) monographs on [biquadratic reciprocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biquadratic_reciprocity) (1832) states that cubic reciprocity is most easily described in the ring of Eisenstein integers.[^o6]

From his diary and other unpublished sources, it appears that Gauss knew the rules for the cubic and quartic residuacity of integers by 1805, and discovered the full-blown theorems and proofs of cubic and biquadratic reciprocity around 1814.[^o7][^o8] Proofs of these were found in his posthumous papers, but it is not clear if they are his or Eisenstein's.[^o9]

[Jacobi](wikipedia://en/210340) published several theorems about cubic residuacity in 1827, but no proofs.[^o10] In his Königsberg lectures of 1836–37 Jacobi presented proofs.[^o9] The first published proofs were by Eisenstein (1844).[^o11][^o12][^o13]

## Integers {#Integers}

A **cubic residue** (mod *p*) is any number congruent to the third power of an integer (mod *p*). If *x*<sup>3</sup> ≡ *a* (mod *p*) does not have an integer solution, *a* is a **cubic nonresidue** (mod *p*).[^o15]

Cubic residues are usually only defined in modulus *n* such that $\lambda(n)$ (the [Carmichael lambda function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carmichael_lambda_function) of *n*) is divisible by 3, since for other integer *n*, all residues are cubic residues.

As is often the case in number theory, it is easier to work modulo prime numbers, so in this section all moduli *p*, *q*, etc., are assumed to be positive odd primes.[^o15]

We first note that if *q* ≡ 2 (mod 3) is a prime then every number is a cubic residue modulo *q*. Let *q* = 3*n* + 2; since 0 = 0<sup>3</sup> is obviously a cubic residue, assume *x* is not divisible by *q*. Then by [Fermat's little theorem](wikipedia://en/53453),

$$x^q \equiv x \pmod{q}, \qquad x^{q - 1} \equiv 1 \pmod{q}$$

Multiplying the two congruences we have

$$x^{2q-1} \equiv x \pmod{q}$$

Now substituting 3*n* + 2 for *q* we have:

$$x^{2q-1} = x^{6n + 3} = \left (x^{2n+1} \right )^3.$$

Therefore, the only interesting case is when the modulus *p* ≡ 1 (mod 3). In this case the non-zero residue classes (mod *p*) can be divided into three sets, each containing (*p*−1)/3 numbers. Let *e* be a cubic non-residue. The first set is the cubic residues; the second one is *e* times the numbers in the first set, and the third is *e*<sup>2</sup> times the numbers in the first set. Another way to describe this division is to let *e* be a [primitive root](wikipedia://en/186864) (mod *p*); then the first (resp. second, third) set is the numbers whose indices with respect to this root are congruent to 0 (resp. 1, 2) (mod 3). In the vocabulary of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), the cubic residues form a subgroup of [index](wikipedia://en/230581) 3 of the multiplicative group $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$ and the three sets are its cosets.

### Primes ≡ 1 (mod 3) {#Primes_≡_1_(mod_3)}

A theorem of Fermat[^o17][^o18] states that every prime *p* ≡ 1 (mod 3) can be written as *p* = *a*<sup>2</sup> + 3*b*<sup>2</sup> and (except for the signs of *a* and *b*) this representation is unique.

Letting *m* = *a* + *b* and *n* = *a* − *b*, we see that this is equivalent to *p* = *m*<sup>2</sup> − *mn* + *n*<sup>2</sup> (which equals (*n* − *m*)<sup>2</sup> − (*n* − *m*)*n* + *n*<sup>2</sup> = *m*<sup>2</sup> + *m*(*n* − *m*) + (*n* − *m*)<sup>2</sup>, so *m* and *n* are not determined uniquely). Thus,

$$\begin{aligned} 4p &= (2m-n)^2 + 3n^2 \\ &= (2n-m)^2 + 3m^2 \\ &= (m+n)^2 + 3(m-n)^2 \end{aligned}$$

and it is a straightforward exercise to show that exactly one of *m*, *n*, or *m* − *n* is a multiple of 3, so

$$p = \frac14 (L^2+ 27M^2),$$

and this representation is unique up to the signs of *L* and *M*.[^o19]

For relatively prime integers *m* and *n* define the **rational cubic residue symbol** as

$$\left[\frac{m}{n}\right]_3 = \begin{cases} 1 & m \text{ is a cubic residue } \pmod{n} \\ -1 & m \text{ is a cubic non-residue }\pmod{n} \end{cases}$$

This symbol does *not* have the multiplicative properties of the Legendre symbol; for this, we need the true cubic character with values in the cubic roots of 1 defined below.

**Euler's conjectures (now proved).** Let *p* = *a*<sup>2</sup> + 3*b*<sup>2</sup> be a prime. Then the following hold:[^o20][^o21][^o22]

$$\begin{aligned} \left[\tfrac{2}{p}\right]_3 =1 \quad &\Longleftrightarrow \quad 3\mid b\\ \left[\tfrac{3}{p}\right]_3 =1 \quad &\Longleftrightarrow \quad 9\mid b \text{ or } 9\mid(a\pm b)\\ \left[\tfrac{5}{p}\right]_3 =1 \quad &\Longleftrightarrow \quad 15\mid b \text{ or } 3\mid b \text{ and } 5\mid a \text{ or } 15\mid(a\pm b) \text{ or } 15\mid(2a\pm b)\\ \left[\tfrac{6}{p}\right]_3 =1 \quad &\Longleftrightarrow \quad 9\mid b \text{ or } 9\mid(a\pm 2b)\\ \left[\tfrac{7}{p}\right]_3 =1 \quad &\Longrightarrow \quad (3\mid b\text{ and }7\mid a) \text{ or } 21\mid (b\pm a) \text{ or } 7\mid(4b\pm a) \text{ or } 21\mid b \text{ or } 7\mid(b\pm 2a) \end{aligned}$$

The first two can be restated as follows. Let *p* be a prime that is congruent to 1 modulo 3. Then:[^o23][^o24][^o25]

- 2 is a cubic residue of *p* if and only if *p* = *a*<sup>2</sup> + 27*b*<sup>2</sup>.
- 3 is a cubic residue of *p* if and only if 4*p* = *a*<sup>2</sup> + 243*b*<sup>2</sup>.

**Gauss's theorem.** Let *p* be a positive prime such that

$$p = 3n + 1= \tfrac14 \left(L^2+ 27M^2\right).$$

Then $L(n!)^3\equiv 1 \pmod{p}.$[^o26][^o27]

One can easily see that Gauss's Theorem implies:

$$\left[\tfrac{L}{p}\right]_3 = \left[\tfrac{M}{p}\right]_3 =1.$$

**Jacobi's theorem (stated without proof).**[^o28] Let *q* ≡ *p* ≡ 1 (mod 6) be positive primes. Obviously both *p* and *q* are also congruent to 1 modulo 3, therefore assume:

$$p = \tfrac14 \left(L^2+ 27M^2\right), \qquad q = \tfrac14 \left(L'^2+ 27M'^2\right).$$

Let *x* be a solution of *x*<sup>2</sup> ≡ −3 (mod *q*). Then

$$x\equiv\pm \frac{L'}{3M'}\pmod{q},$$

and we have:

$$\begin{aligned} \left[\frac{q}{p}\right]_3 =1 \quad &\Longleftrightarrow \quad \left[\frac{\frac{L+3Mx}{2}p}{q}\right]_3 =1 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \left[\frac{\frac{L+3Mx}{L-3Mx}}{q}\right]_3 =1 \\ \left[\frac{q}{p}\right]_3 =1 \quad &\Longrightarrow \quad \left[\frac{\frac{LM'+L'M}{LM'-L'M}}{q}\right]_3 =1 \end{aligned}$$

**[Lehmer](wikipedia://en/7682439)'s Theorem.** Let *q* and *p* be primes, with $p = \tfrac14 \left(L^2+ 27M^2\right).$ Then:[^o29]

$$\left[\frac{q}{p}\right]_3 = 1 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad q \mid LM \text{ or } L\equiv\pm \frac{9r}{2u+1} M\pmod{q},$$

where

$$u\not\equiv 0,1,-\tfrac12, -\tfrac13 \pmod{q} \quad \text{and} \quad 3u+1 \equiv r^2 (3u-3)\pmod{q}.$$

Note that the first condition implies that any number that divides *L* or *M* is a cubic residue (mod *p*).

The first few examples[^o30] of this are equivalent to Euler's conjectures:

$$\begin{aligned} \left[\frac{2}{p}\right]_3 =1 \quad &\Longleftrightarrow \quad L \equiv M \equiv 0 \pmod{2} \\ \left[\frac{3}{p}\right]_3 =1 \quad &\Longleftrightarrow \quad M \equiv 0 \pmod{3} \\ \left[\frac{5}{p}\right]_3 =1 \quad &\Longleftrightarrow \quad LM \equiv 0 \pmod{5} \\ \left[\frac{7}{p}\right]_3 =1 \quad &\Longleftrightarrow \quad LM \equiv 0 \pmod{7} \end{aligned}$$

Since obviously *L* ≡ *M* (mod 2), the criterion for *q* = 2 can be simplified as:

$$\left[\frac{2}{p}\right]_3 =1 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad M \equiv 0 \pmod{2}.$$

**Martinet's theorem.** Let *p* ≡ *q* ≡ 1 (mod 3) be primes, $pq = \tfrac14 (L^2 + 27 M^2).$ Then[^o31]

$$\left[\frac{L}{p}\right]_3 \left[\frac{L}{q}\right]_3 =1\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \left[\frac{q}{p}\right]_3 \left[\frac{p}{q}\right]_3 =1.$$

**Sharifi's theorem.** Let *p* = 1 + 3*x* + 9*x*<sup>2</sup> be a prime. Then any divisor of *x* is a cubic residue (mod *p*).[^o32]

## Eisenstein integers {#Eisenstein_integers}

### Background {#Background}

In his second monograph on biquadratic reciprocity, Gauss says:

> The theorems on biquadratic residues gleam with the greatest simplicity and genuine beauty only when the field of arithmetic is extended to **imaginary** numbers, so that without restriction, the numbers of the form *a* + *bi* constitute the object of study ... we call such numbers **integral complex numbers**.[^o35] \[bold in the original\]

These numbers are now called the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) of [Gaussian integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integers), denoted by **Z**\[*i*\]. Note that *i* is a fourth root of 1.

In a footnote he adds

> The theory of cubic residues must be based in a similar way on a consideration of numbers of the form *a* + *bh* where *h* is an imaginary root of the equation *h*<sup>3</sup> = 1 ... and similarly the theory of residues of higher powers leads to the introduction of other imaginary quantities.[^o36]

In his first monograph on cubic reciprocity[^o37] Eisenstein developed the theory of the numbers built up from a cube [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950); they are now called the ring of [Eisenstein integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eisenstein_integers). Eisenstein said that to investigate the properties of this ring one need only consult Gauss's work on **Z**\[*i*\] and modify the proofs. This is not surprising since both rings are [unique factorization domains](wikipedia://en/60546).

The "other imaginary quantities" needed for the "theory of residues of higher powers" are the [rings of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of the [cyclotomic number fields](wikipedia://en/30872462); the Gaussian and Eisenstein integers are the simplest examples of these.

### Facts and terminology {#Facts_and_terminology}

Let

$$\omega = \frac{-1 + i\sqrt 3}{2} = e^\frac{2\pi i}{3}, \qquad \omega^3 = 1.$$

And consider the ring of [Eisenstein integers](wikipedia://en/2353035):

$$\mathbb{Z}[\omega] = \left \{ a + b \omega \ : \ a, b \in \mathbb{Z} \right \}.$$

This is a [Euclidean domain](wikipedia://en/10376) with the [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) function given by:

$$N(a + b \omega) = a^2 -ab + b^2.$$

Note that the norm is always congruent to 0 or 1 (mod 3).

The [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) in $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ (the elements with a [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) or equivalently those with unit norm) is a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of the sixth roots of unity,

$$\left \{ \pm 1, \pm \omega, \pm \omega^2\right \}.$$

$\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ is a [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546). The primes fall into three classes:[^o39]

- 3 is a special case:

$$3 = -\omega^2 (1-\omega)^2.$$

It is the only prime in $\mathbb{Z}$ divisible by the square of a prime in $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$. The prime 3 is said to [ramify](wikipedia://en/1190816) in $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$.

- Positive primes in $\mathbb{Z}$ congruent to 2 (mod 3) are also primes in $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$. These primes are said to remain [inert](wikipedia://en/1190816) in $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$. Note that if $q$ is any inert prime then:

$$N(q) = q^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}.$$

- Positive primes in $\mathbb{Z}$ congruent to 1 (mod 3) are the product of two conjugate primes in $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$. These primes are said to [split](wikipedia://en/1190816) in $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$. Their factorization is given by:

$$p=N (\pi) = N (\overline{\pi})= \pi \overline{\pi}.$$

for example

$$7 = ( 3 + \omega) ( 2 - \omega).$$

A number is **primary** if it is coprime to 3 and congruent to an ordinary integer modulo $(1-\omega)^2,$ which is the same as saying it is congruent to $\pm 2$ modulo 3. If $\gcd(N(\lambda), 3) = 1$ one of $\lambda, \omega \lambda,$ or $\omega^2 \lambda$ is primary. Moreover, the product of two primary numbers is primary and the conjugate of a primary number is also primary.

The unique factorization theorem for $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ is: if $\lambda \neq 0,$ then

$$\lambda = \pm\omega^\mu(1-\omega)^\nu\pi_1^{\alpha_1}\pi_2^{\alpha_2}\pi_3^{\alpha_3} \cdots, \qquad \mu \in \{0, 1, 2\}, \quad \nu, \alpha_1, \alpha_2, \ldots \geqslant 0$$

where each $\pi_i$ is a primary (under Eisenstein's definition) prime. And this representation is unique, up to the order of the factors.

The notions of [congruence](wikipedia://en/20087)[^o40] and [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354)[^o41] are defined the same way in $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ as they are for the ordinary integers $\mathbb{Z}$. Because the units divide all numbers, a congruence modulo $\lambda$ is also true modulo any associate of $\lambda$, and any associate of a GCD is also a GCD.

### Cubic residue character {#Cubic_residue_character}

::: {#o44 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

#### Definition {#Definition}

An analogue of [Fermat's little theorem](wikipedia://en/53453) is true in $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$: if $\alpha$ is not divisible by a prime $\pi$,[^o45]

$$\alpha^{N (\pi) - 1} \equiv 1 \pmod{\pi}.$$

Now assume that $N(\pi) \neq 3$ so that $N(\pi) \equiv 1 \pmod{3}.$ Or put differently $3\mid N(\pi) -1.$ Then we can write:

$$\alpha^{\frac{N ( \pi )- 1}{3}}\equiv \omega^k \pmod{\pi},$$

for a unique unit $\omega^k.$ This unit is called the **cubic residue character** of $\alpha$ modulo $\pi$ and is denoted by[^o46]

$$\left(\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\right)_3 = \omega^k \equiv \alpha^{\frac{N(\pi) - 1}{3}} \pmod{\pi}.$$

:::

::: {#o48 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

#### Properties {#Properties}

The cubic residue character has formal properties similar to those of the [Legendre symbol](wikipedia://en/18567):

- If $\alpha \equiv \beta \pmod{\pi}$ then $\left (\tfrac{\alpha}{\pi}\right )_3=\left (\tfrac{\beta}{\pi}\right )_3.$
- $$\left (\tfrac{\alpha\beta}{\pi}\right )_3=\left (\tfrac{\alpha}{\pi}\right )_3\left (\tfrac{\beta}{\pi}\right )_3.$$
- $\overline{\left (\tfrac{\alpha}{\pi}\right )_3}=\left (\tfrac{\overline{\alpha}}{\overline{\pi}}\right )_3,$ where the bar denotes [complex conjugation](wikipedia://en/173918).
- If $\pi$ and $\theta$ are associates then $\left (\tfrac{\alpha}{\pi}\right )_3=\left (\tfrac{\alpha}{\theta}\right )_3$
- The congruence $x^3 \equiv \alpha \pmod{\pi}$ has a solution in $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ if and only if $\left(\tfrac{\alpha}{\pi}\right)_3 = 1.$[^o49]
- If $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$ are such that $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, 3) = 1,$ then $\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)_3 = 1.$[^o50][^o51]
- The cubic character can be extended multiplicatively to composite numbers (coprime to 3) in the "denominator" in the same way the Legendre symbol is generalized into the [Jacobi symbol](wikipedia://en/97313). As with the Jacobi symbol, this extension sacrifices the "numerator is a cubic residue mod the denominator" meaning: the symbol is still guaranteed to be 1 when the "numerator" is a cubic residue, but the converse no longer holds.

$$\left(\frac{\alpha}{\lambda}\right)_3 = \left(\frac{\alpha}{\pi_1}\right)_3^{\alpha_1} \left(\frac{\alpha}{\pi_2}\right)_3^{\alpha_2} \cdots,$$

where

$$\lambda = \pi_1^{\alpha_1}\pi_2^{\alpha_2}\pi_3^{\alpha_3} \cdots$$

:::

::: {#o53 .statement cue=heading name="Statement of the theorem"}

### Statement of the theorem {#Statement_of_the_theorem}

Let α and β be primary. Then

$$\Bigg(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\Bigg)_3 = \Bigg(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\Bigg)_3.$$

There are supplementary theorems[^o54][^o55] for the units and the prime 1 − ω:

Let α = *a* + *b*ω be primary, *a* = 3*m* + 1 and *b* = 3*n*. (If *a* ≡ 2 (mod 3) replace α with its associate −α; this will not change the value of the cubic characters.) Then

$$\Bigg(\frac{\omega}{\alpha}\Bigg)_3 = \omega^\frac{1-a-b}{3}= \omega^{-m-n},\;\;\; \Bigg(\frac{1-\omega}{\alpha}\Bigg)_3 = \omega^\frac{a-1}{3}= \omega^m,\;\;\; \Bigg(\frac{3}{\alpha}\Bigg)_3 = \omega^\frac{b}{3}= \omega^n.$$

:::
