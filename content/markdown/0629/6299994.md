# Cable theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cable theory"}

*Image: Cable theory Neuron RC circuit v3.svg.* Figure. 1: Cable theory's simplified view of a neuronal fiber

In [neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscience), classical **cable theory** uses [mathematical models](wikipedia://en/20590) to calculate the [electric current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current) (and accompanying [voltage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage)) along passive[^o36] [neurites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurite), particularly the [dendrites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendrite) that receive [synaptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synapse) inputs at different sites and times. Estimates are made by modeling dendrites and [axons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axons) as cylinders composed of segments with [capacitances](wikipedia://en/140711) $c_m$ and [resistances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_resistance) $r_m$ combined in parallel (see Fig. 1). The capacitance of a neuronal fiber comes about because [electrostatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatic) forces are acting through the very thin lipid bilayer (see Figure 2). The resistance in series along the fiber $r_l$ is due to the [axoplasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axoplasm)'s significant resistance to movement of [electric charge](wikipedia://en/9804).

:::

*Image: NeuronCapacitanceRev.jpg.* Figure. 2: Fiber capacitance

## History {#History}

Cable theory in computational [neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscience) has roots leading back to the 1850s, when Professor [William Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thomson,_1st_Baron_Kelvin) (later known as Lord Kelvin) began developing mathematical models of signal decay in submarine (underwater) telegraphic cables. The models resembled the partial differential equations used by [Fourier](wikipedia://en/25767401) to describe heat conduction in a wire.

The 1870s saw the first attempts by Hermann to model [neuronal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuronal) [electrotonic potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrotonic_potential) also by focusing on analogies with heat conduction. However, it was Hoorweg who first discovered the analogies with Kelvin's undersea cables in 1898 and then Hermann and Cremer who independently developed the cable theory for neuronal fibers in the early 20th century. Further mathematical theories of nerve fiber conduction based on cable theory were developed by Cole and [Hodgkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Lloyd_Hodgkin) (1920s–1930s), Offner et al. (1940), and Rushton (1951).

Experimental evidence for the importance of cable theory in modelling the behavior of [axons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axons) began surfacing in the 1930s from work done by Cole, Curtis, Hodgkin, [Sir Bernard Katz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Bernard_Katz), Rushton, Tasaki and others. Two key papers from this era are those of Davis and [Lorente de Nó](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafael_Lorente_de_Nó) (1947) and Hodgkin and Rushton (1946).

The 1950s saw improvements in techniques for measuring the electric activity of individual [neurons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurons). Thus cable theory became important for analyzing data collected from intracellular microelectrode recordings and for analyzing the electrical properties of neuronal [dendrites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendrites). Scientists like Coombs, [Eccles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Eccles_%28neurophysiologist%29), Fatt, Frank, Fuortes and others now relied heavily on cable theory to obtain functional insights of neurons and for guiding them in the design of new experiments.

Later, cable theory with its mathematical derivatives allowed ever more sophisticated [neuron models](wikipedia://en/14408479) to be explored by workers such as Jack, [Rall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilfrid_Rall), Redman, Rinzel, Idan Segev, Tuckwell, Bell, and Iannella. More recently, cable theory has been applied to model electrical activity in bundled neurons in the white matter of the brain.[^o3]

## Deriving the cable equation {#Deriving_the_cable_equation}

Note, various conventions of *r*<sub>*m*</sub> exist. Here *r*<sub>*m*</sub> and *c*<sub>*m*</sub>, as introduced above, are measured per membrane-length unit (per meter (m)). Thus *r*<sub>*m*</sub> is measured in [ohm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohm)·meters (Ω·m) and *c*<sub>*m*</sub> in [farads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farad) per meter (F/m). This is in contrast to *R*<sub>*m*</sub> (in Ω·m<sup>2</sup>) and *C*<sub>*m*</sub> (in F/m<sup>2</sup>), which represent the specific resistance and capacitance respectively of one unit area of membrane (in m<sup>2</sup>). Thus, if the radius, *a*, of the axon is known,[^o37] then its circumference is 2*πa*, and its *r*<sub>*m*</sub>, and its *c*<sub>*m*</sub> values can be calculated as:

::: {#o5 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$r_m = \frac{R_m}{2 \pi a\ } \tag{1}$$

:::

::: {#o6 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$c_m = C_m 2 \pi a\ \tag{2}$$

:::

These relationships make sense intuitively, because the greater the circumference of the axon, the greater the area for charge to escape through its membrane, and therefore the lower the membrane resistance (dividing *R*<sub>*m*</sub> by 2*πa*); and the more membrane available to store charge (multiplying *C*<sub>*m*</sub> by 2*πa*). The [specific electrical resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_electrical_resistance), *ρ*<sub>*l*</sub>, of the axoplasm allows one to calculate the longitudinal intracellular resistance per unit length, *r*<sub>*l*</sub>, (in Ω·m<sup>−1</sup>) by the equation:

::: {#o7 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$r_l = \frac{\rho_l}{\pi a^2\ } \tag{3}$$

:::

The greater the cross sectional area of the axon, *πa*<sup>2</sup>, the greater the number of paths for the charge to flow through its axoplasm, and the lower the axoplasmic resistance.

Several important avenues of extending classical cable theory have recently seen the introduction of endogenous structures in order to analyze the effects of protein polarization within dendrites and different synaptic input distributions over the dendritic surface of a neuron.

To better understand how the cable equation is derived, first consider an idealized neuron with a perfectly sealed membrane (*r*<sub>*m*</sub>=∞) with no loss of current to the outside, and no capacitance (*c*<sub>*m*</sub> = 0). A current injected into the fiber [^o38] at position *x* = 0 would move along the inside of the fiber unchanged. Moving away from the point of injection and by using [Ohm's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohm's_law) (*V* = *IR*) we can calculate the voltage change as:

::: {#o8 .equation cue=numblk name="4"}

$$\Delta V = -i_l r_l \Delta x\ \tag{4}$$

:::

where the negative is because current flows down the potential gradient.

Letting Δ*x* go towards zero and having infinitely small increments of *x*, one can write ((4)) as:

::: {#o9 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} = -i_l r_l\ \tag{5}$$

:::

or

::: {#o10 .equation cue=numblk name="6"}

$$\frac{1}{r_l} \frac{\partial V}{\partial x} = -i_l\ \tag{6}$$

:::

Bringing *r*<sub>*m*</sub> back into the picture is like making holes in a garden hose. The more holes, the faster the water will escape from the hose, and the less water will travel all the way from the beginning of the hose to the end. Similarly, in an axon, some of the current traveling longitudinally through the axoplasm will escape through the membrane.

If *i*<sub>*m*</sub> is the current escaping through the membrane per length unit, m, then the total current escaping along *y* units must be *y·i*<sub>*m*</sub>. Thus, the change of current in the axoplasm, Δ*i*<sub>*l*</sub>, at distance, Δ*x*, from position *x*=0 can be written as:

::: {#o11 .equation cue=numblk name="7"}

$$\Delta i_l = -i_m \Delta x\ \tag{7}$$

:::

or, using continuous, infinitesimally small increments:

::: {#o12 .equation cue=numblk name="8"}

$$\frac{\partial i_l}{\partial x}=-i_m\ \tag{8}$$

:::

$i_m$ can be expressed with yet another formula, by including the capacitance. The capacitance will cause a flow of charge (a current) towards the membrane on the side of the cytoplasm. This current is usually referred to as displacement current (here denoted $i_c$.) The flow will only take place as long as the membrane's storage capacity has not been reached. $i_c$ can then be expressed as:

::: {#o13 .equation cue=numblk name="9"}

$$i_c = c_m \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\ \tag{9}$$

:::

where $c_m$ is the membrane's capacitance and ${\partial V}/{\partial t}$ is the change in voltage over time. The current that passes the membrane ($i_r$) can be expressed as:

::: {#o14 .equation cue=numblk name="10"}

$$i_r=\frac{V}{r_m} \tag{10}$$

:::

and because $i_m = i_r + i_c$ the following equation for $i_m$ can be derived if no additional current is added from an electrode:

::: {#o15 .equation cue=numblk name="11"}

$$\frac{\partial i_l}{\partial x}=-i_m=-(\frac{V}{r_m}+c_m \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}) \tag{11}$$

:::

where ${\partial i_l}/{\partial x}$ represents the change per unit length of the longitudinal current.

Combining equations ((6)) and ((11)) gives a first version of a cable equation:

::: {#o16 .equation cue=numblk name="12"}

$$\frac{1}{r_l} \frac{\partial ^2 V}{\partial x^2}=c_m \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}+\frac{V}{r_m} \tag{12}$$

:::

which is a second-order [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564) (PDE).

By a simple rearrangement of equation ((12)) (see later) it is possible to make two important terms appear, namely the length constant (sometimes referred to as the space constant) denoted $\lambda$ and the time constant denoted $\tau$. The following sections focus on these terms.

## Length constant {#Length_constant}

The length constant, $\lambda$ (lambda), is a parameter that indicates how far a stationary current will influence the voltage along the cable. The larger the value of $\lambda$, the farther the charge will flow. The length constant can be expressed as:

::: {#o18 .equation cue=numblk name="13"}

$$\lambda = \sqrt \frac{r_m}{r_l} \tag{13}$$

:::

The larger the membrane resistance, *r*<sub>*m*</sub>, the greater the value of $\lambda$, and the more current will remain inside the axoplasm to travel longitudinally through the axon. The higher the axoplasmic resistance, $r_l$, the smaller the value of $\lambda$, the harder it will be for current to travel through the axoplasm, and the shorter the current will be able to travel. It is possible to solve equation ((12)) and arrive at the following equation (which is valid in steady-state conditions, i.e. when time approaches infinity):

::: {#o19 .equation cue=numblk name="14"}

$$V_x = V_0 e^{-\frac{x}{\lambda}} \tag{14}$$

:::

Where $V_0$ is the depolarization at $x=0$ (point of current injection), *e* is the exponential constant (approximate value 2.71828) and $V_x$ is the voltage at a given distance *x* from *x*=0. When $x=\lambda$ then

::: {#o20 .equation cue=numblk name="15"}

$$\frac{x}{\lambda}=1 \tag{15}$$

:::

and

::: {#o21 .equation cue=numblk name="16"}

$$V_x = V_0 e^{-1} \tag{16}$$

:::

which means that when we measure $V$ at distance $\lambda$ from $x=0$ we get

::: {#o22 .equation cue=numblk name="17"}

$$V_\lambda = \frac{V_0}{e} = 0.368 V_0 \tag{17}$$

:::

Thus $V_\lambda$ is always 36.8 percent of $V_0$.

## Time constant {#Time_constant}

Neuroscientists are often interested in knowing how fast the membrane potential, $V_m$, of an axon changes in response to changes in the current injected into the axoplasm. The time constant, $\tau$, is an index that provides information about that value. $\tau$ can be calculated as:

::: {#o24 .equation cue=numblk name="18"}

$$\tau = r_m c_m. \ \tag{18}$$

:::

The larger the membrane capacitance, $c_m$, the more current it takes to charge and discharge a patch of membrane and the longer this process will take. The larger the membrane resistance $r_m$, the harder it is for a current to induce a change in membrane potential. So the higher the $\tau$ the slower the nerve impulse can travel. That means, membrane potential (voltage across the membrane) lags more behind current injections. Response times vary from 1–2 milliseconds in neurons that are processing information that needs high temporal precision to 100 milliseconds or longer. A typical response time is around 20 milliseconds.

## Generic form and mathematical structure {#Generic_form_and_mathematical_structure}

If one multiplies equation ((12)) by $r_m$ on both sides of the equal sign we get:

::: {#o26 .equation cue=numblk name="19"}

$$\frac{r_m}{r_l} \frac{\partial ^2 V}{\partial x^2}=c_m r_m \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}+ V \tag{19}$$

:::

and recognize $\lambda^2 = {r_m}/{r_l}$ on the left side and $\tau = c_m r_m$ on the right side. The cable equation can now be written in its perhaps best known form:

::: {#o27 .equation cue=numblk name="20"}

$$\lambda^2 \frac{\partial ^2 V}{\partial x^2}=\tau \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}+ V \tag{20}$$

:::

This is a 1D [heat equation](wikipedia://en/166415) or [diffusion equation](wikipedia://en/645335) for which many solution methods, such as Green's functions and Fourier methods, have been developed.

It is also a special degenerate case of the [Telegrapher's equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telegrapher's_equation), where the inductance $L$ vanishes and the signal propagation speed $1/\sqrt{LC}$ is infinite.
