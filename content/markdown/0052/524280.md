# Ernest Esclangon

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ernest Esclangon"}

*Image: Esclangon.jpg.* Ernest Esclangon, by [Paul Helbronner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Helbronner), 1930.

**Ernest Benjamin Esclangon** (17 March 1876 – 28 January 1954) was a French [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer) and [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Born in [Mison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mison), Alpes-de-Haute-Provence, in 1895 he started to study mathematics at the [École normale supérieure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_normale_supérieure_%28Paris%29), graduating in 1898. Looking for some means of financial support while he completed his doctorate on [quasi-periodic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-periodic_function), he took a post at the [Bordeaux Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bordeaux_Observatory), teaching some mathematics at the [University of Bordeaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bordeaux).

During [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I), he worked on [ballistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistics) and developed a novel method for precisely locating enemy artillery. When a gun is fired, it initiates a spherical [shock wave](wikipedia://en/146253) but the projectile also generates a conical wave. By using the sound of distant guns to compare the two waves, Escaglon was able to make accurate predictions of gun locations.

After the [armistice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armistice) in 1919,[^o3] Esclangon became director of the [Strasbourg Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strasbourg_Observatory) and professor of astronomy at the [university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Strasbourg) the following year. In 1929, he was appointed director of the [Paris Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Observatory) and of the [International Time Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Time_Bureau), and elected to the [Bureau des Longitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_des_Longitudes) in 1932. He is perhaps best remembered for initiating in 1933 the first *[speaking clock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speaking_clock)* service, reportedly to relieve the observatory staff from the numerous telephone calls requesting the exact time. He was elected to the [Académie des Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Académie_des_Sciences) in 1939.

His doctoral students include [Daniel Barbier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Barbier), [Édmée Chandon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Édmée_Chandon), [Louis Couffignal](wikipedia://en/1836048), [André-Louis Danjon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André-Louis_Danjon), and [Nicolas Stoyko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Stoyko).

Esclangon was the President of the [Société astronomique de France (SAF)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Société_astronomique_de_France), the French astronomical society, from 1933–1935.[^o5]

Serving as director of the Paris Observatory throughout [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) and the [German occupation of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France_during_World_War_II), he retired in 1944. He died in [Eyrenville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyrenville), France.

## Honours {#Honours}

In 1935, he received the [Prix Jules Janssen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prix_Jules_Janssen), the highest award of the Société astronomique de France.

The [binary asteroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_asteroid) [1509 Esclangona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1509_Esclangona) is named after him.

The lunar crater [Esclangon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esclangon_%28crater%29) is named after him.
