# Emirp

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Emirp"}

An **emirp** (pronounced or , an [anadrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anadrome) of *prime*) is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) that results in a different prime when its [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214) digits ([digits](wikipedia://en/280582) in base 10) are reversed. This definition excludes the related [palindromic primes](wikipedia://en/424723). The term ***reversible prime*** is used to mean the same as emirp, but may also, ambiguously, include the palindromic primes.

:::

The first few emirps are

13, 17, 31, 37, 71, 73, 79, 97, 107, 113, 149, 157, 167, 179, 199, ... .

The first few reversible primes are

2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 31, 37, 71, 73, 79, 97, 101, 107, ... .

The difference in all pairs of emirps is always a multiple of 18. This follows from all primes bigger than 2 being odd (making their differences even, i.e. multiples of 2) and from differences between pairs of natural numbers with reversed digits being multiples of 9 (which itself is a consequence of $10^n-1$ being a multiple of 9 for every non-negative integer $n$).

All non-palindromic [permutable primes](wikipedia://en/421593) are emirps.

It is not known whether there are infinitely many emirps. The largest *known* emirp as of April 19, 2026 is 10<sup>111956</sup> - 7 × 10<sup>53855</sup> - 1 by Ryan Propper and Serge Batalov.[^o3] The integer's reverse is 10<sup>111956</sup> - 7 × 10<sup>58100</sup> - 1.

## History {#History}

The term "emirp" was coined by American mathematician [Jeremiah Farrell](wikipedia://en/7079021).

## Other bases {#Other_bases}

Emirps also exist for bases other than 10. For example, 10111<sub>2</sub> = 23 is an emirp in the [binary numeral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_numeral_system) because 23 is a prime and its reverse 11101<sub>2</sub> = 29 is also a prime.

The first few binary emirps are

11, 13, 23, 29, 37, 41, 43, 47, 53, 61, 67, 71, 83, 97, 101, ... .

Dartyge et al. (2023) have shown that, in [big-O notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big-O_notation), the number of *n*-digit reversible primes in binary is $O\left(\frac{2^n}{n^2}\right)$.

They also conjecture that the number of *n*-digit reversible primes in binary is $(3+o(1))\frac{2^{n-1}}{(\log2^n)^2}$ as *n* goes large.[^o11]

## Emirpimes {#Emirpimes}

The term **emirpimes** (singular) is used also in places to treat [semiprimes](wikipedia://en/339542) in a similar way. That is, an emirpimes is a semiprime that is also a (distinct) semiprime upon reversing its digits.

The first few emirpimeses are

15, 26, 39, 49, 51, 58, 62, 85, 93, 94, 115, 122, 123, ... .
