# Internal and external angles

*Image: Internal and external angles.png.* The corresponding internal (teal) and external (magenta) angles of a polygon are supplementary (sum to a half [turn](wikipedia://en/855329)). The external angles of a non-self-intersecting closed polygon always sum to a full turn.

*Image: ExternalAngles.svg.* Internal and external angles

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Internal and external angles"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), an [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) of a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) is formed by two adjacent [sides](wikipedia://en/12158034). For a [simple polygon](wikipedia://en/1059530) (non-self-intersecting), regardless of whether it is [convex or non-convex](wikipedia://en/23621#Convexity_and_non-convexity), this angle is called an **internal angle** (or interior angle) if a point within the angle is in the interior of the polygon. A polygon has exactly one internal angle per [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417).

:::

If every internal angle of a simple polygon is less than a [straight angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_angle) ([$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601) [radians](wikipedia://en/26003) or 180°), then the polygon is called [convex](wikipedia://en/660019).

In contrast, an **external angle** (also called a turning angle or exterior angle) is an angle formed by one side of a simple polygon and a [line extended from an adjacent side](wikipedia://en/43958459).[^o2]

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- The sum of the internal angle and the external angle on the same vertex is $\pi$ radians (180°).
- The sum of all the internal angles of a simple polygon is $\pi(n - 2)$ radians or $180(n - 2)$ degrees, where $n$ is the number of sides. The formula can be proved by using [mathematical induction](wikipedia://en/18881): starting with a triangle, for which the angle sum is 180°, then replacing one side with two sides connected at another vertex, and so on.
- The sum of the external angles of any simple polygon, if only one of the two external angles is assumed at each vertex, is $2\pi$ radians (360°).
- The measure of the exterior angle at a vertex is unaffected by which side is extended: the two exterior angles that can be formed at a vertex by extending alternately one side or the other are [vertical angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_angles) and thus are equal.

:::

## Extension to crossed polygons {#Extension_to_crossed_polygons}

The interior angle concept can be extended in a consistent way to [crossed polygons](wikipedia://en/50055537) such as [star polygons](wikipedia://en/313303) by using the concept of [directed angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_angles). In general, the interior angle sum in degrees of any closed polygon, including crossed (self-intersecting) ones, is then given by $180(n - 2k)^{\circ}$, where $n$ is the number of vertices, and the strictly positive integer $k$ is the number of total (360°) revolutions one undergoes by walking around the [perimeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perimeter_of_the_polygon) of the polygon. In other words, the sum of all the exterior angles is $2\pi k$ radians or $360k$ degrees. Example: for ordinary [convex polygons](wikipedia://en/660019) and [concave polygons](wikipedia://en/605156), $k = 1$, since the exterior angle sum is 360°, and one undergoes only one full revolution by walking around the perimeter.

## Extension to polyhedra {#Extension_to_polyhedra}

Consider a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) that is [topologically equivalent](wikipedia://en/13660) to a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859), such as any [convex polyhedron](wikipedia://en/1817885). Any vertex of the polyhedron will have several [facets](wikipedia://en/7186647) that meet at that vertex. Each of these facets will have an interior angle at that vertex and the sum of the interior angles at a vertex can be said to be the interior angle associated with that vertex of the polyhedron. The value of $2\pi$ radians (or 360 degrees) minus that interior angle can be said to be the exterior angle associated with that vertex, also known by other names such as [angular defect](wikipedia://en/673575). The sum of these exterior angles across all vertices of the polyhedron will necessarily be $4\pi$ radians (or 720 degrees), and the sum of the interior angles will necessarily be $2\pi(n - 2)$ radians (or $360(n - 2)$ degrees) where $n$ is the number of vertices. A proof of this can be obtained by using the formulas for the sum of interior angles of each facet together with the fact that the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) of a sphere is 2.

For example, a [rectangular solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_solid) will have three rectangular facets meeting at any vertex, with each of these facets having a 90° internal angle at that vertex, so each vertex of the rectangular solid is associated with an interior angle of $3 \times 90^{\circ} = 270^{\circ}$ and an exterior angle of $360^{\circ} - 270^{\circ} = 90^{\circ}$. The sum of these exterior angles over all eight vertices is $8 \times 90^{\circ} = 720^{\circ}$. The sum of these interior angles over all eight vertices is $8 \times 270^{\circ} = 2160^{\circ}$.
