# Geostrophic wind

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Geostrophic wind"}

In [atmospheric science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_science), **geostrophic flow** () is the theoretical [wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind) that would result from an exact balance between the [Coriolis force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriolis_effect) and the [pressure gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_gradient) force. This condition is called *[geostrophic equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostrophic_equilibrium)* or *geostrophic balance* (also known as *geostrophy*). The geostrophic wind is directed [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) to [isobars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobar_%28meteorology%29) (lines of constant [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_pressure) at a given height). This balance seldom holds exactly in nature. The true wind almost always differs from the geostrophic wind due to other forces such as [friction](wikipedia://en/11062) from the ground. Thus, the actual wind would equal the geostrophic wind only if there were no friction (e.g. above the [atmospheric boundary layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_boundary_layer)) and the isobars were perfectly straight. The geostrophic wind merely reflects the horizontal pressure gradient as the driving force of atmospheric motion, while the actual near-surface wind requires the synergistic modulation of multiple factors including the frictional force, vertical atmospheric stratification and thermal wind. As a result, the ratio between actual wind speed and geostrophic wind speed, and the wind direction angle difference between the actual wind and the geostrophic wind vary significantly with environmental conditions.[^o2] Despite this, much of the atmosphere outside the [tropics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropics) is close to geostrophic flow much of the time and it is a valuable first approximation. Geostrophic flow in air or water is a zero-frequency [inertial wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_waves).

:::

## Origin {#Origin}

A useful heuristic is to imagine [air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air) starting from rest, experiencing a force directed from areas of high [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) toward areas of low pressure, called the [pressure gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_gradient) force. If the air began to move in response to that force, however, the [Coriolis force](wikipedia://en/7783) would deflect it, to the right of the motion in the [Northern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Hemisphere) or to the left in the [Southern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Hemisphere). As the air accelerated, the deflection would increase until the Coriolis force's strength and direction balanced the pressure gradient force, a state called geostrophic balance. At this point, the flow is no longer moving from high to low pressure, but instead moves along [isobars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobar_%28meteorology%29). Geostrophic balance helps to explain why, in the Northern Hemisphere, [low-pressure systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-pressure_system) (or *[cyclones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclone)*) spin counterclockwise and [high-pressure systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-pressure_area) (or *[anticyclones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticyclone)*) spin clockwise, and the opposite in the Southern Hemisphere.

## Geostrophic currents {#Geostrophic_currents}

Flow of ocean water is also largely geostrophic. Just as multiple weather balloons that measure pressure as a function of height in the atmosphere are used to map the atmospheric pressure field and infer the geostrophic wind, measurements of density as a function of depth in the ocean are used to infer geostrophic currents. [Satellite altimeters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_altimetry) are also used to measure sea surface height [anomaly](wikipedia://en/50376013), which permits a calculation of the geostrophic current at the surface.

## Limitations of the geostrophic approximation {#Limitations_of_the_geostrophic_approximation}

The effect of friction, between the air and the land, breaks the geostrophic balance. Friction slows the flow, lessening the effect of the Coriolis force. As a result, the pressure gradient force has a greater effect and the air still moves from high pressure to low pressure, though with great deflection. This explains why high-pressure system winds radiate out from the center of the system, while low-pressure systems have winds that spiral inwards.

The geostrophic wind neglects [frictional](wikipedia://en/11062) effects, which is usually a good [approximation](wikipedia://en/336271) for the [synoptic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synoptic_scale_meteorology) instantaneous flow in the midlatitude mid-[troposphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troposphere).[^o6] Although [ageostrophic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ageostrophic) terms are relatively small, they are essential for the time evolution of the flow and in particular are necessary for the growth and decay of storms. [Quasigeostrophic](wikipedia://en/35173380) and semi geostrophic theory are used to model flows in the atmosphere more widely. These theories allow for a divergence to take place and for weather systems to then develop.

## Formulation {#Formulation}

[Newton's second law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_second_law) can be written as follows if only the pressure gradient, gravity, and friction act on an air parcel, where bold symbols are vectors:

$${D\boldsymbol{U} \over Dt} = - {1 \over \rho} \nabla p - 2\boldsymbol{\Omega} \times \boldsymbol{U} + \boldsymbol{g} + \boldsymbol{F}_r$$

Here ***U*** is the velocity field of the air, **Ω** is the angular velocity vector of the planet, *ρ* is the density of the air, P is the air pressure, **F**<sub>r</sub> is the friction, **g** is the [acceleration vector due to gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_gravity) and $\tfrac{D}{Dt}$ is the [material derivative](wikipedia://en/668449).

Locally this can be expanded in [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates), with a positive *u* representing an eastward direction and a positive *v* representing a northward direction. Neglecting friction and vertical motion, as justified by the [Taylor–Proudman theorem](wikipedia://en/1024614), we have:

$${Du \over Dt} = -{1 \over \rho}{\partial P \over \partial x} + fv + 0 + 0$$

$${Dv \over Dt} = -{1 \over \rho}{\partial P \over \partial y} - fu + 0 + 0$$

$${D w \over Dt}=-{1 \over \rho}{\partial P \over \partial z}+0-g+0$$

With *f* = 2Ω sin *φ* the [Coriolis parameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriolis_frequency) (approximately × 10<sup>\-4</sup> s<sup>−1</sup>, varying with latitude).

Assuming geostrophic balance, the system is stationary and the first two equations become:

$$fv = {1 \over \rho}{\partial P \over \partial x}$$

$$fu = -{1 \over \rho}{\partial P \over \partial y}$$

$$-g -{1 \over \rho}{\partial P \over \partial z}=0$$

By substituting using the third equation above, we have:

$$\begin{aligned} fv &= \frac{\; -g \;}{\; \frac{\partial P}{\partial z} \;} \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} := \frac{\; -g \;}{\; c \;} a \\[5px] fu &= - \frac{\; -g \;}{\; \frac{\partial P}{\partial z} \;} \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} := + \frac{\; g \;}{\; c \;}b \end{aligned}$$

with *z* the [geopotential height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geopotential_height) of the constant pressure surface, satisfying

$${\rm d}P={\partial P \over \partial x}{\rm d}x + {\partial P \over \partial y}{\rm d} y + {\partial P \over \partial z}{\rm d}z := a{\rm d} x+b{\rm d} y+c{\rm d} z = 0$$

Further simplify those formulae above:

$$\begin{aligned} fv & = \frac{\; -g \;}{\; c \;} a = +g\biggl( \frac{{\rm d } z}{ {\rm d} x}\biggr)_{{\rm d}y=0} \\[5px] fu &= + \frac{\; g \;}{\; c \;}b = -g\biggl(\frac{{\rm d} z}{{\rm d} y}\biggr)_{{\rm d} x=0} \end{aligned}$$

This leads us to the following result for the geostrophic wind components:

$$v_g = {g \over f} {{\rm d}z \over {\rm d} x}$$

$$u_g = - {g \over f} {{\rm d} z \over {\rm d} y}$$

The validity of this approximation depends on the local [Rossby number](wikipedia://en/459426). It is invalid at the equator, because *f* is equal to zero there, and therefore generally not used in the [tropics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropics).

Other variants of the equation are possible; for example, the geostrophic wind vector can be expressed in terms of the gradient of the [geopotential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geopotential_height) Φ on a surface of constant pressure:

$$\mathbf{V}_\mathrm{g} = \frac\hat\mathbf{k}{f} \times \nabla_p \Phi$$
