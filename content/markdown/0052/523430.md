# Constructible polygon

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Constructible polygon"}

*Image: Pentagon construct.gif.* Construction of a regular pentagon

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **constructible polygon** is a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) that can be [constructed with compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge_constructions). For example, a regular [pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014) is constructible with compass and straightedge while a regular [heptagon](wikipedia://en/365349) is not. There are infinitely many constructible polygons, but only 31 with an [odd number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_number) of sides are known.

:::

## Conditions for constructibility {#Conditions_for_constructibility}

*Image: Constructible polygon set.svg.* Number of sides of known constructible polygons having up to 1000 sides (bold) or odd side count (red)

*Image: HeptadecagonConstructionAni.gif.* Construction of the regular 17-gon

Some regular polygons are easy to construct with compass and straightedge; others are not. The [ancient Greek mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) knew how to construct a regular polygon with 3, 4, or 5 sides,[^o3] and they knew how to construct a regular polygon with double the number of sides of a given regular polygon.[^o3] This led to the question being posed: is it possible to construct *all* regular polygons with compass and straightedge? If not, which *n*-gons (that is, [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621) with *n* edges) are constructible and which are not?

[Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) proved the constructibility of the regular [17-gon](wikipedia://en/196890) in 1796. Five years later, he developed the theory of [Gaussian periods](wikipedia://en/456085) in his *[Disquisitiones Arithmeticae](wikipedia://en/736713)*. This theory allowed him to formulate a [sufficient condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufficient_condition) for the constructibility of regular polygons. Gauss stated without proof that this condition was also [necessary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_condition),[^o4] but never published his proof.

A full proof of necessity was given by [Pierre Wantzel](wikipedia://en/2610953) in 1837. The result is known as the **Gauss–Wantzel theorem**: A regular *n*-gon can be constructed with compass and straightedge if and only if *n* is the product of a [power of 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_of_2) and any number of distinct (unequal) [Fermat primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_prime). Here, a power of 2 is a number of the form $2^m$, where *m* ≥ 0 is an integer. A Fermat prime is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) of the form $2^{(2^m)} + 1$, where *m* ≥ 0 is an integer. The number of Fermat primes involved can be 0, in which case *n* is a power of 2.

In order to reduce a [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) problem to a problem of pure [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), the proof uses the fact that a regular *n*-gon is constructible if and only if the [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine) $\cos(2\pi/n)$ is a [constructible number](wikipedia://en/7439)—that is, can be written in terms of the four basic arithmetic operations and the extraction of [square roots](wikipedia://en/29208). Equivalently, a regular *n*-gon is constructible if any [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) of the *n*th [cyclotomic polynomial](wikipedia://en/171992) is constructible.

### Detailed results by Gauss's theory {#Detailed_results_by_Gauss's_theory}

Restating the Gauss–Wantzel theorem:

A regular *n*-gon is constructible with straightedge and compass if and only if *n* = 2<sup>*k*</sup>*p*<sub>1</sub>*p*<sub>2</sub>...*p*<sub>*t*</sub> where *k* and *t* are non-negative [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), and the *p*<sub>*i*</sub>'s (when *t* > 0) are distinct Fermat primes.

The five known [Fermat primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_primes) are:

*F*<sub>0</sub> = 3, *F*<sub>1</sub> = 5, *F*<sub>2</sub> = 17, *F*<sub>3</sub> = 257, and *F*<sub>4</sub> = 65537 .

Since there are 31 nonempty subsets of the five known Fermat primes, there are 31 known constructible polygons with an odd number of sides.

The next twenty-eight Fermat numbers, *F*<sub>5</sub> through *F*<sub>32</sub>, are known to be [composite](wikipedia://en/82289).[^o6]

Thus a regular *n*-gon is constructible if

*n* = [3](wikipedia://en/173285), [4](wikipedia://en/659939), [5](wikipedia://en/20741014), [6](wikipedia://en/59733), [8](wikipedia://en/314575), [10](wikipedia://en/327393), [12](wikipedia://en/508486), [15](wikipedia://en/1496229), [16](wikipedia://en/6440317), [17](wikipedia://en/196890), [20](wikipedia://en/445032), [24](wikipedia://en/24082273), [30](wikipedia://en/927478), 32, 34, 40, 48, 51, 60, 64, 68, 80, 85, 96, 102, 120, 128, 136, 160, 170, 192, 204, 240, 255, 256, [257](wikipedia://en/5024265), 272, 320, 340, 384, 408, 480, 510, 512, 514, 544, 640, 680, 768, 771, 816, 960, 1020, 1024, 1028, 1088, 1280, 1285, 1360, 1536, 1542, 1632, 1920, 2040, 2048, ... ,

while a regular *n*-gon is not constructible with compass and straightedge if

*n* = [7](wikipedia://en/365349), [9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enneagon), [11](wikipedia://en/614840), [13](wikipedia://en/654601), [14](wikipedia://en/6058229), [18](wikipedia://en/8728724), 19, 21, 22, [23](wikipedia://en/54460134), 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 31, 33, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 49, 50, 52, 53, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59, 61, 62, 63, 65, 66, 67, 69, 70, 71, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76, 77, 78, 79, 81, 82, 83, 84, 86, 87, 88, 89, 90, 91, 92, 93, 94, 95, 97, 98, 99, 100, 101, 103, 104, 105, 106, 107, 108, 109, 110, 111, 112, 113, 114, 115, 116, 117, 118, 119, 121, 122, 123, 124, 125, 126, 127, ... .

### Connection to Pascal's triangle {#Connection_to_Pascal's_triangle}

Since there are five known Fermat primes, we know of 31 numbers that are products of distinct Fermat primes, and hence we know of 31 constructible odd-sided regular polygons. These are 3, 5, 15, 17, 51, 85, 255, 257, 771, 1285, 3855, 4369, 13107, 21845, 65535, [65537](wikipedia://en/5024281), 196611, 327685, 983055, 1114129, 3342387, 5570645, 16711935, 16843009, 50529027, 84215045, 252645135, 286331153, 858993459, 1431655765, 4294967295 . As [John Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Conway) commented in *The Book of Numbers*, these numbers, when written in [binary](wikipedia://en/238686), are equal to the first 32 rows of the [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087)-2 [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497), minus the top row, which corresponds to a [monogon](wikipedia://en/5483767). (Because of this, the *1*s in such a list form an approximation to the [Sierpiński triangle](wikipedia://en/29638).) This pattern breaks down after this, as the next Fermat number is composite (4294967297 = 641 × 6700417), so the following rows do not correspond to constructible polygons. It is unknown whether any more Fermat primes exist, and it is therefore unknown how many odd-sided constructible regular polygons exist. In general, if there are *q* Fermat primes, then there are 2<sup>*q*</sup>−1 odd-sided regular constructible polygons.

## General theory {#General_theory}

In the light of later work on [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316), the principles of these proofs have been clarified. It is straightforward to show from [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202) that constructible lengths must come from base lengths by the solution of some sequence of [quadratic equations](wikipedia://en/25175).[^o9] In terms of [field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_theory_%28mathematics%29), such lengths must be contained in a [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) generated by a tower of [quadratic extensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_extension). It follows that a field generated by constructions will always have [degree](wikipedia://en/4382697) over the base field that is a power of two.

In the specific case of a regular *n*-gon, the question reduces to the question of [constructing a length](wikipedia://en/7439)

cos $\tfrac{2\pi}{n}$ ,

which is a [trigonometric number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_number) and hence an [algebraic number](wikipedia://en/1158). This number lies in the *n*-th [cyclotomic field](wikipedia://en/30872462) — and in fact in its [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [subfield](wikipedia://en/11634), which is a [totally real field](wikipedia://en/312853) and a [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamel_dimension)

$\tfrac{1}{2}$ φ(*n*),

where φ(*n*) is [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452). Wantzel's result comes down to a calculation showing that φ(*n*) is a power of 2 precisely in the cases specified.

As for the construction of Gauss, when the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) is a 2-group it follows that it has a sequence of [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) of orders

1, 2, 4, 8, ...

that are nested, each in the next (a [composition series](wikipedia://en/297493), in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) terminology), something simple to prove by [induction](wikipedia://en/18881) in this case of an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974). Therefore, there are subfields nested inside the cyclotomic field, each of degree 2 over the one before. Generators for each such field can be written down by [Gaussian period](wikipedia://en/456085) theory. For example, for [*n* = 17](wikipedia://en/196890) there is a period that is a sum of eight [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity), one that is a sum of four roots of unity, and one that is the sum of two, which is

cos $\tfrac{2\pi}{17}$ .

Each of those is a root of a [quadratic equation](wikipedia://en/25175) in terms of the one before. Moreover, these equations have [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) rather than [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) roots, so in principle can be solved by geometric construction: this is because the work all goes on inside a totally real field.

In this way the result of Gauss can be understood in current terms; for actual calculation of the equations to be solved, the periods can be squared and compared with the 'lower' periods, in a quite feasible algorithm.

## Compass and straightedge constructions {#Compass_and_straightedge_constructions}

[Compass and straightedge constructions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge_construction) are known for all known constructible polygons. If *n* = *pq* with *p* = 2 or *p* and *q* [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime), an *n*-gon can be constructed from a *p*-gon and a *q*-gon.

- If *p* = 2, draw a *q*-gon and [bisect](wikipedia://en/152547) one of its central angles. From this, a 2*q*-gon can be constructed.
- If *p* > 2, inscribe a *p*-gon and a *q*-gon in the same circle in such a way that they share a vertex. Because *p* and *q* are coprime, there exist integers *a* and *b* such that *ap* + *bq* = 1. Then 2*a*π/*q* + 2*b*π/*p* = 2π/*pq*. From this, a *pq*-gon can be constructed.

Thus one only has to find a compass and straightedge construction for *n*-gons where *n* is a Fermat prime.

- The construction for an equilateral [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) is simple and has been known since [antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_history); see [Equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285).
- Constructions for the regular [pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014) were described both by [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) (*[Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)*, ca. 300 BC), and by [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979) (*[Almagest](wikipedia://en/148060)*, ca. 150 AD).
- Although Gauss *proved* that the regular [17-gon](wikipedia://en/196890) is constructible, he did not actually *show* how to do it. The first construction is due to Erchinger, a few years after Gauss's work.
- The first explicit constructions of a regular [257-gon](wikipedia://en/5024265) were given by [Magnus Georg Paucker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnus_Georg_Paucker) (1822)[^o11] and [Friedrich Julius Richelot](wikipedia://en/20346321) (1832).[^o12]
- A construction for a regular [65537-gon](wikipedia://en/5024281) was first given by [Johann Gustav Hermes](wikipedia://en/10448726) (1894). The construction is very complex; Hermes spent 10 years completing the 200-page manuscript.[^o13]

## Other constructions {#Other_constructions}

The concept of constructibility as discussed in this article applies specifically to [compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge) constructions. More constructions become possible if other tools are allowed. The so-called [neusis constructions](wikipedia://en/3752339), for example, make use of a *marked* ruler. The constructions are a mathematical idealization and are assumed to be done exactly.

A regular polygon with *n* sides can be constructed with ruler, compass, and [angle trisector](wikipedia://en/91111) if and only if $n=2^r3^sp_1p_2\cdots p_k,$ where *r, s, k* ≥ 0 and where the *p*<sub>*i*</sub> are distinct [Pierpont primes](wikipedia://en/2595060) greater than 3 (primes of the form $2^t3^u +1).$[^o16] These polygons are exactly the regular polygons that can be constructed with [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673), and the regular polygons that can be constructed with [paper folding](wikipedia://en/232840).
