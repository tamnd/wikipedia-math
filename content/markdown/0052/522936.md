# Fallacy of division

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fallacy of division"}

The **fallacy of division**[^o2] is an [informal fallacy](wikipedia://en/1655450) that occurs when one reasons that something true for a whole must also be true of all or some of its parts.

:::

For example:

1. The second grade in Jefferson Elementary eats a lot of ice cream.
1. Carlos is a second-grader in Jefferson Elementary.
1. Therefore, Carlos eats a lot of ice cream.

The converse of this [fallacy](wikipedia://en/53986) is the [fallacy of composition](wikipedia://en/523043), which arises when one fallaciously attributes a property of some part of a thing to the thing as a whole.

If a system as a whole has some property that none of its constituents has (or perhaps, it has it but not as a *result* of some constituents having that property), this is sometimes called an *[emergent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergence)* property of the system.

The term *mereological fallacy* refers to approximately the same incorrect inference that properties of a whole are also properties of its parts.[^o3][^o4][^o5][^o6]

## History {#History}

Both the fallacy of division and the [fallacy of composition](wikipedia://en/523043) were addressed by [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) in *[Sophistical Refutations](wikipedia://en/1692779)*.

In the philosophy of the ancient Greek [Anaxagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaxagoras), as claimed by the Roman [atomist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomist) [Lucretius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucretius),[^o8] it was assumed that the atoms constituting a substance must themselves have the salient observed properties of that substance: so atoms of water would be wet, atoms of iron would be hard, atoms of wool would be soft, etc. This doctrine is called *[homoeomeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homoeomeria_%28philosophy%29)*, and it depends on the fallacy of division.

## Examples in statistics {#Examples_in_statistics}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), an [ecological fallacy](wikipedia://en/475598) is a logical fallacy in the interpretation of statistical data where inferences about the nature of individuals are deduced from inference for the group to which those individuals belong. The four common statistical ecological fallacies are: confusion between ecological correlations and individual correlations, confusion between group average and total average, [Simpson's paradox](wikipedia://en/46096), and other statistical methods.[^o10]
