# Ring of sets

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ring of sets"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), there are two different notions of a **ring of sets**, both referring to certain [families of sets](wikipedia://en/723043).

:::

In [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), a nonempty [family of sets](wikipedia://en/723043) $\mathcal{R}$ is called a ring (of sets) if it is [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under [union](wikipedia://en/47949) and [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429).[^o2] That is, the following two statements are true for all sets $A$ and $B$,

1. $A,B\in\mathcal{R}$ implies $A \cup B \in \mathcal{R}$ and
1. $A,B\in\mathcal{R}$ implies $A \cap B \in \mathcal{R}.$

In [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory), a nonempty family of sets $\mathcal{R}$ is called a ring (of sets) if it is closed under union and [relative complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_complement) (set-theoretic difference).[^o3] That is, the following two statements are true for all sets $A$ and $B$,

1. $A, B \in \mathcal{R}$ implies $A \cup B \in \mathcal{R}$ and
1. $A, B \in \mathcal{R}$ implies $A \setminus B \in \mathcal{R}.$

This implies that a ring in the measure-theoretic sense always contains the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566). Furthermore, for all sets $A$ and $B$,

$$A\cap B=A\setminus(A\setminus B),$$

which shows that a family of sets closed under relative complement is also closed under intersection, so that a ring in the measure-theoretic sense is also a ring in the order-theoretic sense.

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

If $X$ is any set, then the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of $X$ (the family of all subsets of $X$) forms a ring of sets in either sense.

If $(X, \le)$ is a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572), then its [upper sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_set) (the subsets of $X$ with the additional property that if $x$ belongs to an upper set *U* and $x \le y$, then $y$ must also belong to $U$) are closed under both intersections and unions. However, in general it will not be closed under differences of sets.

The [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) and [closed sets](wikipedia://en/47279) of any [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) are closed under both unions and intersections.[^o2]

On the real line $\mathbf{R}$, the family of sets consisting of the empty set and all finite unions of half-open intervals of the form *a*, *b*, with $a, b \in \mathbf{R}$ is a ring in the measure-theoretic sense.

If $T$ is any transformation defined on a space, then the sets that are mapped into themselves by $T$ are closed under both unions and intersections.[^o2]

If two rings of sets are both defined on the same elements, then the sets that belong to both rings themselves form a ring of sets.[^o2]

:::

## Related structures {#Related_structures}

A ring of sets in the order-theoretic sense forms a [distributive lattice](wikipedia://en/310883) in which the intersection and union operations correspond to the lattice's [meet and join](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meet_and_join) operations, respectively. Conversely, every distributive lattice is isomorphic to a ring of sets; in the case of [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) distributive lattices, this is [Birkhoff's representation theorem](wikipedia://en/20489385) and the sets may be taken as the lower sets of a partially ordered set.[^o2]

A family of sets closed under union and relative complement is also closed under [symmetric difference](wikipedia://en/262288) and intersection. Conversely, every family of sets closed under both symmetric difference and intersection is also closed under union and relative complement. This is due to the identities

1. $A \cup B = (A\, \triangle\, B)\, \triangle\, (A \cap B)$ and
1. $$A \setminus B = A\, \triangle\, (A \cap B).$$

Symmetric difference and intersection together give a ring in the measure-theoretic sense the structure of a [boolean ring](wikipedia://en/54356).

In the measure-theoretic sense, a [σ-ring](wikipedia://en/6928351) is a ring closed under countable unions, and a [δ-ring](wikipedia://en/6939387) is a ring closed under countable intersections. Explicitly, a σ-ring over $X$ is a set $\mathcal{F}$ such that for any sequence $\{A_k\}_{k=1}^\infty \subseteq \mathcal{F},$ we have $\bigcup_{k=1}^\infty A_k \in \mathcal{F}.$

Given a set $X,$ a [field of sets](wikipedia://en/1018676) − also called an algebra over $X$ − is a ring that contains $X.$ This definition entails that an algebra is closed under absolute complement $A^c = X \setminus A.$ A [σ-algebra](wikipedia://en/29586) is an algebra that is also closed under countable unions, or equivalently a σ-ring that contains $X.$ In fact, by [de Morgan's laws](wikipedia://en/64669), a δ-ring that contains $X$ is necessarily a σ-algebra as well. Fields of sets, and especially σ-algebras, are central to the modern theory of [probability](wikipedia://en/23542) and the definition of [measures](wikipedia://en/19873).

A **semiring (of sets)** is a family of sets $\mathcal{S}$ with the properties

$$\varnothing \in \mathcal{S},$$

If (3) holds, then $\varnothing \in \mathcal{S}$ if and only if $\mathcal{S} \neq \varnothing.$ $A, B \in \mathcal{S}$ implies $A \cap B \in \mathcal{S},$ and $A, B \in \mathcal{S}$ implies $A \setminus B = \bigcup_{i=1}^n C_i$ for some disjoint $C_1, \ldots, C_n \in \mathcal{S}.$

Every ring (in the measure theory sense) is a semi-ring. On the other hand, $\mathcal{S} := \{\emptyset,\{x\},\{y\}\}$ on $X = \{x,y\}$ is a semi-ring but not a ring, since it is not closed under unions.

A **semialgebra**[^n1] or **elementary family** [^n2] is a collection $\mathcal{S}$ of subsets of $X$ satisfying the semiring properties except with (3) replaced with:

- If $E \in \mathcal{S}$ then there exists a finite number of mutually [disjoint sets](wikipedia://en/39359) $C_1, \ldots, C_n \in \mathcal{S}$ such that $X \setminus E = \bigcup_{i=1}^n C_i.$

This condition is stronger than (3), which can be seen as follows. If $\mathcal{S}$ is a semialgebra and $E, F \in \mathcal{S}$, then we can write $F^c = F_1 \cup \ldots \cup F_n$ for disjoint $F_i \in S$. Then:

$$E \setminus F = E \cap F^c = E \cap (F_1 \cup \ldots \cup F_n) = (E \cap F_1) \cup \ldots \cup (E \cap F_n)$$

and every $E \cap F_i \in S$ since it is closed under intersection, and disjoint since they are contained in the disjoint $F_i$'s. Moreover the condition is *strictly* stronger: any $S$ that is both a ring and a semialgebra is an algebra, hence any ring that is not an algebra is also not a semialgebra (e.g. the collection of finite sets on an infinite set $X$).
