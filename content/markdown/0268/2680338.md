# Jean Paul de Gua de Malves

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Jean Paul de Gua de Malves"}

**Jean Paul de Gua de Malves** (1713 – June 2, 1785[^o2]), known as **Gua**, was a French [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902)

:::

Born at [Malves-en-Minervois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malves-en-Minervois) (Aude), Gua was the son of Jean de Gua, baron de Malves, and his wife Jeanne de Harrugue. His father was ruined by the speculations of [John Law's Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Law's_Company) and had to sell his estates. Gua became a priest and mathematician.

In 1740, he published a work on [analytical geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_geometry) in which he applied geometry, without the aid of [differential calculus](wikipedia://en/50416), to find the [tangents](wikipedia://en/31482), [asymptotes](wikipedia://en/3107), and various [singular points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity) of an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260).

*Image: Gua de Malves - Usages de l'analyse de Descartes, 1740 - BEIC 1460763.jpg.* *Usages de l'analyse de Descartes*, 1740

Gua further showed how singular points and [isolated loops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isolated_loop) were affected by [conical projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conical_projection). He gave the proof of [Descartes's rule of signs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes's_rule_of_signs) which is to be found in most modern works. It is not clear whether Descartes ever proved it strictly, and [Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) seems to have regarded it as obvious.

Gua was acquainted with many of the French philosophes during the last decades of the [Ancien Régime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancien_Régime). He was an early, short-lived, participant, then editor (later replaced by [Diderot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diderot)) of the project that ended up as the *[Encyclopédie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopédie)*. [Condorcet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet) claimed that it was in fact Gua who recruited Diderot to the project, though this claim has never been verified. In either case, Jean-Paul and [Jean le Rond d'Alembert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_le_Rond_d'Alembert), also thought to have been recruited by the de Gua, first show up on the December 1746 payroll of the publishers who were backing the Encyclopédie project. Diderot was added just weeks later and took over as editor on 16 October 1747.

He was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society) in 1743.[^o3]

At the funeral in Paris of the "profound geometrician", as Diderot called Gua, the eulogy was given by Condorcet.
