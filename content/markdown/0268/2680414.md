# Curvature invariant

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Curvature invariant"}

In [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243) and [pseudo-Riemannian geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Riemannian_geometry), **curvature invariants** are [scalar](wikipedia://en/3588331) quantities constructed from tensors that describe the [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) of a space or spacetime. Because they are [scalars](wikipedia://en/3588331), their values do not depend on the coordinate system used, making them useful for comparing the intrinsic curvature of different geometries. These tensors are usually the [Riemann tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_tensor), the [Weyl tensor](wikipedia://en/1026848), the [Ricci tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_tensor) and tensors formed from these by the operations of taking [dual](wikipedia://en/609737) [contractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contraction_%28geometry%29) and [covariant differentiations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariant_differentiation).

:::

## Types of curvature invariants {#Types_of_curvature_invariants}

The invariants most often considered are *polynomial invariants*. These are polynomials constructed from contractions such as traces. Second degree examples are called *quadratic invariants*, and so forth. Invariants constructed using covariant derivatives up to order n are called n-th order *differential invariants*.

The Riemann tensor is a [multilinear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multilinear_operator) of fourth rank acting on [tangent vectors](wikipedia://en/191999). However, it can also be considered a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) acting on [bivectors](wikipedia://en/884040), and as such it has a [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268), whose coefficients and roots ([eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue)) are polynomial scalar invariants.

## Physical applications {#Physical_applications}

In [metric theories of gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_theory_of_gravitation) such as [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), curvature scalars play an important role in telling distinct spacetimes apart.

Two of the most basic curvature invariants in general relativity are the [Kretschmann scalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curvature_invariant_%28general_relativity%29#Principal_invariants)

$$R_{abcd} \, R^{abcd}$$

and the *Chern–Pontryagin scalar*,

$$R_{abcd} \, {{}^\star \!R}^{abcd}$$

These are analogous to two familiar quadratic invariants of the [electromagnetic field tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field_tensor) in classical electromagnetism.

An important unsolved problem in general relativity is to give a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) (and any [syzygies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syzygy_%28mathematics%29)) for the zero-th order invariants of the Riemann tensor.

They have limitations because many distinct spacetimes cannot be distinguished on this basis. In particular, so called [VSI spacetimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VSI_spacetime) (including pp-waves as well as some other Petrov type N and III spacetimes) cannot be distinguished from [Minkowski spacetime](wikipedia://en/230488) using any number of polynomial curvature invariants (of any order).
