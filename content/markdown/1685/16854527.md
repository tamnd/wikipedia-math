# Separoid

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Separoid"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **separoid** is a [binary relation](wikipedia://en/3931) between [disjoint sets](wikipedia://en/39359) which is stable as an [ideal](wikipedia://en/530559) in the canonical order induced by [inclusion](wikipedia://en/27631). Many mathematical objects which appear to be quite different, find a common generalisation in the framework of separoids; e.g., [graphs](wikipedia://en/325806), configurations of [convex sets](wikipedia://en/6292), [oriented matroids](wikipedia://en/24338041), and [polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytopes). Any countable [category](wikipedia://en/53759) is an induced subcategory of separoids when they are endowed with [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13564)[^o2] (viz., mappings that preserve the so-called *[minimal Radon partitions](wikipedia://en/1406385)*).

:::

In this general framework, some results and invariants of different categories turn out to be special cases of the same aspect; e.g., the pseudoachromatic number from graph theory and the [Tverberg theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tverberg_theorem) from combinatorial convexity are simply two faces of the same aspect, namely, complete colouring of separoids.

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/axiom cue=named-heading name="The axioms"}

## The axioms {#The_axioms}

A **separoid**[^o5] is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $S$ endowed with a binary relation $\mid\ \subseteq2^S\times2^S$ on its [power set](wikipedia://en/23799), which satisfies the following simple properties for $A,B\subseteq S$:

$$A\mid B\Leftrightarrow B\mid A,$$

$$A\mid B\Rightarrow A\cap B=\varnothing,$$

$$A\mid B \hbox{ and } A'\subset A\Rightarrow A'\mid B.$$

A related pair $A\mid B$ is called a **separation** and we often say that *A is separated from B*. It is enough to know the *maximal* separations to reconstruct the separoid.

A [mapping](wikipedia://en/516931) $\varphi\colon S\to T$ is a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) of separoids if the preimages of separations are separations; that is, for $A,B\subseteq T$

$$A\mid B\Rightarrow\varphi^{-1}(A)\mid\varphi^{-1}(B).$$

:::

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Examples of separoids can be found in almost every branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831).[^o8][^o9][^o10] Here we list just a few.

\1. Given a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) G=(V,E), we can define a separoid on its [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) by saying that two (disjoint) subsets of V, say A and B, are separated if there are no [edges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_%28graph_theory%29) going from one to the other; i.e.,

$$A\mid B\Leftrightarrow\forall a\in A\hbox{ and }b\in B\colon ab\not\in E.$$

\2. Given an oriented matroid[^o10] *M* = (*E*,*T*), given in terms of its topes *T*, we can define a separoid on *E* by saying that two subsets are separated if they are contained in opposite signs of a tope. In other words, the topes of an oriented matroid are the *maximal* separations of a separoid. This example includes, of course, all [directed graphs](wikipedia://en/19721986).

\3. Given a family of objects in a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), we can define a separoid in it by saying that two subsets are separated if there exists a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) that *separates* them; i.e., leaving them in the two opposite sides of it.

\4. Given a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), we can define a separoid saying that two subsets are separated if there exist two disjoint [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) which contains them (one for each of them).

:::

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=named-heading name="The basic lemma"}

## The basic lemma {#The_basic_lemma}

Every separoid can be represented with a family of convex sets in some Euclidean space and their separations by hyperplanes.

:::
