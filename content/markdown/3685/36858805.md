# Edmond Halley

*Image: Edmond Halley 072.jpg.* Portrait of Halley by [Richard Phillips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Phillips_%28English_painter%29)

::: {#o4 .subject cue=lead name="Edmond Halley"}

**Edmond**[^o81] (or **Edmund**)[^o5] **Halley** (;[^o6][^o7] 8 November – 25 January)[^o8][^o9] was an English astronomer, mathematician and physicist. He was the second [Astronomer Royal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer_Royal) in Britain, succeeding [John Flamsteed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Flamsteed) in 1720.

:::

From an observatory he constructed on [Saint Helena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Helena) in 1676–77, Halley catalogued the [southern celestial hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_celestial_hemisphere) and recorded a [transit of Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transit_of_Mercury) across the Sun. He realised that a similar [transit of Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transit_of_Venus) could be used to determine the distances between Earth, Venus, and the Sun. Upon his return to England, he was made a [fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society), and with the help of King [Charles II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_II_of_England), was granted a master's degree from [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford).

Halley encouraged and helped fund the publication of [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627)'s influential *[Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/48781)* (1687). From observations Halley made in September 1682, he used [Newton's law of universal gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation) to compute the periodicity of [Halley's Comet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halley's_Comet) in his 1705 *Synopsis of the Astronomy of Comets*. It was named after him upon its predicted return in 1758, which he did not live to see.

Beginning in 1698, Halley made sailing expeditions and made observations on the conditions of terrestrial [magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism). In 1718, he discovered the [proper motion](wikipedia://en/52015) of the "[fixed stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_stars)".[^n1]

## Early life {#Early_life}

Halley was born in [Haggerston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haggerston) in [Middlesex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middlesex). His father, Edmond Halley Sr., came from a [Derbyshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derbyshire) family and was a wealthy soap-maker in London. Halley showed an early interest in mathematics. He studied at [St Paul's School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Paul's_School,_London),[^o12] where he developed an interest in astronomy and was elected captain of the school in 1671.[^n2] The following year, his mother, Anne (probably Robinson) died.[^o2] In July 1673,[^n2] Halley entered [The Queen's College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Queen's_College,_Oxford).[^o12] He took a 24 ft long telescope with him, apparently paid for by his father.[^n3] While still an undergraduate, Halley published papers on the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System) and [sunspots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunspots).[^o57] In March 1675, he wrote to [John Flamsteed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Flamsteed), the first [Astronomer Royal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer_Royal), telling him that the leading published tables on the positions of [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter) and [Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn) were erroneous, as were some of [Tycho Brahe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tycho_Brahe)'s star positions. His association with Flamsteed soon led to Halley's first publication and to his expedition to [Saint Helena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Helena).[^n4]

## Career {#Career}

### Publications and inventions {#Publications_and_inventions}

*Image: Halley's Observatory 2020 cropped.jpg.* Site of Halley's Observatory on the island of [Saint Helena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Helena) in the [South Atlantic Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Atlantic_Ocean)

In 1676, Flamsteed helped Halley publish his first paper, titled "A Direct and Geometrical Method of Finding the Aphelia, Eccentricities, and Proportions of the Primary Planets, Without Supposing Equality in Angular Motion", on planetary [orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit), in *[Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Transactions_of_the_Royal_Society)*.[^n4] Influenced by Flamsteed's project to compile a catalogue of stars of the [northern celestial hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_celestial_hemisphere), Halley proposed to do the same for the [southern sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_sky).[^o20] He left Oxford to undertake the project, chosing the South Atlantic island of [Saint Helena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Helena), from which he could observe southern stars as well as some northern stars for comparison.[^n5] King [Charles II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_II_of_England) supported the expedition.[^o22]

Halley sailed to Saint Helena in late 1676 and established an observatory with a large [sextant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sextant) with telescopic sights. Over the following year, he made observations for what became the first telescopic catalogue of the southern sky,[^o23] and observed a [transit of Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transit_of_Mercury) across the Sun. Focusing the Mercury transit, Halley recognised that observations of planetary [solar parallax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_parallax), especially during a [transit of Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transit_of_Venus), could be used to determine [trigonometrically](wikipedia://en/18717261) the distances between Earth, Venus and the Sun.[^o24]

*Image: Royal Society (Great Britain)-8.jpg.* First page to volume I of *Miscellanea curiosa* published by the Royal Society (1705), in which Halley wrote "An estimate of the quantity of vapours raised out of the sea, derived from experiment"

Halley returned to England in May 1678, and used his data to produce a [map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_chart) of the southern stars.[^o25] Oxford would not allow Halley to return because he had violated his residency requirements when he left for Saint Helena. He appealed to Charles II, who signed a letter requesting that Halley be unconditionally awarded his [Master of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts) degree, which the college granted on 3 December 1678.[^n6] Just a few days before,[^o27] Halley had been elected as a [fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society), at the age of 22.[^o28] In 1679, he published *Catalogus Stellarum Australium* ('A catalogue of the stars of the South'), which includes his map and descriptions of 341 stars.[^o25][^n7] [Robert Hooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hooke) presented the catalogue to the Royal Society.[^n8] In mid-1679, Halley went to Danzig ([Gdańsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gdańsk)) on behalf of the society to help resolve a dispute: because astronomer [Johannes Hevelius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Hevelius)' observing instruments were not equipped with [telescopic sights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescopic_sight), Flamsteed and Hooke had questioned the accuracy of his observations; Halley stayed with Hevelius and checked his observations, finding that they were quite precise.[^o31]

By 1681, [Giovanni Domenico Cassini](wikipedia://en/5215428) had told Halley of his theory that comets were objects in orbit.[^n9] In September 1682, Halley carried out a series of observations of what became known as [Halley's Comet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halley's_Comet); his name became associated with it because of his work on its orbit and predicting its return in 1758[^o33] (which he did not live to see). In early 1686, Halley was elected to the Royal Society's new position of secretary, requiring him to give up his fellowship and manage correspondence and meetings, as well as edit the *Philosophical Transactions*.[^n10] Also in 1686, Halley published the second part of the results from his Helenian expedition, being a paper and chart on [trade winds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_wind) and [monsoons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monsoon). The symbols he used to represent trailing winds still exist in most modern day weather chart representations. In this article he identified solar heating as the cause of [atmospheric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth) motions. He also established the relationship between [barometric pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barometer) and height above sea level. His charts were an important contribution to the emerging field of [information visualisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_visualisation).[^o35]

Halley spent most of his time on lunar observations, but was also interested in the problems of [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity). One problem that attracted his attention was the proof of [Kepler's laws of planetary motion](wikipedia://en/17553). In August 1684, he went to [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge) to discuss this with [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627), much as John Flamsteed had done four years earlier, only to find that Newton had solved the problem, at the instigation of Flamsteed with regard to the orbit of [Kirch's Comet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirch's_Comet), without publishing the solution.[^o36] Halley asked to see the calculations and was told by Newton that he could not find them, but promised to redo them and send them on later, which he eventually did, in a short treatise titled *[On the motion of bodies in an orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_motu_corporum_in_gyrum)*. Halley recognised the importance of the work and returned to Cambridge to arrange its publication with Newton, who instead went on to expand it into his *[Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/48781)* published at Halley's expense in 1687.[^o37] Halley's first calculations with comets were thereby for the orbit of Kirch's Comet, based on Flamsteed's observations in 1680–1681. Although he was to accurately calculate the orbit of the comet of 1682, he was inaccurate in his calculations of the orbit of Kirch's Comet. They indicated a periodicity of 575 years, thus appearing in the years 531 and 1106, and presumably heralding [the death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination_of_Julius_Caesar) of [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar) in a like fashion in 45 BC. It is now known to have an orbital period of circa 10,000 years.

In 1691, Halley built a [diving bell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diving_bell), a device in which the atmosphere was replenished by way of weighted barrels of air sent down from the surface.[^o38] In a demonstration, Halley and five companions dived to 60 ft in the [River Thames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Thames), and remained there for over an hour and a half. Halley's bell was of little use for practical salvage work, as it was very heavy, but he made improvements to it over time, later extending his underwater exposure time to over 4 hours.[^o39] Halley suffered one of the earliest recorded cases of middle ear [barotrauma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barotrauma).[^o38] That same year, at a meeting of the Royal Society, Halley introduced a rudimentary working model of a magnetic [compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass) using a liquid-filled housing to damp the swing and wobble of the magnetised needle.[^o40]

In 1691, Halley sought the post of [Savilian Professor of Astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savilian_Professor_of_Astronomy) at Oxford. While a candidate for the position, Halley faced the animosity of the Astronomer Royal, John Flamsteed, and the [Anglican Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Church) questioned his religious views, largely on the grounds that he had doubted the [Earth's age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_age) as given in the [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible).[^n11] After Flamsteed wrote to Newton to rally support against Halley, Newton wrote back in hopes of reconciliation, but was unsuccessful.[^n11] Halley's candidacy was opposed by both the [archbishop of Canterbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury), [John Tillotson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tillotson), and [Bishop Stillingfleet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Stillingfleet), and the post went instead to [David Gregory](wikipedia://en/703683), who had Newton's support.[^o43]

In 1692, Halley put forth the idea of a [hollow Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hollow_Earth) consisting of a shell about 500 miles (800 km) thick, two inner concentric shells and an innermost core.[^o44] He suggested that atmospheres separated these shells, and that each shell had its own [magnetic poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_magnetic_field#Magnetic_poles), with each sphere rotating at a different speed. Halley proposed this scheme to explain anomalous compass readings. He envisaged each inner region as having an [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_body_atmosphere) and being [luminous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminosity) (and possibly inhabited), and speculated that escaping gas caused the [aurora borealis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurora).[^o45] He suggested, "Auroral rays are due to particles, which are affected by the magnetic field, the rays parallel to Earth's magnetic field."[^o46]

In 1693 Halley published an article on [life annuities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_annuity), which featured an analysis of age-at-death on the basis of the [Breslau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrocław) statistics [Caspar Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caspar_Neumann) had been able to provide. This article allowed the British government to sell life annuities at an appropriate price based on the age of the purchaser. Halley's work strongly influenced the development of [actuarial science](wikipedia://en/189373). The construction of the life-table for Breslau, which followed more primitive work by [John Graunt](wikipedia://en/30875381), is now seen as a major event in the history of [demography](wikipedia://en/45642).

In 1694, Halley presented a paper to the Royal Society on the cause of the biblical Flood, in which he suggested a physical explanation involving the close approach of a comet to the Earth.[^n12] In a follow-up paper delivered on 19 December, he wrote that he had been advised to understand the hypothesis as applying to changes before the Creation rather than to the biblical Deluge.[^n13] The papers were not published until 1724, with an accompanying note explaining that Halley had feared censure from the "Sacred Order".[^n14] Although not now accepted, this was an early scientific explanation for a biblical event, and these views did not commend him to some powerful ecclesiastics of his day.[^n15]

In 1696, Newton was appointed as [warden of the Royal Mint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warden_of_the_Royal_Mint) and nominated Halley as deputy comptroller of the Chester mint. Halley spent two years supervising coin production. While there, he caught two clerks pilfering precious metals. He and the local warden spoke out about the scheme, unaware that the local master of the mint was profiting from it.[^n16]

In 1698, the Czar of Russia (later known as [Peter the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_the_Great)) was on a visit to England, and hoped Newton would be available to entertain him. Newton sent Halley in his place. He and the Czar bonded over science and brandy. According to one disputed account, when both of them were drunk one night, Halley jovially pushed the Czar around [Deptford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deptford) in a wheelbarrow.[^n17]

### Exploration years {#Exploration_years}

*Image: Halley isogonic 1701.jpg.* Halley's 1701 map showing [isogonic lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isogonic_lines) of equal magnetic declination in the Atlantic Ocean.

In 1698, at the behest of King [William III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_III_of_England), Halley was given command of the Paramour, a 52 ft [pink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pink_%28ship%29), so that he could carry out investigations in the South Atlantic into the laws governing the [variation of the compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_declination), as well as to refine the coordinates of the [English colonies in the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_America).[^n18] On 19 August 1698, he took command of the ship and, in November 1698, sailed on what was the first purely scientific voyage by an English naval vessel. Unfortunately problems of [insubordination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insubordination) arose over questions of Halley's competence to command a vessel. Halley returned the ship to England to proceed against officers in July 1699. The result was a mild rebuke for his men, and dissatisfaction for Halley, who felt the court had been too lenient.[^o56] Halley thereafter received a temporary commission as a captain in the [Royal Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Navy), recommissioned the *Paramour* on 24 August 1699 and sailed again in September 1699 to make extensive observations on the conditions of terrestrial [magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism). This task he accomplished in a second Atlantic voyage which lasted until 6 September 1700, and extended from 52 degrees north to 52 degrees south. The results were published in *General Chart of the Variation of the Compass* (1701).[^o12] This was the first such chart to be published and the first on which [isogonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isogon_%28geomagnetism%29), or Halleyan, lines appeared.[^o57][^o58] The use of such lines inspired later ideas such as those of isotherms by [Alexander von Humboldt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_von_Humboldt) in his maps.[^o59] In 1701, Halley made a third and final voyage on the *Paramour* to study the tides of the [English Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Channel).[^n19] In 1702, he was dispatched by Queen [Anne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne,_Queen_of_Great_Britain) on diplomatic missions to other European leaders.[^n19]

*Image: Edmond Halley plaque in Westminster Abbey 2.jpg.* Comet-like plaque to Halley in the South Cloister of [Westminster Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Abbey)

The preface to [Awnsham and John Churchill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awnsham_Churchill)'s collection of voyages and travels (1704), supposedly written by John Locke or by Halley, valourised expeditions such as these as part of a grand expansion of European knowledge of the world:

> What was cosmography before these discoveries, but an imperfect fragment of a science, scarce deserving so good a name? When all the known world was only Europe, a small part of Africk, and the lesser portion of Asia; so that of this terraqueous globe not one sixth part had ever been seen or heard of. Nay so great was the ignorance of man in this particular, that learned persons made a doubt of its being round; others no less knowing imagin'd all they were not acquainted with, desart and uninhabitable. But now geography and hydrography have receiv'd some perfection by the pains of so many mariners and travelers, who to evince the rotundity of the earth and water, have sail'd and travell'd round it, as has been here made appear; to show there is no part uninhabitable, unless the frozen polar regions, have visited all other countries, tho never so remote, which they have found well peopl'd, and most of them rich and delightful…. Astronomy has receiv'd the addition of many constellations never seen before. Natural and moral history is embelish'd with the most beneficial increase of so many thousands of plants it had never before receiv'd, so many drugs and spices, such variety of beasts, birds and fishes, such rarities in minerals, mountains and waters, such unaccountable diversity of climates and men, and in them of complexions, tempers, habits, manners, politicks, and religions…. To conclude, the empire of Europe is now extended to the utmost bounds of the earth, where several of its nations have conquests and colonies. These and many more are the advantages drawn from the labours of those, who expose themselves to the dangers of the vast ocean, and of unknown nations; which those who sit still at home abundantly reap in every kind: and the relation of one traveler is an incentive to stir up another to imitate him, whilst the rest of mankind, in their accounts without stirring a foot, compass the earth and seas, visit all countries, and converse with all nations.[^o62]

### Life as an academic {#Life_as_an_academic}

In November 1703, Halley was appointed [Savilian Professor of Geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savilian_Professor_of_Geometry) at the University of Oxford, his theological enemies, [John Tillotson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tillotson) and [Bishop Stillingfleet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Stillingfleet) having died. In 1705, applying [historical astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_astronomy) methods, he published the paper *[Astronomiae cometicae synopsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iarchive:jstor-102980)* (*A Synopsis of the Astronomy of Comets*); in this, he stated his belief that the comet sightings of 1456, 1531, 1607, and 1682 were of the same comet, and that it would return in 1758.[^n20] Halley did not live to witness the comet's return, but when it did, the comet became generally known as Halley's Comet.

By 1706 Halley had learned [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) and completed the translation started by [Edward Bernard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Bernard)[^o65] of Books V–VII of [Apollonius](wikipedia://en/257242)'s *Conics* from copies found at [Leiden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leiden_University_Library) and the [Bodleian Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodleian_Library) at Oxford. He also completed a new translation of the first four books from the original Greek that had been started by the late [David Gregory](wikipedia://en/703683). He published these along with his own reconstruction of Book VIII[^o66] in the first complete Latin edition in 1710. The same year, he received an honorary degree of doctor of laws from Oxford.[^o12]

In 1716, Halley suggested a high-precision measurement of the distance between the Earth and the Sun by timing the [transit of Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transit_of_Venus). In doing so, he was following the method described by [James Gregory](wikipedia://en/220425) in *Optica Promota* (in which the design of the [Gregorian telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_telescope) is also described). It is reasonable to assume Halley possessed and had read this book given that the Gregorian design was the principal telescope design used in astronomy in Halley's day.[^o67] It is not to Halley's credit that he failed to acknowledge Gregory's priority in this matter. In 1717–18 he discovered the [proper motion](wikipedia://en/52015) of the "fixed" stars (publishing this in 1718)[^o68] by comparing his [astrometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrometry) measurements with those given in Ptolemy's *[Almagest](wikipedia://en/148060)*. [Arcturus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcturus) and [Sirius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sirius) were two noted to have moved significantly, the latter having progressed 30 arc minutes (about the diameter of the moon) southwards in 1800 years.[^o69]

In 1720, together with his friend the [antiquarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiquarian) [William Stukeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Stukeley), Halley participated in the first attempt to scientifically date [Stonehenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonehenge). Assuming that the monument had been laid out using a magnetic compass, Stukeley and Halley attempted to calculate the perceived deviation introducing corrections from existing magnetic records, and suggested three dates (460 BC, AD 220 and AD 920), the earliest being the one accepted. These dates were wrong by thousands of years, but the idea that scientific methods could be used to date ancient monuments was revolutionary in its day.[^o70]

*Image: Halley Edmund grave.jpg.* Halley's grave

Halley succeeded John Flamsteed in 1720 as Astronomer Royal, a position Halley held until his death in 1742 at the age of 85.[^o20] He was buried in the graveyard of the old church of St Margaret's, Lee (since rebuilt), at [Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee,_London) Terrace, [Blackheath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackheath,_London).[^o71] He was interred in the same vault as the Astronomer Royal [John Pond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Pond); the unmarked grave of the Astronomer Royal [Nathaniel Bliss](wikipedia://en/379898) is nearby.[^o72] His original [tombstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tombstone) was transferred by the [Admiralty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Admiralty) when the original Lee church was demolished and rebuilt – it can be seen today on the southern wall of the Camera Obscura at the Royal Observatory, Greenwich. His marked grave can be seen at St Margaret's Church, Lee Terrace.[^o73][^o74]

Despite the persistent misconception that Halley received a [knighthood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knighthood), it is not the case. The idea can be tracked back to American astronomical texts such as [William Augustus Norton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Augustus_Norton)'s 1839 *An Elementary Treatise on Astronomy*, possibly due to Halley's royal occupations and connections to [Sir Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Isaac_Newton).[^o75]

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Halley married Mary Tooke in 1682 and settled in [Islington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islington). The couple had three children.[^o2]

## Pronunciation and spelling {#Pronunciation_and_spelling}

There are three pronunciations of the surname *Halley*. These are , , and . As a personal surname, the most common pronunciation in the 21st century, both in Great Britain[^o6] and in the United States,[^o7] is (rhymes with "valley"). This is the personal pronunciation used by most Halleys living in London today.[^o79] The alternative is much more common when referring to the comet than it is when used as a modern surname. [Colin Ronan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Ronan), one of Halley's biographers, preferred . Contemporary accounts spell his name *Hailey, Hayley, Haley, Haly, Halley, Hawley* and *Hawly*, and presumably pronunciations varied similarly.[^o80]

As for his given name, although the spelling "Edmund" is quite common, "Edmond" is what Halley himself used, according to a 1902 article,[^o81] though a 2007 *International Comet Quarterly* article disputes this, commenting that in his published works, he used "Edmund" 22 times and "Edmond" only 3 times,[^o82] with several other variations used as well, such as the Latinised "Edmundus". Much of the debate stems from the fact that, in Halley's own time, English spelling conventions were not yet standardised, and so he himself used multiple spellings.[^o5]
