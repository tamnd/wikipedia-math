# Great-circle navigation

*Image: Grosskreis.jpg.* Orthodromic course drawn on the Earth [globe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Great-circle navigation"}

**Great-circle navigation** or **orthodromic navigation** (related to **orthodromic course**; grc) is the practice of [navigating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation) a vessel (a [ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship) or [aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft)) along a [great circle](wikipedia://en/48082). Such routes yield the shortest [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) between two points on the globe.[^o2]

:::

## Course {#Course}

*Image: Sphere geodesic 4sigma.svg.* Figure 1. The great circle path between (φ<sub>1</sub>, λ<sub>1</sub>) and (φ<sub>2</sub>, λ<sub>2</sub>).

The great circle path may be found using [spherical trigonometry](wikipedia://en/650405); this is the spherical version of the *[inverse geodetic problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_geodetic_problem)*. If a navigator begins at *P*<sub>1</sub> = (φ<sub>1</sub>,λ<sub>1</sub>) and plans to travel the great circle to a point at point *P*<sub>2</sub> = (φ<sub>2</sub>,λ<sub>2</sub>) (see Fig. 1, φ is the latitude, positive northward, and λ is the longitude, positive eastward), the initial and final courses α<sub>1</sub> and α<sub>2</sub> are given by [formulas for solving a spherical triangle](wikipedia://en/22228915#Two_sides_and_the_included_angle_given)

$$\begin{aligned} \tan\alpha_1&=\frac{\cos\phi_2\sin\lambda_{12}}{ \cos\phi_1\sin\phi_2-\sin\phi_1\cos\phi_2\cos\lambda_{12}},\\ \tan\alpha_2&=\frac{\cos\phi_1\sin\lambda_{12}}{-\cos\phi_2\sin\phi_1+\sin\phi_2\cos\phi_1\cos\lambda_{12}},\\ \end{aligned}$$

where λ<sub>12</sub> = λ<sub>2</sub> − λ<sub>1</sub>[^o4] and the quadrants of α<sub>1</sub>,α<sub>2</sub> are determined by the signs of the numerator and denominator in the tangent formulas (e.g., using the [atan2](wikipedia://en/3856703) function). The [central angle](wikipedia://en/462705) between the two points, σ<sub>12</sub>, is given by

$$\tan\sigma_{12}=\frac{\sqrt{(\cos\phi_1\sin\phi_2-\sin\phi_1\cos\phi_2\cos\lambda_{12})^2 + (\cos\phi_2\sin\lambda_{12})^2}}{\sin\phi_1\sin\phi_2+\cos\phi_1\cos\phi_2\cos\lambda_{12}}.$$

(The numerator of this formula contains the quantities that were used to determine tan α<sub>1</sub>.) The distance along the great circle will then be *s*<sub>12</sub> = *R*σ<sub>12</sub>, where *R* is the assumed radius of the Earth and σ<sub>12</sub> is expressed in [radians](wikipedia://en/26003#Conversions). Using the [mean Earth radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_radius#Mean_radius), *R* = *R*<sub>1</sub> ≈ 6371 km yields results for the distance *s*<sub>12</sub> which are within 1% of the [geodesic length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_length) for the [WGS84](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WGS84) ellipsoid; see [Geodesics on an ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/36495578) for details.

### Relation to geocentric coordinate system {#Relation_to_geocentric_coordinate_system}

Position angle

*Image: PositionAngle.svg.* The position angle of the point **t** at the point **s** is the angle at which the green and the dashed great circles intersect at **s**. The unit directions $u_{E}$, $u_{N}$ and the rotation axis $\omega$ are marked by arrows.

Detailed evaluation of the optimum direction is possible if the sea surface is approximated by a sphere surface. The standard computation places the ship at a geodetic [latitude](wikipedia://en/17616) $\varphi_{s}$ and geodetic [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude) $\lambda_{s}$, where $\varphi$ is considered positive if north of the equator, and where $\lambda$ is considered positive if east of [Greenwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_meridian). In the [geocentric coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentric_coordinate_system) centered at the center of the sphere, the Cartesian components are

$${\mathbf s}=R\left(\begin{array}{c} \cos\varphi_s \cos\lambda_s \\ \cos\varphi_s \sin\lambda_s \\ \sin\varphi_s \end{array}\right)$$

and the target position is

$${\mathbf t}=R\left(\begin{array}{c} \cos\varphi_t \cos\lambda_t \\ \cos\varphi_t \sin\lambda_t \\ \sin\varphi_t \end{array}\right).$$

The [North Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pole) is at

$${\mathbf N}=R\left(\begin{array}{c} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{array}\right).$$

The [minimum distance](wikipedia://en/379733) $d$ is the distance along a great circle that runs through $\mathbf{s}$ and $\mathbf{t}$. It is calculated in a plane that contains the sphere center and the [great circle](wikipedia://en/48082),

$$d_{s,t}=R\theta_{s,t}$$

where $\theta$ is the [angular distance](wikipedia://en/3720362) of two points viewed from the center of the sphere, measured in [radians](wikipedia://en/26003). The cosine of the angle is calculated by the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) of the two vectors

$$\mathbf{s}\cdot \mathbf{t} = R^2\cos \theta_{s,t} = R^2(\sin\varphi_s\sin\varphi_t+\cos\varphi_s\cos\varphi_t\cos(\lambda_t-\lambda_s))$$

If the ship steers straight to the North Pole, the travel distance is

$$d_{s,N} = R\theta_{s,N} = R(\pi/2-\varphi_s)$$

If a ship starts at $\mathbf{t}$ and sails straight to the North Pole, the travel distance is

$$d_{t,N} = R\theta_{t,n} =R(\pi/2-\varphi_t)$$

#### Derivation {#Derivation}

The *cosine formula* of [spherical trigonometry](wikipedia://en/650405)[^o7] yields for the angle $p$ between the great circles through $\mathbf{s}$ that point to the North on one hand and to $\mathbf{t}$ on the other hand

$$\cos\theta_{t,N} = \cos\theta_{s,t}\cos\theta_{s,N}+\sin\theta_{s,t}\sin\theta_{s,N}\cos p.$$

$$\sin\varphi_t = \cos\theta_{s,t}\sin\varphi_s +\sin\theta_{s,t}\cos\varphi_s\cos p.$$

The *sine formula* yields

$$\frac{\sin p}{\sin \theta_{t,N}} = \frac{\sin(\lambda_t-\lambda_s)}{\sin\theta_{s,t}}.$$

Solving this for $\sin \theta_{s,t}$ and insertion in the previous formula gives an expression for the tangent of the [position angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_angle),

$$\sin\varphi_t = \cos\theta_{s,t}\sin\varphi_s +\frac{\sin(\lambda_t-\lambda_s)}{\sin p}\cos\varphi_t\cos\varphi_s\cos p;$$

$$\tan p = \frac{\sin(\lambda_t-\lambda_s)\cos\varphi_t\cos\varphi_s}{\sin\varphi_t-\cos\theta_{s,t}\sin\varphi_s}.$$

#### Further details {#Further_details}

Because the brief derivation gives an angle between 0 and $\pi$ which does not reveal the sign (west or east of north ?), a more explicit derivation is desirable which yields separately the sine and the cosine of $p$ such that use of the [correct branch of the inverse tangent](wikipedia://en/3856703) allows to produce an angle in the full range $-\pi \le p \le \pi$.

The computation starts from a construction of the great circle between $\mathbf{s}$ and $\mathbf{t}$. It lies in the plane that contains the sphere center, $\mathbf{s}$ and $\mathbf{t}$ and is constructed rotating $\mathbf{s}$ by the angle $\theta_{s,t}$ around an axis $\omega$. The axis is perpendicular to the plane of the great circle and computed by the normalized vector [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) of the two positions:

$$\mathbf{\omega} = \frac{1}{R^2\sin \theta_{s,t}}\mathbf{s}\times \mathbf{t} = \frac{1}{\sin \theta_{s,t}}\left(\begin{array}{c} \cos\varphi_s\sin\lambda_s\sin\varphi_t -\sin\varphi_s\cos\varphi_t\sin\lambda_t \\ \sin\varphi_s\cos\lambda_t\cos\varphi_t -\cos\varphi_s\sin\varphi_t\cos\lambda_s \\ \cos\varphi_s\cos\varphi_t\sin(\lambda_t-\lambda_s) \end{array}\right).$$

A right-handed tilted coordinate system with the center at the center of the sphere is given by the following three axes: the axis $\mathbf{s}$, the axis

$$\mathbf{s}_\perp = \omega \times \frac{1}{R}\mathbf{s} = \frac{1}{\sin\theta_{s,t}} \left(\begin{array}{c} \cos\varphi_t\cos\lambda_t(\sin^2\varphi_s+\cos^2\varphi_s\sin^2\lambda_s)-\cos\lambda_s(\sin\varphi_s\cos\varphi_s\sin\varphi_t+\cos^2\varphi_s\sin\lambda_s\cos\varphi_t\sin\lambda_t)\\ \cos\varphi_t\sin\lambda_t(\sin^2\varphi_s+\cos^2\varphi_s\cos^2\lambda_s)-\sin\lambda_s(\sin\varphi_s\cos\varphi_s\sin\varphi_t+\cos^2\varphi_s\cos\lambda_s\cos\varphi_t\cos\lambda_t)\\ \cos\varphi_s[\cos\varphi_s\sin\varphi_t-\sin\varphi_s\cos\varphi_t\cos(\lambda_t-\lambda_s)] \end{array}\right)$$

and the axis $\omega$. A position along the great circle is given by the angle $\theta$:

$$\mathbf{s}(\theta) = \cos\theta \mathbf{s}+\sin\theta \mathbf{s}_\perp,\quad 0\le\theta\le 2\pi.$$

The compass direction is given by computing the gradient of the vector with respect to $\theta$ at $\theta=0$.

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\mathbf{s}_{\mid \theta=0}=\mathbf{s}_\perp.$$

The angle $p$ is given by splitting this direction along two orthogonal directions in the plane tangential to the sphere at the point $\mathbf{s}$. The two directions are given by the partial derivatives of $\mathbf{s}$ with respect to $\varphi$ and with respect to $\lambda$, normalized to unit length:

$$\mathbf{u}_N = \left( \begin{array}{c} -\sin\varphi_s\cos\lambda_s\\ -\sin\varphi_s\sin\lambda_s\\ \cos\varphi_s \end{array}\right);$$

$$\mathbf{u}_E = \left(\begin{array}{c} -\sin\lambda_s\\ \cos\lambda_s\\ 0 \end{array} \right);$$

$$\mathbf{u}_N\cdot \mathbf{s} = \mathbf{u}_E\cdot \mathbf{u}_N =0$$

$\mathbf{u}_{N}$ points north and $\mathbf{u}_{E}$ points east at the position $\mathbf{s}$. The position angle $p$ projects $\mathbf{s}_{\perp}$ into these two directions,

$$\mathbf{s}_\perp = \cos p \,\mathbf{u}_N+\sin p\, \mathbf{u}_E,$$

where the positive sign means the positive position angles are defined to be north over east. The values of the cosine and sine of $p$ are computed by multiplying this equation on both sides with the two unit vectors,

$$\cos p = \mathbf{s}_\perp \cdot \mathbf{u}_N =\frac{1}{\sin\theta_{s,t}}[\cos\varphi_s\sin\varphi_t - \sin\varphi_s\cos\varphi_t\cos(\lambda_t-\lambda_s)];$$

$$\sin p = \mathbf{s}_\perp \cdot \mathbf{u}_E =\frac{1}{\sin\theta_{s,t}}[\cos\varphi_t\sin(\lambda_t-\lambda_s)].$$

Instead of inserting the convoluted expression of $\mathbf{s}_{\perp}$, the evaluation may employ that the [triple product](wikipedia://en/978650#Scalar_triple_product) is invariant under a [circular shift](wikipedia://en/1917276) of the three arguments:

$$\cos p = (\mathbf{\omega}\times \frac{1}{R}\mathbf{s})\cdot \mathbf{u}_N = \omega\cdot(\frac{1}{R}\mathbf{s}\times \mathbf{u}_N).$$

If [atan2](wikipedia://en/3856703) is used to compute the value, one can reduce both expressions by division through $\cos \varphi_{t}$ and multiplication by $\sin \theta_{s,t}$, because these values are always positive and that operation does not change signs; then effectively

$$\tan p = \frac{\sin p}{\cos p}=\frac{\sin(\lambda_t-\lambda_s)}{\cos\varphi_s\tan\varphi_t -\sin\varphi_s\cos(\lambda_t-\lambda_s)}.$$

## Finding way-points {#Finding_way-points}

To find the [way-points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Way-point), that is the positions of selected points on the great circle between *P*<sub>1</sub> and *P*<sub>2</sub>, we first extrapolate the great circle back to its *[node](wikipedia://en/978717)* *A*, the point at which the great circle crosses the equator in the northward direction: let the longitude of this point be λ<sub>0</sub> — see Fig 1. The [azimuth](wikipedia://en/47487) at this point, α<sub>0</sub>, is given by

$$\tan\alpha_0 = \frac {\sin\alpha_1 \cos\phi_1}{\sqrt{\cos^2\alpha_1 + \sin^2\alpha_1\sin^2\phi_1}}.$$

Let the angular distances along the great circle from *A* to *P*<sub>1</sub> and *P*<sub>2</sub> be σ<sub>01</sub> and σ<sub>02</sub> respectively. Then using [Napier's rules](wikipedia://en/650405#Napier's_rules_for_quadrantal_triangles) we have

$\tan\sigma_{01} = \frac{\tan\phi_1}{\cos\alpha_1} \qquad$(If φ<sub>1</sub> = 0 and α<sub>1</sub> = $\tfrac{1}{2}$π, use σ<sub>01</sub> = 0).

This gives σ<sub>01</sub>, whence σ<sub>02</sub> = σ<sub>01</sub> + σ<sub>12</sub>.

The longitude at the node is found from

$$\begin{aligned} \tan\lambda_{01} &= \frac{\sin\alpha_0\sin\sigma_{01}}{\cos\sigma_{01}},\\ \lambda_0 &= \lambda_1 - \lambda_{01}. \end{aligned}$$

*Image: Sphere geodesic 2sigma.svg.* Figure 2. The great circle path between a node (an equator crossing) and an arbitrary point (φ,λ).

Finally, calculate the position and azimuth at an arbitrary point, *P* (see Fig. 2), by the spherical version of the *direct geodesic problem*. Napier's rules give

$${\color{white}.\,\qquad)}\tan\phi = \frac {\cos\alpha_0\sin\sigma}{\sqrt{\cos^2\sigma + \sin^2\alpha_0\sin^2\sigma}},$$

$$\begin{aligned} \tan(\lambda - \lambda_0) &= \frac {\sin\alpha_0\sin\sigma}{\cos\sigma},\\ \tan\alpha &= \frac {\tan\alpha_0}{\cos\sigma}. \end{aligned}$$

The [atan2](wikipedia://en/3856703) function should be used to determine σ<sub>01</sub>, λ, and α. For example, to find the midpoint of the path, substitute σ = $\tfrac{1}{2}$(σ<sub>01</sub> + σ<sub>02</sub>); alternatively to find the point a distance *d* from the starting point, take σ = σ<sub>01</sub> + *d*/*R*. Likewise, the *vertex*, the point on the great circle with greatest latitude, is found by substituting σ = +$\tfrac{1}{2}$π. It may be convenient to parameterize the route in terms of the longitude using

$$\tan\phi = \cot\alpha_0\sin(\lambda-\lambda_0).$$

Latitudes at regular intervals of longitude can be found and the resulting positions transferred to the Mercator chart allowing the great circle to be approximated by a series of [rhumb lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhumb_line). The path determined in this way gives the [great ellipse](wikipedia://en/14275961) joining the end points, provided the coordinates $(\phi,\lambda)$ are interpreted as geographic coordinates on the ellipsoid.

These formulas apply to a spherical model of the Earth. They are also used in solving for the great circle on the *auxiliary sphere* which is a device for finding the shortest path, or *geodesic*, on an ellipsoid of revolution; see the article on [geodesics on an ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/36495578).

## Example {#Example}

Compute the great circle route from [Valparaíso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valparaíso), φ<sub>1</sub> = −33°, λ<sub>1</sub> = −71.6°, to [Shanghai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghai), φ<sub>2</sub> = 31.4°, λ<sub>2</sub> = 121.8°.

The formulas for course and distance give λ<sub>12</sub> = −166.6°,[^o12] α<sub>1</sub> = −94.41°, α<sub>2</sub> = −78.42°, and σ<sub>12</sub> = 168.56°. Taking the [earth radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_radius#Mean_radius) to be *R* = 6371 km, the distance is *s*<sub>12</sub> = 18743 km.

To compute points along the route, first find α<sub>0</sub> = −56.74°, σ<sub>01</sub> = −96.76°, σ<sub>02</sub> = 71.8°, λ<sub>01</sub> = 98.07°, and λ<sub>0</sub> = −169.67°. Then to compute the midpoint of the route (for example), take σ = $\tfrac{1}{2}$(σ<sub>01</sub> + σ<sub>02</sub>) = −12.48°, and solve for φ = −6.81°, λ = −159.18°, and α = −57.36°.

If the geodesic is computed accurately on the [WGS84](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WGS84) ellipsoid,[^o13] the results are α<sub>1</sub> = −94.82°, α<sub>2</sub> = −78.29°, and *s*<sub>12</sub> = 18752 km. The midpoint of the geodesic is φ = −7.07°, λ = −159.31°, α = −57.45°.

:::

## Gnomonic chart {#Gnomonic_chart}

*Image: Admiralty Chart No 132 Gnomonic Chart of Indian and Southern Oceans, Published 1914.jpg.* Admiralty Gnomonic Chart of the Indian and Southern Oceans, for use in plotting great circle tracks

A straight line drawn on a [gnomonic chart](wikipedia://en/1331480) is a portion of a great circle. When this is transferred to a [Mercator chart](wikipedia://en/20771), it becomes a curve. The positions are transferred at a convenient interval of [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude) and this track is plotted on the Mercator chart for navigation.
