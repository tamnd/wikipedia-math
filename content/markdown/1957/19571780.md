# Special linear Lie algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Special linear Lie algebra"}

Classical In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **special linear Lie algebra** of order $n$ over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $F$, denoted $\mathfrak{sl}_n F$ or $\mathfrak{sl}(n, F)$, is the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) of all the $n \times n$ [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) (with entries in $F$) with [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) zero and with the [Lie bracket](wikipedia://en/17944) $[X,Y] := XY-YX$ given by the [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193#Ring_theory). This algebra is well studied and understood, and is often used as a model for the study of other Lie algebras. The [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) that it generates is the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823).

:::

::: {#o3 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sl}_2 \mathbb{C}$ is central to the study of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962), [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) and [supersymmetry](wikipedia://en/224636): its [fundamental representation](wikipedia://en/649743) is the so-called [spinor representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinor_representation), while its [adjoint representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_endomorphism) generates the [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489) SO(3,1) of special relativity.

The algebra $\mathfrak{sl}_2 \mathbb{R}$ plays an important role in the study of [chaos](wikipedia://en/6295) and [fractals](wikipedia://en/10913), as it generates the [Möbius group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Möbius_group) [SL(2,**R**)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SL%282,R%29), which describes the [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/241291#Models_of_the_hyperbolic_plane), the simplest [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181) of negative curvature; by contrast, [SL(2,**C**)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SL%282,C%29) describes the automorphisms of the hyperbolic 3-dimensional ball.

:::

## Representation theory {#Representation_theory}

### Representation theory of sl<sub>2</sub>C {#Representation_theory_of_sl2C}

The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sl}_2 \mathbb{C}$ is a three-dimensional [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) Lie algebra. Its defining feature is that it contains a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) $e, h, f$ satisfying the commutation relations

$[e,f]=h$, $[h,f]=-2f$, and $[h,e]=2e$.

This is a [Cartan-Weyl basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartan-Weyl_basis) for $\mathfrak{sl}_2 \mathbb{C}$. It has an explicit realization in terms of 2-by-2 complex matrices with zero trace:

$E = \begin{bmatrix} 0&1\\ 0&0 \end{bmatrix}$, $F = \begin{bmatrix} 0&0\\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix}$, $H = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\ 0&-1 \end{bmatrix}$.

This is the [fundamental](wikipedia://en/649743) or defining representation for $\mathfrak{sl}_2 \mathbb{C}$.

The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sl}_2 \mathbb{C}$ can be viewed as a [subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of its [universal enveloping algebra](wikipedia://en/363551) $U = U(\mathfrak{sl}_2 \mathbb{C})$ and, in $U$, there are the following commutator relations shown by [induction](wikipedia://en/18881):[^o6]

$$[h, f^k] = -2 k f^k, \, [h, e^k] = 2 k e^k,$$

$$[e, f^k] = -k(k - 1)f^{k-1} + k f^{k-1} h.$$

Note that, here, the powers $f^k$, etc. refer to powers as elements of the algebra *U* and not matrix powers. The first basic fact (that follows from the above commutator relations) is:[^o6]

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=math-theorem name="Lemma"}

**Lemma.**

Let $V$ be a [representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representation_of_a_Lie_algebra) of $\mathfrak{sl}_2 \mathbb{C}$ and $v$ a vector in it. Set $v_j = {1 \over j!} f^j \cdot v$ for each $j = 0, 1, \dots$. If $v$ is an [eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) of the action of $h$; i.e., $h \cdot v = \lambda v$ for some complex number $\lambda$, then, for each $j = 0, 1, \dots$,

$$h \cdot v_j = (\lambda - 2j) v_j.$$

$$e \cdot v_j = {1 \over j!} f^j \cdot e \cdot v + (\lambda - j + 1) v_{j-1}.$$

$$f \cdot v_j = (j+1) v_{j+1}.$$

:::

From this [lemma](wikipedia://en/18634), one deduces the following fundamental result:[^o8]

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

Let $V$ be a representation of $\mathfrak{sl}_2 \mathbb{C}$ that may have infinite dimension and $v$ a vector in $V$ that is a $\mathfrak{b} = \mathbb{C} h + \mathbb{C} e$-weight vector ($\mathfrak{b}$ is a [Borel subalgebra](wikipedia://en/62074925)).[^o10] Then

Those $v_j$'s that are nonzero are [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent).

If some $v_j$ is zero, then the $h$-[eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of *v* is a nonnegative [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $N \ge 0$ such that $v_0, v_1, \dots, v_N$ are nonzero and $v_{N+1} = v_{N+2} = \cdots = 0$. Moreover, the subspace spanned by the $v_j$'s is an irreducible $\mathfrak{sl}_2 \mathbb{C}$-subrepresentation of $V$.

:::

The first statement is true since either $v_j$ is zero or has $h$-eigenvalue distinct from the eigenvalues of the others that are nonzero. Saying $v$ is a $\mathfrak{b}$-weight vector is equivalent to saying that it is simultaneously an eigenvector of $h$ and $e$; a short calculation then shows that, in that case, the $e$-eigenvalue of $v$ is zero: $e \cdot v = 0$. Thus, for some integer $N \ge 0$, $v_N \ne 0, v_{N+1} = v_{N+2} = \cdots = 0$ and in particular, by the early lemma,

$$0 = e \cdot v_{N+1} = (\lambda - (N+1) + 1) v_N,$$

which implies that $\lambda = N$. It remains to show $W = \operatorname{span} \{v_j \mid j \ge 0\}$ is irreducible. If $0 \ne W' \subset W$ is a subrepresentation, then it admits an eigenvector, which must have eigenvalue of the form $N - 2j$; thus is proportional to $v_j$. By the preceding lemma, we have $v = v_0$ is in $W$ and thus $W' = W$. $\square$

As a [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865), one deduces:

- If $V$ has finite dimension and is irreducible, then $h$-eigenvalue of *v* is a nonnegative integer $N$ and $V$ has a basis $v, f v, f^2 v, \cdots, f^N v$.
- [Conversely](wikipedia://en/155941), if the $h$-eigenvalue of $v$ is a nonnegative integer and $V$ is irreducible, then $V$ has a basis $v, f v, f^2 v, \cdots, f^N v$; in particular has finite dimension.

The beautiful special case of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ shows a general way to find irreducible representations of Lie algebras. Namely, we divide the algebra to three subalgebras "h" (the [Cartan subalgebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartan_algebra)), "e", and "f", which behave approximately like their namesakes in $\mathfrak{sl}_2$. Namely, in an irreducible representation, we have a "highest" eigenvector of "h", on which "e" acts by zero. The basis of the irreducible representation is generated by the action of "f" on the highest eigenvectors of "h". See the [theorem of the highest weight](wikipedia://en/58092933).

### Representation theory of sl<sub>*n*</sub>C {#Representation_theory_of_slnC}

When $\mathfrak g = \mathfrak{sl}_n \mathbb C = \operatorname{\mathfrak{sl}}V$ for a complex vector space $V$ of dimension $n$, each finite-dimensional irreducible representation of $\mathfrak g$ can be found as a subrepresentation of a [tensor power](wikipedia://en/43079145) of $V$.[^o12]

The Lie algebra can be explicitly realized as a matrix Lie algebra of traceless $n\times n$ matrices. This is the fundamental representation for $\mathfrak{sl}_n \mathbb{C}$.

Set $M_{i,j}$ to be the matrix with one in the $i,j$ entry and zeroes everywhere else. Then

$$H_i := M_{i,i} - M_{i+1,i+1},\text{ with } 1 \leq i \leq n-1$$

$$M_{i,j},\text{ with } i \neq j$$

Form a basis for $\mathfrak{sl}_n \mathbb{C}$. This is technically an abuse of notation, and these are really the image of the basis of $\mathfrak{sl}_n \mathbb{C}$ in the fundamental representation.

Furthermore, this is in fact a Cartan–Weyl basis, with the $H_i$ spanning the Cartan subalgebra. Introducing notation $E_{i,j} = M_{i,j}$ if $j > i$, and $F_{i,j} = M_{i,j}^T = M_{j,i}$, also if $j > i$, the $E_{i,j}$ are positive roots and $F_{i,j}$ are corresponding negative roots.

A basis of [simple roots](wikipedia://en/277087#Positive_roots_and_simple_roots) is given by $E_{i,i+1}$ for $1 \leq i \leq n-1$.
