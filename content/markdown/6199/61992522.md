# Discrete spectrum (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Discrete spectrum"}

In mathematics, specifically in [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713), a **discrete spectrum** of a [closed linear operator](wikipedia://en/64713671) is defined as the set of [isolated points](wikipedia://en/390557) of its spectrum such that the [rank](wikipedia://en/26561) of the corresponding [Riesz projector](wikipedia://en/61861688) is finite.

:::

The discrete spectrum can also be defined as the set of normal eigenvalues.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A point $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$ in the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137) $\sigma(A)$ of a [closed linear operator](wikipedia://en/1422584#Closed_linear_operators) $A:\,\mathfrak{B}\to\mathfrak{B}$ in the [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) $\mathfrak{B}$ with [domain](wikipedia://en/1422584#Definitions_and_basic_properties) $\mathfrak{D}(A)\subset\mathfrak{B}$ is said to belong to the *discrete spectrum* $\sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(A)$ of $A$ if the following two conditions are satisfied:[^o4]

1. $\lambda$ is an isolated point in $\sigma(A)$;
1. The [rank](wikipedia://en/26561) of the corresponding [Riesz projector](wikipedia://en/61861688) $P_\lambda=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_\Gamma(z I_{\mathfrak{B}} - A)^{-1}\,dz$ is finite.

Here, $I_{\mathfrak{B}}$ is the [identity operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_operator) in the Banach space $\mathfrak{B}$, and $\Gamma\subset\mathbb{C}$ is a [simple closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_closed_curve) counterclockwise-oriented curve bounding an open region $\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}$ such that $\lambda$ is the only point of the spectrum of $A$ in the closure of $\Omega$; that is, $\sigma(A)\cap\overline{\Omega}=\{\lambda\}.$

:::

## Normal eigenvalues {#Normal_eigenvalues}

The set of points in the discrete spectrum is equal to the set of normal eigenvalues.[^o6][^o7][^o8]

$$\sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(A)=\{\mbox{normal eigenvalues of }A\}.$$

### Root lineal {#Root_lineal}

Let $\mathfrak{B}$ be a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989). Consider a [partially defined](wikipedia://en/13510193) linear operator $A:\,\mathfrak{B}\to\mathfrak{B}$ with domain $\mathfrak{D}(A)$. The [root lineal](wikipedia://en/1137612#Root_lineal_of_a_linear_operator_in_a_Banach_space) $\mathfrak{L}_\lambda(A)$ corresponding to an eigenvalue $\lambda\in\sigma_p(A)$ is defined as the set of elements $x$ such that $x, (A-\lambda I_{\mathfrak{B}}) x, (A-\lambda I_{\mathfrak{B}})^2 x, \dots$ all belong to $\mathfrak{D}(A)$, and that after finitely many steps, we end up with zero: $(A-\lambda I_{\mathfrak{B}})^k x = 0$.

This set is a [linear manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_manifold) but is not necessarily closed. If it is closed (for example, when it is finite-dimensional), it is called the [generalized eigenspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_eigenspace) of $A$ corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda$.

### Normal eigenvalue {#Normal_eigenvalue}

An [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) $\lambda\in\sigma_p(A)$ of a [closed linear operator](wikipedia://en/1422584#Closed_linear_operators) $A:\,\mathfrak{B}\to\mathfrak{B}$ in the [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) $\mathfrak{B}$ with [domain](wikipedia://en/1422584#Definitions_and_basic_properties) $\mathfrak{D}(A)\subset\mathfrak{B}$ is called **normal** (in the original terminology, *$\lambda$ corresponds to a normally splitting finite-dimensional root subspace*) if the following two conditions are satisfied:[^o11][^o6][^o7]

1. The [algebraic multiplicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_multiplicity) of $\lambda$ is finite: $\nu=\dim\mathfrak{L}_\lambda(A)<\infty$, where $\mathfrak{L}_\lambda(A)$ is the root lineal of $A$ corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda$;
1. The space $\mathfrak{B}$ can be decomposed into a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) $\mathfrak{B}=\mathfrak{L}_\lambda(A)\oplus \mathfrak{N}_\lambda$, where $\mathfrak{N}_\lambda$ is an [invariant subspace](wikipedia://en/585826) of $A$ in which $A-\lambda I_{\mathfrak{B}}$ has a bounded inverse.

### Equivalent characterizations {#Equivalent_characterizations}

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Equivalent characterizations"}

**Equivalent characterizations ([^o8]).**

Let $A:\,\mathfrak{B}\to\mathfrak{B}$ be a closed linear [densely defined operator](wikipedia://en/13510193) in the Banach space $\mathfrak{B}$, then the following are equivalent:

$\lambda\in\sigma(A)$ is a normal eigenvalue;

$\lambda\in\sigma(A)$ is an isolated point in $\sigma(A)$ and $A-\lambda I_{\mathfrak{B}}$ is [semi-Fredholm](wikipedia://en/279722#semi-Fredholm_operators);

$\lambda\in\sigma(A)$ is an isolated point in $\sigma(A)$ and $A-\lambda I_{\mathfrak{B}}$ is [Fredholm](wikipedia://en/279722);

$\lambda\in\sigma(A)$ is an isolated point in $\sigma(A)$ and $A-\lambda I_{\mathfrak{B}}$ is [Fredholm](wikipedia://en/279722) of index zero;

$\lambda\in\sigma(A)$ is an isolated point in $\sigma(A)$ and the rank of the corresponding [Riesz projector](wikipedia://en/61861688) $P_\lambda$ is finite;

$\lambda\in\sigma(A)$ is an isolated point in $\sigma(A)$, its algebraic multiplicity $\nu=\dim\mathfrak{L}_\lambda(A)$ is finite, and the range of $A-\lambda I_{\mathfrak{B}}$ is [closed](wikipedia://en/23483982).[^o11][^o6][^o7]

In all such cases, the root lineal $\mathfrak{L}_\lambda(A)$ is closed, and equals the range of the Riesz projector.[^o7]

:::

## Relation to other spectra {#Relation_to_other_spectra}

### Isolated eigenvalues of finite algebraic multiplicity {#Isolated_eigenvalues_of_finite_algebraic_multiplicity}

In general, the rank of the Riesz projector can be larger than the dimension of the [root lineal](wikipedia://en/1137612#Root_lineal_of_a_linear_operator_in_a_Banach_space) $\mathfrak{L}_\lambda$ of the corresponding eigenvalue, and in particular it is possible to have $\mathrm{dim}\,\mathfrak{L}_\lambda<\infty$, $\mathrm{rank}\,P_\lambda=\infty$. So, there is the following inclusion:

$$\sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(A)\subset\{\mbox{isolated points of the spectrum of }A\mbox{ with finite algebraic multiplicity}\}.$$

In particular, for a [quasinilpotent operator](wikipedia://en/671772)

$$Q:\,l^2(\mathbb{N})\to l^2(\mathbb{N}),\qquad Q:\,(a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots)\mapsto (0,a_1/2,a_2/2^2,a_3/2^3,\dots),$$

one has $\mathfrak{L}_\lambda(Q)=\{0\}$, $\mathrm{rank}\,P_\lambda=\infty$. Therefore, $\lambda=0$ is an isolated eigenvalue of finite algebraic multiplicity, but it is not in the discrete spectrum: $\sigma(Q)=\{0\}$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(Q)=\emptyset$.

### Point spectrum {#Point_spectrum}

The discrete spectrum $\sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(A)$ of an operator $A$ is not to be confused with the [point spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_spectrum) $\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(A)$, which is defined as the set of [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of $A$. Each point of the discrete spectrum is an eigenvalue, so

$$\sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(A)\subset\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(A).$$

However, they may be unequal. An eigenvalue may not be an isolated point of the spectrum, or it may be isolated, but with an infinite-rank Riesz projector. For example, for the *left shift operator*, $L:\,l^2(\mathbb{N})\to l^2(\mathbb{N}), \quad L:\,(a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots)\mapsto (a_2,a_3,a_4,\dots),$ the point spectrum is the open unit disc $\mathbb{D}_1$ in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), the full spectrum is the closed unit disc $\overline{\mathbb{D}_1}$, and the discrete spectrum is empty:

$$\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(L)=\mathbb{D}_1, \qquad \sigma(L)=\overline{\mathbb{D}_1}, \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(L)=\emptyset.$$

This is because $\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(L)$ has no isolated points.

### Spectral decomposition {#Spectral_decomposition}

The spectrum of a closed operator $A:\,\mathfrak{B}\to\mathfrak{B}$ in a Banach space $\mathfrak{B}$ can be decomposed into the union of two [disjoint sets](wikipedia://en/39359): the discrete spectrum and the fifth type of the [essential spectrum](wikipedia://en/1350002) (see page for the definition of each type):

$$\sigma(A)= \sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(A)\cup\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}, 5}(A).$$
