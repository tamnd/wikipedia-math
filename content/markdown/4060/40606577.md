# McKay's approximation for the coefficient of variation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="McKay's approximation for the coefficient of variation"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), **McKay's approximation** of the [coefficient of variation](wikipedia://en/1012687) is a statistic based on a sample from a [normally distributed](wikipedia://en/21462) population. It was introduced in 1932 by A. T. McKay.[^o2] Statistical methods for the coefficient of variation often utilizes McKay's approximation.[^o3][^o4][^o5][^o6]

:::

Let $x_i$, $i = 1, 2,\ldots, n$ be $n$ independent observations from a $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ normal distribution. The population coefficient of variation is $c_v = \sigma / \mu$. Let $\bar{x}$ and $s \,$ denote the [sample mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_mean) and the [sample standard deviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_standard_deviation), respectively. Then $\hat{c}_v = s/\bar{x}$ is the sample coefficient of variation. McKay's approximation is

$$K = \left( 1 + \frac{1}{c_v^2} \right) \ \frac{(n - 1) \ \hat{c}_v^2}{1 + (n - 1) \ \hat{c}_v^2/n}$$

Note that in this expression, the first factor includes the population coefficient of variation, which is usually unknown. When $c_v$ is smaller than 1/3, then $K$ is approximately [chi-square distributed](wikipedia://en/113424) with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom. In the original article by McKay, the expression for $K$ looks slightly different, since McKay defined $\sigma^2$ with denominator $n$ instead of $n - 1$. McKay's approximation, $K$, for the coefficient of variation is approximately chi-square distributed, but exactly [noncentral beta distributed](wikipedia://en/32632892) .[^o7]
