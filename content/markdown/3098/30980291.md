# Edgar Gilbert

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Edgar Gilbert"}

**Edgar Nelson Gilbert** (July 25, 1923 – June 15, 2013) was an American mathematician and [coding theorist](wikipedia://en/321869), a longtime researcher at [Bell Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Laboratories). His accomplishments include the [Gilbert–Varshamov bound](wikipedia://en/1882350) in [coding theory](wikipedia://en/321869), the [Gilbert–Elliott model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert–Elliott_model) of bursty errors in signal transmission, the [Erdős–Rényi–Gilbert model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erdős–Rényi–Gilbert_model) for [random graphs](wikipedia://en/358477), the [Gilbert disk model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_disk_model) of random geometric graphs, the [Gilbert–Shannon–Reeds model](wikipedia://en/39949312) of card shuffling, [Gilbert tessellations](wikipedia://en/36142343), and the formulation of the [Gilbert–Pollak conjecture](wikipedia://en/72307852) on the [Steiner ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steiner_ratio).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Gilbert was born in 1923 in [Woodhaven, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodhaven,_New_York). He did his undergraduate studies in physics at [Queens College, City University of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queens_College,_City_University_of_New_York), graduating in 1943. He taught mathematics briefly at the [University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana–Champaign) but then moved to the [Radiation Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_Laboratory) at the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology), where he designed [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar) [antennas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antenna_%28radio%29) from 1944 to 1946. He finished a Ph.D. in physics at MIT in 1948, with a dissertation entitled *Asymptotic Solution of Relaxation Oscillation Problems* under the supervision of [Norman Levinson](wikipedia://en/1090122), and took a job at [Bell Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Laboratories) where he remained for the rest of his career. He retired in 1996.

He died following a fall in 2013 at [Basking Ridge, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basking_Ridge,_New_Jersey).

## Research {#Research}

### Coding theory {#Coding_theory}

The [Gilbert–Varshamov bound](wikipedia://en/1882350), proved independently in 1952 by Gilbert and in 1957 by Rom Varshamov,G52 is a mathematical theorem that guarantees the existence of [error-correcting codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error-correcting_code) that have a high transmission rate as a function of their length, alphabet size, and [Hamming distance](wikipedia://en/41227) between codewords (a parameter that controls the number of errors that can be corrected). The main idea is that in a [maximal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_element) code (one to which no additional codeword can be added), the Hamming balls of the given distance must cover the entire codespace, so the number of codewords must at least equal the total volume of the codespace divided by the volume of a single ball. For 30 years, until the invention of [algebraic geometry codes](wikipedia://en/1882683) in 1982, codes constructed in this way were the best ones known.

The [Gilbert–Elliott model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert–Elliott_model), developed by Gilbert in 1960 and E. O. Elliot in 1963,G60 is a mathematical model for the analysis of transmission channels in which the errors occur in bursts. It posits that the channel may be in either of two different states, with different error rates, that errors occur independently of each other once the state is known, and that the changes from one state to the other are governed by a [Markov chain](wikipedia://en/60876). It is "very convenient and often used" in the analysis of modern communications systems such as data links to mobile telephones.

### Probability theory {#Probability_theory}

In the mathematics of [shuffling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuffling) [playing cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playing_card), the [Gilbert–Shannon–Reeds model](wikipedia://en/39949312), developed in 1955 by Gilbert and [Claude Shannon](wikipedia://en/5693)G55 and independently in unpublished work in 1981 by Jim Reeds, is a probability distribution on permutations of a set of $n$ items that, according to experiments by [Persi Diaconis](wikipedia://en/65467), accurately models human-generated riffle shuffles. In this model, a deck of cards is split at a point chosen randomly according to a [binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876), and the two parts are [merged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merge_algorithm) with the order of merging chosen uniformly at random among all possible mergers. Equivalently, it is the inverse of a permutation formed by choosing independently at random for each card whether to put it into one of two piles (maintaining the original order of the cards within each pile), and then stacking the two piles on top of each other.

[Gilbert tessellations](wikipedia://en/36142343) are a mathematical model of [crack formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fracture) introduced by Gilbert in 1967.G67 In this model, fractures begin at a set of random points, with random orientations, chosen according to a [Poisson process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_process), and then grow at a constant rate until they terminate by running into previously formed cracks.

### Random networks {#Random_networks}

Central to the theory of [random graphs](wikipedia://en/358477) is the [Erdős–Rényi model](wikipedia://en/10477221), in which edges are chosen randomly for a fixed set of $n$ vertices. It was introduced in two forms in 1959 by Gilbert, [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284), and [Alfréd Rényi](wikipedia://en/614988).G59 In Gilbert's $G(n, p)$ form, each potential edge is chosen to be included in the graph or excluded from it, independently of the other edges, with probability $p$. Thus, the expected number of edges is $pn(n - 1)/2$, but the actual number of edges can vary randomly and all graphs have a nonzero probability of being selected. In contrast, in the $G(n, M)$ model introduced by Erdős and Rényi, the graph is chosen uniformly at random among all $M$-edge graphs; the number of edges is fixed, but the edges are not independent of each other, because the presence of an edge in one position is negatively correlated with the presence of an edge in a different position. Although these two models end up having similar properties, the $G(n, p)$ model is often more convenient to work with due to the independence of its edges.

In 1961, Gilbert introduced the random plane networkG61 (more commonly referred to now as a [random geometric graph](wikipedia://en/12106740) (RGG), or Gilbert disk model, where random points on the plane connect if and only if they are within some critical connection range. He suggested wireless communication networks as the main application for this work, and studied the [percolation theory](wikipedia://en/187805) of these networks, giving rise to the field of [continuum percolation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuum_percolation_theory). Gilbert was able to provide upper and lower bounds for the critical range at which this network contains an infinite connected component.

### Other contributions {#Other_contributions}

Gilbert and [Henry O. Pollak](wikipedia://en/19662367) worked on the [Steiner tree problem](wikipedia://en/498304) in 1968, formulating it in a way that unified it with [network flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_flow) problems.GP68 In their model, one is given a [flow network](wikipedia://en/645676) in which each edge is given both a cost and a capacity, and a matrix of flow amounts between different pairs of terminal vertices; the task is to find a subnetwork of minimum cost whose capacities are sufficient to support a flow with the given flow amounts between any pair of terminals. When the flow amounts are all equal, this reduces to the classical Steiner tree problem. This work also formulated the [Gilbert–Pollak conjecture](wikipedia://en/72307852) on the ratio between the length of the Steiner tree and minimum spanning tree.GP68 Although it was thought to have been proven in the early 1990s, it remains unsolved.

Gilbert discovered [Costas arrays](wikipedia://en/2068445) independently of and in the same year as [Costas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_P._Costas_%28engineer%29),G65 and is also known for his work with [John Riordan](wikipedia://en/11751393) on counting [necklaces](wikipedia://en/6226587) in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170). He collaborated with [Fan Chung](wikipedia://en/246562), [Ron Graham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Graham_%28mathematician%29), and [Jack van Lint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_van_Lint) on partitions of rectangles into smaller rectangles.CGG
