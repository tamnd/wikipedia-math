# Baumslag–Solitar group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Baumslag–Solitar group"}

*Image: Baumslag-Solitar Cayley.svg.* One sheet of the [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of the Baumslag–Solitar group $\operatorname{BS}(1, 2)$. Red edges correspond to $a$ and blue edges correspond to $b$.

*Image: Baumslag-Solitar Cayley 3D.svg.* The sheets of the [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of the Baumslag-Solitar group $\operatorname{BS}(1, 2)$ fit together into an infinite [binary tree](wikipedia://en/4321).

*Image: BS(1,2) Cayley Graph.gif.* Visualization comparing the sheet and the [binary tree](wikipedia://en/4321) [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of $\text{BS}(1,2)$. Red and blue edges correspond to $a$ and $b$, respectively.

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) field of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), the **Baumslag–Solitar groups** are examples of two-generator one-relator groups that play an important role in [combinatorial group theory](wikipedia://en/979834) and [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164) as (counter)examples and test-cases. They are given by the [group presentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_presentation)

:::

$$\left \langle a, b \ : \ b a^m b^{-1} = a^n \right \rangle.$$

For each integer $m$ and $n$, the Baumslag–Solitar group is denoted $\operatorname{BS}(m, n)$. The relation in the presentation is called the **Baumslag–Solitar relation**.

Some of the various $\operatorname{BS}(m, n)$ are well-known groups. $\operatorname{BS}(1, 1)$ is the [free abelian group](wikipedia://en/247296) on two [generators](wikipedia://en/99945), and $\operatorname{BS}(1, -1)$ is the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of the [Klein bottle](wikipedia://en/17412).

The groups were defined by [Gilbert Baumslag](wikipedia://en/11449648) and [Donald Solitar](wikipedia://en/43651852) in 1962 to provide examples of non-[Hopfian groups](wikipedia://en/11486001). The groups contain [residually finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residually_finite) groups, Hopfian groups that are not residually finite, and non-Hopfian groups.

## Linear representation {#Linear_representation}

Define

$$A= \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad B= \begin{pmatrix}\frac{n}{m}&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

The matrix group $G$ generated by $A$ and $B$ is a homomorphic image of $\operatorname{BS}(m, n)$, via the homomorphism induced by

$$a\mapsto A, \qquad b\mapsto B.$$

This will not, in general, be an isomorphism. For instance if $\operatorname{BS}(m, n)$ is not [residually finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residually_finite) (i.e. if it is not the case that $|m| = 1$, $|n| = 1$, or $|m| = |n|$[^o3]) it cannot be isomorphic to a finitely generated [linear group](wikipedia://en/3983656), which is known to be [residually finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residually_finite) by a theorem of [Anatoly Maltsev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Maltsev).[^o4]

## History {#History}

The group $\operatorname{BS}(1, 2)$ first appeared in a 1951 paper of Graham Higman.[^o6] It was for this reason that, according to Meier,[^o7] "Baumslag \[...\] waged a vigorous, sustained, and ultimately doomed campaign against referring to $\operatorname{BS}(1, 2)$ as a Baumslag–Solitar group."
