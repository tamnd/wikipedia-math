# Cage (graph theory)

*Image: Tutte eight cage.svg.* The [Tutte $(3,8)$-cage](wikipedia://en/1396880).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cage"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) field of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **cage** is a [regular graph](wikipedia://en/85821) that has as few [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) as possible for its [girth](wikipedia://en/12013).

:::

Formally, an $(r, g)$-graph is defined to be a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) in which each vertex has exactly $r$ neighbors, and in which the shortest [cycle](wikipedia://en/168609) has a length of exactly $g$. An $(r, g)$-cage is an $(r, g)$-graph with the smallest possible number of vertices, among all $(r, g)$-graphs. A $(3, g)$-cage is often called a $g$-cage.

It is known that an $(r, g)$-graph exists for any combination of $r \ge 2$ and $g \ge 3$. It follows that all $(r, g)$-cages exist.

If a [Moore graph](wikipedia://en/4112719) exists with degree $r$ and girth $g$, it must be a cage. Moreover, the bounds on the sizes of Moore graphs generalize to cages: any cage with [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) girth $g$ must have at least

$$1 + r\sum_{i=0}^{(g-3)/2}(r-1)^i$$

vertices, and any cage with [even](wikipedia://en/143135) girth $g$ must have at least

$$2\sum_{i=0}^{(g-2)/2}(r-1)^i$$

vertices. Any $(r, g)$-graph with exactly this many vertices is by definition a Moore graph and therefore automatically a cage.

There may exist multiple cages for a given combination of $r$ and $g$. For instance there are three [non-isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) $(3, 10)$-cages, each with 70 vertices: the [Balaban 10-cage](wikipedia://en/23714047), the [Harries graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harries_graph) and the [Harries–Wong graph](wikipedia://en/23702758). But there is only one $(3, 11)$-cage: the [Balaban 11-cage](wikipedia://en/23761806) (with 112 vertices).

## Known cages {#Known_cages}

A 1-regular graph has no cycle, and a [connected](wikipedia://en/1614492) 2-regular graph has girth equal to its number of vertices, so cages are only of interest for *r* ≥ 3. The (*r*,3)-cage is a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) *K*<sub>*r*+1</sub> on *r* + 1 vertices, and the (*r*,4)-cage is a [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) *K*<sub>*r*,*r*</sub> on 2*r* vertices.

Notable cages include:

- (3,5)-cage: the [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926), 10 vertices
- (3,6)-cage: the [Heawood graph](wikipedia://en/1396870), 14 vertices
- (3,7)-cage: the [McGee graph](wikipedia://en/23714466), 24 vertices
- (3,8)-cage: the [Tutte–Coxeter graph](wikipedia://en/1396880), 30 vertices
- (3,10)-cage: the [Balaban 10-cage](wikipedia://en/23714047), 70 vertices
- (3,11)-cage: the [Balaban 11-cage](wikipedia://en/23761806), 112 vertices
- (3,12)-cage: the [Tutte 12-cage](wikipedia://en/24384368), 126 vertices
- (4,5)-cage: the [Robertson graph](wikipedia://en/24107869), 19 vertices
- (7,5)-cage: The [Hoffman–Singleton graph](wikipedia://en/4112667), 50 vertices.
- When *r* − 1 is a [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483), the (*r*,6) cages are the incidence graphs of [projective planes](wikipedia://en/24350).
- When *r* − 1 is a prime power, the (*r*,8) and (*r*,12) cages are [generalized polygons](wikipedia://en/4027364).

## Asymptotics {#Asymptotics}

For large values of *g*, the Moore bound implies that the number *n* of vertices must grow at least [exponentially](wikipedia://en/191933) as a function of *g*. Equivalently, *g* can be at most proportional to the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) of *n*. More precisely,

$$g \le 2\log_{r-1} n + O(1).$$

It is believed that this bound is tight or close to tight (Bollobás Szemerédi 2002). The best known lower bounds on *g* are also logarithmic, but with a smaller constant factor (implying that *n* grows exponentially but at a higher rate than the Moore bound). Specifically, the construction of [Ramanujan graphs](wikipedia://en/849412) defined by (Lubotzky Phillips Sarnak 1988) satisfy the bound

$$g \ge \frac{4}{3}\log_{r-1} n + O(1).$$

This bound was improved slightly by (Lazebnik Ustimenko Woldar 1995).

It is unlikely that these graphs are themselves cages, but their existence gives an upper bound to the number of vertices needed in a cage.
