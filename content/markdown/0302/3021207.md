# Current (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Current"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), more particularly in [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), [differential topology](wikipedia://en/8562), and [geometric measure theory](wikipedia://en/12169694), a ***k*-current** in the sense of [Georges de Rham](wikipedia://en/398699) is a [functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) on the space of [compactly supported](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compactly_supported) [differential *k*-forms](wikipedia://en/221519), on a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) *M*. Currents formally behave like [Schwartz distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwartz_distribution) on a space of differential forms, but in a geometric setting, they can represent integration over a [submanifold](wikipedia://en/525095), generalizing the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021), or more generally even [directional derivatives](wikipedia://en/487859) of delta functions ([multipoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multipole)) spread out along subsets of *M*.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $\Omega_c^m(M)$ denote the space of smooth *m*-[forms](wikipedia://en/221519) with [compact support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support) on a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) $M.$ A current is a [linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) on $\Omega_c^m(M)$ which is continuous in the sense of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29). Thus a linear functional

$$T : \Omega_c^m(M)\to \mathbb{R}$$

is an *m*-dimensional current if it is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) in the following sense: If a sequence $\omega_k$ of smooth forms, all supported in the same [compact set](wikipedia://en/6042), is such that all derivatives of all their coefficients tend uniformly to 0 when $k$ tends to infinity, then $T(\omega_k)$ tends to 0.

The space $\mathcal D_m(M)$ of *m*-dimensional currents on $M$ is a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) with operations defined by

$$(T+S)(\omega) := T(\omega)+S(\omega),\qquad (\lambda T)(\omega) := \lambda T(\omega).$$

Much of the theory of distributions carries over to currents with minimal adjustments. For example, one may define the **support** of a current $T \in \mathcal{D}_m(M)$ as the complement of the biggest [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) $U \subset M$ such that

$$T(\omega) = 0$$

whenever $\omega \in \Omega_c^m(U)$

The [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of $\mathcal D_m(M)$ consisting of currents with support (in the sense above) that is a compact subset of $M$ is denoted $\mathcal E_m(M).$

:::

## Homological theory {#Homological_theory}

[Integration](wikipedia://en/15532) over a compact [rectifiable](wikipedia://en/2644238) [oriented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29) submanifold *M* ([with boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold_with_boundary)) of dimension *m* defines an *m*-current, denoted by $[[M]]$:

$$[[M]](\omega)=\int_M \omega.$$

If the [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) ∂*M* of *M* is rectifiable, then it too defines a current by integration, and by virtue of [Stokes' theorem](wikipedia://en/25441497) one has:

$$[[\partial M]](\omega) = \int_{\partial M}\omega = \int_M d\omega = [[M]](d\omega).$$

This relates the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) *d* with the [boundary operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_operator) ∂ on the [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) of *M*.

In view of this formula we can *define* a **boundary operator** on arbitrary currents

$$\partial : \mathcal D_{m+1} \to \mathcal D_m$$

via duality with the exterior derivative by

$$(\partial T)(\omega) := T(d\omega)$$

for all compactly supported *m*-forms $\omega.$

Certain subclasses of currents which are closed under $\partial$ can be used instead of all currents to create a homology theory, which can satisfy the [Eilenberg–Steenrod axioms](wikipedia://en/3122600) in certain cases. A classical example is the subclass of integral currents on Lipschitz neighborhood retracts.

## Topology and norms {#Topology_and_norms}

The space of currents is naturally endowed with the [weak-\* topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak-*_topology), which will be further simply called *weak convergence*. A [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $T_k$ of currents, [converges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point) to a current $T$ if

$$T_k(\omega) \to T(\omega),\qquad \forall \omega.$$

It is possible to define several [norms](wikipedia://en/990534) on subspaces of the space of all currents. One such norm is the *mass norm*. If $\omega$ is an *m*-form, then define its **comass** by

$$\|\omega\| := \sup\{\left|\langle \omega,\xi\rangle\right| : \xi \mbox{ is a unit, simple, }m\mbox{-vector}\}.$$

So if $\omega$ is a [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_rank) *m*-form, then its mass norm is the usual L<sup>∞</sup>-norm of its coefficient. The **mass** of a current $T$ is then defined as

$$\mathbf M (T) := \sup\{ T(\omega) : \sup_x |\vert\omega(x)|\vert\le 1\}.$$

The mass of a current represents the *weighted area* of the generalized surface. A current such that **M**(*T*) \< ∞ is representable by integration of a regular [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322) by a version of the [Riesz representation theorem](wikipedia://en/26452). This is the starting point of [homological integration](wikipedia://en/23029956).

An intermediate norm is Whitney's *flat norm*, defined by

$$\mathbf F (T) := \inf \{\mathbf M(T - \partial A) + \mathbf M(A) : A\in\mathcal E_{m+1}\}.$$

Two currents are close in the mass norm if they coincide away from a small part. On the other hand, they are close in the flat norm if they coincide up to a small deformation.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

Recall that

$$\Omega_c^0(\mathbb{R}^n)\equiv C^\infty_c(\mathbb{R}^n)$$

so that the following defines a 0-current:

$$T(f) = f(0).$$

In particular every [signed](wikipedia://en/2985811) [regular measure](wikipedia://en/3390453) $\mu$ is a 0-current:

$$T(f) = \int f(x)\, d\mu(x).$$

Let (*x*, *y*, *z*) be the coordinates in $\mathbb{R}^3.$ Then the following defines a 2-current (one of many):

$$T(a\,dx\wedge dy + b\,dy\wedge dz + c\,dx\wedge dz) := \int_0^1 \int_0^1 b(x,y,0)\, dx \, dy.$$
