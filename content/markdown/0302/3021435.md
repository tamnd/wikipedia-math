# Primary ideal

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Primary ideal"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990), a proper [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) $\mathfrak q$ of a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) $A$ is said to be **primary** if whenever $xy$ is an element of $\mathfrak q$ then $x$ or $y^n$ is also an element of $\mathfrak q$, for some $n>0$. For example, in the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) $\mathbb Z$, $(p^n)$ is a primary ideal if $p$ is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666).

:::

The notion of primary ideals is important in commutative [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424) because every ideal of a [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129) has a [primary decomposition](wikipedia://en/1208391), that is, can be written as an intersection of finitely many primary ideals. This result is known as the [Lasker–Noether theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasker–Noether_theorem). Consequently,[^o2] an [irreducible ideal](wikipedia://en/22260381) of a Noetherian ring is primary.

Various methods of generalizing primary ideals to noncommutative rings exist,[^o3] but the topic is most often studied for commutative rings. Therefore, the rings in this article are assumed to be commutative rings with identity.

## Examples and properties {#Examples_and_properties}

- The definition can be rephrased in a more apparently symmetrical manner: a proper ideal $\mathfrak{q}$ is primary if, whenever $x y \in \mathfrak{q}$, $x$ or $y$ are elements of $\mathfrak{q}$, or both $x$ and $y$ lie in $\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}$, the [radical](wikipedia://en/252009) of $\mathfrak{q}$; i.e., $xy\in\mathfrak{q}\implies(x\in\mathfrak{q})\lor(y\in\mathfrak{q})\lor((x\in\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}})\land (y\in\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}})).$
- A proper ideal ${\mathfrak q}$ of $R$ is primary if and only if every [zero divisor](wikipedia://en/51441) in $R/{\mathfrak q}$ is nilpotent. (Compare this to the case of prime ideals, where ${\mathfrak p}$ is prime if and only if every zero divisor in $R/{\mathfrak p}$ is actually zero.)
- Any [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) is primary, and moreover an ideal is prime if and only if it is primary and [semiprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiprime_ideal) (also called [radical ideal](wikipedia://en/252009) in the commutative case).
- Every primary ideal is [primal](wikipedia://en/25360385).[^o5]
- If ${\mathfrak q}$ is a primary ideal, then the [radical](wikipedia://en/252009) of ${\mathfrak q}$ is necessarily a prime ideal ${\mathfrak p}$, and this ideal is called the [associated prime ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associated_prime_ideal) of ${\mathfrak q}$. In this situation, ${\mathfrak q}$ is said to be **${\mathfrak p}$-primary**.
  - On the other hand, an ideal whose radical is prime is not necessarily primary: for example, if $R = k[x,y,z]/(x y - z^2)$, $\mathfrak{p} = (\overline{x}, \overline{z})$, and $\mathfrak{q} = \mathfrak{p}^2$, then $\mathfrak{p}$ is prime and $\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}} = \mathfrak{p}$, but we have $\overline{x} \overline{y} = {\overline{z}}^2 \in \mathfrak{p}^2 = \mathfrak{q}$, $\overline{x} \not \in \mathfrak{q}$, and ${\overline{y}}^n \not \in \mathfrak{q}$ for all $n>0$, so $\mathfrak{q}$ is not primary. The primary decomposition of $\mathfrak{q}$ is $(\overline{x}) \cap ({\overline{x}}^2, \overline{x} \overline{z}, \overline{y})$; here $(\overline{x})$ is $\mathfrak{p}$-primary and $({\overline{x}}^2, \overline{x} \overline{z}, \overline{y})$ is $(\overline{x}, \overline{y}, \overline{z})$-primary.
    - An ideal whose radical is *maximal*, however, is primary.
    - Every ideal ${\mathfrak q}$ with radical ${\mathfrak p}$ *is* contained in a smallest ${\mathfrak p}$-primary ideal: all elements $a$ such that $ax\in {\mathfrak q}$ for some $x\notin {\mathfrak p}$. The smallest ${\mathfrak p}$-primary ideal containing ${\mathfrak p}^n$ is called the $n$th **[symbolic power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_power_of_a_prime_ideal)** of ${\mathfrak p}$.
- If ${\mathfrak p}$ is a maximal prime ideal, then any ideal whose radical is ${\mathfrak p}$ is ${\mathfrak p}$-primary (and vice versa). In particular, a power of ${\mathfrak p}$ or an ideal containing a power of ${\mathfrak p}$ is ${\mathfrak p}$-primary. But a ${\mathfrak p}$-primary ideal need not be a power of ${\mathfrak p}$ and need not contain a power of ${\mathfrak p}$; for example, the ideal $(x,y^2)$ is ${\mathfrak p}$-primary for the ideal ${\mathfrak p}=(x,y)$ in the ring $k[x,y]$, but is not a power of ${\mathfrak p}$; however, it contains ${\mathfrak p}^2$.
- If $A$ is a [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129) and ${\mathfrak p}$ a prime ideal, then the kernel of $A \to A_{\mathfrak p}$, the map from $A$ to the [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) of $A$ at ${\mathfrak p}$, is the intersection of all ${\mathfrak p}$-primary ideals.[^o6]
- If $\mathfrak{p}$ is maximal, a finite nonempty product of $\mathfrak{p}$-primary ideals is $\mathfrak{p}$-primary but an [infinite product](wikipedia://en/26382006) of $\mathfrak{p}$-primary ideals may not be $\mathfrak p$-primary; since for example, in a Noetherian local ring with maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$, $\textstyle\bigcap_{n > 0} \mathfrak{m}^n = 0$ ([Krull intersection theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krull_intersection_theorem)) where each $\mathfrak{m}^n$ is $\mathfrak{m}$-primary, for example the infinite product of the maximal (and hence prime and hence primary) ideal $m=\langle x,y \rangle$ of the local ring $K[x,y]/\langle x^2, xy\rangle$ yields the zero ideal, which in this case is not primary (because the zero divisor $y$ is not nilpotent). In fact, in a Noetherian ring, a nonempty product of $\mathfrak{p}$-primary ideals $Q_i$ is $\mathfrak{p}$-primary if and only if there exists some integer $n > 0$ such that $\mathfrak{p}^n \subset \textstyle\bigcap_i Q_i$.[^o7]

## Decomposition of ideals into primary ideals {#Decomposition_of_ideals_into_primary_ideals}

The primary decomposition of ideals by the Lasker–Noether theorem may be seen as a generalization of the [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556), which applies to the integers $\mathbb{Z}$ and other [unique factorization domains](wikipedia://en/60546), to general Noetherian rings. While the unique factorization of elements of a ring into the product of irreducible elements (up to units and reordering) fails in the general case, the Lasker–Noether theorem states that the *ideals* of a Noetherian ring do still have a type of "unique factorization": any ideal in a Noetherian ring can be written as an intersection of primary ideals of the ring in a *[primary decomposition](wikipedia://en/1208391)*, and while these component primary ideals are not necessarily unique, the radicals of these components, the *[associated primes](wikipedia://en/17286442)* of the ideal, are unique up to reordering:

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem (Lasker–Noether)"}

**Theorem (Lasker–Noether).**

Let $A$ be a commutative Noetherian ring and let $\mathfrak{a}$ be an ideal of $A$. Then $\mathfrak{a}$ may be written as the intersection of finitely many primary ideals; that is:

$$\mathfrak{a}=\bigcap _{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{q}_{i}$$

with each $\mathfrak{q}_i$ primary. The expression of $\mathfrak{a}$ in this way is said to be a *primary decomposition* of $\mathfrak{a}.$ Furthermore, if (1) the $\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}_i}$ are all distinct and (2) $\mathfrak{q}_i\nsupseteq \bigcap_{j\neq i} \mathfrak{q}_j$ for each $i$, then the primary decomposition is said to be *irredundant*. Any primary decomposition can be reduced to an irredundant one, and, if the aforementioned primary decomposition of $\mathfrak{a}$ is irredundant, and

$$\mathfrak{a}=\bigcap _{i=1}^{n'}\mathfrak{q}'_{i}$$

is another irredundant primary decomposition of $\mathfrak{a}$, then $n=n'$ and $\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}_i}=\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}'_i}$ for each $i,$ after possibly reindexing the $\mathfrak{q}'_i.$

:::
