# Discriminant of an algebraic number field

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Discriminant of an algebraic number field"}

*Image: Discriminant49CubicFieldFundamentalDomain.png.* A fundamental domain of the ring of integers of the field *K* obtained from **Q** by adjoining a root of *x*<sup>3</sup> − *x*<sup>2</sup> − 2*x* + 1. This fundamental domain sits inside *K* ⊗<sub>**Q**</sub> **R**. The discriminant of *K* is 49 = 7<sup>2</sup>. Accordingly, the volume of the fundamental domain is 7 and *K* is only [ramified](wikipedia://en/384644) at 7.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **discriminant** of an [algebraic number field](wikipedia://en/28730822) is a numerical [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) that, loosely speaking, measures the size of the ([ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of the) algebraic number field. More specifically, it is proportional to the squared volume of the [fundamental domain](wikipedia://en/469365) of the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613), and it regulates which [primes](wikipedia://en/23666) are [ramified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramified_prime#In_algebraic_number_theory).

:::

The discriminant is one of the most basic invariants of a number field, and occurs in several important [analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_Number_Theory) formulas such as the [functional equation](wikipedia://en/595708) of the [Dedekind zeta function](wikipedia://en/1131243) of $K$, and the [analytic class number formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_class_number_formula) for $K$. [A theorem](wikipedia://en/39606032) of [Hermite](wikipedia://en/22870422) states that there are only finitely many number fields of bounded discriminant, however determining this quantity is still an [open problem](wikipedia://en/1333335), and the subject of current research.[^o2]

The discriminant of $K$ can be referred to as the **absolute discriminant** of $K$ to distinguish it from the **relative discriminant** of an [extension](wikipedia://en/11634) $K/L$ of number fields. The latter is an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) in the ring of integers of $L$, and like the absolute discriminant it indicates which primes are ramified in $K/L$. It is a generalization of the absolute discriminant allowing for $L$ to be bigger than $\mathbb{Q}$; in fact, when $L=\mathbb{Q}$, the relative discriminant of $K/\mathbb{Q}$ is the [principal ideal](wikipedia://en/145126) of $\mathbb{Z}$ generated by the absolute discriminant of $K$.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $K$ be an algebraic number field, and let $\mathcal{O}_K$ be its [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613). Let $b_1,\dots,b_n$ be an [integral basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_basis) of $\mathcal{O}_K$ (i.e. a basis as a [$\mathbb{Z}$-module](wikipedia://en/276410)), and let $\{\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_n\}$ be the set of embeddings of $K$ into the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) (i.e. [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) [ring homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/26411) $K\to\mathbb{C}$). The **discriminant** of $K$ is the [square](wikipedia://en/659942) of the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the $n\times n$ [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $B$ whose $(i,j)$-entry is $\sigma_i(b_j)$. Symbolically,

$$\Delta_K=\det\left(\begin{array}{cccc} \sigma_1(b_1) & \sigma_1(b_2) &\cdots & \sigma_1(b_n) \\ \sigma_2(b_1) & \ddots & & \vdots \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\ \sigma_n(b_1) & \cdots & \cdots & \sigma_n(b_n) \end{array}\right)^2.$$

Equivalently, the [trace](wikipedia://en/457105) from $K$ to $\mathbb{Q}$ can be used. Specifically, define the [trace form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace_form#Trace_form_and_discriminant) to be the matrix whose $(i,j)$-entry is $\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(b_ib_j)$. This matrix equals $B^T\!B$, so defining the discriminant of $K$ as the determinant of this matrix gives us the same result as the preceding definition.

The discriminant of an [order](wikipedia://en/1114171) in $K$ with [integral basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_basis) $b_1,\dots,b_n$ is defined in the same way.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- [Quadratic number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_fields): let $d$ be a [square-free integer](wikipedia://en/29525); then the discriminant of $K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ is[^o8]

$$\Delta_K=\left\{\begin{array}{ll} d &\text{if }d\equiv 1\pmod 4 \\ 4d &\text{if }d\equiv 2,3\pmod 4. \\\end{array}\right.$$

An integer that occurs as the discriminant of a quadratic number field is called a [fundamental discriminant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_discriminant).[^o7]

- [Cyclotomic fields](wikipedia://en/30872462): let $n>2$ be an integer, let $\zeta_n$ be a [primitive *n*-th root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950), and let $K_n=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$ be the $n$-th cyclotomic field. The discriminant of $K_n$ is given by[^o8][^o9]

$$\Delta_{K_n} = (-1)^{\varphi(n)/2} \frac{n^{\varphi(n)}}{\displaystyle\prod_{p|n} p^{\varphi(n)/(p-1)}}$$

where $\varphi(n)$ is [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452), and the product in the denominator is over primes $p$ dividing $n$.

- Power bases: In the case where the ring $\mathcal{O}_K$ of algebraic integers has a [power integral basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_integral_basis), that is, can be written as $\mathcal{O}_K=\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$, the discriminant of $K$ is equal to the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607) of the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106) of $\alpha$. To see this, one can choose the integral basis of $\mathcal{O}_K$ to be

$$b_1=1, b_2=\alpha, b_3=\alpha^2, \dots,b_n = \alpha^{n-1}.$$

Then, the matrix $B$ in the definition is the [Vandermonde matrix](wikipedia://en/348641) associated to $\alpha_i=\sigma_i(\alpha)$, whose squared determinant is

$$\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(\alpha_i-\alpha_j)^2,$$

which is exactly the definition of the discriminant of the minimal polynomial.

- Let $K=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ be the number field obtained by [adjoining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjunction_%28field_theory%29) a [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) $\alpha$ of the [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) $x^3-x^2-2x-8$. This is [Richard Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106)'s original example of a number field whose ring of integers does not possess a power basis. An integral basis is given by

$$\left\{1,\alpha,\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)}{2}\right\}$$

and the discriminant of $K$ is −503.[^o10][^o11]

- Repeated discriminants: the discriminant of a quadratic field uniquely identifies it, but this is not true, in general, for [higher-degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_of_a_number_field) number fields. For example, there are two [non-isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) [cubic fields](wikipedia://en/22076179) of discriminant 3969. They are obtained by adjoining a root of the polynomial $x^3-21x+28$ or $x^3-21x-35$, respectively.[^o12]

## Basic results {#Basic_results}

- **Brill's theorem**:[^o15] The [sign](wikipedia://en/7951270) of the discriminant is $(-1)^{r_2}$ where $r_2$ is the number of [complex places](wikipedia://en/28730822#Archimedean_places) of $K$.[^o16]
- A prime $p$ ramifies in $K$ if and only if $p$ divides $\Delta_K$.[^o17][^o18]
- **Stickelberger's theorem**:[^o19]

$$\Delta_K\equiv 0\text{ or }1 \pmod 4.$$

- **[Minkowski's bound](wikipedia://en/8491791)**:[^o20] Let $n$ denote the [degree](wikipedia://en/4382697) of the extension $K/\mathbb{Q}$ and $r_2$ the number of complex places of $K$, then

$$|\Delta_K|^{1/2}\geq \frac{n^n}{n!}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)^{r_2} \geq \frac{n^n}{n!}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)^{n/2}.$$

- **Minkowski's theorem**:[^o21] If $K$ is not $\mathbb{Q}$, then $|\Delta_K|>1$ (this follows directly from the Minkowski bound).
- **[Hermite–Minkowski theorem](wikipedia://en/39606032)**:[^o22] Let $N$ be a positive integer. There are only finitely many (up to isomorphisms) algebraic number fields $K$ with $|\Delta_K|<N$. Again, this follows from the Minkowski bound together with Hermite's theorem (that there are only finitely many algebraic number fields with prescribed discriminant).

:::

## History {#History}

*Image: Dedekind.jpeg.* [Richard Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106) showed that every number field possesses an integral basis, allowing him to define the discriminant of an arbitrary number field.[^o24]

The definition of the discriminant of a general algebraic number field, *K*, was given by Dedekind in 1871.[^o24] At this point, he already knew the relationship between the discriminant and ramification.[^o25]

Hermite's theorem predates the general definition of the discriminant with Charles Hermite publishing a proof of it in 1857.[^n1] In 1877, [Alexander von Brill](wikipedia://en/28565454) determined the sign of the discriminant.[^n2] [Leopold Kronecker](wikipedia://en/318450) first stated Minkowski's theorem in 1882,[^n3] though the first proof was given by Hermann Minkowski in 1891.[^n4] In the same year, Minkowski published his bound on the discriminant.[^n5] Near the end of the nineteenth century, [Ludwig Stickelberger](wikipedia://en/18870377) obtained his theorem on the residue of the discriminant modulo four.[^n6][^o32]

## Relative discriminant {#Relative_discriminant}

The discriminant defined above is sometimes referred to as the *absolute* discriminant of *K* to distinguish it from the **relative discriminant** Δ<sub>*K*/*L*</sub> of an extension of number fields *K*/*L*, which is an ideal in *O*<sub>*L*</sub>. The relative discriminant is defined in a fashion similar to the absolute discriminant, but must take into account that ideals in *O*<sub>*L*</sub> may not be principal and that there may not be an *O*<sub>*L*</sub> basis of *O*<sub>*K*</sub>. Let {σ<sub>1</sub>, ..., σ<sub>*n*</sub>} be the set of embeddings of *K* into **C** which are the identity on *L*. If *b*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *b*<sub>*n*</sub> is any basis of *K* over *L*, let *d*(*b*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *b*<sub>*n*</sub>) be the square of the determinant of the *n* by *n* matrix whose (*i*,*j*)-entry is σ<sub>*i*</sub>(*b*<sub>*j*</sub>). Then, the relative discriminant of *K*/*L* is the ideal generated by the *d*(*b*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *b*<sub>*n*</sub>) as {*b*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *b*<sub>*n*</sub>} varies over all integral bases of *K*/*L*. (i.e. bases with the property that *b<sub>i</sub>* ∈ *O<sub>K</sub>* for all *i*.) Alternatively, the relative discriminant of *K*/*L* is the [norm](wikipedia://en/460672) of the [different](wikipedia://en/2371783) of *K*/*L*.[^o35] When *L* = **Q**, the relative discriminant Δ<sub>*K*/**Q**</sub> is the principal ideal of **Z** generated by the absolute discriminant Δ<sub>*K*</sub> . In a [tower of fields](wikipedia://en/28194169) *K*/*L*/*F* the relative discriminants are related by

$$\Delta_{K/F} = \mathcal{N}_{L/F}\left({\Delta_{K/L}}\right) \Delta_{L/F}^{[K:L]}$$

where $\mathcal{N}$ denotes relative [norm](wikipedia://en/450555).[^o36]

### Ramification {#Ramification}

The relative discriminant regulates the [ramification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tame_ramification) data of the field extension *K*/*L*. A prime ideal *p* of *L* ramifies in *K* if, and only if, it divides the relative discriminant Δ<sub>*K*/*L*</sub>. An extension is unramified if, and only if, the discriminant is the unit ideal.[^o35] The Minkowski bound above shows that there are no non-trivial unramified extensions of **Q**. Fields larger than **Q** may have unramified extensions: for example, for any field with [class number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_number_%28number_theory%29) greater than one, its [Hilbert class field](wikipedia://en/3021036) is a non-trivial unramified extension.

:::

## Root discriminant {#Root_discriminant}

The **root discriminant** of a degree *n* number field *K* is defined by the formula

$\operatorname{rd}_K = |\Delta_K|^{1/n}.$[^o40]

The relation between relative discriminants in a tower of fields shows that the root discriminant does not change in an unramified extension.

### Asymptotic lower bounds {#Asymptotic_lower_bounds}

Given nonnegative rational numbers *ρ* and *σ*, not both 0, and a positive integer *n* such that the pair (*r*,2*s*) = (*ρn*,*σn*) is in **Z** × 2**Z**, let *α*<sub>*n*</sub>(*ρ*, *σ*) be the infimum of rd<sub>*K*</sub> as *K* ranges over degree *n* number fields with *r* real embeddings and 2*s* complex embeddings, and let *α*(*ρ*, *σ*) = liminf<sub>*n*→∞</sub>*α*<sub>*n*</sub>(*ρ*, *σ*). Then

$$\alpha(\rho,\sigma) \ge 60.8^\rho 22.3^\sigma,$$

and the [generalized Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/152567) implies the stronger bound

$\alpha(\rho,\sigma) \ge 215.3^\rho 44.7^\sigma .$[^o42]

There is also a lower bound that holds in all degrees, not just asymptotically: For totally real fields, the root discriminant is > 14, with 1229 exceptions.[^o43]

### Asymptotic upper bounds {#Asymptotic_upper_bounds}

On the other hand, the existence of an infinite [class field tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_field_tower) can give upper bounds on the values of *α*(*ρ*, *σ*). For example, the infinite class field tower over **Q**($\sqrt{-m}$) with *m* = 3·5·7·11·19 produces fields of arbitrarily large degree with root discriminant 2$\sqrt{m}$ ≈ 296.276,[^o42] so *α*(0,1) \< 296.276. Using *tamely ramified* towers, Hajir and Maire have shown that *α*(1,0) \< 954.3 and *α*(0,1) \< 82.2,[^o40] improving upon earlier bounds of Martinet.[^o42][^o45]

:::

## Relation to other quantities {#Relation_to_other_quantities}

- When embedded into $K\otimes_\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}$, the volume of the fundamental domain of *O*<sub>*K*</sub> is $\sqrt{|\Delta_K|}$ (sometimes a different [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) is used and the volume obtained is $2^{-r_2}\sqrt{|\Delta_K|}$, where *r*<sub>2</sub> is the number of complex places of *K*).
- Due to its appearance in this volume, the discriminant also appears in the functional equation of the Dedekind zeta function of *K*, and hence in the analytic class number formula, and the [Brauer–Siegel theorem](wikipedia://en/7515919).
- The relative discriminant of *K*/*L* is the [Artin conductor](wikipedia://en/34170016) of the [regular representation](wikipedia://en/360011) of the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of *K*/*L*. This provides a relation to the Artin conductors of the [characters](wikipedia://en/1384568) of the Galois group of *K*/*L*, called the [conductor-discriminant formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conductor-discriminant_formula).[^o47]
