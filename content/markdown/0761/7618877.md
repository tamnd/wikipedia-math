# Primon gas

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Primon gas"}

In [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416), the **primon gas** or **Riemann gas**[^o2] discovered by [Bernard Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Julia)[^o3] is a [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model) illustrating correspondences between [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) and methods in [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481) and [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems) such as the [Lee–Yang theorem](wikipedia://en/19661214). It is a quantum field theory of a set of non-interacting particles, the **primons**; it is called a [gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas) or a *free model* because the particles are non-interacting. The idea of the primon gas was independently discovered by Donald Spector.[^o4] Later works by Ioannis Bakas and [Mark Bowick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Bowick),[^o5] and Spector[^o6] explored the connection of such systems to [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305).

:::

## The model {#The_model}

### State space {#State_space}

Consider a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) **H** with an orthonormal basis of states $|p\rangle$ labelled by the [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) *p*. [Second quantization](wikipedia://en/418156) gives a new Hilbert space **K**, the [bosonic Fock space](wikipedia://en/230491) on **H**, where states describe collections of primes - which we can call **primons** if we think of them as analogous to particles in quantum field theory. This Fock space has an orthonormal basis given by finite [multisets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multisets) of primes. In other words, to specify one of these basis elements we can list the number $k_p = 0 , 1, 2, \dots$ of primons for each prime $p$:

$$|k_2, k_3, k_5, k_7, k_{11}, \ldots, k_p, \ldots\rangle$$

where the total $\sum_p k_p$ is finite. Since any positive natural number $n$ has a unique factorization into primes:

$$n = 2^{k_2} \cdot 3^{k_3} \cdot 5^{k_5} \cdot 7^{k_7} \cdot 11^{k_{11}} \cdots p^{k_p} \cdots$$

we can also denote the basis elements of the Fock space as simply $|n\rangle$ where $n = 1,2,3, \dots.$

In short, the Fock space for primons has an orthonormal basis given by the positive natural numbers, but we think of each such number $n$ as a collection of primons: its prime factors, counted with multiplicity.

### Identifying the Hamiltonian via the Koopman operator {#Identifying_the_Hamiltonian_via_the_Koopman_operator}

Given the state $x_n = n$, we may use the Koopman operator[^o10] $\Phi$ to lift dynamics from the space of states to the space of observables:

$$\Phi \circ \textbf{log} \circ x_n = \textbf{log} \circ F \circ x_n = \textbf{log} \circ x_{n+1}$$

where $\textbf{log}$ is an algorithm for integer factorisation, analogous to the discrete logarithm, and $F$ is the successor function. Thus, we have:

$$\textbf{log} \circ x_n = \bigoplus_k a_k \cdot \ln p_k$$

A precise motivation for defining the Koopman operator $\Phi$ is that it represents a global linearisation of $F$, which views linear combinations of eigenstates as integer partitions. In fact, the reader may easily check that the successor function is not a linear function:

$$\forall n \in \mathbb{N}, F(n) = n+1 \implies \forall x,y \in \mathbb{N}^*, F(x+y) \neq F(x)+F(y)$$

Hence, $\Phi$ is canonical.

### Energies {#Energies}

If we take a simple [quantum Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_Hamiltonian) *H* to have eigenvalues proportional to log *p*, that is,

$$H|p\rangle = E_p |p\rangle$$

with

$$E_p=E \log p$$

for some positive constant $E$, we are naturally led to

$$E_n = \sum_p k_p E_p = E \cdot \sum_p k_p \log p = E \log n$$

### Statistics of the phase-space dimension {#Statistics_of_the_phase-space_dimension}

Let's suppose we would like to know the average time, suitably-normalised, that the Riemann gas spends in a particular subspace. How might this frequency be related to the dimension of this subspace?

If we characterize distinct linear subspaces as Erdős-Kac data which have the form of sparse binary vectors, using the [Erdős–Kac theorem](wikipedia://en/6046005) we may actually demonstrate that this frequency depends upon nothing more than the dimension of the subspace. In fact, if $\omega(n)$ counts the number of unique prime divisors of $n \in \mathbb{N}$ then the Erdős–Kac law tells us that for large $n$:

$$\frac{\omega(n)-\ln \ln n}{\sqrt{\ln \ln n}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

has the standard normal distribution.

What is even more remarkable is that although the Erdős-Kac theorem has the form of a statistical observation, it could not have been discovered using statistical methods.[^o13] Indeed, for $X \sim U([1,N])$ the normal order of $\omega(X)$ only begins to emerge for $N \geq 10^{100}$.

### Statistical mechanics {#Statistical_mechanics}

The [partition function](wikipedia://en/16846849) *Z* of the primon gas is given by the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809):

$$Z(T) := \sum_{n=1}^\infty \exp \left(\frac{-E_n}{k_\text{B} T}\right) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \exp \left(\frac{-E \log n}{k_\text{B} T}\right) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^s} = \zeta (s)$$

with *s* = *E*/*k*<sub>B</sub>*T* where *k*<sub>B</sub> is the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant) and *T* is the absolute [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050).

The divergence of the zeta function at *s* = 1 corresponds to the divergence of the partition function at a [Hagedorn temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hagedorn_temperature) of *T*<sub>H</sub> = *E*/*k*<sub>B</sub>.

## Supersymmetric model {#Supersymmetric_model}

The above second-quantized model takes the particles to be [bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson). If the particles are taken to be [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion), then the [Pauli exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/24669) prohibits multi-particle states which include squares of primes. By the [spin–statistics theorem](wikipedia://en/352827), field states with an even number of particles are bosons, while those with an odd number of particles are fermions. The fermion operator [(−1)<sup>F</sup>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%28-1%29^F) has a very concrete realization in this model as the [Möbius function](wikipedia://en/20961) $\mu(n)$, in that the Möbius function is positive for bosons, negative for fermions, and zero on exclusion-principle-prohibited states.

## More complex models {#More_complex_models}

The connections between number theory and quantum field theory can be somewhat further extended into connections between [topological field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_field_theory) and [K-theory](wikipedia://en/246748), where, corresponding to the example above, the [spectrum of a ring](wikipedia://en/37291) takes the role of the spectrum of energy eigenvalues, the [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) take the role of the prime numbers, the [group representations](wikipedia://en/12695) take the role of integers, [group characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_character) taking the place the [Dirichlet characters](wikipedia://en/174515), and so on.
