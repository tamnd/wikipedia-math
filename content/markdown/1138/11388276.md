# Two-dimensionalism

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Two-dimensionalism"}

**Two-dimensionalism** is an approach to [semantics](wikipedia://en/29107) in [analytic philosophy](wikipedia://en/159211). It is a theory of how to determine the [sense and reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sense_and_reference) of a [word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word) and the [truth-value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth-value) of a [sentence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence_%28linguistics%29). It is intended to resolve the puzzle: How is it possible to discover empirically that a [necessary truth](wikipedia://en/3986852) is [true](wikipedia://en/29920)? Two-dimensionalism provides an analysis of the semantics of words and sentences that makes sense of this possibility. The theory was first developed by [Robert Stalnaker](wikipedia://en/4050095), but it has been advocated by numerous philosophers since, including [David Chalmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Chalmers).

:::

## Two-dimensional semantic analysis {#Two-dimensional_semantic_analysis}

According to two-dimensionalism, any statement, for example "Water is H<sub>2</sub>O", is taken to express two distinct [propositions](wikipedia://en/81094), often referred to as a *primary intension* and a *secondary intension*, which together compose its [meaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meaning_%28philosophy_of_language%29).[^o3][^o4]

The *primary intension* of a word or sentence is its [sense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sense_and_reference), i.e., is the idea or method by which we find its referent. In other words, it's how we identify something in any possible world before knowing its actual nature. The primary intension of "water" might be a description, such as *watery stuff* or "the clear, drinkable liquid that fills oceans and lakes." The entity identified by this intension could vary in different hypothetical worlds. In the [twin Earth thought experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_Earth_thought_experiment), for example, inhabitants might use "water" to mean their equivalent of water, even if its chemical composition is not H<sub>2</sub>O but XYZ. Thus, for that world, "water" does not refer to H<sub>2</sub>O.[^o3]

The *secondary intension* of "water" is whatever "water" refers to in this world. It's determined after we discover water's actual composition in our world. So, if we assign "water" the primary intension *watery stuff*, then the secondary intension of "water" is H<sub>2</sub>O, since H<sub>2</sub>O is *watery stuff* in this world. The secondary intension of "water" in our world is H<sub>2</sub>O, which is H<sub>2</sub>O in every world because unlike *watery stuff* it is impossible for H<sub>2</sub>O to be other than H<sub>2</sub>O. When considered according to its secondary intension, "Water is H<sub>2</sub>O" is true in every world. This explains how "water is XYZ" can be conceivable (using the primary intension) but not possible in this world (using the secondary intension).[^o3] Or in simpler words, water could conceivably be composed of molecules other than H<sub>2</sub>O, but is **actually not**.

## Impact {#Impact}

If two-dimensionalism is workable it solves some very important problems in the philosophy of language. [Saul Kripke](wikipedia://en/48039) has argued that "Water is H<sub>2</sub>O" is an example of a necessary truth which is true *[a posteriori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_evidence)*, since we had to discover that water was H<sub>2</sub>O, but given that it is true (which it is) it cannot be false. It would be absurd to claim that something that is water is not H<sub>2</sub>O, for these are known to be *identical*.

However, this contention that one and the same proposition can be both [a posteriori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_posteriori_%28epistemology%29) and necessary is considered absurd by some philosophers (as is Kripke's paired claim that the same proposition can be both [a priori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_priori_%28epistemology%29) and [contingent](wikipedia://en/17753389)).

For example, Robert Stalnaker's account of knowledge represents knowledge as a [relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relation_%28philosophy%29) on [possible worlds](wikipedia://en/780566), which entails that it is impossible for a proposition to fail to be a priori given that it is necessary. This can be proven as follows: If a proposition *P* is necessary it is true in all possible worlds. If *P* is true at all possible worlds and what we know are sets of possible worlds, then it is not possible *not* to know that *P*, for *P* is the case at all possible worlds in the set of worlds that we know. So if *P* is necessary then we know it necessarily, and *ipso facto* we know it a priori.[^o6]

Under two-dimensionalism, the problem disappears. The primary intension of "Water is H<sub>2</sub>O" is the *a posteriori* component, since it is contingent that the referent of "water" is H<sub>2</sub>O, while the secondary intension is the *necessary* component of the sentence, since it is necessary that the stuff we in fact call water is H<sub>2</sub>O. Neither intension gives us both a necessary and an *a posteriori* component. But one gets the false impression that the sentence expresses a *necessary a posteriori* proposition because this single sentence expresses two propositions, one *a posteriori* and one *necessary*.[^o6]

### In the philosophy of mind {#In_the_philosophy_of_mind}

Two-dimensional semantics has been used by David Chalmers to counter objections to the various arguments against [materialism in the philosophy of mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_materialism). Specifically, Chalmers deploys two-dimensional semantics to "bridge the (gap between) [epistemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemic) and [modal](wikipedia://en/333365) domains" in arguing from knowability or epistemic conceivability to what is necessary or possible (modalities).[^o8]

The reason Chalmers employs two-dimensional semantics is to avoid objections to conceivability implying possibility. For instance, it's claimed that we can conceive of water not having been H, but it's not possible that water isn't H. Chalmers replies that it *is* 1-possible that water wasn't H because we can imagine another substance XYZ with watery properties, but it's not 2-possible. Hence, objections to conceivability implying possibility are unfounded when these words are used more carefully.[^o8]

Chalmers then advances the following "two-dimensional argument against materialism".[^o8] Define P as all physical truths about the universe and Q as a truth about [phenomenal experience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenal_consciousness), such as that someone is conscious. Let "1-possible" refer to possibility relative to primary intension and "2-possible" relative to secondary intension.

> P&~Q is conceivable \[i.e., [zombies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_zombie) are conceivable\]
>
> If P&~Q is conceivable, then P&~Q is 1-possible
>
> If P&~Q is 1-possible, then P&~Q is 2-possible or [Russellian monism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_monism) is true.
>
> If P&~Q is 2-possible, materialism is false.
>
> Materialism is false or Russellian monism is true.

## Criticism {#Criticism}

[Scott Soames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott_Soames) is a notable opponent of two-dimensionalism, which he sees as an attempt to revive [Russelian](wikipedia://en/4163)–[Fregean](wikipedia://en/48416) [descriptivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descriptivist_theory_of_names) and to overturn what he sees as a "revolution" in semantics begun by [Kripke](wikipedia://en/48039) and others.[^o10] Soames argues that two-dimensionalism stems from a misreading of passages in Kripke (1980) as well as [Kaplan](wikipedia://en/1479115) (1989).[^o11]
