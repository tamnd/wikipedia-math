# Sources and sinks

*Image: ChampSelonSigneDeDiv.pdf.* Three examples of vector fields. From left to right: a field with a source, a field with a sink, a field without either.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sources and sinks"}

In the [physical sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_sciences), [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **sources and sinks** (sometimes **sources and drains**) is an analogy used to describe properties of [vector fields](wikipedia://en/62641). It generalizes the idea of fluid sources and sinks (like the [faucet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tap_%28valve%29) and [drain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drain_%28plumbing%29) of a bathtub) across different scientific disciplines. These terms describe points, regions, or entities where a vector field originates or terminates. This analogy is usually invoked when discussing the [continuity equation](wikipedia://en/410793), the [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) of the field and the [divergence theorem](wikipedia://en/151864). The analogy sometimes includes **swirls** and **saddles** for points that are neither of the two.

:::

In the case of [electric fields](wikipedia://en/41092) the idea of flow is replaced by [field lines](wikipedia://en/5000404) and the sources and sinks are [electric charges](wikipedia://en/9804).

## Description and fluid dynamics analogy {#Description_and_fluid_dynamics_analogy}

In physics, a vector field $\mathbf b(x,y,z)$ is a function that returns a [vector](wikipedia://en/23218956) and is defined for each point (with coordinates $x,y,z$) in a region of space. The idea of sources and sinks applies to $\mathbf b$ if it follows a [continuity equation](wikipedia://en/410793) of the form

$$\frac{\partial a}{\partial t}+\nabla \cdot \mathbf b = s,$$

where $t$ is time, $a$ is some quantity density associated to $\mathbf b$, and $s$ is the source-sink term. The points in space where $s>0$ are called a sources and when $s<0$ are called sinks. The integral version of the continuity equation is given by the [divergence theorem](wikipedia://en/151864).

These concepts originate from sources and sinks in [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034), where the flow is conserved per the continuity equation related to [conservation of mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_mass), given by

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}+\nabla \cdot \mathbf v = Q$$

where $\rho$ is the [mass density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) of the fluid, $\mathbf v$ is the [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity) vector, and $Q$ is the source-sink flow (fluid mass per unit volume per unit time). This equation implies that any emerging or disappearing amount of flow in a given volume must have a source or a sink, respectively. Sources represent locations where fluid is added to the region of space, and sinks represent points of removal of fluid. The term $Q$ is positive for a source and negative for a sink.[^o3] Note that for [incompressible flow](wikipedia://en/461477) or time-independent systems, $Q$ is directly related to the divergence as

$$\nabla\cdot \mathbf v=Q.$$

For this kind of flow, [solenoidal vector fields](wikipedia://en/419444) (no divergence) have no source or sinks. When at a given point $Q=0$ but the curl $\nabla\times \mathbf v \neq 0$, the point is sometimes called a swirl.[^o4][^o5] And when both divergence and curl are zero, the point is sometimes called a saddle.[^o5]

## Other examples in physics {#Other_examples_in_physics}

### Electromagnetism {#Electromagnetism}

*Image: Electric-dipole-field-lines-and-equipotential-lines.svg.* Field lines of an [electric dipole](wikipedia://en/30876071). Field lines go from positive charge (source) to the negative charge (sink).

In [electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrodynamics), the [current density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_density) behaves similar to hydrodynamics as it also follows a continuity equation due to the [charge conservation](wikipedia://en/3094621):

$$\frac{\partial \rho_e}{\partial t}+\nabla \cdot \mathbf j = \sigma,$$

where this time $\rho_e$ is the [charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_density), $\mathbf j$ is the [current density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_density) vector, and $\sigma$ is the current source-sink term. The current source and current sinks are where the current density emerges $\sigma>0$ or vanishes $\sigma<0$, respectively (for example, the source and sink can represent the two poles of an [electrical battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_battery) in a closed circuit).[^o8]

The concept is also used for the electromagnetic fields, where fluid flow is replaced by [field lines](wikipedia://en/5000404).[^o9] For an [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) $\mathbf E$, a source is a point where electric field lines emanate, such as a positive [charge](wikipedia://en/9804) ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf E>0$), while a sink is where field lines converge ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf E<0$), such as a negative charge.[^o10] This happens because electric fields follow [Gauss's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_law) given by

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf E=\rho_e/\epsilon_0,$$

where $\epsilon_0$ is the [vacuum permittivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_permittivity). In this sense, for a [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563) $\mathbf B$ there are no sources or sinks because there are no [magnetic monopoles](wikipedia://en/175470) as described by [Gauss's law for magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_law_for_magnetism) which states that

$\nabla \cdot \mathbf B=0$.[^o11]

Electric and magnetic fields also carry energy as described by [Poynting's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poynting's_theorem), given by

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} +\nabla\cdot\mathbf{S}= -\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$$

where $u$ is the [electromagnetic energy density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_density), $\mathbf S$ is the [Poynting vector](wikipedia://en/49718) and $- \mathbf J\cdot \mathbf E$ can be considered as an energy source-sink term.[^o12]

### Newtonian gravity {#Newtonian_gravity}

*Image: Earth-moon-field.svg.* Gravitational field lines between Earth and the Moon. For gravity there are only sinks.

Similar to electric and magnetic fields, one can discuss the case of a Newtonian [gravitational field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_field) $\mathbf g$ described by [Gauss's law for gravity](wikipedia://en/16704344),

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf g=-4\pi G \rho,$$

where $G$ is the [gravitational constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_constant). As gravity is only attractive ($\rho\geq0$), there are only gravitational sinks but no sources. Sinks are represented by [point masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_particle).[^o14]

### Thermodynamics and transport {#Thermodynamics_and_transport}

In thermodynamics, the source and sinks correspond to two types of [thermal reservoirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_reservoir), where energy is supplied or extracted, such as [heat flux](wikipedia://en/14581057) sources or [heat sinks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_sink). In [thermal conduction](wikipedia://en/72536) this is described by the [heat equation](wikipedia://en/166415).[^o16] The terms are also used in [non-equilibrium thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/472429) by introducing the idea of sources and sinks of [entropy flux](wikipedia://en/9891).[^o17]

### Chaos theory {#Chaos_theory}

In [chaos theory](wikipedia://en/6295) and [complex system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_system), the idea of sources and sinks is used to describes [repellors and attractors](wikipedia://en/191094), respectively.[^o19][^o20]

## In mathematics {#In_mathematics}

### Complex functions {#Complex_functions}

This terminology is also used in [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), as [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) can be described as vectors in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628). Sources and sinks are associated with [zeros and poles](wikipedia://en/81560) of [meromorphic functions](wikipedia://en/80857), representing inflows and outflows in a [harmonic function](wikipedia://en/55762). A complex function is defined as a source or a sink if it has a pole of order one.[^o23]

### Topology {#Topology}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), the terminology of sources and sinks is used when discussing a vector field over a compact [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219). In this context the [index of a vector field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_of_a_vector_field) is +1 if it is a source or a sink, if the value is -1 it is called a saddle point. This concept is useful to introduce the [Poincaré–Hopf theorem](wikipedia://en/1267288) and the [hairy ball theorem](wikipedia://en/485168).[^o25]

## Other uses {#Other_uses}

Other areas where this terminology is used include [source–sink dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source–sink_dynamics) in [ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecology) and [current source density analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_source_density_analysis) in neuroscience, as well as in the A/V industry where installers refer to signal destinations, such as video projectors or video monitors as 'sinks.'[^o27][^o28]
