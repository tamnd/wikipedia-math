# Eccentricity (mathematics)

*Image: Eccentricity.png.* A family of conic sections of varying eccentricity share a focus point $F$ and directrix line $L$, including an ellipse (red, $e = 1/2$), a parabola (green, $e = 1$), and a hyperbola (blue, $e = 2$). The conic of eccentricity $0$ in this figure is an [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) circle centered at the focus, and the conic of eccentricity $\infty$ is an infinitesimally separated pair of lines. A circle of finite radius has an infinitely distant directrix, while a pair of lines of finite separation have an infinitely distant focus.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Eccentricity"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **eccentricity** of a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673#Eccentricity) is a non-negative real number that uniquely characterizes its shape. One can think of the eccentricity as a measure of how much a conic section deviates from being circular. In particular:

:::

- The eccentricity of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) is 0.
- The eccentricity of a non-circular [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) is between 0 and 1.
- The eccentricity of a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) is 1.
- The eccentricity of a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) is greater than 1.
- The eccentricity of a pair of [lines](wikipedia://en/946975) is $\infty.$

Two conic sections with the same eccentricity are [similar](wikipedia://en/39377).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

*Image: Exzentr3d-s.svg.* Plane section of a cone

Any conic section can be defined as the [locus of points](wikipedia://en/542465) whose distances to a point (the focus) and a line (the directrix) are in a constant ratio. That ratio is called the *eccentricity*, commonly denoted as $e$.

The eccentricity can also be defined in terms of the intersection of a plane and a [double-napped cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_%28geometry%29) associated with the conic section. If the cone is oriented with its axis vertical, the eccentricity is[^o4]

$$e = \frac{\sin \beta}{\sin \alpha}, \ \ 0<\alpha<90^\circ, \ 0\le\beta\le90^\circ \ ,$$

where *β* is the angle between the plane and the horizontal and α is the angle between the cone's slant generator and the horizontal. For $\beta=0$ the plane section is a circle, for $\beta=\alpha$ a parabola. (The plane must not meet the vertex of the cone.)

The **linear eccentricity** of an ellipse or hyperbola, denoted $c$ (or sometimes $f$ or $e$), is the distance between its center and either of its two [foci](wikipedia://en/1032607). The eccentricity can be defined as the ratio of the linear eccentricity to the [semimajor axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semimajor_axis) $a$: that is, $e = \frac{c}{a}$ (lacking a center, the linear eccentricity for parabolas is not defined). A parabola can be treated as a [limiting case](wikipedia://en/40400729) of an ellipse or a hyperbola with one [focal point at infinity](wikipedia://en/1032607#Defining_conics_in_terms_of_two_foci).

:::

## Alternative names {#Alternative_names}

The eccentricity is sometimes called the *first eccentricity* to distinguish it from the *second eccentricity* and *third eccentricity* defined for ellipses (see below). The eccentricity is also sometimes called the *numerical eccentricity*.

In the case of ellipses and hyperbolas the linear eccentricity is sometimes called the *half-focal separation*.

::: {#o7 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

## Notation {#Notation}

Three notational conventions are in common use:

1. $e$ for the eccentricity and $c$ for the linear eccentricity.
1. $\varepsilon$ for the eccentricity and $e$ for the linear eccentricity.
1. e or $\epsilon<$ for the eccentricity and $f$ for the linear eccentricity (mnemonic for half-*f*ocal separation).

This article uses the first notation.

:::

## Values {#Values}

### Standard form {#Standard_form}

::: {#o10 .table cue=table}

| Conic section | Equation | Eccentricity ($e$) | Linear eccentricity ($c$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Circle](wikipedia://en/6220) | $x^2+y^2=r^2$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| [Ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) | $\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1$ or $\frac{y^2}{a^2}+\frac{x^2}{b^2}=1$ where $a>b$ | $\sqrt{1-\frac{b^2}{a^2}}$(e\<1) | $\sqrt{a^2-b^2}$ |
| [Parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) | $x^2=4ay$ | $1$ | undefined ($\infty$) |
| [Hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) | $\frac{x^2}{a^2}-\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1$ or $\frac{y^2}{a^2}-\frac{x^2}{b^2}=1$ | (e>1)$\sqrt{1+\frac{b^2}{a^2}}$ | $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ |

:::

Here, for the ellipse and the hyperbola, $a$ is the length of the semi-major axis and $b$ is the length of the semi-minor axis.

### General form {#General_form}

When the conic section is given in the general quadratic form

$$Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 +Dx + Ey + F = 0,$$

the following formula gives the eccentricity $e$ if the conic section is not a parabola (which has eccentricity equal to 1), not a [degenerate hyperbola or degenerate ellipse](wikipedia://en/392586), and not an imaginary ellipse:[^o16]

$$e=\sqrt{\frac{2\sqrt{(A-C)^2 + B^2}}{\eta (A+C) + \sqrt{(A-C)^2 + B^2}}}$$

where $\eta = 1$ if the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the 3×3 matrix

$$\begin{bmatrix}A & B/2 & D/2\\B/2 & C & E/2\\D/2&E/2&F\end{bmatrix}$$

is negative or $\eta = -1$ if that determinant is positive.

## Ellipses {#Ellipses}

The eccentricity of an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) is strictly less than 1. When circles (which have eccentricity 0) are counted as ellipses, the eccentricity of an ellipse is greater than or equal to 0; if circles are given a special category and are excluded from the category of ellipses, then the eccentricity of an ellipse is strictly greater than 0.

For any ellipse, let $a$ be the length of its [semi-major axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-major_axis) and $b$ be the length of its [semi-minor axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-minor_axis). In the coordinate system with origin at the ellipse's center and $x$-axis aligned with the major axis, points on the ellipse satisfy the equation

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1,$$

with foci at coordinates $(\pm c, 0)$ for $c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2}.$

We define a number of related additional concepts (only for ellipses):

::: {#o18 .table cue=table}

| Name | Symbol | in terms of $a$ and $b$ | in terms of $e$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| First eccentricity | $e$ | $\sqrt{1-\frac{b^2}{a^2}}$ | $e$ |
| Second eccentricity | $e'$ | $\sqrt{\frac{a^2}{b^2}-1}$ | $\frac{e}{\sqrt{1-e^2}}$ |
| Third eccentricity | $e''=\sqrt m$ | $\frac{\sqrt{a^2-b^2}}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}$ | $\frac{e}{\sqrt{2-e^2}}$ |
| [Angular eccentricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_eccentricity) | $\alpha$ | $\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$ | $\sin^{-1} e$ |

:::

*Image: Pythagorean\_theorem\_ellipse\_eccentricity.svg.* First eccentricity *e* in terms of semi-major *a* and semi-minor *b* axes: *e*² + (*b/a*)² = 1

### Other formulae for the eccentricity of an ellipse {#Other_formulae_for_the_eccentricity_of_an_ellipse}

The eccentricity of an ellipse is, most simply, the ratio of the linear eccentricity $c$ (distance between the center of the ellipse and each focus) to the length of the semimajor axis $a$.

$$e = \frac{c}{a}.$$

The eccentricity is also the ratio of the semimajor axis $a$ to the distance $d$ from the center to the directrix:

$$e = \frac{a}{d}.$$

The eccentricity can be expressed in terms of the [flattening](wikipedia://en/2043875) $f$ (defined as $f = 1 - b / a$ for semimajor axis $a$ and semiminor axis $b$):

$$e = \sqrt{1-(1-f)^2} = \sqrt{f(2-f)}.$$

(Flattening may be denoted by $g$ in some subject areas if $f$ is linear eccentricity.)

Define the maximum and minimum radii $r_\text{max}$ and $r_\text{min}$ as the maximum and minimum distances from either focus to the ellipse (that is, the distances from either focus to the two ends of the major axis). Then with semimajor axis $a$, the eccentricity is given by

$$e = \frac{r_\text{max}-r_\text{min}}{r_\text{max}+r_\text{min}} = \frac{r_\text{max}-r_\text{min}}{2a},$$

which is the distance between the foci divided by the length of the major axis.

## Hyperbolas {#Hyperbolas}

The eccentricity of a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) can be any real number greater than 1, with no upper bound. The eccentricity of a [rectangular hyperbola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_hyperbola) is $\sqrt{2}$.

For a hyperbola given by the equation $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ with eccentricity $e$, its [conjugate hyperbola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_hyperbola) is given by $\frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{x^2}{a^2} = 1$. If the eccentricity of the conjugate hyperbola is $e'$, the two are related by the equation $\frac{1}{e^2} + \frac{1}{(e')^2} = 1$. This can be shown from their definitions: $e^2 = 1 + \frac{b^2}{a^2} = \frac{a^2+b^2}{a^2}$ and $(e')^2 = 1 + \frac{a^2}{b^2} = \frac{b^2+a^2}{b^2}$. From this, it follows that $\frac{1}{e^2} = \frac{a^2}{a^2+b^2}$ and $\frac{1}{(e')^2} = \frac{b^2}{a^2+b^2}$, and the sum of these two expressions is 1. From this relationship, it can be seen that one of $e$ and $e'$ must be greater than $\sqrt{2}$ and the other smaller, unless $a=b$, in which case they are both equal to $\sqrt{2}$.

## Quadrics {#Quadrics}

*Image: Cubic surface.gif.* Ellipses, hyperbolas with all possible eccentricities from zero to infinity and a parabola on one cubic surface.

The eccentricity of a three-dimensional [quadric](wikipedia://en/145570) is the eccentricity of a designated [section](wikipedia://en/1337370) of it. For example, on a triaxial ellipsoid, the *meridional eccentricity* is that of the ellipse formed by a section containing both the longest and the shortest axes (one of which will be the polar axis), and the *equatorial eccentricity* is the eccentricity of the ellipse formed by a section through the centre, perpendicular to the polar axis (i.e. in the equatorial plane). But: conic sections may occur on surfaces of higher order, too (see image).

## Celestial mechanics {#Celestial_mechanics}

In [celestial mechanics](wikipedia://en/102182), for bound orbits in a spherical potential, the definition above is informally generalized. When the [apocenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apocenter) distance is close to the [pericenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pericenter) distance, the orbit is said to have low eccentricity; when they are very different, the orbit is said be eccentric or having eccentricity near unity. This definition coincides with the mathematical definition of eccentricity for ellipses, in Keplerian, i.e., $1/r$ potentials.

## Analogous classifications {#Analogous_classifications}

A number of classifications in mathematics use derived terminology from the classification of conic sections by eccentricity:

- [Classification of elements](wikipedia://en/11105238#Classification_of_elements) of [SL<sub>2</sub>(R)](wikipedia://en/11105238) as elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic – and similarly for [classification of elements](wikipedia://en/314493#Classification) of PSL<sub>2</sub>(R), the real [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493).
- Classification of discrete distributions by [variance-to-mean ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variance-to-mean_ratio); see [cumulants of some discrete probability distributions](wikipedia://en/359684#Cumulants_of_some_discrete_probability_distributions) for details.
- Classification of [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations) is by analogy with the conic sections classification; see [elliptic](wikipedia://en/662787), [parabolic](wikipedia://en/7466971) and [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/1575825) partial differential equations.[^o25]
