# Pointed space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pointed space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **pointed space** or **based space** is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) with a distinguished point, the **basepoint**. The distinguished point is simply one particular point, picked out from the space, and given a name, such as $x_0,$ that remains unchanged during subsequent discussion, and is kept track of during all operations. Common examples of naturally pointed spaces include spheres (with the north pole as basepoint), closed intervals \[0,1\] (with 0 as basepoint), and topological groups (with the identity element as basepoint).

:::

Maps of pointed spaces (**based maps**) are [continuous maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_%28topology%29) preserving basepoints, i.e., a map $f$ between a pointed space $X$ with basepoint $x_0$ and a pointed space $Y$ with basepoint $y_0$ is a based map if it is continuous with respect to the topologies of $X$ and $Y$ and if $f\left(x_0\right) = y_0.$ This is usually denoted

$$f : \left(X, x_0\right) \to \left(Y, y_0\right).$$

Pointed spaces are important in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), particularly in [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831), where many constructions, such as the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004), depend on a choice of basepoint.

The [pointed set](wikipedia://en/1242444) concept is less important; it is anyway the case of a pointed [discrete space](wikipedia://en/56061).

Pointed spaces are often taken as a special case of the [relative topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_topology), where the subset is a single point. Thus, much of [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831) is usually developed on pointed spaces, and then moved to relative topologies in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801).

## Category of pointed spaces {#Category_of_pointed_spaces}

The [class](wikipedia://en/20696) of all pointed spaces forms a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) **Top**<sub>$\bullet$</sub> with basepoint-preserving continuous maps as [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891). Another way to think about this category is as the [comma category](wikipedia://en/840950), ($\{ \bullet \} \downarrow$ **Top**) where $\{ \bullet \}$ is any one point space and **Top** is the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051). (This is also called a [coslice category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coslice_category) denoted $\{ \bullet \} /$**Top**.) Objects in this category are continuous maps $\{ \bullet \} \to X.$ Such maps can be thought of as picking out a basepoint in $X.$ Morphisms in ($\{ \bullet \} \downarrow$ **Top**) are morphisms in **Top** for which the following diagram [commutes](wikipedia://en/138484):

*Image: PointedSpace-01.svg.*

It is easy to see that commutativity of the diagram is equivalent to the condition that $f$ preserves basepoints.

As a pointed space, $\{ \bullet \}$ is a [zero object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_object) in **Top**<sub>$\{ \bullet \}$</sub>, while it is only a [terminal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_object) in **Top**.

There is a [forgetful functor](wikipedia://en/844783) **Top**<sub>$\{ \bullet \}$</sub> $\to$ **Top** which "forgets" which point is the basepoint. This functor has a [left adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_functor) which assigns to each topological space $X$ the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) of $X$ and a one-point space $\{ \bullet \}$ whose single element is taken to be the basepoint.

## Operations on pointed spaces {#Operations_on_pointed_spaces}

- A **subspace** of a pointed space $X$ is a [topological subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspace_%28topology%29) $A \subseteq X$ which shares its basepoint with $X$ so that the [inclusion map](wikipedia://en/492516) is basepoint preserving.
- One can form the **[quotient](wikipedia://en/237213)** of a pointed space $X$ under any [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259). The basepoint of the quotient is the image of the basepoint in $X$ under the quotient map.
- One can form the **[product](wikipedia://en/237868)** of two pointed spaces $\left(X, x_0\right),$ $\left(Y, y_0\right)$ as the [topological product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_%28topology%29) $X \times Y$ with $\left(x_0, y_0\right)$serving as the basepoint.
- The **[coproduct](wikipedia://en/310889)** in the category of pointed spaces is the [wedge sum](wikipedia://en/684143), which can be thought of as the 'one-point union' of spaces.
- The **[smash product](wikipedia://en/698010)** of two pointed spaces is essentially the [quotient](wikipedia://en/237213) of the direct product and the wedge sum. We would like to say that the smash product turns the category of pointed spaces into a [symmetric monoidal category](wikipedia://en/3865249) with the pointed [0-sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0-sphere) as the unit object, but this is false for general spaces: the associativity condition might fail. But it is true for some more restricted categories of spaces, such as [compactly generated](wikipedia://en/899382) [weak Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/19500167) ones.
- The **[reduced suspension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_suspension)** $\Sigma X$ of a pointed space $X$ is (up to a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660)) the smash product of $X$ and the pointed circle $S^1.$
- The reduced suspension is a functor from the category of pointed spaces to itself. This functor is [left adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_adjoint) to the functor $\Omega$ taking a pointed space $X$ to its [loop space](wikipedia://en/2174332) $\Omega X$.
