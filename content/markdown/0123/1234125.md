# Kleene fixed-point theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kleene fixed-point theorem"}

Kleene's fixed-point theorem in lattice theory

*Image: Kleene fixpoint svg.svg.* Computation of the least fixpoint of *f*(*x*) = $\tfrac{1}{10}$*x*<sup>2</sup>+[atan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctangent)(*x*)+1 using Kleene's theorem in the real [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) \[0,7\] with the usual order

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) areas of [order](wikipedia://en/361924) and [lattice theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_theory), the **Kleene fixed-point theorem**, named after American mathematician [Stephen Cole Kleene](wikipedia://en/28805), states the following:

:::

**Kleene Fixed-Point Theorem.** Suppose $(L, \sqsubseteq)$ is a [directed-complete partial order](wikipedia://en/572352) (dcpo) with a least element, and let $f: L \to L$ be a [Scott-continuous](wikipedia://en/743106) (and therefore [monotone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotone_function)) [function](wikipedia://en/185427). Then $f$ has a [least fixed point](wikipedia://en/1211913), which is the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) of the ascending Kleene chain of $f.$

The **ascending Kleene chain** of *f* is the [chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_%28order_theory%29)

$$\bot \sqsubseteq f(\bot) \sqsubseteq f(f(\bot)) \sqsubseteq \cdots \sqsubseteq f^n(\bot) \sqsubseteq \cdots$$

obtained by [iterating](wikipedia://en/1448702) *f* on the [least element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_element) ⊥ of *L*. Expressed in a formula, the theorem states that

$$\textrm{lfp}(f) = \sup \left(\left\{f^n(\bot) \mid n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\right)$$

where $\textrm{lfp}$ denotes the least fixed point.

Although [Tarski's fixed point theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarski's_fixed_point_theorem) does not consider how fixed points can be computed by iterating *f* from some seed (also, it pertains to [monotone functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotone_function) on [complete lattices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_lattices)), this result is often attributed to [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) who proves it for additive functions.[^o2] Moreover, the Kleene fixed-point theorem can be extended to [monotone functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotone_function) using transfinite iterations.[^o3]

::: {#o5 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

Source:[^o6]

We first have to show that the ascending Kleene chain of $f$ exists in $L$. To show that, we prove the following:

**Lemma.** If $L$ is a dcpo with a least element, and $f: L \to L$ is Scott-continuous, then $f^n(\bot) \sqsubseteq f^{n+1}(\bot), n \in \mathbb{N}_0$

**Proof.** We use induction:

- Assume n = 0. Then $f^0(\bot) = \bot \sqsubseteq f^1(\bot),$ since $\bot$ is the least element.
- Assume n > 0. Then we have to show that $f^n(\bot) \sqsubseteq f^{n+1}(\bot)$. By rearranging we get $f(f^{n-1}(\bot)) \sqsubseteq f(f^n(\bot))$. By inductive assumption, we know that $f^{n-1}(\bot) \sqsubseteq f^n(\bot)$ holds, and because f is monotone (property of Scott-continuous functions), the result holds as well.

As a corollary of the Lemma we have the following directed ω-chain:

$$\mathbb{M} = \{ \bot, f(\bot), f(f(\bot)), \ldots\}.$$

From the definition of a dcpo it follows that $\mathbb{M}$ has a supremum, call it $m.$ What remains now is to show that $m$ is the least fixed-point.

First, we show that $m$ is a fixed point, i.e. that $f(m) = m$. Because $f$ is [Scott-continuous](wikipedia://en/743106), $f(\sup(\mathbb{M})) = \sup(f(\mathbb{M}))$, that is $f(m) = \sup(f(\mathbb{M}))$. Also, since $\mathbb{M} = f(\mathbb{M})\cup\{\bot\}$ and because $\bot$ has no influence in determining the supremum we have: $\sup(f(\mathbb{M})) = \sup(\mathbb{M})$. It follows that $f(m) = m$, making $m$ a fixed-point of $f$.

The proof that $m$ is in fact the *least* fixed point can be done by showing that any element in $\mathbb{M}$ is smaller than any fixed-point of $f$ (because by property of [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum), if all elements of a set $D \subseteq L$ are smaller than an element of $L$ then also $\sup(D)$ is smaller than that same element of $L$). This is done by induction: Assume $k$ is some fixed-point of $f$. We now prove by induction over $i$ that $\forall i \in \mathbb{N}: f^i(\bot) \sqsubseteq k$. The base of the induction $(i = 0)$ obviously holds: $f^0(\bot) = \bot \sqsubseteq k,$ since $\bot$ is the least element of $L$. As the induction hypothesis, we may assume that $f^i(\bot) \sqsubseteq k$. We now do the induction step: From the induction hypothesis and the monotonicity of $f$ (again, implied by the Scott-continuity of $f$), we may conclude the following: $f^i(\bot) \sqsubseteq k ~\implies~ f^{i+1}(\bot) \sqsubseteq f(k).$ Now, by the assumption that $k$ is a fixed-point of $f,$ we know that $f(k) = k,$ and from that we get $f^{i+1}(\bot) \sqsubseteq k.$

:::
