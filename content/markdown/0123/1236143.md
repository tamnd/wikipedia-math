# Alternating knot

*Image: Knot 8sb19.svg.* One of three non-alternating knots with [crossing number](wikipedia://en/18298598) 8

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Alternating knot"}

In [knot theory](wikipedia://en/153008), a [knot](wikipedia://en/366808) or [link](wikipedia://en/1161784) diagram is **alternating** if the crossings alternate under, over, under, over, as one travels along each component of the link. A link is **alternating** if it has an alternating diagram.

:::

Many of the knots with [crossing number](wikipedia://en/18298598) less than 10 are alternating. This fact and useful properties of alternating knots, such as the [Tait conjectures](wikipedia://en/2749975), was what enabled early knot tabulators, such as Tait, to construct tables with relatively few mistakes or omissions. The simplest non-alternating [prime knots](wikipedia://en/315030) have 8 crossings (and there are three such: 8<sub>19</sub>, 8<sub>20</sub>, 8<sub>21</sub>).

It is conjectured that as the crossing number increases, the percentage of knots that are alternating goes to 0 exponentially quickly.

Alternating links end up having an important role in knot theory and [3-manifold](wikipedia://en/1018257) theory, due to their [complements](wikipedia://en/1350120) having useful and interesting geometric and topological properties. This led [Ralph Fox](wikipedia://en/1343918) to ask, "What is an alternating knot?" By this he was asking what non-diagrammatic properties of the knot complement would characterize alternating knots.[^o2]

In November 2015, Joshua Evan Greene published a preprint that established a characterization of alternating links in terms of definite spanning surfaces, i.e. a definition of alternating links (of which alternating knots are a special case) without using the concept of a [link diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Link_diagram).[^o3]

Various geometric and topological information is revealed in an alternating diagram. Primeness and [splittability](wikipedia://en/3687789) of a link is easily seen from the diagram. The crossing number of a [reduced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_diagram), alternating diagram is the crossing number of the knot. This last is one of the celebrated Tait conjectures.

An alternating [knot diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_diagram) is in one-to-one correspondence with a [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314). Each crossing is associated with an edge and half of the connected components of the complement of the diagram are associated with vertices in a checker board manner.

## Tait conjectures {#Tait_conjectures}

The Tait conjectures are:

1. Any reduced diagram of an alternating link has the fewest possible crossings.
1. Any two reduced diagrams of the same alternating knot have the same [writhe](wikipedia://en/968591).
1. Given any two reduced alternating diagrams D<sub>1</sub> and D<sub>2</sub> of an oriented, prime alternating link: D<sub>1</sub> may be transformed to D<sub>2</sub> by means of a sequence of certain simple moves called *[flypes](wikipedia://en/16891247)*. Also known as the Tait flyping conjecture.[^o6]

[Morwen Thistlethwaite](wikipedia://en/16705411), [Louis Kauffman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Kauffman) and [K. Murasugi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K._Murasugi) proved the first two Tait conjectures in 1987 and [Morwen Thistlethwaite](wikipedia://en/16705411) and [William Menasco](wikipedia://en/16854432) proved the Tait flyping conjecture in 1991.

:::

## Hyperbolic volume {#Hyperbolic_volume}

[Menasco](wikipedia://en/16854432), applying [Thurston](wikipedia://en/39190)'s [hyperbolization theorem](wikipedia://en/9155837) for [Haken manifolds](wikipedia://en/236567), showed that any prime, non-split alternating link is [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/1248299), i.e. the link complement has a [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), unless the link is a [torus link](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torus_link).

Thus hyperbolic volume is an invariant of many alternating links. [Marc Lackenby](wikipedia://en/49182345) has shown that the volume has upper and lower linear bounds as functions of the number of *twist regions* of a reduced, alternating diagram.
