# Non-Archimedean geometry

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Non-Archimedean geometry"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **non-Archimedean geometry**[^o2] is any of a number of forms of [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) in which the [axiom of Archimedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_of_Archimedes) is negated. An example of such a geometry is the [Dehn plane](wikipedia://en/4207234). Non-Archimedean geometries may, as the example indicates, have properties significantly different from [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417).

:::

There are two senses in which the term may be used, referring to geometries over [fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28algebra%29) which violate one of the two senses of the [Archimedean property](wikipedia://en/264158) (i.e. with respect to order or magnitude).

## Geometry over a non-Archimedean ordered field {#Geometry_over_a_non-Archimedean_ordered_field}

The first sense of the term is the geometry over a [non-Archimedean ordered field](wikipedia://en/31046646), or a subset thereof. The aforementioned Dehn plane takes the self-product of the finite portion of a certain non-Archimedean ordered field based on the field of [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210). In this geometry, there are significant differences from Euclidean geometry; in particular, there are infinitely many parallels to a straight line through a point—so the [parallel postulate](wikipedia://en/33731493) fails—but the sum of the angles of a triangle is still a straight angle.[^o4]

Intuitively, in such a space, the points on a line cannot be described by the real numbers or a subset thereof, and there exist segments of "infinite" or "[infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990)" length.

## Geometry over a non-Archimedean valued field {#Geometry_over_a_non-Archimedean_valued_field}

The second sense of the term is the [metric geometry](wikipedia://en/20018) over a non-Archimedean [valued field](wikipedia://en/5042667),[^o6] or [ultrametric space](wikipedia://en/333996). In such a space, even more contradictions to Euclidean geometry result. For example, all triangles are isosceles, and overlapping [balls](wikipedia://en/160556) nest. An example of such a space is the [p-adic numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_numbers).

Intuitively, in such a space, distances fail to "add up" or "accumulate".
