# F-test of equality of variances

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="F-test of equality of variances"}

In statistics, an ***F*-test of equality of variances** is a [test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_test) for the [null hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis) that two [normal](wikipedia://en/21462) populations have the same [variance](wikipedia://en/32344). Notionally, any [*F*-test](wikipedia://en/318976) can be regarded as a comparison of two variances, but the specific case being discussed in this article is that of two populations, where the [test statistic](wikipedia://en/1416975) used is the ratio of two [sample variances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_variance).[^o2] This particular situation is of importance in [mathematical statistics](wikipedia://en/888711) since it provides a basic exemplar case in which the [*F*-distribution](wikipedia://en/332116) can be derived.[^o3] For application in [applied statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_statistics), there is concern[^o4] that the test is so sensitive to the assumption of normality that it would be inadvisable to use it as a routine test for the equality of variances. In other words, this is a case where "approximate normality" (which in similar contexts would often be justified using the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406)), is not good enough to make the test procedure approximately valid to an acceptable degree.

:::

## The test {#The_test}

Let *X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*n*</sub> and *Y*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *Y*<sub>*m*</sub> be [independent and identically distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_and_identically_distributed) samples from two populations which each has a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462). The [expected values](wikipedia://en/9653) for the two populations can be different, and the hypothesis to be tested is that the variances are equal. Let

$$\overline{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\text{ and }\overline{Y} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m Y_i$$

be the [sample means](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_mean). Let

$$S_X^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n \left(X_i - \overline{X}\right)^2\text{ and }S_Y^2 = \frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^m \left(Y_i - \overline{Y}\right)^2$$

be the [sample variances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_variance). Then the test statistic

$$F = \frac{S_X^2}{S_Y^2}$$

has an [F-distribution](wikipedia://en/332116) with *n* − 1 and *m* − 1 degrees of freedom if the [null hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis) of equality of variances is true. Otherwise it follows an F-distribution scaled by the ratio of true variances. The null hypothesis is rejected if *F* is either too large or too small based on the desired alpha level (i.e., [statistical significance](wikipedia://en/160995)).

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

This F-test is known to be extremely sensitive to [non-normality](wikipedia://en/21462),[^o8][^o9] so [Levene's test](wikipedia://en/4833235), [Bartlett's test](wikipedia://en/2223782), or the [Brown–Forsythe test](wikipedia://en/15317159) are better tests for testing the equality of two variances. (However, all of these tests create experiment-wise [type I error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_I_error) inflations when conducted as a test of the assumption of [homoscedasticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homoscedasticity) prior to a test of effects.[^o10]) F-tests for the equality of variances can be used in practice, with care, particularly where a quick check is required, and subject to associated diagnostic checking: practical text-books[^o11] suggest both graphical and formal checks of the assumption.

[F-tests](wikipedia://en/318976) are used for other statistical [tests of hypotheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis_test), such as testing for differences in means in three or more groups, or in factorial layouts. These F-tests are generally not [robust](wikipedia://en/2885691) when there are violations of the assumption that each population follows the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462), particularly for small alpha levels and unbalanced layouts.[^o12] However, for large alpha levels (e.g., at least 0.05) and balanced layouts, the F-test is relatively robust, although (if the normality assumption does not hold) it suffers from a loss in comparative statistical power as compared with non-parametric counterparts.

:::

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

The immediate generalization of the problem outlined above is to situations where there are more than two groups or populations, and the hypothesis is that all of the variances are equal. This is the problem treated by [Hartley's test](wikipedia://en/13640881) and [Bartlett's test](wikipedia://en/2223782).
