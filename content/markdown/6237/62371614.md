# William Chapple (surveyor)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="William Chapple"}

**William Chapple** (1718–1781) was an English [surveyor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying) and [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902). His mathematical discoveries were mostly in [plane geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_geometry) and include:

:::

- the first proof of the existence of the [orthocentre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthocentre) of a triangle,
- a formula for the [distance between the incentre and circumcentre](wikipedia://en/3338987) of a triangle,
- the discovery of [Poncelet's porism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poncelet's_porism) on triangles with a common incircle and circumcircle.

He was also one of the earliest mathematicians to calculate the values of [annuities](wikipedia://en/47078873).

## Life {#Life}

Chapple was born in [Witheridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witheridge) on 25 January, the son of a poor farmer and parish clerk. He was a devoted bibliophile, and gained much of his knowledge of mathematics from Ward's *The Young Mathematician's Guide: Being a Plain and Easie Introduction to the Mathematicks, in Five Parts*. He became an assistant to the parish priest, and a regular contributor to *[The Ladies' Diary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ladies'_Diary)*, especially concerning mathematical problems. He also later contributed work on [West Country English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Country_English) to *[The Gentleman's Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gentleman's_Magazine)*.

His correspondence led him to become, in 1738, the clerk for a surveyor in [Exeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter). He married the surveyor's niece, supervised the construction of a new hospital in Exeter, and became secretary of the hospital. He also worked as the estate steward for [William Courtenay, 1st Viscount Courtenay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Courtenay,_1st_Viscount_Courtenay). In 1772 he began work on an update to [Tristram Risdon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tristram_Risdon)'s *Survey of the County of Devon*, and spent much of the rest of his life working on it; it was published in part throughout his life, and in complete form posthumously in 1785.

He died in early September 1781. A tablet in his memory could be found in the west end of the nave of the [Church of St Mary Major, Exeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_St_Mary_Major,_Exeter), prior to that church's demolition in 1971. Chapple Road in Witheridge is named after him.

## Contributions to mathematics {#Contributions_to_mathematics}

Andrea del Centina writes that:

"To illustrate the work of Chapple, whose arguments are often confused and whose logic is very poor, even for the standard of his time, is not easy especially when trying to keep as faithful as possible to his thought."

Nevertheless, Chapple made several significant discoveries in mathematics.

### Plane geometry {#Plane_geometry}

*Image: PonceletPorism.gif.* The triangular case of [Poncelet's closure theorem](wikipedia://en/2412120)

[Euler's theorem in geometry](wikipedia://en/3338987) gives a formula for the distance $d$ between the incentre and circumcentre of a circle, as a function of the inradius $r$ and circumradius $R$:

$$d=\sqrt{R(R-2r)}.$$

An immediate consequence is the related [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489) $R\ge 2r$. Although these results are named for [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902), who published them in 1765, they were found earlier by Chapple, in a 1746 essay in *The Gentleman's Magazine*. In the same work he stated that, when two circles are the incircle and circumcircle of a triangle, then there is an infinite family of triangles for which they are the incircle and circumcircle. This is the triangular case of [Poncelet's closure theorem](wikipedia://en/2412120), which applies more generally to polygons of any number of sides and to [conics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic) other than circles. It is the first known mathematical publication on pairs of inscribed and circumscribed circles of polygons, and significantly predates Poncelet's own 1822 work in this area.

*Image: Triangle.Orthocenter.svg.* Three altitudes of a triangle meet at the orthocentre

In 1749, Chapple published the first known proof of the existence of the [orthocentre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthocentre) of a triangle, the point where the three perpendiculars from the vertices to the sides meet. The orthocentre itself was known previously, but Chapple writes that its existence was "often taken for granted, but no where demonstrated".

### Finance {#Finance}

Chapple learned of the problem of valuation of [annuities](wikipedia://en/47078873) through his correspondence with [John Rowe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Rowe_%28minister%29) and [Thomas Simpson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Simpson), and carried out this valuation for Courtenay. In this, he became one of the first mathematicians to work on this problem, along with Simpson, [Abraham de Moivre](wikipedia://en/57327), [James Dodson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Dodson_%28mathematician%29), and [William Jones](wikipedia://en/239126).
