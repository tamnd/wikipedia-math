# Fundamental unit (number theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fundamental unit"}

In [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705), a **fundamental unit** is a generator (modulo the [roots of unity](wikipedia://en/171950)) for the [unit group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_group) of the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/98981) of a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field), when that group has [rank](wikipedia://en/244541) 1 (i.e. when the unit group modulo its [torsion subgroup](wikipedia://en/144052) is [infinite cyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_cyclic)). [Dirichlet's unit theorem](wikipedia://en/460227) shows that the unit group has rank 1 exactly when the number field is a [real quadratic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_quadratic_field), a [complex cubic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_cubic_field), or a [totally imaginary](wikipedia://en/34962418) [quartic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartic_field). When the unit group has rank ≥ 1, a basis of it modulo its torsion is called a **fundamental system of units**.[^o2] Some authors use the term **fundamental unit** to mean any element of a fundamental system of units, not restricting to the case of rank 1 (e.g. (Neukirch 1999, p. 42)).

:::

## Real quadratic fields {#Real_quadratic_fields}

For the real quadratic field $K=\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ (with *d* square-free), the fundamental unit ε is commonly normalized so that $\varepsilon > 1$ (as a real number). Then it is uniquely characterized as the minimal unit among those that are greater than 1. If Δ denotes the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/3022963) of *K*, then the fundamental unit is

$$\varepsilon=\frac{a+b\sqrt{\Delta}}{2}$$

where (*a*, *b*) is the smallest solution to[^o5]

$$x^2-\Delta y^2=\pm4$$

in positive integers. This equation is basically [Pell's equation](wikipedia://en/24877) or the negative Pell equation and its solutions can be obtained similarly using the [continued fraction expansion](wikipedia://en/46802) of $\sqrt{\Delta}$.

Whether or not *x*<sup>2</sup> − Δ*y*<sup>2</sup> = −4 has a solution determines whether or not the [class group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_group) of *K* is the same as its [narrow class group](wikipedia://en/2567750), or equivalently, whether or not there is a unit of norm −1 in *K*. This equation is known to have a solution if, and only if, the period of the continued fraction expansion of $\sqrt{\Delta}$ is odd. A simpler relation can be obtained using congruences: if Δ is divisible by a prime that is congruent to 3 modulo 4, then *K* does not have a unit of norm −1. However, the converse does not hold as shown by the example *d* = 34.[^o6] In the early 1990s, Peter Stevenhagen proposed a probabilistic model that led him to a conjecture on how often the converse fails. Specifically, if *D*(*X*) is the number of real quadratic fields whose discriminant Δ \< *X* is not divisible by a prime congruent to 3 modulo 4 and *D*<sup>−</sup>(*X*) is those who have a unit of norm −1, then[^o7]

$$\lim_{X\rightarrow\infty}\frac{D^-(X)}{D(X)}=1-\prod_{j\geq1\text{ odd}}\left(1-2^{-j}\right).$$

In other words, the converse fails about 42% of the time. As of March 2012, a recent result towards this conjecture was provided by [Étienne Fouvry](wikipedia://en/46799655) and Jürgen Klüners[^o8] who show that the converse fails between 33% and 59% of the time. In 2022, Peter Koymans and Carlo Pagano[^o9] claimed a complete proof of Stevenhagen's conjecture.

## Cubic fields {#Cubic_fields}

If *K* is a complex cubic field then it has a unique real embedding and the fundamental unit ε can be picked uniquely such that |ε| > 1 in this embedding. If the discriminant Δ of *K* satisfies |Δ| ≥ 33, then[^o11]

$$\epsilon^3>\frac{|\Delta|-27}{4}.$$

For example, the fundamental unit of $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ is $\epsilon = 1+\sqrt[3]{2}+\sqrt[3]{2^2},$ and $\epsilon^3\approx 56.9$ whereas the discriminant of this field is −108 thus

$$\frac{|\Delta|-27}{4}=20.25$$

so $\epsilon^3 \approx 56.9 > 20.25$.
