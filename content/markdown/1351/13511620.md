# Bicircular matroid

*Image: Bicircular matroid.svg.* The **bicircular matroid** of a graph $G$. Each independent set forms a [pseudoforest](wikipedia://en/13511542), where each [connected component](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28graph_theory%29) contains at most one [cycle](wikipedia://en/168609). The only dependent set in the given matroid is the set $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ describing the whole graph $G$, which forms a [theta graph](wikipedia://en/2472919).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bicircular matroid"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) subject of [matroid](wikipedia://en/244321) theory, the **bicircular matroid** of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) *G* is the matroid *B*(*G*) whose points are the edges of *G* and whose independent sets are the edge sets of [pseudoforests](wikipedia://en/13511542) of *G*, that is, the edge sets in which each [connected component](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28graph_theory%29) contains at most one [cycle](wikipedia://en/168609).

:::

The bicircular matroid was introduced by (Simões-Pereira 1972) and explored further by (Matthews 1977) and others. It is a special case of the [frame matroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_matroid) of a [biased graph](wikipedia://en/2472880).

## Circuits {#Circuits}

The circuits, or minimal dependent sets, of this matroid are the **bicircular graphs** (or **bicycles**, but that term has other meanings in graph theory); these are connected graphs whose [circuit rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circuit_rank) is exactly two.

There are three distinct types of bicircular graph:

- The [theta graph](wikipedia://en/325802#Walks) consists of three paths joining the same two vertices but not intersecting each other.
- The figure eight graph (or tight handcuff) consists of two cycles having just one common vertex.
- The loose handcuff (or barbell) consists of two disjoint cycles and a minimal connecting path.

All these definitions apply to [multigraphs](wikipedia://en/1774970), i.e., they permit multiple edges (edges sharing the same endpoints) and loops (edges whose two endpoints are the same vertex).

## Flats {#Flats}

The [closed sets](wikipedia://en/244321#Closed_sets_%28flats%29) (flats) of the bicircular matroid of a graph $G$ can be described as the [forests](wikipedia://en/12401) $F$ of $G$ such that in the [induced subgraph](wikipedia://en/1655188) of $V(G) - V(F)$, every connected component has a cycle. Since the flats of a matroid form a [geometric lattice](wikipedia://en/17243191) when [partially ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_ordering) by set inclusion, these forests of $G$ also form a geometric lattice. In the partial ordering for this lattice, that $F_{1} \le F_{2}$ if

- each component tree of $F_{1}$ is either contained in or vertex-disjoint from every tree of $F_{2}$, and
- each vertex of $F_{2}$ is a vertex of $F_{1}$.

For the most interesting example, let $G\,^{o}$ be $G$ with a loop added to every vertex. Then the flats of $B(G\,^{o})$ are all the forests of $G$, spanning or nonspanning. Thus, all forests of a graph $G$ form a geometric lattice, the **forest lattice** of *G* (Zaslavsky 1982).

## As transversal matroids {#As_transversal_matroids}

Bicircular matroids can be characterized as the [transversal matroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transversal_matroid) that arise from a [family of sets](wikipedia://en/723043) in which each set element belongs to at most two sets. That is, the independent sets of the matroid are the subsets of elements that can be used to form a system of distinct representatives for some or all of the sets. In this description, the elements correspond to the edges of a graph, and there is one set per vertex, the set of edges having that vertex as an endpoint.

## Minors {#Minors}

Unlike transversal matroids in general, bicircular matroids form a [minor-closed class](wikipedia://en/36749660); that is, any submatroid or contraction of a bicircular matroid is also a bicircular matroid, as can be seen from their description in terms of [biased graphs](wikipedia://en/2472880) (Zaslavsky 1991). Here is a description of deletion and contraction of an edge in terms of the underlying graph: To delete an edge from the matroid, remove it from the graph. The rule for contraction depends on what kind of edge it is. To contract a link (a non-loop) in the matroid, contract it in the graph in the usual way. To contract a loop *e* at vertex *v*, delete *e* and *v* but not the other edges incident with v; rather, each edge incident with *v* and another vertex *w* becomes a loop at *w*. Any other graph loops at *v* become matroid loops—to describe this correctly in terms of the graph one needs half-edges and loose edges; see [biased graph minors](wikipedia://en/2472880#Minors).

## Characteristic polynomial {#Characteristic_polynomial}

The [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/244321#Characteristic_polynomial) of the bicircular matroid *B*(*G* <sup>o</sup>) expresses in a simple way the numbers of spanning [forests](wikipedia://en/48560) (forests that contain all vertices of *G*) of each size in *G*. The formula is

$$p_{B(G)}(\lambda) := \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k f_k (\lambda-1)^{n-k},$$

where *f*<sub>*k*</sub> equals the number of *k*-edge spanning forests in *G*. See (Zaslavsky 1982).

## Vector representation {#Vector_representation}

Bicircular matroids, like all other transversal matroids, can be [represented](wikipedia://en/37165960) by vectors over any infinite [field](wikipedia://en/10603). However, unlike [graphic matroids](wikipedia://en/745714), they are not [regular](wikipedia://en/13415343): they cannot be represented by vectors over an arbitrary [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615). The question of the fields over which a bicircular matroid has a vector representation leads to the largely unsolved problem of finding the fields over which a graph has multiplicative [gains](wikipedia://en/5297369). See (Zaslavsky 2007).
