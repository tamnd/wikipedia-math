# Robert Hues

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Robert Hues"}

**Robert Hues** (1553 – 24 May 1632) was an English [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and [geographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographer). He attended [St. Mary Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mary_Hall,_Oxford) at [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University), and graduated in 1578. Hues became interested in [geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography) and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and studied [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation) at a school set up by [Walter Raleigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Raleigh). During a trip to [Newfoundland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newfoundland_%28island%29), he made observations which caused him to doubt the accepted published values for [variations of the compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_declination). Between 1586 and 1588, Hues travelled with [Thomas Cavendish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Cavendish) on a [circumnavigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumnavigation) of the globe, performing [astronomical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical) observations and taking the latitudes of places they visited. Beginning in August 1591, Hues and Cavendish again set out on another [circumnavigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumnavigation) of the globe. During the voyage, Hues made astronomical observations in the South Atlantic, and continued his observations of the variation of the compass at various [latitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latitudes) and at the [Equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator). Cavendish died on the journey in 1592, and Hues returned to England the following year.

:::

In 1594, Hues published his discoveries in the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) work *Tractatus de globis et eorum usu* (*Treatise on Globes and Their Use*) which was written to explain the use of the terrestrial and celestial globes that had been made and published by [Emery Molyneux](wikipedia://en/15442947) in late 1592 or early 1593, and to encourage English sailors to use practical astronomical navigation. Hues's work subsequently went into at least 12 other printings in Dutch, English, French and Latin.

Hues continued to have dealings with Raleigh in the 1590s, and later became a servant of [Thomas Grey, 15th Baron Grey de Wilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Grey,_15th_Baron_Grey_de_Wilton). While Grey was imprisoned in the [Tower of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_of_London) for participating in the [Bye Plot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bye_Plot), Hues stayed with him. Following Grey's death in 1614, Hues attended upon [Henry Percy, the 9th Earl of Northumberland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Percy,_9th_Earl_of_Northumberland), when he was confined in the Tower; one source states that Hues, [Thomas Harriot](wikipedia://en/780445) and [Walter Warner](wikipedia://en/21433743) were Northumberland's constant companions and known as his "Three [Magi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magi)", although this is disputed. Hues tutored Northumberland's son [Algernon Percy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algernon_Percy,_10th_Earl_of_Northumberland) (who was to become the 10th [Earl of Northumberland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_of_Northumberland)) at Oxford, and subsequently (in 1622–1623) Algernon's younger brother Henry. In later years, Hues lived in [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford) where he was a fellow of the university, and discussed mathematics and related subjects with like-minded friends. He died on 24 May 1632 in the city and was buried in [Christ Church Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_Church_Cathedral,_Oxford).

## Early years and education {#Early_years_and_education}

*Image: Henry Percy-9th Earl of Northumberland.jpg.* [Van Dyck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_van_Dyck)'s portrait of [Henry Percy, the 9th Earl of Northumberland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Percy,_9th_Earl_of_Northumberland), with whom Hues was associated

Robert Hues was born in 1553 at [Little Hereford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Hereford) in Herefordshire, England. In 1571, at the age of 18 years, he entered [Brasenose College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brasenose_College,_Oxford).[^o4][^o5] English [antiquarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiquarian) [Anthony à Wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_à_Wood) wrote that when Hues arrived at Oxford he was "only a poor scholar or servitor ... he continued for some time a very sober and serious servant ... but being sensible of the loss of time which he sustained there by constant attendance, he transferred himself to [St Mary's Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mary_Hall,_Oxford)".[^o6] Hues graduated with a Bachelor of Arts (B.A.) degree on 12 July 1578,[^o7] having shown marked skill in [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language). He later gave advice to the dramatist and poet [George Chapman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Chapman) for his 1616 English translation of [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer),[^o8] and Chapman referred to him as his "learned and valuable friend".[^o9] According to the *[Oxford Dictionary of National Biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography)*, there is unsubstantiated evidence that after completing his degree Hues was held in the [Tower of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_of_London), though no reason is given for this, then went abroad after his release.[^o4] It is possible he travelled to [Continental Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Europe).[^o10]

Hues was a friend of the [geographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographer) [Richard Hakluyt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Hakluyt), who was then regent master of [Christ Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_Church,_Oxford). In the 1580s, Hakluyt introduced him to [Walter Raleigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Raleigh) and [explorers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration) and [navigators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigator) whom Raleigh knew. In addition, it is likely that Hues came to know [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer) and mathematician [Thomas Harriot](wikipedia://en/780445) and Walter Warner at [Thomas Allen](wikipedia://en/3612804)'s lectures in mathematics. The four men were later associated with [Henry Percy, the 9th Earl of Northumberland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Percy,_9th_Earl_of_Northumberland),[^o4][^o11] who was known as the "Wizard Earl" for his interest in scientific and [alchemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy) experiments and his library.[^o12]

## Career {#Career}

*Image: Thomas Cavendish.jpg.* An engraving of [Thomas Cavendish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Cavendish) by Willem and Magdalena van de Passe from Holland's *[Herōologia Anglica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herōologia_Anglica)* (*Praise of English Heroes*, 1620),[^o14] titled "Thomas Candish, [Armiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armiger). *Animum fortuna sequatur* \[The soul follows chance\]". Hues [circumnavigated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumnavigation) the globe with Cavendish between 1586 and 1588.

Hues became interested in [geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography) and mathematics – an undated source indicates that he disputed accepted values of [variations of the compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_declination) after making observations off the [Newfoundland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newfoundland_%28island%29) coast. He either went there on a fishing trip, or may have joined a 1585 voyage to [Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia) arranged by Raleigh and led by [Richard Grenville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Grenville), which passed Newfoundland on the return journey to England. Hues perhaps become acquainted with the sailor [Thomas Cavendish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Cavendish) at this time, as both of them were taught by Harriot at Raleigh's school of navigation. An anonymous 17th-century manuscript states that Hues [circumnavigated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumnavigation) the world with Cavendish [between 1586 and 1588](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Cavendish's_circumnavigation) "purposely for taking the true [Latitude](wikipedia://en/17616) of places";[^o15] he may have been the "NH" who wrote a brief account of the voyage that was published by Hakluyt in his 1589 work *The Principall Navigations, Voiages, and Discoveries of the English Nation*.[^o16] In the year that book appeared, Hues was with [Edward Wright](wikipedia://en/17214829) on the [Earl of Cumberland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Clifford,_3rd_Earl_of_Cumberland)'s raiding [expedition to the Azores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azores_Voyage_of_1589) to capture Spanish [galleons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galleon).[^o4]

Beginning in August 1591, Hues joined Cavendish on another attempt to circumnavigate the globe. Sailing on the *Leicester*, they were accompanied by the explorer [John Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Davis_%28English_explorer%29) on the *Desire*. Cavendish and Davis agreed that they would part company once they had cleared the [Strait of Magellan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strait_of_Magellan) between [Chile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chile) and [Isla Grande de Tierra del Fuego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isla_Grande_de_Tierra_del_Fuego), as Davis intended to sail to America to search for the [Northwest Passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Passage). The expedition was ultimately unsuccessful, although Davis did discover the [Falkland Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falkland_Islands).[^o17] In the meantime, delayed in small harbours in the Strait with crew members dying from the cold, illness and starvation, Cavendish turned back eastwards to return to England. He was plagued by [mutinous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutiny) crewmen, and also by natives and Portuguese who attacked his sailors seeking food and water on shore. Increasingly depressed, Cavendish died in 1592 somewhere in the Atlantic Ocean, possibly a suicide.[^o10][^o18]

During the voyage, Hues made [astronomical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) observations of the [Southern Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crux) and other stars of the [Southern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Hemisphere) while in the South Atlantic, and also observed the variation of the compass there and at the [Equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator). He returned to England after Cavendish died,[^o19] and published his discoveries in the work *Tractatus de globis et eorum usu* (*Treatise on Globes and Their Use*, 1594),[^o20] which he dedicated to Raleigh.[^o4] The book was written to explain the use of the terrestrial and celestial globes that had been made and published by [Emery Molyneux](wikipedia://en/15442947) in late 1592 or early 1593.[^o21] Apparently, the book was also intended to encourage English sailors to use practical astronomical navigation,[^o4] although [Lesley Cormack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesley_Cormack) has observed that the fact it was written in [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) suggests that it was aimed at scholarly readers on the Continent.[^o22] In 1595, William Sanderson, a London merchant who had largely financed the globes' construction, presented a small globe together with Hues's "Latin booke that teacheth the use of my great globes"[^o23] to [Robert Cecil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Cecil,_1st_Earl_of_Salisbury), a [statesman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politician) who was [spymaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spymaster) and minister to [Elizabeth I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_I_of_England) and [James I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_I_of_England). Hues's work subsequently went into at least 12 other printings in Dutch (1597, 1613 and 1622), English (1638 and 1659), French (1618) and Latin (1611, 1613, 1617, 1627, 1659 and 1663).[^o24] In his book *An Accidence or The Path-way to Experience: Necessary for all Young Sea-men* (1626),[^o25] [John Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Smith_of_Jamestown), who founded the first permanent English settlement in North America at [Jamestown, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamestown,_Virginia), listed Hues's book among the works that a young seaman should study.[^o26]

*Image: RobertHues-TractaetGlobe-AertscheGlobe-1623.jpg.* A drawing of [Molyneux](wikipedia://en/15442947)'s terrestrial globe from *Tractaet ofte Handelinge van het gebruyck der Hemelscher ende Aertscher Globe*, the 1623 Dutch version of Hues's *Tractatus de globis*

*Tractatus de globis* begins with a letter by Hues dedicated to Raleigh that recalled geographical discoveries made by Englishmen during Elizabeth I's reign. However, he felt that his countrymen would have surpassed the [Spaniards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spain) and [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portugal) if they had a complete knowledge of [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) and [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), which were essential to successful navigation.[^o27] In the preface of the book, Hues rehearsed arguments that proved the earth is a sphere, and refuted opposing theories.[^o28] The treatise was divided into five parts. The first part described elements common to Molyneux's terrestrial and celestial globes, including the circles and lines inscribed on them, zones and climates, and the use of each globe's wooden horizon circle and brass meridian.[^o29] The second part described [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet), [fixed stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_stars) and [constellations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constellation); while the third part described the lands and seas shown on the terrestrial globe, and discussed the length of the [circumference of the earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) and of a [degree](wikipedia://en/1195294) of a [great circle](wikipedia://en/48082).[^o30] Part 4, which Hues considered the most important part of the work, explained how the globes enabled seamen to determine the sun's position, latitude, course and distance, amplitudes and [azimuths](wikipedia://en/47487), and time and [declination](wikipedia://en/8612).[^o31] The final part of the work contained a treatise inspired by Harriot on [rhumb lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhumb_line).[^o30][^o32] In the work, Hues also published for the first time the six fundamental navigational propositions involved in solving what was later termed the "nautical triangle" used for [plane sailing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_sailing). Difference of latitude and departure (or [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude)) are two sides of the triangle forming a [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956), the distance travelled is the [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366), and the angle between difference of latitude and distance is the course. If any two elements are known, the other two can be determined by plotting or calculation using tables of [sines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_%28trigonometric_function%29), [tangents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_%28trigonometric_function%29) and [secants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secant_function).[^o33]

In the 1590s, Hues continued to have dealings with Raleigh – he was one of the executors of Raleigh's will[^o19] – and he may have been the "Hewes" who dined with Northumberland regularly in 1591. He later became a servant of [Thomas Grey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Grey,_15th_Baron_Grey_de_Wilton), the 15th and last [Baron Grey de Wilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron_Grey_de_Wilton) (1575–1614). For participating in the [Bye Plot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bye_Plot), a conspiracy by Roman Catholic priest [William Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Watson_%28priest%29) to kidnap James I and force him to repeal anti-Catholic legislation, Grey was [attainted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attainder) and forfeited his title in 1603. The following year, he was imprisoned in the Tower of London. Grey was given consent for Hues to stay in the Tower with him.[^o4] Between 1605 and 1621, Northumberland was also confined in the Tower; he was suspected of involvement in the [Gunpowder Plot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunpowder_Plot) of 1605 because his relative [Thomas Percy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Percy_%28plotter%29) was among the conspirators.

In 1616, following Grey's death, Hues began to be "attendant upon th'aforesaid Earle of Northumberland for matters of learning",[^o34] and was paid a yearly sum of £40 to support his research until Northumberland's death in 1632.[^o4][^o19] Wood stated that Harriot, Hues and Warner were Northumberland's "constant companions, and were usually called the Earl of Northumberland's Three Magi. They had a table at the Earl's charge, and the Earl himself did constantly converse with them, and with Sir Walter Raleigh, then in the Tower".[^o35] Together with the scientist Nathanael Toporley and the mathematician Thomas Allen, the men kept abreast of developments in astronomy, mathematics, [physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology) and the [physical sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outline_of_physical_science), and made important contributions in these areas.[^o36] According to the letter writer [John Chamberlain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Chamberlain_%28letter_writer%29), Northumberland refused a pardon offered to him in 1617, preferring to remain with Harriot, Hues and Warner.[^o37] However, the fact that these companions of Northumberland were his "Three Magi" studying with him in the Tower of London has been regarded as a romanticisation by the antiquarian [John Aubrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Aubrey) and disputed for lack of evidence.[^o4][^o38] Hues was tutor to Northumberland's sons: first [Algernon Percy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algernon_Percy,_10th_Earl_of_Northumberland), who subsequently became the 10th [Earl of Northumberland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_of_Northumberland), at Oxford where he [matriculated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matriculation) at [Christ Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_Church,_Oxford) in 1617; and later Algernon's younger brother Henry in 1622–1623. Hues lived at Christ Church at this time, but may have occasionally attended upon Northumberland at [Petworth House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petworth_House) in [Petworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petworth), West Sussex, and at [Syon House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syon_House) in London after the latter's release from the Tower in 1622.[^o36] Hues sometimes met Walter Warner in London, and they are known to have discussed the reflection of bodies.[^o4]

## Later life {#Later_life}

*Image: Cathedral oxford.jpg.* [Christ Church Cathedral, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_Church_Cathedral,_Oxford), the burial place of Robert Hues

In later years, Hues lived in [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford) where he discussed mathematics and allied subjects with like-minded friends.[^o4][^o40] Cormack states he was a fellow at the university.[^o22] Under the terms of the will of Thomas Harriot, who died on 2 July 1621, Hues and Warner were given the responsibility of helping Harriot's executor [Nathaniel Torporley](wikipedia://en/23591248) to prepare Harriot's mathematical papers for publication. Hues was also required to help price Harriot's books and other possessions for sale to the [Bodleian Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodleian_Library).[^o4]

Hues, who did not marry, died on 24 May 1632 in Stone House, [St. Aldate's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Aldate's,_Oxford) (opposite the Blue Boar in central Oxford).[^o41] This was the house of John Smith, [M.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts_%28Oxford,_Cambridge_and_Dublin%29), the son of a cook at Christ Church named J. Smith.[^o19] In his will, Hues made many small [bequests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bequest) to his friends, including a sum of £20 to his "kinswoman" Mary Holly (of whom nothing is known), and 20 [nobles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_%28English_coin%29) to each of her three sisters. He was buried in [Christ Church Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_Church_Cathedral,_Oxford), and a [monumental brass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monumental_brass) to him was placed in Christ Church with the following inscription:[^o4]

*Depositum viri literatissimi, morum ac religionis integerrimi, Roberti Husia, ob eruditionem omnigenem*\[sic:*omnigenam*?\]*, Theologicam tum Historicam, tum Scholasticam, Philologicam, Philosophiam, præsertim vero Mathematicam (cujus insigne monumentum in typis reliquit) Primum Thomæ Candishio conjunctissimi, cujus in consortio, explorabundis*\[sic:*explorabundus*?\]*velis ambivit orbem: deinde Domino Baroni Gray; cui solator accessit in arca Londinensi. Quo defuncto, ad studia henrici Comitis Northumbriensis ibidem vocatus est, cujus filio instruendo cum aliquot annorum operam in hac Ecclesia dedisset et Academiae confinium locum valetudinariae senectuti commodum censuisset; in ædibus Johannis Smith, corpore exhaustus, sed animo vividus, expiravit die Maii 24, anno reparatae salutis 1632, aetatis suæ 79.*[^o42]

\[Here lies a highly lettered man, of the highest moral and religious integrity, Robert Hues, on account of his erudition in all subjects, both Theology and History, and Rhetoric, Philology, and Philosophy, but especially Mathematics (of which a notable volume \[*i.e.*, his book\] remains in print). He was most closely associated with Thomas Cavendish, in whose company he explored the world by sail; then with Lord Baron Gray, for whom he came as consoler in the Tower of London. When Gray died, he was summoned to study in the same place with Henry Earl of Northumberland, to teach his son, and when he had worked for some years in this Church \[*i.e.*, Christ Church Cathedral\], and had decided that the place next to the School \[*i.e.*, Christ Church, Oxford\] was suitable for his health in his old age, he breathed his last at the house of John Smith, his body exhausted, but with a lively spirit, on 24 May, in the year of our salvation 1632, at the age of 79.\]
