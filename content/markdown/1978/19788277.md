# Johann Euler

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Johann Euler"}

**Johann Albrecht Euler** (27 November 1734[^o9] – 17 September 1800) was a Swiss-Russian [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer) and [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who made contributions to [electrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics). The eldest son of the renowned mathematician [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902), he served as professor of physics at the [Imperial Academy of Sciences in Saint Petersburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences) and later as secretary of conferences overseeing the Academy's correspondence. His work *Disquisitio de Causa Physica Electricitatis* represented one of the earliest attempts to mathematize electrical theory through a mechanical framework based on compressible, elastic [aether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aether_theories).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Also known as *Johann Albert Euler* or *John-Albert Euler*, Johann Albrecht Euler was the first child born to the great Swiss mathematician [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902), who had emigrated (for the first time) to Saint-Petersburg on 17 May 1727. His mother was Katharina Gsell (1707–1773) whose maternal grandmother was the famous scientific illustrator [Maria Sibylla Merian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Sibylla_Merian) (1647–1717). Katharina married Leonhard Euler on 7 January 1734, and Johann Albert would be the eldest of their 13 children (only 5 of whom survived childhood).[^o9] Johann's godfather was [Christian Goldbach](wikipedia://en/6290).[^o9]

He spent his youth in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin) from 1741 to 1766, after which he returned to Russia and was appointed Professor of Physics at the [Imperial Academy of Sciences in Saint Petersburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences) in 1766. From 1769, he served as secretary of conferences, overseeing the Academy’s correspondence.[^o10]

## Contributions to electrostatics {#Contributions_to_electrostatics}

In 1754, Euler composed *Disquisitio de Causa Physica Electricitatis* (published 1757), in which he advanced a mechanical theory of [electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity) based on a compressible, elastic [aether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aether_theories). Drawing on methods from [hydrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodynamics) and [differential calculus](wikipedia://en/50416), he formulated equations linking the density and velocity of ether flowing through the pores of charged bodies, thereby providing a quantitative account of [attraction and repulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism) phenomena. Euler interpreted electrification as the expulsion of ether from a body's pores, with positive and negative states determined by the relative elasticity of ether inside the body compared to the surrounding medium. His approach represents one of the earliest attempts to mathematise electrical theory prior to the development of the [action at a distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_at_a_distance) framework later formalised by [Charles-Augustin de Coulomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles-Augustin_de_Coulomb).[^o10]

## Legacy {#Legacy}

Although Euler's mechanistic and mathematical treatment of electricity was innovative, it attracted little contemporary attention—partly because it did not address the emerging phenomena of the [Leyden jar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leyden_jar) and because he was based outside the principal research centres in London and Paris. Nevertheless, his work stands as a pioneering example of applying rigorous mathematical analysis to [electrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics) and influenced subsequent debates on the nature of physical interactions in the late eighteenth century.[^o10]
