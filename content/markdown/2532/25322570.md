# Higher-dimensional gamma matrices

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Higher-dimensional gamma matrices"}

In [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416), **higher-dimensional gamma matrices** generalize to arbitrary dimension the four-dimensional [Gamma matrices](wikipedia://en/2907966) of [Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742), which are a mainstay of [relativistic quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/19389837). They are utilized in relativistically invariant wave equations for fermions (such as spinors) in arbitrary space-time dimensions, notably in [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305) and [supergravity](wikipedia://en/432632). The [Weyl–Brauer matrices](wikipedia://en/11120053) provide an explicit construction of higher-dimensional gamma matrices for [Weyl spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_spinor). Gamma matrices also appear in generic settings in [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243), particularly when a [spin structure](wikipedia://en/2844303) can be defined.

:::

## Introduction {#Introduction}

Consider a space-time of dimension $d$ with the flat [Minkowski metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_metric),

$$\eta = \|\eta_{a b}\| = \text{diag}(+1, \dots, +1, -1, \dots, -1) ~,$$

with $p$ positive entries, $q$ negative entries, $p + q = d$ and $a, b = 0, 1, ..., d - 1$. Set $N = 2^{\lfloor\tfrac{1}{2}d\rfloor}$. The standard [Dirac matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_matrices) correspond to taking $d = N = 4$ and $p, q = 1, 3$ or $3, 1$.

In higher (and lower) dimensions, one may define a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), the **gamma group**, behaving in the same fashion as the Dirac matrices.[^o3] More precisely, if one selects a [basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_space_basis) $\{e_a\}$ for the (complexified) [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305#Physics) $\mathrm{Cl}_{p,q}(\mathbb{C}) \cong \mathrm{Cl}^\mathbb{C}(p, q)$, then the gamma group [generated by](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_generator) $\{\Gamma_a\}$ is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the *multiplicative* subgroup generated by the basis elements $e_a$ (ignoring the additive aspect of the Clifford algebra).

By convention, the gamma group is realized as a collection of matrices, the gamma matrices, although the group definition does not require this. In particular, many important properties, including the [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C-symmetry), [P](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-symmetry) and [T symmetries](wikipedia://en/151013) do not require a specific matrix representation, and one obtains a clearer definition of [chirality](wikipedia://en/32703814) in this way.[^o3] Several matrix representations are possible, some given below, and others in the article on the [Weyl–Brauer matrices](wikipedia://en/11120053). In the matrix representation, the spinors are $N$-dimensional, with the gamma matrices acting on the spinors. A detailed construction of spinors is given in the article on [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305). Jost provides a standard reference for spinors in the general setting of Riemmannian geometry.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Gamma group"}

## Gamma group {#Gamma_group}

Most of the properties of the gamma matrices can be captured by a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), the **gamma group**. This group can be defined without reference to the real numbers, the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), or even any direct appeal to the [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305).[^o3] The matrix representations of this group then provide a concrete realization that can be used to specify the action of the [gamma matrices](wikipedia://en/2907966) on [spinors](wikipedia://en/29276). For $(p,q)=(1,3)$ dimensions, the matrix products behave just as the conventional [Dirac matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_matrices). The [Pauli group](wikipedia://en/5045613) is a [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) of the gamma group for $(p,q)=(3,0)$ although the Pauli group has more relationships (is [less free](wikipedia://en/59735)); see the note about the chiral element below for an example. The [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) provide a representation for $(p,q)=(0,3).$

The [presentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_presentation) of the **gamma group** $G = G_{p,q}$ is as follows.

- A [neutral element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_element) is denoted as $I$.
- The element $i$ with $i^4 = I$ is a stand-in for the complex number $i$; it [commutes with all other elements](wikipedia://en/7125),
- There is a collection of generators $\Gamma_a$ indexed by $a = 0, \ldots, p - 1$ with $\Gamma_a^2 = I~,$
- The remaining generators $\Gamma_a,\ a = p, \ldots, p + q - 1$ obey $\Gamma_a^2 = i^2~,$
- The anticommutator is defined as $\Gamma_a \Gamma_b = i^2 \Gamma_b \Gamma_a$ for $a \ne b ~.$

These generators completely define the gamma group. It can be shown that, for all $x \in G$ that $x^4 = I$ and so $x^{-1} = x^3~.$ Every element $x \in G$ can be uniquely written as a product of a finite number of generators placed in canonical order as

$$x = i^n \Gamma_a\Gamma_b \cdots \Gamma_c$$

with the indexes in ascending order

$$a < b < \cdots < c$$

and $0 \le n \le 3.$ The gamma group is finite, and has at most $2^{p+q+2}$ elements in it.

The gamma group is a [2-group](wikipedia://en/24664) but not a [regular p-group](wikipedia://en/15442661). The [commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847) (derived subgroup) is $[G, G] = \left\{I, i^2\right\} ~,$ therefore it is not a [powerful p-group](wikipedia://en/1841291). In general, 2-groups have a large number of [involutions](wikipedia://en/293450); the gamma group does likewise. Three particular ones are singled out below, as they have a specific interpretation in the context of [Clifford algebras](wikipedia://en/45305), in the context of the representations of the gamma group (where transposition and Hermitian conjugation literally correspond to those actions on matrices), and in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), where the "main involution" $\alpha$ corresponds to a combined [P-symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-symmetry) and [T-symmetry](wikipedia://en/151013).

### Transposition {#Transposition}

Given elements $\Gamma_i$ of the generating set of the gamma group, the [transposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposition_%28mathematics%29) or *reversal* is given by

$$(\Gamma_a\Gamma_b\cdots\Gamma_c)^\textsf{t} = \Gamma_c\cdots\Gamma_b\Gamma_a$$

If there are $k$ elements $\Gamma_i$ all distinct, then

$$(\Gamma_a\Gamma_b\cdots\Gamma_c)^\textsf{t} = \left(i^2\right)^{\frac{1}{2}k(k-1)}\Gamma_a\Gamma_b\cdots \Gamma_c$$

### Hermitian conjugation {#Hermitian_conjugation}

Another [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) of the gamma group is given by conjugation, defined on the generators as

$$\Gamma_a^\dagger = \begin{cases} \Gamma_a & \mbox{for } 0 \le a < p \\ i^2\Gamma_a & \mbox{for } p \le a < p + q \\ \end{cases}$$

supplemented with $i^\dagger = i^3$ and $I^\dagger = I.$ For general elements in the group, one takes the transpose: $(ab)^\dagger = b^\dagger a^\dagger~.$ From the properties of transposition, it follows that, for all elements $x\in G$ that either $xx^\dagger = x^\dagger x = I$ or that $xx^\dagger = x^\dagger x = i^2 ~,$ that is, all elements are either Hermitian or unitary.

If one interprets the $p$ dimensions as being "time-like", and the $q$ dimensions as being "space-like", then this corresponds to [P-symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-symmetry) in physics. That this is the "correct" identification follows from the conventional Dirac matrices, where $\gamma_0$ is associated with the time-like direction, and the $\gamma_i$ the spatial directions, with the "conventional" (+−−−) metric. Other metric and representational choices suggest other interpretations.

### Main involution {#Main_involution}

The **main involution** is the map that "flips" the generators: $\alpha(\Gamma_a) = i^2\Gamma_a$ but leaves $i$ alone: $\alpha(i) = i ~.$ This map corresponds to the combined [P-symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-symmetry) and [T-symmetry](wikipedia://en/151013) in physics; all directions are reversed.

### Chiral element {#Chiral_element}

Define the chiral element $\omega \equiv \Gamma_\mathrm{chir}$ as

$$\omega = \Gamma_\mathrm{chir} = \Gamma_0\Gamma_1\cdots\Gamma_{d-1}$$

where $d = p + q$. The chiral element commutes with the generators as

$$\Gamma_a\omega = \left(i^2\right)^{d-1}\omega \Gamma_a$$

It squares to

$$\omega^2 = \left(i^2\right)^{q+\frac{1}{2}d(d-1)}$$

For the Dirac matrices, the chiral element corresponds to $\gamma_5 ~,$ thus its name, as it plays an important role in distinguishing the chirality of spinors.

For the [Pauli group](wikipedia://en/5045613), the chiral element is $\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3 = i$ whereas for the gamma group $G_{3,0}$, one cannot deduce any such relationship for $\Gamma_1\Gamma_2\Gamma_3$ other than that it squares to $i^2~.$ This is an example of where a representation may have more identities than the represented group. For the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440), which provide a representation of $G_{0,3}$ the chiral element is $ijk = i^2~.$

### Charge conjugation {#Charge_conjugation}

None of the above automorphisms (transpose, conjugation, main involution) are [inner automorphisms](wikipedia://en/61839); that is they *cannot* be represented in the form $CxC^{-1}$ for some existing element $C$ in the gamma group, as presented above. Charge conjugation requires extending the gamma group with two new elements; by convention, these are

$$C_{+}\Gamma_a C_{+}^{-1} = \Gamma_a^\textsf{t}$$

and

$$C_{-}\Gamma_a C_{-}^{-1} = i^2\Gamma_a^\textsf{t}$$

The above relations are not sufficient to define a group; $C^2$ and other products are undetermined.

### Matrix representation {#Matrix_representation}

The gamma group has a matrix representation given by complex $N \times N$ matrices with $N = 2^{\left\lfloor \frac{1}{2}d\right\rfloor}$ and $d = p + q$ and $\lfloor x \rfloor$ the [floor function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_function), the largest [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) less than or equal to $x.$ The group presentation for the matrices can be written compactly in terms of the [anticommutator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticommutator#Ring_theory) relation from the [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305#Physics) $C\ell_{p,q}(\mathbf{R})$

$$\{ \Gamma_a ~,~ \Gamma_b \} = \Gamma_a\Gamma_b + \Gamma_b\Gamma_a = 2 \eta_{a b} I_N ~,$$

where the matrix $I_{N}$ is the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) in $N$ dimensions. Transposition and Hermitian conjugation correspond to their usual meaning on matrices.

:::

## Charge conjugation {#Charge_conjugation}

For the remainder of this article, it is assumed that $p = 1$ and so $q = d - 1$. That is, the [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305#Physics) $C\ell_{1,d-1}(\mathbf{R})$ is assumed. In this case, the gamma matrices have the following property under [Hermitian conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_conjugation),

$$\begin{aligned} \Gamma_0^\dagger &= +\Gamma_0 ~, & \Gamma_a^\dagger &= -\Gamma_a ~(a = 1, \dots, d - 1) ~. \end{aligned}$$

Transposition will be denoted with a minor change of notation, by mapping $\Gamma_a^\textsf{t} \mapsto \Gamma_a^\textsf{T}$ where the element on the left is the abstract group element, and the one on the right is the literal [matrix transpose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_transpose).

As before, the generators $\Gamma_{a}, -\Gamma_{a}^{T}, \Gamma_{a}^{T}$ all generate the same group (the generated groups are all [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic); the operations are still [involutions](wikipedia://en/293450)). However, since the $\Gamma_{a}$ are now matrices, it becomes plausible to ask whether there is a matrix that can act as a [similarity transformation](wikipedia://en/341149) that embodies the automorphisms. In general, such a matrix can be found. By convention, there are two of interest; in the physics literature, both referred to as [charge conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_conjugation) matrices. Explicitly, these are

$$\begin{aligned} C_{(+)} \Gamma_a C_{(+)}^{-1} &= + \Gamma_a^\textsf{T} \\ C_{(-)} \Gamma_a C_{(-)}^{-1} &= - \Gamma_a^\textsf{T} ~. \end{aligned}$$

They can be constructed as real matrices in various dimensions, as the following table shows. In even dimension both $C_\pm$ exist, in odd dimension just one.

::: {#o14 .table cue=table}

| *d* | $C^*_{(+)} = C_{(+)}$ | $C^*_{(-)} = C_{(-)}$ |
|---|---|---|
| $2$ | $C^\textsf{T}_{(+)} = C_{(+)};~~~C^2_{(+)} = 1$ | $C^\textsf{T}_{(-)} = -C_{(-)};~~~C^2_{(-)} = -1$ |
| $3$ |  | $C^\textsf{T}_{(-)} = -C_{(-)};~~~C^2_{(-)} = -1$ |
| $4$ | $C^\textsf{T}_{(+)} = -C_{(+)};~~~C^2_{(+)} = -1$ | $C^\textsf{T}_{(-)} = -C_{(-)};~~~C^2_{(-)} = -1$ |
| $5$ | $C^\textsf{T}_{(+)} = -C_{(+)};~~~C^2_{(+)} = -1$ |  |
| $6$ | $C^\textsf{T}_{(+)} = -C_{(+)};~~~C^2_{(+)} = -1$ | $C^\textsf{T}_{(-)} = C_{(-)};~~~C^2_{(-)} = 1$ |
| $7$ |  | $C^\textsf{T}_{(-)} = C_{(-)};~~~C^2_{(-)} = 1$ |
| $8$ | $C^\textsf{T}_{(+)} = C_{(+)};~~~C^2_{(+)} = 1$ | $C^\textsf{T}_{(-)} = C_{(-)};~~~C^2_{(-)} = 1$ |
| $9$ | $C^\textsf{T}_{(+)} = C_{(+)};~~~C^2_{(+)} = 1$ |  |
| $10$ | $C^\textsf{T}_{(+)} = C_{(+)};~~~C^2_{(+)} = 1$ | $C^\textsf{T}_{(-)} = -C_{(-)};~~~C^2_{(-)} = -1$ |
| $11$ |  | $C^\textsf{T}_{(-)} = -C_{(-)};~~~C^2_{(-)} = -1$ |

:::

Note that $C^*_{(\pm)} = C_{(\pm)}$ is a basis choice.

## Symmetry properties {#Symmetry_properties}

We denote a product of gamma matrices by

$$\Gamma_{abc \dotsm} = \Gamma_a \cdot \Gamma_b \cdot \Gamma_c \cdots {}$$

and note that the anti-commutation property allows us to simplify any such sequence to one in which the indices are distinct and increasing. Since distinct $\Gamma_a$ anti-commute this motivates the introduction of an anti-symmetric "average". We introduce the anti-symmetrised products of distinct $n$-tuples from 0, ..., $d$ − 1:

$$\Gamma_{a_1 \dots a_n} = \frac{1}{n!} \sum_{\pi \in S_n} \epsilon(\pi) \Gamma_{a_{\pi(1)}} \cdots \Gamma_{a_{\pi(n)}} ~,$$

where $\pi$ runs over all the [permutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutations) of $n$ symbols, and $\epsilon$ is the [alternating character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_character). There are 2<sup>*d*</sup> such products, but only $N$<sup>2</sup> are independent, spanning the space of $N$×$N$ matrices.

Typically, $\Gamma_{ab}$ provide the (bi)spinor representation of the $\tfrac{1}{2}d(d - 1)$ generators of the [higher-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher-dimensional) [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489), $\operatorname{SO}^{+}(1, d - 1)$, generalizing the [6 matrices *σ<sup>μν</sup>*](wikipedia://en/2249718#Reducible_representations) of the [spin representation](wikipedia://en/10160091) of the Lorentz group in four dimensions.

For even $d$, one may further define the hermitian *chiral matrix*

$$\Gamma_\text{chir} = i^{\frac{d}{2}-1} \Gamma_0 \Gamma_1 \dotsm \Gamma_{d-1} ~,$$

such that $\{\Gamma_{\operatorname{chir}}, \Gamma_{a}\} = 0$ and $\Gamma_{\operatorname{chir}}^{2} = 1$. (In odd dimensions, such a matrix would commute with all $\Gamma$<sub>*a*</sub>s and would thus be proportional to the identity, so it is not considered.)

A $\Gamma$ matrix is called symmetric if

$$( C \Gamma_{a_1 \dotsm a_n} )^\textsf{T} = +( C \Gamma_{a_1 \dotsm a_n} ) ~;$$

otherwise, for a − sign, it is called antisymmetric.

In the previous expression, $C$ can be either $C_{(+)}$ or $C_{(-)}$. In odd dimension, there is no ambiguity, but in even dimension it is better to choose whichever one of $C_{(+)}$ or $C_{(-)}$ allows for Majorana spinors. In $d$ = 6, there is no such criterion and therefore we consider both.

::: {#o16 .table cue=table}

| *d* | *C* | Symmetric | Antisymmetric |
|---|---|---|---|
| $3$ | $C_{(-)}$ | $\gamma_{a}$ | $I_2$ |
| $4$ | $C_{(-)}$ | $\gamma_{a} ~,~ \gamma_{a_1 a_2}$ | $I_4 ~,~ \gamma_\text{chir} ~,~ \gamma_\text{chir} \gamma_a$ |
| $5$ | $C_{(+)}$ | $\Gamma_{a_1 a_2}$ | $I_4 ~,~ \Gamma_a$ |
| $6$ | $C_{(-)}$ | $I_8 ~,~ \Gamma_\text{chir} \Gamma_{a_1 a_2} ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 a_2 a_3}$ | $\Gamma_a ~,~ \Gamma_\text{chir}~,~ \Gamma_\text{chir} \Gamma_a ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 a_2}$ |
| $7$ | $C_{(-)}$ | $I_8 ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 a_2 a_3}$ | $\Gamma_a ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 a_2}$ |
| $8$ | $C_{(+)}$ | $I_{16} ~,~ \Gamma_{a} ~,~ \Gamma_\text{chir} ~,~ \Gamma_\text{chir}\Gamma_{a_1 a_2 a_3} ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 \dots a_4}$ | $\Gamma_\text{chir} \Gamma_a ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 a_2} ~,~ \Gamma_\text{chir} \Gamma_{a_1 a_2} ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 a_2 a_3}$ |
| $9$ | $C_{(+)}$ | $I_{16} ~,~ \Gamma_{a} ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 \dotsm a_4} ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 \dotsm a_5}$ | $\Gamma_{a_1 a_2} ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 a_2 a_3}$ |
| $10$ | $C_{(-)}$ | $\Gamma_{a} ~,~ \Gamma_\text{chir} ~,~ \Gamma_\text{chir} \Gamma_a ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 a_2} ~,~ \Gamma_\text{chir} \Gamma_{a_1 \dotsm a_4} ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 \dotsm a_5}$ | $I_{32} ~,~ \Gamma_\text{chir} \Gamma_{a_1 a_2} ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 a_2 a_3} ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 \dotsm a_4} ~,~ \Gamma_\text{chir} \Gamma_{a_1 a_2 a_3}$ |
| $11$ | $C_{(-)}$ | $\Gamma_a ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 a_2} ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 \dotsm a_5}$ | $I_{32} ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 a_2 a_3} ~,~ \Gamma_{a_1 \dotsm a_4}$ |

## Identities {#Identities}

The proof of the trace identities for gamma matrices hold for all even dimension. One therefore only needs to remember the [4D case](wikipedia://en/2907966#Trace_identities) and then change the overall factor of 4 to $\operatorname{tr}(I_N)$. For [other identities](wikipedia://en/2907966#Miscellaneous_identities) (the ones that involve a contraction), explicit functions of $d$ will appear. $\gamma^\mu\gamma_\mu = d I_N$; $\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma_\mu = (2 - d) \gamma^\nu$; $\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma_\mu = 2 \gamma^\rho\gamma^\nu + (d - 2) \gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho$; $\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma\gamma_\mu = 2 \gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma\gamma^\nu - 2 \gamma^\nu\gamma^\sigma\gamma^\rho - (d - 2) \gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma$ Even when the number of physical dimensions is four, these more general identities are ubiquitous in loop calculations due to [dimensional regularization](wikipedia://en/1934335).

## Example of an explicit construction {#Example_of_an_explicit_construction}

The $\Gamma$ matrices can be constructed recursively, first in all even dimensions, $d$ = 2$k$, and thence in odd ones, 2$k$ + 1.

### *d* = 2 {#d_=_2}

Using the [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868), take

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma_0 &= \sigma_1, & \gamma_1 &= -i \sigma_2 \end{aligned}$$

and one may easily check that the charge conjugation matrices are

$$\begin{aligned} C_{(+)} = \sigma_1 = C_{(+)}^* = s_{(2,+)} C_{(+)}^\textsf{T} &= s_{(2,+)} C_{(+)}^{-1} & s_{(2,+)} &= +1 \\ C_{(-)} = i \sigma_2 = C_{(-)}^* = s_{(2,-)} C_{(-)}^\textsf{T} &= s_{(2,-)} C_{(-)}^{-1} & s_{(2,-)} &= -1~. \end{aligned}$$

One may finally define the hermitian chiral $\gamma$<sub>chir</sub> to be

$$\gamma_\text{chir} = \gamma_0 \gamma_1 = \sigma_3 = \gamma_\text{chir}^\dagger ~.$$

### Generic even *d* = 2*k* {#Generic_even_d_=_2k}

One may now construct the $\Gamma_{a}, (a = 0, ... , d + 1)$, matrices and the charge conjugations $C$<sub>(±)</sub> in $d$ + 2 dimensions, starting from the $\gamma_{a'}$ , ($a' = 0, ... , d - 1$), and $c$<sub>(±)</sub> matrices in $d$ dimensions.

Explicitly,

$$\begin{aligned} \Gamma_{a'} &= \gamma_{a'} \otimes \sigma_3 ~~~ \left(a' = 0, \dots, d - 1\right)~, & \Gamma_{d} &= I \otimes (i \sigma_1) ~, & \Gamma_{d+1} &= I \otimes (i \sigma_2) ~. \end{aligned}$$

One may then construct the charge conjugation matrices,

$$\begin{aligned} C_{(+)} &= c_{(-)} \otimes \sigma_1 ~, & C_{(-)} &= c_{(+)} \otimes (i \sigma_2) ~, \end{aligned}$$

with the following properties,

$$\begin{aligned} C_{(+)} = C_{(+)}^* = s_{(d+2,+)} C_{(+)}^\textsf{T} &= s_{(d+2,+)} C_{(+)}^{-1} & s_{(d+2,+)} &= s_{(d,-)} \\ C_{(-)} = C_{(-)}^* = s_{(d+2,-)} C_{(-)}^\textsf{T} &= s_{(d+2,-)} C_{(-)}^{-1} & s_{(d+2,-)} &= -s_{(d,+)} ~. \end{aligned}$$

Starting from the sign values for $d$ = 2, $s$<sub>(2,+)</sub> = +1 and $s$<sub>(2,−)</sub> = −1, one may fix all subsequent signs $s$<sub>(*d*,±)</sub> which have periodicity 8; explicitly, one finds

::: {#o21 .table cue=table}

|  | $d = 8 k$ | $d = 8 k + 2$ | $d = 8 k + 4$ | $d = 8 k + 6$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $s_{(d,+)}$ | \+1 | \+1 | −1 | −1 |
| $s_{(d,-)}$ | \+1 | −1 | −1 | \+1 |

:::

Again, one may define the hermitian chiral matrix in $d$+2 dimensions as

$$\begin{aligned} \Gamma_\text{chir} &= \alpha_{d+2} \Gamma_0 \Gamma_1 \dotsm \Gamma_{d+1} = \gamma_\text{chir} \otimes \sigma_3~, & \alpha_d &= i^{\frac{1}{2}d-1} ~, \end{aligned}$$

which is diagonal by construction and transforms under charge conjugation as

$$\begin{aligned} C_{(\pm)} \Gamma_\text{chir} C_{(\pm)}^{-1} &= \beta_{d+2} \Gamma_\text{chir}^\textsf{T}~, & \beta_d &= (-)^{\frac{1}{2}d(d-1)} ~. \end{aligned}$$

It is thus evident that $\{\Gamma_{\operatorname{chir}} , \Gamma_{a}\}$ = 0. Once a permutation is applied to make the +1 and -1 eigenvalues of the chiral matrix consecutive, this choice becomes the direct analogue of the [chiral basis](wikipedia://en/2907966#Weyl_%28chiral%29_basis) in four dimensions.

### Generic odd *d* = 2*k* + 1 {#Generic_odd_d_=_2k_+_1}

Consider the previous construction for $d$ − 1 (which is even) and simply take all $\Gamma_{a} (a = 0, ..., d - 2)$ matrices, to which append its $i\Gamma_{\operatorname{chir}} \equiv \Gamma_{d-1}$. (The $i$ is required in order to yield an antihermitian matrix, and extend into the spacelike metric).

Finally, compute the charge conjugation matrix: choose between $C_{(+)}$ and $C_{(-)}$, in such a way that $\Gamma_{d-1}$ transforms as all the other $\Gamma$ matrices. Explicitly, require

$$C_{(s)} \Gamma_\text{chir} C_{(s)}^{-1} = \beta_{d} \Gamma_\text{chir}^\textsf{T} = s \Gamma_\text{chir}^\textsf{T} ~.$$

As the dimension $d$ ranges, patterns typically repeat themselves with period 8. (cf. the [Clifford algebra clock](wikipedia://en/782099#Geometric_model_of_loop_spaces).)
