# Quaquaversal tiling

*Image: Quaquaversal Tiling.svg.* The substitution rule for the quaquaversal tiling.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quaquaversal tiling"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **quaquaversal tiling** is a [nonperiodic tiling](wikipedia://en/868145) of Euclidean 3-space introduced by [John Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) and [Charles Radin](wikipedia://en/39630926). It is analogous to the [pinwheel tiling](wikipedia://en/9402045) in 2 dimensions having tile orientations that are dense in $\operatorname{SO}(3)$. The basic solid tiles are [30-60-90](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/30-60-90) [triangular prisms](wikipedia://en/2859584) arranged in a pattern such that some copies are rotated by $\pi/3$, and some are rotated by $\pi/2$ in a perpendicular direction.

:::

They construct the group $G(p,q)$ given by a rotation of $2\pi/p$ and a perpendicular rotation by $2\pi/q$; the orientations in the quaquaversal tiling are given by $G(6,4)$. $G(p,1)$ are [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327), $G(p,2)$ are [dihedral groups](wikipedia://en/100267), $G(4,4)$ is the [octahedral group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octahedral_group), and all other $G(p,q)$ are infinite and dense in $\operatorname{SO}(3)$; if $p$ and $q$ are odd and ≥3, then $G(p,q)$ is a [free group](wikipedia://en/59735).

Radin and Lorenzo Sadun constructed similar honeycombs based on a tiling related to the [Penrose tilings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penrose_tilings) and the pinwheel tiling; the former has orientations in $G(10,4)$, and the latter has orientations in $G(p,4)$ with the irrational rotation $2\pi/p = \arctan(1/2)$. They show that $G(p,4)$ is dense in $\operatorname{SO}(3)$ for the aforementioned value of $p$, and whenever $\cos(2\pi/p)$ is [transcendental](wikipedia://en/30325).
