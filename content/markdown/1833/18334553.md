# Inequalities in information theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Inequalities in information theory"}

[Inequalities](wikipedia://en/89489) are very important in the study of [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773). There are a number of different contexts in which these inequalities appear.

:::

## Entropic inequalities {#Entropic_inequalities}

Consider a tuple $X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n$ of $n$ [finitely (or at most countably) supported](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_probability_distribution) [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) on the same [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325). There are 2<sup>*n*</sup> subsets, for which ([joint](wikipedia://en/910967)) entropies can be computed. For example, when *n* = 2, we may consider the entropies $H(X_1),$ $H(X_2),$ and $H(X_1, X_2)$. They satisfy the following inequalities (which together characterize the range of the marginal and joint entropies of two random variables):

- $$H(X_1) \ge 0$$
- $$H(X_2) \ge 0$$
- $$H(X_1) \le H(X_1, X_2)$$
- $$H(X_2) \le H(X_1, X_2)$$
- $$H(X_1, X_2) \le H(X_1) + H(X_2).$$

In fact, these can all be expressed as special cases of a single inequality involving the **[conditional mutual information](wikipedia://en/18867510)**, namely

$$I(A;B|C) \ge 0,$$

where $A$, $B$, and $C$ each denote the joint distribution of some arbitrary (possibly empty) subset of our collection of random variables. Inequalities that can be derived as linear combinations of this are known as **Shannon-type** inequalities.

For larger $n$ there are further restrictions on possible values of entropy. To make this precise, a vector $h$ in $\mathbb{R}^{2^n}$ indexed by subsets of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ is said to be *[entropic](wikipedia://en/17519721)* if there is a joint, discrete distribution of *n* random variables $X_1,\dots,X_n$ such that $h_I = H(X_i \colon i \in I)$ is their [joint entropy](wikipedia://en/910967), for each subset $I$. The set of entropic vectors is denoted $\Gamma^*_n$, following the notation of Yeung.[^o3] It is not closed nor convex for $n \geq 3$, but its [topological closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_closure) $\overline{\Gamma^*_n}$ is known to be convex and hence it can be characterized by the (infinitely many) [linear inequalities](wikipedia://en/19575563) satisfied by all entropic vectors, called **[entropic inequalities](wikipedia://en/17519721)**.

The set of all vectors that satisfy Shannon-type inequalities (but not necessarily other entropic inequalities) contains $\overline{\Gamma^*_n}$. This containment is strict for $n \geq 4$ and further inequalities are known as *non-Shannon type* inequalities. Zhang and Yeung reported the first non-Shannon-type inequality,[^o4] often referred to as the Zhang-Yeung inequality. Matus[^o5] proved that no [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) of inequalities can characterize (by linear combinations) all entropic inequalities. In other words, the region $\overline{\Gamma^*_n}$ is not a [polytope](wikipedia://en/1817885).

## Lower bounds for the Kullback–Leibler divergence {#Lower_bounds_for_the_Kullback–Leibler_divergence}

A great many important inequalities in information theory are actually lower bounds for the [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052). Even the Shannon-type inequalities can be considered part of this category, since the [interaction information](wikipedia://en/6964629) can be expressed as the Kullback–Leibler divergence of the joint distribution with respect to the product of the marginals, and thus these inequalities can be seen as a special case of [Gibbs' inequality](wikipedia://en/2035678).

On the other hand, it seems to be much more difficult to derive useful upper bounds for the Kullback–Leibler divergence. This is because the Kullback–Leibler divergence *D*<sub>*KL*</sub>(*P*||*Q*) depends very sensitively on events that are very rare in the reference distribution *Q*. *D*<sub>*KL*</sub>(*P*||*Q*) increases without bound as an event of finite non-zero probability in the distribution *P* becomes exceedingly rare in the reference distribution *Q*, and in fact *D*<sub>*KL*</sub>(*P*||*Q*) is not even defined if an event of non-zero probability in *P* has zero probability in *Q*. (Hence the requirement that *P* be absolutely continuous with respect to *Q*.)

::: {#o8 .statement cue=named-heading name="Gibbs' inequality"}

### Gibbs' inequality {#Gibbs'_inequality}

This fundamental inequality states that the [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052) is non-negative.

:::

::: {#o10 .statement cue=named-heading name="Kullback's inequality"}

### Kullback's inequality {#Kullback's_inequality}

Another inequality concerning the Kullback–Leibler divergence is known as **Kullback's inequality**.[^o11] If *P* and *Q* are [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) on the real line with *P* [absolutely continuous](wikipedia://en/252670) with respect to *Q,* and whose first moments exist, then

$$D_{KL}(P\parallel Q) \ge \Psi_Q^*(\mu'_1(P)),$$

where $\Psi_Q^*$ is the [large deviations](wikipedia://en/3772909) **[rate function](wikipedia://en/3772816)**, i.e. the [convex conjugate](wikipedia://en/1001490) of the [cumulant](wikipedia://en/359684)-generating function, of *Q*, and $\mu'_1(P)$ is the first [moment](wikipedia://en/368684) of *P*.

The [Cramér–Rao bound](wikipedia://en/581124) is a corollary of this result.

:::

::: {#o13 .statement cue=named-heading name="Pinsker's inequality"}

### Pinsker's inequality {#Pinsker's_inequality}

Pinsker's inequality relates [Kullback&ndash;Leibler divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kullback&ndash;Leibler_divergence) and [total variation distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_variation_distance). It states that if *P*, *Q* are two [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543), then

$$\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}D_{KL}^{(e)}(P\parallel Q)} \ge \sup \{ |P(A) - Q(A)| : A\text{ is an event to which probabilities are assigned.} \}.$$

where

$$D_{KL}^{(e)}(P\parallel Q)$$

is the Kullback–Leibler divergence in [nats](wikipedia://en/3070481) and

$$\sup_A |P(A) - Q(A)|$$

is the total variation distance.

:::

## Other inequalities {#Other_inequalities}

### Hirschman uncertainty {#Hirschman_uncertainty}

In 1957,[^o16] Hirschman showed that for a (reasonably well-behaved) function $f:\mathbb R \rightarrow \mathbb C$ such that $\int_{-\infty}^\infty |f(x)|^2\,dx = 1,$ and its [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) $g(y)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) e^{-2 \pi i x y}\,dx,$ the sum of the [differential entropies](wikipedia://en/3504168) of $|f|^2$ and $|g|^2$ is non-negative, i.e.

$$-\int_{-\infty}^\infty |f(x)|^2 \log |f(x)|^2 \,dx -\int_{-\infty}^\infty |g(y)|^2 \log |g(y)|^2 \,dy \ge 0.$$

Hirschman conjectured, and it was later proved,[^o17] that a sharper bound of $\log(e/2),$ which is attained in the case of a [Gaussian distribution](wikipedia://en/21462), could replace the right-hand side of this inequality. This is especially significant since it implies, and is stronger than, Weyl's formulation of Heisenberg's [uncertainty principle](wikipedia://en/31883).

::: {#o19 .statement cue=named-heading name="Tao's inequality"}

### Tao's inequality {#Tao's_inequality}

Given discrete random variables $X$, $Y$, and $Y'$, such that $X$ takes values only in the interval \[−1, 1\] and $Y'$ is determined by $Y$ (such that $H(Y'|Y)=0$), we have[^o20][^o21]

$$\operatorname E \big( \big| \operatorname E(X|Y') - \operatorname E(X\mid Y) \big| \big) \le \sqrt {I(X;Y\mid Y') \, 2 \log 2 },$$

relating the [conditional expectation](wikipedia://en/435099) to the [conditional mutual information](wikipedia://en/18867510). This is a simple consequence of [Pinsker's inequality](wikipedia://en/20064401). (Note: the correction factor log 2 inside the radical arises because we are measuring the conditional mutual information in [bits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit) rather than [nats](wikipedia://en/3070481).)

:::

## Machine based proof checker of information-theoretic inequalities {#Machine_based_proof_checker_of_information-theoretic_inequalities}

Several machine based proof checker algorithms are now available. Proof checker algorithms typically verify the inequalities as either true or false. More advanced proof checker algorithms can produce proof or counterexamples.[^o23] Quantum information-theoretic inequalities can be checked by the contraction map proof method.[^o24]
