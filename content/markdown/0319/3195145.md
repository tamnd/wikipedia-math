# Binary icosahedral group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Binary icosahedral group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **binary icosahedral group** 2*I* or $\langle 2,3,5\rangle$[^o2] is a certain [nonabelian group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonabelian_group) of [order](wikipedia://en/493527) 120. It is an [extension](wikipedia://en/345807) of the [icosahedral group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icosahedral_group) *I* or (2,3,5) of order 60 by the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order 2, and is the [preimage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preimage) of the icosahedral group under the 2:1 [covering homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covering_homomorphism)

:::

$$\operatorname{Spin}(3) \to \operatorname{SO}(3)\,$$

of the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) by the [spin group](wikipedia://en/411231). It follows that the binary icosahedral group is a [discrete subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_subgroup) of Spin(3) of order 120.

It should not be confused with the [full icosahedral group](wikipedia://en/2877844#Commonly_confused_groups), which is a different group of order 120, and is rather a subgroup of the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) O(3).

In the algebra of [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440), the binary icosahedral group is concretely realized as a discrete subgroup of the [versors](wikipedia://en/2644621), which are the quaternions of norm one. For more information see [Quaternions and spatial rotations](wikipedia://en/186057).

## Elements {#Elements}

*Image: Binary icosahedral group elements 12-fold.svg.* 120 quaternion elements seen in 12-fold projection. Element orders are given: 1,2,3,4,5,6,10

Explicitly, the binary icosahedral group is given as the union of all [even permutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_permutation) of the following vectors:

- 8 even permutations of $(\pm 1, 0, 0, 0)$
- 16 even permutations of $(\pm 1/2, \pm 1/2, \pm 1/2, \pm 1/2)$
- 96 even permutations of $(0, \pm 1/2, \pm 1/2\varphi, \pm \varphi/2)$

Here $\varphi = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}} {2}$ is the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386).

In total there are 120 elements, namely the unit [icosians](wikipedia://en/27977943). They all have unit magnitude and therefore lie in the unit quaternion group Sp(1).

The 120 elements in 4-dimensional space match the 120 vertices of the [600-cell](wikipedia://en/501447), a [regular 4-polytope](wikipedia://en/18801624).p. 372

## Properties {#Properties}

### Central extension {#Central_extension}

The binary icosahedral group, denoted by 2*I*, is the [universal perfect central extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_perfect_central_extension) of the icosahedral group, and thus is [quasisimple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasisimple): it is a perfect central extension of a [simple group](wikipedia://en/47600).[^o7]

Explicitly, it fits into the [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence)

$$1\to\{\pm 1\}\to 2I\to I \to 1.\,$$

This sequence does not [split](wikipedia://en/1531404), meaning that 2*I* is *not* a [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) of { ±1 } by *I*. In fact, there is no subgroup of 2*I* isomorphic to *I*.

The [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_a_group) of 2*I* is the subgroup { ±1 }, so that the [inner automorphism group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_automorphism_group) is isomorphic to *I*. The full [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) is isomorphic to *S*<sub>5</sub> (the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) on 5 letters), just as for $I\cong A_5$ - any automorphism of 2*I* fixes the non-trivial element of the center ($-1$), hence descends to an automorphism of *I,* and conversely, any automorphism of *I* lifts to an automorphism of 2*I,* since the lift of generators of *I* are generators of 2*I* (different lifts give the same automorphism).

### Superperfect {#Superperfect}

The binary icosahedral group is [perfect](wikipedia://en/585271), meaning that it is equal to its [commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847). In fact, 2*I* is the unique perfect group of order 120. It follows that 2*I* is not [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012).

Further, the binary icosahedral group is [superperfect](wikipedia://en/9915961), meaning abstractly that its first two [group homology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_homology) groups vanish: $H_1(2I;\mathbf{Z})\cong H_2(2I;\mathbf{Z})\cong 0.$ Concretely, this means that its abelianization is trivial (it has no non-trivial abelian quotients) and that its [Schur multiplier](wikipedia://en/1121587) is trivial (it has no non-trivial perfect central extensions). In fact, the binary icosahedral group is the smallest (non-trivial) superperfect group.

The binary icosahedral group is not [acyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acyclic_group), however, as H<sub>*n*</sub>(2*I*,**Z**) is cyclic of order 120 for *n* = 4*k*+3, and trivial for *n* > 0 otherwise, (Adem Milgram 1994, 279).

### Isomorphisms {#Isomorphisms}

Concretely, the binary icosahedral group is a subgroup of Spin(3), and covers the icosahedral group, which is a subgroup of SO(3). Abstractly, the icosahedral group is isomorphic to the symmetries of the 4-[simplex](wikipedia://en/39781), which is a subgroup of SO(4), and the binary icosahedral group is isomorphic to the double cover of this in Spin(4). Note that the symmetric group $S_5$ *does* have a 4-dimensional representation (its usual lowest-dimensional [irreducible representation](wikipedia://en/385982) as the full symmetries of the $(n-1)$-simplex), and that the full symmetries of the 4-simplex are thus $S_5,$ not the full icosahedral group (these are two different groups of order 120).

The binary icosahedral group can be considered as the [double cover of the alternating group](wikipedia://en/25118486) $A_5,$ denoted $2\cdot A_5 \cong 2I;$ this isomorphism covers the isomorphism of the icosahedral group with the alternating group $A_5 \cong I.$ Just as $I$ is a discrete subgroup of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, $2I$ is a discrete subgroup of the double cover of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, namely $\mathrm{Spin}(3) \cong \mathrm{SU}(2)$. The 2-1 homomorphism from $\mathrm{Spin}(3)$ to $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ then restricts to the 2-1 homomorphism from $2I$ to $I$.

One can show that the binary icosahedral group is isomorphic to the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) SL(2,5) — the group of all 2×2 matrices over the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) **F**<sub>5</sub> with unit determinant; this covers the [exceptional isomorphism](wikipedia://en/49726#Exceptional_isomorphisms) of $I\cong A_5$ with the [projective special linear group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_special_linear_group) PSL(2,5).

Note also the exceptional isomorphism $PGL(2,5) \cong S_5,$ which is a different group of order 120, with the commutative square of SL, GL, PSL, PGL being isomorphic to a commutative square of $2\cdot A_5, 2\cdot S_5, A_5, S_5,$ which are isomorphic to subgroups of the commutative square of Spin(4), Pin(4), SO(4), O(4).

### Presentation {#Presentation}

The group 2*I* has a [presentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_presentation) given by

$$\langle r,s,t \mid r^2 = s^3 = t^5 = rst \rangle$$

or equivalently,

$\langle s,t \mid (st)^2 = s^3 = t^5 \rangle.$[^o12]

Generators in the group of unit quaternions with these relations are given by

$$s = \tfrac{1}{2}(1+i+j+k) \qquad t = \tfrac{1}{2}(\varphi+\varphi^{-1}i+j).$$

### Subgroups {#Subgroups}

*Image: Binary icosahedral group subgroups.png.* Subgroups: • [binary tetrahedral group](wikipedia://en/10997586): **2T**=⟨2,3,3⟩ • 3 [binary dihedral groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_dihedral_group): **Q**<sub>20</sub>=⟨2,2,5⟩, **Q**<sub>12</sub>=⟨2,2,3⟩, **Q**<sub>8</sub>=⟨2,2,2⟩ • 3 [binary cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/14423377): **Z**<sub>10</sub>=⟨5⟩, **Z**<sub>6</sub>=⟨3⟩, **Z**<sub>4</sub>=⟨2⟩ • 3 [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327): **Z**<sub>5</sub>=(5), **Z**<sub>3</sub>=(3), **Z**<sub>2</sub>=(2) • 1 [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059): ( )

The only proper [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of 2*I* is the center { ±1 }.

By the [third isomorphism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_isomorphism_theorem), there is a [Galois connection](wikipedia://en/156411) between subgroups of 2*I* and subgroups of *I*, where the [closure operator](wikipedia://en/483120) on subgroups of 2*I* is multiplication by { ±1 }.

$-1$ is the only element of order 2, hence it is contained in all subgroups of even order: thus every subgroup of 2*I* is either of odd order or is the preimage of a subgroup of *I*.

Besides the [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) generated by the various elements (which can have odd order), the only other subgroups of 2*I* (up to conjugation) are:[^o14]

- [binary dihedral groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_dihedral_group), Dic<sub>5</sub>=Q20=⟨2,2,5⟩, order 20 and Dic<sub>3</sub>=Q12=⟨2,2,3⟩ of order 12
- the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130), Q8=⟨2,2,2⟩, consisting of the 8 [Lipschitz units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipschitz_unit) forms a subgroup of [index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_%28group_theory%29) 15, which is also the [dicyclic group](wikipedia://en/154963) Dic<sub>2</sub>; this covers the stabilizer of an edge.
- the 24 [Hurwitz units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurwitz_unit) form an index 5 subgroup called the [binary tetrahedral group](wikipedia://en/10997586); this covers a chiral [tetrahedral group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedral_group). This group is [self-normalizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-normalizing) so its [conjugacy class](wikipedia://en/49176) has 5 members (this gives a map $2I \to S_5$ whose image is $A_5$).

## Relation to 4-dimensional symmetry groups {#Relation_to_4-dimensional_symmetry_groups}

The 4-dimensional analog of the [icosahedral symmetry group](wikipedia://en/2877844) *I*<sub>h</sub> is the symmetry group of the [600-cell](wikipedia://en/501447) (also that of its dual, the [120-cell](wikipedia://en/717358)). Just as the former is the [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004) of type *H*<sub>3</sub>, the latter is the Coxeter group of type *H*<sub>4</sub>, also denoted \[3,3,5\]. Its rotational subgroup, denoted [\[3,3,5\]<sup>\+</sup>](wikipedia://en/32088502#Rank_four_groups) is a group of order 7200 living in [SO(4)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SO%284%29). SO(4) has a [double cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_covering_group) called [Spin(4)](wikipedia://en/411231) in much the same way that Spin(3) is the double cover of SO(3). Similar to the isomorphism Spin(3) = Sp(1), the group Spin(4) is isomorphic to Sp(1) × Sp(1).

The preimage of \[3,3,5\]<sup>\+</sup> in Spin(4) (a four-dimensional analogue of 2*I*) is precisely the [product group](wikipedia://en/3706015) 2*I* × 2*I* of order 14400. The rotational symmetry group of the 600-cell is then

\[3,3,5\]<sup>\+</sup> = ( 2*I* × 2*I* ) / { ±1 }.

Various other 4-dimensional symmetry groups can be constructed from 2*I*.[^n1]

## Applications {#Applications}

The [coset space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coset_space) Spin(3) / 2*I* = *S*<sup>3</sup> / 2*I* is a [spherical 3-manifold](wikipedia://en/1205989) called the [Poincaré homology sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_homology_sphere). It is an example of a [homology sphere](wikipedia://en/585388), i.e. a 3-manifold whose [homology groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_group) are identical to those of a [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792). The [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of the Poincaré sphere is isomorphic to the binary icosahedral group, as the Poincaré sphere is the quotient of a 3-sphere by the binary icosahedral group.
