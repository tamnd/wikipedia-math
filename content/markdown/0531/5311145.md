# Lebesgue spine

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lebesgue spine"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the area of [potential theory](wikipedia://en/1431342), a **Lebesgue spine** or **Lebesgue thorn** is a type of [set](wikipedia://en/26691) used for discussing solutions to the [Dirichlet problem](wikipedia://en/571109) and related problems of potential theory. The Lebesgue spine was introduced in 1912 by [Henri Lebesgue](wikipedia://en/49514) to demonstrate that the Dirichlet problem does not always have a solution, particularly when the boundary has a sufficiently sharp edge protruding into the interior of the region.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A typical Lebesgue spine in $\mathbb{R}^n$, for $n\ge 3,$ is defined as follows

$$S = \{(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n)\in\mathbb{R}^n : x_n>0, x_1^2+x_2^2+\cdots +x_{n-1}^2 \le \exp(-1/x_n^2) \}.$$

The important features of this set are that it is [connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_set) and [path-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path-connected) in the [euclidean topology](wikipedia://en/1482085) in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and the origin is a [limit point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point) of the set, and yet the set is thin at the origin, as defined in the article [Fine topology (potential theory)](wikipedia://en/5170051).

:::

## Observations {#Observations}

The set $S$ is not closed in the [euclidean topology](wikipedia://en/1482085) since it does not contain the origin which is a [limit point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point) of $S$, but the set is closed in the [fine topology](wikipedia://en/5170051) in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

In comparison, it is not possible in $\mathbb{R}^2$ to construct such a connected set which is thin at the origin.
