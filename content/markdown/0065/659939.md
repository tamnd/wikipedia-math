# Square

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Square"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **square** is a [regular](wikipedia://en/333306) [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278). It has four straight sides of equal length and four equal [angles](wikipedia://en/1196). Squares are special cases of [rectangles](wikipedia://en/70048), which have four equal angles, and of [rhombuses](wikipedia://en/183884), which have four equal sides. As with all rectangles, a square's angles are [right angles](wikipedia://en/76956) (90 [degrees](wikipedia://en/1195294), or [$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601)/2 [radians](wikipedia://en/26003)), making adjacent sides [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944). The [area](wikipedia://en/1209) of a square is the side length multiplied by itself, and so in [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), multiplying a number by itself is called [squaring](wikipedia://en/659942).

:::

Equal squares can tile the plane edge-to-edge in the [square tiling](wikipedia://en/2863809). Square tilings are ubiquitous in [tiled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tile) floors and walls, [graph paper](wikipedia://en/293606), image [pixels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixel), and [game boards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_board). Square shapes are also often seen in building [floor plans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_plan), [origami paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origami_paper), food servings, in [graphic design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphic_design) and [heraldry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldry), and in instant photos and fine art.

The formula for the area of a square forms the basis of the calculation of area and motivates the search for methods for [squaring the circle](wikipedia://en/201359) by [compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge), now known to be impossible. Squares can be inscribed in any smooth or convex curve, such as a circle or triangle, but it remains unsolved [whether a square can be inscribed in every simple closed curve](wikipedia://en/27631070). Several problems of [squaring the square](wikipedia://en/67903) involve subdividing squares into unequal squares. Mathematicians have also studied packing squares as tightly as possible into other shapes.

Squares can be constructed by [straightedge and compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straightedge_and_compass), through their [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates), or by repeated multiplication by $i$ in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628). They form the [metric balls](wikipedia://en/160556) for [taxicab geometry](wikipedia://en/408354) and [Chebyshev distance](wikipedia://en/1503963), two forms of non-Euclidean geometry. Although [spherical geometry](wikipedia://en/174026) and [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291) both lack [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621) with four equal sides and right angles, they have square-like regular polygons with four sides and other angles, or with right angles and different numbers of sides.

## Definitions and characterizations {#Definitions_and_characterizations}

*Image: The square among a family of rectangles or rhombuses.png.* Among rectangles (top row), the square is the shape with equal sides (blue, middle). Among rhombuses (bottom row), the square is the shape with right angles (blue, middle).

Squares can be defined or characterized in many equivalent ways. If a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) satisfies any one of the following criteria, it satisfies all of them:

- A square is a polygon with four equal sides and four [right angles](wikipedia://en/76956); that is, it is a quadrilateral that is both a rhombus and a rectangle
- A square is a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048) with four equal sides.
- A square is a [rhombus](wikipedia://en/183884) with a right angle between a pair of adjacent sides.
- A square is a rhombus with all angles equal.
- A square is a [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496) with one right angle and two adjacent equal sides.
- A square is a quadrilateral where the diagonals are equal, and are the perpendicular bisectors of each other. That is, it is a rhombus with equal diagonals.
- A square is a quadrilateral with successive sides $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ whose area is $$A=\frac14(a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2).$$

Squares are the only [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306) whose [internal angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_and_external_angle), [central angle](wikipedia://en/462705#Central_angle_of_a_regular_polygon), and [external angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_and_external_angle) are all equal (they are all right angles).

## Properties {#Properties}

A square is a special case of a [rhombus](wikipedia://en/183884) (equal sides, opposite equal angles), a [kite](wikipedia://en/84840) (two pairs of adjacent equal sides), a [trapezoid](wikipedia://en/201826) (one pair of opposite sides parallel), a [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496) (all opposite sides parallel), a [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278) or tetragon (four-sided polygon), and a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048) (opposite sides equal, right-angles), and therefore has all the properties of all these shapes, namely:

- All four internal angles of a square are equal (each being 90°, a right angle).
- The central angle of a square is equal to 90°.
- The external angle of a square is equal to 90°.
- The diagonals of a square are equal and [bisect](wikipedia://en/152547) each other, meeting at 90°.
- The diagonals of a square bisect its internal angles, forming [adjacent angles](wikipedia://en/1196#adjacent) of 45°.
- All four sides of a square are equal.[^n1]
- Opposite sides of a square are [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497).[^n2]

All squares are [similar](wikipedia://en/39377) to each other, meaning they have the same shape. One parameter (typically the length of a side or diagonal) suffices to specify a square's size. Squares of the same size are [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330).

### Measurement {#Measurement}

*Image: YBC-7289-OBV-labeled.jpg.* [YBC 7289](wikipedia://en/11490196), a [Babylonian](wikipedia://en/3861353) calculation of a square's diagonal from between 1800 and 1600 BCE

*Image: Five Squared.svg.* The area of a square is the product of the lengths of its sides.

A square whose four sides have length $\ell$ has [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636)[^n3] $P=4\ell$ and [diagonal](wikipedia://en/349251) length $d=\sqrt2\ell$.[^n4] The [square root of 2](wikipedia://en/341682), appearing in this formula, is [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689), meaning that it cannot be written exactly as a [fraction](wikipedia://en/1704824). It is approximately equal to 1.414, and its approximate value was already known in [Babylonian mathematics](wikipedia://en/3861353). A square's [area](wikipedia://en/1209) is[^n4]

$$A=\ell^2=\tfrac12 d^2.$$

This formula for the area of a square as the second power of its side length led to the use of the term *[squaring](wikipedia://en/659942)* to mean raising any number to the second power. Reversing this relation, the side length of a square of a given area is the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of the area. Squaring an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563), or taking the area of a square with integer sides, results in a [square number](wikipedia://en/213919); these are [figurate numbers](wikipedia://en/325260) representing the numbers of points that can be arranged into a square grid.[^n5]

Since four squared equals sixteen, a four by four square has an area equal to its perimeter. That is, it is an [equable shape](wikipedia://en/11435202). The only other equable integer rectangle is a three-by-six rectangle.

Because it is a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306), a square is the quadrilateral of least perimeter enclosing a given area. Dually, a square is the quadrilateral containing the largest area within a given perimeter. Indeed, if *A* and *P* are the area and perimeter enclosed by a quadrilateral, then the following [isoperimetric inequality](wikipedia://en/326182) holds:

$$16A\le P^2$$

with equality if and only if the quadrilateral is a square.[^n6]

### Symmetry {#Symmetry}

The square is the most symmetrical of the quadrilaterals. Eight [rigid transformations](wikipedia://en/27832980) of the plane take the square to itself:

*Image: Quadrilateral symmetries.svg.* The axes of reflection symmetry and centers of rotation symmetry of a square (top), rectangle and rhombus (center), [isosceles trapezoid](wikipedia://en/511691), kite, and parallelogram (bottom)

For an axis-parallel square centered at the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432), each symmetry acts by a combination of negating and swapping the [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinate) of points. The symmetries permute the eight isosceles triangles between the half-edges and the square's center (which stays in place); any of these triangles can be taken as the [fundamental region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_region) of the transformations. Each two vertices, each two edges, and each two half-edges are mapped one to the other by at least one symmetry (exactly one for half-edges). All regular polygons also have these properties, which are expressed by saying that symmetries of a square and, more generally, a regular polygon act [transitively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_action) on vertices and edges, and [simply transitively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_transitively) on half-edges.

Combining any two of these transformations by performing one after the other continues to take the square to itself, and therefore produces another symmetry. Repeated rotation produces another rotation with the summed rotation angle. Two reflections with the same axis return to the identity transformation, while two reflections with different axes rotate the square. A rotation followed by a reflection, or vice versa, produces a different reflection. This [composition operation](wikipedia://en/195947) gives the eight symmetries of a square the mathematical structure of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), called the *group of the square* or the *[dihedral group of order eight](wikipedia://en/78749515)*. Other quadrilaterals, like the rectangle and rhombus, have only a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of these symmetries.

*Image: Perspective-3point.svg.* Three-point perspective of a cube, showing perspective transformations of its six square faces into six different quadrilaterals

The shape of a square, but not its size, is preserved by [similarities](wikipedia://en/39377) of the plane. Other kinds of transformations of the plane can take squares to other kinds of quadrilateral. An [affine transformation](wikipedia://en/38449) can take a square to any parallelogram, or vice versa; a [projective transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_transformation) can take a square to any convex [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278), or vice versa. This implies that, when [viewed in perspective](wikipedia://en/380405), a square can look like any convex quadrilateral, or vice versa. A [Möbius transformation](wikipedia://en/314493) can take the vertices of a square (but not its edges) to the vertices of a [harmonic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/61186948).

The [wallpaper groups](wikipedia://en/386214) are symmetry groups of two-dimensional repeating patterns. For many of these groups the basic unit of repetition (the unit cell of its [period lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Period_lattice)) can be a square, and for three of these groups, p4, p4m, and p4g, it must be a square.

### Inscribed and circumscribed circles {#Inscribed_and_circumscribed_circles}

*Image: Incircle and circumcircle of a square.png.* The [inscribed circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscribed_circle) (purple) and [circumscribed circle](wikipedia://en/161262) (red) of a square (black)

The [inscribed circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscribed_circle) of a square is the largest circle that can fit inside that square. Its center is the center point of the square, and its radius (the [inradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inradius) of the square) is $r=\ell/2$. Because this circle touches all four sides of the square (at their midpoints), the square is a [tangential quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/9176412). The [circumscribed circle](wikipedia://en/161262) of a square passes through all four vertices, making the square a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131). Its radius, the [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius), is $R=\ell/\sqrt2$.[^n7] If the inscribed circle of a square $ABCD$ has tangency points $E$ on $AB$, $F$ on $BC$, $G$ on $CD$, and $H$ on $DA$, then for any point $P$ on the inscribed circle,

$$2(PH^2-PE^2) = PD^2-PB^2.$$

If $d_i$ is the distance from an arbitrary point in the plane to the $i$th vertex of a square and $R$ is the [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) of the square, then

$$\frac{d_1^4+d_2^4+d_3^4+d_4^4}{4} + 3R^4 = \left(\frac{d_1^2+d_2^2+d_3^2+d_4^2}{4} + R^2\right)^2.$$

If $L$ and $d_i$ are the distances from an arbitrary point in the plane to the centroid of the square and its four vertices respectively, then

$$d_1^2 + d_3^2 = d_2^2 + d_4^2 = 2(R^2+L^2)$$

and

$$d_1^2d_3^2 + d_2^2d_4^2 = 2(R^4+L^4),$$

where $R$ is the circumradius of the square.

## Applications {#Applications}

Squares are so well-established as the shape of [tiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiles) that the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language) word [tessera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessera), for a small tile as used in [mosaics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosaic), comes from an ancient Greek word for the number four, referring to the four corners of a square tile. [Graph paper](wikipedia://en/293606), preprinted with a [square tiling](wikipedia://en/2863809), is widely used for [data visualization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_visualization) using [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates). The [pixels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixel) of [bitmap images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitmap_image), as recorded by [image scanners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_scanner) and [digital cameras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_camera) or displayed on [electronic visual displays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_visual_display), conventionally lie at the intersections of a square grid, and are often considered as small squares, arranged in a square tiling. Standard techniques for [image compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_compression) and [video compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_compression), including the [JPEG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JPEG) format, are based on the subdivision of images into larger square blocks of pixels. The [quadtree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadtree) data structure used in data compression and [computational geometry](wikipedia://en/176927) is based on the [recursive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursive) subdivision of squares into smaller squares.

*Image: 20240815 Site of Luoyang City from Han to Wei Dynasty - Site of the Pagoda of Yongning Temple 04.jpg.* Site of the [Yongning Pagoda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yongning_Pagoda)

Architectural structures from both ancient and modern cultures have featured a square floor plan, base, or footprint. Ancient examples include the [Egyptian pyramids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_pyramids), [Mesoamerican pyramids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_pyramids) such as those at [Teotihuacan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teotihuacan), the [Chogha Zanbil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chogha_Zanbil) ziggurat in Iran, the four-fold design of Persian walled gardens, said to model the four rivers of Paradise, and later structures inspired by their design such as the [Taj Mahal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taj_Mahal) in India, the square bases of Buddhist [stupas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stupa), and East Asian [pagodas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pagoda), buildings that symbolically face to the four points of the compass and reach to the heavens. Norman [keeps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keep) such as the [Tower of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_of_London) often take the form of a low square tower. In modern architecture, a majority of [skyscrapers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skyscraper) feature a square plan for pragmatic rather than aesthetic or symbolic reasons.

The stylized nested squares of a Tibetan [mandala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala), like the design of a stupa, function as a miniature model of the cosmos. Some formats for film photography use a square [aspect ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspect_ratio_%28image%29), notably [Polaroid cameras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polaroid_camera), [medium format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium_format) cameras, and [Instamatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instamatic) cameras. Painters known for their frequent and prominent use of square forms and frames include [Josef Albers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josef_Albers), [Kazimir Malevich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazimir_Malevich), [Piet Mondrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piet_Mondrian), and [Theo van Doesburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theo_van_Doesburg).

[Baseball diamonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseball_diamond) and [boxing rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxing_ring) are square despite being named for other shapes. In the [quadrille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrille) and [square dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_dance), four couples form the sides of a square. In [Samuel Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett)'s minimalist television play *[Quad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quad_%28play%29)*, four actors walk along the sides and diagonals of a square.

The square [go board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go_board) is said to represent the earth, with the 361 crossings of its lines representing days of the year. The [chessboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chessboard) inherited its square shape from a [pachisi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pachisi)-like Indian race game and in turn passed it on to [checkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Checkers). In two ancient games from [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia) and [Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt), the [Royal Game of Ur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Game_of_Ur) and [Senet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senet), the game board itself is not square, but rectangular, subdivided into a grid of squares. The ancient Greek [Ostomachion](wikipedia://en/14620737) puzzle (according to some interpretations) involves rearranging the pieces of a square cut into smaller polygons, as does the Chinese [tangram](wikipedia://en/54419). Another set of puzzle pieces, the [polyominos](wikipedia://en/72634), are formed from squares glued edge-to-edge; in this context, a single square is called a *monomino*. Medieval and Renaissance [horoscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horoscope) were arranged in a square format, across Europe, the Middle East, and China. Other recreational uses of squares include the shape of [origami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origami) paper, and a common style of [quilting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quilting) involving the use of square quilt blocks. *[Scrabble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrabble)* players place square lettered tiles onto a grid of $15\times 15$ squares on a square board.

Squares are a common element of [graphic design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphic_design), used to give a sense of stability, symmetry, and order. In [heraldry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldry), a [canton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canton_%28heraldry%29) (a design element in the top left of a shield) is normally square, and a square flag is called a banner. The [flag of Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Switzerland) is square, as are the [flags of the Swiss cantons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flags_and_arms_of_cantons_of_Switzerland). [QR codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QR_code) are square and feature prominent nested square alignment marks in three corners. [Robertson screws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robertson_screw) have a square drive socket. [Crackers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cracker_%28food%29) and sliced [cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheese) are often square, as are [waffles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waffle). Square foods named for their square shapes include [caramel squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caramel_square), [date squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Date_square), [lemon squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemon_square), [square sausage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_sausage), and [Carré de l'Est](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carré_de_l'Est) cheese.

Squares can be found in nature and the sciences. In [optical illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_illusion), the [Orbison illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbison_illusion), named after American psychologist [William Orbison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Orbison), consists of a two-dimensional circle and square, superimposed over a background of radial lines or concentric circles. In biological living, *[Haloquadratum walsbyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haloquadratum_walsbyi)* is an [Archaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaea) species with square shape. In [stereochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereochemistry), a [square planar molecular geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_planar_molecular_geometry) is a chemical structure with atoms at the corners of a square. An example is [xenon tetrafluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenon_tetrafluoride).

## Constructions {#Constructions}

### Coordinates and equations {#Coordinates_and_equations}

*Image: Square equation plot.svg.* The equation $|x| + |y| = 2$ plotted on Cartesian coordinates

A [unit square](wikipedia://en/285301) is a square of side length one. Often it is represented in [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinate) as the square enclosing the points $(x,y)$ that have $0\le x\le 1$ and $0\le y\le 1$. Its vertices are the four points that have 0 or 1 in each of their coordinates.

An axis-parallel square with its center at the point $(x_c,y_c)$ and sides of length $2r$ (where $r$ is the inradius, half the side length) has vertices at the four points $(x_c\pm r,y_c\pm r)$. Its interior consists of the points $(x,y)$ with $\max(|x-x_c|,|y-y_c|) < r$, and its boundary consists of the points with $\max(|x-x_c|,|y-y_c|)=r$.

A diagonal square with its center at the point $(x_c,y_c)$ and diagonal of length $2R$ (where $R$ is the circumradius, half the diagonal) has vertices at the four points $(x_c\pm R,y_c)$ and $(x_c,y_c\pm R)$. Its interior consists of the points $(x,y)$ with $|x-x_c|+|y-y_c|<R$, and its boundary consists of the points with $|x-x_c|+|y-y_c|=R$. For instance the illustration shows a diagonal square centered at the origin $(0,0)$ with circumradius 2, given by the equation $|x|+|y|=2$.

*Image: A square with Gaussian integer vertices.png.* A square formed by multiplying the complex number $p$ by powers of $i$, and its translation obtained by adding another complex number $c$. The background grid shows the [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628).

In the [plane of complex numbers](wikipedia://en/217628), multiplication by the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358) $i$ rotates the other term in the product by 90° around the origin (the number zero). Therefore, if any nonzero complex number $p$ is repeatedly multiplied by $i$, giving the four numbers $p$, $ip$, $-p$, and $-ip$, these numbers will form the vertices of a square centered at the origin. If one interprets the [real part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part) and [imaginary part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_part) of these four complex numbers as Cartesian coordinates, with $p=x+iy$, then these four numbers have the coordinates $(x,y)$, $(-y,x)$, $(-x,-y)$, and $(-y,-x)$. This square can be translated to have any other complex number $c$ is center, using the fact that the [translation](wikipedia://en/204682) from the origin to $c$ is represented in complex number arithmetic as addition with $c$. The [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628), complex numbers with integer real and imaginary parts, form a [square lattice](wikipedia://en/2874853) in the complex plane.

### Compass and straightedge {#Compass_and_straightedge}

The construction of a square with a given side, using a [compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge_constructions), is given in [Euclid's *Elements*](wikipedia://en/244107) I.46. The existence of this construction means that squares are [constructible polygons](wikipedia://en/523430). A regular $n$-gon is constructible exactly when the odd [prime factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor) of $n$ are distinct [Fermat primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_prime), and in the case of a square $n=4$ has no odd prime factors so this condition is [vacuously true](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuously_true).

*Elements* IV.6–7 also give constructions for a square inscribed in a circle and circumscribed about a circle, respectively.

## Related topics {#Related_topics}

The [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) of a square is $\{4\}$. A [truncated](wikipedia://en/4224429) square is an [octagon](wikipedia://en/314575).[^n8] The square belongs to a family of [regular polytopes](wikipedia://en/518693) that includes the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) in three dimensions and the [hypercubes](wikipedia://en/39783) in higher dimensions,[^n9] and to another family that includes the [regular octahedron](wikipedia://en/22458) in three dimensions and the [cross-polytopes](wikipedia://en/716401) in higher dimensions.[^n10] The cube and hypercubes can be given vertex coordinates that are all $\pm 1$, giving an axis-parallel square in two dimensions, while the octahedron and cross-polytopes have one coordinate $\pm 1$ and the rest zero, giving a diagonal square in two dimensions.[^n11] As with squares, the [symmetries of these shapes](wikipedia://en/24268961) can be obtained by applying a [signed permutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signed_permutation) to their coordinates.

The [Sierpiński carpet](wikipedia://en/29635) is a square [fractal](wikipedia://en/10913), with square holes. [Space-filling curves](wikipedia://en/352398) including the [Hilbert curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_curve), [Peano curve](wikipedia://en/358330), and [Sierpiński curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpiński_curve) cover a square as the continuous image of a line segment. The [Z-order curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z-order_curve) is analogous but not continuous. Other mathematical functions associated with squares include [Arnold's cat map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold's_cat_map) and the [baker's map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baker's_map), which generate chaotic [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087) on a square, and the [lemniscate elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/3754835), complex functions periodic on a square grid.

*Image: Hadwiger finsler theorem.svg.* Illustration of Finsler–Hadwiger theorem

Many theorems involve squares. The [Finsler–Hadwiger theorem](wikipedia://en/27007324) states that for two squares $ABCD$ and $AB'C'D'$, the center of both squares and the midpoint of $BD'$ and $B'D$ form a third square. This theorem can be applied repeatedly to prove [van Aubel's theorem](wikipedia://en/3021425), that the centers of four squares constructed on the sides of a quadrilateral form a [midsquare quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/45005169).[^n12] A square cannot be dissected into an odd number of equal-area triangles, a result of [Monsky's theorem](wikipedia://en/35098575). [Cross's theorem](wikipedia://en/82566796) or Vecten's theorem states that, for a triangle formed by the sides of three squares, and connecting the squares' vertices to form another three triangles, all triangles have the same area.

*Image: Great Britain Box.svg.* Squares are used for measuring the [fractal dimension](wikipedia://en/285907) of the [Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain)'s map

[Minkowski–Bouligand dimension](wikipedia://en/601025) or box-counting dimension involves squares to determine the [fractal dimension](wikipedia://en/285907) of a set in Euclidean space or [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018), the squares are named "boxes". The fractal dimension of a set is attainable by lying on an evenly spaced grid and counting the number of squares required to cover the set. The method of [box counting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box_counting) is used when the grid is finer.

Mathematical puzzles that include squares are square arrays that are filled with numbers in [Sudoku](wikipedia://en/1365807) with generalized symbols in [Latin square](wikipedia://en/37447), and with color or blank in [nonogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonogram), as well as paradoxical optical illusions such as the [missing square puzzle](wikipedia://en/365627) and the [chessboard paradox](wikipedia://en/66687082). The [Langton's ant](wikipedia://en/105016), in [cellular automaton](wikipedia://en/54342), is a two-dimensional square lattice consisting of a black-or-white colored grid wherein an ant assigns the combination of those colors and its current direction of motion.

A [square trisection](wikipedia://en/31982221) is a dissection problem of cutting a square into multiple pieces and then reassembling them into three identical squares. [Tarski's circle-squaring problem](wikipedia://en/216811) challenges the [equidecomposability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equidecomposable) between a square and a circle; that is, cutting the [disc](wikipedia://en/329542) of a circle into finitely many pieces and reassembling them into a square.

### Inscribed squares {#Inscribed_squares}

*Image: Calabi triangle.svg.* The [Calabi triangle](wikipedia://en/37447398) and the three placements of its largest square.[^n13] The placement on the long side of the triangle is inscribed; the other two are not.

A square is [inscribed](wikipedia://en/4470203) in a curve when all four vertices of the square lie on the curve. The unsolved [inscribed square problem](wikipedia://en/27631070) asks whether every [simple closed curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_closed_curve) has an inscribed square. It is true for every [smooth curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_curve), and for any closed [convex curve](wikipedia://en/29747989). The only other regular polygon that can always be inscribed in every closed convex curve is the [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285), as there exists a convex curve on which no other regular polygon can be inscribed.

For an [inscribed square in a triangle](wikipedia://en/79255114), at least one side of the square lies on a side of the triangle. Every [acute triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_triangle) has three inscribed squares, one for each of its three sides. A [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420) has two inscribed squares, one touching its right angle and the other lying on its hypotenuse. An [obtuse triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obtuse_triangle) has only one inscribed square, on its longest side. A square inscribed in a triangle can cover at most half the triangle's area.

### Area and quadrature {#Area_and_quadrature}

*Image: Pythagorean.svg.* The [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034): the two smaller squares on the sides of a right triangle have equal total area to the larger square on the hypotenuse.

*Image: Squaring the Circle J.svg.* A circle and square with the same area

Since ancient times, many units for surface [area](wikipedia://en/1209) have been defined from squares, typically with a standard unit of [length](wikipedia://en/18542) as its side, for example, a [square meter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_meter) or [square inch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_inch).

In [ancient Greek deductive geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), the area of a planar shape was measured and compared by constructing a square with the same area by using only a finite number of steps with [compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge), a process called *[quadrature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrature_%28geometry%29)* or *squaring*. [Euclid's *Elements*](wikipedia://en/244107) shows how to do this for rectangles, parallelograms, triangles, and then more generally for [simple polygons](wikipedia://en/1059530) by breaking them into triangular pieces. Some shapes with curved sides could also be squared, such as the [lune of Hippocrates](wikipedia://en/21854422) and the [parabola](wikipedia://en/12745577).

This use of a square as the defining shape for area measurement also occurs in the Greek formulation of the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034): squares constructed on the two sides of a [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420) have equal total area to a square constructed on the [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366). Stated in this form, the theorem would be equally valid for other shapes on the sides of the triangle, such as equilateral triangles or semicircles, but the Greeks used squares. In modern mathematics, this formulation of the theorem using areas of squares has been replaced by an algebraic formulation involving [squaring numbers](wikipedia://en/659942): the lengths of the sides and hypotenuse of the right triangle obey the equation $a^2+b^2=c^2$.

Because of this focus on quadrature as a measure of area, the Greeks and later mathematicians sought unsuccessfully to [square the circle](wikipedia://en/201359), constructing a square with the same area as a given circle, again using finitely many steps with a compass and straightedge. In 1882, the task was proven to be impossible as a consequence of the [Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/348976). This theorem proves that [pi](wikipedia://en/23601) ($\pi$) is a [transcendental number](wikipedia://en/30325) rather than an [algebraic irrational number](wikipedia://en/1158); that is, it is not the [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) of any [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) with [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) coefficients. A construction for squaring the circle could be translated into a polynomial formula for $\pi$, which does not exist. In philosophy, the concept of a "[square circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round_square_copula)" has been used as an example of an [oxymoron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxymoron) since [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308), sparking attempts to find contexts such as [taxicab geometry](wikipedia://en/408354) (below) in which this phrase is meaningful.

### Tiling and packing {#Tiling_and_packing}

The [square tiling](wikipedia://en/2863809), familiar from flooring and game boards, is one of three [regular tilings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiling_by_regular_polygons) of the plane. The other two use the [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285) and the [regular hexagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_hexagon). The vertices of a square tiling form a [square lattice](wikipedia://en/2874853).[^n14] Squares of more than one size can also tile the plane,[^n15] for instance in the [Pythagorean tiling](wikipedia://en/33371056), named for its connection to proofs of the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034).

*Image: Packing 11 unit squares in a square with side length 3.87708359....svg.* The smallest known square that can contain 11 unit squares has side length approximately 3.877084.

[Square packing](wikipedia://en/31948840) problems seek the smallest square or circle into which a given number of [unit squares](wikipedia://en/285301) can fit. A chessboard optimally packs a square number of unit squares into a larger square, but beyond a few special cases such as this, the optimal solutions to these problems remain unsolved; the same is true for [circle packing in a square](wikipedia://en/27471721). Packing squares into other shapes can have high [computational complexity](wikipedia://en/6511): testing whether a given number of unit squares can fit into an [orthogonally convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_convexity) [rectilinear polygon](wikipedia://en/8779182) with [half-integer](wikipedia://en/1568608) vertex coordinates is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete).

[Squaring the square](wikipedia://en/67903) involves subdividing a given square into smaller squares, all having integer side lengths. A subdivision with distinct smaller squares is called a perfect squared square. Another variant of squaring the square called "Mrs. Perkins's quilt" allows repetitions, but uses as few smaller squares as possible in order to make the [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) of the side lengths be 1. The entire plane can be tiled by squares, with exactly one square of each integer side length.

In higher dimensions, other surfaces than the plane can be tiled by equal squares, meeting edge-to-edge. One of these surfaces is the [Clifford torus](wikipedia://en/7950934), the four-dimensional [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of two congruent circles; it has the same intrinsic geometry as a single square with each pair of opposite edges glued together. Another square-tiled surface, a [regular skew apeirohedron](wikipedia://en/45592828) in three dimensions, has six squares meeting at each vertex. The [paper bag problem](wikipedia://en/2035985) seeks the maximum volume that can be enclosed by a surface tiled with two squares glued edge to edge; its exact answer is unknown. Gluing two squares in a different pattern, with the vertex of each square attached to the midpoint of an edge of the other square (or alternatively subdividing these two squares into eight squares glued edge-to-edge) produces a pincushion shape called a [biscornu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biscornu). The surfaces tiled with finitely many squares of the three-dimensional integer lattice are called [polyominoids](wikipedia://en/22339952).

### Counting {#Counting}

*Image: Two square counting puzzles.svg.* Two square-counting puzzles: There are 14 squares in a $3 \times 3$ grid of squares (top), but as a $4 \times 4$ grid of points it has six more off-axis squares (bottom) for a total of 20.

A common [mathematical puzzle](wikipedia://en/366445) involves counting the squares of all sizes in a square grid of $n\times n$ squares. For instance, a square grid of nine squares has 14 squares: the nine squares that form the grid, four more $2\times 2$ squares, and one $3\times 3$ square. The answer to the puzzle is $n(n+1)(2n+1)/6$, a [square pyramidal number](wikipedia://en/542343). For $n=1,2,3,\dots$ these numbers are:

1, 5, 14, 30, 55, 91, 140, 204, 285, ...

A variant of the same puzzle asks for the number of squares formed by a grid of $n\times n$ points, allowing squares that are not axis-parallel. For instance, a grid of nine points has five axis-parallel squares as described above, but it also contains one more diagonal square for a total of six. In this case, the answer is given by the *4-dimensional pyramidal numbers* $n^2(n^2-1)/12$. For $n=1,2,3,\dots$ these numbers are:

0, 1, 6, 20, 50, 105, 196, 336, 540, ...

*Image: Plastic square partitions.svg.* Partitions of a square into three similar rectangles

Another counting problem involving squares asks for the number of different shapes of rectangles that can be used when [dividing a square into similar rectangles](wikipedia://en/73246459). A square can be divided into two similar rectangles only in one way, by bisecting it, but when dividing a square into three similar rectangles there are three possible [aspect ratios](wikipedia://en/1021) of the rectangles, 3:1, 3:2, and the square of the [plastic ratio](wikipedia://en/2148532), approximately 1.755:1. The number of proportions that are possible when dividing into $n$ rectangles is known for small values of $n$, but not as a general formula. For $n=1,2,3,\dots$ these numbers are: 1, 1, 3, 11, 51, 245, 1372, ...

A [magic square](wikipedia://en/52202) is a square array of numbers, where the sums of the positive numbers in each row, each column, and both main diagonals are the same. For $n \times n$ array, the sum can be formulated as $n(n^2 + 1)/2$; the numbers for $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ are: 1, 5, 15, 34, 65, 111, 175, 260, 369, 505, ...

### Other geometries {#Other_geometries}

In the familiar Euclidean geometry, space is flat, and every convex quadrilateral has internal angles summing to 360°, so a square (a regular quadrilateral) has four equal sides and four right angles (each 90°). By contrast, in [spherical geometry](wikipedia://en/174026) and [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), space is curved and the internal angles of a convex quadrilateral never sum to 360°, so quadrilaterals with four right angles do not exist. Both of these geometries feature regular quadrilaterals, characterized by four equal sides and four equal angles, often referred to as squares, although some authors prefer to avoid this name because they lack right angles. These geometries also feature regular polygons with right angles, but with the number of sides differing from four.

In spherical geometry, space has uniform positive curvature, and every convex quadrilateral (a [polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_polygon) with four [great-circle arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great-circle_arc) edges) has angles whose sum exceeds 360° by an amount called the [angular excess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_excess), proportional to its surface area. Small spherical squares are approximately Euclidean, and the angles of larger squares increase with area. One special case is the face of a [spherical cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_cube) with four 120° angles, covering one sixth of the sphere's surface. Another is a [hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemisphere_%28geometry%29), the face of a spherical square [dihedron](wikipedia://en/6109457), with four [straight angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_angle); the [Peirce quincuncial projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peirce_quincuncial_projection) for [world maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_map) [conformally maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_mapping) two such faces to Euclidean squares. An [octant of a sphere](wikipedia://en/74955167) is a regular [spherical triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_triangle), with three equal sides and three right angles; eight of them tile the sphere, with four meeting at each vertex, to form a [spherical octahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_octahedron). A [spherical lune](wikipedia://en/23024648) is a regular [digon](wikipedia://en/5442728), with two semicircular sides and two equal angles at [antipodal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipodal_points) vertices; a right-angled lune covers one quarter of the sphere, one face of a four-lune [hosohedron](wikipedia://en/2316192).

In [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), space has uniform negative curvature, and every convex quadrilateral has angles whose sum falls short of 360° by an amount called the [angular defect](wikipedia://en/673575), proportional to its surface area. Small hyperbolic squares are approximately Euclidean, and larger squares decrease with increasing area. Special cases include the squares with angles of $360^{\circ}/n$ for every value of $n$ larger than $4$, each of which can tile the [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane). In the infinite limit, an [ideal square](wikipedia://en/4995922#Polygons_with_ideal_vertices) has four sides of infinite length and four vertices at [ideal points](wikipedia://en/4995922) outside the hyperbolic plane, with $0^{\circ}$ internal angles; an ideal square, like every ideal quadrilateral, has finite area proportional to its angular defect of $360^{\circ}$. It is also possible to make a regular hyperbolic polygon with right angles at every vertex and any number of sides greater than four; such polygons can [uniformly tile the hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/18640974), [dual](wikipedia://en/8815) to the tiling with $n$ squares about each vertex.

*Image: Metric circles.png.* Metric circles using Chebyshev, Euclidean, and taxicab distance functions

The Euclidean plane can be defined in terms of the [real coordinate plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_coordinate_plane) by adoption of the [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) function, according to which the distance between any two points $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$ is $\textstyle \sqrt{(x_1-x_2)^2+(y_1-y_2)^2}$. Other metric geometries are formed when a different [distance function](wikipedia://en/20018) is adopted instead, and in some of these geometries, shapes that would be Euclidean squares become the "[circles](wikipedia://en/160556#In_normed_vector_spaces)" (set of points of equal distance from a center point). Squares tilted at 45° to the coordinate axes are the circles in [taxicab geometry](wikipedia://en/408354), based on the $L_1$ distance $|x_1-x_2|+|y_1-y_2|$. The points with taxicab distance $d$ from any given point form a diagonal square, centered at the given point, with diagonal length $2d$. In the same way, axis-parallel squares are the circles for the $L_{\infty}$ or [Chebyshev distance](wikipedia://en/1503963), $\max(|x_1-x_2|,|y_1-y_2|)$. In this metric, the points with distance $d$ from some point form an axis-parallel square, centered at the given point, with side length $2d$. Relatedly, a square and circle can become an intermediate shape known as [squircle](wikipedia://en/5565333).
