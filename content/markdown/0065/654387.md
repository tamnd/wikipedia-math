# Distance geometry

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Distance geometry"}

**Distance geometry** is the branch of mathematics concerned with [characterizing](wikipedia://en/780886) and studying [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) of points based *only* on given values of the [distances](wikipedia://en/39378) between pairs of points.[^o34][^o32][^o33] More abstractly, it is the study of [semimetric spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semimetric_space) and the [isometric transformations](wikipedia://en/254777) between them. In this view, it can be considered as a subject within [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649).[^o31]

:::

Historically, the first result in distance geometry is [Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123) in the 1st century AD. The modern theory began in the 19th century with work by [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034), followed by more extensive developments in the 20th century by [Karl Menger](wikipedia://en/149431) and others.

Distance geometry problems arise whenever one needs to infer the shape of a configuration of points ([relative positions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_position)) from the distances between them, such as in [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology),[^o31] [sensor networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensor_network),[^o29] [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying), [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation), [cartography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartography), and [physics](wikipedia://en/22939).

## Introduction and definitions {#Introduction_and_definitions}

The concepts of distance geometry will first be explained by describing two particular problems.

*Image: Hyperbolic\_Navigation.svg.* Problem of hyperbolic navigation

### First problem: [hyperbolic navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_navigation) {#First_problem:_hyperbolic_navigation}

Consider three ground radio stations A, B, C, whose locations are known. A radio receiver is at an unknown location. The times it takes for a radio signal to travel from the stations to the receiver, $t_A,t_B,t_C$, are unknown, but the time differences, $t_A-t_B$ and $t_A-t_C$, are known. From them, one knows the distance differences $c(t_A-t_B)$ and $c(t_A-t_C)$, from which the position of the receiver can be found.

### Second problem: [dimension reduction](wikipedia://en/579867) {#Second_problem:_dimension_reduction}

In [data analysis](wikipedia://en/2720954), one is often given a list of data represented as vectors $\mathbf{v} = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)\in \mathbb{R}^n$, and one needs to find out whether they lie within a low-dimensional affine subspace. A low-dimensional representation of data has many advantages, such as saving storage space, computation time, and giving better insight into data.

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

### Definitions {#Definitions}

Now we formalize some definitions that naturally arise from considering our problems.

#### Semimetric space {#Semimetric_space}

Given a list of points on $R = \{P_0, \ldots, P_n\}$, $n \ge 0$, we can arbitrarily specify the distances between pairs of points by a list of $d_{ij}> 0$, $0 \le i < j \le n$. This defines a [semimetric space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi_metric_space): a metric space without [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941).

Explicitly, we define a semimetric space as a nonempty set $R$ equipped with a semimetric $d: R\times R \to [0, \infty)$ such that, for all $x, y\in R$,

1. Positivity: $d(x, y) = 0$ if and only if $x = y$.
1. Symmetry: $d(x, y) = d(y, x)$.

Any metric space is [*a fortiori*](wikipedia://en/13474947) a semimetric space. In particular, $\mathbb{R}^k$, the $k$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), is the [canonical](wikipedia://en/515096) metric space in distance geometry.

The triangle inequality is omitted in the definition, because we do not want to enforce more constraints on the distances $d_{ij}$ than the mere requirement that they be positive.

In practice, semimetric spaces naturally arise from inaccurate measurements. For example, given three points $A, B, C$ on a line, with $d_{AB} = 1, d_{BC} = 1, d_{AC} = 2$, an inaccurate measurement could give $d_{AB} = 0.99, d_{BC} = 0.98, d_{AC} = 2.00$, violating the triangle inequality.

#### Isometric embedding {#Isometric_embedding}

Given two semimetric spaces, $(R, d), (R', d')$, an [isometric embedding](wikipedia://en/254777) from $R$ to $R'$ is a map $f: R \to R'$ that preserves the semimetric, that is, for all $x, y\in R$, $d(x, y) = d'(f(x), f(y))$.

For example, given the finite semimetric space $(R, d)$ defined above, an isometric embedding from $R$ to $\mathbb{R}^k$ is defined by points $A_0, A_1,\ldots, A_n \in \mathbb R^k$, such that $d(A_i, A_j) = d_{ij}$ for all $0 \le i < j \le n$.

#### Affine independence {#Affine_independence}

Given the points $A_0, A_1,\ldots, A_n \in \mathbb R^k$, they are defined to be [affinely independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_independence), [iff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iff) they cannot fit inside a single $l$-dimensional affine subspace of $\mathbb{R}^k$, for any $\ell < n$, iff the $n$*\-*[simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) they span, $v_n$, has positive $n$-volume, that is, $\operatorname{Vol}_n(v_n) > 0$.

In general, when $k\ge n$, they are affinely independent, since a [generic](wikipedia://en/12886758) *n*-simplex is nondegenerate. For example, 3 points in the plane, in general, are not collinear, because the triangle they span does not degenerate into a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860). Similarly, 4 points in space, in general, are not coplanar, because the tetrahedron they span does not degenerate into a flat triangle.

When $n > k$, they must be affinely dependent. This can be seen by noting that any $n$-simplex that can fit inside $\mathbb{R}^k$ must be "flat".

:::

## Cayley–Menger determinants {#Cayley–Menger_determinants}

Cayley–Menger determinants, named after Arthur Cayley and Karl Menger, are determinants of matrices of distances between sets of points.

Let $A_0, A_1,\ldots, A_n$ be *n* + 1 points in a semimetric space, their [Cayley–Menger determinant](wikipedia://en/2826108) is defined by

$$\operatorname{CM}(A_0, \cdots, A_n) = \begin{vmatrix} 0 & d_{01}^2 & d_{02}^2 & \cdots & d_{0n}^2 & 1 \\ d_{01}^2 & 0 & d_{12}^2 & \cdots & d_{1n}^2 & 1 \\ d_{02}^2 & d_{12}^2 & 0 & \cdots & d_{2n}^2 & 1 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\ d_{0n}^2 & d_{1n}^2 & d_{2n}^2 & \cdots & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 & 0 \end{vmatrix}$$

If $A_0, A_1,\ldots, A_n \in \mathbb R^k$, then they make up the vertices of a possibly [degenerate](wikipedia://en/232952) *n*-simplex $v_n$ in $\mathbb{R}^k$. It can be shown that[^o11] the *n*-dimensional volume of the simplex $v_n$ satisfies

$$\operatorname{Vol}_n(v_n)^2 = \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{(n!)^2 2^n} \operatorname{CM}(A_0, \ldots, A_n).$$

Note that, for the case of $n=0$, we have $\operatorname{Vol}_0(v_0) = 1$, meaning the "0-dimensional volume" of a 0-simplex is 1, that is, there is 1 point in a 0-simplex.

$A_0, A_1,\ldots, A_n$ are affinely independent iff $\operatorname{Vol}_n(v_n) > 0$, that is, $(-1)^{n+1} \operatorname{CM}(A_0, \ldots, A_n) > 0$. Thus Cayley–Menger determinants give a computational way to prove affine independence.

If $k < n$, then the points must be affinely dependent, thus $\operatorname{CM}(A_0, \ldots, A_n) = 0$. Cayley's 1841 paper studied the special case of $k = 3, n = 4$, that is, any five points $A_0, \ldots, A_4$ in 3-dimensional space must have $\operatorname{CM}(A_0, \ldots, A_4) = 0$.

## History {#History}

The first result in distance geometry is [Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123), from 1st century AD, which gives the area of a triangle from the distances between its 3 vertices. [Brahmagupta's formula](wikipedia://en/194129), from 7th century AD, generalizes it to [cyclic quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/194131). [Tartaglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niccolò_Fontana_Tartaglia), from 16th century AD, generalized it to give the [volume of tetrahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niccolò_Fontana_Tartaglia#Volume_of_a_tetrahedron) from the distances between its 4 vertices.

The modern theory of distance geometry began with [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034) and [Karl Menger](wikipedia://en/149431).[^o13] Cayley published the Cayley determinant in 1841,[^o14] which is a special case of the general Cayley–Menger determinant. Menger proved in 1928 a characterization theorem of all semimetric spaces that are isometrically embeddable in the *n*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$.[^o15][^o16] In 1931, Menger used distance relations to give an axiomatic treatment of Euclidean geometry.[^o17]

[Leonard Blumenthal](wikipedia://en/2354487)'s book[^o30] gives a general overview for distance geometry at the graduate level, a large part of which is treated in English for the first time when it was published.

## Menger characterization theorem {#Menger_characterization_theorem}

Menger proved the following [characterization theorem](wikipedia://en/780886) of semimetric spaces:[^o32]

> A semimetric space $(R, d)$ is isometrically embeddable in the $n$-dimensional Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$, but not in $\mathbb{R}^m$ for any $0 \le m < n$, if and only if:
>
> $R$ contains an $(n+1)$-point subset $S$ that is isometric with an affinely independent $(n+1)$-point subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$;
>
> any $(n+3)$-point subset $S'$, obtained by adding any two additional points of $R$ to $S$, is congruent to an $(n+3)$-point subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

A proof of this theorem in a slightly weakened form (for metric spaces instead of semimetric spaces) is in.[^o20]

:::

## Characterization via Cayley–Menger determinants {#Characterization_via_Cayley–Menger_determinants}

The following results are proved in Blumethal's book.[^o30]

### Embedding *n* + 1 points in the real numbers {#Embedding_n_+_1_points_in_the_real_numbers}

Given a semimetric space $(S,d)$ , with $S = \{P_0, \ldots, P_n\}$, and $d(P_i, P_j) = d_{ij}\ge 0$, $0 \le i < j \le n$, an isometric embedding of $(S, d)$ into $\mathbb{R}^n$ is defined by $A_0, A_1,\ldots, A_n \in \mathbb R^n$, such that $d(A_i, A_j) = d_{ij}$ for all $0 \le i < j \le n$.

Again, one asks whether such an isometric embedding exists for $(S,d)$.

A necessary condition is easy to see: for all $k = 1, \ldots, n$, let $v_k$ be the *k*-simplex formed by $A_0, A_1,\ldots, A_k$, then

$$(-1)^{k+1} \operatorname{CM}(P_0, \ldots, P_k) = (-1)^{k+1} \operatorname{CM}(A_0, \ldots, A_k) = 2^k (k!)^k \operatorname{Vol}_k(v_k)^2 \ge 0$$

The converse also holds. That is, if for all $k = 1, \ldots, n$,

$$(-1)^{k+1}\operatorname{CM}(P_0, \ldots, P_k) \ge 0,$$

then such an embedding exists.

Further, such embedding is unique up to isometry in $\mathbb{R}^n$. That is, given any two isometric embeddings defined by $A_0, A_1,\ldots, A_n$, and $A'_0, A'_1,\ldots, A'_n$, there exists a (not necessarily unique) isometry $T : \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^n$, such that $T(A_k) = A'_k$ for all $k = 0, \ldots, n$. Such $T$ is unique if and only if $\operatorname{CM}(P_0, \ldots, P_n) \neq 0$, that is, $A_0, A_1,\ldots, A_n$ are affinely independent.

### Embedding *n* + 2 and *n* + 3 points {#Embedding_n_+_2_and_n_+_3_points}

If $n+2$ points $P_0, \ldots, P_{n+1}$ can be embedded in $\mathbb{R}^n$ as $A_0, \ldots, A_{n+1}$, then other than the conditions above, an additional necessary condition is that the $(n+1)$-simplex formed by $A_0, A_1,\ldots, A_{n+1}$, must have no $(n+1)$-dimensional volume. That is, $\operatorname{CM}(P_0, \ldots, P_n, P_{n+1}) = 0$.

The converse also holds. That is, if for all $k = 1, \ldots, n$,

$$(-1)^{k+1} \operatorname{CM}(P_0, \ldots, P_k) \ge 0,$$

and

$$\operatorname{CM}(P_0, \ldots, P_n, P_{n+1}) = 0,$$

then such an embedding exists.

For embedding $n+3$ points in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the necessary and sufficient conditions are similar:

1. For all $k = 1, \ldots, n$, $(-1)^{k+1} \operatorname{CM}(P_0, \ldots, P_k) \ge 0$;
1. $$\operatorname{CM}(P_0, \ldots, P_n, P_{n+1}) = 0;$$
1. $$\operatorname{CM}(P_0, \ldots, P_n, P_{n+2}) = 0;$$
1. $$\operatorname{CM}(P_0, \ldots, P_n, P_{n+1}, P_{n+2}) = 0.$$

### Embedding arbitrarily many points {#Embedding_arbitrarily_many_points}

The $n+3$ case turns out to be sufficient in general.

In general, given a semimetric space $(R, d)$, it can be isometrically embedded in $\mathbb{R}^n$ if and only if there exists $P_0, \ldots, P_n\in R$, such that, for all $k = 1, \ldots, n$, $(-1)^{k+1} \operatorname{CM}(P_0, \ldots, P_k) \ge 0$, and for any $P_{n+1}, P_{n+2} \in R$,

1. $$\operatorname{CM}(P_0, \ldots, P_n, P_{n+1}) = 0;$$
1. $$\operatorname{CM}(P_0, \ldots, P_n, P_{n+2}) = 0;$$
1. $$\operatorname{CM}(P_0, \ldots, P_n, P_{n+1}, P_{n+2}) = 0.$$

And such embedding is unique up to isometry in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Further, if $\operatorname{CM}(P_0, \ldots, P_n) \neq 0$, then it cannot be isometrically embedded in any $\mathbb{R}^m, m < n$. And such embedding is unique up to unique isometry in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Thus, Cayley–Menger determinants give a concrete way to calculate whether a semimetric space can be embedded in $\mathbb{R}^n$, for some finite $n$, and if so, what is the minimal $n$.

## Applications {#Applications}

There are many applications of distance geometry.[^o33]

In telecommunication networks such as [GPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Positioning_System), the positions of some sensors are known (which are called anchors) and some of the distances between sensors are also known: the problem is to identify the positions for all sensors.[^o29] [Hyperbolic navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_navigation) is one pre-GPS technology that uses distance geometry for locating ships based on the time it takes for signals to reach anchors.

There are many applications in chemistry.[^o31][^o30] Techniques such as [NMR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_resonance) can measure distances between pairs of atoms of a given molecule, and the problem is to infer the 3-dimensional shape of the molecule from those distances.

Some software packages for applications are:

- [DGSOL](http://www.mcs.anl.gov/~more/dgsol/). Solves large distance geometry problems in [macromolecular modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_modelling).
- [Xplor-NIH](https://nmr.cit.nih.gov/xplor-nih/). Based on [X-PLOR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-PLOR), to determine the structure of molecules based on data from NMR experiments. It solves distance geometry problems with heuristic methods (such as [simulated annealing](wikipedia://en/172244)) and local search methods (such as [conjugate gradient minimization](wikipedia://en/1448821)).
- [TINKER](http://dasher.wustl.edu/tinker/). Molecular modeling and design. It can solve distance geometry problems.
- [SNLSDPclique](https://sites.google.com/site/nathankrislock/software). [MATLAB](wikipedia://en/20412) code for locating sensors in a sensor network based on the distances between the sensors.
