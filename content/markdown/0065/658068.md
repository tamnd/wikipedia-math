# Infinitesimal transformation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Infinitesimal transformation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **infinitesimal transformation** is a [limiting](wikipedia://en/26551602) form of *small* [transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_%28geometry%29). For example one may talk about an [infinitesimal rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal_rotation) of a [rigid body](wikipedia://en/455769), in three-dimensional space. This is conventionally represented by a 3×3 [skew-symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/174055) *A*. It is not the matrix of an actual [rotation](wikipedia://en/39789) in space; but for small real values of a parameter ε the transformation

:::

$$T=I+\varepsilon A$$

is a small rotation, up to quantities of order ε<sup>2</sup>.

## History {#History}

A comprehensive theory of infinitesimal transformations was first given by [Sophus Lie](wikipedia://en/54010). This was at the heart of his work, on what are now called [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945) and their accompanying [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944); and the identification of their role in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) and especially the theory of [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309). The properties of an abstract [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) are exactly those definitive of infinitesimal transformations, just as the axioms of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) embody [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741). The term "Lie algebra" was introduced in 1934 by [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544), for what had until then been known as the *algebra of infinitesimal transformations* of a Lie group.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

For example, in the case of infinitesimal rotations, the Lie algebra structure is that provided by the [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092), once a skew-symmetric matrix has been identified with a 3-[vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29). This amounts to choosing an axis vector for the rotations; the defining [Jacobi identity](wikipedia://en/294370) is a well-known property of cross products.

The earliest example of an infinitesimal transformation that may have been recognised as such was in [Euler's theorem on homogeneous functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_theorem_on_homogeneous_functions). Here it is stated that a function *F* of *n* variables *x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub> that is homogeneous of degree *r*, satisfies

$$\Theta F=rF \,$$

with

$$\Theta=\sum_i x_i{\partial\over\partial x_i},$$

the [Theta operator](wikipedia://en/38541578). That is, from the property

$$F(\lambda x_1,\dots, \lambda x_n)=\lambda^r F(x_1,\dots,x_n)\,$$

it is possible to differentiate with respect to λ and then set λ equal to 1. This then becomes a [necessary condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_condition) on a [smooth function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) *F* to have the homogeneity property; it is also sufficient (by using [Schwartz distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwartz_distribution) one can reduce the [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) considerations here). This setting is typical, in that there is a [one-parameter group](wikipedia://en/754851) of [scalings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaling_%28mathematics%29) operating; and the information is coded in an infinitesimal transformation that is a [first-order differential operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-order_differential_operator).

## Operator version of Taylor's theorem {#Operator_version_of_Taylor's_theorem}

The operator equation

$$e^{tD}f(x)=f(x+t)\,$$

where

$$D={d\over dx}$$

is an [operator](wikipedia://en/22213) version of [Taylor's theorem](wikipedia://en/51714) — and is therefore only valid under *caveats* about *f* being an [analytic function](wikipedia://en/61478). Concentrating on the operator part, it shows that *D* is an infinitesimal transformation, generating translations of the real line via the [exponential](wikipedia://en/9678). In Lie's theory, this is generalised a long way. Any [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) Lie group can be built up by means of its [infinitesimal generators](wikipedia://en/17945#The_Lie_algebra_associated_with_a_Lie_group) (a basis for the Lie algebra of the group); with explicit if not always useful information given in the [Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula](wikipedia://en/285422).
