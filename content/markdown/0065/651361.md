# Curvature of Riemannian manifolds

*Image: Gaussian curvature.svg.* From left to right: a surface of negative [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) ([hyperboloid](wikipedia://en/145844)), a surface of zero Gaussian curvature ([cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29)), and a surface of positive Gaussian curvature ([sphere](wikipedia://en/27859)). In higher dimensions, a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) may have different curvatures in different directions, described by the [Riemann curvature tensor](wikipedia://en/130526).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Curvature of Riemannian manifolds"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), the [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) geometry of [Riemannian manifolds](wikipedia://en/144652) with [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) greater than 2 is too complicated to be described by a single number at a given point. [Riemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann) introduced an abstract and rigorous way to define curvature for these manifolds, now known as the *[Riemann curvature tensor](wikipedia://en/130526)*. Similar notions have found applications everywhere in [differential geometry of surfaces](wikipedia://en/15513875) and other objects. The [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) of a [pseudo-Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/287555) can be expressed in the same way with only slight modifications.

:::

## Ways to express the curvature of a Riemannian manifold {#Ways_to_express_the_curvature_of_a_Riemannian_manifold}

### Riemann curvature tensor {#Riemann_curvature_tensor}

The curvature of a Riemannian manifold can be described in various ways; the most standard one is the curvature tensor, given in terms of a [Levi-Civita connection](wikipedia://en/249176) (or [covariant differentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariant_differentiation)) $\nabla$ and [Lie bracket](wikipedia://en/294437) $[\cdot,\cdot]$ by the following formula:

$$R(u,v)w=\nabla_u\nabla_v w - \nabla_v \nabla_u w -\nabla_{[u,v]} w .$$

Here $R(u,v)$ is a linear transformation of the tangent space of the manifold; it is linear in each argument. If $u=\partial/\partial x_i$ and $v=\partial/\partial x_j$ are coordinate vector fields then $[u,v]=0$ and therefore the formula simplifies to

$$R(u,v)w=\nabla_u\nabla_v w - \nabla_v \nabla_u w ,$$

i.e. the curvature tensor measures *noncommutativity of the covariant derivative*.

The linear transformation $w\mapsto R(u,v)w$ is also called the **curvature transformation** or **endomorphism**.

*N.B.* There are a few books where the curvature tensor is defined with opposite sign.

#### Symmetries and identities {#Symmetries_and_identities}

The curvature tensor has the following symmetries:

$$R(u,v)=-R(v,u)^{}_{}$$

$$\langle R(u,v)w,z \rangle=-\langle R(u,v)z,w \rangle$$

$$R(u,v)w+R(v,w)u+R(w,u)v=0$$

The last identity was discovered by [Ricci](wikipedia://en/499223), but is often called the *first Bianchi identity*, just because it looks similar to the Bianchi identity below. The first two should be addressed as *antisymmetry* and *Lie algebra property* respectively, since the second means that the $R(u, v)$ for all $u, v$ are elements of the pseudo-orthogonal Lie algebra. All three together should be named *pseudo-orthogonal curvature structure*. They give rise to a *tensor* only by identifications with objects of the tensor algebra – but likewise there are identifications with concepts in the Clifford-algebra. Let us note that these three axioms of a curvature structure give rise to a well-developed structure theory, formulated in terms of projectors (a Weyl projector, giving rise to *Weyl curvature* and an Einstein projector, needed for the setup of the Einsteinian gravitational equations). This structure theory is compatible with the action of the pseudo-orthogonal groups plus [dilations](wikipedia://en/1591770). It has strong ties with the theory of [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945) and algebras, Lie triples and Jordan algebras. See the references given in the discussion.

The three identities form a complete list of symmetries of the curvature tensor, i.e. given any tensor that satisfies the identities above, one could find a Riemannian manifold with such a curvature tensor at some point. Simple calculations show that such a tensor has $n^2(n^2-1)/12$ independent components. Yet another useful identity follows from these three:

$$\langle R(u,v)w,z \rangle=\langle R(w,z)u,v \rangle$$

The **Bianchi identity** (often the **second Bianchi identity**) involves the covariant derivatives:

$$\nabla_uR(v,w)+\nabla_vR(w,u)+\nabla_w R(u,v)=0$$

### Sectional curvature {#Sectional_curvature}

Sectional curvature is a further, equivalent but more geometrical, description of the curvature of Riemannian manifolds. It is a function $K(\sigma)$ that depends on a section $\sigma$ (i.e. a 2-plane in the tangent spaces). It is the [Gauss curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) of the $\sigma$-*section* at $p$; here $\sigma$-section is a locally defined piece of surface that has the plane $\sigma$ as a tangent plane at $p$, obtained from geodesics that start at $p$ in the directions of the image of $\sigma$ under the [exponential map](wikipedia://en/276548) at $p$.

If $v,u$ are two linearly independent vectors in $\sigma$ then

$$K(\sigma)= K(u,v)/|u\wedge v|^2\text{ where }K(u,v)=\langle R(u,v)v,u \rangle .$$

The following formula indicates that sectional curvature describes the curvature tensor completely:

$$6\langle R(u,v)w,z \rangle =$$

$$[K(u+z,v+w)-K(u+z,v)-K(u+z,w)-K(u,v+w)-K(z,v+w)+K(u,w)+K(v,z)] -$$

$$[K(u+w,v+z)-K(u+w,v)-K(u+w,z)-K(u,v+z)-K(w,v+z)+K(v,w)+K(u,z)] .$$

Or in a simpler formula:

$$\langle R(u,v)w,z\rangle=\frac 16 \left.\frac{\partial^2}{\partial s\partial t} \left(K(u+sz,v+tw)-K(u+sw,v+tz)\right)\right|_{(s,t)=(0,0)}$$

### Curvature form {#Curvature_form}

The [connection form](wikipedia://en/706412) gives an alternative way to describe curvature. It is used more for general [vector bundles](wikipedia://en/276524), and for [principal bundles](wikipedia://en/310950), but it works just as well for the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) with the [Levi-Civita connection](wikipedia://en/249176). The curvature of an $n$-dimensional Riemannian manifold is given by an [antisymmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisymmetric_matrix) $n \times n$ matrix $\Omega = \Omega^i{{(}}{{)}}_j$ of [2-forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-form) (or equivalently a 2-form with values in $\mathfrak{{(}}so{{)}}(n)$, the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) of the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) $\operatorname{{(}}O{{)}}(n)$, which is the [structure group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_group) of the tangent bundle of a Riemannian manifold).

Let $e_i$ be a local section of orthonormal bases. Then one can define the connection form, an antisymmetric matrix of 1-forms $\omega=\omega^i_{{(}}\ j{{)}}$ that satisfy from the following identity

$$\omega^k{}_j(e_i)=\langle \nabla_{e_i}e_j,e_k\rangle$$

Then the curvature form $\Omega=\Omega^i{{(}}{{)}}_j$ is defined by

$$\Omega = d\omega + \omega\wedge\omega .$$

Note that the expression "$\omega\wedge\omega$" is shorthand for $\omega^i_{{(}}\ j{{)}}\wedge\omega^j_{{(}}\ k{{)}}$ and hence does not necessarily vanish. The following describes relation between curvature form and curvature tensor:

$$R(u,v)w=\Omega(u\wedge v)w .$$

This approach builds in all symmetries of curvature tensor except the *first Bianchi identity*, which takes form

$$\Omega\wedge\theta=0$$

where $\theta=\theta^i$ is an $n$-vector of 1-forms defined by $\theta^i(v)=\langle e_i,v\rangle$. The *second Bianchi identity* takes form

$$D\Omega=0 ,$$

where $D$ denotes the [exterior covariant derivative](wikipedia://en/849738).

### Curvature operator {#Curvature_operator}

It is sometimes convenient to think about curvature as an [operator](wikipedia://en/22213) $Q$ on tangent [bivectors](wikipedia://en/103109) (elements of $\Lambda^2(T)$), which is uniquely defined by the following identity:

$$\langle Q (u\wedge v),w\wedge z\rangle=\langle R(u,v)z,w \rangle.$$

It is possible to do this precisely because of the symmetries of the curvature tensor (namely antisymmetry in the first and last pairs of indices, and block-symmetry of those pairs).

## Further curvature tensors {#Further_curvature_tensors}

In general the following tensors and functions do not describe the curvature tensor completely; however they play an important role.

### Scalar curvature {#Scalar_curvature}

Scalar curvature is a function on any Riemannian manifold, denoted variously by $S, R$ or $\text{{(}}Sc{{)}}$. It is the full [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) of the curvature tensor; given an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) $\{e_i\}$ in the tangent space at a point we have

$$S =\sum_{i,j}\langle R(e_i,e_j)e_j,e_i\rangle=\sum_{i}\langle \text{Ric}(e_i),e_i\rangle,$$

where $\mathrm{{(}}Ric{{)}}$ denotes the [Ricci tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_tensor). The result does not depend on the choice of orthonormal basis. Starting with dimension 3, scalar curvature does not describe the curvature tensor completely.

### Ricci curvature {#Ricci_curvature}

Ricci curvature is a linear operator on tangent space at a point, usually denoted by $\mathrm{{(}}Ric{{)}}$. Given an orthonormal basis $\{e_i\}$ in the tangent space at $p$ we have

$$\mathrm{Ric}(u)=\sum_{i} R(u,e_i)e_i .$$

The result does not depend on the choice of orthonormal basis. With four or more dimensions, Ricci curvature does not describe the curvature tensor completely.

Explicit expressions for the [Ricci tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_tensor) in terms of the [Levi-Civita connection](wikipedia://en/249176) is given in the article on [Christoffel symbols](wikipedia://en/1401020).

### Weyl curvature tensor {#Weyl_curvature_tensor}

The **Weyl curvature tensor** has the same symmetries as the Riemann curvature tensor, but with one extra constraint: its trace (as used to define the Ricci curvature) must vanish.

The [Weyl tensor](wikipedia://en/1026848) is invariant with respect to a [conformal](wikipedia://en/50627) change of metric: if two metrics are related as $\tilde{{(}}g{{)}} = f g$ for some positive scalar function $f$, then $\tilde{W} = W$.

In dimensions 2 and 3 the Weyl tensor vanishes, but in 4 or more dimensions the Weyl tensor can be non-zero. For a manifold of [constant curvature](wikipedia://en/3638803), the Weyl tensor is zero. Moreover, $W = 0$ if and only if the metric is locally conformal to the [Euclidean metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_metric).

### Ricci decomposition {#Ricci_decomposition}

Although individually, the Weyl tensor and Ricci tensor do not in general determine the full curvature tensor, the Riemann curvature tensor can be decomposed into a Weyl part and a Ricci part. This decomposition is known as the Ricci decomposition, and plays an important role in the [conformal geometry](wikipedia://en/295907) of Riemannian manifolds. In particular, it can be used to show that if the metric is rescaled by a conformal factor of $e^{{(}}2f{{)}}$, then the Riemann curvature tensor changes to (seen as a (0, 4)-tensor):

$$e^{2f}\left(R+\left(\text{Hess}(f)-df\otimes df+\frac{1}{2}\|\text{grad}(f)\|^2 g\right) {~\wedge\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\;\bigcirc~} g\right) ,$$

where ${~\wedge\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\;\bigcirc~}$denotes the [Kulkarni–Nomizu product](wikipedia://en/4322202) and Hess is the Hessian.

## Calculation of curvature {#Calculation_of_curvature}

For calculation of curvature

- of hypersurfaces and submanifolds see *[Second fundamental form](wikipedia://en/652078)*
- in coordinates see *[List of formulas in Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/5783569)* or *[Covariant derivative](wikipedia://en/431848)*
- by moving frames see *[Cartan connection](wikipedia://en/412272)* and *[Curvature form](wikipedia://en/489440).*
- the [Jacobi equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_equation) can help if one knows something about the behaviour of [geodesics](wikipedia://en/91096#Riemannian_and_pseudo-Riemannian_manifolds).
