# P (complexity)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="P"}

In [computational complexity theory](wikipedia://en/7543), **P**, also known as **PTIME** or **[DTIME](wikipedia://en/658538)**(*n*<sup>O(1)</sup>), is a fundamental [complexity class](wikipedia://en/502426). It contains all [decision problems](wikipedia://en/8336) that can be solved by a [deterministic Turing machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic_Turing_machine) using a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) amount of [computation time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computation_time), or [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time).

:::

[Cobham's thesis](wikipedia://en/3262179) holds that P is the class of computational problems that are "efficiently solvable" or "[tractable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tractable_problem)". This is inexact: in practice, some problems not known to be in P have practical solutions, and some that are in P do not, but this is a useful [rule of thumb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_thumb).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A [language](wikipedia://en/10939) *L* is in P if and only if there exists a deterministic Turing machine *M*, such that

- *M* runs for polynomial time on all inputs
- For all *x* in *L*, *M* outputs 1
- For all *x* not in *L*, *M* outputs 0

P can also be viewed as a uniform family of [Boolean circuits](wikipedia://en/7087423). A language *L* is in P if and only if there exists a [polynomial-time uniform](wikipedia://en/7641969#Polynomial-time_uniform) family of Boolean circuits $\{C_n:n \in \mathbb{N}\}$, such that

- For all $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $C_n$ takes *n* bits as input and outputs 1 bit
- For all *x* in *L*, $C_{|x|}(x)=1$
- For all *x* not in *L*, $C_{|x|}(x)=0$

The circuit definition can be weakened to use only a [logspace uniform](wikipedia://en/7641969#Logspace_uniform) family without changing the complexity class.

:::

## Notable problems in P {#Notable_problems_in_P}

P is known to contain many natural problems, including the decision versions of [linear programming](wikipedia://en/43730), and finding a [maximum matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_matching). In 2002, it was shown that the problem of determining if a number is [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) is in P.[^n1] The related class of [function problems](wikipedia://en/663345) is [FP](wikipedia://en/663347).

Several natural problems are complete for P, including [*st*-connectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St-connectivity) (or [reachability](wikipedia://en/2833097)) on alternating graphs [^n2] The article on [P-complete problems](wikipedia://en/54683) lists further relevant problems in P.

## Relationships to other classes {#Relationships_to_other_classes}

*Image: Complexity subsets pspace.svg.* A representation of the relation among complexity classes

*Image: Complexity-classes-polynomial.svg.* Inclusions of complexity classes including P, [NP](wikipedia://en/21562), [co-NP](wikipedia://en/6184), [BPP](wikipedia://en/4079), [P/poly](wikipedia://en/2052415), [PH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH_%28complexity%29), and [PSPACE](wikipedia://en/39351)

A generalization of P is [NP](wikipedia://en/21562), which is the class of [decision problems](wikipedia://en/8336) decidable by a [non-deterministic Turing machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-deterministic_Turing_machine) that runs in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time). Equivalently, it is the class of decision problems where each "yes" instance has a polynomial size certificate, and certificates can be checked by a polynomial time deterministic Turing machine. The class of problems for which this is true for the "no" instances is called [co-NP](wikipedia://en/6184). P is trivially a subset of NP and of co-NP; most experts believe it is a proper subset,[^n3] although this belief (the $\mathsf{P} \subsetneq \mathsf{NP}$ hypothesis) [remains unproven](wikipedia://en/6115). Another open problem is whether NP = co-NP; since P = co-P,[^n4] a negative answer would imply $\mathsf{P} \subsetneq \mathsf{NP}$.

P is also known to be at least as large as [L](wikipedia://en/1149999), the class of problems decidable in a [logarithmic](wikipedia://en/17860) amount of [memory space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_space_%28computational_resource%29). A decider using $O(\log n)$ space cannot use more than $2^{O(\log n)} = n^{O(1)}$ time, because this is the total number of possible configurations; thus, L is a subset of P. Another important problem is whether L = P. We do know that P = AL, the set of problems solvable in logarithmic memory by [alternating Turing machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_Turing_machine). P is also known to be no larger than [PSPACE](wikipedia://en/39351), the class of problems decidable in polynomial space. PSPACE is equivalent to NPSPACE by [Savitch's theorem](wikipedia://en/658501). Again, whether P = PSPACE is an open problem. To summarize:

$$\mathsf{L} \subseteq \mathsf{AL} = \mathsf{P} \subseteq \mathsf{NP} \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE} = \mathsf{NPSPACE} \subseteq \mathsf{EXPTIME}.$$

Here, [EXPTIME](wikipedia://en/54694) is the class of problems solvable in exponential time. Of all the classes shown above, only two strict containments are known:

- P is strictly contained in EXPTIME. Consequently, all EXPTIME-hard problems lie outside P, and at least one of the containments to the right of P above is strict (in fact, it is widely believed that all three are strict).
- L is strictly contained in PSPACE.

The most difficult problems in P are [P-complete](wikipedia://en/54683) problems.

Another generalization of P is [P/poly](wikipedia://en/2052415), or Nonuniform Polynomial-Time. If a problem is in P/poly, then it can be solved in deterministic polynomial time provided that an [advice string](wikipedia://en/665843) is given that depends only on the length of the input. Unlike for NP, however, the polynomial-time machine doesn't need to detect fraudulent advice strings; it is not a verifier. P/poly is a large class containing nearly all practical problems, including all of [BPP](wikipedia://en/4079). If it contains NP, then the [polynomial hierarchy](wikipedia://en/658651) collapses to the second level. On the other hand, it also contains some impractical problems, including some [undecidable problems](wikipedia://en/15631055) such as the unary version of any undecidable problem.

In 1999, [Jin-Yi Cai](wikipedia://en/68266753) and D. Sivakumar, building on work by [Mitsunori Ogihara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsunori_Ogihara), showed that if there exists a [sparse language](wikipedia://en/12769596) that is P-complete, then L = P.[^n5]

*Image: Randomised Complexity Classes 2.svg.* P in relation to probabilistic complexity classes ([ZPP](wikipedia://en/54772), [RP](wikipedia://en/54771), co-RP, [BPP](wikipedia://en/4079), [BQP](wikipedia://en/4080), [PP](wikipedia://en/659322)), all within [PSPACE](wikipedia://en/39351). It is unknown if any of these containments are strict.

P is contained in [BQP](wikipedia://en/4080); it is unknown whether this containment is strict.

## Properties {#Properties}

Polynomial-time algorithms are closed under composition. Intuitively, this says that if one writes a function that is polynomial-time assuming that function calls are constant-time, and if those called functions themselves require polynomial time, then the entire algorithm takes polynomial time. One consequence of this is that P is [low](wikipedia://en/2006468) for itself. This is also one of the main reasons that P is considered to be a machine-independent class; any machine "feature", such as [random access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_access), that can be simulated in polynomial time can simply be composed with the main polynomial-time algorithm to reduce it to a polynomial-time algorithm on a more basic machine.

Languages in P are also closed under reversal, [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429), [union](wikipedia://en/47949), [concatenation](wikipedia://en/64474), [Kleene closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleene_closure), inverse [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564), and [complementation](wikipedia://en/1929955).[^n6]

## Pure existence proofs of polynomial-time algorithms {#Pure_existence_proofs_of_polynomial-time_algorithms}

Some problems are known to be solvable in polynomial time, but no concrete algorithm is known for solving them. For example, the [Robertson–Seymour theorem](wikipedia://en/351769) guarantees that there is a finite list of [forbidden minors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forbidden_minor) that characterizes (for example) the set of graphs that can be embedded on a torus; moreover, Robertson and Seymour showed that there is an O(*n*<sup>3</sup>) algorithm for determining whether a graph has a given graph as a minor. This yields a [nonconstructive proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonconstructive_proof) that there is a polynomial-time algorithm for determining if a given graph can be embedded on a torus, despite the fact that no concrete algorithm is known for this problem.

## Alternative characterizations {#Alternative_characterizations}

In [descriptive complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descriptive_complexity), P can be described as the problems expressible in [FO(LFP)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FO%28LFP%29), the [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) with a [least fixed point](wikipedia://en/1211913) operator added to it, on ordered structures. In Immerman's 1999 textbook on descriptive complexity,[^n7] [Immerman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Immerman) ascribes this result to [Vardi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moshe_Vardi)[^n8] and to Immerman 1982.[^n9]

In 1992 an alternative characterization of [FP](wikipedia://en/663347)[^o18] was given by Bellantoni and [Cook](wikipedia://en/39432), who define polynomial-time computable functions using a safe recursion scheme, providing a machine-independent, structural definition within the framework of [implicit computational complexity](wikipedia://en/53458309).[^n10]

It was published in 2001 that PTIME corresponds to (positive) [range concatenation grammars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Range_concatenation_grammars).

P can also be defined as an algorithmic complexity class for problems that are not [decision problems](wikipedia://en/8336)[^n11] (even though, for example, finding the solution to a [2-satisfiability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-satisfiability) instance in polynomial time automatically gives a polynomial algorithm for the corresponding decision problem). In that case P is not a subset of NP, but $P \cap DEC$ is, where $DEC$ is the class of decision problems.

## History {#History}

[Kozen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dexter_Kozen)[^n12] states that [Cobham](wikipedia://en/68162815) and [Edmonds](wikipedia://en/3625459) are "generally credited with the invention of the notion of polynomial time," though [Rabin](wikipedia://en/298404) also invented the notion independently and around the same time (Rabin's paper[^n13] was in a 1967 proceedings of a 1966 conference, while Cobham's[^n14] was in a 1965 proceedings of a 1964 conference and Edmonds's[^n15] was published in a journal in 1965, though Rabin makes no mention of either and was apparently unaware of them). Cobham invented the class as a robust way of characterizing efficient algorithms, leading to [Cobham's thesis](wikipedia://en/3262179). However, [H. C. Pocklington](wikipedia://en/9384084), in a 1910 paper,[^n16][^n17] analyzed two algorithms for solving quadratic congruences, and observed that one took time "proportional to a power of the logarithm of the modulus" and contrasted this with one that took time proportional "to the modulus itself or its square root", thus explicitly drawing a distinction between an algorithm that ran in polynomial time versus one that ran in (moderately) exponential time.
