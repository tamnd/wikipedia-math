# Material conditional

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Material conditional"}

The **material conditional** (also known as **material implication**) is a [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) commonly used in [logic](wikipedia://en/19636). When the conditional symbol $\to$ is [interpreted](wikipedia://en/14511671) as material implication, a formula $P \to Q$ is true unless $P$ is true and $Q$ is false.

:::

Material implication is used in all the basic systems of [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868) as well as some [nonclassical logics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonclassical_logic). It is assumed as a model of correct conditional reasoning within mathematics and serves as the basis for commands in many [programming languages](wikipedia://en/23015). However, many logics replace material implication with other operators such as the [strict conditional](wikipedia://en/663772) and the [variably strict conditional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variably_strict_conditional). Due to the [paradoxes of material implication](wikipedia://en/12857474) and related problems, material implication is not generally considered a viable analysis of [conditional sentences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_sentence) in [natural language](wikipedia://en/21173).

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

## Notation {#Notation}

In logic and related fields, the material conditional is customarily notated with an infix operator $\to$ (2192).[^n1] The material conditional is also notated using the infixes $\supset$[^o6] and $\Rightarrow$ (2283 and 21D2 respectively).[^n2] In the prefixed [Polish notation](wikipedia://en/25056), conditionals are notated as $Cpq$. In a conditional formula $p\to q$, the subformula $p$ is referred to as the *[antecedent](wikipedia://en/804755)* and $q$ is termed the *[consequent](wikipedia://en/804778)* of the conditional. Conditional statements may be nested such that the antecedent or the consequent may themselves be conditional statements, as in the formula $(p\to q)\to(r\to s)$.

:::

## History {#History}

In *[Arithmetices Principia: Nova Methodo Exposita](wikipedia://en/51988929)* (1889), [Peano](wikipedia://en/13043) expressed the proposition "If $A$, then $B$" as $A$ Ɔ $B$ with the symbol Ɔ, which is the opposite of C.[^n3] He also expressed the proposition $A\supset B$ as $A$ Ɔ $B$.[^o10][^n4] [Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) expressed the proposition "If *A*, then *B*" as $A\to B$ in 1918.[^n1] [Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) followed Peano in his *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)* (1910–1913), in which he expressed the proposition "If *A*, then *B*" as $A\supset B$. Following Russell, [Gentzen](wikipedia://en/46027) expressed the proposition "If *A*, then *B*" as $A\supset B$. [Heyting](wikipedia://en/1180911) expressed the proposition "If *A*, then *B*" as $A\supset B$ at first but later came to express it as $A\to B$ with a right-pointing arrow. [Bourbaki](wikipedia://en/167394) expressed the proposition "If *A*, then *B*" as $A \Rightarrow B$ in 1954.[^n5][^o14]

## Semantics {#Semantics}

### Truth table {#Truth_table}

From a [classical](wikipedia://en/55868) [semantic perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantics_of_logic), material implication is the [binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_operator) [truth functional](wikipedia://en/604707) operator which returns "true" unless its first argument is true and its second argument is false. This semantics can be shown graphically in the following [truth table](wikipedia://en/25512250): 1 One can also consider the equivalence $A \to B \equiv \neg (A \land \neg B) \equiv \neg A \lor B$.

The conditionals $(A \to B)$ where the antecedent $A$ is false, are called "[vacuous truths](wikipedia://en/51672)". Examples are ...

- ... with $B$ false: *"If [Marie Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Curie) is a sister of [Galileo Galilei](wikipedia://en/29688374), then Galileo Galilei is a brother of Marie Curie."*
- ... with $B$ true: *"If Marie Curie is a sister of Galileo Galilei, then Marie Curie has a sibling."*

### Analytic tableaux {#Analytic_tableaux}

Formulas over the set of connectives $\{\to, \bot\}$[^o18] are called **f-implicational**.[^n6] In [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868) the other connectives, such as $\neg$ ([negation](wikipedia://en/161019)), $\land$ ([conjunction](wikipedia://en/18152)), $\lor$ ([disjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjunction)) and $\leftrightarrow$ ([equivalence](wikipedia://en/14922)), can be defined in terms of $\to$ and $\bot$ ([falsity](wikipedia://en/229823#False,_negation_and_contradiction)):[^o20]

$$\begin{aligned} \neg A & \quad \overset{\text{def}}{=} \quad A \to \bot \\ A \land B & \quad \overset{\text{def}}{=} \quad (A \to (B \to \bot)) \to \bot \\ A \lor B & \quad \overset{\text{def}}{=} \quad (A \to \bot) \to B \\ A \leftrightarrow B & \quad \overset{\text{def}}{=} \quad \{(A \to B) \to [(B \to A) \to \bot]\} \to \bot \\ \end{aligned}$$

The validity of f-implicational formulas can be semantically established by the [method of analytic tableaux](wikipedia://en/1027229). The logical rules are

{| style="border: none; border-spacing: 1px; border-collapse: separate;"

\|- | style="vertical-align: top;" | $\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}(A \to B)}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}(A) \quad \mid \quad \boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}(B)}$ || valign="top" | $\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}(A \to B)}{\begin{array}{c} \boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}(A) \\ \boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}(B)\end{array}}$ |- |colspan="2" | $\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}(\bot)$ : Close the branch (contradiction) $\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}(\bot)$ : Do nothing (since it just asserts no contradiction) |}

::: {#o21 .proof cue=collapse name="Example: proof of p \\to \\neg \\neg p\\quad, by method of analytic tableaux"}

```
         F[p → ((p → ⊥) → ⊥)]
          |
         T[p]
         F[(p → ⊥) → ⊥]
          |
         T[p → ⊥]
         F[⊥]
 ┌────────┴────────┐
F[p]              T[⊥]
 |                 |
CONTRADICTION     CONTRADICTION
(T[p], F[p])      (⊥ is true)
```

:::

## Syntactical properties {#Syntactical_properties}

The semantic definition by truth tables does not permit the examination of structurally identical propositional forms in various [logical systems](wikipedia://en/396102), where different properties may be demonstrated. The language considered here is restricted to **f-implicational formulas**.

Consider the following (candidate) [natural deduction](wikipedia://en/51072) rules.

::: {#o25 .table cue=table}

| **Implication Introduction** ($\to$I) If assuming $A$ one can derive $B$, then one can conclude $A \to B$. $\frac{\begin{array}{c} [A] \\ \vdots \\ B \end{array}}{A \to B}$ ($\to$I) $[A]$ is an assumption that is discharged when applying the rule. | **Implication Elimination** ($\to$E) This rule corresponds to [modus ponens](wikipedia://en/18900). $\frac{A \to B \quad A}{B}$ ($\to$E) $\frac{A \quad A \to B}{B}$ ($\to$E) |
|---|---|
| **[Double Negation Elimination](wikipedia://en/379833)** ($\neg\neg$E) $\frac{\begin{array}{c} (A \to \bot) \to \bot \\ \end{array}}{A}$ ($\neg\neg$E) | **Falsum Elimination** ($\bot$E) From falsum ($\bot$) one can derive any formula. (ex falso quodlibet) $\frac{\bot}{A}$ ($\bot$E) |

:::

- **[Minimal logic](wikipedia://en/26267678)**: By limiting the [natural deduction](wikipedia://en/51072) rules to *Implication Introduction* ($\to$I) and *Implication Elimination* ($\to$E), one obtains (the implicational fragment of)[^o20] minimal logic (as defined by [Johansson](wikipedia://en/26269027)).[^n7]

::: {#o28 .proof cue=collapse name="Proof of P \\to \\neg \\neg P\\quad, within minimal logic"}

{| |1.1 |\[ P \] |1// Assume |- |2.1 |\[ P → ⊥ \] |1// Assume |- |3.1 |⊥ |1// $\to$E (1, 2) |- |4.1 |(P → ⊥) → ⊥) |1// $\to$I (2, 3), discharging 2 |- |5.1 |P → ((P → ⊥) → ⊥) |1// $\to$I (1, 4), discharging 1 |}

:::

- **[Intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262)**: By adding *Falsum Elimination* ($\bot$E) as a rule, one obtains (the implicational fragment of)[^o20] intuitionistic logic.

The statement $P \to \neg \neg P$ is valid (already in minimal logic), unlike the reverse implication which would entail the [law of excluded middle](wikipedia://en/17637).

- **[Classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868)**: If *[Double Negation Elimination](wikipedia://en/379833)* ($\neg\neg$E) is also permitted, the system defines (full!) classical logic.[^n8][^n9][^n10]

## A selection of theorems (classical logic) {#A_selection_of_theorems_(classical_logic)}

In [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868) material implication validates the following:

{| |1.1 |\[ (P → Q) → P \] |1// Assume (to discharge at 11) |- |2.1 |\[ P → ⊥ \] |1// Assume (to discharge at 9) |- |3.1 |\[ P \] |1// Assume (to discharge at 6) |- |4.1 |⊥ |1// $\to$E (2, 3) |- |5.1 |Q |1// $\bot$E (4) |- |6.1 |P → Q |1// $\to$I (3, 5) (discharging 3) |- |7.1 |P |1// $\to$E (1, 6) |- |8.1 |⊥ |1// $\to$E (2, 7) |- |9.1 |(P → ⊥) → ⊥ |1// $\to$I (2, 8) (discharging 2) |- |10.1 |P |1// $\neg \neg$E (9) |- |11.1 |((P → Q) → P) → P |1// $\to$I (1, 10) (discharging 1) |}

{| |1.1 |$[ P \to \bot ]$ |1// Assume |- |2.1 |$[ P ]$ |1// Assume |- |3.1 | $\bot$ |1// $\to$E (1, 2) |- |4.1 |$Q$ |1// $\bot$E (3) |- |5.1 |$P \to Q$ |1// $\to$I (2, 4) (discharging 2) |- |6.1 |$( P \to \bot ) \to ( P \to Q )$ |1// $\to$I (1, 5) (discharging 1) |}

- [Import-export](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Import-Export_%28logic%29): $P \to (Q \to R) \equiv (P \land Q) \to R$
- Negated conditionals: $\neg(P \to Q) \equiv P \land \neg Q$
- Or-and-if: $P \to Q \equiv \neg P \lor Q$
- Commutativity of antecedents: $\big(P \to (Q \to R)\big) \equiv \big(Q \to (P \to R)\big)$
- [Left distributivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_distributivity): $\big(R \to (P \to Q)\big) \equiv \big((R \to P) \to (R \to Q)\big)$

Similarly, on classical interpretations of the other connectives, material implication validates the following [entailments](wikipedia://en/37531624#Semantic_consequence):

- Antecedent strengthening: $P \to Q \models (P \land R) \to Q$
- [Transitivity](wikipedia://en/200463): $(P \to Q) \land (Q \to R) \models P \to R$
- [Simplification of disjunctive antecedents](wikipedia://en/66659052): $(P \lor Q) \to R \models (P \to R) \land (Q \to R)$

[Tautologies](wikipedia://en/4495335) involving material implication include:

- [Reflexivity](wikipedia://en/200458): $\models P \to P$
- [Totality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connex_relation): $\models (P \to Q) \lor (Q \to P)$
- [Conditional excluded middle](wikipedia://en/17637): $\models (P \to Q) \lor (P \to \neg Q)$

## The relationship between the material conditional and logical consequence {#The_relationship_between_the_material_conditional_and_logical_consequence}

The material conditional is a sentential connective within a formal language. It should not be confused with the relation of [logical consequence](wikipedia://en/37531624) (also called logical implication or entailment), which is standardly treated as a relation between sentences expressed in a [metalanguage](wikipedia://en/402929).

The relationship between the material conditional and the logical consequence relation is given by the [deduction theorem](wikipedia://en/480010).

$\Gamma \cup \{A\} \vdash B \;$ if and only if $\; \Gamma \vdash A \to B$

This can be read as stating that the set of sentences $\Gamma$ together with *A* logically implies *B* if and only if $\Gamma$ logically implies the material conditional $A \to B$.

In the special case where $\Gamma$ is empty, this reduces to:

$A \vdash B \;$ if and only if $\; \vdash A \to B$

This states *A* logically implies *B* if and only if the material conditional $A \to B$ is a theorem of the logic.

Many textbooks reserve the term logical consequence (or logical implication) for the [semantic consequence](wikipedia://en/37531624#Semantic_consequence) relation with the symbol $\models$.[^n11][^n12] In which case the relation becomes

$A \models B \;$ if and only if $\; \models A \to B$

*A* logically implies *B* if and only if the material conditional $A \to B$ is a [tautology](wikipedia://en/4495335).

## Discrepancies with natural language {#Discrepancies_with_natural_language}

Material implication does not closely match the usage of [conditional sentences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_sentence) in [natural language](wikipedia://en/21173). For example, even though material conditionals with false antecedents are [vacuously true](wikipedia://en/51672), the natural language statement "If 8 is odd, then 3 is prime" is typically judged false. Similarly, any material conditional with a true consequent is itself true, but speakers typically reject sentences such as "If I have a penny in my pocket, then Paris is in France". These classic problems have been called the [paradoxes of material implication](wikipedia://en/12857474).[^n13] In addition to the paradoxes, a variety of other arguments have been given against a material implication analysis. For instance, [counterfactual conditionals](wikipedia://en/491578) would all be vacuously true on such an account, when in fact some are false.

In the mid-20th century, a number of researchers including [H. P. Grice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Grice) and [Frank Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Cameron_Jackson) proposed that [pragmatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics) principles could explain the discrepancies between natural language conditionals and the material conditional. On their accounts, conditionals [denote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denotation) material implication but end up conveying additional information when they interact with conversational norms such as [Grice's maxims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#Grice's_maxims).[^n13][^n14] Recent work in [formal semantics](wikipedia://en/31395652) and [philosophy of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_language) has generally eschewed material implication as an analysis for natural-language conditionals.[^n14] In particular, such work has often rejected the assumption that natural-language conditionals are [truth functional](wikipedia://en/604707) in the sense that the truth value of "If *P*, then *Q*" is determined solely by the truth values of *P* and *Q*.[^n13] Thus semantic analyses of conditionals typically propose alternative interpretations built on foundations such as [modal logic](wikipedia://en/333365), [relevance logic](wikipedia://en/185076), [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), and [causal models](wikipedia://en/44239711).[^n14][^n13][^n15]

Similar discrepancies have been observed by psychologists studying conditional reasoning, for instance, by the notorious [Wason selection task](wikipedia://en/823236) study, where less than 10% of participants reasoned according to the material conditional. Some researchers have interpreted this result as a failure of the participants to conform to normative laws of reasoning, while others interpret the participants as reasoning normatively according to nonclassical laws.[^n16][^n17][^n18]
