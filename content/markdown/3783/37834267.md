# Binary Goppa code

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Binary Goppa code"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), the **binary Goppa code** is an [error-correcting code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error-correcting_code) that belongs to the class of general Goppa codes originally described by [Valerii Denisovich Goppa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valerii_Denisovich_Goppa), but the binary structure gives it several mathematical advantages over non-binary variants, also providing a better fit for common usage in computers and telecommunication. Binary Goppa codes have interesting properties suitable for [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432) in [McEliece-like cryptosystems](wikipedia://en/735452) and similar setups.

:::

## Construction and properties {#Construction_and_properties}

An irreducible binary Goppa code is defined by a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) $g(x)$ of degree $t$ over a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) $GF(2^m)$ with no repeated roots, and a sequence $L_1, ..., L_n$ of $n$ distinct elements from $GF(2^m)$ that are not roots of $g$.

Codewords belong to the kernel of the syndrome function, forming a subspace of $\{0,1\}^n$:

$$\Gamma(g,L)=\left\{ c \in \{0,1\}^n \,\Bigg\vert\, \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{c_i}{x-L_i} \equiv 0 \bmod g(x) \right\}$$

The code defined by a tuple $(g,L)$ has dimension at least $n-mt$ and distance at least $2t+1$, thus it can encode messages of length at least $n-mt$ using codewords of size $n$ while correcting at least $\left\lfloor \frac{(2t+1)-1}{2} \right\rfloor=t$ errors. It possesses a convenient [parity-check matrix](wikipedia://en/10557570) $H$ in form

$$H=VD=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1\\ L_1^1 & L_2^1 & L_3^1 & \cdots & L_{n}^1\\ L_1^2 & L_2^2 & L_3^2 & \cdots & L_{n}^2\\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ L_1^{t-1} & L_2^{t-1} & L_3^{t-1} & \cdots & L_{n}^{t-1} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{g(L_1)} & & & & \\ & \frac{1}{g(L_2)} & & & \\ & & \frac{1}{g(L_3)} & & \\ & & & \ddots & \\ & & & & \frac{1}{g(L_{n})} \end{pmatrix}$$

Note that this form of the parity-check matrix, being composed of a [Vandermonde matrix](wikipedia://en/348641) $V$ and [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080) $D$, shares the form with check matrices of [alternant codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternant_code), thus alternant decoders can be used on this form. Such decoders usually provide only limited error-correcting capability (in most cases $t/2$).

For practical purposes, parity-check matrix of a binary Goppa code is usually converted to a more computer-friendly binary form by a trace construction, that converts the $t$-by-$n$ matrix over $GF(2^m)$ to a $mt$-by-$n$ [binary matrix](wikipedia://en/4109196) by writing polynomial coefficients of $GF(2^m)$ elements on $m$ successive rows.

## Decoding {#Decoding}

Decoding of binary Goppa codes is traditionally done by Patterson algorithm, which gives good error-correcting capability (it corrects all $t$ design errors), and is also fairly simple to implement.

Patterson algorithm converts a syndrome to a vector of errors. The syndrome of a binary word $c=(c_1,\dots,c_n)$ is expected to take a form of

$$s(x) \equiv \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{c_i}{x - L_i} \mod g(x)$$

Alternative form of a parity-check matrix based on formula for $s(x)$ can be used to produce such syndrome with a simple [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280).

The algorithm then computes $v(x) \equiv \sqrt{s(x)^{-1}-x} \mod g(x)$. That fails when $s(x) \equiv 0$, but that is the case when the input word is a codeword, so no error correction is necessary.

$v(x)$ is reduced to polynomials $a(x)$ and $b(x)$ using the [extended euclidean algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_euclidean_algorithm), so that $a(x) \equiv b(x)\cdot v(x) \mod g(x)$, while $\deg(a)\leq\lfloor t/2 \rfloor$ and $\deg(b)\leq\lfloor (t-1)/2 \rfloor$.

Finally, the *error locator polynomial* is computed as $\sigma(x) = a(x)^2 + x\cdot b(x)^2$. Note that in binary case, locating the errors is sufficient to correct them, as there's only one other value possible. In non-binary cases a separate error correction polynomial has to be computed as well.

If the original codeword was decodable and the $e=(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ was the binary error vector, then

$$\sigma(x) = \prod_{i=1}^n (x-L_i)^{e_i}$$

Factoring or evaluating all roots of $\sigma(x)$ therefore gives enough information to recover the error vector and fix the errors.

## Properties and usage {#Properties_and_usage}

Binary Goppa codes viewed as a special case of Goppa codes have the interesting property that they correct full $\deg(g)$ errors, while only $\deg(g)/2$ errors in ternary and all other cases. Asymptotically, this error correcting capability meets the famous [Gilbert–Varshamov bound](wikipedia://en/1882350).

Because of the high error correction capacity compared to code rate and form of parity-check matrix (which is usually hardly distinguishable from a random binary matrix of full rank), the binary Goppa codes are used in several [post-quantum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-quantum) [cryptosystems](wikipedia://en/506383), notably [McEliece cryptosystem](wikipedia://en/735452) and [Niederreiter cryptosystem](wikipedia://en/21177402).
