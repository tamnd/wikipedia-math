# Isotropic line

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Isotropic line"}

In the geometry of [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478), an **isotropic line** or **null line** is a [line](wikipedia://en/946975) for which the quadratic form applied to the [displacement vector](wikipedia://en/859275) between any pair of its points is zero. An isotropic line occurs only with an [isotropic quadratic form](wikipedia://en/10917170), and never with a [definite quadratic form](wikipedia://en/20437320).

:::

Using [complex geometry](wikipedia://en/186101), [Edmond Laguerre](wikipedia://en/1011080) first suggested the existence of two isotropic lines through the point (*α*, *β*) that depend on the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358) $i$:[^o2]

First system: $(y - \beta) = (x - \alpha) i,$

Second system: $(y - \beta) = -i (x - \alpha) .$

Laguerre then interpreted these lines as [geodesics](wikipedia://en/91096):

An essential property of isotropic lines, and which can be used to define them, is the following: the distance between any two points of an isotropic line *situated at a finite distance in the plane* is zero. In other terms, these lines satisfy the [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309) d*s*<sup>2</sup> = 0. On an arbitrary [surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_%28differential_geometry%29) one can study curves that satisfy this differential equation; these curves are the geodesic lines of the surface, and we also call them *isotropic lines*.[^o2]

In the [complex projective plane](wikipedia://en/737155), points are represented by [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316) $(x_1, x_2, x_3)$ and lines by homogeneous coordinates $(a_1, a_2, a_3)$. An **isotropic line** in the complex projective plane satisfies the equation:[^o3]

$$a_3(x_2 \pm i x_1) = (a_2 \pm i a_1) x_2 .$$

In terms of the affine subspace $x_{3} = 1$, an isotropic line through the origin is

$$x_2 = \pm i x_1 .$$

In projective geometry, the isotropic lines are the ones passing through the [circular points at infinity](wikipedia://en/8088700).

In the real orthogonal geometry of [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351), isotropic lines occur in pairs:

A non-singular plane which contains an isotropic vector shall be called a [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/10917170#Hyperbolic_plane). It can always be spanned by a pair $\mathbf{n}$, $\mathbf{m}$ of vectors which satisfy $\mathbf n^2 = \mathbf m^2 = 0, \quad \mathbf{nm} = 1.$

We shall call any such ordered pair $\mathbf{n}$, $\mathbf{m}$ a hyperbolic pair. If $V$ is a non-singular plane with orthogonal geometry and $\mathbf{n} \ne 0$ is an isotropic vector of $V$, then there exists precisely one $\mathbf{m}$ in $V$ such that $\mathbf{n}$, $\mathbf{m}$ is a hyperbolic pair. The vectors $x\mathbf{n}$ and $y\mathbf{m}$ are then the only isotropic vectors of $V$.[^o4]

## Relativity {#Relativity}

Isotropic lines have been used in cosmological writing to carry light. For example, in a mathematical encyclopedia, light consists of [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon): "The [worldline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worldline) of a zero rest mass (such as a non-quantum model of a photon and other elementary particles of mass zero) is an isotropic line."[^o6] For isotropic lines through the origin, a particular point is a [null vector](wikipedia://en/1453977), and the collection of all such isotropic lines forms the [light cone](wikipedia://en/310474) at the origin.

[Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) expanded the concept of isotropic lines to [multivectors](wikipedia://en/2727288) in his book on [spinors in three dimensions](wikipedia://en/10159868).[^o7]
