# Odo Van Maelcote

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Odo Van Maelcote"}

**Odo van Maelcote**, (also known as **Malcotius**)[^o2](b. [Brussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels) 28 July 1572, d. [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome) 14 May 1615) was a [Jesuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuit) priest, scientist and mathematician from the [Spanish Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Netherlands) (present day [Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgium)).[^o3]

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Odo van Maelcote, lord of [Kessel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kessel,_Belgium), was the oldest son of Johannes Van Maelcote, a doctor of both civil and canon law at the [University of Louvain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_University_of_Leuven), First Counsellor and Vice-Chancellor in the [chancellery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancery_%28medieval_office%29) of [Brabant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Brabant), and his wife, Marie de Viron. He entered the [noviciate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noviciate) with the [Jesuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuits) at [Tournai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tournai) on 12 February 1590 and studied at the [University of Douai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Douai).

In 1607, he published in [Brussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels) a small tract on the equinoctial [astrolabe](wikipedia://en/73664) that he had designed and manufactured, which involved two conjoined hemispheres. The device was useful to missionary ventures around the world, as it could be used in both northern and southern latitudes.[^o5] This made his name known among scientists, and when he sent an astrolabe as a gift to [Christopher Clavius](wikipedia://en/633645) in Rome,[^o6] Clavius responded by inviting him to [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome) to teach mathematics and [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew)[^o7] at the [Roman College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_College) in 1608, where he soon became assistant to [Christoph Grienberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christoph_Grienberger).

In 1610, [Cardinal Bellarmine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roberto_Bellarmine) asked four Jesuits, [Grienberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christoph_Grienberger), [Clavius](wikipedia://en/633645), it:Paolo Lembo and Maelcote, for their opinion on the new phenomena discovered by [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo) using his telescope. Reporting on behalf of his colleagues, Maelcote advised that they agreed with most of Galileo's findings, although they were uncertain whether Saturn consisted of three bodies as Galileo stated (the fact that Saturn had rings was not evident from the first telescopes); rather, it seemed to be elliptical in shape. Maelcote also reported that Clavius did not believe that the Moon had mountains and craters as appeared through the telescope; its surface was smooth, and the uneven distribution of some substance within the Moon merely created the illusion of an uneven surface.[^o8]

Galileo was invited to a banquet at the Roman College, on 18 May 1611, during which Maelcote pronounced a long formal [eulogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eulogy), praising Galileo's work.[^o9][^o10] Nevertheless, they were called to order by the Superior General of the Jesuits, [Claudio Acquaviva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claudio_Acquaviva), and obliged to uphold the traditional [Aristotelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelianism) view of the universe, which Galileo's discoveries overturned.

In 1611, Van Maelcote was back in Brussels, and from 1612 to 1614, he began a correspondence with [Kepler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler), particularly seeking his views on sunspots, following the publication of observations and commentary from both [Christoph Scheiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christoph_Scheiner) and [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo). Kepler agreed with Maelcote and Galileo that sunspots did not behave like satellites.[^o11]

He returned to Rome, where he worked at the observatory of the Roman College until his death. Among his pupils was [Orazio Grassi](wikipedia://en/17093165).[^o12]
