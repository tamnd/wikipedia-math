# Diffusion wavelets

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Diffusion wavelets"}

**Diffusion wavelets** are a fast multiscale framework for the analysis of functions on discrete (or discretized continuous) structures like [graphs](wikipedia://en/325806), [manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold_%28mathematics%29), and [point clouds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_cloud) in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). Diffusion wavelets are an extension of classical [wavelet theory](wikipedia://en/50903) from [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147). Unlike classical wavelets whose basis functions are predetermined, diffusion wavelets are adapted to the geometry of a given diffusion operator $T$ (e.g., a [heat kernel](wikipedia://en/3904336) or a [random walk](wikipedia://en/235451)). Moreover, the diffusion wavelet basis functions are constructed by dilation using the dyadic powers (powers of two) of $T$. These dyadic powers of $T$ diffusion over the space and propagate local relationships in the function throughout the space until they become global. And if the rank of higher powers of $T$ decrease (i.e., its spectrum decays), then these higher powers become compressible. From these decaying dyadic powers of $T$ comes a chain of decreasing subspaces. These subspaces are the [scaling function](wikipedia://en/50903#Scaling_function) approximation subspaces, and the differences in the subspace chain are the wavelet subspaces.

:::

Diffusion wavelets were first introduced in 2004 by [Ronald Coifman](wikipedia://en/1558980) and Mauro Maggioni at Yale University.[^o2]

## Algorithm {#Algorithm}

This algorithm constructs the scaling basis functions and the wavelet basis functions along with the representations of the diffusion operator $T$ at these scales.

In the algorithm below, the subscript notation $\Phi_a$ and $\Psi_b$ represents the scaling basis functions at scale $a$ and the wavelet basis functions at scale $b$ respectively. The notation $[\Phi_b]_{\Phi_a}$ denotes the matrix representation of the scaling basis $\Phi_b$ represented with respect to the basis $\Phi_a$. Lastly, the notation $[T]_{\Phi_a}^{\Phi_b}$ denotes the matrix represents of the operator $T$, where the [row space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Row_space) of $T$ is represented with respect to the basis $\Phi_a$, and the [column space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_space) of $T$ is represented with respect to the basis $\Phi_b$. Otherwise put, the domain of operator $T$ is represented with respect to the basis $\Phi_a$ and the range is represented with respect to the basis $\Phi_b$. The function $QR$ is a sparse [QR decomposition](wikipedia://en/305223) with $\epsilon$ precision.[^o5]

// Input: // $T$ is the matrix representation of the diffusion operator. // $\epsilon$ is the precision of the QR decomposition, e.g., 1e-6. // $J$ is the maximum number of scale levels (note: this is an *optional* upper bound, it may converge sooner.) // Output: // $\lbrace\Phi_j\rbrace$ is the set of scaling basis functions indexed by scale $j$. // $\lbrace\Psi_j\rbrace$ is the set of wavelet basis functions indexed by scale $j$.

$\lbrace\Phi_j\rbrace, \lbrace\Psi_j\rbrace \leftarrow \text{function DiffusionWaveletTree} ( T , \epsilon , J ):$ $\textbf{for } j\leftarrow 0 \text{ to } J-1$: $[\Phi_{j+1}]_{\Phi_j}, [T^{2^j}]_{\Phi_j}^{\Phi_{j+1}} \leftarrow QR\left([T^{2^j}]_{\Phi_j}^{\Phi_{j}}, \epsilon\right)$ $[T^{2^{j+1}}]_{\Phi_{j+1}}^{\Phi_{j+1}} \leftarrow \left([T^{2^j}]_{\Phi_j}^{\Phi_{j+1}} [\Phi_{j+1}]_{\Phi_j}\right)^2$ $[\Psi_j]_{\Phi_j} \leftarrow QR\left(I_{\langle\Phi_j\rangle}-[\Phi_{j+1}]_{\Phi_j}\left([\Phi_{j+1}]_{\Phi_j}\right)^*, \epsilon\right)$ $\textbf{end for}$

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

### Mathematics {#Mathematics}

Diffusion wavelets are of general interest in mathematics. Specifically, they allow for the direct calculation of the [Green′s function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green′s_function) and the inverse [graph Laplacian](wikipedia://en/1448472).

### Computer science {#Computer_science}

Diffusion wavelets have been used extensively in computer science, especially in [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488). They have been applied to the following fields:

- solving [Markov decision processes](wikipedia://en/1125883) and [Markov chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chains) for machine learning,[^o10]
- [transfer learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfer_learning),[^o11]
- value [function approximation](wikipedia://en/336897) in [reinforcement learning](wikipedia://en/66294),[^o12]
- [dimensionality reduction](wikipedia://en/579867),[^o13]
- mesh compression for 3D graphics,[^o14]
- [topic model](wikipedia://en/28934119) analysis of document corpora.[^o15]
- relation extraction. [^o16]
