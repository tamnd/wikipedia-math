# Phase reduction

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Phase reduction"}

**Phase reduction** is a method used to reduce a multi-dimensional dynamical equation describing a nonlinear [limit cycle](wikipedia://en/452950) [oscillator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscillator) into a one-dimensional phase equation.[^o2][^o3] Many phenomena in our world such as chemical reactions, electric circuits, mechanical vibrations, cardiac cells, and spiking neurons are examples of [rhythmic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhythm) phenomena, and can be considered as nonlinear limit cycle oscillators.[^o3]

:::

## History {#History}

The theory of phase reduction method was first introduced in the 1950s, the existence of [periodic solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_solution) to nonlinear oscillators under perturbation, has been discussed by Malkin in,[^o5] in the 1960s, Winfree illustrated the importance of the notion of phase and formulated the [phase model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_model) for a population of nonlinear oscillators in his studies on biological synchronization.[^o6] Since then, many researchers have discovered different rhythmic phenomena related to phase reduction theory.

## Phase model of reduction {#Phase_model_of_reduction}

Consider the [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087) of the form

$$\frac{dx}{dt}=f(x),$$

where $x\in \mathbb{R}^N$ is the oscillator [state variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_variable), $f(x)$ is the baseline vector field. Let $\varphi:\mathbb{R}^N\times \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^N$ be the [flow](wikipedia://en/2619735) induced by the system, that is, $\varphi(x_0,t)$ is the solution of the system for the initial condition $x(0)=x_0$. This system of differential equations can describe for a neuron model for conductance with $x=(V,n)\in \mathbb{R}^N$, where $V$ represents the voltage difference across the membrane and $n$ represents the $(N-1)$-dimensional vector that defines [gating variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gating_variable).[^o8] When a neuron is perturbed by a stimulus current, the dynamics of the perturbed system will no longer be the same with the dynamics of the baseline neural oscillator.

*Image: Isochron-stable limit cycle.png.* Isochrons and a stable limit cycle of the planar system $\dot{x}=x - y - x(x^2+y^2); \dot{y}= x + y - y(x^2+y^2)$. The system has a unique stable limit cycle (solid circle). Only isochrons corresponding to phases $nT/5, n=1, 2, 3, 4, 5$, where $T=2\pi$ is the period of the orbit, are shown (dotted lines). Neighbouring trajectories (blue dotted curves) with different initial conditions are attracted to the cycle (except the origin).

The target here is to reduce the system by defining a [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29) for each point in some neighbourhood of the limit cycle. The allowance of sufficiently small perturbations (e.g. external forcing or stimulus effect to the system) might cause a large deviation of the phase, but the amplitude is perturbed slightly because of the attracting of the limit cycle.[^o9] Hence we need to extend the definition of the phase to points in the neighborhood of the cycle by introducing the definition of [asymptotic phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_phase) (or latent phase).[^o10] This helps us to assign a phase to each point in the [basin of attraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basin_of_attraction) of a periodic orbit. The set of points in the basin of attraction of $\gamma$ that share the same asymptotic phase $\Phi(x)$ is called the [isochron](wikipedia://en/15390784) (e.g. see Figure 1), which were first introduced by Winfree.[^o11] Isochrons can be shown to exist for such a stable hyperbolic limit cycle $\gamma$.[^o12] So for all point $x$ in some neighbourhood of the cycle, the evolution of the phase $\varphi=\Phi(x)$ can be given by the relation $\frac{d\varphi}{dt}=\omega$, where $\omega=\frac{2\pi}{T_0}$ is the [natural frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_frequency) of the oscillation.[^o8][^o13] By the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) we then obtain an equation that govern the evolution of the phase of the neuron model is given by the phase model:

$$\frac{d\varphi}{dt}=\nabla\Phi(x)\cdot f(x)=\omega,$$

where $\nabla\Phi(x)$ is the gradient of the phase function $\Phi(x)$ with respect to the vector of the neuron's state vector $x$, for the derivation of this result, see [^o3][^o8][^o13] This means that the $N$-dimensional system describing the oscillating neuron dynamics is then reduced to a simple one-dimensional phase equation. One can notice that, it is impossible to retrieve the full information of the oscillator $x$ from the phase $\Phi$ because $\Phi(x)$ is not one-to-one mapping.[^o3]

## Phase model with external forcing {#Phase_model_with_external_forcing}

Consider now a [weakly perturbed system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weakly_perturbed_system) of the form

$$\frac{dx(t)}{dt}=f(x)+\varepsilon g(t),$$

where $f(x)$ is the baseline vector field, $\varepsilon g(t)$ is a weak periodic external forcing (or stimulus effect) of period $T$, which can be different from $T_0$ (in general), and frequency $\Omega=2\pi/T$, which might depend on the oscillator state $x$. Assuming that the baseline neural oscillator (that is, when $\varepsilon=0$) has an [exponentially stable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponentially_stable) [limit cycle](wikipedia://en/452950) $\gamma$ with period $T_0$ (example, see Figure 1) $\gamma$ that is [normally hyperbolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normally_hyperbolic),[^o15] it can be shown that $\gamma$ persists under small perturbations.[^o16] This implies that for a small perturbation, the perturbed system will remain close to the limit cycle. Hence we assume that such a limit cycle always exists for each neuron.

The evolution of the perturbed system in terms of the isochrons is [^o17]

$$\frac{d\varphi}{dt}=\omega +\varepsilon \, \nabla\Phi(x)\cdot g(t),$$

where $\nabla\Phi(x)$ is the gradient of the phase $\Phi(x)$ with respect to the vector of the neuron's state vector $x$, and $g(t)$ is the stimulus effect driving the firing of the neuron as a function of time $t$. This phase equation is a [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564) (PDE).

For a sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, a reduced phase model evaluated on the limit cycle $\gamma$ of the unperturbed system can be given by, up to the first order of $\varepsilon$,

$$\frac{d\varphi}{dt}=\omega + \varepsilon \, Z(\varphi) \cdot g(t),$$

where function $Z(\varphi):=\nabla\Phi(\gamma(t))$ measures the normalized phase shift due to a small perturbation delivered at any point $x$ on the limit cycle $\gamma$, and is called the [phase sensitivity function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_sensitivity_function) or infinitesimal [phase response curve](wikipedia://en/1556918).[^o11][^o17]

In order to analyze the reduced phase equation corresponding to the perturbed [nonlinear system](wikipedia://en/146103), we need to solve a PDE, which is not a trivial one. So we need to simplify it into an [autonomous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous) phase equation for $\varphi$, which can more easily be analyzed.[^o17] Assuming that the frequencies $\omega$ and $\Omega$ are sufficiently small so that $\omega-\Omega=\varepsilon\delta$, where $\delta$ is $O(1)$, we can introduce a new phase function $\psi(t)=\varphi(t)-\Omega t$.[^o17]

By the [method of averaging](wikipedia://en/3942941),[^o18] assuming that $\psi(t)$ does not vary within $T$, we obtain an approximated phase equation

$$\frac{d\psi(t)}{dt}=\Delta_\varepsilon + \varepsilon\Gamma(\psi),$$

where $\Delta_\varepsilon=\varepsilon\delta$, and $\Gamma(\psi)$ is a $2\pi$-periodic function representing the effect of the periodic external forcing on the oscillator phase,[^o17] defined by

$$\Gamma(\psi)= \frac 1 {2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi}Z(\psi+\eta)\cdot g\left(\frac\eta\Omega\right) \, d\eta .$$

The graph of this function $\Gamma(\psi)$ can be shown to exhibit the dynamics of the approximated phase model, for more illustrations see.[^o3]
