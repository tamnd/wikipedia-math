# Abel Tassin d'Alonne

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Abel Tassin d'Alonne"}

**Abel Tassin d'Alonne** ([The Hague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hague), 1646 – The Hague, 24 October 1723[^n1]) was a Dutch [courtier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courtier) and [diplomat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diplomat), who was private secretary of [Mary II of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_II_of_England), [William III of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_III_of_England), and [Anthonie Heinsius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthonie_Heinsius), and played a secret role as the chief of Heinsius' [Cabinet noir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_noir) and as a [cryptographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptographer) of note.

:::

## Life {#Life}

### Personal life {#Personal_life}

Abel was born as the son of Jeanne de Bommert Silvercroon, the daughter of a Swedish diplomat to the [Dutch Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Republic), and presumably Dutch [stadtholder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stadtholder) [Frederick Henry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Henry,_Prince_of_Orange)'s son, [William](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_II,_Prince_of_Orange), who was married to [Mary, Princess Royal and Princess of Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary,_Princess_Royal_and_Princess_of_Orange) at the time.[^o4] He was never legitimized by William. His mother later married a Walloon officer in the service of the Dutch Republic by the name of Charles Tassin d'Alonne, and Abel took his name. But as an illegitimate son of Prince William he was a half-brother of [the later stadtholder William III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_III_of_England), who would become king of England in 1689.[^o5] Abel would remain unmarried and childless. He would later be granted [Pickering Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pickering_Castle) and other lands by his half-brother William III on 18 May 1697.[^o6]

### Career {#Career}

After Prince William III married [Princess Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_II_of_England), the daughter of [James, Duke of York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_II_of_England), the brother of king [Charles II of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_II_of_England) in 1677, d'Alonne became her private secretary. He remained at her side until her death in 1694. When king William's own private secretary [Constantijn Huygens Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantijn_Huygens_Jr.) died in 1697, d'Alonne was appointed in his stead. When William in his turn died in 1702, d'Alonne became the private secretary of the [Grand pensionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_pensionary) [Anthonie Heinsius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthonie_Heinsius) of the Dutch Republic. As such he also became involved in the latter's secret diplomacy (as he had been in William's service) and he took over Heinsius' [Cabinet noir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_noir), the center of Dutch espionage during the [War of the Spanish Succession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Spanish_Succession).[^o8]

But he long before embarked on a secret career as a spymaster. As secretary of Princess Mary he intercepted a letter of Mary's chaplain, [John Covel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Covel), proving his intent to abduct the princess, and bring her back to her father. After the [Glorious Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glorious_Revolution) he became even more involved in espionage, especially on the Jacobite Court in France. Probably to enable him to intercept correspondence, he was nominated to become head of the general post office in London, though that position went to another. But in 1690 he was involved in the interception of mail between France and the Jacobite army in Ireland.[^n2] As secretary to Queen Mary he was privy to many secrets at the [Court of St. James's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_St._James's) as witnessed by a bundle of correspondence between an anonymous writer at the court (very Probably d'Alonne) with [Everhard van Weede Dijkvelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everhard_van_Weede_Dijkvelt), a confidant of William, who at the time was at The Hague, giving intimate details of events at court in the early 1690s.[^o9]

He was also employed as a [cryptographer](wikipedia://en/18934432). The first suspected instance was when the French ambassador to the Dutch Republic [the Comte d'Avaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Antoine_de_Mesmes_%28diplomat%29) got himself involved in a scandal in 1684, when he wrote an indiscreet letter to pro-French [regenten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regenten) in Amsterdam. The letter was intercepted, and though it was encrypted, successfully decrypted, possibly by d'Alonne.[^o10][^o11] There is more certainty that he was the cryptographer who decrypted intercepted correspondence between the French envoys to Bavaria and Sweden, Rouillé and [Bonnac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Louis_d'Usson) in 1707. The correspondence was intercepted in Brussels and apparently given to both Dutch and British cryptographers. These each decrypted the correspondence independently, but developed different decryptions, which proves that the Dutch version was not simply a copy of the British version.[^o12]

De Leeuw tells us that in the Dutch archives the existence of a *Black Chamber* or *Cabinet noir* can only be inferred from the personal archives of Heinsius, and from a number of cryptographic worksheets in the handwriting of d'Alonne, that were accidentally stored with the archives of the Dutch legation at the [Sublime Porte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublime_Porte).[^o13] De Leeuw explains that for decryption purposes the Dutch government usually relied upon the British, or on the Hanoverian Cabinet noir, during the War of the Spanish Succession, at least until the relations with Great Britain became strained with the ascent of the [Tory Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tories_%28British_political_party%29) under [Harley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Harley,_1st_Earl_of_Oxford_and_Earl_Mortimer) and [Bolingbroke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_St_John,_1st_Viscount_Bolingbroke) in 1710. The use of the Hanoverian cryptographers was definitely ended in 1711, because the *Secreete Besogne* (Secret Committee) of the [States General of the Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_General_of_the_Netherlands) that handled foreign affairs for the Dutch Republic, had been indiscreet with material that had been handled by the Hanoverians, and the Hanoverians therefore lost trust. De Leeuw therefore surmises that d'Alonne was only used to decrypt intercepts when the Hanoverians could not be asked. This would only concern intercepts that would have been provided by the Brussels Post Office,"under the nose and without the consent of the British." As d'Alonne worked very closely with Heinsius (they even shared the same office) the decryption did not even need to leave the premises, so secrecy could be assured.[^o14][^o15]
