# BSAFE

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="BSAFE"}

**Dell BSAFE**, formerly known as **RSA BSAFE**, is a [FIPS 140-2](wikipedia://en/885948) validated [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432) library, available in both [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28programming_language%29) and [Java.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_%28programming_language%29) BSAFE was initially created by [RSA Security](wikipedia://en/301240), which was purchased by [EMC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dell_EMC) and then, in turn, by Dell. When Dell sold the RSA business to [Symphony Technology Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_Technology_Group) in 2020, Dell elected to retain the BSAFE product line.[^o3][^o4] BSAFE was one of the most common encryption toolkits before the [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28cryptosystem%29#Patent) patent expired in September 2000. It also contained implementations of the RC*x* ciphers, with the most common one being [RC4](wikipedia://en/25831). From 2004 to 2013 the default [random number generator](wikipedia://en/2736939) in the library was a [NIST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology)-approved RNG standard, widely known to be insecure from at least 2006, containing a [kleptographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleptographic) [backdoor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backdoor_%28computing%29) from the American [National Security Agency](wikipedia://en/21939) (NSA), as part of its secret [Bullrun](wikipedia://en/40455685) program.[^o5] In 2013 Reuters revealed that RSA had received a payment of \$10 million to set the compromised algorithm as the default option.[^o5] The RNG standard was subsequently withdrawn in 2014, and the RNG removed from BSAFE beginning in 2015.

:::

## Cryptography backdoors {#Cryptography_backdoors}

### Dual\_EC\_DRBG random number generator {#Dual_EC_DRBG_random_number_generator}

From 2004 to 2013, the default [cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generator](wikipedia://en/182249) (CSPRNG) in BSAFE was [Dual\_EC\_DRBG](wikipedia://en/14259066), which contained an alleged backdoor from [NSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NSA), in addition to being a biased and slow CSPRNG.[^o8] The cryptographic community had been aware that Dual\_EC\_DRBG was a very poor CSPRNG since shortly after the specification was posted in 2005, and by 2007 it had become apparent that the CSPRNG seemed to be designed to contain a hidden backdoor for NSA, usable only by NSA via a secret key.[^o9] In 2007, [Bruce Schneier](wikipedia://en/36732) described the backdoor as "too obvious to trick anyone to use it."[^o9] The backdoor was confirmed in the [Snowden leaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_surveillance_disclosure) in 2013, and it was insinuated that NSA had paid RSA Security US\$10 million to use Dual\_EC\_DRBG by default in 2004,[^o5] though RSA Security denied that they knew about the backdoor in 2004. The Reuters article which revealed the secret \$10 million contract to use Dual\_EC\_DRBG described the deal as "handled by business leaders rather than pure technologists".[^o5] RSA Security has largely declined to explain their choice to continue using Dual\_EC\_DRBG even after the defects and potential backdoor were discovered in 2006 and 2007, and has denied knowingly inserting the backdoor.[^o10]

As a cryptographically secure random number generator is often the basis of cryptography, much data encrypted with BSAFE was not secure against NSA. Specifically it has been shown that the backdoor makes SSL/[TLS](wikipedia://en/187813) completely breakable by the party having the private key to the backdoor (i.e. NSA).[^o9] Since the US government and US companies have also used the vulnerable BSAFE, NSA can potentially have made US data less safe, if NSA's secret key to the backdoor had been stolen. It is also possible to derive the secret key by solving a single instance of the algorithm's [elliptic curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curve_cryptography) problem[^o9] (breaking an instance of elliptic curve cryptography is considered unlikely with current computers and algorithms, but a breakthrough may occur).

In June 2013, [Edward Snowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Snowden) began leaking NSA documents. In November 2013, RSA switched the default to HMAC DRBG with SHA-256 as the default option. The following month, Reuters published the report based on the Snowden leaks stating that RSA had received a payment of \$10 million to set Dual\_EC\_DRBG as the default.[^o5]

With subsequent releases of Crypto-C Micro Edition 4.1.2 (April 2016), Micro Edition Suite 4.1.5 (April 2016) and Crypto-J 6.2 (March 2015), Dual\_EC\_DRBG was removed entirely.

### Extended Random TLS extension {#Extended_Random_TLS_extension}

"Extended Random" was a proposed extension for the [Transport Layer Security](wikipedia://en/187813) (TLS) protocol, submitted for standardization to [IETF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Engineering_Task_Force) by an NSA employee,[^o12] although it never became a standard. The extension would otherwise be harmless, but together with the Dual\_EC\_DRBG, it would make it easier to take advantage of the backdoor.[^o13][^o14]

The extension was previously not known to be enabled in any implementations, but in December 2017, it was found enabled on some [Canon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_Inc.) printer models, which use the RSA BSAFE library, because the extension number conflicted a part of TLS version 1.3.[^o14]

## Product suite history {#Product_suite_history}

- Crypto-J is a Java encryption library. In 1997, [RSA Data Security](wikipedia://en/301240) licensed Baltimore Technologies' J/CRYPTO library, with plans to integrate it as part of its new JSAFE encryption toolkit[^o16] and released the first version of JSAFE the same year.[^o17] JSAFE 1.0 was featured in the January 1998 edition of [Byte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byte_%28magazine%29) magazine.[^o18]
- Cert-J is a [Public Key Infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Key_Infrastructure) [API](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Application_Programming_Interface) software library, written in [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_%28programming_language%29). It contains the cryptographic support necessary to generate certificate requests, create and sign [digital certificates](wikipedia://en/223796), and create and distribute [certificate revocation lists](wikipedia://en/296365). As of Cert-J 6.2.4, the entire API has been deprecated in favor of similar functionality provided BSAFE Crypto-J [JCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_Cryptography_Extension) API.
- BSAFE Crypto-C Micro Edition (Crypto-C ME) was initially released in June 2001 under the name "RSA BSAFE Wireless Core 1.0". The initial release targeted [Microsoft Windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Windows), [EPOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EPOC_%28operating_system%29), [Linux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux), [Solaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solaris_%28operating_system%29) and [Palm OS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm_OS).
- BSAFE Micro Edition Suite is a cryptography [SDK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_development_kit) in C. BSAFE Micro Edition Suite was initially announced in February 2002[^o19] as a combined offering of BSAFE SSL-C Micro Edition, BSAFE Cert-C Micro Edition and BSAFE Crypto-C Micro Edition. Both SSL-C Micro Edition and Cert-C Micro Edition reached EOL in September 2014, while Micro Edition Suite remains supported with Crypto-C Micro Edition as its [FIPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Information_Processing_Standards)-validated cryptographic provider.
- SSL-C is an [SSL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secure_Sockets_Layer) toolkit in the BSAFE suite. It was originally written by Eric A. Young and Tim J. Hudson, as a fork of the open library [SSLeay](wikipedia://en/3881085), that they developed prior to joining RSA.[^o20][^o21] SSL-C reached End Of Life in December 2016.
- SSL-J is a Java toolkit that implements [TLS](wikipedia://en/187813). SSL-J was released as part of RSA JSAFE initial product offering in 1997.[^o22] Crypto-J is the default cryptographic provider of SSL-J.

## Product suite support status {#Product_suite_support_status}

On November 25, 2015, RSA announced End of Life (EOL) dates for BSAFE.[^o24] The End of Primary Support (EOPS) was to be reached on January 31, 2017, and the End of Extended Support (EOXS) was originally set to be January 31, 2019. That date was later further extended by RSA for some versions until January 31, 2022.[^o25] During Extended Support, even though the support policy stated that only the most severe problems would be patched, new versions were released containing bugfixes, security fixes and new algorithms.[^o26]

On December 12, 2020, Dell announced the reversal of RSA's past decision, allowing BSAFE product support beyond January 2022 as well as the possibility to soon acquire new licenses. Dell also announced it was rebranding the toolkits to **Dell BSAFE**.[^o27]
