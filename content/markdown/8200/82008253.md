# Berk-Jones test

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Berk-Jones test"}

The **Berk-Jones test** (or **BJ test**) refers to a class of non-parametric [goodness-of-fit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodness-of-fit) statistical tests used to determine whether a set of observed data follow a specific [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543). Introduced by [Robert H. Berk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_H._Berk) and [Douglas H. Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_H._Jones) in 1979, the test is specifically designed to be more powerful than the [Kolmogorov-Smirnov test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov-Smirnov_test) in the tails of the distribution.[^o2]

:::

## Background {#Background}

In [statistical hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing), a goodness-of-fit test compares an [empirical distribution function](wikipedia://en/2690455) (EDF) to a theoretical [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) (CDF). While the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test is arguably the most well-known method, it is often criticized for its lack of sensitivity to deviations occurring at the extremes (tails) of the distribution.

The Berk-Jones test addresses this shortcoming by adopting a "pointwise" maximum [likelihood ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Likelihood_ratio) approach. It still belongs to the family of supremum-type statistics but also incorporates information-theoretic properties, specifically the [Kullback-Leibler divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kullback-Leibler_divergence).

## Mathematical formulation {#Mathematical_formulation}

Let $X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n$ be [independent and identically distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_and_identically_distributed) (i.i.d.) random variables with an empirical distribution function $\mathbb{F}_n(x)$. We wish to test the [null hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis) $H_0: F = F_0$.

The Berk-Jones statistic $R_n$ is defined as:

$$R_n = \sup_{x} K(\mathbb{F}_n(x), F_0(x))$$

where $K(p,q)$ is the binary Kullback-Leibler divergence (or [relative entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_entropy)), defined as:

$$K(p,q) = p \log \left(\frac{p}{q}\right) + \left(1-p\right)\log\left(\frac{1-p}{1-q}\right)$$

The statistic can also be expressed in terms of the [order statistics](wikipedia://en/160986) $X_{(1)} < X_{(2)} < \cdots <X_{(n)}$. For a fixed $x$, $n\,\mathbb{F}_n(x)$ follows a [Binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876) $\text{Bin}(n, F_0(x))$. The BJ statistic effectively finds the $x$ that maximizes the likelihood ratio for testing the success probability of this binomial.[^o5]

## Relationship with Tukey's Higher Criticism statistic {#Relationship_with_Tukey's_Higher_Criticism_statistic}

The Berk-Jones test is closely related to the [Higher Criticism statistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_Criticism_statistic) (HC), originally suggested by [John Tukey](wikipedia://en/415974) in 1976 and further developed in 2004 by [David Donoho](wikipedia://en/26137723) and [Jianshun Jin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jianshun_Jin). Both tests are used for sparse [signal detection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_detection).

While the BJ test is based on the likelihood ratio, the HC statistic is based on a standardized distance (essentially a [Z score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z_score) approach):

$$\text{HC}_n = \sup_{x} \frac{\sqrt{n}\left(\mathbb{F}_n(x) - F_0(x)\right)}{\sqrt{F_0(x)\left(1 - F_0(x)\right)}}$$

Asymptotically (as $n$ approaches infinity), the BJ test and HC are equivalent in their ability to detect sparse mixtures. Specifically, they both belong to the [Rényi family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rényi_family) of statistics. However, the BJ test is over viewed as the "information-theoretic version" of HC. When applying a [Taylor expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_expansion) to the Kullback-Leibler divergence $K(p,q)$ around $p=q$, the first non-vanishing term in the expansion is proportional to the square of the HC statistic:[^o7]

$$K(p,q) \approxeq \frac{(p-q)^2}{2q(1-q)}$$

This shows that the Higher Criticism statistic is essentially a local [quadratic approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_approximation) of the Berk-Jones statistic.

In high-dimensional settings where the signal is "rare and weak," both tests have the same [detection boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detection_boundary) (the point at which it becomes mathematically possible to distinguish signal from noise). However, the BJ test is often preferred in finite samples of the $[0,1]$ interval more naturally than the $Z$-score denominator in HC.

## Comparison of power {#Comparison_of_power}

The effectiveness of the Berk-Jones test compared with other methods depends heavily on the nature of the deviation from the null hypothesis.

::: {#o9 .table cue=table}

| Test Method | Sensitivity Focus | Best Use Case |
|---|---|---|
| **Kolmogorov–Smirnov** | Median / Center | General deviations in the bulk of the data. |
| **Anderson–Darling** | Weighted Tails | Moderate tail sensitivity; common in engineering. |
| **Berk–Jones** | Extreme Tails | Sparse signals; likelihood-ratio based optimality. |
| **Higher Criticism** | Extreme Tails | Sparse signals; $Z$-score based optimality. |

:::

::: {#o11 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The Berk-Jones test is widely used in:

- [Genomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genomics): for identifying differentially expressed genes where only a small fraction of genes are expected to show a signal.
- [Signal detection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_detection): for detecting weak, sparse signals against a background of noise.
- [Meta-analysis](wikipedia://en/62329): for combining p-values from multiple independent studies to detect a global effect.
