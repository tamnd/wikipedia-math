# F-algebra

*Image: F algebra.svg.* The commutative diagram, which defines a property required by morphisms of the original [category](wikipedia://en/53759), so that they can be morphisms of the newly defined category of *F*-algebras.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="F-algebra"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically in [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), *F*-**algebras** generalize the notion of [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364). Rewriting the algebraic laws in terms of [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) eliminates all references to quantified elements from the axioms, and these algebraic laws may then be glued together in terms of a single [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) *F*, the *[signature](wikipedia://en/7788156)*.

:::

*F*-algebras can also be used to represent [data structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_structures) used in [programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_programming), such as [lists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_%28computing%29) and [trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_%28data_structure%29).

The main related concepts are [initial](wikipedia://en/59405) *F*-algebras which may serve to encapsulate the induction principle, and the [dual](wikipedia://en/310885) construction [*F*-coalgebras](wikipedia://en/1983769).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

If $C$ is a [category](wikipedia://en/53759), and $F : C \rightarrow C$ is an [endofunctor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endofunctor) of $C$, then an $F$-**algebra** is a tuple $(A, \alpha)$, where $A$ is an [object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) of $C$ and $\alpha$ is a $C$-[morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) $F(A) \rightarrow A$. The object $A$ is called the *carrier* of the algebra. When it is permissible from context, algebras are often referred to by their carrier only instead of the tuple.

A [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) from an $F$-algebra $(A, \alpha)$ to an $F$-algebra $(B, \beta)$ is a $C$-morphism $f : A \rightarrow B$ such that $f \circ \alpha = \beta \circ F(f)$, according to the following [commutative diagram](wikipedia://en/138484):

*Image: F algebra.svg.*

Equipped with these morphisms, $F$-algebras constitute a category.

The dual construction are $F$-coalgebras, which are objects $A^*$ together with a morphism $\alpha^* : A^* \rightarrow F(A^*)$.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Groups {#Groups}

Classically, a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) is a set $G$ with a *group law* $m : G \times G \rightarrow G$, with $m(x,y)=x\cdot y$, satisfying three axioms: the existence of an identity element, the existence of an inverse for each element of the group, and associativity.

To put this in a categorical framework, first define the identity and inverse as functions (morphisms of the set $G$) by $e :1 \rightarrow G$ with $e(*)=1$, and $i : G \rightarrow G$ with $i(x)=x^{-1}$. Here $1$ denotes the set with one element $1=\left\{*\right\}$, which allows one to identify elements $x\in G$ with morphisms $1 \rightarrow G$.

It is then possible to write the axioms of a group in terms of functions (note how the existential quantifier is absent):

- $$\forall x\in G, \forall y\in G, \forall z\in G, m(m(x, y), z) = m(x, m(y, z)),$$
- $$\forall x\in G, m(e(*), x) = m(x, e(*)) = x,$$
- $$\forall x\in G, m(i(x), x) = m(x, i(x)) = e(*).$$

Then this can be expressed with commutative diagrams:[^o7][^o8]

Now use the [coproduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coproduct_%28category_theory%29) (the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) of sets) to glue the three morphisms in one: $\alpha = e + i + m$ according to

$$\begin{matrix} \alpha: {1}+G+G \times G & \to & G,\\ * & \mapsto & 1,\\ x & \mapsto & x^{-1},\\ (x,y) & \mapsto & x \cdot y. \end{matrix}$$

Thus a group is an $F$-algebra where $F$ is the functor $F(G) = 1 + G + G \times G$. However the reverse is not necessarily true. Some $F$-algebra where $F$ is the functor $F(G) = 1 + G + G \times G$ are not groups.

The above construction is used to define [group objects](wikipedia://en/53507) over an arbitrary category with [finite products](wikipedia://en/237868) and a [terminal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_object) $1$. When the category admits finite [coproducts](wikipedia://en/310889), the group objects are $F$-algebras. For example, [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707) are $F$-algebras in the category of [finite sets](wikipedia://en/11742) and [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945) are $F$-algebras in the category of [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) with [smooth maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_map).

### Algebraic structures {#Algebraic_structures}

Going one step ahead of [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200), most algebraic structures are *F*-algebras. For example, [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) are *F*-algebras for the same functor *F*(*G*) = 1 + *G* + *G*×*G* as for groups, with an additional axiom for commutativity: *m*∘*t* = *m*, where *t*(*x*,*y*) = (*y*,*x*) is the transpose on *G*x*G*.

[Monoids](wikipedia://en/19652) are *F*-algebras of signature *F*(*M*) = 1 + *M*×*M*. In the same vein, [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799) are *F*-algebras of signature *F*(*S*) = *S*×*S*

[Rings](wikipedia://en/48404), [domains](wikipedia://en/487627) and [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) are also *F*-algebras with a signature involving two laws +,•: *R*×*R* → R, an additive identity 0: 1 → *R*, a multiplicative identity 1: 1 → *R*, and an additive inverse for each element -: *R* → *R*. As all these functions share the same [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) *R* they can be glued into a single signature function 1 + 1 + *R* + *R*×*R* + *R*×*R* → *R*, with axioms to express associativity, [distributivity](wikipedia://en/103118), and so on. This makes rings *F*-algebras on the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829) with signature 1 + 1 + *R* + *R*×*R* + *R*×*R*.

Alternatively, we can look at the functor *F*(*R*) = 1 + *R*×*R* in the [category of abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454748). In that context, the multiplication is a homomorphism, meaning *m*(*x* + *y*, *z*) = *m*(*x*,*z*) + *m*(*y*,*z*) and *m*(*x*,*y* + *z*) = *m*(*x*,*y*) + *m*(*x*,*z*), which are precisely the distributivity conditions. Therefore, a ring is an *F*-algebra of signature 1 + *R*×*R* over the category of abelian groups which satisfies two axioms (associativity and identity for the multiplication).

When we come to [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) and [modules](wikipedia://en/276410), the signature functor includes a [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373) *k*×*E* → *E*, and the signature *F*(*E*) = 1 + *E* + *k*×*E* is parametrized by *k* over the category of fields, or rings.

[Algebras over a field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebras_over_a_field) can be viewed as *F*-algebras of signature 1 + 1 + *A* + *A*×*A* + *A*×*A* + *k*×*A* over the category of sets, of signature 1 + *A*×*A* over the [category of modules](wikipedia://en/11231812) (a module with an internal multiplication), and of signature *k*×*A* over the [category of rings](wikipedia://en/15300181) (a ring with a scalar multiplication), when they are associative and unitary.

### Lattice {#Lattice}

Not all mathematical structures are *F*-algebras. For example, a [poset](wikipedia://en/23572) *P* may be defined in categorical terms with a morphism *s*:*P* × *P* → Ω, on a [subobject classifier](wikipedia://en/366909) (Ω = {0,1} in the category of sets and *s*(*x*,*y*)=1 precisely when *x*≤*y*). The axioms restricting the morphism *s* to define a poset can be rewritten in terms of morphisms. However, as the codomain of *s* is Ω and not *P*, it is not an *F*-algebra.

However, [lattices](wikipedia://en/469304), which are partial orders in which every two elements have a supremum and an infimum, and in particular [total orders](wikipedia://en/30330), are *F*-algebras. This is because they can equivalently be defined in terms of the algebraic operations: *x*∨*y* = inf(*x*,*y*) and *x*∧*y* = sup(*x*,*y*), subject to certain axioms (commutativity, associativity, absorption and idempotency). Thus they are *F*-algebras of signature *P* x *P* + *P* x *P*. It is often said that lattice theory draws on both order theory and universal algebra.

### Recurrence {#Recurrence}

Consider the functor $F: \mathrm{\bf{Set}} \to \mathrm{\bf{Set}}$ that sends a set $X$ to $1+X$. Here, $\mathrm{\bf{Set}}$ denotes the category of sets, $+$ denotes the usual [coproduct](wikipedia://en/310889) given by the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946), and $1$ is a terminal object (i.e. any [singleton](wikipedia://en/381798) set). Then, the set $\mathbb{N}$ of [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) together with the function $[\mathrm{zero},\mathrm{succ}] : 1+\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$—which is the coproduct of the functions $\mathrm{zero} : 1 \mapsto 0$ and $\mathrm{succ} : n \mapsto n+1$—is an *F*-algebra.

:::

## Initial *F*-algebra {#Initial_F-algebra}

If the category of *F*-algebras for a given endofunctor *F* has an [initial object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_object), it is called an **initial algebra**. The algebra $(\mathbb{N}, [\mathrm{zero},\mathrm{succ}])$ in the above example is an initial algebra. Various [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) [data structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_structures) used in [programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_programming), such as [lists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_%28computing%29) and [trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_%28data_structure%29), can be obtained as initial algebras of specific endofunctors.

Types defined by using [least fixed point](wikipedia://en/1211913) construct with functor *F* can be regarded as an initial *F*-algebra, provided that [parametricity](wikipedia://en/9315395) holds for the type.[^o13]

See also [Universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200).

## Terminal *F*-coalgebra {#Terminal_F-coalgebra}

In a [dual](wikipedia://en/609737) way, a similar relationship exists between notions of [greatest fixed point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_fixed_point) and terminal *F*-coalgebra. These can be used for allowing [potentially infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actual_infinity) objects while maintaining [strong normalization property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalization_property_%28lambda-calculus%29).[^o13] In the strongly normalizing [Charity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charity_%28programming_language%29) programming language (i.e. each program terminates in it), [coinductive](wikipedia://en/10904266) data types can be used to achieve surprising results, enabling the definition of [lookup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lookup_table) constructs to implement such [“strong”](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computability_theory_%28computer_science%29) functions like the [Ackermann function](wikipedia://en/2925).[^o15]
