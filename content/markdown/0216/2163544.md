# Sieve (category theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sieve"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **sieve** is a way of choosing [arrows](wikipedia://en/27716891) with a common [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264). It is a categorical analogue of a collection of open [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of a fixed [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954). In a [Grothendieck topology](wikipedia://en/12910), certain sieves become categorical analogues of [open covers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_cover) in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954). Sieves were introduced by (Giraud 1964) in order to reformulate the notion of a Grothendieck topology.

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let **C** be a locally small [category](wikipedia://en/53759), and let *c* be an object of **C**. A **sieve** $S\colon C^{\rm op} \to {\rm Set}$ on *c* is a [subfunctor](wikipedia://en/2151745) of Hom(−, *c*), i.e., for all objects *c*′ of **C**, *S*(*c*′) ⊆ Hom(*c*′, *c*), and for all arrows *f*:*c*″→*c*′, *S*(*f*) is the restriction of Hom(*f*, *c*), the [pullback](wikipedia://en/9413508) by *f* (in the sense of precomposition, not of fiber products), to *S*(*c*′); see the next section, below.

Put another way, a sieve is a collection *S* of arrows with a common codomain that satisfies the condition, "If *g*:*c*′→*c* is an arrow in *S*, and if *f*:*c*″→*c*′ is any other arrow in **C**, then *gf* is in *S*." Consequently, sieves are similar to right [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) in [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424) or [filters](wikipedia://en/19719) in [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924).

:::

## Pullback of sieves {#Pullback_of_sieves}

The most common operation on a sieve is *pullback*. Pulling back a sieve *S* on *c* by an arrow *f*:*c*′→*c* gives a new sieve *f*<sup>\*</sup>*S* on *c*′. This new sieve consists of all the arrows in *S* that factor through *c*′.

There are several equivalent ways of defining *f*<sup>\*</sup>*S*. The simplest is:

For any object *d* of **C**, *f*<sup>\*</sup>*S*(*d*) = { *g*:*d*→*c*′ | fg ∈ *S*(*d*)}

A more abstract formulation is:

*f*<sup>\*</sup>*S* is the image of the [fibered product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibered_product) *S*×<sub>Hom(−, *c*)</sub>Hom(−, *c*′) under the natural projection *S*×<sub>Hom(−, *c*)</sub>Hom(−, *c*′)→Hom(−, *c*′).

Here the map Hom(−, *c*′)→Hom(−, *c*) is Hom(−, *f*), the push forward by *f*.

The latter formulation suggests that we can also take the image of *S*×<sub>Hom(−, *c*)</sub>Hom(−, *c*′) under the natural map to Hom(−, *c*). This will be the image of *f*<sup>\*</sup>*S* under composition with *f*. For each object *d* of **C**, this sieve will consist of all arrows *fg*, where *g*:*d*→*c*′ is an arrow of *f*<sup>\*</sup>*S*(*d*). In other words, it consists of all arrows in *S* that can be factored through *f*.

If we denote by ∅<sub>*c*</sub> the empty sieve on *c*, that is, the sieve for which ∅(*d*) is always the empty set, then for any *f*:*c*′→*c*, *f*<sup>\*</sup>∅<sub>*c*</sub> is ∅<sub>*c*′</sub>. Furthermore, *f*<sup>\*</sup>Hom(−, *c*) = Hom(−, *c*′).

## Properties of sieves {#Properties_of_sieves}

Let *S* and *S*′ be two sieves on *c*. We say that *S* ⊆ *S*′ if for all objects *c*′ of **C**, *S*(*c*′) ⊆ *S*′(*c*′). For all objects *d* of **C**, we define (*S* ∪ *S*′)(*d*) to be *S*(*d*) ∪ *S*′(*d*) and (*S* ∩ *S*′)(*d*) to be *S*(*d*) ∩ *S*′(*d*). We can clearly extend this definition to infinite unions and intersections as well.

If we define Sieve<sub>**C**</sub>(*c*) (or Sieve(*c*) for short) to be the set of all sieves on *c*, then Sieve(*c*) becomes partially ordered under ⊆. It is easy to see from the definition that the union or intersection of any family of sieves on *c* is a sieve on *c*, so Sieve(*c*) is a [complete lattice](wikipedia://en/44777).

A [Grothendieck topology](wikipedia://en/12910) is a collection of sieves subject to certain properties. These sieves are called *covering sieves*. The set of all covering sieves on an object *c* is a subset *J*(*c*) of Sieve(*c*). *J*(*c*) satisfies several properties in addition to those required by the definition:

- If *S* and *S*′ are sieves on *c*, *S* ⊆ *S*′, and *S* ∈ *J*(*c*), then *S*′ ∈ *J*(*c*).
- Finite intersections of elements of *J*(*c*) are in *J*(*c*).

Consequently, *J*(*c*) is also a [distributive lattice](wikipedia://en/310883), and it is [cofinal](wikipedia://en/1633290) in Sieve(*c*).
