# Dini derivative

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dini derivative"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and, specifically, [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478), the **Dini derivatives** (or **Dini derivates**) are a class of generalizations of the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921). They were introduced by [Ulisse Dini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulisse_Dini), who studied continuous but nondifferentiable functions.

:::

The **upper Dini derivative**, which is also called an **upper right-hand derivative**,[^o2] of a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122)

$$f:{\mathbb R} \rightarrow {\mathbb R},$$

is denoted by $f+$ and defined by

$$f'_+(t) = \limsup_{h \to {0+}} \frac{f(t + h) - f(t)}{h},$$

where $\lim \sup$ is the [supremum limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum_limit) and the limit is a [one-sided limit](wikipedia://en/379840). The **lower Dini derivative**, $f-$, is defined by

$$f'_-(t) = \liminf_{h \to {0+}} \frac{f(t) - f(t - h)}{h},$$

where $\lim \inf$ is the [infimum limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum_limit).

If $f$ is defined on a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370), then the upper Dini derivative at $t$ in the direction $d$ is defined by

$$f'_+ (t,d) = \limsup_{h \to {0+}} \frac{f(t + hd) - f(t)}{h}.$$

If $f$ is [locally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally) [Lipschitz](wikipedia://en/51731), then $f+$ is finite. If $f$ is [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) at $t$, then the Dini derivative at $t$ is the usual [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) at $t$.

## Remarks {#Remarks}

- The functions are defined in terms of the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) and [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) in order to make the Dini derivatives as "bullet proof" as possible, so that the Dini derivatives are well-defined for almost all functions, even for functions that are not conventionally differentiable. The upshot of Dini's analysis is that a function is differentiable at the point $t$ on the real line ($\mathbb{R}$), only if all the Dini derivatives exist, and have the same value.
- Sometimes the notation $D^{+} f(t)$ is used instead of $f+(t)$ and $D_{-} f(t)$ is used instead of $f-(t)$.[^o2]
- Also,

$$D^{+}f(t) = \limsup_{h \to {0+}} \frac{f(t + h) - f(t)}{h}$$

and

$$D_{-}f(t) = \liminf_{h \to {0+}} \frac{f(t) - f(t - h)}{h}.$$

- So when using the $D$ notation of the Dini derivatives, the plus or minus sign indicates the left- or right-hand limit, and the placement of the sign indicates the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) or [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) limit.
- There are two further Dini derivatives, defined to be

$$D_{+}f(t) = \liminf_{h \to {0+}} \frac{f(t + h) - f(t)}{h}$$

and

$$D^{-}f(t) = \limsup_{h \to {0+}} \frac{f(t) - f(t - h)}{h}.$$

which are the same as the first pair, but with the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) and the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) reversed. For only moderately ill-behaved functions, the two extra Dini derivatives aren't needed. For particularly badly behaved functions, if all four Dini derivatives have the same value ($D^{+}f(t) = D_{+}f(t) = D^{-}f(t) = D_{-}f(t)$) then the function $f$ is differentiable in the usual sense at the point $t$ .

- On the [extended reals](wikipedia://en/51698), each of the Dini derivatives always exist; however, they may take on the values $+\infty$ or $-\infty$ at times (i.e., the Dini derivatives always exist in the [extended](wikipedia://en/51698) sense).
