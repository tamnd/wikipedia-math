# Ghosh–Pratt identity

::: {#o1 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=infobox-mathematical-statement name="Ghosh-Pratt identity"}

**Ghosh-Pratt identity.**

$$\text{E}_{\theta_0} [ \text{vol}(C(X))] = \int_{\theta \neq \theta_0} P_{\theta_0} (\theta \in C(X)) \, d\theta$$

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Ghosh–Pratt identity"}

In [mathematical statistics](wikipedia://en/888711), the **Ghosh-Pratt identity** is a theorem that establishes a formal relationship between the expected volume of a [confidence set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_set) and its probability of false coverage. It is a cornerstone of [optimal estimation](wikipedia://en/34426869), as it allows the problem of finding the shortest [confidence interval](wikipedia://en/280911) to be framed as a problem of maximizing the power of a [statistical test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_test).

:::

The identity was independently discovered and published in 1961 by the Indian statistician [Jayanta Kumar Ghosh](wikipedia://en/20937465) and the American statistician [John Winsor Pratt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Winsor_Pratt).

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Formal statement"}

## Formal statement {#Formal_statement}

Let $X$ be a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) with a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) indexed by a parameter $\theta \in \Theta$. Let $C(X)$ denote a random variable representing a confidence set for $\theta$. The Ghosh-Pratt identity states that the expected volume (or length, if $\Theta$ is one dimensional) of the confidence set $C(X)$, calculated under the true parameter value $\theta_0$, is equal to the integral of the probabilities of including false values of $\theta$ in the set:

$$\operatorname{E}_{\theta_0} [ \text{vol}(C(X))] = \int_{\theta \neq \theta_0} P_{\theta_0} (\theta \in C(X)) \, d\theta$$

In simpler terms, the expected length of a confidence interval is the sum (integral) of the probabilities of covering all possible incorrect values of $\theta$.[^o5]

:::

## Derivation {#Derivation}

The proof of the identity relies on the relationship between the volume of a set and the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790). Given a specific realization of data $x$, let $C(x)$ be an arbitrary confidence set for the parameter $\theta$. The volume of this set, $\text{vol}(C(x))$, can be expressed as:

$$\text{vol}(C(x)) = \int_{\Theta} I(\theta \in C(x)) \, d\theta$$

where $I(\cdot)$ is the indicator function. To find the expected volume under the true parameter $\theta_0$, we consider all possible confidence sets across the data generating process $X$ and take the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) with respect to the distribution of $X$ given $\theta_0$:

$$\text{E}_{\theta_0} [\text{vol}(C(X))] = \text{E}_{\theta_0} \left[ \int_{\Theta} I(\theta \in C(X)) \, d\theta \right]$$

By applying [Fubini's theorem](wikipedia://en/255245), we can reverse the order of expectation:

$$\text{E}_{\theta_0} [\text{vol}(C(X))] = \int_{\Theta} \text{E}_{\theta_0} [I(\theta \in C(X))] \, d\theta$$

Since the expectation of an indicator function is simply the probability of the event indicated:

$$\text{E}_{\theta_0} [I(\theta \in C(X))] = \text{P}_{\theta_0}(\theta \in C(X))$$

Substituting this false coverage probability back into the integral yields the identity:

$$E_{\theta_0} [\text{vol}(C(X))] = \int_{\Theta} P_{\theta_0}(\theta \in C(X)) \, d\theta$$

This result shows that the expected volume is the integral of the probability that the confidence set covers any value $\theta$ (both the true value $\theta_0$ as well as all false values $\theta \neq \theta_0$).

## Generalization {#Generalization}

While the identity is often presented in the context of a [continuous random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_random_variable), it can be generalized using [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory) to cover [discrete random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_random_variable) and mixed distributions as well. If $\mu$ is a [sigma-finite measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-finite_measure) on the parameter space $\Theta$, the expected measure of the confidence set $C(X)$ under the distribution $P_{\theta_0}$ is given by:

$$\text{E}_{\theta_0} [\mu(C(X))] = \int_{\Theta} P_{\theta_0}(\theta \in C(X)) \, \mu(d\theta)$$

This general form demonstrates that the identity is independent of the underlying distribution of the data $X$: whether it is a [continuous random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_random_variable) or a [discrete random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_random_variable), the relationship between the expected size of the set and the probability of coverage still holds. In other words, the choice of the measure $\mu$ is application-specific:

- If $\mu$ is the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198), we obtain the standard formula for the expected volume of an interval in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
- If $\mu$ is the [counting measure](wikipedia://en/191786), we obtain the formula for the expected number of points in a discrete set.

This mathematical framework ensures the identity is a robust tool across diverse statistical models.[^o12]

:::

## Significance and applications {#Significance_and_applications}

The identity is significant because it connects [estimation theory](wikipedia://en/1565926) with [hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis_testing). Since a confidence set is often constructed by inverting a family of hypothesis tests, the identity shows that:

- to minimize the expected length of a confidence interval, one must minimize the probability of covering false values, and
- minimizing the probability of covering false values is equivalent to maximizing the [statistical power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_power) of the underlying hypothesis test.

By applying the [Neyman-Pearson lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neyman-Pearson_lemma), which identifies [uniformly most powerful tests](wikipedia://en/15433591), statisticians can use the Ghosh-Pratt identity to construct confidence intervals that are mathematically guaranteed to be the shortest possible on average.[^o10]

## History {#History}

The identity was published nearly simultaneously in 1961. J.K. Ghosh published his findings in the *[Calcutta Statistical Association Bulletin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcutta_Statistical_Association_Bulletin)*, focusing on the relationship between interval types, while John Pratt published his in the *[Journal of the American Statistical Association](wikipedia://en/16179313)* focusing on the [decision-theoretic](wikipedia://en/446216) implications. While the two approached the problem from slightly different perspectives, their results were mathematically equivalent.[^o12]
