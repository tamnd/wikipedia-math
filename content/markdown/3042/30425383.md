# Orthodiagonal quadrilateral

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Orthodiagonal quadrilateral"}

*Image: Orthodiagonal quadrilateral.svg.* An orthodiagonal quadrilateral (yellow). According to the characterization of these quadrilaterals, the two red [squares](wikipedia://en/659939) on two opposite sides of the quadrilateral have the same total [area](wikipedia://en/1209) as the two blue squares on the other pair of opposite sides.

In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), an **orthodiagonal quadrilateral** is a [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278) in which the [diagonals](wikipedia://en/349251) cross at [right angles](wikipedia://en/76956). In other words, it is a four-sided figure in which the [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860) between non-adjacent [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) are [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) (perpendicular) to each other.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Special cases"}

## Special cases {#Special_cases}

A [kite](wikipedia://en/84840) is an orthodiagonal quadrilateral in which one diagonal is a [line of symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_of_symmetry). The kites are exactly the orthodiagonal quadrilaterals that contain a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to all four of their sides; that is, the kites are the [tangential](wikipedia://en/9176412) orthodiagonal quadrilaterals.[^o4]

A [rhombus](wikipedia://en/183884) is an orthodiagonal quadrilateral with two pairs of [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) sides (that is, an orthodiagonal quadrilateral that is also a [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496)).

A [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) is a limiting case of both a kite and a rhombus.

Orthodiagonal quadrilaterals that are also [equidiagonal quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/35012663) are called [midsquare quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/45005169).[^o5]

## Characterizations {#Characterizations}

For any orthodiagonal quadrilateral, the sum of the squares of two opposite sides equals that of the other two opposite sides: for successive sides *a*, *b*, *c*, and *d*, we have [^o8][^o9]

$$\displaystyle a^2+c^2=b^2+d^2.$$

This follows from the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034), by which either of these two sums of two squares can be expanded to equal the sum of the four squared distances from the quadrilateral's vertices to the point where the diagonals intersect. [Conversely](wikipedia://en/155941), any quadrilateral in which *a*<sup>2</sup> + *c*<sup>2</sup> = *b*<sup>2</sup> + *d*<sup>2</sup> must be orthodiagonal.[^o10] This can be [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) in a number of ways, including using the [law of cosines](wikipedia://en/19480890), [vectors](wikipedia://en/32533), an [indirect proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_contradiction), and [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826).[^o11]

The diagonals of a [convex](wikipedia://en/660019) quadrilateral are perpendicular [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the two [bimedians](wikipedia://en/25278#Special_line_segments) have equal length.[^o11]

According to another characterization, the diagonals of a convex quadrilateral *ABCD* are perpendicular if and only if

$$\angle PAB + \angle PBA + \angle PCD + \angle PDC = \pi$$

where *P* is the point of intersection of the diagonals. From this equation it follows almost immediately that the diagonals of a convex quadrilateral are perpendicular if and only if the [projections](wikipedia://en/3909097) of the diagonal intersection onto the sides of the quadrilateral are the vertices of a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131).[^o11]

*Image: 8 point circle.svg.* An orthodiagonal quadrilateral ABCD (in blue). The *Varignon parallelogram* (in green) formed by the midpoints of the edges of ABCD is a rectangle. Additionally, the four midpoints (grey) and the four feet of the maltitudes (red) are cocyclic on the *8-point-circle*.

A convex quadrilateral is orthodiagonal if and only if its [Varignon parallelogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varignon_parallelogram) (whose vertices are the [midpoints](wikipedia://en/437979) of its sides) is a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048).[^o11] A related characterization states that a convex quadrilateral is orthodiagonal if and only if the midpoints of the sides and the feet of the four [maltitudes](wikipedia://en/25278#Special_line_segments) are eight [concyclic points](wikipedia://en/504404); the **eight point circle**. The center of this circle is the [centroid](wikipedia://en/25278#Special_line_segments) of the quadrilateral. The quadrilateral formed by the feet of the maltitudes is called the *principal orthic quadrilateral*.[^o12]

*Image: Second 8 point circle.svg.* A second 8-point circle can be constructed from an orthodiagonal quadrilateral ABCD (in blue). The lines perpendicular to each side through the intersection of the diagonals intersect the sides in 8 different points, which are all cocyclic.

If the [normals](wikipedia://en/173224) to the sides of a convex quadrilateral *ABCD* through the diagonal intersection intersect the opposite sides in *R*, *S*, *T*, *U*, and *K*, *L*, *M*, *N* are the feet of these normals, then *ABCD* is orthodiagonal if and only if the eight points *K*, *L*, *M*, *N*, *R*, *S*, *T* and *U* are concyclic; the *second eight point circle*. A related characterization states that a convex quadrilateral is orthodiagonal if and only if *RSTU* is a rectangle whose sides are parallel to the diagonals of *ABCD*.[^o11]

There are several metric characterizations regarding the four [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654) formed by the diagonal intersection *P* and the vertices of a convex quadrilateral *ABCD*. Denote by *m*<sub>1</sub>, *m*<sub>2</sub>, *m*<sub>3</sub>, *m*<sub>4</sub> the [medians](wikipedia://en/1072006) in triangles *ABP*, *BCP*, *CDP*, *DAP* from *P* to the sides *AB*, *BC*, *CD*, *DA* respectively. If *R*<sub>1</sub>, *R*<sub>2</sub>, *R*<sub>3</sub>, *R*<sub>4</sub> and *h*<sub>1</sub>, *h*<sub>2</sub>, *h*<sub>3</sub>, *h*<sub>4</sub> denote the [radii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radii) of the [circumcircles](wikipedia://en/1926432) and the [altitudes](wikipedia://en/161241) respectively of these triangles, then the quadrilateral *ABCD* is orthodiagonal if and only if any one of the following equalities holds:[^o11]

- $$m_1^2+m_3^2=m_2^2+m_4^2$$
- $$R_1^2+R_3^2=R_2^2+R_4^2$$
- $$\frac{1}{h_1^2}+\frac{1}{h_3^2}=\frac{1}{h_2^2}+\frac{1}{h_4^2}$$

Furthermore, a quadrilateral *ABCD* with intersection *P* of the diagonals is orthodiagonal if and only if the [circumcenters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) of the triangles *ABP*, *BCP*, *CDP* and *DAP* are the midpoints of the sides of the quadrilateral.[^o11]

### Comparison with a tangential quadrilateral {#Comparison_with_a_tangential_quadrilateral}

A few metric characterizations of [tangential quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/9176412) and orthodiagonal quadrilaterals are very similar in appearance, as can be seen in this table.[^o11] The notations on the sides *a*, *b*, *c*, *d*, the [circumradii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) *R*<sub>1</sub>, *R*<sub>2</sub>, *R*<sub>3</sub>, *R*<sub>4</sub>, and the altitudes *h*<sub>1</sub>, *h*<sub>2</sub>, *h*<sub>3</sub>, *h*<sub>4</sub> are the same as above in both types of quadrilaterals.

::: {#o14 .table cue=table}

| Tangential quadrilateral | Orthodiagonal quadrilateral |
|---|---|
| $a+c=b+d$ | $a^2+c^2=b^2+d^2$ |
| $R_1+R_3=R_2+R_4$ | $R_1^2+R_3^2=R_2^2+R_4^2$ |
| $\frac{1}{h_1}+\frac{1}{h_3}=\frac{1}{h_2}+\frac{1}{h_4}$ | $\frac{1}{h_1^2}+\frac{1}{h_3^2}=\frac{1}{h_2^2}+\frac{1}{h_4^2}$ |

## Area {#Area}

The area *K* of an orthodiagonal quadrilateral equals one half the product of the lengths of the diagonals *p* and *q*:[^o16]

$$K = \frac{pq}{2}.$$

Conversely, any convex quadrilateral where the area can be calculated with this formula must be orthodiagonal.[^o11] The orthodiagonal quadrilateral has the biggest area of all convex quadrilaterals with given diagonals.

## Other properties {#Other_properties}

- Orthodiagonal quadrilaterals are the only quadrilaterals for which the sides and the [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) formed by the diagonals do not uniquely determine the area.[^o9] For example, two rhombi both having common side *a* (and, as for all rhombi, both having a right angle between the diagonals), but one having a smaller [acute angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_angle) than the other, have different areas (the area of the former approaching zero as the acute angle approaches zero).
- If squares are erected outward on the sides of any quadrilateral (convex, concave, or crossed), then their [centres](wikipedia://en/4850775) ([centroids](wikipedia://en/187926)) are the vertices of an orthodiagonal quadrilateral that is also [equidiagonal](wikipedia://en/35012663) (that is, having diagonals of equal length). This is called [Van Aubel's theorem](wikipedia://en/3021425).
- Each side of an orthodiagonal quadrilateral has at least one common point with the [Pascal points circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_points_circle).[^o18]

## Properties of orthodiagonal quadrilaterals that are also cyclic {#Properties_of_orthodiagonal_quadrilaterals_that_are_also_cyclic}

### Circumradius and area {#Circumradius_and_area}

For a [cyclic](wikipedia://en/194131) orthodiagonal quadrilateral (one that can be [inscribed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscribed) in a circle), suppose the intersection of the diagonals divides one diagonal into segments of lengths *p*<sub>1</sub> and *p*<sub>2</sub> and divides the other diagonal into segments of lengths *q*<sub>1</sub> and *q*<sub>2</sub>. Then[^o21] (the first equality is Proposition 11 in [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844)' [Book of Lemmas](wikipedia://en/17941791))

$$D^2=p_1^2+p_2^2+q_1^2+q_2^2=a^2+c^2=b^2+d^2$$

where *D* is the [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) of the circumcircle. This holds because the diagonals are perpendicular [chords of a circle](wikipedia://en/6220#Chord). These equations yield the circumradius expression

$$R = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{p_1^2+p_2^2+q_1^2+q_2^2}$$

or, in terms of the sides of the quadrilateral, as[^o8]

$$R = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{a^2+c^2}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{b^2+d^2}.$$

It also follows that[^o8]

$$a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2=8R^2.$$

Thus, according to [Euler's quadrilateral theorem](wikipedia://en/25278#Diagonals), the circumradius can be expressed in terms of the diagonals *p* and *q*, and the distance *x* between the midpoints of the diagonals as

$$R = \sqrt{\frac{p^2+q^2+4x^2}{8}}\,.$$

A formula for the area *K* of a cyclic orthodiagonal quadrilateral in terms of the four sides is obtained directly when combining [Ptolemy's theorem](wikipedia://en/194131#Diagonals) and the formula for the [area of an orthodiagonal quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/30425383#Area). The result is[^o22]

$$K=\tfrac{1}{2}(ac+bd).$$

### Other properties {#Other_properties}

- In a cyclic orthodiagonal quadrilateral, the [anticenter](wikipedia://en/194131#Anticenter_and_collinearities) coincides with the point where the diagonals intersect.[^o8]
- [Brahmagupta's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmagupta's_theorem) states that for a cyclic orthodiagonal quadrilateral, the perpendicular from any side through the point of intersection of the diagonals bisects the opposite side.[^o8]
- If an orthodiagonal quadrilateral is also cyclic, the distance from the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) (the center of the circumscribed circle) to any side equals half the length of the opposite side.[^o8]
- In a cyclic orthodiagonal quadrilateral, the distance between the midpoints of the diagonals equals the distance between the circumcenter and the point where the diagonals intersect.[^o8]

## Infinite sets of inscribed rectangles {#Infinite_sets_of_inscribed_rectangles}

*Image: A set of inscribed rectangles whose sides are parallel to the diagonals of the quadrilateral.png.* $ABCD$ is an orthodiagonal quadrilateral, $P_{1}X_{1}Z_{1}Y_{1}$ and $P_{2}X_{2}Z_{2}Y_{2}$ are rectangles whose sides are parallel to the diagonals of the quadrilateral.

*Image: A set of inscribed rectangles defined by Pascal-points circles.png.* $ABCD$ is an orthodiagonal quadrilateral. $P_{1}$ and $Q_{1}$ are Pascal points formed by the circle $\omega_{1}$, $\sigma_{P_{1}Q_{1}}$ is Pascal-points circle which defines the rectangle $P_{1}V_{1}Q_{1}W_{1}$. $P_{2}$ and $Q_{2}$ are Pascal points formed by the circle $\omega_{2}$, $\sigma_{P_{2}Q_{2}}$ is Pascal-points circle which defines the rectangle $P_{2}V_{2}Q_{2}W_{2}$.

For every orthodiagonal quadrilateral, we can inscribe two infinite sets of rectangles:

(i) a set of rectangles whose sides are parallel to the diagonals of the quadrilateral

(ii) a set of rectangles defined by Pascal-points circles.[^o25]
