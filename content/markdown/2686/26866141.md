# Black–Karasinski model

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Black–Karasinski model"}

In [financial mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_mathematics), the **Black–Karasinski model** is a [mathematical model](wikipedia://en/20590) of the [term structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Term_structure) of [interest rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interest_rate); see [short-rate model](wikipedia://en/1219048). It is a one-factor model as it describes interest rate movements as driven by a single source of randomness. It belongs to the class of no-arbitrage models, i.e. it can fit today's [zero-coupon bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-coupon_bond) prices, and in its most general form, today's prices for a set of caps, floors or European [swaptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swaption). The model was introduced by [Fischer Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fischer_Black) and [Piotr Karasinski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piotr_Karasinski) in 1991.

:::

## Model {#Model}

The main state variable of the model is the short rate, which is assumed to follow the stochastic differential equation (under the [risk-neutral measure](wikipedia://en/441911)):

$$d\ln(r) = [\theta_t-\phi_t \ln(r)] \, dt + \sigma_t\, dW_t$$

where *dW*<sub>*t*</sub> is a standard [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/149984). The model implies a [log-normal distribution](wikipedia://en/102476) for the short rate and therefore the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) of the money-market account is infinite for any maturity.

In the original article by Fischer Black and Piotr Karasinski the model was implemented using a [binomial tree](wikipedia://en/250074) with variable spacing, but a [trinomial tree](wikipedia://en/20262149) implementation is more common in practice, typically a log-normal application of the [Hull–White lattice](wikipedia://en/1224131#Trees_and_lattices).

::: {#o4 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The model is used mainly for the pricing of [exotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exotic_option) [interest rate derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interest_rate_derivative) such as [American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_option) and [Bermudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermudan_option) [bond options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_option) and [swaptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swaptions), once its parameters have been calibrated to the current term structure of interest rates and to the prices or [implied volatilities](wikipedia://en/253568) of [caps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interest_rate_cap_and_floor), [floors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interest_rate_cap_and_floor) or [European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_option) swaptions. [Numerical methods](wikipedia://en/1678715) ([usually trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_model_%28finance%29#Interest_rate_derivatives)) are used in the calibration stage as well as for pricing. It can also be used in modeling [credit default risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_default_risk), where the Black–Karasinski short rate expresses the (stochastic) intensity of default events driven by a [Cox process](wikipedia://en/6196078); the guaranteed positive rates are an important feature of the model here. Recent work on [Perturbation Methods in Credit Derivatives](https://www.wiley.com/en-gb/Perturbation+Methods+in+Credit+Derivatives%3A+Strategies+for+Efficient+Risk+Management-p-9781119609612) has shown how analytic prices can be conveniently deduced in many such circumstances, as well as for interest rate options.
