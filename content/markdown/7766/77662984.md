# Law (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Law"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **law** is a [formula](wikipedia://en/404582) that is always true within a given context.[^o2] Laws describe a [relationship](wikipedia://en/22517558), between two or more [expressions](wikipedia://en/609125) or [terms](wikipedia://en/24885593) (which may contain [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109)), usually using [equality](wikipedia://en/90446) or [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489),[^o3] or between formulas themselves, for instance, in [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636). For example, the formula $a^2 \geq 0$ is true for all [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) *$a$*, and is therefore a law. Laws over an equality are called [identities](wikipedia://en/298428).[^o4] For example, $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$ and $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta =1$ are identities.[^o5] Mathematical laws are distinguished from [scientific laws](wikipedia://en/244629) which are based on [observations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation), and try to describe or [predict](wikipedia://en/246066) a range of [natural phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_phenomena).[^o6]

:::

In modern mathematics, the term is rarely used, except for historical terminology, such as in the [law of sines](wikipedia://en/113222). The terms of "[theorem](wikipedia://en/30977)" or "[property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property_%28mathematics%29)" are generally preferred, as more accurate.

## Notable examples {#Notable_examples}

::: {#o9 .statement cue=named-heading name="Geometric laws"}

### Geometric laws {#Geometric_laws}

[Triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941): If *$a$*, *$b$*, and *$c$* are the lengths of the sides of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) then the triangle inequality states that

$$c \leq a + b,$$

with equality only in the degenerate case of a triangle with zero [area](wikipedia://en/1209). In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417) and some other geometries, the triangle inequality is a theorem about vectors and vector lengths ([norms](wikipedia://en/990534)):

$$\|\mathbf u + \mathbf v\| \leq \|\mathbf u\| + \|\mathbf v\| ,$$

where the length of the third side has been replaced by the length of the vector sum $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$. When $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are real numbers, they can be viewed as vectors in $\mathbb{R}^1$, and the triangle inequality expresses a relationship between [absolute values](wikipedia://en/991).

[Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034): It states that the area of the [square](wikipedia://en/659939) whose side is the [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) (the side opposite the [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956)) is equal to the sum of the areas of the squares on the other two sides. The [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) can be written as an [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) relating the lengths of the sides *a*, *b* and the hypotenuse *c*, sometimes called the Pythagorean equation:[^o10]

$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2 .$$

#### Trigonometric identities {#Trigonometric_identities}

Geometrically, [trigonometric identities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_identity) are identities involving certain functions of one or more [angles](wikipedia://en/1196).[^o12] They are distinct from [triangle identities](wikipedia://en/18717261#Triangle_identities), which are identities involving both angles and side lengths of a triangle. Only the former are covered in this article.

These identities are useful whenever expressions involving trigonometric functions need to be simplified. Another important application is the [integration](wikipedia://en/15532) of non-trigonometric functions: a common technique which involves first using the [substitution rule with a trigonometric function](wikipedia://en/375033), and then simplifying the resulting integral with a trigonometric identity.

One of the most prominent examples of trigonometric identities involves the equation $\sin^2 \theta + \cos^2 \theta = 1,$ which is true for all [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) values of $\theta$. On the other hand, the equation

$$\cos\theta = 1$$

is only true for certain values of $\theta$, not all. For example, this equation is true when $\theta = 0,$ but false when $\theta = 2$.

Another group of trigonometric identities concerns the so-called addition/subtraction formulas (e.g. the double-angle identity $\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin\theta \cos\theta$, the addition formula for $\tan(x + y)$), which can be used to break down expressions of larger angles into those with smaller constituents.

:::

::: {#o14 .statement cue=named-heading name="Algebraic laws"}

### Algebraic laws {#Algebraic_laws}

[Cauchy–Schwarz inequality](wikipedia://en/38128): An upper bound on the [inner product](wikipedia://en/14856) between two [vectors](wikipedia://en/23218956) in an inner product space in terms of the product of the vector [norms](wikipedia://en/990534). It is considered one of the most important and widely used [inequalities](wikipedia://en/89489) in mathematics.[^o15]

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality states that for all vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ of an [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856)

$$\left \vert \langle \mathbf u, \mathbf v \rangle \right \vert \leq \langle \mathbf u, \mathbf u \rangle \cdot \langle \mathbf v, \mathbf v \rangle$$

where $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ is the [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product). Examples of inner products include the real and complex [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093); see the [examples in inner product](wikipedia://en/14856#Some_examples). Every inner product gives rise to a Euclidean $l_2$ [norm](wikipedia://en/990534), called the canonical or [induced norm](wikipedia://en/14856#Norm), where the norm of a vector $\mathbf{u}$ is denoted and defined by

$$\|\mathbf{u}\| := \sqrt{\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{u} \rangle},$$

where $\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{u} \rangle$ is always a non-negative real number (even if the inner product is complex-valued). By taking the square root of both sides of the above inequality, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality can be written in its more familiar form in terms of the norm:[^o16][^o17]

$$\left \vert \langle \mathbf u, \mathbf v \rangle \right \vert \leq \langle \mathbf u, \mathbf u \rangle \cdot \langle \mathbf v, \mathbf v \rangle$$

Moreover, the two sides are equal if and only if $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are [linearly dependent](wikipedia://en/101863).[^o18][^o19][^o20]

::: {#o22 .statement cue=named-heading name="Combinatorial laws"}

### Combinatorial laws {#Combinatorial_laws}

[Pigeonhole principle](wikipedia://en/54217): If *$n$* items are put into *$m$* containers, with $n > m$, then at least one container must contain more than one item.[^o23] For example, of three gloves (none of which is ambidextrous/reversible), at least two must be right-handed or at least two must be left-handed, because there are three objects but only two categories of handedness to put them into.

:::

::: {#o25 .statement cue=named-heading name="Logical laws"}

### Logical laws {#Logical_laws}

[De Morgan's laws](wikipedia://en/64669): In [propositional logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus) and [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/54476844), **De Morgan's laws**,[^o26][^o27][^o28] also known as **De Morgan's theorem**,[^o29] are a pair of transformation rules that are both [valid](wikipedia://en/21304742) [rules of inference](wikipedia://en/252311). They are named after [Augustus De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289), a 19th-century British mathematician. The rules allow the expression of [conjunctions](wikipedia://en/18152) and [disjunctions](wikipedia://en/7962) purely in terms of each other via [negation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_negation). The rules can be expressed in English as:

- not (A or B) = (not A) and (not B)
- not (A and B) = (not A) or (not B) where "A or B" is an "[inclusive or](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusive_or)" meaning *at least* one of A or B rather than an "[exclusive or](wikipedia://en/105979)" that means *exactly* one of A or B. In [formal language](wikipedia://en/10939), the rules are written as $$\begin{aligned} \neg(P\lor Q) &\iff (\neg P) \land (\neg Q), \quad \text{ and } \\ \neg(P\land Q) &\iff (\neg P) \lor (\neg Q), \end{aligned}$$ where *P* and *Q*are [propositions](wikipedia://en/81094),
- [$\neg$](wikipedia://en/161019) is the negation logic operator (NOT),
- [$\land$](wikipedia://en/18152) is the conjunction logic operator (AND),
- [$\lor$](wikipedia://en/7962) is the disjunction logic operator (OR),
- [$\iff$](wikipedia://en/14922) is a [metalogical](wikipedia://en/387934) symbol meaning "can be replaced in a [logical proof](wikipedia://en/1197184) with", often read as "if and only if". For any combination of true/false values for P and Q, the left and right sides of the arrow will hold the same truth value after evaluation.

The three [Laws of thought](wikipedia://en/1252308) are:

- The [law of identity](wikipedia://en/1403190): 'Whatever is, is.'[^o30] For all a: a = a.
- The [law of non-contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_non-contradiction) (alternately the 'law of contradiction'[^o31]): 'Nothing can both be and not be.'[^o30]
- The law of excluded middle: 'Everything must either be or not be.'[^o30] In accordance with the [law of excluded middle](wikipedia://en/17637) or excluded third, for every proposition, either its positive or negative form is true: A[∨](wikipedia://en/7962)¬A.

:::

::: {#o33 .statement cue=named-heading name="Phenomenological laws"}

### Phenomenological laws {#Phenomenological_laws}

[Benford's law](wikipedia://en/36782) is an observation that in many real-life sets of numerical [data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data), the [leading digit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading_digit) is likely to be small.[^o34] In sets that obey the law, the number 1 appears as the leading significant digit about 30% of the time, while 9 appears as the leading significant digit less than 5% of the time. Uniformly distributed digits would each occur about 11.1% of the time.[^o35]

[Strong law of small numbers](wikipedia://en/18093481), in a humorous way, states any given small number appears in far more contexts than may seem reasonable, leading to many apparently surprising coincidences in mathematics, simply because small numbers appear so often and yet are so few.
