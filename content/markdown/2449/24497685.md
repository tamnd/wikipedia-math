# Algebraic theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Algebraic theory"}

Informally in [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), an **algebraic theory** is a [theory](wikipedia://en/3512103) that uses [axioms](wikipedia://en/928) stated entirely in terms of [equations](wikipedia://en/9284) between [terms](wikipedia://en/24885593) with [free variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_variable). [Inequalities](wikipedia://en/89489) and [quantifiers](wikipedia://en/43507260) are specifically disallowed. [Sentential logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentential_logic) is the subset of [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) involving only algebraic sentences.

:::

The notion is very close to the notion of [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364), which, arguably, may be just a synonym.

Saying that a theory is algebraic is a stronger condition than saying it is [elementary](wikipedia://en/24497705).

## Informal interpretation {#Informal_interpretation}

An algebraic theory consists of a collection of *n*-ary operation symbols with additional rules (axioms).

For example, the theory of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) is an algebraic theory because it has three operation symbols: a [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) *a* × *b*, a nullary operation 1 ([neutral element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_element)), and a unary operation *x* ↦ *x*<sup>−1</sup> with the rules of [associativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity), neutrality and [inverses](wikipedia://en/45199) respectively. Other examples include:

- the theory of [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799)
- the theory of [lattices](wikipedia://en/469304)
- the theory of [rings](wikipedia://en/48404)

This is opposed to [geometric theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_theory), which involve [partial functions](wikipedia://en/23577) (or [binary relationships](wikipedia://en/3931)) or [existential quantifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_quantifier)—see e.g. [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), where the existence of points or lines is postulated.

## Category-based model-theoretical interpretation {#Category-based_model-theoretical_interpretation}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), an algebraic theory **T** is a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) whose [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) are [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) 0, 1, 2,..., and which, for each *n*, has an *n*-tuple of [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891):

*proj<sub>i</sub>*: *n* → 1, *i* = 1, ..., *n*

This allows interpreting *n* as a [cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of *n* copies of 1.

Example: Let's define an algebraic theory **T** taking hom(*n*, *m*) to be *m*-tuples of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) of *n* free variables *X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*n*</sub> with [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) and with substitution as composition. In this case *proj<sub>i</sub>* is equal to *X<sub>i</sub>*. This theory **T** is called the theory of [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346).

In an algebraic theory, any morphism *n* → *m* can be described as *m* morphisms of [signature](wikipedia://en/7788156) *n* → 1. These latter morphisms are called *n*-ary *operations* of the theory.

If *E* is a category with finite [products](wikipedia://en/237868), the [full subcategory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_subcategory) Alg(**T**, *E*) of the [category of functors](wikipedia://en/407039) \[**T**, *E*\] consisting of those functors that preserve finite products is called *the category of* **T**-*models* or **T**-*algebras*.

Note that for the case of a morphism 2 → 1, the appropriate algebra *A* will define a morphism

*A*(2) ≈ *A*(1) × *A*(1) → *A*(1).
