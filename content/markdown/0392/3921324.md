# Q-derivative

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Q-derivative"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the area of [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) and [quantum calculus](wikipedia://en/2933361), the ***q*-derivative**, or **Jackson derivative**, is a [*q*-analog](wikipedia://en/1619396) of the [ordinary derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_derivative), introduced by [Frank Hilton Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Hilton_Jackson). It is the inverse of [Jackson's *q*-integration](wikipedia://en/10335099). For other forms of q-derivative, see (Chung Chung Nam Kang 1994).

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The *q*-derivative of a function *f*(*x*) is defined as[^n1][^n2][^n3]

$$\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)_q f(x)=\frac{f(qx)-f(x)}{qx-x}.$$

It is also often written as $D_qf(x)$. The *q*-derivative is also known as the **Jackson derivative**.

Formally, in terms of Lagrange's [shift operator](wikipedia://en/467384) in logarithmic variables, it amounts to the operator

$$D_q= \frac{1}{x} ~ \frac{q^{d~~~ \over d (\ln x)} -1}{q-1} ~,$$

which goes to the plain derivative, $D_q \to \frac{d}{dx}$ as $q \to 1$.

It is manifestly linear,

$$\displaystyle D_q (f(x)+g(x)) = D_q f(x) + D_q g(x)~.$$

It has a [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988) analogous to the ordinary derivative product rule, with two equivalent forms

$$\displaystyle D_q (f(x)g(x)) = g(x)D_q f(x) + f(qx)D_q g(x) = g(qx)D_q f(x) + f(x)D_q g(x).$$

Similarly, it satisfies a [quotient rule](wikipedia://en/209128),

$$\displaystyle D_q (f(x)/g(x)) = \frac{g(x)D_q f(x) - f(x)D_q g(x)}{g(qx)g(x)},\quad g(x)g(qx)\neq 0.$$

There is also a rule similar to the chain rule for ordinary derivatives. Let $g(x) = c x^k$. Then

$$\displaystyle D_q f(g(x)) = D_{q^k}(f)(g(x))D_q(g)(x).$$

The [eigenfunction](wikipedia://en/228601) of the *q*-derivative is the [*q*-exponential](wikipedia://en/4120642) *e<sub>q</sub>*(*x*).

:::

## Relationship to ordinary derivatives {#Relationship_to_ordinary_derivatives}

*Q*-differentiation resembles ordinary differentiation, with curious differences. For example, the *q*-derivative of the [monomial](wikipedia://en/357416) is:[^n2]

$$\left(\frac{d}{dz}\right)_q z^n = \frac{1-q^n}{1-q} z^{n-1} = [n]_q z^{n-1}$$

where $[n]_q$ is the [*q*-bracket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q-bracket) of *n*. Note that $\lim_{q\to 1}[n]_q = n$ so the ordinary derivative is regained in this limit.

The *n*-th *q*-derivative of a function may be given as:[^n3]

$$(D^n_q f)(0)= \frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!} \frac{(q;q)_n}{(1-q)^n}= \frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!} [n]!_q$$

provided that the ordinary *n*-th derivative of *f* exists at *x* = 0. Here, $(q;q)_n$ is the [*q*-Pochhammer symbol](wikipedia://en/11149717), and $[n]!_q$ is the [*q*-factorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q-factorial). If $f(x)$ is [analytic](wikipedia://en/61478) we can apply the [Taylor formula](wikipedia://en/51714) to the definition of $D_q(f(x))$ to get

$$\displaystyle D_q(f(x)) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(q-1)^k}{(k+1)!} x^k f^{(k+1)}(x).$$

A *q*-analog of the Taylor expansion of a function about zero follows:[^n2]

$$f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty f^{(n)}(0)\,\frac{z^n}{n!} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty (D^n_q f)(0)\,\frac{z^n}{[n]!_q}.$$

## Higher order *q*-derivatives {#Higher_order_q-derivatives}

The following representation for higher order $q$-derivatives is known:[^n4][^n5]

$$D_q^nf(x)=\frac{1}{(1-q)^nx^n}\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k\binom{n}{k}_q q^{\binom{k}{2}-(n-1)k}f(q^kx).$$

$\binom{n}{k}_q$ is the $q$-binomial coefficient. By changing the order of summation as $r=n-k$, we obtain the next formula:[^n4][^n6]

$$D_q^nf(x)=\frac{(-1)^n q^{-\binom{n}{2}}}{(1-q)^nx^n}\sum_{r=0}^n(-1)^r\binom{n}{r}_q q^{\binom{r}{2}}f(q^{n-r}x).$$

Higher order $q$-derivatives are used to $q$-Taylor formula and the $q$-[Rodrigues' formula](wikipedia://en/1003242) (the formula used to construct $q$-[orthogonal polynomials](wikipedia://en/32811718)[^n4]).

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### Post Quantum Calculus {#Post_Quantum_Calculus}

Post quantum calculus is a generalization of the theory of [quantum calculus](wikipedia://en/2933361), and it uses the following operator:[^o21][^n7]

$$D_{p,q}f(x):=\frac{f(px)-f(qx)}{(p-q)x},\quad x\neq 0.$$

### Hahn difference {#Hahn_difference}

[Wolfgang Hahn](wikipedia://en/32782993) introduced the following operator (Hahn difference):[^o24][^o25]

$$D_{q,\omega}f(x):=\frac{f(qx+\omega)-f(x)}{(q-1)x+\omega},\quad 0<q<1,\quad\omega>0.$$

When $\omega\to0$ this operator reduces to $q$-derivative, and when $q\to1$ it reduces to forward difference. This is a successful tool for constructing families of [orthogonal polynomials](wikipedia://en/32811718) and investigating some approximation problems.[^n8][^o27][^o28]

### *β*-derivative {#β-derivative}

$\beta$-derivative is an operator defined as follows:[^o30][^n9]

$$D_\beta f(t):=\frac{f(\beta(t))-f(t)}{\beta(t)-t},\quad\beta\neq t,\quad\beta:I\to I.$$

In the definition, $I$ is a given interval, and $\beta(t)$ is any [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) that strictly monotonically increases (i.e. $t>s\rightarrow\beta(t)>\beta(s)$). When $\beta(t)=qt$ then this operator is $q$-derivative, and when $\beta(t)=qt+\omega$ this operator is Hahn difference.

## Applications {#Applications}

The q-calculus has been used in machine learning for designing stochastic activation functions.[^n10]
