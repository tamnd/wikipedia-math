# Rotation number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rotation number"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **rotation number** is an [invariant](wikipedia://en/1337282) of [homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13660) of the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220).

:::

## History {#History}

It was first defined by [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) in 1885, in relation to the [precession](wikipedia://en/24714) of the [perihelion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perihelion) of a [planetary orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_orbit). Poincaré later proved a theorem characterizing the existence of [periodic orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_orbit) in terms of [rationality](wikipedia://en/19727024) of the rotation number.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Suppose that $f:S^1 \to S^1$ is an orientation-preserving [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) of the [circle](wikipedia://en/508177) $S^1 = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}.$ Then $f$ may be [lifted](wikipedia://en/10504570) to a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) $F: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ of the real line, satisfying

$$F(x + m) = F(x) +m$$

for every real number $x$ and every integer $m$.

The **rotation number** of $f$ is defined in terms of the [iterates](wikipedia://en/1448702) of $F$:

$$\omega(f)=\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{F^n(x)-x}{n}.$$

[Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) proved that the limit exists and is independent of the choice of the starting point $x$. The lift $F$ is unique modulo integers, therefore the rotation number is a well-defined element of $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}.$ Intuitively, it measures the average rotation angle along the [orbits](wikipedia://en/252250) of $f$.

:::

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

If $f$ is a rotation by $2\pi a$ (where $0 < a < 1$), then

$$F(x)=x+a,$$

and its rotation number is $a$ (cf. [irrational rotation](wikipedia://en/9445837)).

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The rotation number is invariant under [topological conjugacy](wikipedia://en/5506149), and even monotone topological **semiconjugacy**: if $f$ and $g$ are two homeomorphisms of the circle and

$$h\circ f = g\circ h$$

for a monotone continuous map $h$ of the circle into itself (not necessarily homeomorphic) then $f$ and $g$ have the same rotation numbers. It was used by Poincaré and [Arnaud Denjoy](wikipedia://en/1485520) for topological classification of homeomorphisms of the circle. There are two distinct possibilities.

- The rotation number of $f$ is a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024) $p/q$ (in the lowest terms). Then $f$ has a [periodic orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_orbit), every periodic orbit has period $q$, and the order of the points on each such orbit coincides with the order of the points for a rotation by $p/q$. Moreover, every forward orbit of $f$ converges to a periodic orbit. The same is true for *backward* orbits, corresponding to iterations of $f^{-1}$, but the limiting periodic orbits in forward and backward directions may be different.
- The rotation number of $f$ is an [irrational number](wikipedia://en/20647689) $\theta$. Then $f$ has no periodic orbits (this follows immediately by considering a periodic point $x$ of $f$). There are two subcases.
1. There exists a dense orbit. In this case $f$ is topologically conjugate to the [irrational rotation](wikipedia://en/9445837) by the angle $\theta$ and all orbits are [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594). Denjoy proved that this possibility is always realized when $f$ is twice continuously differentiable.
1. There exists a [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172) $C$ invariant under $f$. Then $C$ is a unique minimal set and the orbits of all points both in forward and backward direction converge to $C$. In this case, $f$ is semiconjugate to the irrational rotation by $\theta$, and the semiconjugating map $h$ of degree 1 is constant on components of the complement of $C$.

The rotation number is *continuous* when viewed as a map from the group of homeomorphisms (with $C^{0}$ topology) of the circle into the circle.

:::
