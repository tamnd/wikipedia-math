# Geometrical continuity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Geometrical continuity"}

The concept of **geometrical continuity** was primarily applied to the [conic sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_sections) (and related shapes) by mathematicians such as [Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz), [Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736), and [Poncelet](wikipedia://en/764818). The concept was an early attempt at describing, through geometry rather than algebra, the concept of [continuity](wikipedia://en/6122) as expressed through a parametric function.[^o2]

:::

The basic idea behind geometrical continuity was that the five conic sections were really five different versions of the same shape. An [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) tends to a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) as the [eccentricity](wikipedia://en/1239472) approaches zero, or to a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) as it approaches one; and a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) tends to a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) as the eccentricity drops toward one; it can also tend to intersecting [lines](wikipedia://en/946975). Thus, there was *continuity* between the conic sections. These ideas led to other concepts of continuity. For instance, if a circle and a straight line were two expressions of the same shape, perhaps a line could be thought of as a circle of infinite [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815). For such to be the case, one would have to make the line closed by allowing the point $x =\infty$ to be a point on the circle, and for $x =+\infty$ and $x =-\infty$ to be identical. Such ideas were useful in crafting the modern, algebraically defined, idea of the [continuity](wikipedia://en/6122) of a function and of $\infty$ (see [projectively extended real line](wikipedia://en/1014534) for more).[^o2]
