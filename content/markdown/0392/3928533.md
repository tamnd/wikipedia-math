# Spherical law of cosines

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Spherical law of cosines"}

In [spherical trigonometry](wikipedia://en/650405), the **law of cosines** (or, more specifically, the **law of cosines for sides**[^o3]) is a theorem relating the three sides and one of the angles of a [spherical triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_triangle), analogous to the [planar law of cosines](wikipedia://en/19480890) for a triangle in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410).

:::

*Image: Law-of-haversines.svg.* Spherical triangle solved by the law of cosines.

A *spherical triangle* is a shape on a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) consisting of three *vertices* (corner [points](wikipedia://en/593693)) connected by three sides, each of which is part of a *[great circle](wikipedia://en/48082)*, the analog on the sphere of a [straight line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_line) in the plane (for example the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) and [meridians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_%28geography%29) of a globe). The arc lengths of the sides are proportional to the measures of the [central angles](wikipedia://en/462705) they [subtend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtend). The angles between sides are [dihedral angles](wikipedia://en/338046) between the [planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) containing them. In the image, $\mathbf{u}$, $\mathbf{v}$, and $\mathbf{w}$ represent three points on the sphere. If the angular lengths of the sides are $a$ (from $\mathbf{u}$ to $\mathbf{v}), b$ (from $\mathbf{u}$ to $\mathbf{w}$), and $c$ (from $\mathbf{v}$ to $\mathbf{w}$), and the angle of the corner opposite $c$ is $C$, then the (first) spherical law of cosines states:[^o2][^o3]

$$\cos c = \cos a \cos b + \sin a \sin b \cos C\,$$

As a special case, when $C = \tfrac{\pi}{2}$ then $\cos C = 0$, resulting in the spherical analogue of the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034):

$$\cos c = \cos a \cos b\,$$

If the law of cosines is used to solve for $c$, the necessity of inverting the cosine magnifies [rounding errors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rounding_error) when $c$ is small. In this case, the alternative formulation of the [law of haversines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_haversines) is preferable.[^o4]

A second spherical law of cosines, sometimes called the **law of cosines for angles**,[^o3] relates the three angles and one of the sides of a triangle:[^o5]

$$\cos C = -\cos A \cos B + \sin A \sin B \cos c\,$$

where $A$ and $B$ are the angles of the corners opposite to sides $a$ and $b$, respectively. It is related to the first law by [polar duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_duality).

::: {#o7 .proof cue=heading name="Proofs"}

## Proofs {#Proofs}

### First proof {#First_proof}

Let $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$, and $\mathbf{w}$ denote the [unit vectors](wikipedia://en/167053) from the center of a unit sphere to those corners of the triangle. The angles and distances do not change if the coordinate system is rotated, so we can rotate the coordinate system so that $\mathbf{u}$ is at the [north pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_pole) and $\mathbf{v}$ is somewhere on the [prime meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_meridian) (longitude of 0). With this rotation, the [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates) for $\mathbf{v}$ are $(r, \theta, \phi) = (1, a, 0) ,$ where $\theta$ is the angle measured from the north pole not from the equator, and the spherical coordinates for $\mathbf{w}$ are $(r, \theta, \phi) = (1, b, C) .$ The [Cartesian coordinates](wikipedia://en/7706) for $\mathbf{v}$ are $(x, y, z) = (\sin a, 0, \cos a)$ and the Cartesian coordinates for $\mathbf{w}$ are $(x, y, z) = (\sin b \cos C, \sin b \sin C, \cos b) .$ The value of $\cos c$ is the dot product of the two Cartesian vectors, which is $\sin a \sin b \cos C + \cos a \cos b .$

### Second proof {#Second_proof}

Let $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$, and $\mathbf{w}$ denote the [unit vectors](wikipedia://en/167053) from the center of the sphere to those corners of the triangle. We have $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} = 1$, $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} = \cos c$, $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \cos a$, and $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{w} = \cos b$. The vectors $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{w}$ have lengths $\sin a$ and $\sin b$ respectively and the angle between them is $C$, so

$$\begin{aligned} \sin a \sin b \cos C &= (\mathbf u \times \mathbf v) \cdot (\mathbf u \times \mathbf w) \\ &= (\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf u)(\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf w) - (\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf w)(\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf u) \\ &= \cos c - \cos a \cos b \end{aligned}$$

using [cross products](wikipedia://en/157092), [dot products](wikipedia://en/157093), and the [Binet–Cauchy identity](wikipedia://en/3535789)

$$(\mathbf p \times \mathbf q) \cdot (\mathbf r \times \mathbf s) = (\mathbf p \cdot \mathbf r)(\mathbf q \cdot \mathbf s) - (\mathbf p \cdot \mathbf s)(\mathbf q \cdot \mathbf r).$$

### Third proof {#Third_proof}

There is a proof using [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440).[^o11] Let $\mathbf{u}$, $\mathbf{v}$, and $\mathbf{w}$ denote the [unit vectors](wikipedia://en/167053) from the center of the unit sphere to those corners of the triangle. We define the quaternion $u = (0, \mathbf{u}) = 0 + u_{x}\mathbf{i} + u_{y}\mathbf{j} + u_{z}\mathbf{k}$. The quaternion $u$ is used to represent a rotation by 180° around the axis indicated by the vector $\mathbf{u}$. We note that using $-\mathbf{u}$ as the axis of rotation gives the same result, and that the rotation is its own inverse. We also define $v = (0, \mathbf{v})$ and $w = (0, \mathbf{w})$.

We compute the [product of quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton_product), which also gives the composition of the corresponding rotations:

$$q = vu^{-1} = (v)(-u) = (-(\mathbf v \cdot -\mathbf u), \mathbf v \times -\mathbf u) = (\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v, \mathbf u \times \mathbf v) = (\cos a, \mathbf w' \sin a)$$

where $(f, \mathbf{g})$ represents the real (scalar) and imaginary (vector) parts of a quaternion, $a$ is the angle between $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$, and $\mathbf{w}' = (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) / \lvert \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\rvert$ is the axis of the rotation that moves $\mathbf{u}$ to $\mathbf{v}$ along a great circle. Similarly we define:

$$\begin{aligned} r &= wv^{-1} = (\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf w, \mathbf v \times \mathbf w) = (\cos c, \mathbf u' \sin c) \\ s &= uw^{-1} = (\mathbf w \cdot \mathbf u, \mathbf w \times \mathbf u) = (\cos b, \mathbf v' \sin b). \\ \end{aligned}$$

The quaternions $q$, $r$, and $s$ are used to represent rotations with axes of rotation $\mathbf{w}'$, $\mathbf{u}'$, and $\mathbf{v}'$, respectively, and angles of rotation $2a$, $2b$, and $2c$, respectively. (Because these are double angles, each of $q$, $r$, and $s$ represents two applications of the rotation implied by an edge of the spherical triangle.)

From the definitions, it follows that

$$qsr = vu^{-1}uw^{-1}wv^{-1} = 1,$$

which tells us that the composition of these rotations is the identity transformation. In particular, $qs = r^{-1}$ gives us

$$(\cos a, \mathbf w' \sin a) (\cos b, \mathbf v' \sin b) = (\cos c, -\mathbf u' \sin c).$$

Expanding the left-hand side, we obtain

$$(\cos a \cos b - \mathbf w' \cdot \mathbf v' \sin a \sin b, \mathbf w' \cos a \sin b + \mathbf v' \sin a \cos b + \mathbf w' \times \mathbf v' \sin a \sin b).$$

Equating the real parts on both sides of the identity, we obtain

$$\cos a \cos b - \mathbf w' \cdot \mathbf v' \sin a \sin b = \cos c.$$

Because $\mathbf{w}'$ is parallel to $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{v}'$ is parallel to $\mathbf{w} \times \mathbf{u} = -\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{w}$, and $C$ is the angle between $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{w}$, it follows that $\mathbf w' \cdot \mathbf v' = -\cos C$. Thus,

$$\cos a \cos b + \cos C \sin a \sin b = \cos c.$$

:::

## Rearrangements {#Rearrangements}

The first and second spherical laws of cosines can be rearranged to put the sides ($a, b, c$) and angles ($A, B, C$) on opposite sides of the equations:

$$\begin{aligned} \cos C &= \frac{\cos c - \cos a \cos b}{\sin a \sin b} \\ \cos c &= \frac{\cos C + \cos A \cos B}{\sin A \sin B} \\ \end{aligned}$$

## Planar limit: small angles {#Planar_limit:_small_angles}

For *small* spherical triangles, i.e. for small $a, b$, and $c$, the spherical law of cosines is approximately the same as the ordinary planar law of cosines,

$$c^2 \approx a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C \,.$$

To prove this, we will use the [small-angle approximation](wikipedia://en/2009207) obtained from the [Maclaurin series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maclaurin_series) for the cosine and sine functions:

$$\begin{aligned} \cos a &= 1 - \frac{a^2}{2} + O\left(a^4\right) \\ \sin a &= a + O\left(a^3\right) \end{aligned}$$

Substituting these expressions into the spherical law of cosines nets:

$$1 - \frac{c^2}{2} + O\left(c^4\right) = 1 - \frac{a^2}{2} - \frac{b^2}{2} + \frac{a^2 b^2}{4} + O\left(a^4\right) + O\left(b^4\right) + \cos(C)\left(ab + O\left(a^3 b\right) + O\left(ab^3\right) + O\left(a^3 b^3\right)\right)$$

or after simplifying:

$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C + O\left(c^4\right) + O\left(a^4\right) + O\left(b^4\right) + O\left(a^2 b^2\right) + O\left(a^3 b\right) + O\left(ab^3\right) + O\left(a^3 b^3\right).$$

The [big O](wikipedia://en/44578) terms for $a$ and $b$ are dominated by $O(a^{4}) + O(b^{4})$ as $a$ and $b$ get small, so we can write this last expression as:

$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C + O\left(a^4\right) + O\left(b^4\right) + O\left(c^4\right).$$

## History {#History}

Various trigonometric equations equivalent to the spherical law of cosines were used in the course of solving astronomical problems by medieval Islamic astronomers [al-Khwārizmī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Musa_al-Khwarizmi) (9th century) and [al-Battānī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Battani) (c. 900), Indian astronomer [Nīlakaṇṭha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilakantha_Somayaji) (15th century), and Austrian astronomer [Georg von Peuerbach](wikipedia://en/172394) (15th century) but none of them treated it as a general method for solving spherical triangles.[^o15] For example, al-Khwārizmī calculated the azimuth $\alpha$ of the Sun in terms of its altitude $h$, terrestrial latitude $\phi$, and ortive amplitude $\psi$ ([angular distance](wikipedia://en/3720362) between due East and the Sun's rising place on the horizon) as $\cos \alpha = (\sin\psi - \tan\phi \sin h)/\cos h$.[^n1] (See [Horizontal coordinate system](wikipedia://en/48386).)

The spherical law of cosines appeared as an independent trigonometrical identity for solving spherical triangles in Peuerbach's student [Regiomontanus](wikipedia://en/25929)'s De triangulis omnimodis (unfinished at Regiomontanus's death in 1476, published posthumously 1533), a foundational work for European trigonometry and astronomy which comprehensively described how to solve plane and spherical triangles. Regiomontanus used nearly the modern form, but written in terms of the [versine](wikipedia://en/555782), $\operatorname{vers} x = 1 - \cos x$, rather than the cosine,[^n1]

$$\frac{\operatorname{vers} C}{\operatorname{vers} c - \operatorname{vers}(a-b)} = \frac{1}{\sin a \sin b}.$$

Mathematical historians have speculated that Regiomontanus may have adapted the result from specific astronomical examples in al-Battānī's ar, which was published in Latin translation annotated by Regiomontanus in 1537.[^n2]
