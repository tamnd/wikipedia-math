# Snellius–Pothenot problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Snellius–Pothenot problem"}

The **Snellius–Pothenot problem** is a [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261) problem first described in the context of planar [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying) where known points are used to solve an unknown one. Given three known points $A, B, C$, can the location of an observer at an unknown point $P$ be found?

*Image: SnellPotFigure1.png.*

Given these points, and that $C$ is between $A$ and $B$ as seen from $P$, an observer at P can resolve that the [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) $\overline{AC}$ [subtends](wikipedia://en/1999527) an [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) $\alpha$ and the segment $\overline{CB}$ subtends an angle $\beta$; the solution to establishing the position of the point $P$ can be variously found through graphical geometry, rational trigonometry, and geometric algebra.

:::

An indeterminate case exists when all four points fall on the same circle, giving an infinite number of solutions. Thus the circle through $ABC$ is known as the "danger circle", and observations made on (or very close to) this circle should be avoided.

Since it involves the observation of known points from an unknown point, the problem is an example of [resection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_resection_and_intersection). Historically it was first studied by [Willebrord Snellius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willebrord_Snellius), who found a solution around 1615.

## Formulating the equations {#Formulating_the_equations}

### First equation {#First_equation}

Denoting the (unknown) angles $\angle CAP$ as $x$ and $\angle CBP$ as $y$ gives:

$$x+y = 2 \pi - \alpha - \beta - C$$

by using the sum of the angles formula for the [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278) $PACB$. The variable $C$ represents the (known) internal angle in this quadrilateral at point $C$. (Note that in the case where the points $C$ and $P$ are on the same side of the line $AB$, the angle $\angle C$ will be greater than $\pi$).

### Second equation {#Second_equation}

Applying the [law of sines](wikipedia://en/113222) in triangles $\triangle PAC$ and $\triangle PBC$, $\overline{PC}$ can be expressed in two different ways:

$$\overline{PC} = \frac{\overline{AC} \sin x }{\sin \alpha} = \frac{\overline{BC} \sin y}{\sin \beta}.$$

A useful trick at this point is to define an auxiliary angle $\varphi$ such that

$$\tan \phi = \frac{{\rm BC} \sin \alpha}{{\rm AC} \sin \beta}.$$

(A minor note: one should be concerned about [division by zero](wikipedia://en/185663), but consider that the problem is symmetric, so if one of the two given angles is zero one can, if needed, rename that angle $\alpha$ and call the other (non-zero) angle $\beta$, reversing the roles of $A$ and $B$ as well. This will suffice to guarantee that the ratio above is well defined. An alternative approach to the zero angle problem is given in the algorithm below.)

With this substitution the equation becomes

$$\frac{\sin x}{\sin y}=\tan \phi.$$

Now two known [trigonometric identities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_identity) can be used, namely

$$\tan \left(\tfrac{\pi}{4} - \phi\right) = \frac{1 - \tan\phi}{\tan\phi+1}\ , \qquad \frac{\tan\tfrac12(x-y)}{\tan\tfrac12(x+y)} = \frac{\sin x - \sin y}{\sin x + \sin y}\ ,$$

to put this in the form of the second equation;

$$\tan\tfrac12(x-y) = \tan\tfrac12(\alpha + \beta + C) \tan \left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}-\phi\right).$$

Now these two equations in two unknowns must be solved. Once $x$ and $y$ are known the various triangles can be solved straightforwardly to determine the position of $P$.[^o5] The detailed procedure is shown below.

## Solution algorithm {#Solution_algorithm}

Given are two lengths $\overline{AC}, \overline{BC}$, and three angles $\alpha, \beta, C$, the solution proceeds as follows.

- calculate $\phi= \mathsf{atan2}\left( \overline{BC} \sin \alpha,\ \overline{AC} \sin\beta \right),$ where `[[atan2]]` is a [computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer) function, also called the arctangent of two arguments, that returns the [arctangent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctangent) of the ratio of the two values given. Note that in [Microsoft Excel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Excel) the two arguments are reversed, so the proper syntax would be `= atan2(AC*\sin(beta), BC*\sin(alpha))`. The `atan2` function correctly handles the case where one of the two arguments is zero.
- calculate $K = 2 \pi -\alpha-\beta-C.$
- calculate $W = 2 \cdot \arctan\left[ \tan(\tfrac{\pi}{4} - \phi) \, \tan\tfrac12(\alpha+\beta+C)\right].$
- find $x = \frac{K+W}{2}, \ y = \frac{K-W}{2}.$
- find $\overline{PC} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\overline{BC} \sin y}{\sin \beta} & \text{if } |\sin \beta| > |\sin \alpha|, \\[4pt] \dfrac{\overline{AC} \sin x}{\sin \alpha} & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$
- find $\overline{PA} = \sqrt{\overline{AC}^2 + \overline{PC}^2 - 2 \cdot \overline{AC} \cdot \overline{PC} \cdot \cos(\pi-\alpha-x)}.$ (This comes from the [law of cosines](wikipedia://en/19480890).)
- find $\overline{PB} = \sqrt{\overline{BC}^2 + \overline{PC}^2 - 2\cdot \overline{BC} \cdot \overline{PC} \cdot \cos(\pi-\beta-y)}.$

If the coordinates of $A: x_A,y_A$ and $C: x_C,y_C$ are known in some appropriate Cartesian [coordinate system](wikipedia://en/81931) then the coordinates of $P$ can be found as well.

## Geometric (graphical) solution {#Geometric_(graphical)_solution}

By the [inscribed angle theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscribed_angle_theorem) the locus of points from which $\overline{AC}$ subtends an angle $\alpha$ is a circle having its center on the midline of $\overline{AC}$; from the center $O$ of this circle, $\overline{AC}$ subtends an angle $2\alpha$. Similarly the locus of points from which $\overline{CB}$ subtends an angle $\beta$ is another circle. The desired point $P$ is at the intersection of these two loci.

Therefore, on a map or [nautical chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nautical_chart) showing the points $A, B, C$, the following graphical construction can be used:

- Draw the segment $\overline{AC}$, the midpoint $M$ and the midline, which crosses $\overline{AC}$ perpendicularly at $M$. On this line find the point $O$ such that $\overline{MO} = \tfrac{\overline{AC}}{2 \tan \alpha}.$ Draw the circle with center at $O$ passing through $A$ and $C$.
- Repeat the same construction with points $B, C$ and the angle $\beta$.
- Mark $P$ at the intersection of the two circles (the two circles intersect at two points; one intersection point is $C$ and the other is the desired point $P$.)

This method of solution is sometimes called **Cassini's method**.

## Rational trigonometry approach {#Rational_trigonometry_approach}

The following solution is based upon a paper by N. J. Wildberger.[^o9] It has the advantage that it is almost purely algebraic. The only place trigonometry is used is in converting the [angles](wikipedia://en/1196) to [spreads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_trigonometry#Spread). There is only one [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) required.

- define the following: $$\begin{aligned} & s(x) = \sin^2x \\[4pt] & A(x,y,z) = (x+y+z)^2 - 2(x^2 + y^2 + z^2) \\[2pt] &\begin{alignedat}{5} r_1 &= s(\beta) &\qquad Q_1 &= \overline{BC}^2 \\ r_2 &= s(\alpha) & Q_2 &= \overline{AC}^2 \\ r_3 &= s(\alpha + \beta) & Q_3 &= \overline{AB}^2 \end{alignedat}\end{aligned}$$
- now let: $$\begin{aligned} R_1 &= \frac{r_2 Q_3}{r_3} \qquad R_2 = \frac{r_1 Q_3}{r_3} \\[4pt] C_0 &= \frac{(Q_1 + Q_2 + Q_3) (r_1 + r_2 + r_3) - 2 (Q_1 r_1 + Q_2 r_2 + Q_3 r_3)}{2 r_3} \\[4pt] D_0 &= \frac{r_1 r_2 A(Q_1,Q_2,Q_3)}{r_3} \end{aligned}$$
- the following equation gives two possible values for $R_{3}$: $$(R_3 - C_0)^2 = D_0$$
- choosing the larger of these values, let: $$\begin{aligned} v_1 &= 1 - \frac{(R_1 + R_3 - Q_2)^2}{4 R_1 R_3} \\[4pt] v_2 &= 1 - \frac{(R_2 + R_3 - Q_1)^2}{4 R_2 R_3} \end{aligned}$$

finally:

$$\begin{aligned} \overline{AP}^2 &= \frac{v_1 R_1}{r_2} = \frac{v_1 Q_3}{r_3} \\[4pt] \overline{BP}^2 &= \frac{v_2 R_2}{r_1} = \frac{v_2 Q_3}{r_3} \end{aligned}$$

## Solution via geometric algebra {#Solution_via_geometric_algebra}

Ventura et al. [^o11] solve the planar and three-dimensional Snellius-Pothenot problem via [vector geometric algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_geometric_algebra) and [conformal geometric algebra](wikipedia://en/34629138). The authors also characterize the solutions' sensitivity to measurement errors.

## The indeterminate case {#The_indeterminate_case}

When the point $P$ happens to be located on the same circle as $A, B, C$, the problem has an infinite number of solutions; the reason is that from any other point $P'$ located on the arc $APB$ of this circle the observer sees the same angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ as from $P$ ([inscribed angle theorem](wikipedia://en/462730#Theorem)). Thus the solution in this case is not uniquely determined.

The circle through $ABC$ is known as the "danger circle", and observations made on (or very close to) this circle should be avoided. It is helpful to plot this circle on a map before making the observations.

A theorem on [cyclic quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/194131) is helpful in detecting the indeterminate situation. The quadrilateral $APBC$ is cyclic [iff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iff) a pair of opposite angles (such as the angle at P and the angle at $C$) are supplementary i.e. iff $\alpha+\beta+C = k \pi, (k=1,2,\cdots)$. If this condition is observed the computer/spreadsheet calculations should be stopped and an [error message](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_message) ("indeterminate case") returned.

## Solved examples {#Solved_examples}

(Adapted from Bowser,[^o14] exercise 140, page 203). $A, B, C$ are three objects such that $\overline{AC}$ = 435 ([yards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yard)), $\overline{CB}$ = 320, and $\angle C$ = 255.8 degrees. From a station $P$ it is observed that $\angle APC$ = 30 degrees and $\angle CPB$ = 15 degrees. Find the distances of $P$ from $A, B, C$. (Note that in this case the points $C$ and $P$ are on the same side of the line $AB$, a different configuration from the one shown in the figure).

Answer: $\overline{PA}$ = 790, $\overline{PB}$ = 777, $\overline{PC}$ = 502.

A slightly more challenging test case for a [computer program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_program) uses the same data but this time with $\angle CPB$ = 0. The program should return the answers 843, 1157 and 837.

## Naming controversy {#Naming_controversy}

*Image: Plaquete huis Willebrord Snellius.jpg.* Plaque on Snellius' house in [Leiden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leiden)

The British authority on geodesy, [George Tyrrell McCaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Tyrrell_McCaw) (1870–1942) wrote that the proper term in English was **Snellius problem**, while **Snellius-Pothenot** was the continental European usage.[^o16]

McCaw thought the name of [Laurent Pothenot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurent_Pothenot) (1650–1732) did not deserve to be included as he had made no original contribution, but merely restated Snellius 75 years later.
