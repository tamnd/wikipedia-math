# Perfect power

*Image: Perfect power number Cuisenaire rods 9.png.* Demonstration, with [Cuisenaire rods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuisenaire_rods), of the perfect power nature of 4, 8, and 9

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Perfect power"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **perfect power** is a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) that is a product of equal natural factors, or, in other words, an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) that can be expressed as a square or a higher integer [power](wikipedia://en/99491) of another integer greater than one. More formally, *n* is a perfect power if there exist [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) *m* > 1, and *k* > 1 such that *m<sup>k</sup>* = *n*. In this case, *n* may be called a **perfect *k*th power**. If *k* = 2 or *k* = 3, then *n* is called a [perfect square](wikipedia://en/213919) or [perfect cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_cube), respectively. Sometimes 0 and 1 are also considered perfect powers (0<sup>*k*</sup> = 0 for any *k* > 0, 1<sup>*k*</sup> = 1 for any *k*).

:::

## Examples and sums {#Examples_and_sums}

A [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of perfect powers can be generated by iterating through the possible values for *m* and *k*. The first few ascending perfect powers in numerical order (showing duplicate powers) are :

$2^2 = 4,\ 2^3 = 8,\ 3^2 = 9,\ 2^4 = 16,\ 4^2 = 16,\ 5^2 = 25,\ 3^3 = 27,$ $2^5 = 32,\ 6^2 = 36,\ 7^2 = 49,\ 2^6 = 64,\ 4^3 = 64,\ 8^2 = 64, \dots$

The [sum](wikipedia://en/15287) of the [reciprocals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_%28mathematics%29) of the perfect powers (including duplicates such as 3<sup>4</sup> and 9<sup>2</sup>, both of which equal 81) is 1:

$$\sum_{m=2}^{\infty} \sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m^k}=1.$$

which can be proved as follows:

$$\sum_{m=2}^{\infty} \sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m^k} =\sum_{m=2}^{\infty} \frac {1}{m^2} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m^k} =\sum_{m=2}^{\infty} \frac {1}{m^2} \left( \frac{m}{m-1} \right) =\sum_{m=2}^{\infty} \frac {1}{m(m-1)} =\sum_{m=2}^{\infty} \left( \frac {1}{m-1} - \frac {1}{m} \right) = 1 \, .$$

The first perfect powers without duplicates are:

(sometimes 0 and 1), 4, 8, 9, 16, 25, 27, 32, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 125, 128, 144, 169, 196, 216, 225, 243, 256, 289, 324, 343, 361, 400, 441, 484, 512, 529, 576, 625, 676, 729, 784, 841, 900, 961, 1000, 1024, ...

The sum of the reciprocals of the perfect powers *p* without duplicates is:

$$\sum_{p}\frac{1}{p}=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mu(k)(1-\zeta(k)) \approx 0.874464368 \dots$$

where μ(*k*) is the [Möbius function](wikipedia://en/20961) and ζ(*k*) is the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809).

According to [Euler](wikipedia://en/17902), [Goldbach](wikipedia://en/6290) showed (in a now-lost letter) that the sum of $\tfrac{1}{p - 1}$ over the set of perfect powers *p*, excluding 1 and excluding duplicates, is 1:

$$\sum_{p}\frac{1}{p-1}= {\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{8}+ \frac{1}{15} + \frac{1}{24} + \frac{1}{26}+ \frac{1}{31}}+ \cdots = 1.$$

This is sometimes known as the [Goldbach–Euler theorem](wikipedia://en/14498167).

## Detecting perfect powers {#Detecting_perfect_powers}

Detecting whether or not a given natural number *n* is a perfect power may be accomplished in many different ways, with varying levels of [complexity](wikipedia://en/7543). One of the simplest such methods is to consider all possible values for *k* across each of the [divisors](wikipedia://en/49492) of *n*, up to $k \leq \log_2 n$. So if the divisors of $n$ are $n_1, n_2, \dots, n_j$ then one of the values $n_1^2, n_2^2, \dots, n_j^2, n_1^3, n_2^3, \dots$ must be equal to *n* if *n* is indeed a perfect power.

This method can immediately be simplified by instead considering only [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) values of *k*. This is because if $n = m^k$ for a [composite](wikipedia://en/82289) $k = ap$ where *p* is prime, then this can simply be rewritten as $n = m^k = m^{ap} = (m^a)^p$. Because of this result, the [minimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_element) value of *k* must necessarily be prime.

If the full factorization of *n* is known, say $n = p_1^{\alpha_1}p_2^{\alpha_2} \cdots p_r^{\alpha_r}$ where the $p_i$ are distinct primes, then *n* is a perfect power [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $\gcd(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \ldots, \alpha_r) > 1$ where gcd denotes the [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354). As an example, consider *n* = 2<sup>96</sup>·3<sup>60</sup>·7<sup>24</sup>. Since gcd(96, 60, 24) = 12, *n* is a perfect 12th power (and a perfect 6th power, 4th power, cube and square, since 6, 4, 3 and 2 divide 12).

## Gaps between perfect powers {#Gaps_between_perfect_powers}

In 2002 Romanian mathematician [Preda Mihăilescu](wikipedia://en/639556) proved that the only pair of consecutive perfect powers is 2<sup>3</sup> = 8 and 3<sup>2</sup> = 9, thus proving [Catalan's conjecture](wikipedia://en/62382).

Pillai's conjecture states that for any given positive integer *k* there are only a finite number of pairs of perfect powers whose difference is *k*. This is an unsolved problem.
