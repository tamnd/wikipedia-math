# Generalized polygon

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Generalized polygon"}

*Image: Split Cayley Hexagon.png.* The split Cayley hexagon of order 2

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **generalized polygon** is an [incidence structure](wikipedia://en/723125) introduced by [Jacques Tits](wikipedia://en/219570) in 1959. Generalized *n*-gons encompass as special cases [projective planes](wikipedia://en/24350) (generalized triangles, *n* = 3) and [generalized quadrangles](wikipedia://en/3947006) (*n* = 4). Many generalized polygons arise from [groups of Lie type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groups_of_Lie_type), but there are also exotic ones that cannot be obtained in this way. Generalized polygons satisfying a technical condition known as the *[Moufang](wikipedia://en/2003831) property* have been completely classified by Tits and Weiss. Every generalized *n*-gon with *n* even is also a [near polygon](wikipedia://en/41024203).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A generalized *2*-gon (or a [digon](wikipedia://en/5442728)) is an [incidence structure](wikipedia://en/723125) with at least 2 points and 2 lines where each point is incident to each line.

For *$n \geq 3$* a generalized *n*-gon is an [incidence structure](wikipedia://en/723125) ($P,L,I$), where $P$ is the set of points, $L$ is the set of lines and $I\subseteq P\times L$ is the [incidence relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incidence_relation), such that:

- It is a [partial linear space](wikipedia://en/11197155).
- It has no ordinary *m*-gons as subgeometry for *$2 \leq m < n$*.
- It has an ordinary *n*-gon as a subgeometry.
- For any $\{A_1, A_2\} \subseteq P \cup L$ there exists a subgeometry ($P', L', I'$) isomorphic to an ordinary *n*-gon such that $\{A_1, A_2\} \subseteq P' \cup L'$.

An equivalent but sometimes simpler way to express these conditions is: consider the [bipartite incidence graph](wikipedia://en/1396888) with the vertex set $P \cup L$ and the edges connecting the incident pairs of points and lines.

- The [girth](wikipedia://en/12013) of the incidence graph is twice the [diameter](wikipedia://en/2979341) *n* of the incidence graph.

A generalized polygon is of order *(s,t)* if:

- all vertices of the incidence graph corresponding to the elements of $L$ have the same degree *s* + 1 for some natural number *s*; in other words, every line contains exactly *s* + 1 points,
- all vertices of the incidence graph corresponding to the elements of $P$ have the same degree *t* + 1 for some natural number *t*; in other words, every point lies on exactly *t* + 1 lines.

We say a generalized polygon is thick if every point (line) is incident with at least three lines (points). All thick generalized polygons have an order.

The dual of a generalized *n*-gon ($P,L,I$), is the incidence structure with notion of points and lines reversed and the incidence relation taken to be the [converse relation](wikipedia://en/3941020) of $I$. It can easily be shown that this is again a generalized *n*-gon.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The incidence graph of a generalized digon is a [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) K<sub>*s*+1,*t*+1</sub>.
- For any natural *n* ≥ 3, consider the boundary of the ordinary [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) with *n* sides. Declare the vertices of the polygon to be the points and the sides to be the lines, with set inclusion as the incidence relation. This results in a generalized *n*-gon with *s* = *t* = 1.
- For each [group of Lie type](wikipedia://en/1691490) *G* of rank 2 there is an associated generalized *n*-gon *X* with *n* equal to 3, 4, 6 or 8 such that *G* acts transitively on the set of flags of *X*. In the finite case, for *n=6*, one obtains the Split Cayley hexagon of order (*q*, *q*) for [*G*<sub>2</sub>(*q*)](wikipedia://en/1749665#G2.28q.29_Chevalley_groups) and the twisted triality hexagon of order (*q*<sup>3</sup>, *q*) for [<sup>3</sup>*D*<sub>4</sub>(*q*<sup>3</sup>)](wikipedia://en/1749665#3D4.28q3.29_Steinberg_groups), and for *n=8*, one obtains the Ree-Tits octagon of order (*q*, *q*<sup>2</sup>) for [<sup>2</sup>*F*<sub>4</sub>(*q*)](wikipedia://en/1749665#3D4.28q3.29_Steinberg_groups) with *q* = 2<sup>2*n*+1</sup>. Up to duality, these are the only known thick finite generalized hexagons or octagons.

:::

## Restriction on parameters {#Restriction_on_parameters}

[Walter Feit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Feit) and [Graham Higman](wikipedia://en/2600152) proved that *finite* generalized *n*-gons of order (*s*, *t*) with *s* ≥ 2, *t* ≥ 2 can exist only for the following values of *n*:

2, 3, 4, 6 or 8.

Another proof of the Feit-Higman result was given by Kilmoyer and Solomon.

Generalized "n"-gons for these values are referred to as generalized digons, triangles, quadrangles, hexagons and octagons.

When Feit-Higman theorem is combined with the Haemers-Roos inequalities, we get the following restrictions,

- If *n* = 2, the incidence graph is a complete bipartite graph and thus "s", "t" can be arbitrary integers.
- If *n* = 3, the structure is a finite [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350), and *s* = *t*.
- If *n* = 4, the structure is a finite [generalized quadrangle](wikipedia://en/3947006), and *t*<sup>1/2</sup> ≤ *s* ≤ *t*<sup>2</sup>.
- If *n* = 6, then *st* is a [square](wikipedia://en/213919), and *t*<sup>1/3</sup> ≤ *s* ≤ *t*<sup>3</sup>.
- If *n* = 8, then *2st* is a square, and *t*<sup>1/2</sup> ≤ *s* ≤ *t*<sup>2</sup>.
- If *s* or *t* is allowed to be 1 and the structure is not the ordinary *n*-gon then besides the values of *n* already listed, only *n* = 12 may be possible.

Every known finite generalized hexagon of order (*s*, *t*) for *s*, *t* > 1 has order

- (*q*, *q*): the split Cayley hexagons and their duals,
- (*q*<sup>3</sup>, *q*): the twisted triality hexagon, or
- (*q*, *q*<sup>3</sup>): the dual twisted triality hexagon,

where *q* is a prime power.

Every known finite generalized octagon of order (*s*, *t*) for *s*, *t* > 1 has order

- (*q*, *q*<sup>2</sup>): the Ree-Tits octagon or
- (*q*<sup>2</sup>, *q*): the dual Ree-Tits octagon,

where *q* is an odd power of 2.

## Semi-finite generalized polygons {#Semi-finite_generalized_polygons}

If *s* and *t* are both infinite then generalized polygons exist for each *n* greater or equal to 2. It is unknown whether or not there exist generalized polygons with one of the parameters finite (and bigger than *1*) while the other infinite (these cases are called *semi-finite*). [Peter Cameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Cameron_%28mathematician%29) proved the non-existence of semi-finite generalized quadrangles with three points on each line, while [Andries Brouwer](wikipedia://en/22709916) and Bill Kantor independently proved the case of four points on each line. The non-existence result for five points on each line was proved by G. Cherlin using [Model Theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_Theory).[^o8] No such results are known without making any further assumptions for generalized hexagons or octagons, even for the smallest case of three points on each line.

## Combinatorial applications {#Combinatorial_applications}

As noted before the incidence graphs of generalized polygons have important properties. For example, every generalized *n*-gon of order *(s,s)* is a *(s+1,2n)* [cage](wikipedia://en/7735022). They are also related to [expander graphs](wikipedia://en/9313) as they have nice expansion properties.[^o10] Several classes of extremal expander graphs are obtained from generalized polygons.[^o11] In [Ramsey theory](wikipedia://en/157175), graphs constructed using generalized polygons give us some of the best known constructive lower bounds on offdiagonal Ramsey numbers.[^o12]
