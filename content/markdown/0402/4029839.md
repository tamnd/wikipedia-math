# Quasifield

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quasifield"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **quasifield** is an [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) $(Q,+,\cdot)$ where $+$ and $\cdot$ are [binary operations](wikipedia://en/3948) on $Q,$ much like a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067), but with some weaker conditions. All division rings, and thus all [fields](wikipedia://en/10603), are quasifields.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A quasifield $(Q,+,\cdot)$ is a structure, where $+$ and $\cdot$ are binary operations on $Q$, satisfying these axioms:

- $(Q,+)$ is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447)
- $(Q_{0},\cdot)$ is a [loop](wikipedia://en/25223), where $Q_{0} = Q \setminus \{0\} \,$
- $a \cdot (b+c)=a \cdot b+a \cdot c \quad\forall a,b,c \in Q$ (left [distributivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributivity))
- $a \cdot x=b \cdot x+c$ has exactly one solution for $x$, $\forall a,b,c \in Q, a\neq b$

Strictly speaking, this is the definition of a *left* quasifield. A *right* quasifield is similarly defined, but satisfies right distributivity instead. A quasifield satisfying both distributive laws is called a **[semifield](wikipedia://en/10417428)**, in the sense in which the term is used in [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849).

Although not assumed, one can prove that the axioms imply that the additive group $(Q,+)$ is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974). Thus, when referring to an *abelian quasifield*, one means that $(Q_0, \cdot)$ is abelian.

:::

## Kernel {#Kernel}

The kernel $K$ of a quasifield $Q$ is the set of all elements $c$ such that:

- $$a \cdot(b \cdot c)=(a \cdot b) \cdot c\quad \forall a,b\in Q$$
- $$(a+b) \cdot c=(a \cdot c)+(b \cdot c)\quad \forall a,b\in Q$$

Restricting the binary operations $+$ and $\cdot$ to $K$, one can shown that $(K,+,\cdot)$ is a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067).

One can now make a vector space of $Q$ over $K$, with the following [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373) : $v \otimes l = v \cdot l\quad \forall v\in Q,l\in K$

As a finite division ring is a finite field by [Wedderburn's theorem](wikipedia://en/7132002), the order of the kernel of a finite quasifield is a [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483). The vector space construction implies that the order of any finite quasifield must also be a prime power.

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

All division rings, and thus all fields, are quasifields.

A (right) [near-field](wikipedia://en/4033255#Notes_on_the_definition) that is a (right) quasifield is called a "planar near-field".

The smallest quasifields are abelian and unique. They are the [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615) of orders up to and including eight. The smallest quasifields that are not division rings are the four non-abelian quasifields of order nine; they are presented in (Hall 1959) and (Weibel 2007).

:::

## Projective planes {#Projective_planes}

Given a quasifield $Q$, we define a ternary map $T\colon Q\times Q\times Q\to Q$ by

$$T(a,b,c)=a \cdot b+c \quad \forall a,b,c\in Q$$

One can then verify that $(Q,T)$ satisfies the axioms of a [planar ternary ring](wikipedia://en/4027813). Associated to $(Q,T)$ is its corresponding [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350). The projective planes constructed this way are characterized as follows; the details of this relationship are given in (Hall 1959). A projective plane is a [translation plane](wikipedia://en/2628762) with respect to the [line at infinity](wikipedia://en/403142) if and only if any (or all) of its associated planar ternary rings are right quasifields. It is called a *shear plane* if any (or all) of its ternary rings are left quasifields.

The plane does not uniquely determine the ring; all 4 nonabelian quasifields of order 9 are ternary rings for the unique non-Desarguesian translation plane of order 9. These differ in the [fundamental quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/2605272) used to construct the plane (see Weibel 2007).

## History {#History}

Quasifields were called "Veblen–Wedderburn systems" in the literature before 1975, since they were first studied in the 1907 paper (Veblen-Wedderburn 1907) by [Oswald Veblen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Veblen) and [Joseph Wedderburn](wikipedia://en/2969494). Surveys of quasifields and their applications to [projective planes](wikipedia://en/24350) may be found in (Hall 1959) and (Weibel 2007).
