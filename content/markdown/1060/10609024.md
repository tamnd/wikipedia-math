# Deterministic context-free grammar

This article has serious correctness issues

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Deterministic context-free grammar"}

In [formal grammar](wikipedia://en/18020716) theory, the **deterministic context-free grammars** (**DCFGs**) are a [proper subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_subset) of the [context-free grammars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Context-free_grammars). They are the subset of context-free grammars that can be derived from [deterministic pushdown automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic_pushdown_automata), and they generate the [deterministic context-free languages](wikipedia://en/7962589). DCFGs are always [unambiguous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unambiguous_grammar), and are an important subclass of unambiguous CFGs; there are non-deterministic unambiguous CFGs, however.

:::

DCFGs are of great practical interest, as they can be parsed in [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time) and in fact a parser can be automatically generated from the grammar by a [parser generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parser_generator). They are thus widely used throughout computer science. Various restricted forms of DCFGs can be parsed by simpler, less resource-intensive parsers, and thus are often used. These grammar classes are referred to by the type of parser that parses them, and important examples are [LALR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LALR), [SLR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SLR_parser), and [LL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LL_parser).

## History {#History}

In the 1960s, theoretical research in computer science on [regular expressions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_expressions) and [finite automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_automata) led to the discovery that [context-free grammars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Context-free_grammars) are equivalent to nondeterministic [pushdown automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pushdown_automata).[^o3][^o4][^o5] These grammars were thought to capture the syntax of computer programming languages. The first high-level computer programming languages were under development at the time (see [History of programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_programming_languages)) and writing [compilers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compilers) was difficult. But using [context-free grammars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Context-free_grammars) to help automate the parsing part of the compiler simplified the task. Deterministic context-free grammars were particularly useful because they could be parsed sequentially by a [deterministic pushdown automaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic_pushdown_automaton), which was a requirement due to computer memory constraints.[^o6] In 1965, [Donald Knuth](wikipedia://en/8095) invented the [LR(k) parser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LR_parser) and proved that there exists an LR(k) grammar for every deterministic context-free language.[^o7] This parser still required a lot of memory. In 1969 [Frank DeRemer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_DeRemer) invented the [LALR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LALR) and [Simple LR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SLR_parser) parsers, both based on the LR parser and having greatly reduced memory requirements at the cost of less language recognition power. The LALR parser was the stronger alternative.[^o8] These two parsers have since been widely used in compilers of many computer languages. Recent research has identified methods by which canonical LR parsers may be implemented with dramatically reduced table requirements over Knuth's table-building algorithm.[^o9]
