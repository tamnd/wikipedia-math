# Commandino's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Commandino's theorem"}

*Image: Tetrahedron centroid gimp.png.* Medians of a tetrahedron intersecting in a point $S$ (its centroid), such that$\frac{|AS|}{|SS_{BCD}|}=\frac{|BS|}{|SS_{ACD}|}=\frac{|CS|}{|SS_{ABD}|}=\frac{|DS|}{|SS_{ABC}|}=\frac{3}{1}$

**Commandino's theorem**, named after [Federico Commandino](wikipedia://en/2828269) (1509–1575), states that the four [medians](wikipedia://en/1072006) of a [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606) are concurrent at a point *S*, which divides them in a 3:1 ratio. In a tetrahedron a median is a line segment that connects a vertex with the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) of the opposite [face](wikipedia://en/149926) – that is, the centroid of the opposite triangle. The point *S* is also the centroid of the tetrahedron.[^o2][^o3][^o4]

:::

## History {#History}

The theorem is attributed to Commandino, who stated, in his work *De Centro Gravitatis Solidorum* (The Center of Gravity of Solids, 1565), that the four medians of the tetrahedron are concurrent. However, according to the 19th century scholar Guillaume Libri, [Francesco Maurolico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Maurolico) (1494–1575) claimed to have found the result earlier. Libri nevertheless thought that it had been known even earlier to [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci), who seemed to have used it in his work. [Julian Coolidge](wikipedia://en/4379392) shared that assessment but pointed out that he couldn't find any explicit description or mathematical treatment of the theorem in da Vinci's works.[^o6] Other scholars have speculated that the result may have already been known to Greek mathematicians during antiquity.[^o7]

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Commandino's theorem has a direct analog for [simplexes](wikipedia://en/39781) of any [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398):[^o10]

Let $\Delta$ be a $d$-simplex of some dimension $d>1$ in $\mathbb{R}^n \; (d,n \in \mathbb{N} , n \geq d)$ and let $V_0,V_1,\ldots,V_p$ be its vertices. Furthermore, let $\ell_0, \ell_1,\ldots,\ell_d$, be the medians of $\Delta$, the lines joining each vertex $V_i$ with the centroid of the opposite $(d-1)$-dimensional [facet](wikipedia://en/7186647) $V_0\ldots V_{i-1}V_{i+1}\ldots V_d$. Then, these lines intersect each other in a point $S$, in a ratio of $d:1$.

### Full generality {#Full_generality}

The former analog is easy to prove via the following, more general result, which is analogous to the way [levers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lever) in physics work:[^o12]

Let $m$ and $k$ be [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474), so that in an $\mathbb{R}$-[vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $\mathcal {V}$, $m+k$ pairwise different [points](wikipedia://en/593693) $X_1, \dots, X_m, Y_1, \dots, Y_k \in \mathcal {V}$ are given.

Let $S_X$ be the centroid of the points $X_i \; (i=1, \dots, m)$, let $S_Y$ be the centroid of the points $Y_j \; (j=1, \dots, k)$, and let $S$ be the centroid of all of these $m+k$ points.

Then, one has

$$S = S_X + \frac{k}{m+k} (S_Y-S_X) = \frac{m}{m+k} S_X + \frac{k}{m+k} S_Y.$$

In particular, the centroid $S$ lies on the line $\overline{ {S_X} {S_Y}}$ and divides it in a ratio of $k:m$.

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Reusch's theorem"}

### Reusch's theorem {#Reusch's_theorem}

The previous theorem has further interesting consequences other than the aforementioned generalization of Commandino's theorem. It can be used to prove the following theorem about the centroid of a tetrahedron, first described in the *Mathematische Unterhaltungen* by the German [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist) Friedrich Eduard Reusch:[^o15][^o16] One may find the centroid of a tetrahedron by taking the [midpoints](wikipedia://en/437979) of two pairs of two of its opposite [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034) and connecting the corresponding midpoints through their respective midline. The intersection point of both midlines will be the centroid of the tetrahedron.

Since a tetrahedron has six edges in three opposite pairs, one obtains the following corollary:[^o15] In a tetrahedron, the three midlines corresponding to opposite edge midpoints are [concurrent](wikipedia://en/2206157), and their intersection point is the centroid of the tetrahedron.

:::

::: {#o18 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Varignon's theorem"}

### Varignon's theorem {#Varignon's_theorem}

A specific case of Reusch's theorem where all four vertices of a tetrahedron are [coplanar](wikipedia://en/1508434) and lie on a single plane, thereby degenerating into a [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278), Varignon's theorem, named after [Pierre Varignon](wikipedia://en/1193511), states the following:[^o19][^o20] Let a quadrilateral in $\mathbb{R}^2$ be given. Then the two midlines connecting opposite edge midpoints intersect in the centroid of the quadrilateral and are divided in half by it.
