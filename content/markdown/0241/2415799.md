# Giulio Carlo de' Toschi di Fagnano

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Giulio Carlo de' Toschi di Fagnano"}

**Giulio Carlo, Count Fagnano, Marquis de Toschi** (26 September 1682 — 18 May 1766)[^o12] was an Italian [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902). He was probably the first to direct attention to the theory of [elliptic integrals](wikipedia://en/9960). Fagnano’s work is considered the forerunner of the theory of [elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/69939).[^o15]p. 516

:::

## Life {#Life}

Giulio Fagnano was born to Francesco Fagnano and Camilla Bartolini in [Senigallia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senigallia) (at the time spelled "Sinigaglia") on 26 September 1682.[^n1]

He received his higher education at the [Collegio Clementino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collegio_Clementino) in Rome, where he excelled in his studies.[^o12] However, despite his later mathematical achievements, he initially displayed an aversion to mathematics during his time at the college. It was only after completing his studies there that he turned to mathematics, mastering it independently. In 1705 he married Francesca Conciatti, by whom he had twelve children.[^o12] One, [Giovanni Fagnano](wikipedia://en/42789700), was also well-known as a mathematician. Another of Fagnano's children became a Benedictine nun.[^o12]

His knowledge of architecture caused [Benedict XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedict_XIV) to call him to Rome in 1748 to examine the [cupola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupola) of [St. Peter's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Peter's_Basilica), which was rapidly disintegrating. As a reward for his services, [Pope Benedict XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XIV) commissioned the publication of his [complete works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_works).[^n2]

In 1721, Fagnano was made a [count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count) by [Louis XV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XV);[^o14] in 1723, he was appointed *[gonfaloniere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonfaloniere)* of Senigallia[^o14] and elected to the [Royal Society of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_London);[^o14] He was also a member to the [Prussian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_Academy_of_Sciences) and was proposed for the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences) in 1766 but died before he could be elected.[^o14] Fagnano died in his hometown on 18 May 1766, and was buried in the church of Santa Maria Maddalena, Senigallia.[^n2]

## Mathematical work {#Mathematical_work}

Trained as a philosopher and theologian, Fagnano taught himself mathematics after his college studies.[^o12] He was chiefly interested in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) and in the study of [algebraic equations](wikipedia://en/404001). Most of his important researches were published in the [scientific journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_journal) *Giornale de' Letterati d'Italia*.[^o12]

*Image: Produzioni Matematiche-Fagnani (cropped).jpg.* *Produzioni matematiche*, 1750

Fagnano is best known for investigations on the length and division of arcs of certain curves, especially the [lemniscate](wikipedia://en/250908) (cf. [lemniscate elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/3754835)); this seems also to have been in his own estimation his most important work, since he had the figure of the lemniscate with the inscription "Multifariam divisa atque dimensa Deo veritatis gloria" engraved on the title-page of his *Produzioni Matematiche*,[^o13] which he published in two volumes (Pesaro, 1750), and dedicated to [Pope Benedict XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XIV). The same figure and words "Deo veritatis gloria" also appear on his tomb.

Failing to [rectify](wikipedia://en/1898401) the [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) or [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052), Fagnano attempted to determine [arcs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_%28geometry%29) whose difference is rectifiable. The word "rectifiable" meant at that time that the length can be found explicitly, which is different from its modern meaning. He also pointed out the remarkable analogy existing between the [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532) which represent the arc of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) and the arc of a lemniscate. He also proved the formula

$$\pi = 2i\log{1-i\over 1+i}$$

where $i$ stands for $\sqrt{-1}$.

Fagnano's works on elliptical functions gained him international reputation. In 1751, [Maupertuis](wikipedia://en/196221) submitted his work to the [German Academy of Sciences at Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Academy_of_Sciences_at_Berlin) for consideration of Fagnano as a foreign member. [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) was assigned the task of evaluating the quality of Fagnano's works. He was so impressed by Fagnano's discoveries in the theory of the lemniscate that he immediately commenced his own research in the same direction.[^o14] Euler developed and generalised Fagnano's methods and results, in particular giving the famous addition formula for elliptic integrals.[^o14] Fagnano corresponded with the leading mathematicians of the day, most notably [Luigi Guido Grandi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luigi_Guido_Grandi), [Jacopo Riccati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacopo_Riccati), Thomas Leseur, and [François Jacquier](wikipedia://en/10915235); his work was highly praised by [Bernard Le Bovier de Fontenelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Le_Bovier_de_Fontenelle), the permanent secretary of the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences).[^o15]p. 515 [Joseph-Louis Lagrange](wikipedia://en/87793) dedicated his first scientific publication to him.[^o8]
