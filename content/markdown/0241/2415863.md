# Differential graded algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Differential graded algebra"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) – particularly in [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410), [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) – a **differential graded algebra** (or **DGA**, or **DG algebra**) is an [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/18716923) often used to capture information about a [topological](wikipedia://en/30450) or [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) space. Explicitly, a differential graded algebra is a [graded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded_algebra) [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) with a [chain complex](wikipedia://en/138404) structure that is compatible with the [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) structure.

:::

In geometry, the [de Rham algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_rham_cohomology) of [differential forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_forms) on a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) has the structure of a differential graded algebra, and it encodes the [de Rham cohomology](wikipedia://en/221530) of the manifold. In algebraic topology, the [singular cochains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_cohomology) of a topological [space form](wikipedia://en/1087009) a DGA encoding the [singular cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060#Singular_cohomology). Moreover, American mathematician [Dennis Sullivan](wikipedia://en/7349608) developed a DGA to encode the [rational homotopy type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_homotopy_type) of topological spaces.[^n1]

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Let $A_\bullet = \bigoplus\nolimits_{i \in \mathbb{Z}} A_i$ be a $\mathbb{Z}$-[graded algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded_algebra), with product $\cdot$, equipped with a map $d\colon A_\bullet \to A_\bullet$ of [degree](wikipedia://en/1155734#Homomorphisms) $-1$ (homologically graded) or degree $+1$ (cohomologically graded). We say that $(A_\bullet,d,\cdot)$ is a **differential graded algebra** if $d$ is a differential, giving $A_\bullet$ the structure of a [chain complex](wikipedia://en/138404) or [cochain complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochain_complex) (depending on the degree), and satisfies a graded [Leibniz rule](wikipedia://en/248988). In what follows, we will denote the "degree" of a [homogeneous element](wikipedia://en/1155734#Integer_gradation) $a\in A_i$ by $|a| = i$. Explicitly, the map $d$ satisfies the conditions[^n2] $d \circ d = 0$, often written $d^2=0$.; $d(a \cdot b) = (da) \cdot b + (-1)^{|a|}a \cdot (db)$. Often one omits the differential and multiplication and simply writes $A_\bullet$ or $A$ to refer to the DGA $(A_\bullet,d,\cdot)$.

A [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) $f: A_\bullet \to B_\bullet$ between [graded vector spaces](wikipedia://en/1155734) is said to be of **degree *n*** if $f(A_i) \subseteq B_{i+n}$ for all $i$. When considering (co)chain complexes, we restrict our attention to [chain maps](wikipedia://en/138404#Chain_maps), that is, maps of degree 0 that commute with the differentials $f \circ d_A = d_B \circ f$. The morphisms in the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of DGAs are chain maps that are also [algebra homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/191788#Algebra_homomorphisms).

### Categorical definition {#Categorical_definition}

One can also define DGAs more abstractly using [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869). There is a [category of chain complexes](wikipedia://en/138404#Category_of_chain_complexes) over a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$, often denoted $\operatorname{Ch}_R$, whose objects are chain complexes and whose morphisms are chain maps. We define the [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) of chain complexes $(V,d_V)$ and $(W,d_W)$ by[^n3]

$$(V \otimes W)_n = \bigoplus_{i+j=n} V_i \otimes_R W_j$$

with differential

$$d (v \otimes w) = (d_V v) \otimes w + (-1)^{|v|} v \otimes (d_W w)$$

This operation makes $\operatorname{Ch}_R$ into a [symmetric monoidal category](wikipedia://en/3865249). Then, we can equivalently define a differential graded algebra as a [monoid object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoid_object) in $\operatorname{Ch}_R$. Heuristically, it is an object in $\operatorname{Ch}_R$ with an associative and unital multiplication.

### Homology and cohomology {#Homology_and_cohomology}

Associated to any chain complex $(A_\bullet,d)$ is its [homology](wikipedia://en/142432). Since $d \circ d = 0$, it follows that $\operatorname{im}(d:A_{i+1} \to A_i)$ is a [subobject](wikipedia://en/540476) of $\operatorname{ker}(d:A_i \to A_{i-1})$. Thus, we can form the quotient

$$H_i(A_\bullet) = \operatorname{ker}(d:A_i \to A_{i-1}) / \operatorname{im}(d:A_{i+1} \to A_i)$$

This is called the $i$th homology group, and all together they form a graded vector space $H_\bullet(A)$. In fact, the homology groups form a DGA with zero differential. Analogously, one can define the [cohomology groups](wikipedia://en/194060) of a cochain complex, which also form a graded algebra with zero differential.

Every chain map $f: (A_\bullet,d_A) \to (B_\bullet,d_B)$ of complexes induces a map on (co)homology, often denoted $f_*: H_\bullet(A) \to H_\bullet(B)$ (respectively $f^*: H^\bullet(B) \to H^\bullet(A)$). If this induced map is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) on all (co)homology groups, the map $f$ is called a [quasi-isomorphism](wikipedia://en/3653039). In many contexts, this is the natural notion of equivalence one uses for (co)chain complexes. We say a morphism of DGAs is a quasi-isomorphism if the chain map on the underlying (co)chain complexes is.

:::

## Properties of DGAs {#Properties_of_DGAs}

### Commutative differential graded algebras {#Commutative_differential_graded_algebras}

A **commutative differential graded algebra** (or **CDGA**) is a differential graded algebra, $(A_\bullet, d, \cdot)$, which satisfies a graded version of commutativity. Namely,

$$a \cdot b = (-1)^{|a| |b|} b \cdot a$$

for homogeneous elements $a \in A_i, b \in A_j$. Many of the DGAs commonly encountered in math happen to be CDGAs, like the de Rham algebra of differential forms.

### Differential graded Lie algebras {#Differential_graded_Lie_algebras}

A **[differential graded Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/39908921)** (or **DGLA**) is a differential graded analogue of a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944). That is, it is a differential graded vector space, $(L_\bullet, d)$, together with an operation $[,]: L_i \otimes L_j \to L_{i+j}$, satisfying the following graded analogues of the Lie algebra axioms. Graded skew-symmetry: $[x,y] = -(-1)^{|x| |y|} [y,x]$ for homogeneous elements $x\in L_i, y \in L_j$.; Graded [Jacobi identity](wikipedia://en/294370): $(-1)^{|x||z|}[x,[y,z]] +(-1)^{|y||x|}[y,[z,x]] +(-1)^{|z||y|}[z,[x,y]] = 0$.; Graded [Leibniz rule](wikipedia://en/248988): $d [x,y] = [d x,y] + (-1)^{|x|}[x, d y]$.

An example of a DGLA is the de Rham algebra $\Omega^\bullet(M)$ tensored with a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) $\mathfrak{g}$, with the bracket given by the [exterior product](wikipedia://en/221519#Exterior_product) of the [differential forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_forms) and Lie bracket; elements of this DGLA are known as [Lie algebra–valued differential forms](wikipedia://en/2677220). DGLAs also arise frequently in the study of [deformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_theory) of algebraic structures where, over a field of characteristic 0, "nice" deformation problems are described by the space of [Maurer-Cartan elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurer-Cartan_form) of some suitable DGLA.[^n4]

### Formal DGAs {#Formal_DGAs}

A (co)chain complex $C_\bullet$ is called **formal** if there is a chain map to its (co)homology $H_\bullet(C_\bullet)$ (respectively $H^\bullet(C_\bullet)$), thought of as a complex with 0 differential, that is a quasi-isomorphism. We say that a DGA $A$ is **formal** if there exists a morphism of DGAs $A \to H_\bullet(A)$ (respectively $A \to H^\bullet(A)$) that is a quasi-isomorphism.[^n5] This notion is important, for instance, when one wants to consider quasi-isomorphic chain complexes or DGAs as being equivalent, as in the [derived category](wikipedia://en/985897).

## Examples {#Examples}

### Trivial DGAs {#Trivial_DGAs}

Notice that any [graded algebra](wikipedia://en/103094) $A=\bigoplus\nolimits_i A_i$ has the structure of a DGA with trivial differential, i.e., $d=0$. In particular, as noted above, the (co)homology of any DGA forms a trivial DGA, since it is a graded algebra.

### The de-Rham algebra {#The_de-Rham_algebra}

Let $M$ be a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470). Then, the [differential forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_forms) on $M$, denoted by $\Omega^\bullet(M)$, naturally have the structure of a (cohomologically graded) DGA. The graded vector space is $\Omega^\bullet(M)$, where the grading is given by form degree. This vector space has a product, given by the [exterior product](wikipedia://en/221519#Exterior_product), which makes it into a graded algebra. Finally, the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) $d: \Omega^i(M) \to \Omega^{i+1}(M)$ satisfies $d^2=0$ and the graded Leibniz rule. In fact, the exterior product is graded-commutative, which makes the de Rham algebra an example of a CDGA.[^n6]

### Singular cochains {#Singular_cochains}

Let $X$ be a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450). Recall that we can associate to $X$ its complex of [singular cochains](wikipedia://en/194060#Singular_Cohomology) with coefficients in a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$, denoted $(C^\bullet(X;R),d)$, whose cohomology is the [singular cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060#Singular_Cohomology) of $X$. On $C^\bullet(X;R)$, one can define the [cup product](wikipedia://en/848684) of cochains, which gives this cochain complex the structure of a DGA.[^n7] In the case where $X$ is a smooth manifold and $R=\mathbb{R}$, the [de Rham theorem](wikipedia://en/77429163) states that the singular cohomology is isomorphic to the [de Rham cohomology](wikipedia://en/221530) and, moreover, the cup product and exterior product of differential forms induce the same operation on cohomology.

Note, however, that while the cup product induces a graded-commutative operation on cohomology, it is not graded commutative directly on cochains. This is an important distinction, and the failure of a DGA to be commutative is referred to as the "commutative cochain problem". This problem is important because if, for any topological space $X$, one can associate a **commutative** DGA whose cohomology is the singular cohomology of $X$ over $R$, then this CDGA determines the $R$-homotopy type of $X$.[^n7]

### The free DGA {#The_free_DGA}

Let $V$ be a (non-graded) vector space over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $k$. The [tensor algebra](wikipedia://en/363628) $T(V)$ is defined to be the graded algebra

$$T(V) = \bigoplus_{i\geq 0} T^i(V) = \bigoplus_{i \geq 0} V^{\otimes i}$$

where, by convention, we take $T^0(V) = k$. This vector space can be made into a graded algebra with the multiplication $T^i(V) \otimes T^j(V) \to T^{i+j}(V)$ given by the tensor product $\otimes$. This is the [free algebra](wikipedia://en/494669) on $V$, and can be thought of as the algebra of all non-commuting polynomials in the elements of $V$.

One can give the tensor algebra the structure of a DGA as follows. Let $f: V \to k$ be any linear map. Then, this extends uniquely to a [derivation](wikipedia://en/897658) of $T(V)$ of degree $-1$ (homologically graded) by the formula[^n8]

$$d_f(v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n (-1)^{i-1} v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes f(v_i) \otimes \cdots \otimes v_n$$

One can think of the minus signs on the right-hand side as coming from "jumping" the map $f$ over the elements $v_1, \ldots, v_{i-1}$, which are all of degree 1 in $T(V)$. This is commonly referred to as the [Koszul sign rule](wikipedia://en/519992).

One can extend this construction to differential graded vector spaces. Let $(V_\bullet,d_V)$ be a differential graded vector space, i.e., $d_V: V_i \to V_{i-1}$ and $d^2=0$. Here we work with a homologically graded DG vector space, but this construction works equally well for a cohomologically graded one. Then, we can endow the tensor algebra $T(V)$ with a DGA structure which extends the DG structure on $V$. The differential is given by

$$d(v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n (-1)^{|v_1|+\ldots+|v_{i-1}|} v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes d_V(v_i) \otimes \cdots \otimes v_n$$

This is similar to the previous case, except that now the elements of $V$ can have different degrees, and $T(V)$ is no longer graded by the number of tensor products but instead by the sum of the degrees of the elements of $V$, i.e., $|v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_n| = |v_1| + \ldots + |v_n|$.[^n9]

### The free CDGA {#The_free_CDGA}

Similar to the previous case, one can also construct the free CDGA. Given a graded vector space $V_\bullet$, we define the free graded commutative algebra on it by

$$S(V) = \operatorname{Sym}\left( \bigoplus_{i=2k} V_i \right) \otimes \bigwedge \left( \bigoplus_{i=2k+1} V_i \right)$$

where $\operatorname{Sym}$ denotes the [symmetric algebra](wikipedia://en/654098) and $\bigwedge$ denotes the [exterior algebra](wikipedia://en/221537). If we begin with a DG vector space $(V_\bullet, d)$ (either homologically or cohomologically graded), then we can extend $d$ to $S(V)$ such that $(S(V),d)$ is a CDGA in a unique way.[^n10]

:::

## Models for DGAs {#Models_for_DGAs}

As mentioned previously, oftentimes one is most interested in the (co)homology of a DGA. As such, the specific (co)chain complex we use is less important, as long as it has the right (co)homology. Given a DGA $A$, we say that another DGA $M$ is a **model** for $A$ if it comes with a [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) DGA morphism $p : M \to A$ that is a quasi-isomorphism.[^n11]

### Minimal models {#Minimal_models}

Since one could form arbitrarily large (co)chain complexes with the same cohomology, it is useful to consider the "smallest" possible model of a DGA. We say that a DGA $(A,d,\cdot)$ is **minimal** if it satisfies the following conditions.[^n12] It is free as a graded algebra, i.e., $A \cong T(V)$ for some graded vector space $V$.; The differential satisfies $d(A) \subseteq A^+ \cdot A^+$, where $A^+ = \bigoplus\nolimits_{i>0} A_i$ consists of the positive degree parts of $A$. Note that some conventions, often used in algebraic topology, additionally require that $A$ be **simply connected**, which means that $A^0=k$ and $A^1=0$.[^n12] This condition on the 0th and 1st degree components of $A$ mirror the (co)homology groups of a [simply connected space](wikipedia://en/523879).

Finally, we say that $M$ is a **minimal model** for $A$ if it is both minimal and a model for $A$. The fundamental theorem of minimal models[^n11] states that if $A$ is simply connected then it admits a minimal model, and that if a minimal model exists it is unique up to (non-unique) isomorphism.[^n13]

### The Sullivan minimal model {#The_Sullivan_minimal_model}

Minimal models were used with great success by Dennis Sullivan in his work on [rational homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/13998981). Given a [simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/252686) $X$, one can define a rational analogue of the (real) de Rham algebra: the DGA $A_{PL}(X)$ of "piecewise polynomial" differential forms with $\mathbb{Q}$-coefficients. Then, $A_{PL}(X)$ has the structure of a CDGA over the field $\mathbb{Q}$, and in fact the cohomology is isomorphic to the singular cohomology of $X$.[^n14] In particular, if $X$ is a simply connected topological space then $A_{PL}(X)$ is simply connected as a DGA, thus there exists a minimal model.

Moreover, since $A_{PL}(X)$ is a CDGA whose cohomology is the singular cohomology of $X$ with $\mathbb{Q}$-coefficients, it is a solution to the commutative cochain problem. Thus, if $X$ is a simply connected [CW complex](wikipedia://en/353022) with finite dimensional rational homology groups, the minimal model of the CDGA $A_{PL}(X)$ captures entirely the rational homotopy type of $X$.[^n15]
