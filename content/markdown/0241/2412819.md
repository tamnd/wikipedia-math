# Ramanujan–Nagell equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ramanujan–Nagell equation"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), the **Ramanujan–Nagell equation** is an [equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equation_%28mathematics%29) between a [square number](wikipedia://en/213919) and a number that is seven less than a [power of two](wikipedia://en/376948). It is an example of an [exponential Diophantine equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_Diophantine_equation), an equation to be solved in integers where one of the variables appears as an [exponent](wikipedia://en/99491).

:::

The equation is named after [Srinivasa Ramanujan](wikipedia://en/47717), who conjectured that it has only five [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) solutions, and after [Trygve Nagell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trygve_Nagell), who proved the conjecture. It implies non-existence of [perfect binary codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_code) with the minimum [Hamming distance](wikipedia://en/41227) 5 or 6.

## Equation and solution {#Equation_and_solution}

The equation is

$$2^n-7=x^2 \,$$

and solutions in [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) *n* and *x* exist just when *n* = 3, 4, 5, 7 and 15 .

This was conjectured in 1913 by Indian [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Srinivasa Ramanujan](wikipedia://en/47717), proposed independently in 1943 by the Norwegian mathematician [Wilhelm Ljunggren](wikipedia://en/34115342), and proved in 1948 by the Norwegian mathematician [Trygve Nagell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trygve_Nagell). The values of *n* correspond to the values of *x* as:-

*x* = 1, 3, 5, 11 and 181 .[^n1]

## Triangular Mersenne numbers {#Triangular_Mersenne_numbers}

The problem of finding all numbers of the form 2<sup>*b*</sup> − 1 ([Mersenne numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_number)) which are [triangular](wikipedia://en/213915) is equivalent:

$$\begin{aligned} & \ 2^b-1 = \frac{y(y+1)}{2} \\[2pt] \Longleftrightarrow & \ 8(2^b-1) = 4y(y+1) \\ \Longleftrightarrow & \ 2^{b+3}-8 = 4y^2+4y \\ \Longleftrightarrow & \ 2^{b+3}-7 = 4y^2+4y+1 \\ \Longleftrightarrow & \ 2^{b+3}-7 = (2y+1)^2 \end{aligned}$$

The values of *b* are just those of *n* − 3, and the corresponding triangular Mersenne numbers (also known as **Ramanujan–Nagell numbers**) are:

$$\frac{y(y+1)}{2} = \frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{8}$$

for *x* = 1, 3, 5, 11 and 181, giving 0, 1, 3, 15, 4095 and no more .

:::

## Equations of Ramanujan–Nagell type {#Equations_of_Ramanujan–Nagell_type}

An equation of the form

$$x^2 + D = A B^n$$

for fixed *D*, *A*, *B* and variable *x*, *n* is said to be of *Ramanujan–Nagell type*. The result of [Siegel](wikipedia://en/716602)[^n2] implies that the number of solutions in each case is finite.[^n3] By representing $n = 3m + r$ with $r\in\{0,1,2\}$ and $B^n = B^r y^3$ with $y=B^m$, the equation of Ramanujan–Nagell type is reduced to three [Mordell curves](wikipedia://en/3132697) (indexed by $r$), each of which has a finite number of integer solutions:

$$r=0:\qquad (Ax)^2 = (Ay)^3 - A^2D,$$

$$r=1:\qquad (ABx)^2 = (ABy)^3 - A^2B^2D,$$

$$r=2:\qquad (AB^2x)^2 = (AB^2y)^3 - A^2B^4D.$$

The equation with $A=1,\ B=2,\ D>0$ has at most two solutions, except in the case $D=7$ corresponding to the Ramanujan–Nagell equation. This does not hold for $D<0$, such as $D=-17$, where $x^2 - 17 = 2^n$ has the four solutions $(x,n) = (5,3),(7,5),(9,6),(23,9)$. In general, if $D=-(4^k-3\cdot2^{k+1}+1)$ for an integer $k\geqslant3$ there are at least the four solutions

$$(x,n) = \begin{cases}(2^k-3,3)\\(2^k-1,k+2)\\(2^k+1,k+3)\\(3\cdot2^k-1,2k+3)\end{cases}$$

and these are the only four if $D>-10^{12}$.[^n4] There are infinitely many values of *D* for which there are exactly two solutions, including $D = 2^m - 1$.[^n1]

## Equations of Lebesgue–Nagell type {#Equations_of_Lebesgue–Nagell_type}

An equation of the form

$$x^2 + D = A y^n$$

for fixed *D*, *A* and variable *x*, *y*, *n*, where *n* > 2, is said to be of *Lebesgue–Nagell type*. This is named after [Victor-Amédée Lebesgue](wikipedia://en/43386105), who proved that the equation

$$x^2 + 1 = y^n$$

has no nontrivial solutions.[^n5]

Results of Shorey and [Tijdeman](wikipedia://en/9006087)[^n6] imply that the number of solutions in each case is finite.[^n7] Bugeaud, Mignotte and Siksek[^n8] solved equations of this type with *A* = 1 and 1 ≤ *D* ≤ 100. In particular, the following generalization of the Ramanujan–Nagell equation:

$$y^n=x^2+7 \,$$

has positive integer solutions only when *x* = 1, 3, 5, 11, or 181.

:::
