# Einstein problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Einstein problem"}

*Image: Aperiodic monotile tiling.png.* Aperiodic tiling with "Tile(1,1)", a type of the Spectre tiles. The tiles are colored according to their rotational [orientation](wikipedia://en/2544098) modulo 60 degrees. (Smith, Myers, Kaplan, and Goodman-Strauss)

In plane [discrete geometry](wikipedia://en/386468), the **einstein problem** asks about the existence of a single [prototile](wikipedia://en/39372) that by itself forms an [aperiodic set of prototiles](wikipedia://en/41287104); that is, a shape that can [tessellate](wikipedia://en/321671) space but only in a [nonperiodic](wikipedia://en/868145) way. Such a shape is called an **einstein**, a [word play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_play) on *ein Stein*, German for "one stone", and physicist [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736).[^o2]

:::

Several variants of the problem, depending on the particular definitions of nonperiodicity and the specifications of what sets may qualify as tiles and what types of matching rules are permitted, were solved beginning in the 1990s. The strictest version of the problem was solved in 2023, after an initial discovery in 2022.

The einstein problem can be seen as a natural extension of the second part of [Hilbert's eighteenth problem](wikipedia://en/2336224), which asks for a single [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) that tiles [Euclidean 3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_3-space), but such that no tessellation by this polyhedron is [isohedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isohedral).[^o3] Such [anisohedral tiles](wikipedia://en/13180391) were found by [Karl Reinhardt](wikipedia://en/35727144) in 1928, but these anisohedral tiles all tile space periodically.

## Partial solutions {#Partial_solutions}

In 1988, Peter Schmitt discovered a single aperiodic prototile in three-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). While no tiling by this prototile admits a [translation](wikipedia://en/204682) as a symmetry, some have a [screw symmetry](wikipedia://en/3656192). The screw operation involves a combination of a translation and a rotation through an irrational multiple of $\pi$, so no number of repeated operations ever yield a pure translation. This construction was subsequently extended by [John Horton Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) and Ludwig Danzer to a [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) aperiodic prototile, the [Schmitt–Conway–Danzer tile](wikipedia://en/1177963#Schmitt–Conway–Danzer_biprism). The presence of the screw symmetry resulted in a reevaluation of the requirements for non-periodicity.[^o5] [Chaim Goodman-Strauss](wikipedia://en/56181508) suggested that a tiling be considered *strongly aperiodic* if it admits no [infinite cyclic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_cyclic_group) of [Euclidean motions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_motion) as symmetries, and that only tile sets which enforce strong aperiodicity be called strongly aperiodic, while other sets are to be called *weakly aperiodic*.[^o6]

*Image: Socolar-Taylor tile.svg.* The [Socolar–Taylor tile](wikipedia://en/39570044) was proposed in 2010 as a solution to the einstein problem, but this tile is not a connected set.

In 1996, Petra Gummelt constructed a decorated decagonal tile and showed that when two kinds of overlaps between pairs of tiles are allowed, the tiles can cover the plane, but only non-periodically.[^o7] A tiling is usually understood to be a covering with no overlaps, and so the Gummelt tile is not considered an aperiodic prototile. An aperiodic tile set in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) that consists of just one tile (the [Socolar–Taylor tile](wikipedia://en/39570044)) was proposed in early 2010 by Joshua Socolar and Joan Taylor.[^o8] This construction requires matching rules, rules that restrict the relative orientation of two tiles and that make reference to decorations drawn on the tiles, and these rules apply to pairs of nonadjacent tiles. Alternatively, an undecorated tile with no matching rules may be constructed, but the tile is not connected. The construction can be extended to a three-dimensional, connected tile with no matching rules, but this tile allows tilings that are periodic in one direction, and so it is only weakly aperiodic. Moreover, the tile is not simply connected.

## The hat and the spectre {#The_hat_and_the_spectre}

In November 2022, the amateur mathematician [David Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Smith_%28amateur_mathematician%29) discovered a "hat"-shaped tile formed from eight copies of a 60°–90°–120°–90° [kite](wikipedia://en/84840) ([deltoidal trihexagonals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deltoidal_trihexagonal_tiling)), glued edge-to-edge, which seemed to only tile the plane aperiodically.[^o11] Smith recruited mathematician [Craig S. Kaplan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craig_S._Kaplan), and subsequently Joseph Samuel Myers and [Chaim Goodman-Strauss](wikipedia://en/56181508), and in March 2023 they posted a preprint proving that the "hat", when considered with its mirror image, forms an aperiodic prototile set.[^o12][^o13] Furthermore, the hat can be generalized to an infinite family of tiles with the same aperiodic property. This result was formally published in the journal *Combinatorial Theory* in July 2024.[^o12]

In May 2023 they (Smith, Myers, Kaplan, and Goodman-Strauss) posted a new preprint about a family of shapes related to the "hat", called "spectres", each of which can tile the plane using only rotations and translations.[^o14] Furthermore, the "spectre" is a "strictly chiral" aperiodic monotile: even with reflections, every tiling is non-periodic and uses only one [chirality](wikipedia://en/32703814) of the spectre. That is, there are no tilings of the plane that use both the spectre and its mirror.

In 2023, a public contest run by the [National Museum of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Mathematics) in [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City) and the [United Kingdom Mathematics Trust](wikipedia://en/552624) in [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London) asked people to submit creative renditions of the hat einstein. Out of over 245 submissions from 32 countries, three winners were chosen and received awards at a ceremony at the [House of Commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Commons_of_the_United_Kingdom).[^o15][^o16]

## Applications {#Applications}

Einstein tiles' molecular analogs may be used to form chiral, two dimensional [quasicrystals](wikipedia://en/25350).[^o19]

Macroscopic applications in engineering include [honeycombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeycomb_structure) based on extrusions of the 2-dimensional hat tiling into 2.5 dimensional forms[^o20] and [composite materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_material) using the hat tiling to create continuous ductile networks within a hard, brittle, matrix.[^o21] The honeycombs are found to have zero [Poisson's ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson's_ratio) for a wide range of relative densities and therefore for a range of values of [elastic modulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastic_modulus). The composites offer increased stiffness alongside increased toughness when compared to similar composites with a hexagon-based network.

Applications of the infinite family of tiles, based on the hat, are also of interest. They are defined by a continuously varying geometry, which can give rise to continuously varying properties. For example, honeycombs based on this family demonstrate a continuously variable [Poisson's ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson's_ratio).[^o22]
