# Edmund Stone

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Edmund Stone"}

**Edmund Stone** (c. 1690 – March or April 1768) was an [autodidact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autodidact) [Scottish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who lived in [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London) and primarily worked as an editor of mathematical and scientific texts and translator from French and Latin into English. He is especially known for his translations of [Nicholas Bion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Bion)'s *[Mathematical Instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Construction_and_Principal_Uses_of_Mathematical_Instruments)* (1723, 1758) and the [Marquis de l'Hospital](wikipedia://en/1728613)'s [Analyse des Infiniment Petits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analyse_des_Infiniment_Petits_pour_l'Intelligence_des_Lignes_Courbes) (1730), and for his *New Mathematical Dictionary* (1726, 1743). Stone was celebrated for having risen from uneducated gardener's son to accomplished scholar.

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

The date and place of Edmund Stone's birth are unknown, as are the names of his parents, but he was probably born in [Argyllshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argyllshire), Scotland, at least a few years before 1700.[^n1] What little is known about his early life comes from a letter by [Andrew Michael Ramsay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Michael_Ramsay) to [Louis-Bertrand Castel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis-Bertrand_Castel), excerpted by the [Journal de Trévoux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_de_Trévoux).[^o5] (See #Appendix: Letter from Ramsay below for the letter and a translation.) According to this letter, Stone was the son of the gardener of [John Campbell, 2nd Duke of Argyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Campbell,_2nd_Duke_of_Argyll). He never attended any formal school, but after being taught by a servant to read at age 18, he taught himself arithmetic, geometry, Latin, and French. As the story goes, the Duke found a copy of [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627)'s [Principia](wikipedia://en/48781) in the grass in his garden, and was astonished to find it belonged to the 28-year-old Stone,[^o6] and that he understood Latin and advanced mathematics. However, Stone's description of himself having studied [mathematical instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_instruments) from the age of twelve seems inconsistent with this story.[^o7] The Duke became his patron.[^n2]

With the Duke's support, Stone moved to London c. 1720,[^o9] where he likely worked as a mathematics tutor. He published translations of the [Marquis de l'Hospital](wikipedia://en/1728613)'s posthumous book about [conic sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_sections) in 1720 and [Christopher Clavius](wikipedia://en/633645)'s translation of [Theodosius](wikipedia://en/2828050)'s *[Spherics](wikipedia://en/37098504)* in 1721. In 1723 he published a translation of [Nicholas Bion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Bion)'s [*Construction and Principal Uses of Mathematical Instruments*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Construction_and_Principal_Uses_of_Mathematical_Instruments), to which he added descriptions of the English variants of the French instruments described by Bion; this book became the standard reference about the subject in English throughout the 18th century.[^o10] In 1725 he was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society),[^o11] and from 1725 until at least 1736 he was a member of the [Board of Green Cloth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_of_Green_Cloth).[^n3] His *New Mathematical Dictionary* appeared in 1726, a cheaper alternative to [John Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Harris_%28writer%29)'s *[Lexicon Technicum](wikipedia://en/780025)*.[^n4] He also translated [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s *[Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)* (1728); l'Hospital's [differential calculus](wikipedia://en/50416) book [Analyse des Infiniment Petits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analyse_des_Infiniment_Petits_pour_l'Intelligence_des_Lignes_Courbes), to which he adjoined a second part about [integral calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_calculus), as *The Method of Fluxions* (1730);[^o14] and [Isaac Barrow](wikipedia://en/97805)'s *Geometrical Lectures* (1735).

*Image: Edmund Stone (1740) cubic curves.jpg.* Two cubic curves published by Stone in 1740[^o15]

In 1736 Stone submitted a paper to the *[Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Transactions_of_the_Royal_Society)* (published 1740) about two [cubic plane curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_plane_curve) not cataloged by Isaac Newton or [James Stirling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Stirling_%28mathematician%29),[^o15] but unbeknownst to him the two had been previously published in 1731 by [François Nicole](wikipedia://en/2680315) and 1733 by [Nicolaus Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/1150998), respectively.[^n5] In 1742 Stone submitted a 21-page paper "On Sir Isaac Newton's five diverging Parabolas", which was read to the Society but apparently never published.[^o17]

In 1742, Stone resigned as a Fellow of the Royal Society, perhaps for inability to pay the small annual membership fee.[^o18] In October 1743 Stone's patron the Duke of Argyll died.[^n5] Little is known about Stone's life afterward, though he made another translation of Euclid's *Elements* in 1752, and he published a second edition of Bion's *Mathematical Instruments* in 1758, with a long appendix covering advancements of the intervening years. In a 1760 review in *[The Critical Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Critical_Review_%28newspaper%29)*, [Tobias Smollett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobias_Smollett) wrote of Stone's situation, "His abilities are universally acknowledged, his reputation unblemished, his services to the public uncontested, and yet he lives to an advanced age unrewarded, except by a mean employment that reflects dishonour on the donor".[^o20] In 1766 Stone published a contrarian polemic contesting the scientific validity of the [spherical shape of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_Earth) and suggesting contemporary evidence was insufficient to discount the possibility Earth is an irregular roundish [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470); biographers have suggested this book was the product of [cognitive decline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_decline).[^o21] Stone died in March or April 1768.[^o22]

The [1st English ed.](https://archive.org/details/b30551018_0002/) (1700) was translated by *J. R.* A. M. & R. S. S.
- 1724, *[An Essay on Perspective](https://books.google.com/books?id=jWtbAAAAQAAJ&pg=PP7)* translated from [Willem 's Gravesande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willem_'s_Gravesande)'s [Essai de perspective](https://books.google.com/books?id=ipQ5AAAAcAAJ&pg=PP7) (1711).
- 1726, *[A New Mathematical Dictionary](https://books.google.com/books?id=JWxbAAAAQAAJ&pg=PP1)*. [2nd ed. 1743](https://books.google.com/books?id=qxR67BvXiBYC&pg=PP5). - 1728, *The Elements of Physical and Geometrical Astronomy*, 2nd English ed., translated from [David Gregory](wikipedia://en/703683)'s Astronomiæ Physicæ & Geometricæ Elementa (2nd ed. 1726, [Vol. 1](https://archive.org/details/b30416036_0001/) & [Vol 2](https://archive.org/details/b30416036_0002/)). - 1730, [*The Method of Fluxions, both Direct and Inverse*](https://books.google.com/books?id=9MGb6dgwPjEC&pg=PR1), the first part translated from l'Hospital's differential calculus book [Analyse des infiniment petits](https://archive.org/details/libria_353620/) (2nd ed., 1715) \[1st ed. 1696\], and the second part on integral calculus written by Stone. - 1731, *Euclid's Elements, Vol. II. Containing the seventh, eighth, ninth, tenth, thirteenth, and fifteenth Books, with the Data.* Translated from David Gregory's [Euclidis quæ supersunt omnia](https://books.google.com/books?id=041oAAAAcAAJ&pg=PP5) (1703). ## Appendix: Letter from Ramsay {#Appendix:_Letter_from_Ramsay}

A letter from [Andrew Michael Ramsay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Michael_Ramsay) to [Louis-Bertrand Castel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis-Bertrand_Castel) was excerpted by the [Journal de Trévoux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_de_Trévoux) 1732, [pp. 109–112](https://archive.org/details/memoirespourlhis1732unse/page/103), as part of a review of Stone's *The Method of Fluxions* (1730). Here is the excerpt reproduced, along with an English translation:

Lettre de M. le C. D. R. au P. C. J. Le véritable génie surmonte tous les désavantages de la fortune, de la naissance, de l'education. M. *Stone* en est un rare exemple: né fils du Jardinier du Duc d*Argyle*, il parvint à l'âge de 18. ans, sans sçavoir lire; son pére ne sçavoit pas lui apprendre son métier de cette maniére élevée, qui rend le jardinage & l'agriculture une partie très-utile & très-noble de la Cosmographie & de la Physique. Par hazard, un domestique ayant appris au jeune *Stone* les Lettres de l'Alphabet, il n'en falut pas d'avantage pour faire éclore son génie & pour le déveloper. Il s'appliqua lui-même, il étudia, il parvint aux connoissances de la plus sublime géométrie, & du calcul, sans maître, sans conducteur, sans autre guide que le pur génie. A l'âge de 28. ans il avoit fait tous ces progrès sans être connu, & sans connoître lui-même les prodiges qui se passoient en lui. Mylord Duc d*Argyle*, qui joint à toutes les vertus militaires & à tous les sentimens d'un Héros, une connoissance universelle de tout ce qui peut orner & perfectioner l'esprit d'un homme de son rang, se promenant un jour dans son jardin, vit fur l'herbe le fameux Livre du Chevalier Newton en Latin. Il appella quelqu'un pour le ramasser & le reporter dans la Bibliothéque. Le jeune Jardinier lui dit que ce Livre lui appartenoit. *A vous*, répondit le Mylord? *Entendés-vous la Géometrie, le Latin, M. Newton?* *J'entends un peu de tout cela*, repliqua *Stone* avec un air de simplicité fondé sur l'ignorance profonde de ses propres talens, & de l'excès de son sçavoir. Mylord Duc fut très surpris: mais comme il a le goût de ces sciences, il daigna entrer en conversation avec le nouveau Géométre: il lui fit plusieurs questions, & demeura étonné de la force, de la justesse, & de la candeur de ses réponses. „Mais comment, dit le Mylord, es-tu parvenu à toutes ces connoissances? L'autre répond: un domestique m'apprit, il y a dix ans, à lire: a-t'on besoin de sçavoir autre chose que les 24. lettres pour apprendre tout ce qu'on veut„? La curiosité du Duc redouble; il soupçonne que les démarches de ce génie marveilleux étoient encore plus surprenantes que ses progrès; il s'affeoit sur un banc, & lui demande le detail de tout ce qu'il a fait pour devenir habile. „J'appris d'abord à lire, dit *Stone*, les massons travailloient alors à vôtre maison: je m'approchai d'eux un jour, & je vis que l'Architecte usoit d'une régle, d'un compas, & qu'il calculoit. Je demandai ce qu'il faisoit là, & à quoi tout cela étoit bon; & je compris qu'il y avoit une science qu'on appelloit Arithmétique. J'achetai un Livre d'Arithmetique, & je l'appris. „On m'avoit dit qu'il y en avoit une autre appellée Géométrie: j'achetai des Livres, & j'appris la Géometrie. Je vis à force de lire qu'il y avoit de beaux Livres sur ces deux sciences en Latin: j'achetai un Dictionaire, & j'appris le Latin. J'appris aussi qu'il y avoit de beaux Livres de même espéce en François: j'achetai un Dictionaire, & j'appris le François. Voila, Monseigneur, tout ce que j'ai fait, il me semble qu'on peut tout apprendre quand on sçait les 24. lettres de l'Alphabet„. Ce récit charma Mylord Duc. Il tira ce génie merveilleux de l'obscurité; & il le pourvut d'un emploi que lui laisse tout le tems de s'appliquer aux sciences. Il découvrit en lui le même génie pour la musique, pour la peinture, pour l'architecture, pour toutes les sciences que dépandent du calcul & des proportions. J'ai vû le Sr. *Stone*. C'est un homme d'une simplicité admirable. Il sçait à présent qu'il sçait: mais il n'en est pas enflé. Il est possédé d'un amour pur & desintéressé pour la Géométrie. Il ne se soucie pas de passer pour Géométre. Le bel esprit & la vanité n'ont aucune part aux travaux infinis, qu'il subit pour exceller dans ce genre. Il méprise aussi la fortune, & m'a sollicité vingt fois de prier Milord de lui donner un moindre emploi, qui ne valoit que la moitié de celui qu'il a, afin d'être plus solitaire, & moins distraît de ses études favorites. Il découvre quelquesfois, par des méthodes qui lui sont propres, les mêmes vérités que d'autres on déja trouvées. Il est charmé de voir qu'il n'en est pas l'inventeur, & que les hommes ont fait plus de progrès qu'il ne croyoit. Loin d'être plagiare, il attribuë les solutions ingénieuses & admirables, qu'il donne de certains Problémes, aux indices qu'il en trouve dans les autres, quoiqu'elles n'en découlent que par des conséquences fort éloignées, &c.
