# Backtesting

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Backtesting"}

**Backtesting** is a term used in modeling to refer to testing a [predictive model](wikipedia://en/2538775) on historical data. Backtesting is a type of [retrodiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrodiction), and a special type of [cross-validation](wikipedia://en/416612) applied to previous time period(s). In quantitative finance, backtesting is an important step before deploying algorithmic strategies in live markets.

:::

## Financial analysis {#Financial_analysis}

In [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics) and [finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance), backtesting seeks to estimate the performance of a strategy or model if it had been employed during a past period. This requires simulating past conditions with sufficient detail, which means that backtesting is limited by the need for detailed historical data, the inability to model strategies that would affect historic prices, and potential [overfitting](wikipedia://en/173332). That is, it is often possible to find a strategy that would have worked well in the past, but will not work well in the future.[^o3] Despite these limitations, backtesting provides information not available when models and strategies are tested on synthetic data.

Historically, backtesting was only performed by large institutions and professional money managers due to the expense of obtaining and using detailed datasets. However, backtesting is increasingly used on a wider basis, and independent web-based backtesting platforms have emerged.[^o4] Although the technique is widely used, it is prone to weaknesses.[^o5] [Basel financial regulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basel_Accords) require large financial institutions to backtest certain risk models.

## Hindcast {#Hindcast}

*Image: Hindcasting.jpeg.* Temporal representation of hindcasting.[^o11]

In [oceanography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanography)[^o12] and [meteorology](wikipedia://en/19904),[^o13] *backtesting* is also known as *hindcasting*: a **hindcast** is a way of testing a [mathematical model](wikipedia://en/20590); researchers enter known or closely estimated inputs for past events into the model to see how well the output matches the known results.

Hindcasting usually refers to a [numerical-model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_simulation) integration of a historical period where no observations have been [assimilated](wikipedia://en/2458875). This distinguishes a hindcast run from a [reanalysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorological_reanalysis). Oceanographic observations of [salinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salinity) and [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050) as well as observations of [surface-wave parameters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_surface_wave) such as the [significant wave height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Significant_wave_height) are much scarcer than meteorological observations, making hindcasting more common in oceanography than in meteorology. Also, since surface waves represent a forced system where the wind is the only generating force, [wave hindcasting](wikipedia://en/20260077) is often considered adequate for generating a reasonable representation of the wave [climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate) with little need for a full reanalysis. Hydrologists use hindcasting for model stream flows.[^o14]

An example of hindcasting would be entering [climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate) forcings (events that force change) into a [climate model](wikipedia://en/47513). If the hindcast showed reasonably-accurate climate response, the model would be considered successful.

The [ECMWF re-analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ECMWF_re-analysis) is an example of a combined atmospheric reanalysis coupled with a wave-model integration where no wave parameters were assimilated, making the wave part a hindcast run.
