# Unbounded operator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unbounded operator"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), more specifically [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) and [operator theory](wikipedia://en/712675), the notion of **unbounded operator** provides an abstract framework for dealing with [differential operators](wikipedia://en/263886), unbounded [observables](wikipedia://en/294248) in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), and other cases.

:::

The term "unbounded operator" can be misleading, since

- "unbounded" should sometimes be understood as "not necessarily bounded";
- "operator" should be understood as "[linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator)" (as in the case of "bounded operator");
- the domain of the operator is a [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357), not necessarily the whole space;
- this linear subspace is not necessarily [closed](wikipedia://en/47279); often (but not always) it is assumed to be [dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_%28topology%29);
- in the special case of a bounded operator, still, the domain is usually assumed to be the whole space.

In contrast to [bounded operators](wikipedia://en/455961), unbounded operators on a given space do not form an [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788), nor even a linear space, because each one is defined on its own domain.

The term "operator" often means "bounded linear operator", but in the context of this article it means "unbounded operator", with the reservations made above.

## Short history {#Short_history}

The theory of unbounded operators developed in the late 1920s and early 1930s as part of developing a rigorous mathematical framework for [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/20598932#quantum_mechanics).[^o3] The theory's development is due to [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942)[^o4] and [Marshall Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Stone).[^o5] Von Neumann introduced using [graphs](wikipedia://en/87352) to analyze unbounded operators in 1932.[^o6]

## Definitions and basic properties {#Definitions_and_basic_properties}

Let $X, Y$ be [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989). An *unbounded operator* (or simply *operator*) $T : D(T) \to Y$ is a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) $T$ from a linear subspace $D(T) \subseteq X$—the domain of $T$—to the space $Y$.[^o8] Contrary to the usual convention, $T$ may not be defined on the whole space $X$.

The *graph* $\Gamma(T)$ of an operator $T$ from $X$ to $Y$ is a linear subspace of the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899#Direct_sum_of_Hilbert_spaces) $X \oplus Y$, defined as the set of all pairs $(x, Tx)$, where $x$ runs over the domain of $T$ . An operator $T$ is said to be [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_operator) if its graph $\Gamma(T)$ is a [closed set](wikipedia://en/47279).[^o9] Explicitly, this means that for every sequence $\{x_{n}\}$ of points from the domain of $T$ such that $x_{n} \to x$ and $Tx_{n} \to y$, it holds that $x$ belongs to the domain of $T$ and $Tx = y$.[^o9] The closedness can also be formulated in terms of the *graph norm*: an operator $T$ is closed if and only if its domain $D(T)$ is a [complete space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) with respect to the norm:[^o10]

$$\|x\|_T = \sqrt{ \|x\|^2 + \|Tx\|^2 }.$$

An operator $T$ is said to be [densely defined](wikipedia://en/13510193) if its domain is [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in $X$.[^o8] This also includes operators defined on the entire space $X$, since the whole space is dense in itself. The denseness of the domain is necessary and sufficient for the existence of the adjoint (if $X$ and $Y$ are Hilbert spaces) and the transpose; see the sections below.

If $T : D(T) \to Y$ is closed, densely defined and [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_operator) on its domain, then its domain is all of $X$.[^o11]

## Example {#Example}

Let $C([0, 1])$ denote the space of continuous functions on the unit interval, and let $C^{1}([0, 1])$ denote the space of continuously differentiable functions on the unit interval. We equip $C([0,1])$ with the [supremum norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum_norm), $\|\cdot\|_{\infty}$, making it a Banach space. Define the classical differentiation operator $\tfrac{d}{dx} : C^{1}([0, 1]) \to C([0, 1])$ by the usual formula:

$$\left (\frac{d}{dx}f \right )(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}, \qquad \forall x \in [0, 1].$$

Every differentiable function is continuous, so $C^{1}([0, 1]) \subseteq C([0, 1])$. We claim that $\tfrac{d}{dx} : C([0, 1]) \to C([0, 1])$ is a well-defined unbounded operator, with domain $C^{1}([0, 1])$. For this, we need to show that $\frac{d}{dx}$ is linear and then, for example, exhibit some $\{f_n\}_n \subset C^1([0,1])$ such that $\|f_n\|_\infty=1$ and $\sup_n \|\frac{d}{dx} f_n\|_\infty=+\infty$.

This is a linear operator, since a linear combination $a\,f\, + bg$ of two continuously differentiable functions $\,f\,, g$ is also continuously differentiable, and

$$\left (\tfrac{d}{dx} \right )(af+bg)= a \left (\tfrac{d}{dx} f \right ) + b \left (\tfrac{d}{dx} g \right ).$$

The operator is not bounded. For example,

$$\begin{cases} f_n : [0, 1] \to [-1, 1] \\ f_n(x) = \sin (2\pi n x) \end{cases}$$

satisfy

$$\left \|f_n \right \|_{\infty} = 1,$$

but

$$\left \| \left (\tfrac{d}{dx} f_n \right ) \right \|_{\infty} = 2\pi n \to \infty$$

as $n\to\infty$.

The operator is densely defined (which can be shown by the Weierstrass approximation theorem, since the set of polynomial functions on \[0,1\] is contained in $C^{1}([0, 1])$, while also being dense in $C([0, 1])$) and closed.

The same operator can be treated as an operator $Z \to Z$ for many choices of Banach space $Z$ and not be bounded between any of them. At the same time, it can be bounded as an operator $X \to Y$ for other pairs of Banach spaces $X, Y$, and also as operator $Z \to Z$ for some topological vector spaces $Z$. As an example let $I \subset \mathbf{R}$ be an open interval and consider

$$\frac{d}{dx} : \left (C^1 (I), \|\cdot \|_{C^1} \right ) \to \left ( C (I), \| \cdot \|_{\infty} \right),$$

where:

$$\| f \|_{C^1} = \| f \|_{\infty} + \| f' \|_{\infty}.$$

:::

## Adjoint {#Adjoint}

The adjoint of an unbounded operator can be defined in two equivalent ways. Let $T : D(T) \subseteq H_1 \to H_2$ be an unbounded operator between Hilbert spaces.

First, it can be defined in a way analogous to how one defines the adjoint of a bounded operator. Namely, the adjoint $T^* : D\left(T^*\right) \subseteq H_2 \to H_1$ of $T$ is defined as an operator with the property:

$$\langle Tx \mid y \rangle_2 = \left \langle x \mid T^*y \right \rangle_1, \qquad x \in D(T).$$

More precisely, $T^* y$ is defined in the following way. If $y \in H_2$ is such that $x \mapsto \langle Tx \mid y \rangle$ is a continuous linear functional on the domain of $T$, then $y$ is declared to be an element of $D\left(T^*\right),$ and after extending the linear functional to the whole space via the [Hahn–Banach theorem](wikipedia://en/13860), it is possible to find some $z$ in $H_1$ such that

$$\langle Tx \mid y \rangle_2 = \langle x \mid z \rangle_1, \qquad x \in D(T),$$

since [Riesz representation theorem](wikipedia://en/26452) allows the continuous dual of the Hilbert space $H_1$ to be identified with the set of linear functionals given by the inner product. This vector $z$ is uniquely determined by $y$ if and only if the linear functional $x \mapsto \langle Tx \mid y \rangle$ is densely defined; or equivalently, if $T$ is densely defined. Finally, letting $T^* y = z$ completes the construction of $T^*,$ which is necessarily a linear map. The adjoint $T^* y$ exists if and only if $T$ is densely defined.

By definition, the domain of $T^*$ consists of elements $y$ in $H_2$ such that $x \mapsto \langle Tx \mid y \rangle$ is continuous on the domain of $T$. Consequently, the domain of $T^*$ could be anything; it could be trivial (that is, contains only zero).[^o15] It may happen that the domain of $T^*$ is a closed [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) and $T^*$ vanishes everywhere on the domain.[^o16][^o17] Thus, boundedness of $T^*$ on its domain does not imply boundedness of $T$. On the other hand, if $T^*$ is defined on the whole space then $T$ is bounded on its domain and therefore can be extended by continuity to a bounded operator on the whole space.[^o18] If the domain of $T^*$ is dense, then it has its adjoint $T^{**}.$[^o20] A closed densely defined operator $T$ is bounded if and only if $T^*$ is bounded.[^o19]

The other equivalent definition of the adjoint can be obtained by noticing a general fact. Define a linear operator $J$ as follows:[^o20]

$$\begin{cases} J: H_1 \oplus H_2 \to H_2 \oplus H_1 \\ J(x \oplus y) = -y \oplus x \end{cases}$$

Since $J$ is an isometric surjection, it is unitary. Hence: $J(\Gamma(T))^{\bot}$ is the graph of some operator $S$ if and only if $T$ is densely defined.[^o21] A simple calculation shows that this "some" $S$ satisfies:

$$\langle Tx \mid y \rangle_2 = \langle x \mid Sy \rangle_1,$$

for every $x$ in the domain of $T$. Thus $S$ is the adjoint of $T$.

It follows immediately from the above definition that the adjoint $T^*$ is closed.[^o20] In particular, a self-adjoint operator (meaning $T = T^*$) is closed. An operator $T$ is closed and densely defined if and only if $T^{**} = T.$[^o22]

Some well-known properties for bounded operators generalize to closed densely defined operators. The kernel of a closed operator is closed. Moreover, the kernel of a closed densely defined operator $T : H_1 \to H_2$ coincides with the orthogonal complement of the range of the adjoint. That is,[^o23]

$$\operatorname{ker}(T) = \operatorname{ran}(T^*)^\bot.$$

[von Neumann's theorem](wikipedia://en/12388866) states that $T^* T$ and $T T^*$ are self-adjoint, and that $I + T^* T$ and $I + T T^*$ both have bounded inverses.[^o24] If $T^*$ has trivial kernel, $T$ has dense range (by the above identity.) Moreover:

$T$ is surjective if and only if there is a $K > 0$ such that $\|f\|_2 \leq K \left\|T^* f\right\|_1$ for all $f$ in $D\left(T^*\right).$[^o25] (This is essentially a variant of the so-called [closed range theorem](wikipedia://en/23483982).) In particular, $T$ has closed range if and only if $T^*$ has closed range.

In contrast to the bounded case, it is not necessary that $(T S)^* = S^* T^*,$ since, for example, it is even possible that $(T S)^*$ does not exist. This is, however, the case if, for example, $T$ is bounded.[^o26]

A densely defined, closed operator $T$ is called *[normal](wikipedia://en/190167)* if it satisfies the following equivalent conditions:[^o27]

- $$T^* T = T T^*;$$
- the domain of $T$ is equal to the domain of $T^*,$ and $\|T x\| = \left\|T^* x\right\|$ for every $x$ in this domain;
- there exist self-adjoint operators $A, B$ such that $T = A + i B,$$T^* = A - i B,$ and $\|T x\|^2 = \|A x\|^2 + \|B x\|^2$ for every $x$ in the domain of $T$.

Every self-adjoint operator is normal.

## Transpose {#Transpose}

Let $T : B_1 \to B_2$ be an operator between Banach spaces. Then the *[transpose](wikipedia://en/173844)* (or *dual*) ${}^t T: {B_2}^* \to {B_1}^*$ of $T$ is the linear operator satisfying:

$$\langle T x, y' \rangle = \langle x, \left({}^t T\right) y' \rangle$$

for all $x \in B_1$ and $y \in B_2^*.$ Here, we used the notation: $\langle x, x' \rangle = x'(x).$[^o29]

The necessary and sufficient condition for the transpose of $T$ to exist is that $T$ is densely defined (for essentially the same reason as to adjoints, as discussed above.)

For any Hilbert space $H,$ there is the anti-linear isomorphism:

$$J: H^* \to H$$

given by $J f = y$ where $f(x) = \langle x \mid y \rangle_H, (x \in H).$ Through this isomorphism, the transpose ${}^t T$ relates to the adjoint $T^*$ in the following way:[^o30]

$$T^* = J_1 \left({}^t T\right) J_2^{-1},$$

where $J_j: H_j^* \to H_j$. (For the finite-dimensional case, this corresponds to the fact that the adjoint of a matrix is its conjugate transpose.) Note that this gives the definition of adjoint in terms of a transpose.

## Closed linear operators {#Closed_linear_operators}

Closed linear operators are a class of [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) on [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989). They are more general than [bounded operators](wikipedia://en/455961), and therefore not necessarily [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122), but they still retain nice enough properties that one can define the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137) and (with certain assumptions) [functional calculus](wikipedia://en/965348) for such operators. Many important linear operators which fail to be bounded turn out to be closed, such as the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) and a large class of [differential operators](wikipedia://en/263886).

Let $X, Y$ be two [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989). A [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) $A : D(A) \subseteq X \to Y$ is *closed* if for every [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $\{x_{n}\}$ in $D(A)$ [converging](wikipedia://en/285773) to $x$ in $X$ such that $Ax_{n} \to y \in Y$ as $n \to \infty$ one has $x \in D(A)$ and $Ax = y$. Equivalently, $A$ is closed if its [graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_graph) is [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) in the [direct sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_sum_of_Banach_spaces) $X \oplus Y$.

Given a linear operator $A$, not necessarily closed, if the closure of its graph in $X \oplus Y$ happens to be the graph of some operator, that operator is called the *closure* of $A$, and we say that $A$ is *closable*. Denote the closure of $A$ by $\overline{A}$. It follows that $A$ is the [restriction](wikipedia://en/185427) of $\overline{A}$ to $D(A)$.

A *core* (or *essential domain*) of a closable operator is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $C$ of $D(A)$ such that the closure of the restriction of $A$ to $C$ is $\overline{A}$.

::: {#o33 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

Consider the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) operator $A = \tfrac{d}{dx}$ where $X = Y = C([a, b])$ is the Banach space of all [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) on an [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $[a, b]$. If one takes its domain $D(A)$ to be $C^{1}([a, b])$, then $A$ is a closed operator which is not bounded.[^o34] On the other hand if $D(A) = \operatorname{smooth} \operatorname{function}|C^{\infty}([a, b])$, then $A$ will no longer be closed, but it will be closable, with the closure being its extension defined on $C^{1}([a, b])$.

:::

## Symmetric operators and self-adjoint operators {#Symmetric_operators_and_self-adjoint_operators}

An operator *T* on a Hilbert space is *symmetric* if and only if for each *x* and *y* in the domain of $T$ we have $\langle Tx \mid y \rangle = \langle x \mid Ty \rangle$. A densely defined operator $T$ is symmetric if and only if it agrees with its adjoint *T*<sup>∗</sup> restricted to the domain of *T*, in other words when *T*<sup>∗</sup> is an extension of $T$.[^o36]

In general, if *T* is densely defined and symmetric, the domain of the adjoint *T*<sup>∗</sup> need not equal the domain of *T*. If *T* is symmetric and the domain of *T* and the domain of the adjoint coincide, then we say that *T* is *self-adjoint*.[^o37] Note that, when *T* is self-adjoint, the existence of the adjoint implies that *T* is densely defined and since *T*<sup>∗</sup> is necessarily closed, *T* is closed.

A densely defined operator *T* is *symmetric*, if the subspace $\Gamma(T)$ (defined in a previous section) is orthogonal to its image $J(\Gamma(T))$ under *J* (where *J*(*x*,*y*):=(*y*,-*x*)).[^o38]

Equivalently, an operator *T* is *self-adjoint* if it is densely defined, closed, symmetric, and satisfies the fourth condition: both operators $T - i$, $T + i$ are surjective, that is, map the domain of *T* onto the whole space *H*. In other words: for every *x* in *H* there exist *y* and *z* in the domain of *T* such that $Ty - iy = x$ and $Tz + iz = x$.[^o39]

An operator *T* is *self-adjoint*, if the two subspaces $\Gamma(T)$, $J(\Gamma(T))$ are orthogonal and their sum is the whole space $H \oplus H .$[^o20]

This approach does not cover non-densely defined closed operators. Non-densely defined symmetric operators can be defined directly or via graphs, but not via adjoint operators.

A symmetric operator is often studied via its [Cayley transform](wikipedia://en/882010).

An operator *T* on a complex Hilbert space is symmetric if and only if the number $\langle Tx \mid x \rangle$ is real for all *x* in the domain of *T*.[^o36]

A densely defined closed symmetric operator *T* is self-adjoint if and only if *T*<sup>∗</sup> is symmetric.[^o40] It may happen that it is not.[^o41][^o42]

A densely defined operator *T* is called *positive*[^o43] (or *nonnegative*[^o44]) if its quadratic form is nonnegative, that is, $\langle Tx \mid x \rangle \ge 0$ for all *x* in the domain of *T*. Such operator is necessarily symmetric.

The operator *T*<sup>∗</sup>*T* is self-adjoint[^o45] and positive[^o43] for every densely defined, closed *T*.

A densely defined symmetric operator $T$ on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) $H$ is called *bounded from below* if $T + a$ is a positive operator for some real number $a$. That is, $\langle Tx|x\rangle \ge -a ||x||^{2}$ for all $x$ in the domain of $T$ (or alternatively $\langle Tx|x\rangle \ge a ||x||^{2}$ since $a$ is arbitrary).[^o43] If both $T$ and $-T$ are bounded from below then $T$ is bounded.[^o43]

The [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/187408#Spectral_theorem) applies to self-adjoint operators [^o46] and moreover, to normal operators,[^o47][^o48] but not to densely defined, closed operators in general, since in this case the spectrum can be empty.[^o49][^o50]

A symmetric operator defined everywhere is closed, therefore bounded,[^o9] which is the [Hellinger–Toeplitz theorem](wikipedia://en/592897).[^o51]

## Extension-related {#Extension-related}

By definition, an operator *T* is an *extension* of an operator *S* if $\Gamma(S) \subseteq \Gamma(T)$.[^o53] An equivalent direct definition: for every *x* in the domain of *S*, *x* belongs to the domain of *T* and $Sx = Tx$.[^o8][^o53]

Note that an everywhere defined extension exists for every operator, which is a purely algebraic fact explained at Discontinuous linear map#General existence theorem and based on the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840). If the given operator is not bounded then the extension is a [discontinuous linear map](wikipedia://en/3531066). It is of little use since it cannot preserve important properties of the given operator (see below), and usually is highly non-unique.

An operator *T* is called *closable* if it satisfies the following equivalent conditions:[^o9][^o53][^o54]

- *T* has a closed extension;
- the closure of the graph of *T* is the graph of some operator;
- for every sequence (*x<sub>n</sub>*) of points from the domain of *T* such that *x<sub>n</sub>* → 0 and also *Tx<sub>n</sub>* → *y* it holds that $y = 0$.

Not all operators are closable.[^o55]

A closable operator *T* has the least closed extension $\overline T$ called the *closure* of *T*. The closure of the graph of *T* is equal to the graph of $\overline T.$[^o9][^o53] Other, non-minimal closed extensions may exist.[^o41][^o42]

A densely defined operator *T* is closable if and only if *T*<sup>∗</sup> is densely defined. In this case $\overline T = T^{**}$ and $(\overline T)^* = T^*.$[^o20][^o56]

If *S* is densely defined and *T* is an extension of *S* then *S*<sup>∗</sup> is an extension of *T*<sup>∗</sup>.[^o57]

Every symmetric operator is closable.[^o58]

A symmetric operator is called *maximal symmetric* if it has no symmetric extensions, except for itself.[^o36] Every self-adjoint operator is maximal symmetric.[^o36] The converse is false.[^o59]

An operator is called *essentially self-adjoint* if its closure is self-adjoint.[^o58] An operator is essentially self-adjoint if and only if it has one and only one self-adjoint extension.[^o40]

A symmetric operator may have more than one self-adjoint extension, and even a continuum of them.[^o42]

A densely defined, symmetric operator *T* is essentially self-adjoint if and only if both operators $T - i$, $T + i$ have dense range.[^o60]

Let *T* be a densely defined operator. Denoting the relation "*T* is an extension of *S*" by *S* ⊂ *T* (a conventional abbreviation for Γ(*S*) ⊆ Γ(*T*)) one has the following.[^o61]

- If *T* is symmetric then *T* ⊂ *T*<sup>∗∗</sup> ⊂ *T*<sup>∗</sup>.
- If *T* is closed and symmetric then *T* = *T*<sup>∗∗</sup> ⊂ *T*<sup>∗</sup>.
- If *T* is self-adjoint then *T* = *T*<sup>∗∗</sup> = *T*<sup>∗</sup>.
- If *T* is essentially self-adjoint then *T* ⊂ *T*<sup>∗∗</sup> = *T*<sup>∗</sup>.

## Importance of self-adjoint operators {#Importance_of_self-adjoint_operators}

The class of self-adjoint operators is especially important in mathematical physics. Every self-adjoint operator is densely defined, closed and symmetric. The converse holds for bounded operators but fails in general. Self-adjointness is substantially more restricting than these three properties. The famous [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/187408#Spectral_theorem) holds for self-adjoint operators. In combination with [Stone's theorem on one-parameter unitary groups](wikipedia://en/893698) it shows that self-adjoint operators are precisely the infinitesimal generators of strongly continuous one-parameter unitary groups. Such unitary groups are especially important for describing [time evolution](wikipedia://en/937739) in classical and quantum mechanics.

If $T T^* f_j$ is convergent then $f_j$ is convergent by the estimate since $\|T^*f_j\|_1^2 = | \langle T^*f_j \mid T^*f_j \rangle_1| \leq \|TT^*f_j\|_2 \|f_j\|_2.$ Say, $f_j \to g.$ Since $T T^*$ is self-adjoint; thus, closed, (von Neumann's theorem), $T T^* f_j \to T T^* g.$ QED
