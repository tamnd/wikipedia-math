# Tournament (graph theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Tournament"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **tournament** is a [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) with exactly one edge between each two [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899), in one of the two possible directions. Equivalently, a tournament is an [orientation](wikipedia://en/36619818) of an [undirected complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816). (However, as directed graphs, tournaments are not complete: complete directed graphs have two edges, in both directions, between each two vertices.) Equivalently, a tournament is a [complete](wikipedia://en/57495962) [asymmetric](wikipedia://en/1229368) [relation](wikipedia://en/3931).[^o4][^o5]

:::

The name *tournament* comes from interpreting the graph as the outcome of a [round-robin tournament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round-robin_tournament), a game where each player is paired against every other exactly once. In a tournament, the vertices represent the players, and the edges between players point from the winner to the loser.

Many of the important properties of tournaments were investigated by [H. G. Landau](wikipedia://en/70501900) in 1953 to model dominance relations in flocks of chickens.[^n1] Tournaments are also heavily studied in [voting theory](wikipedia://en/1745670), where they can represent partial information about voter preferences among multiple candidates, and are central to the definition of [Condorcet methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet_methods).

If every player beats the same number of other players ([indegree − outdegree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copeland_score) = 0) the tournament is called *regular*. The number of unlabeled regular tournaments with 2n+1 vertices goes:

1, 1, 1, 3, 15, 1223, 1495297, 18400989629, 2406183070160597,...

## Paths and cycles {#Paths_and_cycles}

*Image: Tournament Hamiltonian path.svg.* $a$ is inserted between $v_{2}$ and $v_{3}$.

Any tournament on a [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) number $n$ of vertices contains a [Hamiltonian path](wikipedia://en/244437), i.e., directed path on all $n$ vertices ([Rédei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/László_Rédei) 1934).

This is shown by [induction](wikipedia://en/18881) on $n$: suppose that the statement holds for $n$, and consider any tournament $T$ on $n+1$ vertices. Choose a vertex $v_0$ of $T$ and consider a directed path $v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n$ in $T\smallsetminus \{v_0\}$. There is some $i \in \{0,\ldots,n\}$ such that $(i=0 \vee v_i \rightarrow v_0) \wedge (v_0 \rightarrow v_{i+1} \vee i=n)$. (One possibility is to let $i \in \{0,\ldots,n\}$ be maximal such that for every $j \leq i, v_j \rightarrow v_0$. Alternatively, let $i$ be minimal such that $\forall j > i, v_0 \rightarrow v_j$.)

$$v_1,\ldots,v_i,v_0,v_{i+1},\ldots,v_n$$

is a directed path as desired. This argument also gives an algorithm for finding the Hamiltonian path. More efficient algorithms, that require examining only $O(n \log n)$ of the edges, are known. The Hamiltonian paths are in one-to-one correspondence with the minimal [feedback arc sets](wikipedia://en/1860374) of the tournament.[^n2] Rédei's theorem is the special case for complete graphs of the [Gallai–Hasse–Roy–Vitaver theorem](wikipedia://en/36620043), relating the lengths of paths in orientations of graphs to the [chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number) of these graphs.[^n3]

Another basic result on tournaments is that every [strongly connected](wikipedia://en/684680) tournament has a [Hamiltonian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle).[^n4] More strongly, every strongly connected tournament is [vertex pancyclic](wikipedia://en/28691490): for each vertex $v$, and each $k$ in the range from three to the number of vertices in the tournament, there is a cycle of length $k$ containing $v$.[^n5] A tournament $T$ is $k$-strongly connected if for every set $U$ of $k-1$ vertices of $T$, $T-U$ is strongly connected. If the tournament is 4‑strongly connected, then each pair of vertices can be connected with a Hamiltonian path.[^n6] For every set $B$ of at most $k-1$ arcs of a $k$-strongly connected tournament $T$, we have that $T-B$ has a Hamiltonian cycle.[^n7] This result was extended by (Bang-Jensen Gutin Yeo 1997).[^n8]

## Transitivity {#Transitivity}

*Image: 8-tournament-transitive.svg.* A transitive tournament on 8 vertices

A tournament with the property that $a \rightarrow b$ and $b \rightarrow c$ implies $a \rightarrow c$ is called **[transitive](wikipedia://en/200463)**. In other words, in a transitive tournament, the vertices may be (strictly) [totally ordered](wikipedia://en/30330) by the edge relation, and the edge relation is the same as [reachability](wikipedia://en/2833097).

### Equivalent conditions {#Equivalent_conditions}

The following statements are equivalent for a tournament $T$ on $n$ vertices:

1. $T$ is transitive.
1. $T$ is a strict total ordering.
1. $T$ is [acyclic](wikipedia://en/204002).
1. $T$ does not contain a cycle of length 3.
1. The score sequence (set of outdegrees) of $T$ is $\{0,1,2,\ldots,n-1\}$.
1. $T$ has exactly one Hamiltonian path.

### Ramsey theory {#Ramsey_theory}

Transitive tournaments play a role in [Ramsey theory](wikipedia://en/157175) analogous to that of [cliques](wikipedia://en/524466) in undirected graphs. In particular, every tournament on $n$ vertices contains a transitive subtournament on $1+\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor$ vertices. The proof is simple: choose any one vertex $v$ to be part of this subtournament, and form the rest of the subtournament recursively on either the set of incoming neighbors of $v$ or the set of outgoing neighbors of $v$, whichever is larger. For instance, every tournament on seven vertices contains a three-vertex transitive subtournament; the [Paley tournament](wikipedia://en/5719307) on seven vertices shows that this is the most that can be guaranteed.[^n9] However, (Reid Parker 1970) showed that this bound is not tight for some larger values of $n$.[^n10]

(Erdős Moser 1964) proved that there are tournaments on $n$ vertices without a transitive subtournament of size $2+2\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor$ Their proof uses a [counting argument](wikipedia://en/741875): the number of ways that a $k$-element transitive tournament can occur as a subtournament of a larger tournament on $n$ labeled vertices is

$$\binom{n}{k}k!2^{\binom{n}{2}-\binom{k}{2}},$$

and when $k$ is larger than $2+2\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor$, this number is too small to allow for an occurrence of a transitive tournament within each of the $2^{\binom{n}{2}}$ different tournaments on the same set of $n$ labeled vertices.[^n9]

### Paradoxical tournaments {#Paradoxical_tournaments}

A player who wins all games would naturally be the tournament's winner. However, as the existence of non-transitive tournaments shows, there may not be such a player. A tournament for which every player loses at least one game is called a 1-paradoxical tournament. More generally, a tournament $T=(V,E)$ is called $k$-paradoxical if for every $k$-element subset $S$ of $V$ there is a vertex $v_0$ in $V\setminus S$ such that $v_0 \rightarrow v$ for all $v \in S$. By means of the [probabilistic method](wikipedia://en/173525), [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) showed that for any fixed value of $k$, if $|V| \geq k^22^k\ln(2+o(1))$, then almost every tournament on $V$ is $k$-paradoxical.[^o26] On the other hand, an easy argument shows that any $k$-paradoxical tournament must have at least $2^{k+1}-1$ players, which was improved to $(k+2)2^{k-1}-1$ by [Esther](wikipedia://en/2575958) and [George Szekeres](wikipedia://en/2563194) in 1965.[^n11] There is an explicit construction of $k$-paradoxical tournaments with $k^24^{k-1}(1+o(1))$ players by [Graham](wikipedia://en/240694) and Spencer (1971) namely the Paley tournament.

### Condensation {#Condensation}

The [condensation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensation_%28graph_theory%29) of any tournament is itself a transitive tournament. Thus, even for tournaments that are not transitive, the strongly connected components of the tournament may be totally ordered.[^o29]

## Score sequences and score sets {#Score_sequences_and_score_sets}

The score sequence of a tournament is the nondecreasing sequence of outdegrees of the vertices of a tournament. The score set of a tournament is the set of integers that are the outdegrees of vertices in that tournament.

**Landau's Theorem (1953)** A nondecreasing sequence of integers $(s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_n)$ is a score sequence if and only if:[^n1]

1. $$0 \le s_1 \le s_2 \le \cdots \le s_n$$
1. $$s_1 + s_2 + \cdots + s_i \ge {i \choose 2}, \text{ for }i = 1, 2, \ldots, n - 1$$
1. $$s_1 + s_2 + \cdots + s_n = {n \choose 2}.$$

Let $s(n)$ be the number of different score sequences of size $n$. The sequence $s(n)$ starts as:

1, 1, 1, 2, 4, 9, 22, 59, 167, 490, 1486, 4639, 14805, 48107, ...

Winston and Kleitman proved that for sufficiently large *n*:

$$s(n) > c_1 4^n n^{-5/2},$$

where $c_1 = 0.049.$ Takács later showed, using some reasonable but unproven assumptions, that

$$s(n) < c_2 4^n n^{-5/2},$$

where $c_2 < 4.858.$[^n12]

Together these provide evidence that:

$$s(n) \in \Theta (4^n n^{-5/2}).$$

Here $\Theta$ signifies an [asymptotically tight bound](wikipedia://en/44578#Related_asymptotic_notations).

Yao showed that every nonempty set of nonnegative integers is the score set for some tournament.[^n13]

## Majority relations {#Majority_relations}

In [social choice theory](wikipedia://en/1745670), tournaments naturally arise as majority relations of preference profiles.[^n14] Let $A$ be a finite set of alternatives, and consider a list $P = (\succ_1, \dots, \succ_n)$ of [linear orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_order) over $A$. We interpret each order $\succ_i$ as the [preference ranking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preference_relation) of a voter $i$. The (strict) majority relation $\succ_{\text{maj}}$ of $P$ over $A$ is then defined so that $a \succ_{\text{maj}} b$ if and only if a majority of the voters prefer $a$ to $b$, that is $|\{ i \in [n] : a \succ_i b \}| > |\{ i \in [n] : b \succ_i a \}|$. If the number $n$ of voters is odd, then the majority relation forms the dominance relation of a tournament on vertex set $A$.

By a lemma of McGarvey, every tournament on $m$ vertices can be obtained as the majority relation of at most $m(m-1)$ voters.[^o36] Results by [Stearns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_E._Stearns) and Erdős & Moser later established that $\Theta(m/\log m)$ voters are needed to induce every tournament on $m$ vertices.[^o37]

Laslier (1997) studies in what sense a set of vertices can be called the set of "winners" of a tournament.[^n15] This revealed to be useful in [political science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science) to study, in formal models of [political economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_economy), what can be the outcome of a democratic process.[^n16]
