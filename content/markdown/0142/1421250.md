# Roger Apéry

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Roger Apéry"}

**Roger Apéry** (; 14 November 1916 – 18 December 1994) was a Greek-French [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) most remembered for [Apéry's theorem](wikipedia://en/1711336), which states that $\zeta(3)$ is an [irrational number](wikipedia://en/20647689). Here, $\zeta(s)$ denotes the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Apéry was born in [Rouen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rouen) in 1916 to a [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France) mother and [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece) father. His childhood was spent in [Lille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lille) until 1926, when the family moved to [Paris](wikipedia://en/22989), where he studied at the Lycée Ledru-Rollin and the [Lycée Louis-le-Grand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Louis-le-Grand). He was admitted at the [École normale supérieure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_normale_supérieure_%28Paris%29) in 1935. His studies were interrupted at the start of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II); he was mobilized in September 1939, taken [prisoner of war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prisoner_of_war) in June 1940, repatriated with pleurisy in June 1941, and hospitalized until August 1941. He wrote his doctoral thesis in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) under the direction of [Paul Dubreil](wikipedia://en/1389591) and René Garnier in 1947.

In 1947 Apéry was appointed [Maître de conférences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_ranks_in_France) (lecturer) at the [University of Rennes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Rennes). In 1949 he was appointed Professor at the [University of Caen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Caen), where he remained until his retirement.

In 1979 he published an unexpected proof of the [irrationality](wikipedia://en/20647689) of $\zeta(3)$, which is the sum of the inverses of the cubes of the [positive integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_integer). An indication of the difficulty is that the corresponding problem for other odd powers remains unsolved. Nevertheless, many mathematicians have since worked on the so-called Apéry sequences to seek alternative proofs that might apply to other odd powers ([Frits Beukers](wikipedia://en/36707078), [Alfred van der Poorten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_van_der_Poorten), Marc Prévost, [Keith Ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Martin_Ball), [Tanguy Rivoal](wikipedia://en/78396540), [Wadim Zudilin](wikipedia://en/4311292), and others).

Apéry was active in politics and for a few years in the 1960s was president of the [Calvados](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvados_%28department%29) [Radical Party of the Left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_Party_of_the_Left). He abandoned politics after the reforms instituted by [Edgar Faure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Faure) after the [1968 revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_1968_in_France), when he realised that university life was running against the tradition he had always upheld.

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Apéry married in 1947 and had three sons, including mathematician [François Apéry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_Apéry). His first marriage ended in divorce in 1971. He then remarried in 1972 and divorced in 1977.

In 1994, Apéry died from [Parkinson's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkinson's_disease) after a long illness in [Caen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caen). He was buried next to his parents at the [Père Lachaise Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Père_Lachaise_Cemetery) in Paris. His tombstone has a mathematical inscription stating his theorem.

$$1 + \frac{1}{8} + \frac{1}{27} + \frac{1}{64} + \cdots \neq \frac{p}{q}$$
