# Nonlinear Schrödinger equation

*Image: NLS breathers - Akhmediev Peregrine and Kuznetsov-Ma breather.jpg.* [Absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of the [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [envelope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envelope_%28waves%29) of exact analytical [breather](wikipedia://en/1546530) solutions of the nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation in [nondimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondimensionalization) form. (A) The Akhmediev breather; (B) the [Peregrine breather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peregrine_soliton); (C) the Kuznetsov–Ma breather.[^o1]

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Nonlinear Schrödinger equation"}

In [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028), the (one-dimensional) **nonlinear Schrödinger equation** (**NLSE**) is a [nonlinear](wikipedia://en/16678376) variation of the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874). It is a [classical field equation](wikipedia://en/1293340) whose principal applications are to the propagation of light in nonlinear [optical fibers](wikipedia://en/3372377), planar [waveguides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waveguide)[^o3] and hot rubidium vapors[^o4] and to [Bose–Einstein condensates](wikipedia://en/4474) confined to highly [anisotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anisotropic), cigar-shaped [traps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_trap_%28atoms%29), in the [mean-field regime](wikipedia://en/555768).[^o5] Additionally, the equation appears in the studies of small-amplitude [gravity waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_waves) on the surface of deep [inviscid](wikipedia://en/5877457) (zero-viscosity) water;[^o3] the [Langmuir waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langmuir_waves) in hot [plasmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_%28physics%29);[^o3] the propagation of plane-diffracted wave beams in the focusing regions of the ionosphere;[^o6] the propagation of [Davydov's alpha-helix solitons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davydov_soliton), which are responsible for energy transport along molecular chains;[^o7] and many others. More generally, the NLSE appears as one of universal equations that describe the evolution of slowly varying packets of quasi-monochromatic waves in weakly nonlinear media that have [dispersion](wikipedia://en/853778).[^o3] Unlike the linear [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874), the NLSE never describes the time evolution of a quantum state. The 1D NLSE is an example of an [integrable model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_model).

:::

In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), the 1D NLSE is a special case of the classical nonlinear [Schrödinger field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schrödinger_field), which in turn is a classical limit of a quantum Schrödinger field. Conversely, when the classical Schrödinger field is [canonically quantized](wikipedia://en/418156), it becomes a quantum field theory (which is linear, despite the fact that it is called ″quantum *nonlinear* Schrödinger equation″) that describes bosonic point particles with delta-function interactions—the particles either repel or attract when they are at the same point. In fact, when the number of particles is finite, this quantum field theory is equivalent to the [Lieb–Liniger model](wikipedia://en/24952147). Both the quantum and the classical 1D nonlinear Schrödinger equations are integrable. Of special interest is the limit of infinite strength repulsion, in which case the Lieb–Liniger model becomes the [Tonks–Girardeau gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonks–Girardeau_gas) (also called the hard-core Bose gas, or impenetrable Bose gas). In this limit, the bosons may, by a change of variables that is a continuum generalization of the [Jordan–Wigner transformation](wikipedia://en/18007622), be transformed to a system one-dimensional noninteracting spinless [^o8] fermions.[^o9]

The nonlinear Schrödinger equation is a simplified 1+1-dimensional form of the [Ginzburg–Landau equation](wikipedia://en/4597084) introduced in 1950 in their work on superconductivity, and was written down explicitly by (txt, equation (5)) in their study of optical beams.

Multi-dimensional version replaces the second spatial derivative by the Laplacian. In more than one dimension, the equation is not integrable, it allows for a collapse and wave turbulence.[^o11]

::: {#o13 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The nonlinear Schrödinger equation is a [nonlinear partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/16678376), applicable to [classical](wikipedia://en/19555586) and [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202).

### Classical equation {#Classical_equation}

The classical field equation (in [dimensionless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless) form) is:[^o15]

::: {#o16 .equation cue=equation-box-1 name="Nonlinear Schrödinger equation (Classical field theory)"}

**Nonlinear Schrödinger equation** *(Classical field theory).**

:::

for the [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) field $\psi(x, t)$.

This equation arises from the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381)[^o15]

$$H=\int \mathrm{d}x \left[{1\over 2}|\partial_x\psi|^2+{\kappa \over 2}|\psi|^4\right]$$

with the [Poisson brackets](wikipedia://en/294340)

$$\{\psi(x),\psi(y)\}=\{\psi^*(x),\psi^*(y)\}=0 \,$$

$$\{\psi^*(x),\psi(y)\}=i\delta(x-y). \,$$

Unlike its linear counterpart, it never describes the time evolution of a quantum state.

The case with negative κ is called focusing and allows for [bright soliton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bright_soliton) solutions (localized in space, and having spatial attenuation towards infinity) as well as [breather](wikipedia://en/1546530) solutions. It can be [solved exactly](wikipedia://en/2573213#Exactly_solvable_models) by use of the [inverse scattering transform](wikipedia://en/4916481), as shown by (Zakharov Shabat 1972) (see below). The other case, with κ positive, is the defocusing NLS which has [dark soliton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_soliton) solutions (having constant amplitude at infinity, and a local spatial dip in amplitude).[^o18]

### Quantum mechanics {#Quantum_mechanics}

To get the [quantized version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schrödinger_field), simply replace the Poisson brackets by commutators

$$\begin{aligned} {}[\psi(x),\psi(y)] &= [\psi^*(x),\psi^*(y)] = 0\\ {}[\psi^*(x),\psi(y)] &= -\delta(x-y) \end{aligned}$$

and [normal order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_order) the Hamiltonian

$$H=\int dx \left[{1\over 2}\partial_x\psi^\dagger\partial_x\psi+{\kappa \over 2}\psi^\dagger\psi^\dagger\psi\psi\right].$$

The quantum version was solved by [Bethe ansatz](wikipedia://en/4327884) by [Lieb and Liniger](wikipedia://en/24952147). Thermodynamics was described by [Chen-Ning Yang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen-Ning_Yang). Quantum correlation functions also were evaluated by Korepin in 1993.[^o9] The model has higher conservation laws - Davies and Korepin in 1989 expressed them in terms of local fields.[^o20]

:::

## Solution {#Solution}

The nonlinear Schrödinger equation is integrable in 1D: (txt) solved it with the [inverse scattering transform](wikipedia://en/4916481). The corresponding linear system of equations is known as the [Zakharov–Shabat system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zakharov–Shabat_system):

$$\begin{aligned} \phi_x &= J\phi\Lambda + U\phi \\ \phi_t &= 2J\phi\Lambda^2 + 2U\phi\Lambda + \left(JU^2 - JU_x\right)\phi, \end{aligned}$$

where

$$\Lambda = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1&0\\ 0&\lambda_2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad J = i\sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & -i \end{pmatrix}, \quad U = i \begin{pmatrix} 0 & q \\ r & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The nonlinear Schrödinger equation arises as compatibility condition of the Zakharov–Shabat system:

$$\phi_{xt} = \phi_{tx} \quad \Rightarrow \quad U_t = -JU_{xx} + 2JU^2 U \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad \begin{cases} iq_t = q_{xx} + 2qrq \\ ir_t = -r_{xx} - 2qrr. \end{cases}$$

By setting $q = r^*$ or $q = -r^*$ the nonlinear Schrödinger equation with attractive or repulsive interaction is obtained.

An alternative approach uses the Zakharov–Shabat system directly and employs the following [Darboux transformation](wikipedia://en/78854296):

$$\begin{aligned} \phi \to \phi[1] &= \phi\Lambda - \sigma\phi \\ U \to U[1] &= U + [J, \sigma] \\ \sigma &= \varphi\Omega\varphi^{-1} \end{aligned}$$

which leaves the system invariant.

Here, *φ* is another invertible matrix solution (different from $\varphi$) of the Zakharov–Shabat system with spectral parameter $\Omega$:

$$\begin{aligned} \varphi_x &= J\varphi\Omega + U\varphi \\ \varphi_t &= 2J\varphi\Omega^2 + 2U\varphi\Omega + \left(JU^2 - JU_x\right)\varphi. \end{aligned}$$

Starting from the trivial solution $U = 0$ and iterating, one obtains the solutions with n [solitons](wikipedia://en/51654). This can be achieved via direct numerical simulation using, for example, the [split-step method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split-step_method).[^o23] This method has been implemented on both CPU and GPU.[^o24][^o25]

## Applications {#Applications}

### Fiber optics {#Fiber_optics}

In [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics), the nonlinear Schrödinger equation occurs in the [Manakov system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manakov_system), a model of wave propagation in fiber optics. The function ψ represents a wave and the nonlinear Schrödinger equation describes the propagation of the wave through a nonlinear medium. The second-order derivative represents the dispersion, while the *κ* term represents the nonlinearity. The equation models many nonlinearity effects in a fiber, including but not limited to [self-phase modulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-phase_modulation), [four-wave mixing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-wave_mixing), [second-harmonic generation](wikipedia://en/7334318), [stimulated Raman scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raman_scattering), [optical solitons](wikipedia://en/9485301), [ultrashort pulses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrashort_pulse), etc.

### Water waves {#Water_waves}

*Image: Sech soliton.svg.* A [hyperbolic secant (sech)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function) envelope soliton for surface waves on deep water.Blue line: water waves.Red line: envelope soliton.

For [water waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_surface_waves), the nonlinear Schrödinger equation describes the evolution of the [envelope](wikipedia://en/880235) of [modulated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulation) wave groups. In a paper in 1968, [Vladimir E. Zakharov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_E._Zakharov) describes the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319) structure of water waves. In the same paper Zakharov shows that, for slowly modulated wave groups, the wave [amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude) satisfies the nonlinear Schrödinger equation, approximately.[^o30] The value of the nonlinearity parameter *к* depends on the relative water depth. For deep water, with the water depth large compared to the [wave length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_length) of the water waves, *к* is negative and [envelope](wikipedia://en/880235) [solitons](wikipedia://en/51654) may occur. Additionally, the group velocity of these envelope solitons could be increased by an acceleration induced by an external time-dependent water flow.[^o31]

For shallow water, with wavelengths longer than 4.6 times the water depth, the nonlinearity parameter *к* is positive and *wave groups* with *envelope* solitons do not exist. In shallow water *surface-elevation* solitons or [waves of translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_of_translation) do exist, but they are not governed by the nonlinear Schrödinger equation.

The nonlinear Schrödinger equation is thought to be important for explaining the formation of [rogue waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogue_wave).[^o32]

The [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) field $\psi$, as appearing in the nonlinear Schrödinger equation, is related to the amplitude and phase of the water waves. Consider a slowly modulated [carrier wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrier_wave) with water surface [elevation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevation) *η* of the form:

$$\eta = a(x_0,t_0)\; \cos \left[ k_0\, x_0 - \omega_0\, t_0 - \theta(x_0,t_0) \right],$$

where $a(x_0, t_0)$ and $\theta(x_0, t_0)$ are the slowly modulated amplitude and [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29). Further $\omega_0$ and $k_0$ are the (constant) [angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency) and [wavenumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavenumber) of the carrier waves, which have to satisfy the [dispersion](wikipedia://en/1744650) relation $\omega_0 = \Omega(k_0)$. Then

$$\psi = a\; \exp \left( i \theta \right).$$

So its [modulus](wikipedia://en/5826#Polar_form) $|\psi|$ is the wave amplitude $a$, and its [argument](wikipedia://en/5826#Polar_form) $\arg(\psi)$ is the phase $\theta$.

The relation between the physical coordinates $(x_0, t_0)$ and the $(x, t)$ coordinates, as used in the nonlinear Schrödinger equation given above, is given by:

$$x = k_0 \left[ x_0 - \Omega'(k_0)\; t_0 \right], \quad t = k_0^2 \left[ -\Omega''(k_0) \right]\; t_0$$

Thus $(x, t)$ is a transformed coordinate system moving with the [group velocity](wikipedia://en/12778) $\Omega'(k_0)$ of the carrier waves, The dispersion-relation [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) $\Omega''(k_0)$ – representing [group velocity dispersion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_velocity_dispersion) – is always negative for water waves under the action of gravity, for any water depth.

For waves on the water surface of deep water, the coefficients of importance for the nonlinear Schrödinger equation are:

$\kappa = - 2 k_0^2, \quad \Omega(k_0) = \sqrt{g k_0} = \omega_0 \,\!$ 1em so 1em $\Omega'(k_0) = \frac{1}{2} \frac{\omega_0}{k_0}, \quad \Omega''(k_0) = -\frac{1}{4} \frac{\omega_0}{k_0^2}, \,\!$

where *g* is the [acceleration due to gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_gravity) at the Earth's surface.

In the original $(x_0, t_0)$ coordinates the nonlinear Schrödinger equation for water waves reads:[^o33]

$$i\, \partial_{t_0} A + i\, \Omega'(k_0)\, \partial_{x_0} A + \tfrac12 \Omega''(k_0)\, \partial_{x_0 x_0} A - \nu\, |A|^2\, A = 0,$$

with $A=\psi^*$ (i.e. the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) of $\psi$) and $\nu=\kappa\, k_0^2\, \Omega''(k_0).$ So $\nu = \tfrac12 \omega_0 k_0^2$ for deep water waves.

### Vortices {#Vortices}

(Hasimoto 1972) showed that the work of (txt) on [vortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vortex) filaments is closely related to the nonlinear Schrödinger equation. Subsequently, (Salman 2013) used this correspondence to show that [breather](wikipedia://en/1546530) solutions can also arise for a vortex filament.

## Symmetries {#Symmetries}

### Scale invariance {#Scale_invariance}

The nonlinear Schrödinger equation is [scale invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance) in the following sense:

Given a solution $\psi(x, t)$ a new solution can be obtained by rescaling $\psi$, $x$ and $t$ by a function of a parameter $\alpha$:

$$\psi(x,t) \mapsto \psi_{[\alpha]}(x,t)=\alpha \psi(\alpha x,\alpha^2 t).$$

### Galilean invariance {#Galilean_invariance}

The nonlinear Schrödinger equation is [Galilean invariant](wikipedia://en/286788) in the following sense:

Given a solution $\psi(x, t)$ a new solution can be obtained by replacing $x$ with $x + vt$ everywhere in $\psi(x, t)$ and by appending a phase factor of $e^{-iv(x+vt/2)}\,$:

$$\psi(x,t) \mapsto \psi_{[v]}(x,t)=\psi(x+vt,t)\; e^{-iv(x+vt/2)}.$$

:::

## Gauge equivalent counterpart {#Gauge_equivalent_counterpart}

NLSE (1) is gauge equivalent to the following isotropic [Landau–Lifshitz equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landau–Lifshitz_model) (LLE) or [Heisenberg ferromagnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heisenberg_ferromagnet) equation

$$\vec{S}_t=\vec{S}\wedge \vec{S}_{xx}. \qquad$$

Note that this equation admits several integrable and non-integrable generalizations in 2 + 1 dimensions like the [Ishimori equation](wikipedia://en/16617601) and so on.

## Zero-curvature formulation {#Zero-curvature_formulation}

The NLSE is equivalent to the [curvature](wikipedia://en/489440) of a particular $\mathfrak{su}(2)$-[connection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_connection) on $\mathbb{R}^2$ being equal to zero.[^o44]

Explicitly, with coordinates $(x,t)$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$, the connection components $A_\mu$ are given by

$$A_x = \begin{pmatrix}i\lambda & i\varphi^* \\ i\varphi & -i\lambda\end{pmatrix}$$

$$A_t = \begin{pmatrix} 2i\lambda^2 - i|\varphi|^2 & 2i\lambda\varphi^* + \varphi_x^* \\ 2i\lambda\varphi - \varphi_x & -2i\lambda^2 + i|\varphi|^2\end{pmatrix}$$

Then the zero-curvature equation

$$\partial_t A_x - \partial_x A_t + [A_x, A_t] = 0$$

is equivalent to the NLSE $i\varphi_t + \varphi_{xx} + 2|\varphi|^2\varphi = 0$. The zero-curvature equation is so named as it corresponds to the curvature being equal to zero if it is defined $F_{\mu\nu} = [\partial_\mu - A_\mu, \partial_\nu - A_\nu]$.

The pair of matrices $A_x$ and $A_t$ are also known as a [Lax pair](wikipedia://en/5527029) for the NLSE, in the sense that the zero-curvature equation recovers the PDE rather than them satisfying Lax's equation.
