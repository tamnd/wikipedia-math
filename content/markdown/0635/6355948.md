# Representation (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Representation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **representation** is a very general relationship that expresses similarities (or equivalences) between mathematical objects or [structures](wikipedia://en/1809181). Roughly speaking, a collection $Y$ of mathematical objects may be said to *represent* another collection $X$ of objects, provided that the properties and relationships existing among the representing objects $y_{i}$ conform, in some consistent way, to those existing among the corresponding represented objects $x_{i}$. More specifically, given a set $\Pi$ of properties and [relations](wikipedia://en/22517558), a $\Pi$-representation of some structure $X$ is a structure $Y$ that is the image of $X$ under a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) that preserves $\Pi$. The label *representation* is sometimes also applied to the homomorphism itself (such as [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890)).[^o2][^o3]

:::

## Representation theory {#Representation_theory}

Perhaps the most well-developed example of this general notion is the subfield of [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) called **[representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200)**, which studies the representing of elements of [algebraic structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_structures) by [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformations) of [vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_spaces).[^o3]

## Other examples {#Other_examples}

Although the term *representation theory* is well established in the algebraic sense discussed above, there are many other uses of the term *representation* throughout mathematics.

### Graph theory {#Graph_theory}

An active area of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401) is the exploration of isomorphisms between [graphs](wikipedia://en/325806) and other structures. A key class of such problems stems from the fact that, like [adjacency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjacency_relation) in [undirected graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph), [intersection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersection_%28mathematics%29) of sets (or, more precisely, [non-disjointness](wikipedia://en/39359)) is a [symmetric relation](wikipedia://en/200459). This gives rise to the study of [intersection graphs](wikipedia://en/7726759) for innumerable families of sets.[^o7] One foundational result here, due to [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) and his colleagues, is that every *n*-[vertex](wikipedia://en/638899) graph may be represented in terms of intersection among [subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsets) of a set of size no more than *n*<sup>2</sup>/4.[^o8]

Representing a graph by such algebraic structures as its [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) and [Laplacian matrix](wikipedia://en/1448472) gives rise to the field of [spectral graph theory](wikipedia://en/534914).[^o9]

### Order theory {#Order_theory}

[Dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_%28mathematics%29) to the observation above that every graph is an intersection graph is the fact that every [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) (also known as poset) is isomorphic to a collection of sets ordered by the [inclusion](wikipedia://en/27631) (or containment) relation ⊆. Some posets that arise as the [inclusion orders](wikipedia://en/11728075) for natural classes of objects include the [Boolean lattices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_lattice) and the [orders of dimension *n*](wikipedia://en/3121852).[^o11]

Many partial orders arise from (and thus can be represented by) collections of [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) objects. Among them are the [*n*-ball](wikipedia://en/39782#n-ball) orders. The 1-ball orders are the interval-containment orders, and the 2-ball orders are the so-called *circle orders*—the posets representable in terms of containment among disks in the plane. A particularly nice result in this field is the characterization of the [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314), as those graphs whose vertex-edge incidence relations are circle orders.[^o12]

There are also geometric representations that are not based on inclusion. Indeed, one of the best studied classes among these are the [interval orders](wikipedia://en/11680645),[^o13] which represent the partial order in terms of what might be called *disjoint precedence* of intervals on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line): each element *x* of the poset is represented by an interval \[*x*<sub>1</sub>, *x*<sub>2</sub>\], such that for any *y* and *z* in the poset, *y* is below *z* if and only if *y*<sub>2</sub> \< *z*<sub>1</sub>.

### Logic {#Logic}

In [logic](wikipedia://en/19636), the representability of [algebras](wikipedia://en/45200) as [relational structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relational_structure) is often used to prove the equivalence of [algebraic](wikipedia://en/2848519) and [relational semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relational_semantics). Examples of this include [Stone's representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone's_representation_theorem) of [Boolean algebras](wikipedia://en/54476844) as [fields of sets](wikipedia://en/1018676),[^o15] [Esakia's representation](wikipedia://en/26600432) of [Heyting algebras](wikipedia://en/309343) as Heyting algebras of sets,[^o16] and the study of representable [relation algebras](wikipedia://en/4363670) and representable [cylindric algebras](wikipedia://en/7852887).[^o17]

### Polysemy {#Polysemy}

Under certain circumstances, a single function *f* : *X* → *Y* is at once an isomorphism from several mathematical structures on *X*. Since each of those structures may be thought of, intuitively, as a meaning of the image *Y* (one of the things that *Y* is trying to tell us), this phenomenon is called **polysemy**—a [term borrowed from linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysemy). Some examples of polysemy include:

- **intersection polysemy**—pairs of graphs *G*<sub>1</sub> and *G*<sub>2</sub> on a common vertex set *V* that can be simultaneously represented by a single collection of sets *S<sub>v</sub>*, such that any distinct vertices *u* and *w* in *V* are adjacent in *G*<sub>1</sub>, if and only if their corresponding sets intersect ( *S<sub>u</sub>* ∩ *S<sub>w</sub>* ≠ Ø ), and are adjacent in *G*<sub>2</sub> if and only if the [complements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_complement) do ( *S<sub>u</sub>*<sup>C</sup> ∩ *S<sub>w</sub>*<sup>C</sup> ≠ Ø ).[^o19]
- **competition polysemy**—motivated by the study of [ecological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecology) [food webs](wikipedia://en/145772), in which pairs of species may have prey in common or have predators in common. A pair of graphs *G*<sub>1</sub> and *G*<sub>2</sub> on one vertex set is competition polysemic, if and only if there exists a single [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) *D* on the same vertex set, such that any distinct vertices *u* and *v* are adjacent in *G*<sub>1,</sub> if and only if there is a vertex *w* such that both *uw* and *vw* are [arcs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_%28graph_theory%29) in *D*, and are adjacent in *G*<sub>2,</sub> if and only if there is a vertex *w* such that both *wu* and *wv* are arcs in *D*.[^o20]
- **interval polysemy**—pairs of posets *P*<sub>1</sub> and *P*<sub>2</sub> on a common ground set that can be simultaneously represented by a single collection of real intervals, that is an interval-order representation of *P*<sub>1</sub> and an interval-containment representation of *P*<sub>2</sub>.[^o21]
