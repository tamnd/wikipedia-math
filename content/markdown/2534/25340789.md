# Geomagnetic secular variation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Geomagnetic secular variation"}

**Geomagnetic secular variation** is the changes in the [Earth's magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_magnetic_field) on time scales of about a year or more. These changes mostly reflect changes in the [Earth's interior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_structure_of_Earth), while more rapid changes mostly originate in the [ionosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionosphere) or [magnetosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetosphere).[^o2]

:::

The geomagnetic field changes on time scales from milliseconds to millions of years. Shorter time scales mostly arise from currents in the [ionosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionosphere) and [magnetosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetosphere), and some changes can be traced to [geomagnetic storms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomagnetic_storms) or daily variations in currents. Changes over time scales of a year or more mostly reflect changes in the Earth's interior, particularly the iron-rich [core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_core). These changes are referred to as *[secular variation](wikipedia://en/6746761)*.[^o2] In most [models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_modelling), the secular variation is the amortized time [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of the magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$, $\dot{\mathbf{B}}$. The second derivative, $\ddot{\mathbf{B}}$ is the *secular acceleration*.[^o3]

## Recent changes {#Recent_changes}

*Image: Earth Magnetic Field Declination from 1590 to 1990.gif.* Estimated declination contours by year, 1590 to 1990 (click to see variation).

*Image: Geomagnetic\_axial\_dipole\_strength.svg.* Strength of the axial dipole component of Earth's magnetic field from 1600 to 2020, according to three models.

Secular variation can be observed in measurements at magnetic observatories, some of which have been operating for hundreds of years (the [Kew Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kew_Observatory), for example). Over such a time scale, [magnetic declination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_declination) is observed to vary over tens of degrees.[^o2] A movie on the right shows how global declinations have changed over the last few centuries.[^o5]

To analyze global patterns of change in the geomagnetic field, geophysicists fit the field data to a [spherical harmonic](wikipedia://en/203056) expansion (see [International Geomagnetic Reference Field](wikipedia://en/17640193)). The terms in this expansion can be divided into a [dipolar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipole) part, like the field around a bar magnet, and a non-dipolar part. The dipolar part dominates the geomagnetic field and determines the direction of the [geomagnetic poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomagnetic_pole#North_Geomagnetic_Pole). The direction and intensity of the dipole change over time.[^o2] Over the last two centuries the dipole strength has been decreasing at a rate of about 6.3% per century. At this rate of decrease, the field would reach zero in about 1600 years.[^o6] However, this strength is about average for the last 7 thousand years, and the current rate of change is not unusual.[^o7]

A prominent feature in the non-dipolar part of the secular variation is a *westward drift* at a rate of about 0.2 degrees per year.[^o6] This drift is not the same everywhere and has varied over time. The globally averaged drift has been westward since about 1400 AD but eastward between about 1000 AD and 1400 AD.[^o8]

## Paleomagnetic secular variation {#Paleomagnetic_secular_variation}

Changes that predate magnetic observatories are recorded in archaeological and geological materials. Such changes are referred to as *paleomagnetic secular variation* or *paleosecular variation (PSV)*. The records typically include long periods of small change with occasional large changes reflecting [geomagnetic excursions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomagnetic_excursion) and [geomagnetic reversals](wikipedia://en/2203131).[^o10]

The Levantine Iron Age anomaly was a fast and spatially localized geomagnetic positive anomaly which took place in the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant), with maxima at about 950, 750 and 500 BCE.[^o11]
