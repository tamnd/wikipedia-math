# Minimal polynomial (linear algebra)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Minimal polynomial"}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), the **minimal polynomial** $\mu_{A}$ of an $n \times n$ [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $A$ over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $\mathbf{F}$ is the [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730) $\mu_{A}$ over $\mathbf{F}$ of least [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) such that $\mu_{A}(A)= 0$. Any other [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) $Q$ with $Q(A) = 0$ is a (polynomial) multiple of $\mu_{A}$.

:::

The following three statements are [equivalent](wikipedia://en/169324):

1. $\lambda$ is a [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) of $\mu_{A}$,
1. $\lambda$ is a root of the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) $\chi_{A}$ of $A$,
1. $\lambda$ is an [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of matrix $A$.

The multiplicity of a root $\lambda$ of $\mu_{A}$ is the largest power $m$ such that $\ker((A - \lambda I_{n})^{m})$ *strictly* contains $\ker((A - \lambda I_{n})^{m-1})$. In other words, increasing the exponent up to $m$ will give ever larger [kernels](wikipedia://en/1072915), but further increasing the exponent beyond $m$ will just give the same kernel.

If the field $\mathbf{F}$ is not [algebraically closed](wikipedia://en/1018), then the minimal and characteristic polynomials need not factor according to their roots (in $\mathbf{F}$) alone, in other words they may have [irreducible polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) factors of degree greater than $1$. For irreducible polynomials $P$ one has similar equivalences:

1. $P$ divides $\mu_{A}$,
1. $P$ divides $\chi_{A}$,
1. the kernel of $P(A)$ has [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) at least $1$.
1. the kernel of $P(A)$ has dimension at least $\deg(P)$.

Like the characteristic polynomial, the minimal polynomial does not depend on the base field. In other words, considering the matrix as one with coefficients in a larger field does not change the minimal polynomial. The reason for this differs from the case with the characteristic polynomial (where it is immediate from the definition of [determinants](wikipedia://en/8468)), namely by the fact that the minimal polynomial is determined by the relations of [linear dependence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_dependence) between the powers of $A$: extending the base field will not introduce any new such relations (nor of course will it remove existing ones).

The minimal polynomial is often the same as the characteristic polynomial, but not always. For example, if $A$ is a multiple $aI_{n}$ of the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718), then its minimal polynomial is $X - a$ since the kernel of $aI_{n} - A = 0$ is already the entire space; on the other hand, its characteristic polynomial is $(X - a)^{n}$ (the only eigenvalue is $a$, and the degree of the characteristic polynomial is always equal to the dimension of the space). The minimal polynomial always divides the characteristic polynomial, which is one way of formulating the [Cayley–Hamilton theorem](wikipedia://en/173547) (for the case of matrices over a field), while the characteristic polynomial always divides some power of the minimal polynomial.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Given an [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569) $T$ on a finite-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $V$ over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $\mathbf{F}$, let $I_{T}$ be the set defined as

$$\mathit{I}_T = \{ p \in \mathbf{F}[t] \mid p(T) = 0 \} ,$$

where $\mathbf{F}[t]$ is the space of all polynomials over the field $\mathbf{F}$. $I_{T}$ is a [proper ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) of $\mathbf{F}[t]$. Since $\mathbf{F}$ is a field, $\mathbf{F}[t]$ is a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039), thus any ideal is generated by a single polynomial, which is unique up to a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) in $\mathbf{F}$. A particular choice among the generators can be made, since precisely one of the generators is [monic](wikipedia://en/314730). The **minimal polynomial** is thus defined to be the monic polynomial that generates $I_{T}$. It is the monic polynomial of least degree in $I_{T}$.

:::

::: {#o5 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

An [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569) $\varphi$ of a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $\mathbf{F}$ is [diagonalizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagonalizable) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its minimal polynomial factors completely over $\mathbf{F}$ into *distinct* linear factors. The fact that there is only one factor $X - \lambda$ for every eigenvalue $\lambda$ means that the [generalized eigenspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_eigenspace) for $\lambda$ is the same as the [eigenspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenspace) for $\lambda$: every [Jordan block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_block) has size $1$. More generally, if $\varphi$ satisfies a polynomial equation $P(\varphi) = 0$ where $P$ factors into distinct linear factors over $\mathbf{F}$, then it will be diagonalizable: its minimal polynomial is a [divisor](wikipedia://en/49492) of $P$ and therefore also factors into distinct linear factors. In particular one has:

- $P= X^k-1$: finite order endomorphisms of [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) vector spaces are diagonalizable. For the special case $k = 2$ of [involutions](wikipedia://en/293450), this is even true for endomorphisms of vector spaces over any field of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) other than $2$, since $X^2-1=(X-1)(X+1)$ is a factorization into distinct factors over such a field. This is a part of [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200) of [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327).
- $P= X^2-X = X(X-1)$: endomorphisms satisfying $\varphi^{2} = \varphi$ are called [projections](wikipedia://en/519182), and are always diagonalizable (moreover their only eigenvalues are $0$ and $1$).
- By contrast if $\mu_\phi = X^k$ with $k \ge 2$ then $\varphi$ (a [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) endomorphism) is not necessarily diagonalizable, since $X^k$ has a repeated root $0$.

These cases can also be [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) directly, but the minimal polynomial gives a unified perspective and proof.

## Computation {#Computation}

For a nonzero vector $v$ in $V$ define:

$$\mathit{I}_{T, v} = \{ p \in \mathbf{F}[t] \; | \; p(T)(v) = 0 \}.$$

This definition satisfies the properties of a proper ideal. Let $\mu_{T,v}$ be the monic polynomial which generates it.

### Example {#Example}

Define $T$ to be the endomorphism of $\mathbf{R}^{3}$ with matrix, on the [canonical basis](wikipedia://en/1028158),

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & -1 \\ 1 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Taking the first canonical [basis vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_vector) $e_{1}$ and its repeated images by $T$ one obtains

$$e_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad T \cdot e_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \quad T^2\! \cdot e_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \mbox{ and}\quad T^3\! \cdot e_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix}$$

of which the first three are easily seen to be [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent), and therefore [span](wikipedia://en/56353) all of $\mathbf{R}^{3}$. The last one then necessarily is a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of the first three, in fact

$$T^{\,3}\,\cdot e_{1} = -4T^{\,2}\,\cdot e_{1} - T \cdot e_{1} + e_{1},$$

so that:

$$\mu_{T,e_{1}} = X^{\,3} + 4X^{\,2} + X - I.$$

This is in fact also the minimal polynomial $\mu_{T}$ and the characteristic polynomial $\chi_{T}$: indeed $\mu_{T,e_{1}}$ divides $\mu_{T}$ which divides $\chi_{T}$, and since the first and last are of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) $3$ and all are monic, they must all be the same. Another reason is that in general if any polynomial in $T$ annihilates a vector $v$, then it also annihilates $T\cdot v$ (just apply $T$ to the equation that says that it annihilates $v$), and therefore by iteration it annihilates the entire space generated by the iterated images by $T$ of $v$; in the current case we have seen that for $v = e_{1}$ that space is all of $\mathbf{R}^{3}$, so $\mu_{T,e_{1}}(T) = 0$. Indeed one verifies for the full matrix that $T^{\,3} + 4T^{\,2} + T - I_{3}$ is the [zero matrix](wikipedia://en/1168888):

$$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & -3 \\ 3 & -13 & 23 \\ -4 & 19 & -36 \end{bmatrix} + 4\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 4 & -6 \\ 1 & -5 & 10 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & -1 \\ 1 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
