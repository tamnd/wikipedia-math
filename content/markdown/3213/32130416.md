# Zero to the power of zero

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Zero to the power of zero"}

**Zero to the power of zero**, denoted as $0^{0}$, is a [mathematical expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_expression) with different interpretations depending on the context. In certain areas of mathematics, such as [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) and [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), $0^{0}$ is defined as 1 because this simplifies many [formulas](wikipedia://en/164040) and ensures consistency in operations involving [exponents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponents). For instance, defining $0^{0} = 1$ aligns with the interpretation of choosing 0 elements from a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) and simplifies [polynomial](wikipedia://en/4173350) and [binomial expansions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_expansions).

:::

In other contexts, particularly in [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), $0^{0}$ is often considered an [indeterminate form](wikipedia://en/366723). This is because the value of $x^{y}$ as both $x$ and $y$ approach 0 can lead to different results based on the [limiting process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_process). The expression arises in limit problems and may result in a range of values or diverge to [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824), making it difficult to assign a single consistent value in these cases.

The treatment of $0^{0}$ varies across different [computer programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_programming_language) and [software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software). While many follow the convention $0^{0} = 1$, others leave it [undefined](wikipedia://en/9920876) or return errors, depending on the context.

## Discrete exponents {#Discrete_exponents}

Many widely used formulas involving [natural-number](wikipedia://en/21474) exponents require $0^{0}$ to be defined as $1$. For example, the following three interpretations of $b^{0}$ make just as much sense for $b = 0$ as they do for positive integers $b$:

- The interpretation of $b^{0}$ as an [empty product](wikipedia://en/181289) assigns it the value $1$.
- The [combinatorial interpretation](wikipedia://en/99491#Combinatorial_interpretation) of $b^{0}$ is the number of [0-tuples](wikipedia://en/132729) of elements from a $b$-element set; there is exactly one 0-tuple.
- The [set-theoretic interpretation](wikipedia://en/99491#Powers_of_sets) of $b^{0}$ is the number of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) from the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) to a $b$-element set; there is exactly one such function, namely, the [empty function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empty_function).[^o18]

All three of these specialize to give $0^{0} = 1$.

## Polynomials and power series {#Polynomials_and_power_series}

When evaluating [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), it is convenient to define $0^{0}$ as $1$. A (real) polynomial is an expression of the form $a_{0}x^{0} + \cdot\cdot\cdot + a_{n}x^{n}$, where $x$ is an indeterminate, and the coefficients $a_{i}$ are [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438). Polynomials are added termwise, and multiplied by applying the [distributive law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributive_law) and the usual rules for exponents. With these operations, polynomials form a [ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $\mathbf{R}[x]$. The [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity) of $\mathbf{R}[x]$ is the polynomial $x^{0}$; that is, $x^{0}$ times any polynomial $p(x)$ is just $p(x)$.[^o19] Also, polynomials can be evaluated by specializing $x$ to a real number. More precisely, for any given real number $r$, there is a unique unital [$\mathbf{R}$-algebra homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_homomorphism) $\operatorname{ev}_{r} : \mathbf{R}[x] \to \mathbf{R}$ such that $\operatorname{ev}_{r}(x) = r$. Because $\operatorname{ev}_{r}$ is unital, $\operatorname{ev}_{r}(x^{0}) = 1$. That is, $r^{0} = 1$ for each real number $r$, including 0. The same argument applies with $\mathbf{R}$ replaced by any [ring](wikipedia://en/48404).[^o20]

Defining $0^{0} = 1$ is necessary for many polynomial identities. For example, the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677) $(1+x)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}x^{k}$ holds for $x = 0$ only if $0^{0} = 1$.[^o30]

Similarly, rings of [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) require $x^{0}$ to be defined as 1 for all specializations of $x$. For example, identities like $\frac{1}{1-x}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n$ and $e^x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n!}$ hold for $x = 0$ only if $0^{0} = 1$.[^o31]

In order for the polynomial $x^{0}$ to define a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) $\mathbf{R} \to \mathbf{R}$, one must define $0^{0} = 1$.

In [calculus](wikipedia://en/50416), the [power rule](wikipedia://en/147912) $\frac{d}{dx}x^n=nx^{n-1}$ is valid for $n = 1$ at $x = 0$ only if $0^{0} = 1$.

## Continuous exponents {#Continuous_exponents}

*Image: X^y.png.* Plot of $z = x^{y}$. The red curves (with $z$ constant) yield different limits as $(x, y)$ approaches $(0, 0)$. The green curves (of finite constant slope, $y = ax$) all yield a limit of $1$.

Limits involving [algebraic operations](wikipedia://en/5438924) can often be evaluated by replacing subexpressions with their limits; if the resulting expression does not determine the original limit, the expression is known as an [indeterminate form](wikipedia://en/366723).[^o32] The expression $0^{0}$ is an indeterminate form: Given real-valued functions $f(t)$ and $g(t)$ approaching $0$ (as $t$ approaches a real number or $\pm\infty$) with $f(t) > 0$, the limit of $f(t)^{g(t)}$ can be any non-negative real number or $+\infty$, or it can [diverge](wikipedia://en/26551602), depending on $f$ and $g$. For example, each limit below involves a function $f(t)^{g(t)}$ with $f(t), g(t) \to 0$ as $t \to 0^{+}$ (a [one-sided limit](wikipedia://en/379840)), but their values are different:

$$\begin{aligned} \lim_{t \to 0^+} {t}^{t} &= 1 , \\ \lim_{t \to 0^+} \left(e^{-1/t^2}\right)^t &= 0, \\ \lim_{t \to 0^+} \left(e^{-1/t^2}\right)^{-t} &= +\infty, \\ \lim_{t \to 0^+} \left(a^{-1/t}\right)^{-t} &= a. \end{aligned}$$

Thus, the two-variable function $x^{y}$, though continuous on the set $\{(x, y) : x > 0\}$, [cannot be extended](wikipedia://en/2661487#Essential_discontinuity) to a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) on $\{(x, y) : x > 0\} \cup \{(0, 0)\}$, no matter how one chooses to define $0^{0}$.[^o33]

On the other hand, if $f$ and $g$ are [analytic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_functions) on an open neighborhood of a number $c$, then $f(t)^{g(t)} \to 1$ as $t$ approaches $c$ from any side on which $f$ is positive.[^o21] This and more general results can be obtained by studying the limiting behavior of the function $\log(f(t)^{g(t)})=g(t)\log f(t)$.[^o5][^o6]

## Complex exponents {#Complex_exponents}

In the [complex domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_domain), the function $z^{w}$ may be defined for nonzero $z$ by choosing a [branch](wikipedia://en/4870290#Branches_of_the_complex_logarithm) of $\log z$ and defining $z^{w}$ as $e^{w \log z}$. This does not define $0^{w}$ since there is no branch of $\log z$ defined at $z = 0$, let alone in a neighborhood of $0$.[^o34][^o35][^o36]

## History {#History}

### As a value {#As_a_value}

In 1752, [Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) in *[Introductio in analysin infinitorum](wikipedia://en/19777721)* wrote that $a^{0} = 1$[^o22] and explicitly mentioned that $0^{0} = 1$.[^o37] An annotation attributed[^o23] to [Mascheroni](wikipedia://en/88116) in a 1787 edition of Euler's book *[Institutiones calculi differentialis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutiones_calculi_differentialis)*[^o38] offered the "justification"

$$0^0 = (a-a)^{n-n} = \frac{(a-a)^n}{(a-a)^n} = 1$$

as well as another more involved justification. In the 1830s, [Libri](wikipedia://en/2642486)[^o39][^o23] published several further arguments attempting to justify the claim $0^{0} = 1$, though these were far from convincing, even by standards of rigor at the time.[^o24]

### As a limiting form {#As_a_limiting_form}

Euler, when setting $0^{0} = 1$, mentioned that consequently the values of the function $0^{x}$ take a "huge jump", from $\infty$ for $x < 0$, to $1$ at $x = 0$, to $0$ for $x > 0$.[^o22] In 1814, [Pfaff](wikipedia://en/1669324) used a [squeeze theorem](wikipedia://en/330994) argument to prove that $x^{x} \to 1$ as $x \to 0^{+}$.[^o21]

On the other hand, in 1821 [Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842)[^o40] explained why the limit of $x^{y}$ as positive numbers $x$ and $y$ approach $0$ *while being constrained by some fixed relation* could be made to assume any value between $0$ and $\infty$ by choosing the relation appropriately. He deduced that the limit of the full *two-variable* function $x^{y}$ without a specified constraint is "indeterminate". With this justification, he listed $0^{0}$ along with expressions like $\tfrac{0}{0}$ in a [table of indeterminate forms](wikipedia://en/366723#List_of_indeterminate_forms).

Apparently unaware of Cauchy's work, [Möbius](wikipedia://en/37875)[^o21] in 1834, building on Pfaff's argument, claimed incorrectly that $f(x)^{g(x)} \to 1$ whenever $f(x), g(x) \to 0$ as $x$ approaches a number $c$ (presumably $f$ is assumed positive away from $c$). Möbius reduced to the case $c = 0$, but then made the mistake of assuming that each of $f$ and $g$ could be expressed in the form $Px^{n}$ for some continuous function $P$ not vanishing at $0$ and some nonnegative integer $n$, which is true for analytic functions, but not in general. An anonymous commentator pointed out the unjustified step;[^o25] then another commentator who signed his name simply as "S" provided the explicit counterexamples $(e^{-1/x})^{x} \to e^{-1}$ and $(e^{-1/x})^{2x} \to e^{-2}$ as $x \to 0^{+}$ and expressed the situation by writing that "$0^{0}$ can have many different values".[^o25]

### Current situation {#Current_situation}

- Some authors define $0^{0}$ as $1$ because it simplifies many theorem statements. According to Benson (1999), "The choice whether to define $0^{0}$ is based on convenience, not on correctness. If we refrain from defining $0^{0}$, then certain assertions become unnecessarily awkward. ... The consensus is to use the definition $0^{0} = 1$, although there are textbooks that refrain from defining $0^{0}.$"[^o26] [Knuth](wikipedia://en/8095) (1992) contends more strongly that $0^{0}$ "*has* to be $1$"; he draws a distinction between the *value* $0^{0}$, which should equal $1$, and the *limiting form* $0^{0}$ (an abbreviation for a limit of $f(t)^{g(t)}$ where $f(t), g(t) \to 0$), which is an indeterminate form: "Both Cauchy and Libri were right, but Libri and his defenders did not understand why truth was on their side."[^o24]
- Other authors leave $0^{0}$ undefined because $0^{0}$ is an indeterminate form.[^o41][^o42]

There do not seem to be any authors assigning $0^{0}$ a specific value other than 1.[^o26]

## Treatment on computers {#Treatment_on_computers}

### IEEE floating-point standard {#IEEE_floating-point_standard}

The [IEEE 754-2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_754-2008) floating-point standard is used in the design of most floating-point libraries. It recommends a number of operations for computing a power:[^o27]

- `pown` (whose exponent is an integer) treats $0^{0}$ as $1$; see .
- `pow` (whose intent is to return a non-[NaN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NaN) result when the exponent is an integer, like `pown`) treats $0^{0}$ as $1$.
- `powr` treats $0^{0}$ as NaN (Not-a-Number) due to the indeterminate form; see .

The `pow` variant is inspired by the `pow` function from [C99](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C99), mainly for compatibility.[^o43] It is useful mostly for languages with a single power function. The `pown` and `powr` variants have been introduced due to conflicting usage of the power functions and the different points of view (as stated above).[^o44]

### Programming languages {#Programming_languages}

The C and C++ standards do not specify the result of $0^{0}$ (a domain error may occur). But for C, as of [C99](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C99), if the [normative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normative) annex F is supported, the result for real floating-point types is required to be $1$ because there are significant applications for which this value is more useful than [NaN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NaN)[^o45] (for instance, with discrete exponents); the result on complex types is not specified, even if the informative annex G is supported. The [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_%28programming_language%29) [method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_%28computer_science%29) `java.lang.Math.pow`,[^o46] the [.NET Framework](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/.NET_Framework) [method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_%28computer_science%29) `System.Math.Pow`,[^o47] [Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_%28programming_language%29), and [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29)[^o48][^o49] also treat $0^{0}$ as $1$. Some languages document that their exponentiation operation corresponds to the `pow` function from the [C mathematical library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_mathematical_functions); this is the case with [Lua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lua_%28programming_language%29)'s `^` operator[^o50] and [Perl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perl)'s `**` operator[^o51] (where it is explicitly mentioned that the result of `0**0` is platform-dependent).

### Mathematical and scientific software {#Mathematical_and_scientific_software}

[R](wikipedia://en/376707),[^o52] [SageMath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SageMath),[^o53] and [PARI/GP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PARI/GP)[^o29] evaluate $x^{0}$ to $1$. [Mathematica](wikipedia://en/49024)[^o28] simplifies $x^{0}$ to $1$ even if no constraints are placed on $x$; however, if $0^{0}$ is entered directly, it is treated as an error or indeterminate. [Mathematica](wikipedia://en/49024)[^o28] and [PARI/GP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PARI/GP)[^o29][^o54] further distinguish between integer and floating-point values: If the exponent is a zero of integer type, they return a $1$ of the type of the base; exponentiation with a floating-point exponent of value zero is treated as undefined, indeterminate or error.
