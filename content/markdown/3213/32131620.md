# Cyclically ordered group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cyclically ordered group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **cyclically ordered group** is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) with both a [group structure](wikipedia://en/19447) and a [cyclic order](wikipedia://en/434673), such that left and right multiplication both preserve the cyclic order.

:::

Cyclically ordered groups were first studied in depth by [Ladislav Rieger](wikipedia://en/31627584) in 1947.[^n1] They are a generalization of [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327): the [infinite cyclic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_cyclic_group) $\mathbf{Z}$ and the [finite cyclic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_cyclic_group) $\mathbf{Z}/n$. Since a [linear order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_order) induces a cyclic order, cyclically ordered groups are also a generalization of [linearly ordered groups](wikipedia://en/349014): the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) $\mathbf{Q}$, the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\mathbf{R}$, and so on. Some of the most important cyclically ordered groups fall into neither previous category: the [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177) $\mathbf{T}$ and its [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011), such as the [subgroup of rational points](wikipedia://en/29508075).

## Quotients of linear groups {#Quotients_of_linear_groups}

It is natural to depict cyclically ordered groups as [quotients](wikipedia://en/11526): one has $\mathbf{Z}_{n} = \mathbf{Z}/n\mathbf{Z}$ and $\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}$. Even a once-linear group like $\mathbf{Z}$, when bent into a circle, can be thought of as $\mathbf{Z}^{2} / \mathbf{Z}$. (txt) showed that this picture is a generic phenomenon. For any ordered group $L$ and any [central](wikipedia://en/7125) element $z$ that generates a [cofinal subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofinal_subgroup) $Z$ of $L$, the quotient group $L / Z$ is a cyclically ordered group. Moreover, every cyclically ordered group can be expressed as such a quotient group.[^n2]

## The circle group {#The_circle_group}

(Świerczkowski 1959a) built upon Rieger's results in another direction. Given a cyclically ordered group $K$ and an ordered group $L$, the product $K \times L$ is a cyclically ordered group. In particular, if $\mathbf{T}$ is the circle group and $L$ is an ordered group, then any subgroup of $\mathbf{T} \times L$ is a cyclically ordered group. Moreover, every cyclically ordered group can be expressed as a subgroup of such a product with $\mathbf{T}$.[^n3]

By analogy with an [Archimedean linearly ordered group](wikipedia://en/264169), one can define an Archimedean cyclically ordered group as a group that does not contain any pair of elements $x, y$ such that $[e, x^{n}, y]$ for every positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $n$.[^n3] Since only positive $n$ are considered, this is a stronger condition than its linear counterpart. For example, $\mathbf{Z}$ no longer qualifies, since one has $[0, n, -1]$ for every $n$.

As a corollary to Świerczkowski's proof, every Archimedean cyclically ordered group is a subgroup of $\mathbf{T}$ itself.[^n3] This result is analogous to [Otto Hölder](wikipedia://en/191652)'s 1901 theorem that every Archimedean linearly ordered group is a subgroup of $\mathbf{R}$.[^o11]

## Topology {#Topology}

Every [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) cyclically ordered group is a subgroup of $\mathbf{T}$.

## Related structures {#Related_structures}

(Gluschankof 1993) showed that a certain [subcategory](wikipedia://en/310959) of cyclically ordered groups, the "projectable Ic-groups with weak unit", is [equivalent](wikipedia://en/379051) to a certain subcategory of [MV-algebras](wikipedia://en/2483815), the "projectable MV-algebras".[^n4]
