# Closed point

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Closed point"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **closed point** of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) is a [point](wikipedia://en/593693) whose [singleton](wikipedia://en/381798) is [closed](wikipedia://en/47279). In many areas of [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) and [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), all spaces under consideration are [T<sub>1</sub> spaces](wikipedia://en/148721) that only have closed points. The distinction between closed and non-closed points is most often made in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), where [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) can have non-closed points.

:::

## Definition and motivation {#Definition_and_motivation}

If $X$ is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), a point $x \in X$ is called **closed** if the singleton $\{x\}$ is [closed](wikipedia://en/47279).[^o3] An equivalent statement is that the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) $\overline{\{x\}}$ only contains $x$.

The closed points of a space $X$ can also be defined using the [specialization preorder](wikipedia://en/542347) on $X$. Given points $x,y \in X$, $x$ specializes to $y$ if $y \in \overline{\{x\}}$. This means that the closed points of a topological space are those that specialize to no point except themselves, that is, the [most specific](wikipedia://en/303398) points.

Spaces where every point is closed, called [T<sub>1</sub> spaces](wikipedia://en/148721), are common.[^o4] In most branches of mathematics, it is rare to encounter spaces that have any non-closed points. Many mathematicians regard such spaces as somewhat strange. For example, if $x$ specializes to $y$, the [constant sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constant_sequence) $(x,x,x,...)$ [converges](wikipedia://en/26551602) to $y$ (as well as $x$).[^o5][^o6]

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) usually have many non-closed points, including points whose closure $\overline{\{x\}}$ is rather large. In particular, every [irreducible component](wikipedia://en/2652476) of a scheme $X$ is of the form $\overline{\{x\}}$ for some $x \in X$. This can make the study of schemes easier since some properties of $x$ extend to the entirety of $\overline{\{x\}}$.[^o7]

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: SpecZ original.png.* The spectrum of **[Z](wikipedia://en/14563)**, which is a [PID](wikipedia://en/25039). The generic point is depicted as a grey line that contains all other points.

- Most of the typical examples of spaces are [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637), and in particular [T<sub>1</sub>](wikipedia://en/148721).[^o10] This includes [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470).[^o11] Other examples of T<sub>1</sub> spaces include [non-Hausdorff manifolds](wikipedia://en/14194283),[^o12] [algebraic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_varieties),[^o13] and spaces with the [cofinite topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofinite_topology).[^o14]
- When an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) is considered as a [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754), every [Zariski](wikipedia://en/241744)-closed [subvariety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subvariety_%28algebraic_geometry%29) of it is endowed with an additional point, which is [generic](wikipedia://en/3043978) in that subvariety. The original points of the variety are the closed points of the resulting scheme.[^o15]
- In the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/37291) of a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346), the points are the [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) of the ring, and the closed points are the [maximal ideals](wikipedia://en/48164).[^o3]
- In particular, the points of the spectrum of a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039) are the ideals generated by [prime elements](wikipedia://en/60692) (defined [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666)) and the [zero ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_ideal). The points that correspond to prime elements are closed, and the point that corresponds to zero is generic.[^o16]
- The [Sierpiński space](wikipedia://en/387651) has two points. One of them is closed, and the other is non-closed since it specializes to the first point.[^o17]
- The closed points of a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) with the [Alexandrov topology](wikipedia://en/542198#Correspondence_with_preordered_sets) are the [minimal elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_element).[^o18]
- If two points are [topologically indistinguishable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologically_indistinguishable), the closure of either of them contains both and hence neither of them is closed.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

In any scheme that is locally [of finite type](wikipedia://en/70728366) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), the set of closed points is [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594).[^o21] In particular, this is true for schemes that correspond to algebraic varieties.[^o3] This is not always the case, even for an [affine scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_scheme). For example, the spectrum of a [discrete valuation ring](wikipedia://en/848633) is (topologically) the aforementioned [Sierpiński space](wikipedia://en/387651).[^o22] Nonempty [quasi-compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-compact) schemes (and in particular affine schemes) must have at least one closed point.[^o23] However, there are schemes without any closed points at all,[^o24] including [irreducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreducible_scheme) schemes.[^o23]

In any scheme that is locally of finite type over a field $k$, the [residue field](wikipedia://en/10789680) is [finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_extension) over $k$ at closed points and [transcendental](wikipedia://en/287364) over $k$ at non-closed points.[^o25] In particular, if $k$ is algebraically closed, the closed points are exactly those where the residue field is $k$ itself.[^o26][^o27] This implies that every $k$-[rational point](wikipedia://en/2960200) is closed, and if $k$ is algebraically closed then the closed points are exactly the $k$-rational points.[^o28] In a scheme of finite type over $\mathbb{Z}$, the closed points are exactly the points where the residue field is [finite](wikipedia://en/11615), and each finite field is the residue field at only finitely many points. This makes it possible to define the [arithmetic zeta function](wikipedia://en/34471935) of such a scheme.[^o29][^o30]

Let $X$ be affine scheme (or equivalently, a [spectral space](wikipedia://en/544273)). $X$ is [normal](wikipedia://en/48629) if and only if its closed points can be [separated by neighborhoods](wikipedia://en/148911).[^o31] If the space of closed points of $X$ is [connected](wikipedia://en/6233), $X$ is connected too, and the converse holds if $X$ is normal.[^o32] If $X$ is normal, the space of closed points of $X$ is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) (and [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637)).[^o33] A normal affine scheme is simply the spectrum of a commutative [Gelfand ring](wikipedia://en/43823678),[^o34] so these are in fact properties of the [maximal spectra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_spectrum) of such rings.

:::

## Locally closed point {#Locally_closed_point}

A **locally closed point**, or a **Goldman point**, is a point $x \in X$ such that the singleton $\{x\}$ is [locally closed](wikipedia://en/26136898). This is equivalent to the condition that $x$ is [isolated](wikipedia://en/390557) in $\overline{\{x\}}$. Every closed point is locally closed.[^o36]

Unlike the case of closed points, the locally closed points are dense in every affine scheme.[^o37]
