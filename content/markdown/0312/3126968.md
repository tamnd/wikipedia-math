# Boolean expression

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Boolean expression"}

In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), a **Boolean expression** (also known as **logical expression**) is an [expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expression_%28programming%29) used in [programming languages](wikipedia://en/23015) that produces a [Boolean value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_value) when evaluated. A Boolean value is either **true** or **false**. A Boolean expression may be composed of a combination of the Boolean constants **True/False** or **Yes/No**, [Boolean-typed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_data_type) variables, Boolean-valued operators, and [Boolean-valued functions](wikipedia://en/2757237).[^o2]

:::

Boolean expressions correspond to [propositional formulas](wikipedia://en/1557634) in logic and are associated to [Boolean circuits](wikipedia://en/7087423).[^o3]

::: {#o5 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Boolean operator (computer programming)"}

## Boolean operators {#Boolean_operators}

Most [programming languages](wikipedia://en/23015) have the Boolean operators [OR](wikipedia://en/7962), [AND](wikipedia://en/18152) and [NOT](wikipedia://en/161019); in [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28programming_language%29) and some [languages inspired by it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_C-family_programming_languages), these are represented by "||" (double pipe character), "&&" (double [ampersand](wikipedia://en/59153)) and "!" ([exclamation point](wikipedia://en/7712754)) respectively, while the corresponding [bitwise operations](wikipedia://en/264399) are represented by "|", "&" and "~" (tilde).[^o6] In the mathematical literature the symbols used are often "+" ([plus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plus_sign)), "**·**" ([dot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_stop)) and [overbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overbar), or "∨" ([vel](wikipedia://en/37701611)), "∧" ([et](wikipedia://en/37702563)) and "¬" ([not](wikipedia://en/161019)) or "′" (prime).

Some languages, e.g., [Perl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perl) and [Ruby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruby_%28programming_language%29), have two sets of Boolean operators, with identical functions but different precedence. Typically these languages use **and**, **or** and **not** for the lower precedence operators.

Some programming languages derived from [PL/I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PL/I) have a bit string type and use BIT(1) rather than a separate Boolean type. In those languages the same operators serve for Boolean operations and bitwise operations. The languages represent OR, AND, NOT and EXCLUSIVE OR by "|", "&", "¬" (infix) and "¬" (prefix).

### Short-circuit operators {#Short-circuit_operators}

Some programming languages, e.g., [Ada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_%28programming_language%29), have [short-circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short-circuit_evaluation) Boolean operators. These operators use a [lazy evaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lazy_evaluation), that is, if the value of the expression can be determined from the left hand Boolean expression then they do not evaluate the right hand Boolean expression. As a result, there may be [side effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Side_effect_%28computer_science%29) that only occur for one value of the left hand operand.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- The expression 5 > 3 is evaluated as true.
- The expression 3 > 5 is evaluated as false.
- 5>=3 and 3\<=5 are equivalent Boolean expressions, both of which are evaluated as true.
- Of course, most Boolean expressions will contain at least one variable (X > 3), and often more (X > Y).
