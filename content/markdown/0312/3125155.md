# Left-right planarity test

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Left-right planarity test"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **left-right planarity test** or **de Fraysseix–Rosenstiehl planarity criterion**[^o2] is a characterization of [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314) based on the properties of the [depth-first search trees](wikipedia://en/30247317), published by (txt)[^o4][^o5] and used by them with [Patrice Ossona de Mendez](wikipedia://en/47882601) to develop a [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time) [planarity testing](wikipedia://en/15972636) [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775).[^o6][^o7] In a 2003 experimental comparison of six planarity testing algorithms, this was one of the fastest algorithms tested.[^o8]

:::

## T-alike and T-opposite edges {#T-alike_and_T-opposite_edges}

For any [depth-first search](wikipedia://en/97034) of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) *G*, the [edges](wikipedia://en/12401) encountered when discovering a [vertex](wikipedia://en/638899) for the first time define a depth-first search tree *T* of *G*. This is a [Trémaux tree](wikipedia://en/30247317), meaning that the remaining edges (the **cotree**) each connect a pair of vertices that are related to each other as an ancestor and descendant in *T*. Three types of patterns can be used to define two relations between pairs of cotree edges, named the ***T*-alike** and ***T*-opposite** relations.

In the following figures, simple circle nodes represent vertices, double circle nodes represent subtrees, twisted segments represent tree paths, and curved arcs represent cotree edges. The root of each tree is shown at the bottom of the figure. In the first figure, the edges labeled $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are *T*-alike, meaning that, at the endpoints nearest the root of the tree, they will both be on the same side of the tree in every planar drawing. In the next two figures, the edges with the same labels are *T*-opposite, meaning that they will be on different sides of the tree in every planar drawing.

## The characterization {#The_characterization}

Let *G* be a graph and let *T* be a Trémaux tree of *G*. The graph *G* is planar if and only if there exists a partition of the cotree edges of *G* into two classes so that any two edges belong to the same class if they are *T*-alike and any two edges belong to different classes if they are *T*-opposite.

This characterization immediately leads to an (inefficient) planarity test: determine for all pairs of edges whether they are *T*-alike or *T*-opposite, form an auxiliary graph that has a vertex for each [connected component](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28graph_theory%29) of *T*-alike edges and an edge for each pair of *T*-opposite edges, and check whether this auxiliary graph is [bipartite](wikipedia://en/244431). Making this algorithm efficient involves finding a subset of the *T*-alike and *T*-opposite pairs that is sufficient to carry out this method without determining the relation between all edge pairs in the input graph.
