# Decomposition of spectrum (functional analysis)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Decomposition of spectrum"}

The [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137) of a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) $T$ that operates on a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) $X$ is a fundamental concept of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180). The spectrum consists of all [scalars](wikipedia://en/3588331) $\lambda$ such that the operator $T-\lambda$ does not have a bounded [inverse](wikipedia://en/14907) on $X$. The spectrum has a standard **decomposition** into three parts:

:::

- a **point spectrum**, consisting of the [eigenvalues](wikipedia://en/2161429) of $T$;
- a **continuous spectrum**, consisting of the scalars that are not eigenvalues but make the range of $T-\lambda$ a [proper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_subset) [dense subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_subset) of the space;
- a **residual spectrum**, consisting of all other scalars in the spectrum.

This decomposition is relevant to the study of [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309), and has applications to many branches of science and engineering. A well-known example from [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) is the explanation for the [discrete spectral lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_spectrum_%28physics%29) and the continuous band in the light emitted by [excited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excited_state) atoms of [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen).

## Decomposition into point spectrum, continuous spectrum, and residual spectrum {#Decomposition_into_point_spectrum,_continuous_spectrum,_and_residual_spectrum}

### For bounded Banach space operators {#For_bounded_Banach_space_operators}

Let *X* be a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), *B*(*X*) the family of [bounded operators](wikipedia://en/455961) on *X*, and $T \in B(X)$. By [definition](wikipedia://en/206137), a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) *λ* is in the **spectrum** of *T*, denoted *σ*(*T*), if $T - \lambda$ does not have an inverse in *B*(*X*).

If $T - \lambda$ is [one-to-one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) and [onto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective), i.e. [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective), then its inverse is bounded; this follows directly from the [open mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/17395276) of functional analysis. So, *λ* is in the spectrum of *T* if and only if $T - \lambda$ is not one-to-one or not onto. One distinguishes three separate cases:

1. $T - \lambda$ is not [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective). That is, there exist two distinct elements *x*,*y* in *X* such that $(T - \lambda)(x) = (T - \lambda)(y)$. Then $z = x - y$ is a non-zero vector such that $T(z) = \lambda z$. In other words, *λ* is an eigenvalue of *T* in the sense of [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422). In this case, *λ* is said to be in the **point spectrum** of *T*, denoted $\sigma_{p}(T)$.
1. $T - \lambda$ is injective, and its [range](wikipedia://en/275991) is a [dense subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_subset) *R* of *X*; but is not the whole of *X*. In other words, there exists some element *x* in *X* such that $(T - \lambda)(y)$ can be as close to *x* as desired, with *y* in *X*; but is never equal to *x*. It can be proved that, in this case, $T - \lambda$ is not bounded below (i.e. it sends far apart elements of *X* too close together). Equivalently, the inverse linear operator $(T - \lambda)^{-1}$, which is defined on the dense subset *R*, is not a bounded operator, and therefore cannot be extended to the whole of *X*. Then *λ* is said to be in the **continuous spectrum**, $\sigma_{c}(T)$, of *T*.
1. $T - \lambda$ is injective but does not have dense range. That is, there is some element *x* in *X* and a neighborhood *N* of *x* such that $(T - \lambda)(y)$ is never in *N*. In this case, the map $(T - \lambda)^{-1} x \to x$ may be bounded or unbounded, but in any case does not admit a unique extension to a bounded linear map on all of *X*. Then *λ* is said to be in the **residual spectrum** of *T*, $\sigma_{r}(T)$.

So *σ*(*T*) is the disjoint union of these three sets,

$$\sigma(T) = \sigma_p (T) \cup \sigma_c (T) \cup \sigma_r (T).$$

The complement of the spectrum $\sigma(T)$ is known as [resolvent set](wikipedia://en/8721698) $\rho(T)$ that is $\rho(T)=\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(T)$.

::: {#o4 .table cue=table}

| Surjectivity of $T - \lambda$ | Injectivity of $T - \lambda$ |  |  |
|---|---|---|---|
|  | Injective and bounded below | Injective but not bounded below | not injective |
| Surjective | Resolvent set $\rho(T)$ | Nonexistent | Point spectrum $\sigma_{p}(T)$ |
| Not surjective but has dense range | Nonexistent | Continuous spectrum $\sigma_{c}(T)$ |  |
| Does not have dense range | Residual spectrum $\sigma_{r}(T)$ |  |  |

:::

In addition, when $T - \lambda$ does not have dense range, whether is injective or not, then *λ* is said to be in the **compression spectrum** of *T*, *σ<sub>cp</sub>*(*T*). The compression spectrum consists of the whole residual spectrum and part of point spectrum.

### For unbounded operators {#For_unbounded_operators}

The spectrum of an [unbounded operator](wikipedia://en/1422584) can be divided into three parts in the same way as in the bounded case, but because the operator is not defined everywhere, the definitions of domain, inverse, etc. are more involved.

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

#### Multiplication operator {#Multiplication_operator}

Given a σ-finite [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270) (*S*, *Σ*, *μ*), consider the Banach space [*L<sup>p</sup>*(*μ*)](wikipedia://en/45194). A function *h*: *S* → **C** is called [essentially bounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essentially_bounded) if *h* is bounded *μ*-almost everywhere. An essentially bounded *h* induces a bounded multiplication operator *T<sub>h</sub>* on *L<sup>p</sup>*(*μ*):

$$(T_h f)(s) = h(s) \cdot f(s).$$

The operator norm of *T* is the essential supremum of *h*. The [essential range](wikipedia://en/18380182) of *h* is defined in the following way: a complex number *λ* is in the essential range of *h* if for all *ε* > 0, the preimage of the open ball *B<sub>ε</sub>*(*λ*) under *h* has strictly positive measure. We will show first that *σ*(*T<sub>h</sub>*) coincides with the essential range of *h* and then examine its various parts.

If *λ* is not in the essential range of *h*, take *ε* > 0 such that *h*<sup>−1</sup>(*B<sub>ε</sub>*(*λ*)) has zero measure. The function *g*(*s*) = 1/(*h*(*s*) − *λ*) is bounded almost everywhere by 1/*ε*. The multiplication operator *T<sub>g</sub>* satisfies $T_{g} \cdot (T_{h} - \lambda) = (T_{h} - \lambda) \cdot T_{g} = I$. So *λ* does not lie in spectrum of *T<sub>h</sub>*. On the other hand, if *λ* lies in the essential range of *h*, consider the sequence of sets $\{S_{n} = h^{-1}(B_{1/n}(\lambda))\}$. Each *S<sub>n</sub>* has positive measure. Let *f<sub>n</sub>* be the characteristic function of *S<sub>n</sub>*. We can compute directly

$$\| (T_h - \lambda) f_n \|_p ^p = \| (h - \lambda) f_n \|_p ^p = \int_{S_n} | h - \lambda \; |^p d \mu \leq \frac{1}{n^p} \; \mu(S_n) = \frac{1}{n^p} \| f_n \|_p ^p.$$

This shows $T_{h} - \lambda$ is not bounded below, therefore not invertible.

If *λ* is such that *μ*( *h*<sup>−1</sup>({*λ*})) > 0, then *λ* lies in the point spectrum of *T<sub>h</sub>* as follows. Let *f* be the characteristic function of the measurable set *h*<sup>−1</sup>(*λ*), then by considering two cases, we find

$$\forall s \in S, \; (T_h f)(s) = \lambda f(s),$$

so λ is an eigenvalue of *T*<sub>*h*</sub>.

Any *λ* in the essential range of *h* that does not have a positive measure preimage is in the continuous spectrum of *T<sub>h</sub>*. To show this, we must show that $T_{h} - \lambda$ has dense range. Given $f \in L^{p}(\mu)$, again we consider the sequence of sets $\{S_{n} = h^{-1}(B_{1/n}(\lambda))\}$. Let *g<sub>n</sub>* be the characteristic function of $S - S_{n}$. Define

$$f_n(s) = \frac{1}{ h(s) - \lambda} \cdot g_n(s) \cdot f(s).$$

Direct calculation shows that *f<sub>n</sub>* ∈ *L<sup>p</sup>*(*μ*), with $\| f_n\|_p\leq n \|f\|_p$. Then by the [dominated convergence theorem](wikipedia://en/351853),

$$(T_h - \lambda) f_n \rightarrow f$$

in the *L<sup>p</sup>*(*μ*) norm.

Therefore, multiplication operators have no residual spectrum. In particular, by the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495), [normal operators](wikipedia://en/190167) on a Hilbert space have no residual spectrum.

#### Shifts {#Shifts}

In the special case when *S* is the set of natural numbers and *μ* is the counting measure, the corresponding *L<sup>p</sup>*(*μ*) is denoted by l<sup>*p*</sup>. This space consists of complex valued sequences {*x<sub>n</sub>*} such that

$$\sum_{n \geq 0} | x_n |^p < \infty.$$

For 1 \< *p* \< ∞, *l <sup>p</sup>* is [reflexive](wikipedia://en/210091). Define the [left shift](wikipedia://en/467384) *T* : *l <sup>p</sup>* → *l <sup>p</sup>* by

$$T(x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots) = (x_2, x_3, x_4, \dots).$$

*T* is a [partial isometry](wikipedia://en/1348079) with operator norm 1. So *σ*(*T*) lies in the closed unit disk of the complex plane.

*T\** is the right shift (or [unilateral shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unilateral_shift)), which is an isometry on *l <sup>q</sup>*, where 1/*p* + 1/*q* = 1:

$$T^*(x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots) = (0, x_1, x_2, \dots).$$

For *λ* ∈ **C** with |*λ*| \< 1,

$$x = (1, \lambda, \lambda ^2, \dots) \in l^p$$

and *T x* = *λ x*. Consequently, the point spectrum of *T* contains the open unit disk. Now, *T\** has no eigenvalues, i.e. *σ<sub>p</sub>*(*T\**) is empty. Thus, invoking reflexivity and the theorem in [Spectrum\_(functional\_analysis)#Spectrum\_of\_the\_adjoint\_operator](wikipedia://en/206137#Spectrum_of_the_adjoint_operator) (that *σ<sub>p</sub>*(*T*) ⊂ *σ<sub>r</sub>*(*T*\*) ∪ *σ<sub>p</sub>*(*T*\*)), we can deduce that the open unit disk lies in the residual spectrum of *T\**.

The spectrum of a bounded operator is closed, which implies the unit circle, { |*λ*| = 1 } ⊂ **C**, is in *σ*(*T*). Again by reflexivity of *l <sup>p</sup>* and the theorem given above (this time, that $\sigma_{r}(T) \subset \sigma_{p}(T*)$), we have that *σ<sub>r</sub>*(*T*) is also empty. Therefore, for a complex number *λ* with unit norm, one must have *λ* ∈ *σ<sub>p</sub>*(*T*) or *λ* ∈ *σ<sub>c</sub>*(*T*). Now if |*λ*| = 1 and

$$T x = \lambda x, \qquad i.e. \; (x_2, x_3, x_4, \dots) = \lambda (x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots),$$

then

$$x = x_1 (1, \lambda, \lambda^2, \dots),$$

which cannot be in *l <sup>p</sup>*, a contradiction. This means the unit circle must lie in the continuous spectrum of *T*.

So for the left shift *T*, *σ<sub>p</sub>*(*T*) is the open unit disk and *σ<sub>c</sub>*(*T*) is the unit circle, whereas for the right shift *T\**, *σ<sub>r</sub>*(*T\**) is the open unit disk and *σ<sub>c</sub>*(*T\**) is the unit circle.

For *p* = 1, one can perform a similar analysis. The results will not be exactly the same, since reflexivity no longer holds.

:::

## Self-adjoint operators on Hilbert space {#Self-adjoint_operators_on_Hilbert_space}

[Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932) are Banach spaces, so the above discussion applies to bounded operators on Hilbert spaces as well. A subtle point concerns the spectrum of *T*\*. For a Banach space, *T*\* denotes the transpose and *σ*(*T\**) = *σ*(*T*). For a Hilbert space, *T*\* normally denotes the [adjoint](wikipedia://en/667175) of an operator *T* ∈ *B*(*H*), not the transpose, and *σ*(*T\**) is not *σ*(*T*) but rather its image under complex conjugation.

For a self-adjoint *T* ∈ *B*(*H*), the [Borel functional calculus](wikipedia://en/876294) gives additional ways to break up the spectrum naturally.

### Borel functional calculus {#Borel_functional_calculus}

Borel functional calculus

This subsection briefly sketches the development of this calculus. The idea is to first establish the continuous functional calculus, and then pass to measurable functions via the [Riesz–Markov–Kakutani representation theorem](wikipedia://en/38948504). For the continuous functional calculus, the key ingredients are the following:

1. If *T* is self-adjoint, then for any polynomial *P*, the operator norm satisfies $$\| P(T) \| = \sup_{\lambda \in \sigma(T)} |P(\lambda)|.$$
1. The [Stone–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/28858), which implies that the family of polynomials (with complex coefficients), is dense in *C*(*σ*(*T*)), the continuous functions on *σ*(*T*).

The family *C*(*σ*(*T*)) is a [Banach algebra](wikipedia://en/4665) when endowed with the uniform norm. So the mapping

$$P \rightarrow P(T)$$

is an isometric homomorphism from a dense subset of *C*(*σ*(*T*)) to *B*(*H*). Extending the mapping by continuity gives *f*(*T*) for *f* ∈ C(*σ*(*T*)): let *P<sub>n</sub>* be polynomials such that *P<sub>n</sub>* → *f* uniformly and define *f*(*T*) = lim *P<sub>n</sub>*(*T*). This is the continuous functional calculus.

For a fixed *h* ∈ *H*, we notice that

$$f \rightarrow \langle h, f(T) h \rangle$$

is a positive linear functional on *C*(*σ*(*T*)). According to the Riesz–Markov–Kakutani representation theorem a unique measure *μ<sub>h</sub>* on *σ*(*T*) exists such that

$$\int_{\sigma(T)} f \, d \mu_h = \langle h, f(T) h \rangle.$$

This measure is sometimes called the **[spectral measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_measure)** associated to *h*. The spectral measures can be used to extend the continuous functional calculus to bounded Borel functions. For a bounded function *g* that is Borel measurable, define, for a proposed *g*(*T*)

$$\int_{\sigma(T)} g \, d \mu_h = \langle h, g(T) h \rangle.$$

Via the [polarization identity](wikipedia://en/2047965), one can recover (since *H* is assumed to be complex)

$$\langle k, g(T) h \rangle.$$

and therefore *g*(*T*) *h* for arbitrary *h*.

In the present context, the spectral measures, combined with a result from measure theory, give a decomposition of *σ*(*T*).

### Decomposition into absolutely continuous, singular continuous, and pure point {#Decomposition_into_absolutely_continuous,_singular_continuous,_and_pure_point}

Let *h* ∈ *H* and *μ<sub>h</sub>* be its corresponding spectral measure on *σ*(*T*). According to a refinement of [Lebesgue's decomposition theorem](wikipedia://en/4316238), *μ<sub>h</sub>* can be decomposed into three mutually singular parts:

$$\mu_h = \mu_{\mathrm{ac}} + \mu_{\mathrm{sc}} + \mu_{\mathrm{pp}}$$

where *μ*<sub>ac</sub> is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, *μ*<sub>sc</sub> is singular with respect to the Lebesgue measure and atomless, and *μ*<sub>pp</sub> is a pure point measure.[^n1][^n2]

All three types of measures are invariant under linear operations. Let *H*<sub>ac</sub> be the subspace consisting of vectors whose spectral measures are absolutely continuous with respect to the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198). Define *H*<sub>pp</sub> and *H*<sub>sc</sub> in analogous fashion. These subspaces are invariant under *T*. For example, if *h* ∈ *H*<sub>ac</sub> and *k* = *T h*. Let *χ* be the characteristic function of some Borel set in *σ*(*T*), then

$$\langle k, \chi(T) k \rangle = \int_{\sigma(T)} \chi(\lambda) \cdot \lambda^2 d \mu_{h}(\lambda) = \int_{\sigma(T)} \chi(\lambda) \; d \mu_k(\lambda).$$

So

$$\lambda^2 d \mu_{h} = d \mu_{k}$$

and *k* ∈ *H*<sub>ac</sub>. Furthermore, applying the spectral theorem gives

$$H = H_{\mathrm{ac}} \oplus H_{\mathrm{sc}} \oplus H_{\mathrm{pp}}.$$

This leads to the following definitions:

1. The spectrum of *T* restricted to *H*<sub>ac</sub> is called the **absolutely continuous spectrum** of *T*, *σ*<sub>ac</sub>(*T*).
1. The spectrum of *T* restricted to *H*<sub>sc</sub> is called its **singular spectrum**, *σ*<sub>sc</sub>(*T*).
1. The set of eigenvalues of *T* is called the **pure point spectrum** of *T*, *σ*<sub>pp</sub>(*T*).

The closure of the eigenvalues is the spectrum of *T* restricted to *H*<sub>pp</sub>.[^n3][^o16] So

$$\sigma(T) = \sigma_{\mathrm{ac}}(T) \cup \sigma_{\mathrm{sc}}(T) \cup {\bar \sigma_{\mathrm{pp}}(T)}.$$

### Comparison {#Comparison}

A bounded self-adjoint operator on Hilbert space is, a fortiori, a bounded operator on a Banach space. Therefore, one can also apply to *T* the decomposition of the spectrum that was achieved above for bounded operators on a Banach space. Unlike the Banach space formulation, the union

$$\sigma(T) = {\bar \sigma_{\mathrm{pp}}(T)} \cup \sigma_{\mathrm{ac}}(T) \cup \sigma_{\mathrm{sc}}(T)$$

need not be disjoint. It is disjoint when the operator *T* is of uniform multiplicity, say *m*, i.e. if *T* is unitarily equivalent to multiplication by *λ* on the direct sum

$$\bigoplus _{i = 1} ^m L^2(\mathbb{R}, \mu_i)$$

for some Borel measures $\mu_i$. When more than one measure appears in the above expression, we see that it is possible for the union of the three types of spectra to not be disjoint. If $\lambda \in \sigma_{ac}(T) \cap \sigma_{pp}(T)$, *λ* is sometimes called an eigenvalue *embedded* in the absolutely continuous spectrum.

When *T* is unitarily equivalent to multiplication by *λ* on

$$L^2(\mathbb{R}, \mu),$$

the decomposition of *σ*(*T*) from Borel functional calculus is a refinement of the Banach space case.

### Quantum mechanics {#Quantum_mechanics}

The preceding comments can be extended to the unbounded self-adjoint operators since Riesz-Markov holds for [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) [Hausdorff spaces](wikipedia://en/13637).

In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/20728), observables are (often unbounded) [self-adjoint operators](wikipedia://en/187408) and their spectra are the possible outcomes of measurements.

The [pure point spectrum](wikipedia://en/187408#Pure_point_spectrum) corresponds to [bound states](wikipedia://en/696912) in the following way:

- A [quantum state](wikipedia://en/30876419) is a bound state if and only if it is finitely [normalizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_amplitude#Normalization) for all times $t\in\mathbb{R}$.[^n4]
- An observable has pure point spectrum if and only if its [eigenstates](wikipedia://en/30876419#Eigenstates_and_pure_states) form an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) of $H$.[^n5]

A particle is said to be in a bound state if it remains "localized" in a bounded region of space.[^n6] Intuitively one might therefore think that the "discreteness" of the spectrum is intimately related to the corresponding states being "localized". However, a careful mathematical analysis shows that this is not true in general.[^n7] For example, consider the function

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} n & \text{if }x \in \left[n, n+\frac{1}{n^4}\right] \\ 0 & \text{else} \end{cases}, \quad \forall n \in \mathbb{N}.$$

This function is normalizable (i.e. $f\in L^2(\mathbb{R})$) as

$$\int_{n}^{n+\frac{1}{n^4}}n^2\,dx = \frac{1}{n^2} \Rightarrow \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |f(x)|^2\,dx = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^2}.$$

Known as the [Basel problem](wikipedia://en/490303), this series converges to $\frac{\pi^2}{6}$. Yet, $f$ increases as $x \to \infty$, i.e., the state "escapes to infinity". The phenomena of [Anderson localization](wikipedia://en/2213942) and [dynamical localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_localization) describe when the eigenfunctions are localized in a physical sense. Anderson Localization means that eigenfunctions decay exponentially as $x \to \infty$. Dynamical localization is more subtle to define.

Sometimes, when performing quantum mechanical measurements, one encounters "[eigenstates](wikipedia://en/30876419#Eigenstates_and_pure_states)" that are not localized, e.g., quantum states that do not lie in *L*<sup>2</sup>(**R**). These are [free states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_particle) belonging to the absolutely continuous spectrum. In the [spectral theorem for unbounded self-adjoint operators](wikipedia://en/123495#Unbounded_self-adjoint_operators), these states are referred to as "generalized eigenvectors" of an observable with "generalized eigenvalues" that do not necessarily belong to its spectrum. Alternatively, if it is insisted that the notion of eigenvectors and eigenvalues survive the passage to the rigorous, one can consider operators on [rigged Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/393048).[^n8]

An example of an observable whose spectrum is purely absolutely continuous is the [position operator](wikipedia://en/4838571) of a free particle moving on the entire real line. Also, since the [momentum operator](wikipedia://en/2288549) is unitarily equivalent to the position operator, via the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247), it has a purely absolutely continuous spectrum as well.

The singular spectrum correspond to physically impossible outcomes. It was believed for some time that the singular spectrum was something artificial. However, examples such as the [almost Mathieu operator](wikipedia://en/16628707) and [random Schrödinger operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_Schrödinger_operator) have shown that all types of spectra arise naturally in physics.[^n9][^n10]

## Decomposition into essential spectrum and discrete spectrum {#Decomposition_into_essential_spectrum_and_discrete_spectrum}

Let $A:\,X\to X$ be a closed operator defined on the domain $D(A)\subset X$ which is dense in *X*. Then there is a decomposition of the spectrum of *A* into a [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946),[^n11]

$$\sigma(A)=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},5}(A)\sqcup\sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(A),$$

where

1. $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},5}(A)$ is the fifth type of the [essential spectrum](wikipedia://en/1350002) of *A* (if *A* is a [self-adjoint operator](wikipedia://en/187408), then $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},k}(A)=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(A)$ for all $1\le k\le 5$);
1. $\sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(A)$ is the [discrete spectrum](wikipedia://en/61992522) of *A*, which consists of [normal eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_eigenvalue), or, equivalently, of [isolated points](wikipedia://en/390557) of $\sigma(A)$ such that the corresponding [Riesz projector](wikipedia://en/61861688) has a finite rank. It is a proper subset of the [point spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_spectrum), i.e., $\sigma_d(A)\subset\sigma_p(A)$, as the set of eigenvalues of *A* need not necessarily be isolated points of the spectrum.
