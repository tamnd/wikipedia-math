# Orbital node

*Image: Orbit1.svg.* The [longitude of the ascending node](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude_of_the_ascending_node) is one of several [orbital elements](wikipedia://en/98663).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Orbital node"}

An **orbital node** is either of the two points where an [orbiting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit) object [intersects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersection_%28Euclidean_geometry%29) a [plane of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_of_reference) inclined with respect to the [orbital plane](wikipedia://en/3074135).[^o2] A [non-inclined orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-inclined_orbit), which is [contained](wikipedia://en/1508434) in the reference plane, has no nodes. The **line of nodes** is the [straight line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_line) resulting from the intersection of the object's orbital plane with the plane of reference; it passes through the two nodes.[^o4]

:::

## Planes of reference {#Planes_of_reference}

Common [planes of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_of_reference) include the following:

- For a [geocentric orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentric_orbit), [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228)'s [equatorial plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_plane). In this case, non-inclined orbits are called *equatorial*.[^o4]
- For a [heliocentric orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentric_orbit), the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264) or [invariable plane](wikipedia://en/1673352). In this case, non-inclined orbits are called *ecliptic*.[^o4]
- For an orbit outside the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System), the plane through the [primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_%28astronomy%29) perpendicular to a line through the observer and the primary (called the *[plane of the sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_of_the_sky)*).[^o5]

::: {#o7 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Ascending node"}

## Node distinction {#Node_distinction}

*Image: Planet orbit nodes 2 animation.gif.* Animation about nodes of two elliptic trajectories. (Click on image.)

If a reference direction from one side of the plane of reference to the other is defined, the two nodes can be distinguished. For geocentric and heliocentric orbits, the **ascending node** (or **north node**) is where the orbiting object moves north through the plane of reference, and the **descending node** (or **south node**) is where it moves south through the plane.[^o8] In the case of objects outside the Solar System, the ascending node is the node where the orbiting secondary passes away from the observer, and the descending node is the node where it moves towards the observer.[^o9]<sup>, p. 137.</sup>

The position of the node may be used as one of a set of parameters, called *[orbital elements](wikipedia://en/98663)*, which describe the orbit. This is done by specifying the [longitude of the ascending node](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude_of_the_ascending_node) (or, sometimes, the [longitude of the node](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude_of_the_node)).

:::

## Symbols and nomenclature {#Symbols_and_nomenclature}

The symbol of the ascending node is

*Image: Ascending node (bold).svg.*

([Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode): U+260A, ☊), and the symbol of the descending node is

*Image: Descending node (bold).svg.*

([Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode): U+260B, ☋).

In medieval and early modern times, the ascending and descending nodes of the Moon in the ecliptic plane were called the "dragon's head" (caput draconis, [رأس الجوزهر](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gochihr_%28Zoroastrianism%29)) and "dragon's tail" (cauda draconis), respectively.[^o11][^o12] These terms originally referred to the times when the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon) crossed the apparent path of the sun in the sky (as in a [solar eclipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_eclipse)). Also, corruptions of the Arabic term such as *ganzaar*, *genzahar*, *geuzaar* and *zeuzahar* were used in the medieval West to denote either of the nodes.[^o13][^o14][^o15]

The [Koine Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koine_Greek) terms αναβιβάζων and καταβιβάζων were also used for the ascending and descending nodes, giving rise to the English terms *anabibazon* and *catabibazon*.[^o16][^o17]

## Lunar nodes {#Lunar_nodes}

*Image: Lunar eclipse diagram-en.svg.* Nodes of the Moon

For the [orbit of the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_of_the_Moon) around [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228), the [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_of_reference) is taken to be the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264), not the [equatorial plane](wikipedia://en/153681). The [gravitational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) pull of the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) upon the Moon causes [its nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_node) to gradually [precess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_precession) westward, completing a cycle in approximately 18.6 years.[^o2][^o19]

### Use in astrology {#Use_in_astrology}

The image of the ascending and descending orbital nodes as the head and tail of a dragon, 180 degrees apart in the sky, goes back to the Chaldeans; it was used by the Zoroastrians, and then by Arabic astronomers and astrologers. In Middle Persian, its head and tail were respectively called gōzihr sar and gōzihr dumb; in Arabic, al-ra's al-jawzihr and al-dhanab al-jawzihr — or in the case of the Moon, \_\_\_ al-tennin.[^o21] Among the arguments against astrologers made by [Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Qayyim_al-Jawziyya) (1292–1350), in his *Miftah Dar al-SaCadah:* "Why is it that you have given an influence to al-Ra's \[the head\] and al-Dhanab \[the tail\], which are two imaginary points \[ascending and descending nodes\]?"[^o22]
