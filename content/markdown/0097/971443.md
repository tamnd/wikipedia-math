# Shihab al-Din Yahya ibn Habash Suhrawardi

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Shihab al-Din Yahya ibn Habash Suhrawardi"}

Notable early

[Istanbul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul), dated 1477-8 (882 AH). [Topkapı Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topkapı_Palace) Museum

**Shihāb al-Dīn Abū al-Futūḥ Yaḥyā ibn Ḥabash ibn Amīrak al-Suhrawardī**[^o6] (شهاب‌الدین سهروردی, also known as **Sohrevardi**; 1154–1191) was a Persian philosopher and founder of the Iranian school of [Illuminationism](wikipedia://en/26117670), an important school in [Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_philosophy) and [Islamic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_philosophy).

:::

He is referred to by the honorific title *Shaikh al-ʿIshraq* "Master of Illumination" and *Shaikh al-Maqtul* "the Murdered Master", in reference to his execution for [heresy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heresy_in_Islam).[^o7] [Mulla Sadra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulla_Sadra), the Persian sage of the [Safavid era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Iran) described Suhrawardi as the "Reviver of the Traces of the Pahlavi (Iranian) Sages",[^o8] and Suhrawardi, in his magnum opus "The Philosophy of Illumination", thought of himself as a reviver or resuscitator of the ancient tradition of Persian wisdom.[^o9] Suhrawardi provided a new [Platonistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism) critique of the [peripatetic school](wikipedia://en/529396) of [Avicenna](wikipedia://en/1130) that was dominant in his time, writing on [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology), [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology), and [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics).[^o10][^o11]

## Life {#Life}

Of [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persians) stock, Suhrawardi was born in 1154 in [Suhraward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sohrevard), a village located between the towns of [Zanjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zanjan_%28city%29) and [Bijar Garrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijar_%28city%29) in [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran).[^n1] He learned wisdom and jurisprudence in the city of [Maragheh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maragheh). His teacher was Majd al-Dīn Jīlī who was also [Fakhr al-Din al-Razi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fakhr_al-Din_al-Razi)’s teacher. He then went to [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq) and [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria) for several years and developed his knowledge while he was there.

His life spanned a period of less than forty years during which he produced a series of works that established him as the founder of a new school of philosophy, called "Illuminism" (*hikmat al-Ishraq*). According to [Henry Corbin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Corbin), Suhrawardi "came later to be called the Master of Illumination (*Shaikh-i-Ishraq*) because his great aim was the renaissance of ancient [Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_peoples) wisdom".[^o13] which Corbin specifies in various ways as the "project of reviving the [philosophy of ancient Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_philosophy#Ancient_Iranian_philosophy)".[^o14]

In 1186, at the age of thirty-two, he completed his magnum opus, *The Philosophy of Illumination.*

There are several contradictory reports of his death. The most commonly held view is that he was executed sometime between 1191 and 1208 in [Aleppo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleppo) on charges of cultivating [Batini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batiniyya) teachings and philosophy, by the order of [al-Malik al-Zahir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Az-Zahir_Ghazi), son of [Saladin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saladin).[^o7] Other traditions hold that he starved himself to death, others tell that he was suffocated or thrown from the wall of the fortress, then burned by some people.[^o15]

## Influences on Suhrawardi {#Influences_on_Suhrawardi}

Suhrawardi was a strong defender of Peripatetic philosophy, until he was influenced by those whom he described as those who "have traveled the path of God", like - as noted by Suhrawardi - [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) from the Greek tradition, Hermes ([Thoth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoth)) from Egypt, and [Pythagoras](wikipedia://en/23275) the Phoenician, and also figures in the Persian tradition. His philosophical project aims to revive the lost *[hikma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hikmah)* of east and west.[^o11]

It is also apparent that Suhrawardi was strongly influenced by the [Sufi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism) tradition, bearing in mind his [mystical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystical) spiritual journeys that he considered a necessary prerequisite for understanding his Illuminationist philosophy.[^o11] [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism) also appears to have influenced Suhrawardi, as its symbols appear in some of his works.[^o11]

## Teachings {#Teachings}

Arising out of [peripatetic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peripatetic_philosophy) as developed by [Ibn Sina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Sina) (Avicenna), Suhrawardi's illuminationist philosophy is critical of several of Ibn Sina's positions and radically departs from him in creating a symbolic language (mainly derived from ancient Iranian culture or *Farhang-e Khosravani*) to give expression to his wisdom (*[hikma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hikmah)*).

Suhrawardi taught a complex and profound [emanationist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emanationist) [cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esoteric_cosmology), in which all creation is a successive outflow from the original Supreme Light of Lights (*Nur al-Anwar*). The fundamental of his philosophy is pure immaterial light, where nothing is manifest, and which unfolds from the Light of Lights in a descending order of ever-diminishing intensity and, through complex interaction, gives rise to a "horizontal" array of lights, similar in conception to [Platonic forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_forms), that governs mundane reality. In other words, the universe and all levels of existence are but varying degrees of Light—light and darkness. In his division of bodies, he categorises objects in terms of their reception or non-reception of light.

Suhrawardi considers a previous existence for every soul in the [angelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel) realm before its descent to the realm of the body. The soul is divided into two parts, one remains in heaven and the other descends into the dungeon of the body. The human soul is always sad because it has been divorced from its other half. Therefore, it aspires to become reunited with it. The soul can only reach felicity again when it is united with its celestial part, which has remained in heaven. He holds that the soul should seek felicity by detaching itself from its tenebrous body and worldly matters and access the world of immaterial lights. The souls of the [gnostics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnostic) and [saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint), after leaving the body, ascend even above the angelic world to enjoy proximity to the Supreme Light, which is the only absolute [Reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reality).

Suhrawardi elaborated the [Neoplatonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonist) idea of an independent intermediary world, the [imaginal world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malakut) (*ʿalam-i mithal* عالم مثال). His views have exerted a powerful influence down to this day, particularly through [Mulla Sadra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulla_Sadra)’s combined peripatetic and illuminationist description of reality.

## Influence {#Influence}

Suhrawardi's Illuminationist project was to have far-reaching consequences for Islamic philosophy in Shi'ite Iran. His teachings had a strong influence on subsequent esoteric Iranian thought and the idea of “Decisive Necessity” is believed to be one of the most important innovations in the history of logical philosophical speculation, stressed by the majority of [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim) logicians and philosophers. In the 17th century, it was to initiate an Illuminationist [Zoroastrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrian) revival in the figure of the 16th century sage [Azar Kayvan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azar_Kayvan).

Many later philosophers were influenced by the Illuminationist philosophy of Suhrawardi including [Athir al-Din al-Abhari](wikipedia://en/1740825), [Al-Allama al-Hilli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Allama_al-Hilli), [Ibn Abi Jumhur al-Ahsa'i](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Abi_Jumhur_al-Ahsa'i), [Jalal al-Din Davani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jalal_al-Din_Davani), and also [Mulla Sadra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulla_Sadra).[^o11]

## Suhrawardi and pre-Islamic Iranian thought {#Suhrawardi_and_pre-Islamic_Iranian_thought}

Suhrawardi thought of himself as a reviver or resuscitator of the ancient Persian wisdom.[^o9] He states in *Hikmat al-'Ishraq* that:There was among the ancient Persians a community of people guided by God who thus walked the true way, worthy Sage-Philosophers, with no resemblance to the [Magi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magi) ([Dualists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dualism_in_cosmology)). It is their precious philosophy of Light, the same as that to which the [mystical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystical) [experience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experience) of Plato and his predecessors bear witness, that we have revived in our book called *Illuminationist Philosophy (Hikmat al-'Ishraq)*, and I have had no precursor in the way of such project.

Suhrawardi uses pre-Islamic [Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran) [gnosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnosis), synthesising it with Greek and [Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic) [wisdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom). The main influence from pre-Islamic Iranian thought on Suhrawardi is in the realm of [angelology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angelology) and [cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmology). He believed that the ancient Persians' wisdom was shared by Greek philosophers such as Plato as well as by the Egyptian Hermes and considered his philosophy of illumination a rediscovery of this ancient wisdom. According to Nasr, Suhrawardi provides an important link between the thought of pre-Islamic and post-Islamic Iran and a harmonious synthesis between the two. And [Henry Corbin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Corbin) states: "In northwestern Iran, Sohravardi (d. 1191) carried out the great project of reviving the wisdom or theosophy of ancient pre-Islamic Zoroastrian Iran."[^o20]

In his work *Alwah Imadi*, Suhrawardi offers an esoteric interpretation of [Ferdowsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdowsi)'s *Epic of Kings* (*Shah Nama*)[^o21] in which figures such as [Fereydun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fereydun), Zahak, Kay Khusraw[^o21] and Jamshid are seen as manifestations of the divine light. [Seyyed Hossein Nasr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seyyed_Hossein_Nasr) states: "Alwah 'Imadi is one of the most brilliant works of Suhrawardi in which the tales of ancient Persia and the wisdom of gnosis of antiquity in the context of the esoteric meaning of the Quran have been synthesized".[^o21]

In this Persian work *Partaw Nama* and his main Arabic work *Hikmat al-Ishraq*, Suhrawardi makes extensive use of [Zoroastrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrian) symbolism[^o21] and his elaborate angelology is also based on Zoroastrian models.[^o21] The supreme light he calls both by its Quranic and [Mazdean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazdean) names, *al-nur al-a'zam* (the Supreme Light) and *Vohuman* (*Bahman*). Suhrawardi refers to the *hukamayya-fars* (Persian philosophers) as major practitioners of his *Ishraqi* wisdom and considers [Zoroaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroaster), [Jamasp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamasp), Goshtasp, [Kay Khusraw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kay_Khusraw), Frashostar and [Bozorgmehr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bozorgmehr) as possessors of this ancient wisdom.

Among pre-Islamic Iranian [symbols](wikipedia://en/37673) and [concepts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept) used by Suhrawardi are: *minu* (incorporeal world), *giti* (corporeal world), *Surush* (messenger, Gabriel), *[Farvardin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farvardin)* (the lower world), *gawhar* (pure essence), *Bahram*, *Hurakhsh* (the Sun), *shahriyar* (archetype of species), *isfahbad* (light in the body), *Amordad* (Zoroastrian angel), *Shahrivar* (Zoroastrian angel), and the *Kiyani [Khvarenah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khvarenah)*.

With regards to the pre-Islamic Iranian concept of [Khvarenah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khvarenah) (glory), Suhrawardi mentions:[^o22]

> "Whoever knows philosophy (*hikmat*) and perseveres in thanking and sanctifying the Light of the Lights, will be endowed with royal glory (*kharreh*) and with luminous splendor (*farreh*), and—as we have said elsewhere—[divine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine) light will further bestow upon him the cloak of royal power and value. Such a person shall then become the natural ruler of the universe. He shall be given aid from the high heavens, and whatever he commands shall be obeyed; and his [dreams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream) and inspirations will reach their uppermost, perfect pinnacle."
>
> و هر که حکمت بداند و بر سپاس و تقدیس نور الانوار مداومت نماید، او را خرّه کیانی بدهند و فرّ نورانی ببخشند، و بارقی الاهی او را کسوت هیبت و بهاء بپوشاند و رئیس طبیعی شود عالم را، و او را از عالم اعلا نصرت رسد و سخن او در عالم علوی مسموع باشد، و خواب و الهام او به کمال رسد.»

## Suhrawardi and Illumination school {#Suhrawardi_and_Illumination_school}

According to [Hossein Nasr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hossein_Nasr) since Sheykh Ishraq was not translated into Western languages in the medieval period, Europeans had little knowledge about Suhrawardi and his philosophy. His school is ignored even now by later scholars.[^o24] Sheykh Ishraq tried to pose a new perspective on questions like the question of Existence. He not only caused peripatetic philosophers to confront new questions but also gave new life to the body of philosophy after Avicenna.[^o25]

According to John Walbridge, Suhrawardi's critique on peripatetic philosophy can be counted as an important turning point for his successors. Suhrawardi tried to criticize Avicennism in a new approach. Although Suhrawardi first was a pioneer of peripatetic philosophy, he later became a Platonist following a mystical experience. He is also considered as the one who revived the ancient wisdom of Persia by his philosophy of Illumination. His followers include other [Kurdish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurds) and Persian philosophers such as [Shahrazuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahrazuri) and [Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qutb_al-Din_al-Shirazi) who tried to continue the way of their teacher. Suhrawardi made a distinction between two approaches in his [Illuminationism](wikipedia://en/26117670): one approach is discursive and the other is intuitive.[^o26]

## Scholarly views on Suhrawardi {#Scholarly_views_on_Suhrawardi}

There are different and contradictory views regarding the character of Suhrawardi's school. Some scholars such as Hossein Ziai believe that the most important aspects of his thought are his logic and critique of the peripatetic [conception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept) of definitions.[^o21] On the other hand, [scholars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholar) like Mehdi Hairi and Sayyid Jalal Addin Ashtiyyani, believe that Suhrawardi remained within the framework of peripatetic and neo-Avicennian philosophy. Mehdi Amin Razavi criticises both these groups for ignoring the mystical dimension of Suhrawardi's writings.[^o21] In turn, scholars such as [Henry Corbin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Corbin) and [Hossein Nasr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hossein_Nasr) view Suhrawardi as a [theosophist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theosophist) and focus on the mystical dimension of his work. Viewing in another way, [Nadia Maftouni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nadia_Maftouni) has analysed Suhrawardi's works to figure out the elements of philosophy as a way of life. As she holds, the priority of [practical reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Practical_reason) to [theoretical reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_reason), the emphasis on [intuition](wikipedia://en/154170), taking philosophy as a practice of attaining optional death, and proposing ways to heal mental diseases may well be considered the main elements of philosophy as a way of life in Suhrawardi's [allegorical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegorical) [treatises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise).[^o28]

## Writings {#Writings}

Suhrawardi left over 50 writings in Persian and Arabic.

### Persian writings {#Persian_writings}

- *Partaw Nama* ("Treatise on Illumination")
- *Hayakal al-Nur* al-Suhrawardi \[Sohravardi, Shihaboddin Yahya\] (1154–91) *Hayakil al-nur* ("The Temples of Light"), ed. M.A. Abu Rayyan, Cairo: al-Maktaba al-Tijariyyah al-Kubra, 1957. (The Persian version appears in oeuvres vol. III.)
- *Alwah-i Imadi* ("The tablets dedicated to Imad al-Din")
- *Lughat-i Muran* ("The language of Termites")
- *Risalat al-Tayr* ("The Treatise of the Bird")
- *Safir-i Simurgh* ("The Calling of the [Simurgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simurgh)")
- *Ruzi ba Jama'at Sufiyaan* ("A Day with the Community of Sufis")
- *Fi Halat al-Tufulliyah* ("On the State of Childhood")
- *Awaz-i Par-i Jebrail* ("The Chant of Gabriel's Wing")
- *Aql-i Surkh* ("The Red Intellect")
- *Fi Haqiqat al-'Ishaq* ("On the Reality of Love")
- *Bustan al-Qolub* ("The Garden of Hearts")
