# Graham number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Graham number"}

The **Graham number** or **Benjamin Graham number** is a figure used in [securities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Securities) investing that measures a [stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Share_capital)'s so-called fair value.[^o2] Named after [Benjamin Graham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Graham), the founder of [value investing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_investing), the Graham number can be calculated as follows:

:::

$$\sqrt{22.5\times(\text{earnings per share})\times(\text{book value per share})}$$

The final number is, theoretically, the maximum price that a [defensive investor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defensive_investing) should pay for the given stock. Put another way, a stock priced below the Graham number would be considered a good value, if it also meets a number of other criteria.

The number represents the [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046) of the maximum that one would pay based on earnings and based on book value. Graham writes:[^o3]

## Derivation {#Derivation}

The constant 22.5 in the formula is derived from Graham's two criteria for defensive stock selection:[^o3]

1. The [price-to-earnings ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price-to-earnings_ratio) (P/E) should not exceed 15
1. The [price-to-book ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price-to-book_ratio) (P/B) should not exceed 1.5

The product of these two maximum multiples is 15 x 1.5 = 22.5. Since:

$$\text{P/E} \times \text{P/B} = \frac{\text{Price}}{\text{EPS}} \times \frac{\text{Price}}{\text{BVPS}} = \frac{\text{Price}^2}{\text{EPS} \times \text{BVPS}}$$

Setting this product equal to 22.5 and solving for Price yields the Graham number formula:[^o5]

$$\text{Price} \leq \sqrt{22.5 \times \text{EPS} \times \text{BVPS}}$$

This derivation shows that the Graham number simultaneously enforces both the P/E and P/B constraints in a single metric.

## Alternative calculation {#Alternative_calculation}

Earnings per share is calculated by dividing *net income* by *shares outstanding*. Book value is another way of saying *[shareholders' equity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shareholders'_equity)*. Therefore, book value per share is calculated by dividing *equity* by *shares outstanding*. Consequently, the formula for the Graham number can also be written as follows:

$$\sqrt{15 \times 1.5 \times \left(\frac{\text{net income}}{\text{shares outstanding}}\right) \times \left(\frac{\mathrm{shareholders'\ equity}}{\text{shares outstanding}}\right)}$$

## Practical example {#Practical_example}

Consider a company with trailing twelve-month [earnings per share](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earnings_per_share) of \$5.00 and a [book value per share](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_value_per_share) of \$30.00. The Graham number would be:

$$\sqrt{22.5 \times 5.00 \times 30.00} = \sqrt{3375} \approx 58.09$$

Under Graham's framework, a defensive investor should consider paying no more than approximately \$58.09 per share for this stock. If the stock is trading at \$45, the stock would be trading below its Graham number, suggesting it may be undervalued by this metric. If it is trading at \$75, it would exceed the Graham number, indicating the stock may be overvalued relative to its earnings and book value.[^o5]

## History {#History}

The Graham number was first mentioned in Benjamin Graham's famous 1949 book, *[The Intelligent Investor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Intelligent_Investor)*.[^o5] Graham's defensive investment strategy mainly focused on a "[margin of safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margin_of_safety_%28financial%29)" and reducing losses as opposed to maximizing gains. The Graham number was developed based on this concept to quickly [value a stock](wikipedia://en/644426).[^o5]

Graham himself never gave a specific formula or equation. The Graham number was derived from guidelines he laid down in the book.[^o9] The formula has since become widely used by [value investors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_investing) as a quick screening tool to identify potentially undervalued stocks.[^o2]

## Limitations {#Limitations}

The Graham number has several limitations that investors should consider:[^o5][^o9]

- **No growth consideration**: The formula does not account for future [earnings growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earnings_growth), making it unsuitable for evaluating [growth stocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growth_stock), particularly in sectors such as technology where much of a company's value derives from expected future earnings.
- **Requires positive values**: The equation requires both positive [earnings per share](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earnings_per_share) and positive [book value per share](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_value_per_share). Companies with negative earnings or negative equity produce an undefined result under the square root, making the metric inapplicable.
- **Sector limitations**: The metric is most applicable to industrial and manufacturing companies. It is less useful for [financial institutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_institution), [REITs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_estate_investment_trust), and asset-light businesses where [book value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_value) may not reflect the company's true economic value.[^o2]
- **Backward-looking**: The formula uses historical financial data and does not incorporate forward-looking estimates or qualitative factors such as management quality or competitive advantage.
- **Single-point estimate**: The Graham number produces a single value and does not account for the range of possible fair values that more sophisticated methods such as [discounted cash flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discounted_cash_flow) analysis can provide.

Despite these limitations, the Graham number remains a popular initial screening tool among value investors due to its simplicity and its direct connection to Benjamin Graham's investment philosophy.[^o3]
