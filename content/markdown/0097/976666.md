# Monomial basis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Monomial basis"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) the **monomial basis** of a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) is its [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) (as a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) or [free module](wikipedia://en/326454) over the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) or [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) of [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203)) that consists of all [monomials](wikipedia://en/357416). The monomials form a basis because every [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) may be uniquely written as a finite [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of monomials (this is an immediate consequence of the definition of a polynomial).

:::

## One indeterminate {#One_indeterminate}

The [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $K[x]$ of [univariate polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Univariate_polynomial) over a field $K$ is a $K$-vector space, which has

$$1, x, x^2, x^3, \ldots$$

as an (infinite) basis.[^n1] More generally, if $K$ is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) then $K[x]$ is a [free module](wikipedia://en/326454) which has the same basis.

The polynomials of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) at most $d$ form also a vector space (or a free module in the case of a ring of coefficients), which has

$$\{ 1, x, x^2, \ldots, x^{d-1}, x^d \}$$

as a basis.

The **canonical form** of a polynomial is its expression on this basis:

$$a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \dots + a_d x^d,$$

or, using the shorter [sigma notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_notation):

$$\sum_{i=0}^d a_ix^i.$$

The monomial basis is naturally [totally ordered](wikipedia://en/30330), either by increasing degrees

$$1 < x < x^2 < \cdots,$$

or by decreasing degrees

$$1 > x > x^2 > \cdots.$$

## Several indeterminates {#Several_indeterminates}

In the case of several indeterminates $x_1, \ldots, x_n,$ a [monomial](wikipedia://en/357416) is a product

$$x_1^{d_1}x_2^{d_2}\cdots x_n^{d_n},$$

where the $d_i$ are non-negative [integers](wikipedia://en/14563).[^n2] As $x_i^0 = 1,$ an exponent equal to zero means that the corresponding indeterminate does not appear in the monomial; in particular $1 = x_1^0 x_2^0\cdots x_n^0$ is a monomial.

Similar to the case of univariate polynomials, the polynomials in $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ form a vector space (if the coefficients belong to a field) or a free module (if the coefficients belong to a ring), which has the set of all monomials as a basis, called the **monomial basis**.

The [homogeneous polynomials](wikipedia://en/1698977) of degree $d$ form a [subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) which has the monomials of degree $d = d_1+\cdots+d_n$ as a basis. The [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) of this subspace is the number of monomials of degree $d$, which is

$$\binom{d+n-1}{d} = \frac{n(n+1)\cdots (n+d-1)}{d!},$$

where $\binom{d+n-1}{d}$ is a [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668).

The polynomials of degree at most $d$ form also a subspace, which has the monomials of degree at most $d$ as a basis. The number of these monomials is the dimension of this subspace, equal to[^n3]

$$\binom{d + n}{d}= \binom{d + n}{n}=\frac{(d+1)\cdots(d+n)}{n!}.$$

In contrast to the univariate case, there is no natural [total order](wikipedia://en/30330) of the monomial basis in the multivariate case. For problems which require choosing a total order, such as [Gröbner basis](wikipedia://en/357328) computations,[^n4] one generally chooses an *admissible* [monomial order](wikipedia://en/696408) – that is, a total order on the set of monomials such that

$$m<n \iff mq < nq$$

and

$$1 \leq m$$

for every monomial $m, n, q.$

## In analysis and numerical applications {#In_analysis_and_numerical_applications}

The coefficients of a polynomial (or infinite series) in a monomial basis represent the local behavior near the origin in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), and are proportional to the values of the various derivatives of the function there (cf. [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448)). When the series is the Taylor series of some non-polynomial function, it will converge within an origin-centered [disk](wikipedia://en/329542) in the complex plane, whose [radius](wikipedia://en/61476) is as large as possible such that the function is [analytic](wikipedia://en/61478) inside the disk.

Polynomials in monomial basis are generally poor choices for numerical evaluation away from the origin, and other polynomial bases are much better suited for representing a polynomial over a specific real interval or arbitrary region in the complex plane.
