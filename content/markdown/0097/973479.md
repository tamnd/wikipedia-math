# Pseudo-differential operator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pseudo-differential operator"}

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) a **pseudo-differential operator** is an extension of the concept of [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886). Pseudo-differential operators are used extensively in the theory of [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations) and [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), e.g. in mathematical models that include ultrametric [pseudo-differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-differential_equations) in a [non-Archimedean](wikipedia://en/264158) space.

:::

## History {#History}

The study of pseudo-differential operators began in the mid 1960s with the work of [Kohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_J._Kohn), [Nirenberg](wikipedia://en/1814725), [Hörmander](wikipedia://en/500958), Unterberger and Bokobza.[^o3]

They played an influential role in the second proof of the [Atiyah–Singer index theorem](wikipedia://en/324752) via [K-theory](wikipedia://en/2912468). Atiyah and Singer thanked Hörmander for assistance with understanding the theory of pseudo-differential operators.[^o4]

## Motivation {#Motivation}

### Linear differential operators with constant coefficients {#Linear_differential_operators_with_constant_coefficients}

Consider a linear [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886) with [constant coefficients](wikipedia://en/379868),

$$P(D) := \sum_\alpha a_\alpha \, D^\alpha$$

which acts on smooth functions $u$ with compact support in **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. This operator can be written as a composition of a [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247), a simple *multiplication* by the polynomial function (called the **[symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_multiplier)**)

$$P(\xi) = \sum_\alpha a_\alpha \, \xi^\alpha,$$

and an inverse Fourier transform, in the form:

::: {#o7 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\quad P(D) u (x) = \frac{1}{(2 \pi)^n} \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} e^{i (x - y) \xi} P(\xi) u(y)\, dy \, d\xi \tag{1}$$

:::

Here, $\alpha = (\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n)$ is a [multi-index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-index), $a_\alpha$ are complex numbers, and

$$D^\alpha=(-i \partial_1)^{\alpha_1} \cdots (-i \partial_n)^{\alpha_n}$$

is an iterated [partial derivative](wikipedia://en/52565), where ∂<sub>*j*</sub> means differentiation with respect to the *j*-th variable. We introduce the constants $-i$ to facilitate the calculation of Fourier transforms.

**Derivation of formula ((1))**

The Fourier transform of a smooth function *u*, [compactly supported](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support) in **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, is

$$\hat u (\xi) := \int e^{- i y \xi} u(y) \, dy$$

and [Fourier's inversion formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier's_inversion_formula) gives

$$u (x) = \frac{1}{(2 \pi)^n} \int e^{i x \xi} \hat u (\xi) d\xi = \frac{1}{(2 \pi)^n} \iint e^{i (x - y) \xi} u (y) \, dy \, d\xi$$

By applying *P*(*D*) to this representation of *u* and using

$$P(D_x) \, e^{i (x - y) \xi} = e^{i (x - y) \xi} \, P(\xi)$$

one obtains formula ((1)).

### Representation of solutions to partial differential equations {#Representation_of_solutions_to_partial_differential_equations}

To solve the partial differential equation

$$P(D) \, u = f$$

we (formally) apply the Fourier transform on both sides and obtain the *algebraic* equation

$$P(\xi) \, \hat u (\xi) = \hat f(\xi).$$

If the symbol *P*(ξ) is never zero when ξ ∈ **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, then it is possible to divide by *P*(ξ):

$$\hat u(\xi) = \frac{1}{P(\xi)} \hat f(\xi)$$

By Fourier's inversion formula, a solution is

$$u (x) = \frac{1}{(2 \pi)^n} \int e^{i x \xi} \frac{1}{P(\xi)} \hat f (\xi) \, d\xi.$$

Here it is assumed that:

1. *P*(*D*) is a linear differential operator with *constant* coefficients,
1. its symbol *P*(ξ) is never zero,
1. both *u* and ƒ have a well defined Fourier transform.

The last assumption can be weakened by using the theory of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29). The first two assumptions can be weakened as follows.

In the last formula, write out the Fourier transform of ƒ to obtain

$$u (x) = \frac{1}{(2 \pi)^n} \iint e^{i (x-y) \xi} \frac{1}{P(\xi)} f (y) \, dy \, d\xi.$$

This is similar to formula ((1)), except that 1/*P*(ξ) is not a polynomial function, but a function of a more general kind.

## Definition of pseudo-differential operators {#Definition_of_pseudo-differential_operators}

Here we view pseudo-differential operators as a generalization of differential operators. We extend formula (1) as follows. A **pseudo-differential operator** *P*(*x*,*D*) on **R**<sup>*n*</sup> is an operator whose value on the function *u(x)* is the function of *x*:

::: {#o10 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\quad P(x,D) u (x) = \frac{1}{(2 \pi)^n} \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} e^{i x\cdot \xi} P(x,\xi) \hat{u}(\xi) \, d\xi \tag{2}$$

:::

where $\hat{u}(\xi)$ is the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of *u* and the symbol *P*(*x*,ξ) in the integrand belongs to a certain *symbol class*. For instance, if *P*(*x*,ξ) is an [infinitely differentiable](wikipedia://en/1408000) function on **R**<sup>*n*</sup> × **R**<sup>*n*</sup> with the property

$$|\partial_\xi^\alpha \partial_x^\beta P(x,\xi)| \leq C_{\alpha,\beta} \, (1 + |\xi|)^{m - |\alpha|}$$

for all *x*,ξ ∈**R**<sup>*n*</sup>, all multiindices α,β, some constants *C*<sub>α, β</sub> and some real number *m*, then *P* belongs to the symbol class $\scriptstyle{S^m_{1,0}}$ of [Hörmander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hörmander). The corresponding operator *P*(*x*,*D*) is called a **pseudo-differential operator of order m** and belongs to the class $\Psi^m_{1,0}.$

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Linear differential operators of order m with smooth bounded coefficients are pseudo-differential operators of order *m*. The composition *PQ* of two pseudo-differential operators *P*, *Q* is again a pseudo-differential operator and the symbol of *PQ* can be calculated by using the symbols of *P* and *Q*. The adjoint and transpose of a pseudo-differential operator is a pseudo-differential operator.

If a differential operator of order *m* is [(uniformly) elliptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_differential_operator) (of order *m*) and invertible, then its inverse is a pseudo-differential operator of order −*m*, and its symbol can be calculated. This means that one can solve linear elliptic differential equations more or less explicitly by using the theory of pseudo-differential operators.

Differential operators are *local* in the sense that one only needs the value of a function in a neighbourhood of a point to determine the effect of the operator. Pseudo-differential operators are *pseudo-local*, which means informally that when applied to a [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwartz_distribution) they do not create a singularity at points where the distribution was already smooth.

Just as a differential operator can be expressed in terms of *D* = −id/d*x* in the form

$$p(x, D)\,$$

for a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) *p* in *D* (which is called the *symbol*), a pseudo-differential operator has a symbol in a more general class of functions. Often one can reduce a problem in analysis of pseudo-differential operators to a sequence of algebraic problems involving their symbols, and this is the essence of [microlocal analysis](wikipedia://en/6108841).

:::

## Kernel of pseudo-differential operator {#Kernel_of_pseudo-differential_operator}

Pseudo-differential operators can be represented by [kernels](wikipedia://en/430912). The singularity of the kernel on the diagonal depends on the degree of the corresponding operator. In fact, if the symbol satisfies the above differential inequalities with m ≤ 0, it can be shown that the kernel is a [singular integral kernel](wikipedia://en/12791220).
