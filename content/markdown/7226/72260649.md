# Richard Jack (mathematician)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Richard Jack"}

**Richard Jack** (died 1759) was a Scottish mathematician, [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer), and [engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortifications_engineer) active in the mid-18th century. He provided the only testimony against [Lt. Gen. John Cope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cope_%28British_Army_officer%29) at the [court martial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_martial) following the [Battle of Prestonpans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Prestonpans) during the 1745 [Jacobite uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobite_rising_of_1745), but Having exaggerated his own accomplishments and lacking corroboration, Jack had his testimony discounted by the judges, who found Cope blameless. Jack was later involved in the development of [achromatic lenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achromatic_lens) and his work on [conic sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_sections) was a source for the mathematical sections of the [first edition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopædia_Britannica_First_Edition) of the *[Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopaedia_Britannica)*.

:::

## Life {#Life}

Richard Jack was born in [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland), in the [Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Britain), probably between 1710 and 1715.[^n1] He married Elizabeth Brown on 14 March 1737,[^n2] around the time that he was teaching mathematics in [Newcastle-upon-Tyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcastle-upon-Tyne) in 1737.[^n1] The couple later had a son, also named Richard.[^n3] Jack lectured on mathematics in [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh), probably from 1739 to 1743 when he ran advertisements in the *[Caledonian Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caledonian_Mercury)*.[^n1] He enjoyed the patronage of [Hugh Hume-Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Hume-Campbell,_3rd_Earl_of_Marchmont), the 3rd [earl of Marchmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_of_Marchmont),[^n4] whom he was helping to observe [sunspots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunspot).[^n5]

During [Charles Edward Stuart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Edward_Stuart)'s [Jacobite uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobite_rising_of_1745) in 1745, Jack remained loyal to [George II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_II_of_Great_Britain). Volunteering, he was entrusted with arranging some of the placement of the [cannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannon) at Edinburgh[^o16] as a [fortifications engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortifications_engineer)[^n4] under the guidance of [Prof. Colin MacLaurin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_MacLaurin).[^o11] He fled with most of the other [Hanoverian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanoverians) forces ahead of Charles's unopposed entrance into Edinburgh on 17 September.[^n4] He then performed [reconnaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconnaissance) on the [Stuart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuarts) forces, counting and evaluating the men in the main force encamped on [Arthur's Seat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur's_Seat) on 19 September.[^o16] He claimed that on the next day he had assisted with the planning of the artillery placement[^o13] and then personally fired two cannons, dislodging Stuart men from [the church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tranent_Parish_Church) at [Tranent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tranent). Other witnesses later averred Jack had claimed knowledge of the [theory of gunnery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistics) but had not been involved in any of the army's strategy;[^o14] he had scouted some areas and helped direct artillery fire against the men in Tranent after almost being killed by them, but he had proven so completely inept at the cannons' operation that he never fired them himself.[^o14] The following day was the 21 September [Battle of Prestonpans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Prestonpans), during which Jack said he and four sailors worked the same two cannon while Lt. Col. Alan Whiteford and five sailors worked the other four; Lt. Col. Whiteford and other witnesses, however, said the nine sailors had fled before the battle, Jack had been sent away as useless and wasn't seen in the fighting, and Whiteford had been forced to operate all six guns on his own.[^o15] A year later, on 24 September 1746,[^o16] Jack was the only eyewitness to [testify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testify_under_oath) under [oath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oath) at the [court martial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_martial) of the [Whig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whigs_%28British_political_party%29) [commander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander-in-Chief,_Scotland) [Lt. Gen. John Cope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cope_%28British_Army_officer%29).[^n1] He testified that he had seen three officers—probably but not certainly including Cope—flee the battlefield ahead of the general defeat after a [Highland charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highland_charge).[^o18] Given his inflated claims in other matters and lack of corroboration, however, the court discounted his testimony and exonerated Cope,[^n4][^o20] although the general never again held high position.[^n6]

On 25 May 1750, Jack received [patent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_Property_Office_%28United_Kingdom%29) No. 656 for a "[quadrant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrant_%28instrument%29) for taking the altitude of the sun or moon by [refraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refraction)" and also "a [refracting telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refracting_telescope) with four spherical lenses"[^n7] jointly with the successful London [instrument maker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_instrument) [George Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Adams_%28scientist,_died_1773%29).[^n5][^n8] They claimed that their design offered 30 levels of [magnification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnification)[^n9] and [eliminated color aberrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achromatic_lens). Although those claims were vigorously disputed by rival instrument makers,[^n10] Jack and Adams seem to have been vindicated by some practical and authoritative test in early 1752 and made a sizeable profit on the design.[^n11] None of the devices are known to still exist[^o33] but, on the basis of surviving records, Millburn considers it likely that representatives of the [Admiralty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_of_Admiralty) or [Board of Ordnance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_of_Ordnance) praised the patent telescope's high level of magnification—the most essential attribute for long-range fire—despite the accuracy of other complaints about its faults.[^n12] Jack advertised his lectures on math in London in 1751 and 1754.[^n1] He also lectured on [experimental philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_philosophy), [fortification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortification), and [gunnery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artillery).[^n13]

Jack died in 1759.[^n1] The advertisement for his [probate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probate) [auction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_auction) stated that he had been "assistant engineer in [the late expedition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Guadeloupe_%281759%29) against [Guadaloupe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guadeloupe)",[^n3] a [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_France) [colony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_colonial_empire) captured by British forces under [Maj. Gen. Peregrine Hopson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peregrine_Hopson) after a six-month siege during the [Seven Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Years'_War). His effects included an [air pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_pump), [microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscope), [telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope), and other scientific instruments.[^o33]

## Works {#Works}

Jack wrote three major works:[^n1]

- Jack, Richard. (1742). "Elements of Conic Sections in Three Books, in Which Are Demonstrated the Principal Properties of the Parabola, Ellipse, and Hyperbola". Walter and Thomas Ruddiman. <https://books.google.com/books?id=sbk2AAAAMAAJ>.
- Jack, Richard. (1747). "The Mathematical Principles of Theology, or, The Existence of God Geometrically Demonstrated in Three Books...". George Hawkins. <https://books.google.com/books?id=EOo2AAAAMAAJ&pg=PR1>.
- Jack, Richard. (1756). "Euclid's Data, Restored to Their True and Genuine Order, Agreeable to Pappus Alexandrinus's Account of Them, in His Preface to the Seventh Book of His Mathematical Collections". Andrew Millar. <https://books.google.com/books?id=3-2yAOlfSvYC>.

Jack also composed most of a fourth book, *The Doctrine of Proportion Geometrically Demonstrated*, prior to 1745 but lost its manuscript when Stuart forces ransacked his home during their occupation of Edinburgh.[^n14]

Jack's work on [conic sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_sections) was a major reference for the 1771 [first edition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopædia_Britannica_First_Edition) of the *[Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopaedia_Britannica)*.[^n1] His work on a [geometrical proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometrical_proof) of the [existence of God](wikipedia://en/621169), however, was generally held in low repute and considered by [MacFarlane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_MacFarlane) to be "one of the most absurd" attempts to apply [mathematical reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_reasoning) to [theological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology) [questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodicy).[^n15] Gillespie described it as "a specimen of impure Mathematics, gone deplorably out of their road".[^n16] Writing under the pen name [Antitheos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antitheos), George Simpson offered that Jack's arguments "may afford grounds for curious speculation respecting that bias toward [absurdity](wikipedia://en/1745810) which is too frequently found to beset the [human mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_mind)".[^n17]
