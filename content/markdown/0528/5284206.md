# Bertrand's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bertrand's theorem"}

*Image: Bertrand.jpg.* Joseph Bertrand

In [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), **Bertrand's theorem** states that among [central-force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central-force) [potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential) with bound orbits, there are only two types of [central-force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central-force) (radial) [scalar potentials](wikipedia://en/461654) with the property that all bound orbits are also [closed orbits](wikipedia://en/252250).[^o2][^o3] The theorem is named after its discoverer, [Joseph Bertrand](wikipedia://en/488196) who published it in 1873.[^o4][^o5]

:::

The first such potential is an [inverse-square central force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse-square_law) such as the [gravitational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) or [electrostatic potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics):

$$V(r) = -\frac{k}{r}$$

with force

$$\mathbf F(r) = -\nabla V = -\frac{k}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf r}.$$

The second is the [radial harmonic oscillator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_harmonic_oscillator) potential:

$$V(r) = \frac{1}{2} k r^2$$

with force

$$\mathbf F(\mathbf r) = -\nabla V = -k r \,\hat{\mathbf r}.$$

## Derivation {#Derivation}

*Image: Closed orbits.gif.* Small changes in the power of the force scaling with distance will lead to significantly different kind of orbits.

All attractive [central forces](wikipedia://en/1888817) can produce [circular](wikipedia://en/6220) orbits, which are naturally [closed orbits](wikipedia://en/252250). The only requirement is that the central force exactly equals the [centripetal force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centripetal_force), which determines the required [angular velocity](wikipedia://en/65927) for a given circular radius. Non-central forces (i.e., those that depend on the angular variables as well as the radius) are ignored here, since they do not produce circular orbits in general.

The equation of motion for the radius $r$ of a particle of mass $m$ moving in a [central potential](wikipedia://en/1888817) $V(r)$ is given by [motion equations](wikipedia://en/294995)

$$m\frac{d^2 r}{dt^2} - mr \omega^2 = m\frac{d^2 r}{dt^2} - \frac{L^2}{mr^3} = -\frac{dV}{dr},$$

where $\omega \equiv \frac{d\theta}{dt}$, and the [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839) $L = mr^2\omega$ is conserved. For illustration, the first term on the left is zero for circular orbits, and the applied inwards force $\frac{dV}{dr}$ equals the [centripetal force requirement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centripetal_force) $mr^2\omega$, as expected.

The definition of [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839) allows a change of independent variable from $t$ to $\theta$:

$$\frac{d}{dt} = \frac{L}{mr^2} \frac{d}{d\theta},$$

giving the new equation of motion that is independent of time:

$$\frac{L}{r^2} \frac{d}{d\theta} \left( \frac{L}{mr^2} \frac{dr}{d\theta} \right) - \frac{L^2}{mr^3} = -\frac{dV}{dr}.$$

This equation becomes quasilinear on making the change of variables $u \equiv \frac{1}{r}$ and multiplying both sides by $\frac{mr^2}{L^2}$ (see also [Binet equation](wikipedia://en/26290265)):

$$\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + u = -\frac{m}{L^2} \frac{d}{du} V{\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)}.$$

As noted above, all [central forces](wikipedia://en/1888817) can produce [circular orbits](wikipedia://en/252250) given an appropriate initial velocity. However, if some [radial velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radial_velocity) is introduced, these orbits need not be stable (i.e., remain in orbit indefinitely) nor closed (repeatedly returning to exactly the same path). Here we show that a [necessary condition](wikipedia://en/169319) for stable, exactly closed non-circular orbits is an inverse-square force or radial harmonic oscillator potential. In the following sections, we show that those two force laws produce [stable, exactly closed orbits](wikipedia://en/252250).

Define $J(u)$ as

$$\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + u = J(u) \equiv -\frac{m}{L^2} \frac{d}{du} V{\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)} = -\frac{m}{L^2 u^2} f{\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)},$$

where $f$ represents the radial force. The criterion for perfectly [circular](wikipedia://en/6220) motion at a radius $r_0$ is that the first term on the left be zero:

::: {#o7 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$u_0 = J(u_0) = -\frac{m}{L^2 u_0^2} f{\left(\frac{1}{u_0}\right)}, \tag{1}$$

:::

where $u_0 \equiv 1/r_0$.

The next step is to consider the equation for $u$ under *[small perturbations](wikipedia://en/102140)* $\eta \equiv u - u_0$ from perfectly circular orbits. On the right, the $J$ function can be expanded in a standard [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448):

$$J(u) \approx J(u_0) + \eta J'(u_0) + \frac{1}{2} \eta^2 J''(u_0) + \frac{1}{6} \eta^3 J'''(u_0) + \cdots$$

Substituting this expansion into the equation for $u$ and subtracting the constant terms yields

$$\frac{d^2 \eta}{d\theta^2} + \eta = \eta J'(u_0) + \frac{1}{2} \eta^2 J''(u_0) + \frac{1}{6} \eta^3 J'''(u_0) + \cdots,$$

which can be written as

::: {#o8 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\frac{d^2 \eta}{d\theta^2} + \beta^2 \eta = \frac{1}{2} \eta^2 J''(u_0) + \frac{1}{6} \eta^3 J'''(u_0) + \cdots, \tag{2}$$

:::

where $\beta^2 \equiv 1 - J'(u_0)$ is a constant. $\beta^2$ must be non-negative; otherwise, the radius of the orbit would vary exponentially away from its initial radius. (The solution $\beta=0$ corresponds to a perfectly circular orbit.) If the right side may be neglected (i.e., for small perturbations), the solutions are

$$\eta(\theta) = h_1 \cos(\beta\theta),$$

where the amplitude $h_1$ is a [constant of integration](wikipedia://en/147939). For the orbits to be closed, $\beta$ must be a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024). What's more, it must be the *same* rational number for all radii, since $\beta$ cannot change continuously; the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) are [totally disconnected](wikipedia://en/303356) from one another. Using the definition of $J$ along with (equation 1),

$$\begin{aligned} J'(u_0) &= \frac{2}{u_0} \left[\frac{m}{L^2 u_0^2} f{\left(\frac{1}{u_0}\right)}\right] - \left[\frac{m}{L^2 u_0^2} f{\left(\frac{1}{u_0}\right)}\right] \frac{1}{f{\left(\frac{1}{u_0}\right)}} \frac{d}{du_0} f{\left(\frac1{u_0}\right)} \\[1ex] &= -2 + \frac{u_0}{f{\left(\frac{1}{u_0}\right)}} \frac{d}{du_0}f{\left(\frac{1}{u_0}\right)} = 1 - \beta^2. \end{aligned}$$

Since this must hold for any value of $u_0$,

$$\frac{df}{dr} = \left(\beta^2 - 3\right) \frac{f}{r},$$

which implies that the force must follow a [power law](wikipedia://en/24522)

$$f(r) = -\frac{k}{r^{3-\beta^2}}.$$

Hence, $J$ must have the general form

::: {#o9 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$J(u) = \frac{mk}{L^2} u^{1-\beta^2}. \tag{3}$$

:::

For more general deviations from circularity (i.e., when we cannot neglect the higher-order terms in the Taylor expansion of $J$ ), $\eta$ may be expanded in a [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038), e.g.,

$$\eta(\theta) = h_0 + h_1 \cos \beta \theta + h_2 \cos 2\beta \theta + h_3 \cos 3\beta \theta + \cdots$$

We substitute this into (equation 2) and equate the coefficients belonging to the same frequency, keeping only the lowest-order terms. As we show below, $h_0$ and $h_2$ are smaller than $h_1$, being of order $h_1^2$. $h_3$, and all further coefficients, are at least of order $h_1^3$. This makes sense, since $h_0, h_2, h_3, \ldots$ must all vanish faster than $h_1$ as a circular orbit is approached.

$$\begin{aligned} h_0 &= h_1^2 \frac{J''(u_0)}{4\beta^2}, \\ h_2 &= -h_1^2 \frac{J''(u_0)}{12\beta^2}, \\ h_3 &= -\frac{1}{8\beta^2} \left[ h_1 h_2 \frac{J''(u_0)}{2} + h_1^3 \frac{J'''(u_0)}{24} \right]. \end{aligned}$$

From the $\cos\beta\theta$ term, we get

$$\begin{aligned} 0 &= \left(2 h_1 h_0 + h_1 h_2\right) \frac{J''(u_0)}{2} + h_1^3 \frac{J'''(u_0)}{8} \\ &= \frac{h_1^3}{24 \beta^2} \left(3 \beta^2 J'''(u_0) + 5 J''(u_0)^2\right), \end{aligned}$$

where in the last step we substituted in the values of $h_0$ and $h_2$.

Using equations ((3)) and ((1)), we can calculate the second and third derivatives of $J$ evaluated at $u_0$:

$$\begin{aligned} J''(u_0) &= -\frac{\beta^2 \left(1 - \beta^2\right)}{u_0}, \\ J'''(u_0) &= \frac{\beta^2 \left(1 - \beta^2\right) \left(1 + \beta^2\right)}{u_0^2}. \end{aligned}$$

Substituting these values into the last equation yields the main result of **Bertrand's theorem**:

$$\beta^2 \left(1 - \beta^2\right) \left(4 - \beta^2\right) = 0.$$

Hence, the only [potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential) that can produce stable closed non-circular orbits are the inverse-square force law ($\beta=1$) and the radial harmonic-oscillator potential ($\beta=2$). The solution $\beta=0$ corresponds to perfectly circular orbits, as noted above.

## Classical field potentials {#Classical_field_potentials}

For an inverse-square force law such as the [gravitational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) or [electrostatic potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics), the [potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential) can be written

$$V(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{-k}{r} = -ku.$$

The orbit *u*(*θ*) can be derived from the general equation

$$\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + u = -\frac{m}{L^2} \frac{d}{du} V{\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)} = \frac{km}{L^2},$$

whose solution is the constant $\frac{km}{L^2}$ plus a simple sinusoid:

$$u \equiv \frac{1}{r} = \frac{km}{L^2} [1 + e \cos(\theta - \theta_0)],$$

where *e* (the *eccentricity*), and θ<sub>0</sub> (the *phase offset*) are constants of integration.

This is the general formula for a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673) that has one focus at the origin; *e* = 0 corresponds to a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), 0 \< *e* \< 1 corresponds to an ellipse, *e* = 1 corresponds to a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231), and *e* > 1 corresponds to a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052). The eccentricity *e* is related to the total [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) *E* (see [Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector](wikipedia://en/719460)):

$$e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2EL^2}{k^2 m}}.$$

Comparing these formulae shows that *E* \< 0 corresponds to an ellipse, *E* = 0 corresponds to a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231), and *E* > 0 corresponds to a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052). In particular, $E = -\frac{k^2 m}{2L^2}$ for perfectly [circular](wikipedia://en/6220) orbits.

## Harmonic oscillator {#Harmonic_oscillator}

To solve for the orbit under a **radial harmonic-oscillator** potential, it's easier to work in [components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29) **r** = (*x*, *y*, *z*). The potential can be written as

$$V(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{2} kr^2 = \frac{1}{2} k (x^2 + y^2 + z^2).$$

The equation of motion for a particle of mass *m* is given by three independent [Euler equations](wikipedia://en/294995):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} + \omega_0^2 x &= 0, \\ \frac{d^2 y}{dt^2} + \omega_0^2 y &= 0, \\ \frac{d^2 z}{dt^2} + \omega_0^2 z &= 0, \end{aligned}$$

where the constant $\omega_0^2 \equiv \frac{k}{m}$ must be positive (i.e., *k* > 0) to ensure bounded, closed orbits; otherwise, the particle will fly off to [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824). The solutions of these [simple harmonic oscillator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_harmonic_oscillator) equations are all similar:

$$\begin{aligned} x &= A_x \cos(\omega_0 t + \phi_x), \\ y &= A_y \cos(\omega_0 t + \phi_y), \\ z &= A_z \cos(\omega_0 t + \phi_z), \end{aligned}$$

where the positive constants *A<sub>x</sub>*, *A<sub>y</sub>* and *A<sub>z</sub>* represent the *amplitudes* of the oscillations, and the angles φ<sub>*x*</sub>, φ<sub>*y*</sub> and φ<sub>*z*</sub> represent their *phases*. The resulting orbit **r**(*t*) = \[*x*(*t*), *y*(*y*), *z*(*t*)\] is closed because it repeats exactly after one period

$$T \equiv \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}.$$

The system is also stable because small perturbations in the amplitudes and phases cause correspondingly small changes in the overall orbit.
