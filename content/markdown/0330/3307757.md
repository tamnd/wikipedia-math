# Simson line

*Image: Pedal Line.svg.* The Simson line *LN* (red) of the triangle $ABC$ with respect to point $P$ on the circumcircle

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Simson line"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), given a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) $ABC$ and a [point](wikipedia://en/593693) $P$ on its [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432), the three closest points to $P$ on lines $AB$, $AC$, and $BC$ are [collinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear).[^o2] The line through these points is the **Simson line** of $P$, named for [Robert Simson](wikipedia://en/152984).[^o3] The concept was first published, however, by [William Wallace](wikipedia://en/16955407) in 1799,[^o4] and is sometimes called the **Wallace line**.[^o5][^o6]

:::

The [converse](wikipedia://en/30977#Converse) is also true; if the three closest points to $P$ on three lines are collinear, and no two of the lines are parallel, then $P$ lies on the circumcircle of the triangle formed by the three lines. Or in other words, the Simson line of a triangle $ABC$ and a point $P$ is just the [pedal triangle](wikipedia://en/152983) of $ABC$ and $P$ that has degenerated into a straight line and this condition constrains the [locus](wikipedia://en/542465) of $P$ to trace the circumcircle of triangle $ABC$.

## Equation {#Equation}

Placing the triangle in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), let the triangle $ABC$ with unit [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432) have vertices whose locations have complex coordinates $a$, $b$, $c$, and let P with complex coordinates $p$ be a point on the circumcircle. The Simson line is the set of points $z$ satisfying[^o8]

$$2abc\bar{z} -2pz+p^2+(a+b+c)p -(bc+ca+ab)-\frac{abc}{p} =0,$$

where an overbar indicates [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation).

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

*Image: Simson-deltoid-anim.gif.* Simson lines (in red) are tangents to the Steiner [deltoid](wikipedia://en/769176) (in blue).

- The Simson line of a vertex of the triangle is the [altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude_%28geometry%29) of the triangle dropped from that vertex, and the Simson line of the point [diametrically opposite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diametrically_opposite) to the vertex is the side of the triangle opposite to that vertex.
- If $P$ and $Q$ are points on the circumcircle, then the angle between the Simson lines of $P$ and $Q$ is half the angle of the arc $PQ$. In particular, if the points are diametrically opposite, their Simson lines are perpendicular and in this case the intersection of the lines lies on the [nine-point circle](wikipedia://en/161243).
- Letting $H$ denote the [orthocenter](wikipedia://en/161244) of the triangle $ABC$, the Simson line of $P$ [bisects](wikipedia://en/152547) the segment $PH$ in a point that lies on the nine-point circle.
- Given two triangles with the same circumcircle, the angle between the Simson lines of a point $P$ on the circumcircle for both triangles does not depend of $P$.
- The set of all Simson lines, when drawn, form an [envelope](wikipedia://en/880235) in the shape of a [deltoid](wikipedia://en/769176) known as the Steiner deltoid of the reference triangle. The Steiner deltoid circumscribes the nine-point circle and its center is the [nine-point center](wikipedia://en/4517570).
- The construction of the Simson line that coincides with a side of the reference triangle (see first property above) yields a nontrivial point on this side line. This point is the reflection of the foot of the altitude (dropped onto the side line) about the midpoint of the side line being constructed. Furthermore, this point is a tangent point between the side of the reference triangle and its Steiner deltoid.
- A [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278) that is not a [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496) has one and only one pedal point, called the Simson point, with respect to which the feet on the quadrilateral are collinear.[^o11] The Simson point of a [trapezoid](wikipedia://en/201826) is the point of intersection of the two nonparallel sides.[^o12]
- No [convex polygon](wikipedia://en/660019) with at least 5 sides has a Simson line.[^o13]

:::

## Proof of existence {#Proof_of_existence}

It suffices to show that $\angle NMP + \angle PML = 180^\circ$.

$PCAB$ is a cyclic quadrilateral, so $\angle PBA + \angle ACP = \angle PBN + \angle ACP = 180^\circ$. $PMNB$ is a cyclic quadrilateral (since $\angle PMB=\angle PNB = 90^\circ$), so $\angle PBN + \angle NMP = 180^\circ$. Hence $\angle NMP = \angle ACP$. Now $PLCM$ is cyclic, so $\angle PML = \angle PCL = 180^\circ - \angle ACP$.

Therefore $\angle NMP + \angle PML = \angle ACP + (180^\circ - \angle ACP) = 180^\circ$.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### Generalization 1 {#Generalization_1}

*Image: A generalization of the Simson line.svg.* The projections of Ap, Bp, Cp onto BC, CA, AB are three collinear points

- Let *ABC* be a triangle, let a line ℓ go through circumcenter *O*, and let a point *P* lie on the circumcircle. Let *AP, BP, CP* meet ℓ at *A<sub>p</sub>, B<sub>p</sub>, C<sub>p</sub>* respectively. Let *A*<sub>0</sub>, *B*<sub>0</sub>, *C*<sub>0</sub> be the projections of *A<sub>p</sub>, B<sub>p</sub>, C<sub>p</sub>* onto *BC, CA, AB*, respectively. Then *A*<sub>0</sub>, *B*<sub>0</sub>, *C*<sub>0</sub> are collinear. Moreover, the new line passes through the midpoint of *PH*, where *H* is the orthocenter of Δ*ABC*. If ℓ passes through *P*, the line coincides with the Simson line.[^o19][^o20][^o21]

*Image: A propjective Simson line.svg.* A projective version of a Simson line

### Generalization 2 {#Generalization_2}

- Let the vertices of the triangle *ABC* lie on the [conic](wikipedia://en/19008673) Γ, and let *Q, P* be two points in the plane. Let *PA, PB, PC* intersect the conic at *A*<sub>1</sub>, *B*<sub>1</sub>, *C*<sub>1</sub> respectively. *QA*<sub>1</sub> intersects *BC* at *A*<sub>2</sub>, *QB*<sub>1</sub> intersects *AC* at *B*<sub>2</sub>, and *QC*<sub>1</sub> intersects *AB* at *C*<sub>2</sub>. Then the four points *A*<sub>2</sub>, *B*<sub>2</sub>, *C*<sub>2</sub>, and *P* are collinear if only if *Q* lies on the conic Γ.[^o23]

### Generalization 3 {#Generalization_3}

- R. F. Cyster generalized the theorem to [cyclic quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/194131) in [The Simson Lines of a Cyclic Quadrilateral](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3606490)
