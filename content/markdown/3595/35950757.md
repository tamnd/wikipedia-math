# Apophantic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Apophantic"}

In [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), **Apophantic** (ἀποφαντικός, "declaratory", from ἀποφαίνειν *apophainein*, "to show, to make known") statements are declaratory statements whose truth-value can be determined by examining whether its predicate can be logically attributed to its [subject](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subject_%28grammar%29).

:::

For example, consider the two sentences "All [penguins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penguins) are [birds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birds)" and "All [bachelors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelors) are unhappy". In the first sentence, the set of all birds is a [category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_Categories) which penguins can or cannot [necessarily](wikipedia://en/169319) be placed into. In the second sentence, "unhappy" is not a category that all bachelors must necessarily be placed into; it is [contingent](wikipedia://en/17753389) on the happiness of the individual bachelors. However, because no penguins need to be consulted or examined to determine that all penguins are birds, the conclusion that the first statement must be true is apophantic.

The term "apophantic" first appeared in the works of [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308). The concept appears in the Arabic Aristotelian tradition as *jâzim*.[^o2] In [phenomenology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenology_%28philosophy%29),[^o3] [Edmund Husserl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Husserl) considered apophantic judgment central to his 'transcendental logic',[^o4] but his student [Martin Heidegger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Heidegger) argued later that apophantic judgements are the *least* reliable means of obtaining truth because they are cut from the original interpretive framework of relations to the subject.[^o5]
