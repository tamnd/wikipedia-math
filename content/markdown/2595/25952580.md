# Conformal radius

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Conformal radius"}

In mathematics, the **conformal radius** is a way to measure the size of a [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) planar domain *D* viewed from a point *z* in it. As opposed to notions using [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) (say, the radius of the largest inscribed disk with center *z*), this notion is well-suited to use in [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), in particular in [conformal maps](wikipedia://en/50627) and [conformal geometry](wikipedia://en/295907).

:::

A closely related notion is the **transfinite diameter** or **(logarithmic) [capacity](wikipedia://en/14368398)** of a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) simply connected set *D*, which can be considered as the inverse of the conformal radius of the [complement](wikipedia://en/54347) *E* = *D<sup>c</sup>* viewed from [infinity](wikipedia://en/30876799).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given a simply connected domain *D* ⊂ **C**, and a point *z* ∈ *D*, it then follows from the [Riemann mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/26215) that there exists a unique conformal homeomorphism *f* : *D* → **D** onto the open [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738) (usually referred to as the **uniformizing map**) with *f*(*z*) = 0 ∈ **D** and *f*′(*z*) ∈ **R**<sub>\+</sub>. The conformal radius of *D* from *z* is then defined as

$$\operatorname{rad}(z,D) := \frac{1}{f'(z)}\,.$$

The simplest example is that the conformal radius of the disk of radius *r* viewed from its center is also *r*, shown by the uniformizing map *x* ↦ *x*/*r*. See below for more examples.

One reason for the usefulness of this notion is that it behaves well under conformal maps: if φ: *D* → *D*′ is a conformal [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) and *z* in *D*, then $\operatorname{rad}(\varphi(z),D') = |\varphi'(z)|\operatorname{rad}(z,D)$.

The conformal radius can also be expressed as $\exp(\xi_x(x))$ where $\xi_x(y)$ is the harmonic extension of $\log(|x-y|)$ from $\partial D$ to $D$.

:::

## A special case: the upper-half plane {#A_special_case:_the_upper-half_plane}

Let *K* ⊂ **H** be a subset of the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826) such that *D*:= **H**\\*K* is connected and simply connected, and let *z* ∈ *D* be a point. (This is a usual scenario, say, in the [Schramm–Loewner evolution](wikipedia://en/6706108)). By the Riemann mapping theorem, there is a conformal bijection *g*: *D* → **H**. Then, for any such map *g*, a simple computation gives that

$$\operatorname{rad}(z,D) = \frac{2\operatorname{Im}(g(z))}{|g'(z)|}\,.$$

For example, when *K* = ∅ and *z* = *i*, then *g* can be the identity map, and we get rad(*i*, **H**) = 2. Checking that this agrees with the original definition: the uniformizing map *f* : **H** → **D** is

$$f(z)=i\frac{z-i}{z+i},$$

and then the derivative can be easily calculated.

## Relation to inradius {#Relation_to_inradius}

That it is a good measure of radius is shown by the following immediate consequence of the [Schwarz lemma](wikipedia://en/1432589) and the [Koebe 1/4 theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koebe_1/4_theorem): for *z* ∈ *D* ⊂ **C**,

$$\frac{\operatorname{rad}(z,D)}{4} \leq \operatorname{dist} (z,\partial D) \leq \operatorname{rad}(z,D),$$

where dist(*z*, ∂*D*) denotes the Euclidean distance between *z* and the [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) of *D*, or in other words, the radius of the largest inscribed disk with center *z*.

Both inequalities are best possible:

The upper bound is clearly attained by taking *D* = **D** and *z* = 0.

The lower bound is attained by the following “slit domain”: *D* = **C**\\**R**<sub>\+</sub> and *z* = −*r* ∈ **R**<sub>−</sub>. The [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) map φ takes *D* onto the upper half-plane **H**, with $\varphi(-r) = i\sqrt{r}$ and derivative $|\varphi'(-r)|=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}$. The above formula for the upper half-plane gives $\operatorname{rad}(i\sqrt{r},\mathbb{H})=2\sqrt{r}$, and then the formula for transformation under conformal maps gives rad(−*r*, *D*) = 4*r*, while, of course, dist(−*r*, ∂*D*) = *r*.

## Version from infinity: transfinite diameter and logarithmic capacity {#Version_from_infinity:_transfinite_diameter_and_logarithmic_capacity}

When *D* ⊂ **C** is a connected, simply connected compact set, then its complement *E* = *D<sup>c</sup>* is a connected, simply connected domain in the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799) that contains ∞, and one can define

$$\operatorname{rad}(\infty,D) := \frac{1}{\operatorname{rad}(\infty,E)} := \lim_{z\to\infty} \frac{f(z)}{z},$$

where *f* : **C**\\**D** → *E* is the unique bijective conformal map with f(∞) = ∞ and that limit being positive real, i.e., the conformal map of the form

$$f(z)=c_1z+c_0 + c_{-1}z^{-1} + \cdots, \qquad c_1\in\mathbf{R}_+.$$

The coefficient *c*<sub>1</sub> = rad(∞, *D*) equals the **transfinite diameter** and the **(logarithmic) capacity** of *D*; see Chapter 11 of (Pommerenke 1975)[^o9] and (Kuz′mina 2002).

The coefficient *c*<sub>0</sub> is called the **conformal center** of *D*. It can be shown to lie in the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of *D*; moreover,

$$D\subseteq \{z: |z-c_0|\leq 2 c_1\}\,,$$

where the radius 2*c*<sub>1</sub> is sharp for the straight [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) of length 4*c*<sub>1</sub>. See pages 12–13 and Chapter 11 of (Pommerenke 1975).[^o9]

:::

## The Fekete, Chebyshev and modified Chebyshev constants {#The_Fekete,_Chebyshev_and_modified_Chebyshev_constants}

We define three other quantities that are equal to the transfinite diameter even though they are defined from a very different point of view. Let

$$d(z_1,\ldots,z_k):=\prod_{1\le i<j\le k} |z_i-z_j|$$

denote the product of pairwise distances of the points $z_1,\ldots,z_k$ and let us define the following quantity for a compact set *D* ⊂ **C**:

$$d_n(D):=\sup_{z_1,\ldots,z_n\in D} d(z_1,\ldots,z_n)^{1\left/\binom n 2\right.}$$

In other words, $d_n(D)$ is the supremum of the geometric mean of pairwise distances of *n* points in *D*. Since *D* is compact, this supremum is actually attained by a set of points. Any such *n*-point set is called a **Fekete set**.

The limit $d(D):=\lim_{n\to\infty} d_n(D)$ exists and it is called the **Fekete constant**.

Now let $\mathcal P_n$ denote the set of all monic polynomials of degree *n* in **C**\[*x*\], let $\mathcal Q_n$ denote the set of polynomials in $\mathcal P_n$ with all zeros in *D* and let us define

$\mu_n(D):=\inf_{p\in\mathcal P_n} \sup_{z\in D} |p(z)|$ and $\tilde{\mu}_n(D):=\inf_{p\in\mathcal Q_n} \sup_{z\in D} |p(z)|$

Then the limits

$\mu(D):=\lim_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(D)^{1/n}$ and $\mu(D):=\lim_{n\to\infty} \tilde{\mu}_n(D)^{1/n}$

exist and they are called the **Chebyshev constant** and **modified Chebyshev constant**, respectively. [Michael Fekete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Fekete) and [Gábor Szegő](wikipedia://en/46065) proved that these constants are equal.

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

The conformal radius is a very useful tool, e.g., when working with the [Schramm–Loewner evolution](wikipedia://en/6706108). A beautiful instance can be found in (Lawler Schramm Werner 2002).[^o18]
