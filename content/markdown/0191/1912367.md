# Electromagnetic tensor

Electromagnetic field strength

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Electromagnetic tensor"}

In [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism), the **electromagnetic tensor** or **electromagnetic field tensor** (sometimes called the **field strength tensor**, **Faraday tensor** or **Maxwell bivector**) is a [tensor](wikipedia://en/29965) that describes the [electromagnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field) in [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758). The EM tensor [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28physics%29) was developed by [Arnold Sommerfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Sommerfeld) after the [four-dimensional tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-dimensional_tensor) formulation of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) was introduced by [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537).[^o2]p. 22 The EM tensor allows related physical laws to be written concisely, and allows for the [quantization of the electromagnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantization_of_the_electromagnetic_field) by a [Lagrangian formulation](wikipedia://en/1912367#Quantum_electrodynamics_and_field_theory).

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The electromagnetic tensor, conventionally labelled *F*, is defined as the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782#Exterior_derivative_of_a_k-form) of the [electromagnetic four-potential](wikipedia://en/731884), *A*, a differential 1-form:[^o5][^o6]

$$F \ \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\ \mathrm{d}A.$$

Therefore, *F* is a [differential 2-form](wikipedia://en/221519)— an antisymmetric rank-2 tensor field—on Minkowski space. In component form,

$$F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu.$$

where $\partial$ is the [four-gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-gradient) and $A$ is the [four-potential](wikipedia://en/731884).

[SI units for Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737#Conventional_formulation_in_SI_units) and the [particle physicist's sign convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_convention#Metric_signature) for the [signature](wikipedia://en/463225) of [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space) (+ − − −), will be used throughout this article.

### Relationship with the classical fields {#Relationship_with_the_classical_fields}

The Faraday [differential 2-form](wikipedia://en/221519) is given by

$$\begin{aligned} F ={}&(E_x/c)\ dx \wedge dt + (E_y/c)\ dy \wedge dt + (E_z/c)\ dz \wedge dt \\ &+ B_x\ dy \wedge dz + B_y\ dz \wedge dx + B_z\ dx \wedge dy, \end{aligned}$$

where $dt$ is the time element times the speed of light $c$.

This is the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) of its 1-form antiderivative, the covariant form of the four-potential, is[^o8]p. 315

$$A = (\phi/c)\, dt - A_x\, dx - A_y\, dy - A_z\, dz,$$

where $\phi(\mathbf{x},t)$ has $-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi = \mathbf{E}$ ($\phi$ is a scalar potential for the [irrotational/conservative vector field](wikipedia://en/461454) $\mathbf{E}$) and $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x},t)$ has $\boldsymbol{\nabla} \times \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{B}$ ($\mathbf{A}$ is a vector potential for the [solenoidal vector field](wikipedia://en/419444) $\mathbf{B}$).

The [electric](wikipedia://en/41092) and [magnetic fields](wikipedia://en/36563) can be obtained from the components of the electromagnetic tensor. The relationship is simplest in [Cartesian coordinates](wikipedia://en/7706):

$$E_i = c F_{0i},$$

where *c* is the speed of light, and

$$B_i = -\tfrac{1}{2} \varepsilon_{ijk} F^{jk},$$

where $\varepsilon_{ijk}$ is the [Levi-Civita tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi-Civita_tensor). This gives the fields in a particular reference frame; if the reference frame is changed, the components of the electromagnetic tensor will [transform covariantly](wikipedia://en/443235), and the fields in the new frame will be given by the new components.

In contravariant [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) form with metric signature (+,−,−,−),[^o8]p. 313

$$F^{\mu\nu} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -E_x/c & -E_y/c & -E_z/c \\ E_x/c & 0 & -B_z & B_y \\ E_y/c & B_z & 0 & -B_x \\ E_z/c & -B_y & B_x & 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$

The covariant form is given by [index lowering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raising_and_lowering_indices#Order-2),

$$\begin{aligned} F_{\mu\nu} &= \eta_{\alpha\nu} F^{\beta\alpha} \eta_{\mu\beta} \\[1ex] &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & E_x/c & E_y/c & E_z/c \\ -E_x/c & 0 & -B_z & B_y \\ -E_y/c & B_z & 0 & -B_x \\ -E_z/c & -B_y & B_x & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned}$$

The Faraday tensor's [Hodge dual](wikipedia://en/310914) is

$$\begin{aligned} G^{\alpha\beta} &= \tfrac{1}{2} \varepsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}F_{\gamma\delta} \\[1ex] &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -B_x & -B_y & -B_z \\ B_x & 0 & E_z/c & -E_y/c \\ B_y & -E_z/c & 0 & E_x/c \\ B_z & E_y/c & -E_x/c & 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

From now on in this article, when the electric or magnetic fields are mentioned, a Cartesian coordinate system is assumed, and the electric and magnetic fields are with respect to the coordinate system's reference frame, as in the equations above.

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

The matrix form of the field tensor yields the following properties:[^o5]

1. **[Antisymmetry](wikipedia://en/174055):** $$F^{\mu\nu} = - F^{\nu\mu}$$
1. **Six independent components:** In Cartesian coordinates, these are simply the three spatial components of the electric field (*E<sub>x</sub>, E<sub>y</sub>, E<sub>z</sub>*) and magnetic field (*B<sub>x</sub>, B<sub>y</sub>, B<sub>z</sub>*).
1. **Inner product:** If one forms an inner product of the field strength tensor a [Lorentz invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_invariant) is formed $$F_{\mu\nu} F^{\mu\nu} = 2 \left( B^2-\frac{E^2}{c^2} \right)$$ meaning this number does not change from one [frame of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference) to another.
1. **[Pseudoscalar](wikipedia://en/475035) invariant:** The product of the tensor $F^{\mu\nu}$ with its [Hodge dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_dual) $G^{\mu\nu}$ gives a [Lorentz invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_invariant): $$G_{\gamma\delta}F^{\gamma\delta} = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}F^{\alpha\beta} F^{\gamma\delta} = -\frac{4}{c} \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{E} \,$$ where $\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}$ is the rank-4 [Levi-Civita symbol](wikipedia://en/195982). The sign for the above depends on the convention used for the Levi-Civita symbol. The convention used here is $\varepsilon_{0123} = -1$. This and the previous Lorentz invariant vanish in the crossed field case.
1. **[Determinant](wikipedia://en/8468):** $$\det F = \frac{1}{c^2} \left( \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{E} \right)^2$$ which is proportional to the square of the above invariant.
1. **[Trace](wikipedia://en/43270):** $$\operatorname{tr} F = {F^{\mu}}_{\mu} = 0$$

:::

### Significance {#Significance}

This tensor simplifies and reduces [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737) as four vector calculus equations into two tensor field equations. In [electrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatic) and [electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrodynamic), [Gauss's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_law) and [Ampère's circuital law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampère's_circuital_law) are respectively:

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} &= \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, & \nabla \times \mathbf{B} & = \frac{1}{c^2} \frac{ \partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} + \mu_0 \mathbf{J} \end{aligned}$$

and reduce to the inhomogeneous Maxwell equation:

$$\partial_{\alpha} F^{\beta\alpha} = - \mu_0 J^{\beta},$$

where $J^{\alpha} = ( c\rho, \mathbf{J} )$ is the [four-current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-current).

In [magnetostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetostatic) and magnetodynamics, [Gauss's law for magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_law_for_magnetism) and [Maxwell–Faraday equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday's_law_of_induction) are respectively:

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} &= 0, & \nabla \times \mathbf{E} &= - \frac{ \partial \mathbf{B}}{ \partial t } \end{aligned}$$

which reduce to the [Bianchi identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bianchi_identity):

$$\partial_\gamma F_{ \alpha \beta } + \partial_\alpha F_{ \beta \gamma } + \partial_\beta F_{ \gamma \alpha } = 0$$

or using the [index notation with square brackets](wikipedia://en/35456546#Symmetric_and_antisymmetric_parts) for the antisymmetric part of the tensor:

$$\partial_{ [ \alpha } F_{ \beta \gamma ] } = 0$$

Using the expression relating the Faraday tensor to the four-potential, one can prove that the above antisymmetric quantity turns to zero identically ($\equiv 0$). This tensor equation reproduces the homogeneous Maxwell's equations.

:::

## Relativity {#Relativity}

The field tensor derives its name from the fact that the electromagnetic field is found to obey the [tensor transformation law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_transformation_law), this general property of physical laws being recognised after the advent of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962). This theory stipulated that all the laws of physics should take the same form in all coordinate systems – this led to the introduction of [tensors](wikipedia://en/29965). The tensor formalism also leads to a mathematically simpler presentation of physical laws.

The inhomogeneous Maxwell equation leads to the [continuity equation](wikipedia://en/410793):

$$\partial_\alpha J^\alpha = J^\alpha{}_{,\alpha} = 0$$

implying [conservation of charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_charge).

Maxwell's laws above can be generalised to [curved spacetime](wikipedia://en/1016422) by simply replacing [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565) with [covariant derivatives](wikipedia://en/431848):

$$F_{[\alpha\beta;\gamma]} = 0$$

and $F^{\alpha\beta}{}_{;\alpha} = \mu_0 J^{\beta}$

where the [semicolon notation](wikipedia://en/431848#Notation) represents a covariant derivative, as opposed to a partial derivative. These equations are sometimes referred to as the [curved space Maxwell equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell's_equations_in_curved_spacetime). Again, the second equation implies charge conservation (in curved spacetime):

$$J^\alpha{}_{;\alpha} \, = 0$$

The stress-energy tensor of electromagnetism

$$T^{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{\mu_0} \left[ F^{\mu \alpha}F^\nu{}_{\alpha} - \frac{1}{4} \eta^{\mu\nu}F_{\alpha\beta} F^{\alpha\beta}\right] \,,$$

satisfies

$${T^{\alpha\beta}}_{,\beta} + F^{\alpha\beta} J_\beta = 0\,.$$

## Lagrangian formulation of classical electromagnetism {#Lagrangian_formulation_of_classical_electromagnetism}

[Classical electromagnetism](wikipedia://en/426219) and [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737) can be derived from the [action](wikipedia://en/312881):

$$\mathcal{S} = \int \left( -\begin{matrix} \frac{1}{4 \mu_0} \end{matrix} F_{\mu\nu} F^{\mu\nu} - J^\mu A_\mu \right) \mathrm{d}^4 x \,$$

where $\mathrm{d}^4 x$ is over space and time.

This means the [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/40752010) density is

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L} &= -\frac{1}{4\mu_0} F_{\mu\nu} F^{\mu\nu} - J^\mu A_\mu \\ &= -\frac{1}{4\mu_0} \left( \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu \right) \left( \partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu \right) - J^\mu A_\mu \\ &= -\frac{1}{4\mu_0} \left( \partial_\mu A_\nu \partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu \partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial_\mu A_\nu \partial^\nu A^\mu + \partial_\nu A_\mu \partial^\nu A^\mu \right) - J^\mu A_\mu \\ \end{aligned}$$

The two middle terms in the parentheses are the same, as are the two outer terms, so the Lagrangian density is

$$\mathcal{L} = - \frac{1}{2\mu_0} \left( \partial_\mu A_\nu \partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu \partial^\mu A^\nu \right) - J^\mu A_\mu.$$

Substituting this into the [Euler–Lagrange equation](wikipedia://en/294995) of motion for a field:

$$\partial_\mu \left( \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial ( \partial_\mu A_\nu )} \right) - \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial A_\nu} = 0$$

So the Euler–Lagrange equation becomes:

$$- \partial_\mu \frac{1}{\mu_0} \left( \partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu \right) + J^\nu = 0. \,$$

The quantity in parentheses above is just the field tensor, so this finally simplifies to

$$\partial_\mu F^{\mu \nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$$

That equation is another way of writing the two inhomogeneous [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737) (namely, [Gauss's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_law) and [Ampère's circuital law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampère's_circuital_law)) using the substitutions:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{c}E^i &= -F^{0 i} \\ \varepsilon^{ijk} B_k &= -F^{ij} \end{aligned}$$

where *i, j, k* take the values 1, 2, and 3.

### Hamiltonian form {#Hamiltonian_form}

The [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/47392500) density can be obtained with the usual relation,

$$\mathcal{H}(\phi^i,\pi_i) = \pi_i \dot{\phi}^i(\phi^i,\pi_i) - \mathcal{L} \,.$$

Here $\phi^i=A^{i}$ are the fields and the momentum density of the EM field is

$$\pi_i= T_{0i}=\frac{1}{\mu_0} F_0{}^{ \alpha}F_{i\alpha}=\frac{1}{\mu_0 c} \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B} \,.$$

such that the conserved quantity associated with translation from [Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159) is the total momentum

$$\mathbf{P}= \sum_{\alpha} m_\alpha \dot{\mathbf{x}}_{\alpha} + \frac{1}{\mu_0 c}\int_{\mathcal{V}} \mathrm{d}^3 x\, \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B} \,.$$

The Hamiltonian density for the electromagnetic field is related to the [electromagnetic stress-energy tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_stress-energy_tensor)

$$T^{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{\mu_0} \left[ F^{\mu \alpha}F^\nu{}_{\alpha} - \frac{1}{4} \eta^{\mu\nu}F_{\alpha\beta} F^{\alpha\beta}\right] \,.$$

as

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{H} = T_{0 0} &= \frac{1}{2}\left(\varepsilon_0 \mathbf{E}^2+\frac{1}{\mu_0}\mathbf{B}^2\right) \\[1ex] &= \frac{1}{8\pi}\left(\mathbf{E}^2+\mathbf{B}^2\right)\,. \end{aligned}$$

where we have neglected the [energy density of matter](wikipedia://en/2137509), assuming only the EM field, and the last equality assumes the CGS system. The momentum of nonrelativistic charges interacting with the EM field in the [Coulomb gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb_gauge) ($\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot \mathbf{A}=\nabla_i A^i = 0$) is

$$\mathbf{p}_\alpha = m_\alpha \dot{\mathbf{x}}_{\alpha} + \frac{q_{\alpha}}{c} \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}_\alpha) \,.$$

The total Hamiltonian of the matter + EM field system is

$$H = \int_\mathcal{V} d^3 x \,T_{00} = H_\text{mat} + H_\text{em} \,.$$

where for nonrelativistic point particles in the Coulomb gauge

$$\begin{aligned} H_\text{mat} &= \sum_\alpha m_{\alpha} \left|\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{\alpha}\right|^2 + \sum_{\alpha<\beta} \frac{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}{\left|\mathbf{x}_{\alpha} - \mathbf{x}_{\beta}\right| } \\[1ex] &= \sum_\alpha \frac{1}{2m_\alpha} \left[\mathbf{p}_{\alpha} - \frac{q_{\alpha}}{c} \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}_\alpha)\right]^2 + \sum_{\alpha<\beta} \frac{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}{\left|\mathbf{x}_{\alpha} - \mathbf{x}_{\beta}\right| } \,. \end{aligned}$$

where the last term is identically $\frac{1}{8\pi} \int_\mathcal{V} d^3 x \, \mathbf{E}_{\parallel}^2$ where ${E}_{\parallel i} = {\nabla_i}A_0$ and

$$H_\text{em} = \frac{1}{8\pi} \int_\mathcal{V} d^3 x \left(\mathbf{E}_{\perp}^2+\mathbf{B}^2\right) \,.$$

where and ${E}_{\perp i} = -\frac{1}{c}\partial_0 A_i$.

### Quantum electrodynamics and field theory {#Quantum_electrodynamics_and_field_theory}

The [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/40752010) of [quantum electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/25268) extends beyond the classical Lagrangian established in relativity to incorporate the creation and annihilation of photons (and electrons):

$$\mathcal{L} = \bar\psi \left(i\hbar c \, \gamma^\alpha D_\alpha - mc^2\right) \psi - \frac{1}{4\mu_0} F_{\alpha\beta} F^{\alpha\beta},$$

where the first part in the right hand side, containing the [Dirac spinor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_spinor) $\psi$, represents the [Dirac field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_field). In [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267) it is used as the template for the gauge field strength tensor. By being employed in addition to the local interaction Lagrangian it reprises its usual role in QED.
