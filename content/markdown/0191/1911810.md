# Language model

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Language model"}

A **language model** is a computational [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model#Conceptual_model) that predicts sequences in [natural language](wikipedia://en/21173).[^o2][^o3] Language models are useful for a variety of tasks, including [speech recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_recognition),[^o4] [machine translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_translation),[^o5] [natural language generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_language_generation) (generating more human-like text), [optical character recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_character_recognition), [route optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Route_optimization),[^o6] [handwriting recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handwriting_recognition),[^o7] [grammar induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammar_induction),[^o8] [information retrieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_retrieval)[^o9][^o10] and [disaster response](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disaster_response).[^o11]

:::

[Large language models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_model) (LLMs), currently their most advanced form as of 2026, are predominantly based on [transformers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer_%28machine_learning%29) trained on larger [datasets](wikipedia://en/8495) (frequently using texts [scraped](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_scraping) from the public [internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet)). They have superseded [recurrent neural network](wikipedia://en/1706303)-based models, which had previously superseded the purely [statistical models](wikipedia://en/27576), such as the [word *n*-gram language model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_n-gram_language_model).

## History {#History}

During the 1950s, [Noam Chomsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noam_Chomsky) developed influential formal approaches to describing language, including models based on [formal grammar](wikipedia://en/18020716).[^o13]

In the 1970s, [Frederick Jelinek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Jelinek) and colleagues at [IBM Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Research) developed statistical approaches to language modeling as part of their work on [speech recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_recognition). The group used probabilistic [n-gram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-gram) models of word sequences and introduced [perplexity](wikipedia://en/4631023) as an information-theoretic measure of the uncertainty of a language model.[^o14] Jelinek became a leading proponent of statistical language modeling.[^o15]

Neural language models became increasingly important in the 2000s, with [Yoshua Bengio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshua_Bengio) and co-authors introducing a probabilistic [neural network](wikipedia://en/21523) language model that learned distributed [word representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding) while estimating the probability of word sequences.[^o16]

[Recurrent neural networks](wikipedia://en/1706303) extended this approach by retaining information from earlier words as the sequence was processed. [Tomáš Mikolov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomáš_Mikolov) and colleagues demonstrated in 2010 that recurrent neural-network language models could substantially outperform conventional n-gram models.[^o17]

## Pure statistical models {#Pure_statistical_models}

In 1980, the first significant statistical language model was proposed, and during the decade [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM) performed '[Shannon](wikipedia://en/5693)-style' experiments, in which potential sources for language modeling improvement were identified by observing and analyzing the performance of human subjects in predicting or correcting text.[^o19]

### Exponential {#Exponential}

[Maximum entropy](wikipedia://en/201718) language models encode the relationship between a word and the *n*-gram history using feature functions. The [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) is

$$P(w_m \mid w_1,\ldots,w_{m-1}) = \frac{1}{Z(w_1,\ldots,w_{m-1})} \exp (a^T f(w_1,\ldots,w_m))$$

where $Z(w_1,\ldots,w_{m-1})$ is the [partition function](wikipedia://en/16846849), $a$ is the parameter vector, and $f(w_1,\ldots,w_m)$ is the feature function. In the simplest case, the feature function is just an indicator of the presence of a certain *n*-gram. It is helpful to use a prior on $a$ or some form of [regularization](wikipedia://en/2009061).

The log-bilinear model is another example of an exponential language model.

## Neural models {#Neural_models}

### Recurrent neural network {#Recurrent_neural_network}

Continuous representations or [embeddings of words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding) are produced in [recurrent neural network](wikipedia://en/1706303)-based language models (known also as *continuous space language models*).[^o26] Such continuous space embeddings help to alleviate the [curse of dimensionality](wikipedia://en/787776), which is the consequence of the number of possible sequences of words increasing [exponentially](wikipedia://en/191933) with the size of the vocabulary, further causing a data sparsity problem. Neural networks avoid this problem by representing words as non-linear combinations of weights in a neural net.[^o27]

### Large language models {#Large_language_models}

Large language model

Although sometimes matching human performance, it is not clear whether they are plausible [cognitive models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_model). At least for recurrent neural networks, it has been shown that they sometimes learn patterns that humans do not, but fail to learn patterns that humans typically do.[^o29]

## Evaluation and benchmarks {#Evaluation_and_benchmarks}

Evaluation of the quality of language models is mostly done by comparison to human created sample [benchmarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benchmark_%28computing%29) created from typical language-oriented tasks. Other, less established, quality tests examine the intrinsic character of a language model or compare two such models. Since language models are typically intended to be dynamic and to learn from data they see, some proposed models investigate the rate of learning, e.g., through inspection of learning curves.[^o32]

Various data sets have been developed for use in evaluating language processing systems.[^o33] These include:

- [Massive Multitask Language Understanding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massive_Multitask_Language_Understanding) (MMLU)[^o34]
- [Corpus of Linguistic Acceptability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_of_Linguistic_Acceptability)[^o35]
- GLUE benchmark[^o36]
- Microsoft Research Paraphrase Corpus[^o37]
- Multi-Genre Natural Language Inference[^o38]
- Question Natural Language Inference
- Quora Question Pairs[^o39]
- Recognizing Textual Entailment[^o40]
- Semantic Textual Similarity Benchmark
- SQuAD question answering Test[^o41]
- Stanford Sentiment [Treebank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treebank)[^o42]
- Winograd NLI
- BoolQ, PIQA, SIQA, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, ARC, OpenBookQA, NaturalQuestions, TriviaQA, RACE, BIG-bench hard, GSM8k, RealToxicityPrompts, WinoGender, CrowS-Pairs[^o43]
