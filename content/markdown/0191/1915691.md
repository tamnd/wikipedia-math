# Off-the-Record Messaging protocol

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Off-the-Record Messaging protocol"}

**Off-the-Record Messaging** (**OTR**) is a [cryptographic protocol](wikipedia://en/591994) that provides encryption for [instant messaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant_messaging) conversations. OTR uses a combination of [AES](wikipedia://en/1260) [symmetric-key algorithm](wikipedia://en/53042) with 128 bits key length, the [Diffie–Hellman key exchange](wikipedia://en/7903) with 1536 bits group size, and the [SHA-1](wikipedia://en/26672) hash function. In addition to [authentication](wikipedia://en/47967) and [encryption](wikipedia://en/10294), OTR provides [forward secrecy](wikipedia://en/2191130) and [malleable encryption](wikipedia://en/87027).

:::

The primary motivation behind the protocol was providing [deniable authentication](wikipedia://en/9246275) for the conversation participants while keeping conversations confidential, like a private conversation in real life, or [off the record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journalism_sourcing#Using_confidential_information) in [journalism sourcing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journalism_sourcing). This is in contrast with cryptography tools that produce output which can be later used as a verifiable record of the communication event and the identities of the participants. The initial introductory paper was named "Off-the-Record Communication, or, Why Not to Use [PGP](wikipedia://en/23080)".[^o15]

The OTR protocol was designed by cryptographers [Ian Goldberg](wikipedia://en/829135) and [Nikita Borisov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikita_Borisov) and released on 26 October 2004.[^o17] They provide a client [library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_%28computer_science%29) to facilitate support for instant messaging client developers who want to implement the protocol. A [Pidgin](wikipedia://en/188211) and [Kopete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kopete) plugin exists that allows OTR to be used over any IM protocol supported by Pidgin or Kopete, offering an [auto-detection](wikipedia://en/4299490) feature that starts the OTR session with the buddies that have it enabled, without interfering with regular, unencrypted conversations. Version 4 of the protocol[^o16] has been in development since 2017[^o2] by a team led by Sofía Celi, and reviewed by Nik Unger and Ian Goldberg. This version aims to provide online and offline deniability, to update the cryptographic primitives, and to support [out-of-order delivery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Out-of-order_delivery) and asynchronous communication.

According to classified [NSA documents](https://cdn.prod.www.spiegel.de/media/af108995-0001-0014-0000-000000035552/media-35552.pdf) published in the *[Der Spiegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_Spiegel)* article on 28 December 2014, the [NSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010s_global_surveillance_disclosures) intercepted a conversation between two users, but messages could not be decrypted by the NSA because the users were using the OTR protocol.[^o3]

## History {#History}

OTR was presented in 2004 by Nikita Borisov, [Ian Avrum Goldberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Avrum_Goldberg), and [Eric A. Brewer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Brewer_%28scientist%29) as an improvement over the OpenPGP and the S/MIME system at the "Workshop on Privacy in the Electronic Society" (WPES).[^o15] The first version 0.8.0 of the reference implementation was published on 21 November 2004. In 2005 an analysis was presented by Mario Di Raimondo, Rosario Gennaro, and Hugo Krawczyk that called attention to several vulnerabilities and proposed appropriate fixes, most notably including a flaw in the key exchange.[^o23] As a result, version 2 of the OTR protocol was published in 2005 implementing a variation of the proposed modification that also hides the public keys. Moreover, the possibility to fragment OTR messages was introduced in order to deal with chat systems that have a limited message size, and a simpler method of verification against man-in-the-middle attacks was implemented.[^o20]

In 2007 [Olivier Goffart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivier_Goffart) published `mod_otr`[^o5] for [ejabberd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ejabberd), making it possible to perform [man-in-the-middle attacks](wikipedia://en/144676) on OTR users who don't check key fingerprints. OTR developers countered this attack by introducing a [socialist millionaire protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_millionaires) implementation in libotr. Instead of comparing key checksums, knowledge of an arbitrary shared secret can be utilised for which relatively low [entropy](wikipedia://en/15445) can be tolerated.[^o24]

Version 3 of the protocol was published in 2012. As a measure against the repeated reestablishment of a session in case of several competing chat clients being signed on to the same user address at the same time, more precise identification labels for sending and receiving client instances were introduced in version 3. Moreover, an additional key is negotiated which can be used for another data channel.[^o19]

Several solutions have been proposed for supporting conversations with multiple participants. A method proposed in 2007 by Jiang Bian, Remzi Seker, and Umit Topaloglu uses the system of one participant as a "virtual server".[^o25] The method called "Multi-party Off-the-Record Messaging" (mpOTR) which was published in 2009 works without a central management host and was introduced in [Cryptocat](wikipedia://en/33741859) by Ian Goldberg et al.[^o26]

In 2013, the [Signal Protocol](wikipedia://en/50607084) was introduced, which is based on OTR Messaging and the [Silent Circle Instant Messaging Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_Circle_Instant_Messaging_Protocol) (SCIMP). It brought about support for asynchronous communication ("offline messages") as its major new feature, as well as better resilience with distorted order of messages and simpler support for conversations with multiple participants.[^o27] [OMEMO](wikipedia://en/48005845), introduced in an Android XMPP client called [Conversations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversations_%28software%29) in 2015, integrates the [Double Ratchet Algorithm](wikipedia://en/49111333) used in Signal into the instant messaging protocol [XMPP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XMPP) ("Jabber") and also enables encryption of file transfers. In the autumn of 2015 it was submitted to the [XMPP Standards Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XMPP_Standards_Foundation) for standardisation.[^o28][^o29]

Currently, version 4 of the protocol has been designed. It was presented by Sofía Celi and Ola Bini on PETS2018.[^o30]

## Implementation {#Implementation}

In addition to providing encryption and authentication — features also provided by typical public-key cryptography suites, such as [PGP](wikipedia://en/23080), [GnuPG](wikipedia://en/38809), and [X.509](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X.509) ([S/MIME](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S/MIME)) — OTR also offers some less common features:

**[Forward secrecy](wikipedia://en/2191130)**

Messages are only [encrypted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encrypted) with temporary per-message [AES](wikipedia://en/1260) keys, negotiated using the [Diffie–Hellman key exchange](wikipedia://en/7903) protocol. The compromise of any long-lived cryptographic keys does not compromise any previous conversations, even if an attacker is in possession of [ciphertexts](wikipedia://en/175560).

**[Deniable authentication](wikipedia://en/9246275)**

Messages in a conversation do not have [digital signatures](wikipedia://en/59644), and after a conversation is complete, anyone is able to forge a message to appear to have come from one of the participants in the conversation, assuring that it is impossible to prove that a specific message came from a specific person. Within the conversation the recipient can be sure that a message is coming from the person they have identified.

:::

## Authentication {#Authentication}

As of OTR 3.1, the protocol supports mutual authentication of users using a shared secret through the [socialist millionaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_millionaire) protocol. This feature makes it possible for users to verify the identity of the remote party and avoid a [man-in-the-middle attack](wikipedia://en/144676) without the inconvenience of manually comparing [public key fingerprints](wikipedia://en/7943312) through an outside channel.

## Limitations {#Limitations}

Due to limitations of the protocol, OTR does not support multi-user group chat [^o18] but it may be implemented in the future. As of version 3[^o19] of the protocol specification, during authenticated key exchanges an extra symmetric key is derived that can be used for secure communication (e.g., encrypted [file transfers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File_transfer)) over a different channel. Support for encrypted audio or video is not planned. ([SRTP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secure_Real-time_Transport_Protocol) with [ZRTP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZRTP) exists for that purpose.) A project to produce a protocol for multi-party off-the-record messaging (mpOTR) has been organized by [Cryptocat](wikipedia://en/33741859), [eQualitie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EQualitie), and other contributors, including Ian Goldberg.[^o26][^o21]

Since the OTR protocol v3 (libotr 4.0.0) the plugin supports multiple OTR conversations with the same buddy who is logged in at multiple locations.[^o22]
