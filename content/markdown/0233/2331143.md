# Chen prime

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Chen prime"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) *p* is called a **Chen prime** if *p* + 2 is either a prime or a [product of two primes](wikipedia://en/339542) (also called a semiprime). The [even number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_number) 2*p* + 2 therefore satisfies [Chen's theorem](wikipedia://en/1400840).

:::

The Chen primes are named after [Chen Jingrun](wikipedia://en/352985), who [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) in 1966 that there are [infinitely](wikipedia://en/142679) many such primes. This result would also follow from the truth of the [twin prime conjecture](wikipedia://en/41997) as the lower member of a pair of [twin primes](wikipedia://en/41997) is by definition a Chen prime.

The first few Chen primes are

[2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2_%28number%29), [3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3_%28number%29), [5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5_%28number%29), [7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7_%28number%29), [11](wikipedia://en/208156), [13](wikipedia://en/37231), [17](wikipedia://en/208288), [19](wikipedia://en/208292), [23](wikipedia://en/362203), [29](wikipedia://en/362212), [31](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/31_%28number%29), [37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/37_%28number%29), [41](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/41_%28number%29), [47](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/47_%28number%29), [53](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/53_%28number%29), [59](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/59_%28number%29), [67](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/67_%28number%29), [71](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/71_%28number%29), [83](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/83_%28number%29), [89](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/89_%28number%29), [101](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/101_%28number%29), ... .

The first few Chen primes that are not the lower member of a pair of twin primes are

2, 7, 13, 19, 23, 31, 37, 47, 53, 67, 83, 89, 109, 113, 127, ... .

The first few non-Chen primes are

43, 61, 73, 79, 97, 103, 151, 163, 173, 193, 223, 229, 241, ... .

All of the [supersingular primes](wikipedia://en/22965333) are Chen primes.

[Rudolf Ondrejka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Ondrejka) discovered the following 3 × 3 [magic square](wikipedia://en/52202) of nine Chen primes:[^o4]

| 17 | 89 | 71 |
|---|---|---|
| 113 | 59 | 5 |
| 47 | 29 | 101 |

, the largest known Chen prime is 2996863034895 × 2<sup>1290000</sup> − 1, with 388342 decimal digits.

The sum of the [reciprocals](wikipedia://en/229940) of Chen primes [converges](wikipedia://en/1743431).

## Further results {#Further_results}

Chen also proved the following generalization: For any even [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *h*, there exist infinitely many primes *p* such that *p* + *h* is either a prime or a [semiprime](wikipedia://en/339542).

[Ben Green](wikipedia://en/1927952) and [Terence Tao](wikipedia://en/1118498) showed that the Chen primes contain infinitely many [arithmetic progressions](wikipedia://en/168389) of length 3.[^o6] Binbin Zhou generalized this result by showing that the Chen primes contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions.[^o7]
