# Hilbert's eighteenth problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hilbert's eighteenth problem"}

**Hilbert's eighteenth problem** is [one of the 23 problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_problems) set out in a celebrated list compiled in 1900 by mathematician [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302). It asks three separate questions on the space group in $n$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), [anisohedral tiling](wikipedia://en/13180391) in three-dimensional Euclidean space, and the densest sphere packing in [Kepler conjecture](wikipedia://en/368311). Respectively, these questions were answered affirmatively by [Ludwig Bieberbach](wikipedia://en/616186), [Karl Reinhardt](wikipedia://en/35727144), and [Thomas Callister Hales](wikipedia://en/2388356).

:::

## First question: symmetry groups in $n$ dimensions {#First_question:_symmetry_groups_in_dimensions}

Space group#Bieberbach's theorems The first question in Hilbert's problem asks whether there are only finitely many essentially different [space groups](wikipedia://en/463721) in $n$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). Here, a space group or crystallographic group is the [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) of a repeating pattern in space, usually in three dimensions, with its [symmetry operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_operation) as the [rigid transformations](wikipedia://en/27832980) of the pattern that leave it unchanged.

This question was answered affirmatively by [Ludwig Bieberbach](wikipedia://en/616186). Named as Bieberbach group, Bieberbach proved that the subgroup of translations of any such group contains $n$ linearly independent translations, and is a [free abelian](wikipedia://en/247296) subgroup of finite index, and is also the unique maximal normal abelian subgroup. Bieberbach also showed that, in any dimension $n$, there are only a finite number of possibilities for the [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/45241) class of the underlying group of a space group, and the action of the group on Euclidean space is moreover unique up to [conjugation](wikipedia://en/2977884) by [affine transformations](wikipedia://en/38449).

## Second question: anisohedral tiling in three dimensions {#Second_question:_anisohedral_tiling_in_three_dimensions}

The second question in Hilbert's problem asks whether there exists a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) in [three-dimensional Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/3054853) that [tiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessellation_of_space) but not the [fundamental region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_region) of any space group. In other words, the second problem asked which tiles, but does not admit an [isohedral](wikipedia://en/6286120) (tile-[transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29)) tiling. Such tiles are not equivalent under any symmetry of the tiling, which are now known as [anisohedral](wikipedia://en/13180391). Posing the problem in three dimensions, Hilbert probably assumed that no such tile exists in two dimensions; this assumption later proved to be incorrect.

Such a three dimensions polyhedron was found by [Karl Reinhardt](wikipedia://en/35727144) in his 1928 booklet titled *Zur Zerlegung der euklidischen Räume in kongruente Polytope*.[^o4] The first example in two dimensions was found by [Heinrich Heesch](wikipedia://en/1156248) in 1935.

The [einstein problem](wikipedia://en/43542926), where "einstein" means "one stone" in German's [word play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_play), is the natural extension of Hilbert's eighteenth problem's second question. The problem asks for a shape that can tile space but not with an [infinite cyclic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_cyclic_group) of symmetries. Equivalently, it asks the existence of a single [prototile](wikipedia://en/39372) that forms an [aperiodic set](wikipedia://en/41287104), a shape that can tessellate space but only in a non-periodic way. It was solved in 2024 by [David Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Smith_%28amateur_mathematician%29) and his colleagues.

## Third question: densest sphere packing {#Third_question:_densest_sphere_packing}

The third question in Hilbert's problem asks for the densest [sphere packing](wikipedia://en/368621) or packing of other specified shapes. Although it expressly includes shapes other than spheres, it is generally taken as equivalent to the [Kepler conjecture](wikipedia://en/368311). The conjecture was named after 17th-century German mathematician [Johannes Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736) in his booklet titled *Strena, seu, de Nieu Sexangula*. It states that the packing of equal volume spheres has a density greater than the packing of the [face-centered cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face-centered_cubic) arrangement. Such a density is

$$\frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}} = 0.74048.$$

In 1998, American mathematician [Thomas Callister Hales](wikipedia://en/2388356) gave a [computer-aided proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer-aided_proof) of the Kepler conjecture. It shows that the most space-efficient way to pack spheres is in a pyramid shape.
