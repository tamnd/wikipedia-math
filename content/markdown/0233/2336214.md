# Hilbert's seventeenth problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hilbert's seventeenth problem"}

**Hilbert's seventeenth problem** is one of the 23 of [Hilbert's problems](wikipedia://en/154584) set out in a celebrated list compiled in 1900 by [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302). It concerns the expression of [positive definite](wikipedia://en/649616) [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) as [sums](wikipedia://en/246160) of [quotients](wikipedia://en/332640) of [squares](wikipedia://en/659942). The original question may be reformulated as:

:::

- Given a multivariate polynomial that takes only non-negative values over the reals, can it be represented as a sum of squares of rational functions?

Hilbert's question can be restricted to [homogeneous polynomials](wikipedia://en/1698977) of even degree, since a polynomial of odd degree changes sign, and the [homogenization of a polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogenization_of_a_polynomial) takes only nonnegative values if and only if the same is true for the polynomial.

## Original statement {#Original_statement}

In one English translation, Hilbert's seventeenth problem is stated as follows:[^o3]

> A rational integral function or form in any number of variables with real coefficients such that it becomes negative for no real values of these variables, is said to be definite. The system of all definite forms is invariant with respect to the operations of addition and multiplication, but the quotient of two definite forms—in case it should be an integral function of the variables—is also a definite form. The square of any form is evidently always a definite form. But since, as I have shown,[^o6] not every definite form can be compounded by addition from squares of forms, the question arises—which I have answered affirmatively for ternary forms[^o11]—whether every definite form may not be expressed as a quotient of sums of squares of forms. At the same time it is desirable, for certain questions as to the possibility of certain geometrical constructions, to know whether the coefficients of the forms to be used in the expression may always be taken from the realm of rationality given by the coefficients of the form represented.

## Motivation {#Motivation}

The formulation of the question takes into account that there are [non-negative polynomials](wikipedia://en/28416554), for example[^o5]

$$f(x,y,z)=z^6+x^4y^2+x^2y^4-3x^2y^2z^2,$$

which cannot be represented as a [sum of squares of other polynomials](wikipedia://en/17001761). In 1888, Hilbert showed that every non-negative homogeneous polynomial in *n* variables and degree 2*d* can be represented as sum of squares of other polynomials if and only if either (a) *n* = 2 or (b) 2*d* = 2 or (c) *n* = 3 and 2*d* = 4.[^o6] Hilbert's proof did not exhibit any explicit counterexample: only in 1967 the first explicit counterexample was constructed by [Motzkin](wikipedia://en/13554055).[^o7] Furthermore, if the polynomial has a degree 2*d* greater than two, there are significantly many more non-negative polynomials that cannot be expressed as sums of squares.[^o8]

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|  |  | 2 | 4 | ≥6 |  |  |  | 2 | 4 | ≥6 |

## Solution and generalizations {#Solution_and_generalizations}

The particular case of *n* = 2 was already solved by Hilbert in 1893.[^o11] The general problem was solved in the affirmative, in 1927, by [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351),[^o12] for positive semidefinite functions over the reals or more generally [real-closed fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-closed_field). An algorithmic solution was found by [Charles Delzell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Neal_Delzell) in 1984.[^o13] A result of [Albrecht Pfister](wikipedia://en/35551178)[^o14] shows that a positive semidefinite form in *n* variables can be expressed as a sum of 2<sup>*n*</sup> squares.[^o15]

Dubois showed in 1967 that the answer is negative in general for [ordered fields](wikipedia://en/22430).[^o16] In this case one can say that a positive polynomial is a sum of weighted squares of rational functions with positive coefficients.[^o17] McKenna showed in 1975 that all positive semidefinite polynomials with coefficients in an ordered field are sums of weighted squares of rational functions with positive coefficients only if the field is dense in its real closure in the sense that any interval with endpoints in the real closure contains elements from the original field.[^o18]

A generalization to the matrix case (matrices with polynomial function entries that are always positive semidefinite can be expressed as sum of squares of symmetric matrices with rational function entries) was given by Gondard, [Ribenboim](wikipedia://en/9129242)[^o19] and Procesi, Schacher,[^o20] with an [elementary proof](wikipedia://en/9318685) given by Hillar and Nie.[^o21]

In [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759) and [complex geometry](wikipedia://en/186101), the Hermitian analogue, requiring the squares to be squared norms of holomorphic polynomials, was proven for strictly positive polynomials by [Quillen](wikipedia://en/435519) using techniques based on [elliptic partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/662787).[^o22] The sum-of-squares representation is unique if it exists, which was first observed by Putinar in the context of optimization.[^o23]

## Minimum number of square rational terms {#Minimum_number_of_square_rational_terms}

::: {#o25 .problem cue=unsolved}

What is the minimum number of rational functions needed to represent any non-negative n-variate, degree d polynomial?

:::

It is an open question what is the smallest number

$$v(n,d),$$

such that any *n*-variate, non-negative polynomial of degree *d* can be written as sum of at most $v(n,d)$ square rational functions over the reals. An [upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound) due to Pfister in 1967 is:[^o14]

$$v(n,d)\leq2^n,$$

In the other direction, a conditional lower bound can be derived from [computational complexity theory](wikipedia://en/7543). An *n*-variable instance of [3-SAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-SAT) can be realized as a positivity problem on a polynomial with *n* variables and *d=4*. This proves that positivity testing is [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard). More precisely, assuming the [exponential time hypothesis](wikipedia://en/26434552) to be true, $v(n,d) = 2^{\Omega(n)}$.

The result of Pfister fails in the Hermitian case, that is there is no bound on the number of squares required, see D'Angelo–Lebl.[^o26]
