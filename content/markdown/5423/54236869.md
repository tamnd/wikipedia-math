# Francesco Eschinardi

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Francesco Eschinardi"}

**Francesco Eschinardi**, (also known under the pseudonym **Costanzo Amichevoli**; 13 December 1623 – 12 January 1703), was an Italian [Jesuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuit), [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist) and [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902).[^o4][^o5]

:::

## Life {#Life}

Eschinardi was born in [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome) on 13 December 1623. He entered the [Society of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_Jesus) in 1637 at the age of 14 and subsequently joined the faculty of the [Roman College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_College), teaching [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) in 1658, [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) in 1659, and [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics) in 1660. Traveling extensively, he taught at various Jesuit [seminaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminary) and Italian universities. He became professor of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) in [Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence) and at [Perugia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perugia), then from 1665 he was again teaching at the Roman College, where he held the chair of mathematics, [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) and [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy).[^o4] He was [Filippo Bonanni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filippo_Bonanni)'s tutor and [Giuseppe Campani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Campani)'s scientific mentor.[^n1]

Eschinardi had a solid scientific background and knowledge of the works of the proponents of the ‘[new science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science)’, from [Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Copernicus) to [Galilei](wikipedia://en/29688374) and [Borelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Alfonso_Borelli).[^o4] He was concerned with a great number of scientific projects, ranging from the physics of [sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound) to [perpetual motion](wikipedia://en/54077), and he also was involved with attempting to graduate [thermometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermometer) on a scientific principle.[^o8] He was also a skilled astronomer who carried out much valuable work. From his observatory at the Roman College, he was the first to observe the great [comet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet) of 1668.[^o4] Together with Marco Antonio Cellio and Giuseppe Pontio, Eschinardi was one of the first to see the [great comet of 1680](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Comet_of_1680) on 17 November while observing the [moons of Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moons_of_Jupiter) to determine [longitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude).[^o9] Cassini received the observations of the comet from Eschinardi while [Edmond Halley](wikipedia://en/36858805) was in Paris in the first weeks of 1681, and in the autumn of 1681 Halley met Eschinardi in Rome, where he learnt at first hand of the observations.[^o9] Eschinardi had a deep interest in the [archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeology) of Rome and the conversations between the two probably stimulated the interest in Roman archaeology that led to some of Halley's papers in *[Philosophical Transactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Transactions_of_the_Royal_Society)*.[^o10]

Eschinardi was an active member of the Physical-Mathematical Academy founded by [Giovanni Ciampini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Ciampini) and contributed regularly to the *[Giornale de' Letterati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giornale_de'_Letterati)* from 1668 to 1675.[^o4] He often collaborated with his fellow Jesuits [Athanasius Kircher](wikipedia://en/93815) and [Mario Bettinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Bettinus). Bettinus' *Apiaria universae philosophiae mathematicae*, published in 1648, included an appendix describing a [water clock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_clock) (*horologium hydraulicum*) invented by Eschinardi.[^o11] He died in Rome on 12 January 1703.

## Contributions {#Contributions}

Eschinardi was one of the great Jesuit [experimentalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimentalism) of the latter half of the Seventeenth century. Though still tied to certain traditional patterns, especially in astronomy with the reaffirmation of the [geocentric theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentrism), he showed a sincere admiration for the innovative work of modern scholars and the desire to follow in their footsteps by using the [experimental method](wikipedia://en/59861).[^o4]

In 1680 Eschinardi published an account of experiments, many of which he designed and analyzed, performed at the Physical-Mathematical Academy (*Raguagli ... dati ad un'amico in Parigi* \[[G. D. Cassini](wikipedia://en/5215428)\] *sopra alcuni pensieri sperimentali proposti nell'Accademia Fisicomatematica di Roma*. Rome: Tinassi, 1680). Almost half of the *Raguagli* deals with traditional [mechanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics) subjects, like load-bearing wheels (the principles of carriages being a chief interest of Ciampini's), the operation of the [rudder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudder), and the improvement of clocks. Eschinardi follows Galileo's lead in mechanics in most respects, including the [kinematics](wikipedia://en/65914) of [free fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_fall) and [projectile motion](wikipedia://en/862717).[^n2] The *Raguagli* report various plans and results, for example, a proposal by Ciampini to build in Rome a great [sundial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundial) for the exact observation of the [solstices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solstice) and [equinoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox), as Cassini had done in Bologna, a project later accomplished by [Francesco Bianchini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Bianchini) on commission from [Pope Clement XI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Clement_XI).[^n3] For the rest, Eschinardi's work discusses all the current problems in [observational astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_astronomy): how best to determine [atmospheric refraction](wikipedia://en/1784072), observe [eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclipse), measure the moon's [libration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libration), diversify [gnomonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnomonics), and so on. Following Borelli, he maintains an anti-Aristotelian position that explains the condensation and rarefaction of air as the outcome of the motions of corpuscles having the shape of small spirals. He also states that, thanks to the presence of minute empty spaces, they are able to undergo contraction and dilatation.[^o15] The *Raguagli* discuss also physical problems, including the improvement and use of thermometers, and [Torricelli's experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torricelli's_experiment).[^n3]

Eschinardi showed a keen interest in geography and engineering. In his “Speech on the cutting of the Isthmus between the Red Sea and the Mediterranean”[^o17] delivered in 1680 at the Physical-Mathematical Academy, Eschinardi explored the idea of a proto-[Suez Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal) between the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea) and the [Red Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sea).[^o4] As a geographer, he mapped the *[Ager Romanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ager_Romanus)*. Eschinardi was also the first to provide information about the nocturnal [projection clock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projection_clock) invented by [Matteo Campani-Alimenis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matteo_Campani-Alimenis).[^o18]

Historians of science today emphasize the importance of Eschinardi's work in optics and in the development of the [thermometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermometer).[^o19] Eschinardi and [James Gregory](wikipedia://en/220425) were engaged in a dispute regarding who was the initiator of the principle of optical equivalence.[^n4]
