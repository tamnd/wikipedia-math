# D-module

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="D-module"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a ***D*-module** is a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) over a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *D* of [differential operators](wikipedia://en/263886). The major interest of such *D*-modules is as an approach to the theory of [linear partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_partial_differential_equation). Since around 1970, *D*-module theory has been built up, mainly as a response to the ideas of [Mikio Sato](wikipedia://en/373336) on [algebraic analysis](wikipedia://en/20846707), and expanding on the work of Sato and [Joseph Bernstein](wikipedia://en/5815383) on the [Bernstein–Sato polynomial](wikipedia://en/1636721).

:::

Early major results were the Kashiwara constructibility theorem and Kashiwara index theorem of [Masaki Kashiwara](wikipedia://en/4376209). The methods of *D*-module theory have always been drawn from [sheaf theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheaf_theory) and other techniques with inspiration from the work of [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997). This approach is global in character, and differs from the [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) techniques traditionally used to study differential operators. The strongest results are obtained for [over-determined systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Over-determined_system) ([holonomic systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holonomic_system)), and on the [characteristic variety](wikipedia://en/40030306) cut out by the [symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbol_of_a_differential_operator), which in the good case is a [Lagrangian submanifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_submanifold) of the [cotangent bundle](wikipedia://en/221600) of maximal dimension ([involutive systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Involutive_system)). The techniques were taken up from the side of the Grothendieck school by [Zoghman Mebkhout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoghman_Mebkhout), who obtained a general, [derived category](wikipedia://en/985897) version of the [Riemann–Hilbert correspondence](wikipedia://en/10478956) in all dimensions.

## Modules over the Weyl algebra {#Modules_over_the_Weyl_algebra}

The first case of algebraic *D*-modules are modules over the [Weyl algebra](wikipedia://en/491095) *A*<sub>*n*</sub>(*K*) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K* of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) zero. It is the algebra consisting of polynomials in the following variables

*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>, ∂<sub>1</sub>, ..., ∂<sub>*n*</sub>.

where the variables *x*<sub>*i*</sub> and ∂<sub>*j*</sub> separately commute with each other, and *x*<sub>*i*</sub> and ∂<sub>*j*</sub> commute for *i* ≠ *j*, but the [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193) satisfies the relation

\[∂<sub>*i*</sub>, *x*<sub>*i*</sub>\] = ∂<sub>*i*</sub>*x*<sub>*i*</sub> − x<sub>*i*</sub>*∂*<sub>*i*</sub> = 1.

For any polynomial *f*(*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>), this implies the relation

\[∂<sub>*i*</sub>, *f*\] = ∂*f* / ∂*x*<sub>*i*</sub>,

thereby relating the Weyl algebra to differential equations.

An (algebraic) *D*-module is, by definition, a [left module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_module) over the ring *A*<sub>*n*</sub>(*K*). Examples for *D*-modules include the Weyl algebra itself (acting on itself by left multiplication), the (commutative) [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) *K*\[*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>\], where *x*<sub>*i*</sub> acts by multiplication and ∂<sub>*j*</sub> acts by [partial differentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differentiation) with respect to *x*<sub>*j*</sub> and, in a similar vein, the ring $\mathcal O(\mathbf C^n)$ of holomorphic functions on **C**<sup>*n*</sup> (functions of *n* complex variables.)

Given some [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886) *P* = *a*<sub>*n*</sub>(*x*) ∂<sup>*n*</sup> + ... + *a*<sub>1</sub>(*x*) ∂<sup>1</sup> + *a*<sub>0</sub>(*x*), where *x* is a complex variable, *a*<sub>*i*</sub>(*x*) are polynomials, the quotient module *M* = *A*<sub>1</sub>(**C**)/*A*<sub>1</sub>(**C**)*P* is closely linked to space of solutions of the differential equation

*P f* = 0,

where *f* is some [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110) in **C**, say. The [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) consisting of the solutions of that equation is given by the space of homomorphisms of *D*-modules $\mathrm{Hom} (M, \mathcal O(\mathbf C))$.

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Sheaf of differential operators"}

## *D*-modules on algebraic varieties {#D-modules_on_algebraic_varieties}

The general theory of *D*-modules is developed on a [smooth](wikipedia://en/7837041) [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) *X* defined over an algebraically closed field *K* of characteristic zero, such as *K* = **C**. The [sheaf](wikipedia://en/245466) of differential operators *D*<sub>*X*</sub> is defined to be the *O*<sub>*X*</sub>-algebra generated by the [vector fields](wikipedia://en/62641) on *X*, interpreted as [derivations](wikipedia://en/1816587). A (left) *D*<sub>*X*</sub>-module *M* is an *O*<sub>*X*</sub>-module with a left [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of *D*<sub>*X*</sub> on it. Giving such an action is equivalent to specifying a *K*-linear map

$$\nabla: D_X \rightarrow \operatorname{End}_K(M), v \mapsto \nabla_v$$

satisfying

$$\nabla_{f v}(m) = f \, \nabla_v (m)$$

$\nabla_v (f m) = v(f) m + f \, \nabla_v (m)$ ([Leibniz rule](wikipedia://en/248988))

$$\nabla_{[v, w]}(m) = [\nabla_v, \nabla_w](m)$$

Here *f* is a regular function on *X*, *v* and *w* are vector fields, $m \in M$, and \[−, −\] denotes the [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193). Therefore, if *M* is in addition a locally free *O*<sub>*X*</sub>-module, giving *M* a *D*-module structure is nothing else than equipping the [vector bundle](wikipedia://en/276524) associated to *M* with a flat (or integrable) [connection](wikipedia://en/391816).[^n1]

As the ring *D*<sub>*X*</sub> is noncommutative, left and right *D*-modules have to be distinguished. However, the two notions can be exchanged, since there is an [equivalence of categories](wikipedia://en/379051) between both types of modules, given by mapping a left module *M* to the [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) *M* ⊗ Ω<sub>*X*</sub>, where Ω<sub>*X*</sub> is the [line bundle](wikipedia://en/361366) given by the highest [exterior power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_power) of [differential 1-forms](wikipedia://en/221519) on *X*. This bundle has a natural *right* action determined by

ω ⋅ *v* := − Lie<sub>*v*</sub> (ω),

where *v* is a differential operator of order one, that is to say a vector field, ω a *n*-form (*n* = dim *X*), and Lie denotes the [Lie derivative](wikipedia://en/294437).[^n2]

Locally, after choosing some [system of coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_system_of_parameters) *x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub> (*n* = dim *X*) on *X*, which determine a basis ∂<sub>1</sub>, ..., ∂<sub>*n*</sub> of the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) of *X*, sections of *D*<sub>*X*</sub> can be uniquely represented as expressions

$\sum f_{i_1, \dots, i_n} \partial_1^{i_1} \cdots \partial_n^{i_n}$, where the $f_{i_1, \dots, i_n}$ are [regular functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_function) on *X*.

In particular, when *X* is the *n*-dimensional [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834), this *D*<sub>*X*</sub> is the Weyl algebra in *n* variables.

Many basic properties of *D*-modules are local and parallel the situation of [coherent sheaves](wikipedia://en/603780). This builds on the fact that *D*<sub>*X*</sub> is a [locally free sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_free_sheaf) of *O*<sub>*X*</sub>-modules, albeit of infinite rank, as the above-mentioned *O*<sub>*X*</sub>-basis shows. A *D*<sub>*X*</sub>-module that is coherent as an *O*<sub>*X*</sub>-module can be shown to be necessarily locally free (of finite rank).

### Functoriality {#Functoriality}

*D*-modules on different algebraic varieties are connected by [pullback and pushforward functors](wikipedia://en/15769518) comparable to the ones for coherent sheaves. For a [map](wikipedia://en/7482797) *f*: *X* → *Y* of smooth varieties, the definitions are this:

*D*<sub>*X*→*Y*</sub> := *O*<sub>*X*</sub> ⊗<sub>*f*<sup>−1</sup>(*O*<sub>*Y*</sub>)</sub> *f*<sup>−1</sup>(*D*<sub>*Y*</sub>)

This is equipped with a left *D*<sub>*X*</sub> action in a way that emulates the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113), and with the natural right action of *f*<sup>−1</sup>(*D*<sub>*Y*</sub>). The pullback is defined as

*f*<sup>∗</sup>(*M*) := *D*<sub>*X*→*Y*</sub> ⊗<sub>*f*<sup>−1</sup>(*D*<sub>*Y*</sub>)</sub> *f*<sup>−1</sup>(*M*).

Here *M* is a left *D*<sub>*Y*</sub>-module, while its pullback is a left module over *X*. This functor is [right exact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_exact_functor), its left [derived functor](wikipedia://en/433875) is denoted L*f*<sup>∗</sup>. Conversely, for a right *D*<sub>*X*</sub>-module *N*,

*f*<sub>∗</sub>(*N*) := *f*<sub>∗</sub>(*N* ⊗<sub>*D*<sub>*X*</sub></sub> *D*<sub>*X*→*Y*</sub>)

is a right *D*<sub>*Y*</sub>-module. Since this mixes the right exact tensor product with the left exact pushforward, it is common to set instead

*f*<sub>∗</sub>(*N*) := R*f*<sub>∗</sub>(*N* ⊗<sup>L</sup><sub>*D*<sub>*X*</sub></sub> *D*<sub>*X*→*Y*</sub>).

Because of this, much of the theory of *D*-modules is developed using the full power of [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410), in particular [derived categories](wikipedia://en/985897).

:::

## Holonomic modules {#Holonomic_modules}

### Holonomic modules over the Weyl algebra {#Holonomic_modules_over_the_Weyl_algebra}

It can be shown that the Weyl algebra is a (left and right) [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129). Moreover, it is [simple](wikipedia://en/443387), that is to say, its only two-sided [ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_%28algebra%29) are the [zero ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_ideal) and the whole ring. These properties make the study of *D*-modules manageable. Notably, standard notions from [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990) such as [Hilbert polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_polynomial), multiplicity and [length](wikipedia://en/816070) of modules carry over to *D*-modules. More precisely, *D*<sub>*X*</sub> is equipped with the *Bernstein filtration*, that is, the [filtration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filtration_%28algebra%29) such that *F*<sup>*p*</sup>*A*<sub>*n*</sub>(*K*) consists of *K*-linear combinations of differential operators *x*<sup>*α*</sup>∂<sup>*β*</sup> with |*α*| + |*β*| ≤ *p* (using [multiindex notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiindex_notation)). The associated [graded ring](wikipedia://en/103094) is seen to be isomorphic to the polynomial ring in 2*n* indeterminates. In particular it is commutative.

[Finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) *D*-modules *M* are endowed with so-called "good" filtrations *F*<sup>∗</sup>*M*, which are ones compatible with *F*<sup>∗</sup>*A*<sub>*n*</sub>(*K*), essentially parallel to the situation of the [Artin–Rees lemma](wikipedia://en/9553854). The Hilbert polynomial is defined to be the [numerical polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_polynomial) that agrees with the function

*n* ↦ dim<sub>*K*</sub> *F*<sup>*n*</sup>*M*

for large *n*. The dimension *d*(*M*) of an *A*<sub>*n*</sub>(*K*)-module *M* is defined to be the degree of the Hilbert polynomial. It is bounded by the *Bernstein inequality*

*n* ≤ *d*(*M*) ≤ 2*n*.

A module whose dimension attains the least possible value, *n*, is called *holonomic*.

The *A*<sub>1</sub>(*K*)-module *M* = *A*<sub>1</sub>(*K*)/*A*<sub>1</sub>(*K*)*P* (see above) is holonomic for any nonzero differential operator *P*, but a similar claim for higher-dimensional Weyl algebras does not hold.

::: {#o11 .definition cue=heading name="General definition"}

### General definition {#General_definition}

As mentioned above, modules over the Weyl algebra correspond to *D*-modules on affine space. The Bernstein filtration not being available on *D*<sub>*X*</sub> for general varieties *X*, the definition is generalized to arbitrary affine smooth varieties *X* by means of *order filtration* on *D*<sub>*X*</sub>, defined by the [order of differential operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_differential_operator). The associated graded ring gr *D*<sub>*X*</sub> is given by regular functions on the [cotangent bundle](wikipedia://en/221600) T<sup>∗</sup>*X*.

The *[characteristic variety](wikipedia://en/40030306)* is defined to be the subvariety of the [cotangent bundle](wikipedia://en/221600) cut out by the [radical](wikipedia://en/252009) of the [annihilator](wikipedia://en/328684) of gr *M*, where again *M* is equipped with a suitable filtration (with respect to the order filtration on *D*<sub>*X*</sub>). As usual, the affine construction then glues to arbitrary varieties.

The Bernstein inequality continues to hold for any (smooth) variety *X*. While the upper bound is an immediate consequence of the above interpretation of gr *D*<sub>*X*</sub> in terms of the cotangent bundle, the lower bound is more subtle.

:::

### Properties and characterizations {#Properties_and_characterizations}

Holonomic modules have a tendency to behave like finite-dimensional vector spaces. For example, their length is finite. Also, *M* is holonomic if and only if all cohomology groups of the complex L*i*<sup>∗</sup>(*M*) are finite-dimensional *K*-vector spaces, where *i* is the [closed immersion](wikipedia://en/1623986) of any point of *X*.

For any *D*-module *M*, the *dual module* is defined by

$$\mathrm D(M) := \mathcal R \operatorname{Hom} (M, D_X) \otimes \Omega^{-1}_X [\dim X].$$

Holonomic modules can also be characterized by a [homological](wikipedia://en/139410) condition: *M* is holonomic if and only if D(*M*) is concentrated (seen as an object in the derived category of *D*-modules) in degree 0. This fact is a first glimpse of [Verdier duality](wikipedia://en/7190735) and the [Riemann–Hilbert correspondence](wikipedia://en/10478956). It is proven by extending the homological study of [regular rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_ring) (especially what is related to [global homological dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_homological_dimension)) to the filtered ring *D*<sub>*X*</sub>.

Another characterization of holonomic modules is via [symplectic geometry](wikipedia://en/294298). The characteristic variety Ch(*M*) of any *D*-module *M* is, seen as a subvariety of the cotangent bundle T<sup>∗</sup>*X* of *X*, an [involutive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Involutive_system) variety. The module is holonomic if and only if Ch(*M*) is [Lagrangian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_submanifold).

## Applications {#Applications}

One of the early applications of holonomic *D*-modules was the [Bernstein–Sato polynomial](wikipedia://en/1636721).

::: {#o16 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Kazhdan–Lusztig conjecture"}

### Kazhdan–Lusztig conjecture {#Kazhdan–Lusztig_conjecture}

The [Kazhdan–Lusztig conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazhdan–Lusztig_conjecture) was proved using *D*-modules.

:::

### Riemann–Hilbert correspondence {#Riemann–Hilbert_correspondence}

The [Riemann–Hilbert correspondence](wikipedia://en/10478956) establishes a link between certain *D*-modules and constructible sheaves. As such, it provided a motivation for introducing [perverse sheaves](wikipedia://en/1714012).

### Geometric representation theory {#Geometric_representation_theory}

*D*-modules are also applied in geometric representation theory. A main result in this area is the [Beilinson–Bernstein localization](wikipedia://en/59129360). It relates *D*-modules on [flag varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_variety) *G*/*B* to representations of the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) $\mathfrak g$ of a [reductive group](wikipedia://en/1088386) *G*. *D*-modules are also crucial in the formulation of the [geometric Langlands program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_Langlands_program).

:::
