# Semiconductor luminescence equations

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Semiconductor luminescence equations"}

The **semiconductor luminescence equations** (**SLEs**)[^o2][^o3] describe [luminescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminescence) of [semiconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor) resulting from spontaneous [recombination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrier_generation_and_recombination) of electronic excitations, producing a [flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminous_flux) of [spontaneously emitted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_emission) light. This description established the first step toward [semiconductor quantum optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor_quantum_optics) because the SLEs simultaneously includes the quantized [light–matter interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light–matter_interaction) and the [Coulomb-interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb's_law) coupling among electronic excitations within a semiconductor. The SLEs are one of the most accurate methods to describe light emission in semiconductors and they are suited for a systematic modeling of semiconductor emission ranging from [excitonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exciton) luminescence to [lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser).

:::

Due to randomness of the [vacuum-field fluctuations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_fluctuation), semiconductor luminescence is [incoherent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incoherent_superposition) whereas the extensions of the SLEs include[^o3] the possibility to study [resonance fluorescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resonance_fluorescence) resulting from [optical pumping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_pumping) with [coherent](wikipedia://en/240011) [laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser) light. At this level, one is often interested to control and access higher-order [photon-correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon_Correlation_Spectroscopy) effects, distinct many-body states, as well as light–semiconductor [entanglement](wikipedia://en/25336). Such investigations are the basis of realizing and developing the field of [quantum-optical spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum-optical_spectroscopy) which is a branch of [quantum optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_optics).

## Starting point {#Starting_point}

The derivation of the SLEs starts from a system [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381) that fully includes many-body interactions, quantized light field, and quantized light–matter interaction. Like almost always in [many-body physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Many-body_theory), it is most convenient to apply the [second-quantization formalism](wikipedia://en/418156). For example, a light field corresponding to frequency $\omega$ is then described through [Boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson) [creation and annihilation operators](wikipedia://en/701991) $\hat{B}^\dagger_\omega$ and $\hat{B}_\omega$, respectively, where the "hat" over $B$ signifies the operator nature of the quantity. The operator-combination $\hat{B}^\dagger_\omega\,\hat{B}_\omega$ determines the [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon)-number operator.

When the photon coherences, here the [expectation value](wikipedia://en/11371560) $\langle \hat{B}_\omega \rangle$, vanish and the system becomes [quasistationary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-stationary_distribution), semiconductors emit [incoherent](wikipedia://en/240011) light spontaneously, commonly referred to as [luminescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminescence) (L). (This is the underlying principle behind [light-emitting diodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-emitting_diodes).) The corresponding luminescence [flux](wikipedia://en/43590) is proportional to the temporal change in photon number,[^o3]

$$\mathrm{L}(\omega) = \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \langle \hat{B}^\dagger_{\omega} \hat{B}_{\omega} \rangle = 2\,\mathrm{Re}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{k}} \mathcal{F}_{\omega}^\star\, \Pi_{\mathbf{k},\omega} \right]\,.$$

As a result, the luminescence becomes directly generated by a **photon-assisted electron–hole recombination**,

$$\Pi_{\mathbf{k},\omega} \equiv \Delta \langle \hat{B}^\dagger_\omega \hat{P}_{\mathbf{k}} \rangle$$

that describes a correlated emission of a photon $(\hat{B}^\dagger_\omega)$ when an electron with [wave vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_vector) $\mathbf{k}$ recombines with a [hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_hole), i.e., an electronic vacancy. Here, $\hat{P}_\mathbf{k}$ determines the corresponding electron–hole recombination operator defining also the microscopic polarization within semiconductor. Therefore, $\Pi_{\mathbf{k},\omega}$ can also be viewed as **photon-assisted polarization**.

Many electron–hole pairs contribute to the photon emission at frequency $\omega$; the explicit $\Delta$ notation within $\Pi_{\mathbf{k},\omega}$ denotes that the correlated part of the expectation value $\langle \hat{B}^\dagger_\omega P_\mathbf{k} \rangle$ is constructed using the [cluster-expansion approach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cluster-expansion_approach). The quantity $\mathcal{F}_{\omega}$ contains the [dipole-matrix element](wikipedia://en/30876071) for [interband transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interband_transition), light-mode's [mode function](wikipedia://en/478195), and [vacuum-field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_state) amplitude.

where the first contribution, $\tilde{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{k}}$, contains the [Coulomb-renormalized single-particle energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb-renormalized_single-particle_energy) that is determined by the [bandstructure](wikipedia://en/706247) of the [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid). The Coulomb renormalization are identical to those that appear in the [semiconductor Bloch equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor_Bloch_equations) (SBEs), showing that *all* photon-assisted polarizations are coupled with each other via the unscreened Coulomb-interaction $V_\mathbf{k}$. The three-particle correlations that appear are indicated symbolically via the $T[\Pi]$ contributions – they introduce [excitation-induced dephasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excitation-induced_dephasing), [screening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric-field_screening) of Coulomb interaction, and additional highly correlated contributions such as phonon-sideband emission. The explicit form of a spontaneous-emission source $\Omega^\mathrm{spont}_{\mathbf{k},\omega}$ and a stimulated contribution $\Omega_{\omega}^\mathrm{stim}$ are discussed below.

The excitation level of a semiconductor is characterized by electron and hole occupations, $f^e_\mathbf{k}$ and $f^h_\mathbf{k}$, respectively. They modify the $\Pi_{\mathbf{k},\omega}$ via the [Coulomb renormalizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb_renormalizations) and the [Pauli-blocking factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauli-blocking_factor), $\left( 1-f^e_{\mathbf{k}}-f^h_{\mathbf{k}}\right)$. These occupations are changed by spontaneous recombination of electrons and holes, yielding

$$\left. \frac{\partial}{\partial t} f^e_\mathbf{k} \right|_\mathrm{L} = \left. \frac{\partial}{\partial t} f^h_\mathbf{k} \right|_\mathrm{L} = -2\,\mathrm{Re} \left[ \sum_{\omega} \mathcal{F}^{\star}_\omega \, \Pi_{\mathbf{k},\omega} \right]\,.$$

In its full form, the occupation dynamics also contains Coulomb-correlation terms.[^o3] It is straight forward to verify that the photon-assisted recombination[^o8][^o9][^o10] destroys as many electron–hole pairs as it creates photons because due to the general conservation law $\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \sum_\omega \langle \hat{B}^\dagger_{\omega} \hat{B}_{\omega} \rangle = -\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \sum_\mathbf{k} f^e_\mathbf{k}$.

Besides the terms already described above, the photon-assisted polarization dynamics contains a **spontaneous-emission source**

$$\Omega^\mathrm{spont}_{\mathbf{k},\omega} = \mathrm{i} \mathcal{F}_{\omega} \Bigl(f^e_{\mathbf{k}} f^h_{\mathbf{k}} + \sum_{\mathbf{k'}} c_\mathrm{X}^{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k'}} \Bigr)\,.$$

Intuitively, $f^e_\mathbf{k} \, f^h_\mathbf{k}$ describes the probability to find electron and hole with same $\mathbf{k}$ when electrons and holes are uncorrelated, i.e., plasma. Such form is to be expected for a probability of two uncorrelated events to occur simultaneously at a desired $\mathbf{k}$ value. The possibility to have truly correlated electron–hole pairs is defined by a two-particle correlation $c_\mathrm{X}^{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k'}}$; the corresponding probability is directly proportional to the correlation. In practice, $c_\mathrm{X}^{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k'}}$ becomes large when electron–hole pairs are bound as [excitons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excitons) via their mutual Coulomb attraction. Nevertheless, both the presence of electron–hole plasma and excitons can equivalently induce the spontaneous-emission source.

As the semiconductor emits light spontaneously, the luminescence is further altered by a **[stimulated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stimulated_emission) contribution**

$$\Delta\Omega_{\omega}^\mathrm{stim} = \mathrm{i} \sum_{\omega'} \mathcal{F}_{\omega'} \,\Delta \langle \hat{B}^\dagger_{\omega} \hat{B}_{\omega'} \rangle$$

that is particularly important when describing spontaneous emission in semiconductor microcavities and [lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasers) because then spontaneously emitted light can return to the emitter (i.e., the semiconductor), either stimulating or inhibiting further spontaneous-emission processes. This term is also responsible for the [Purcell effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purcell_effect).

To complete the SLEs, one must additionally solve the [quantum dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_dynamics) of exciton correlations

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t} c_\mathrm{X}^{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k'}} = & \left( \tilde{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{k}} - \tilde{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{k'}} \right)\, c_\mathrm{X}^{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k'}} + S_\mathrm{X}^{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k'}} \\ &+ \Bigl( 1-f^e_{\mathbf{k'}}-f^h_{\mathbf{k'}} \Bigr) \sum_{\mathbf{l}} V_{\mathbf{l}-\mathbf{k}'} \,c_\mathrm{X}^{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l}} - \Bigl( 1-f^e_{\mathbf{k}}-f^h_{\mathbf{k}} \Bigr) \sum_{\mathbf{l}} V_{\mathbf{l}-\mathbf{k}'} \,c_\mathrm{X}^{\mathbf{l},\mathbf{k'}} \\ &+ D_\mathrm{X,\,rest}^{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k'}}+ T_\mathrm{X}^{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k'}}\,. \end{aligned}$$

The first line contains the Coulomb-renormalized [kinetic energy](wikipedia://en/17327) of electron–hole pairs and the second line defines a source that results from a [Boltzmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_scattering)-type in- and out-scattering of two electrons and two holes due to the Coulomb interaction. The second line contains the main Coulomb sums that correlate electron–hole pairs into [excitons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exciton) whenever the excitation conditions are suitable. The remaining two- and three-particle correlations are presented symbolically by $D_\mathrm{X,\,rest}^{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k'}}$ and $T_\mathrm{X}^{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k'}}$, respectively.[^o3][^o11]

## Interpretation and consequences {#Interpretation_and_consequences}

Microscopically, the luminescence processes are initiated whenever the semiconductor is excited because at least the electron and hole distributions, that enter the spontaneous-emission source, are nonvanishing. As a result, $\Omega^\mathrm{spont}_{\mathbf{k},\omega}$ is finite and it drives the [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon)-assisted processes for all those $\mathbf{k}$ values that correspond to the excited states. This means that $\Pi_{\omega,\mathbf{k}}$ is simultaneously generated for many $\mathbf{k}$ values. Since the Coulomb interaction couples $\Pi_{\omega,\mathbf{k}}$ with all $\mathbf{k}$ values, the characteristic transition energy follows from the exciton energy, not the bare kinetic energy of an electron–hole pair. More mathematically, the homogeneous part of the $\Pi_{\omega,\mathbf{k}}$ dynamics has [eigenenergies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenstates) that are defined by the [generalized Wannier equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Wannier_equation) not the free-carrier energies. For low electron–hole densities, the Wannier equation produces a set of bound eigenstates which define the [exciton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exciton) resonances.

*Image: Photoluminescence schema.png.* Buildup of photon-assisted polarization (Π correlation) that is initiated by the spontaneous-emission source. The buildup occurs equally for all momentum states. In a many-body system, a photon (wave arrow) is generated collectively through multiple coupled Π-transition correlations.

Therefore, $\Pi_{\omega,\mathbf{k}}$ shows a discrete set of exciton resonances regardless which many-body state initiated the emission through the spontaneous-emission source. These resonances are directly transferred to excitonic peaks in the luminescence itself. This yields an unexpected consequence; the excitonic resonance can equally well originate from an electron–hole plasma or the presence of excitons.[^o13] At first, this consequence of SLEs seems counterintuitive because in few-particle picture an unbound electron–hole pair cannot recombine and release energy corresponding to the exciton resonance because that energy is well below the energy an unbound electron–hole pair possesses.

However, the excitonic plasma luminescence is a genuine many-body effect where plasma emits *collectively* to the exciton resonance. Namely, when a high number of electronic states participate in the emission of a single photon, one can always distribute the energy of initial many-body state between the one photon at exciton energy and remaining many-body state (with one electron–hole pair removed) without violating the energy conservation. The Coulomb interaction mediates such energy rearrangements very efficiently. A thorough analysis of energy and many-body state rearrangement is given in Ref.[^o3]

In general, excitonic plasma luminescence explains many nonequilibrium emission properties observed in present-day semiconductor luminescence experiments. In fact, the dominance of excitonic plasma luminescence has been measured in both [quantum-well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_well)[^o14] and [quantum-dot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_dot) systems.[^o15] Only when excitons are present abundantly, the role of excitonic plasma luminescence can be ignored.

## Connections and generalizations {#Connections_and_generalizations}

Structurally, the SLEs resemble the [semiconductor Bloch equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor_Bloch_equations) (SBEs) if the $\Pi_{\omega,\mathbf{k}}$ are compared with the microscopic polarization within the SBEs. As the main difference, $\Pi_{\omega,\mathbf{k}}$ also has a photon index $\omega$, its dynamics is driven spontaneously, and it is directly coupled to three-particle correlations. Technically, the SLEs are more difficult to solve numerically than the SBEs due to the additional $\omega$ degree of freedom. However, the SLEs often are the only (at low carrier densities) or more convenient (lasing regime) to compute luminescence accurately. Furthermore, the SLEs not only yield a full predictability without the need for [phenomenological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenological_model) approximations but they also can be used as a systematic starting point for more general investigations such as laser design[^o17][^o18] and disorder studies.[^o19]

The presented SLEs discussion does not specify the dimensionality or the band structure of the system studied. As one analyses a specified system, one often has to explicitly include the electronic bands involved, the dimensionality of wave vectors, photon, and [exciton center-of-mass momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exciton_center-of-mass_momentum). Many explicit examples are given in Refs.[^o11][^o20] for [quantum-well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_well) and [quantum-wire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_wire) systems, and in Refs.[^o9][^o21][^o22] for [quantum-dot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_dot) systems.

Semiconductors also can show several resonances well below the fundamental exciton resonance when phonon-assisted electron–hole recombination takes place. These processes are describable by three-particle correlations (or higher) where photon, electron–hole pair, and a lattice vibration, i.e., a phonon, become correlated. The dynamics of phonon-assisted correlations are similar to the phonon-free SLEs. Like for the excitonic luminescence, also excitonic phonon sidebands can equally well be initiated by either electron–hole plasma or excitons.[^o23]

The SLEs can also be used as a systematic starting point for [semiconductor quantum optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor_quantum_optics).[^o3][^o24][^o25] As a first step, one also includes [two-photon absorption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-photon_absorption) correlations, $\Delta \langle \hat{B}_\omega \hat{B}_{\omega'} \rangle$, and then continues toward higher-order photon-correlation effects. This approach can be applied to analyze the [resonance fluorescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resonance_fluorescence) effects and to realize and understand the [quantum-optical spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum-optical_spectroscopy).
