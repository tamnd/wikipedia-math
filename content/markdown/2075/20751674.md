# Clique complex

*Image: VR complex.svg.* The clique complex of a graph. Cliques of size one are shown as small red disks; cliques of size two are shown as black line segments; cliques of size three are shown as light blue triangles; and cliques of size four are shown as dark blue tetrahedra.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Clique complex"}

**Clique complexes**, **independence complexes, flag complexes**, **Whitney complexes** and **conformal hypergraphs** are closely related [mathematical objects](wikipedia://en/19453961) in [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401) and [geometric topology](wikipedia://en/621732) that each describe the [cliques](wikipedia://en/524466) (complete subgraphs) of an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph).

:::

## Clique complex {#Clique_complex}

The **clique complex** $X(G)$ of an undirected graph $G$ is an [abstract simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/723105) (that is, a family of finite sets closed under the operation of taking subsets), formed by the [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) of [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) in the cliques of $G$. Any [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of a clique is itself a clique, so this family of sets meets the requirement of an abstract simplicial complex that every subset of a set in the family should also be in the family.

The clique complex can also be viewed as a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) in which each clique of $k$ vertices is represented by a [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) of dimension $k - 1$. The [1-skeleton](wikipedia://en/2977910) of $X(G)$ (also known as the *underlying graph* of the complex) is an undirected graph with a vertex for every 1-element set in the family and an edge for every 2-element set in the family; it is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to $G$.[^o3]

### Negative example {#Negative_example}

Every clique complex is an abstract simplicial complex, but the opposite is not true. For example, consider the abstract simplicial complex over $\{1,2,3,4\}$ with maximal sets $\{1,2,3\},$ $\{2,3,4\},$ $\{4,1\}.$ If it were the $X(G)$ of some graph $G$, then $G$ had to have the edges $\{1,2\},$ $\{1,3\},$ $\{2,3\},$ $\{2,4\},$ $\{3,4\},$ $\{4,1\},$ so $X(G)$ should also contain the clique $\{1,2,3,4\}.$

## Independence complex {#Independence_complex}

The **[independence complex](wikipedia://en/64659151)** $I(G)$ of an undirected graph $G$ is an [abstract simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/723105) formed by the [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) of [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) in the independent sets of $G$. The clique complex of $G$ is equivalent to the [independence complex](wikipedia://en/64659151) of the [complement graph](wikipedia://en/1175649) of $G$.

## Flag complex {#Flag_complex}

A **flag complex** is an [abstract simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/723105) with an additional property called "2-determined": for every subset *S* of vertices, if every pair of vertices in *S* is in the complex, then *S* itself is in the complex too.

Every clique complex is a flag complex: if every pair of vertices in *S* is a clique of size 2, then there is an edge between them, so *S* is a clique.

Every flag complex is a clique complex: given a flag complex, define a graph *G* on the set of all vertices, where two vertices u,v are adjacent in *G* iff {u,v} is in the complex (this graph is called the *[1-skeleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1-skeleton)* of the complex). By definition of a flag complex, every set of vertices that are pairwise-connected, is in the complex. Therefore, the flag complex is equal to the clique complex on *G*.

Thus, flag complexes and clique complexes are essentially the same thing. However, in many cases it is convenient to define a flag complex directly from some data other than a graph, rather than indirectly as the clique complex of a graph derived from that data.[^o7]

[Mikhail Gromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Leonidovich_Gromov) defined the *no-Δ condition* to be the condition of being a flag complex.

## Whitney complex {#Whitney_complex}

Clique complexes are also known as **Whitney complexes**, after [Hassler Whitney](wikipedia://en/391794). A [Whitney triangulation](wikipedia://en/1440976) or clean triangulation of a two-dimensional [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) is an [embedding](wikipedia://en/8149170) of a graph $G$ onto the manifold in such a way that every face is a triangle and every triangle is a face. If a graph $G$ has a Whitney triangulation, it must form a cell complex that is isomorphic to the Whitney complex of $G$. In this case, the complex (viewed as a topological space) is [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) to the underlying manifold. A graph $G$ has a 2-manifold clique complex, and can be embedded as a Whitney triangulation, if and only if $G$ is [locally cyclic](wikipedia://en/7309251); this means that, for every vertex $v$ in the graph, the [induced subgraph](wikipedia://en/1655188) formed by the neighbors of $v$ forms a single cycle.[^o9]

## Conformal hypergraph {#Conformal_hypergraph}

The [primal graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primal_graph_%28hypergraphs%29) *G*(*H*) of a [hypergraph](wikipedia://en/232535) is the graph on the same vertex set that has as its edges the pairs of vertices appearing together in the same [hyperedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperedge). A hypergraph is said to be **conformal** if every maximal clique of its primal graph is a hyperedge, or equivalently, if every clique of its primal graph is contained in some hyperedge.[^o11] If the hypergraph is required to be downward-closed (so it contains all hyperedges that are contained in some hyperedge) then the hypergraph is conformal precisely when it is a flag complex. This relates the language of hypergraphs to the language of simplicial complexes.

## Examples and applications {#Examples_and_applications}

The [barycentric subdivision](wikipedia://en/583651) of any [cell complex](wikipedia://en/353022) *C* is a flag complex having one vertex per cell of *C*. A collection of vertices of the barycentric subdivision form a simplex if and only if the corresponding collection of cells of *C* form a [flag](wikipedia://en/9352497) (a chain in the inclusion ordering of the cells).[^o7] In particular, the barycentric subdivision of a cell complex on a 2-manifold gives rise to a Whitney triangulation of the manifold.

The [order complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_complex) of a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) consists of the chains ([totally ordered](wikipedia://en/30330) subsets) of the partial order. If every pair of some subset is itself ordered, then the whole subset is a chain, so the order complex satisfies the no-Δ condition. It may be interpreted as the clique complex of the [comparability graph](wikipedia://en/6978672) of the partial order.[^o7]

The [matching complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matching_complex) of a graph consists of the sets of edges no two of which share an endpoint; again, this family of sets satisfies the no-Δ condition. It may be viewed as the clique complex of the [complement graph](wikipedia://en/1175649) of the [line graph](wikipedia://en/675231) of the given graph. When the matching complex is referred to without any particular graph as context, it means the matching complex of a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816). The matching complex of a [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) *K*<sub>*m*,*n*</sub> is known as a [chessboard complex](wikipedia://en/73384654). It is the clique graph of the complement graph of a [rook's graph](wikipedia://en/7324959),[^o13] and each of its simplices represents a placement of rooks on an *m* × *n* chess board such that no two of the rooks attack each other. When *m* = *n* ± 1, the chessboard complex forms a [pseudo-manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-manifold).

The [Vietoris–Rips complex](wikipedia://en/13056778) of a set of points in a metric space is a special case of a clique complex, formed from the [unit disk graph](wikipedia://en/3686677) of the points; however, every clique complex *X(G)* may be interpreted as the Vietoris–Rips complex of the [shortest path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortest_path) metric on the underlying graph *G*.

(Hodkinson Otto 2003) describe an application of conformal hypergraphs in the logics of relational structures. In that context, the [Gaifman graph](wikipedia://en/19718112) of a relational structure is the same as the underlying graph of the hypergraph representing the structure, and a structure is [guarded](wikipedia://en/19349240) if it corresponds to a conformal hypergraph.

Gromov showed that a cubical complex (that is, a family of [hypercubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercubes) intersecting face-to-face) forms a [CAT(0) space](wikipedia://en/12941912) if and only if the complex is simply connected and the link of every vertex forms a flag complex. A cubical complex meeting these conditions is sometimes called a [cubing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubing_%28topology%29) or a *space with walls*.[^o3][^o15]

## Homology groups {#Homology_groups}

Meshulam[^o17] proves the following theorem on the homology of the clique complex. Given integers $l\geq 1, t\geq 0$, suppose a graph *G* satisfies a property called $P(l,t)$, which means that:

- Every set of $l$ vertices in *G* has a common neighbor;
- There exists a set *A* of vertices, that contains a common neighbor to every set of $l$ vertices, and in addition, the induced graph *G*\[*A*\] does not contain, as an induced subgraph, a copy of the [1-skeleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1-skeleton) of the *t*-dimensional [octahedral sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octahedral_sphere).

Then the *j*-th reduced homology of the clique complex X(*G*) is trivial for any *j* between 0 and $\max(l-t, \lfloor {l}/{2}\rfloor)-1$.
