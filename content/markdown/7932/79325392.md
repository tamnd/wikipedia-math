# Assembly (realizability)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Assembly"}

In [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392) and [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), **assemblies** can be informally described as sets equipped with representations for elements. [Realizability toposes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realizability_topos) are completions of assembly categories.

:::

## Motivation {#Motivation}

Algorithms do not directly manipulate objects such as graphs or lists, but representations of these objects. There may be several types of representations available. For example, graphs (viewed [modulo isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulo_isomorphism), i.e., two isomorphic graphs are the same) may be represented with [adjacency lists](wikipedia://en/392431) or [adjacency matrices](wikipedia://en/244463). Furthermore, a given object may have several equivalent representations, e.g., a graph could be presented with any order of the vertices, and graph algorithms should give equivalent results whatever the order of the vertices in the input is. The basic idea behind assemblies is to consider sets (such as the set of finite graphs, modulo isomorphism) where each element has a number of *realizers*, which are understood as its algorithmic representations. A morphism between assemblies is a function between their underlying sets which can be “algorithmically realized”, in the sense that given a representation of an element of the domain, one can compute a representation of its image. For example, the function which maps a graph to the [multiset](wikipedia://en/305303) of its [connected components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28graph_theory%29) is realized: there is an algorithm which, given a representation of a graph, computes a list (which is a reasonable representation for a multiset) of representations of its connected components.p. 44–45

In the context of realizability, it is useful not to restrict the definition to algorithms in the [Church–Turing sense](wikipedia://en/6854). Instead, assemblies are defined over a specific [partial combinatory algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_combinatory_algebra), which abstracts the [model of computation](wikipedia://en/1773278). It is a set equipped with an operation thought of as program execution. The archetypal example, modelling Church–Turing computability, is the [first Kleene algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Kleene_algebra), which is $\mathbb{N}$ where $p\,x$ is the output (if defined) of the [Turing machine](wikipedia://en/30403) represented by the integer $p$ when run on the input $x$ (thus integers are used to represent all data as well as all programs).

A possibly surprising aspect of the definition is that elements may share realizers. While unusual algorithmically, this is important to the logical side of realizability.p. 46

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $A$ be a fixed [partial combinatory algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_combinatory_algebra).

An **assembly** $S$ (over $A$) is simply a set $|S|$ equipped with a relation $\Vdash_S$, read “realizes”, between $A$ and $|S|$, such that every element of $|S|$ has a realizer, i.e., for all $s \in |S|$, there exists $a \in A$ with $a \Vdash_S s$.p. 45p. 24

Assemblies are equipped with a [categorical](wikipedia://en/53759) structure as follows. A morphism $f : S \to T$ between assemblies $S$ and $T$ is a function $f : |S| \to |T|$ between their underlying sets, for which there exists an element $p$ such that for all element $s \in |S|$ realized by $a \in A$, the application $p\,a$ is defined in the pca $A$ and the element $f(s) \in |T|$ is realized by $p\,a$. The category of assemblies over $A$ is denoted by $\operatorname{Asm}(A)$.p. 45p. 24

An assembly $S$ is said to be **modest** when elements do not share realizers, i.e., for all $s, s' \in S, a \in A$, if $a \Vdash_S s$ and $a \Vdash_S s'$ then $s = s'$. The category of modest assemblies over $A$ is denoted $\operatorname{Mod}(A)$ and is a [full subcategory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_subcategory) of $\operatorname{Asm}(A)$.p. 46

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

The *unit assembly* $1$ is carried by a singleton $\{\star\}$, where $\star$ is realized all elements of the pca (alternatively, giving $\star$ any non-zero number of realizers gives an isomorphic object).p. 46

The *empty assembly* $0$ is carried by the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566).p. 46

The *assembly of natural numbers* is carried by $\mathbb{N}$ where each natural number $n$ is realized only by its associated Curry numeral $\bar{n}$.p. 47

At the extreme opposite of modest assemblies, the *constant assembly* $\nabla X$ is carried by the set $X$, where all elements of $X$ are realized by all elements of the pca.p. 47

The assembly of *classical truth values* is the constant assembly on a two-element set, i.e., $\nabla \{0, 1\}$. The assembly of *decidable truth values* or *booleans*, is carried by $\{0, 1\}$, where 0 is realized by $\bar{0}$ and 1 is realized by the $\bar{1}$.p. 48

An intermediate between decidable and classical truth values is the assembly of *semidecidable truth values*. It is carried by $\{0, 1\}$, and all realizers are programs which compute infinite sequences of bits, i.e., elements $p$ from the pca such that for all $n$ the application $p\,\bar{n}$ is defined and has value $\bar{0}$ or $\bar{1}$. The realizers of 1 are those which compute a sequence that contains the bit 1, and the realizers of 0 are the others.p. 48

Given two assemblies $S$ and $T$, we may form their *binary product* $S \times T$ as follows: its carrier is the Cartesian product of the carriers $|S| \times T$, and a pair $(a, b) \in |S| \times |T|$ is realized by elements $p$ such that $\mathsf{fst}\,p$ realizes $a$ and $\mathsf{snd}\,p$ realizes $b$. Here, $\mathsf{fst}$ and $\mathsf{snd}$ are the projection functions in some encoding of pairs inside the pca, e.g., $\mathsf{pair} := \langle x y z \rangle z x y, \mathsf{fst} := \langle p \rangle p (\langle x y \rangle x), \mathsf{snd} := \langle p \rangle p (\langle x y \rangle y)$.)p. 63

We may also form the *binary coproduct* $S + T$, whose carrier is the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) $|S| \sqcup |T|$ and where a realizer of $s \in S$ is $\mathsf{inl}\,a$ for $a$ a realizer of $s$, while a realizer of $t$ is $\mathsf{inr}\,b$ for $b$ a realizer of $t$. Here, $\mathsf{inl}$ and $\mathsf{inr}$ are the constructors for some encoding of disjoint unions, e.g., $\mathsf{inl} := \langle x \rangle \mathsf{pair}\,\bar{0}\,x, \mathsf{inr} := \langle x \rangle \mathsf{pair}\,\bar{1}\,x$.p. 68

:::

## Categorical structure {#Categorical_structure}

The category $\operatorname{Asm}(A)$ has the following properties:

- It is [finitely complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_complete_category). The unit assembly is a [terminal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_object). Binary products correspond to category-theoretic [binary products](wikipedia://en/237868). The [equalizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equalizer_%28category_theory%29) of two morphisms $f, g : S \to T$ may be formed exactly as in the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829), as the canonical injection to $|S|$ from the subset $\{s \in |S| \ | f(s) = g(s)\}$. Each element of the subset is equipped with the same realizers as in $S$, and the canonical injection is realized by $i := \langle x \rangle x$.p. 64p. 25
- It is [finitely cocomplete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_cocomplete_category). The empty assembly is initial and binary coproducts correspond to category-theoretic [binary coproducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coproduct_%28category_theory%29). Similarly to equalizers, the coequalizer of two morphisms $f, g : S \to T$ may be formed exactly as in the category of sets, as the canonical surjection from $|S|$ to the quotient $S/\equiv$, where $\equiv$ is the equivalence relation generated by the relations $f(s) \equiv g(s), s \in S$. The realizers of an equivalence class are all the realizers of its elements in $S$. Again, the canonical surjection is realized by $i := \langle x \rangle x$.p. 70p. 25
- It is [regular](wikipedia://en/9790950)p. 74p. 25 and [cartesian closed](wikipedia://en/237037).p. 75p. 25
- A morphism is [mono](wikipedia://en/59538) if and only if its underlying function is injective, and [epi](wikipedia://en/59539) if and only if its underlying function is surjective.p. 70p. 25
