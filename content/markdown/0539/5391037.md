# Constant of motion

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Constant of motion"}

In [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics), a **constant of motion** is a [physical quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_quantity) [conserved](wikipedia://en/6956) throughout the motion, imposing in effect a constraint on the motion. However, it is a [*mathematical* constraint](wikipedia://en/7418540), the natural consequence of the [equations of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equation_of_motion), rather than a [*physical* constraint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constraint_%28mechanics%29) (which would require extra [constraint forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constraint_force)). Common examples include [energy](wikipedia://en/67088), [linear momentum](wikipedia://en/20431#Conservation_of_linear_momentum), [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839#Conservation_of_angular_momentum) and the [Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector](wikipedia://en/719460) (for [inverse-square force laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse-square_law)).

:::

::: {#o3 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Constants of motion are useful because they allow properties of the motion to be derived without solving the [equations of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equation_of_motion). In fortunate cases, even the [trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectory) of the motion can be derived as the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of [isosurfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isosurface) corresponding to the constants of motion. For example, [Poinsot's construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poinsot's_ellipsoid) shows that the torque-free [rotation](wikipedia://en/39789) of a [rigid body](wikipedia://en/455769) is the intersection of a sphere (conservation of total angular momentum) and an ellipsoid (conservation of energy), a trajectory that might be otherwise hard to derive and visualize. Therefore, the identification of constants of motion is an important objective in [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics).

:::

## Methods for identifying constants of motion {#Methods_for_identifying_constants_of_motion}

There are several methods for identifying constants of motion.

- The simplest but least systematic approach is the intuitive ("psychic") derivation, in which a quantity is hypothesized to be constant (perhaps because of [experimental data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_data)) and later shown mathematically to be conserved throughout the motion.
- The [Hamilton–Jacobi equations](wikipedia://en/897539) provide a commonly used and straightforward method for identifying constants of motion, particularly when the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319) adopts recognizable functional forms in [orthogonal coordinates](wikipedia://en/5006088).
- Another approach is to recognize that a [conserved quantity](wikipedia://en/6956) corresponds to a [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) of the [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/23371726). [Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159) provides a systematic way of deriving such quantities from the symmetry. For example, [conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088) results from the invariance of the [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/23371726) under shifts in the origin of [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time), [conservation of linear momentum](wikipedia://en/20431#Conservation_of_linear_momentum) results from the invariance of the [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/23371726) under shifts in the origin of [space](wikipedia://en/27667) (*translational symmetry*) and [conservation of angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839#Conservation_of_angular_momentum) results from the invariance of the [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/23371726) under [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789). The converse is also true; every symmetry of the [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/23371726) corresponds to a constant of motion, often called a *conserved charge* or *current*.
- A quantity $A$ is a constant of the motion if its total time derivative is zero $$0 = \frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{\partial A}{\partial t} + \{A, H\},$$ which occurs when $A$'s [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340) with the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319) equals minus its partial derivative with respect to time[^o5] $$\frac{\partial A}{\partial t} = -\{A, H\}.$$

Another useful result is **Poisson's theorem**, which states that if two quantities $A$ and $B$ are constants of motion, so is their Poisson bracket $\{A, B\}$.

A system with *n* degrees of freedom, and *n* constants of motion, such that the Poisson bracket of any pair of constants of motion vanishes, is known as a completely [integrable system](wikipedia://en/2573213). Such a collection of constants of motion are said to be in [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) with each other. For a [closed system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_system) ([Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/23371726) not explicitly dependent on time), the energy of the system is a constant of motion (a [conserved quantity](wikipedia://en/23371726#Conservation)).

## In quantum mechanics {#In_quantum_mechanics}

An observable quantity *Q* will be a constant of motion if it [commutes](wikipedia://en/7193) with the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319), *H*, and it does not itself depend explicitly on time. This is because

$$\frac{d}{dt} \langle \psi | Q | \psi \rangle = -\frac{1}{i \hbar} \left\langle \psi\right| \left[ H,Q \right] \left|\psi \right\rangle + \left\langle \psi \right| \frac{dQ}{dt} \left| \psi \right\rangle \,$$

where

$$[H,Q] = HQ - QH \,$$

is the commutator relation.

### Derivation {#Derivation}

Say there is some observable quantity $Q$ which depends on position, momentum and time,

$$Q = Q(x,p,t)$$

And also, that there is a [wave function](wikipedia://en/145343) which obeys [Schrödinger's equation](wikipedia://en/59874)

$$i\hbar \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = H \psi .$$

Taking the time derivative of the expectation value of $Q$ requires use of the [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988), and results in

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt} \left\langle Q \right\rangle &= \frac{d}{dt} \left\langle \psi \right| Q \left| \psi \right\rangle \\[1ex] &= \left(\frac{d}{dt}\left\langle \psi \right|\right) Q \left| \psi \right\rangle + \left\langle \psi \right| \frac{dQ}{dt} \left| \psi \right\rangle + \left\langle \psi \right| Q \left(\frac{d}{dt}\left| \psi \right\rangle\right) \\[1ex] &= -\frac{1}{i\hbar} \left\langle H \psi \right| Q \left| \psi \right\rangle + \left\langle \psi \right| \frac{dQ}{dt} \left| \psi \right\rangle + \frac{1}{i\hbar} \left\langle \psi \right| Q \left| H \psi \right\rangle \\[1ex] &= -\frac{1}{i\hbar} \left\langle \psi \right| HQ \left| \psi \right\rangle + \left\langle \psi \right| \frac{dQ}{dt} \left| \psi \right\rangle + \frac{1}{i\hbar} \left\langle \psi \right| QH \left| \psi \right\rangle \\[1ex] &= -\frac{1}{i\hbar} \left\langle \psi\right| \left[H,Q\right] \left|\psi \right\rangle + \left\langle \psi \right| \frac{dQ}{dt} \left| \psi \right\rangle \end{aligned}$$

So finally,

| $\frac{d}{dt} \langle \psi \| Q \| \psi \rangle = \frac{-1}{i \hbar} \langle \psi\| \left[ H,Q \right]\|\psi \rangle + \langle \psi \| \frac{dQ}{dt} \| \psi \rangle \,$ |
|---|

### Comment {#Comment}

For an arbitrary state of a Quantum Mechanical system, if $H$ and $Q$ commute, i.e. if

$$\left[ H,Q \right] = 0$$

and $Q$ is not explicitly dependent on time, then

$$\frac{d}{dt} \langle Q \rangle = 0$$

But if $\psi$ is an eigenfunction of the Hamiltonian, then even if

$$\left[H,Q\right] \neq 0$$

it is still the case that

$$\frac{d}{dt}\langle Q \rangle = 0$$

provided $Q$ is independent of time.

### Derivation {#Derivation}

$$\frac{d}{dt} \langle Q \rangle = -\frac{1}{i\hbar} \langle \psi | \left[ H,Q \right] | \psi\rangle = -\frac{1}{i\hbar} \langle \psi | \left(HQ - QH\right) | \psi \rangle$$

Since

$$H|\psi\rangle = E |\psi \rangle \,$$

then

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt} \langle Q \rangle &= -\frac{1}{i\hbar} \left( E \langle \psi | Q | \psi \rangle - E \langle \psi | Q | \psi \rangle \right) \\[1ex] &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

This is the reason why eigenstates of the Hamiltonian are also called stationary states.

## Relevance for quantum chaos {#Relevance_for_quantum_chaos}

In general, an [integrable system](wikipedia://en/2573213) has constants of motion other than the energy. By contrast, [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) is the only constant of motion in a [non-integrable system](wikipedia://en/9087); such systems are termed chaotic. In general, a classical mechanical system can be [quantized](wikipedia://en/25202) only if it is integrable; as of , there is no known consistent method for quantizing chaotic dynamical systems.

## Integral of motion {#Integral_of_motion}

A constant of motion may be defined in a given force field as any function of [phase-space](wikipedia://en/191101) coordinates (position and velocity, or position and momentum) and time that is constant throughout a trajectory. A subset of the constants of motion are the **integrals of motion**, or **first integrals**, defined as any functions of only the phase-space coordinates that are constant along an orbit. Every integral of motion is a constant of motion, but the converse is not true because a constant of motion may depend on time.[^o13] Examples of integrals of motion are the angular momentum vector, $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{x} \times \mathbf{v}$, or a Hamiltonian without time dependence, such as $H(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}) = \frac{1}{2} v^2 + \Phi(\mathbf{x})$. An example of a function that is a constant of motion but not an integral of motion would be the function $C(x,v,t) = x - vt$ for an object moving at a constant speed in one dimension.

:::

## Dirac observables {#Dirac_observables}

In order to extract physical information from [gauge theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_theories), one either constructs [gauge invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_invariant) observables or fixes a gauge. In a canonical language, this usually means either constructing functions which Poisson-commute on the constraint surface with the gauge generating [first class constraints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_class_constraints) or to fix the flow of the latter by singling out points within each [gauge orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_orbit). Such gauge invariant observables are thus the \`constants of motion' of the gauge generators and referred to as Dirac observables.
