# Special right triangle

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Special right triangle"}

45-45-90 triangle

*Image: Euler diagram of triangle types.svg.* Position of some special triangles in an [Euler diagram](wikipedia://en/2427526) of types of triangles, using the definition that [isosceles triangles](wikipedia://en/315428) have at least two equal sides, i.e. that [equilateral triangles](wikipedia://en/173285) are isosceles

A **special right triangle** is a [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420) with some notable feature that makes calculations on the [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) easier, or for which simple formulas exist.

:::

The various relationships between the angles and sides of such triangles allow one to quickly calculate some useful quantities in [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) problems without resorting to more advanced methods.

## Angle-based {#Angle-based}

*Image: Special right triangles for trig.svg.* Special angle-based triangles inscribed in a unit circle are handy for visualizing and remembering [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367) of multiples of 30 and 45 degrees.

*Angle-based* special right triangles are those involving some special relationship between the triangle's three angle measures. The angles of these triangles are such that the larger (right) angle, which is 90 [degrees](wikipedia://en/1195294) or $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ [radians](wikipedia://en/26003), is equal to the sum of the other two angles.

The side lengths of these triangles can be deduced based on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071), or with the use of other [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) methods; and these approaches may be extended to produce the values of [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367) for some common angles, shown in the table below.

The 45°–45°–90° triangle, the 30°–60°–90° triangle, and the [equilateral](wikipedia://en/173285)/equiangular (60°–60°–60°) triangle are the three [Möbius triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Möbius_triangle) in the plane, meaning that they [tessellate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessellate) the plane via [reflections](wikipedia://en/295829) in their sides; see [Triangle group](wikipedia://en/2069342).

### 45°–45°–90° triangle {#45°–45°–90°_triangle}

*Image: Squadra 45.jpg.* [Set square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_square) shaped as 45°–45°–90° triangle

*Image: 45-45-triangle.svg.* The side lengths of a 45°–45°–90° triangle

*Image: 45° 45° 90° Special Right Triangle.svg.* 45°–45°–90° [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420) of [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) length 1

In [plane geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_geometry), dividing a [square](wikipedia://en/659939) along its diagonal results in two **isosceles right triangles**, each with one right angle (90°, $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ radians) and two other congruent angles each measuring half of a right angle (45°, or $\tfrac{\pi}{4}$ radians). The sides in this triangle are in the ratio 1 : 1 : $\sqrt{2}$, which follows immediately from the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034).

Of all right triangles, such 45°–45°–90° degree triangles have the smallest ratio of the [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) to the sum of the legs, namely $\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$.[^o6] and the greatest ratio of the [altitude](wikipedia://en/161241) from the hypotenuse to the sum of the legs, namely $\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{4}$.[^o6]

Triangles with these angles are the only possible right triangles that are also [isosceles triangles](wikipedia://en/315428) in [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417). However, in [spherical geometry](wikipedia://en/174026) and [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), there are infinitely many different shapes of right isosceles triangles.

### 30°–60°–90° triangle {#30°–60°–90°_triangle}

*Image: Squadra 30 60.jpg.* Set square, shaped as 30°–60°–90° triangle

*Image: Equilateral triangle with height square root of 3.svg.* The side lengths of a 30°–60°–90° triangle

*Image: 30° 60° 90° Special Right Triangle.svg.* 30°–60°–90° [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420) of [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) length 1

Another type of special right triangle is the 30°-60°-90° triangle, which refers to any triangle with those three angle measures. Notably, these angles are in the ratio 1 : 2 : 3.

A useful property of such triangles is that their side lengths are in the ratio 1 : [$\sqrt{3}$](wikipedia://en/9126665) : 2. This property can be proven using [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261), or via the [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) proof below:

Draw an equilateral triangle *ABC* with side length 2, and with point *M* as the midpoint of segment *BC*. Draw an altitude line from *A* to *M*. Then *ABM* is a 30°–60°–90° triangle with hypotenuse of length 2, and base *BM* of length 1.

The fact that the remaining leg *AM* has length [$\sqrt{3}$](wikipedia://en/9126665) follows immediately from the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034).

The 30°–60°–90° triangle is the only right triangle whose angles are in an [arithmetic progression](wikipedia://en/168389). The proof of this fact is simple and follows on from the fact that if *α*, *α* + *δ*, *α* + 2*δ* are the angles in the progression then the sum of the angles 3*α* + 3*δ* = 180°. After dividing by 3, the angle *α* + *δ* must be 60°. The right angle is 90°, leaving the remaining angle to be 30°.

:::

## Side-based {#Side-based}

Right triangles whose sides are of [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) lengths, with the sides collectively known as [Pythagorean triples](wikipedia://en/24172), possess angles that cannot all be [rational numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_numbers) of [degrees](wikipedia://en/1195294).[^o10] (This follows from [Niven's theorem](wikipedia://en/33937103).) They are most useful in that they may be easily remembered and any [multiple](wikipedia://en/3912709) of the sides produces the same relationship. Using Euclid's formula for generating Pythagorean triples, the sides must be in the ratio

*m*${}^{2}$ − *n*${}^{2}$ : 2*mn* : *m*${}^{2}$ + *n*${}^{2}$

where *m* and *n* are any positive integers such that *m* > *n*.

The 3 : 4 : 5 triangles are the only right triangles with edges in [arithmetic progression](wikipedia://en/168389). Triangles based on Pythagorean triples are [Heronian](wikipedia://en/731959), meaning they have integer [area](wikipedia://en/1209) as well as integer sides.

The possible use of the 3 : 4 : 5 triangle in [Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt), with the supposed use of a knotted rope to lay out such a triangle, and the question whether Pythagoras' theorem was known at that time, have been much debated.[^o12] It was first conjectured by the historian [Moritz Cantor](wikipedia://en/307975) in 1882.[^o12] It is known that right angles were laid out accurately in Ancient Egypt; that their surveyors did use ropes for measurement;[^o12] that [Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch) recorded in *[Isis and Osiris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Iside_et_Osiride)* (around 100 AD) that the Egyptians admired the 3 : 4 : 5 triangle;[^o12] and that the [Berlin Papyrus 6619](wikipedia://en/4233960) from the [Middle Kingdom of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Kingdom_of_Egypt) (before 1700 BC) stated that "the area of a square of 100 is equal to that of two smaller squares. The side of one is $\tfrac{1}{2}$ + $\tfrac{1}{4}$ the side of the other."[^o13] The historian of mathematics Roger L. Cooke observes that "It is hard to imagine anyone being interested in such conditions without knowing the Pythagorean theorem."[^o12] Against this, Cooke notes that no Egyptian text before 300 BC actually mentions the use of the theorem to find the length of a triangle's sides, and that there are simpler ways to construct a right angle. Cooke concludes that Cantor's conjecture remains uncertain: he guesses that the Ancient Egyptians probably did know the Pythagorean theorem, but that "there is no evidence that they used it to construct right angles".[^o12]

### Almost-isosceles Pythagorean triples {#Almost-isosceles_Pythagorean_triples}

Isosceles right-angled triangles cannot have sides with integer values, because the ratio of the hypotenuse to either other side is $\sqrt{2}$ and [$\sqrt{2}$ cannot be expressed as a ratio of two integers](wikipedia://en/341682#Proofs_of_irrationality). However, infinitely many *almost-isosceles* right triangles do exist. These are right-angled triangles with integer sides for which the lengths of the [non-hypotenuse edges](wikipedia://en/2568071) differ by one.[^o15][^o16] Such almost-isosceles right-angled triangles can be obtained recursively,

*a*<sub>0</sub> = 1, *b*<sub>0</sub> = 2

*a*<sub>*n*</sub> = 2*b*<sub>*n*−1</sub> + *a*<sub>*n*−1</sub>

*b*<sub>*n*</sub> = 2*a*<sub>*n*</sub> + *b*<sub>*n*−1</sub>

*a*<sub>*n*</sub> is length of hypotenuse, *n* = 1, 2, 3, .... Equivalently,

$$(\tfrac{x-1}{2})^2+(\tfrac{x+1}{2})^2 = y^2$$

where {*x*, *y*} are solutions to the [Pell equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pell_equation) *x*${}^{2}$ − 2*y*${}^{2}$ = −1, with the hypotenuse *y* being the odd terms of the [Pell numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pell_numbers) **1**, 2, **5**, 12, **29**, 70, **169**, 408, **985**, 2378... .. The smallest Pythagorean triples resulting are:

### Arithmetic and geometric progressions {#Arithmetic_and_geometric_progressions}

*Image: Kepler triangle.svg.* A **Kepler triangle** is a right triangle formed by three squares with areas in geometric progression according to the **[golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386)**.

The Kepler triangle is a right triangle whose sides are in [geometric progression](wikipedia://en/23474670). If the sides are formed from the geometric progression *a*, *ar*, *ar*<sup>2</sup> then its common ratio *r* is given by *r* = $\sqrt{\varphi}$ where *φ* is the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386). Its sides are therefore in the ratio 1 : $\sqrt{\varphi}$ : *φ*. Thus, the shape of the Kepler triangle is uniquely determined (up to a scale factor) by the requirement that its sides be in geometric progression.

The 3–4–5 triangle is the unique right triangle (up to scaling) whose sides are in [arithmetic progression](wikipedia://en/168389).[^o20]

### Sides of regular polygons {#Sides_of_regular_polygons}

*Image: Euclid XIII.10.svg.* The sides of a pentagon, hexagon, and decagon, inscribed in [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) circles, form a right triangle.

Let

$$a=2\sin\frac{\pi}{10}=\frac{-1+\sqrt5}{2}=\frac1\varphi\approx 0.618$$

be the side length of a regular [decagon](wikipedia://en/327393) inscribed in the unit circle, where $\varphi$ is the golden ratio. Let

$$b=2\sin\frac{\pi}{6}=1$$

be the side length of a regular [hexagon](wikipedia://en/59733) in the unit circle, and let

$$c=2\sin\frac{\pi}{5}=\sqrt{\frac{5-\sqrt5}{2}}\approx 1.176$$

be the side length of a regular [pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014) in the unit circle. Then $a^2+b^2=c^2$, so these three lengths form the sides of a right triangle.[^o22] The same triangle forms half of a [golden rectangle](wikipedia://en/219374). It may also be found within a [regular icosahedron](wikipedia://en/14968) of side length $c$: the shortest line segment from any vertex $V$ to the plane of its five neighbors has length $a$, and the endpoints of this line segment together with any of the neighbors of $V$ form the vertices of a right triangle with sides $a$, $b$, and $c$.[^o23]
