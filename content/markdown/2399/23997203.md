# Differential of a function

differential Differential

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Differential of a function"}

In [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), the **differential** represents the [principal part](wikipedia://en/1225337#Calculus) of the change in a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $y = f(x)$ with respect to changes in the independent variable. The differential $dy$ is defined by

$$dy = f'(x)\,dx,$$

where $f'(x)$ is the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of $f$ with respect to $x$, and $dx$ is an additional real [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109) (so that $dy$ is a function of $x$ and $dx$). The notation is such that the equation

:::

$$dy = \frac{dy}{dx}\, dx$$

holds, where the derivative is represented in the [Leibniz notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz_notation) $dy/dx$, and this is consistent with regarding the derivative as the quotient of the differentials. One also writes

$$df(x) = f'(x)\,dx.$$

The precise meaning of the variables $dy$ and $dx$ depends on the context of the application and the required level of mathematical rigor. The domain of these variables may take on a particular geometrical significance if the differential is regarded as a particular [differential form](wikipedia://en/221519), or analytical significance if the differential is regarded as a [linear approximation](wikipedia://en/1107596) to the increment of a function. Traditionally, the variables $dx$ and $dy$ are considered to be very small ([infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990)), and this interpretation is made rigorous in [non-standard analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-standard_analysis).

## History and usage {#History_and_usage}

The differential was first introduced via an intuitive or heuristic definition by [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) and furthered by [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz), who thought of the differential $dy$ as an infinitely small (or [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990)) change in the value $y$ of the function, corresponding to an infinitely small change $dx$ in the function's argument $x$. For that reason, the instantaneous rate of change of $y$ with respect to $x$, which is the value of the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of the function, is denoted by the fraction

$$\frac{dy}{dx}$$

in what is called the [Leibniz notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz_notation) for derivatives. The quotient $dy/dx$ is not infinitely small; rather it is a [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438).

The use of infinitesimals in this form was widely criticized, for instance by the famous pamphlet [The Analyst](wikipedia://en/341394) by Bishop Berkeley. [Augustin-Louis Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842) (1823) defined the differential without appeal to the atomism of Leibniz's infinitesimals.[^o3][^o4] Instead, Cauchy, following [d'Alembert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_le_Rond_d'Alembert), inverted the logical order of Leibniz and his successors: the derivative itself became the fundamental object, defined as a [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602) of difference quotients, and the differentials were then defined in terms of it. That is, one was free to *define* the differential $dy$ by an expression

$$dy = f'(x)\,dx$$

in which $dy$ and $dx$ are simply new variables taking finite real values,[^o5] not fixed infinitesimals as they had been for Leibniz.[^o6]

According to (Boyer 1959, 12), Cauchy's approach was a significant logical improvement over the infinitesimal approach of Leibniz because, instead of invoking the metaphysical notion of infinitesimals, the quantities $dy$ and $dx$ could now be manipulated in exactly the same manner as any other real quantities in a meaningful way. Cauchy's overall conceptual approach to differentials remains the standard one in modern analytical treatments,[^o8] although the final word on rigor, a fully modern notion of the limit, was ultimately due to [Karl Weierstrass](wikipedia://en/93459).[^o9]

In physical treatments, such as those applied to the theory of [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952), the infinitesimal view still prevails. (Courant John 1999, 184) reconcile the physical use of infinitesimal differentials with the mathematical impossibility of them as follows. The differentials represent finite non-zero values that are smaller than the degree of accuracy required for the particular purpose for which they are intended. Thus "physical infinitesimals" need not appeal to a corresponding mathematical infinitesimal in order to have a precise sense.

Following twentieth-century developments in [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) and [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), it became clear that the notion of the differential of a function could be extended in a variety of ways. In [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478), it is more desirable to deal directly with the differential as the principal part of the increment of a function. This leads directly to the notion that the differential of a function at a point is a [linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) of an increment $\Delta x$. This approach allows the differential (as a linear map) to be developed for a variety of more sophisticated spaces, ultimately giving rise to such notions as the [Fréchet](wikipedia://en/2735727) or [Gateaux derivative](wikipedia://en/2253139). Likewise, in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), the differential of a function at a point is a linear function of a [tangent vector](wikipedia://en/191999) (an "infinitely small displacement"), which exhibits it as a kind of one-form: the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) of the function. In [non-standard calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-standard_calculus), differentials are regarded as infinitesimals, which can themselves be put on a rigorous footing (see [differential (infinitesimal)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_%28infinitesimal%29)).

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: Sentido geometrico del diferencial de una funcion.png.* The differential of a function $f(x)$ at a point $x_0$.

The differential is defined in modern treatments of differential calculus as follows.[^o13] The differential of a function $f(x)$ of a single real variable $x$ is the function $df$ of two independent real variables $x$ and $\Delta x$ given by

$$df(x, \Delta x) \ \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} \ f'(x)\,\Delta x.$$

One or both of the arguments may be suppressed, i.e., one may see $df(x)$ or simply $df$. If $y = f(x)$, the differential may also be written as $dy$. Since $dx(x,\Delta x)=\Delta x$, it is conventional to write $dx=\Delta x$ so that the following equality holds:

$$df(x) = f'(x) \, dx$$

This notion of differential is broadly applicable when a [linear approximation](wikipedia://en/1107596) to a function is sought, in which the value of the increment $\Delta x$ is small enough. More precisely, if $f$ is a [differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206) at $x$, then the difference in $y$-values

$$\Delta y \ \stackrel{\rm{def}}{=}\ f(x+\Delta x) - f(x)$$

satisfies

$$\Delta y = f'(x)\,\Delta x + \varepsilon = df(x) + \varepsilon\,$$

where the error $\varepsilon$ in the approximation satisfies $\varepsilon /\Delta x\rightarrow 0$ as $\Delta x\rightarrow 0$. In other words, one has the approximate identity

$$\Delta y \approx dy$$

in which the error can be made as small as desired relative to $\Delta x$ by constraining $\Delta x$ to be sufficiently small; that is to say,

$$\frac{\Delta y - dy}{\Delta x}\to 0$$

as $\Delta x\rightarrow 0$. For this reason, the differential of a function is known as the [principal (linear) part](wikipedia://en/1225337) in the increment of a function: the differential is a [linear function](wikipedia://en/152111) of the increment $\Delta x$, and although the error $\varepsilon$ may be nonlinear, it tends to zero rapidly as $\Delta x$ tends to zero.

## Differentials in several variables {#Differentials_in_several_variables}

::: {#o15 .table cue=table}

| Operator / Function | $f(x)$ | $f(x, y, u(x, y), v(x, y))$ |
|---|---|---|
| Differential | 1: $df \, \overset{\underset{\mathrm{def}}{}}{=} \, f'_x\,dx$ | 2: $d_x f \, \overset{\underset{\mathrm{def}}{}}{=} \, f'_x\,dx$ 3: [$df \, \overset{\underset{\mathrm{def}}{}}{=} \, f'_x dx + f'_y dy + f'_u du + f'_v dv$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_derivative) |
| [Partial derivative](wikipedia://en/52565) | $f'_x \, \overset{\underset{\mathrm{(1)}}{}}{=} \, \frac{df}{dx}$ | $f'_x \, \overset{\underset{\mathrm{(2)}}{}}{=} \, \frac{d_x f}{dx} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ |
| [Total derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_derivative) | $\frac{df}{dx} \, \overset{\underset{\mathrm{(1)}}{}}{=} \, f'_x$ | $\frac{df}{dx} \, \overset{\underset{\mathrm{(3)}}{}}{=} \, f'_x + f'_u \frac{du}{dx} + f'_v \frac{dv}{dx}; (f'_y \frac{dy}{dx} = 0)$ |

:::

Following (Goursat 1904, I, §15), for functions of more than one independent variable,

$$y = f(x_1,\dots,x_n),$$

the **partial differential** of $y$ with respect to any one of the variables $x_{i}$ is the principal part of the change in $y$ resulting from a change $dx_{i}$ in that one variable. The partial differential is therefore

$$\frac{\partial y}{\partial x_i} dx_i$$

involving the [partial derivative](wikipedia://en/52565) of $y$ with respect to $x_{i}$. The sum of the partial differentials with respect to all of the independent variables is the **total differential**

$$dy = \frac{\partial y}{\partial x_1} dx_1 + \cdots + \frac{\partial y}{\partial x_n} dx_n,$$

which is the principal part of the change in $y$ resulting from changes in the independent variables $x_{i}$.

More precisely, in the context of multivariable calculus, following (Courant 1937b), if $f$ is a differentiable function, then by the [definition of differentiability](wikipedia://en/2735727), the increment

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta y &{}~\stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}~ f(x_1+\Delta x_1, \dots, x_n+\Delta x_n) - f(x_1,\dots,x_n)\\ &{}= \frac{\partial y}{\partial x_1} \Delta x_1 + \cdots + \frac{\partial y}{\partial x_n} \Delta x_n + \varepsilon_1\Delta x_1 +\cdots+\varepsilon_n\Delta x_n \end{aligned}$$

where the error terms $\varepsilon_{i}$ tend to zero as the increments $\Delta x_{i}$ jointly tend to zero. The total differential is then rigorously defined as

$$dy = \frac{\partial y}{\partial x_1} \Delta x_1 + \cdots + \frac{\partial y}{\partial x_n} \Delta x_n.$$

Since, with this definition,

$$dx_i(\Delta x_1,\dots,\Delta x_n) = \Delta x_i,$$

one has

$$dy = \frac{\partial y}{\partial x_1}\,d x_1 + \cdots + \frac{\partial y}{\partial x_n}\,d x_n.$$

As in the case of one variable, the approximate identity holds

$$dy \approx \Delta y$$

in which the total error can be made as small as desired relative to $\sqrt{\Delta x_1^2+\cdots +\Delta x_n^2}$ by confining attention to sufficiently small increments.

### Application of the total differential to error estimation {#Application_of_the_total_differential_to_error_estimation}

In measurement, the total differential is used in [estimating the error](wikipedia://en/21923920) $\Delta f$ of a function $f$ based on the errors $\Delta x,\Delta y,\ldots$ of the parameters $x, y, \ldots$. Assuming that the interval is short enough for the change to be approximately linear:

$$\Delta f(x)=f'(x)\Delta x$$

and that the parameters $x, y, \ldots$ are [functionally independent](wikipedia://en/1141208), so that the total differential applies, the change in $f$ induced by simultaneous changes in all parameters is

$$\Delta f \approx f_x \Delta x + f_y \Delta y + \cdots .$$

The derivative $f_x$ with respect to the parameter $x$ gives the sensitivity of the function $f$ to a change in $x$, in particular to the error $\Delta x$. How the individual errors $\Delta x, \Delta y, \ldots$ combine into a bound on $\Delta f$ depends on what is known about them.

If the errors are known only as bounds on magnitude (i.e., $|\Delta x| \le \delta x$, etc.) and may have arbitrary signs, the corresponding worst-case bound on $\Delta f$ is obtained by taking absolute values, since signed contributions could otherwise cancel:

$$|\Delta f| \le |f_x|\, \delta x + |f_y|\, \delta y + \cdots .$$

If instead the errors are modelled as [statistically independent](wikipedia://en/27593) random variables with [standard deviations](wikipedia://en/27590) $\sigma_x, \sigma_y, \ldots$, then by [propagation of variance](wikipedia://en/593908) the standard deviation of $\Delta f$ adds in quadrature:

$$\sigma_f^2 = f_x^2\, \sigma_x^2 + f_y^2\, \sigma_y^2 + \cdots .$$

The first formula is a deterministic upper bound, the second a typical (one-standard-deviation) estimate; the two coincide only in the trivial single-variable case.

From the worst-case principle the familiar error rules of summation, multiplication, etc. are derived, e.g.:

That is to say, in multiplication, the total [relative error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_error) is the sum of the relative errors of the parameters.

To illustrate how this depends on the function considered, consider the case where the function is $f(a,b)=a\ln b$ instead. Then, it can be computed that the error estimate is

$$\frac{\Delta f}{f} = \frac{\Delta a}{a} + \frac{\Delta b}{b \ln b}$$

with an extra $\ln b$ factor not found in the case of a simple product. This additional factor tends to make the error smaller, as the denominator $b \ln b$ is larger than a bare $b$.

## Higher-order differentials {#Higher-order_differentials}

Higher-order differentials of a function $y = f(x)$ of a single variable $x$ can be defined via:[^o22]

$$d^2y = d(dy) = d(f'(x)dx) = (df'(x))dx = f''(x)\,(dx)^2,$$

and, in general,

$$d^ny = f^{(n)}(x)\,(dx)^n.$$

Informally, this motivates Leibniz's notation for higher-order derivatives

$$f^{(n)}(x) = \frac{d^n f}{dx^n}.$$

When the independent variable $x$ itself is permitted to depend on other variables, then the expression becomes more complicated, as it must include also higher order differentials in $x$ itself. Thus, for instance,

$$\begin{aligned} d^2 y &= f''(x)\,(dx)^2 + f'(x)d^2x \\[1ex] d^3 y &= f'''(x)\, (dx)^3 + 3f''(x)dx\,d^2x + f'(x)d^3x \end{aligned}$$

and so forth.

Similar considerations apply to defining higher order differentials of functions of several variables. For example, if $f$ is a function of two variables $x$ and $y$, then

$$d^nf = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k}\frac{\partial^n f}{\partial x^k \partial y^{n-k}}(dx)^k(dy)^{n-k},$$

where $\binom{n}{k}$ is a [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668). In more variables, an analogous expression holds, but with an appropriate [multinomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multinomial_coefficient) expansion rather than binomial expansion.[^o23]

Higher order differentials in several variables also become more complicated when the independent variables are themselves allowed to depend on other variables. For instance, for a function $f$ of $x$ and $y$ which are allowed to depend on auxiliary variables, one has

$$d^2f = \left(\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2} (dx)^2 + 2\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x\partial y} dx\,dy + \frac{\partial^2f}{\partial y^2} (dy)^2\right) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}d^2x + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} d^2y.$$

Because of this notational awkwardness, the use of higher order differentials was roundly criticized by (Hadamard 1935), who concluded: Enfin, que signifie ou que représente l'égalité

$$d^2z = r\,dx^2 + 2s\,dx\,dy + t\,dy^2\,?$$

A mon avis, rien du tout.

That is: *Finally, what is meant, or represented, by the equality \[...\]? In my opinion, nothing at all.* In spite of this skepticism, higher order differentials did emerge as an important tool in analysis.[^o25]

In these contexts, the $n$-th order differential of the function $f$ applied to an increment $\Delta x$ is defined by

$$d^nf(x,\Delta x) = \left.\frac{d^n}{dt^n} f(x+t\Delta x)\right|_{t=0}$$

or an equivalent expression, such as

$$\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\Delta^n_{t\Delta x} f}{t^n}$$

where $\Delta^n_{t\Delta x} f$ is an *n*th [forward difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forward_difference) with increment $t\Delta x$.

This definition makes sense as well if $f$ is a function of several variables (for simplicity taken here as a vector argument). Then the $n$-th differential defined in this way is a [homogeneous function](wikipedia://en/622844) of degree $n$ in the vector increment $\Delta x$. Furthermore, the [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) of $f$ at the point $x$ is given by

$$f(x+\Delta x) \sim f(x) + df(x,\Delta x) + \frac{1}{2}d^2f(x,\Delta x) + \cdots + \frac{1}{n!} d^n f(x,\Delta x) + \cdots$$

The higher order [Gateaux derivative](wikipedia://en/2253139) generalizes these considerations to infinite dimensional spaces.

## Properties {#Properties}

A number of properties of the differential follow in a straightforward manner from the corresponding properties of the derivative, partial derivative, and total derivative. These include:[^o28]

- [Linearity](wikipedia://en/91591): For constants $a$ and $b$ and differentiable functions $f$ and $g$, $$d(af+bg) = a\,df + b\,dg.$$
- [Product rule](wikipedia://en/248988): For two differentiable functions $f$ and $g$, $$d(fg) = f\,dg+g\,df.$$

An operation $d$ with these two properties is known in [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) as a [derivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivation_%28abstract_algebra%29). They imply the power rule

$$d( f^n ) = n f^{n-1} df$$

In addition, various forms of the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) hold, in increasing level of generality:[^o29]

- If $y = f(u)$ is a differentiable function of the variable $u$ and $u = g(x)$ is a differentiable function of $x$, then $$dy = f'(u)\,du = f'(g(x))g'(x)\,dx.$$
- If $y = f(x_{1}, ..., x_{n})$ and all of the variables $x_{1}, ..., x_{n}$ depend on another variable $t$, then by the [chain rule for partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/6113#Chain_rule_for_several_variables), one has $$\begin{aligned} dy = \frac{dy}{dt} dt &= \frac{\partial y}{\partial x_1} dx_1 + \cdots + \frac{\partial y}{\partial x_n} dx_n\\[1ex] &= \frac{\partial y}{\partial x_1} \frac{dx_1}{dt} \, dt + \cdots + \frac{\partial y}{\partial x_n} \frac{dx_n}{dt} \, dt. \end{aligned}$$ Heuristically, the chain rule for several variables can itself be understood by dividing through both sides of this equation by the infinitely small quantity $dt$.
- More general analogous expressions hold, in which the intermediate variables $x_{i}$ depend on more than one variable.

:::

## General formulation {#General_formulation}

A consistent notion of differential can be developed for a function $f : \mathbf{R}^{n} \to \mathbf{R}^{m}$ between two [Euclidean spaces](wikipedia://en/9697). Let $\mathbf{x},\Delta\mathbf{x} \in \mathbf{R}^{n}$ be a pair of [Euclidean vectors](wikipedia://en/32533). The increment in the function $f$ is

$$\Delta f = f(\mathbf{x}+\Delta\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{x}).$$

If there exists an $m \times n$ [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $A$ such that

$$\Delta f = A\Delta\mathbf{x} + \|\Delta\mathbf{x}\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$$

in which the vector $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \to 0$ as $\Delta\mathbf{x} \to 0$, then $f$ is by definition differentiable at the point $\mathbf{x}$. The matrix $A$ is sometimes known as the [Jacobian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix), and the [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) that associates to the increment $\Delta\mathbf{x} \in \mathbf{R}^{n}$ the vector $A\Delta\mathbf{x} \in \mathbf{R}^{m}$ is, in this general setting, known as the differential $df(x)$ of $f$ at the point $x$. This is precisely the [Fréchet derivative](wikipedia://en/2735727), and the same construction can be made to work for a function between any [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989).

Another fruitful point of view is to define the differential directly as a kind of [directional derivative](wikipedia://en/487859):

$$df(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}) = \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{f(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{h})-f(\mathbf{x})}{t} = \left.\frac{d}{dt} f(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{h})\right|_{t=0},$$

which is the approach already taken for defining higher order differentials (and is most nearly the definition set forth by Cauchy). If $t$ represents time and **x** position, then **h** represents a velocity instead of a displacement as we have heretofore regarded it. This yields yet another refinement of the notion of differential: that it should be a linear function of a kinematic velocity. The set of all velocities through a given point of space is known as the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807), and so $df$ gives a linear function on the tangent space: a [differential form](wikipedia://en/221519). With this interpretation, the differential of $f$ is known as the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782), and has broad application in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) because the notion of velocities and the tangent space makes sense on any [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219). If, in addition, the output value of $f$ also represents a position (in a Euclidean space), then a dimensional analysis confirms that the output value of *df* must be a velocity. If one treats the differential in this manner, then it is known as the [pushforward](wikipedia://en/1558510) since it "pushes" velocities from a source space into velocities in a target space.

## Other approaches {#Other_approaches}

Although the notion of having an infinitesimal increment $dx$ is not well-defined in modern [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), a variety of techniques exist for defining the [infinitesimal differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_%28infinitesimal%29) so that the differential of a function can be handled in a manner that does not clash with the [Leibniz notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz_notation). These include:

- Defining the differential as a kind of [differential form](wikipedia://en/221519), specifically the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) of a function. The infinitesimal increments are then identified with vectors in the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) at a point. This approach is popular in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) and related fields, because it readily generalizes to mappings between [differentiable manifolds](wikipedia://en/2119219).
- Differentials as [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) elements of [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346). This approach is popular in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997).[^o32]
- Differentials in smooth models of set theory. This approach is known as [synthetic differential geometry](wikipedia://en/3869427) or [smooth infinitesimal analysis](wikipedia://en/3869419) and is closely related to the algebraic geometric approach, except that ideas from [topos theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topos_theory) are used to *hide* the mechanisms by which nilpotent infinitesimals are introduced.[^o33]
- Differentials as infinitesimals in [hyperreal number](wikipedia://en/51429) systems, which are extensions of the real numbers which contain invertible infinitesimals and infinitely large numbers. This is the approach of [nonstandard analysis](wikipedia://en/21708) pioneered by [Abraham Robinson](wikipedia://en/46024).[^o34]

## Examples and applications {#Examples_and_applications}

Differentials may be effectively used in [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506) to study the propagation of experimental errors in a calculation, and thus the overall [numerical stability](wikipedia://en/233807) of a problem (Courant 1937a). Suppose that the variable $x$ represents the outcome of an experiment and $y$ is the result of a numerical computation applied to *x*. The question is to what extent errors in the measurement of $x$ influence the outcome of the computation of *y*. If the $x$ is known to within Δ*x* of its true value, then [Taylor's theorem](wikipedia://en/51714) gives the following estimate on the error Δ*y* in the computation of *y*:

$$\Delta y = f'(x)\Delta x + \frac{(\Delta x)^2}{2}f''(\xi)$$

where $\xi = x + \theta\Delta x$ for some $0 < \theta < 1$. If $\Delta x$ is small, then the second order term is negligible, so that Δ*y* is, for practical purposes, well-approximated by $dy = f\boldsymbol{(x) \Delta x}$.

The differential is often useful to rewrite a [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309)

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = g(x)$$

in the form

$$dy = g(x)\,dx,$$

in particular when one wants to [separate the variables](wikipedia://en/387750).
