# Hartogs's extension theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hartogs's extension theorem"}

In the theory of functions of [several complex variables](wikipedia://en/360243), **Hartogs's extension theorem** is a statement about the [singularities](wikipedia://en/54240) of [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110) of several variables. Informally, it states that the [support](wikipedia://en/381013) of the singularities of such functions cannot be [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), therefore the singular set of a function of several complex variables must (loosely speaking) 'go off to infinity' in some direction. More precisely, it shows that an [isolated singularity](wikipedia://en/334325) is always a [removable singularity](wikipedia://en/81644) for any [analytic function](wikipedia://en/61478) of $n > 1$ complex variables. A first version of this theorem was proved by [Friedrich Hartogs](wikipedia://en/2977779),[^o2] and as such it is known also as **Hartogs's lemma** and **Hartogs's principle**: in earlier [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) literature,[^o3] it is also called the **Osgood–Brown theorem**, acknowledging later work by [Arthur Barton Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Barton_Brown) and [William Fogg Osgood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Fogg_Osgood).[^o4] This property of holomorphic functions of several variables is also called **Hartogs's phenomenon**: however, the locution "Hartogs's phenomenon" is also used to identify the property of solutions of [systems](wikipedia://en/249617) of [partial differential](wikipedia://en/52564) or [convolution equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convolution_operator) satisfying Hartogs-type theorems.[^o5]

:::

## Historical note {#Historical_note}

The original proof was given by [Friedrich Hartogs](wikipedia://en/2977779) in 1906, using [Cauchy's integral formula](wikipedia://en/72827) for [functions of several complex variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functions_of_several_complex_variables).[^o2] Today, usual proofs rely on either the [Bochner–Martinelli–Koppelman formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bochner–Martinelli–Koppelman_formula) or the solution of the inhomogeneous [Cauchy–Riemann equations](wikipedia://en/7583) with compact support. The latter approach is due to [Leon Ehrenpreis](wikipedia://en/14162704) who initiated it in the paper (Ehrenpreis 1961). Yet another very simple proof of this result was given by [Gaetano Fichera](wikipedia://en/22175811) in the paper (Fichera 1957), by using his solution of the [Dirichlet problem](wikipedia://en/571109) for [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110) of several variables and the related concept of [CR-function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CR-function):[^o9] later he extended the theorem to a certain class of [partial differential operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_operator) in the paper (Fichera 1983), and his ideas were later further explored by Giuliano Bratti.[^o11] Also the Japanese school of the theory of [partial differential operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_operator) worked much on this topic, with notable contributions by Akira Kaneko.[^o12] Their approach is to use [Ehrenpreis's fundamental principle](wikipedia://en/39993538).

## Hartogs's phenomenon {#Hartogs's_phenomenon}

For example, in two variables, consider the interior domain

$$H_\varepsilon = \{z=(z_1,z_2)\in\Delta^2:|z_1|<\varepsilon\ \ \text{or}\ \ 1-\varepsilon< |z_2|\}$$

in the two-dimensional polydisk $\Delta^2=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^2;|z_1|<1,|z_2|<1\}$ where $0 < \varepsilon < 1.$

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem** (Hartogs 1906): Any holomorphic function $f$ on $H_\varepsilon$ can be analytically continued to $\Delta^2.$ Namely, there is a holomorphic function $F$ on $\Delta^2$ such that $F=f$ on $H_\varepsilon.$

Such a phenomenon is called **Hartogs's phenomenon**, which lead to the notion of this Hartogs's extension theorem and the [domain of holomorphy](wikipedia://en/3236377).

:::

## Formal statement and proof {#Formal_statement_and_proof}

Let $f$ be a [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110) on a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $G \backslash K$, where $G$ is an open subset of $\mathbf{C}^{n}$ ($n \ge 2$) and $K$ is a compact subset of $G$. If the [complement](wikipedia://en/54347) $G \backslash K$ is connected, then $f$ can be extended to a unique holomorphic function $F$ on $G$.

Ehrenpreis' proof is based on the existence of smooth [bump functions](wikipedia://en/815799), unique continuation of holomorphic functions, and the [Poincaré lemma](wikipedia://en/600030) — the last in the form that for any smooth and compactly supported differential (0,1)-form $\omega$ on $\mathbf{C}^{n}$ with $\overline{\partial}\omega = 0$, there exists a smooth and compactly supported function $\eta$ on $\mathbf{C}^{n}$ with $\overline{\partial}\eta = \omega$. The crucial assumption $n \ge 2$ is required for the validity of this Poincaré lemma; if $n = 1$ then it is generally impossible for $\eta$ to be compactly supported.

The [ansatz](wikipedia://en/1359360) for $F$ is $\varphi f - v$ for smooth functions $\varphi$ and $v$ on $G$; such an expression is meaningful provided that $\varphi$ is identically equal to zero where $f$ is undefined (namely on $K$). Furthermore, given any holomorphic function on $G$ which is equal to $f$ on *some* [open set](wikipedia://en/39358), unique continuation (based on connectedness of $G \backslash K$) shows that it is equal to $f$ on *all* of $G \backslash K$.

The holomorphicity of this function is identical to the condition $\overline{\partial}v = f \overline{\partial}\varphi$. For any smooth function $\varphi$, the differential (0,1)-form $f \overline{\partial}\varphi$ is $\overline{\partial}$-closed. Choosing $\varphi$ to be a [smooth function](wikipedia://en/1408000) which is identically equal to zero on $K$ and identically equal to one on the complement of some compact subset $L$ of $G$, this (0,1)-form additionally has compact support, so that the Poincaré lemma identifies an appropriate $v$ of compact support. This defines $F$ as a holomorphic function on $G$; it only remains to show (following the above comments) that it coincides with $f$ on some open set.

On the set $\mathbf{C}^{n} \backslash L$, $v$ is holomorphic since $\varphi$ is identically constant. Since it is zero near infinity, unique continuation applies to show that it is identically zero on some open subset of $G \backslash L$.[^o17] Thus, on this open subset, $F$ equals $f$ and the existence part of Hartog's theorem is proved. Uniqueness is automatic from unique continuation, based on connectedness of $G$.

## Counterexamples in dimension one {#Counterexamples_in_dimension_one}

The theorem does not hold when $n = 1$. To see this, it suffices to consider the function $f(z) = z^{-1}$, which is clearly holomorphic in $\mathbf{C} \backslash \{0\},$ but cannot be continued as a holomorphic function on the whole of $\mathbf{C}$. Therefore, the Hartogs's phenomenon is an elementary phenomenon that highlights the difference between the theory of functions of one and several complex variables.
