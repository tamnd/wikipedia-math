# Grigorchuk group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Grigorchuk group"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) area of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), the **Grigorchuk group** or **the first Grigorchuk group** is a [finitely generated group](wikipedia://en/963042) constructed by [Rostislav Grigorchuk](wikipedia://en/18697527) that provided the first example of a [finitely generated group](wikipedia://en/963042) of intermediate (that is, faster than polynomial but slower than exponential) [growth](wikipedia://en/498727). The group was originally constructed by Grigorchuk in a 1980 paper[^o7] and he then proved in a 1984 paper[^o8] that this group has intermediate growth, thus providing an answer to an important [open problem](wikipedia://en/1333335) posed by [John Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632) in 1968. The Grigorchuk group remains a key object of study in [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164), particularly in the study of the so-called branch groups and automata groups, and it has important connections with the theory of [iterated monodromy groups](wikipedia://en/4649761).[^o2]

:::

## History and significance {#History_and_significance}

The [growth](wikipedia://en/498727) of a [finitely generated group](wikipedia://en/963042) measures the asymptotics, as $n \to \infty$ of the size of an *n*-ball in the [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of the group (that is, the number of elements of *G* that can be expressed as words of length at most *n* in the generating set of *G*). The study of growth rates of [finitely generated groups](wikipedia://en/963042) goes back to the 1950s and is motivated in part by the notion of [volume entropy](wikipedia://en/19346619) (that is, the growth rate of the volume of balls) in the [universal covering space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_covering_space) of a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625). It is obvious that the growth rate of a finitely generated group is at most [exponential](wikipedia://en/9678) and it was also understood early on that finitely generated [nilpotent groups](wikipedia://en/144569) have polynomial growth. In 1968 [John Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632) posed a question[^o4] about the existence of a finitely generated group of *intermediate growth*, that is, faster than any polynomial function and slower than any exponential function. An important result in the subject is [Gromov's theorem on groups of polynomial growth](wikipedia://en/635483), obtained by [Gromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gromov_%28mathematician%29) in 1981, which shows that a finitely generated group has polynomial growth [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) this group has a [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569) [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of finite [index](wikipedia://en/230581). Prior to Grigorchuk's work, there were many results establishing growth dichotomy (that is, that the growth is always either polynomial or exponential) for various classes of finitely generated groups, such as [linear groups](wikipedia://en/3983656), [solvable groups](wikipedia://en/105012),[^o5][^o6] etc.

Grigorchuk's group *G* was constructed in a 1980 paper of [Rostislav Grigorchuk](wikipedia://en/18697527),[^o7] where he proved that this group is infinite, [periodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_group) and [residually finite](wikipedia://en/5780875). In a subsequent 1984 paper[^o8] Grigorchuk proved that this group has intermediate growth (this result was announced by Grigorchuk in 1983).[^o9] More precisely, he proved that *G* has growth *b*(*n*) that is faster than $\exp(\sqrt n)$ but slower than $\exp(n^s)$ where $s=\log_{32}31\approx 0.991$. The upper bound was later improved by [Laurent Bartholdi](wikipedia://en/80158818)[^o10] to

$$s=\frac{\log2}{\log2-\log\eta} \approx 0.7674, \qquad \eta^3+\eta^2+\eta=2.$$

A lower bound of $\exp(n^{0.504})$ was proved by [Yurii Leonov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yurii_Leonov).[^o11] It was conjectured that the limit

$$\lim_{n\to \infty} \log_n \log b(n),$$

and this remained a major open problem until the problem was resolved in 2020 by [Anna Erschler](wikipedia://en/48303774) and [Tianyi Zheng](wikipedia://en/77586987)[^o12] in which it was shown that the limit equals $s$.

Grigorchuk's group was also the first example of a group that is [amenable](wikipedia://en/657430) but not [elementary amenable](wikipedia://en/5393997), thus answering a problem posed by [Mahlon Marsh Day](wikipedia://en/71432032) in 1957.[^o13]

Originally, Grigorchuk's group *G* was constructed as a group of Lebesgue-measure-preserving transformations on the unit interval, but subsequently simpler descriptions of *G* were found and it is now usually presented as a group of automorphisms of the infinite regular [binary](wikipedia://en/4321) [rooted tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rooted_tree). The study of Grigorchuk's group informed in large part the development of the theory of branch groups, automata groups and self-similar groups in the 1990s–2000s and Grigorchuk's group remains a central object in this theory. Recently important connections between this theory and [complex dynamics](wikipedia://en/650518), particularly the notion of [iterated monodromy groups](wikipedia://en/4649761), have been uncovered in the work of [Volodymyr Nekrashevych](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volodymyr_Nekrashevych),[^o14] and others.

After Grigorchuk's 1984 paper, there were many subsequent extensions and generalizations.[^o15][^o16][^o17][^o18]

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: T2 tree.svg.* The infinite binary tree $T_{2}$. Its nodes are labeled by strings of 0s and 1s.

Although initially the Grigorchuk group was defined as a group of [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198)-preserving transformations of the unit interval, at present this group is usually given by its realization as a group of automorphisms of the infinite regular [binary](wikipedia://en/4321) [rooted tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rooted_tree) $T_{2}$. The tree $T_{2}$ is the set $\Sigma^{*}$ of all finite strings in the alphabet $\Sigma = \{0,1\}$, including the [empty string](wikipedia://en/835827) $\emptyset$, which roots $T_{2}$. For a vertex $x$ of $T_{2}$ the string $x0$ is the [left child](wikipedia://en/4321) of $x$ and the string $x1$ is the [right child](wikipedia://en/4321) of $x$ in $T_{2}$. The group of all automorphisms $\operatorname{Aut}(T_{2})$ can thus be thought of as the group of all length-preserving [permutations](wikipedia://en/24634) $\sigma$ of $\Sigma^{*}$ that also respect the *initial segment* relation: whenever a string $x$ is an initial segment of a string $y$ then $\sigma(x)$ is an initial segment of $\sigma(y)$.

The **Grigorchuk group** $G$ is the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of $\operatorname{Aut}(T_{2})$ [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by four specific elements of $\operatorname{Aut}(T_{2})$ defined as follows (note that $\emptyset$ is [fixed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixpoint) by *any* tree-automorphism):

$$G = \langle a, b, c, d \rangle \leq \text{Aut}(T_2)\text{,}$$

where

$$a(0) = 1\text{,} \qquad a(1) = 0\text{,} \qquad \forall x \in \Sigma^*\quad \begin{cases} a(0x) = 1x \\ a(1x) =0x \end{cases}$$

and

$$\begin{aligned} &b(0) = c(0) = d(0) = 0 \\ &b(1) = c(1) = d(1) = 1\text{,} \end{aligned} \qquad \forall x \in \Sigma^*\quad \begin{aligned} &\begin{cases} b(0x) = 0a(x) \\ b(1x) = 1c(x) \end{cases} \\ &\begin{cases} c(0x) = 0a(x) \\ c(1x) = 1d(x) \end{cases} \\ &\begin{cases} d(0x) = 0x \\ d(1x) = 1b(x)\text{.}\end{cases} \end{aligned}$$

*Image: Grigorchuk group generators.svg.* The action of the standard generating set of the Grigorchuk group on the tree *T*<sub>2</sub>. The triangles denote infinite subtrees that remain unchanged.

Only the element $a$ is defined explicitly; it swaps the child trees of $\emptyset$. The elements $b$, $c$, and $d$ are defined through a [mutual recursion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_recursion).

To understand the effect of the latter operations, consider the rightmost branch $B$ of $T_{2}$, which begins $\{\emptyset, 1, 11, 111, ...\}$. As a branch, $B$ is [order-isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order-isomorphic) to $\mathbb{N}.$ The original tree $T_{2}$ can be obtained by rooting a tree isomorphic to $T_{2}$ at each element of $B$; conversely, one can decompose $T_{2}$ into isomorphic subtrees indexed by elements of $B \cong \mathbb{N}$.

The operations $b$, $c$, and $d$ all respect this decomposition: they fix each element of $B$ and act as automorphisms on each indexed subtree. When $b$ acts, it fixes every subtree with index $\equiv 2\pmod{3}$, but acts as $a$ on the rest. Likewise, when $c$ acts, it fixes only the subtrees of index $\equiv 1\pmod{3}$; and $d$ fixes those of index $\equiv 0\pmod{3}$.

A compact notation for these operations is as follows: let the left (resp. right) branch of $T_{2}$ be $T_{L} = 0\Sigma^{*}$ (resp. $T_{R} = 1\Sigma^{*}$), so that

$$a(T_L) \subseteq T_R, \quad a(T_R) \subseteq T_L\text{.}$$

We write $f = (g, h)$ to mean that $f$ acts as $g$ on $T_{L}$ and as $h$ on $T_{R}$. Thus

$$\begin{aligned} b =(a,c) \quad &\Longleftrightarrow \quad b(x) = \begin{cases} a(x) & x \in T_L \\ c(x) & x \in T_R \end{cases} \\ c =(a,d) \quad &\Longleftrightarrow \quad c(x) = \begin{cases} a(x) & x \in T_L \\ d(x) & x \in T_R \end{cases} \\ d =(\text{id},b) \quad &\Longleftrightarrow \quad d(x) = \begin{cases} x & x \in T_L \\ b(x) & x \in T_R\text{.} \end{cases} \end{aligned}$$

Similarly

$$aba = (c, a), \quad aca = (d, a), \quad ada = (b, \text{id})\text{,}$$

where $\operatorname{id}$ is the [identity function](wikipedia://en/15069).

## Properties {#Properties}

The following are basic algebraic properties of the Grigorchuk group (see[^o23] for proofs):

- The group $G$ is infinite.[^o8]
- The group $G$ is [residually finite](wikipedia://en/5780875).[^o8] Let $\rho_n : G \to \text{Aut}(T[n])$ be the restriction homomorphism that sends every element of $G$ to its restriction to the first $n$ levels of $T_{2}$. The groups Aut(*T*\[*n*\]) are finite and for every nontrivial *g* in $G$ there exists *n* such that $\rho_n (g) \neq 1.$
- The group $G$ is generated by *a* and any two of the three elements *b,c,d*. For example, we can write $G = \langle a, b, c\rangle.$
- The elements *a*, *b*, *c*, *d* are [involutions](wikipedia://en/293450#Group_theory).
- The elements *b*, *c*, *d* pairwise commute and *bc* = *cb* = *d*, *bd* = *db* = *c*, *dc* = *cd* = *b*, so that $\langle b, c, d \rangle \leqslant G$ is an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) of order 4 [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to the [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of two [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) of order 2.
- Combining the previous two properties we see that every element of $G$ can be written as a (positive) word in *a*, *b*, *c*, *d* such that this word does not contain any subwords of the form *aa*, *bb*, *cc*, *dd*, *cd*, *dc*, *bc*, *cb*, *bd*, *db*. Such words are called *reduced*.
- The group $G$ is a [2-group](wikipedia://en/24664), that is, every element in *G* has finite [order](wikipedia://en/493527) that is a power of 2.[^o7]
- The group $G$ is periodic (as a 2-group) and not locally finite (as it is finitely generated). As such, it is a counterexample to the [Burnside problem](wikipedia://en/151452).
- The group $G$ has intermediate growth.[^o8]
- The group $G$ is [amenable](wikipedia://en/657430) but not [elementary amenable](wikipedia://en/5393997).[^o8]
- The group $G$ is *[just infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_infinite_group)*, that is *G* is infinite but every proper [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) of *G* is finite.
- The group $G$ has the *congruence subgroup property*: a subgroup *H* has finite [index](wikipedia://en/230581) in $G$ if and only if there is a positive integer *n* such that $\ker(\rho_n) \leqslant H.$
- The group $G$ has solvable [subgroup membership problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subgroup_membership_problem), that is, there is an algorithm that, given arbitrary words *w*, *u*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *u<sub>n</sub>* decides whether or not *w* represents an element of the subgroup generated by *u*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *u<sub>n</sub>*.[^o24]
- The group $G$ is [subgroup separable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LERF), that is, every finitely generated subgroup is closed in the [pro-finite topology](wikipedia://en/24266) on $G$.[^o24]
- Every [maximal subgroup](wikipedia://en/1472649) of $G$ has finite [index](wikipedia://en/230581) in $G$.[^o25]
- The group $G$ is finitely generated but not [finitely presentable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_presented_group).[^o8][^o26]
- The [stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of the level one vertices in $T_2$ in $G$ (the subgroup of elements that act as identity on the strings 0 and 1), is generated by the following elements:

$$G_{\{0, 1\}} = \langle b, c, d, aba, aca, ada \rangle.$$

$G_{\{0, 1\}}$ is a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of [index](wikipedia://en/230581) 2 in *G* and

$$G = G_{\{0, 1\}} \sqcup a G_{\{0, 1\}}.$$

- A reduced word represents an element of $G_{\{0, 1\}}$if and only if this word involves an even number of occurrences of *a*.
- If *w* is a reduced word in *G* with a positive even number of occurrences of *a*, then there exist words *u*, *v* (not necessarily reduced) such that:

$$w = (u,v) \quad \text{and} \quad \begin{cases} |u|, |v| \leqslant \tfrac{1}{2} |w| & |w| \text{ odd} \\ |u|, |v| \leqslant \tfrac{1}{2} (|w|+1) & |w| \text{ even} \end{cases}$$

This is sometimes called the *contraction property*. It plays a key role in many proofs regarding *G* since it allows to use inductive arguments on the length of a word.

- The group *G* has solvable [word problem](wikipedia://en/33563) and solvable [conjugacy problem](wikipedia://en/4942384) (consequence of the contraction property).
