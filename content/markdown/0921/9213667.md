# Field of definition

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Field of definition"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **field of definition** of an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) *V* is essentially the smallest [field](wikipedia://en/10603) to which the coefficients of the [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) defining *V* can belong. Given polynomials, with coefficients in a field *K*, it may not be obvious whether there is a smaller field *k*, and other polynomials defined over *k*, which still define *V*.

:::

The issue of field of definition is of concern in [diophantine geometry](wikipedia://en/502205).

::: {#o3 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

## Notation {#Notation}

Throughout this article, *k* denotes a field. The [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129) of a field is denoted by adding a superscript of "alg", e.g. the algebraic closure of *k* is *k*<sup>alg</sup>. The symbols **Q**, **R**, **C**, and **F**<sub>*p*</sub> represent, respectively, the field of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), the field of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), the field of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), and the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) containing *p* elements. [Affine *n*-space](wikipedia://en/298834) over a field *F* is denoted by **A**<sup>*n*</sup>(*F*).

:::

## Definitions for affine and projective varieties {#Definitions_for_affine_and_projective_varieties}

Results and definitions stated below, for [affine varieties](wikipedia://en/248808#Affine_varieties), can be translated to [projective varieties](wikipedia://en/248808#Projective_variety), by replacing **A**<sup>*n*</sup>(*k*<sup>alg</sup>) with [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) of dimension *n* − 1 over *k*<sup>alg</sup>, and by requiring all polynomials to be [homogeneous](wikipedia://en/1698977).

A ***k*-[algebraic set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_set)** is the zero-locus in **A**<sup>*n*</sup>(*k*<sup>alg</sup>) of a subset of the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) *k*\[*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>\]. A ***k*-variety** is a *k*-algebraic set that is irreducible, i.e. is not the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of two strictly smaller *k*-algebraic sets. A ***k*-morphism** is a [regular function](wikipedia://en/1997#Morphism_of_affine_varieties) between *k*-algebraic sets whose defining polynomials' coefficients belong to *k*.

One reason for considering the zero-locus in **A**<sup>*n*</sup>(*k*<sup>alg</sup>) and not **A**<sup>*n*</sup>(*k*) is that, for two distinct *k*-algebraic sets *X*<sub>1</sub> and *X*<sub>2</sub>, the [intersections](wikipedia://en/23476429) *X*<sub>1</sub> ∩ **A**<sup>*n*</sup>(*k*) and *X*<sub>2</sub> ∩ **A**<sup>*n*</sup>(*k*) can be identical; in fact, the zero-locus in **A**<sup>*n*</sup>(*k*) of any subset of *k*\[*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>\] is the zero-locus of a *single* element of *k*\[*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>\] if *k* is not algebraically closed.

A *k*-variety is called a **variety** if it is *[absolutely irreducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_irreducible)*, i.e. is not the union of two strictly smaller *k*<sup>alg</sup>-algebraic sets. A variety *V* is **defined over *k*** if every polynomial in *k*<sup>alg</sup>\[*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>\] that vanishes on *V* is the [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) (over *k*<sup>alg</sup>) of polynomials in *k*\[*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>\] that vanish on *V*. A *k*-algebraic set is also an *L*-algebraic set for infinitely many subfields *L* of *k*<sup>alg</sup>. A **field of definition** of a variety *V* is a subfield *L* of *k*<sup>alg</sup> such that *V* is an *L*-variety defined over *L*.

Equivalently, a *k*-variety *V* is a variety defined over *k* if and only if the [function field](wikipedia://en/2783843) *k*(*V*) of *V* is a [regular extension](wikipedia://en/26047190) of *k*, in the sense of [Weil](wikipedia://en/2019). That means every subset of *k*(*V*) that is [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) over *k* is also linearly independent over *k*<sup>alg</sup>. In other words those extensions of *k* are [linearly disjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_disjoint).

[André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019) proved that the intersection of all fields of definition of a variety *V* is itself a field of definition. This justifies saying that any variety possesses a unique, minimal field of definition. However, this does not apply to abstract varieties, and in fact, [counterexamples](wikipedia://en/143151) exist.[^o5]

## Examples {#Examples}

1. The zero-locus of *x*<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup>+ *x*<sub>2</sub><sup>2</sup> is both a **Q**-variety and a **Q**<sup>alg</sup>-algebraic set but neither a variety nor a **Q**<sup>alg</sup>-variety, since it is the union of the **Q**<sup>alg</sup>-varieties defined by the polynomials *x*<sub>1</sub> + i*x*<sub>2</sub> and *x*<sub>1</sub> − i*x*<sub>2</sub>.
1. With **F**<sub>*p*</sub>(*t*) a [transcendental extension](wikipedia://en/287364) of **F**<sub>*p*</sub>, the polynomial *x*<sub>1</sub><sup>*p*</sup> − *t* equals (*x*<sub>1</sub> − *t*<sup>1/*p*</sup>) <sup>*p*</sup> in the polynomial ring (**F**<sub>*p*</sub>(*t*))<sup>alg</sup>\[*x*<sub>1</sub>\]. The **F**<sub>*p*</sub>(*t*)-algebraic set *V* defined by *x*<sub>1</sub><sup>*p*</sup> − *t* is a variety; it is absolutely irreducible because it consists of a single point. But *V* is not defined over **F**<sub>*p*</sub>(*t*), since *V* is also the zero-locus of *x*<sub>1</sub> − *t*<sup>1/*p*</sup>.
1. The [complex projective line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_projective_line) is a projective **R**-variety. (In fact, it is a variety with **Q** as its minimal field of definition.) Viewing the [real projective line](wikipedia://en/46379102) as being the equator on the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799), the coordinate-wise action of [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation) on the complex projective line swaps points with the same longitude but opposite latitudes.
1. The projective **R**-variety *W* defined by the homogeneous polynomial *x*<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup> + *x*<sub>2</sub><sup>2</sup> + *x*<sub>3</sub><sup>2</sup> is also a variety with minimal field of definition **Q**. The following map defines a **C**-isomorphism from the complex projective line to *W*: (*a*,*b*) → (2*ab*, *a*<sup>2</sup>−*b*<sup>2</sup>, −i(*a*<sup>2</sup>+*b*<sup>2</sup>)). Identifying *W* with the Riemann sphere using this map, the coordinate-wise action of complex conjugation on *W* interchanges opposite points of the sphere. The complex projective line cannot be **R**-isomorphic to *W* because the former has *real points*, points fixed by complex conjugation, while the latter does not.

:::

## Scheme-theoretic definitions {#Scheme-theoretic_definitions}

One advantage of defining varieties over arbitrary fields through the theory of [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) is that such definitions are intrinsic and free of embeddings into ambient affine *n*-space.

A ***k*-algebraic set** is a [separated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_scheme_theory#Separated_and_proper_morphisms) and [reduced](wikipedia://en/35457783#reduced) scheme of [finite type](wikipedia://en/2139357) over [Spec](wikipedia://en/37291)(*k*). A ***k*-variety** is an [irreducible](wikipedia://en/35457783#irreducible) *k*-algebraic set. A ***k*-morphism** is a [morphism](wikipedia://en/364754#The_category_of_schemes) between *k*-algebraic sets regarded as schemes [over](wikipedia://en/840950#Category_of_objects_over_A) Spec(*k*).

To every algebraic extension *L* of *k*, the *L*-algebraic set associated to a given *k*-algebraic set *V* is the [fiber product of schemes](wikipedia://en/56444110) *V* ×<sub>Spec(*k*)</sub> Spec(*L*). A *k*-variety is absolutely irreducible if the associated *k*<sup>alg</sup>-algebraic set is an irreducible scheme; in this case, the *k*-variety is called a **variety**. An absolutely irreducible *k*-variety is **defined over *k*** if the associated *k*<sup>alg</sup>-algebraic set is a reduced scheme. A **field of definition** of a variety *V* is a subfield *L* of *k*<sup>alg</sup> such that there exists a *k*∩*L*-variety *W* such that *W* ×<sub>Spec(*k*∩*L*)</sub> Spec(*k*) is isomorphic to *V* and the [final object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Final_object) in the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of reduced schemes over *W* ×<sub>Spec(*k*∩*L*)</sub> Spec(*L*) is an *L*-variety defined over *L*.

Analogously to the definitions for affine and projective varieties, a *k*-variety is a variety defined over *k* if the [stalk](wikipedia://en/245466#Stalks_of_a_sheaf) of the [structure sheaf](wikipedia://en/248014) at the [generic point](wikipedia://en/3043978) is a regular extension of *k*; furthermore, every variety has a minimal field of definition.

One disadvantage of the scheme-theoretic definition is that a scheme over *k* cannot have an [*L*-valued point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_scheme_theory#Points) if *L* is not an extension of *k*. For example, the [rational point](wikipedia://en/9109#Hilbert's_tenth_problem) (1,1,1) is a solution to the equation *x*<sub>1</sub> + i*x*<sub>2</sub> - (1+i)*x*<sub>3</sub> but the corresponding **Q**\[i\]-variety *V* has no Spec(**Q**)-valued point. The two definitions of *field of definition* are also discrepant, e.g. the (scheme-theoretic) minimal field of definition of *V* is **Q**, while in the first definition it would have been **Q**\[i\]. The reason for this discrepancy is that the scheme-theoretic definitions only keep track of the polynomial set *up to change of basis*. In this example, one way to avoid these problems is to use the **Q**-variety Spec(**Q**\[*x*<sub>1</sub>,*x*<sub>2</sub>,*x*<sub>3</sub>\]/(*x*<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup>+ *x*<sub>2</sub><sup>2</sup>+ 2*x*<sub>3</sub><sup>2</sup>- 2*x*<sub>1</sub>*x*<sub>3</sub> - 2*x*<sub>2</sub>*x*<sub>3</sub>)), whose associated **Q**\[i\]-algebraic set is the union of the **Q**\[i\]-variety Spec(**Q**\[i\]\[*x*<sub>1</sub>,*x*<sub>2</sub>,*x*<sub>3</sub>\]/(*x*<sub>1</sub> + i*x*<sub>2</sub> - (1+i)*x*<sub>3</sub>)) and its complex conjugate.

## Action of the absolute Galois group {#Action_of_the_absolute_Galois_group}

The [absolute Galois group](wikipedia://en/2996781) Gal(*k*<sup>alg</sup>/*k*) of *k* naturally [acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) on the zero-locus in **A**<sup>n</sup>(*k*<sup>alg</sup>) of a subset of the polynomial ring *k*\[*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>\]. In general, if *V* is a scheme over *k* (e.g. a *k*-algebraic set), Gal(*k*<sup>alg</sup>/*k*) naturally acts on *V* ×<sub>Spec(*k*)</sub> Spec(*k*<sup>alg</sup>) via its action on Spec(*k*<sup>alg</sup>).

When *V* is a variety defined over a [perfect field](wikipedia://en/505088) *k*, the scheme *V* can be recovered from the scheme *V* ×<sub>Spec(*k*)</sub> Spec(*k*<sup>alg</sup>) together with the action of Gal(*k*<sup>alg</sup>/*k*) on the latter scheme: the sections of the structure sheaf of *V* on an open subset *U* are exactly the [sections](wikipedia://en/245466#Presheaves) of the structure sheaf of *V* ×<sub>Spec(*k*)</sub> Spec(*k*<sup>alg</sup>) on *U* ×<sub>Spec(*k*)</sub> Spec(*k*<sup>alg</sup>) whose [residues](wikipedia://en/73102) are constant on each Gal(*k*<sup>alg</sup>/*k*)-[orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Orbits_and_stabilizers) in *U* ×<sub>Spec(*k*)</sub> Spec(*k*<sup>alg</sup>). In the affine case, this means the action of the absolute Galois group on the zero-locus is sufficient to recover the subset of *k*\[*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>\] consisting of vanishing polynomials.

In general, this information is not sufficient to recover *V*. In the example of the zero-locus of *x*<sub>1</sub><sup>*p*</sup> − *t* in (**F**<sub>*p*</sub>(*t*))<sup>alg</sup>, the variety consists of a single point and so the action of the absolute Galois group cannot distinguish whether the ideal of vanishing polynomials was generated by *x*<sub>1</sub> − *t*<sup>1/*p*</sup>, by *x*<sub>1</sub><sup>*p*</sup> − *t*, or, indeed, by *x*<sub>1</sub> − *t*<sup>1/*p*</sup> raised to some other power of *p*.

For any subfield *L* of *k*<sup>alg</sup> and any *L*-variety *V*, an automorphism σ of *k*<sup>alg</sup> will map *V* isomorphically onto a σ(*L*)-variety.

- Fried, Michael D.; Moshe Jarden. (2005). "Field Arithmetic". Springer. p. 780. doi:10.1007/b138352. isbn:3-540-22811-X
  - The terminology in this article matches the terminology in the text of Fried and Jarden, who adopt Weil's nomenclature for varieties. The second edition reference here also contains a subsection providing a dictionary between this nomenclature and the more modern one of schemes.
- Kunz, Ernst. (1985). "Introduction to Commutative Algebra and Algebraic Geometry". Birkhäuser. p. 256. isbn:0-8176-3065-1
  - Kunz deals strictly with affine and projective varieties and schemes but to some extent covers the relationship between Weil's definitions for varieties and [Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042)'s definitions for schemes.

  - Mumford only spends one section of the book on arithmetic concerns like the field of definition, but in it covers in full generality many scheme-theoretic results stated in this article.
