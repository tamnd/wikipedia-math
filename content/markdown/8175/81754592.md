# Miriam Yevick

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Miriam Yevick"}

**Miriam Yevick** (Lipschutz; August 28, 1924 — September 5, 2018) was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and professor at [Rutgers University College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutgers_University_College). She was the fifth woman to receive a [Doctor of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) from [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology) in math. She immigrated to the United States with her family after fleeing [Nazi Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German-occupied_Europe) in 1940. She is known for her collaboration with [David Bohm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bohm) on his [quantum theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_physics).

:::

## Education and early life {#Education_and_early_life}

Yevick was born in [Scheveningen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheveningen) to prominent [diamond merchant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamonds_as_an_investment) Max Lipschutz.[^o4] She was of Jewish descent.[^o5] She attended [Montessori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montessori_education) school in [the Hague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hague), after which time her family relocated to [Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgium) where she attended [Collège Marie-José](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collège_Marie-José_%28Antwerp%29).[^o6] When she was 16, they immigrated to the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) in 1940 from [Antwerp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerp),[^o8] fleeing the [Nazi regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German-occupied_Europe) and was a survivor of [The Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust).[^o5] The escape took three months.[^o5][^o7] She graduated from [Lycée Français de New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Français_de_New_York) in 1942.[^o8]

She received her [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts) from [New York University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_University) in 1943. She then enrolled at [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology) (MIT) where she earned a [Master of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Science) in [Physics](wikipedia://en/22939) in 1945,[^o6] and in 1947 she became the fifth woman in history to receive a [Doctor of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) in [Mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) from MIT.[^o9] While at MIT, she met her husband, physicist [George Yevick](wikipedia://en/81754589). Their son, [David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Yevick), and granddaughter, [Hannah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannah_Yevick), are also physicists.[^o10]

## Research and career {#Research_and_career}

Yevick began her career at the [National Bureau of Standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Standards) in [Washington, D.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_D.C.),[^o14] shortly after she met [David Bohm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bohm) in 1948,[^o12] at which time they began an affair.[^o14] Yevick and her husband remained in close contact with Bohm, and corresponded with him while he was at [University of São Paulo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_São_Paulo) on his [causal interpretation of quantum theory](wikipedia://en/54717). Bohm requested from Yevick a [mathematical proof](wikipedia://en/82285) to support a statistical mechanics argument that rare exceptions are negligible, such that typical behavior could be treated as physically reliable.[^o13][^o14] On Bohm's [holonomic brain theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holonomic_brain_theory), Yevick postulated that the brain has the capacity to process [waveforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waveform) with a [Fourier logic](wikipedia://en/11659).[^o15]

She held visiting posts at the [University of Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Victoria), [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University), and [City College of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_College_of_New_York).[^o16] In 1964,[^o17] she was appointed to a professorship at [Rutgers University College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutgers_University_College), where she spent the rest of her career teaching [remedial math](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remedial_education) until she retired.[^o16]

On her work *Holographic or fourier logic* (1975),[^o18] cognitive scientists William Benzon and [David G. Hays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_G._Hays) formulated 'Yevick's Law' in 1988.[^o19] Benzon later wrote that Miriam's work provides a foundational framework for understanding [intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence). He argues that her work shows how intelligence depends on choosing between modes of [symbolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_artificial_intelligence) or [holographic](wikipedia://en/66338) computation and that systems of [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence) struggle to balance them.[^o20][^o21]

In 2012, she published a memoir in the form of letters she had written to her first grandchild,[^o20] *A Testament for Ariela*, about her life escaping from a Nazi regime to become a mathematician.[^o9][^o7]
