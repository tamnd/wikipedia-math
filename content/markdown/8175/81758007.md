# Centered coloring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Centered coloring"}

*Image: Centered coloring.svg.* A centered coloring of a graph. Each connected subgraph has a color used by exactly one vertex. The number of colors, four, equals the [tree-depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree-depth) of the graph.

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **centered coloring** is a type of [graph coloring](wikipedia://en/426743) related to [treedepth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treedepth). The minimum number of colors in a centered coloring of a graph equals the graph's [treedepth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treedepth). A parameterized variant, a $q$-centered coloring, provides a way of covering graphs with a small number of [subgraphs](wikipedia://en/325802#subgraph) of treedepth at most $q$, and can be used in algorithms for [subgraph isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subgraph_isomorphism) and related problems. The number of colors needed for a $q$-centered coloring is bounded as a function of $q$ in the graphs of [bounded expansion](wikipedia://en/47843724).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A centered coloring is a graph coloring, an assignment of colors to the vertices of a given graph, with the following property: every connected subgraph (or equivalently every connected [induced subgraph](wikipedia://en/1655188)) has at least one vertex whose color is unique: no other vertex in the same subgraph has the same color.[^n1]

A $q$-centered coloring is a centered coloring with the weaker property that every connected subgraph (or equivalently every connected [induced subgraph](wikipedia://en/1655188)) either has at least $q$ colors, or it has at least one vertex whose color is unique.[^n2] For $q=2$ this is an ordinary graph coloring: every edge, and therefore every larger connected subgraph, must have at least two colors.[^o6] For $q=3$ a $q$-centered coloring is the same thing as a [star coloring](wikipedia://en/20933302): every subgraph with only two colors must be a [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/9938459) of [stars](wikipedia://en/19310129), centered at the uniquely colored vertices of each component. For $q>n$, where $n$ is the number of vertices in the graph, it is impossible to have $q$ colors, and a $q$-centered coloring is the same thing as a centered coloring without the parameter. More strongly, whenever $q$ is at least equal to the treedepth $t$ of a given graph, the number of colors in a $q$-centered coloring is also at least equal to $t$.[^n2]

:::

## Equivalence with treedepth {#Equivalence_with_treedepth}

The minimum number of colors in a centered coloring of a given graph $G$ equals its [tree-depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree-depth), the minimum height of a rooted forest $F$ on the same vertex set as $G$ such that every edge in $G$ connects an ancestor–descendant pair in $F$. In one direction, if $F$ is a forest with this property, a centered coloring with a number of colors equal to its height can be obtained by grouping the vertices of $F$ by distance from the roots of their trees and using one color for each group. In the other direction, from a centered coloring of $G$, one can obtain a forest $F$ with the desired properties, by choosing a tree root with a unique color in each component of $G$, with the children of each root constructed recursively from the connected subgraphs obtained by removing the root from $G$.[^n3]

## Algorithmic application {#Algorithmic_application}

In a $q$-centered coloring, every subset of $k$ colors where $k<q$ induces a subgraph on which the coloring is a centered coloring. This induced subgraph therefore has tree-depth at most $k$.[^n2] By choosing all subsets of a given number of colors, any graph with a $q$-centered coloring can be covered by graphs of bounded tree-depth. In particular, if one wishes to search for copies of a graph $H$ with $h$ vertices as subgraphs of a larger graph $G$ (the subgraph isomorphism problem), and $G$ has an $(h+1)$-centered coloring with $k$ colors, then any copy of $H$ can use at most $h$ of the colors. One can find all copies of $H$ in the subgraphs of $G$ induced by all $h$-tuples of colors. This idea reduces the subgraph isomorphism problem to $O(k^h)$ subproblems, each of which can be solved quickly because of its low tree-depth. The same idea applies also to checking whether $G$ models any first-order formula in the [logic of graphs](wikipedia://en/47857242) that uses only $h$ variables.[^n4]

For this method to be efficient, it is important that the number of colors in a $q$-centered coloring be small. For graphs of [bounded expansion](wikipedia://en/47843724), this number is bounded by a polynomial of $q$ whose (large) exponent depends on the family.[^n5] More generally, for graphs in a [nowhere-dense family of graphs](wikipedia://en/38768125#Classification_of_graph_families), this number is $o(|V(G)|).$ However, for graphs classes that are somewhere dense (that is, not nowhere-dense), no such bound is possible.[^n6] For graphs of bounded degree, the number of colors is $O(q)$, for [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314) it is $O(q^3\log q)$, and for graphs with a forbidden [topological minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_minor) it is polynomial in $q$.[^n7]
