# Synthetic mathematics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Synthetic mathematics"}

**Synthetic mathematics** is an approach to mathematics in which a [mathematical theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_theory) is built around a set of [postulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postulate) specific to the theory. This contrasts with the traditional *analytic* approach, in which the objects studied by the theory are first constructed and their basic properties are deduced.

:::

An early historical example of synthetic mathematics is [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417) as developed in [Euclid's Elements](wikipedia://en/244107), which starts from basic postulates on points, lines, circles and angles. This form of [synthetic geometry](wikipedia://en/267484) is in contrast to [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202), in which points are defined by their coordinates.

In modern terminology of [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), a synthetic approach consists in reasoning about a mathematical structure using an appropriate [internal language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_language). In other words, synthetic mathematics is developed in a given logical system, of which the structure is a [model](wikipedia://en/909888). Theorems proved inside the internal language can then be translated to external theorems about the model. This model is frequently an [elementary topos](wikipedia://en/815802) or an [∞-topos](wikipedia://en/44533341), and hence the synthetic approach requires not only restricting to a set of postulates but also weakening the ambient logic to be [intuitionistic](wikipedia://en/169262). This makes [constructive mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_mathematics) the common basis of much of synthetic mathematics.

An advantage of synthetic mathematics is that once proved synthetically, theorems can become applicable to multiple different models. For example, reasoning in [univalent foundations](wikipedia://en/44554214) yields theorems about arbitrary (∞, 1)-toposes. Furthermore, synthetic approaches have been employed to ease the [formalization of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formalization_of_mathematics).

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- [Homotopy type theory](wikipedia://en/33974223) is a synthetic approach to [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831) using the language of [univalent foundations](wikipedia://en/44554214) to reason about [simplicial sets](wikipedia://en/1027784) and [cubical sets](wikipedia://en/46250924).
- Synthetic computability theory develops [computability theory](wikipedia://en/155414) in constructive mathematics by postulating, among other principles, that there are countably many [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) subsets of the natural numbers. The main model of the theory is the [effective topos](wikipedia://en/30939514), where this axiom corresponds to the basic fact from classical computability theory that there exists an [admissible numbering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admissible_numbering) of [partial computable functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_computable_function).
- Synthetic [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) has been developed in the internal language of [Zariski toposes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski_topos).
- [Synthetic differential geometry](wikipedia://en/3869427)
