# Type A Cipher Machine

*Image: Japanese Navy RED cryptographic device captured by US Navy - National Cryptologic Museum - DSC07868.JPG.* Japanese Navy ORANGE cryptographic device captured by US Navy

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Type A Cipher Machine"}

In the [history of cryptography](wikipedia://en/520066), or *Angōki Taipu-A*, codenamed **Red** by the United States, was a diplomatic cryptographic machine used by the [Japanese Foreign Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Foreign_Office) before and during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II). A relatively simple device, it was quickly broken by western cryptographers. The Red cipher was succeeded by the [Type B "Purple" machine](wikipedia://en/50373) which used some of the same principles. Parallel usage of the two systems assisted in the breaking of the Purple system.

:::

The Red cipher should not be confused with the [Red naval code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_naval_codes#Red_code), which was used by the [Imperial Japanese Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Japanese_Navy) between the wars. The latter was a [codebook](wikipedia://en/530786) system, not a cipher.

## Operation {#Operation}

The Red machine encrypted and decrypted texts written in [Latin characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_character) (alphabetic only) for transmission through the cable services. Per [International Telegraph Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Telegraph_Union) regulations at the time, pronounceable words in telegrams were charged a lower rate than unpronounceable code groups;[^o3] therefore the machine produced [telegraph code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telegraph_code) by enciphering the [vowels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vowel) separately from the [consonants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consonant), so that the text remained a series of syllables.[^o4][^o5] (The letter "Y" was treated as a vowel.) The "sixes and twenties" effect (as American analysts referred to it) was a major weakness which the Japanese continued in the Purple system.

Encryption itself was provided through a single half-rotor; input contacts were through [slip rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slip_ring), each of which connected to a single output contact on the rotor.[^o6] Since both the vowels and consonants were passed through the same rotor, it had sixty contacts (the [least common multiple](wikipedia://en/17961) of six and twenty); wiring ensured that the two groups were kept separate. The slip rings were connected to the input [keyboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphanumeric_keyboard) through a [plugboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plugboard); again this was organized to keep the vowels and consonants separate.[^o6]

The rotor turned at least one step after each letter. The amount of rotation was controlled by the break wheel, which was connected to the rotor, and which had up to forty-seven pins in it. Up to eleven of these pins (in a predetermined set of positions) were removable; in practice, from four to six pins were removed. Rotation of the wheel stopped when the next pin was reached; therefore, if the next pin were removed, the rotor would advance two places instead of one.[^o4] The irregular pattern of rotation produced an [Alberti cipher](wikipedia://en/7900075).[^o6]

## History {#History}

The vulnerability of Japanese code systems was made public in 1931 when [Herbert Yardley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Yardley) published *[The American Black Chamber](wikipedia://en/33084398)*, a popular account of his code breaking activities for the US government in which he discussed the breaking of Japanese codes and their use during the [Washington Naval Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Naval_Conference). These revelations prompted Japanese to look into machine ciphers.[^o8]

The system was introduced in 1930–1931 (the 91 in the designation refers to the [Japanese imperial year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_imperial_year) 2591),[^o9] using a [reverse-engineered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse_engineering) version of a machine supplied by the firm of [Boris Hagelin](wikipedia://en/578033).[^o10] Hagelin's most sophisticated systems were [rotor machines](wikipedia://en/196223) similar to those used in [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), but as he did not trust the Japanese to honor his [patents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patent), he sent a more primitive device designed by [Arvid Damm](wikipedia://en/1386610) instead.[^o10] It was this machine which the Japanese used as the basis for their design; the separate encryption of the vowels, however, was strictly a Japanese contribution.[^o10]

*Image: RED analogs - National Cryptologic Museum - DSC07861.JPG.* Manual and automated means by which Americans deciphered RED messages

The code was broken successfully by three independently working groups. The British solution came first, with [Hugh Foss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Foss) and [Oliver Strachey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Strachey) working out the code in 1934, and [Harold Kenworthy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Kenworthy)'s shop producing a replica, the "J machine", a year later.[^o6][^o9] American attempts to break the system waited until 1935. In the Army [SIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signals_Intelligence_Service) group, the system was broken by [Frank Rowlett](wikipedia://en/477018) and [Solomon Kullback](wikipedia://en/506420); for the navy, [Agnes Driscoll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnes_Driscoll) is generally credited. (She actually solved the [Orange](wikipedia://en/43063354) (or M-1) cipher used by naval attaches, but as it turned out the two systems were essentially the same.) The Americans also constructed a replica machine to expedite solutions; this machine had two half-rotors to solve the vowels and consonants separately.[^o4] The SIS group originally referred to it simply as the "Japanese code machine", but decided that so descriptive a term was a security risk; as it was the first Japanese machine cipher solved, they decided to start at the beginning of the spectrum, and named it "RED".[^o11]

The PURPLE machine began to replace the RED system in 1938, but initial installations were at major posts; less important embassies and consulates continued to use the old system.[^o8] This was one of many deficiencies in Japanese use of encryption that helped make the PURPLE system vulnerable to cracking, for now there was some identical traffic on both systems, which allowed [cribbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crib_%28cryptanalysis%29).[^o8][^o12] A far more serious deficiency was that the PURPLE machine maintained the "sixes/twenties" division, even though the RED machines had since been modified to allow any six letters to be used for the vowel encryption. After eighteen months of work, the PURPLE device was cracked, and produced important intelligence up to the end of the war.

Intelligence results of RED intercepts were not as dramatic, but important intelligence was obtained. For instance, American cryptanalysts were able to provide details of the [Tripartite Pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripartite_Pact) between the Axis powers.[^o8][^o13] Reports of the [sea trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_trial) of the [battleship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battleship) *[Nagato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_battleship_Nagato)* were also decoded, leading to important changes to the [USS *North Carolina* (BB-55)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_North_Carolina_%28BB-55%29), then being designed, in order to match the performance of the Japanese ship.[^o5]
