# Lens space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lens space"}

*Image: Lens Space L(2;5) Side View.png.* The lens space L(2;5) consists of the "lens" between the red and yellow walls using a double rotation that aligns the slits. Five "lens" regions are shown in the picture in total.

*Image: Lens Space L(2;5) Animation.gif.* The double-rotation that identifies the walls of the lens space. In this stereographic view, the double-rotation rotates both around the z-axis and along it.

A **lens space** is an example of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), considered in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831). The term often refers to a specific class of [3-manifolds](wikipedia://en/1018257), but in general can be defined for higher dimensions.

:::

In the 3-manifold case, a lens space can be visualized as the result of gluing two [solid tori](wikipedia://en/1251473) together by a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) of their boundaries. Often the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792) and $S^2 \times S^1$, both of which can be obtained as above, are not counted as they are considered trivial special cases.

The three-dimensional lens spaces $L(p;q)$ were introduced by [Heinrich Tietze](wikipedia://en/1368572) in 1908. They were the first known examples of 3-manifolds which were not determined by their [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) and [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) alone, and the simplest examples of closed manifolds whose homeomorphism type is not determined by their homotopy type. [J. W. Alexander](wikipedia://en/9848523) in 1919 showed that the lens spaces $L(5;1)$ and $L(5;2)$ were not homeomorphic even though they have isomorphic fundamental groups and the same homology, though they do not have the same homotopy type. Other lens spaces (such as $L(7;1)$ and $L(7;2)$) have even the same homotopy type (and thus isomorphic fundamental groups and homology), but not the same homeomorphism type; they can thus be seen as the birth of [geometric topology](wikipedia://en/621732) of manifolds as distinct from [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801).

There is a complete classification of three-dimensional lens spaces, by [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) and [Reidemeister torsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reidemeister_torsion).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The three-dimensional lens spaces $L(p;q)$ are quotients of $S^3$ by $\mathbb{Z}/p$-actions. More precisely, let $p$ and $q$ be [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) integers and consider $S^3$ as the [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090) in $\mathbb{C}^2$. Then the $\mathbb{Z}/p$-action on $S^3$ generated by the homeomorphism

$$(z_1,z_2) \mapsto (e^{2\pi i /p} \cdot z_1, e^{2\pi i q/p}\cdot z_2)$$

is free. The resulting [quotient space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Orbits_and_stabilizers) is called the **lens space** $L(p;q)$.

This can be generalized to higher dimensions as follows: Let $p,q_1,\ldots,q_n$ be integers such that the $q_i$ are coprime to $p$ and consider $S^{2n-1}$ as the unit sphere in $\mathbb C^n$. The lens space $L(p;q_1,\ldots q_n)$ is the quotient of $S^{2n-1}$ by the free $\mathbb Z/p$-action generated by

$$(z_1,\ldots,z_n) \mapsto (e^{2\pi iq_1/p} \cdot z_1,\ldots, e^{2\pi i q_n/p}\cdot z_n).$$

In three dimensions we have $L(p;q)=L(p;1,q).$

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

The fundamental group of all the lens spaces $L(p;q_1,\ldots, q_n)$ is $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ independent of the $q_i$.

The homology of the lens space $L(p;q_1,\ldots, q_n)$ is given by[^o6]

$$H_k(L(p;q_1,\ldots, q_n)) = \begin{cases} \mathbb{Z} & \text{for }k = 0, 2n-1 \\ \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} & \text{for }k\text{ odd, }0<k<2n-1 \\ \{0\} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Lens spaces are [locally symmetric spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_symmetric_space), but not (fully) symmetric, with the exception of $L(2;1)$, three-dimensional [real projective space](wikipedia://en/1014694), which is symmetric. (Locally symmetric spaces are symmetric spaces that are quotiented by an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) that has no fixed points; lens spaces meet this definition.)

## Alternative definitions of three-dimensional lens spaces {#Alternative_definitions_of_three-dimensional_lens_spaces}

*Image: Topological\_lens\_space.png.* A topological lens space.

The three dimensional lens space $L(p;q)$ is often defined to be a solid ball with the following identification: first mark *p* equally spaced points on the equator of the solid ball, denote them $a_0$ to $a_{p-1}$, then on the boundary of the ball, draw geodesic lines connecting the points to the north and south pole. Now identify spherical triangles by identifying the north pole to the south pole and the points $a_i$ with $a_{i+q}$ and $a_{i+1}$ with $a_{i+q+1}$. The resulting space is homeomorphic to the lens space $L(p;q)$.

Another related definition is to view the solid ball as the following solid [bipyramid](wikipedia://en/4153): construct a planar regular *p* sided [polygon](wikipedia://en/333306). Put two points *n* and *s* directly above and below the center of the polygon. Construct the bipyramid by joining each point of the regular *p* sided polygon to *n* and *s*. Fill in the bipyramid to make it solid and give the triangles on the boundary the same identification as above.

## Classification of 3-dimensional lens spaces {#Classification_of_3-dimensional_lens_spaces}

Classifications up to homeomorphism and homotopy equivalence are known, as follows. The three-dimensional spaces $L(p;q_1)$ and $L(p;q_2)$ are:

1. homotopy equivalent if and only if $q_1 q_2 \equiv \pm n^2 \pmod{p}$ for some $n \in \mathbb{N}$;
1. homeomorphic if and only if $q_1 \equiv \pm q_2^{\pm 1} \pmod{p}$.

If $q_1 \equiv \pm q_2^{\pm 1} \pmod{p}$ as in case 2., they are "obviously" homeomorphic, as one can easily produce a homeomorphism. It is harder to show that these are the only homeomorphic lens spaces.

The invariant that gives the homotopy classification of 3-dimensional lens spaces is the [torsion linking form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion_linking_form).

The homeomorphism classification is more subtle, and is given by [Reidemeister torsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reidemeister_torsion). This was given in (Reidemeister 1935) as a classification up to [PL homeomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PL_homeomorphism), but it was shown in (Brody 1960) to be a homeomorphism classification. In modern terms, lens spaces are determined by [*simple* homotopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_homotopy) type, and there are no [normal invariants](wikipedia://en/21298614) (like [characteristic classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_classes)) or [surgery obstruction](wikipedia://en/21298802).

A [knot-theoretic](wikipedia://en/153008) classification is given in (Przytycki Yasukhara 2003): let *C* be a closed curve in the lens space which lifts to a knot in the universal cover of the lens space. If the lifted knot has a trivial [Alexander polynomial](wikipedia://en/1107647), compute the torsion linking form on the pair (C,C) – then this gives the homeomorphism classification.

Another invariant is the homotopy type of the [configuration spaces](wikipedia://en/54981574) – (Salvatore Longoni 2005) showed that homotopy equivalent but not homeomorphic lens spaces may have configuration spaces with different homotopy types, which can be detected by different [Massey products](wikipedia://en/2676948).
