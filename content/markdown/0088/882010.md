# Cayley transform

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cayley transform"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Cayley transform**, named after [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034), is any of a cluster of related things. As originally described by (Cayley 1846), the Cayley transform is a mapping between [skew-symmetric matrices](wikipedia://en/174055) and [special orthogonal matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_matrix). The transform is a [homography](wikipedia://en/3051962) used in [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478), [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), and [quaternionic analysis](wikipedia://en/23779912). In the theory of [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932), the Cayley transform is a mapping between [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) (Nikolski 1988).

:::

## Real homography {#Real_homography}

A simple example of a Cayley transform can be done on the [real projective line](wikipedia://en/46379102). The Cayley transform here will permute the elements of {1, 0, −1, ∞} in sequence. For example, it maps the [positive real numbers](wikipedia://en/47172571) to the interval \[−1, 1\]. Thus the Cayley transform is used to adapt [Legendre polynomials](wikipedia://en/100349) for use with functions on the positive real numbers with [Legendre rational functions](wikipedia://en/6329366).

As a real [homography](wikipedia://en/3051962), points are described with [projective coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_coordinates), and the mapping is

$$[y,\ 1] = \left[\frac {x - 1}{x +1},\ 1\right] \thicksim [x - 1, \ x + 1] = [x,\ 1]\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .$$

## Complex homography {#Complex_homography}

*Image: Cayley transform in complex plane.png.* Cayley transform of upper complex half-plane to unit disk

On the [upper half](wikipedia://en/287826) of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), the Cayley transform is:[^o6][^o7]

$$f(z) = \frac {z - i}{z + i} .$$

Since $\{\infty, 1, -1\}$ is mapped to $\{1, -i, i\}$, and [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493) permute the [generalised circles](wikipedia://en/10766404) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), $f$ maps the real line to the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071). Furthermore, since $f$ is a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) and $i$ is taken to 0 by $f$, the upper half-plane is mapped to the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738).

In terms of the [models](wikipedia://en/20590) of [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), this Cayley transform relates the [Poincaré half-plane model](wikipedia://en/461942) to the [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836).

In electrical engineering the Cayley transform has been used to map a [reactance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_reactance) half-plane to the [Smith chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smith_chart) used for [impedance matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impedance_matching) of transmission lines.

## Quaternion homography {#Quaternion_homography}

In the [four-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/1364622) of [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) $a+b\vec{i}+c\vec{j}+d\vec{k}$, the [versors](wikipedia://en/2644621)

$u(\theta, r) = \cos \theta + r \sin \theta$ form the unit [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792).

Since quaternions are non-commutative, elements of its [projective line](wikipedia://en/1271901) have homogeneous coordinates written $U[a,b]$ to indicate that the homogeneous factor multiplies on the left. The quaternion transform is

$$f(u,q) = U[q,1]\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\ -u & u \end{pmatrix} = U[q - u,\ q + u] \sim U[(q + u)^{-1}(q - u),\ 1].$$

The real and complex homographies described above are instances of the quaternion homography where $\theta$ is zero or $\pi/2$, respectively. Evidently the transform takes $u\to 0\to -1$ and takes $-u \to \infty \to 1$.

Evaluating this homography at $q=1$ maps the versor $u$ into its axis:

$$f(u,1) =(1+u)^{-1}(1-u) = (1+u)^*(1-u)/ |1+u|^2.$$

But $|1+u|^2 = (1+u)(1+u^*) = 2 + 2 \cos \theta ,\quad \text{and}\quad (1+u^*)(1-u) = -2 r \sin \theta .$

Thus $f(u,1) = -r \frac {\sin \theta}{1 + \cos \theta} = -r \tan \frac{\theta}{2} .$

In this form the Cayley transform has been described as a rational parametrization of rotation: Let $t=\tan\phi/2$ in the complex number identity[^o9]

$$e^{-i \varphi} = \frac{1 - ti}{1 + ti}$$

where the right hand side is the transform of $ti$ and the left hand side represents the rotation of the plane by negative $\phi$ radians.

### Inverse {#Inverse}

Let $u^* = \cos \theta - r \sin \theta = u^{-1} .$ Since

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -u & u \end{pmatrix}\ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -u^* \\ 1 & u^* \end{pmatrix} \ = \ \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \ \sim \ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \ ,$$

where the equivalence is in the [projective linear group](wikipedia://en/382667) over quaternions, the [inverse](wikipedia://en/14907) of $f(u,1)$ is

$$U[p,1] \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -u^* \\ 1 & u^* \end{pmatrix} \ = \ U[p+1,\ (1-p)u^*] \sim U[u(1-p)^{-1} (p+1), \ 1] .$$

Since homographies are [bijections](wikipedia://en/3942), $f^{-1} (u,1)$ maps the vector quaternions to the 3-sphere of versors. As versors represent rotations in 3-space, the homography $f^{-1}$ produces rotations from the ball in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

## Matrix map {#Matrix_map}

Among *n*×*n* [square matrices](wikipedia://en/166022) over the [reals](wikipedia://en/20646438), with *I* the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718), let *A* be any [skew-symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/174055) (so that *A*<sup>T</sup> = −*A*).

Then *I* + *A* is [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122), and the Cayley transform

$$Q = (I - A)(I + A)^{-1} \,\!$$

produces an [orthogonal matrix](wikipedia://en/105620), *Q* (so that *Q*<sup>T</sup>*Q* = *I*). The matrix multiplication in the definition of *Q* above is commutative, so *Q* can be alternatively defined as $Q = (I + A)^{-1}(I - A)$. In fact, *Q* must have determinant +1, so is special orthogonal.

Conversely, let *Q* be any orthogonal matrix which does not have −1 as an [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue); then

$$A = (I - Q)(I + Q)^{-1} \,\!$$

is a skew-symmetric matrix. (See also: [Involution](wikipedia://en/293450).) The condition on *Q* automatically excludes matrices with determinant −1, but also excludes certain special orthogonal matrices.

However, any rotation (special orthogonal) matrix *Q* can be written as

$$Q = \bigl((I - A)(I + A)^{-1}\bigr)^2$$

for some skew-symmetric matrix *A*; more generally any orthogonal matrix *Q* can be written as

$$Q = E(I - A)(I + A)^{-1}$$

for some skew-symmetric matrix *A* and some diagonal matrix *E* with ±1 as entries.[^o12]

A slightly different form is also seen,[^o13][^o14] requiring different mappings in each direction,

$$\begin{aligned} Q &= (I - A)^{-1}(I + A), \\[5mu] A &= (Q - I)(Q + I)^{-1}. \end{aligned}$$

The mappings may also be written with the order of the factors reversed;[^o15][^o16] however, *A* always commutes with (μ*I* ± *A*)<sup>−1</sup>, so the reordering does not affect the definition.

### Examples {#Examples}

In the 2×2 case, we have

$$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & \tan \frac{\theta}{2} \\ -\tan \frac{\theta}{2} & 0 \end{bmatrix} \leftrightarrow \begin{bmatrix} \cos \theta & -\sin \theta \\ \sin \theta & \cos \theta \end{bmatrix} .$$

The 180° [rotation matrix](wikipedia://en/856005), −*I*, is excluded, though it is the limit as tan <sup>θ</sup>⁄<sub>2</sub> goes to infinity.

In the 3×3 case, we have

$$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & z & -y \\ -z & 0 & x \\ y & -x & 0 \end{bmatrix} \leftrightarrow \frac{1}{K} \begin{bmatrix} w^2+x^2-y^2-z^2 & 2 (x y-w z) & 2 (w y+x z) \\ 2 (x y+w z) & w^2-x^2+y^2-z^2 & 2 (y z-w x) \\ 2 (x z-w y) & 2 (w x+y z) & w^2-x^2-y^2+z^2 \end{bmatrix} ,$$

where *K* = *w*<sup>2</sup> + *x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> + *z*<sup>2</sup>, and where *w* = 1. This we recognize as the rotation matrix corresponding to [quaternion](wikipedia://en/51440)

$$w + \mathbf{i} x + \mathbf{j} y + \mathbf{k} z \,\!$$

(by a formula Cayley had published the year before), except scaled so that *w* = 1 instead of the usual scaling so that *w*<sup>2</sup> + *x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> + *z*<sup>2</sup> = 1. Thus vector (*x*,*y*,*z*) is the unit axis of rotation scaled by tan <sup>θ</sup>⁄<sub>2</sub>. Again excluded are 180° rotations, which in this case are all *Q* which are [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474) (so that *Q*<sup>T</sup> = *Q*).

:::

### Other matrices {#Other_matrices}

One can extend the mapping to [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) matrices by substituting "[unitary](wikipedia://en/129222)" for "orthogonal" and "[skew-Hermitian](wikipedia://en/509788)" for "skew-symmetric", the difference being that the transpose (·<sup>T</sup>) is replaced by the [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) (·<sup>H</sup>). This is consistent with replacing the standard real [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) with the standard complex inner product. In fact, one may extend the definition further with choices of [adjoint](wikipedia://en/667175) other than transpose or conjugate transpose.

Formally, the definition only requires some invertibility, so one can substitute for *Q* any matrix *M* whose eigenvalues do not include −1. For example,

$$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -a & ab - c \\ 0 & 0 & -b \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \leftrightarrow \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2a & 2c \\ 0 & 1 & 2b \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} .$$

Note that *A* is skew-symmetric (respectively, skew-Hermitian) if and only if *Q* is orthogonal (respectively, unitary) with no eigenvalue −1.

## Operator map {#Operator_map}

An infinite-dimensional version of an [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856) is a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), and one can no longer speak of [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859). However, matrices are merely representations of [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator), and these can be used. So, generalizing both the matrix mapping and the complex plane mapping, one may define a Cayley transform of operators.[^n1]

$$\begin{aligned} U &{}= (A - \mathbf{i}I) (A + \mathbf{i}I)^{-1} \\ A &{}= \mathbf{i}(I + U) (I - U)^{-1} \end{aligned}$$

Here the domain of *U*, dom *U*, is (*A*+**i***I*) dom *A*. See [self-adjoint operator](wikipedia://en/187408#Extensions_of_symmetric_operators) for further details.
