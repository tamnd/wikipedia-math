# Friedrichs extension

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Friedrichs extension"}

In [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), the **Friedrichs extension** is a [canonical](wikipedia://en/515096) [self-adjoint](wikipedia://en/187408) extension of a non-negative densely defined [symmetric operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_operator). It is named after the [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Kurt Friedrichs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Friedrichs). This extension is particularly useful in situations where an operator may fail to be [essentially self-adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essentially_self-adjoint) or whose essential self-adjointness is difficult to show.

:::

An operator *T* is non-negative if

$$\langle \xi \mid T \xi \rangle \geq 0, \;\; \forall \; \xi \in \operatorname{dom}\ T$$

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

::: {#o4 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example**. Multiplication by a non-negative function on an *L*<sup>2</sup> space is a non-negative self-adjoint operator.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example**. Let *U* be an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) in **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. On *L*<sup>2</sup>(*U*) we consider [differential operators](wikipedia://en/263886) of the form

$$[T \phi](x) = -\sum_{i,j} \partial_{x_i} \{a_{i j}(x) \partial_{x_j} \phi(x)\} \quad x \in U, \phi \in \operatorname{C}_c^\infty(U),$$

where the functions *a*<sub>*i j*</sub> are infinitely differentiable real-valued functions on *U*. We consider *T* acting on the dense subspace of infinitely differentiable complex-valued functions of compact support, in symbols

$$\operatorname{C}_c^\infty(U) \subseteq L^2(U).$$

If for each *x* ∈ *U* the *n* × *n* matrix

$$\begin{bmatrix} a_{1 1}(x) & a_{1 2}(x) & \cdots & a_{1 n}(x) \\ a_{2 1}(x) & a_{2 2} (x) & \cdots & a_{2 n}(x) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{n 1}(x) & a_{n 2}(x) & \cdots & a_{n n}(x) \end{bmatrix}$$

is non-negative semi-definite, then *T* is a non-negative operator. This means (a) that the matrix is [hermitian](wikipedia://en/189682) and

$$\sum_{i, j} a_{i j }(x) c_i \overline{c_j} \geq 0$$

for every choice of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) *c*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *c*<sub>n</sub>. This is proved using [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252).

These operators are [elliptic](wikipedia://en/550137) although in general elliptic operators may not be non-negative. They are however bounded from below.

:::

:::

## Definition of Friedrichs extension {#Definition_of_Friedrichs_extension}

The definition of the Friedrichs extension is based on the theory of closed positive forms on [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932). If *T* is non-negative, then

$$\operatorname{Q}(\xi, \eta) = \langle \xi \mid T \eta \rangle + \langle \xi \mid \eta \rangle$$

is a [sesquilinear form](wikipedia://en/860138) on dom *T* and

$$\operatorname{Q}(\xi, \xi) = \langle \xi \mid T \xi\rangle + \langle \xi \mid \xi \rangle \geq \|\xi\|^2.$$

Thus Q defines an inner product on dom *T*. Let *H*<sub>1</sub> be the [completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) of dom *T* with respect to Q. *H*<sub>1</sub> is an abstractly defined space; for instance its elements can be represented as [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of [Cauchy sequences](wikipedia://en/6085) of elements of dom *T*. It is not obvious that all elements in *H*<sub>1</sub> can be identified with elements of *H*. However, the following can be proved:

The canonical inclusion

$$\operatorname{dom} T \rightarrow H$$

extends to an *injective* continuous map *H*<sub>1</sub> → *H*. We regard *H*<sub>1</sub> as a subspace of *H*.

Define an operator *A* by

$$\operatorname{dom}\ A = \{\xi \in H_1: \phi_\xi: \eta \mapsto \operatorname{Q}(\xi, \eta) \mbox{ is bounded linear.} \}$$

In the above formula, *bounded* is relative to the topology on *H*<sub>1</sub> inherited from *H*. By the [Riesz representation theorem](wikipedia://en/26452) applied to the linear functional φ<sub>ξ</sub> extended to *H*, there is a unique *A* ξ ∈ *H* such that

$$\operatorname{Q}(\xi,\eta) = \langle A \xi \mid \eta \rangle \quad \eta \in H_1$$

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem**[^o9]. *A* is a non-negative self-adjoint operator such that *T*<sub>1</sub>=*A* - I extends *T*.

*T*<sub>1</sub> is called the **Friedrichs extension** of *T*.

Another way to obtain this extension is as follows. Let :$L:H_1\rightarrow H$ be the bounded inclusion operator. The inclusion is a bounded injective with dense image. Hence $LL^*:H\rightarrow H$ is a bounded injective operator with dense image, where $L^*$ is the adjoint of $L$ as an operator between abstract Hilbert spaces. Therefore, the operator $A:=(LL^*)^{-1}$ is a non-negative self-adjoint operator whose domain is the image of $LL^*$. Then $A-I$ extends T.

:::

:::

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Krein's theorem on non-negative self-adjoint extensions"}

## Krein's theorem on non-negative self-adjoint extensions {#Krein's_theorem_on_non-negative_self-adjoint_extensions}

[M. G. Krein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._G._Krein) has given an elegant characterization of all non-negative self-adjoint extensions of a non-negative symmetric operator *T*.

If *T*, *S* are non-negative self-adjoint operators, write

$$T \leq S$$

if, and only if,

- $$\operatorname{dom}(S^{1/2}) \subseteq \operatorname{dom}(T^{1/2})$$
- $$\langle T^{1/2} \xi \mid T^{1/2} \xi \rangle \leq \langle S^{1/2} \xi \mid S^{1/2} \xi \rangle \quad \forall \xi \in \operatorname{dom}(S^{1/2})$$

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem** [^o13]. There are unique self-adjoint extensions *T*<sub>min</sub> and *T*<sub>max</sub> of any non-negative symmetric operator *T* such that every non-negative self-adjoint extension *S* of *T* is between *T*<sub>min</sub> and *T*<sub>max</sub>, i.e.

$$T_{\mathrm{min}} \leq S \leq T_{\mathrm{max}}.$$

$T_{\mathrm{max}}$ is the Friedrichs extension, while $T_{\mathrm{min}}$ is called the **Krein extension**.

:::
