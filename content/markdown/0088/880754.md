# Restricted representation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Restricted representation"}

In [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), **restriction** forms a [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) of a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) using a known representation of the whole [group](wikipedia://en/19447). Restriction is a fundamental construction in representation theory of groups. Often the restricted representation is simpler to understand. Rules for decomposing the restriction of an [irreducible representation](wikipedia://en/385982) into irreducible representations of the subgroup are called **branching rules**, and have important applications in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939). For example, in case of [explicit symmetry breaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explicit_symmetry_breaking), the [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) of the problem is reduced from the whole group to one of its subgroups. In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), this reduction in symmetry appears as a splitting of [degenerate energy levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_energy_levels) into [multiplets](wikipedia://en/12416124), as in the [Stark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stark_effect) or [Zeeman effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeeman_effect).

:::

The [induced representation](wikipedia://en/357452) is a related operation that forms a representation of the whole group from a representation of a subgroup. The relation between restriction and induction is described by [Frobenius reciprocity](wikipedia://en/55690071) and the Mackey theorem. Restriction to a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) behaves particularly well and is often called [Clifford theory](wikipedia://en/4777875) after the theorem of A. H. Clifford.[^o2] Restriction can be generalized to other [group homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/12396) and to other [rings](wikipedia://en/48404).

For any group *G*, its [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) *H*, and a [linear representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_representation) *ρ* of *G*, the restriction of *ρ* to *H*, denoted

$$\rho \, \Big|_H$$

is a representation of *H* on the same [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) by the same operators:

$$\rho\,\Big|_H(h) = \rho(h).$$

## Classical branching rules {#Classical_branching_rules}

**Classical branching rules** describe the restriction of an irreducible [complex representation](wikipedia://en/695060) ($\pi$, *V*) of a [classical group](wikipedia://en/10172878) *G* to a classical subgroup *H*, i.e. the multiplicity with which an irreducible representation (*σ*, *W*) of *H* occurs in $\pi$. By Frobenius reciprocity for [compact groups](wikipedia://en/750326), this is equivalent to finding the multiplicity of $\pi$ in the [unitary representation induced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induced_representations) from σ. Branching rules for the classical groups were determined by

- (Weyl 1946) between successive [unitary groups](wikipedia://en/173993);
- (Murnaghan 1938) between successive [special orthogonal groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) and [unitary symplectic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_symplectic_group);
- (Littlewood 1950) from the unitary groups to the unitary symplectic groups and special orthogonal groups.

The results are usually expressed graphically using [Young diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_diagram) to encode the signatures used classically to label irreducible representations, familiar from [classical invariant theory](wikipedia://en/363762). [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) and [Richard Brauer](wikipedia://en/605134) discovered a systematic method for determining the branching rule when the groups *G* and *H* share a common [maximal torus](wikipedia://en/360136): in this case the [Weyl group](wikipedia://en/296332) of *H* is a subgroup of that of *G*, so that the rule can be deduced from the [Weyl character formula](wikipedia://en/5011916).[^o7][^o8] A systematic modern interpretation has been given by (Howe 1995) in the context of his theory of [dual pairs](wikipedia://en/11802934). The special case where σ is the [trivial representation](wikipedia://en/450573) of *H* was first used extensively by [Hua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hua_Loo-keng) in his work on the [Szegő kernels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproducing_kernel) of [bounded symmetric domains](wikipedia://en/4127357) in [several complex variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Several_complex_variables), where the [Shilov boundary](wikipedia://en/13520887) has the form *G*/*H*.[^o10][^o11] More generally the [Cartan-Helgason theorem](wikipedia://en/17708915#Cartan–Helgason_theorem) gives the decomposition when *G*/*H* is a compact symmetric space, in which case all multiplicities are one;[^o12] a generalization to arbitrary σ has since been obtained by (Kostant 2004). Similar geometric considerations have also been used by (Knapp 2003) to rederive Littlewood's rules, which involve the celebrated [Littlewood–Richardson rules](wikipedia://en/16004359) for tensoring irreducible representations of the unitary groups. (Littelmann 1995) has found generalizations of these rules to arbitrary compact semisimple [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945), using his [path model](wikipedia://en/11167326), an approach to representation theory close in spirit to the theory of [crystal bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_basis) of [Lusztig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Lusztig) and [Kashiwara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashiwara). His methods yield branching rules for restrictions to subgroups containing a maximal torus. The study of branching rules is important in classical invariant theory and its modern counterpart, [algebraic combinatorics](wikipedia://en/14835582).[^o16][^o17]

::: {#o18 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example**. The unitary group *U*(*N*) has irreducible representations labelled by signatures

$$\mathbf{f} \,\colon \,f_1\ge f_2\ge \cdots \ge f_N$$

where the *f*<sub>*i*</sub> are integers. In fact if a unitary matrix *U* has eigenvalues *z*<sub>*i*</sub>, then the character of the corresponding irreducible representation $\pi$<sub>**f**</sub> is given by

$$\operatorname{Tr} \pi_{\mathbf{f}}(U) = {\det z_j^{f_i +N -i}\over \prod_{i<j} (z_i-z_j)}.$$

The branching rule from *U*(*N*) to *U*(*N* – 1) states that

{| border="1" cellspacing="0" cellpadding="5"

\|$\pi_{\mathbf{f}}|_{U(N-1)}= \bigoplus_{f_1\ge g_1 \ge f_2\ge g_2\ge \cdots \ge f_{N-1}\ge g_{N-1}\ge f_N} \pi_{\mathbf{g}}$ |}

:::

::: {#o19 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example**. The unitary symplectic group or [quaternionic unitary group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternionic_unitary_group), denoted Sp(*N*) or *U*(*N*, **H**), is the group of all transformations of **H**<sup>*N*</sup> which commute with right multiplication by the [quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternions) **H** and preserve the **H**-valued hermitian inner product

$$(q_1,\ldots,q_N)\cdot (r_1,\ldots,r_N) = \sum r_i^*q_i$$

on **H**<sup>*N*</sup>, where *q*\* denotes the quaternion conjugate to *q*. Realizing quaternions as 2 x 2 complex matrices, the group Sp(*N*) is just the group of [block matrices](wikipedia://en/457464) (*q*<sub>*ij*</sub>) in SU(2*N*) with

$$q_{ij}=\begin{pmatrix} \alpha_{ij}&\beta_{ij}\\ -\overline{\beta}_{ij}&\overline{\alpha}_{ij} \end{pmatrix},$$

where *α*<sub>*ij*</sub> and *β*<sub>*ij*</sub> are [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826).

Each matrix *U* in Sp(*N*) is conjugate to a block [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080) with entries

$$q_i=\begin{pmatrix} z_i&0\\ 0&\overline{z}_i \end{pmatrix},$$

where |*z*<sub>*i*</sub>| = 1. Thus the eigenvalues of *U* are (*z*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>±1</sup>). The irreducible representations of Sp(*N*) are labelled by signatures

$$\mathbf{f} \,\colon \,f_1\ge f_2\ge \cdots \ge f_N\ge 0$$

where the *f*<sub>*i*</sub> are integers. The character of the corresponding irreducible representation *σ*<sub>**f**</sub> is given by[^o20]

$$\operatorname{Tr} \sigma_{\mathbf{f}}(U) = {\det z_j^{f_i +N -i +1 } - z_j^{-f_i - N +i -1}\over \prod (z_i-z_i^{-1})\cdot \prod_{i<j} (z_i +z_i^{-1} - z_j - z_j^{-1})}.$$

The branching rule from Sp(*N*) to Sp(*N* – 1) states that[^o21]

{| border="1" cellspacing="0" cellpadding="5"

\|$\sigma_{\mathbf{f}}|_{\mathrm{Sp}(N-1)}= \bigoplus_{f_i \ge g_i\ge f_{i+2}} m(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}) \sigma_{\mathbf{g}}$ |}

Here *f*<sub>*N* + 1</sub> = 0 and the [multiplicity](wikipedia://en/19211790) *m*(**f**, **g**) is given by

$$m(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g})=\prod_{i=1}^N (a_i - b_i +1)$$

where

$$a_1\ge b_1 \ge a_2 \ge b_2 \ge \cdots \ge a_N \ge b_N=0$$

is the non-increasing rearrangement of the 2*N* non-negative integers (*f*<sub>i</sub>), (*g*<sub>*j*</sub>) and 0.

:::

::: {#o22 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example**. The branching from U(2*N*) to Sp(*N*) relies on two identities of [Littlewood](wikipedia://en/7603792):[^o23][^o24][^o25][^o26]

$$\begin{aligned} & \sum_{f_1\ge f_2\ge f_N\ge 0} \operatorname{Tr}\Pi_{\mathbf{f},0}(z_1,z_1^{-1},\ldots, z_N,z_N^{-1}) \cdot \operatorname{Tr}\pi_{\mathbf{f}}(t_1,\ldots,t_N) \\[5pt] = {} & \sum_{f_1\ge f_2\ge f_N\ge 0} \operatorname{Tr}\sigma_{\mathbf{f}}(z_1,\ldots, z_N) \cdot \operatorname{Tr}\pi_{\mathbf{f}}(t_1,\ldots,t_N)\cdot \prod_{i<j} (1-z_iz_j)^{-1}, \end{aligned}$$

where Π<sub>**f**,0</sub> is the irreducible representation of *U*(2*N*) with signature *f*<sub>1</sub> ≥ ··· ≥ *f*<sub>*N*</sub> ≥ 0 ≥ ··· ≥ 0.

$$\prod_{i<j} (1-z_iz_j)^{-1} = \sum_{f_{2i-1}=f_{2i}} \operatorname{Tr} \pi_{f}(z_1,\ldots,z_N),$$

where *f*<sub>*i*</sub> ≥ 0.

The branching rule from U(2*N*) to Sp(*N*) is given by

{| border="1" cellspacing="0" cellpadding="5"

\|$\Pi_{\mathbf{f},0}|_{\mathrm{Sp}(N)}= \bigoplus_{\mathbf{h}, \,\,\mathbf{g},\,\, g_{2i-1}=g_{2i}} M(\mathbf{g}, \mathbf{h};\mathbf{f}) \sigma_{\mathbf{h}}$ |}

where all the signature are non-negative and the coefficient *M* (**g**, **h**; **k**) is the multiplicity of the irreducible representation $\pi$<sub>**k**</sub> of *U*(*N*) in the tensor product $\pi$<sub>**g**</sub> $\otimes$ $\pi$<sub>**h**</sub>. It is given combinatorially by the Littlewood–Richardson rule, the number of lattice permutations of the [skew diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew_tableau#Skew_tableaux) **k**/**h** of weight **g**.[^o17]

There is an extension of Littlewood's branching rule to arbitrary signatures due to (Sundaram 1990, 203). The Littlewood–Richardson coefficients *M* (**g**, **h**; **f**) are extended to allow the signature **f** to have 2*N* parts but restricting **g** to have even column-lengths (*g*<sub>2*i* – 1</sub> = *g*<sub>2*i*</sub>). In this case the formula reads

{| border="1" cellspacing="0" cellpadding="5"

\|$\Pi_{\mathbf{f}}|_{\operatorname{Sp}(N)}= \bigoplus_{\mathbf{h}, \,\,\mathbf{g},\,\, g_{2i-1}=g_{2i}} M_N(\mathbf{g}, \mathbf{h};\mathbf{f}) \sigma_{\mathbf{h}}$ |}

where *M*<sub>*N*</sub> (**g**, **h**; **f**) counts the number of lattice permutations of **f**/**h** of weight **g** are counted for which 2*j* + 1 appears no lower than row *N* + *j* of **f** for 1 ≤ *j* ≤ |*g*|/2.

:::

::: {#o28 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example**. The special orthogonal group SO(*N*) has irreducible ordinary and [spin representations](wikipedia://en/10160091) labelled by signatures[^o7][^o16][^o29][^o30]

- $f_1\ge f_2 \ge \cdots \ge f_{n-1}\ge|f_n|$ for *N* = 2*n*;
- $f_1 \ge f_2 \ge \cdots \ge f_n \ge 0$ for *N* = 2*n*+1.

The *f*<sub>*i*</sub> are taken in **Z** for ordinary representations and in ½ + **Z** for spin representations. In fact if an orthogonal matrix *U* has eigenvalues *z*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>±1</sup> for 1 ≤ *i* ≤ *n*, then the character of the corresponding irreducible representation $\pi$<sub>**f**</sub> is given by

$$\operatorname{Tr} \, \pi_{\mathbf{f}}(U) = {\det (z_j^{f_i +n -i} + z_j^{-f_i-n +i}) \over \prod_{i<j} (z_i +z_i^{-1}-z_j-z_j^{-1})}$$

for *N* = 2*n* and by

$$\operatorname{Tr} \pi_{\mathbf{f}}(U) = {\det (z_j^{f_i +1/2 +n -i} - z_j^{-f_i -1/2-n +i})\over \prod_{i<j} (z_i +z_i^{-1}-z_j-z_j^{-1}) \cdot\prod_k(z_k^{1/2} -z_k^{-1/2})}$$

for *N* = 2*n*+1.

The branching rules from SO(*N*) to SO(*N* – 1) state that[^o31]

{| border="1" cellspacing="0" cellpadding="5"

\|$\pi_{\mathbf{f}}|_{SO(2n)}= \bigoplus_{f_1\ge g_1 \ge f_2\ge g_2\ge \cdots \ge f_{n-1}\ge g_{n-1}\ge f_n \ge |g_n|} \pi_{\mathbf{g}}$ |}

for *N* = 2*n* + 1 and

{| border="1" cellspacing="0" cellpadding="5"

\|$\pi_{\mathbf{f}}|_{SO(2n-1)}= \bigoplus_{f_1\ge g_1 \ge f_2\ge g_2\ge \cdots \ge f_{n-1}\ge g_{n-1}\ge |f_n|} \pi_{\mathbf{g}}$ |}

for *N* = 2*n*, where the differences *f*<sub>*i*</sub> − *g*<sub>*i*</sub> must be integers.

:::

## Gelfand–Tsetlin basis {#Gelfand–Tsetlin_basis}

Since the branching rules from $U(N)$ to $U(N-1)$ or $SO(N)$ to $SO(N-1)$ have multiplicity one, the irreducible summands corresponding to smaller and smaller *N* will eventually terminate in one-dimensional subspaces. In this way [Gelfand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I._M._Gelfand) and [Tsetlin](wikipedia://en/37832091) were able to obtain a basis of any irreducible representation of $U(N)$ or $SO(N)$ labelled by a chain of interleaved signatures, called a **Gelfand–Tsetlin pattern**. Explicit formulas for the action of the Lie algebra on the **Gelfand–Tsetlin basis** are given in (Želobenko 1973). Specifically, for $N=3$, the Gelfand-Testlin basis of the irreducible representation of $SO(3)$ with dimension $2l+1$ is given by the complex [spherical harmonics](wikipedia://en/203056) $\{Y_m^l | -l\leq m\leq l\}$.

For the remaining classical group $Sp(N)$, the branching is no longer multiplicity free, so that if *V* and *W* are irreducible representation of $Sp(N-1)$ and $Sp(N)$ the space of intertwiners $Hom_{Sp(N-1)}(V,W)$ can have dimension greater than one. It turns out that the [Yangian](wikipedia://en/15272567) $Y(\mathfrak{gl}_2)$, a [Hopf algebra](wikipedia://en/325714) introduced by [Ludwig Faddeev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Faddeev) and [collaborators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LOMI), acts irreducibly on this multiplicity space, a fact which enabled (Molev 2006) to extend the construction of Gelfand–Tsetlin bases to $Sp(N)$.[^o35]

## Clifford's theorem {#Clifford's_theorem}

In 1937 [Alfred H. Clifford](wikipedia://en/22683347) proved the following result on the restriction of finite-dimensional irreducible representations from a group *G* to a normal subgroup *N* of finite [index](wikipedia://en/230581):[^o38]

::: {#o39 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem**. Let $\pi$: *G* $\rightarrow$ GL(*n*,*K*) be an irreducible representation with *K* a [field](wikipedia://en/10603). Then the restriction of $\pi$ to *N* breaks up into a direct sum of irreducible representations of *N* of equal dimensions. These irreducible representations of *N* lie in one orbit for the action of *G* by conjugation on the equivalence classes of irreducible representations of *N*. In particular the number of distinct summands is no greater than the index of *N* in *G*.

Twenty years later [George Mackey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Mackey) found a more precise version of this result for the restriction of irreducible [unitary representations](wikipedia://en/292788) of [locally compact groups](wikipedia://en/1651204) to closed normal subgroups in what has become known as the "Mackey machine" or "Mackey normal subgroup analysis".[^o40]

:::

## Abstract algebraic setting {#Abstract_algebraic_setting}

From the point of view of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), restriction is an instance of a [forgetful functor](wikipedia://en/844783). This functor is [exact](wikipedia://en/453945), and its [left adjoint functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_functor) is called *induction*. The relation between restriction and induction in various contexts is called the Frobenius reciprocity. Taken together, the operations of induction and restriction form a powerful set of tools for analyzing representations. This is especially true whenever the representations have the property of [complete reducibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_reducible), for example, in [representation theory of finite groups](wikipedia://en/874400) over a [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28algebra%29) of [characteristic zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_zero).

::: {#o43 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

This rather evident construction may be extended in numerous and significant ways. For instance we may take any group homomorphism φ from *H* to *G*, instead of the [inclusion map](wikipedia://en/492516), and define the restricted representation of *H* by the composition

$$\rho\circ\varphi \,$$

We may also apply the idea to other categories in [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384): [associative algebras](wikipedia://en/2112), rings, [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944), [Lie superalgebras](wikipedia://en/297501), Hopf algebras to name some. Representations or [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) *restrict* to subobjects, or via homomorphisms.
