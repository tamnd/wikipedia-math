# Mathematical statistics

Mathematics and statistics

*Image: Linear regression.svg.* Illustration of linear regression on a data set. [Regression analysis](wikipedia://en/826997) is an important part of mathematical statistics.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mathematical statistics"}

**Mathematical statistics** is the application of [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and other mathematical concepts to [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), as opposed to techniques for collecting statistical data.[^o2] Specific mathematical techniques that are commonly used in statistics include [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), [stochastic analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_analysis), [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations), and [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory).[^o3][^o4]

:::

## Introduction {#Introduction}

Statistical data collection is concerned with the planning of studies, especially with the [design of randomized experiments](wikipedia://en/9541) and with the planning of [surveys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_survey) using [random sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_sampling). The initial analysis of the data often follows the study protocol specified prior to the study being conducted. The data from a study can also be analyzed to consider secondary hypotheses inspired by the initial results, or to suggest new studies. A secondary analysis of the data from a planned study uses tools from [data analysis](wikipedia://en/2720954), and the process of doing this is mathematical statistics.

Data analysis is divided into:

- [descriptive statistics](wikipedia://en/8187) – the part of statistics that describes data, i.e. summarises the data and their typical properties.
- [inferential statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inferential_statistics) – the part of statistics that draws conclusions from data (using some model for the data): For example, inferential statistics involves selecting a model for the data, checking whether the data fulfill the conditions of a particular model, and with quantifying the involved uncertainty (e.g. using [confidence intervals](wikipedia://en/280911)).

While the tools of data analysis work best on data from randomized studies, they are also applied to other kinds of data. For example, from [natural experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_experiments) and [observational studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_studies), in which case the inference is dependent on the model chosen by the statistician, and so subjective.[^o6][^o7]

## Topics {#Topics}

The following are some of the important topics in mathematical statistics:[^o9][^o10]

### Probability distributions {#Probability_distributions}

A [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) that assigns a [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) to each [measurable subset](wikipedia://en/19873) of the possible outcomes of a random [experiment](wikipedia://en/19621951), [survey](wikipedia://en/272134), or procedure of [statistical inference](wikipedia://en/27577). Examples are found in experiments whose [sample space](wikipedia://en/22958) is non-numerical, where the distribution would be a [categorical distribution](wikipedia://en/10072717); experiments whose sample space is encoded by discrete [random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variables), where the distribution can be specified by a [probability mass function](wikipedia://en/154725); and experiments with sample spaces encoded by continuous random variables, where the distribution can be specified by a [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487). More complex experiments, such as those involving [stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_processes) defined in [continuous time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_time), may demand the use of more general [probability measures](wikipedia://en/45437).

A probability distribution can either be [univariate](wikipedia://en/9253219) or [multivariate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_distribution). A univariate distribution gives the probabilities of a single [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) taking on various alternative values; a multivariate distribution (a [joint probability distribution](wikipedia://en/879637)) gives the probabilities of a [random vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_vector)—a set of two or more random variables—taking on various combinations of values. Important and commonly encountered univariate probability distributions include the [binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876), the [hypergeometric distribution](wikipedia://en/180841), and the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462). The [multivariate normal distribution](wikipedia://en/50347) is a commonly encountered multivariate distribution.

#### Special distributions {#Special_distributions}

- [Normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462), the most common continuous distribution
- [Bernoulli distribution](wikipedia://en/199189), for the outcome of a single Bernoulli trial (e.g. success/failure, yes/no)
- [Binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876), for the number of "positive occurrences" (e.g. successes, yes votes, etc.) given a fixed total number of [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_%28statistics%29) occurrences
- [Negative binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/45177), for binomial-type observations but where the quantity of interest is the number of failures before a given number of successes occurs
- [Geometric distribution](wikipedia://en/45922), for binomial-type observations but where the quantity of interest is the number of failures before the first success; a special case of the negative binomial distribution, where the number of successes is one.
- [Discrete uniform distribution](wikipedia://en/1699214), for a finite set of values (e.g. the outcome of a fair die)
- [Continuous uniform distribution](wikipedia://en/1699223), for continuously distributed values
- [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144), for the number of occurrences of a Poisson-type event in a given period of time
- [Exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906), for the time before the next Poisson-type event occurs
- [Gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079), for the time before the next k Poisson-type events occur
- [Chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/113424), the distribution of a sum of squared [standard normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal) variables; useful e.g. for inference regarding the [sample variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_variance) of normally distributed samples (see [chi-squared test](wikipedia://en/226680))
- [Student's t distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student's_t_distribution), the distribution of the ratio of a [standard normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal) variable and the square root of a scaled [chi squared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi_squared_distribution) variable; useful for inference regarding the [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) of normally distributed samples with unknown variance (see [Student's t-test](wikipedia://en/536080))
- [Beta distribution](wikipedia://en/207074), for a single probability (real number between 0 and 1); conjugate to the [Bernoulli distribution](wikipedia://en/199189) and [binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876)

### Statistical inference {#Statistical_inference}

[Statistical inference](wikipedia://en/27577) is the process of drawing conclusions from data that are subject to random variation, for example, observational errors or sampling variation.[^o14] Initial requirements of such a system of procedures for [inference](wikipedia://en/317465) and [induction](wikipedia://en/393736) are that the system should produce reasonable answers when applied to well-defined situations and that it should be general enough to be applied across a range of situations. Inferential statistics are used to test hypotheses and make estimations using sample data. Whereas [descriptive statistics](wikipedia://en/8187) describe a sample, inferential statistics infer predictions about a larger population that the sample represents.

The outcome of statistical inference may be an answer to the question "what should be done next?", where this might be a decision about making further experiments or surveys, or about drawing a conclusion before implementing some organizational or governmental policy. For the most part, statistical inference makes propositions about populations, using data drawn from the population of interest via some form of random sampling. More generally, data about a random process is obtained from its observed behavior during a finite period of time. Given a parameter or hypothesis about which one wishes to make inference, statistical inference most often uses:

- a [statistical model](wikipedia://en/27576) of the random process that is supposed to generate the data, which is known when randomization has been used, and
- a particular realization of the random process; i.e., a set of data.

### Regression {#Regression}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), **regression analysis** is a statistical process for estimating the relationships among variables. It includes many ways for modeling and analyzing several variables, when the focus is on the relationship between a [dependent variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependent_variable) and one or more [independent variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_variable). More specifically, regression analysis helps one understand how the typical value of the dependent variable (or 'criterion variable') changes when any one of the independent variables is varied, while the other independent variables are held fixed. Most commonly, regression analysis estimates the [conditional expectation](wikipedia://en/435099) of the dependent variable given the independent variables – that is, the [average value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Average_value) of the dependent variable when the independent variables are fixed. Less commonly, the focus is on a [quantile](wikipedia://en/25184), or other [location parameter](wikipedia://en/17516) of the conditional distribution of the dependent variable given the independent variables. In all cases, the estimation target is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) of the independent variables called the **regression function**. In regression analysis, it is also of interest to characterize the variation of the dependent variable around the regression function which can be described by a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543).

Many techniques for carrying out regression analysis have been developed. Familiar methods, such as [linear regression](wikipedia://en/48758386), are [parametric](wikipedia://en/223366), in that the regression function is defined in terms of a finite number of unknown [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065) that are estimated from the [data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data) (e.g. using [ordinary least squares](wikipedia://en/1651906)). [Nonparametric regression](wikipedia://en/4536068) refers to techniques that allow the regression function to lie in a specified set of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427), which may be [infinite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398).

### Nonparametric statistics {#Nonparametric_statistics}

**Nonparametric statistics** are values calculated from data in a way that is not based on [parameterized](wikipedia://en/413291) families of [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543). They include both [descriptive](wikipedia://en/8187) and [inferential](wikipedia://en/27577) statistics. The typical parameters are the expectations, variance, etc. Unlike [parametric statistics](wikipedia://en/223366), nonparametric statistics make no assumptions about the [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) of the variables being assessed.[^o17]

Non-parametric methods are widely used for studying populations that take on a ranked order (such as movie reviews receiving one to four stars). The use of non-parametric methods may be necessary when data have a [ranking](wikipedia://en/1482394) but no clear numerical interpretation, such as when assessing [preferences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preferences). In terms of [levels of measurement](wikipedia://en/821148), non-parametric methods result in "ordinal" data.

As non-parametric methods make fewer assumptions, their applicability is much wider than the corresponding parametric methods. In particular, they may be applied in situations where less is known about the application in question. Also, due to the reliance on fewer assumptions, non-parametric methods are more [robust](wikipedia://en/2885691#Introduction).

One drawback of non-parametric methods is that since they do not rely on assumptions, they are generally less [powerful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_of_a_test) than their parametric counterparts.[^o18] Low power non-parametric tests are problematic because a common use of these methods is for when a sample has a low sample size.[^o18] Many parametric methods are proven to be the most powerful tests through methods such as the [Neyman–Pearson lemma](wikipedia://en/685158) and the [Likelihood-ratio test](wikipedia://en/45035).

Another justification for the use of non-parametric methods is simplicity. In certain cases, even when the use of parametric methods is justified, non-parametric methods may be easier to use. Due both to this simplicity and to their greater robustness, non-parametric methods are seen by some statisticians as leaving less room for improper use and misunderstanding.

## Statistics, mathematics, and mathematical statistics {#Statistics,_mathematics,_and_mathematical_statistics}

Mathematical statistics is a key subset of the discipline of [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685). [Statistical theorists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statisticians) study and improve statistical procedures with mathematics, and statistical research often raises mathematical questions.

Mathematicians and statisticians like [Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss), [Laplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace), and [C. S. Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) used [decision theory](wikipedia://en/16414027) with [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) and [loss functions](wikipedia://en/442137) (or [utility functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_function)). The decision-theoretic approach to statistical inference was reinvigorated by [Abraham Wald](wikipedia://en/1239430) and his successors[^o20][^o21][^o22][^o23][^o24][^o25][^o26] and makes extensive use of [scientific computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_computing), [analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), and [optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimization_%28mathematics%29); for the [design of experiments](wikipedia://en/9541), statisticians use [algebra](wikipedia://en/17314682) and [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/2761968). But while statistical practice often relies on [probability](wikipedia://en/23542) and [decision theory](wikipedia://en/16414027), their application can be controversial [^o7]
