# Circulant matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Circulant matrix"}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), a **circulant matrix** is a [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) in which each row is a cyclic shift, by one position, of the row above it. Equivalently, its entries depend only on the difference of the row and column indices modulo the matrix size: if rows and columns are indexed from $0$ to $n-1$, the $(r,s)$ entry equals $c_{(r-s)\bmod n}$, where $(c_0,c_1,\ldots,c_{n-1})$ is a single generating vector. Circulant matrices are a special class of [Toeplitz matrices](wikipedia://en/151569).[^o2][^o3]

:::

Every complex circulant matrix is [diagonalized](wikipedia://en/246325#Diagonalization) by the [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811) (DFT). As a consequence, circulant matrices form a [commutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_algebra_%28structure%29) of [normal matrices](wikipedia://en/170366); the eigenvalues of a circulant matrix are the DFT of its generating vector; and matrix–vector products and nonsingular [linear systems](wikipedia://en/17570) involving a circulant matrix can be computed with a [fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512) (FFT) in $O(n\log n)$ arithmetic operations. Algebraically, multiplication by a circulant matrix is [circular convolution](wikipedia://en/3367262) on the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$.[^o3]

Circulant matrices arise wherever a linear operation is invariant under cyclic shifts. They can be interpreted analytically as the [integral kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_kernel) of a [convolution operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convolution_operator) on the cyclic group $C_n$, and hence appear in signal and image processing with periodic boundary conditions, in discretizations of translation-invariant problems on periodic domains, and in the study of [stationary](wikipedia://en/329898) stochastic processes. In [orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_frequency-division_multiplexing), the [cyclic prefix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_prefix) prepended to each transmitted block makes the effective channel matrix circulant, so that channel equalization can be carried out separately on each component in the [frequency domain](wikipedia://en/370346).

In [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), a circulant matrix is used in the [MixColumns](wikipedia://en/2105621) step of the [Advanced Encryption Standard](wikipedia://en/1260).

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

An $n \times n$ **circulant matrix** $C$ takes the form

$$C = \begin{bmatrix} c_0 & c_{n-1} & \cdots & c_2 & c_1 \\ c_1 & c_0 & c_{n-1} & & c_2 \\ \vdots & c_1 & c_0 & \ddots & \vdots \\ c_{n-2} & & \ddots & \ddots & c_{n-1} \\ c_{n-1} & c_{n-2} & \cdots & c_1 & c_0 \\ \end{bmatrix}$$

or the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) of this form (by choice of notation). If each $c_i$ is a $p \times p$ square [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859), then the $np \times np$ matrix $C$ is called a **block-circulant matrix**.

A circulant matrix is fully specified by one vector, $c$, which appears as the first column (or row) of $C$. The remaining columns (and rows, resp.) of $C$ are each [cyclic permutations](wikipedia://en/449166) of the vector $c$ with offset equal to the column (or row, resp.) index, if lines are indexed from $0$ to $n-1$. (Cyclic permutation of rows has the same effect as cyclic permutation of columns.) The last row of $C$ is the vector $c$ shifted by one in reverse.

Different sources define the circulant matrix in different ways, for example as above, or with the vector $c$ corresponding to the first row rather than the first column of the matrix; and possibly with a different direction of shift (which is sometimes called an **anti-circulant matrix**).

The [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) $f(x) = c_0 + c_1 x + \dots + c_{n-1} x^{n-1}$ is called the *associated polynomial* of the matrix $C$.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

### Eigenvectors and eigenvalues {#Eigenvectors_and_eigenvalues}

The normalized [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) of a circulant matrix are the Fourier modes, namely,

$$v_j=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \left(1, \omega^j, \omega^{2j}, \ldots, \omega^{(n-1)j}\right)^{T},\quad j = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1,$$

where $\omega=\exp \left(\tfrac{2\pi i}{n}\right)$ is a primitive $n$-th [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950) and $i$ is the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358).

(This can be understood by realizing that multiplication with a circulant matrix implements a convolution. In Fourier space, convolutions become multiplication. Hence the product of a circulant matrix with a Fourier mode yields a multiple of that Fourier mode, i.e. it is an eigenvector.)

The corresponding [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) are given by

$$\lambda_j = c_0+c_{1} \omega^{-j} + c_{2} \omega^{-2j} + \dots + c_{n-1} \omega^{-(n-1)j},\quad j = 0, 1, \dots, n-1.$$

::: {#o10 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Circulant determinant"}

### Determinant {#Determinant}

As a consequence of the explicit formula for the eigenvalues above, the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of a circulant matrix can be computed as:

$$\det C = \prod_{j=0}^{n-1} (c_0 + c_{n-1} \omega^j + c_{n-2} \omega^{2j} + \dots + c_1\omega^{(n-1)j}).$$

Since taking the transpose does not change the eigenvalues of a matrix, an equivalent formulation is

$$\det C = \prod_{j=0}^{n-1} (c_0 + c_1 \omega^j + c_2 \omega^{2j} + \dots + c_{n-1}\omega^{(n-1)j}) = \prod_{j=0}^{n-1} f(\omega^j).$$

:::

### Rank {#Rank}

The [rank](wikipedia://en/26561) of a circulant matrix $C$ is equal to $n - d$ where $d$ is the [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) of the polynomial $\gcd( f(x), x^n - 1)$.[^o12]

### Other properties {#Other_properties}

- Any circulant is a matrix polynomial (namely, the associated polynomial) in the cyclic [permutation matrix](wikipedia://en/238849) $P$: $$C = c_0 I + c_1 P + c_2 P^2 + \dots + c_{n-1} P^{n-1} = f(P),$$ where $P$ is given by the [companion matrix](wikipedia://en/532542) $$P = \begin{bmatrix} 0&0&\cdots&0&1\\ 1&0&\cdots&0&0\\ 0&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\ \vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0&0\\ 0&\cdots&0&1&0 \end{bmatrix}.$$
- The [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of $n \times n$ circulant matrices forms an $n$-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) with respect to addition and scalar multiplication. This space can be interpreted as the space of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) on the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) $n$, $C_n$, or equivalently as the [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) of $C_n$.
- Circulant matrices form a [commutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_algebra_%28structure%29), since for any two given circulant matrices $A$ and $B$, the sum $A + B$ is circulant, the product $AB$ is circulant, and $AB = BA$.
- For a [nonsingular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonsingular_matrix) circulant matrix $A$, its [inverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_matrix) $A^{-1}$ is also circulant. For a singular circulant matrix, its [Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse](wikipedia://en/331258) $A^+$ is circulant.
- The [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811) matrix of order $n$ is defined as by

$$F_n = (f_{jk}) \text{ with } f_{jk} = e^{-2\pi i/n \cdot jk}, \,\text{for } 0 \leq j,k \leq n-1.$$

There are important connections between circulant matrices and the DFT matrices. In fact, it can be shown that

$$C = F_n^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(F_n c) F_n ,$$

where $c$ is the first column of $C$. The eigenvalues of $C$ are given by the product $F_n c$. This product can be readily calculated by a [fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512).[^o14]

- Let $p(x)$ be the ([monic](wikipedia://en/314730)) [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) of an $n \times n$ circulant matrix $C$. Then the scaled [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) $\frac{1}{n}p'(x)$ is the characteristic polynomial of the following $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ submatrix of $C$: $$C_{n-1} = \begin{bmatrix} c_0 & c_{n-1} & \cdots & c_3 & c_2 \\ c_1 & c_0 & c_{n-1} & & c_3 \\ \vdots & c_1 & c_0 & \ddots & \vdots \\ c_{n-3} & & \ddots & \ddots & c_{n-1} \\ c_{n-2} & c_{n-3} & \cdots & c_{1} & c_0 \\ \end{bmatrix}$$ (see [^o15] for the [proof](wikipedia://en/82285)).

:::

## Analytic interpretation {#Analytic_interpretation}

Circulant matrices can be interpreted [geometrically](wikipedia://en/18973446), which explains the connection with the discrete Fourier transform.

Consider vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ as functions on the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) with period $n$, (i.e., as periodic bi-infinite sequences: $\dots,a_0,a_1,\dots,a_{n-1},a_0,a_1,\dots$) or equivalently, as functions on the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order $n$ (denoted $C_n$ or $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$) geometrically, on (the vertices of) the [regular $n$-gon](wikipedia://en/333306): this is a discrete analog to periodic functions on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) or [circle](wikipedia://en/6220).

Then, from the perspective of [operator theory](wikipedia://en/712675), a circulant matrix is the kernel of a discrete [integral transform](wikipedia://en/430912), namely the [convolution operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convolution_operator) for the function $(c_0,c_1,\dots,c_{n-1})$; this is a discrete [circular convolution](wikipedia://en/3367262). The formula for the convolution of the functions $(b_i) := (c_i) * (a_i)$ is

$$b_k = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} a_i c_{k-i}$$

(recall that the sequences are periodic) which is the product of the vector $(a_i)$ by the circulant matrix for $(c_i)$.

The discrete Fourier transform then converts convolution into multiplication, which in the matrix setting corresponds to diagonalization.

The $C^*$-algebra of all circulant matrices with [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) entries is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the group $C^*$-algebra of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}.$

## Relation to stationary processes {#Relation_to_stationary_processes}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) and [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324), circulant matrices arise naturally as the [covariance matrices](wikipedia://en/191752) of [wide-sense stationary](wikipedia://en/329898) processes observed on a circular array or with periodic boundary conditions. More generally, the covariance matrix of a stationary process on a linear array is [Toeplitz](wikipedia://en/151569), and Toeplitz matrices are asymptotically circulant as the dimension grows.[^o18] This asymptotic equivalence is the reason that [DFT](wikipedia://en/8811)-based [spectral analysis](wikipedia://en/13070117) is asymptotically optimal for stationary processes: the DFT diagonalizes circulant matrices exactly and approximately diagonalizes Toeplitz matrices, so the DFT coefficients are asymptotically uncorrelated for stationary data.

## Symmetric circulant matrices {#Symmetric_circulant_matrices}

For a [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474) circulant matrix $C$ one has the extra condition that $c_{n-i}=c_i$. Thus it is determined by $\lfloor n/2\rfloor + 1$ elements.

$$C = \begin{bmatrix} c_0 & c_1 & \cdots & c_2 & c_1 \\ c_1 & c_0 & c_1 & & c_2 \\ \vdots & c_1 & c_0 & \ddots & \vdots \\ c_2 & & \ddots & \ddots & c_1 \\ c_1 & c_2 & \cdots & c_1 & c_0 \\ \end{bmatrix}.$$

The eigenvalues of any [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) symmetric matrix are real. The corresponding eigenvalues $\vec{\lambda}= \sqrt n \cdot F_n^{\dagger} c$ become:

$$\begin{array}{lcl} \lambda_k & = & c_0 + c_{n/2} e^{-\pi i \cdot k} + 2\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{n}{2}-1} c_j \cos{(-\frac{2\pi}{n}\cdot k j )} \\ & = & c_0+ c_{n/2} \omega_k^{n/2} + 2 c_1 \Re \omega_k + 2 c_2 \Re \omega_k^2 + \dots + 2c_{n/2-1} \Re \omega_k^{n/2-1} \end{array}$$

for $n$ [even](wikipedia://en/143135), and

$$\begin{array}{lcl} \lambda_k & = & c_0 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}} c_j \cos{(-\frac{2\pi}{n}\cdot k j )} \\ & = & c_0 + 2 c_1 \Re \omega_k + 2 c_2 \Re \omega_k^2 + \dots + 2c_{(n-1)/2} \Re \omega_k^{(n-1)/2} \end{array}$$

for $n$ [odd](wikipedia://en/143135), where $\Re z$ denotes the [real part](wikipedia://en/5826) of $z$. This can be further simplified by using the fact that $\Re \omega_k^j = \Re e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{n} \cdot kj} = \cos(-\frac{2\pi}{n} \cdot kj)$ and $\omega_k^{n/2}=e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{n} \cdot k \frac{n}{2}} =e^{-\pi i \cdot k}$ depending on $k$ even or odd.

Symmetric circulant matrices belong to the class of [bisymmetric matrices](wikipedia://en/13714248).

## Hermitian circulant matrices {#Hermitian_circulant_matrices}

The complex version of the circulant matrix, ubiquitous in communications theory, is usually [Hermitian](wikipedia://en/189682). In this case $c_{n-i} = c_i^*, \; i \le n/2$ and its determinant and all eigenvalues are real.

If *n* is even the first two rows necessarily takes the form

$$\begin{bmatrix} r_0 & z_1 & z_2 & r_3 & z_2^* & z_1^* \\ z_1^* & r_0 & z_1 & z_2 & r_3 & z_2^* \\ \dots \\ \end{bmatrix}.$$

in which the first element $r_3$ in the top second half-row is real.

If *n* is odd we get

$$\begin{bmatrix} r_0 & z_1 & z_2 & z_2^* & z_1^* \\ z_1^* & r_0 & z_1 & z_2 & z_2^* \\ \dots\\ \end{bmatrix}.$$

Tee[^o21] has discussed constraints on the eigenvalues for the Hermitian condition.

## Applications {#Applications}

### In linear equations {#In_linear_equations}

Given a matrix equation

$$C \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b},$$

where $C$ is a circulant matrix of size $n$, we can write the equation as the [circular convolution](wikipedia://en/3367262)

$$\mathbf{c} \star \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b},$$

where $\mathbf c$ is the first column of $C$, and the vectors $\mathbf c$, $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf b$ are cyclically extended in each direction. Using the [circular convolution theorem](wikipedia://en/8811#Circular_convolution_theorem_and_cross-correlation_theorem), we can use the [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811) to transform the cyclic convolution into component-wise multiplication

$$\mathcal{F}_{n}(\mathbf{c} \star \mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{F}_{n}(\mathbf{c}) \mathcal{F}_{n}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{F}_{n}(\mathbf{b})$$

so that

$$\mathbf{x} = \mathcal{F}_n^{-1} \left[ \left( \frac{(\mathcal{F}_n(\mathbf{b}))_{\nu}} {(\mathcal{F}_n(\mathbf{c}))_{\nu}} \right)_{\!\nu\in\mathbb{Z}}\, \right]^{\rm T}.$$

This algorithm is much faster than the standard [Gaussian elimination](wikipedia://en/13035), especially if a [fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512) is used.

### In graph theory {#In_graph_theory}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) or [digraph](wikipedia://en/19721986) whose [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) is circulant is called a [circulant graph](wikipedia://en/3498787)/digraph. Equivalently, a graph is circulant if its [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) contains a full-length cycle. The [Möbius ladders](wikipedia://en/7355278) are examples of circulant graphs, as are the [Paley graphs](wikipedia://en/5719307) for [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) of [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) order.
