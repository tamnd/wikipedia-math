# Banach–Mazur compactum

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Banach–Mazur compactum"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) study of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), the **Banach–Mazur distance** is a way to define a [distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distance_function) on the set $Q(n)$ of $n$-dimensional [normed spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space). With this distance, the set of [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) classes of $n$-dimensional normed spaces becomes a [compact metric space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_metric_space), called the **Banach–Mazur compactum**.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

If $X$ and $Y$ are two finite-dimensional normed spaces with the same dimension, let $\operatorname{GL}(X, Y)$ denote the collection of all linear isomorphisms $T : X \to Y.$ Denote by $\|T\|$ the [operator norm](wikipedia://en/246722) of such a linear map — the maximum factor by which it "lengthens" vectors. The Banach–Mazur distance between $X$ and $Y$ is defined by

$$\delta(X, Y) = \log \Bigl( \inf \left\{ \left\|T\right\| \left\|T^{-1}\right\| : T \in \operatorname{GL}(X, Y) \right\} \Bigr).$$

We have $\delta(X, Y) = 0$ if and only if the spaces $X$ and $Y$ are isometrically isomorphic. Equipped with the metric *δ*, the space of isometry classes of $n$-dimensional normed spaces becomes a [compact metric space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_metric_space), called the Banach–Mazur compactum.

Many authors prefer to work with the **multiplicative Banach–Mazur distance**

$$d(X, Y) := \mathrm{e}^{\delta(X, Y)} = \inf \left\{ \left\|T\right\| \left\|T^{-1}\right\| : T \in \operatorname{GL}(X, Y) \right\},$$

for which $d(X, Z) \leq d(X, Y) \, d(Y, Z)$ and $d(X, X) = 1.$

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

[F. John's theorem](wikipedia://en/11840458) on the maximal ellipsoid contained in a convex body gives the estimate:

$d(X, \ell_n^2) \le \sqrt{n}, \,$ [^o6]

where $\ell_n^2$ denotes $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the [Euclidean norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_norm) (see the article on [$L^p$ spaces](wikipedia://en/45194)).

From this it follows that $d(X, Y) \leq n$ for all $X, Y \in Q(n).$ However, for the classical spaces, this upper bound for the diameter of $Q(n)$ is far from being approached. For example, the distance between $\ell_n^1$ and $\ell_n^{\infty}$ is (only) of order $n^{1/2}$ (up to a multiplicative constant independent from the dimension $n$).

A major achievement in the direction of estimating the diameter of $Q(n)$ is due to E. Gluskin, who proved in 1981 that the (multiplicative) diameter of the Banach–Mazur compactum is bounded below by $c\,n,$ for some universal $c > 0.$

Gluskin's method introduces a class of random symmetric [polytopes](wikipedia://en/23471) $P(\omega)$ in $\mathbb{R}^n,$ and the normed spaces $X(\omega)$ having $P(\omega)$ as unit ball (the vector space is $\mathbb{R}^n$ and the norm is the [gauge](wikipedia://en/990534) of $P(\omega)$). The proof consists in showing that the required estimate is true with large probability for two independent copies of the normed space $X(\omega).$

$Q(2)$ is an [absolute extensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_extensor).[^o7] On the other hand, $Q(2)$ is not homeomorphic to a [Hilbert cube](wikipedia://en/216389).

:::
