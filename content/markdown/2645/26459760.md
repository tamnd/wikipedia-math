# Āryabhaṭa's sine table

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Āryabhaṭa's sine table"}

*Image: Arc and chord.svg.* Arc and chord of a circle

**Āryabhata's sine table** is a set of twenty-four numbers given in the astronomical treatise [Āryabhatiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhatiya) composed by the fifth century [Indian mathematician](wikipedia://en/1848052) and astronomer [Āryabhata](wikipedia://en/297204) (476–550 CE), for the computation of the [half-chords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jya) of a certain set of arcs of a circle. The set of numbers appears in verse 12 in Chapter 1 *Dasagitika* of Aryabhatiya and is the first table of sines.[^o2][^o3] It is not a table in the modern sense of a [mathematical table](wikipedia://en/239112); that is, it is not a set of numbers arranged into rows and columns.[^o4][^o5][^o6] Āryabhaṭa's table is also not a set of values of the trigonometric sine function in a conventional sense; it is a table of the [first differences](wikipedia://en/39147) of the values of [trigonometric sines](wikipedia://en/5010838) expressed in [arcminutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcminutes), and because of this the table is also referred to as *Āryabhaṭa's table of sine-differences*.[^o7][^o8]

:::

Āryabhaṭa's table was the first sine table ever constructed in the [history of mathematics](wikipedia://en/14220).[^o9] The now lost tables of [Hipparchus](wikipedia://en/13600) (c. 190 BC – c. 120 BC) and [Menelaus](wikipedia://en/46301) (c. 70–140 CE) and [those of](wikipedia://en/30684123) [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979) (c. AD 90 – c. 168) were all tables of [chords](wikipedia://en/297947) and not of half-chords.[^o9] Āryabhaṭa's table remained as the standard sine table of ancient India. There were continuous attempts to improve the accuracy of this table. These endeavors culminated in the eventual discovery of the [power series expansions](wikipedia://en/26149716) of the sine and cosine functions by [Madhava of Sangamagrama](wikipedia://en/2793007) (c. 1350 – c. 1425), the founder of the [Kerala school of astronomy and mathematics](wikipedia://en/3099132), and the tabulation of a [sine table by Madhava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhava's_sine_table) with values accurate to seven or eight decimal places.

## The table {#The_table}

### In modern notations {#In_modern_notations}

The values encoded in Āryabhaṭa's Sanskrit verse can be decoded using the [numerical scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhata_numeration) explained in [Āryabhaṭīya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Āryabhaṭīya), and the decoded numbers are listed in the table below. In the table, the angle measures relevant to Āryabhaṭa's sine table are listed in the second column. The third column contains the list the numbers contained in the Sanskrit verse given above in [Devanagari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devanagari) script. For the convenience of users unable to read Devanagari, these word-numerals are reproduced in the fourth column in [ISO 15919](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_15919) transliteration. The next column contains these numbers in the [Hindu-Arabic numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu-Arabic_numerals). Āryabhaṭa's numbers are the first differences in the values of sines. The corresponding value of sine (or more precisely, of *[jya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jya)*) can be obtained by summing up the differences up to that difference. Thus the value of *jya* corresponding to 18° 45′ is the sum 225 + 224 + 222 + 219 + 215 = 1105. For assessing the accuracy of Āryabhaṭa's computations, the modern values of *jya*s are given in the last column of the table.

In the Indian mathematical tradition, the sine ( or *jya*) of an angle is not a ratio of numbers. It is the length of a certain line segment, a certain half-chord. The radius of the base circle is basic parameter for the construction of such tables. Historically, several tables have been constructed using different values for this parameter. Āryabhaṭa has chosen the number 3438 as the value of radius of the base circle for the computation of his sine table. The rationale of the choice of this parameter is the idea of measuring the circumference of a circle in angle measures. In astronomical computations distances are measured in [degrees](wikipedia://en/1195294), [minutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcminutes), [seconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcseconds), etc. In this measure, the circumference of a circle is 360° = (60 × 360) minutes = 21600 minutes. The radius of the circle, the measure of whose circumference is 21600 minutes, is 21600 / 2π minutes. Computing this using the value [π](wikipedia://en/23601) = 3.1416 known to [Aryabhata](wikipedia://en/297204) one gets the radius of the circle as 3438 minutes approximately. Āryabhaṭa's sine table is based on this value for the radius of the base circle. It has not yet been established who is the first ever to use this value for the base radius. But [Aryabhatiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhatiya) is the earliest surviving text containing a reference to this basic constant.[^o12]

## Āryabhaṭa's computational method {#Āryabhaṭa's_computational_method}

The second section of Āryabhaṭiya, titled Ganitapādd, a contains a stanza indicating a method for the computation of the sine table. There are several ambiguities in correctly interpreting the meaning of this verse. For example, the following is a translation of the verse given by Katz wherein the words in square brackets are insertions of the translator and not translations of texts in the verse.[^o12]

- "When the second half-\[chord\] partitioned is less than the first half-chord, which is \[approximately equal to\] the \[corresponding\] arc, by a certain amount, the remaining \[sine-differences\] are less \[than the previous ones\] each by that amount of that divided by the first half-chord."

This may be referring to the fact that the second derivative of the sine function is equal to the negative of the sine function.

## Alleged influence hypothesis and rebuttal {#Alleged_influence_hypothesis_and_rebuttal}

Some historians of mathematics have argued that the sine table given in Āryabhaṭiya was an adaptation of earlier such tables constructed by mathematicians and astronomers of ancient Greece.[^o17] Historian [David Pingree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Pingree) was an exponent of such a view. Assuming this hypothesis, [G. J. Toomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._J._Toomer)[^o18][^o19][^o20] writes, "Hardly any documentation exists for the earliest arrival of Greek astronomical models in India, or for that matter what those models would have looked like. So it is very difficult to ascertain the extent to which what has come down to us represents transmitted knowledge, and what is original with Indian scientists. ... The truth is probably a tangled mixture of both."[^o21]

However, this notion of transmission has been challenged and refuted by multiple scholars.[^o22][^o23][^o24] Bo Klintberg demonstrated that the scholarly consensus advocating for a Greek-origin promoted by historians like Pingree and others, relied almost entirely on Toomer's 1973 paper, which Toomer himself cast doubt on his earlier mathematical reconstructions in the footnote in his 1984 paper, yet subsequent historians continued to cite the original claims of Toomers as conclusive proof completely ignoring the discrepancies. [^o25]
