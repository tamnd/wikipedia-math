# 2-factor theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="2-factor theorem"}

In the [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical) [discipline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discipline) of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **2-factor theorem**, discovered by [Julius Petersen](wikipedia://en/76861), is one of the earliest works in graph theory. It can be stated as follows:

:::

> Let $G$ be a [regular graph](wikipedia://en/85821) whose [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) is an even number, $2k$. Then the edges of $G$ can be partitioned into $k$ edge-disjoint 2-factors.

Here, a 2-factor is a subgraph of $G$ in which all vertices have degree two; that is, it is a collection of cycles that together touch each vertex exactly once.

::: {#o3 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

In order to prove this generalized form of the theorem, Petersen first proved that a 4-regular graph can be [factorized](wikipedia://en/3298854) into two 2-factors by taking alternate edges in a Eulerian trail. He noted that the same technique used for the 4-regular graph yields a factorization of a $2k$-regular graph into two $k$-factors.

To prove this theorem, it is sufficient to consider connected graphs. A connected graph with even degree has an Eulerian trail. Traversing this Eulerian trail generates an [orientation](wikipedia://en/36619818) $D$ of $G$ such that every point has indegree and outdegree $= k$. Next, replace every vertex $v \in V(D)$ by two vertices $v'$ and $v''$, and replace every directed edge $uv$ of the oriented graph by an undirected edge from $u'$ to $v''$. Since $D$ has in- and outdegrees equal to $k$ the resulting [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431) $G'$ is $k$-regular. The edges of $G'$ can be partitioned into $k$ perfect matchings by [a theorem of Kőnig](wikipedia://en/5465118#History). Now merging $v'$ with $v''$ for every $v$ recovers the graph $G$, and maps the $k$ perfect matchings of $G'$ onto $k$ 2-factors of $G$ which partition its edges.

:::

## History {#History}

The theorem was discovered by [Julius Petersen](wikipedia://en/76861), a Danish mathematician. It is one of the first results ever discovered in the field of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401). The theorem appears first in the 1891 article *"Die Theorie der regulären graphs"*. To prove the theorem, Petersen's fundamental idea was to 'colour' the edges of a trail or a path alternatively red and blue, and then to use the edges of one or both colours for the construction of other paths or trials.
