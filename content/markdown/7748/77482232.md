# APOS Theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="APOS Theory"}

In [mathematics education](wikipedia://en/326471), **APOS Theory** is a framework of how mathematical concepts are learned. APOS Theory was developed by Ed Dubinsky and others and is based on [Jean Piaget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Piaget)'s notion of [reflective abstraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflective_abstraction). APOS stands for Actions, Processes, Objects, Schemas, the four main mental structures involved in the theory. APOS Theory takes a [constructivist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructivism_%28philosophy_of_education%29) view towards mathematical learning. Implementations of APOS Theory in classrooms typically use the **ACE Teaching Cycle**, a pedagogical strategy with three chronological components: activities, classroom discussion, and exercises. Implementations also often use mathematical [programming languages](wikipedia://en/23015), most commonly [ISETL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISETL).

:::

## Mental structures and mechanisms {#Mental_structures_and_mechanisms}

APOS Theory emphasizes four [mental structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_structure): Actions, Processes, Objects, and Schemas and its [mental mechanisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_mechanism): Interiorization, Encapsulation, Coordination, Reversal, among others.

1. *Actions* are specific manipulations or transformations done to already understood [mathematical objects](wikipedia://en/19453961).[^n1] Actions are *external*, meaning that each step of the transformation needs to be performed explicitly and guided by external instructions.[^n1] For example, someone who can only think about [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) by substituting a number into an expression has an action understanding of functions.[^n1]
1. *Processes* are actions that have been fully internalized. Someone who has internalized an action into a process can do the action without external instruction, can skip steps, and can imagine performing the process in the abstract without specific inputs being present.[^n2] The shift from an action understanding to a process understanding is called *interiorization*.[^n3] For example, someone with a process understanding of a function is able to think about a function as taking in unspecified inputs and applying a transformation to them to produce an output, and someone who has a process understanding of an [$n$-tuple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-tuple) is able to mentally consider the concept of an $n$-tuple for unspecified $n$.[^n2]
1. *Objects* are cognitive objects coming from the ability to conceptualize the totality of a process and understanding how to put additional structure on that totality.[^n2] The shift from a process understanding to an object understanding is called *encapsulation*.[^n2] Someone with an object understanding of functions is able to form a set of functions and define additional structure on that set, such as operations or a [topology](wikipedia://en/30450).[^n2] Encapsulation is considered the most difficult step in APOS-based instruction.[^n4]
1. *Schemas* are collections of actions, processes, and objects, and other schemas, and the interactions between them, and the ability to understand in what situations it applies.[^n5] For example, for someone who understands the theory of [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370), the mental structure of a vector space is a schema.[^n6]

The mental structures are not necessarily gone through in a linear progression.[^n7]
