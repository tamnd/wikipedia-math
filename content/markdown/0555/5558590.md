# Shapiro inequality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Shapiro inequality"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Shapiro inequality** is an [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489) proposed by [Harold S. Shapiro](wikipedia://en/1781665) in 1954.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .statement cue=named-heading name="Statement of the inequality"}

## Statement of the inequality {#Statement_of_the_inequality}

Suppose $n$ is a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) and $x_{1}, x_{2}, \ldots, x_{n}$ are [positive numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number) and:

- $n$ is [even](wikipedia://en/143135) and less than or equal to $12$, or
- $n$ is [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) and less than or equal to $23$.

Then the **Shapiro inequality** states that

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{x_i}{x_{i+1}+x_{i+2}} \geq \frac{n}{2},$$

where $x_{n+1} = x_{1}$ and $x_{n+2} = x_{2}$. The special case with $n = 3$ is [Nesbitt's inequality](wikipedia://en/1457846).

For greater values of $n$ the inequality does not hold, and the strict [lower bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_bound) is $\gamma \tfrac{n}{2}$ with $\gamma \approx 0.9891\ldots$ .

The initial [proofs](wikipedia://en/82285) of the inequality in the pivotal cases $n = 12$[^o5] and $n = 23$[^o6] rely on numerical computations. In 2002, P.J. Bushell and J.B. McLeod published an analytical proof for $n = 12$.[^o7]

The value of $\gamma$ was determined in 1971 by [Vladimir Drinfeld](wikipedia://en/1053747). Specifically, he proved that the strict lower bound $\gamma$ is given by $\psi(0)$, where the [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $\psi$ is the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of $f(x) = e^{-x}$ and $g(x) = 2 / (e^{x} + e^{x/2})$. (That is, the region above the [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of $\psi$ is the convex hull of the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of the regions above the graphs of $f$ and $g$.)[^o8]

Interior local minima of the left-hand side are always $\ge n / 2$.[^o10]

Similar to [Nesbitt’s inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nesbitt’s_inequality), there is no corresponding [upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound) as the cyclic sum [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489) can be made arbitrarily large.

:::

## Counter-examples for higher *n* {#Counter-examples_for_higher_n}

The first [counter-example](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter-example) was found by Lighthill in 1956, for $n = 20$:[^o12]

$$x_{20} = (1+5\epsilon,\ 6\epsilon,\ 1+4\epsilon,\ 5\epsilon,\ 1+3\epsilon,\ 4\epsilon,\ 1+2\epsilon,\ 3\epsilon,\ 1+\epsilon,\ 2\epsilon,\ 1+2\epsilon,\ \epsilon,\ 1+3\epsilon,\ 2\epsilon,\ 1+4\epsilon,\ 3\epsilon,\ 1+5\epsilon,\ 4\epsilon,\ 1+6\epsilon,\ 5\epsilon),$$

where $\epsilon$ is close to 0. Then the left-hand side is equal to $10 - \epsilon^2 + O(\epsilon^3)$, thus lower than 10 when $\epsilon$ is small enough.

The following counter-example for $n = 14$ is by Troesch (1985):

$x_{14} = (0, 42, 2, 42, 4, 41, 5, 39, 4, 38, 2, 38, 0, 40)$ (Troesch, 1985)
