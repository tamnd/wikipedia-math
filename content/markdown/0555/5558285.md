# Polarization of an algebraic form

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Polarization of an algebraic form"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in particular in [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), **polarization** is a technique for expressing a [homogeneous polynomial](wikipedia://en/1698977) in a simpler fashion by adjoining more variables. Specifically, given a homogeneous polynomial, polarization produces a unique symmetric [multilinear form](wikipedia://en/832212) from which the original polynomial can be recovered by evaluating along a certain diagonal.

:::

Although the technique is deceptively simple, it has applications in many areas of abstract mathematics: in particular to [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), [invariant theory](wikipedia://en/363762), and [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200). Polarization and related techniques form the foundations for [Weyl's invariant theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl's_invariant_theory).

## The technique {#The_technique}

The fundamental ideas are as follows. Let $f(\mathbf{u})$ be a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) in $n$ variables $\mathbf{u} = \left(u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_n\right).$ Suppose that $f$ is homogeneous of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) $d,$ which means that

$$f(t \mathbf{u}) = t^d f(\mathbf{u}) \quad \text{ for all } t.$$

Let $\mathbf{u}^{(1)}, \mathbf{u}^{(2)}, \ldots, \mathbf{u}^{(d)}$ be a collection of [indeterminates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_%28variable%29) with $\mathbf{u}^{(i)} = \left(u^{(i)}_1, u^{(i)}_2, \ldots, u^{(i)}_n\right),$ so that there are $d n$ variables altogether. The **polar form** of $f$ is a polynomial

$$F\left(\mathbf{u}^{(1)}, \mathbf{u}^{(2)}, \ldots, \mathbf{u}^{(d)}\right)$$

which is linear separately in each $\mathbf{u}^{(i)}$ (that is, $F$ is multilinear), symmetric in the $\mathbf{u}^{(i)},$ and such that

$$F\left(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{u}, \ldots, \mathbf{u}\right) = f(\mathbf{u}).$$

The polar form of $f$ is given by the following construction

$$F\left({\mathbf u}^{(1)}, \dots, {\mathbf u}^{(d)}\right) = \frac{1}{d!}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_1} \dots \frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_d}f(\lambda_1{\mathbf u}^{(1)} + \dots + \lambda_d{\mathbf u}^{(d)})|_{\lambda=0}.$$

In other words, $F$ is a constant multiple of the coefficient of $\lambda_1 \lambda_2 \ldots \lambda_d$ in the expansion of $f\left(\lambda_1 \mathbf{u}^{(1)} + \cdots + \lambda_d \mathbf{u}^{(d)}\right).$

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

**A quadratic example.** Suppose that $\mathbf{x} = (x,y)$ and $f(\mathbf{x})$ is the [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478)

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = x^2 + 3 x y + 2 y^2.$$

Then the polarization of $f$ is a function in $\mathbf{x}^{(1)} = (x^{(1)}, y^{(1)})$ and $\mathbf{x}^{(2)} = (x^{(2)}, y^{(2)})$ given by

$$F\left(\mathbf{x}^{(1)}, \mathbf{x}^{(2)}\right) = x^{(1)} x^{(2)} + \frac{3}{2} x^{(2)} y^{(1)} + \frac{3}{2} x^{(1)} y^{(2)} + 2 y^{(1)} y^{(2)}.$$

More generally, if $f$ is any quadratic form then the polarization of $f$ agrees with the conclusion of the [polarization identity](wikipedia://en/2047965).

**A cubic example.** Let $f(x,y) = x^3 + 2xy^2.$ Then the polarization of $f$ is given by

$$F\left(x^{(1)}, y^{(1)}, x^{(2)}, y^{(2)}, x^{(3)}, y^{(3)}\right) = x^{(1)} x^{(2)} x^{(3)} + \frac{2}{3} x^{(1)} y^{(2)} y^{(3)} + \frac{2}{3} x^{(3)} y^{(1)} y^{(2)} + \frac{2}{3} x^{(2)} y^{(3)} y^{(1)}.$$

:::

## Mathematical details and consequences {#Mathematical_details_and_consequences}

The polarization of a homogeneous polynomial of degree $d$ is valid over any [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346) [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) in which $d!$ is a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666). In particular, it holds over any [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) zero or whose characteristic is strictly greater than $d.$

### The polarization isomorphism (by degree) {#The_polarization_isomorphism_(by_degree)}

For simplicity, let $k$ be a field of characteristic zero and let $A = k[\mathbf{x}]$ be the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) in $n$ variables over $k.$ Then $A$ is [graded](wikipedia://en/103094) by degree, so that

$$A = \bigoplus_d A_d.$$

The polarization of algebraic forms then induces an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) of [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) in each degree

$$A_d \cong \operatorname{Sym}^d k^n$$

where $\operatorname{Sym}^d$ is the $d$-th [symmetric power](wikipedia://en/57047243).

These isomorphisms can be expressed independently of a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) as follows. If $V$ is a [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) vector space and $A$ is the ring of $k$-valued polynomial functions on $V$ graded by homogeneous degree, then polarization yields an isomorphism

$$A_d \cong \operatorname{Sym}^d V^*.$$

### The algebraic isomorphism {#The_algebraic_isomorphism}

Furthermore, the polarization is compatible with the algebraic structure on $A$, so that

$$A \cong \operatorname{Sym}^{\bullet} V^*$$

where $\operatorname{Sym}^{\bullet} V^*$ is the full [symmetric algebra](wikipedia://en/654098) over $V^*.$

### Remarks {#Remarks}

- For fields of positive characteristic $p,$ the foregoing isomorphisms apply if the graded algebras are truncated at degree $p - 1.$
- There do exist generalizations when $V$ is an infinite-dimensional [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752).
