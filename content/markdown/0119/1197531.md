# Hamiltonian system

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hamiltonian system"}

*Image: Hamiltonian\_and\_Equation\_of\_motion.jpg.*

A **Hamiltonian system** is a [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087) governed by [Hamilton's equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton's_equations). In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), this dynamical system describes the evolution of a [physical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_system) such as a [planetary system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_system) or an [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) in an [electromagnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field). These systems can be studied in both [Hamiltonian mechanics](wikipedia://en/198319) and [dynamical systems theory](wikipedia://en/990632).

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

Informally, a Hamiltonian system is a mathematical formalism developed by [William Rowan Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817) to describe the [evolution equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_equation) of a physical system. The advantage of this description is that it gives important insights into the dynamics, even if the [initial value problem](wikipedia://en/498383) cannot be solved analytically. One example is the [planetary movement of three bodies](wikipedia://en/1208420): while there is no [closed-form solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed-form_solution) to the general problem, [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) showed for the first time that it exhibits [deterministic chaos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic_chaos).

Formally, a Hamiltonian system is a dynamical system characterised by the scalar function $H(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p},t)$, also known as the Hamiltonian.[^o3] The state of the system, $\boldsymbol{r}$, is described by the [generalized coordinates](wikipedia://en/473514) $\boldsymbol{p}$ and $\boldsymbol{q}$, corresponding to generalized momentum and position respectively. Both $\boldsymbol{p}$ and $\boldsymbol{q}$ are real-valued vectors with the same dimension *N*. Thus, the state is completely described by the 2*N*-dimensional vector

$$\boldsymbol{r} = (\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p})$$

and the evolution equations are given by [Hamilton's equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton's_equations):

$$\begin{aligned} & \frac{d\boldsymbol{p}}{dt} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial \boldsymbol{q}}, \\[5pt] & \frac{d\boldsymbol{q}}{dt} = +\frac{\partial H}{\partial \boldsymbol{p}}. \end{aligned}$$

The trajectory $\boldsymbol{r}(t)$ is the solution of the [initial value problem](wikipedia://en/498383) defined by Hamilton's equations and the initial condition $\boldsymbol{r}(t = 0) = \boldsymbol{r}_0\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}$.

and thus the Hamiltonian is a [constant of motion](wikipedia://en/5391037), whose constant equals the total [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) of the system: $H = E$. Examples of such systems are the [undamped pendulum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendulum), the [harmonic oscillator](wikipedia://en/13899), and [dynamical billiards](wikipedia://en/2160429).

### Example {#Example}

An example of a time-independent Hamiltonian system is the harmonic oscillator. Consider the system defined by the coordinates $\boldsymbol{p} = m\dot{x}$ and $\boldsymbol{q} = x$. Then the Hamiltonian is given by

$$H = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{kq^2}{2}.$$

The Hamiltonian of this system does not depend on time and thus the energy of the system is conserved.

:::

## Symplectic structure {#Symplectic_structure}

One important property of a Hamiltonian dynamical system is that it has a [symplectic structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_structure).[^o3] Writing

$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol{r}} H(\boldsymbol{r}) = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial H(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p})}{\partial \boldsymbol{q}} \\ \frac{\partial H(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p})}{\partial \boldsymbol{p}} \\ \end{bmatrix}$$

the evolution equation of the dynamical system can be written as

$$\frac{d\boldsymbol{r}}{dt} = M_N \nabla_{\boldsymbol{r}} H(\boldsymbol{r})$$

where

$$M_N = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & I_N \\ -I_N & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix}$$

and *I*<sub>*N*</sub> is the *N*×*N* [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718).

One important consequence of this property is that an infinitesimal phase-space volume is preserved.[^o3] A corollary of this is [Liouville's theorem](wikipedia://en/312301), which states that on a Hamiltonian system, the phase-space volume of a closed surface is preserved under time evolution.[^o3]

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\oint_{\partial V} d\boldsymbol{r} &= \oint_{\partial V}\frac{d\boldsymbol{r}}{dt}\cdot d\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\partial V} \\ &= \oint_{\partial V} \left(M_N \nabla_{\boldsymbol{r}} H(\boldsymbol{r})\right) \cdot d\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\partial V} \\ &= \int_{V}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{r}}\cdot \left(M_N \nabla_{\boldsymbol{r}} H(\boldsymbol{r})\right) \, dV \\ &= \int_{V}\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^N\left(\frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial q_i \partial p_j} - \frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial p_i \partial q_j}\right) \, dV \\ &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

where the third equality comes from the [divergence theorem](wikipedia://en/151864).

## Hamiltonian chaos {#Hamiltonian_chaos}

Certain Hamiltonian systems exhibit [chaotic behavior](wikipedia://en/6295). When the evolution of a Hamiltonian system is highly sensitive to initial conditions, and the motion appears random and erratic, the system is said to exhibit Hamiltonian chaos.

### Origins {#Origins}

The concept of chaos in Hamiltonian systems has its roots in the works of [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740), who in the late 19th century made pioneering contributions to the understanding of the [three-body problem](wikipedia://en/1208420) in [celestial mechanics](wikipedia://en/102182). Poincaré showed that even a simple [gravitational system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation) of three bodies could exhibit complex behavior that could not be predicted over the long term. His work is considered to be one of the earliest explorations of chaotic behavior in [physical systems](wikipedia://en/22939).[^o11]

### Characteristics {#Characteristics}

Hamiltonian chaos is characterized by the following features:[^o3]

**Sensitivity to Initial Conditions**: A hallmark of chaotic systems, small differences in initial conditions can lead to vastly different trajectories. This is known as the butterfly effect.[^o13]

**Mixing**: Over time, the phases of the system become uniformly distributed in phase space.[^o14]

**Recurrence**: Though unpredictable, the system eventually revisits states that are arbitrarily close to its initial state, known as [Poincaré recurrence](wikipedia://en/2404544).

Hamiltonian chaos is also associated with the presence of *chaotic invariants* such as the [Lyapunov exponent](wikipedia://en/166441) and [Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov–Sinai_entropy), which quantify the rate at which nearby trajectories diverge and the complexity of the system, respectively.[^o3]

::: {#o16 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

### Applications {#Applications}

Hamiltonian chaos is prevalent in many areas of physics, particularly in classical mechanics and statistical mechanics. For instance, in [plasma physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_physics), the behavior of charged particles in a magnetic field can exhibit Hamiltonian chaos, which has implications for [nuclear fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fusion) and [astrophysical plasmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysical_plasma). Moreover, in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), Hamiltonian chaos is studied through [quantum chaos](wikipedia://en/313779), which seeks to understand the quantum analogs of classical chaotic behavior. Hamiltonian chaos also plays a role in [astrophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysics), where it is used to study the dynamics of [star clusters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_clusters) and the stability of [galactic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy) structures.[^o17]
