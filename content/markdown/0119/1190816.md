# Splitting of prime ideals in Galois extensions

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Splitting of prime ideals in Galois extensions"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the interplay between the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) *G* of a [Galois extension](wikipedia://en/457064) *L* of a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) *K*, and the way the [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) *P* of the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) *O*<sub>*K*</sub> factorise as products of prime ideals of *O*<sub>*L*</sub>, provides one of the richest parts of [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705). The **splitting of prime ideals in Galois extensions** is sometimes attributed to [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) by calling it **Hilbert theory**. There is a geometric analogue, for [ramified coverings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramified_covering) of [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181), which is simpler in that only one kind of subgroup of *G* need be considered, rather than two. This was certainly familiar before Hilbert.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Let *L*/*K* be a finite extension of number fields, and let *O<sub>K</sub>* and *O<sub>L</sub>* be the corresponding [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of *K* and *L*, respectively, which are defined to be the [integral closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_closure) of the integers **Z** in the field in question.

$$\begin{array}{ccc} O_K & \hookrightarrow & O_L \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ K & \hookrightarrow & L \end{array}$$

Finally, let *p* be a non-zero prime ideal in *O<sub>K</sub>*, or equivalently, a [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164), so that the residue *O<sub>K</sub>*/*p* is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603).

From the basic theory of one-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/56079) rings follows the existence of a unique decomposition

$$pO_L = \prod_{j=1}^{g} P_j^{e_j}$$

of the ideal *pO<sub>L</sub>* generated in *O<sub>L</sub>* by *p* into a product of distinct maximal ideals *P*<sub>*j*</sub>, with multiplicities *e*<sub>*j*</sub>.

The field *F* = *O<sub>K</sub>*/*p* naturally embeds into *F*<sub>*j*</sub> = *O<sub>L</sub>*/*P*<sub>*j*</sub> for every *j*, the degree *f*<sub>*j*</sub> = \[*O<sub>L</sub>*/*P*<sub>*j*</sub> : *O<sub>K</sub>*/*p*\] of this **residue field extension** is called **inertia degree** of *P*<sub>*j*</sub> over *p*.

The multiplicity *e*<sub>*j*</sub> is called **ramification index** of *P*<sub>*j*</sub> over *p*. If it is bigger than 1 for some *j*, the field extension *L*/*K* is called **ramified** at *p* (or we say that *p* ramifies in *L*, or that it is ramified in *L*). Otherwise, *L*/*K* is called **unramified** at *p*. If this is the case then by the [Chinese remainder theorem](wikipedia://en/7713) the quotient *O<sub>L</sub>*/*pO<sub>L</sub>* is a product of fields *F*<sub>*j*</sub>. The extension *L*/*K* is ramified in exactly those primes that divide the [relative discriminant](wikipedia://en/3022963#Relative_discriminant), hence the extension is unramified in all but finitely many prime ideals.

Multiplicativity of [ideal norm](wikipedia://en/460672) implies

$$[L:K]=\sum_{j=1}^{g} e_j f_j.$$

If *f*<sub>*j*</sub> = *e*<sub>*j*</sub> = 1 for every *j* (and thus *g* = \[*L* : *K*\]), we say that *p* **splits completely** in *L*. If *g* = 1 and *f*<sub>*1*</sub> = 1 (and so *e*<sub>*1*</sub> = \[*L* : *K*\]), we say that *p* **ramifies completely** in *L*. Finally, if *g* = 1 and *e*<sub>*1*</sub> = 1 (and so *f*<sub>*1*</sub> = \[*L* : *K*\]), we say that *p* is **inert** in *L*.

### The Galois situation {#The_Galois_situation}

In the following, the extension *L*/*K* is assumed to be a [Galois extension](wikipedia://en/457064). Then the [prime avoidance lemma](wikipedia://en/43471536) can be used to show the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) $G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ [acts transitively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_group_action) on the *P*<sub>*j*</sub>. That is, the prime ideal factors of *p* in *L* form a single [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) under the [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of *L* over *K*. From this and the [unique factorisation theorem](wikipedia://en/11556), it follows that *f* = *f*<sub>*j*</sub> and *e* = *e*<sub>*j*</sub> are independent of *j*; something that certainly need not be the case for extensions that are not Galois. The basic relations then read

$$pO_L = \left(\prod_{j=1}^{g} P_j\right)^e.$$

and

$$[L:K]=efg.$$

The relation above shows that \[*L* : *K*\]/*ef* equals the number *g* of prime factors of *p* in *O<sub>L</sub>*. By the [orbit-stabilizer formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit-stabilizer_formula) this number is also equal to $G/D_{P_{j}}$ for every *j*, where *D*<sub>*P*<sub>*j*</sub></sub>, the **decomposition group** of *P*<sub>*j*</sub>, is the subgroup of elements of *G* sending a given *P*<sub>*j*</sub> to itself. Since the degree of *L*/*K* and the order of *G* are equal by basic Galois theory, it follows that the order of the decomposition group *D*<sub>*P*<sub>*j*</sub></sub> is *ef* for every *j*.

This decomposition group contains a subgroup $I_{P_{j}}$, called **inertia group** of *P*<sub>*j*</sub>, consisting of automorphisms of *L*/*K* that induce the identity automorphism on *F*<sub>*j*</sub>. In other words, $I_{P_{j}}$ is the kernel of reduction map $D_{P_j}\to\operatorname{Gal}(F_j/F)$. It can be shown that this map is surjective, and it follows that $\operatorname{Gal}(F_j/F)$ is isomorphic to $D_{P_{j}}/I_{P_{j}}$ and the order of the inertia group $I_{P_{j}}$ is *e*.

The theory of the [Frobenius element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_element) goes further, to identify an element of $D_{P_{j}} / I_{P_{j}}$ for given *j* which corresponds to the Frobenius automorphism in the Galois group of the finite field extension *F*<sub>*j*</sub> / *F*. In the unramified case the order of *D*<sub>*P*<sub>*j*</sub></sub> is *f* and $I_{P_{j}}$ is trivial, so the Frobenius element is in this case an element of *D*<sub>*P*<sub>*j*</sub></sub>, and thus also an element of *G*. For varying *j*, the groups *D*<sub>*P*<sub>*j*</sub></sub> are conjugate subgroups inside *G*: Recalling that *G* acts transitively on the *P*<sub>*j*</sub>, one checks that if $\sigma$ maps *P*<sub>*j*</sub> to *P*<sub>*j*</sub>, $\sigma D_{P_j} \sigma^{-1} = D_{P_{j'}}$. Therefore, if *G* is an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974), the Frobenius element of an unramified prime *P* does not depend on which *P*<sub>*j*</sub> we take. Furthermore, in the abelian case, associating an unramified prime of *K* to its Frobenius and extending multiplicatively defines a homomorphism from the group of unramified ideals of *K* into *G*. This map, known as the **Artin map**, is a crucial ingredient of [class field theory](wikipedia://en/242695), which studies the finite abelian extensions of a given number field *K*.[^o5]

In the geometric analogue, for [complex manifolds](wikipedia://en/509742) or [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) over an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018), the concepts of *decomposition group* and *inertia group* coincide. There, given a Galois ramified cover, all but finitely many points have the same number of [preimages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preimage).

The splitting of primes in extensions that are not Galois may be studied by using a [splitting field](wikipedia://en/174440) initially, i.e. a Galois extension that is somewhat larger. For example, [cubic fields](wikipedia://en/22076179) usually are 'regulated' by a degree 6 field containing them.

:::

## Example — the Gaussian integers {#Example_—_the_Gaussian_integers}

This section describes the splitting of prime ideals in the field extension **Q**()/**Q**. That is, we take *K* = **Q** and *L* = **Q**(), so *O*<sub>*K*</sub> is simply **Z**, and *O*<sub>*L*</sub> = **Z**\[\] is the ring of [Gaussian integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integers). Although this case is far from representative — after all, **Z**\[\] has [unique factorisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unique_factorisation), and [there aren't many imaginary quadratic fields with unique factorization](wikipedia://en/522147) — it exhibits many of the features of the theory.

Writing *G* for the Galois group of **Q**()/**Q**, and σ for the complex conjugation automorphism in *G*, there are three cases to consider.

### The prime *p* = 2 {#The_prime_p_=_2}

The prime 2 of **Z** ramifies in **Z**\[\]:

$$(2)=(1+i)^2$$

The ramification index here is therefore *e* = 2. The residue field is

$$O_L / (1+i)O_L$$

which is the finite field with two elements. The decomposition group must be equal to all of *G*, since there is only one prime of **Z**\[\] above 2. The inertia group is also all of *G*, since

$$a+bi\equiv a-bi\bmod1+i$$

for any integers *a* and *b*, as $a+bi = 2bi + a-bi =(1+i) \cdot (1-i)bi + a-bi \equiv a-bi \bmod 1+i$ .

In fact, 2 is the *only* prime that ramifies in **Z**\[\], since every prime that ramifies must divide the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/3022963) of **Z**\[\], which is −4.

### Primes *p* ≡ 1 mod 4 {#Primes_p_&equiv;_1_mod_4}

Any prime *p* ≡ 1 mod 4 *splits* into two distinct prime ideals in **Z**\[\]; this is a manifestation of [Fermat's theorem on sums of two squares](wikipedia://en/1850216). For example:

$$13=(2+3i)(2-3i)$$

The decomposition groups in this case are both the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059) {1}; indeed the automorphism σ *switches* the two primes () and (), so it cannot be in the decomposition group of either prime. The inertia group, being a subgroup of the decomposition group, is also the trivial group. There are two residue fields, one for each prime,

$$O_L / (2 \pm 3i)O_L\ ,$$

which are both isomorphic to the finite field with 13 elements. The Frobenius element is the trivial automorphism; this means that

$$(a+bi)^{13}\equiv a + bi\bmod2\pm3i$$

for any integers *a* and *b*.

### Primes *p* ≡ 3 mod 4 {#Primes_p_&equiv;_3_mod_4}

Any prime *p* ≡ 3 mod 4 remains *inert* in **Z**\[\]; that is, it does *not* split. For example, (7) remains prime in **Z**\[\]. In this situation, the decomposition group is all of *G*, again because there is only one prime factor. However, this situation differs from the *p* = 2 case, because now σ does *not* act trivially on the residue field

$$O_L / (7)O_L\ ,$$

which is the finite field with 7<sup>2</sup> = 49 elements. For example, the difference between $1+i$ and $\sigma(1 + i) = 1 - i$ is $2i$, which is certainly not divisible by 7. Therefore, the inertia group is the trivial group {1}. The Galois group of this residue field over the subfield **Z**/7**Z** has order 2, and is generated by the image of the Frobenius element. The Frobenius element is none other than σ; this means that

$$(a+bi)^7\equiv a-bi\bmod7$$

for any integers *a* and *b*.

### Summary {#Summary}

::: {#o11 .table cue=table}

| Prime in **Z** | How it splits in **Z**\[\] | Inertia group | Decomposition group |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Ramifies with index 2 | *G* | *G* |
| p ≡ 1 mod 4 | Splits into two distinct factors | 1 | 1 |
| p ≡ 3 mod 4 | Remains inert | 1 | *G* |

:::

## Computing the factorisation {#Computing_the_factorisation}

Suppose that we wish to determine the factorisation of a prime ideal *P* of *O*<sub>*K*</sub> into primes of *O*<sub>*L*</sub>. The following procedure (Neukirch, p. 47) solves this problem in many cases. The strategy is to select an integer θ in *O*<sub>*L*</sub> so that *L* is generated over *K* by θ (such a θ is guaranteed to exist by the [primitive element theorem](wikipedia://en/448933)), and then to examine the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106) *H*(*X*) of θ over *K*; it is a [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730) with coefficients in *O*<sub>*K*</sub>. Reducing the coefficients of *H*(*X*) modulo *P*, we obtain a monic polynomial *h*(*X*) with coefficients in *F*, the (finite) residue field *O*<sub>*K*</sub>/*P*. Suppose that *h*(*X*) factorises in the polynomial ring *F*\[*X*\] as

$$h(X) = h_1(X)^{e_1} \cdots h_n(X)^{e_n},$$

where the *h*<sub>*j*</sub> are distinct monic irreducible polynomials in *F*\[*X*\]. Then, as long as *P* is not one of finitely many exceptional primes (the precise condition is described below), the factorisation of *P* has the following form:

$$P O_L = Q_1^{e_1} \cdots Q_n^{e_n},$$

where the *Q*<sub>*j*</sub> are distinct prime ideals of *O*<sub>*L*</sub>. Furthermore, the inertia degree of each *Q*<sub>*j*</sub> is equal to the degree of the corresponding polynomial *h*<sub>*j*</sub>, and there is an explicit formula for the *Q*<sub>*j*</sub>:

$$Q_j = P O_L + h_j(\theta) O_L,$$

where *h*<sub>*j*</sub> denotes here a lifting of the polynomial *h*<sub>*j*</sub> to *K*\[*X*\].

In the Galois case, the inertia degrees are all equal, and the ramification indices *e*<sub>1</sub> = ... = *e*<sub>*n*</sub> are all equal.

The exceptional primes, for which the above result does not necessarily hold, are the ones not relatively prime to the [conductor](wikipedia://en/18582715) of the ring *O*<sub>*K*</sub>\[θ\]. The conductor is defined to be the ideal

$$\{ y \in O_L : yO_L \subseteq O_K[\theta]\};$$

it measures how far the [order](wikipedia://en/1114171) *O*<sub>*K*</sub>\[θ\] is from being the whole ring of integers (maximal order) *O*<sub>*L*</sub>.

A significant caveat is that there exist examples of *L*/*K* and *P* such that there is *no* available θ that satisfies the above hypotheses (see for example [^o13]). Therefore, the algorithm given above cannot be used to factor such *P*, and more sophisticated approaches must be used, such as that described in.[^o14]

::: {#o16 .example cue=heading name="An example"}

### An example {#An_example}

Consider again the case of the Gaussian integers. We take θ to be the imaginary unit $i$, with minimal polynomial *H*(*X*) = *X*<sup>2</sup> + 1. Since **Z**\[$i$\] is the whole ring of integers of **Q**($i$), the [conductor](wikipedia://en/26355302) is the unit ideal, so there are no exceptional primes.

For *P* = (2), we need to work in the field **Z**/(2)**Z**, which amounts to factorising the polynomial *X*<sup>2</sup> + 1 modulo 2:

$$X^2 + 1 = (X+1)^2 \pmod 2.$$

Therefore, there is only one prime factor, with inertia degree 1 and ramification index 2, and it is given by

$$Q = (2)\mathbf Z[i] + (i+1)\mathbf Z[i] = (1+i)\mathbf Z[i].$$

The next case is for *P* = (*p*) for a prime *p* ≡ 3 mod 4. For concreteness we will take *P* = (7). The polynomial *X*<sup>2</sup> + 1 is irreducible modulo 7. Therefore, there is only one prime factor, with inertia degree 2 and ramification index 1, and it is given by

$$Q = (7)\mathbf Z[i] + (i^2 + 1)\mathbf Z[i] = 7\mathbf Z[i].$$

The last case is *P* = (*p*) for a prime *p* ≡ 1 mod 4; we will again take *P* = (13). This time we have the factorisation

$$X^2 + 1 = (X + 5)(X - 5) \pmod{13}.$$

Therefore, there are *two* prime factors, both with inertia degree and ramification index 1. They are given by

$$Q_1 = (13)\mathbf Z[i] + (i + 5)\mathbf Z[i] = \cdots = (2+3i)\mathbf Z[i]$$

and

$$Q_2 = (13)\mathbf Z[i] + (i - 5)\mathbf Z[i] = \cdots = (2-3i)\mathbf Z[i].$$
