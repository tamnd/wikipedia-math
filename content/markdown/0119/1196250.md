# Triangular orthobicupola

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Triangular orthobicupola"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **triangular orthobicupola** is the 27th [Johnson solid](wikipedia://en/16034). As the name suggests, it can be constructed by attaching two [triangular cupolae](wikipedia://en/1128465) along their bases. It has an equal number of squares and triangles at each vertex; however, it is not [vertex-transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex-transitive) because their vertex configurations differ. It is also called the **anticuboctahedron**, **twisted cuboctahedron**, or **disheptahedron**. In chemistry, the triangular orthobicupola can be found in the [coordination structure of crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordination_chemistry) with [hexagonal closed-packed spheres](wikipedia://en/901260). The [dual polyhedron](wikipedia://en/8815) of the triangular orthobicupola is the [trapezo-rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3521213). It is a [plesiohedron](wikipedia://en/53396858), a space-filling polyhedron defined by [Voronoi diagram](wikipedia://en/177668). It is also a [canonical polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_polyhedron).

:::

## Construction and naming {#Construction_and_naming}

The triangular orthobicupola is constructed by attaching two [triangular cupolae](wikipedia://en/1128465) at their bases. This construction also applies to the [cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/6280), which could also be called the triangular gyrobicupola; the difference is that the two triangular cupolas that make up the triangular orthobicupola are joined so that the triangular bases have the same orientation (hence, "ortho"); the cuboctahedron is joined so that the triangular bases have opposite orientations. Given a triangular orthobicupola, a 60° rotation of one cupola before the joining yields a cuboctahedron. Hence, another name for the triangular orthobicupola is the anticuboctahedron.

The triangular orthobicupola is a [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron) with [regular polygonal](wikipedia://en/333306) faces (eight [equilateral triangles](wikipedia://en/173285) and six [squares](wikipedia://en/659939)) and is therefore a [Johnson solid](wikipedia://en/16034), named after American mathematician [Norman W. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_W._Johnson), who enumerated the 92 such polyhedra (excluding the [uniform polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_polyhedra)). It is enumerated as the twenty-seventh Johnson solid, $J_{27}$. It is a [composite polyhedron](wikipedia://en/77684519), because it can be sliced into two triangular cupolae.

## Properties {#Properties}

The [surface area](wikipedia://en/27725) $A$ and the [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) $V$ of a triangular orthobicupola are the same as those of a cuboctahedron. The surface is the sum of all of its polygonal faces: six squares and eight equilateral triangles. For edge length $a$, the areas of these of polygons are given by $a^2$ and $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a^2$, respectively. Therefore,

$$A = 6(a^2) + 8\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a^2\right) = \left(6 + 2\sqrt{3}\right)a^2 \approx 9.464a^2.$$

Its volume is twice the volume of a triangular cupola:

$$V = \frac{5\sqrt{2}}{3}a^3 \approx 2.357a^3.$$

*Image: J27 triangular orthobicupola.stl.* 3D model of a triangular orthobicupola

A triangular orthobicupola has the same symmetry as a [triangular prism](wikipedia://en/2859584), the dihedral group $\mathrm{D}_{3\mathrm{h}}$ of order six, which contains one three-fold axis and three two-fold axes.

A triangular orthobicupola has three different [dihedral angles](wikipedia://en/338046) (angles between two polygonal faces):

- An angle between two adjacent triangles is around 141°. This is obtained by adding the two triangular cupolae's triangle-to-hexagon angles.
- An angle between two adjacent squares is around 109.4°. This is obtained by adding the two triangular cupolae's square-to-hexagon angles.
- A square-to-triangle angle is the same angle as in the triangular cupola, around 125.3°.

:::

## Sphere packing {#Sphere_packing}

The [packing of congruent spheres can be arranged densely](wikipedia://en/901260) into a triangular orthobicupola. The twelve vertices represent the spheres, or [ligancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligancy). Such an arrangement is called the hexagonal close-packing, and is found in [crystal coordination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordination_chemistry). [Magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium) and [helium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium) are [chemical elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table_of_the_elements) with such a structure.

## Dual polyhedron {#Dual_polyhedron}

*Image: Trapezo-rhombic dodecahedron (green) 01.gif.* The dual of a triangular orthobicupola

The dual polyhedron of a triangular orthobicupola is the *trapezo-rhombic dodecahedron* or *trapezoidal dodecahedron*, a twelve-faced solid whose faces are six rhombi and six trapezoids. It is obtained by cutting a [rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631) in half through the hexagonal cross-section and rotating the halves 60° with respect to each other. It is a [plesiohedron](wikipedia://en/53396858), a special kind of [polyhedron that can tile a space with its copy](wikipedia://en/58607583), defined as the [Voronoi cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voronoi_cell) of a symmetric [Delone set](wikipedia://en/38607512), generated by the hexagonal close-packing.
