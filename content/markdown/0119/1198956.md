# Conditional convergence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Conditional convergence"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [series](wikipedia://en/15287) or [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) is said to be **conditionally convergent** if it converges, but it does not [converge absolutely](wikipedia://en/61532).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

More precisely, a series of real numbers $\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n$ is said to **converge conditionally** if $\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\,\sum_{n=0}^m a_n$ exists (as a finite real number, i.e. not $\infty$ or $-\infty$), but $\sum_{n=0}^\infty \left|a_n\right| = \infty.$

A classic example is the [alternating](wikipedia://en/494308) harmonic series given by

$$1 - {1 \over 2} + {1 \over 3} - {1 \over 4} + {1 \over 5} - \cdots =\sum\limits_{n=1}^\infty {(-1)^{n+1} \over n},$$

which converges to $\ln (2)$, but is not absolutely convergent (see [Harmonic series](wikipedia://en/142488)).

[Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) proved that a conditionally convergent series may be [rearranged](wikipedia://en/44027) to converge to any value at all, including ∞ or −∞; see [Riemann series theorem](wikipedia://en/1895800). [Agnew's theorem](wikipedia://en/77212117) describes rearrangements that preserve convergence for all convergent series.

The [Lévy–Steinitz theorem](wikipedia://en/57275032) identifies the set of values to which a series of terms in **R**<sup>*n*</sup> can converge.

Indefinite integrals may also be conditionally convergent. A typical example of a conditionally convergent integral is (see [Fresnel integral](wikipedia://en/271143))

$$\int_{0}^{\infty} \sin(x^2) dx,$$

where the integrand oscillates between positive and negative values indefinitely, but enclosing smaller areas each time.
