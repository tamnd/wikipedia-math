# Pairing

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pairing"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **pairing** is an *R*-[bilinear map](wikipedia://en/4365#Modules) from the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of two *R*-[modules](wikipedia://en/276410), where the underlying [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *R* is [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let *R* be a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) with [unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_%28mathematics%29), and let *M*, *N* and *L* be [*R*-modules](wikipedia://en/276410).

A **pairing** is any *R*-bilinear map $e:M \times N \to L$. That is, it satisfies

$$e(r\cdot m,n)=e(m,r \cdot n)=r\cdot e(m,n),$$

$e(m_1+m_2,n)=e(m_1,n)+e(m_2,n)$ and $e(m,n_1+n_2)=e(m,n_1)+e(m,n_2)$

for any $r \in R$ and any $m,m_1,m_2 \in M$ and any $n,n_1,n_2 \in N$. Equivalently, a pairing is an *R*-linear map

$$M \otimes_R N \to L$$

where $M \otimes_R N$ denotes the [tensor product](wikipedia://en/2993836) of *M* and *N*.

A pairing can also be considered as an [*R*-linear map](wikipedia://en/474118) $\Phi : M \to \operatorname{Hom}_{R} (N, L)$, which matches the first definition by setting $\Phi (m) (n) := e(m,n)$.

A pairing is called **perfect** if the above map $\Phi$ is an isomorphism of *R*-modules and the other evaluation map $\Phi'\colon N\to \operatorname{Hom}_{R}(M,L)$ is an isomorphism also. In nice cases, it suffices that just one of these be an isomorphism, e.g. when *R* is a field, *M,N* are finite dimensional vector spaces and *L=R*.

A pairing is called **non-degenerate on the right** if for the above map we have that $e(m,n) = 0$ for all $m$ implies $n=0$; similarly, $e$ is called **non-degenerate on the left** if $e(m,n) = 0$ for all $n$ implies $m=0$.

When $N=M$, a pairing is called **symmetric** if $e(m,n)=e(n,m)$ for all *m*, *n*, and **alternating** if $e(m,m) = 0$ for all *m*. In particular, this implies $e(m+n,m+n)=0$, while bilinearity shows $e(m+n,m+n)=e(m,m)+e(m,n)+e(n,m)+e(n,n)=e(m,n)+e(n,m)$. Thus, for an alternating pairing, $e(m,n)=-e(n,m)$.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

For a vector space $V$ over a field $k$, a pairing $V\times V\to k$ is also called a [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477). Examples include any [scalar product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_product) on a **real** vector space *V*, or the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) map (2 × 2 matrices over *k*) → *k* when viewed as a pairing $k^2 \times k^2 \to k$.

For the [dual module](wikipedia://en/44902322) $M^*$ of $M$, the evaluation map $M^*\times M\to R$, $(\lambda,m)\mapsto\lambda(m)$ is known as the natural pairing.

Other examples of pairings occur in various [duality](wikipedia://en/609737) theories, such as [Serre duality](wikipedia://en/359175) or [Poincaré duality](wikipedia://en/540732). See also [Dual system](wikipedia://en/63735167).

Another example is the [Yoneda product](wikipedia://en/46783690) between [Ext groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ext_group).

The [Hopf map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopf_map) $S^3 \to S^2$ written as $h:S^2 \times S^2 \to S^2$ is an example of a pairing. For instance, Hardie et al.[^o6] present an explicit construction of the map using poset models.

:::

## Pairings in cryptography {#Pairings_in_cryptography}

In [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), often the following specialized definition is used:[^o8]

Let $\textstyle G_1, G_2$ be additive groups and $\textstyle G_T$ a multiplicative [group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_%28Mathematics%29), all of prime [order](wikipedia://en/493527) $\textstyle p$. Let $\textstyle P \in G_1, Q \in G_2$ be [generators](wikipedia://en/99945) of $\textstyle G_1$ and $\textstyle G_2$ respectively.

A pairing is a map: $e: G_1 \times G_2 \rightarrow G_T$

for which the following holds:

1. [Bilinearity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilinearity): $\textstyle \forall a,b \in \mathbb{Z}:\ e\left(aP, bQ\right) = e\left(P, Q\right)^{ab}$
1. [Non-degeneracy](wikipedia://en/232952): $\textstyle e\left(P, Q\right) \neq 1$
1. For practical purposes, $\textstyle e$ has to be [computable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computable) in an efficient manner

Note that it is also common in cryptographic literature for all groups to be written in multiplicative notation.

In cases when $\textstyle G_1 = G_2 = G$, the pairing is called symmetric. As $\textstyle G$ is [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327), the map $e$ will be [commutative](wikipedia://en/294390); that is, for any $P,Q \in G$, we have $e(P,Q) = e(Q,P)$. This is because for a generator $g \in G$, there exist integers $p$, $q$ such that $P = g^p$ and $Q=g^q$. Therefore $e(P,Q) = e(g^p,g^q) = e(g,g)^{pq} = e(g^q, g^p) = e(Q,P)$.

The [Weil pairing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weil_pairing) is an important concept in [elliptic curve cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curve_cryptography); e.g., it may be used to attack certain elliptic curves (see [MOV attack](https://crypto.stackexchange.com/q/1871)). It and other pairings have been used to develop [identity-based encryption](wikipedia://en/385892) schemes.

## Slightly different usages of the notion of pairing {#Slightly_different_usages_of_the_notion_of_pairing}

Scalar products on [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) are sometimes called pairings, although they are not bilinear. For example, in [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200), one has a scalar product on the characters of complex representations of a finite group which is frequently called **character pairing**.
