# Whitehead manifold

*Image: Whitehead manifold.png.* First three tori of Whitehead manifold construction

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Whitehead manifold"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Whitehead manifold** is an open [3-manifold](wikipedia://en/1018257) that is [contractible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contractible), but not [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to $\mathbb{R}^3.$ It was discovered by (txt) while trying to prove the [Poincaré conjecture](wikipedia://en/23798), correcting an error in an earlier paper (Whitehead 1934, theorem 3) where he incorrectly claimed that no such manifold exists.

:::

A contractible [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) is one that can continuously be shrunk to a point inside the manifold itself. For example, an [open ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_ball) is a contractible manifold. All manifolds homeomorphic to the ball are contractible, too. One can ask whether *all* contractible manifolds are homeomorphic to a ball. For dimensions 1 and 2, the answer is classical and it is "yes". In dimension 2, it follows, for example, from the [Riemann mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/26215). Dimension 3 presents the first [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151): the Whitehead manifold.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

Take a copy of $S^3,$ the [three-dimensional sphere](wikipedia://en/39792). Now find a compact unknotted [solid torus](wikipedia://en/1251473) $T_1$ inside the sphere. (A solid torus is the topological space $S^1\times D^2$. Intuitively, it is an ordinary three-dimensional [doughnut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doughnut), that is, a filled-in [torus](wikipedia://en/74800), which is topologically the [product](wikipedia://en/24910) of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) and a [disk](wikipedia://en/329542).) The [closed](wikipedia://en/42309) complement of the unknotted solid torus inside $S^3$ is another solid torus.

*Image: Whiteheadlink.png.* A thickened Whitehead link. In the Whitehead manifold construction, the blue (untwisted) torus is a [tubular neighborhood](wikipedia://en/3192042) of the meridian curve of $T_1$, and the orange torus is $T_2.$ Everything must be contained within $T_1.$

Now take a second unknotted solid torus $T_2$ inside $T_1$ so that $T_2$ and a [tubular neighborhood](wikipedia://en/3192042) of the meridian curve of $T_1$ is a thickened [Whitehead link](wikipedia://en/1966668).

Note that $T_2$ is [null-homotopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null-homotopic) in the complement of the meridian of $T_1.$ This can be seen by considering $S^3$ as $\mathbb{R}^3 \cup \{\infty\}$ and the meridian curve as the *z*-axis together with $\infty.$ The torus $T_2$ has zero [winding number](wikipedia://en/86113) around the *z*-axis. Thus the necessary null-homotopy follows. Since the Whitehead link is symmetric, that is, a homeomorphism of the 3-sphere switches components, it is also true that the meridian of $T_1$ is also null-homotopic in the complement of $T_2.$

Now embed $T_3$ inside $T_2$ in the same way as $T_2$ lies inside $T_1,$ and so on; to infinity. Define *W*, the **Whitehead continuum**, to be $W = T_\infty,$ or more precisely the intersection of all the $T_k$ for $k = 1,2,3,\dots.$

The Whitehead manifold is defined as $X = S^3 \setminus W,$ which is a non-compact manifold without boundary. It follows from our previous observation, the [Hurewicz theorem](wikipedia://en/591587), and [Whitehead's theorem](wikipedia://en/976793) on homotopy equivalence, that *X* is contractible. In fact, a closer analysis involving a result of [Morton Brown](wikipedia://en/27699417) shows that $X \times \mathbb{R} \cong \mathbb{R}^4.$ However, *X* is not homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^3.$ The reason is that it is not [simply connected at infinity](wikipedia://en/17208553).

The one point compactification of *X* is the space $S^3/W$ (with *W* crunched to a point). It is not a manifold. However, $\left(\mathbb{R}^3/W\right) \times \mathbb{R}$ is homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^4.$

[David Gabai](wikipedia://en/6129609) showed that *X* is the union of two copies of $\mathbb{R}^3$ whose intersection is also homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^3.$[^o4]

:::

## Related spaces {#Related_spaces}

More examples of open, contractible 3-manifolds may be constructed by proceeding in similar fashion and picking different embeddings of $T_{i+1}$ in $T_i$ in the iterative process. Each embedding should be an unknotted solid torus in the 3-sphere. The essential properties are that the meridian of $T_i$ should be [null-homotopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null-homotopic) in the complement of $T_{i+1},$ and in addition the longitude of $T_{i+1}$ should not be null-homotopic in $T_i \setminus T_{i+1}.$

Another variation is to pick several subtori at each stage instead of just one. The cones over some of these continua appear as the complements of [Casson handles](wikipedia://en/7024176) in a 4-ball.

The [dogbone space](wikipedia://en/25781757) is not a manifold but its product with $\mathbb{R}^1$ is homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^4.$
