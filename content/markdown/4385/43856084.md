# Icosahedron

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Icosahedron"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), an **icosahedron** ( or ) is a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) with 20 faces. The name comes grc. The plural can be either "icosahedra" () or "icosahedrons".

:::

There are infinitely many non-[similar](wikipedia://en/39377) shapes of icosahedra, some of them being more symmetrical than others. The best known is the ([convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron), non-[stellated](wikipedia://en/180253)) [regular icosahedron](wikipedia://en/14968)—one of the [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905)—whose faces are 20 [equilateral triangles](wikipedia://en/173285).

## Regular icosahedra {#Regular_icosahedra}

There are two objects, one convex and one nonconvex, that can both be referred to as regular icosahedra, characterized by 30 edges and 20 triangular faces. The term "regular icosahedron" generally refers to [a convex polyhedron](wikipedia://en/14968), both a [deltahedron](wikipedia://en/493995) and a [Platonic solid](wikipedia://en/23905); it is also called "icosahedron" for a plain term.[^o3] A non-convex polyhedron version is the [great icosahedron](wikipedia://en/3029898), a [Kepler&ndash;Poinsot polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler&ndash;Poinsot_polyhedron). Both have [icosahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2877844).

There are 59 stellations of a regular icosahedron (including the original icosahedron itself) according to Coxeter et al. in *[The Fifty-Nine Icosahedra](wikipedia://en/13181245)*. Being [stellated](wikipedia://en/180253) means that a polyhedron extends its faces or edges until they meet to form a new polyhedron. It is done symmetrically so that the resulting figure retains the overall symmetry of the parent figure. The regular icosahedron and the great icosahedron are among them. Other stellations have more than one face in each plane or form compounds of simpler polyhedra. These are not strictly icosahedra, although they are often referred to as such.[^o4]

## Pyritohedral icosahedra {#Pyritohedral_icosahedra}

*Image: Truncated octahedron internal rectangles.png.* Construction from the vertices of a [truncated octahedron](wikipedia://en/279654), showing internal rectangles.

A regular icosahedron can be distorted or marked up as a lower [pyritohedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyritohedral) symmetry,[^o6][^o8] and is called a **snub octahedron**, **snub tetratetrahedron**, **snub tetrahedron**, and **pseudo-icosahedron**.[^o7] This can be seen as an [alternated](wikipedia://en/4349117) [truncated octahedron](wikipedia://en/279654). If all the triangles are [equilateral](wikipedia://en/173285), the symmetry can also be distinguished by colouring the 8 and 12 triangle sets differently. [Pyritohedral symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyritohedral_symmetry) has the symbol (3\*2), \[3<sup>\+</sup>,4\], with order 24. [Tetrahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2904472) has the symbol (332), \[3,3\]<sup>\+</sup>, with order 12. These lower symmetries allow geometric distortions from 20 equilateral triangular faces, instead having 8 equilateral triangles and 12 congruent [isosceles triangles](wikipedia://en/315428). These symmetries offer [Coxeter diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram): and respectively, each representing the lower symmetry to the [regular icosahedron](wikipedia://en/14968) , (\*532), \[5,3\] [icosahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2877844) of order 120.

The [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) of the 12 vertices can be defined by the vectors defined by all the possible cyclic permutations and sign-flips of coordinates of the form (2, 1, 0). These coordinates represent the [truncated octahedron](wikipedia://en/279654) with [alternated](wikipedia://en/4349117) vertices deleted. This construction is called a *snub tetrahedron* in its regular icosahedron form, generated by the same operations carried out starting with the vector (*φ*, 1, 0), where *φ* is the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386).[^o8]

*Image: A3-P5-P3.gif.* Progressions between an [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635), [pseudoicosahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoicosahedron), and cuboctahedron. The cuboctahedron can flex this way even if its edges (but not its faces) are rigid.

A regular icosahedron is topologically identical to a [cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/6280) with its 6 square faces bisected on diagonals with pyritohedral symmetry. The icosahedra with pyritohedral symmetry constitute an infinite family of polyhedra which include the cuboctahedron, regular icosahedron, Jessen's icosahedron, and [double cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientable_double_cover) octahedron. [Cyclical kinematic transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinematics_of_the_cuboctahedron) occur among the members of this family.
