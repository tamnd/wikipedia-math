# Right circular cylinder

*Image: Cylinder geometry.svg.* Illustration of a cylinder

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Right circular cylinder"}

A **right circular cylinder** is a [cylinder](wikipedia://en/859283) whose [generatrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generatrices) are perpendicular to the bases. Thus, in a right circular cylinder, the [generatrix](wikipedia://en/24801595) and the height have the same measurements.[^o2] It is also less often called a cylinder of revolution, because it can be obtained by rotating a rectangle of sides $r$ and $g$ around one of its sides. Fixing $g$ as the side on which the revolution takes place, we obtain that the side $r$, perpendicular to $g$, will be the measure of the [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) of the cylinder.[^o3]

:::

In addition to the right circular cylinder, within the study of spatial geometry there is also the *[oblique circular cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblique_cylinder)*, characterized by not having the generatrices perpendicular to the bases.[^o4]

## Elements of the right circular cylinder {#Elements_of_the_right_circular_cylinder}

**Bases**: the two [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) and [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) circles of the bases;[^o6]

**Axis**: the [line](wikipedia://en/946975) determined by the two [points](wikipedia://en/593693) of the centers of the cylinder's bases;[^o2]

**Height**: the distance between the two [planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) of the cylinder's bases;[^o3]

**Generatrices**: the line segments parallel to the axis and that have ends at the points of the bases' [circles](wikipedia://en/6220).[^o3]

## Lateral and total areas {#Lateral_and_total_areas}

*Image: Kreiszylinder.svg.* Illustration of a cylinder and the planification of its lateral surface

The lateral surface of a right cylinder is the meeting of the generatrices.[^o4] It can be obtained by the product between the length of the circumference of the base and the height of the cylinder. Therefore, the lateral surface area is given by:

- $L=2\pi rh$**.[^o3]**

Where:

$L\,$represents the lateral surface area of the cylinder;

$\pi\,$is approximately 3.14159;

$r\,$is the distance between the lateral surface of the cylinder and the axis, i.e. it is the value of the radius of the base;

$h\,$is the height of the cylinder;

$2 \pi r$ is the length of the circumference of the base, since $\pi = \frac{C}{2r}$, that is, $C = 2\pi r$.[^o8]

Note that in the case of the right circular cylinder, the height and the generatrix have the same measure, so the lateral area can also be given by:

$$L = 2 \pi r g.$$

The area of the base of a cylinder is the area of a circle (in this case, we define that the circle has a radius with measure $r$):

$$B = \pi r^2.$$

To calculate the total area of a right circular cylinder, you simply [add](wikipedia://en/61338) the lateral area to the area of the two bases:

$$A = L + 2 \cdot B.$$

Replacing $L = 2 \pi r h$ and $B = \pi r^2$, we have:

$A=2\pi rh + 2\pi r^2$ $\Rightarrow A = 2 \pi r (h + r)$

or even

$$A = 2 \pi r (g + r).$$

## Volume {#Volume}

*Image: Volume cylindre parallelepipede rectangle.svg.* Illustration of a cylinder and a prism, both with height $h$. Note that the area of the base of each solid is $S$.

[Cavalieri's principle](wikipedia://en/21697672) states that if two solids of the same height and congruent base areas, are positioned on the same plane, such that any other plane parallel to this plane sections both solids, determining from this section two [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621) with the same area,[^o10] then the volume of the two solids will be the same. One can use Cavalieri's principle to determine the volume of the cylinder.

This is because the volume of a cylinder can be obtained in the same way as the volume of a [prism](wikipedia://en/283007) with the same height and the same area of the base. Therefore, simply multiply the area of the base by the height:

$$V = B \cdot h.$$

Since the area of a circle of radius $r\,$, which is the base of the cylinder, is given by $B = \pi r^2$ it follows that:

$V = \pi r^2 h$[^o3]

or even

$$V = \pi r^2 g.$$

## Equation in Cartesian analytic geometry {#Equation_in_Cartesian_analytic_geometry}

The traditional representation of right circular cylinders in 3D [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202) uses the equation

$$x^2 + y^2 = r^2$$

with

$$0 \leq z \leq h$$

where $r$ is the radius of the circular base and $h$ is the height of the cylinder. However, this representation has a noticeable limitation in that the end caps of the cylinder are missing. In some applications, it is desirable to include the end caps. Moreover, this equation for the cylinder is disingenuous in three dimensions because the $z$ variable is disjoint from the main equation. In and of itself, this equation is quasi-3D.

Meanwhile, it is possible to represent the cylinder with end caps by using the [Fernandez-Guasti squircle](wikipedia://en/5565333#Fernández-Guasti_squircle).[^o12] The cylinder can be represented by the equation

$$\frac{x^2 + y^2}{r^2} + \frac{z^2}{h^2} - \frac{x^2 z^2}{r^2 h^2} - \frac{y^2 z^2}{r^2 h^2} = 1$$

with

$$-r \leq x \leq r$$

$$-r \leq y \leq r$$

$$\frac{-h}{2} \leq z \leq \frac{h}{2}$$

This equation provides a genuine 3D representation of the cylinder with end caps. Furthermore, this algebraic equation is a low degree [quartic](wikipedia://en/20133775). This [quartic surface](wikipedia://en/20133775) can be visualized using online graphing calculators such as [Desmos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desmos).[^o13]

## Equilateral cylinder {#Equilateral_cylinder}

*Image: Esfera Arquímedes.svg.* Illustration of a cylinder circumscribed by a sphere of radius $r$. Note that the cylinder is equilateral.

The equilateral cylinder is characterized by being a right circular cylinder in which the diameter of the base is equal to the value of the height (generatrix).[^o6]

Then, assuming that the radius of the base of an equilateral cylinder is $r\,$ then the diameter of the base of this cylinder is $2r\,$ and its height is $2r\,$.[^o6]

Its lateral area can be obtained by replacing the height value by $2r$:

$L = 2 \pi r \cdot 2r$ $\Rightarrow L = 4 \pi r^2$.

The result can be obtained in a similar way for the total area:

$T = 2 \pi r (h + r)$ $\Rightarrow T = 2 \pi r (2r + r)$ $\Rightarrow T = 2 \pi r \cdot 3 r$ $\Rightarrow T = 6 \pi r^2$.

For the equilateral cylinder it is possible to obtain a simpler formula to calculate the volume. Simply substitute the radius and height measurements defined earlier into the volume formula for a straight circular cylinder:

$V = \pi r^2 \cdot h$ $\Rightarrow V = \pi r^2 \cdot 2r$ $\Rightarrow V = 2\pi r^3$

## Meridian section {#Meridian_section}

It is the intersection between a plane containing the axis of the cylinder and the cylinder.[^o6]

In the case of the right circular cylinder, the meridian section is a rectangle, because the generatrix is perpendicular to the base. The equilateral cylinder, on the other hand, has a square meridian section because its height is congruent to the diameter of the base.[^o2]**[^o6]**
