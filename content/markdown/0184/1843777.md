# Brownian bridge

*Image: Brownian bridge.png.* Brownian motion, pinned at both ends. This represents a Brownian bridge.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Brownian bridge"}

A **Brownian bridge** is a continuous-time [gaussian process](wikipedia://en/302944) *B*(*t*) whose [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) is the [conditional probability distribution](wikipedia://en/504458) of a standard [Wiener process](wikipedia://en/149984) *W*(*t*) (a mathematical model of [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436)) subject to the condition (when standardized) that *W*(*T*) = 0, so that the process is pinned to the same value at both *t* = 0 and *t* = *T*. More precisely:

:::

$$B_t := (W_t\mid W_T=0),\;t \in [0,T]$$

The expected value of the bridge at any $t$ in the interval $[0,T]$ is zero, with variance $\frac{t(T-t)}{T}$, implying that the most uncertainty is in the middle of the bridge, with zero uncertainty at the nodes. The [covariance](wikipedia://en/157059) of *B*(*s*) and *B*(*t*) is $\min(s,t)-\frac{s\,t}{T}$, or $\frac{s(T-t)}{T}$ if $s < t$. The increments in a Brownian bridge are not independent.

## Relation to other stochastic processes {#Relation_to_other_stochastic_processes}

If $W(t)$ is a standard Wiener process (i.e., for $t \geq 0$, $W(t)$ is [normally distributed](wikipedia://en/21462) with expected value $0$ and variance $t$, and the [increments are stationary and independent](wikipedia://en/1025748)), then

$$B(t) = W(t) - \frac{t}{T} W(T)\,$$

is a Brownian bridge for $t \in [0, T]$. It is independent of $W(T)$[^o3]

Conversely, if $B(t)$ is a Brownian bridge for $t \in [0, 1]$ and $Z$ is a standard [normal](wikipedia://en/21462) random variable independent of $B$, then the process

$$W(t) = B(t) + tZ\,$$

is a Wiener process for $t \in [0, 1]$. More generally, a Wiener process $W(t)$ for $t \in [0, T]$ can be decomposed into

$$W(t) = \sqrt{T}B\left(\frac{t}{T}\right) + \frac{t}{\sqrt{T}} Z.$$

Another representation of the Brownian bridge based on the Brownian motion is, for $t \in [0, T]$

$$B(t) = \frac{T-t}{\sqrt T} W\left(\frac{t}{T-t}\right).$$

Conversely, for $t \in [0, \infty]$

$$W(t) = \frac{T+t}{T} B\left(\frac{Tt}{T+t}\right).$$

The Brownian bridge may also be represented as a Fourier series with stochastic coefficients, as

$$B_t = \sum_{k=1}^\infty Z_k \frac{\sqrt{2 T} \sin(k \pi t / T)}{k \pi}$$

where $Z_1, Z_2, \ldots$ are [independent identically distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_identically_distributed) standard normal random variables (see the [Karhunen–Loève theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karhunen–Loève_theorem)).

A Brownian bridge is the result of [Donsker's theorem](wikipedia://en/3828419) in the area of [empirical processes](wikipedia://en/3690433). It is also used in the [Kolmogorov–Smirnov test](wikipedia://en/16714) in the area of [statistical inference](wikipedia://en/27577).

Let $K=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|B(t)|$, for a Brownian bridge with $T = 1$; then the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) of $K$ is given by[^o4]

$$\operatorname{Pr}(K\leq x)=1-2\sum_{k=1}^\infty (-1)^{k-1} e^{-2k^2 x^2}=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{x}\sum_{k=1}^\infty e^{-(2k-1)^2\pi^2/(8x^2)}.$$

### Decomposition by zero-crossings {#Decomposition_by_zero-crossings}

The Brownian bridge can be "split" by finding the last zero $\tau_{-}$ before the midpoint, and the first zero $\tau_{+}$ after, forming a (scaled) bridge over $[0,\tau_{-}]$, an [excursion](wikipedia://en/27097375) over $[\tau_{-},\tau_{+}]$, and another bridge over $[\tau_{+},1]$. The joint pdf of $\tau_{-},\tau_{+}$ is given by

$$\rho \left( \tau_{-},\tau_{+} \right) = \frac{1}{2 \pi \sqrt{\tau_{-} (1-\tau_{+}) (\tau_{+}-\tau_{-})^3 }}$$

which can be conditionally sampled as

$$\tau_{+} = \frac{1}{1+\sin^2 \left( \frac{\pi}{2} U_1 \right)} \in \left( \frac{1}{2}, 1\right)$$

$$\tau_{-} = \frac{U_2^2 \tau_{+}}{2\tau_{+} + U_2^2 -1} \in \left( 0,\frac{1}{2} \right)$$

where $U_1,U_2$ are uniformly distributed random variables over (0,1).

## Intuitive remarks {#Intuitive_remarks}

A standard Wiener process satisfies *W*(0) = 0 and is therefore "tied down" to the origin, but other points are not restricted. In a Brownian bridge process on the other hand, not only is *B*(0) = 0 but we also require that *B*(*T*) = 0, that is the process is "tied down" at *t* = *T* as well. Just as a literal bridge is supported by pylons at both ends, a Brownian Bridge is required to satisfy conditions at both ends of the interval \[0,*T*\]. (In a slight generalization, one sometimes requires *B*(*t*<sub>1</sub>) = *a* and *B*(*t*<sub>2</sub>) = *b* where *t*<sub>1</sub>, *t*<sub>2</sub>, *a* and *b* are known constants.)

Suppose we have generated a number of points *W*(0), *W*(1), *W*(2), *W*(3), etc. of a Wiener process path by computer simulation. It is now desired to fill in additional points in the interval \[0,*T*\], that is to interpolate between the already generated points. The solution is to use a collection of *T* Brownian bridges, the first of which is required to go through the values *W*(0) and *W*(1), the second through *W*(1) and *W*(2) and so on until the *T*th goes through *W*(*T*-1) and *W*(*T*).

## General case {#General_case}

For the general case when *W*(*t*<sub>1</sub>) = *a* and *W*(*t*<sub>2</sub>) = *b*, the distribution of *B* at time *t* ∈ (*t*<sub>1</sub>, *t*<sub>2</sub>) is [normal](wikipedia://en/21462), with [mean](wikipedia://en/9653)

$$a + \frac{t-t_1}{t_2-t_1}(b-a)$$

and [variance](wikipedia://en/32344)

$$\frac{(t_2-t)(t-t_1)}{t_2-t_1},$$

and the [covariance](wikipedia://en/157059) between *B*(*s*) and *B*(*t*), with *s* \< *t* is

$$\frac{(t_2-t)(s-t_1)}{t_2-t_1}.$$
