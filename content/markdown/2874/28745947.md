# 1/3–2/3 conjecture

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="1/3–2/3 conjecture"}

*Image: Aigner poset.svg.* A partial order formed by the [series composition](wikipedia://en/28826384) of one-element and three-element partial orders. Among its 27 linear extensions, the bottom left element occurs prior to the bottom right element in 9 out of 27. Partial orders with this structure are the only known extreme cases for the 1/3–2/3 conjecture.

In [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), a branch of mathematics, the **1/3–2/3 conjecture** states that, if one is [comparison sorting](wikipedia://en/3189304) a set of items then, no matter what comparisons may have already been performed, it is always possible to choose the next comparison in such a way that it will reduce the number of possible sorted orders by a factor of 2/3 or better. Equivalently, in every finite [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) that is not [totally ordered](wikipedia://en/30330), there exists a pair of elements *x* and *y* with the property that at least 1/3 and at most 2/3 of the [linear extensions](wikipedia://en/11726298) of the partial order place *x* earlier than *y*.

:::

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

The partial order formed by three elements *a*, *b*, and *c* with a single comparability relationship, *a* ≤ *b*, has three linear extensions, *a* ≤ *b* ≤ *c*, *a* ≤ *c* ≤ *b*, and *c* ≤ *a* ≤ *b*. In all three of these extensions, *a* is earlier than *b*. However, *a* is earlier than *c* in only two of them, and later than *c* in the third. Therefore, the pair of *a* and *c* have the desired property, showing that this partial order obeys the 1/3–2/3 conjecture.

This example shows that the constants 1/3 and 2/3 in the conjecture are tight; if *q* is any fraction strictly between 1/3 and 2/3, then there would not exist a pair *x*, *y* in which *x* is earlier than *y* in a number of partial orderings that is between *q* and 1 − *q* times the total number of partial orderings.[^o4]

More generally, let *P* be any [series composition](wikipedia://en/28826384) of three-element partial orders and of one-element partial orders, such as the one in the figure. Then *P* forms an extreme case for the 1/3–2/3 conjecture in the sense that, for each pair *x*, *y* of elements, one of the two elements occurs earlier than the other in at most 1/3 of the linear extensions of *P*. Partial orders with this structure are necessarily [series-parallel](wikipedia://en/28826384) [semiorders](wikipedia://en/28771796); they are the only known extreme cases for the conjecture and can be proven to be the only extreme cases with width two.[^n1]

:::

## Definitions {#Definitions}

A partially ordered set is a set *X* together with a [binary relation](wikipedia://en/3931) ≤ that is [reflexive](wikipedia://en/200458), [antisymmetric](wikipedia://en/1176), and [transitive](wikipedia://en/200463). A total order is a partial order in which every pair of elements is comparable. A linear extension of a finite partial order is a sequential ordering of the elements of *X*, with the property that if *x* ≤ *y* in the partial order, then *x* must come before *y* in the linear extension. In other words, it is a total order compatible with the partial order. If a finite partially ordered set is totally ordered, then it has only one linear extension, but otherwise it will have more than one. The 1/3–2/3 conjecture states that one can choose two elements *x* and *y* such that, among this set of possible linear extensions, between 1/3 and 2/3 of them place *x* earlier than *y*, and symmetrically between 1/3 and 2/3 of them place *y* earlier than *x*.[^n2]

There is an alternative and equivalent statement of the 1/3–2/3 conjecture in the language of [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542). One may define a [uniform probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28discrete%29) on the linear extensions in which each possible linear extension is equally likely to be chosen. The 1/3–2/3 conjecture states that, under this probability distribution, there exists a pair of elements *x* and *y* such that the probability that *x* is earlier than *y* in a random linear extension is between 1/3 and 2/3.[^n2]

In 1984, [Jeff Kahn](wikipedia://en/26919476) and [Michael Saks](wikipedia://en/25011970) defined δ(*P*), for any partially ordered set *P*, to be the largest [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) δ such that *P* has a pair *x*, *y* with *x* earlier than *y* in a number of linear extensions that is between δ and 1 − δ of the total number of linear extensions. In this notation, the 1/3–2/3 conjecture states that every finite partial order that is not total has δ(*P*) ≥ 1/3.[^o10]

:::

## History {#History}

The 1/3–2/3 conjecture was formulated by [Sergey Kislitsyn](wikipedia://en/72061753) in 1968,[^o12] and later made independently by [Michael Fredman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Fredman)[^o13] and by [Nati Linial](wikipedia://en/28755276) in 1984.[^n2] It was listed as a featured unsolved problem at the founding of the journal *[Order](wikipedia://en/28780245)*, and remains unsolved; being called "one of the most intriguing problems in the combinatorial theory of posets."[^n2]

A survey of the conjecture was produced in 1999.[^o16]

## Partial results {#Partial_results}

The 1/3–2/3 conjecture is known to be true for certain special classes of partial orders, including partial orders of [width](wikipedia://en/348973) two,[^o18] partial orders of height two,[^n3] partial orders with at most 13 elements,[^o20] partial orders in which each element is incomparable to at most six others,[^o21] [series-parallel partial orders](wikipedia://en/28826384), partial orders whose [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199) is *N*-free,[^n4] [semiorders](wikipedia://en/28771796),[^n5] and [polytrees](wikipedia://en/1731488).[^n6] A 2026 preprint reports verification through 14 elements.[^n7] In the limit as *n* goes to infinity, the proportion of *n*-element partial orders that obey the 1/3–2/3 conjecture approaches 100%.[^o26]

In 1995, Graham Brightwell, Stefan Felsner, and William Trotter proved that, for any finite partial order *P* that is not total, $\delta(P) \ge 1 / 2 - \sqrt{5} / 10 \approx 0.276$. Their results improve previous weaker bounds of the same type.[^o27] They use the probabilistic interpretation of $\delta(P)$ to extend its definition to certain infinite partial orders; in that context, they show that their bounds are optimal, in that there exist infinite partial orders with $\delta(P) = 1 / 2 - \sqrt{5} / 10$.

## Applications {#Applications}

In 1984 [Jeff Kahn](wikipedia://en/26919476) and Saks proposed the following application for the problem: suppose one wishes to [comparison sort](wikipedia://en/3189304) a totally ordered set *X*, for which some partial order information is already known in the form of a partial order on *X*. In the worst case, each additional comparison between a pair *x* and *y* of elements may yield as little information as possible, by resolving the comparison in a way that leaves as many linear extensions as possible compatible with the comparison result. The 1/3–2/3 conjecture states that, at each step, one may choose a comparison to perform that reduces the remaining number of linear extensions by a factor of 2/3; therefore, if there are *E* linear extensions of the partial order given by the initial information, the sorting problem can be completed in at most $\log_{3/2} E$ additional comparisons.[^o10]

However, this analysis neglects the complexity of selecting the optimal pair *x* and *y* to compare. Additionally, it may be possible to sort a partial order using a number of comparisons that is better than this analysis would suggest, because it may not be possible for this worst-case behavior to occur at each step of a [sorting algorithm](wikipedia://en/28442). In this direction, it has been conjectured that $\log_{\phi} E$ comparisons may suffice, where $\phi$ denotes the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386).[^o26]

A closely related class of comparison sorting problems was considered by Fredman in 1976, among them the problem of comparison sorting a set *X* when the sorted order of *X* is known to lie in some set *S* of permutations of *X*. Here *S* is not necessarily generated as the set of linear extensions of a partial order. Despite this added generality, Fredman showed that *X* can be sorted using $\log_2 |S| + O(|X|)$ comparisons, expressed in [big O notation](wikipedia://en/44578). This same bound applies as well to the case of partial orders and shows that $\log_2 E + O(n)$ comparisons suffice.[^o30]

:::

## Generalizations and related results {#Generalizations_and_related_results}

In 1984, Kahn and Saks conjectured that, in the limit as $w$ tends to infinity, the value of $\delta(P)$ for partially ordered sets of width $w$ should tend to $1/2$. In particular, they expect that only partially ordered sets of width two can achieve the worst case value $\delta(P) = 1/3$[^n8] and in 1985 [Martin Aigner](wikipedia://en/24448177) stated this explicitly as a conjecture.[^n1] The smallest known value of $\delta(P)$ for posets of width three is $14/39$,[^n9] and computer searches have shown that no smaller value is possible for width-3 posets with nine or fewer elements.[^n3] Another related conjecture, again based on computer searches, states that there is a gap between $1/3$ and the other possible values of $\delta(P)$: whenever a partial order does not have $\delta(P)$ exactly $1/3$, it has $\delta(P) \ge 0.348843$.[^n10]

Marcin Peczarski[^o26][^o21] has formulated a "gold partition conjecture" stating that in each partial order that is not a total order one can find two consecutive comparisons such that, if $t_i$ denotes the number of linear extensions remaining after $i$ of the comparisons have been made, then (in each of the four possible outcomes of the comparisons) $t_0 \ge t_1 + t_2$. If this conjecture is true, it would imply the 1/3–2/3 conjecture: the first of the two comparisons must be between a pair that splits the remaining comparisons by at worst a 1/3–2/3 ratio. The gold partition conjecture would also imply that a partial order with $E$ linear extensions can be sorted in at most $\log_\phi E$ comparisons; the name of the conjecture is derived from this connection with the golden ratio.

It is [\#P-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharp-P-complete), given a finite partial order $P$ and a pair of elements $x$ and $y$, to calculate the proportion of the linear extensions of $P$ that place $x$ earlier than $y$.[^o37]
