# Nakagami distribution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nakagami distribution"}

The **Nakagami distribution** or the **Nakagami-*m* distribution** is a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) related to the [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079). The family of Nakagami distributions has two parameters: a [shape parameter](wikipedia://en/9210865) $m\geq 1/2$ and a [scale parameter](wikipedia://en/769503) $\Omega > 0$. It is used to model physical phenomena such as those found in medical ultrasound imaging, communications engineering, meteorology, hydrology, multimedia, and seismology.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Characterization"}

## Characterization {#Characterization}

Its [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) (pdf) is[^o4]

$$f(x;\,m,\Omega) = \frac{2m^m}{\Gamma(m)\Omega^m}x^{2m-1}\exp\left(-\frac{m}{\Omega}x^2\right) \text{ for } x\geq 0.$$

where $m\geq 1/2$ and $\Omega>0$.

Its [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) (CDF) is[^o4]

$$F(x;\,m,\Omega) = \frac{\gamma\left(m, \frac{m}{\Omega}x^2\right)}{\Gamma(m)} = P\left(m, \frac{m}{\Omega}x^2\right)$$

where *P* is the regularized (lower) [incomplete gamma function](wikipedia://en/456050#Regularized_gamma_functions_and_Poisson_random_variables).

:::

## Parameterization {#Parameterization}

The parameters $m$ and $\Omega$ are[^o6]

$$m = \frac{\left( \operatorname{E} [X^2] \right)^2 } {\operatorname{Var} [X^2]},$$

and

$$\Omega = \operatorname{E} [X^2].$$

No closed form solution exists for the [median](wikipedia://en/18837) of this distribution, although special cases do exist, such as $\sqrt{\Omega \ln(2)}$ when *m* = 1. For practical purposes the median would have to be calculated as the 50th-percentile of the observations.

## Parameter estimation {#Parameter_estimation}

An alternative way of fitting the distribution is to re-parametrize $\Omega$ as *σ* = Ω/*m*.[^o8]

Given [independent](wikipedia://en/27593) observations $X_1=x_1,\ldots,X_n=x_n$ from the Nakagami distribution, the [likelihood function](wikipedia://en/44968) is

$$L( \sigma, m) = \left( \frac{2}{\Gamma(m)\sigma^m} \right)^n \left( \prod_{i=1}^n x_i\right)^{2m-1} \exp\left(-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2} \sigma \right).$$

Its logarithm is

$$\ell(\sigma, m) = \log L(\sigma,m) = -n \log \Gamma(m) - nm\log\sigma + (2m-1) \sum_{i=1}^n \log x_i - \frac{ \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2} \sigma.$$

Therefore

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\sigma} = \frac{-nm\sigma+\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}{\sigma^2} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial m} = -n\frac{\Gamma'(m)}{\Gamma(m)} -n \log\sigma + 2\sum_{i=1}^n \log x_i. \end{aligned}$$

These derivatives vanish only when

$$\sigma= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}{nm}$$

and the value of *m* for which the derivative with respect to *m* vanishes is found by numerical methods including the [Newton–Raphson method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton–Raphson_method).

It can be shown that at the critical point a global maximum is attained, so the critical point is the maximum-likelihood estimate of (*m*,*σ*). Because of the [equivariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivariance) of maximum-likelihood estimation, a maximum likelihood estimate for Ω is obtained as well.

## Random variate generation {#Random_variate_generation}

The Nakagami distribution is related to the [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079). In particular, given a random variable $Y \, \sim \textrm{Gamma}(k, \theta)$, it is possible to obtain a random variable $X \, \sim \textrm{Nakagami} (m, \Omega)$, by setting $k=m$, $\theta=\Omega / m$, and taking the square root of $Y$:

$$X = \sqrt{Y}. \,$$

Alternatively, the Nakagami distribution $f(y; \,m,\Omega)$ can be generated from the [chi distribution](wikipedia://en/1901933) with parameter $k$ set to $2m$ and then following it by a scaling transformation of random variables. That is, a Nakagami random variable $X$ is generated by a simple scaling transformation on a chi-distributed random variable $Y \sim \chi(2m)$ as below.

$$X = \sqrt{(\Omega / 2 m)}Y .$$

For a chi-distribution, the degrees of freedom $2m$ must be an integer, but for Nakagami the $m$ can be any real number greater than 1/2. This is the critical difference and accordingly, Nakagami-m is viewed as a generalization of chi-distribution, similar to a gamma distribution being considered as a generalization of chi-squared distributions.

## History and applications {#History_and_applications}

The Nakagami distribution is relatively new, being first proposed in 1960 by Minoru Nakagami as a mathematical model for small-scale fading in long-distance high-frequency radio wave propagation.[^o11] It has been used to model attenuation of [wireless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless) signals [traversing multiple paths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multipath_propagation)[^o12] and to study the impact of [fading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fading) channels on wireless communications.[^o13]

## Related distributions {#Related_distributions}

- Restricting *m* to the unit interval (*q* = *m*; 0 \< *q* \< 1) defines the **Nakagami**-***q*** distribution, also known as **Hoyt distribution**, first studied by R.S. Hoyt in the 1940s.[^o15][^o16][^o17] In particular, the [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) around the true mean in a [bivariate normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bivariate_normal) random variable, re-written in [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) (radius and angle), follows a Hoyt distribution. Equivalently, the [modulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulus_of_complex_number) of a [complex normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_normal) random variable also does.
- With 2*m* = *k*, the Nakagami distribution gives a scaled [chi distribution](wikipedia://en/1901933).
- With $m = \tfrac 1 2$, the Nakagami distribution gives a scaled [half-normal distribution](wikipedia://en/15906455).
- A Nakagami distribution is a particular form of [generalized gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/24553589), with *p* = 2 and *d* = 2*m*.
