# Electromagnetic stress–energy tensor

Covariance

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Electromagnetic stress–energy tensor"}

In [relativistic physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_physics), the **electromagnetic stress–energy tensor** is the contribution to the [stress–energy tensor](wikipedia://en/70671) due to the [electromagnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field).[^o2] The stress–energy tensor describes the flow of energy and momentum in [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758). The electromagnetic stress–energy tensor contains the negative of the classical [Maxwell stress tensor](wikipedia://en/3170723) that governs the electromagnetic interactions.

:::

It was introduced by [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537) in 1907.[^o3][^o4]

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### ISQ convention {#ISQ_convention}

The electromagnetic stress–energy tensor in the [International System of Quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Quantities) (ISQ), which underlies the [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI), is[^o2]

$$T^{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{\mu_0} \left[ F^{\mu \alpha}F^\nu{}_{\alpha} - \frac{1}{4} \eta^{\mu\nu}F_{\alpha\beta} F^{\alpha\beta}\right] \,,$$

where $F^{\mu\nu}$ is the [electromagnetic tensor](wikipedia://en/1912367) and where $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ is the [Minkowski metric tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_tensor_%28general_relativity%29#Flat_spacetime) of [metric signature](wikipedia://en/463225) (− + + +) and the [Einstein summation convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_summation_convention) over repeated indices is used.

Explicitly in matrix form:

$$T^{\mu\nu} = \begin{bmatrix} u & \frac{1}{c}S_\text{x} & \frac{1}{c}S_\text{y} & \frac{1}{c}S_\text{z} \\ \frac{1}{c}S_\text{x} & -\sigma_\text{xx} & -\sigma_\text{xy} & -\sigma_\text{xz} \\ \frac{1}{c}S_\text{y} & -\sigma_\text{yx} & -\sigma_\text{yy} & -\sigma_\text{yz} \\ \frac{1}{c}S_\text{z} & -\sigma_\text{zx} & -\sigma_\text{zy} & -\sigma_\text{zz} \end{bmatrix},$$

where

$$u = \frac{1}{2}\left(\epsilon_0 \mathbf{E}^2+\frac{1}{\mu_0}\mathbf{B}^2\right)$$

is the volumetric energy density,

$$\mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}$$

is the [Poynting vector](wikipedia://en/49718),

$$\sigma_{ij} = \epsilon_0 E_i E_j + \frac{1}{{\mu _0}}B_i B_j - \frac{1}{2} \left( \epsilon_0 \mathbf{E}^2 + \frac{1}{\mu _0}\mathbf{B}^2 \right)\delta _{ij}$$

is the [Maxwell stress tensor](wikipedia://en/3170723), and $c$ is the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light). Thus, each component of $T^{\mu\nu}$ is dimensionally equivalent to pressure (with SI unit [pascal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_%28unit%29)).

### Gaussian CGS conventions {#Gaussian_CGS_conventions}

The constants in the [Gaussian system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_units) (shown here with a prime) that correspond to the [permittivity of free space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permittivity_of_free_space) and [permeability of free space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permeability_of_free_space) are

$$\epsilon_0' = \frac{1}{4\pi},\quad \mu_0' = 4\pi$$

then:

$$T^{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{4\pi} \left[F'^{\mu\alpha}F'^{\nu}{}_{\alpha} - \frac{1}{4} \eta^{\mu\nu}F'_{\alpha\beta}F'^{\alpha\beta}\right]$$

and in explicit matrix form:

$$T^{\mu\nu} = \begin{bmatrix} u & \frac{1}{c}S_\text{x} & \frac{1}{c}S_\text{y} & \frac{1}{c}S_\text{z} \\ \frac{1}{c}S_\text{x} & -\sigma_\text{xx} & -\sigma_\text{xy} & -\sigma_\text{xz} \\ \frac{1}{c}S_\text{y} & -\sigma_\text{yx} & -\sigma_\text{yy} & -\sigma_\text{yz} \\ \frac{1}{c}S_\text{z} & -\sigma_\text{zx} & -\sigma_\text{zy} & -\sigma_\text{zz} \end{bmatrix}$$

where the energy density becomes

$$u = \frac{1}{8\pi}\left(\mathbf{E}'^2 + \mathbf{B}'^2\right)$$

and the [Poynting vector](wikipedia://en/49718) becomes

$$\mathbf{S} = \frac{c}{4\pi}\mathbf{E}'\times\mathbf{B}'.$$

The stress–energy tensor for an electromagnetic field in a [dielectric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielectric) medium is less well understood and is the subject of the [Abraham–Minkowski controversy](wikipedia://en/1190648).[^o9]

The element $T^{\mu\nu}$ of the stress–energy tensor represents the flux of the component with index $\mu$ of the [four-momentum](wikipedia://en/204680) of the electromagnetic field, $P^{\mu}$, going through a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862). It represents the contribution of electromagnetism to the source of the gravitational field (curvature of spacetime) in [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024).

:::

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Algebraic properties"}

## Algebraic properties {#Algebraic_properties}

::: {#o12 .proof cue=math-proof}

The electromagnetic stress–energy tensor has several algebraic properties: It is a [symmetric tensor](wikipedia://en/23087738): $T^{\mu\nu} = T^{\nu\mu}$; The tensor $T^{\nu}{}_{\alpha}$ is [traceless](wikipedia://en/43270) (in 4D): $T^{\alpha}{}_{\alpha} = 0.$ Starting with $$T^{\mu}{}_{\mu} = \eta_{\mu\nu}T^{\mu\nu}$$ Using the explicit form of the tensor, $$T^{\mu}{}_{\mu} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\left[\eta_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\alpha}F^{\nu}{}_{\alpha} - \eta_{\mu\nu}\eta^{\mu\nu}\frac{1}{4}F^{\alpha\beta}F_{\alpha\beta}\right]$$ Lowering the indices and using the fact that $\eta^{\mu\nu}\eta_{\mu\nu} = \delta^{\mu}_{\mu}$, $$T^{\mu}{}_{\mu} = \frac{1}{\mu_0} \left[F^{\mu\alpha} F_{\mu\alpha} - \delta^{\mu}_{\mu} \frac{1}{4} F^{\alpha\beta} F_{\alpha\beta}\right]$$ Then, using $\delta^{\mu}_{\mu} = 4$, $$T^{\mu}{}_{\mu} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\left[F^{\mu\alpha} F_{\mu\alpha} - F^{\alpha\beta} F_{\alpha\beta}\right]$$ Note that in the first term, $\mu$ and $\alpha$ are dummy indices, so we relabel them as $\alpha$ and $\beta$ respectively. $$T^{\alpha}{}_{\alpha} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\left[F^{\alpha\beta} F_{\alpha\beta} - F^{\alpha\beta} F_{\alpha\beta}\right] = 0$$; The energy density is [positive-definite](wikipedia://en/649616): $T^{00} \ge 0$

:::

The symmetry of the tensor is as for a general stress–energy tensor in [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024). The trace of the energy–momentum tensor is a [Lorentz scalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_scalar); the electromagnetic field (and in particular electromagnetic waves) has no [Lorentz-invariant](wikipedia://en/18404) energy scale, so its energy–momentum tensor must have a vanishing trace. This tracelessness eventually relates to the masslessness of the [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon).[^o13]

:::

::: {#o15 .statement cue=named-heading name="Conservation laws"}

## Conservation laws {#Conservation_laws}

The electromagnetic stress–energy tensor allows a compact way of writing the [conservation laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_laws) of linear [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431) and [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) in electromagnetism. The divergence of the stress–energy tensor is:

$$\partial_\nu T^{\mu \nu} + \eta^{\mu \rho} \, f_\rho = 0 \,$$

where $f_\rho$ is the (4D) [Lorentz force](wikipedia://en/18631) per unit volume on [matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter).

This equation is equivalent to the following 3D conservation laws

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial u_\mathrm{em}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{\nabla} \cdot \mathbf{S} + \mathbf{J} \cdot \mathbf{E} &= 0 \\ \frac{\partial \mathbf{p}_\mathrm{em}}{\partial t} - \mathbf{\nabla}\cdot \sigma + \rho \mathbf{E} + \mathbf{J} \times \mathbf{B} &= 0 \ \Leftrightarrow\ \epsilon_0 \mu_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{S}}{\partial t} - \nabla \cdot \mathbf{\sigma} + \mathbf{f} = 0 \end{aligned}$$

respectively describing the electromagnetic energy density

$$u_\mathrm{em} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}^2 + \frac{1}{\mu_0}\mathbf{B}^2 \right)$$

and electromagnetic momentum density

$$\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{em} = {\mathbf{S} \over {c^2}} ,$$

where $\mathbf{J}$ is the [electric current density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current_density), $\rho$ the [electric charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge_density), and $\mathbf{f}$ is the Lorentz force density.
