# Bose–Mesner algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bose–Mesner algebra"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Bose–Mesner algebra** is a special set of [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) which arise from a combinatorial structure known as an [association scheme](wikipedia://en/2238152), together with the usual set of rules for combining (forming the products of) those matrices, such that they form an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112), or, more precisely, a [unitary commutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_algebra). Among these rules are:

:::

- the result of a product is also within the set of matrices,
- there is an identity matrix in the set, and
- taking products is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity).

Bose–Mesner algebras have applications in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) to [spin models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_model), and in [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) to the [design of experiments](wikipedia://en/9541). They are named for [R. C. Bose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._C._Bose) and Dale Marsh Mesner.[^o2]

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let *X* be a set of *v* elements. Consider a partition of the 2-element subsets of *X* into *n* non-empty subsets, *R*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *R*<sub>*n*</sub>, such that:

- given an $x \in X$, the number of $y \in X$ such that $\{x,y\} \in R_i$ depends only on i (and not on *x*). This number will be denoted by v<sub>i</sub>, and
- given $x,y \in X$ with $\{x,y\} \in R_k$, the number of $z \in X$ such that $\{x,z\} \in R_i$ and $\{z,y\} \in R_j$ depends only on *i*,*j* and *k* (and not on *x* and *y*). This number will be denoted by $p^k_{ij}$.

This structure is enhanced by adding all pairs of repeated elements of *X* and collecting them in a subset *R*<sub>0</sub>. This enhancement permits the parameters *i*, *j*, and *k* to take on the value of zero, and lets some of *x*,*y* or *z* be equal. A set with such an enhanced partition is called an [association scheme](wikipedia://en/2238152).[^o5]

A *symmetric* association scheme is one in which each $R_i$ is a [symmetric relation](wikipedia://en/200459). That is:

- if (*x*, *y*) ∈ *R*<sub>*i*</sub>, then (*y*, *x*) ∈ *R*<sub>*i*</sub>. (Or equivalently, *R*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>T</sup> = *R*<sub>*i*</sub>.)

An association scheme is *commutative* if $p_{ij}^k = p_{ji}^k$ for all $i$, $j$ and $k$. These two properties are closely related, as every symmetric association scheme is commutative.

It is important to keep in mind that there is some ambiguity when coming across the term "association scheme" in the literature, as some authors use it synonymously with "symmetric association scheme" and "commutative association scheme". Therefore, one should always verify whether *R*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>T</sup> = *R*<sub>*i*</sub> or $p^k_{ij} = p^k_{ji}$ is taken axiomatically in order to help avoid any potential confusion. The remainder of this article will assume that the association scheme is symmetric, which would imply that the association scheme is also commutative.

One may view an association scheme as a partition of the edges of a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) (with vertex set *X*) into n classes, often thought of as color classes. In this representation, there is a loop at each vertex and all the loops receive the same 0th color.

The association scheme can also be represented algebraically. Consider the [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) *D*<sub>*i*</sub> defined by:

$$(D_i)_{x,y} = \begin{cases} 1,& \text{if } \left(x,y\right)\in R_{i},\\ 0,& \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \qquad (1)$$

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be the [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) consisting of all [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) $\sideset{}{_{i=0}^{n}}\sum a_{i}D_{i}$, with $a_{i}$ complex.[^o6][^o7]

The definition of an [association scheme](wikipedia://en/2238152) is equivalent to saying that the $D_{i}$ are $v \times v$ (0,1)-[matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) which satisfy

1. $D_i$ is symmetric,
1. $\sum_{i=0}^n D_{i}=J$ (the all-ones matrix),
1. $$D_0=I,$$
1. $$D_i D_j = \sum_{k=0}^n p^k_{ij} D_k = D_j D_i,\qquad i,j=0,\ldots,n.$$

The (*x*,*y*)-th entry of the left side of 4. is the number of two colored paths of length two joining *x* and *y* (using "colors" *i* and *j*) in the graph. Note that the rows and columns of $D_i$ contain $v_i$ 1s:

$$D_i J=J D_i = v_i J. \qquad (2)$$

From 1., these [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) are [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474). From 2., $D_{0},\ldots,D_{n}$ are [linearly independent](wikipedia://en/101863), and the dimension of $\mathcal{A}$ is $n+1$. From 4., $\mathcal{A}$ is closed under multiplication, and multiplication is always associative. This [associative](wikipedia://en/2112) [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990) $\mathcal{A}$ is called the **Bose–Mesner algebra** of the [association scheme](wikipedia://en/2238152). Since the [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) in $\mathcal{A}$ are symmetric and commute with each other, they can be simultaneously diagonalized. This means that there is a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $S$ such that to each $A\in\mathcal{A}$ there is a [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080) $\Lambda_{A}$ with $S^{-1}A S=\Lambda_{A}$. This means that $\mathcal{A}$ is semi-simple and has a unique basis of primitive idempotents $J_{0},\ldots,J_{n}$. These are complex n × n [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) satisfying

$$J_i^2 =J_i, i=0,\ldots,n, \qquad (3)$$

$$J_i J_k=0, i\neq k, \qquad (4)$$

$$\sum_{i=0}^n J_i = I. \qquad (5)$$

The **Bose–Mesner algebra** has two distinguished bases: the basis consisting of the [adjacency matrices](wikipedia://en/244463) $D_i$, and the basis consisting of the irreducible [idempotent matrices](wikipedia://en/2974863) $J_k$. By definition, there exist well-defined [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) such that

$$D_{i}=\sum_{k=0}^n p_i (k) J_k, \qquad (6)$$

and

$$|X|J_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^n q_k\left(i\right)D_i. \qquad (7)$$

The p-numbers $p_i (k)$, and the q-numbers $q_k(i)$, play a prominent role in the theory.[^o8] They satisfy well-defined orthogonality relations. The p-numbers are the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) of the [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) $D_i$.

:::

::: {#o10 .statement cue=heading name="Theorem"}

## Theorem {#Theorem}

The [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) of $p_i(k)$ and $q_k(i)$, satisfy the orthogonality conditions:

$$\sum_{k=0}^n \mu_i p_i (k)p_\ell (k)=v v_i \delta_{i \ell}, \quad(8)$$

$$\sum_{k=0}^n \mu_i q_k (i) q_\ell (i)=v \mu_k \delta_{k \ell}. \quad(9)$$

Also

$$\mu_j p_i (j) = v_i q_ j (i),\quad i,j=0,\ldots,n. \quad(10)$$

In [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) notation, these are

$$P^T \Delta_\mu P=v\Delta_v, \quad(11)$$

$$Q^T \Delta_v Q=v\Delta_\mu, \quad(12)$$

where $\Delta_v = \operatorname{diag} \{v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_n\},\qquad \Delta_\mu = \operatorname{diag} \{\mu_0,\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_n\}.$

:::

::: {#o12 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of theorem"}

## Proof of theorem {#Proof_of_theorem}

The [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of $D_i D_\ell$ are $p_i (k)p_\ell (k)$ with multiplicities $\mu_k$. This implies that

$$v v_i \delta_{i\ell} = \operatorname{trace}D_i D_\ell = \sum_{k=0}^n \mu_i p_i(k) p_\ell (k), \quad(13)$$

which proves Equation $\left(8\right)$ and Equation $\left(11\right)$,

$$Q = v P^{-1} = \Delta_v^{-1} P^T \Delta_\mu, \quad(14)$$

which gives Equations $(9)$, $(10)$ and $(12)$.$\Box$

There is an analogy between extensions of [association schemes](wikipedia://en/2238152) and [extensions](wikipedia://en/605727) of [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615). The cases we are most interested in are those where the extended schemes are defined on the $n$-th [Cartesian power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_power) $X=\mathcal{F}^{n}$ of a set $\mathcal{F}$ on which a basic [association scheme](wikipedia://en/2238152) $\left(\mathcal{F},K\right)$ is defined. A first [association scheme](wikipedia://en/2238152) defined on $X=\mathcal{F}^{n}$ is called the $n$-th [Kronecker power](wikipedia://en/712430) $\left(\mathcal{F},K\right)_{\otimes}^{n}$ of $\left(\mathcal{F},K\right)$. Next the extension is defined on the same set $X=\mathcal{F}^{n}$ by gathering classes of $\left(\mathcal{F},K\right)_{\otimes}^{n}$. The [Kronecker power](wikipedia://en/712430) corresponds to the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $F\left[X\right]$ first defined on a [field](wikipedia://en/11615) $\mathbb{F}$, while the extension scheme corresponds to the [extension field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_field) obtained as a quotient. An example of such an extended scheme is the [Hamming scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamming_scheme).

[Association schemes](wikipedia://en/2238152) may be merged, but merging them leads to non-symmetric [association schemes](wikipedia://en/2238152), whereas all usual [codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code) are [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) in symmetric [Abelian schemes](wikipedia://en/247152).[^o13][^o14][^o15]
