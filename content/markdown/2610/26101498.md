# William C. Waterhouse

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="William C. Waterhouse"}

**William Charles Waterhouse** (December 31, 1941 – June 26, 2016) was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902). He was a [professor emeritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor_emeritus) of Mathematics at [Pennsylvania State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania_State_University),[^o25] after having taught there for over 35 years.[^o3] The early part of his career was at [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University).[^o3] His research interests included [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), [group schemes](wikipedia://en/510523), and the [history of mathematics](wikipedia://en/14220).[^o4]

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Waterhouse was born in [Galveston, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galveston,_Texas), on December 31, 1941, the son of William T. Waterhouse and Grace D. Waterhouse,[^o26] but grew up in [Denver, Colorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denver,_Colorado).[^o6] His father was an engineer who was employed with the [United States Bureau of Reclamation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Bureau_of_Reclamation).[^o9]

He attended [East High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_High_School_%28Denver,_Colorado%29) in Denver.[^o7] In a high school mathematics competition spanning the states of Colorado, South Dakota, and Wyoming, he received the highest score in the competition's history and helped his school gain the top mark.[^o7] As a senior, he took the [Scholastic Aptitude Test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholastic_Aptitude_Test) and received a near-perfect 797 on the verbal portion and a perfect 800 on the math portion.[^o9] He then received perfect 800 scores on three different college board [Achievement Tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achievement_Tests), those [for English Composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achievement_Test_in_English_Composition), [for Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achievement_Test_in_Chemistry), and [for advanced Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achievement_Test_in_Mathematics_Level_II),[^o9] a feat that the [Associated Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associated_Press) filed a story about and that ran in a number of newspapers around the country.[^o8] *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_%28magazine%29)* magazine ran a profile of him as well.[^o9] Waterhouse received the [National Merit Scholarship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Merit_Scholarship) and the General Motors Scholarship;[^o9] he graduated from East High in 1959.[^o7]

Waterhouse attended [Harvard College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_College). There he was a standout in the [Putnam Competition](wikipedia://en/378276): As a sophomore in the 1960 competition, he was not part of Harvard's three-person team that finished second overall, but he did achieve a top-ten individual mark;[^o10] as a junior in the 1961 competition, he attained the highest individual level – a top-five score – while helping his Harvard team to a fourth-place finish overall;[^o11] and as a senior in the 1962 competition, he again was a Putnam Fellow with a top-five score and helped his Harvard team to a third-place finish overall.[^o12]

After graduating from Harvard College with a bachelor's degree *summa cum laude* and being elected to [Phi Beta Kappa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phi_Beta_Kappa), Waterhouse continued at [Harvard Graduate School of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_Graduate_School_of_Arts_and_Sciences) where he received a master's degree.[^o15] While in the graduate school, he was awarded a [National Science Foundation Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NSF-GRFP).[^o15] He then received his Ph.D. in 1968 from Harvard for his thesis *Abelian Varieties over Finite Fields* under the supervision of [John Tate](wikipedia://en/245933).[^o28]

## Career {#Career}

*Image: Cornell University notebook and exam pages for Math 221, Spring 1973.jpg.* Student's notebook pages from a 1973 calculus class at Cornell showing Waterhouse illustrating the applicability of Fourier series to music theory by singing notes and commenting on their harmonics

Waterhouse began teaching at Cornell University in 1968.[^o26]

He had a career-long interest in the history of mathematics,[^o4] and while at Cornell wrote a history of the early years of that university's Oliver Club, a discussion forum begun by pioneering Cornell mathematician [James Edward Oliver](wikipedia://en/72580196) in the 1890s.[^o14]

Waterhouse remained an [assistant professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assistant_professor) at Cornell until 1975, at which point he was appointed an [associate professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associate_professor) at Penn State.[^o15] At Penn State, he subsequently became a [full professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_professor).[^o6]

In 1980 he married Betty Ann Senk, a doctoral student and teacher in comparative literature at Penn State.[^o16] They lived in [State College, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_College,_Pennsylvania).[^o16]

According to his obituary published in the *[Centre Daily Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_Daily_Times)*, during his career Waterhouse published over 250 articles in scholarly journals and other publications.[^o26] He was the author of the 1979 textbook *Introduction to Affine Group Schemes* for [Springer-Verlag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springer-Verlag).[^o17] [Telegraphic Reviews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telegraphic_Reviews) characterized the work as a "fairly intuitive and accessible" development of the topic, suitable for second-year graduate students.[^o18] In a 1986 volume for Springer-Verlag, he edited the 1966 translation by Arthur A. Clarke of [Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125)'s *[Disquisitiones Arithmeticae](wikipedia://en/736713)*.[^o19] Waterhouse and his wife collaborated on several translations of works by German mathematicians.[^o20] He was a member of the [Mathematical Association of America](wikipedia://en/198772) and the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822).[^o15]

Waterhouse long had an interest in [classical studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_studies);[^o26] as such, he was a member of the [Classical Association of the Middle West and South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Association_of_the_Middle_West_and_South).[^o21] He published a number of entries about language- and classics-focused matters in the journal *[Notes and Queries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notes_and_Queries)*,[^o22] as well as, in the journal *[Classical World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_World_%28journal%29)*, an exegesis of an unusual word form found in [Ovid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovid)'s *[Amores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amores_%28Ovid%29)*.[^o23] Waterhouse also had an interest in investigating quotations, whether via a [Usenet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usenet) newsgroup or publishing with [The Skeptics Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Skeptics_Society).[^o24]

By 2012, Waterhouse had moved to emeritus status.[^o25]

Waterhouse died on June 26, 2016, in State College, Pennsylvania.[^o26]

## Awards and honors {#Awards_and_honors}

Waterhouse twice won the [Lester R. Ford Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_R._Ford_Award) of the [Mathematical Association of America](wikipedia://en/198772), given to authors of articles of expository excellence. The first was in 1984 for his paper "Do Symmetric Problems Have Symmetric Solutions?"[^o28] and the second was in 1995 for his paper "A Counterexample for Germain".[^o29] The latter has been characterized as "a historical and mathematical detective story" that investigated an aspect of the correspondence between [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) and [Sophie Germain](wikipedia://en/27791), a French mathematician who used a pseudonym to disguise the fact that she was a woman.[^o30] According to his obituary, Waterhouse had a special pride in having won the two Lester R. Ford Awards.[^o26]
