# Distance modulus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Distance modulus"}

The **distance modulus** is a way of expressing [distances](wikipedia://en/39378) that is often used in [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy). It describes distances on a [logarithmic scale](wikipedia://en/164055) based on the [astronomical magnitude system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnitude_%28astronomy%29).[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The distance modulus $\mu=m-M$ is the difference between the [apparent magnitude](wikipedia://en/1962) $m$ (ideally, corrected from the effects of [interstellar absorption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_reddening)) and the [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963) $M$ of an [astronomical object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_object). It is related to the luminous distance $d$ in [parsecs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsec) by:

$$\begin{aligned} \log_{10}(d) &= 1 + \frac{\mu}{5} \\ \mu &= 5\log_{10}(d) - 5 \end{aligned}$$

This definition is convenient because the observed brightness of a light source is related to its distance by the [inverse square law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_square_law) (a source twice as far away appears one quarter as bright) and because brightnesses are usually expressed not directly, but in [magnitudes](wikipedia://en/1962).

Absolute magnitude $M$ is defined as the apparent magnitude of an object when seen at a distance of 10 [parsecs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsec). If a light source has flux $F(d)$ when observed from a distance of $d$ parsecs, and flux $F(10)$ when observed from a distance of 10 parsecs, the inverse-square law is then written like:

$$F(d) = \frac{F(10)}{\left(\frac{d}{10}\right)^2}$$

The magnitudes and flux are related by:

$$\begin{aligned} m &= -2.5 \log_{10} F(d) \\[1ex] M &= -2.5 \log_{10} F(d=10) \end{aligned}$$

Substituting and rearranging, we get:

$$\mu = m - M = 5 \log_{10}(d) - 5 = 5 \log_{10}\left(\frac{d}{10\,\mathrm{pc}}\right)$$

which means that the apparent magnitude is the absolute magnitude plus the distance modulus.

Isolating $d$ from the equation $5 \log_{10}(d) - 5 = \mu$, finds that the distance (or, the [luminosity distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminosity_distance)) in parsecs is given by

$$d = 10^{\frac{\mu}{5}+1}$$

The uncertainty in the distance in parsecs ($\delta d$) can be computed from the uncertainty in the distance modulus ($\delta\mu$) using

$$\delta d = 0.2 \ln(10) 10^{0.2\mu+1} \delta\mu \approx 0.461 d \ \delta\mu$$

which is derived using [standard error](wikipedia://en/552520) analysis.[^o5]

:::

## Different kinds of distance moduli {#Different_kinds_of_distance_moduli}

Distance is not the only quantity relevant in determining the difference between absolute and apparent magnitude. In the above, the two magnitudes correspond to [bolometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolometric_magnitude) ones, i.e. measured across all wavelengths.[^o2] In reality, detectors are more sensitive in specific [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779) ranges, where other factors, like [calibration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calibration) or [absorption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absorption_spectroscopy), could play an important role.[^o7] Absorption may even be a dominant one in particular cases (*e.g.*, in the direction of the [Galactic Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galactic_Center)). Thus, a distinction is made between distance moduli uncorrected for [interstellar absorption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_reddening), the values of which would overestimate distances if used naively, and absorption-corrected moduli.

The first ones are termed *visual distance moduli* and are denoted by ${(m - M)}_{v}$, while the second ones are called *true distance moduli* and denoted by ${(m - M)}_{0}$.

Visual distance moduli are computed by calculating the difference between the observed apparent magnitude and some theoretical estimate of the absolute magnitude. True distance moduli require a further theoretical step; that is, the estimation of the [interstellar absorption coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_absorption_coefficient).

## Usage {#Usage}

Distance moduli are most commonly used when expressing the distance to other [galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy) in the relatively nearby [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe). For example, the [Large Magellanic Cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_Magellanic_Cloud) (LMC) is at a distance modulus of 18.5,[^o9] the [Andromeda Galaxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andromeda_Galaxy)'s distance modulus is 24.4,[^o10] and the galaxy [NGC 4548](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NGC_4548) in the [Virgo Cluster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgo_Cluster) has a DM of 31.0.[^o11] In the case of the LMC, this means that [Supernova 1987A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SN_1987A), with a peak apparent magnitude of 2.8, had an absolute magnitude of −15.7, which is low by supernova standards.

Using distance moduli makes computing magnitudes easy. As for instance, a solar type star (M= 5) in the Andromeda Galaxy (DM= 24.4) would have an apparent magnitude (m) of 5 + 24.4 = 29.4, so it would be barely visible for the [Hubble Space Telescope](wikipedia://en/40203) which has a [limiting magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limiting_magnitude) of about 30.[^o12] Since it is apparent magnitudes which are actually measured at a telescope, many discussions about distances in astronomy are really discussions about the putative or derived absolute magnitudes of the distant objects being observed.
