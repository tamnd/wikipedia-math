# Strongly regular graph

*Image: Paley13.svg.* The [Paley graph](wikipedia://en/5719307) of order 13, a strongly regular graph with parameters $(13,6,2,3)$.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Strongly regular graph"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **strongly regular graph** (**SRG**) is a [regular graph](wikipedia://en/85821) $G = (V, E)$ with $v$ vertices and [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) $k$ such that for some given integers $\lambda, \mu \ge 0$

:::

- every two [adjacent vertices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjacent_vertices) have $\lambda$ common neighbours, and
- every two non-adjacent vertices have $\mu$ common neighbours.

Such a strongly regular graph is denoted by $\operatorname{srg}(v, k, \lambda, \mu)$. Its [complement graph](wikipedia://en/1175649) is also strongly regular: it is an $\operatorname{srg}(v, v - k - 1, v - 2 - 2k + \mu, v - 2k + \lambda)$.

If a graph $G$ is strongly regular with $\mu > 0$, then $G$ is [distance-regular](wikipedia://en/7768943) with diameter 2. Likewise, if $G$ is strongly regular with $\lambda = 1$, then it is [locally linear](wikipedia://en/59938063).

## Etymology {#Etymology}

A strongly regular graph is denoted as an srg(*v*, *k*, λ, μ) in the literature. By convention, graphs which satisfy the definition trivially are excluded from detailed studies and lists of strongly regular graphs. These include the disjoint union of one or more equal-sized [complete graphs](wikipedia://en/85816),[^o3][^o4] and their [complements](wikipedia://en/1175649), the complete [multipartite graphs](wikipedia://en/39469099) with equal-sized independent sets.

[Andries Brouwer](wikipedia://en/22709916) and Hendrik van Maldeghem (see #References) use an alternate but fully equivalent definition of a strongly regular graph based on [spectral graph theory](wikipedia://en/534914): a strongly regular graph is a finite regular graph that has exactly three eigenvalues, only one of which is equal to the degree *k*, of multiplicity 1. This automatically rules out fully connected graphs (which have only two distinct eigenvalues, not three) and disconnected graphs (for which the multiplicity of the degree *k* is equal to the number of different connected components, which would therefore exceed one). Much of the literature, including Brouwer, refers to the larger [eigenvalue](wikipedia://en/2161429) as *r* (with multiplicity *f*) and the smaller one as *s* (with multiplicity *g*).

## History {#History}

Strongly regular graphs were introduced by [R.C. Bose](wikipedia://en/1162266) in 1963.[^o6] They built upon earlier work in the 1950s in the then-new field of [spectral graph theory](wikipedia://en/534914).

## Examples {#Examples}

- The [cycle](wikipedia://en/878962) of length 5 is an srg(5, 2, 0, 1).
- The [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926) is an srg(10, 3, 0, 1).
- The [Clebsch graph](wikipedia://en/23970662) is an srg(16, 5, 0, 2).
- The [Shrikhande graph](wikipedia://en/13672415) is an srg(16, 6, 2, 2) which is not a [distance-transitive graph](wikipedia://en/7768222).
- The *n* × *n* square [rook's graph](wikipedia://en/7324959), i.e., the line graph of a balanced complete [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431) *K*<sub>*n*,*n*</sub>, is an srg(*n*<sup>2</sup>, 2*n* − 2, *n* − 2, 2). The parameters for *n* = 4 coincide with those of the Shrikhande graph, but the two graphs are not isomorphic. (The vertex neighborhood for the Shrikhande graph is a hexagon, while that for the rook graph is two triangles.)
- The [line graph](wikipedia://en/675231) of a complete graph *K<sub>n</sub>* is an $\operatorname{srg}\left(\binom{n}{2}, 2(n - 2), n - 2, 4\right)$.
- The three [Chang graphs](wikipedia://en/28990313) are srg(28, 12, 6, 4), the same as the line graph of *K*<sub>8</sub>, but these four graphs are not isomorphic.
- Every [generalized quadrangle](wikipedia://en/3947006) of order (s, t) gives an srg((s + 1)(st + 1), s(t + 1), s − 1, t + 1) as its [line graph](wikipedia://en/675231). For example, GQ(2, 4) gives srg(27, 10, 1, 5) as its line graph.
- The [Schläfli graph](wikipedia://en/28200770) is an srg(27, 16, 10, 8) and is the complement of the aforementioned line graph on GQ(2, 4).
- The [Hoffman–Singleton graph](wikipedia://en/4112667) is an srg(50, 7, 0, 1).
- The [Gewirtz graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gewirtz_graph) is an srg(56, 10, 0, 2).
- The [M22 graph](wikipedia://en/57545193) aka the [Mesner graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesner_graph) is an srg(77, 16, 0, 4).
- The [Brouwer–Haemers graph](wikipedia://en/28189558) is an srg(81, 20, 1, 6).
- The [Higman–Sims graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higman–Sims_graph) is an srg(100, 22, 0, 6).
- The [Local McLaughlin graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_McLaughlin_graph) is an srg(162, 56, 10, 24).
- The [Cameron graph](wikipedia://en/47090773) is an srg(231, 30, 9, 3).
- The [Berlekamp–van Lint–Seidel graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlekamp–van_Lint–Seidel_graph) is an srg(243, 22, 1, 2).
- The [McLaughlin graph](wikipedia://en/46573575) is an srg(275, 112, 30, 56).
- The [Paley graph](wikipedia://en/5719307) of order *q* is an srg(*q*, (*q* − 1)/2, (*q* − 5)/4, (*q* − 1)/4). The smallest Paley graph, with *q* = 5, is the 5-cycle (above).
- [Self-complementary](wikipedia://en/15093884) [arc-transitive](wikipedia://en/1577896) graphs are strongly regular.

A strongly regular graph is called **primitive** if both the graph and its complement are connected. All the above graphs are primitive, as otherwise μ = 0 or *v* + λ = 2*k*.

[Conway's 99-graph problem](wikipedia://en/59939359) asks for the construction of an srg(99, 14, 1, 2). It is unknown whether a graph with these parameters exists, and [John Horton Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) offered a \$1000 prize for the solution to this problem.[^o10]

### Triangle-free graphs {#Triangle-free_graphs}

The strongly regular graphs with λ = 0 are [triangle free](wikipedia://en/7667996). Apart from the complete graphs on at most two vertices and all regular complete bipartite graphs, the seven listed earlier (pentagon, Petersen, Clebsch, Hoffman-Singleton, Gewirtz, Mesner-M22, and Higman-Sims) are the only known ones.

### Geodetic graphs {#Geodetic_graphs}

Every strongly regular graph with $\mu = 1$ is a [geodetic graph](wikipedia://en/65318914), a graph in which every two vertices have a unique [shortest path](wikipedia://en/41985).[^o13] The only known strongly regular graphs with $\mu = 1$ are those where $\lambda$ is 0, therefore triangle-free as well. These are called the Moore graphs and are explored below in more detail. Other combinations of parameters such as (400, 21, 2, 1) have not yet been ruled out. Despite ongoing research on the properties that a strongly regular graph with $\mu=1$ would have,[^o14][^o15] it is not known whether any more exist or even whether their number is finite.[^o13] Only the elementary result is known, that $\lambda$ cannot be 1 for such a graph.

:::

## Algebraic properties of strongly regular graphs {#Algebraic_properties_of_strongly_regular_graphs}

### Basic relationship between parameters {#Basic_relationship_between_parameters}

The four parameters in an srg(*v*, *k*, λ, μ) are not independent: In order for an srg(*v*, *k*, λ, μ) to exist, the parameters must obey the following relation:

$$(v - k - 1)\mu = k(k - \lambda - 1)$$

The above relation is derived through a counting argument as follows:

1. Imagine the vertices of the graph to lie in three levels. Pick any vertex as the root, in Level 0. Then its *k* neighbors lie in Level 1, and all other vertices lie in Level 2.
1. Vertices in Level 1 are directly connected to the root, hence they must have λ other neighbors in common with the root, and these common neighbors must also be in Level 1. Since each vertex has degree *k*, there are $k - \lambda - 1$ edges remaining for each Level 1 node to connect to vertices in Level 2. Therefore, there are $k (k - \lambda - 1)$ edges between Level 1 and Level 2.
1. Vertices in Level 2 are not directly connected to the root, hence they must have μ common neighbors with the root, and these common neighbors must all be in Level 1. There are $(v - k - 1)$ vertices in Level 2, and each is connected to μ vertices in Level 1. Therefore the number of edges between Level 1 and Level 2 is $(v - k - 1)\mu$.
1. Equating the two expressions for the edges between Level 1 and Level 2, the relation follows.

This relation is a [necessary condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_condition) for the existence of a strongly regular graph, but not a [sufficient condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufficient_condition). For instance, the quadruple (21,10,4,5) obeys this relation, but there does not exist a strongly regular graph with these parameters.[^o18]

### Adjacency matrix equations {#Adjacency_matrix_equations}

Let *I* denote the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) and let *J* denote the [matrix of ones](wikipedia://en/690246), both matrices of order *v*. The [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) *A* of a strongly regular graph satisfies two equations.

First:

$$AJ = JA = kJ,$$

which is a restatement of the regularity requirement. This shows that *k* is an eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix with the all-ones eigenvector.

Second:

$$A^2 = kI + \lambda{A} + \mu(J - I - A)$$

which expresses strong regularity. The *ij*-th element of the left hand side gives the number of two-step paths from *i* to *j*. The first term of the right hand side gives the number of two-step paths from *i* back to *i*, namely *k* edges out and back in. The second term gives the number of two-step paths when *i* and *j* are directly connected. The third term gives the corresponding value when *i* and *j* are not connected. Since the three cases are [mutually exclusive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutually_exclusive) and [collectively exhaustive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectively_exhaustive), the simple additive equality follows.

Conversely, a graph whose adjacency matrix satisfies both of the above conditions and which is not a complete or [null graph](wikipedia://en/1520758) is a strongly regular graph.[^o20]

### Eigenvalues and graph spectrum {#Eigenvalues_and_graph_spectrum}

Since the adjacency matrix A is symmetric, it follows that [its eigenvectors are orthogonal](wikipedia://en/755400). We already observed one eigenvector above which is made of all ones, corresponding to the eigenvalue *k*. Therefore the other eigenvectors *x* must all satisfy $Jx = 0$ where *J* is the all-ones matrix as before. Take the previously established equation:

$$A^2 = kI + \lambda{A} + \mu(J - I - A)$$

and multiply the above equation by eigenvector *x*:

$$A^2 x = kIx + \lambda{A}x + \mu(J - I - A)x$$

Call the corresponding eigenvalue *p* (not to be confused with $\lambda$ the graph parameter) and substitute $Ax = px$, $Jx = 0$ and $Ix = x$:

$$p^2 x = kx + \lambda p x - \mu x - \mu p x$$

Eliminate x and rearrange to get a quadratic:

$$p^2 + (\mu - \lambda ) p - (k - \mu) = 0$$

This gives the two additional eigenvalues $\frac{1}{2}\left[(\lambda - \mu) \pm \sqrt{(\lambda - \mu)^2 + 4(k - \mu)}\,\right]$. There are thus exactly three eigenvalues for a strongly regular matrix.

Conversely, a connected regular graph with only three eigenvalues is strongly regular.[^o22]

Following the terminology in much of the strongly regular graph literature, the larger eigenvalue is called *r* with multiplicity *f* and the smaller one is called *s* with multiplicity *g*.

Since the sum of all the eigenvalues is the [trace of the adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/43270), which is zero in this case, the respective multiplicities *f* and *g* can be calculated:

- Eigenvalue *k* has [multiplicity](wikipedia://en/19211790) 1.
- Eigenvalue $r = \frac{1}{2}\left[(\lambda - \mu) + \sqrt{(\lambda - \mu)^2 + 4(k - \mu)}\,\right]$ has multiplicity $f = \frac{1}{2}\left[(v - 1) - \frac{2k + (v - 1)(\lambda - \mu)}{\sqrt{(\lambda - \mu)^2 + 4(k - \mu)}}\right]$
- Eigenvalue $s = \frac{1}{2}\left[(\lambda - \mu) - \sqrt{(\lambda - \mu)^2 + 4(k-\mu)}\,\right]$ has multiplicity $g = \frac{1}{2}\left[(v - 1) + \frac{2k + (v - 1)(\lambda - \mu)}{\sqrt{(\lambda - \mu)^2 + 4(k - \mu)}}\right]$

As the multiplicities must be integers, their expressions provide further constraints on the values of *v*, *k*, *μ*, and *λ*.

Strongly regular graphs for which $2k + (v - 1)(\lambda - \mu) \ne 0$ have integer eigenvalues with unequal multiplicities.

Strongly regular graphs for which $2k + (v - 1)(\lambda - \mu) = 0$ are called [conference graphs](wikipedia://en/7769842) because of their connection with symmetric [conference matrices](wikipedia://en/7770047). Their parameters reduce to

$$\operatorname{srg}\left(v, \frac{1}{2}(v - 1), \frac{1}{4}(v - 5), \frac{1}{4}(v - 1)\right).$$

Their eigenvalues are $r =\frac{-1 + \sqrt{v}}{2}$ and $s = \frac{-1 - \sqrt{v}}{2}$, both of whose multiplicities are equal to $\frac{v-1}{2}$. Further, in this case, *v* must equal the sum of two squares, related to the [Bruck–Ryser–Chowla theorem](wikipedia://en/456781).

Further properties of the eigenvalues and their multiplicities are:[^o23]

- $(A - rI)\times(A - sI) = \mu.J$, therefore $(k - r).(k - s) = \mu v$
- $$\lambda - \mu = r + s$$
- $$k - \mu = -r\times s$$
- $$k \ge r$$
- Given an srg(*v*, *k*, λ, μ) with eigenvalues *r* and *s*, its complement srg(*v*, *v* − *k* − 1, *v* − 2 − 2*k* + μ, *v* − 2*k* + λ) has eigenvalues *\-1-s* and *\-1-r*.
- Alternate equations for the multiplicities are $f =\frac{(s+1)k(k-s)}{\mu(s-r)}$ and $g =\frac{(r+1)k(k-r)}{\mu(r-s)}$
- The frame quotient condition: $v k (v-k-1) = f g (r-s)^2$. As a corollary, $v = (r-s)^2$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) ${f,g} = {k, v-k-1}$ in some order.
- Krein conditions: $(v-k-1)^2 (k^2 + r^3) \ge (r+1)^3 k^2$ and $(v-k-1)^2 (k^2 + s^3) \ge (s+1)^3 k^2$
- Absolute bound: $v \le \frac{f(f+3)}{2}$ and $v \le \frac{g(g+3)}{2}$.
- Claw bound: if $r + 1 > \frac{s(s+1)(\mu+1)}{2}$, then $\mu = s^2$ or $\mu = s(s+1)$.

If any of the above conditions are violated for a set of parameters, then there exists no strongly regular graph for those parameters. Brouwer has compiled such lists of existence or non-existence [here](https://www.win.tue.nl/~aeb/graphs/srg/srgtab.html) with reasons for non-existence if any. For example, there exists no srg(28,9,0,4) because that violates one of the Krein conditions and one of the absolute bound conditions.

::: {#o25 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="The Hoffman–Singleton theorem"}

### The Hoffman–Singleton theorem {#The_Hoffman–Singleton_theorem}

As noted above, the multiplicities of the eigenvalues are given by

$$M_{\pm} = \frac{1}{2}\left[(v - 1) \pm \frac{2k + (v - 1)(\lambda - \mu)}{\sqrt{(\lambda - \mu)^2 + 4(k - \mu)}}\right]$$

which must be integers.

In 1960, [Alan Hoffman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_J._Hoffman) and Robert Singleton examined those expressions when applied on [Moore graphs](wikipedia://en/4112719), which are strongly regular graphs that have *λ* = 0 and *μ* = 1. Such graphs are free of triangles (otherwise *λ* would exceed zero) and quadrilaterals (otherwise *μ* would exceed 1), hence they have a girth (smallest cycle length) of 5. Substituting the values of *λ* and *μ* in the equation $(v - k - 1)\mu = k(k - \lambda - 1)$, it can be seen that $v = k^2 + 1$, and the eigenvalue multiplicities reduce to

$$M_{\pm} = \frac{1}{2}\left[k^2 \pm \frac{2k - k^2}{\sqrt{4k - 3}}\right]$$

For the multiplicities to be integers, the quantity $\frac{2k - k^2}{\sqrt{4k - 3}}$ must be rational, therefore either the numerator $2k - k^2$ is zero or the denominator $\sqrt{4k - 3}$ is an integer.

If the numerator $2k - k^2$ is zero, the possibilities are:

- *k* = 0 and *v* = 1 yields a trivial graph with one vertex and no edges, and
- *k* = 2 and *v* = 5 yields the 5-vertex [cycle graph](wikipedia://en/878962) $C_5$, usually drawn as a [regular pentagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_pentagon).

If the denominator $\sqrt{4k - 3}$ is an integer *t*, then $4k - 3$ is a perfect square $t^2$, so $k = \frac{t^2 + 3}{4}$. Substituting:

$$\begin{aligned} M_{\pm} &= \frac{1}{2} \left[\left(\frac{t^2 + 3}{4}\right)^2 \pm \frac{\frac{t^2 + 3}{2} - \left(\frac{t^2 + 3}{4}\right)^2}{t}\right], \end{aligned}$$

so

$$\begin{aligned} 32 M_{\pm} &= (t^2 + 3)^2 \pm \frac{8(t^2 + 3) - (t^2 + 3)^2}{t} \\ &= t^4 + 6t^2 + 9 \pm \frac{- t^4 + 2t^2 + 15}{t} \\ &= t^4 + 6t^2 + 9 \pm \left(-t^3 + 2t + \frac{15}{t}\right). \end{aligned}$$

Since both sides are integers, $\frac{15}{t}$ must be an integer, therefore *t* is a factor of 15, namely $t \in \{\pm 1, \pm 3, \pm 5, \pm 15\}$, therefore $k \in \{1, 3, 7, 57\}$. In turn:

- *k* = 1 and *v* = 2 yields a trivial graph of two vertices joined by an edge,
- *k* = 3 and *v* = 10 yields the [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926),
- *k* = 7 and *v* = 50 yields the [Hoffman–Singleton graph](wikipedia://en/4112667), discovered by Hoffman and Singleton in the course of this analysis, and
- *k* = 57 and *v* = 3250 famously predicts a graph that has neither been discovered since 1960, nor has its existence been disproven.[^o26]

The Hoffman-Singleton theorem states that there are no girth-5 Moore graphs except the ones listed above.
