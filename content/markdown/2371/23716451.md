# Generalized Petersen graph

*Image: Dürer graph.svg.* The [Dürer graph](wikipedia://en/23453065) $G(6, 2)$.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Generalized Petersen graph"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **generalized Petersen graphs** are a family of [cubic graphs](wikipedia://en/980508) formed by connecting the vertices of a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) to the corresponding vertices of a [star polygon](wikipedia://en/313303). They include the [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926) and generalize one of the ways of constructing the Petersen graph. The generalized Petersen graph family was introduced in 1950 by [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328)[^o2] and was given its name in 1969 by Mark Watkins.[^o3]

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition and notation"}

## Definition and notation {#Definition_and_notation}

In Watkins' notation, $G(n,k)$ is a graph with vertex set

$$\{u_0, u_1, \ldots, u_{n-1}, v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_{n-1}\}$$

and edge set

$$\{u_iu_{i+1}, u_iv_i, v_iv_{i+k} \mid 0 \le i \le n-1 \}$$

where subscripts are to be read modulo $n$ and where $k<n/2$. Some authors use the notation $GPG(n,k)$. Coxeter's notation for the same graph would be $\{n\}+\{n/k\}$, a combination of the [Schläfli symbols](wikipedia://en/489394) for the [regular $n$-gon](wikipedia://en/333306) and [star polygon](wikipedia://en/313303) from which the graph is formed. The Petersen graph itself is $G(5,2)$ or $\{5\}+\{5/2\}$. Some authors also allow $k=n/2$, producing a graph that is not a [regular graph](wikipedia://en/85821).[^o13]

Any generalized Petersen graph can also be constructed from a [voltage graph](wikipedia://en/8802504) with two vertices, two self-loops, and one other edge.[^o6]

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Among the generalized Petersen graphs are the $n$-prism $G(n,1)$, the [Dürer graph](wikipedia://en/23453065) $G(6,2)$, the [Möbius-Kantor graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Möbius-Kantor_graph) $G(8,3)$, the [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/7149361) $G(10,2)$, the [Desargues graph](wikipedia://en/1578059) $G(10,3)$ and the [Nauru graph](wikipedia://en/24052345) $G(12,5)$.

Four generalized Petersen graphs – the 3-prism, the 5-prism, the Dürer graph, and $G(7,2)$ – are among the seven graphs that are [cubic](wikipedia://en/980508), [3-vertex-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph), and [well-covered](wikipedia://en/32696387) (meaning that all of their [maximal independent sets](wikipedia://en/1793590) have equal size).[^o9]

## Properties {#Properties}

*Image: Generalized Petersen 9 2 Hamiltonicity.svg.* One of the three Hamiltonian cycles in *G*(9, 2). The other two Hamiltonian cycles in the same graph are symmetric under 40° rotations of the drawing.

This family of graphs possesses a number of interesting properties. For example:

- $G(n,k)$ is [vertex-transitive](wikipedia://en/581175) (meaning that it has symmetries that take any vertex to any other vertex) if and only if $(n,k)=(10,2)$ or $k^2\equiv\pm1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)$.
- $G(n,k)$ is [edge-transitive](wikipedia://en/584406) (having symmetries that take any edge to any other edge) only in the following seven cases: $(n,k)$ is (4, 1), (5, 2), (8, 3), (10, 2), (10, 3), (12, 5), or (24, 5).[^o12] These seven graphs are therefore the only [symmetric](wikipedia://en/1577896) generalized Petersen graphs.
- $G(n,k)$ is [bipartite](wikipedia://en/244431) if and only if $n$ is even and $k$ is odd.
- $G(n,k)$ is a [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) if and only if $k^2\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)$.
- $G(n,k)$ is [hypohamiltonian](wikipedia://en/18301304) when $n$ is congruent to 5 modulo 6 and $k$ is 2, $n-2$, or $(n\pm1)/2$ (these four choices of *k* lead to isomorphic graphs). It is also non-[Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_graph) when $n$ is divisible by 4, at least equal to 8, and $k=n/2$. In all other cases it has a [Hamiltonian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle).[^o13] When $n$ is congruent to 3 modulo 6, $G(n,2)$ has exactly three Hamiltonian cycles.[^o14] For $G(n,2)$, the number of Hamiltonian cycles can be computed by a formula that depends on the congruence class of $n$ modulo 6 and involves the [Fibonacci numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number).[^o15] Linear recurrence relations for the number of Hamiltonian cycles have also been found for $G(n,3)$ and $G(n,4)$.[^o16]
- Every generalized Petersen graph is a [unit distance graph](wikipedia://en/7158665).[^o17]

### Isomorphisms {#Isomorphisms}

$G(n,k)$ is isomorphic to $G(n,\ell)$ if and only if $k\equiv\pm\ell\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)$ or $k\ell\equiv\pm1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)$.[^o19]

### Girth {#Girth}

The [girth](wikipedia://en/12013) of $G(n,k)$ is at least 3 and at most 8, in particular:[^o21]

$$g(G(n,k)) \le \min \left \{8,k+3,\frac{n}{\gcd(n,k)} \right \}.$$

### Chromatic number and chromatic index {#Chromatic_number_and_chromatic_index}

Generalized Petersen graphs are [regular graphs](wikipedia://en/85821) of [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) three, so according to [Brooks' theorem](wikipedia://en/21042117) their chromatic number can only be two or three. More exactly:

$$\chi(G(n,k))= \begin{cases} 2 & 2 \mid n \land 2 \nmid k \\ 3 & 2 \nmid n \lor 2 \mid k \\ \end{cases}$$

Where $\land$ denotes the logical [AND](wikipedia://en/18152), while $\lor$ the logical [OR](wikipedia://en/7962). Here, $\mid$ denotes divisibility, and $\nmid$ denotes its negation. For example, the chromatic number of $G(5,2)$ is 3.

The [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926), being a [snark](wikipedia://en/727811), has a [chromatic index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_index) of 4: its edges require four colors. All other generalized Petersen graphs have chromatic index 3. These are the only possibilities, by [Vizing's theorem](wikipedia://en/5449464).[^o24]

The generalized Petersen graph $G(9,2)$ is one of the few graphs known to have [only one 3-edge-coloring](wikipedia://en/676304#Unique_edge_colorability).[^o25]

The Petersen graph itself is the only generalized Petersen graph that is not 3-[edge-colorable](wikipedia://en/690647).[^o26]
