# Philip Saffman

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Philip Saffman"}

**Philip Geoffrey Saffman** [FRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society)[^o4] (19 March 1931 – 17 August 2008) was a mathematician and the [Theodore von Kármán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_von_Kármán) Professor of [Applied Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_Mathematics) and [Aeronautics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeronautics) at the [California Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology).[^o5][^o24][^o6][^o8][^o9][^o10]

:::

## Education and early life {#Education_and_early_life}

Saffman was born to a [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish) family[^o13] in [Leeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeds), England, and educated at [Roundhay School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundhay_School) and [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge) which he entered aged 15.[^o4] He received his Bachelor of Arts degree in 1953,[^o4] studied for Part III of the [Cambridge Mathematical Tripos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Mathematical_Tripos) in 1954 and was awarded his PhD in 1956 for research supervised by [George Batchelor](wikipedia://en/1763397).

## Career and research {#Career_and_research}

Saffman started his academic career as a lecturer at the University of Cambridge, then joined [King's College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College_London) as a [Reader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reader_%28academic_rank%29).[^o4] Saffman joined the Caltech faculty in 1964 and was named the Theodore von Kármán Professor in 1995. According to Dan Meiron, Saffman "really was one of the leading figures in fluid mechanics," and he influenced almost every subfield of that discipline. He is known (with his co-author [Geoffrey Ingram Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Ingram_Taylor)) for the Saffman–Taylor instability in [viscous fingering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscous_fingering) of fluid boundaries,[^o16] a phenomenon important for its applications in [enhanced oil recovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enhanced_oil_recovery), and for the [Saffman–Delbrück model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saffman–Delbrück_model) of [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein) [diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion) in membranes which he published with his Caltech colleague and Pasadena neighbour [Max Delbrück](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Delbrück). He made important contributions to the theory of [vorticity](wikipedia://en/166010) arising from the motion of ships and aircraft through water and air; his work on [wake turbulence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wake_turbulence) led the airlines to increase the minimum time between takeoffs of aircraft on the same runway.[^o5][^o25][^o17] Saffman also studied the flow of [spheroidal](wikipedia://en/82365) particles in a fluid, such as bubbles in a carbonated beverage or corpuscles in blood; his work overturned previous assumptions that inertia was an important factor in these particles' motion and showed instead that [Non-Newtonian](wikipedia://en/75367) properties of fluids play a significant role.[^o9][^o18]

Along with his many research papers,[^o11] Saffman wrote a book, *Vortex Dynamics*,[^o19][^o20] surveying a field to which he had been a principal contributor. [Russel E. Caflisch](wikipedia://en/42603924) writes that "This book should be read by everyone interested in vortex dynamics or fluid dynamics in general."[^o21][^o22]

## Awards and honours {#Awards_and_honours}

Saffman was elected a [Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow) of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences) and a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society) in 1986,[^o4] and the recipient of the [American Physical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Physical_Society)'s [Otto Laporte Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Laporte_Award).[^o5][^o24][^o25][^o26][^o27] His nomination for the Royal Society reads: Distinguished for his important contributions to [fluid mechanics](wikipedia://en/2684988) in more than 140 papers characterised by originality, powerful [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), and deep physical insight. The problems he has illuminated include the following: dispersion in [porous media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porous_medium), fingering in a [Hele-Shaw cell](wikipedia://en/16691615), the interaction of molecular and macroscopic mixing in turbulent fluid, the forces on a small particle in moving fluid, the effect of dust particles on stability of gas flows, the dynamics of homogeneous [turbulence](wikipedia://en/154664), the shear layers bounding a Taylor column, [vortex rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vortex_ring), trailing line vortices from aircraft wings, stability of vortex streets, diffusion of [macromolecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macromolecule) in [cell walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_wall), nonlinear gravity-[capillary waves](wikipedia://en/669713), and instability of finite-amplitude [water waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_wave).[^o28]

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Saffman was survived by his wife (Ruth Arion whom he married in 1954), three children ([Mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Saffman), Louise, Emma), and eight grandchildren (Timothy, Gregory, Rae, Jenny, Nadine, Aaron, Miriam, Alexandra and Andrey).[^o4][^o24]
