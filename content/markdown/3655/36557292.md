# Regular tuning

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Regular tuning"}

Among [alternative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guitar_tunings#Alternative) [guitar-tunings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guitar_tunings), **regular tunings** have equal [musical intervals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_%28music%29) between the paired [notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Note_%28music%29) of their successive [open strings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_string_%28music%29).

:::

*Guitar [tunings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuning_%28music%29)* assign [pitches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_%28music%29) to the [open strings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_string_%28music%29) of [guitars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guitar). Tunings can be described by the particular pitches that are denoted by notes in Western music. By convention, the notes are ordered from lowest to highest. The *standard tuning* defines the string pitches as E, A, D, G, B, and E. Between the open-strings of the standard tuning are three [perfect-fourths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fourth) (E–A, A–D, D–G), then the [major third](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_third) G–B, and the fourth perfect-fourth B–E.

In contrast, regular tunings have constant intervals between their successive open-strings:

- 3 semitones (minor third): Minor-thirds, or *Diminished* tuning
- 4 semitones (major third): [Major-thirds or *Augmented* tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major-thirds_tuning),
- 5 semitones (perfect fourth): [All-fourths tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-fourths_tuning),
- 6 semitones ([augmented fourth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augmented_fourth), [tritone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tritone), or [diminished fifth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diminished_fifth)): [Augmented-fourths tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augmented-fourths_tuning),
- 7 semitones ([perfect fifth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fifth)): [All-fifths tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-fifths_tuning)

For the regular tunings, chords may be moved *diagonally* around the fretboard, as well as *vertically* for the repetitive regular tunings (minor thirds, major thirds, and augmented fourths). Regular tunings thus often appeal to new guitarists and also to jazz-guitarists, as they facilitate key transpositions without requiring a completely new set of fingerings for the new key. On the other hand, some conventional major/minor system chords are easier to play in standard tuning than in regular tuning.[^o3] Left-handed guitarists may use the chord charts from one class of regular tunings for its left-handed tuning; for example, the chord charts for all-fifths tuning may be used for guitars strung with left-handed all-fourths tuning.

The class of regular tunings has been named and described by Professor [William Sethares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Sethares). Sethares's 2001 chapter *Regular tunings* (in his revised 2010–2011 *Alternate tuning guide*) is the leading source for this article.[^o3] This article's descriptions of particular regular-tunings use other sources also.

## Standard and alternative guitar-tunings: A review {#Standard_and_alternative_guitar-tunings:_A_review}

Standard diagonal shifting of C major chord.png This summary of standard tuning also introduces the terms for discussing alternative tunings.

### Standard {#Standard}

Standard tuning has the following open-string [notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_note):

E2–A2–D3–G3–B3–E4.

In standard tuning, the separation of the second (B), and third (G) string is by a [major-third](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major-third) [interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_%28music%29), which has a width of four [semitones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitone).

::: {#o6 .table cue=table}

| Standard tuning | open | 1st fret (index) | 2nd fret (middle) | 3rd fret (ring) | 4th fret (little) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1st string | E4 | F4 | F♯4/G♭4 | G4 | G♯4/A♭4 |
| 2nd string | B3 | C4 | C♯4/D♭4 | D4 | D♯4/E♭4 |
| 3rd string | G3 | G♯3/A♭3 | A3 | A♯3/B♭3 | B3 |
| 4th string | D3 | D♯3/E♭3 | E3 | F3 | F♯3/G♭3 |
| 5th string | A2 | A♯2/B♭2 | B2 | C3 | C♯3/D♭3 |
| 6th string | E2 | F2 | F♯2/G♭2 | G2 | G♯2/A♭2 |
| *Chromatic note progression* |  |  |  |  |  |

:::

The irregularity has a price. Chords cannot be shifted around the fretboard in the standard tuning E–A–D–G–B–E, which requires four chord-shapes for the major chords. There are separate chord-forms for chords having their [root note](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_note) on the third, fourth, fifth, and sixth strings.[^o7]

### Alternative {#Alternative}

[*Alternative ("alternate")* tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scordatura) refers to any open-string note-arrangement other than standard tuning. Such alternative tuning arrangements offer different chord voicing and sonorities. Alternative tunings necessarily change the chord shapes associated with standard tuning, which eases the playing of some, often "non-standard", chords at the cost of increasing the difficulty of some traditionally-voiced chords. As with other scordatura tuning, regular tunings may require re-stringing the guitar with different string gauges. For example, [all-fifths tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-fifths_tuning) has been difficult to implement on conventional guitars, due to the extreme high pitch required from the top string. Even a common approximation to all-fifths tuning, [new standard tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_standard_tuning), requires a special set of strings.

## Properties {#Properties}

*Image: Tuning ADGBE5 ADGBE0.svg.* In the *standard* guitar-tuning, one major-third interval is interjected amid four perfect-fourth intervals. In each *regular* tuning, all string successions have the same interval.

Diagonal shift of C-major chord in major-thirds tuning.png

*Image: Chords in major-thirds tuning C, D, G, minor, major, dominant seventh.png.* In regular tunings like M3 tuning, chords maintain the same shape, unlike the chords of standard tuning.

*Image: Segmented fretboard of major thirds tuning for six-string guitar.png.* The fretboard of major-thirds tuning is segmented into four-fret intervals, which simplifies its learning and also reduces the need for shifting the left hand.

With standard tuning, and with all tunings, chord patterns can be moved twelve frets down, where the notes repeat in a higher octave.

For the standard tuning, there is exactly one interval of a third between the second and third strings, and all the other intervals are fourths. Working around the irregular third of standard tuning, guitarists have to memorize chord-patterns for at least three regions: The first four strings tuned in perfect fourths; two or more fourths and the third; and one or more initial fourths, the third, and the last fourth.

In contrast, regular tunings have constant intervals between their successive open-strings. In fact, the class of each regular tuning is characterized by its musical interval as shown by the following list:

- 3 semitones ([minor third](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_third)): Minor-thirds tuning,
- 4 semitones ([major third](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_third)): [Major-thirds tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major-thirds_tuning),
- 5 semitones ([perfect fourth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fourth)): [All-fourths tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-fourths_tuning),
- 6 semitones ([augmented fourth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augmented_fourth), [tritone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tritone), or [diminished fifth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diminished_fifth)): [Augmented-fourths tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augmented-fourths_tuning),
- 7 semitones ([perfect fifth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fifth)): [All-fifths tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-fifths_tuning)

The regular tunings whose number of semitones *s* divides 12 (the number of notes in the [octave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octave)) repeat their open-string notes (raised one octave) after 12/*s* strings: For example,

- having three semitones in its interval, minor-thirds tuning repeats its open notes after four (12/3) strings;
- having four semitones in its interval, major-thirds tuning repeats its open notes after three (12/4) strings;
- having six semitones in its interval, augmented-fourths tuning repeats its notes after two (12/6) strings.

Regular tunings have symmetrical scales all along the fretboard. This makes it simpler to translate chords into new keys. For the regular tunings, chords may be moved diagonally around the fretboard.

The shifting of chords is especially simple for the regular tunings that repeat their open strings, in which case chords can be moved vertically: Chords can be moved three strings up (or down) in major-thirds tuning,[^o11] and chords can be moved two strings up (or down) in augmented-fourths tuning. Regular tunings thus appeal to new guitarists and also to jazz-guitarists, whose improvisation is simplified by regular intervals.

### Particular conventional chords are more difficult to play {#Particular_conventional_chords_are_more_difficult_to_play}

On the other hand, particular traditional chords may be more difficult to play in a regular tuning than in standard tuning. It can be difficult to play conventional chords especially in augmented-fourths tuning and all-fifths tuning,[^o3] in which the wide ([tritone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tritone) and [perfect-fifth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect-fifth)) intervals require hand stretching. Some chords that are conventional in folk music are difficult to play even in all-fourths and major-thirds tunings, which do not require more hand-stretching than standard tuning.[^o28] On the other hand, minor-thirds tuning features many [barre chords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barre_chord) with repeated notes,[^o13] properties that appeal to beginners.

### Frets covered by the hand {#Frets_covered_by_the_hand}

The [chromatic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_scale) climbs from one string to the next after a number of frets that is specific to each regular tuning. The chromatic scale climbs after exactly four frets in major-thirds tuning, so reducing the extensions of the little and index fingers ("hand stretching").[^o15] For other regular tunings, the successive strings have intervals that are minor thirds, perfect fourths, augmented fourths, or perfect fifths; thus, the fretting hand covers three, five, six, or seven frets respectively to play a chromatic scale. (Of course, the lowest chromatic-scale uses the open strings and so requires one less fret to be covered.)

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Diminished seventh chord in the chromatic circle.png.* Every minor-thirds tuning repeats its open notes after four strings.

The following regular tunings are discussed by Sethares, who also mentions other regular tunings that are difficult to play or have had little musical interest, to date.

::: {#o19 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Minor thirds tuning"}

### Minor thirds {#Minor_thirds}

C2–Eb2–Gb2–A2–C3–Eb3,[^o22][^o20] or

B2–D3–F3–Ab3–B3–D4[^o21]

In each minor-thirds ([m3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_third)) tuning, every interval between successive strings is a [minor third](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_third). Thus each [repeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repetitive_tuning) its open-notes after four strings. In the minor-thirds tuning beginning with C, the open strings contain the notes (C, Eb, Gb) of the [diminished C triad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diminished_triad).[^o22]

Minor-thirds tuning features many [barre chords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barre_chord) with repeated notes,[^o13] properties that appeal to acoustic guitarists and to beginners. [Doubled notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubling_%28voicing%29) have different sounds because of differing "string widths, tensions and tunings, and \[they\] reinforce each other, like the doubled strings of a twelve string guitar add chorusing and depth," according to [William&nbsp;Sethares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William&nbsp;Sethares).[^o22]

Achieving the same range as a standard-tuned guitar using minor-thirds tuning would require a nine-string guitar (e.g. Eb2–Gb2–A2–C3–Eb3–Gb3–A3–C4–Eb4).

:::

### Major thirds {#Major_thirds}

*Image: Augmented chord in the chromatic circle.png.* The major-thirds tuning Ab2–C3–E3–Ab3–C4–E5 repeats its three open-notes in the higher octave after three strings.

*Major-thirds tuning* is a regular tuning in which the [musical&nbsp;intervals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical&nbsp;interval) between successive strings are each [major thirds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_third).[^o25][^o26] Like minor-thirds tuning (and unlike all-fourths and all-fifths tuning), major-thirds tuning is a [repetitive tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repetitive_tuning); it repeats its octave after three strings, which again simplifies the learning of chords and improvisation;[^o27] similarly, minor-thirds tuning repeats itself after four strings while augmented-fourths tuning repeats itself after two strings.

Neighboring the standard tuning is the all-thirds tuning that has the open strings

E2–G#2–B#2–E3–G#3–B#3 (or Fb2–Ab2–C3–Fb3–Ab3–C4).[^o28]

With six strings, major-thirds tuning has a smaller range than standard tuning; with seven strings, the major-thirds tuning covers the range of standard tuning on six strings.[^o25][^o26] With the repetition of three open-string notes, each major-thirds tuning provides the guitarist with many options for fingering chords. Indeed, the fingering of two successive frets suffices to play pure major and minor chords, while the fingering of three successive frets suffices to play seconds, fourths, sevenths, and ninths.[^o25][^o29]

For the standard Western guitar, which has six strings, major-thirds tuning has a smaller range than standard tuning; on a guitar with [*seven* strings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven-string_guitar), the major-thirds tuning covers the range of standard tuning on *six* strings. Even greater range is possible with guitars with [*eight* strings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight-string_guitar).[^o28][^o30]

Major-thirds tuning was heavily used in 1964 by the American jazz-guitarist [Ralph&nbsp;Patt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph&nbsp;Patt) to facilitate his style of [improvisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Improvisation).[^o28][^o31]

### All fourths {#All_fourths}

*Image: All fourths tuning in the chromatic circle.png.* The consecutive notes of all-fourths tuning are spaced apart by five semi-tones on the chromatic circle.

*Image: All fourths tuning.png.* All fourths tuning.

E2–A2–D3–G3–C4–F4

This tuning is like that of the lowest four strings in standard tuning.[^o33][^o34] Consequently, of all the regular tunings, it is the closest approximation to standard tuning, and thus it best allows the transfer of a knowledge of chords from standard tuning to a regular tuning. Jazz musician [Stanley&nbsp;Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley&nbsp;Jordan) plays guitar in all-fourths tuning; he has stated that all-fourths tuning "simplifies the fingerboard, making it logical".[^o35]

For all-fourths tuning, all twelve [major&nbsp;chords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major&nbsp;chord) (in the first or open positions) are generated by two chords, the open F major chord and the D major chord. The regularity of chord-patterns reduces the number of finger positions that need to be memorized.[^o36]

The left-handed involute of an all-fourths tuning is an all-fifths tuning. All-fourths tuning is based on the [perfect fourth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fourth) (five semitones), and all-fifths tuning is based on the [perfect fifth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fifth) (seven semitones). Consequently, chord charts for all-fifths tunings may be used for left-handed all-fourths tuning.[^o60]

### Augmented fourths {#Augmented_fourths}

*Image: Tritone in the chromatic circle.png.* A line segment bisecting the chromatic circle specifies an augmented-fourths (tritone) tuning.

C2–F#2–C3–F#3–C4–F#4 and B1–F1–B2–F3–B3–F4 etc.

Between the all-fifths and all-fourths tunings are *[augmented-fourth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tritone)* tunings, which are also called "*[diminished-fifths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diminished_fifth)*" or "*[tritone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tritone)*" tunings. It is a [repetitive tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repetitive_tuning) that repeats its notes after two strings. With augmented-fourths tunings, the fretboard has greatest symmetry.[^o62] In fact, every augmented-fourths tuning lists the notes of all the other augmented-fourths tunings on the frets of its fretboard. Professor Sethares wrote that

> "The augmented-fourth interval is the only interval whose inverse is the same as itself. The augmented-fourths tuning is the only tuning (other than the 'trivial' tuning C–C–C–C–C–C) for which all chords-forms remain unchanged when the strings are reversed. Thus the augmented-fourths tuning is its own 'lefty' tuning."[^o39]

Of all the augmented-fourths tunings, the C2–F#2–C3–F#3–C4–F#4 tuning is the closest approximation to the standard tuning, and its fretboard is displayed next:

::: {#o38 .table cue=table}

| *Tritone*:[^o39] Each fret displays the open strings of an augmented-fourths tuning |  |  |  |  |  |  |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|  | open (0th fret) | 1st fret | 2nd fret | 3rd fret | 4th fret | 5th fret |
| 1st string | F#4 | G4 | G#4 | A4 | A#4 | B4 |
| 2nd string | C4 | C#4 | D4 | D#4 | E4 | F4 |
| 3rd string | F#3 | G3 | G#3 | A3 | A#3 | B3 |
| 4th string | C3 | C#3 | D3 | D#3 | E3 | F3 |
| 5th string | F#2 | G2 | G#2 | A2 | A#2 | B2 |
| 6th string | C2 | C#2 | D2 | D#2 | E2 | F2 |

:::

An augmented-fourths tuning "makes it very easy for playing half-whole scales, diminished 7 licks, and whole tone scales," stated guitarist [Ron&nbsp;Jarzombek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ron&nbsp;Jarzombek).[^o40]

### All fifths: "Mandoguitar" {#All_fifths:_"Mandoguitar"}

*Image: All fifths tuning in the chromatic circle.png.* The consecutive open-notes of all-fifths tuning are spaced seven semi-tones apart on the chromatic circle. While the notes of the open-notes of the all-fifths and all-fourths tunings agree, their orderings are reversed.

*Image: New standard tuning in the chromatic circle.png.* New standard tuning substitutes a G for the high B of all-fifths tuning

C2–G2–D3–A3–E4–B4

All-fifths tuning is a tuning in intervals of [perfect fifths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fifth) like that of a [mandolin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandolin), [cello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cello) or [violin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violin); other names include "perfect fifths" and "fifths".[^o42] Consequently, classical compositions written for violin or guitar may be adapted to all-fifths tuning more easily than to standard tuning.

When he was asked whether tuning in fifths facilitates "new intervals or harmonies that aren't readily available in standard tuning", [Robert Fripp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Fripp) responded, "It's a more rational system, but it's also better sounding—better for chords, better for single notes." To build chords, Fripp uses "perfect intervals in fourths, fifths and octaves", so avoiding [minor thirds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_third) and especially [major thirds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_third),[^o43] which are sharp in [equal temperament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_temperament) tuning (in comparison to thirds in [just intonation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_intonation)). It is a challenge to adapt conventional guitar-chords to new standard tuning, which is based on all-fifths tuning.[^o44] Some closely voiced [jazz chords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz_chords) become impractical in NST and all-fifths tuning.[^o45]

It has a wide range, thus its implementation can be difficult. The high *B4* requires a taut, thin string, and consequently is prone to breaking. This can be ameliorated by using a shorter [scale length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_length_%28string_instruments%29) guitar, by shifting to a different [key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_%28music%29), or by shifting down a fifth. All-fifths tuning was used by the jazz-guitarist [Carl Kress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Kress).

The left-handed involute of an all-fifths tuning is an all-fourths tuning. All-fifths tuning is based on the [perfect fifth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fifth) (seven semitones), and all-fourths tuning is based on the [perfect fourth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fourth) (five semitones). Consequently, chord charts for all-fifths tunings are used for left-handed all-fourths tuning.[^o60]

All-fifths tuning has been approximated with tunings in the Through The Looking Glass Guitar[^o46] of Kei Nakano, which has been played by him since 2015. This new tuning is like a mirror to all kinds of string instruments including guitar. Also it can adapt to any other tunings of guitar. If tuned to usual conventional guitar for the right handed person, it is able to use for lefty guitar in general, and vice versa.

#### New standard tuning {#New_standard_tuning}

*Image: New standard tuning.png.* New standard tuning.

*Image: Guitar Crafty Tuning.ogg.* New Standard Tuning's open strings.

All-fifths tuning has been approximated with tunings that avoid the high B4 or the low C2. The B4 has been replaced with a G4 in the [new standard tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_standard_tuning) (NST) of [King&nbsp;Crimson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King&nbsp;Crimson)'s [Robert&nbsp;Fripp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert&nbsp;Fripp). The original version of NST was all-fifths tuning. However, in the 1980s, Fripp never attained the all fifth's high B4. While he could attain A4, the string's [life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_life)-time [distribution](wikipedia://en/2690455) was too short. Experimenting with a g string, Fripp succeeded. "Originally, seen in 5ths. all the way, the top string would not go to B. so, as on a tenor banjo, I adopted an A on the first string. These kept breaking, so G was adopted."[^o48] In 2012, Fripp experimented with A String (0.007);[^o49][^o50] if successful, the experiment could lead to "the NST 1.2", CGDAE-A, according to Fripp.[^o49] Fripp's NST has been taught in [Guitar&nbsp;Craft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guitar&nbsp;Craft) courses.[^o51][^o52] Guitar Craft and its successor [Guitar Circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guitar_Circle) have taught Fripp's tuning to three-thousand students.[^o53]

### Extreme intervals {#Extreme_intervals}

For regular tunings, intervals wider than a perfect fifth or narrower than a minor third have, thus far, had limited interest.

#### Wide intervals {#Wide_intervals}

Two regular-tunings based on sixths, having intervals of [minor&nbsp;sixths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor&nbsp;sixth) (eight semitones) and of [major&nbsp;sixths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major&nbsp;sixth) (nine semitones), have received scholarly discussion.[^o56] The chord charts for minor-sixths tuning are useful for left-handed guitarists playing in major-thirds tuning; the chord charts for major-sixths tuning, for left-handed guitarists playing in minor-thirds tuning.[^o60]

The regular tunings with [minor-seventh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_seventh) (ten semitones) or [major-seventh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_seventh) (eleven semitones) intervals would make conventional major/minor chord-playing very difficult, as would octave intervals.[^o60]

#### Narrow intervals {#Narrow_intervals}

There are regular-tunings that have as their intervals either zero semi-tones ([unison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unison)), one semi-tone ([minor second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_second)), or two semi-tones ([major second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_second)). These tunings tend to increase the difficulty in playing the major/minor system chords of conventionally tuned guitars.[^o60]

The "trivial" class of unison tunings (such as C3–C3–C3–C3–C3–C3) are each their own left-handed tuning.[^o60] Unison tunings are briefly discussed in the article on [ostrich tunings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostrich_tuning). Having exactly one note, unison tunings are also ostrich tunings, which have exactly one [pitch class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_class) (but may have two or more octaves, for example, E2, E3, and E4'); non-unison ostrich tunings are not regular.

## Left-handed involution {#Left-handed_involution}

The class of regular tunings is preserved under the [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) from right-handed to left-handed tunings, as observed by [William Sethares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Sethares).[^o60] The present discussion of left-handed tunings is of interest to [musical theorists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_theory), mathematicians, and left-handed persons, but may be skipped by other readers.

For [left-handed guitars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_handed_guitar), the ordering of the strings reverses the ordering of the strings for right-handed guitars. For example, the left-handed [involute](wikipedia://en/293450) of the standard tuning E–A–D–G–B–E is the "lefty" tuning E–B–G–D–A–E. Similarly, the "left-handed" involute of the "lefty" tuning is the standard ("righty") tuning.[^o60]

The reordering of open-strings in left-handed tunings has an important consequence. The chord fingerings for the right-handed tunings must be changed for left-handed tunings. However, the left-handed involute of a regular tuning is easily recognized: it is another regular tuning. Thus the chords for the involuted regular-tuning may be used for the left-handed involute of a regular tuning.

For example, the right-handed version of [all-fourths tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-fourths_tuning) is [all-fifths tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-fifths_tuning), and the left-handed version of all-fifths tuning is all-fourths tuning. In general, the left-handed involute of the regular tuning based on the interval with $n$ [semitones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitone) is the regular tuning based on its [involuted interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_inversion) with $12-n$ semitones: All-fourths tuning is based on the [perfect fourth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fourth) (five semitones), and all-fifths tuning is based on the [perfect fifth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fifth) (seven semitones), as mentioned previously.[^o60] The following table summarizes the lefty-righty pairings discussed by Sethares.[^o60]

*Left-handed tunings[^o60]*

::: {#o61 .table cue=table name="Left-handed tunings"}

| Right-handed | Left-handed |
|---|---|
| Minor thirds | [Major sixths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_sixth) |
| Major thirds | [Minor sixths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_sixth) |
| All fourths | All fifths |
| Augmented fourths | Diminished fifths |
| All fifths | All fourths |
| Minor sixths | Major thirds |
| Major sixths | Minor thirds |

:::

The left-handed involute of a left-handed involute is the original right-handed tuning. The left-handed version of the trivial tuning C–C–C–C–C–C is also C–C–C–C–C–C. Among non-trivial tunings, only the class of augmented-fourths tunings is [fixed](wikipedia://en/449738) under the lefty involution.[^o60][^o62]

:::

## Summary {#Summary}

The principal regular-tunings have their properties summarized in the following table:

::: {#o64 .table cue=table}

| Regular tuning | [Interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_%28music%29) (Number of [semitones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitone)) | [Repetition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repetitive_tuning) | Advantages: Each facilitates learning and improvisation. | Disadvantages: None use standard-tuning's [open chords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_chord). | Left-handed [involution](wikipedia://en/293450)[^o60] | Guitarist(s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Major thirds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major-thirds_tuning) | [Major third](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_third) (4) | After 3 strings | Chromatic scale on four successive frets. Hence, reduced hand-stretching: Major and minor chords are played on 2 successive frets; others (seconds, fourths, sevenths, and ninths) on 3.[^o29] | Smaller range (without [7 strings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7-string_guitar)) Only three open-notes. | [Minor-sixth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_sixth) tuning | [Ralph Patt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Patt) |
| [All fourths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-fourths_tuning) | [Perfect fourth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fourth) (5) | Non-repetitive[^o67] | Uses chords from lowest 4 strings of standard tuning. Same tuning as [bass guitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bass_guitar) | Difficult to play folk chords | All-fifths tuning | [Stanley Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Jordan) |
| [Augmented fourths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augmented-fourths_tuning) | [Tritone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tritone) (6) | After 2 strings | symmetry ("left-handed") | Only 2 open notes | Augmented-fourths tuning | [Shawn Lane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shawn_Lane) |
| [All fifths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-fifths_tuning) | [Perfect fifth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fifth) (7) | Non-repetitive[^o67] | Wide scope facilitates ensemble playing and single-note picking (rather than conventional chords) Natural for all-fifths music (violin, cello, mandolin) | Very difficult to play conventional chord-voicings. Requires extreme (light and heavy) strings. | All-fourths tuning | [Carl Kress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Kress) Kei Nakano |

:::
