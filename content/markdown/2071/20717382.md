# Alternating polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Alternating polynomial"}

In algebra, an **alternating polynomial** is a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) $f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ such that if one switches any two of the variables, the polynomial changes sign:

:::

$$f(x_1,\dots,x_j,\dots,x_i,\dots,x_n) = -f(x_1,\dots,x_i,\dots,x_j,\dots,x_n).$$

Equivalently, if one [permutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutes) the variables, the polynomial changes in value by the [sign of the permutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_of_a_permutation):

$$f\left(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)}\right)= \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) f(x_1,\dots,x_n).$$

More generally, a polynomial $f(x_1,\dots,x_n,y_1,\dots,y_t)$ is said to be *alternating in* $x_1,\dots,x_n$ if it changes sign if one switches any two of the $x_i$, leaving the $y_j$ fixed.[^n1]

## Relation to symmetric polynomials {#Relation_to_symmetric_polynomials}

Products of [symmetric](wikipedia://en/1440207) and alternating polynomials (in the same variables $x_1,\dots,x_n$) behave thus:

- the product of two symmetric polynomials is symmetric,
- the product of a symmetric polynomial and an alternating polynomial is alternating, and
- the product of two alternating polynomials is symmetric.

This is exactly the addition table for [parity](wikipedia://en/143135), with "symmetric" corresponding to "even" and "alternating" corresponding to "odd". Thus, the direct sum of the spaces of symmetric and alternating polynomials forms a [superalgebra](wikipedia://en/469950) (a $\mathbf{Z}_2$-[graded algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded_algebra)), where the symmetric polynomials are the even part, and the alternating polynomials are the odd part. This grading is unrelated to the grading of polynomials by [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652).

In particular, alternating polynomials form a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) over the algebra of symmetric polynomials (the odd part of a superalgebra is a module over the even part); in fact it is a [free module](wikipedia://en/326454) of rank 1, with the [Vandermonde polynomial](wikipedia://en/20714332) in *n* variables as generator.

If the [characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_of_a_ring) of the coefficient [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) is 2, there is no difference between the two concepts: the alternating polynomials are precisely the symmetric polynomials.

## Vandermonde polynomial {#Vandermonde_polynomial}

The basic alternating polynomial is the [Vandermonde polynomial](wikipedia://en/20714332):

$$v_n = \prod_{1\le i<j\le n} (x_j-x_i).$$

This is clearly alternating, as switching two variables changes the sign of one term and does not change the others.[^o5]

The alternating polynomials are exactly the Vandermonde polynomial times a symmetric polynomial: $a = v_n \cdot s$ where $s$ is symmetric. This is because:

- $v_n$ is a factor of every alternating polynomial: $(x_j-x_i)$ is a factor of every alternating polynomial, as if $x_i=x_j$, the polynomial is zero (since switching them does not change the polynomial, we get

$$f(x_1,\dots,x_i,\dots,x_j,\dots,x_n) = f(x_1,\dots,x_j,\dots,x_i,\dots,x_n) = -f(x_1,\dots,x_i,\dots,x_j,\dots,x_n),$$

so $(x_j-x_i)$ is a factor), and thus $v_n$ is a factor.

- an alternating polynomial times a symmetric polynomial is an alternating polynomial; thus all multiples of $v_n$ are alternating polynomials

Conversely, the ratio of two alternating polynomials is a symmetric function, possibly rational (not necessarily a polynomial), though the ratio of an alternating polynomial over the Vandermonde polynomial is a polynomial. [Schur polynomials](wikipedia://en/3147062) are defined in this way, as an alternating polynomial divided by the Vandermonde polynomial.

### Ring structure {#Ring_structure}

Thus, denoting the ring of symmetric polynomials by Λ<sub>*n*</sub>, the ring of symmetric and alternating polynomials is $\Lambda_n[v_n]$, or more precisely $\Lambda_n[v_n]/\langle v_n^2-\Delta\rangle$, where $\Delta=v_n^2$ is a symmetric polynomial, the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607).

That is, the ring of symmetric and alternating polynomials is a [quadratic extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_extension) of the ring of symmetric polynomials, where one has adjoined a square root of the discriminant.

Alternatively, it is:

$$R[e_1,\dots,e_n,v_n]/\langle v_n^2-\Delta\rangle.$$

If 2 is not invertible, the situation is somewhat different, and one must use a different polynomial $W_n$, and obtains a different relation; see Romagny.

## Representation theory {#Representation_theory}

From the perspective of [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200), the symmetric and alternating polynomials are subrepresentations of [the action of the symmetric group](wikipedia://en/874641) on *n* letters on the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) in *n* variables. (Formally, the symmetric group acts on *n* letters, and thus acts on derived objects, particularly [free objects](wikipedia://en/333835) on *n* letters, such as the ring of polynomials.)

The symmetric group has two 1-dimensional representations: the [trivial representation](wikipedia://en/450573) and the sign representation. The symmetric polynomials are the trivial representation, and the alternating polynomials are the sign representation. Formally, the scalar span of any symmetric (resp., alternating) polynomial is a trivial (resp., sign) representation of the symmetric group, and multiplying the polynomials tensors the representations.

In characteristic 2, these are not distinct representations, and the analysis is more complicated.

If $n>2$, there are also other subrepresentations of the action of the symmetric group on the ring of polynomials, as discussed in [representation theory of the symmetric group](wikipedia://en/874641).

## Unstable {#Unstable}

Alternating polynomials are an unstable phenomenon: the ring of symmetric polynomials in *n* variables can be obtained from the ring of symmetric polynomials in arbitrarily many variables by evaluating all variables above $x_n$ to zero: symmetric polynomials are thus *stable* or *compatibly defined.* However, this is not the case for alternating polynomials, in particular the [Vandermonde polynomial](wikipedia://en/20714332).
