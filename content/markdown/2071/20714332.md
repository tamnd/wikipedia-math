# Vandermonde polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Vandermonde polynomial"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), the **Vandermonde polynomial** of an ordered set of *n* variables $X_1,\dots, X_n$, named after [Alexandre-Théophile Vandermonde](wikipedia://en/457143), is the [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000):

:::

$$V_n = \prod_{1\le i<j\le n} (X_j-X_i).$$

(Some sources use the opposite order $(X_i-X_j)$, which changes the sign $\binom{n}{2}$ times: thus in some dimensions the two formulas agree in sign, while in others they have opposite signs.)

It is also called the **Vandermonde determinant,** as it is the value of the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the [Vandermonde matrix](wikipedia://en/348641)

$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & X_1 & X_1^2 & \dots & X_1^{n-1}\\ 1 & X_2 & X_2^2 & \dots & X_2^{n-1}\\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots &\vdots \\ 1 & X_n & X_n^2 & \dots & X_n^{n-1} \end{bmatrix}.$$

The value depends on the order of the terms: it is an [alternating polynomial](wikipedia://en/20717382), not a [symmetric polynomial](wikipedia://en/1440207).

## Alternating {#Alternating}

A main property of the Vandermonde polynomial is that it is *alternating* in the entries, meaning that permuting the $X_i$ by an [odd permutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_permutation) changes the sign, while permuting them by an [even permutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_permutation) does not change the value of the polynomial – in fact, it is the basic alternating polynomial, as will be made precise below.

It thus depends on the order, and is zero if two entries are equal – this also follows from the formula, but is also consequence of being alternating: if two variables are equal, then switching them both does not change the value and inverts the value, yielding $V_n = -V_n,$ and thus $V_n = 0$ (assuming the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) is not 2, otherwise being alternating is equivalent to being symmetric).

Among all alternating polynomials, the Vandermonde polynomial is the lowest degree monic polynomial.

Conversely, the Vandermonde polynomial is a factor of every alternating polynomial: as shown above, an alternating polynomial vanishes if any two variables are equal, and thus must have $(X_i - X_j)$ as a factor for all $i \neq j$.

### Alternating polynomials {#Alternating_polynomials}

Thus, the Vandermonde polynomial (together with the [symmetric polynomials](wikipedia://en/1440207)) generates the [alternating polynomials](wikipedia://en/20717382).

## Derivatives {#Derivatives}

The first derivative is $\partial_{i} \Delta_n= \Delta_n \sum_{1 \leq j \leq n: i \neq j} \frac{1}{X_i - X_j}$.

Since it is the lowest degree monic alternating polynomial, and $\sum_i \partial_i^2 V_n$ is also alternating, this implies $\sum_i \partial_i^2 V_n = 0$, i.e. it is a [harmonic function](wikipedia://en/55762).

## Discriminant {#Discriminant}

Its square is widely called the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607), though some sources call the Vandermonde polynomial itself the discriminant.

The discriminant (the square of the Vandermonde polynomial: $\Delta=V_n^2$) does not depend on the order of terms, as $(-1)^2=1$, and is thus an invariant of the *unordered* set of points.

If one adjoins the Vandermonde polynomial to the ring of symmetric polynomials in *n* variables $\Lambda_n$, one obtains the [quadratic extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_extension) $\Lambda_n[V_n]/\langle V_n^2-\Delta\rangle$, which is the ring of [alternating polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_polynomials).

## Vandermonde polynomial of a polynomial {#Vandermonde_polynomial_of_a_polynomial}

Given a polynomial, the Vandermonde polynomial of its roots is defined over the [splitting field](wikipedia://en/174440); for a non-[monic](wikipedia://en/314730) polynomial, with leading [coefficient](wikipedia://en/51203) *a*, one may define the Vandermonde polynomial as

$$V_n = a^{n-1}\prod_{1\le i<j\le n} (X_j-X_i),$$

(multiplying with a leading term) to accord with the discriminant.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Over arbitrary [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), one instead uses a different polynomial to generate the alternating polynomials – see (Romagny, 2005).

The Vandermonde determinant is a very special case of the [Weyl denominator formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_denominator_formula) applied to the [trivial representation](wikipedia://en/450573) of the [special unitary group](wikipedia://en/173997) $\mathrm{SU}(n)$.
