# Fredholm theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fredholm theory"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Fredholm theory** is a theory of [integral equations](wikipedia://en/474234). In the narrowest sense, Fredholm theory concerns itself with the solution of the [Fredholm integral equation](wikipedia://en/363377). In a broader sense, the abstract structure of Fredholm's theory is given in terms of the [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713) of [Fredholm operators](wikipedia://en/279722) and [Fredholm kernels](wikipedia://en/2702780) on [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932). It therefore forms a branch of [operator theory](wikipedia://en/712675) and [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180). The theory is named in honour of [Erik Ivar Fredholm](wikipedia://en/669963).

:::

## Fredholm equation of the first kind {#Fredholm_equation_of_the_first_kind}

Much of Fredholm theory concerns itself with the following [integral equation](wikipedia://en/474234) for $f$ when $g$ and $K$ are given:

$$g(x)=\int_a^b K(x,y) f(y)\,dy.$$

This equation arises naturally in many problems in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and mathematics, as the inverse of a [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309). That is, one is asked to solve the differential equation

$$Lg(x)=f(x)$$

where the function $f$ is given and $g$ is unknown. Here, $L$ stands for a linear [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886).

For example, one might take $L$ to be an [elliptic operator](wikipedia://en/550137), such as

$$L=\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\,$$

in which case the equation to be solved becomes the [Poisson equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_equation).

A general method of solving such equations is by means of [Green's functions](wikipedia://en/311001), namely, rather than a direct attack, one first finds the function $K=K(x,y)$ such that for a given pair $x,y$,

$$LK(x,y) = \delta(x-y),$$

where $\delta(x)$ is the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021).

The desired solution to the above differential equation is then written as an integral in the form of a [Fredholm integral equation](wikipedia://en/363377),

$$g(x)=\int K(x,y) f(y)\,dy.$$

The function $K(x,y)$ is variously known as a Green's function, or the [kernel of an integral](wikipedia://en/430912). It is sometimes called the **nucleus** of the integral, whence the term [nuclear operator](wikipedia://en/63684284) arises.

In the general theory, $x$ and $y$ may be points on any [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470); the [real number line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number_line) or $m$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) in the simplest cases. The general theory also often requires that the functions belong to some given [function space](wikipedia://en/340630): often, the space of [square-integrable functions](wikipedia://en/28680558) is studied, and [Sobolev spaces](wikipedia://en/611964) appear often.

The actual function space used is often determined by the solutions of the [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) problem of the differential operator; that is, by the solutions to

$$L\psi_n(x)=\omega_n \psi_n(x)$$

where the $\omega_{n}$ are the eigenvalues, and the $\psi_{n}(x)$ are the eigenvectors. The set of eigenvectors span a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), and, when there is a natural [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product), then the eigenvectors span a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), at which point the [Riesz representation theorem](wikipedia://en/26452) is applied. Examples of such spaces are the [orthogonal polynomials](wikipedia://en/32811718) that occur as the solutions to a class of second-order [ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/32742753).

Given a Hilbert space as above, the kernel may be written in the form

$$K(x,y)=\sum_n \frac{\psi_n(x) \psi_n(y)} {\omega_n}.$$

In this form, the object $K(x,y)$ is often called the [Fredholm operator](wikipedia://en/279722) or the [Fredholm kernel](wikipedia://en/2702780). That this is the same kernel as before follows from the [completeness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) of the basis of the Hilbert space, namely, that one has

$$\delta(x-y)=\sum_n \psi_n(x) \psi_n(y).$$

Since the $\omega_{n}$ are generally increasing, the resulting eigenvalues of the operator $K(x,y)$ are thus seen to be decreasing towards zero.

## Inhomogeneous equations {#Inhomogeneous_equations}

The inhomogeneous Fredholm integral equation

$$f(x)=- \omega \varphi(x) + \int K(x,y) \varphi(y)\,dy$$

may be written formally as

$$f = (K-\omega) \varphi$$

which has the formal solution

$$\varphi=\frac{1}{K-\omega} f.$$

A solution of this form is referred to as the [resolvent formalism](wikipedia://en/3378540), where the resolvent is defined as the operator

$$R(\omega)= \frac{1}{K-\omega I}.$$

Given the collection of eigenvectors and eigenvalues of *K*, the resolvent may be given a concrete form as

$$R(\omega; x,y) = \sum_n \frac{\psi_n(y)\psi_n(x)}{\omega_n - \omega}$$

with the solution being

$$\varphi(x)=\int R(\omega; x,y) f(y)\,dy.$$

A necessary and sufficient condition for such a solution to exist is one of [Fredholm's theorems](wikipedia://en/1955708). The resolvent is commonly expanded in powers of $\lambda=1/\omega$, in which case it is known as the [Liouville-Neumann series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liouville-Neumann_series). In this case, the integral equation is written as

$$g(x)= \varphi(x) - \lambda \int K(x,y) \varphi(y)\,dy$$

and the resolvent is written in the alternate form as

$$R(\lambda)= \frac{1}{I-\lambda K}.$$

## Fredholm determinant {#Fredholm_determinant}

The [Fredholm determinant](wikipedia://en/2685562) is commonly defined as

$$\det(I-\lambda K) = \exp \left[ -\sum_n \frac{\lambda^n}{n} \operatorname{Tr}\, K^n \right]$$

where

$$\operatorname{Tr}\, K = \int K(x,x)\,dx$$

and

$$\operatorname{Tr}\, K^2 = \iint K(x,y) K(y,x) \,dx\,dy$$

and so on. The corresponding [zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809) is

$$\zeta(s) = \frac{1}{\det(I-s K)}.$$

The zeta function can be thought of as the determinant of the [resolvent](wikipedia://en/3378540).

The zeta function plays an important role in studying [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems). Note that this is the same general type of zeta function as the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809); however, in this case, the corresponding kernel is not known. The existence of such a kernel is known as the [Hilbert–Pólya conjecture](wikipedia://en/648004).

## Main results {#Main_results}

The classical results of the theory are [Fredholm's theorems](wikipedia://en/1955708), one of which is the [Fredholm alternative](wikipedia://en/3050954).

One of the important results from the general theory is that the kernel is a [compact operator](wikipedia://en/577441) when the space of functions are [equicontinuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equicontinuous).

A related celebrated result is the [Atiyah–Singer index theorem](wikipedia://en/324752), pertaining to index (dim ker – dim coker) of elliptic operators on [compact manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_manifold).

## History {#History}

Fredholm's 1903 paper in *Acta Mathematica* is considered to be one of the major landmarks in the establishment of [operator theory](wikipedia://en/712675). [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) developed the abstraction of [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) in association with research on integral equations prompted by Fredholm's (amongst other things).
