# Orthogonal complement

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Orthogonal complement"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) fields of [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) and [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), the **orthogonal complement** of a [subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) $W$ of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $V$ equipped with a [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477) $B$ is the set $W^\perp$ of all vectors in $V$ that are [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) to every vector in $W$. Informally, it is called the **perp**, short for **perpendicular complement**. It is a subspace of $V$.

:::

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Let $V = (\mathbb{R}^5, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)$ be the vector space equipped with the usual [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ (thus making it an [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856)), and let

$$W = \{\mathbf{u} \in V: \mathbf{A}x = \mathbf{u},\ x\in \mathbb{R}^2\},$$

with

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0 & 1\\ 2 & 6\\ 3 & 9\\ 5 & 3\\ \end{pmatrix}.$$

then its orthogonal complement

$$W^\perp = \{\mathbf{v}\in V:\langle \mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle = 0 \ \ \forall \ \mathbf{u} \in W\}$$

can also be defined as

$$W^\perp = \{\mathbf{v} \in V: \mathbf{\tilde{A}}y = \mathbf{v},\ y \in \mathbb{R}^3\},$$

being

$$\mathbf{\tilde{A}} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & -3 & -5 \\ -6 & -9 & -3 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The fact that every [column vector](wikipedia://en/149968) in $\mathbf{A}$ is orthogonal to every column vector in $\mathbf{\tilde{A}}$ can be checked by direct computation. The fact that the spans of these vectors are orthogonal then follows by bilinearity of the dot product. Finally, the fact that these spaces are orthogonal complements follows from the dimension relationships given below.

:::

## General bilinear forms {#General_bilinear_forms}

Let $V$ be a vector space over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $\mathbb{F}$ equipped with a [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477) $B.$ We define $\mathbf{u}$ to be left-orthogonal to $\mathbf{v}$, and $\mathbf{v}$ to be right-orthogonal to $\mathbf{u}$, when $B(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}) = 0.$ For a subset $W$ of $V,$ define the left-orthogonal complement $W^\perp$ to be

$$W^\perp = \left\{ \mathbf{x} \in V : B(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = 0 \ \ \forall \ \mathbf{y} \in W \right\}.$$

There is a corresponding definition of the right-orthogonal complement. For a [reflexive bilinear form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflexive_bilinear_form), where $B(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}) = 0 \implies B(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}) = 0 \ \ \forall \ \mathbf{u} , \mathbf{v} \in V$, the left and right complements coincide. This will be the case if $B$ is a [symmetric](wikipedia://en/1803375) or an [alternating form](wikipedia://en/449477#Symmetric,_skew-symmetric_and_alternating_forms).

The definition extends to a bilinear form on a [free module](wikipedia://en/326454) over a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346), and to a [sesquilinear form](wikipedia://en/860138) extended to include any free module over a commutative ring with [conjugation](wikipedia://en/458467).[^o5]

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

- An orthogonal complement is a subspace of $V$;
- If $X \subseteq Y$ then $X^\perp \supseteq Y^\perp$;
- The [radical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_of_a_quadratic_space) $V^\perp$ of $V$ is a subspace of every orthogonal complement;
- $$W \subseteq (W^\perp)^\perp;$$
- If $B$ is [non-degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-degenerate) and $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $\dim(W)+\dim (W^\perp)=\dim (V)$.
- If $L_1, \ldots, L_r$ are subspaces of a finite-dimensional space $V$ and $L_* = L_1 \cap \cdots \cap L_r,$ then $L_*^\perp = L_1^\perp + \cdots + L_r^\perp$.

:::

## Inner product spaces {#Inner_product_spaces}

This section considers orthogonal complements in an [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856) $H$.[^o9]

Two vectors $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ are called [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_vectors_%28inner_product_space%29) if $\langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \rangle = 0$, which happens [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $\| \mathbf{x} \| \le \| \mathbf{x} + s\mathbf{y} \| \ \forall$ scalars $s$.[^n1]

If $C$ is any subset of an inner product space $H$ then its orthogonal complement in $H$ is the vector subspace

$$\begin{aligned} C^\perp :&= \{\mathbf{x} \in H : \langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{c} \rangle = 0 \ \ \forall \ \mathbf{c} \in C\} \\ &= \{\mathbf{x} \in H : \langle \mathbf{c}, \mathbf{x} \rangle = 0 \ \ \forall \ \mathbf{c} \in C\} \end{aligned}$$

which is always a [closed subset](wikipedia://en/47279) (hence, a closed vector subspace) of $H$[^n1][^o12] that satisfies:

- $$C^{\bot} = \left(\operatorname{cl}_H \left(\operatorname{span} C\right)\right)^{\bot};$$
- $$C^{\bot} \cap \operatorname{cl}_H \left(\operatorname{span} C\right) = \{ 0 \};$$
- $$C^{\bot} \cap \left(\operatorname{span} C\right) = \{ 0 \};$$
- $$C \subseteq \left(C^{\bot}\right)^{\bot};$$
- $$\operatorname{cl}_H \left(\operatorname{span} C\right) \subseteq \left(C^{\bot}\right)^{\bot}.$$

If $C$ is a vector subspace of an inner product space $H$ then

$$C^{\bot} = \left\{\mathbf{x} \in H : \|\mathbf{x}\| \leq \|\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{c}\| \ \ \forall \ \mathbf{c} \in C \right\}.$$

If $C$ is a closed vector subspace of a Hilbert space $H$ then[^n1]

$$H = C \oplus C^{\bot} \qquad \text{ and } \qquad \left(C^{\bot}\right)^{\bot} = C$$

where $H = C \oplus C^{\bot}$ is called the orthogonal decomposition of $H$ into $C$ and $C^{\bot}$ and it indicates that $C$ is a [complemented subspace](wikipedia://en/63033543) of $H$ with complement $C^{\bot}.$

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

The orthogonal complement is always closed in the [metric topology](wikipedia://en/20018). In finite-dimensional spaces, that is merely an instance of the fact that all subspaces of a vector space are closed. In infinite-dimensional [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932), some subspaces are not closed, but all orthogonal complements are closed. If $W$ is a vector subspace of a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) the orthogonal complement of the orthogonal complement of $W$ is the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) of $W,$ that is,

$$\left(W^\bot\right)^\bot = \overline W.$$

Some other useful properties that always hold are the following. Let $H$ be a Hilbert space and let $X$ and $Y$ be linear subspaces. Then:

- $$X^\bot = \overline{X}^{\bot};$$
- if $Y \subseteq X$ then $X^\bot \subseteq Y^\bot$;
- $$X \cap X^\bot = \{ 0 \};$$
- $$X \subseteq (X^\bot)^\bot;$$
- if $X$ is a closed linear subspace of $H$ then $(X^\bot)^\bot = X$;
- if $X$ is a closed linear subspace of $H$ then $H = X \oplus X^\bot,$ the (inner) [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687).

The orthogonal complement generalizes to the [annihilator](wikipedia://en/328684), and gives a [Galois connection](wikipedia://en/156411) on subsets of the inner product space, with associated [closure operator](wikipedia://en/483120) the topological closure of the span.

:::

### Finite dimensions {#Finite_dimensions}

For a finite-dimensional inner product space of dimension $n$, the orthogonal complement of a $k$-dimensional subspace is an $(n-k)$-dimensional subspace, and the double orthogonal complement is the original subspace:

$$\left(W^{\bot}\right)^{\bot} = W.$$

If $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{M}_{mn}$, where $\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A})$, $\mathcal{C} (\mathbf{A})$, and $\mathcal{N} (\mathbf{A})$ refer to the [row space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Row_space), [column space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_space), and [null space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_space) of $\mathbf{A}$ (respectively), then[^o17]

$$\left(\mathcal{R} (\mathbf{A}) \right)^{\bot} = \mathcal{N} (\mathbf{A}) \qquad \text{ and } \qquad \left(\mathcal{C} (\mathbf{A}) \right)^{\bot} = \mathcal{N} (\mathbf{A}^{\operatorname{T}}).$$

## Banach spaces {#Banach_spaces}

There is a natural analog of this notion in general [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989). In this case one defines the orthogonal complement of $W$ to be a subspace of the [dual](wikipedia://en/7988) of $V$ defined similarly as the [annihilator](wikipedia://en/7988#Annihilators)

$$W^\bot = \left\{ x\in V^* : \forall y\in W, x(y) = 0 \right\}.$$

It is always a closed subspace of $V^*$. There is also an analog of the double complement property. $W^{\perp \perp}$ is now a subspace of $V^{**}$(which is not identical to $V$). However, the [reflexive spaces](wikipedia://en/210091) have a [natural](wikipedia://en/45022) [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) $i$ between $V$ and $V^{**}$. In this case we have

$$i\overline{W} = W^{\perp\perp}.$$

This is a rather straightforward consequence of the [Hahn–Banach theorem](wikipedia://en/13860).

::: {#o20 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

In [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) the orthogonal complement is used to determine the [simultaneous hyperplane](wikipedia://en/165487#Simultaneous_hyperplane) at a point of a [world line](wikipedia://en/165487). The bilinear form $\eta$ used in [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space) determines a [pseudo-Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/10962546) of events.[^o21] The origin and all events on the [light cone](wikipedia://en/310474) are self-orthogonal. When a [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time) event and a [space](wikipedia://en/27667) event evaluate to zero under the bilinear form, then they are [hyperbolic-orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic-orthogonal). This terminology stems from the use of [conjugate hyperbolas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_hyperbola) in the pseudo-Euclidean plane: [conjugate diameters](wikipedia://en/24531598) of these hyperbolas are hyperbolic-orthogonal.

:::
