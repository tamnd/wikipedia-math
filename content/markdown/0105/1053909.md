# Stein manifold

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Stein manifold"}

In mathematics, in the theory of [several complex variables](wikipedia://en/360243) and [complex manifolds](wikipedia://en/509742), a **Stein manifold** is a closed, complex [submanifold](wikipedia://en/525095) of the [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of *n* [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) dimensions. More intrinsically it can be defined as a complex manifold admitting a [proper](wikipedia://en/1190262) [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110) [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119) into $\mathbb{C}^n$ for some $n$. They were introduced by and named after (txt). A **Stein space** is similar to a Stein manifold but is allowed to have singularities. Stein spaces are the analogues of [affine varieties](wikipedia://en/320468) or [affine schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_schemes) in algebraic geometry.

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Suppose $X$ is a [complex manifold](wikipedia://en/509742) of complex dimension $n$ and let $\mathcal O(X)$ denote the ring of [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110) on $X.$ We call $X$ a **Stein manifold** if the following two conditions hold:[^o5]

- $X$ is holomorphically convex, i.e. for every [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) subset $K \subset X$, the so-called *[holomorphic hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphically_convex) (or envelope of holomorphy) of* $K$,$\widehat K := \left \{z \in X \,\left|\, |f(z)| \leq \sup_{w \in K} |f(w)| \ \forall f \in \mathcal O(X) \right. \right \},$

is also a *compact* subset of $X$.

- $X$ is holomorphically separable, i.e. if $x \neq y$ are two distinct points in $X$, then there exists $f \in \mathcal O(X)$ such that $f(x) \neq f(y).$

:::

## Non-compact Riemann surfaces are Stein manifolds {#Non-compact_Riemann_surfaces_are_Stein_manifolds}

Let *X* be a connected, non-compact [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181). A deep [theorem](wikipedia://en/67660345) of [Heinrich Behnke](wikipedia://en/22922678) and Stein (1948) asserts that *X* is a Stein manifold.[^o7]

Another result, attributed to [Hans Grauert](wikipedia://en/2979031) and [Helmut Röhrl](wikipedia://en/40932800) (1956), states moreover that every [holomorphic vector bundle](wikipedia://en/4794668), and in particular every holomorphic line bundle, on this Riemann surface *X* is trivial. In particular, every line bundle is trivial. This is related to the solution of the [second Cousin problem](wikipedia://en/622966).

## Properties and examples of Stein manifolds {#Properties_and_examples_of_Stein_manifolds}

- The standard complex space $\mathbb{C}^n$ is a Stein manifold.
- Every [domain of holomorphy](wikipedia://en/3236377) in $\mathbb{C}^n$ is a Stein manifold.
- Every [Fatou–Bieberbach domain](wikipedia://en/4684859) in $\mathbb{C}^n$ is a Stein manifold.
- Every closed complex submanifold of a Stein manifold is a Stein manifold, too.
- The embedding theorem for Stein manifolds states the following: Every Stein manifold $X$ of complex dimension $n$ can be embedded into $\mathbb{C}^{2 n+1}$ by a [biholomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biholomorphic) [proper map](wikipedia://en/1190262).

These facts imply that a Stein manifold is a closed complex submanifold of complex space, whose complex structure is that of the [ambient space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambient_space) (because the embedding is biholomorphic).

- Every Stein manifold of (complex) dimension *n* has the homotopy type of an *n*-dimensional CW-complex.
- In one complex dimension the Stein condition can be simplified: a connected [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181) is a Stein manifold [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is not compact. This can be proved using a version of the [Runge theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runge_theorem) for Riemann surfaces, due to Behnke and Stein.
- Every Stein manifold $X$ is holomorphically spreadable, i.e. for every point $x \in X$, there are $n$ holomorphic functions defined on all of $X$ which form a local coordinate system when restricted to some open neighborhood of $x$.
- Being a Stein manifold is equivalent to being a (complex) *strongly pseudoconvex manifold*. The latter means that it has a strongly pseudoconvex (or [plurisubharmonic](wikipedia://en/2519085)) exhaustive function, i.e. a smooth real function $\psi$ on $X$ (which can be assumed to be a [Morse function](wikipedia://en/348560)) with $i \partial \bar \partial \psi >0$, such that the subsets $\{z \in X \mid \psi (z)\leq c \}$ are compact in $X$ for every real number $c$. This result, due to Grauert[^o9] and generalized to Stein spaces by Narasimhan,[^o10] is usually called the solution to the **Levi problem**, named after [Eugenio Levi](wikipedia://en/11114879) (1911). The function $\psi$ invites a generalization of *Stein manifold* to the idea of a corresponding class of compact complex manifolds with boundary called **Stein domains**. A Stein domain is the preimage $\{z \mid -\infty\leq\psi(z)\leq c\}$. Some authors therefore call such manifolds strictly pseudoconvex manifolds.
- Related to the previous item, another equivalent and more topological definition in complex dimension 2 is the following: a Stein surface is a complex surface *X* with a real-valued Morse function *f* on *X* such that, away from the critical points of *f*, the field of complex tangencies to the preimage $X_c=f^{-1}(c)$ is a [contact structure](wikipedia://en/476398) that induces an orientation on *X<sub>c</sub>* agreeing with the usual orientation as the boundary of $f^{-1}(-\infty, c).$ That is, $f^{-1}(-\infty, c)$ is a Stein [filling](wikipedia://en/6998364) of *X<sub>c</sub>*.

Numerous further characterizations of such manifolds exist, in particular capturing the property of their having "many" [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110) taking values in the complex numbers. See for example [Cartan's theorems A and B](wikipedia://en/621677), relating to [sheaf cohomology](wikipedia://en/1055357). The initial impetus was to have a description of the properties of the domain of definition of the (maximal) [analytic continuation](wikipedia://en/80842) of an [analytic function](wikipedia://en/61478).

In the [GAGA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GAGA) set of analogies, Stein manifolds correspond to [affine varieties](wikipedia://en/320468).

Stein manifolds are in some sense dual to the [elliptic manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_manifold) in complex analysis which admit "many" holomorphic functions from the complex numbers into themselves. It is known that a Stein manifold is elliptic if and only if it is [fibrant](wikipedia://en/1782993) in the sense of so-called "holomorphic homotopy theory".

## Relation to smooth manifolds {#Relation_to_smooth_manifolds}

Every compact smooth manifold of dimension 2*n*, which has only handles of index ≤ *n*, has a Stein structure provided *n* > 2, and when *n* = 2 the same holds provided the 2-handles are attached with certain framings (framing less than the [Thurston–Bennequin framing](wikipedia://en/7418819)).[^o13][^o14] Every closed smooth 4-manifold is a union of two Stein 4-manifolds glued along their common boundary.[^o15]

## Stein manifolds and curvature {#Stein_manifolds_and_curvature}

If X is a Stein manifold, then as it embeds into $\mathbb{C}^n$and since holomorphic bisectional curvature decreases on submanifolds, X has a Kähler metric of nonpositive holomorphic bisectional curvature. The reciprocal is known to be false: there are examples of simply-connected compact (and hence not Stein) projective manifolds admitting a metric of nonpositive holomorphic bisectional curvature.[^o17] There is a weaker result, due to Wu in 1967, which states that any simply-connected complete Kähler manifold of nonpositive sectional curvature is a Stein manifold.[^o18]
