# Pythagorean trigonometric identity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pythagorean trigonometric identity"}

The **Pythagorean trigonometric identity**, also called simply the **Pythagorean identity**, is an [identity](wikipedia://en/298428) expressing the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) in terms of [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367). Along with the [sum-of-angles formulae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_identity#Angle_sum_and_difference_identities), it is one of the basic relations between the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) and [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine) functions.

:::

The identity is

$$\sin^2 \theta + \cos^2 \theta = 1,$$

where $\sin^2 \theta$ means $(\sin\theta)^2$ and $\cos^2 \theta$ means $(\cos\theta)^2$.

::: {#o3 .proof cue=heading name="Proofs and their relationships to the Pythagorean theorem"}

### Proof based on right-angle triangles {#Proof_based_on_right-angle_triangles}

Any [similar triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similar_triangles) have the property that if we select the same [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) in all of them, the ratio of the two sides defining the angle is the same regardless of which similar triangle is selected, regardless of its actual size: the ratios depend upon the three angles, not the lengths of the sides. Thus for either of the similar [right triangles](wikipedia://en/39420) in the figure, the ratio of its horizontal side to its [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) is the same, namely $\cos\,\theta$.

The elementary definitions of the sine and cosine functions in terms of the sides of a right triangle are:

$$\begin{alignedat}{3} \sin \theta &= \frac{\mathrm{opposite}}{\mathrm{hypotenuse}} = \frac{b}{c} \\ \cos \theta &= \frac{\mathrm{adjacent}}{\mathrm{hypotenuse}} = \frac{a}{c} \end{alignedat}$$

The Pythagorean identity follows by [squaring](wikipedia://en/659942) both definitions above, and adding; the [left-hand side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-hand_side) of the identity then becomes

$$\frac{\mathrm{opposite}^2 + \mathrm{adjacent}^2}{\mathrm{hypotenuse}^2}$$

which by the Pythagorean theorem is equal to 1. This definition is valid for all angles, due to the definition of defining $x = \cos \theta$ and $y \sin \theta$ for the unit circle and thus $x = c\,\cos \theta$ and $y = c\,\sin \theta$ for a circle of radius $c$ and reflecting our triangle in the $y$-axis and setting $a = x$ and $b = y$.

Alternatively, the identities found at [Trigonometric symmetry, shifts, and periodicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_identity#Reflections,_shifts,_and_periodicity) may be employed. By the [periodicity](wikipedia://en/76848) identities we can say if the formula is true for $-\pi < \theta \le \pi$ then it is true for all [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\theta$. Next we [prove](wikipedia://en/82285) the identity in the range $\tfrac{\pi}{2} < \theta \le \pi$. To do this we let $t = \theta - \tfrac{\pi}{2}$, $t$ will now be in the range $0 < t \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}$. We can then make use of squared versions of some basic shift identities (squaring conveniently removes the minus signs):

$$\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = \sin^2\left(t + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) + \cos^2\left(t + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1.$$

Finally, what remains is to prove the formula for $-\pi < \theta < 0$; this can be done by squaring the symmetry identities to get

$$\sin^2\theta = \sin^2(-\theta)\text{ and }\cos^2\theta = \cos^2(-\theta).$$

#### Related identities {#Related_identities}

*Image: Trig functions2.svg.* Similar right triangles illustrating the tangent and secant trigonometric functions

*Image: Trigonometric functions and their reciprocals on the unit circle.svg.* Trigonometric functions and their reciprocals on the unit circle. The [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) applied to the blue triangle shows the identity $1 + \cot^{2}\,\theta = \csc^{2}\,\theta$, and applied to the red triangle shows that $1 + \tan^{2}\,\theta = \sec^{2}\,\theta$.

The two identities

$$\begin{aligned} 1 + \tan^2 \theta &= \sec^2 \theta \\ 1 + \cot^2 \theta &= \csc^2 \theta \end{aligned}$$

are also called Pythagorean trigonometric identities.[^o7] If one leg of a right triangle has length 1, then the [tangent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_function) of the angle adjacent to that leg is the length of the other leg, and the [secant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secant_%28trigonometry%29) of the angle is the length of the hypotenuse.

$$\begin{aligned} \tan \theta &= \frac{b}{a}\,, \\ \sec \theta &= \frac{c}{a}\,. \end{aligned}$$

In this way, this trigonometric identity involving the tangent and the secant follows from the Pythagorean theorem. The angle opposite the leg of length 1 (this angle can be labeled $\varphi = \tfrac{\pi}{2} - \theta$) has [cotangent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotangent) equal to the length of the other leg, and [cosecant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosecant) equal to the length of the hypotenuse. In that way, this trigonometric identity involving the cotangent and the cosecant also follows from the Pythagorean theorem.

The following table gives the identities with the factor or [divisor](wikipedia://en/49492) that relates them to the main identity.

*Identities derived by dividing $\, + \, = 1$*

| $\,$ | $\frac{\sin^2 \theta}{\cos^2 \theta} + \frac{\cos^2 \theta}{\cos^2 \theta} = \frac{1}{\cos^2 \theta}$ | $\tan^2 \theta + 1 = \sec^2 \theta$ | $\begin{aligned} \sec^2\theta - \tan^2\theta = 1\\ (\sec\theta - \tan\theta)(\sec\theta + \tan\theta) = 1\\ \end{aligned}$ |

### Proof using the unit circle {#Proof_using_the_unit_circle}

*Image: Trig functions on unit circle.svg.* Point $P(x,y)$ on the circle of unit radius at an [obtuse angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obtuse_angle) $\theta > \tfrac{\pi}{2}$

*Image: Periodic sine.svg.* Sine function on unit circle (top) and its [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) (bottom)

The unit circle centered at the origin in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) is defined by the equation:[^o11]

$$x^2 + y^2 = 1.$$

Given an angle *θ*, there is a unique point *P* on the unit circle at an anticlockwise angle of *θ* from the *x*-axis, and the *x*- and *y*-coordinates of *P* are:[^o12]

$$x = \cos\theta \ \text{ and }\ y = \sin\theta.$$

Consequently, from the equation for the unit circle,

$$\cos^2 \theta + \sin^2 \theta = 1,$$

the Pythagorean identity.

In the figure, the point $P$ has a negative $x$-coordinate, and is appropriately given by $x = \cos\,\theta$, which is a [negative number](wikipedia://en/154616): $\cos\,\theta = -\cos(\pi - \theta)$. Point $P$ has a positive $y$-coordinate, and $\sin\,\theta = \sin(\pi - \theta) > 0$. As $\theta$ increases from zero to the full circle $\theta = 2\pi$, the sine and cosine change signs in the various quadrants to keep $x$ and $y$ with the correct signs. The figure shows how the sign of the sine function varies as the angle changes quadrant.

Because the $x$- and $y$-axes are perpendicular, this Pythagorean identity is equivalent to the Pythagorean theorem for triangles with hypotenuse of length 1 (which is in turn equivalent to the full Pythagorean theorem by applying a similar-triangles argument). See [Unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) for a short explanation.

### Proof using power series {#Proof_using_power_series}

The trigonometric functions may also be defined using [power series](wikipedia://en/59958), namely for $x$ (an angle measured in [radians](wikipedia://en/26003)):[^o15][^o16]

$$\begin{aligned} \sin x &= \sum_{n = 0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n}{(2n + 1)!} x^{2n + 1},\\ \cos x &= \sum_{n = 0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n}{(2n)!} x^{2n}. \end{aligned}$$

Using the multiplication formula for power series at [Multiplication and division of power series](wikipedia://en/59958#Multiplication_and_division) (suitably modified to account for the form of the series here) we obtain

$$\begin{aligned} \sin^2 x & = \sum_{i = 0}^\infty \sum_{j = 0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^i}{(2i + 1)!} \frac{(-1)^j}{(2j + 1)!} x^{(2i + 1) + (2j + 1)} \\ & = \sum_{n = 1}^\infty \left(\sum_{i = 0}^{n - 1} \frac{(-1)^{n - 1}}{(2i + 1)!(2(n - i - 1) + 1)!}\right) x^{2n} \\ & = \sum_{n = 1}^\infty \left( \sum_{i = 0}^{n - 1} {2n \choose 2i + 1} \right) \frac{(-1)^{n - 1}}{(2n)!} x^{2n},\\ \cos^2 x & = \sum_{i = 0}^\infty \sum_{j = 0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^i}{(2i)!} \frac{(-1)^j}{(2j)!} x^{(2i) + (2j)} \\ & = \sum_{n = 0}^\infty \left(\sum_{i = 0}^n \frac{(-1)^n}{(2i)!(2(n - i))!}\right) x^{2n} \\ & = \sum_{n = 0}^\infty \left( \sum_{i = 0}^n {2n \choose 2i} \right) \frac{(-1)^n}{(2n)!} x^{2n}. \end{aligned}$$

In the expression for $\sin^{2}$, $n$ must be at least 1, while in the expression for $\cos^{2}$, the [constant term](wikipedia://en/79861) is equal to 1. The remaining terms of their sum are (with common factors removed)

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_{i = 0}^n {2n \choose 2i} - \sum_{i = 0}^{n - 1} {2n \choose 2i + 1} &= \sum_{j = 0}^{2n} (-1)^j {2n \choose j} \\ &= (1 - 1)^{2n} = 0 \end{aligned}$$

by the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677). Consequently,

$$\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1,$$

which is the Pythagorean trigonometric identity.

When the trigonometric functions are defined in this way, the identity in combination with the Pythagorean theorem shows that these power series [parameterize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_plot) the unit circle, which we used in the previous section. This definition constructs the sine and cosine functions in a rigorous fashion and proves that they are [differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable), so that in fact it subsumes the previous two.

:::

### Proof using the differential equation {#Proof_using_the_differential_equation}

Sine and cosine [can be defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function#Definition_by_differential_equations) as the two solutions to the [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309):[^o19]

$$y'' + y = 0$$

satisfying respectively $y(0) = 0$, $y(0) = 1$ and $y(0) = 1$, $y(0) = 0$. It follows from the theory of [ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/32742753) that the first solution, sine, has the second, cosine, as its [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921), and it follows from this that the derivative of cosine is the negative of the sine. The identity is equivalent to the assertion that the function

$$z = \sin^2 x + \cos^2 x$$

is constant and equal to 1. [Differentiating](wikipedia://en/7921) using the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) gives:

$$\frac{d}{dx} z = 2 \sin x \cos x + 2 \cos x(-\sin x) = 0,$$

so $z$ is constant. A calculation confirms that $z(0) = 1$, and $z$ is a constant so $z = 1$ for all $x$, so the Pythagorean identity is established.

A similar proof can be completed using power series as above to establish that the sine has as its derivative the cosine, and the cosine has as its derivative the negative sine. In fact, the definitions by ordinary differential equation and by power series lead to similar derivations of most identities.

This proof of the identity has no direct connection with [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s demonstration of the Pythagorean theorem.

:::

::: {#o21 .proof cue=heading name="Proof using Euler's formula"}

### Proof using Euler's formula {#Proof_using_Euler's_formula}

Factoring $\cos^2 \theta + \sin^2 \theta$ as the complex [difference of two squares](wikipedia://en/1102297) and using [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613) $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$,

$$\begin{aligned} \cos^2 \theta + \sin^2 \theta &= \cos^2 \theta - i^2 \sin^2 \theta \\[3mu] &= (\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)(\cos\theta - i\sin\theta) \\[3mu] &= e^{i\theta}e^{-i\theta} \\ &= e^{i\theta-i\theta} = e^0 = 1 \end{aligned}$$
