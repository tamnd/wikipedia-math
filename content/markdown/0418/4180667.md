# Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit"}

The **Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit** (or **TOV limit**) is an upper bound to the [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star#Mass) of cold, non-rotating [neutron stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star), analogous to the [Chandrasekhar limit](wikipedia://en/6813) for [white dwarf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_dwarf) stars. Stars more massive than the TOV limit collapse into a [black hole](wikipedia://en/4650), or possibly to a [quark star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark_star). The original calculation in 1939, which neglected complications such as nuclear forces between neutrons, placed this limit at approximately 0.7 [solar masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_mass) (). Later, more refined analyses have resulted in larger values.

:::

Theoretical work in 1996 placed the limit at approximately 1.5 to 3.0 ,[^o2] corresponding to an original stellar mass of 15 to 20 ; additional work in the same year gave a more precise range of 2.2 to 2.9 .[^o3]

Data from [GW170817](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GW170817), the first gravitational wave observation attributed to [merging neutron stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star_merger) (thought to have collapsed into a black hole[^o4] within a few seconds after merging[^o5]) placed the limit in the range of 2.01 to 2.17 .[^o6]

In the case of a rigidly spinning neutron star, meaning that different levels in the interior of the star all rotate at the same angular velocity, the mass limit is thought to increase by up to 18–20%.[^o5][^o6]

## History {#History}

The idea that there should be an absolute upper limit for the mass of a cold (as distinct from thermal pressure-supported) self-gravitating body dates back to the 1932 work of [Lev Landau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lev_Landau), based on the [Pauli exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/24669). Pauli's principle shows that the [fermionic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion) particles in sufficiently compressed matter would be forced into energy states so high that their [rest mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rest_mass) contribution would become negligible when compared with the relativistic kinetic contribution (RKC). RKC is determined just by the relevant [quantum wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter_wave) $\lambda$, which would be of the order of the mean interparticle separation. In terms of [Planck units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_units), with the [reduced Planck constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_Planck_constant) $\text{ħ}$, the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) $c$, and the [gravitational constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_constant) $G$ all set equal to one, there will be a corresponding [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) given roughly by $P=\frac{1}{\lambda^4}$. At the upper mass limit, that pressure will equal the pressure needed to resist gravity. The pressure to resist gravity for a body of mass $M$ will be given according to the [virial theorem](wikipedia://en/32664) roughly by $P^3=M^2\rho^4$, where $\rho$ is the density. This will be given by $\rho = \tfrac{m}{\lambda^{3}}$, where $m$ is the relevant mass per particle. It can be seen that the wavelength cancels out so that one obtains an approximate mass limit formula of the very simple form $M=\frac{1}{m^2}$. In this relationship, $m$ can be taken to be given roughly by the [proton mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton_mass). This even applies in the [white dwarf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_dwarf) case (that of the [Chandrasekhar limit](wikipedia://en/6813)) for which the fermionic particles providing the pressure are electrons. This is because the mass density is provided by the nuclei in which the neutrons are at most about as numerous as the protons. Likewise, the protons, for charge neutrality, must be exactly as numerous as the electrons outside.

In the case of [neutron stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_stars), this limit was first worked out by [J. Robert Oppenheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Robert_Oppenheimer) and [George Volkoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Volkoff) in 1939, using the work of [Richard Chace Tolman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Chace_Tolman). Oppenheimer and Volkoff assumed that the [neutrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron) in a neutron star formed a [degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_matter) cold [Fermi gas](wikipedia://en/181983). They thereby obtained a limiting mass of approximately 0.7 [solar masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_mass),[^o8][^o9] which was less than the [Chandrasekhar limit](wikipedia://en/6813) for white dwarfs.

Oppenheimer and Volkoff's paper notes that "the effect of repulsive forces, i.e., of raising the pressure for a given density above the value given by the Fermi equation of state ... could tend to prevent the collapse."[^o9] And indeed, the most massive neutron star detected so far, [PSR J0952–0607](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PSR_J0952–0607), is estimated to be much heavier than Oppenheimer and Volkoff's TOV limit at \2.35 ± \0.17 *M*<sub>☉</sub>.[^o10][^o11] More realistic models of neutron stars that include [baryon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baryon) [strong force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_interaction) repulsion predict a neutron star mass limit of 2.2 to 2.9 M<sub>☉</sub>.[^o12][^o13] The uncertainty in the value reflects the fact that the [equations of state](wikipedia://en/1540704) for [extremely dense matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QCD_matter) are not well known.

::: {#o15 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

In a star less massive than the limit, the gravitational compression is balanced by short-range repulsive neutron–neutron interactions mediated by the strong force and also by the quantum degeneracy pressure of neutrons, preventing collapse.[^o16] If its mass is above the limit, the star will collapse to some denser form. It could form a [black hole](wikipedia://en/4650), or change composition and be supported in some other way (for example, by [quark degeneracy pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_matter#Quark_degeneracy) if it becomes a [quark star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark_star)). Because the properties of hypothetical, more exotic forms of [degenerate matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_matter) are even more poorly known than those of neutron-degenerate matter, most astrophysicists assume, in the absence of evidence to the contrary, that a neutron star above the limit collapses directly into a black hole.

A [black hole formed by the collapse of an individual star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_black_hole) must have mass exceeding the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit. Theory predicts that because of [mass loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_mass_loss) during [stellar evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_evolution), a black hole formed from an isolated star of solar [metallicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallicity) can have a mass of no more than approximately 10 [solar masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_mass).[^o17]<sup>: Fig. 16</sup> Observationally, because of their large mass, relative faintness, and X-ray spectra, several massive objects in [X-ray binaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_binaries) are thought to be stellar black holes. These black hole candidates are estimated to have masses between 3 and 20 [solar masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_mass).[^o18][^o19] [LIGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIGO) has [detected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_gravitational_wave_observations) black hole mergers involving black holes in the 7.5–50 solar mass range; it is possible – although unlikely – that these black holes were themselves the result of previous mergers.

Oppenheimer and Volkoff discounted the influence of heat, stating in reference to work by Landau (1932), 'even \[at\] 10<sup>7</sup> degrees... the pressure is determined essentially by the density only and not by the temperature'[^o9] – yet it has been estimated[^o20] that temperatures can reach up to approximately > 10<sup>9</sup> K during formation of a neutron star, mergers and binary accretion. Another source of heat and therefore collapse-resisting pressure in neutron stars is 'viscous friction in the presence of differential rotation.'[^o20]

Oppenheimer and Volkoff's calculation of the mass limit of neutron stars also neglected to consider the rotation of neutron stars. We now know that neutron stars are capable of spinning at much faster rates than were known in Oppenheimer and Volkoff's time. The fastest-spinning neutron star known is PSR J1748-2446ad, rotating at a rate of 716 times per second[^o21][^o22] or 43,000 revolutions per minute, giving a linear (tangential) speed at the surface on the order of 0.24c (i.e., nearly a quarter the speed of light). Star rotation interferes with convective heat loss during supernova collapse, so rotating stars are more likely to collapse directly to form a black hole [^o23]
