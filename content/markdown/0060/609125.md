# Expression (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Expression"}

*Image: Equation\_vs\_Expression.png.* In the [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) 7x − 5 = 2, the [sides of the equation](wikipedia://en/166352) are expressions.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **expression** is an arrangement of [symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbol_%28mathematics%29) following the context-dependent, [syntactic](wikipedia://en/546039) conventions of [mathematical notation](wikipedia://en/277184). Symbols can denote [numbers](wikipedia://en/21690), [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109), [operations](wikipedia://en/4140245), and [functions](wikipedia://en/185427).[^o2] Other symbols include [punctuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punctuation) marks and [brackets](wikipedia://en/11219603), used for [grouping](wikipedia://en/73545085) where there is not a well-defined [order of operations](wikipedia://en/212980).

:::

Expressions are commonly distinguished from *[formulas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_formula)*: expressions usually denote [mathematical objects](wikipedia://en/19453961), whereas formulas are statements *about* mathematical objects, such as an [equality](wikipedia://en/90446).[^o3] This is analogous to [natural language](wikipedia://en/21173), where a [noun phrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noun_phrase) refers to an object, and a whole [sentence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence_%28linguistics%29) refers to a [fact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fact). For example, $8x-5$ and $3$ are both expressions, while the [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489) $8x-5 \geq 3$ is a formula. However, formulas are often considered as expressions that can be evaluated to the [Boolean values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_value) *true* or *false*.

To *evaluate* an expression means to find a numerical [value](wikipedia://en/4095147) equivalent to the expression.[^o4][^o5] Expressions can be *evaluated* or *simplified* by replacing operations that appear in them with their result. For example, the expression $8\times 2-5$ simplifies to $16-5$, and evaluates to $11.$

An expression is often used to define a [function](wikipedia://en/185427), by taking the variables to be [arguments](wikipedia://en/32842052), or inputs, of the function, and assigning the output to be the evaluation of the resulting expression.[^o6] For example, $x\mapsto x^2+1$ and $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ define the function that associates to each number its [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_function) plus one. An expression with no variables would define a [constant function](wikipedia://en/320861). Usually, two expressions are considered equal or *equivalent* if they define the same function; such an equality is called a "[semantic](wikipedia://en/31395652) equality", that is, both expressions "mean the same thing."

## Elementary mathematics {#Elementary_mathematics}

### Variables and evaluation {#Variables_and_evaluation}

In [elementary algebra](wikipedia://en/9710), a [*variable*](wikipedia://en/3728109) in an expression is a [letter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_%28alphabet%29) that represents a number whose value may change. To *evaluate an expression* with a variable means to find the value of the expression when the variable is [substituted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substitution_%28algebra%29) with given number. Expressions can be *simplified* by replacing [operations](wikipedia://en/4140245) that appear in them with their result, or by combining [like-terms](wikipedia://en/19222837). The *evaluation* of an expression consists of repeating simplification steps until getting eventually a single number.[^o9]

For example, take the expression $4 x^2 + 8$; it can be evaluated for $x = 3$ in the following steps:

$4\cdot3^2 + 8$, (replace x with 3)

$4 \cdot 9 + 8$ (evaluate the square)

$36 + 8$ (evaluate the multiplication)

$44$ (evaluate the addition)

A *term* is a constant or the [product](wikipedia://en/24977) of a constant and one or more variables. Some examples include $7, \; 5x, \; 13x^2y, \; 4b$ The constant of the product is called the [coefficient](wikipedia://en/51203). Terms that are either constants or have the same variables raised to the same powers are called *[like terms](wikipedia://en/19222837)*. If there are like terms in an expression, one can simplify the expression by combining the like terms. One adds the coefficients and keeps the same variable.

$$4x + 7x +2x = 13x$$

Any variable can be classified as being either a [free variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_variable) or a [bound variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bound_variable). For a given combination of values for the free variables, an expression may be evaluated, although for some combinations of values of the free variables, the value of the expression may be [undefined](wikipedia://en/9920876). Thus an expression represents an [operation](wikipedia://en/4140245) over constants and free variables and whose output is the resulting value of the expression.[^o10]

For a non-formalized language, that is, in most mathematical texts outside of [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), for an individual expression it is not always possible to identify which variables are free and bound. For example, in $\sum_{i < k} a_{ik}$, depending on the context, the variable $i$ can be free and $k$ bound, or vice-versa, but they cannot both be free. Determining which value is assumed to be free depends on context and [semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantics_of_logic).[^o11]

### Equivalence {#Equivalence}

An expression is often used to define a [function](wikipedia://en/185427), or denote [compositions](wikipedia://en/195947) of functions, by taking the variables to be [arguments](wikipedia://en/32842052), or inputs, of the function, and assigning the output to be the evaluation of the resulting expression.[^o13] For example, $x\mapsto x^2+1$ and $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ define the function that associates to each number its [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_function) plus one. An expression with no variables would define a [constant function](wikipedia://en/320861). In this way, two expressions are said to be equivalent if, for each combination of values for the free variables, they have the same output, i.e., they represent the same function.[^o14][^o15] The equivalence between two expressions is called an [identity](wikipedia://en/298428) and is sometimes denoted with $\equiv.$

For example, in the expression $\sum_{n=1}^{3} (2nx),$ the variable $n$ is bound, and the variable $x$ is free. This expression is equivalent to the simpler expression $12 x$; that is

$$\sum_{n=1}^{3} (2nx)\equiv 12x.$$

The value for $x = 3$ is 36, which can be denoted

$$\sum_{n=1}^{3} (2nx)\Big|_{x=3}= 36.$$

## Well-defined expressions {#Well-defined_expressions}

The [language of mathematics](wikipedia://en/373299) exhibits a kind of [grammar](wikipedia://en/12569) (called [formal grammar](wikipedia://en/18020716)) about how expressions may be written. There are two considerations for well-definedness of mathematical expressions, [syntax](wikipedia://en/546039) and [semantics](wikipedia://en/31395652). Syntax is concerned with the rules used for constructing, or transforming the symbols of an expression without regard to any [interpretation](wikipedia://en/14511671) or [meaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meaning_%28linguistics%29) given to them. Expressions that are syntactically correct are called [well-formed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-formedness). Semantics is concerned with the meaning of these well-formed expressions. Expressions that are semantically correct are called [well-defined](wikipedia://en/253456).

### Well-formed {#Well-formed}

The syntax of mathematical expressions can be described somewhat informally as follows: the allowed [operators](wikipedia://en/22213) must have the correct number of inputs in the correct places (usually written with [infix notation](wikipedia://en/199014)), the sub-expressions that make up these inputs must be well-formed themselves, have a clear [order of operations](wikipedia://en/212980), etc. Strings of symbols that conform to the rules of syntax are called [*well-formed*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-formedness), and those that are not well-formed are called, *ill-formed*, and do not constitute mathematical expressions.[^o18]

For example, in [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118), the expression *1 + 2 × 3* is well-formed, but

$$\times4)x+,/y$$

. is not.

However, being well-formed is not enough to be considered well-defined. For example in arithmetic, the expression $\frac{1}{0}$ is well-formed, but it is not well-defined (see [Division by zero](wikipedia://en/185663)). Such expressions are called [undefined](wikipedia://en/9920876).

### Well-defined {#Well-defined}

[Semantics](wikipedia://en/29107) is the study of meaning. [Formal semantics](wikipedia://en/31395652) is about attaching meaning to expressions. An expression that defines a unique [value](wikipedia://en/4095147) or meaning is said to be [well-defined](wikipedia://en/253456). Otherwise, the expression is said to be ill defined or ambiguous.**[^o20]** In general the meaning of expressions is not limited to designating values; for instance, an expression might designate a condition, or an [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) that is to be solved, or it can be viewed as an object in its own right that can be manipulated according to certain rules. Certain expressions that designate a value simultaneously express a condition that is assumed to hold, for instance those involving the operator $\oplus$ to designate an internal [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687).

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), an expression may be used to designate a value, which might depend on values assigned to [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109) occurring in the expression. The determination of this value depends on the [semantics](wikipedia://en/29107) attached to the symbols of the expression. The choice of semantics depends on the context of the expression. The same syntactic expression *1 + 2 × 3* can have different values (mathematically 7, but also 9), depending on the [order of operations](wikipedia://en/212980) implied by the context (See also [Operations § Calculators](wikipedia://en/212980#Calculators)).

For [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), the product $a \times b \times c$ is unambiguous because $(a \times b)\times c = a \times (b \times c)$; hence the notation is said to be *well defined*.[^o20] This property, also known as [associativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity) of multiplication, guarantees the result does not depend on the sequence of multiplications; therefore, a specification of the sequence can be omitted. The [subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141) operation is non-associative; despite that, there is a convention that $a-b-c$ is shorthand for $(a-b)-c$, thus it is considered "well-defined". On the other hand, [Division](wikipedia://en/53696) is non-associative, and in the case of $a/b/c$, parenthesization conventions are not well established; therefore, this expression is often considered ill-defined.

Unlike with functions, notational ambiguities can be overcome by means of additional definitions (e.g., rules of [precedence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_precedence), associativity of the operator). For example, in the programming language [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28programming_language%29), the operator `-` for subtraction is *left-to-right-associative*, which means that `a-b-c` is defined as `(a-b)-c`, and the operator `=` for assignment is *right-to-left-associative*, which means that `a=b=c` is defined as `a=(b=c)`.[^o21] In the programming language [APL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/APL_%28programming_language%29) there is only one rule: from [right to left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/APL_%28programming_language%29#Design) – but parentheses first.

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

The term 'expression' is part of the [language of mathematics](wikipedia://en/373299), that is to say, it is not defined *within* mathematics, but taken as a [primitive](wikipedia://en/23706) part of the language. To attempt to define the term would not be doing mathematics, but rather, one would be engaging in a kind of [metamathematics](wikipedia://en/183508) (the [metalanguage](wikipedia://en/402929) of mathematics), usually [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636). Within mathematical logic, mathematics is usually described as a kind of [formal language](wikipedia://en/10939), and a well-formed expression can be [defined recursively](wikipedia://en/1047605) as follows:[^o10]

The [alphabet](wikipedia://en/4292269) consists of:

- A set of individual [constants](wikipedia://en/24758132): Symbols representing fixed [objects](wikipedia://en/19453961) in the [domain of discourse](wikipedia://en/229180), such as [numerals](wikipedia://en/21170) (1, 2.5, 1/7, ...), [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) ($\varnothing, \{1,2,3\}$, ...), [truth values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth_values) (T or F), etc.
- A set of individual variables: A [countably infinite](wikipedia://en/6026) amount of symbols representing [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109) used for representing an unspecified object in the domain. (Usually letters like $x$, or $y$)
- A set of operations: [Function symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_symbols) representing [operations](wikipedia://en/4140245) that can be performed on elements over the domain, like addition (+), multiplication (×), or set operations like union (∪), or intersection (∩). (Functions can be understood as [unary operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unary_operations))
- Brackets ( )

With this alphabet, the recursive rules for forming a well-formed expression (WFE) are as follows:

- Any constant or variable as defined are the [atomic expressions](wikipedia://en/4472066), the simplest well-formed expressions (WFE's). For instance, the constant $2$ or the variable $x$ are syntactically correct expressions.
- Let $F$ be a [metavariable](wikipedia://en/29557417) for any [n-ary operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-ary_operation) over the domain, and let $\phi_1, \phi_2, ... \phi_n$ be metavariables for any WFE's.

Then $F(\phi_1, \phi_2, ... \phi_n)$ is also well-formed. Here represented with [prefix notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefix_notation), but other notations may be used such as [Infix notation](wikipedia://en/199014) like $3+4$, or possibly non-linear notations such as with [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) or [summation notation](wikipedia://en/246160#Notation) if allowed.

For instance, if the domain of discourse is the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), $F$ can denote the [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) +, then $\phi_1 + \phi_2$ is well-formed. Or $F$ can be the unary operation $\surd$ so $\sqrt{\phi_1}$ is well-formed.

Brackets are initially around each non-atomic expression, but they can be deleted in cases where there is a defined [order of operations](wikipedia://en/212980), or where order doesn't matter (i.e. where operations are [associative](wikipedia://en/1335)).

A well-formed expression can be thought as a [syntax tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_syntax_tree).[^o24] The [leaf nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Node_%28computer_science%29) are always atomic expressions. Operations $+$ and $\cup$ have exactly two child nodes, while operations $\sqrt{x}$, $\text{ln}(x)$ and $\frac{d}{dx}$ have exactly one. There are countably infinitely many WFE's, however, each WFE has a finite number of nodes.

:::

## Computer science {#Computer_science}

In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), an *expression* is a [syntactic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntax_%28programming_languages%29) entity in a [programming language](wikipedia://en/23015) that may be evaluated to determine its [value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_%28computer_science%29)[^o26] or fail to terminate, in which case the expression is undefined.[^o27] It is a combination of one or more [constants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constant_%28programming%29), [variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_%28programming%29), [functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_%28programming%29), and [operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_%28programming%29) that the programming language interprets (according to its particular [rules of precedence](wikipedia://en/212980) and of [association](wikipedia://en/1335)) and computes to produce ("to return", in a [stateful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_%28computer_science%29) environment) another value. This process, for mathematical expressions, is called *evaluation*. In simple settings, the [resulting value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_type) is usually one of various [primitive types](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_data_type), such as [string](wikipedia://en/27701), [Boolean](wikipedia://en/3126968), or numerical (such as [integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_%28computer_science%29), [floating-point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating-point_number), or [complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_data_type)).

In [computer algebra](wikipedia://en/23659805), formulas are viewed as expressions that can be evaluated as a Boolean, depending on the values that are given to the variables occurring in the expressions. For example $8x-5 \geq 3$ takes the value *false* if $x$ is given a value less than 1, and the value *true* otherwise.

Expressions are often contrasted with [statements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statement_%28computer_science%29)—syntactic entities that have no value (an instruction).

*Image: Cassidy.1985.015.gif.* Representation of the expression $(8 - 6) \times (3 + 1)$ as a [Lisp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisp_%28programming_language%29) tree, from a 1985 Master's Thesis[^o28]

Except for [numbers](wikipedia://en/21690) and [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109), every mathematical expression may be viewed as the symbol of an operator followed by a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of operands. In computer algebra software, the expressions are usually represented in this way. This representation is very flexible, and many things that seem not to be mathematical expressions at first glance, may be represented and manipulated as such. For example, an equation is an expression with "=" as an operator, a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) may be represented as an expression with "matrix" as an operator and its rows as operands.

See: [Computer algebra expression](wikipedia://en/23659805#Expressions)

### Computation {#Computation}

A [computation](wikipedia://en/5926) is any type of [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118) or non-arithmetic [calculation](wikipedia://en/239121) that is "well-defined".[^o31] The notion that mathematical statements should be 'well-defined' had been argued by mathematicians since at least the [1600s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1600s_%28decade%29),[^o32] but agreement on a suitable definition proved elusive.[^o33] A candidate definition was proposed independently by several mathematicians in the 1930s.[^o34] The best-known variant was formalised by the mathematician [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208), who defined a well-defined statement or calculation as any statement that could be expressed in terms of the initialisation parameters of a [Turing machine](wikipedia://en/30403).[^o35] Turing's definition apportioned "well-definedness" to a very large class of mathematical statements, including all well-formed [algebraic statements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations), and all statements written in modern computer programming languages.[^o36]

Despite the widespread uptake of this definition, there are some mathematical concepts that have no well-defined characterisation under this definition. This includes [the halting problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_halting_problem) and [the busy beaver game](wikipedia://en/67911). It remains an open question as to whether there exists a more powerful definition of 'well-defined' that is able to capture both computable and 'non-computable' statements.[^o37] All statements characterised in modern programming languages are well-defined, including [C++](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++), [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29), and [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_%28programming_language%29).[^o36]

Common examples of computation are basic [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118) and the [execution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Execution_%28computing%29) of computer [algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithms). A [calculation](wikipedia://en/239121) is a deliberate mathematical process that transforms one or more inputs into one or more outputs or *results*. For example, [multiplying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplying) 7 by 6 is a simple algorithmic calculation. Extracting the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) or the [cube root](wikipedia://en/292506) of a number using mathematical models is a more complex algorithmic calculation.

#### Rewriting {#Rewriting}

Expressions can be computed by means of an *[evaluation strategy](wikipedia://en/2977119).*[^o39] To illustrate, executing a function call `f(a,b)` may first evaluate the arguments `a` and `b`, store the results in [references](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_%28computer_science%29) or memory locations `ref_a` and `ref_b`, then evaluate the function's body with those references passed in. This gives the function the ability to look up the original argument values passed in through dereferencing the parameters (some languages use specific operators to perform this), to modify them via [assignment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assignment_%28computer_science%29) as if they were local variables, and to return values via the references. This is the call-by-reference evaluation strategy.[^o40] Evaluation strategy is part of the semantics of the programming language definition. Some languages, such as [PureScript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PureScript), have variants with different evaluation strategies. Some [declarative languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declarative_language), such as [Datalog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Datalog), support multiple evaluation strategies. Some languages define a [calling convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calling_convention).

In [rewriting](wikipedia://en/415847), a [reduction strategy](wikipedia://en/40712456) or rewriting strategy is a relation specifying a rewrite for each object or term, compatible with a given reduction relation. A rewriting strategy specifies, out of all the reducible subterms ([redexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redex)), which one should be reduced (*contracted*) within a term. One of the most common systems involves [lambda calculus](wikipedia://en/18203).

:::

### Polynomial evaluation {#Polynomial_evaluation}

A polynomial consists of variables and [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203), that involve only the operations of [addition](wikipedia://en/61338), [subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141), [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) and [exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491) to [nonnegative integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonnegative_integer) powers, and has a finite number of terms. The problem of [polynomial evaluation](wikipedia://en/64691694) arises frequently in practice. In [computational geometry](wikipedia://en/176927), polynomials are used to compute function approximations using [Taylor polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_polynomials). In [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432) and [hash tables](wikipedia://en/13833), polynomials are used to compute [*k*-independent hashing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-independent_hashing).

In the former case, polynomials are evaluated using [floating-point arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11376), which is not exact. Thus different schemes for the evaluation will, in general, give slightly different answers. In the latter case, the polynomials are usually evaluated in a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615), in which case the answers are always exact.

For evaluating the [univariate polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Univariate_polynomial) $a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots +a_0,$ the most naive method would use $n$ multiplications to compute $a_nx^n$, use $n-1$ multiplications to compute $a_{n-1} x^{n-1}$ and so on for a total of $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ multiplications and $n$ additions. Using better methods, such as [Horner's rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horner's_rule), this can be reduced to $n$ multiplications and $n$ additions. If some preprocessing is allowed, even more savings are possible

## Types of expressions {#Types_of_expressions}

### Algebraic expression {#Algebraic_expression}

An *[algebraic expression](wikipedia://en/4283745)* is an expression built up from [algebraic constants](wikipedia://en/1158), [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109), and the [algebraic operations](wikipedia://en/5438924) ([addition](wikipedia://en/61338), [subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141), [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845), [division](wikipedia://en/53696) and [exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491) by a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024)).[^o44] For example, $3x^{2} - 2xy + c$ is an algebraic expression. Since taking the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) is the same as raising to the power $\tfrac{1}{2}$, the following is also an algebraic expression:

$$\sqrt{\frac{1-x^2}{1+x^2}}$$

See also: [Algebraic equation](wikipedia://en/404001) and [Algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129)

### Polynomial expression {#Polynomial_expression}

A [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) is an expression built with [scalars](wikipedia://en/3588331) (numbers of elements of some field), [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109), and the operators of addition, multiplication, and exponentiation to nonnegative integer powers; for example $3(x+1)^2 - xy.$

Using [associativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity), [commutativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity) and [distributivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributivity), every polynomial expression is equivalent to a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000), that is an expression that is a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of products of integer powers of the indeterminates. For example the above polynomial expression is equivalent (denote the same polynomial as $3x^2-xy+6x+3.$

Many author do not distinguish polynomials and polynomial expressions. In this case the expression of a polynomial expression as a linear combination is called the *canonical form*, *normal form*, or *expanded form* of the polynomial.

### Formal expression {#Formal_expression}

A **formal expression** is a kind of [string](wikipedia://en/27701) of [symbols](wikipedia://en/24028642), created by the same [production rules](wikipedia://en/609125#Formal_definition) as standard expressions, however, they are used without regard to the meaning of the expression. In this way, two *formal expressions* are considered equal only if they are [syntactically](wikipedia://en/546039) equal, that is, if they are the exact same expression.[^o47][^o48] For instance, the formal expressions "2" and "1+1" are not equal.

### Lambda calculus {#Lambda_calculus}

Formal languages allow [formalizing](wikipedia://en/396102) the concept of well-formed expressions.

In the 1930s, a new type of expression, the [lambda expression](wikipedia://en/18203#Definition), was introduced by [Alonzo Church](wikipedia://en/49731) and [Stephen Kleene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Kleene) for formalizing [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) and their evaluation.[^o50] The lambda operators (lambda abstraction and function application) form the basis for lambda calculus, a formal system used in [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636) and [programming language theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programming_language_theory).

The equivalence of two lambda expressions is [undecidable](wikipedia://en/8336) (but see [unification (computer science)](wikipedia://en/54432)). This is also the case for the expressions representing real numbers, which are built from the integers by using the arithmetical operations, the logarithm and the exponential ([Richardson's theorem](wikipedia://en/13463844)).

## History {#History}

History of mathematics

### Early written mathematics {#Early_written_mathematics}

The earliest written mathematics likely began with [tally marks](wikipedia://en/47962742), where each mark represented one unit, carved into wood or stone. An example of early [counting](wikipedia://en/275768) is the [Ishango bone](wikipedia://en/5508956), found near the [Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile) and dating back over [20,000 years ago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Paleolithic), which is thought to show a six-month [lunar calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_calendar).[^o53] [Ancient Egypt](wikipedia://en/1180105) developed a symbolic system using [hieroglyphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hieroglyphics), assigning symbols for powers of ten and using addition and subtraction symbols resembling legs in motion.[^o54][^o55] This system, recorded in texts like the [Rhind Mathematical Papyrus](wikipedia://en/9210114) (c. 2000–1800 BC), influenced other [Mediterranean cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Mediterranean_region). In [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia), a similar system evolved, with numbers written in a base-60 ([sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316)) format on [clay tablets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clay_tablets) written in [Cuneiform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuneiform), a technique originating with the [Sumerians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumerians) around 3000 BC. This base-60 system persists today in measuring time and [angles](wikipedia://en/1196).

### Syncopated stage {#Syncopated_stage}

The "syncopated" stage of mathematics introduced symbolic abbreviations for commonly used operations and quantities, marking a shift from purely [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) reasoning. [Ancient Greek mathematics](wikipedia://en/1503369), largely geometric in nature, drew on [Egyptian numerical systems](wikipedia://en/1149201) (especially [Attic numerals](wikipedia://en/1455703)),[^o57] with little interest in algebraic symbols, until the arrival of [Diophantus](wikipedia://en/9110) of [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Alexandria),[^o58] who pioneered a form of [syncopated algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syncopated_algebra) in his *[Arithmetica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetica),* which introduced symbolic manipulation of expressions.[^o59] His notation represented unknowns and powers symbolically, but without modern symbols for [relations](wikipedia://en/22517558) (such as [equality](wikipedia://en/90446) or [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489)) or [exponents](wikipedia://en/99491).[^o60] An unknown number was called $\zeta$.[^o61] The square of $\zeta$ was $\Delta^v$; the cube was $K^v$; the fourth power was $\Delta^v\Delta$; the fifth power was $\Delta K^v$; and $\pitchfork$ meant to subtract everything on the right from the left.[^o62] So for example, what would be written in modern notation as:

$$x^3 - 2x^2 + 10x -1,$$

Would be written in Diophantus's syncopated notation as:

$$\mathrm{K}^{\upsilon} \overline{\alpha} \; \zeta \overline{\iota} \;\, \pitchfork \;\, \Delta^{\upsilon} \overline{\beta} \; \mathrm{M} \overline{\alpha} \,\;$$

In the 7th century, [Brahmagupta](wikipedia://en/297206) used different colours to represent the unknowns in algebraic equations in the *[Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta)*. Greek and other ancient mathematical advances, were often trapped in cycles of bursts of creativity, followed by long periods of stagnation, but this began to change as knowledge spread in the [early modern period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_period).

### Symbolic stage and early arithmetic {#Symbolic_stage_and_early_arithmetic}

*Image: Johannes\_Widmann-Mercantile\_Arithmetic\_1489.jpg.* The 1489 use of the [plus and minus signs](wikipedia://en/245206) in print.

The transition to fully symbolic algebra began with [Ibn al-Banna' al-Marrakushi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Banna'_al-Marrakushi) (1256–1321) and [Abū al-Ḥasan ibn ʿAlī al-Qalaṣādī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abū_al-Ḥasan_ibn_ʿAlī_al-Qalaṣādī), (1412–1482) who introduced symbols for operations using [Arabic characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_script).[^o65] The [plus sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plus_sign) (+) appeared around 1351 with [Nicole Oresme](wikipedia://en/593659),[^o67] likely derived from the Latin *et* (meaning "and"), while the minus sign (−) was first used in 1489 by [Johannes Widmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Widmann).[^o68] [Luca Pacioli](wikipedia://en/18473) included these symbols in his works, though much was based on earlier contributions by [Piero della Francesca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_della_Francesca). The [radical symbol](wikipedia://en/24971503) (√) for [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) was introduced by [Christoph Rudolff](wikipedia://en/23511221) in the 1500s, and [parentheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parentheses) for [precedence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precedence_%28mathematics%29) by [Niccolò Tartaglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niccolò_Tartaglia) in 1556. [François Viète](wikipedia://en/310238)’s *New Algebra* (1591) formalized modern symbolic manipulation. The [multiplication sign](wikipedia://en/249033) (×) was first used by [William Oughtred](wikipedia://en/475249) and the [division sign](wikipedia://en/276918) (÷) by [Johann Rahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Rahn).

[René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) further advanced algebraic symbolism in *[La Géométrie](wikipedia://en/239981)* (1637), where he introduced the use of letters at the end of the alphabet (x, y, z) for [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109), along with the [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706), which bridged algebra and geometry.[^o69] [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) and [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) independently developed [calculus](wikipedia://en/171882) in the late 17th century, with [Leibniz's notation](wikipedia://en/410712) becoming the standard.
