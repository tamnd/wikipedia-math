# Kähler differential

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kähler differential"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Kähler differentials** provide an adaptation of [differential forms](wikipedia://en/221519) to arbitrary [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346) or [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754). The notion was introduced by [Erich Kähler](wikipedia://en/383501) in the 1930s. It was adopted as standard in [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990) and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) somewhat later, once the need was felt to adapt methods from [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) and geometry over the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) to contexts where such methods are not available.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $R$ and $S$ be commutative rings and $\varphi : R \to S$ be a [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411). An important example is for $R$ a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) and $S$ a unital [algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) over $R$ (such as the [coordinate ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_ring) of an [affine variety](wikipedia://en/320468)). Kähler differentials formalize the observation that the derivatives of polynomials are again polynomial. In this sense, differentiation is a notion which can be expressed in purely algebraic terms. This observation can be turned into a definition of the module

$$\Omega_{S/R}$$

of differentials in different, but equivalent ways.

### Definition using derivations {#Definition_using_derivations}

An $R$-linear *[derivation](wikipedia://en/897658)* on $S$ is an $R$-[module homomorphism](wikipedia://en/474118) $d : S \to M$ to an $S$-module $M$ satisfying the [Leibniz rule](wikipedia://en/248988) $d(fg) = f\,dg + g\,df$ (it automatically follows from this definition that the image of $R$ is in the kernel of $d$ [^o5]). The [module](wikipedia://en/276410) of Kähler differentials is defined as the $S$-module $\Omega_{S/R}$ for which there is a universal derivation $d : S \to \Omega_{S/R}$. As with other [universal properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_properties), this means that $d$ is the *best possible* derivation in the sense that any other derivation may be obtained from it by composition with an $S$-module homomorphism. In other words, the [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) with $d$ provides, for every $S-\operatorname{module}$ $M$, an $S$-module isomorphism

$$\operatorname{Hom}_S(\Omega_{S/R},M) \xrightarrow{\cong} \operatorname{Der}_R(S,M).$$

One construction of $\Omega_{S/R}$ and $d$ proceeds by constructing a free $S$-module with one formal generator $ds$ for each $s$ in $S$, and imposing the relations

- $$dr = 0,$$
- $$d(s + t) = ds + dt,$$
- $$d(st) = s dt + t ds,$$

for all $r$ in $R$ and all $s$ and $t$ in $S$. The universal derivation sends $s$ to $ds$. The relations imply that the universal derivation is a homomorphism of $R$-modules.

### Definition using the augmentation ideal {#Definition_using_the_augmentation_ideal}

Another construction proceeds by letting $I$ be the ideal in the [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) $S \otimes_R S$ defined as the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of the multiplication map

$$\begin{cases} S \otimes_R S\to S \\ \sum s_i \otimes t_i \mapsto \sum s_i\cdot t_i \end{cases}$$

Then the module of Kähler differentials of $S$ can be equivalently defined by[^o7]

$$\Omega_{S/R} = I/I^2,$$

and the universal derivation is the homomorphism $d$ defined by

$$ds = 1 \otimes s - s \otimes 1.$$

This construction is equivalent to the previous one because $I$ is the kernel of the projection

$$\begin{cases} S \otimes_R S\to S \otimes_R R \\ \sum s_i \otimes t_i \mapsto \sum s_i \cdot t_i \otimes 1 \end{cases}$$

Thus we have:

$$S \otimes_R S \equiv I \oplus S \otimes_R R.$$

Then $S \otimes_R S / S \otimes_R R$ may be identified with $I$ by the map induced by the complementary projection

$$\sum s_i \otimes t_i \mapsto \sum s_i \otimes t_i - \sum s_i\cdot t_i \otimes 1.$$

This identifies $I$ with the $S$-module generated by the formal generators $ds$ for $s$ in $S$, subject to $d$ being a homomorphism of $R$-modules which sends each element of $R$ to zero. Taking the quotient by $I^{2}$ precisely imposes the Leibniz rule.

:::

## Examples and basic facts {#Examples_and_basic_facts}

For any commutative ring $R$, the Kähler differentials of the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $S=R[t_1, \dots, t_n]$ are a free $S$-module of rank *n* generated by the differentials of the variables:

$$\Omega^1_{R[t_1, \dots, t_n]/R} = \bigoplus_{i=1}^n R[t_1, \dots t_n] \, dt_i.$$

Kähler differentials are compatible with [extension of scalars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_of_scalars), in the sense that for a second $R$-algebra $R'$ and $S' = S \otimes_R R'$, there is an isomorphism

$$\Omega_{S/R} \otimes_S S' \cong \Omega_{ S'/R'}.$$

As a particular case of this, Kähler differentials are compatible with [localizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring), meaning that if $W$ is a [multiplicative set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_set) in $S$, then there is an isomorphism

$$W^{-1}\Omega_{S/R} \cong \Omega_{W^{-1}S/R}.$$

Given two ring homomorphisms $R \to S \to T$, there is a [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) of $T$-modules

$$\Omega_{S/R} \otimes_S T \to \Omega_{T/R} \to \Omega_{T/S} \to 0.$$

If $T=S/I$ for some ideal $I$, the term $\Omega_{T/S}$ vanishes and the sequence can be continued at the left as follows:

$$I/I^2 \xrightarrow{[f] \mapsto df \otimes 1} \Omega_{S/R} \otimes_S T \to \Omega_{T/R} \to 0.$$

A generalization of these two short exact sequences is provided by the [cotangent complex](wikipedia://en/17346860).

The latter sequence and the above computation for the polynomial ring allows the computation of the Kähler differentials of finitely generated $R$-algebras $T=R[t_1, \ldots, t_n]/(f_1, \ldots, f_m)$. Briefly, these are generated by the differentials of the variables and have relations coming from the differentials of the equations. For example, for a single polynomial in a single variable,

$$\Omega_{(R[t]/(f)) / R} \cong (R[t]\,dt \otimes R[t]/(f)) / (df) \cong R[t]/(f, df/dt)\,dt.$$

## Kähler differentials for schemes {#Kähler_differentials_for_schemes}

Because Kähler differentials are compatible with localization, they may be constructed on a general scheme by performing either of the two definitions above on affine open subschemes and gluing. However, the second definition has a geometric interpretation that globalizes immediately. In this interpretation, $I$ represents the *ideal defining the [diagonal](wikipedia://en/349251)* in the [fiber product](wikipedia://en/56444110) of $\operatorname{Spec}(S)$ with itself over $\operatorname{Spec}(S) \to \operatorname{Spec}(R)$. This construction therefore has a more geometric flavor, in the sense that the notion of *first infinitesimal neighbourhood* of the diagonal is thereby captured, via functions vanishing [modulo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulo_%28jargon%29) functions vanishing at least to second order (see [cotangent space](wikipedia://en/6620#Alternative_definition) for related notions). Moreover, it extends to a general morphism of schemes $f : X \to Y$ by setting $\mathcal{I}$ to be the ideal of the diagonal in the fiber product $X \times_Y X$. The *[cotangent sheaf](wikipedia://en/44901757)* $\Omega_{X/Y} = \mathcal{I} / \mathcal{I}^2$, together with the derivation $d: \mathcal{O}_X \to \Omega_{X/Y}$ defined analogously to before, is universal among $f^{-1}\mathcal{O}_Y$-linear derivations of $\mathcal{O}_X$-modules. If $U$ is an open affine subscheme of $X$ whose image in $Y$ is contained in an open affine subscheme $V$, then the cotangent sheaf restricts to a sheaf on $U$ which is similarly universal. It is therefore the sheaf associated to the module of Kähler differentials for the rings underlying $U$ and $V$.

Similar to the commutative algebra case, there exist exact sequences associated to morphisms of schemes. Given morphisms $f:X\to Y$ and $g:Y\to Z$ of schemes there is an exact sequence of sheaves on $X$

$$f^*\Omega_{Y/Z} \to \Omega_{X/Z} \to \Omega_{X/Y} \to 0$$

Also, if $X \subset Y$ is a closed subscheme given by the ideal sheaf $\mathcal{I}$, then $\Omega_{X/Y}=0$ and there is an exact sequence of sheaves on $X$

$$\mathcal{I}/\mathcal{I}^2 \to \Omega_{Y/Z}|_X \to \Omega_{X/Z} \to 0$$

### Examples {#Examples}

#### Finite separable field extensions {#Finite_separable_field_extensions}

If $K/k$ is a [finite field extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_field_extension), then $\Omega^1_{K/k}=0$ if and only if $K/k$ is [separable](wikipedia://en/363340). Consequently, if $K/k$ is a finite separable field extension and $\pi:Y \to \operatorname{Spec}(K)$ is a [smooth variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_variety) (or scheme), then the relative cotangent sequence

$$\pi^*\Omega^1_{K/k} \to \Omega^1_{Y/k} \to \Omega^1_{Y/K} \to 0$$

proves $\Omega^1_{Y/k} \cong \Omega^1_{Y/K}$.

#### Cotangent modules of a projective variety {#Cotangent_modules_of_a_projective_variety}

Given a [projective scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_scheme) $X\in \operatorname{Sch}/\mathbb{k}$, its cotangent sheaf can be computed from the sheafification of the cotangent module on the underlying [graded algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded_algebra). For example, consider the complex curve

$$\operatorname{Proj}\left(\frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]}{(x^n + y^n - z^n)} \right)=\operatorname{Proj}(R)$$

then we can compute the cotangent module as

$$\Omega_{R/\mathbb{C}} = \frac{R\cdot dx \oplus R \cdot dy \oplus R \cdot dz}{nx^{n-1}dx + ny^{n-1}dy - nz^{n-1}dz}$$

Then,

$$\Omega_{X/\mathbb{C}} = \widetilde{\Omega_{R/\mathbb{C}}}$$

#### Morphisms of schemes {#Morphisms_of_schemes}

Consider the morphism

$$X = \operatorname{Spec}\left( \frac{\mathbb{C}[t,x,y]}{(xy-t)} \right)=\operatorname{Spec}(R) \to \operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[t]) = Y$$

in $\operatorname{Sch}/\mathbb{C}$. Then, using the first sequence we see that

$$\widetilde{R\cdot dt} \to \widetilde{\frac{R\cdot dt \oplus R \cdot dx \oplus R \cdot dy}{ydx + xdy - dt}} \to \Omega_{X/Y} \to 0$$

hence

$$\Omega_{X/Y} = \widetilde{\frac{R \cdot dx \oplus R \cdot dy}{ydx + xdy}}$$

:::

## Higher differential forms and algebraic de Rham cohomology {#Higher_differential_forms_and_algebraic_de_Rham_cohomology}

### de Rham complex {#de_Rham_complex}

As before, fix a map $X \to Y$. Differential forms of higher degree are defined as the [exterior powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_power) (over $\mathcal O_X$),

$$\Omega^n_{X/Y} := \bigwedge^n \Omega_{X/Y}.$$

The derivation $\mathcal O_X \to \Omega_{X/Y}$ extends in a natural way to a sequence of maps

$$0 \to \mathcal{O}_X \xrightarrow{d} \Omega^1_{X/Y} \xrightarrow{d} \Omega^2_{X/Y} \xrightarrow{d} \cdots$$

satisfying $d \circ d=0.$ This is a [cochain complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochain_complex) known as the *de Rham complex*.

The de Rham complex enjoys an additional multiplicative structure, the [wedge product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedge_product)

$$\Omega^n_{X/Y} \otimes \Omega^m_{X/Y} \to \Omega^{n+m}_{X/Y}.$$

This turns the de Rham complex into a commutative [differential graded algebra](wikipedia://en/2415863). It also has a [coalgebra](wikipedia://en/310886) structure inherited from the one on the [exterior algebra](wikipedia://en/221537).[^o17]

### de Rham cohomology {#de_Rham_cohomology}

The [hypercohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercohomology) of the de Rham complex of sheaves is called the *algebraic de Rham cohomology* of $X$ over $Y$ and is denoted by $H^n_\text{dR}(X / Y)$ or just $H^n_\text{dR}(X)$ if $Y$ is clear from the context. (In many situations, $Y$ is the spectrum of a field of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) zero.) Algebraic de Rham cohomology was introduced by (Grothendieck 1966a). It is closely related to [crystalline cohomology](wikipedia://en/10220067).

As is familiar from [coherent cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherent_cohomology) of other quasi-coherent sheaves, the computation of de Rham cohomology is simplified when $X = \operatorname{Spec} S$ and $Y = \operatorname{Spec} R$ are affine schemes. In this case, because affine schemes have no higher cohomology, $H^n_\text{dR}(X / Y)$ can be computed as the cohomology of the complex of abelian groups

$$0 \to S \xrightarrow{d} \Omega^1_{S/R} \xrightarrow{d} \Omega^2_{S/R} \xrightarrow{d} \cdots$$

which is, termwise, the global sections of the sheaves $\Omega^r_{X/Y}$.

To take a very particular example, suppose that $X=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Q} \left [x,x^{-1} \right ]$ is the multiplicative group over $\mathbb{Q}.$ Because this is an affine scheme, hypercohomology reduces to ordinary cohomology. The algebraic de Rham complex is

$$\mathbb{Q}[x, x^{-1}] \xrightarrow{d} \mathbb{Q}[x, x^{-1}]\,dx.$$

The differential $d$ obeys the usual rules of calculus, meaning $d(x^n) = nx^{n-1}\,dx.$ The kernel and [cokernel](wikipedia://en/279715) compute algebraic de Rham cohomology, so

$$\begin{aligned} H_\text{dR}^0(X) &= \mathbb{Q} \\ H_\text{dR}^1(X) &= \mathbb{Q} \cdot x^{-1} dx \end{aligned}$$

and all other algebraic de Rham cohomology groups are zero. By way of comparison, the algebraic de Rham cohomology groups of $Y=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{F}_p \left [x,x^{-1} \right ]$ are much larger, namely,

$$\begin{aligned} H_\text{dR}^0(Y) &= \bigoplus_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{F}_p \cdot x^{kp} \\ H_\text{dR}^1(Y) &= \bigoplus_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{F}_p \cdot x^{kp-1}\,dx \end{aligned}$$

Since the Betti numbers of these cohomology groups are not what is expected, [crystalline cohomology](wikipedia://en/10220067) was developed to remedy this issue; it defines a [Weil cohomology theory](wikipedia://en/2960213) over finite fields.

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Grothendieck's comparison theorem"}

### Grothendieck's comparison theorem {#Grothendieck's_comparison_theorem}

If $X$ is a smooth [complex algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/39219687), there is a natural comparison map of complexes of sheaves

$$\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/\mathbb{C}}(-) \to \Omega^{\bullet}_{X^\text{an}}((-)^\text{an})$$

between the algebraic de Rham complex and the [smooth](wikipedia://en/1408000) de Rham complex defined in terms of (complex-valued) differential forms on $X^\text{an}$, the [complex manifold](wikipedia://en/509742) associated to *X*. Here, $(-)^{\text{an}}$ denotes the complex analytification functor. This map is far from being an isomorphism. Nonetheless, (Grothendieck 1966a) showed that the comparison map induces an isomorphism

$$H^\ast_\text{dR}(X/\mathbb{C}) \cong H^\ast_\text{dR}(X^\text{an})$$

from algebraic to smooth [de Rham cohomology](wikipedia://en/221530) (and thus to singular cohomology $H^*_{\text{sing}}(X^{\text{an}}; \mathbb{C})$ by [de Rham's theorem](wikipedia://en/221530#De_Rham's_theorem)). In particular, if *X* is a smooth [affine algebraic variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_algebraic_variety) embedded in $\mathbb{C}^n$, then the inclusion of the subcomplex of algebraic differential forms into that of all smooth forms on *X* is a [quasi-isomorphism](wikipedia://en/3653039). For example, if

$$X = \{ (w,z) \in \mathbb{C}^2: w z =1 \},$$

then as shown above, the computation of algebraic de Rham cohomology gives explicit generators $\{ 1, z^{-1} dz \}$ for $H^0_{\text{dR}}(X/\mathbb{C})$ and $H^1_{\text{dR}}(X/ \mathbb{C})$, respectively, while all other cohomology groups vanish. Since *X* is [homotopy equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_equivalent) to a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), this is as predicted by Grothendieck's theorem.

Counter-examples in the singular case can be found with non-[Du Bois singularities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Du_Bois_singularities) such as the graded ring $k[x,y]/(y^2-x^3)$ with $y$ where $\deg(y)=3$ and $\deg(x)=2$.[^o23] Other counterexamples can be found in algebraic plane curves with isolated singularities whose [Milnor](wikipedia://en/18882483) and Tjurina numbers are non-equal.[^o24]

A proof of Grothendieck's theorem using the concept of a mixed [Weil cohomology theory](wikipedia://en/2960213) was given by (Cisinski Déglise 2013).

## Applications {#Applications}

### Canonical divisor {#Canonical_divisor}

If $X$ is a smooth variety over a field $k$, then $\Omega_{X/k}$ is a [vector bundle](wikipedia://en/276524) (i.e., a locally free $\mathcal O_X$-module) of rank equal to the [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) of $X$. This implies, in particular, that

$$\omega_{X/k} := \bigwedge^{\dim X} \Omega_{X/k}$$

is a [line bundle](wikipedia://en/361366) or, equivalently, a [divisor](wikipedia://en/1693055). It is referred to as the *[canonical divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_divisor)*. The canonical divisor is, as it turns out, a [dualizing complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dualizing_complex) and therefore appears in various important theorems in algebraic geometry such as [Serre duality](wikipedia://en/359175) or [Verdier duality](wikipedia://en/7190735).

### Classification of algebraic curves {#Classification_of_algebraic_curves}

The [geometric genus](wikipedia://en/3115807) of a smooth [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) $X$ of [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) $d$ over a field $k$ is defined as the dimension

$$g := \dim H^0(X, \Omega^d_{X/k}).$$

For curves, this purely algebraic definition agrees with the topological definition (for $k=\mathbb{C}$) as the "number of handles" of the [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181) associated to *X*. There is a rather sharp trichotomy of geometric and arithmetic properties depending on the genus of a curve, for $g$ being 0 ([rational curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_curve)), 1 ([elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225)), and greater than 1 (hyperbolic Riemann surfaces, including [hyperelliptic curves](wikipedia://en/499361)), respectively.

::: {#o31 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Tangent bundle and Riemann–Roch theorem"}

### Tangent bundle and Riemann–Roch theorem {#Tangent_bundle_and_Riemann–Roch_theorem}

The [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) of a smooth variety $X$ is, by definition, the dual of the cotangent sheaf $\Omega_{X/k}$. The [Riemann–Roch theorem](wikipedia://en/247261) and its far-reaching generalization, the [Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem](wikipedia://en/1532606), contain as a crucial ingredient the [Todd class](wikipedia://en/1568489) of the tangent bundle.

:::

### Unramified and smooth morphisms {#Unramified_and_smooth_morphisms}

The sheaf of differentials is related to various algebro-geometric notions. A morphism $f: X \to Y$ of schemes is [unramified](wikipedia://en/58653736) if and only if $\Omega_{X/Y}$ is zero.[^o33] A special case of this assertion is that for a field $k$, $K := k[t]/f$ is [separable](wikipedia://en/363340) over $k$ iff $\Omega_{K/k} = 0$, which can also be read off the above computation.

A morphism $f$ of finite type is a [smooth morphism](wikipedia://en/7837041) if it is [flat](wikipedia://en/542520) and if $\Omega_{X/Y}$ is a [locally free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_free_module) $\mathcal O_X$-module of appropriate rank. The computation of $\Omega_{R[t_1, \ldots, t_n]/R}$ above shows that the projection from [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) $\mathbb A^n_R \to \operatorname{Spec}(R)$ is smooth.

### Periods {#Periods}

*[Periods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_periods)* are, broadly speaking, integrals of certain arithmetically defined differential forms.[^o35] The simplest example of a period is $2 \pi i$, which arises as

$$\int_{S^1} \frac {dz} z = 2 \pi i.$$

Algebraic de Rham cohomology is used to construct periods as follows:[^o36] For an algebraic variety $X$ defined over $\mathbb{Q},$ the above-mentioned compatibility with base-change yields a natural isomorphism

$$H^n_\text{dR}(X / \mathbb{Q}) \otimes_{\mathbb{Q}} \mathbb{C} = H^n_\text{dR}(X \otimes_{\mathbb{Q}} \mathbb{C} / \mathbb{C}).$$

On the other hand, the right hand cohomology group is isomorphic to de Rham cohomology of the [complex manifold](wikipedia://en/509742) $X^\text{an}$ associated to $X$, denoted here $H^n_\text{dR}(X^\text{an}).$ Yet another classical result, [de Rham's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Rham's_theorem), asserts an isomorphism of the latter cohomology group with [singular cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_cohomology) (or [sheaf cohomology](wikipedia://en/1055357)) with complex coefficients, $H^n(X^\text{an}, \mathbb{C})$, which by the [universal coefficient theorem](wikipedia://en/996107) is in its turn isomorphic to $H^n(X^\text{an}, \mathbb{Q}) \otimes_{\mathbb{Q}} \mathbb{C}.$ Composing these isomorphisms yields two *rational* vector spaces which, after tensoring with $\mathbb{C}$ become isomorphic. Choosing bases of these rational subspaces (also called lattices), the determinant of the base-change matrix is a complex number, well defined up to multiplication by a rational number. Such numbers are *[periods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periods_%28ring%29)*.

### Algebraic number theory {#Algebraic_number_theory}

In [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705), Kähler differentials may be used to study the [ramification](wikipedia://en/384644#In_algebraic_number_theory) in an extension of [algebraic number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_number_fields). If $L / K$ is a finite extension with rings of integers $R$ and $S$ respectively then the [different ideal](wikipedia://en/2371783) $\delta_{L / K}$, which encodes the ramification data, is the annihilator of the $R$-module $\Omega_{R/S}$:[^o38]

$$\delta_{L/K} = \{ x \in R : x \,dy = 0 \text{ for all } y \in R \}.$$

:::

## Related notions {#Related_notions}

[Hochschild homology](wikipedia://en/10499629) is a homology theory for associative rings that turns out to be closely related to Kähler differentials. This is because of the Hochschild-Kostant-Rosenberg theorem which states that the Hochschild homology $HH_\bullet(R)$ of an algebra of a smooth variety is isomorphic to the de-Rham complex $\Omega^\bullet_{R/k}$ for $k$ a field of characteristic $0$. A [derived](wikipedia://en/40778281) enhancement of this theorem states that the Hochschild homology of a differential graded algebra is isomorphic to the derived de-Rham complex.

The [de Rham–Witt complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Rham–Witt_complex) is, in very rough terms, an enhancement of the de Rham complex for the ring of [Witt vectors](wikipedia://en/5573710).
