# H-space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="H-space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **H-space**[^o2] is a [homotopy-theoretic](wikipedia://en/61994831) version of a generalization of the notion of [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315), in which the axioms on [associativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity) and [inverses](wikipedia://en/45199) are removed.

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

An H-space consists of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$, together with an element $e$ of $X$ and a [continuous map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) $\mu : X \times X \to X$, such that $\mu(e, e) = e$ and the maps $x \mapsto \mu(x, e)$ and $x \mapsto \mu(e, x)$ are both [homotopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopic) to the [identity map](wikipedia://en/15069) through maps sending $e$ to $e$.[^o5] This may be thought of as a [pointed topological space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointed_topological_space) together with a continuous multiplication for which the basepoint is an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) up to basepoint-preserving homotopy.

One says that a topological space $X$ is an H-space if there exists $e$ and $\mu$ such that the triple $(X, e, \mu)$ is an H-space as in the above definition.[^o6] Alternatively, an H-space may be defined without requiring homotopies to fix the basepoint $e$, or by requiring $e$ to be an exact identity, without any consideration of homotopy.[^o7] In the case of a [CW complex](wikipedia://en/353022), all three of these definitions are in fact equivalent.[^o8]

:::

## Examples and properties {#Examples_and_properties}

The standard definition of the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004), together with the fact that it is a group, can be rephrased as saying that the [loop space](wikipedia://en/2174332) of a [pointed topological space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointed_topological_space) has the structure of an H-group, as equipped with the standard operations of concatenation and inversion.[^o10] Furthermore, a continuous basepoint-preserving map of pointed topological spaces induces an H-homomorphism of the corresponding loop spaces; this reflects the [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) on fundamental groups induced by a continuous map.[^o11]

It is straightforward to verify that, given a pointed [homotopy equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_equivalence) from an H-space to a pointed topological space, there is a natural H-space structure on the latter space.[^o12] As such, the existence of an H-space structure on a given space is only dependent on its pointed homotopy type.

The multiplicative structure of an H-space adds structure to its [homology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_group) and [cohomology groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_group). For example, the [cohomology ring](wikipedia://en/1069723) of a [path-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path-connected) H-space with finitely generated and free cohomology groups is a [Hopf algebra](wikipedia://en/325714).[^o13] Also, one can define the [Pontryagin product](wikipedia://en/34640104) on the homology groups of an H-space.[^o14]

The [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of an H-space is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974). To see this, let *X* be an H-space with identity *e* and let *f* and *g* be [loops](wikipedia://en/1189560) at *e*. Define a map *F*: \[0,1\] × \[0,1\] → *X* by *F*(*a*,*b*) = *f*(*a*)*g*(*b*). Then *F*(*a*,0) = *F*(*a*,1) = *f*(*a*)*e* is homotopic to *f*, and *F*(0,*b*) = *F*(1,*b*) = *eg*(*b*) is homotopic to *g*. It is clear how to define a homotopy from \[*f*\]\[*g*\] to \[*g*\]\[*f*\].

Adams' [Hopf invariant one](wikipedia://en/4417198) theorem, named after [Frank Adams](wikipedia://en/1223959), states that *S*<sup>0</sup>, *S*<sup>1</sup>, *S*<sup>3</sup>, and *S*<sup>7</sup> are the only [spheres](wikipedia://en/39782) that are H-spaces. Each of these spaces forms an H-space by viewing it as the subset of norm-one elements of the [reals](wikipedia://en/20646438), [complexes](wikipedia://en/5826), [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440), and [octonions](wikipedia://en/51436), respectively, and using the multiplication operations from these algebras. In fact, *S*<sup>0</sup>, *S*<sup>1</sup>, and *S*<sup>3</sup> are groups ([Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945)) with these multiplications. But *S*<sup>7</sup> is not a group in this way because octonion multiplication is not associative, nor can it be given any other continuous multiplication for which it is a group.
