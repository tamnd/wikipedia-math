# Sunrise problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sunrise problem"}

*Image: Buck Creek IN - sunrise.jpg.* Usually inferred from repeated observations: *"The sun always rises in the east"*.

The **sunrise problem** can be expressed as follows: "What is the probability that the sun will rise tomorrow?" The sunrise problem illustrates the difficulty of using [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) when evaluating the plausibility of statements or beliefs.

:::

According to the [Bayesian](wikipedia://en/4890) [interpretation of probability](wikipedia://en/23538), probability theory can be used to evaluate the plausibility of the statement, "The sun will rise tomorrow."

The sunrise problem was first introduced publicly in 1763 by [Richard Price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Price) in his famous coverage of [Thomas Bayes](wikipedia://en/149117)' foundational work in [Bayesianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesianism).[^o2]

## Laplace's approach {#Laplace's_approach}

[Pierre-Simon Laplace](wikipedia://en/344783) approached the problem by means of his [rule of succession](wikipedia://en/798571).[^o4][^o5] Let *p* be the long-run frequency of sunrises, i.e., the sun rises on 100 × *p*% of days. *Prior* to knowing of any sunrises, one is completely ignorant of the value of *p*. Laplace represented this prior ignorance by means of a [uniform probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29) on *p*.

For instance, the probability that *p* is between 20% and 50% is just 30%. This must not be interpreted to mean that in 30% of all cases, *p* is between 20% and 50%. Rather, it means that one's state of knowledge (or ignorance) justifies one in being 30% sure that the sun rises between 20% of the time and 50% of the time. *Given* the value of *p*, and no other information relevant to the question of whether the sun will rise tomorrow, the probability that the sun will rise tomorrow is *p*. But we are *not* "given the value of *p*". What we are given is the observed data: the sun has risen every day on record. Laplace inferred the number of days by saying that the universe was created about 6000 years ago, based on a [young-earth creationist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Earth_Creationism) reading of the [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible).

To find the [conditional probability](wikipedia://en/24104134) distribution of *p* given the data, one uses [Bayes' theorem](wikipedia://en/49569), which some call the *Bayes–Laplace rule*. Having found the conditional probability distribution of *p* given the data, one may then calculate the conditional probability, given the data, that the sun will rise tomorrow. That conditional probability is given by the [rule of succession](wikipedia://en/798571). The plausibility that the sun will rise tomorrow increases with the number of days on which the sun has risen so far. Specifically, assuming *p* has an a-priori distribution that is uniform over the interval \[0,1\], and that, given the value of *p*, the sun independently rises each day with probability *p*, the desired conditional probability is:

$$\Pr(\text{Sun rises tomorrow} \mid \text{It has risen } k \text{ times previously}) = \frac{\int_0^1 p^{k+1}\,dp}{\int_0^1 p^k \,dp}= \frac{k+1}{k+2}.$$

By this formula, if one has observed the sun rising 10000 times previously, the probability it rises the next day is $10001/10002 \approx 0.99990002$. Expressed as a percentage, this is approximately a $99.990002 \%$ chance.

However, Laplace recognized this to be a misapplication of the rule of succession through not taking into account all the prior information available immediately after deriving the result:

But this number \[the probability of the sun coming up tomorrow\] is far greater for him who, seeing in the totality of phenomena the principle regulating the days and seasons, realizes that nothing at present moment can arrest the course of it.

[E.T. Jaynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Thompson_Jaynes) noted that Laplace's warning had gone unheeded by workers in the field.[^o6]

A [reference class problem](wikipedia://en/1791332) arises: the plausibility inferred will depend on whether we take the past experience of one person, of humanity, or of the earth. A consequence is that each referent would hold different plausibility of the statement. In Bayesianism, any probability is a [conditional probability](wikipedia://en/24104134) given what one knows. That varies from one person to another.
