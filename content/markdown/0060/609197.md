# Studentized residual

Studentization

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Studentized residual"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), a **studentized residual** is the [dimensionless ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless_ratio) resulting from the division of a [residual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errors_and_residuals_in_statistics) by an [estimate](wikipedia://en/10043) of its [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590), both expressed in the same [units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_measurement). It is a form of a [Student's *t*-statistic](wikipedia://en/22474343), with the estimate of error varying between points.

:::

This is an important technique in the detection of [outliers](wikipedia://en/160951). It is among several named in honor of [William Sealey Gosset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Sealey_Gosset), who wrote under the pseudonym "Student" (e.g., [Student's distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student's_distribution)). Dividing a statistic by a [sample standard deviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_standard_deviation) is called ***studentizing***, in analogy with *[standardizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standardizing)* and *[normalizing](wikipedia://en/2978513)*.

## Motivation {#Motivation}

The key reason for studentizing is that, in [regression analysis](wikipedia://en/826997) of a [multivariate distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_distribution), the variances of the *residuals* at different input variable values may differ, even if the variances of the *errors* at these different input variable values are equal. The issue is the difference between [errors and residuals in statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errors_and_residuals_in_statistics), particularly the behavior of residuals in regressions.

Consider the [simple linear regression](wikipedia://en/2593771) (SLR) model

$$Y = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 X + \varepsilon. \,$$

Given a random sample (*X*<sub>*i*</sub>, *Y*<sub>*i*</sub>), *i* = 1, ..., *n*, each pair (*X*<sub>*i*</sub>, *Y*<sub>*i*</sub>) satisfies

$$Y_i = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 X_i + \varepsilon_i,\,$$

where the *errors* $\varepsilon_i$, are [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence) and all have the same variance $\sigma^2$. The *residuals* are not the true errors, but *estimates*, based on the observable data. When the [method of least squares](wikipedia://en/82359) is used to estimate $\alpha_0$ and $\alpha_1$, then the residuals $\widehat{\varepsilon\,}$, unlike the errors $\varepsilon$, cannot be independent since they satisfy the two constraints

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \widehat{\varepsilon\,}_i=0$$

and

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \widehat{\varepsilon\,}_i x_i=0.$$

(Here *ε*<sub>*i*</sub> is the *i*th error, and $\widehat{\varepsilon\,}_i$ is the *i*th residual.)

The residuals, unlike the errors, *do not all have the same variance:* the variance decreases as the corresponding *x*-value gets farther from the average *x*-value. This is not a feature of the data itself, but of the regression better fitting values at the ends of the domain. It is also reflected in the [influence functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Influence_function_%28statistics%29) of various data points on the [regression coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regression_coefficient): endpoints have more influence. This can also be seen because the residuals at endpoints depend greatly on the slope of a fitted line, while the residuals at the middle are relatively insensitive to the slope. The fact that *the variances of the residuals differ,* even though *the variances of the true errors are all equal* to each other, is the *principal reason* for the need for studentization.

It is not simply a matter of the population parameters (mean and standard deviation) being unknown – it is that *regressions* yield *different residual distributions* at *different data points,* unlike *point [estimators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estimators)* of [univariate distributions](wikipedia://en/9253219), which share a *common distribution* for residuals.

## Variance of a residual {#Variance_of_a_residual}

The [hat matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hat_matrix) *H* is the matrix of the [orthogonal projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_projection) onto the column space of the [design matrix](wikipedia://en/949189) *X*:

$$H=X(X^T X)^{-1}X^T.\,$$

The [leverage](wikipedia://en/20742677) *h*<sub>*ii*</sub> is the *i*th diagonal entry in the hat matrix.

The variance of the *i*th residual is

$$\operatorname{var}(\widehat{\varepsilon\,}_i)=\sigma^2(1-h_{ii}).$$

In simple linear regression, the design matrix *X* has only two columns:

$$X=\left[\begin{matrix}1 & x_1 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_n \end{matrix}\right]$$

In this simple case, the variance of a residual is:

$$\operatorname{var}(\widehat{\varepsilon\,}_i)=\sigma^2\left( 1 - \frac1n -\frac{(x_i-\bar x)^2}{\sum_{j=1}^n (x_j - \bar x)^2 } \right).$$

In the case of an [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612), the design matrix *X* has only one column (a [vector of ones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_of_ones)), and this is simply:

$$\operatorname{var}(\widehat{\varepsilon\,}_i)=\sigma^2\left( 1 - \frac1n \right)=\sigma^2\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right).$$

The last fraction is related to [Bessel's correction](wikipedia://en/13384414).

## Calculation {#Calculation}

Given the definitions above, the **Studentized residual** is then

$$t_i = {\widehat{\varepsilon\,}_i\over \widehat{\sigma} \sqrt{1-h_{ii}\ }}$$

where *h*<sub>*ii*</sub> is the [leverage](wikipedia://en/20742677), and $\widehat{\sigma}$ is an appropriate estimate of *σ* (see below).

In the case of a mean, this is equal to:

$$t_i = {\widehat{\varepsilon\,}_i\over \widehat{\sigma} \sqrt{(n-1)/n}}$$

## Internal and external studentization {#Internal_and_external_studentization}

The usual estimate of *σ*<sup>2</sup> is the *internally studentized* residual

$$\widehat{\sigma}^2={1 \over n-m}\sum_{j=1}^n \widehat{\varepsilon\,}_j^{\,2}.$$

where *m* is the number of parameters in the model (2 in our example).

But if the *i* th case is suspected of being improbably large, then it would also not be normally distributed. Hence it is prudent to exclude the *i* th observation from the process of estimating the variance when one is considering whether the *i* th case may be an outlier, and instead use the *externally studentized* residual, which is

$$\widehat{\sigma}_{(i)}^2={1 \over n-m-1}\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}j = 1\\j \ne i\end{smallmatrix}}^n \widehat{\varepsilon\,}_j^{\,2},$$

based on all the residuals *except* the suspect *i* th residual. Here is to emphasize that $\widehat{\varepsilon\,}_j^{\,2} (j \ne i)$ for suspect *i* are computed with *i* th case excluded.

If the estimate *σ*<sup>2</sup> *includes* the *i* th case, then it is called the ***internally studentized*** residual, $t_i$ (also known as the *standardized residual* [^o7]). If the estimate $\widehat{\sigma}_{(i)}^2$ is used instead, *excluding* the *i* th case, then it is called the ***externally studentized***, $t_{i(i)}$.

:::

## Distribution {#Distribution}

Tau distribution If the errors are independent and [normally distributed](wikipedia://en/21462) with [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) 0 and variance *σ*<sup>2</sup>, then the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of the *i*th externally studentized residual $t_{i(i)}$ is a [Student's t-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375) with *n* − *m* − 1 [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321606), and can range from $\scriptstyle-\infty$ to $\scriptstyle+\infty$.

On the other hand, the internally studentized residuals are in the range $0 \,\pm\, \sqrt{\nu}$, where *ν* = *n* − *m* is the number of residual degrees of freedom. If *t*<sub>*i*</sub> represents the internally studentized residual, and again assuming that the errors are independent identically distributed Gaussian variables, then:[^o10]

$$t_i \sim \sqrt{\nu} {t \over \sqrt{t^2+\nu-1}}$$

where *t* is a random variable distributed as [Student's t-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375) with *ν* − 1 degrees of freedom. In fact, this implies that *t*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>2</sup> /*ν* follows the [beta distribution](wikipedia://en/207074) *B*(1/2,(*ν* − 1)/2). The distribution above is sometimes referred to as the **tau distribution**;[^o10] it was first derived by Thompson in 1935.[^o11]

:::

## Discussion {#Discussion}

When *ν* = 3, the internally studentized residuals are [uniformly distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29) between $\scriptstyle-\sqrt{3}$ and $\scriptstyle+\sqrt{3}$. If there is only one residual degree of freedom, the above formula for the distribution of internally studentized residuals doesn't apply. In this case, the *t*<sub>*i*</sub> are all either +1 or −1, with 50% chance for each.

The standard deviation of the distribution of internally studentized residuals is always 1, but this does not imply that the standard deviation of all the *t*<sub>*i*</sub> of a particular experiment is 1. For instance, the internally studentized residuals when fitting a straight line going through (0, 0) to the points (1, 4), (2, −1), (2, −1) are $\sqrt{2},\ -\sqrt{5}/5,\ -\sqrt{5}/5$, and the standard deviation of these is not 1.

Note that any pair of studentized residual *t*<sub>*i*</sub> and *t*<sub>*j*</sub> (where $i \neq j$), are NOT i.i.d. They have the same distribution, but are not independent due to constraints on the residuals having to sum to 0 and to have them be orthogonal to the design matrix.
