# Affine connection

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Affine connection"}

*Image: Parallel transport sphere.svg.* An affine connection on the sphere rolls the affine tangent plane from one point to another. As it does so, the point of contact traces out a curve in the plane: the [development](wikipedia://en/10016722).

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), an **affine connection** is a geometric object on a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) which *connects* nearby [tangent spaces](wikipedia://en/30807), so it permits [tangent vector fields](wikipedia://en/62641) to be [differentiated](wikipedia://en/7921) as if they were functions on the manifold with values in a fixed [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370). Connections are among the simplest methods of defining differentiation of the [sections](wikipedia://en/367621) of [vector bundles](wikipedia://en/276524).[^n1]

:::

The notion of an affine connection has its roots in 19th-century geometry and [tensor calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_calculus), but was not fully developed until the early 1920s, by [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) (as part of his general theory of [connections](wikipedia://en/412272)) and [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) (who used the notion as a part of his foundations for [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024)). The terminology is due to Cartan and has its origins in the identification of tangent spaces in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ by translation: the idea is that a choice of affine connection makes a manifold look infinitesimally like Euclidean space not just smoothly, but as an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834).

On any manifold of positive dimension there are infinitely many affine connections. If the manifold is further endowed with a [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) then there is a natural choice of affine connection, called the [Levi-Civita connection](wikipedia://en/249176). The choice of an affine connection is equivalent to prescribing a way of differentiating vector fields which satisfies several reasonable properties ([linearity](wikipedia://en/91591) and the [Leibniz rule](wikipedia://en/248988)). This yields a possible definition of an affine connection as a [covariant derivative](wikipedia://en/431848) or (linear) [connection](wikipedia://en/391816) on the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794). A choice of affine connection is also equivalent to a notion of [parallel transport](wikipedia://en/285595), which is a method for transporting tangent vectors along curves. This also defines a parallel transport on the [frame bundle](wikipedia://en/366550). Infinitesimal parallel transport in the frame bundle yields another description of an affine connection, either as a [Cartan connection](wikipedia://en/412272) for the [affine group](wikipedia://en/298828) or as a [principal connection](wikipedia://en/470632) on the frame bundle.

The main invariants of an affine connection are its [torsion](wikipedia://en/4342484) and its [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770). The torsion measures how closely the [Lie bracket](wikipedia://en/294437) of vector fields can be recovered from the affine connection. Affine connections may also be used to define (affine) [geodesics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesics) on a manifold, generalizing the *straight lines* of Euclidean space, although the geometry of those straight lines can be very different from usual [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417); the main differences are encapsulated in the curvature of the connection.

## Motivation and history {#Motivation_and_history}

A [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) is a [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical) object which looks locally like a smooth deformation of Euclidean space $\mathbf{R}^{n}$: for example a smooth curve or surface looks locally like a smooth deformation of a line or a plane. [Smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) and [vector fields](wikipedia://en/62641) can be defined on manifolds, just as they can on Euclidean space, and [scalar](wikipedia://en/3588331) functions on manifolds can be differentiated in a natural way. However, differentiation of vector fields is less straightforward: this is a simple matter in Euclidean space, because the tangent space of based vectors at a point $p$ can be identified naturally (by translation) with the tangent space at a nearby point $q$. On a general manifold, there is no such natural identification between nearby tangent spaces, and so tangent vectors at nearby points cannot be compared in a well-defined way. The notion of an affine connection was introduced to remedy this problem by *connecting* nearby tangent spaces. The origins of this idea can be traced back to two main sources: [surface theory](wikipedia://en/15513875) and [tensor calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_calculus).

### Motivation from surface theory {#Motivation_from_surface_theory}

Consider a smooth surface $S$ in a 3-dimensional Euclidean space. Near any point, $S$ can be approximated by its [tangent plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_plane) at that point, which is an [affine subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_subspace) of Euclidean space. Differential geometers in the 19th century were interested in the notion of [development](wikipedia://en/10016722) in which one surface was *rolled* along another, without *slipping* or *twisting*. In particular, the tangent plane to a point of $S$ can be rolled on $S$: this should be easy to imagine when $S$ is a surface like the 2-sphere, which is the smooth boundary of a [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) region. As the tangent plane is rolled on $S$, the point of contact traces out a curve on $S$. Conversely, given a curve on $S$, the tangent plane can be rolled along that curve. This provides a way to identify the tangent planes at different points along the curve: in particular, a tangent vector in the tangent space at one point on the curve is identified with a unique tangent vector at any other point on the curve. These identifications are always given by [affine transformations](wikipedia://en/38449) from one tangent plane to another.

This notion of parallel transport of tangent vectors, by affine transformations, along a curve has a characteristic feature: the point of contact of the tangent plane with the surface *always moves* with the curve under parallel translation (i.e., as the tangent plane is rolled along the surface, the point of contact moves). This generic condition is characteristic of [Cartan connections](wikipedia://en/412272). In more modern approaches, the point of contact is viewed as the *origin* in the tangent plane (which is then a vector space), and the movement of the origin is corrected by a translation, so that parallel transport is linear, rather than affine.

In the point of view of Cartan connections, however, the affine subspaces of Euclidean space are *model* surfaces — they are the simplest surfaces in Euclidean 3-space, and are homogeneous under the affine group of the plane — and every smooth surface has a unique model surface tangent to it at each point. These model surfaces are *Klein geometries* in the sense of [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887)'s [Erlangen programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erlangen_programme). More generally, an $n$-dimensional affine space is a [Klein geometry](wikipedia://en/2908224) for the [affine group](wikipedia://en/298828) $\operatorname{Aff}(n)$, the stabilizer of a point being the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) $\operatorname{GL}(n)$. An affine $n$-manifold is then a manifold which looks infinitesimally like $n$-dimensional affine space.

### Motivation from tensor calculus {#Motivation_from_tensor_calculus}

*Image: Affine connection example.svg.* Historically, people used the covariant derivative (or Levi-Civita connection given by the metric) to describe the variation rate of a vector along the direction of another vector. Here on the punctured 2-dimensional Euclidean space, the blue vector field $X$ sends the [one-form](wikipedia://en/314692) $dr$ to 0.07 everywhere. The red vector field $Y$ sends the one-form $rd\theta$ to $0.5r$ everywhere. Endorsed by the metric $ds^{2} = dr^{2} + r^{2}d\theta^{2}$, the Levi-Civita connection $\nabla_{Y}X$ is 0 everywhere, indicating $X$ has no change along $Y$. In other words, $X$ [parallel transports](wikipedia://en/285595) along each [concentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentric) circle. $\nabla_{X}Y = Y/r$ everywhere, which sends $rd\theta$ to 0.5 everywhere, implying $Y$ has a "constant" changing rate on the radial direction.

The second motivation for affine connections comes from the notion of a [covariant derivative](wikipedia://en/431848) of vector fields. Before the advent of coordinate-independent methods, it was necessary to work with vector fields by [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119) their respective [Euclidean vectors](wikipedia://en/32533) into an [atlas](wikipedia://en/47436). These components can be differentiated, but the derivatives do not transform in a manageable way under changes of coordinates. Correction terms were introduced by [Elwin Bruno Christoffel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elwin_Bruno_Christoffel) (following ideas of [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980)) in the 1870s so that the (corrected) derivative of one vector field along another transformed [covariantly](wikipedia://en/443235) under coordinate transformations — these correction terms subsequently came to be known as [Christoffel symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christoffel_symbol).

This idea was developed into the theory of *absolute differential calculus* (now known as [tensor calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_calculus)) by [Gregorio Ricci-Curbastro](wikipedia://en/499223) and his student [Tullio Levi-Civita](wikipedia://en/499180) between 1880 and the turn of the 20th century.

Tensor calculus really came to life, however, with the advent of [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736)'s theory of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) in 1915. A few years after this, Levi-Civita formalized the unique connection associated to a Riemannian metric, now known as the [Levi-Civita connection](wikipedia://en/249176). More general affine connections were then studied around 1920, by [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544),[^o6] who developed a detailed mathematical foundation for general relativity, and [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274),[^o7] who made the link with the geometrical ideas coming from surface theory.

### Approaches {#Approaches}

The complex history has led to the development of widely varying approaches to and generalizations of the affine connection concept.

The most popular approach is probably the definition motivated by covariant derivatives. On the one hand, the ideas of Weyl were taken up by physicists in the form of [gauge theory](wikipedia://en/23912155) and [gauge covariant derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_covariant_derivative). On the other hand, the notion of covariant differentiation was abstracted by [Jean-Louis Koszul](wikipedia://en/5147910), who defined (linear or Koszul) [connections](wikipedia://en/391816) on [vector bundles](wikipedia://en/276524). In this language, an affine connection is simply a [covariant derivative](wikipedia://en/431848) or (linear) [connection](wikipedia://en/391816) on the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794).

However, this approach does not explain the geometry behind affine connections nor how they acquired their name. The term really has its origins in the identification of tangent spaces in Euclidean space by translation: this property means that Euclidean $n$-space is an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834). (Alternatively, Euclidean space is a [principal homogeneous space](wikipedia://en/363441) or [torsor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsor) under the group of translations, which is a subgroup of the affine group.) As mentioned in the introduction, there are several ways to make this precise: one uses the fact that an affine connection defines a notion of [parallel transport](wikipedia://en/285595) of vector fields along a curve. This also defines a parallel transport on the [frame bundle](wikipedia://en/366550). Infinitesimal parallel transport in the frame bundle yields another description of an affine connection, either as a Cartan connection for the affine group $\operatorname{Aff}(n)$ or as a principal $\operatorname{GL}(n)$ connection on the frame bundle.

## Formal definition as a differential operator {#Formal_definition_as_a_differential_operator}

Let $M$ be a smooth [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) and let $\Gamma(TM)$ be the space of [vector fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_fields) on $M$, that is, the space of [smooth sections](wikipedia://en/367621) of the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) $TM$. Then an **affine connection** on $M$ is a [bilinear map](wikipedia://en/4365)

$$\begin{aligned} \Gamma(\mathrm{T}M)\times \Gamma(\mathrm{T}M) & \rightarrow \Gamma(\mathrm{T}M)\\ (X,Y) & \mapsto \nabla_X Y\,,\end{aligned}$$

such that for all $f$ in the set of smooth functions on $M$, written $C^{\infty}(M, \mathbf{R})$, and all vector fields $X, Y$ on $M$:

1. $\nabla_{fX}Y = f \nabla_{X}Y$, that is, $\nabla$ is $C^{\infty}(M, \mathbf{R})$-*linear* in the first variable;
1. $\nabla_{X}(fY) = (\partial_{X}f) Y + f \nabla_{X}Y$, where $\partial_{X}$ denotes the [directional derivative](wikipedia://en/487859); that is, $\nabla$ satisfies *Leibniz rule* in the second variable.

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Elementary properties"}

### Elementary properties {#Elementary_properties}

- It follows from property 1 above that the value of $\nabla_{X}Y$ at a point $x \in M$ depends only on the value of $X$ at $x$ and not on the value of $X$ on $M - \{x\}$. It also follows from property 2 above that the value of $\nabla_{X}Y$ at a point $x \in M$ depends only on the value of $Y$ on a neighbourhood of $x$.
- If $\nabla^{1}, \nabla^{2}$ are affine connections then the value at $x$ of $\nabla^{1}_{X}Y - \nabla^{2}_{X}Y$ may be written $\Gamma_{x}(X_{x}, Y_{x})$ where $$\Gamma_x : \mathrm{T}_xM \times \mathrm{T}_xM \to \mathrm{T}_xM$$ is bilinear and depends smoothly on $x$ (i.e., it defines a smooth [bundle homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundle_homomorphism)). Conversely if $\nabla$ is an affine connection and $\Gamma$ is such a smooth bilinear bundle homomorphism (called a [connection form](wikipedia://en/706412) on $M$) then $\nabla + \Gamma$ is an affine connection.
- If $M$ is an open subset of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$, then the tangent bundle of $M$ is the [trivial bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivial_bundle) $M \times \mathbf{R}^{n}$. In this situation there is a canonical affine connection $d$ on $M$: any vector field $Y$ is given by a smooth function $V$ from $M$ to $\mathbf{R}^{n}$; then $d_{X}Y$ is the vector field corresponding to the smooth function $dV(X) = \partial_{X}Y$ from $M$ to $\mathbf{R}^{n}$. Any other affine connection $\nabla$ on $M$ may therefore be written $\nabla = d + \Gamma$, where $\Gamma$ is a connection form on $M$.
- More generally, a [local trivialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_trivialization) of the tangent bundle is a [bundle isomorphism](wikipedia://en/7220589) between the restriction of $TM$ to an open subset $U$ of $M$, and $U \times \mathbf{R}^{n}$. The restriction of an affine connection $\nabla$ to $U$ may then be written in the form $d + \Gamma$ where $\Gamma$ is a connection form on $U$.

:::

## Parallel transport for affine connections {#Parallel_transport_for_affine_connections}

*Image: Parallel transport sphere2.svg.* Parallel transport of a tangent vector along a curve in the sphere.

Comparison of tangent vectors at different points on a manifold is generally not a well-defined process. An affine connection provides one way to remedy this using the notion of [parallel transport](wikipedia://en/285595), and indeed this can be used to give a definition of an affine connection.

Let $M$ be a manifold with an affine connection $\nabla$. Then a vector field $X$ is said to be **parallel** if $\nabla X = 0$ in the sense that for any vector field $Y$, $\nabla_{Y}X = 0$. Intuitively speaking, parallel vectors have *all their [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) equal to zero* and are therefore in some sense *constant*. By evaluating a parallel vector field at two points $x$ and $y$, an identification between a tangent vector at $x$ and one at $y$ is obtained. Such tangent vectors are said to be **parallel transports** of each other.

Nonzero parallel vector fields do not, in general, exist, because the equation $\nabla X = 0$ is a [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564) which is [overdetermined](wikipedia://en/10160606): the [integrability condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrability_condition) for this equation is the vanishing of the **curvature** of $\nabla$ (see below). However, if this equation is restricted to a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) from $x$ to $y$ it becomes an [ordinary differential equation](wikipedia://en/32742753). There is then a unique solution for any initial value of $X$ at $x$.

More precisely, if $\gamma : I \to M$ a [smooth curve](wikipedia://en/89246) parametrized by an interval $[a, b]$ and $\xi \in T_{x}M$, where $x = \gamma(a)$, then a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) $X$ along $\gamma$ (and in particular, the value of this vector field at $y = \gamma(b)$) is called the **parallel transport of $\xi$ along $\gamma$** if

1. $\nabla_{\gamma'(t)}X = 0$, for all $t \in [a, b]$
1. $$X_{\gamma(a)} = \xi.$$

Formally, the first condition means that $X$ is parallel with respect to the [pullback connection](wikipedia://en/494995) on the [pullback bundle](wikipedia://en/1665392) $\gamma^{\ast}TM$. However, in a [local trivialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_trivialization) it is a first-order system of [linear ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/379868), which has a unique solution for any initial condition given by the second condition (for instance, by the [Picard–Lindelöf theorem](wikipedia://en/666177)).

Thus parallel transport provides a way of moving tangent vectors along a curve using the affine connection to keep them "pointing in the same direction" in an intuitive sense, and this provides a [linear isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_isomorphism) between the tangent spaces at the two ends of the curve. The isomorphism obtained in this way will in general depend on the choice of the curve: if it does not, then parallel transport along every curve can be used to define parallel vector fields on $M$, which can only happen if the curvature of $\nabla$ is zero.

A linear isomorphism is determined by its action on an [ordered basis](wikipedia://en/18420#Ordered_bases_and_coordinates) or **frame**. Hence parallel transport can also be characterized as a way of transporting elements of the (tangent) [frame bundle](wikipedia://en/366550) $\operatorname{GL}(M)$ along a curve. In other words, the affine connection provides a **lift** of any curve $\gamma$ in $M$ to a curve $\gamma\text{̃}$ in $\operatorname{GL}(M)$.

## Formal definition on the frame bundle {#Formal_definition_on_the_frame_bundle}

An affine connection may also be defined as a [principal $\operatorname{GL}(n)$ connection](wikipedia://en/470632) $\omega$ on the [frame bundle](wikipedia://en/366550) $FM$ or $\operatorname{GL}(M)$ of a manifold $M$. In more detail, $\omega$ is a smooth map from the tangent bundle $T(FM)$ of the frame bundle to the space of $n \times n$ matrices (which is the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) $\mathbf{gl}(n)$ of the [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) $\operatorname{GL}(n)$ of invertible $n \times n$ matrices) satisfying two properties:

1. $\omega$ is [equivariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivariant) with respect to the action of $\operatorname{GL}(n)$ on $T(FM)$ and $\mathbf{gl}(n)$;
1. $\omega(X_{\xi}) = \xi$ for any $\xi$ in $\mathbf{gl}(n)$, where $X_{\xi}$ is the vector field on $FM$ corresponding to $\xi$.

Such a connection $\omega$ immediately defines a [covariant derivative](wikipedia://en/431848) not only on the tangent bundle, but on [vector bundles](wikipedia://en/276524) [associated](wikipedia://en/398874) to any [group representation](wikipedia://en/12695) of $\operatorname{GL}(n)$, including bundles of [tensors](wikipedia://en/29965) and [tensor densities](wikipedia://en/1763396). Conversely, an affine connection on the tangent bundle determines an affine connection on the frame bundle, for instance, by requiring that $\omega$ vanishes on tangent vectors to the lifts of curves to the frame bundle defined by parallel transport.

The frame bundle also comes equipped with a [solder form](wikipedia://en/366550#Solder_form) $\theta : T(FM) \to \mathbf{R}^{n}$ which is **horizontal** in the sense that it vanishes on [vertical vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_bundle) such as the point values of the vector fields $X_{\xi}$: Indeed $\theta$ is defined first by projecting a tangent vector (to $FM$ at a frame $f$) to $M$, then by taking the components of this tangent vector on $M$ with respect to the frame $f$. Note that $\theta$ is also $\operatorname{GL}(n)$-equivariant (where $\operatorname{GL}(n)$ acts on $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ by matrix multiplication).

The pair $(\theta, \omega)$ defines a [bundle isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundle_isomorphism) of $T(FM)$ with the trivial bundle $FM \times \mathbf{aff}(n)$, where $\mathbf{aff}(n)$ is the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ and $\mathbf{gl}(n)$ (viewed as the Lie algebra of the affine group, which is actually a [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) – see below).

## Affine connections as Cartan connections {#Affine_connections_as_Cartan_connections}

Affine connections can be defined within Cartan's general framework.[^o15] In the modern approach, this is closely related to the definition of affine connections on the frame bundle. Indeed, in one formulation, a Cartan connection is an [absolute parallelism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_parallelism) of a principal bundle satisfying suitable properties. From this point of view the $\mathbf{aff}(n)$-valued one-form $(\theta, \omega) : T(FM) \to \mathbf{aff}(n)$ on the frame bundle (of an [affine manifold](wikipedia://en/9996788)) is a Cartan connection. However, Cartan's original approach was different from this in a number of ways:

- the concept of frame bundles or principal bundles did not exist;
- a connection was viewed in terms of parallel transport between infinitesimally nearby points;
- this parallel transport was affine, rather than linear;
- the objects being transported were not tangent vectors in the modern sense, but elements of an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) with a marked point, which the Cartan connection ultimately *identifies* with the tangent space.

### Explanations and historical intuition {#Explanations_and_historical_intuition}

The points just raised are easiest to explain in reverse, starting from the motivation provided by surface theory. In this situation, although the planes being rolled over the surface are tangent planes in a naive sense, the notion of a [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) is really an [infinitesimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_%28infinitesimal%29) notion, whereas the planes, as [affine subspaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_subspace) of $\mathbf{R}^{3}$, are [infinite](wikipedia://en/20110824) in extent. However these affine planes all have a marked point, the point of contact with the surface, and they are tangent to the surface at this point. The confusion therefore arises because an affine space with a marked point can be identified with its tangent space at that point. However, the parallel transport defined by rolling does not fix this origin: it is [affine](wikipedia://en/38449) rather than linear; the linear parallel transport can be recovered by applying a translation.

Abstracting this idea, an affine manifold should therefore be an $n$-manifold $M$ with an affine space $A_{x}$, of dimension $n$, *attached* to each $x \in M$ at a marked point $a_{x} \in A_{x}$, together with a method for transporting elements of these affine spaces along any curve $C$ in $M$. This method is required to satisfy several properties:

1. for any two points $x, y$ on $C$, parallel transport is an [affine transformation](wikipedia://en/38449) from $A_{x}$ to $A_{y}$;
1. parallel transport is defined infinitesimally in the sense that it is differentiable at any point on $C$ and depends only on the tangent vector to $C$ at that point;
1. the derivative of the parallel transport at $x$ determines a [linear isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_isomorphism) from $T_{x}M$ to $T_{a_{x}}A_{x}$.

These last two points are quite hard to make precise,[^o17] so affine connections are more often defined infinitesimally. To motivate this, it suffices to consider how affine [frames of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frames_of_reference) transform infinitesimally with respect to parallel transport. (This is the origin of Cartan's [method of moving frames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_moving_frames).) An affine frame at a point consists of a list $(p, \mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots \mathbf{e}_{n})$, where $p \in A_{x}$ and the $\mathbf{e}_{i}$ form a basis of $T_{p}(A_{x})$. The affine connection is then given symbolically by a first order [differential system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_system)

$$(*) \begin{cases} \mathrm{d}{p} &= \theta^1\mathbf{e}_1 + \cdots + \theta^n\mathbf{e}_n \\ \mathrm{d}\mathbf{e}_i &= \omega^1_i\mathbf{e}_1 + \cdots + \omega^n_i\mathbf{e}_n \end{cases} \quad i=1,2,\ldots,n$$

defined by a collection of [one-forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_forms) $(\theta^{j}, \omega^{j}_{i})$. Geometrically, an affine frame undergoes a displacement travelling along a curve $\gamma$ from $\gamma(t)$ to $\gamma(t + \delta t)$ given (approximately, or infinitesimally) by

$$\begin{aligned} p(\gamma(t+\delta t)) - p(\gamma(t)) &= \left(\theta^1\left(\gamma'(t)\right)\mathbf{e}_1 + \cdots + \theta^n\left(\gamma'(t)\right)\mathbf{e}_n\right)\mathrm \delta t \\ \mathbf{e}_i(\gamma(t+\delta t)) - \mathbf{e}_i(\gamma(t)) &= \left(\omega^1_i\left(\gamma'(t)\right)\mathbf{e}_1 + \cdots + \omega^n_i\left(\gamma'(t)\right) \mathbf{e}_n\right)\delta t\,. \end{aligned}$$

Furthermore, the affine spaces $A_{x}$ are required to be tangent to $M$ in the informal sense that the displacement of $a_{x}$ along $\gamma$ can be identified (approximately or infinitesimally) with the tangent vector $\gamma'(t)$ to $\gamma$ at $x = \gamma(t)$ (which is the infinitesimal displacement of $x$). Since

$$a_x (\gamma(t + \delta t)) - a_x (\gamma(t)) = \theta\left(\gamma'(t)\right) \delta t \,,$$

where $\theta$ is defined by $\theta(X) = \theta^{1}(X)\mathbf{e}_{1} + \ldots + \theta^{n}(X)\mathbf{e}_{n}$, this identification is given by $\theta$, so the requirement is that $\theta$ should be a linear isomorphism at each point.

The tangential affine space $A_{x}$ is thus identified intuitively with an *infinitesimal affine neighborhood* of $x$.

The modern point of view makes all this intuition more precise using principal bundles (the essential idea is to replace a frame or a *variable* frame by the space of all frames and functions on this space). It also draws on the inspiration of [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887)'s [Erlangen programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erlangen_programme),[^o18] in which a *geometry* is defined to be a [homogeneous space](wikipedia://en/363325). Affine space is a geometry in this sense, and is equipped with a *flat* Cartan connection. Thus a general affine manifold is viewed as *curved* deformation of the flat model geometry of affine space.

### Affine space as the flat model geometry {#Affine_space_as_the_flat_model_geometry}

#### Definition of an affine space {#Definition_of_an_affine_space}

Informally, an **[affine space](wikipedia://en/298834)** is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) without a fixed choice of [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432). It describes the geometry of [points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_%28mathematics%29) and [free vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29) in space. As a consequence of the lack of origin, points in affine space cannot be added together as this requires a choice of origin with which to form the parallelogram law for vector addition. However, a vector $v$ may be added to a point $p$ by placing the initial point of the vector at $p$ and then transporting $p$ to the terminal point. The operation thus described $p \to p + v$ is the **translation** of $p$ along $v$. In technical terms, affine $n$-space is a set $\mathbf{A}^{n}$ equipped with a [free transitive action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of the vector group $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ on it through this operation of translation of points: $\mathbf{A}^{n}$ is thus a [principal homogeneous space](wikipedia://en/363441) for the vector group $\mathbf{R}^{n}$.

The [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) $\operatorname{GL}(n)$ is the [group of transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_group) of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ which preserve the *linear structure* of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ in the sense that $T(av + bw) = aT(v) + bT(w)$. By analogy, the **[affine group](wikipedia://en/298828)** $\operatorname{Aff}(n)$ is the group of transformations of $\mathbf{A}^{n}$ preserving the *affine structure*. Thus $\varphi \in \operatorname{Aff}(n)$ must *preserve translations* in the sense that

$$\varphi(p+v)=\varphi(p)+T(v)$$

where $T$ is a general linear transformation. The map sending $\varphi \in \operatorname{Aff}(n)$ to $T \in \operatorname{GL}(n)$ is a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396). Its [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) is the group of translations $\mathbf{R}^{n}$. The [stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Orbits_and_stabilizers) of any point $p$ in $A$ can thus be identified with $\operatorname{GL}(n)$ using this projection: this realises the affine group as a [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) of $\operatorname{GL}(n)$ and $\mathbf{R}^{n}$, and affine space as the [homogeneous space](wikipedia://en/363325) $\operatorname{Aff}(n)/\operatorname{GL}(n)$.

#### Affine frames and the flat affine connection {#Affine_frames_and_the_flat_affine_connection}

An *affine frame* for $A$ consists of a point $p \in A$ and a basis $(\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots \mathbf{e}_{n})$ of the vector space $T_{p}A = \mathbf{R}^{n}$. The general linear group $\operatorname{GL}(n)$ acts freely on the set $FA$ of all affine frames by fixing $p$ and transforming the basis $(\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots \mathbf{e}_{n})$ in the usual way, and the map $\pi$ sending an affine frame $(p; \mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots \mathbf{e}_{n})$ to $p$ is the [quotient map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_map). Thus $FA$ is a [principal $\operatorname{GL}(n)$-bundle](wikipedia://en/310950) over $A$. The action of $\operatorname{GL}(n)$ extends naturally to a free transitive action of the affine group $\operatorname{Aff}(n)$ on $FA$, so that $FA$ is an $\operatorname{Aff}(n)$-[torsor](wikipedia://en/363441), and the choice of a reference frame identifies $FA \to A$ with the principal bundle $\operatorname{Aff}(n) \to \operatorname{Aff}(n)/\operatorname{GL}(n)$.

On $FA$ there is a collection of $n + 1$ functions defined by

$$\pi(p;\mathbf{e}_1, \dots ,\mathbf{e}_n) = p$$

(as before) and

$$\varepsilon_i(p;\mathbf{e}_1,\dots , \mathbf{e}_n) = \mathbf{e}_i\,.$$

After choosing a basepoint for $A$, these are all functions with values in $\mathbf{R}^{n}$, so it is possible to take their [exterior derivatives](wikipedia://en/220782) to obtain [differential 1-forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_1-form) with values in $\mathbf{R}^{n}$. Since the functions $\varepsilon_{i}$ yield a basis for $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ at each point of $FA$, these 1-forms must be expressible as sums of the form

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}\pi &= \theta^1\varepsilon_1+\cdots+\theta^n\varepsilon_n\\ \mathrm{d}\varepsilon_i &= \omega^1_i\varepsilon_1+\cdots+\omega^n_i\varepsilon_n \end{aligned}$$

for some collection $(\theta^{i}, \omega^{k}_{j})_{1 \le i, j, k \le n}$ of real-valued one-forms on $\operatorname{Aff}(n)$. This system of one-forms on the principal bundle $FA \to A$ defines the affine connection on $A$.

Taking the exterior derivative a second time, and using the fact that $d^{2} = 0$ as well as the [linear independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) of the $\varepsilon_{i}$, the following relations are obtained:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}\theta^j - \sum_i\omega^j_i\wedge\theta^i &=0\\ \mathrm{d}\omega^j_i - \sum_k \omega^j_k\wedge\omega^k_i &=0\,. \end{aligned}$$

These are the [Maurer–Cartan equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurer–Cartan_equation) for the Lie group $\operatorname{Aff}(n)$ (identified with $FA$ by the choice of a reference frame). Furthermore:

- the [Pfaffian system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pfaffian_system) $\theta^{j} = 0$ (for all $j$) is [integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrability_condition), and its [integral manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_manifold) are the fibres of the principal bundle $\operatorname{Aff}(n) \to A$.
- the Pfaffian system $\omega^{j}_{i} = 0$ (for all $i, j$) is also integrable, and its integral manifolds define parallel transport in $FA$.

Thus the forms $(\omega^{j}_{i})$ define a flat [principal connection](wikipedia://en/470632) on $FA \to A$.

For a strict comparison with the motivation, one should actually define parallel transport in a principal $\operatorname{Aff}(n)$-bundle over $A$. This can be done by [pulling back](wikipedia://en/1665392) $FA$ by the smooth map $\varphi : \mathbf{R}^{n} \times A \to A$ defined by translation. Then the composite $\varphi' \ast FA \to FA \to A$ is a principal $\operatorname{Aff}(n)$-bundle over $A$, and the forms $(\theta^{i}, \omega^{k}_{j})$ [pull back](wikipedia://en/494995) to give a flat principal $\operatorname{Aff}(n)$-connection on this bundle.

### General affine geometries: formal definitions {#General_affine_geometries:_formal_definitions}

An affine space, as with essentially any smooth [Klein geometry](wikipedia://en/2908224), is a manifold equipped with a flat Cartan connection. More general affine manifolds or affine geometries are obtained easily by dropping the flatness condition expressed by the Maurer-Cartan equations. There are several ways to approach the definition and two will be given. Both definitions are facilitated by the realisation that 1-forms $(\theta^{i}, \omega^{k}_{j})$ in the flat model fit together to give a 1-form with values in the Lie algebra $\mathbf{aff}(n)$ of the affine group $\operatorname{Aff}(n)$.

In these definitions, $M$ is a smooth $n$-manifold and $A = \operatorname{Aff}(n)/\operatorname{GL}(n)$ is an affine space of the same dimension.

#### Definition via absolute parallelism {#Definition_via_absolute_parallelism}

Let $M$ be a manifold, and $P$ a principal $\operatorname{GL}(n)$-bundle over $M$. Then an **affine connection** is a 1-form $\eta$ on $P$ with values in $\mathbf{aff}(n)$ satisfying the following properties

1. $\eta$ is equivariant with respect to the action of $\operatorname{GL}(n)$ on $P$ and $\mathbf{aff}(n)$;
1. $\eta(X_{\xi}) = \xi$ for all $\xi$ in the Lie algebra $\mathbf{gl}(n)$ of all $n \times n$ matrices;
1. $\eta$ is a linear isomorphism of each tangent space of $P$ with $\mathbf{aff}(n)$.

The last condition means that $\eta$ is an **[absolute parallelism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_parallelism)** on $P$, i.e., it identifies the tangent bundle of $P$ with a trivial bundle (in this case $P \times \mathbf{aff}(n)$). The pair $(P, \eta)$ defines the structure of an **affine geometry** on $M$, making it into an **affine manifold**.

The affine Lie algebra $\mathbf{aff}(n)$ splits as a semidirect product of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ and $\mathbf{gl}(n)$ and so $\eta$ may be written as a pair $(\theta, \omega)$ where $\theta$ takes values in $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ and $\omega$ takes values in $\mathbf{gl}(n)$. Conditions 1 and 2 are equivalent to $\omega$ being a principal $\operatorname{GL}(n)$-connection and $\theta$ being a horizontal equivariant 1-form, which induces a [bundle homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundle_homomorphism) from $TM$ to the [associated bundle](wikipedia://en/398874) $P \times_{\operatorname{GL}(n)} \mathbf{R}^{n}$. Condition 3 is equivalent to the fact that this bundle homomorphism is an isomorphism. (However, this decomposition is a consequence of the rather special structure of the affine group.) Since $P$ is the [frame bundle](wikipedia://en/366550) of $P \times_{\operatorname{GL}(n)} \mathbf{R}^{n}$, it follows that $\theta$ provides a bundle isomorphism between $P$ and the frame bundle $FM$ of $M$; this recovers the definition of an affine connection as a principal $\operatorname{GL}(n)$-connection on $FM$.

The 1-forms arising in the flat model are just the components of $\theta$ and $\omega$.

#### Definition as a principal affine connection {#Definition_as_a_principal_affine_connection}

An **affine connection** on $M$ is a principal $\operatorname{Aff}(n)$-bundle $Q$ over $M$, together with a principal $\operatorname{GL}(n)$-subbundle $P$ of $Q$ and a principal $\operatorname{Aff}(n)$-connection $\alpha$ (a 1-form on $Q$ with values in $\mathbf{aff}(n)$) which satisfies the following (generic) *Cartan condition*. The $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ component of pullback of $\alpha$ to $P$ is a horizontal equivariant 1-form and so defines a bundle homomorphism from $TM$ to $P \times_{\operatorname{GL}(n)} \mathbf{R}^{n}$: this is required to be an isomorphism.

#### Relation to the motivation {#Relation_to_the_motivation}

Since $\operatorname{Aff}(n)$ acts on $A$, there is, associated to the principal bundle $Q$, a bundle $\boldsymbol{A} = Q \times_{\operatorname{Aff}(n)} A$, which is a fiber bundle over $M$ whose fiber at $x$ in $M$ is an affine space $A_{x}$. A [section](wikipedia://en/367621) $a$ of $\boldsymbol{A}$ (defining a marked point $a_{x}$ in $A_{x}$ for each $x \in M$) determines a principal $\operatorname{GL}(n)$-subbundle $P$ of $Q$ (as the bundle of stabilizers of these marked points) and vice versa. The principal connection $\alpha$ defines an [Ehresmann connection](wikipedia://en/5731754) on this bundle, hence a notion of parallel transport. The Cartan condition ensures that the distinguished section $a$ always moves under parallel transport.

## Further properties {#Further_properties}

### Curvature and torsion {#Curvature_and_torsion}

Curvature and torsion are the main invariants of an affine connection. As there are many equivalent ways to define the notion of an affine connection, so there are many different ways to define curvature and torsion.

From the Cartan connection point of view, the curvature is the failure of the affine connection $\eta$ to satisfy the Maurer–Cartan equation

$$\mathrm{d}\eta + \tfrac12[\eta\wedge\eta] = 0,$$

where the second term on the left hand side is the [wedge product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedge_product) using the [Lie bracket](wikipedia://en/10282799) in $\mathbf{aff}(n)$ to contract the values. By expanding $\eta$ into the pair $(\theta, \omega)$ and using the structure of the Lie algebra $\mathbf{aff}(n)$, this left hand side can be expanded into the two formulae

$$\mathrm{d}\theta + \omega\wedge\theta \quad \text{and} \quad \mathrm{d}\omega + \omega\wedge\omega\,,$$

where the wedge products are evaluated using matrix multiplication. The first expression is called the [torsion](wikipedia://en/4342484) of the connection, and the second is also called the curvature.

These expressions are differential 2-forms on the total space of a frame bundle. However, they are horizontal and equivariant, and hence define tensorial objects. These can be defined directly from the induced covariant derivative $\nabla$ on $TM$ as follows.

The [torsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion_of_connection) is given by the formula

$$T^\nabla(X,Y) = \nabla_X Y - \nabla_Y X - [X,Y].$$

If the torsion vanishes, the connection is said to be *torsion-free* or *symmetric*.

The curvature is given by the formula

$$R^\nabla_{X,Y}Z = \nabla_X\nabla_Y Z - \nabla_Y\nabla_X Z - \nabla_{[X,Y]}Z.$$

Note that $[X, Y]$ is the [Lie bracket of vector fields](wikipedia://en/10282799)

$$[X,Y]=\left(X^j \partial_j Y^i - Y^j \partial_j X^i\right)\partial_i$$

in [Einstein notation](wikipedia://en/195407). This is independent of coordinate system choice and

$$\partial_i = \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^i}\right)_p\,,$$

the tangent vector at point $p$ of the $i$th [coordinate curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_curve). The $\partial_{i}$ are a natural basis for the tangent space at point $p$, and the $X^{i}$ the corresponding coordinates for the vector field $X = X^{i} \partial_{i}$.

When both curvature and torsion vanish, the connection defines a [pre-Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/30722934) structure on the space of global sections of the tangent bundle.

### The Levi-Civita connection {#The_Levi-Civita_connection}

If $(M, g)$ is a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) then there is a unique affine connection $\nabla$ on $M$ with the following two properties:

- the connection is torsion-free, i.e., $T^{\nabla}$ is zero, so that $\nabla_{X}Y - \nabla_{Y}X = [X, Y]$;
- parallel transport is an isometry, i.e., the inner products (defined using $g$) between tangent vectors are preserved.

This connection is called the **[Levi-Civita connection](wikipedia://en/249176)**.

The term "symmetric" is often used instead of torsion-free for the first property. The second condition means that the connection is a **[metric connection](wikipedia://en/4794503)** in the sense that the Riemannian metric $g$ is parallel: $\nabla g = 0$. For a torsion-free connection, the condition is equivalent to the identity $X g(Y, Z)$ = $g(\nabla_{X}Y, Z)$ + $g(Y, \nabla_{X} Z)$, "compatibility with the metric".[^o29] In local coordinates the components of the form are called [Christoffel symbols](wikipedia://en/1401020): because of the uniqueness of the Levi-Civita connection, there is a formula for these components in terms of the components of $g$.

### Geodesics {#Geodesics}

Since straight lines are a concept in affine geometry, affine connections define a generalized notion of (parametrized) straight lines on any affine manifold, called affine geodesics. Abstractly, a parametric curve $\gamma : I \to M$ is a straight line if its tangent vector remains parallel with itself when it is transported along $\gamma$. That is, a smooth curve $\gamma : I \to M$ is an **affine geodesic** if $\dot\gamma$ is parallel transported along $\gamma$ up to scaling, that is

$$\tau_t^s\dot\gamma(s) = k(t)\dot\gamma(t)$$

where $\tau^{s}_{t} : T_{\gamma_{s}}M \to T_{\gamma_{t}}M$ is the parallel transport map defining the connection and $k(t)$ is some smooth and positive-valued function.

In terms of the infinitesimal connection $\nabla$, the derivative of this equation implies

$$\nabla_{\dot\gamma(t)}\dot\gamma(t) = s(t)\dot\gamma(t)$$

for all $t \in I$ and some smooth function $s$.

There is no [arc-length parameterization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc-length_parametrization), since tangent vectors no longer have lengths. But the ratio between collinear tangent vectors is still well-defined. This then allows us to define **affine parameterization**: that $\dot\gamma$ is exactly parallel transported along $\gamma$:

$$\tau_t^s\dot\gamma(s) = \dot\gamma(t), \quad \nabla_{\dot\gamma(t)}\dot\gamma(t) = 0$$

That is, the tangent vectors are parallel and *equipollent* along the geodesic.

Affine parameterization is unique up to a choice of affine reparametrization $\gamma(t) \to \gamma(at + b)$, where $a$ and $b$ are constants.

For every $x \in M$ and every $X \in T_{x}M$, there exists a unique affine geodesic $\gamma : I \to M$ with $\gamma(0) = x$ and $\gamma\text{̇}(0) = X$ and where $I$ is the maximal open interval in $\mathbf{R}$, containing 0, on which the geodesic is defined. This follows from the [Picard–Lindelöf theorem](wikipedia://en/666177), and allows for the definition of an [exponential map](wikipedia://en/276548) associated to the affine connection. In particular, when $M$ is a ([pseudo](wikipedia://en/287555)-)[Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) and $\nabla$ is the [Levi-Civita connection](wikipedia://en/249176), then the affine geodesics are the usual [geodesics](wikipedia://en/91096) of Riemannian geometry and are the locally distance minimizing curves.

Unparametrized geodesics are often studied from the point of view of [projective differential geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_differential_geometry), where even the ratio between tangent vectors is no longer well-defined, but straightness is still well-defined.

### Development {#Development}

An affine connection defines a notion of **[development](wikipedia://en/10016722)** of curves. Intuitively, development captures the notion that if $x_{t}$ is a curve in $M$, then the affine tangent space at $x_{0}$ may be *rolled* along the curve. As it does so, the marked point of contact between the tangent space and the manifold traces out a curve $C_{t}$ in this affine space: the development of $x_{t}$.

In formal terms, let $\tau^{0}_{t} : T_{x_{t}}M \to T_{x_{0}}M$ be the linear parallel transport map associated to the affine connection. Then the development $C_{t}$ is the curve in $T_{x_{0}}M$ starts off at 0 and is parallel to the tangent of $x_{t}$ for all time $t$:

$$\dot{C}_t = \tau_t^0\dot{x}_t\,,\quad C_0 = 0.$$

In particular, $x_{t}$ is a *geodesic* if and only if its development is an affinely parametrized straight line in $T_{x_{0}}M$.[^o32]

## Surface theory revisited {#Surface_theory_revisited}

If $M$ is a surface in $\mathbf{R}^{3}$, it is easy to see that $M$ has a natural affine connection. From the linear connection point of view, the covariant derivative of a vector field is defined by differentiating the vector field, viewed as a map from $M$ to $\mathbf{R}^{3}$, and then projecting the result orthogonally back onto the tangent spaces of $M$. It is easy to see that this affine connection is torsion-free. Furthermore, it is a metric connection with respect to the Riemannian metric on $M$ induced by the inner product on $\mathbf{R}^{3}$, hence it is the Levi-Civita connection of this metric.

### Example: the unit sphere in Euclidean space {#Example:_the_unit_sphere_in_Euclidean_space}

Let $\langle , \rangle$ be the usual [scalar product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_product) on $\mathbf{R}^{3}$, and let $\mathbf{S}^{2}$ be the unit sphere. The tangent space to $\mathbf{S}^{2}$ at a point $x$ is naturally identified with the vector subspace of $\mathbf{R}^{3}$ consisting of all vectors orthogonal to $x$. It follows that a vector field $Y$ on $\mathbf{S}^{2}$ can be seen as a map $Y : \mathbf{S}^{2} \to \mathbf{R}^{3}$ which satisfies

$$\langle Y_x, x\rangle = 0\,, \quad \forall x\in \mathbf{S}^2.$$

Denote as $dY$ the differential (Jacobian matrix) of such a map. Then we have:

**Lemma**. The formula

$$(\nabla_Z Y)_x = \mathrm{d}Y_x(Z_x) + \langle Z_x,Y_x\rangle x$$

defines an affine connection on $\mathbf{S}^{2}$ with vanishing torsion.

**Proof**. It is straightforward to prove that $\nabla$ satisfies the Leibniz identity and is $C^{\infty}(\mathbf{S}^{2})$ linear in the first variable. So all that needs to be proved here is that the map above does indeed define a tangent vector field. That is, we need to prove that for all $x$ in $\mathbf{S}^{2}$

$$\bigl\langle(\nabla_Z Y)_x,x\bigr\rangle = 0\,.\qquad \text{(Eq.1)}$$

Consider the map

$$\begin{aligned} f: \mathbf{S}^2&\to \mathbf{R}\\ x &\mapsto \langle Y_x, x\rangle\,.\end{aligned}$$

The map *f* is constant, hence its differential vanishes. In particular

$$\mathrm{d}f_x(Z_x) = \bigl\langle (\mathrm{d} Y)_x(Z_x),x(\gamma'(t))\bigr\rangle + \langle Y_x, Z_x\rangle = 0\,.$$

Equation 1 above follows. [Q.E.D.](wikipedia://en/25228)
