# Proper morphism

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Proper morphism"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a **proper morphism** between [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) is an analog of a [proper map](wikipedia://en/1190262) between [complex analytic spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_analytic_space).

:::

Some authors call a proper [variety](wikipedia://en/248808) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $k$ a [complete variety](wikipedia://en/1047627). For example, every [projective variety](wikipedia://en/320469) over a field $k$ is proper over $k$. A scheme $X$ of [finite type](wikipedia://en/70728366) over the [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) (for example, a variety) is proper over **C** if and only if the space $X$(**C**) of complex points with the classical (Euclidean) topology is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042).

A [closed immersion](wikipedia://en/1623986) is proper. A morphism is [finite](wikipedia://en/2139357) if and only if it is proper and [quasi-finite](wikipedia://en/2143056).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A [morphism](wikipedia://en/7482797) $f:X\to Y$ of schemes is called **universally closed** if for every scheme $Z$ with a morphism $Z\to Y$, the projection from the [fiber product](wikipedia://en/56444110)

$$X \times_Y Z \to Z$$

is a [closed map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_map) of the underlying [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450). A morphism of schemes is called **proper** if it is [separated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separated_morphism), of [finite type](wikipedia://en/70728366), and universally closed (\[EGA\] II, 5.4.1 <https://web.archive.org/web/20051108184937/http://modular.fas.harvard.edu/scans/papers/grothendieck/PMIHES_1961__8__5_0.pdf>). One also says that $X$ is proper over $Y$. In particular, a variety $X$ over a field $k$ is said to be proper over $k$ if the morphism $X\to\operatorname{Spec}(k)$ is proper.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

For any natural number *n*, [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) **P**<sup>*n*</sup> over a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) *R* is proper over *R*. [Projective morphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_morphism) are proper, but not all proper morphisms are projective. For example, there is a [smooth](wikipedia://en/35033819) proper complex variety of dimension 3 which is not projective over **C**.[^o6] [Affine varieties](wikipedia://en/320468) of positive dimension over a field *k* are never proper over *k*. More generally, a proper [affine morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_morphism) of schemes must be finite.[^o7] For example, it is not hard to see that the [affine line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_line) *A*<sup>1</sup> over a field *k* is not proper over *k*, because the morphism *A*<sup>1</sup> → Spec(*k*) is not universally closed. Indeed, the pulled-back morphism

$$\mathbb{A}^1 \times_k \mathbb{A}^1 \to \mathbb{A}^1$$

(given by (*x*,*y*) ↦ *y*) is not closed, because the image of the closed subset *xy* = 1 in *A*<sup>1</sup> × *A*<sup>1</sup> = *A*<sup>2</sup> is *A*<sup>1</sup> − 0, which is not closed in *A*<sup>1</sup>.

:::

## Properties and characterizations of proper morphisms {#Properties_and_characterizations_of_proper_morphisms}

In the following, let *f*: *X* → *Y* be a morphism of schemes.

- The composition of two proper morphisms is proper.
- Any [base change](wikipedia://en/56444110) of a proper morphism *f*: *X* → *Y* is proper. That is, if *g*: Z → *Y* is any morphism of schemes, then the resulting morphism *X* ×<sub>*Y*</sub> *Z* → *Z* is proper.
- Properness is a [local property](wikipedia://en/1462457) on the base (in the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744)). That is, if *Y* is covered by some open subschemes *Y<sub>i</sub>* and the restriction of *f* to all *f<sup>−1</sup>(Y<sub>i</sub>)* is proper, then so is *f*.
- More strongly, properness is local on the base in the [fpqc topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fpqc_topology). For example, if *X* is a scheme over a field *k* and *E* is a field extension of *k*, then *X* is proper over *k* if and only if the base change *X*<sub>*E*</sub> is proper over *E*.[^o9]
- [Closed immersions](wikipedia://en/1623986) are proper.
- More generally, finite morphisms are proper. This is a consequence of the [going up](wikipedia://en/2083445) theorem.
- By [Deligne](wikipedia://en/394544), a morphism of schemes is finite if and only if it is proper and quasi-finite.[^o10] This had been shown by [Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) if the morphism *f*: *X* → *Y* is [locally of finite presentation](wikipedia://en/35457783#finite_presentation), which follows from the other assumptions if *Y* is [noetherian](wikipedia://en/2783891).[^o11]
- For *X* proper over a scheme *S*, and *Y* separated over *S*, the image of any morphism *X* → *Y* over *S* is a closed subset of *Y*.[^o12] This is analogous to the theorem in topology that the image of a continuous map from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a closed subset.
- The [Stein factorization](wikipedia://en/1205940) theorem states that any proper morphism to a locally noetherian scheme can be factored as *X* → *Z* → *Y*, where *X* → *Z* is proper, surjective, and has geometrically connected fibers, and *Z* → *Y* is finite.[^o13]
- [Chow's lemma](wikipedia://en/11127518) says that proper morphisms are closely related to [projective morphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_morphism). One version is: if *X* is proper over a [quasi-compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-compact) scheme *Y* and *X* has only finitely many irreducible components (which is automatic for *Y* noetherian), then there is a projective surjective morphism *g*: *W* → *X* such that *W* is projective over *Y*. Moreover, one can arrange that *g* is an isomorphism over a dense open subset *U* of *X*, and that *g*<sup>−1</sup>(*U*) is dense in *W*. One can also arrange that *W* is integral if *X* is integral.[^o14]
- [Nagata's compactification theorem](wikipedia://en/37613671), as generalized by Deligne, says that a separated morphism of finite type between quasi-compact and [quasi-separated](wikipedia://en/28281191) schemes factors as an open immersion followed by a proper morphism.[^o15]
- Proper morphisms between locally noetherian schemes preserve [coherent sheaves](wikipedia://en/603780), in the sense that the [higher direct images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_direct_image) *R<sup>i</sup>f*<sub>∗</sub>(*F*) (in particular the [direct image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_image) *f*<sub>∗</sub>(*F*)) of a coherent sheaf *F* are coherent (EGA III, 3.2.1). (Analogously, for a proper map between complex analytic spaces, [Grauert](wikipedia://en/2979031) and [Remmert](wikipedia://en/856331) showed that the higher direct images preserve coherent analytic sheaves.) As a very special case: the ring of regular functions on a proper scheme *X* over a field *k* has finite dimension as a *k*-vector space. By contrast, the ring of regular functions on the affine line over *k* is the polynomial ring *k*\[*x*\], which does not have finite dimension as a *k*-vector space.
- There is also a slightly stronger statement of this:(EGA III, 3.2.4) let $f\colon X \to S$ be a morphism of finite type, *S* locally noetherian and $F$ a $\mathcal{O}_X$-module. If the support of *F* is proper over *S*, then for each $i \ge 0$ the [higher direct image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_direct_image) $R^i f_* F$ is coherent.
- For a scheme *X* of finite type over the complex numbers, the set *X*(**C**) of complex points is a [complex analytic space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_analytic_space), using the classical (Euclidean) topology. For *X* and *Y* separated and of finite type over **C**, a morphism *f*: *X* → *Y* over **C** is proper if and only if the continuous map *f*: *X*(**C**) → *Y*(**C**) is proper in the sense that the inverse image of every compact set is compact.[^o17]
- If *f*: *X*→*Y* and *g*: *Y*→*Z* are such that *gf* is proper and *g* is separated, then *f* is proper. This can for example be easily proven using the following criterion.

## Valuative criterion of properness {#Valuative_criterion_of_properness}

There is a very intuitive criterion for properness which goes back to [Chevalley](wikipedia://en/366040). It is commonly called the **valuative criterion of properness**. Let *f*: *X* → *Y* be a morphism of finite type of [Noetherian schemes](wikipedia://en/2783891). Then *f* is proper if and only if for all [discrete valuation rings](wikipedia://en/848633) *R* with [fraction field](wikipedia://en/25271) *K* and for any *K*-valued point *x* ∈ *X*(*K*) that maps to a point *f*(*x*) that is defined over *R*, there is a unique lift of *x* to $\overline{x} \in X(R)$. (EGA II, 7.3.8). More generally, a quasi-separated morphism *f*: *X* → *Y* of finite type (note: finite type includes quasi-compact) of 'any' schemes *X*, *Y* is proper if and only if for all [valuation rings](wikipedia://en/966856) *R* with [fraction field](wikipedia://en/25271) *K* and for any *K*-valued point *x* ∈ *X*(*K*) that maps to a point *f*(*x*) that is defined over *R*, there is a unique lift of *x* to $\overline{x} \in X(R)$. (Stacks project Tags 01KF and 01KY). Noting that *Spec K* is the [generic point](wikipedia://en/3043978) of *Spec R* and discrete valuation rings are precisely the [regular](wikipedia://en/506959) [local](wikipedia://en/153391) one-dimensional rings, one may rephrase the criterion: given a regular curve on *Y* (corresponding to the morphism *s*: Spec *R* → *Y*) and given a lift of the generic point of this curve to *X*, *f* is proper if and only if there is exactly one way to complete the curve.

Similarly, *f* is separated if and only if in every such diagram, there is at most one lift $\overline{x} \in X(R)$.

For example, given the valuative criterion, it becomes easy to check that projective space **P**<sup>*n*</sup> is proper over a field (or even over **Z**). One simply observes that for a discrete valuation ring *R* with fraction field *K*, every *K*-point \[*x*<sub>0</sub>,...,*x*<sub>*n*</sub>\] of projective space comes from an *R*-point, by scaling the coordinates so that all lie in *R* and at least one is a unit in *R*.

### Geometric interpretation with disks {#Geometric_interpretation_with_disks}

One of the motivating examples for the valuative criterion of properness is the interpretation of $\text{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[[t]])$ as an infinitesimal disk, or complex-analytically, as the disk $\Delta = \{x \in \mathbb{C} : |x| < 1 \}$. This comes from the fact that every power series

> $$f(t) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_nt^n$$

converges in some disk of radius $r$ around the origin. Then, using a change of coordinates, this can be expressed as a power series on the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738). Then, if we invert $t$, this is the ring $\mathbb{C}[[t]][t^{-1}] = \mathbb{C}((t))$ which are the power series which may have a pole at the origin. This is represented topologically as the open disk $\Delta^* = \{x \in \mathbb{C} : 0<|x| < 1 \}$ with the origin removed. For a morphism of schemes over $\text{Spec}(\mathbb{C})$, this is given by the commutative diagram

> $$\begin{matrix} \Delta^* & \to & X \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ \Delta & \to & Y \end{matrix}$$

Then, the valuative criterion for properness would be a filling in of the point $0 \in \Delta$ in the image of $\Delta^*$.

::: {#o22 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

#### Example {#Example}

It's instructive to look at a counter-example to see why the valuative criterion of properness should hold on spaces analogous to closed compact manifolds. If we take $X = \mathbb{P}^1 - \{x \}$ and $Y = \text{Spec}(\mathbb{C})$, then a morphism $\text{Spec}(\mathbb{C}((t))) \to X$ factors through an affine chart of $X$, reducing the diagram to

> $$\begin{matrix} \text{Spec}(\mathbb{C}((t))) & \to & \text{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]) \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ \text{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[[t]]) & \to & \text{Spec}(\mathbb{C}) \end{matrix}$$

where $\text{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]) = \mathbb{A}^1 - \{0\}$ is the chart centered around $\{x \}$ on $X$. This gives the [commutative diagram](wikipedia://en/138484) of commutative algebras

> $$\begin{matrix} \mathbb{C}((t)) & \leftarrow & \mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}] \\ \uparrow & & \uparrow \\ \mathbb{C}[[t]] & \leftarrow & \mathbb{C} \end{matrix}$$

Then, a lifting of the diagram of schemes, $\text{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[[t]]) \to \text{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}])$, would imply there is a morphism $\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}] \to \mathbb{C}[[t]]$ sending $t \mapsto t$ from the commutative diagram of algebras. This, of course, cannot happen. Therefore $X$ is not proper over $Y$.

:::

### Geometric interpretation with curves {#Geometric_interpretation_with_curves}

There is another similar example of the valuative criterion of properness which captures some of the intuition for why this theorem should hold. Consider a curve $C$ and the complement of a point $C-\{p\}$. Then the valuative criterion for properness would read as a diagram

> $$\begin{matrix} C-\{p\} & \rightarrow & X \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ C & \rightarrow & Y \end{matrix}$$

with a lifting of $C \to X$. Geometrically this means every curve in the scheme $X$ can be completed to a compact curve. This bit of intuition aligns with what the scheme-theoretic interpretation of a morphism of topological spaces with compact fibers, that a sequence in one of the fibers must converge. Because this geometric situation is a problem locally, the diagram is replaced by looking at the local ring $\mathcal{O}_{C,\mathfrak{p}}$, which is a DVR, and its fraction field $\text{Frac}(\mathcal{O}_{C,\mathfrak{p}})$. Then, the lifting problem then gives the commutative diagram

> $$\begin{matrix} \text{Spec}(\text{Frac}(\mathcal{O}_{C,\mathfrak{p}}) ) & \rightarrow & X \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ \text{Spec}(\mathcal{O}_{C,\mathfrak{p}} ) & \rightarrow & Y \end{matrix}$$

where the scheme $\text{Spec}(\text{Frac}(\mathcal{O}_{C,\mathfrak{p}}))$ represents a local disk around $\mathfrak{p}$ with the [closed point](wikipedia://en/33086408) $\mathfrak{p}$ removed.

:::

## Proper morphism of formal schemes {#Proper_morphism_of_formal_schemes}

Let $f\colon \mathfrak{X} \to \mathfrak{S}$ be a morphism between [locally noetherian formal schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_noetherian_formal_scheme). We say *f* is **proper** or $\mathfrak{X}$ is **proper** over $\mathfrak{S}$ if (i) *f* is an [adic morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adic_morphism) (i.e., maps the ideal of definition to the ideal of definition) and (ii) the induced map $f_0\colon X_0 \to S_0$ is proper, where $X_0 = (\mathfrak{X}, \mathcal{O}_\mathfrak{X}/I), S_0 = (\mathfrak{S}, \mathcal{O}_\mathfrak{S}/K), I = f^*(K) \mathcal{O}_\mathfrak{X}$ and *K* is the ideal of definition of $\mathfrak{S}$.(EGA III, 3.4.1) The definition is independent of the choice of *K*.

For example, if *g*: *Y* → *Z* is a proper morphism of locally noetherian schemes, *Z*<sub>0</sub> is a closed subset of *Z*, and *Y*<sub>0</sub> is a closed subset of *Y* such that *g*(*Y*<sub>0</sub>) ⊂ *Z*<sub>0</sub>, then the morphism $\widehat{g}\colon Y_{/Y_0} \to Z_{/Z_0}$ on formal completions is a proper morphism of formal schemes.

Grothendieck proved the coherence theorem in this setting. Namely, let $f\colon \mathfrak{X} \to \mathfrak{S}$ be a proper morphism of locally noetherian formal schemes. If *F* is a coherent sheaf on $\mathfrak{X}$, then the higher direct images $R^i f_* F$ are coherent.[^o26]
