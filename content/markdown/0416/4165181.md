# Supporting hyperplane

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Supporting hyperplane"}

*Image: Supporting hyperplane1.svg.* A [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292) $S$ (in pink), a supporting hyperplane of $S$ (the dashed line), and the supporting half-space delimited by the hyperplane which contains $S$ (in light blue).

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **supporting hyperplane** of a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $S$ in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb R^n$ is a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) that has both of the following two properties:[^o2]

:::

- $S$ is entirely contained in one of the two [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) [half-spaces](wikipedia://en/647297) bounded by the hyperplane,
- $S$ has at least one boundary-point on the hyperplane.

Here, a closed half-space is the half-space that includes the points within the hyperplane.

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Supporting hyperplane theorem"}

## Supporting hyperplane theorem {#Supporting_hyperplane_theorem}

*Image: Supporting hyperplane2.svg.* A convex set can have more than one supporting hyperplane at a given point on its boundary.

*Image: Supporting hyperplane3.svg.* A supporting hyperplane containing a given point on the boundary of $S$ may not exist if $S$ is not convex.

This [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) states that if $S$ is a [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292) in the [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) $X=\mathbb{R}^n,$ and $x_0$ is a point on the [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) of $S,$ then there exists a supporting hyperplane containing $x_0.$ If $x^* \in X^* \backslash \{0\}$ ($X^*$ is the [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) of $X$, $x^*$ is a nonzero [linear functional](wikipedia://en/214137)) such that $x^*\left(x_0\right) \geq x^*(x)$ for all $x \in S$, then

$$H = \{x \in X: x^*(x) = x^*\left(x_0\right)\}$$

defines a supporting hyperplane.[^o5]

Conversely, if $S$ is a [closed set](wikipedia://en/47279) with nonempty [interior](wikipedia://en/55610) such that every point on the boundary has a supporting hyperplane, then $S$ is a convex set, and is the intersection of all its supporting closed half-spaces.[^o5]

The hyperplane in the theorem may not be unique, as noticed in the second picture on the right. If the closed set $S$ is not convex, the statement of the theorem is not true at all points on the boundary of $S,$ as illustrated in the third picture on the right.

The supporting hyperplanes of convex sets are also called **tac-planes** or **tac-hyperplanes**.[^o6]

The forward direction can be proved as a special case of the [separating hyperplane theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separating_hyperplane_theorem) (see [the page for the proof](wikipedia://en/4739827#Case_with_possible_intersections)). For the converse direction,

::: {#o7 .proof cue=math-proof name="Proof"}

**Proof.**

Define $T$ to be the intersection of all its supporting closed half-spaces. Clearly $S \subset T$. Now let $y\not \in S$, show $y \not\in T$.

Let $x\in \mathrm{int}(S)$, and consider the line segment $[x, y]$. Let $t$ be the largest number such that $[x, t(y-x) + x]$ is contained in $S$. Then $t\in (0, 1)$.

Let $b = t(y-x) + x$, then $b\in \partial S$. Draw a supporting hyperplane across $b$. Let it be represented as a nonzero linear functional $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ such that $\forall a\in T, f(a) \geq f(b)$. Then since $x\in \mathrm{int}(S)$, we have $f(x) > f(b)$. Thus by $\frac{f(y) - f(b)}{1-t} = \frac{f(b) - f(x)}{t-0} < 0$, we have $f(y) < f(b)$, so $y \not\in T$.

:::
