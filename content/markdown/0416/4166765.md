# Split-biquaternion

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Split-biquaternion"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **split-biquaternion** is a [hypercomplex number](wikipedia://en/51438) of the form

:::

$$q = w + x\mathrm{i} + y\mathrm{j} + z\mathrm{k} ,$$

where *w*, *x*, *y*, and *z* are [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559) and i, j, and k multiply as in the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130). Since each [coefficient](wikipedia://en/51203) *w*, *x*, *y*, *z* spans two [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [dimensions](wikipedia://en/8398), the split-biquaternion is an element of an eight-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370). Considering that it carries a multiplication, this vector space is an [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) over the real field, or an [algebra over a ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_over_a_ring) where the split-complex numbers form the ring. This algebra was introduced by [William Kingdon Clifford](wikipedia://en/250820) in an 1873 article for the [London Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/591097). It has been repeatedly noted in mathematical literature since then, variously as a deviation in terminology, an illustration of the [tensor product of algebras](wikipedia://en/372395), and as an illustration of the [direct sum of algebras](wikipedia://en/58899#Direct_sum_of_algebras). The split-biquaternions have been identified in various ways by algebraists; see below.

## Modern definition {#Modern_definition}

A split-biquaternion is [ring isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_isomorphism) to the [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305) Cl<sub>0,3</sub>(**R**). This is the [geometric algebra](wikipedia://en/12939) generated by three orthogonal imaginary unit basis directions, $\{e_{1}, e_{2}, e_{3}\}$ under the combination rule

$$e_i e_j = \begin{cases} -1 & i=j, \\ - e_j e_i & i \neq j \end{cases}$$

giving an algebra spanned by the 8 basis elements $\{1, e_{1}, e_{2}, e_{3}, e_{1}e_{2}, e_{2}e_{3}, e_{3}e_{1}, e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}\}$, with (*e*<sub>1</sub>*e*<sub>2</sub>)<sup>2</sup> = (*e*<sub>2</sub>*e*<sub>3</sub>)<sup>2</sup> = (*e*<sub>3</sub>*e*<sub>1</sub>)<sup>2</sup> = −1 and ω<sup>2</sup> = (*e*<sub>1</sub>*e*<sub>2</sub>*e*<sub>3</sub>)<sup>2</sup> = +1. The sub-algebra spanned by the 4 elements $\{1, i = e_{1}, j = e_{2}, k = e_{1}e_{2}\}$ is the [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) of Hamilton's [quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternions), **H** = Cl<sub>0,2</sub>(**R**). One can therefore see that

$$\mathrm{Cl}_{0,3}(\mathbf{R}) \cong \mathbf{H} \otimes \mathbf{D}$$

where **D** = Cl<sub>1,0</sub>(**R**) is the algebra spanned by $\{1, \omega\}$, the algebra of the [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559). Equivalently,

$$\mathrm{Cl}_{0,3}(\mathbf{R}) \cong \mathbf{H} \oplus \mathbf{H}.$$

## Split-biquaternion group {#Split-biquaternion_group}

*Image: Common\_associative\_algebras\_over\_the\_reals.svg.* The basis elements of split-biquaternions generate the group Q<sub>8</sub> x C<sub>2</sub>

The split-biquaternions form an [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity) [ring](wikipedia://en/250424) as is clear from considering multiplications in its [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) $\{1, \omega, i, j, k, \omega i, \omega j, \omega k\}$. When [hyperbolic unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_unit) ω is adjoined to the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130) one obtains a 16 element group

( {1, i, j, k, −1, −i, −j, −k, ω, ωi, ωj, ωk, −ω, −ωi, −ωj, −ωk}, × ),

which is the internal [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of the quaternion group and the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) {1, ω} of order 2.

## Module {#Module}

Since elements $\{1, i, j, k\}$ of the quaternion group can be taken as a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) of the space of split-biquaternions, it may be compared to a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370). But split-complex numbers form a ring, not a field, so *vector space* is not appropriate. Rather the space of split-biquaternions forms a [free module](wikipedia://en/326454). This standard term of [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424) expresses a similarity to a vector space, and this structure by Clifford in 1873 is an instance. Split-biquaternions form an [algebra over a ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_over_a_ring), but not a [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223).

## Direct sum of two quaternion rings {#Direct_sum_of_two_quaternion_rings}

The direct sum of the division ring of quaternions with itself is denoted $\mathbf{H} \oplus \mathbf{H}$. The product of two elements $(a \oplus b)$ and $(c \oplus d)$ is $a c \oplus b d$ in this [direct sum algebra](wikipedia://en/58899#Direct_sum_of_algebras).

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=inline-head name="Proposition"}

**Proposition:** The algebra of split-biquaternions is isomorphic to $\mathbf{H} \oplus \mathbf{H}.$

proof: Every split-biquaternion has an expression *q* = *w* + *z* ω where *w* and *z* are quaternions and ω<sup>2</sup> = +1. Now if *p* = *u* + *v* ω is another split-biquaternion, their product is

$$pq = uw + vz + (uz + vw) \omega .$$

The isomorphism mapping from split-biquaternions to $\mathbf{H} \oplus \mathbf{H}$ is given by

$$p \mapsto (u + v) \oplus (u - v) , \quad q \mapsto (w + z) \oplus (w - z).$$

In $\mathbf{H} \oplus \mathbf{H}$, the product of these images, according to the algebra-product of $\mathbf{H} \oplus \mathbf{H}$ indicated above, is

$$(u + v)(w + z) \oplus (u - v)(w - z).$$

This element is also the image of pq under the mapping into $\mathbf{H} \oplus \mathbf{H}.$ Thus the products agree, the mapping is a homomorphism; and since it is [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective), it is an isomorphism.

Though split-biquaternions form an [eight-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/25555117) like Hamilton's biquaternions, on the basis of the Proposition it is apparent that this algebra splits into the direct sum of two copies of the real quaternions.

:::

## Hamilton biquaternion {#Hamilton_biquaternion}

The split-biquaternions should not be confused with the (ordinary) biquaternions previously introduced by [William Rowan Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817). Hamilton's [biquaternions](wikipedia://en/1207070) are elements of the algebra

$$\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\mathbf{C}) = \mathbf{H} \otimes \mathbf{C}.$$

$$\mathrm{Cl}_{3,0}(\mathbf{R}) = \mathbf{H} \otimes \mathbf{C}.$$

## Synonyms {#Synonyms}

The following terms and compounds refer to the split-biquaternion algebra:

- elliptic biquaternions – (Clifford 1873), (Rooney 2007)
- Clifford biquaternion – (Joly 1905), (van der Waerden 1985)
- dyquaternions – (Rosenfeld 1997)
- $\mathbf{D} \otimes \mathbf{H}$ where **D** = [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559) – (Bourbaki 2013), (Rosenfeld 1997)
- $\mathbf{H} \oplus \mathbf{H}$, the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899#Direct_sum_of_algebras) of two quaternion algebras – (van der Waerden 1985)
