# Unitary matrix

matrices with orthogonality over the real number field

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unitary matrix"}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), an [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122) [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [square matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $U$ is **unitary** if its [matrix inverse](wikipedia://en/217122) $U^{-1}$ equals its [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) $U^{*}$, that is, if

:::

$$U^* U = UU^* = I,$$

where $I$ is the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718).

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), especially in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), the conjugate transpose is referred to as the [Hermitian adjoint](wikipedia://en/667175) of a matrix and is denoted by a [dagger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagger_%28mark%29) ($\dagger$), so the equation above is written

$$U^\dagger U = UU^\dagger = I.$$

A complex matrix $U$ is **special unitary** if it is unitary and its [matrix determinant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_determinant) equals $1$.

For [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), the analogue of a unitary matrix is an [orthogonal matrix](wikipedia://en/105620). Unitary matrices have significant importance in quantum mechanics because they preserve the [normalization](wikipedia://en/990534) of [state vectors](wikipedia://en/30876419) and the [inner products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) between them.[^o2][^o3]

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

For any unitary matrix $U$ of finite size, the following hold:

- Given two complex vectors $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$, multiplication by $U$ preserves their [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product); that is, $\langle U\mathbf{x}, U\mathbf{y}\rangle = \langle\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}\rangle$.
- $U$ is [normal](wikipedia://en/170366) ($U^* U = UU^*$).
- $U$ is [diagonalizable](wikipedia://en/246325); that is, $U$ is [unitarily similar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similar_matrix) to a diagonal matrix, as a consequence of the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495). Thus, $U$ has a decomposition of the form $U = VDV^*,$ where $V$ is unitary, and $D$ is diagonal and unitary.
- The [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) of $U$ lie on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/5826#Unit). That is, if the complex number $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $U$ then $\lvert \lambda\rvert = 1$.
- $|\det(U)| = 1$[^o6]
- The [eigenspaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenspace) of $U$ are orthogonal.
- $U$ can be written as $U = e^{iH}$, where $e$ indicates the [matrix exponential](wikipedia://en/672731), $i$ is the imaginary unit, and $H$ is a [Hermitian matrix](wikipedia://en/189682).

For any nonnegative [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $n$, the set of all $n\,\times\,n$ unitary matrices with matrix multiplication forms a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), called the [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) $U(n)$.

Every square matrix with unit Euclidean norm is the average of two unitary matrices.[^o7]

:::

## Equivalent conditions {#Equivalent_conditions}

If *U* is a square, complex matrix, then the following conditions are equivalent:[^o9]

1. $U$ is unitary.
1. $U^*$ is unitary.
1. $U$ is invertible with $U^{-1} = U^*$.
1. The columns of $U$ form an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) of $\mathbb{C}^n$ with respect to the usual inner product. In other words, $U^*U = I$.
1. The rows of $U$ form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{C}^n$ with respect to the usual inner product. In other words, $UU^* = I$.
1. $U$ is an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) with respect to the usual norm. That is, $\|Ux\|_2 = \|x\|_2$ for all $x \in \mathbb{C}^n$, where $\|x\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n |x_i|^2}$.
1. $U$ is a [normal matrix](wikipedia://en/170366) (equivalently, there is an orthonormal basis formed by eigenvectors of $U$) with eigenvalues lying on the unit circle.

## Elementary constructions {#Elementary_constructions}

### 2 × 2 unitary matrix {#2_×_2_unitary_matrix}

One general expression of a $\times$ unitary matrix is

$$U = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ -e^{i\varphi} b^* & e^{i\varphi} a^* \\ \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \left| a \right|^2 + \left| b \right|^2 = 1\ ,$$

which depends on 4 real parameters (the phase of $a$, the phase of $b$, the relative magnitude between $a$ and $b$, and the angle $\varphi$) and \* is the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918). The form is configured so the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of such a matrix is

$$\det(U) = e^{i \varphi} ~.$$

The sub-group of those elements $U$ with $\det(U) = 1$ is called the [special unitary group](wikipedia://en/173997#The_group_SU%282%29) SU(2).

Among several alternative forms, the matrix $U$ can be written in this form:

$$\ U = e^{i\varphi / 2} \begin{bmatrix} e^{i\alpha} \cos \theta & e^{i\beta} \sin \theta \\ -e^{-i\beta} \sin \theta & e^{-i\alpha} \cos \theta \\ \end{bmatrix}\ ,$$

where $e^{i\alpha} \cos \theta = a$ and $e^{i\beta} \sin \theta = b,$ above, and the angles $\varphi, \alpha, \beta, \theta$ can take any values.

By introducing $\alpha = \psi + \delta$ and $\beta = \psi - \delta,$ has the following factorization:

$$U = e^{i\varphi /2} \begin{bmatrix} e^{i\psi} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{-i\psi} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \cos \theta & \sin \theta \\ -\sin \theta & \cos \theta \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} e^{i\delta} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{-i\delta} \end{bmatrix} ~.$$

This expression highlights the relation between $\times$ unitary matrices and $\times$ [orthogonal matrices](wikipedia://en/105620) of angle $\theta$.

Another factorization is[^o12]

$$U = \begin{bmatrix} \cos \rho & -\sin \rho \\ \sin \rho & \;\cos \rho \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} e^{i\xi} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\zeta} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \;\cos \sigma & \sin \sigma \\ -\sin \sigma & \cos \sigma \\ \end{bmatrix} ~.$$

Many other factorizations of a unitary matrix in basic matrices are possible.[^o13][^o14][^o15][^o16][^o17][^o18]
