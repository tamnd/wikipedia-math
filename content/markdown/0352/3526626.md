# Bernstein's theorem on monotone functions

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bernstein's theorem on monotone functions"}

In [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Bernstein's theorem**, named after [Sergei Bernstein](wikipedia://en/976516), states that every [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued [function](wikipedia://en/185427) on the half-line 0, ∞ that is **[completely monotone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_monotone_function)** is a mixture of [exponential functions](wikipedia://en/9678) or in more abstract language, that it is the [Laplace transform](wikipedia://en/18610) of a positive [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322) on 0, ∞. In one important special case the mixture is a [weighted average](wikipedia://en/189822), or [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653). It is also known as the **Bernstein–Widder theorem** or **Hausdorff–Bernstein–Widder theorem**.

:::

## History {#History}

The result was first proved by Bernstein in 1928,[^o3] and similar results were discussed by [David Widder](wikipedia://en/14428679) in 1931[^o4] who refers to Bernstein but states that "The author had completed the proof of this theorem a few months after the publication of Bernstein's paper without being aware of its existence". The most cited reference is the 1941 book by Widder called The Laplace Transform.[^o5][^o6] Later a simpler proof[^o7][^o8][^o9] was given by [Boris Korenblum](wikipedia://en/56609708). At around the same time [Gustave Choquet](wikipedia://en/17301193) studied the much more general concept of monotone functions on [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799) and gave a more abstract proof[^o10][^o11][^o12][^o17][^o13] based on the [Krein–Milman theorem](wikipedia://en/3206654). [Felix Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/10989) had earlier characterised [completely monotone sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_monotonicity). These are the sequences occurring in the [Hausdorff moment problem](wikipedia://en/3216387).

::: {#o15 .statement cue=heading name="Statement of the theorem"}

## Statement of the theorem {#Statement_of_the_theorem}

*Complete monotonicity* of a function $f$ means that $f$ is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) on 0, ∞, [infinitely differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitely_differentiable) on 0, ∞, and satisfies

$$(-1)^n \frac{d^n}{dt^n} f(t) \geq 0$$

for all nonnegative [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $n$ and for all $t > 0$.

The "weighted average" statement can be characterized thus: there is a non-negative finite [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322) on 0, ∞ with [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) $g$ such that

$$f(t) = \int_0^\infty e^{-tx} \, dg(x),$$

the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) being a [Riemann–Stieltjes integral](wikipedia://en/330491).

:::

## Bernstein functions {#Bernstein_functions}

Nonnegative functions whose [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) is completely monotone are called *Bernstein functions*.[^o17] Every Bernstein function has the [Lévy–Khintchine representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lévy–Khintchine_representation):

$$f(t) = a + bt + \int_0^\infty \left(1 - e^{-tx}\right) \mu(dx),$$

where $a,b \geq 0$ and $\mu$ is a measure on the positive real half-line such that

$$\int_0^\infty \left(1\wedge x\right) \mu(dx) < \infty.$$

::: {#o19 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Schoenberg–Williamson theorem"}

## Schoenberg–Williamson theorem {#Schoenberg–Williamson_theorem}

The Schoenberg–Williamson theorem (also called Schoenberg's theorem on multiply monotone functions, Williamson's representation theorem) is the finite-order version of Bernstein's theorem. A k-monotone (or k-times monotone) function satisfies

$$(-1)^n \frac{d^n}{dt^n} f(t) \geq 0 \quad\text{for}\,\, \, 0 \leq n \leq k$$

The Schoenberg–Williamson theorem says that f is k-monotone on 0, ∞ if and only if

$$f(t)=\int_0^\infty (x-t)_+^{k-1} \, d\mu(x)$$

for some positive measure $\mu$ on 0, ∞.

The proof was published by Williamson in 1956[^o20] but in his paper he mentions that "This formula was discovered by Schoenberg in 1940 but has remained unpublished".

Using the Taylor series of $f$ with integral remainder, a more precise formula can be given[^o21]

$$f(t) = \int_0^\infty \left(1-\frac{tx}{n}\right)_+^{n-1} f_n(x) \, dx$$

with

$$f_n(x) = \frac{(-1)^n}{n!} \left(\frac{n}{x}\right)^{n+1} f^{(n)} \left(\frac{n}{x}\right) I(x>0)$$

where $f^{(n)}(t) = \frac{d^n}{dt^n} f(t)$ and $I(x>0)$ is the indicator function of $\mathbb{R}_+^*$.

Note then that if $f$ is completely monotone then it is k-monotone for all $k \in \mathbb{N}$ and the [Post–Widder inversion formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post_inversion_formula) states that $f_n$converge in distribution to $\mu$ and $\left(1-\frac{tx}{n}\right)_+^{n-1}$ converges to $e^{-tx}$as $n$ goes to infinity, and we recover Bernstein's theorem.[^o21]

:::
