# Rose (topology)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rose"}

*Image: Topological Rose.png.* A rose with four petals.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **rose** (also known as a **bouquet of *n* circles**) is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) obtained by [gluing](wikipedia://en/237213) together a collection of [circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_%28topology%29) along a single point. The circles of the rose are called **petals**. Roses are important in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), where they are closely related to [free groups](wikipedia://en/59735).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: Marked 2-rose.svg.* The [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of the figure eight is the [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) generated by $a$ and $b$

A rose is a [wedge sum](wikipedia://en/684143) of [circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_%28topology%29). That is, the rose is the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) *C*/*S*, where *C* is a disjoint union of circles and *S* a set consisting of one point from each circle. As a [cell complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_complex), a rose has a single vertex, and one edge for each circle. This makes it a simple example of a [topological graph](wikipedia://en/37439184).

A rose with *n* petals can also be obtained by identifying *n* points on a single circle. The rose with two petals is known as the **figure eight**.

:::

## Relation to free groups {#Relation_to_free_groups}

*Image: Hyperbolic Free Group.png.* The [universal cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_cover) of the figure eight can be visualized by the Cayley graph of the [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) on two generators $a$ and $b$

The [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of a rose is [free](wikipedia://en/59735), with one [generator](wikipedia://en/99945) for each petal. The [universal cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_cover) is an infinite tree, which can be identified with the [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of the free group. (This is a special case of the [presentation complex](wikipedia://en/12821736) associated to any [presentation of a group](wikipedia://en/99494).)

The intermediate [covers](wikipedia://en/250323) of the rose correspond to [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) of the free group. The observation that any cover of a rose is a [graph](wikipedia://en/37439184) provides a simple proof that every subgroup of a free group is free (the [Nielsen–Schreier theorem](wikipedia://en/18401725))

Because the universal cover of a rose is [contractible](wikipedia://en/1752414), the rose is actually an [Eilenberg–MacLane space](wikipedia://en/1073230) for the associated free group *F*. This implies that the [cohomology](wikipedia://en/248635) groups *H<sup>n</sup>*(*F*) are trivial for *n* ≥ 2.

## Other properties {#Other_properties}

*Image: Torus cycles.png.* A figure eight in the [torus](wikipedia://en/74800).

- Any [connected graph](wikipedia://en/1614492) is [homotopy equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_equivalent) to a rose. Specifically, the rose is the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) of the graph obtained by collapsing a [spanning tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanning_tree_%28mathematics%29).
- A [disc](wikipedia://en/160556) with *n* points removed (or a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) with *n* + 1 points removed) [deformation retracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_retract) onto a rose with *n* petals. One petal of the rose surrounds each of the removed points.
- A [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) with one point removed deformation retracts onto a figure eight, namely the union of two generating circles. More generally, a surface of [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) *g* with one point removed deformation retracts onto a rose with 2*g* petals, namely the boundary of a [fundamental polygon](wikipedia://en/1668732).
- A rose can have infinitely many petals, leading to a fundamental group which is free on infinitely many generators. The rose with countably infinitely many petals is similar to the [Hawaiian earring](wikipedia://en/910263): there is a continuous bijection from this rose onto the Hawaiian earring, but the two are not [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic). A rose with infinitely many petals is not compact, whereas the Hawaiian earring is compact.
