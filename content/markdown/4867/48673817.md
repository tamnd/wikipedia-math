# Chamberlain's approach to unobserved effects models

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Chamberlain's approach to unobserved effects models"}

In linear [panel analysis](wikipedia://en/2369609), it can be desirable to estimate the magnitude of the [fixed effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_effects), as they provide measures of the [unobserved components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latent_variable). For instance, in [wage equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wage_equation) regressions, fixed effects capture [unobservables](wikipedia://en/2649330) that are constant over time, such as motivation. [Chamberlain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Chamberlain)'s approach to unobserved effects models is a way of estimating the linear unobserved effects, under fixed effect (rather than [random effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_effects)) assumptions, in the following unobserved effects model

:::

$$y_{it} = x_{it} b + c_i + u_{it}$$

where *c<sub>i</sub>* is the unobserved effect and *x<sub>it</sub>* contains only time-varying [explanatory variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explanatory_variables).[^o2] Rather than [differencing out](wikipedia://en/34422496) the unobserved effect *c<sub>i</sub>*, Chamberlain proposed to replace it with the [linear projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_projection) of it onto the explanatory variables in all time periods. Specifically, this leads to the following equation

$$c_i = d + x_{i1} \lambda_1 + x_{i2} \lambda_2 + \dots + x_{iT} \lambda_T + e_i$$

where the [conditional distribution](wikipedia://en/504458) of *c<sub>i</sub>* given *x<sub>it</sub>* is unspecified, as is standard in fixed effects models. Combining these equations then gives rise to the following model.[^o3][^o4]

$$y_{it} = d + x_{i1} \lambda_1 + \dots + x_{it} (b + \lambda_t) + \dots + x_{iT} \lambda_T + e_i + u_{it}$$

An important advantage of this approach is the computational requirement. Chamberlain uses [minimum distance estimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_distance_estimation), but a [generalized method of moments](wikipedia://en/1975550) approach would be another valid way of estimating this model. The latter approach also gives rise to a larger number of [instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_variable) than moment conditions, which leads to useful [overidentifying restrictions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overidentifying_restrictions) that can be used to test the [strict exogeneity restrictions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strict_exogeneity_restrictions) imposed by many static Fixed Effects models.[^o2]

Similar approaches have been proposed to model the unobserved effect. For instance, Mundlak follows a very similar approach, but rather projects the unobserved effect *c<sub>i</sub>* onto the average of all *x<sub>it</sub>* across all *T* time periods, more specifically [^o5]

$$c_i = d + \overline{x}_i \lambda + e_i$$

It can be shown that the Chamberlain method is a generalization of Mundlak's model. The Chamberlain method has been popular in [empirical work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_economics), ranging from studies trying to estimate the causal returns to [union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_union) membership,[^o6] to studies investigating [growth convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growth_convergence),[^o7] and estimating product characteristics in [demand estimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supply_and_demand).[^o8]
