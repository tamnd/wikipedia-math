# Unobserved heterogeneity in duration models

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unobserved heterogeneity in duration models"}

Issues of [heterogeneity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterogeneity) in [duration models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duration_models) can take on different forms. On the one hand, unobserved heterogeneity can play a crucial role when it comes to different [sampling methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampling_methods), such as [stock](wikipedia://en/54248805) or [flow sampling](wikipedia://en/53547121).[^o2] On the other hand, duration models have also been extended to allow for different [subpopulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subpopulations), with a strong link to [mixture models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixture_models). Many of these models impose the assumptions that heterogeneity is [independent](wikipedia://en/27593) of the observed [covariates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariates), it has a [distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) that depends on a finite number of [parameters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameters) only, and it enters the [hazard function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazard_function) [multiplicatively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicatively).[^o3]

:::

One can define the conditional hazard as the hazard function conditional on the observed covariates and the unobserved heterogeneity.[^o4] In the general case, the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) of *t<sub>i</sub>\** associated with the conditional hazard is given by *F(t|x<sub>i</sub> , v<sub>i</sub> ; θ)*. Under the first assumption above, the unobserved component can be integrated out and we obtain the cumulative distribution on the observed covariates only, i.e.

*G(t ∨ x<sub>i</sub> ; θ , ρ) = ∫ F (t ∨ x<sub>i</sub>, ν ; θ ) h ( ν ; ρ ) dν* [^o5]

where the additional parameter ρ parameterizes the density of the unobserved component *v*. Now, the different estimation methods for stock or flow sampling data are available to estimate the relevant parameters.

A specific example is described by Lancaster. Assume that the conditional hazard is given by

*λ(t ; x<sub>i</sub> , v<sub>i</sub> ) = v<sub>i</sub> exp (x [^o6] β) α t <sup>α-1</sup>*

where *x* is a vector of observed characteristics, *v* is the unobserved heterogeneity part, and a [normalization](wikipedia://en/2978513) (often E\[*v<sub>i</sub>*\] = 1) needs to be imposed. It then follows that the average hazard is given by exp(x'β) αt<sup>α-1</sup>. More generally, it can be shown that as long as the hazard function exhibits proportional properties of the form *λ ( t ; x<sub>i</sub>, v<sub>i</sub> ) = v<sub>i</sub> κ (x<sub>i</sub> ) λ<sub>0</sub> (t)*, one can identify both the covariate function *κ*(.) and the hazard function *λ*(.).[^o7]

Recent examples provide a [nonparametric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonparametric) approaches to estimating the baseline hazard and the distribution of the unobserved heterogeneity under fairly weak assumptions.[^o8] In [grouped data](wikipedia://en/27705422), the strict [exogeneity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exogeneity) assumptions for time-varying covariates are hard to relax. [Parametric](wikipedia://en/576108) forms can be imposed for the distribution of the unobserved heterogeneity,[^o9] even though [semiparametric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiparametric) methods that do not specify such parametric forms for the unobserved heterogeneity are available.[^o10]
