# Bailey–Borwein–Plouffe formula

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bailey–Borwein–Plouffe formula"}

The **Bailey–Borwein–Plouffe formula** (**BBP formula**) is a formula for [$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601). It was discovered in 1995 by [Simon Plouffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Plouffe) and is named after the authors of the article in which it was published, [David H. Bailey](wikipedia://en/184462), [Peter Borwein](wikipedia://en/1339736), and Plouffe.[^o2] The formula is:

:::

$$\pi = \sum_{k = 0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{1}{16^k} \left(\frac{4}{8k+1}-\frac{2}{8k+4}-\frac{1}{8k + 5}-\frac{1}{8k+6}\right)\right]$$

The BBP formula gives rise to a [spigot algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spigot_algorithm) for computing the *n*th [base-16](wikipedia://en/13263) (hexadecimal) digit of $\pi$ (and therefore also the *4n*th [binary digit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_digit) of $\pi$) without computing the preceding digits. This does *not* compute the *n*th decimal digit of $\pi$ (i.e., in base 10).[^o3] In 2022, Plouffe published a formula that allows for extracting the *n*th digit of $\pi$ in decimal, but it effectively requires computing the previous digits, as it takes the *n*th digit of an approximation.[^o4] BBP and BBP-inspired algorithms have been used in projects such as [PiHex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PiHex)[^o5] for calculating many digits of $\pi$ using [distributed computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_computing). The existence of this formula came as a surprise because it had been widely believed that computing the *n*th digit of $\pi$ is just as hard as computing the first *n* digits.[^o2]

Since its discovery, formulas of the general form:

$$\alpha = \sum_{k = 0}^\infty \left[ \frac{1}{b^k} \frac{p(k)}{q(k)} \right]$$

have been discovered for many other [irrational numbers](wikipedia://en/20647689) $\alpha$, where $p(k)$ and $q(k)$ are [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) with integer coefficients and $b \geq 2$ is an integer [base](wikipedia://en/701207). Formulas of this form are known as **BBP-type formulas**. Given a number $\alpha$, there is no known systematic algorithm for finding appropriate $p(k)$, $q(k)$, and $b$; such formulas are often discovered [experimentally](wikipedia://en/256363).

## Specializations {#Specializations}

A specialization of the general formula that has produced many results is:

$$P(s, b, m, A) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{1}{b^k} \sum_{j=1}^m \frac{a_j}{(mk+j)^s} \right],$$

where *s*, *b*, and *m* are integers, and $A = (a_1, a_2, \dots, a_m)$ is a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of integers. The *P* function leads to a compact notation for some solutions. For example, the original BBP formula:

$$\pi = \sum_{k = 0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{1}{16^k} \left(\frac{4}{8k+1}-\frac{2}{8k+4}-\frac{1}{8k + 5}-\frac{1}{8k+6}\right)\right]$$

can be written as:

$$\pi = P\bigl(1, 16, 8, (4, 0, 0, -2, -1, -1, 0, 0) \bigr).$$

### Previously known BBP-type formulae {#Previously_known_BBP-type_formulae}

Some of the simplest formulae of this type that were well known before BBP and for which the *P* function leads to a compact notation, are:

$$\begin{aligned} \ln\frac{10}{9} &= \frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{200} + \frac{1}{3\ 000} + \frac{1}{40\,000} + \frac{1}{500\,000} + \cdots \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{10^k \cdot k} = \frac{1}{10} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \left[ \frac{1}{10^k} \left( \frac{1}{k+1} \right) \right] \\ &= \frac{1}{10} P\bigl(1, 10, 1, (1) \bigr), \end{aligned}$$

$$\begin{aligned} \ln 2 &= \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2 \cdot 2^2} + \frac{1}{3 \cdot 2^3} + \frac{1}{4 \cdot 2^4} + \frac{1}{5 \cdot 2^5} + \cdots \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{2^k \cdot k} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \left[ \frac{1}{2^k} \left( \frac{1}{k + 1} \right) \right] \\ &= \frac{1}{2} P\bigl( 1, 2, 1, (1) \bigr). \end{aligned}$$

(In fact, this identity holds true for *a* > 1:

$\ln\frac{a}{a-1} = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{a^k \cdot k}$.)

Plouffe was also inspired by the [arctan power series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_tangent#Infinite_series) of the form (the *P* notation can be also generalized to the case where *b* is not an integer):

$$\begin{aligned} \arctan\frac{1}{b} &= \frac{1}{b} - \frac{1}{b^3 3} + \frac{1}{b^5 5} - \frac{1}{b^7 7} + \frac{1}{b^9 9} + \cdots \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^\infty \left[ \frac{1}{b^{k}} \frac{\sin\frac{k\pi}{2}}{k} \right] = \frac{1}{b} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \left[ \frac{1}{b^{4k}} \left( \frac{1}{4k+1} + \frac{-b^{-2}}{4k+3} \right) \right] \\ &= \frac{1}{b} P\left( 1, b^4, 4, \left(1, 0, -b^{-2}, 0\right) \right). \end{aligned}$$

In 2023, it was observed that Euler had discovered two BBP-type formulas.[^o9] The first, which was rediscovered in [^o10], is:

$$\pi=\sum_{k=0}^\infty{(-1)^k\over4^k}\left({2\over4k+1}+{2\over4k+2}+{1\over4k+3}\right)$$

and the second, more unwieldy but computationally efficient, from unravelling the series expansion of $\pi=8\arctan\frac13+4\arctan\frac17$:

$$\pi={1\over2^{26}}\sum_{k=0}^\infty{(-1)^k\over2^{30k}}\begin{bmatrix} {2^{27}\over20k+1}+{2^{25}\over12k+1}+{2^{25}\over10k+1}+{2^{24}\over20k+3}+{2^{22}\over6k+1}\\ -{2^{20}\over60k+15}-{2^{19}\over10k+3}-{2^{18}\over20k+7}-{2^{15}\over12k+5}\\ +{2^{15}\over20k+9}+{2^{12}\over30k+15}+{2^{12}\over20k+11}-{2^{10}\over12k+7}\\ -{2^9\over20k+13}-{2^7\over10k+7}-{2^5\over60k+45}+{2^3\over20k+17}\\ +{2^2\over6k+5}+{2\over10k+9}+{1\over12k+11}+{1\over20k+19} \end{bmatrix}$$

which obtains asymptotically 30 binary digits per 21 divisions, making it tantamount to the simpler [Bellard's formula](wikipedia://en/5957331)'s 10 per 7.

### The search for new equalities {#The_search_for_new_equalities}

Using the *P* function mentioned above, the simplest known formula for $\pi$ is for *s* = 1, but *m* > 1. Many now-discovered formulae are known for *b* as an exponent of 2 or 3 and *m* as an exponent of 2 or it some other factor-rich value, but where several of the terms of sequence *A* are zero. The discovery of these formulae involves a computer search for such linear combinations after computing the individual sums. The search procedure consists of choosing a range of parameter values for *s*, *b*, and *m*, evaluating the sums out to many digits, and then using an [integer relation-finding algorithm](wikipedia://en/13644054) (typically [Helaman Ferguson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helaman_Ferguson)'s [PSLQ algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PSLQ_algorithm)) to find a sequence *A* that adds up those intermediate sums to a well-known constant or perhaps to zero.

::: {#o13 .statement cue=named-heading name="The BBP formula for"}

### The BBP formula for $\pi$ {#The_BBP_formula_for}

The original BBP $\pi$ summation formula was found in 1995 by Plouffe using [PSLQ](wikipedia://en/13644054). It is also representable using the *P* function:

$$\begin{aligned} \pi &= \sum_{k = 0}^\infty \left[ \frac{1}{16^k} \left( \frac{4}{8k + 1} - \frac{2}{8k + 4} - \frac{1}{8k + 5} - \frac{1}{8k + 6} \right) \right] \\ &= P\bigl( 1, 16, 8, (4, 0, 0, -2, -1, -1, 0, 0) \bigr), \end{aligned}$$

which also reduces to this equivalent ratio of two polynomials:

$$\pi = \sum_{k = 0}^\infty \left[ \frac{1}{16^k} \left( \frac{120k^2 + 151k + 47}{512k^4 + 1024k^3 + 712k^2 + 194k + 15} \right) \right].$$

This formula has been shown through a fairly simple proof to equal $\pi$.[^o14]

#### BBP digit-extraction algorithm for $\pi$ {#BBP_digit-extraction_algorithm_for}

We would like to define a formula that returns the ($n + 1$)-th (with $n \geq 0$) [hexadecimal](wikipedia://en/13263) digit of $\pi$. A few manipulations are required to implement a [spigot algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spigot_algorithm) using this formula.

We must first rewrite the formula as:

$$\pi = 4 \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{\left(16^k\right)(8k+1)} - 2 \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{\left(16^k\right)(8k+4)} - \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{\left(16^k\right)(8k+5)} - \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{\left(16^k\right)(8k+6)}.$$

Now, for a particular value of *n* and taking the first sum, we split the [sum](wikipedia://en/15287) to [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824) across the *n*th term:

$$\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{\left(16^k\right)(8k+1)} = \sum_{k=0}^n \frac{1}{\left(16^k\right)(8k+1)} + \sum_{k=n+1}^\infty \frac{1}{\left(16^k\right)(8k+1)}.$$

We now multiply by 16<sup>*n*</sup>, so that the hexadecimal point (the divide between fractional and integer parts of the number) shifts (or remains, if *n = 0*) to the left of the *(n+1)*-th fractional digit:

$$\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{16^{n-k}}{8k+1} = \sum_{k=0}^n \frac{16^{n-k}}{8k+1} + \sum_{k=n+1}^\infty \frac{16^{n-k}}{8k+1}.$$

Since we only care about the fractional part of the sum, we look at our two terms and realise that only the first sum contains terms with an integer part; conversely, the second sum doesn't contain terms with an integer part, since the numerator can never be larger than the denominator for *k* > *n*. Therefore, we need a trick to remove the integer parts, that we don't need, from the terms of the first sum, in order to speed up and increase the precision of the calculations. That trick is to reduce modulo 8*k* + 1. Our first sum (out of four) to compute the fractional part then becomes:

$$\sum_{k=0}^n \frac{16^{n-k} \bmod (8k+1)}{8k+1} + \sum_{k=n+1}^\infty \frac{16^{n-k}}{8k+1}.$$

Notice how the [modulus](wikipedia://en/20087) operator always guarantees that only the fractional parts of the terms of the first sum will be kept. To calculate 16<sup>*n*−*k*</sup> mod (8*k* + 1) quickly and efficiently, the [modular exponentiation](wikipedia://en/903032) algorithm is done at the same loop level, not [nested](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nested_loop). When its running 16*x* product becomes greater than one, the modulus is taken, just as for the running total in each sum.

Now to complete the calculation, this must be applied to each of the four sums in turn. Once this is done, the four summations are put back into the sum to $\pi$:

$$4 \Sigma_1 - 2 \Sigma_2 - \Sigma_3 - \Sigma_4.$$

Since only the fractional part is accurate, extracting the wanted digit requires that one removes the integer part of the final sum, multiplies it by 16 and keeps the integer part to "skim off" the hexadecimal digit at the desired position (in theory, the next few digits up to the accuracy of the calculations used would also be accurate).

This process is similar to performing [long multiplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_multiplication), but only having to perform the summation of some middle columns. While there are some [carries](wikipedia://en/1629687) that are not counted, computers usually perform arithmetic for many bits (32 or 64) and round, and we are only interested in the most significant digit(s). There is a possibility that a particular computation will be akin to failing to add a small number (e.g. 1) to the number 999999999999999, and that the error will propagate to the most significant digit.

:::

## BBP compared to other methods of computing $\pi$ {#BBP_compared_to_other_methods_of_computing}

This algorithm computes $\pi$ without requiring custom data types having thousands or even millions of digits. The method calculates the *n*th digit *without* calculating the first *n* − 1 digits and can use small, efficient data types. [Fabrice Bellard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabrice_Bellard) found a variant of BBP, [Bellard's formula](wikipedia://en/5957331), which is faster.

Though the BBP formula can directly calculate the value of any given digit of $\pi$ with less computational effort than formulas that must calculate all intervening digits, BBP remains [linearithmic](wikipedia://en/405944#Linearithmic_time) ($O(n \log n)$), whereby successively larger values of *n* require increasingly more time to calculate; that is, the "further out" a digit is, the longer it takes BBP to calculate it, as with standard $\pi$-computing algorithms.[^o17]

::: {#o19 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

D. J. Broadhurst provides a generalization of the BBP algorithm that may be used to compute a number of other constants in nearly linear time and logarithmic space.[^o20] Explicit results are given for [Catalan's constant](wikipedia://en/46860), $\pi^3$, $\pi^4$, [Apéry's constant](wikipedia://en/2216711) $\zeta(3)$, $\zeta(5)$, (where $\zeta(x)$ is the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809)), $\log^32$, $\log^42$, $\log^52$, and various products of powers of $\pi$ and $\log2$. These results are obtained primarily by the use of [polylogarithm ladders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polylogarithm_ladder).
