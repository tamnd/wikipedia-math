# Stereographic projection

*Image: Stereographic projection in 3D.svg.* 3D illustration of a stereographic projection from the north pole onto a plane below the sphere

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Stereographic projection"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **stereographic projection** is a [perspective projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perspective_transform) of the [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859), through a specific [point](wikipedia://en/593693) on the sphere (the *pole* or *center of projection*), onto a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) (the *projection plane*) [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to the [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) through the point. It is a [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function), [bijective](wikipedia://en/3942) [function](wikipedia://en/185427) from the entire sphere except the center of projection to the entire plane. It maps [circles on the sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_of_a_sphere) to [circles or lines](wikipedia://en/10766404) on the plane, and is [conformal](wikipedia://en/50627), meaning that it preserves [angles](wikipedia://en/1196) at which curves meet and thus [locally](wikipedia://en/1462457) approximately preserves [shapes](wikipedia://en/39377). It is neither [isometric](wikipedia://en/254777) (distance preserving) nor [equiareal](wikipedia://en/8953468) (area preserving).[^o2]

:::

The stereographic projection gives a way to [represent](wikipedia://en/6355948) a sphere by a plane. The [metric](wikipedia://en/195795) [induced](wikipedia://en/1253303) by the inverse stereographic projection from the plane to the sphere defines a [geodesic](wikipedia://en/91096) distance between points in the plane equal to the [spherical distance](wikipedia://en/379733) between the spherical points they represent. A two-dimensional [coordinate system](wikipedia://en/81931) on the stereographic plane is an alternative setting for [spherical](wikipedia://en/174026) [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202) instead of [spherical polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_polar_coordinates) or three-dimensional [cartesian coordinates](wikipedia://en/7706). This is the spherical analog of the [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836) of the [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane).

Intuitively, the stereographic projection is a way of picturing the sphere as the plane, with some inevitable compromises. Because the sphere and the plane appear in many areas of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and its applications, so does the stereographic projection; it finds use in diverse fields including [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), [cartography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartography), [geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology), and [photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photography). Sometimes stereographic computations are done graphically using a special kind of [graph paper](wikipedia://en/293606) called a **stereographic net**, shortened to **stereonet**, or **Wulff net**.

## History {#History}

*Image: RubensAguilonStereographic.jpg.* Illustration by [Rubens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Paul_Rubens) for "Opticorum libri sex philosophis juxta ac mathematicis utiles", by [François d'Aguilon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_d'Aguilon). It demonstrates the principle of a general perspective projection, of which the stereographic projection is a special case.

The origin of the stereographic projection is not known, but it is believed to have been discovered by [Ancient Greek astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_astronomy) and used for projecting the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239) to the plane so that the motions of stars and planets could be analyzed using [plane geometry](wikipedia://en/9417). Its earliest extant description is found in [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979)'s [*Planisphere*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planisphaerium) (2nd century AD), but it was ambiguously attributed to [Hipparchus](wikipedia://en/13600) (2nd century BC) by [Synesius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synesius) (c. 400 AD),[^o4] and [Apollonius](wikipedia://en/661433)'s *Conics* (c. 200 BC) contains a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) which is crucial in proving the property that the stereographic projection maps circles to circles. Hipparchus, Apollonius, [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844), and even [Eudoxus](wikipedia://en/48388) (4th century BC) have sometimes been speculatively credited with inventing or knowing of the stereographic projection,[^o5] but some experts consider these attributions unjustified. Ptolemy refers to the use of the stereographic projection in a "horoscopic instrument", perhaps the anaphoric clock described by [Vitruvius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitruvius) (1st century BC).[^o6][^o7]

By the time of [Theon of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theon_of_Alexandria) (4th century), the planisphere had been combined with a [dioptra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dioptra) to form the [planispheric astrolabe](wikipedia://en/73664) ("star taker"), a capable portable device which could be used for measuring star positions and performing a wide variety of astronomical calculations. The astrolabe was in continuous use by [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) astronomers, and was significantly further developed by [medieval Islamic astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world). It was transmitted to Western Europe during the 11th–12th century, with Arabic texts translated into Latin.

In the 16th and 17th century, the [equatorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) aspect of the stereographic projection was commonly used for maps of the [Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Hemisphere) and [Western Hemispheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Hemisphere). It is believed that already the map created in 1507 by [Gualterius Lud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gualterius_Lud)[^o8] was in stereographic projection, as were later the maps of [Jean Rotz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Rotz) (1542), [Rumold Mercator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumold_Mercator) (1595), and many others.[^o9] In star charts, even this equatorial aspect had been utilised already by the ancient astronomers like [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979).[^o10]

[François d'Aguilon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_d'Aguilon) gave the stereographic projection its current name in his 1613 work *Opticorum libri sex philosophis juxta ac mathematicis utiles* (Six Books of Optics, useful for philosophers and mathematicians alike).[^o11]

In the late 16th century, [Thomas Harriot](wikipedia://en/780445) proved that the stereographic projection is [conformal](wikipedia://en/50627); however, this proof was never published and sat among his papers in a box for more than three centuries.[^o12] In 1695, [Edmond Halley](wikipedia://en/36858805), motivated by his interest in [star charts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_chart), was the first to publish a proof.[^o13] He used the recently established tools of [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), invented by his friend [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627).

## Definition {#Definition}

### First formulation {#First_formulation}

*Image: Stereoprojzero.svg.* Stereographic projection of the unit sphere from the north pole onto the plane $z=0$, shown here in [cross section](wikipedia://en/1337370)

The [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090) $\mathcal{S}^2$ in three-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3$ is the set of points $(x,y,z)$ such that $x^2+y^2+z^2=1$. Let $N=(0,0,1)$ be the "north pole", and let $\mathcal{M}$ be the rest of the sphere. The plane $z=0$ runs through the center of the sphere; the "equator" is the intersection of the sphere with this plane.

For any point $P$ on $\mathcal{M}$, there is a unique line through $N$ and $P$, and this line intersects the plane $z=0$ in exactly one point $P'$, known as the **stereographic projection** of $P$ onto the plane.

In [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) $(x,y,z)$ on the sphere and $(X,Y)$ on the plane, the projection and its inverse are given by the formulas

$$\begin{aligned}(X, Y) &= \left(\frac{x}{1 - z}, \frac{y}{1 - z}\right),\\ (x, y, z) &= \left(\frac{2 X}{1 + X^2 + Y^2}, \frac{2 Y}{1 + X^2 + Y^2}, \frac{-1 + X^2 + Y^2}{1 + X^2 + Y^2}\right).\end{aligned}$$

In [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates) $(\varphi,\theta)$ on the sphere (with $\varphi$ the [zenith angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenith_angle), $0\leq\varphi\leq\pi$, and $\theta$ the [azimuth](wikipedia://en/47487), $0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi$) and [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) $(R,\Theta)$ on the plane, the projection and its inverse are

$$\begin{aligned}(R, \Theta) &= \left(\frac{\sin \varphi}{1 - \cos \varphi}, \theta\right) = \left(\cot\frac{\varphi}{2}, \theta\right),\\ (\varphi, \theta) &= \left(2 \arctan \frac{1}{R}, \Theta\right).\end{aligned}$$

Here, $\varphi$ is understood to have value $\pi$ when $R=0$. Also, there are many ways to rewrite these formulas using [trigonometric identities](wikipedia://en/25804104). In [cylindrical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylindrical_coordinates) $(r,\theta,z)$ on the sphere and polar coordinates $(R,\Theta)$ on the plane, the projection and its inverse are

$$\begin{aligned}(R, \Theta) &= \left(\frac{r}{1 - z}, \theta\right),\\ (r, \theta, z) &= \left(\frac{2 R}{1 + R^2}, \Theta, \frac{R^2 - 1}{R^2 + 1}\right).\end{aligned}$$

### Other conventions {#Other_conventions}

*Image: Stereoprojnegone.svg.* Stereographic projection of the unit sphere from the north pole onto the plane $z = -1$, shown here in cross section

Some authors[^o18] define stereographic projection from the north pole (0, 0, 1) onto the plane $z = -1$, which is tangent to the unit sphere at the south pole (0, 0, −1). This can be described as a [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of a projection onto the equatorial plane described above, and a [homothety](wikipedia://en/386138) from it to the polar plane. The homothety scales the image by a factor of 2 (a ratio of a diameter to a radius of the sphere); hence, the values $X$ and $Y$ produced by this projection are exactly twice those produced by the equatorial projection described in the preceding section. For example, this projection sends the equator to the circle of radius 2 centered at the origin. While the equatorial projection produces no infinitesimal area distortion along the equator, this pole-tangent projection instead produces no infinitesimal area distortion at the south pole.

Other authors[^o19] use a sphere of radius $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and the plane $z = -\tfrac{1}{2}$. In this case the formulae become

$$\begin{aligned}(x,y,z) \rightarrow (\xi, \eta) &= \left(\frac{x}{\frac{1}{2} - z}, \frac{y}{\frac{1}{2} - z}\right),\\ (\xi, \eta) \rightarrow (x,y,z) &= \left(\frac{\xi}{1 + \xi^2 + \eta^2}, \frac{\eta}{1 + \xi^2 + \eta^2}, \frac{-1 + \xi^2 + \eta^2}{2 + 2\xi^2 + 2\eta^2}\right).\end{aligned}$$

*Image: StereographicGeneric.svg.* Stereographic projection of a sphere from a point $Q$ onto the plane $E$, shown here in cross section

In general, one can define a stereographic projection from any point $Q$ on the sphere onto any plane $E$ such that

- $E$ is perpendicular to the diameter through $Q$, and
- $E$ does not contain $Q$.

As long as $E$ meets these conditions, then for any point $P$ other than $Q$ the line through $P$ and $Q$ meets $E$ in exactly one point $P$, which is defined to be the stereographic projection of *P* onto *E*.[^o20]

::: {#o22 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

### Generalizations {#Generalizations}

More generally, stereographic projection may be applied to the unit [$n$-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) $S^{n}$ in ($n + 1$)-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbf{E}^{n+1}$. If $Q$ is a point of $S^{n}$ and $E$ a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) in $\mathbf{E}^{n+1}$, then the stereographic projection of a point $P \in S^{n} - \{Q\}$ is the point $P$ of intersection of the line $\overline{QP}$ with $E$. In [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) ($x_{i}$, $i$ from 0 to $n$) on $S^{n}$ and ($X_{i}$, $i$ from 1 to *n*) on $E$, the projection from $Q = (1, 0, 0, ..., 0) \in S^{n}$ is given by

$$X_i = \frac{x_i}{1 - x_0} \quad (i = 1, \dots, n).$$

Defining

$$s^2=\sum_{j=1}^n X_j^2 = \frac{1+x_0}{1-x_0},$$

the inverse is given by

$$x_0 = \frac{s^2-1}{s^2+1} \quad \text{and} \quad x_i = \frac{2 X_i}{s^2+1} \quad (i = 1, \dots, n).$$

Still more generally, suppose that $S$ is a (nonsingular) [quadric hypersurface](wikipedia://en/145570) in the [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) $\mathbf{P}^{n+1}$. In other words, $S$ is the locus of zeros of a non-singular quadratic form $f(x_{0}, ..., x_{n+1})$ in the [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316) $x_{i}$. Fix any point $Q$ on $S$ and a hyperplane $E$ in $\mathbf{P}^{n+1}$ not containing $Q$. Then the stereographic projection of a point $P$ in $S - \{Q\}$ is the unique point of intersection of $\overline{QP}$ with $E$. As before, the stereographic projection is conformal and invertible on a non-empty Zariski open set. The stereographic projection presents the quadric hypersurface as a [rational hypersurface](wikipedia://en/865939).[^o23] This construction plays a role in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) and [conformal geometry](wikipedia://en/295907).

## Properties {#Properties}

The first stereographic projection defined in the preceding section sends the "south pole" (i.e., (0, 0, −1)) of the [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090) to (0, 0), the equator to the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071), the southern hemisphere to the region inside the circle, and the northern hemisphere to the region outside the circle.

The projection is not defined at the projection point $N$ = (0, 0, 1). Small neighborhoods of this point are sent to subsets of the plane far away from (0, 0). The closer $P$ is to (0, 0, 1), the more distant its image is from (0, 0) in the plane. For this reason it is common to speak of (0, 0, 1) as mapping to "infinity" in the plane, and of the sphere as completing the plane by adding a [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139). This notion finds utility in [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) and complex analysis. On a merely [topological](wikipedia://en/29954) level, it illustrates how the sphere is [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) to the [one-point compactification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-point_compactification) of the plane.

In [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) a point $P(x, y, z)$ on the sphere and its image $P(X, Y)$ on the plane either both are [rational points](wikipedia://en/2960200) or none of them:

$$P \in \mathbb Q^3 \iff P' \in \mathbb Q^2$$

*Image: CartesianStereoProj.png.* A Cartesian grid on the plane appears distorted on the sphere. The grid lines are still perpendicular, but the areas of the grid squares shrink as they approach the north pole.

*Image: PolarStereoProj.png.* A polar grid on the plane appears distorted on the sphere. The grid curves are still perpendicular, but the areas of the grid sectors shrink as they approach the north pole.

Stereographic projection is conformal, meaning that it preserves the angles at which curves cross each other (see figures). On the other hand, stereographic projection does not preserve area; in general, the area of a region of the sphere does not equal the area of its projection onto the plane. The area element is given in $(X, Y)$ coordinates by

$$dA = \frac{4}{(1 + X^2 + Y^2)^2} \; dX \; dY.$$

Along the unit circle, where $X^{2} + Y^{2} = 1$, there is no inflation of area in the limit, giving a scale factor of 1. Near (0, 0) areas are inflated by a factor of 4, and near infinity areas are inflated by arbitrarily small factors.

The metric is given in $(X, Y)$ coordinates by

$$\frac{4}{(1 + X^2 + Y^2)^2} \; ( dX^2 + dY^2),$$

and is the unique formula found in [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980)'s *Habilitationsschrift* on the foundations of geometry, delivered at Göttingen in 1854, and entitled *Über die Hypothesen welche der Geometrie zu Grunde liegen*.

No map from the sphere to the plane can be both conformal and area-preserving. If it were, then it would be a local [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) and would preserve [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623). The sphere and the plane have different Gaussian curvatures, so this is impossible.

[Circles on the sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_of_a_sphere) that do *not* pass through the point of projection are projected to circles on the plane.[^o26][^o27] Circles on the sphere that *do* pass through the point of projection are projected to straight lines on the plane. These lines are sometimes thought of as circles through the point at infinity, or circles of infinite radius. These properties can be verified by using the expressions of $x,y,z$ in terms of $X, Y, Z,$ given in : using these expressions for a substitution in the equation $ax+by+cz-d=0$ of the plane containing a circle on the sphere, and clearing denominators, one gets the equation of a circle, that is, a second-degree equation with $(c-d)(X^2+Y^2)$ as its quadratic part. The equation becomes linear if $c=d,$ that is, if the plane passes through the point of projection.

All lines in the plane, when transformed to circles on the sphere by the inverse of stereographic projection, meet at the projection point. Parallel lines, which do not intersect in the plane, are transformed to circles tangent at projection point. Intersecting lines are transformed to circles that intersect [transversally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transversality_%28mathematics%29) at two points in the sphere, one of which is the projection point. (Similar remarks hold about the [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325), but the intersection relationships are different there.)

*Image: Riemann Sphere.jpg.* The sphere, with various [loxodromes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loxodrome) shown in distinct colors

The [loxodromes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loxodrome) of the sphere map to curves on the plane of the form

$$R = e^{\Theta/a},\,$$

where the parameter $a$ measures the "tightness" of the loxodrome. Thus, loxodromes correspond to [logarithmic spirals](wikipedia://en/42373). These spirals intersect radial lines in the plane at equal angles, just as the loxodromes intersect meridians on the sphere at equal angles.

*Image: Inversion by Stereographic.png.*

The stereographic projection relates to the plane inversion in a simple way. Let $P$ and $Q$ be two points on the sphere with projections $P$ and $Q$ on the plane. Then $P$ and $Q$ are inversive images of each other in the image of the equatorial circle if and only if $P$ and $Q$ are reflections of each other in the equatorial plane.

In other words, if:

- $P$ is a point on the sphere, but not a 'north pole' $N$ and not its [antipode](wikipedia://en/367577), the 'south pole' $S$,
- $P$ is the image of $P$ in a stereographic projection with the projection point $N$ and
- $P$ is the image of $P$ in a stereographic projection with the projection point $S$,

then $P$ and $P$ are inversive images of each other in the unit circle.

$$\triangle NOP^\prime \sim \triangle P^{\prime\prime}OS \implies OP^\prime:ON = OS : OP^{\prime\prime} \implies OP^\prime \cdot OP^{\prime\prime} = r^2$$

### Metric {#Metric}

The associated [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) of the specific stereographic projection described in the beginning of this section (which sends the "south pole" (0, 0, −1) of the unit sphere to (0, 0), and the equator to the unit circle) is given by

$$ds^2 = 4 \frac{\sum_i dx_i^2}{\left(1+\sum_i x_i^2\right)^2} = \frac{ 4\, \lVert d \mathbf{x} \rVert \vphantom{l}^2 }{ \bigl(1 + \lVert \mathbf{x} \rVert \vphantom{l}^2 \bigr) ^2 }$$

where the *x*<sub>*i*</sub> are the Cartesian coordinates of the Euclidean plane onto which the sphere is projected. In comparison, the equation for the corresponding metric of the [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836#Metric_and_curvature) of hyperbolic space looks equivalent, except for a sign difference in the denominator.

## Wulff net {#Wulff_net}

*Image: Wulffnet.svg.* Wulff net or stereonet, used for making plots of the stereographic projection by hand

*Image: Sphere-stgrpr-wn.svg.* The generation of a Wulff net (circular net within the red circle) by a stereographic projection with center *C* and projection plane $\pi$

Stereographic projection plots can be carried out by a computer using the explicit formulas given above. However, for graphing by hand these formulas are unwieldy. Instead, it is common to use graph paper designed specifically for the task. This special graph paper is called a **stereonet** or **Wulff net**, after the Russian mineralogist [George (Yuri Viktorovich) Wulff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Wulff).[^o31]

The Wulff net shown here is the stereographic projection of the grid of [parallels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_of_latitude) and meridians of a [hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemispheres_of_Earth) centred at a point on the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) (such as the Eastern or Western hemisphere of a planet).

In the figure, the area-distorting property of the stereographic projection can be seen by comparing a grid sector near the center of the net with one at the far right or left. The two sectors have equal areas on the sphere. On the disk, the latter has nearly four times the area of the former. If the grid is made finer, this ratio approaches exactly 4.

On the Wulff net, the images of the parallels and meridians intersect at right angles. This orthogonality property is a consequence of the angle-preserving property of the stereographic projection. (However, the angle-preserving property is stronger than this property. Not all projections that preserve the orthogonality of parallels and meridians are angle-preserving.)

*Image: Wulffnetanimation.gif.* Illustration of steps 1–4 for plotting a point on a Wulff net

For an example of the use of the Wulff net, imagine two copies of it on thin paper, one atop the other, aligned and tacked at their mutual center. Let $P$ be the point on the lower unit hemisphere whose spherical coordinates are (140°, 60°) and whose Cartesian coordinates are (0.321, 0.557, −0.766). This point lies on a line oriented 60° counterclockwise from the positive $x$-axis (or 30° clockwise from the positive $y$-axis) and 50° below the horizontal plane $z = 0$. Once these angles are known, there are four steps to plotting $P$:

1. Using the grid lines, which are spaced 10° apart in the figures here, mark the point on the edge of the net that is 60° counterclockwise from the point (1, 0) (or 30° clockwise from the point (0, 1)).
1. Rotate the top net until this point is aligned with (1, 0) on the bottom net.
1. Using the grid lines on the bottom net, mark the point that is 50° toward the center from that point.
1. Rotate the top net oppositely to how it was oriented before, to bring it back into alignment with the bottom net. The point marked in step 3 is then the projection that we wanted.

To plot other points, whose angles are not such round numbers as 60° and 50°, one must visually interpolate between the nearest grid lines. It is helpful to have a net with finer spacing than 10°. Spacings of 2° are common.

To find the [central angle](wikipedia://en/462705) between two points on the sphere based on their stereographic plot, overlay the plot on a Wulff net and rotate the plot about the center until the two points lie on or near a meridian. Then measure the angle between them by counting grid lines along that meridian.

## Applications within mathematics {#Applications_within_mathematics}

### Complex analysis {#Complex_analysis}

*Image: Riemann sphere1.svg.* The stereographic projection from the North pole of a sphere to its equatorial plane establishes a [one to one correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_to_one_correspondence) between the sphere and the equatorial plane extended with a [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139) denoted $\infty$. When the equatorial plane is the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), this provides a visualization of the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799)

*Image: Earth as riemann sphere large 500mio 254dpi.jpg.* Riemann sphere: almost the whole earth in stereographic azimuthal projection 1:500.000.000 (254 dpi)

Although any stereographic projection misses one point on the sphere (the projection point), the entire sphere can be mapped using two projections from distinct projection points. In other words, the sphere can be covered by two stereographic [parametrizations](wikipedia://en/453198) (the inverses of the projections) from the plane. The parametrizations can be chosen to induce the same [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28space%29) on the sphere. Together, they describe the sphere as an oriented [surface](wikipedia://en/27865) (or two-dimensional [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470)).

This construction has special significance in complex analysis. The point $(X, Y)$ in the real plane can be identified with the [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $\zeta = X + iY$. The stereographic projection from the north pole onto the equatorial plane is then

$$\begin{aligned} \zeta &= \frac{x + i y}{1 - z},\\ \\ (x, y, z) &= \left(\frac{2 \operatorname{Re} \zeta}{1 + \bar \zeta \zeta}, \frac{2 \operatorname{Im} \zeta}{1 + \bar \zeta \zeta}, \frac{-1 + \bar \zeta \zeta}{1 + \bar \zeta \zeta}\right).\end{aligned}$$

Similarly, letting $\xi = X - iY$ be another complex coordinate, the functions

$$\begin{aligned} \xi &= \frac{x - i y}{1 + z},\\ (x, y, z) &= \left(\frac{2 \operatorname{Re} \xi}{1 + \bar \xi \xi}, \frac{-2 \operatorname{Im} \xi}{1 + \bar \xi \xi}, \frac{1 - \bar \xi \xi}{1 + \bar \xi \xi}\right)\end{aligned}$$

define a stereographic projection from the south pole onto the equatorial plane. The transition maps between the $\zeta$- and $\xi$-coordinates are then $\zeta = \tfrac{1}{\xi}$ and $\xi = \tfrac{1}{\zeta}$, with $\zeta$ approaching 0 as $\xi$ goes to infinity, and *vice versa*. This facilitates an elegant and useful notion of infinity for the complex numbers and indeed an entire theory of [meromorphic functions](wikipedia://en/80857) mapping to the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799). The [standard metric](wikipedia://en/144652#Riemannian_metrics_and_Riemannian_manifolds) on the unit sphere agrees with the [Fubini–Study metric](wikipedia://en/2574486) on the Riemann sphere.

### Visualization of lines and planes {#Visualization_of_lines_and_planes}

*Image: Sfsp111.gif.* Animation of [Kikuchi lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kikuchi_line_%28solid_state_physics%29) of four of the eight \<111> zones in an fcc crystal. Planes edge-on (banded lines) intersect at fixed angles.

The set of all lines through the origin in three-dimensional space forms a space called the [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325). This plane is difficult to visualize, because it cannot be [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119) in three-dimensional space.

However, one can visualize it as a disk, as follows. Any line through the origin intersects the southern hemisphere $z$ ≤ 0 in a point, which can then be stereographically projected to a point on a disk in the XY plane. Horizontal lines through the origin intersect the southern hemisphere in two [antipodal points](wikipedia://en/367577) along the equator, which project to the boundary of the disk. Either of the two projected points can be considered part of the disk; it is understood that antipodal points on the equator represent a single line in 3 space and a single point on the boundary of the projected disk (see [quotient topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_topology)). So any set of lines through the origin can be pictured as a set of points in the projected disk. But the boundary points behave differently from the boundary points of an ordinary 2-dimensional disk, in that any one of them is simultaneously close to interior points on opposite sides of the disk (just as two nearly horizontal lines through the origin can project to points on opposite sides of the disk).

Also, every plane through the origin intersects the unit sphere in a great circle, called the *trace* of the plane. This circle maps to a circle under stereographic projection. So the projection lets us visualize planes as circular arcs in the disk. Prior to the availability of computers, stereographic projections with great circles often involved drawing large-radius arcs that required use of a [beam compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_compass). Computers now make this task much easier.

Further associated with each plane is a unique line, called the plane's *pole*, that passes through the origin and is perpendicular to the plane. This line can be plotted as a point on the disk just as any line through the origin can. So the stereographic projection also lets us visualize planes as points in the disk. For plots involving many planes, plotting their poles produces a less-cluttered picture than plotting their traces.

This construction is used to visualize directional data in crystallography and geology, as described below.

### Other visualization {#Other_visualization}

Stereographic projection is also applied to the visualization of [polytopes](wikipedia://en/23471). In a [Schlegel diagram](wikipedia://en/7201595), an $n$-dimensional polytope in $\mathbf{R}^{n+1}$ is projected onto an $n$-dimensional sphere, which is then stereographically projected onto $\mathbf{R}^{n}$. The reduction from $\mathbf{R}^{n+1}$ to $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ can make the polytope easier to visualize and understand.

### Arithmetic geometry {#Arithmetic_geometry}

*Image: Stereographic projection of rational points.svg.* The [rational points](wikipedia://en/2960200) on a circle correspond, under stereographic projection, to the rational points of the line.

In elementary [arithmetic geometry](wikipedia://en/1973177), stereographic projection from the unit circle provides a means to describe all primitive [Pythagorean triples](wikipedia://en/24172). Specifically, stereographic projection from the north pole (0,1) onto the $x$-axis gives a one-to-one correspondence between the [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024) points $(x, y)$ on the unit circle (with $y \ne 1$) and the [rational points](wikipedia://en/2960200) of the $x$-axis. If $(\tfrac{m}{n}, 0)$ is a rational point on the $x$-axis, then its inverse stereographic projection is the point

$$\left(\frac{2mn}{m^2+n^2}, \frac{m^2-n^2}{m^2+n^2}\right)$$

which gives Euclid's formula for a Pythagorean triple.

### Tangent half-angle substitution {#Tangent_half-angle_substitution}

*Image: WeierstrassSubstitution.svg.*

The pair of trigonometric functions $(\sin x, \cos x)$ can be thought of as parametrizing the unit circle. The stereographic projection gives an alternative parametrization of the unit circle:

$$\cos x = \frac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2},\quad \sin x = \frac{2 t}{t^2 + 1}.$$

Under this reparametrization, the length element $dx$ of the unit circle goes over to

$$dx = \frac{2 \, dt}{t^2 + 1}.$$

This substitution can sometimes simplify [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532) involving trigonometric functions.

## Applications to other disciplines {#Applications_to_other_disciplines}

### Cartography {#Cartography}

The fundamental problem of cartography is that no map from the sphere to the plane can accurately represent both angles and areas. In general, area-preserving [map projections](wikipedia://en/51784) are preferred for [statistical](wikipedia://en/26685) applications, while angle-preserving (conformal) map projections are preferred for [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation).

Stereographic projection falls into the second category. When the projection is centered at the Earth's north or south pole, it has additional desirable properties: It sends [meridians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_%28geography%29) to rays emanating from the origin and [parallels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_of_latitude) to circles centered at the origin.

### Planetary science {#Planetary_science}

*Image: LRO WAC North Pole Mosaic (PIA14024).jpg.* A stereographic projection of the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon), showing regions polewards of 60° North. Craters which are [circles on the sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_of_a_sphere) appear circular in this projection, regardless of whether they are close to the pole or the edge of the map.

The stereographic is the only projection that maps all [circles on a sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_of_a_sphere) to [circles on a plane](wikipedia://en/6220). This property is valuable in planetary mapping where craters are typical features. The set of circles passing through the point of projection have unbounded radius, and therefore [degenerate](wikipedia://en/232952) into lines.

### Crystallography {#Crystallography}

*Image: DiamondPoleFigure111.png.* A crystallographic pole figure for the [diamond lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_cubic) in [\[111\] direction](wikipedia://en/1089079)

In [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography), the orientations of [crystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal) axes and faces in three-dimensional space are a central geometric concern, for example in the interpretation of [X-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_diffraction) and [electron diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_diffraction) patterns. These orientations can be visualized as in the section [Visualization of lines and planes](wikipedia://en/143431#Visualization_of_lines_and_planes) above. That is, crystal axes and poles to crystal planes are intersected with the northern hemisphere and then plotted using stereographic projection. A plot of poles is called a **pole figure**.

In [electron diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_diffraction), [Kikuchi line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kikuchi_line_%28solid_state_physics%29) pairs appear as bands decorating the intersection between lattice plane traces and the [Ewald sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ewald_sphere) thus providing *experimental access* to a crystal's stereographic projection. Model Kikuchi maps in reciprocal space,[^o42] and fringe visibility maps for use with bend contours in direct space,[^o43] thus act as road maps for exploring orientation space with crystals in the [transmission electron microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_electron_microscope).

### Geology {#Geology}

*Image: Stero projection structural geology.png.* Use of lower hemisphere stereographic projection to plot planar and linear data in structural geology, using the example of a fault plane with a slickenside lineation

Researchers in [structural geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_geology) are concerned with the orientations of planes and lines for a number of reasons. The [foliation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foliation_%28geology%29) of a rock is a planar feature that often contains a linear feature called [lineation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lineation_%28geology%29). Similarly, a [fault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_%28geology%29) plane is a planar feature that may contain linear features such as [slickensides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slickenside).

These orientations of lines and planes at various scales can be plotted using the methods of the [Visualization of lines and planes](wikipedia://en/143431#Visualization_of_lines_and_planes) section above. As in crystallography, planes are typically plotted by their poles. Unlike crystallography, the southern hemisphere is used instead of the northern one (because the geological features in question lie below the Earth's surface). In this context the stereographic projection is often referred to as the **equal-angle lower-hemisphere projection**. The equal-area lower-hemisphere projection defined by the [Lambert azimuthal equal-area projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambert_azimuthal_equal-area_projection) is also used, especially when the plot is to be subjected to subsequent statistical analysis such as density [contouring](wikipedia://en/650086).[^o45]

### Rock mechanics {#Rock_mechanics}

The stereographic projection is one of the most widely used methods for evaluating rock slope stability. It allows for the representation and analysis of three-dimensional orientation data in two dimensions. Kinematic analysis within stereographic projection is used to assess the potential for various modes of rock slope failures—such as plane, wedge, and toppling failures—which occur due to the presence of unfavorably oriented discontinuities.[^o47][^o48] This technique is particularly useful for visualizing the orientation of rock slopes in relation to discontinuity sets, facilitating the assessment of the most likely failure type.[^o47] For instance, plane failure is more likely when the strike of a discontinuity set is parallel to the slope, and the discontinuities dip towards the slope at an angle steep enough to allow sliding, but not steeper than the slope itself.

Additionally, some authors have developed graphical methods based on stereographic projection to easily calculate geometrical correction parameters—such as those related to the parallelism between the slope and discontinuities, the dip of the discontinuity, and the relative angle between the discontinuity and the slope—for rock mass classifications in slopes, including [slope mass rating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slope_mass_rating) (SMR)[^o49] and [rock mass rating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_mass_rating).[^o50]

### Photography {#Photography}

*Image: Cmglee\_Wikimania2016\_Esino\_Lario\_Last\_Supper\_tinyplanet.jpg.* Stereographic projection of the spherical panorama of the Last Supper sculpture by [Michele Vedani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michele_Vedani) in [Esino Lario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esino_Lario), Lombardy, Italy during [Wikimania 2016](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikimania_2016)

*Image: Vue circulaire des montagnes qu ‘on decouvre du sommet du Glacier de Buet, from Horace-Benedict de Saussure, Voyage dans les Alpes, précédés d'un essai sur l'histoire naturelle des environs de Geneve. Neuchatel, l779-96, pl. 8.jpg.* "Vue circulaire des montagnes qu'on découvre du sommet du Glacier de Buet", Horace-Benedict de Saussure, *Voyage dans les Alpes, précédés d'un essai sur l'histoire naturelle des environs de Geneve*. Neuchatel, 1779–96, pl. 8.

Some [fisheye lenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisheye_lens) use a stereographic projection to capture a wide-angle view.[^o53] Compared to more traditional fisheye lenses which use an equal-area projection, areas close to the edge retain their shape, and straight lines are less curved. However, stereographic fisheye lenses are typically more expensive to manufacture.[^o54] Image remapping software, such as [Panotools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panotools), allows the automatic remapping of photos from an equal-area fisheye to a stereographic projection.

The stereographic projection has been used to map spherical [panoramas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panorama), starting with [Horace Bénédict de Saussure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace_Bénédict_de_Saussure)'s in 1779. This results in effects known as a *little planet* (when the center of projection is the [nadir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nadir)) and a *tube* (when the center of projection is the [zenith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenith)).[^o55]

The popularity of using stereographic projections to map panoramas over other azimuthal projections is attributed to the shape preservation that results from the conformality of the projection.[^o55]
