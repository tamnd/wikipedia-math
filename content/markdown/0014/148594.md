# Euler's constant

*Image: gamma-area.svg.* The area of the blue region converges to Euler's constant.

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Euler's constant"}

**Euler's constant** (sometimes called the **Euler–Mascheroni constant**) is a [mathematical constant](wikipedia://en/48779551), usually denoted by the lowercase Greek letter [gamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma) ($\gamma$), defined as the [limiting](wikipedia://en/285773) difference between the [harmonic series](wikipedia://en/142488) and the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476), denoted here by $\log$:

:::

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma &= \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\sum_{k=1}^n \frac1{k}-\log n \right)\\ &=\int_1^\infty\left(\frac1{\lfloor x\rfloor}-\frac1x\right)\,\mathrm dx. \end{aligned}$$

Here, $\lfloor\cdot\rfloor$ represents the [floor function](wikipedia://en/54267).

The numerical value of Euler's constant, to 50 [decimal places](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_Places), is:

\0.57721 56649 01532 86060 65120 90082 40243 10421 59335 93992 ...

## Introduction {#Introduction}

The usual definition of Euler's constant is, as stated above, given via the limiting difference between the harmonic series and the natural logarithm. The [divergence](wikipedia://en/876428) of the former series was already known centuries prior,[^o4] and Euler, who sought to find a [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) [interpolation](wikipedia://en/14569) of the discrete harmonic series, found its growth rate to be [asymptotically equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_equivalence) to that of the natural logarithm. In particular, he proved that:[^n1]

$$\gamma = \lim_{n \to \infty}\left(\frac{1}{1}+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3} + \ldots + \frac{1}{n} - \log(n)\right)$$

exists. This can be seen by setting $a_n=H_n-\log n$ as the partial terms of the limit ($H_{n}$ are the [harmonic numbers](wikipedia://en/214729)) and noting that this [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) is [monotonically decreasing](wikipedia://en/48260):

$$a_{n+1}-a_{n} = \frac{1}{n+1} - \log\left(1+\frac1n\right) < 0$$

as well as [bounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_sequence) from below:

$$a_{n} = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac1k - \int_1^n \frac1t \,dt > 0.$$

and hence convergent to a constant $0\leq \gamma <1=a_1$.

Euler showed that the following [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series) approaches $\gamma$:

$$\gamma = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \left(\frac 1 k - \log\left(1+\frac 1 k \right)\right).$$

This can be seen by noting that its partial sums form a [telescoping series](wikipedia://en/508070), such that the series again yields a limit between the harmonic series and a shifted natural logarithm.

In general:

$$\gamma = \lim_{n \to \infty}\left(\frac{1}{1}+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3} + \ldots + \frac{1}{n} - \log(n+\alpha) \right) \equiv \lim_{n \to \infty} \gamma_n(\alpha)$$

for any $\alpha > -n$. However, the [rate of convergence](wikipedia://en/999701) of this expansion depends significantly on $\alpha$. In particular, $\gamma_{n}(1/2)$ exhibits much more rapid convergence than the conventional expansion $\gamma_{n}(0)$.[^n2] This is because

$$\frac{1}{2(n+1)} < \gamma_n(0) - \gamma < \frac{1}{2n},$$

while

$$\frac{1}{24(n+1)^2} < \gamma_n(1/2) - \gamma < \frac{1}{24n^2}.$$

This can also be seen in the following asymptotic formulas:

- $\gamma \sim H_n - \log n - \frac1{2n} + \frac1{12n^2} - \frac1{120n^4} + \cdots$ (*Euler*)
- $\gamma \sim H_n - \log\left({n + \frac1{2} + \frac1{24n} - \frac1{48n^2} + \cdots}\right)$ (*Negoi*)
- $\gamma \sim H_n - \frac{\log n + \log(n+1)}{2} - \frac1{6n(n+1)} + \frac1{30n^2(n+1)^2} - \cdots$ (*[Cesàro](wikipedia://en/605144)*)

The third formula is also called the Ramanujan expansion.

Even so, there exist other series expansions which converge more rapidly than this; some of these are discussed below.

## History {#History}

The constant first appeared in a 1734 paper by the Swiss mathematician [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902), titled *De Progressionibus harmonicis observationes* (*Observations on harmonic progressions*; [Eneström Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eneström_index) 43), where he described it as "worthy of serious consideration".[^o82][^n1] Euler initially calculated the constant's value to 6 decimal places. In 1781, he calculated it to 16 decimal places. Euler used the notations $C$ and $O$ for the constant. The Italian mathematician [Lorenzo Mascheroni](wikipedia://en/88116) attempted to calculate the constant to 32 decimal places, but made errors in the 20th–22nd and 31st–32nd decimal places; starting from the 20th digit, he calculated ...**181**12090082**39** when the correct value is ...**065**12090082**40**. In 1790, he used the notations $A$ and $a$ for the constant. Other computations were done by [Johann von Soldner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Georg_von_Soldner) in 1809, who used the notation $H$. The notation $\gamma$ appears nowhere in the writings of either Euler or Mascheroni, and was chosen at a later time, perhaps because of the constant's connection to the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316).[^n1] For example, the German mathematician [Carl Anton Bretschneider](wikipedia://en/30738616) used the notation $\gamma$ in 1835,[^n3] and [Augustus De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289) used it in a textbook published in parts from 1836 to 1842. Other notations were also occasionally used.[^o11] Euler's constant was also studied by the Indian mathematician [Srinivasa Ramanujan](wikipedia://en/47717) who published one paper on it in 1917.[^o12] [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) mentioned the irrationality of $\gamma$ as an unsolved problem that seems "unapproachable" and, allegedly, the English mathematician [Godfrey Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599) offered to give up his [Savilian Chair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savilian_Professor_of_Geometry) at [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University) to anyone who could prove this.[^o82]

## Properties {#Properties}

### Irrationality and transcendence {#Irrationality_and_transcendence}

The number $\gamma$ has not been proved [algebraic](wikipedia://en/1158) or [transcendental](wikipedia://en/30325). In fact, it is not even known whether $\gamma$ is [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689). The ubiquity of $\gamma$ revealed by the large number of equations below and the fact that $\gamma$ has been called the third most important mathematical constant after [$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601) and [$e$](wikipedia://en/9633)[^o44][^o45] makes the irrationality of $\gamma$ a major open question in mathematics.[^o82][^o46][^o47]

::: {#o48 .problem cue=unsolved}

Is Euler's constant irrational? If so, is it transcendental?

:::

However, some progress has been made. In 1959 Andrei Shidlovsky proved that at least one of Euler's constant $\gamma$ and the [Gompertz constant](wikipedia://en/42809296) $\delta$ is irrational;[^o49] [Tanguy Rivoal](wikipedia://en/78396540) proved in 2012 that at least one of them is transcendental. [Kurt Mahler](wikipedia://en/4421496) showed in 1968 that the number $\frac \pi 2\frac{Y_0(2)}{J_0(2)}-\gamma$ is transcendental, where $J_0$ and $Y_0$ are the usual [Bessel functions](wikipedia://en/4700).[^n1] It is known that the [transcendence degree](wikipedia://en/287364) of the field $\mathbb Q(e,\gamma,\delta)$ is at least two.[^n1]

In 2010, [M. Ram Murty](wikipedia://en/14988649) and N. Saradha showed that at most one of the Euler-Lehmer constants, i. e. the numbers of the form

$$\gamma(a,q) = \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sum_{k=0}^n{\frac{1}{a+kq}} - \frac{\log{(a+nq})}{q} \right)$$

is algebraic, if $q \ge 2$ and $1 \le a < q$; this family includes the special case $\gamma(2,4) = \gamma/4$.[^n1]

Using the same approach, in 2013, M. Ram Murty and A. Zaytseva showed that the generalized Euler constants have the same property, [^n1] [^o54] where the generalized Euler constant are defined as

$$\gamma(\Omega) = \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \left( \sum_{n=1}^x \frac{1_\Omega(n)}{n} - \log x \cdot \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{ \sum_{n=1}^x 1_\Omega (n) }{x} \right),$$

where $\Omega$ is a fixed list of prime numbers, $1_\Omega(n) =0$ if at least one of the primes in $\Omega$ is a prime factor of $n$, and $1_\Omega(n) =1$ otherwise. In particular, $\gamma(\emptyset)=\gamma$.

Using a [continued fraction](wikipedia://en/739199) analysis, Papanikolaou showed in 1997 that if $\gamma$ is [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024), its denominator must be greater than 10<sup>244663</sup>. If $e^{1\operatorname{px}}$ is a rational number, then its denominator must be greater than 10<sup>15000</sup>.[^n1]

Euler's constant is conjectured not to be an [algebraic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Period_%28algebraic_geometry%29),[^n1] but the values of its first 10<sup>9</sup> decimal digits seem to indicate that it could be a [normal number](wikipedia://en/204277).[^o57]

### Continued fraction {#Continued_fraction}

The [simple continued fraction](wikipedia://en/46802) expansion of Euler's constant is given by:

$$\gamma=0+\cfrac{1}{1+\cfrac{1}{1+\cfrac{1}{2+\cfrac{1}{1+\cfrac{1}{2+\cfrac{1}{1+\cfrac{1}{4+\dots}}}}}}}$$

which has no *apparent* pattern. It is known to have at least 16,695,000,000 terms, and it has infinitely many terms [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $\gamma$ is irrational.

*Image: KhinchinBeispiele.svg.* The Khinchin limits for $\pi$ (red), $\gamma$ (blue) and $\sqrt[3]{2}$ (green).

Numerical evidence suggests that both Euler's constant $\gamma$ as well as the constant $e^{1\operatorname{px}}$ are among the numbers for which the [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046) of their simple continued fraction terms converges to [Khinchin's constant](wikipedia://en/435315). Similarly, when $p_n/q_n$ are the convergents of their respective continued fractions, the limit $\lim_{n\to\infty}q_n^{1/n}$ appears to converge to [Lévy's constant](wikipedia://en/1067914) in both cases.[^o59] However neither of these limits has been proven.[^o60]

There also exists a [generalized continued fraction](wikipedia://en/739199) for Euler's constant.[^o61]

A good simple [approximation](wikipedia://en/336271) of $\gamma$ is given by the [reciprocal](wikipedia://en/229940) of the [square root of 3](wikipedia://en/9126665) or about 0.57735:[^o62]

$$\frac1\sqrt {3}=0+\cfrac{1}{1+\cfrac{1}{1+\cfrac{1}{2+\cfrac{1}{1+\cfrac{1}{2+\cfrac{1}{1+\cfrac{1}{2+\dots}}}}}}}$$

with the difference being about 1 in 7,429.

## Formulas and identities {#Formulas_and_identities}

### Relation to gamma function {#Relation_to_gamma_function}

$\gamma$ is related to the [digamma function](wikipedia://en/406935) $\Psi$ and hence to the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316) $\Gamma$, when both functions are evaluated at 1. Specifically:

$$-\gamma = \Gamma'(1) = \psi(1).$$

This is equal to the limits:

$$\begin{aligned}-\gamma &= \lim_{z\to 0}\left(\Gamma(z) - \frac1{z}\right) \\&= \lim_{z\to 0}\left(\psi(z) + \frac1{z}\right).\end{aligned}$$

Further limit results are:

$$\begin{aligned} \lim_{z\to 0}\frac1{z}\left(\frac1{\Gamma(1+z)} - \frac1{\Gamma(1-z)}\right) &= 2\gamma \\ \lim_{z\to 0}\frac1{z}\left(\frac1{\psi(1-z)} - \frac1{\psi(1+z)}\right) &= \frac{\pi^2}{3\gamma^2}. \end{aligned}$$

A limit related to the [beta function](wikipedia://en/245660) (expressed in terms of [gamma functions](wikipedia://en/12316)) is

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma &= \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{ \Gamma\left(\frac1{n}\right) \Gamma(n+1)\, n^{1+\frac1{n}}}{\Gamma\left(2+n+\frac1{n}\right)} - \frac{n^2}{n+1}\right) \\ &= \lim\limits_{m\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^m{m \choose k}\frac{(-1)^k}{k}\log\big(\Gamma(k+1)\big). \end{aligned}$$

### Relation to the zeta function {#Relation_to_the_zeta_function}

$\gamma$ can also be expressed as an [infinite sum](wikipedia://en/15287) whose terms involve the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809) evaluated at positive integers:

$$\begin{aligned}\gamma &= \sum_{m=2}^{\infty} (-1)^m\frac{\zeta(m)}{m} \\ &= \log\frac4{\pi} + \sum_{m=2}^{\infty} (-1)^m\frac{\zeta(m)}{2^{m-1}m}.\end{aligned}$$

The constant $\gamma$ can also be expressed in terms of the sum of the reciprocals of [non-trivial zeros](wikipedia://en/19344125) $\rho$ of the zeta function:[^o67]

$$\gamma = \log 4\pi + \sum_{\rho} \frac{2}{\rho} - 2$$

Other series related to the zeta function include:

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma &= \tfrac3{2}- \log 2 - \sum_{m=2}^\infty (-1)^m\,\frac{m-1}{m}\big(\zeta(m)-1\big) \\ &= \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{2n-1}{2n} - \log n + \sum_{k=2}^n \left(\frac1{k} - \frac{\zeta(1-k)}{n^k}\right)\right) \\ &= \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{2^n}{e^{2^n}} \sum_{m=0}^\infty \frac{2^{mn}}{(m+1)!} \sum_{t=0}^m \frac1{t+1} - n \log 2+ O \left (\frac1{2^{n}\, e^{2^n}}\right)\right).\end{aligned}$$

The error term in the last equation is a rapidly decreasing function of $n$. As a result, the formula is well-suited for efficient computation of the constant to high precision.

Other interesting limits equaling Euler's constant are the antisymmetric limit:

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma &= \lim_{s\to 1^+}\sum_{n=1}^\infty \left(\frac1{n^s}-\frac1{s^n}\right) \\&= \lim_{s\to 1}\left(\zeta(s) - \frac{1}{s-1}\right) \\&= \lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\zeta(1+s)+\zeta(1-s)}{2} \end{aligned}$$

and the following formula, established in 1898 by [de la Vallée-Poussin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Jean_de_la_Vallée-Poussin):

$$\gamma = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1{n}\, \sum_{k=1}^n \left(\left\lceil \frac{n}{k} \right\rceil - \frac{n}{k}\right)$$

where $\lceil \rceil$ are [ceiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceiling_function) brackets. This formula indicates that when taking any positive integer $n$ and dividing it by each positive integer $k$ less than $n$, the average fraction by which the quotient $n/k$ falls short of the next integer tends to $\gamma$ (rather than 0.5) as $n$ tends to infinity.

Closely related to this is the [rational zeta series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_zeta_series) expression. By taking separately the first few terms of the series above, one obtains an estimate for the classical series limit:

$$\gamma =\lim_{n\to\infty}\left( \sum_{k=1}^n \frac1{k} - \log n -\sum_{m=2}^\infty \frac{\zeta(m,n+1)}{m}\right),$$

where $\zeta(s, k)$ is the [Hurwitz zeta function](wikipedia://en/406952). The sum in this equation involves the harmonic numbers, $H_{n}$. Expanding some of the terms in the Hurwitz zeta function gives:

$$H_n = \log(n) + \gamma + \frac1{2n} - \frac1{12n^2} + \frac1{120n^4} - \varepsilon,$$

where $0 < \varepsilon < \tfrac{1}{252n^{6}}.$

$\gamma$ can also be expressed as follows where $A$ is the [Glaisher–Kinkelin constant](wikipedia://en/2076831):

$$\gamma =12\,\log(A)-\log(2\pi)+\frac{6}{\pi^2}\,\zeta'(2)$$

$\gamma$ can also be expressed as follows, which can be proven by expressing the [zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809) as a [Laurent series](wikipedia://en/61213):

$$\gamma=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(-n+\zeta\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)\right)$$

### Relation to triangular numbers {#Relation_to_triangular_numbers}

Numerous formulations have been derived that express $\gamma$ in terms of sums and logarithms of [triangular numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangular_numbers).[^o69][^o70][^o71][^o72] One of the earliest of these is a formula[^o73][^o74] for the $n$th [harmonic number](wikipedia://en/214729) attributed to [Srinivasa Ramanujan](wikipedia://en/47717) where $\gamma$ is related to $\textstyle \log 2T_{k}$ in a series that considers the powers of $\textstyle \frac{1}{T_{k}}$ (an earlier, less-generalizable proof[^o75][^o76] by [Ernesto Cesàro](wikipedia://en/605144) gives the first two terms of the series, with an error term):

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma &= H_u - \frac{1}{2} \log 2T_u - \sum_{k=1}^{v}\frac{R(k)}{T_{u}^{k}}-\Theta_{v}\,\frac{R(v+1)}{T_{u}^{v+1}} \end{aligned}$$

From [Stirling's approximation](wikipedia://en/151783)[^o69][^o77] follows a similar series:

$$\gamma = \log 2\pi - \sum_{k=2}^{\infty} \frac{\zeta(k)}{T_{k}}$$

The series of inverse triangular numbers also features in the study of the [Basel problem](wikipedia://en/490303)[^n4][^o79] posed by [Pietro Mengoli](wikipedia://en/11335853). Mengoli proved that $\textstyle \sum_{k = 1}^\infty \frac{1}{2T_k} = 1$, a result [Jacob Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/64740) later used to estimate the [value](wikipedia://en/490303#The_Riemann_zeta_function) of $\zeta(2)$, placing it between $1$ and $\textstyle \sum_{k = 1}^\infty \frac{2}{2T_k} = \sum_{k = 1}^\infty \frac{1}{T_{k}} = 2$. This identity appears in a formula used by [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) to compute [roots of the zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eulers_constant#Relation_to_the_zeta_function),[^o80] where $\gamma$ is expressed in terms of the sum of roots $\rho$ plus the difference between Boya's expansion and the series of exact [unit fractions](wikipedia://en/491316) $\textstyle \sum_{k = 1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{T_{k}}$:

$$\gamma - \log 2 = \log 2\pi + \sum_{\rho} \frac{2}{\rho} - \sum_{k = 1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{T_k}$$

### Integrals {#Integrals}

$\gamma$ equals the value of a number of definite [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532):

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma &= - \int_0^\infty e^{-x} \log x \,dx \\ &= -\int_0^1\log\left(\log\frac 1 x \right) dx \\ &= \int_0^\infty \left(\frac1{e^x-1}-\frac1{x\cdot e^x} \right)dx \\ &= \int_0^1\frac{1-e^{-x}}{x} \, dx -\int_1^\infty \frac{e^{-x}}{x}\, dx\\ &= \int_0^1\left(\frac1{\log x} + \frac1{1-x}\right)dx\\ &= \int_0^\infty \left(\frac1{1+x^k}-e^{-x}\right)\frac{dx}{x},\quad k>0\\ &= 2\int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-x^2}-e^{-x}}{x} \, dx ,\\ &= \frac{1}{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\log\left(1+\frac{\log\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\right)dt \\ &= \frac{1}{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2x\,dx}{(x^2+1)(e^{2\pi x}-1)} \\ &= \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin x}{x}e^{x\cot x}\log\left(\frac{\sin x}{x}e^{x\cot x}\right)dx \\ &= \int_0^1 H_x \, dx, \\ &= 1-\int_0^1 \{1/x\} dx \end{aligned}$$

where $H_{x}$ is the [fractional harmonic number](wikipedia://en/214729#Harmonic_numbers_for_real_and_complex_values), and $\{1/x\}$ is the [fractional part](wikipedia://en/501532) of $1/x$.

Definite integrals in which $\gamma$ appears include:[^o82][^o83]

$$\begin{aligned} \int_0^\infty e^{-x^2} \log x \,dx &= -\frac{(\gamma+2\log 2)\sqrt{\pi}}{4} \\ \int_0^\infty e^{-x} \log^2 x \,dx &= \gamma^2 + \frac{\pi^2}{6} \\ \int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-x}\log x}{e^x +1} \,dx &= \frac12 \log^2 2 - \gamma \\ \int_0^\infty \frac{\log x}{\cosh^2x} \, dx &= \log\frac{\pi}{4}-\gamma \end{aligned}$$

We also have [Catalan](wikipedia://en/639553)'s 1875 integral

$$\gamma = \int_0^1 \left(\frac1{1+x}\sum_{n=1}^\infty x^{2^n-1}\right)\,dx.$$

One can express $\gamma$ using a special case of [Hadjicostas's formula](wikipedia://en/19423821) as a [double integral](wikipedia://en/2757224#Double_integral) with equivalent series:

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma &= \int_0^1 \int_0^1 \frac{x-1}{(1-xy)\log xy}\,dx\,dy \\ &= \sum_{n=1}^\infty \left(\frac 1 n -\log\frac{n+1} n \right). \end{aligned}$$

An interesting comparison by Sondow is the double integral and alternating series

$$\begin{aligned} \log\frac 4 \pi &= \int_0^1 \int_0^1 \frac{x-1}{(1+xy)\log xy} \,dx\,dy \\ &= \sum_{n=1}^\infty \left((-1)^{n-1}\left(\frac 1 n -\log\frac{n+1} n \right)\right). \end{aligned}$$

It shows that $\log \tfrac{4}{\pi}$ may be thought of as an "alternating Euler constant".

### Series expansions {#Series_expansions}

The two constants are also related by the pair of series

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma &= \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{N_1(n) + N_0(n)}{2n(2n+1)} \\ \log\frac4{\pi} &= \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{N_1(n) - N_0(n)}{2n(2n+1)} , \end{aligned}$$

where $N_{1}(n)$ and $N_{0}(n)$ are the number of 1s and 0s, respectively, in the [base 2](wikipedia://en/238686) expansion of $n$.

This series for $\gamma$ is equivalent to a series [Nielsen](wikipedia://en/33935937) found in 1897:[^n5]

$$\gamma = 1 - \sum_{k=2}^\infty (-1)^k\frac{\left\lfloor\log_2 k\right\rfloor}{k+1}.$$

In 1910, [Vacca](wikipedia://en/2452847) found the closely related series

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma & = \sum_{k=1}^\infty (-1)^k\frac{\left\lfloor\log_2 k\right\rfloor} k \\[5pt] & = \tfrac12-\tfrac13 + 2\left(\tfrac14 - \tfrac15 + \tfrac16 - \tfrac17\right) + 3\left(\tfrac18 - \tfrac19 + \tfrac1{10} - \tfrac1{11} + \cdots - \tfrac1{15}\right) + \cdots, \end{aligned}$$

where $\log_{2}$ is the [logarithm to base 2](wikipedia://en/249992) and $\lfloor \rfloor$ is the [floor function](wikipedia://en/54267).

This can be generalized to:[^o86]

$$\gamma= \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\left\lfloor\log_B k\right\rfloor}{k} \varepsilon(k)$$

where:

$$\varepsilon(k)= \begin{cases} B-1, &\text{if } B\mid n \\ -1, &\text{if }B\nmid n \end{cases}$$

In 1926 Vacca found a second series:

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma + \zeta(2) & = \sum_{k=2}^\infty \left( \frac1{\left\lfloor\sqrt{k}\right\rfloor^2} - \frac1{k}\right) \\[5pt] & = \sum_{k=2}^\infty \frac{k - \left\lfloor\sqrt{k}\right\rfloor^2}{k \left\lfloor \sqrt{k} \right\rfloor^2} \\[5pt] &= \frac12 + \frac23 + \frac1{2^2}\sum_{k=1}^{2\cdot 2} \frac{k}{k+2^2} + \frac1{3^2}\sum_{k=1}^{3\cdot 2} \frac{k}{k+3^2} + \cdots \end{aligned}$$

From the [Malmsten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Johan_Malmsten)–[Kummer](wikipedia://en/319117) expansion for the logarithm of the gamma function[^o83] we get:

$$\gamma = \log\pi - 4\log\left(\Gamma(\tfrac34)\right) + \frac 4 \pi \sum_{k=1}^\infty (-1)^{k+1}\frac{\log(2k+1)}{2k+1}.$$

Ramanujan, in his [lost notebook](wikipedia://en/6034957) gave a series that approaches $\gamma$:

$$\gamma = \log 2 - \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sum_{k=\frac{3^{n-1}+1}{2}}^{\frac{3^{n}-1}{2}} \frac{2n}{(3k)^3-3k}$$

An important expansion for Euler's constant is due to [Fontana](wikipedia://en/6942423) and [Mascheroni](wikipedia://en/88116)

$$\gamma = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{|G_n|}{n} = \frac12 + \frac1{24} + \frac1{72} + \frac{19}{2880} + \frac3{800} + \cdots,$$

where $G_{n}$ are [Gregory coefficients](wikipedia://en/50593506). This series is the special case $k = 1$ of the expansions

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma &= H_{k-1} - \log k + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(n-1)!|G_n|}{k(k+1) \cdots (k+n-1)} && \\ &= H_{k-1} - \log k + \frac1{2k} + \frac1{12k(k+1)} + \frac1{12k(k+1)(k+2)} + \frac{19}{120k(k+1)(k+2)(k+3)} + \cdots && \end{aligned}$$

convergent for $k = 1, 2, ...$

A similar series with the Cauchy numbers of the second kind $C_{n}$ is

$$\gamma = 1 - \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{C_{n}}{n \, (n+1)!} =1- \frac{1}{4} -\frac{5}{72} - \frac{1}{32} - \frac{251}{14400} - \frac{19}{1728} - \ldots$$

Blagouchine (2018) found a generalisation of the Fontana–Mascheroni series

$$\gamma=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2n}\Big\{\psi_{n}(a)+ \psi_{n}\Big(-\frac{a}{1+a}\Big)\Big\}, \quad a>-1$$

where $\psi_{n}(a)$ are the [Bernoulli polynomials of the second kind](wikipedia://en/58580000), which are defined by the [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993)

$$\frac{z(1+z)^s}{\log(1+z)}= \sum_{n=0}^\infty z^n \psi_n(s) ,\qquad |z|<1.$$

For any rational $a$ this series contains rational terms only. For example, at $a = 1$, it becomes

$$\gamma=\frac{3}{4} - \frac{11}{96} - \frac{1}{72} - \frac{311}{46080} - \frac{5}{1152} - \frac{7291}{2322432} - \frac{243}{100352} - \ldots$$

Other series with the same polynomials include these examples:

$$\gamma= -\log(a+1) - \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n \psi_{n}(a)}{n},\qquad \Re(a)>-1$$

and

$$\gamma= -\frac{2}{1+2a} \left\{\log\Gamma(a+1) -\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi) + \frac{1}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n \psi_{n+1}(a)}{n}\right\},\qquad \Re(a)>-1$$

where $\Gamma(a)$ is the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316).

A series related to the Akiyama–Tanigawa algorithm is

$$\gamma= \log(2\pi) - 2 - 2 \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n G_{n}(2)}{n}= \log(2\pi) - 2 + \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{24}+ \frac{7}{540} + \frac{17}{2880}+ \frac{41}{12600} + \ldots$$

where $G_{n}(2)$ are the [Gregory coefficients](wikipedia://en/50593506) of the second order.

As a series of [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666):

$$\gamma = \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\log n - \sum_{p\le n}\frac{\log p}{p-1}\right).$$

Alabdulmohsin derived closed-form expressions for the sums of errors of approximations for the harmonic numbers:

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_{n=1}^\infty \Big(\log n +\gamma + \frac{1}{2n} -H_n\Big) &= \frac{\log (2\pi)-1-\gamma}{2} \\ \sum_{n=1}^\infty \Big(\log \sqrt{n(n+1)} +\gamma - H_n \Big) &= \frac{\log (2\pi)-1}{2}-\gamma \\ \sum_{n=1}^\infty (-1)^n\Big(\log n +\gamma - H_n\Big) &= \frac{\log \pi-\gamma}{2} \end{aligned}$$

### Exponential {#Exponential}

The constant $e^{1\operatorname{px}}$ is important in number theory. Its numerical value is:

\1.78107. $e^{1\operatorname{px}}$ equals the following [limit](wikipedia://en/285773), where $p_{n}$ is the $n$th [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666):

$$e^\gamma = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1{\log p_n} \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{p_i}{p_i-1}.$$

This restates the third of [Mertens' theorems](wikipedia://en/982970).

We further have the following product involving the three constants $e$, $\pi$ and $\gamma$:[^o88]

$$\frac{\pi^2}{6e^\gamma}=\lim_{n\to\infty} \log p_n \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{p_i}{p_i+1}.$$

The exponential also appears in the [Weierstrass product formula](wikipedia://en/1585155) for the reciprocal of the gamma function:[^o16]

$$\frac1{\Gamma(z)}=ze^{\gamma z}\prod_{n=1}^\infty\left(e^{-\frac{z}{n}}\left(1+\frac{z}{n}\right)\right).$$

It also holds that

$$\frac{e^\frac{\pi}{2}+e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}}}{\pi e^\gamma}=\prod_{n=1}^\infty\left(e^{-\frac{1}{n}}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{2n^2}\right)\right).$$

Other [infinite products](wikipedia://en/26382006) relating to $e^{1\operatorname{px}}$ include:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{e^{1+\frac{\gamma}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}} &= \prod_{n=1}^\infty e^{-1+\frac1{2n}}\left(1+\frac1{n}\right)^n \\ \frac{e^{3+2\gamma}}{2\pi} &= \prod_{n=1}^\infty e^{-2+\frac2{n}}\left(1+\frac2{n}\right)^n. \end{aligned}$$

These products result from the [Barnes $G$-function](wikipedia://en/2060183).

In addition,

$$e^{\gamma} = \sqrt{\frac2{1}} \cdot \sqrt[3]{\frac{2^2}{1\cdot 3}} \cdot \sqrt[4]{\frac{2^3\cdot 4}{1\cdot 3^3}} \cdot \sqrt[5]{\frac{2^4\cdot 4^4}{1\cdot 3^6\cdot 5}} \cdots$$

where the $n$th factor is the $(n + 1)$th root of

$$\prod_{k=0}^n (k+1)^{(-1)^{k+1}{n \choose k}}.$$

This infinite product, first discovered by Ser in 1926, was rediscovered by Sondow using [hypergeometric functions](wikipedia://en/2055223).

:::

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### Stieltjes constants {#Stieltjes_constants}

*Image: Generalisation of Euler–Mascheroni constant.jpg.* Euler's generalized constants $\operatorname{abm}$$(-\alpha)$ for $\alpha>0$.

*Euler's generalized constants* are given by

$$\gamma_\alpha = \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\sum_{k=1}^n \frac1{k^\alpha} - \int_1^n \frac1{x^\alpha}\,dx\right)$$

for $0 < \alpha < 1$, with $\gamma$ as the special case $\alpha = 1$.[^n6] Extending for $\alpha > 1$ gives:

$$\gamma_{\alpha} = \zeta(\alpha) - \frac1{\alpha-1}$$

with again the limit:

$$\gamma = \lim_{a\to 1}\left(\zeta(a) - \frac1{a-1}\right)$$

This can be further generalized to

$$c_f = \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\sum_{k=1}^n f(k) - \int_1^n f(x)\,dx\right)$$

for some arbitrary decreasing function $f$. Setting

$$f_n(x) = \frac{(\log x)^n}{x}$$

gives rise to the [Stieltjes constants](wikipedia://en/1479085) $\gamma_n$, that occur in the [Laurent series](wikipedia://en/61213) expansion of the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809):

$$\zeta(1+s)=\frac{1}{s}+\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n}{n!} \gamma_n s^n.$$

with $\gamma_0 = \gamma = 0.577\dots$

### Euler–Lehmer constants {#Euler–Lehmer_constants}

*Euler–Lehmer constants* are given by summation of inverses of numbers in a common modulo class:

$$\gamma(a,q) = \lim_{x\to \infty}\left (\sum_{0<n\le x \atop n\equiv a \pmod q} \frac1{n}-\frac{\log x}{q}\right).$$

The basic properties are

$$\begin{aligned} &\gamma(0,q) = \frac{\gamma -\log q}{q}, \\ &\sum_{a=0}^{q-1} \gamma(a,q)=\gamma, \\ &q\gamma(a,q) = \gamma-\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}e^{-\frac{2\pi aij}{q}}\log\left(1-e^{\frac{2\pi ij}{q}}\right), \end{aligned}$$

and if the [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) $\gcd(a,q) = d$ then

$$q\gamma(a,q) = \frac{q}{d}\gamma\left(\frac{a}{d},\frac{q}{d}\right)-\log d.$$

### Masser–Gramain constant {#Masser–Gramain_constant}

A two-dimensional generalization of Euler's constant is the [Masser–Gramain constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masser–Gramain_constant). It is defined as the following limiting difference:[^o115]

$$\delta = \lim_{n\to\infty} \left( -\log n + \sum_{k=2}^n \frac{1}{\pi r_k^2} \right)$$

where $r_k$ is the smallest radius of a disk in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) containing at least $k$ [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628).

The following bounds have been established: $1.819776 < \delta < 1.819833$.[^o116]
