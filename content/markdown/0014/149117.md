# Thomas Bayes

::: {#o5 .subject cue=lead name="Thomas Bayes"}

**Thomas Bayes** ( ; c. 1701 – 7 April 1761[^o3][^o6]) was an English [statistician](wikipedia://en/48661), [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher) and [Presbyterian minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian_minister) who is known for formulating a specific case of the theorem that bears his name: [Bayes' theorem](wikipedia://en/49569).

:::

Bayes never published what would become his most famous accomplishment; his notes were edited and published posthumously by [Richard Price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Price).[^o7]

## Biography {#Biography}

*Image: Former Presbyterian Meeting House, Little Mount Sion, Tunbridge Wells.JPG.* Mount Sion Chapel, where Bayes served as minister.

Thomas Bayes was the son of London Presbyterian minister [Joshua Bayes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua_Bayes), and was possibly born in [Hertfordshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertfordshire).[^o10] He came from a prominent [nonconformist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonconformist_%28Protestantism%29) family from [Sheffield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheffield). In 1719, he enrolled at the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh) to study [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) and theology. On his return around 1722, he assisted his father at the latter's chapel in London before moving to [Tunbridge Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Tunbridge_Wells), Kent, around 1734. There he was minister of the Mount Sion Chapel, until 1752.[^o11]

He is known to have published two works in his lifetime, one theological and one mathematical:

1. *Divine Benevolence, or an Attempt to Prove That the Principal End of the Divine Providence and Government is the Happiness of His Creatures* (1731)
1. *An Introduction to the Doctrine of Fluxions, and a Defence of the Mathematicians Against the Objections of the Author of The Analyst* (published anonymously in 1736), in which he defended the logical foundation of [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627)'s [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) ("fluxions") against the criticism by [George Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Berkeley), a bishop and noted philosopher, the author of *[The Analyst](wikipedia://en/341394)*

Bayes was elected as a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society) in 1742. His nomination letter was signed by [Philip Stanhope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Stanhope,_2nd_Earl_Stanhope), [Martin Folkes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Folkes), [James Burrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Burrow), [Cromwell Mortimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cromwell_Mortimer), and [John Eames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Eames). It is speculated that he was accepted by the society on the strength of the *Introduction to the Doctrine of Fluxions*, as he is not known to have published any other mathematical work during his lifetime.[^o12]

In his later years he took a deep interest in probability. Historian [Stephen Stigler](wikipedia://en/1276686) thinks that Bayes became interested in the subject while reviewing a work written in 1755 by [Thomas Simpson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Simpson),[^o13] but [George Alfred Barnard](wikipedia://en/2792579) thinks he learned mathematics and probability from a book by [Abraham de Moivre](wikipedia://en/57327).[^o14] Others speculate he was motivated to rebut [David Hume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hume)'s argument against believing in miracles on the evidence of testimony in *[An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Enquiry_Concerning_Human_Understanding)*.[^o15] His work and findings on probability theory were passed in manuscript form to his friend [Richard Price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Price) after his death.

*Image: Bayes-Cotton Tomb at Bunhill Fields - geograph.org.uk - 702746.jpg.* Monument to members of the Bayes and Cotton families, including Thomas Bayes and his father Joshua, in [Bunhill Fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunhill_Fields) burial ground

By 1755, he was ill, and by 1761, he had died in Tunbridge Wells. He was buried in [Bunhill Fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunhill_Fields) burial ground in Moorgate, London, where many [nonconformists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonconformist_%28Protestantism%29) lie.

In 2018, the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh) opened a £45 million research centre connected to its informatics department named after its alumnus, Bayes.[^o16]

In April 2021, it was announced that [Cass Business School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cass_Business_School), whose [City of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London) campus is on [Bunhill Row](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunhill_Row), was to be renamed after Bayes.[^o16]

## Bayes' theorem {#Bayes'_theorem}

Bayes's solution to a problem of [inverse probability](wikipedia://en/908703) was presented in *[An Essay Towards Solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances](wikipedia://en/33200324)*, which was read to the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society) in 1763 after Bayes's death. [Richard Price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Price) shepherded the work through this presentation and its publication in the *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London* the following year.[^o19] This was an argument for using a uniform prior distribution for a binomial parameter and not merely a general postulate.[^o20] This essay gives the following theorem (stated here in present-day terminology).

> Suppose a quantity *R* is [uniformly distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29) between 0 and 1. Suppose each of *X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*n*</sub> is equal to either 1 or 0 and the [conditional probability](wikipedia://en/24104134) that any of them is equal to 1, given the value of *R*, is *R*. Suppose they are [conditionally independent](wikipedia://en/801135) given the value of *R*. Then the conditional probability distribution of *R*, given the values of *X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*n*</sub>, is
>
> $$\frac {(n+1)!}{S!(n-S)!} r^S (1-r)^{n-S} \, dr \quad \text{for }0\le r\le 1, \text{ where } S=X_1+\cdots+X_n.$$

Thus, for example,

$$\Pr(R \le r_0 \mid X_1,\ldots,X_n) = \frac{(n+1)!}{S!(n-S)!} \int_0^{r_0} r^S (1-r)^{n-S} \, dr.$$

This is a special case of the [Bayes' theorem](wikipedia://en/49569).

In the first decades of the eighteenth century, many problems concerning the probability of certain events, given specified conditions, were solved. For example: given a specified number of white and black balls in an urn, what is the probability of drawing a black ball? Or the converse: given that one or more balls has been drawn, what can be said about the number of white and black balls in the urn? These are sometimes called "[inverse probability](wikipedia://en/908703)" problems.

Bayes's *Essay* contains his solution to a similar problem posed by [Abraham de Moivre](wikipedia://en/57327), author of *[The Doctrine of Chances](wikipedia://en/161715)* (1718).

In addition, a paper by Bayes on [asymptotic series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_series) was only published posthumously.

## Bayesianism {#Bayesianism}

[Bayesian probability](wikipedia://en/4890) is the name given to several related interpretations of [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) as an amount of epistemic confidence – the strength of beliefs, hypotheses etc. – rather than a frequency. This allows the application of probability to all sorts of propositions rather than just ones that come with a reference class. "Bayesian" has been used in this sense since about 1950. Since its rebirth in the 1950s, advancements in computing technology have allowed scientists from many disciplines to pair traditional Bayesian statistics with [random walk](wikipedia://en/236801) techniques. The use of the [Bayes' theorem](wikipedia://en/49569) has been extended in science and in other fields.[^o22]

Bayes himself might not have embraced the broad interpretation now called Bayesian, which was in fact pioneered and popularised by [Pierre-Simon Laplace](wikipedia://en/344783);[^o23] it is difficult to assess Bayes's philosophical views on probability, since his essay does not go into questions of interpretation. There, Bayes defines *probability* of an event as "the ratio between the value at which an expectation depending on the happening of the event ought to be computed, and the value of the thing expected upon its happening" (Definition 5). In modern [utility](wikipedia://en/45479) theory, the same definition would result by rearranging the definition of expected utility (the probability of an event times the payoff received in case of that event – including the special cases of buying risk for small amounts or buying security for big amounts) to solve for the probability. As Stigler points out,[^o13] this is a subjective definition, and does not require repeated events; however, it does require that the event in question be observable, for otherwise it could never be said to have "happened". Stigler argues that Bayes intended his results in a more limited way than modern Bayesians. Given Bayes's definition of probability, his result concerning the parameter of a [binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876) makes sense only to the extent that one can bet on its observable consequences.

The philosophy of Bayesian statistics is at the core of almost every modern estimation approach that includes conditioned probabilities, such as sequential estimation, probabilistic machine learning techniques, risk assessment, simultaneous localization and mapping, regularization or information theory. The rigorous axiomatic framework for probability theory as a whole, however, was developed 200 years later during the early and middle 20th century, starting with insightful results in [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986) by [Plancherel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plancherel) in 1913.
