# Magma (algebra)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Magma"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **magma**, **binar**,[^o2] or, rarely, **groupoid** is a basic kind of [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364). Specifically, a magma consists of a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) equipped with a single [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) that must be [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closure_%28binary_operation%29) by definition. No other properties are imposed.

:::

## History and terminology {#History_and_terminology}

The term *groupoid* was introduced in 1927 by [Heinrich Brandt](wikipedia://en/600885) describing his [Brandt groupoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandt_groupoid). The term was then appropriated by B. A. Hausmann and [Øystein Ore](wikipedia://en/1643733) (1937)[^o4] in the sense (of a set with a binary operation) used in this article. In a couple of reviews of subsequent papers in [Zentralblatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zentralblatt), Brandt strongly disagreed with this overloading of terminology. The Brandt groupoid is a [groupoid](wikipedia://en/12543) in the sense used in category theory, but not in the sense used by Hausmann and Ore. Nevertheless, influential books in semigroup theory, including [Clifford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Hoblitzelle_Clifford) and [Preston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._B._Preston) (1961) and [Howie](wikipedia://en/2916430) (1995) use groupoid in the sense of Hausmann and Ore. Hollings (2014) writes that the term *groupoid* is "perhaps most often used in modern mathematics" in the sense given to it in category theory.[^o5]

According to Bergman and Hausknecht (1996): "There is no generally accepted word for a set with a not necessarily associative binary operation. The word *groupoid* is used by many universal algebraists, but workers in category theory and related areas object strongly to this usage because they use the same word to mean 'category in which all morphisms are invertible'. The term *magma* was used by [Serre](wikipedia://en/205483) \[Lie Algebras and Lie Groups, 1965\]."[^o6] It also appears in [Bourbaki](wikipedia://en/167394)'s [Éléments de mathématique](wikipedia://en/38333812), Algèbre, chapitres 1 à 3, 1970.[^o7]

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A magma is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) *M* with an [operation](wikipedia://en/3948) • that sends any two [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29) *a*, *b* ∈ *M* to another element, *a* • *b* ∈ *M*. The symbol • is a general placeholder for a properly defined operation. This requirement that for all *a*, *b* in *M*, the result of the operation *a* • *b* also be in *M*, is known as the *magma* or [closure property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closure_property). In mathematical notation:

$$\forall a,b\colon a, b \in M \implies a \cdot b \in M.$$

If • is instead a [partial operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_operation), then (*M*, •) is called a **partial magma**[^o10] or, more often, a [partial groupoid](wikipedia://en/43662522).[^o10][^o11]

:::

## Morphism of magmas {#Morphism_of_magmas}

A [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) of magmas is a function *f* : *M* → *N* that maps a magma (*M*, •) to a magma (*N*, ∗) and preserves the binary operation:

*f* (*x* • *y*) = *f*(*x*) ∗ *f*(*y*).

For example, with *M* equal to the [positive real numbers](wikipedia://en/47172571) and • as the [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046), *N* equal to the real number line, and ∗ as the [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612), a [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) *f* is a morphism of the magma (*M*, •) to (*N*, ∗).

proof: $\log{\sqrt{x y} } \ = \ \frac{\log x + \log y}{2}.$

Note that these commutative magmas are not associative; nor do they have an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962). This morphism of magmas has been used in [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics) since 1863 when [William Stanley Jevons](wikipedia://en/207021) calculated the rate of [inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation) in 39 commodities in England in his *A Serious Fall in the Value of Gold Ascertained*.[^o13]

## Notation and combinatorics {#Notation_and_combinatorics}

The magma operation may be applied repeatedly, and in the general, non-associative case, the order matters, which is notated with parentheses. Also, the operation • is often omitted and notated by juxtaposition:

$$(a \bullet (b \bullet c)) \bullet d \equiv (a(bc))d.$$

A shorthand is often used to reduce the number of parentheses, in which the innermost operations and pairs of parentheses are omitted, being replaced just with juxtaposition: $xy \bullet z \equiv (x \bullet y) \bullet z$. For example, the above is abbreviated to the following expression, still containing parentheses:

$$(a \bullet bc)d.$$

A way to avoid completely the use of parentheses is [prefix notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefix_notation), in which the same expression would be written $\bullet\bullet a\bullet bcd$. Another way, familiar to programmers, is [postfix notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postfix_notation) ([reverse Polish notation](wikipedia://en/26513)), in which the same expression would be written $abc\bullet\bullet d\bullet$, in which the order of execution is simply left-to-right (no [currying](wikipedia://en/6600)).

The set of all possible [strings](wikipedia://en/27701) consisting of symbols denoting elements of the magma, and sets of balanced parentheses is called the [Dyck language](wikipedia://en/1852078). The total number of different ways of writing $n$ applications of the magma operator is given by the [Catalan number](wikipedia://en/216488) $C_{n}$. Thus, for example, $C_{2} = 2$, which is just the statement that $(ab)c$ and $a(bc)$ are the only two ways of pairing three elements of a magma with two operations. Less trivially, $C_{3} = 5$: $((ab)c)d$, $(a(bc))d$, $(ab)(cd)$, $a((bc)d)$, and $a(b(cd))$.

For an $n$-element set, there are $n^{n^{2}}$ magmas associated with that set, so for $n = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, ...$, there are 1, 1, 16, 19683, 4294967296, ... magmas. The corresponding numbers of magmas up to [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) are 1, 1, 10, 3330, 178981952, ... and the numbers of magmas up to isomorphism and [antiisomorphism](wikipedia://en/2152465) are 1, 1, 7, 1734, 89521056, ... .

## Free magma {#Free_magma}

A **free magma** $M_X$ on a set $X$ is the "most general possible" magma generated by $X$ (i.e., there are no relations or axioms imposed on the generators; see [free object](wikipedia://en/333835)). The binary operation on $M_X$ is formed by wrapping each of the two operands in parentheses and juxtaposing them in the same order. For example

$$a\cdot b = (a)(b),$$

$$a\cdot (a\cdot b) = (a)((a)(b)),$$

$$(a\cdot a)\cdot b = ((a)(a))(b).$$

$M_X$ is essentially the set of non-associative words on $X$ with parentheses retained.[^n1] It can also be viewed as the magma of full [binary trees](wikipedia://en/4321) with leaves labelled by elements of $X$. The operation is that of joining trees at the root.

A free magma $M_X$ on a set $X$ has the [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245) that if $f:X\to N$ is a function to any magma $N$, then there is a unique extension of $f$ to a morphism of magmas $f':M_X\to N$.

:::

## Types of magma {#Types_of_magma}

*Image: Magma to group2.svg.* Diagram showing the relationships between different algebraic structures

Magmas are not often studied as such; instead there are several different kinds of magma, depending on what axioms the operation is required to satisfy. Commonly studied types of magma include:

- **[Quasigroup](wikipedia://en/25223)**: A magma where [division](wikipedia://en/53696) is always possible.
  - [**Loop**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loop_%28algebra%29): A quasigroup with an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962).
- **[Semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799)**: A magma where the operation is [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative).
  - **[Monoid](wikipedia://en/19652)**: A semigroup with an identity element.
- [**Group**](wikipedia://en/19447): A magma with inverse, associativity, and an identity element.

Note that each of divisibility and invertibility imply the [cancellation property](wikipedia://en/490646).

**Magmas with [commutativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative)**

- **[Commutative magma](wikipedia://en/7741863)**: A magma with commutativity.
- **[Commutative monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_monoid)**: A monoid with commutativity.
- **[Abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974)**: A group with commutativity.

## Classification by properties {#Classification_by_properties}

A magma $(S, \bullet)$, with $x, y, u, z$ ∈ $S$, is called

**[Medial](wikipedia://en/502296)**

If it satisfies the identity $xy \bullet uz \equiv xu \bullet yz$

**Left semimedial**

If it satisfies the identity $xx \bullet yz \equiv xy \bullet xz$

**Right semimedial**

If it satisfies the identity $yz \bullet xx \equiv yx \bullet zx$

**Semimedial**

If it is both left and right semimedial

**Left distributive**

If it satisfies the identity $x \bullet yz \equiv xy \bullet xz$

**Right distributive**

If it satisfies the identity $yz \bullet x \equiv yx \bullet zx$

**Autodistributive**

If it is both left and right distributive

**[Commutative](wikipedia://en/7741863)**

If it satisfies the identity $xy \equiv yx$

**[Idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent)**

If it satisfies the identity $xx \equiv x$

**Unipotent**

If it satisfies the identity $xx \equiv yy$

**Zeropotent**

If it satisfies the identities $xx \bullet y \equiv xx \equiv y \bullet xx$[^o22]

**[Alternative](wikipedia://en/186050)**

If it satisfies the identities $xx \bullet y \equiv x \bullet xy$ and $x \bullet yy \equiv xy \bullet y$

**[Power-associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power-associative)**

If the submagma generated by any element is associative

**[Flexible](wikipedia://en/36035264)**

if $xy \bullet x \equiv x \bullet yx$

**[Associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative)**

If it satisfies the identity $x \bullet yz \equiv xy \bullet z$, called a [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799)

**A left unar**

If it satisfies the identity $xy \equiv xz$

**A right unar**

If it satisfies the identity $yx \equiv zx$

**Semigroup with zero multiplication, or [null semigroup](wikipedia://en/22694038)**

If it satisfies the identity $xy \equiv uv$

**Unital**

If it has an identity element

**Left-[cancellative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancellative)**

If, for all $x, y, z$, relation $xy = xz$ implies $y = z$

**Right-cancellative**

If, for all $x, y, z$, relation $yx = zx$ implies $y = z$

**Cancellative**

If it is both right-cancellative and left-cancellative

**A [semigroup with left zeros](wikipedia://en/22694038#Left_zero_semigroup)**

If it is a semigroup and it satisfies the identity $xy \equiv x$

**A [semigroup with right zeros](wikipedia://en/22694038#Right_zero_semigroup)**

If it is a semigroup and it satisfies the identity $yx \equiv x$

**Trimedial**

If any triple of (not necessarily distinct) elements generates a medial submagma

**Entropic**

If it is a [homomorphic image](wikipedia://en/45200) of a medial [cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancellative) magma.[^o23]

**[Central](wikipedia://en/78106994)**

If it satisfies the identity $xy \bullet yz \equiv y$

:::

## Category of magmas {#Category_of_magmas}

The category of magmas, denoted **Mag**, is the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) whose objects are magmas and whose [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) are [magma homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/141916#Morphism_of_magmas). The category **Mag** has [direct products](wikipedia://en/237868), and there is an [inclusion functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusion_functor): **[Set](wikipedia://en/26829) ↪ Mag** as trivial magmas, with [operations](wikipedia://en/3948) given by [projection](wikipedia://en/3909097) $x\,T\,y = y\,$. More generally, because **Mag** is [algebraic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_category), it is a [complete category](wikipedia://en/62781).[^o28]

An important property is that an [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569) can be extended to an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) of a magma [extension](wikipedia://en/2125), just the [colimit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colimit) of the ([constant](wikipedia://en/320861) sequence of the) [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569).
