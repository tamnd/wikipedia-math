# Kernel (category theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kernel"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869) and its applications to other branches of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **kernels** are a generalization of the kernels of [group homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/12396), the kernels of [module homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/474118) and certain other [kernels from algebra](wikipedia://en/45240).[^o2] Intuitively, the kernel of the [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) *f* : *X* → *Y* is the "most general" morphism *k* : *K* → *X* that yields zero when composed with (followed by) *f*.[^o3]

:::

[Kernel pairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_pair) and [difference kernels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_kernel) (also known as binary [equalisers](wikipedia://en/310767)) sometimes go by the name "kernel", and are not discussed in this article.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let **C** be a [category](wikipedia://en/5869). In order to define a kernel in the general category-theoretical sense, **C** needs to have [zero morphisms](wikipedia://en/320638). In that case, if *f* : *X* → *Y* is an arbitrary [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) in **C**, then a kernel of *f* is an [equaliser](wikipedia://en/310767) of *f* and the zero morphism from *X* to *Y*.[^o3] In symbols:

ker(*f*) = eq(*f*, 0<sub>*XY*</sub>)

To be more explicit, the following [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245) can be used. A kernel of *f* is an [object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) *K* together with a morphism *k* : *K* → *X* such that the following two assertions hold:

- *f*∘*k* is the zero morphism from *K* to *Y*;

*Image: First\_property\_of\_the\_kernel.svg.*

- Given any morphism *k* : *K* → *X* such that *f*∘*k* is the zero morphism, there is a unique morphism *u* : *K* → *K* such that *k*∘*u* = *k*.

*Image: Properties\_of\_a\_kernel.svg.*

As for every universal property, there is a unique isomorphism between two kernels of the same morphism, and the morphism *k* is always a [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538) (in the categorical sense). So, it is common to talk of *the* kernel of a morphism. In [concrete categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete_categories), one can thus take a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of *X* for *K*, in which case, the morphism *k* is the [inclusion map](wikipedia://en/492516). This allows one to talk of *K* as the kernel, since *k* is implicitly defined by *K*. There are non-concrete categories, where one can similarly define a "natural" kernel, such that *K* defines *k* implicitly.

Not every morphism needs to have a kernel, but if it does, then all its kernels are isomorphic in a strong sense: if *k* : *K* → *X* and $: L \to X$ are kernels of *f* : *X* → *Y*, then there exists a unique [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) φ : *K* → *L* such that ∘φ = *k*.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

Kernels are familiar in many categories from [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), such as the category of [groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_%28algebra%29) or the category of (left) [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) over a fixed [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) (including [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) over a fixed [field](wikipedia://en/10603)). To be explicit, if *f* : *X* → *Y* is a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) in one of these categories, and *K* is its [kernel in the usual algebraic sense](wikipedia://en/45240), then *K* is a [subobject](wikipedia://en/540476) of *X* and the inclusion homomorphism from *K* to *X* is a kernel in the categorical sense.

Note that in the category of [monoids](wikipedia://en/19652), category-theoretic kernels exist just as for groups, but these kernels don't carry sufficient information for algebraic purposes. Therefore, the notion of kernel studied in monoid theory is slightly different (see #Relationship to algebraic kernels below).

In the [category of unital rings](wikipedia://en/15300181), there are no kernels in the category-theoretic sense; indeed, this category does not even have zero morphisms. Nevertheless, there is still a notion of kernel studied in ring theory that corresponds to kernels in the [category of non-unital rings](wikipedia://en/15300181#Rings_without_identity).

In the category of [pointed topological spaces](wikipedia://en/1236666), if *f* : *X* → *Y* is a continuous pointed map, then the preimage of the distinguished point, *K*, is a subspace of *X*. The inclusion map of *K* into *X* is the categorical kernel of *f*.

:::

## Relation to other categorical concepts {#Relation_to_other_categorical_concepts}

The dual concept to that of kernel is that of [cokernel](wikipedia://en/279715). That is, the kernel of a morphism is its cokernel in the [opposite category](wikipedia://en/372399), and vice versa.

As mentioned above, a kernel is a type of binary equaliser, or [difference kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_kernel). Conversely, in a [preadditive category](wikipedia://en/59521), every binary equaliser can be constructed as a kernel. To be specific, the equaliser of the morphisms *f* and *g* is the kernel of the [difference](wikipedia://en/74141) *g* − *f*. In symbols:

eq (*f*, *g*) = ker (*g* − *f*).

It is because of this fact that binary equalisers are called "difference kernels", even in non-preadditive categories where morphisms cannot be subtracted.

Every kernel, like any other equaliser, is a [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538). Conversely, a monomorphism is called *[normal](wikipedia://en/142626)* if it is the kernel of some morphism. A category is called *normal* if every monomorphism is normal.

[Abelian categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_categories), in particular, are always normal. In this situation, the kernel of the [cokernel](wikipedia://en/279715) of any morphism (which always exists in an abelian category) turns out to be the [image](wikipedia://en/342899) of that morphism; in symbols:

im *f* = ker coker *f* (in an abelian category)

When *m* is a monomorphism, it must be its own image; thus, not only are abelian categories normal, so that every monomorphism is a kernel, but we also know *which* morphism the monomorphism is a kernel of, to wit, its cokernel. In symbols:

*m* = ker (coker *m*) (for monomorphisms in an abelian category)

## Relationship to algebraic kernels {#Relationship_to_algebraic_kernels}

[Universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200) defines a [notion of kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_%28universal_algebra%29) for homomorphisms between two [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364) of the same kind. This concept of kernel measures how far the given homomorphism is from being [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective). There is some overlap between this algebraic notion and the categorical notion of kernel since both generalize the situation of groups and modules mentioned above. In general, however, the universal-algebraic notion of kernel is more like the category-theoretic concept of [kernel pair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_pair). In particular, kernel pairs can be used to interpret kernels in monoid theory or ring theory in category-theoretic terms.
