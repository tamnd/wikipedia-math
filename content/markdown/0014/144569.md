# Nilpotent group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nilpotent group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), a **nilpotent group** *G* is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) that has an [upper central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_central_series) that terminates with *G*. Equivalently, it has a [central series](wikipedia://en/15202655) of finite length or its [lower central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_central_series) terminates with {1}.

:::

Intuitively, a nilpotent group is a group that is "almost [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974)". This idea is motivated by the fact that nilpotent groups are [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012), and for [finite](wikipedia://en/323707) nilpotent groups, two elements having [relatively prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_prime) [orders](wikipedia://en/493527) must [commute](wikipedia://en/294390). It is also true that finite nilpotent groups are [supersolvable](wikipedia://en/4966866). The concept is credited to work in the 1930s by Russian mathematician [Sergei Chernikov](wikipedia://en/51015113).[^o2]

Nilpotent groups arise in [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316), as well as in the classification of groups. They also appear prominently in the classification of [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945).

Analogous terms are used for [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944) (using the [Lie bracket](wikipedia://en/10282799)) including [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/3358867), lower central series, and upper central series.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The definition uses the idea of a [central series](wikipedia://en/15202655) for a group. The following are equivalent definitions for a nilpotent group $G$:$G$ has a [central series](wikipedia://en/15202655) of finite length. That is, a series of [normal subgroups](wikipedia://en/21918) $\{1\} = G_0 \triangleleft G_1 \triangleleft \dots \triangleleft G_n = G$ where $G_{i+1}/G_i \leq Z(G/G_i)$, or equivalently $[G,G_{i+1}] \leq G_i$.; $G$ has a [lower central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_central_series) terminating in the [trivial](wikipedia://en/193059) [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) after finitely many steps. That is, a series of normal subgroups $G = G_0 \triangleright G_1 \triangleright \dots \triangleright G_n = \{1\}$ where $G_{i+1} = [G_i, G]$.; $G$ has an [upper central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_central_series) terminating in the whole group after finitely many steps. That is, a series of normal subgroups $\{1\} = Z_0 \triangleleft Z_1 \triangleleft \dots \triangleleft Z_n = G$ where $Z_1 = Z(G)$ and $Z_{i+1}$ is the subgroup such that $Z_{i+1}/Z_i = Z(G/Z_i)$.

For a nilpotent group, the smallest $n$ such that $G$ has a central series of length $n$ is called the **nilpotency class** of $G$; and $G$ is said to be **nilpotent of class $n$**. (By definition, the length is $n$ if there are $n + 1$ different subgroups in the series, including the trivial subgroup and the whole group.)

Equivalently, the nilpotency class of $G$ equals the length of the lower central series or upper central series. If a group has nilpotency class at most $n$, then it is sometimes called a **nil-$n$ group**.

It follows immediately from any of the above forms of the definition of nilpotency, that the trivial group is the unique group of nilpotency class $0$, and groups of nilpotency class $1$ are exactly the non-trivial abelian groups.[^o8][^o5]

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: HeisenbergCayleyGraph.png.* A portion of the [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of the discrete [Heisenberg group](wikipedia://en/448518), a well-known nilpotent group.

- As noted above, every abelian group is nilpotent.[^o8][^o9]
- For a small non-abelian example, consider the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130) *Q*<sub>8</sub>, which is a smallest non-abelian *p*-group. It has [center](wikipedia://en/7125) {1, −1} of [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) 2, and its upper central series is {1}, {1, −1}, *Q*<sub>8</sub>; so it is nilpotent of class 2.
- The [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887) of two nilpotent groups is nilpotent.[^o10]
- All finite [*p*-groups](wikipedia://en/24664) are in fact nilpotent ([proof](wikipedia://en/24664#Non-trivial_center)). For *n* > 1, the maximal nilpotency class of a group of order *p*<sup>*n*</sup> is *n* - 1 (for example, a group of order *p*<sup>*2*</sup> is abelian). The 2-groups of maximal class are the generalised [quaternion groups](wikipedia://en/153130), the [dihedral groups](wikipedia://en/100267), and the [semidihedral groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semidihedral_group).
- Furthermore, every finite nilpotent group is the direct product of *p*-groups.[^o10]
- The multiplicative group of upper [unitriangular](wikipedia://en/374222#Unitriangular_matrix) *n* × *n* matrices over any field *F* is a [nilpotent group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unipotent_algebraic_group) of nilpotency class *n* − 1. In particular, taking *n* = 3 yields the [Heisenberg group](wikipedia://en/448518) *H*, an example of a non-abelian[^o11] infinite nilpotent group.[^o12] It has nilpotency class 2 with central series 1, *Z*(*H*), *H*.
- The multiplicative group of [invertible upper triangular](wikipedia://en/588356) *n* × *n* matrices over a field *F* is not in general nilpotent, but is [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012).
- Any nonabelian group *G* such that *G*/*Z*(*G*) is abelian has nilpotency class 2, with central series {1}, *Z*(*G*), *G*.

The [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) *k* for which any group of order *k* is nilpotent have been characterized .

:::

## Explanation of term {#Explanation_of_term}

Nilpotent groups are called so because the "adjoint action" of any element is [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235), meaning that for a nilpotent group $G$ of nilpotence degree $n$ and an element $g$, the function $\operatorname{ad}_g \colon G \to G$ defined by $\operatorname{ad}_g(x) := [g,x]$ (where $[g,x]=g^{-1} x^{-1} g x$ is the [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193) of $g$ and $x$) is nilpotent in the sense that the $n$th iteration of the function is trivial: $\left(\operatorname{ad}_g\right)^n(x)=e$ for all $x$ in $G$.

This is not a defining characteristic of nilpotent groups: groups for which $\operatorname{ad}_g$ is nilpotent of degree $n$ (in the sense above) are called $n$-[Engel groups](wikipedia://en/17228297),[^o14] and need not be nilpotent in general. They are proven to be nilpotent if they have finite [order](wikipedia://en/493527), and are [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) to be nilpotent as long as they are [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/963042).

An abelian group is precisely one for which the adjoint action is not just nilpotent but trivial (a 1-Engel group).

## Properties {#Properties}

Since each successive [factor group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_group) $Z_{i+1}/Z_{i}$ in the [upper central series](wikipedia://en/15202655) is abelian, and the series is finite, every nilpotent group is a [solvable group](wikipedia://en/105012) with a relatively simple structure.

Every subgroup of a nilpotent group of class $n$ is nilpotent of class at most $n$;[^o17] in addition, if $f$ is a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) of a nilpotent group of class $n$, then the image of $f$ is nilpotent[^o17] of class at most $n$.

::: {#o18 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

The following statements are equivalent for finite groups,[^o19] revealing some useful properties of nilpotency:$G$ is a nilpotent group.; If $H$ is a proper subgroup of $G$, then $H$ is a proper [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of $N_{G}(H)$ (the [normalizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalizer) of $H$ in $G$). This is called the **normalizer property** and can be phrased simply as "normalizers grow".; Every [Sylow subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylow_subgroup) of $G$ is normal.; $G$ is the [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of its Sylow subgroups.; If $d$ divides the [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) of $G$, then $G$ has a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of order $d$.

:::

::: {#o20 .proof cue=math-proof}

(a)→(b): By induction on $|G|$. If $G$ is abelian, then for any $H$, $N_{G}(H) = G$. If not, if $Z(G)$ is not contained in $H$, then $h_{Z}H_{Z}^{-1}h^{-1} = h'\boldsymbol{H'}h^{-1} = H$, so $H\cdot Z(G)$ normalizers $H$. If $Z(G)$ is contained in $H$, then $H/Z(G)$ is contained in $G/Z(G)$. Note, $G/Z(G)$ is a nilpotent group. Thus, there exists a subgroup of $G/Z(G)$ which normalizes $H/Z(G)$ and $H/Z(G)$ is a proper subgroup of it. Therefore, pullback this subgroup to the subgroup in $G$ and it normalizes $H$. (This proof is the same argument as for $p$-groups – the only fact we needed was if $G$ is nilpotent then so is $G/Z(G)$ – so the details are omitted.)

(b)→(c): Let $p_{1},p_{2},...,p_{s}$ be the distinct primes dividing its order and let $P_{i}$ in $\operatorname{Syl}_{p_{i}}(G)$, $1 \le i \le s$. Let $P = P_{i}$ for some $i$ and let $N = N_{G}(P)$. Since $P$ is a normal Sylow subgroup of $N$, $P$ is [characteristic](wikipedia://en/7198) in $N$. Since $P \operatorname{char} N$ and $N$ is a normal subgroup of $N_{G}(N)$, we get that $P$ is a normal subgroup of $N_{G}(N)$. This means $N_{G}(N)$ is a subgroup of $N$ and hence $N_{G}(N) = N$. By (b) we must therefore have $N = G$, which gives (c).

(c)→(d): Let $p_{1},p_{2},...,p_{s}$ be the distinct primes dividing its order and let $P_{i}$ in $\operatorname{Syl}_{p_{i}}(G)$, $1 \le i \le s$. For any $t$, $1 \le t \le s$ we show inductively that $P_{1}P_{2}\cdot\cdot\cdot P_{t}$ is isomorphic to $P_{1}\times P_{2}\times\cdot\cdot\cdot\times P_{t}$. Note first that each $P_{i}$ is normal in $G$ so $P_{1}P_{2}\cdot\cdot\cdot P_{t}$ is a subgroup of $G$. Let $H$ be the product $P_{1}P_{2}\cdot\cdot\cdot P_{t-1}$ and let $K = P_{t}$, so by induction $H$ is isomorphic to $P_{1}\times P_{2}\times\cdot\cdot\cdot\times P_{t-1}$. In particular, $|H| = |P_{1}|\cdot|P_{2}|\cdot\cdot\cdot\cdot\cdot|P_{t-1}|$. Since $|K| = |P_{t}|$, the orders of $H$ and $K$ are relatively prime. Lagrange's Theorem implies the intersection of $H$ and $K$ is equal to 1. By definition, $P_{1}P_{2}\cdot\cdot\cdot P_{t} = HK$, hence $HK$ is isomorphic to $H\times K$ which is equal to $P_{1}\times P_{2}\times\cdot\cdot\cdot\times P_{t}$. This completes the induction. Now take $t = s$ to obtain (d).

(d)→(e): Note that a [p-group](wikipedia://en/24664) of order $p^{k}$ has a normal subgroup of order $p^{m}$ for all $1\le m\le k$. Since $G$ is a direct product of its Sylow subgroups, and normality is preserved upon direct product of groups, $G$ has a normal subgroup of order $d$ for every divisor $d$ of $|G|$.

(e)→(a): For any prime $p$ dividing $|G|$, the [Sylow $p$-subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylow_group) is normal. Thus we can apply (d) (since we already proved (c)→(d)). Because every finite p-group is nilpotent, inffer G (a product of p-groups) is nilpotent.

:::

Statement (d) can be extended to infinite groups: if $G$ is a nilpotent group, then every Sylow subgroup $G_{p}$ of $G$ is normal, and the direct product of these Sylow subgroups is the subgroup of all elements of finite order in $G$ (see [torsion subgroup](wikipedia://en/144052)).

Many properties of nilpotent groups are shared by [hypercentral groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercentral_group).

:::
