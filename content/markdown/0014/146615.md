# Japanese numerals

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Japanese numerals"}

The **Japanese numerals** are [numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numeral_%28linguistics%29) that are used in [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_language). In writing, they are the same as the [Chinese numerals](wikipedia://en/5781), and large numbers follow the Chinese style of grouping by 10,000. Two pronunciations are used: the [Sino-Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On'yomi) (ja) readings of the [Chinese characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_characters) and the Japanese ja (native words, ja readings).

:::

## Numbering in Japanese {#Numbering_in_Japanese}

There are two ways of writing the numbers in Japanese: in [Arabic numerals](wikipedia://en/1786) (1, 2, 3) or in [Chinese numerals](wikipedia://en/5781) (一, 二, 三). The Arabic numerals are more often used in [horizontal writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_and_vertical_writing_in_East_Asian_scripts), and the Chinese numerals are more common in [vertical writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_and_vertical_writing_in_East_Asian_scripts).

Most numbers have [two readings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanji#Readings), one derived from Chinese used for [cardinal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_numeral) ([ja reading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On'yomi)) and a native Japanese reading ([ja reading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kun'yomi)) used somewhat less formally for numbers up to 10. In some cases (listed below) the Japanese reading is generally preferred for all uses. Archaic readings are marked with †.

\* The special reading is also found. It may be optionally used when reading individual digits of a number one after another, instead of as a full number. A popular example is the [famous 109 store](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/109_%28department_store%29) in [Shibuya, Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibuya) which is read as ja (Kanji: 一〇九). (It can also be read as 'ten-nine'—pronounced ja—which is a pun on the name of the Tokyo department store which owns the building.) This usage of ja for numerical 0 is similar to reading numeral 0 in English as *oh*. However, as a number, it is only written as 0 or . Additionally, two and five are pronounced with a long vowel in phone numbers (i.e. にい ja and ごお ja). Numbers after ja are rarely used. They are presented here nonetheless.

As noted above, ja (4) and ja (7) are preferred to ja and ja. It is purported that this is because ja is also the reading of the word , which makes it an unlucky reading (see [tetraphobia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetraphobia)); while ja may sound too similar to ja (1), ja or ja (8). However, in quite a number of established words and phrases, ja and ja are preferred; additionally, when counting (as in "ja"), ja and ja may be preferred.

The number 9 is also considered unlucky; when pronounced ja, it is a homophone for . The number 13 is sometimes considered unlucky, though this is a carryover from [Western tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triskaidekaphobia). In contrast, 7 and sometimes 8 are considered lucky in Japanese.[^o40]

In modern Japanese, cardinal numbers except 4 and 7 are generally given the ja readings. Alternate readings are used in month names, day-of-month names, and fixed phrases; for instance, April, July, and September are called ja (4th month), ja (7th month), and ja (9th month) respectively (for further detail see [Japanese counter word#Exceptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_counter_word#Exceptions)). The ja readings are also used when shouting out headcounts (e.g. ja).

Larger numbers are made by combining these elements:

- Tens from 20 to 90 are "(digit)ja" as in to .
- Hundreds from 200 to 900 are "(digit)ja".
- Thousands from 2000 to 9000 are "(digit)ja".

Starting at a myriad, numbers begin with if no digit would otherwise precede. That is, 100 is just , and 1000 is just , but 10000 is , not just \*ja. (This differs from Chinese, where numbers begin with 一 if no digit would otherwise precede starting at 100.) And, if directly precedes the name of powers of [myriad](wikipedia://en/20088), is normally attached before , which yields . That is, 10,000,000 (parsed as 1000,0000) is normally read as . But if does *not* directly precede the name of powers of myriad, attaching is optional. That is, 15,000,000 (1500,0000) is read as or , just as 1500 is read as or .

There are some phonetic modifications to larger numbers involving [voicing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consonant_voicing_and_devoicing) or [gemination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemination) of certain consonants, as typically occurs in Japanese (i.e. ja): e.g. ja "six" and ja "hundred" yield ja "six hundred".

### Strings of digits and decimal numbers {#Strings_of_digits_and_decimal_numbers}

The above digits from 1 to 9 are used primarily in isolation. Strings of digits make use of slightly different readings, with lengthened vowels, for 2 (*nī*) and 5 (*gō*), and less frequently, 4 (*shī*) and 9 (*kū*). This is because when digits are enumerated, if possible, they must be so in groups of two, each of which must consist of exactly four [morae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_%28Japanese_prosody%29), which necessitates the lengthening of normally unimoraic digits such as *ni*, *go*, *shi* and *ku*.[^o44][^o45] For example, such string as **5**4**2**62 is pronounced ***gō****\-yon **nī**-roku ni*, with the first two groups being quadrimoraic. The [accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_pitch_accent) is placed on the penultimate mora of each group if possible, hence .[^o46]

This rule also applies to the last digit of the integer part and all the digits of the decimal part of a decimal number.[^o44][^o46] For example, such decimal number as 25**2**.**25**5 is pronounced *nihyaku gojū **nī**-ten **nī-gō** go* , with *ten* meaning "point".

### Other types of numerals {#Other_types_of_numerals}

For [ordinal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinal_numeral), see [Japanese counter word#Ordinal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_counter_word#Ordinal_numbers).

[Distributive numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributive_numeral) are formed regularly from a cardinal number, a counter word, and the suffix , as in one person at a time, one person each.

### Decimal fractions {#Decimal_fractions}

Japanese has two systems of numerals for decimal fractions. They are no longer in general use, but are still used in some instances such as batting and fielding averages of baseball players, winning percentages for sports teams, and in some idiomatic phrases such as , and when representing a rate or discount. The ja fractions are also used when talking about fevers—for example for 9 and two parts—referring to the temperature 39.2°C.

One system is as follows:

| Character | ja | ja | ja | ja | ja | ja | ja | ja | ja | ja |
| Reading | ja | ja | ja | ja | ja | ja | ja | ja | ja | ja |

This is the system used with the traditional [Japanese units of measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_units_of_measurement). Several of the names are used "as is" to represent a fraction of a [ja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cun_%28unit%29).

The other system of representing these decimal fractions of rate or discount uses a system "shifted down" with a ja becoming a "one hundredth" and so on, and the unit for "tenth" becoming ja:

::: {#o52 .table cue=table}

| Rank | 10${}^{-1}$ | 10${}^{-2}$ | 10${}^{-3}$ | 10${}^{-4}$ | 10${}^{-5}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Reading | ja | ja | ja | ja | ja |

This is often used with prices. For example:

- : 15% discount
- : batting average .389

With the exception of ja, these are rarely seen in modern usage. Decimal fractions are typically written with either kanji numerals (vertically) or Arabic numerals (horizontally), preceded by a decimal point, and are read as successive digits, as in Western convention. Note that, in written form, they can be combined with either the traditional system of expressing numerals (42.195 kilometers: 四十二・一九五 キロメートル), in which powers of ten are written, or with the place value system, which uses zero (50.04 percent: 五〇・〇四 パーセント.) In both cases, however, the reading follows the traditional system (ja for 42.195 kilometers; ja for 50.04 percent.)

## Formal numbers {#Formal_numbers}

*Image: Shoe Boxes (743286602).jpg.* ja

As with Chinese numerals, there exists in Japanese a separate set of kanji for numerals called used in legal and financial documents to prevent unscrupulous individuals from adding a stroke or two, turning a one into a two or a three. The formal numbers are identical to the [Chinese formal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_financial_numerals) except for minor stroke variations. Today, the numbers for one, two, three, and ten are written only in their formal form in legal documents (the numbers 4 to 9 as well as 100, 1000 and 10000 are written identically to the common ones, cf. table below).[^o55][^o56][^o57][^o58] These numbers' common forms can be changed to a higher value by adding strokes (1 and 2 were explained above, while 3 can be changed to 5, and 10 to 1000). In some cases, the digit 1 is explicitly written like 壱百壱拾 for 110, as opposed to 百十 in common writing.

## Hand counting {#Hand_counting}

Japanese uses separate systems for counting for oneself and for displaying numbers to others, which both proceed up to ten. For counting, one begins with the palm open, then counts up to five by curling up (folding down) the fingers, starting from the thumb – thus one has just the thumb down (and others extended), while four has only the little finger extended, and five has a fist. One then counts up to ten by proceeding in the reverse order, extending the fingers, starting at the little finger – thus six is the same as four, seven the same as three, and so forth, with ten ending with the palm open. While this introduces ambiguity, it is not used to present to others, so this is generally not a problem. When displaying for others, one starts with the hand closed, and extends fingers, starting with the index, going to the little finger, then ending with the thumb, as in the United States. For numbers above five, one uses an open hand (indicating five) and places the appropriate number of fingers from the other hand against the palm (palms facing each other) – so six has the index finger against the palm, and so forth.[^o63] To display ten, one presents both hands open and palm outwards.

## Digits in written words {#Digits_in_written_words}

Since the adoption of [Arabic numerals](wikipedia://en/1786), it has become more and more common for numbers to be written using them. Counters and ordinal numbers are typically written in Arabic numbers, such as three people, July, "seventh-month", age 20, etc., although 三人, 七月 and 二十歳 are also acceptable to write (albeit less common). However, numbers that are part of lexemes are typically written in kanji. For example, the term 'vegetable stand / grocer' translates into "800 store" and uses the Old Japanese pronunciation for 800, ojp. The notorious Japanese organized crime syndicate, the [yakuza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakuza), can be written 八九三 (or 893), a hand in ja that is worth 0 points, indicating that yakuza are "worthless persons" or "gambling persons".[^o65]
