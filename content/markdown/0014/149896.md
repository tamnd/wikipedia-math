# Emmy Noether

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Emmy Noether"}

**Amalie Emmy Noether** (23 March 1882 – 14 April 1935) was a German mathematician who made many important contributions to [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) and [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416). Noether was described by [Pavel Alexandrov](wikipedia://en/576245), [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736), [Jean Dieudonné](wikipedia://en/1070084), [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544), and [Norbert Wiener](wikipedia://en/63185) as the most important [woman in the history of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_women_in_mathematics).[^o3][^n1][^n2] As one of the leading mathematicians of her time, she developed theories of [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), [fields](wikipedia://en/10603), and [algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebras). She also proved [Noether's first](wikipedia://en/150159) and [second theorems](wikipedia://en/18021657), which play a fundamental role in mathematical physics,[^o6] by explaining the connection between [symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_%28physics%29) and [conservation laws](wikipedia://en/6956).[^o7]

:::

Noether was born to a [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish) family in the [Franconian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franconia) town of [Erlangen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erlangen); her father was the mathematician [Max Noether](wikipedia://en/2482337). She originally planned to teach French and English after passing the required examinations, but instead studied mathematics at the [University of Erlangen–Nuremberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Erlangen–Nuremberg), where her father lectured. After completing her doctorate in 1907 under the supervision of [Paul Gordan](wikipedia://en/1059850), she worked at the Mathematical Institute of Erlangen without pay for seven years.[^n3] At the time, women were largely excluded from academic positions. In 1915, [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) and [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) invited her to join the mathematics department at the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen), a world-renowned center of mathematical research. The philosophical faculty objected, and she spent four years lecturing under Hilbert's name. Her [habilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation) was approved in 1919, allowing her to obtain the rank of *[Privatdozent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privatdozent)*.[^n3]

Noether remained a leading member of the Göttingen mathematics department until 1933; her students were sometimes called the "Noether Boys". In 1924, Dutch mathematician [B. L. van der Waerden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B._L._van_der_Waerden) joined her circle and soon became the leading expositor of Noether's ideas; her work was the foundation for the second volume of his influential 1931 textbook *[Moderne Algebra](wikipedia://en/37252648)*. By the time of her plenary address at the 1932 [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) in [Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zürich), her algebraic acumen was recognized worldwide. In 1933, Germany's Nazi government [dismissed Jews from university positions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Jewish_legislation_in_pre-war_Nazi_Germany), and Noether moved to the United States to take a position at [Bryn Mawr College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryn_Mawr_College) in [Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania). There, she taught graduate and post-doctoral women including [Marie Johanna Weiss](wikipedia://en/68210910) and [Olga Taussky-Todd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olga_Taussky-Todd). At the same time, she lectured and conducted research at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey).[^n3]

Noether's mathematical work has been divided into three "[epochs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoch)".[^o11] In the first (1908–1919), she made contributions to the theories of [algebraic invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_invariant) and [number fields](wikipedia://en/10603). Her work on differential invariants in the [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882), [Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159), has been called "one of the most important mathematical theorems ever proved in guiding the development of modern physics".[^n4] In the second epoch (1920–1926), she began work that "changed the face of \[abstract\] algebra".[^o334] In her classic 1921 paper *Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen* (*Theory of Ideals in Ring Domains*), Noether developed the theory of [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) in [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346) into a tool with wide-ranging applications. She made elegant use of the [ascending chain condition](wikipedia://en/3189), and objects satisfying it are named *[Noetherian](wikipedia://en/150169)* in her honor. In the third epoch (1927–1935), she published works on [noncommutative algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noncommutative_algebra) and [hypercomplex numbers](wikipedia://en/51438) and united the [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200) of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) with the theory of [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) and ideals. In addition to her own publications, Noether was generous with her ideas and is credited with several lines of research published by other mathematicians, even in fields far removed from her main work, such as [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801).

## Biography {#Biography}

### Early life {#Early_life}

*Image: Erlangen 1916.jpg.* Noether grew up in the Bavarian city of [Erlangen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erlangen), depicted here in a 1916 postcard.

Amalie Emmy Noether was born on 23 March 1882 in [Erlangen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erlangen), Bavaria.[^n5] She was the first of four children of mathematician [Max Noether](wikipedia://en/2482337) and Ida Amalia Kaufmann, both from wealthy Jewish merchant families.[^n6] Her first name was "Amalie", but she began using her middle name at a young age and continued to do so in her adult life and her publications.

In her youth, Noether did not stand out academically, but she was known for being clever and friendly. She was nearsighted and had a minor [lisp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisp) during her childhood. A family friend recounted a story years later about young Noether quickly solving a brain teaser at a children's party, showing logical acumen at an early age.[^n7] She was taught to cook and clean, as were most girls of the time, and took piano lessons. She pursued none of these activities with passion, but loved to dance.[^n8]

*Image: NoetherFamily MFO3120.jpg.* Emmy Noether with her brothers Alfred, [Fritz](wikipedia://en/7522370), and Robert, before 1918

Noether had three younger brothers. The eldest, Alfred Noether, was born in 1883 and was awarded a doctorate in [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry) from Erlangen in 1909, but died nine years later.[^n9] [Fritz Noether](wikipedia://en/7522370) was born in 1884, studied at the [Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität_München), and made contributions to [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969).[^n10] He was likely executed in the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) in 1941 during the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War).[^o21] The youngest, Gustav Robert Noether, was born in 1889. Very little is known about his life; he suffered from chronic illness and died in 1928.[^n11][^n12]

### Education {#Education}

Noether showed early proficiency in French and English. In 1900, she took the examination for teachers of these languages and received an overall score of *sehr gut* (very good). Her performance qualified her to teach languages at schools reserved for girls, but she chose instead to continue her studies at the [University of Erlangen–Nuremberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Erlangen–Nuremberg),[^n13] where her father was a professor.[^n14]

This was an unconventional decision; two years earlier, the Academic Senate of the university had declared that allowing [mixed-sex education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed-sex_education) would "overthrow all academic order".[^n15] One of just two women in a university of 986 students, Noether was allowed only to [audit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_audit) classes rather than participate fully, and she required the permission of individual professors whose lectures she wished to attend. Despite these obstacles, on 14 July 1903 she passed the graduation exam at a *[Realgymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realgymnasium)* in [Nuremberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg).[^n13][^n16][^n17]

During the 1903–04 winter semester, Noether studied at the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen), attending lectures given by astronomer [Karl Schwarzschild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Schwarzschild) and mathematicians [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537), [Otto Blumenthal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Blumenthal), [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887), and [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302).[^n18]

*Image: Paul Albert Gordan.jpg.* [Paul Gordan](wikipedia://en/1059850) supervised Noether's doctoral dissertation on [invariants](wikipedia://en/1126638) of biquadratic forms.

In 1903, restrictions on women's full enrollment in Bavarian universities were rescinded.[^n19] Noether returned to Erlangen and officially reentered the university in October 1904, declaring her intention to focus solely on mathematics. She was one of six women in her year (two auditors) and the only woman in her chosen school.[^n20] Under the supervision of [Paul Gordan](wikipedia://en/1059850), she wrote her dissertation, *Über die Bildung des Formensystems der ternären biquadratischen Form* (*On Complete Systems of Invariants for Ternary Biquadratic Forms*),[^n21] in 1907, graduating *summa cum laude* that year.[^n22] Gordan was a member of the "computational" school of invariant researchers, and Noether's thesis ended with a list of over 300 explicitly worked-out invariants. This approach to invariants was later superseded by the more abstract and general approach pioneered by Hilbert.[^n23][^n24] It was well received, but Noether later called her thesis and some subsequent similar papers of hers "crap". All her later work was in a completely different field.[^n24][^n25]

### University of Erlangen–Nuremberg {#University_of_Erlangen–Nuremberg}

From 1908 to 1915, Noether taught at Erlangen's Mathematical Institute without pay, occasionally substituting for her father, [Max Noether](wikipedia://en/2482337), when he was too ill to lecture.[^n26] She joined the [Circolo Matematico di Palermo](wikipedia://en/32146789) in 1908 and the [Deutsche Mathematiker-Vereinigung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Mathematiker-Vereinigung) in 1909.[^n27] In 1910 and 1911, she published an extension of her thesis work from three variables to *n* variables.[^n28]

*Image: Emmy noether postcard 1915.jpg.* Noether sometimes used postcards to discuss abstract algebra with her colleague, [Ernst Fischer](wikipedia://en/5721087). This card is postmarked 10 April 1915.

Gordan retired in 1910,[^n29] and Noether taught under his successors, [Erhard Schmidt](wikipedia://en/672191) and [Ernst Fischer](wikipedia://en/5721087), who took over from Schmidt in 1911.[^n30] According to her colleague [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) and her biographer [Auguste Dick](wikipedia://en/57454541), Fischer was an important influence on Noether, in particular by introducing her to David Hilbert's work.[^n31][^n32] Noether and Fischer shared lively enjoyment of mathematics and often discussed lectures long after they were over; Noether is known to have sent Fischer postcards continuing her train of mathematical thoughts.[^n33][^n34]

From 1913 to 1916, Noether published several papers extending and applying Hilbert's methods to mathematical objects such as [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) of [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) and the [invariants](wikipedia://en/363762) of [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707).[^n35] This phase marked Noether's first exposure to [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a field to which she made groundbreaking contributions.[^n36]

In Erlangen, Noether advised two doctoral students: Hans Falckenberg and Fritz Seidelmann, who defended their theses in 1911 and 1916.[^n37][^n38] Despite Noether's significant role, they were both officially under her father's supervision. After completing his doctorate, Falckenberg spent time in [Braunschweig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braunschweig) and [Königsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Königsberg) before becoming a professor at the [University of Giessen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Giessen).[^n39] Seidelmann became a professor at the [Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität_München).[^o52]

### University of Göttingen {#University_of_Göttingen}

::: {#o58 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Habilitation and Noether's theorem"}

#### Habilitation and Noether's theorem {#Habilitation_and_Noether's_theorem}

In early 1915, Noether was invited to return to the University of Göttingen by Hilbert and [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887). Their effort to recruit her was initially blocked by the [philologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philologists) and [historians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historian) among the philosophical faculty, who insisted that women should not become *[privatdozenten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privatdozent)*. In a joint department meeting on the matter, one faculty member protested: "What will our soldiers think when they return to the university and find that they are required to learn at the feet of a woman?"[^n40][^n41] Hilbert, who believed Noether's qualifications were the only important issue and that gender was irrelevant, objected with indignation and scolded those protesting her habilitation. His exact words have not been preserved, but his objection is often said to have included the remark that the university was "not a bathhouse".[^n31][^n40][^n42][^n43] [Pavel Alexandrov](wikipedia://en/576245) recalled that faculty members' opposition to Noether was based not just in sexism, but also in their objections to her [social-democratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_democracy) political beliefs and Jewish ancestry.[^n43]

*Image: Hilbert.jpg.* [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) invited Noether to join Göttingen mathematics department in 1915, challenging the views of some of his colleagues that a woman should not teach at a university.

Noether left for Göttingen in late April; two weeks later her mother died suddenly in Erlangen. She had previously received medical care for an eye condition, but its nature and relation to her death is unknown. Around the same time, Noether's father retired and her brother joined the [German Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Army_%28German_Empire%29) to serve in [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I). She returned to Erlangen for several weeks, mostly to care for her aging father.[^n44]

During her first years teaching at Göttingen, she had no official position and was not paid. Her lectures often were advertised under Hilbert's name, with Noether providing "assistance".[^n45]

Soon after arriving at Göttingen, she demonstrated her capabilities by proving the [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) now known as [Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159), which shows that a [conservation law](wikipedia://en/6956) is associated with any differentiable [symmetry of a physical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_in_physics).[^n41][^n46] The paper, *Invariante Variationsprobleme*, was presented by Felix Klein on 26 July 1918 at a meeting of the Royal Society of Sciences at Göttingen.[^n47][^n48] Noether presumably did not present it herself because she was not a member of the society.[^n49] In their book *Symmetry and the Beautiful Universe*, physicists [Leon M. Lederman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_M._Lederman) and [Christopher T. Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_T._Hill) write that Noether's theorem is "certainly one of the most important mathematical theorems ever proved in guiding the development of modern physics, possibly on a par with the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034)".[^n4]

*Image: Mathematik Göttingen.jpg.* The University of Göttingen allowed Noether's *[habilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation)* in 1919, four years after she had begun lecturing at the school.

When World War I ended, the [German Revolution of 1918–19](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Revolution_of_1918–19) brought a significant change in social attitudes, including more rights for women. In 1919, the University of Göttingen allowed Noether to proceed with her *[habilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation)* (eligibility for tenure). Her oral examination was in May, and she successfully delivered her *habilitation* lecture in June.[^n50] Noether became a *privatdozent*,[^n51] and that fall delivered the first lectures listed under her name.[^n52] She was still not paid for her work.[^n45]

Three years later, Noether received a letter from Otto Boelitz, the [Prussian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussia) Minister for Science, Art, and Public Education, in which he conferred on her the title of *nicht beamteter ausserordentlicher Professor* (an untenured professor with limited internal administrative rights and functions).[^n53] This was an unpaid "extraordinary" professorship, not the higher "ordinary" professorship, which was a civil-service position. It recognized the importance of her work but still provided no salary. Noether was not paid for her lectures until she was appointed to the special position of *Lehrbeauftragte für Algebra* (*Lecturer for Algebra*) a year later.[^n54][^n55]

:::

#### Work in abstract algebra {#Work_in_abstract_algebra}

Noether's theorem had a significant effect upon classical and quantum mechanics, but among mathematicians she is best remembered for her contributions to [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384). In his introduction to Noether's *Collected Papers*, [Nathan Jacobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Jacobson) wrote:

> The development of abstract algebra, which is one of the most distinctive innovations of twentieth century mathematics, is largely due to her—in published papers, in lectures, and in personal influence on her contemporaries.[^n56]

Noether's work in algebra began in 1920 when, in collaboration with her protégé Werner Schmeidler, she published a paper about the [theory of ideals](wikipedia://en/1248704) in which they defined [left and right ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) in a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404).[^n36]

In 1921, she published the paper *Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen*,[^n57] analyzing [ascending chain conditions](wikipedia://en/3189) with regard to (mathematical) [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977), in which she proved the [Lasker–Noether theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasker–Noether_theorem) in its full generality. Noted algebraist [Irving Kaplansky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irving_Kaplansky) called this work "revolutionary".[^n58] The publication gave rise to the term *[Noetherian](wikipedia://en/150169)* for objects that satisfy the ascending chain condition.[^n58][^n59]

*Image: ETH-BIB-Waerden, Bartel Leendert van der (1903-1996)-Portr 12109.tif.* [B. L. van der Waerden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B._L._van_der_Waerden) (pictured in 1980) was heavily influenced by Noether at Göttingen.

In 1924, the young Dutch mathematician [Bartel Leendert van der Waerden](wikipedia://en/91390) arrived at the University of Göttingen. He immediately began working with Noether, who taught him methods of abstract conceptualization. Van der Waerden later said her originality was "absolute beyond comparison".[^n60] After returning to Amsterdam, he wrote *[Moderne Algebra](wikipedia://en/37252648)*, a central two-volume text in the field; its second volume, published in 1931, borrowed heavily from Noether's work.[^o154] Noether did not seek recognition, but he included as a note in the seventh edition "based in part on lectures by E. Artin and E. Noether".[^n61][^n62][^n63] Beginning in 1927, Noether worked closely with [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351), [Richard Brauer](wikipedia://en/605134), and [Helmut Hasse](wikipedia://en/727118) on [noncommutative algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noncommutative_algebra).[^n31][^o154]

Van der Waerden's visit was part of a convergence of mathematicians from all over the world to Göttingen, which had become a major hub of mathematical and physical research. Russian mathematicians [Pavel Alexandrov](wikipedia://en/576245) and [Pavel Urysohn](wikipedia://en/23770) were the first of several in 1923.[^n64] Between 1926 and 1930, Alexandrov regularly lectured at the university, and he and Noether became good friends.[^n65] He dubbed her *der Noether*, using *der* as an epithet rather than a masculine German article.[^n65] She tried to arrange a position for him as a regular professor at Göttingen, but could only help him secure a scholarship from the [Rockefeller Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_Foundation) to [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) for the 1927–28 academic year.[^n65][^n66]

#### Graduate students {#Graduate_students}

*Image: EmmyNoether MFO3096.jpg.* Noether c. 1930

In Göttingen, Noether supervised more than a dozen doctoral students;[^o52] most were with [Edmund Landau](wikipedia://en/222230) and others as she was not allowed to supervise dissertations on her own.[^n67][^n68] Her first was [Grete Hermann](wikipedia://en/1208377), who defended her dissertation in February 1925.[^n69] She is best remembered for her work on the foundations of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), but her dissertation was considered an important contribution to [ideal theory](wikipedia://en/1248704).[^n70][^n71] Hermann later spoke reverently of her "dissertation-mother".[^n69]

Around the same time, Heinrich Grell and Rudolf Hölzer wrote their dissertations under Noether. Hölzer died of [tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis) shortly before his defense.[^n69][^n72][^n73] Grell defended his thesis in 1926 and went on to work at the [University of Jena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Jena) and the [University of Halle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Halle) before losing his teaching license in 1935 due to accusations of homosexual acts.[^o52] He was later reinstated and became a professor at [Humboldt University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humboldt_University) in 1948.[^o52][^n69]

Noether then supervised [Werner Weber](wikipedia://en/53613774)[^n74] and [Jakob Levitzki](wikipedia://en/23686739),[^n75] who both defended their theses in 1929.[^n76][^n77] Weber, who was considered only a modest mathematician,[^n67] later took part in driving Jewish mathematicians out of Göttingen.[^n78] Levitzki worked first at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University) and then at the [Hebrew University of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_University_of_Jerusalem) in then British-ruled [Mandatory Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandatory_Palestine), making significant contributions (in particular [Levitzky's theorem](wikipedia://en/24015132) and the [Hopkins–Levitzki theorem](wikipedia://en/29435296)) to [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424).[^n77]

Other "Noether Boys" included [Max Deuring](wikipedia://en/5638258), [Hans Fitting](wikipedia://en/6069614), [Ernst Witt](wikipedia://en/2619911), [Chiungtze C. Tsen](wikipedia://en/10143482), and [Otto Schilling](wikipedia://en/31260972). Deuring, who had been considered the most promising of Noether's students, received his doctorate in 1930.[^n79][^n80] He worked in Hamburg, Marden, and Göttingen and is known for his contributions to [arithmetic geometry](wikipedia://en/1973177).[^n81] Fitting graduated in 1931 with a thesis on abelian groups[^n82] and is remembered for his work in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), particularly [Fitting's theorem](wikipedia://en/4081611) and the [Fitting lemma](wikipedia://en/794492).[^n83] He died at the age of 31 of a bone disease.[^n84]

Witt was initially supervised by Noether, but her position was revoked in April 1933 and he was reassigned to [Gustav Herglotz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Herglotz).[^n84] He received his PhD in July 1933 with a thesis on the [Riemann-Roch theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann-Roch_theorem) and [zeta-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeta-function),[^n85] and went on to make several contributions that [bear his name](wikipedia://en/39482629).[^n83] Tsen, best remembered for proving [Tsen's theorem](wikipedia://en/10129446), received his doctorate that December.[^n86] He returned to [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China) in 1935 to teach at [National Chekiang University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Chekiang_University),[^n83] but died five years later. Schilling also began studying under Noether, but was forced to find a new advisor due to Noether's emigration. Under [Helmut Hasse](wikipedia://en/727118), he completed his PhD in 1934 at the [University of Marburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Marburg).[^n83][^n87] He later worked as a [postdoc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postdoctoral_researcher) at [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge), before moving to the United States.[^o52]

Noether's other students were Wilhelm Dörnte, who received his doctorate in 1927 with a thesis on groups;[^n88] Werner Vorbeck, who did so in 1935 with a thesis on [splitting fields](wikipedia://en/174440);[^o52] and Wolfgang Wichmann, who did so in 1936 with a thesis on [p-adic theory](wikipedia://en/51423).[^n89] There is no information about the first two, but it is known that Wichmann supported a student initiative that unsuccessfully attempted to revoke Noether's dismissal[^n90] and died as a soldier on the [Eastern Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Front_%28World_War_II%29) during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II).[^o52]

#### Noether school {#Noether_school}

Noether developed a close circle of mathematicians beyond just her doctoral students who shared her approach to abstract algebra and contributed to the field's development,[^n91] a group often called the Noether school.[^n92][^n93] An example is her close work with [Wolfgang Krull](wikipedia://en/964328), who greatly advanced [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990) with his [*Hauptidealsatz*](wikipedia://en/542421) and his [dimension theory](wikipedia://en/56079) for commutative rings.[^n94] Another is [Gottfried Köthe](wikipedia://en/27641787), who contributed to the development of the theory of [hypercomplex quantities](wikipedia://en/51438) using Noether and Krull's methods.[^n94]

In addition to her mathematical insight, Noether was respected for her consideration of others. She sometimes acted rudely toward those who disagreed with her, but gained a reputation for helpfulness and patient guidance of new students. Her loyalty to mathematical precision caused one colleague to call her "a severe critic", but she combined this demand for accuracy with a nurturing attitude.[^n95] In Noether's obituary, Van der Waerden wrote:

> Completely unegotistical and free of vanity, she never claimed anything for herself, but promoted the works of her students above all.[^n60]

Noether showed a devotion to her subject and her students that extended beyond the academic day. Once, when the building was closed for a holiday, she gathered the class on the steps outside, led them through the woods, and lectured at a local coffee house.[^n96] Later, after [Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany) dismissed her from teaching, she invited students into her home to discuss their plans for the future and mathematical concepts.[^n97]

#### Influential lectures {#Influential_lectures}

Noether's frugal lifestyle was at first due to her being denied pay for her work. Even after the university began paying her a small salary in 1923, she continued to live a simple and modest life. She was paid more generously later in her life, but saved half of her salary to bequeath to her nephew, [Gottfried E. Noether](wikipedia://en/11071873).[^n98]

Biographers suggest that she was mostly unconcerned about appearance and manners, focusing on her studies. [Olga Taussky-Todd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olga_Taussky-Todd), a distinguished algebraist taught by Noether, described a luncheon during which Noether, wholly engrossed in a discussion of mathematics, "gesticulated wildly" as she ate and "spilled her food constantly and wiped it off from her dress, completely unperturbed".[^n99] Appearance-conscious students cringed as she retrieved the handkerchief from her blouse and ignored the increasing disarray of her hair during a lecture. Two female students once approached her during a break in a two-hour class to express their concern, but were unable to break through the energetic mathematical discussion she was having with other students.[^n100]

Noether did not follow a lesson plan in her lectures.[^n60] She spoke quickly, and many found her lectures difficult to follow, including [Carl Ludwig Siegel](wikipedia://en/716602) and [Paul Dubreil](wikipedia://en/1389591).[^n101][^n102] Students who disliked her style often felt alienated.[^n103] "Outsiders" who occasionally visited Noether's lectures usually spent only half an hour in the room before leaving in frustration or confusion. A regular student said of one such instance: "The enemy has been defeated; he has cleared out."[^n104]

Noether used her lectures as spontaneous discussion time with her students, to think through and clarify important problems. Some of her most important results were developed in these lectures, and her students' notes formed the basis for several important textbooks, such as van der Waerden's and Deuring's.[^n60] Noether's most dedicated students inherited her enthusiasm for mathematics and relished their conversations with her.[^n105][^n106]

Several of Noether's colleagues attended her lectures, and she sometimes allowed others (including her students) to receive credit for her ideas, so that much of her work appeared in papers not under her name.[^n61] Noether was recorded as having given at least five semester-long courses at Göttingen:[^o156]

- Winter 1924–25: *Gruppentheorie und hyperkomplexe Zahlen* \[*Group Theory and Hypercomplex Numbers*\]
- Winter 1927–28: *Hyperkomplexe Grössen und Darstellungstheorie* \[*Hypercomplex Quantities and Representation Theory*\]
- Summer 1928: *Nichtkommutative Algebra* \[*Noncommutative Algebra*\]
- Summer 1929: *Nichtkommutative Arithmetik* \[*Noncommutative Arithmetic*\]
- Winter 1929–30: *Algebra der hyperkomplexen Grössen* \[*Algebra of Hypercomplex Quantities*\]

### Moscow State University {#Moscow_State_University}

*Image: Moscow 05-2012 Mokhovaya 05.jpg.* Noether taught at [Moscow State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_State_University) in 1928–1929.

*Image: Paul S Alexandroff 2.jpg.* [Pavel Alexandrov](wikipedia://en/576245)

In 1928–1929, Noether accepted an invitation to [Moscow State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_State_University), where she continued working with [P. S. Alexandrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._S._Alexandrov). In addition to carrying on with her research, she taught classes in abstract algebra and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997). She worked with the topologists [Lev Pontryagin](wikipedia://en/582075) and [Nikolai Chebotaryov](wikipedia://en/3033696), who later praised her contributions to the development of [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316).[^n107][^n108][^n109]

Politics was not central to her life, but Noether took a keen interest in political matters and, according to Alexandrov, showed considerable support for the [Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution). She was especially happy to see [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet) advances in the fields of science and mathematics, which she considered indicative of new opportunities made possible by the [Bolshevik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolshevik) project. This attitude caused her problems in Germany, culminating in her eviction from a [pension lodging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pension_lodging) building, after student leaders complained of living with "a Marxist-leaning Jewess".[^n110] [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) recalled that "During the wild times after the [Revolution of 1918](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Revolution_of_1918–1919)," Noether "sided more or less with the [Social Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Democratic_Party_of_Germany)".[^n31] She was a member of the [Independent Social Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Social_Democratic_Party_of_Germany) from 1919 to 1922, a short-lived splinter party. In the words of logician and historian [Colin McLarty](wikipedia://en/26427352), "she was not a Bolshevist, but was not afraid to be called one."[^n111]

Noether planned to return to Moscow, an effort for which she received support from Alexandrov. After she left Germany in 1933, he tried to help her gain a chair at Moscow State University through the [Soviet Education Ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narkompros). This proved unsuccessful, but they corresponded frequently during the 1930s, and in 1935 she made plans for a return to the Soviet Union.[^n110]

### Recognition {#Recognition}

In 1932, Emmy Noether and [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351) received the [Ackermann–Teubner Memorial Award](wikipedia://en/19181338) for their contributions to mathematics.[^o154] The prize included a monetary reward of 500 and was seen as a long-overdue official recognition of her considerable work in the field. Nevertheless, her colleagues expressed frustration at the fact that she was not elected to the [Göttingen *Gesellschaft der Wissenschaften*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Göttingen_Academy_of_Sciences) (academy of sciences) and was never promoted to the position of *[Ordentlicher Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordentlicher_Professor)*[^n112][^n113] (full professor).[^n53]

Noether's colleagues celebrated her fiftieth birthday, in 1932, in typical mathematicians' style. [Helmut Hasse](wikipedia://en/727118) dedicated an article to her in the *[Mathematische Annalen](wikipedia://en/1423381)*, wherein he confirmed her suspicion that some aspects of [noncommutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noncommutative_algebra) are simpler than those of [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990), by proving a noncommutative [reciprocity law](wikipedia://en/25272).[^n114] This pleased her immensely. He also sent her a mathematical riddle, which he called the "m<sub>μν</sub>-riddle of syllables". She solved it immediately, but the riddle has been lost.[^n112][^n113]

*Image: Internationaler Mathematikerkongress Zürich 1932 - ETH BIB Portr 10680-FL (Johannes Meiner).jpg.* Noether visited [Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zürich) in 1932 to deliver a [plenary address at the International Congress of Mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plenary_address_at_the_International_Congress_of_Mathematicians).

In September of the same year, Noether delivered a plenary address (*großer Vortrag*) on "Hyper-complex systems in their relations to commutative algebra and to number theory" at the [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) in [Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zürich). The congress was attended by 800 people, including Noether's colleagues [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544), [Edmund Landau](wikipedia://en/222230), and [Wolfgang Krull](wikipedia://en/964328). There were 420 official participants and twenty-one plenary addresses presented. Apparently, Noether's prominent speaking position was a recognition of the importance of her contributions to mathematics. The 1932 congress is sometimes described as the high point of her career.[^n113][^n115]

### Expulsion from Göttingen by Nazi Germany {#Expulsion_from_Göttingen_by_Nazi_Germany}

When [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler) became the [German *Reichskanzler*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_of_Germany_%28German_Reich%29) in January 1933, [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi) activity around the country increased dramatically. At the University of Göttingen, the German Student Association led the attack on the "un-German spirit" attributed to Jews and was aided by *[privatdozent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privatdozent)* and Noether's former student [Werner Weber](wikipedia://en/53613774). [Antisemitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisemitic) attitudes created a climate hostile to Jewish professors. One young protester reportedly demanded: "Aryan students want [Aryan mathematics](wikipedia://en/31018614) and not Jewish mathematics."[^n78]

One of the first actions of Hitler's administration was the [Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_for_the_Restoration_of_the_Professional_Civil_Service) which removed Jews and politically suspect government employees (including university professors) from their jobs unless they had "demonstrated their loyalty to Germany" by [serving in World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frontkämpferprivileg). In April 1933, Noether received a notice from the Prussian Ministry for Sciences, Art, and Public Education which read: "On the basis of paragraph 3 of the Civil Service Code of 7 April 1933, I hereby withdraw from you the right to teach at the University of Göttingen."[^n116][^n117] Several of Noether's colleagues, including [Max Born](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Born) and [Richard Courant](wikipedia://en/715238), also had their positions revoked.[^n116][^n117]

Noether accepted the decision calmly, providing support for others during this difficult time. [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) later wrote that "Emmy Noether – her courage, her frankness, her unconcern about her own fate, her conciliatory spirit – was in the midst of all the hatred and meanness, despair and sorrow surrounding us, a moral solace."[^n78] Typically, Noether remained focused on mathematics, gathering students in her apartment to discuss [class field theory](wikipedia://en/242695). When one of her students appeared in the uniform of the Nazi [paramilitary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramilitary) organization *[Sturmabteilung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturmabteilung)* (SA), she showed no sign of agitation and, reportedly, even laughed about it later.[^n116][^n117]

### Refuge at Bryn Mawr and Princeton {#Refuge_at_Bryn_Mawr_and_Princeton}

*Image: Entrance Bryn Mawr.JPG.* [Bryn Mawr College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryn_Mawr_College) provided a welcoming home for Noether during the last two years of her life.

As dozens of newly unemployed professors began searching for positions outside Germany, their colleagues in the United States provided assistance and job opportunities for them. [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736) and [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) were appointed by the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) in [Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey), while others worked to find a sponsor required for legal [immigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration). Noether was contacted by representatives of two educational institutions: [Bryn Mawr College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryn_Mawr_College), in the U.S., and [Somerville College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somerville_College) at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford), in England. After a series of negotiations with the [Rockefeller Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_Foundation), a grant to Bryn Mawr was approved for Noether and she took a position there in late 1933.[^n118][^n119]

At Bryn Mawr, Noether met and befriended [Anna Wheeler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Johnson_Pell_Wheeler), who had studied at Göttingen just before Noether arrived there. Another source of support at the college was Bryn Mawr's president, [Marion Edwards Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marion_Edwards_Park), who enthusiastically invited mathematicians in the area to "see Dr. Noether in action!"[^n120][^n121]

At Bryn Mawr, Noether formed a group, sometimes called the Noether girls,[^n122] of four post-doctoral (Grace Shover Quinn, [Marie Johanna Weiss](wikipedia://en/68210910), [Olga Taussky-Todd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olga_Taussky-Todd)) and doctoral (Ruth Stauffer) students.[^n123] They enthusiastically worked through van der Waerden's *Moderne Algebra I* and parts of [Erich Hecke](wikipedia://en/1233990)'s *Theorie der algebraischen Zahlen* (*Theory of algebraic numbers*).[^n124] Stauffer was Noether's only doctoral student in the U.S.; Noether died shortly before she graduated.[^n125] She took her examination with [Richard Brauer](wikipedia://en/605134) and received her degree in June 1935,[^n126] with a thesis on separable [normal extensions](wikipedia://en/929925).[^n127] After her doctorate, Stauffer worked as a teacher for a short period and as a statistician for over 30 years.[^o52][^n126]

In 1934, Noether began lecturing at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton upon the invitation of [Abraham Flexner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Flexner) and [Oswald Veblen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Veblen).[^o195] She also worked with [Abraham Albert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Albert) and [Harry Vandiver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Vandiver).[^n128] She said of [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) that she was not welcome at "the men's university, where nothing female is admitted".[^n129]

Her time in the U.S. was pleasant; she was surrounded by supportive colleagues and absorbed in her favorite subjects.[^n130] In mid-1934, she briefly returned to Germany to see Emil Artin and her brother [Fritz](wikipedia://en/7522370).[^n131] The latter, after having been forced out of his job at the [Technische Hochschule Breslau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technische_Hochschule_Breslau), had accepted a position at the Research Institute for Mathematics and Mechanics in [Tomsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomsk), in the Siberian Federal District of Russia.[^n131]

Many of her former colleagues had been forced out of the universities, but she was able to use the library in Göttingen as a "foreign scholar". Without incident, Noether returned to the U.S. and her studies at Bryn Mawr.[^n132][^n133]

### Death {#Death}

In April 1935, doctors discovered a tumor in Noether's pelvis. Worried about complications from surgery, they ordered two days of bed rest first. During the operation they discovered an [ovarian cyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovarian_cyst) "the size of a large cantaloupe".[^n134] Two smaller tumors in her uterus appeared to be benign and were not removed to avoid prolonging surgery. For three days she appeared to convalesce normally, and she recovered quickly from a [circulatory collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulatory_collapse) on the fourth. On 14 April, Noether fell unconscious, her temperature soared to 109 °F, and she died. "\[I\]t is not easy to say what had occurred in Dr. Noether", one of the physicians wrote. "It is possible that there was some form of unusual and virulent infection, which struck the base of the brain where the heat centers are supposed to be located." She was 53.[^n134]

*Image: Bryn Mawr College Cloisters.JPG.* Noether's ashes were placed under the cloistered walkway of Bryn Mawr's [Old Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryn_Mawr_College#Old_Library_%28previously_M._Carey_Thomas_Library_and_College_Hall%29).

A few days after Noether's death, her friends and associates at Bryn Mawr held a small memorial service at College President Park's house.[^n135] Hermann Weyl and Richard Brauer both traveled from Princeton and delivered eulogies.[^n136] In the months that followed, written tributes began to appear: Albert Einstein joined van der Waerden, Weyl, and [Pavel Alexandrov](wikipedia://en/576245) in paying their respects.[^o3] Her body was cremated and the ashes interred under the walkway around the cloisters of the [Old Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryn_Mawr_College#Old_Library_%28previously_M._Carey_Thomas_Library_and_College_Hall%29) at Bryn Mawr.[^n137][^o209]

## Contributions to mathematics and physics {#Contributions_to_mathematics_and_physics}

Noether's work in [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) and [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) was influential in mathematics, while [Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159) has widespread consequences for [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028) and [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087). Noether showed an acute propensity for abstract thought, which allowed her to approach problems of mathematics in fresh and original ways.[^n33] Her friend and colleague [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) described her scholarly output in three epochs: (1) the period of relative dependence, 1907–1919

(2) the investigations grouped around the general theory of ideals 1920–1926

(3) the study of the non-commutative algebras, their representations by linear transformations, and their application to the study of commutative number fields and their arithmetics

In the first epoch (1907–1919), Noether dealt primarily with [differential and algebraic invariants](wikipedia://en/363762), beginning with her dissertation under [Paul Gordan](wikipedia://en/1059850). Her mathematical horizons broadened, and her work became more general and abstract, as she became acquainted with the work of [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302), through close interactions with a successor to Gordan, [Ernst Sigismund Fischer](wikipedia://en/5721087). Shortly after moving to Göttingen in 1915, she proved the two [Noether's theorems](wikipedia://en/150159), "one of the most important mathematical theorems ever proved in guiding the development of modern physics".[^n4]

In the second epoch (1920–1926), Noether devoted herself to developing the theory of [mathematical rings](wikipedia://en/48404).[^n138] In the third epoch (1927–1935), Noether focused on [noncommutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noncommutative_algebra), [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformations), and commutative number fields.[^n139] The results of Noether's first epoch were impressive and useful, but her fame among mathematicians rests more on the groundbreaking work she did in her second and third epochs, as noted by Hermann Weyl and B. L. van der Waerden in their obituaries of her.[^n31][^n60]

In these epochs, she was not merely applying ideas and methods of the earlier mathematicians; rather, she was crafting new systems of mathematical definitions that would be used by future mathematicians. In particular, she developed a completely new theory of [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) in [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), generalizing the earlier work of [Richard Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106). She is also renowned for developing ascending chain conditions – a simple finiteness condition that yielded powerful results in her hands.[^o218] Such conditions and the theory of ideals enabled Noether to generalize many older results and to treat old problems from a new perspective, such as the topics of [algebraic invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_invariant) that had been studied by her father and [elimination theory](wikipedia://en/245978), discussed below.

Noether's most important contributions to mathematics were to the development of an emerging new field, [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384).[^n140]

Unlike most mathematicians, she did not make abstractions by generalizing from known examples; rather, she worked directly with the abstractions. In his obituary of Noether, van der Waerden recalled that The maxim by which Emmy Noether was guided throughout her work might be formulated as follows: "Any relationships between numbers, functions, and operations become transparent, generally applicable, and fully productive only after they have been isolated from their particular objects and been formulated as universally valid concepts."[^n141]

This is the *begriffliche Mathematik* (purely conceptual mathematics) that was characteristic of Noether. This style of mathematics was consequently adopted by other mathematicians, especially in the (then new) field of abstract algebra.[^n142]

### First epoch (1908–1919) {#First_epoch_(1908–1919)}

#### Algebraic invariant theory {#Algebraic_invariant_theory}

*Image: Emmy Noether - Table of invariants 2.jpg.* Table 2 from Noether's dissertation[^n21] on invariant theory. This table collects 202 of the 331 invariants of ternary biquadratic forms. These forms are graded in two variables *x* and *u*. The horizontal direction of the table lists the invariants with increasing grades in *x*, while the vertical direction lists them with increasing grades in *u*.

Much of Noether's work in the first epoch of her career was associated with [invariant theory](wikipedia://en/363762), principally [algebraic invariant theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_invariant_theory). Invariant theory is concerned with expressions that remain constant (invariant) under a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of transformations.[^n143] As an everyday example, if a rigid [metre-stick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre-stick) is rotated, the coordinates of its endpoints change, but its length remains the same. A more sophisticated example of an *invariant* is the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607) $B^{2} - 4AC$ of a homogeneous quadratic polynomial $Ax^{2} + Bxy + Cy^{2}$, where $x$ and $y$ are [indeterminates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_%28variable%29). The discriminant is called "invariant" because it is not changed by linear substitutions $x \to ax + by$ and $y \to cx + dy$ with determinant $ad - bc = 1$. These substitutions form the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) $SL_{2}$.[^o226]

One can ask for all polynomials in $A$, $B$, and $C$ that are unchanged by the action of $SL_{2}$; these turn out to be the polynomials in the discriminant.[^n144] More generally, one can ask for the invariants of [homogeneous polynomials](wikipedia://en/1698977) $A_{0}x^{r}y^{0} + ... + A_{r}x^{0}y^{r}$ of higher degree, which will be certain polynomials in the coefficients $A_{0}, ..., A_{r}$, and more generally still, one can ask the similar question for homogeneous polynomials in more than two variables.[^n145]

One of the main goals of invariant theory was to solve the "*finite basis problem*". The sum or product of any two invariants is invariant, and the finite basis problem asked whether it was possible to get all the invariants by starting with a finite list of invariants, called *generators*, and then, adding or multiplying the generators together.[^n146] For example, the discriminant gives a finite basis (with one element) for the invariants of a quadratic polynomial.[^n144]

Noether's advisor, Paul Gordan, was known as the "king of invariant theory", and his chief contribution to mathematics was his 1870 solution of the finite basis problem for invariants of homogeneous polynomials in two variables.[^n147][^n148] He proved this by giving a constructive method for finding all of the invariants and their generators, but was not able to carry out this constructive approach for invariants in three or more variables. In 1890, David Hilbert proved a similar statement for the invariants of homogeneous polynomials in any number of variables.[^n149][^n150] Furthermore, his method worked, not only for the special linear group, but also for some of its subgroups such as the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group).[^n151]

Noether followed Gordan's lead, writing her doctoral dissertation and several other publications on invariant theory. She extended Gordan's results and also built upon Hilbert's research. Later, she would disparage this work, finding it of little interest and admitting to forgetting the details of it.[^n152] Hermann Weyl wrote,

> \[A\] greater contrast is hardly imaginable than between her first paper, the dissertation, and her works of maturity; for the former is an extreme example of formal computations and the latter constitute an extreme and grandiose example of conceptual axiomatic thinking in mathematics.[^n153]

#### Galois theory {#Galois_theory}

[Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316) concerns transformations of [number fields](wikipedia://en/10603) that [permute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permute) the roots of an equation.[^o239] Consider a polynomial equation of a variable $x$ of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) $n$, in which the coefficients are drawn from some [ground field](wikipedia://en/27161748), which might be, for example, the field of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), or the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) 7. There may or may not be choices of $x$, which make this polynomial evaluate to zero. Such choices, if they exist, are called [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function).[^n154] For example, if the polynomial is $x^{2} + 1$ and the field is the real numbers, then the polynomial has no roots, because any choice of $x$ makes the polynomial greater than or equal to one.[^n155] If the field is [extended](wikipedia://en/11634) then the polynomial may gain roots,[^n156] and if it is extended enough, then it always has a number of roots equal to its degree.[^n157]

Continuing the previous example, if the field is enlarged to the complex numbers, then the polynomial gains two roots, $+i$ and $-i$, where $i$ is the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358), that is, $i^{2} = -1.$ More generally, the extension field in which a polynomial can be factored into its roots is known as the [splitting field](wikipedia://en/174440) of the polynomial.[^o244]

The [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of a polynomial is the set of all transformations of the splitting field which preserve the ground field and the roots of the polynomial.[^o245] (These transformations are called [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160).) The Galois group of $x^{2} + 1$ consists of two elements: The identity transformation, which sends every complex number to itself, and [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation), which sends $+i$ to $-i$. Since the Galois group does not change the ground field, it leaves the coefficients of the polynomial unchanged, so it must leave the set of all roots unchanged. Each root can move to another root, so transformation determines a [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) of the $n$ roots among themselves. The significance of the Galois group derives from the [fundamental theorem of Galois theory](wikipedia://en/1947635), which proves that the fields lying between the ground field and the splitting field are in one-to-one correspondence with the [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) of the Galois group.[^o246]

In 1918, Noether published a paper on the [inverse Galois problem](wikipedia://en/739166).[^o247] Instead of determining the Galois group of transformations of a given field and its extension, Noether asked whether, given a field and a group, it always is possible to find an extension of the field that has the given group as its Galois group. She reduced this to "[Noether's problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether's_problem)", which asks whether the fixed field of a subgroup *G* of the [permutation group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_{n}$ acting on the field $k(x_{1}, ..., x_{n})$ always is a pure [transcendental extension](wikipedia://en/287364) of the field $k$. (She first mentioned this problem in a 1913 paper,[^o248] where she attributed the problem to her colleague [Fischer](wikipedia://en/5721087).) She showed this was true for $n$ = 2, 3, or 4. In 1969, [Richard Swan](wikipedia://en/18384954) found a counterexample to Noether's problem, with $n$ = 47 and $G$ a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order 47[^o249] (although this group can be realized as a [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) over the rationals in other ways). The inverse Galois problem remains unsolved.[^o250]

#### Physics {#Physics}

Noether was brought to [Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Göttingen) in 1915 by Hilbert and Klein, who wanted her expertise in invariant theory to help them understand [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024),[^n158] a geometrical theory of [gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation) developed mainly by Einstein. Hilbert had observed that the [conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088) seemed to be violated in general relativity, because gravitational energy could itself gravitate. Noether provided the resolution of this paradox, and a fundamental tool of modern [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028), in a 1918 paper.[^o253] This paper presented two theorems, of which the first is known as [Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159).[^o254] Together, these theorems not only solve the problem for general relativity, but also determine the conserved quantities for *every* system of physical laws that possesses some continuous symmetry.[^o255] Upon receiving her work, Einstein wrote to Hilbert:Yesterday I received from Miss Noether a very interesting paper on invariants. I'm impressed that such things can be understood in such a general way. The old guard at Göttingen should take some lessons from Miss Noether! She seems to know her stuff.[^o256]

For illustration, if a physical system behaves the same, regardless of how it is oriented in space, the physical laws that govern it are rotationally symmetric; from this symmetry, Noether's theorem shows the [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839) of the system must be conserved.[^n159][^o258] The physical system itself need not be symmetric; a jagged asteroid tumbling in space [conserves angular momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_angular_momentum) despite its asymmetry. Rather, the symmetry of the *physical laws* governing the system is responsible for the conservation law. As another example, if a physical experiment works the same way at any place and at any time, then its laws are symmetric under continuous translations in space and time; by Noether's theorem, these symmetries account for the [conservation laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_laws) of [linear momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_momentum) and [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) within this system, respectively.[^n160][^o260]

At the time, physicists were not familiar with [Sophus Lie](wikipedia://en/54010)'s theory of [continuous groups](wikipedia://en/17945), on which Noether had built. Many physicists first learned of Noether's theorem from an article by Edward Lee Hill that presented only a special case of it. Consequently, the full scope of her result was not immediately appreciated.[^o261] During the latter half of the 20th century, Noether's theorem became a fundamental tool of modern [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028), because of the insight it gives into conservation laws, and also as a practical calculation tool. Her theorem allows researchers to determine the conserved quantities from the observed symmetries of a physical system. Conversely, it facilitates the description of a physical system based on classes of hypothetical physical laws. For illustration, suppose that a new physical phenomenon is discovered. Noether's theorem provides a test for theoretical models of the phenomenon: If the theory has a continuous symmetry, then Noether's theorem guarantees that the theory has a conserved quantity, and for the theory to be correct, this conservation must be observable in experiments.[^o7]

### Second epoch (1920–1926) {#Second_epoch_(1920–1926)}

#### Ascending and descending chain conditions {#Ascending_and_descending_chain_conditions}

In this epoch, Noether became famous for her deft use of ascending (*Teilerkettensatz*) or descending (*Vielfachenkettensatz*) chain conditions.[^o218] A sequence of [non-empty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-empty) [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) $A_{1}, A_{2}, A_{3}$, ... of a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $S$ is usually said to be *ascending* if each is a subset of the next:

$$A_{1} \subseteq A_{2} \subseteq A_{3} \subseteq \cdots.$$

Conversely, a sequence of subsets of $S$ is called *descending* if each contains the next subset:

$$A_{1} \supseteq A_{2} \supseteq A_{3} \supseteq \cdots.$$

A chain *becomes constant after a finite number of steps* if there is an $n$ such that $A_n = A_m$ for all $m \ge n$. A collection of subsets of a given set satisfies the [ascending chain condition](wikipedia://en/3189) if every ascending sequence becomes constant after a finite number of steps. It satisfies the descending chain condition if any descending sequence becomes constant after a finite number of steps.[^n161] Chain conditions can be used to show that every set of sub-objects has a maximal/minimal element, or that a complex object can be generated by a smaller number of elements.[^n162]

Many types of objects in [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) can satisfy chain conditions, and usually if they satisfy an ascending chain condition, they are called *[Noetherian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_%28disambiguation%29)* in her honor.[^n163] By definition, a [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129) satisfies an ascending chain condition on its left and right ideals, whereas a [Noetherian group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_group) is defined as a group in which every strictly ascending chain of subgroups is finite. A [Noetherian module](wikipedia://en/542431) is a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) in which every strictly ascending chain of submodules becomes constant after a finite number of steps.[^n164][^n165] A [Noetherian space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_space) is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) whose open subsets satisfy the ascending chain condition; this definition makes the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/37291) of a Noetherian ring a Noetherian topological space.[^n166][^n167]

The chain condition often is "inherited" by sub-objects. For example, all subspaces of a Noetherian space are Noetherian themselves; all subgroups and quotient groups of a Noetherian group are Noetherian; and, *[mutatis mutandis](wikipedia://en/742665)*, the same holds for submodules and quotient modules of a Noetherian module.[^n168] The chain condition also may be inherited by combinations or extensions of a Noetherian object. For example, finite direct sums of Noetherian rings are Noetherian, as is the [ring of formal power series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_formal_power_series) over a Noetherian ring.[^n169]

Another application of such chain conditions is in Noetherian induction—also known as [well-founded induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-founded_induction)—which is a generalization of [mathematical induction](wikipedia://en/18881). It is frequently used to reduce general statements about collections of objects to statements about specific objects in that collection. Suppose that $S$ is a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572). One way of proving a statement about the objects of $S$ is to assume the existence of a [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) and deduce a contradiction, thereby proving the [contrapositive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrapositive) of the original statement. The basic premise of Noetherian induction is that every non-empty subset of $S$ contains a minimal element. In particular, the set of all counterexamples contains a minimal element, the *minimal counterexample*. In order to prove the original statement, therefore, it suffices to prove something seemingly much weaker: For any counterexample, there is a smaller counterexample.[^o273]

#### Commutative rings, ideals, and modules {#Commutative_rings,_ideals,_and_modules}

Noether's paper *Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen* (*Theory of Ideals in Ring Domains*, 1921)[^n57] is the foundation of general commutative [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424), and gives one of the first general definitions of a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346).[^n170] Before her paper, most results in commutative algebra were restricted to special examples of commutative rings, such as polynomial rings over fields or rings of algebraic integers. Noether proved that in a ring that satisfies the ascending chain condition on [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977), every ideal is finitely generated. In 1943, French mathematician [Claude Chevalley](wikipedia://en/366040) coined the term *[Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129)* to describe this property.[^n170] A major result in Noether's 1921 paper is the [Lasker–Noether theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasker–Noether_theorem), which extends Lasker's theorem on the primary decomposition of ideals of polynomial rings to all Noetherian rings.[^n36][^n171] The Lasker–Noether theorem can be viewed as a generalization of the [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556) which states that any positive integer can be expressed as a product of [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666), and that this decomposition is unique.[^n172]

Noether's work *Abstrakter Aufbau der Idealtheorie in algebraischen Zahl- und Funktionenkörpern* (*Abstract Structure of the Theory of Ideals in Algebraic Number and Function Fields*, 1927)[^o281] characterized the rings in which the ideals have unique factorization into prime ideals (now called [Dedekind domains](wikipedia://en/145132)).[^n173] Noether showed that these rings were characterized by five conditions: they must satisfy the ascending and descending chain conditions, they must possess a unit element, but no [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441), and they must be [integrally closed](wikipedia://en/21420209) in their associated field of fractions.[^n173][^n174] This paper also contains what now are called the [isomorphism theorems](wikipedia://en/45241),[^n175] which describe some fundamental [natural isomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_isomorphism), and some other basic results on Noetherian and [Artinian modules](wikipedia://en/542336).[^n176]

#### Elimination theory {#Elimination_theory}

In 1923–1924, Noether applied her ideal theory to [elimination theory](wikipedia://en/245978) in a formulation that she attributed to her student, Kurt Hentzelt. She showed that fundamental theorems about the [factorization of polynomials](wikipedia://en/3776351) could be carried over directly.[^n177][^n178][^n179]

Traditionally, elimination theory is concerned with eliminating one or more variables from a system of polynomial equations, often by the method of [resultants](wikipedia://en/2195020).[^n180] For illustration, a system of equations often can be written in the form

$$\operatorname{Mv} = 0$$

where a matrix (or [linear transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transform)) $M$ (without the variable $x$) times a vector $v$ (that only has non-zero powers of $x$) is equal to the zero vector, $0$. Hence, the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the matrix $M$ must be zero, providing a new equation in which the variable $x$ has been eliminated.

#### Invariant theory of finite groups {#Invariant_theory_of_finite_groups}

Techniques such as Hilbert's original non-constructive solution to the finite basis problem could not be used to get quantitative information about the invariants of a group action, and furthermore, they did not apply to all group actions. In her 1915 paper,[^n181] Noether found a solution to the finite basis problem for a finite group of transformations $G$ acting on a finite-dimensional vector space over a field of characteristic zero. Her solution shows that the ring of invariants is generated by homogeneous invariants whose degree is less than, or equal to, the order of the finite group; this is called **Noether's bound**. Her paper gave two proofs of Noether's bound, both of which also work when the characteristic of the field is [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) to $\left|G\right|!$ (the [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606) of the order $\left|G\right|$ of the group $G$). The degrees of generators need not satisfy Noether's bound when the characteristic of the field divides the number $\left|G\right|$,[^n182] but Noether was not able to determine whether this bound was correct when the characteristic of the field divides $\left|G\right|!$ but not $\left|G\right|$. For many years, determining the truth or falsehood of this bound for this particular case was an open problem, called "Noether's gap". It was finally solved independently by Fleischmann in 2000 and Fogarty in 2001, who both showed that the bound remains true.[^n183][^n184]

In her 1926 paper,[^n185] Noether extended Hilbert's theorem to representations of a finite group over any field; the new case that did not follow from Hilbert's work is when the characteristic of the field divides the order of the group. Noether's result was later extended by [William Haboush](wikipedia://en/16374262) to all reductive groups by his proof of the [Mumford conjecture](wikipedia://en/632762).[^n186] In this paper Noether also introduced the *[Noether normalization lemma](wikipedia://en/3003553)*, showing that a finitely generated [domain](wikipedia://en/15462) $A$ over a field $k$ has a set $\{x_{1}, ..., x_{n}$} of [algebraically independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_independent) elements such that $A$ is [integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrality) over $k[x_{1}, ..., x_{n}]$.

#### Topology {#Topology}

*Image: Mug and Torus morph.gif.* A continuous deformation ([homotopy](wikipedia://en/212250)) of a coffee cup into a doughnut ([torus](wikipedia://en/74800)) and back

As noted by [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) in his obituary, Noether's contributions to [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) illustrate her generosity with ideas and how her insights could transform entire fields of mathematics.[^n31] In topology, mathematicians study the properties of objects that remain invariant even under deformation, properties such as their [connectedness](wikipedia://en/6233). An old joke is that "*a topologist cannot distinguish a donut from a coffee mug*", since they can be [continuously deformed](wikipedia://en/13660) into one another.[^n187]

Noether is credited with fundamental ideas that led to the development of [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) from the earlier [combinatorial topology](wikipedia://en/363400), specifically, the idea of [homology groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_theory#Towards_algebraic_topology).[^n188] According to Alexandrov, Noether attended lectures given by him and [Heinz Hopf](wikipedia://en/543082) in 1926 and 1927, where "she continually made observations which were often deep and subtle"[^n189] and: When ... she first became acquainted with a systematic construction of combinatorial topology, she immediately observed that it would be worthwhile to study directly the [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) of algebraic complexes and cycles of a given polyhedron and the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of the cycle group consisting of cycles homologous to zero; instead of the usual definition of [Betti numbers](wikipedia://en/346262), she suggested immediately defining the Betti group as the [complementary (quotient) group](wikipedia://en/11526) of the group of all cycles by the subgroup of cycles homologous to zero. This observation now seems self-evident, but in those years (1925–1928) this was a completely new point of view.[^n190]

Noether's suggestion that topology be studied algebraically was adopted immediately by Hopf, Alexandrov, and others,[^n190] and it became a frequent topic of discussion among the mathematicians of Göttingen.[^o306] Noether observed that her idea of a [Betti group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betti_group) makes the [Euler–Poincaré formula](wikipedia://en/148420) simpler to understand, and Hopf's own work on this subject[^n191] "bears the imprint of these remarks of Emmy Noether".[^n192] Noether mentions her own topology ideas only as an aside in a 1926 publication,[^n193] where she cites it as an application of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890).[^o310]

This algebraic approach to topology was also developed independently in [Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria). In a 1926–1927 course given in [Vienna](wikipedia://en/55866), [Leopold Vietoris](wikipedia://en/105043) defined a [homology group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_group), which was developed by [Walther Mayer](wikipedia://en/6004027) into an axiomatic definition in 1928.[^o311]

*Image: Helmut Hasse.jpg.* [Helmut Hasse](wikipedia://en/727118) worked with Noether and others to found the theory of [central simple algebras](wikipedia://en/404082).

### Third epoch (1927–1935) {#Third_epoch_(1927–1935)}

#### Hypercomplex numbers and representation theory {#Hypercomplex_numbers_and_representation_theory}

Much work on [hypercomplex numbers](wikipedia://en/51438) and [group representations](wikipedia://en/12695) was carried out in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, but remained disparate. Noether united these earlier results and gave the first general representation theory of groups and algebras.[^n194][^n195] This single work by Noether was said to have ushered in a new period in modern algebra and to have been of fundamental importance for its development.[^n196]

Briefly, Noether subsumed the structure theory of [associative algebras](wikipedia://en/2112) and the representation theory of groups into a single arithmetic theory of [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) and [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) in [rings](wikipedia://en/48404) satisfying [ascending chain conditions](wikipedia://en/3189).[^n195]

#### Noncommutative algebra {#Noncommutative_algebra}

Noether also was responsible for a number of other advances in the field of algebra. With [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351), [Richard Brauer](wikipedia://en/605134), and [Helmut Hasse](wikipedia://en/727118), she founded the theory of [central simple algebras](wikipedia://en/404082).[^n197]

A paper by Noether, Hasse, and Brauer pertains to [division algebras](wikipedia://en/26549625),[^o320] which are algebraic systems in which division is possible. They proved two important theorems: a [local-global theorem](wikipedia://en/489707) stating that if a finite-dimensional central division algebra over a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) splits locally everywhere then it splits globally (so is trivial), and from this, deduced their *Hauptsatz* ("main theorem"):

> Every finite-dimensional [central](wikipedia://en/404082) [division algebra](wikipedia://en/26549625) over an [algebraic number](wikipedia://en/1158) [field](wikipedia://en/10603) F splits over a [cyclic cyclotomic extension](wikipedia://en/174448).

These theorems allow one to classify all finite-dimensional central division algebras over a given number field. A subsequent paper by Noether showed, as a special case of a more general theorem, that all maximal subfields of a division algebra $D$ are [splitting fields](wikipedia://en/404082#Splitting_field).[^n198] This paper also contains the [Skolem–Noether theorem](wikipedia://en/693453), which states that any two embeddings of an extension of a field $k$ into a finite-dimensional central simple algebra over $k$ are conjugate. The [Brauer–Noether theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brauer–Noether_theorem)[^n199] gives a characterization of the splitting fields of a central division algebra over a field.[^n200]

## Legacy {#Legacy}

*Image: Emmy-noether-campus siegen.jpg.* The Emmy–Noether–Campus at the [University of Siegen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Siegen) is home to its mathematics and physics departments.[^o325]

*Image: Ruhmeshalle Muenchen Emmy Noether Mathematikerin-1 retusche.jpg.* Noether is one of the carved stone busts displayed in Germany's [Ruhmeshalle München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruhmeshalle_München) (or Munich Hall of Fame in English).

Noether's work continues to be relevant for the development of theoretical physics and mathematics, and she is considered one of the most important mathematicians of the 20th century.[^o209][^o326] She has been called the greatest woman mathematician in recorded history[^n1][^n2][^n201] by mathematicians such as [Pavel Alexandrov](wikipedia://en/576245),[^n202] [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544),[^n203] and [Jean Dieudonné](wikipedia://en/1070084).[^n204]

In a letter to *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times)*, [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736) wrote:[^o3]

In the judgment of the most competent living mathematicians, Fräulein Noether was the most significant creative mathematical genius thus far produced since the higher education of women began. In the realm of algebra, in which the most gifted mathematicians have been busy for centuries, she discovered methods which have proved of enormous importance in the development of the present-day younger generation of mathematicians.

In his obituary, algebraist [B. L. van der Waerden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B._L._van_der_Waerden) wrote that her mathematical originality was "absolute beyond comparison",[^n205] and Weyl said that Noether "changed the face of \[abstract\] algebra by her work".[^o334] Mathematician and historian [Jeremy Gray](wikipedia://en/24721410) wrote that all textbooks on abstract algebra bear the evidence of Noether's contributions: "Mathematicians simply do ring theory her way."[^n163] Several things [now bear her name](wikipedia://en/49991105), including many mathematical objects,[^n163] and an asteroid, 7001 Noether.[^n206] In 2019 *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_%28magazine%29)* created 89 new covers to celebrate women of the year starting from 1920; it chose Noether for 1921.[^o338]
