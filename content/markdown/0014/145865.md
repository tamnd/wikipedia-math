# Parts-per notation

*Image: Fluorescein (2).jpg.* [Fluorescein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorescein) aqueous solutions, diluted from 10000 to 1part per million in intervals of ten-fold dilution. At 10000 ppm the solution is a deep red colour. As the concentration decreases, the colour becomes greenish-orange, then a vibrant yellowish-green, with the final 1ppm sample a very pale yellowish-green.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Parts-per notation"}

In [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science) and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), ***parts-per* notation** is a set of pseudo-units to describe the small values of miscellaneous [dimensionless quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless_quantity), e.g. [mole fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_fraction) or [mass fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_fraction_%28chemistry%29).

:::

Since these [fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29) are quantity-per-quantity measures, they are pure numbers with no associated [units of measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Units_of_measurement). Commonly used are

- **parts-per-million – ppm**, 10<sup>−6</sup>
- **parts-per-billion – ppb**, 10<sup>−9</sup>
- **parts-per-trillion – ppt**, 10<sup>−12</sup>
- **parts-per-quadrillion – ppq**, 10<sup>−15</sup>

This notation is not part of the [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) (the SI system) and its meaning is ambiguous. In chemistry, ambiguity arises because parts-per notation is able to be used to refer to a [mole fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_fraction) and a [mass fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_fraction_%28chemistry%29), which are unequal depending on the [molar mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_mass) of the substance.

::: {#o3 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Parts-per notation is often used describing dilute solutions in [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry), for instance, the relative abundance of dissolved minerals or pollutants in [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water). The quantity "1 ppm" can be used for a mass fraction if a water-borne pollutant is present at one-millionth of a [gram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram) per gram of sample solution. When working with [aqueous solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueous_solution), it is common to assume that the density of water is 1.00 g/mL. Therefore, it is common to equate 1 kilogram of water with 1 L of water. Consequently, 1 ppm corresponds to 1 mg/L and 1 ppb corresponds to 1 μg/L.

Similarly, parts-per notation is used also in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) to express the value of various proportional phenomena. For instance, a special metal alloy might expand 1.2 [micrometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micrometre) per [meter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre) of length for every [degree Celsius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celsius) and this would be expressed as "[$\alpha$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficient_of_thermal_expansion) = 1.2 ppm/°C". Parts-per notation is also employed to denote the change, stability, or [uncertainty](wikipedia://en/27590) in measurements. For instance, the accuracy of land-survey distance measurements when using a [laser rangefinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_rangefinder) might be 1 millimeter per kilometer of distance; this could be expressed as "[Accuracy](wikipedia://en/41932) = 1 ppm."

Parts-per notations are all dimensionless quantities: in mathematical expressions, the units of measurement always cancel. In fractions like "2 nanometers per meter" (2 nm/m = 2 × −9 = 2 ppb = 2 × \0.000,000,001), so the [quotients](wikipedia://en/332640) are pure-number [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) with positive values less than or equal to 1. When parts-per notations, including the [percent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percent) symbol (%), are used in regular prose (as opposed to mathematical expressions), they are still pure-number dimensionless quantities. However, they generally take the literal "parts per" meaning of a comparative ratio (e.g. "2 ppb" would generally be interpreted as "two parts in a billion parts").[^o4]

Parts-per notations may be expressed in terms of any unit of the same measure. For instance, the [expansion coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficient_of_thermal_expansion) of some [brass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brass) alloy, [$\alpha$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficient_of_thermal_expansion) = 18.7 ppm/°C, may be expressed as 18.7 ([μm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micrometre)/[m](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meter))/°C, or as 18.7 (μ [in](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inch)/[in](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inch))/°C; the numeric value representing a relative proportion does not change with the adoption of a different unit of length. Similarly, a [metering pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metering_pump) that injects a trace chemical into the main process line at the proportional flow rate [$Q$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volumetric_flow_rate)${}_{p}$ = 12 ppm, is doing so at a rate that may be expressed in a variety of volumetric units, including 125 μ[L](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litre)/[L](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litre), 125 μ[gal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallon)/[gal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallon), 125 cm<sup>3</sup>/[m<sup>3</sup>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_metre), etc.

In [nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_resonance_spectroscopy) (NMR), [chemical shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_shift) is usually expressed in ppm. It represents the difference of a measured frequency in parts per million from the reference frequency. The reference frequency depends on the instrument's magnetic field and the element being measured. It is usually expressed in [MHz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MHz). Typical chemical shifts are rarely more than a few hundred Hz from the reference frequency, so chemical shifts are conveniently expressed in ppm ([Hz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertz_%28unit%29)/MHz). Parts-per notation gives a dimensionless quantity that does not depend on the instrument's field strength.

- *One part per [hundred](wikipedia://en/407384)* is generally represented by the [percent sign](wikipedia://en/1712680) (%) and denotes [one part per 100 (2) parts](wikipedia://en/64493), and a value of -2. This is equivalent to about fourteen minutes out of one day.
- *One part per [thousand](wikipedia://en/442472)* should generally be spelled out in full and **not** as "ppt" (which is usually understood to represent "parts per [trillion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trillion_%28short_scale%29)"). It may also be denoted by the [permille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_mille) sign (‰). Note however, that specific disciplines such as oceanography, as well as educational exercises, do use the "ppt" abbreviation. "One part per thousand" denotes one part per 1,000 (3) parts, and a value of -3. This is equivalent to about ninety seconds out of one day.
- *One part per [ten thousand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_thousand)* is denoted by the [permyriad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permyriad) sign (‱). Although rarely used in science (ppm is typically used instead), one permyriad has an unambiguous value of one part per 10,000 (4) parts, and a value of -4. This is equivalent to about nine seconds out of one day. In contrast, in [finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance), the [basis point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_point) is typically used to denote changes in or differences between percentage interest rates (although it can also be used in other cases where it is desirable to express quantities in hundredths of a percent). For instance, a change in an interest rate from 5.15% per annum to 5.35% per annum could be denoted as a change of 20 basis points (per annum). As with interest rates, the words "per annum" (or "per year") are often omitted. In that case, the basis point is a quantity with a dimension of (time<sup>−1</sup>).[^o7]
- *One part per [hundred thousand](wikipedia://en/1462080)*, *[per cent mille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_cent_mille)* (**pcm**) or *milli-percent* denotes one part per 100,000 (5) parts, and a value of -5. It is commonly used in [epidemiology](wikipedia://en/66997) for mortality, crime and disease prevalence rates, and nuclear reactor engineering as a unit of reactivity. In [time measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_measurement) it is equivalent to about 5 minutes out of a year; in [distance measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distance_measurement), it is equivalent to 1 cm of error per km of distance traversed.
- *One part per [million](wikipedia://en/1045999)* (**ppm**) denotes one part per 1,000,000 (6) parts, and a value of -6. It is equivalent to about 32 seconds out of a year or 1 mm of error per km of distance traversed. In [mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining), it is also equivalent to one [gram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram) per [metric ton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_ton), expressed as g/t.
- *One part per [billion](wikipedia://en/1136363)* (**ppb**) denotes one part per 1,000,000,000 (9) parts, and a value of -9. This is equivalent to about three seconds out of a [century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Century).
- *One part per [trillion](wikipedia://en/81145927)* (**ppt**) denotes one part per 1,000,000,000,000 (12) parts, and a value of -12. This is equivalent to about thirty seconds out of every million years.
- *One part per [quadrillion](wikipedia://en/260914#1015)* (**ppq**) denotes one part per 1,000,000,000,000,000 (15) parts, and a value of -15. This is equivalent to about two and a half minutes out of the [age of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_the_Earth) (4.5 billion years). Although relatively uncommon in analytical chemistry, measurements at the ppq level are sometimes performed.[^o8]

## Criticism {#Criticism}

Although the [International Bureau of Weights and Measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Bureau_of_Weights_and_Measures) (an international standards organization known also by its [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France)-language initials BIPM) recognizes the use of parts-per notation, it is not formally part of the [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) (SI).[^o4] Note that although "[percent](wikipedia://en/64493)" (%) is not formally part of the SI, both the BIPM and the [International Organization for Standardization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Organization_for_Standardization) (ISO) take the position that "in mathematical expressions, the internationally recognized symbol % (percent) may be used with the SI to represent the number 0.01" for dimensionless quantities.[^o4][^o10] According to [IUPAP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Union_of_Pure_and_Applied_Physics), "a continued source of annoyance to unit purists has been the continued use of percent, ppm, ppb, and ppt".[^o11][^o12] Although SI-compliant expressions should be used as an alternative, the parts-per notation remains nevertheless widely used in technical disciplines. The main problems with the parts-per notation are set out below.

### Long and short scales {#Long_and_short_scales}

Because the [named numbers](wikipedia://en/682403) starting with a "[billion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_and_short_scales)" have different values in different countries, the BIPM suggests avoiding the use of "ppb" and "ppt" to prevent misunderstanding. The U.S. [National Institute of Standards and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology) (NIST) takes the stringent position, stating that "the language-dependent terms \[...\] are not acceptable for use with the SI to express the values of quantities".[^o14]

### Thousand vs. trillion {#Thousand_vs._trillion}

Although "ppt" usually means "parts per trillion", it occasionally means "parts per thousand". Unless the meaning of "ppt" is defined explicitly, it has to be determined from the context.[^o12]

### Mass fraction vs. mole fraction vs. volume fraction {#Mass_fraction_vs._mole_fraction_vs._volume_fraction}

Another problem of the parts-per notation is that it may refer to [mass fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_fraction_%28chemistry%29), [mole fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_fraction) or [volume fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume_fraction). Since it is usually not stated which quantity is used, it is better to write the units out, such as kg/kg, mol/mol or m<sup>3</sup>/m<sup>3</sup>, even though they are all dimensionless.[^o12] The difference is quite significant when dealing with gases, and it is very important to specify which quantity is being used. For example, the conversion factor between a mass fraction of 1 ppb and a mole fraction of 1 ppb is about 4.7 for the greenhouse gas [CFC-11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CFC-11) in air (Molar mass of CFC-11 / Mean molar mass of air = 137.368 / 28.97 = 4.74).

For volume fraction, the suffix *V* or *v* is sometimes appended to the parts-per notation (e.g. ppmV, ppbv, pptv).[^o17] To distinguish the mass fraction from volume fraction or mole fraction, the letter *m* (for mass, but is ambiguous with mole fraction) or *w* (standing for weight) is sometimes added to the abbreviation (e.g. ppmw, ppbw).[^o12] Expressions such as "parts per (million) by volume" and "parts per (million) by mass" are sometimes used.

## Uno (proposed dimensionless unit) {#Uno_(proposed_dimensionless_unit)}

Because of the cumbersome nature of expressing certain dimensionless quantities per SI guidelines, the [International Union of Pure and Applied Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Union_of_Pure_and_Applied_Physics) (IUPAP) in 1999 proposed the adoption of the special name "uno" (symbol: U) to represent the number 1 in dimensionless quantities.[^o11] In 2004, a report to the [International Committee for Weights and Measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Committee_for_Weights_and_Measures) (CIPM) stated that the response to the proposal of the uno "had been almost entirely negative", and the principal proponent "recommended dropping the idea".[^o24] To date, the uno has not been adopted by any [standards organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standards_organization).
