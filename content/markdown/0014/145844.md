::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Hyperboloid"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **hyperboloid of revolution**, sometimes called a **circular hyperboloid**, is the [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) generated by rotating a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) around one of its [principal axes](wikipedia://en/14052#Equation). A **hyperboloid** is the surface obtained from a hyperboloid of revolution by deforming it by means of directional [scalings](wikipedia://en/651752), or more generally, of an [affine transformation](wikipedia://en/38449).

:::

A hyperboloid is a [quadric surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadric_surface), that is, a [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) defined as the [zero set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_set) of a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) of degree two in three variables. Among quadric surfaces, a hyperboloid is characterized by not being a [cone](wikipedia://en/706244) or a [cylinder](wikipedia://en/859283), having a [center of symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_symmetry), and intersecting many [planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) into hyperbolas. A hyperboloid has three pairwise [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) [axes of symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096), and three pairwise perpendicular [planes of symmetry](wikipedia://en/1390873).

Given a hyperboloid, one can choose a [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706) such that the hyperboloid is defined by one of the following equations:

$${x^2 \over a^2} + {y^2 \over b^2} - {z^2 \over c^2} = 1,$$

or

$${x^2 \over a^2} + {y^2 \over b^2} - {z^2 \over c^2} = -1.$$

The coordinate axes are axes of symmetry of the hyperboloid and the origin is the center of symmetry of the hyperboloid. In any case, the hyperboloid is [asymptotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic) to the cone of the equations:

$${x^2 \over a^2} + {y^2 \over b^2} - {z^2 \over c^2} = 0 .$$

One has a hyperboloid of revolution if and only if $a^2=b^2.$ Otherwise, the axes are uniquely defined ([up to](wikipedia://en/44787) the exchange of the *x*-axis and the *y*-axis).

There are two kinds of hyperboloids. In the first case ($+1$ in the right-hand side of the equation): a **one-sheet hyperboloid**, also called a **hyperbolic hyperboloid**. It is a [connected surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_set), which has a negative [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) at every point. This implies near every point the intersection of the hyperboloid and its [tangent plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_plane) at the point consists of two branches of curve that have distinct tangents at the point. In the case of the one-sheet hyperboloid, these branches of curves are [lines](wikipedia://en/946975) and thus the one-sheet hyperboloid is a [doubly ruled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_ruled) surface.

In the second case ($-1$ in the right-hand side of the equation): a **two-sheet hyperboloid**, also called an **elliptic hyperboloid**. The surface has two [connected components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28topology%29) and a positive Gaussian curvature at every point. The surface is *convex* in the sense that the tangent plane at every point intersects the surface only in this point.

## Parametric representations {#Parametric_representations}

*Image: Cylinder - hyperboloid - cone.gif.* Animation of a hyperboloid of revolution

Cartesian coordinates for the hyperboloids can be defined, similar to [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates), keeping the [azimuth](wikipedia://en/47487) angle $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$, but changing inclination $v$ into [hyperbolic trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_trigonometric_function):

One-surface hyperboloid: $v \in (-\infty, \infty)$

$$\begin{aligned} x&=a \cosh v \cos\theta \\ y&=b \cosh v \sin\theta \\ z&=c \sinh v \end{aligned}$$

Two-surface hyperboloid: $v \in [0, \infty)$

$$\begin{aligned} x&=a \sinh v \cos\theta \\ y&=b \sinh v \sin\theta \\ z&=\pm c \cosh v \end{aligned}$$

*Image: Hyperboloid-1s.svg.* hyperboloid of one sheet: generation by a rotating hyperbola (top) and line (bottom: red or blue)

*Image: Hyperbo-1s-cut-all.svg.* hyperboloid of one sheet: plane sections

The following parametric representation includes hyperboloids of one sheet, two sheets, and their common boundary cone, each with the $z$-axis as the axis of symmetry:

$$\mathbf x(s,t) = \left( \begin{array}{lll} a \sqrt{s^2+d} \cos t\\ b \sqrt{s^2+d} \sin t\\ c s \end{array} \right)$$

- For $d>0$ one obtains a hyperboloid of one sheet,
- For $d<0$ a hyperboloid of two sheets, and
- For $d=0$ a double cone.

One can obtain a parametric representation of a hyperboloid with a different coordinate axis as the axis of symmetry by shuffling the position of the $c s$ term to the appropriate component in the equation above.

### Generalised equations {#Generalised_equations}

More generally, an arbitrarily oriented hyperboloid, centered at $\mathbf{v}$, is defined by the equation

$$(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v})^\mathrm{T} A (\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v}) = 1,$$

where $A$ is a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) and $\mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{v}$ are [vectors](wikipedia://en/32533).

The [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) of $A$ define the principal directions of the hyperboloid and the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of A are the [reciprocals](wikipedia://en/229940) of the squares of the semi-axes: ${1/a^2}$, ${1/b^2}$ and ${1/c^2}$. The one-sheet hyperboloid has two positive eigenvalues and one negative eigenvalue. The two-sheet hyperboloid has one positive eigenvalue and two negative eigenvalues.

## Properties {#Properties}

### Hyperboloid of one sheet {#Hyperboloid_of_one_sheet}

#### Lines on the surface {#Lines_on_the_surface}

- A hyperboloid of one sheet contains two pencils of lines. It is a [doubly ruled surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_ruled_surface).

If the hyperboloid has the equation ${x^2 \over a^2} + {y^2 \over b^2} - {z^2 \over c^2}= 1$ then the lines

$$g^{\pm}_{\alpha}: \mathbf{x}(t) = \begin{pmatrix} a\cos\alpha \\ b\sin\alpha \\ 0\end{pmatrix} + t\cdot \begin{pmatrix} -a\sin\alpha\\ b\cos\alpha\\ \pm c\end{pmatrix}\ ,\quad t\in \mathbb{R},\ 0\le \alpha\le 2\pi\$$

are contained in the surface.

In case $a = b$ the hyperboloid is a surface of revolution and can be generated by rotating one of the two lines $g^{+}_{0}$ or $g^{-}_{0}$, which are skew to the rotation axis (see picture). This property is called *[Wren](wikipedia://en/52946)'s theorem*.[^o9] The more common generation of a one-sheet hyperboloid of revolution is rotating a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) around its [semi-minor axis](wikipedia://en/23409699#Hyperbola) (see picture; rotating the hyperbola around its other axis gives a two-sheet hyperbola of revolution).

A hyperboloid of one sheet is *[projectively](wikipedia://en/243849)* equivalent to a [hyperbolic paraboloid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_paraboloid).

#### Plane sections {#Plane_sections}

For simplicity the plane sections of the *unit hyperboloid* with equation $\ H_1: x^2+y^2-z^2=1$ are considered. Because a hyperboloid in general position is an affine image of the unit hyperboloid, the result applies to the general case, too.

- A plane with a slope less than 1 (1 is the slope of the lines on the hyperboloid) intersects $H_1$ in an *ellipse*,
- A plane with a slope equal to 1 containing the origin intersects $H_1$ in a *pair of parallel lines*,
- A plane with a slope equal 1 not containing the origin intersects $H_1$ in a *parabola*,
- A tangential plane intersects $H_1$ in a *pair of intersecting lines*,
- A non-tangential plane with a slope greater than 1 intersects $H_1$ in a *hyperbola*.[^o11]

Obviously, any one-sheet hyperboloid of revolution contains circles. This is also true, but less obvious, in the general case (see [circular section](wikipedia://en/54435709)).

### Hyperboloid of two sheets {#Hyperboloid_of_two_sheets}

*Image: Hyperboloid-2s.svg.* hyperboloid of two sheets: generation by rotating a hyperbola

*Image: Hyperbo-2s-ca.svg.* hyperboloid of two sheets: plane sections

The hyperboloid of two sheets does *not* contain lines. The discussion of plane sections can be performed for the *unit hyperboloid of two sheets* with equation

$$H_2: \ x^2+y^2-z^2 = -1.$$

which can be generated by a rotating [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) around one of its axes (the one that cuts the hyperbola)

- A plane with slope less than 1 (1 is the slope of the asymptotes of the generating hyperbola) intersects $H_2$ either in an *ellipse* or in a *point* or not at all,
- A plane with slope equal to 1 containing the origin (midpoint of the hyperboloid) does *not intersect* $H_2$,
- A plane with slope equal to 1 not containing the origin intersects $H_2$ in a *parabola*,
- A plane with slope greater than 1 intersects $H_2$ in a *hyperbola*.[^o13]

Obviously, any two-sheet hyperboloid of revolution contains circles. This is also true, but less obvious, in the general case (see [circular section](wikipedia://en/54435709)).

*Remark*: A hyperboloid of two sheets is *projectively* equivalent to a sphere.

### Other properties {#Other_properties}

::: {#o16 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Symmetries"}

#### Symmetries {#Symmetries}

The hyperboloids with equations

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1 , \quad \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{z^2}{c^2} = -1$$

are

- *pointsymmetric* to the origin,
- *symmetric to the coordinate planes* and
- *rotational symmetric* to the z-axis and symmetric to any plane containing the z-axis, in case of $a=b$ (hyperboloid of revolution).

:::

#### Curvature {#Curvature}

Whereas the [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) of a hyperboloid of one sheet is negative, that of a two-sheet hyperboloid is positive. In spite of its positive curvature, the hyperboloid of two sheets with another suitably chosen metric can also be used as a [model](wikipedia://en/4771189) for hyperbolic geometry.

:::

## In more than three dimensions {#In_more_than_three_dimensions}

Hyperboloids are frequently found in mathematics of higher dimensions. For example, in a [pseudo-Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/10962546) one has the use of a [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478):

$$q(x) = \left(x_1^2+\cdots + x_k^2\right)-\left(x_{k+1}^2+\cdots + x_n^2\right), \quad k < n .$$

When $c$ is any [constant](wikipedia://en/24758132), then the part of the space given by

$$\lbrace x \ :\ q(x) = c \rbrace$$

is called a *hyperboloid*. The degenerate case corresponds to $c = 0$.

As an example, consider the following passage:[^o19]

> ... the velocity vectors always lie on a surface which Minkowski calls a four-dimensional hyperboloid since, expressed in terms of purely real coordinates $(y_{1}, ..., y_{4})$, its equation is $y^{2}_{1} + y^{2}_{2} + y^{2}_{3} - y^{2}_{4} = -1$, analogous to the hyperboloid $y^{2}_{1} + y^{2}_{2} - y^{2}_{3} = -1$ of three-dimensional space.

However, the term **quasi-sphere** is also used in this context since the sphere and hyperboloid have some commonality (See below).

## Hyperboloid structures {#Hyperboloid_structures}

One-sheeted hyperboloids are used in construction, with the structures called [hyperboloid structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperboloid_structure). A hyperboloid is a [doubly ruled surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_ruled_surface); thus, it can be built with straight steel beams, producing a strong structure at a lower cost than other methods. Examples include [cooling towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooling_tower), especially of [power stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_station), and [many other structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_hyperboloid_structures).

## Relation to the sphere {#Relation_to_the_sphere}

In 1853 [William Rowan Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817) published his *Lectures on Quaternions* which included presentation of [biquaternions](wikipedia://en/1207070). The following passage from page 673 shows how Hamilton uses biquaternion algebra and vectors from [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) to produce hyperboloids from the equation of a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859):

> ... the *equation of the unit sphere* $\rho^{2} + 1 = 0$, and change the vector $\rho$ to a *bivector form*, such as $\sigma + \tau \sqrt{-1}$. The equation of the sphere then breaks up into the system of the two following,
>
> and suggests our considering $\sigma$ and $\tau$ as two real and rectangular vectors, such that
>
> Hence it is easy to infer that if we assume $\sigma || \lambda$, where $\lambda$ is a vector in a given position, the *new real vector* $\sigma + \tau$ will terminate on the surface of a *double-sheeted and equilateral hyperboloid*; and that if, on the other hand, we assume $\tau || \lambda$, then the locus of the extremity of the real vector $\sigma + \tau$ will be an *equilateral but single-sheeted hyperboloid*. The study of these two hyperboloids is, therefore, in this way connected very simply, through biquaternions, with the study of the sphere; ...

In this passage $\mathbf{S}$ is the operator giving the scalar part of a quaternion, and $\mathbf{T}$ is the "tensor", now called [norm](wikipedia://en/990534), of a quaternion.
