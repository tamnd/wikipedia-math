# Face (geometry)

*Image: Vertex edge face (face).svg.* The face (red) of a cube (black)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Face"}

In [solid geometry](wikipedia://en/507960), a **face** is a flat [surface](wikipedia://en/50328015) (a [planar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) [region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Region_%28mathematics%29)) that forms part of the boundary of a solid object. For example, a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) has six faces in this sense.

:::

In more modern treatments of the geometry of [polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedra) and higher-dimensional [polytopes](wikipedia://en/23471), a "face" is defined in such a way that it may have any dimension. The [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417), [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034), and (2-dimensional) faces of a polyhedron are all faces in this more general sense.[^n1]

## Polygonal face {#Polygonal_face}

In elementary geometry, polyhedra are defined in various ways as shapes defined by systems of vertices (points), edges (line segments), and faces (polygons), that in many but not all of these definitions are required to form a surface that encloses a solid volume; the faces are the two-dimensional polygons of these definitions.[^n1][^o5] Other names for a polygonal face include **polyhedron side** and Euclidean plane *[tile](wikipedia://en/321671)*.

For example, any of the six [squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) that bound a cube is a face of the cube. Sometimes "face" is also used to refer to the 2-dimensional features of a [4-polytope](wikipedia://en/24979). With this meaning, the 4-dimensional [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112) has 24 square faces, each sharing two of 8 [cubic](wikipedia://en/6285) cells.

### Number of polygonal faces of a polyhedron {#Number_of_polygonal_faces_of_a_polyhedron}

Any [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron)'s surface has [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420)

$$V - E + F = 2,$$

where $V$ is the number of [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417), $E$ is the number of [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034), and $F$ is the number of faces. This equation is known as [Euler's polyhedron formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_polyhedron_formula). Thus the number of faces is 2 more than the excess of the number of edges over the number of vertices. For example, a cube has 12 edges and 8 vertices, and hence 6 faces.

## *k*-face {#k-face}

In higher-dimensional geometry, the faces of a [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) are features of all dimensions.[^n2][^n3] A face of dimension $k$ is sometimes called a $k$-face. For example, the polygonal faces of an ordinary polyhedron are 2-faces. The word "face" is defined differently in different areas of mathematics. For example, many but not all authors allow the polytope itself and the empty set as faces of a polytope, where the empty set is for consistency given a "dimension" of −1. For any $n$-dimensional polytope, faces have dimension $k$ with $-1 \leq k \leq n$.

For example, with this meaning, the faces of a cube comprise the cube itself (a 3-face), its (square) [facets](wikipedia://en/149926#Facet) (2-faces), its (line segment) edges (1-faces), its (point) vertices (0-faces), and the empty set.

In some areas of mathematics, such as [polyhedral combinatorics](wikipedia://en/19696519), a polytope is by definition [convex](wikipedia://en/6292). In this setting, there is a precise definition: a face of a polytope $P$ in Euclidean space $\mathbf{R}^n$ is the intersection of $P$ with any [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) [halfspace](wikipedia://en/647297) whose boundary is disjoint from the relative interior of $P$.[^o12] According to this definition, the set of faces of a polytope includes the polytope itself and the empty set.[^n2][^n3] For convex polytopes, this definition is equivalent to the general definition of a face of a convex set, given [below](wikipedia://en/149926#Face_of_a_convex_set).

In other areas of mathematics, such as the theories of [abstract polytopes](wikipedia://en/1211056) and [star polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_polytope), the requirement of convexity is relaxed. One precise combinatorial concept that generalizes some earlier types of polyhedra is the notion of a [simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/252686). More generally, there is the notion of a [polytopal complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytopal_complex).

An $n$-dimensional [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) (line segment ($n = 1$), triangle ($n = 2$), tetrahedron ($n = 3$), etc.), defined by $n + 1$ vertices, has a face for each subset of the vertices, from the empty set up through the set of all vertices. In particular, there are $2^{n + 1}$ faces in total. The number of $k$-faces, for $k \in \{-1, 0, ..., n\}$, is the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668) $\binom{n+1}{k+1}$.

There are specific names for $k$-faces depending on the value of $k$ and, in some cases, how close $k$ is to the dimension $n$ of the polytope.

### Vertex or 0-face {#Vertex_or_0-face}

**Vertex** is the common name for a 0-face.

### Edge or 1-face {#Edge_or_1-face}

**Edge** is the common name for a 1-face.

### Face or 2-face {#Face_or_2-face}

The use of **face** in a context where a specific $k$ is meant for a $k$-face but is not explicitly specified is commonly a 2-face.

### Cell or 3-face {#Cell_or_3-face}

A **cell** is a [polyhedral](wikipedia://en/23470) element (**3-face**) of a 4-dimensional polytope or 3-dimensional tessellation, or higher. Cells are [facets](wikipedia://en/7186647) for 4-polytopes and 3-honeycombs.

### Facet or (*n* − 1)-face {#Facet_or_(n_−_1)-face}

In higher-dimensional geometry, the **facets** of a $n$-polytope are the ($n - 1$)-faces (faces of dimension one less than the polytope itself).[^o22] A polytope is bounded by its facets.

For example:

- The facets of a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) are its 0-faces or [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417).
- The facets of a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) are its 1-faces or [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034).
- The facets of a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) or plane [tiling](wikipedia://en/12126635) are its [2-faces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-face). However, in some contexts, a [facet of a polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facet_of_a_polyhedron) means any polygon formed from a subset of three or more vertices of a 2-face.
- The facets of a [4D polytope](wikipedia://en/24979) or [3-honeycomb](wikipedia://en/2961) are its [3-faces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-face) or cells.
- The facets of a [5D polytope](wikipedia://en/6821670) or 4-honeycomb are its [4-faces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-face).

### Ridge or (*n* − 2)-face {#Ridge_or_(n_−_2)-face}

In related terminology, the ($n - 2$)-*face*s of an $n$-polytope are called **ridges** (also **subfacets**).[^o24] A ridge is seen as the boundary between exactly two facets of a polytope or honeycomb.

For example:

- The ridges of a 2D [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) or 1D tiling are its 0-faces or [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417).
- The ridges of a 3D [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) or plane [tiling](wikipedia://en/12126635) are its 1-faces or [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034).
- The ridges of a [4D polytope](wikipedia://en/24979) or [3-honeycomb](wikipedia://en/2961) are its 2-faces.
- The ridges of a [5D polytope](wikipedia://en/6821670) or 4-honeycomb are its 3-faces or [cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_%28geometry%29).

### Peak or (*n* − 3)-face {#Peak_or_(n_−_3)-face}

The ($n - 3$)-*face*s of an $n$-polytope are called **peaks**. A peak contains a rotational axis of facets and ridges in a regular polytope or honeycomb.

For example:

- The peaks of a 3D [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) or plane [tiling](wikipedia://en/12126635) are its 0-faces or [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417).
- The peaks of a [4D polytope](wikipedia://en/24979) or [3-honeycomb](wikipedia://en/2961) are its 1-faces or [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034).
- The peaks of a [5D polytope](wikipedia://en/6821670) or 4-honeycomb are its 2-faces.

## Face of a convex set {#Face_of_a_convex_set}

*Image: Extremenotexposed.png.* The two distinguished points are examples of extreme points of a convex set that are not exposed points. Therefore, not every face of a convex set is an exposed face.

The notion of a face can be generalized from convex polytopes to all [convex sets](wikipedia://en/6292), as follows. Let $C$ be a convex set in a real [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $V$. A face or [extreme set](wikipedia://en/78645068) of $C$ is a convex subset $F\subseteq C$ such that whenever a point $p\in F$ lies strictly between two points $x$ and $y$ in $C$, both $x$ and $y$ must be in $F$. Equivalently, for any $x,y\in C$ and any real number $0<\theta<1$ such that $\theta x+(1-\theta)y$ is in $F$, $x$ and $y$ must be in $F$.[^n4]
