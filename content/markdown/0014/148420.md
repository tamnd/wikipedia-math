# Euler characteristic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Euler characteristic"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and more specifically in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) and [polyhedral combinatorics](wikipedia://en/19696519), the **Euler characteristic** (or **Euler number**, or **Euler–Poincaré characteristic**) is a [topological invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_invariant), a number that describes a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450)'s shape or structure regardless of the way it is bent. It is commonly denoted by $\chi$ ([Greek lower-case letter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet) [chi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi_%28letter%29)).

:::

The Euler characteristic was originally defined for [polyhedra](wikipedia://en/23470) and used to prove various theorems about them, including the classification of the [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905). It was stated for Platonic solids in 1537 in an unpublished manuscript by [Francesco Maurolico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Maurolico).[^o2] [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902), for whom the concept is named, introduced it for convex polyhedra more generally but failed to rigorously prove that it is an invariant. In modern mathematics, the Euler characteristic arises from [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) and, more abstractly, [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410).

## Polyhedra {#Polyhedra}

*Image: Vertex edge face.svg.* Vertex, edge and face of a cube

The Euler characteristic was classically defined for the surface of a three-dimensional polyhedron. If the polyhedron has $V$ [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) (corners), $E$ [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034), and $F$ [faces](wikipedia://en/149926), then the **Euler characteristic** $\chi$ of its surface is

$$\chi = V - E + F.$$

Any three-dimensional [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron)'s surface has an Euler characteristic of $\chi = 2$. This equation, stated by [Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) in 1758,[^o4] is known as **Euler's polyhedron formula**.[^n1] It corresponds to the Euler characteristic of the [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) (i.e. $\ \chi = 2\$), and applies identically to [spherical polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_polyhedra). An illustration of the formula for all Platonic solids is given below.

For regular polyhedra, [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034) derived a modified form of Euler's formula using the [density](wikipedia://en/34257549) $D$, [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719) density $\ d_v\ ,$ and face density $\ d_f\ :$

$$\ d_v V - E + d_f F = 2 D ~.$$

This version holds both for convex polyhedra (where the densities are all 1) and the non-convex [Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra](wikipedia://en/16814).

[Projective polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_polyhedra) all have Euler characteristic 1, like the [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325), while the surfaces of [toroidal polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toroidal_polyhedra) all have Euler characteristic 0, like the [torus](wikipedia://en/74800).

### Plane graphs {#Plane_graphs}

The Euler characteristic can be defined for [connected](wikipedia://en/1614492) [plane graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_graph) by the same $\ V - E + F\$ formula as for polyhedral surfaces, where $F$ is the number of faces in the graph, including the exterior face.

The Euler characteristic of any plane connected graph $G$ is 2. This is easily proved by induction on the number of faces determined by $G$, starting with a tree as the base case. For [trees](wikipedia://en/48560), $\ E = V - 1\$ and $\ F = 1 ~.$ If $G$ has $C$ components (disconnected graphs), the same argument by induction on $F$ shows that $\ V - E + F - C = 1 ~.$ One of the few graph theory papers of Cauchy also proves this result.

Via [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431) the plane maps to the 2-sphere, such that a connected graph maps to a polygonal decomposition of the sphere, which has Euler characteristic 2. This viewpoint is implicit in Cauchy's proof of Euler's formula given below.

::: {#o20 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of Euler's formula"}

### Proof of Euler's formula {#Proof_of_Euler's_formula}

*Image: V-E+F=2 Proof Illustration.svg.* First steps of the proof in the case of a cube

There are many proofs of Euler's formula. One was given by [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustin_Louis_Cauchy) in 1811, as follows. It applies to any convex polyhedron, and more generally to any polyhedron whose boundary is topologically equivalent to a sphere and whose faces are topologically equivalent to disks.

Remove one face of the polyhedral surface. By pulling the edges of the missing face away from each other, deform all the rest into a planar graph of points and curves, in such a way that the perimeter of the missing face is placed externally, surrounding the graph obtained, as illustrated by the first of the three graphs for the special case of the cube. (The assumption that the polyhedral surface is homeomorphic to the sphere at the beginning is what makes this possible.) After this deformation, the regular faces are generally not regular anymore. The number of vertices and edges has remained the same, but the number of faces has been reduced by 1. Therefore, proving Euler's formula for the polyhedron reduces to proving $\ V - E + F = 1\$ for this deformed, planar object.

If there is a face with more than three sides, draw a diagonal—that is, a curve through the face connecting two vertices that are not yet connected. Each new diagonal adds one edge and one face and does not change the number of vertices, so it does not change the quantity $\ V - E + F ~.$ (The assumption that all faces are disks is needed here, to show via the [Jordan curve theorem](wikipedia://en/267444) that this operation increases the number of faces by one.) Continue adding edges in this manner until all of the faces are triangular.

Apply repeatedly either of the following two transformations, maintaining the invariant that the exterior boundary is always a [simple cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_cycle):

1. Remove a triangle with only one edge adjacent to the exterior, as illustrated by the second graph. This decreases the number of edges and faces by one each and does not change the number of vertices, so it preserves $\ V - E + F ~.$
1. Remove a triangle with two edges shared by the exterior of the network, as illustrated by the third graph. Each triangle removal removes a vertex, two edges and one face, so it preserves $\ V - E + F ~.$

These transformations eventually reduce the planar graph to a single triangle. (Without the simple-cycle invariant, removing a triangle might disconnect the remaining triangles, invalidating the rest of the argument. A valid removal order is an elementary example of a [shelling](wikipedia://en/32457161).)

At this point the lone triangle has $\ V = 3\ ,$$\ E = 3\ ,$ and $\ F = 1\ ,$ so that $\ V - E + F = 1 ~.$ Since each of the two above transformation steps preserved this quantity, we have shown $\ V - E + F = 1\$ for the deformed, planar object thus demonstrating $\ V - E + F = 2\$ for the polyhedron. This proves the theorem.

For additional proofs, see [Eppstein](wikipedia://en/4015871) (2013).[^o21] Multiple proofs, including their flaws and limitations, are used as examples in *[Proofs and Refutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proofs_and_Refutations)* by [Lakatos](wikipedia://en/38455) (1976).[^o22]

:::

## Topological definition {#Topological_definition}

The polyhedral surfaces discussed above are, in modern language, two-dimensional finite [CW-complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CW-complex). (When only triangular faces are used, they are two-dimensional finite [simplicial complexes](wikipedia://en/252686).) In general, for any finite CW-complex, the **Euler characteristic** can be defined as the alternating sum

$$\chi = k_0 - k_1 + k_2 - k_3 + \cdots,$$

where *k*<sub>*n*</sub> denotes the number of cells of dimension *n* in the complex.

Similarly, for a [simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/252686), the **Euler characteristic** equals the alternating sum

$$\chi = k_0 - k_1 + k_2 - k_3 + \cdots,$$

where *k*<sub>*n*</sub> denotes the number of [*n*-simplexes](wikipedia://en/39781) in the complex.

### Betti number alternative {#Betti_number_alternative}

More generally still, for any [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), we can define the *n*th [Betti number](wikipedia://en/346262) *b*<sub>*n*</sub> as the [rank](wikipedia://en/244541) of the *n*-th [singular homology](wikipedia://en/431041) group. The **Euler characteristic** can then be defined as the alternating sum

$$\chi = b_0 - b_1 + b_2 - b_3 + \cdots.$$

This quantity is well-defined if the Betti numbers are all finite and if they are zero beyond a certain index *n*<sub>0</sub>. For simplicial complexes, this is not the same definition as in the previous paragraph but a homology computation shows that the two definitions will give the same value for $\chi$.

### Differentiable point of view {#Differentiable_point_of_view}

For an oriented, compact smooth manifold without boundary, $X$, one defines

$$\chi(X)= I(\Delta, \Delta)$$

The number of self oriented intersections of the diagonal of $X$, $\Delta$, inside $X\times X.$

::: {#o27 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The Euler characteristic behaves well with respect to many basic operations on topological spaces, as follows.

### Homotopy invariance {#Homotopy_invariance}

Homology is a topological invariant, and moreover a [homotopy invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_invariant): Two topological spaces that are [homotopy equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_equivalent) have [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) homology groups. It follows that the Euler characteristic is also a homotopy invariant.

For example, any [contractible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contractible) space (that is, one homotopy equivalent to a point) has trivial homology, meaning that the 0th Betti number is 1 and the others 0. Therefore, its Euler characteristic is 1. This case includes [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$ of any dimension, as well as the solid unit ball in any Euclidean space — the one-dimensional interval, the two-dimensional disk, the three-dimensional ball, etc.

For another example, any convex polyhedron is homeomorphic to the three-dimensional [ball](wikipedia://en/160556), so its surface is homeomorphic (hence homotopy equivalent) to the two-dimensional [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859), which has Euler characteristic 2. This explains why the surface of a convex polyhedron has Euler characteristic 2.

::: {#o30 .statement cue=named-heading name="Inclusion–exclusion principle"}

### Inclusion–exclusion principle {#Inclusion–exclusion_principle}

If *M* and *N* are any two topological spaces, then the Euler characteristic of their [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) is the sum of their Euler characteristics, since homology is additive under disjoint union:

$$\chi(M \sqcup N) = \chi(M) + \chi(N).$$

More generally, if *M* and *N* are subspaces of a larger space *X*, then so are their union and intersection. In some cases, the Euler characteristic obeys a version of the [inclusion–exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/342684):

$$\chi(M \cup N) = \chi(M) + \chi(N) - \chi(M \cap N).$$

This is true in the following cases:

- if *M* and *N* are an [excisive couple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excisive_couple). In particular, if the [interiors](wikipedia://en/55610) of *M* and *N* inside the union still cover the union.[^o31]
- if *X* is a [locally compact space](wikipedia://en/48632), and one uses Euler characteristics with [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [supports](wikipedia://en/381013), no assumptions on *M* or *N* are needed.
- if *X* is a [stratified space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologically_stratified_space) all of whose strata are even-dimensional, the inclusion–exclusion principle holds if *M* and *N* are unions of strata. This applies in particular if *M* and *N* are subvarieties of a [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808).[^o32]

In general, the inclusion–exclusion principle is false. A [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) is given by taking *X* to be the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line), *M* a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) consisting of one point and *N* the [complement](wikipedia://en/54347) of *M*.

:::

### Quotient space {#Quotient_space}

If *X* is a finite CW-complex and *A* is a subcomplex, then

$$\chi(X) = \chi(A) + \chi(X/A) -1.$$

The formula could be more succinctly written for the reduced Euler characteristic—the alternating sum of ranks of reduced homology groups.

### Connected sum {#Connected_sum}

For two connected closed *n*-manifolds $M, N$ one can obtain a new connected manifold $M \mathbin{\#} N$ via the [connected sum](wikipedia://en/490134) operation. The Euler characteristic is related by the formula[^o35]

$$\chi(M \mathbin{\#} N) = \chi(M) + \chi(N) - \chi(S^n).$$

### Product property {#Product_property}

Also, the Euler characteristic of any [product space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_space) *M* × *N* is

$$\chi(M \times N) = \chi(M) \cdot \chi(N).$$

Using this and the formula for quotient spaces, one obtains

$$\hat\chi(M \wedge N) = \hat\chi(M) \cdot \hat\chi(N),$$

where $\hat\chi$ is the reduced Euler characteristic, and $M\wedge N$ is the [smash product](wikipedia://en/698010).

These addition and multiplication properties are also enjoyed by [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of [sets](wikipedia://en/26691). In this way, the Euler characteristic can be viewed as a generalisation of cardinality; see <http://math.ucr.edu/home/baez/counting/>.

### Covering spaces {#Covering_spaces}

Similarly, for a *k*-sheeted [covering space](wikipedia://en/250323) $\tilde{M} \to M,$ one has

$$\chi(\tilde{M}) = k \cdot \chi(M).$$

More generally, for a [ramified covering space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramified_covering_space), the Euler characteristic of the cover can be computed from the above, with a correction factor for the ramification points, which yields the [Riemann–Hurwitz formula](wikipedia://en/497413).

### Fibration property {#Fibration_property}

The product property holds much more generally, for [fibrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibrations) with certain conditions.

If $p\colon E \to B$ is a fibration with fiber *F,* with the base *B* [path-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path-connected), and the fibration is orientable over a field *K,* then the Euler characteristic with coefficients in the field *K* satisfies the product property:[^o39]

$$\chi(E) = \chi(F)\cdot \chi(B).$$

This includes product spaces and covering spaces as special cases, and can be proven by the [Serre spectral sequence](wikipedia://en/3085825) on homology of a fibration.

For fiber bundles, this can also be understood in terms of a [transfer map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfer_map) $\tau\colon H_*(B) \to H_*(E)$ – note that this is a lifting and goes "the wrong way" – whose composition with the projection map $p_*\colon H_*(E) \to H_*(B)$ is multiplication by the [Euler class](wikipedia://en/2932393) of the fiber:[^o40]

$$p_* \circ \tau = \chi(F) \cdot 1.$$

### Football {#Football}

It is common to construct [footballs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_%28association_football%29) by stitching together regular pentagons and hexagons, with three faces meeting at each vertex. If $P$ pentagons and $H$ hexagons are used, then there are $F = P + H\$ faces, $V = \tfrac{1}{3}\left( 5 P + 6 H \right)$ vertices, and $E = \tfrac{1}{2} \left(5P + 6H \right)$ edges. The Euler characteristic is thus

$$V - E + F = \tfrac{1}{3} \left( 5 P + 6 H \right) - \tfrac{1}{2} \left( 5 P + 6 H \right) + P + H = \tfrac{1}{6} P.$$

Because the sphere has Euler characteristic 2, it follows that $P = 12$. That is, a football constructed in this way always has 12 pentagons. The number of hexagons can be any [nonnegative integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonnegative_integer) except 1.[^o56] This result is applicable to [fullerenes](wikipedia://en/10628) and [Goldberg polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldberg_polyhedra).

Specifically, footballs are commonly made using 12 pentagonal and 20 hexagonal panels. The result is a truncated icosahedron spherical geometry. An example is the [Adidas Telstar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adidas_Telstar).

The $n$-dimensional sphere has singular [homology groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_group) equal to

$$H_k(\mathrm{S}^n) = \begin{cases} \mathbb{Z}, & \text{if } k = 0 \text{ or } k = n, \\ \{0\} & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}$$

hence has Betti number 1 in dimensions 0 and $n$, and all other Betti numbers are 0. Its Euler characteristic is then $\chi = 1 + (-1)$${}^{n}$ ; that is, either 0 if $n$ is [odd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_number), or 2 if $n$ is [even](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_number).

The $n$-dimensional real [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) is the quotient of the $n$-sphere by the [antipodal map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipodal_map). It follows that its Euler characteristic is exactly half that of the corresponding sphere – either 0 or 1.

The $n$-dimensional torus is the product space of $n$ circles. Its Euler characteristic is 0 by the product property. More generally, any compact [parallelizable manifold](wikipedia://en/3108161), including any compact [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945), has Euler characteristic 0.[^o65]

The Euler characteristic of any [closed](wikipedia://en/669475) odd-dimensional manifold is also 0.[^o66] The case for [orientable](wikipedia://en/187446) examples is a corollary of [Poincaré duality](wikipedia://en/540732). This property applies more generally to any [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [stratified space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologically_stratified_space) all of whose strata have odd dimension. It also applies to closed odd-dimensional non-orientable manifolds, via the two-to-one [orientable double cover](wikipedia://en/187446#Orientable_double_cover).

:::

## Relations to other invariants {#Relations_to_other_invariants}

The Euler characteristic of a closed [orientable](wikipedia://en/187446) [surface](wikipedia://en/27865) can be calculated from its [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) $g$ (the number of [tori](wikipedia://en/74800) in a [connected sum](wikipedia://en/490134) decomposition of the surface; intuitively, the number of "handles") as

$$\chi = 2 - 2g ~.$$

The Euler characteristic of a closed non-orientable surface can be calculated from its non-orientable genus $k$ (the number of [real projective planes](wikipedia://en/411325) in a connected sum decomposition of the surface) as

$$\chi = 2 - k ~.$$

For closed smooth manifolds, the Euler characteristic coincides with the **Euler number**, i.e., the [Euler class](wikipedia://en/2932393) of its [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) evaluated on the [fundamental class](wikipedia://en/669440) of a manifold. The Euler class, in turn, relates to all other [characteristic classes](wikipedia://en/363530) of [vector bundles](wikipedia://en/276524).

For closed [Riemannian manifolds](wikipedia://en/144652), the Euler characteristic can also be found by integrating the curvature; see the [Gauss–Bonnet theorem](wikipedia://en/139229) for the two-dimensional case and the [generalized Gauss–Bonnet theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Gauss–Bonnet_theorem) for the general case.

A discrete analog of the Gauss–Bonnet theorem is [Descartes'](wikipedia://en/25525) theorem that the "total [defect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defect_%28geometry%29)" of a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470), measured in full circles, is the Euler characteristic of the polyhedron.

[Hadwiger's theorem](wikipedia://en/629554) characterizes the Euler characteristic as the *unique* ([up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373)) translation-invariant, finitely additive, not-necessarily-nonnegative [set function](wikipedia://en/25911217) defined on [finite unions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_unions) of [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron) sets in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ that is "homogeneous of degree 0".

::: {#o69 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

For every combinatorial [cell complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_complex), one defines the Euler characteristic as the number of 0-cells, minus the number of 1-cells, plus the number of 2-cells, etc., if this alternating sum is finite. In particular, the Euler characteristic of a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) is simply its cardinality, and the Euler characteristic of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) is the number of vertices minus the number of edges.

More generally, one can define the Euler characteristic of any [chain complex](wikipedia://en/138404) to be the alternating sum of the [ranks](wikipedia://en/244541) of the homology groups of the chain complex, assuming that all these ranks are finite and only finitely many are non-zero.

A version of Euler characteristic used in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) is as follows. For any [coherent sheaf](wikipedia://en/603780) $\mathcal{F}$ on a [proper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_scheme) [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) $X$, one defines its Euler characteristic to be

$$\chi ( \mathcal{F})= \sum_i (-1)^i h^i(X,\mathcal{F})\ ,$$

where $\ h^i(X, \mathcal{F})\$ is the dimension of the $i$-th [sheaf cohomology](wikipedia://en/1055357) group of $\mathcal{F}$. In this case, the dimensions are all finite by [Grothendieck's finiteness theorem](wikipedia://en/23535337#Finite-dimensionality_of_cohomology). This is an instance of the Euler characteristic of a chain complex, where the chain complex is a finite resolution of $\ \mathcal{F}\$ by [acyclic sheaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acyclic_sheaf).

Another generalization of the concept of Euler characteristic on manifolds comes from [orbifolds](wikipedia://en/293504) (see [Euler characteristic of an orbifold](wikipedia://en/54504773)). While every manifold has an integer Euler characteristic, an orbifold can have a fractional Euler characteristic. For example, the teardrop orbifold has Euler characteristic 1 + $\tfrac{1}{p}$ , where $p$ is a prime number corresponding to the cone angle $\tfrac{2\pi}{p}$.

The concept of Euler characteristic of the [reduced homology](wikipedia://en/3110036) of a bounded finite [poset](wikipedia://en/23572) is another generalization, important in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170). A poset is "bounded" if it has smallest and largest elements; call them 0 and 1. The Euler characteristic of such a poset is defined as the integer $\mu(0,1)$, where $\mu$ is the [Möbius function](wikipedia://en/166980) in that poset's [incidence algebra](wikipedia://en/166980).

This can be further generalized by defining a [rational valued](wikipedia://en/19727024) Euler characteristic for certain finite [categories](wikipedia://en/53759), a notion compatible with the Euler characteristics of graphs, orbifolds and posets mentioned above. In this setting, the Euler characteristic of a finite [group](wikipedia://en/19447) or [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) $G$ is $\tfrac{1}{\lvert G\rvert}$, and the Euler characteristic of a finite [groupoid](wikipedia://en/12543) is the sum of $\tfrac{1}{\lvert G_{i}\rvert}$, where we picked one representative group $G$${}_{i}$ for each connected component of the groupoid.[^o71]
