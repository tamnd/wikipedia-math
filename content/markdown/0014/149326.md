# Phylogenetic tree

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Phylogenetic tree"}

A **phylogenetic tree** or **phylogeny** is a graphical representation which shows the [evolutionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution) history between a set of [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species) or [taxa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxon) during a specific time.[^o2][^o3] In other words, it is a branching [diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagram) or a [tree](wikipedia://en/48560) showing the evolutionary relationships among various biological species or other entities based upon similarities and differences in their physical or genetic characteristics. In evolutionary biology, all life on Earth is theoretically part of a single phylogenetic tree, indicating [common ancestry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_ancestry). [Phylogenetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phylogenetics) is the study of phylogenetic trees. The main challenge is to find a phylogenetic tree representing optimal evolutionary ancestry between a set of species or taxa. [Computational phylogenetics (also phylogeny inference)](wikipedia://en/3986130) focuses on the algorithms involved in finding optimal phylogenetic tree in the phylogenetic landscape.[^o2][^o3]

:::

Phylogenetic trees may be rooted or unrooted. In a *rooted* phylogenetic tree, each node with descendants represents the inferred [most recent common ancestor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Most_recent_common_ancestor) of those descendants,[^o4] and the edge lengths in some trees may be interpreted as time estimates. Each node is called a taxonomic unit. Internal nodes are generally called hypothetical taxonomic units, as they cannot be directly observed. Trees are useful in fields of biology such as [bioinformatics](wikipedia://en/4214), [systematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematics), and [phylogenetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phylogenetics). *Unrooted* trees illustrate only the relatedness of the [leaf nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf_nodes) and do not require the ancestral root to be known or inferred.

## History {#History}

The idea of a [tree of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_of_life_%28biology%29) arose from ancient notions of a ladder-like progression from lower into higher forms of [life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life) (such as in the [Great Chain of Being](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Chain_of_Being)). Early representations of "branching" phylogenetic trees include a "paleontological chart" showing the geological relationships among plants and animals in the book *Elementary Geology*, by [Edward Hitchcock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Hitchcock) (first edition: 1840).

[Charles Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin) featured a diagrammatic [evolutionary "tree"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_of_life_%28biology%29) in his 1859 book *[On the Origin of Species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Origin_of_Species)*. Over a century later, [evolutionary biologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_biology) still use [tree diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_structure) to depict [evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution) because such diagrams effectively convey the concept that [speciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speciation) occurs through the [adaptive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptation) and [semirandom](wikipedia://en/19196523) splitting of lineages.

The term *phylogenetic*, or *phylogeny*, derives from the two [ancient greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_greek) words grc (φῦλον), meaning "race, lineage", and grc (γένεσις), meaning "origin, source".[^o6][^o7]

### Rooted tree {#Rooted_tree}

*Image: Phylogenetic treePureThickBraille.jpg.* Rooted phylogenetic tree optimized for blind people. The lowest point of the tree is the root, which symbolizes the universal common ancestor to all living beings. The tree branches out into three main groups: Bacteria (left branch, letters a to i), Archea (middle branch, letters j to p) and Eukaryota (right branch, letters q to z). Each letter corresponds to a group of organisms, listed below this description. These letters and the description should be converted to Braille font, and printed using a Braille printer. The figure can be 3D printed by copying the png file and using Cura or other software to generate the Gcode for 3D printing.

A rooted phylogenetic [tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_%28data_structure%29) (see two graphics at top) is a [directed](wikipedia://en/19721986) tree with a unique node — the root — corresponding to the (usually [imputed](wikipedia://en/1309220)) most recent common ancestor of all the entities at the [leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf_node) of the tree. The root node does not have a parent node, but serves as the parent of all other nodes in the tree. The root is therefore a node of [degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Node_%28computer_science%29#Nodes_and_trees) 2, while other internal nodes have a minimum degree of 3 (where "degree" here refers to the total number of incoming and outgoing edges).

The most common method for rooting trees is the use of an uncontroversial [outgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outgroup_%28cladistics%29)—close enough to allow inference from trait data or molecular sequencing, but far enough to be a clear outgroup. Another method is midpoint rooting, or a tree can also be rooted by using a non-stationary [substitution model](wikipedia://en/1937800).[^o12]

:::

::: {#o14 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Unrooted tree"}

### Unrooted tree {#Unrooted_tree}

*Image: MyosinUnrootedTree.jpg.* An unrooted phylogenetic tree for [myosin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myosin), a [superfamily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_family) of [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein)[^o15]

Unrooted trees illustrate the relatedness of the leaf nodes without making assumptions about ancestry. They do not require the ancestral root to be known or inferred.[^o16] Rooted trees can be generated from unrooted ones by inserting a root. Inferring the root of an unrooted tree requires some means of identifying ancestry. This is normally done by including an outgroup in the input data so that the root is necessarily between the outgroup and the rest of the taxa in the tree, or by introducing additional assumptions about the relative rates of evolution on each branch, such as an application of the [molecular clock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_clock) [hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis).[^o17]

:::

### Bifurcating versus multifurcating {#Bifurcating_versus_multifurcating}

Both rooted and unrooted trees can be either bifurcating or multifurcating. A rooted bifurcating tree has exactly two descendants arising from each [interior node](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interior_node) (that is, it forms a [binary tree](wikipedia://en/4321)), and an unrooted bifurcating tree takes the form of an [unrooted binary tree](wikipedia://en/27950476), a [free tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_tree) with exactly three neighbors at each internal node. In contrast, a rooted multifurcating tree may have more than two children at some nodes and an unrooted multifurcating tree may have more than three neighbors at some nodes.

### Labeled versus unlabeled {#Labeled_versus_unlabeled}

Both rooted and unrooted trees can be either labeled or unlabeled. A labeled tree has specific values assigned to its leaves, while an unlabeled tree, sometimes called a tree shape, defines a topology only. Some sequence-based trees built from a small genomic locus, such as Phylotree,[^o20] feature internal nodes labeled with inferred ancestral haplotypes.

### Enumerating trees {#Enumerating_trees}

*Image: Number of trees as a function of the number of leaves.svg.* Increase in the total number of phylogenetic trees as a function of the number of labeled leaves: unrooted binary trees (blue diamonds), rooted binary trees (red circles), and rooted multifurcating or binary trees (green: triangles). The Y-axis scale is [logarithmic](wikipedia://en/164055).

The number of possible trees for a given number of leaf nodes depends on the specific type of tree, but there are always more labeled than unlabeled trees, more multifurcating than bifurcating trees, and more rooted than unrooted trees. The last distinction is the most biologically relevant; it arises because there are many places on an unrooted tree to put the root. For bifurcating labeled trees, the total number of rooted trees is:

$(2n-3)!! = \frac{(2n-3)!}{2^{n-2}(n-2)!}$ for $n \ge 2$, $n$ represents the number of leaf nodes.[^o22]

For bifurcating labeled trees, the total number of unrooted trees is:[^o22]

$(2n-5)!! = \frac{(2n-5)!}{2^{n-3}(n-3)!}$ for $n \ge 3$.

Among labeled bifurcating trees, the number of unrooted trees with $n$ leaves is equal to the number of rooted trees with $n-1$ leaves.[^o3]

The number of rooted trees grows quickly as a function of the number of tips. For 10 tips, there are more than $34 \times 10^6$ possible bifurcating trees, and the number of multifurcating trees rises faster, with ca. 7 times as many of the latter as of the former.

## Special tree types {#Special_tree_types}

### Dendrogram {#Dendrogram}

A [dendrogram](wikipedia://en/1409693) is a general name for a tree, whether phylogenetic or not, and hence also for the diagrammatic representation of a phylogenetic tree.[^o26] In biological classification, hierarchical diagrams representing taxonomic ranks (sometimes referred to as **taxonomy trees**) are dendrograms that illustrate nested groupings rather than explicit evolutionary descent.

*Image: Phylogenetic tree of dogs.png.* Dendrogram of the phylogeny of some dog breeds

### Cladogram {#Cladogram}

A [cladogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cladogram) only represents a branching pattern; i.e., its branch lengths do not represent time or relative amount of character change, and its internal nodes do not represent ancestors.[^o28]

::: {#o30 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Phylogram"}

### Phylogram {#Phylogram}

A phylogram is a phylogenetic tree that has branch lengths proportional to the amount of character change.[^o31]

:::

::: {#o33 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Chronogram (genetics)"}

### Chronogram {#Chronogram}

A chronogram is a phylogenetic tree that explicitly represents time through its branch lengths.[^o34]

*Image: Phylogenetic chart of Lepidoptera chronogram.svg.* A chronogram of [Lepidoptera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepidoptera).[^o35] In this phylogenetic tree type, branch lengths are proportional to geological time.

:::

### Dahlgrenogram {#Dahlgrenogram}

A [Dahlgrenogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolf_Dahlgren) is a diagram representing a cross section of a phylogenetic tree.

### Phylogenetic network {#Phylogenetic_network}

A [phylogenetic network](wikipedia://en/3657198) is not strictly speaking a tree, but rather a more general [graph](wikipedia://en/325806), or a [directed acyclic graph](wikipedia://en/204002) in the case of rooted networks. They are used to overcome some of the limitations inherent to trees.

### Spindle diagram {#Spindle_diagram}

*Image: Spindle diagram.jpg.* A spindle diagram, showing the evolution of the [vertebrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertebrate) at class level, width of spindles indicating number of families. Spindle diagrams are often used in [evolutionary taxonomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_taxonomy).

A spindle diagram, or bubble diagram, is often called a romerogram, after its popularisation by the American palaeontologist [Alfred Romer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Romer).[^o39] It represents taxonomic diversity (horizontal width) against [geological time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologic_time_scale) (vertical axis) in order to reflect the variation of abundance of various taxa through time. A spindle diagram is not an evolutionary tree:[^o40] the taxonomic spindles obscure the actual relationships of the parent taxon to the daughter taxon[^o39] and have the disadvantage of involving the [paraphyly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraphyly) of the parental group.[^o41] This type of diagram is no longer used in the form originally proposed.[^o41]

### Coral of life {#Coral_of_life}

*Image: The Coral Of Life Prototype.svg.* The Coral of Life

Darwin[^o43] also mentioned that the *coral* may be a more suitable metaphor than the *tree*. Indeed, [phylogenetic corals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral_of_life) are useful for portraying past and present life, and they have some advantages over trees ([anastomoses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anastomosis) allowed, etc.).[^o41]

## Construction {#Construction}

Phylogenetic trees composed with a nontrivial number of input sequences are constructed using [computational phylogenetics](wikipedia://en/3986130) methods. Distance-matrix methods such as [neighbor-joining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighbor-joining) or [UPGMA](wikipedia://en/355968), which calculate [genetic distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_distance) from [multiple sequence alignments](wikipedia://en/4066308), are simplest to implement, but do not invoke an evolutionary model. Many sequence alignment methods such as [ClustalW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ClustalW) also create trees by using the simpler algorithms (i.e. those based on distance) of tree construction. [Maximum parsimony](wikipedia://en/1130020) is another simple method of estimating phylogenetic trees, but implies an implicit model of evolution (i.e. parsimony). More advanced methods use the [optimality criterion](wikipedia://en/15015787) of [maximum likelihood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_likelihood), often within a [Bayesian framework](wikipedia://en/49571), and apply an explicit model of evolution to phylogenetic tree estimation.[^o3] Identifying the optimal tree using many of these techniques is [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard),[^o3] so [heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic) search and [optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimization_%28mathematics%29) methods are used in combination with tree-scoring functions to identify a reasonably good tree that fits the data.

Tree-building methods can be assessed on the basis of several criteria:[^o46]

- efficiency (how long does it take to compute the answer, how much memory does it need?)
- power (does it make good use of the data, or is information being wasted?)
- consistency (will it converge on the same answer repeatedly, if each time given different data for the same model problem?)
- robustness (does it cope well with violations of the assumptions of the underlying model?)
- falsifiability (does it alert us when it is not good to use, i.e. when assumptions are violated?)

Tree-building techniques have also gained the attention of mathematicians. Trees can also be built using [T-theory](wikipedia://en/7767296).[^o47]

### File formats {#File_formats}

Trees can be encoded in a number of different formats, all of which must represent the nested structure of a tree. They may or may not encode branch lengths and other features. Standardized formats are critical for distributing and sharing trees without relying on graphics output that is hard to import into existing software. Commonly used formats are

- [Nexus file format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nexus_file)[^o49]
- [Newick format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newick_format)[^o49]

:::

## Limitations of phylogenetic analysis {#Limitations_of_phylogenetic_analysis}

Although phylogenetic trees produced on the basis of sequenced [genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene) or [genomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genome) data in different species can provide evolutionary insight, these analyses have important limitations. Most importantly, the trees that they generate are not necessarily correct – they do not necessarily accurately represent the evolutionary history of the included taxa. As with any scientific result, they are subject to [falsification](wikipedia://en/11283) by further study (e.g., gathering of additional data, analyzing the existing data with improved methods). The data on which they are based may be [noisy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_noise);[^o51] the analysis can be confounded by [genetic recombination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_recombination),[^o52] [horizontal gene transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_gene_transfer),[^o53] [hybridisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_%28biology%29) between species that were not nearest neighbors on the tree before hybridisation takes place, and [conserved sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conserved_sequence).

Also, there are problems in basing an analysis on a single type of character, such as a single [gene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene) or [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein) or only on morphological analysis, because such trees constructed from another unrelated data source often differ from the first, and therefore great care is needed in inferring phylogenetic relationships among species. This is most true of genetic material that is subject to lateral gene transfer and [recombination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_recombination), where different [haplotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplotype) blocks can have different histories. In these types of analysis, the output tree of a phylogenetic analysis of a single gene is an estimate of the gene's phylogeny (i.e. a gene tree) and not the phylogeny of the [taxa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxa) (i.e. species tree) from which these characters were sampled, though ideally, both should be very close. For this reason, serious phylogenetic studies generally use a combination of genes that come from different genomic sources (e.g., from mitochondrial or plastid vs. nuclear genomes),[^o54] or genes that would be expected to evolve under different selective regimes, so that [homoplasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homoplasy) (false [homology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_%28biology%29)) would be unlikely to result from natural selection.

When extinct species are included as [terminal nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf_node) in an analysis (rather than, for example, to constrain internal nodes), they are considered not to represent direct ancestors of any extant species. Extinct species do not typically contain high-quality [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_DNA).

The range of useful DNA materials has expanded with advances in extraction and sequencing technologies. Development of technologies able to infer sequences from smaller fragments, or from spatial patterns of DNA degradation products, would further expand the range of DNA considered useful.

Phylogenetic trees can also be inferred from a range of other data types, including morphology, the presence or absence of particular types of genes, insertion and deletion events – and any other observation thought to contain an evolutionary signal.

[Phylogenetic networks](wikipedia://en/3657198) are used when bifurcating trees are not suitable, due to these complications which suggest a more reticulate evolutionary history of the organisms sampled.
