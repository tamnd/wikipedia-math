# Probabilistic metric space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Probabilistic metric space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **probabilistic metric spaces** are a generalization of [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018) where the [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) no longer takes values in the non-negative [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) $\mathbf{R}_{\ \ge\ 0}$, but in distribution functions.[^o2]

:::

Let *D+* be the set of all [probability distribution functions](wikipedia://en/5793) *F* such that *F*(0) = 0 (*F* is a nondecreasing, left [continuous mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_mapping) from **R** into \[0, 1\] such that [max](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum)(*F*) = 1).

Then given a [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566) set *S* and a function *F*: *S* × *S* → *D+* where we denote *F*(*p*, *q*) by *F*<sub>*p*,*q*</sub> for every (*p*, *q*) ∈ *S* × *S*, the [ordered pair](wikipedia://en/22362) (*S*, *F*) is said to be a probabilistic metric space if:

- For all *u* and *v* in *S*, $u = v$ if and only if $F_{u,v}(x) = 1$ for all *x* > 0.
- For all *u* and *v* in *S*, $F_{u,v} = F_{v,u}$.
- For all *u*, *v* and *w* in *S*, $F_{u,v}(x) = 1$ and $F_{v,w}(y) = 1 \Rightarrow F_{u,w}(x + y) = 1$ for $x, y > 0$.[^o3]

## History {#History}

Probabilistic metric spaces are initially introduced by Menger, which were termed **statistical metrics**.[^o5] Shortly after, Wald criticized the generalized [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941) and proposed an alternative one.[^o6] However, both authors had come to the conclusion that in some respects the Wald inequality was too stringent a requirement to impose on all probability metric spaces, which is partly included in the work of Schweizer and Sklar.[^o7] Later, the probabilistic metric spaces found to be very suitable to be used with fuzzy sets[^o8] and further called fuzzy metric spaces[^o9]

## Probability metric of random variables {#Probability_metric_of_random_variables}

A probability metric *D* between two [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) *X* and *Y* may be defined, for example, as

$$D(X, Y) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \int_{-\infty}^\infty |x-y| F(x, y) \, dx \, dy$$

where *F*(*x*, *y*) denotes the joint probability density function of the random variables *X* and *Y*. If *X* and *Y* are independent from each other, then the equation above transforms into

$$D(X, Y) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \int_{-\infty}^\infty |x-y| f(x) g(y) \, dx \, dy$$

where *f*(*x*) and *g*(*y*) are probability density functions of *X* and *Y* respectively.

One may easily show that such probability metrics do not satisfy the first [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) axiom or satisfies it [if, and only if](wikipedia://en/14922), both of arguments *X* and *Y* are certain events described by [Dirac delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_delta) density [probability distribution functions](wikipedia://en/43487). In this case:

$$D(X, Y) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \int_{-\infty}^\infty |x-y| \delta(x-\mu_x) \delta(y-\mu_y) \, dx \, dy = |\mu_x - \mu_y|$$

the probability metric simply transforms into the metric between [expected values](wikipedia://en/9653) $\mu_x$, $\mu_y$ of the variables *X* and *Y*.

For all other [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) *X*, *Y* the probability metric does not satisfy the [identity of indiscernibles](wikipedia://en/630398) condition required to be satisfied by the metric of the metric space, that is:

$$D\left(X, X\right) > 0.$$

### Example {#Example}

For example if both [probability distribution functions](wikipedia://en/43487) of random variables *X* and *Y* are [normal distributions](wikipedia://en/21462) (N) having the same [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590) $\sigma$, integrating $D\left(X, Y\right)$ yields:

$$D_{NN}(X, Y) = \mu_{xy} + \frac{2\sigma}{\sqrt\pi} \exp\left(-\frac{\mu_{xy}^2}{4\sigma^2}\right) - \mu_{xy} \operatorname{erfc} \left(\frac{\mu_{xy}}{2\sigma}\right)$$

where

$$\mu_{xy} = \left|\mu_x - \mu_y\right|,$$

and $\operatorname{erfc}(x)$ is the complementary [error function](wikipedia://en/177111).

In this case:

$$\lim_{\mu_{xy} \to 0} D_{NN}(X, Y) = D_{NN}(X, X) = \frac{2\sigma}{\sqrt\pi}.$$

:::

## Probability metric of random vectors {#Probability_metric_of_random_vectors}

The probability metric of random variables may be extended into metric *D*(**X**, **Y**) of [random vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_vector) **X**, **Y** by substituting $|x-y|$ with any metric operator *d*(**x**, **y**):

$$D(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Y}) = \int_\Omega \int_\Omega d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) F(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) \, d\Omega_x d\Omega_y$$

where *F*(**X**, **Y**) is the joint probability density function of random vectors **X** and **Y**. For example substituting *d*(**x**, **y**) with [Euclidean metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_metric) and providing the vectors **X** and **Y** are mutually independent would yield to:

$$D(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Y}) = \int_{\Omega} \int_{\Omega} \sqrt{\sum_i |x_i - y_i|^2} F(\mathbf{x}) G(\mathbf{y}) \, d\Omega_x d\Omega_y.$$
