# Damgård–Jurik cryptosystem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Damgård–Jurik cryptosystem"}

The **Damgård–Jurik cryptosystem**[^o2] is a generalization of the [Paillier cryptosystem](wikipedia://en/480685). It uses computations modulo $n^{s+1}$ where $n$ is an [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29) modulus and $s$ a (positive) [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474). Paillier's scheme is the special case with $s=1$. The order $\varphi(n^{s+1})$ ([Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452)) of $Z^*_{n^{s+1}}$ can be divided by $n^s$. Moreover, $Z^*_{n^{s+1}}$ can be written as the [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887) of $G \times H$. $G$ is cyclic and of order $n^s$, while $H$ is isomorphic to $Z^*_n$. For encryption, the message is transformed into the corresponding [coset](wikipedia://en/98759) of the [factor group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_group) $G\times H/H$ and the security of the scheme relies on the difficulty of distinguishing random elements in different cosets of $H$. It is [semantically secure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantically_secure) if it is hard to decide if two given elements are in the same coset. Like Paillier, the security of Damgård–Jurik can be proven under the [decisional composite residuosity assumption](wikipedia://en/23702343).

:::

## Key generation {#Key_generation}

1. Choose two large [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) *p* and *q* randomly and independently of each other.
1. Compute $n=pq$ and $\lambda=\operatorname{lcm}(p-1,q-1)$.
1. Choose an element $g \in \mathbb{Z}^*_{n^{s+1}}$ such that $g=(1+n)^j x \mod n^{s+1}$ for a known $j$ [relative prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_prime) to $n$ and $x \in H$.
1. Using the [Chinese Remainder Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Remainder_Theorem), choose $d$ such that $d \mod n \in \mathbb{Z}^*_n$ and $d= 0 \mod \lambda$. For instance $d$ could be $\lambda$ as in Paillier's original scheme.

<!-- -->

- The public (encryption) key is $(n, g)$.
- The private (decryption) key is $d$.

## Encryption {#Encryption}

1. Let $m$ be a message to be encrypted where $m\in \mathbb Z_{n^s}$.
1. Select random $r$ where $r\in \mathbb Z^{*}_{n}$.
1. Compute ciphertext as: $c=g^m \cdot r^{n^s} \mod n^{s+1}$.

## Decryption {#Decryption}

1. Ciphertext $c\in \mathbb Z^{*}_{n^{s+1}}$
1. Compute $c^d \;mod\;n^{s+1}$. If *c* is a valid ciphertext then $c^d = (g^m r^{n^s})^d = ((1+n)^{jm}x^m r^{n^s})^d = (1+n)^{jmd \;mod\; n^s} (x^m r^{n^s})^{d \;mod\; \lambda} = (1+n)^{jmd \;mod\; n^s}$.
1. Apply a recursive version of the Paillier decryption mechanism to obtain $jmd$. As $jd$ is known, it is possible to compute $m=(jmd)\cdot (jd)^{-1} \;mod\;n^s$.

## Simplification {#Simplification}

At the cost of no longer containing the classical [Paillier cryptosystem](wikipedia://en/480685) as an instance, Damgård–Jurik can be simplified in the following way:

- The base *g* is fixed as $g=n+1$.
- The decryption exponent *d* is computed such that $d=1 \;mod\; n^s$ and $d=0 \;mod\; \lambda$.

In this case decryption produces $c^d = (1+n)^{m} \;mod\; n^{s+1}$. Using recursive Paillier decryption this gives us directly the plaintext *m*.
