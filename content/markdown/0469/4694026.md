# Bounded mean oscillation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bounded mean oscillation"}

In [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147) in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a function of **bounded mean oscillation**, also known as a **BMO function**, is a [real-valued function](wikipedia://en/22284647) whose mean oscillation is bounded (finite). The space of functions of **bounded mean oscillation** (**BMO**) is a [function space](wikipedia://en/340630) that, in some precise sense, plays the same role in the theory of [Hardy spaces](wikipedia://en/314780) *H<sup>p</sup>* that the space *L*<sup>∞</sup> of [essentially bounded functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essential_supremum) plays in the theory of [*L<sup>p</sup>*-spaces](wikipedia://en/45194). It is also called **John–Nirenberg space**, after [Fritz John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_John) and [Louis Nirenberg](wikipedia://en/1814725) who introduced and studied it for the first time.

:::

## Historical note {#Historical_note}

According to (Nirenberg 1985, 703 and p. 707),[^o4] the space of functions of bounded mean oscillation was introduced by (John 1961, 410–411) in connection with his studies of [mappings](wikipedia://en/516931) from a [bounded set](wikipedia://en/48258) $\Omega$ belonging to $\mathbb{R}^n$ into $\mathbb{R}^n$ and the corresponding problems arising from [elasticity theory](wikipedia://en/458866), precisely from the concept of [elastic strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_%28mechanics%29#Strain): the basic notation was introduced in a closely following paper by (John Nirenberg 1961),[^o7] where several properties of this function spaces were proved. The next important step in the development of the theory was the proof by [Charles Fefferman](wikipedia://en/502831)[^o8] of the [duality](wikipedia://en/609737) between **BMO** and the [Hardy space](wikipedia://en/314780) $H^1$, in the noted paper (Fefferman Stein 1972): a constructive proof of this result, introducing new methods and starting a further development of the theory, was given by [Akihito Uchiyama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akihito_Uchiyama).[^o10]

::: {#o12 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

(Definition 1.) The **mean oscillation** of a [locally integrable function](wikipedia://en/4258398) $u$ over a [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783)[^o13] $Q$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is defined as the value of the following [integral](wikipedia://en/15532):

$$\frac 1 {|Q|} \int_Q |u(y)-u_Q|\,\mathrm{d}y$$

where

- $|Q|$ is the [volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume_%28mathematics%29) of $Q$, i.e. its [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198)
- $u_Q$ is the average value of $u$ on the cube $Q$, i.e. $$u_Q = \frac 1 {|Q|}\int_Q u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.$$

(Definition 2.) A **BMO function** is a locally integrable function $u$ whose mean oscillation [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum), taken over the set of all [cubes](wikipedia://en/6285) $Q$ contained in $\mathbb{R}^n$, is finite.

The supremum of the mean oscillation is called the **BMO norm** of $u$[^o14] and is denoted by $\|u\|_\operatorname{BMO}$ (in some instances it is also denoted $\| u \|_*$). The space of BMO functions on a given domain $\Omega$ is denoted $\operatorname{BMO}(\Omega)$: when $\Omega = \mathbb{R}^n$, $\operatorname{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is abbreviated as $\operatorname{BMO}$.

**Note**. The use of [cubes](wikipedia://en/6285) $Q$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ as the [integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integration_%28mathematics%29) [domains](wikipedia://en/16796643) on which the (mean oscillation) is calculated, is not mandatory: (Wiegerinck 2001) uses [balls](wikipedia://en/160556) instead and, as remarked by (Stein 1993, 140), in doing so a perfectly equivalent definition of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) of bounded mean oscillation arises.

:::

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

### BMO functions are locally *p*-integrable {#BMO_functions_are_locally_p-integrable}

$\operatorname{BMO}$ functions are locally $L^p$ if $0<p<\infty$, but need not be locally bounded. In fact, using the John-Nirenberg inequality, we can prove that

$$\|u\|_\text{BMO}\simeq\sup_Q\left(\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_Q|u-u_Q|^p dx\right)^{1/p}.$$

### BMO is a Banach space {#BMO_is_a_Banach_space}

[Constant functions](wikipedia://en/24758132) have zero mean oscillation, therefore functions differing for a constant $c>0$ can share the same $\operatorname{BMO}$ norm value even if their difference is not zero [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688). Therefore, the function $\| \cdot \|_\operatorname{BMO}$ is properly a norm on the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/898784) of $\operatorname{BMO}$ functions [modulo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulo_%28jargon%29) the space of [constant functions](wikipedia://en/320861) on the domain considered.

### Averages of adjacent cubes are comparable {#Averages_of_adjacent_cubes_are_comparable}

As the name suggests, the mean or average of a function in $\operatorname{BMO}$ does not oscillate very much when computing it over cubes close to each other in position and scale. Precisely, if $Q$ and $R$ are [dyadic cubes](wikipedia://en/30151810) such that their boundaries touch and the side length of $Q$ is no less than one-half the side length of $R$ (and vice versa), then

$$|f_R-f_Q|\leq C\|f\|_\text{BMO}$$

where $C > 0$ is some universal constant. This property is, in fact, equivalent to $f$ being in $\operatorname{BMO}$, that is, if $f$ is a locally integrable function such that $|f_R-f_Q| \leq C$ for all dyadic cubes $Q$ and $R$ adjacent in the sense described above and $f$ is in dyadic $\operatorname{BMO}$ (where the supremum is only taken over dyadic cubes $Q$), then $f$ is in $\operatorname{BMO}$.[^o22]

### BMO is the dual vector space of *H*<sup>1</sup> {#BMO_is_the_dual_vector_space_of_H1}

(Fefferman 1971) showed that the $\operatorname{BMO}$ space is dual to $H^1$, the [Hardy space](wikipedia://en/314780) with $p = 1$.[^o25] The pairing between $f \in H^1$ and $g \in \operatorname{BMO}$ is given by

$$(f,g) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f(x) g(x) \, \mathrm{d}x$$

though some care is needed in defining this integral, as it does not in general converge absolutely.

::: {#o27 .statement cue=named-heading name="The John–Nirenberg inequality"}

### The John–Nirenberg inequality {#The_John–Nirenberg_inequality}

The **John–Nirenberg inequality** is an estimate that governs how far a function of bounded mean oscillation may deviate from its average by a certain amount.

::: {#o29 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

#### Statement {#Statement}

For each $f\in\operatorname{BMO}\left(\mathbb{R}^n\right)$, there are constants $c_1,c_2>0$ (independent of $f$), such that for any cube $Q$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$,

$$\left | \left \{x\in Q: |f-f_{Q}|>\lambda \right \} \right |\leq c_{1}\exp \left (-c_2 \frac{\lambda}{\|f\|_\operatorname{BMO}} \right )|Q|.$$

Conversely, if this inequality holds over all [cubes](wikipedia://en/6285) with some constant $C$ in place of $\| f \|_\operatorname{BMO}$, then $f$ is in $\operatorname{BMO}$ with norm at most a constant times $C$.

:::

#### A consequence: the distance in BMO to *L<sup>∞</sup>* {#A_consequence:_the_distance_in_BMO_to_L∞}

The John–Nirenberg inequality can actually give more information than just the $\operatorname{BMO}$ norm of a function. For a locally integrable function $f$, let $A(f)$ be the infimal $A>0$ for which

$$\sup_{Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n}\frac{1}{|Q|} \int_{Q}e^{\left|f-f_{Q}\right|/A} \mathrm{d}x<\infty.$$

The John–Nirenberg inequality implies that $A(f)\leq C \| f \|_\operatorname{BMO}$ for some universal constant $C$. For an [$L^\infty$](wikipedia://en/45194#Lp_spaces_and_Lebesgue_integrals) function, however, the above inequality will hold for all $A>0$. In other words, $A(f)=0$ if $f$ is in $L^\infty$. Hence the constant $A(f)$ gives us a way of measuring how far a function in $\operatorname{BMO}$ is from the subspace $L^\infty$. This statement can be made more precise:[^o31] there is a constant $C$, depending only on the [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28mathematics%29) $n$, such that for any function $f\in \operatorname{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ the following two-sided inequality holds

$$\frac 1 C A(f) \leq \inf_{g\in L^\infty} \|f-g\|_\text{BMO}\leq CA(f).$$

:::

:::

## Generalizations and extensions {#Generalizations_and_extensions}

### The spaces BMOH and BMOA {#The_spaces_BMOH_and_BMOA}

When the [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28mathematics%29) of the ambient space is 1, the space BMO can be seen as a [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of [harmonic functions](wikipedia://en/55762) on the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738) and plays a major role in the theory of [Hardy spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardy_spaces): by using (definition 2), it is possible to define the BMO(*T*) space on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) as the space of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) *f* : *T* → **R** such that

$$\frac 1 {|I|}\int_I|f(y)-f_I|\,\mathrm{d}y < C <+\infty$$

i.e. such that its (mean oscillation) over every arc I of the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071)[^o34] is bounded. Here as before *f<sub>I</sub>* is the mean value of f over the arc I.

(Definition 3.) An [Analytic function](wikipedia://en/61478) on the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738) is said to belong to the **Harmonic BMO** or in the **BMOH space** if and only if it is the [Poisson integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_integral) of a BMO(*T*) function. Therefore, BMOH is the space of all functions *u* with the form:

$$u(a) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{\mathbf{T}}\frac{1-|a|^2}{\left|a-e^{i\theta}\right|^2} f(e^{i\theta})\,\mathrm{d}\theta$$

equipped with the norm:

$$\|u\|_\text{BMOH}=\sup _ {|a|<1} \left\{\frac 1 {2\pi} \int_{\mathbf{T}}\frac{1-|a|^2}{|a-e^{i\theta}|^2} \left|f(e^{i\theta})-u(a)\right| \mathrm{d}\theta\right\}$$

The subspace of analytic functions belonging BMOH is called the **Analytic BMO space** or the **BMOA space**.

#### BMOA as the dual space of *H*<sup>1</sup>(*D*) {#BMOA_as_the_dual_space_of_H1(D)}

[Charles Fefferman](wikipedia://en/502831) in his original work proved that the real BMO space is dual to the real valued harmonic Hardy space on the upper [half-space](wikipedia://en/647297) **R**<sup>*n*</sup> × (0, ∞\].[^o36] In the theory of Complex and Harmonic analysis on the unit disk, his result is stated as follows.[^o37] Let *H<sup>p</sup>*(*D*) be the Analytic [Hardy space](wikipedia://en/314780) on the [unit Disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_Disc). For *p* = 1 we identify (*H*<sup>1</sup>)\* with BMOA by pairing *f* ∈ *H*<sup>1</sup>(*D*) and *g* ∈ BMOA using the *anti-linear transformation* *T<sub>g</sub>*

$$T_g(f) = \lim_{r \to 1} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \bar{g}(e^{i\theta}) f(re^{i\theta}) \, \mathrm{d}\theta$$

Notice that although the limit always exists for an *H*<sup>1</sup> function f and *T<sub>g</sub>* is an element of the dual space (*H*<sup>1</sup>)\*, since the transformation is *anti-linear*, we don't have an isometric isomorphism between (*H*<sup>1</sup>)\* and BMOA. However, one can obtain an isometry if they consider a kind of *space of conjugate BMOA functions*.

::: {#o39 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="VMO function"}

### The space *VMO* {#The_space_VMO}

The space **VMO** of functions of **vanishing mean oscillation** is the closure in BMO of the continuous functions that [vanish at infinity](wikipedia://en/461986). It can also be defined as the space of functions whose "mean oscillations" on cubes *Q* are not only bounded, but also tend to zero uniformly as the radius of the cube *Q* tends to 0 or ∞. The space VMO is a sort of Hardy space analogue of the space of continuous functions vanishing at infinity, and in particular the real valued harmonic Hardy space *H*<sup>1</sup> is the dual of VMO.[^o40]

:::

### Relation to the Hilbert transform {#Relation_to_the_Hilbert_transform}

A locally integrable function *f* on **R** is BMO if and only if it can be written as

$$f=f_1 + H f_2 + \alpha$$

where *f<sub>i</sub>* ∈ *L*<sup>∞</sup>, α is a constant and *H* is the [Hilbert transform](wikipedia://en/574024).

The BMO norm is then equivalent to the infimum of $\|f_1\|_\infty + \|f_2\|_\infty$ over all such representations.

Similarly *f* is VMO if and only if it can be represented in the above form with *f<sub>i</sub>* bounded uniformly continuous functions on **R**.[^o42]

::: {#o44 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Dyadic BMO"}

### The dyadic BMO space {#The_dyadic_BMO_space}

Let *Δ* denote the set of [dyadic cubes](wikipedia://en/30151810) in **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. The space **dyadic BMO**, written BMO<sub>d</sub>, is the space of functions satisfying the same inequality as for BMO functions, only that the supremum is over all dyadic cubes. This supremum is sometimes denoted ‖·‖<sub>BMO<sub>*d*</sub></sub>.

This space properly contains BMO. In particular, the function log(*x*)*χ*<sub>\[0,∞)</sub> is a function that is in dyadic BMO but not in BMO. However, if a function *f* is such that ‖*f*(·−*x*)‖<sub>BMO<sub>d</sub></sub> ≤ *C* for all *x* in **R**<sup>*n*</sup> for some *C* > 0, then by the [one-third trick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-third_trick) *f* is also in BMO. In the case of BMO on **T**<sup>*n*</sup> instead of **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, a function *f* is such that ‖*f*(·−*x*)‖<sub>BMO<sub>d</sub></sub> ≤ *C* for *n*+1 suitably chosen *x*, then *f* is also in BMO. This means BMO(**T**<sup>*n*</sup>) is the intersection of *n*+1 translations of dyadic BMO. By duality, H<sup>1</sup>(**T**<sup>*n*</sup>) is the sum of *n*+1 translations of dyadic H<sup>1</sup>.[^o45]

Although dyadic BMO is a much narrower class than BMO, many theorems that are true for BMO are much simpler to prove for dyadic BMO, and in some cases one can recover the original BMO theorems by proving them first in the special dyadic case.[^o46]

:::

::: {#o48 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Examples of $\operatorname{BMO}$ functions include the following:

- All bounded (measurable) functions. If $f$ is in $L^\infty$, then $\| f \|_\operatorname{BMO} \leq 2\| f \|_\infty$:[^o49] however, the converse is not true as the following example shows.
- The function $\log|P|$ for any polynomial $P$ that is not identically zero: in particular, this is true also for $|P(x)| = |x|$.[^o49]
- If $w$ is an [$A_\infty$ weight](wikipedia://en/16358346), then $\log(w)$ is $\operatorname{BMO}$. Conversely, if $f$ is $BMO$, then $e^{\delta f}$ is an $A_\infty$ weight for $\delta>0$ small enough: this fact is a consequence of the [John–Nirenberg inequality](wikipedia://en/4694026#The_John–Nirenberg_inequality).[^o50]

:::
