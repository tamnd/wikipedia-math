# Subgroup distortion

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Subgroup distortion"}

In [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164), a discipline of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **subgroup distortion** measures the extent to which an [overgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overgroup) can reduce the complexity of a group's [word problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_problem_%28groups%29).[^o2] Like much of geometric group theory, the concept is due to [Misha Gromov](wikipedia://en/771562), who introduced it in 1993.[^o3]

:::

Formally, let $S$ generate group $H$, and let $G$ be an overgroup for $H$ generated by $S \cup T$. Then each generating set defines a [word metric](wikipedia://en/1289716) on the corresponding group; the distortion of $H$ in $G$ is the [asymptotic equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_equivalence) class of the function

$$R\mapsto\frac{\operatorname{diam}_H(B_G(0,R)\cap H)}{\operatorname{diam}_H(B_H(0,R))}\text{,}$$

where $B_{X}(x, r)$ is the [ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_%28math%29) of [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) $r$ about center $x$ in $X$ and $\operatorname{diam}(S)$ is the [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) of $S$.[^o3]

A subgroup with bounded distortion is called **undistorted**, and is the same thing as a [quasi-isometrically embedded](wikipedia://en/12747259) subgroup.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

For example, consider the [infinite cyclic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_cyclic_group) $Z = \langle b\rangle$, embedded as a normal subgroup of the [Baumslag–Solitar group](wikipedia://en/11485892) $\operatorname{BS}(1, 2) = \langle a, b\rangle$. With respect to the chosen generating sets, the element

$$b^{2^n}=a^nba^{-n}$$

is distance $2^{n}$ from the [origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origin_%28math%29) in $Z$, but distance $2n + 1$ from the origin in $\operatorname{BS}(1, 2)$. In particular, $Z$ is at least exponentially distorted with base $2$.[^o3][^o15]

On the other hand, any embedded copy of $Z$ in the [free abelian group](wikipedia://en/247296) on two generators $Z^{2}$ is undistorted, as is any embedding of $Z$ into itself.[^o3][^o15]

:::

## Elementary properties {#Elementary_properties}

In a [tower of groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_of_groups) $K \le H \le G$, the distortion of $K$ in $G$ is at least the distortion of $K$ in $H$.

A [normal](wikipedia://en/21918) [abelian subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_subgroup) has distortion determined by the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) of the [conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugation_%28group_theory%29) [overgroup representation](wikipedia://en/12695); formally, if $g \in G$ acts on $V \le G$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, then $V$ is at least exponentially distorted with base $\lambda$. For many non-normal but still abelian subgroups, the distortion of the [normal core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_core) gives a strong lower bound.[^o2]

:::

## Known values {#Known_values}

Every [computable function](wikipedia://en/1139338) with at most [exponential growth](wikipedia://en/191933) can be a subgroup distortion,[^o10] but [Lie subgroups](wikipedia://en/17945) of a [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569) Lie group always have distortion $n \mapsto n^{r}$ for some [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) $r$.[^o11]

The denominator in the definition is always $2R$; for this reason, it is often omitted.[^o12][^o13] In that case, a subgroup that is not [locally finite](wikipedia://en/4939270) has [superadditive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superadditive) distortion; conversely every superadditive function (up to asymptotic equivalence) can be found this way.[^o13]

## In cryptography {#In_cryptography}

The simplification in a word problem induced by subgroup distortion suffices to construct a [cryptosystem](wikipedia://en/506383), algorithms for encoding and decoding secret messages.[^o15] Formally, the [plaintext](wikipedia://en/157935) message is any object (such as [text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain_text), [images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Images), or [numbers](wikipedia://en/21690)) that can be encoded as a number $n$. The transmitter then encodes $n$ as an element $g \in H$ with [word length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_length) $n$. In a public overgroup $G$ with that distorts $H$, the element $g$ has a word of much smaller length, which is then transmitted to the receiver along with a number of "decoys" from $G \backslash H$, to obscure the secret subgroup $H$. The receiver then picks out the element of $H$, re-expresses the word in terms of generators of $H$, and recovers $n$.[^o15]
