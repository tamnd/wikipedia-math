# Lattice Boltzmann methods for solids

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lattice Boltzmann methods for solids"}

The **Lattice Boltzmann methods for solids (LBMS)** are a set of methods for solving [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564) (PDE) in solid mechanics. The methods use a discretization of the [Boltzmann equation](wikipedia://en/1026522)(BM), and their use is known as the lattice Boltzmann methods for solids.

:::

LBMS methods are categorized by their reliance on:

- Vectorial distributions[^o15]
- Wave solvers[^o12]
- Force tuning[^o14]

The LBMS subset remains highly challenging from a computational aspect as much as from a theoretical point of view. Solving solid equations within the LBM framework is still a very active area of research. If solids are solved, this shows that the [Boltzmann equation](wikipedia://en/1026522) is capable of describing solid motions as well as fluids and gases: thus unlocking complex physics to be solved such as [fluid-structure interaction](wikipedia://en/6061729) (FSI) in biomechanics.

## Proposed insights {#Proposed_insights}

### Vectorial distributions {#Vectorial_distributions}

The first attempt[^o15] of LBMS tried to use a Boltzmann-like equation for force (vectorial) distributions. The approach requires more computational memory but results are obtained in fracture and solid cracking.

### Wave solvers {#Wave_solvers}

Another approach consists in using LBM as acoustic solvers to capture waves propagation in solids.[^o12][^o17][^o16][^o18]

### Force tuning {#Force_tuning}

#### Introduction {#Introduction}

This idea consists of introducing a modified version of the forcing term:[^o13] (or equilibrium distribution[^o19]) into the LBM as a stress divergence force. This force is considered space-time dependent and contains solid properties[^o9]

$$\vec{g} = \frac{1}{\rho} \vec{\mathbf{\nabla}_{x}} \cdot \overline{\overline{\sigma}},$$

where $\overline{\overline{\sigma}}$ denotes the [Cauchy stress tensor](wikipedia://en/3323565). $\vec{g}$ and $\rho$ are respectively the gravity vector and solid matter density. The stress tensor is usually computed across the lattice aiming [finite difference schemes](wikipedia://en/6054681).

#### Some results {#Some_results}

*Image: LBMS solid displacement.png.* 2D displacement magnitude on a solid system using force tuning. Obtained field is in accordance with [finite element methods](wikipedia://en/18233581) results.

Force tuning[^o14] has recently proven its efficiency with a maximum error of 5% in comparison with standard [finite element](wikipedia://en/18233581) solvers in mechanics. Accurate validation of results can also be a tedious task since these methods are very different, common issues are:

- Meshes or lattice discretization
- Location of computed fields at elements or nodes
- Hidden information in software used for [finite element analysis](wikipedia://en/18233581) comparison
- Non-linear materials
- Steady state convergence for LBMS
