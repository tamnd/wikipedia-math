# Second derivative

Differential

*Image: 4 fonctions du second degré.svg.* The second derivative of a [quadratic function](wikipedia://en/187240) is [constant](wikipedia://en/320861).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Second derivative"}

In [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), the **second derivative**, or the **second-order derivative**, of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f$ is the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of the derivative of $f$. Informally, the second derivative can be phrased as "the rate of change of the rate of change"; for example, the second derivative of the position of an object with respect to time is the [instantaneous acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instantaneous_acceleration) of the object, or the rate at which the [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) of the object is changing with respect to time. In [Leibniz notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz_notation):

$$a = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2 x}{dt^2},$$

where $a$ is acceleration, $v$ is velocity, $t$ is time, $x$ is position, and d is the instantaneous "delta" or change. The last expression $\tfrac{d^2 x}{dt^2}$ is the second derivative of position ($x$) with respect to time.

:::

On the [graph of a function](wikipedia://en/87352), the sign of the second derivative is related to the [concavity](wikipedia://en/245568) of the graph. The graph of a function with a positive second derivative is upwardly concave, while the graph of a function with a negative second derivative curves in the opposite way.

## Second derivative power rule {#Second_derivative_power_rule}

The [power rule](wikipedia://en/147912) for the first derivative, if applied twice, will produce the second derivative power rule as follows:

$$\frac{d^2}{dx^2} x^n = \frac{d}{dx}\frac{d}{dx} x^n = \frac{d}{dx} \left(n x^{n-1}\right) = n \frac{d}{dx} x^{n-1} = n(n - 1)x^{n-2}.$$

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

## Notation {#Notation}

The second derivative of a function $f(x)$ is usually denoted $f''(x)$.[^o5] That is:

$$f'' = \left(f'\right)'$$

When using [Leibniz's notation](wikipedia://en/410712) for derivatives, the second derivative of a dependent variable $y$ with respect to an independent variable $x$ is written

$$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}.$$

This notation is derived from the following formula:

$$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} \,=\, \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right).$$

:::

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Given the function

$$f(x) = x^3,$$

the derivative of $f$ is the function

$$f'(x) = 3x^2.$$

The second derivative of $f$ is the derivative of $f'$, namely

$$f''(x) = 6x.$$

:::

## Relation to the graph {#Relation_to_the_graph}

*Image: Animated illustration of inflection point.gif.* A plot of $f(x) = \sin(2x)$ from $-\pi/4$ to $5\pi/4$. The tangent line is blue where the curve is concave up, green where the curve is concave down, and red at the inflection points (0, $\pi$/2, and $\pi$).

::: {#o10 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Concavity"}

### Concavity {#Concavity}

The second derivative of a function $f$ can be used to determine the **concavity** of the graph of $f$. A function whose second derivative is positive is said to be [concave up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concave_up) (also referred to as convex), meaning that the [tangent line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_line) near the point where it touches the function will lie below the graph of the function. Similarly, a function whose second derivative is negative will be [concave down](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concave_down) (sometimes simply called concave), and its tangent line will lie above the graph of the function near the point of contact.

:::

### Inflection points {#Inflection_points}

If the second derivative of a function changes sign, the graph of the function will switch from concave down to concave up, or vice versa. A point where this occurs is called an **inflection point**. Assuming the second derivative is continuous, it must take a value of zero at any inflection point, although not every point where the second derivative is zero is necessarily a point of inflection.

### Second derivative test {#Second_derivative_test}

The relation between the second derivative and the graph can be used to test whether a [stationary point](wikipedia://en/885651) for a function (i.e., a point where $f'(x) = 0$) is a [local maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_maximum) or a [local minimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_minimum). Specifically,

- If $f''(x) < 0$, then $f$ has a local maximum at $x$.
- If $f''(x) > 0$, then $f$ has a local minimum at $x$.
- If $f''(x) = 0$, the second derivative test says nothing about the point $x$, a possible inflection point.

The reason the second derivative produces these results can be seen by way of a real-world analogy. Consider a vehicle that at first is moving forward at a great velocity, but with a negative acceleration. Clearly, the position of the vehicle at the point where the velocity reaches zero will be the maximum distance from the starting position – after this time, the velocity will become negative and the vehicle will reverse. The same is true for the minimum, with a vehicle that at first has a very negative velocity but positive acceleration.

## Limit {#Limit}

It is possible to write a single [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602) for the second derivative:

$$f''(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - 2f(x) + f(x-h)}{h^2}.$$

The limit is called the [second symmetric derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_symmetric_derivative).[^o14][^o15] The second symmetric derivative may exist even when the (usual) second derivative does not.

The expression on the right can be written as a [difference quotient](wikipedia://en/241863) of difference quotients:

$$\frac{f(x+h) - 2f(x) + f(x-h)}{h^2} = \frac{\dfrac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} - \dfrac{f(x) - f(x-h)}{h}}{h}.$$

This limit can be viewed as a continuous version of the [second difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_difference) for [sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence_%28mathematics%29).

However, the existence of the above limit does not mean that the function $f$ has a second derivative. The limit above just gives a possibility for calculating the second derivative—but does not provide a definition. A counterexample is the [sign function](wikipedia://en/505449) $\operatorname{sgn}(x)$, which is defined as:

$$\operatorname{sgn}(x) = \begin{cases} -1 & \text{if } x < 0, \\ 0 & \text{if } x = 0, \\ 1 & \text{if } x > 0. \end{cases}$$

The sign function is not continuous at zero, and therefore the second derivative for $x = 0$ does not exist. But the above limit exists for $x = 0$:

$$\begin{aligned} \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\operatorname{sgn}(0+h) - 2\operatorname{sgn}(0) + \operatorname{sgn}(0-h)}{h^2} &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\operatorname{sgn}(h) - 2\cdot 0 + \operatorname{sgn}(-h)}{h^2} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\operatorname{sgn}(h) + (-\operatorname{sgn}(h))}{h^2} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{0}{h^2} = 0. \end{aligned}$$

## Quadratic approximation {#Quadratic_approximation}

Just as the first derivative is related to [linear approximations](wikipedia://en/1107596), the second derivative is related to the best [quadratic approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_approximation) for a function $f$. This is the [quadratic function](wikipedia://en/187240) whose first and second derivatives are the same as those of $f$ at a given point. The formula for the best quadratic approximation to a function $f$ around the point $x = a$ is

$$f(x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(a)(x-a)^2.$$

This quadratic approximation is the second-order [Taylor polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_polynomial) for the function centered at $x = a$.

## Eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the second derivative {#Eigenvalues_and_eigenvectors_of_the_second_derivative}

For many combinations of [boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_conditions) explicit formulas for [eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the second derivative](wikipedia://en/26992157) can be obtained. For example, assuming $x \in [0,L]$ and homogeneous [Dirichlet boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_boundary_conditions) (i.e., $v(0) = v(L) = 0$ where $v$ is the eigenvector), the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) are $\lambda_j = -\tfrac{j^2 \pi^2}{L^2}$ and the corresponding [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvectors) (also called [eigenfunctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenfunctions)) are $v_j(x) = \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{L}} \sin\left(\tfrac{j \pi x}{L}\right)$. Here, $v''_j(x) = \lambda_j v_j(x)$, for $j = 1,\ldots,\infty$.

For other well-known cases, see [Eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the second derivative](wikipedia://en/26992157).

## Generalization to higher dimensions {#Generalization_to_higher_dimensions}

### The Hessian {#The_Hessian}

The second derivative generalizes to higher dimensions through the notion of second [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565). For a function $f: \mathbf{R}^{3} \to \mathbf{R}$, these include the three second-order partials

$$\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}, \; \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}, \text{ and }\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z^2}$$

and the mixed partials

$$\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \, \partial y}, \; \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \, \partial z}, \text{ and } \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \, \partial z}.$$

If the function's image and domain both have a potential, then these fit together into a [symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/126474) known as the **Hessian**. The [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of this matrix can be used to implement a multivariable analogue of the second derivative test. (See also the [second partial derivative test](wikipedia://en/3880982).)

### The Laplacian {#The_Laplacian}

Another common generalization of the second derivative is the **Laplacian**. This is the differential operator $\nabla^2$ (or $\Delta$) defined by

$$\nabla^2 f = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z^2}.$$

The Laplacian of a function is equal to the [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) of the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461), and the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) of the [Hessian matrix](wikipedia://en/412108).
