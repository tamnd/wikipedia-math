# Characteristic function (probability theory)

*Image: Sinc simple.svg.* The characteristic function of a uniform *U*(–1,1) random variable. This function is real-valued because it corresponds to a random variable that is symmetric around the origin; however characteristic functions may generally be complex-valued.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Characteristic function"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **characteristic function** of any [real-valued](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-valued) [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) completely defines its [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543). If a random variable admits a [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487), then the characteristic function is the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) (with sign reversal) of the probability density function. Thus it provides an alternative route to analytical results compared with working directly with [probability density functions](wikipedia://en/43487) or [cumulative distribution functions](wikipedia://en/5793). There are particularly simple results for the characteristic functions of distributions defined by the weighted sums of random variables.

:::

In addition to [univariate distributions](wikipedia://en/9253219), characteristic functions can be defined for [multivariate probability distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_probability_distribution) of vector- or matrix-valued random variables, and can also be extended to more generic cases.

The characteristic function always exists when treated as a function of a real-valued argument, unlike the [moment-generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment-generating_function). There are relations between the behavior of the characteristic function of a distribution and properties of the distribution, such as the existence of moments and the existence of a density function.

## Introduction {#Introduction}

The characteristic function is a way to describe a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) $X$. The **characteristic function**,

$$\varphi_X(t) = \operatorname{E} \left [ e^{itX} \right ],$$

a function of $t$, determines the behavior and properties of the probability distribution of $X$. It is equivalent to a [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) (if it exists) or [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793), in the sense that knowing one of these functions allows computation of the others, but they provide different insights into the features of the random variable.

In particular cases, one or another of these equivalent functions may be easier to represent in terms of simple standard functions.

If a random variable admits a [density function](wikipedia://en/43487), then the characteristic function is its [Fourier dual](wikipedia://en/609737), in the sense that each of them is a [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of the other. If a random variable has a [moment-generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment-generating_function) $M_X(t)$, then the domain of the characteristic function can be extended to the complex plane, and

$\varphi_X(-it) = M_X(t).$[^n1]

Note however that the characteristic function of a distribution is well defined for all [real values](wikipedia://en/20646438) of $t$, even when the [moment-generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment-generating_function) is not well defined for all real values of $t$.

The characteristic function approach is particularly useful in analysis of linear combinations of independent random variables: a classical proof of the [Central Limit Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Limit_Theorem) uses characteristic functions and [Lévy's continuity theorem](wikipedia://en/1775513). Another important application is to the theory of the [decomposability](wikipedia://en/5761408) of random variables.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $X$ be a real-valued random variable. Its characteristic function $\varphi_X:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}$ is defined for each $t\in\mathbb{R}$ as the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) of $e^{itX}$, where $i$ is the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358).

$$\varphi_X(t) = \operatorname{E}\left[e^{itX}\right] = \int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{itx}\,dF_X(x) = \int_0^1 e^{it Q_X(p)}\,dp = \int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{itx} f_X(x)\,dx.$$

In the equalites, there is the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) $F_X$, the inverse cumulative distribution function $Q_X$ (also called the [quantile function](wikipedia://en/10167616)[^o6]), and the [density function](wikipedia://en/43487) $f_X$. The integrals are in the [Riemann–Stieltjes](wikipedia://en/330491) sense. The last equality holds if $f_X$ exists, which then implies that $\varphi_X$ is its [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) with sign reversal in the complex exponential.[^o7][^n2] The convention for the constant appearing in the above definition differs from the usual convention for the Fourier transform.[^n3] For example, some authors[^n4] define $\varphi_X=\left[e^{-2\pi itX}\right]$, which is essentially a change of parameter. Other notation may be encountered in the literature: $\hat{p}$ as the characteristic function for a probability measure $p$, or $\hat{f}$ as the characteristic function corresponding to a density $f$.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The notion of characteristic functions generalizes to multivariate random variables and more complicated [random elements](wikipedia://en/5215393). The argument of the characteristic function will always belong to the [continuous dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_dual) of the space where the random variable $X$ takes its values. For common cases such definitions are listed below:

- If $X$ is a $k$-dimensional [random vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_vector), then for $t \in \mathbf{R}^{k}$ $$\varphi_X(t) = \operatorname{E}\left[\exp( i t^T\!X)\right],$$ where $t^T$ is the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) of the vector $t$,
- If $X$ is a $k \times p$-dimensional [random matrix](wikipedia://en/1648765), then for $t \in \mathbf{R}^{k\times p}$ $$\varphi_X(t) = \operatorname{E}\left[\exp \left( i \operatorname{tr}(t^T\!X) \right )\right],$$ where $\operatorname{tr}(\cdot)$ is the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) operator,
- If $X$ is a [complex random variable](wikipedia://en/49434972), then for $t \in \mathbf{C}$[^n5] $$\varphi_X(t) = \operatorname{E}\left[\exp\left( i \operatorname{Re}\left(\overline{t}X\right) \right)\right],$$ where $\overline t$ is the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) of $t$ and $\operatorname{Re}(z)$ is the [real part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part) of the [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $z$,
- If $X$ is a $k$-dimensional [complex random vector](wikipedia://en/56126093), then for $t \in \mathbf{C}^{k}$ [^n6] $$\varphi_X(t) = \operatorname{E}\left[\exp(i\operatorname{Re}(t^*\!X))\right],$$ where $t^*$ is the [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) of the vector $t$,
- If $X(s)$ is a [stochastic process](wikipedia://en/47895), then for all functions $t(s)$ such that the integral $\int_{\mathbb R} t(s)X(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$ converges for almost all realizations of $X$[^n7] $$\varphi_X(t) = \operatorname{E}\left[\exp \left ( i\int_\mathbf{R} t(s)X(s) \, ds \right ) \right].$$

## Properties {#Properties}

- The characteristic function of a real-valued random variable always exists, since it is an integral of a bounded [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) over a space whose [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) is finite.
- A characteristic function is [uniformly continuous](wikipedia://en/32337) on the entire space.
- It is non-vanishing in a region around zero: $\varphi(0) = 1$.
- It is bounded: $\lvert \varphi(t)\rvert \le 1$.
- It is [Hermitian](wikipedia://en/3108737): $\varphi(-t) = \overline{\varphi(t)}$. In particular, the characteristic function of a symmetric (around the origin) random variable is real-valued and [even](wikipedia://en/592151).
- There is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) and characteristic functions on $\mathbf{R}^k$, $k\in\mathbb{N}$. That is, for any two random variables $X_{1}$, $X_{2}$, with values in $\mathbf{R}^k$, both have the same probability distribution if and only if $\varphi_{X_1}=\varphi_{X_2}$.[^o42]
- If a random variable $X$ has [moments](wikipedia://en/368684) up to $k$-th order, then the characteristic function $\varphi_{X}$ is $k$ times continuously differentiable on the entire real line. In this case $$\operatorname{E}[X^k] = i^{-k} \varphi_X^{(k)}(0).$$
- If a characteristic function $\varphi_{X}$ has a $k$-th derivative at zero, then the random variable $X$ has all moments up to $k$ if $k$ is even, but only up to $k - 1$ if $k$ is odd.[^n8] $$\varphi_X^{(k)}(0) = i^k \operatorname{E}[X^k]$$
- If $X_{1}, ..., X_{n}$ are independent random variables, and $a_{1}, ..., a_{n}$ are some constants, then the characteristic function of the linear combination of the $X_{i}$ variables is $$\varphi_{a_1X_1+\cdots+a_nX_n}(t) = \varphi_{X_1}(a_1t)\cdots \varphi_{X_n}(a_nt).$$ One specific case is the sum of two independent random variables $X_{1}$ and $X_{2}$ in which case one has $$\varphi_{X_1+X_2}(t) = \varphi_{X_1}(t)\cdot\varphi_{X_2}(t).$$
- Let $X$ and $Y$ be two random variables with characteristic functions $\varphi_{X}$ and $\varphi_{Y}$. $X$ and $Y$ are independent if and only if $\varphi_{X, Y}(s, t)= \varphi_{X}(s) \varphi_{Y}(t) \quad \text { for all } \quad(s, t) \in \mathbb{R}^{2}$.
- The tail behavior of the characteristic function determines the [smoothness](wikipedia://en/24140640) of the corresponding density function.
- Let the random variable $Y = aX + b$ be the linear transformation of a random variable $X$. The characteristic function of $Y$ is $\varphi_Y(t)=e^{itb}\varphi_X(at)$. For random vectors $X$ and $Y = AX + B$ (where $A$ is a constant matrix and $B$ a constant vector), we have $\varphi_Y(t) = e^{it^\top B}\varphi_X(A^\top t)$.[^o44]

::: {#o46 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Continuity"}

### Continuity {#Continuity}

The bijection stated above between probability distributions and characteristic functions is *sequentially continuous*. That is, whenever a sequence of distribution functions $F_{j}(x)$ converges (weakly) to some distribution $F(x)$, the corresponding sequence of characteristic functions $\varphi_{j}(t)$ will also converge, and the limit $\varphi(t)$ will correspond to the characteristic function of law $F$. More formally, this is stated as

**[Lévy’s continuity theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lévy’s_continuity_theorem):** A sequence $X_{j}$ of $n$-variate random variables [converges in distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergence_in_distribution) to random variable $X$ if and only if the sequence $\varphi_{X_{j}}$ converges pointwise to a function $\varphi$ which is continuous at the origin. Where $\varphi$ is the characteristic function of $X$.[^n9]

This theorem can be used to prove the [law of large numbers](wikipedia://en/157055#Proof_using_convergence_of_characteristic_functions) and the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406#Proof).

:::

### Inversion formula {#Inversion_formula}

There is a [one-to-one correspondence](wikipedia://en/3942) between cumulative distribution functions and characteristic functions, so it is possible to find one of these functions if we know the other. The formula in the definition of characteristic function allows us to compute $\varphi$ when we know the distribution function $F$ (or density $f$). If, on the other hand, we know the characteristic function $\varphi$ and want to find the corresponding distribution function, then one of the following **inversion theorems** can be used.

::: {#o50 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem**. If the characteristic function $\varphi_{X}$ of a random variable $X$ is [integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_function), then $F_{X}$ is absolutely continuous, and therefore $X$ has a [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487). In the univariate case (i.e. when $X$ is scalar-valued) the density function is given by

$$f_X(x) = F_X'(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbf{R}} e^{-itx}\varphi_X(t)\,dt.$$

In the multivariate case it is

$$f_X(x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^n} \int_{\mathbf{R}^n} e^{-i(t\cdot x)}\varphi_X(t)\lambda(dt)$$

where $t\cdot x$ is the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093).

The density function is the [Radon–Nikodym derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon–Nikodym_derivative) of the distribution $\mu_{X}$ with respect to the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) $\lambda$:

$$f_X(x) = \frac{d\mu_X}{d\lambda}(x).$$

:::

::: {#o51 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem (Lévy)"}

**Theorem (Lévy)**.named after the French mathematician [Paul Lévy](wikipedia://en/556462) If $\varphi_{X}$ is characteristic function of distribution function $F_{X}$, two points $a < b$ are such that $\{x | a < x < b\}$ is a [continuity set](wikipedia://en/23251647) of $\mu_{X}$ (in the univariate case this condition is equivalent to continuity of $F_{X}$ at points $a$ and $b$), then

- If $X$ is scalar: $$F_X(b) - F_X(a) = \frac{1} {2\pi} \lim_{T \to \infty} \int_{-T}^{+T} \frac{e^{-ita} - e^{-itb}} {it}\, \varphi_X(t)\, dt.$$ This formula can be re-stated in a form more convenient for numerical computation as[^n10] $$\frac{F(x+h) - F(x-h)}{2h} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\sin ht}{ht} e^{-itx} \varphi_X(t) \, dt .$$ For a random variable bounded from below one can obtain $F(b)$ by taking $a$ such that $F(a)=0.$ Otherwise, if a random variable is not bounded from below, the limit for $a\to-\infty$ gives $F(b)$, but is numerically impractical.[^n10]
- If $X$ is a vector random variable: $$\mu_X\big(\{a<x<b\}\big) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^n} \lim_{T_1\to\infty}\cdots\lim_{T_n\to\infty} \int\limits_{-T_1\leq t_1\leq T_1} \cdots \int\limits_{-T_n \leq t_n \leq T_n} \prod_{k=1}^n\left(\frac{e^{-i t_k a_k}-e^{-i t_k b_k}}{it_k}\right)\varphi_X(t)\lambda(dt_1 \times \cdots \times dt_n)$$

:::

::: {#o54 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem**. If $a$ is (possibly) an atom of $X$ (in the univariate case this means a point of discontinuity of $F_{X}$) then

- If $X$ is scalar: $$F_X(a) - F_X(a-0) = \lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T} \int_{-T}^{+T} e^{-ita}\varphi_X(t)\,dt$$
- If $X$ is a vector random variable:[^n11] $$\mu_X(\{a\}) = \lim_{T_1\to\infty}\cdots\lim_{T_n\to\infty} \left(\prod_{k=1}^n\frac{1}{2T_k}\right) \int\limits_{[-T_1, T_1] \times \dots \times [-T_n, T_n]} e^{-i(t\cdot a)}\varphi_X(t)\lambda(dt)$$

:::

::: {#o56 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem (Gil-Pelaez)"}

**Theorem (Gil-Pelaez)**.[^n12] For a univariate random variable $X$, if $x$ is a [continuity point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_point) of $F_{X}$ then

$$F_X(x) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\infty \frac{\operatorname{Im}[e^{-itx}\varphi_X(t)]}{t}\,dt$$

where the imaginary part of a complex number $z$ is given by $\mathrm{Im}(z) = (z - z^*)/2i$.

And its density function is:

$$f_X(x) = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\infty \operatorname{Re}[e^{-itx}\varphi_X(t)]\,dt$$

The integral may be not [Lebesgue-integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue-integrable); for example, when $X$ is the [discrete random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_random_variable) that is always 0, it becomes the [Dirichlet integral](wikipedia://en/1235977).

Inversion formulas for multivariate distributions are available.[^n10][^n13]

### Criteria for characteristic functions {#Criteria_for_characteristic_functions}

The set of all characteristic functions is closed under certain operations:

- A [convex linear combination](wikipedia://en/794534) $\sum_n a_n\varphi_n(t)$ (with $a_n\geq0,\ \sum_n a_n=1$) of a finite or a countable number of characteristic functions is also a characteristic function.
- The product of a finite number of characteristic functions is also a characteristic function. The same holds for an [infinite product](wikipedia://en/26382006) provided that it converges to a function continuous at the origin.
- If $\varphi$ is a characteristic function and $\alpha$ is a real number, then $\bar{\varphi}$, $\operatorname{Re}(\varphi), \lvert \varphi\rvert^{2}$, and $\varphi(\alpha t)$ are also characteristic functions.

It is well known that any non-decreasing [càdlàg](wikipedia://en/30862839) function $F$ with limits $F(-\infty) = 0$, $F(+\infty) = 1$ corresponds to a [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) of some random variable. There is also interest in finding similar simple criteria for when a given function $\varphi$ could be the characteristic function of some random variable. The central result here is [Bochner’s theorem](wikipedia://en/2369850), although its usefulness is limited because the main condition of the theorem, [non-negative definiteness](wikipedia://en/649616), is very hard to verify. Other theorems also exist, such as Khinchine’s, Mathias’s, or Cramér’s, although their application is just as difficult. [Pólya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Pólya)’s theorem, on the other hand, provides a very simple convexity condition which is sufficient but not necessary. Characteristic functions which satisfy this condition are called Pólya-type.[^n14]

**[Bochner’s theorem](wikipedia://en/2369850)**. An arbitrary function $\varphi : \mathbf{R}^{n} \to \mathbf{C}$ is the characteristic function of some random variable if and only if $\varphi$ is [positive definite](wikipedia://en/649616), continuous at the origin, and if $\varphi(0) = 1$.

**Khinchine’s criterion**. A complex-valued, absolutely continuous function $\varphi$, with $\varphi(0) = 1$, is a characteristic function if and only if it admits the representation

$$\varphi(t) = \int_{\mathbf{R}} g(t+\theta)\overline{g(\theta)} \, d\theta .$$

**Mathias’ theorem**. A real-valued, even, continuous, absolutely integrable function $\varphi$, with $\varphi(0) = 1$, is a characteristic function if and only if

$$(-1)^n \left ( \int_{\mathbf{R}} \varphi(pt)e^{-t^2/2} H_{2n}(t) \, dt \right ) \geq 0$$

for $n = 0,1,2,...$, and all $p > 0$. Here $H_{2n}$ denotes the [Hermite polynomial](wikipedia://en/195984) of degree $2n$.

*Image: 2 cfs coincide over a finite interval.svg.* Pólya’s theorem can be used to construct an example of two random variables whose characteristic functions coincide over a finite interval but are different elsewhere.

**Pólya’s theorem**. If $\varphi$ is a real-valued, even, continuous function which satisfies the conditions

- $$\varphi(0) = 1,$$
- $\varphi$ is [convex](wikipedia://en/245568) for $t>0$,
- $$\varphi(\infty) = 0,$$

then $\varphi(t)$ is the characteristic function of an absolutely continuous distribution symmetric about 0.

## Uses {#Uses}

Because of the [continuity theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lévy_continuity_theorem), characteristic functions are used in the most frequently seen proof of the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406). The main technique involved in making calculations with a characteristic function is recognizing the function as the characteristic function of a particular distribution.

### Basic manipulations of distributions {#Basic_manipulations_of_distributions}

Characteristic functions are particularly useful for dealing with linear functions of [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence) random variables. For example, if $X_{1}$, $X_{2}$, ..., $X_{n}$ is a sequence of independent (and not necessarily identically distributed) random variables, and

$$S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i X_i,\,\!$$

where the $a_{i}$ are constants, then the characteristic function for $S_{n}$ is given by

$$\varphi_{S_n}(t)=\varphi_{X_1}(a_1t)\varphi_{X_2}(a_2t)\cdots \varphi_{X_n}(a_nt) \,\!$$

In particular, $\varphi_{X+Y}(t) = \varphi_{X}(t)\varphi_{Y}(t)$. To see this, write out the definition of characteristic function:

$$\varphi_{X+Y}(t)= \operatorname{E}\left [e^{it(X+Y)}\right]= \operatorname{E}\left [e^{itX}e^{itY}\right] = \operatorname{E}\left [e^{itX}\right] \operatorname{E}\left [e^{itY}\right] =\varphi_X(t) \varphi_Y(t)$$

The independence of $X$ and $Y$ is required to establish the equality of the third and fourth expressions.

Another special case of interest for identically distributed random variables is when $a_{i} = 1 / n$ and then *S<sub>n</sub>* is the sample mean. In this case, writing $\overline{X}$ for the mean,

$$\varphi_{\overline{X}}(t)= \varphi_X\!\left(\tfrac{t}{n} \right)^n$$

### Moments {#Moments}

Characteristic functions can also be used to find [moments](wikipedia://en/368684) of a random variable. Provided that the $n$-<sup>th</sup> moment exists, the characteristic function can be differentiated $n$ times:

$$\operatorname{E}\left[ X^n\right] = i^{-n}\left[\frac{d^n}{dt^n}\varphi_X(t)\right]_{t=0} = i^{-n}\varphi_X^{(n)}(0) ,\!$$

This can be formally written using the derivatives of the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021):

$$f_X(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n}{n!}\delta^{(n)}(x)\operatorname{E}[X^n]$$

which allows a formal solution to the [moment problem](wikipedia://en/1244992). For example, suppose $X$ has a standard [Cauchy distribution](wikipedia://en/7003). Then $\varphi_{X}(t) = e^{-|t|}$. This is not [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) at $t = 0$, showing that the Cauchy distribution has no [expectation](wikipedia://en/9653). Also, the characteristic function of the sample mean $\overline{X}$ of $n$ [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence) observations has characteristic function $\varphi_{\overline{X}}(t) = (e^{-|t|/n})^{n} = e^{-|t|}$, using the result from the previous section. This is the characteristic function of the standard Cauchy distribution: thus, the sample mean has the same distribution as the population itself.

As a further example, suppose $X$ follows a [Gaussian distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_distribution) i.e. $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$. Then $\varphi_{X}(t) = e^{\mu i t - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 t^2}$ and

$$\operatorname{E}\left[ X\right] = i^{-1} \left[\frac{d}{dt}\varphi_X(t)\right]_{t=0} = i^{-1} \left[(i \mu - \sigma^2 t) \varphi_X(t) \right]_{t=0} = \mu$$

A similar calculation shows $\operatorname{E}\left[ X^2\right] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2$ and is easier to carry out than applying the definition of expectation and using [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252) to evaluate $\operatorname{E}\left[ X^2\right]$.

The logarithm of a characteristic function is a [cumulant generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumulant_generating_function), which is useful for finding [cumulants](wikipedia://en/359684); some instead define the cumulant generating function as the logarithm of the [moment-generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment-generating_function), and call the logarithm of the characteristic function the *second* cumulant generating function.

### Data analysis {#Data_analysis}

Characteristic functions can be used as part of procedures for fitting probability distributions to samples of data. Cases where this provides a practicable option compared to other possibilities include fitting the [stable distribution](wikipedia://en/1117869) since closed form expressions for the density are not available which makes implementation of [maximum likelihood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_likelihood) estimation difficult. Estimation procedures are available which match the theoretical characteristic function to the [empirical characteristic function](wikipedia://en/50621216), calculated from the data. Paulson et al. (1975)[^n15] and Heathcote (1977)[^n16] provide some theoretical background for such an estimation procedure. In addition, Yu (2004)[^n17] describes applications of empirical characteristic functions to fit [time series](wikipedia://en/406624) models where likelihood procedures are impractical. Empirical characteristic functions have also been used by Ansari et al. (2020)[^n18] and Li et al. (2020)[^n19] for training [generative adversarial networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_adversarial_networks).

### Example {#Example}

The [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079) with scale parameter θ and a [shape parameter](wikipedia://en/9210865) $k$ has the characteristic function

$$(1 - \theta i t)^{-k}.$$

Now suppose that we have

$$X ~\sim \Gamma(k_1,\theta) \mbox{ and } Y \sim \Gamma(k_2,\theta)$$

with $X$ and $Y$ independent from each other, and we wish to know what the distribution of $X + Y$ is. The characteristic functions are

$$\varphi_X(t)=(1 - \theta i t)^{-k_1},\,\qquad \varphi_Y(t)=(1 - \theta it)^{-k_2}$$

which by independence and the basic properties of characteristic function leads to

$$\varphi_{X+Y}(t)=\varphi_X(t)\varphi_Y(t)=(1 - \theta i t)^{-k_1}(1 - \theta i t)^{-k_2}=\left(1 - \theta i t\right)^{-(k_1+k_2)}.$$

This is the characteristic function of the gamma distribution scale parameter $\theta$ and shape parameter $k_{1} + k_{2}$, and we therefore conclude

$$X+Y \sim \Gamma(k_1+k_2,\theta)$$

The result can be expanded to $n$ independent gamma distributed random variables with the same scale parameter and we get

$$\forall i \in \{1,\ldots, n\} : X_i \sim \Gamma(k_i,\theta) \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \sum_{i=1}^n X_i \sim \Gamma\left(\sum_{i=1}^nk_i,\theta\right).$$

## Entire characteristic functions {#Entire_characteristic_functions}

As defined above, the argument of the characteristic function is treated as a real number: however, certain aspects of the theory of characteristic functions are advanced by extending the definition into the complex plane by [analytic continuation](wikipedia://en/80842), in cases where this is possible.[^n20]

## Characteristic function of a random variable with values in a locally compact Abelian group {#Characteristic_function_of_a_random_variable_with_values_in_a_locally_compact_Abelian_group}

Let $X$ be a locally compact [Abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974), let $Y$ be its [character group](wikipedia://en/1384568), and let $(x, y)$ the value of a character $y \in Y$ at an element $x \in X$. Let $\xi$ be a random variable with values in $X$, and let $\mu$ be its distribution. The characteristic function of the random variable $\xi$ (distribution $\mu$) is defined by[^o77]

$$\mathbf{E}[(\xi, y)]=\widehat{\mu}(y) = \int_X (x, y)\, d\mu(x), \quad y \in Y.$$

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

Related concepts include the [moment-generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment-generating_function) and the [probability-generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability-generating_function). The characteristic function exists for all probability distributions. This is not the case for the moment-generating function.

The characteristic function is closely related to the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247): the characteristic function of a probability density function $p(x)$ is the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) of the [continuous Fourier transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_Fourier_transform) of $p(x)$ (according to the usual convention; see [continuous Fourier transform – other conventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_Fourier_transform#Other_conventions)).

$$\varphi_X(t) = \langle e^{itX} \rangle = \int_{\mathbf{R}} e^{itx}p(x)\, dx = \overline{\left( \int_{\mathbf{R}} e^{-itx}p(x)\, dx \right)} = \overline{P(t)},$$

where $P(t)$ denotes the [continuous Fourier transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_Fourier_transform) of the probability density function $p(x)$. Likewise, $p(x)$ may be recovered from $\varphi_{X}(t)$ through the inverse Fourier transform:

$$p(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{\mathbf{R}} e^{itx} P(t)\, dt = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{\mathbf{R}} e^{itx} \overline{\varphi_X(t)}\, dt.$$

Indeed, even when the random variable does not have a density, the characteristic function may be seen as the Fourier transform of the measure corresponding to the random variable.

Another related concept is the representation of probability distributions as elements of a [reproducing kernel Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/651196) via the [kernel embedding of distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_embedding_of_distributions). This framework may be viewed as a generalization of the characteristic function under specific choices of the [kernel function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_function).
