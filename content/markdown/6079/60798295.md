# Data sonification

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Data sonification"}

*Image: Geiger counter sound KCl.oga.* The sound of a [geiger counter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geiger_counter). One of the first examples of data sonification.

**Data sonification** is the presentation of data as sound using [sonification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonification). It is the auditory equivalent of the more established practice of [data visualization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_visualization).

:::

## Process {#Process}

The usual process for data sonification is directing digital media of a dataset through a [software synthesizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_synthesizer) and into a [digital-to-analog converter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital-to-analog_converter) to produce sound for humans to experience.[^o3][^o4][^o5] Benefits to interpreting data through sonification include accessibility, pattern recognition, education, and artistic expression.[^o12]

::: {#o7 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Applications of data sonification include astronomy studies of star creation,[^o8] interpreting [cluster analysis](wikipedia://en/669675),[^o9] and geoscience.[^o10] Various projects describe the production of sonifications as a collaboration between scientists and musicians.[^o11][^o12][^o13]

A target demographic for using data sonification is the blind community because of the inaccessibility of data visualizations.[^o14]

One of the earliest examples of data sonification is the [Geiger counter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geiger_counter), which measures ionizing radiation through sound.[^o15] Another notable example of data sonification is [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA)'s processing of images from space telescopes into sounds.[^o16]

:::
