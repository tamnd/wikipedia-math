# Capacity of a set

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Capacity of a set"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **capacity of a set** in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is a measure of the "size" of that set. Unlike, say, [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198), which measures a set's [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) or physical extent, capacity is a mathematical analogue of a set's ability to hold [electrical charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_charge). More precisely, it is the [capacitance](wikipedia://en/140711) of the set: the total charge a set can hold while maintaining a given [potential energy](wikipedia://en/23703). The potential energy is computed with respect to an idealized ground at infinity for the **harmonic** or **Newtonian capacity**, and with respect to a surface for the **condenser capacity**.

:::

## Historical note {#Historical_note}

The notion of capacity of a set and of "capacitable" set was introduced by [Gustave Choquet](wikipedia://en/17301193) in 1950: for a detailed account, see reference (Choquet 1986).

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

### Condenser capacity {#Condenser_capacity}

Let Σ be a [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_surface), smooth, (*n* − 1)-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) [hypersurface](wikipedia://en/525101) in *n*-dimensional Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$, $n \ge 3; K$ will denote the *n*-dimensional [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) (i.e., [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) and [bounded](wikipedia://en/48258)) set of which Σ is the [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824). Let *S* be another (*n* − 1)-dimensional hypersurface that encloses Σ: in reference to its origins in [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism), the pair (Σ, *S*) is known as a [condenser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacitor). The **condenser capacity** of Σ relative to *S*, denoted *C*(Σ, *S*) or cap(Σ, *S*), is given by the surface integral

$$C(\Sigma, S) = - \frac1{(n - 2) \sigma_{n}} \int_{S'} \frac{\partial u}{\partial \nu}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma',$$

where:

- *u* is the unique [harmonic function](wikipedia://en/55762) defined on the region *D* between Σ and *S* with the [boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_condition) *u*(*x*) = 1 on Σ and *u*(*x*) = 0 on *S*;
- *S* is any intermediate surface between Σ and *S*;
- $\nu$ is the outward [unit normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_normal) [field](wikipedia://en/62641) to *S* and

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial \nu} (x) = \nabla u (x) \cdot \nu (x)$$

is the [normal derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_derivative) of *u* across *S*; and

- *σ*<sub>*n*</sub> = 2*π* <sup>*n*/2</sup> / Γ(*n* ⁄ 2) is the surface area of the [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090) in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

*C*(Σ, *S*) can be equivalently defined by the volume integral

$$C(\Sigma, S) = \frac1{(n - 2) \sigma_{n}} \int_{D} | \nabla u |^{2}\mathrm{d}x.$$

The condenser capacity also has a [variational characterization](wikipedia://en/171882): *C*(Σ, *S*) is the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) of the [Dirichlet's energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet's_energy) [functional](wikipedia://en/1374948)

$$I[v] = \frac1{(n - 2) \sigma_{n}} \int_{D} | \nabla v |^{2}\mathrm{d}x$$

over all [continuously differentiable functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) *v* on *D* with *v*(*x*) = 1 on Σ and *v*(*x*) = 0 on *S*.

### Harmonic capacity {#Harmonic_capacity}

[Heuristically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic), the harmonic capacity of *K*, the region bounded by Σ, can be found by taking the condenser capacity of Σ with respect to infinity. More precisely, let *u* be the harmonic function in the complement of *K* satisfying *u* = 1 on Σ and *u*(*x*) → 0 as *x* → ∞. Thus *u* is the [Newtonian potential](wikipedia://en/814816) of the simple layer Σ. Then the **harmonic capacity** or **Newtonian capacity** of *K*, denoted *C*(*K*) or cap(*K*), is then defined by

$$C(K) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n\setminus K} |\nabla u|^2\mathrm{d}x.$$

If *S* is a [rectifiable hypersurface](wikipedia://en/2644238) completely enclosing *K*, then the harmonic capacity can be equivalently rewritten as the integral over *S* of the outward normal derivative of *u*:

$$C(K) = \int_S \frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.$$

The harmonic capacity can also be understood as a limit of the condenser capacity. To wit, let *S*<sub>*r*</sub> denote the [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) of radius *r* about the origin in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Since *K* is bounded, for sufficiently large *r*, *S*<sub>*r*</sub> will enclose *K* and (Σ, *S*<sub>*r*</sub>) will form a condenser pair. The harmonic capacity is then the [limit](wikipedia://en/285759) as *r* tends to infinity:

$$C(K) = \lim_{r \to \infty} C(\Sigma, S_{r}).$$

The harmonic capacity is a mathematically abstract version of the [electrostatic capacity](wikipedia://en/140711) of the conductor *K* and is always non-negative and finite: 0 ≤ *C*(*K*) \< +∞.

The **Wiener capacity** or **Robin constant** *W(K)* of *K* is given by

$$C(K) = e^{-W(K)}$$

### Logarithmic capacity {#Logarithmic_capacity}

In two dimensions, the capacity is defined as above, but dropping the factor of $(n-2)$ in the definition:

$$C(\Sigma, S) = - \frac1{2\pi} \int_{S'} \frac{\partial u}{\partial \nu}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma' = \frac1{2\pi} \int_{D} | \nabla u |^{2}\mathrm{d}x$$

This is often called the **logarithmic capacity**, the term *logarithmic* arises, as the potential function goes from being an inverse power to a logarithm in the $n\to 2$ limit. This is articulated below. It may also be called the **conformal capacity**, in reference to its relation to the [conformal radius](wikipedia://en/25952580).

## Properties {#Properties}

The harmonic function *u* is called the **capacity potential**. It is called the [Newtonian potential](wikipedia://en/814816) when $n \ge 3$ and called the **logarithmic potential** when $n=2$. It can be obtained via a [Green's function](wikipedia://en/311001) *G* as

$$u(x)=\int_S G(x-y)d\mu(y)$$

with *x* a point exterior to *S*, where *G* is defined as

$$G(x-y)=\frac1{|x-y|^{n-2}}$$

when $n\ge 3$ and

$$G(x-y)=\log\frac1{|x-y|}$$

for $n=2$.

The [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) $\mu$ is called the **capacitary measure** or **equilibrium measure**. It is generally taken to be a [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322). It is related to the capacity as

$$C(K)=\int_Sd\mu(y)=\mu(S)$$

The variational definition of capacity over the [Dirichlet energy](wikipedia://en/14377820) can be re-expressed as

$$C(K)=\left[\inf_\lambda E(\lambda)\right]^{-1}$$

with the infimum taken over all positive Borel measures $\lambda$ concentrated on *K*, normalized so that $\lambda(K)=1$ and with $E(\lambda)$ is the energy integral

$$E(\lambda)=\int\int_{K\times K} G(x-y) d\lambda(x) d\lambda(y)$$

:::

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The characterization of the capacity of a set as the minimum of an [energy functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_functional) achieving particular boundary values, given above, can be extended to other energy functionals in the [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882).

### Divergence form elliptic operators {#Divergence_form_elliptic_operators}

Solutions to a uniformly [elliptic partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/662787) with divergence form

$$\nabla \cdot ( A \nabla u ) = 0$$

are minimizers of the associated energy functional

$$I[u] = \int_D (\nabla u)^T A (\nabla u)\,\mathrm{d}x$$

subject to appropriate boundary conditions.

The capacity of a set *E* with respect to a domain *D* containing *E* is defined as the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) of the energy over all [continuously differentiable functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) *v* on *D* with *v*(*x*) = 1 on *E*; and *v*(*x*) = 0 on the boundary of *D*.

The minimum energy is achieved by a function known as the *capacitary potential* of *E* with respect to *D*, and it solves the [obstacle problem](wikipedia://en/19487617) on *D* with the obstacle function provided by the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of *E*. The capacitary potential is alternately characterized as the unique solution of the equation with the appropriate boundary conditions.
