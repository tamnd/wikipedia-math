# Grothendieck category

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Grothendieck category"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Grothendieck category** is a certain kind of [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063), introduced in [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042)'s [Tôhoku paper of 1957](wikipedia://en/44572732)[^o2] in order to develop the machinery of [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410) for [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) and for [sheaves](wikipedia://en/245466) in a unified manner. The theory of these categories was further developed in [Pierre Gabriel](wikipedia://en/30716698)'s 1962 thesis.[^o3]

:::

To every [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) $V$ one can associate a Grothendieck category $\operatorname{Qcoh}(V)$, consisting of the [quasi-coherent sheaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-coherent_sheaf) on $V$. This category encodes all the relevant geometric information about $V$, and $V$ can be recovered from $\operatorname{Qcoh}(V)$ (the [Gabriel–Rosenberg reconstruction theorem](wikipedia://en/57426815)). This example gives rise to one approach to [noncommutative algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/25493347): the study of "non-commutative varieties" is then nothing but the study of (certain) Grothendieck categories.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

By definition, a Grothendieck category $\mathcal{A}$ is an [AB5 category](wikipedia://en/30051646) with a [generator](wikipedia://en/9550415). Spelled out, this means that

- $\mathcal{A}$ is an [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063);
- every (possibly infinite) family of objects in $\mathcal{A}$ has a [coproduct](wikipedia://en/310889) (also known as direct sum) in $\mathcal{A}$;
- [direct limits](wikipedia://en/211566) of [short exact sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) are exact; this means that if a direct system of [short exact sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) in $\mathcal{A}$ is given, then the induced sequence of direct limits is a short exact sequence as well. (Direct limits are always [right-exact](wikipedia://en/453945); the important point here is that we require them to be [left-exact](wikipedia://en/453945) as well.)
- $\mathcal{A}$ possesses a generator, i.e. there is an object $G$ in $\mathcal{A}$ such that $\operatorname{Hom}(G,-)$ is a [faithful functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faithful_functor) from $\mathcal{A}$ to the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829). (In our situation, this is equivalent to saying that every object $X$ of $\mathcal{A}$ admits an [epimorphism](wikipedia://en/59539) $G^{(I)}\rightarrow X$, where $G^{(I)}$ denotes a direct sum of copies of $G$, one for each element of the (possibly infinite) set $I$.)

The name "Grothendieck category" appeared neither in Grothendieck's Tôhoku paper[^o2] nor in Gabriel's thesis;[^o3] it came into use in the second half of the 1960s in the work of several authors, including Jan-Erik Roos, Bo Stenström, Ulrich Oberst, and Bodo Pareigis. (Some authors use a different definition, not requiring the existence of a generator.)

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- The prototypical example of a Grothendieck category is the [category of abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454748); the abelian group $\mathbb{Z}$ of integers is a generator.
- More generally, given any [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$ (associative, with $1$, but not necessarily commutative), the category $\operatorname{Mod}(R)$ of all right (or alternatively: left) [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) over $R$ is a Grothendieck category; $R$ itself is a generator.
- Given a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$, the category of all [sheaves](wikipedia://en/245466) of abelian groups on $X$ is a Grothendieck category.[^o2] (More generally: the category of all sheaves of right $R$-modules on $X$ is a Grothendieck category for any ring $R$.)
- Given a [ringed space](wikipedia://en/248014) $(X,\mathcal{O}_X)$, the category of [sheaves of *O<sub>X</sub>*-modules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheaves_of_modules) is a Grothendieck category.[^o2]
- Given an (affine or projective) [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) $V$ (or more generally: any [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) or [algebraic stack](wikipedia://en/717377)), the category $\operatorname{Qcoh}(V)$ of [quasi-coherent sheaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-coherent_sheaf) on $V$ is a Grothendieck category.[^o9]
- Given a small site (*C*, *J*) (i.e. a small category *C* together with a [Grothendieck topology](wikipedia://en/12910) *J*), the category of all sheaves of abelian groups on the site is a Grothendieck category.

### Constructing further Grothendieck categories {#Constructing_further_Grothendieck_categories}

- Any category that's [equivalent](wikipedia://en/379051) to a Grothendieck category is itself a Grothendieck category.
- Given Grothendieck categories $\mathcal{A_1},\ldots,\mathcal{A_n}$, the [product category](wikipedia://en/4587226) $\mathcal{A_1}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{A_n}$ is a Grothendieck category.
- Given a [small category](wikipedia://en/53759#Small_and_large_categories) $\mathcal{C}$ and a Grothendieck category $\mathcal{A}$, the [functor category](wikipedia://en/407039) $\operatorname{Funct}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})$, consisting of all [covariant functors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariant_functor) from $\mathcal{C}$ to $\mathcal{A}$, is a Grothendieck category.[^o2]
- Given a small [preadditive](wikipedia://en/59521) category $\mathcal{C}$ and a Grothendieck category $\mathcal{A}$, the functor category $\operatorname{Add}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})$ of all additive covariant functors from $\mathcal{C}$ to $\mathcal{A}$ is a Grothendieck category.[^o11]
- If $\mathcal{A}$ is a Grothendieck category and $\mathcal{C}$ is a [localizing subcategory](wikipedia://en/44974120) of $\mathcal{A}$, then both $\mathcal{C}$ and the [Serre quotient category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serre_quotient_category) $\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{C}$ are Grothendieck categories.[^o3]

## Properties and theorems {#Properties_and_theorems}

Every Grothendieck category contains an [injective cogenerator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective_cogenerator). For example, an injective cogenerator of the category of abelian groups is the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$.

Every object in a Grothendieck category $\mathcal{A}$ has an [injective hull](wikipedia://en/665821) in $\mathcal{A}$.[^o2][^o3] This allows to construct [injective resolutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective_resolution) and thereby the use of the tools of [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410) in $\mathcal{A}$, in order to define [derived functors](wikipedia://en/433875). (Note that not all Grothendieck categories allow [projective resolutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_resolution) for all objects; examples are categories of sheaves of abelian groups on many topological spaces, such as on the space of real numbers.)

In a Grothendieck category, any family of [subobjects](wikipedia://en/540476) $(U_i)$ of a given object $X$ has a [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) (or "sum") $\sum_i U_i$ as well as an [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) (or "intersection") $\cap_i U_i$, both of which are again subobjects of $X$. Further, if the family $(U_i)$ is directed (i.e. for any two objects in the family, there is a third object in the family that contains the two), and $V$ is another subobject of $X$, we have[^o14]

$$\sum_{i}(U_i\cap V) = \left(\sum_{i}U_i\right) \cap V.$$

Grothendieck categories are [well-powered](wikipedia://en/540476) (sometimes called *locally small*, although that term is also used for a different concept), i.e. the collection of subobjects of any given object forms a set (rather than a [proper class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_class)).[^o11]

It is a rather deep result that every Grothendieck category $\mathcal{A}$ is [complete](wikipedia://en/62781),[^o15] i.e. that arbitrary [limits](wikipedia://en/39248) (and in particular [products](wikipedia://en/237868)) exist in $\mathcal{A}$. By contrast, it follows directly from the definition that $\mathcal{A}$ is co-complete, i.e. that arbitrary [colimits](wikipedia://en/39248) and [coproducts](wikipedia://en/310889) (direct sums) exist in $\mathcal{A}$. Coproducts in a Grothendieck category are exact (i.e. the coproduct of a family of short exact sequences is again a short exact sequence), but products need not be exact.

A functor $F \colon {\cal A} \to {\cal X}$ from a Grothendieck category $\mathcal{A}$ to an arbitrary category $\mathcal{X}$ has a [left adjoint](wikipedia://en/53991) if and only if it commutes with all limits, and it has a right adjoint if and only if it commutes with all colimits. This follows from [Peter J. Freyd](wikipedia://en/4593361)'s *special adjoint functor theorem* and its dual.[^o16]

The [Gabriel–Popescu theorem](wikipedia://en/35884241) states that any Grothendieck category $\mathcal{A}$ is equivalent to a [full subcategory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_subcategory) of the category $\operatorname{Mod}(R)$ of right modules over some unital ring $R$ (which can be taken to be the [endomorphism ring](wikipedia://en/59623) of a generator of $\mathcal{A}$), and $\mathcal{A}$ can be obtained as a [Gabriel quotient](wikipedia://en/48897477) of $\operatorname{Mod}(R)$ by some [localizing subcategory](wikipedia://en/44974120).[^o17]

As a consequence of Gabriel–Popescu, one can show that every Grothendieck category is [locally presentable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_presentable_category).[^o18] Furthermore, Gabriel-Popescu can be used to see that every Grothendieck category is complete, being a [reflective subcategory](wikipedia://en/3967424) of the complete category $\operatorname{Mod}(R)$ for some $R$.

Every small abelian category $\mathcal{C}$ can be embedded in a Grothendieck category, in the following fashion. The category $\mathcal{A}:=\operatorname{Lex}(\mathcal{C}^{op},\mathrm{Ab})$ of [left-exact](wikipedia://en/453945) additive (covariant) functors $\mathcal{C}^{op}\rightarrow\mathrm{Ab}$ (where $\mathrm{Ab}$ denotes the [category of abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454748)) is a Grothendieck category, and the functor $h\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}$, with $C\mapsto h_C=\operatorname{Hom}(-,C)$, is full, faithful and exact. A generator of $\mathcal{A}$ is given by the coproduct of all $h_C$, with $C\in\mathcal{C}$.[^o3] The category $\mathcal{A}$ is equivalent to the category $\text{Ind}(\mathcal C)$ of [ind-objects](wikipedia://en/4322449) of $\mathcal{C}$ and the embedding $h$ corresponds to the natural embedding $\mathcal{C}\to\text{Ind}(\mathcal C)$. We may therefore view $\mathcal{A}$ as the co-completion of $\mathcal{C}$.

### Special kinds of objects and Grothendieck categories {#Special_kinds_of_objects_and_Grothendieck_categories}

An object $X$ in a Grothendieck category is called *finitely generated* if, whenever $X$ is written as the sum of a family of subobjects of $X$, then it is already the sum of a finite subfamily. (In the case ${\cal A} = \operatorname{Mod}(R)$ of module categories, this notion is equivalent to the familiar notion of [finitely generated modules](wikipedia://en/326430).) Epimorphic images of finitely generated objects are again finitely generated. If $U\subseteq X$ and both $U$ and $X/U$ are finitely generated, then so is $X$. The object $X$ is finitely generated if, and only if, for any directed system $(A_i)$ in ${\cal A}$ in which each morphism is a monomorphism, the natural morphism $\varinjlim \mathrm{Hom}(X,A_i)\to \mathrm{Hom}(X,\varinjlim A_i)$ is an isomorphism.[^o20] A Grothendieck category need not contain any non-zero finitely generated objects.

A Grothendieck category is called *locally finitely generated* if it has a set of finitely generated generators (i.e. if there exists a family $(G_i)_{i\in I}$ of finitely generated objects such that to every object $X$ there exist $i\in I$ and a non-zero morphism $G_{i}\rightarrow X$; equivalently: $X$ is epimorphic image of a direct sum of copies of the $G_{i}$). In such a category, every object is the sum of its finitely generated subobjects.[^o11] Every category ${\cal A} = \operatorname{Mod}(R)$ is locally finitely generated.

An object $X$ in a Grothendieck category is called *finitely presented* if it is finitely generated and if every epimorphism $W\to X$ with finitely generated domain $W$ has a finitely generated kernel. Again, this generalizes the notion of [finitely presented modules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_presented_module). If $U\subseteq X$ and both $U$ and $X/U$ are finitely presented, then so is $X$. In a locally finitely generated Grothendieck category ${\cal A}$, the finitely presented objects can be characterized as follows:[^o21] $X$ in ${\cal A}$ is finitely presented if, and only if, for every directed system $(A_i)$ in ${\cal A}$, the natural morphism $\varinjlim \mathrm{Hom}(X,A_i)\to \mathrm{Hom}(X,\varinjlim A_i)$ is an isomorphism.

A Grothendieck category is *locally finitely presented* if it has a set of finitely presented generators, which is equivalent to saying that every object is a direct limit of finitely presented objects.

An object $X$ in a Grothendieck category ${\cal A}$ is called *coherent* if it is finitely presented and if each of its finitely generated subobjects is also finitely presented.[^o22] (This generalizes the notion of [coherent sheaves](wikipedia://en/603780) on a ringed space.) The full subcategory of all coherent objects in ${\cal A}$ is abelian and the inclusion functor is [exact](wikipedia://en/453945).[^o22]

An object $X$ in a Grothendieck category is called *[Noetherian](wikipedia://en/150169)* if the set of its subobjects satisfies the [ascending chain condition](wikipedia://en/3189), i.e. if every sequence $X_1\subseteq X_2 \subseteq \cdots$ of subobjects of $X$ eventually becomes stationary. This is the case if and only if every subobject of X is finitely generated. (In the case ${\cal A} = \operatorname{Mod}(R)$, this notion is equivalent to the familiar notion of [Noetherian modules](wikipedia://en/542431).) A Grothendieck category is called *locally Noetherian* if it has a set of Noetherian generators; an example is the category of left modules over a left-[Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129).

A Grothendieck category is called *spectral* if every short exact sequence [splits](wikipedia://en/1531404). To a given Grothendieck category ${\cal A}$ one can define a spectral Grothendieck category $\operatorname{Spec} {\cal A}$ and a canonical functor $P : {\cal A} \to \operatorname{Spec} {\cal A}$. The objects of $\operatorname{Spec} {\cal A}$ correspond, up to isomorphism, to the injective objects of ${\cal A}$.[^o23] The functor $P$ turns all [essential monomorphisms](wikipedia://en/47451366) into isomorphisms and thus identifies each object of ${\cal A}$ with its injective hull. This allows to define useful dimension-like functions on ${\cal A}$: for each indecomposable injective object $I$ in ${\cal A}$ and every object $A$ in ${\cal A}$, the [cardinal number](wikipedia://en/6173) $r_I(A)$ measures, roughly speaking, how many direct summands isomorphic to *$I$* appear in the injective hull of $A$.
