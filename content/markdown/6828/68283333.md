# Diameter of a set

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Diameter of a set"}

*Image: Diameter of a Set.svg.* Diameter of a set

In mathematics, the **diameter of a set** of points in a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) is the largest distance between points in the set. As an important special case, the **diameter of a metric space** is the largest distance between any two points in the space. This generalizes the [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) of a circle, the largest distance between two points on the circle. This usage of diameter also occurs in medical terminology concerning a [lesion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesion) or in [geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology) concerning a rock.

:::

A [bounded set](wikipedia://en/48258) is a set whose diameter is finite. Within a bounded set, all distances are at most the diameter.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

The diameter of an object is the [least upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_upper_bound) (denoted "sup") of the set of all distances between pairs of points in the object. Explicitly, if $S$ is a set of points with distances measured by a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) $\rho$, the diameter is

$$\operatorname{diam}(S) = \sup_{x, y \in S} \rho(x, y).$$

### Of the empty set {#Of_the_empty_set}

The diameter of the empty set is a matter of convention. It can be defined to be zero, $-\infty$, or undefined.

:::

## In Euclidean spaces {#In_Euclidean_spaces}

For any bounded set in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) or [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), the diameter of the object or set is the same as the diameter of its [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634). For any [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) shape in the [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29), the diameter is the largest distance that can be formed between two opposite [parallel lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_lines) [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to its boundary.

::: {#o7 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Isodiametric inequality"}

### Relation to other measures {#Relation_to_other_measures}

The diameter of a circle is exactly twice its radius. However, this is true only for a circle, and only in the [Euclidean metric](wikipedia://en/53932). [Jung's theorem](wikipedia://en/7503941) provides more general inequalities relating the diameter to the radius. The *isodiametric inequality* or *Bieberbach inequality*, a relative of the [isoperimetric inequality](wikipedia://en/326182), states that, for a given diameter, the planar shape with the largest area is a disk, and the three-dimensional shape with the largest volume is a sphere.[^n1] The [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621) of maximum area for a given diameter and number of sides are the [biggest little polygons](wikipedia://en/33751968).

Just as the diameter of a two-dimensional convex set is the largest distance between two parallel lines tangent to and enclosing the set, the width is often defined to be the smallest such distance. The diameter and width are equal only for a [body of constant width](wikipedia://en/580238), for which all pairs of parallel tangent lines have the same distance. Every set of bounded diameter in the Euclidean plane is a subset of a body of constant width with the same diameter.

:::

::: {#o10 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Computation"}

### Computation {#Computation}

The diameter or width of a two-dimensional point set or polygon can be calculated efficiently using [rotating calipers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotating_calipers). Algorithms for computing diameters in higher-dimensional Euclidean spaces have also been studied in [computational geometry](wikipedia://en/176927); see [diameter (computational geometry)](wikipedia://en/78733484).

:::

::: {#o12 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Diameter of a manifold"}

## In differential geometry {#In_differential_geometry}

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), the diameter is an important global [Riemannian](wikipedia://en/195243) [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638). Every [compact set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set) in a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652), and every compact Riemannian manifold itself, has finite diameter. For instance, the [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090) of any dimension, viewed as a Riemannian manifold, has diameter $\pi$. This differs from its diameter as a subset of Euclidean space (which would equal two) because, as a Riemannian manifold, distances are measured along [geodesics](wikipedia://en/91096) within the manifold.

In a Riemannian manifold whose [Ricci curvature](wikipedia://en/276582) has a positive constant lower bound, the diameter is also bounded by [Myers's theorem](wikipedia://en/932460). According to [Cheng's maximal diameter theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheng's_maximal_diameter_theorem), the unique manifold with the largest diameter for a given curvature lower bound is a sphere with that curvature. The theorem is named after [Shiu-Yuen Cheng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiu-Yuen_Cheng), who published it in 1975.

:::

## In graphs {#In_graphs}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the diameter of a connected undirected graph is the farthest distance between any two of its vertices. That is, it is the diameter of a set, for the set of vertices of the graph, and for the [shortest-path distance](wikipedia://en/1020021) in the graph. Diameter may be considered either for weighted or for unweighted graphs. Researchers have studied the problem of computing the diameter, both in arbitrary graphs and in special classes of graphs.

Special cases of graph diameter include [the diameter of a group](wikipedia://en/47277561), defined using a [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) with the largest diameter possible for a given group, and the [diameter of the flip graph](wikipedia://en/75252094#Diameter_of_the_flip_graph) of triangulations of a point set, the minimum number of local moves needed to transform one triangulation into another for two triangulations chosen to be as far apart as possible.
