# Force of infection

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Force of infection"}

In [epidemiology](wikipedia://en/66997), **force of infection** (denoted $\lambda$) is the rate at which [susceptible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susceptible_individual) individuals acquire an [infectious disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infectious_disease).[^o2] Because it takes account of susceptibility it can be used to compare the rate of transmission between different [groups](wikipedia://en/1509191) of the [population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_%28human_biology%29) for the same infectious disease, or even between different infectious diseases. That is to say, $\lambda$ is directly proportional to $\beta$; the effective [transmission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_%28medicine%29) rate.

:::

$$\lambda = \frac {\mbox{number of new infections}} {\mbox{number of susceptible persons exposed} \times \mbox{average duration of exposure}}$$

Such a calculation is difficult because not all new infections are reported, and it is often difficult to know how many susceptibles were exposed. However, $\lambda$ can be calculated for an infectious disease in an [endemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endemic_%28epidemiology%29) state if homogeneous mixing of the population and a rectangular population distribution (such as that generally found in developed countries), rather than a [pyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_Pyramid), is assumed. In this case, $\lambda$ is given by:

$$\lambda = \frac {1} {A}$$

where $A$ is the average age of infection. In other words, $A$ is the average time spent in the susceptible group before becoming infected. The rate of becoming infected ($\lambda$) is therefore $1/A$ (since rate is 1/time). The advantage of this method of calculating $\lambda$ is that data on the average age of infection is very easily obtainable, even if not all cases of the disease are reported.
