# Carmichael's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Carmichael's theorem"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **Carmichael's theorem**, named after the American mathematician [R. D. Carmichael](wikipedia://en/605140), states that, for any nondegenerate [Lucas sequence](wikipedia://en/734635) of the first kind *U<sub>n</sub>*(*P*, *Q*) with [relatively prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_prime) parameters *P*, *Q* and positive discriminant, an element *U<sub>n</sub>* with *n* ≠ 1, 2, 6 has at least one [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) divisor that does not divide any earlier one except the 12th [Fibonacci number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number) F(12) = *U*<sub>12</sub>(1, −1) = 144 and its equivalent *U*<sub>12</sub>(−1, −1) = −144.

:::

In particular, for *n* greater than 12, the *n*th [Fibonacci number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number) F(*n*) has at least one prime divisor that does not divide any earlier Fibonacci number.

Carmichael (1913, Theorem 21) [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) this [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977). Recently, Yabuta (2001)[^o2] gave a simple proof. Bilu, Hanrot, Voutier and Mignotte (2001)[^o3] extended it to the case of negative discriminants (where it is true for all *n* > 30).

::: {#o5 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Given two relatively prime integers *P* and *Q*, such that $D=P^2-4Q>0$ and $PQ \ne 0$, let $U_{n}(P, Q)$ be the [Lucas sequence](wikipedia://en/734635) of the first kind defined by

$$\begin{aligned} U_0(P,Q)&=0, \\ U_1(P,Q)&=1, \\ U_n(P,Q)&=P\cdot U_{n-1}(P,Q)-Q\cdot U_{n-2}(P,Q) \qquad\mbox{ for }n>1. \end{aligned}$$

Then, for *n* ≠ 1, 2, 6, *U<sub>n</sub>*(*P*, *Q*) has at least one prime divisor that does not divide any *U<sub>m</sub>*(*P*, *Q*) with *m* \< *n*, except *U*<sub>12</sub>(±1, −1) = ±F(12) = ±144. Such a prime *p* is called a *characteristic factor* or a *primitive prime divisor* of *U<sub>n</sub>*(*P*, *Q*). Indeed, Carmichael showed a slightly stronger theorem: For *n* ≠ 1, 2, 6, *U<sub>n</sub>*(*P*, *Q*) has at least one primitive prime divisor not dividing *D*[^o6] except *U*<sub>3</sub>(±1, −2) = 3, *U*<sub>5</sub>(±1, −1) = F(5) = 5, or *U*<sub>12</sub>(1, −1) = −*U*<sub>12</sub>(−1, −1) = F(12) = 144.

In Carmichael's theorem, *D* should be greater than 0; thus the cases *U*<sub>13</sub>(1, 2), *U*<sub>18</sub>(1, 2) and *U*<sub>30</sub>(1, 2), etc. are not included, since in this case *D* = −7 \< 0.

:::

## Fibonacci and Pell cases {#Fibonacci_and_Pell_cases}

The only exceptions in Fibonacci case for *n* up to 12 are:

F(1) = 1 and F(2) = 1, which have no prime divisors

F(6) = 8, whose only prime divisor is 2 (which is F(3))

F(12) = 144, whose only prime divisors are 2 (which is F(3)) and 3 (which is F(4))

The smallest primitive prime divisor of F(*n*) are

1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 1, 13, 7, 17, 11, 89, 1, 233, 29, 61, 47, 1597, 19, 37, 41, 421, 199, 28657, 23, 3001, 521, 53, 281, 514229, 31, 557, 2207, 19801, 3571, 141961, 107, 73, 9349, 135721, 2161, 2789, 211, 433494437, 43, 109441, ...

Carmichael's [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) says that every Fibonacci number, apart from the exceptions listed above, has at least one primitive prime divisor.

If *n* > 1, then the *n*th [Pell number](wikipedia://en/1355482) has at least one prime divisor that does not divide any earlier Pell number. The smallest primitive prime divisor of *n*th Pell number are

1, 2, 5, 3, 29, 7, 13, 17, 197, 41, 5741, 11, 33461, 239, 269, 577, 137, 199, 37, 19, 45697, 23, 229, 1153, 1549, 79, 53, 113, 44560482149, 31, 61, 665857, 52734529, 103, 1800193921, 73, 593, 9369319, 389, 241, ...
