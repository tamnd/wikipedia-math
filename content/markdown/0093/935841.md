# Albert W. Tucker

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Albert W. Tucker"}

**Albert William Tucker** (28 November 1905 – 25 January 1995) was a Canadian [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who made important contributions in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), [game theory](wikipedia://en/11924), and [non-linear programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linear_programming).[^o4]

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Albert Tucker was born in [Oshawa, Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oshawa,_Ontario), Canada, and earned his [B.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts) at the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto) in 1928 and his [M.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts) at the same institution in 1929.[^o6] In 1932, he earned his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) under the supervision of [Solomon Lefschetz](wikipedia://en/384384), with a dissertation entitled *An Abstract Approach to Manifolds*.[^o7] In 1932–33 he was a National Research Fellow at [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge), [Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard), and then [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago).

## Career {#Career}

Tucker then returned to Princeton to join the faculty in 1933, where he stayed until 1974. He chaired the mathematics department for about twenty years, one of the longest tenures. His extensive relationships within the field made him a great source for oral histories of the mathematics community.

In 1950, Albert Tucker gave the name and interpretation "[prisoner's dilemma](wikipedia://en/43717)" to [Merrill M. Flood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merrill_M._Flood) and [Melvin Dresher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvin_Dresher)'s model of cooperation and conflict, resulting in the most well-known game theoretic paradox.[^n1] He is also well known for the [Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions](wikipedia://en/2397362), a basic result in non-linear programming, which was published in conference proceedings, rather than in a journal.

In the 1960s, he was heavily involved in mathematics education, as chair of the [AP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Placement_Program) Calculus committee for the College Board (1960–1963), through work with the Committee on the Undergraduate Program in Mathematics (CUPM) of the [MAA](wikipedia://en/198772) (he was president of the MAA in 1961–1962), and through many [NSF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation) summer workshops for high school and college teachers. [George B. Thomas Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_B._Thomas) acknowledged Tucker's contribution of many exercises to Thomas's classic textbook, *Calculus and Analytic Geometry.*[^o10]

In the early 1980s, Tucker recruited Princeton history professor [Charles Coulston Gillispie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Coulston_Gillispie) to help him set up an oral history project to preserve stories about the Princeton mathematical community in the 1930s. With funding from the [Sloan Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloan_Foundation), this project later expanded its scope. Among those who shared their memories of such figures as [Einstein](wikipedia://en/736), [von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942), and [Gödel](wikipedia://en/16736) were computer pioneer [Herman Goldstine](wikipedia://en/763708) and Nobel laureates [John Bardeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bardeen) and [Eugene Wigner](wikipedia://en/10101).

## Students and legacy {#Students_and_legacy}

Tucker's Ph.D. students include [Michel Balinski](wikipedia://en/41213698), [David Gale](wikipedia://en/1227675), [Alan J. Goldman](wikipedia://en/50339482), [John Isbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Isbell), [Stephen Maurer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Maurer), Turing Award winner [Marvin Minsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marvin_Minsky), Nobel Prize winner [John Nash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Forbes_Nash), [Torrence Parsons](wikipedia://en/1722759), Nobel Prize winner [Lloyd Shapley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lloyd_Shapley), Robert Singleton, and [Marjorie Stein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marjorie_Stein). Tucker advised and collaborated with [Harold W. Kuhn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_W._Kuhn) on a number of papers and mathematical models.

Tucker noticed the leadership ability and talent of a young mathematics graduate student named [John G. Kemeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_George_Kemeny), whose hiring Tucker suggested to [Dartmouth College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_College). Following Tucker's advice, Dartmouth recruited Kemeny, who became Chair of the Mathematics Department and later College President. Years later, Dartmouth College recognized Albert Tucker with an honorary degree.

Tucker died in [Hightstown, N.J.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hightstown,_New_Jersey) in 1995 at age 89. His sons, [Alan Tucker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Tucker) and [Thomas W. Tucker](wikipedia://en/44249205), and his grandson [Thomas J. Tucker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_J._Tucker) are all professional mathematicians.

### Tucker Prize {#Tucker_Prize}

At each (triennial) International Symposium of the [Mathematical Optimization Society](wikipedia://en/5751407) (MOS) the [Tucker Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tucker_Prize), in honour of A. W. Tucker, is given for outstanding thesis in the area of [discrete mathematics](wikipedia://en/8492).[^o13]
