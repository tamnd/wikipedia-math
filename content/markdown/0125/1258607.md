# Proof assistant

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Proof assistant"}

*Image: CoqProofOfDecidablityOfEqualityOnNaturalNumbers.png.* An interactive proof session in RocqIDE, showing the proof script on the left and the proof state on the right

In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) and [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), a **proof assistant** or **interactive theorem prover** is a software tool to assist with the development of [formal proofs](wikipedia://en/1197184) by human–machine collaboration. This involves some sort of interactive proof editor, or other [interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User_interface), with which a human can guide the search for proofs, the details of which are stored in, and some steps provided by, a [computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer).

:::

A recent effort within this field is making these tools use [artificial intelligence to automate the formalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_in_mathematics) of ordinary mathematics.[^o2]

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Automated proof checking"}

## Automated proof checking {#Automated_proof_checking}

**Automated proof checking** is the process of using software for checking [proofs](wikipedia://en/82285) for correctness. It is one of the most developed fields in [automated reasoning](wikipedia://en/2884728). Automated proof checking differs from [automated theorem proving](wikipedia://en/2546) in that automated proof checking simply mechanically checks the formal workings of an existing proof, instead of trying to develop new proofs or theorems itself. Because of this, the task of automated proof verification is much simpler than that of automated theorem proving, allowing automated proof checking software to be much simpler than automated theorem proving software.

Because of this small size, some automated proof checking systems can have less than a thousand lines of core code, and are thus themselves amenable to both hand-checking and automated software verification. The [Mizar system](wikipedia://en/209074), [HOL Light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HOL_Light), and [Metamath](wikipedia://en/6023946) are examples of automated proof checking systems. Automated proof checking can be done either as a batch operation, or interactively, as part of an [interactive theorem proving](wikipedia://en/1258607) system.

:::

## History {#History}

[Automath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automath), which was developed by [Nicolaas Govert de Bruijn](wikipedia://en/3166399) starting in 1967, is often considered the first proof checker and the first system to utilize the [Curry–Howard correspondence](wikipedia://en/254299) between programs and proofs.[^o6] Automath was used by L.S. van Benthem Jutting in 1977 to formalize Landau's *Foundations of Analysis*, which was the first formalization of the real numbers.[^o7]

In 1973, [Robert Boyer](wikipedia://en/4384637) and [J Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J_Strother_Moore) published *Proving Theorems about LISP Functions* which aimed to verify programs, not mathematics.[^o8] Their theorem prover is now known as [ACL2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACL2).

In the 1970s, [Edinburgh LCF](wikipedia://en/161900) introduced the idea of using a [functional programming language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_programming_language) as the metalanguage for a theorem prover, and led to the [HOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HOL_%28proof_assistant%29) family of proof assistants.[^o7]

The 1990s saw the rise of [Rocq](wikipedia://en/581974), (then known as Coq), which has been used for many large-scale formalization projects. Since the late 2010s, [Lean](wikipedia://en/62889984), a proof assistant strongly influenced by Rocq, has become another popular choice, especially for formalizing mathematics.

- [ACL2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACL2) – a programming language, a first-order logical theory, and a theorem prover (with both interactive and automatic modes) in the Boyer–Moore tradition.
- [HOL theorem provers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HOL_theorem_prover) – A family of tools ultimately derived from the [LCF theorem prover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LCF_theorem_prover). In these systems, the logical core is a library of their programming language. Theorems represent new elements of the language and can only be introduced via "strategies" which guarantee logical correctness. Strategy composition gives users the ability to produce significant proofs with relatively few interactions with the system. Members of the family include:
  - [HOL4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HOL4) – The "primary descendant", still under active development. Support for both [Moscow ML](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_ML) and [Poly/ML](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poly/ML). Has a [BSD-style license](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BSD-style_license).
  - [HOL Light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HOL_Light) – A thriving "minimalist fork". [OCaml](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OCaml) based.
  - ProofPower – Went proprietary, then returned to open source. Based on [Standard ML](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_ML).
- IMPS, An Interactive Mathematical Proof System.[^o31]
- [Isabelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isabelle_theorem_prover) is an interactive theorem prover where other systems can be encoded. Isabelle/HOL is its most popular instance, whose foundation is close to that of the HOL prover. Other instances include Isabelle/ZF and Isabelle/FOL[^o32]. The main code-base is BSD-licensed, but the Isabelle distribution bundles many add-on tools with different licenses.
- [Jape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jape_%28software%29) – Java based.
- [Lean](wikipedia://en/62889984) is both an interactive theorem prover and a functional, dependently-typed programming language. It is based on the [calculus of inductive constructions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus_of_inductive_constructions) with non-cumulative universes. Since version 4 (released in 2023), it is self-hosting. It can be used to formalize mathematics (and has a large, coherent library for formal mathematics), but also for software and hardware verification.
- [LEGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LEGO_%28proof_assistant%29)
- [Matita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matita) – A light system based on the calculus of inductive constructions.
- [MINLOG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MINLOG) – A proof assistant based on first-order minimal logic.
- [Mizar](wikipedia://en/209074) – A proof assistant based on first-order logic, in a [natural deduction](wikipedia://en/51072) style, and [Tarski–Grothendieck set theory](wikipedia://en/6105873).
- [PhoX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhoX) – A proof assistant based on higher-order logic which is eXtensible.
- [Prototype Verification System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prototype_Verification_System) (PVS) – a proof language and system based on higher-order logic.
- [Rocq](wikipedia://en/581974) (formerly named *Coq*) – A popular interactive theorem prover based on the calculus of inductive constructions.
- [Theorem Proving System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorem_Proving_System) (TPS) and ETPS – Interactive theorem provers also based on simply typed lambda calculus, but based on an independent [formulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q0_Logic) of the logical theory and independent implementation.

## User interfaces {#User_interfaces}

A commonly used front-end for proof assistants was the [Emacs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emacs)-based Proof General, developed at the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh). Nowadays, many provers include their own editor. Rocq includes RocqIDE, which is based on OCaml/[Gtk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gtk). Isabelle includes Isabelle/jEdit, which is based on [jEdit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JEdit) and the Isabelle/[Scala](wikipedia://en/3254510) infrastructure for document-oriented proof processing. More recently, [Visual Studio Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_Studio_Code) extensions have been developed for Rocq,[^o34] Isabelle by Makarius Wenzel,[^o35] and for Lean 4 by the leanprover developers.[^o36]

## Formalization extent {#Formalization_extent}

Freek Wiedijk has been keeping a ranking of proof assistants by the amount of formalized theorems out of a list of 100 well-known theorems. As of September 2025, only six systems have formalized proofs of more than 70% of the theorems, namely Isabelle, HOL Light, Lean, Rocq, Metamath and Mizar.[^o38][^o39]
