# Pseudogroup

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pseudogroup"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **pseudogroup** is a set of [homeomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphisms) between open sets of a space, satisfying [group](wikipedia://en/19447)-like and [sheaf](wikipedia://en/245466)-like properties. It is a generalisation of the concept of a [transformation group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_group), originating however from the geometric approach of [Sophus Lie](wikipedia://en/54010)[^o2] to investigate symmetries of differential equations, rather than out of [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) (such as [quasigroup](wikipedia://en/25223), for example). The modern theory of pseudogroups was developed by [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) in the early 1900s.[^o3][^o4]

:::

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A pseudogroup imposes several conditions on sets of [homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13660) (respectively, [diffeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/8564)) defined on [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) $U$ of a given [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) or more generally of a fixed [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) (respectively, [smooth manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219)). Since two homeomorphisms $h : U \to V$ and $g : V \to W$ compose to a homeomorphism from $U$ to $W$, one asks that the pseudogroup is closed under composition and inversion. However, unlike those for a group, the axioms defining a pseudogroup are not purely algebraic; the further requirements are related to the possibility of restricting and of patching homeomorphisms (similar to the [gluing axiom](wikipedia://en/1204294) for sections of a sheaf).

More precisely, a **pseudogroup** on a topological space $S$ is a collection $\Gamma$ of homeomorphisms between open subsets of $S$ satisfying the following properties:[^o7][^o8]

1. The domains of the elements $g$ in $\Gamma$ [cover](wikipedia://en/317552) $S$ ("**cover**").
1. The restriction of an element $g$ in $\Gamma$ to any open set contained in its domain is also in $\Gamma$ ("**restriction**").
1. The composition $g \circ h$ of two elements of $\Gamma$, when defined, is in $\Gamma$ ("**composition**").
1. The inverse of an element of $g$ is in $\Gamma$ ("**inverse**").
1. The property of lying in $\Gamma$ is local, i.e. if $g : U \to V$ is a homeomorphism between open sets of $S$ and $U$ is covered by open sets $U_{i}$ with $g$ restricted to $U_{i}$ lying in $\Gamma$ for each $i$, then $g$ also lies in $\Gamma$ ("**local**").

As a consequence the **identity** homeomorphism of any open subset of $S$ lies in $\Gamma$.

Similarly, a pseudogroup on a smooth manifold $X$ is defined as a collection $\Gamma$ of diffeomorphisms between open subsets of $X$ satisfying analogous properties (where we replace homeomorphisms with diffeomorphisms).[^o9]

Two points in $X$ are said to be in the same **orbit** if an element of $\Gamma$ sends one to the other. Orbits of a pseudogroup clearly form a partition of $X$; a pseudogroup is called **transitive** if it has only one orbit.

::: {#o11 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

A widespread class of examples is given by pseudogroups preserving a given geometric structure. For instance, if $(X, g)$ is a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652), one has the pseudogroup of its local [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777); if $(X, \omega)$ is a [symplectic manifold](wikipedia://en/28356), one has the pseudogroup of its local [symplectomorphisms](wikipedia://en/312304); etc. These pseudogroups should be thought as the set of the *local symmetries* of these structures.

:::

## Pseudogroups of symmetries and geometric structures {#Pseudogroups_of_symmetries_and_geometric_structures}

Manifolds with additional structures can often be defined using the pseudogroups of symmetries of a fixed local model. More precisely, given a pseudogroup $\Gamma$, a [$\Gamma$-atlas](wikipedia://en/47436) on a topological space $S$ consists of a standard atlas on $S$ such that the changes of coordinates (i.e. the transition maps) belong to $\Gamma$. An equivalent class of Γ-atlases is also called a **$\Gamma$-structure** on $S$.

In particular, when $\Gamma$ is the pseudogroup of all locally defined diffeomorphisms of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$, one recovers the standard notion of a smooth atlas and a [smooth structure](wikipedia://en/1542238). More generally, one can define the following objects as $\Gamma$-structures on a topological space $S$:

- [flat Riemannian structures](wikipedia://en/12756616), for $\Gamma$ pseudogroups of isometries of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ with the canonical Euclidean metric;
- [symplectic structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_structure), for $\Gamma$ the pseudogroup of symplectomorphisms of $\mathbf{R}^{2n}$ with the canonical symplectic form;
- [analytic structures](wikipedia://en/3183798), for $\Gamma$ the pseudogroup of [(real-)analytic diffeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/61478) of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$;
- [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181), for $\Gamma$ the pseudogroup of [invertible](wikipedia://en/14907) [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110) of a [complex variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_variable).

More generally, any integrable [$G$-structure](wikipedia://en/1277699) and any [$(G, X)$-manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%28G,X%29-manifold) are special cases of $\Gamma$-structures, for suitable pseudogroups $\Gamma$.

## Pseudogroups and Lie theory {#Pseudogroups_and_Lie_theory}

In general, pseudogroups were studied as a possible theory of [infinite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/17945#Infinite-dimensional_Lie_groups) [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945). The concept of a **local Lie group**, namely a pseudogroup of functions defined in [neighbourhoods](wikipedia://en/1529485) of the origin of a Euclidean space $E$, is actually closer to Lie's original concept of Lie group, in the case where the transformations involved depend on a finite number of [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065), than the contemporary definition via [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470). One of Cartan's achievements was to clarify the points involved, including the point that a local Lie group always gives rise to a *global* group, in the current sense (an analogue of [Lie's third theorem](wikipedia://en/10875756), on [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944) determining a group). The [formal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_group) is yet another approach to the specification of Lie groups, infinitesimally. It is known, however, that *local [topological groups](wikipedia://en/42315)* do not necessarily have global counterparts.

Examples of infinite-dimensional pseudogroups abound, beginning with the pseudogroup of all [diffeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/8564) of $E$. The interest is mainly in sub-pseudogroups of the diffeomorphisms, and therefore with objects that have a Lie algebra analogue of [vector fields](wikipedia://en/62641). Methods proposed by Lie and by Cartan for studying these objects have become more practical given the progress of [computer algebra](wikipedia://en/23659805).

In the 1950s, Cartan's theory was reformulated by [Shiing-Shen Chern](wikipedia://en/391905), and a general [deformation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_theory) for pseudogroups was developed by [Kunihiko Kodaira](wikipedia://en/646326)[^o14] and [D. C. Spencer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D._C._Spencer).[^o15] In the 1960s [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410) was applied to the basic [PDE](wikipedia://en/52564) questions of over-determination; however, this revealed that the algebra of the theory is potentially very heavy. In the same decade the interest for [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028) of infinite-dimensional Lie theory appeared for the first time, in the shape of [current algebra](wikipedia://en/1146294).

Intuitively, a **Lie pseudogroup** should be a pseudogroup which "originates" from a system of PDEs. There are many similar but inequivalent notions in the literature;[^o16][^o17][^o18][^o19][^o20] the "right" one depends on which application one has in mind. However, all these various approaches involve the (finite- or infinite-dimensional) [jet bundles](wikipedia://en/928060) of $\Gamma$, which are asked to be a [Lie groupoid](wikipedia://en/310923). In particular, a Lie pseudogroup is called of **finite order $k$** if it can be "reconstructed" from the space of its $k$-[jets](wikipedia://en/1521924).
