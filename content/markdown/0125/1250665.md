# Proof calculus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Proof calculus"}

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), a **proof calculus** or a **proof system** is built to prove [statements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statement_%28logic%29).

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

A proof system includes the components:[^o3][^o4]

- [Formal language](wikipedia://en/10939): The set *L* of formulas admitted by the system, for example, [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154) or [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983).
- [Rules of inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rules_of_inference): List of rules that can be employed to prove theorems from axioms and theorems.
- [Axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axioms): Formulas in *L* assumed to be valid. All [theorems](wikipedia://en/30977) are derived from axioms.

A [formal proof](wikipedia://en/1197184) of a [well-formed formula](wikipedia://en/404582) in a proof system is a set of axioms and rules of inference of the proof system that infers that the well-formed formula is a theorem of the proof system.[^o4]

Usually a given proof calculus encompasses more than a single particular formal system, since many proof calculi are under-determined and can be used for radically different logics. For example, a paradigmatic case is the [sequent calculus](wikipedia://en/252329), which can be used to express the [consequence relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consequence_relation) of both [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262) and [relevance logic](wikipedia://en/185076). Thus, loosely speaking, a proof calculus is a template or [design pattern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design_pattern), characterized by a certain style of formal inference, that may be specialized to produce specific formal systems, namely by specifying the actual inference rules for such a system. There is no consensus among logicians on how best to define the term.

## Examples of proof calculi {#Examples_of_proof_calculi}

The most widely known proof calculi are those classical calculi that are still in widespread use:

- The class of [Hilbert systems](wikipedia://en/8529655),[^o4] of which the most famous example is the 1928 [Hilbert–Ackermann system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert-Ackermann_system) of [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983);
- [Gerhard Gentzen](wikipedia://en/46027)'s calculus of [natural deduction](wikipedia://en/51072), which is the first formalism of [structural proof theory](wikipedia://en/929998), and which is the cornerstone of the [formulae-as-types correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formulae-as-types_correspondence) relating logic to [functional programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_programming);
- Gentzen's [sequent calculus](wikipedia://en/252329), which is the most studied formalism of structural proof theory.

Many other proof calculi were, or might have been, seminal, but are not widely used today.

- [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308)'s [syllogistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllogistic) calculus, presented in the *[Organon](wikipedia://en/499019)*, readily admits formalisation. There is still some modern interest in [syllogisms](wikipedia://en/48046), carried out under the [aegis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegis) of [term logic](wikipedia://en/321652).
- [Gottlob Frege](wikipedia://en/48416)'s two-dimensional notation of the *[Begriffsschrift](wikipedia://en/900125)* (1879) is usually regarded as introducing the modern concept of [quantifier](wikipedia://en/43507260) to logic.
- [C.S. Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117)'s [existential graph](wikipedia://en/2235037) easily might have been seminal, had history worked out differently.

Modern research in logic teems with rival proof calculi:

- Several systems have been proposed that replace the usual textual syntax with some graphical syntax. [proof nets](wikipedia://en/921927) and [cirquent calculus](wikipedia://en/47037875) are among such systems.
- Recently, many logicians interested in [structural proof theory](wikipedia://en/929998) have proposed calculi with [deep inference](wikipedia://en/1250664), for instance [display logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Display_logic), [hypersequents](wikipedia://en/54262912), the [calculus of structures](wikipedia://en/1250632), and [bunched implication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunched_implication).
