# Planar process

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Planar process"}

*Image: 74LS244 F 8314 annotated sm.jpg.* Annotated die photo of a Fairchild chip

The **planar process** is a [manufacturing process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor_device_fabrication) used in the [semiconductor industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor_industry) to build individual components of a [transistor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transistor), and in turn, connect those transistors together. It is the primary process by which [silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon) [integrated circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_circuit) chips are built, and it is the most commonly used method of producing [junctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junction_%28electricity%29) during the manufacture of [semiconductor devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor_device).[^o2] The process utilizes the [surface passivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_passivation) and [thermal oxidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_oxidation) methods.

:::

The planar process was developed at [Fairchild Semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairchild_Semiconductor) in 1959 and it proved to be one of the most important single advances in semiconductor technology.[^o2]

## Overview {#Overview}

The key concept is to view a circuit in its two-dimensional projection (a plane), thus allowing the use of [photographic processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photographic_processing) concepts such as film negatives to mask the projection of light exposed chemicals. This allows the use of a series of exposures on a substrate ([silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon)) to create [silicon oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_oxide) (insulators) or doped regions (conductors). Together with the use of metallization, and the concepts of [p–n junction isolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P–n_junction_isolation) and [surface passivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_passivation), it is possible to create circuits on a single silicon crystal slice (a wafer) from a monocrystalline silicon boule.

The process involves the basic procedures of [silicon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_dioxide) (SiO<sub>2</sub>) oxidation, SiO<sub>2</sub> etching and heat diffusion. The final steps involves [oxidizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_oxidation) the entire wafer with an SiO<sub>2</sub> layer, etching contact vias to the transistors, and depositing a covering metal layer over the [oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxide), thus connecting the transistors without manually wiring them together.

## History {#History}

### Development {#Development}

In 1955 at [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs), [Carl Frosch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Frosch) and Lincoln Derick accidentally grew a layer of silicon dioxide over a silicon wafer, for which they observed [surface passivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passivation_%28chemistry%29) properties.[^o6][^o7] In 1957, Frosch and Derick were able to manufacture the first silicon dioxide field effect transistors, the first transistors in which drain and source were adjacent at the surface, showing that silicon dioxide surface passivation protected and insulated silicon wafers.[^o8]

At Bell Labs, the importance of Frosch's technique was immediately realized. Results of theirs circulated around Bell Labs in the form of BTL memos before being published in 1957. At [Shockley Semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shockley_Semiconductor_Laboratory), Shockley had circulated the preprint of their article in December 1956 to all his senior staff, including [Jean Hoerni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Hoerni).[^o9][^o10][^o11][^o12] Later, Hoerni attended a meeting where [Atalla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohamed_M._Atalla) presented a paper about passivation based on the previous results at Bell Labs.[^o12] Taking advantage of silicon dioxide's passivating effect on the silicon surface, Hoerni proposed to make transistors that were protected by a layer of silicon dioxide.[^o12]

Jean Hoerni, while at [Fairchild Semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairchild_Semiconductor), first patented the planar process in 1959.[^o13][^o14] K. E. Daburlos and H. J. Patterson of Bell Laboratories continued on the efforts of C. Frosch and L. Derick, and developed a process similar to Hoerni’s about the same time.[^o12] Together with the use of metallization (to join together the integrated circuits), and the concept of [p–n junction isolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P–n_junction_isolation) (from [Kurt Lehovec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Lehovec)), the researchers at Fairchild were able to create circuits on a single silicon crystal slice (a wafer) from a [monocrystalline silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monocrystalline_silicon) [boule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boule_%28crystal%29).

In 1959, [Robert Noyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Noyce) built on Hoerni's work with his conception of an [integrated circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_circuit) (IC), which added a layer of metal to the top of Hoerni's basic structure to connect different components, such as transistors, [capacitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacitors), or [resistors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resistors), located on the same piece of silicon. The planar process provided a powerful way of implementing an integrated circuit that was superior to earlier conceptions of the integrated circuit.[^o15] Noyce's invention was the first monolithic IC chip.[^o16][^o17]

Early versions of the planar process used a [photolithography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photolithography) process using near-ultraviolet light from a mercury vapor lamp. As of 2011, small features are typically made with 193 nm "deep" UV lithography.[^o18] As of 2022, the [ASML](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASML_Holding) NXE platform uses 13.5 nm extreme ultraviolet (EUV) light, generated by a tin-based plasma source, as part of the [extreme ultraviolet lithography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_ultraviolet_lithography) process.
