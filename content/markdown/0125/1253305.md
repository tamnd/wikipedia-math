# Wheeler–DeWitt equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Wheeler–DeWitt equation"}

The **Wheeler–DeWitt equation**[^o2] for [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028) and [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969), is a [field equation](wikipedia://en/1410595) attributed to [John Archibald Wheeler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Archibald_Wheeler) and [Bryce DeWitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryce_DeWitt). The equation attempts to mathematically combine the ideas of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) and [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), a step towards a theory of [quantum gravity](wikipedia://en/25312).

:::

In this approach, [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_in_physics) plays a role different from what it does in non-relativistic quantum mechanics, leading to the so-called "[problem of time](wikipedia://en/42598658)".[^o3] More specifically, the equation describes the quantum version of the [Hamiltonian constraint](wikipedia://en/886930) using metric variables. Its commutation relations with the [diffeomorphism constraints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphism_constraint) generate the Bergman–Komar "group" (which *is* the [diffeomorphism group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphism_group) [on-shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-shell)).

## Motivation and background {#Motivation_and_background}

In [canonical gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_quantum_gravity), spacetime is [foliated](wikipedia://en/371227) into spacelike submanifolds. The three-metric (i.e., metric on the hypersurface) is $\gamma_{ij}$ and given by

$$g_{\mu\nu}\,\mathrm{d}x^{\mu}\,\mathrm{d}x^\nu = (-N^2 + \beta_k\beta^k)\,\mathrm{d}t^2 + 2\beta_k\,\mathrm{d}x^k\,\mathrm{d}t + \gamma_{ij}\,\mathrm{d}x^i\,\mathrm{d}x^j.$$

In that equation the Latin indices run over the values 1, 2, 3, and the Greek indices run over the values 1, 2, 3, 4. The three-metric $\gamma_{ij}$ is the field, and its conjugate momenta are denoted as $\pi^{ij}$. The Hamiltonian is a constraint (characteristic of most relativistic systems)

$$\mathcal{H} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\gamma}} G_{ijkl}\pi^{ij}\pi^{kl} - \sqrt{\gamma}\,{}^{(3)}\!R = 0,$$

where $\gamma = \det(\gamma_{ij})$, and $G_{ijkl} = (\gamma_{ik}\gamma_{jl} + \gamma_{il}\gamma_{jk} - \gamma_{ij}\gamma_{kl})$ is the Wheeler–DeWitt metric. In index-free notation, the Wheeler–DeWitt metric on the space of positive definite quadratic forms *g* in three dimensions is

$$\operatorname{tr}((g^{-1}dg)^2) - (\operatorname{tr}(g^{-1}dg))^2.$$

Quantization "puts hats" on the momenta and field variables; that is, the functions of numbers in the classical case become operators that modify the state function in the quantum case. Thus the operator

$$\hat{\mathcal{H}} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\gamma}} \hat{G}_{ijkl} \hat{\pi}^{ij} \hat{\pi}^{kl} - \sqrt{\gamma}\,{}^{(3)}\!\hat{R}.$$

is obtained.

Working in "position space", these operators are

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\gamma}_{ij}(t, x^k) &\to \gamma_{ij}(t, x^k), \\ \hat{\pi}^{ij}(t,x^k) &\to -i \frac{\delta}{\delta \gamma_{ij}(t,x^k)}. \end{aligned}$$

One can apply the operator to a general wave functional of the metric $\hat{\mathcal{H}} \Psi[\gamma] = 0,$ where

$$\Psi[\gamma] = a + \int \psi(x)\gamma(x) \,dx^3 + \iint \psi(x, y)\gamma(x)\gamma(y) \,dx^3 \,dy^3 + \dots,$$

which would give a set of constraints amongst the coefficients $\psi(x, y, \dots)$. This means that the amplitudes for $N$ gravitons at certain positions are related to the amplitudes for a different number of gravitons at different positions. Or, one could use the two-field formalism, treating $\omega(g)$ as an independent field, so that the wave function is $\Psi[\gamma, \omega]$.

## Mathematical formalism {#Mathematical_formalism}

The Wheeler–DeWitt equation[^o2] is a [functional differential](wikipedia://en/301504) equation. It is ill-defined in the general case, but very important in [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028), especially in [quantum gravity](wikipedia://en/25312). It is a functional differential equation on the space of three-dimensional spatial metrics. The Wheeler–DeWitt equation has the form of an operator acting on a wave functional; the functional reduces to a function in cosmology. Contrary to the general case, the Wheeler–DeWitt equation is well defined in [minisuperspaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minisuperspace) like the configuration space of cosmological theories. An example of such a [wave function](wikipedia://en/145343) is the [Hartle–Hawking state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartle–Hawking_state). [Bryce DeWitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryce_DeWitt) first published this equation in 1967 under the name "Einstein–Schrödinger equation"; it was later renamed the "Wheeler–DeWitt equation".[^o6]

where $\hat{H}(x)$ is the [Hamiltonian constraint](wikipedia://en/886930) in quantized [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), and $|\psi\rangle$ stands for the [wave function of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_wavefunction). Unlike ordinary quantum field theory or quantum mechanics, the Hamiltonian is a [first-class constraint](wikipedia://en/880145) on physical states. We also have an independent constraint for each point in space.

Although the symbols $\hat{H}$ and $|\psi\rangle$ may appear familiar, their interpretation in the Wheeler–DeWitt equation is substantially different from non-relativistic quantum mechanics. $|\psi\rangle$ is no longer a spatial wave function in the traditional sense of a complex-valued function that is defined on a 3-dimensional space-like surface and normalized to unity. Instead it is a [functional](wikipedia://en/1374948) of field configurations on all of spacetime. This wave function contains all of the information about the geometry and matter content of the universe. $\hat{H}$ is still an operator that acts on the [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) of wave functions, but it is not the same Hilbert space as in the nonrelativistic case, and the Hamiltonian no longer determines the evolution of the system, so the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874) $\hat{H} |\psi\rangle = i \hbar \partial / \partial t |\psi\rangle$ no longer applies. This property is known as timelessness. Various attempts to incorporate time in a fully quantum framework have been made, starting with the "Page and Wootters mechanism" and other subsequent proposals.[^o9][^o10] The reemergence of time was also proposed as arising from quantum correlations between an evolving system and a reference quantum clock system, the concept of **system-time entanglement** is introduced as a quantifier of the actual distinguishable evolution undergone by the system.[^o11][^o12]

### Momentum constraint {#Momentum_constraint}

We also need to augment the Hamiltonian constraint with [momentum constraints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum_constraint)

$$\vec{\mathcal{P}}(x) |\psi\rangle = 0$$

associated with spatial diffeomorphism invariance.

In [minisuperspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minisuperspace) approximations, we only have one Hamiltonian constraint (instead of infinitely many of them).

In fact, the principle of [general covariance](wikipedia://en/681875) in general relativity implies that global evolution per se does not exist; the time $t$ is just a label we assign to one of the coordinate axes. Thus, what we think about as time evolution of any physical system is just a [gauge transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_transformation), similar to that of [QED](wikipedia://en/25268) induced by U(1) local gauge transformation $\psi \to e^{i\theta(\vec{r})} \psi,$ where $\theta(\vec{r})$ plays the role of local time. The role of a Hamiltonian is simply to restrict the space of the "kinematic" states of the Universe to that of "physical" states—the ones that follow gauge orbits. For this reason we call it a "Hamiltonian constraint". Upon quantization, physical states become wave functions that lie in the [kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_%28matrix%29) of the Hamiltonian operator.

In general, the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381) vanishes for a theory with general covariance or time-scaling invariance.
