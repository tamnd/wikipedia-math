# No-interaction theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="No-interaction theorem"}

In [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), the **no-interaction theorem** is a [no-go theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No-go_theorem) that shows that a system of many interacting particles cannot exist when [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) is taken into account. It concludes that the only allowed configuration is that of a system of [free particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_particle) only. The theorem is important for [modern physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_physics), as it suggest the necessity of introducing [fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28physics%29) to describe interactions between particles. This theorem excludes the possibility of [action at a distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_at_a_distance). The modern understanding of the [fundamental interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_interaction) ([gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity), [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism) and [nuclear forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_force)) is that objects can interact by locally perturbing the fields in which each interaction propagates.

:::

Various formulations of the theorem exist. The earliest version was given by Douglas G. Currie, Thomas F. Jordan and [E. C. George Sudarshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._C._George_Sudarshan) in 1963,[^o2] extended by John T. Cannon and Jordan in 1964,[^o3] and generalized by [Heinrich Leutwyler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Leutwyler) in 1965.[^o4] An alternative formulation was given by Hendrik van Dam and [Eugene Wigner](wikipedia://en/10101)[^o5] in 1966.[^o6][^o7][^o8]

## Assumptions {#Assumptions}

Consider a system with a finite number *N* of particles described by [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) using [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/23371726) or [Hamiltonian mechanics](wikipedia://en/198319). The no-interaction theorem states that, aside from [contact forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_force), there can be no direct interaction between any two particles in a form that is manifestly invariant under [Lorentz transformations](wikipedia://en/18404) (covariant).[^o7][^o10] The theorem can be explicitly shown using three postulates:

1. Conservation of total linear momentum: the total [four-momentum](wikipedia://en/204680) at a given time $t$ is expressed as $P^\mu(t)=\sum_{a=1}^N P_{(a)}^\mu(t)$, where $P_{(a)}^\mu(t)$ is the four-momentum of particle $a$. The postulate assumes that $P^\mu(t)=\text{constant}$ at any time.
1. No coincidence: there are no two distinct particles $a$ and $b$ with proper times $t_a,t_b$ such that $P_{(a)}^\mu(t_a)=P_{(b)}^\mu(t_b)$.
1. Asymptotically straight [world lines](wikipedia://en/165487): for large times, $t_a\to \pm \infty$, $P_{(a)}^\mu(t_a)\to \text{constant}$ .

which leads to the momentum of individual particles being constant (no interactions) at any time. The third postulate implies that the total momentum transforms under [Lorentz transformations](wikipedia://en/18404).[^o11]

More advanced proofs rely on [group theoretical](wikipedia://en/41890) arguments.[^o12][^o11]

## Case of two identical particles {#Case_of_two_identical_particles}

The no-interaction theorem indicates that two classical particles of mass $m$ cannot interact. If $u^\mu_{(1)}$ is the [four-velocity](wikipedia://en/226829) of particle 1 and $u^\mu_{(2)}$ the four-velocity of particle 2, the total momentum is[^o8]

$$P^\mu=mu_{(1)}^\mu+mu_{(2)}^\mu.$$

In another frame, the four-momentum transforms as

$$P'^\mu=a^{\mu}{}_\nu P^\nu$$

for some Lorentz boost $a^\mu{}_\nu$.

Van Dam and Wigner [prove by contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_contradiction) that momentum is not conserved unless the particles come into contact.[^o8] Supposing particle 1 is at point *P* at time $t=0$ and also at point *P* at time $t'=0$ (following its world line), and particle 2 is at point *Q* at $t=0$ and at *Q**at $t'=0$, the transformation of the total momentum is given by***

$$mu_{(1)}^{\prime\mu}|_P+mu_{(2)}^{\prime\mu}|_Q=a^{\mu}{}_\nu \left(mu_{(1)}^\nu|_P+mu_{(2)}^\nu |_{Q'}\right),$$

but as the four-velocity at point *P* also transforms as

$$u_{(1)}^{\prime\mu}|_P=a^{\mu}{}_\nu u_{(1)}^\nu|_P,$$

and at point *Q* as

$$u_{(2)}^{\prime\mu}|_Q=a^{\mu}{}_\nu u_{(2)}^\nu|_Q,$$

as $a^\mu{}_\nu$ is arbitrary we conclude that

$$u_{(2)}^{\mu}|_{Q'}= u_{(2)}^\mu|_Q,$$

meaning that the velocity of particle 2 remains constant when moving from *Q* to *Q**. This result implies that the momentum of each particle remains constant, which would be the case if the particles did not interact.***
