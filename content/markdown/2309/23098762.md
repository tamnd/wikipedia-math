# Jacobi coordinates

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Jacobi coordinates"}

*Image: Two-body Jacobi coordinates.svg.* Jacobi coordinates for [two-body problem](wikipedia://en/277468); Jacobi coordinates are $\boldsymbol{R}=\frac {m_1}{M} \boldsymbol{x}_1 + \frac {m_2}{M} \boldsymbol{x}_2$ and $\boldsymbol{r} = \boldsymbol{x}_1 - \boldsymbol{x}_2$ with $M = m_1+m_2$.[^o2]

*Image: Jacobi coordinates — illustration for four bodies.svg.* A possible set of Jacobi coordinates for four-body problem; the Jacobi coordinates are **r**<sub>1</sub>, **r**<sub>2</sub>, **r**<sub>3</sub> and the center of mass **R**. See Cornille.[^o6]

In the theory of many-particle systems, **Jacobi coordinates** often are used to simplify the mathematical formulation. These coordinates are particularly common in treating polyatomic [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule) and [chemical reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction),[^o3] and in [celestial mechanics](wikipedia://en/102182).[^o4] An algorithm for generating the Jacobi coordinates for *N* bodies may be based upon [binary trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_trees).[^o5] In words, the algorithm may be described as follows:[^o5]

> We choose two of the *N* bodies with position coordinates **x**<sub>*j*</sub> and **x**<sub>*k*</sub> and we replace them with one virtual body at their centre of mass. We define the relative position coordinate **r**<sub>*jk*</sub> = **x**<sub>*j*</sub> − **x**<sub>*k*</sub>.
>
> We then repeat the process with the *N* − 1 bodies consisting of the other *N* − 2 plus the new virtual body. After *N* − 1 such steps we will have Jacobi coordinates consisting of the relative positions and one coordinate giving the position of the last defined centre of mass.

:::

For the [*N*-body problem](wikipedia://en/23859945) the result is:[^o6]

$$\boldsymbol{r}_j= \frac{1}{m_{0j}} \sum_{k=1}^j m_k\boldsymbol {x}_k \ - \ \boldsymbol{x}_{j+1}\ , \quad j \in \{1, 2, \dots, N-1\}$$

$$\boldsymbol{r}_N= \frac{1}{m_{0N}} \sum_{k=1}^N m_k\boldsymbol {x}_k \ ,$$

with

$$m_{0j} = \sum_{k=1}^j \ m_k \ .$$

The vector $\boldsymbol{r}_N$ is the [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961) of all the bodies and $\boldsymbol{r}_1$ is the relative coordinate between the particles 1 and 2:

The result one is left with is thus a system of *N*-1 translationally invariant coordinates $\boldsymbol{r}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{r}_{N-1}$ and a center of mass coordinate $\boldsymbol{r}_N$, from iteratively reducing two-body systems within the many-body system.

This change of coordinates has associated [Jacobian](wikipedia://en/195351) equal to $1$.

If one is interested in evaluating a free energy operator in these coordinates, one obtains

$$H_0=-\sum_{j=1}^N\frac{\hbar^2}{2 m_j}\, \nabla^2_{\boldsymbol{x}_j} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2 m_{0N}}\,\nabla^2_{\boldsymbol{r}_{N}}\!-\frac{\hbar^2}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{m_{j+1}}+\frac{1}{m_{0j}}\right)\nabla^2_{\boldsymbol{r}_j}$$

In the calculations can be useful the following identity

$$\sum_{k=j+1}^N \frac{m_k}{m_{0k}m_{0k-1}}=\frac{1}{m_{0j}}-\frac{1}{m_{0N}}.$$
