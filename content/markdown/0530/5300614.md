# Spiric section

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Spiric section"}

*Image: Torus-spir.svg.* Spiric sections as planar sections of a torus

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **spiric section**, sometimes called a **spiric of Perseus**, is a quartic [plane curve](wikipedia://en/971691) defined by equations of the form

:::

$$(x^2+y^2)^2=dx^2+ey^2+f. \,$$

Equivalently, spiric sections can be defined as [bicircular](wikipedia://en/24215692) quartic curves that are symmetric with respect to the *x* and *y*-axes. Spiric sections are included in the family of [toric sections](wikipedia://en/5350369) and include the family of [hippopedes](wikipedia://en/2737959) and the family of [Cassini ovals](wikipedia://en/2040906). The name is from σπειρα meaning "torus" in ancient Greek.[^o2]

A spiric section is sometimes defined as the curve of intersection of a [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) and a plane parallel to its [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096) axis. However, this definition does not include all of the curves given by the previous definition unless [imaginary](wikipedia://en/14554) planes are allowed.

Spiric sections were first described by the ancient Greek geometer [Perseus](wikipedia://en/5300446) in roughly 150 BC, and are assumed to be the first toric sections to be described. The name *spiric* is due to the ancient notation *spira* of a torus.,[^o3][^o4]

## Equations {#Equations}

*Image: Spiric section.svg.* *a* = 1, *b* = 2, *c* = 0, 0.8, 1

Start with the usual equation for the torus:

$$(x^2+y^2+z^2+b^2-a^2)^2 = 4b^2(x^2+y^2). \,$$

Interchanging *y* and *z* so that the axis of revolution is now on the *xy*-plane, and setting *z*=*c* to find the curve of intersection gives

$$(x^2+y^2-a^2+b^2+c^2)^2 = 4b^2(x^2+c^2). \,$$

In this formula, the [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) is formed by rotating a circle of radius *a* with its center following another circle of radius *b* (not necessarily larger than *a*, self-intersection is permitted). The parameter *c* is the distance from the intersecting plane to the axis of revolution. There are no spiric sections with *c* > *b* + *a*, since there is no intersection; the plane is too far away from the torus to intersect it.

Expanding the equation gives the form seen in the definition

$$(x^2+y^2)^2=dx^2+ey^2+f \,$$

where

$$d=2(a^2+b^2-c^2),\ e=2(a^2-b^2-c^2),\ f=-(a^4+b^4+c^4-2a^2b^2-2a^2c^2-2b^2c^2). \,$$

In [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) this becomes

$$(r^2-a^2+b^2+c^2)^2 = 4b^2(r^2\cos^2\theta+c^2) \,$$

or

$$r^4=r^2(d\cos^2\theta+e\sin^2\theta)+f.$$

Spiric sections on a spindle torus, whose planes intersect the spindle (inner part), consist of an outer and an inner curve (s. picture).

## Spiric sections as isoptics {#Spiric_sections_as_isoptics}

[Isoptics](wikipedia://en/31526496) of ellipses and hyperbolas are spiric sections. (S. also weblink *The Mathematics Enthusiast*.)

## Examples of spiric sections {#Examples_of_spiric_sections}

Examples include the [hippopede](wikipedia://en/2737959) and the [Cassini oval](wikipedia://en/2040906) and their relatives, such as the [lemniscate of Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/250908). The [Cassini oval](wikipedia://en/2040906) has the remarkable property that the *product* of distances to two foci are constant. For comparison, the sum is constant in [ellipses](wikipedia://en/9277), the difference is constant in [hyperbolae](wikipedia://en/14052) and the ratio is constant in [circles](wikipedia://en/6220).
