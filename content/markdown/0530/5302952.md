# Defective matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Defective matrix"}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), a **defective matrix** is a [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) that does not have a complete [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) of [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector), and is therefore not [diagonalizable](wikipedia://en/246325). In particular, an $n \times n$ [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) is defective [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it does not have $n$ [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) eigenvectors.[^o2] A complete basis is formed by augmenting the eigenvectors with [generalized eigenvectors](wikipedia://en/1137612), which are necessary for solving defective systems of [ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/32742753) and other problems.

:::

An $n \times n$ defective matrix always has fewer than $n$ distinct [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue), since distinct eigenvalues always have linearly independent eigenvectors. In particular, a defective matrix has one or more eigenvalues $\lambda$ with [algebraic multiplicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_multiplicity) $m > 1$ (that is, they are multiple [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) of the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268)), but fewer than $m$ linearly independent eigenvectors associated with $\lambda$. If the algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda$ exceeds its [geometric multiplicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_multiplicity) (that is, the number of linearly independent eigenvectors associated with $\lambda$), then $\lambda$ is said to be a **defective eigenvalue**.[^o2] However, every eigenvalue with algebraic multiplicity $m$ always has $m$ linearly independent generalized eigenvectors.

A [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/126474) and more generally a [Hermitian matrix](wikipedia://en/189682), and a [unitary matrix](wikipedia://en/129222), is never defective; more generally, a [normal matrix](wikipedia://en/170366) (which includes Hermitian and unitary matrices as special cases) is never defective.

## Jordan block {#Jordan_block}

Any nontrivial [Jordan block](wikipedia://en/4495764) of size $2 \times 2$ or larger (that is, not completely diagonal) is defective. (A diagonal matrix is a special case of the Jordan normal form with all trivial Jordan blocks of size $1 \times 1$ and is not defective.) For example, the $n \times n$ Jordan block

$$J = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda & 1 & \; & \; \\ \; & \lambda & \ddots & \; \\ \; & \; & \ddots & 1 \\ \; & \; & \; & \lambda \end{bmatrix},$$

has an [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue), $\lambda$ with algebraic multiplicity $n$ (or greater if there are other Jordan blocks with the same eigenvalue), but only one distinct eigenvector $J v_1 = \lambda v_1$, where $v_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.$ The other canonical basis vectors $v_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, ~ \ldots, ~ v_n = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ form a chain of generalized eigenvectors such that $J v_k = \lambda v_k + v_{k-1}$ for $k=2,\ldots,n$.

Any defective matrix has a nontrivial [Jordan normal form](wikipedia://en/312877), which is as close as one can come to [diagonalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_diagonalization) of such a matrix.

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

A simple example of a defective matrix is

$$\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix},$$

which has a double [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of 3 but only one distinct eigenvector

$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

(and constant multiples thereof).

:::
