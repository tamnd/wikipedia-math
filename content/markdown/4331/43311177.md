# Closed-subgroup theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Closed-subgroup theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **closed-subgroup theorem** (sometimes referred to as **Cartan's theorem**) is a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) in the theory of [Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_group_theory). It states that if $H$ is a [closed subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_subgroup) of a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) $G$, then $H$ is an [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119) [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) with the [smooth structure](wikipedia://en/1542238) (and hence the [group topology](wikipedia://en/42315)) agreeing with the embedding.[^n1][^n2][^n3] One of several results known as [Cartan's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartan's_theorem_%28disambiguation%29), it was first published in 1930 by [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274),[^n4] who was inspired by [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942)'s 1929 proof of a special case for groups of [linear transformations](wikipedia://en/18102).[^n5][^n6]

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

Let $G$ be a Lie group with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$. Now let $H$ be an arbitrary closed subgroup of $G$. It is necessary to show that $H$ is a smooth embedded submanifold of $G$. The first step is to identify something that could be the Lie algebra of $H$, that is, the tangent space of $H$ at the identity. The challenge is that $H$ is not assumed to have any smoothness and therefore it is not clear how one may define its tangent space. To proceed, define the "Lie algebra" $\mathfrak{h}$ of $H$ by the formula

$$\mathfrak{h} = \left\{ X \mid e^{tX}\in H, \,\, \forall t\in\mathbf{R}\right\}.$$

It is not difficult to show that $\mathfrak{h}$ is a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{g}$.[^n7] In particular, $\mathfrak{h}$ is a subspace of $\mathfrak{g}$, which one might hope to be the tangent space of $H$ at the identity. For this idea to work, however, $\mathfrak{h}$ must be big enough to capture some interesting information about $H$. If, for example, $H$ were some large subgroup of $G$ but $\mathfrak{h}$ turned out to be zero, $\mathfrak{h}$ would not be helpful.

The key step, then, is to show that $\mathfrak{h}$ actually captures all the elements of $H$ that are sufficiently close to the identity. That is to say, it is necessary to prove the following critical lemma:

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=math-theorem name="Lemma"}

**Lemma.**

Take a small neighborhood $U$ of the origin in $\mathfrak{g}$ such that the exponential map sends $U$ diffeomorphically onto some neighborhood $V$ of the identity in $G$, and let $\log: V \to U$ be the inverse of the exponential map. Then there is some smaller neighborhood $W \subset V$ such that if $h$ belongs to $W \cap H$, then $\log(h)$ belongs to $\mathfrak{h}$.[^n8]

:::

Once this has been established, one can use [exponential coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_coordinates) on $W$, that is, writing each $g \in W$ (not necessarily in $H$) as $g = e^{X}$ for $X = \log(g)$. In these coordinates, the lemma says that $X$ corresponds to a point in $H$ precisely if $X$ belongs to $\mathfrak{h} \subset \mathfrak{g}$. That is to say, in exponential coordinates near the identity, $H$ looks like $\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{g}$. Since $\mathfrak{h}$ is just a subspace of $\mathfrak{g}$, this means that $\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{g}$ is just like $\mathbf{R}^{k} \subset \mathbf{R}^{n}$, with $k = \dim(\mathfrak{h})$ and $n = \dim(\mathfrak{g})$. Thus, we have exhibited a "[slice coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slice_coordinate_system)" in which $H \subset G$ looks locally like $\mathbf{R}^{k} \subset \mathbf{R}^{n}$, which is the condition for an embedded submanifold.[^n9]

It is worth noting that Rossmann shows that for *any* subgroup $H$ of $G$ (not necessarily closed), the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{h}$ of $H$ is a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{g}$.[^n10] Rossmann then goes on to introduce coordinates[^n11] on $H$ that make the identity component of $H$ into a Lie group. The topology on $H$ coming from these coordinates is not the subset topology. That is to say, the identity component of $H$ is an immersed submanifold of $G$ but not an embedded submanifold.

In particular, the lemma stated above does not hold if $H$ is not closed.

## Example of a non-closed subgroup {#Example_of_a_non-closed_subgroup}

*Image: Torus.png.* The torus $G$. Imagine a bent [helix](wikipedia://en/179924) laid out on the surface picturing $H$. If $a = \tfrac{p}{q}$ in lowest terms, the helix will close up on itself at $(1, 1)$ after $p$ rotations in $\varphi$ and *q* rotations in $\theta$. If $a$ is irrational, the helix winds indefinitely.

For an example of a subgroup that is not an embedded Lie subgroup, consider the [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) and an "[irrational winding of the torus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrational_winding_of_a_torus)".

$$G = \mathbb{T}^2 = \left\{\left .\begin{pmatrix}e^{2\pi i\theta} & 0\\0 & e^{2\pi i\phi} \end{pmatrix}\right| \theta, \phi \in \mathbf{R}\right\},$$

and its subgroup

$$H = \left\{\left. \begin{pmatrix}e^{2\pi i\theta} & 0\\0 & e^{2\pi ia\theta}\end{pmatrix} \right| \theta \in \mathbf{R}\right\} \text{with Lie algebra } \mathfrak{h} = \left\{\left. \begin{pmatrix} i\theta & 0\\0 & ia\theta\end{pmatrix}\right| \theta \in \mathbf{R}\right\},$$

with $a$ irrational. Then $H$ is [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in $G$ and hence not closed.[^n12] In the [relative topology](wikipedia://en/910926), a small open subset of $H$ is composed of infinitely many almost parallel line segments on the surface of the torus. This means that $H$ is not [locally path connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_path_connected). In the group topology, the small open sets are *single* line segments on the surface of the torus and $H$ *is* locally path connected.

The example shows that for some groups $H$ one can find points in an arbitrarily small neighborhood $U$ in the relative topology $\tau_{r}$ of the identity that are exponentials of elements of $\mathbf{h}$, yet they cannot be connected to the identity with a path staying in $U$.[^n13] The group $(H, \tau_{r})$ is not a Lie group. While the map $\exp : \mathbf{h} \to (H, \tau_{r})$ is an analytic bijection, its inverse is not continuous. That is, if $U \subset \mathbf{h}$ corresponds to a small open interval $-\varepsilon < \theta < \varepsilon$, there is no open $V \subset (H, \tau_{r})$ with $\log(V) \subset U$ due to the appearance of the sets $V$. However, with the group topology $\tau_{g}$, $(H, \tau_{g})$ is a Lie group. With this topology the injection $\iota : (H, \tau_{g}) \to G$ is an analytic [injective](wikipedia://en/45196) immersion, but not a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660), hence not an embedding. There are also examples of groups $H$ for which one can find points in an arbitrarily small neighborhood (in the relative topology) of the identity that are *not* exponentials of elements of $\mathbf{h}$.[^n14] For closed subgroups this is not the case as the proof below of the theorem shows.

::: {#o20 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Because of the conclusion of the theorem, some authors chose to *define* **linear Lie groups** or **matrix Lie groups** as closed subgroups of $\operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$ or $\operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{C})$.[^o21] In this setting, one proves that every element of the group sufficiently close to the identity is the exponential of an element of the Lie algebra.[^n8] (The proof is practically identical to the proof of the closed subgroup theorem presented below.) It follows every closed subgroup is an *embedded* submanifold of $\operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{C})$[^n15]

::: {#o24 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="The homogeneous space construction theorem"}

**The **homogeneous space construction theorem.**

If $H \subset G$ is a *closed Lie subgroup*, then $G/H$, the left coset space, has a unique [real-analytic manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-analytic_manifold) structure such that the quotient map $\pi:G \to G/H$ is an analytic [submersion](wikipedia://en/356413). The left action given by $g_{1} \cdot (g_{2}H) = (g_{1}g_{2})H$ turns $G/H$ into a [homogeneous $G$-space](wikipedia://en/363325).

:::

The closed subgroup theorem now simplifies the hypotheses considerably, a priori widening the class of homogeneous spaces. Every closed subgroup yields a homogeneous space.

In a similar way, the closed subgroup theorem simplifies the hypothesis in the following theorem.

If $X$ is a set with [transitive group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_group_action) and the [isotropy group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotropy_group) or **stabilizer** of a point $x \in X$ is a closed Lie subgroup, then $X$ has a unique smooth manifold structure such that the action is smooth.

:::

## Conditions for being closed {#Conditions_for_being_closed}

A few sufficient conditions for $H \subset G$ being closed, hence an embedded Lie group, are given below.

- All [classical groups](wikipedia://en/10172878) are closed in $\operatorname{GL}(F, n)$, where $F$ is $\mathbf{R}$, $\mathbf{C}$, or $\mathbf{H}$, the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440).
- A subgroup that is *locally closed* is closed.[^n16] A subgroup is locally closed if every point has a neighborhood in $U \subset G$ such that $H \cap U$ is closed in $U$.
- If $H = AB = \{ab\mid a \in A, b \in B\}$, where $A$ is a compact group and $B$ is a closed set, then $H$ is closed.[^n17]
- If $\mathbf{h} \subset \mathbf{g}$ is a Lie subalgebra such that for no $X \in \mathbf{g} \setminus \mathbf{h}, [X, \mathbf{h}] \in \mathbf{h}$, then $\Gamma(\mathbf{h})$, the group generated by $e^{\mathbf{h}}$, is closed in $G$.[^n18]
- If $X \in \mathbf{g}$, then the [one-parameter subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-parameter_subgroup) generated by $X$ is *not closed* if and only if $X$ is similar over $\mathbf{C}$ to a diagonal matrix with two entries of irrational ratio.[^n19]
- Let $\mathbf{h} \subset \mathbf{g}$ be a Lie subalgebra. If there is a [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) compact group $\mathbf{k}$ with $\mathbf{k}$ isomorphic to $\mathbf{h}$, then $\Gamma(\mathbf{h})$ is closed in $G$. [^n20]
- If *G* is simply connected and $\mathbf{h} \subset \mathbf{g}$ is an [ideal](wikipedia://en/17944#Subalgebras.2C_ideals_and_homomorphisms), then the connected Lie subgroup with Lie algebra $\mathbf{h}$ is closed. [^n21]

::: {#o33 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Converse"}

## Converse {#Converse}

An embedded Lie subgroup $H \subset G$ is closed[^n22] so a subgroup is an embedded Lie subgroup if and only if it is closed. Equivalently, $H$ is an embedded Lie subgroup if and only if its group topology equals its relative topology.[^n23]

:::

## Proof {#Proof}

*Image: JohnvonNeumann-LosAlamos.gif.* [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) in 1929 proved the theorem in the case of [matrix groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_group) as given here. He was prominent in many areas, including [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) and the [foundations of mathematics](wikipedia://en/169358).

The proof is given for [matrix groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_group) with $G = \operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$ for concreteness and relative simplicity, since matrices and their exponential mapping are easier concepts than in the general case. Historically, this case was proven first, by John von Neumann in 1929, and inspired Cartan to prove the full closed subgroup theorem in 1930.[^n5][^n6] The proof for general $G$ is formally identical,[^o40] except that elements of the Lie algebra are **left invariant** [vector fields](wikipedia://en/62641) on $G$ and the exponential mapping is the time one **flow** of the vector field. If $H \subset G$ with $G$ closed in $\operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$, then $H$ is closed in $\operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$, so the specialization to $\operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$ instead of arbitrary $G \subset \operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$ matters little.

::: {#o42 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of the key lemma"}

### Proof of the key lemma {#Proof_of_the_key_lemma}

We begin by establishing the key lemma stated in the "overview" section above.

Endow $\mathbf{g}$ with an [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) (e.g., the [Hilbert–Schmidt inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert–Schmidt_inner_product)), and let $\mathbf{h}$ be the Lie algebra of $H$ defined as $\mathbf{h} = \{X \in M_{n}(\mathbf{R}) = \mathbf{g}\mid e^{tX} \in H \forall t \in \mathbf{R}\}$. Let $\mathbf{s} = \{S \in \mathbf{g}\mid (S, T) = 0 \forall T \in \mathbf{h}\}$, the [orthogonal complement](wikipedia://en/1058833) of $\mathbf{h}$. Then $\mathbf{g}$ decomposes as the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) $\mathbf{g} = \mathbf{s} \oplus \mathbf{h}$, so each $X \in \mathbf{g}$ is uniquely expressed as $X = S + T$ with $S \in \mathbf{s}, T \in \mathbf{h}$.

Define a map $\Phi : \mathbf{g} \to \operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$ by $(S, T) \mapsto e^{S}e^{T}$. Expand the exponentials,

$$\Phi(S,T) = e^{tS}e^{tT} = I + tS + tT + O(t^2),$$

and the [pushforward](wikipedia://en/1558510) or **differential** at $0$, $\Phi_{\ast}(S, T) = \tfrac{d}{dt}\Phi(tS, tT)|_{t = 0}$ is seen to be $S + T$, i.e. $\Phi_{\ast} = \operatorname{Id}$, the identity. The hypothesis of the [inverse function theorem](wikipedia://en/287229) is satisfied with $\Phi$ analytic, and thus there are open sets $U_{1} \subset \mathbf{g}, V_{1} \subset \operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{R})$ with $0 \in U_{1}$ and $I \in V_{1}$ such that $\Phi$ is a [real-analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-analytic) bijection from $U_{1}$ to $V_{1}$ with analytic inverse. It remains to show that $U_{1}$ and $V_{1}$ contain open sets $U$ and $V$ such that the conclusion of the theorem holds.

Consider a [countable](wikipedia://en/6026) [neighborhood basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_basis) $\mathrm{B}$ at $0 \in \mathbf{g}$, linearly ordered by reverse inclusion with $B_{1} \subset U_{1}$. Suppose for the purpose of obtaining a contradiction that for all $i$, $\Phi(B_{i}) \cap H$ contains an element $h_{i}$ that is *not* on the form $h_{i} = e^{T_{i}}, T_{i} \in \mathbf{h}$. Then, since $\Phi$ is a bijection on the $B_{i}$, there is a unique sequence $X_{i} = S_{i} + T_{i}$, with $0 \ne S_{i} \in \mathbf{s}$ and $T_{i} \in \mathbf{h}$ such that $X_{i} \in B_{i}$ converging to $0$ because $\mathrm{B}$ is a neighborhood basis, with $e^{S_{i}}e^{T_{i}} = h_{i}$. Since $e^{T_{i}} \in H$ and $h_{i} \in H$, $e^{S_{i}} \in H$ as well.

Normalize the sequence in $\mathbf{s}$, $Y_{i} = \tfrac{S_{i}}{||S_{i}||}$. It takes its values in the unit sphere in $\mathbf{s}$ and since it is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), there is a convergent subsequence converging to $Y \in \mathbf{s}$.[^n24] The index $i$ henceforth refers to this subsequence. It will be shown that $e^{tY} \in H, \forall t \in \mathbf{R}$. Fix $t$ and choose a sequence $m_{i}$ of integers such that $m_{i} ||S_{i}|| \to t$ as $i \to \infty$. For example, $m_{i}$ such that $m_{i} ||S_{i}|| \le t \le (m_{i} + 1) ||S_{i}||$ will do, as $S_{i} \to 0$. Then

$$(e^{S_i})^{m_i} = e^{m_iS_i} = e^{m_i\|S_i\| Y_i} \rightarrow e^{t Y}.$$

Since $H$ is a group, the left hand side is in $H$ for all $i$. Since $H$ is closed, $e^{tY} \in H, \forall t$,[^n25] hence $Y \in \mathbf{h}$. This is a contradiction. Hence, for some $i$ the sets $U = \mathrm{B}_{i}$ and $V = \Phi(\mathrm{B}_{i})$ satisfy $e^{U\cap\mathbf{h}} = H \cap V$ and the exponential restricted to the open set $(U \cap \mathbf{h}) \subset \mathbf{h}$ is in analytic bijection with the open set $\Phi(U) \cap H \subset H$. This proves the lemma.

:::

::: {#o46 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of the theorem"}

### Proof of the theorem {#Proof_of_the_theorem}

For $j \ge i$, the image in $H$ of $B_{j}$ under $\Phi$ form a neighborhood basis at $I$. This is, by the way it is constructed, a neighborhood basis both in the group topology and the [relative topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_topology). Since multiplication in $G$ is analytic, the left and right translates of this neighborhood basis by a group element $g \in G$ gives a neighborhood basis at $g$. These bases restricted to $H$ gives neighborhood bases at all $h \in H$. The topology generated by these bases is the relative topology. The conclusion is that the relative topology is the same as the group topology.

Next, construct coordinate charts on $H$. First define $\varphi_{1} : e^{(U)} \subset G \to \mathbf{g}, g \mapsto \log(g)$. This is an analytic bijection with analytic inverse. Furthermore, if $h \in H$, then $\varphi_{1}(h) \in \mathbf{h}$. By fixing a basis for $\mathbf{g} = \mathbf{h} \oplus \mathbf{s}$ and identifying $\mathbf{g}$ with $\mathbf{R}^{n}$, then in these coordinates $\varphi_{1}(h) = (x_{1}(h), ..., x_{m}(h), 0, ..., 0)$, where $m$ is the dimension of $\mathbf{h}$. This shows that $(e^{U}, \varphi_{1})$ is a [slice chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slice_chart). By translating the charts obtained from the countable neighborhood basis used above one obtains slice charts around every point in $H$. This shows that $H$ is an embedded submanifold of $G$.

Moreover, multiplication $m$, and inversion $i$ in $H$ are analytic since these operations are analytic in $G$ and restriction to a submanifold (embedded or immersed) with the relative topology again yield analytic operations $m : H \times H \to G$ and $i : H \times H \to G$.[^n26] But since $H$ is embedded, $m : H \times H \to H$ and $i : H \times H \to H$ are analytic as well.[^n27]

:::
