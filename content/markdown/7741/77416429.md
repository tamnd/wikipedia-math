# Glossary of number theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Glossary of number theory"}

This is a glossary of concepts and results in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), a field of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831). Concepts and results in [arithmetic geometry](wikipedia://en/1973177) and [diophantine geometry](wikipedia://en/502205) can be found in [Glossary of arithmetic and diophantine geometry](wikipedia://en/3141761).

:::

See also [List of number theory topics](wikipedia://en/346955).

## A {#A}

::: {#o3 .entry cue=term name="abc conjecture"}

**abc conjecture**

The [abc conjecture](wikipedia://en/174108) says that for all $\varepsilon > 0$, there are only finitely many coprime positive integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ satisfying $a+b=c$ such that the product of the distinct prime factors of $abc$ raised to the power of $1+\varepsilon$ is less than $c$.

## B {#B}

::: {#o12 .entry cue=term name="Bézout's identity"}

**Bézout's identity**

[Bézout's identity](wikipedia://en/4947), also called Bézout's lemma, states that if $d$ is the greatest common divisor of two integers $a$ and $b$, then there exists integers $x$ and $y$ such that $ax + by = d$, and in fact the integers of the form $as + bt$ are exactly the multiples of $d$.

::: {#o30 .entry cue=term name="Euclid's lemma"}

**Euclid's lemma**

[Euclid's lemma](wikipedia://en/826617) states that if a prime $p$ divides the product of two integers $ab$, then $p$ must divide at least one of $a$ or $b$.

:::

::: {#o31 .entry cue=term name="Euler's criterion"}

**Euler's criterion**

Let $p$ is an odd prime and $a$ is an integer not divisible by $p$. [Euler's criterion](wikipedia://en/105622) provides a slick way to determine whether $a$ is a quadratic residue mod $p$. It says that $a^{\tfrac{p-1}{2}}$ is congruent to 1 mod $p$ if $a$ is a quadratic residue mod $p$ and is congruent to -1 mod $p$ if not. This can be written using Legendre symbols as

$$\left(\frac{a}{p}\right) \equiv a^{\tfrac{p-1}{2}} \pmod p.$$

:::

::: {#o32 .entry cue=term name="Euler's theorem"}

**Euler's theorem**

[Euler's theorem](wikipedia://en/69566) states that if $n$ and $a$ are coprime positive integers, then $a^{\varphi(n)}$ is congruent to $1$ mod $n$. Euler's theorem generalizes Fermat's little theorem.

:::

::: {#o33 .entry cue=term name="Euler's totient function"}

**Euler's totient function**

For a positive integer $n$, [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452) of $n$, denoted $\varphi(n)$, is the number of integers coprime to $n$ between $1$ and $n$ inclusive. For example, $\varphi(4) = 2$ and $\varphi(p) = p - 1$ for any prime $p$.

**field extension**

A [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) $L/K$ is a pair of fields $K$ and $L$ such that $K$ is a subfield of $L$. Given a field extension $L/K$, the field $L$ is a $K$-vector space.

## G {#G}

::: {#o42 .entry cue=term name="Galois"}

**Galois**

A [Galois extension](wikipedia://en/457064) is a finite field extension $L/K$ such that one of the following equivalent conditions are satisfied:

$L/K$ is a [normal extension](wikipedia://en/929925) and a [separable extension](wikipedia://en/363340),

$L$ is a [splitting field](wikipedia://en/174440) of a [separable polynomial](wikipedia://en/362400) with coefficients in $K$,

$|\operatorname{Aut}(L/K)| = [L:K]$, that is, the number of automorphisms of the extension equals its [degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_%28field_theory%29). The automorphism group $\operatorname{Aut}(L/K)$ of a Galois extension is called its [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) and it is denoted $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K).$

**ideal**

The [ideal class group](wikipedia://en/150907) of a number field is the group of fractional ideals in the ring of integers in the field modulo principal ideals. The cardinality of the group is called the class number of the number field. It measures the extent of the failure of unique factorization.

In algebraic number theory, an integer sometimes means an element of a ring of integers; e.g., a [Gaussian integer](wikipedia://en/48628). To avoid ambiguity, an integer contained in $\mathbb{Q}$ is sometimes called a rational integer.

::: {#o56 .entry cue=term name="Legendre symbol"}

**Legendre symbol**

Let $p$ be an odd prime and let $a$ be an integer. The [Legendre symbol](wikipedia://en/18567) of $a$ and $p$ is

$\left(\frac{a}{p}\right) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } a \text{ is a quadratic residue mod } p \text{ and } a \not\equiv 0\pmod p, \\ -1 & \text{if } a \text{ is not a quadratic residue mod } p \text{ and } a \not\equiv 0\pmod p, \\ 0 & \text{if } a \equiv 0 \pmod p. \end{cases}$ The Legendre symbol provides a convenient notational package for several results, including the law of quadratic reciprocity and Euler's criterion.

## Q {#Q}

::: {#o77 .entry cue=term name="quadratic reciprocity"}

**quadratic reciprocity**

Let *p* and *q* be distinct odd prime numbers, and define the [Legendre symbol](wikipedia://en/18567) as

$\left(\frac{q}{p}\right) =\begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } n^2 \equiv q \bmod p \text{ for some integer } n\\ -1 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$ The law of [quadratic reciprocity](wikipedia://en/25272) states that

$\left(\frac{p}{q}\right) \left(\frac{q}{p}\right) = (-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}\frac{q-1}{2}}.$ This result aids in the computation of Legendre symbols and thus helps determine whether an integer is a quadratic residue.

:::

::: {#o78 .entry cue=term name="quadratic residue"}

**quadratic residue**

An integer $q$ is called a [quadratic residue](wikipedia://en/200091) mod $n$ if it is congruent to a perfect square mod $n$, i.e., if there exists an integer $x$ such that $x^2\equiv q \pmod{n}.$
