# Periodic graph (crystallography)

*Image: Diamond Cubic-F lattice animation.gif.* A (large) unit cell of the [diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_lattice) crystal net; the balls represent [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon) atoms and the sticks represent covalent bonds

*Image: A-quartz.png.* A (large) unit cell of the [alpha-quartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartz_crystal) crystal net; the black balls are [silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon) atoms and the red ones are [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Periodic graph"}

In [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography), a **periodic graph** or **crystal net** is a three-dimensional [periodic graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_Graph_%28Geometry%29), i.e., a [three-dimensional](wikipedia://en/3054853) [Euclidean graph](wikipedia://en/3313527) whose vertices or nodes are points in [three-dimensional Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/3054853), and whose edges (or bonds or spacers) are line segments connecting pairs of vertices, periodic in three [linearly independent](wikipedia://en/101863#Geometric_meaning) axial directions. There is usually an implicit assumption that the set of vertices are [uniformly discrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformly_discrete_set), i.e., that there is a fixed minimum distance between any two vertices. The vertices may represent positions of [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atoms) or complexes or clusters of atoms such as single-metal [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion), molecular building blocks, or [secondary building units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_building_units), while each edge represents a [chemical bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bond) or a [polymeric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer) [ligand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligand).

:::

Although the notion of a periodic graph or crystal net is ultimately mathematical (actually a crystal net is nothing but a periodic realization of an abelian covering graph over a finite graph [^o2]), and is closely related to that of a [Tessellation of space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessellation_of_space) (or honeycomb) in the theory of [polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytopes) and similar areas, much of the contemporary effort in the area is motivated by [crystal engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_engineering) and [prediction (design)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_structure_prediction), including [metal-organic frameworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal-organic_framework) (MOFs) and [zeolites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeolite).

## History {#History}

A crystal net is an infinite [molecular model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_model) of a crystal. Similar models existed in [Antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity), notably the atomic theory associated with [Democritus](wikipedia://en/8211), which was criticized by [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) because such a theory entails a vacuum, which Aristotle believed [nature abhors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horror_vacui_%28physics%29). Modern [atomic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_theory) traces back to [Johannes Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736) and his work on geometric [packing problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Packing_problem). Until the twentieth century, graph-like models of crystals focused on the positions of the (atomic) components, and these pre-20th century models were the focus of two controversies in chemistry and materials science.

The two controversies were (1) the controversy over [Robert Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Boyle)’s corpuscular theory of matter, which held that all material substances were composed of particles, and (2) the controversy over whether crystals were minerals or some kind of vegetative phenomenon.[^o4] During the eighteenth century, Kepler, [Nicolas Steno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Steno), [René Just Haüy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/René_Just_Haüy), and others gradually associated the packing of Boyle-type corpuscular units into arrays with the apparent emergence of polyhedral structures resembling crystals as a result. During the nineteenth century, there was considerably more work done on [polyhedra](wikipedia://en/23470) and also of [crystal structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_structure), notably in the derivation of the [Crystallographic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallographic_group) based on the assumption that a crystal could be regarded as a regular array of [unit cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_cell). During the early twentieth century, the physics and chemistry community largely accepted Boyle's corpuscular theory of matter—by now called the atomic theory—and [X-ray crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-Ray_Diffraction) was used to determine the position of the atomic or molecular components within the unit cells (by the early twentieth century, unit cells were regarded as physically meaningful).

However, despite the growing use of [stick-and-ball molecular models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_model#Skeletal_models), the use of graphical edges or line segments to represent chemical bonds in specific crystals have become popular more recently, and the publication of[^o5] encouraged efforts to determine graphical structures of known crystals, to generate crystal nets of as yet unknown crystals, and to synthesize crystals of these novel crystal nets. The coincident expansion of interest in [tilings](wikipedia://en/321671) and [tessellations](wikipedia://en/6008654), especially those modeling [quasicrystals](wikipedia://en/25350), and the development of modern [Nanotechnology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanoscience), all facilitated by the dramatic increase in computational power, enabled the development of algorithms from [computational geometry](wikipedia://en/176927) for the construction and analysis of crystal nets. Meanwhile, the ancient association between models of crystals and tessellations has expanded with [Algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801). There is also a thread of interest in the [very-large-scale integration (VLSI)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vlsi) community for using these crystal nets as circuit designs.[^o6]

## Basic formulation {#Basic_formulation}

A [Euclidean graph](wikipedia://en/3313527) in [three-dimensional space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-space) is a pair (*V*, *E*), where *V* is a set of vertices (sometimes called points or nodes) and *E* is a set of edges (sometimes called bonds or spacers) where each edge joins two vertices. There is a tendency in the polyhedral and chemical literature to refer to geometric graphs as nets (contrast with [polyhedral nets](wikipedia://en/1148129)), and the nomenclature in the chemical literature differs from that of graph theory.[^o8]

### Symmetries and periodicity {#Symmetries_and_periodicity}

A [symmetry](wikipedia://en/2714149#In_geometry) of a Euclidean graph is an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) of the underlying Euclidean space whose [restriction](wikipedia://en/185427#Restrictions_and_extensions) to the graph is an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/15094186); the [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) of the Euclidean graph is the group of its symmetries. A Euclidean graph in three-dimensional Euclidean space is periodic if there exist three [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) [translations](wikipedia://en/204682) whose restrictions to the net are symmetries of the net. Often (and always, if one is dealing with a crystal net), the periodic net has finitely many orbits, and is thus [uniformly discrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformly_discrete_set) in that there exists a minimum distance between any two vertices.

The result is a three-dimensional [periodic graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_Graph_%28Geometry%29) as a geometric object.

The resulting crystal net will induce a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) of vectors so that given three vectors that [generate](wikipedia://en/99945) the lattice, those three vectors will bound a [unit cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_structure#Unit_cell), i.e. a [parallelepiped](wikipedia://en/23975) which, placed anywhere in space, will enclose a fragment of the net that repeats in the directions of the three axes.

### Symmetry and kinds of vertices and edges {#Symmetry_and_kinds_of_vertices_and_edges}

Two vertices (or edges) of a periodic graph are symmetric if they are in the same [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29#Orbits_and_stabilizers) of the symmetry group of the graph; in other words, two vertices (or edges) are symmetric if there is a symmetry of the net that moves one onto the other. In chemistry, there is a tendency to refer to orbits of vertices or edges as “kinds” of vertices or edges, with the recognition that from any two vertices or any two edges (similarly oriented) of the same orbit, the geometric graph “looks the same”. Finite colorings of vertices and edges (where symmetries are to preserve colorings) may be employed.

The symmetry group of a crystal net will be a (group of restrictions of a) [crystallographic space group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallographic_group), and many of the most common crystals are of very high symmetry, i.e. very few orbits. A crystal net is uninodal if it has one orbit of vertex (if the vertices were [colored](wikipedia://en/426743) and the symmetries preserve colorings, this would require that a corresponding crystal have atoms of one element or molecular building blocks of one compound – but not vice versa, for it is possible to have a crystal of one element but with several orbits of vertices). Crystals with uninodal crystal nets include [cubic diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_cubic) and some representations of [quartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartz) crystals. Uninodality corresponds with [isogonality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex_transitive) in geometry and [vertex-transitivity](wikipedia://en/581175) in graph theory, and produces examples objective structures.[^o11] A crystal net is binodal if it has two orbits of vertex; crystals with binodal crystal nets include [boracite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boracite) and [anatase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatase). It is [edge-transitive](wikipedia://en/584406) or [isotoxal](wikipedia://en/6289189) if it has one orbit of edges; crystals with edge-transitive crystal nets include boracite but not anatase – which has two orbits of edges.[^o12]

### Geometry of crystal nets {#Geometry_of_crystal_nets}

In the geometry of crystal nets, one can treat edges as line segments. For example, in a crystal net, it is presumed that edges do not “collide” in the sense that when treating them as line segments, they do not intersect. Several polyhedral constructions can be derived from crystal nets. For example, a [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719) can be obtained by [subdividing](wikipedia://en/353021) each edge (treated as a line segment) by the insertion of subdividing points, and then the vertex figure of a given vertex is the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of the adjacent subdividing points (i.e., the [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_&_concave_polygons) whose vertices are the adjacent subdividing points).

Another polyhedral construction is to determine the [neighborhood](wikipedia://en/7309251) of a vertex in the crystal net. One application is to define an [energy function](wikipedia://en/52033#Optimization_problems) as a (possibly weighted) sum of squares of distances from vertices to their neighbors, and with respect to this energy function, the net is in equilibrium (with respect to this energy function) if each vertex is positioned at the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) of its neighborhood,[^o14] this is the basis of the crystal net identification program SYSTRE.[^o15] (mathematicians [^o16] use the term \`\`harmonic realizations" instead of \`\`crystal nets in equilibrium positions" because the positions are characterized by the discrete Laplace equation; they also introduced the notion of standard realizations which are special harmonic realizations characterized by a certain minimal principle as well;see [^o17]). Some crystal nets are isomorphic to crystal nets in equilibrium positions, and since an equilibrium position is a [normal form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_form_%28mathematics%29), the *crystal net isomorphism problem* (i.e., the [query](wikipedia://en/7726870) whether two given crystal nets are isomorphic as graphs; not to be confused with [crystal isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_%28crystallography%29)) is readily computed even though, as a subsumption of the [graph isomorphism problem](wikipedia://en/247577), it is apparently computationally difficult in general.

## Active areas of crystal design using crystal nets {#Active_areas_of_crystal_design_using_crystal_nets}

It is conjectured[^o19] that crystal nets may minimize [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) in the following sense. Suppose one is given an ensemble of uniformly discrete Euclidean graphs that fill space, with vertices representing atoms or molecular building blocks and with edges representing bonds or ligands, extending through all space to represent a solid. For some restrictions, there may be a unique Euclidean graph that minimizes a reasonably defined [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) function, and the conjecture is that that Euclidean graph may necessarily be periodic. This question is still open, but some researchers observe crystal nets of high symmetry tending to predominate observed Euclidean graphs derived from some classes of materials.[^o20][^o21]

Historically, crystals were developed by experimentation, currently formalized as [combinatorial chemistry](wikipedia://en/36680), but one contemporary desideratum is the synthesis of materials designed in advance, and one proposal is to design crystals (the designs being crystal nets, perhaps represented as one unit cell of a crystal net) and then synthesize them from the design.[^o22] This effort, in what [Omar Yaghi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omar_M._Yaghi) described as reticular chemistry is proceeding on several fronts, from the theoretical[^o23] to synthesizing highly porous crystals.[^o24]

One of the primary issues in annealing crystals is controlling the constituents, which can be difficult if the constituents are individual atoms, e.g., in [zeolites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeolites), which are typically porous crystals primarily of silicon and oxygen and occasional impurities. Synthesis of a specific zeolite de novo from a novel crystal net design remains one of the major goals of contemporary research. There are similar efforts in [sulfides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfide_mineral) and [phosphates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate_minerals).

Control is more tractable if the constituents are molecular building blocks, i.e., stable molecules that can be readily induced to assemble in accordance with geometric restrictions. Typically, while there may be many species of constituents, there are two main classes: somewhat compact and often polyhedral [secondary building units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal-organic_framework) (SBUs), and linking or bridging building units. A popular class of examples are the [Metal-Organic Frameworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal_organic_framework) (MOFs), in which (classically) the secondary building units are metal [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion) or clusters of ions and the linking building units are organic [ligands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligand). These SBUs and ligands are relatively controllable, and some new crystals have been synthesized using designs of novel nets.[^o25] An organic variant are the [Covalent Organic Frameworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covalent_organic_frameworks) (COFs), in which the SBUs might (but not necessarily) be themselves organic. The greater control over the SBUs and ligands can be seen in the fact that while no novel zeolites have been synthesized per design, several MOFs have been synthesized from crystal nets designed for zeolite synthesis, such as Zeolite-like Metal-Organic Frameworks (Z-MOFs) and zeolitic imidazolate framework (ZIFs).
