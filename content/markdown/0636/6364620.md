# Kramers' theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kramers' theorem"}

In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), **Kramers' theorem** or **Kramers' degeneracy theorem** states that for every energy [eigenstate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenstate) of a [time-reversal symmetric](wikipedia://en/151013) system with half-integer total [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829), there is another eigenstate with the same energy related by time-reversal. In other words, the [degeneracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degeneracy_%28quantum_mechanics%29) of every energy level is an even number if it has half-integer spin. The theorem is named after Dutch physicist [H. A. Kramers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Anthony_Kramers).

:::

In theoretical physics, the [time reversal symmetry](wikipedia://en/151013) is the symmetry of physical laws under a time reversal transformation:

$$T: t \mapsto -t.$$

If the [Hamiltonian operator](wikipedia://en/14381) commutes with the time-reversal operator, that is

$$[H,T]=0,$$

then, for every energy eigenstate $|n\rangle$, the time reversed state $T|n\rangle$ is also an eigenstate with the same energy. These two states are sometimes called a **Kramers pair**.[^o2] In general, this time-reversed state may be identical to the original one, but that is not possible in a half-integer spin system: since time reversal reverses all angular momenta, reversing a half-integer spin cannot yield the same state (the [magnetic quantum number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_quantum_number) is never zero).

## Mathematical statement and proof {#Mathematical_statement_and_proof}

In quantum mechanics, the time reversal operation is represented by an [antiunitary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiunitary_operator) operator $T : \mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{H}$ acting on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) $\mathcal{H}$. If it happens that $T^2 = -1$, then we have the following simple theorem:

If $T : \mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{H}$ is an antiunitary operator acting on a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ satisfying $T^2 = -1$ and $v$ a vector in $\mathcal{H}$, then $T v$ is orthogonal to $v$.

::: {#o5 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

### Proof {#Proof}

By the definition of an antiunitary operator, $\langle T u, T w \rangle = \langle w, u \rangle$, where $u$ and $w$ are vectors in $\mathcal{H}$. Replacing $u = T v$ and $w = v$ and using that $T^2 = -1$, we get $\langle T^2 v, T v \rangle = - \langle v, T v \rangle = \langle v, T v \rangle$, which implies that $\langle v, T v \rangle = 0$.

Consequently, if a [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381) $H$ is time-reversal symmetric, i.e., it commutes with $T,$ then all its energy [eigenspaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenspace) have even degeneracy, since applying $T$ to an arbitrary energy eigenstate $|n\rangle$ gives another energy eigenstate $T | n \rangle$ that is orthogonal to the first one. The orthogonality property is crucial, as it means that the two eigenstates $| n \rangle$ and $T | n \rangle$ represent different physical states. If, on the contrary, they were the same physical state, then $T |n\rangle = e^{i \alpha} |n\rangle$ for an angle $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$, which would imply

$$T^2 |n\rangle = T (e^{i \alpha} |n\rangle) = e^{- i \alpha} e^{i \alpha} |n \rangle = |n \rangle$$

To complete Kramers degeneracy theorem, we just need to prove that the time-reversal operator $T$ acting on a [half-odd-integer](wikipedia://en/1568608) spin Hilbert space satisfies $T^2 = -1$. This follows from the fact that the [spin operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_operator) $\mathbf{S}$ represents a type of [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839), and, as such, should reverse direction under $T$:

$$\mathbf{S} \to T^{-1} \mathbf{S} T = - \mathbf{S}.$$

Concretely, an operator $T$ that has this property is usually written as

$$T = e^{- i \pi S_y} K$$

where $S_y$ is the spin operator in the $y$ direction and $K$ is the complex conjugation map in the $S_z$ spin basis.[^o6]

Since $i S_y$ has real matrix components in the $S_z$ basis, then

$$T^2 = e^{- i \pi S_y} K e^{- i \pi S_y} K = e^{- i 2 \pi S_y} K^2 = (-1)^{2 S}.$$

Hence, for half-odd-integer spins $S = \frac{1}{2}, \frac{3}{2}, \ldots$, we have $T^2 = -1$. This is the same minus sign that appears when one does a full $2 \pi$ rotation on [systems with half-odd-integer spins](wikipedia://en/352827), such as [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion).

:::

## Consequences {#Consequences}

The [energy levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_level) of a system with an odd total number of fermions (such as [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron), [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton) and [neutrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron)) remain at least doubly [degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_energy_level) in the presence of purely [electric fields](wikipedia://en/41092) (i.e. no external [magnetic fields](wikipedia://en/36563)). It was first discovered in 1930 by [H. A. Kramers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Anthony_Kramers)[^o8] as a consequence of the [Breit equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breit_equation). As shown by [Eugene Wigner](wikipedia://en/10101) in 1932.[^o9] It is a consequence of the [time reversal invariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_reversal_invariance) of [electric fields](wikipedia://en/41092), and follows from an application of the [antiunitary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiunitary) *T*-operator to the wavefunction of an odd number of fermions. The theorem is valid for any configuration of static or time-varying electric fields.

For example, the [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) (H) atom contains one proton and one electron, so that the Kramers theorem does not apply. Indeed, the lowest (hyperfine) energy level of H is nondegenerate, although a generic system might have degeneracy for other reasons. The [deuterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deuterium) (D) isotope on the other hand contains an extra neutron, so that the total number of fermions is three, and the theorem does apply. The ground state of D contains two hyperfine components, which are twofold and fourfold degenerate.
