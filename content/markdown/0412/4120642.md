# Q-exponential

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Q-exponential"}

The term ***q*-exponential** occurs in two contexts. The [q-exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/32195081), based on the [Tsallis q-exponential](wikipedia://en/3599748#q-exponential) is discussed in elsewhere.

:::

In [combinatorial](wikipedia://en/5170) [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a ***q*-exponential** is a [*q*-analog](wikipedia://en/1619396) of the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678), namely the [eigenfunction](wikipedia://en/228601) of a *q*-derivative. There are many *q*-derivatives, for example, the classical [*q*-derivative](wikipedia://en/3921324), the [Askey–Wilson operator](wikipedia://en/19582010), etc. Therefore, unlike the classical exponentials, *q*-exponentials are not unique. For example, $e_q(z)$ is the *q*-exponential corresponding to the classical [*q*-derivative](wikipedia://en/3921324) while $\mathcal{E}_q(z)$ are eigenfunctions of the Askey–Wilson operators.

The *q*-exponential is also known as the [quantum dilogarithm](wikipedia://en/31601344).[^o2][^o3]

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The *q*-exponential $e_q(z)$ is defined as

$$e_q(z)= \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{z^n}{[n]!_q} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{z^n (1-q)^n}{(q;q)_n} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty z^n\frac{(1-q)^n}{(1-q^n)(1-q^{n-1}) \cdots (1-q)}$$

where $[n]!_q$ is the [*q*-factorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q-factorial) and

$$(q;q)_n=(1-q^n)(1-q^{n-1})\cdots (1-q)$$

is the [*q*-Pochhammer symbol](wikipedia://en/11149717). That this is the *q*-analog of the exponential follows from the property

$$\left(\frac{d}{dz}\right)_q e_q(z) = e_q(z)$$

where the derivative on the left is the [*q*-derivative](wikipedia://en/3921324). The above is easily verified by considering the *q*-derivative of the [monomial](wikipedia://en/357416)

$$\left(\frac{d}{dz}\right)_q z^n = z^{n-1} \frac{1-q^n}{1-q} =[n]_q z^{n-1}.$$

Here, $[n]_q$ is the [*q*-bracket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q-bracket). For other definitions of the *q*-exponential function, see (Exton 1983), (Ismail Zhang 1994), and (Cieśliński 2011).

:::

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

For real $q>1$, the function $e_q(z)$ is an [entire function](wikipedia://en/10372) of $z$. For $q<1$, $e_q(z)$ is regular in the disk $|z|<1/(1-q)$.

Note the inverse, $~e_q(z) ~ e_{1/q} (-z) =1$.

::: {#o12 .statement cue=named-heading name="Addition Formula"}

### Addition Formula {#Addition_Formula}

The analogue of $\exp(x)\exp(y)=\exp(x+y)$ does not hold for real numbers $x$ and $y$. However, if these are operators satisfying the commutation relation $xy=qyx$, then $e_q(x)e_q(y)=e_q(x+y)$ holds true.[^o13]

:::

:::

## Relations {#Relations}

For $-1<q<1$, a function that is closely related is $E_q(z).$ It is a special case of the [basic hypergeometric series](wikipedia://en/2233526),

$$E_{q}(z)=\;_{1}\phi_{1}\left({\scriptstyle{0\atop 0}}\, ;\,z\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{\binom{n}{2}}(-z)^{n}}{(q;q)_{n}}=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}(1-q^{n}z)=(z;q)_\infty.$$

Clearly,

$$\lim_{q\to1}E_{q}\left(z(1-q)\right)=\lim_{q\to1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{\binom{n}{2}}(1-q)^{n}}{(q;q)_{n}} (-z)^{n}=e^{-z} .~$$

### Relation with Dilogarithm {#Relation_with_Dilogarithm}

$e_q(x)$ has the following infinite product representation:

$$e_q(x)=\left(\prod_{k=0}^\infty(1-q^k(1-q)x)\right)^{-1}.$$

On the other hand, $\log(1-x)=-\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{x^n}{n}$ holds. When $|q|<1$,

$$\begin{aligned} \log e_q(x) &= -\sum_{k=0}^\infty\log(1-q^k(1-q)x) \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(q^k(1-q)x)^n}{n} \\ &= \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{((1-q)x)^n}{(1-q^n)n} \\ &= \frac{1}{1-q}\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{((1-q)x)^n}{[n]_qn} \end{aligned}.$$

By taking the limit $q\to 1$,

$$\lim_{q\to 1}(1-q)\log e_q(x/(1-q))=\mathrm{Li}_2(x),$$

where $\mathrm{Li}_2(x)$ is the [dilogarithm](wikipedia://en/2398437).
