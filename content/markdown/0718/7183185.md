# L. R. Ford Jr.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="L. R. Ford Jr."}

**Lester Randolph Ford Jr.** (September 23, 1927 – February 26, 2017) was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) specializing in [network flow](wikipedia://en/645676) problems. He was the son of mathematician [Lester R. Ford Sr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_R._Ford)

:::

Ford's paper with [D. R. Fulkerson](wikipedia://en/239238) on the [maximum flow problem](wikipedia://en/403165) and the [Ford–Fulkerson algorithm](wikipedia://en/53777) for solving it, published as a technical report in 1954 and in a journal in 1956, established the [max-flow min-cut theorem](wikipedia://en/78130).[^o4][^o5] In 1962 they published *Flows in Networks* with [Princeton University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University_Press).[^o6] According to the preface, it "included topics that were purely mathematically motivated, together with those that are strictly utilitarian in concept." In his review, [S.W. Golomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S.W._Golomb) wrote, "This book is an attractive, well-written account of a fairly new topic in pure and applied combinatorial analysis."[^o7] As a topic of continued interest, a new edition was published in 2010 with a new foreword by [Robert G. Bland](wikipedia://en/34886045) and [James B. Orlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_B._Orlin).[^o8]

In 1956, Ford developed the [Bellman–Ford algorithm](wikipedia://en/221244) for finding [shortest paths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortest_path) in [graphs](wikipedia://en/325806) that have negative weights,[^o9] two years before [Richard Bellman](wikipedia://en/730173) also published the algorithm.[^o10]

With [Selmer M. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selmer_M._Johnson), he developed the [Ford–Johnson algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford–Johnson_algorithm) for sorting, which is of theoretical interest in connection with the problem of doing [comparison sort](wikipedia://en/3189304) with the fewest comparisons. For 20 years, this algorithm required the minimum number of comparisons.[^o11]

In 1963 along with his father Lester R. Ford, he published an innovative textbook on [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176).[^o12] For a given function *f* and point *x*, they defined a *frame* as a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048) containing (*x*, *f*(*x*)) with sides parallel to the axes of the plane (page 9). Frames are then exploited to define [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) (page 10) and to describe [integrable functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_function) (page 148).

## Personal information {#Personal_information}

Lester was born in [Houston, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houston,_Texas) on September 23, 1927. He learned to play [piano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piano) and the [flute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flute) as a young man, continuing to play piano as he got older. He was also a whistler, frequently heard whistling while he worked on math or for fun.[^o14]. For higher education he considered [Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) and [Oberlin Conservatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oberlin_Conservatory), but chose the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago) which provided him a scholarship. He earned his bachelor's degree in 1949 and a masters in 1950. Ford continued his studies at [University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana-Champaign) where he earned a Ph.D. in mathematics in 1953.

Ford's employers included the [U. S. Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U._S._Army), [University of North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_North_Carolina) and [RAND Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAND_Corporation). The Defense Research Corporation of [Goleta, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goleta,_California) employed him for forty years as he kept pace with [digital revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_revolution). Ford married twice. With his first wife, Janet Johnson, he had nine children, including [Fred Ford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Ford_%28programmer%29), programmer of the *[Star Control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Control)* Universe. His second wife was Naoma Gower.[^o15]
