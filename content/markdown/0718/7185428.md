# Random dynamical system

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Random dynamical system"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **random dynamical system** is a [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087) in which the [equations of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations_of_motion) have an element of randomness to them. Random dynamical systems are characterized by a [state space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_space) *S*, a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of [maps](wikipedia://en/516931) $\Gamma$ from *S* into itself that can be thought of as the set of all possible equations of motion, and a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) *Q* on the set $\Gamma$ that represents the random choice of map. Motion in a random dynamical system can be informally thought of as a state $X \in S$ evolving according to a succession of maps randomly chosen according to the distribution *Q*.[^o2]

:::

An example of a random dynamical system is a [stochastic differential equation](wikipedia://en/1361454); in this case the distribution Q is typically determined by *noise terms*. It consists of a [base flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_flow_%28random_dynamical_systems%29), the "noise", and a [cocycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oseledec_theorem) dynamical system on the "physical" [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101). Another example is discrete state random dynamical system; some elementary contradistinctions between Markov chain and random dynamical system descriptions of a stochastic dynamics are discussed.[^o3]

## Motivation 1: Solutions to a stochastic differential equation {#Motivation_1:_Solutions_to_a_stochastic_differential_equation}

Let $f : \mathbb{R}^{d} \to \mathbb{R}^{d}$ be a $d$-dimensional [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641), and let $\varepsilon > 0$. Suppose that the solution $X(t, \omega; x_{0})$ to the stochastic differential equation

$$\left\{ \begin{matrix} \mathrm{d} X = f(X) \, \mathrm{d} t + \varepsilon \, \mathrm{d} W (t); \\ X (0) = x_{0}; \end{matrix} \right.$$

exists for all positive time and some (small) interval of negative time dependent upon $\omega \in \Omega$, where $W : \mathbb{R} \times \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^{d}$ denotes a $d$-dimensional [Wiener process](wikipedia://en/149984) ([Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436)). Implicitly, this statement uses the [classical Wiener](wikipedia://en/6810363) [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325)

$$(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P}) := \left( C_{0} (\mathbb{R}; \mathbb{R}^{d}), \mathcal{B} (C_{0} (\mathbb{R}; \mathbb{R}^{d})), \gamma \right).$$

In this context, the Wiener process is the coordinate process.

Now define a **flow map** or (**solution operator**) $\varphi : \mathbb{R} \times \Omega \times \mathbb{R}^{d} \to \mathbb{R}^{d}$ by

$$\varphi (t, \omega, x_{0}) := X(t, \omega; x_{0})$$

(whenever the right hand side is [well-defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-defined)). Then $\varphi$ (or, more precisely, the pair $(\mathbb{R}^{d}, \varphi)$) is a (local, left-sided) random dynamical system. The process of generating a "flow" from the solution to a stochastic differential equation leads us to study suitably defined "flows" on their own. These "flows" are random dynamical systems.

## Motivation 2: Connection to Markov Chain {#Motivation_2:_Connection_to_Markov_Chain}

An i.i.d random dynamical system in the discrete space is described by a triplet $(S, \Gamma, Q)$.

- $S$ is the state space, $\{s_1, s_2,\cdots, s_n\}$.
- $\Gamma$ is a family of maps of $S\rightarrow S$. Each such map has a $n\times n$ matrix representation, called *deterministic transition matrix*. It is a binary matrix but it has exactly one entry 1 in each row and 0s otherwise.
- $Q$ is the probability measure of the $\sigma$-field of $\Gamma$.

The discrete random dynamical system comes as follows,

1. The system is in some state $x_0$ in $S$, a map $\alpha_1$ in $\Gamma$ is chosen according to the probability measure $Q$ and the system moves to the state $x_1=\alpha_1(x_0)$ in step 1.
1. Independently of previous maps, another map $\alpha_2$ is chosen according to the probability measure $Q$ and the system moves to the state $x_2=\alpha_2(x_1)$.
1. The procedure repeats.

The random variable $X_n$ is constructed by means of composition of independent random maps, $X_n=\alpha_n\circ \alpha_{n-1}\circ \dots \circ \alpha_1(X_0)$. Clearly, $X_n$ is a [Markov Chain](wikipedia://en/60876).

Reversely, can, and how, a given MC be represented by the compositions of i.i.d. random transformations? Yes, it can, but not unique. The proof for existence is similar with Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem for [doubly stochastic matrix](wikipedia://en/2906180).

Here is an example that illustrates the existence and non-uniqueness.

::: {#o6 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example:** If the state space $S=\{1, 2\}$ and the set of the transformations $\Gamma$ expressed in terms of deterministic transition matrices. Then a Markov transition matrix$M =\left(\begin{array}{cc} 0.4 & 0.6 \\ 0.7 & 0.3 \end{array}\right)$ can be represented by the following decomposition by the min-max algorithm, $M =0.6\left(\begin{array}{cc} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{array}\right)+0.3 \left(\begin{array}{cc} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{array}\right)+ 0.1\left(\begin{array}{cc} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{array}\right).$

In the meantime, another decomposition could be $M = 0.18 \left(\begin{array}{cc} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{array}\right)+ 0.28\left(\begin{array}{cc} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{array}\right) +0.42\left(\begin{array}{cc} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{array}\right)+0.12\left(\begin{array}{cc} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{array}\right).$

:::

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Formally,[^o9] a **random dynamical system** consists of a base flow, the "noise", and a cocycle dynamical system on the "physical" phase space. In detail.

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325), the **noise** space. Define the **base flow** $\vartheta : \mathbb{R} \times \Omega \to \Omega$ as follows: for each "time" $s \in \mathbb{R}$, let $\vartheta_{s} : \Omega \to \Omega$ be a measure-preserving [measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055):

$\mathbb{P} (E) = \mathbb{P} (\vartheta_{s}^{-1} (E))$ for all $E \in \mathcal{F}$ and $s \in \mathbb{R}$;

Suppose also that

1. $\vartheta_{0} = \mathrm{id}_{\Omega} : \Omega \to \Omega$, the [identity function](wikipedia://en/15069) on $\Omega$;
1. for all $s, t \in \mathbb{R}$, $\vartheta_{s} \circ \vartheta_{t} = \vartheta_{s + t}$.

That is, $\vartheta_{s}$, $s \in \mathbb{R}$, forms a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of measure-preserving transformation of the noise $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$. For one-sided random dynamical systems, one would consider only positive indices $s$; for discrete-time random dynamical systems, one would consider only integer-valued $s$; in these cases, the maps $\vartheta_{s}$ would only form a [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) instead of a group.

While true in most applications, it is not usually part of the formal definition of a random dynamical system to require that the [measure-preserving dynamical system](wikipedia://en/398931) $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P}, \vartheta)$ is [ergodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic).

Now let $(X, d)$ be a [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018), the **phase space**. Let $\varphi : \mathbb{R} \times \Omega \times X \to X$ be a $(\mathcal{B} (\mathbb{R}) \otimes \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{B} (X), \mathcal{B} (X))$-measurable function such that

1. for all $\omega \in \Omega$, $\varphi (0, \omega) = \mathrm{id}_{X} : X \to X$, the identity function on $X$;
1. for (almost) all $\omega \in \Omega$, $(t,x) \mapsto \varphi (t, \omega,x)$ is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122);
1. $\varphi$ satisfies the (crude) **cocycle property**: for [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) $\omega \in \Omega$,

$$\varphi (t, \vartheta_{s} (\omega)) \circ \varphi (s, \omega) = \varphi (t + s, \omega).$$

In the case of random dynamical systems driven by a Wiener process $W : \mathbb{R} \times \Omega \to X$, the base flow $\vartheta_{s} : \Omega \to \Omega$ would be given by

$$W (t, \vartheta_{s} (\omega)) = W (t + s, \omega) - W(s, \omega).$$

This can be read as saying that $\vartheta_{s}$ "starts the noise at time $s$ instead of time 0". Thus, the cocycle property can be read as saying that evolving the initial condition $x_{0}$ with some noise $\omega$ for $s$ seconds and then through $t$ seconds with the same noise (as started from the $s$ seconds mark) gives the same result as evolving $x_{0}$ through $(t + s)$ seconds with that same noise.

:::

## Attractors for random dynamical systems {#Attractors_for_random_dynamical_systems}

The notion of an [attractor](wikipedia://en/191094) for a random dynamical system is not as straightforward to define as in the deterministic case. It is necessary to "rewind time", as in the definition of a [pullback attractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pullback_attractor), so that the noise close to the final time remains consistent.[^o11] Moreover, the attractor is dependent upon the realisation $\omega$ of the noise.
