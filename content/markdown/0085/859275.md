# Displacement (geometry)

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Displacement"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) and [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics), a **displacement** is a [vector](wikipedia://en/32533) whose length is the shortest [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) from the initial to the final [position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_%28vector%29) of a point P undergoing [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion).[^o3] It quantifies both the distance and [direction](wikipedia://en/54516626) of the net or total motion along a straight line from the initial position to the final position of the point [trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectory). A displacement may be identified with the [translation](wikipedia://en/204682) that maps the initial position to the final position. Displacement is the shift in location when an object in motion changes from one position to another.[^o4] For motion over a given interval of time, the displacement divided by the length of the time interval defines the [average velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Average_velocity) (a vector), whose magnitude is the [average speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Average_speed) (a scalar quantity), over the motion on this time interval.

:::

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Formulation"}

## Formulation {#Formulation}

A displacement may be formulated as a *[relative position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_position)* (resulting from the motion), that is, as the final position $x_{f}$ of a point relative to its initial position $x_{i}$. The corresponding displacement vector can be defined as the [difference](wikipedia://en/298834#Subtraction_and_Weyl's_axioms) between the final and initial positions:

$$s = x_\textrm{f} - x_\textrm{i} = \Delta{x}$$

:::

## Rigid body {#Rigid_body}

In dealing with the motion of a [rigid body](wikipedia://en/455769), the term *displacement* or *motion* ([rigid body motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigid_body_motion)) may also include the [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789) of the body. In this case, the displacement of a particle of the body is called **linear displacement** (displacement along a line), while the rotation of the body is called *[angular displacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_displacement)*.[^o8]

## Derivatives {#Derivatives}

For a position vector $\mathbf{s}$ that is a function of time $t$, the derivatives can be computed with respect to $t$. The first two derivatives are frequently encountered in physics.

**[Velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903)**

$$\mathbf{v} = \frac{d\mathbf{s}}{dt}$$

**[Acceleration](wikipedia://en/2443)**

$$\mathbf{a} = \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = \frac{d^2\mathbf{s}}{dt^2}$$

**[Jerk](wikipedia://en/16290)**

$$\mathbf{j} = \frac{d\mathbf{a}}{dt} = \frac{d^2\mathbf{v}}{dt^2}=\frac{d^3\mathbf{s}}{dt^3}$$

These common names correspond to terminology used in basic kinematics.[^o10] By extension, the higher order derivatives can be computed in a similar fashion. Study of these higher order derivatives can improve approximations of the original displacement function. Such higher-order terms are required in order to accurately represent the displacement function as [a sum of an infinite series](wikipedia://en/30448), enabling several analytical techniques in engineering and physics. The fourth order derivative is called [jounce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jounce).

## Discussion {#Discussion}

In considering motions of objects over time, the instantaneous [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) of the object is the rate of change of the displacement as a function of time. The instantaneous [speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed), then, is distinct from velocity, or the [time rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_rate) of change of the distance travelled along a specific path. The velocity may be equivalently defined as the time rate of change of the position vector. If one considers a moving initial position, or equivalently a moving origin (e.g. an initial position or origin which is fixed to a train wagon, which in turn moves on its rail track), the velocity of P (e.g. a point representing the position of a passenger walking on the train) may be referred to as a *[relative velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_velocity)*; this is opposed to an *[absolute velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_velocity)*, which is computed with respect to a point and coordinate axes which are considered to be at rest (a [inertial frame of reference](wikipedia://en/14838) such as, for instance, a point fixed on the floor of the train station and the usual vertical and horizontal directions).
