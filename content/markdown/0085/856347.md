# Tor functor

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Tor functor"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Tor functors** are the [derived functors](wikipedia://en/433875) of the [tensor product of modules](wikipedia://en/2993836) over a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404). Along with the [Ext functor](wikipedia://en/840758), Tor is one of the central concepts of [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410), in which ideas from [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) are used to construct invariants of algebraic structures. The [homology of groups](wikipedia://en/248635#Group_homology), [Lie algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_algebra_homology), and [associative algebras](wikipedia://en/10499629) can all be defined in terms of Tor. The name comes from a relation between the first Tor group Tor<sub>1</sub> and the [torsion subgroup](wikipedia://en/144052) of an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974).

:::

In the special case of abelian groups, Tor was introduced by [Eduard Čech](wikipedia://en/859830) in 1935[^o2] and named by [Samuel Eilenberg](wikipedia://en/362501) around 1950.[^o3] It was first applied to the [Künneth theorem](wikipedia://en/918047) and [universal coefficient theorem](wikipedia://en/996107) in topology. For modules over any [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), Ext was defined by [Henri Cartan](wikipedia://en/362536) and Eilenberg in 1956.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $R$ be a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404). Write $R\textsf{-Mod}$ for the [category](wikipedia://en/5869) of [left $R$-modules](wikipedia://en/276410) and $\textsf{Mod-}R$ for the category of right $R$-modules. (If $R$ is [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346), the two categories can be identified.) For a fixed left $R$-module $B$, let $T(A) = A\otimes_R B$ for $A$ in $\textsf{Mod-}R$. This is a [right exact functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_exact_functor) from $\textsf{Mod-}R$ to the [category of abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454748) $\textbf{Ab}$, and so it has left [derived functors](wikipedia://en/433875) $\mathcal L_i T$. The Tor groups are the abelian groups defined by

$$\operatorname{Tor}_i^R(A,B) = (\mathcal L_iT)(A),$$

for an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $i$. By definition, this means: take any [projective resolution](wikipedia://en/364488#Projective_resolutions)

$$\cdots\to P_2 \to P_1 \to P_0 \to A\to 0,$$

and remove $A$, and form the [chain complex](wikipedia://en/138404):

$$\cdots \to P_2\otimes_R B \to P_1\otimes_R B \to P_0\otimes_R B \to 0$$

For each integer $i$, the group $\operatorname{Tor}_i^R(A,B)$ is the [homology](wikipedia://en/138404) of this complex at position $i$. It is zero for $i$ negative. Moreover, $\operatorname{Tor}_0^R(A,B)$ is the [cokernel](wikipedia://en/279715) of the map $P_1\otimes_R B \to P_0\otimes_R B$, which is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to $A \otimes_R B$.

Alternatively, one can define $\mathrm{Tor}$ by fixing $A$ and taking the left derived functors of the right exact functor $G(B)=A\otimes_RB$. That is, tensor $A$ with a projective resolution of $B$ and take homology. Cartan and Eilenberg showed that these constructions are independent of the choice of projective resolution, and that both constructions yield the same Tor groups.[^o7] Moreover, for a fixed ring $R$, $\mathrm{Tor}$ is a functor in each variable (from $R$-modules to abelian groups).

For a commutative ring $R$ and $R$-modules $A$ and $B$, $\operatorname{Tor}^R_i(A,B)$ is an $R$-module (using that $A\otimes_RB$ is an $R$-module in this case). For a non-commutative ring $R$, $\operatorname{Tor}^R_i(A,B)$ is only an abelian group, in general. If $R$ is an [algebra over a ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_over_a_ring) $S$ (which means in particular that $S$ is commutative), then $\operatorname{Tor}^R_i(A,B)$ is at least an $S$-module.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

Here are some of the basic properties and computations of Tor groups.[^o10]

- $\mathrm{Tor}_0^R(A,B)\cong A\otimes_RB$ for any right $R$-module $A$ and left $R$-module $B$.
- $\mathrm{Tor}_i^R(A,B)=0$ for all $i>0$ if either $A$ or $B$ is [flat](wikipedia://en/492616) (for example, [free](wikipedia://en/326454)) as an $R$-module. In fact, one can compute Tor using a flat resolution of either $A$ or $B$; this is more general than a projective (or free) resolution.[^o11]
- If $A, B$ are finitely generated abelian groups, then $\operatorname{Tor}_1^\mathbb{Z}(A,B) \cong A_{\text{tor}} \otimes_\mathbb{Z} B_{\text{tor}}$, where $A_{\text{tor}}$ is the [torsion subgroup](wikipedia://en/144052) of $A$.
- There are converses to the previous statement:
  - If $\mathrm{Tor}_1^R(A,B)=0$ for all $B$, then $A$ is flat (and hence $\mathrm{Tor}_i^R(A,B)=0$ for all $i>0$).
  - If $\mathrm{Tor}_1^R(A,B)=0$ for all $A$, then $B$ is flat (and hence $\mathrm{Tor}_i^R(A,B)=0$ for all $i>0$).
- By the general properties of [derived functors](wikipedia://en/433875), every [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) $0\to K\to L\to M\to 0$ of right $R$-modules induces a [long exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_exact_sequence) of the form[^o12] $$\cdots \to \operatorname{Tor}_2^R(M,B) \to \operatorname{Tor}_1^R(K,B) \to \operatorname{Tor}_1^R(L,B) \to \operatorname{Tor}_1^R (M,B) \to K\otimes_R B\to L\otimes_R B\to M\otimes_R B\to 0,$$ for any left $R$-module $B$. The analogous exact sequence also holds for Tor with respect to the second variable.
- Symmetry: for a commutative ring $R$, there is a [natural isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_isomorphism) $\mathrm{Tor}_i^R(A,B)\cong \mathrm{Tor}_i^R(B,A)$.[^o13] (For $R$ commutative, there is no need to distinguish between left and right $R$-modules.)
- If $R$ is a commutative ring and $u$ in $R$ is not a [zero divisor](wikipedia://en/51441), then for any $R$-module $B$, $$\operatorname{Tor}^R_i(R/(u),B)\cong\begin{cases} B/uB & i=0\\ B[u] & i=1\\ 0 &\text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$ where $$B[u] = \{x \in B : ux =0 \}$$ is the $u$-torsion subgroup of $B$. This is the explanation for the name Tor. Taking $R$ to be the ring $\mathbb{Z}$ of integers, this calculation can be used to compute $\operatorname{Tor}^{\mathbb{Z}}_1(A,B)$ for any [finitely generated abelian group](wikipedia://en/58527) $A$.
- Generalizing the previous example, one can compute Tor groups that involve the quotient of a commutative ring by any [regular sequence](wikipedia://en/505119), using the [Koszul complex](wikipedia://en/519992).[^o14] For example, if $R$ is the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ over a field $k$, then $\operatorname{Tor}_*^R(k,k)$ is the [exterior algebra](wikipedia://en/221537) over $k$ on $n$ generators in $\mathrm{Tor}_1$.
- $\operatorname{Tor}^{\mathbb{Z}}_i(A,B)=0$ for all $i\ge 2$. The reason: every [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) $A$ has a free resolution of length 1, since every subgroup of a [free abelian group](wikipedia://en/247296) is free abelian.
- Generalizing the previous example, $\operatorname{Tor}^{R}_i(A,B)=0$ for all $i\ge 2$ if $R$ is a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039) (PID). The reason: every module $A$ over a PID has a free resolution of length 1, since every submodule of a [free module](wikipedia://en/326454) over a PID is free.
- For any ring $R$, Tor preserves [direct sums](wikipedia://en/58899) (possibly infinite) and [filtered colimits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filtered_colimit) in each variable.[^o15] For example, in the first variable, this says that $$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Tor}_i^R \left (\bigoplus_{\alpha} M_{\alpha}, N \right ) &\cong \bigoplus_{\alpha} \operatorname{Tor}_i^R(M_{\alpha},N) \\ \operatorname{Tor}_i^R \left (\varinjlim_{\alpha} M_{\alpha}, N \right ) &\cong \varinjlim_{\alpha} \operatorname{Tor}_i^R(M_{\alpha},N) \end{aligned}$$
- Flat base change: for a commutative flat $R$-algebra $T$, $R$-modules $A$ and $B$, and an integer $i$,[^o16] $$\mathrm{Tor}_i^R(A,B)\otimes_R T \cong \mathrm{Tor}_i^T(A\otimes_R T,B\otimes_R T).$$ It follows that Tor commutes with [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring). That is, for a [multiplicatively closed set](wikipedia://en/21653550) $S$ in $R$, $$S^{-1} \operatorname{Tor}_i^R(A, B) \cong \operatorname{Tor}_i^{S^{-1} R} \left (S^{-1} A, S^{-1} B \right ).$$
- For a commutative ring $R$ and commutative $R$-algebras $A$ and $B$, $\mathrm{Tor}_*^R(A,B)$ has the structure of a [graded-commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded-commutative) algebra over $R$. Moreover, elements of odd degree in the Tor algebra have square zero, and there are [divided power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divided_power) operations on the elements of positive even degree.[^o17]

:::

## Important special cases {#Important_special_cases}

- [Group homology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_homology) is defined by $H_*(G,M)=\operatorname{Tor}^{\mathbb{Z}[G]}_*(\mathbb{Z}, M),$ where *G* is a group, *M* is a [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) of *G* over the integers, and $\mathbb{Z}[G]$ is the [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) of *G*.
- For an [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) *A* over a field *k* and an *A*-[bimodule](wikipedia://en/492612) *M*, [Hochschild homology](wikipedia://en/10499629) is defined by $$HH_*(A,M)=\operatorname{Tor}_*^{A\otimes_k A^{\text{op}}}(A, M).$$
- [Lie algebra homology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_algebra_homology) is defined by $H_*(\mathfrak g,M)=\operatorname{Tor}_*^{U\mathfrak g}(R,M)$, where $\mathfrak g$ is a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) over a commutative ring *R*, *M* is a $\mathfrak g$-module, and $U\mathfrak g$ is the [universal enveloping algebra](wikipedia://en/363551).
- For a commutative ring *R* with a homomorphism onto a field *k*, $\operatorname{Tor}_*^R(k,k)$ is a graded-commutative [Hopf algebra](wikipedia://en/325714) over *k*.[^o19] (If *R* is a [Noetherian local ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_local_ring) with residue field *k*, then the dual Hopf algebra to $\operatorname{Tor}_*^R(k,k)$ is [Ext](wikipedia://en/840758#Important_special_cases)\*(*k*,*k*).) As an algebra, $\operatorname{Tor}_*^R(k,k)$ is the free graded-commutative divided power algebra on a graded vector space π<sub>\*</sub>(*R*).[^o20] When *k* has [characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_of_a_field) zero, π<sub>\*</sub>(*R*) can be identified with the [André-Quillen homology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André-Quillen_homology) *D*<sub>\*</sub>(*k*/*R*,*k*).[^o21]
