# Marston Morse

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Marston Morse"}

**Harold Calvin Marston Morse** (March 24, 1892 – June 22, 1977) was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) best known for his work on the *[calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882) in the large*, a subject where he introduced the technique of [differential topology](wikipedia://en/8562) now known as [Morse theory](wikipedia://en/348560). The [Morse–Palais lemma](wikipedia://en/9610679), one of the key results in Morse theory, is named after him, as is the [Thue–Morse sequence](wikipedia://en/491903), an infinite binary sequence with many applications.

:::

He was elected to the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences) in 1929,[^o3] the United States [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences) in 1932,[^o4] and the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society) in 1936.[^o5] In 1933 he was awarded the [Bôcher Memorial Prize](wikipedia://en/1099872) for his work in [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396). [J. Robert Oppenheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Robert_Oppenheimer) described Morse as "almost a statesman of mathematics."

## Biography {#Biography}

Morse was born in [Waterville, Maine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterville,_Maine) to Ella Phoebe Marston and Howard Calvin Morse in 1892. He received his [bachelor's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor's_degree) from [Colby College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colby_College) (also in Waterville) in 1914.[^o8] At [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University), he received both his [master's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master's_degree) in 1915 and his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD) in 1917. He wrote his PhD thesis, *Certain Types of Geodesic Motion of a Surface of Negative Curvature*, under the direction of [George David Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/30871906).

Morse was married on June 20, 1922 to Celeste Phelps and they had two children, [Meroe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meroë_Morse) and Dryden. The couple divorced. He later married Louise Jefferys on June 13, 1940. They had five children, Julia, William, Elizabeth, Peter, and Louise.[^o4]

Morse was a Benjamin Peirce Instructor at Harvard in 1919–1920, after which he served as an assistant professor at [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University) from 1920 to 1925 and at [Brown University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_University) in 1925–1926. He returned to Harvard in 1926, advancing to professor in 1929, and teaching there until 1935. That year, he accepted a position at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) in [Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey), where he remained until his retirement in 1962.

Morse spent most of his career on a single subject, now known as Morse theory, a branch of [differential topology](wikipedia://en/8562) that enables one to analyze the [topology](wikipedia://en/30450) of a [smooth manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) by studying [differentiable functions](wikipedia://en/330206) on that manifold. Morse originally applied his theory to [geodesics](wikipedia://en/91096) ([critical points](wikipedia://en/1951424) of the [energy](wikipedia://en/198319) [functional](wikipedia://en/1374948) on [paths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_%28mathematics%29)); these techniques were used in [Raoul Bott](wikipedia://en/337457)'s proof of his [periodicity theorem](wikipedia://en/782099). Morse theory is a very important subject in modern [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416), such as [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305).

Morse died on June 22, 1977, at his home in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey).[^o11] His second wife, Louise Jeffreys, died in 2016.[^o12]
