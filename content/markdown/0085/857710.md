# Time–frequency representation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Time–frequency representation"}

A **time–frequency representation** (**TFR**) is a view of a [signal](wikipedia://en/29324) (taken to be a function of time) represented over both time and [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779).[^o2] [Time–frequency analysis](wikipedia://en/857897) means analysis into the time–frequency domain provided by a TFR. This is achieved by using a formulation often called "Time–Frequency Distribution", abbreviated as TFD.

:::

TFRs are often complex-valued fields over time and frequency, where the [modulus](wikipedia://en/991#Complex_numbers) of the field represents either amplitude or "energy density" (the concentration of the [root mean square](wikipedia://en/66819) over time and frequency), and the [argument](wikipedia://en/14699765) of the field represents phase.

## Background and motivation {#Background_and_motivation}

A [signal](wikipedia://en/29324), as a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) of time, may be considered as a representation with perfect *time resolution*. In contrast, the [magnitude](wikipedia://en/577301) of the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) (FT) of the signal may be considered as a representation with perfect *spectral resolution* but with no time information because the magnitude of the FT conveys frequency content but it fails to convey when, in time, different events occur in the signal.

TFRs provide a bridge between these two representations in that they provide *some* temporal information ***and*** *some* spectral information simultaneously. Thus, TFRs are useful for the representation and analysis of signals containing multiple time-varying frequencies.

## Formulation of TFRs and TFDs {#Formulation_of_TFRs_and_TFDs}

One form of TFR (or TFD) can be formulated by the multiplicative comparison of a signal with itself, expanded in different directions about each point in time. Such representations and formulations are known as [quadratic](wikipedia://en/187240) or "bilinear" TFRs or TFDs (QTFRs or QTFDs) because the representation is quadratic in the signal (see [Bilinear time–frequency distribution](wikipedia://en/15249674)). This formulation was first described by [Eugene Wigner](wikipedia://en/10101) in 1932 in the context of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) and, later, reformulated as a general TFR by Ville in 1948 to form what is now known as the [Wigner–Ville distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wigner–Ville_distribution), as it was shown in [^o5] that Wigner's formula needed to use the [analytic signal](wikipedia://en/950777) defined in Ville's paper to be useful as a representation and for a practical analysis. Today, QTFRs include the [spectrogram](wikipedia://en/263317) (squared magnitude of [short-time Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/436912)), the [scaleogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaleogram) (squared magnitude of Wavelet transform) and the smoothed pseudo-Wigner distribution.

Although quadratic TFRs offer perfect temporal and spectral resolutions simultaneously, the quadratic nature of the transforms creates cross-terms, also called "interferences". The cross-terms caused by the bilinear structure of TFDs and TFRs may be useful in some applications such as classification as the cross-terms provide extra detail for the recognition algorithm. However, in some other applications, these cross-terms may plague certain quadratic TFRs and they would need to be reduced. One way to do this is obtained by comparing the signal with a different function. Such resulting representations are known as linear TFRs because the representation is linear in the signal. An example of such a representation is the *windowed Fourier transform* (also known as the [short-time Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/436912)) which localises the signal by modulating it with a [window function](wikipedia://en/244097), before performing the Fourier transform to obtain the frequency content of the signal in the region of the window.

## Wavelet transforms {#Wavelet_transforms}

Wavelet transforms, in particular the [continuous wavelet transform](wikipedia://en/679596), expand the signal in terms of wavelet functions which are localised in both time and frequency. Thus the wavelet transform of a signal may be represented in terms of both time and frequency. Continuous wavelet transform analysis is very useful for identifying non-stationary signals in [time series](wikipedia://en/406624),[^o7] such as those related to climate[^o8] or landslides.[^o9]

The notions of time, frequency, and amplitude used to generate a TFR from a wavelet transform were originally developed intuitively. In 1992, a quantitative derivation of these relationships was published, based upon a [stationary phase approximation](wikipedia://en/1459010).[^o10]

## Linear canonical transformation {#Linear_canonical_transformation}

[Linear canonical transformations](wikipedia://en/4415205) are the [linear transforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transform) of the time–frequency representation that preserve the [symplectic form](wikipedia://en/292852). These include and generalize the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247), [fractional Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/1103773), and others, thus providing a unified view of these transforms in terms of their action on the time–frequency domain.
