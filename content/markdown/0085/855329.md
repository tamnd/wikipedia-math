# Turn (angle)

360 degrees

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Turn"}

The **turn** (symbol **tr** or **pla**) is a unit of [plane angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_angle) measurement that is the measure of a [complete angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_angle)—the angle [subtended](wikipedia://en/1999527) by a complete [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) at its center. One turn is equal to $2\pi$ [radians](wikipedia://en/26003), 360 [degrees](wikipedia://en/1195294) or 400 [gradians](wikipedia://en/201629). As an [angular unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_unit), one turn also corresponds to one **cycle** (symbol **cyc** or **c**)[^o35] or to one **revolution** (symbol **rev** or **r**).[^o36] Common related [units of frequency](wikipedia://en/10779#Unit) are *[cycles per second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycles_per_second)* (cps) and *[revolutions per minute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_per_minute)* (rpm). The angular unit of the turn is useful in connection with, among other things, [electromagnetic coils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_coil) (e.g., [transformers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer)), rotating objects, and the [winding number](wikipedia://en/86113) of curves. Divisions of a turn include the half-turn and quarter-turn, spanning a [straight angle](wikipedia://en/1196#Individual_angles) and a [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956), respectively; [metric prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefixes) can also be used as in, e.g., centiturns (ctr), milliturns (mtr), etc.

:::

In the [ISQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Quantities), an arbitrary "number of turns" (also known as "number of revolutions" or "number of cycles") is formalized as a [dimensionless quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless_quantity) called ***rotation***, defined as the [ratio](wikipedia://en/87837) of a given angle and a full turn. It is represented by the symbol *N*. (See below for the formula.)

Because one turn is $2\pi$ radians, some have proposed representing $2\pi$ with the single letter [𝜏 (tau)](wikipedia://en/32341736).[^o3]

## Unit symbols {#Unit_symbols}

There are several unit symbols for the turn.

### EU and Switzerland {#EU_and_Switzerland}

The German standard [DIN 1315](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DIN_1315) (March 1974) proposed the unit symbol "pla" (from Latin: plenus angulus 'full angle') for turns.[^o27][^o28] Covered in DIN 1301-1 (October 2010), the so-called Vollwinkel ('full angle') is not an [SI unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_unit). However, it is a [legal unit of measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_unit_of_measurement) in the EU[^o24][^o25] and Switzerland.[^o26]

### Calculators {#Calculators}

The scientific calculators [HP&nbsp;39gII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP&nbsp;39gII) and [HP&nbsp;Prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP&nbsp;Prime) support the unit symbol "tr" for turns since 2011 and 2013, respectively. Support for "tr" was also added to [newRPL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NewRPL) for the [HP&nbsp;50g](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP&nbsp;50g) in 2016, and for the [hp&nbsp;39g+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hp&nbsp;39g+), [HP&nbsp;49g+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP&nbsp;49g+), [HP&nbsp;39gs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP&nbsp;39gs), and [HP&nbsp;40gs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP&nbsp;40gs) in 2017.[^o29][^o30] An angular mode TURN was suggested for the [WP&nbsp;43S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WP&nbsp;43S) as well,[^o32] but the calculator instead implements "MUL$\pi$" (*[multiples of $\pi$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiples_of_π)*) as mode and unit since 2019.[^o33][^o34]

## Divisions {#Divisions}

Many angle units are defined as a division of the turn. For example, the [degree](wikipedia://en/1195294) is defined such that one turn is 360 degrees.

Using [metric prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefix), the turn can be divided in 100 centiturns or 1000 milliturns, with each milliturn corresponding to an [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) of 0.36°, which can also be written as [21′ 36″](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minute_and_second_of_arc).[^o20][^o21] A [protractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protractor) divided in centiturns is normally called a "[percentage](wikipedia://en/64493) protractor". While percentage protractors have existed since 1922,[^o19] the terms centiturns, milliturns and microturns were introduced much later by the British astronomer [Fred Hoyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Hoyle) in 1962.[^o20][^o21] Some measurement devices for artillery and [satellite watching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_watching) carry milliturn scales.[^o22][^o23]

[Binary fractions of a turn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_angular_measurement) are also used. Sailors have traditionally divided a turn into 32 [compass points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Points_of_the_compass), which implicitly have an angular separation of $\tfrac{1}{32}$ turn. The *binary degree*, also known as the *[binary radian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_radian)* (or *brad*), is $\tfrac{1}{256}$ turn.[^o17] The binary degree is used in computing so that an angle can be represented to the maximum possible precision in a single [byte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byte). Other measures of angle used in computing may be based on dividing one whole turn into $2^{n}$ equal parts for other values of $n$.[^o18]

## In the ISQ/SI {#In_the_ISQ/SI}

In the [International System of Quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Quantities) (ISQ), **rotation** (symbol ***N***) is a [physical quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_quantity) defined as **number of revolutions**:[^o37]

> *N* is the number (not necessarily an integer) of revolutions, for example, of a rotating body about a given axis. Its value is given by:
>
> $N = \frac{\varphi}{2 \pi \text{ rad}}$ where denotes the measure of [rotational displacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_displacement).

The above definition is part of the ISQ, formalized in the international standard [ISO 80000-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_80000-3) (Space and time),[^o37] and adopted in the [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) (SI).[^o38][^o39]

Rotation count or number of revolutions is a [quantity of dimension one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantity_of_dimension_one), resulting from a ratio of angular displacement. It can be negative and also greater than 1 in modulus. The relationship between quantity rotation, *N*, and unit turns, tr, can be expressed as:

$$N = \frac \varphi \text{tr} = \{ \varphi \}_\text{tr}$$

where {}<sub>tr</sub> is the numerical value of the angle in units of turns (see *Physical quantity#Components*).

In the ISQ/SI, rotation is used to derive [rotational frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_frequency) (the [rate of change](wikipedia://en/7921) of rotation with respect to time), denoted by $n$:

$$n = \frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t}$$

The SI unit of rotational frequency is the [reciprocal second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_second) (s<sup>−1</sup>). Common related [units of frequency](wikipedia://en/10779#Unit) are *[hertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertz)* (Hz), *[cycles per second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycles_per_second)* (cps), and *[revolutions per minute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_per_minute)* (rpm).

The superseded version ISO 80000-3:2006 defined "revolution" as a special name for the [dimensionless unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless_unit) "one", which also received other special names, such as the radian. Despite their [dimensional homogeneity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensional_homogeneity), these two specially named dimensionless units are applicable for non-comparable [kinds of quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kind_of_quantity): rotation and angle, respectively.[^o13] "Cycle" is also mentioned in ISO 80000-3, in the definition of *[period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Period_%28physics%29)*.
