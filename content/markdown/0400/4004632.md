# Quotient category

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quotient category"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **quotient category** is a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) obtained from another category by identifying sets of [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891). Formally, it is a [quotient object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_object) in the [category of (locally small) categories](wikipedia://en/2072741), analogous to a [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) or [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213), but in the categorical setting.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $\mathbf C$ be a category. A *[congruence relation](wikipedia://en/48167)* $\mathcal R$ on $\mathbf C$ is given by: for each pair of objects $X$, $Y$ in $\mathbf C$, an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) $\mathcal R_{X,Y}$ on $\mathrm{Hom}(X,Y)$, such that the equivalence relations respect composition of morphisms. That is, if

$$f_1,f_2 : X \to Y\,$$

are related in $\mathrm{Hom}(X, Y)$ and

$$g_1,g_2 : Y \to Z\,$$

are related in $\mathrm{Hom}(Y, Z)$, then $g_1f_1$ and $g_2f_2$ are related in $\mathrm{Hom}(X, Z)$.

Given a congruence relation $\mathcal R$ on $\mathbf C$ we can define the **quotient category** $\mathbf C/\mathcal R$ as the category whose objects are those of $\mathbf C$ and whose morphisms are [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of morphisms in $\mathbf C$. That is,

$$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{C}/\mathcal R}(X,Y) = \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{C}}(X,Y)/\mathcal R_{X,Y}.$$

Composition of morphisms in $\mathbf C/\mathcal R$ is [well-defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-defined) since $\mathcal R$ is a congruence relation.

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

There is a natural quotient [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from $\mathbf C$ to $\mathbf C/\mathcal R$ which sends each morphism to its equivalence class. This functor is bijective on objects and surjective on Hom-sets (i.e. it is a [full functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_functor)).

Every functor $F\colon \mathbf C \to \mathbf D$ determines a congruence on $\mathbf C$ by saying $f\sim g$ [iff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iff) $F(f)=F(g)$. The functor $F$ then factors through the quotient functor $\mathbf C\to\mathbf C/\sim$ in a unique manner. This may be regarded as the "[first isomorphism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_isomorphism_theorem)" for categories.

:::

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- [Monoids](wikipedia://en/19652) and [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) may be regarded as categories with one object. In this case the quotient category coincides with the notion of a [quotient monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_monoid) or a [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526).
- The [homotopy category of topological spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_category_of_topological_spaces) **hTop** is a quotient category of **Top**, the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051). The equivalence classes of morphisms are [homotopy classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_class) of continuous maps.
- Let *k* be a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) and consider the [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063) Mod(*k*) of all [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) over *k* with *k*-linear maps as morphisms. To "kill" all finite-dimensional spaces, we can call two linear maps *f*,*g* : *X* → *Y* congruent iff their difference has finite-dimensional image. In the resulting quotient category, all finite-dimensional vector spaces are isomorphic to 0. \[This is actually an example of a quotient of additive categories, see below.\]

:::

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

### Quotients of additive categories modulo ideals {#Quotients_of_additive_categories_modulo_ideals}

If *C* is an [additive category](wikipedia://en/60854) and we require the congruence relation ~ on *C* to be additive (i.e. if *f*<sub>1</sub>, *f*<sub>2</sub>, *g*<sub>1</sub> and *g*<sub>2</sub> are morphisms from *X* to *Y* with *f*<sub>1</sub> ~ *f*<sub>2</sub> and *g*<sub>1</sub> ~*g*<sub>2</sub>, then *f*<sub>1</sub> + *g*<sub>1</sub> ~ *f*<sub>2</sub> + *g*<sub>2</sub>), then the quotient category *C*/~ will also be additive, and the quotient functor *C* → *C*/~ will be an additive functor.

The concept of an additive congruence relation is equivalent to the concept of a *two-sided ideal of morphisms*: for any two objects *X* and *Y* we are given an additive subgroup *I*(*X*,*Y*) of Hom<sub>*C*</sub>(*X*, *Y*) such that for all *f* ∈ *I*(*X*,*Y*), *g* ∈ Hom<sub>*C*</sub>(*Y*, *Z*) and *h*∈ Hom<sub>*C*</sub>(*W*, *X*), we have *gf* ∈ *I*(*X*,*Z*) and *fh* ∈ *I*(*W*,*Y*). Two morphisms in Hom<sub>*C*</sub>(*X*, *Y*) are congruent iff their difference is in *I*(*X*,*Y*).

Every unital [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) may be viewed as an additive category with a single object, and the quotient of additive categories defined above coincides in this case with the notion of a [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) modulo a two-sided ideal.

### Localization of a category {#Localization_of_a_category}

The [localization of a category](wikipedia://en/611460) introduces new morphisms to turn several of the original category's morphisms into isomorphisms. This tends to increase the number of morphisms between objects, rather than decrease it as in the case of quotient categories. But in both constructions it often happens that two objects become isomorphic that weren't isomorphic in the original category.

### Serre quotients of abelian categories {#Serre_quotients_of_abelian_categories}

The [Serre quotient](wikipedia://en/48897477) of an [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063) by a [Serre subcategory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serre_subcategory) is a new abelian category which is similar to a quotient category but also in many cases has the character of a localization of the category.
