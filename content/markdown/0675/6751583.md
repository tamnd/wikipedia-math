# Bochner space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bochner space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Bochner spaces** are a generalization of the concept of [$L^p$ spaces](wikipedia://en/45194) to functions whose values lie in a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) which is not necessarily the space $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ of real or complex numbers.

:::

The space $L^p(X)$ consists of (equivalence classes of) all [Bochner measurable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bochner_measurable) functions $f$ with values in the Banach space $X$ whose [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) $\|f\|_X$ lies in the standard $L^p$ space. Thus, if $X$ is the set of complex numbers, it is the standard Lebesgue $L^p$ space.

Almost all standard results on $L^p$ spaces do hold on Bochner spaces too; in particular, the Bochner spaces $L^p(X)$ are Banach spaces for $1 \leq p \leq \infty.$

Bochner spaces are named for the [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Salomon Bochner](wikipedia://en/987847).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given a [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270) $(T, \Sigma; \mu),$ a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) $\left(X, \|\,\cdot\,\|_X\right)$ and $1 \leq p \leq \infty,$ the **Bochner space** $L^p(T; X)$ is defined to be the [Kolmogorov quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov_quotient) (by equality [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688)) of the space of all [Bochner measurable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bochner_measurable) functions $u : T \to X$ such that the corresponding norm is finite:

$$\|u\|_{L^p(T; X)} := \left( \int_{T} \| u(t) \|_{X}^{p} \, \mathrm{d} \mu (t) \right)^{1/p} < + \infty \mbox{ for } 1 \leq p < \infty,$$

$$\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(T; X)} := \mathrm{ess\,sup}_{t \in T} \|u(t)\|_{X} < + \infty.$$

In other words, as is usual in the study of $L^p$ spaces, $L^p(T; X)$ is a space of [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of functions, where two functions are defined to be equivalent if they are equal everywhere except upon a $\mu$-[measure zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_zero) subset of $T.$ As is also usual in the study of such spaces, it is usual to [abuse notation](wikipedia://en/1698066) and speak of a "function" in $L^p(T; X)$ rather than an equivalence class (which would be more technically correct).

:::

::: {#o5 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Bochner spaces are often used in the [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) approach to the study of [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564) that depend on time, e.g. the [heat equation](wikipedia://en/166415): if the temperature $g(t, x)$ is a scalar function of time and space, one can write $(f(t))(x) := g(t,x)$ to make $f$ a family $f(t)$ (parametrized by time) of functions of space, possibly in some Bochner space.

### Application to PDE theory {#Application_to_PDE_theory}

Very often, the space $T$ is an [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) of time over which we wish to solve some partial differential equation, and $\mu$ will be one-dimensional [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198). The idea is to regard a function of time and space as a collection of functions of space, this collection being parametrized by time. For example, in the solution of the heat equation on a region $\Omega$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and an interval of time $[0, T],$ one seeks solutions

$$u \in L^2\left([0, T]; H_0^1(\Omega)\right)$$

with time derivative

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} \in L^2 \left([0, T]; H^{-1}(\Omega)\right).$$

Here $H_0^1(\Omega)$ denotes the [Sobolev](wikipedia://en/611964) [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) of once-[weakly differentiable](wikipedia://en/1411087) functions with first weak derivative in $L^2(\Omega)$ that vanish at the [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) of Ω (in the sense of trace, or, equivalently, are limits of smooth functions with [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [support](wikipedia://en/381013) in Ω); $H^{-1} (\Omega)$ denotes the [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) of $H_0^1(\Omega).$

(The "[partial derivative](wikipedia://en/52565)" with respect to time $t$ above is actually a [total derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_derivative), since the use of Bochner spaces removes the space-dependence.)
