# Moser's worm problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Moser's worm problem"}

::: {#o2 .problem cue=unsolved}

What is the minimum area of a shape that can cover every unit-length curve?

:::

*Image: moser\_worm\_problem.svg.* }

**Moser's worm problem** (also known as **mother worm's blanket problem**) is an unsolved problem in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) formulated by the Austrian-Canadian mathematician [Leo Moser](wikipedia://en/745180) in 1966. The problem asks for the region of smallest [area](wikipedia://en/1209) that can accommodate every [plane curve](wikipedia://en/971691) of length 1. Here "accommodate" means that the curve may be [rotated and translated](wikipedia://en/39330) to fit inside the region. In some variations of the problem, the region is restricted to be [convex](wikipedia://en/6292).

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

For example, a [circular disk](wikipedia://en/6220) of radius 1/2 can accommodate any plane curve of length 1 by placing the midpoint of the curve at the center of the disk. Another possible solution has the shape of a [rhombus](wikipedia://en/183884) with vertex angles of 60° and 120° and with a long diagonal of unit length.[^o6] However, these are not optimal solutions; other shapes are known that solve the problem with smaller areas.

:::

## Solution properties {#Solution_properties}

It is not completely trivial that a minimum-area cover exists. An alternative possibility would be that there is some minimal area that can be approached but not actually attained. However, there does exist a smallest [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) cover. Its existence follows from the [Blaschke selection theorem](wikipedia://en/11956019).[^o8]

It is also not trivial to determine whether a given shape forms a cover. (Gerriets Poole 1974) conjectured that a shape accommodates every unit-length curve if and only if it accommodates every unit-length [polygonal chain](wikipedia://en/12056032) with three segments, a more easily tested condition,[^o10] but (Panraksa Wetzel Wichiramala 2007) showed that no finite bound on the number of segments in a polychain would suffice for this test.[^o12]

## Known bounds {#Known_bounds}

The problem remains open, but over a series of papers, researchers have tightened the gap between the known lower and upper bounds. In particular, (Norwood Poole 2003) constructed a (nonconvex) universal cover and showed that the minimum shape has area at most 0.260437;[^o15] (Gerriets Poole 1974) and (Norwood Poole Laidacker 1992) gave weaker upper bounds.[^o18] In the convex case, (Wang 2006) improved an upper bound to 0.270911861.[^o20] (Khandhawit Pagonakis Sriswasdi 2013) used a min-max strategy for the area of a convex set containing a segment, a triangle, and a rectangle to show a lower bound of 0.232239 for a convex cover.[^o22]

In the 1970s, John Wetzel conjectured that a 30° [circular sector](wikipedia://en/1967249) of unit radius is a cover with area $\pi/12 \approx 0.2618$. This conjecture was partially proved for "drapeable" unit arcs in (Movshovich Wetzel 2017);[^o24] it was finally settled in general by (Panraksa Wichiramala 2021).[^o26] This improves the upper bound for the convex cover by about 3%.

## See also {#See_also}

- [Moving sofa problem](wikipedia://en/11413257), the problem of finding a maximum-area shape that can be rotated and translated through an L-shaped corridor
- [Kakeya set](wikipedia://en/588260), a set of minimal area that can accommodate every unit-length line segment (with translations allowed, but not rotations)
- [Lebesgue's universal covering problem](wikipedia://en/52153555), find the smallest convex area that can cover any planar set of unit diameter
- [Bellman's lost-in-a-forest problem](wikipedia://en/55828944), find the shortest path to escape from a forest of known size and shape.
