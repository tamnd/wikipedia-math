# Shapiro–Francia test

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Shapiro–Francia test"}

The **Shapiro–Francia test** is a [statistical test for the normality](wikipedia://en/8617378) of a population, based on sample data. It was introduced by [S. S. Shapiro](wikipedia://en/47099472) and R. S. Francia in 1972 as a simplification of the [Shapiro–Wilk test](wikipedia://en/2690983).[^o2]

:::

## Theory {#Theory}

Let $x_{(i)}$ be the $i$-th ordered value from our size-$n$ sample. For example, if the sample consists of the values $\left\{ 5.6, -1.2, 7.8, 3.4 \right\}$, $x_{(2)} = 3.4$, because that is the second-lowest value. Let $m_{i:n}$ be the [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) of the $i$th [order statistic](wikipedia://en/160986) when making $n$ independent draws from a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462). For example, $m_{2:4} \approx -0.297$, meaning that the second-lowest value in a sample of four draws from a normal distribution is typically about 0.297 standard deviations below the mean.[^o4] Form the [Pearson correlation coefficient](wikipedia://en/221708) between the $x$ and the $m$:

$$W' = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(x, m)}{\sigma_x \sigma_m} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_{(i)} - \bar{x}) (m_i - \bar{m})}{\sqrt{\left( \sum_{i=1}^n (x_{(i)} - \bar{x})^2 \right) \left( \sum_{i=1}^n (m_i - \bar{m})^2 \right)}}$$

Under the [null hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis) that the data is drawn from a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462), this correlation will be strong, so $W'$ values will cluster just under 1, with the peak becoming narrower and closer to 1 as $n$ increases. If the data deviate strongly from a normal distribution, $W'$ will be smaller.[^o2]

This test is a formalization of the older practice of forming a [Q–Q plot](wikipedia://en/4031859) to compare two distributions, with the $x$ playing the role of the quantile points of the sample distribution and the $m$ playing the role of the corresponding quantile points of a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462).

Compared to the [Shapiro–Wilk test](wikipedia://en/2690983) statistic $W$, the Shapiro–Francia test statistic $W'$ is easier to compute, because it does not require that we form and invert the matrix of covariances between order statistics. It can be expressed in the more analogous form

$$W' = \frac{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^n b_i x_{(i)}\right)}^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n {\left(x_i-\overline{x}\right)}^2},$$

where $b = {(m^\intercal m)}^{-1/2} m$.[^o6]

## Practice {#Practice}

There is no known [closed-form analytic expression](wikipedia://en/585143) for the values of $m_{i:n}$ required by the test. There, are however, several approximations that are adequate for most practical purposes.[^o4]

The exact form of the null distribution of $W'$ is known only for $n=3$.[^o2] [Monte-Carlo](wikipedia://en/56098) simulations have shown that the transformed statistic $\ln(1-W')$ is nearly normally distributed, with values of the mean and standard deviation that vary slowly with $n$ in an easily parametrized form. A transformation validated up to $n=5000$ is available.[^o6]

A common further simplification due to Welsberg and Bingham substitutes *m* with the Blom scores $\tilde{m}_i = \Phi^{-1}\left[(i-\frac{3}{8})/(n+{1}{4})\right]$. This is used in Royston's method of approximating the *a* of Shapiro-Wilk.[^o6]

## Power {#Power}

Comparison studies have concluded that order statistic correlation tests such as Shapiro–Francia and [Shapiro–Wilk](wikipedia://en/2690983) are among the most [powerful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_power) of the established [statistical tests for normality](wikipedia://en/8617378).[^o8] One might assume that the covariance-adjusted weighting of different order statistics used by the [Shapiro–Wilk test](wikipedia://en/2690983) should make it slightly better, but in practice the Shapiro–Wilk and Shapiro–Francia variants are about equally good. In fact, the Shapiro–Francia variant actually exhibits more power to distinguish some alternative hypothesis.[^o9]
