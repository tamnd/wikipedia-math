# Hyperbolic group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hyperbolic group"}

In [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), more precisely in [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164), a **hyperbolic group**, also known as a *word hyperbolic group* or *Gromov hyperbolic group*, is a finitely generated [group](wikipedia://en/19447) equipped with a [word metric](wikipedia://en/1289716) satisfying certain properties abstracted from classical [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291). The notion of a hyperbolic group was introduced and developed by (). The inspiration came from various existing mathematical theories: hyperbolic geometry but also low-dimensional topology (in particular the results of [Max Dehn](wikipedia://en/570602) concerning the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of a hyperbolic [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181), and more complex phenomena in [three-dimensional topology](wikipedia://en/1018257)), and [combinatorial group theory](wikipedia://en/979834). In a very influential (over 1000 citations[^o3]) chapter from 1987, Gromov proposed a wide-ranging research program. Ideas and foundational material in the theory of hyperbolic groups also stem from the work of [George Mostow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Mostow), [William Thurston](wikipedia://en/39190), [James W. Cannon](wikipedia://en/19404886), [Eliyahu Rips](wikipedia://en/673693), and many others.

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $G$ be a finitely generated group, and $X$ be its [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) with respect to some finite set $S$ of generators. The set $X$ is endowed with its [graph metric](wikipedia://en/1020021) (in which edges are of length one and the distance between two vertices is the minimal number of edges in a path connecting them) which turns it into a [length space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Length_space). The group $G$ is then said to be *hyperbolic* if $X$ is a [hyperbolic space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Δ-hyperbolic_space) in the sense of Gromov. Shortly, this means that there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that any geodesic triangle in $X$ is $\delta$-slim, as illustrated in the figure on the right (the space is then said to be $\delta$-hyperbolic).

A priori this definition depends on the choice of a finite generating set $S$. That this is not the case follows from the two following facts:

- the Cayley graphs corresponding to two finite generating sets are always [quasi-isometric](wikipedia://en/12747259) one to the other;
- any geodesic space which is quasi-isometric to a geodesic Gromov-hyperbolic space is itself Gromov-hyperbolic.

Thus we can legitimately speak of a finitely generated group $G$ being hyperbolic without referring to a generating set. On the other hand, a space which is quasi-isometric to a $\delta$-hyperbolic space is itself $\delta'$-hyperbolic for some $\delta' > 0$ but the latter depends on both the original $\delta$ and on the quasi-isometry, thus it does not make sense to speak of $G$ being $\delta$-hyperbolic.

### Remarks {#Remarks}

The [Švarc–Milnor lemma](wikipedia://en/51457085)[^n1] states that if a group $G$ acts [properly discontinuously](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Remarkable_properties_of_actions) and with compact quotient (such an action is often called *geometric*) on a proper length space $Y$, then it is finitely generated, and any Cayley graph for $G$ is quasi-isometric to $Y$. Thus a group is (finitely generated and) hyperbolic if and only if it has a geometric action on a proper hyperbolic space.

If $G' \subset G$ is a subgroup with finite index (i.e., the set $G/G'$ is finite), then the inclusion induces a quasi-isometry on the vertices of any locally finite Cayley graph of $G'$ into any locally finite Cayley graph of $G$. Thus $G'$ is hyperbolic if and only if $G$ itself is. More generally, if two groups are [commensurable](wikipedia://en/29343178), then one is hyperbolic if and only if the other is.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Elementary hyperbolic groups {#Elementary_hyperbolic_groups}

The simplest examples of hyperbolic groups are [finite groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_groups) (whose Cayley graphs are of finite diameter, hence $\delta$-hyperbolic with $\delta$ equal to this diameter).

Another simple example is given by the infinite cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}$: the Cayley graph of $\mathbb{Z}$ with respect to the generating set $\{ \pm 1 \}$ is a line, so all triangles are line segments and the graph is $0$-hyperbolic. It follows that any group which is [virtually cyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtually_cyclic_group) (contains a copy of $\mathbb{Z}$ of finite index) is also hyperbolic, for example the [infinite dihedral group](wikipedia://en/4296050).

Members in this class of groups are often called *elementary hyperbolic groups* (the terminology is adapted from that of actions on the hyperbolic plane).

### Free groups and groups acting on trees {#Free_groups_and_groups_acting_on_trees}

Let $S = \{a_1, \ldots, a_n\}$ be a finite set and $F$ be the [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) with generating set $S$. Then the Cayley graph of $F$ with respect to $S$ is a locally finite [tree](wikipedia://en/48560) and hence a 0-hyperbolic space. Thus $F$ is a hyperbolic group.

More generally we see that any group $G$ which acts properly discontinuously on a locally finite tree (in this context this means exactly that the stabilizers in $G$ of the vertices are finite) is hyperbolic. Indeed, this follows from the fact that $G$ has an invariant subtree on which it acts with compact quotient, and the Svarc—Milnor lemma. Such groups are in fact virtually free (i.e. contain a finitely generated free subgroup of finite index), which gives another proof of their hyperbolicity.

An interesting example is the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019) $G = \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$: it acts on the tree given by the 1-skeleton of the associated [tessellation of the hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/325019#Tessellation_of_the_hyperbolic_plane) and it has a finite index free subgroup (on two generators) of index 6 (for example the set of matrices in $G$ which reduce to the identity modulo 2 is such a group). Note an interesting feature of this example: it acts properly discontinuously on a hyperbolic space (the [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane)) but the action is not cocompact (and indeed $G$ is *not* quasi-isometric to the hyperbolic plane).

### Fuchsian groups {#Fuchsian_groups}

Generalising the example of the modular group a [Fuchsian group](wikipedia://en/471365) is a group admitting a properly discontinuous action on the hyperbolic plane (equivalently, a discrete subgroup of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb R)$). The hyperbolic plane is a $\delta$-hyperbolic space and hence the Svarc—Milnor lemma tells us that cocompact Fuchsian groups are hyperbolic.

Examples of such are the [fundamental groups](wikipedia://en/11004) of [closed surfaces](wikipedia://en/27865#Closed_surfaces) of negative [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420). Indeed, these surfaces can be obtained as quotients of the hyperbolic plane, as implied by the Poincaré—Koebe [Uniformisation theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformisation_theorem#Geometric_classification_of_surfaces).

Another family of examples of cocompact Fuchsian groups is given by [triangle groups](wikipedia://en/2069342#The_hyperbolic_case): all but finitely many are hyperbolic.

### Negative curvature {#Negative_curvature}

Generalising the example of closed surfaces, the fundamental groups of compact [Riemannian manifolds](wikipedia://en/144652) with strictly negative [sectional curvature](wikipedia://en/285618) are hyperbolic. For example, cocompact [lattices](wikipedia://en/15782871) in the [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) or [unitary](wikipedia://en/173993) group preserving a form of signature $(n,1)$ are hyperbolic.

A further generalisation is given by groups admitting a geometric action on a [CAT(k) space](wikipedia://en/12941912), when $k$ is any negative number.[^o14] There exist examples which are not commensurable to any of the previous constructions (for instance groups acting geometrically on hyperbolic [buildings](wikipedia://en/275015)).

### Small cancellation groups {#Small_cancellation_groups}

Groups having presentations which satisfy [small cancellation](wikipedia://en/19882883) conditions are hyperbolic. This gives a source of examples which do not have a geometric origin as the ones given above. In fact one of the motivations for the initial development of hyperbolic groups was to give a more geometric interpretation of small cancellation.

### Random groups {#Random_groups}

In some sense, "most" finitely presented groups with large defining relations are hyperbolic. For a quantitative statement of what this means see [Random group](wikipedia://en/31868831).

### Non-examples {#Non-examples}

- The simplest example of a group which is not hyperbolic is the [free rank 2 abelian group](wikipedia://en/247296) $\mathbb Z^2$. Indeed, it is quasi-isometric to the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) which is easily seen not to be hyperbolic (for example because of the existence of [homotheties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothetic_transformation)).
- More generally, any group which contains $\mathbb{Z}^2$ as a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) is not hyperbolic.[^n2][^n3] In particular, [lattices](wikipedia://en/15782871) in higher rank [semisimple Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_Lie_group) and the [fundamental groups](wikipedia://en/11004) $\pi_1(S^3\setminus K)$ of nontrivial [knot](wikipedia://en/366808) complements fall into this category and therefore are not hyperbolic. This is also the case for [mapping class groups](wikipedia://en/793295) of closed hyperbolic surfaces.
- The [Baumslag–Solitar groups](wikipedia://en/11485892) *B*(*m*,*n*) and any group that contains a subgroup isomorphic to some *B*(*m*,*n*) fail to be hyperbolic (since *B*(1,1) = $\mathbb{Z}^2$, this generalizes the previous example).
- A non-uniform lattice in a rank 1 simple Lie group is hyperbolic if and only if the group is [isogenous](wikipedia://en/1085176) to $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ (equivalently the associated symmetric space is the hyperbolic plane). An example of this is given by [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/1248299) [knot groups](wikipedia://en/1164126). Another is the [Bianchi groups](wikipedia://en/3238478), for example $\mathrm{SL}_2(\sqrt{-1})$.

## Properties {#Properties}

::: {#o24 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Algebraic properties"}

### Algebraic properties {#Algebraic_properties}

- Hyperbolic groups satisfy the [Tits alternative](wikipedia://en/12704641): they are either virtually solvable (this possibility is satisfied only by elementary hyperbolic groups) or they have a subgroup isomorphic to a nonabelian free group.
- Non-elementary hyperbolic groups are not [simple](wikipedia://en/47600) in a very strong sense: if $G$ is non-elementary hyperbolic then there exists an infinite subgroup $H \triangleleft G$ such that $H$ and $G/H$ are both infinite.
- It is not known whether there exists a hyperbolic group which is not [residually finite](wikipedia://en/5780875).

:::

### Geometric properties {#Geometric_properties}

- Non-elementary (infinite and not virtually cyclic) hyperbolic groups have always exponential [growth rate](wikipedia://en/498727) (this is a consequence of the Tits alternative).
- Hyperbolic groups satisfy a linear [isoperimetric inequality](wikipedia://en/20017026#Isomperimetric_function).[^n4]

### Homological properties {#Homological_properties}

- Hyperbolic groups are always [finitely presented](wikipedia://en/99494). In fact one can explicitly construct a complex (the [Rips complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rips_complex)) which is [contractible](wikipedia://en/1752414) and on which the group acts geometrically[^n5] so it is of [type *F*<sub>∞</sub>](wikipedia://en/60316348). When the group is torsion-free the action is free, showing that the group has finite [cohomological dimension](wikipedia://en/9880197).
- In 2002, I. Mineyev showed that hyperbolic groups are exactly those finitely generated groups for which the comparison map between the [bounded cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_cohomology) and [ordinary cohomology](wikipedia://en/248635) is surjective in all degrees, or equivalently, in degree 2.[^n6]

### Algorithmic properties {#Algorithmic_properties}

- Hyperbolic groups have a solvable [word problem](wikipedia://en/33563). They are [biautomatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biautomatic_group) and [automatic](wikipedia://en/4224977).[^n7] Indeed, they are [strongly geodesically automatic](wikipedia://en/4224977), that is, there is an automatic structure on the group, where the language accepted by the word acceptor is the set of all geodesic words.
- It was shown in 2010 that hyperbolic groups have a [decidable](wikipedia://en/913118) marked isomorphism problem.[^n8] It is notable that this means that the isomorphism problem, orbit problems (in particular the conjugacy problem) and Whitehead's problem are all decidable.
- Cannon and Swenson have shown that hyperbolic groups with a 2-sphere at infinity have a natural [subdivision rule](wikipedia://en/36799559).[^n9] This is related to [Cannon's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannon's_conjecture).

:::

## Generalisations {#Generalisations}

### Relatively hyperbolic groups {#Relatively_hyperbolic_groups}

[Relatively hyperbolic groups](wikipedia://en/21993809) are a class generalising hyperbolic groups. *Very* roughly[^n10] $G$ is hyperbolic relative to a collection $\mathcal G$ of subgroups if it admits a (*not necessarily cocompact*) properly discontinuous action on a proper hyperbolic space $X$ which is "nice" on the boundary of $X$ and such that the stabilisers in $G$ of points on the boundary are subgroups in $\mathcal G$. This is interesting when both $X$ and the action of $G$ on $X$ are not elementary (in particular $X$ is infinite: for example every group is hyperbolic relatively to itself via its action on a single point!).

Interesting examples in this class include in particular non-uniform lattices in rank 1 semisimple [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945), for example fundamental groups of non-compact hyperbolic manifolds of finite volume. Non-examples are lattices in higher-rank Lie groups and mapping class groups.

### Acylindrically hyperbolic groups {#Acylindrically_hyperbolic_groups}

An even more general notion is that of an acylindrically hyperbolic group.[^n11] Acylindricity of an action of a group $G$ on a metric space $X$ is a weakening of proper discontinuity of the action.[^o39]

A group is said to be acylindrically hyperbolic if it admits a non-elementary acylindrical action on a (*not necessarily proper*) Gromov-hyperbolic space. This notion includes mapping class groups via their actions on [curve complexes](wikipedia://en/51024643). Lattices in higher-rank Lie groups are (still!) not acylindrically hyperbolic.

### CAT(0) groups {#CAT(0)_groups}

In another direction one can weaken the assumption about curvature in the examples above: a *CAT(0) group* is a group admitting a geometric action on a [CAT(0) space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CAT%280%29_space). This includes [Euclidean crystallographic groups](wikipedia://en/463721) and uniform lattices in higher-rank Lie groups.

It is not known whether there exists a hyperbolic group which is not CAT(0).[^o41]
