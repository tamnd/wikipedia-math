# Integral symbol

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Integral symbol"}

The **integral symbol** (*see below*) is used to denote [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532) and [antiderivatives](wikipedia://en/2823) in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially in [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176).

:::

## History {#History}

The notation was introduced by the [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany) mathematician [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) in 1675 in his private writings;[^o4][^o5] it first appeared publicly in the article "De Geometria Recondita et analysi indivisibilium atque infinitorum" (On a hidden geometry and analysis of indivisibles and infinites), published in *[Acta Eruditorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acta_Eruditorum)* in June 1686.[^o6][^o7] The symbol was based on the ſ ([long s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_s)) character and was chosen because Leibniz thought of the integral as an infinite [sum](wikipedia://en/246160) of infinitesimal [summands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summand).

## Typography in Unicode and LaTeX {#Typography_in_Unicode_and_LaTeX}

### Fundamental symbol {#Fundamental_symbol}

The integral symbol is 222B in [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode)[^o10] and `\int` in [LaTeX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LaTeX). In [HTML](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTML), it is written as `&#x222b;` ([hexadecimal](wikipedia://en/13263)), `&#8747;` ([decimal](wikipedia://en/8214)) and `&int;` ([named entity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_XML_and_HTML_character_entity_references#Character_entity_references_in_HTML)).

The original [IBM PC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_PC) [code page 437](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_page_437) character set included a couple of characters ⌠,⎮ and ⌡ (codes 244 and 245 respectively) to build the integral symbol. These were deprecated in subsequent [MS-DOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MS-DOS) [code pages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_page), but they still remain in [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode) ([U+2320 and U+2321](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miscellaneous_Technical#%282300–233F%29) respectively) for compatibility.

The ∫ symbol is very similar to, but not to be confused with, the letter [ʃ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esh_%28letter%29) ("[esh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esh_%28letter%29)").

## Typography in other languages {#Typography_in_other_languages}

*Image: Integral Uprightness.svg.* Regional variations ([English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language), [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language), and [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language) from left to right) of the integral symbol

In other languages, the shape of the integral symbol differs slightly from the shape commonly seen in English-language textbooks. While the [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) integral symbol leans to the right, the [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language) symbol (used throughout [Central Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Europe)) is upright, and the [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language) variant leans slightly to the left to occupy less horizontal space.[^o23]

Another difference is in the placement of [limits](wikipedia://en/12313191) for [definite integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_integral). Generally, in English-language books, limits go to the right of the integral symbol:

$$\int_0^5 f(t) \,\mathrm{d}t, \quad \int_{g(t) = a}^{g(t) = b} f(t) \,\mathrm{d}t.$$

By contrast, in German and Russian texts, the limits are placed above and below the integral symbol, and, as a result, the notation requires larger line spacing but is more compact horizontally, especially when using longer expressions in the limits:

$$\int\limits_0^T f(t) \,\mathrm{d}t, \quad \int\limits_{\!\!\!\!\!g(t) = a\!\!\!\!\!}^{\!\!\!\!\!g(t) = b\!\!\!\!\!} f(t) \,\mathrm{d}t.$$
