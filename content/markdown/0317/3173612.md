# Inductive dimension

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Inductive dimension"}

In the mathematical field of [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), the **inductive dimension** of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X* is either of two values, the **small inductive dimension** ind(*X*) or the **large inductive dimension** Ind(*X*). These are based on the observation that, in *n*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, the [boundaries](wikipedia://en/72824) of [balls](wikipedia://en/160556) have dimension *n* − 1. Therefore it should be possible to define the dimension of a general space [inductively](wikipedia://en/18881) in terms of the dimensions of the boundaries of suitable [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) in that space.

:::

The small and large inductive dimensions are two of the three most usual ways of capturing the notion of "dimension" for a topological space, in a way that depends only on the topology (and not, say, on the properties of a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018)). The other is the [Lebesgue covering dimension](wikipedia://en/398886). The term "topological dimension" is ordinarily understood to refer to the Lebesgue covering dimension. For "sufficiently nice" spaces, the three measures of dimension are equal.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definitions"}

## Formal definitions {#Formal_definitions}

We want the dimension of a point to be 0, and a point has empty boundary, so we start with

$$\operatorname{ind}(\varnothing)=\operatorname{Ind}(\varnothing)=-1.$$

Then inductively, ind(*X*) is the smallest natural number *n* with the following property: for every *$x \in X$* and every open set *U* containing *x*, there is an open set *V* that contains *x* and whose [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) is contained in *U*, such that the [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) of *V* has small inductive dimension less than *n*. Here, the boundary of *V* is considered as a topological space using the [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926) inherited from *X.* (In the case of subspaces of Euclidean space, we may think of the sets *V* as tiny balls centered at *x*.) If no such *n* exists, we write ind(*X*) = ∞.

The large inductive dimension Ind(*X*) is defined to be the smallest *n* such that, for every [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) subset *F* and every open subset *U* containing *F*, there is an open *V* that contains *F* and whose closure is contained in *U*, such that the boundary of *V* has large inductive dimension less than *n*. If no such *n* exists, we write Ind(*X*) = ∞.[^o4]

:::

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

For nice and tame spaces, the inductive dimensions yield the expected answer. Consider for instance the set

$$X=\{(x,y,0) \mid x^2+y^2\leq 1\} \cup \{(0,0,z)\mid 0\leq z < 1\}\cup \{(0,0,-1)\}$$

with the topology inherited from Euclidean space **R**<sup>3</sup>. Intuitively, *X* consists of a 2-dimensional piece attached to a 1-dimensional piece, together with a disjoint 0-dimensional point. Both large and small inductive dimensions of *X* turn out to be 2.

Maybe less expected is $\operatorname{ind} \mathbb{Q} = \operatorname{Ind} \mathbb{Q} = 0.$ This holds because for [irrational numbers](wikipedia://en/20647689) *a* and *b*, the set $\{r\in\mathbb{Q}\mid a<r<b\}$ is both open and closed in $\mathbb{Q}$ and therefore has empty boundary.

:::

## Relationships between dimensions {#Relationships_between_dimensions}

Let $\dim$ be the Lebesgue covering dimension. For any [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X*, we have

$\dim X = 0$ if and only if $\operatorname{Ind} X = 0.$

**Urysohn's theorem** states that when *X* is a [normal space](wikipedia://en/48629) with a [countable base](wikipedia://en/901459), then

$$\dim X = \operatorname{Ind} X = \operatorname{ind} X.$$

Such spaces are exactly the [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) and [metrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrizable) spaces (see [Urysohn's metrization theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urysohn's_metrization_theorem)).

The **Nöbeling–Pontryagin theorem** then states that such spaces with finite dimension are characterised up to homeomorphism as the subspaces of the [Euclidean spaces](wikipedia://en/9697), with their usual topology. The **Menger–Nöbeling theorem** (1932) states that if $X$ is compact metric separable and of dimension $n$, then it embeds as a subspace of Euclidean space of dimension $2 n + 1$. ([Georg Nöbeling](wikipedia://en/14175633) was a student of [Karl Menger](wikipedia://en/149431). He introduced **Nöbeling space**, the subspace of $\mathbf{R}^{2 n + 1}$ consisting of points with at least $n + 1$ co-ordinates being [irrational numbers](wikipedia://en/20647689), which has universal properties for embedding spaces of dimension $n$.)

Assuming only *X* metrizable we have ([Miroslav Katětov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miroslav_Katětov))

ind *X* ≤ Ind *X* = dim *X*;

or assuming *X* [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) and [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) ([P. S. Aleksandrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._S._Aleksandrov))

dim *X* ≤ ind *X* ≤ Ind *X*.

Either inequality here may be strict; an example of Vladimir V. Filippov shows that the two inductive dimensions may differ.

A separable metric space *X* satisfies the inequality $\operatorname{Ind}X\le n$ if and only if for every closed sub-space $A$ of the space $X$ and each continuous mapping $f:A\to S^n$ there exists a continuous extension $\bar f:X\to S^n$.
