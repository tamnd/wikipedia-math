# David Singmaster

::: {#o5 .subject cue=lead name="David Singmaster"}

**David Breyer Singmaster** (14 December 1938 – 13 February 2023) was an American-British mathematician who was emeritus professor of mathematics at [London South Bank University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_South_Bank_University), England. He had a huge personal collection of [mechanical puzzles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_puzzle) and books of [brain teasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain_teaser). He was most famous for being an early adopter and enthusiastic promoter of the [Rubik's Cube](wikipedia://en/25971). His *Notes on Rubik's "Magic Cube"* which he began compiling in 1979 provided the first mathematical analysis of the Cube as well as providing one of the first published solutions. The book contained [his cube notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singmaster_notation) which allowed the recording of [Rubik's Cube](wikipedia://en/25971) moves, and which quickly became the standard.

:::

Singmaster was both a puzzle historian and a composer of puzzles, and many of his puzzles were published in newspapers and magazines. In [combinatorial number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_number_theory), [Singmaster's conjecture](wikipedia://en/4374033) states that there is an upper bound on the number of times a number other than 1 can appear in [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497).

## Career {#Career}

David Singmaster was a student at the [California Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology) in the late 1950s.[^o7] His intention was to become a civil engineer, but he became interested in chemistry and then physics.[^o15][^o8] However he was thrown out of college in his third year for "lack of academic ability".[^o8] After a year working, he switched to the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley).[^o8] He only became really interested in mathematics in his final year when he took some courses in [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527).[^o15] In the autumn semester, his number theory teacher [Dick Lehmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derrick_Henry_Lehmer) posed a prize problem which Singmaster won.[^o15][^o8] In his last semester, his algebra teacher posed a question the teacher didn't know the answer to and Singmaster solved it, eventually leading to two papers.[^o15] He gained his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD) from Berkeley, in 1966. He taught at the [American University of Beirut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_University_of_Beirut), and then lived for a while in [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus).[^o10]

Singmaster moved to [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London) in 1970.[^o11] The "Polytechnic of the South Bank" had been created from a merger of institutions in 1970, and Singmaster became a lecturer in the Department of Mathematical Sciences.[^o17] His academic interests were in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527).[^o10]

In August 1971 he joined an archaeological expedition off the coast of [Sicily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicily), acting as photographer.[^o12] He went off course one day and noticed a timber sticking up out of the sand. This led to the discovery of the [Marsala Punic shipwreck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsala_Punic_shipwreck).[^o12]

Around 1972, he attended the Istituto di Matematica in [Pisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisa) for a year having won a research scholarship.[^o15] He was promoted to a Readership (a Research Professorship) at the South Bank Polytechnic in September 1984.[^o28] The polytechnic college became [London South Bank University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_South_Bank_University) in 1992, and Singmaster was the professor of mathematics at the "School of Computing, Information Systems and Mathematics". He retired in 1996.[^o10] He became an honorary research fellow at [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London).[^o13] He was designated [emeritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emeritus) at [London South Bank University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_South_Bank_University) in 2020.[^o10]

## Rubik's Cubes {#Rubik's_Cubes}

The power of [conjugation](wikipedia://en/49176) ... was the last point I understood; I remember lying awake thinking about it, seeing that I could move any four edges into the working locations and realising that this completed the general method for restoring the cube to its original state.

Singmaster's association with [Rubik's Cubes](wikipedia://en/25971) dates from August 1978, when he saw a Cube (at that time a rarity) at the [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) in [Helsinki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsinki).[^o15] Some other mathematicians at the conference, including [John Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) and [Roger Penrose](wikipedia://en/26193), already had one.[^o15]

Singmaster quickly acquired a Cube (in exchange for a copy of an [M. C. Escher](wikipedia://en/20127) book) and was able to solve it by early September 1978.[^o15] He said that it took him "two weeks, on and off" to find a general solution for the Cube.[^o16] He devised his notation for recording moves (now known as the Singmaster notation) in December 1978.[^o17] In June 1979 he wrote one of the first articles about the Cube in *[The Observer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Observer)* newspaper.[^o18]

In October 1979, he self-published his *Notes on the "Magic Cube"*.[^o19] The booklet contained his mathematical analysis of Rubik's Cube, allowing a solution to be constructed using basic [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890).[^o20] In August 1980, he published an expanded 5th edition of the book retitled as *Notes on Rubik's "Magic Cube"*.[^o19] It included the results of his correspondence with other "cubologists", and included details on monotwists, U-flips, [Cayley graphs](wikipedia://en/358277), and [wreath products](wikipedia://en/146625).[^o20] The book contained his own "step-by-step solution" for the Cube,[^o45] and it is accepted that he was a pioneer of the general [Layer-by-Layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Layer_by_Layer) approach for solving the Cube.[^o46] The book also contained a catalogue of pretty patterns, including his "cube in a cube in a cube" pattern which he had discovered himself "and was very pleased with".[^o21] In 1981, at the height of the Rubik's Cube craze, the book was republished by [Penguin Books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penguin_Books), with a US edition by [Enslow Publishers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enslow_Publishers).[^o19] There were also Dutch and Spanish translations.[^o19] He estimated that he sold around 50,000 to 60,000 copies of his book.[^o15] Much of the mathematical content of the book was later reworked by Alexander H. Frey in collaboration with Singmaster to create their *Handbook of Cubik Math* published in 1982.

Singmaster was described as "one of the most enthusiastic and prolific promoters of the Cube".[^o22] In September 1981, he was said to be devoting "almost 100%" of his time to promoting, reporting, marketing and analysing the Cube.[^o23] He soon began publishing a quarterly newsletter called the *Cubic Circular*, which was published between 1981 and 1985.[^o15][^o23]

## Puzzles {#Puzzles}

Singmaster had one of the world's largest collections of books on recreational mathematics which he had accumulated starting in the late 1970s.[^o25] In 1996, he reported that the collection contained over 4700 works.[^o26] He also collected books on cartoons, humour, and language.[^o15] In 2013, his book collection was reported to be "nearly 10,000 items".[^o25] Many of the books were housed in a library added as an extension to Singmaster's study.[^o25] He had a huge collection of [mechanical puzzles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_puzzle), which he stated in 2002 contained "perhaps 3000 puzzles, of which about 400 are about Rubik's Cube and its variants".[^o15]

From around 1980 to 1982, he ran his own puzzle company, David Singmaster Ltd, which stocked "over 100 puzzles and books".[^o27] However, the venture lost him "a fair amount of money" and led to prolonged tax negotiations.[^o28] He referred to this period of his life as "a massive overdose of cubism".[^o28]

Singmaster was both a puzzle historian and a composer of puzzles, and he described himself as a "metagrobologist". Many of his puzzles appeared in publications such as *[BBC Focus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Focus)*, *[Games & Puzzles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Games_&_Puzzles)*, the *[Los Angeles Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles_Times)*, and the *[Weekend Telegraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Daily_Telegraph)*.[^o29] He published a collection of his puzzles in his 2016 book *Problems for Metagrobologists*.[^o29] From around 2006, Singmaster was a director at the New York–based [Conjuring Arts Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjuring_Arts_Research_Center), retiring from the position (becoming Director Emeritus) in 2013.[^o30] He was instrumental in the re-discovery of one of the world's oldest books on puzzles and [magic illusions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_%28illusion%29) when he came across a reference to the work in a 19th-century manuscript. The recovered text, *De viribus quantitatis* (*On The Powers Of Numbers*), was penned by [Luca Pacioli](wikipedia://en/18473), a Franciscan friar who lived around 1500.[^o31]

## Singmaster's conjecture {#Singmaster's_conjecture}

In [combinatorial number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_number_theory), Singmaster's conjecture states that there is a finite [upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound) on the number of times a number other than 1 can appear in [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497). [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) suspected that the conjecture is true, but thought it would probably be very difficult to prove. The [empirical evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_evidence) is consistent with the proposition that the smallest upper bound is 8.

:::

## Media appearances {#Media_appearances}

In November 1981, Singmaster appeared on the sci-fi-themed [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC) puzzle show *[The Adventure Game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Adventure_Game)*.[^o15] From 1998 to 1999 he was a frequent panelist on the [BBC Radio 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_4) show *[Puzzle Panel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puzzle_Panel)*.[^o15]

## Personal life and death {#Personal_life_and_death}

Singmaster was married twice, the second time to Deborah in 1972. They had one daughter, Jessica, adopted in 1976.[^o15]

Singmaster died on 13 February 2023, at the age of 84.[^o36][^o37]
