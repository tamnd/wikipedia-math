# Kiyosi Itô

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kiyosi Itô"}

**Kiyosi Itô** was a Japanese [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who made fundamental contributions to [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), in particular, the theory of [stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/47895). He invented the concept of [stochastic integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_integral) and [stochastic differential equation](wikipedia://en/1361454), and is known as the founder of so-called [Itô calculus](wikipedia://en/1498680). He also pioneered the connections between stochastic calculus and differential geometry, known as [stochastic differential geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_differential_geometry). He was invited for the [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) in [Stockholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm) in 1962.[^o3] So much were Itô's results useful to financial mathematics that he was sometimes called "the most famous Japanese in Wall Street".[^o16]

:::

Itô was a member of the faculty at [University of Kyoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Kyoto) for most of his career and eventually became the director of their [Research Institute for Mathematical Sciences](wikipedia://en/1978962). But he also spent multi-year stints at several foreign institutions, the longest of which took place at [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University).

## Overview {#Overview}

*Image: Issei Shiraishi and Kiyoshi Ito 1935.jpg.* Itô (right) with Issei Shiraishi in 1935. Shiraishi later became a mathematician.

Itô pioneered the theory of [stochastic integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_integration) and [stochastic differential equations](wikipedia://en/1361454), now known as [Itô calculus](wikipedia://en/1498680). Its basic concept is the [Itô integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itô_integral), and among the most important results is a change of variable formula known as [Itô's lemma](wikipedia://en/203962) (also known as the Itô formula). Itô also made contributions to the study of [diffusion processes](wikipedia://en/1667059) on [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470), known as [stochastic differential geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_differential_geometry).

Itô calculus is a method used in the mathematical study of [random events](wikipedia://en/47895) and is applied in various fields, and is perhaps best known for its use in [mathematical finance](wikipedia://en/33589680). In particular, the Itô's lemma's best known application is in the derivation of the [Black–Scholes equation](wikipedia://en/17656561) for option values.[^o16]

Itô's methods are also used in other fields, including biology and physics.[^o16] His results have been applied to [population models](wikipedia://en/11397469), [white noise](wikipedia://en/46182), [chemical reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction), and [quantum physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_physics), in addition to uses in various mathematical subjects such as [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564), [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), and [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147) and [potential theory](wikipedia://en/1431342).[^o5]

Fellow mathematician [Daniel W. Stroock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_W._Stroock) noted that "People all over realized that what Ito had done explained things that were unexplainable before."[^o12] Economist [Robert C. Merton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_C._Merton) stated that Itô's work had provided him "a very useful tool" in his own prize-winning work.[^o12]

Although the standard [Hepburn romanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepburn_romanization) of his name is *Kiyoshi Itō*, he used the spelling Kiyosi Itô ([Kunrei-shiki romanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kunrei-shiki_romanization)). The alternative spellings Itoh and Ito are also sometimes seen in the [Western world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world).

Itô was married with three daughters.[^o12]

## Biography {#Biography}

*Image: Seizo Ito and Kiyoshi Ito cropped Seizo Ito Kiyoshi Ito Tsuyo Ito Seitaro Ito Seihachi Ito and Mishi Ito 1937.jpg.* Kiyosi Itô (right) with his brother Seizō Itō in 1937. Seizō later became a mathematician.

Itô was born on 7 September 1915 in a farming area located west of [Nagoya, Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagoya,_Japan),[^o12] that being the town of [Hokusei-cho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hokusei,_Mie) in [Mie Prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mie_Prefecture).[^o7] He excelled in his studies as a youth.[^o12] Admitted to the [Imperial University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_University_of_Tokyo), he studied mathematics and became interested in the underdeveloped field of [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), graduating from there in 1938,[^o7] with his degree in mathematics being granted by the [university's Faculty of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graduate_School_of_Science_and_Faculty_of_Science,_University_of_Tokyo).[^o10]

*Image: Kiyoshi Ito 1940.jpg.* Itô at the Cabinet Statistics Bureau in 1940

From 1939 to 1943 he worked as a Statistical Officer with the [Statistics Bureau of the Cabinet Secretariat](wikipedia://en/51926179),[^o10] There he was given rein by management to continue his research.[^o7] His breakthrough paper, "On Stochastic Processes", appeared in 1942.[^o9] In 1943, he was appointed an assistant professor at [Nagoya Imperial University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagoya_Imperial_University),[^o7] where he benefited from discussions with the mathematicians [Kōsaku Yosida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kōsaku_Yosida) and [Shizuo Kakutani](wikipedia://en/2572542).[^o8] From investigations done during this period he published a series of articles in which he defined the [stochastic integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_integral) and laid the foundations of the [Itō calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itō_calculus). Meanwhile, he received his [Doctor of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Science) degree from the Imperial University of Tokyo in 1945.[^o10]

These works were published despite the difficulties of life in [Japan during World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_during_World_War_II), including problems accessing libraries and especially the loss of contact with Western mathematicians and the lack of awareness of results from them.[^o5][^o7] For instance, the only other Japanese mathematician actively interested in Itô's work during the war, [Gisiro Maruyama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gisiro_Maruyama), read a mimeographed copy of a paper while in a military camp.[^o8] Scholarly activity during the [Occupation of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Japan) had its difficulties; in one case, paper shortages were such that a lengthy Itô article could not be published in a Japanese journal and he had to arrange for an American journal to publish it instead.[^o8] Ito later referred to his time at Nagoya as having been during "the dark age of World War II and its aftermath."[^o8]

*Image: Kiyoshi Ito 1954.jpg.* Itô, 1954

After this period he continued to develop his ideas on stochastic analysis with many important papers on the topic. In 1952, he became a professor at the [University of Kyoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Kyoto).[^o16] His most well-known text, *Probability Theory*, appeared in 1953.[^o9] Itô remained affiliated with Kyoto until his retirement in 1979. [^o7] However, beginning in the 1950s, Itô spent long periods of time away from Japan.[^o12] He was at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) from 1954 to 1956 while on a [Fulbright fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulbright_fellowship);[^o10] while there he worked closely with [William Feller](wikipedia://en/206644) and [Henry McKean](wikipedia://en/35051081) who were at nearby [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University).[^o8] He was a professor at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University) from 1961 to 1964 and a professor at [Aarhus University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aarhus_University) from 1966 to 1969.[^o10]

Then in 1969 Itô arrived at [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University), where he was a professor of mathematics for six years until 1975.[^o9] This was his longest stint outside Japan.[^o10] Among the courses he taught at Cornell was one in Higher Calculus.[^o11]

Itô wrote not only in [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_language) but also in [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_language), [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language), [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) and [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language).[^o12] However, his ability to converse in foreign languages was a different matter, and by his own admission his accent made him largely incomprehensible to Americans.[^o12]

When Itô left Cornell and returned to the University of Kyoto, he served as director of their [Research Institute for Mathematical Sciences](wikipedia://en/1978962).[^o16] After his retirement, he became professor emeritus at Kyoto University.[^o16] He also had a post-retirement position as a professor at the private [Gakushuin University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gakushuin_University) for several years,[^o10] a common practice among higher-ranking Japanese academics.[^o7]

*Image: Kiyoshi Ito 19911212 large.jpg.* Itô c. 1991

Itô was recipient of the [Wolf Prize](wikipedia://en/714278) and the [Kyoto Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto_Prize).[^o12] He was a member of the [Japan Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Academy),[^o10] and also a foreign member of the [Académie des sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Académie_des_sciences) in France and the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences) in the United States. [^o12]

In his later years, Itô struggled with poor health.[^o7] Itô was awarded the inaugural [Gauss Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss_Prize) in 2006 by the [International Mathematical Union](wikipedia://en/14868) for his lifetime achievements.[^o16] As he due to his health was unable to travel to [Madrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrid), his youngest daughter, [Junko Ito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junko_Ito), received the Gauss Prize from [King Juan Carlos I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Juan_Carlos_I) on his behalf.[^o13] Later, IMU President [Sir John Macleod Ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_John_Macleod_Ball) personally presented the medal to Itô at a special ceremony held in Kyoto.[^o14] In October 2008, Itô was honored with Japan's [Order of Culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Culture), and an awards ceremony for the Order of Culture was held at the Imperial Palace.[^o15]

Itô died on November 10, 2008, in [Kyoto, Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto,_Japan), at age 93, of [respiratory failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Respiratory_failure).[^o16]
