# Catmull–Clark subdivision surface

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Catmull–Clark subdivision surface"}

*Image: Catmull-Clark subdivision of a cube.svg.* Catmull–Clark level-3 subdivision of a cube with the limit [subdivision surface](wikipedia://en/1079432) shown below. (Note that although it looks like the [bi-cubic interpolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi-cubic_interpolation) approaches a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859), an actual sphere is [quadric](wikipedia://en/145570).)

*Image: Catmull-Clark\_limit\_surface\_of\_Cube\_(compare\_sphere).png.* Visual difference between sphere (green) and Catmull-Clark subdivision surface (magenta) from a cube

The **Catmull–Clark** [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) is a technique used in [3D computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_computer_graphics) to create curved surfaces by using [subdivision surface](wikipedia://en/1079432) [modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_modeling). It was devised by [Edwin Catmull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Catmull) and [Jim Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_H._Clark) in 1978 as a generalization of [bi-cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi-cubic) *[uniform](wikipedia://en/457680#Definition)* [B-spline](wikipedia://en/21834) surfaces to arbitrary [topology](wikipedia://en/29954).[^o3]

:::

In 2005/06, Edwin Catmull, together with [Tony DeRose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_DeRose) and [Jos Stam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos_Stam), received an [Academy Award for Technical Achievement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Technical_Achievement) for their invention and application of subdivision surfaces. DeRose wrote about "efficient, fair interpolation" and character animation. Stam described a technique for a direct evaluation of the limit surface without recursion.

## Recursive evaluation {#Recursive_evaluation}

Catmull–Clark surfaces are defined [recursively](wikipedia://en/25407), using the following *refinement scheme.*[^o3]

Start with a [mesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygon_mesh) of an arbitrary [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470). All the [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) in this mesh shall be called *original points*.

- For each face, add a *face point*
  - Set each face point to be the [average](wikipedia://en/60167) of all *original points* for the respective face *Image: Catmull-Clark Recursive Step 1.png.* Face points (blue spheres)
- For each edge, add an *edge point*.
  - Set each edge point to be the average of the two neighbouring face points *(A,F)* and the two endpoints of the edge *(M,E)* $\frac{A+F+M+E}{4}$[^o4] *Image: Catmull-Clark Recursive Step 2.png.* Edge points (magenta cubes)
- For each *original point* (*P)*, take the average (*F)* of all *n* (recently created) face points for faces touching *P*, and take the average *(R)* of all *n* edge midpoints for original edges touching *P*, where each edge midpoint is the average of its two endpoint vertices (not to be confused with new *edge points* above). (Note that from the perspective of a vertex *P*, the number of edges neighboring *P* is also the number of adjacent faces, hence *n*)
  - Move each *original point* to the new *vertex point* $\frac{F + 2R + (n-3)P}{n}$ (This is the [barycenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycenter) of *P*, *R* and *F* with respective weights (*n* − 3), 2 and 1) *Image: Catmull-Clark Recursive Step 3.png.* New vertex points (green cones)
- Form edges and faces in the new mesh
  - Connect each new *face point* to the new *edge points* of all original edges defining the original face *Image: Catmull-Clark Recursive Step 4.png.* New edges, 4 per face point
  - Connect each new *vertex point* to the new *edge points* of all original edges incident on the original vertex *Image: Catmull-Clark Recursive Step 5.png.* 3 new edges per vertex point of shifted original vertices
  - Define new faces as enclosed by edges *Image: Catmull-Clark recursive step 6.png.* Final faces to the mesh

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

The new mesh will consist only of [quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/25278), which in general will *not* be [planar](wikipedia://en/19469852). The new mesh will generally look "smoother" (i.e. less "jagged" or "pointy") than the old mesh. Repeated subdivision results in meshes that are more and more rounded.

The arbitrary-looking [barycenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycenter) formula was chosen by Catmull and Clark based on the [aesthetic appearance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics) of the resulting surfaces rather than on a [mathematical derivation](wikipedia://en/82285), although they do go to great lengths to rigorously show that the method [converges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergence_%28math%29) to bicubic B-spline surfaces.[^o3]

It can be shown that the limit surface obtained by this refinement process is [$G^1$ (tangent plane) continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_continuity) at extraordinary vertices (valence not equal to 4) and $\mathcal{C}^2$ continuous at regular points (valence equal to 4).[^o7] (when *n* indicates [how many derivatives are continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function#Differentiability_classes), we speak of $\mathcal{C}^n$ [continuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuously_differentiable)). After one iteration, the number of extraordinary points on the surface remains constant.

:::

## Exact evaluation {#Exact_evaluation}

The limit surface of Catmull–Clark subdivision surfaces can also be evaluated directly, without any recursive refinement. This can be accomplished by means of the technique of [Jos Stam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos_Stam) (1998).[^o9] This method reformulates the recursive refinement process into a [matrix exponential](wikipedia://en/672731) problem, which can be solved directly by means of [matrix diagonalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_diagonalization).

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Extensions"}

## Extensions {#Extensions}

### Semi-sharp creases {#Semi-sharp_creases}

Catmull–Clark surfaces were extended to support semi-sharp creases by using different subdivision rules for a fixed number of iterations determined by a sharpness value.[^o13] This approach, introduced by [Tony DeRose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_DeRose) et al. in 1998, allows for a controllable transition from sharp to smooth subdivision rules.

:::
