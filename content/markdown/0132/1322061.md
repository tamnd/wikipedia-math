# Integral curve

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Integral curve"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **integral curve** is a [parametric curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_curve) that represents a specific solution to an [ordinary differential equation](wikipedia://en/32742753) or system of equations.

:::

## Name {#Name}

Integral curves are known by various other names, depending on the nature and interpretation of the differential equation or vector field. In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), integral curves for an [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) or [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563) are known as *[field lines](wikipedia://en/5000404)*, and integral curves for the [velocity field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity_field) of a [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid) are known as [*streamlines*](wikipedia://en/166086). In [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/990632), the integral curves for a differential equation that governs a [system](wikipedia://en/9087) are referred to as [*trajectories*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectory) or [*orbits*](wikipedia://en/252250).

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Suppose that $\mathbf{F}$ is a static [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641), that is, a [vector-valued function](wikipedia://en/3675281) with components $(F_{1},F_{2},...,F_{n})$ in a [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706), and that $\mathbf{x}(t)$ is a [parametric curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_curve) with Cartesian coordinates $(x_{1}(t),x_{2}(t),...,x_{n}(t))$. Then $\mathbf{x}(t)$ is an **integral curve** of $\mathbf{F}$ if it is a solution of the [autonomous system](wikipedia://en/312398) of ordinary differential equations,

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{dx_1}{dt} &= F_1(x_1,\ldots,x_n) \\ &\;\, \vdots \\ \frac{dx_n}{dt} &= F_n(x_1,\ldots,x_n). \end{aligned}$$

Such a system may be written as a single vector equation,

$$\mathbf{x}'(t) = \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}(t)).$$

This equation says that the vector tangent to the curve at any point $\mathbf{x}(t)$ along the curve is precisely the vector $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}(t))$, and so the curve $\mathbf{x}(t)$ is tangent at each point to the vector field **F**.

If a given vector field is [Lipschitz continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipschitz_continuous), then the [Picard–Lindelöf theorem](wikipedia://en/666177) implies that there exists a unique flow for small time.

:::

## Generalization to differentiable manifolds {#Generalization_to_differentiable_manifolds}

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

Let $M$ be a [Banach manifold](wikipedia://en/2504397) of class $C^{r}$ with $r \ge 2$. As usual, $TM$ denotes the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) of $M$ with its natural [projection](wikipedia://en/3909097) $\pi_{M} : TM \to M$ given by

$$\pi_M : (x, v) \mapsto x.$$

A vector field on $M$ is a [cross-section](wikipedia://en/276526#Sections) of the tangent bundle $TM$, i.e. an assignment to every point of the manifold $M$ of a tangent vector to $M$ at that point. Let $X$ be a vector field on $M$ of class $C^{r-1}$ and let $p \in M$. An **integral curve** for $X$ passing through $p$ at time $t_{0}$ is a curve $\alpha : J \to M$ of class $C^{r-1}$, defined on an [open interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $J$ of the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) $\mathbf{R}$ containing $t_{0}$, such that

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha(t_0) &= p; \\ \alpha' (t) &= X (\alpha (t)) \text{ for all } t \in J. \end{aligned}$$

:::

### Relationship to ordinary differential equations {#Relationship_to_ordinary_differential_equations}

The above definition of an integral curve $\alpha$ for a vector field $X$, passing through $p$ at time $t_{0}$, is the same as saying that $\alpha$ is a local solution to the ordinary differential equation/initial value problem

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha(t_0) &= p; \\ \alpha' (t) &= X (\alpha (t)). \end{aligned}$$

It is local in the sense that it is defined only for times in $J$, and not necessarily for all $t \ge t_{0}$ (let alone $t \le t_{0}$). Thus, the problem of proving the existence and uniqueness of integral curves is the same as that of finding solutions to ordinary differential equations/initial value problems and showing that they are unique.

### Remarks on the time derivative {#Remarks_on_the_time_derivative}

In the above, $\alpha'(t)$ denotes the derivative of $\alpha$ at time $t$, the "direction $\alpha$ is pointing" at time $t$. From a more abstract viewpoint, this is the [Fréchet derivative](wikipedia://en/2735727):

$$(\mathrm{d}_t\alpha) (+1) \in \mathrm{T}_{\alpha (t)} M.$$

In the special case that $M$ is some [open subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$, this is the familiar derivative

$$\left( \frac{\mathrm{d} \alpha_1}{\mathrm{d} t}, \dots, \frac{\mathrm{d} \alpha_n}{\mathrm{d} t} \right),$$

where $\alpha_{1}, ..., \alpha_{n}$ are the coordinates for $\alpha$ with respect to the usual coordinate directions.

The same thing may be phrased even more abstractly in terms of [induced maps](wikipedia://en/17018463). Note that the tangent bundle $TJ$ of $J$ is the [trivial bundle](wikipedia://en/276526#Trivial_bundle) $J \times \mathbf{R}$ and there is a [canonical](wikipedia://en/515096) cross-section $\iota$ of this bundle such that $\iota(t) = 1$ (or, more precisely, $(t, 1) \in \iota$) for all $t \in J$. The curve $\alpha$ induces a [bundle map](wikipedia://en/7220589) $\alpha_{\ast} : TJ \to TM$ so that the following diagram commutes:

*Image: CommDiag TJtoTM.png.*

Then the time derivative $\alpha'$ is the [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) $\alpha' = \alpha_{\ast} o \iota,\text{ and } \alpha'(t)$ is its value at some point $t \in J$.
