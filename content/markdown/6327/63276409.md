# Gram–Euler theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gram–Euler theorem"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **Gram–Euler theorem**,[^o6] **Gram-Sommerville, Brianchon-Gram** or **Gram relation**[^o2] (named after [Jørgen Pedersen Gram](wikipedia://en/851232), [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902), [Duncan Sommerville](wikipedia://en/8276753) and [Charles Julien Brianchon](wikipedia://en/1402609)) is a generalization of the [internal angle sum formula](wikipedia://en/524003#Properties) of [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621) to higher-dimensional [polytopes](wikipedia://en/23471). The equation constrains the sums of the interior angles of a polytope in a manner analogous to the [Euler relation](wikipedia://en/148420) on the number of d-dimensional [faces](wikipedia://en/149926).

:::

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Let $P$ be an $n$-dimensional [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471). For each *k*-[face](wikipedia://en/149926) $F$, with $k = \dim(F)$ its dimension (0 for vertices, 1 for edges, 2 for faces, etc., up to *n* for *P* itself), its [interior](wikipedia://en/524003) (higher-dimensional) [solid angle](wikipedia://en/94102) $\angle(F)$ is defined by choosing a small enough $(n - 1)$-[sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) centered at some point in the interior of $F$ and finding the surface area contained inside $P$. Then the Gram–Euler theorem states:[^o5][^o6]

$$\sum_{F \subset P} (-1)^{\dim F} \angle(F) = 0$$

In [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610) of constant curvature (i.e. [spherical](wikipedia://en/174026), $\epsilon = 1$, and [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/241291), $\epsilon = -1$, geometry) the relation gains a volume term, but only if the dimension *n* is even:

$$\sum_{F \subset P} (-1)^{\dim F} \angle(F) = \epsilon^{n/2}(1 + (-1)^n)\operatorname{Vol}(P)$$

Here, $\operatorname{Vol}(P)$ is the normalized (hyper)volume of the polytope (i.e, the fraction of the *n*-dimensional spherical or hyperbolic space); the angles $\angle(F)$ also have to be expressed as fractions (of the (*n*-1)-sphere).[^o2]

When the polytope is [simplicial](wikipedia://en/8853373) additional angle restrictions known as *Perles relations* hold, analogous to the [Dehn-Sommerville equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dehn-Sommerville_equations) for the number of faces.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

For a two-dimensional polygon, the statement expands into:

$$\sum_{v} \alpha_v - \sum_e \pi + 2\pi = 0$$

where the first term $A=\textstyle\sum \alpha_v$ is the sum of the internal vertex angles, the second sum is over the edges, each of which has internal angle $\pi$, and the final term corresponds to the entire polygon, which has a full internal angle $2\pi$. For a polygon with $n$ faces, the theorem tells us that $A - \pi n + 2\pi = 0$, or equivalently, $A = \pi (n - 2)$. For a polygon on a sphere, the relation gives the spherical surface area or [solid angle](wikipedia://en/94102) as the [spherical excess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_excess): $\Omega = A - \pi (n - 2)$.

For a three-dimensional [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) the theorem reads:

$$\sum_{v} \Omega_v - 2\sum_e \theta_e + \sum_f 2\pi - 4\pi = 0$$

where $\Omega_v$ is the solid angle at a vertex, $\theta_e$ the [dihedral angle](wikipedia://en/338046) at an edge (the solid angle of the corresponding [lune](wikipedia://en/23024648) is twice as big), the third sum counts the faces (each with an interior hemisphere angle of $2\pi$) and the last term is the interior solid angle (full sphere or $4\pi$).

:::

## History {#History}

The n-dimensional relation was first proven by [Sommerville](wikipedia://en/8276753), Heckman and [Grünbaum](wikipedia://en/1731976) for the spherical, hyperbolic and Euclidean case, respectively.[^o2]
