# Euler's critical load

*Image: FIG2.png.* *Fig. 1: Critical stress vs slenderness ratio for steel, for E = 200 GPa, yield strength = 240 MPa*.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Euler's critical load"}

**Euler's critical load** or **Euler's buckling load** is the compressive [load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_load) at which a slender [column](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column) will suddenly bend or [buckle](wikipedia://en/815969). It is given by the formula:[^o2]

:::

$$P_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 EI}{(KL)^2}$$

where

- $P_{cr}$, Euler's critical load (longitudinal compression load on column),
- $E$, [Young's modulus](wikipedia://en/228108) of the column material,
- $I$, minimum [second moment of area](wikipedia://en/1736264) of the cross section of the column (area moment of inertia),
- $L$, unsupported [length](wikipedia://en/18542) of column,
- $K$, column effective length factor

This formula was derived in [1744](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1744) by the [Swiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switzerland) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902).[^o3] The column will remain straight for loads less than the critical load. The *critical load* is the greatest load that will not cause lateral deflection (buckling). For loads greater than the critical load, the column will deflect laterally. The critical load puts the column in a state of [unstable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instability) equilibrium. A load beyond the critical load causes the column to [fail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_integrity_and_failure) by [buckling](wikipedia://en/815969). As the load is increased beyond the critical load the lateral deflections increase, until it may fail in other modes such as yielding of the material. Loading of columns beyond the critical load are not addressed in this article.

[Johnson's parabolic formula](wikipedia://en/52325106), an alternative used for low slenderness ratios was constructed by John Butler Johnson (1850–1902) in 1893.[^o4]

## Assumptions of the model {#Assumptions_of_the_model}

*Image: ColumnEffectiveLength.png.* *Fig. 2: Column effective length factors for Euler's critical load. In practical design, it is recommended to increase the factors as shown above.*

The following assumptions are made while deriving Euler's formula:[^o6]

1. The [material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Material) of the column is homogeneous and [isotropic](wikipedia://en/14865).
1. The compressive load on the column is axial only.
1. The column is free from initial [stress](wikipedia://en/228107).
1. The [weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weight) of the column is neglected.
1. The column is initially straight (no eccentricity of the axial load).
1. Pin joints are [friction](wikipedia://en/11062)-less (no moment constraint) and fixed ends are rigid (no rotation deflection).
1. The [cross-section](wikipedia://en/1337370) of the column is uniform throughout its length.
1. The direct stress is very small as compared to the [bending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bending) stress (the material is compressed only within the elastic range of strains).
1. The length of the column is very large as compared to the cross-sectional dimensions of the column.
1. The column fails only by buckling. This is true if the compressive stress in the column does not exceed the [yield strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yield_strength) $\sigma_y$ (see figure 1): $$\sigma = \frac{P_{cr}}{A} = \frac{\pi^2E}{(L_e/r)^2} < \sigma_y$$ where:
   - ${L_e}/{r}$ is the slenderness ratio,
   - $L_e = KL$ is the effective length,
   - $r = \sqrt{I / A}$ is the [radius of gyration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radius_of_gyration),
   - $I$ is the second moment of area (area moment of inertia),
   - $A$ is the area cross section.

For slender columns, the critical buckling stress is usually lower than the yield stress. In contrast, a stocky column can have a critical buckling stress higher than the yield, i.e. it yields prior to buckling.

## Mathematical derivation {#Mathematical_derivation}

### Pin ended column {#Pin_ended_column}

The following model applies to columns simply supported at each end ($K = 1$).

Firstly, we will put attention to the fact there are no reactions in the hinged ends, so we also have no shear force in any cross-section of the column. The reason for no reactions can be obtained from [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) (so the reactions should be in the same direction) and from moment equilibrium (so the reactions should be in opposite directions).

Using the [free body diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_body_diagram) in the right side of figure 3, and making a summation of moments about point $x$:

$$\Sigma M = 0 \Rightarrow M(x) + Pw = 0$$

where $w$ is the lateral deflection.

According to [Euler–Bernoulli beam theory](wikipedia://en/2064935), the [deflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflection_%28engineering%29) of a beam is related with its [bending moment](wikipedia://en/6018334) by:

$$M = -EI\frac{d^2 w}{dx^2}.$$

*Image: Pin ended column under the effect of Buckling load.png.* *Fig. 3: Pin ended column under the effect of Buckling load*

so:

$$EI\frac{d^2w}{dx^2} + Pw = 0$$

Let $\lambda^2 = \frac{P}{EI}$, so:

$$\frac{d^2w}{dx^2} + \lambda^2 w = 0$$

We get a classical homogeneous second-order [ordinary differential equation](wikipedia://en/32742753).

The general solutions of this equation is: $w(x) = A \cos(\lambda x) + B \sin(\lambda x)$, where $A$ and $B$ are constants to be determined by [boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_conditions), which are:

- Left end pinned: $w(0) = 0 \rightarrow A = 0$
- Right end pinned: $w(\ell) = 0 \rightarrow B \sin(\lambda \ell) = 0$

*Image: FIG4.png.* *Fig. 4: First three modes of buckling loads*

If $B = 0$, no bending moment exists and we get the [trivial solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivial_solution) of $w(x) = 0$.

However, from the other solution $\sin(\lambda \ell) = 0$ we get $\lambda_n \ell = n\pi$, for $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$

Together with $\lambda^2 = \frac{P}{EI}$ as defined before, the various critical loads are:

$$P_{n} = \frac{n^2 \pi^2 EI}{\ell^2} \; , \quad \text{ for } n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$$

and depending upon the value of $n$, different buckling [modes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitudinal_mode) are produced[^o9] as shown in figure 4. The load and mode for n=0 is the nonbuckled mode.

Theoretically, any buckling mode is possible, but in the case of a slowly applied load only the first modal shape is likely to be produced.

**The critical load of Euler** for a pin ended column is therefore:

$$P_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 EI}{\ell^2}$$

and the obtained shape of the buckled column in the first mode is:

$$w(x) = B \sin \left({\pi \over \ell} x\right) .$$

### General approach {#General_approach}

*Image: FIG 5.png.* *Fig. 5: forces and moments acting on a column.*

The differential equation of the axis of a beam[^o11] is:

$$\frac{d^4 w}{dx^4} + \frac{P}{EI}\frac{d^2 w}{dx^2} = \frac{q}{EI}$$

For a column with axial load only, the lateral load $q(x)$ vanishes and substituting $\lambda^2 = \frac{P}{EI}$, we get:

$$\frac{d^4 w}{dx^4} + \lambda^2\frac{d^2 w}{dx^2} = 0$$

This is a homogeneous fourth-order differential equation and its general solution is

$$w(x) = A\sin(\lambda x) + B\cos(\lambda x) + Cx + D$$

The four constants $A, B, C, D$ are determined by the boundary conditions (end constraints) on $w(x)$, at each end. There are three cases:

1. Pinned end:

$w = 0$ and $M = 0 \rightarrow {d^2w \over dx^2} = 0$

1. Fixed end:

$w = 0$ and ${dw \over dx} = 0$

1. Free end:

$M = 0 \rightarrow {d^2w \over dx^2} = 0$ and $V = 0 \rightarrow {d^3w \over dx^3} + \lambda^2{dw \over dx} = 0$

For each combination of these boundary conditions, an [eigenvalue problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigen_value_problem) is obtained. Solving those, we get the values of Euler's critical load for each one of the cases presented in Figure 2.
