# Poincaré–Hopf theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Poincaré–Hopf theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Poincaré–Hopf theorem** (also known as the **Poincaré–Hopf index formula**, **Poincaré–Hopf index theorem**, or **Hopf index theorem**) is an important theorem in [differential topology](wikipedia://en/8562) that relates zeros of continuous [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) on [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [differential manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_manifold) to [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) of that manifold. It is named after [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) who proved it for 2-dimensional case and [Heinz Hopf](wikipedia://en/543082) who proved it in general.

:::

Some authors include also additional assumptions like [orientability](wikipedia://en/187446) of manifold or [smoothness](wikipedia://en/1408000) of vector field, but these assumptions are not required in general, and are usually taken to simplify proof or demonstration of theorem in specific context.

The Poincaré–Hopf theorem is often illustrated by the special case of the [hairy ball theorem](wikipedia://en/485168), which simply states that there is no continuous [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) on an even-dimensional [n-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) having no zeros.

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Formal statement"}

## Formal statement {#Formal_statement}

Let $M$ be a compact differentiable manifold, of dimension $n$, and $v$ a continuous vector field on $M$. If $M$ has a [boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold_with_boundary), then we insist that $v$ be pointing in the outward normal direction along the boundary and have no zeros on the boundary.

Suppose that $x$ is an isolated zero of $v$, and fix some [local coordinates](wikipedia://en/304139) near $x$. Pick a closed ball $D$ centered at $x$, so that $x$ is the only zero of $v$ in $D$. Then the [index](wikipedia://en/62641#Index_of_a_vector_field) of $v$ at $x$, $\operatorname{index}_x(v)$, can be defined as the [degree](wikipedia://en/3094450#Differential_topology) of the map $u : \partial D \to \mathbb S^{n-1}$ from the [boundary](wikipedia://en/2073470#Boundary_and_interior) of $D$ to the $(n-1)$-sphere given by $u(z)=v(z)/\|v(z)\|$.

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.** Let $M$ be a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) and $v$ be a continuous [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) on $M$ that satisfies assumptions. Then we have the formula:

$$\sum_i \operatorname{index}_{x_i}(v) = \chi(M)\,$$

where the sum of the indices is over all the isolated zeroes of $v$ and $\chi(M)$ is the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) of $M$.

The theorem was proven for two dimensions by [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740)[^o5] and later generalized to higher dimensions by [Heinz Hopf](wikipedia://en/543082).[^o6]

:::

:::

## Significance {#Significance}

The Euler characteristic of a closed surface is a purely [topological](wikipedia://en/29954) concept, whereas the index of a vector field is purely [analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_%28mathematics%29). Thus, this theorem establishes a deep link between two seemingly unrelated areas of mathematics. It is perhaps as interesting that the proof of this theorem relies heavily on [integration](wikipedia://en/15532), and, in particular, [Stokes' theorem](wikipedia://en/25441497), which states that the integral of the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) of a [differential form](wikipedia://en/221519) is equal to the integral of that form over the boundary. In the special case of a [manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold_%28mathematics%29) without boundary, this amounts to saying that the integral is 0. But by examining vector fields in a sufficiently small neighborhood of a source or sink, we see that sources and sinks contribute [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) amounts (known as the index) to the total, and they must all sum to 0. This result may be considered one of the earliest of a whole series of theorems (e.g. [Atiyah–Singer index theorem](wikipedia://en/324752), [De Rham's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Rham's_theorem), [Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem](wikipedia://en/1532606)) establishing deep relationships between [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) and [analytical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_%28mathematics%29) or [physical](wikipedia://en/22939) concepts. They play an important role in the modern study of both fields.

::: {#o9 .proof cue=heading name="Sketch of proof"}

## Sketch of proof {#Sketch_of_proof}

1. Embed *M* in some high-dimensional Euclidean space. (Use the [Whitney embedding theorem](wikipedia://en/477578).)
1. Take a small neighborhood of *M* in that Euclidean space, *N*<sub>ε</sub>. Extend the vector field to this neighborhood so that it still has the same zeroes and the zeroes have the same indices. In addition, make sure that the extended vector field at the boundary of *N*<sub>ε</sub> is directed outwards.
1. The sum of indices of the zeroes of the old (and new) vector field is equal to the degree of the [Gauss map](wikipedia://en/378881) from the boundary of *N*<sub>ε</sub> to the (*n*–1)-dimensional sphere. Thus, the sum of the indices is independent of the actual vector field, and depends only on the manifold *M*. Technique: cut away all zeroes of the vector field with small neighborhoods. Then use the fact that the degree of a map from the boundary of an n-dimensional manifold to an (*n*–1)-dimensional sphere, that can be extended to the whole n-dimensional manifold, is zero.
1. Finally, identify this sum of indices as the Euler characteristic of *M*. To do that, construct a very specific vector field on *M* using a [triangulation](wikipedia://en/1440976) of *M* for which it is clear that the sum of indices is equal to the Euler characteristic.

:::

## Consequences {#Consequences}

- A particularly useful corollary from the theorem is that when there is a non-vanishing continuous vector field on and compact differential manifold, Euler characteristic of this manifold is 0.
- A [hairy ball theorem](wikipedia://en/485168) theorem is immediate consequence of the Poincare-Hopf theorem. Since even-dimensional sphere have Euler characteristic 2, there cannot exist a non-vanishing vector field on such sphere.
- Poincare-Hopf theorem shows that for a continuous vector field defined on plane a closed trajectory cannot encircle arbitrary zeros of vector field. A closed trajectory excise in this plane a manifold with boundary. Vector field restricted to that manifold goes along the boundary, so satisfies assumptions of the theorem. Such manifold is homeomorphic with closed ball, so have Euler characteristic 1, then indices of all zeros inside a closed trajectory must sum up to 1.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

It is still possible to define the index for a vector field with nonisolated zeroes. A construction of this index and the extension of Poincaré–Hopf theorem for vector fields with nonisolated zeroes is outlined in Section 1.1.2 of (Brasselet Seade Suwa 2009).

Another generalization that uses only compact triangulable spaces and continuous mappings with finitely many fixed points is the [Lefschetz-Hopf theorem](wikipedia://en/582530). Since every vector field induces a flow on manifolds and fixed points of small flows correspond to zeroes of vector fields (and indices of zeroes equal indices of fixed points), the Poincare-Hopf theorem follows immediately from this.
