# Hyperbolic coordinates

*Image: Hyperbolic coordinates.svg.* Hyperbolic coordinates plotted on the Euclidean plane: all points on the same blue ray share the same coordinate value *u*, and all points on the same red hyperbola share the same coordinate value *v*.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hyperbolic coordinates"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **hyperbolic coordinates** are a method of locating points in quadrant I of the [Cartesian plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_plane)

:::

$$\{(x, y) \ :\ x > 0,\ y > 0\ \} = Q.$$

Hyperbolic coordinates take values in the [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane) defined as:

$$HP = \{(u, v) : u \in \mathbb{R}, v > 0 \}.$$

These coordinates in *HP* are useful for studying [logarithmic](wikipedia://en/164055) comparisons of [direct proportion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_proportion) in *Q* and measuring deviations from direct proportion.

For $(x,y)$ in $Q$ take

$$u = \ln \sqrt{\frac{x}{y}}$$

and

$$v = \sqrt{xy}.$$

The parameter *u* is the [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) to (*x, y*) and *v* is the [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046) of *x* and *y*.

The inverse mapping is

$$x = v e^u ,\quad y = v e^{-u}.$$

The function $Q \rightarrow HP$ is a [continuous mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_mapping), but not an [analytic function](wikipedia://en/61478).

## Alternative quadrant metric {#Alternative_quadrant_metric}

Since *HP* carries the [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) structure of the [Poincaré half-plane model](wikipedia://en/461942) of [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), the bijective correspondence $Q \leftrightarrow HP$ brings this structure to *Q*. It can be grasped using the notion of [hyperbolic motions](wikipedia://en/1229416). Since [geodesics](wikipedia://en/91096) in *HP* are semicircles with centers on the boundary, the geodesics in *Q* are obtained from the correspondence and turn out to be [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_%28mathematics%29#Ray) from the origin or [petal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petal)-shaped [curves](wikipedia://en/89246) leaving and re-entering the origin. And the hyperbolic motion of *HP* given by a left-right shift corresponds to a [squeeze mapping](wikipedia://en/1140043) applied to *Q*.

Since [hyperbolas](wikipedia://en/14052) in *Q* correspond to lines parallel to the boundary of *HP*, they are [horocycles](wikipedia://en/11084460) in the metric geometry of *Q*.

If one only considers the [Euclidean topology](wikipedia://en/1482085) of the plane and the topology inherited by *Q*, then the lines bounding *Q* seem close to *Q*. Insight from the metric space *HP* shows that the [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) *Q* has only the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) as boundary when viewed through the correspondence. Indeed, consider rays from the origin in *Q*, and their images, vertical rays from the boundary *R* of *HP*. Any point in *HP* is an infinite distance from the point *p* at the foot of the perpendicular to *R*, but a sequence of points on this perpendicular may tend in the direction of *p*. The corresponding sequence in *Q* tends along a ray toward the origin. The old Euclidean boundary of *Q* is no longer relevant.

## Applications in physical science {#Applications_in_physical_science}

Fundamental physical variables are sometimes related by equations of the form *k* = *x y*. For instance, *V* = *I R* ([Ohm's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohm's_law)), *P* = *V I* ([electrical power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_power)), *P V* = *k T* ([ideal gas law](wikipedia://en/59881)), and *f* λ = *v* (relation of [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125), [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779), and velocity in the wave medium). When the *k* is constant, the other variables lie on a hyperbola, which is a [horocycle](wikipedia://en/11084460) in the appropriate *Q* quadrant.

For example, in [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952) the [isothermal process](wikipedia://en/409951) explicitly follows the hyperbolic path and [work](wikipedia://en/3616613) can be interpreted as a hyperbolic angle change. Similarly, a given mass *M* of gas with changing volume will have variable density δ = *M / V*, and the ideal gas law may be written *P = k T* δ so that an [isobaric process](wikipedia://en/414765) traces a hyperbola in the quadrant of absolute temperature and gas density.

For hyperbolic coordinates in the [theory of relativity](wikipedia://en/30001) see the History section.

## Statistical applications {#Statistical_applications}

- Comparative study of [population density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_density) in the quadrant begins with selecting a reference nation, region, or [urban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_density) area whose population and area are taken as the point (1,1).
- Analysis of the [elected representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislator) of regions in a [representative democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_democracy) begins with selection of a standard for comparison: a particular represented group, whose magnitude and slate magnitude (of representatives) stands at (1,1) in the quadrant.

## Economic applications {#Economic_applications}

There are many natural applications of hyperbolic coordinates in [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics):

- Analysis of currency [exchange rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_rate) fluctuation:The unit currency sets $x = 1$. The price currency corresponds to $y$. For $$0 < y < 1$$ we find $u > 0$, a positive hyperbolic angle. For a *fluctuation* take a new price $$0 < z < y.$$ Then the change in *u* is: $$\Delta u = \ln \sqrt{\frac{y}{z}}.$$ Quantifying exchange rate fluctuation through hyperbolic angle provides an objective, symmetric, and consistent [measure](wikipedia://en/19873). The quantity $\Delta u$ is the length of the left-right shift in the hyperbolic motion view of the currency fluctuation.
- Analysis of inflation or deflation of prices of a [basket of consumer goods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basket_of_consumer_goods).
- Quantification of change in marketshare in [duopoly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duopoly).
- Corporate [stock splits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_split) versus stock buy-back.

## Trigonometry {#Trigonometry}

*Image: Cartesian\_hyperbolic\_rhombus.svg.* Right triangles with legs proportional to sinh and cosh

The [hyperbolic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function) sinh, cosh, and tanh can be illustrated with hyperbolic coordinates. Let

$$A = (e^{-u}, e^u), \ B=(e^u, e^{-u}), \ C = (e^u + e^{-u}, \ e^u + e^{-u}) .$$

Then BCAO forms a [rhombus](wikipedia://en/183884) with diagonals intersecting at $M = (\frac{e^u + e^{-u} }{2},\ \frac{e^u + e^{-u} }{2} )$. The hyperbolic cosine is defined as $\cosh u = \frac{e^u + e^{-u} }{2},$ so *M* = ( cosh *u*, cosh *u*).

The semi-diagonal MA is [equipollent](wikipedia://en/24022514) to $(\frac{-e^{-u} + e^u }{2}, \ \frac{e^u - e^{-u} }{2}) = (- \sinh u,\ \sinh u)$. Evidently the diagonals divide the rhombus into four congruent right triangles.

The angle MOA is the [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) parameter *u* of cosh and sinh, and $\tanh u = \frac{\sinh u}{\cosh u}$ has a value in the [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) (–1, 1).

## History {#History}

The [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046) is an ancient concept, but [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) was developed in this configuration by [Gregoire de Saint-Vincent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregoire_de_Saint-Vincent). He was attempting to perform [quadrature](wikipedia://en/1618671) with respect to the rectangular hyperbola *y* = 1/*x*. That challenge was a standing [open problem](wikipedia://en/1333335) since [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) performed the [quadrature of the parabola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrature_of_the_parabola). The curve passes through (1,1) where it is opposite the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) in a [unit square](wikipedia://en/285301). The other points on the curve can be viewed as [rectangles](wikipedia://en/70048) having the same [area](wikipedia://en/1209) as this square. Such a rectangle may be obtained by applying a [squeeze mapping](wikipedia://en/1140043) to the square. Another way to view these mappings is via [hyperbolic sectors](wikipedia://en/1139926). Starting from (1,1) the hyperbolic sector of unit area ends at (e, 1/e), where [e](wikipedia://en/9633) is 2.71828…, according to the development of [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) in *[Introduction to the Analysis of the Infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introduction_to_the_Analysis_of_the_Infinite)* (1748).

Taking (e, 1/e) as the vertex of rectangle of unit area, and applying again the squeeze that made it from the unit square, yields $(e^2, \ e^{-2}).$ Generally n squeezes yields $(e^n, \ e^{-n}).$ [A. A. de Sarasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._A._de_Sarasa) noted a similar observation of G. de Saint Vincent, that as the abscissas increased in a [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630), the sum of the areas against the hyperbola increased in [arithmetic series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_series), and this property corresponded to the **logarithm** already in use to reduce multiplications to additions. Euler’s work made the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476) a standard mathematical tool, and elevated mathematics to the realm of [transcendental functions](wikipedia://en/297021). The hyperbolic coordinates are formed on the original picture of G. de Saint-Vincent, which provided the quadrature of the hyperbola, and transcended the limits of [algebraic functions](wikipedia://en/974169).

In 1875 [Johann von Thünen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_von_Thünen) published a theory of natural wages[^o8] which used geometric mean of a subsistence wage and market value of the labor using the employer's capital.

In [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) the focus is on the 3-dimensional [hypersurface](wikipedia://en/525101) in the future of spacetime where various velocities arrive after a given [proper time](wikipedia://en/494418). Scott Walter[^o9] explains that in November 1907 [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537) alluded to a well-known three-dimensional hyperbolic geometry while speaking to the Göttingen Mathematical Society, but not to a four-dimensional one.[^o10] In tribute to [Wolfgang Rindler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Rindler), the author of a standard introductory university-level textbook on relativity, hyperbolic coordinates of spacetime are called [Rindler coordinates](wikipedia://en/1327701).
