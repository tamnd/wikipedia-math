# Hard spheres

*Image: Tormaysala.svg.* Orientations of two hard spheres in collision.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hard spheres"}

In [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481), **hard spheres** are widely used as model particles in fluids and solids. They are defined simply as impenetrable spheres that cannot overlap in space. They mimic the extremely strong ("infinitely elastic bouncing") repulsion that atoms and spherical molecules experience at very close distances. Hard spheres systems are studied by analytical means, by [molecular dynamics](wikipedia://en/198608) simulations, and by the experimental study of certain [colloidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colloid) model systems.

:::

Besides being a model of theoretical significance, the hard-sphere system is used as a basis in the formulation of several modern, predictive [equations of state](wikipedia://en/9908) for real fluids through the [statistical associating fluid theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_associating_fluid_theory) (SAFT) approach, and models for transport properties in gases through [Chapman–Enskog theory](wikipedia://en/24388881).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Hard spheres of diameter $\sigma$ are particles with the following pairwise interaction potential:

$$V(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if} \quad |\mathbf{r}_1-\mathbf{r}_2| \geq \sigma \\ \infty & \text{if} \quad |\mathbf{r}_1-\mathbf{r}_2| < \sigma \end{cases}$$

where $\mathbf{r}_1$ and $\mathbf{r}_2$ are the positions of the two particles.

:::

## Hard-spheres gas {#Hard-spheres_gas}

The first three [virial coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virial_coefficient) for hard spheres can be determined analytically

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{B_2}{v_0} &= 4 \\ \frac{B_3}{{v_0}^2} &= 10 \\ \frac{B_4}{{v_0}^3} &= -\frac{712}{35} + \frac{219 \sqrt{2}}{35 \pi}+\frac{4131}{35 \pi} \arccos{\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}}\approx 18.365 \end{aligned}$$

Higher-order ones can be determined numerically using [Monte Carlo integration](wikipedia://en/1112960). We list

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{B_5}{{v_0}^4} &= 28.24 \pm 0.08 \\ \frac{B_6}{{v_0}^5} &= 39.5 \pm 0.4 \\ \frac{B_7}{{v_0}^6} &= 56.5 \pm 1.6 \end{aligned}$$

A table of virial coefficients for up to eight dimensions can be found on the page [Hard sphere: virial coefficients](http://www.sklogwiki.org/SklogWiki/index.php/Hard_sphere:_virial_coefficients).[^o5]

*Image: Hard-sphere phase diagram pressure packing fraction.svg.* Phase diagram of hard sphere system (Solid line - stable branch, dashed line - metastable branch): Pressure $P$ as a function of the volume fraction (or packing fraction) $\eta$

The hard sphere system exhibits a fluid-solid phase transition between the [volume fractions](wikipedia://en/43518103) of freezing $\eta_\mathrm{f}\approx 0.494$ and melting $\eta_\mathrm{m} \approx 0.545$. The pressure diverges at [random close packing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_close_pack) $\eta_\mathrm{rcp}\approx 0.644$ for the metastable liquid branch and at [close packing](wikipedia://en/901260) $\eta_\mathrm{cp} = \sqrt{2}\pi/6 \approx 0.74048$ for the stable solid branch.

## Hard-spheres liquid {#Hard-spheres_liquid}

The [static structure factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_structure_factor) of the hard-spheres liquid can be calculated using the [Percus–Yevick approximation](wikipedia://en/10159772).

## Carnahan–Starling equation of state {#Carnahan–Starling_equation_of_state}

A simple, yet popular [equation of state](wikipedia://en/9908) describing systems of pure hard spheres was developed in 1969 by [Norman F. Carnahan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N._F._Carnahan) and [K. E. Starling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K._E._Starling).[^o8] By expressing the compressibility of a hard-sphere system as a geometric series, the expression

$$Z = \frac{pV}{nRT} = \frac{1 + \eta + \eta^2 - \eta^3}{{\left(1 - \eta\right)}^3}$$

is obtained, where $\eta$ is the [packing fraction](wikipedia://en/43518103), given by

$$\eta = \frac{N_A \pi n \sigma^3}{6V}$$

where $N_A$ is [Avogadro's number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avogadro_constant), $n / V$ is the [molar density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_concentration) of the fluid, and $\sigma$ is the diameter of the hard-spheres. From this Equation of State, one can obtain the [residual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residual_property_%28physics%29) [Helmholtz energy](wikipedia://en/255447),[^o9]

$$\frac{A_\text{res}}{nRT} = \frac{4 \eta - 3 \eta^2}{{\left( 1 - \eta \right)}^2} ,$$

which yields the [residual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residual_property_%28physics%29) [chemical potential](wikipedia://en/218628)

$$\frac{\mu_\text{res}}{RT} = \frac{8 \eta - 9 \eta^2 + 3 \eta^3}{{\left(1 - \eta\right)}^3}.$$

One can also obtain the value of the [radial distribution function](wikipedia://en/4538599), $g(r)$, evaluated at the surface of a sphere,[^o9]

$$g(\sigma) = \frac{1 - \frac{1}{2} \eta}{{\left(1 - \eta\right)}^3}.$$

The latter is of significant importance to accurate descriptions of more advanced intermolecular potentials based on [perturbation theory](wikipedia://en/102140), such as [SAFT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_associating_fluid_theory), where a system of hard spheres is taken as a reference system, and the complete [pair-potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pair_potential) is described by perturbations to the underlying hard-sphere system. Computation of the transport properties of hard-sphere gases at moderate densities using [revised Enskog theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revised_Enskog_theory) also relies on an accurate value for $g(\sigma)$, and the Carnahan–Starling equation of state has been used for this purpose to large success.[^o10]
