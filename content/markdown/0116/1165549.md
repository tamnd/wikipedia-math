# N-vector model

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="N-vector model"}

In [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481), the ***n*-vector model** or **O(*n*) model** is a simple system of interacting [spins](wikipedia://en/19593829) on a [crystalline lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystalline_lattice). It was developed by [H. Eugene Stanley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._Eugene_Stanley) as a generalization of the [Ising model](wikipedia://en/292744), [XY model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XY_model) and [Heisenberg model](wikipedia://en/1877286).[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

In the *n*-vector model, *n*-component unit-length classical [spins](wikipedia://en/19593829) $\mathbf{s}_i$ are placed on the vertices of a *d*-dimensional lattice. The [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319) of the *n*-vector model is given by:

$$H = K{\sum}_{\langle i,j \rangle}\mathbf{s}_i \cdot \mathbf{s}_j$$

where the sum runs over all pairs of neighboring spins $\langle i, j \rangle$ and $\cdot$ denotes the standard Euclidean inner product. Special cases of the *n*-vector model are:

$n=0$: The [self-avoiding walk](wikipedia://en/11186496)[^o5][^o6]

$n=1$: The [Ising model](wikipedia://en/292744)

$n=2$: The [XY model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XY_model)

$n=3$: The [Heisenberg model](wikipedia://en/1877286)

$n=4$: [Toy model](wikipedia://en/701142) for the [Higgs sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_sector) of the [Standard Model](wikipedia://en/47641)

The general mathematical formalism used to describe and solve the *n*-vector model and certain generalizations are developed in the article on the [Potts model](wikipedia://en/1162226).

The Hamiltonian of the *n*-vector model is also the same as the potential term of the [quantum rotor model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_rotor_model).

:::

## Reformulation as a loop model {#Reformulation_as_a_loop_model}

In a small coupling expansion, the weight of a configuration may be rewritten as

$$e^H \underset{K\to 0}{\sim} \prod_{\langle i,j \rangle}\left(1+K\mathbf{s}_i \cdot \mathbf{s}_j \right)$$

Integrating over the vector $\mathbf{s}_i$ gives rise to expressions such as

$$\int d\mathbf{s}_i\ \prod_{j=1}^4\left(\mathbf{s}_i \cdot \mathbf{s}_j\right) = \left(\mathbf{s}_1\cdot \mathbf{s}_2\right)\left(\mathbf{s}_3\cdot \mathbf{s}_4\right) + \left(\mathbf{s}_1\cdot \mathbf{s}_4\right)\left(\mathbf{s}_2\cdot \mathbf{s}_3\right) + \left(\mathbf{s}_1\cdot \mathbf{s}_3\right)\left(\mathbf{s}_2\cdot \mathbf{s}_4\right)$$

which is interpreted as a sum over the 3 possible ways of connecting the vertices $1,2,3,4$ pairwise using 2 lines going through vertex $i$. Integrating over all vectors, the corresponding lines combine into closed loops, and the partition function becomes a sum over loop configurations:

$$Z = \sum_{L\in\mathcal{L}} K^{E(L)}n^{|L|}$$

where $\mathcal{L}$ is the set of loop configurations, with $|L|$ the number of loops in the configuration $L$, and $E(L)$ the total number of lattice edges.

In two dimensions, it is common to assume that loops do not cross: either by choosing the lattice to be trivalent, or by considering the model in a dilute phase where crossings are irrelevant, or by forbidding crossings by hand. The resulting model of non-intersecting loops can then be studied using powerful algebraic methods, and its spectrum is exactly known.[^o12] Moreover, the model is closely related to the [random cluster model](wikipedia://en/60657382), which can also be formulated in terms of non-crossing loops. Much less is known in models where loops are allowed to cross, and in higher than two dimensions.

## Continuum limit {#Continuum_limit}

The [continuum limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuum_limit) can be understood to be the [sigma model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_model). This can be easily obtained by writing the Hamiltonian in terms of the product

$$-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{s}_i - \mathbf{s}_j) \cdot (\mathbf{s}_i - \mathbf{s}_j) = \mathbf{s}_i \cdot \mathbf{s}_j - 1$$

where $\mathbf{s}_i \cdot \mathbf{s}_i=1$ is the "bulk magnetization" term. Dropping this term as an overall constant factor added to the energy, the limit is obtained by defining the Newton [finite difference](wikipedia://en/39147) as

$$\delta_h[\mathbf{s}](i,j)=\frac{\mathbf{s}_i - \mathbf{s}_j}{h}$$

on neighboring lattice locations $i,j.$ Then $\delta_h[\mathbf{s}]\to\nabla_\mu\mathbf{s}$ in the limit $h\to 0$, where $\nabla_\mu$ is the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) in the $(i,j)\to\mu$ direction. Thus, in the limit,

$$-\mathbf{s}_i\cdot \mathbf{s}_j\to \tfrac{1}{2}\nabla_\mu\mathbf{s} \cdot \nabla_\mu\mathbf{s}$$

which can be recognized as the kinetic energy of the field $\mathbf{s}$ in the [sigma model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_model). One still has two possibilities for the spin $\mathbf{s}$: it is either taken from a discrete set of spins (the [Potts model](wikipedia://en/1162226)) or it is taken as a point on the [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) $S^{n-1}$; that is, $\mathbf{s}$ is a continuously-valued vector of unit length. In the later case, this is referred to as the $O(n)$ non-linear sigma model, as the [rotation group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_group) $O(n)$ is group of [isometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometries) of $S^{n-1}$, and obviously, $S^{n-1}$ isn't "flat", *i.e.* isn't a [linear field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28physics%29).

## Conformal field theory {#Conformal_field_theory}

At the critical temperature and in the continuum limit, the model gives rise to a [conformal field theory](wikipedia://en/364774#Critical_O%28N%29_model) called the critical *O(n)* model. This CFT can be analyzed using expansions in the dimension *d* or in *n*, or using the conformal bootstrap approach. Its conformal data are functions of *d* and *n*, on which many results are known.[^o11]
