# Completely metrizable space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Completely metrizable space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **completely metrizable space**[^o2] (**metrically topologically complete space**[^o3]) is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) (*X*, *T*) for which there exists at least one [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) *d* on *X* such that (*X*, *d*) is a [complete metric space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) and *d* induces the topology *T*. It is as such a special case of [metrizable space](wikipedia://en/19738).

:::

The term **topologically complete space** is employed by some authors as a synonym for *completely metrizable space*,[^o4] but sometimes also used for other classes of topological spaces, like [completely uniformizable spaces](wikipedia://en/27351720)[^o5] or [Čech-complete spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Čech-complete_space).

## Difference between *complete metric space* and *completely metrizable space* {#Difference_between_complete_metric_space_and_completely_metrizable_space}

The distinction between a *completely metrizable space* and a *complete metric space* lies in the words *there exists at least one metric* in the definition of completely metrizable space, which is not the same as *there is given a metric* (the latter would yield the definition of complete metric space). Once we make the choice of the metric on a completely metrizable space (out of all the complete metrics compatible with the topology), we get a complete metric space. In other words, the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of completely metrizable spaces is a [subcategory](wikipedia://en/310959) of that of topological spaces, while the category of complete metric spaces is not (instead, it is a subcategory of the category of metric spaces). Complete metrizability is a topological property while completeness is a property of the metric.[^o7]

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The space $(0,1) \subset \mathbf{R}$, the open unit interval, is not a complete metric space with its usual metric inherited from $\mathbf{R}$, but it is completely metrizable since it is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to $\mathbf{R}$.[^o10]
- The space $\mathbf{Q}$ of [rational numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_numbers) with the subspace topology inherited from $\mathbf{R}$ is metrizable but not completely metrizable.[^o11]

:::

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- A topological space *X* is completely metrizable if and only if *X* is [metrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrizable) and a [G<sub>δ</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-delta_set) in its [Stone–Čech compactification](wikipedia://en/52897) β*X*.[^o14]
- A subspace of a completely metrizable space $X$ is completely metrizable if and only if it is $G_{\delta}$ in $X$.[^o15]
- A countable product of nonempty metrizable spaces is completely metrizable in the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910) if and only if each factor is completely metrizable.[^o16] Hence, a product of nonempty metrizable spaces is completely metrizable if and only if at most countably many factors have more than one point and each factor is completely metrizable.[^o17]
- For every metrizable space there exists a completely metrizable space containing it as a dense subspace, since every metric space has a [completion](wikipedia://en/20018#Completion).[^o18] In general, there are many such completely metrizable spaces, since completions of a topological space with respect to different metrics compatible with its topology can give topologically different completions.

:::

## Completely metrizable abelian topological groups {#Completely_metrizable_abelian_topological_groups}

When talking about spaces with more structure than just topology, like [topological groups](wikipedia://en/42315), the natural meaning of the words “completely metrizable” would arguably be the existence of a complete metric that is also compatible with that extra structure, in addition to inducing its topology. For [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) topological groups and [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752), “compatible with the extra structure” might mean that the metric is invariant under translations.

However, no confusion can arise when talking about an abelian topological group or a topological vector space being completely metrizable: it can be proven that every abelian topological group (and thus also every topological vector space) that is completely metrizable as a topological space (i. e., admits a complete metric that induces its topology) also admits an invariant complete metric that induces its topology.[^o20]

This implies e. g. that every completely metrizable topological vector space is complete. Indeed, a topological vector space is called complete if and only if its [uniformity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformity_%28topology%29) (induced by its topology and addition operation) is complete; the uniformity induced by a translation-invariant metric that induces the topology coincides with the original uniformity.
