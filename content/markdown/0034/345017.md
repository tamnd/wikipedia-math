# Finite volume method

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Finite volume method"}

The **finite volume method** (**FVM**) is a method for representing and evaluating [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564) in the form of algebraic equations.[^o2] In the finite volume method, volume integrals in a partial differential equation that contain a [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) term are converted to [surface integrals](wikipedia://en/528867), using the [divergence theorem](wikipedia://en/151864). These terms are then evaluated as fluxes at the surfaces of each finite volume. Because the flux entering a given volume is identical to that leaving the adjacent volume, these methods are [conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_law_%28physics%29). Another advantage of the finite volume method is that it is easily formulated to allow for unstructured meshes. The method is used in many [computational fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/305924) packages. "Finite volume" refers to the small volume surrounding each node point on a mesh.[^o3]

:::

Finite volume methods can be compared and contrasted with the [finite difference methods](wikipedia://en/6054681), which approximate derivatives using nodal values, or [finite element methods](wikipedia://en/18233581), which create local approximations of a solution using local data, and construct a global approximation by stitching them together. In contrast a finite volume method evaluates exact expressions for the *average* value of the solution over some volume, and uses this data to construct approximations of the solution within cells.[^o4][^o5]

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Consider a simple 1D [advection](wikipedia://en/166377) problem:

::: {#o8 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=0,\quad t\ge0. \tag{1}$$

:::

Here, $\rho=\rho \left( x,t \right)$ represents the state variable and $f=f \left( \rho \left( x,t \right) \right)$ represents the [flux](wikipedia://en/43590) or flow of $\rho$. Conventionally, positive $f$ represents flow to the right while negative $f$ represents flow to the left. If we assume that equation ((1)) represents a flowing medium of constant area, we can sub-divide the spatial domain, $x$, into *finite volumes* or *cells* with cell centers indexed as $i$. For a particular cell, $i$, we can define the *volume average* value of ${\rho }_i \left( t \right) = \rho \left( x, t \right)$ at time ${t=t_1}$ and ${ x \in \left[ x_{i-1/2} , x_{i+1/2} \right] }$, as

::: {#o9 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\bar{\rho}_i \left( t_1 \right) = \frac{1}{ x_{i+1/2} - x_{i-1/2}} \int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}} \rho \left(x,t_1 \right)\, dx , \tag{2}$$

:::

and at time $t = t_2$ as,

::: {#o10 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$\bar{\rho}_i \left( t_2 \right) = \frac{1}{x_{i+1/2} - x_{i-1/2}} \int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}} \rho \left(x,t_2 \right)\, dx , \tag{3}$$

:::

where $x_{i-1/2}$ and $x_{i+1/2}$ represent locations of the upstream and downstream faces or edges respectively of the $i^\text{th}$ cell.

Integrating equation ((1)) in time, we have:

::: {#o11 .equation cue=numblk name="4"}

$$\rho \left( x, t_2 \right) = \rho \left( x, t_1 \right) - \int_{t_1}^{t_2} f_x \left( x,t \right)\, dt, \tag{4}$$

:::

where $f_x=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$.

To obtain the volume average of $\rho\left(x,t\right)$ at time $t=t_{2}$, we integrate $\rho\left(x,t_2 \right)$ over the cell volume, $\left[ x_{i-1/2} , x_{i+1/2} \right]$ and divide the result by $\Delta x_i = x_{i+1/2}-x_{i-1/2}$, i.e.

::: {#o12 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$$\bar{\rho}_{i}\left( t_{2}\right) =\frac{1}{\Delta x_i}\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}\left\{ \rho\left( x,t_{1}\right) - \int_{t_{1}}^{t_2} f_{x} \left( x,t \right) dt \right\} dx. \tag{5}$$

:::

We assume that $f \$ is well behaved and that we can reverse the order of integration. Also, recall that flow is normal to the unit area of the cell. Now, since in one dimension $f_x \triangleq \nabla \cdot f$, we can apply the [divergence theorem](wikipedia://en/151864), i.e. $\oint_{v}\nabla\cdot fdv=\oint_{S}f\, dS$, and substitute for the volume integral of the [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) with the values of $f(x)$ evaluated at the cell surface (edges $x_{i-1/2}$ and $x_{i+1/2}$) of the finite volume as follows:

::: {#o13 .equation cue=numblk name="6"}

$$\bar{\rho}_i \left( t_2 \right) = \bar{\rho}_i \left( t_1 \right) - \frac{1}{\Delta x_{i}} \left( \int_{t_1}^{t_2} f_{i + 1/2} dt - \int_{t_1}^{t_2} f_{i - 1/2} dt \right) . \tag{6}$$

:::

where $f_{i \pm 1/2} =f \left( x_{i \pm 1/2}, t \right)$.

We can therefore derive a *semi-discrete* numerical scheme for the above problem with cell centers indexed as $i$, and with cell edge fluxes indexed as $i\pm1/2$, by differentiating ((6)) with respect to time to obtain:

::: {#o14 .equation cue=numblk name="7"}

$$\frac{d \bar{\rho}_i}{d t} + \frac{1}{\Delta x_i} \left[ f_{i + 1/2} - f_{i - 1/2} \right] =0 , \tag{7}$$

:::

where values for the edge fluxes, $f_{i \pm 1/2}$, can be reconstructed by [interpolation](wikipedia://en/14569) or [extrapolation](wikipedia://en/546415) of the cell averages. Equation ((7)) is *exact* for the volume averages; i.e., no approximations have been made during its derivation.

This method can also be applied to a [2D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_volume_method_for_two_dimensional_diffusion_problem) situation by considering the north and south faces along with the east and west faces around a node.

:::

## General conservation law {#General_conservation_law}

We can also consider the general [conservation law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_law_%28physics%29) problem, represented by the following [PDE](wikipedia://en/52564),

::: {#o17 .equation cue=numblk name="8"}

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf u}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot {\mathbf f}\left( {\mathbf u } \right) = {\mathbf 0} . \tag{8}$$

:::

Here, $\mathbf u$ represents a vector of states and $\mathbf f$ represents the corresponding [flux](wikipedia://en/43590) tensor. Again we can sub-divide the spatial domain into finite volumes or cells. For a particular cell, $i$, we take the volume integral over the total volume of the cell, $v _{i}$, which gives,

::: {#o18 .equation cue=numblk name="9"}

$$\int _{v_{i}} \frac{\partial \mathbf u}{\partial t}\, dv + \int _{v_{i}} \nabla \cdot {\mathbf f}\left( {\mathbf u } \right)\, dv = {\mathbf 0} . \tag{9}$$

:::

On integrating the first term to get the *volume average* and applying the *divergence theorem* to the second, this yields

::: {#o19 .equation cue=numblk name="10"}

$$v_{i} {{d {\mathbf {\bar u} }_{i} } \over dt} + \oint _{S_{i} } {\mathbf f} \left( {\mathbf u } \right) \cdot {\mathbf n }\ dS = {\mathbf 0}, \tag{10}$$

:::

where $S_{i}$ represents the total surface area of the cell and ${\mathbf n}$ is a unit vector normal to the surface and pointing outward. So, finally, we are able to present the general result equivalent to ((8)), i.e.

::: {#o20 .equation cue=numblk name="11"}

$${{d {\mathbf {\bar u} }_{i} } \over {dt}} + {{1} \over {v_{i}} } \oint _{S_{i} } {\mathbf f} \left( {\mathbf u } \right)\cdot {\mathbf n }\ dS = {\mathbf 0} . \tag{11}$$

:::

Again, values for the edge fluxes can be reconstructed by interpolation or extrapolation of the cell averages. The actual numerical scheme will depend upon problem geometry and mesh construction. [MUSCL](wikipedia://en/5732212) reconstruction is often used in [high resolution schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_resolution_scheme) where shocks or discontinuities are present in the solution.

Finite volume schemes are conservative as cell averages change through the edge fluxes. In other words, *one cell's loss is always another cell's gain*!

:::
