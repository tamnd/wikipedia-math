# Linearly ordered group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Linearly ordered group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **linearly ordered** or **totally ordered group** is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G* equipped with a [total order](wikipedia://en/30330) "≤" that is *translation-invariant*. This may have different meanings. We say that (*G*, ≤) is a:

:::

- **left-ordered group** if ≤ is left-invariant, that is *a* ≤ *b* implies *ca* ≤ *cb* for all *a*, *b*, *c* in *G*,
- **right-ordered group** if ≤ is right-invariant, that is *a* ≤ *b* implies *ac* ≤ *bc* for all *a*, *b*, *c* in *G*,
- **bi-ordered group** if ≤ is bi-invariant, that is it is both left- and right-invariant.

A group *G* is said to be **left-orderable** (or **right-orderable**, or **bi-orderable**) if there exists a left- (or right-, or bi-) invariant order on *G*. A simple necessary condition for a group to be left-orderable is to have no elements of finite order; however this is not a sufficient condition. It is equivalent for a group to be left- or right-orderable; however there exist left-orderable groups which are not bi-orderable.

## Further definitions {#Further_definitions}

In this section, $\le$ is a left-invariant order on a group $G$ with [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) $e$. All that is said applies to right-invariant orders with the obvious modifications. Note that $\le$ being left-invariant is equivalent to the order $\le'$ defined by $g \le' h$ if and only if $h^{-1} \le g^{-1}$ being right-invariant. In particular, a group being left-orderable is the same as it being right-orderable.

In analogy with ordinary numbers, we call an element $g \not= e$ of an ordered group **positive** if $e \le g$. The set of positive elements in an ordered group is called the **positive cone**, it is often denoted with $G_+$; the slightly different notation $G^+$ is used for the positive cone together with the identity element.[^n1]

The positive cone $G_+$ characterises the order $\le$; indeed, by left-invariance we see that $g \le h$ if and only if $g^{-1} h \in G_+$. In fact, a left-ordered group can be defined as a group $G$ together with a subset $P$ satisfying the two conditions that:

1. for $g, h \in P$ we have also $gh \in P$;
1. let $P^{-1} = \{g^{-1} \mid g \in P\}$, then $G$ is the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) of $P, P^{-1}$ and $\{e\}$.

The order $\le_P$ associated with $P$ is defined by $g \le_P h \Leftrightarrow g^{-1} h \in P$; the first condition amounts to left-invariance and the second to the order being well-defined and total. The positive cone of $\le_P$ is $P$.

The left-invariant order $\le$ is bi-invariant if and only if it is conjugacy-invariant, that is if $g \le h$ then for any $x \in G$ we have $xgx^{-1} \le xhx^{-1}$ as well. This is equivalent to the positive cone being stable under [inner automorphisms](wikipedia://en/61839).

If $a \in G$, then the **absolute value** of $a$, denoted by $|a|$, is defined to be:

$$|a|:=\begin{cases}a, & \text{if }a \ge 0,\\ a^{-1}, & \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$$

If in addition the group $G$ is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974), then for any $a, b \in G$ a [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941) is satisfied: $|a+b| \le |a|+|b|$.[^o4]

## Examples {#Examples}

Any left- or right-orderable group is [torsion](wikipedia://en/3039330)-free, that is it contains no elements of finite order besides the identity. Conversely, [F. W. Levi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._W._Levi) showed that a torsion-free [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) is bi-orderable;[^n2] this is still true for [nilpotent groups](wikipedia://en/144569)[^n3] but there exist torsion-free, [finitely presented groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_presented_group) which are not left-orderable.

### Archimedean ordered groups {#Archimedean_ordered_groups}

[Otto Hölder](wikipedia://en/191652) showed that every [Archimedean group](wikipedia://en/264169) (a bi-ordered group satisfying an [Archimedean property](wikipedia://en/264158)) is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of the additive group of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), (Fuchs Salce 2001, 61). If we write the Archimedean l.o. group multiplicatively, this may be shown by considering the [Dedekind completion](wikipedia://en/16667323), $\widehat{G}$ of the closure of a l.o. group under $n$th roots. We endow this space with the usual [topology](wikipedia://en/30450#Definitions) of a linear order, and then it can be shown that for each $g\in\widehat{G}$ the exponential maps $g^{\cdot}:(\mathbb{R},+)\to(\widehat{G},\cdot) :\lim_{i}q_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}\mapsto \lim_{i}g^{q_{i}}$ are well defined order preserving/reversing, [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) isomorphisms. Completing a l.o. group can be difficult in the non-Archimedean case. In these cases, one may classify a group by its [rank](wikipedia://en/18720088): which is related to the order type of the largest sequence of convex subgroups.

### Other examples {#Other_examples}

[Free groups](wikipedia://en/59735) are left-orderable. More generally this is also the case for [right-angled Artin groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-angled_Artin_group).[^o12] [Braid groups](wikipedia://en/550138) are also left-orderable.[^o13]

The group given by the presentation $\langle a, b | a^2ba^2b^{-1}, b^2ab^2a^{-1}\rangle$ is torsion-free but not left-orderable;[^n4] note that it is a 3-dimensional [crystallographic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallographic_group) (it can be realised as the group generated by two glided half-turns with orthogonal axes and the same translation length), and it is the same group that was proven to be a counterexample to the [unit conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaplansky_conjectures). More generally the topic of orderability of 3--manifold groups is interesting for its relation with various topological invariants.[^o15] There exists a 3-manifold group which is left-orderable but not bi-orderable[^o16] (in fact it does not satisfy the weaker property of being locally indicable).

Left-orderable groups have also attracted interest from the perspective of [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087) as it is known that a countable group is left-orderable if and only if it acts on the real line by homeomorphisms.[^n5] Non-examples related to this paradigm are [lattices](wikipedia://en/15782871) in higher rank [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945); it is known that (for example) finite-index subgroups in $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb Z)$ are not left-orderable;[^o18] a wide generalisation of this was announced in 2020.[^o19]
