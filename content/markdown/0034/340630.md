# Function space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Function space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **function space** is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) between two fixed sets. Often, the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) and/or [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) will have additional [structure](wikipedia://en/1809181) which is inherited by the function space. For example, the set of functions from any set $X$ into a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) has a [natural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mathematical_jargon#natural) vector space structure given by [pointwise](wikipedia://en/4786797) addition and scalar multiplication. In other scenarios, the function space might inherit a [topological](wikipedia://en/30450) or [metric](wikipedia://en/20018) structure, hence the name function *space*. Often in mathematical jargon, especially in analysis or geometry, a function could refer to a map of the form $X\to \mathbb{R}$ or $X\to \mathbb{C}$ where $X$ is the space in question. Whilst other maps of the form $X\to Y$ between any two spaces are simply referred to as maps. Example of this can be the space of compactly supported functions on a topological space. However in a larger context a function space could just consist of a set of functions (set theoretically) equipped with possibly some extra structure.

:::

## In linear algebra {#In_linear_algebra}

Let $F$ be a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) and let $X$ be any set. The set of functions $X$ → $F$ can be given the structure of a vector space over $F$ where the operations are defined pointwise[^o3]. That is, for any $f$, $g$ : $X$ → $F$, any $x$ in $X$, and any $c$ in $F$, define

$$\begin{aligned} (f+g)(x) &= f(x)+g(x) \\ (c\cdot f)(x) &= c\cdot f(x). \end{aligned}$$

When the domain $X$ has additional structure, one might consider instead the [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) (or [subspace](wikipedia://en/56357)) of all such functions which respect that structure. For example, if $V$ and also $X$ itself are vector spaces over $F$, the set of [linear maps](wikipedia://en/18102) $X$ → $V$ form a vector space over $F$ with pointwise operations (often denoted [Hom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hom_set)($X$,$V$)). One such space is the [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) of $X$: the set of [linear functionals](wikipedia://en/214137) $X$ → $F$ with addition and scalar multiplication defined pointwise.

The cardinal [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) of a function space with no extra structure can be found by the [Erdős–Kaplansky theorem](wikipedia://en/73198875).

## Examples {#Examples}

Function spaces appear in various areas of mathematics:

- In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), the set of functions from *X* to *Y* may be denoted {*X* → *Y*} or *Y*<sup>*X*</sup>.
  - As a special case, the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of a set *X* may be identified with the set of all functions from *X* to {0, 1}, denoted 2<sup>*X*</sup>.
- The set of [bijections](wikipedia://en/3942) from *X* to *Y* is denoted $X \leftrightarrow Y$. The factorial notation *X*! may be used for permutations of a single set *X*.
- In [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), the same is seen for [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) linear transformations, including [topologies on the vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) in the above, and many of the major examples are function spaces carrying a [topology](wikipedia://en/29954); the best known examples include [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932) and [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989).
- In [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), the set of all functions from the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) to some set *X* is called a *[sequence space](wikipedia://en/1701075)*. It consists of the set of all possible [sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequences) of elements of *X*.
- In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), one may attempt to put a topology on the space of continuous functions from a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X* to another one *Y*, with utility depending on the nature of the spaces. A commonly used example is the [compact-open topology](wikipedia://en/726335), e.g. [loop space](wikipedia://en/2174332). Also available is the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910) on the space of set theoretic functions (i.e. not necessarily continuous functions) *Y*<sup>*X*</sup>. In this context, this topology is also referred to as the [topology of pointwise convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology_of_pointwise_convergence).
- In [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), the study of [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831) is essentially that of discrete invariants of function spaces;
- In the theory of [stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/47895), the basic technical problem is how to construct a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) on a function space of *paths of the process* (functions of time);
- In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), the function space is called an [exponential object](wikipedia://en/2237858) or [map object](wikipedia://en/2237858). It appears in one way as the representation [canonical bifunctor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_bifunctor); but as (single) functor, of type $[X,-]$, it appears as an [adjoint functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_functor) to a functor of type $- \times X$ on objects;
- In [functional programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_programming) and [lambda calculus](wikipedia://en/18203), [function types](wikipedia://en/583532) are used to express the idea of [higher-order functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher-order_function)
- In programming more generally, many [higher-order function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher-order_function) concepts occur with or without explicit typing, such as [closures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closure_%28computer_programming%29).
- In [domain theory](wikipedia://en/325077), the basic idea is to find constructions from [partial orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) that can model lambda calculus, by creating a well-behaved [Cartesian closed category](wikipedia://en/237037).
- In the [representation theory of finite groups](wikipedia://en/874400), given two finite-dimensional representations $V$ and $W$ of a group $G$, one can form a representation of $G$ over the vector space of linear maps Hom($V$,$W$) called the [Hom representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hom_representation).[^o6]

## Exponential law {#Exponential_law}

For (not necessarily continuous) functions, there is the exponential law that relates functions on a product on one hand and function-valued functions on the other; namely,

$$\operatorname{Fun}(X \times Y, Z) \underset{\sim}\overset{f \mapsto \widetilde{f}}\to \operatorname{Fun}(X, \operatorname{Fun}(Y, Z)), \, \widetilde{f}(x)(y) = f(x, y)$$

is bijective, where $\operatorname{Fun}(X, Y)$ denotes the set of functions from a set $X$ to a set $Y$. (That is, $- \times Y$ is [left adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_functor) to $\operatorname{Fun}(Y, -).$)

In practice, we are usually interested in the analog of the above for continuous functions. In general, however, the exponential law for continuous maps can fail. What we have is this. We write $C(X, Y)$ for the set of all continuous maps from a topological space $X$ to another $Y$. Suppose $X, Y, Z$ are topological spaces. *If* $C(Y, Z)$ has a coarsest topology such that the evaluation map $e : C(Y, Z) \times Y \to Z$ is continuous, the exponential law holds:

$$C(X \times Y, Z) \underset{\sim}\overset{f \mapsto \widetilde{f}}\to C(X, C(Y, Z))$$

is bijective.[^o9] But such a coarsest topology may not exist in general (in some cases, a [compact-open topology](wikipedia://en/726335) is such a coarsest topology).

:::

## Functional analysis {#Functional_analysis}

A main theme of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) is to study function spaces and vector spaces with more structure than the bare minimum of linear structure. Specifically, some are [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752), some are [Banach spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_spaces), some are [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932), etc. This allows mathematicians to apply intuitions from finite-dimensional vector spaces.

The functional spaces have intricate interrelationships, such as [interpolation](wikipedia://en/18587912), embedding, representation, Banach space isomorphism, etc. Many fundamental theorems and constructions in functional analysis deals with their relationships, such as the [Riesz representation theorem](wikipedia://en/26452), the [Riesz–Thorin theorem](wikipedia://en/967440), the [Gagliardo–Nirenberg interpolation inequality](wikipedia://en/38500906), the [Rellich–Kondrachov theorem](wikipedia://en/7907151), the [Hardy–Littlewood maximal function](wikipedia://en/10094198), etc.

Let $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be an open subset.

- $B(\Omega)$ [bounded functions](wikipedia://en/311509)
- continuous ones
  - $C(\Omega)$ [continuous functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_functions) endowed with the [uniform norm](wikipedia://en/349755) topology
  - $C_c(\Omega)$ continuous functions with [compact support](wikipedia://en/381013#Compact_support)
  - $C_b(\Omega)$ continuous bounded functions
  - $C_0(\Omega)$ continuous functions which vanish at infinity; a closed subspace of $C_b(\Omega)$[^o11]
  - $C^r(\Omega)$ continuous functions that have *r* continuous derivatives.
- smooth ones
  - $C^{\infty}(\Omega)$ [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_functions)
  - $C^{\infty}_c(\Omega)$ [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_functions) with [compact support](wikipedia://en/381013#Compact_support) (i.e. the set of [bump functions](wikipedia://en/815799))
  - $C^\omega(\Omega)$ [real analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478)
- $L^p(\Omega)$, for $1\leq p \leq \infty$, is the [L<sup>p</sup> space](wikipedia://en/45194) of [measurable](wikipedia://en/44055) functions whose *p*-norm $\|f\|_p = \left( \int_\Omega |f|^p \right)^{1/p}$ is finite
- $\mathcal{S}(\Omega)$, the [Schwartz space](wikipedia://en/4277567) of [rapidly decreasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapidly_decreasing) [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_functions) and its continuous dual, $\mathcal{S}'(\Omega)$ [tempered distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tempered_distributions)
- $D(\Omega)$ compact support in limit topology
- $\text{Lip}_0(\Omega)$, the space of all [Lipschitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipschitz_continuous) functions on $\Omega$ that vanish at zero.
- $W^{k,p}$ [Sobolev space](wikipedia://en/611964) of functions whose [weak derivatives](wikipedia://en/1411087) up to order *k* are in $L^p$
- $\mathcal{O}_U$ holomorphic functions
- $BMO(\Omega)$, space of [bounded mean oscillation](wikipedia://en/4694026). Also called John–Nirenberg space
- linear functions
- piecewise linear functions
- continuous functions, compact open topology
- all functions, space of pointwise convergence
- [Hardy space](wikipedia://en/314780)
- [Hölder space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hölder_space)
- [Skorokhod space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skorokhod_space): the space of [càdlàg](wikipedia://en/30862839) functions.
- [Besov space](wikipedia://en/18542594)
- [Souček space](wikipedia://en/15164481)
- [Triebel–Lizorkin space](wikipedia://en/26732419)
- [Barron space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barron_space)

## Uniform norm {#Uniform_norm}

If $y$ is an element of the function space $\mathcal {C}(a,b)$ of all [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) that are defined on a [closed interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_interval) *a*, *b*, the **[norm](wikipedia://en/990534) $\|y\|_\infty$** defined on $\mathcal {C}(a,b)$ is the maximum [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of $y (x)$ for $a \le x \le b$,[^o13]

$$\| y \|_\infty \equiv \max_{a \le x \le b} |y(x)| \qquad \text{where} \ \ y \in \mathcal {C}(a,b)$$

is called the *[uniform norm](wikipedia://en/349755)* or *supremum norm* ('sup norm').
