# Dimension theorem for vector spaces

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dimension theorem for vector spaces"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **dimension theorem for vector spaces** states that all [bases](wikipedia://en/18420) of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) have equally many elements. This number of elements may be finite or infinite (in the latter case, it is a [cardinal number](wikipedia://en/6173)), and defines the [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) of the vector space.

:::

Formally, the dimension theorem for vector spaces states that:

As a basis is a [generating set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generating_set) that is [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent), the dimension theorem is a consequence of the following [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977), which is also useful:

In particular if $V$ is [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430), then all its bases are finite and have the same number of elements.

While the [proof](wikipedia://en/82285) of the existence of a basis for any vector space in the general case requires [Zorn's lemma](wikipedia://en/51442) and is in fact equivalent to the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840), the uniqueness of the cardinality of the basis requires only the [ultrafilter lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrafilter_lemma),[^o2] which is strictly weaker (the proof given below, however, assumes [trichotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichotomy_%28mathematics%29), i.e., that all [cardinal numbers](wikipedia://en/6173) are comparable, a statement which is also equivalent to the axiom of choice). The theorem can be generalized to arbitrary [$R$-modules](wikipedia://en/276410) for rings $R$ having [invariant basis number](wikipedia://en/3327998).

In the finitely generated case the proof uses only elementary arguments of [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), and does not require the axiom of choice nor its weaker variants.

::: {#o4 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

Let $V$ be a vector space, $\{a_{i} : i \in I\}$ be a [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) set of elements of $V$, and $\{b_{j} : j \in J\}$ be a [generating set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generating_set). One has to prove that the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of $I$ is not larger than that of $J$.

If $J$ is finite, this results from the [Steinitz exchange lemma](wikipedia://en/20506374). (Indeed, the Steinitz exchange lemma implies every finite subset of $I$ has cardinality not larger than that of $J$, hence $I$ is finite with cardinality not larger than that of $J$.) If $J$ is finite, a proof based on [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) theory is also possible.[^o5]

Assume that $J$ is infinite. If $I$ is finite, there is nothing to prove. Thus, we may assume that $I$ is also infinite. Let us suppose that the cardinality of $I$ is larger than that of $J$.[^o6] We have to prove that this leads to a contradiction.

By [Zorn's lemma](wikipedia://en/51442), every linearly independent set is contained in a maximal linearly independent set $K$. This maximality implies that $K$ spans $V$ and is therefore a basis (the maximality implies that every element of $V$ is linearly dependent from the elements of $K$, and therefore is a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of elements of $K$). As the cardinality of $K$ is greater than or equal to the cardinality of $I$, one may replace $\{a_{i}: i \in I\}$ with $K$; that is, one may suppose, [without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000), that $\{a_{i} : i \in I\}$ is a basis.

Thus, every $b_{j}$ can be written as a finite sum

$$b_j = \sum_{i\in E_j} \lambda_{i,j} a_i,$$

where $E_j$ is a finite subset of $I.$ Let $E=\textstyle\bigcup_{j \in J} E_j$. Since $V$ is spanned by $\{b_j\colon j\in J\}$, which is itself spanned by the $\{a_i\colon i\in E\}$, the latter set spans $V$. Since this set is a subset of a basis, this implies that $K=I$ and $I=\textstyle\bigcup_{j \in J} E_j$. This shows that the cardinality of $I$ is at most the one of $J$, since the cardinality of an infinite [indexed family](wikipedia://en/23624339) of finite sets is at most the cardinality of the index set.[^o6]

:::
