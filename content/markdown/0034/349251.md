# Diagonal

*Image: Cube diagonals.svg.* The diagonals of a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) with side length 1. AC' (shown in blue) is a [space diagonal](wikipedia://en/1337505) with length $\sqrt 3$, while AC (shown in red) is a [face diagonal](wikipedia://en/10830506) and has length $\sqrt 2$.

*Image: Horizontal vertical and diagonal.svg.* Horizontal (left), vertical (center) and diagonal (right) double arrows.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Diagonal"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **diagonal** is a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) joining two [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) of a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) or [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470), when those vertices are not on the same [edge](wikipedia://en/12158034). Informally, any sloping line is called diagonal. The word *diagonal* derives from the [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) διαγώνιος *diagonios*,[^o2] "from corner to corner" (from διά- *dia-*, "through", "across" and γωνία *gonia*, "corner", related to *gony* "knee"); it was used by both [Strabo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strabo)[^o3] and [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)[^o4] to refer to a line connecting two vertices of a [rhombus](wikipedia://en/183884) or [cuboid](wikipedia://en/156802),[^o5] and later adopted into Latin as *diagonus* ("slanting line").

:::

## Polygons {#Polygons}

As applied to a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621), a diagonal is a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) joining any two non-consecutive vertices. Therefore, a [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278) has two diagonals, joining opposite pairs of vertices. For any [convex polygon](wikipedia://en/660019), all the diagonals are inside the polygon, but for [re-entrant polygons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Re-entrant_polygon), some diagonals are outside of the polygon.

Any *n*-sided polygon (*n* ≥ 3), [convex](wikipedia://en/660019) or [concave](wikipedia://en/605156), has $\tfrac{n(n-3)}{2}$ *total* diagonals, as each vertex has diagonals to all other vertices except itself and the two adjacent vertices, or $n - 3$ diagonals, and each diagonal is shared by two vertices.

In general, a regular *n*-sided polygon has $\left\lfloor\frac {n-2}{2}\right\rfloor$ *distinct* diagonals in length, which follows the pattern 1,1,2,2,3,3... starting from a square.

### Regions formed by diagonals {#Regions_formed_by_diagonals}

In a [convex polygon](wikipedia://en/660019), if no three diagonals are [concurrent](wikipedia://en/2206157) at a single point in the interior, the number of regions that the diagonals divide the interior into is given by[^o8]

$$\binom n4 + \binom {n-1}2 = \frac{(n-1)(n-2)(n^2-3n+12)}{24}.$$

For *n*-gons with *n*=3, 4, ... the number of regions is

1, 4, 11, 25, 50, 91, 154, 246...

This is [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OEIS) sequence A006522.

### Intersections of diagonals {#Intersections_of_diagonals}

If no three diagonals of a convex polygon are concurrent at a point in the interior, the number of interior intersections of diagonals is given by $\textstyle \binom n4$.[^o11][^o12] This holds, for example, for any [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) with an odd number of sides. The formula follows from the fact that each intersection is uniquely determined by the four endpoints of the two intersecting diagonals: the number of intersections is thus the number of combinations of the *n* vertices four at a time.

### Regular polygons {#Regular_polygons}

Although the number of distinct diagonals in a polygon increases as its number of sides increases, the length of any diagonal can be calculated.

In a regular n-gon with side length *a*, the length of the *xth* shortest distinct diagonal is:

$$\sin \left(\frac{\pi(x+1)}{n}\right) \csc \left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right) \cdot a$$

This formula shows that as the number of sides approaches infinity, the *xth* shortest diagonal approaches the length $(x+1)a$. Additionally, the formula for the shortest diagonal simplifies in the case of x = 1:

$$\sin \left(\frac{2\pi}{n}\right) \csc \left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right) \cdot a = 2\cos \left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right) \cdot a$$

If the number of sides is even, the longest diagonal will be equivalent to the diameter of the polygon's [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432) because the long diagonals all intersect each other at the polygon's center.

Special cases include:

A [square](wikipedia://en/659939) has two diagonals of equal length, which intersect at the center of the square. The ratio of a diagonal to a side is $\sqrt{2}\approx 1.414.$

A [regular pentagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_pentagon) has five diagonals all of the same length. The ratio of a diagonal to a side is the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386), $\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\approx 1.618.$

#### Short and long diagonals {#Short_and_long_diagonals}

A regular [hexagon](wikipedia://en/59733) has nine diagonals: the six shorter ones are equal to each other in length; the three longer ones are equal to each other in length and intersect each other at the center of the hexagon. The ratio of a long diagonal to a side is 2, and the ratio of a short diagonal to a side is $\sqrt{3}$.

A regular [heptagon](wikipedia://en/365349) has 14 diagonals. The seven shorter ones equal each other, and the seven longer ones equal each other. The reciprocal of the side equals the sum of the reciprocals of a short and a long diagonal.

## Polyhedra {#Polyhedra}

A [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) (a [solid object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_object) in [three-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/3054853), bounded by [two-dimensional](wikipedia://en/19469852) [faces](wikipedia://en/149926)) may have two different types of diagonals: face diagonals on the various faces, connecting non-adjacent vertices on the same face; and space diagonals, entirely in the interior of the polyhedron (except for the endpoints on the vertices).

## Higher dimensions {#Higher_dimensions}

### N-Cube {#N-Cube}

The lengths of an n-dimensional [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783)'s diagonals can be calculated by [mathematical induction](wikipedia://en/18881). The longest diagonal of an n-cube is $\sqrt{n}$. Additionally, there are $2^{n-1}\binom{n}{x+1}$ of the *xth* shortest diagonal. As an example, a 5-cube would have the diagonals:

| Diagonal length Number of diagonals $\sqrt{2}$ 160 $\sqrt{3}$ 160 2 80 $\sqrt{5}$ 16 |
|---|

Its total number of diagonals is 416. In general, an n-cube has a total of $2^{n-1}(2^n-n-1)$ diagonals. This follows from the more general form of $\frac{v(v-1)}{2}-e$ which describes the total number of face and space diagonals in convex [polytopes](wikipedia://en/23471).[^o18] Here, v represents the number of vertices and e represents the number of edges.

## Geometry {#Geometry}

By analogy, the [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) *X*×*X* of any set *X* with itself, consisting of all pairs $(x,x)$, is called the diagonal, and is the [graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_of_a_relation) of the [equality](wikipedia://en/90446) [relation](wikipedia://en/22517558) on *X* or equivalently the [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of the [identity function](wikipedia://en/15069) from *X* to *X*. This plays an important part in geometry; for example, the [fixed points](wikipedia://en/449738) of a [mapping](wikipedia://en/185427) *F* from *X* to itself may be obtained by intersecting the graph of *F* with the diagonal.

In geometric studies, the idea of intersecting the diagonal *with itself* is common, not directly, but by perturbing it within an [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260). This is related at a deep level with the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) and the zeros of [vector fields](wikipedia://en/62641). For example, the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) *S*<sup>1</sup> has [Betti numbers](wikipedia://en/346262) 1, 1, 0, 0, 0, and therefore Euler characteristic 0. A geometric way of expressing this is to look at the diagonal on the two-[torus](wikipedia://en/74800) *S*<sup>1</sup>×S<sup>1</sup> and observe that it can move *off itself* by the small motion (*θ*, *θ*) to (*θ*, *θ* + *ε*). In general, the intersection number of the graph of a function with the diagonal may be computed using [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) via the [Lefschetz fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/582530); the self-intersection of the diagonal is the special case of the identity function.
