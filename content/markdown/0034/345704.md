# Poisson algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Poisson algebra"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Poisson algebra** is an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) together with a [Lie bracket](wikipedia://en/17944) that also satisfies [Leibniz's law](wikipedia://en/248988); that is, the bracket is also a [derivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivation_%28abstract_algebra%29). Poisson algebras appear naturally in [Hamiltonian mechanics](wikipedia://en/198319), and are also central in the study of [quantum groups](wikipedia://en/340678). [Manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) with a Poisson algebra structure are known as [Poisson manifolds](wikipedia://en/882902), of which the [symplectic manifolds](wikipedia://en/28356) and the [Poisson–Lie groups](wikipedia://en/4257207) are a special case. The algebra is named in honour of [Siméon Denis Poisson](wikipedia://en/44670).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A Poisson algebra is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K* equipped with two [bilinear](wikipedia://en/4365) products, ⋅ and {, }, having the following properties:

- The product ⋅ forms an [associative *K*-algebra](wikipedia://en/2112).
- The product {, }, called the [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340), forms a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944), and so it is anti-symmetric, and obeys the [Jacobi identity](wikipedia://en/294370).
- The Poisson bracket acts as a [derivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivation_%28abstract_algebra%29) of the associative product ⋅, so that for any three elements *x*, *y* and *z* in the algebra, one has {*x*, *y* ⋅ *z*} = {*x*, *y*} ⋅ *z* + *y* ⋅ {*x*, *z*}.

The last property often allows a variety of different formulations of the algebra to be given, as noted in the examples below.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

Poisson algebras occur in various settings.

### Symplectic manifolds {#Symplectic_manifolds}

The space of real-valued [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) over a [symplectic manifold](wikipedia://en/28356) forms a Poisson algebra. On a symplectic manifold, every real-valued function *H* on the manifold induces a vector field *X<sub>H</sub>*, the [Hamiltonian vector field](wikipedia://en/1818270). Then, given any two smooth functions *F* and *G* over the symplectic manifold, the Poisson bracket may be defined as:

$$\{F,G\}=dG(X_F) = X_F(G)\,.$$

This definition is consistent in part because the Poisson bracket acts as a derivation. Equivalently, one may define the bracket {,} as

$$X_{\{F,G\}}=[X_F,X_G]\,$$

where \[,\] is the [Lie derivative](wikipedia://en/294437). When the symplectic manifold is **R**<sup>2*n*</sup> with the standard symplectic structure, then the Poisson bracket takes on the well-known form

$$\{F,G\}=\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial F}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial G}{\partial p_i}-\frac{\partial F}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial G}{\partial q_i}.$$

Similar considerations apply for [Poisson manifolds](wikipedia://en/882902), which generalize symplectic manifolds by allowing the symplectic bivector to be rank deficient.

### Lie algebras {#Lie_algebras}

The [tensor algebra](wikipedia://en/363628) of a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) has a Poisson algebra structure. A very explicit construction of this is given in the article on [universal enveloping algebras](wikipedia://en/363551).

The construction proceeds by first building the [tensor algebra](wikipedia://en/363628) of the underlying vector space of the Lie algebra. The tensor algebra is simply the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) ([direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) ⊕) of all tensor products of this vector space. One can then show that the Lie bracket can be consistently lifted to the entire tensor algebra: it obeys both the product rule, and the Jacobi identity of the Poisson bracket, and thus is the Poisson bracket, when lifted. The pair of products {,} and ⊗ then form a Poisson algebra. Observe that ⊗ is neither commutative nor is it anti-commutative: it is merely associative.

Thus, one has the general statement that the tensor algebra of any Lie algebra is a Poisson algebra. The universal enveloping algebra is obtained by modding out the Poisson algebra structure.

### Associative algebras {#Associative_algebras}

If *A* is an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112), then imposing the commutator \[*x*, *y*\] = *xy* − *yx* turns it into a Poisson algebra (and thus, also a Lie algebra) *A*<sub>*L*</sub>. Note that the resulting *A*<sub>*L*</sub> should not be confused with the tensor algebra construction described in the previous section. If one wished, one could also apply that construction as well, but that would give a different Poisson algebra, one that would be much larger.

### Vertex operator algebras {#Vertex_operator_algebras}

For a [vertex operator algebra](wikipedia://en/1287577) (*V*, *Y*, *ω*, 1), the space *V*/*C*<sub>2</sub>(*V*) is a Poisson algebra with {*a*, *b*} = *a*<sub>0</sub>*b* and *a* ⋅ *b* = *a*<sub>−1</sub>*b*. For certain vertex operator algebras, these Poisson algebras are finite-dimensional.

### Z<sub>2</sub> grading {#Z2_grading}

Poisson algebras can be given a **Z**<sub>2</sub>-[grading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded_algebra) in one of two different ways. These two result in the [Poisson superalgebra](wikipedia://en/882793) and the [Gerstenhaber algebra](wikipedia://en/883034). The difference between the two is in the grading of the product itself. For the Poisson superalgebra, the grading is given by

$$|\{a,b\}| = |a|+|b|$$

whereas in the Gerstenhaber algebra, the bracket decreases the grading by one:

$$|\{a,b\}| = |a|+|b| - 1$$

In both of these expressions $|a|=\deg a$ denotes the grading of the element $a$; typically, it counts how $a$ can be decomposed into an even or odd product of generating elements. Gerstenhaber algebras conventionally occur in [BRST quantization](wikipedia://en/6769980).
