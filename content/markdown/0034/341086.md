# Non-monotonic logic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Non-monotonic logic"}

A **non-monotonic logic** is a [formal logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_logic) whose [entailment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entailment) relation is not [monotonic](wikipedia://en/693185). In other words, non-monotonic logics are devised to capture and represent [defeasible inferences](wikipedia://en/2628057), i.e., a kind of inference in which reasoners draw tentative conclusions, enabling reasoners to retract their conclusion(s) based on further evidence.[^o2] Most studied formal logics have a monotonic entailment relation, meaning that adding a formula to the hypotheses never produces a pruning of its set of conclusions. Intuitively, monotonicity indicates that learning a new piece of knowledge cannot reduce the set of what is known. Monotonic logics cannot handle various reasoning tasks such as [reasoning by default](wikipedia://en/889639) (conclusions may be derived only because of lack of evidence of the contrary), [abductive reasoning](wikipedia://en/60459) (conclusions are only deduced as most likely explanations), some important approaches to reasoning about knowledge (the ignorance of a conclusion must be retracted when the conclusion becomes known), and similarly, [belief revision](wikipedia://en/1187311) (new knowledge may contradict old beliefs).

:::

## Abductive reasoning {#Abductive_reasoning}

[Abductive reasoning](wikipedia://en/60459) is the process of deriving a sufficient explanation of the known facts. An abductive logic should not be monotonic because the likely explanations are not necessarily correct. For example, the likely explanation for seeing wet grass is that it rained; however, this explanation has to be retracted when learning that the real cause of the grass being wet was a sprinkler. Since the old explanation (it rained) is retracted because of the addition of a piece of knowledge (a sprinkler was active), any logic that models explanations is non-monotonic.

## Reasoning about knowledge {#Reasoning_about_knowledge}

If a logic includes formulae that mean that something is not known, this logic should not be monotonic. Indeed, learning something that was previously not known leads to the removal of the formula specifying that this piece of knowledge is not known. This second change (a removal caused by an addition) violates the condition of monotonicity. A logic for reasoning about knowledge is the [autoepistemic logic](wikipedia://en/2738697).

## Belief revision {#Belief_revision}

[Belief revision](wikipedia://en/1187311) is the process of changing beliefs to accommodate a new belief that might be inconsistent with the old ones. In the assumption that the new belief is correct, some of the old ones have to be retracted in order to maintain consistency. This retraction in response to an addition of a new belief makes any logic for belief revision non-monotonic. The belief revision approach is alternative to [paraconsistent logics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraconsistent_logics), which tolerate inconsistency rather than attempting to remove it.

## Proof-theoretic versus model-theoretic formalizations of non-monotonic logics {#Proof-theoretic_versus_model-theoretic_formalizations_of_non-monotonic_logics}

[Proof-theoretic](wikipedia://en/183478) formalization of a non-monotonic logic begins with adoption of certain non-monotonic [rules of inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rules_of_inference), and then prescribes contexts in which these non-monotonic rules may be applied in admissible deductions. This typically is accomplished by means of fixed-point equations that relate the sets of premises and the sets of their non-monotonic conclusions. [Default logic](wikipedia://en/889639) and [autoepistemic logic](wikipedia://en/2738697) are the most common examples of non-monotonic logics that have been formalized that way.[^o8]

[Model-theoretic](wikipedia://en/19858) formalization of a non-monotonic logic begins with restriction of the [semantics](wikipedia://en/29107) of a suitable monotonic logic to some special models, for instance, to minimal models,[^o9][^o10] and then derives a set of non-monotonic [rules of inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rules_of_inference), possibly with some restrictions on which contexts these rules may be applied in, so that the resulting deductive system is [sound](wikipedia://en/26819) and [complete](wikipedia://en/31271451) with respect to the restricted [semantics](wikipedia://en/29107).[^o11] Unlike some proof-theoretic formalizations that suffered from well-known paradoxes and were often hard to evaluate with respect of their consistency with the intuitions they were supposed to capture, model-theoretic formalizations were paradox-free and left little, if any, room for confusion about what non-monotonic patterns of reasoning they covered. Examples of proof-theoretic formalizations of non-monotonic reasoning, which revealed some undesirable or paradoxical properties or did not capture the desired intuitive comprehensions, that have been successfully (consistent with respective intuitive comprehensions and with no paradoxical properties, that is) formalized by model-theoretic means include [first-order circumscription](wikipedia://en/2634917), [closed-world assumption](wikipedia://en/2526582),[^o11] and [autoepistemic logic](wikipedia://en/2738697).[^o8]
