# Multiplicative order

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Multiplicative order"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), given a positive integer *n* and an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *a* [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) to *n*, the **multiplicative order** of *a* modulo *n* is the smallest positive integer *k* such that *a*<sup>*k*</sup> ≡ 1 (mod *n*).[^o2]

:::

In other words, the multiplicative order of *a* modulo *n* is the [order](wikipedia://en/493527) of *a* in the [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742) of the [units](wikipedia://en/289666) in the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) of the integers [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) *n*.

The order of *a* modulo *n* is sometimes written as ord<sub>*n*</sub>(*a*).[^o3]

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

The powers of 4 modulo 7 are as follows:

$$\begin{array}{llll} 4^0 &= 1 &=0 \times 7 + 1 &\equiv 1\pmod7 \\ 4^1 &= 4 &=0 \times 7 + 4 &\equiv 4\pmod7 \\ 4^2 &= 16 &=2 \times 7 + 2 &\equiv 2\pmod7 \\ 4^3 &= 64 &=9 \times 7 + 1 &\equiv 1\pmod7 \\ 4^4 &= 256 &=36 \times 7 + 4 &\equiv 4\pmod7 \\ 4^5 &= 1024 &=146 \times 7 + 2 &\equiv 2\pmod7 \\ \vdots\end{array}$$

The smallest positive integer *k* such that 4<sup>*k*</sup> ≡ 1 (mod 7) is 3, so the order of 4 (mod 7) is 3. Note that $a^0 = 1 \equiv 1 \pmod n$ is trivially true for any non-zero $a$, but since zero is not a positive integer, trivial solutions are not valid.

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Even without knowledge that we are working in the [multiplicative group of integers modulo n](wikipedia://en/1195234), we can show that *a* actually has an order by noting that the powers of *a* can only take a finite number of different values modulo *n*, so according to the [pigeonhole principle](wikipedia://en/54217) there must be two powers, say *s* and *t* and [without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000) *s* > *t*, such that *a*<sup>*s*</sup> ≡ *a*<sup>*t*</sup> (mod *n*). Since *a* and *n* are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime), *a* has an inverse element *a*<sup>−1</sup> and we can multiply both sides of the congruence with *a*<sup>−*t*</sup>, yielding *a*<sup>*s*−*t*</sup> ≡ 1 (mod *n*).

The concept of multiplicative order is a special case of the [order of group elements](wikipedia://en/493527). The multiplicative order of a number *a* modulo *n* is the order of *a* in the [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1195234) whose elements are the residues modulo *n* of the numbers coprime to *n*, and whose group operation is multiplication modulo *n*. This is the [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) of the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) **Z**<sub>*n*</sub>; it has *φ*(*n*) elements, *φ* being [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452), and is denoted as *U*(*n*) or *U*(**Z**<sub>*n*</sub>).

As a consequence of [Lagrange's theorem](wikipedia://en/31150), the order of *a* (mod *n*) always [divides](wikipedia://en/49492) *φ*(*n*). If the order of *a* is actually equal to *φ*(*n*), and therefore as large as possible, then *a* is called a [primitive root](wikipedia://en/186864) modulo *n*. This means that the group *U*(*n*) is [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327) and the residue class of *a* [generates](wikipedia://en/52327#Definition_and_notation) it.

The order of *a* (mod *n*) also divides *λ*(*n*), a value of the [Carmichael function](wikipedia://en/1181756), which is an even stronger statement than the divisibility of *φ*(*n*).

:::
