# Lévy process

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lévy process"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), a **Lévy process**, named after the French mathematician [Paul Lévy](wikipedia://en/556462), is a [stochastic process](wikipedia://en/47895) with independent, stationary increments: it represents the motion of a point whose successive displacements are [random](wikipedia://en/25685), in which displacements in pairwise disjoint time intervals are independent, and displacements in different time intervals of the same length have identical probability distributions. A Lévy process may thus be viewed as the continuous-time analog of a [random walk](wikipedia://en/235451).

:::

The most well known examples of Lévy processes are the [Wiener process](wikipedia://en/149984), often called the [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436) process, and the [Poisson process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_process). Further important examples include the [Gamma process](wikipedia://en/4183150), the Pascal process, and the Meixner process. Aside from Brownian motion with drift, all other proper (that is, not deterministic) Lévy processes have [discontinuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discontinuous) paths. All Lévy processes are [additive processes](wikipedia://en/64384314).[^o2]

## Mathematical definition {#Mathematical_definition}

A Lévy process is a [stochastic process](wikipedia://en/47895) $X=\{X_t:t \geq 0\}$ that satisfies the following properties:

1. $X_0=0 \,$ [almost surely](wikipedia://en/351908);
1. **[Independence of increments](wikipedia://en/877315):** For any $0 \leq t_1 < t_2<\cdots <t_n <\infty$, $X_{t_2}-X_{t_1}, X_{t_3}-X_{t_2},\dots,X_{t_n}-X_{t_{n-1}}$ are mutually [independent](wikipedia://en/27593);
1. **[Stationary increments](wikipedia://en/22497977):** For any $s<t \,$, $X_t-X_s \,$ is equal in distribution to $X_{t-s}; \,$
1. **[Continuity in probability](wikipedia://en/70217363):** For any $\varepsilon>0$ and $t\ge 0$ it holds that $\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} P(|X_{t+h}-X_t|>\varepsilon)=0.$

If $X$ is a Lévy process then one may construct a [version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Version_%28probability_theory%29) of $X$ such that $t \mapsto X_t$ is [almost surely](wikipedia://en/351908) [right-continuous with left limits](wikipedia://en/30862839).

## Properties {#Properties}

### Independent increments {#Independent_increments}

A continuous-time stochastic process assigns a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) *X*<sub>*t*</sub> to each point *t* ≥ 0 in time. In effect it is a random function of *t*. The **increments** of such a process are the differences *X*<sub>*s*</sub> − *X*<sub>*t*</sub> between its values at different times *t* \< *s*. To call the increments of a process **independent** means that increments *X*<sub>*s*</sub> − *X*<sub>*t*</sub> and *X*<sub>*u*</sub> − *X*<sub>*v*</sub> are [independent](wikipedia://en/27593) random variables whenever the two time intervals do not overlap and, more generally, any finite number of increments assigned to pairwise non-overlapping time intervals are mutually (not just [pairwise](wikipedia://en/804155)) independent.

### Stationary increments {#Stationary_increments}

To call the increments stationary means that the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of any increment *X*<sub>*t*</sub> − *X*<sub>*s*</sub> depends only on the length *t* − *s* of the time interval; increments on equally long time intervals are identically distributed.

If $X$ is a [Wiener process](wikipedia://en/149984), the probability distribution of *X*<sub>*t*</sub> − *X*<sub>*s*</sub> is [normal](wikipedia://en/21462) with [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) 0 and [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) *t* − *s*.

If $X$ is a [Poisson process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_process), the probability distribution of *X*<sub>*t*</sub> − *X*<sub>*s*</sub> is a [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) with expected value λ(*t* − *s*), where λ > 0 is the "intensity" or "rate" of the process.

If $X$ is a [Cauchy process](wikipedia://en/38454328), the probability distribution of *X*<sub>*t*</sub> − *X*<sub>*s*</sub> is a [Cauchy distribution](wikipedia://en/7003) with density $f(x; t) = { 1 \over \pi } \left[ { \gamma \over x^2 + \gamma^2 } \right]$ where $\gamma=t-s$.

### Infinite divisibility {#Infinite_divisibility}

The distribution of a Lévy process has the property of [infinite divisibility](wikipedia://en/13588803): given any integer *n*, the [law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_%28stochastic_processes%29) of a Lévy process at time t can be represented as the law of the sum of *n* independent random variables, which are precisely the increments of the Lévy process over time intervals of length *t*/*n,* which are independent and identically distributed by assumptions 2 and 3. Conversely, for each infinitely divisible probability distribution $F$, there is a Lévy process $X$ such that the law of $X_1$ is given by $F$.

### Moments {#Moments}

In any Lévy process with finite [moments](wikipedia://en/368684), the *n*th moment $\mu_n(t) = E(X_t^n)$, is a [polynomial function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_function) of *t*; [these functions satisfy a binomial identity](wikipedia://en/200077):

$$\mu_n(t+s)=\sum_{k=0}^n {n \choose k} \mu_k(t) \mu_{n-k}(s).$$

## Lévy–Khintchine representation {#Lévy–Khintchine_representation}

The distribution of a Lévy process is characterized by its [characteristic function](wikipedia://en/3229401), which is given by the **Lévy–Khintchine formula** (general for all [infinitely divisible distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitely_divisible_distribution)):[^o12]

> If $X = (X_t)_{t\geq 0}$ is a Lévy process, then its characteristic function $\varphi_X(\theta)$ is given by
>
> $\varphi_X(\theta)(t) := \mathbb{E}\left[e^{i\theta X(t)}\right] = \exp{\left(t\left(ai\theta - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\theta^2 + \int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}{\left(e^{i\theta x}-1 -i\theta x\mathbf{1}_{|x|<1}\right)\,\Pi(dx)}\right)\right)}$ where $a \in \mathbb{R}$, $\sigma\ge 0$, and $\Pi$ is a $\sigma$-finite measure called the **Lévy measure** of $X$, satisfying the property
>
> $$\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}{\min(1,x^2)\,\Pi(dx)} < \infty.$$

In the above, $\mathbf{1}$ is the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790). Because [characteristic functions](wikipedia://en/3229401) uniquely determine their underlying probability distributions, each Lévy process is uniquely determined by the "Lévy–Khintchine triplet" $(a,\sigma^2, \Pi)$. The terms of this triplet suggest that a Lévy process can be seen as having three independent components: a linear drift, a [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/149984), and a Lévy jump process, as described below. This immediately gives that the only (nondeterministic) continuous Lévy process is a Brownian motion with drift; similarly, every Lévy process is a [semimartingale](wikipedia://en/9653775).[^o13]

::: {#o15 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Lévy-Itō decomposition"}

### Lévy–Itô decomposition {#Lévy–Itô_decomposition}

Because the characteristic functions of independent random variables multiply, the Lévy–Khintchine theorem suggests that every Lévy process is the sum of Brownian motion with drift and another independent random variable, a Lévy jump process. The Lévy–Itô decomposition describes the latter as a (stochastic) sum of independent Poisson random variables.

Let $\nu=\frac{\Pi|_{\mathbb{R}\setminus(-1,1)}}{\Pi(\mathbb{R}\setminus(-1,1))}$— that is, the restriction of $\Pi$ to $\mathbb{R}\setminus(-1,1)$, normalized to be a probability measure; similarly, let $\mu=\Pi|_{(-1,1)\setminus\{0\}}$ (but do not rescale). Then

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}{\left(e^{i\theta x}-1 -i\theta x\mathbf{1}_{|x|<1}\right)\,\Pi(dx)}=\Pi(\mathbb{R}\setminus(-1,1))\int_{\mathbb{R}}{(e^{i\theta x}-1)\,\nu(dx)}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}{(e^{i\theta x}-1-i\theta x)\,\mu(dx)}.$$

The former is the characteristic function of a [compound Poisson process](wikipedia://en/1082294) with intensity $\Pi(\mathbb{R}\setminus(-1,1))$ and child distribution $\nu$. The latter is that of a [compensated generalized Poisson process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compensated_generalized_Poisson_process) (CGPP): a process with countably many jump discontinuities on every interval [a.s.](wikipedia://en/351908), but such that those discontinuities are of magnitude less than $1$. If $\int_{\mathbb{R}}{|x|\,\mu(dx)}<\infty$, then the CGPP is a [pure jump process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_jump_model).[^o16][^o17] Therefore, in terms of processes one may decompose $X$ in the following way

$$X_t=\sigma B_t + at+Y_t+Z_t, t\geq 0,$$

where $Y$ is the compound Poisson process with jumps larger than $1$ in absolute value and $Z_t$ is the aforementioned compensated generalized Poisson process which is also a zero-mean martingale.

## Generalization {#Generalization}

A Lévy [random field](wikipedia://en/540838) is a multi-dimensional generalization of Lévy process.[^o20][^o21] Still more general are decomposable processes.[^o22]
