# Primitive polynomial (field theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Primitive polynomial"}

In [field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_theory_%28mathematics%29), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **primitive polynomial** is the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106) of a [primitive element](wikipedia://en/19127149) of the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) $\operatorname{GF}(p^{m})$. This means that a polynomial $F(X)$ of degree $m$ with coefficients in $\operatorname{GF}(p) = \mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z}$ is a *primitive polynomial* if it is [monic](wikipedia://en/314730) and has a root $\alpha$ in $\operatorname{GF}(p^{m})$ such that $\{0,1,\alpha, \alpha^2,\alpha^3, \ldots \alpha^{p^m-2}\}$ is the entire field $\operatorname{GF}(p^{m})$. This implies that $\alpha$ is a [primitive ($p^{m} - 1$)-root of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_root_of_unity) in $\operatorname{GF}(p^{m})$.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- Because all minimal polynomials are [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725), all primitive polynomials are also irreducible.
- A primitive polynomial must have a non-zero constant term, for otherwise it will be divisible by *x*. Over [GF(2)](wikipedia://en/11137263), *x* + 1 is a primitive polynomial and all other primitive polynomials have an odd number of terms, since any polynomial mod 2 with an even number of terms is divisible by *x* + 1 (it has 1 as a root).
- An [irreducible polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) *F*(*x*) of degree *m* over GF(*p*), where *p* is prime, is a primitive polynomial if the smallest positive integer *n* such that *F*(*x*) divides *x*<sup>*n*</sup> − 1 is *n* = *p*<sup>*m*</sup> − 1.
- A primitive polynomial of degree $m$ has $m$ different roots in $\operatorname{GF}(p^{m})$, which all have [order](wikipedia://en/493527) $p^{m} - 1$, meaning that any of them generates the [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/11615#Multiplicative_structure) of the field.
- Over GF(*p*) there are exactly $\varphi(p^{m} - 1)$ primitive elements and $\varphi(p^{m} - 1) / m$ primitive polynomials, each of degree $m$, where $\varphi$ is [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452).[^o4]
- The [algebraic conjugates](wikipedia://en/458467) of a primitive element $\alpha$ in $\operatorname{GF}(p^{m})$ are $\alpha$, $\alpha^{p}$, $\alpha^{p^{2}}$, …, $\alpha^{p^{m-1}}$ and so the primitive polynomial $F(x)$ has explicit form $F(x) = (x - \alpha) (x - \alpha^{p}) (x - \alpha^{p^{2}}) \ldots (x - \alpha^{p^{m-1}})$. That the coefficients of a polynomial of this form, for any $\alpha$ in $\operatorname{GF}(p^{n})$, not necessarily primitive, lie in $\operatorname{GF}(p)$ follows from the property that the polynomial is invariant under application of the [Frobenius automorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_automorphism) to its coefficients (using $\alpha^{p^{n}} = \alpha$) and from the fact that the fixed field of the Frobenius automorphism is $\operatorname{GF}(p)$.

:::

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Over $\operatorname{GF}(3)$ the polynomial $x^{2} + 1$ is irreducible but not primitive because it divides $x^{4} - 1$: its roots generate a cyclic group of order 4, while the multiplicative group of $\operatorname{GF}(3^{2})$ is a cyclic group of order 8. The polynomial $x^{2} + 2x + 2$, on the other hand, is primitive. Denote one of its roots by $\alpha$. Then, because the natural numbers less than and relatively prime to $3^{2} - 1 = 8$ are 1, 3, 5, and 7, the four primitive roots in $\operatorname{GF}(3^{2})$ are $\alpha$, $\alpha^{3} = 2\alpha + 1$, $\alpha^{5} = 2\alpha$, and $\alpha^{7} = \alpha + 2$. The primitive roots $\alpha$ and $\alpha^{3}$ are algebraically conjugate. Indeed $x^{2} + 2x + 2 = (x - \alpha) (x - (2\alpha + 1))$. The remaining primitive roots $\alpha^{5}$ and $\alpha^{7} = (\alpha^{5})^{3}$ are also algebraically conjugate and produce the second primitive polynomial: $x^{2} + x + 2 = (x - 2\alpha) (x - (\alpha + 2))$.

For degree 3, $\operatorname{GF}(3^{3})$ has $\varphi(3^{3} - 1) = \varphi(26) = 12$ primitive elements. As each primitive polynomial of degree 3 has three roots, all necessarily primitive, there are $12 / 3 = 4$ primitive polynomials of degree 3. One primitive polynomial is $x^{3} + 2x + 1$. Denoting one of its roots by $\gamma$, the algebraically conjugate elements are $\gamma^{3}$ and $\gamma^{9}$. The other primitive polynomials are associated with algebraically conjugate sets built on other primitive elements $\gamma^{r}$ with $r$ relatively prime to 26:

$$\begin{aligned}x^3+2x+1 & = (x-\gamma)(x-\gamma^3)(x-\gamma^9)\\ x^3+2x^2+x+1 &= (x-\gamma^5)(x-\gamma^{5\cdot3})(x-\gamma^{5\cdot9}) = (x-\gamma^5)(x-\gamma^{15})(x-\gamma^{19})\\ x^3+x^2+2x+1 &= (x-\gamma^7)(x-\gamma^{7\cdot3})(x-\gamma^{7\cdot9}) = (x-\gamma^7)(x-\gamma^{21})(x-\gamma^{11})\\ x^3+2x^2+1 &= (x-\gamma^{17})(x-\gamma^{17\cdot3})(x-\gamma^{17\cdot9}) = (x-\gamma^{17})(x-\gamma^{25})(x-\gamma^{23}). \end{aligned}$$

## Applications {#Applications}

### Field element representation {#Field_element_representation}

Primitive polynomials can be used to represent the elements of a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615). If *α* in GF(*p*<sup>*m*</sup>) is a root of a primitive polynomial *F*(*x*), then the nonzero elements of GF(*p*<sup>*m*</sup>) are represented as successive powers of *α*:

$$\mathrm{GF}(p^m) = \{ 0, 1= \alpha^0, \alpha, \alpha^2, \ldots, \alpha^{p^m-2} \} .$$

This allows an economical representation in a [computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer) of the nonzero elements of the finite field, by representing an element by the corresponding exponent of $\alpha.$ This representation makes multiplication easy, as it corresponds to addition of exponents [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) $p^m-1.$

### Pseudo-random bit generation {#Pseudo-random_bit_generation}

Primitive polynomials over GF(2), the field with two elements, can be used for [pseudorandom bit generation](wikipedia://en/45524). In fact, every [linear-feedback shift register](wikipedia://en/58992) with maximum cycle length (which is 2<sup>*n*</sup> − 1, where *n* is the length of the linear-feedback shift register) may be built from a primitive polynomial.[^o11]

In general, for a primitive polynomial of degree *m* over GF(2), this process will generate 2<sup>*m*</sup> − 1 pseudo-random bits before repeating the same sequence.

### CRC codes {#CRC_codes}

The [cyclic redundancy check](wikipedia://en/38838) (CRC) is an error-detection code that operates by interpreting the message bitstring as the coefficients of a polynomial over GF(2) and dividing it by a fixed generator polynomial also over GF(2); see [Mathematics of CRC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_of_CRC). Primitive polynomials, or multiples of them, are sometimes a good choice for generator polynomials because they can reliably detect two bit errors that occur far apart in the message bitstring, up to a distance of 2<sup>*n*</sup> − 1 for a degree *n* primitive polynomial.

:::

## Primitive trinomials {#Primitive_trinomials}

A useful class of primitive polynomials is the primitive trinomials, those having only three nonzero terms: *x<sup>r</sup>* + *x<sup>k</sup>* + 1. Their simplicity makes for particularly small and fast [linear-feedback shift registers](wikipedia://en/58992).[^o14] A number of results give techniques for locating and testing primitiveness of trinomials.[^o15]

For polynomials over GF(2), where 2<sup>*r*</sup> − 1 is a [Mersenne prime](wikipedia://en/18908), a polynomial of degree *r* is primitive if and only if it is irreducible. (Given an irreducible polynomial, it is *not* primitive only if the period of *x* is a non-trivial factor of 2<sup>*r*</sup> − 1. Primes have no non-trivial factors.) Although the [Mersenne Twister](wikipedia://en/45538) pseudo-random number generator does not use a trinomial, it does take advantage of this.

[Richard Brent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Brent_%28scientist%29) has been tabulating primitive trinomials of this form, such as *x*<sup>74207281</sup> + *x*<sup>30684570</sup> + 1.[^o16][^o17] This can be used to create a pseudo-random number generator of the huge period 2<sup>74207281</sup> − 1 ≈ 3 × 10<sup>22338617</sup>.
