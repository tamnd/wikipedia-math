# Lie–Kolchin theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lie–Kolchin theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Lie–Kolchin theorem** is a theorem in the [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200) of [linear algebraic groups](wikipedia://en/395885); **[Lie's theorem](wikipedia://en/2975251)** is the analog for [linear Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/39331795).

:::

It states that if *G* is a [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) and [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012) linear algebraic group defined over an [algebraically closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_closed) [field](wikipedia://en/10603) and

$$\rho\colon G \to GL(V)$$

a [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) on a nonzero [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V*, then there is a 1-dimensional [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) *L* of *V* such that

$$\rho(G)(L) = L.$$

That is, ρ(*G*) has an invariant line *L*, on which *G* therefore acts through a 1-dimensional representation. This is equivalent to the statement that *V* contains a nonzero vector *v* that is a common (simultaneous) [eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) for all $\rho(g), \,\, g \in G$.

It follows directly that every [irreducible](wikipedia://en/385982) finite-dimensional representation of a connected and solvable linear algebraic group *G* has dimension 1. In fact, this is another way to state the Lie–Kolchin theorem.

The result for Lie algebras was proved by (txt) and for algebraic groups was proved by (txt, p.19).

The [Borel fixed point theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_fixed_point_theorem) generalizes the Lie–Kolchin theorem.

## Triangularization {#Triangularization}

Sometimes the theorem is also referred to as the *Lie–Kolchin triangularization theorem* because by induction it implies that with respect to a suitable [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) of *V* the image $\rho(G)$ has a *triangular shape*; in other words, the image group $\rho(G)$ is conjugate in GL(*n*,*K*) (where *n* = dim *V*) to a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of the group T of [upper triangular matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_triangular_matrices), the standard [Borel subgroup](wikipedia://en/588356) of GL(*n*,*K*): the image is [simultaneously triangularizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simultaneously_triangularizable).

The theorem applies in particular to a [Borel subgroup](wikipedia://en/588356) of a [semisimple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_algebraic_group) [linear algebraic group](wikipedia://en/395885) *G*.

## Counter-example {#Counter-example}

If the field *K* is not algebraically closed, the theorem can fail. The standard [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071), viewed as the set of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\{ x+iy \in \mathbb{C} \mid x^2+y^2=1 \}$ of absolute value one is a 1-dimensional [commutative](wikipedia://en/2974) (and therefore solvable) [linear algebraic group](wikipedia://en/395885) over the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) which has a 2-dimensional representation into the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) SO(2) without an invariant (real) line. Here the image $\rho(z)$ of $z=x+iy$ is the [orthogonal matrix](wikipedia://en/105620)

$$\begin{pmatrix} x & y \\ -y & x \end{pmatrix}.$$
