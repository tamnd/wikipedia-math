# Infinity Laplacian

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Infinity Laplacian"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **infinity Laplace** (or $L^\infty$-Laplace) operator is a 2nd-order [partial differential operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_operator), commonly abbreviated $\Delta_\infty$. It is alternately defined, for a function $u:\mathbb{R}^n\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}$ of the variables $x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, by

:::

$$\Delta_\infty u = \langle Du, D^2 u \, Du \rangle = \sum_{i,j=1}^n \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x_i \, \partial x_j} \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_i} \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_j}$$

or

$$\Delta_\infty u = \frac{\langle Du, D^2 u \, Du \rangle}{|Du|^2} = \frac{1}{|Du|^2} \sum_{i,j=1}^n \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x_i \, \partial x_j} \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_i} \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_j},$$

where $Du$ denotes the [gradient vector](wikipedia://en/12461), $D^2u$ denotes the [Hessian matrix](wikipedia://en/412108), and $\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle$ denotes the [Euclidean inner product](wikipedia://en/157093). The first version avoids the singularity which occurs when the gradient vanishes, while the second version is homogeneous of order zero in the gradient. Verbally, the second version is the **second derivative in the direction of the gradient**. In the case of the infinity Laplace equation $\Delta_\infty u = 0$, the two definitions are equivalent.

While the equation involves second derivatives, usually (generalized) solutions are not twice differentiable, as evidenced by the well-known Aronsson solution $u(x,y) = |x|^{4/3} - |y|^{4/3}$. For this reason the correct notion of solutions is that given by the [viscosity solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscosity_solutions).

Viscosity solutions to the equation $\Delta_\infty u = 0$ are also known as **infinity harmonic functions**. This terminology arises from the fact that the infinity [Laplace operator](wikipedia://en/174706) first arose in the study of absolute minimizers for $\| Du \|_{L^\infty}$, and it can be viewed in a certain sense as the limit of the [p-Laplacian](wikipedia://en/18518396) as $p \rightarrow \infty$. More recently, viscosity solutions to the infinity Laplace equation have been identified with the payoff functions from **randomized tug-of-war** [games](wikipedia://en/17507367). The game theory point of view has significantly improved the understanding of the [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564) itself.

## Discrete version and game theory {#Discrete_version_and_game_theory}

A defining property of the usual $L^2$-[harmonic functions](wikipedia://en/55762) is the [mean value property](wikipedia://en/55762#The_mean_value_property). That has a natural and important discrete version: a real-valued function $f$ on a finite or infinite [graph](wikipedia://en/12401) $G(V,E)$ is **discrete harmonic** on a subset $U\subseteq V$ if

$$f(x)=\frac{1}{\deg(x)}\sum_{y\sim x} f(y)$$

for all $x\in U$. Similarly, the vanishing second derivative in the direction of the gradient has a natural discrete version:

$$f(x)=\frac{\sup \{f(y): y\sim x\} + \inf \{f(y): y\sim x\}}{2}.$$

In this equation, we used sup and inf instead of max and min because the graph $G(V,E)$ does not have to be locally finite (i.e., to have finite degrees): a key example is when $V(G)$ is the set of points in a domain in $\mathbb{R}^d$, and $(x,y)\in E(G)$ if their [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) is at most $\epsilon$. The importance of this example lies in the following.

Consider a bounded open set $D\subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ with smooth boundary $\partial D$, and a continuous function $f:\partial D\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}$. In the $L^2$-case, an approximation of the harmonic extension of *f* to *D* is given by taking a lattice $\mathbb{Z}_\epsilon^d$ with small mesh size $\epsilon$, letting $G_\epsilon(V,E)=D\cap\mathbb{Z}_\epsilon^d$ and $\partial V\subset V$ be the set of vertices with degree less than *2d*, taking a natural approximation $f_\epsilon:\partial V\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}$, and then taking the unique discrete harmonic extension of $f_\epsilon$ to *V*. However, it is easy to see by examples that this does not work for the $L^\infty$-case. Instead, as it turns out, one should take the *continuum graph* with all edges of length at most $\epsilon$, mentioned above.

Now, a **probabilistic way** of looking at the $L^2$-harmonic extension of $f_\epsilon$ from $\partial V$ to $V$ is that

$$f_\epsilon(x)=\mathbb{E}\big[f_\epsilon(X_\tau)\,|\, X_0=x\big],$$

where $X_0,X_1,\dots$ is the [simple random walk](wikipedia://en/235451#On_graphs) on $G_\epsilon(V,E)$ started at $X_0=x$, and $\tau$ is the [hitting time](wikipedia://en/7059985) of $\partial V$.

For the $L^\infty$-case, we need [game theory](wikipedia://en/11924). A token is started at location $x\in V(G)$, and $f:\partial V\longrightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is given. There are two players, in each turn they flip a [fair coin](wikipedia://en/9597100), and the winner can move the token to any neighbour of the current location. The game ends when the token reaches $\partial V$ at some time $\tau$ and location $X_\tau$, at which point the first player gets the amount $f(X_\tau)$ from the second player. Therefore, the first player wants to maximize $f(X_\tau)$, while the second player wants to minimize it. If both players play optimally (which has a well-defined meaning in game theory), the expected payoff $\mathbb{E}[f(X_\tau)\,|\, X_0=x]$ to the first player is a discrete infinity harmonic function, as defined above.

There is a game theory approach to the [p-Laplacian](wikipedia://en/18518396), too, interpolating between simple random walk and the above random tug-of-war game.
