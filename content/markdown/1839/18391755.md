# Standard model (set theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Standard model"}

In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), a **standard model** for a [theory](wikipedia://en/3512103) $T$ (in the language of set theory) is a [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_%28mathematical_logic%29) $M$ for $T$ where the membership relation $\in_{M}$ is the same as the membership relation $\in$ of a set theoretical [universe](wikipedia://en/296838) $V$ (restricted to the domain of $M$). In other words, $M$ is a [substructure](wikipedia://en/2057747) of $V$. A standard model $M$ that satisfies the additional [transitivity](wikipedia://en/2462396) condition that $x \in y \in M$ implies $x \in M$ is a **standard transitive model** (or simply a **transitive model**).

:::

Often, when one talks about a model $M$ of set theory, it is assumed that $M$ is a **set model**, i.e. the domain of $M$ is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) in $V$. If the domain of $M$ is a [proper class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_class), then $M$ is a **class model**. An [inner model](wikipedia://en/1118686) is necessarily a class model, because inner models are required to contain all the [ordinals](wikipedia://en/26547932) of $V$.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

It is difficult to exhibit an explicit set model of ZFC, because the very existence of a set model implies the [consistency](wikipedia://en/75802) of ZFC, which is unprovable within ZFC. However, the universe $V$ itself, when equipped with the ordinary set membership relation $\in$, is an intuitive example of a class model that is standard transitive.[^n1]

To better illustrate the concepts of "standard" and "transitive", we compare the model $(V, \in)$ with other models [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to it. An arbitrary isomorphism such as $f(x) = \{x, \emptyset\}$ will usually yield a non-standard class model, since $x \in y$ does not imply $\{x, \emptyset\} \in \{y, \emptyset\}$ in general. To construct a class model that is standard but not transitive, consider a function $f$ defined by [$\in$-recursion](wikipedia://en/2476462) as $f(y) = \{f(x) | x \in y\} \cup \{\emptyset\}$ (essentially, we add $\emptyset$ to every set and to its elements recursively). Denote the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of $f$ as $M$. Since $\emptyset$ itself is not in $M$, we have $x \in y$ iff $f(x) \in f(y)$, and thus $(M, \in)$ is indeed a standard model, but it is not transitive because $\emptyset \in f(\emptyset) \in M$ but $\emptyset$ is not in $M$.[^n2] Essentially, non-standard models have a membership relation different from the universe, and standard non-transitive models have elements with "superfluous" members.

:::

## Absoluteness {#Absoluteness}

A standard transitive model $M$ will in many aspects behave "exactly like $V$". For example, the element that satisfies the [axiom of empty set](wikipedia://en/52534) in $M$ will be $\emptyset$, the empty set of $V$. Similarly, all sets that can be built up from the empty set, the [axiom of pairing](wikipedia://en/52385) and the [axiom of union](wikipedia://en/52553) (i.e., all [hereditarily finite sets](wikipedia://en/332621)) are all the same as their counterparts in $V$. In other words, sentences such as "$x$ is the empty set" or "$x = \{\emptyset, \{\emptyset\}, \{\emptyset, \{\emptyset\}\}$ (the [von Neumann ordinal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Neumann_ordinal) 3)" have the truth value for the same $x$, whether they are interpreted in $V$ or any standard transitive model $M$. Such sentences are known as [absolute](wikipedia://en/12374236) for standard transitive models.

An example of a sentence that is not absolute for standard transitive models is "$y$ is the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of $x$", which by definition means "for all $z$, $z \in y$ if and only if $z \subseteq x$", or more formally:

$$\forall z (z \in y \leftrightarrow \forall w \in z (w \in x)).$$

The qualifier $\forall w \in z$ means the same thing whether interpreted in $V$ or $M$: As long as $z \in M$, transitivity ensures that all elements of $z$ must also be in $M$. Therefore, the right hand side of the [biconditional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biconditional) does mean "$z$ is a subset of $x$". However, the qualifier $\forall z$ means $\forall z \in V$ when interpreted in $V$, but $\forall z \in M$ when interpreted in $M$. In the latter case, only subsets of $x$ that is in $M$ is required to be in $y$ (in fact, only those subsets could be in $y$ due to transitivity). Since an arbitrary subset of $x$ is not necessarily in $M$ (the [axiom of separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_of_separation) only works for [definable sets](wikipedia://en/5569486)), the $y$ that satisfies this sentence in $M$ may be a proper subset of the power set of $x$ in $V$ (i.e., the $y$ that satisfies this sentence in $V$).[^n3]

In general, a sentence is absolute as long as it is equivalent to a formula with only [bounded quantifiers](wikipedia://en/5824808) like $\forall w \in z$.[^n4] For example, assuming the [axiom of regularity](wikipedia://en/2113):

- "$x$ is an [ordinal number](wikipedia://en/26547932)" is equivalent to "$x$ is a [transitive set](wikipedia://en/2462396) strictly totally ordered by set membership" and thus is absolute.
- "$x = \omega$ (the first infinite ordinal)" is equivalent to "$x$ is an [inductive set](wikipedia://en/246449) containing only $0$ and $S(y)$ for $y \in x$" and thus is absolute.

On the other hand:

- "$x$ and $y$ are [equinumerous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinumerous)" is not absolute, even though "$z$ is a bijection between $x$ and $y$" is absolute — the existence of $z$ is exactly the unbounded qualifier that allows the sentence to be not absolute, as it may be the case that $z$ exists in $V$, but is not in $M$. In other words, two sets can be equinumerous in $V$, but not in $M$.
- "$x = \omega_{1}$ (the first [uncountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountable) ordinal)" is not absolute, because countability is based on equinumerosity and thus is not absolute. There may exist ordinals that are countable in $V$ but uncountable in $M$, and the first such ordinal will "play the role of $\omega_{1}$" in $M$.

In fact, the downward [Löwenheim–Skolem theorem](wikipedia://en/341482) together with [Mostowski collapse lemma](wikipedia://en/1251604) can convert any standard (set) model of [ZFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZFC) into a standard transitive model $M$ that is itself countable. Every set in $M$ must be countable in $V$, but at the same time there must exist sets in $M$ that are uncountable in $M$, such as the sets playing the role of $\omega_{1}$ or $\text{𝔓}(\omega)$ (the power set of $\omega$). This does not lead to any contradiction because countability is not absolute.[^n3]
