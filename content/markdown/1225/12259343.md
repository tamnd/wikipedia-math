# Dialling (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dialling"}

In somewhat archaic applied mathematics, **dialling** is the mathematics required to create a [sundial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundial) face to determine [solar time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_time) based on the position of the sun. Those skilled in the art were referred to as dialists or gnomonists, the latter derived from the word [gnomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnomon), which was a device that used a shadow as an indicator.

:::

The mathematician [William Oughtred](wikipedia://en/475249) published a book, *Easy Method of Mathematical Dialling*, around 1600.[^o2] Samuel Walker (1716–1782) was a Yorkshire mathematician and diallist.[^o3] In his later years, [Thomas Jefferson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson) was known to practice dialling as a mental exercise.[^o4] Professor of [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) at [Gresham College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gresham_College) (London, UK), [Samuel Foster](wikipedia://en/23443186) (d. 1652), developed reflex dialling, which describes a device of his own invention: a sundial capable of reflecting a spot of light onto the ceiling of a room.[^o5]

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The word *dial* derives from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) term *dialis* (*daily*), and comes from the fact that a [sundial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundial) throws a [shadow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow) related to the time of day. It was also used to describe the [gear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gear) in a medieval [clock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clock) which turned once per day.
