# Timeline of numerals and arithmetic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Timeline of numerals and arithmetic"}

A [timeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline) of **numerals** and **[arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118)**.

:::

## Before 2000 BC {#Before_2000_BC}

- c. [20,000 BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Paleolithic) — [Nile Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile_Valley), [Ishango Bone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishango_Bone): suggested, though disputed, as the earliest reference to [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) as also a common number.[^o3]
- c. 3400 BC — the [Sumerians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer) invent the first so-known [numeral system](wikipedia://en/21170), and a system of [weights and measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Mesopotamian_units_of_measurement).
- c. 3100 BC — [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt), earliest known [decimal system](wikipedia://en/8214) allows indefinite counting by way of introducing new symbols, <http://www.math.buffalo.edu/mad/Ancient-Africa/mad_ancient_egyptpapyrus.html#berlin>.
- c. 2800 BC — [Indus Valley civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_civilization) on the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent), earliest use of [decimal ratios](wikipedia://en/8214) in a uniform system of [ancient weights and measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Indus_Valley_units_of_measurement), the smallest unit of measurement used is 1.704 millimetres and the smallest unit of mass used is 28 grams.
- c. 2000 BC — [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia), the [Babylonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonians) use a base-60 decimal system, and compute the first known approximate value of [π](wikipedia://en/23601) at 3.125.

## 1st millennium BC {#1st_millennium_BC}

- c. 1000 BC — [Vulgar fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulgar_fraction) used by the [Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptians).
- second half of 1st millennium BC — The [Lo Shu Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lo_Shu_Square), the unique normal [magic square](wikipedia://en/52202) of order three, was discovered in [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China).
- c. 400 BC — [Jaina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain) mathematicians in [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_India) write the “Surya Prajinapti”, a mathematical text which classifies all numbers into three sets: enumerable, innumerable and [infinite](wikipedia://en/20110824). It also recognises five different types of [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824): infinite in one and two directions, infinite in area, infinite everywhere, and infinite perpetually.
- c. 300 BC — [Brahmi numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmi_numeral) are conceived in [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_India).
- 300 BC — [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia), the [Babylonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonians) invent the earliest calculator, the [abacus](wikipedia://en/655).[^o5]
- c. 300 BC — [Indian mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_mathematicians) [Pingala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pingala) writes the “Chhandah-shastra”, which contains the first Indian use of [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29) as a digit (indicated by a dot) and also presents a description of a [binary numeral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_numeral_system), along with the first use of [Fibonacci numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_numbers) and [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497).
- c. 250 BC — late [Olmecs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olmec) had already begun to use a true [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29) (a shell glyph) several centuries before [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979) in the New World. See [0 (number)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29).
- 150 BC — [Jain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism) mathematicians in [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_India) write the “Sthananga Sutra”, which contains work on the theory of numbers, arithmetical operations, [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), operations with [fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractions), simple equations, [cubic equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_equations), quartic equations, and [permutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutations) and [combinations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinations).
- 50 BC — [Indian numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_numerals), the first [positional notation](wikipedia://en/437052) [base-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base-10) [numeral system](wikipedia://en/21170), begins developing in [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_India).

## 1st millennium AD {#1st_millennium_AD}

- 300 — the earliest known use of [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29) as a decimal digit in the Old World is introduced by [Indian mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_mathematicians).
- c. 400 — the [Bakhshali manuscript](wikipedia://en/4305817) uses numerals with a place-value system, using a dot as a place holder for zero .
- 550 — [Hindu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu) mathematicians give [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29) a numeral representation in the [positional notation](wikipedia://en/437052) [Indian numeral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_numerals) system.
- 628 — [Brahmagupta](wikipedia://en/297206) writes the *[Brahma-sphuta-siddhanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmasphutasiddhanta)*, where zero is clearly explained, and where the modern [place-value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place-value) [Indian numeral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_numerals) system is fully developed. It also gives rules for manipulating both [negative and positive numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_and_positive_numbers), methods for computing [square roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_roots), methods of solving [linear](wikipedia://en/17570) and [quadratic equations](wikipedia://en/25175), and rules for summing [series](wikipedia://en/15287), [Brahmagupta's identity](wikipedia://en/5161105), and the [Brahmagupta theorem](wikipedia://en/1884241).
- 940 — [Abu'l-Wafa al-Buzjani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu'l-Wafa_al-Buzjani) extracts [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) using the Indian numeral system.
- 953 — The [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118) of the [Hindu–Arabic numeral system](wikipedia://en/3393371) at first required the use of a dust board (a sort of handheld [blackboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackboard)) because “the methods required moving the numbers around in the calculation and rubbing some out as the calculation proceeded.” [Al-Uqlidisi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Uqlidisi) modified these methods for [pen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pen) and [paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper) use. Eventually the advances enabled by the [decimal system](wikipedia://en/8214) led to its standard use throughout the region and the world.

## 1000–1500 {#1000–1500}

- c. 1000 — [Pope Sylvester II](wikipedia://en/24614) introduces the [abacus](wikipedia://en/655) using the [Hindu–Arabic numeral system](wikipedia://en/3393371) to Europe.
- 1030 — [Ali Ahmad Nasawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Ahmad_Nasawi) writes a treatise on the [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214) and [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) number systems. His arithmetic explains the division of fractions and the extraction of square and cubic roots (square root of 57,342; cubic root of 3, 652, 296) in an almost modern manner.
- 12th century — [Indian numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_numerals) have been modified by [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_people) mathematicians [al-Khwārizmī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khwārizmī) to form the modern [Arabic numerals](wikipedia://en/1786) (used universally in the modern world.)
- 12th century — the [Arabic numerals](wikipedia://en/1786) reach [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe) through the [Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabs).
- 1202 — [Leonardo Fibonacci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_of_Pisa) demonstrates the utility of [Hindu–Arabic numeral system](wikipedia://en/3393371) in his *Book of the Abacus*.
- c. 1400 — [Ghiyath al-Kashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghiyath_al-Kashi) “contributed to the development of [decimal fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_fraction) not only for approximating [algebraic numbers](wikipedia://en/1158), but also for [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) such as [pi](wikipedia://en/23601). His contribution to decimal fractions is so major that for many years he was considered as their inventor. Although not the first to do so, al-Kashi gave an algorithm for calculating [nth roots](wikipedia://en/235029) which is a special case of the methods given many centuries later by [Ruffini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paolo_Ruffini_%28mathematician%29) and [Horner](wikipedia://en/998647).” He is also the first to use the [decimal point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_point) notation in [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118) and [Arabic numerals](wikipedia://en/1786). His works include *The Key of arithmetics, Discoveries in mathematics, The Decimal point*, and *The benefits of the zero*. The contents of the *Benefits of the Zero* are an introduction followed by five essays: “On whole number arithmetic”, “On fractional arithmetic”, “On astrology”, “On areas”, and “On finding the unknowns \[unknown variables\]”. He also wrote the *Thesis on the sine and the chord* and *Thesis on finding the first degree sine*.
- 15th century — [Ibn al-Banna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Banna) and [al-Qalasadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Qalasadi) introduced [symbolic notation](wikipedia://en/277184) for algebra and for mathematics in general.
- 1427 — [Al-Kashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kashi) completes *The Key to Arithmetic* containing work of great depth on [decimal fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_fraction). It applies arithmetical and algebraic methods to the solution of various problems, including several geometric ones.
- 1478 — An anonymous author writes the [Treviso Arithmetic](wikipedia://en/8272539).

## 17th century {#17th_century}

- 1614 — [John Napier](wikipedia://en/15993) publishes a table of Napierian [logarithms](wikipedia://en/17860) in *[Mirifici Logarithmorum Canonis Descriptio](wikipedia://en/26291704)*,
- 1617 — [Henry Briggs](wikipedia://en/210399) discusses decimal logarithms in *Logarithmorum Chilias Prima*,
- 1618 — [John Napier](wikipedia://en/15993) publishes the first references to [*e*](wikipedia://en/9633) in a work on [logarithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithms).

## 18th century {#18th_century}

- 1758 — *Arithmetika Horvatzka*, [Croatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croatia)'s first arithmetic textbook is published in [Zagreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zagreb) by [Mihalj Šilobod Bolšić](wikipedia://en/64106864) (1724–1787).
- 1794 — [Jurij Vega](wikipedia://en/40546) publishes *[Thesaurus Logarithmorum Completus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thesaurus_Logarithmorum_Completus)*.

## Calculation of Pi {#Calculation_of_Pi}

- 1706 — [John Machin](wikipedia://en/274756) develops a quickly converging inverse-tangent series for π and computes π to 100 decimal places.
- 1789 — [Jurij Vega](wikipedia://en/40546) improves Machin's formula and computes π to 140 decimal places.
- 1949 — John von Neumann computes π to 2,037 decimal places using [ENIAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ENIAC).
- 1961 — [Daniel Shanks](wikipedia://en/18171362) and [John Wrench](wikipedia://en/22533779) compute π to 100,000 decimal places using an inverse-tangent identity and an IBM-7090 computer.
- 1987 — [Yasumasa Kanada](wikipedia://en/561940), [David Bailey](wikipedia://en/184462), [Jonathan Borwein](wikipedia://en/1145010), and [Peter Borwein](wikipedia://en/1339736) use iterative modular equation approximations to elliptic integrals and a [NEC SX-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NEC_SX-2) [supercomputer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercomputer) to compute π to 134 million decimal places.
- 2002 — [Yasumasa Kanada](wikipedia://en/561940), Y. Ushiro, [Hisayasu Kuroda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hisayasu_Kuroda), [Makoto Kudoh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makoto_Kudoh) and a team of nine more compute π to 1241.1 billion digits using a [Hitachi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitachi,_Ltd.) 64-node [supercomputer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercomputer).
