# Metric circle

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Metric circle"}

In mathematics, a **metric circle** is the [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) of [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) on a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), or equivalently on any [rectifiable simple closed curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectifiable_curve) of bounded length. The metric spaces that can be embedded into metric circles can be characterized by a four-point [triangle equality](wikipedia://en/53941).

:::

Some authors have called metric circles **Riemannian circles**, especially in connection with the [filling area conjecture](wikipedia://en/12083818) in [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243), but this term has also been used for other concepts. A metric circle, defined in this way, is unrelated to and should be distinguished from a [metric ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_ball), the subset of a metric space within a given radius from a central point.

## Characterization of subspaces {#Characterization_of_subspaces}

A metric space is a subspace of a metric circle (or of an equivalently defined metric line, interpreted as a [degenerate](wikipedia://en/232952) case of a metric circle) if every four of its points can be permuted and labeled as $a,b,c,d$ so that they obey the equalities of distances $D(a,b)+D(b,c)=D(a,c)$ and $D(b,c)+D(c,d)=D(b,d)$. A space with this property has been called a *circular metric space*.

## Filling {#Filling}

*Image: Sphere halve.png.* Arc distances on a [great circle](wikipedia://en/48082) are the same as the distance between the same points on a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859), and on the [hemispheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemisphere_%28geometry%29) into which the circle divides the sphere.

The Riemannian [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) of length 2$\pi$ can be embedded, without any change of distance, into the metric of [geodesics](wikipedia://en/91096) on a [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090), by mapping the circle to a [great circle](wikipedia://en/48082) and its metric to [great-circle distance](wikipedia://en/379733). The same metric space would also be obtained from distances on a [hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemisphere_%28geometry%29). This differs from the boundary of a [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738), for which opposite points on the unit disk would have distance 2, instead of their distance $\pi$ on the Riemannian circle. This difference in internal metrics between the hemisphere and the disk led [Mikhael Gromov](wikipedia://en/771562) to pose his [filling area conjecture](wikipedia://en/12083818), according to which the unit hemisphere is the minimum-area surface having the Riemannian circle as its boundary.
