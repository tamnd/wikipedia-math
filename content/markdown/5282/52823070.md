# Lovelock's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lovelock's theorem"}

**Lovelock's theorem** of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) says that from a local gravitational action which contains only second derivatives of the four-dimensional [spacetime metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_tensor_%28general_relativity%29), then the only possible equations of motion are the [Einstein field equations](wikipedia://en/424540).[^o2][^o3][^o6] The theorem was described by British physicist [David Lovelock](wikipedia://en/47456833) in 1971.

:::

::: {#o5 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

In four dimensional spacetime, any tensor $A^{\mu\nu}$ whose components are functions of the metric tensor $g^{\mu\nu}$ and its first and second derivatives (but linear in the second derivatives of $g^{\mu\nu}$), and also symmetric and divergence-free, is necessarily of the form

$$A^{\mu\nu}=a G^{\mu\nu}+b g^{\mu\nu}$$

where $a$ and $b$ are constant numbers and $G^{\mu\nu}$ is the [Einstein tensor](wikipedia://en/1057638).[^o6]

The only possible second-order Euler–Lagrange expression obtainable in a four-dimensional space from a scalar density of the form $\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(g_{\mu\nu})$ is[^o2] $E^{\mu\nu} = \alpha \sqrt{-g} \left[R^{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2} g^{\mu\nu} R \right] + \lambda \sqrt{-g} g^{\mu\nu}$

:::

## Consequences {#Consequences}

Lovelock's theorem means that if we want to modify the Einstein field equations, then we have five options.[^o2]

- Add other fields rather than the metric tensor;
- Use more or fewer than four spacetime dimensions;
- Add more than second order derivatives of the metric;
- Non-locality, e.g. for example the inverse d'Alembertian;
- Emergence – the idea that the field equations don't come from the action.
