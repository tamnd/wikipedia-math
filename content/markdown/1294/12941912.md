# CAT(k) space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="CAT(k) space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a $\mathbf{\operatorname{\textbf{CAT}}}(k)$ **space**, where $k$ is a real number, is a specific type of [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018). Intuitively, [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654) in a $\operatorname{CAT}(k)$ space (with $k<0$) are "slimmer" than corresponding "model triangles" in a standard space of [constant curvature](wikipedia://en/3638803) $k$. In a $\operatorname{CAT}(k)$ space, the curvature is bounded from above by $k$. A notable special case is $k=0$; [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) $\operatorname{CAT}(0)$ spaces are known as "[Hadamard spaces](wikipedia://en/2850665)" after the [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Jacques Hadamard](wikipedia://en/312675).

:::

Originally, [Aleksandrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleksandr_Danilovich_Aleksandrov) called these spaces “$\mathfrak{R}_k$ domains”. The terminology $\operatorname{CAT}(k)$ was coined by [Mikhail Gromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gromov_%28mathematician%29) in 1987 and is an [acronym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acronym) for [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274), [Aleksandr Danilovich Aleksandrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleksandr_Danilovich_Aleksandrov) and [Victor Andreevich Toponogov](wikipedia://en/12957669) (although Toponogov never explored curvature bounded above in publications).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

*Image: End of universe.jpg.* Model triangles in spaces of positive (top), negative (middle) and zero (bottom) curvature.

For a [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $k$, let $M_k$ denote the unique complete [simply connected](wikipedia://en/523879) [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) (real 2-dimensional [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652)) with constant curvature $k$. Denote by $D_k$ the [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) of $M_k$, which is $\infty$ if $k \leq 0$ and is $\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{k}}$ if $k>0$.

Let $(X,d)$ be a [geodesic metric space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_metric_space), i.e. a metric space for which every two points $x,y\in X$ can be joined by a geodesic segment, an [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) parametrized [continuous curve](wikipedia://en/89246) $\gamma\colon [a,b] \to X,\ \gamma(a) = x,\ \gamma(b) = y$, whose length

$$L(\gamma) = \sup \left\{ \left. \sum_{i = 1}^r d \big( \gamma(t_{i-1}), \gamma(t_i) \big) \right| a = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_r = b, r\in \mathbb{N} \right\}$$

is precisely $d(x,y)$. Let $\Delta$ be a triangle in $X$ with geodesic segments as its sides. $\Delta$ is said to satisfy the $\mathbf{\operatorname{\textbf{CAT}}}(k)$ **inequality** if there is a [comparison triangle](wikipedia://en/5845706) $\Delta'$ in the model space $M_k$, with sides of the same length as the sides of $\Delta$, such that distances between points on $\Delta$ are less than or equal to the distances between corresponding points on $\Delta'$.

The geodesic metric space $(X,d)$ is said to be a $\mathbf{\operatorname{\textbf{CAT}}}(k)$ **space** if every [geodesic triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_triangle) $\Delta$ in $X$ with [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636) less than $2D_k$ satisfies the $\operatorname{CAT}(k)$ inequality. A (not-necessarily-geodesic) metric space $(X,\,d)$ is said to be a space with curvature $\leq k$ if every point of $X$ has a [geodesically convex](wikipedia://en/12677098) $\operatorname{CAT}(k)$ [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485). A space with curvature $\leq 0$ may be said to have [non-positive curvature](wikipedia://en/12989803).

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- Any $\operatorname{CAT}(k)$ space $(X,d)$ is also a $\operatorname{CAT}(\ell)$ space for all $\ell>k$. In fact, the converse holds: if $(X,d)$ is a $\operatorname{CAT}(\ell)$ space for all $\ell>k$, then it is a $\operatorname{CAT}(k)$ space.
- The $n$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbf{E}^n$ with its usual metric is a $\operatorname{CAT}(0)$ space. More generally, any real [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856) (not necessarily complete) is a $\operatorname{CAT}(0)$ space; conversely, if a real [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538) is a $\operatorname{CAT}(k)$ space for some real $k$, then it is an inner product space.
- The $n$-dimensional [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742) $\mathbf{H}^n$ with its usual metric is a $\operatorname{CAT}(-1)$ space, and hence a $\operatorname{CAT}(0)$ space as well.
- The $n$-dimensional [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090) $\mathbf{S}^n$ is a $\operatorname{CAT}(1)$ space.
- More generally, the standard space $M_k$ is a $\operatorname{CAT}(k)$ space. So, for example, regardless of dimension, the sphere of radius $r$ (and constant curvature $\frac{1}{r^2}$) is a $\operatorname{CAT}\left(\frac{1}{r^2}\right)$ space. Note that the diameter of the sphere is $\pi r$ (as measured on the surface of the sphere) not $2r$ (as measured by going through the centre of the sphere).
- The [punctured plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punctured_plane) $\Pi = \mathbf{E}^2\smallsetminus\{\mathbf{0}\}$ is not a $\operatorname{CAT}(0)$ space since it is not geodesically convex (for example, the points $(0,1)$ and $(0,-1)$ cannot be joined by a geodesic in $\Pi$ with arc length 2), but every point of $\Pi$ does have a $\operatorname{CAT}(0)$ geodesically convex neighbourhood, so $\Pi$ is a space of curvature $\leq 0$.
- The closed subspace $X$ of $\mathbf{E}^3$ given by $X = \mathbf{E}^{3} \smallsetminus \{ (x, y, z) \mid x > 0, y > 0 \text{ and } z > 0 \}$ equipped with the induced length metric is *not* a $\operatorname{CAT}(k)$ space for any $k$.
- Any product of $\operatorname{CAT}(0)$ spaces is $\operatorname{CAT}(0)$. (This does not hold for negative arguments.)

:::

## Hadamard spaces {#Hadamard_spaces}

As a special case, a complete CAT(0) space is also known as a **Hadamard space**; this is by analogy with the situation for [Cartan-Hadamard manifolds](wikipedia://en/12471936). A Hadamard space is [contractible](wikipedia://en/1752414) (it has the [homotopy type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_type) of a single point) and, between any two points of a Hadamard space, there is a unique geodesic segment connecting them (in fact, both properties also hold for general, possibly incomplete, CAT(0) spaces). Most importantly, distance functions in Hadamard spaces are [convex](wikipedia://en/245568): if $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ are two geodesics in *X* defined on the same [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) of time *I*, then the function $I\to \mathbb{R}$ given by

$$t \mapsto d \big( \sigma_{1} (t), \sigma_{2} (t) \big)$$

is convex in *t*.

## Properties of CAT(*k*) spaces {#Properties_of_CAT(k)_spaces}

Let $(X,d)$ be a $\operatorname{CAT}(k)$ space. Then the following properties hold:

- Given any two points $x,y\in X$ (with $d(x,y)< D_k$ if $k> 0$), there is a unique geodesic segment that joins $x$ to $y$; moreover, this segment varies continuously as a function of its endpoints.
- Every local geodesic in $X$ with length at most $D_k$ is a geodesic.
- The $d$-[balls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_ball) in $X$ of radius less than $D_k/2$ are (geodesically) convex.
- The $d$-balls in $X$ of radius less than $D_k$ are contractible.
- Approximate midpoints are close to midpoints in the following sense: for every $\lambda < D_k$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists a $\delta = \delta(k,\lambda,\varepsilon) > 0$ such that, if $m$ is the midpoint of a geodesic segment from $x$ to $y$ with $d(x,y)\leq \lambda$ and $$\max \bigl\{ d(x, m'), d(y, m') \bigr\} \leq \frac1{2} d(x, y) + \delta,$$ then $d(m,m') < \varepsilon$.
- It follows from these properties that, for $k\leq 0$ the universal cover of every $\operatorname{CAT}(k)$ space is contractible; in particular, the higher [homotopy groups](wikipedia://en/297506) of such a space are [trivial](wikipedia://en/193059). As the example of the $n$-sphere $\mathbf{S}^n$ shows, there is, in general, no hope for a $\operatorname{CAT}(k)$ space to be contractible if $k > 0$.

## Surfaces of non-positive curvature {#Surfaces_of_non-positive_curvature}

In a region where the curvature of the surface satisfies $K \le 0$, geodesic triangles satisfy the CAT(0) inequalities of **comparison geometry**, studied by [Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274), [Alexandrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleksandr_Danilovich_Aleksandrov) and [Toponogov](wikipedia://en/12957669), and considered later from [a different point of view](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruhat–Tits_building) by [Bruhat](wikipedia://en/9169813) and [Tits](wikipedia://en/219570). Thanks to the vision of [Gromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gromov_%28mathematician%29), this characterisation of non-positive curvature in terms of the underlying metric space has had a profound impact on modern geometry and in particular [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164). Many results known for smooth surfaces and their geodesics, such as Birkhoff's method of constructing geodesics by his curve-shortening process or van Mangoldt and Hadamard's theorem that a [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) surface of non-positive curvature is homeomorphic to the plane, are equally valid in this more general setting.

### Alexandrov's comparison inequality {#Alexandrov's_comparison_inequality}

*Image: Median diagram.svg.* The [median](wikipedia://en/1072006) in the comparison triangle is always longer than the actual median.

The simplest form of the comparison inequality, first proved for surfaces by Alexandrov around 1940, states that The distance between a vertex of a geodesic triangle and the midpoint of the opposite side is always less than the corresponding distance in the comparison triangle in the plane with the same side-lengths.

The inequality follows from the fact that if $c(t)$ describes a geodesic parametrized by arclength and $a$ is a fixed point, then

$$f(t) = d(a,c(t))^{2} - t^{2}$$

is a [convex function](wikipedia://en/245568), i.e.

$$\ddot{f}(t) \ge 0.$$

Taking geodesic polar coordinates with origin at $a$ so that $\|c(t)\| = r(t)$, convexity is equivalent to

$$r\ddot{r} + \dot{r}^2 \ge 1.$$

Changing to normal coordinates $u$, $v$ at $c(t)$, this inequality becomes

$$u^{2} + H^{-1}H_{r}v^{2} \ge 1,$$

where $(u,v)$ corresponds to the unit vector $\text{ċ}(t)$. This follows from the inequality $H_{r} \ge H$, a consequence of the non-negativity of the derivative of the [Wronskian](wikipedia://en/302846) of $H$ and $r$ from [Sturm–Liouville theory](wikipedia://en/490990).[^o11]

:::
