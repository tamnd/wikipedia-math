# Lax pair

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lax pair"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the theory of [integrable systems](wikipedia://en/2573213), a **Lax pair** is a pair of time-dependent matrices or [operators](wikipedia://en/22213) that satisfy a corresponding [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309), called the *Lax equation*. Lax pairs were introduced by [Peter Lax](wikipedia://en/486365) to discuss [solitons](wikipedia://en/51654) in [continuous media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_media). The [inverse scattering transform](wikipedia://en/4916481) makes use of the Lax equations to solve such systems.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A Lax pair is a pair of matrices or operators $L(t), P(t)$ dependent on time, acting on a fixed [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), and satisfying **Lax's equation**:

$$\frac{dL}{dt} = [P, L],$$

where $[P, L] = PL - LP$ is the [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193). Often, as in the example below, $P$ depends on $L$ in a prescribed way, so this is a nonlinear equation for $L$ as a function of $t$.

:::

## Isospectral property {#Isospectral_property}

It can then be shown that the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) and more generally the [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_spectrum) of *L* are independent of *t*. The matrices/operators *L* are said to be *[isospectral](wikipedia://en/386031)* as $t$ varies.

The core observation is that the matrices $L(t)$ are all similar by virtue of

$$L(t) = U(t, s) L(s) U(t, s)^{-1},$$

where $U(t, s)$ is the solution of the [Cauchy problem](wikipedia://en/1725027)

$$\frac{d}{dt} U(t, s) = P(t) U(t, s), \quad U(s, s) = I,$$

where *I* denotes the identity matrix. Note that if *P*(*t*) is [skew-adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew-adjoint), *U*(*t*, *s*) will be [unitary](wikipedia://en/225999).

In other words, to solve the eigenvalue problem *Lψ* = *λψ* at time *t*, it is possible to solve the same problem at time 0, where *L* is generally known better, and to propagate the solution with the following formulas:

$\lambda(t) = \lambda(0)$ (no change in spectrum),

$$\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} = P \psi.$$

### Through principal invariants {#Through_principal_invariants}

The result can also be shown using the invariants $\operatorname{tr}(L^n)$ for any $n$. These satisfy

$$\frac{d}{dt} \operatorname{tr}(L^n) = 0$$

due to the Lax equation, and since the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) can be written in terms of these traces, the spectrum is preserved by the flow.[^o6]

### Link with the inverse scattering method {#Link_with_the_inverse_scattering_method}

The above property is the basis for the inverse scattering method. In this method, *L* and *P* act on a [functional space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_space) (thus *ψ* = *ψ*(*t*, *x*)) and depend on an unknown function *u*(*t*, *x*) which is to be determined. It is generally assumed that *u*(0, *x*) is known, and that *P* does not depend on *u* in the scattering region where $\|x\| \to \infty.$ The method then takes the following form:

1. Compute the spectrum of $L(0)$, giving $\lambda$ and $\psi(0, x).$
1. In the scattering region where $P$ is known, propagate $\psi$ in time by using $\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t}(t, x) = P \psi(t, x)$ with initial condition $\psi(0, x).$
1. Knowing $\psi$ in the scattering region, compute $L(t)$ and/or $u(t, x).$

::: {#o9 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Spectral curve"}

### Spectral curve {#Spectral_curve}

If the Lax matrix additionally depends analytically on a complex parameter $z$ (as is the case for, say, [sine-Gordon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine-Gordon)), and the deformation is isospectral, the equation

$$\det\big(wI - L(z)\big) = 0,$$

defines an analytic subset of $\mathbb{C}^2$ with coordinates $w, z,$ which under suitable hypotheses is a curve, often after removing singularities. By the isospectral property, this curve is preserved under time translation. This is called the **spectral curve**, often after taking a suitable compactification. When the dependence of *L* on *z* is algebraic, it is an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260). Such curves appear in the theory of [Hitchin systems](wikipedia://en/23652892).[^o10]

:::

## Zero-curvature representation {#Zero-curvature_representation}

Any PDE which admits a Lax-pair representation also admits a zero-curvature representation.[^o12] In fact, the zero-curvature representation is more general and for other integrable PDEs, such as the [sine-Gordon equation](wikipedia://en/306645), the Lax pair refers to matrices that satisfy the zero-curvature equation rather than the Lax equation. Furthermore, the zero-curvature representation makes the link between integrable systems and geometry manifest, culminating in [Ward](wikipedia://en/5980017)'s programme to formulate known integrable systems as solutions to the [anti-self-dual Yang–Mills](wikipedia://en/35544664) (ASDYM) equations.

### Zero-curvature equation {#Zero-curvature_equation}

The zero-curvature equations are described by a pair of matrix-valued functions $A_x(x, t), A_t(x, t),$ where the subscripts denote coordinate indices rather than derivatives. Often the $(x, t)$ dependence is through a single scalar function $\varphi(x, t)$ and its derivatives. The zero-curvature equation is then

$$\partial_t A_x - \partial_x A_t + [A_x, A_t] = 0.$$

It is so called as it corresponds to the vanishing of the [curvature tensor](wikipedia://en/489440), which in this case is $F_{\mu\nu} = [\partial_\mu - A_\mu, \partial_\nu - A_\nu] = -\partial_\mu A_\nu + \partial_\nu A_\mu + [A_\mu, A_\nu]$. This differs from the conventional expression by some minus signs, which are ultimately unimportant.

### Lax pair to zero-curvature {#Lax_pair_to_zero-curvature}

For an eigensolution to the Lax operator $L$, one has

$$L\psi = \lambda \psi, \psi_t + A\psi = 0.$$

If we instead enforce these, together with time independence of $\lambda$, instead the Lax equation arises as a consistency equation for an overdetermined system.

The Lax pair $(L, P)$ can be used to define the connection components $(A_x, A_t)$. When a PDE admits a zero-curvature representation but not a Lax equation representation, the connection components $(A_x, A_t)$ are referred to as the Lax pair, and the connection as a Lax connection.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Korteweg–de Vries equation {#Korteweg–de_Vries_equation}

The [Korteweg–de Vries equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korteweg–de_Vries_equation)

$$u_t = 6uu_x - u_{xxx}$$

can be reformulated as the Lax equation

$$L_t = [P, L]$$

with

$L = -\partial_x^2 + u$ (a [Sturm–Liouville operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturm–Liouville_operator)),

$$P = -4\partial_x^3 + 6u\partial_x + 3u_x,$$

where all derivatives act on all objects to the right. This accounts for the infinite number of [first integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_integrals) of the KdV equation.

### Kovalevskaya top {#Kovalevskaya_top}

The previous example used an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. Examples are also possible with finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. These include [Kovalevskaya top](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kovalevskaya_top) and the generalization to include an electric field $\vec{h}$.[^o19]

$$\begin{aligned} L &= \begin{pmatrix} g_1 + h_2 & g_2 + h_1 & g_3 & h_3\\ g_2 + h_1 & -g_1 + h_2 & h_3 & -g_3\\ g_3 & h_3 & -g_1 - h_2 & g_2 - h_1\\ h_3 & -g_3 & g_2 - h_1 & g_1 + h_2\\ \end{pmatrix} \lambda^{-1}\\ &+ \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & -l_2 & -l_1\\ 0 & 0 & l_1 & -l_2\\ l_2 & -l_1 & -2 \lambda & -2 l_3 \\ l_1 & l_2 & 2 l_3 & 2 \lambda\\ \end{pmatrix}, \\ P &= \frac{-1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 l_3 & l_2 & l_1\\ 2 l_3 & 0 & -l_1 & l_2\\ -l_2 & l_1 & 2 \lambda & 2 l_3 + \gamma\\ -l_1 & -l_2 & -2 l_3 & -2\lambda\\ \end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned}$$

### Heisenberg picture {#Heisenberg_picture}

In the [Heisenberg picture](wikipedia://en/306706) of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), an [observable](wikipedia://en/294248) $A$ without explicit time $t$ dependence satisfies

$$\frac{d}{dt} A(t) = \frac{i}{\hbar} [H, A(t)],$$

with $H$ the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381) and $\text{ħ}$ the reduced [Planck constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_constant). Aside from a factor, observables (without explicit time dependence) in this picture can thus be seen to form Lax pairs together with the Hamiltonian. The [Schrödinger picture](wikipedia://en/419200) is then interpreted as the alternative expression in terms of isospectral evolution of these observables.

### Further examples {#Further_examples}

Further examples of systems of equations that can be formulated as a Lax pair include:

- [Benjamin–Ono equation](wikipedia://en/16697638)
- One-dimensional cubic [non-linear Schrödinger equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linear_Schrödinger_equation)
- [Davey–Stewartson system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davey–Stewartson_equation)
- Integrable systems with contact Lax pairs[^o23]
- [Kadomtsev–Petviashvili equation](wikipedia://en/4211219)
- [Korteweg–de Vries equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korteweg–de_Vries_equation)
- [KdV hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KdV_hierarchy)
- [Marchenko equation](wikipedia://en/5644561)
- [Modified Korteweg–de Vries equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modified_Korteweg–de_Vries_equation)
- [Sine-Gordon equation](wikipedia://en/306645)
- [Toda lattice](wikipedia://en/3240033)
- [Lagrange, Euler, and Kovalevskaya tops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange,_Euler,_and_Kovalevskaya_tops)
- [Belinski–Zakharov transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belinski–Zakharov_transform), in general relativity.

The last is remarkable, as it implies that both the [Schwarzschild metric](wikipedia://en/264606) and the [Kerr metric](wikipedia://en/456715) can be understood as solitons.

#### Discrete time dynamics {#Discrete_time_dynamics}

The concept of Lax pair can also be defined for [dynamical systems with discrete time](wikipedia://en/9087#Discrete_dynamical_system), for instance the [pentagram map](wikipedia://en/26179254#The_spectral_curve).[^o25]
