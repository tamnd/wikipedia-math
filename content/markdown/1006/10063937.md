# Dual cone and polar cone

*Image: Dual cone illustration.svg.* A set *C* and its dual cone *C${}^{*}$*.

*Image: Polar cone illustration1.svg.* A set *C* and its polar cone *C<sup>o</sup>*. The dual cone and the polar cone are symmetric to each other with respect to the origin.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dual cone and polar cone"}

**Dual cone** and **polar cone** are closely related concepts in [convex analysis](wikipedia://en/3237201), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831).

:::

## Dual cone {#Dual_cone}

### In a vector space {#In_a_vector_space}

The **dual cone** *C${}^{*}$* of a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) *C* in a [linear space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_space) *X* over the [reals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers), e.g. [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, with [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) *X${}^{*}$* is the set

$$C^* = \left \{y\in X^*: \langle y , x \rangle \geq 0 \quad \forall x\in C \right \},$$

where $\langle y, x \rangle$ is the [duality pairing](wikipedia://en/63735167) between *X* and *X${}^{*}$*, i.e. $\langle y, x\rangle = y(x)$.

The set $C^*$ is always a [convex cone](wikipedia://en/3558266), even if $C$ is neither [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) nor a [cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_cone).

### In a topological vector space {#In_a_topological_vector_space}

If *X* is a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) over the real or complex numbers, then the **dual cone** of a subset *C* ⊆ *X* is the following set of continuous linear functionals on *X*:

$C^{\prime} := \left\{ f \in X^{\prime} : \operatorname{Re} \left( f (x) \right) \geq 0 \text{ for all } x \in C \right\}$,[^n1]

which is the [polar](wikipedia://en/1764022) of the set -*C*.[^n1] No matter what *C* is, $C^{\prime}$ will be a convex cone. If *C* ⊆ {0} then $C^{\prime} = X^{\prime}$.

### In a Hilbert space (internal dual cone) {#In_a_Hilbert_space_(internal_dual_cone)}

Alternatively, many authors define the dual cone in the context of a real [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) (such as **R**<sup>*n*</sup> equipped with the Euclidean inner product) to be what is sometimes called the *internal dual cone*.

$$C^*_\text{internal} := \left \{y\in X: \langle y , x \rangle \geq 0 \quad \forall x\in C \right \}.$$

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

Using this latter definition for *C${}^{*}$*, we have that when *C* is a cone, the following properties hold:[^o10]

- A non-zero vector *y* is in *C${}^{*}$* if and only if both of the following conditions hold:

<!-- -->

1. *y* is a [normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_normal) at the origin of a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) that [supports](wikipedia://en/4165181) *C*.
1. *y* and *C* lie on the same side of that supporting hyperplane.

<!-- -->

- *C${}^{*}$* is [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) and convex.
- $C_1 \subseteq C_2$ implies $C_2^* \subseteq C_1^*$.
- If *C* has nonempty interior, then *C${}^{*}$* is *pointed*, i.e. *C\** contains no line in its entirety.
- If *C* is a cone and the closure of *C* is pointed, then *C${}^{*}$* has nonempty interior.
- *C${}^{**}$* is the closure of the smallest convex cone containing *C* (a consequence of the [hyperplane separation theorem](wikipedia://en/4739827))

:::

## Self-dual cones {#Self-dual_cones}

A cone *C* in a vector space *X* is said to be *self-dual* if *X* can be equipped with an [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) ⟨⋅,⋅⟩ such that the internal dual cone relative to this inner product is equal to *C*.[^o12] Those authors who define the dual cone as the internal dual cone in a real Hilbert space usually say that a cone is self-dual if it is equal to its internal dual. This is slightly different from the above definition, which permits a change of inner product. For instance, the above definition makes a cone in **R**<sup>*n*</sup> with ellipsoidal base self-dual, because the inner product can be changed to make the base spherical, and a cone with spherical base in **R**<sup>*n*</sup> is equal to its internal dual.

The nonnegative [orthant](wikipedia://en/1283725) of **R**<sup>*n*</sup> and the space of all [positive semidefinite matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_semidefinite_matrix) are self-dual, as are the cones with ellipsoidal base (often called "spherical cones", "Lorentz cones", or sometimes "ice-cream cones"). So are all cones in **R**<sup>3</sup> whose base is the convex hull of a regular polygon with an odd number of vertices. A less regular example is the cone in **R**<sup>3</sup> whose base is the "house": the convex hull of a square and a point outside the square forming an equilateral triangle (of the appropriate height) with one of the sides of the square.

## Polar cone {#Polar_cone}

*Image: Polar cone illustration.svg.* The polar of the closed convex cone *C* is the closed convex cone *C<sup>o</sup>*, and vice versa.

For a set *C* in *X*, the **polar cone** of *C* is the set[^o14]

$$C^o = \left \{y\in X^*: \langle y , x \rangle \leq 0 \quad \forall x\in C \right \}.$$

It can be seen that the polar cone is equal to the negative of the dual cone, i.e. *C<sup>o</sup>* = −*C${}^{*}$*.

For a closed convex cone *C* in *X*, the polar cone is equivalent to the [polar set](wikipedia://en/1764022) for *C*.[^o15]
