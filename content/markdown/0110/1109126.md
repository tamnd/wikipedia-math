# Hasse–Minkowski theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hasse–Minkowski theorem"}

The **Hasse–Minkowski theorem** is a fundamental result in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) which states that two [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478) over a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) are equivalent if and only if they are equivalent *locally at all places*, i.e. equivalent over every topological [completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completion_%28ring_theory%29) of the field (which may be [real](wikipedia://en/20646438), [complex](wikipedia://en/5826), or [p-adic](wikipedia://en/51423)). A related result is that a [quadratic space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_space) over a number field is [isotropic](wikipedia://en/10917170) if and only if it is isotropic locally everywhere, or equivalently, that a quadratic form over a number field nontrivially represents zero if and only if this holds for all completions of the field. The theorem was proved in the case of the field of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) by [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537) and generalized to number fields by [Helmut Hasse](wikipedia://en/727118). The same statement holds even more generally for all [global fields](wikipedia://en/288358).

:::

## Importance {#Importance}

The importance of the Hasse–Minkowski theorem lies in the novel paradigm it presented for answering arithmetical questions: in order to determine whether an equation of a certain type has a solution in rational numbers, it is sufficient to test whether it has solutions over complete fields of real and *p*-adic numbers, where one can apply analytic techniques such as [Newton's method](wikipedia://en/22145) and its *p*-adic analogue [Hensel's lemma](wikipedia://en/1633368). This is the first significant example of a [local-global principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local-global_principle), one of the most fundamental techniques in [arithmetic geometry](wikipedia://en/1973177).

## Application to the classification of quadratic forms {#Application_to_the_classification_of_quadratic_forms}

The Hasse–Minkowski theorem reduces the problem of classifying quadratic forms over a number field *K* up to equivalence to the set of analogous but much simpler questions over [local fields](wikipedia://en/145375). Basic invariants of a nonsingular quadratic form are its **dimension**, which is a positive integer, and its **[discriminant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discriminant_of_a_quadratic_form)** modulo the squares in **K**, which is an element of the multiplicative group [**K**<sup>\*</sup>/**K**<sup>\*2</sup>](wikipedia://en/36362488). In addition, for every [place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place_%28mathematics%29) *v* of *K*, there is an invariant coming from the completion **K**<sub>*v*</sub>. Depending on the choice of *v*, this completion may be the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) **R**, the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) **C**, or a [p-adic number](wikipedia://en/51423) field, each of which has different kinds of invariants:

- *Case of* **R**. By [Sylvester's law of inertia](wikipedia://en/1127460), the signature (or, alternatively, the negative index of inertia) is a complete invariant.
- *Case of* **C**. All nonsingular quadratic forms of the same dimension are equivalent.
- *Case of* **Q**<sub>*p*</sub> *and its [algebraic extensions](wikipedia://en/2125)*. Forms of the same dimension are classified up to equivalence by their [Hasse invariant](wikipedia://en/3028413).

These invariants must satisfy some compatibility conditions: a parity relation (the sign of the discriminant must match the negative index of inertia) and a product formula (a local–global relation). Conversely, for every set of invariants satisfying these relations, there is a quadratic form over **K** with these invariants.
