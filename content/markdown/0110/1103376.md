# Planimeter

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Planimeter"}

A **planimeter**, also known as a **platometer**, is a [measuring instrument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measuring_instrument) used to determine the [area](wikipedia://en/1209) of an arbitrary two-dimensional shape.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

There are several kinds of planimeters, but all operate in a similar way. The precise way in which they are constructed varies, with the main types of mechanical planimeter being polar, linear, and Prytz or "hatchet" planimeters. The Swiss [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Jakob Amsler-Laffon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jakob_Amsler-Laffon) built the first modern planimeter in 1854, the concept having been pioneered by Johann [Martin Hermann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Herrmann) in 1818.[^o4] Many developments followed Amsler's famous planimeter, including electronic versions.

The Amsler (polar) type consists of a two-bar linkage. At the end of one link is a pointer, used to trace around the boundary of the shape to be measured. The other end of the linkage pivots freely on a weight that keeps it from moving. Near the junction of the two links is a [measuring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measuring) wheel of calibrated diameter, with a scale to show fine rotation, and worm gearing for an auxiliary turns counter scale. As the area outline is traced, this wheel rolls on the [surface](wikipedia://en/50328015) of the [drawing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drawing). The operator sets the wheel, turns the counter to zero, and then traces the pointer around the perimeter of the shape. When the tracing is complete, the scales at the measuring wheel show the shape's [area](wikipedia://en/1209).

When the planimeter's measuring wheel moves [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to its axis, it rolls, and this movement is recorded. When the measuring wheel moves parallel to its axis, the wheel skids without rolling, so this movement is ignored. That means the planimeter measures the distance that its measuring wheel travels, projected perpendicularly to the measuring wheel's axis of rotation. The area of the shape is proportional to the number of turns through which the measuring wheel rotates.

The polar planimeter is restricted by design to measuring areas within limits determined by its size and [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446). However, the linear type has no restriction in one dimension, because it can roll. Its wheels must not slip, because the movement must be constrained to a straight line.

Developments of the planimeter can establish the position of the [first moment of area](wikipedia://en/5039189) ([center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961)), and even the [second moment of area](wikipedia://en/1736264).

The images show the principles of a linear and a polar planimeter. The pointer M at one end of the planimeter follows the contour C of the surface S to be measured. For the linear planimeter the movement of the "elbow" E is restricted to the *y*-axis. For the polar planimeter the "elbow" is connected to an arm with its other endpoint O at a fixed position. Connected to the arm ME is the measuring wheel with its axis of rotation parallel to ME. A movement of the arm ME can be factored into a movement perpendicular to ME, causing the wheel to rotate, and a movement parallel to ME, causing the wheel to skid, with no contribution to its reading.

## Principle {#Principle}

*Image: MAD-PlanimeterLinBasis01.png.* Principle of the linear planimeter

The working of the linear planimeter may be explained by measuring the area of a rectangle ABCD (see image). Moving with the pointer from A to B the arm EM moves through the yellow [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496), with area equal to PQ×EM. This area is also equal to the area of the parallelogram A"ABB". The measuring wheel measures the distance PQ (perpendicular to EM). Moving from C to D the arm EM moves through the green parallelogram, with area equal to the area of the rectangle D"DCC". The measuring wheel now moves in the opposite direction, subtracting this reading from the former. The movements along BC and DA are the same but opposite, so they cancel each other with no net effect on the reading of the wheel. The net result is the measuring of the difference of the yellow and green areas, which is the area of ABCD.

:::

## Mathematical derivation {#Mathematical_derivation}

The operation of a linear planimeter can be justified by applying [Green's theorem](wikipedia://en/243334), though the design of the major varieties predates the theorem's proof. Apply it to the components of the [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) given by

$$\begin{aligned}(P,Q)=(0,x)\end{aligned}.$$

Note that any other vector field satisfying $\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 1 \end{aligned}$ also suffices, for example $(P,Q)=(-y,0),$ or $(-\frac{ 1}{ 2} y, \frac{ 1}{ 2}x)$.

Here $x$ is the direction perpendicular to the measuring arm and $y$ is parallel. As the measuring arm traces the contour, the device records the quantity $x \Delta y$ in small intervals, by taking a product of the current perpendicular distance $x$ and the parallel displacement $dy$. By summing up $x \Delta y$ along the contour $C$ one approximates the integral $\oint_C x dy$ through [Riemann sum](wikipedia://en/176478).

Then:

$$\begin{aligned} \oint_C x dy &= \oint_C(P \, dx + Q \, dy) = \iint_D\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) \, dx \, dy \\[8pt] &= \iint_D \, dx \, dy = Area(D) \end{aligned}$$

where $D$ is the region enclosed by the contour.

### Polar coordinates {#Polar_coordinates}

The connection with Green's theorem can be understood in terms of [integration in polar coordinates](wikipedia://en/25120#Integral_calculus_%28area%29): in polar coordinates, area is computed by the integral $\int_\theta \tfrac{1}{2} (r(\theta))^2\,d\theta,$ where the form being integrated is *quadratic* in *r,* meaning that the rate at which area changes with respect to change in angle varies quadratically with the radius.

For a [parametric equation](wikipedia://en/576108) in polar coordinates, where both *r* and *θ* vary as a function of time, this becomes

$$\int_t \tfrac{1}{2} (r(t))^2 \, d(\theta(t)) = \int_t \tfrac{1}{2} (r(t))^2\, \dot \theta(t)\,dt.$$

For a polar planimeter the total rotation of the wheel is proportional to $\int_t r(t)\, \dot \theta(t)\,dt,$ as the rotation is proportional to the distance traveled, which at any point in time is proportional to radius and to change in angle, as in the circumference of a circle ($\int r\,d\theta = 2\pi r$).

This last integrand $r(t) \,\dot \theta(t)$ can be recognized as the derivative of the earlier integrand $\tfrac{1}{2} (r(t))^2 \dot \theta(t)$ (with respect to *r*), and shows that a polar planimeter computes the area integral in terms of the *derivative*, which is reflected in [Green's theorem](wikipedia://en/243334), which equates a line integral of a function on a (1-dimensional) contour to the (2-dimensional) integral of the derivative.
