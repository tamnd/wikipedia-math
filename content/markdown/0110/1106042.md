# Reciprocal polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Reciprocal polynomial"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), given a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000)

$$p(x) = a_0 + a_1x + a_2x^2 + \cdots + a_nx^n,$$

with [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) from an arbitrary [field](wikipedia://en/10603), its **reciprocal polynomial** or **reflected polynomial**,[^o2][^o3] denoted by $p^{\ast}$ or $p^{R}$,[^o3][^o2] is the polynomial with the same coefficients in the reverse order,[^o4]

:::

$$p^*(x) = a_n + a_{n-1}x + \cdots + a_0x^n = x^n p(x^{-1}).$$

That is, the coefficients of $p^*$ are the coefficients of $p$ in reverse order. Reciprocal polynomials arise naturally in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) as the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) of the [inverse of a matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_of_a_matrix).

In the special case where the field is the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), when

$$p(z) = a_0 + a_1z + a_2z^2 + \cdots + a_nz^n,$$

the **conjugate reciprocal polynomial**, denoted $p^\dagger$, is defined by,

$$p^{\dagger}(z) = \overline{a_n} + \overline{a_{n-1}}z + \cdots + \overline{a_0}z^n = z^n\overline{p(\bar{z}^{-1})},$$

where $\overline{a_i}$ denotes the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) of $a_i$, and is also called the reciprocal polynomial when no confusion can arise.

A polynomial $p$ is called **self-reciprocal** or **palindromic** if $p(x)=p^*(x)$. The coefficients of a self-reciprocal polynomial satisfy $a_i=a_{n-i}$ for all $i$.

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Reciprocal polynomials have several connections with their original polynomials, including:

1. $\deg p=\deg p^*$ if $a_0$ is not 0.
1. $p(x)=x^np^*(x^{-1})$.[^o3]
1. $\alpha$ is a [root](wikipedia://en/264210) of a polynomial $p$ if and only if $\alpha^{-1}$ is a root of $p^*$ or if $\alpha=0$ and $p^*$ is of lower degree than $p$.[^o7]
1. If $x\nmid p(x)$ then $p$ is [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725) if and only if $p^*$ is irreducible.[^o8]
1. $p$ is [primitive](wikipedia://en/1029177) if and only if $p^*$ is primitive.[^o7]

Other properties of reciprocal polynomials may be obtained, for instance:

- A self-reciprocal polynomial of odd degree is divisible by $x+1$, hence is not irreducible if its degree is $>1$.

:::

## Palindromic and antipalindromic polynomials {#Palindromic_and_antipalindromic_polynomials}

A self-reciprocal polynomial is also called palindromic because its coefficients, when the polynomial is written in the order of ascending or descending powers, form a [palindrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palindrome). That is, if

$$P(x) = \sum_{i=0}^n a_ix^i$$

is a polynomial of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) $n$, then $P$ is *palindromic* if $a_i=a_{n-i}$ for $i=0,1,\dots,n$, or equivalently if $P(x)=P^*(x)$.

Similarly, a polynomial $P$ of degree $n$ is called **antipalindromic** if $a_i=-a_{n-i}$ for $i=0,1,\dots,n$. That is, a polynomial $P$ is *antipalindromic* if $P(x)=-P^*(x)$.

::: {#o11 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

From the properties of the [binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/4668), it follows that the polynomials $P(x)=(x+1)^n$ are palindromic for all positive [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $n$, while the polynomials $Q(x)=(x-1)^n$ are palindromic when $n$ is even and antipalindromic when $n$ is [odd](wikipedia://en/143135).

Other examples of palindromic polynomials include [cyclotomic polynomials](wikipedia://en/171992) and [Eulerian polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eulerian_polynomial).

:::

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

- If $a$ is a root of a polynomial that is either palindromic or antipalindromic, then $\tfrac1a$ is also a root and has the same [multiplicity](wikipedia://en/19211790).[^o14]
- The converse is true: If for each root $a$ of polynomial, the value $\tfrac1a$ is also a root of the same multiplicity, then the polynomial is either palindromic or antipalindromic.
- For any polynomial $q$, the polynomial $q+q^*$ is palindromic and the polynomial $q-q^*$ is antipalindromic.
- It follows that any polynomial $q$ can be written as the sum of a palindromic and an antipalindromic polynomial, since[^o15]

$$q=\frac{q+q^*}{2}+\frac{q-q^*}{2}.$$

- The product of two palindromic or antipalindromic polynomials is palindromic.
- The product of a palindromic polynomial and an antipalindromic polynomial is antipalindromic.
- A palindromic polynomial of odd degree is a multiple of $x+1$ (it has $-1$ as a root) and its quotient by $x+1$ is also palindromic.
- An antipalindromic polynomial over a field $k$ with odd [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) is a multiple of $x-1$ (it has $1$ as a root) and its quotient by $x-1$ is palindromic.
- An antipalindromic polynomial of even degree is a multiple of $x^2-1$ (it has $-1$ and $1$ as roots) and its quotient by $x^2-1$ is palindromic.
- If $p(x)$ is a palindromic polynomial of even degree $2d$, then there is a polynomial $q$ of degree $d$ such that $p(x)=x^dq(x+\tfrac1x)$.[^o16]
- If $p(x)$ is a [monic](wikipedia://en/314730) antipalindromic polynomial of even degree $2d$ over a field $k$ of odd [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621), then it can be written uniquely as $p(x)=x^d\bigl(Q(x)-Q(\tfrac1x)\bigr)$, where $Q$ is a monic polynomial of degree $d$ with no constant term.[^o17]
- If an antipalindromic polynomial $P$ has even degree $2n$ over a field $k$ of odd characteristic, then its "middle" coefficient (of power $n$ is $0$ since $a_n=-a_{2n-n}$.

:::

### Real coefficients {#Real_coefficients}

A polynomial with [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) coefficients all of whose [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) roots lie on the unit circle in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) (that is, all the roots have modulus 1) is either palindromic or antipalindromic.[^o19]

## Conjugate reciprocal polynomials {#Conjugate_reciprocal_polynomials}

A polynomial is **conjugate reciprocal** if $p(x) \equiv p^{\dagger}(x)$ and **self-inversive** if $p(x) = \omega p^{\dagger}(x)$ for a scale factor $\omega$ on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071).[^o21]

If $p(z)$ is the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106) of $z_0$ with $|z_0|=1$, $z_0\ne 1$, and $p(z)$ has [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) coefficients, then $p(z)$ is conjugate-reciprocal. This follows because

$$z_0^n\overline{p(1/\bar{z_0})} = z_0^n\overline{p(z_0)} = z_0^n\bar{0} = 0.$$

So $z_0$ is a root of the polynomial $z^n\overline{p(\bar{z}^{-1})}$ which has degree $n$. But, the minimal polynomial is unique, hence

$$cp(z) = z^n\overline{p(\bar{z}^{-1})}$$

for some constant $c$, i.e. $ca_i=\overline{a_{n-i}}=a_{n-i}$. Sum from $i=0$ to $n$ and note that $1$ is not a root of $p$. We conclude that $c=1$.

A consequence is that the [cyclotomic polynomials](wikipedia://en/171992) $\Phi_n$ are conjugate-reciprocal for $n>1$. This is used in the [special number field sieve](wikipedia://en/589132) to allow numbers of the form $x^{11}\pm 1$, $x^{13}\pm 1$, $x^{15}\pm 1$ and $x^{21}\pm 1$ to be factored taking advantage of the algebraic factors by using polynomials of degree 5, 6, 4 and 6 respectively – note that $\varphi$ ([Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452)) of the exponents are 10, 12, 8 and 12.

Per [Cohn's theorem](wikipedia://en/56811127), a self-inversive polynomial has as many roots in the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738) $\{z\in\mathbb{C}: |z| < 1\}$ as the reciprocal polynomial of its [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921).[^o22][^o23]

## Application in coding theory {#Application_in_coding_theory}

The reciprocal polynomial finds a use in the theory of [cyclic error correcting codes](wikipedia://en/4706825). Suppose $x^n-1$ can be factored into the product of two polynomials, say $x^n-1=g(x)p(x)$. When $g(x)$ generates a cyclic code $C$, then the reciprocal polynomial $p^*$ generates $C^\perp$, the [orthogonal complement](wikipedia://en/1058833) of $C$.[^o25] Also, $C$ is *self-orthogonal* (that is, $C\subseteq C^\perp$), if and only if $p^*$ divides $g(x)$.[^o26]
