# Affine focal set

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Affine focal set"}

In mathematics, and especially [affine differential geometry](wikipedia://en/14222703), the **affine focal set** of a [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) [submanifold](wikipedia://en/525095) *M* [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119) in a smooth [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) *N* is the [caustic](wikipedia://en/880406) generated by the affine normal lines. It can be realised as the bifurcation set of a certain family of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427). The bifurcation set is the set of parameter values of the family which yield functions with degenerate [singularities](wikipedia://en/581763). This is not the same as the [bifurcation diagram](wikipedia://en/79173) in [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems).

:::

Assume that *M* is an *n*-[dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensional) smooth [hypersurface](wikipedia://en/525101) in real (*n*+1)-space. Assume that *M* has no points where the [second fundamental form](wikipedia://en/652078) is [degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_form). From the article [affine differential geometry](wikipedia://en/14222703), there exists a unique [transverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transversality_%28mathematics%29) [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) over *M*. This is the affine normal vector field, or the [Blaschke normal field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaschke_normal_field). A special (i.e. det = 1) [affine transformation](wikipedia://en/38449) of real (*n* + 1)-space will carry the affine normal vector field of *M* onto the affine normal vector field of the image of *M* under the transformation.

## Geometric interpretation {#Geometric_interpretation}

Consider a [local](wikipedia://en/1462457) [parametrisation](wikipedia://en/453198) of *M*. Let $U \subset \mathbb{R}^{n}$ be an [open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_%28topology%29) [neighbourhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighbourhood) of 0 with coordinates $\mathbf{u} = (u_1,\ldots,u_n)$, and let $\mathbf{X} : U \to \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ be a smooth parametrisation of *M* in a neighbourhood of one of its points.

The affine normal [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) will be denoted by $\mathbf{A}$. At each point of *M* it is [transverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transversality_%28mathematics%29) to the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) of *M*, i.e.

$$\mathbf{A} : U \to T_{\mathbf{X}(U)}\mathbb{R}^{n+1}. \,$$

For a fixed $\mathbf{u}_0 \in U$ the affine normal line to *M* at $\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{u}_0)$ may be parametrised by *t* where

$$t \mapsto \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{u}_0) + t \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{u}_0).$$

The affine focal set is given [geometrically](wikipedia://en/18973446) as the [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) [intersections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line-line_intersection) of the *n*-parameter family of affine normal lines. To calculate, choose an affine normal line, say at point *p*; then look at the affine normal lines at points infinitesimally close to *p* and see if any intersect the one at *p*. If *p* is infinitesimally close to $\mathbf{u} \in U$, then it may be expressed as $\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{u}$ where $d\mathbf{u}$ represents the infinitesimal difference. Thus $\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{u})$ and $\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{u})$ will be our *p* and its neighbour.

Solve for *t* and $d\mathbf{u}$.

$$\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{u}) + t \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{u}) + t \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{u}).$$

This can be done by using [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) expansions, and is not too difficult; it is lengthy and has thus been omitted.

Recalling from the article [affine differential geometry](wikipedia://en/14222703), the affine shape operator *S* is a type (1,1)-[tensor field](wikipedia://en/256738) on *M*, and is given by $Sv = D_v\mathbf{A}$, where *D* is the [covariant derivative](wikipedia://en/431848) on real (*n* + 1)-space (for those well read: it is the usual [flat](wikipedia://en/130526) and [torsion](wikipedia://en/4342484) free [connexion](wikipedia://en/607690)).

The solutions to $\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{u}) + t \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{u}) + t \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{u})$ are when 1/*t* is an [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of *S* and that $d\mathbf{u}$ is a corresponding [eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector). The eigenvalues of *S* are not always distinct: there may be repeated roots, there may be complex roots, and *S* may not always be [diagonalisable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagonalisable). For $0 \le k \le [n/2]$, where $[-]$ denotes the [greatest integer function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_integer_function), there will generically be (*n* − 2*k*)-pieces of the affine focal set above each point *p*. The −2*k* corresponds to pairs of eigenvalues becoming complex (like the [solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solution_%28mathematics%29) to $x^2 + a = 0$ as *a* changes from [negative](wikipedia://en/154616) to [positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number)).

The affine focal set need not be made up of smooth hypersurfaces. In fact, for a [generic](wikipedia://en/12886758) hypersurface *M*, the affine focal set will have [singularities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity). The singularities could be found by calculation, but that may be difficult, and there is no idea of what the singularity looks like up to [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564). Using [singularity theory](wikipedia://en/581763) gives much more information.

## Singularity theory approach {#Singularity_theory_approach}

The idea here is to define a family of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) over *M*. The family will have the ambient real (*n* + 1)-space as its parameter space, i.e. for each choice of ambient point there is function defined over *M*. This family is the family of affine distance functions:

$$\Delta : \mathbb{R}^{n+1} \times M \to \mathbb{R}.\,$$

Given an ambient point $\mathbf{x}$ and a surface point *p*, it is possible to decompose the [chord](wikipedia://en/297947) joining *p* to $\mathbf{x}$ as a [tangential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangential) component and a transverse component [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) to $\mathbf{A}$. The value of Δ is given implicitly in the equation

$$\mathbf{x} - p = Z(\mathbf{x},p) + \Delta(\mathbf{x},p) \mathbf{A}(p)$$

where *Z* is a [tangent vector](wikipedia://en/30807). Now, what is sought is the bifurcation set of the family Δ, i.e. the ambient points for which the restricted function

$$\Delta : \{\mathbf{x}\} \times M \to \mathbb{R}$$

has degenerate singularity at some *p*. A function has degenerate singularity if both the [Jacobian matrix](wikipedia://en/195351) of first order [partial derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_derivatives) and the [Hessian matrix](wikipedia://en/412108) of second order partial derivatives have zero [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468).

To discover if the Jacobian matrix has zero determinant, differentiating the equation *x - p = Z + ΔA* is needed. Let *X* be a tangent vector to *M*, and differentiate in that direction:

$$D_X(\mathbf{x}-p) = D_X(Z + \Delta \mathbf{A}),$$

$$-X = \nabla_XZ + h(X,Z)\mathbf{A} + d_X\Delta \mathbf{A} - \Delta SX ,$$

$$(\nabla_XZ + (I - \Delta S)X) + (h(X,Z) + d_X\Delta)\mathbf{A} = 0 ,$$

where *I* is the [identity](wikipedia://en/14962). This means that $\nabla_XZ = (\Delta S - I)X$ and $h(X,Z) = -d_X\Delta$. The last equality says that we have the following equation of [differential one-forms](wikipedia://en/221519) $h(-,Z) = d\Delta$. The Jacobian matrix will have zero determinant if, and only if, $d\Delta$ is [degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_form) as a one-form, i.e. $d_X\Delta = 0$ for all tangent vectors *X*. Since $h(-,Z) = d\Delta$ it follows that $d\Delta$ is degenerate if, and only if, $h(-,Z)$ is degenerate. Since *h* is a non-degenerate two-form it follows that *Z = 0*. Notice that since *M* has a non-degenerate second fundamental form it follows that *h* is a non-degenerate two-form. Since *Z = 0* the set of ambient points *x* for which the restricted function $\Delta : \{\mathbf{x}\} \times M \to \mathbb{R}$ has a singularity at some *p* is the affine normal line to *M* at *p*.

To compute the Hessian matrix, consider the differential two-form $(X,Y) \mapsto d_Y(d_X\Delta)$. This is the two-form whose matrix representation is the Hessian matrix. It has already been seen that $h(X,Z) = -d_X\Delta$ and that $d_Y(d_X\Delta) = -d_Y(h(X,Z)).$ What remains is

$$(X,Y) \mapsto -d_Y(h(X,Z)) = -(\nabla_Yh)(X,Z) - h(\nabla_YX,Z) - h(X,\nabla_YZ).$$

Now assume that Δ has a singularity at *p*, i.e. Z = 0, then we have the two-form

$$(X,Y) \mapsto - h(X,\nabla_YZ).$$

It also has been seen that $\nabla_XZ = (\Delta S - I)X$, and so the two-form becomes

$$(X,Y) \mapsto h(X,(I- \Delta S)Y).$$

This is degenerate as a two-form if, and only if, there exists non-zero *X* for which it is zero for all *Y*. Since *h* is non-degenerate it must be that $\det(I- \Delta S) = 0$ and $Y \in \ker(I- \Delta S)$. So the singularity is degenerate if, and only if, the ambient point *x* lies on the affine normal line to *p* and the reciprocal of its distance from *p* is an eigenvalue of *S*, i.e. points $\mathbf{x} = p + t\mathbf{A}$ where 1/*t* is an eigenvalue of *S*. The affine focal set!

## Singular points {#Singular_points}

The affine focal set can be the following:

$$\{ p + t \mathbf{A}(p) : p \in M, \det(I - tS) = 0\} \ .$$

To find the singular points, simply differentiate *p + tA* in some tangent direction *X*:

$$D_X(p + t \mathbf{A}) = (I-tS)X + d_Xt \mathbf{A}.$$

The affine focal set is singular if, and only if, there exists non-zero *X* such that $D_X(p + t \mathbf{A}) = 0$, i.e. if, and only if, *X* is an eigenvector of *S* and the derivative of *t* in that direction is zero. This means that the derivative of an affine [principal curvature](wikipedia://en/2072472) in its own affine [principal direction](wikipedia://en/2072472) is zero.

## Local structure {#Local_structure}

Standard ideas can be used in singularity theory to classify, up to local diffeomorphism, the affine focal set. If the family of affine distance functions can be shown to be a certain kind of family then the local structure is known. The family of affine distance functions should be a [versal unfolding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unfolding_%28geometry%29) of the singularities which arise.

The affine focal set of a [plane curve](wikipedia://en/971691) will [generically](wikipedia://en/12886758) consist of smooth pieces of curve and ordinary [cusp](wikipedia://en/6851758) points (semi-cubical parabolae).

The affine focal set of a surface in three-space will generically consist of smooth pieces of surface, [cuspidal cylinder points](wikipedia://en/6851758) ($A_3$), [swallowtail points](wikipedia://en/434288#Swallowtail_catastrophe) ($A_4$), [purse points](wikipedia://en/434288#Hyperbolic_umbilic_catastrophe) ($D_4^+$), and [pyramid points](wikipedia://en/434288#Elliptic_umbilic_catastrophe) ($D_4^-$). The $A_k$ and $D_k$ series are as in [Arnold's](wikipedia://en/32490) [list](wikipedia://en/434288#Arnold's_notation).

The question of the local structure in much higher dimension is of great interest. For example, it is possible to construct a discrete list of singularity types (up to local diffeomorphism). In much higher dimensions, no such discrete list can be constructed, as there are [functional moduli](wikipedia://en/964161).
