# Transform theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Transform theory"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **transform theory** is the study of transforms, which relate a function in one domain to another function in a second domain. The essence of transform theory is that by a suitable choice of [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) a problem may be simplified—or *diagonalized* as in [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713).

:::

Main examples of transforms that are both well known and widely applicable include [integral transforms](wikipedia://en/430912)[^o2] such as the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247), the [fractional Fourier Transform](wikipedia://en/1103773),[^o3] the [Laplace transform](wikipedia://en/18610), and [linear canonical transformations](wikipedia://en/4415205).[^o4] These transformations are used in [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324), [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics), and [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202).

## Spectral theory {#Spectral_theory}

In [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713), the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495) says that if *A* is an *n*×*n* [self-adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-adjoint) matrix, there is an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) of [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) of *A*. This implies that *A* is [diagonalizable](wikipedia://en/246325).

Furthermore, each [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) is [real](wikipedia://en/20646438).
