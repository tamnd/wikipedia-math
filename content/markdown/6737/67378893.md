::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Spirolateral"}

In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), a **spirolateral** is a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) created by a sequence of fixed vertex [internal angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_angle) and sequential edge lengths 1,2,3,...,*n* which repeat until the figure closes. The number of repeats needed is called its cycles.[^o3] A *simple spirolateral* has only positive angles. A simple spiral approximates of a portion of an [archimedean spiral](wikipedia://en/61559). A *general spirolateral* allows positive and negative angles.

:::

A *spirolateral* which completes in one [turn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_%28geometry%29) is a [simple polygon](wikipedia://en/1059530), while requiring more than 1 turn is a [star polygon](wikipedia://en/313303) and must be self-crossing.[^o9] A simple spirolateral can be an equangular simple polygon \<*p*> with *p* vertices, or an equiangular star polygon \<*p*/*q*> with *p* vertices and *q* turns.

Spirolaterals were invented and named by Frank C. Odds as a teenager in 1962, as *square spirolaterals* with 90° angles, drawn on [graph paper](wikipedia://en/293606). In 1970, Odds discovered *triangular and hexagonal spirolateral*, with 60° and 120° angles, can be drawn on isometric[^o6] (triangular) graph paper.[^o4] Odds wrote to [Martin Gardner](wikipedia://en/20435) who encouraged him to publish the results in *Mathematics Teacher*[^o5] in 1973.[^o6]

The process can be represented in [turtle graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turtle_graphics), alternating turn angle and move forward instructions, but limiting the turn to a fixed rational angle.[^o9]

The smallest [golygon](wikipedia://en/4303848) is a spirolateral, 7<sub>90°</sub><sup>4</sup>, made with 7 right angles, and length 4 follow concave turns. [Golygons](wikipedia://en/4303848) are different in that they must close with a single sequence 1,2,3,..*n*, while a spirolateral will repeat that sequence until it closes.

A **simple spirolateral** has turns all the same direction.[^o9] It is denoted by ***n*<sub>θ</sub>**, where *n* is the number of sequential [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) edge lengths and θ is the [internal angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_angle), as any [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) [divisor](wikipedia://en/49492) of 360°. Sequential edge lengths can be expressed explicitly as **(1,2,...,*n*)<sub>θ</sub>**.

Note: The angle θ can be confusing because it represents the internal angle, while the supplementary [turn angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_angle) can make more sense. These two angles are the same for 90°.

This defines an [equiangular polygon](wikipedia://en/2476494) of the form \<*kp*/*kq*>, where angle θ = 180(1−2*q*/*p*), with *k* = *n*/*d*, and *d* = [gcd](wikipedia://en/12354)(*n*,*p*). If *d* = *n*, the pattern never closes. Otherwise it has *kp* vertices and *kq* [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_%28polygon%29). The cyclic symmetry of a simple spirolateral is *p*/*d*-fold.

A [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306), {*p*} is a [special case](wikipedia://en/31308010) of a spirolateral, 1<sub>180(1−2/*p*)°</sub>. A [regular star polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_star_polygon), {*p*/*q*}, is a [special case](wikipedia://en/31308010) of a spirolateral, 1<sub>180(1−2*q*/*p*)°</sub>. An [isogonal polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isogonal_polygon), is a special case spirolateral, 2<sub>180(1−2/*p*)°</sub> or 2<sub>180(1−2*q*/*p*)°</sub>.

A **general spirolateral** can turn left or right.[^o9] It is denoted by ***n*<sub>θ</sub><sup>*a*<sub>1</sub>,...,*a*<sub>*k*</sub></sup>**, where *a*<sub>*i*</sub> are indices with negative or concave angles. For example, 2<sub>60°</sub><sup>2</sup> is a [crossed rectangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossed_rectangle) with ±60° internal angles, bending left or right.

An **unexpected closed spirolateral** returns to the first vertex on a single cycle. Only general spirolaterals may not close. A [golygon](wikipedia://en/4303848) is a **regular unexpected closed spirolateral** that closes from the expected direction. An **irregular unexpected closed spirolateral** is one that returns to the first point but from the wrong direction. For example 7<sub>90°</sub><sup>4</sup>. It takes 4 cycles to return to the start in the correct direction.[^o9]

A **modern spirolateral**, also called a [loop-de-loops](https://recipesforpi.wordpress.com/2015/09/28/loop-de-loops-and-a-contest/)[^o11] by Educator Anna Weltman, is denoted by **(*i*<sub>1</sub>,...,*i*<sub>*n*</sub>)<sub>θ</sub>**, allowing any sequence of integers as the edge lengths, *i*<sub>1</sub> to *i*<sub>*n*</sub>.[^o12] For example, (2,3,4)<sub>90°</sub> has edge lengths 2,3,4 repeating. Opposite direction turns can be given a negative integer edge length. For example, a crossed rectangle can be given as (1,2,−1,−2)<sub>θ</sub>.

An **open spirolateral** never closes. A simple spirolateral, *n*<sub>θ</sub>, never closes if *n*θ is a [multiple](wikipedia://en/3912709) of 360°, gcd(*p*,*n*) = *p*. A *general spirolateral* can also be open if half of the angles are positive, half negative.

## Closure {#Closure}

The number of cycles it takes to close a *spirolateral*, *n*<sub>θ</sub>, with *k* opposite turns can be computed like so. Define *p* and *q* such that *p*/*q*=360/(180-*θ*). if the fraction (*p*-2*q*)(*n*-2*k*)/2*p* is reduced fully to *a*/*b,* then the figure repeats after *b* cycles, and complete *a* total [turns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_%28geometry%29). If *b*=1, the figure never closes.[^o3]

Explicitly, the number of cycles is 2*p*/*d*, where d=[gcd](wikipedia://en/12354)((*p*-2*q*)(*n*-2*k*),2*p*). If *d*=2*p*, it closes on 1 cycle or never.

The number of cycles can be seen as the [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096) order of the spirolateral.
