# Parker Hitt

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Parker Hitt"}

**Colonel Parker Hitt** (August 27, 1878 – March 2, 1971) was a United States Army officer and a pioneer in the field of [cryptology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptology). Often cited as the "father of modern American military cryptology," Hitt authored the first practical work on military ciphers in the United States in over a century and invented foundational cryptographic devices that remained in use through [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II).[^o3][^o4]

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Parker Hitt was born in [Indianapolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indianapolis), Indiana, on August 27, 1878.[^o3] He attended [Purdue University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_University) to study civil engineering but dropped out during his senior year in 1898 to enlist for service in the [Spanish–American War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish–American_War).[^o4][^o6] He served as a sergeant in the 2nd U.S. Volunteer Engineers in [Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuba) before being commissioned as a second lieutenant in the 22nd Infantry in 1899.[^o3][^o6]

## Military career {#Military_career}

Hitt's early military career included postings in the [Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippines), [California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California), and [Alaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska).[^o3] In 1911, he attended the Signal Corps School at [Fort Leavenworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Leavenworth), where his technical aptitude led to an appointment as an instructor.[^o3][^o4] Between 1912 and 1915, he taught courses on codes, ciphers, and radio theory, significantly influencing the Army's communication doctrine.[^o6][^o4]

During the [Punitive Expedition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pancho_Villa_Expedition) in 1916, Hitt and his wife, [Genevieve Young Hitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genevieve_Young_Hitt), solved intercepted Mexican government communications.[^o4][^o8] Following the U.S. entry into [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I), General [John J. Pershing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_J._Pershing) selected Hitt for his staff in the [American Expeditionary Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Expeditionary_Forces) (AEF).[^o3] Hitt served as a Senior Signal Officer, overseeing the development of the "Radio Service Code" and supervising the coding and decoding of all AEF messages until a permanent headquarters was established in Paris.[^o3][^o6] He was also instrumental in the employment of American female telephone operators, popularly known as the "[Hello Girls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hello_Girls)," to manage switchboards for the First Army.[^o3][^o9]

Hitt retired as a colonel in 1928 but was recalled to active duty in 1940 at the age of 62.[^o3][^o6] During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), he served as the Signal Officer for the Fifth Corps Area at Fort Hayes, Ohio, until his final retirement in 1944.[^o3][^o8]

## Contributions to American cryptology {#Contributions_to_American_cryptology}

Hitt is credited with modernizing American cryptanalysis through his teaching and inventions. He famously stated that success in cryptanalysis relied on four factors: "perseverance, careful methods of analysis, intuition, and luck."[^o11]

### *Manual for the Solution of Military Ciphers* {#Manual_for_the_Solution_of_Military_Ciphers}

In 1916, Hitt published his seminal work, *Manual for the Solution of Military Ciphers*. It was the first practical U.S. instructional text on cryptanalysis in a century.[^o3][^o6] The manual provided the foundation for training American cryptologists during World War I and was cited by [William F. Friedman](wikipedia://en/175115) as the work that "launched" his career in the science.[^o13]

### Inventions {#Inventions}

Finding the official Army field cipher insecure, Hitt proposed mechanical alternatives:

- **The Cylinder Cipher:** In 1912, Hitt built a prototype cylindrical device. This design was later refined by Major [Joseph Mauborgne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Mauborgne) into the [M-94](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M-94), which the Army adopted in 1922 and used for over two decades.[^o15][^o16]
- **The Sliding Strip Cipher:** Hitt developed a flat version of the cylinder device using alphabetic strips. This evolved into the [M-138-A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M-138-A), used extensively by the [State Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_State) and [U.S. Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Navy) during the 1930s and World War II.[^o3][^o15]

While working for [IT&T](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITT_Inc.) in the 1930s, Hitt invented a teletypewriter cipher machine. Legally obtained details of his scrambler design were reportedly utilized by the German Lorenz Company to develop the [Lorenz SZ (Tunny)](wikipedia://en/301173) machine used by Germany in World War II.[^o6][^o8]

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

In 1911, Hitt married [Genevieve Young Hitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genevieve_Young_Hitt). Genevieve became the first woman to break ciphers for the U.S. government while working as a paid codebreaker for the Army's Southern Department.[^o3][^o18] Their daughter, Mary Lueise Hitt, also served as a "code girl" and teleprinter operator during World War II.[^o4][^o8]

Hitt spent his final years on a farm in [Front Royal, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Front_Royal,_Virginia), where he raised dogs. He died on March 2, 1971, at the age of 92.[^o6] His daughter recalled his modest final words as, "I should have had more dogs."[^o3][^o6]

## Legacy {#Legacy}

Hitt was posthumously inducted into the [Military Intelligence Hall of Fame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Intelligence_Hall_of_Fame) in 1988 and the [NSA/CSS Cryptologic Hall of Honor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NSA_Hall_of_Honor) in 2011.[^o3][^o20] **Hitt Hall** at [Fort Huachuca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Huachuca) was named in his honor in 1995.[^o3] During the 1930s, he was a key mentor to the [American Cryptogram Association](wikipedia://en/3793866) (ACA), providing technical advice to authors like [Helen Fouché Gaines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_Fouché_Gaines).[^o4][^o8]
