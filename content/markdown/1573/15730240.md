# Haller index

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Haller index"}

*Image: Haller index PE.jpg.* A CT scan showing a 3.58 index

The **Haller index**, created in 1987 by [J. Alex Haller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Alex_Haller), S. S. Kramer, and S. A. Lietman,[^o2] is a mathematical relationship that exists in a human [chest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chest) section observed with a [CT scan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CT_scan). It is defined as the [ratio](wikipedia://en/87837) of the transverse diameter (the horizontal distance of the inside of the [ribcage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribcage)) and the anteroposterior diameter (the shortest distance between the [vertebrae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertebrae) and [sternum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_sternum)).[^o3]

:::

$$\ HI = \frac {\text{distance 1}}{\text{distance 2}}$$

where:

**HI** is the Haller Index

*distance 1* is the distance of the inside ribcage (at the level of maximum deformity or at the lower third of the sternum)

*distance 2* is the distance between the sternal notch and vertebrae.

More recent studies show that simple [chest x-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chest_x-ray) are just as effective as CT scans for calculating the Haller index and recommend replacing CT scans with CXR to reduce radiation exposure in all but gross deformities.[^o4][^o5][^o6]

A normal Haller index should be about 2.5. Chest wall deformities such as [pectus excavatum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pectus_excavatum) can cause the sternum to invert, thus increasing the index.[^o7][^o8] In severe asymmetric cases, where the sternum dips below the level of the vertebra, the index can be a negative value.[^o9]
