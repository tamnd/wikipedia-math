# Commensurability (group theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Commensurability"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), two groups are **commensurable** if they differ only by a finite amount, in a precise sense. The **commensurator** of a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) is another subgroup, related to the [normalizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalizer).

:::

## Abstract commensurability {#Abstract_commensurability}

Two [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) *G*<sub>1</sub> and *G*<sub>2</sub> are said to be (**abstractly**) **commensurable** if there are subgroups *H*<sub>1</sub> ⊂ *G*<sub>1</sub> and *H*<sub>2</sub> ⊂ *G*<sub>2</sub> of [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) [index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_%28group_theory%29) such that *H*<sub>1</sub> is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to *H*<sub>2</sub>.[^o3] For example:

- A group is finite if and only if it is commensurable with the trivial group.
- Any two finitely generated [free groups](wikipedia://en/59735) on at least 2 generators are commensurable with each other.[^o4] The group [*SL*(2,**Z**)](wikipedia://en/325019) is also commensurable with these free groups.
- Any two [surface groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_group) of [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) at least 2 are commensurable with each other.

In [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164), a [finitely generated group](wikipedia://en/963042) is viewed as a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) using the [word metric](wikipedia://en/1289716). If two groups are (abstractly) commensurable, then they are [quasi-isometric](wikipedia://en/12747259).[^o5] It has been fruitful to ask when the converse holds.

## Commensurability of subgroups {#Commensurability_of_subgroups}

A different but related notion is used for subgroups of a given group. Namely, two subgroups Γ<sub>1</sub> and Γ<sub>2</sub> of a group *G* are said to be **commensurable** if the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) Γ<sub>1</sub> ∩ Γ<sub>2</sub> is of finite index in both Γ<sub>1</sub> and Γ<sub>2</sub>. Clearly this implies that Γ<sub>1</sub> and Γ<sub>2</sub> are abstractly commensurable.

Example: for nonzero [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) *a* and *b*, the subgroup of **R** [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by *a* is commensurable with the subgroup generated by *b* if and only if the real numbers *a* and *b* are [commensurable](wikipedia://en/753944), meaning that *a*/*b* belongs to the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) **Q**. If *a* and *b* are commensurable, with smallest positive common integer multiple *c*, then $\langle a \rangle \cap \langle b \rangle = \langle c \rangle$, which has index *c*/*\|a|* in $\langle a \rangle$ and *c*/*\|b|* in $\langle b \rangle$.

## Related notions {#Related_notions}

### In linear algebra {#In_linear_algebra}

There is an analogous notion in linear algebra: two [linear subspaces](wikipedia://en/56357) *S* and *T* of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V* are **commensurable** if the intersection *S* ∩ *T* has finite [codimension](wikipedia://en/525149) in both *S* and *T*.

### In topology {#In_topology}

Two [path-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path-connected) [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) are sometimes called *commensurable* if they have [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) finite-sheeted [covering spaces](wikipedia://en/250323). Depending on the type of space under consideration, one might want to use [homotopy equivalences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_equivalence) or [diffeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/8564) instead of homeomorphisms in the definition. By the relation between covering spaces and the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004), commensurable spaces have commensurable fundamental groups.

Example: the [Gieseking manifold](wikipedia://en/23010120) is commensurable with the complement of the [figure-eight knot](wikipedia://en/59565); these are both [noncompact](wikipedia://en/6042) [hyperbolic 3-manifolds](wikipedia://en/1237700) of finite volume. On the other hand, there are infinitely many different commensurability classes of compact hyperbolic 3-manifolds, and also of noncompact hyperbolic 3-manifolds of finite volume.[^o10]

## Commensurators {#Commensurators}

The **commensurator** of a subgroup Γ of a group *G*, denoted Comm<sub>*G*</sub>(Γ), is the set of elements *g* of *G* that such that the [conjugate](wikipedia://en/61839) subgroup *g*Γ*g*<sup>−1</sup> is commensurable with Γ.[^o12] In other words,

$$\operatorname{Comm}_G(\Gamma)=\{g\in G : g\Gamma g^{-1} \cap \Gamma \text{ has finite index in both } \Gamma \text{ and } g\Gamma g^{-1}\}.$$

This is a subgroup of *G* that contains the [normalizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalizer) N<sub>*G*</sub>(Γ) (and hence contains Γ).

For example, the commensurator of the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) *SL*(*n*,**Z**) in *SL*(*n*,**R**) contains *SL*(*n*,**Q**). In particular, the commensurator of *SL*(*n*,**Z**) in *SL*(*n*,**R**) is [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in *SL*(*n*,**R**). More generally, [Grigory Margulis](wikipedia://en/1104948) showed that the commensurator of a [lattice](wikipedia://en/15782871) Γ in a [semisimple Lie group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_Lie_group) *G* is dense in *G* if and only if Γ is an [arithmetic subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_subgroup) of *G*.[^o13]

## Abstract commensurators {#Abstract_commensurators}

The **abstract commensurator** of a group $G$, denoted $\text{Comm}(G)$, is the group of equivalence classes of isomorphisms $\phi : H \to K$, where $H$ and $K$ are finite index subgroups of $G$, under composition.[^o15] Elements of $\text{Comm}(G)$ are called **commensurators** of $G$.

If $G$ is a connected [semisimple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_Lie_group) [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) not isomorphic to $\text{PSL}_2(\mathbb{R})$, with trivial center and no compact factors, then by the [Mostow rigidity theorem](wikipedia://en/2782164), the abstract commensurator of any irreducible [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) $\Gamma \leq G$ is linear. Moreover, if $\Gamma$ is arithmetic, then Comm$(\Gamma)$ is virtually isomorphic to a dense subgroup of $G$, otherwise Comm$(\Gamma)$ is virtually isomorphic to $\Gamma$.
