# Discrete Morse theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Discrete Morse theory"}

**Discrete Morse theory** is a [combinatorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial) adaptation of [Morse theory](wikipedia://en/348560) developed by [Robin Forman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Forman) and [Kenneth Brown](wikipedia://en/41104429).[^o2] The theory has various practical applications in diverse fields of [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969) and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), such as [configuration spaces](wikipedia://en/54981574),[^o3] [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) computation,[^o4][^o5] [denoising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denoising),[^o6] [mesh compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lossless_data_compression),[^o7] and [topological data analysis](wikipedia://en/17740009).[^o8]

:::

## Notation regarding CW complexes {#Notation_regarding_CW_complexes}

Let $X$ be a [CW complex](wikipedia://en/353022) and denote by $\mathcal{X}$ its set of cells. Define the *incidence function* $\kappa\colon\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{Z}$ in the following way: given two cells $\sigma$ and $\tau$ in $\mathcal{X}$, let $\kappa(\sigma,~\tau)$ be the [degree](wikipedia://en/18203720) of the [attaching map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attaching_map) from the boundary of $\sigma$ to $\tau$. The [boundary operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_operator) is the endomorphism $\partial$ of the [free abelian group](wikipedia://en/247296) generated by $\mathcal{X}$ defined by

$$\partial(\sigma) = \sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{X}}\kappa(\sigma,\tau)\tau.$$

It is a defining property of boundary operators that $\partial\circ\partial \equiv 0$. In more axiomatic definitions[^o5] one can find the requirement that $\forall \sigma,\tau^{\prime} \in \mathcal{X}$

$$\sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{X}} \kappa(\sigma,\tau) \kappa(\tau,\tau^{\prime}) = 0$$

which is a consequence of the above definition of the boundary operator and the requirement that $\partial\circ\partial \equiv 0$.

## Discrete Morse functions {#Discrete_Morse_functions}

A [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued function $\mu\colon\mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a *discrete Morse function* if it satisfies the following two properties:

1. For any cell $\sigma \in \mathcal{X}$, the number of cells $\tau \in \mathcal{X}$ in the boundary of $\sigma$ which satisfy $\mu(\sigma) \leq \mu(\tau)$ is at most one.
1. For any cell $\sigma \in \mathcal{X}$, the number of cells $\tau \in \mathcal{X}$ containing $\sigma$ in their boundary which satisfy $\mu(\sigma) \geq \mu(\tau)$ is at most one.

It can be shown[^o11] that the cardinalities in the two conditions cannot both be one simultaneously for a fixed cell $\sigma$, provided that $\mathcal{X}$ is a *regular* CW complex. In this case, each cell $\sigma \in \mathcal{X}$ can be paired with at most one exceptional cell $\tau \in \mathcal{X}$: either a boundary cell with larger $\mu$ value, or a co-boundary cell with smaller $\mu$ value. The cells which have no pairs, i.e., whose function values are strictly higher than their boundary cells **and** strictly lower than their co-boundary cells are called *critical* cells. Thus, a discrete Morse function partitions the CW complex into three distinct cell collections: $\mathcal{X} = \mathcal{A} \sqcup \mathcal{K} \sqcup \mathcal{Q}$, where:

1. $\mathcal{A}$ denotes the **critical** cells which are unpaired,
1. $\mathcal{K}$ denotes cells which are paired with boundary cells, and
1. $\mathcal{Q}$ denotes cells which are paired with co-boundary cells.

By construction, there is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) of [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) between $k$-dimensional cells in $\mathcal{K}$ and the $(k-1)$-dimensional cells in $\mathcal{Q}$, which can be denoted by $p^k\colon\mathcal{K}^k \to \mathcal{Q}^{k-1}$ for each [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $k$. It is an additional technical requirement that for each $K \in \mathcal{K}^k$, the degree of the attaching map from the boundary of $K$ to its paired cell $p^k(K) \in \mathcal{Q}$ is a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) in the underlying [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) of $\mathcal{X}$. For instance, over the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $\mathbb{Z}$, the only allowed values are $\pm 1$. This technical requirement is guaranteed, for instance, when one assumes that $\mathcal{X}$ is a regular CW complex over $\mathbb{Z}$.

The fundamental result of discrete Morse theory establishes that the CW complex $\mathcal{X}$ is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) on the level of [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) to a new complex $\mathcal{A}$ consisting of only the critical cells. The paired cells in $\mathcal{K}$ and $\mathcal{Q}$ describe *gradient paths* between adjacent critical cells which can be used to obtain the boundary operator on $\mathcal{A}$. Some details of this construction are provided in the next section.

## The Morse complex {#The_Morse_complex}

A *gradient path* is a sequence of paired cells

$$\rho = (Q_1, K_1, Q_2, K_2, \ldots, Q_M, K_M)$$

satisfying $Q_m = p(K_m)$ and $\kappa(K_m,~Q_{m+1}) \neq 0$. The *index* of this gradient path is defined to be the integer

$$\nu(\rho) = \frac{\prod_{m=1}^{M-1}-\kappa(K_m,Q_{m+1})}{\prod_{m=1}^{M}\kappa(K_m,Q_m)}.$$

The division here makes sense because the incidence between paired cells must be $\pm 1$. Note that by construction, the values of the discrete Morse function $\mu$ must decrease across $\rho$. The path $\rho$ is said to *connect* two critical cells $A,A' \in \mathcal{A}$ if $\kappa(A,Q_1) \neq 0 \neq \kappa(K_M,A')$. This relationship may be expressed as $A \stackrel{\rho}{\to} A'$. The *multiplicity* of this connection is defined to be the integer $m(\rho) = \kappa(A,Q_1)\cdot\nu(\rho)\cdot\kappa(K_M,A')$. Finally, the **Morse boundary operator** on the critical cells $\mathcal{A}$ is defined by

$$\Delta(A) = \kappa(A,A') + \sum_{A \stackrel{\rho}{\to} A'}m(\rho) A'$$

where the sum is taken over all gradient path connections from $A$ to $A'$.

## Basic results {#Basic_results}

Many of the familiar results from continuous Morse theory apply in the discrete setting.

### The Morse inequalities {#The_Morse_inequalities}

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a Morse complex associated to the CW complex $\mathcal{X}$. The number $m_q = |\mathcal{A}_q|$ of $q$-cells in $\mathcal{A}$ is called the $q$-th *Morse number*. Let $\beta_q$ denote the $q$-th [Betti number](wikipedia://en/346262) of $\mathcal{X}$. Then, for any $N > 0$, the following inequalities[^o15] hold

$m_N \geq \beta_N$, and

$$m_N - m_{N-1} + \dots \pm m_0 \geq \beta_N - \beta_{N-1} + \dots \pm \beta_0$$

Moreover, the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) $\chi(\mathcal{X})$ of $\mathcal{X}$ satisfies

$$\chi(\mathcal{X}) = m_0 - m_1 + \dots \pm m_{\dim \mathcal{X}}$$

### Discrete Morse homology and homotopy type {#Discrete_Morse_homology_and_homotopy_type}

Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a regular CW complex with boundary operator $\partial$ and a discrete Morse function $\mu\colon\mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$. Let $\mathcal{A}$ be the associated Morse complex with Morse boundary operator $\Delta$. Then, there is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397)[^o17] of [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) groups

$$H_*(\mathcal{X},\partial) \simeq H_*(\mathcal{A},\Delta),$$

and similarly for the [homotopy groups](wikipedia://en/297506).

## Applications {#Applications}

Discrete Morse theory finds its application in molecular shape analysis,[^o20] skeletonization of digital images/volumes,[^o21] graph reconstruction from noisy data,[^o22] denoising noisy point clouds[^o23] and analysing [lithic tools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone_tool) in [archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeology).[^o26]
