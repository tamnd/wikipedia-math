# Positive real numbers

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Positive real numbers"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of **positive real numbers**, $\mathbb{R}_{>0} = \left\{ x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x > 0 \right\},$ is the subset of those [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) that are greater than zero. The **non-negative real numbers**, $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} = \left\{ x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x \geq 0 \right\},$ also include zero. Although the symbols $\mathbb{R}_{+}$ and $\mathbb{R}^{+}$ are ambiguously used for either of these, the notation $\mathbb{R}_{+}$ or $\mathbb{R}^{+}$ for $\left\{ x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x \geq 0 \right\}$ and $\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}$ or $\mathbb{R}^{+}_{*}$ for $\left\{ x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x > 0 \right\}$ has also been widely employed, is aligned with the practice in algebra of denoting the exclusion of the zero element with a star, and should be understandable to most practicing mathematicians.[^o2]

:::

In a [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is identified with the **positive real axis** (or **positive real half-axis**), and is usually drawn as a horizontal [ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28geometry%29). This ray is used as reference in the [polar form of a complex number](wikipedia://en/5826#Polar_form). The real positive axis corresponds to [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $z = |z| \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi},$ with [argument](wikipedia://en/14699765) $\varphi = 0.$

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The set $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under addition, multiplication, and division. It inherits a [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) from the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) and, thus, has the structure of a multiplicative [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) or of an additive [topological semigroup](wikipedia://en/17359213).

For a given positive real number $x,$ the [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $\left\{x^n\right\}$ of its integral powers has three different fates: When $x \in (0, 1),$ the [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602) is zero; when $x = 1,$ the sequence is constant; and when $x > 1,$ the sequence is [unbounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unbounded_set).

$\mathbb{R}_{>0} = (0,1) \cup \{ 1 \} \cup (1,\infty)$ and the [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) function exchanges the intervals. The functions [floor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_function), $\operatorname{floor} : [ 1 , \infty ) \to \mathbb{N},\, x \mapsto \lfloor x \rfloor,$ and [excess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sawtooth_function), $\operatorname{excess} : [ 1 , \infty ) \to (0,1),\, x \mapsto x - \lfloor x \rfloor,$ have been used to describe an element $x \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ as a [continued fraction](wikipedia://en/739199) $\left[ n_0; n_1, n_2, \ldots\right],$ which is a sequence of integers obtained from the floor function after the excess has been reciprocated. For rational $x,$ the sequence terminates with an exact fractional expression of $x,$ and for [quadratic irrational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_irrational) $x,$ the sequence becomes a [periodic continued fraction](wikipedia://en/10875031).

The ordered set $\left(\mathbb{R}_{>0}, >\right)$ forms a [total order](wikipedia://en/30330) but is not a [well-ordered set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-ordered_set). The [doubly infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_infinite) [geometric progression](wikipedia://en/23474670) $10^n,$ where $n$ is an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563), lies entirely in $\left(\mathbb{R}_{>0}, >\right)$ and serves to section it for access. $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ forms a **ratio scale**, the highest [level of measurement](wikipedia://en/821148). Elements may be written in [scientific notation](wikipedia://en/59715) as $a \times 10^b,$ where $1 \leq a < 10$ and $b$ is the integer in the doubly infinite progression, and is called the [decade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decade_%28log_scale%29). In the study of physical magnitudes, the order of decades provides positive and negative ordinals referring to an ordinal scale implicit in the ratio scale.

In the study of [classical groups](wikipedia://en/10172878), for every $n \in \mathbb{N},$ the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) gives a map from $n \times n$ matrices over the reals to the real numbers: $\mathrm{M}(n, \mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}.$ Restricting to invertible matrices gives a map from the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) to non-zero real numbers: $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}^\times.$ Restricting to matrices with a positive determinant gives the map $\operatorname{GL}^+(n, \mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}_{> 0}$; interpreting the image as a [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) by the [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) $\operatorname{SL}(n, \mathbb{R}) \triangleleft \operatorname{GL}^+(n, \mathbb{R}),$ called the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823), expresses the positive reals as a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945).

## Ratio scale {#Ratio_scale}

Among the [levels of measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levels_of_measurement), the **ratio scale** provides the finest detail. The [division](wikipedia://en/53696) function takes a value of one when [numerator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerator) and [denominator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denominator) are equal. Other ratios are compared to one by logarithms, often [common logarithm](wikipedia://en/174482) using base 10. The ratio scale then segments by [orders of magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orders_of_magnitude) used in science and technology, expressed in various [units of measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Units_of_measurement).

An early expression of ratio scale was articulated geometrically by [Eudoxus](wikipedia://en/48388): "it was ... in geometrical language that the general theory of [proportion](wikipedia://en/81863) of Eudoxus was developed, which is equivalent to a theory of positive real numbers."[^o7]

## Logarithmic measure {#Logarithmic_measure}

If $[a,b] \subseteq \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is an [interval](wikipedia://en/49172), then $\mu([a,b]) = \log(b / a) = \log b - \log a$ determines a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) on certain subsets of $\mathbb{R}_{>0},$ corresponding to the [pullback](wikipedia://en/9413508) of the usual [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on the real numbers under the logarithm: it is the length on the [logarithmic scale](wikipedia://en/164055). In fact, it is an [invariant measure](wikipedia://en/7635201) with respect to multiplication $[a,b] \to [az, bz]$ by a $z \in \mathbb{R}_{>0},$ just as the Lebesgue measure is invariant under addition. In the context of topological groups, this measure is an example of a [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645).

The utility of this measure is shown in its use for describing [stellar magnitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_magnitude) and noise levels in [decibels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decibel), among other applications of the [logarithmic scale](wikipedia://en/164055). For purposes of international standards [ISO 80000-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_80000-3), the dimensionless quantities are referred to as [levels](wikipedia://en/39999171).

## Applications {#Applications}

The non-negative reals serve as the [image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_of_a_function) for [metrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29), [norms](wikipedia://en/990534), and [measures](wikipedia://en/19873) in mathematics.

Including 0, the set $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ has a [semiring](wikipedia://en/371468) structure (0 being the [additive identity](wikipedia://en/4178225)), known as the [probability semiring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_semiring); taking logarithms (with a choice of base giving a [logarithmic unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_unit)) gives an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) with the [log semiring](wikipedia://en/37563991) (with 0 corresponding to $- \infty$), and its units (the finite numbers, excluding $- \infty$) correspond to the positive real numbers.

### Square {#Square}

Let $Q = \mathbb{R}_{> 0} \times \mathbb{R}_{> 0},$ the first quadrant of the Cartesian plane. The quadrant itself is divided into four parts by the line $L = \{(x,y): x = y \}$ and the standard hyperbola $H = \{(x,y): xy = 1 \}.$

The $L \cup H$ forms a trident while $L \cap H = (1, 1)$ is the central point. It is the identity element of two [one-parameter groups](wikipedia://en/754851) that intersect there:

$$\{\left\{\left(e^a, \ e^a\right): a \in R \right\}, \times \} \text{ on } L \quad \text{ and } \quad \{\left\{\left(e^a, \ e^{-a}\right): a \in R\right\},\times \} \text{ on } H.$$

Since $\mathbb{R}_{> 0}$ is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), $Q$ is a [direct product of groups](wikipedia://en/3706015). The one-parameter subgroups *L* and *H* in *Q* profile the activity in the product, and $L \times H$ is a resolution of the types of group action.

The realms of business and science abound in ratios, and any change in ratios draws attention. The study refers to [hyperbolic coordinates](wikipedia://en/1264398) in *Q*. Motion against the *L* axis indicates a change in the [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046) $\sqrt{xy},$ while a change along *H* indicates a new [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981).
