# Herzog–Schönheim conjecture

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Herzog–Schönheim conjecture"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Herzog–Schönheim conjecture** is a combinatorial problem in the area of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), posed by Marcel Herzog and Jochanan Schönheim in 1974.[^o2]

:::

Let $G$ be a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), and let

$$A=\{a_1G_1,\ \ldots,\ a_kG_k\}$$

be a finite system of left [cosets](wikipedia://en/98759) of [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) $G_1,\ldots,G_k$ of $G$.

Herzog and Schönheim conjectured that if $A$ forms a [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) of $G$ with $k>1$, then the (finite) indices $[G:G_1],\ldots,[G:G_k]$ cannot be distinct. In contrast, if repeated indices are allowed, then partitioning a group into cosets is easy: if $H$ is any subgroup of $G$ with [index](wikipedia://en/230581) $k=[G:H]<\infty$ then $G$ can be partitioned into $k$ left cosets of $H$.

## Subnormal subgroups {#Subnormal_subgroups}

In 2004, [Zhi-Wei Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhi-Wei_Sun) proved a special case of the Herzog–Schönheim conjecture in the case where $G_1,\ldots,G_k$ are [subnormal](wikipedia://en/2774415) in $G$.[^o4] A basic lemma in Sun's proof states that if $G_1,\ldots,G_k$ are subnormal and of finite index in $G$, then

$$\bigg[G:\bigcap_{i=1}^kG_i\bigg]\ \bigg|\ \prod_{i=1}^k[G:G_i]$$

and hence

$$P\bigg(\bigg[G:\bigcap_{i=1}^kG_i\bigg]\ \bigg) =\bigcup_{i=1}^kP([G:G_i]),$$

where $P(n)$ denotes the set of [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) [divisors](wikipedia://en/49492) of $n$.

## Mirsky–Newman theorem {#Mirsky–Newman_theorem}

When $G$ is the additive group $\mathbb{Z}$ of integers, the cosets of $G$ are the [arithmetic progressions](wikipedia://en/168389). In this case, the Herzog–Schönheim conjecture states that every [covering system](wikipedia://en/2297820), a family of arithmetic progressions that together cover all the integers, must either cover some integers more than once or include at least one pair of progressions that have the same difference as each other. This result was conjectured in 1950 by [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) and proved soon thereafter by [Leon Mirsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Mirsky) and [Donald J. Newman](wikipedia://en/10419510). However, Mirsky and Newman never published their proof. The same proof was also found independently by [Harold Davenport](wikipedia://en/991111) and [Richard Rado](wikipedia://en/614984).[^o7]

In 1970, a geometric coloring problem equivalent to the Mirsky–Newman theorem was given as Problem 8 in the Soviet mathematical olympiad for 10th grade: suppose that the vertices of a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) are colored in such a way that every color class itself forms the vertices of a regular polygon. Then, there exist two color classes that form congruent polygons.[^o7] Nobody in the contestant had solved the problem.[^o8]

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Newman-Znám theorem"}

### Newman-Znám theorem {#Newman-Znám_theorem}

[Štefan Znám](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Štefan_Znám) in 1968[^o11] and subsequently [Morris Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris_Newman) in 1971[^o12] proved a general form of Mirsky–Newman theorem, namely given a disjoint [covering system](wikipedia://en/2297820), suppose the maximum of the moduli $n$ occurs $l$ times, then $l \geq p$, the smallest prime dividing $n$. In 1986, an non-analytic proof of this general result was given.[^o13]
