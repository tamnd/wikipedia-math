# Malmquist bias

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Malmquist bias"}

The **Malmquist bias** is an effect in [observational astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_astronomy) which leads to the preferential detection of intrinsically bright objects. It was first described in 1922 by Swedish astronomer [Gunnar Malmquist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunnar_Malmquist) (1893–1982), who then greatly elaborated upon this work in 1925.[^o2][^o3] In statistics, this bias is referred to as a *[selection bias](wikipedia://en/394392)* or [*data censoring*](wikipedia://en/11548952). It affects the results in a [brightness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brightness)-limited [survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_survey), where stars below a certain apparent brightness cannot be included. Since observed [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stars) and [galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxies) appear dimmer when farther away, the brightness that is measured will fall off with distance until their brightness falls below the observational threshold. Objects which are more [luminous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminosity), or intrinsically brighter, can be observed at a greater distance, creating a false trend of increasing intrinsic brightness, and other related quantities, with distance. This effect has led to many spurious claims in the field of astronomy. Properly correcting for these effects has become an area of great focus.

:::

## Understanding the bias {#Understanding_the_bias}

### Magnitudes and brightness {#Magnitudes_and_brightness}

*Image: inverse square law.svg.* As light rays leave the star homogeneously in every direction, they fill a sphere whose radius grows with time. As the sphere grows, the surface area increases; however, the number of light rays stays the same. Following a patch on the surface of the sphere with area $A$, fewer light rays pass through that patch as the light travels further from the star.

In everyday life it is easy to see that light dims as it gets farther away. This can be seen with car headlights, candles, flashlights, and many other lit objects. This dimming follows the [inverse square law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_square_law), which states that the brightness of an object decreases as $\tfrac{1}{r^{2}}$, where $r$ is the distance between the observer and the object.

Starlight also follows the inverse square law. Light rays leave the [star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star) in equal amounts in all directions. The light rays create a sphere of light surrounding the star. As time progresses, the sphere grows as the light rays travel through space away from the star. While the sphere of light grows, the number of light rays stays the same. So, the amount of light per unit of surface area of the sphere (called [flux](wikipedia://en/43590) in astronomy) decreases with distance and therefore time. When observing a star, only the light rays that are in the given area being viewed can be detected. This is why a star appears dimmer the farther away it is.

If there are two stars with the same intrinsic brightness (called [luminosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminosity) in astronomy), each at a different distance, the closer star will appear brighter while the further will appear dimmer. In astronomy, the apparent brightness of a star, or any other luminous object, is called the [apparent magnitude](wikipedia://en/1962). The apparent magnitude depends on the intrinsic brightness (also called [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963)) of the object and its distance.

If all stars had the same luminosity, the distance from Earth to a particular star could be easily determined. However, stars have a wide range in luminosities. Therefore, it can be difficult to distinguish a very luminous star that is very far away from a less luminous star that is closer. This is why it is so hard to calculate the distance to astronomical objects.

### Source of the Malmquist bias {#Source_of_the_Malmquist_bias}

*Image: bias2.png.* In a volume of space filled with stars, the stars have a range of luminosities and have some average luminosity shown by the dashed blue line. However, the more distant, less luminous stars will not be seen. This lowest luminosity that can be seen at a given distance is depicted by the red curve. Any star below that curve will not be seen. The average luminosity of only the stars that will be seen will be higher, as shown by the dashed red line.

Typically, when looking at an area of sky filled with stars, only stars that are brighter than a limiting [apparent magnitude](wikipedia://en/1962) can be seen. As discussed above, the very luminous stars that are farther away will be seen, as well as luminous and faint stars that are closer. There will appear to be more luminous objects within a certain distance from Earth than faint objects. However, there are many more faint stars:[^o7] they simply cannot be seen because they are so dim. The bias towards luminous stars when observing a patch of sky affects calculations of the average [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963) and average distance to a group of stars. Because of the luminous stars that are at a further distance, it will appear as if our sample of stars is farther away than it actually is, and that each star is intrinsically brighter than it actually is. This effect is known as the Malmquist bias.[^o2]

When studying a sample of luminous objects, whether they be stars or [galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxies), it is important to correct for the bias towards the more luminous objects. There are many different methods that can be used to correct for the Malmquist bias as discussed below.

The Malmquist bias is not limited to luminosities. It affects any observational quantity whose detectability diminishes with distance.[^o8]

## Correction methods {#Correction_methods}

The ideal situation is to avoid this [bias](wikipedia://en/184099) from entering a data [survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_survey). However, [magnitude limited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limiting_magnitude) [surveys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_survey) are the simplest to perform, and other methods are difficult to put together, with their own uncertainties involved, and may be impossible for first observations of objects. As such, many different methods exist to attempt to correct the data, removing the [bias](wikipedia://en/184099) and allowing the [survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_survey) to be usable. The methods are presented in order of increasing difficulty, but also increasing accuracy and effectiveness.

### Limiting the sample {#Limiting_the_sample}

The simplest method of correction is to only use the non-biased portions of the data set, if any, and throw away the rest of the data.[^o11] Depending on the [limiting magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limiting_magnitude) selected, there may be a range of distances in the data set over which all objects of any possible [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963) could be seen. As such, this small subset of data should be free of the Malmquist bias. This is easily accomplished by cutting off the data at the edge of where the lowest [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963) objects would be hitting the [limiting magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limiting_magnitude). Unfortunately, this method would waste a great deal of good data, and would limit the analysis to nearby objects only, making it less than desirable. (Looking at the figure to the right, only the first fifth of the data in distance could be kept before a data point is lost to the bias.) Of course, this method assumes that distances are known with relatively good accuracy, which as mentioned before, is a difficult process in astronomy.

### Traditional correction {#Traditional_correction}

The first solution, proposed by Malmquist in his 1922 work, was to correct the calculated average [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963) ($\overline{M}$) of the sample back to the true average [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963) (*M*<sub>0</sub>).[^o2] The correction would be

$$\overline{\Delta M} = \overline{M}- M_0$$

To calculate the [bias](wikipedia://en/184099) correction, Malmquist and others following this method follow six main assumptions:[^o13]

1. There exists no [interstellar absorption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_medium), or that the stuff in space between stars (like gas and dust) is not affecting the light and absorbing parts of it. This assumes that the [brightness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brightness) is simply following the [inverse square law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_square_law), mentioned above.
1. The [luminosity function](wikipedia://en/13347172) (Φ) is independent of the distance (*r*). This basically just means that the universe is the same everywhere, and that stars will be similarly distributed somewhere else as they are here.
1. For a given area on the sky, or more specifically the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239), the spatial density of stars (*ρ*) depends only on distance. This assumes that there are the same number of stars in each direction, on average.
1. There is completeness, meaning the sample is complete and nothing is missed, to an [apparent magnitude limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limiting_magnitude) (*m*<sub>lim</sub>).
1. The [luminosity function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminosity_function) can be approximated as a [Gaussian function](wikipedia://en/245552), centered on an intrinsic mean [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963) *M*<sub>0</sub>.
1. Stars are of the same [spectral type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_type), with intrinsic mean [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963) *M*<sub>0</sub> and dispersion *σ*.

Obviously, this is a very ideal situation, with the final assumption being particularly troubling, but allows for an approximate correction of simple form. By integrating the [luminosity function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminosity_function) over all distances and all magnitudes brighter than *m*<sub>lim</sub>,

$\overline{\Delta M} = - \sigma^2 \frac{\operatorname{d} \ln A(m_\lim)}{\operatorname{d} m_\lim}$[^o2][^o13]

where A(m<sub>lim</sub>) is the total number of stars brighter than m<sub>lim</sub>. If the spatial distribution of stars can be assumed to be homogeneous, this relation is simplified even further, to the generally accepted form of

$\overline{\Delta M} = - 1.382\sigma^2$[^o2][^o13]

#### Multiple-band observation corrections {#Multiple-band_observation_corrections}

The traditional method assumes that the measurements of [apparent magnitude](wikipedia://en/1962) and the measurements from which distance is determined are from the same band, or predefined range, of wavelengths (e.g. the [H band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H_band_%28infrared%29), a range of [infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_astronomy) wavelengths from roughly about 1300–2000 [nanometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanometers)), and this leads to the correction form of *cσ*<sup>2</sup>, where *c* is some constant. Unfortunately, this is rarely the case, as many samples of objects are selected from one wavelength band but the distance is calculated from another. For example, astronomers frequently select [galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxies) from B-band catalogs, which are the most complete, and use these B band magnitudes, but the distances for the galaxies are calculated using the [Tully–Fisher relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tully–Fisher_relation) and the H band. When this happens, the variance is replaced by the [covariance](wikipedia://en/157059) between the scatter in the distance measurements and in the galaxy selection property (e.g. magnitude).[^o15]

### Volume weighting {#Volume_weighting}

Another fairly straightforward correction method is to use a [weighted mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weighted_mean) to properly account for the relative contributions at each magnitude. Since the objects at different [absolute magnitudes](wikipedia://en/1963) can be seen out to different distances, each point's contribution to the average [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963) or to the [luminosity function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminosity_function) can be weighted by 1/V<sub>max</sub>, where V<sub>max</sub> is the maximum volume over which the objects could have been seen. Brighter objects (that is, objects with smaller [absolute magnitudes](wikipedia://en/1963)) will have a larger volume over which they could have been detected, before falling under the threshold, and thus will be given less weight through this method since these bright objects will be more fully sampled.[^o17] The maximum volume can be approximated as a sphere with radius found from the [distance modulus](wikipedia://en/1395554), using the object's [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963) and the [limiting apparent magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limiting_magnitude).

However, there are two major complications to calculating V<sub>max</sub>. First is the completeness of the area covered in the sky, which is the percentage of the sky that the objects were taken from.[^o17] A full sky [survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_survey) would collect objects from the entire sphere, 4π [steradians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steradians), of sky but this is usually impractical, both from time constraints and geographical limitations (ground-based telescopes can only see a limited amount of sky due to the Earth being in the way). Instead, astronomers will generally look at a small patch or area of sky and then infer universal distributions by assuming that space is either [isotropic](wikipedia://en/14865), that it is generally the same in every direction, or is following a known distribution, such as that one will see more stars by looking toward the center of a galaxy than by looking directly away. Generally, the volume can be simply scaled down by the percentage actually viewed, giving the correct number of objects to volume relation. This effect could potentially be ignored in a single sample, all from the same [survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_survey), as the objects will basically all be altered by the same numerical factor, but it is incredibly important to account for in order to be able to compare between different surveys with different sky coverage.

The second complication is [cosmological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmology) concerns of [redshift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmological_redshift) and the [expanding universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expanding_universe), which must be considered when looking at distant objects. In these cases, the quantity of interest is the [comoving distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comoving_distance), which is a constant distance between two objects assuming that they are moving away from each other solely with the expansion of the universe, known as the [Hubble flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble_flow). In effect, this [comoving distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comoving_distance) is the object's separation if the universe's expansion were neglected, and it can be easily related to the actual distance by accounting for how it would have expanded. The [comoving distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comoving_distance) can be used to calculate the respective comoving volume as usual, or a relation between the actual and comoving volumes can also be easily established. If z is the object's [redshift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmological_redshift), relating to how far emitted light is shifted toward longer wavelengths as a result of the object moving away from us with the universal expansion, D<sub>A</sub> and V<sub>A</sub> are the actual distance and volume (or what would be measured today) and D<sub>C</sub> and V<sub>C</sub> are the [comoving distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comoving_distance) and volumes of interest, then

$$D_C = D_A (1 + z)$$

$V_C = V_A (1 + z)^3$[^o18]

A large downside of the volume weighting method is its sensitivity to [large-scale structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observable_universe), or parts of the universe with more or less objects than average, such as a [star cluster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_cluster) or a [void](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Void_%28astronomy%29).[^o19] Having very overdense or underdense regions of objects will cause an inferred change in our average [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963) and [luminosity function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminosity_function), according with the structure. This is a particular issue with the faint objects in calculating a luminosity function, as their smaller maximum volume means that a large-scale structure therein will have a large impact. Brighter objects with large maximum volumes will tend to average out and approach the correct value in spite of some large-scale structures.

### Advanced methods {#Advanced_methods}

Many more methods exist which become increasingly complicated and powerful in application. A few of the most common are summarized here, with more specific information found in the references.

#### Stepwise maximum likelihood method {#Stepwise_maximum_likelihood_method}

This method is based on the [distribution functions](wikipedia://en/365876) of objects (such as stars or galaxies), which is a relation of how many objects are expected with certain intrinsic [brightnesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brightness), distances, or other fundamental values. Each of these values have their own [distribution function](wikipedia://en/365876) which can be combined with a random number generator to create a theoretical sample of stars. This method takes the [distribution function](wikipedia://en/365876) of distances as a known, definite quantity, and then allows the [distribution function](wikipedia://en/365876) of [absolute magnitudes](wikipedia://en/1963) to change. In this way, it can check different [distribution functions](wikipedia://en/365876) of the [absolute magnitudes](wikipedia://en/1963) against the actual distribution of detected objects, and find the relation that provides the maximum probability of recreating the same set of objects. By starting with the detected, biased distribution of objects and the appropriate limits to detection, this method recreates the true [distribution function](wikipedia://en/365876). However, this method requires heavy calculations and generally relies on computer programs.[^o19][^o22]

#### Schechter estimators {#Schechter_estimators}

[Paul Schechter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_L._Schechter) found a very interesting relation between the logarithm of a [spectral line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line)'s [line width](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line_width) and its [apparent magnitude](wikipedia://en/1962), when working with [galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxies).[^o24] In a perfect, stationary case, [spectral lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_lines) should be incredibly narrow bumps, looking like lines, but motions of the object such as rotation or motion in our line of sight will cause shifts and broadening of these lines. The relation is found by starting with the [Tully–Fisher relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tully–Fisher_relation), wherein the distance to a [galaxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy) is related to its [apparent magnitude](wikipedia://en/1962) and its velocity width, or the 'maximum' speed of its [rotation curve](wikipedia://en/183083). From macroscopic [Doppler broadening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_broadening), the logarithm of the [line width](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line_width) of an observed spectral line can be related to the width of the velocity distribution. If the distances are assumed to be known very well, then the [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963) and the [line width](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line_width) are closely related.[^o24] For example, working with the commonly used [21cm line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_line), an important line relating to neutral hydrogen, the relation is generally calibrated with a [linear regression](wikipedia://en/48758386) and given the form

$$\overline{M} = \alpha P + \beta$$

where P is log(line width) and α and β are constants.

The reason that this estimator is useful is that the inverse regression line is actually unaffected by the Malmquist bias, so long as the selection effects are only based on magnitude. As such, the expected value of P given M will be unbiased and will give an unbiased log distance estimator. This estimator has many properties and ramifications which can make it a very useful tool.[^o25]

#### Complex mathematical relations {#Complex_mathematical_relations}

Advanced versions of the traditional correction mentioned above can be found in the literature, limiting or changing the initial assumptions to suit the appropriate author's needs. Often, these other methods will provide very complicated mathematical expressions with very powerful but specific applications. For example, work by Luri et al. found a relation for the bias for [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stars) in a [galaxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy) which relates the correction to the variance of the sample and the [apparent magnitude](wikipedia://en/1962), [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963), and the height above the [galactic disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disc_%28galaxy%29). This gave a much more exact and accurate result, but also required an assumption about the spatial distribution of [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stars) in the desired [galaxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy).[^o27] While useful individually, and there are many examples published, these have very limited scope and are not generally as broadly applicable as the other methods mentioned above.

## Applications {#Applications}

*Image: lumfunc.png.* The dashed red line is an example luminosity function when the Malmquist bias is not corrected for. The more numerous low luminosity objects are underrepresented because of the apparent magnitude limit of the survey. The solid blue line is the properly corrected luminosity function using the volume-weighted correction method.

Anytime a magnitude-limited sample is used, one of the methods described above should be used to correct for the Malmquist bias. For instance, when trying to obtain a [luminosity function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminosity_function), calibrate the [Tully–Fisher relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tully–Fisher_relation), or obtain the value of the [Hubble constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble_constant), the Malmquist bias can strongly change the results.

The luminosity function gives the number of stars or galaxies per luminosity or absolute magnitude bin. When using a magnitude-limited sample, the number of faint objects is underrepresented as discussed above. This shifts the peak of the luminosity function from the faint end to a brighter luminosity and changes the shape of the luminosity function. Typically, the volume weighting method is used to correct the Malmquist bias so that the survey is equivalent to a distance-limited survey rather than a magnitude-limited survey.[^o30] The figure to the right shows two luminosity functions for an example population of stars that is magnitude-limited. The dashed luminosity function shows the effect of the Malmquist bias, while the solid line shows the corrected luminosity function. Malmquist bias drastically changes the shape of the luminosity function.

Another application that is affected by the Malmquist bias is the [Tully–Fisher relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tully–Fisher_relation), which relates the luminosity of spiral galaxies to their respective velocity width. If a nearby cluster of galaxies is used to calibrate the Tully–Fisher relation, and then that relation is applied to a distant cluster, the distance to the farther cluster will be systematically underestimated.[^o25] By underestimating the distance to clusters, anything found using those clusters will be incorrect; for example, when finding the value of the Hubble constant.

These are just a few examples where the Malmquist bias can strongly affect results. As mentioned above, anytime a magnitude-limited sample is used, the Malmquist bias needs to be corrected for. A correction is not limited to just the examples above.

:::

## Alternatives {#Alternatives}

Some alternatives do exist to attempt to avoid the Malmquist bias, or to approach it in a different way, with a few of the more common ones summarized below.

### Distance-limited sampling {#Distance-limited_sampling}

One ideal method to avoid the Malmquist bias is to only select objects within a set distance, and have no [limiting magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limiting_magnitude) but instead observe all objects within this volume.[^o11] Clearly, in this case, the Malmquist bias is not an issue as the volume will be fully populated and any [distribution](wikipedia://en/365876) or [luminosity function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminosity_function) will be appropriately sampled. Unfortunately, this method is not always practical. Finding distances to astronomical objects is very difficult, and even with the aid of objects with easily determined distances, called [standard candles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_candles), and similar things, there are great uncertainties. Further, distances are not generally known for objects until after they have already been observed and analyzed, and so a distance limited [survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_survey) is usually only an option for a second round of observations, and not initially available. Finally, distance limited [surveys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_surveys) are generally only possible over small volumes where the distances are reliably known, and thus it is not practical for large [surveys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_surveys).

### Homogeneous and inhomogeneous Malmquist correction {#Homogeneous_and_inhomogeneous_Malmquist_correction}

This method attempts to correct the [bias](wikipedia://en/184099) again, but through very different means. Rather than trying to fix the [absolute magnitudes](wikipedia://en/1963), this method takes the distances to the objects as being the random variables and attempts to rescale those.[^o25] In effect, rather than giving the stars in the sample the correct distribution of [absolute magnitudes](wikipedia://en/1963) (and average [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963)), it attempts to 'move' the stars such that they would have a correct distribution of distances. Ideally, this should have the same end result as the magnitude correction methods and should result in a correctly represented sample. In either the homogeneous or inhomogeneous case, the bias is defined in terms of a [prior distribution](wikipedia://en/472877) of distances, the distance estimator, and the [likelihood function](wikipedia://en/44968) of these two being the same distribution. The homogeneous case is much simpler and rescales the raw distance estimates by a constant factor. Unfortunately, this will be very insensitive to [large scale structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observable_universe) such as clustering as well as observational selection effects, and will not give a very accurate result. The inhomogeneous case attempts to correct this by creating a more complicated prior distribution of objects by taking into account structures seen in the observed distribution. In both cases though, it is assumed that the [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) is Gaussian with constant variance and a mean of the true average log distance, which is far from accurate. However, this method is debated and may not be accurate in any implementation due to uncertainties in calculating the raw, observed distance estimates causing the assumptions to use this method to be invalid.[^o25]

### Historical alternatives {#Historical_alternatives}

The term 'Malmquist bias' has not always been definitively used to refer to the bias outlined above. As recently as the year 2000, the Malmquist bias has appeared in the literature clearly referring to different types of bias and statistical effect.[^o35] The most common of these other uses is to refer to an effect that takes place with a [magnitude limited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limiting_magnitude) sample, but in this case the low [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963) objects are overrepresented. In a sample with a [magnitude limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limiting_magnitude), there will be a margin of error near that boundary where objects that should be bright enough to make the cut are excluded and objects that are slightly below the limit are instead included. Since low [absolute magnitude](wikipedia://en/1963) objects are more common than brighter ones, and since these dimmer galaxies are more likely to be below the cutoff line and scattered up, while the brighter ones are more likely to be above the line and scattered down, an over-representation of the lower [luminosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminosity) objects result. However, in modern-day literature and consensus, the Malmquist bias refers to the effect outlined above.

## Gravitational wave astronomy {#Gravitational_wave_astronomy}

The Malmquist bias significantly affects [gravitational wave astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_wave_astronomy). Second-generation gravitational wave observatories like [LIGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIGO) can most easily detect chirp signals from merging [black holes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_holes) with tens of times the [solar mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_mass), while signals from merging binary [neutron star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star) can only be observed within a much shorter distance range.[^o37] Hence, in estimating the merger rate of binary in the universe, sophisticated statistical methods must be employed to correct for this observational bias, as the raw detection rates would otherwise provide a severely distorted view of the true cosmic merger rate distribution across different compact object masses.[^o38]
