# Picard–Fuchs equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Picard–Fuchs equation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Picard–Fuchs equation**, named after [Émile Picard](wikipedia://en/42000) and [Lazarus Fuchs](wikipedia://en/2817087), is a linear [ordinary differential equation](wikipedia://en/32742753) whose solutions describe the periods of [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let

$$j=\frac{g_2^3}{g_2^3-27g_3^2}$$

be the [j-invariant](wikipedia://en/451988) with $g_2$ and $g_3$ the [modular invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_modular_function) of the elliptic curve in [Weierstrass form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_equation):

$$y^2=4x^3-g_2x-g_3.\,$$

Note that the *j*-invariant is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) from the [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181) $\mathbb{H}/\Gamma$ to the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799) $\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}$; where $\mathbb{H}$ is the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826) and $\Gamma$ is the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019). The Picard–Fuchs equation is then

$$\frac{d^2y}{dj^2} + \frac{1}{j} \frac{dy}{dj} + \frac{31j -4}{144j^2(1-j)^2} y=0.\,$$

Written in [Q-form](wikipedia://en/555745), one has

$$\frac{d^2f}{dj^2} + \frac{1-1968j + 2654208j^2}{4j^2 (1-1728j)^2} f=0.\,$$

:::

## Solutions {#Solutions}

This equation can be cast into the form of the [hypergeometric differential equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypergeometric_differential_equation). It has two linearly independent solutions, called the **periods** of elliptic functions. The ratio of the two periods is equal to the [period ratio](wikipedia://en/728209) τ, the standard coordinate on the upper-half plane. However, the ratio of two solutions of the hypergeometric equation is also known as a [Schwarz triangle map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwarz_triangle_map).

The Picard–Fuchs equation can be cast into the form of [Riemann's differential equation](wikipedia://en/2071666), and thus solutions can be directly read off in terms of [Riemann P-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_P-function). One has

$$y(j)=P \left\{ \begin{matrix} 0 & 1 & \infty & \; \\ {1/6} & {1/4} & 0 & j \\ {-1/6\;} & {3/4} & 0 & \; \end{matrix} \right\}\,$$

At least four methods to find the [j-function inverse](wikipedia://en/451988#Inverse_and_special_values) can be given.

Dedekind defines the *j*-function by its Schwarz derivative in his letter to Borchardt. As a partial fraction, it reveals the geometry of the fundamental domain:

$$2(S\tau) (j) = \frac{1-\frac{1}{4}}{(1-j)^2} + \frac{1-\frac{1}{9}}{j^2} + \frac{1-\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{9}}{j(1-j)} = \frac{3}{4(1-j)^2} + \frac{8}{9j^2} + \frac{23}{36j(1-j)}$$

where (*Sƒ*)(*x*) is the [Schwarzian derivative](wikipedia://en/555745) of *ƒ* with respect to *x*.

## Generalization {#Generalization}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), this equation has been shown to be a very special case of a general phenomenon, the [Gauss&ndash;Manin connection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss&ndash;Manin_connection).

:::
