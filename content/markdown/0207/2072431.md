# Willmore energy

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Willmore energy"}

*Image: Willmore Surface sculpture Durham.jpg.* "Willmore Surface" sculpture at Durham University in memory of Thomas Willmore

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), the **Willmore energy** is a quantitative measure of how much a given [surface](wikipedia://en/27865) deviates from a round [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859). Mathematically, the Willmore energy of a [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) [closed surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_surface) [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119) in three-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is defined to be the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) of the square of the [mean curvature](wikipedia://en/684210) minus the [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623). It is named after the English geometer [Thomas Willmore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Willmore).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Expressed symbolically, the Willmore energy of *S* is:

$$\mathcal{W} = \int_S H^2 \, dA - \int_S K \, dA$$

where $H$ is the [mean curvature](wikipedia://en/684210), $K$ is the [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623), and *dA* is the area form of *S*. For a closed surface, by the [Gauss–Bonnet theorem](wikipedia://en/139229), the integral of the Gaussian curvature may be computed in terms of the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) $\chi(S)$ of the surface, so

$$\int_S K \, dA = 2 \pi \chi(S),$$

which is a [topological invariant](wikipedia://en/1337282) and thus independent of the particular embedding in $\mathbb{R}^3$ that was chosen. Thus the Willmore energy can be expressed as

$$\mathcal{W} = \int_S H^2 \, dA - 2 \pi \chi(S)$$

An alternative, but equivalent, formula is

$$\mathcal{W} = {1 \over 4} \int_S (k_1 - k_2)^2 \, dA$$

where $k_1$ and $k_2$ are the [principal curvatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_curvatures) of the surface.

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

The Willmore energy is always greater than or equal to zero. A round [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) has zero Willmore energy.

The Willmore energy can be considered a functional on the space of embeddings of a given surface, in the sense of the [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882), and one can vary the embedding of a surface, while leaving it topologically unaltered.

The form $(k_1-k_2)^2 \, dA$ is unchanged after a [conformal transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_transformation), so the value of the Willmore energy is conformally invariant.[^o6]

:::

## Critical points {#Critical_points}

A basic problem in the [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882) is to find the [critical points](wikipedia://en/1951424) and minima of a functional. The critical points of Willmore energy are called *Willmore surfaces*.

For a given topological space, this is equivalent to finding the critical points of the function

$$\int_S H^2 \, dA$$

since the Euler characteristic is constant.

One can find (local) minima for the Willmore energy by [gradient descent](wikipedia://en/201489), which in this context is called Willmore flow.

For embeddings of the sphere in 3-space, the critical points have been classified: they are all equivalent to [minimal surfaces](wikipedia://en/276734) by a [conformal transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_transformation), the round sphere is the minimum, and all other critical values are integer multiples of 4$\pi$.[^o9]

:::

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Willmore flow"}

## Willmore flow {#Willmore_flow}

The **Willmore flow** is the [geometric flow](wikipedia://en/14617515) corresponding to the Willmore energy; it is an $L^2$-[gradient flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradient_flow).

$$e[{\mathcal{M}}]=\frac{1}{2} \int_{\mathcal{M}} H^2\, \mathrm{d}A$$

where *H* stands for the [mean curvature](wikipedia://en/684210) of the [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) $\mathcal{M}$.

Flow lines satisfy the differential equation:

$$\partial_t x(t) = -\nabla \mathcal{W}[x(t)] \,$$

where $x$ is a point belonging to the surface.

This flow leads to an evolution problem in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625): the surface $\mathcal{M}$ is evolving in time to follow variations of steepest descent of the energy. Like [surface diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_diffusion_%28mathematics%29) it is a fourth-order flow, since the variation of the energy contains fourth derivatives.

:::

::: {#o13 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

- In [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), the Willmore energy arises in the study of quasi-local notions of [gravitational mass](wikipedia://en/6513985). In particular, surfaces that are critical points of the Willmore energy under an area constraint, known as area-constrained Willmore surfaces, play a role in the analysis of the [Hawking quasi-local energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawking_energy), where they provide geometrically distinguished families for studying properties such as positivity, monotonicity, and asymptotic behavior.
- [Cell membranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane) tend to position themselves so as to minimize Willmore energy.[^o14]
- Willmore energy is used in constructing a class of optimal [sphere eversions](wikipedia://en/478672), the [minimax eversions](wikipedia://en/11465576).
