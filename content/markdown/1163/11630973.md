# Torsion-free abelian group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Torsion-free abelian group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically in [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **torsion-free abelian group** is an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) which has no non-trivial [torsion](wikipedia://en/3039330) elements; that is, a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) in which the [group operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_operation) is [commutative](wikipedia://en/294390) and the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) is the only element with finite [order](wikipedia://en/493527).

:::

While [finitely generated abelian groups](wikipedia://en/58527) are completely classified, not much is known about infinitely generated abelian groups, even in the torsion-free countable case.[^o2]

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

An [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) $\langle G, + ,0\rangle$ is said to be torsion-free if no element other than the identity $e$ is of finite [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group_element).[^o5][^o6][^o7] Explicitly, for any $n > 0$, the only element $x \in G$ for which $nx = 0$ is $x = 0$.

A natural example of a torsion-free group is $\langle \mathbb Z,+,0\rangle$, as only the integer 0 can be added to itself finitely many times to reach 0. More generally, the [free abelian group](wikipedia://en/247296) $\mathbb Z^r$ is torsion-free for any $r \in \mathbb N$. An important step in the proof of the classification of finitely generated abelian groups is that every such torsion-free group is isomorphic to a $\mathbb Z^r$.

A non-finitely generated countable example is given by the additive group of the polynomial ring $\mathbb Z[X]$ (the free abelian group of countable rank).

More complicated examples are the additive group of the rational field $\mathbb Q$, or its subgroups such as $\mathbb Z[p^{-1}]$ (rational numbers whose denominator is a power of $p$). Yet more involved examples are given by [groups of higher rank](wikipedia://en/244541#Groups_of_higher_rank).

:::

## Groups of rank 1 {#Groups_of_rank_1}

### Rank {#Rank}

The *rank* of an abelian group $A$ is the dimension of the $\mathbb Q$-vector space $\mathbb Q \otimes_{\mathbb Z} A$. Equivalently it is the maximal cardinality of a linearly independent (over $\mathbb{Z}$) subset of $A$.

If $A$ is torsion-free then it injects into $\mathbb Q \otimes_{\mathbb Z} A$. Thus, torsion-free abelian groups of rank 1 are exactly subgroups of the additive group $\mathbb Q$.

### Classification {#Classification}

Torsion-free abelian groups of rank 1 have been completely classified. To do so one associates to a group $A$ a subset $\tau(A)$ of the prime numbers, as follows: pick any $x \in A \setminus \{0\}$, for a prime $p$ we say that $p \in \tau(A)$ if and only if $x \in p^kA$ for every $k \in \mathbb N$. This does not depend on the choice of $x$ since for another $y \in A\setminus \{0\}$ there exists $n, m \in \mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}$ such that $ny = mx$. Baer proved[^o12][^o13] that $\tau(A)$ is a complete isomorphism invariant for rank-1 torsion free abelian groups.

:::

## Classification problem in general {#Classification_problem_in_general}

The hardness of a classification problem for a certain type of structures on a countable set can be quantified using [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858) and [descriptive set theory](wikipedia://en/396619). In this sense it has been proved that the classification problem for countable torsion-free abelian groups is as hard as possible.[^o15]
