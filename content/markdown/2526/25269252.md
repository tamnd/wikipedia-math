# Coordinate singularity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Coordinate singularity"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), a **coordinate singularity** occurs when an apparent [singularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity) or [discontinuity](wikipedia://en/2661487) occurs in one [coordinate frame](wikipedia://en/81931) that can be removed by choosing a different frame.

:::

An example is the apparent (longitudinal) singularity at the 90 degree latitude in [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates). An object moving due north (for example, along the line [0 degrees longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenwich_meridian)) on the surface of a sphere will suddenly experience an instantaneous change in longitude at the [pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographical_pole) (i.e., jumping from longitude 0 to longitude 180 degrees). In fact, longitude is not uniquely defined at the poles. This discontinuity, however, is only apparent; it is an artifact of the coordinate system chosen, which is singular at the poles. A different coordinate system would eliminate the apparent discontinuity, e.g. by replacing the latitude/longitude representation with an [$n$-vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-vector) representation.

English theoretical physicist [Stephen Hawking](wikipedia://en/19376148) aptly summed this up, when once asking the question, "What lies north of the [North Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pole)?".[^o2]
