# Quasi-polynomial time

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quasi-polynomial time"}

In [computational complexity theory](wikipedia://en/7543) and the [analysis of algorithms](wikipedia://en/2230), an algorithm is said to take **quasi-polynomial time** if its [time complexity](wikipedia://en/405944) is [quasi-polynomially bounded](wikipedia://en/75468672). That is, there should exist a constant $c$ such that the [worst-case running time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worst-case_analysis) of the algorithm, on inputs of size $n$, has an [upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound) of the form

$$2^{O\bigl((\log n)^c\bigr)}.$$

:::

The [decision problems](wikipedia://en/8336) with quasi-polynomial time algorithms are natural candidates for being [NP-intermediate](wikipedia://en/4637081), neither having [polynomial time](wikipedia://en/658550) nor likely to be [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard).

## Complexity class {#Complexity_class}

The complexity class **QP** consists of all problems that have quasi-polynomial time algorithms. It can be defined in terms of [DTIME](wikipedia://en/658538) as follows.

$$\mathsf{QP} = \bigcup_{c \in \mathbb{N}} \mathsf{DTIME} \left(2^{(\log n)^c}\right)$$

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

An early example of a quasi-polynomial time algorithm was the [Adleman–Pomerance–Rumely primality test](wikipedia://en/8198761). However, the problem of testing whether a number is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) has subsequently been shown to have a polynomial time algorithm, the [AKS primality test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AKS_primality_test).

In some cases, quasi-polynomial time bounds can be proven to be optimal under the [exponential time hypothesis](wikipedia://en/26434552) or a related [computational hardness assumption](wikipedia://en/6158383). For instance, this is true for the following problems:

- Finding the largest disjoint subset of a collection of [unit disks](wikipedia://en/252738) in the [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane) can be solved in time $n^{O(\log n)}$, and requires time $n^{\Omega(\log n)}$ under the exponential time hypothesis.
- Finding a graph with the fewest vertices that does not appear as an [induced subgraph](wikipedia://en/1655188) of a given graph can be solved in time $n^{O(\log n)}$, and requires time $n^{\Omega(\log n)}$ under the exponential time hypothesis.
- Finding the smallest [dominating set](wikipedia://en/1747972) in a [tournament](wikipedia://en/1427502). This is a subset of the vertices of the tournament that has at least one directed edge to all other vertices. It can be solved in time $n^{O(\log n)}$, and requires time $n^{\Omega(\log n)}$ under the exponential time hypothesis.
- Computing the [Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension](wikipedia://en/305846) of a [family of sets](wikipedia://en/723043). This is the size of the largest set $S$ (not necessarily in the family) that is *shattered* by the family, meaning that each subset of $S$ can be formed by intersecting $S$ with a member of the family. It can be solved in time $n^{O(\log n)}$, and requires time $n^{(\log n)^{1/3-o(1)}}$ under the exponential time hypothesis.

Other problems for which the best known algorithm takes quasi-polynomial time include:

- The [planted clique](wikipedia://en/48563244) problem, of determining whether a [random graph](wikipedia://en/358477) has been modified by adding edges between all pairs of a subset of its vertices.
- [Monotone dualization](wikipedia://en/75122486), several equivalent problems of converting logical formulas between conjunctive and disjunctive normal form, listing all minimal hitting sets of a family of sets, or listing all minimal set covers of a family of sets, with time complexity measured in the combined input and output size.
- [Parity games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_game), involving token-passing along the edges of a colored directed graph. The paper giving a quasi-polynomial algorithm for these games won the 2021 [Nerode Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nerode_Prize).
- [3-coloring](wikipedia://en/426743) [circle graphs](wikipedia://en/1399873). These are the [intersection graphs](wikipedia://en/7726759) of chords of a circle. Finding their optimal colorings, in general, is NP-hard, as is testing whether they can be colored with four colors, but colorings with three colors can be found in time $n^{O(\log n)}$.

Problems for which a quasi-polynomial time algorithm has been announced but not fully published include:

- The [graph isomorphism problem](wikipedia://en/1950766), determining whether two graphs can be made equal to each other by relabeling their vertices, announced in 2015 and updated in 2017 by [László Babai](wikipedia://en/3461604).
- The [unknotting problem](wikipedia://en/4956355), recognizing whether a [knot diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_diagram) describes the [unknot](wikipedia://en/60075), announced by [Marc Lackenby](wikipedia://en/49182345) in 2021.

:::

## In approximation algorithms {#In_approximation_algorithms}

Quasi-polynomial time has also been used to study [approximation algorithms](wikipedia://en/563105). In particular, a *quasi-polynomial-time approximation scheme* (QPTAS) is a variant of a [polynomial-time approximation scheme](wikipedia://en/666431) whose running time is quasi-polynomial rather than polynomial. Problems with a QPTAS include [minimum-weight triangulation](wikipedia://en/22231180), finding the [maximum clique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_clique) on the [intersection graph](wikipedia://en/7726759) of disks, and determining the probability that a [hypergraph](wikipedia://en/232535) becomes disconnected when some of its edges fail with given independent probabilities.

More strongly, the problem of finding an approximate [Nash equilibrium](wikipedia://en/45337) has a QPTAS, but cannot have a PTAS under the exponential time hypothesis.
