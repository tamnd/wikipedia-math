# Thomas G. Kurtz

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Thomas G. Kurtz"}

*Image: Prof.Kurtz.jpg.* Thomas G. Kurtz

**Thomas G. Kurtz** (born 14 July 1941 in [Kansas City, Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansas_City,_Missouri), United States, died 19 April 2025[^o2]) was an American emeritus professor of Mathematics[^o3] and Statistics[^o4] at [University of Wisconsin-Madison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin–Madison) known for his research contributions to many areas of [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/47895). In particular, Kurtz’s research focused on convergence, approximation and representation of several important classes of [Markov processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_process). His findings appear in scientific disciplines such as [systems biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_biology), [population genetics](wikipedia://en/219268), [telecommunications networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications_network) and [mathematical finance](wikipedia://en/33589680).

:::

## Education {#Education}

Kurtz obtained his Ph.D. from [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University) in 1967 under the supervision of James L. McGregor.[^o6] As an undergraduate student he attended [University of Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Missouri) where he graduated in 1963 with a Bachelor's degree in Mathematics. Kurtz is also an alumnus of La Plata High School in [La Plata, Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Plata,_Missouri).

## Academic career {#Academic_career}

After completing his Ph.D. in 1967, Kurtz joined the Department of Mathematics at the [University of Wisconsin–Madison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin–Madison) where he remained for his entire career. He received a joint appointment in the Statistics department in 1985. In 1996, he was awarded the WARF-University Houses Professorship, which he chose to identify as the [Paul Lévy](wikipedia://en/556462) Professorship to honor one of the founders of modern probability theory. At UW Madison, Kurtz served as the Mathematics Department Chair from 1985 to 1988 and as the Director of the Center for Mathematical Sciences from 1990 to 1996. He retired from active teaching in 2008 but he continued to work as an emeritus professor. During his academic career Kurtz supervised twenty-nine Ph.D. students[^o8] and lectured extensively at UW Madison and elsewhere. For almost a decade, he organized a Summer Internship Program in Madison, which helped in grooming the next generation of probabilists.

Kurtz gave several invited seminars and tutorials around the world. Over the years he held many visiting positions including:

- Nelder Visiting Fellow, *[Imperial College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_College_London)*, London, U.K. April–May 2016.[^o9]
- Guest Professor, *[Goethe University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethe_University_Frankfurt)*, Frankfurt, Germany, May–June, 2013.
- Visiting Fellow, *[Isaac Newton Institute for Mathematical Sciences](wikipedia://en/736837)*, Cambridge, U.K, February–May, 2010.
- Very Important Visitor (VIV), *[Institute for Mathematics and its Applications](wikipedia://en/10324088)*, Minneapolis, 2003–2004.
- Visiting Professor, *[Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University),* April–June, 1989
- Visiting Professor, *[University of Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Utah)*, Salt Lake City, January–March, 1989
- Visiting Professor, *[Université de Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Strasbourg),* France, 1977–1978.
- Visiting Fellow, *[Australian National University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_National_University),* Canberra, 1973.

Kurtz served in many scientific committees and editorial boards of academic journals. He was a trustee of the [Mathematical Biosciences Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Biosciences_Institute) in Columbus, Ohio.

## Awards and honors {#Awards_and_honors}

Kurtz was a former president of the [Institute of Mathematical Statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Mathematical_Statistics) (2005–2006) and a former editor of the *[Annals of Probability](wikipedia://en/34673748)* (2000–2002). He was a Fellow of the Institute of Mathematical Statistics (IMS) and of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences). He was chosen to give the Wald Memorial Lectures in 2014 at the IMS Annual Meeting in Sydney, Australia.[^o11]

He was elected as a Fellow of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822) in the 2020 Class, for "research in probability and its applications, especially for contributions to the study of Markov processes".[^o12]

## Publications {#Publications}

In his five decades of research, Kurtz published more than 100 peer-reviewed articles[^o14] in prominent mathematical journals. He also authored four books, which are as follows:

1. *Markov Processes: Characterization and Convergence* (John Wiley & Sons Inc. 1986):[^o15] This book with his former Ph.D. student [Stewart Ethier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stewart_Ethier) is one of Kurtz’s most well-known works and it is a standard reference for advanced theory of Markov processes. This book develops an intricate, yet elegant mathematical framework for establishing the convergence of Markov processes and characterising the limiting process.
1. *Stochastic Analysis of Biochemical Systems* (Springer 2015): This book with David Anderson provides a timely survey of the wide array of methods and techniques that can be employed to analyze stochastic models of chemical reaction networks. Such models are frequently encountered in the rapidly growing field of Systems Biology. Mindful of the interdisciplinary nature of the research community in this field, the authors present the material in such a way that it is accessible to anyone who is familiar with the standard undergraduate mathematics curriculum.
1. *Large Deviations for Stochastic Processes* (American Mathematical Society 2006): This book with his former Ph.D. student Jin Feng, presents a general theory for obtaining large deviation results for a large class of stochastic processes. This theory is based on the idea that the large deviation principle for a sequence of Markov processes can be obtained by proving the convergence of an associated family of nonlinear semigroups. To overcome the formidable theoretical challenge of proving this convergence of nonlinear semigroups, the authors employ tools from the modern theory of [viscosity solutions](wikipedia://en/2300687) that has been developed for solving [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564). With these viscosity methods, the authors demonstrate that large deviation results can be readily derived using their approach for a range of interesting examples.
1. *Approximation of Population Processes* (Society of Industrial and Applied Mathematics 1981): This book provides a self-contained treatment of the limiting behavior of a wide class of population processes as the population-size approaches infinity. Diffusion approximation results are developed for population processes on a very general state-space, thereby allowing the results to be applied in a variety of examples from Branching Processes, Population Genetics, Epidemics and Chemical Reaction Networks. Furthermore, random time-change formulas are introduced that provide sample path representations of complicated stochastic processes in terms of its simpler counterparts such as Poisson processes or Brownian motions. These formulas are used to derive approximation results and also examine the relationship between the stochastic process and the corresponding deterministic process in the “law of large numbers” limit.
