# Lacunary function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lacunary function"}

*Image: Lacunary domain coloring.png.* Domain coloring of the 128th partial sum of the lacunary function $\sum_{n=0}^\infty z^{2^n}$.

In [analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), a **lacunary function** or **series** is an [analytic function](wikipedia://en/61478) containing many zero coefficients in its [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) decomposition. A series can be lacunary in the sense of having Hadamard or Fabry gaps (or lacunae). They are associated with the [Hadamard gap theorem](wikipedia://en/13021753) and the [Fabry gap theorem](wikipedia://en/39697327) stating that a lacunary [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/59958) cannot be [analytically continued](wikipedia://en/80842) outside its [disk of convergence](wikipedia://en/61476). More recent investigations examine lacunary [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) as well.

:::

The word *lacunary* is derived from lacuna (*pl.* lacunae), meaning gap, or vacancy.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="A simple example"}

## A simple example {#A_simple_example}

Pick an integer $a \geq 2$. Consider the following function defined by a power series:

$$f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty z^{a^n} = z + z^a + z^{a^2} + z^{a^3} + z^{a^4} + \cdots\,$$

The power series converges locally uniform on any open domain |*z*| \< 1. This can be proved by comparing *f* with the [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630), which is absolutely convergent when |*z*| \< 1. So *f* is analytic on the open [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738). Nevertheless, *f* has [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) singularities on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071), and cannot be analytically continued outside of the open unit disk, as the following argument demonstrates.

Clearly *f* has a singularity at *z* = 1, because

$$f(1) = 1 + 1 + 1 + \cdots\,$$

is a [divergent series](wikipedia://en/876428). But if *z* is allowed to be non-real, problems arise, since

$$f(z^a) = f(z) - z \qquad f\bigl(z^{a^2}\bigr) = f(z^a) - z^a \qquad f\bigl(z^{a^3}\bigr) = f\bigl(z^{a^2}\bigr) - z^{a^2} \qquad \cdots \qquad f\bigl(z^{a^{n+1}}\bigr) = f\bigl(z^{a^n}\bigr)-z^{a^n}$$

we can see that *f* has a singularity at a point *z* when *z*<sup>a</sup> = 1, and also when *z*<sup>a<sup>2</sup></sup> = 1. By the induction suggested by the above equations, *f* must have a singularity at each of the *a*<sup>*n*</sup>-th [roots of unity](wikipedia://en/171950) for all natural numbers *n.* The set of all such singularities is [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) on the unit circle; so it is impossible to define *f* on any [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) containing the unit circle, making analytic continuation impossible as well.[^o4]

:::

## An elementary result {#An_elementary_result}

The argument above shows that certain series define lacunary functions. What is not so evident is that the gaps between the powers of *z* can grow much more slowly, and the resulting series will still define a lacunary function. To make this notion more precise some additional notation is needed.

We write

$$f(z) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_kz^{\lambda_k} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n z^n\,$$

where *b*<sub>*n*</sub> = *a*<sub>*k*</sub> when *n* = λ<sub>*k*</sub>, and *b*<sub>*n*</sub> = 0 otherwise. The stretches where the coefficients *b*<sub>*n*</sub> in the second series are all zero are the *lacunae* in the coefficients. The monotonically increasing sequence of positive natural numbers {λ<sub>*k*</sub>} specifies the powers of *z* which are in the power series for *f*(*z*).

Now a theorem of [Hadamard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaques_Hadamard) can be stated.[^o6] If

$$\frac{\lambda_k}{\lambda_{k-1}} > 1 + \delta \,$$

for all *k*, where *δ* > 0 is an arbitrary positive constant, then *f*(*z*) is a lacunary function that cannot be [continued](wikipedia://en/80842) outside its circle of convergence. In other words, the sequence {λ<sub>*k*</sub>} doesn't have to grow as fast as 2<sup>*k*</sup> for *f*(*z*) to be a lacunary function – it just has to grow as fast as some [geometric progression](wikipedia://en/23474670) (1 + δ)<sup>*k*</sup>. A series for which λ<sub>*k*</sub> grows this quickly is said to contain **Hadamard gaps**; see the [Hadamard gap theorem](wikipedia://en/13021753) and its generalization, the [Fabry gap theorem](wikipedia://en/39697327).

## Lacunary trigonometric series {#Lacunary_trigonometric_series}

Mathematicians have also investigated the properties of lacunary trigonometric series

$$S((\lambda_k)_k,\theta) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k \cos(\lambda_k\theta) \qquad S((\lambda_k)_k,\theta,\omega) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k \cos(\lambda_k\theta + \omega) \,$$

for which the λ<sub>*k*</sub> are far apart. Here the coefficients *a*<sub>*k*</sub> are real numbers. In this context, attention has been focused on criteria sufficient to guarantee convergence of the trigonometric series [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688) (that is, for almost every value of the angle *θ* and of the distortion factor *ω*).

- [Kolmogorov](wikipedia://en/91161) showed that if the sequence {*λ*<sub>*k*</sub>} contains Hadamard gaps, then the series *S*(*λ*<sub>*k*</sub>, *θ*, *ω*) converges almost everywhere if and only if $\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k^2$ converges.
- [Zygmund](wikipedia://en/362477) showed under the same condition that *S*(*λ*<sub>*k*</sub>, *θ*, *ω*) is not a Fourier series representing an [integrable function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_function) when this sum of squares of the *a*<sub>*k*</sub> is a divergent series.[^o8]
