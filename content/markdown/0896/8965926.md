# Sand table

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sand table"}

A **sand table**, or **sandboard**, uses constrained sand for writing, modelling, or educational purposes. The original version of a sand table may be the grc used by early Greek students. They were later used throughout the [Arab world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world). In the modern era, one common use for a sand table is to make terrain models for [military planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_plan) and [wargaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wargaming).

:::

::: {#o3 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Sand abacus"}

## Abax {#Abax}

*Image: Abax from Darius Vase.jpg.* grc depicted on the [Darius Vase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_Vase)[^o4]

An grc (ἄβαξ) was a table covered with sand commonly used by students, particularly in Greece, to perform studies such as writing, geometry, and calculations.[^n1]

The grc was the predecessor to the [abacus](wikipedia://en/655). Objects, such as stones, were added for counting and then columns for [place-value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place-value) arithmetic. The demarcation between an grc and an abacus seems to be poorly defined in history;[^n2] moreover, modern definitions of the word abacus universally describe it as a frame with rods and beads[^n3] and, in general, do not include the definition of "sand table".

The sand table may well have been the predecessor to some [board games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_games). ("The word ar, or abacus, is used both for the reckoning-board with its counters and the play-board with its pieces, ").[^n4] grc is from the [old Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Greek#Evolution_from_Ancient_to_Modern_Greek) for "sand table".[^n3]

## Ghubār {#Ghubār}

Ghubār numerals

An Arabic word for sand (or dust) is ar (or ar), and Western numerals (the [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214) digits 0–9) are derived from the style of digits written on dust boards (or ar) in North-West Africa and Iberia, also described as the 'West Arabic' or 'ar' style.[^o11]

## Military use {#Military_use}

*Image: Exercise Desert Punch Marine sand table 2.gif.* Marines view a sand table model of the operating area of [Exercise Desert Punch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exercise_Desert_Punch) constructed on the desert floor near [Yuma, Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuma,_Arizona)

Sand tables have been used for [military planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_plan) and [wargaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wargaming) for many years as a field expedient, [small-scale map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_%28map%29), and in [training](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_training) for [military actions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle). In 1890 a Sand table room was built at the [Royal Military College of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Military_College_of_Canada) for use in teaching cadets military tactics; this replaced the old sand table room in a pre-college building, in which the weight of the sand had damaged the floor.[^o13] The use of sand tables increasingly fell out of favour with improved maps, aerial and satellite photography, and later, with digital terrain simulations. More modern sand tables have incorporated [Augmented Reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augmented_reality), such as the [Augmented Reality Sandtable (ARES)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augmented_Reality_Sandtable_%28ARES%29) developed by the [Army Research Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Research_Laboratory).[^o14] Today, virtual and conventional sand tables are used in operations training.

In 1991, "Special Forces teams discovered an elaborate sand-table model of the Iraqi military plan for the defense of Kuwait City. Four huge red arrows from the sea pointed at the coastline of Kuwait City and the huge defensive effort positioned there. Small fences of concertina wire marked the shoreline and models of artillery pieces lined the shore area. Throughout the city were plastic models of other artillery and air defense positions, while thin, red-painted strips of board designated supply routes and main highways."[^o15]

In 2006, [Google Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Earth) users looking at satellite photography of China found a several *kilometre* large "sand table" scale model, strikingly reminiscent of a mountainous region ([Aksai Chin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aksai_Chin)) which China occupies militarily in a disputed zone with India, 2400 km from the model's location. Speculation has been rife that the terrain is used for military exercises of familiarisation.[^o16][^o17]

## Education {#Education}

A sand table is a device useful for teaching in the [early grades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Educational_stages) and for [special needs children](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_education).[^n5][^n6]
