# Fiber product of schemes

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fiber product of schemes"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), the **fiber product of schemes** is a fundamental construction. It has many interpretations and special cases. For example, the fiber product describes how an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) over one [field](wikipedia://en/10603) determines a variety over a bigger field, or the pullback of a family of varieties, or a fiber of a family of varieties. **Base change** is a closely related notion.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) is a broad setting for algebraic geometry. A fruitful philosophy (known as [Grothendieck's relative point of view](wikipedia://en/1816636)) is that much of algebraic geometry should be developed for a [morphism of schemes](wikipedia://en/7482797) *X* → *Y* (called a scheme *X* **over** *Y*), rather than for a single scheme *X*. For example, rather than simply studying [algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/253260), one can study families of curves over any base scheme *Y*. Indeed, the two approaches enrich each other.

In particular, a scheme over a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) *R* means a scheme *X* together with a morphism *X* → [Spec](wikipedia://en/37291)(*R*). The older notion of an algebraic variety over a field *k* is equivalent to a scheme over *k* with certain properties. (There are different conventions for exactly which schemes should be called "varieties". One standard choice is that a variety over a field *k* means an [integral separated](wikipedia://en/35457783) scheme of [finite type](wikipedia://en/70728366) over *k*.[^o4])

In general, a morphism of schemes *X* → *Y* can be imagined as a family of schemes parametrized by the points of *Y*. Given a morphism from some other scheme *Z* to *Y*, there should be a "pullback" family of schemes over *Z*. This is exactly the fiber product *X* ×<sub>*Y*</sub> *Z* → *Z*.

Formally: it is a useful property of the category of schemes that the [fiber product](wikipedia://en/841689) always exists.[^o5] That is, for any morphisms of schemes *X* → *Y* and *Z* → *Y*, there is a scheme *X* ×<sub>*Y*</sub> *Z* with morphisms to *X* and *Z*, making the diagram

[commutative](wikipedia://en/138484), and which is [universal](wikipedia://en/32245) with that property. That is, for any scheme *W* with morphisms to *X* and *Z* whose compositions to *Y* are equal, there is a unique morphism from *W* to *X* ×<sub>*Y*</sub> *Z* that makes the diagram commute. As always with universal properties, this condition determines the scheme *X* ×<sub>*Y*</sub> *Z* up to a unique isomorphism, if it exists. The proof that fiber products of schemes always do exist reduces the problem to the [tensor product of commutative rings](wikipedia://en/372395) (cf. [gluing schemes](wikipedia://en/49016437)). In particular, when *X*, *Y*, and *Z* are all [affine schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_scheme), so *X* = Spec(*A*), *Y* = Spec(*B*), and *Z* = Spec(*C*) for some commutative rings *A*,*B*,*C*, the fiber product is the affine scheme

$$X\times_Y Z = \operatorname{Spec}(A\otimes_B C).$$

The morphism *X* ×<sub>*Y*</sub> *Z* → *Z* is called the **base change** or **pullback** of the morphism *X* → *Y* via the morphism *Z* → *Y*.

In some cases, the fiber product of schemes has a right adjoint, the [restriction of scalars](wikipedia://en/361168).

## Interpretations and special cases {#Interpretations_and_special_cases}

- In the category of schemes over a field *k*, the **product** *X* × *Y* means the fiber product *X* ×<sub>*k*</sub> *Y* (which is shorthand for the fiber product over Spec(*k*)). For example, the product of affine spaces A<sup>*m*</sup> and A<sup>*n*</sup> over a field *k* is the affine space A<sup>*m*+*n*</sup> over *k*.
- For a scheme *X* over a field *k* and any [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) *E* of *k*, the **base change** *X*<sub>*E*</sub> means the fiber product *X* ×<sub>Spec(*k*)</sub> Spec(*E*). Here *X*<sub>*E*</sub> is a scheme over *E*. For example, if *X* is the curve in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) **P**2 over the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) **R** defined by the equation *xy*<sup>2</sup> = 7*z*<sup>3</sup>, then *X*<sub>**C**</sub> is the [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) curve in **P**2 defined by the same equation. Many properties of an algebraic variety over a field *k* can be defined in terms of its base change to the [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129) of *k*, which makes the situation simpler.
- Let *f*: *X* → *Y* be a morphism of schemes, and let *y* be a point in *Y*. Then there is a morphism Spec(*k*(*y*)) → *Y* with image *y*, where *k*(*y*) is the [residue field](wikipedia://en/10789680) of *y*. The **fiber** of *f* over *y* is defined as the fiber product *X* ×<sub>*Y*</sub> Spec(*k*(*y*)); this is a scheme over the field *k*(*y*).[^o8] This concept helps to justify the rough idea of a morphism of schemes *X* → *Y* as a family of schemes parametrized by *Y*.
- Let *X*, *Y*, and *Z* be schemes over a field *k*, with morphisms *X* → *Y* and *Z* → *Y* over *k*. Then the set of *k*-[rational points](wikipedia://en/2960200) of the fiber product *X* ×<sub>*Y*</sub> *Z* is easy to describe:

$$(X\times_Y Z)(k)=X(k)\times_{Y(k)}Z(k).$$

That is, a *k*-point of *X* ×<sub>*Y*</sub> *Z* can be identified with a pair of *k*-points of *X* and *Z* that have the same image in *Y*. This is immediate from the universal property of the fiber product of schemes.

- If *X* and *Z* are closed subschemes of a scheme *Y*, then the fiber product *X* ×<sub>*Y*</sub> *Z* is exactly the **[intersection](wikipedia://en/52580781)** *X* ∩ *Z*, with its natural scheme structure.[^o9] The same goes for open subschemes.

## Base change and descent {#Base_change_and_descent}

Some important properties P of morphisms of schemes are **preserved under arbitrary base change**. That is, if *X* → *Y* has property P and *Z* → *Y* is any morphism of schemes, then the base change *X* x<sub>*Y*</sub> *Z* → *Z* has property P. For example, [flat morphisms](wikipedia://en/542520), [smooth morphisms](wikipedia://en/7837041), [proper morphisms](wikipedia://en/603782), and many other classes of morphisms are preserved under arbitrary base change.[^o12]

The word **descent** refers to the reverse question: if the pulled-back morphism *X* x<sub>*Y*</sub> *Z* → *Z* has some property P, must the original morphism *X* → *Y* have property P? Clearly this is impossible in general: for example, *Z* might be the empty scheme, in which case the pulled-back morphism loses all information about the original morphism. But if the morphism *Z* → *Y* is flat and surjective (also called **faithfully flat**) and [quasi-compact](wikipedia://en/12410777), then many properties do descend from *Z* to *Y*. Properties that descend include flatness, smoothness, properness, and many other classes of morphisms.[^o13] These results form part of [Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042)'s theory of **[faithfully flat descent](wikipedia://en/361449)**.

Example: for any field extension *k* ⊂ *E*, the morphism Spec(*E*) → Spec(*k*) is faithfully flat and quasi-compact. So the descent results mentioned imply that a scheme *X* over *k* is smooth over *k* if and only if the base change *X*<sub>*E*</sub> is smooth over *E*. The same goes for properness and many other properties.
