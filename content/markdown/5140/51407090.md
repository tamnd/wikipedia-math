# Evelyn M. Kitagawa

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Evelyn M. Kitagawa"}

**Evelyn Mae Kitagawa** (1920 – September 15, 2007) was an American [sociologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociologist) and [demographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographer) who worked as a professor at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago) and became president of the [Population Association of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_Association_of_America) and chair of the [U.S. Census Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Census_Bureau)'s Advisory Committee on Population Statistics.[^o3] She is known for her book with [Philip Hauser](wikipedia://en/31251004), *Differential Mortality in the United States: A Study in Socioeconomic Epidemiology*, which discovered systematic correlations between the death rates of Americans and their income and level of education.[^o3][^o4] Kitagawa wrote the first paper on decomposing statistics into components associated with the joint movement of the levels and returns to predictors, known as [Kitagawa decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitagawa_decomposition).[^o5]

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

She was born as Evelyn Mae Rose, in 1920[^o7] in [Hanford, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanford,_California), to a family of Portuguese Catholic descent.[^o8] After earning a bachelor's degree in mathematics from the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley) in 1941, she began working for the [War Relocation Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_Relocation_Authority), which ran the [internment camps of Japanese-Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internment_of_Japanese_Americans) during World War II, as head of its statistics unit. In one of the camps, she met her future husband, [Joseph Mitsuo Kitagawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Kitagawa),[^o3] who had come to the US in 1941 as a divinity student and became an [Episcopalian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_Church_%28United_States%29) minister while interned. After marrying him, her family disowned her and she lost contact with them.[^o8]

Kitagawa earned her Ph.D. from the University of Chicago in 1951. She worked for a local urban research center, and then became an assistant professor at Chicago in 1954. She stayed there for the rest of her career, with a promotion to full professor in 1970, until her 1989 retirement. Her husband also worked at Chicago, as professor of history of religions and dean of the divinity school.[^o3]

Her honors included election as a [fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow) of the [American Sociological Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Sociological_Association) (1959) and [American Statistical Association](wikipedia://en/341988) (1968).

Her daughter, [Anne Rose Kitagawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Rose_Kitagawa), is notable as a curator of Asian art.[^o8]
