# Quasi-sphere

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quasi-sphere"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028), a **quasi-sphere** is a generalization of the [hypersphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere) and the [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) to the context of a [pseudo-Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/10962546). It may be described as the set of points for which the [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) for the space applied to the displacement vector from a centre point is a constant value, with the inclusion of hyperplanes as a limiting case.

:::

## Notation and terminology {#Notation_and_terminology}

This article uses the following notation and terminology:

- A **pseudo-Euclidean vector space**, denoted $\mathbf{R}^{s,t}$, is a real [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) with a [nondegenerate quadratic form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondegenerate_quadratic_form) with [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_%28quadratic_form%29) $(s, t)$. The quadratic form is permitted to be [definite](wikipedia://en/20437320) (where $s = 0$ or $t = 0$), making this a generalization of a [Euclidean vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_vector_space).
- A **pseudo-Euclidean space**, denoted $E^{s,t}$, is a real [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) in which [displacement vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_vector) are the elements of the space $\mathbf{R}^{s,t}$. It is distinguished from the vector space.
- The [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) $Q$ acting on a vector $x \in \mathbf{R}^{s,t}$, denoted $Q(x)$, is a generalization of the [squared Euclidean distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squared_Euclidean_distance) in a Euclidean space. [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) calls $Q(x)$ the *scalar square* of $x$.
- The [symmetric bilinear form](wikipedia://en/1803375) $B$ acting on two vectors $x, y \in \mathbf{R}^{s,t}$ is denoted $B(x, y)$ or $x \cdot y$. This is associated with the quadratic form $Q$.
- Two vectors $x, y \in \mathbf{R}^{s,t}$ are **orthogonal** if $x \cdot y = 0$.
- A **normal vector** at a point of a quasi-sphere is a nonzero vector that is orthogonal to each vector in the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) at that point.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A **quasi-sphere** is a [submanifold](wikipedia://en/525095) of a pseudo-Euclidean space $E^{s,t}$ consisting of the points $u$ for which the displacement vector $x = u - o$ from a reference point $o$ satisfies the equation

$$a x \cdot x + b \cdot x + c = 0,$$

where $a, c \in \mathbf{R}$ and $b, x \in \mathbf{R}^{s,t}$.

Since $a = 0$ is permitted, this definition includes hyperplanes; it is thus a generalization of [generalized circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_circle) and their analogues in any number of dimensions. This inclusion provides a more regular structure under [conformal transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_transformation) than if they are omitted.

This definition has been generalized to [affine spaces](wikipedia://en/298834) over [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) and [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) by replacing the quadratic form with a [Hermitian form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_form).

A quasi-sphere $P = \{x \in X : Q(x) = k\}$ in a quadratic space $(X, Q)$ has a **counter-sphere** $N = \{x \in X : Q(x) = -k\}$. Furthermore, if $k \ne 0$ and $L$ is an [isotropic line](wikipedia://en/8088814) in $X$ through $x = 0$, then $L \cap (P \cup N) = \emptyset$, puncturing the union of quasi-sphere and counter-sphere. One example is the [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122) that forms a quasi-sphere of the [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane_%28quadratic_forms%29), and its [conjugate hyperbola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_hyperbola), which is its counter-sphere.

:::

## Geometric characterizations {#Geometric_characterizations}

### Centre and radial scalar square {#Centre_and_radial_scalar_square}

The *centre* of a quasi-sphere is a point that has equal scalar square from every point of the quasi-sphere, the point at which the [pencil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pencil_%28mathematics%29) of lines normal to the tangent hyperplanes meet. If the quasi-sphere is a hyperplane, the centre is the [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139) defined by this pencil.

When $a \ne 0$, the displacement vector $p$ of the centre from the reference point and the radial scalar square $r$ may be found as follows. We put $Q(x - p) = r$, and comparing to the defining equation above for a quasi-sphere, we get

$$p = -\frac b {2a},$$

$$r = p\cdot p - \frac c a.$$

The case of $a = 0$ may be interpreted as the centre $p$ being a well-defined point at infinity with either infinite or zero radial scalar square (the latter for the case of a null hyperplane). Knowing $p$ (and $r$) in this case does not determine the hyperplane's position, though, only its orientation in space.

The radial scalar square may take on a positive, zero or negative value. When the quadratic form is definite, even though $p$ and $r$ may be determined from the above expressions, the set of vectors $x$ satisfying the defining equation may be empty, as is the case in a Euclidean space for a negative radial scalar square.

### Diameter and radius {#Diameter_and_radius}

Any pair of points, which need not be distinct, (including the option of up to one of these being a point at infinity) defines a diameter of a quasi-sphere. The quasi-sphere is the set of points for which the two displacement vectors from these two points are orthogonal.

Any point may be selected as a centre (including a point at infinity), and any other point on the quasi-sphere (other than a point at infinity) define a radius of a quasi-sphere, and thus specifies the quasi-sphere.

## Partitioning {#Partitioning}

Referring to the quadratic form applied to the displacement vector of a point on the quasi-sphere from the centre (i.e. $Q(x - p)$) as the *radial scalar square*, in any pseudo-Euclidean space the quasi-spheres may be separated into three disjoint sets: those with positive radial scalar square, those with negative radial scalar square, those with zero radial scalar square.

In a space with a positive-definite quadratic form (i.e. a Euclidean space), a quasi-sphere with negative radial scalar square is the empty set, one with zero radial scalar square consists of a single point, one with positive radial scalar square is a standard $n$-sphere, and one with zero curvature is a hyperplane that is partitioned with the $n$-spheres.
