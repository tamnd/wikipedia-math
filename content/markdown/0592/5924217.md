# Hilbert symbol

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hilbert symbol"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Hilbert symbol** or **norm-residue symbol** is a function (–, –) from *K*<sup>×</sup> × *K*<sup>×</sup> to the group of *n*th [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity) in a [local field](wikipedia://en/145375) *K* such as the fields of [reals](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [p-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423). It is related to [reciprocity laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocity_law_%28mathematics%29), and can be defined in terms of the [Artin symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin_symbol) of [local class field theory](wikipedia://en/669532). The Hilbert symbol was introduced by (txt, sections 64, 131) in his [Zahlbericht](wikipedia://en/33462303), with the slight difference that he defined it for elements of [global fields](wikipedia://en/288358) rather than for the larger local fields.

:::

The Hilbert symbol has been generalized to [higher local fields](wikipedia://en/32091871).

## Quadratic Hilbert symbol {#Quadratic_Hilbert_symbol}

Over a local field $K$ with [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742) of non-zero elements $K^\times$, the quadratic Hilbert symbol is the [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $K^\times\times K^\times\to\{\pm 1\}$ defined by

$$(a,b)=\begin{cases}+1,&\mbox{ if }z^2=ax^2+by^2\mbox{ has a non-zero solution }(x,y,z)\in K^3;\\-1,&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

Equivalently, $(a, b) = 1$ if and only if $b$ is equal to the [norm](wikipedia://en/450555) of an element of the quadratic extension $K[\sqrt{a}]$.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

The following three properties follow directly from the definition, by choosing suitable solutions of the [Diophantine equation](wikipedia://en/9109) above:

- If $a$ is a square, then $(a,b)=1$ for all $b$.
- For all $a,b$ in $K^\times$, $(a,b)=(b,a)$.
- For any $a$ in $K^\times$ such that $a-1$ is also in $K^\times$, we have $(a,1-a)=1$.

The (bi)multiplicativity, i.e.,

$$(a,b_1b_2)=(a,b_1)\cdot(a,b_2)$$

for any $a,b_1$ and $b_2$ in $K^\times$ is, however, more difficult to prove, and requires the development of [local class field theory](wikipedia://en/669532).

The third property shows that the Hilbert symbol is an example of a [Steinberg symbol](wikipedia://en/35903941) and thus factors over the second [Milnor K-group](wikipedia://en/2914128) $K^M_2 (K)$, which is by definition

*K*<sup>×</sup> ⊗ *K*<sup>×</sup> / (*a* ⊗ (1−*a)*, *a* ∈ *K*<sup>×</sup> \\ {1})

By the first property it even factors over $K^M_2 (K) / 2$. This is the first step towards the [Milnor conjecture](wikipedia://en/2914126).

:::

### Interpretation as an algebra {#Interpretation_as_an_algebra}

The Hilbert symbol can also be used to denote the [central simple algebra](wikipedia://en/404082) over *K* with basis 1,*i*,*j*,*k* and multiplication rules $i^2=a$, $j^2=b$, $ij=-ji=k$. In this case the algebra represents an element of order dividing 2 in the [Brauer group](wikipedia://en/404078) of *K*, which is identified with -1 if it is a division algebra and +1 if it is isomorphic to the algebra of 2 by 2 matrices.

### Hilbert symbols over the rationals {#Hilbert_symbols_over_the_rationals}

For a [place](wikipedia://en/347049) *v* of the [rational number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_number_field) and rational numbers *a*, *b* we let (*a*, *b*)<sub>*v*</sub> denote the value of the Hilbert symbol in the corresponding [completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) **Q**<sub>*v*</sub>. As usual, if *v* is the valuation attached to a prime number *p* then the corresponding completion is the [p-adic field](wikipedia://en/51423) and if *v* is the infinite place then the completion is the [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) field.

Over the reals, (*a*, *b*)<sub>∞</sub> is +1 if at least one of *a* or *b* is positive, and −1 if both are negative.

Over the p-adics with *p* odd, writing $a = p^{\alpha} u$ and $b = p^{\beta} v$, where *u* and *v* are integers [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) to *p*, we have

$(a,b)_p = (-1)^{\alpha\beta\epsilon(p)} \left(\frac{u}{p}\right)^\beta \left(\frac{v}{p}\right)^\alpha$, where $\epsilon(p) = (p-1)/2$

and the expression involves two [Legendre symbols](wikipedia://en/18567).

Over the 2-adics, again writing $a = 2^\alpha u$ and $b = 2^\beta v$, where *u* and *v* are [odd numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_number), we have

$(a,b)_2 = (-1)^{\epsilon(u)\epsilon(v) + \alpha\omega(v) + \beta\omega(u)}$, where $\omega(x) = (x^2-1)/8.$

It is known that if *v* ranges over all places, (*a*, *b*)<sub>*v*</sub> is 1 for almost all places. Therefore, the following product formula

$$\prod_v (a,b)_v = 1$$

makes sense. It is equivalent to the law of [quadratic reciprocity](wikipedia://en/25272).

### Kaplansky radical {#Kaplansky_radical}

The Hilbert symbol on a field *F* defines a map

$$(\cdot,\cdot) : F^*/F^{*2} \times F^*/F^{*2} \rightarrow \mathop{Br}(F)$$

where Br(*F*) is the Brauer group of *F*. The kernel of this mapping, the elements *a* such that (*a*,*b*)=1 for all *b*, is the **Kaplansky radical** of *F*.[^o11]

The radical is a subgroup of F<sup>\*</sup>/F<sup>\*2</sup>, identified with a subgroup of F<sup>\*</sup>. The radical is equal to F<sup>\*</sup> if and only if *F* has [*u*-invariant](wikipedia://en/36821900) at most 2.[^o12] In the opposite direction, a field with radical F<sup>\*2</sup> is termed a **Hilbert field**.[^o13]

:::

## The general Hilbert symbol {#The_general_Hilbert_symbol}

If *K* is a local field containing the group of *n*th roots of unity for some positive integer *n* prime to the characteristic of *K*, then the Hilbert symbol (,) is a function from *K*\*×*K*\* to μ<sub>*n*</sub>. In terms of the Artin symbol it can be defined by[^o15]

$$(a,b)\sqrt[n]{b} = (a,K(\sqrt[n]{b})/K)\sqrt[n]{b}$$

Hilbert originally defined the Hilbert symbol before the Artin symbol was discovered, and his definition (for *n* prime) used the power residue symbol when *K* has residue characteristic coprime to *n*, and was rather complicated when *K* has residue characteristic dividing *n*.

::: {#o17 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

The Hilbert symbol is (multiplicatively) bilinear:

(*ab*,*c*) = (*a*,*c*)(*b*,*c*)

(*a*,*bc*) = (*a*,*b*)(*a*,*c*)

skew symmetric:

(*a*,*b*) = (*b*,*a*)<sup>−1</sup>

nondegenerate:

(*a*,*b*)=1 for all *b* if and only if *a* is in *K*\*<sup>*n*</sup>

It detects norms (hence the name norm residue symbol):

(*a*,*b*)=1 if and only if *a* is a norm of an element in *K*($\sqrt[n]{b}$)

It has the ["symbol" properties](wikipedia://en/35903941):

(*a*,1–*a*)=1, (*a*,–*a*)=1.

:::

::: {#o19 .statement cue=named-heading name="Hilbert's reciprocity law"}

### Hilbert's reciprocity law {#Hilbert's_reciprocity_law}

Hilbert's reciprocity law states that if *a* and *b* are in an algebraic number field containing the *n*th roots of unity then[^o20]

$$\prod_p (a,b)_p=1$$

where the product is over the finite and infinite primes *p* of the number field, and where (,)<sub>*p*</sub> is the Hilbert symbol of the completion at *p*. Hilbert's reciprocity law follows from the [Artin reciprocity law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin_reciprocity_law) and the definition of the Hilbert symbol in terms of the Artin symbol.

:::

### Power residue symbol {#Power_residue_symbol}

If *K* is a number field containing the *n*th roots of unity, *p* is a prime ideal not dividing *n*, π is a prime element of the local field of *p*, and *a* is coprime to *p*, then the [power residue symbol](wikipedia://en/36953973) (${}^{a}_{p}$) is related to the Hilbert symbol by[^o22]

$$\binom{a}{p} = (\pi,a)_p$$

The power residue symbol is extended to fractional ideals by multiplicativity, and defined for elements of the number field by putting (${}^{a}_{b}$)=(${}^{a}_{(b)}$) where (*b*) is the principal ideal generated by *b*. Hilbert's reciprocity law then implies the following reciprocity law for the residue symbol, for *a* and *b* prime to each other and to *n*:

$$\binom{a}{b}=\binom{b}{a}\prod_{p|n,\infty}(a,b)_p$$
