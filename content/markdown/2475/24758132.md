# Constant (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Constant"}

a narrower treatment related to this subject

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the word **constant** conveys multiple meanings. As an adjective, it refers to non-variance (i.e. unchanging with respect to some other [value](wikipedia://en/4095147)); as a noun, it has two different meanings:

:::

- A fixed and well-defined [number](wikipedia://en/21690) or other non-changing [mathematical object](wikipedia://en/19453961), or the [symbol](wikipedia://en/73634) denoting it. The terms *[mathematical constant](wikipedia://en/48779551)* or *[physical constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_constant)* are sometimes used to distinguish this meaning.[^o4]
- A [function](wikipedia://en/185427) whose value remains unchanged (i.e., a *[constant function](wikipedia://en/320861)*). Such a constant is commonly represented by a [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109) which does not depend on the main variable(s) in question.

For example, a general [quadratic function](wikipedia://en/187240) is commonly written as $a x^2 + b x + c$, where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are constants ([coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) or parameters), and $x$ a [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109)—a placeholder for the [argument](wikipedia://en/32842052) of the function being studied. A more explicit way to denote this function is $x\mapsto a x^2 + b x + c$, which makes the function-argument status of $x$ (and by extension the constancy of $a$, $b$, and $c$) clear. In this example $a$, $b$, and $c$ are coefficients of the [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000). Since $c$ occurs in a term that does not involve $x$, it is called the [constant term of the polynomial](wikipedia://en/79861) and can be thought of as the coefficient of $x^{0}$. More generally, any polynomial term or expression of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) zero (no variable) is a constant.[^o6]

## Constant function {#Constant_function}

A constant may be used to define a [constant function](wikipedia://en/320861) that ignores its arguments and always gives the same value.[^o8] A constant function of a single variable, such as $f(x)=5$, has a [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of a horizontal line parallel to the *x*-axis.[^o9] Such a function always takes the same value (in this case 5), because the variable does not appear in the expression defining the function.

## Context-dependence {#Context-dependence}

The context-dependent nature of the concept of "constant" can be seen in this example from elementary calculus:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx} 2^x & = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{2^{x+h} - 2^x} h = \lim_{h\to 0} 2^x\frac{2^h - 1} h \\[8pt] & = 2^x \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{2^h - 1} h & & \text{since } x \text{ is constant (i.e. does not depend on } h\text{)} \\[8pt] & = 2^x \cdot\mathbf{constant,} & & \text{where }\mathbf{constant}\text{ means not depending on } x. \end{aligned}$$

"Constant" means not depending on some variable; not changing as that variable changes. In the first case above, it means not depending on *h*; in the second, it means not depending on *x*. A constant in a narrower context could be regarded as a variable in a broader context.

## Notable mathematical constants {#Notable_mathematical_constants}

Some values occur frequently in mathematics and are conventionally denoted by a specific symbol. These standard symbols and their values are called mathematical constants. Examples include:

- 0 ([zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero)).
- 1 ([one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One)), the [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) after zero.
- $\pi$ ([pi](wikipedia://en/23601)), the constant representing the [ratio](wikipedia://en/87837) of a circle's circumference to its diameter, approximately equal to 3.142.[^o12]
- $e$, approximately equal to 2.718.
- $i$, the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358) such that $i^{2} = -1$.
- $\sqrt{2}$ ([square root of 2](wikipedia://en/341682)), the length of the diagonal of a square with unit sides, approximately equal to 1.414.
- $\varphi$ ([golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386)), approximately equal to 1.618, or algebraically, $1+ \sqrt{5} \over 2$.

## Constants in calculus {#Constants_in_calculus}

In [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), constants are treated in several different ways depending on the operation. For example, the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) (rate of change) of a constant function is zero. This is because constants, by definition, do not change. Their derivative is hence zero.

Conversely, when [integrating](wikipedia://en/2823) a constant function, the constant is multiplied by the variable of integration.

During the evaluation of a [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602), a constant remains the same as it was before and after evaluation.

Integration of a function of one variable often involves a [constant of integration](wikipedia://en/147939). This arises because the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) is the [inverse](wikipedia://en/14907) (opposite) of the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) meaning that the aim of integration is to recover the original function before differentiation. The derivative of a constant function is zero, as noted above, and the differential operator is a linear operator, so functions that only differ by a constant term have the same derivative. To acknowledge this, a constant of integration is added to an [indefinite integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_integral); this ensures that all possible solutions are included. The constant of integration is generally written as 'c', and represents a constant with a fixed but undefined value.

::: {#o19 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

If $f$ is the constant function such that $f(x) = 72$ for every $x$ then

$$\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 0 \\ \int f(x) \,dx &= 72x + c\\ \lim_{x\rightarrow0}f(x)&=72 \end{aligned}$$
