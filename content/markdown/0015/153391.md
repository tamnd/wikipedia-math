# Local ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Local ring"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), more specifically in [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424), **local rings** are certain [rings](wikipedia://en/48404) that are comparatively simple, and serve to describe what is called "local behaviour", in the sense of functions defined on [algebraic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_varieties) or [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470), or of [algebraic number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_number_fields) examined at a particular [place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place_%28mathematics%29), or prime. **Local algebra** is the branch of [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990) that studies [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346) local rings and their [modules](wikipedia://en/276410).

:::

In practice, a commutative local ring often arises as the result of the [localization of a ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) at a [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928).

The concept of local rings was introduced by [Wolfgang Krull](wikipedia://en/964328) in 1938 under the name *Stellenringe*.[^o2] The English term *local ring* is due to [Zariski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski).[^o3]

## Definition and first consequences {#Definition_and_first_consequences}

A [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *R* is a **local ring** if it has any one of the following equivalent properties:

- *R* has a unique [maximal](wikipedia://en/48164) left [ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_ideal).
- *R* has a unique maximal right ideal.
- 1 ≠ 0 and the sum of any two non-[units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_%28algebra%29) in *R* is a non-unit.
- 1 ≠ 0 and if *x* is any element of *R*, then *x* or 1 − *x* is a unit.
- If a finite sum is a unit, then it has a term that is a unit (this says in particular that the empty sum cannot be a unit, so it implies 1 ≠ 0).

If these properties hold, then the unique maximal left ideal coincides with the unique maximal right ideal and with the ring's [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656). The third of the properties listed above says that the set of non-units in a local ring forms a (proper) ideal,[^o5] necessarily contained in the Jacobson radical. The fourth property can be paraphrased as follows: a ring *R* is local if and only if there do not exist two [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) proper ([principal](wikipedia://en/145126)) (left) ideals, where two ideals *I*<sub>1</sub>, *I*<sub>2</sub> are called *coprime* if *R* = *I*<sub>1</sub> + *I*<sub>2</sub>.

In the case of [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346), one does not have to distinguish between left, right and two-sided ideals: a commutative ring is local if and only if it has a unique maximal ideal. Before about 1960 many authors required that a local ring be (left and right) [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129), and (possibly non-Noetherian) local rings were called **quasi-local rings**. In this article this requirement is not imposed.

A local ring that is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) is called a **local domain**.

## Examples {#Examples}

- All [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) (and [skew fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew_field)) are local rings, since {0} is the only maximal ideal in these rings.
- The ring $\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ is a local ring ($p$ prime, $n \ge 1$). The unique maximal ideal consists of all multiples of $p$.
- More generally, a nonzero ring in which every element is either a unit or [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) is a local ring.
- An important class of local rings are [discrete valuation rings](wikipedia://en/848633), which are local [principal ideal domains](wikipedia://en/25039) that are not fields.
- The ring $\mathbb{C}[[x]]$, whose elements are infinite series $\sum_{i=0}^\infty a_ix^i$ where multiplications are given by $(\sum_{i=0}^\infty a_ix^i)(\sum_{i=0}^\infty b_ix^i)=\sum_{i=0}^\infty c_ix^i$ such that $c_n=\sum_{i+j=n}a_ib_j$, is local. Its unique maximal ideal consists of all elements that are not invertible. In other words, it consists of all elements with constant term zero.
- More generally, every ring of [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012) over a local ring is local; the maximal ideal consists of those power series with [constant term](wikipedia://en/79861) in the maximal ideal of the base ring.
- Similarly, the [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) of [dual numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_numbers) over any field is local. More generally, if *F* is a local ring and *n* is a positive integer, then the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) *F*\[*X*\]/(*X*<sup>*n*</sup>) is local with maximal ideal consisting of the classes of polynomials with constant term belonging to the maximal ideal of *F*, since one can use a [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630) to invert all other polynomials [modulo](wikipedia://en/25977) *X*<sup>*n*</sup>. If *F* is a field, then elements of *F*\[*X*\]/(*X*<sup>*n*</sup>) are either [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) or [invertible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible). (The dual numbers over *F* correspond to the case *n* = 2.)
- Nonzero quotient rings of local rings are local.
- The ring of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) with [odd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_number) denominator is local; its maximal ideal consists of the fractions with even numerator and odd denominator. It is $\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}$, the integers [localized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) at 2.
- More generally, given any [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) *R* and any [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) *P* of *R*, the [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) of *R* at *P* is local; the maximal ideal is the ideal generated by *P* in this localization; that is, the maximal ideal consists of all elements *a*/*s* with *a* ∈ *P* and *s* ∈ *R* - *P*.

::: {#o9 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=heading name="Non-examples"}

### Non-examples {#Non-examples}

- The [ring of polynomials](wikipedia://en/373065) $K[x]$ over a field $K$ is not local, since $x$ and $1 - x$ are non-units, but their sum is a unit.
- The ring of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ is not local since it has a maximal ideal $(p)$ for every prime $p$.
- $\mathbb{Z}$/(*pq*)$\mathbb{Z}$, where *p* and *q* are distinct prime numbers. Both (*p*) and (*q*) are maximal ideals here.

:::

### Ring of germs {#Ring_of_germs}

To motivate the name "local" for these rings, we consider real-valued [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) defined on some [open interval](wikipedia://en/49172) around $0$ of the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line). We are only interested in the behavior of these functions near $0$ (their "local behavior") and we will therefore identify two functions if they agree on some (possibly very small) open interval around $0$. This identification defines an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259), and the [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) are what are called the "[germs](wikipedia://en/352714) of real-valued continuous functions at $0$". These germs can be added and multiplied and form a commutative ring.

To see that this ring of germs is local, we need to characterize its invertible elements. A germ $f$ is invertible if and only if $f(0)\neq 0$. The reason: if $f(0)\neq 0$, then by continuity there is an open interval around $0$ where $f$ is non-zero, and we can form the function $g(x)=\frac 1{f(x)}$ on this interval. The function $g$ gives rise to a germ, and the product of $fg$ is equal to $1$. (Conversely, if $f$ is invertible, then there is some $g$ such that $f(0)g(0)=1$, hence $f(0)\neq 0$.)

With this characterization, it is clear that the sum of any two non-invertible germs is again non-invertible, and we have a commutative local ring. The maximal ideal of this ring consists precisely of those germs $f$ with $f(0)=0$.

Exactly the same arguments work for the ring of germs of continuous real-valued functions on any [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) at a given point, or the ring of germs of [differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable) functions on any [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) at a given point, or the ring of germs of [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) on any [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) at a given point. All these rings are therefore local. These examples help to explain why [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754), the generalizations of varieties, are defined as special [locally ringed spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_ringed_space).

:::

### Valuation theory {#Valuation_theory}

Local rings play a major role in valuation theory. By definition, a [valuation ring](wikipedia://en/966856) of a field *K* is a subring *R* such that for every non-zero element *x* of *K*, at least one of *x* and *x*<sup>−1</sup> is in *R*. Any such subring will be a local ring. For example, the ring of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) with [odd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_number) denominator (mentioned above) is a valuation ring in $\mathbb{Q}$.

Given a field *K*, which may or may not be a [function field](wikipedia://en/2783843), we may look for local rings in it. If *K* were indeed the function field of an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) *V*, then for each point *P* of *V* we could try to define a valuation ring *R* of functions "defined at" *P*. In cases where *V* has dimension 2 or more there is a difficulty that is seen this way: if *F* and *G* are rational functions on *V* with

*F*(*P*) = *G*(*P*) = 0,

the function

*F*/*G*

is an [indeterminate form](wikipedia://en/366723) at *P*. Considering a simple example, such as

*Y*/*X*,

approached along a line

*Y* = *tX*,

one sees that the *value at* *P* is a concept without a simple definition. It is replaced by using valuations.

### Non-commutative {#Non-commutative}

Non-commutative local rings arise naturally as [endomorphism rings](wikipedia://en/59623) in the study of [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) decompositions of [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) over some other rings. Specifically, if the endomorphism ring of the module *M* is local, then *M* is [indecomposable](wikipedia://en/810549); conversely, if the module *M* has finite [length](wikipedia://en/816070) and is indecomposable, then its endomorphism ring is local.

If *k* is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) *p* > 0 and *G* is a finite [*p*-group](wikipedia://en/24664), then the [group algebra](wikipedia://en/349223) *kG* is local.

## Some facts and definitions {#Some_facts_and_definitions}

### Commutative case {#Commutative_case}

We also write (*R*, *m*) for a commutative local ring *R* with maximal ideal *m*. Every such ring becomes a [topological ring](wikipedia://en/60097) in a natural way if one takes the powers of *m* as a [neighborhood base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_base) of 0. This is the [*m*-adic topology](wikipedia://en/60123) on *R*. If (*R*, *m*) is a commutative [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) local ring, then

$$\bigcap_{i=1}^\infty m^i = \{0\}$$

(**Krull's intersection theorem**), and it follows that *R* with the *m*-adic topology is a [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637). The theorem is a consequence of the [Artin–Rees lemma](wikipedia://en/9553854) together with [Nakayama's lemma](wikipedia://en/1835001), and, as such, the "Noetherian" assumption is crucial. Indeed, let *R* be the ring of germs of infinitely differentiable functions at 0 in the real line and *m* be the maximal ideal $(x)$. Then a nonzero function $e^{-{1 \over x^2}}$ belongs to $m^n$ for any *n*, since that function divided by $x^n$ is still smooth.

As for any topological ring, one can ask whether (*R*, *m*) is [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_uniform_space) (as a [uniform space](wikipedia://en/32339)); if it is not, one considers its [completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completion_%28ring_theory%29), again a local ring. Complete Noetherian local rings are classified by the [Cohen structure theorem](wikipedia://en/27443678).

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), especially when *R* is the local ring of a scheme at some point *P*, *R* / *m* is called the *[residue field](wikipedia://en/10789680)* of the local ring or residue field of the point *P*.

If (*R*, *m*) and (*S*, *n*) are local rings, then a **local ring homomorphism** from *R* to *S* is a [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) *f* : *R* → *S* with the property *f*(*m*) ⊆ *n*.[^o17] These are precisely the ring homomorphisms that are continuous with respect to the given topologies on *R* and *S*. For example, consider the ring morphism $\mathbb{C}[x]/(x^3) \to \mathbb{C}[x,y]/(x^3,x^2y,y^4)$ sending $x \mapsto x$. The preimage of $(x,y)$ is $(x)$. Another example of a local ring morphism is given by $\mathbb{C}[x]/(x^3) \to \mathbb{C}[x]/(x^2)$.

### General case {#General_case}

The [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656) *m* of a local ring *R* (which is equal to the unique maximal left ideal and also to the unique maximal right ideal) consists precisely of the non-units of the ring; furthermore, it is the unique maximal two-sided ideal of *R*. However, in the non-commutative case, having a unique maximal two-sided ideal is not equivalent to being local.[^o19]

For an element *x* of the local ring *R*, the following are equivalent:

- *x* has a left inverse
- *x* has a right inverse
- *x* is invertible
- *x* is not in *m*.

If (*R*, *m*) is local, then the [factor ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_ring) *R*/*m* is a [skew field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew_field). If *J* ≠ *R* is any two-sided ideal in *R*, then the factor ring *R*/*J* is again local, with maximal ideal *m*/*J*.

A [deep theorem](wikipedia://en/62610482) by [Irving Kaplansky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irving_Kaplansky) says that any [projective module](wikipedia://en/364488) over a local ring is [free](wikipedia://en/326454), though the case where the module is finitely-generated is a simple corollary to [Nakayama's lemma](wikipedia://en/1835001). This has an interesting consequence in terms of [Morita equivalence](wikipedia://en/3296134). Namely, if *P* is a [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) projective *R* module, then *P* is isomorphic to the free module *R*<sup>*n*</sup>, and hence the ring of endomorphisms $\mathrm{End}_R(P)$ is isomorphic to the full ring of matrices $\mathrm{M}_n(R)$. Since every ring Morita equivalent to the local ring *R* is of the form $\mathrm{End}_R(P)$ for such a *P*, the conclusion is that the only rings Morita equivalent to a local ring *R* are (isomorphic to) the matrix rings over *R*.

:::
