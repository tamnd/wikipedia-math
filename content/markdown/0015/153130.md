In [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), the **quaternion group** Q<sub>8</sub> (sometimes just denoted by Q) is a [non-abelian group](wikipedia://en/4791400) of [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_order) eight, isomorphic to the eight-element subset $\{1,i,j,k,-1,-i,-j,-k\}$ of the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) under multiplication. It is given by the [group presentation](wikipedia://en/99494)

:::

$$\mathrm{Q}_8 = \langle \bar{e},i,j,k \mid \bar{e}^2 = e, \;i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = \bar{e} \rangle ,$$

where *e* is the identity element and $\overline{e}$ [commutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity) with the other elements of the group. These relations, discovered by [W. R. Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._R._Hamilton), also generate the quaternions as an algebra over the real numbers.

Another presentation of Q<sub>8</sub> is

$$\mathrm{Q}_8 = \langle a,b \mid a^4 = e, a^2 = b^2, ba = a^{-1}b\rangle.$$

Like many other finite groups, it [can be realized](wikipedia://en/739166) as the Galois group of a certain field of [algebraic numbers](wikipedia://en/1158).[^o22]

## Compared to dihedral group {#Compared_to_dihedral_group}

The quaternion group Q<sub>8</sub> has the same order as the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) [D<sub>4</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Examples_of_groups#The_symmetry_group_of_a_square:_dihedral_group_of_order_8), but a different structure, as shown by their Cayley and cycle graphs:

::: {#o4 .table cue=table}

|  | Q<sub>8</sub> | [D<sub>4</sub>](wikipedia://en/78749515) |
|---|---|---|
| [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) | *Image: Cayley graph Q8.svg.* Red arrows connect *g*→*gi*, green connect *g*→*gj*. | *Image: Dih 4 Cayley Graph; generators a, b.svg.* |
| [Cycle graph](wikipedia://en/1681010) | *Image: GroupDiagramQ8.svg.* | *Image: Dih4 cycle graph.svg.* |

:::

In the diagrams for D<sub>4</sub>, the group elements are marked with their action on a letter F in the defining representation **R**<sup>2</sup>. The same cannot be done for Q<sub>8</sub>, since it has no faithful representation in **R**<sup>2</sup> or **R**<sup>3</sup>. D<sub>4</sub> can be realized as a subset of the [split-quaternions](wikipedia://en/1461265) in the same way that Q<sub>8</sub> can be viewed as a subset of the quaternions.

## Properties {#Properties}

The elements *i*, *j*, and *k* all have [order](wikipedia://en/493527) four in Q<sub>8</sub> and any two of them generate the entire group. Another [presentation](wikipedia://en/99494) of Q<sub>8</sub>[^o12] based in only two elements to skip this redundancy is:

$$\left \langle x,y \mid x^4 = 1, x^2 = y^2, y^{-1}xy = x^{-1} \right \rangle.$$

This presentation is still redundant, since the relation $x^4 = 1$ is implied by the other two relations, and may be dropped. The quaternion group has also a shorter presentation with two generators and two relations[^n1]

$$\left \langle x,y \mid x^2 = y^2, xyx = y \right \rangle.$$

For instance, writing the group elements in [lexicographically](wikipedia://en/696408#lexicographic_order) minimal normal forms, one may identify:

> $$\{e, \bar e, i, \bar{i}, j, \bar{j}, k, \bar{k}\} \leftrightarrow \{e, x^2, x, x^3, y, x^2 y, xy, x^3 y \}.$$

The quaternion group has the unusual property of being [Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_group): Q<sub>8</sub> is non-abelian, but every [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) is [normal](wikipedia://en/21918).[^o14] Every Hamiltonian group contains a copy of Q<sub>8</sub>.[^o15]

The quaternion group Q<sub>8</sub> and the dihedral group D<sub>4</sub> are the two smallest examples of a [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569) non-abelian group.

The [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_a_group) and the [commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847) of Q<sub>8</sub> is the subgroup $\{e,\bar{e}\}$. The [inner automorphism group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_automorphism_group) of Q<sub>8</sub> is given by the group modulo its center, i.e. the [factor group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_group) $\mathrm{Q}_8/\{e,\bar{e}\},$ which is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the [Klein four-group](wikipedia://en/41928) V. The full [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of Q<sub>8</sub> is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to S<sub>4</sub>, the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) on four letters (see *Matrix representations* below), and the [outer automorphism group](wikipedia://en/628466) of Q<sub>8</sub> is thus S<sub>4</sub>/V, which is isomorphic to S<sub>3</sub>.

The quaternion group Q<sub>8</sub> has five conjugacy classes, $\{e\}, \{\bar{e}\}, \{i,\bar{i}\}, \{j,\bar{j}\}, \{k,\bar{k}\},$ and so five [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982) over the complex numbers, with dimensions 1, 1, 1, 1, 2:

**Trivial representation**.

**Sign representations with i, j, k-kernel**: Q<sub>8</sub> has three maximal normal subgroups: the cyclic subgroups generated by i, j, and k respectively. For each maximal normal subgroup *N*, we obtain a one-dimensional representation factoring through the 2-element [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) *G*/*N*. The representation sends elements of *N* to 1, and elements outside *N* to −1.

**2-dimensional representation**: Described below in *Matrix representations*. It is not [realizable over the real numbers](wikipedia://en/695101), but is a complex representation: indeed, it is just the quaternions $\mathbb{H}$ considered as an algebra over $\mathbb C$, and the action is that of left multiplication by $Q_8\subset \mathbb H$.

The [character table](wikipedia://en/911520) of Q<sub>8</sub> turns out to be the same as that of D<sub>4</sub>:

::: {#o16 .table cue=table}

| Representation(ρ)/Conjugacy class | { e } | { $\overline{e}$ } | { i, $\overline{i}$ } | { j, $\overline{j}$ } | { k, $\overline{k}$ } |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Trivial representation | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| Sign representation with i-kernel | 1 | 1 | 1 | −1 | −1 |
| Sign representation with j-kernel | 1 | 1 | −1 | 1 | −1 |
| Sign representation with k-kernel | 1 | 1 | −1 | −1 | 1 |
| 2-dimensional representation | 2 | −2 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

:::

Nevertheless, all the irreducible characters $\chi_\rho$ in the rows above have real values, this gives the [decomposition](wikipedia://en/3876151#Classification) of the real [group algebra](wikipedia://en/349223) of $G = \mathrm{Q}_8$ into minimal two-sided [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977):

$$\mathbb{R}[\mathrm{Q}_8]=\bigoplus_\rho (e_\rho),$$

where the [idempotents](wikipedia://en/361940) $e_\rho\in \mathbb{R}[\mathrm{Q}_8]$ correspond to the irreducibles:

$$e_\rho = \frac{\dim(\rho)}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G} \chi_\rho(g^{-1})g,$$

so that

$$\begin{aligned} e_{\text{triv}} &= \tfrac 18(e + \bar e + i +\bar i+j+\bar j+k+\bar k) \\ e_{i\text{-ker}} &= \tfrac 18(e + \bar e + i +\bar i-j-\bar j-k-\bar k) \\ e_{j\text{-ker}} &= \tfrac 18(e + \bar e - i -\bar i+j+\bar j-k-\bar k) \\ e_{k\text{-ker}} &= \tfrac 18(e + \bar e - i -\bar i-j-\bar j+k+\bar k) \\ e_{2} &= \tfrac 28(2e - 2\bar e) = \tfrac 12(e - \bar e) \end{aligned}$$

Each of these irreducible ideals is isomorphic to a real [central simple algebra](wikipedia://en/404082), the first four to the real field $\mathbb{R}$. The last ideal $(e_2)$ is isomorphic to the [skew field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew_field) of [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) $\mathbb{H}$ by the correspondence:

$$\begin{aligned} \tfrac12(e-\bar e) &\longleftrightarrow 1, \\ \tfrac12(i-\bar i) &\longleftrightarrow i, \\ \tfrac12(j-\bar j) &\longleftrightarrow j, \\ \tfrac12(k-\bar k) &\longleftrightarrow k. \end{aligned}$$

Furthermore, the projection homomorphism $\mathbb{R}[\mathrm{Q}_8]\to (e_2)\cong \mathbb{H}$ given by $r\mapsto re_2$ has kernel ideal generated by the idempotent:

$$e_2^\perp = e_1+e_{i\text{-ker}}+e_{j\text{-ker}}+e_{k\text{-ker}} = \tfrac{1}{2}(e+\bar e),$$

so the quaternions can also be obtained as the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) $\mathbb{R}[\mathrm{Q}_8]/(e+\bar e)\cong \mathbb H$. Note that this is irreducible as a real representation of $Q_8$, but splits into two copies of the two-dimensional irreducible when extended to the complex numbers. Indeed, the complex group algebra is $\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Q}_8] \cong \mathbb{C}^{\oplus 4} \oplus M_2(\mathbb{C}),$ where $M_2(\mathbb{C}) \cong \mathbb{H} \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C}$ is the algebra of [biquaternions](wikipedia://en/1207070).

:::

## Matrix representations {#Matrix_representations}

*Image: Quaternion group; Cayley table; subgroup of SL(2,C).svg.* Multiplication table of quaternion group as a subgroup of [SL](wikipedia://en/144823)(2,[**C**](wikipedia://en/5826)). The entries are represented by sectors corresponding to their arguments: 1 (green), *i* (blue), −1 (red), −*i* (yellow).

The two-dimensional irreducible complex [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) described above gives the quaternion group Q<sub>8</sub> as a subgroup of the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) $\operatorname{GL}(2, \mathbb{C})$. The quaternion group is a multiplicative subgroup of the quaternion algebra:

$$\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{R} 1 + \mathbb{R} i + \mathbb{R} j + \mathbb{R} k= 1 \mathbb{C}+ j \mathbb{C} ,$$

which has a [regular representation](wikipedia://en/360011) $\rho:\mathbb{H} \to \operatorname{M}(2, \mathbb{C})$ by left multiplication on itself considered as a right complex vector space with basis $\{1,j\},$ so that $z \in \mathbb{H}$ corresponds to the $\mathbb{C}$-linear mapping $\rho_z: a + jb \mapsto z\cdot(a + jb).$ The resulting representation

$$\begin{cases} \rho:\mathrm{Q}_8 \to \operatorname{GL}(2,\mathbb{C})\\ g\longmapsto\rho_g \end{cases}$$

is given by:

$$\begin{matrix} e \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} & i \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & \!\!\!\!-i \end{pmatrix}& j \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \!\!\!\!-1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}& k \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \!\!\!\!-i \\ \!\!\!-i & 0 \end{pmatrix} \\ \overline{e} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \!\!\!-1 & 0 \\ 0 & \!\!\!\!-1 \end{pmatrix} & \overline{i} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \!\!\!-i & 0 \\ 0 & i \end{pmatrix}& \overline{j} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ \!\!\!-1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}& \overline{k} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{matrix}$$

Since all of the above matrices have unit determinant, this is a representation of Q<sub>8</sub> in the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) $\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})$.[^o18]

A variant gives a representation by [unitary matrices](wikipedia://en/129222) (table at right). Let $g\in \mathrm{Q}_8$ correspond to the linear mapping $\rho_g:a+bj\mapsto (a + bj)\cdot jg^{-1}j^{-1},$ so that $\rho:\mathrm{Q}_8 \to \operatorname{SU}(2)$ is given by:

$$\begin{matrix} e \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} & i \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & \!\!\!\!-i \end{pmatrix}& j \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ \!\!\!-1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}& k \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix} \\ \overline{e} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \!\!\!-1 & 0 \\ 0 & \!\!\!\!-1 \end{pmatrix} & \overline{i} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \!\!\!-i & 0 \\ 0 & i \end{pmatrix}& \overline{j} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \!\!\!\!-1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}& \overline{k} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \!\!\!\!-i \\ \!\!\!-i & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{matrix}$$

It is worth noting that physicists exclusively use a different convention for the $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ matrix representation to make contact with the usual [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/19593829#Pauli_matrices):

$$\begin{matrix} &e \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \quad\, 1_{2\times2} &i \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \!\!\!-i\! \\ \!\!-i\!\! & 0 \end{pmatrix} = -i \sigma_x &j \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \!\!\!-1\! \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = -i \sigma_y &k \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \!\!-i\!\! & 0 \\ 0 & i \end{pmatrix} = -i \sigma_z\\ &\overline{e} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \!\!-1\! & 0 \\ 0 & \!\!\!-1\! \end{pmatrix} = -1_{2\times2} &\overline{i} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \,\,\,\, i \sigma_x &\overline{j} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ \!\!-1\!\! & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \,\,\,\, i \sigma_y &\overline{k} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & \!\!\!-i\! \end{pmatrix} = \,\,\,\, i \sigma_z. \end{matrix}$$

This particular choice is convenient and elegant when one describes [spin-1/2 states](wikipedia://en/29276) in the $(\vec{J}^2, J_z)$ basis and considers [angular momentum ladder operators](wikipedia://en/3797203#Derivation_using_ladder_operators) $J_{\pm} = J_x \pm iJ_y.$

*Image: Quaternion group; Cayley table; subgroup of SL(2,3).svg.* Multiplication table of the quaternion group as a subgroup of SL(2,3). The field elements are denoted 0, +, −.

There is also an important action of Q<sub>8</sub> on the 2-dimensional vector space over the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) $\mathbb{F}_3 =\{0, 1, -1\}$ (table at right). A [modular representation](wikipedia://en/1110685) $\rho: \mathrm{Q}_8 \to \operatorname{SL}(2, 3)$ is given by

$$\begin{matrix} e \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} & i \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & \!\!\!\!-1 \end{pmatrix} & j \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \!\!\!-1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} & k \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \!\!\!\!-1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \\ \overline{e} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \!\!\!-1 & 0 \\ 0 & \!\!\!\!-1 \end{pmatrix} & \overline{i} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \!\!\!-1 & \!\!\!\!-1 \\ \!\!\!-1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} & \overline{j} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \!\!\!\!-1 \\ \!\!\!-1 & \!\!\!\!-1 \end{pmatrix} & \overline{k} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ \!\!\!-1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{matrix}$$

This representation can be obtained from the [extension field](wikipedia://en/11634):

$$\mathbb{F}_9 = \mathbb{F}_3 [k] = \mathbb{F}_3 1 + \mathbb{F}_3 k,$$

where $k^2=-1$ and the multiplicative group $\mathbb{F}_9^{\times}$ has four generators, $\pm(k\pm1),$ of order 8. For each $z \in \mathbb{F}_9,$ the two-dimensional $\mathbb{F}_3$-vector space $\mathbb{F}_9$ admits a linear mapping:

$$\begin{cases} \mu_z: \mathbb{F}_9 \to \mathbb{F}_9 \\ \mu_z(a+bk)=z\cdot(a+bk) \end{cases}$$

In addition we have the [Frobenius automorphism](wikipedia://en/1088270) $\phi(a+bk)=(a+bk)^3$ satisfying $\phi^2 = \mu_1$ and $\phi\mu_z = \mu_{\phi(z)}\phi.$ Then the above representation matrices are:

$$\begin{aligned} \rho(\bar e) &=\mu_{-1}, \\ \rho(i) &=\mu_{k+1}\phi, \\ \rho(j)&=\mu_{k-1} \phi, \\ \rho(k)&=\mu_{k}. \end{aligned}$$

This representation realizes Q<sub>8</sub> as a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of GL(2, 3). Thus, for each matrix $m\in \operatorname{GL}(2,3)$, we have a group automorphism

$$\begin{cases} \psi_m:\mathrm{Q}_8\to\mathrm{Q}_8 \\ \psi_m(g)=mgm^{-1} \end{cases}$$

with $\psi_I =\psi_{-I}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{Q}_8}.$ In fact, these give the full automorphism group as:

$$\operatorname{Aut}(\mathrm{Q}_8) \cong \operatorname{PGL}(2, 3) = \operatorname{GL}(2,3)/\{\pm I\}\cong S_4.$$

This is isomorphic to the symmetric group S<sub>4</sub> since the linear mappings $m:\mathbb{F}_3^2 \to \mathbb{F}_3^2$ permute the four one-dimensional subspaces of $\mathbb{F}_3^2,$ i.e., the four points of the [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) $\mathbb{P}^1 (\mathbb{F}_3) = \operatorname{PG}(1,3).$

Also, this representation permutes the eight non-zero vectors of $\mathbb{F}_3^2,$ giving an embedding of Q<sub>8</sub> in the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) S<sub>8</sub>, in addition to the embeddings given by the regular representations.

## Galois group {#Galois_group}

[Richard Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106) considered the field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})$ in attempting to relate the quaternion group to [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316).[^o20] In 1936 [Ernst Witt](wikipedia://en/2619911) published his approach to the quaternion group through Galois theory.[^o21]

In 1981, Richard Dean showed the quaternion group can be realized as the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) Gal(T/**Q**) where **Q** is the field of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) and T is the [splitting field](wikipedia://en/174440) of the polynomial

$$x^8 - 72 x^6 + 180 x^4 - 144 x^2 + 36.$$

The development uses the [fundamental theorem of Galois theory](wikipedia://en/1947635) in specifying four intermediate fields between **Q** and T and their Galois groups, as well as two theorems on cyclic extension of degree four over a field.[^o22]

## Generalized quaternion group {#Generalized_quaternion_group}

A **generalized quaternion group** Q<sub>4*n*</sub> of order 4*n* is defined by the presentation[^o12]

$$\langle x,y \mid x^{2n} = y^4 = 1, x^n = y^2, y^{-1}xy = x^{-1}\rangle$$

for an integer *n* ≥ 2, with the usual quaternion group given by *n* = 2.[^o25] [Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Scott_MacDonald_Coxeter) calls Q<sub>4*n*</sub> the [dicyclic group](wikipedia://en/154963) $\langle 2, 2, n\rangle$, a special case of the [binary polyhedral group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_polyhedral_group) $\langle \ell, m, n\rangle$ and related to the [polyhedral group](wikipedia://en/24192415) $(p,q,r)$ and the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) $(2,2,n)$. The generalized quaternion group can be realized as the subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb{C})$ generated by

$$\left(\begin{array}{cc} \omega_n & 0 \\ 0 & \overline{\omega}_n \end{array} \right) \mbox{ and } \left(\begin{array}{cc} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{array} \right)$$

where $\omega_n = e^{i\pi/n}$.[^o12] It can also be realized as the subgroup of unit quaternions generated by[^o26] $x=e^{i\pi/n}$ and $y=j$.

The generalized quaternion groups have the property that every [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) subgroup is cyclic.[^o27] It can be shown that a finite [*p*-group](wikipedia://en/24664) with this property (every abelian subgroup is cyclic) is either cyclic or a generalized quaternion group as defined above.[^o28] Another characterization is that a finite *p*-group in which there is a unique subgroup of order *p* is either cyclic or a 2-group isomorphic to generalized quaternion group.[^o29] In particular, for a finite field *F* with odd characteristic, the 2-Sylow subgroup of SL<sub>2</sub>(*F*) is non-abelian and has only one subgroup of order 2, so this 2-Sylow subgroup must be a generalized quaternion group, (Gorenstein 1980, 42). Letting *p<sup>r</sup>* be the size of *F*, where *p* is prime, the size of the 2-Sylow subgroup of SL<sub>2</sub>(*F*) is 2<sup>*n*</sup>, where *n* = ord<sub>2</sub>(*p*<sup>2</sup> − 1) + ord<sub>2</sub>(*r*).

The [Brauer–Suzuki theorem](wikipedia://en/8239308) shows that the groups whose Sylow 2-subgroups are generalized quaternion cannot be simple.

Another terminology reserves the name "generalized quaternion group" for a dicyclic group of order a power of 2,[^o31] which admits the presentation

$$\langle x,y \mid x^{2^m} = y^4 = 1, x^{2^{m-1}} = y^2, y^{-1}xy = x^{-1}\rangle.$$

:::
