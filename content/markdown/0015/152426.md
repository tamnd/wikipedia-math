# Giovanni Ceva

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Giovanni Ceva"}

**Giovanni Ceva** (September 1, 1647 – May 13, 1734) was an Italian [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) widely known for proving [Ceva's theorem](wikipedia://en/152428) in elementary [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446). His brother, [Tommaso Ceva](wikipedia://en/2005652), was also a well-known poet and mathematician.

:::

## Life {#Life}

Ceva was born in Milan in 1647 and received his first education at the Brera College, the [Jesuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuits) school in Milan. His brother [Tommaso](wikipedia://en/2005652) was to become a distinguished teacher of this college, as well as a humanist and a mathematician of renown. Unlike Tommaso, Giovanni left the Brera College and went to the [University of Pisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pisa) where he studied mathematics and geometry under the guidance of Donato Rossetti and [Alessandro Marchetti](wikipedia://en/12108478), both pupils of [Borelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Alfonso_Borelli).[^n1] In Pisa he befriended the mathematician [Michelangelo Ricci](wikipedia://en/34287470), a pupil of [Castelli](wikipedia://en/855653) and a friend of [Torricelli](wikipedia://en/210413).[^n2]

After teaching for a brief period at the University of Pisa, Ceva went back to Milan, where he published in 1678 the *De lineis rectis se invicem secantibus statica* *constructio* and, in 1682, the *Opuscula mathematica de potentiis obliquis, de pendulis, de vasis et de fluminibus*. By 1684 Ceva had settled in [Mantua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantua) as mathematician and engineer at the [Gonzaga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Gonzaga) court. He was designated as the Professor of [Mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) at the University of Mantua in 1686. Ceva continued to undertake mathematical research until the end of his life. He corresponded with many of the leading scientists of the day, including [Vincenzo Viviani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Viviani) and [Giovanni Girolamo Saccheri](wikipedia://en/30875270).

Giovanni Ceva died in Mantua on May 13, 1734 and was buried in the church of St. Teresa de Carmelitani Scalzi.[^n1] The 6.5-kilometer sized main-belt asteroid [12579 Ceva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/12579_Ceva), discovered at the [San Vittore Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Vittore_Observatory) in 1999, was named in honor of him and his brother, Tommaso.[^o7]

## Work {#Work}

*Image: Ceva - De lineis rectis se invicem secantibus statica constructio, 1678 - 828340.jpg.* *De lineis rectis se invicem secantibus statica constructio*, 1678

Ceva studied [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) for most of his long life. In 1678, he published a now famous theorem on [synthetic geometry](wikipedia://en/267484) in a triangle called Ceva's Theorem. The theorem states that if three line segments are drawn from the vertices of a triangle to the opposite sides, then the three line segments are concurrent if, and only if, the product of the ratios of the newly created line segments on each side of the triangle is equal to one. Ceva published this theorem in *De lineis rectis*. The theorem was already known to [Yusuf Al-Mu'taman ibn Hűd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yusuf_al-Mu'taman_ibn_Hud) in 11th century but had by then fallen into oblivion and was unknown in the West.

Ceva also rediscovered and published [Menelaus's theorem](wikipedia://en/1480484). He published *Opuscula mathematica* in 1682 and *Geometria Motus* in 1692, as well. In *Geometria Motus*, he anticipated the [infinitesimal calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal_calculus). Finally, Ceva wrote *De Re Nummaria* in 1711, which was one of the first books in [mathematical economics](wikipedia://en/18967255).

Giovanni Ceva also studied applications of mechanics and [statics](wikipedia://en/28767) to geometric systems. At one point, however, he incorrectly resolved that the periods of [oscillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscillation) of two [pendulums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendulum) were in the same ratio as their lengths, but he later realized and corrected the error. Ceva also worked on [hydraulics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulics). In 1728, he published *Opus hydrostaticum* which discusses his work in [hydraulics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulics). In fact, he used his knowledge of [hydraulics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulics) to stop a project from diverting the river Reno into the river [Po](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Po_River).
