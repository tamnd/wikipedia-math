# Negative number

*Image: US Navy 070317-N-3642E-379 During the warmest part of the day, a thermometer outside of the Applied Physics Laboratory Ice Station's (APLIS) mess tent still does not break out of the sub-freezing temperatures.jpg.* Thermometer indicating a negative [Fahrenheit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fahrenheit) temperature (−4 °F).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Negative number"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **negative number** is the [opposite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposite_%28mathematics%29) of a positive [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438).[^o2] Equivalently, a negative number is a real number that is [less than](wikipedia://en/89489) [zero](wikipedia://en/34513). Negative numbers are often used to represent the [magnitude](wikipedia://en/577301) of a loss or deficiency. A [debt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt) that is owed may be thought of as a negative asset. If a quantity, such as the charge on an electron, may have either of two opposite senses, then one may choose to distinguish between those senses—perhaps arbitrarily—as *positive* and *negative*. Negative numbers are used to describe values on a scale that goes below zero, such as the [Celsius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celsius) and [Fahrenheit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fahrenheit) scales for temperature. The laws of arithmetic for negative numbers ensure that the common-sense idea of an opposite is reflected in arithmetic. For example, −(−3) = 3 because the opposite of an opposite is the original value.

:::

Negative numbers are usually written with a [minus sign](wikipedia://en/245206) in front. For example, −3 represents a negative quantity with a magnitude of three, and is pronounced and read as "minus three" or "negative three". Conversely, a number that is greater than zero is called *positive*; zero is usually ([but not always](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signed_zero)) thought of as neither positive nor [negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_zero).[^o3] The positivity of a number may be emphasized by placing a plus sign before it, e.g. +3. In general, the negativity or positivity of a number is referred to as its [sign](wikipedia://en/7951270).

Every real number other than zero is either positive or negative. The non-negative whole numbers are referred to as [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) (i.e., 0, 1, 2, 3, ...), while the positive and negative whole numbers (together with zero) are referred to as [integers](wikipedia://en/14563). (Some definitions of the natural numbers exclude zero.)

In [bookkeeping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bookkeeping), amounts owed are often represented by red numbers, or a number in parentheses, as an alternative notation to represent negative numbers.

Negative numbers were used in the *[Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nine_Chapters_on_the_Mathematical_Art)*, which in its present form dates from the period of the Chinese [Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty) (202 BC – AD 220), but may well contain much older material.[^o4] [Liu Hui](wikipedia://en/202719) (c. 3rd century) established rules for adding and subtracting negative numbers.[^o44] By the 7th century, Indian mathematicians such as [Brahmagupta](wikipedia://en/297206) were describing the use of negative numbers. [Islamic mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_mathematicians) further developed the rules of subtracting and multiplying negative numbers and solved problems with negative [coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficients).[^o50] Prior to the concept of negative numbers, mathematicians such as [Diophantus](wikipedia://en/9110) considered negative solutions to problems "false" and equations requiring negative solutions were described as absurd.[^o5] Western mathematicians like [Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) held that negative numbers were invalid, but still used them in calculations.[^o6][^o7]

### Signed numbers {#Signed_numbers}

In the context of negative numbers, a number that is greater than zero is referred to as **positive**. Thus every [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) other than zero is either positive or negative, while zero itself is not considered to have a sign. Positive numbers are sometimes written with a [plus sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plus_sign) in front, e.g. $+3$ denotes a positive three.

Because zero is neither positive nor negative, the term **nonnegative** is sometimes used to refer to a number that is either positive or zero, while **nonpositive** is used to refer to a number that is either negative or zero. Zero is a neutral number.

## Arithmetic involving negative numbers {#Arithmetic_involving_negative_numbers}

The [minus sign](wikipedia://en/245206) "−" signifies the [operator](wikipedia://en/22213) for both the binary (two-[operand](wikipedia://en/22656)) [operation](wikipedia://en/4140245) of [subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141) (as in $y - z$) and the unary (one-operand) operation of [negation](wikipedia://en/228312) (as in $-x$, or twice in $-(-x)$). A special case of unary negation occurs when it operates on a positive number, in which case the result is a negative number (as in $-5$).

The ambiguity of the "−" symbol does not generally lead to ambiguity in arithmetical expressions, because the order of operations makes only one interpretation or the other possible for each "−". However, it can lead to confusion and be difficult for a person to understand an expression when operator symbols appear adjacent to one another. A solution can be to parenthesize the unary "−" along with its operand.

For example, the expression $7 + -5$ may be clearer if written $7 + (-5)$ (even though they mean exactly the same thing formally). The [subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141) expression $7 - 5$ is a different expression that doesn't represent the same operations, but it evaluates to the same result.

### Uniqueness {#Uniqueness}

The additive inverse of a number is unique, as is shown by the following proof. As mentioned above, an additive inverse of a number is defined as a value which when added to the number yields zero.

Let *x* be a number and let *y* be its additive inverse. Suppose *y′* is another additive inverse of *x*. By definition,

$$x + y' = 0, \quad \text{and} \quad x + y = 0.$$

And so, *x* + *y′* = *x* + *y*. Using the law of cancellation for addition, it is seen that *y′* = *y*. Thus *y* is equal to any other additive inverse of *x*. That is, *y* is the unique additive inverse of *x*.

## History {#History}

For a long time, understanding of negative numbers was delayed by the impossibility of having a negative-number amount of a physical object, for example "minus-three apples", and negative solutions to problems were considered "false".

In [Hellenistic Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_Egypt), the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) mathematician [Diophantus](wikipedia://en/9110) in the 3rd century AD referred to an equation that was equivalent to $4x + 20 = 4$ (which has a negative solution) in *[Arithmetica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetica)*, saying that the equation was absurd.[^o42] For this reason Greek geometers were able to solve geometrically all forms of the quadratic equation which give positive roots, while they could take no account of others.[^o43]

Negative numbers appear for the first time in history in the *[Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nine_Chapters_on_the_Mathematical_Art)* (九章算術, *Jiǔ zhāng suàn-shù*), which in its present form dates from the [Han period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty), but may well contain much older material.[^o4] The mathematician [Liu Hui](wikipedia://en/202719) (c. 3rd century) established rules for the addition and subtraction of negative numbers. The historian Jean-Claude Martzloff theorized that the importance of duality in Chinese [natural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy) made it easier for the Chinese to accept the idea of negative numbers.[^o44] The Chinese were able to solve simultaneous equations involving negative numbers. The *Nine Chapters* used red [counting rods](wikipedia://en/26661047) to denote positive [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) and black rods for negative.[^o44][^o45] This system is the exact opposite of contemporary printing of positive and negative numbers in the fields of banking, accounting, and commerce, wherein red numbers denote negative values and black numbers signify positive values. Liu Hui writes: Now there are two opposite kinds of counting rods for gains and losses, let them be called positive and negative. Red counting rods are positive, black counting rods are negative.[^o44]

The ancient Indian *[Bakhshali Manuscript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakhshali_Manuscript)* carried out calculations with negative numbers, using "+" as a negative sign.[^o46] The date of the manuscript is uncertain. LV Gurjar dates it no later than the 4th century,[^o47] Hoernle dates it between the third and fourth centuries, Ayyangar and Pingree dates it to the 8th or 9th centuries,[^o48] and George Gheverghese Joseph dates it to about AD 400 and no later than the early 7th century.[^o49]

During the 7th century AD, negative numbers were used in India to represent debts. The [Indian mathematician](wikipedia://en/1848052) [Brahmagupta](wikipedia://en/297206), in *[Brahma-Sphuta-Siddhanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmasphutasiddhanta)* (written c. AD 630), discussed the use of negative numbers to produce a general form [quadratic formula](wikipedia://en/59217) similar to the one in use today.[^o42]

In the 9th century, [Islamic mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_mathematicians) were familiar with negative numbers from the works of Indian mathematicians, but the recognition and use of negative numbers during this period remained timid.[^o50] [Al-Khwarizmi](wikipedia://en/649861) in his *[Al-jabr wa'l-muqabala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Compendious_Book_on_Calculation_by_Completion_and_Balancing)* (from which the word "algebra" derives) did not use negative numbers or negative coefficients.[^o50] But within fifty years, [Abu Kamil](wikipedia://en/2479369) illustrated the rules of signs for expanding the multiplication $(a \pm b)(c \pm d)$,[^o51] and [al-Karaji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Karaji) wrote in his *al-Fakhrī* that "negative quantities must be counted as terms".[^o50] In the 10th century, [Abū al-Wafā' al-Būzjānī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abū_al-Wafā'_al-Būzjānī) considered debts as negative numbers in *[A Book on What Is Necessary from the Science of Arithmetic for Scribes and Businessmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Book_on_What_Is_Necessary_from_the_Science_of_Arithmetic_for_Scribes_and_Businessmen)*.[^o51]

By the 12th century, al-Karaji's successors were to state the general rules of signs and use them to solve [polynomial divisions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_division).[^o50] As [al-Samaw'al](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Samaw'al) writes:

> the product of a negative number—*al-nāqiṣ* (loss)—by a positive number—*al-zāʾid* (gain)—is negative, and by a negative number is positive. If we subtract a negative number from a higher negative number, the remainder is their negative difference. The difference remains positive if we subtract a negative number from a lower negative number. If we subtract a negative number from a positive number, the remainder is their positive sum. If we subtract a positive number from an empty power (*martaba khāliyya*), the remainder is the same negative, and if we subtract a negative number from an empty power, the remainder is the same positive number.[^o50]

In the 12th century in India, [Bhāskara II](wikipedia://en/480509) gave negative roots for quadratic equations but rejected them because they were inappropriate in the context of the problem. He stated that a negative value is "in this case not to be taken, for it is inadequate; people do not approve of negative roots."

[Fibonacci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_of_Pisa#Important_publications) allowed negative solutions in financial problems where they could be interpreted as debits (chapter 13 of *[Liber Abaci](wikipedia://en/167699)*, 1202) and later as losses (in [*Flos*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_of_Pisa#Works), 1225).

In the 15th century, [Nicolas Chuquet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Chuquet), a Frenchman, used negative numbers as [exponents](wikipedia://en/99491)[^o52] but referred to them as "absurd numbers".[^o53]

[Michael Stifel](wikipedia://en/26485293) dealt with negative numbers in his [1544](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1544) AD *[Arithmetica Integra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetica_Integra)*, where he also called them *numeri absurdi* (absurd numbers).

In 1545, [Gerolamo Cardano](wikipedia://en/13145), in his [*Ars Magna*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ars_Magna_%28Gerolamo_Cardano%29), provided the first satisfactory treatment of negative numbers in Europe.[^o42] He did not allow negative numbers in his consideration of [cubic equations](wikipedia://en/180787), so he had to treat, for example, $x^3 + a x = b$ separately from $x^3 = a x + b$ (with $a, b > 0$ in both cases). In all, Cardano was driven to the study of thirteen types of cubic equations, each with all negative terms moved to the other side of the = sign to make them positive. (Cardano also dealt with [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers), but understandably liked them even less.)
