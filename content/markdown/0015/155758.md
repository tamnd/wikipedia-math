# Gravity assist

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gravity assist"}

A **gravity assist**, **gravity assist maneuver**, **swing-by**, or generally a **gravitational slingshot** in [orbital mechanics](wikipedia://en/182146), is a type of [spaceflight flyby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flyby_%28spaceflight%29) which makes use of the relative movement (e.g. orbit around the Sun) and [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) of a planet or other [astronomical object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_object) to alter the path and speed of a spacecraft, typically to save propellant and reduce expense.

:::

Gravity assistance can be used to [accelerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accelerate) a spacecraft, that is, to increase or decrease its speed or redirect its path. The "assist" is provided by the motion of the gravitating body as it pulls on the spacecraft.[^o2] Any gain or loss of [kinetic energy](wikipedia://en/17327) and [linear momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_momentum) by a passing spacecraft is correspondingly lost or gained by the gravitational body, in accordance with [Newton's Third Law](wikipedia://en/55212#Newton's_third_law). The gravity assist maneuver was first used in 1959 when the Soviet probe [Luna 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_3) photographed the far side of Earth's Moon, and it was used by interplanetary probes from [Mariner 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariner_10) onward, including the two [Voyager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_program) probes' notable [flybys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_flyby) of Jupiter and Saturn.

## Explanation {#Explanation}

*Image: GravAssis.gif.* Example encounter.[^o4]In the planet's frame of reference, the space probe leaves with the exact same speed at which it had arrived. But when observed in the reference frame of the Solar System (fixed to the Sun), the benefit of this maneuver becomes apparent. Here it can be seen how the probe gains speed by tapping energy from the speed of the planet as it orbits the Sun. (If the trajectory is designed to pass in front of the planet instead of behind it, the gravity assist can be used as a braking maneuver rather than accelerating.) Because the mass of the probe is many orders of magnitude smaller than that of the planet, while the result on the probe is quite significant, the deceleration reaction experienced by the planet, according to [Newton's third law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_third_law), is utterly imperceptible.

*Image: GravPoss.gif.* Possible outcomes of a gravity assist maneuver depending on the velocity vector and flyby position of the incoming spacecraft

A gravity assist around a planet changes a spacecraft's [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) (relative to the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun)) by entering and leaving the gravitational sphere of influence of a planet. The sum of the kinetic energies of both bodies remains constant (see [elastic collision](wikipedia://en/65907)). A slingshot maneuver can therefore be used to change the spaceship's trajectory and speed relative to the Sun.[^o5]

A close terrestrial analogy is provided by a tennis ball bouncing off the front of a moving train. Imagine standing on a train platform, and throwing a ball at 30 km/h toward a train approaching at 50 km/h. The driver of the train sees the ball approaching at 80 km/h and then departing at 80 km/h after the ball bounces elastically off the front of the train. Because of the train's motion, however, that departure is at 130 km/h relative to the train platform; the ball has added twice the train's velocity to its own.[^o6]

Translating this analogy into space: in the planet [reference frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference), the spaceship has a vertical velocity of *v* relative to the planet. After the slingshot occurs the spaceship is leaving on a course 90 degrees to that which it arrived on. It will still have a velocity of *v*, but in the horizontal direction.[^o4] In the Sun reference frame, the planet has a horizontal velocity of v, and by using the Pythagorean Theorem, the spaceship initially has a total velocity of $\sqrt{2}$*v*. After the spaceship leaves the planet, it will have a velocity of *v + v =* 2*v*, gaining approximately 0.6*v*.[^o4]

This oversimplified example cannot be refined without additional details regarding the orbit, but if the spaceship travels in a path which forms a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052), it can leave the planet in the opposite direction without firing its engine. This example is one of many trajectories and gains of speed the spaceship can experience.

This explanation might seem to violate the conservation of energy and momentum, apparently adding velocity to the spacecraft out of nothing, but the spacecraft's effects on the planet must also be taken into consideration to provide a complete picture of the mechanics involved. The linear momentum gained by the spaceship is equal in magnitude to that lost by the planet, so the spacecraft gains velocity and the planet loses velocity. However, the planet's enormous mass compared to the spacecraft makes the resulting change in its speed negligibly small even when compared to the [orbital perturbations](wikipedia://en/1490148) planets undergo due to interactions with other celestial bodies on astronomically short timescales. For example, one [metric ton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_ton) is a typical mass for an interplanetary space probe whereas [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter) [has a mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orders_of_magnitude_%28mass%29) of almost 2 × 10<sup>24</sup> metric tons. Therefore, a one-ton spacecraft passing Jupiter will theoretically cause the planet to lose approximately 5 × 10<sup>−25</sup> km/s of orbital velocity for every km/s of velocity relative to the Sun gained by the spacecraft. For all practical purposes the effects on the planet can be ignored in the calculation.[^o7]

Realistic portrayals of encounters in space require the consideration of three dimensions. The same principles apply as above except adding the planet's velocity to that of the spacecraft requires [vector addition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_addition) as shown below.

*Image: Grav slingshot diag.svg.* Two-dimensional schematic of gravitational slingshot. The arrows show the direction in which the spacecraft is traveling before and after the encounter. The length of the arrows shows the spacecraft's speed.

*Image: Mdis depart anot.ogv.* A view from MESSENGER as it uses Earth as a gravitational slingshot to decelerate to allow insertion into an orbit around Mercury

Due to the [reversibility of orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reversibility_of_orbits), gravitational slingshots can also be used to reduce the speed of a spacecraft. Both [Mariner 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariner_10) and [MESSENGER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MESSENGER) performed this maneuver to reach [Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_%28planet%29).

If more speed is needed than available from gravity assist alone, a rocket burn near the [periapsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periapsis) (closest planetary approach) uses the least fuel. A given rocket burn always provides the same change in velocity ([Δv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Δv)), but the change in kinetic energy is proportional to the vehicle's velocity at the time of the burn. Therefore the maximum kinetic energy is obtained when the burn occurs at the vehicle's maximum velocity (periapsis). The [Oberth effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oberth_effect) describes this technique in more detail.

## Historical origins {#Historical_origins}

In his paper "To Those Who Will Be Reading in Order to Build" ("Тем, кто будет читать, чтобы строить"),[^o9] published in 1938 but dated 1918–1919, [Yuri Kondratyuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuri_Kondratyuk) suggested that a spacecraft traveling between two planets could be accelerated at the beginning and end of its trajectory by using the gravity of the two planets' moons. The portion of his manuscript considering gravity-assists received no later development and was not published until the 1960s.[^o10] In his 1925 paper "Problems of Flight by Jet Propulsion: Interplanetary Flights" ("Проблема полета при помощи реактивных аппаратов: межпланетные полеты"),[^o11] [Friedrich Zander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Zander) showed a deep understanding of the physics behind the concept of gravity assist and its potential for the interplanetary exploration of the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System).[^o10]

Italian engineer [Gaetano Crocco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaetano_Crocco) was first to calculate an interplanetary journey considering multiple gravity-assists in 1956.[^o10]

The gravity assist maneuver was first used in 1959 when the Soviet probe [Luna 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_3) photographed the far side of the Moon. The maneuver relied on research performed under the direction of [Mstislav Keldysh](wikipedia://en/2769461) at the [Keldysh Institute of Applied Mathematics](wikipedia://en/984473).[^o12][^o13][^o14][^o15]

In 1961, [Michael Minovitch](wikipedia://en/1942312), [UCLA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCLA) graduate student who worked at NASA's [Jet Propulsion Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_Propulsion_Laboratory) (JPL), developed a gravity assist technique, that would later be used for the [Gary Flandro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Flandro)'s [Planetary Grand Tour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_Grand_Tour) idea.[^o16][^o17]

During the summer of 1964 at the NASA JPL, Gary Flandro was assigned the task of studying techniques for exploring the outer planets of the Solar System. In this study he discovered the rare alignment of the outer planets (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune) and conceived the Planetary Grand Tour multi-planet mission utilizing gravity assist to reduce mission duration from forty years to less than ten.[^o18]

## Purpose {#Purpose}

*Image: Voyager 2 velocity vs distance from sun.svg.* Plot of Voyager 2's heliocentric velocity against its distance from the Sun, illustrating the use of gravity assist to accelerate the spacecraft by Jupiter, Saturn and Uranus. To observe [Triton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triton_%28moon%29), Voyager 2 passed over Neptune's north pole resulting in an acceleration out of the plane of the ecliptic and reduced velocity away from the Sun.[^o2]

A spacecraft traveling from Earth to an inner planet will increase its relative speed because it is falling toward the Sun, and a spacecraft traveling from Earth to an outer planet will decrease its speed because it is leaving the vicinity of the Sun.

[Rocket engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_engine) can certainly be used to increase and decrease the speed of the spacecraft. However, rocket thrust takes propellant, propellant has mass, and even a small change in velocity (known as Δ*v*, or "delta-*v*", the [delta symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_%28letter%29) being used to represent a change and "v" signifying [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903)) translates to a far larger requirement for propellant needed to escape Earth's [gravity well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_well). This is because not only must the primary-stage engines lift the extra propellant, they must also lift the extra propellant beyond that which is needed to lift *that* additional propellant. The liftoff mass requirement [increases exponentially](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_equation) with an increase in the required delta-*v* of the spacecraft.

Because additional fuel is needed to lift fuel into space, space missions are designed with a tight propellant "budget", known as the "[delta-v budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta-v_budget)". The delta-v budget is in effect the total propellant that will be available after leaving the earth, for speeding up, slowing down, stabilization against external buffeting (by particles or other external effects), or direction changes, if it cannot acquire more propellant. The entire mission must be planned within that capability. Therefore, methods of speed and direction change that do not require fuel to be burned are advantageous, because they allow extra maneuvering capability and course enhancement, without spending fuel from the limited amount which has been carried into space. Gravity assist maneuvers can greatly change the speed of a spacecraft without expending propellant, and can save significant amounts of propellant, so they are a useful technique to save fuel.

## Limits {#Limits}

*Image: Voyager Path.svg.* The trajectories that enabled NASA's twin Voyager spacecraft to tour the four giant planets and achieve velocity to escape the Solar System

The main practical limit to the use of a gravity assist maneuver is that planets and other large masses are seldom in the right places to enable a voyage to a particular destination. For example, the [Voyager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_1) missions which started in the late 1970s were made possible by the "[Grand Tour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_Grand_Tour)" alignment of Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. A similar alignment will not occur again until the middle of the 22nd century. That is an extreme case, but even for less ambitious missions there are years when the planets are scattered in unsuitable parts of their orbits.

Another limitation is the distance of closest approach to the planet. The magnitude of the change in velocity depends on the spacecraft's approach velocity and the planet's escape velocity at the point of closest approach. The closer to the center of the planet that approach is, the greater the achievable change in velocity. The atmosphere, if any, of the available planet will set a limit to the approach distance; for bodies with no atmosphere, like the moon, the closest approach is set by the constraint that the trajectory must not intersect the surface. For planets with atmosphere, as a spacecraft gets deep into the atmosphere, the energy lost to drag can exceed that gained from the planet's velocity. (On the other hand, this drag can be used to accomplish a different delta-V maneuver, [aerobraking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerobraking)). There have also been theoretical proposals to use [aerodynamic lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lift_%28force%29) as the spacecraft flies through the atmosphere. This maneuver, called an [aerogravity assist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerogravity_assist), could bend the trajectory through a larger angle than gravity alone, and hence increase the gain in energy.[^o21]

Interplanetary slingshots using the Sun itself are not possible because the Sun is at rest relative to the Solar System as a whole. However, thrusting when near the Sun has a related effect, the [Oberth effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oberth_effect). This has the potential to magnify a spacecraft's thrusting power enormously, but is limited by the spacecraft's ability to resist the heat. For planetary gravity assists, a thrust applied near the closest approach (a "powered periapsis maneuver") can add the Oberth effect to the gravity slingshot effect, producing a larger change in orbital velocity than either effect by itself.

A [rotating black hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotating_black_hole) might provide additional assistance, if its spin axis is aligned the right way. [General relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) predicts that a large spinning mass produces [frame-dragging](wikipedia://en/27086745)—close to the object, space itself is dragged around in the direction of the spin. Any ordinary rotating object produces this effect. Although attempts to measure frame dragging about the Sun have produced no clear evidence, experiments performed by [Gravity Probe B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_Probe_B) have detected frame-dragging effects caused by Earth.[^o22] General relativity predicts that a spinning black hole is surrounded by a region of space, called the [ergosphere](wikipedia://en/1508445), within which standing still (with respect to the black hole's spin) is impossible, because space itself is dragged at the speed of light in the same direction as the black hole's spin. The [Penrose process](wikipedia://en/2975616) may offer a way to gain energy from the ergosphere, although it would require the spaceship to dump some "ballast" into the black hole, and the spaceship would have had to expend energy to carry the "ballast" to the black hole.

## Notable examples of use {#Notable_examples_of_use}

**Luna 3**

The gravity assist maneuver was first attempted in 1959 for [Luna 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_3), to photograph the far side of the Moon.[^o24] The satellite did not gain speed, but its orbit was changed in a way that allowed successful transmission of the photos.[^o25]

**Pioneer 10**

NASA's *[Pioneer 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pioneer_10)* is a space probe launched in 1972 that completed the first mission to the planet [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter).[^o68] Thereafter, *Pioneer 10* became the [first of five artificial objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_artificial_objects_leaving_the_Solar_System) to achieve the [escape velocity](wikipedia://en/37913) needed to [leave the Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System#Farthest_regions). In December 1973, *Pioneer 10* spacecraft was the first one to use the gravitational slingshot effect to reach escape velocity to leave Solar System.[^o26][^o27]

**Pioneer 11**

[Pioneer 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pioneer_11) was launched by NASA in 1973, to study the [asteroid belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid_belt), the environment around [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter) and [Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn), [solar winds](wikipedia://en/28538), and [cosmic rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_ray).[^o68] It was the first probe to [encounter Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration_of_Saturn), the second to fly through the [asteroid belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid_belt#Exploration), and the second to fly [by Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration_of_Jupiter) (3 December 1974). To get to Saturn, the spacecraft got a gravity assist on Jupiter.[^o28][^o29][^o30]

**Mariner 10**

The [Mariner 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariner_10) probe was the first spacecraft to use the gravitational slingshot effect to reach another planet, passing by Venus on 5 February 1974 on its way to becoming the first spacecraft to explore [Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_%28planet%29).[^o31]

**Voyager 1**

*[Voyager 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_1)* was launched by NASA on September 5, 1977. It gained the energy to escape the Sun's gravity by performing slingshot maneuvers around Jupiter and Saturn.[^o6] Having operated for 5 September 1977 12:56:00 as of UTC, the spacecraft still communicates with the [Deep Space Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_Space_Network) to receive routine commands and to transmit data to Earth. Real-time distance and velocity data is provided[^o32] by NASA and JPL. At a distance of \152.2 AU from Earth as of January 12, 2020,[^o32] it is the most distant human-made object from Earth.[^o33]

**Voyager 2**

*[Voyager 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_2)* was launched by NASA on August 20, 1977, to study the [outer planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_planets). Its trajectory took longer to reach [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter) and [Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn) than its twin spacecraft but enabled further encounters with [Uranus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranus) and [Neptune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptune).[^o34]

**Galileo**

The *[Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_%28spacecraft%29)* spacecraft was launched by [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA) in 1989 and on its route to [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter) got three gravity assists, one from Venus (February 10, 1990), and two from Earth (December 8, 1990 and December 8, 1992). Spacecraft reached Jupiter in December 1995. Gravity assists also allowed *Galileo* to flyby two asteroids, [243 Ida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/243_Ida) and [951 Gaspra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/951_Gaspra).[^o35][^o36]

**Ulysses**

In 1990, NASA launched the [ESA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Space_Agency) spacecraft *[Ulysses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_%28spacecraft%29)* to study the [polar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographical_pole) regions of the Sun. All the planets orbit approximately in a plane aligned with the equator of the Sun. Thus, to enter an orbit passing over the poles of the Sun, the spacecraft would have to eliminate the speed it inherited from the Earth's orbit around the Sun and gain the speed needed to orbit the Sun in the pole-to-pole plane. It was achieved by a gravity assist from Jupiter on February 8, 1992.[^o37][^o38]

**MESSENGER**

The [MESSENGER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MESSENGER) mission (launched in August 2004) made extensive use of gravity assists to slow its speed before orbiting Mercury. The MESSENGER mission included one flyby of Earth, two flybys of Venus, and three flybys of Mercury before finally arriving at Mercury in March 2011 with a velocity low enough to permit orbit insertion with available fuel. Although the flybys were primarily orbital maneuvers, each provided an opportunity for significant scientific observations.[^o39][^o40]

**Cassini**

The [*Cassini–Huygens* spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassini–Huygens) was launched from Earth on 15 October 1997, followed by gravity assist flybys of Venus (26 April 1998 and 21 June 1999), Earth (18 August 1999), and Jupiter (30 December 2000). Transit to Saturn took 6.7 years, the spacecraft arrived at 1 July 2004.[^o41][^o42] Its trajectory was called "the Most Complex Gravity-Assist Trajectory Flown to Date" in 2019.[^o43]

After entering orbit around Saturn, the *Cassini* spacecraft used multiple [Titan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titan_%28moon%29) gravity assists to achieve significant changes in the inclination of its orbit as well so that instead of staying nearly in the equatorial plane, the spacecraft's flight path was inclined well out of the plane of the rings.[^o44] A typical Titan encounter changed the spacecraft's velocity by 0.75 km/s, and the spacecraft made 127 Titan encounters. These encounters enabled an orbital tour with a wide range of periapsis and apoapsis distances, various alignments of the orbit with respect to the Sun, and orbital inclinations from 0° to 74°. The multiple flybys of Titan also allowed Cassini to flyby other moons, such as [Rhea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhea_%28moon%29) and [Enceladus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enceladus_%28moon%29).

*Image: Animation of Rosetta trajectory.gif.* Animation of *Rosetta* trajectory from 2 March 2004 to 9 September 2016 magentalimeRoyalbluemaroonCyanGold

**Rosetta**

The *[Rosetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosetta_%28spacecraft%29)* probe, launched in March 2004, used four gravity assist maneuvers (including one just 250 km from the surface of Mars, and three assists from Earth) to accelerate throughout the inner Solar System. That enabled it to flyby the asteroids [21 Lutetia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/21_Lutetia) and [2867 Šteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2867_Šteins) as well as eventually match the velocity of the [67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko) comet at the rendezvous point in August 2014.[^o45][^o46]

**New Horizons**

*[New Horizons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Horizons_%28spacecraft%29)* was launched by NASA in 2006, and reached [Pluto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluto) in 2015. In 2007 it performed a gravity assist on Jupiter.[^o47][^o48]

**Juno**

The [Juno spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juno_%28spacecraft%29) was launched on August 5, 2011 (UTC). The trajectory used a gravity assist speed boost from Earth, accomplished by an Earth flyby in October 2013, two years after its launch on August 5, 2011.[^o49] In that way *Juno* changed its orbit (and speed) toward its final goal, [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter), after only five years.

**Parker Solar Probe**

Velocity of Parker Solar Probe wide.svg

*Image: Animation of Parker Solar Probe trajectory.gif.* An animation of the Parker Solar Probe trajectory from August 7, 2018, to August 29, 2025:magentaYellowLimeCyanRoyalBlue

The [Parker Solar Probe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parker_Solar_Probe), launched by NASA in 2018, has seven planned Venus gravity assists. Each gravity assist brings the Parker Solar Probe progressively closer to the Sun. As of 2022, the spacecraft has performed five of its seven assists. The Parker Solar Probe's mission will make the closest approach to the Sun by any space mission.[^o50][^o51][^o52] The mission's final planned gravity assist maneuver, completed on November 6, 2024, prepared it for three final solar flybys reaching just 3.8 million miles from the surface of the sun on December 24, 2024 (see figure).[^o53]

**Solar Orbiter**

[Solar Orbiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_Orbiter) was launched by ESA in 2020. In its initial cruise phase, which lasts until November 2021, Solar Orbiter performed two gravity-assist manoeuvres around Venus and one around Earth to alter the spacecraft's trajectory, guiding it towards the innermost regions of the Solar System. The first close solar pass took place on 26 March 2022 at around a third of Earth's distance from the Sun.[^o54]

**BepiColombo**

[BepiColombo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BepiColombo) is a joint mission of the [European Space Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Space_Agency) (ESA) and the [Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JAXA) (JAXA) to the planet [Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_%28planet%29). It was launched on 20 October 2018. It will use the gravity assist technique with [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228) once, with [Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus) twice, and six times with [Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_%28planet%29). It will arrive in 2026. BepiColombo is named after [Giuseppe (Bepi) Colombo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Colombo) who was a pioneer thinker with this way of maneuvers.[^o55]

**Lucy**

*[Lucy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucy_%28spacecraft%29)* was launched by NASA on 16 October 2021. It gained one gravity assist from Earth on the 16th of October, 2022,[^o56] and after a flyby of the main-belt asteroid [152830 Dinkinesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/152830_Dinkinesh) it will gain another in 2024.[^o57] In 2025, it will fly by the inner main-belt asteroid [52246 Donaldjohanson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/52246_Donaldjohanson).[^o58] In 2027, it will arrive at the Trojan cloud (the [Greek camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_camp) of asteroids that orbits about 60° ahead of Jupiter), where it will fly by four Trojans, [3548 Eurybates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3548_Eurybates) (with its satellite), [15094 Polymele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/15094_Polymele), [11351 Leucus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/11351_Leucus), and [21900 Orus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/21900_Orus).[^o59] After these flybys, *Lucy* will return to Earth in 2031 for another gravity assist toward the Trojan cloud (the [Trojan camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_camp) which trails about 60° behind Jupiter), where it will visit the [binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_asteroid) Trojan [617 Patroclus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/617_Patroclus) with its [satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor-planet_moon) [Menoetius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/617_Patroclus) in 2033.

**JUICE**

[Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter_Icy_Moons_Explorer), launched by ESA in 2023 using the [Ariane 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_5), will arrive at Jupiter in 2031. It needs a series of gravity assists for it to reach its destination. [^o60] After launching in April 2023, it performed a Moon-Earth flyby in August 2024, a Venus flyby in August 2025. In order to reach Jupiter it must fly by Earth twice more - in September 2026 and January 2029, arriving at Jupiter in July 2031. [^o61]

**Europa Clipper**

*Image: Animation\_of\_Europa\_Clipper\_trajectory.gif.* An animation of Europa Clipper's trajectory from October 14, 2024, to April 11, 2030: magenta · yellow · blue · tan · green

[Europa Clipper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europa_Clipper), launched by NASA on October 14, 2024,[^o62] uses gravity assists from Mars and Earth on its way to Jupiter. It gained its first gravity assist from Mars on March 1, 2025,[^o63] and is scheduled to gain its second from Earth during its flyby on December 3, 2026.[^o63] The probe is expected to enter orbit around Jupiter in April 2030, from which it will perform multiple flybys of [Europa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europa_%28moon%29) during the duration of its mission.[^o64]

- [Dynamical friction](wikipedia://en/804039)
- [Flyby anomaly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flyby_anomaly), an anomalous delta-*v* increase during gravity assists
- [Gravitational keyhole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_keyhole)
- [Interplanetary Transport Network](wikipedia://en/194206)
- [*n*-body problem](wikipedia://en/23859945)
- [Oberth effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oberth_effect), applying thrust near closest approach in a gravity well
- *[Pioneer H](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pioneer_H)*, first Out-Of-The-Ecliptic mission (OOE) proposed, for Jupiter and solar (Sun) observations
- [STEREO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STEREO), a gravity-assisted mission which used Earth's Moon to eject two spacecraft from Earth's orbit into [heliocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentric) orbit
