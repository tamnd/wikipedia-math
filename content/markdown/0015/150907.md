# Ideal class group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ideal class group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **ideal class group** (or **class group**) of an [algebraic number field](wikipedia://en/28730822) $K$ is the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) $J_K/P_K$ where $J_K$ is the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of [fractional ideals](wikipedia://en/460700) of the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of $K$, and $P_K$ is its [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of [principal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_fractional_ideal) ideals. The class group is a measure of the extent to which [unique factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unique_factorization) fails in the ring of integers of $K$. The [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) of the group, which is [finite](wikipedia://en/323707), is called the **class number** of $K$.

:::

The theory extends to [Dedekind domains](wikipedia://en/145132) and their [fields of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271), for which the multiplicative properties are intimately tied to the structure of the class group. For example, the class group of a Dedekind domain is [trivial](wikipedia://en/193059) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the ring is a [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546).

## History and origin of the ideal class group {#History_and_origin_of_the_ideal_class_group}

Ideal class groups (or, rather, what were effectively ideal class groups) were studied some time before the idea of an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) was formulated. These groups appeared in the theory of [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478): in the case of [binary](wikipedia://en/1867129) integral quadratic forms, as put into something like a final form by [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125), a composition law was defined on certain [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of forms. This gave a finite [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974), as was recognised at the time.

Later [Ernst Kummer](wikipedia://en/319117) was working towards a theory of [cyclotomic fields](wikipedia://en/30872462). It had been realised (probably by several people) that failure to complete [proofs](wikipedia://en/82285) in the general case of [Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953) by factorisation using the [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity) was for a very good reason: a failure of unique factorization – i.e., the [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556) – to hold in the [rings](wikipedia://en/48404) generated by those roots of unity was a major obstacle. Out of Kummer's work for the first time came a study of the obstruction to the factorization. We now recognise this as part of the ideal class group: in fact Kummer had isolated the $p$-[torsion](wikipedia://en/144052) in that group for the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of $p$-roots of unity, for any [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) $p$, as the reason for the failure of the standard method of attack on the Fermat problem (see [regular prime](wikipedia://en/323689)).

Somewhat later again [Richard Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106) formulated the concept of an ideal, Kummer having worked in a different way. At this point the existing examples could be unified. It was shown that while rings of [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981) do not always have unique factorization into primes (because they need not be [principal ideal domains](wikipedia://en/25039)), they do have the property that every [proper ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_ideal) admits a unique factorization as a product of [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) (that is, every ring of algebraic integers is a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132)). The size of the ideal class group can be considered as a measure for the deviation of a ring from being a principal ideal domain; a ring is a principal ideal domain if and only if it has a trivial ideal class group.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

If $R$ is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462), define a [relation](wikipedia://en/22517558) $\sim$ on nonzero [fractional ideals](wikipedia://en/460700) of $R$ by $I\sim J$ whenever there exist nonzero elements $a$ and $b$ of $R$ such that $(a)I=(b)J$. It is easily shown that this is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259). The equivalence classes are called the *ideal classes* of $R$. Ideal classes can be multiplied: if $[I]$ denotes the equivalence class of the ideal $I$, then the multiplication $[I][J]=[IJ]$ is well-defined and [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative). The principal ideals form the ideal class $[R]$ which serves as an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) for this multiplication. Thus a class $[I]$ has an [inverse](wikipedia://en/45199) $[J]$ if and only if there is an ideal $J$ such that $IJ$ is a principal ideal. In general, such a $J$ may not exist and consequently the set of ideal classes of $R$ may only be a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652).

However, if $R$ is the ring of [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981) in an [algebraic number field](wikipedia://en/28730822), or more generally a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132), the multiplication defined above turns the set of fractional ideal classes into an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974), the **ideal class group** of $R$. The group property of existence of inverse elements follows easily from the fact that, in a Dedekind domain, every non-zero ideal (except $R$) is a product of [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928).

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

The ideal class group is trivial (i.e. has only one element) if and only if all ideals of $R$ are principal. In this sense, the ideal class group measures how far $R$ is from being a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039), and hence from satisfying unique prime factorization (Dedekind domains are [unique factorization domains](wikipedia://en/60546) if and only if they are principal ideal domains).

The number of ideal classes—the **class number** of $R$—may be infinite in general. In fact, every abelian group is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to the ideal class group of some Dedekind domain.[^o7] But if $R$ is a ring of algebraic integers, then the class number is always *finite*. This is one of the main results of classical [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705).

Computation of the class group is hard, in general; it can be done by hand for the ring of integers in an [algebraic number field](wikipedia://en/28730822) of small [discriminant](wikipedia://en/3022963), using [Minkowski's bound](wikipedia://en/8491791). This result gives a bound, depending on the ring, such that every ideal class contains an [ideal norm](wikipedia://en/460672) less than the bound. In general the bound is not sharp enough to make the calculation practical for fields with large discriminant, but computers are well suited to the task.

The mapping from rings of integers $R$ to their corresponding class groups is [functorial](wikipedia://en/10987), and the class group can be subsumed under the heading of [algebraic K-theory](wikipedia://en/598500), with $K_0(R)$ being the functor assigning to $R$ its ideal class group; more precisely, $K_0(R)=\mathbb{Z}\times C(R)$, where $C(R)$ is the class group. Higher $K$-groups can also be employed and interpreted arithmetically in connection to rings of integers.

:::

## Relation with the group of units {#Relation_with_the_group_of_units}

It was remarked above that the ideal class group provides part of the answer to the question of how much ideals in a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132) behave like elements. The other part of the answer is provided by the [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) of the Dedekind domain, since passage from principal ideals to their generators requires the use of [units](wikipedia://en/289666) (and this is the rest of the reason for introducing the concept of fractional ideal, as well).

Define a map from $K^\times$ to the set of all nonzero fractional ideals of $R$ by sending every element to the fractional ideal it generates. This is a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396); its [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) is the group of units of $R$, and its [cokernel](wikipedia://en/279715) is the ideal class group of $R$. The failure of these groups to be trivial is a measure of the failure of the map to be an isomorphism: that is the failure of ideals to act like ring elements, that is to say, like numbers.

## Examples of ideal class groups {#Examples_of_ideal_class_groups}

- The rings $\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}[i]$, and $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$, respectively the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563#Algebraic_properties), [Gaussian integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integers), and [Eisenstein integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eisenstein_integers), are all principal ideal domains (and in fact are all [Euclidean domains](wikipedia://en/10376)), and so have class number 1: i.e., they have trivial ideal class groups.
- If $K$ is a field, then the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $K[x_1,x_2,x_3,\dots]$ is an integral domain. It has a countably infinite set of ideal classes.

### Class numbers of quadratic fields {#Class_numbers_of_quadratic_fields}

If $d$ is a [square-free integer](wikipedia://en/29525) (a product of distinct primes) other than 1, then $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ is a [quadratic extension](wikipedia://en/454781) of $\mathbb{Q}$. If $d < 0$, then the class number of the ring $R$ of algebraic integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ is equal to 1 for precisely the following values of $d$: $d = -1,-2,-3,-7,-11, -19, -43, -67, -163$. This result was first [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) by [Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) and proven by [Kurt Heegner](wikipedia://en/621799), although Heegner's proof was not believed until [Harold Stark](wikipedia://en/788807) gave a later proof in 1967 (see [Stark–Heegner theorem](wikipedia://en/391251)). This is a special case of the famous [class number problem](wikipedia://en/596282).

If, on the other hand, $d > 0$, then it is unknown whether there are infinitely many fields $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ with class number 1. Computational results indicate that there are a great many such fields. However, it is not even known if there are infinitely many [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) with class number 1.[^o11]

For $d < 0$, the ideal class group of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ is isomorphic to the class group of integral [binary quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/1867129) of [discriminant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discriminant_of_a_quadratic_form) equal to the discriminant of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$. For $d > 0$, the ideal class group may be half the size since the class group of integral binary quadratic forms is isomorphic to the [narrow class group](wikipedia://en/2567750) of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$.[^o12]

For [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [quadratic integer](wikipedia://en/21834785) rings, the class number is given in [OEIS A003649](https://oeis.org/A003649); for the [imaginary](wikipedia://en/14554) case, they are given in [OEIS A000924](https://oeis.org/A000924).

#### Example of a non-trivial class group {#Example_of_a_non-trivial_class_group}

The quadratic integer ring $R=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is the ring of integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$. It does not possess unique factorization; in fact the class group of $R$ is [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327) of order 2. Indeed, the ideal

$$J=(2,1+\sqrt{-5})$$

is not principal, which can be [proved by contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_contradiction) as follows: $R$ has a [multiplicative](wikipedia://en/20059) [norm](wikipedia://en/450555) function defined by $N(a + b \sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5 b^2$, which satisfies $N(u) = 1$ if and only if $u$ is a unit in $R$.

Firstly, $J \neq R$, because the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) of $R$ modulo the ideal $(1 + \sqrt{-5})$ is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_isomorphism) to $\mathbb{Z} / 6\mathbb{Z}$, so that the quotient ring of $R$ modulo $J$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z} / 3\mathbb{Z}$. Now if $J=(a)$ were principal (that is, generated by an element $a$ of $R$), then $a$ would divide both $2$ and $1+\sqrt{-5}$. Then the norm $N(a)$ would divide both $N(2) = 4$ and $N(1 + \sqrt{-5}) = 6$, so $N(a)$ would divide 2. If $N(a) = 1$ then $a$ is a unit and so $J = R$, a contradiction. But $N(a)$ cannot be 2 either, because $R$ has no elements of norm 2, because the [Diophantine equation](wikipedia://en/9109) $b^2 + 5 c^2 = 2$ has no solutions in integers, as it has no solutions [modulo 5](wikipedia://en/20087).

One also computes that $J^2=(2)$, which is principal, so the class of $J$ in the ideal class group has order two. Showing that there aren't any other ideal classes requires more effort.

The fact that this $J$ is not principal is also related to the fact that the element $6$ has two distinct factorisations into [irreducibles](wikipedia://en/60693):

$$6 = 2\times 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5}).$$

## Connections to class field theory {#Connections_to_class_field_theory}

[Class field theory](wikipedia://en/242695) is a branch of [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705) which seeks to classify all the [abelian extensions](wikipedia://en/174448) of a given algebraic number field, meaning [Galois extensions](wikipedia://en/457064) with abelian [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673). A particularly beautiful example is found in the [Hilbert class field](wikipedia://en/3021036) of a number field, which can be defined as the maximal [unramified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unramified) abelian extension of such a field. The Hilbert class field *L* of a number field *K* is unique and has the following properties:

- Every ideal of the ring of integers of *K* becomes principal in *L*, i.e., if *I* is an integral ideal of *K* then the image of *I* is a principal ideal in *L*.
- *L* is a Galois extension of *K* with Galois group isomorphic to the ideal class group of *K*.

Neither property is particularly easy to prove.
