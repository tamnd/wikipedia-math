# Fermi–Dirac statistics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fermi–Dirac statistics"}

**Fermi–Dirac statistics** is a type of [quantum statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_statistics) that applies to the [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) of a [system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_system) consisting of many non-interacting, [identical particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identical_particles) that obey the [Pauli exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/24669). A result is the Fermi–Dirac distribution of particles over [energy states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_level). It is named after [Enrico Fermi](wikipedia://en/10264) and [Paul Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742), each of whom derived the distribution independently in 1926.[^o4][^o5] Fermi–Dirac statistics is a part of the field of [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481) and uses the principles of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202).

:::

Fermi–Dirac statistics applies to identical and indistinguishable particles with [half-integer](wikipedia://en/1568608) [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829) (1/2, 3/2, etc.), called [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion), in [thermodynamic equilibrium](wikipedia://en/265823). For the case of negligible interaction between particles, the system can be described in terms of single-particle [energy states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_eigenstates). A result is the Fermi–Dirac distribution of particles over these states where no two particles can occupy the same state, which has a considerable effect on the properties of the system. Fermi–Dirac statistics is most commonly applied to [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron), a type of fermion with [spin&nbsp;1/2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin&nbsp;1/2).

A counterpart to Fermi–Dirac statistics is [Bose–Einstein statistics](wikipedia://en/188935), which applies to identical and indistinguishable particles with integer spin (0, 1, 2, etc.) called [bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosons). In classical physics, [Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics](wikipedia://en/170167) is used to describe particles that are identical and treated as distinguishable. For both Bose–Einstein and Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics, more than one particle can occupy the same state, unlike Fermi–Dirac statistics.

## History {#History}

Before the introduction of Fermi–Dirac statistics in 1926, understanding some aspects of electron behavior was difficult due to seemingly contradictory phenomena. For example, the electronic [heat capacity](wikipedia://en/194227) of a metal at [room temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_temperature) seemed to come from 100 times fewer [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) than were in the [electric current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current).[^o3] It was also difficult to understand why the [emission currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_electron_emission) generated by applying high electric fields to metals at room temperature were almost independent of temperature.

The difficulty encountered by the [Drude model](wikipedia://en/542575), the electronic theory of metals at that time, was due to considering that electrons were (according to classical statistics theory) all equivalent. In other words, it was believed that each electron contributed to the specific heat an amount on the order of the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant) *k*<sub>B</sub>. This problem remained unsolved until the development of Fermi–Dirac statistics.

Fermi–Dirac statistics was first published in 1926 by [Enrico Fermi](wikipedia://en/10264)[^o4] and [Paul Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742).[^o5] According to [Max Born](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Born), [Pascual Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascual_Jordan) developed in 1925 the same statistics, which he called *[Pauli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Pauli) statistics*, but it was not published in a timely manner.[^o6][^o7][^o8] According to Dirac, it was first studied by Fermi, and Dirac called it "Fermi statistics" and the corresponding particles "fermions".[^o9]

Fermi–Dirac statistics was applied in 1926 by [Ralph Fowler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Fowler) to describe the collapse of a [star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star) to a [white dwarf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_dwarf).[^o10] In 1927 [Arnold Sommerfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Sommerfeld) applied it to electrons in metals and developed the [free electron model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_electron_model),[^o11] and in 1928 Fowler and [Lothar Nordheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lothar_Wolfgang_Nordheim) applied it to [field electron emission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_electron_emission) from metals.[^o12] Fermi–Dirac statistics continue to be an important part of physics.

## Fermi–Dirac distribution {#Fermi–Dirac_distribution}

For a system of identical fermions in thermodynamic equilibrium, the average number of fermions in a single-particle state $i$ is given by the **Fermi–Dirac (F–D) distribution**:[^o14]

where $k_{B}$ is the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant), $T$ is the absolute [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050), $\varepsilon_{i}$ is the energy of the single-particle state $i$, and $\mu$ is the [total chemical potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_chemical_potential). The distribution is normalized by the condition

$$\sum_i \bar n_i = N$$

that can be used to express $\mu = \mu(T, N)$ in that $\mu$ can assume either a positive or negative value.[^o16]

At zero absolute temperature, $\mu$ is equal to the [Fermi energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_energy) plus the potential energy per fermion, provided it is in a [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of positive spectral density. In the case of a spectral gap, such as for electrons in a semiconductor, the point of symmetry $\mu$ is typically called the [Fermi level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_level) or—for electrons—the [electrochemical potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_potential), and will be located in the middle of the gap.[^o17][^o18]

The Fermi–Dirac distribution is only valid if the number of fermions in the system is large enough so that adding one more fermion to the system has negligible effect on $\mu$.[^o19] Since the Fermi–Dirac distribution was derived using the [Pauli exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/24669), which allows at most one fermion to occupy each possible state, a result is that $0 < \bar{n}_i < 1$.

The [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) of the number of particles in state *i* can be calculated from the above expression for $\bar{n}_i$:[^o21][^o22]

$$V(n_i) = k_\text{B}T \frac{\partial}{\partial \mu} \bar{n}_i = \bar{n}_i(1 - \bar{n}_i).$$

### Distribution of particles over energy {#Distribution_of_particles_over_energy}

*Image: Fermi-Dirac distribution.gif.* Fermi function $F(\epsilon)$ with $\mu = 0.55~\text{eV}$ for various temperatures in the range $2~\text{K} \leq T \leq 375~\text{K}$

From the Fermi–Dirac distribution of particles over states, one can find the distribution of particles over energy. The average number of fermions with energy $\varepsilon_i$ can be found by multiplying the Fermi–Dirac distribution $\bar{n}_i$ by the [degeneracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_energy_level) $g_i$ (i.e. the number of states with energy $\varepsilon_i$),[^o24]

$$\begin{aligned} \bar{n}(\varepsilon_i) &= g_i \bar{n}_i \\ &= \frac{g_i}{e^{(\varepsilon_i - \mu) / k_\text{B} T} + 1}. \end{aligned}$$

When $g_i \ge 2$, it is possible that $\bar{n}(\varepsilon_i) > 1$, since there is more than one state that can be occupied by fermions with the same energy $\varepsilon_i$.

When a quasi-continuum of energies $\varepsilon$ has an associated [density of states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_of_states) $g(\varepsilon)$ (i.e. the number of states per unit energy range per unit volume[^o25]), the average number of fermions per unit energy range per unit volume is

$$\bar{\mathcal{N}}(\varepsilon) = g(\varepsilon) F(\varepsilon),$$

where $F(\varepsilon)$ is called the **Fermi function** and is the same [function](wikipedia://en/185427) that is used for the Fermi–Dirac distribution $\bar{n}_i$:[^o26]

$$F(\varepsilon) = \frac{1}{e^{(\varepsilon - \mu) / k_\text{B}T} + 1},$$

so that

$$\bar{\mathcal{N}}(\varepsilon) = \frac{g(\varepsilon)}{e^{(\varepsilon - \mu) / k_\text{B} T} + 1}.$$

## Quantum and classical regimes {#Quantum_and_classical_regimes}

The Fermi–Dirac distribution approaches the [Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution](wikipedia://en/170167#Applicability) in the limit of high temperature and low particle density, without the need for any ad hoc assumptions:

- In the limit of low particle density, $\bar{n}_i = \frac{1}{e^{(\varepsilon_i-\mu)/k_{\rm B}T}+1} \ll 1$, therefore $e^{(\varepsilon_i-\mu)/k_{\rm B}T}+1 \gg 1$ or equivalently $e^{(\varepsilon_i-\mu)/k_{\rm B}T} \gg 1$. In that case, $\bar{n}_i \approx \frac{1}{e^{(\varepsilon_i-\mu)/k_{\rm B}T}}=\frac{N}{Z}e^{-\varepsilon_i/k_{\rm B}T}$, which is the result from Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics.
- In the limit of high temperature, the particles are distributed over a large range of energy values, therefore the occupancy on each state (especially the high energy ones with $\varepsilon_i - \mu \gg k_{\rm B}T$) is again very small, $\bar{n}_i = \frac{1}{e^{(\varepsilon_i-\mu)/k_{\rm B}T}+1} \ll 1$. This again reduces to Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics.

The classical regime, where [Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics](wikipedia://en/170167) can be used as an approximation to Fermi–Dirac statistics, is found by considering the situation that is far from the limit imposed by the [Heisenberg uncertainty principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heisenberg_uncertainty_principle) for a particle's position and [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431). For example, in physics of semiconductor, when the density of states of conduction band is much higher than the doping concentration, the energy gap between conduction band and fermi level could be calculated using Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics. Otherwise, if the doping concentration is not negligible compared to density of states of conduction band, the Fermi–Dirac distribution should be used instead for accurate calculation. It can then be shown that the classical situation prevails when the [concentration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_concentration) of particles corresponds to an [average interparticle separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_inter-particle_distance) $\bar{R}$ that is much greater than the average [de Broglie wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Broglie_wavelength) $\bar{\lambda}$ of the particles:[^o28]

$$\bar{R} \gg \bar{\lambda} \approx \frac{h}{\sqrt{3mk_{\rm B}T}},$$

where $h$ is the [Planck constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_constant), and $m$ is the [mass of a particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle).

For the case of conduction electrons in a typical metal at $T$ = 300 [K](wikipedia://en/19593121) (i.e. approximately room temperature), the system is far from the classical regime because $\bar{R} \approx \bar{\lambda}/25$ . This is due to the small mass of the electron and the high concentration (i.e. small $\bar{R}$) of conduction electrons in the metal. Thus Fermi–Dirac statistics is needed for conduction electrons in a typical metal.[^o28]

Another example of a system that is not in the classical regime is the system that consists of the electrons of a star that has collapsed to a white dwarf. Although the temperature of white dwarf is high (typically $T$ = 10000 K on its surface[^o29]), its high electron concentration and the small mass of each electron precludes using a classical approximation, and again Fermi–Dirac statistics is required.[^o10]

## Derivations {#Derivations}

### Grand canonical ensemble {#Grand_canonical_ensemble}

The Fermi–Dirac distribution, which applies only to a quantum system of non-interacting fermions, is easily derived from the [grand canonical ensemble](wikipedia://en/1129074).[^o32] In this ensemble, the system is able to exchange energy and exchange particles with a reservoir (temperature *T* and chemical potential *μ* fixed by the reservoir).

Due to the non-interacting quality, each available single-particle level (with energy level *ϵ*) forms a separate thermodynamic system in contact with the reservoir. In other words, each single-particle level is a separate, tiny grand canonical ensemble. By the Pauli exclusion principle, there are only two possible [microstates](wikipedia://en/3066350) for the single-particle level: no particle (energy *E* = 0), or one particle (energy *E* = *ε*). The resulting [partition function](wikipedia://en/312252) for that single-particle level therefore has just two terms:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal Z &= \exp\big(0(\mu - \varepsilon)/k_{\rm B} T\big) + \exp\big(1(\mu - \varepsilon)/k_{\rm B} T\big) \\ &= 1 + \exp\big((\mu - \varepsilon)/k_{\rm B} T\big), \end{aligned}$$

and the average particle number for that single-particle level substate is given by

$$\langle N\rangle = k_{\rm B} T \frac{1}{\mathcal Z} \left(\frac{\partial \mathcal Z}{\partial \mu}\right)_{V,T} = \frac{1}{\exp\big((\varepsilon-\mu)/k_{\rm B} T\big) + 1}.$$

This result applies for each single-particle level, and thus gives the Fermi–Dirac distribution for the entire state of the system.[^o32]

The variance in particle number (due to [thermal fluctuations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_fluctuations)) may also be derived (the particle number has a simple [Bernoulli distribution](wikipedia://en/199189)):

$$\big\langle (\Delta N)^2 \big\rangle = k_{\rm B} T \left(\frac{d\langle N\rangle}{d\mu}\right)_{V,T} = \langle N\rangle \big(1 - \langle N\rangle\big).$$

This quantity is important in transport phenomena such as the [Mott relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seebeck_coefficient) for electrical conductivity and [thermoelectric coefficient](wikipedia://en/448321#Charge_carrier_diffusion) for an [electron gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_gas),[^o33] where the ability of an energy level to contribute to transport phenomena is proportional to $\big\langle (\Delta N)^2 \big\rangle$.

### Canonical ensemble {#Canonical_ensemble}

It is also possible to derive Fermi–Dirac statistics in the [canonical ensemble](wikipedia://en/1137672). Consider a many-particle system composed of *N* identical fermions that have negligible mutual interaction and are in thermal equilibrium.[^o19] Since there is negligible interaction between the fermions, the energy $E_R$ of a state $R$ of the many-particle system can be expressed as a sum of single-particle energies:

$$E_R = \sum_{r} n_r \varepsilon_r,$$

where $n_r$ is called the occupancy number and is the number of particles in the single-particle state $r$ with energy $\varepsilon_r$. The summation is over all possible single-particle states $r$.

The probability that the many-particle system is in the state $R$ is given by the normalized [canonical distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_distribution):[^o35]

$$P_R = \frac{e^{-\beta E_R}}{\displaystyle\sum_{R'} e^{-\beta E_{R'}}},$$

where $\beta = 1/k_\text{B}T$, $e^{-\beta E_R}$ is called the [Boltzmann factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_factor), and the summation is over all possible states $R'$ of the many-particle system. The average value for an occupancy number $n_i$ is[^o35]

$$\bar{n}_i = \sum_R n_i P_R.$$

Note that the state $R$ of the many-particle system can be specified by the particle occupancy of the single-particle states, i.e. by specifying $n_1, n_2, \ldots,$ so that

$$P_R = P_{n_1, n_2, \ldots} = \frac{e^{-\beta(n_1 \varepsilon_1 + n_2 \varepsilon_2 + \cdots)}} {\displaystyle\sum_{{n_1}',{n_2}',\ldots} e^{-\beta(n_1' \varepsilon_1 + n_2' \varepsilon_2 + \cdots)}},$$

and the equation for $\bar{n}_i$ becomes

$$\begin{aligned} \bar{n}_i &= \sum_{n_1,n_2,\dots} n_i P_{n_1, n_2, \dots} \\ &= \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{n_1,n_2,\dots} n_i e^{-\beta(n_1\varepsilon_1 + n_2\varepsilon_2 + \cdots + n_i\varepsilon_i + \cdots)}} {\displaystyle\sum_{n_1,n_2,\dots} e^{-\beta(n_1\varepsilon_1 + n_2\varepsilon_2 + \cdots + n_i\varepsilon_i + \cdots)}}, \end{aligned}$$

where the summation is over all combinations of values of $n_1, n_2, \ldots$ which obey the Pauli exclusion principle, and $n_r = 0$ = 0 or $1$ for each $r$. Furthermore, each combination of values of $n_1, n_2, \ldots$ satisfies the constraint that the total number of particles is $N$:

$$\sum_r n_r = N.$$

Rearranging the summations,

$$\bar{n}_i = \frac {\displaystyle\sum_{n_i=0}^1 n_i e^{-\beta(n_i\varepsilon_i)} \sideset{}{^{(i)}}\sum_{n_1,n_2,\dots} e^{-\beta(n_1\varepsilon_1+n_2\varepsilon_2+\cdots)}} {\displaystyle\sum_{n_i=0}^1 e^{-\beta(n_i\varepsilon_i)} \sideset{}{^{(i)}}\sum_{n_1,n_2,\dots} e^{-\beta(n_1\varepsilon_1 + n_2\varepsilon_2 + \cdots)}},$$

where the upper index $(i)$ on the summation sign indicates that the sum is not over $n_i$ and is subject to the constraint that the total number of particles associated with the summation is $N_i = N - n_i$. Note that $\textstyle\sum^{(i)}$ still depends on $n_i$ through the $N_i$ constraint, since in one case $n_i = 0$ and $\textstyle\sum^{(i)}$ is evaluated with $N_i = N,$ while in the other case $n_i = 1,$ and $\textstyle\sum^{(i)}$ is evaluated with $N_i = N - 1.$ To simplify the notation and to clearly indicate that $\textstyle\sum^{(i)}$ still depends on $n_i$ through $N - n_i,$ define

$$Z_i(N - n_i) \equiv \sideset{}{^{(i)}}\sum_{n_1,n_2,\ldots} e^{-\beta(n_1\varepsilon_1+n_2\varepsilon_2+\cdots)},$$

so that the previous expression for $\bar{n}_i$ can be rewritten and evaluated in terms of the $Z_i$:

$$\begin{aligned} \bar{n}_i &= \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{n_i=0}^1 n_i e^{-\beta(n_i\varepsilon_i)} \, Z_i(N - n_i)} {\displaystyle\sum_{n_i=0}^1 e^{-\beta(n_i\varepsilon_i)} \, Z_i(N - n_i)} \\ &= \frac{0 + e^{-\beta\varepsilon_i} \, Z_i(N - 1)}{Z_i(N) + e^{-\beta\varepsilon_i} \, Z_i(N - 1)} \\ &= \frac{1}{[Z_i(N)/Z_i(N - 1)] \, e^{\beta\varepsilon_i}+1}. \end{aligned}$$

The following approximation[^o36] will be used to find an expression to substitute for $Z_i(N)/Z_i(N - 1)$:

$$\begin{aligned} \ln Z_i(N - 1) &\simeq \ln Z_i(N) - \frac{\partial \ln Z_i(N)}{\partial N } \\ &= \ln Z_i(N) - \alpha_i, \end{aligned}$$

where $\alpha_i \equiv \frac{\partial \ln Z_i(N)}{\partial N}.$

If the number of particles $N$ is large enough so that the change in the chemical potential $\mu$ is very small when a particle is added to the system, then $\alpha_i \simeq - \mu / k_\text{B}T.$[^o37] Applying the exponential function to both sides, substituting for $\alpha_i$ and rearranging,

$$Z_i(N) / Z_i(N - 1) = e^{-\mu / k_\text{B}T}.$$

Substituting the above into the equation for $\bar{n}_i$ and using a previous definition of $\beta$ to substitute $1/k_\text{B}T$ for $\beta$, results in the Fermi–Dirac distribution:

$$\bar{n}_i = \frac{1}{e^{(\varepsilon_i - \mu)/k_\text{B}T} + 1}.$$

Like the [Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution](wikipedia://en/19830) and the [Bose–Einstein distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bose–Einstein_distribution), the Fermi–Dirac distribution can also be derived by the [Darwin–Fowler method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darwin–Fowler_method) of mean values.[^o38]

### Microcanonical ensemble {#Microcanonical_ensemble}

A result can be achieved by directly analyzing the multiplicities of the system and using [Lagrange multipliers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange_multipliers).[^o40]

Suppose we have a number of energy levels, labeled by index *i*, each level having energy ε*<sub>i</sub>* and containing a total of *n<sub>i</sub>* particles. Suppose each level contains *g<sub>i</sub>* distinct sublevels, all of which have the same energy, and which are distinguishable. For example, two particles may have different momenta (i.e. their momenta may be along different directions), in which case they are distinguishable from each other, yet they can still have the same energy. The value of *g<sub>i</sub>* associated with level *i* is called the "degeneracy" of that energy level. The [Pauli exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/24669) states that only one fermion can occupy any such sublevel.

The number of ways of distributing *n<sub>i</sub>* indistinguishable particles among the *g<sub>i</sub>* sublevels of an energy level, with a maximum of one particle per sublevel, is given by the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668), using its [combinatorial interpretation](wikipedia://en/4668#Combinatorial_interpretation):

$$w(n_i, g_i) = \frac{g_i!}{n_i!(g_i - n_i)!}.$$

For example, distributing two particles in three sublevels will give population numbers of 110, 101, or 011 for a total of three ways which equals 3!/(2!1!).

The number of ways that a set of occupation numbers *n*<sub>*i*</sub> can be realized is the product of the ways that each individual energy level can be populated:

$$W = \prod_i w(n_i, g_i) = \prod_i \frac{g_i!}{n_i!(g_i - n_i)!}.$$

Following the same procedure used in deriving the [Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics](wikipedia://en/170167), we wish to find the set of *n<sub>i</sub>* for which *W* is maximized, subject to the constraint that there be a fixed number of particles and a fixed energy. We constrain our solution using [Lagrange multipliers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange_multipliers) forming the function:

$$f(n_i) = \ln W + \alpha\left(N - \sum n_i\right) + \beta\left(E - \sum n_i \varepsilon_i\right).$$

Using [Stirling's approximation](wikipedia://en/151783) for the factorials, taking the derivative with respect to *n<sub>i</sub>*, setting the result to zero, and solving for *n<sub>i</sub>* yields the Fermi–Dirac population numbers:

$$n_i = \frac{g_i}{e^{\alpha + \beta\varepsilon_i} + 1}.$$

By a process similar to that outlined in the [Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics](wikipedia://en/170167) article, it can be shown thermodynamically that $\beta = \tfrac{1}{k_\text{B}T}$ and $\alpha = -\tfrac{\mu}{k_\text{B}T}$, so that finally, the probability that a state will be occupied is

$$\bar{n}_i = \frac{n_i}{g_i} = \frac{1}{e^{(\varepsilon_i - \mu)/k_\text{B}T} + 1}.$$
