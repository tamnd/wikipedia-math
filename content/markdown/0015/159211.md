# Analytic philosophy

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Analytic philosophy"}

**Analytic philosophy** is a broad [school of thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_of_thought) or style in [contemporary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemporary_philosophy) [Western philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_philosophy), especially [anglophone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English-speaking_world) philosophy,[^n1] with an emphasis on [analysis](wikipedia://en/1134), clear [prose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prose), rigorous [arguments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arguments), [formal logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_logic), mathematics, and the [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_sciences) (with less emphasis on the [humanities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanities)).[^n2][^n3] It is further characterized by the [linguistic turn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_turn), or a concern with [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language) and [meaning](wikipedia://en/4102640).[^n4]

:::

Analytic philosophy is often contrasted with [continental philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_philosophy),[^n1][^n5][^n6] a catch-all term for other methods prominent in [continental Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Europe), most notably [existentialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existentialism), [phenomenology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenology_%28philosophy%29), and [Hegelianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hegelianism).[^n7][^n8][^n9] The distinction has also been drawn between "analytic" being [academic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy) or technical philosophy and "continental" being [literary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_criticism) philosophy.[^n10]

The proliferation of analytic philosophy began around the turn of the twentieth century and has been dominant since the second half of the century.[^n11][^n12][^n13] Central figures in its history include [Gottlob Frege](wikipedia://en/48416), [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163), [G. E. Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._E._Moore), and [Ludwig Wittgenstein](wikipedia://en/17741). Other important figures include [Franz Brentano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Brentano), the [logical positivists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_positivism) (especially [Rudolf Carnap](wikipedia://en/57987)), and the [ordinary language philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_language_philosophy).

[Wilfrid Sellars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilfrid_Sellars), [W. V. O. Quine](wikipedia://en/33833), [Saul Kripke](wikipedia://en/48039), [David Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lewis_%28philosopher%29), and others, led a decline of logical positivism and a subsequent revival in [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics). Analytic philosophy has also developed several new branches of philosophy and logic, notably [philosophy of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_language), [mathematics](wikipedia://en/46439), and [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science), and modern [predicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_logic) and [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636).

## History {#History}

The **history of analytic philosophy** (taken in the narrower sense of "[20th-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20th-century_philosophy)/[21st-century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/21st-century_philosophy) analytic philosophy") is usually thought to begin with the rejection of [British idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_idealism), a [neo-Hegelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Hegelian) movement.[^n14] But important precursors include the work of [Gottlob Frege](wikipedia://en/48416) in formal logic and the philosophy of language, as well as the [Austrian tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_philosophy) associated with [Bernard Bolzano](wikipedia://en/302185), [Franz Brentano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Brentano), [Alexius Meinong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexius_Meinong), and the early [Edmund Husserl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Husserl).

### Austrian philosophy as a precursor {#Austrian_philosophy_as_a_precursor}

Analytic philosophy was significantly influenced by [Austrian realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_realism) in the former state of [Austria-Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria-Hungary), so much so that [Michael Dummett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Dummett) has remarked it is better characterized as Anglo-Austrian rather than the usual Anglo-American.[^n15]

#### Brentano {#Brentano}

*Image: Franz Brentano in Vienna, 1890.png.* Franz Brentano introduced the problem of intentionality.

In *[Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology_from_an_Empirical_Standpoint)* (1874), [University of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Vienna) philosopher and psychologist [Franz Brentano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Brentano) gave to philosophy the problem of [intentionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intentionality), or aboutness.[^n16] For Brentano, all mental events or acts of [consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consciousness) had a real, non-mental intentional object, which the thinking is directed at or "about".[^n17] Intentionality is "the mark of the mental."[^n17] Intentionality is to be distinguished from [intention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intention) or [intension](wikipedia://en/50453901).[^o25]

Every mental phenomenon is characterized by what the [Scholastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism) of the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) called the intentional (or mental) inexistence of an object, and what we might call, though not wholly unambiguously, reference to a content, direction towards an object (which is not to be understood here as meaning a thing), or immanent objectivity. Every mental phenomenon includes something as object within itself, although they do not all do so in the same way. In presentation something is presented, in judgement something is affirmed or denied, in love loved, in hate hated, in desire desired and so on. This intentional in-existence is characteristic exclusively of mental phenomena. No physical phenomenon exhibits anything like it. We could, therefore, define mental phenomena by saying that they are those phenomena which contain an object intentionally within themselves.The [School of Brentano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_of_Brentano) included [Edmund Husserl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Husserl) and [Alexius Meinong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexius_Meinong). Meinong founded the [Graz School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graz_School), and is known for his unique [ontology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology) of real, [nonexistent objects](wikipedia://en/7201028); a solution to the problem of [empty names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empty_name). This view is known as [Meinongianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existence#Meinongianism), or pejoratively as [Meinong's jungle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meinong's_jungle). According to Meinong, objects like [flying pigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/When_pigs_fly) or golden mountains are real and have being, even though they do not exist.[^n18][^o27][^o28] The [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_philosophy_in_Poland) [Lwów–Warsaw school](wikipedia://en/1426957), founded by [Kazimierz Twardowski](wikipedia://en/1022980), was also influenced by Brentano. Twardowski emphasized "small philosophy", or the detailed, systematic analysis of specific problems.[^n19] Twardowski was further influenced by the Bohemian [logical realist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_realism) [Bernard Bolzano](wikipedia://en/302185).[^n20][^n21]

### Frege {#Frege}

*Image: Young frege.jpg.* Gottlob Frege, the father of analytic philosophy

Gottlob Frege was a German [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) professor at the [University of Jena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Jena), logician, and philosopher who is understood as the father of analytic philosophy.[^n22] He advocated [logicism](wikipedia://en/350672), the project of reducing [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118) to pure logic, supporting [Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) and opposing [Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant) in the philosophy of mathematics.[^n23]

#### Logic {#Logic}

Frege developed modern, mathematical and [predicate logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_logic) with [quantifiers](wikipedia://en/43507260) in his book *[Begriffsschrift](wikipedia://en/900125)* (English: *Concept-script*, 1879). Frege unified the two strains of ancient logic: [Aristotelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelian_logic) and [Stoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoic_logic); allowing for a much greater range of sentences to be parsed into logical form. An example of this is the [problem of multiple generality](wikipedia://en/931381).

#### Number {#Number}

[Neo-Kantianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Kantianism) dominated the late nineteenth century in [German philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_philosophy). Husserl's book *[Philosophie der Arithmetik](wikipedia://en/6704759)* (1891) argued the concept of a [cardinal number](wikipedia://en/6173) derived from mental acts of grouping objects and counting them.[^n24] In contrast to this "[psychologism](wikipedia://en/2598499)", Frege, in *[The Foundations of Arithmetic](wikipedia://en/8983001)* (1884) and *The Basic Laws of Arithmetic* (Grundgesetze der Arithmetik, 1893–1903), argued that mathematics and logic have their own public objects, independent of one's private judgments or mental states.[^n25] Following Frege, the logicists tended to advocate a kind of [mathematical Platonism](wikipedia://en/2845761).[^n26]

The modern study of [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) was initiated by the German mathematicians [Richard Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106) and [Georg Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216). Italian mathematician [Giuseppe Peano](wikipedia://en/13043) simplified Dedekind's work to systematize mathematics with [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic).[^n27] Frege extended this work in an attempt to reduce arithmetic to logic, developing [naive set theory](wikipedia://en/4944) and a [set-theoretic definition of natural numbers](wikipedia://en/428484).[^n28]

#### Language {#Language}

Frege also proved influential in the philosophy of language. Dummett traces the linguistic turn to Frege's *Foundations of Arithmetic* and his [context principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Context_principle).[^n29] Frege writes "never ... ask for the meaning of a word in isolation, but only in the context of a [proposition](wikipedia://en/81094)."[^n30] As Dummett explains, in order to answer a Kantian question, "How are numbers given to us, granted that we have no idea or intuition of them?", Frege finds the solution in defining "the sense of a proposition in which a number word occurs."[^n31] Thus a problem, traditionally solved along [idealist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idealism) lines, is instead solved along [linguistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics) ones.[^n29]

##### Sense and reference {#Sense_and_reference}

*Image: Fregeantriangleofreference.png.* A [triangle of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangle_of_reference) illustrating Frege's conception.

Frege's paper "[On Sense and Reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Sense_and_Reference)" (1892) is seminal, containing [Frege's puzzles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frege's_puzzles) about [identity](wikipedia://en/89532) and advancing a [mediated reference theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediated_reference_theory).[^n32] Frege points out the reference of "[the Morning Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus_%28morning_star%29)" and "[the Evening Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hesperus)" is the same: both refer to the planet [Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus). Therefore, substituting one term for the other doesn't change the [truth value](wikipedia://en/161711) (*[salva veritate](wikipedia://en/3775601)*). However, they differ in what Frege calls cognitive value or the mode of presentation. One has to distinguish between two notions of meaning: the [reference](wikipedia://en/20110874) of a term and the sense of a term. As Frege points out, "the Morning Star is the Morning Star" is uninformative, but "the Morning Star is the Evening Star" is informative; thus, the two expressions must differ in a way other than reference.[^n32]

A related puzzle is also known as Frege's puzzle, concerning intensional contexts and [propositional attitude](wikipedia://en/425467) reports. Consider the statement "The ancients believed the morning star is the evening star." This statement might be false. However, the statement "The ancients believed the morning star is the morning star" is obviously true. Here again, the morning star and the evening star have different meanings, despite having the same reference.[^n32][^n33]

In Frege's paper "On [Concept and Object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept_and_Object)" (1892) he distinguishes between a concept which is the reference of a [predicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_%28grammar%29), and an object which is the reference of a [proper name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_name).[^n34]

##### Thought {#Thought}

*Image: 3realms.png.* A diagram of the "three realms"

The paper "[The Thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Thought): A Logical Inquiry" (1918) reflects Frege's anti-[idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idealism).[^n35] He argues for a [Platonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonist) account of propositions or thoughts. Frege claims propositions are intangible, like ideas; yet publicly available, like an object. In addition to the physical, public "first realm" of objects and the private, mental "second realm" of ideas, Frege posits a "[third realm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Realm_%28Frege%29)" of Platonic propositions, such as the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034).[^n35]

### Revolt against British idealism {#Revolt_against_British_idealism}

[British philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_philosophy) in the nineteenth century saw a revival of logic started by [Richard Whately](wikipedia://en/207580), in reaction to the anti-logical aspects of [British empiricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_empiricism). The major figure of this period is mathematician [George Boole](wikipedia://en/38298). Other figures include Scottish metaphysician [William Hamilton](wikipedia://en/206346), mathematician and [laws](wikipedia://en/64669) namesake [Augustus De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289), economist [William Stanley Jevons](wikipedia://en/207021), [diagram](wikipedia://en/61701) namesake [John Venn](wikipedia://en/251482), *[Alice's Adventures in Wonderland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice's_Adventures_in_Wonderland)* author [Lewis Carroll](wikipedia://en/26586083), Scottish mathematician [Hugh MacColl](wikipedia://en/3380850), and [American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_philosophy) [pragmatist](wikipedia://en/95170) [Charles Sanders Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117).[^n36]

However, British philosophy in the late nineteenth century was dominated by [British idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_idealism), a [neo-Hegelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Hegelian) movement, as taught by philosophers such as [F. H. Bradley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._H._Bradley) and [T. H. Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._H._Green).[^n37] Bradley's work *[Appearance and Reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appearance_and_Reality)* (1893) exemplified the school.[^n38]

*Image: 1914 George Edward Moore (cropped).jpg.* G. E. Moore led the revolt against idealism.

Analytic philosophy in the narrower sense of twentieth-century anglophone philosophy is usually thought to begin with [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge) philosophers Bertrand Russell and G. E. Moore's rejection of Hegelianism for being obscure; or the "revolt against idealism."[^n14][^n39] Russell summed up Moore's [common sense](wikipedia://en/21478754) influence: "G. E. Moore...took the lead in rebellion, and I followed, with a sense of emancipation. Bradley had argued that everything common sense believes in is mere appearance; we reverted to the opposite extreme, and that everything is real that common sense, uninfluenced by philosophy or [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology), supposes real. With a sense of escaping from prison, we allowed ourselves to think that grass is green, that the sun and stars would exist if no one was aware of them, and also that there is a pluralistic timeless world of Platonic ideas."[^n40]

Russell and Moore contributed to the [philosophy of perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_perception) with a [naïve realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naïve_realism) and [sense-data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sense-data) theory.[^n39][^n41] In America, [George Santayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Santayana) and the [New Realists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_realism_%28philosophy%29) opposed idealism and its [egocentric predicament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egocentric_predicament).[^n42]

#### Logical atomism {#Logical_atomism}

An important aspect of Hegelianism and British idealism was [logical holism](wikipedia://en/2173686)—the belief that aspects of the world can be known only by knowing the whole world. This is closely related to the [doctrine of internal relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctrine_of_internal_relations), the belief that [relations](wikipedia://en/508057#Relations) between items are *internal relations*, or [essential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essentialism) [properties](wikipedia://en/508057) the items have by nature. Russell and Moore in response promulgated [logical atomism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_atomism) and the doctrine of *external relations*—the belief that the world consists of independent facts.[^n39][^n43][^n44]

### Russell {#Russell}

*Image: Russell1907-2.jpg.* Bertrand Russell in 1907

In 1901, Russell famously discovered the [paradox](wikipedia://en/46095) in [Basic Law V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_Law_V) (also known as [unrestricted comprehension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_schema_of_unrestricted_comprehension)), which undermined Frege's set theory.[^o488] However, Russell was still a logicist, and in *[The Principles of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Principles_of_Mathematics)* (1903), he also argued for Meinongianism.[^n45]

#### Theory of descriptions {#Theory_of_descriptions}

During his early career, Russell adopted Frege's predicate logic as his primary philosophical method, thinking it could expose the underlying structure of philosophical problems. This was done most famously in his [theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_descriptions) of [definite descriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_description) in "[On Denoting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Denoting)", published in *[Mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind_%28journal%29)* in 1905.[^n46] The essay has been called a "[paradigm](wikipedia://en/175308#Other_uses) of philosophy."[^n47]

In this essay, Russell responds to both Meinong and Frege. Russell uses his analysis of descriptions to solve ascriptions of nonexistence, such as with "the present [King of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_French_monarchs)". He argues all [proper names](wikipedia://en/23484) (aside from [demonstratives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demonstrative) like *this* or *that*) are disguised definite descriptions; for example, "[Walter Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Scott)" can be replaced with "the author of *[Waverley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waverley_%28novel%29)*". This position came to be called [descriptivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descriptivist_theory_of_names).[^n48]

Russell presents his own version of Frege's second puzzle.

> "If a is identical with b, whatever is true of the one is true of the other, and either may be substituted for the other without altering the truth or falsehood of that proposition. Now [George IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_IV) wished to know whether Scott was the author of *Waverley*; and in fact Scott was the author of *Waverley*. Hence we may substitute “Scott” for “the author of *Waverley*” and thereby prove that George IV wished to know whether Scott was Scott. Yet an interest in the [law of identity](wikipedia://en/1403190) can hardly be attributed to the first gentleman of Europe.”[^n46]

The essay also illustrates the concept of [scope](wikipedia://en/48347082) [ambiguity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scope_ambiguity) by showing how denying "The present King of France is bald" can mean either "There is no King of France" or "The present King of France is not bald". Russell quips "Hegelians, who love a [synthesis](wikipedia://en/52813#Hegelian_dialectic), will probably conclude that he wears a wig."[^n46] For Russell, there was knowledge by description and, from sense-data theory, [knowledge by acquaintance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_by_acquaintance).[^n49]

#### *Principia Mathematica* {#Principia_Mathematica}

Russell's book written with [Alfred North Whitehead](wikipedia://en/43395), *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)* (1910–1913), was the seminal text of [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868) and of the logicist project, and encouraged many philosophers to renew their interest in symbolic logic. It used a [notation](wikipedia://en/12226979) from Peano, and a [theory of types](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_types) to avoid the pitfalls of Russell's paradox.[^n50] Whitehead developed [process metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process_metaphysics) in *[Process and Reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process_and_Reality)* (1929).[^n51][^n52][^n53]

#### Ideal language {#Ideal_language}

Russell claimed the problems of philosophy can be solved by showing the simple constituents of complex notions.[^n3] [Logical form](wikipedia://en/3331706) would be made clear by [syntax](wikipedia://en/26860). For example, the English word *is* has three distinct meanings, which predicate logic can express as follows:

- For the sentence 'the cat *is* asleep', the *is* of [predication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predication_%28philosophy%29) means that "x is P" (denoted as P(x)).
- For the sentence 'there *is* a cat', the *is* of [existence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existence) means that "there is an x" (∃x).
- For the sentence 'three *is* half of six', the *is* of identity means that "x is the same as y" (x=y).

From about 1910 to 1930, analytic philosophers emphasized creating an [ideal language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_language_philosophy) for philosophical analysis, which would be free from the ambiguities of ordinary language that, in their opinion, often led philosophers astray.[^n54]

### Early Wittgenstein {#Early_Wittgenstein}

*Image: Ludwig Wittgenstein 1929.jpg.* Ludwig Wittgenstein

Russell's student [Ludwig Wittgenstein](wikipedia://en/17741) developed a comprehensive system of logical atomism, with a [picture theory of meaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picture_theory_of_meaning), in his *[Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus](wikipedia://en/30062)* (Logisch-Philosophische Abhandlung, 1921), sometimes known as simply the *Tractatus*. Wittgenstein thought he had solved all the problems of philosophy with the *Tractatus.*[^n55][^n56]

The book starts, "The world is all that is the case."[^n57] Wittgenstein claims the universe is the totality of actual [states of affairs](wikipedia://en/25718122) and that these states of affairs can be expressed and mirrored by the language of first-order predicate logic. Thus, a picture of the universe can be constructed by expressing facts in the form of [atomic propositions](wikipedia://en/943457) and linking them using [logical operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_operator).[^n58][^n59][^n60]

The *Tractatus* introduced philosophers to the terms [tautology](wikipedia://en/4495335), [truth conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth_conditions), and to the [truth table](wikipedia://en/25512250) method.[^n61][^n59] Wittgenstein believed tautologies or [logical truths](wikipedia://en/3986852) *say* nothing, but *show* the logical structure of the world,[^n62][^n63] and has been labeled a [mystic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysticism) who believed in the [ineffable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ineffability) by some readers.[^n64] The *Tractatus* further ultimately concludes that all of its propositions are [meaningless](wikipedia://en/387403), illustrated with a [ladder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wittgenstein's_ladder) one must toss away after climbing up it.[^n65] The book ends, "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent."[^n66]

### Logical positivism {#Logical_positivism}

During the late 1920s to 1940s, two groups of philosophers known as the [Vienna Circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_Circle) and the [Berlin Circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Circle) developed Russell and Wittgenstein's philosophy into a doctrine known as "[logical positivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_positivism)" (or "logical empiricism").[^n67][^n68] The Vienna Circle (previously the [Ernst Mach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Mach) Society) was led by [Moritz Schlick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moritz_Schlick) and included Rudolf Carnap and [Otto Neurath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Neurath).[^n69][^o105] The Berlin Circle was led by [Hans Reichenbach](wikipedia://en/1546863) and included [Carl Hempel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Hempel) and mathematician [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302).[^n67] There was also the Dutch [signific circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Significs), which included [L. E. J. Brouwer](wikipedia://en/172896), founder of [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262).[^n70]

Logical positivists used formal logical methods to develop an [empiricist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricism) account of knowledge. They adopted the [verification principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verification_principle), according to which every meaningful statement is either [analytic or synthetic](wikipedia://en/6395956).[^n71] The truths of logic and mathematics were tautologies, and those of science were verifiable empirical claims. These two constituted the entire universe of meaningful judgments; anything else was nonsense. Thus the principle rejected statements of metaphysics, theology, [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics), and [aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics) as *cognitively meaningless*.[^n65]

The logical positivists saw their verificationism as a recapitulation of a quote by [David Hume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hume), the closing lines from *[An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Enquiry_Concerning_Human_Understanding)* (1748):

> If we take in our hand any volume; of divinity or school metaphysics, for instance; let us ask, Does it contain any abstract reasoning concerning quantity or number? No. Does it contain any experimental reasoning concerning matter of fact and existence? No. Commit it then to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion.[^n72][^n73]

This led the logical positivists to reject many traditional problems of philosophy. They typically considered philosophy to have a minimal function, concerning the clarification of thoughts, rather than having a distinct subject matter of its own.

[Epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology) was still discussed. Schlick was a [foundationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundationalist), believing knowledge was like a pyramid, built on prior layers of knowledge except for the first layer.[^n74] Neurath was an [anti-foundationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-foundationalism), [coherentist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherentism) who famously gave the analogy of reconstructing a [ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurath's_boat) while on the open sea.[^n74][^n75]

[Friedrich Waismann](wikipedia://en/1724365) introduced the concept of *[open texture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_texture)* to describe the universal possibility of [vagueness](wikipedia://en/32369) in empirical statements.[^n76] Waismann never finished a book titled *Logik, Sprache, Philosophie* intended to present the ideas of logical positivism to a wider audience.[^n77]

Carnap and Reichenbach started the journal *[Erkenntnis](wikipedia://en/18761111)*.[^n78] Carnap advocated solving problems by "[semantic ascent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_ascent)", talking [about language](wikipedia://en/402929) instead of its objects.[^n79] Carnap also distinguished between trivial [internal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal–external_distinction) questions and meaningless external questions.[^n80] He is best known for works like *Der logische Aufbau der Welt* (translated as *The Logical Structure of the World*, 1967) and *The Elimination of Metaphysics Through Logical Analysis of Language* (1959).[^n81][^n82]

Several logical positivists were [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews), such as Neurath, Waismann, [Hans Hahn](wikipedia://en/1075212), and Reichenbach. Others, like Carnap, were [gentiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentile) but [socialists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism) or [pacifists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacifism). With [the coming to power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler's_rise_to_power) of [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler) and [Nazism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism) in 1933, many members of the Vienna and Berlin Circles fled to [Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain) and the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States), which helped to reinforce the dominance of logical positivism and analytic philosophy in anglophone countries.[^n83][^n84]

In 1936, Schlick was murdered in Vienna by his former student, [Hans Nelböck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Nelböck).[^n85] The same year, [A. J. Ayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._J._Ayer)'s work *[Language, Truth and Logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language,_Truth_and_Logic)* introduced the English speaking world to logical positivism.[^n71][^n86]

### Ordinary language {#Ordinary_language}

After [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), analytic philosophy became interested in ordinary language philosophy, in contrast to ideal language philosophy. Rather than rely on logical constructions, philosophers emphasized the use of [natural language](wikipedia://en/21173). There were two strains of ordinary language philosophy: the later Wittgenstein and Oxford philosophies.

#### Later Wittgenstein {#Later_Wittgenstein}

Wittgenstein's later philosophy, from the posthumous *[Philosophical Investigations](wikipedia://en/23674)* (1953), differed dramatically from his early work of the *Tractatus*.[^n87] Philosophers refer to them like two different philosophers: "early Wittgenstein" and "later Wittgenstein". One notion found in both early and later Wittgenstein is that "Philosophy is a battle against the bewitchment of our intelligence by means of language."[^n88] Philosophers had been misusing language and asking meaningless questions, and it was Wittgenstein's job "to show the fly the way out of the [fly bottle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fly_bottle)."[^n89][^n60]

##### Ramsey {#Ramsey}

The criticisms of [Frank Ramsey](wikipedia://en/238285) on the "color-exclusion problem" led to some of Wittgenstein's first doubts with regard to his early philosophy.[^n90] Wittgenstein in the *Tractatus* thought the only necessity is logical necessity; yet that no point in space can have two different colors at the same time seems a [necessary truth](wikipedia://en/741170) but not a logical one.[^n56][^n91] Wittgenstein responded to Ramsey in "[Some Remarks on Logical Form](wikipedia://en/28359539)" (1929), the only academic paper he ever published.[^n92][^n93] Ramsey died of [jaundice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaundice) the next year at the age of 26.[^n94]

##### Sraffa's gesture {#Sraffa's_gesture}

[Norman Malcolm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Malcolm) also famously credits [Piero Sraffa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_Sraffa) for providing Wittgenstein with the conceptual break from his earlier philosophy, by means of a rude gesture:[^n95][^n96]

> Wittgenstein was insisting that a proposition and what it describes must have the same 'logical form', the same 'logical multiplicity'. Sraffa made a gesture, familiar to [Neapolitans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naples) as meaning something like disgust or contempt, of brushing the underneath of his chin with an outward sweep of the fingertips of one hand. And he asked: 'What is the logical form of that?'

Prior to the publication of the *Philosophical Investigations*, philosophers like [John Wisdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wisdom) and [Rush Rhees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rush_Rhees) were some of the few sources of information about Wittgenstein's later philosophy.[^n85][^n97] Wisdom's 1936 essay "Philosophical Perplexity"[^n98] was described by [J. O. Urmson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._O._Urmson) as the first article "which throughout embodied the new philosophical outlook, it is something of a landmark in the history of philosophy."[^n99] Another work of Wisdom's was *Other Minds* (1952) which discusses the [problem of other minds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem_of_other_minds).[^n100]

The later Wittgenstein develops a [therapeutic approach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therapeutic_approach). He introduces the concept of a "[language-game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language-game)" as a "[form of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Form_of_life)". By "language-game", he meant a language simpler than an entire language.[^n101] Wittgenstein argued that a word or sentence has meaning only as a result of the "rule" of the "game" being played. Depending on the context, for example, the utterance "Water!" could be an order, the answer to a question, or some other form of communication. Rather than his prior picture theory of meaning, the later Wittgenstein advocates a theory of [meaning as use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meaning_as_use), according to which words are defined by how they are used within the language-game.[^n87]

*Image: Duck-Rabbit illusion.jpg.* The [duck-rabbit illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duck-rabbit_illusion) became famous when Wittgenstein used it to distinguish "seeing that" from "seeing as".

The notion of [family resemblance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_resemblance) thinks things thought to be connected by one essential, common feature may in fact be connected by a series of overlapping [similarities](wikipedia://en/66431011), where no one feature is common to all of them. [Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game), which Wittgenstein used as an example to explain the notion, have become the classic example of a group that is related by family resemblance.[^n87]

*Philosophical Investigations* also contains the [private language argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_language_argument). Another point Wittgenstein makes against the possibility of a private language involves the [beetle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beetle)-in-a-box thought experiment.[^n102] He asks the reader to imagine that each person has a box, inside which is something that everyone intends to refer to with the word *beetle*. Further, suppose that no one can look inside another's box. Under such a situation, Wittgenstein says the word beetle is meaningless.

He also famously uses the [duck-rabbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbit–duck_illusion), an [ambiguous image](wikipedia://en/1316947), as a means of describing two different ways of seeing: ["seeing that" versus "seeing as"](wikipedia://en/23674#Seeing_that_vs._seeing_as).

### Oxford philosophy {#Oxford_philosophy}

The other trend of ordinary language philosophy was known as "[Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford) philosophy", in contrast to the earlier analytic Cambridge philosophers. Influenced by Moore's common sense and the later Wittgenstein's [quietism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quietism_%28philosophy%29), the Oxford philosophers claimed ordinary language already represented many subtle distinctions not recognized in traditional philosophy. The most prominent Oxford philosophers were [Gilbert Ryle](wikipedia://en/1004355), [Peter Strawson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._F._Strawson), and [John L. Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._L._Austin).[^o153]

#### Ryle {#Ryle}

Ryle, in *[The Concept of Mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Concept_of_Mind)* (1949), criticized [Cartesian](wikipedia://en/25525) [dualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind–body_dualism), arguing in favor of disposing of "Descartes' myth" of the [ghost in the machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_in_the_machine) by recognizing "[category errors](wikipedia://en/391475)".[^n103] Ryle sees Descartes' error as similar to saying one sees the campus, buildings, faculty, students, and so on, but still goes on to ask "Where is the university?"[^n103]

#### Strawson {#Strawson}

Strawson first became well known with his article "On Referring" (1950), a criticism of Russell's theory of descriptions.[^n104] On Strawson's account, the use of a description [presupposes](wikipedia://en/1965727) the existence of the object fitting the description.[^n104] In his book *Individuals* (1959), Strawson examines our conceptions of basic [particulars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particular).[^n105]

#### Austin {#Austin}

Austin, in the posthumously published *[How to Do Things with Words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Do_Things_with_Words)* (1962), articulated the theory of [speech acts](wikipedia://en/44836) and emphasized the ability of words to [do things](wikipedia://en/646571) (e.g. "I promise") and not just say things.[^n106] This influenced several fields to undertake what is called a [performative turn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performative_turn). In *[Sense and Sensibilia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sense_and_Sensibilia_%28Austin_book%29)* (1962), Austin criticized sense-data theories.[^n107]

### Spread to other countries {#Spread_to_other_countries}

#### Australia and New Zealand {#Australia_and_New_Zealand}

[Samuel Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Alexander)'s realism influenced [Australian philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_philosophy).[^n108] The school known as [Australian realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_realism) began when [John Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Anderson_%28philosopher%29) accepted the Challis Chair of Philosophy at the [University of Sydney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Sydney) in 1927.[^n109] American philosopher David Lewis later became closely associated with Australia, whose philosophical community he visited almost annually for more than 30 years.[^n110] In New Zealand, South African [J. N. Findlay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._N._Findlay), a student of Austrian realist [Ernst Mally](wikipedia://en/1891598), taught at the [University of Otago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Otago).[^n111] [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper) lectured at the [Canterbury University College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canterbury_University_College) in [Christchurch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christchurch).[^n112]

#### Sweden and Finland {#Sweden_and_Finland}

In Sweden, [Axel Hägerström](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axel_Hägerström) broke away from [Christopher Jacob Boström](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Jacob_Boström)'s idealism, founding the Uppsala School of Philosophy.[^n113] The [Finnish philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_philosophy_in_Finland) [Eino Kaila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eino_Kaila) is considered to have founded Finnish analytic philosophy.[^n114] Kaila's student [Georg Henrik von Wright](wikipedia://en/12250) succeeded Wittgenstein at Cambridge in 1948.[^n115]

#### China {#China}

[Chinese philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_philosophy) [Zhang Shenfu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhang_Shenfu) first introduced Russell's ideas to [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China), and later translated the *Tractatus*.[^n116] In 1920, Russell visited China at the invitation of [Liang Qichao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liang_Qichao).[^n116] This begins the first phase of analytic philosophy in China.[^n117] Tscha Hung then introduced logical positivism to China with *The Philosophy of the Vienna Circle* (1945).[^n118][^n119] During the second phase, scholars such as [Jin Yuelin](wikipedia://en/5484556) and Hong Qian spread analytic philosophy, until [Communist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communism) political pressure sidelined research.[^n116]

After the [reform and opening up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reform_and_opening_up) of the 1970s, analytic philosophy in China is now in its third phase, and is an active and growing area of study.[^n116][^n117]

## Metaphysics {#Metaphysics}

During the second half of the twentieth century, analytic philosophy saw the demise of logical positivism and a revival of metaphysical theorizing.[^n120]

### Sellars {#Sellars}

Kant scholar [Wilfrid Sellars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilfrid_Sellars), the son of [Roy Wood Sellars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Wood_Sellars), "revolutionized both the content and the method of philosophy in the United States".[^o187] Sellars's criticism of the "[Myth of the Given](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myth_of_the_Given)", in *Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind* (1956), challenged logical positivism by arguing against sense-data theories and knowledge by acquaintance.[^n121] In his "Philosophy and the Scientific Image of Man" (1962), Sellars's [critical realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_realism_%28philosophy_of_perception%29) distinguishes between the "manifest image" and the "scientific image" of the world.[^n122] Sellars's goal of a [synoptic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synoptic_philosophy) uniting the everyday and scientific views of reality is the basis of what is sometimes called the Pittsburgh School, whose members include [Robert Brandom](wikipedia://en/875857), [John McDowell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_McDowell), and [John Haugeland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Haugeland).[^n123]

### Quine {#Quine}

*Image: Willard Van Orman Quine on Bluenose II in Halifax NS harbor 1980.jpg.* W. V. O. Quine helped to undermine logical positivism.

[Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) philosopher W. V. O. Quine shaped much of subsequent philosophy and is recognized as "one of the most influential philosophers of the twentieth century".[^n124] He is regularly cited as the greatest philosopher of the second half of the twentieth century,[^n125] or the next great philosopher after Wittgenstein.[^o194]

Quine was a student of Carnap.[^n84] He was an empiricist who sought to [naturalize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalized_epistemology) philosophy and saw philosophy as continuous with science, distinguished only by philosophy being the most general science.[^n126] However, Quine doubted usual theories of meaning, and, instead of logical positivism, advocated a kind of [semantic holism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_holism) and [ontological relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontological_relativity), which explained that every term in any statement has its meaning contingent on a vast network of knowledge and belief, the speaker's conception of the entire world.[^n127][^n128]

#### *Word and Object* {#Word_and_Object}

In his *[magnum opus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnum_opus)* *[Word and Object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_and_Object)* (1960), Quine introduces the idea of [radical translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_translation), an introduction to his theory of the [indeterminacy of translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminacy_of_translation), and specifically to prove the [inscrutability of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscrutability_of_reference).[^n129] The *gavagai* [thought experiment](wikipedia://en/49535) tells about a linguist, who tries to find out what the expression *gavagai* means when uttered by a speaker of a yet-unknown native language upon seeing a [rabbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbit). At first glance, it seems that *gavagai* simply translates with *rabbit*. Quine points out there is no way to tell that the speaker did not mean, for instance, "undetached rabbit-part" (such as its ear) as well as several other scenarios.[^n130]

#### On What There Is {#On_What_There_Is}

Quine's essay on ontology, "On What There Is" (1948) elucidates Russell's theory of descriptions.[^n26] Quine uses [Pegasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pegasus) instead of "the present King of France" and dubs the problem of nonexistence [Plato's beard](wikipedia://en/29465119). The essay contains Quine's famous dictum of [ontological commitment](wikipedia://en/53828), "To be is to be the value of a [variable](wikipedia://en/147460)". One is committed to the entities his theory posits by use of the [existential quantifier](wikipedia://en/91420), like "There are some so-and-sos".[^n26] Other parts of speech do not commit one to entities and so for Quine are [syncategorematic](wikipedia://en/20522132).[^n131]

#### Two Dogmas of Empiricism {#Two_Dogmas_of_Empiricism}

Also among the developments that resulted in the decline of logical positivism and the revival of metaphysics was Quine's attack on the analytic–synthetic distinction in "[Two Dogmas of Empiricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Dogmas_of_Empiricism)" (1951), published in *[The Philosophical Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Philosophical_Review)*,[^n132][^n133] a paper "sometimes regarded as the most important in all of twentieth-century philosophy".[^n134] The paper made Quine the most dominant philosopher in America before Kripke.[^n135]

### Kripke {#Kripke}

*Image: Kripke.JPG.* Saul Kripke helped to revive interest in metaphysics among analytic philosophers.

Saul Kripke is widely regarded as having revived theories of [essence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essence) and identity as respectable topics of philosophical discussion.[^n136] He was influential in arguing that flaws in common theories of descriptions and proper names are indicative of larger misunderstandings of the metaphysics of modality, or of necessity and [possibility](wikipedia://en/17753389).[^n136]

[Modal](wikipedia://en/2783063) [logic](wikipedia://en/333365) was developed by pragmatist [C. I. Lewis](wikipedia://en/433707) to deal with the [paradoxes of material implication](wikipedia://en/12857474).[^n137] Carnap also contributed to modal logic with works like *[Meaning and Necessity](wikipedia://en/35811096)* (1947).[^n138] [Ruth Barcan Marcus](wikipedia://en/369743) introduced the now standard "box" operator for necessity and "diamond" operator for possibility in her treatment of the [Barcan formula](wikipedia://en/245298).[^n139] Kripke provided a [semantics](wikipedia://en/911960) for modal logic; he and Barcan both argued [identity is a necessary relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessity_of_identity).[^n140]

#### *Naming and Necessity* {#Naming_and_Necessity}

Especially important was Kripke's book *[Naming and Necessity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naming_and_Necessity)* (1980). According to one author, *Naming and Necessity* "played a large role in the implicit, but widespread, rejection of the view—so popular among ordinary language philosophers—that philosophy is nothing more than the analysis of language."[^n141] Kripke argued proper names are [rigid designators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigid_designators), or designate the same thing in all [possible worlds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Possible_worlds), unlike descriptions. For example, an election may have turned out differently, so the description "winner of the [1968 US presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_United_States_presidential_election)" might have designated [Hubert Humphrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubert_Humphrey) instead of [Richard Nixon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nixon). However, the name "Richard Nixon" designates the man Richard Nixon, regardless of the election results.[^n142]

Kant stated in the *[Critique of Pure Reason](wikipedia://en/347543)* (1781) that necessity is the criterion for *[a priori](wikipedia://en/6679056)* knowledge.[^n143] Kripke argued that necessity is a metaphysical notion distinct from the epistemic notion of *a priori*, and that there are necessary truths that are known *[a posteriori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_posteriori_necessity)*, such as that water is H<sub>2</sub>O, or [gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold) is [atomic number](wikipedia://en/673) 79.[^n144][^n136] Kripke and Quine's colleague [Hilary Putnam](wikipedia://en/75233) argued for realism about [natural kinds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_kind). Putnam's [Twin Earth thought experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_Earth_thought_experiment) is used to argue water is a natural kind.[^n145][^n146][^o226]

### David Lewis {#David_Lewis}

*Image: David\_Lewis\_(1962)\_(cropped).webp.* David Lewis

David Lewis defended a number of elaborate metaphysical theories. In works such as *[On the Plurality of Worlds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Plurality_of_Worlds)* (1986) and *Counterfactuals* (1973), Lewis argued for [modal realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_realism) and [counterpart theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterpart_theory) – the belief in real, concrete possible worlds, and argued against any "[ersatz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ersatz_good)" conception of possibility.[^n147][^n148] According to Lewis, "actual" is merely an [indexical](wikipedia://en/1029182) label we give a world when we are in it. Lewis applied Quine's dictum of ontological commitment to the statement "There are other ways things could have been;" committing Lewis (by his lights) to the real existence of other ways things could have been.[^n149] He also defended what he called Humean [supervenience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supervenience), and a [counterfactual theory](wikipedia://en/491578) of [causation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causality), another view of Hume's.[^n150]

### Truth {#Truth}

Frege questioned standard theories of [truth](wikipedia://en/29920), and sometimes advocated a [deflationary](wikipedia://en/601131), [redundancy theory of truth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redundancy_theory_of_truth), i. e. that the predicate "is true" does not express anything above and beyond the statement to which it is attributed.[^n35] Frank Ramsey also advocated a redundancy theory.[^n151]

*Image: AlfredTarski1968.jpeg.* Alfred Tarski has an influential theory of truth.

[Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) put forward an influential [semantic theory of truth](wikipedia://en/601240), that truth is a property of sentences.[^n152] Tarski's semantic methods culminated in [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858), as opposed to [proof theory](wikipedia://en/183478).[^n152]

In *Truth-Makers* (1984), [Kevin Mulligan](wikipedia://en/11782633), [Peter Simons](wikipedia://en/1569373), and [Barry Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Smith_%28academic%29) introduced the [truth-maker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truthmaker_theory) idea as a contribution to the [correspondence theory of truth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correspondence_theory_of_truth).[^n153] A truth-maker is contrasted with a [truth-bearer](wikipedia://en/4919653). A truth-bearer's truth is [grounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grounding_%28metaphysics%29) by the truth-maker.

### Universals {#Universals}

In response to the [problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem_of_universals) of [universals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_%28metaphysics%29), Australian [David Armstrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Malet_Armstrong) defended a kind of [moderate realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moderate_realism).[^n154] David Lewis and [Anthony Quinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Quinton) defended [nominalism](wikipedia://en/21176).[^n148][^n155]

### Mereology {#Mereology}

Polish philosopher [Stanisław Leśniewski](wikipedia://en/422271), along with [Nelson Goodman](wikipedia://en/1179596), established [mereology](wikipedia://en/375380), the formal study of parts and wholes. Mereology was originally a variant of nominalism arguing one should dispense with set theory, but the now broader subject of parts and wholes arguably goes back to the time of the [pre-Socratics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Socratic_philosophy).[^n156]

David Lewis introduced the term 'atomless [gunk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunk_%28mereology%29)' for something not made up of [simples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_%28philosophy%29), which instead divides forever into smaller and smaller parts.[^n157] [Peter Van Inwagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Van_Inwagen) believes in [mereological nihilism](wikipedia://en/3303412), except for living beings, a view called [organicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organicism).[^n158] According to mereological nihilism, there are no (say) chairs, just [fundamental particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_particles) arranged chair-wise.[^n159]

### Personal identity {#Personal_identity}

Since [John Locke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Locke)'s *[An Essay Concerning Human Understanding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Essay_Concerning_Human_Understanding)* (1690), philosophers have been concerned with the problem of [personal identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_identity). Locke thought psychological continuity or [memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory) made one the same person over time.[^n160] [Bernard Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Williams) in *The Self and the Future* (1970) takes the opposite view, and argues that personal identity is bodily identity rather than mental continuity.[^n161][^n162]

[Derek Parfit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek_Parfit) in *[Reasons and Persons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reasons_and_Persons)* (1984) defends a kind of [bundle theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundle_theory) of personal identity.[^n163] Parfit issues several thought experiments including [teleportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teleportation), and cases of [fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fission_%28biology%29), where one person splits into two, say surviving with half of their brain, while the other half is put into a new body.[^n164] David Lewis defends [perdurantism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perdurantism), where people are [four-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-dimensionalism), so a person at any one time is only a [part](wikipedia://en/7453251) or slice of the whole person.[^n147]

:::

### Free will and determinism {#Free_will_and_determinism}

Peter van Inwagen's [monograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monograph) *An Essay on Free Will* (1983) played an important role in rehabilitating [libertarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libertarianism_%28metaphysics%29), with respect to [free will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_will), in mainstream analytic philosophy.[^n165][^n166] He introduces the [consequence argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consequence_argument) and the term [incompatibilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incompatibilism) about free will and [determinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinism), to stand in contrast to [compatibilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compatibilism)—the view that free will is compatible with determinism. [Charlie Broad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._D._Broad) had previously made similar arguments.[^n167]

### Principle of sufficient reason {#Principle_of_sufficient_reason}

Since Leibniz philosophers have discussed the [principle of sufficient reason](wikipedia://en/1137736), or PSR. Van Inwagen criticizes the PSR,[^n165] while [Alexander Pruss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Pruss) defends it.[^n168]

### Philosophy of time {#Philosophy_of_time}

Analytic [philosophy of time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_space_and_time) traces its roots to British idealist [John McTaggart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._M._E._McTaggart)'s article "[The Unreality of Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Unreality_of_Time)" (1908). McTaggart distinguishes between the dynamic or tensed [A-theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_series_and_B_series) of time (past, present, future), in which [time flows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_of_time); and the static or tenseless [B-theory of time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B-theory_of_time) (earlier than, simultaneous with, later than).[^n169][^n170] [Arthur Prior](wikipedia://en/308764), who invented [tense logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tense_logic), advocated the A-theory of time.[^n171] Along with David Lewis's perdurantism, the [theory of special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) seems to advocate a B-theory of time.[^n172][^n173]

[Eternalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eternalism_%28philosophy_of_time%29) holds that past, present, and future are equally real. In contrast, [presentism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presentism_%28philosophy_of_time%29) holds that only entities in the present exist.[^n174] The [moving spotlight theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moving_spotlight_theory_of_time) is a kind of hybrid view where all moments exist, but only one moment is present.[^n175] [Growing block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growing_block_universe), advocated by Charlie Broad, holds that only the past and present exist, but the future does not (yet) exist (there is also the reverse, a shrinking block).[^n175][^n176]

### Logical pluralism {#Logical_pluralism}

[Many-valued](wikipedia://en/38024) and [non-classical](wikipedia://en/3192556) logics have been popular since the Polish logician [Jan Łukasiewicz](wikipedia://en/305064).[^n36] [Graham Priest](wikipedia://en/3659382) is a [dialetheist](wikipedia://en/591359), denying the [law of non-contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_non-contradiction), seeing it as the most natural solution to problems such as the [liar paradox](wikipedia://en/17967).[^n177] [JC Beall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JC_Beall), together with [Greg Restall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greg_Restall), is a pioneer of a widely discussed version of [logical pluralism](wikipedia://en/49180254), the view that there is more than one correct logic.[^n178]

## Epistemology {#Epistemology}

*Image: Edmund L Gettier III ca 1960s umass.jpg.* Edmund Gettier helped to revitalize analytic epistemology.

Owing largely to [Edmund Gettier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Gettier)'s paper "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" (1963)[^n179] and the so-called [Gettier problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gettier_problem), epistemology has since enjoyed a resurgence as a topic of analytic philosophy. Using [epistemic luck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemic_luck), Gettier provided counterexamples to the "justified true belief" (JTB) definition of knowledge, found as early as [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954)'s dialogue *[Theaetetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theaetetus_%28dialogue%29)*.[^n180] Philosophers give alternatives to the JTB account or develop [theories of justification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_justification) to deal with Gettier's examples. For example, [Timothy Williamson](wikipedia://en/3055177) argues in *[Knowledge and Its Limits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_and_Its_Limits)* (2000) that knowledge is *[sui generis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sui_generis)* and indefinable.[^n181]

### Theories of justification {#Theories_of_justification}

American philosopher [Roderick Chisholm](wikipedia://en/408329) defended foundationalism.[^n182] [Michael Huemer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Huemer) defends a type of foundationalism called [phenomenal conservatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenal_conservatism).[^n183] Quine defended coherentism, a "[web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spider_web) of [belief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief)",[^n128] and thought all beliefs are open to revision; some are just held stronger than others, and so [hold come what may](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hold_come_what_may). [Ernest Sosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Sosa) proposed [virtue epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtue_epistemology) in "The Raft and the Pyramid" (1980).[^n184] [Alvin Goldman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvin_Goldman) developed a [causal theory of knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causal_theory_of_knowledge).[^n185]

The debate between [internalism and externalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internalism_and_externalism) still exists in analytic philosophy.[^n186] Huemer is an internalist.[^n183] Goldman is an externalist known for developing a popular form of externalism called [reliabilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reliabilism).[^n185] Most externalists reject the [KK thesis](wikipedia://en/13793087), which has been disputed since the introduction of [epistemic logic](wikipedia://en/3827197) by [Jaakko Hintikka](wikipedia://en/1710903) in 1962.[^n187][^n188] [Fallibilists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallibilism) also often reject the KK thesis.[^n189]

::: {#o294 .problem cue=named-heading name="Problem of the criterion"}

### Problem of the criterion {#Problem_of_the_criterion}

Discussed since antiquity, Chisholm, in his *Theory of Knowledge* (1966), details the [problem of the criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem_of_the_criterion) with two sets of questions:

1. What do we know? or What is the extent of our knowledge?
1. How do we know? or What is the criterion for deciding whether we have knowledge in any particular case?[^n190]

Answering the former question first is called *[particularism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemological_particularism)*, whereas answering the latter first is called *[methodism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodism_%28philosophy%29)*. A third solution is *[skepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_skepticism)*, or doubting there is such a thing as knowledge.[^n190][^n191]

:::

### Closure {#Closure}

[Epistemic closure](wikipedia://en/11073025) is the claim that knowledge is closed under [entailment](wikipedia://en/37531624); in other words, epistemic closure is a property or the [principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept) that if a subject $S$ knows $p$, and $S$ knows that $p$ entails $q$, then $S$ can thereby come to know $q$.[^o299] Most epistemological theories involve a closure principle, as do many skeptical arguments (e. g. the [dream argument](wikipedia://en/4897231)). In *Proof of An External World* (1939), G. E. Moore uses closure in his famous anti-skeptical "[here is one hand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Here_is_one_hand)" argument.[^n192] Shortly before his death, Wittgenstein wrote the posthumously published *[On Certainty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Certainty)* (1969) in response to Moore.[^n193][^n194]

While the closure principle is generally regarded as intuitive, philosophers, such as [Fred Dretske](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Dretske) with [relevant alternatives theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relevant_alternatives_theory)[^n195] and [Robert Nozick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Nozick)'s truth tracking theory of knowledge in *[Philosophical Explanations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Explanations)* (1981), have argued against it.[^n196] Others argue it is true but only given a specific [context](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contextualism).[^n197]

### Induction {#Induction}

*Image: Зүмірет.jpg.* All emeralds are "grue".

In his book *[Fact, Fiction, and Forecast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fact,_Fiction,_and_Forecast)* (1955), Nelson Goodman introduced the "[new riddle of induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_riddle_of_induction)", so-called by analogy with Hume's classical [problem of induction](wikipedia://en/177456).[^n198] Goodman's famous example was to introduce the predicates [grue and bleen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_riddle_of_induction). "Grue" applies to all things before a certain arbitrary time *t*, just in case they are green, but also just in case they are blue after time *t*; and "bleen" applies to all things before time *t*, just in the case they are blue, but also just in case they are green after time *t*. So the inductive inference "All emeralds are grue" will be true before time *t* but "All emeralds are bleen" will be true after *t.*[^n198]

### Other topics {#Other_topics}

Other, related topics of research include debates over cases of knowledge, the value of knowledge, the nature of [evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence), the role of [intuitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuition_%28philosophy%29) in justification, and [abduction](wikipedia://en/60459).[^n199]

## Ethics {#Ethics}

Early analytic philosophers often thought ethics could not be made rigorous enough to merit any attention.[^n200] It was only with the emergence of ordinary-language philosophers that ethics started to become acceptable.[^n200] Analytic philosophers have gradually come to distinguish three major types of moral philosophy.

- [Meta-ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meta-ethics), which investigates moral terms and concepts;
- [Normative ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normative_ethics), which examines and produces ethical judgments;
- [Applied ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_ethics), which applies normative principles to specific, practical issues.[^n201]

### Meta-ethics {#Meta-ethics}

As well as Hume's famous [is–ought problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Is–ought_problem),[^n202] twentieth-century meta-ethics has two original strains.

#### *Principia Ethica* {#Principia_Ethica}

The first strain is based on G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* (1903), which advances [non-naturalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethical_non-naturalism) [moral realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_realism). The work is known for the [open question argument](wikipedia://en/886000) and identifying the [naturalistic fallacy](wikipedia://en/21826), major topics for analytic philosophers. According to Moore, goodness is *sui generis*, a simple (undefinable), non-natural property.[^n203][^n204] Contemporary philosophers, such as [Russ Shafer-Landau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russ_Shafer-Landau) in *[Moral Realism: A Defence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_Realism:_A_Defence)* (2003), still defend ethical non-naturalism.[^n205]

After Moore's work, not much was done in analytic philosophy with ethics until the 1950s and 1960s, when there was a renewed interest in traditional moral philosophy.[^n200] [Philippa Foot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippa_Foot) defended [naturalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethical_naturalism) moral realism and contributed several essays attacking other theories. Foot introduced the famous "[trolley problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trolley_problem)" into the ethical discourse.[^n206] A student and friend of Wittgenstein, [Elizabeth Anscombe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Anscombe), wrote a monograph *Intention* (1957) with an influential treatment of [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_theory_%28philosophy%29).[^n207] Her article "[Modern Moral Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Moral_Philosophy)" (1958) criticized [consequentialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consequentialism).[^n208]

#### Emotivism {#Emotivism}

The second strain is founded on logical positivism and its attitude that unverifiable statements are meaningless. As a result, they avoided normative ethics and instead pursued meta-ethics. The logical positivists thought statements about [value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_%28ethics%29)—including all ethical and aesthetic judgments—are [non-cognitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-cognitivism). As a result, they adopted an [emotivist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotivism) theory, also known as the hurrah/boo theory, according to which valuing judgments expressed the attitude of the speaker.[^n209] On this view, saying, "Murder is wrong", is equivalent to saying, "[Boo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booing) to murder", or saying the word "murder" with a particular tone of disapproval.

J. O. Urmson gave his own "grading theory" in "On Grading" (1950) and was admiring but critical of emotivism in *The Emotive Theory of Ethics* (1968).[^n210][^n211][^n212] Emotivism evolved into more sophisticated non-cognitivist theories, such as the [expressivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expressivism) of [Charles Stevenson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Stevenson_%28philosopher%29) in *[Ethics and Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics_and_Language)* (1944)*,* and the [universal prescriptivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_prescriptivism) of [R. M. Hare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._M._Hare), which was based on Austin's philosophy of speech acts.[^n213][^n214] Other [anti-realist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_anti-realism) moral theorists include Australian [John Mackie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._L._Mackie), who in *[Ethics: Inventing Right And Wrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics:_Inventing_Right_and_Wrong)* (1977) defended [error theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_theory) and the [argument from queerness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument_from_queerness).[^n215] Bernard Williams also influenced ethics by advocating a kind of [moral relativism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_relativism) and rejecting all other theories.[^n216]

### Normative ethics {#Normative_ethics}

*Image: Alasdair MacIntyre.jpg.* Alasdair MacIntyre

As the influence of logical positivism declined, analytic philosophers had a renewed interest in normative ethics. Contemporary normative ethics is dominated by three schools: [consequentialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consequentialism), [deontology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deontology), and [virtue ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtue_ethics).

At first, consequentialism or [utilitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarianism) was the only non-skeptical theory to remain popular among analytic philosophers. [Henry Sidgwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Sidgwick)'s *[The Methods of Ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Methods_of_Ethics)* (1874) exemplified the common theory.[^n217] Robert Nozick criticizes utilitarianism with the [utility monster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_monster).[^n218] [John Rawls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Rawls)'s *[A Theory of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Theory_of_Justice)* (1971) restored interest in Kantian, deontological ethical philosophy.[^n219] [Thomas Nagel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Nagel) also defended deontology.[^n220]

Anscombe, Foot, and [Alasdair Macintyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alasdair_MacIntyre)'s *After Virtue* (1981) sparked a revival of Aristotle's [virtue ethical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtue_ethics) approach.[^n221][^n222][^n223] This increased interest in virtue ethics has been dubbed by some the "[aretaic turn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtue_ethics)".[^n224] Similar to Aristotle's notion of *[eudaimonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eudaimonia)*,[^n223] [Władysław Tatarkiewicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Władysław_Tatarkiewicz) proposed a conception of [happiness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_happiness) as a full and lasting satisfaction with one's whole life.[^n225]

### Applied ethics {#Applied_ethics}

Since around 1970, a significant feature of analytic philosophy has been the emergence of applied ethics. Difficult cases are often created by new technology or new scientific knowledge.[^n201] Topics of special interest include education, such as [equal opportunity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_opportunity) and punishment in schools,[^n226] [environmental ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_ethics),[^n53][^o349] [animal rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_rights),[^o350] and the many challenges created by advancing [medical science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioethics), such as [abortion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abortion) or [euthanasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euthanasia).[^n206][^o352] [Peter Singer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Singer) argues for [vegetarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetarianism) in the book *[Animal Liberation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_Liberation_%28book%29)* (1975).[^n227]

## Political philosophy {#Political_philosophy}

One of the most influential figures in the [philosophy of law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_law) was ordinary language philosopher [H. L. A. Hart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._L._A._Hart), who was instrumental in the development of [legal positivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_positivism), popularized by his book *[The Concept of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Concept_of_Law)* (1961).[^n228] Influenced by Hart and [Ronald Dworkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Dworkin), [Matthew Kramer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Kramer) also proposed ethical (or normative) legal positivism.[^n229]

### Liberalism {#Liberalism}

*Image: John Rawls (1971 photo portrait).jpg.* John Rawls

During World War II, [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper) defended the [open society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_society) in *[The Open Society and its Enemies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Open_Society_and_its_Enemies)* (1945).[^n230] [Isaiah Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaiah_Berlin) had a lasting influence with his lecture "[Two Concepts of Liberty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Concepts_of_Liberty)" (1958).[^n231][^o360] Berlin defined 'negative liberty' as absence of coercion or interference in private actions. 'Positive liberty' could be thought of as self-mastery, which asks not what we are free from, but what we are free to do.

Current analytic political philosophy owes much to [John Rawls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Rawls), who in a series of papers (most notably "Two Concepts of Rules" (1955) and "Justice as Fairness" (1958)) and his book *[A Theory of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Theory_of_Justice)* (1971) produced a sophisticated defense of a generally liberal [egalitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egalitarianism) account of distributive justice.[^n232][^n233][^n219] Rawls introduced the thought experiment of the [veil of ignorance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Original_position).

Rawls's colleague Robert Nozick's book *[Anarchy, State, and Utopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarchy,_State,_and_Utopia)* (1974) defends [free-market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free-market) [libertarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libertarianism).[^n234] It is notable for the [Wilt Chamberlain argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilt_Chamberlain_argument).[^n235][^n236] Nozick also famously considers an objection to the [labor theory of property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_theory_of_property) found in Locke's *[Second Treatise on Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Treatises_of_Government)* (1689):[^n237]

### Analytical Marxism {#Analytical_Marxism}

Another development was the school of [analytical Marxism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_Marxism), which applies analytic techniques to the theories of [Karl Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx) and his successors. The best-known member is [G. A. Cohen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._A._Cohen), whose book *[Karl Marx's Theory of History: A Defence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx's_Theory_of_History:_A_Defence)* (1978) defends Marx's [historical materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_materialism), and is generally considered the genesis of the school.[^n238] Cohen jettisons the [labor theory of value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_theory_of_value),[^n239] and describes the structure of proletarian unfreedom,[^n240] emphasizing power and freedom rather than labor-power and value.[^n241][^n242][^n243] Other prominent analytical Marxists include the economist [John Roemer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Roemer), the social scientist [Jon Elster](wikipedia://en/2159322), and the sociologist [Erik Olin Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erik_Olin_Wright). These later philosophers furthered Cohen's work by bringing to bear modern social science methods, such as [rational choice theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_choice_theory).[^n244]

Although a continental philosopher, [Frankfurt School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt_School) theorist [Jürgen Habermas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jürgen_Habermas) is another influential—if controversial—author in contemporary analytic political philosophy. Habermas's social theory is a blend of social science, Marxism, post-Kantianism, and pragmatism.[^n245]

### Communitarianism {#Communitarianism}

[Communitarians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communitarian) such as Alasdair MacIntyre, [Charles Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Taylor_%28philosopher%29), [Michael Walzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Walzer), and Michael Sandel use analytic techniques to challenge liberal assumptions.[^n221] In particular, communitarians challenge whether the individual can be considered apart from the community in which he is brought up and lives. While in the analytic tradition, its major exponents often also engage at length with figures generally considered continental, notably Hegel and [Friedrich Nietzsche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Nietzsche).

### Other critics of liberalism {#Other_critics_of_liberalism}

Other critics of liberalism include the [feminist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feminism) critiques by [Catharine MacKinnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catharine_MacKinnon) and [Andrea Dworkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_Dworkin), and the [multiculturalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiculturalism) critiques by [Amy Gutmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amy_Gutmann), [Charles Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Taylor_%28philosopher%29), and left-libertarians such as [Hillel Steiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillel_Steiner).

## Aesthetics {#Aesthetics}

While pragmatist [George Santayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Santayana) wrote *[The Sense of Beauty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sense_of_Beauty)* (1896), and British idealist [R. G. Collingwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._G._Collingwood) developed a theory of [aesthetic expressivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetic_expressivism) in *The Principles of Art* (1938),[^n246] aesthetics was not addressed in the analytic style until the 1950s and 1960s, by the likes of [Susanne Langer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susanne_Langer), [Frank Sibley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Sibley_%28philosopher%29), [Morris Weitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris_Weitz), and Nelson Goodman.[^n247][^n248] Since Goodman and *[Languages of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Art)* (1968), aesthetics as a discipline for analytic philosophers has flourished.[^n249]

### Definitions of art {#Definitions_of_art}

Sibley, Weitz, and Goodman were anti-essentialists. In "The Role of Theory in Aesthetics" (1956), Weitz famously argues [necessary and sufficient conditions](wikipedia://en/169319) will never exist for the concept 'art' because it is an "open concept".[^n250] Goodman thought art is not so different from science, and is another branch of epistemology.[^o388]

[Arthur Danto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Danto) argued for an "[institutional definition of art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_art#Institutional)" in the essay "The Artworld" (1964) in which Danto coined the term "artworld" (as opposed to the existing "[art world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_world)", though they mean the same), by which he meant cultural context or "an atmosphere of [art theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_theory)".[^n251][^o390] [George Dickie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Dickie_%28philosopher%29) similarly states "a work of art in the classificatory sense is 1) an artifact 2) on which some person or persons acting on behalf of a certain social institution (the artworld) has conferred the status of candidate for appreciation."[^n252] Dickie's student [Noël Carroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noël_Carroll) is a leading philosopher of art contributing to the [philosophy of film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_film).

There is also the historical definition, best exemplified by [Jerrold Levinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerrold_Levinson). For Levinson, "a work of art is a thing intended for regard-as-a-work-of-art: regard in any of the ways works of art existing prior to it have been correctly regarded."[^n253] In the opinion of historian of aesthetics Władysław Tatarkiewicz, there are six conditions for the presentation of art: beauty, form, representation, reproduction of reality, artistic expression, and innovation.[^n254] Nicholas Wolterstorff emphasizes the social aspect of art, not as mere contemplation but as action.[^n255] Langer, Levinson and Wolterstorff have all contributed to the [philosophy of music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_music).

### Beauty {#Beauty}

[Guy Sircello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy_Sircello)'s work resulted in new analytic theories of love,[^n256] sublimity,[^n257] and beauty.[^n258] For Sircello, beauty is an objective, [qualitative property](wikipedia://en/1901895). One author claims Sircello's theory is similar to Hume's.[^n259] [Mary Mothersill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Mothersill) sought to restore earlier conceptions of beauty in *Beauty Restored* (1984).[^n260] [Roger Scruton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Scruton) also advanced theories of beauty. According to Kant scholar [Paul Guyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Guyer), "After [Wollheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Wollheim), the most significant British aesthetician has been Roger Scruton."[^n261] Scruton contributed to the [philosophy of architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_architecture).

::: {#o403 .statement cue=named-heading name="Paradox of fiction"}

### Paradox of fiction {#Paradox_of_fiction}

[Colin Radford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Radford) and Michael Weston introduced the [paradox of fiction](wikipedia://en/36266017) in their paper "How Can We Be Moved by the Fate of Anna Karenina?" (1975)[^n262] The paper discusses emotional responses to fiction, such as [Leo Tolstoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Tolstoy)'s novel *[Anna Karenina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Karenina)*.[^n262] Their question is how people can be moved by things that do not exist. The paper concluded people's emotional responses to fiction are irrational.[^n262] American philosopher [Kendall Walton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kendall_Walton)'s paper "Fearing Fictions" (1978) addresses the paradox.[^n263][^n264] This paper served as the impetus for [make-believe theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kendall_Walton#Make-believe_theory).

:::

## Philosophy of language {#Philosophy_of_language}

Philosophy of language is still strongly influenced by earlier authors.

### Semantics {#Semantics}

John Searle advocated Russell's descriptivism and tried to elaborate it with a "cluster theory" of descriptions.[^o411] Kripke challenged the descriptivist theory with a [causal theory of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causal_theory_of_reference) in *Naming and Necessity*.[^n265] According to one author, "In the philosophy of language, *Naming and Necessity* is among the most important works ever."[^n141] Ruth Barcan Marcus also challenged descriptivism with a [direct reference theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_reference_theory), in her case a [tag theory of names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tag_theory_of_names).[^n266] [Keith Donnellan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Donnellan) too challenged descriptivism.[^n267]

Hilary Putnam used the Twin Earth and [brain in a vat](wikipedia://en/265952) thought experiments to argue for [semantic externalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_externalism), or the view that the meanings of words are not psychological.[^n145][^n146][^n268] [Donald Davidson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Davidson_%28philosopher%29) uses the thought experiment of [Swampman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swampman) to advocate for semantic externalism.[^n269] [Tyler Burge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyler_Burge) uses the thought experiment of [arthritis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthritis) in one's thigh.[^n270]

Kripke in *[Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wittgenstein_on_Rules_and_Private_Language)* (1982) provides a skeptical, rule-following paradox, undermining the possibility of our ever following rules, and so calls into question the idea of meaning. Kripke writes this paradox is "the most radical and original skeptical problem that philosophy has seen to date".[^n271] The [portmanteau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portmanteau) "Kripkenstein" has been coined as a term for a fictional person who holds the views expressed by Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein.

[Alonzo Church](wikipedia://en/49731) pioneered intensional logic.[^o422] Czech philosopher [Pavel Tichý](wikipedia://en/4565688) developed [transparent intensional logic](wikipedia://en/5868245).[^o422]

### Pragmatics {#Pragmatics}

[Paul Grice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Grice) and his [maxims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grice's_maxims) and theory of [implicature](wikipedia://en/393671) established the discipline of [pragmatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics).[^n272] Austin and [John Searle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Searle) also influenced the field. Pragmatics focuses on [deixis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deixis) and presuppositions and other context-dependent features of language.[^n273][^n274][^o427]

## Philosophy of mind {#Philosophy_of_mind}

*Image: John searle2.jpg.* John Searle

Analytic philosophy's interest in philosophy of language has arguably been superseded by an interest in the philosophy of mind.[^n6] Two common notions in analytic philosophy of mind are intentionality, as above, and [qualia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qualia), a term introduced by C. I. Lewis.[^n275]

### Physicalism {#Physicalism}

[Emergent materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergent_materialism) holds that mental properties emerge as novel properties of complex material systems.[^n276] It can be divided into emergence which denies [mental causation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_causation) and emergence which allows for causal effect. A version of the latter type was advocated by John Searle, called [biological naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_naturalism). [Douglas Hofstadter](wikipedia://en/8758) also believes the mind is emergent in *[Gödel, Escher, Bach: An Eternal Golden Braid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel,_Escher,_Bach:_An_Eternal_Golden_Braid)*, from [self-referential](wikipedia://en/28545) "[strange loops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strange_loops)".[^n277]

The other main group of materialist views in the philosophy of mind can be labeled non-emergent (or non-emergentist) materialism, and includes philosophical [behaviorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_behaviorism), [type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type-token_distinction) [identity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_theory_of_mind) (reductive materialism), [functionalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functionalism_%28philosophy_of_mind%29), and pure physicalism ([eliminative materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eliminative_materialism)).

#### Behaviorism {#Behaviorism}

Motivated by the logical positivists, behaviorism was the most prominent theory of mind in analytic philosophy for the first half of the twentieth century.[^n278] Behaviorists believed either that statements about the mind were equivalent to *statements about* behavior and [dispositions](wikipedia://en/4265764) to behave in particular ways; or that mental states were directly equivalent to behavior and dispositions to behave. Hilary Putnam criticized behaviorism by arguing that it confuses the symptoms of mental states with the mental states themselves, positing "super Spartans" who never display signs of pain.[^n279]

*Image: Hilary Putnam.jpg.* Hilary Putnam

::: {#o438 .statement cue=named-heading name="Type identity"}

#### Type identity {#Type_identity}

Behaviorism later became much less popular in favor of either type identity theory or functionalism.[^o439] Type identity theory or type physicalism identified mental states with brain states. Former students of Ryle at the [University of Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Adelaide) [Jack Smart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._C._Smart) and [Ullin Place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ullin_Place) argued for type physicalism.[^n280][^n281] Type identity was criticized by Putnam and others using [multiple realizability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_realizability).[^n282] The criticism spawned [anomalous monism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anomalous_monism).[^n283]

:::

#### Functionalism {#Functionalism}

Functionalism remains the dominant theory. [Computationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_theory_of_mind) is a kind of functionalism. The view was first associated with Sellars.[^n284] Putnam was also a functionalist.[^n282] Another functionalist was [Jerry Fodor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_Fodor), who is known for proposing the [modularity of mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modularity_of_mind), a theory of [innateness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_nativism).[^n285] He also introduced the [language of thought hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_of_thought_hypothesis), which describes thought as possessing syntax or compositional structure (sometimes known as mentalese).[^n286] Searle's [Chinese room](wikipedia://en/6216) argument criticized functionalism and holds that while a computer can understand syntax, it could never understand semantics.[^n287] A similar idea is [Ned Block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ned_Block)'s [China brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_brain).[^n288]

#### Eliminativism {#Eliminativism}

The view of eliminative materialism is most closely associated with [Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Churchland) and [Patricia Churchland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patricia_Churchland), who deny the existence of propositional attitudes;[^n289] and with [Daniel Dennett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Dennett), who in works like *[Consciousness Explained](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consciousness_Explained)* (1991) is generally considered an eliminativist about qualia and phenomenal aspects of consciousness (but not about [intentionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intentional_stance)[^n290]).[^n291][^n292] Dennett coined the term "[intuition pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuition_pump)."[^n293] Thomas Nagel's paper "[What Is It Like to Be a Bat?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_Is_It_Like_to_Be_a_Bat?)" challenged the physicalist account of mind;[^n294] so did [Frank Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Jackson_%28philosopher%29)'s [knowledge argument](wikipedia://en/539988), which argues for qualia.[^n295][^n296]

### Dualism {#Dualism}

*Image: David chalmers.jpg.* David Chalmers

Finally, analytic philosophy has featured a certain number of philosophers who were dualists, and recently forms of [property dualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property_dualism) have had a resurgence; the most prominent representative is [David Chalmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Chalmers).[^o461] Chalmers introduced the notion of the [hard problem of consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_problem_of_consciousness). He has criticized [interactionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interactionism_%28philosophy_of_mind%29) and shown sympathy with [neutral monism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_monism). Kripke also makes a notable argument for dualism.[^n297][^n298] [Epiphenomenalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epiphenomenalism), the view that mental events are caused by physical events in the brain but do not cause anything else in return, is sometimes classed as a kind of property dualism.[^n295][^n299]

### Panpsychism {#Panpsychism}

Yet another view is [panpsychism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panpsychism), or the view that mentality is fundamental and ubiquitous in the natural world.[^n300] Panpsychism can be contrasted with [idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idealism) by still believing in matter.

### Perception and consciousness {#Perception_and_consciousness}

In recent years, a central focus of research in the philosophy of mind has been consciousness and the philosophy of perception. The [homunculus argument](wikipedia://en/1079427) is an objection raised against many older theories of perception.[^n301] While there is now a general consensus for the global neuronal workspace model of consciousness,[^n302] there are many opinions as to the specifics. The best known theories in analytic philosophy are Searle's naive realism, Fred Dretske and [Michael Tye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Tye_%28philosopher%29)'s [representationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_and_indirect_realism), Dennett's [heterophenomenology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterophenomenology), and the [higher-order theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher-order_theories_of_consciousness) of either [David M. Rosenthal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_M._Rosenthal_%28philosopher%29)—who advocates a higher-order thought (HOT) model—or David Armstrong and [William Lycan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lycan)—who advocate a higher-order perception (HOP) model.[^n303]

## Philosophy of mathematics {#Philosophy_of_mathematics}

*Image: Kurt gödel.jpg.* Kurt Gödel

[Kurt Gödel](wikipedia://en/16736), a student of Hans Hahn of the Vienna Circle, produced his [incompleteness theorems](wikipedia://en/58863) showing that *Principia Mathematica* also failed to reduce arithmetic to logic, and that [Hilbert's program](wikipedia://en/607286) was unattainable.[^n304] [Ernst Zermelo](wikipedia://en/178655) and [Abraham Fraenkel](wikipedia://en/528451) established [Zermelo Fraenkel Set Theory](wikipedia://en/152214) (with the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840), abbreviated as ZFC).[^o488] Quine developed his own system, dubbed [New Foundations](wikipedia://en/945957).[^n305]

Physicist [Eugene Wigner](wikipedia://en/10101)'s seminal paper "[The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences](wikipedia://en/171377)" (1960) poses the question of why a formal pursuit like mathematics can have real utility.[^n306]

[Hilbert's Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_Hotel) shows some of the counterintuitive properties of [infinite sets](wikipedia://en/142679).[^n307] [José Benardete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/José_Benardete) in *Infinity: An Essay in Metaphysics* (1964) argued for the reality of [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824).[^n308] The [Grim Reaper paradox](wikipedia://en/77336294) stems from his work.[^n309] [Finitists](wikipedia://en/152900) reject infinity.[^o488]

Akin to the medieval debate on universals, between realists, idealists, and nominalists; the philosophy of mathematics has the debate between logicists or platonists, conceptualists or [intuitionists](wikipedia://en/19513), and [formalists](wikipedia://en/16258342).[^n26]

### Platonism {#Platonism}

Gödel was a platonist who postulated a special kind of intuition that lets one perceive mathematical objects directly.[^n310] Quine and Putnam argued for platonism with the [indispensability argument](wikipedia://en/58885745).[^o488] [Edward Zalta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_N._Zalta) devised [abstract object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_and_concrete) [theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_object_theory).[^n311] [Crispin Wright](wikipedia://en/302883), along with [Bob Hale](wikipedia://en/13931788), led a Neo-Fregean revival with the work *Frege's Conception of Numbers as Objects* (1983).[^n312] Physicist [Roger Penrose](wikipedia://en/26193) is also a mathematical platonist, in works like *[The Road to Reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Road_to_Reality)* (2004).[^n313]

[Structuralist](wikipedia://en/29391369) [Paul Benacerraf](wikipedia://en/1432190) has two well-known objections to mathematical platonism; one is about [identification](wikipedia://en/42148664),[^n314] and the other [epistemological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benacerraf's_epistemological_problem).[^n315] In the latter, Benacerraf argues that while platonism explains mathematical semantics, it does not simultaneously explain mathematical knowledge. It is hard to know anything about a far-removed, platonic object.[^n315] [Predicativism](wikipedia://en/1930406) is another alternative to platonism, utilizing [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740)'s response to Russell's paradox.[^o488] There are also [Aristotelians](wikipedia://en/68103938) in mathematics, such as [Dale Jacquette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dale_Jacquette).[^n316]

### Intuitionism {#Intuitionism}

The intuitionists, led by Dutch mathematician [L. E. J. Brouwer](wikipedia://en/172896), are a [constructivist](wikipedia://en/21059) school which sees mathematics as a [cognitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognition) [construct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construct_%28philosophy%29) rather than a type of [objective truth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objective_truth).[^o488] Brouwer also influenced Wittgenstein's abandonment of the *Tractatus*.[^n317]

### Formalism {#Formalism}

The formalists, best exemplified by David Hilbert, considered mathematics to be merely the investigation of [formal axiom systems](wikipedia://en/396102).[^o488] [Hartry Field](wikipedia://en/1535742) defended [mathematical fictionalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_fictionalism) in *[Science Without Numbers](wikipedia://en/80162456)* (1980), arguing numbers are dispensable.[^n318]

## Philosophy of religion {#Philosophy_of_religion}

In *Analytic Philosophy of Religion*, [James Franklin Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Franklin_Harris) noted that:

...analytic philosophy has been a very heterogeneous 'movement'.... some forms of analytic philosophy have proven very sympathetic to the philosophy of religion and have provided a philosophical mechanism for responding to other more radical and hostile forms of analytic philosophy.[^n319]

Analytic philosophy tended to avoid the study of [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_religion), largely dismissing (as per the logical positivists) the subject as a part of metaphysics and therefore meaningless. The demise of logical positivism led to a renewed interest in the philosophy of religion, prompting philosophers not only to introduce new problems, but to re-study perennial topics such as the [existence of God](wikipedia://en/621169), the rationality of belief, the nature of [miracles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miracle), the [problem of evil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem_of_evil), and several others.[^n320] The [Society of Christian Philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_Christian_Philosophers) was established in 1978.

### Reformed epistemology {#Reformed_epistemology}

Analytic philosophy formed the basis for some sophisticated Christian arguments, such as those of the [reformed epistemologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformed_epistemology) including [Alvin Plantinga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvin_Plantinga), [William Alston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Alston), and [Nicholas Wolterstorff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Wolterstorff).

*Image: AlvinPlantinga.JPG.* Alvin Plantinga

Plantinga was once described by *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_%28magazine%29)* magazine as "America's leading orthodox [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism) philosopher of God".[^o498] His seminal work *[God and Other Minds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_and_Other_Minds)* (1967) argues belief in God is a properly [basic belief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_belief) akin to the belief in other minds.[^n321] Plantinga also developed a modal [ontological argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontological_argument#Alvin_Plantinga) in *The Nature of Necessity* (1974).[^n322] Plantinga, John Mackie, and [Antony Flew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antony_Flew) debated the use of the *[free will defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvin_Plantinga's_free-will_defense)* as a way to solve the problem of evil.[^n323] Plantinga further issued a trilogy on epistemology, *[Warrant: The Current Debate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warrant:_The_Current_Debate)* (1993), *[Warrant and Proper Function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warrant_and_Proper_Function)* (1993), and *[Warranted Christian Belief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warranted_Christian_Belief)* (2000).[^n324][^n325][^n326] Plantinga's [evolutionary argument against naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_argument_against_naturalism) contends there is a problem in asserting both evolution and naturalism.[^n327]

Alston defended [divine command theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_command_theory).[^n328] [Robert Merrihew Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Merrihew_Adams) also defended divine command theory, and the virtue of [faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faith).[^n329] [William Lane Craig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lane_Craig) defends the [Kalam cosmological argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument) in the [book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Kalām_Cosmological_Argument) of the same name.[^n330]

### Analytic Thomism {#Analytic_Thomism}

Catholic analytic philosophers—such as Elizabeth Anscombe, her husband [Peter Geach](wikipedia://en/302884), MacIntyre, [Anthony Kenny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Kenny), [John Haldane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Haldane_%28philosopher%29), [Eleonore Stump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleonore_Stump), and others—developed [Analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_Thomism) [Thomism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomism).[^n331][^n332]

### Eastern Orthodoxy {#Eastern_Orthodoxy}

Eastern Orthodox convert [Richard Swinburne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Swinburne) wrote a trilogy of books arguing for God, *The Coherence of Theism* (1977), *[The Existence of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Existence_of_God_%28book%29)* (1979), and *Faith and Reason* (1981).[^n333][^n334][^n335] Swinburne is notable for his belief that God's existence is contingent rather than necessary (it is possible God does not exist), but that nonetheless He does exist as a [brute fact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brute_fact).[^n336]

### Wittgenstein and religion {#Wittgenstein_and_religion}

The analytic philosophy of religion has been preoccupied with Wittgenstein, as well as his interpretation of [Søren Kierkegaard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Søren_Kierkegaard).[^n337] Wittgenstein fought for the Austrian army in [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I) and came upon a copy of Leo Tolstoy's *[The Gospel in Brief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gospel_in_Brief)* (1896). He subsequently underwent some kind of religious conversion.[^n338][^n339] "[Swansea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swansea_University) school" philosophers such as Rush Rhees, [Peter Winch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Winch), and [D. Z. Phillips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D._Z._Phillips), among others, founded a school of religious thought based on Wittgenstein. The name "contemplative philosophy" was coined by Phillips in *Philosophy's Cool Place* (1999), after a passage quoted in Wittgenstein's *[Culture and Value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_and_Value)* (1980).[^n340]

## Philosophy of science {#Philosophy_of_science}

The weight given to scientific evidence is largely due to philosophers' commitments to [scientific realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_realism) and [naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_%28philosophy%29). Some such as [Friedrich Hayek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Hayek) in *[The Counter-Revolution of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Counter-Revolution_of_Science)* (1952) see using science in philosophy as [scientism](wikipedia://en/167742).[^n341] Nonetheless, science has had an increasingly significant role in analytic philosophy. The theory of special relativity has had a profound effect on the philosophy of time,[^n172] and [quantum physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_physics) is routinely discussed in the free will debate.[^n342] [Ernest Nagel](wikipedia://en/1216778)'s book *[The Structure of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Structure_of_Science)* (1961) practically inaugurated the field of philosophy of science.[^n343]

### Theories {#Theories}

Carl Hempel advocated confirmation theory or [Bayesian epistemology](wikipedia://en/55503854). He introduced the famous [raven's paradox](wikipedia://en/37391).[^n344]

*Image: Karl Popper.jpg.* Karl Popper

In reaction to what he considered excesses of logical positivism, Karl Popper, in *[The Logic of Scientific Discovery](wikipedia://en/157720)* (1959), rejects the standard [inductivist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductivist) views on the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method) in favor of a highly influential theory of [falsification](wikipedia://en/11283), using it to solve the [demarcation problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demarcation_problem).[^n345]

Quine and French scientist [Pierre Duhem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Duhem) seemed to have similar views in certain respects. The [Duhem–Quine thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duhem–Quine_thesis), or problem of [underdetermination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underdetermination), posits that no [scientific hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis) can be understood in isolation, a viewpoint called [confirmation holism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation_holism).[^n132] Following Quine and Duhem, subsequent theories emphasized [theory-ladenness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory-ladenness).

In reaction to both the logical positivists and Popper, philosophy became dominated by [social constructivist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_constructivism) and [cognitive relativist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_relativism) theories of science. Significant for these discussions is [Thomas Kuhn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Samuel_Kuhn), who in *[The Structure of Scientific Revolutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Structure_of_Scientific_Revolutions)* (1962) formulated the idea of [paradigm shifts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradigm_shift) and sparked a "revolt against positivism" known as the "[historical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historicist) turn".[^n346][^n347] [Paul Feyerabend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Feyerabend)'s book *[Against Method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Against_Method)* (1975) advocates [epistemological anarchism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemological_anarchism); that there are no universal rules for scientific inquiry.[^n348]

### Branches {#Branches}

Philosophers like [Tim Maudlin](wikipedia://en/37708243) focus on the [philosophy of physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_physics). Maudlin argues in *The Metaphysics Within Physics* (2007) that philosophy must reflect on physics, and that [scientific laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_laws) are *sui generis*.[^n349] Recently there has also been work in the [philosophy of chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_chemistry),[^n350] and the [philosophy of biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_biology) has undergone considerable growth, especially due to the debate over the nature of [evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution), particularly [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection).[^n351] Daniel Dennett and his book *[Darwin's Dangerous Idea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darwin's_Dangerous_Idea)* (1995), which defends [Neo-Darwinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Darwinism), stand at the forefront of this debate.[^n352][^n353] Jerry Fodor criticizes natural selection in *[What Darwin Got Wrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_Darwin_Got_Wrong)* (2010).[^n354]

The [philosophy of social science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_social_science) has also received increased interest. Peter Winch takes a Wittgensteinian perspective in *The Idea of a Social Science and its Relation to Philosophy* (1958).[^n355] Searle contributed to [social ontology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_ontology) and the theory of [social constructs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_constructs) with *The Construction of Social Reality* (1995).[^n356]
