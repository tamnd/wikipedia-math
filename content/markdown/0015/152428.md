# Ceva's theorem

*Image: Ceva's theorem 1.svg.* Ceva's theorem, case 1: the three lines are concurrent at a point $O$ inside $\triangle ABC$

*Image: Ceva's theorem 2.svg.* Ceva's theorem, case 2: the three lines are concurrent at a point $O$ outside $\triangle ABC$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ceva's theorem"}

In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), **Ceva's theorem** is a theorem about [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654). Given a triangle $\triangle ABC$, let the [lines](wikipedia://en/946975) $AO, BO, CO$ be drawn from the [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) to a common point $O$ (not on one of the sides of $\triangle ABC$), to meet opposite sides at $D, E, F$ respectively. (The segments $\overline{AD}, \overline{BE}, \overline{CF}$ are known as [cevians](wikipedia://en/6135790).) Then, using [signed lengths of segments](wikipedia://en/22634860#Directed_line_segment),

:::

$$\frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}} \cdot \frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}} \cdot \frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}} = 1.$$

In other words, the length $\overline{XY}$ is taken to be positive or negative according to whether $X$ is to the left or right of $Y$ in some fixed orientation of the line. For example, $\overline{AF} / \overline{FB}$ is defined as having positive value when $F$ is between $A$ and $B$ and negative otherwise.

Ceva's theorem is a theorem of [affine geometry](wikipedia://en/243890), in the sense that it may be stated and proved without using the concepts of angles, areas, and lengths (except for the ratio of the lengths of two [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860) that are [collinear](wikipedia://en/3189581)). It is therefore true for triangles in any [affine plane](wikipedia://en/30764139) over any [field](wikipedia://en/10603).

A slightly adapted [converse](wikipedia://en/30977#Converse) is also true: If points $D, E, F$ are chosen on $BC, AC, AB$ respectively so that

$$\frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}} \cdot \frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}} \cdot \frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}} = 1,$$

then $AD, BE, CF$ are [concurrent](wikipedia://en/2206157), or all three [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497). The converse is often included as part of the theorem.

The theorem is often attributed to [Giovanni Ceva](wikipedia://en/152426), who published it in his 1678 work *De lineis rectis*. But it was proven much earlier by [Yusuf Al-Mu'taman ibn Hűd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yusuf_al-Mu'taman_ibn_Hud), an eleventh-century king of [Zaragoza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaragoza).[^o2][^o3] Ibn Hűd's work, however, had fallen into oblivion, and was rediscovered only in 1985.

Associated with the figures are several terms derived from Ceva's name: [cevian](wikipedia://en/6135790) (the lines $AD, BE, CF$ are the cevians of $O$), **cevian triangle** (the triangle $\triangle DEF$ is the cevian triangle of $O$); cevian nest, anticevian triangle, Ceva conjugate. (*Ceva* is pronounced Chay'va; *cevian* is pronounced chev'ian.)

The theorem is very similar to [Menelaus' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelaus'_theorem) in that their equations differ only in sign. By re-writing each in terms of [cross-ratios](wikipedia://en/401767), the two theorems may be seen as [projective duals](wikipedia://en/539925).[^o4]

::: {#o6 .proof cue=heading name="Proofs"}

## Proofs {#Proofs}

Several proofs of the theorem have been created.[^o7][^o8] Two proofs are given in the following.

The first one is very elementary, using only basic properties of triangle areas.[^o7] However, several cases have to be considered, depending on the position of the point $O$.

The second proof uses [barycentric coordinates](wikipedia://en/298834#Barycentric_coordinates) and [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometry%29), but is not case dependent. Moreover, it works in any [affine plane](wikipedia://en/30764139) over any [field](wikipedia://en/10603).

### Using triangle areas {#Using_triangle_areas}

First, the sign of the [left-hand side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-hand_side) is positive since either all three of the ratios are positive, the case where $O$ is inside the triangle (upper diagram), or one is positive and the other two are negative, the case $O$ is outside the triangle (lower diagram shows one case).

To check the magnitude, note that the area of a triangle of a given height is proportional to its base. So

$$\frac{|\triangle BOD|}{|\triangle COD|}=\frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}}=\frac{|\triangle BAD|}{|\triangle CAD|}.$$

Therefore,

$$\frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}}= \frac{|\triangle BAD|-|\triangle BOD|}{|\triangle CAD|-|\triangle COD|} =\frac{|\triangle ABO|}{|\triangle CAO|}.$$

(Replace the minus with a plus if $A$ and $O$ are on opposite sides of $BC$.) Similarly,

$$\frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}}=\frac{|\triangle BCO|}{|\triangle ABO|},$$

and

$$\frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}}=\frac{|\triangle CAO|}{|\triangle BCO|}.$$

Multiplying these three equations gives

$$\left|\frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}} \cdot \frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}} \cdot \frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}} \right|= 1,$$

as required.

The theorem can also be proven easily using [Menelaus's theorem](wikipedia://en/1480484).[^o10] From the transversal $BOE$ of triangle $\triangle ACF$,

$$\frac{\overline{AB}}{\overline{BF}} \cdot \frac{\overline{FO}}{\overline{OC}} \cdot \frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}} = -1$$

and from the transversal $AOD$ of triangle $\triangle BCF$,

$$\frac{\overline{BA}}{\overline{AF}} \cdot \frac{\overline{FO}}{\overline{OC}} \cdot \frac{\overline{CD}}{\overline{DB}} = -1.$$

The theorem follows by dividing these two equations.

The converse follows as a corollary.[^o7] Let $D, E, F$ be given on the lines $BC, AC, AB$ so that the equation holds. Let $AD, BE$ meet at $O$ and let $F'$ be the point where $CO$ crosses $AB$. Then by the theorem, the equation also holds for $D, E, F'$. Comparing the two,

$$\frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}} = \frac{\overline{AF'}}{\overline{F'B}}$$

But at most one point can cut a segment in a given ratio so $F = F\text{’}$.

### Using barycentric coordinates {#Using_barycentric_coordinates}

Given three points $A, B, C$ that are not [collinear](wikipedia://en/3189581), and a point $O$, that belongs to the same [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29), the [barycentric coordinates](wikipedia://en/298834#Barycentric_coordinates) of $O$ with respect of $A, B, C$ are the unique three numbers $\lambda_A, \lambda_B, \lambda_C$ such that

$$\lambda_A + \lambda_B + \lambda_C =1,$$

and

$$\overrightarrow{XO}=\lambda_A\overrightarrow{XA} + \lambda_B\overrightarrow{XB} + \lambda_C\overrightarrow{XC},$$

for every point $X$ (for the definition of this arrow notation and further details, see [Affine space](wikipedia://en/298834)).

For Ceva's theorem, the point $O$ is supposed to not belong to any line passing through two vertices of the triangle. This implies that $\lambda_A \lambda_B \lambda_C\ne 0.$

If one takes for $X$ the intersection $F$ of the lines $AB$ and $OC$ (see figures), the last equation may be rearranged into

$$\overrightarrow{FO}-\lambda_C\overrightarrow{FC}=\lambda_A\overrightarrow{FA} + \lambda_B\overrightarrow{FB}.$$

The left-hand side of this equation is a vector that has the same direction as the line $CF$, and the right-hand side has the same direction as the line $AB$. These lines have different directions since $A, B, C$ are not collinear. It follows that the two members of the equation equal the zero vector, and

$$\lambda_A\overrightarrow{FA} + \lambda_B\overrightarrow{FB}=0.$$

It follows that

$$\frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}}=\frac{\lambda_B}{\lambda_A},$$

where the left-hand-side fraction is the signed ratio of the lengths of the collinear [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860) $\overline{AF}$ and $\overline{FB}$.

The same reasoning shows

$$\frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}}=\frac{\lambda_C}{\lambda_B}\quad \text{and}\quad \frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}}=\frac{\lambda_A}{\lambda_C}.$$

Ceva's theorem results immediately by taking the product of the three last equations.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The theorem can be generalized to higher-dimensional [simplexes](wikipedia://en/39781) using [barycentric coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycentric_coordinates_%28mathematics%29). Define a cevian of an $n$-simplex as a ray from each vertex to a point on the opposite ($n - 1$)-face ([facet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facet_%28mathematics%29)). Then the cevians are concurrent [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) a [mass distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_distribution) can be assigned to the vertices such that each cevian intersects the opposite facet at its [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961). Moreover, the intersection point of the cevians is the center of mass of the simplex.[^o14][^o15]

Another generalization to higher-dimensional [simplexes](wikipedia://en/39781) extends the conclusion of Ceva's theorem that the product of certain ratios is 1. Starting from a point in a simplex, a point is defined inductively on each $k$-face. This point is the foot of a cevian that goes from the vertex opposite the $k$-face, in a ($k + 1$)-face that contains it, through the point already defined on this ($k + 1$)-face. Each of these points divides the face on which it lies into lobes. Given a cycle of pairs of lobes, the product of the ratios of the volumes of the lobes in each pair is 1.[^o16]

[Routh's theorem](wikipedia://en/4639094) gives the area of the triangle formed by three cevians in the case that they are not concurrent. Ceva's theorem can be obtained from it by setting the area equal to zero and solving.

The analogue of the theorem for general [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621) in the plane has been known since the early nineteenth century.[^o17] The theorem has also been generalized to triangles on other surfaces of [constant curvature](wikipedia://en/3638803).[^o18]

The theorem also has a well-known generalization to spherical and [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), replacing the lengths in the ratios with their sines and hyperbolic sines, respectively.
