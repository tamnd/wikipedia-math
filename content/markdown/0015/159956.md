# Goodhart's law

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Goodhart's law"}

**Goodhart's law** is an [adage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adage) that has been stated as, "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure".[^o15] It is named after British economist [Charles Goodhart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Goodhart), who is credited with expressing the core idea of the adage in a 1975 article on [monetary policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetary_policy) in the United Kingdom:[^o2]

:::

## Priority and background {#Priority_and_background}

*Image: Charles Goodhart delives the 2012 Long Finance conference keynote speech.JPG.* Charles Goodhart, for whom the adage is named, delivering a speech in 2012

Numerous concepts are related to this idea, at least one of which predates Goodhart's statement.[^o5] Notably, [Campbell's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campbell's_law) likely has precedence, as Jeff Rodamar has argued, since various formulations date to 1969.[^o6] Other academics had similar insights at the time. [Jerome Ravetz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome_Ravetz)'s 1971 book *[Scientific Knowledge and Its Social Problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Knowledge_and_Its_Social_Problems)*[^o7] also predates Goodhart, though it does not formulate the same law. Ravetz discusses how systems in general can be [gamed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_the_system), focusing on cases where the goals of a task are complex, sophisticated, or subtle. In such cases, the skilled person executing the tasks properly wants to meet their own goals to the detriment of the assigned tasks. When the goals are instantiated as metrics, this could be seen as equivalent to Goodhart and Campbell's claim.

Shortly after Goodhart's publication, others suggested closely related ideas, including the [Lucas critique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucas_critique) (1976). As applied in [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), the law is also implicit in the idea of [rational expectations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_expectations), an economic theory that those aware of a system of rewards and punishments will optimize their actions within that system to achieve their desired results. For example, if an employee is rewarded based on the number of cars sold each month, they will try to sell more cars, even at a loss.

## Examples {#Examples}

- The [San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Declaration_on_Research_Assessment) denounces several problems in science, and as Goodhart's law explains, one of them is that measurement has become a target. The correlation between [h-index](wikipedia://en/3604693) and scientific awards has decreased since the widespread usage of the h-index.[^o18]
- The [International Union for Conservation of Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Union_for_Conservation_of_Nature)'s (IUCN) measure of [extinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinction) can be used to remove [environmental protections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_protection), which resulted in IUCN becoming more conservative in labeling something as extinct.[^o19][^o20]
- In [healthcare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthcare), the misapplication of metrics can lead to adverse outcomes. For instance, hospitals striving to reduce length of stay (LOS) may inadvertently discharge patients prematurely, leading to increased emergency readmissions.[^o21]
- According to Tom and David Chivers in *[How to Read Numbers](wikipedia://en/76090046)*, the law applied to the [British government response to the COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_government_response_to_the_COVID-19_pandemic) when it announced a target of 100,000 COVID-19 tests per day—initially a target for tests actually carried out and later for maximum capacity of test-taking. The number of useful diagnostic tests was far lower than the government-reported number when it announced it had met the target.[^o22]
- It was used to criticize the British [Thatcher government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premiership_of_Margaret_Thatcher) for trying to conduct monetary policy based on targets for [broad and narrow money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broad_money),[^o23] but the law reflects a much more general phenomenon.[^o24]
- The [No Child Left Behind Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Child_Left_Behind_Act) passed by the [United States Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Congress) often led to teachers forcing students through grades before they were ready, to ensure funding for their school for the foreseeable future.

## See also {#See_also}

- [Campbell's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campbell's_law) – "The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures"
- [Cobra effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobra_effect) – when incentives designed to solve a problem end up rewarding people for making it worse
- [Confirmation bias](wikipedia://en/59160) – the tendency to search for and recall information that confirms or supports one's prior beliefs
- [Gaming the system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaming_the_system) – manipulating rules and procedures to obtain a desired outcome
- [Lucas critique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucas_critique) – the observation that it is naive to try to predict the effects of a change in economic policy entirely based on relationships observed in historical data
- [Map–territory relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Map–territory_relation) – a type of [reification fallacy](wikipedia://en/10822899) where a [model](wikipedia://en/20590) is confused with the thing being modeled
- [McNamara fallacy](wikipedia://en/24290703) – ignoring qualitative factors on the basis that they cannot be measured
- Metric fixation
- [Peter principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_principle) – individuals are promoted based on success in their previous roles, and not the role of the new position
- Reification (fallacy)
- Reflexivity (social theory)
- [Reward hacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reward_hacking) – occurs when artificial intelligence optimizes a poorly specified reward without reaching the intended outcome
- [Surrogation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrogation) – in business, when a measure of a construct of interest evolves to replace that construct
