# Frequency analysis

*Image: English letter frequency (alphabetic).svg.* A typical distribution of letters in [English language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) text. Weak ciphers do not sufficiently mask the distribution, and this might be exploited by a cryptanalyst to read the message.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Frequency analysis"}

In [cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715), **frequency analysis** (also known as **counting letters**) is the study of the [frequency of letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_frequencies) or groups of letters in a [ciphertext](wikipedia://en/175560). The method is used as an aid to breaking [classical ciphers](wikipedia://en/705892).

:::

Frequency analysis is based on the fact that, in any given stretch of written language, certain letters and combinations of letters occur with varying frequencies. Moreover, there is a characteristic distribution of letters that is roughly the same for almost all samples of that language. For instance, given a section of [English language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language), E, T, A and O are the most common, while Z, Q, X and J are rare. Likewise, TH, ER, ON, and AN are the most common pairs of letters (termed *[bigrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bigram)* or *digraphs*), and SS, EE, TT, and FF are the most common repeats.[^o2] The nonsense phrase *[etaoin shrdlu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etaoin_shrdlu)* represents the 12 most frequent letters in typical English language text.

In some ciphers, such properties of the natural language plaintext are preserved in the ciphertext, and these patterns have the potential to be exploited in a [ciphertext-only attack](wikipedia://en/522938).

## Frequency analysis for simple substitution ciphers {#Frequency_analysis_for_simple_substitution_ciphers}

In a simple [substitution cipher](wikipedia://en/48356), each letter of the [plaintext](wikipedia://en/157935) is replaced with another, and any particular letter in the plaintext will always be transformed into the same letter in the ciphertext. For instance, if all occurrences of the letter e turn into the letter X, a ciphertext message containing numerous instances of the letter X would suggest to a cryptanalyst that X represents e.

The basic use of frequency analysis is to first count the frequency of ciphertext letters and then associate guessed plaintext letters with them. More Xs in the ciphertext than anything else suggests that X corresponds to e in the plaintext, but this is not certain; t and a are also very common in English, so X might be either of them. It is unlikely to be a plaintext z or q, which are less common. Thus the cryptanalyst may need to try several combinations of mappings between ciphertext and plaintext letters.

More complex use of statistics can be conceived, such as considering counts of pairs of letters (*bigrams*), triplets (*trigrams*), and so on. This is done to provide more information to the cryptanalyst, for instance, Q and U nearly always occur together in that order in English, even though Q itself is rare.

It is also possible to construct artificially skewed texts. For example, entire novels have been written that omit the letter e altogether — a form of literature known as a [lipogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipogram).

## History and usage {#History_and_usage}

*Image: Al-kindi-cryptanalysis.png.* First page of [Al-Kindi](wikipedia://en/272065)'s 9th century *Manuscript on Deciphering Cryptographic Messages*

*Image: HurufUNCsort.png.* [Arabic Letter Frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_Letter_Frequency) distribution

The first known recorded explanation of frequency analysis (indeed, of any kind of cryptanalysis) was given in the 9th century by [Al-Kindi](wikipedia://en/272065), an [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab) [polymath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath), in *A Manuscript on Deciphering Cryptographic Messages*.[^o8] It has been suggested that a close textual study of the [Qur'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qur'an) first brought to light that [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language) has a characteristic letter frequency.[^o9] Its use spread, and similar systems were widely used in European states by the time of the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance). By 1474, [Cicco Simonetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicco_Simonetta) had written a manual on deciphering encryptions of [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language) and [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_language) text.[^o10]

Several schemes were invented by cryptographers to defeat this weakness in simple substitution encryptions. These included:

- *[Homophonic substitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homophonic_substitution_cipher)*: Use of *homophones* — several alternatives to the most common letters in otherwise monoalphabetic substitution ciphers. For example, for English, both X and Y ciphertext might mean plaintext E.
- *[Polyalphabetic substitution](wikipedia://en/157918)*, that is, the use of several alphabets — chosen in assorted, more or less devious, ways ([Leone Alberti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leone_Alberti) seems to have been the first to propose this); and
- *[Polygraphic substitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygraphic_substitution)*, schemes where pairs or triplets of plaintext letters are treated as units for substitution, rather than single letters, for example, the [Playfair cipher](wikipedia://en/173586) invented by [Charles Wheatstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Wheatstone) in the mid-19th century.

A disadvantage of all these attempts to defeat frequency counting attacks is that it increases complication of both enciphering and deciphering, leading to mistakes. Famously, a British Foreign Secretary is said to have rejected the Playfair cipher because, even if school boys could cope successfully as Wheatstone and Playfair had shown, "our attachés could never learn it!".

The [rotor machines](wikipedia://en/196223) of the first half of the 20th century (for example, the [Enigma machine](wikipedia://en/9256)) were essentially immune to straightforward frequency analysis. However, other kinds of analysis ("attacks") successfully decoded messages from some of those machines.[^o11]

*Image: Frecuencia de uso de letras en español.PNG.* Letter frequency in Spanish

Frequency analysis requires only a basic understanding of the statistics of the plaintext language and some problem-solving skills, and, if performed by hand, tolerance for extensive letter bookkeeping. During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), both the [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom) and the [Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) recruited codebreakers by placing [crossword](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossword) puzzles in major newspapers and running contests for who could solve them the fastest. Several of the ciphers used by the [Axis powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_powers) were breakable using frequency analysis, for example, some of the consular ciphers used by the Japanese. Mechanical methods of letter counting and statistical analysis (generally [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM) card type machinery) were first used in World War II, possibly by the US Army's [SIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signals_Intelligence_Service). Today, the work of letter counting and analysis is done by [computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer) [software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software), which can carry out such analysis in seconds. With modern computing power, classical ciphers are unlikely to provide any real protection for confidential data.

## Frequency analysis in fiction {#Frequency_analysis_in_fiction}

*Image: Dancing men.svg.* Part of the cryptogram in *The Dancing Men*

Frequency analysis has been described in fiction. [Edgar Allan Poe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Allan_Poe)'s "[The Gold-Bug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gold-Bug)" and [Sir Arthur Conan Doyle's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Conan_Doyle) [Sherlock Holmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes) tale "[The Adventure of the Dancing Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Adventure_of_the_Dancing_Men)" are examples of stories which describe the use of frequency analysis to attack simple substitution ciphers. The cipher in the Poe story is encrusted with several deception measures, but this is more a literary device than anything significant cryptographically.
