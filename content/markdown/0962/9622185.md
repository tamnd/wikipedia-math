# Hodges–Lehmann estimator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hodges–Lehmann estimator"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **Hodges–Lehmann estimator** is a [robust](wikipedia://en/2885691) and [nonparametric](wikipedia://en/223369) [estimator](wikipedia://en/1565926) of a population's [location parameter](wikipedia://en/17516). For populations that are symmetric about one [median](wikipedia://en/18837), such as the Gaussian or [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) or the [Student *t*-distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student_t-distribution), the Hodges–Lehmann estimator is a [consistent](wikipedia://en/1497569) and median-unbiased estimate of the population median. For non-symmetric populations, the Hodges–Lehmann estimator estimates the "[pseudo–median](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo–median)", which is closely related to the population median.

:::

The Hodges–Lehmann estimator was proposed originally for estimating the location parameter of one-dimensional populations, but it has been used for many more purposes. It has been used to estimate the [differences](wikipedia://en/17516) between the members of two populations. It has been generalized from univariate populations to [multivariate populations](wikipedia://en/19384), which produce samples of [vectors](wikipedia://en/32370).

It is based on the [Wilcoxon signed-rank statistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilcoxon_signed_rank_test). In statistical theory, it was an early example of a [rank-based estimator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R-estimator), an important class of estimators both in nonparametric statistics and in robust statistics. The Hodges–Lehmann estimator was proposed in 1963 independently by [Pranab Kumar Sen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pranab_Kumar_Sen) and by [Joseph Hodges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Lawson_Hodges,_Jr.) and [Erich Lehmann](wikipedia://en/16388476), and so it is also called the "**Hodges–Lehmann–Sen estimator**".[^o2]

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

In the simplest case, the "Hodges–Lehmann" statistic estimates the location parameter for a univariate population.[^o5][^o6] Its computation can be described quickly. For a dataset with *n* measurements, the set of all possible two-element subsets of it $(z_i,z_j)$ such that $i$ ≤ $j$ (i.e. specifically including self-pairs; many secondary sources incorrectly omit this detail), which set has *n*(*n* + 1)/2 elements. For each such subset, the mean is computed; finally, the median of these *n*(*n* + 1)/2 averages is defined to be the Hodges–Lehmann estimator of location.

The two-sample Hodges–Lehmann statistic is an estimate of a location-shift type [difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_%28mathematics%29) between two populations. For two sets of data with *m* and *n* observations, the set of two-element sets made of them is their Cartesian product, which contains *m* × *n* pairs of points (one from each set); each such pair defines one difference of values. The Hodges–Lehmann statistic is the [median](wikipedia://en/18837) of the *m* × *n* differences.[^o7]

:::

## Estimating the population median of a symmetric population {#Estimating_the_population_median_of_a_symmetric_population}

In the general case the Hodges-Lehmann statistic estimates the population's [pseudomedian](wikipedia://en/9679418),[^o9] a [location parameter](wikipedia://en/17516) that is closely related to the [median](wikipedia://en/18837). The difference between the median and pseudo-median is relatively small, and so this distinction is neglected in elementary discussions. Like the [spatial median](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatial_median),[^o10] the pseudo–median is well defined for all distributions of random variables having dimension two or greater; for one-dimensional distributions, there exists some pseudo–median, which need not be unique, however. Like the median, the pseudo–median is defined for even heavy–tailed distributions that lack any (finite) [mean](wikipedia://en/9653).[^o11]

For a population that is symmetric, the Hodges–Lehmann statistic also estimates the population's median. It is a robust statistic that has a [breakdown point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakdown_point) of 0.29, which means that the statistic remains bounded even if nearly 30 percent of the data have been contaminated. This robustness is an important advantage over the sample mean, which has a zero breakdown point, being proportional to any single observation and so liable to being misled by even one [outlier](wikipedia://en/160951). The [sample median](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_median) is even more robust, having a breakdown point of 0.50.[^o12] The Hodges–Lehmann estimator is much better than the sample mean when estimating mixtures of normal distributions, also.[^o13]

For symmetric distributions, the Hodges–Lehmann statistic sometimes has greater [efficiency](wikipedia://en/27250866) at estimating the center of symmetry (population median) than does the sample median. For the normal distribution, the Hodges-Lehmann statistic is nearly as efficient as the sample mean. For the [Cauchy distribution](wikipedia://en/7003) (Student t-distribution with one degree of freedom), the Hodges-Lehmann is infinitely more efficient than the sample mean, which is not a consistent estimator of the median,[^o12] but it is not more efficient than the median in that instance.

The one-sample Hodges–Lehmann statistic need not estimate any population mean, which for many distributions does not exist. The two-sample Hodges–Lehmann estimator need not estimate the difference of two means or the difference of two (pseudo-)medians; rather, it estimates the median of the distribution of the difference between pairs of random–variables drawn respectively from the two populations.[^o7]

## In general statistics {#In_general_statistics}

The Hodges–Lehmann *univariate* statistics have several generalizations in [*multivariate* statistics](wikipedia://en/19384):[^o15]

- Multivariate ranks and signs[^o16]
- Spatial sign tests and spatial medians[^o10]
- Spatial signed-rank tests[^o17]
- Comparisons of tests and estimates[^o18]
- Several-sample location problems[^o19]
