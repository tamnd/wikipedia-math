# Prolate spheroidal coordinates

*Image: Prolate spheroidal coordinates.png.* The three [coordinate surfaces](wikipedia://en/81931#Coordinate_surface) of prolate spheroidal coordinates. The red prolate spheroid (stretched sphere) corresponds to *μ* = 1, and the blue two-sheet [hyperboloid](wikipedia://en/145844) corresponds to *ν* = 45°. The yellow half-plane corresponds to *φ* = −60°, which is measured relative to the *x*-axis (highlighted in green). The black sphere represents the intersection point of the three surfaces, which has [Cartesian coordinates](wikipedia://en/7706) of roughly (0.831, −1.439, 2.182).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Prolate spheroidal coordinates"}

**Prolate spheroidal coordinates** are a three-dimensional [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/5006088) [coordinate system](wikipedia://en/81931) that results from rotating the two-dimensional [elliptic coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_coordinates) about the focal axis of the ellipse, i.e., the symmetry axis on which the foci are located. Rotation about the other axis produces [oblate spheroidal coordinates](wikipedia://en/5007562). Prolate spheroidal coordinates can also be considered as a [limiting case](wikipedia://en/40400729) of [ellipsoidal coordinates](wikipedia://en/5007598) in which the two smallest [principal axes](wikipedia://en/145381) are equal in length.

:::

Prolate spheroidal coordinates can be used to solve various [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564) in which the boundary conditions match its symmetry and shape, such as solving for a field produced by two centers, which are taken as the foci on the *z*-axis. One example is solving for the [wavefunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavefunction) of an [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) moving in the [electromagnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field) of two positively charged [nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus), as in the [hydrogen molecular ion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_molecular_ion), H<sub>2</sub><sup>\+</sup>. Another example is solving for the [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) generated by two small [electrode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrode) tips. Other limiting cases include areas generated by a line segment (*μ* = 0) or a line with a missing segment (ν=0). The electronic structure of general diatomic molecules with many electrons can also be solved to excellent precision in the prolate spheroidal coordinate system.[^o2]

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: ProlateSpheroidCoord.png.* Prolate spheroidal coordinates *μ* and *ν* for *a* = 1. The lines of equal values of μ and ν are shown on the *xz*-plane, i.e. for *φ* = 0. The surfaces of constant *μ* and *ν* are obtained by rotation about the *z*-axis, so that the diagram is valid for any plane containing the *z*-axis: i.e. for any *φ*.

The most common definition of prolate spheroidal coordinates $(\mu, \nu, \varphi)$ is

$$x = a \sinh \mu \sin \nu \cos \varphi$$

$$y = a \sinh \mu \sin \nu \sin \varphi$$

$$z = a \cosh \mu \cos \nu$$

where $\mu$ is a nonnegative real number and $\nu \in [0, \pi]$. The azimuthal angle $\varphi$ belongs to the interval $[0, 2\pi]$.

The trigonometric identity

$$\frac{z^2}{a^2 \cosh^2 \mu} + \frac{x^2 + y^2}{a^2 \sinh^2 \mu} = \cos^2 \nu + \sin^2 \nu = 1$$

shows that surfaces of constant $\mu$ form [prolate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prolate) [spheroids](wikipedia://en/82365), since they are [ellipses](wikipedia://en/9277) rotated about the axis joining their foci. Similarly, the hyperbolic trigonometric identity

$$\frac{z^2}{a^2 \cos^2 \nu} - \frac{x^2 + y^2}{a^2 \sin^2 \nu} = \cosh^2 \mu - \sinh^2 \mu = 1$$

shows that surfaces of constant $\nu$ form [hyperboloids](wikipedia://en/145844) of revolution.

The distances from the foci located at $(x, y, z) = (0, 0, \pm a)$ are

$$r_\pm = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + (z \mp a)^2} = a(\cosh \mu \mp \cos \nu).$$

:::

## Scale factors {#Scale_factors}

The scale factors for the elliptic coordinates $(\mu, \nu)$ are equal

$$h_\mu = h_\nu = a\sqrt{\sinh^2\mu + \sin^2\nu}$$

whereas the azimuthal scale factor is

$$h_\varphi = a \sinh\mu \sin\nu,$$

resulting in a metric of

$$\begin{aligned} ds^2 &= h_\mu^2 d\mu^2 + h_\nu^2 d\nu^2 + h_\varphi^2 d\varphi^2 \\ &= a^2 \left[ (\sinh^2\mu + \sin^2\nu) d\mu^2 + (\sinh^2\mu + \sin^2\nu) d\nu^2 + (\sinh^2\mu \sin^2\nu) d\varphi^2 \right]. \end{aligned}$$

Consequently, an infinitesimal [volume element](wikipedia://en/1920694) equals

$$dV = a^3 \sinh\mu \sin\nu ( \sinh^2 \mu + \sin^2 \nu) \, d\mu \, d\nu \, d\varphi$$

and the Laplacian can be written

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla^2 \Phi = {} & \frac{1}{a^2 (\sinh^2 \mu + \sin^2 \nu)} \left[ \frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial \mu^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial \nu^2} + \coth \mu \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \mu} + \cot \nu \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \nu} \right] \\[6pt] & {} + \frac{1}{a^2 \sinh^2 \mu \sin^2\nu} \frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial \varphi^2} \end{aligned}$$

Other differential operators such as $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}$ and $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$ can be expressed in the coordinates $(\mu, \nu, \varphi)$ by substituting the scale factors into the general formulae found in [orthogonal coordinates](wikipedia://en/5006088).

## Alternative definition(1) {#Alternative_definition(1)}

*Image: Prolate spheroidal coordinates degenerate.png.* In principle, a definition of prolate spheroidal coordinates could be degenerate. In other words, a single set of coordinates might correspond to two points in [Cartesian coordinates](wikipedia://en/7706); this is illustrated here with two black spheres, one on each sheet of the hyperboloid and located at (*x*, *y*, ±*z*). However, neither of the definitions presented here are degenerate.

An alternative and geometrically intuitive set of prolate spheroidal coordinates $(\sigma, \tau, \phi)$ are sometimes used, where $\sigma = \cosh \mu$ and $\tau = \cos \nu$. Hence, the curves of constant $\sigma$ are prolate spheroids, whereas the curves of constant $\tau$ are hyperboloids of revolution. The coordinate $\tau$ belongs to the interval \[−1, 1\], whereas the $\sigma$ coordinate must be greater than or equal to one.

The coordinates $\sigma$ and $\tau$ have a simple relation to the distances to the foci $F_{1}$ and $F_{2}$. For any point in the plane, the *sum* $d_{1}+d_{2}$ of its distances to the foci equals $2a\sigma$, whereas their *difference* $d_{1}-d_{2}$ equals $2a\tau$. Thus, the distance to $F_{1}$ is $a(\sigma+\tau)$, whereas the distance to $F_{2}$ is $a(\sigma-\tau)$. (Recall that $F_{1}$ and $F_{2}$ are located at $z=-a$ and $z=+a$, respectively.) This gives the following expressions for $\sigma$, $\tau$, and $\varphi$:

$$\sigma = \frac 1 {2a} \left(\sqrt{x^2+y^2+(z+a)^2}+\sqrt{x^2+y^2+(z-a)^2}\right)$$

$$\tau = \frac 1 {2a} \left(\sqrt{x^2+y^2+(z+a)^2}-\sqrt{x^2+y^2+(z-a)^2}\right)$$

$$\varphi = \arctan\left(\frac y x \right)$$

Unlike the analogous [oblate spheroidal coordinates](wikipedia://en/5007562), the prolate spheroid coordinates (σ, τ, φ) are *not* degenerate; in other words, there is a [unique, reversible correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1-to-1_mapping) between them and the [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates)

$$x = a \sqrt{(\sigma^2 - 1) (1 - \tau^2)} \cos \varphi$$

$$y = a \sqrt{(\sigma^2 - 1) (1 - \tau^2)} \sin \varphi$$

$$z = a\ \sigma\ \tau$$

## Alternative scale factors {#Alternative_scale_factors}

The scale factors for the alternative elliptic coordinates $(\sigma, \tau, \varphi)$ are

$$h_{\sigma} = a\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2} - \tau^{2}}{\sigma^{2} - 1}}$$

$$h_{\tau} = a\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2} - \tau^{2}}{1 - \tau^{2}}}$$

while the azimuthal scale factor is now

$$h_\varphi = a \sqrt{\left( \sigma^{2} - 1 \right) \left( 1 - \tau^{2} \right)}$$

Hence, the infinitesimal volume element becomes

$$dV = a^3 (\sigma^2 - \tau^2) \, d\sigma \, d\tau \, d\varphi$$

and the Laplacian equals

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla^2 \Phi = {} & \frac{1}{a^2 (\sigma^2 - \tau^2)} \left\{ \frac{\partial}{\partial \sigma} \left[ \left( \sigma^2 - 1 \right) \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \sigma} \right] + \frac{\partial}{\partial \tau} \left[ (1 - \tau^2) \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \tau} \right] \right\} \\ & {} + \frac{1}{a^2 (\sigma^2 - 1) (1 - \tau^2)} \frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial \varphi^2} \end{aligned}$$

Other differential operators such as $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}$ and $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$ can be expressed in the coordinates $(\sigma, \tau)$ by substituting the scale factors into the general formulae found in [orthogonal coordinates](wikipedia://en/5006088).

As is the case with [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates), [Laplace's equation](wikipedia://en/36941) may be solved by the method of [separation of variables](wikipedia://en/387750) to yield solutions in the form of **[prolate spheroidal harmonics](wikipedia://en/13305328)**, which are convenient to use when boundary conditions are defined on a surface with a constant prolate spheroidal coordinate (See Smythe, 1968).

## Alternative definition(2) {#Alternative_definition(2)}

Prolate spheroidal coordinates (η, θ, ϕ; d)[^o9] are defined in terms of Cartesian coordinates (x,y,z) by:

$$\begin{aligned} x &= \sqrt{\eta^2 - d^2}\,\sin\theta\cos\phi,\\ y &= \sqrt{\eta^2 - d^2}\,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\\ z &= \eta\,\cos\theta, \end{aligned}$$

where

$$0\le\theta\le\pi,\quad 0\le\phi<2\pi,$$

and 𝑑>0 denotes the focal distance (half the separation between the foci of a cross-sectional ellipse). Note that if d=0, the cross-section is a circle, and coordinates become those for a sphere. The normal to the surface at each point is η, which has its length equal to the semi-major axis of a cross-sectional ellipse.
