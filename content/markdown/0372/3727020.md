# Quasitriangular Hopf algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quasitriangular Hopf algebra"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **quasitriangular Hopf algebra** is a particular kind of [Hopf algebra](wikipedia://en/325714) $H$, equipped with a special element $R\in H \otimes H$, called the [R-matrix](wikipedia://en/8498996). The R-matrix naturally provides the structure of a [braiding](wikipedia://en/1470466) on the [rigid category](wikipedia://en/25383437) of [representations](wikipedia://en/854978) of $H$, which makes quasitriangular Hopf algebras useful in [knot theory](wikipedia://en/153008).

:::

The most important examples of quasitriangular Hopf algebras are [quantum groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_groups).[^o2]

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A [Hopf algebra](wikipedia://en/325714) *H* is **quasitriangular**[^o5] if there exists an [invertible](wikipedia://en/45199) element $R\in H \otimes H$ such that

- $R \ \Delta(x)R^{-1} = (T \circ \Delta)(x)$ for all $x \in H$, where $\Delta$ is the [coproduct](wikipedia://en/310889) on *H*, and the linear map $T : H \otimes H \to H \otimes H$ is given by $T(x \otimes y) = y \otimes x$,
- $$(\Delta \otimes 1)(R) = R_{13} \ R_{23},$$
- $$(1 \otimes \Delta)(R) = R_{13} \ R_{12},$$

where $R_{12} = \phi_{12}(R)$, $R_{13} = \phi_{13}(R)$, and $R_{23} = \phi_{23}(R)$, where $\phi_{12} : H \otimes H \to H \otimes H \otimes H$, $\phi_{13} : H \otimes H \to H \otimes H \otimes H$, and $\phi_{23} : H \otimes H \to H \otimes H \otimes H$, are algebra [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) determined by

$$\phi_{12}(a \otimes b) = a \otimes b \otimes 1,$$

$$\phi_{13}(a \otimes b) = a \otimes 1 \otimes b,$$

$$\phi_{23}(a \otimes b) = 1 \otimes a \otimes b.$$

*R* is called the [R-matrix](wikipedia://en/8498996).

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

As a consequence of the properties of quasitriangularity, the R-matrix, *R*, is a solution of the [Yang–Baxter equation](wikipedia://en/3720810) (and so a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) *V* of *H* can be used to determine quasi-invariants of [braids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braid_theory), [knots](wikipedia://en/366808) and [links](wikipedia://en/1161784)). Also as a consequence of the properties of quasitriangularity, $(\epsilon \otimes 1) R = (1 \otimes \epsilon) R = 1 \in H$; moreover $R^{-1} = (S \otimes 1)(R)$, $R = (1 \otimes S)(R^{-1})$, and $(S \otimes S)(R) = R$. One may further show that the antipode *S* must be a linear isomorphism, and thus *S<sup>2</sup>* is an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160). In fact, *S<sup>2</sup>* is given by conjugating by an invertible element: $S^2(x)= u x u^{-1}$ where $u := m ((S \otimes 1) \circ T)R$ (cf. [Ribbon Hopf algebras](wikipedia://en/6801871)).

It is possible to construct a quasitriangular Hopf algebra from a Hopf algebra and its dual, using the [Drinfeld](wikipedia://en/1053747) quantum double construction.

If the Hopf algebra *H* is quasitriangular, then the [category of modules](wikipedia://en/11231812) over *H* is braided with braiding

$$c_{U,V}(u\otimes v) = T \left( R \cdot (u \otimes v )\right) = T \left( R_1 u \otimes R_2 v\right).$$

:::

::: {#o9 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Drinfeld Twist"}

## Twisting {#Twisting}

The property of being a [quasi-triangular Hopf algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-triangular_Hopf_algebra) is preserved by [twisting](wikipedia://en/4899414#Twisting) via an invertible element $F = \sum_i f^i \otimes f_i \in \mathcal{A \otimes A}$ such that $(\varepsilon \otimes id )F = (id \otimes \varepsilon)F = 1$ and satisfying the cocycle condition

$$(F \otimes 1) \cdot (\Delta \otimes id)( F) = (1 \otimes F) \cdot (id \otimes \Delta)( F)$$

Furthermore, $u = \sum_i f^i S(f_i)$ is invertible and the twisted antipode is given by $S'(a) = u S(a)u^{-1}$, with the twisted comultiplication, R-matrix and co-unit change according to those defined for the [quasi-triangular quasi-Hopf algebra](wikipedia://en/4965178). Such a twist is known as an admissible (or Drinfeld) twist.

:::
