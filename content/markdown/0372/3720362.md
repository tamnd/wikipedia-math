# Angular distance

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Angular distance"}

**Angular distance** or **angular separation** is the measure of the [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) between the [orientation](wikipedia://en/2544098) of two [straight lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_line), [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28geometry%29), or [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometry%29) – typically in [three-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/3054853) – or the [central angle](wikipedia://en/462705) [subtended](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtended) by the [radii](wikipedia://en/1780815) through two points on a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859). When the rays are [lines of sight](wikipedia://en/70797458) from an observer to two points in space, it is known as the **apparent distance** or **apparent separation**.

:::

Angular distance appears in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) (in particular [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) and [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261)) and all [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science) (e.g., [kinematics](wikipedia://en/65914), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), and [geophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysics)). In the [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586) of rotating objects, it appears alongside [angular velocity](wikipedia://en/65927), [angular acceleration](wikipedia://en/65929), [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839), [moment of inertia](wikipedia://en/157700) and [torque](wikipedia://en/30400).

## Measurement {#Measurement}

As an angle, the angular separation is measured using [angular units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_unit) such as [degrees](wikipedia://en/1195294) or [radians](wikipedia://en/26003), using instruments such as [goniometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goniometer) or optical instruments specially designed to point in well-defined directions and record the corresponding angles (such as [telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope)).

## Celestial coordinates {#Celestial_coordinates}

*Image: Fig angle separation.png.* Angular separation $\theta$ between points A and B as seen from O

To derive the equation that describes the angular separation of two points located on the surface of a sphere as seen from the center of the sphere, we use the example of two [astronomical objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_objects) $A$ and $B$ observed from the Earth. The objects $A$ and $B$ are defined by their [celestial coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_coordinate_system), namely their [right ascensions (RA)](wikipedia://en/26073), $(\alpha_A, \alpha_B)\in [0, 2\pi]$; and [declinations (dec)](wikipedia://en/8612), $(\delta_A, \delta_B) \in [-\pi/2, \pi/2]$. Let $O$ indicate the observer on Earth, assumed to be located at the center of the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239). The [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) of the vectors $\mathbf{OA}$ and $\mathbf{OB}$ is equal to:

$$\mathbf{OA}\cdot\mathbf{OB}= R^2 \cos\theta$$

which is equivalent to:

$$\mathbf{n_A} \cdot \mathbf{n_B} = \cos\theta$$

In the $(x,y,z)$ frame, the two unitary vectors are decomposed into:

$$\mathbf{n_A} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\delta_A \cos\alpha_A\\ \cos\delta_A \sin\alpha_A\\ \sin\delta_A \end{pmatrix} \mathrm{\qquad and \qquad } \mathbf{n_B} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\delta_B \cos\alpha_B\\ \cos\delta_B \sin\alpha_B\\ \sin\delta_B \end{pmatrix} .$$

Therefore,

$$\mathbf{n_A} \cdot \mathbf{n_B} = \cos\delta_A \cos\alpha_A \cos\delta_B \cos\alpha_B + \cos\delta_A \sin\alpha_A \cos\delta_B \sin\alpha_B + \sin\delta_A \sin\delta_B \equiv \cos\theta$$

then:

$$\theta = \cos^{-1}\left[\sin\delta_A \sin\delta_B + \cos\delta_A \cos\delta_B \cos(\alpha_A - \alpha_B)\right]$$

### Small angular distance approximation {#Small_angular_distance_approximation}

The above expression is valid for any position of A and B on the sphere. In astronomy, it often happens that the considered objects are really close in the sky: stars in a telescope field of view, binary stars, the satellites of the giant planets of the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System), etc. In the case where $\theta\ll 1$ radian, implying $\alpha_A-\alpha_B\ll 1$ and $\delta_A-\delta_B\ll 1$, we can develop the above expression and simplify it. In the [small-angle approximation](wikipedia://en/2009207), at second order, the above expression becomes:

$$\cos\theta \approx 1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2} \approx \sin\delta_A \sin\delta_B + \cos\delta_A \cos\delta_B \left[1 - \frac{(\alpha_A - \alpha_B)^2}{2}\right]$$

meaning

$$1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2} \approx \cos(\delta_A-\delta_B) - \cos\delta_A\cos\delta_B \frac{(\alpha_A - \alpha_B)^2}{2}$$

hence

$$1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2} \approx 1 - \frac{(\delta_A-\delta_B)^2}{2} - \cos\delta_A\cos\delta_B \frac{(\alpha_A - \alpha_B)^2}{2}.$$

Given that $\delta_A-\delta_B\ll 1$ and $\alpha_A-\alpha_B\ll 1$, at a second-order development it turns that $\cos\delta_A\cos\delta_B \frac{(\alpha_A - \alpha_B)^2}{2} \approx \cos^2\delta_A \frac{(\alpha_A - \alpha_B)^2}{2}$, so that

$$\theta \approx \sqrt{\left[(\alpha_A - \alpha_B)\cos\delta_A\right]^2 + (\delta_A-\delta_B)^2}$$

### Small angular distance: planar approximation {#Small_angular_distance:_planar_approximation}

*Image: Fig angle separation planar.png.* Planar approximation of angular distance on sky

If we consider a detector imaging a small sky field (dimension much less than one radian) with the $y$-axis pointing up, parallel to the meridian of right ascension $\alpha$, and the $x$-axis along the parallel of declination $\delta$, the angular separation can be written as:

$$\theta \approx \sqrt{\delta x^2 + \delta y^2}$$

where $\delta x = (\alpha_A - \alpha_B)\cos\delta_A$ and $\delta y=\delta_A-\delta_B$.

Note that the $y$-axis is equal to the declination, whereas the $x$-axis is the right ascension modulated by $\cos\delta_A$ because the section of a sphere of radius $R$ at declination (latitude) $\delta$ is $R' = R \cos\delta_A$ (see Figure).
