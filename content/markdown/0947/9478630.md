# Integral element

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Integral element"}

In [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990), an element *b* of a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) *B* is said to be **integral over** a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) *A* of *B* if *b* is a [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) of some [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730) over *A*.[^o2]

:::

If *A*, *B* are [fields](wikipedia://en/10603), then the notions of "integral over" and of an "integral extension" are precisely "[algebraic](wikipedia://en/186291) over" and "[algebraic extensions](wikipedia://en/2125)" in [field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_theory_%28mathematics%29) (since the root of any [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) is the root of a monic polynomial).

The case of greatest interest in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) is that of [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) integral over **Z** (e.g., $\sqrt{2}$ or $1+i$); in this context, the integral elements are usually called [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981). The algebraic integers in a finite [extension field](wikipedia://en/11634) *k* of the [rationals](wikipedia://en/19727024) **Q** form a subring of *k*, called the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of *k*, a central object of study in [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705).

In this article, the term *[ring](wikipedia://en/48404)* will be understood to mean *commutative ring* with a multiplicative identity.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $B$ be a ring and let $A \subset B$ be a subring of $B.$ An element $b$ of $B$ is said to be **integral over** $A$ if for some $n \geq 1,$ there exists $a_0,\ a_1, \ \dots,\ a_{n-1}$ in $A$ such that

$$b^n + a_{n-1} b^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1 b + a_0 = 0.$$

The set of elements of $B$ that are integral over $A$ is called the **integral closure** of $A$ in $B.$ The integral closure of any subring $A$ in $B$ is, itself, a subring of $B$ and contains $A.$ If every element of $B$ is integral over $A,$ then we say that $B$ is **integral over** $A$, or equivalently $B$ is an **integral extension** of $A.$

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

### Integral closure in algebraic number theory {#Integral_closure_in_algebraic_number_theory}

There are many examples of integral closure which can be found in algebraic number theory since it is fundamental for defining the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) for an [algebraic field extension](wikipedia://en/2125) $K/\mathbb{Q}$ (or $L/\mathbb{Q}_p$).

#### Integral closure of integers in rationals {#Integral_closure_of_integers_in_rationals}

[Integers](wikipedia://en/14563) are the only elements of **Q** that are integral over **Z**. In other words, **Z** is the integral closure of **Z** in **Q**.

#### Quadratic extensions {#Quadratic_extensions}

The [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628) are the complex numbers of the form $a + b \sqrt{-1},\, a, b \in \mathbf{Z}$, and are integral over **Z**. $\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-1}]$ is then the integral closure of **Z** in $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-1})$. Typically this ring is denoted $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}[i]}$.

The integral closure of **Z** in $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{5})$ is the ring

$$\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{5}]} = \mathbb{Z}\!\left[ \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2} \right]$$

This example and the previous one are examples of [quadratic integers](wikipedia://en/21834785). The integral closure of a quadratic extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ can be found by constructing the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106) of an arbitrary element $a + b \sqrt{d}$ and finding number-theoretic criterion for the polynomial to have integral coefficients. This analysis can be found in the [quadratic extensions article](wikipedia://en/21834785#Determining_the_ring_of_integers).

#### Roots of unity {#Roots_of_unity}

Let ζ be a [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950). Then the integral closure of **Z** in the [cyclotomic field](wikipedia://en/30872462) **Q**(ζ) is **Z**\[ζ\].[^o11] This can be found by using the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106) and using [Eisenstein's criterion](wikipedia://en/385989).

#### Ring of algebraic integers {#Ring_of_algebraic_integers}

The integral closure of **Z** in the field of complex numbers **C**, or the algebraic closure $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ is called the *ring of [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981)*.

#### Other {#Other}

The [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity), [nilpotent elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilpotent_element) and [idempotent elements](wikipedia://en/361940) in any ring are integral over **Z**.

### Integral closure in algebraic geometry {#Integral_closure_in_algebraic_geometry}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), integral closure is closely related with [normalization](wikipedia://en/3003553) and [normal schemes](wikipedia://en/2629423). It is the first step in [resolution of singularities](wikipedia://en/9914115) since it gives a process for resolving singularities of [codimension](wikipedia://en/525149) 1.

- For example, the integral closure of $\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]/(xy)$ is the ring $\mathbb{C}[x,z] \times \mathbb{C}[y,z]$ since geometrically, the first ring corresponds to the $xz$-plane unioned with the $yz$-plane. They have a codimension 1 singularity along the $z$-axis where they intersect.
- Let a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) *G* [act](wikipedia://en/12781) on a ring *A*. Then *A* is integral over *A*<sup>*G*</sup>, the set of elements fixed by *G*; see [Ring of invariants](wikipedia://en/47933712).
- Let *R* be a ring and *u* a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) in a ring containing *R*. Then[^o15]

<!-- -->

1. *u*<sup>−1</sup> is integral over *R* [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) *u*<sup>−1</sup> ∈ *R*\[*u*\].
1. $R[u] \cap R[u^{-1}]$ is integral over *R*.
1. The integral closure of the [homogeneous coordinate ring](wikipedia://en/23833909) of a normal [projective variety](wikipedia://en/320469) *X* is the [ring of sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_sections)[^o16]

$$\bigoplus_{n \ge 0} \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X(n)).$$

### Integrality in algebra {#Integrality_in_algebra}

- If $\overline{k}$ is an [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129) of a field *k*, then $\overline{k}[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is integral over $k[x_1, \dots, x_n].$
- The integral closure of **C**\[\[*x*\]\] in a finite extension of **C**((*x*)) is of the form $\mathbf{C}[[x^{1/n}]]$ (cf. [Puiseux series](wikipedia://en/2604429))

## Equivalent definitions {#Equivalent_definitions}

Let *B* be a ring, and let *A* be a subring of *B*. Given an element *b* in *B*, the following conditions are equivalent:

(i) *b* is integral over *A*;

(ii) the subring *A*\[*b*\] of *B* generated by *A* and *b* is a [finitely generated *A*-module](wikipedia://en/326430);

(iii) there exists a subring *C* of *B* containing *A*\[*b*\] and which is a finitely generated *A*-module;

(iv) there exists a [faithful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faithful_module) *A*\[*b*\]-module *M* such that *M* is finitely generated as an *A*-module.

The usual [proof](wikipedia://en/82285) of this uses the following variant of the [Cayley–Hamilton theorem](wikipedia://en/173547) on [determinants](wikipedia://en/8468):

**Theorem** Let *u* be an [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569) of an *A*-module *M* generated by *n* elements and *I* an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) of *A* such that $u(M) \subset IM$. Then there is a relation:

$$u^n + a_1 u^{n-1} + \cdots + a_{n-1} u + a_n = 0, \, a_i \in I^i.$$

This theorem (with *I* = *A* and *u* multiplication by *b*) gives (iv) ⇒ (i) and the rest is easy. Coincidentally, [Nakayama's lemma](wikipedia://en/1835001) is also an immediate consequence of this theorem.

:::

## Elementary properties {#Elementary_properties}

### Integral closure forms a ring {#Integral_closure_forms_a_ring}

It follows from the above four equivalent statements that the set of elements of $B$ that are integral over $A$ forms a subring of *$B$* containing $A$. (Proof: If *x*, *y* are elements of *$B$* that are integral over $A$, then $x + y, xy, -x$ are integral over $A$ since they stabilize $A[x]A[y]$, which is a finitely generated module over $A$ and is annihilated only by zero.)[^o23] This ring is called the **integral closure** of $A$ in $B$.

### Transitivity of integrality {#Transitivity_of_integrality}

Another consequence of the above equivalence is that "integrality" is [transitive](wikipedia://en/200463), in the following sense. Let $C$ be a ring containing $B$ and $c \in C$. If $c$ is integral over *$B$* and *$B$* integral over $A$, then $c$ is integral over $A$. In particular, if $C$ is itself integral over *$B$* and *$B$* is integral over $A$, then $C$ is also integral over $A$.

### Integral closed in fraction field {#Integral_closed_in_fraction_field}

If $A$ happens to be the integral closure of $A$ in *$B$*, then *A* is said to be **integrally closed** in *$B$*. If $B$ is the [total ring of fractions](wikipedia://en/4276418) of $A$, (e.g., the [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) when $A$ is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462)), then one sometimes drops the qualification "in $B$ and simply says "integral closure of $A$" and "$A$ is [integrally closed](wikipedia://en/21420209)."[^o26] For example, the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ is integrally closed in the field $K$.

#### Transitivity of integral closure with integrally closed domains {#Transitivity_of_integral_closure_with_integrally_closed_domains}

Let *A* be an integral domain with the field of fractions *K* and *A'* the integral closure of *A* in an [algebraic field extension](wikipedia://en/2125) *L* of *K*. Then the field of fractions of *A'* is *L*. In particular, *A'* is an [integrally closed domain](wikipedia://en/21420209).

##### Transitivity in algebraic number theory {#Transitivity_in_algebraic_number_theory}

This situation is applicable in algebraic number theory when relating the ring of integers and a field extension. In particular, given a field extension $L/K$ the integral closure of $\mathcal{O}_K$ in $L$ is the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_L$.

#### Remarks {#Remarks}

Note that transitivity of integrality above implies that if $B$ is integral over $A$, then $B$ is a union (equivalently an [inductive limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_limit)) of subrings that are finitely generated $A$-modules.

If $A$ is [noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129), transitivity of integrality can be weakened to the statement:

There exists a finitely generated $A$-submodule of $B$ that contains $A[b]$.

### Relation with finiteness conditions {#Relation_with_finiteness_conditions}

Finally, the assumption that $A$ be a subring of $B$ can be modified a bit. If $f:A \to B$ is a [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411), then one says $f$ is **integral** if $B$ is integral over $f(A)$. In the same way one says $f$ is **finite** ($B$ finitely generated $A$-module) or of **finite type** ($B$ finitely generated $A$-[algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_over_a_ring)). In this viewpoint, one has that

$f$ is finite if and only if $f$ is integral and of finite type.

Or more explicitly,

$B$ is a finitely generated $A$-module if and only if $B$ is generated as an $A$-algebra by a finite number of elements integral over $A$.

## Integral extensions {#Integral_extensions}

::: {#o33 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Cohen-Seidenberg theorems"}

### Cohen-Seidenberg theorems {#Cohen-Seidenberg_theorems}

An integral extension *A* ⊆ *B* has the [going-up property](wikipedia://en/2083445), the [lying over](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lying_over) property, and the [incomparability](wikipedia://en/2083445#Lying_over_and_incomparability) property ([Cohen–Seidenberg theorems](wikipedia://en/2083445)). Explicitly, given a chain of [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) $\mathfrak{p}_1 \subset \cdots \subset \mathfrak{p}_n$ in *A* there exists a $\mathfrak{p}'_1 \subset \cdots \subset \mathfrak{p}'_n$ in *B* with $\mathfrak{p}_i = \mathfrak{p}'_i \cap A$ (going-up and lying over) and two distinct prime ideals with inclusion relation cannot contract to the same prime ideal (incomparability). In particular, the [Krull dimensions](wikipedia://en/56079) of *A* and *B* are the same. Furthermore, if *A* is an integrally closed domain, then the going-down holds (see below).

In general, the going-up implies the lying-over.[^o34] Thus, in the below, we simply say the "going-up" to mean "going-up" and "lying-over".

When *A*, *B* are domains such that *B* is integral over *A*, *A* is a field if and only if *B* is a field. As a [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865), one has: given a prime ideal $\mathfrak{q}$ of *B*, $\mathfrak{q}$ is a [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164) of *B* if and only if $\mathfrak{q} \cap A$ is a maximal ideal of *A*. Another corollary: if *L*/*K* is an algebraic extension, then any subring of *L* containing *K* is a field.

::: {#o36 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

#### Applications {#Applications}

Let *B* be a ring that is integral over a subring *A* and *k* an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018). If $f: A \to k$ is a homomorphism, then *f* extends to a homomorphism *B* → *k*.[^o37] This follows from the going-up.

:::

#### Geometric interpretation of going-up {#Geometric_interpretation_of_going-up}

Let $f: A \to B$ be an integral extension of rings. Then the induced map

$$\begin{cases} f^\#: \operatorname{Spec} B \to \operatorname{Spec} A \\ p \mapsto f^{-1}(p)\end{cases}$$

is a [closed map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_map); in fact, $f^\#(V(I)) = V(f^{-1}(I))$ for any ideal *I* and $f^\#$ is [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective_map) if *f* is [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective_map). This is a geometric interpretation of the going-up.

#### Geometric interpretation of integral extensions {#Geometric_interpretation_of_integral_extensions}

Let *B* be a ring and *A* a subring that is a noetherian integrally closed domain (i.e., $\operatorname{Spec} A$ is a [normal scheme](wikipedia://en/2629423)). If *B* is integral over *A*, then $\operatorname{Spec} B \to \operatorname{Spec} A$ is [submersive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submersion_%28algebra%29); i.e., the [topology](wikipedia://en/30450) of $\operatorname{Spec} A$ is the [quotient topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_topology).[^o40] The proof uses the notion of [constructible sets](wikipedia://en/23234121). (See also: [Torsor (algebraic geometry)](wikipedia://en/41910974).)

:::

### Integrality, base-change, universally-closed, and geometry {#Integrality,_base-change,_universally-closed,_and_geometry}

If $B$ is integral over $A$, then $B \otimes_A R$ is integral over *R* for any *A*-algebra *R*.[^o42] In particular, $\operatorname{Spec} (B \otimes_A R) \to \operatorname{Spec} R$ is closed; i.e., the integral extension induces a "**universally closed**" map. This leads to a geometric characterization of integral extension. Namely, let *B* be a ring with only finitely many [minimal prime ideals](wikipedia://en/16143832) (e.g., integral domain or noetherian ring). Then *B* is integral over a (subring) *A* if and only if $\operatorname{Spec} (B \otimes_A R) \to \operatorname{Spec} R$ is closed for any *A*-algebra *R*.[^o43] In particular, every **proper map** is universally closed.[^o44]

### Galois actions on integral extensions of integrally closed domains {#Galois_actions_on_integral_extensions_of_integrally_closed_domains}

**Proposition.** Let *A* be an integrally closed domain with the field of fractions *K*, *L* a finite [normal extension](wikipedia://en/929925) of *K*, *B* the integral closure of *A* in *L*. Then the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G = \operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ acts [transitively](wikipedia://en/12781#Remarkable_properties_of_actions) on each fiber of $\operatorname{Spec} B \to \operatorname{Spec} A$.

::: {#o46 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof.** Suppose $\mathfrak{p}_2 \ne \sigma(\mathfrak{p}_1)$ for any $\sigma$ in *G*. Then, by [prime avoidance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_avoidance), there is an element *x* in $\mathfrak{p}_2$ such that $\sigma(x) \not\in \mathfrak{p}_1$ for any $\sigma$. *G* fixes the element $y = \prod\nolimits_{\sigma} \sigma(x)$ and thus *y* is [purely inseparable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purely_inseparable) over *K*. Then some power $y^e$ belongs to *K*; since *A* is integrally closed we have: $y^e \in A.$ Thus, we found $y^e$ is in $\mathfrak{p}_2 \cap A$ but not in $\mathfrak{p}_1 \cap A$; i.e., $\mathfrak{p}_1 \cap A \ne \mathfrak{p}_2 \cap A$.

:::

#### Application to algebraic number theory {#Application_to_algebraic_number_theory}

The Galois group $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ then acts on all of the prime ideals $\mathfrak{q}_1,\ldots, \mathfrak{q}_k \in \text{Spec}(\mathcal{O}_L)$ lying over a fixed prime ideal $\mathfrak{p} \in \text{Spec}(\mathcal{O}_K)$.[^o48] That is, if

$$\mathfrak{p} = \mathfrak{q}_1^{e_1}\cdots\mathfrak{q}_k^{e_k} \subset \mathcal{O}_L$$

then there is a Galois action on the set $S_\mathfrak{p} = \{\mathfrak{q}_1,\ldots,\mathfrak{q}_k \}$. This is called the [Splitting of prime ideals in Galois extensions](wikipedia://en/1190816).

#### Remarks {#Remarks}

The same idea in the proof shows that if $L/K$ is a purely inseparable extension (need not be normal), then $\operatorname{Spec} B \to \operatorname{Spec} A$ is [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective).

Let *A*, *K*, etc. as before but assume *L* is only a finite field extension of *K*. Then

(i) $\operatorname{Spec} B \to \operatorname{Spec} A$ has finite fibers.

(ii) the going-down holds between *A* and *B*: given $\mathfrak{p}_1 \subset \cdots \subset \mathfrak{p}_n = \mathfrak{p}'_n \cap A$, there exists $\mathfrak{p}'_1 \subset \cdots \subset \mathfrak{p}'_n$ that contracts to it.

Indeed, in both statements, by enlarging *L*, we can assume *L* is a normal extension. Then (i) is immediate. As for (ii), by the going-up, we can find a chain $\mathfrak{p}''_i$ that contracts to $\mathfrak{p}'_i$. By transitivity, there is $\sigma \in G$ such that $\sigma(\mathfrak{p}''_n) = \mathfrak{p}'_n$ and then $\mathfrak{p}'_i = \sigma(\mathfrak{p}''_i)$ are the desired chain.

## Integral closure {#Integral_closure}

Let *A* ⊂ *B* be rings and *A'* the integral closure of *A* in *B*. (See above for the definition.)

Integral closures behave nicely under various constructions. Specifically, for a [multiplicatively closed subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicatively_closed_subset) *S* of *A*, the [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) *S*<sup>−1</sup>*A'* is the integral closure of *S*<sup>−1</sup>*A* in *S*<sup>−1</sup>*B*, and $A'[t]$ is the integral closure of $A[t]$ in $B[t]$.[^o51] If $A_i$ are subrings of rings $B_i, 1 \le i \le n$, then the integral closure of $\prod A_i$ in $\prod B_i$ is $\prod {A_i}'$ where ${A_i}'$ are the integral closures of $A_i$ in $B_i$.[^o52]

The integral closure of a [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) *A* in, say, *B*, need not be local. (If this is the case, the ring is called [unibranch](wikipedia://en/26130615).) This is the case for example when *A* is [Henselian](wikipedia://en/14623383) and *B* is a field extension of the field of fractions of *A*.

If *A* is a subring of a field *K*, then the integral closure of *A* in *K* is the intersection of all [valuation rings](wikipedia://en/966856) of *K* containing *A*.

Let *A* be an $\mathbb{N}$-graded subring of an $\mathbb{N}$-[graded ring](wikipedia://en/103094) *B*. Then the integral closure of *A* in *B* is an $\mathbb{N}$-graded subring of *B*.[^o53]

There is also a concept of the [integral closure of an ideal](wikipedia://en/39944913). The integral closure of an ideal $I \subset R$, usually denoted by $\overline I$, is the set of all elements $r \in R$ such that there exists a monic polynomial

$$x^n + a_{1} x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_{n-1} x^1 + a_n$$

with $a_i \in I^i$ with $r$ as a root.[^o54][^o55] The [radical of an ideal](wikipedia://en/252009) is integrally closed.[^o56][^o57]

For noetherian rings, there are alternate definitions as well.

- $r \in \overline I$ if there exists a $c \in R$ not contained in any minimal prime, such that $c r^n \in I^n$ for all $n \ge 1$.
- $r \in \overline I$ if in the normalized blow-up of *I*, the pull back of *r* is contained in the inverse image of *I*. The blow-up of an ideal is an operation of schemes which replaces the given ideal with a principal ideal. The normalization of a scheme is simply the scheme corresponding to the integral closure of all of its rings.

The notion of integral closure of an ideal is used in some proofs of the [going-down theorem](wikipedia://en/2083445).

## Conductor {#Conductor}

Let *B* be a ring and *A* a subring of *B* such that *B* is integral over *A*. Then the [annihilator](wikipedia://en/328684) of the *A*-module *B*/*A* is called the *conductor* of *A* in *B*. Because the notion has origin in [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705), the conductor is denoted by $\mathfrak{f} = \mathfrak{f}(B/A)$. Explicitly, $\mathfrak{f}$ consists of elements *a* in *A* such that $aB \subset A$. (cf. [idealizer](wikipedia://en/24575820) in abstract algebra.) It is the largest [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) of *A* that is also an ideal of *B*.[^o59] If *S* is a multiplicatively closed subset of *A*, then

$$S^{-1}\mathfrak{f}(B/A) = \mathfrak{f}(S^{-1}B/S^{-1}A).$$

If *B* is a subring of the [total ring of fractions](wikipedia://en/4276418) of *A*, then we may identify

$$\mathfrak{f}(B/A)=\operatorname{Hom}_A(B, A).$$

Example: Let *k* be a field and let $A = k[t^2, t^3] \subset B = k[t]$ (i.e., *A* is the [coordinate ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_ring) of the [affine curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_curve) $x^2 = y^3$). *B* is the integral closure of *A* in $k(t)$. The conductor of *A* in *B* is the ideal $(t^2, t^3) A$. More generally, the conductor of $A = k[[t^a, t^b]]$, *a*, *b* relatively prime, is $(t^c, t^{c+1}, \dots) A$ with $c = (a-1)(b-1)$.[^o60]

Suppose *B* is the integral closure of an integral domain *A* in the field of fractions of *A* such that the *A*-module $B/A$ is finitely generated. Then the conductor $\mathfrak{f}$ of *A* is an ideal defining the [support of](wikipedia://en/16073360) $B/A$; thus, *A* coincides with *B* in the complement of $V(\mathfrak{f})$ in $\operatorname{Spec}A$. In particular, the set $\{ \mathfrak{p} \in \operatorname{Spec}A \mid A_\mathfrak{p} \text{ is integrally closed} \}$, the complement of $V(\mathfrak{f})$, is an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358).

## Finiteness of integral closure {#Finiteness_of_integral_closure}

An important but difficult question is on the finiteness of the integral closure of a [finitely generated algebra](wikipedia://en/11985661). There are several known results.

The integral closure of a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132) in a finite extension of the field of fractions is a Dedekind domain; in particular, a noetherian ring. This is a consequence of the [Krull–Akizuki theorem](wikipedia://en/35947857). In general, the integral closure of a noetherian domain of dimension at most 2 is noetherian; Nagata gave an example of dimension 3 noetherian domain whose integral closure is not noetherian.[^o62] A nicer statement is this: the integral closure of a noetherian domain is a [Krull domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krull_domain) ([Mori–Nagata theorem](wikipedia://en/35957110)). Nagata also gave an example of dimension 1 noetherian local domain such that the integral closure is not finite over that domain.

Let *A* be a noetherian integrally closed domain with field of fractions *K*. If *L*/*K* is a finite [separable extension](wikipedia://en/363340), then the integral closure $A'$ of *A* in *L* is a finitely generated *A*-module.[^o63] This is easy and standard (uses the fact that the trace defines a non-degenerate [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477)).

Let *A* be a finitely generated algebra over a field *k* that is an integral domain with field of fractions *K*. If *L* is a finite extension of *K*, then the integral closure $A'$ of *A* in *L* is a finitely generated *A*-module and is also a finitely generated *k*-algebra.[^o64] The result is due to Noether and can be shown using the [Noether normalization lemma](wikipedia://en/3003553) as follows. It is clear that it is enough to show the assertion when *L*/*K* is either separable or purely inseparable. The separable case is noted above, so assume *L*/*K* is purely inseparable. By the normalization lemma, *A* is integral over the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $S = k[x_1, ..., x_d]$. Since *L*/*K* is a finite purely inseparable extension, there is a power *q* of a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) such that every element of *L* is a *q*-th root of an element in *K*. Let $k'$ be a finite extension of *k* containing all *q*-th roots of coefficients of finitely many rational functions that generate *L*. Then we have: $L \subset k'(x_1^{1/q}, ..., x_d^{1/q}).$ The ring on the right is the field of fractions of $k'[x_1^{1/q}, ..., x_d^{1/q}]$, which is the integral closure of *S*; thus, contains $A'$. Hence, $A'$ is finite over *S*; a fortiori, over *A*. The result remains true if we replace *k* by **Z**.

The integral closure of a complete local noetherian domain *A* in a finite extension of the field of fractions of *A* is finite over *A*.[^o65] More precisely, for a local noetherian ring *R*, we have the following chains of implications:[^o66]

(i) *A* complete $\Rightarrow$ *A* is a [Nagata ring](wikipedia://en/10092186)

(ii) *A* is a Nagata domain $\Rightarrow$ *A* [analytically unramified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytically_unramified) $\Rightarrow$ the integral closure of the completion $\widehat{A}$ is finite over $\widehat{A}$ $\Rightarrow$ the integral closure of *A* is finite over A.

::: {#o68 .statement cue=named-heading name="The Grauert–Remmert–de Jong Criterion"}

## The Grauert–Remmert–de Jong Criterion {#The_Grauert–Remmert–de_Jong_Criterion}

The **Grauert–Remmert–de Jong criterion** provides a necessary and sufficient condition for a ring to be normal.

::: {#o70 .statement cue=named-heading name="The Criterion"}

### The Criterion {#The_Criterion}

Let $A$ be a reduced Noetherian domain and $I \subset A$ be an ideal satisfying the following conditions:

1. $I$ is a **radical ideal** (i.e., $\sqrt{I} = I$).
1. The variety $V(I)$ contains the **non-normal locus** of $I$ (i.e., for every prime ideal $P \in \operatorname{Spec}(A)$ where the local ring $A_P$ is not normal, $P \supseteq I$).

Then: the ring $A$ is **normal** if and only if the natural inclusion into the [endomorphism ring](wikipedia://en/59623) of $I$

$$\phi: A \hookrightarrow \operatorname{Hom}_A(I, I)$$

is an isomorphism.

:::

### Algorithmic Application (The de Jong Algorithm) {#Algorithmic_Application_(The_de_Jong_Algorithm)}

If $A$ is not normal, the endomorphism ring $S = \operatorname{Hom}_A(I, I)$ provides a strictly larger integral ring extension $A \subsetneq S$ within the quotient field $Q(A)$.

- **Iteration:** By iteratively constructing a sequence of ring extensions $A = A_0 \subset A_1 \subset A_2 \subset \dots \subset \overline{A}$, the procedure finally leads to the **normalization** $\overline{A}$, provided that the normalization is a finitely generated module over $A$.
- **Generalization of the Zassenhaus Algorithm:** This approach generalizes the **Zassenhaus algorithm** (also known as the Round-Two algorithm) from the specific context of rings of integers in **number fields** to **general Noetherian rings**.

[^o72]

:::

## Noether's normalization lemma {#Noether's_normalization_lemma}

Noether's normalisation lemma is a theorem in [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990). Given a field *K* and a finitely generated *K*-algebra *A*, the theorem says it is possible to find elements *y*<sub>1</sub>, *y*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *y*<sub>*m*</sub> in *A* that are [algebraically independent](wikipedia://en/287808) over *K* such that *A* is finite (and hence integral) over *B* = *K*\[*y*<sub>1</sub>,..., *y*<sub>*m*</sub>\]. Thus the extension *K* ⊂ *A* can be written as a composite *K* ⊂ *B* ⊂ *A* where *K* ⊂ *B* is a purely [transcendental extension](wikipedia://en/287364) and *B* ⊂ *A* is finite.[^o81]

:::

## Integral morphisms {#Integral_morphisms}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a morphism $f:X \to Y$ of [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) is *integral* if it is [affine](wikipedia://en/56371096#Affine_morphism) and if for some (equivalently, every) affine open cover $U_i$ of *Y*, every map $f^{-1}(U_i)\to U_i$ is of the form $\operatorname{Spec}(A)\to\operatorname{Spec}(B)$ where *A* is an integral *B*-algebra. The class of integral morphisms is more general than the class of [finite morphisms](wikipedia://en/2139357) because there are integral extensions that are not finite, such as, in many cases, the algebraic closure of a field over the field.

::: {#o84 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Absolute integral closure"}

## Absolute integral closure {#Absolute_integral_closure}

Let *A* be an integral domain and *L* (some) [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129) of the field of fractions of *A*. Then the integral closure $A^+$ of *A* in *L* is called the **absolute integral closure** of *A*.[^o85] It is unique up to a non-canonical [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828). The [ring of all algebraic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_all_algebraic_integers) is an example (and thus $A^+$ is typically not noetherian).

:::
