# Tesseract

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Tesseract"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **tesseract** or **4-cube** is a four-dimensional [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783), analogous to a two-dimensional [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) and a three-dimensional [cube](wikipedia://en/6285). Just as the perimeter of the square consists of four edges and the surface of the cube consists of six square [faces](wikipedia://en/149926), the [hypersurface](wikipedia://en/525101) of the tesseract consists of eight cubical [cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_%28geometry%29), meeting at [right angles](wikipedia://en/76956). The tesseract is one of the six [convex regular 4-polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_regular_4-polytope).

:::

The tesseract is also called an **8-cell**, **C<sub>8</sub>**, (regular) **octachoron**, or **cubic prism**. It is the four-dimensional **measure polytope**, taken as a unit for hypervolume.[^o3] [Harold Scott MacDonald Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Scott_MacDonald_Coxeter) labels it the $\gamma_{4}$ polytope. The term *hypercube* without a dimension reference is frequently treated as a synonym for this specific [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471).

::: {#o5 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

The construction of a tesseract can be visualized through the analogy of dimensions in the following steps:

1. One can take out two points with a certain length that form a line segment.
1. If another identical line segment is its length in a perpendicular direction from itself, it sweeps out and forms a [square](wikipedia://en/659939) (2-cube). The results have four points and four line segments, which are called vertices and edges, respectively.
1. Moving the square with the same length in the direction perpendicular to the plane it lies on generates a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) (3-cube). The results have eight vertices, twelve edges, and six squares. The squares are called the faces.
1. Moving the cube with the same length again into the fourth-dimensional space generates a tesseract (4-cube).

A tesseract is bounded by eight cubes (its [cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_%28mathematics%29)). Each cube shares each of its faces with another cube. Three cubes and three squares meet at each edge. Four cubes, six squares, and four edges meet at every vertex. Collectively, the tesseract consists of eight cubes, twenty-four squares, thirty-two edges, and sixteen vertices. The tesseract, like both the square and the cube, is a member of the [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783)'s family.

An unfolding of a [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) is called a [net](wikipedia://en/1148129). There are 261 distinct nets of the tesseract,[^o6] each of which can tile 3-space.[^o7] The unfoldings of the tesseract can be counted by mapping the nets to *paired trees* (a [tree](wikipedia://en/48560) together with a [perfect matching](wikipedia://en/44031) in its [complement](wikipedia://en/1175649)). One of these unfoldings is the [Dali cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dali_cross), named after Spanish surrealist artist [Salvador Dalí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvador_Dalí), whose 1954 painting *[Corpus Hypercubus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_Hypercubus)* depicted it. It consists of eight cubes, four cubes stacked vertically and four more attached to the second-from-top of the first four.

:::

## Etymology {#Etymology}

tesseract The *[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary)* traces the word *tesseract* to [Charles Howard Hinton](wikipedia://en/562977)'s 1888 book *[A New Era of Thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_New_Era_of_Thought)*. Hinton originally spelled the word as *tessaract*, changing it to *tesseract* in his 1904 book *The Fourth Dimension*. The term derives from the [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) téssara (grc "four") and aktís (grc 'ray'), referring to the four edges from each vertex to other vertices. The word "tesseract" has been adopted for numerous other uses in popular culture, including as a plot device in works of science fiction, often with little or no connection to the four-dimensional hypercube.

## Properties {#Properties}

The eight cells of a tesseract may be regarded in three different ways as two interlocked rings of four cubes.[^o12] As a [regular polytope](wikipedia://en/518693) with three [cubes](wikipedia://en/6285) folded together around every edge, it has [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) {4,3,3} with [hyperoctahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/24268961#By_dimension) of order 384. Constructed as a 4D [hyperprism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperprism) made of two parallel cubes, it can be named as a composite Schläfli symbol {4,3} × { }, with symmetry order 96. As a 4-4 [duoprism](wikipedia://en/2114155), a [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of two [squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29), it can be named by a composite Schläfli symbol {4}×{4}, with symmetry order 64. As an [orthotope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthotope) it can be represented by composite Schläfli symbol { } × { } × { } × { } or { }<sup>4</sup>, with symmetry order 16.

Since each vertex of a tesseract is adjacent to four edges, the [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719) of the tesseract is a regular [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606). The [dual polytope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_polytope) of the tesseract is the [16-cell](wikipedia://en/716422) with Schläfli symbol {3,3,4}. The tesseract, with 16 vertices, is the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of a compound of two 16-cells, with 8 vertices each, in [an exact dimensional analogy](wikipedia://en/6833695) to the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285), with 8 vertices, which is the convex hull of a [compound of two regular tetrahedra](wikipedia://en/2601866), with 4 vertices each.

Each edge of a regular tesseract is of the same length. This is of interest when using tesseracts as the basis for a [network topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_topology) to link multiple processors in [parallel computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_computing): the distance between two nodes is at most 4, and there are many different paths to allow weight balancing.

The tesseract can be decomposed into smaller 4-polytopes. It is the convex hull of the compound of two [demitesseracts](wikipedia://en/6833695) ([16-cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demitesseract)). It can also be [triangulated](wikipedia://en/1081285) into 4-dimensional [simplices](wikipedia://en/39781) ([irregular 5-cells](wikipedia://en/513821#Irregular_5-cells)) that share their vertices with the tesseract. It is known that there are 92487256 such triangulations and that the fewest 4-dimensional simplices in any of them is 16.

The dissection of the tesseract into instances of its [characteristic simplex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthoscheme#Characteristic_simplex_of_the_general_regular_polytope) (a particular [orthoscheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthoscheme) with Coxeter diagram ) is the most basic direct construction of the tesseract possible. The **[characteristic 5-cell of the 4-cube](wikipedia://en/513821#Orthoschemes)** is a [fundamental region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_region) of the tesseract's defining [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/297004), the group which generates the [B<sub>4</sub> polytopes](wikipedia://en/45355843). The tesseract's characteristic simplex directly *generates* the tesseract through the actions of the group, by reflecting itself in its own bounding facets (its *mirror walls*).

### Unit tesseract {#Unit_tesseract}

A *unit tesseract* has side length $1$, and is typically taken as the basic unit for [hypervolume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypervolume) in 4-dimensional space. *The* unit tesseract in a [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706) for 4-dimensional space has two opposite vertices at coordinates $[0, 0, 0, 0]$ and $[1, 1, 1, 1]$, and other vertices with coordinates at all possible combinations of $0$s and $1$s. It is the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of the closed [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) $[0, 1]$ in each axis.

Sometimes a unit tesseract is centered at the origin, so that its coordinates are the more symmetrical $\bigl({\pm\tfrac12}, \pm\tfrac12, \pm\tfrac12, \pm\tfrac12 \bigr).$ This is the Cartesian product of the closed interval $\bigl[{-\tfrac12}, \tfrac12\bigr]$ in each axis.

Another commonly convenient tesseract is the Cartesian product of the closed interval $[-1,1]$ in each axis, with vertices at coordinates $(\pm 1, \pm 1, \pm 1, \pm 1)$. This tesseract has side length 2 and hypervolume $2^4 = 16$.

### Radial equilateral symmetry {#Radial_equilateral_symmetry}

The radius of a [hypersphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere) circumscribed about a regular polytope is the distance from the polytope's center to one of the vertices, and for the tesseract this radius is equal to its edge length; the diameter of the sphere, the length of the diagonal between opposite vertices of the tesseract, is twice the edge length. Only a few uniform [polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytopes) have this property, including the four-dimensional tesseract and [24-cell](wikipedia://en/293485#Radially_equilateral_honeycomb), the three-dimensional [cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/6280#Radial_equilateral_symmetry), and the two-dimensional [hexagon](wikipedia://en/59733). In particular, the tesseract is the only hypercube (other than a zero-dimensional point) that is *radially equilateral*. The longest vertex-to-vertex diagonal of an $n$-dimensional hypercube of unit edge length is $\sqrt{n\vphantom{t}},$ which for the square is $\sqrt2,$ for the cube is $\sqrt3,$ and only for the tesseract is $\sqrt4 = 2$ edge lengths.

An axis-aligned tesseract inscribed in a unit-radius 3-sphere has vertices with coordinates $\bigl({\pm\tfrac12}, \pm\tfrac12, \pm\tfrac12, \pm\tfrac12\bigr).$

### Formulas {#Formulas}

150px For a tesseract with side length $s$:

- [Hypervolume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypervolume) (4D): $H=s^4$
- Surface "volume" (3D): $SV=8s^3$
- [Face diagonal](wikipedia://en/10830506): $d_\mathrm{2}=\sqrt{2} s$
- [Cell diagonal](wikipedia://en/1337505): $d_\mathrm{3}=\sqrt{3} s$
- 4-space diagonal: $d_\mathrm{4}=2s$

:::

### As a configuration {#As_a_configuration}

The tesseract can be represented by [configuration matrix](wikipedia://en/18801624#As_configurations)

$$\begin{bmatrix}\begin{matrix}16 & 4 & 6 & 4 \\ 2 & 32 & 3 & 3 \\ 4 & 4 & 24 & 2 \\ 8 & 12 & 6 & 8 \end{matrix}\end{bmatrix}.$$

Here, the rows and columns correspond to vertices, edges, faces, and cells. The diagonal numbers say how many of each element occur in the whole tesseract, which reduces to the [f-vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-vector) $(16,32,24,8)$. The nondiagonal numbers say how many of the column's element occur in or at the row's element. For example, the 2 in the first column of the second row indicates that there are two vertices in (i.e., at the extremes of) each edge; the 4 in the second column of the first row indicates that four edges meet at each vertex. The bottom row defines they facets, here cubes, have f-vector $(8,12,6)$. The next row left of diagonal is ridge elements (facet of cube), here a square, $(4,4)$. The upper row is the f-vector of the [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719), here tetrahedra, $(4,6,4)$. The next row is vertex figure ridge, here a triangle, $(3,3)$.[^n1]

:::

## Projections {#Projections}

It is possible to project tesseracts into three- and two-dimensional spaces, similarly to projecting a cube into two-dimensional space.

*Image: Orthogonal projection envelopes tesseract.png.* Parallel projection envelopes of the tesseract (each cell is drawn with different color faces, inverted cells are undrawn)

*Image: Hypercubeorder binary.svg.* The [rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631) forms the convex hull of the tesseract's vertex-first parallel-projection. The number of vertices in the layers of this projection is 1 4 6 4 1—the fourth row in [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497).

The *cell-first* parallel [projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphical_projection) of the tesseract into three-dimensional space has a [cubical](wikipedia://en/6285) envelope. The nearest and farthest cells are projected onto the cube, and the remaining six cells are projected onto the six square faces of the cube.

The *face-first* parallel projection of the tesseract into three-dimensional space has a [cuboidal](wikipedia://en/156802) envelope. Two pairs of cells project to the upper and lower halves of this envelope, and the four remaining cells project to the side faces.

The *edge-first* parallel projection of the tesseract into three-dimensional space has an envelope in the shape of a [hexagonal prism](wikipedia://en/2859668). Six cells project onto rhombic prisms, which are laid out in the hexagonal prism in a way analogous to how the faces of the 3D cube project onto six rhombs in a hexagonal envelope under vertex-first projection. The two remaining cells project onto the prism bases.

The *vertex-first* parallel projection of the tesseract into three-dimensional space has a [rhombic dodecahedral](wikipedia://en/607631) envelope. Two vertices of the tesseract are projected to the origin. There are exactly two ways of [dissecting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissection_%28geometry%29) a rhombic dodecahedron into four congruent [rhombohedra](wikipedia://en/2706406), giving a total of eight possible rhombohedra, each a projected [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) of the tesseract. This projection is also the one with maximal volume. One set of projection vectors are *u* = (1,1,−1,−1), *v* = (−1,1,−1,1), *w* = (1,−1,−1,1).

## Tessellation {#Tessellation}

The tesseract, like all [hypercubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercubes), [tessellates](wikipedia://en/321671) [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). The self-dual [tesseractic honeycomb](wikipedia://en/9400139) consisting of 4 tesseracts around each face has [Schläfli](wikipedia://en/1452408) symbol **{4,3,3,4}**. Hence, the tesseract has a [dihedral angle](wikipedia://en/338046) of 90°.[^n2]

The tesseract's radial equilateral symmetry makes its tessellation the [unique regular body-centered cubic lattice](wikipedia://en/9400139#Sphere_packing) of equal-sized spheres, in any number of dimensions.

## Related polytopes and honeycombs {#Related_polytopes_and_honeycombs}

The regular tesseract, along with the [16-cell](wikipedia://en/716422), exists in a set of 15 [uniform 4-polytopes with the same symmetry](wikipedia://en/4023176#Related_uniform_polytopes_in_tesseract_symmetry). The tesseract {4,3,3} exists in a [sequence of regular 4-polytopes and honeycombs](wikipedia://en/40281224#Polytopes_and_honeycombs_with_tetrahedral_vertex_figures), {*p*,3,3} with [tetrahedral](wikipedia://en/30606) [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719), {3,3}. The tesseract is also in a [sequence of regular 4-polytope and honeycombs](wikipedia://en/3870617#Related_polytopes_and_honeycombs_with_cubic_cells), {4,3,*p*} with [cubic](wikipedia://en/6285) [cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_%28geometry%29).

## In popular culture {#In_popular_culture}

*Image: La Grande Arche de la Défense.jpg.* *[La Grande Arche de la Défense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Grande_Arche_de_la_Défense)*, a three-dimensional projected tesseract-shaped building

In addition to Salvador Dali's 1954 *Corpus Hypercubus*, tesseracts have been a popular theme in other arts. Notable examples include:

- "[And He Built a Crooked House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/And_He_Built_a_Crooked_House)", [Robert Heinlein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_A._Heinlein)'s 1940 science fiction story featuring a building in the form of an unfolded tesseract that folds up in an earthquake into its four-dimensional form. This and [Martin Gardner](wikipedia://en/20435)'s "The No-Sided Professor", published in 1946, are among the first in science fiction to introduce readers to the [Moebius band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moebius_band), the [Klein bottle](wikipedia://en/17412), and the tesseract.
- The [Grande Arche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grande_Arche), a monument and building near Paris, France, completed in 1989. According to the monument's engineer, [Erik Reitzel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erik_Reitzel), the Grande Arche was designed to resemble the projection of a tesseract.[^o36]
- *[Fez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fez_%28video_game%29)*, a video game where one plays a character who can see beyond the two dimensions that other characters can see, and must use this ability to solve platforming puzzles. Features "Dot", a tesseract who helps the player navigate the world and tells how to use abilities, fitting the theme of seeing beyond human perception of known dimensional space.[^o37]
