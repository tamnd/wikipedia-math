# Hypercube

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hypercube"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **hypercube** is an [*n*-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-dimensional_space) analogue of a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) ([*n* = 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-dimensional)) and a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) ([*n* = 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-dimensional)); the special case for [*n* = 4](wikipedia://en/1364622) is known as a *[tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112)*. It is a [closed](wikipedia://en/47279), [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), [convex](wikipedia://en/1817885) figure whose 1-[skeleton](wikipedia://en/2977910) consists of groups of opposite [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860) aligned in each of the space's [dimensions](wikipedia://en/8398), [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to each other and of the same length. A unit hypercube's longest diagonal in *n* dimensions is equal to $\sqrt{n}$.

:::

An *n*-dimensional hypercube is more commonly referred to as an ***n*-cube** or sometimes as an ***n*-dimensional cube**.[^o2][^o3] The term **measure polytope** (originally from Elte, 1912)[^o4] is also used, notably in the work of [H. S. M. Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Scott_MacDonald_Coxeter) who also labels the hypercubes the γn polytopes.[^n1]

The hypercube is the special case of a [hyperrectangle](wikipedia://en/1688550) (also called an *n-orthotope*).

A *unit hypercube* is a hypercube whose side has length one [unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_%28number%29). Often, the hypercube whose corners (or *vertices*) are the 2<sup>*n*</sup> points in **R**<sup>*n*</sup> with each coordinate equal to 0 or 1 is called *the* unit hypercube.

### By the number of dimensions {#By_the_number_of_dimensions}

*Image: From Point to Tesseract (Looped Version).gif.* An animation showing how to create a tesseract from a point

A hypercube can be defined by increasing the numbers of dimensions of a shape:

**0** – A point is a hypercube of dimension zero.

**1** – If one moves this point one unit length, it will sweep out a line segment, which is a unit hypercube of dimension one.

**2** – If one moves this line segment its length in a [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) direction from itself; it sweeps out a 2-dimensional square.

**3** – If one moves the square one unit length in the direction perpendicular to the plane it lies on, it will generate a 3-dimensional cube.

**4** – If one moves the cube one unit length into the fourth dimension, it generates a 4-dimensional unit hypercube (a unit [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112)).

This can be generalized to any number of dimensions. This process of sweeping out volumes can be formalized mathematically as a [Minkowski sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_sum): the *d*-dimensional hypercube is the Minkowski sum of *d* mutually perpendicular unit-length line segments, and is therefore an example of a [zonotope](wikipedia://en/669406).

The [1-skeleton](wikipedia://en/74718534) of a hypercube is a [hypercube graph](wikipedia://en/6706815).

:::

### Vertex coordinates {#Vertex_coordinates}

*Image: 8-cell.gif.* Projection of a [rotating](wikipedia://en/39789) [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112)

A unit hypercube of dimension $n$ is the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of all the $2^n$ points whose $n$ [Cartesian coordinates](wikipedia://en/7706) are each equal to either $0$ or $1$. These points are its [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417). The hypercube with these coordinates is also the [cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) $[0,1]^n$ of $n$ copies of the unit [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $[0,1]$. Another unit hypercube, centered at the origin of the ambient space, can be obtained from this one by a [translation](wikipedia://en/204682). It is the convex hull of the $2^n$ points whose vectors of Cartesian coordinates are

$$\left(\pm \frac{1}{2}, \pm \frac{1}{2}, \cdots, \pm \frac{1}{2}\right)\!.$$

Here the symbol $\pm$ means that each coordinate is either equal to $1/2$ or to $-1/2$. This unit hypercube is also the cartesian product $[-1/2,1/2]^n$. Any unit hypercube has an edge length of $1$ and an $n$-dimensional volume of $1$.

The $n$-dimensional hypercube obtained as the convex hull of the points with coordinates $(\pm 1, \pm 1, \cdots, \pm 1)$ or, equivalently as the Cartesian product $[-1,1]^n$ is also often considered due to the simpler form of its vertex coordinates. Its edge length is $2$, and its $n$-dimensional volume is $2^n$.

:::

## Faces {#Faces}

Every hypercube admits, as its faces, hypercubes of a lower dimension contained in its boundary. A hypercube of dimension $n$ admits $2n$ facets, or faces of dimension $n-1$: a ($1$-dimensional) line segment has $2$ endpoints; a ($2$-dimensional) square has $4$ sides or edges; a $3$-dimensional cube has $6$ square faces; a ($4$-dimensional) tesseract has $8$ three-dimensional cubes as its facets. The number of vertices of a hypercube of dimension $n$ is $2^n$ (a usual, $3$-dimensional cube has $2^3=8$ vertices, for instance).[^o12]

The number of the $m$-dimensional hypercubes (just referred to as $m$-cubes from here on) contained in the boundary of an $n$-cube is

$E_{m,n} = 2^{n-m}{n \choose m}$,[^n2] where ${n \choose m}=\frac{n!}{m!\,(n-m)!}$ and $n!$ denotes the [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606) of $n$.

For example, the boundary of a $4$-cube ($n=4$) contains $8$ cubes ($3$-cubes), $24$ squares ($2$-cubes), $32$ line segments ($1$-cubes) and $16$ vertices ($0$-cubes). This identity can be proven by a simple combinatorial argument: for each of the $2^n$ vertices of the hypercube, there are $\tbinom n m$ ways to choose a collection of $m$ edges incident to that vertex. Each of these collections defines one of the $m$-dimensional faces incident to the considered vertex. Doing this for all the vertices of the hypercube, each of the $m$-dimensional faces of the hypercube is counted $2^m$ times since it has that many vertices, and we need to divide $2^n\tbinom n m$ by this number.

The number of facets of the hypercube can be used to compute the $(n-1)$-dimensional volume of its boundary: that volume is $2n$ times the volume of a $(n-1)$-dimensional hypercube; that is, $2ns^{n-1}$ where $s$ is the length of the edges of the hypercube.

These numbers can also be generated by the linear [recurrence relation](wikipedia://en/146806).

$E_{m,n} = 2E_{m,n-1} + E_{m-1,n-1} \!$, with $E_{0,0}= 1$, and $E_{m,n}=0$ when $n < m$, $n < 0$, or $m < 0$.

For example, extending a square via its 4 vertices adds one extra line segment (edge) per vertex. Adding the opposite square to form a cube provides $E_{1,3}=12$ line segments.

The extended [f-vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-vector) for an *n*-cube can also be computed by expanding $(2x+1)^n$ (concisely, (2,1)<sup>*n*</sup>), and reading off the coefficients of the resulting [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000#Multiplication). For example, the elements of a tesseract is (2,1)<sup>4</sup> = (4,4,1)<sup>2</sup> = (16,32,24,8,1).

## Related families of polytopes {#Related_families_of_polytopes}

The hypercubes are one of the few families of [regular polytopes](wikipedia://en/518693) that are represented in any number of dimensions.[^o33]

The **hypercube** family is one of three [regular polytope](wikipedia://en/518693) families, labeled by [Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter) as *γ<sub>n</sub>*. The other two are the hypercube dual family, the **[cross-polytopes](wikipedia://en/716401)**, labeled as *β<sub>n,</sub>* and the **[simplices](wikipedia://en/39781)**, labeled as *α<sub>n</sub>*. A fourth family, the [infinite tessellations of hypercubes](wikipedia://en/12237982), is labeled as *δ<sub>n</sub>*.

Another related family of semiregular and [uniform polytopes](wikipedia://en/6842392) is the **[demihypercubes](wikipedia://en/6833695)**, which are constructed from hypercubes with alternate vertices deleted and [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) facets added in the gaps, labeled as *hγ<sub>n</sub>*.

*n*-cubes can be combined with their duals (the [cross-polytopes](wikipedia://en/716401)) to form compound polytopes:

- In two dimensions, we obtain the [octagrammic](wikipedia://en/2667613) star figure {8/2},
- In three dimensions we obtain the [compound of cube and octahedron](wikipedia://en/8010874),
- In four dimensions we obtain the compound of tesseract and 16-cell.

## Relation to (*n*−1)-simplices {#Relation_to_(n−1)-simplices}

The graph of the *n*-hypercube's edges is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to the [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199) of the (*n*−1)-[simplex](wikipedia://en/39781)'s [face lattice](wikipedia://en/1817885#The_face_lattice). This can be seen by orienting the *n*-hypercube so that two opposite vertices lie vertically, corresponding to the (*n*−1)-simplex itself and the null polytope, respectively. Each vertex connected to the top vertex then uniquely maps to one of the (*n*−1)-simplex's facets (*n*−2 faces), and each vertex connected to those vertices maps to one of the simplex's *n*−3 faces, and so forth, and the vertices connected to the bottom vertex map to the simplex's vertices.

This relation may be used to generate the face lattice of an (*n*−1)-simplex efficiently, since face lattice enumeration algorithms applicable to general polytopes are more computationally expensive.

## Generalized hypercubes {#Generalized_hypercubes}

Regular [complex polytopes](wikipedia://en/8581934#Regular_complex_polytopes) can be defined in [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) called *generalized hypercubes*, γ*p* = <sub>*p*</sub>{4}<sub>2</sub>{3}...<sub>2</sub>{3}<sub>2</sub>, or ... Real solutions exist with *p* = 2, i.e. γ2 = γ<sub>*n*</sub> = <sub>2</sub>{4}<sub>2</sub>{3}...<sub>2</sub>{3}<sub>2</sub> = {4,3,..,3}. For *p* > 2, they exist in $\mathbb{C}^n$. The facets are generalized (*n*−1)-cube and the [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719) are regular [simplexes](wikipedia://en/39781).

The [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) perimeter seen in these orthogonal projections is called a [Petrie polygon](wikipedia://en/18571869). The generalized squares (*n* = 2) are shown with edges outlined as red and blue alternating color *p*-edges, while the higher *n*-cubes are drawn with black outlined *p*-edges.

The number of *m*-face elements in a *p*-generalized *n*-cube are: $p^{n-m}{n \choose m}$. This is *p*<sup>*n*</sup> vertices and *pn* facets.[^o37]

## Relation to exponentiation {#Relation_to_exponentiation}

Any positive integer raised to another positive integer power will yield a third integer, with this third integer being a specific type of [figurate number](wikipedia://en/325260) corresponding to an *n*-cube with a number of dimensions corresponding to the exponential. For example, the exponent 2 will yield a [square number](wikipedia://en/213919) or "perfect square", which can be arranged into a square shape with a side length corresponding to that of the base. Similarly, the exponent 3 will yield [a perfect cube](wikipedia://en/451445#In_integers), an integer which can be arranged into a cube shape with a side length of the base. As a result, the act of raising a number to 2 or 3 is more commonly referred to as "[squaring](wikipedia://en/659942)" and "cubing", respectively. However, the names of higher-order hypercubes do not appear to be in common use for higher powers.
