# Magnetic field

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Magnetic field"}

In [magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism) and [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism), **magnetic field** is a physical property of space that quantifies the magnetic influence at a given location.

:::

Magnetic fields

- deflect moving [electric charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charges) (including [electric currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current))
- apply torques on [magnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnet) to twist them in the direction of the magnetic field
- attract or repel magnets and magnetic material such as [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron)
- [induce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_induction) electric currents in electically conducting material.

Magnetic fields are created by

- [permanent magnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permanent_magnet) materials
- moving electric charges (including electric current).

Magnetic fields are used throughout modern science and technology. In [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering) and [electromechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromechanics), it is important in the design and use of [electric motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_motor), [generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_generator), [transformers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer), [electromagnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnets), and [inductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductor) among many other devices. In [material science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Material_science), magnetic forces give information about the charge carriers in a material through the [Hall effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hall_effect) in addition to other uses.

In [geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology) and [geophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysics), [Earth's magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_magnetic_field) gives information about earth's interior while local magnetic field measurements are used in [mineral exploration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral_exploration) and other measurements. Too, Earth's magnetic field creates a [magnetosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetosphere) which shields the Earth's [ozone layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_layer) and the rest of the planet from the [solar wind](wikipedia://en/28538). In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), the relationship between the magnetic and electric fields forms the field of [electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrodynamics) which is important to understand a wide range of phenomena including [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light) (also known as [electromagnetic radiation](wikipedia://en/9426)) and the properties of [antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antenna_%28radio%29) and [transmission lines](wikipedia://en/41811).

Since both strength and direction of a magnetic field may vary with location, it is described mathematically by assigning a [vector](wikipedia://en/32533) to each point of space, making it a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641).[^o2] There are two different, but closely related, vector fields which together or separately are called "magnetic field". These are written as $\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{H}$.The letters B and H were originally chosen by Maxwell in his *[Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise_on_Electricity_and_Magnetism)* (Vol. II, pp. 236–237). For many quantities, he simply started choosing letters from the beginning of the alphabet. See Ralph Baierlein. (2000). "Answer to Question #73. S is for entropy, Q is for charge". *American Journal of Physics*. 68 (8). p. 691. doi:10.1119/1.19524 While the best names for these fields is the subject of long running debate, the underlying physics is uncontested.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

The international [ISO 80000-6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_80000-6) standard defines **magnetic field** as "that component of an electromagnetic field which is characterized by the magnetic field strength vector $\mathbf{H}$ and the magnetic flux density vector $\mathbf{B}$."[^o7] This standard also defines $\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ as given in the sections below. While there is wide agreement on these definitions of $\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{H}$, there are many alternative names for both (see sidebars in the corresponding sections).

### The B-field {#The_B-field}

Also known as **magnetic flux density**, the magnetic $\mathbf{B}$ field causes magnetic forces, magnetic torques and electromagnetic induction. Therefore, it can be defined by any equation that describes these phenomena.

For example, the magnetic field vector $\mathbf{B}$ at any point can be defined as the vector field that, when used in the [Lorentz force law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_force_law), correctly predicts the force on a moving charged particle at that point:[^n1][^n2]

::: {#o15 .equation cue=equation-box-1 name="Lorentz force law (vector form, SI units)"}

**Lorentz force law** (*[vector](wikipedia://en/32533) form, [SI units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units)*).**

:::

Here $\mathbf{F}$ is the force on the particle, $q$ is the particle's [electric charge](wikipedia://en/9804), $\mathbf{E}$ is the external electric field, $\mathbf{v}$, is the particle's [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903), and × denotes the [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092).

In other words,[^n3] \[T\]he command, "Measure the direction and magnitude of the vector $\mathbf{B}$ at such and such a place," calls for the following operations: Take a particle of known charge $q$. Measure the force on $q$ at rest, to determine $\mathbf{E}$. Then measure the force on the particle when its velocity is $\mathbf{v}$; repeat with $\mathbf{v}$ in some other direction. Now find a $\mathbf{B}$ that makes the Lorentz force law fit all these results—that is the magnetic field at the place in question. For more details see [Lorentz Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_Force) or the section below.

The [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI) unit of $\mathbf{B}$ is [tesla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla_%28unit%29) (symbol: T).[^o17] The [Gaussian-cgs unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_units) of $\mathbf{B}$ is the [gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss_%28unit%29) (symbol: G).[^o18] (The conversion is 1 T ≘ 10000 G.[^o19][^o20]) One nanotesla corresponds to 1 gamma (symbol: γ).[^o20]

### The H-field {#The_H-field}

While $\mathbf{B}$ creates magnetic forces and torques on objects and induces currents in conducting wires, it is not always easy to calculate. For this reason, it is useful to define a magnetic $\mathbf{H}$ field[^o25], also known as **magnetic field strength**, such that[^n4]:

::: {#o28 .equation cue=equation-box-1 name="Definition of the field (vector form, SI units)"}

**Definition of the $H$ field** *([vector](wikipedia://en/32533) form, [SI units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units)).**

:::

where $\mu_0$ is the [vacuum permeability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_permeability), and $\mathbf{M}$ is the [magnetization vector](wikipedia://en/2404348) which represents how magnetized a given region of material is and is defined below. In a vacuum, $\mathbf{B} = \mu_{0}\mathbf{H}$ making them equivalent to each other. Inside a material they are different.

Defined this way, $\mathbf{H}$ can in many circumstance[^o29] be treated as if it is only due to electrical currents with corrections accounting for $\mathbf{H}$ due to nearby magnetic material.[^o30] In any case, $\mathbf{B}$ still needs to be calculated from $\mathbf{H}$ if forces, torques, induced currents, or energy changes need to be calculated.

The SI unit of $\mathbf{H}$ is the [ampere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampere) per metre (A/m)[^o31] and the Gaussian unit is the [oersted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oersted) (Oe).[^o19]

:::

## Measurement and visualization {#Measurement_and_visualization}

### Magnetometers {#Magnetometers}

*Image: Magnetometr transduktorowy by Zureks.jpg.* A uniaxial fluxgate magnetometer

Instruments used to measure the local magnetic $\mathbf{B}$-field are known as a [magnetometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetometer). Important classes of magnetometers include [induction magnetometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_coil) (or search-coil magnetometers) which measure only varying magnetic fields, [rotating coil magnetometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetometer#Rotating_coil_magnetometer), [Hall effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hall_effect) magnetometers, [NMR magnetometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton_magnetometer), [SQUID magnetometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQUID), and [fluxgate magnetometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetometer#Fluxgate_magnetometer). The magnetic fields of distant [astronomical objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_object) are measured through their effects on local charged particles. For instance, electrons spiraling around a field line produce [synchrotron radiation](wikipedia://en/170808) that is detectable in [radio waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_waves). The finest precision for a magnetic field measurement was attained by [Gravity Probe B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_Probe_B) at 5 aT (5 × 10<sup>\-18</sup> T).[^o34]

The $\mathbf{H}$-field cannot be directly measured but can be inferred from the currents that create it.

::: {#o36 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Magnetic field lines"}

### Magnetic field lines {#Magnetic_field_lines}

Magnetic field can be visualized by a set of *magnetic field lines*, that follow the direction of the field at each point. The direction of the magnetic field at any point is parallel to the direction of nearby field lines, and the local density of field lines can be made proportional to its strength. Magnetic field lines are like [streamlines](wikipedia://en/166086) in [fluid flow](wikipedia://en/11034), in that they represent a continuous distribution, and a different resolution would show more or fewer lines.

Magnetic field lines have the following properties:[^n5]

- The direction of the magnetic field is tangent to the field line at any point. A small compass points in the direction of the field line.
- The strength of the field is proportional to the closeness of the lines.
- Magnetic field lines never cross.
- Magnetic field lines are directed from the north pole to the south pole.

An advantage of using magnetic field lines as a representation is that many laws of magnetism (and electromagnetism) can be stated completely and concisely using simple concepts such as the "number" of field lines through a surface. These concepts can then be "translated" to their mathematical form. For example, the number of field lines through a given surface is the [surface integral](wikipedia://en/528867) of the magnetic field.[^n6]

:::

### Different unit systems {#Different_unit_systems}

This article uses the SI unit system. But other unit systems, most importantly the [Gaussian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_units) unit system (which is the most used system of [cgs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cgs) units for electromagnetism), are still being used in some disciplines, countries, and textbooks. The equations for each unit system can be, and often are, different for different unit systems.

## Force on moving charges and current {#Force_on_moving_charges_and_current}

Moving electric charges including electrical currents experience a force due to magnetic $\mathbf{B}$ fields.

### Magnetic force on a charged particle {#Magnetic_force_on_a_charged_particle}

A [charged particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charged_particle) moving in a $\mathbf{B}$-field experiences a *sideways* force that is proportional to the strength of the magnetic field, the component of the velocity that is perpendicular to the magnetic field and the charge of the particle. This force is known as the *Lorentz force*, and is given by:[^n1][^n2]

::: {#o44 .equation cue=equation-box-1 name="Lorentz force law (vector form, SI units)"}

**Lorentz force law** (*[vector](wikipedia://en/32533) form, [SI units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units)*).**

:::

where $\mathbf{F}$ is the [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force), $q$ is the [electric charge](wikipedia://en/9804) of the particle, $\mathbf{v}$ is the instantaneous [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) of the particle, and $\mathbf{B}$ is the magnetic field (in [teslas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla_%28unit%29)). The direction of force on the charge can be determined by the [right-hand rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-hand_rule) (see the figure).

The Lorentz force is always perpendicular to both the velocity of the particle and the magnetic field that created it. When a charged particle moves in a static magnetic field, it traces a helical path in which the helix axis is parallel to the magnetic field, and in which the speed of the particle remains constant.

### Net force and torque on current loops {#Net_force_and_torque_on_current_loops}

## Magnetic field due to electrical currents {#Magnetic_field_due_to_electrical_currents}

All moving charged particles produce magnetic fields. Moving [point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_particle) charges, such as [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron), produce [complicated but well known magnetic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jefimenko's_equations) that depend on the charge, velocity, and acceleration of the particles.[^n14] These equations become much simpler when the moving charges form a steady state electrical current, the study of which is called [magnetostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetostatics).

### Ampere's law {#Ampere's_law}

A slightly more general[^n24][^o85] way of relating the current $I$ to the $\mathbf{B}$-field is through [Ampère's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampère's_circuital_law):[^n25][^n26]

## Force between magnets {#Force_between_magnets}

### Magnets {#Magnets}

[Magnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnet) are objects that both create their own magnetic field and respond to the magnetic field of other magnets and magnetized materials. The interaction between [magnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnet) and their interaction with magnetic field is extremely complicated. The correct description involves describing each magnet as being made of many small volumes of magnetic material each of which creates its own magnetic field and responds to the magnetic field of the other volumes. Such models are often extremely complex. Fortunately, in many cases, it is sufficient to understand magnets as objects that have 2 equal but opposite magnetic poles: the magnetic north and south poles. Opposite poles attract with a force that increases with smaller distances while like poles repel in the same way. Such a model is called a *magnetic pole model* and it, in some cases described below, can be used to make good quantitative predictions.

Specifying the [force between magnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_between_magnets) is quite complicated because it depends on the strength and [orientation](wikipedia://en/2544098) of both magnets and their distance and direction relative to each other. The force is particularly sensitive to rotations of the magnets due to magnetic torque. The force on each magnet depends on its magnetic moment and the magnetic field[^o92] of the other. For short distances (small $r$) the forces can be quite strong but it decreases quite rapidly ($1/r^{4}$) for large distances.

## Magnetic field due to magnetized material {#Magnetic_field_due_to_magnetized_material}

Most materials respond to an applied magnetic field by becoming magnetized (at least temporarily) which causes them to produce their own magnetic field. Typically, the response is weak and exists only when the magnetic field is applied. There are many different types of material that respond differently to the applied magnetic field.

### Types of magnetic materials {#Types_of_magnetic_materials}

The term *magnet* is typically reserved for objects that produce their own persistent magnetic field even in the absence of an applied magnetic field. Only certain classes of materials can do this. Most materials, however, produce a magnetic field in response to an applied magnetic field – a phenomenon known as [magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism). There are several types of magnetism, and all materials exhibit at least one of them.

The overall magnetic behavior of a material can vary widely, depending on the structure of the material, particularly on its [electron configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_configuration). It can also vary with temperature, pressure, and magnetic field strength such that a given material may have more than one magnetic [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28matter%29). Several forms of magnetic behavior have been observed in different materials, including:

- [Diamagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamagnetism)[^o109] produces a magnetization that opposes the magnetic field.
- [Paramagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramagnetism)[^o109] produces a magnetization in the same direction as the applied magnetic field.
- [Ferromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism) and the closely related [Ferrimagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrimagnetism) and [Antiferromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiferromagnetism)[^o110][^o111] can produce a magnetization independent of the applied magnetic field with a complicated and often [hysteretic](wikipedia://en/147003) relationship. Materials in these states can be used to make permanent magnets.
- [Superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity) (and [ferromagnetic superconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetic_superconductor))[^o112][^o113] is characterized by perfect conductivity below a critical temperature and magnetic field. They also are highly magnetic and can be perfect diamagnets below a lower [critical magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_magnetic_field). Superconductors often have a broad range of temperatures and magnetic fields (the so-named [mixed state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_II_superconductor#Mixed_state)) under which they exhibit a complicated and often hysteretic relationship between how the material is magnetized and the applied magnetic field.

In the case of paramagnetism and diamagnetism, the relationship between the applied magnetic field and the magnetization is often [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear). However, superconductors and ferromagnets have a more complicated relation between the applied magnetic field and magnetization produced (see [magnetic hysteresis](wikipedia://en/147003#Magnetic_hysteresis)). *Permanent magnets* are objects that produce their own persistent magnetic fields. They are made of [ferromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism)[^o114] materials, such as iron and [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel), that have been magnetized.

### Magnetic dipole moment {#Magnetic_dipole_moment}

The magnetic field of magnetized material is created at the atomic level. The proper description of this effect involves [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202). Fortunately, the net effect of adding up these magnetic interactions can often be calculated using much simpler models for the magnetic field created by the constituent atoms in the magnetic material. This occurs because at large enough distance (or equivalently for small enough magnets) all the magnetic properties of any magnetic object can be described by a single (vector) quantity, the [magnetic dipole moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_dipole_moment), $\mathbf{m}$. (See and above). Objects that can be modeled this way, for example atoms, are called [magnetic dipoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_dipoles).

Magnetic dipoles, therefore, are the building blocks of magnetization. The magnetic field produced by magnetized material then is the net magnetic field of these dipoles. Too, the net force (and torque) on a magnetized material is a result of adding up the forces and torques on the individual dipoles that make up the magnetized material.

### Magnetization {#Magnetization}

The *magnetization* vector field $\mathbf{M}$ represents how strongly a region of material is magnetized. It is defined as the net [magnetic dipole moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_dipole_moment) per unit volume of that region.[^n28] The magnetization of a uniformly magnetized magnet is therefore a constant, equal to the magnetic moment $\mathbf{m}$ of the magnet divided by its volume. Since the SI unit of magnetic moment is A⋅m<sup>2</sup>, the SI unit of magnetization $\mathbf{M}$ is ampere per meter, identical to that of the $\mathbf{H}$-field.

The magnetization $\mathbf{M}$ field of a region points in the direction of the average magnetic dipole moment in that region. Magnetization field lines, therefore, begin (inside the magnetized material) near the magnetic south pole and ends (inside the magnetized material) near the magnetic north pole. (Magnetization does not exist outside magnetized material.)

In the Amperian loop model, the magnetization is due to combining many tiny magnetic dipole loops to form a resultant current called *[bound current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bound_current)*. This bound current, then, is the source of the magnetic $\mathbf{B}$ field due to the magnet. Given the definition of the magnetic dipole, the magnetization field follows a similar law to that of Ampere's law:[^n29]

## Electrodynamics {#Electrodynamics}

For time varying magnetic fields (and more generally changing electrical currents or accelerating electrical charges), the magnetic and electric fields become linked such that a change in one induces the other. Together, the electric and magnetic fields form an [electromagnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field). The study of how the electric and magnetic fields interact in this way is called [electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrodynamics) and includes many phenomenon that are important in physics and electrical engineering. It underlies transformers, and the generation and transmission of electrical power through wires and through space in the form of [electromagnetic radiation](wikipedia://en/9426) of which [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light) is one form. Too, it allow magnetic fields to store and transmit energy.

### Maxwell's equations {#Maxwell's_equations}

It is sometimes useful to calculate the magnetic field for a given set of time varying charges and currents, without having to use the [complicated equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jefimenko's_equations) used to directly calculate it. An example of this is calculating the magnetic field of a light wave as it reflects and refracts at a surface. In such cases [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737) are used to solve for both the magnetic and electric fields. (In electrodynamics the electric and magnetic fields are coupled.)

Maxwell's equations are a powerful set of [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309) that allows the calculation of the magnetic and electric fields for simple (and complex using computers and [Finite Element Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_Element_Analysis)) geometries. Maxwell's Equations together with the Lorentz force law form a complete description of [classical electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/426219) including both electricity and magnetism.

Maxwell's equations takes advantage of the fact that all vector fields (such as the electric and magnetic fields) can be expressed in terms of 2 types of *sources* and an appropriate set of boundary conditions.[^o154] The first type of *source* (an outflow source) causes the vector field to flow out (or in for a sink) to a given point. The second (or circulation) source causes the vector field to rotate around a given point (forming [vortices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vortex)). Both of these sources have well defined definitions and can be calculated from the vector field they create using a well-understood vector operator.

The [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) of a vector field $\mathbf{A}$, $\mathbf{\nabla} \cdot \mathbf{A}$ is defined such that applying the divergence operator to a given vector field will yield the outflow sources. The [curl](wikipedia://en/6123) is defined such that $\mathbf{\nabla} \times \mathbf{A}$ yields the circulation source. An example of the power of these vector operators is: since it is an experimental fact that magnetic charges do not exist (and therefore there are no source nor sinks of $\mathbf{B}$) the divergence of $\mathbf{B}$ must be zero, $\mathbf{\nabla} \cdot \mathbf{B}$ = 0, which is one of Maxwell's equations.

Maxwell's equation has 2 major versions: a microscopic version which necessitates knowing all of the charges and currents (including the complex ones at the atomic level) and the macroscopic version which depends only on the know 'free' charge and 'free' currents. Here the term 'free' means any charge or current that is directly controlled by the experiment and does not include the atomic level 'bound' charges and currents in a material which happen as a response to the electric and magnetic fields present in that material.

Maxwell's macroscopic equations are written as:

::: {#o155 .equation cue=equation-box-1 name="Maxwell's equations in matter (vector form, SI units)"}

**Maxwell's equations in matter** (*[vector](wikipedia://en/32533) form, [SI units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units)*).**

:::

In these equations, $\mathbf{D}$ is the [electric displacement field](wikipedia://en/1191101), $\mathbf{E}$ the [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092), $\rho_f$ the free [electric charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge_density), and $\mathbf{J}_f$ the free [current density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_density).

The first of Maxwell's equations is known as [Gauss' Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss'_Law) but does not involve magnetic field so does not warrant further discussion here. The second equation is [Gauss' law for magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss'_law_for_magnetism) which reflects the non-existence of magnetic charge and allows $\mathbf{B}$ to be determined as the curl of a vector potential $\mathbf{A}$. The third equation is [Faraday's law of induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday's_law_of_induction). And, the fourth equation is [Ampère's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampère's_circuital_law) with Maxwell's correction.

### Magnetic and electric fields are different aspects of the same phenomenon {#Magnetic_and_electric_fields_are_different_aspects_of_the_same_phenomenon}

Magnetic field is inherently a [relativistic phenomena](wikipedia://en/26962). More specifically, both electric and magnetic fields are the same phenomenon as seen in different [reference frames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference#Observational_frames_of_reference): An electric force perceived by one observer may be perceived by another (in a different frame of reference) as a magnetic force, or a mixture of electric and magnetic forces. (Here different reference frames means one reference frame is moving relative to the other.) For relativistic phenomena, a [lorentz transformation](wikipedia://en/18404) must be used to move (or transform) from one reference system to another.

It is a straightforward task[^n43] to show how the electric and magnetic fields transform from one reference frame to another. The [transformation rules](wikipedia://en/18404#Transformation_of_the_electromagnetic_field), however are quite messy. ### Quantum electrodynamics {#Quantum_electrodynamics}

The classical electromagnetic field incorporated into quantum mechanics forms what is known as the semi-classical theory of radiation. However, it is not able to make experimentally observed predictions such as [spontaneous emission process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_emission) or [Lamb shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamb_shift) implying the need for quantization of fields. In modern physics, the electromagnetic field is understood to be not a *[classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_physics)* [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28physics%29), but rather a [quantum field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field); it is represented not as a vector of three [numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) at each point, but as a vector of three [quantum operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_%28physics%29) at each point. The most accurate modern description of the electromagnetic interaction (and much else) is *quantum electrodynamics* (QED),[^o175] which is incorporated into a more complete theory known as the *Standard Model of particle physics*.

In QED, the magnitude of the electromagnetic interactions between charged particles (and their [antiparticles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiparticle)) is computed using [perturbation theory](wikipedia://en/297069). These rather complex formulas produce a remarkable pictorial representation as [Feynman diagrams](wikipedia://en/11617) in which [virtual photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_photon) are exchanged.

Predictions of QED agree with experiments to an extremely high degree of accuracy: currently about 10<sup>−12</sup> (and limited by experimental errors); for details see [precision tests of QED](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precision_tests_of_QED). This makes QED one of the most accurate physical theories constructed thus far.

All equations in this article are in the [classical approximation](wikipedia://en/426219), which is less accurate than the quantum description mentioned here. However, under most everyday circumstances, the difference between the two theories is negligible.

## Applications {#Applications}

### Uses in geology {#Uses_in_geology}

#### Earth's magnetic field {#Earth's_magnetic_field}

*Image: VFPt Earths Magnetic Field Confusion.svg.* A sketch of Earth's magnetic field representing the source of the field as a magnet. The south pole of the magnetic field is near the geographic north pole of the Earth.

The Earth's magnetic field is produced by [convection](wikipedia://en/47526) of a liquid iron alloy in the [outer core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_core). In a [dynamo process](wikipedia://en/255217), the movements drive a feedback process in which electric currents create electric and magnetic fields that in turn act on the currents.[^o180]

The field at the surface of the Earth is approximately the same as if a giant bar magnet were positioned at the center of the Earth and tilted at an angle of about 11° off the rotational axis of the Earth (see the figure).[^o181] The north pole of a magnetic compass needle points roughly north, toward the [North Magnetic Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Magnetic_Pole). However, because a magnetic pole is attracted to its opposite, the North Magnetic Pole is actually the south pole of the geomagnetic field. This confusion in terminology arises because the pole of a magnet is defined by the geographical direction it points.[^o182]

Earth's magnetic field is not constant—the strength of the field and the location of its poles vary.[^o183] Moreover, the poles periodically reverse their orientation in a process called [geomagnetic reversal](wikipedia://en/2203131). The [most recent reversal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunhes–Matuyama_reversal) occurred 780,000 years ago.[^o184]

#### Magnetic surveys {#Magnetic_surveys}

*Image: 3030-mag.jpg.* Magnetic gradiometer map of Prehistoric fire-hearths

**Magnetic surveying** is one of a number of methods used in [archaeological geophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysical_survey_%28archaeology%29). Magnetic surveys record spatial variation in the Earth's magnetic field. In [archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeology), magnetic surveys are used to detect and map archaeological [artefacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artefact_%28archaeology%29) and [features](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feature_%28archaeology%29). Magnetic surveys are used in both terrestrial and [marine archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_archaeology). In terrestrial archaeology, magnetic surveys are typically used for detailed mapping of archaeological features on known archaeological sites. More exceptionally, magnetometers are used for low-resolution exploratory surveys. Magnetic survey help to prove that a survey area has the potential for more detailed studies and scientific excavation. Magnetic surveys are extremely useful in the excavation and exploration of underwater archaeological sites. In maritime archaeology, these are often used to map the geology of wreck sites and determine the composition of magnetic materials found on the seafloor.

Measuring the Earths' magnetic field is a very useful tool in [mineral exploration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral_exploration), [oil exploration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_exploration), and [geological mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geological_mapping). To cover large areas with uniform data, aircraft such as [helicopters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helicopter), [airplanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airplanes), and [drones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unmanned_aerial_vehicle) are employed. The amount of detail is a function of flight height and sample density, in addition to instrument sensitivity. For surveys, drones are used which helps greatly in the process. Aeromagnetic surveys are also used to perform reconnaissance mapping of [unexploded ordnance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unexploded_ordnance).

### Uses in engineering {#Uses_in_engineering}

#### Rotating magnetic fields {#Rotating_magnetic_fields}

The *rotating magnetic field* is a common design principle in the operation of [alternating-current motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_motor#AC_motors). A permanent magnet in such a field rotates so as to maintain its alignment with the external field.

Magnetic torque is used to drive [electric motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_motor). In one simple motor design, a magnet is fixed to a freely rotating shaft and is subjected to a magnetic field from an array of [electromagnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet). By continuously switching the electric current through each of the electromagnets, thereby flipping the polarity of their magnetic fields, like poles are kept next to the rotor; the resultant torque is transferred to the shaft.

A rotating magnetic field can be constructed using two coils at right angles with a phase difference of 90 degrees between their AC currents. In practice, [three-phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-phase_electric_power) systems are used where the three currents are equal in magnitude and have a phase difference of 120 degrees. Three similar coils at mutual geometrical angles of 120 degrees create the rotating magnetic field. The ability of the three-phase system to create a rotating field, utilized in electric motors, is one of the main reasons why three-phase systems dominate the world's [electrical power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_power) supply systems.

[Synchronous motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchronous_motor) use DC-voltage-fed rotor windings, which lets the excitation of the machine be controlled—and [induction motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induction_motor) use short-circuited [rotors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotor_%28electric%29) (instead of a magnet) following the rotating magnetic field of a multicoiled [stator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stator_%28electric_machines%29). The short-circuited turns of the rotor develop [eddy currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eddy_current) induced by the rotating field of the stator, and these currents in turn produce a torque on the rotor through the Lorentz force.

The Italian physicist [Galileo Ferraris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Ferraris) and the Serbian-American [electrical engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineer) [Nikola Tesla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikola_Tesla) independently researched the use of rotating magnetic fields in electric motors. In 1888, Ferraris published his research in a paper to the *Royal Academy of Sciences* in [Turin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turin) and Tesla gained 381968 for his work.

#### Magnetic circuits {#Magnetic_circuits}

*Image: Magnetischer Kreis.svg.* Magnetic circuit

An important use of $\mathbf{H}$ is in *magnetic circuits*. A magnetic circuit is made up of one or more closed loop paths containing a [magnetic flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_flux). The flux is usually generated by [permanent magnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permanent_magnet) or [electromagnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet) and confined to the path by [magnetic cores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_core) consisting of [ferromagnetic materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetic_material) like iron, although there may be air gaps or other materials in the path. Magnetic circuits are employed to efficiently channel [magnetic fields](wikipedia://en/36563) in many devices such as [electric motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_motor), [generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_generator), [transformers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer), [relays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relay), lifting [electromagnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet), [SQUIDs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQUID), [galvanometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galvanometer), and magnetic [recording heads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recording_head).

#### Magnetic levitation {#Magnetic_levitation}

*Image: Maglev june2005.jpg.* The [Transrapid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transrapid) system uses servomechanisms to pull the train up from underneath the track and maintains a constant gap while travelling at high speed

**Magnetic levitation** (**maglev**) or **magnetic suspension** is a method by which an object is [suspended](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levitation_%28physics%29) with no support other than magnetic fields. [Magnetic force](wikipedia://en/18631) is used to counteract the effects of the [gravitational force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_force) and any other forces.[^o192] The two primary issues involved in magnetic levitation are (a)lifting forces– providing an upward force sufficient to counteract gravity, and (b)stability– ensuring that the system does not spontaneously slide or flip into a configuration where the lift is neutralized.

Magnetic levitation is used for [maglev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maglev) trains, [contactless melting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levitation_melting), [magnetic bearings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_bearing), and for product display purposes.

### Uses in material science {#Uses_in_material_science}

Magnetic field affects materials in a large number of ways.

#### Hall effect {#Hall_effect}

The charge carriers of a current-carrying conductor placed in a transverse magnetic field experience a sideways Lorentz force; this results in a charge separation in a direction perpendicular to the current and to the magnetic field. The resultant voltage in that direction is proportional to the applied magnetic field. This is known as the *Hall effect*.

The *Hall effect* is often used to measure the magnitude of a magnetic field. It is used as well to find the sign of the dominant charge carriers in materials such as semiconductors (negative electrons or positive holes).

### Largest magnitude magnetic fields {#Largest_magnitude_magnetic_fields}

The largest magnitude magnetic field produced over a macroscopic volume outside a lab setting is 2.8 kT ([VNIIEF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VNIIEF) in [Sarov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarov), [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia), 1998).[^o196] The largest magnitude magnetic field produced in a laboratory over a macroscopic volume was 1.2 kT by researchers at the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo) in 2018.[^o197] The largest magnitude microscopic magnetic fields produced in a laboratory occur in particle accelerators, such as [RHIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_Heavy_Ion_Collider), inside the collisions of heavy ions, where microscopic fields reach 10<sup>14</sup> T.[^o198][^o199] [Magnetars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetar) have the strongest known macroscopic magnetic fields of any naturally occurring object, ranging from 0.1 to 100 GT (10<sup>8</sup> to 10<sup>11</sup> T).[^o200]

## History {#History}

*Image: Descartes magnetic field.jpg.* One of the first drawings of a magnetic field, by [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525), 1644, showing the Earth attracting [lodestones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lodestone). It illustrated his theory that magnetism was caused by the circulation of tiny helical particles, "threaded parts", through threaded pores in magnets.

### Early developments {#Early_developments}

While magnets and some properties of magnetism were known to ancient societies, the research of magnetic fields began in 1269 when French scholar [Petrus Peregrinus de Maricourt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrus_Peregrinus_de_Maricourt) mapped out the magnetic field on the surface of a spherical magnet using iron needles. Noting the resulting field lines crossed at two points he named those points "poles" in analogy to Earth's poles. He also articulated the principle that magnets always have both a north and south pole, no matter how finely one slices them.[^o205]

In 1600 (almost three centuries later), [William Gilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Gilbert_%28astronomer%29) of [Colchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colchester) published *[De Magnete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Magnete)*. In *De Magnete*, Gilbert replicated Petrus Peregrinus' work and was the first to state explicitly that Earth is a magnet.[^o206] Too, he argued that electricity and magnetism were separate phenomenon.

### Magnetostatics {#Magnetostatics}

*Image: Hans Christian Ørsted, Der Geist in der Natur, 1854.tiff.* [Hans Christian Ørsted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Christian_Ørsted), *Der Geist in der Natur*, 1854

In 1750, [John Michell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Michell) stated that magnetic poles attract and repel in accordance with an [inverse square law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_square_law)[^o206]p. 56 [Charles-Augustin de Coulomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles-Augustin_de_Coulomb) experimentally verified this in 1785 and stated explicitly that north and south poles cannot be separated.[^o206]p. 59 Building on this force between poles, [Siméon Denis Poisson](wikipedia://en/44670) (1781–1840) created the first successful model of the magnetic field, which he presented in 1824.[^o206]p. 64

Three discoveries in 1820 challenged this foundation of magnetism. [Hans Christian Ørsted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Christian_Ørsted) demonstrated that a current-carrying wire is surrounded by a circular magnetic field.[^o208] Then [André-Marie Ampère](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André-Marie_Ampère) showed that parallel wires with currents attract one another if the currents are in the same direction and repel if they are in opposite directions.[^o206]p. 87[^o209] Finally, [Jean-Baptiste Biot](wikipedia://en/75109) and [Félix Savart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Félix_Savart) announced empirical results about the forces that a current-carrying long, straight wire exerted on a small magnet, determining the forces were inversely proportional to the perpendicular distance from the wire to the magnet.[^o210][^o206]p. 86 [Laplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace) later deduced a law of force based on the differential action of a differential section of the wire,[^o210][^o211] which became known as the [Biot–Savart law](wikipedia://en/75110), as Laplace did not publish his findings.[^o212]

Extending these experiments, Ampère published his own successful model of magnetism in 1825. In it, he showed the equivalence of electrical currents to magnets[^o206]p. 88 and proposed that magnetism is due to perpetually flowing loops of current instead of the dipoles of magnetic charge in Poisson's model. Further, Ampère derived both [Ampère's force law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampère's_force_law) describing the force between two currents and [Ampère's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampère's_law), which, like the Biot–Savart law, correctly described the magnetic field generated by a steady current.

### Electrodynamics {#Electrodynamics}

Also in his 1825 work, Ampère introduced the term [electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrodynamics) to describe the relationship between electricity and magnetism.[^o206]p. 88–92

In 1831, [Michael Faraday](wikipedia://en/19727) discovered [electromagnetic induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_induction) when he found that a changing magnetic field generates an encircling electric field, formulating what is now known as [Faraday's law of induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday's_law_of_induction).[^o206]p. 189–192 Later, [Franz Ernst Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Ernst_Neumann) proved that, for a moving conductor in a magnetic field, induction is a consequence of Ampère's force law.[^o206]p. 222 In the process, he introduced the magnetic vector potential, which was later shown to be equivalent to the underlying mechanism proposed by Faraday.[^o206]p. 225 In 1850, [Lord Kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Kelvin), then known as William Thomson, distinguished between two magnetic fields now denoted $\mathbf{H}$ and $\mathbf{B}$. The former applied to Poisson's model and the latter to Ampère's model and induction.[^o206]p. 224 Further, he derived how $\mathbf{H}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ relate to each other and coined the term *permeability*.[^o206]p. 245[^o214]

Between 1861 and 1865, [James Clerk Maxwell](wikipedia://en/28989696) developed and published [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737), which explained and united all of [classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_theory) electricity and magnetism. The first set of these equations was published in a paper entitled *[On Physical Lines of Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Physical_Lines_of_Force)* in 1861. These equations were valid but incomplete. Maxwell completed his set of equations in his later 1865 paper *[A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Dynamical_Theory_of_the_Electromagnetic_Field)* and demonstrated the fact that light is an [electromagnetic wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_wave). [Heinrich Hertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Hertz) published papers in 1887 and 1888 experimentally confirming this fact.[^o215][^o216]

### Modern developments {#Modern_developments}

In 1887, Tesla developed an [induction motor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induction_motor) that ran on [alternating current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_current). The motor used [polyphase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyphase_system) current, which generated a [rotating magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotating_magnetic_field) to turn the motor (a principle that Tesla claimed to have conceived in 1882).[^o218][^o219][^o220] Tesla received a patent for his electric motor in May 1888.[^o221][^o222] In 1885, [Galileo Ferraris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Ferraris) independently researched rotating magnetic fields and subsequently published his research in a paper to the *Royal Academy of Sciences* in [Turin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turin), just two months before Tesla was awarded his patent, in March 1888.[^o223]

The twentieth century showed that classical electrodynamics is already consistent with special relativity, and extended classical electrodynamics to work with quantum mechanics. [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736), in his paper of 1905 that established relativity, showed that both the electric and magnetic fields are part of the same phenomena viewed from different reference frames. Finally, the emergent field of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) was merged with electrodynamics to form [quantum electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/25268) (or QED). QED mathematically describes all [phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenon) involving [electrically charged](wikipedia://en/9804) particles interacting by means of exchange of [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) and represents the [quantum](wikipedia://en/25202) counterpart of [classical electromagnetism](wikipedia://en/426219) giving a complete account of matter and light interaction.[^o224]

- [Dynamo theory](wikipedia://en/255217) – a proposed mechanism for the creation of the Earth's magnetic field
- [Helmholtz coil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmholtz_coil) – a device for producing a region of nearly uniform magnetic field
- [Magnetic field viewing film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field_viewing_film) – Film used to view the magnetic field of an area
- [Magnetic pistol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_pistol) – a device on torpedoes or naval mines that detect the magnetic field of their target
- [Maxwell coil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell_coil) – a device for producing a large volume of an almost constant magnetic field
- [Stellar magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_magnetic_field) – a discussion of the magnetic field of stars
- [Teltron tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teltron_tube) – device used to display an electron beam and demonstrates effect of electric and magnetic fields on moving charges
