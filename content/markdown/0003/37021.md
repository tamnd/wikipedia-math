# Dirac delta function

*Image: Dirac distribution PDF.svg.* [Schematic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schematic) representation of the Dirac delta function by a line surmounted by an arrow. The height of the arrow is usually meant to specify the value of any multiplicative constant, which will give the area under the function. The other convention is to write the area next to the arrowhead.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dirac delta function"}

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), the **Dirac delta function** (or $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ **distribution**), also known as the **unit impulse**,[^n1] is a [generalized function](wikipedia://en/1580246) on the [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers), whose value is zero everywhere except at zero, where it is infinite, and whose [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) over the entire real line is equal to one.[^o3] Thus it can be represented [heuristically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic) as

$$\delta (x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x \neq 0 \\ {\infty} , & x = 0 \end{cases}$$

such that

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(x) \, dx=1.$$

:::

Since no function has this property, modelling the delta "function" rigorously involves the use of [limits](wikipedia://en/26551602) or, as is common in mathematics, [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory) and the theory of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29).

The delta function is named after physicist [Paul Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742), and has been applied routinely in physics and engineering to model point masses and concentrated loads. It is called the delta function because it is a continuous analogue of the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890) function. The mathematical rigor of the delta function was disputed until [Laurent Schwartz](wikipedia://en/317485) developed the theory of distributions, where it is defined as a linear form acting on functions.

## Motivation and overview {#Motivation_and_overview}

The [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of the Dirac delta is usually thought of as following the whole $x$-axis and the positive $y$-axis.[^n2] The Dirac delta is used to model a tall narrow spike function (an *impulse*), and other similar [abstractions](wikipedia://en/2972) such as a [point charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_charge) or [point mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_mass).[^o6] For example, to calculate the [dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_%28mechanics%29) of a [billiard ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billiard_ball) being struck, one can approximate the [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) of the impact by a Dirac delta.[^n3] In doing so, one can simplify the equations and calculate the [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_%28physics%29) of the ball by only considering the total impulse of the collision.[^n4]

In applied mathematics, the delta function is often manipulated as a kind of limit (a [weak limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_limit)) of a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of functions, each member of which has a tall spike at the origin: for example, a sequence of [Gaussian distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_distribution) centered at the origin with [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) tending to zero. (However, even in some applications, highly oscillatory functions are used as approximations to the delta function, see below.)

The Dirac delta, given the desired properties outlined above, cannot be a function with domain and range in [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438).[^n5] For example, the objects $f(x) = \delta(x)$ and $g(x) = 0$ are equal everywhere except at $x = 0$ yet have integrals that are different. According to [Lebesgue integration theory](wikipedia://en/26064288#Basic_theorems_of_the_Lebesgue_integral), if $f$ and $g$ are functions such that $f = g$ [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688), then $f$ is integrable if and only if $g$ is integrable and the integrals of $f$ and $g$ are identical.[^n6] A rigorous approach to regarding the Dirac delta function as a [mathematical object](wikipedia://en/19453961) in its own right uses [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory) or the theory of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29).[^n7]

## History {#History}

As part of his [development of quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_quantum_mechanics), [Paul Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742) introduced the $\delta$-function in a 1927 paper, subsequently popularized in his 1930 book *[The Principles of Quantum Mechanics](wikipedia://en/32975011)*.[^o13] He called it the "delta function" since he used it as a [continuum](wikipedia://en/24758677) analog of the discrete [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890).[^n8] However, it had been used by multiple mathematical scientists in the nineteenth century.[^o15] Dirac biographer [Graham Farmelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Farmelo) surmised that [Oliver Heaviside](wikipedia://en/22831) was likely a direct influence on Dirac, given Dirac's background in engineering.[^n9] Indeed, Heaviside introduced the $\delta$-function in his work on electromagnetism and [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering).[^o17] In a 1963 interview, Dirac stated, "All electrical engineers are familiar with the idea of a pulse, and the $\delta$-function is just a way of expressing a pulse mathematically."[^n10] Mathematicians refer to the same concept as a generalized function or [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) rather than a function in the ordinary sense.[^o19]

The earliest known use of the $\delta$-function is in the works of [Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Joseph_Fourier).[^n9] Fourier presented what is now called the [Fourier integral theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_integral_theorem) in his treatise Théorie analytique de la chaleur (1822) in the form:[^n11]

$$f(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\ \ d\alpha \, f(\alpha) \ \int_{-\infty}^\infty dp\ \cos (px-p\alpha)\ ,$$

which is tantamount to the introduction of the $\delta$-function in the form:[^n12]

$$\delta(x-\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty dp\ \cos (px-p\alpha) \ .$$

Later, an [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) formula for an infinitely tall, unit impulse delta function (infinitesimal version of [Cauchy distribution](wikipedia://en/7003)) explicitly appears in an 1827 text of [Augustin-Louis Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842).[^n13] Cauchy expressed the theorem using exponentials:[^o24]

$$f(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty} ^ \infty \ e^{ipx}\left(\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-ip\alpha }f(\alpha)\,d \alpha \right) \,dp.$$

Cauchy pointed out that in some circumstances the *order* of integration is significant in this result (contrast [Fubini's theorem](wikipedia://en/255245)).[^o25][^o26]

As justified using the [theory of distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29), the Cauchy equation can be rearranged to resemble Fourier's original formulation and expose the $\delta$-function as

$$\begin{aligned} f(x)&=\frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{ipx}\left(\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-ip\alpha }f(\alpha)\,d \alpha \right) \,dp \\[4pt] &=\frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left(\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{ipx} e^{-ip\alpha } \,dp \right)f(\alpha)\,d \alpha =\int_{-\infty}^\infty \delta (x-\alpha) f(\alpha) \,d \alpha, \end{aligned}$$

where the $\delta$-function is expressed as

$$\delta(x-\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{ip(x-\alpha)}\,dp \ .$$

[Siméon Denis Poisson](wikipedia://en/44670) and [Charles Hermite](wikipedia://en/22870422) introduced the $\delta$-function in their investigations of Fourier integrals.[^n14] [Gustav Kirchhoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Kirchhoff) employed it in a paper applying [Green's theorem](wikipedia://en/243334) in wave optics ([Huygens' principle](wikipedia://en/14359)).[^n10] Kirchhoff, [Hermann von Helmholtz](wikipedia://en/53311), and William Thomson ([Lord Kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Kelvin)) viewed it as the limit of a sequence of [Gaussian](wikipedia://en/245552) functions. But it was Heaviside and Dirac who first presented the $\delta$-function explicitly as an independent entity.[^n14]

A rigorous interpretation of the exponential form and the various limitations upon the function $f$ necessary for its application extended over several centuries. The problems with a classical interpretation are explained as follows: The classical notion of a function is too narrow because $f$ must [approach zero sufficiently rapidly](wikipedia://en/461986) at infinity in order for the Fourier integral to exist. For this reason, extending the classical Fourier transform to distributions substantially enlarges the class of objects that could be transformed.[^n15] Further works on the Fourier integral included contributions by [Michel Plancherel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Plancherel) (1910); [Norbert Wiener](wikipedia://en/63185), and [Salomon Bochner](wikipedia://en/987847) (around 1930); and finally [Laurent Schwartz](wikipedia://en/317485) (1945), who established a rigorous theory of distributions.[^n16]

## Definitions {#Definitions}

The Dirac delta function $\delta (x)$ can be loosely thought of as a function on the real line which is zero everywhere except at the origin, where it is infinite,

$$\delta(x) \simeq \begin{cases} +\infty, & x = 0 \\ 0, & x \ne 0 \end{cases}$$

and which is also constrained to satisfy the identity[^o34]

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \delta(x) \, dx = 1.$$

This is merely a [heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic) characterization. The Dirac delta is not a function in the traditional sense as no [extended real number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_real_number) valued function defined on the real numbers has these properties.[^n17]

::: {#o37 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Delta measure"}

### As a measure {#As_a_measure}

One way to rigorously capture the notion of the Dirac delta function is to define a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873), called [Dirac measure](wikipedia://en/1019002), which accepts a subset $A$ of the real line $\mathbb{R}$ as an argument, and returns $\delta(A) = 1$ if $0 \in A$, and $\delta(A) = 0$ if otherwise.[^n18] If the delta function is conceptualized as modeling an idealized point mass at 0, then $\delta(A)$ represents the mass contained in the set $A$. One may then define the integral against $\delta$ as the integral of a function against this mass distribution. Formally, the [Lebesgue integral](wikipedia://en/26064288) provides the necessary analytic device. The Lebesgue integral with respect to the measure $\delta$ satisfies

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) \, \delta(dx) = f(0)$$

for all continuous compactly supported functions $f$. The measure $\delta$ is not [absolutely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_continuous) with respect to the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198)—in fact, it is a [singular measure](wikipedia://en/4304500). Consequently, the delta measure has no [Radon–Nikodym derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon–Nikodym_derivative) (with respect to Lebesgue measure)—no true function for which the property

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\, \delta(x)\, dx = f(0)$$

holds.[^n19] As a result, the latter notation is a convenient [abuse of notation](wikipedia://en/1698066), and not a standard ([Riemann](wikipedia://en/26390) or [Lebesgue](wikipedia://en/26064288)) integral.[^n20]

As a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) on $\mathbb{R}$, the delta measure is characterized by its [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793), which is the [unit step function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_step_function).[^o41]

$$H(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x\ge 0\\ 0 & \text{if } x < 0. \end{cases}$$

This means that $H(x)$ is the integral of the cumulative [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) $\mathbb{1}_{(-\infty, x]}$ with respect to the measure $\delta$; to wit,

$$H(x) = \int_{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,x]}(t)\,\delta(dt) = \delta\!\left((-\infty,x]\right),$$

the latter is the measure of this interval. Thus, in particular, the integration of the delta function against a continuous function can be properly understood as a [Riemann–Stieltjes integral](wikipedia://en/330491):[^n21]

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\,\delta(dx) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) \,dH(x).$$

All higher [moments](wikipedia://en/368684) of $\delta$ are zero. In particular, [characteristic function](wikipedia://en/3229401) and [moment generating function](wikipedia://en/194634) are both equal to one.[^n22]

:::

### As a distribution {#As_a_distribution}

In the theory of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29), a generalized function is considered not a function in itself but only through how it affects other functions when "integrated" against them.[^n23] In keeping with this philosophy, to define the delta function properly, it is enough to say what the "integral" of the delta function is against a sufficiently "good" **test function** $\varphi$.[^n5] If the delta function is already understood as a measure, then the Lebesgue integral of a test function against that measure supplies the necessary integral.[^n24]

A typical space of test functions consists of all [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) on $\mathbb{R}$ with [compact support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support) that have as many derivatives as required. As a distribution, the Dirac delta is a [linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) on the space of test functions and is defined by

for every test function $\varphi$.[^n25]

For $\delta$ to be properly a distribution, it must be continuous in a suitable topology on the space of test functions. In general, for a linear functional $S$ on the space of test functions to define a distribution, it is necessary and sufficient that, for every positive integer $N$, there is an integer $M_N$ and a constant $C_N$, such that for every test function $\varphi$, one has the inequality

$$\left|S[\varphi]\right| \le C_N \sum_{k=0}^{M_N}\sup_{x\in [-N,N]} \left|\varphi^{(k)}(x)\right|$$

where $\sup$ represents the [supremum](wikipedia://en/39382). With the $\delta$ distribution, one has such an inequality (with $C_N = 1$ with $M_N = 0$ for all $N$. Thus, $\delta$ is a distribution of order zero. It is, furthermore, a distribution with compact support; the [support](wikipedia://en/381013) being $\{0\}$.[^n26]

The delta distribution can also be defined in several equivalent ways. For instance, it is the [distributional derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributional_derivative) of the [Heaviside step function](wikipedia://en/87299). This means that for every test function $\varphi$, one has

$$\delta[\varphi] = -\int_{-\infty}^\infty \varphi'(x)\,H(x)\,dx.$$

Intuitively, if [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252) were permitted, then the latter integral should simplify to

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \varphi(x)\,H'(x)\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \varphi(x)\,\delta(x)\,dx,$$

and indeed, a form of integration by parts is permitted for the Stieltjes integral, and in that case, one does have

$$-\int_{-\infty}^\infty \varphi'(x)\,H(x)\, dx = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \varphi(x)\,dH(x).$$

In the context of measure theory, the Dirac measure gives rise to a distribution by integration. Conversely, equation ((1)) defines a [Daniell integral](wikipedia://en/2904201) on the space of all compactly supported continuous functions $\varphi$ which, by the [Riesz representation theorem](wikipedia://en/38948504), can be represented as the Lebesgue integral of $\varphi$ with respect to some [Radon measure](wikipedia://en/521933).[^n27]

Generally, when the term *Dirac delta function* is used, it is in the sense of distributions rather than measures, the [Dirac measure](wikipedia://en/1019002) being among several terms for the corresponding notion in measure theory. Some sources may also use the term *Dirac delta distribution*.

The delta function can be defined in $n$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ as the measure such that

$$\int_{\mathbf{R}^n} f(\mathbf{x})\,\delta(d\mathbf{x}) = f(\mathbf{0})$$

for every compactly supported continuous function $f$. As a measure, the $n$-dimensional delta function is the [product measure](wikipedia://en/2986564) of the 1-dimensional delta functions in each variable separately. Thus, formally, with $\mathbf{x} = (x_{1}, x_{2}, ..., x_{n})$, one has[^n28]

The notion of a [Dirac measure](wikipedia://en/1019002) makes sense on any set.[^n18] Thus if $X$ is a set, $x_{0} \in X$ is a marked point, and $\Sigma$ is any [sigma algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_algebra) of subsets of $X$, then the measure defined on sets $A \in \Sigma$ by

$$\delta_{x_0}(A)=\begin{cases} 1 &\text{if }x_0\in A\\ 0 &\text{if }x_0\notin A \end{cases}$$

is the delta measure or unit mass concentrated at $x_{0}$.

Another common generalization of the delta function is to a [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) where most of its properties as a distribution can also be exploited because of the [differentiable structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable_structure). On a [locally compact Hausdorff space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact_Hausdorff_space) $X$, the Dirac delta measure concentrated at a point $x$ is the [Radon measure](wikipedia://en/521933) associated with the Daniell integral ((3)) on compactly supported continuous functions $\varphi$.[^o59] At this level of generality, calculus as such is no longer possible, however a variety of techniques from abstract analysis are available. For instance, the mapping $x_0\mapsto \delta_{x_0}$ is a continuous embedding of $X$ into the space of finite Radon measures on $X$, equipped with its [vague topology](wikipedia://en/15393951). Moreover, the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of the image of $X$ under this embedding is [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in the space of probability measures on $X$.[^n33]

## Properties {#Properties}

### Scaling and symmetry {#Scaling_and_symmetry}

The delta function satisfies the following scaling property for a non-zero scalar $\alpha$:[^o64]

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \delta(\alpha x)f(x)\,dx =\int_{-\infty}^\infty \delta(u)f(u/\alpha)\,\frac{du}{|\alpha|} =\frac{f(0)}{|\alpha|}$$

and so

In particular, the delta function is an [even](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_function) distribution (symmetry), in the sense that

$$\delta(-x) = \delta(x)$$

which is [homogeneous](wikipedia://en/622844) of degree $-1$.

::: {#o66 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Algebraic properties"}

### Algebraic properties {#Algebraic_properties}

The [distributional product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) of $\delta$ with $x$ is equal to zero:

$$x\,\delta(x) = 0.$$

More generally, $(x-a)^n\delta(x-a) =0$ for all positive integers $n$.

Conversely, if $xf(x) = xg(x)$, where $f$ and $g$ are distributions, then

$$f(x) = g(x) +c \delta(x)$$

for some constant $c$.[^n34]

:::

### Translation {#Translation}

The integral of any function multiplied by the time-delayed Dirac delta $\delta_T(t) {=} \delta(t{-}T)$ is

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(t) \,\delta(t-T)\,dt = f(T).$$

This is sometimes referred to as the *sifting property* or the *sampling property*.[^o71] The delta function is said to "sift out" the value of *f(t)* at *t* = *T*.[^o72]

It follows that the effect of [convolving](wikipedia://en/7519) a function $f(t)$ with the time-delayed Dirac delta is to time-delay $f(t)$ by the same amount:[^o73]

$$\begin{aligned} (f * \delta_T)(t) \ &\stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\ \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(\tau)\, \delta(t-T-\tau) \, d\tau \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(\tau) \,\delta(\tau-(t-T)) \,d\tau \qquad \text{since}~ \delta(-x) = \delta(x) ~~ \text{by (4)}\\ &= f(t-T). \end{aligned}$$

The sifting property holds under the precise condition that $f$ be a [tempered distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29#Tempered_distributions_and_Fourier_transform) (see the discussion of the Fourier transform below). As a special case, for instance, we have the identity (understood in the distribution sense)

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \delta (\xi-x) \delta(x-\eta) \,dx = \delta(\eta-\xi).$$

:::

### Composition with a function {#Composition_with_a_function}

More generally, the delta distribution may be [composed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29#Composition_with_a_smooth_function) with a smooth function $g(x)$ in such a way that the familiar change of variables formula holds (where $u=g(x)$), that

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}} \delta\bigl(g(x)\bigr) f\bigl(g(x)\bigr) \left|g'(x)\right| dx = \int_{g(\mathbb{R})} \delta(u)\,f(u)\,du$$

provided that $g$ is a [continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuously_differentiable) function with $g'$ nowhere zero.[^n35] That is, there is a unique way to assign meaning to the distribution $\delta\circ g$ so that this identity holds for all compactly supported test functions $f$. Therefore, the domain must be broken up to exclude the $g' = 0$ point. This distribution satisfies $\delta(g(x)) = 0$ if $g$ is nowhere zero, and otherwise if $g$ has a real [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) at $x_{0}$, then

$$\delta(g(x)) = \frac{\delta(x-x_0)}{|g'(x_0)|}.$$

It is natural therefore to define the composition $\delta(g(x))$ for continuously differentiable functions $g$ by

$$\delta(g(x)) = \sum_i \frac{\delta(x-x_i)}{|g'(x_i)|}$$

where the sum extends over all roots of $g(x)$, which are assumed to be [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_root). Thus, for example

$$\delta\left(x^2-\alpha^2\right) = \frac{1}{2|\alpha|} \Big[\delta\left(x+\alpha\right)+\delta\left(x-\alpha\right)\Big].$$

In the integral form, the generalized scaling property may be written as

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) \, \delta(g(x)) \, dx = \sum_{i}\frac{f(x_i)}{|g'(x_i)|}.$$

### Indefinite integral {#Indefinite_integral}

For a constant $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and a "well-behaved" arbitrary real-valued function $y(x)$,

$$\displaystyle{\int}y(x)\delta(x-a)dx = y(a)H(x-a) + c,$$

where $H(x)$ is the [Heaviside step function](wikipedia://en/87299) and $c$ is an integration constant.

### Properties in *n* dimensions {#Properties_in_n_dimensions}

The delta distribution in an $n$-dimensional space satisfies the following scaling property instead,

$$\delta(\alpha\boldsymbol{x}) = |\alpha|^{-n}\delta(\boldsymbol{x}) ~,$$

so that $\delta$ is a [homogeneous](wikipedia://en/622844) distribution of degree $-n$.

Under any [reflection](wikipedia://en/295829) or [rotation](wikipedia://en/410009) $\rho$, the delta function is invariant,

$$\delta(\rho \boldsymbol{x}) = \delta(\boldsymbol{x})~.$$

As in the one-variable case, it is possible to define the composition of $\delta$ with a [bi-Lipschitz function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipschitz_function)[^o78] $g: \mathbf{R}^{n} \to \mathbf{R}^{n}$ uniquely so that the following holds

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \delta(g(\boldsymbol{x}))\, f(g(\boldsymbol{x}))\left|\det g'(\boldsymbol{x})\right| d\boldsymbol{x} = \int_{g(\mathbb{R}^n)} \delta(\boldsymbol{u}) f(\boldsymbol{u})\,d\boldsymbol{u}$$

for all compactly supported functions $f$.

Using the [coarea formula](wikipedia://en/17915846) from [geometric measure theory](wikipedia://en/12169694), one can also define the composition of the delta function with a [submersion](wikipedia://en/356413) from one Euclidean space to another one of different dimension; the result is a type of [current](wikipedia://en/3021207). In the special case of a continuously differentiable function $g : \mathbf{R}^{n} \to \mathbf{R}$ such that the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) of $g$ is nowhere zero, the following identity holds[^n36]

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f(\boldsymbol{x}) \, \delta(g(\boldsymbol{x})) \,d\boldsymbol{x} = \int_{g^{-1}(0)}\frac{f(\boldsymbol{x})}{|\boldsymbol{\nabla}g|}\,d\sigma(\boldsymbol{x})$$

where the integral on the right is over $g^{-1}(0)$, the $(n - 1)$-dimensional surface defined by $g(\mathbf{x}) = 0$ with respect to the [Minkowski content](wikipedia://en/15667957) measure. This is known as a *simple layer* integral.

More generally, if $S$ is a smooth hypersurface of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$, then we can associate to $S$ the distribution that integrates any compactly supported smooth function $g$ over $S$:

$$\delta_S[g] = \int_S g(\boldsymbol{s})\,d\sigma(\boldsymbol{s})$$

where $\sigma$ is the hypersurface measure associated to $S$. This generalization is associated with the [potential theory](wikipedia://en/1431342) of [simple layer potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_layer_potential) on $S$. If $D$ is a [domain](wikipedia://en/16796643) in $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ with smooth boundary $S$, then $\delta_{S}$ is equal to the [normal derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_derivative) of the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of $D$ in the distribution sense,

$$-\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}g(\boldsymbol{x})\,\frac{\partial 1_D(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial n}\,d\boldsymbol{x}=\int_S\,g(\boldsymbol{s})\, d\sigma(\boldsymbol{s}),$$

where $n$ is the outward normal.[^n37][^n38]

In three dimensions, the delta function at $\boldsymbol{r}_0=(x_0,y_0,z_0)=(\sin\theta_0\cos\phi_0,\sin\theta_0\sin\phi_0,\cos\theta_0)r_0$ is represented in spherical coordinates by:

$$\delta(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}_0) = \begin{cases} \displaystyle\frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\delta(r-r_0) \delta(\theta-\theta_0)\delta(\phi-\phi_0),&x_0^2+y_0^2>0; \\ \displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi r^2\sin\theta}\delta(r-r_0) \delta(\theta-\theta_0),& x_0=y_0=0,\ z_0=\pm r_0\ne 0; \\ \displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi r^2}\delta(r-r_0), & x_0=y_0=z_0=r_0 = 0. \end{cases}$$

:::

## Derivatives {#Derivatives}

The derivative of the Dirac delta distribution, denoted $\delta'$ and also called the *Dirac delta prime* or *Dirac delta derivative*, is defined on compactly supported smooth test functions $\varphi$ by[^n39]

$$\delta'[\varphi] = -\delta[\varphi']=-\varphi'(0).$$

The first equality here is a kind of [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252), for if $\delta$ were a true function then

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \delta'(x)\varphi(x)\,dx = \delta(x)\varphi(x)|_{-\infty}^{\infty} -\int_{-\infty}^\infty \delta(x) \varphi'(x)\,dx = -\int_{-\infty}^\infty \delta(x) \varphi'(x)\,dx = -\varphi'(0).$$

By [mathematical induction](wikipedia://en/18881), the $k$-th derivative of $\delta$ is defined similarly as the distribution given on test functions by

$$\delta^{(k)}[\varphi] = (-1)^k \varphi^{(k)}(0).$$

In particular, $\delta$ is an infinitely differentiable distribution.

The first derivative of the delta function is the distributional limit of the difference quotients:[^n40]

$$\delta'(x) = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{\delta(x+h)-\delta(x)}{h}.$$

More properly, one has

$$\delta' = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{1}{h}(\tau_h\delta - \delta)$$

where $\tau_{h}$ is the translation operator, defined on functions by $\tau_{h}\varphi(x) = \varphi(x + h)$, and on a distribution $S$ by

$$(\tau_h S)[\varphi] = S[\tau_{-h}\varphi].$$

In the theory of [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism), the first derivative of the delta function represents a point magnetic [dipole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipole) situated at the origin. Accordingly, it is referred to as a dipole or the [doublet function](wikipedia://en/24129233).

The derivative of the delta function satisfies a number of basic properties, including:[^n41]

$$\begin{aligned} \delta'(-x) &= -\delta'(x) \\ x\delta'(x) &= -\delta(x) \end{aligned}$$

which can be shown by applying a test function and integrating by parts.

Furthermore, the convolution of $\delta'$ with a compactly-supported, smooth function $f$ is

$$\delta'*f = \delta*f' = f',$$

which follows from the properties of the distributional derivative of a convolution.

### Higher dimensions {#Higher_dimensions}

More generally, on an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) $U$ in the $n$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$, the Dirac delta distribution centered at a point $a \in U$ is defined by[^n42]

$$\delta_a[\varphi]=\varphi(a)$$

for all $\varphi \in C_c^\infty(U)$, the space of all smooth functions with compact support on $U$. If $\alpha = (\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n)$ is any [multi-index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-index) with $|\alpha|=\alpha_1+\cdots+\alpha_n$ and $\partial^\alpha$ denotes the associated mixed [partial derivative](wikipedia://en/52565) operator, then the $\alpha$-th derivative $\partial^{\alpha}\delta_{a}$ of $\delta_{a}$ is given by[^n42]

$$\left\langle \partial^\alpha \delta_{a}, \, \varphi \right\rangle = (-1)^{| \alpha |} \left\langle \delta_{a}, \partial^{\alpha} \varphi \right\rangle = (-1)^{| \alpha |} \partial^\alpha \varphi (x) \Big|_{x = a} \quad \text{ for all } \varphi \in C_c^\infty(U).$$

That is, the $\alpha$-th derivative of $\delta_{a}$ is the distribution whose value on any test function $\varphi$ is the $\alpha$-th derivative of $\varphi$ at $a$ (with the appropriate positive or negative sign).

The first partial derivatives of the delta function are thought of as [double layers](wikipedia://en/23235850) along the coordinate planes. More generally, the [normal derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_derivative) of a simple layer supported on a surface is a double layer supported on that surface and represents a laminar magnetic monopole. Higher derivatives of the delta function are known in physics as [multipoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multipole).[^o90]

Higher derivatives enter into mathematics naturally as the building blocks for the complete structure of distributions with point support. If $S$ is any distribution on $U$ supported on the set $\{a\}$ consisting of a single point, then there is an integer $m$ and coefficients $c_{\alpha}$ such that[^n42][^n43]

$$S = \sum_{|\alpha|\le m} c_\alpha \partial^\alpha\delta_a.$$

## Representations {#Representations}

The delta function can be viewed as the limit of a sequence of functions

$$\delta (x) = \lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^+} \eta_\varepsilon(x).$$

This limit is meant in a weak sense: either that

:

for all [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) functions $f$ having [compact support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support), or that this limit holds for all [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) functions $f$ with compact support. The former is convergence in the [vague topology](wikipedia://en/15393951) of measures, and the latter is convergence in the sense of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29).

::: {#o96 .statement cue=named-heading name="Approximations to the identity"}

### Approximations to the identity {#Approximations_to_the_identity}

An approximate delta function $\eta_{\varepsilon}$ can be constructed in the following manner. Let $\eta$ be an absolutely integrable function on $\mathbf{R}$ of total integral $1$, and define

$$\eta_\varepsilon(x) = \varepsilon^{-1} \eta \left (\frac{x}{\varepsilon} \right).$$

In $n$ dimensions, one uses instead the scaling

$$\eta_\varepsilon(x) = \varepsilon^{-n} \eta \left (\frac{x}{\varepsilon} \right).$$

Then a simple change of variables shows that $\eta_{\varepsilon}$ also has integral $1$. One may show that ((5)) holds for all continuous compactly supported functions $f$,[^n44] and so $\eta_{\varepsilon}$ converges weakly to $\delta$ in the sense of measures.

The $\eta_{\varepsilon}$ constructed in this way are known as an **approximation to the identity**.[^n45] This terminology is because the space $L^{1}(\mathbf{R})$ of absolutely integrable functions is closed under the operation of [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) of functions: $f \ast g \in L^{1}(\mathbf{R})$ whenever $f$ and $g$ are in $L^{1}(\mathbf{R})$. However, there is no identity in $L^{1}(\mathbf{R})$ for the convolution product: no element $h$ such that $f \ast h = f$ for all $f$. Nevertheless, the sequence $\eta_{\varepsilon}$ does approximate such an identity in the sense that

$$f*\eta_\varepsilon \to f \quad \text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.$$

This limit holds in the sense of [mean convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_convergence) (convergence in $L^{1}$). Further conditions on the $\eta_{\varepsilon}$, for instance that it be a mollifier associated to a compactly supported function,[^o99] are needed to ensure pointwise convergence [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688).

If the initial $\eta = \eta_{1}$ is itself smooth and compactly supported then the sequence is called a [mollifier](wikipedia://en/1596063). The standard mollifier is obtained by choosing $\eta$ to be a suitably normalized [bump function](wikipedia://en/815799), for instance

$$\eta(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{I_n} \exp\Big( -\frac{1}{1-|x|^2} \Big) & \text{if } |x| < 1\\ 0 & \text{if } |x|\geq 1. \end{cases}$$

($I_n$ ensuring that the total integral is 1).

In some situations such as [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506), a [piecewise linear](wikipedia://en/444224) approximation to the identity is desirable. This can be obtained by taking $\eta_{1}$ to be a [hat function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hat_function). With this choice of $\eta_{1}$, one has

$$\eta_\varepsilon(x) = \varepsilon^{-1}\max \left (1-\left|\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right|,0 \right)$$

which are all continuous and compactly supported, although not smooth and so not a mollifier.

:::

### Probabilistic considerations {#Probabilistic_considerations}

In the context of [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), it is natural to impose the additional condition that the initial $\eta_{1}$ in an approximation to the identity should be positive, as such a function then represents a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543). Convolution with a probability distribution is sometimes favorable because it does not result in [overshoot](wikipedia://en/22376472) or undershoot, as the output is a [convex combination](wikipedia://en/794534) of the input values, and thus falls between the maximum and minimum of the input function. Taking $\eta_{1}$ to be any probability distribution at all, and letting $\eta_{\varepsilon}(x) = \eta_{1}(x/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon$ as above will give rise to an approximation to the identity. In general this converges more rapidly to a delta function if, in addition, $\eta$ has mean $0$ and has small higher moments. For instance, if $\eta_{1}$ is the [uniform distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29) on $\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right]$, also known as the [rectangular function](wikipedia://en/1261615), then:[^n46]

$$\eta_\varepsilon(x) = \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\operatorname{rect}\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)= \begin{cases} \frac{1}{\varepsilon},&-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}<x<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}, \\ 0, &\text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

Another example is with the [Wigner semicircle distribution](wikipedia://en/1028321)

$$\eta_\varepsilon(x)= \begin{cases} \frac{2}{\pi \varepsilon^2}\sqrt{\varepsilon^2 - x^2}, & -\varepsilon < x < \varepsilon, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

This is continuous and compactly supported, but not a mollifier because it is not smooth.

### Semigroups {#Semigroups}

Approximations to the delta functions often arise as convolution [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799).[^o103] This amounts to the further constraint that the convolution of $\eta_{\varepsilon}$ with $\eta_{\delta}$ must satisfy

$$\eta_\varepsilon * \eta_\delta = \eta_{\varepsilon+\delta}$$

for all $\varepsilon, \delta > 0$. Convolution semigroups in $L^{1}$ that approximate the delta function are always an approximation to the identity in the above sense, however the semigroup condition is quite a strong restriction.

In practice, semigroups approximating the delta function arise as [fundamental solutions](wikipedia://en/1820858) or [Green's functions](wikipedia://en/311001) to physically motivated [elliptic](wikipedia://en/662787) or [parabolic](wikipedia://en/7466971) [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations). In the context of [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969), semigroups arise as the output of a [linear time-invariant system](wikipedia://en/1383899). Abstractly, if *A* is a linear operator acting on functions of *x*, then a convolution semigroup arises by solving the [initial value problem](wikipedia://en/498383)

$$\begin{cases} \dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\eta(t,x) = A\eta(t,x), \quad t>0 \\[5pt] \displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^+} \eta(t,x) = \delta(x) \end{cases}$$

in which the limit is as usual understood in the weak sense. Setting $\eta_{\varepsilon}(x) = \eta(\varepsilon, x)$ gives the associated approximate delta function.

Some examples of physically important convolution semigroups arising from such a fundamental solution include the following.

#### The heat kernel {#The_heat_kernel}

The [heat kernel](wikipedia://en/3904336), defined by[^n47]

$$\eta_\varepsilon(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\varepsilon}} \mathrm{e}^{-\frac{x^2}{2\varepsilon}}$$

represents the temperature in an infinite wire at time $t > 0$, if a unit of heat energy is stored at the origin of the wire at time $t = 0$. This semigroup evolves according to the one-dimensional [heat equation](wikipedia://en/166415):

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}.$$

*Image: Dirac function approximation.gif.* The Dirac delta as the limit as $a \to 0$ (in the sense of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29)) of the sequence of zero-centered [normal distributions](wikipedia://en/21462) $\delta_a(x) = \frac{1}{\left|a\right| \sqrt{\pi}} e^{-(x/a)^2}$

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), $\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)$ is a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) of [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) $\varepsilon$ and mean $0$. It represents the [probability density](wikipedia://en/43487) at time $t = \varepsilon$ of the position of a particle starting at the origin following a standard [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436). In this context, the semigroup condition is then an expression of the [Markov property](wikipedia://en/306422) of Brownian motion.

In higher-dimensional Euclidean space $\mathbf{R}^{n}$, the heat kernel is

$$\eta_\varepsilon = \frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon)^{n/2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{x\cdot x}{2\varepsilon}},$$

and has the same physical interpretation, [mutatis mutandis](wikipedia://en/742665). It also represents an approximation to the delta function in the sense that $\eta_{\varepsilon} \to \delta$ in the distribution sense as $\varepsilon \to 0$.

#### The Poisson kernel {#The_Poisson_kernel}

The [Poisson kernel](wikipedia://en/2214583)

$$\eta_\varepsilon(x) = \frac{1}{\pi}\mathrm{Im}\left\{\frac{1}{x-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon}\right\}=\frac{1}{\pi} \frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^2 + x^2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i} \xi x-|\varepsilon \xi|}\,d\xi$$

is the fundamental solution of the [Laplace equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace_equation) in the upper half-plane.[^n48] It represents the [electrostatic potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatic_potential) in a semi-infinite plate whose potential along the edge is held at fixed at the delta function. The Poisson kernel is also closely related to the [Cauchy distribution](wikipedia://en/7003) and [Epanechnikov and Gaussian kernel](wikipedia://en/8771473#Kernel_functions_in_common_use) functions.[^o108] This semigroup evolves according to the equation

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = -\left (-\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} \right)^{\frac{1}{2}}u(t,x)$$

where the operator is rigorously defined as the [Fourier multiplier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_multiplier)

$$\mathcal{F}\left[\left(-\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} \right)^{\frac{1}{2}}f\right](\xi) = |2\pi\xi|\mathcal{F}f(\xi).$$

### Oscillatory integrals {#Oscillatory_integrals}

In areas of physics such as [wave propagation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_propagation) and [wave mechanics](wikipedia://en/33516), the equations involved are [hyperbolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_partial_differential_equations) and so may have more singular solutions. As a result, the approximate delta functions that arise as fundamental solutions of the associated [Cauchy problems](wikipedia://en/1725027) are generally [oscillatory integrals](wikipedia://en/3224242). An example, which comes from a solution of the [Euler–Tricomi equation](wikipedia://en/1089161) of [transonic](wikipedia://en/247737) [gas dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_dynamics),[^n49] is the rescaled [Airy function](wikipedia://en/406896)

$$\varepsilon^{-1/3}\operatorname{Ai}\left (x\varepsilon^{-1/3} \right).$$

Although using the Fourier transform, it is easy to see that this generates a semigroup in some sense—it is not absolutely integrable and so cannot define a semigroup in the above strong sense. Many approximate delta functions constructed as oscillatory integrals only converge in the sense of distributions (an example is the [Dirichlet kernel](wikipedia://en/26611926) below), rather than in the sense of measures.

Another example is the Cauchy problem for the [wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691) in $\mathbf{R}^{1+1}$:[^n50]

$$\begin{aligned} c^{-2}\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial t^2} - \Delta u &= 0\\ u=0,\quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \delta &\qquad \text{for }t=0. \end{aligned}$$

The solution $u$ represents the displacement from equilibrium of an infinite elastic string, with an initial disturbance at the origin.

Other approximations to the identity of this kind include the [sinc function](wikipedia://en/610583) (used widely in electronics and telecommunications)

$$\eta_\varepsilon(x)=\frac{1}{\pi x}\sin\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}} \cos(kx)\,dk$$

and the [Bessel function](wikipedia://en/4700)

$$\eta_\varepsilon(x) = \frac{1}{\varepsilon}J_{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}} \left(\frac{x+1}{\varepsilon}\right).$$

### Plane wave decomposition {#Plane_wave_decomposition}

One approach to the study of a linear partial differential equation

$$L[u]=f,$$

where $L$ is a [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886) on $\mathbf{R}^{n}$, is to seek first a fundamental solution, which is a solution of the equation

$$L[u]=\delta.$$

When $L$ is particularly simple, this problem can often be resolved using the Fourier transform directly (as in the case of the Poisson kernel and heat kernel already mentioned). For more complicated operators, it is sometimes easier first to consider an equation of the form

$$L[u]=h$$

where $h$ is a [plane wave](wikipedia://en/41559) function, meaning that it has the form

$$h = h(x\cdot\xi)$$

for some vector $\xi$. Such an equation can be resolved (if the coefficients of $L$ are [analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478)) by the [Cauchy–Kovalevskaya theorem](wikipedia://en/46230112) or (if the coefficients of $L$ are constant) by quadrature. So, if the delta function can be decomposed into plane waves, then one can in principle solve linear partial differential equations.

Such a decomposition of the delta function into plane waves was part of a general technique first introduced essentially by [Johann Radon](wikipedia://en/339331), and then developed in this form by [Fritz John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_John) (1955).[^n51] Choose $k$ so that $n + k$ is an even integer, and for a real number $s$, put

$$g(s) = \operatorname{Re}\left[\frac{-s^k\log(-is)}{k!(2\pi i)^n}\right] =\begin{cases} \frac{|s|^k}{4k!(2\pi i)^{n-1}} &n \text{ odd}\\[5pt] -\frac{|s|^k\log|s|}{k!(2\pi i)^n}&n \text{ even.} \end{cases}$$

Then $\delta$ is obtained by applying a power of the [Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian) to the integral with respect to the unit [sphere measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphere_measure) $d\omega$ of $g(x \cdot \xi)$ for $\xi$ in the [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090) $S^{n-1}$:

$$\delta(x) = \Delta_x^{(n+k)/2} \int_{S^{n-1}} g(x\cdot\xi)\,d\omega_\xi.$$

The Laplacian here is interpreted as a weak derivative, so that this equation is taken to mean that, for any test function $\varphi$,

$$\varphi(x) = \int_{\mathbf{R}^n}\varphi(y)\,dy\,\Delta_x^{\frac{n+k}{2}} \int_{S^{n-1}} g((x-y)\cdot\xi)\,d\omega_\xi.$$

The result follows from the formula for the [Newtonian potential](wikipedia://en/814816) (the fundamental solution of Poisson's equation). This is essentially a form of the inversion formula for the [Radon transform](wikipedia://en/503378) because it recovers the value of $\varphi(x)$ from its integrals over hyperplanes.[^n52] For instance, if $n$ is odd and $k = 1$, then the integral on the right hand side is

$$\begin{aligned} & c_n \Delta^{\frac{n+1}{2}}_x\iint_{S^{n-1}} \varphi(y)|(y-x) \cdot \xi| \, d\omega_\xi \, dy \\[5pt] & \qquad = c_n \Delta^{(n+1)/2}_x \int_{S^{n-1}} \, d\omega_\xi \int_{-\infty}^\infty |p| R\varphi(\xi,p+x\cdot\xi)\,dp \end{aligned}$$

where $R\varphi(\xi, p)$ is the Radon transform of $\varphi$:

$$R\varphi(\xi,p) = \int_{x\cdot\xi=p} f(x)\,d^{n-1}x.$$

An alternative equivalent expression of the plane wave decomposition is:[^n53]

$$\delta(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{(n-1)!}{(2\pi i)^n}\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}(x\cdot\xi)^{-n} \, d\omega_\xi & n\text{ even} \\ \frac{1}{2(2\pi i)^{n-1}}\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}\delta^{(n-1)}(x\cdot\xi)\,d\omega_\xi & n\text{ odd}. \end{cases}$$

### Fourier transform {#Fourier_transform}

The delta function is a [tempered distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29#Tempered_distributions_and_Fourier_transform), and therefore it has a well-defined [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247). Formally, one finds[^o117]

$$\widehat{\delta}(\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-2\pi i x \xi} \,\delta(x)dx = 1.$$

Properly speaking, the Fourier transform of a distribution is defined by imposing [self-adjointness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-adjoint) of the Fourier transform under the [duality pairing](wikipedia://en/63735167) $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ of tempered distributions with [Schwartz functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwartz_functions). Thus $\widehat{\delta}$ is defined as the unique tempered distribution satisfying

$$\langle\widehat{\delta},\varphi\rangle = \langle\delta,\widehat{\varphi}\rangle$$

for all Schwartz functions $\varphi$. And indeed it follows from this that $\widehat{\delta}=1.$

As a result of this identity, the [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) of the delta function with any other tempered distribution $S$ is simply $S$:

$$S*\delta = S.$$

That is to say that $\delta$ is an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) for the convolution on tempered distributions, and in fact, the space of compactly supported distributions under convolution is an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) with identity the delta function. This property is fundamental in [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324), as convolution with a tempered distribution is a [linear time-invariant system](wikipedia://en/1383899), and applying the linear time-invariant system measures its [impulse response](wikipedia://en/545825). The impulse response can be computed to any desired degree of accuracy by choosing a suitable approximation for $\delta$, and once it is known, it characterizes the system completely. See LTI system theory .

The inverse Fourier transform of the tempered distribution $f(\xi) = 1$ is the delta function. Formally, this is expressed as

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty 1 \cdot e^{2\pi i x\xi}\,d\xi = \delta(x)$$

and more rigorously, it follows since

$$\langle 1, \widehat{f}\rangle = f(0) = \langle\delta,f\rangle$$

for all Schwartz functions $f$.

In these terms, the delta function provides a suggestive statement of the orthogonality property of the Fourier kernel on $\mathbf{R}$. Formally, one has

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{i 2\pi \xi_1 t} \left[e^{i 2\pi \xi_2 t}\right]^*\,dt = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{i 2\pi (\xi_1 - \xi_2) t} \,dt = \delta(\xi_1 - \xi_2).$$

This is, of course, shorthand for the assertion that the Fourier transform of the tempered distribution

$$f(t) = e^{i2\pi\xi_1 t}$$

is

$$\widehat{f}(\xi_2) = \delta(\xi_1-\xi_2)$$

which again follows by imposing self-adjointness of the Fourier transform.

By [analytic continuation](wikipedia://en/80842) of the Fourier transform, the [Laplace transform](wikipedia://en/18610) of the delta function is found to be[^n54]

$$\int_{0}^{\infty}\delta(t-a)\,e^{-st} \, dt=e^{-sa}.$$

#### Fourier kernels {#Fourier_kernels}

In the study of [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038), a major question consists of determining whether and in what sense the Fourier series associated with a [periodic function](wikipedia://en/76848) converges to the function. The $n$-th partial sum of the Fourier series of a function $f$ of period $2\pi$ is defined by convolution (on the interval −π,π) with the [Dirichlet kernel](wikipedia://en/26611926):

$$D_N(x) = \sum_{n=-N}^N e^{inx} = \frac{\sin\left(\left(N+\frac12\right)x\right)}{\sin(x/2)}.$$

Thus,

$$s_N(f)(x) = D_N*f(x) = \sum_{n=-N}^N a_n e^{inx}$$

where

$$a_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(y)e^{-iny}\,dy.$$

A fundamental result of elementary Fourier series states that the Dirichlet kernel restricted to the interval −π,π tends to a multiple of the delta function as $N \to \infty$. This is interpreted in the distribution sense, that

$$s_N(f)(0) = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} D_N(x)f(x)\,dx \to 2\pi f(0)$$

for every compactly supported smooth function $f$. Thus, formally one has

$$\delta(x) = \frac1{2\pi} \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty e^{inx}$$

on the interval −π,π.

Despite this, the result does not hold for all compactly supported continuous functions: that is $D_{N}$ does not converge weakly in the sense of measures. The lack of convergence of the Fourier series has led to the introduction of a variety of [summability methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summability_methods) to produce convergence. The method of [Cesàro summation](wikipedia://en/705600) leads to the [Fejér kernel](wikipedia://en/693816)[^n55]

$$F_N(x) = \frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} D_n(x) = \frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{\sin \frac{Nx}{2}}{\sin \frac{x}{2}}\right)^2.$$

The [Fejér kernels](wikipedia://en/693816) tend to the delta function in a stronger sense that[^o121]

$$\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} F_N(x)f(x)\,dx \to 2\pi f(0)$$

for every compactly supported continuous function $f$. The implication is that the Fourier series of any continuous function is Cesàro summable to the value of the function at every point.

### Hilbert space theory {#Hilbert_space_theory}

The Dirac delta distribution is a [densely defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Densely_defined) [unbounded](wikipedia://en/1422584) [linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) on the [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) [L<sup>2</sup>](wikipedia://en/45194) of [square-integrable functions](wikipedia://en/28680558).[^n56] Indeed, smooth compactly supported functions are [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in $L^{2}$, and the action of the delta distribution on such functions is well-defined. In many applications, it is possible to identify subspaces of $L^{2}$ and to give a stronger [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) on which the delta function defines a [bounded linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_functional).

#### Sobolev spaces {#Sobolev_spaces}

The [Sobolev embedding theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sobolev_embedding_theorem) for [Sobolev spaces](wikipedia://en/611964) on the real line $\mathbf{R}$ implies that any square-integrable function $f$ such that

$$\|f\|_{H^1}^2 = \int_{-\infty}^\infty |\widehat{f}(\xi)|^2 (1+|\xi|^2)\,d\xi < \infty$$

is automatically continuous, and satisfies in particular

$$\delta[f]=|f(0)| < C \|f\|_{H^1}.$$

Thus $\delta$ is a bounded linear functional on the Sobolev space $H^{1}$.[^n57] Equivalently $\delta$ is an element of the [continuous dual space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_dual_space) $H^{-1}$ of $H^{1}$. More generally, in $n$ dimensions, one has $\delta \in H^{-s}(\mathbf{R}^{n})$ provided $s > \tfrac{n}{2}$.

#### Spaces of holomorphic functions {#Spaces_of_holomorphic_functions}

In [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), the delta function enters via [Cauchy's integral formula](wikipedia://en/72827), which asserts that if $D$ is a domain in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) with smooth boundary, then

$$f(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_{\partial D} \frac{f(\zeta)\,d\zeta}{\zeta-z},\quad z\in D$$

for all [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110) $f$ in $D$ that are continuous on the closure of $D$. As a result, the delta function $\delta_{z}$ is represented in this class of holomorphic functions by the Cauchy integral:

$$\delta_z[f] = f(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_{\partial D} \frac{f(\zeta)\,d\zeta}{\zeta-z}.$$

Moreover, let $H^{2}(\partial D)$ be the [Hardy space](wikipedia://en/314780) consisting of the closure in $L^{2}(\partial D)$ of all holomorphic functions in $D$ continuous up to the boundary of $D$. Then functions in $H^{2}(\partial D)$ uniquely extend to holomorphic functions in $D$, and the Cauchy integral formula continues to hold. In particular for $z \in D$, the delta function $\delta_{z}$ is a continuous linear functional on $H^{2}(\partial D)$. This is a special case of the situation in [several complex variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Several_complex_variables) in which, for smooth domains $D$, the [Szegő kernel](wikipedia://en/23513282) plays the role of the Cauchy integral.[^n58]

Another representation of the delta function in a space of holomorphic functions is on the space $H(D)\cap L^2(D)$ of square-integrable holomorphic functions in an open set $D\subset\mathbb C^n$. This is a closed subspace of $L^2(D)$, and therefore is a Hilbert space. On the other hand, the functional that evaluates a holomorphic function in $H(D)\cap L^2(D)$ at a point $z$ of $D$ is a continuous functional, and so by the Riesz representation theorem, is represented by integration against a kernel $K_z(\zeta)$, the [Bergman kernel](wikipedia://en/3205923).[^n59] This kernel is the analog of the delta function in this Hilbert space. A Hilbert space having such a kernel is called a [reproducing kernel Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/651196). In the special case of the unit disc, one has

$$\delta_w[f] = f(w) = \frac1\pi\iint_{|z|<1} \frac{f(z)\,dx\,dy}{(1-\bar zw)^2}.$$

::: {#o130 .statement cue=named-heading name="Resolutions of the identity"}

#### Resolutions of the identity {#Resolutions_of_the_identity}

Given a complete [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) set of functions $\{\varphi_{n}\}$ in a separable Hilbert space, for example, the normalized [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) of a [compact self-adjoint operator](wikipedia://en/6690773#Spectral_theorem), any vector $f$ can be expressed as

$$f = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \alpha_n \varphi_n.$$

The coefficients {α<sub>n</sub>} are found as

$$\alpha_n = \langle \varphi_n, f \rangle,$$

which may be represented by the notation:

$$\alpha_n = \varphi_n^\dagger f,$$

a form of the [bra–ket notation](wikipedia://en/4542) of Dirac.[^n60] Adopting this notation, the expansion of $f$ takes the [dyadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyadic_tensor) form:[^n61]

$$f = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \varphi_n \left ( \varphi_n^\dagger f \right).$$

Letting $I$ denote the [identity operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_operator) on the Hilbert space, the expression

$$I = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \varphi_n \varphi_n^\dagger,$$

is called a [resolution of the identity](wikipedia://en/876294#Resolution_of_the_identity). When the Hilbert space is the space $L^{2}(D)$ of square-integrable functions on a domain $D$, the quantity:

$$\varphi_n \varphi_n^\dagger,$$

is an integral operator, and the expression for $f$ can be rewritten

$$f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \int_D\, \left( \varphi_n (x) \varphi_n^*(\xi)\right) f(\xi) \, d \xi.$$

The right-hand side converges to $f$ in the $L^{2}$ sense. It need not hold in a pointwise sense, even when $f$ is a continuous function. Nevertheless, it is common to abuse notation and write

$$f(x) = \int \, \delta(x-\xi) f (\xi)\, d\xi,$$

resulting in the representation of the delta function:[^n62]

$$\delta(x-\xi) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \varphi_n (x) \varphi_n^*(\xi).$$

With a suitable [rigged Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/393048) $(\Phi, L^{2}(D), \Phi*)$ where $\Phi \subset L^{2}(D)$ contains all compactly supported smooth functions, this summation may converge in $\Phi*$, depending on the properties of the basis $\varphi_{n}$. In most cases of practical interest, the orthonormal basis comes from an integral or differential operator (e.g. the [heat kernel](wikipedia://en/3904336)), in which case the series converges in the [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29#Distributions) sense.[^n63]

:::

### Infinitesimal delta functions {#Infinitesimal_delta_functions}

[Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy) used an infinitesimal $\alpha$ to write down a unit impulse, infinitely tall and narrow Dirac-type delta function $\delta_{\alpha}$ satisfying $\int F(x)\delta_\alpha(x) \,dx = F(0)$ in a number of articles in 1827.[^n64] Cauchy defined an infinitesimal in *[Cours d'Analyse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cours_d'Analyse)* (1827) in terms of a sequence tending to zero. Namely, such a null sequence becomes an infinitesimal in Cauchy's and [Lazare Carnot](wikipedia://en/26532764)'s terminology.

[Non-standard analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-standard_analysis) allows one to rigorously treat infinitesimals. The article by (Yamashita 2007) contains a bibliography on modern Dirac delta functions in the context of an infinitesimal-enriched continuum provided by the [hyperreals](wikipedia://en/51429). Here the Dirac delta can be given by an actual function, having the property that for every real function $F$ one has $\int F(x)\delta_\alpha(x) \, dx = F(0)$ as anticipated by Fourier and Cauchy.[^n65]

:::

## Dirac comb {#Dirac_comb}

*Image: Dirac comb.svg.* A Dirac comb is an infinite series of Dirac delta functions spaced at intervals of $T$

A so-called uniform "pulse train" of Dirac delta measures, which is known as a [Dirac comb](wikipedia://en/1314272), or as the [Sha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sha_%28Cyrillic%29) distribution, creates a [sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampling_%28signal_processing%29) function, often used in [digital signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_processing) (DSP) and discrete time signal analysis. The Dirac comb is given as the [infinite sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_sum), whose limit is understood in the distribution sense,

$$\operatorname{\text{Ш}}(x) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty \delta(x-n),$$

which is a sequence of point masses at each of the integers.[^n66]

Up to an overall normalizing constant, the Dirac comb is equal to its own Fourier transform. This is significant because if $f$ is any [Schwartz function](wikipedia://en/4277567), then the [periodization](wikipedia://en/25329528) of $f$ is given by the convolution

$$(f * \operatorname{\text{Ш}})(x) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty f(x-n).$$

In particular,

$$(f*\operatorname{\text{Ш}})^\wedge = \widehat{f}\widehat{\operatorname{\text{Ш}}} = \widehat{f}\operatorname{\text{Ш}}$$

is precisely the [Poisson summation formula](wikipedia://en/502042).[^n67][^n68] More generally, this formula remains to be true if $f$ is a tempered distribution of rapid descent or, equivalently, if $\widehat{f}$ is a slowly growing, ordinary function within the space of tempered distributions.

::: {#o144 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem"}

## Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem {#Sokhotski–Plemelj_theorem}

The [Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem](wikipedia://en/12237167), important in quantum mechanics, relates the delta function to the distribution $p.v. \tfrac{1}{x}$, the [Cauchy principal value](wikipedia://en/408195) of the function $\tfrac{1}{x}$, defined by

$$\left\langle\operatorname{p.v.}\frac{1}{x}, \varphi\right\rangle = \lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^+}\int_{|x|>\varepsilon} \frac{\varphi(x)}{x}\,dx.$$

Sokhotsky's formula states that[^n69]

$$\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^+} \frac{1}{x\pm i\varepsilon} = \operatorname{p.v.}\frac{1}{x} \mp i\pi\delta(x),$$

Here the limit is understood in the distribution sense, that for all compactly supported smooth functions $f$,

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^{+}}\frac{f(x)}{x\pm i\varepsilon}\,dx=\mp i\pi f(0)+\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^{+}}\int_{|x|>\varepsilon}\frac{f(x)}{x}\,dx.$$

:::

## Relationship to the Kronecker delta {#Relationship_to_the_Kronecker_delta}

The [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890) $\delta_{ij}$ is the quantity defined by

$$\delta_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & i=j\\ 0 &i\not=j \end{cases}$$

for all integers $i$, $j$. This function then satisfies the following analog of the sifting property: if $a_{i}$ (for $i$ in the set of all integers) is any [doubly infinite sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_sequence#Doubly-infinite_sequences), then

$$\sum_{i=-\infty}^\infty a_i \delta_{ik}=a_k.$$

Similarly, for any real or complex valued continuous function $f$ on $\mathbf{R}$, the Dirac delta satisfies the sifting property

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\delta(x-x_0)\,dx=f(x_0).$$

This exhibits the Kronecker delta function as a discrete analog of the Dirac delta function.[^n70]

## Applications {#Applications}

### Probability theory {#Probability_theory}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the Dirac delta function is often used to represent a [discrete distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_distribution), or a partially discrete, partially [continuous distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_distribution), using a [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) (which is normally used to represent absolutely continuous distributions). For example, the probability density function $f(x)$ of a discrete distribution consisting of points $\mathbf{x} = \{x_{1}, ..., x_{n}\}$, with corresponding probabilities $p_{1}, ..., p_{n}$, can be written as[^o151]

$$f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n p_i \delta(x-x_i).$$

As another example, consider a distribution in which 6/10 of the time returns a standard [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462), and 4/10 of the time returns exactly the value 3.5 (i.e. a partly continuous, partly discrete [mixture distribution](wikipedia://en/708242)). The density function of this distribution can be written as

$$f(x) = 0.6 \, \frac {1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}} + 0.4 \, \delta(x-3.5).$$

The delta function is also used to represent the resulting probability density function of a random variable that is transformed by continuously differentiable function. If $Y = g(X)$ is a continuous differentiable function, then the density of $Y$ can be written as

$$f_Y(y) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} f_X(x) \delta(y-g(x)) \,dx.$$

The delta function is also used in a completely different way to represent the [local time](wikipedia://en/5678101) of a [diffusion process](wikipedia://en/1667059) (like [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436)).[^n71] The local time of a stochastic process $B(t)$ is given by

$$\ell(x,t) = \int_0^t \delta(x-B(s))\,ds$$

and represents the amount of time that the process spends at the point $x$ in the range of the process. More precisely, in one dimension this integral can be written

$$\ell(x,t) = \lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^+}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_0^t \mathbf{1}_{[x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon]}(B(s))\,ds$$

where $\mathbf{1}_{[x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon]}$ is the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of the interval $[x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon].$

### Quantum mechanics {#Quantum_mechanics}

The delta function is expedient in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202). The [wave function](wikipedia://en/145343) of a particle gives the [probability amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_amplitude) of finding a particle within a given region of space. Wave functions are assumed to be elements of the Hilbert space $L^{2}$ of [square-integrable functions](wikipedia://en/28680558), and the total probability of finding a particle within a given interval is the integral of the magnitude of the wave function squared over the interval. A set $\{\lvert \varphi_{n}\rangle\}$ of wave functions is orthonormal if

$$\langle\varphi_n \mid \varphi_m\rangle = \delta_{nm},$$

where $\delta_{nm}$ is the Kronecker delta. A set of orthonormal wave functions is complete in the space of square-integrable functions if any wave function $\lvert \psi\rangle$ can be expressed as a linear combination of the $\{\lvert \varphi_{n}\rangle\}$ with complex coefficients:

$$\psi = \sum c_n \varphi_n,$$

where $c_{n} = \langle \varphi_{n}\mid \psi\rangle$. Complete orthonormal systems of wave functions appear naturally as the [eigenfunctions](wikipedia://en/228601) of the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381) (of a [bound system](wikipedia://en/696912)) in quantum mechanics that measures the energy levels, which are called the eigenvalues. The set of eigenvalues, in this case, is known as the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137) of the Hamiltonian. In [bra–ket notation](wikipedia://en/4542) this equality implies the [resolution of the identity](wikipedia://en/876294#Resolution_of_the_identity):

$$I = \sum |\varphi_n\rangle\langle\varphi_n|.$$

Here the eigenvalues are assumed to be discrete, but the set of eigenvalues of an [observable](wikipedia://en/294248) can also be continuous. An example is the [position operator](wikipedia://en/4838571), $Q\psi(x) = x\psi(x)$. The spectrum of the position (in one dimension) is the entire real line and is called a [continuous spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_%28physical_sciences%29#In_quantum_mechanics). However, unlike the Hamiltonian, the position operator lacks proper eigenfunctions. The conventional way to overcome this shortcoming is to widen the class of available functions by allowing distributions as well, i.e., to replace the Hilbert space with a [rigged Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/393048).[^n72] In this context, the position operator has a complete set of *generalized eigenfunctions*,[^n73] labeled by the points $y$ of the real line, given by

$$\varphi_y(x) = \delta(x-y).$$

The generalized eigenfunctions of the position operator are called the *eigenkets* and are denoted by $\varphi_{y} = \lvert y\rangle$.[^n74]

Similar considerations apply to any other [(unbounded) self-adjoint operator](wikipedia://en/123495#Unbounded_self-adjoint_operators) with continuous spectrum and no degenerate eigenvalues, such as the [momentum operator](wikipedia://en/2288549) $P$. In that case, there is a set $\Omega$ of real numbers (the spectrum) and a collection of distributions $\varphi_{y}$ with $y \in \Omega$ such that

$$P\varphi_y = y\varphi_y.$$

That is, $\varphi_{y}$ are the generalized eigenvectors of $P$. If they form an "orthonormal basis" in the distribution sense, that is:

$$\langle \varphi_y,\varphi_{y'}\rangle = \delta(y-y'),$$

then for any test function $\psi$,

$$\psi(x) = \int_\Omega c(y) \varphi_y(x) \, dy$$

where $c(y) = \langle \psi, \varphi_{y}\rangle$. That is, there is a resolution of the identity

$$I = \int_\Omega |\varphi_y\rangle\, \langle\varphi_y|\,dy$$

where the operator-valued integral is again understood in the weak sense. If the spectrum of $P$ has both continuous and discrete parts, then the resolution of the identity involves a summation over the discrete spectrum and an integral over the continuous spectrum.

The delta function also has many more specialized applications in quantum mechanics, such as the [delta potential](wikipedia://en/6308405) models for a single and double potential well.

### Structural mechanics {#Structural_mechanics}

The delta function can be used in [structural mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_mechanics) to describe transient loads or point loads acting on structures. The governing equation of a simple [mass–spring system](wikipedia://en/13899) excited by a sudden force [impulse](wikipedia://en/211922) $I$ at time $t = 0$ can be written[^n75][^n76]

$$m \frac{d^2 \xi}{dt^2} + k \xi = I \delta(t),$$

where $m$ is the mass, $\xi$ is the deflection, and $k$ is the [spring constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_constant).

As another example, the equation governing the static deflection of a slender [beam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_%28structure%29) is, according to [Euler–Bernoulli theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Bernoulli_beam_equation),

$$EI \frac{d^4 w}{dx^4} = q(x),$$

where $EI$ is the [bending stiffness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bending_stiffness) of the beam, $w$ is the [deflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflection_%28engineering%29), $x$ is the spatial coordinate, and $q(x)$ is the load distribution. If a beam is loaded by a point force $F$ at $x = x_{0}$, the load distribution is written

$$q(x) = F \delta(x-x_0).$$

As the integration of the delta function results in the [Heaviside step function](wikipedia://en/87299), it follows that the static deflection of a slender beam subject to multiple point loads is described by a set of piecewise [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000).

Also, a point [moment](wikipedia://en/6018334) acting on a beam can be described by delta functions. Consider two opposing point forces $F$ at a distance $d$ apart. They then produce a moment $M = Fd$ acting on the beam. Now, let the distance $d$ approach the [limit](wikipedia://en/285759) zero, while $M$ is kept constant. The load distribution, assuming a clockwise moment acting at $x = 0$, is written

$$\begin{aligned} q(x) &= \lim_{d \to 0} \Big( F \delta(x) - F \delta(x-d) \Big) \\[4pt] &= \lim_{d \to 0} \left( \frac{M}{d} \delta(x) - \frac{M}{d} \delta(x-d) \right) \\[4pt] &= M \lim_{d \to 0} \frac{\delta(x) - \delta(x - d)}{d}\\[4pt] &= M \delta'(x). \end{aligned}$$

Point moments can thus be represented by the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of the delta function. Integration of the beam equation again results in piecewise [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) deflection.
