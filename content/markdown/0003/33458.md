# Well-ordering theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Well-ordering theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **well-ordering theorem**, also known as **Zermelo's theorem**, states that every [set](wikipedia://en/26691) can be [well-ordered](wikipedia://en/33456). A set *X* is *well-ordered* by a [strict total order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strict_total_order) if every non-empty subset of *X* has a [least element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_element) under the ordering. The well-ordering theorem together with [Zorn's lemma](wikipedia://en/51442) are the most important mathematical statements that are equivalent to the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) (often called AC, see also Axiom of choice).[^o2][^o3] [Ernst Zermelo](wikipedia://en/178655) introduced the axiom of choice as an "unobjectionable logical principle" to prove the well-ordering theorem.[^o4] One can conclude from the well-ordering theorem that every set is susceptible to [transfinite induction](wikipedia://en/30461), which is considered by mathematicians to be a powerful technique.[^o4]

:::

## History {#History}

[Georg Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216) considered the well-ordering theorem to be a "fundamental principle of thought".[^o6] However, it is considered difficult or even impossible to visualize a well-ordering of $\mathbb{R}$, the set of all [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438); such a visualization would have to incorporate the axiom of choice.[^o7] In 1904, [Gyula Kőnig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyula_Kőnig) claimed to have proven that such a well-ordering cannot exist. A few weeks later, [Felix Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/10989) found a mistake in the proof.[^o8] It turned out, though, that in [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) the well-ordering theorem is equivalent to the axiom of choice, in the sense that the [Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo–Fraenkel_axioms) with the axiom of choice included are sufficient to prove the well-ordering theorem, and conversely, the Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms without the axiom of choice but with the well-ordering theorem included are sufficient to prove the axiom of choice. (The same applies to [Zorn's lemma](wikipedia://en/51442).) In [second-order logic](wikipedia://en/341428), however, the well-ordering theorem is strictly stronger than the axiom of choice: from the well-ordering theorem one may deduce the axiom of choice, but from the axiom of choice one cannot deduce the well-ordering theorem.[^o9] There is a well-known joke about the three statements, and their relative amenability to intuition:

> The axiom of choice is obviously true, the well-ordering principle obviously false, and who can tell about [Zorn's lemma](wikipedia://en/51442)?[^o10]

::: {#o12 .proof cue=heading name="Proof from axiom of choice"}

## Proof from axiom of choice {#Proof_from_axiom_of_choice}

The well-ordering theorem follows from the axiom of choice as follows.[^o13]

> Let the set we are trying to well-order be $A$, and let $f$ be a choice function for the family of non-empty subsets of $A$. For every [ordinal](wikipedia://en/26547932) $\alpha$, define by [transfinite recursion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfinite_recursion) an element $a_\alpha$ that is in $A$ by setting $a_\alpha\ =\ f(A\setminus\{a_\xi\mid\xi<\alpha\})$ if this complement $A\setminus\{a_\xi\mid\xi<\alpha\}$ is nonempty, or leaves $a_\alpha$ undefined if the complement is empty. That is, $a_\alpha$ is chosen from the set of elements of $A$ that have not yet been assigned a place in the ordering (or undefined if the entirety of $A$ has been successfully enumerated). Then the order $<$ on $A$ defined by $a_\alpha < a_\beta$ if and only if $\alpha<\beta$ (in the usual well-order of the ordinals) is a well-order of $A$ as desired, of order type $\{\alpha \mid a_\alpha\text{ is defined}\}$ (in the [von Neumann representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Neumann_ordinals)).

:::

::: {#o15 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of axiom of choice"}

## Proof of axiom of choice {#Proof_of_axiom_of_choice}

The axiom of choice can be proven from the well-ordering theorem as follows.

To make a choice function for a collection of non-empty sets, $E$, take the union of the sets in $E$ and call it $X$. There exists a well-ordering of $X$; let $R$ be such an ordering. The function that to each set $S$ of $E$ associates the smallest element of $S$, as ordered by (the restriction to $S$ of) $R$, is a choice function for the collection $E$.

An essential point of this proof is that it involves only a single arbitrary choice, that of $R$; applying the well-ordering theorem to each member $S$ of $E$ separately would not work, since the theorem only asserts the existence of a well-ordering, and choosing for each $S$ a well-ordering would require just as many choices as simply choosing an element from each $S$.

:::
