# Demographics of Ukraine

*Image: Ukraine population pyramid 1950-2026.gif.* Animated population pyramid from 1950 to 2026

*Image: Population density in Ukraine.png.* Ukrainian population density by [raion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_raions_of_Ukraine)

::: {#o4 .subject cue=lead name="Demographics of Ukraine"}

According to the [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations), [Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukraine) has a population of 36.7 million as of 2026.[^o5]

:::

In July 2023, Reuters reported that due to refugee outflows, the population of [Ukrainian-controlled areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_control_during_the_Russo-Ukrainian_War) may have decreased to 28 million,[^o6] a steep decline from Ukraine's 2020 population of almost 42 million.[^o7][^o8]

This drop is in large part due to the ongoing [Ukrainian refugee crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_refugee_crisis) and [loss of territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian-occupied_territories_of_Ukraine) caused by [Russo-Ukrainian war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Ukrainian_war_%282022–present%29), which resulted in mass emigration. The demographic decline is also affected by a very low [birth rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_rate) and a high [death rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_rate). The most recent (and only) census of post-Soviet Ukraine occurred [in 2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_Ukrainian_census), and much of the information presented is potentially inaccurate or outdated.[^o9]

Since 2021, the Ukrainian fertility rate has fallen below 1.3, and is now [one of the lowest in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_total_fertility_rate). By 2024, it was one of the ten lowest in the world, falling under 1.00.[^o10]

## History {#History}

### Historical population {#Historical_population}

There were roughly four million Ukrainians at the end of the 17th century.[^o13] However, population estimates from this period are approximate and may vary depending on methodology and available records.

The estimated population figures for Ukraine in the early 20th century are based on various sources. The 1931 population statistics were estimated by Professor Zenon Kuzelia,[^o14] as an official census was not conducted in Soviet Ukraine during that year.

## Famines and migration {#Famines_and_migration}

### Demographic impact of the Holodomor and World War II {#Demographic_impact_of_the_Holodomor_and_World_War_II}

The [Ukrainian famines of the 1930s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holodomor) and the devastation of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) created a demographic catastrophe for Ukraine. In 1933, life expectancy at birth dropped to as low as 10 years for females and 7 years for males. During 1941–1944, life expectancy plateaued around 25 years for females and 15 years for males.[^o24]

According to *The Oxford Companion to World War II*, over 7 million Ukrainians — more than one-sixth of the pre-war population — were killed during the conflict.[^o25]

### Migration trends in independent Ukraine {#Migration_trends_in_independent_Ukraine}

Following Ukraine's independence, significant migration occurred:

- 1991–1992: Over 1 million people moved into Ukraine, primarily from other former Soviet republics.
- 1991–2004: A total of 2.2 million immigrants arrived in Ukraine, with 2 million of these coming from other former Soviet Union states.
- Between 1991 and 2004, 2.5 million emigrated from Ukraine, with 1.9 million migrating to other former Soviet Union republics.[^o27]

As of 2015, immigrants made up an estimated 11.4% of the total population of Ukraine, equating to 4.8 million people.[^o28]

### Ukrainian diaspora {#Ukrainian_diaspora}

In 2006, there were approximately 1.2 million Canadians of Ukrainian descent,[^o30] giving Canada the third-largest Ukrainian population worldwide, behind Ukraine and Russia. Significant Ukrainian diaspora communities also exist in Poland, the United States, Brazil, Kazakhstan, and Argentina.

### Recent migration trends and economic impact {#Recent_migration_trends_and_economic_impact}

Since about 2015, a growing number of Ukrainians have worked in the European Union, particularly in Poland. According to Eurostat, 662,000 Ukrainians received EU residence permits in 2017, with 585,439 of these residing in Poland.

World Bank statistics from 2019 revealed that remittances sent back to Ukraine had approximately doubled from 2015 to 2018, making up about 4% of Ukraine's GDP.[^o32][^o33]

Ukraine records only citizens who apply for foreign citizenship, not those who seek foreign residency.[^o34]

### Migration during the Russo-Ukrainian war {#Migration_during_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war}

After the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine), approximately 9 million people fled Ukraine during the ensuing Ukrainian refugee crisis, Europe's largest refugee crisis since World War II. Most refugees sought asylum in Central Europe.

## Population decline {#Population_decline}

*Image: Population of Ukraine from 1950z.svg.* Population of Ukraine from 1950[^o38][^o39]

According to estimates by the State Statistics Service of Ukraine, the population of Ukraine (excluding Crimea) on 1 May 2021 was 41,442,615.[^o306]

The country's population has been declining since the 1990s because of a high emigration rate, coupled with high death rates and low birth rates. The population has been shrinking by an average of over 300,000 annually since 1993.

In 2007, the country's rate of population decline was the fourth highest in the world.[^o40] But between 2008 and 2010, over 1.5 million children were born in Ukraine, compared with fewer than 1.2 million in 1999–2001. In 2008, Ukraine posted record-breaking birth rates not seen since its 1991 independence. Infant mortality rates also dropped from 10.4 deaths to 8.3 per 1,000 children under one year of age, a lower rate than in 153 other countries.[^o41]

In 2019, the Ukrainian government conducted an electronic census using multiple sources, including mobile phone and pension data, and estimated that Ukraine's population, excluding Crimea and parts of the [Donbas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donbas), to be 37.3 million. About 20 million were of active working age.[^o42][^o43]

The [Russian invasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine) considerably deepened the country's demographic crisis due to the [military occupation and forced annexation of multiple oblasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_annexation_of_Donetsk,_Kherson,_Luhansk_and_Zaporizhzhia_oblasts), [numerous civilians fleeing the country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_refugee_crisis), and [high casualties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casualties_of_the_Russo-Ukrainian_War). A July 2023 study by the [Vienna Institute for International Economic Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Vienna_Institute_for_International_Economic_Studies) stated the following:

> "Regardless of how long the war lasts and whether or not there is further military escalation, Ukraine is unlikely to recover demographically from the consequences of the war. Even in 2040 it will have only about 35 million inhabitants, around 20% fewer than before the war (2021: 42.8 million) and the decline in the working-age population is likely to be the most severe and far-reaching."

The study examined different scenarios, from a "best case", in which the war ended in 2023 without significant further escalation, to a "worst case", ending in 2025 after further escalation. Flight from war particularly affects the southern and eastern regions and especially educated women of child-bearing age and their children. With an estimate of more than 20% of refugees not returning, the author of the study Maryna Tverdostup concludes that this will lead to long-term shrinking and will significantly impair the conditions for reconstruction.[^o44]

## Fertility and natalist policies {#Fertility_and_natalist_policies}

As of 2020, the birth rate in Ukraine was 8.1 live births/1,000 population, and the death rate 14.7 deaths/1,000 population.[^o305]

Lowest-low fertility, defined as total fertility below 1.3, is being encountered across Europe, attributed by many to postponement of the initiation of childbearing. Ukraine, where total fertility (1.1 in 2001), was one of the world's lowest, shows that there is more than one pathway to lowest-low fertility. Although Ukraine underwent immense political and economic transformations from 1991 to 2004, it maintained a young age at first birth and nearly universal childbearing. Analysis of official national statistics and the Ukrainian Reproductive Health Survey show that fertility declined to very low levels without a transition to a later pattern of childbearing. Findings from focus group interviews suggest that the early fertility pattern was explained by the persistence of traditional norms for childbearing and the roles of men and women, concerns about medical complications and infertility at a later age, and the link between early fertility and early marriage.[^o46] Ukraine subsequently has one of the oldest populations in the world, with an average age of 40.8 years.[^o47]

To help mitigate population decline, the government increased child support payments, providing one-time payments of 12,250 [hryvnias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_hryvnia) for the first child, 25,000 hryvnias for the second and 50,000 hryvnias for the third and fourth, along with monthly payments of 154 hryvnias per child.[^o48][^o49] The demographic trend showed signs of improvement as the birth rate grew steadily from 2001 to 2013.[^o50] Five of the country's 24 provinces showed net population growth over the first nine months of 2007, and nationwide population decline showed signs of stabilization. In 2007, the highest birth rates were in the western oblasts.[^o51] In 2008, Ukraine emerged from lowest-low fertility, and the upward trend continued to 2012, with population decline slowing year after year. If early 2010s trends had persisted, the population could have returned to positive growth later that decade. Similar trends occurred in [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia) and [Belarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarus), which experienced population growth in the 2010s.

In 2014, the strong drop in births returned, and 2018 saw fewer than half the number of births of 1989 (see demographic tables). In 2020, the number of births decreased to 293,000, reaching rates not seen in a quarter century.

Mass emigration and property destruction caused by the Russian invasion led Ukraine's birth to drop still further: it was 28% lower in the first half of 2023 than the first half of 2021.[^o52] However, a small but meaningful increase in births may occur in the future, with a projected fertility rate increase to 1.60 children per woman in the year 2030, higher than the 2012 peak of 1.53.[^o53]

### Year in review 2013 {#Year_in_review_2013}

Compared to 2012, the amount of attrition increased in 2013 by 16,278 persons, or 3.1 to 3.5 persons per 1,000 inhabitants (real). Natural decreases were observed in 23 oblasts of the country, while natural increases were recorded only in [Kyiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyiv) and in the [Zakarpattya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zakarpattia_Oblast), [Rivne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivne) and [Volyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volyn) oblasts (5,302, 3,689, 2,889 and 1,034 people, respectively).

Some regions registered a low natural decline, such as [Chernivtsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernivtsi), [Ivano-Frankivsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivano-Frankivsk), [Sevastopol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sevastopol), [Lviv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lviv), [Ternopil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ternopil), [Crimea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_Republic_of_Crimea), [Kherson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kherson) and [Odesa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odesa) (−55, −642, −863, −2,124, −2,875, −2,974, −3,748 and −4,448 people, respectively). The largest declines were recorded in [Donetsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donetsk), [Luhansk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luhansk), [Dnipro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dnipro), [Kharkiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv), [Poltava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poltava) and [Chernihiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernihiv) (−28,311, −15,291, −15,007, −12,765, −10,062 and −10,057, respectively), regions which share a low birth rate and high mortality of a large urban population and rapid aging of the rural population.

## Ethnic groups {#Ethnic_groups}

*Image: Ukraine census 2001 Ethnic groups.svg.* National population structure of Ukraine in 2001 \#F9C700 \#CCCCCC \#008FF9

In 2001, the ethnic composition of Ukraine was: [Ukrainian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainians) 77.8%, [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russians_in_Ukraine) 17.3%, [Romanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanians_in_Ukraine) 1.1% (including [Moldovan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moldovans_in_Ukraine) 0.8%), [Belarusian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarusians_in_Ukraine) 0.6%, [Crimean Tatar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_Tatars) 0.5%, [Bulgarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgarians_in_Ukraine) 0.4%, [Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_people) 0.3%, [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_people) 0.3%, [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews) 1.0%, [Pontic Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontic_Greeks) 0.2% and other 1.6% (including [Armenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians), [Germans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germans), [Romas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romani_people), [Georgians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgians), [Slovaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovaks), [Albanians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanians), [Crimean Karaites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_Karaites), as well as [Muslim Bulgarians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_Bulgarians), otherwise known as [Torbesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torbeši), and a microcosm of [Swedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedes) of [Gammalsvenskby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gammalsvenskby)).[^o212] It is also estimated that there are about 49,817 ethnic [Koreans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koreans) (0.12%) in Ukraine that belong to the [Koryo-saram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koryo-saram) group. Their number may be as high as 100,000 as many ethnic Koreans were assimilated into the majority population.[^o166][^o167] [Rusyns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rusyns) are also not recognised by the Ukrainian government as a distinct ethnic group and are instead treated as a sub-group of Ukrainians.[^o168]

According to the 2021 law “On the Indigenous Peoples of Ukraine”, the [Crimean Tatars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_Tatars), [Crimean Karaites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_Karaites) and [Krymchaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krymchaks) are the [indigenous peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples) of Ukraine.[^o169]

## Religion {#Religion}

A 2018 survey conducted by the [Razumkov Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Razumkov_Centre) found that 71.7% of the population declared themselves believers in any religion, while 4.7% declared themselves non-believers, and 3.0% declared themselves [atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism).[^o270] Of the total Ukrainian population, 87.4% declared they were [Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christians), comprising 67.3% who declared themselves [Eastern Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church), 10.2% [Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church) (split into 9.4% [Ukrainian Greek Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Greek_Catholic_Church) and 0.8% [Latin Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Church)), 7.7% "Christians", and 2.2% [Protestants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism). [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism) comprises 0.4% of the population. In earlier surveys, between 1 and 2% of the population stated that it adhered to [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam).

According to data from 2018, among those Ukrainians declaring themselves Orthodox Christians, 28.7% said they were members of the [Ukrainian Orthodox Church of the Kyivan Patriarchate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Orthodox_Church_–_Kyiv_Patriarchate) (incorporated as of 5 January 2019 into the [Orthodox Church of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodox_Church_of_Ukraine)), while 12.8% said they were members of the [Ukrainian Orthodox Church of the Moscovian Patriarchate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Orthodox_Church_%28Moscow_Patriarchate%29) (an [autonomous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocephaly) Eastern Orthodox church under the [Russian Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church)). A further 0.3% said they were members of the Ukrainian Autocephalous Orthodox Church, which, like the Kyivan Patriarchate, was incorporated in 2019 into the [Orthodox Church of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodox_Church_of_Ukraine). Among the remaining Orthodox Ukrainians, 23.4% declared themselves "simply Orthodox", without affiliation to any patriarchate, while a further 1.9% declared that they "did not know" which patriarchate or Orthodox church they belonged to.[^o270]

## Regional differences {#Regional_differences}

### Regional differences in population change {#Regional_differences_in_population_change}

*Image: Ukraine natural population growth rates.png.* Natural population growth rates by oblast, 2009

Between the Soviet census of 1989 and the Ukrainian census of 2001, Ukraine's population declined from 51,706,600 to 48,457,020,[^o307] a loss of 2,926,700 people, or 5.7% of the 1989 population. However, this trend was quite uneven and subject to regional variation. Two oblasts in western Ukraine, [Rivne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivne_Oblast) and [Zakarpattia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zakarpattia_Oblast), saw slight population increases of 0.3% and 0.5%, respectively. A third western Ukrainian oblast, [Volyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volyn_Oblast), lost less than 0.1% of its population between 1989 and 2001.[^o307] Collectively, between 1989 and 2001, the seven westernmost Ukrainian oblasts lost 167,500 people, or 1.7% of their 1989 population. The total population of these oblasts in 2001 was 9,593,800.[^o307]

Between 1989 and 2001, the population of [Kyiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyiv) City increased by 0.3%[^o307] due to positive net migration. Outside Kyiv, the central, southern and eastern oblasts experienced a severe population declines. Between 1989 and 2001, the [Donetsk Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donetsk_Oblast) lost 491,300 people, or 9.2% of its 1989 population, while the neighbouring [Luhansk Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luhansk_Oblast) lost 11% of its population.[^o307] [Chernihiv Obast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernihiv_Oblast), in central Ukraine (northeast of Kyiv), lost 170,600 people, or 12% of its 1989 population, the highest percentage loss in of any Ukrainian oblast. In southern Ukraine, [Odesa Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odesa_Oblast) lost 173,600 people, or 6.6% of its 1989 population.

By 2001, [Crimea's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_Republic_of_Crimea) population declined by 29,900, representing only a 1.4% loss of its 1989 population.[^o307] This however was due to the influx of approximately 200,000 [Crimean Tatars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_Tatars) – equivalent to approximately 10% of Crimea's 1989 population – who arrived in Crimea after 1989 and whose population in that region increased by a factor of 6.4 (from 38,000 to 243,400 between 1989 and 2001).[^o273] Collectively, the net population loss in Ukraine outside the westernmost oblasts was 2,759,200, or 6.6% of the 1989 population. The total population of these regions in 2001 was 39,186,100.[^o307]

Overall in 1989–2001, the pattern of population change was one of slight growth in Kyiv, slight declines in western Ukraine, large declines in eastern, central and southern Ukraine, and a relatively small decline in Crimea due to a large influx of Crimean Tatars.

### Regional differences in birth and fertility rates {#Regional_differences_in_birth_and_fertility_rates}

Ukraine's [total fertility rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_fertility_rate) is one of the lowest in Europe.[^o275][^o276] However, significant regional differences in birth rates may account for some of the demographic differences. In the third quarter of 2007, for instance, the highest birth rate among Ukrainian oblasts occurred in [Volyn Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volyn_Oblast), with a birth rate of 13.4/1,000 people, compared to the Ukrainian countrywide average of 9.6/1,000 people.[^o277] Volyn's birthrate was higher than the average birth rate of any European country except Iceland and Albania.[^o278]

In 2007, for the first time since 1990, five Ukrainian oblasts ([Zakarpattia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zakarpattia_Oblast), [Rivne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivne_Oblast), [Volyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volyn_Oblast), [Lviv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lviv_Oblast), and [Kyiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyiv_Oblast) oblasts) experienced more births than deaths.[^o279] This demonstrated a positive trend of increasing birthrates in the preceding years throughout Ukraine. The ratio of births to deaths in those oblasts in 2007 was 119%, 117%, 110%, 100.7%, and 108%, respectively.[^o279]

With the exception of the Kyiv Oblast, all of the oblasts with more births than deaths were in the less-industrially developed oblasts of western Ukraine. According to a spokesperson for Ukraine's Ministry of Justice, the overall ratio of births to deaths in Ukraine improved from 1 to 1.7 in 2004–2005 to 1 to 1.4 in 2008. However, the worst birth-to-death ratios in the country were in the eastern and central oblasts of Donetsk, Luhansk, Cherkasy and Poltava. These areas saw 2.1 deaths for every birth.[^o280]

Notably, western Ukraine never experienced the [Holodomor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holodomor), as [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Polish_Republic), [Czechoslovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechoslovakia), and [Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Romania) ruled it at the time, helping to understand the more favorable demographic trends there, as the rural population was never devastated. Specifically, during the Holodomor, Poland ruled [Ivano-Frankivsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivano-Frankivsk_Oblast), [Lviv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lviv_Oblast), [Rivne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivne_Oblast), [Ternopil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ternopil_Oblast), and [Volyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volyn_Oblast) oblasts, whereas [Zakarpattia Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zakarpattia_Oblast) was under Czechoslovak rule, and Romania controlled [Chernivtsi Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernivtsi_Oblast) and the [Budjak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budjak) section of [Odesa Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odesa_Oblast).

While abortion rates in the North, South, East and Center of Ukraine are relatively homogeneous, the Western region differs greatly. Overall, the abortion rate in western Ukraine is three times lower than in other regions. This is not due to increased use of modern contraceptive methods in the West, but to the fact that pregnant women in the Western regions are more likely to keep their babies.[^o281] Donetsk and Dniproptrovsk oblasts in eastern and central Ukraine have the country's highest abortion rate.[^o282]

### Regional differences in death rates and health {#Regional_differences_in_death_rates_and_health}

*Image: Ukraine-death-rate-suicides-by-region.png.* Death rate from suicides per 100.000 people

Death rates also vary widely by region; eastern and southern Ukraine have the highest death rates in the country, and the life expectancy for children born in Chernihiv, Dnipropetrovsk, Donetsk, Kherson, Kropyvnytskyi, Luhansk, Mikolaiv, and Odesa oblasts is 1.5 years lower than the national average.[^o284]

Ukraine had a suicide rate of 16.5 per 100,000 population in 2017, a significant decrease from the suicide rate of 29.6 per 100,000 in 1998. Suicides were more frequent in the central part of the country (the highest suicide rate was in [Kirovohrad Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirovohrad_Oblast); in western Ukraine, the suicide rate was lower than the national average. [Lviv Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lviv_Oblast) had the lowest suicide rate (5.3).[^o285]

Southern and eastern Ukraine also suffered from the highest rates of [HIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV) and [AIDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS), which impacts life expectancy. In late 2000, 60% of all AIDS cases in Ukraine were concentrated in the Odesa, Dnipropetrovsk, and Donetsk oblasts.[^o286] A major reason behind the higher rates was that the urbanized and industrialized oblasts in the East and South of Ukraine suffered most from the economic crisis in the 1990s, leading to the increased spread of unemployment, alcoholism, and drug abuse, setting the conditions for a wider spread of the epidemic.[^o287]

### Regional differences in income {#Regional_differences_in_income}

*Image: AverageMonthlySalary2019.png.* Average monthly salary by oblast in US dollars, 2019.

The western and central oblasts of Ukraine had lower GDP per capita than Kyiv and the industrialized eastern oblasts of Ukraine. In December 2019, the average monthly salary in Ukraine was 12,264 hryvnias (519 US dollars). [Chernihiv Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernihiv_Oblast) (northern Ukraine) and [Kirovohrad Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirovohrad_Oblast) (central Ukraine) had the lowest monthly salary of 8,851 and 9,450 hryvnias, respectively. In contrast, the monthly wage in the city of [Kyiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyiv) was 18,869 hryvnias per month, and in [Kyiv Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyiv_Oblast), 13,259 per month.[^o289] In 2013, outside of the capital city of Kyiv, the wealthiest oblast was [Donetsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donetsk_Oblast) with an annual income of 31,048 hryvnias. But as of 2017, it ranked second poorest after [Luhansk Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luhansk_Oblast), with annual incomes 25,278 hryvnias and 16,416 hryvnias, respectively.[^o290] Both are in eastern Ukraine and sustained direct losses as a consequence of [military actions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Ukrainian_War).

Ukraine recorded one of the sharpest declines in poverty of any transition economy in 2001–2016. The poverty rate, measured against an absolute poverty line (below \$1.25 per day, based on [World Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Bank) numbers) fell from a high of 32 percent in 2001 to 8 percent in 2005. In terms of poverty rates, the central and northern oblasts have the country's highest poverty rates: 10.0%. The western and southern oblasts are 9.1% and 9%, respectively. Kyiv City had the lowest poverty rate: 1.4%.[^o291] The percent of the population living under \$5.50 a day was 19% in 2005 and dropped to 4.0 percent in 2018.[^o292][^o293]

## Migration {#Migration}

*Image: MigrationGrowth2012ua.PNG.* Migration growth rate per 1,000 people, 2012

### Emigration {#Emigration}

Ukraine is the major source of [migrants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration) for many [EU member states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_states_of_the_European_Union). In the 1990s and early 2000s, Ukraine's sputtering economy and political instability contributed to rising [emigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emigration), especially to nearby [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland) and [Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungary), but also to other countries such as [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy), [Portugal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portugal), [Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spain), [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel) and [Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada). Although estimates vary, approximately two to three million Ukrainian citizens were working abroad, in construction, service, housekeeping, and agriculture industries.

Between 1991 and 2004, the government counted 2,537,400 individuals who emigrated; 1,897,500 moved to other post-Soviet states, and 639,900 moved to other, mainly Western, states.[^o298]

By the early 2000s, Ukrainian embassies reported that 300,000 Ukrainian citizens were working in Poland, 200,000 in Italy, approximately 200,000 in the [Czech Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_Republic), 150,000 in Portugal, 100,000 in Spain, 35,000 in Turkey, 20,000 in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) and smaller but significant numbers in [Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria), [Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgium), [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France), [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany), [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece), [Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweden), [Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switzerland) and the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom). The largest number of Ukrainian workers abroad, about one million, were in [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia). Since 1992, 232,072 persons born in Ukraine have emigrated to the United States.

Yet absolute numbers are less relevant to the economic impact on host countries than the volume of immigration as a proportion of the native population. Italy had the highest rate of Ukrainian emigrants as a proportion of the native population, while the far more populous Russia had the largest absolute confirmed number of Ukrainian emigrants (excluding Poland, Portugal and the Czech Republic, for which there was conflicting data).

### Immigration {#Immigration}

Between 1991 and 2003, about 100,000 illegal immigrants were detained at the western borders of Ukraine.[^o300] As of 2005, about 5,000 illegal immigrants were being detained yearly, mostly from [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China), [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India), [Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan) and [Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghanistan).[^o300] At the time, about 3,000 officially registered refugees resided in Ukraine, of whom most were [Afghans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghans).[^o300]

Ukraine accepted some 62,000 refugees from [Transnistria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transnistria) following its [war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transnistria_War) in 1992.[^o300] That same decade, thousands more were also accepted from other post-Soviet conflict zones in [Abkhazia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abkhazia), [Chechnya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chechnya) and [Tajikistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tajikistan).[^o300]

Between the 1989 Soviet census and the 2001 census, an increased number of former [CIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Independent_States) residents moved to Ukraine from war zones. The number of [Armenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians) in Ukraine almost doubled to 99,900 people during this period, while the number of [Georgians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgians) and [Azerbaijanis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azerbaijanis) also increased substantially.[^o300]

As of April 2020, 1.4 million Ukrainians were [internally displaced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internally_displaced) due to the [war in Donbas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_Donbas).[^o301]
