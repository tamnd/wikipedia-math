# Tom Lehrer

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Tom Lehrer"}

**Thomas Andrew Lehrer** (; April 9, 1928 – July 26, 2025) was an American musician and mathematician. He recorded pithy, humorous, and often [political songs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_and_politics) that became popular in the 1950s and 1960s. His songs parodied popular musical forms, often with original melodies.

:::

Lehrer's early performances dealt with non-topical subjects and [dark humor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_humor) in songs such as "[Poisoning Pigeons in the Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisoning_Pigeons_in_the_Park)". In the 1960s, he produced songs about timely social and political issues, particularly for the U.S. version of the television show *[That Was the Week That Was](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/That_Was_the_Week_That_Was)*. Lehrer quoted a friend's explanation: "Always predict the worst and you'll be hailed as a prophet."[^o4] In the early 1970s, Lehrer largely retired from public performance to devote his time to teaching mathematics and musical theater history at the [University of California, Santa Cruz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Santa_Cruz).

## Early life {#Early_life}

*Image: Tom Lehrer in 1943 Loomiscellany.jpg.* Lehrer in [Loomis School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loomis_Chaffee_School)'s 1943 yearbook

Thomas Andrew Lehrer was born in New York City on April 9, 1928, and grew up on [Manhattan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan)'s [Upper East Side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_East_Side).[^o6][^o7] He was the son of Morris James Lehrer (1897–1986), a successful necktie designer, and Anna Lehrer (née Waller; 1905–1978) and older brother of Barry Waller Lehrer (1930–2007).[^o8][^o9] Lehrer told an interviewer that he recalled an idyllic childhood on [Manhattan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan)'s [Upper West Side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_West_Side) that included attending [Broadway shows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadway_theatre) with his family and walking through [Central Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Park) day or night.[^o10] He and his family were ethnically [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews), following a generally secular lifestyle that included attending [Jewish Sunday school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_school) but also [celebrating Christmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews_and_Christmas); he remarked that his ties to Judaism were "more to do with the delicatessen than the synagogue (...) and 'God' was primarily an expletive(.)"[^o11] As a child, he loved logic puzzles and math.[^o12] He began taking classical piano lessons at the age of seven but was more interested in the popular music of the age. Eventually, his mother sent him to a piano teacher who taught him how to play the [Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadway_theatre) show tunes he loved.[^o13] At this early age, he began writing [show tunes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Show_tune), which eventually helped him as a satirical composer and writer in his years of lecturing at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) and later at other universities.[^o14]

Lehrer attended the [Horace Mann School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace_Mann_School) in [Riverdale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverdale,_New_York), part of [the Bronx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bronx) borough of New York.[^o6][^o15] He also attended [Camp Androscoggin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_Androscoggin), both as a camper and a counselor.[^o16] [Stephen Sondheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Sondheim) had Tom Lehrer as a camp counselor.[^o17][^o48]

Lehrer was considered a child prodigy and skipped two grades.[^o18] After graduation from [Loomis School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loomis_Chaffee_School), at the age of 15 he entered [Harvard College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_College), where one of his professors was [Irving Kaplansky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irving_Kaplansky).[^o19][^o39][^o6] As an undergraduate student at Harvard, he began to write [comic songs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comic_song) to entertain his friends, including "[Fight Fiercely, Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fight_Fiercely,_Harvard)".

A fellow student and friend, the physicist [Jeremy Bernstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Bernstein), recalled that he was told to organize a luncheon entertainment for the seniors graduating in 1951.[^o20] He knew only two entertainers, Lehrer and [Al Capp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Capp), both of whom agreed. Lehrer ("and a small group of co-conspirators," Bernstein wrote) performed his songs as *The Physical Revue* for Harvard's physics department then and again in 1952.[^o21] Bernstein said, "This performance had remarkable consequences for Lehrer. Capp had a weekly radio program, and Lehrer became a fixture. I think that this was the first time he had been let loose on the general public, although the program only lasted four weeks."[^o20] The revue was revived by Lehrer in 1993 as *Songs of the Physical Revue* – a special performance for the [American Physical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Physical_Society) to commemorate the centenary of their journal, the *[Physical Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_Review)*.[^o22]

## Academic and military career {#Academic_and_military_career}

*Image: Tom Lehrer in The Harvard Album 1947–1948.jpg.* Lehrer in [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University)'s 1947–1948 yearbook

*Image: Tom Lehrer, Robert H. Welker, David Z. Robinson, and Lewis M. Branscomb perform on Arbor Day at Harvard University, 1951.jpg.* (From left) Lehrer, Robert H. Welker, David Z. Robinson, and [Lewis M. Branscomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_M._Branscomb) perform a hymn for the Harvard Spring rites on Arbor Day, 1951. The quartet performed songs for *The Physical Revue*.

Lehrer graduated from [Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) with a [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts) in mathematics, *[magna cum laude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_cum_laude)*,[^o24] in 1946.[^o25] At Harvard, he was the roommate of the Canadian theologian [Robert Crouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Crouse).[^o26] He received his [MA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts) degree the next year and was inducted into [Phi Beta Kappa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phi_Beta_Kappa).[^o27] He later taught mathematics and other classes at [MIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology), Harvard, [Wellesley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellesley_College), and the [University of California, Santa Cruz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Santa_Cruz).[^o28]

Lehrer remained in Harvard's doctoral program for several years, taking time also for his musical career.[^o29] "I spent many, many years satisfying all the requirements, as many years as possible, and I started on the [thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thesis#United_States)," he once said. "But I just wanted to be a grad student, it's a wonderful life. That's what I wanted to be, and unfortunately, you can't be a Ph.D. and a grad student at the same time."[^o10]

Lehrer reportedly worked briefly as a researcher with [Stan Ulam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stan_Ulam) at the [Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Alamos_Scientific_Laboratory) for two months in 1952.[^o30][^o31]

In 1953, Lehrer left Harvard to work for Baird-Atomic, which made scientific and industrial instruments, including radiation detection and [spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy).[^o32][^o33]

Lehrer was [drafted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscription_in_the_United_States) into the [U.S. Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Army) in 1955. He served until 1957, working at the [National Security Agency](wikipedia://en/21939) (NSA). Lehrer once stated that he invented the [Jello shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jello_shot) during this time, as a means of circumventing the base's ban on alcoholic beverages.[^o29] Despite holding a master's degree in an era when American conscripts often lacked high school diplomas, Lehrer served as an [enlisted soldier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enlisted_soldier), achieving the rank of [specialist third class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specialist_%28rank%29#Specialist_%281955–present%29), which he described as being a "corporal [without portfolio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_without_portfolio)".[^o34] These experiences became fodder for songs, such as "The Wild West is Where I Want to Be" and "It Makes a Fellow Proud to Be a Soldier".[^o35] In 2020 Lehrer publicly revealed that he had been assigned to the NSA; since the mere fact of the NSA's existence was classified at the time, Lehrer found himself in the position of implicitly using nuclear weapons work as a cover story for something more sensitive.[^o36]

In 1960, Lehrer returned to full-time mathematics studies at Harvard.[^o15] From 1962 he taught mathematics in the [political science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science) department at [MIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT) in [Cambridge, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge,_Massachusetts).[^o37] In 1965 he gave up on his mathematics dissertation on [modes](wikipedia://en/1432127) in statistics, after working on it intermittently for 15 years.[^o6]

Over time, Lehrer grew tired of shoveling snow in the harsh Cambridge winters; he already knew he liked California's Bay Area.[^o20][^o38] He contacted botany professor [Kenneth V. Thimann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_V._Thimann), provost of [Crown College, University of California, Santa Cruz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_College,_University_of_California,_Santa_Cruz), and suggested teaching courses in [musical theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_theatre). Since Crown was science-oriented, Thimann asked mathematician [Anthony Joseph Tromba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Joseph_Tromba), who had been at Harvard and knew of Lehrer's musical antics, to find him a position at humanities-heavy [Cowell College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cowell_College) instead. Tromba sold Lehrer to the Fellowship Committee: "No one on the committee had heard of Tom. So I had to convince them that it was a great idea... We had Ph.D.s in music and art history who did scholarly work. But Cowell also wanted to have practicing artists to give students actual meaningful contact with them. Rather than just writing papers about [Michelangelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelangelo), let's have Michelangelo here—and why not?"[^o38] So in 1972, Lehrer joined the faculty of the [University of California, Santa Cruz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Santa_Cruz) (UCSC). Tromba said that Lehrer was officially a "Lecturer in American Studies,"[^o38] not mathematics, though he taught two introductory classes, "The Nature of Mathematics" and "Mathematics in the Social Sciences," to liberal arts majors—"Math for Tenors", according to Lehrer. He occasionally performed songs in his lectures.[^o39]

In 2001, Lehrer taught his last mathematics class, on the topic of [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824), and retired from academia.[^o40] He remained in the area, maintaining homes in both Santa Cruz and Cambridge.[^o83] In 2003 Lehrer confirmed he still "h\[ung\] out" around UCSC.[^o41] Mike Peña of UCSC said in 2025, "Lehrer's reputation matched UC Santa Cruz's creative and irreverent spirit; and his talents played perfectly into the campus's original intent to elevate the humanities and foster deeper connections between scholarship and society... Lehrer taught at UC Santa Cruz until 2001 and last came here about five years ago. His cultural contributions are so woven into the American fabric that they ensure his place as one of the most beloved educators ever to teach at our campus."[^o38]

## Musical career {#Musical_career}

### Style and influences {#Style_and_influences}

Lehrer was mainly influenced by [musical theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_theater). According to [Gerald Nachman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_Nachman_%28journalist%29)'s book *Seriously Funny*,[^o45] the [Broadway musical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadway_musical) *[Let's Face It!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Let's_Face_It!)* made an early and lasting impression on him. Lehrer's style consists of [parodying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parody_music) various forms of [popular song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_song). For example, his appreciation of [list songs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_song) led him to write "[The Elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Elements_%28song%29)", which lists the [chemical elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element) to the tune of [Gilbert and Sullivan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_and_Sullivan)'s "[Major-General's Song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major-General's_Song)" from *[The Pirates of Penzance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pirates_of_Penzance)*.[^o46][^o47]

In author and [Boston University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_University) professor [Isaac Asimov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Asimov)'s second autobiographical volume, *In Joy Still Felt*, Asimov recounted seeing Lehrer perform in a [Boston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston) nightclub on October 9, 1954. Lehrer sang a song about Jim getting it from Louise, and Sally from Jim, "... and after a while you gathered the 'it' was [venereal disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venereal_disease). Suddenly, as the combinations grew more grotesque, you realized he was satirizing every known [perversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_perversion) without using a single naughty phrase. It was clearly unsingable outside a nightclub." Asimov also recalled a song that dealt with the [Boston subway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_subway) system, making use of the stations leading into town from Harvard, observing that the local subject-matter rendered the song useless for general distribution. Lehrer subsequently granted Asimov permission to print the lyrics to "The Subway Song" in his book. "I haven't gone to nightclubs often," said Asimov, "but of all the times I have gone, it was on this occasion that I had by far the best time."[^o49]

### Recordings {#Recordings}

Lehrer was encouraged by the success of his performances, so he paid \$15 (US) to record in a single one-hour session on January 22, 1953, at the [TransRadio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transradio_Press_Service) studio on Boylston Street[^o51] in Boston, *[Songs by Tom Lehrer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songs_by_Tom_Lehrer)*.[^o52][^o53] The initial pressing was 400 copies. Radio stations would not air his songs because of his controversial subjects, so he sold the album on campus at Harvard for \$3, US, while "several stores near the Harvard campus sold it for \$3.50, taking only a minimal markup as a kind of community service. Newsstands on campus sold it for the same price."[^o54] After one summer, he started to receive mail orders from all parts of the country, as far away as San Francisco, after the *[San Francisco Chronicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Chronicle)* wrote an article on the record.[^o55] Interest in his recordings spread by word of mouth. People played their records for friends, who then also wanted a copy.[^o56] Lehrer recalled, "Lacking exposure in the media, my songs spread slowly. Like [herpes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herpes), rather than [ebola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebola)."[^o57]

The album included the macabre "I Hold Your Hand in Mine", the mildly risqué "Be Prepared", and "[Lobachevsky](wikipedia://en/31528482)", regarding plagiarizing mathematicians. It became a [cult success](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cult_following) by word of mouth, despite being self-published and without promotion. The limited distribution of the album led to a knock-off album by Jack "Eljan" (a pseudonym of Jack Nagel) being released in 1958 without Lehrer's approval, where some of the lyrics were mistranscribed.[^o58]

Lehrer embarked on a series of concert tours and recorded a second album in 1959. He released the second album in two versions: the songs were the same, but *[More of Tom Lehrer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/More_of_Tom_Lehrer)* was a studio recording and *[An Evening Wasted with Tom Lehrer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Evening_Wasted_with_Tom_Lehrer)* was recorded live in concert. In 2013, Lehrer recalled the studio session for "Poisoning Pigeons in the Park", which referred to the practice of controlling pigeons in Boston with [strychnine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strychnine)-treated corn:[^o59]

The copyist arrived at the last minute with the parts and passed them out to the band ... And there was no title on it, and there was no lyrics. And so they ran through it, "What a pleasant little waltz" ... And the engineer said, Poisoning Pigeons in the Park,' take one," and the piano player said, "What?" and literally fell off the stool.[^o60]

### Touring {#Touring}

*Image: Tom\_Lehrer\_has\_become\_an\_honorary\_student\_in\_the\_Studenterforeningen\_(Copenhagen\_Student\_Union).jpg.* Lehrer as an honorary member of the Copenhagen Student Union, 1967

Lehrer had a breakthrough in the United Kingdom on December 4, 1957, when the [University of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_London) awarded a [doctor of music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_music) degree *[honoris causa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honoris_causa)* to [Princess Margaret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princess_Margaret), and the public orator, Professor J. R. Sutherland, said it was "in the full knowledge that the Princess is a connoisseur of music and a performer of skill and distinction, her taste being catholic, ranging from Mozart to the [calypso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calypso_music) and from opera to the songs of Miss [Beatrice Lillie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Lillie) and Tom Lehrer."[^o62][^o63][^o64] This prompted significant interest in Lehrer's works and helped to secure distribution in Britain for his five-year-old debut album. It was there that his music achieved real sales popularity, as a result of the proliferation of university newspapers referring to the material, and inadvertently due to the [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC), which in 1958 banned from broadcast 10 of the 12 songs on the album.[^o65] By the end of the 1950s, Lehrer had sold 370,000 records.[^o6]

### *That Was The Week That Was* {#That_Was_The_Week_That_Was}

In 1960, Lehrer essentially retired from touring in the U.S.[^o6] That same year, however, he toured Australia and New Zealand, performing a total of 33 concerts to great acclaim[^o54] and controversy.[^o67] While in New Zealand, he penned lyrics critical of the [All Blacks' upcoming tour of Apartheid-era South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1960_New_Zealand_rugby_union_tour_of_Australia_and_South_Africa) and Prime Minister [Walter Nash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Nash)'s stance on it.[^o68][^o69] Lehrer's tours occurred during a time in which he was, he said, "banned, censored, mentioned in several houses of parliament and threatened with arrest." In particular, "Be Prepared" drew advance resistance in [Brisbane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brisbane) from the commissioner of police. He performed several songs in Australia that were still unreleased, including "The Masochism Tango."[^o41]

In the early 1960s, Lehrer was employed as the resident songwriter for the U.S. edition of *[That Was The Week That Was](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/That_Was_The_Week_That_Was#US_versions)* (TW3), a satirical television show.[^o54] A greater proportion of his output became overtly political, or at least topical, on subjects such as education ("[New Math](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Math_%28song%29)"), the [Second Vatican Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Vatican_Council) ("[The Vatican Rag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Vatican_Rag)"), race relations ("National Brotherhood Week"), air and water pollution ("Pollution"[^o70]), American militarism ("Send the Marines"), and [nuclear proliferation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_proliferation) ("Who's Next?" and "[MLF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multilateral_Force) Lullaby"). He also wrote a song satirizing rocket scientist [Wernher von Braun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wernher_von_Braun), who worked for [Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany) before working for the United States. Lehrer did not appear on TW3; vocalist [Nancy Ames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy_Ames) performed his songs (to Lehrer's chagrin),[^o58] and lines were often cut from his songs.[^o58] Lehrer later performed nine of these songs at the [Hungry i](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungry_i) nightclub in [San Francisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco), and this became the album *[That Was the Year That Was](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/That_Was_the_Year_That_Was)* (1965).[^o72]

In 1966, BBC TV host [David Frost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Frost) returned to the UK with the BBC program *[The Frost Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Frost_Report)*; alongside [Julie Felix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julie_Felix), Lehrer provided musical satire on the weekly subject.[^o71] The show was transmitted live, and he pre-recorded all his segments at one performance.[^o72] Lehrer was not featured in every edition, but his songs featured in an appropriate part of each show.[^o73] At least two of these songs were not included on any of his LPs: a reworking of [Noël Coward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noël_Coward)'s "That is the End of the News" (with some new lyrics)[^o74] and a comic explanation of how Britain might adapt to the [coming of decimal currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_Day).[^o75]

Lehrer's record deal with [Reprise Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reprise_Records) for *That Was The Year That Was* also gave Reprise distribution rights for his earlier recordings, because Lehrer wanted to [wind up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquidation) his own record imprint.[^o58] The Reprise issue of *Songs by Tom Lehrer* was a stereo re-recording. This version was not issued on CD, as Lehrer was unhappy with this version.[^o72] The live recording included bonus tracks "L-Y" and "Silent E", two of the ten songs that he wrote for the [PBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PBS) children's educational series *[The Electric Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Electric_Company_%281971_TV_series%29)*. Lehrer later commented that worldwide sales of the recordings under Reprise surpassed 1.8 million units in 1996. That same year, *That Was The Year That Was* went gold.[^o56] The album [liner notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liner_notes) promote his songs with self-deprecating humor, such as quoting a *[New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Times)* review from 1959: Mr. Lehrer's muse is "not fettered by such inhibiting factors as taste".[^o76]

Lehrer toured Sweden, Norway, and Denmark in 1967;[^o77] his concert in Oslo was recorded for Danish television and subsequently released on DVD some 40 years later.[^o78] He performed as a prominent international guest at the *Studenterforeningen* (student association) in Copenhagen, which was televised, and he commented on stage that he might be America's "revenge for [Victor Borge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Borge)".[^o79] He performed original songs in a [Dodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodge) automobile industrial film distributed primarily to automobile dealers and shown at promotional events in 1967, set in a fictional American wild west town and titled *The Dodge Rebellion Theatre presents Ballads For '67*.[^o54][^o80] He attempted to adapt *[Sweeney Todd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweeney_Todd)* as a Broadway musical, working with [Joe Raposo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Raposo), to star [Jerry Colonna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_Colonna_%28entertainer%29). They started a few songs but, as Lehrer noted, "Nothing ever came of it, and of course twenty years later [Stephen Sondheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Sondheim) [beat me to the punch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweeney_Todd:_The_Demon_Barber_of_Fleet_Street)."[^o81]

### Departure from the music scene {#Departure_from_the_music_scene}

Although Lehrer was "a hero of the [anti-nuclear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-nuclear_movement), [civil rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_rights_movement) [left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-wing_politics)", he disliked the aesthetics of the [counterculture of the 1960s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterculture_of_the_1960s) and largely stopped performing in the United States as the movement gained momentum.[^o6]

In the 1970s, Lehrer concentrated on teaching mathematics and musical theater,[^o83] although he also wrote ten songs for the educational children's television show *[The Electric Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Electric_Company)*. His last public performance for many years took place in 1972, on a fundraising tour for the [anti-Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Vietnam_War) Democratic U.S. presidential candidate [George McGovern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_McGovern).[^o6][^o48]

When asked why he had abandoned his musical career in an interview for the booklet accompanying his CD boxed set, *[The Remains of Tom Lehrer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Remains_of_Tom_Lehrer)* (2000), Lehrer replied: "If an idea came to me, I'd write, and if it didn't I wouldn't—and, gradually, the second option prevailed over the first. Occasionally people ask 'If you enjoyed it'—and I did—'why don't you do it again?' I reply, 'I enjoyed high school but I certainly wouldn't want to do *that* again.[^o84]

Yet at different times he gave other explanations for quitting. In 1973, he had said that "[political satire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_satire) became obsolete when [Henry Kissinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Kissinger) was awarded the [Nobel Peace Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Peace_Prize)."[^o85] He later stated, in an interview with *[The A.V. Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_A.V._Club)* in 2000, that this was only an off-hand remark and was not the actual reason for him quitting.[^o86] In 1981, at a New York performance of *[Tomfoolery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomfoolery_%28musical%29)*, he told *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times)*, "The Vietnam War is what changed it. Everybody got earnest. My purpose was to make people laugh and not applaud. If the audience applauds they’re just showing they agree with me. But that’s not humor. So I dropped out just in time."[^o85][^o87]

Lehrer's musical career was relatively brief. He once mentioned that he performed a mere 109 shows and wrote 37 songs over 20 years.[^o88] Nevertheless, he developed a cult following in subsequent decades.[^o89]

### Revivals and reissues {#Revivals_and_reissues}

*Image: Portrait of Tom Lehrer in c. 1983 (retouched).jpg.* Lehrer c. 1983

Lehrer's music became a staple of *The [Doctor Demento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Demento) Show* when it began national syndication in 1977.[^o91] In 1980, [Cameron Mackintosh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameron_Mackintosh) produced *[Tomfoolery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomfoolery_%28musical%29)*, a revue of Lehrer's songs that was a hit on the London stage. Lehrer was not initially involved with the show, but he was pleased with it; he eventually gave the stage production his full support and updated several of his lyrics for the show. *Tomfoolery* contained 27 songs and led to more than 200 productions,[^o56] including an [Off-Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Off-Broadway) production at the [Village Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Village_Gate) which ran for 120 performances in 1981.[^o92] Lehrer made a rare TV appearance on [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC)'s *[Parkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkinson_%28TV_series%29)* show in conjunction with the *Tomfoolery* premiere in 1980 at the [Criterion Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criterion_Theatre) in London, where he sang "I Got It from Agnes".[^o93][^o94] There were *Tomfoolery* performances in San Francisco about 1982 and in 2018–19. *Tomfoolery* was performed at the [Arena Stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arena_Stage) Theater in Washington, DC, in 1982.[^o95] In 1993, he wrote "That's Mathematics" for the closing credits to a [Mathematical Sciences Research Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Sciences_Research_Institute) video[^o96] celebrating the proof of [Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953).

On June 7 and 8, 1998, Lehrer performed in public for the first time in 18 years at the [Lyceum Theatre, London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyceum_Theatre,_London), as part of the show *[Hey, Mr. Producer!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hey,_Mr._Producer!)* celebrating the career of Cameron Mackintosh, who had produced *Tomfoolery*. The June 8 show was his only performance before Queen [Elizabeth II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_II). Lehrer sang "Poisoning Pigeons in the Park" and an updated version of the nuclear proliferation song "Who's Next?".[^o97]

In 2000, Lehrer commented that he doubted his songs had any real effect on those not already critical of the establishment: "I don't think this kind of thing has an impact on the unconverted, frankly. It's not even preaching to the converted; it's titillating the converted ... I'm fond of quoting [Peter Cook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Cook), who talked about the satirical Berlin [Kabaretts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabarett) of the 1930s, which did so much to stop the rise of [Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitler) and prevent the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War)."[^o98]

Lehrer said, jokingly, of his musical career: "If, after hearing my songs, just one human being is inspired to say something nasty to a friend, or perhaps to strike a loved one, it will all have been worth the while."[^o13] In 2003, Lehrer commented that his particular brand of political satire is more difficult in the modern world: "The real issues I don't think most people touch. The [Clinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Clinton) jokes are all about [Monica Lewinsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monica_Lewinsky) and all that stuff and not about the important things, like the fact that he wouldn't ban [land mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_mines) ... I'm not tempted to write a song about [George W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bush). I couldn't figure out what sort of song I would write. That's the problem: I don't want to satirize George Bush and his puppeteers, I want to vaporize them."[^o41]

In 2000, the boxed CD set *The Remains of Tom Lehrer* was released by [Rhino Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhino_Entertainment). It included live and studio versions of his first two albums, *That Was The Year That Was*, the songs that he wrote for *The Electric Company*, some previously unreleased material, and a small hardbound lyrics book with an introduction by [Dr. Demento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dr._Demento). In 2010, [Shout! Factory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shout!_Factory) launched a reissue campaign, making Lehrer's out-of-print albums available digitally. The CD/DVD combo *The Tom Lehrer Collection* was issued, including his best-known songs, with a DVD featuring an Oslo concert.[^o99]

## Later life and death {#Later_life_and_death}

In a February 2008 phone call, [Gene Weingarten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_Weingarten) of *[The Washington Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Post)* interviewed Lehrer [off the record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Off_the_record_%28journalism%29). When Weingarten asked if there was anything he could print for the record, Lehrer responded, "Just tell the people that I am voting for [Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama)."[^o101]

In 2012, rapper [2 Chainz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2_Chainz) sampled Lehrer's song "[The Old Dope Peddler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Old_Dope_Peddler)" on his debut album, *[Based on a T.R.U. Story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Based_on_a_T.R.U._Story)*. In 2013, Lehrer said he was "very proud" to have his song sampled "literally sixty years after I recorded it". Lehrer went on to describe his official response to the request to use his song: "As sole copyright owner of 'The Old Dope Peddler', I grant you motherfuckers permission to do this. Please give my regards to Mr. Chainz, or may I call him 2?"[^o60][^o102]

In October 2020, Lehrer transferred the music and lyrics for all songs he had ever written into the [public domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_domain):[^o103][^o104]

In November 2022, Lehrer formally relinquished the [copyright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright) and performing/recording rights on his songs, making all music and lyrics composed by him free for anyone to use, and established a website from which all of his recordings and printable copies of all of his songs could be downloaded.[^o105] His statement releasing all his works into the public domain concludes with this note: "This website will be shut down at some date in the not too distant future, so if you want to download anything, don't wait too long."[^o105] , the website is still online.[^o83]

Comedy label [Stand Up! Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand_Up!_Records) released Lehrer's holiday songs "(I'm Spending) [Hanukkah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanukkah) in [Santa Monica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Monica)," which he had written for [Garrison Keillor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrison_Keillor)'s *The American Radio Company of the Air* in 1990 and recorded later,[^o12] and "A Christmas Carol" as a seven-inch single in 2024. The limited-edition release also included sheet music for both songs.[^o106]

Lehrer never married and had no children.[^o83][^o107] He died at his home in Cambridge, Massachusetts, on July 26, 2025, at the age of 97.[^o83]

## Musical legacy {#Musical_legacy}

In 1967, Swedish actor [Lars Ekborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lars_Ekborg), known outside Sweden for his part in [Ingmar Bergman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingmar_Bergman)'s *[Summer with Monika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summer_with_Monika)*, made an album called *I Tom Lehrers vackra värld* ("In the beautiful world of Tom Lehrer"), with 12 of Lehrer's songs translated into [Swedish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_language). Lehrer wrote in a letter to the producer Per-Anders Boquist that, "Not knowing any Swedish, I am obviously not equipped to judge, but it sounds to me as though Mr. Ekborg is perfect for the songs", along with further compliments to pianist Leif Asp for unexpected additional flourishes.[^o109]

In 1971, Argentinian singer [Nacha Guevara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nacha_Guevara) sang Spanish versions of several Lehrer songs for the show/live album *Este es el año que es*.[^o110][^o111] In the 1950s, [Georg Kreisler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Kreisler) wrote two songs likely inspired by Tom Lehrer's "Poisoning Pigeons in the Park" and "I Hold Your Hand in Mine", as mentioned by Lehrer himself even though disputed by Kreisler. Composer [Randy Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randy_Newman) said of Lehrer, "He's one of the great American songwriters without a doubt, right up there with everybody, the top guys. As a lyricist, as good as there's been in the last half of the 20th century."[^o60] Singer and comedian [Dillie Keane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dillie_Keane) has acknowledged Lehrer's influence on her work.[^o112]

[Dr. Demento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dr._Demento) praised Lehrer as "the best musical satirist of the twentieth century." Other artists who cite Lehrer as an influence include ["Weird Al" Yankovic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/"Weird_Al"_Yankovic), whose work generally addresses more [popular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pop_culture) and less technical or political subjects,[^o113] and educator and scientist [H. Paul Shuch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._Paul_Shuch), who tours under the stage name Dr. SETI, and calls himself "a cross between [Carl Sagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Sagan) and Tom Lehrer: He sings like Sagan and lectures like Lehrer."[^o114] Yankovic saw [Daniel Radcliffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Radcliffe) (who called Lehrer his "hero")[^o115] perform "The Elements" on *[The Graham Norton Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Graham_Norton_Show)* in his native United Kingdom,[^o116] which led to Radcliffe starring in *[Weird: The Al Yankovic Story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weird:_The_Al_Yankovic_Story)*.[^o117]

From January 16 to February 25, 2006, the play *[Letters from Lehrer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_from_Lehrer)*, written and performed by Canadian [Richard Greenblatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Greenblatt_%28playwright%29), ran at [CanStage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Stage_Company) in Toronto. It followed Lehrer's musical career, the meaning of several songs, the politics of the time, and Greenblatt's own experiences with Lehrer's music, while playing some of Lehrer's songs.[^o118]

In the March 16, 2006, issue of [*New York* magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_%28magazine%29), [Donald Fagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Fagen) of [Steely Dan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steely_Dan) named Tom Lehrer among the writers who had influenced him and his songwriting partner [Walter Becker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Becker). "We also liked comic songwriting, like Tom Lehrer. He was a piano player and songwriter who wrote these grim, funny songs."[^o119]

In 2010, the German musician-comedian Felix Janosa released an album with the title *Tauben vergiften: Die bösen Lieder von Tom Lehrer* ("Poisoning pigeons: The Evil Songs of Tom Lehrer"), with German versions of some of his best-known songs.[^o120]

In 2024, [Francis Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Beckett) wrote a play *Tom Lehrer Is Teaching Math and Doesn't Want to Talk to You*, which features Lehrer's music and was performed with Lehrer's tacit approval at the [Upstairs at The Gatehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upstairs_at_The_Gatehouse) theatre in [Highgate, London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highgate,_London).[^o121][^o122]

*Image: The Physical Revue (1952).webp.* Lehrer at the piano during The Physical Revue, c. 1952

In 2025, *[The Harvard Crimson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Harvard_Crimson)* recalls how Lehrer expressed his surprise that "well, I wrote 'Fight Fiercely, Harvard' in 1945" and it was still being performed in the 21st century. *The Crimson* comments, "\[As\] [Harvard squares off](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University#Second_presidency_of_Donald_Trump) with the [Trump administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_presidency_of_Donald_Trump), the song — which genteelly urges football players to 'hurl that spheroid down the field' — has become a [protest anthem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protest_song) of sorts, too."[^o85]

Performers influenced by Lehrer's style include American political satirist [Mark Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Russell),[^o123] Canadian comedian and songwriter [Randy Vancourt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randy_Vancourt), and the British duo Kit and The Widow. Composer/cabarettist [Leonard Lehrman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Lehrman) extended three of Lehrer's songs, writing a new verse 4 to "Clementine",[^o124] a new verse 2 to "Hanukkah in Santa Monica",[^o125] and a new verse 3 to "The Elements".[^o126]

Lehrer has been referred to as the "King of Satire" by fans.[^o127]
