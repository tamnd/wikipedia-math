# Casorati–Weierstrass theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Casorati–Weierstrass theorem"}

In [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), a branch of mathematics, the **Casorati–Weierstrass theorem** describes the behaviour of [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110) near their [essential singularities](wikipedia://en/80862). It is named for [Karl Theodor Wilhelm Weierstrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Theodor_Wilhelm_Weierstrass) and [Felice Casorati](wikipedia://en/5697549).[^o2] In Russian literature it is called [Sokhotski's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yulian_Sokhotski) theorem, because it was discovered independently by Sokhotski in 1868.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Formal statement of the theorem"}

In the case that $f$ is an [entire function](wikipedia://en/10372) and $a = \infty$, the theorem says that the values $f(z)$ approach every complex number and $\infty$, as $z$ tends to infinity. It is remarkable that this does not hold for [holomorphic maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_map) in higher dimensions, as the famous example of [Pierre Fatou](wikipedia://en/629100) shows.[^o5]

## Examples {#Examples}

The function $f(z) = \exp(1/z)$ has an essential singularity at 0, but the function $g(z) = 1/z^{3}$ does not (it has a [pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) at 0).

Consider the function

$$f(z) = e^{1/z}.$$

This function has the following [Laurent series](wikipedia://en/61213) about the [essential singular point](wikipedia://en/80862) at 0:

$$f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}z^{-n}.$$

Because $f'(z) = - \frac{e^{{1}/{z}}}{z^2}$ exists for all points $z \ne 0$ we know that $f(z)$ is analytic in a [punctured neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punctured_neighborhood) of $z = 0$. Hence it is an [isolated singularity](wikipedia://en/334325), as well as being an [essential singularity](wikipedia://en/80862).

Using a change of variable to [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) $z=re^{i \theta }$ our function, $f(z) = e^{1/z}$ becomes:

$$f(z)=e^{\frac{1}{r}e^{-i\theta}}=e^{\frac{1}{r}\cos(\theta)}e^{-\frac{1}{r}i \sin(\theta)}.$$

Taking the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of both sides:

$$\left| f(z) \right| = \left| e^{\frac{1}{r}\cos \theta} \right| \left| e^{-\frac{1}{r}i \sin(\theta)} \right | =e^{\frac{1}{r}\cos \theta}.$$

Thus, for values of *θ* such that $\cos \theta > 0$, we have $f(z) \to \infty$ as $r \to 0$, and for $\cos \theta < 0$, $f(z) \to 0$ as $r \to 0$.

Consider what happens, for example when *z* takes values on a circle of diameter $1/R$ tangent to the imaginary axis. This circle is given by $r = (1/R) \cos \theta$. Then,

$$f(z) = e^{R} \left[ \cos \left( R\tan \theta \right) - i \sin \left( R\tan \theta \right) \right]$$

and

$$\left| f(z) \right| = e^R.$$

Thus,$\left| f(z) \right|$ may take any positive value other than zero by the appropriate choice of *R*. As $z \to 0$ on the circle, $\theta \to \frac{\pi}{2}$ with *R* fixed. So this part of the equation:

$$\left[ \cos \left( R \tan \theta \right) - i \sin \left( R \tan \theta \right) \right]$$

takes on all values on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) infinitely often. Hence $f(z)$ takes on the value of every number in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) except for zero infinitely often.

:::

## Proof of the theorem {#Proof_of_the_theorem}

A short proof of the theorem is as follows:

Take as given that function $f$ is [meromorphic](wikipedia://en/80857) on some punctured neighborhood $V \backslash \{z_{0}\}$, and that $z_{0}$ is an essential singularity. Assume by way of contradiction that some value $b$ exists that the function can never get close to; that is: assume that there is some complex value $b$ and some $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $\lVert f(z) - b\rVert \ge \varepsilon$ for all $z$ in $V$ at which $f$ is defined.

Then the new function:

$$g(z) = \frac{1}{f(z) - b}$$

must be holomorphic on $V \backslash \{z_{0}\}$, with [zeroes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_%28complex_analysis%29) at the [poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) of *f*, and bounded by 1/ε. It can therefore be analytically continued (or continuously extended, or holomorphically extended) to *all* of *V* by [Riemann's analytic continuation theorem](wikipedia://en/81644#Riemann's_theorem). So the original function can be expressed in terms of $g$:

$$f(z) = \frac{1}{g(z)} + b$$

for all arguments *z* in *V* \\ {*z*<sub>0</sub>}. Consider the two possible cases for

$$\lim_{z \to z_0} g(z).$$

If the limit is 0, then *f* has a [pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) at *z*<sub>0</sub> . If the limit is not 0, then *z*<sub>0</sub> is a [removable singularity](wikipedia://en/81644) of *f* . Both possibilities contradict the assumption that the point *z*<sub>0</sub> is an [essential singularity](wikipedia://en/80862) of the function *f* . Hence the assumption is false and the theorem holds.

:::

## History {#History}

The history of this important theorem is described by [Collingwood](wikipedia://en/9990753) and [Lohwater](wikipedia://en/14380145).[^o11] It was published by Weierstrass in 1876 (in German) and by Sokhotski in 1868 in his Master thesis (in Russian). So it was called Sokhotski's theorem in the Russian literature and Weierstrass's theorem in the Western literature. The same theorem was published by Casorati in 1868, and by Briot and Bouquet in the *first edition* of their book (1859).[^o12] However, Briot and Bouquet *removed* this theorem from the second edition (1875).
