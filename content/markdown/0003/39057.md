# Straw man

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Straw man"}

*Image: McKinley Destroys Imperialism Straw Man.jpg.* U.S. president [William McKinley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_McKinley) fires a cannon (labeled "McKinley's Letter") at a "straw man" and its constructors ([Carl Schurz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Schurz), [Oswald Garrison Villard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Garrison_Villard), [Richard Olney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Olney)) in a great explosion. Caption: "SMASHED!", *[Harper's Weekly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harper's_Weekly)*, 22 September 1900.

A **straw man** fallacy (sometimes written as **strawman**) is the [informal fallacy](wikipedia://en/1655450) of refuting an argument different from the one actually under discussion, while not recognizing or acknowledging the distinction.[^o2] One who engages in this fallacy is said to be "attacking a straw man".

:::

The typical straw man argument creates the illusion of having refuted or defeated an opponent's proposition through the covert replacement of it with a different proposition (i.e., "stand up a straw man") and the subsequent refutation of that false argument ("knock down a straw man"), instead of the opponent's proposition.[^o3][^o4] Straw man arguments have been used throughout history in [polemical debate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polemic), particularly regarding highly charged emotional subjects.[^o5] It is known colloquially as "putting words in one's mouth", where one assumes an argument that their opponent hasn't formally proposed.

Straw man tactics in the United Kingdom may also be known as an [Aunt Sally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aunt_Sally#Language), after a pub game of the same name, where patrons throw sticks or battens at a post to knock off a [skittle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_cue_sports_terms#skittle) balanced on top.[^o6][^o7]

## Overview {#Overview}

The straw man [fallacy](wikipedia://en/53986) occurs in the following pattern of argument:

1. *Person 1* asserts proposition *X*.
1. *Person 2* argues against a superficially similar proposition *Y*, as though an argument against *Y* were an argument against *X*.

This reasoning is a [fallacy of relevance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallacy_of_relevance): it fails to address the proposition in question by misrepresenting the opposing position.

For example:

- Quoting an opponent's words out of context—i.e., choosing quotations that misrepresent the opponent's intentions (see [fallacy of quoting out of context](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallacy_of_quoting_out_of_context)).[^o4]
- Presenting someone who defends a position poorly as *the* defender, then denying that person's arguments—thus giving the appearance that *every* upholder of that position (and thus the position itself) has been defeated.[^o3]
- Oversimplifying an opponent's argument, then attacking this oversimplified version.
- Exaggerating (sometimes grossly) an opponent's argument, then attacking this exaggerated version.

### Contemporary revisions {#Contemporary_revisions}

In 2006, Robert Talisse and Scott Aikin expanded the application and use of the straw man fallacy beyond that of previous rhetorical scholars, arguing that the straw man fallacy can take two forms: the original form that misrepresents the opponent's position, which they call the *representative form*; and a new form they call the *selection form*.

The selection form focuses on a partial and weaker (and easier to refute) representation of the opponent's position. Then the easier refutation of this weaker position is claimed to refute the opponent's complete position. They point out the similarity of the selection form to the fallacy of [hasty generalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasty_generalization), in which the refutation of an opposing position that is weaker than the opponent's is claimed as a refutation of all opposing arguments. Because they have found significantly increased use of the selection form in modern political argumentation, they view its identification as an important new tool for the improvement of public discourse.[^o10]

Aikin and Casey expanded on this model in 2010, introducing a third form. Referring to the "representative form" as the classic *straw man*, and the "selection form" as the *weak man*, the third form is called the *hollow man*. A hollow man argument is one that is a complete fabrication, where both the viewpoint and the opponent expressing it do not in fact exist, or at the very least the arguer has never encountered them. Such arguments frequently take the form of vague phrasing such as "some say," "someone out there thinks" or similar [weasel words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weasel_word), or it might attribute a non-existent argument to a broad movement in general, rather than an individual or organization.[^o11][^o12]

::: {#o14 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Nut-picking"}

#### Nutpicking {#Nutpicking}

A variation on the selection form, or "weak man" argument, that combines with an [ad hominem](wikipedia://en/2226) and [fallacy of composition](wikipedia://en/523043) is *nutpicking* (or *nut picking*), a neologism coined by [Kevin Drum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Drum).[^o15] A combination of "nut" (i.e., insane person) and "[cherry picking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherry_picking_%28fallacy%29)", as well as a play on the word "nitpicking", nut picking refers to intentionally seeking out extremely fringe, non-representative statements from members of an opposing group and parading these as evidence of that entire group's incompetence or irrationality.[^o11]

:::

::: {#o17 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Steelmanning"}

### Steelmanning {#Steelmanning}

A *steel man argument* (or *steelmanning*) is the opposite of a straw man argument. Steelmanning is the practice of applying the rhetorical [principle of charity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_charity) through addressing the strongest form of the other person's argument, even if it is not the one they explicitly presented. Creating the strongest form of the opponent's argument may involve removing flawed assumptions that could be easily refuted or developing the strongest points which counter one's own position. Developing counters to steel man arguments may produce a stronger argument for one's own position.[^o18]

## Examples {#Examples}

In a 1977 appeal of a U.S. bank robbery conviction, a prosecuting attorney said in his oral argument:[^o21] "I submit to you that if you can't take this evidence and find these defendants guilty on this evidence then we might as well open all the banks and say, 'Come on and get the money, boys', because we'll never be able to convict them." This was a straw man designed to alarm the appellate judges; the chance that the precedent set by one case would literally make it impossible to convict any bank robbers is remote.

Another example of a strawman argument is U.S. president [Richard Nixon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nixon)'s 1952 "[Checkers speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Checkers_speech)".[^o22][^o23] When campaigning for vice president in 1952, Nixon was accused of having appropriated \$18,000 in campaign funds for his personal use. In a televised response, based on Franklin D. Roosevelt's [Fala speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fala_speech), he spoke about another gift, a dog he had been given by a supporter:[^o22][^o23]

It was a little cocker spaniel dog, in a crate he had sent all the way from Texas, black and white, spotted, and our little girl [Tricia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tricia_Nixon), six years old, named it Checkers. And, you know, the kids, like all kids, loved the dog, and I just want to say this right now, that, regardless of what they say about it, we are going to keep it.

This was a straw man response; his critics had never criticized the dog as a gift or suggested he return it. This argument was successful at distracting a number of people from the funds and portraying his critics as nitpicking and heartless. Nixon received an outpouring of public support and remained on the ticket. He and Eisenhower were later elected.

[Christopher Tindale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Tindale) presents, as an example, the following passage from a draft of a bill (HCR 74) considered by the [Louisiana State Legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_State_Legislature) in 2001:[^o28]

Whereas, the writings of [Charles Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin), the father of evolution, promoted the justification of racism, and his books *[On the Origin of Species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Origin_of_Species)* and *[The Descent of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Descent_of_Man)* postulate a hierarchy of superior and inferior races. ... Therefore, be it resolved that the legislature of Louisiana does hereby deplore all instances and all ideologies of [racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racism), does hereby reject the core concepts of Darwinist ideology that certain races and classes of humans are inherently superior to others, and does hereby condemn the extent to which these philosophies have been used to justify and approve racist practices.

Tindale comments that "the portrait painted of Darwinian ideology is a caricature, one not borne out by any objective survey of the works cited." The fact that similar misrepresentations of Darwinian thinking have been used to justify and approve racist practices is beside the point: the position that the legislation is attacking and dismissing is a straw man. In subsequent debate, this error was recognized, and the eventual bill omitted all mention of Darwin and Darwinist ideology.[^o28] Darwin passionately opposed slavery and worked to intellectually confront the notions of "[scientific racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_racism)" that were used to justify it.[^o24]

## Etymology {#Etymology}

As a fallacy, the identification and name of straw man arguments are of relatively recent date, although [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) makes remarks that suggest a similar concern.[^o27] [Isaac Watts](wikipedia://en/239143) writes in his *Logick* (1724): "They dress up the opinion of their adversary as they please, and ascribe sentiments to him which he doth not acknowledge; and when they have with a great deal of pomp attacked and confounded these images of straw of their own making, they triumph over their adversary as though they had utterly confuted his opinion."[^o26]

[Douglas N. Walton](wikipedia://en/5725671) identified "the first inclusion of it we can find in a textbook as an informal fallacy" in [Stuart Chase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Chase)'s *Guides to Straight Thinking* from 1956 (p. 40).[^o27][^o28] By contrast, [Hamblin](wikipedia://en/19501790)'s classic text *Fallacies* (1970) neither mentions it as a distinct type, nor even as a historical term.[^o27][^o28]

The term's origins are a matter of debate, though the usage of the term in rhetoric suggests a human figure made of [straw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straw) that is easy to knock down or destroy—such as a [military training dummy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straw_man_%28dummy%29), [scarecrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scarecrow), or [effigy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effigy).[^o29] A common but [false etymology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_etymology) is that it refers to men who stood outside courthouses with a straw in their shoe to signal their willingness to be a false witness.[^o30] The *Online Etymology Dictionary* states that the term "man of straw" can be traced back to 1620 as "an easily refuted imaginary opponent in an argument".[^o31]

### Related usage {#Related_usage}

Reverend [William Harrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Harrison_%28priest%29), in *A Description of England* (1577), complained that when men lived in houses of willow they were men of oak, but now they lived in houses of oak and had become men of willow and "a great manie altogither of straw, which is a sore alteration.[^o33]

[Martin Luther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther) blames his opponents for misrepresenting his arguments in his work *[On the Babylonian Captivity of the Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Babylonian_Captivity_of_the_Church)* (1520):

| Latin | Unattributed English translation | Philadelphia Edition translation |
|---|---|---|
| Respondeo, id genus disputandi omnibus familiare esse, qui contra Lutherum scribunt, ut hoc asserant quod impugnant, aut fingant quod impugnent.[^o34] | I answer that this kind of discussion is familiar to all who write against Luther, so they can assert (or: 'plant', literally: 'sow') what they attack, or pretend what they attack. | My answer is, that this sort of argument is common to all those who write against Luther. They assert the very things they assail, or they set up a man of straw whom they may attack.[^o35][^o36] |

In the quote, he responds to arguments of the Roman Catholic Church and clergy attempting to delegitimize his criticisms, specifically on the correct way to serve the [Eucharist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucharist). The church claimed Martin Luther is arguing against serving the Eucharist according to one type of serving practice; Martin Luther states he never asserted that in his criticisms towards them and in fact they themselves are making this argument. Luther's Latin text does not use the phrase "man of straw", but it is used in a widespread early 20th century English translation of his work, the Philadelphia Edition.[^o37]
