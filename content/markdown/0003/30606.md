# Tetrahedron

*Image: Triangular pyramid1.png.* A tetrahedron.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Tetrahedron"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **tetrahedron** (: **tetrahedra** or **tetrahedrons**), also known as a **triangular pyramid**, is a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) composed of four triangular [faces](wikipedia://en/149926), six straight [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034), and four [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417). The tetrahedron is the simplest of all the ordinary [convex polyhedra](wikipedia://en/1817885).

:::

The tetrahedron is the [three-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-dimensional) case of the more general concept of a [Euclidean](wikipedia://en/9417) [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781), and may thus also be called a **3-simplex**.

The tetrahedron is one kind of [pyramid](wikipedia://en/3307045), which is a polyhedron with a flat [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) base and triangular faces connecting the base to a common point. In the case of a tetrahedron, the base is a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) (any of the four faces can be considered the base), so a tetrahedron is also known as a "triangular pyramid".

Like all [convex polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedra), a tetrahedron can be folded from a single sheet of paper. It has two such [nets](wikipedia://en/1148129).[^o3]

For any tetrahedron there exists a sphere (called the [circumsphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumsphere)) on which all four vertices lie, and another sphere (the [insphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insphere)) [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to the tetrahedron's faces.[^o4]

## Regular tetrahedron {#Regular_tetrahedron}

A [regular tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/5242521) is a tetrahedron in which all four faces are [equilateral triangles](wikipedia://en/173285). In other words, all of its faces are the same size and shape (congruent) and all edges are the same length. The regular tetrahedron is the simplest [deltahedron](wikipedia://en/493995), a polyhedron all of whose faces are equilateral triangles; there are seven other convex deltahedra.[^n1]

## Irregular tetrahedra {#Irregular_tetrahedra}

If all three pairs of opposite edges of a tetrahedron are [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944), then it is called an *[orthocentric tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/34286384)*. When only one pair of opposite edges are perpendicular, it is called a ***semi-orthocentric tetrahedron***. In a *[trirectangular tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/34275979)* the three face angles at *one* vertex are [right angles](wikipedia://en/76956), as at the corner of a cube.

An **isodynamic tetrahedron** is one in which the [cevians](wikipedia://en/6135790) that join the vertices to the [incenters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle_and_excircles_of_a_triangle) of the opposite faces are [concurrent](wikipedia://en/2206157).

An **isogonic tetrahedron** has concurrent cevians that join the vertices to the points of contact of the opposite faces with the [inscribed sphere](wikipedia://en/991786) of the tetrahedron.

### Disphenoid {#Disphenoid}

*Image: Oblate tetrahedrille cell.png.* A space-filling tetrahedral disphenoid inside a cube. Two edges have [dihedral angles](wikipedia://en/338046) of 90°, and four edges have dihedral angles of 60°.

A [disphenoid](wikipedia://en/8476166) is a tetrahedron with four congruent triangles as faces; the triangles necessarily have all angles acute. The regular tetrahedron is a special case of a disphenoid. Other names for the same shape include bisphenoid, isosceles tetrahedron and equifacial tetrahedron.

::: {#o10 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="3-orthoscheme"}

### Orthoschemes {#Orthoschemes}

*Image: Triangulated cube.svg.* A cube dissected into six characteristic orthoschemes.

A **3-orthoscheme** is a tetrahedron where all four faces are [right triangles](wikipedia://en/30654#By_internal_angles). A 3-orthoscheme is not a disphenoid, because its opposite edges are not of equal length. It is not possible to construct a [disphenoid](wikipedia://en/8476166#Special_cases_and_generalizations) with right triangle or obtuse triangle faces.

An [orthoscheme](wikipedia://en/20740340) is an irregular [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) that is the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of a [tree](wikipedia://en/48560) in which all edges are mutually perpendicular. In a 3-dimensional orthoscheme, the tree consists of three perpendicular edges connecting all four vertices in a linear path that makes two right-angled turns. The 3-orthoscheme is a tetrahedron having two right angles at each of two vertices, so another name for it is ***birectangular tetrahedron***. It is also called a ***quadrirectangular*** tetrahedron because it contains four right angles.[^o11]

Coxeter also calls quadrirectangular tetrahedra "characteristic tetrahedra", because of their integral relationship to the regular polytopes and their symmetry groups.[^n2] For example, the special case of a 3-orthoscheme with equal-length perpendicular edges is [characteristic of the cube](wikipedia://en/23470#Ancient), which means that the cube can be subdivided into instances of this orthoscheme. If its three perpendicular edges are of unit length, its remaining edges are two of length $\sqrt{2}$ and one of length $\sqrt{3}$, so all its edges are edges or diagonals of the cube. The cube can be dissected into six such 3-orthoschemes four different ways, with all six surrounding the same $\sqrt{3}$ cube diagonal. The cube can also be dissected into 48 *smaller* instances of this same characteristic 3-orthoscheme (just one way, by all of its symmetry planes at once). The characteristic tetrahedron of the cube is an example of a [Heronian tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/426551).

Every regular polytope, including the regular tetrahedron, has its [characteristic orthoscheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthoscheme#Characteristic_simplex_of_the_general_regular_polytope). There is a 3-orthoscheme, which is the "characteristic tetrahedron of the regular tetrahedron". The regular tetrahedron node\_1 is subdivided into 24 instances of its characteristic tetrahedron node by its planes of symmetry. The 24 characteristic tetrahedra of the regular tetrahedron occur in two mirror-image forms, 12 of each.

### Space-filling tetrahedra {#Space-filling_tetrahedra}

A **space-filling tetrahedron** packs with directly congruent or enantiomorphous ([mirror image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror_image)) copies of itself to tile space.[^n4] The cube can be dissected into six 3-orthoschemes, three left-handed and three right-handed (one of each at each cube face), and cubes can fill space, so the characteristic 3-orthoscheme of the cube is a space-filling tetrahedron in this sense. (The characteristic orthoscheme of the cube is one of the [Hill tetrahedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill_tetrahedra), a family of space-filling tetrahedra. All space-filling tetrahedra are [scissors-congruent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scissors-congruent) to a cube.)

A disphenoid can be a space-filling tetrahedron in the directly congruent sense, as in the [disphenoid tetrahedral honeycomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disphenoid_tetrahedral_honeycomb). Regular tetrahedra, however, cannot fill space by themselves (moreover, it is not scissors-congruent to any other polyhedra which can fill the space, see [Hilbert's third problem](wikipedia://en/152703)). The [tetrahedral-octahedral honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3735260) fills space with alternating regular tetrahedron cells and regular [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635) cells in a ratio of 2:1.

### Fundamental domains {#Fundamental_domains}

*Image: Coxeter-Dynkin 3-space groups.png.* For Euclidean 3-space, there are 3 simple and related Goursat tetrahedra. They can be seen as points on and within a cube.

An irregular tetrahedron which is the [fundamental domain](wikipedia://en/469365)[^n5] of a [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/297004) is an example of a [Goursat tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/24104450). The Goursat tetrahedra generate all the regular polyhedra (and many other uniform polyhedra) by mirror reflections, a process referred to as [Wythoff's kaleidoscopic construction](wikipedia://en/2950777).

For polyhedra, Wythoff's construction arranges three mirrors at angles to each other, as in a [kaleidoscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaleidoscope). Unlike a cylindrical kaleidoscope, Wythoff's mirrors are located at three faces of a Goursat tetrahedron such that all three mirrors intersect at a single point. (The [Coxeter-Dynkin diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter-Dynkin_diagram) of the generated polyhedron contains three *nodes* representing the three mirrors. The dihedral angle between each pair of mirrors is encoded in the diagram, as well as the location of a single *generating point* which is multiplied by mirror reflections into the vertices of the polyhedron.)

Among the Goursat tetrahedra which generate 3-dimensional [honeycombs](wikipedia://en/6008654) we can recognize an orthoscheme (the characteristic tetrahedron of the cube), a double orthoscheme (the characteristic tetrahedron of the cube face-bonded to its mirror image), and the space-filling disphenoid illustrated above.[^n6] The disphenoid is the double orthoscheme face-bonded to its mirror image (a quadruple orthoscheme). Thus all three of these Goursat tetrahedra, and all the polyhedra they generate by reflections, can be [dissected into characteristic tetrahedra of the cube](wikipedia://en/56244281).

## Subdivision and similarity classes {#Subdivision_and_similarity_classes}

Tetrahedra subdivision is a process used in computational geometry and 3D modeling to divide a tetrahedron into several smaller tetrahedra. This process enhances the complexity and detail of tetrahedral meshes, which is particularly beneficial in numerical simulations, finite element analysis, and computer graphics. One of the commonly used subdivision methods is the **Longest Edge Bisection (LEB)**, which identifies the longest edge of the tetrahedron and bisects it at its midpoint, generating two new, smaller tetrahedra. When this process is repeated multiple times, bisecting all the tetrahedra generated in each previous iteration, the process is called iterative LEB.

A **similarity class** is the set of tetrahedra with the same geometric shape, regardless of their specific position, orientation, and scale. So, any two tetrahedra belonging to the same similarity class may be transformed to each other by an affine transformation. The outcome of having a limited number of similarity classes in iterative subdivision methods is significant for computational modeling and simulation. It reduces the variability in the shapes and sizes of generated tetrahedra, preventing the formation of highly irregular elements that could compromise simulation results.

The iterative LEB of the regular tetrahedron has been shown to produce only 8 similarity classes. Furthermore, in the case of nearly equilateral tetrahedra where their two longest edges are not connected to each other, and the ratio between their longest and their shortest edge is less than or equal to $\sqrt{3/2}$, the iterated LEB produces no more than 37 similarity classes.[^o21]

## General properties {#General_properties}

In general, a tetrahedron is a [three-dimensional](wikipedia://en/3054853) object with four faces, six edges, and four vertices. It can be considered as [pyramid](wikipedia://en/3307045) whenever one of its faces can be considered as the [base](wikipedia://en/9579379). Its [1-skeleton](wikipedia://en/74718534) can be generally seen as a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) by [Steinitz's theorem](wikipedia://en/19762817), known as **tetrahedral graph**, one of the [Platonic graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_graph). It is [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_4$ because every pair of its vertices has a unique edge. In a plane, this graph can be regarded as a triangle in which three vertices connect to its fourth vertex in the center, known as the [universal vertex](wikipedia://en/51352780); hence, the tetrahedral graph is a [wheel graph](wikipedia://en/1655876).[^n7]

The tetrahedron is one of the polyhedra that does not have [space diagonal](wikipedia://en/1337505); the other polyhedra with such a property are [Császár polyhedron](wikipedia://en/11731171) and [Schonhardt polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schonhardt_polyhedron).[^n8] It is also known as [3-simplex](wikipedia://en/39781), the generalization of a triangle in multi-dimension. It is [self-dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-dual), meaning its [dual polyhedron](wikipedia://en/8815) is a tetrahedron itself.[^n9] Many other properties of tetrahedra are explicitly described in the following sections.

### Volume {#Volume}

The volume of a tetrahedron is given by the formula:

$$V = \frac{1}{3}Ah.$$

where $A$ is the [base](wikipedia://en/9579379)'s area and $h$ is the height from the base to the apex. This applies to each of the four choices of the base, so the distances from the apices to the opposite faces are inversely proportional to the areas of these faces. Another way is by dissecting a triangular prism into three pieces.[^n10]

#### Algebraic approach {#Algebraic_approach}

A [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) approach is an alternative way by the given vertices in terms of [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28mathematics%29) as:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{a} &= (a_1, a_2, a_3), \\ \mathbf{b} &= (b_1, b_2, b_3), \\ \mathbf{c} &= (c_1, c_2, c_3), \\ \mathbf{d} &= (d_1, d_2, d_3). \end{aligned}$$

In terms of a [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468), the volume of a tetrahedron is $\frac{1}{6} \det(\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{d}, \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{d}, \mathbf{c} - \mathbf{d})$, one-sixth of any [parallelepiped](wikipedia://en/23975)'s volume sharing three converging edges with it.[^n11]

Similarly by the given vertices, another approach is by the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of the scalar triple product, representing the absolute values of determinants $6 \cdot V = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{a} & \mathbf{b} & \mathbf{c} \end{vmatrix}$. Hence

$$36 \cdot V^2 =\begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{a^2} & \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} & \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{c} \\ \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} & \mathbf{b^2} & \mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{c} \\ \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{c} & \mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{c} & \mathbf{c^2} \end{vmatrix}.$$

Here $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = ab\cos{\gamma}$, $\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{c} = bc\cos{\alpha}$, and $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{c} = ac\cos{\beta}.$ The variables $a$, $b$, and $c$ denotes each [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) of a vector $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$, and $\mathbf{c}$ respectively. This gives

$$V = \frac {abc} {6} \sqrt{1 + 2\cos{\alpha}\cos{\beta}\cos{\gamma}-\cos^2{\alpha}-\cos^2{\beta}-\cos^2{\gamma}}, \,$$

where the [Greek lowercase letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_letter) denotes the plane angles occurring in vertex $\mathbf{d}$: the angle $\alpha$ is an angle between the two edges connecting the vertex $\mathbf{d}$ to the vertices $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{c}$; the angle $\beta$ does so for the vertices $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{c}$; while the angle $\gamma$ is defined by the position of the vertices $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$. Considering that $\mathbf{d} = 0$, then

$$6 \cdot V = \left| \det \left( \begin{matrix} a_1 & b_1 & c_1 & d_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 & d_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 & c_3 & d_3 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \end{matrix} \right) \right|\,.$$

Given the distances between the vertices of a tetrahedron the volume can be computed using the [Cayley–Menger determinant](wikipedia://en/2826108):

$$288 \cdot V^2 = \begin{vmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & d_{12}^2 & d_{13}^2 & d_{14}^2 \\ 1 & d_{12}^2 & 0 & d_{23}^2 & d_{24}^2 \\ 1 & d_{13}^2 & d_{23}^2 & 0 & d_{34}^2 \\ 1 & d_{14}^2 & d_{24}^2 & d_{34}^2 & 0 \end{vmatrix}$$

where the subscripts $i, j \in \{1,2,3,4\}$ represent the vertices $\{\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}, \mathbf{d}\}$, and $d_{ij}$ is the pairwise distance between them, the length of the edge connecting the two vertices. A negative value of the determinant means that a tetrahedron cannot be constructed with the given distances. This formula, sometimes called [Tartaglia's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartaglia's_formula), is essentially due to the painter [Piero della Francesca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_della_Francesca) in the 15th century, as a three-dimensional analogue of the 1st century [Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123) for the area of a triangle.

#### Other approaches {#Other_approaches}

Let $a$, $b$, and $c$ be the lengths of three edges that meet at a point, and $x$, $y$, and $z$ be those of the opposite edges. The volume of the tetrahedron $V$ is:[^n12]

$$V = \frac{\sqrt{4 a^2 b^2 c^2-a^2 X^2-b^2 Y^2-c^2 Z^2+X Y Z}}{12}$$

where

$$\begin{aligned} X &= b^2+c^2-x^2, \\ Y &= a^2+c^2-y^2, \\ Z &= a^2+b^2-z^2. \end{aligned}$$

The above formula uses six lengths of edges, and the following formula uses three lengths of edges and three angles.[^n12]

$$V = \frac{abc}{6} \sqrt{1 + 2\cos{\alpha}\cos{\beta}\cos{\gamma}-\cos^2{\alpha}-\cos^2{\beta}-\cos^2{\gamma}}$$

The volume of a tetrahedron can be ascertained by using the Heron formula. Suppose $U$, $V$, $W$, $u$, $v$, and $w$ are the lengths of the tetrahedron's edges as in the following image. Here, the first three form a triangle, with $u$ opposite $U$, $v$ opposite $V$, and $w$ opposite $W$. Then,

$$V = \frac{\sqrt {\,( - p + q + r + s)\,(p - q + r + s)\,(p + q - r + s)\,(p + q + r - s)}}{192\,u\,v\,w}$$

*Image: tetrahedron\_volume.svg.* Six edge-lengths of Tetrahedron

where

$$\begin{aligned} p = \sqrt{xYZ}, &\quad q = \sqrt{yZX}, \\ r = \sqrt{zXY}, &\quad s = \sqrt {xyz}, \end{aligned}$$

and

$$\begin{aligned} X = (w - U + v)(U + v + w), &\quad x = (U - v + w)(v - w + U), \\ Y = (u - V + w)(V + w + u), &\quad y = (V - w + u)(w - u + V), \\ Z = (v - W + u)(W + u + v), &\quad z = (W - u + v)\,(u - v + W). \end{aligned}$$

Any plane containing a bimedian (connector of opposite edges' midpoints) of a tetrahedron [bisects](wikipedia://en/152547) the volume of the tetrahedron.[^n13]

For tetrahedra in [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742) or in three-dimensional [elliptic geometry](wikipedia://en/236020), the [dihedral angles](wikipedia://en/338046) of the tetrahedron determine its shape and hence its volume. In these cases, the volume is given by the [Murakami–Yano formula](wikipedia://en/34677780), after Jun Murakami and Masakazu Yano.[^n14] However, in Euclidean space, scaling a tetrahedron changes its volume but not its dihedral angles, so no such formula can exist.

Any two opposite edges of a tetrahedron lie on two [skew lines](wikipedia://en/1975821), and the distance between the edges is defined as the distance between the two skew lines. Let $d$ be the distance between the skew lines formed by opposite edges $a$ and $\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{c}$ as calculated [here](wikipedia://en/1975821#Distance_between_two_skew_lines). Then another formula for the volume of a tetrahedron $V$ is given by

$$V = \frac {d |(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{(b-c)})|}{6}.$$

### Analogous to a triangle {#Analogous_to_a_triangle}

The tetrahedron has many properties analogous to those of a triangle, including an insphere, circumsphere, medial tetrahedron, and exspheres. It has respective centers such as incenter, circumcenter, excenters, [Spieker center](wikipedia://en/9502005) and points such as a centroid. However, there is generally no orthocenter in the sense of intersecting altitudes.[^o36]

[Gaspard Monge](wikipedia://en/77840) found a center that exists in every tetrahedron, now known as the **Monge point**: the point where the six midplanes of a tetrahedron intersect. A midplane is defined as a plane that is orthogonal to an edge joining any two vertices that also contains the centroid of an opposite edge formed by joining the other two vertices.[^n15] An orthogonal line dropped from the Monge point to any face meets that face at the midpoint of the line segment between that face's orthocenter and the foot of the altitude dropped from the opposite vertex. If the tetrahedron's altitudes do intersect, then the Monge point and the orthocenter coincide to give the class of [orthocentric tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/34286384).

A line segment joining a vertex of a tetrahedron with the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) of the opposite face is called a *median* and a line segment joining the midpoints of two opposite edges is called a *bimedian* of the tetrahedron. Hence, a tetrahedron has four medians and three bimedians. These seven line segments are all [concurrent](wikipedia://en/2206157) at a point called the *centroid* of the tetrahedron.[^n16] In addition, [the four medians are divided in a 3:1 ratio by the centroid](wikipedia://en/54140352).[^n17] The centroid of a tetrahedron is the midpoint between its Monge point and circumcenter. These points define the *Euler line* of the tetrahedron that is analogous to the [Euler line](wikipedia://en/203622) of a triangle.

The [nine-point circle](wikipedia://en/161243) of the general triangle has an analogue in the circumsphere of a tetrahedron's medial tetrahedron. It is the **twelve-point sphere** and besides the centroids of the four faces of the reference tetrahedron, it passes through four substitute *Euler points*, one third of the way from the Monge point toward each of the four vertices. Finally, it passes through the four base points of orthogonal lines dropped from each Euler point to the face not containing the vertex that generated the Euler point.[^o40]

The center $T$ of the twelve-point sphere also lies on the Euler line. Unlike its triangular counterpart, this center lies one-third of the way from the Monge point $M$ towards the circumcenter. Additionally, an orthogonal line through $T$ to a chosen face is coplanar with two other orthogonal lines to the same face. The first is an orthogonal line passing through the corresponding Euler point to the chosen face. The second is an orthogonal line passing through the centroid of the chosen face. This orthogonal line through the twelve-point center lies midway between the Euler point orthogonal line and the centroidal orthogonal line. Furthermore, for any face, the twelve-point center lies at the midpoint of the corresponding Euler point and the orthocenter for that face.

The radius of the twelve-point sphere is one-third of the circumradius of the reference tetrahedron.

There is a relation among the angles made by the faces of a general tetrahedron given by[^o41]

$$\begin{vmatrix} -1 & \cos{(\alpha_{12})} & \cos{(\alpha_{13})} & \cos{(\alpha_{14})}\\ \cos{(\alpha_{12})} & -1 & \cos{(\alpha_{23})} & \cos{(\alpha_{24})} \\ \cos{(\alpha_{13})} & \cos{(\alpha_{23})} & -1 & \cos{(\alpha_{34})} \\ \cos{(\alpha_{14})} & \cos{(\alpha_{24})} & \cos{(\alpha_{34})} & -1 \\ \end{vmatrix} = 0\,$$

where $\alpha_{ij}$ is the angle between the faces $i$ and $j$.

The [geometric median](wikipedia://en/10324687) of the vertex position coordinates of a tetrahedron and its isogonic center are associated, under circumstances analogous to those observed for a triangle. [Lorenz Lindelöf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorenz_Lindelöf) found that, corresponding to any given tetrahedron is a point now known as an isogonic center, $O$, at which the solid angles subtended by the faces are equal, having a common value of $\pi$ [sr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steradian), and at which the angles subtended by opposite edges are equal.[^o42] A solid angle of $\pi$ sr is one quarter of that subtended by all of space. When all the solid angles at the vertices of a tetrahedron are smaller than $\pi$ sr, $O$ lies inside the tetrahedron. Because the sum of distances from $O$ to the vertices is a minimum, $O$ coincides with the [geometric median](wikipedia://en/10324687) $M$ of the vertices. If the solid angle at one of the vertices $v$ measures exactly $\pi$ sr, then $O$ and $M$ coincide with $v$. However, if a tetrahedron has a vertex $v$ with solid angle greater than $\pi$ sr, $M$ still corresponds to $v$, but $O$ lies outside the tetrahedron.

### Trigonometry and the space of all shapes {#Trigonometry_and_the_space_of_all_shapes}

*Image: tetra.png.* Illustration of the law of sines for tetrahedra

A corollary of the usual [law of sines](wikipedia://en/113222) is that in a tetrahedron with vertices $O$, $A$, $B$, $C$, is given by

$$\sin\angle OAB\cdot\sin\angle OBC\cdot\sin\angle OCA = \sin\angle OAC\cdot\sin\angle OCB\cdot\sin\angle OBA.$$

One may view the two sides of this identity as corresponding to clockwise and counterclockwise orientations of the surface. Putting any of the four vertices in the role of $O$ yields four such identities, but at most three of them are independent. If the "clockwise" sides of three of them are multiplied and the product is inferred to be equal to the product of the "counterclockwise" sides of the same three identities. Hence, common factors are cancelled from both sides, and the result is the fourth identity.

Three angles are the angles of some triangle if and only if their sum is 180° (π radians). What condition on 12 angles is necessary and sufficient for them to be the 12 angles of some tetrahedron? The sum of the angles of any side of the tetrahedron must be 180°. Since there are four such triangles, there are four such constraints on sums of angles, and the number of [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321606) is thereby reduced from 12 to 8. The four relations given by this sine law further reduce the number of degrees of freedom, from 8 down to not 4 but 5, since the fourth constraint is not independent of the first three. Thus, the space of all shapes of tetrahedra is 5-dimensional.[^o44]

Let $P_1$, $P_2$, $P_3$, $P_4$ be the points of a tetrahedron. Let $\Delta_i$ be the area of the face opposite vertex $P_i$ and let $\theta_{ij}$ be the dihedral angle between the two faces of the tetrahedron adjacent to the edge $P_i P_j$. The [law of cosines](wikipedia://en/19480890) for a tetrahedron, which relates the areas of the faces of the tetrahedron to the dihedral angles about a vertex, is given by the following relation:[^n18]

$$\Delta_i^2 = \Delta_j^2 + \Delta_k^2 + \Delta_l^2 - 2(\Delta_j\Delta_k\cos\theta_{il} + \Delta_j\Delta_l \cos\theta_{ik} + \Delta_k\Delta_l \cos\theta_{ij})$$

### Interior point {#Interior_point}

Let $P$ be any interior point of a tetrahedron of volume $V$ for which the vertices are $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$, and for which the areas of the opposite faces are $F_a$, $F_b$, $F_c$, and $F_d$. Then,[^n19]

$$PA \cdot F_\mathrm{a} + PB \cdot F_\mathrm{b} + PC \cdot F_\mathrm{c} + PD \cdot F_\mathrm{d} \geq 9V.$$

For vertices $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$, interior point $P$, and feet $J$, $K$, $L$, and $M$ of the perpendiculars from $P$ to the faces, and suppose the faces have equal areas, then:[^n20]

$$PA+PB+PC+PD \geq 3(PJ+PK+PL+PM).$$

### Inradius {#Inradius}

Denoting the inradius of a tetrahedron as $r$ and the [inradii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inradius) of its triangular faces as $r_1$ for $i=1,2,3,4$, then:[^n21]

$$\frac{1}{r_1^2} + \frac{1}{r_2^2} + \frac{1}{r_3^2} + \frac{1}{r_4^2} \leq \frac{2}{r^2},$$

with equality if and only if the tetrahedron is regular.

If $A_1$, $A_2$, $A_3$ and $A_4$ denote the area of each faces, the value of $r$ is given by

$$r=\frac{3V}{A_1+A_2+A_3+A_4}.$$

This formula is obtained from dividing the tetrahedron into four tetrahedra whose points are the three points of one of the original faces and the incenter. Since the four subtetrahedra fill the volume, then:

$$V = \frac13A_1r+\frac13A_2r+\frac13A_3r+\frac13A_4r .$$

### Circumradius {#Circumradius}

Denote the circumradius of a tetrahedron as *R*. Let *a*, *b*, *c* be the lengths of the three edges that meet at a vertex, and *A*, *B*, *C* the length of the opposite edges. Let *V* be the volume of the tetrahedron. Then[^o52][^o53]

$$R=\frac{\sqrt{(aA+bB+cC)(aA+bB-cC)(aA-bB+cC)(-aA+bB+cC)}}{24V}.$$

### Circumcenter {#Circumcenter}

The circumcenter of a tetrahedron can be found as intersection of three bisector planes. A bisector plane is defined as the plane centered on, and orthogonal to an edge of the tetrahedron. With this definition, the circumcenter $C$ of a tetrahedron with vertices $x_{0}$,$x_{1}$,$x_{2}$,$x_{3}$ can be formulated as matrix-vector product:[^o55]

$$\begin{aligned} C &= A^{-1}B & \text{where} & \ & A = \left(\begin{matrix}\left[x_1 - x_0\right]^T \\ \left[x_2 - x_0\right]^T \\ \left[x_3 - x_0\right]^T \end{matrix}\right) & \ & \text{and} & \ & B = \frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{matrix} \|x_1\|^2 - \|x_0\|^2 \\ \|x_2\|^2 - \|x_0\|^2 \\ \|x_3\|^2 - \|x_0\|^2 \end{matrix}\right) \\ \end{aligned}$$

Unlike obtuse triangle, the circumcenter may not always lie outside of an obtuse tetrahedron (i.e. when one or more [dihedral angles](wikipedia://en/338046) are greater than $\pi/2$), nor does it necessarily lie inside for an acute tetrahedron (i.e. when all dihedral angles are less than $\pi/2$) [^o56]

## Integer tetrahedra {#Integer_tetrahedra}

There exist tetrahedra having integer-valued edge lengths, face areas and volume. These are called [Heronian tetrahedra](wikipedia://en/426551). One example has one edge of 896, the opposite edge of 990 and the other four edges of 1073; two faces are [isosceles triangles](wikipedia://en/315428) with areas of 436800 and the other two are isosceles with areas of 47120, while the volume is 124185600.[^o58]

A tetrahedron can have integer volume and consecutive integers as edges, an example being the one with edges 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, and 11 and volume 48.[^o59]

## Applications {#Applications}

The [Royal Game of Ur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Game_of_Ur), dating from 2600 BC, was played with a set of tetrahedral dice. Especially in [roleplaying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roleplaying), this solid is known as a [4-sided die](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-sided_die), one of the more common [polyhedral dice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedral_dice), with the number rolled appearing around the bottom or on the top vertex. Some [Rubik's Cube](wikipedia://en/25971)-like puzzles are tetrahedral, such as the [Pyraminx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyraminx) and [Pyramorphix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramorphix).

[Stanley Kubrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Kubrick) originally intended the [monolith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monolith_%28Space_Odyssey%29) in *[2001: A Space Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey_%28film%29)* to be a tetrahedron, according to [Marvin Minsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marvin_Minsky), a cognitive scientist and expert on [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence) who advised Kubrick on the [HAL 9000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HAL_9000) computer and other aspects of the movie. Kubrick scrapped the idea of using the tetrahedron when a viewer who saw footage of it did not recognize what it was, and he did not want anything in the film that regular people did not understand.[^o62]

The tetrahedron with regular faces is a solution to an old puzzle asking to form four equilateral triangles using six unbroken matchsticks. The solution places the matchsticks along the edges of a tetrahedron.[^o63]

Several applications of tetrahedra in related fields with engineering are the following:

- In [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506), complicated three-dimensional shapes are commonly broken down into, or [approximated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approximate) by, a [polygonal mesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygon_mesh) of irregular [tetrahedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedra) in the process of setting up the equations for [finite element analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_element_analysis) especially in the [numerical solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_solution) of [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations).[^n22] These methods have wide applications in practical applications in [computational fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/305924),[^n23] [aerodynamics](wikipedia://en/2819), [electromagnetic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field), [civil engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_engineering), [chemical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_engineering), [naval architecture and engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_architecture), and related fields.
- The tetrahedron is [inherently rigid](wikipedia://en/1458875).[^n24] For this reason, it is often used to stiffen frame structures such as [spaceframes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceframe).
- The tetrahedron is used in [caltrops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caltrop) to provide an [area denial weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area_denial_weapon). This is due to their nature of having a sharp corner that always points upwards.[^n25] Large steel tetrahedra have been used as [anti-tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-tank) measures,[^n26] or concrete tetrahedra as [tetrapods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrapod_%28structure%29) to break down waves at coastlines.[^n27]
- At some [airfields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airfield), a large frame in the shape of a tetrahedron with two sides covered with a thin material is mounted on a rotating pivot and always points into the wind. It is built big enough to be seen from the air and is sometimes illuminated. Its purpose is to serve as a reference to pilots, indicating wind direction.[^o70]

The tetrahedron can be described as [tetrahedral molecular geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedral_molecular_geometry) in [stereochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereochemistry).[^n28] This chemical structure can be found in [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) H and [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane) (CH).[^n29] The tetrahedron shape is seen in nature in [covalently bonded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covalent_bond) molecules. All [sp<sup>3</sup>-hybridized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_hybridisation) atoms are surrounded by atoms (or [lone electron pairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lone_pair)) at the four corners of a tetrahedron. For this reason, one of the leading journals in organic chemistry is called *[Tetrahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedron_%28journal%29)*. For a perfect tetrahedron, the [central angle](wikipedia://en/462705) between any two vertices is arccos(−$\tfrac{1}{3}$), or approximately 109.47°.[^n30]

Quaternary [phase diagrams](wikipedia://en/56398) of mixtures of chemical substances are represented graphically as tetrahedra. However, quaternary phase diagrams in [communication engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_engineering) are represented graphically on a two-dimensional plane.

The vertices of a regular tetrahedron applies as the minimum solution known for $n = 4$ in both [Thomson problem](wikipedia://en/3104018) (concerning the minimum-energy configuration of $n$ charged particles on a sphere) and for the [Tammes problem](wikipedia://en/19332734) (of constructing a [spherical code](wikipedia://en/22821969) maximizing the smallest distance among the points).[^n31]

If six equal [resistors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resistor) are [soldered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solder) together to form a tetrahedron, then the resistance measured between any two vertices is half that of one resistor.[^o75] Since [silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon) is the most common [semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor) used in [solid-state electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-state_electronics), and silicon has a [valence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_%28chemistry%29) of four, the tetrahedral shape of the four chemical bonds in silicon is a strong influence on how [crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal) of silicon form and what shapes they assume.

Tetrahedra are used in color space conversion algorithms specifically for cases in which the luminance axis diagonally segments the color space (e.g. RGB, CMY).[^o76]

The [tetrahedral hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedral_hypothesis), originally published by [William Lowthian Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lowthian_Green), explains the formation of the Earth with the shape of a tetrahedron.[^o77] It was popular through the early 20th century.[^o78][^o79]

## Related topics {#Related_topics}

Regular tetrahedra cannot [tessellate space](wikipedia://en/6008654) by themselves, although this result seems likely enough that [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) claimed it was possible. However, two regular tetrahedra can be combined with an octahedron, giving a [rhombohedron](wikipedia://en/2706406) that can tile space as the [tetrahedral-octahedral honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3735260). On the other hand, several irregular tetrahedra are known, of which copies can tile space, for instance, the characteristic orthoscheme of the cube and the disphenoid of the [disphenoid tetrahedral honeycomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disphenoid_tetrahedral_honeycomb). The complete list remains an open problem.[^n32]

If one relaxes the requirement that the tetrahedra be all the same shape, one can tile space using only tetrahedra in many different ways. For example, one can divide an octahedron into four identical tetrahedra and combine them again with two regular ones. (As a side-note: these two kinds of tetrahedron have the same volume.)

The tetrahedron is unique among the [uniform polyhedra](wikipedia://en/2537223) in possessing no parallel faces.
