# Ultrafilter

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ultrafilter"}

*Image: Filter vs ultrafilter\_210div.svg.* [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199) of the [divisors](wikipedia://en/49492) of 210, ordered by the relation *is divisor of*, with the [upper set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_set) ↑14 colored dark green. It is a principal filter, but not an ultrafilter, as it can be extended to the larger nontrivial filter ↑2, by including also the light green elements. Since ↑2 cannot be extended any further, it is an ultrafilter.

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) field of [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), an **ultrafilter** on a given [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) (or "poset") $P$ is a certain subset of $P,$ namely a [maximal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_element) [filter](wikipedia://en/19719) on $P;$ that is, a [proper filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_filter) on $P$ that cannot be enlarged to a bigger proper filter on $P.$

:::

If $X$ is an arbitrary set, its [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) ${\mathcal P}(X),$ ordered by [set inclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_inclusion), is always a [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959) and hence a poset, and ultrafilters on ${\mathcal P}(X)$ are usually called [ultrafilters on the set](wikipedia://en/67416519) $X$.[^o2] An ultrafilter on a set $X$ may be considered as a [finitely additive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_additive) 0-1-valued [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) on ${\mathcal P}(X)$. In this view, every subset of $X$ is either considered "[almost everything](wikipedia://en/44688)" (has measure 1) or "almost nothing" (has measure 0), depending on whether it belongs to the given ultrafilter or not.

Ultrafilters have many applications in set theory, [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858), [topology](wikipedia://en/29954)[^o3] and combinatorics.[^o4]

## Ultrafilters on partial orders {#Ultrafilters_on_partial_orders}

In [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), an **ultrafilter** is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) that is [maximal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_element) among all [proper filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_filter). This implies that any filter that properly contains an ultrafilter has to be equal to the whole poset.

Formally, if $P$ is a set, partially ordered by $\,\leq\,$ then

- a subset $F \subseteq P$ is called a **filter** on $P$ if
  - $F$ is nonempty,
  - for every $x, y \in F,$ there exists some element $z \in F$ such that $z \leq x$ and $z \leq y,$ and
  - for every $x \in F$ and $y \in P,$ $x \leq y$ implies that $y$ is in $F$ too;
- a [proper subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_subset) $U$ of $P$ is called an **ultrafilter** on $P$ if
  - $U$ is a filter on $P,$ and
  - there is no proper filter $F$ on $P$ that properly extends $U$ (that is, such that $U$ is a proper subset of $F$).

### Types and existence of ultrafilters {#Types_and_existence_of_ultrafilters}

Every ultrafilter falls into exactly one of two categories: principal or free. A **principal** (or **fixed**, or **trivial**) ultrafilter is a filter containing a [least element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_element). Consequently, each principal ultrafilter is of the form $F_p = \{x : p \leq x\}$ for some element $p$ of the given poset, although not all filters of the form $F_p$ are ultrafilters as seen in the example of principal ultrafilters of the power set ${\mathcal P}(X)$ below. In the case that $F_p$ is an ultrafilter, $p$ is called the principal element of the ultrafilter. Any ultrafilter that is not principal is called a **free** (or **non-principal**) ultrafilter. For arbitrary $p$, the set $F_p$ is a filter, called the principal filter at $p$; it is a principal ultrafilter only if it is maximal.

For ultrafilters on a powerset ${\mathcal P}(X),$ a principal ultrafilter consists of all subsets of $X$ that contain a given element $x \in X.$ Each ultrafilter on ${\mathcal P}(X)$ that is also a [principal filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_filter) is of this form.[^o3] Therefore, an ultrafilter $U$ on ${\mathcal P}(X)$ is principal if and only if it contains a finite set.[^o7] If $X$ is infinite, an ultrafilter $U$ on ${\mathcal P}(X)$ is hence non-principal if and only if it contains the [Fréchet filter](wikipedia://en/1586291) of [cofinite subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofinite_subset) of $X.$[^o8] If $X$ is finite, every ultrafilter is principal.[^o3] If $X$ is infinite then the [Fréchet filter](wikipedia://en/1586291) is not an ultrafilter on the power set of $X$ but it is an ultrafilter on the [finite–cofinite algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite–cofinite_algebra) of $X.$

Every filter on a Boolean algebra (or more generally, any subset with the [finite intersection property](wikipedia://en/365026)) is contained in an ultrafilter (see [ultrafilter lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrafilter_lemma)) and free ultrafilters therefore exist, but the proofs involve the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) (**AC**) in the form of [Zorn's lemma](wikipedia://en/51442). On the other hand, the statement that every filter is contained in an ultrafilter does not imply **AC**. Indeed, it is equivalent to the [Boolean prime ideal theorem](wikipedia://en/314919) (**BPIT**), a well-known intermediate point between the axioms of [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214) (**ZF**) and the **ZF** theory augmented by the axiom of choice (**ZFC**). In general, proofs involving the axiom of choice do not produce explicit examples of free ultrafilters, though it is possible to find explicit examples in some models of **ZFC**; for example, [Gödel](wikipedia://en/16736) showed that this can be done in the [constructible universe](wikipedia://en/343335) where one can write down an explicit global choice function. In **ZF** without the axiom of choice, it is possible that every ultrafilter is principal.[^o9]

## Ultrafilter on a Boolean algebra {#Ultrafilter_on_a_Boolean_algebra}

An important special case of the concept occurs if the considered poset is a [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959). In this case, ultrafilters are characterized by containing, for each element $x$ of the Boolean algebra, exactly one of the elements $x$ and $\lnot x$ (the latter being the [Boolean complement](wikipedia://en/54476844#Nonmonotone_laws) of $x$):

If $P$ is a Boolean algebra and $F$ is a proper filter on $P,$ then the following statements are equivalent:

1. $F$ is an ultrafilter on $P,$
1. $F$ is a [prime filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_filter) on $P,$[^o11]
1. for each $x \in P,$ either $x \in F$ or ($\lnot x$) $\in F.$[^o3]

Moreover, ultrafilters on a Boolean algebra can be related to [maximal ideals](wikipedia://en/3959#Ideals_and_filters) and [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/3959#Homomorphisms_and_isomorphisms) to the 2-element Boolean algebra {true, false} (also known as [2-valued morphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-valued_morphism)) as follows:

- Given a homomorphism of a Boolean algebra onto {true, false}, the [inverse image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_image) of "true" is an ultrafilter, and the inverse image of "false" is a maximal ideal.
- Given a maximal ideal of a Boolean algebra, its complement is an ultrafilter, and there is a unique homomorphism onto {true, false} taking the maximal ideal to "false".
- Given an ultrafilter on a Boolean algebra, its complement is a maximal ideal, and there is a unique homomorphism onto {true, false} taking the ultrafilter to "true".

## Ultrafilter on the power set of a set {#Ultrafilter_on_the_power_set_of_a_set}

Given an arbitrary set $X,$ its [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) ${\mathcal P}(X),$ ordered by [set inclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_inclusion), is always a Boolean algebra; hence the results of the above section apply. An (ultra)filter on ${\mathcal P}(X)$ is often called just an "(ultra)filter on $X$".[^o2] Given an arbitrary set $X,$ an ultrafilter on ${\mathcal P}(X)$ is a set $\mathcal U$ consisting of subsets of $X$ such that:

1. The empty set is not an element of $\mathcal U$.
1. If $A$ is an element of $\mathcal U$ then so is every superset $B\supset A$.
1. If $A$ and $B$ are elements of $\mathcal U$ then so is the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) $A\cap B$.
1. If $A$ is a subset of $X,$ then either[^o13] $A$ or its complement $X \setminus A$ is an element of $\mathcal U$.

Equivalently, a family $\mathcal U$ of subsets of $X$ is an ultrafilter if and only if for any finite collection $\mathcal F$ of subsets of $X$, there is some $x\in X$ such that $\mathcal U\cap\mathcal F=F_x\cap\mathcal F$ where $F_x=\{Y\subseteq X : x \in Y\}$ is the principal ultrafilter seeded by $x$. In other words, an ultrafilter may be seen as a family of sets which "locally" resembles a principal ultrafilter.

An equivalent form of a given $\mathcal U$ is a [2-valued morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-valued_morphism), a function $m$ on ${\mathcal P}(X)$ defined as $m(A) = 1$ if $A$ is an element of $\mathcal U$ and $m(A) = 0$ otherwise. Then $m$ is [finitely additive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_additive), and hence a [content](wikipedia://en/9999200) on ${\mathcal P}(X),$ and every property of elements of $X$ is either true [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688) or false almost everywhere. However, $m$ is usually not countably additive, and hence does not define a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) in the usual sense.

For a filter $\mathcal F$ that is not an ultrafilter, one can define $m(A) = 1$ if $A \in \mathcal F$ and $m(A) = 0$ if $X \setminus A \in \mathcal F,$ leaving $m$ undefined elsewhere.[^o14]

## Applications {#Applications}

Ultrafilters on [power sets](wikipedia://en/23799) are useful in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), especially in relation to [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) spaces, and in [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858) in the construction of [ultraproducts and ultrapowers](wikipedia://en/273642). Every ultrafilter on a compact Hausdorff space converges to exactly one point. Likewise, ultrafilters on Boolean algebras play a central role in [Stone's representation theorem](wikipedia://en/604111). In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) ultrafilters are used to show that the [axiom of constructibility](wikipedia://en/696472) is incompatible with the existence of a [measurable cardinal](wikipedia://en/248102) $\kappa$. This is proved by taking the ultrapower of the set theoretical universe modulo a $\kappa$-complete, non-principal ultrafilter.[^o17]

The set $G$ of all ultrafilters of a poset $P$ can be topologized in a natural way, that is in fact closely related to the above-mentioned representation theorem. For any element $x$ of $P$, let $D_x = \left\{ U \in G : x \in U \right\}.$ This is most useful when $P$ is again a Boolean algebra, since in this situation the set of all $D_x$ is a base for a compact Hausdorff topology on $G$. Especially, when considering the ultrafilters on a powerset ${\mathcal P}(S)$, the resulting [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) is the [Stone–Čech compactification](wikipedia://en/52897) of a [discrete space](wikipedia://en/56061) of cardinality $| S |.$

The [ultraproduct](wikipedia://en/273642) construction in [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858) uses ultrafilters to produce a new model starting from a sequence of $X$-indexed models; for example, the [compactness theorem](wikipedia://en/152207) can be proved this way. In the special case of ultrapowers, one gets [elementary extensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_extension) of structures. For example, in [nonstandard analysis](wikipedia://en/21708), the [hyperreal numbers](wikipedia://en/51429) can be constructed as an ultraproduct of the [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers), extending the [domain of discourse](wikipedia://en/229180) from real numbers to sequences of real numbers. This sequence space is regarded as a [superset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superset) of the reals by identifying each real with the corresponding constant sequence. To extend the familiar functions and relations (e.g., + and \<) from the reals to the hyperreals, the natural idea is to define them pointwise. But this would lose important logical properties of the reals; for example, pointwise \< is not a total ordering. So instead the functions and relations are defined "[pointwise modulo](wikipedia://en/273642#Definition)" $U$, where $U$ is an ultrafilter on the [index set](wikipedia://en/393055) of the sequences; by [Łoś' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Łoś'_theorem), this preserves all properties of the reals that can be stated in [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983). If $U$ is nonprincipal, then the extension thereby obtained is nontrivial.

In [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164), non-principal ultrafilters are used to define the [asymptotic cone](wikipedia://en/18609360#Asymptotic_cones) of a group. This construction yields a rigorous way to consider looking at the group from infinity, that is the large scale geometry of the group. Asymptotic cones are particular examples of [ultralimits](wikipedia://en/18609360) of [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018).

[Gödel's ontological proof](wikipedia://en/12420) of God's existence uses as an axiom that the set of all "positive properties" is an ultrafilter.

In [social choice theory](wikipedia://en/1745670), non-principal ultrafilters are used to define a rule (called a *social welfare function*) for aggregating the preferences of *infinitely* many individuals. Contrary to [Arrow's impossibility theorem](wikipedia://en/89425) for *finitely* many individuals, such a rule satisfies the conditions (properties) that Arrow proposes.[^o18] However, such rules are practically of limited interest to social scientists, since they are non-algorithmic or non-computable.[^o19][^o20]
