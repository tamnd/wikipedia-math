# Cypherpunk

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cypherpunk"}

A **cypherpunk** is one who advocates the widespread use of strong [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432) and [privacy-enhancing technologies](wikipedia://en/14389259) as a means of effecting social and political change. The cypherpunk movement originated in the late 1980s and gained traction with the establishment of the "Cypherpunks" [electronic mailing list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_mailing_list) in 1992, where informal groups of activists, technologists, and [cryptographers](wikipedia://en/18934432) discussed strategies to enhance individual privacy and resist state or corporate [surveillance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveillance). Deeply [libertarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libertarianism) in philosophy, the movement is rooted in principles of [decentralization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decentralization), individual autonomy, and freedom from [centralized authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authoritarianism).[^o2][^o3] Its influence on society extends to the development of technologies that have reshaped global finance, communication, and privacy practices, such as the creation of [Bitcoin](wikipedia://en/28249265) and other [cryptocurrencies](wikipedia://en/36662188), which embody cypherpunk ideals of decentralized money.

:::

The movement has also contributed to the mainstreaming of encryption in everyday technologies, such as secure messaging apps and privacy-focused web browsers.

## History {#History}

### Before the mailing list {#Before_the_mailing_list}

Until about the 1970s, [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432) was mainly practiced in secret by military or spy agencies. However, that changed when two publications brought it into public awareness: the first publicly available work on [public-key cryptography](wikipedia://en/24222), by [Whitfield Diffie](wikipedia://en/427776) and [Martin Hellman](wikipedia://en/422387),[^o6] and the US government publication of the [Data Encryption Standard](wikipedia://en/7978) (DES), a [block cipher](wikipedia://en/4594) which became very widely used.

The technical roots of Cypherpunk ideas have been traced back to work by cryptographer [David Chaum](wikipedia://en/239365) on topics such as anonymous digital cash and pseudonymous reputation systems, described in his paper "Security without Identification: Transaction Systems to Make Big Brother Obsolete" (1985).[^o7]

In the late 1980s, these ideas coalesced into something like a movement.[^o7]

::: {#o9 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Cypherpunk mailing list"}

### Etymology and the Cypherpunks mailing list {#Etymology_and_the_Cypherpunks_mailing_list}

In late 1992, [Eric Hughes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Hughes_%28cypherpunk%29), [Timothy C. May](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_C._May), and [John Gilmore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gilmore_%28activist%29) founded a small group that met monthly at Gilmore's company [Cygnus Solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cygnus_Solutions) in the [San Francisco Bay Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Bay_Area) and was humorously termed *cypherpunks* by [Jude Milhon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jude_Milhon) at one of the first meetings—derived from *[cipher](wikipedia://en/5244)* and *[cyberpunk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyberpunk)*.[^o10] In November 2006, the word was added to the [Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary).The Cypherpunks [mailing list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mailing_list) was started in 1992, and by 1994 had 700 subscribers.[^o10] At its peak, it was a very active forum with technical discussions ranging over mathematics, cryptography, computer science, political and philosophical discussion, personal arguments and attacks, etc., with some [spam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spam_%28electronic%29) thrown in. An email from [John Gilmore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gilmore_%28activist%29) reports an average of 30 messages a day from December 1, 1996, to March 1, 1999, and suggests that the number was probably higher earlier.[^o11] The number of subscribers is estimated to have reached 2,000 in the year 1997.[^o10]

In early 1997, Jim Choate and Igor Chudov set up the Cypherpunks Distributed Remailer,[^o12] a network of independent mailing list nodes intended to eliminate the [single point of failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_point_of_failure) inherent in a centralized list architecture. At its peak, the Cypherpunks Distributed Remailer included at least seven nodes.[^o13] By mid-2005, al-qaeda.net (Riad S. Wahby[^o14][^o15]) ran the only remaining node.[^o16] In mid-2013, following a brief outage, the al-qaeda.net node's list software was changed from [Majordomo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majordomo_%28software%29) to [GNU Mailman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GNU_Mailman),[^o17] and subsequently the node was renamed to cpunks.org.[^o18] The CDR architecture is now defunct, though the list administrator stated in 2013 that he was exploring a way to integrate this functionality with the new mailing list software.[^o17]

For a time, the cypherpunks mailing list was a popular tool with mailbombers,[^o19] who would subscribe a victim to the mailing list in order to cause a deluge of messages to be sent to them (usually as a prank, in contrast to the style of terrorist referred to as a mailbomber). This precipitated the mailing list sysop(s) to institute a reply-to-subscribe system. Approximately two hundred messages a day was typical for the mailing list, divided between personal arguments and attacks, political discussion, technical discussion, and early spam.[^o20][^o21]

The cypherpunks mailing list had extensive discussions of the public policy issues related to cryptography and on the politics and philosophy of concepts such as anonymity, pseudonyms, reputation, and privacy. These discussions continue both on the remaining node and elsewhere as the list has become increasingly moribund.

Events such as the [GURPS Cyberpunk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GURPS_Cyberpunk) raid[^o22] lent weight to the idea that private individuals needed to take steps to protect their privacy. In its heyday, the list discussed public policy issues related to cryptography, as well as more practical nuts-and-bolts mathematical, computational, technological, and cryptographic matters. The list had a range of viewpoints and there was probably no completely unanimous agreement on anything. The general attitude, though, definitely put personal privacy and personal liberty above all other considerations.[^o23]

#### Early discussion of online privacy {#Early_discussion_of_online_privacy}

The list was discussing questions about privacy, government monitoring, corporate control of information, and related issues in the early 1990s that did not become major topics for broader discussion until at least ten years later. Some list participants were highly radical on these issues.

Those wishing to understand the context of the list might refer to the history of cryptography; in the early 1990s, the US government considered cryptography software a [munition for export purposes](wikipedia://en/826003) ([PGP](wikipedia://en/23080) source code was published as a paper book to bypass these regulations and demonstrate their futility). In 1992, a deal between NSA and SPA allowed export of cryptography based on 40-bit RC2 and RC4 which was considered relatively weak (and especially after SSL was created, there were many contests to break it). The US government had also tried to subvert cryptography through schemes such as [Skipjack](wikipedia://en/474702) and key escrow. It was also not widely known that all communications were logged by government agencies (which would later be revealed during the [NSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NSA_warrantless_surveillance_controversy) and [AT&T scandals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepting_v._AT&T)) though this was taken as an obvious axiom by list members.[^o25]

The original cypherpunk mailing list, and the first list spin-off, *coderpunks*, were originally hosted on [John Gilmore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gilmore_%28activist%29)'s toad.com, but after a falling out with the sysop over moderation, the list was migrated to several cross-linked mail-servers in what was called the "distributed mailing list."[^o26][^o27] The *coderpunks* list, open by invitation only, existed for a time. *Coderpunks* took up more technical matters and had less discussion of public policy implications. There are several lists today that can trace their lineage directly to the original Cypherpunks list: the cryptography list (cryptography@metzdowd.com), the financial cryptography list (fc-announce@ifca.ai), and a small group of closed (invitation-only) lists as well.

Toad.com continued to run with the existing subscriber list, those that didn't unsubscribe, and was mirrored on the new distributed mailing list, but messages from the distributed list didn't appear on toad.com.[^o28] As the list faded in popularity, so too did it fade in the number of cross-linked subscription nodes.

To some extent, the cryptography list[^o29] acts as a successor to cypherpunks; it has many of the people and continues some of the same discussions. However, it is a moderated list, considerably less zany and somewhat more technical. A number of current systems in use trace to the mailing list, including [Pretty Good Privacy](wikipedia://en/23080), [/dev/random](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//dev/random) in the [Linux kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux_kernel) (the actual code has been completely reimplemented several times since then) and today's [anonymous remailers](wikipedia://en/54666).

## Main principles {#Main_principles}

The basic ideas can be found in *[A Cypherpunk's Manifesto](https://www.activism.net/cypherpunk/manifesto.html)*[^o32] ([Eric Hughes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Hughes_%28cypherpunk%29), 1993): "Privacy is necessary for an open society in the electronic age. ... We cannot expect governments, corporations, or other large, faceless organizations to grant us privacy ... We must defend our own privacy if we expect to have any. ... Cypherpunks write code. We know that someone has to write software to defend privacy, and ... we're going to write it."[^o32]

Some are or were senior people at major hi-tech companies and others are well-known researchers (see list with affiliations below).

The first mass media discussion of cypherpunks was in *Crypto Rebels*,[^o33] a 1993 *[Wired](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wired_%28magazine%29)* article by [Steven Levy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Levy). The three masked men on the cover of that edition of *Wired* were prominent cypherpunks [Tim May](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_C._May), [Eric Hughes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Hughes_%28cypherpunk%29) and [John Gilmore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gilmore_%28activist%29). Later, Levy wrote a book, *[Crypto: How the Code Rebels Beat the Government – Saving Privacy in the Digital Age](wikipedia://en/380157)*,[^o34] covering the [crypto wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crypto_wars) of the 1990s in detail. "Code Rebels" in the title is almost synonymous with cypherpunks.

The term *cypherpunk* is mildly ambiguous. In most contexts it means anyone advocating cryptography as a tool for social change, social impact and expression. However, it can also be used to mean a participant in the Cypherpunks [electronic mailing list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_mailing_list) described below. The two meanings obviously overlap, but they are by no means synonymous.

Documents exemplifying cypherpunk ideas include Timothy C. May's *The Crypto Anarchist Manifesto* (1992)[^o35] and *The [Cyphernomicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyphernomicon)* (1994),[^o36] *A Cypherpunk's Manifesto*.[^o32]

### Privacy of communications {#Privacy_of_communications}

A very basic cypherpunk issue is [privacy in communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secrecy_of_communication) and [data retention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_retention). John Gilmore said he wanted "a guarantee – with physics and mathematics, not with laws – that we can give ourselves real privacy of personal communications."[^o38]

Such guarantees require [strong cryptography](wikipedia://en/2223940), so cypherpunks are fundamentally opposed to government policies attempting to control the usage or export of cryptography, which remained an issue throughout the late 1990s. The *Cypherpunk Manifesto* stated "Cypherpunks deplore regulations on cryptography, for encryption is fundamentally a private act."[^o32]

This was a central issue for many cypherpunks. Most were passionately opposed to various government attempts to limit cryptography—[export laws](wikipedia://en/826003), promotion of limited key length ciphers, and especially [escrowed encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escrowed_encryption).

### Anonymity and pseudonyms {#Anonymity_and_pseudonyms}

The questions of [anonymity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anonymity), [pseudonymity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudonym) and [reputation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reputation) were also extensively discussed.

Most cypherpunks have the position that the possibility of [anonymous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anonymity) speech and publication is crucial for an open society and genuine freedom of speech.[^o40]

### Censorship and monitoring {#Censorship_and_monitoring}

In general, cypherpunks opposed the censorship and monitoring from government and police.

In particular, the US government's [Clipper chip](wikipedia://en/409665) scheme for [escrowed encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escrowed_encryption) of telephone conversations (encryption supposedly secure against most attackers, but breakable by government) was seen as [anathema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anathema) by many on the list. This was an issue that provoked strong opposition and brought many new recruits to the cypherpunk ranks. List participant [Matt Blaze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matt_Blaze) found a serious flaw[^o42] in the scheme, helping to hasten its demise.

Steven Schear first suggested the [warrant canary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warrant_canary) in 2002 to thwart the secrecy provisions of [court orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_order) and [national security letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_security_letters).[^o43] , warrant canaries are gaining commercial acceptance.[^o44]

### Hiding the act of hiding {#Hiding_the_act_of_hiding}

An important set of discussions concerns the use of cryptography in the presence of oppressive authorities. As a result, Cypherpunks have discussed and improved [steganographic](wikipedia://en/28733) methods that hide the use of crypto itself, or that allow interrogators to believe that they have forcibly extracted hidden information from a subject. For instance, *[Rubberhose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubberhose_%28file_system%29)* was a tool that partitioned and intermixed secret data on a drive with fake secret data, each of which accessed via a different password. Interrogators, having extracted a password, are led to believe that they have indeed unlocked the desired secrets, whereas in reality the actual data is still hidden. In other words, even its presence is hidden. Likewise, cypherpunks have also discussed under what conditions encryption may be used without being noticed by [network monitoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_monitoring) systems installed by oppressive regimes.

## Activities {#Activities}

As the *Manifesto* says, "Cypherpunks write code";[^o32] the notion that good ideas need to be implemented, not just discussed, is very much part of the culture of the [mailing list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mailing_list). [John Gilmore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gilmore_%28activist%29), whose site hosted the original cypherpunks mailing list, wrote: "We are literally in a race between our ability to build and deploy technology, and their ability to build and deploy laws and treaties. Neither side is likely to back down or wise up until it has definitively lost the race."[^o47]

### Software projects {#Software_projects}

Anonymous remailers such as the [Mixmaster Remailer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixmaster_anonymous_remailer) were almost entirely a cypherpunk development.[^o49] Other cypherpunk-related projects include [PGP](wikipedia://en/23080) for email privacy,[^o50] [FreeS/WAN](wikipedia://en/1658979) for [opportunistic encryption](wikipedia://en/4299490) of the whole net, [Off-the-record messaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Off-the-record_messaging) for privacy in [Internet chat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_chat), and the [Tor](wikipedia://en/20556944) project for anonymous web surfing.

### Hardware {#Hardware}

In 1998, the [Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation), with assistance from the mailing list, built a \$200,000 [machine](wikipedia://en/839358) that could brute-force a [Data Encryption Standard](wikipedia://en/7978) key in a few days.[^o52] The project demonstrated that DES was, without question, insecure and obsolete, in sharp contrast to the US government's recommendation of the algorithm.

### Expert panels {#Expert_panels}

Cypherpunks also participated, along with other experts, in several reports on cryptographic matters.

One such paper was "Minimal Key Lengths for Symmetric Ciphers to Provide Adequate Commercial Security".[^o54] It suggested 75 bits was the *minimum* key size to allow an existing cipher to be considered secure and kept in service. At the time, the [Data Encryption Standard](wikipedia://en/7978) with 56-bit keys was still a US government standard, mandatory for some applications.

Other papers were critical analysis of government schemes. "The Risks of Key Recovery, Key Escrow, and Trusted Third-Party Encryption",[^o55] evaluated [escrowed encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escrowed_encryption) proposals. *Comments on the Carnivore System Technical Review*.[^o56] looked at an [FBI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI) scheme for monitoring email.

Cypherpunks provided significant input to the 1996 [National Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Research_Council) report on encryption policy, *Cryptography's Role In Securing the Information Society* (CRISIS).[^o57] This report, commissioned by the U.S. Congress in 1993, was developed via extensive hearings across the nation from all interested stakeholders, by a committee of talented people. It recommended a gradual relaxation of the existing U.S. government restrictions on encryption. Like many such study reports, its conclusions were largely ignored by policy-makers. Later events such as the final rulings in the cypherpunks lawsuits forced a more complete relaxation of the unconstitutional controls on encryption software.

### Lawsuits {#Lawsuits}

Cypherpunks have filed a number of lawsuits, mostly suits against the US government alleging that some government action is unconstitutional.

[Phil Karn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phil_Karn) sued the State Department in 1994 over cryptography export controls[^o59] after they ruled that, while the book *Applied Cryptography*[^o60] could legally be exported, a floppy disk containing a verbatim copy of code printed in the book was legally a munition and required an export permit, which they refused to grant. Karn also appeared before both House and Senate committees looking at cryptography issues.

[Daniel J. Bernstein](wikipedia://en/198983), supported by the [EFF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation), also sued over the export restrictions, arguing that preventing publication of cryptographic source code is an unconstitutional restriction on freedom of speech. He won, effectively overturning the export law. See *[Bernstein v. United States](wikipedia://en/340772)* for details.

[Peter Junger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Junger) also sued on similar grounds, and won.[^o61]

### Civil disobedience {#Civil_disobedience}

Cypherpunks encouraged civil disobedience, in particular, [US law on the export of cryptography](wikipedia://en/826003).[^o63] Until 1997, cryptographic code was legally a munition and fell under ITAR, and the key length restrictions in the EAR was not removed until 2000.[^o64]

In 1995 Adam Back wrote a version of the [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29) algorithm for [public-key cryptography](wikipedia://en/24222) in three lines of [Perl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perl)[^o65][^o66] and suggested people use it as an email signature file:

```perl
# !/bin/perl -sp0777i<X+d*lMLa^*lN%0]dsXx++lMlN/dsM0<j]dsj
$/=unpack('H*',$_);$_=`echo 16dio\U$k"SK$/SM$n\EsN0p[lN*1
lK[d2%Sa2/d0$^Ixp"|dc`;s/\W//g;$_=pack('H*',/((..)*)$/)
```

[Vince Cate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vince_Cate) put up a web page that invited anyone to become an international arms trafficker; every time someone clicked on the form, an export-restricted item—originally [PGP](wikipedia://en/23080), later a copy of Back's program—would be mailed from a US server to one in Anguilla.[^o67][^o68][^o69]

### Cypherpunk fiction {#Cypherpunk_fiction}

In [Neal Stephenson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neal_Stephenson)'s novel *[Cryptonomicon](wikipedia://en/21861)* many characters are on the "Secret Admirers" mailing list. This is fairly obviously based on the cypherpunks list, and several well-known cypherpunks are mentioned in the acknowledgements. Much of the plot revolves around cypherpunk ideas; the leading characters are building a data haven which will allow anonymous financial transactions, and the book is full of cryptography. But, according to the author[^o71] the book's title is—in spite of its similarity—not based on the Cyphernomicon,[^o36] an online cypherpunk FAQ document.

### Legacy {#Legacy}

Cypherpunk achievements would later also be used on the Canadian e-wallet, the [MintChip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MintChip), and the creation of [bitcoin](wikipedia://en/28249265). It was an inspiration for [CryptoParty](wikipedia://en/37121257) decades later to such an extent that A Cypherpunk's Manifesto is quoted at the header of its Wiki,[^o73] and Eric Hughes delivered the keynote address at the Amsterdam CryptoParty on 27 August 2012.
