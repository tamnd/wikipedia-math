# Bruce Schneier

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Bruce Schneier"}

**Bruce Schneier** (; born January 15, 1963) is an American [cryptographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptographer), [computer security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_security) professional, [privacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privacy) specialist, and writer. Schneier is an adjunct lecturer in public policy at the [Harvard Kennedy School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_Kennedy_School)[^o4] and a Fellow at the [Berkman Klein Center for Internet & Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkman_Klein_Center_for_Internet_&_Society) as of November, 2013.[^o5] He is a board member of the [Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation), [Access Now](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Access_Now), and [The Tor Project](wikipedia://en/43283728); and an advisory board member of [Electronic Privacy Information Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Privacy_Information_Center) and [VerifiedVoting.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VerifiedVoting.org). He is the author of several books on general [security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security) topics, computer security and [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432) and is a [squid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squid) enthusiast.[^o6]

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Bruce Schneier is the son of Martin Schneier, a Brooklyn Supreme Court judge. He grew up in the [Flatbush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flatbush,_Brooklyn) neighborhood of [Brooklyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn), New York, attending P.S. 139 and [Hunter College High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter_College_High_School).[^o8]

After receiving a physics bachelor's degree from the [University of Rochester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Rochester) in 1984,[^o9] he went to [American University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_University) in [Washington, D.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_D.C.), and got his master's degree in computer science in 1988.[^o10]

## Career {#Career}

*Image: Worldcon 2026 (LAcon V) - Bruce Schneier 03.jpg.* Schneier at [Worldcon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worldcon) 2026

In 1991, Schneier was laid off from his job and started writing for computer magazines. Later he decided to write a book on applied cryptography "since [no such book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Books_on_cryptography) existed". He took his articles, wrote a proposal to [John Wiley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiley_%28publisher%29) and they bought the proposal.[^o12]

In 1994, Schneier published *Applied Cryptography*, which details the design, use, and implementation of cryptographic algorithms.

This book allowed me to write more, to start consulting, to start my companies, and really launched me as an expert in this field, and it really was because no one else has written this book. I wanted to read it so I had to write it. And it happened in a really lucky time when everything started to explode on the Internet.[^o12]

In 1999, Schneier was a founder and [chief technology officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_technology_officer) of Counterpane Internet Security (now [BT Managed Security Solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BT_Managed_Security_Solutions)).

In 2000, Schneier published *Secrets and Lies: Digital Security in a Networked World*; in 2003, *[Beyond Fear: Thinking Sensibly About Security in an Uncertain World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beyond_Fear:_Thinking_Sensibly_About_Security_in_an_Uncertain_World)* and in 2012, *[Liars and Outliers: Enabling the Trust that Society Needs to Thrive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liars_and_Outliers)*.

As a [Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow) of Berkman Center for Internet & Society at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) since 2013,[^o5] Schneier has been exploring the intersection of security, technology, and people, with an emphasis on power.[^o13]

He worked for [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM) when they acquired Resilient Systems in 2016, where he was CTO.[^o14][^o15][^o16] until he left at the end of June 2019.[^o17]

Schneier has been an adjunct lecturer in public policy at the [Harvard Kennedy School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_Kennedy_School).[^o4]

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Schneier was married to Karen Cooper in 1997 and lived in Minneapolis;[^o19] they published restaurant reviews in the Pulse of the Twin Cities. The couple divorced in 2022.

## Viewpoints {#Viewpoints}

### Blockchains {#Blockchains}

Schneier has warned about misplaced trust in [blockchain](wikipedia://en/44065971)[^o22] and the lack of use cases, calling blockchain a solution in search of a problem.[^o23]

What blockchain does is shift some of the trust in people and institutions to trust in technology. You need to trust the cryptography, the protocols, the software, the computers and the network. And you need to trust them absolutely, because they're often single points of failure.

I've never seen a legitimate use case for blockchain. I've never seen any system where blockchain provides security in a way that is impossible to provide in any other way.[^o24]

He goes on to say that [cryptocurrencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptocurrencies) are useless and are only used by speculators looking for quick riches.

### Cryptography {#Cryptography}

To Schneier, peer review and expert analysis are important for the security of cryptographic systems.[^o27] Mathematical cryptography is usually not the weakest link in a security chain; effective security requires that cryptography be combined with other things.[^o28]

The term *Schneier's law* was coined by [Cory Doctorow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cory_Doctorow) in a 2004 speech.[^o29] The law is phrased as:

Any person can invent a security system so clever that she or he can't think of how to break it.

He attributes this to Bruce Schneier, who wrote in 1998: "Anyone, from the most clueless amateur to the best cryptographer, can create an algorithm that he himself can't break. It's not even hard. What is hard is creating an algorithm that no one else can break, even after years of analysis."[^o30]

Similar sentiments had been expressed by others before. In *[The Codebreakers](wikipedia://en/518398)*, [David Kahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Kahn_%28writer%29) states: "Few false ideas have more firmly gripped the minds of so many intelligent men than the one that, if they just tried, they could invent a cipher that no one could break", and in "A Few Words On Secret Writing", in July 1841, [Edgar Allan Poe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Allan_Poe) had stated: "Few persons can be made to believe that it is not quite an easy thing to invent a method of secret writing which shall baffle investigation. Yet it may be roundly asserted that human ingenuity cannot concoct a cipher which human ingenuity cannot resolve."[^o31]

Schneier also coined the term "kid sister cryptography", writing in the Preface to *Applied Cryptography*[^o32] that:

There are two kinds of cryptography in this world: cryptography that will stop your kid sister from reading your files, and cryptography that will stop major governments from reading your files. This book is about the latter.

:::

### Digital rights management {#Digital_rights_management}

Schneier is critical of [digital rights management](wikipedia://en/18938226) (DRM) and has said that it allows a vendor to increase [lock-in](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vendor_lock-in).[^o34] Proper implementation of control-based security for the user via [trusted computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trusted_computing) is very difficult, and security is not the same thing as control.[^o34]

Schneier insists that "owning your data is a different way of thinking about data."[^o35]

### Full disclosure {#Full_disclosure}

Schneier is a proponent of [full disclosure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_disclosure_%28computer_security%29), i.e. making security issues public.

If researchers don't go public, things don't get fixed. Companies don't see it as a security problem; they see it as a PR problem.[^o37]

### Homeland security {#Homeland_security}

Schneier has said that [homeland security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeland_security) money should be spent on [intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_agency), investigation, and [emergency response](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_service).[^o39] Defending against the broad threat of terrorism is generally better than focusing on specific potential terrorist plots.[^o39] According to Schneier, analysis of intelligence data is difficult but is one of the better ways to deal with global terrorism.[^o40] Human intelligence has advantages over automated and computerized analysis, and increasing the amount of intelligence data that is gathered does not help to improve the analysis process.[^o40] Agencies that were designed around fighting the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War) may have a culture that inhibits the sharing of information; the practice of sharing information is more important and less of a security threat in itself when dealing with more decentralized and poorly funded adversaries such as al Qaeda.[^o41]

Regarding [PETN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PETN)—the explosive that has become terrorists' weapon of choice—Schneier has written that only swabs and dogs can detect it. He also believes that changes to airport security since 11 September 2001 have done more harm than good and he defeated [Kip Hawley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kip_Hawley), former head of the Transportation Security Administration, in an *[Economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Economist)* online debate by 87% to 13% regarding the issue.[^o42] He is widely credited with coining the term "[security theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_theater)" to describe some such changes.

::: {#o44 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Movie plot security"}

#### Movie plot threat {#Movie_plot_threat}

"Movie-plot threat" is a term Schneier coined that refers to very specific and dramatic [terrorist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrorism) attack scenarios, reminiscent of the behavior of terrorists in movies, rather than what terrorists actually do in the real world.[^o45] Security measures created to protect against movie plot threats do not provide a higher level of real security, because such preparation only pays off if terrorists choose that one particular avenue of attack, which may not even be feasible. Real-world terrorists would also be likely to notice the highly specific security measures, and simply attack in some other way. The specificity of movie plot threats gives them power in the public imagination, however, so even extremely unrealistic security theater countermeasures may receive strong support from the public and legislators. Among many other examples of movie plot threats, Schneier described banning [baby carriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baby_transport) from [subways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapid_transit), for fear that they may contain explosives.[^o46] Starting in April 2006, Schneier has had an annual contest to create the most fantastic movie-plot threat.[^o47] In 2015, during the 8th and the last one, he mentioned that the contest may have run its course.[^o48]

:::

### System design {#System_design}

Schneier has criticized security approaches that try to prevent any malicious incursion, instead arguing that designing systems to [fail well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Failing_badly) is more important.[^o50] The designer of a system should not underestimate the capabilities of an attacker, as technology may make it possible in the future to do things that are not possible at the present.[^o27] Under [Kerckhoffs's Principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerckhoffs's_Principle), the need for one or more parts of a cryptographic system to remain secret increases the fragility of the system; whether details about a system should be obscured depends upon the availability of persons who can make use of the information for beneficial uses versus the potential for attackers to misuse the information.[^o51]

Secrecy and security aren't the same, even though it may seem that way. Only bad security relies on secrecy; good security works even if all the details of it are public.[^o52]

### Service and awards {#Service_and_awards}

Schneier is a board member of the [Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation),[^o54] [Access Now](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Access_Now), and [The Tor Project](wikipedia://en/43283728); and an advisory board member of [Electronic Privacy Information Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Privacy_Information_Center) and [VerifiedVoting.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VerifiedVoting.org).

In 2015, Schneier received the EPIC Lifetime Achievement Award from Electronic Privacy Information Center.[^o55]

In 2011, he was awarded an honorary Ph.D from the [University of Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Westminster) in [London, England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London), by the Department of Electronics and Computer Science in recognition of Schneier's 'hard work and contribution to industry and public life'.

### Publications {#Publications}

Schneier writes a freely available monthly Internet newsletter on computer and other security issues, *Crypto-Gram*, as well as a security [weblog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weblog), *Schneier on Security*.[^o60] The blog focuses on the latest threats, and his own thoughts. The weblog started out as a way to publish essays before they appeared in Crypto-Gram, making it possible for others to comment on them while the stories were still current, but over time the newsletter became a monthly email version of the blog, re-edited and re-organized.[^o61] Schneier is frequently quoted in the press on computer and other security issues, pointing out flaws in security and cryptographic implementations ranging from [biometrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biometrics) to [airline security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_security) after the [September 11 attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_11_attacks).[^o62]
