# Weak topology

article

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Weak topology"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **weak topology** is an alternative term for certain [initial topologies](wikipedia://en/1197123), often on [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) or spaces of [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator), for instance on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932). The term is most commonly used for the initial topology of a topological vector space (such as a [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538)) with respect to its [continuous dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_dual_space). The remainder of this article will deal with this case, which is one of the concepts of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180).

:::

One may call subsets of a topological vector space **weakly closed** (respectively, **weakly compact**, etc.) if they are [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) (respectively, [compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set), etc.) with respect to the weak topology. Likewise, functions are sometimes called **[weakly continuous](wikipedia://en/63735167#Weak_continuity)** (respectively, **weakly differentiable**, **weakly analytic**, etc.) if they are [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) (respectively, [differentiable](wikipedia://en/7921), [analytic](wikipedia://en/61478), etc.) with respect to the weak topology.

## History {#History}

Starting in the early 1900s, [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) and [Marcel Riesz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Riesz) made extensive use of weak convergence. The early pioneers of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) did not elevate norm convergence above weak convergence and oftentimes viewed weak convergence as preferable.[^n1] In 1929, [Banach](wikipedia://en/29646) introduced weak convergence for normed spaces and also introduced the analogous [weak-\* convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak-*_topology).[^n1] The weak topology is called topologie faible in French and schwache Topologie in German.

## The weak and strong topologies {#The_weak_and_strong_topologies}

Let $\mathbb{K}$ be a [topological field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_field), namely a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) with a [topology](wikipedia://en/30450) such that addition, multiplication, and division are [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_%28topology%29). In most applications $\mathbb{K}$ will be either the field of [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) or the field of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) with the familiar topologies.

### Weak topology with respect to a pairing {#Weak_topology_with_respect_to_a_pairing}

Both the weak topology and the weak\* topology are special cases of a more general construction for [pairings](wikipedia://en/63735167), which we now describe. The benefit of this more general construction is that any definition or result proved for it applies to *both* the weak topology and the weak\* topology, thereby making redundant the need for many definitions, theorem statements, and proofs. This is also the reason why the weak\* topology is also frequently referred to as the "weak topology"; because it is just an instance of the weak topology in the setting of this more general construction.

Suppose $(X, Y, b)$ is a [pairing](wikipedia://en/63735167) of vector spaces over a topological field $\mathbb{K}$ (i.e. $X$ and $Y$ are vector spaces over $\mathbb{K}$ and $b : X \times Y \to \mathbb{K}$ is a [bilinear map](wikipedia://en/4365)).

**Notation.** For all $x \in X$, let $b(x, \bullet) : Y \to \mathbb{K}$ denote the linear functional on $Y$ defined by $y b(x, y)$. Similarly, for all $y \in Y$, let $b(\bullet, y) : X \to \mathbb{K}$ be defined by $x b(x, y)$.

**Definition.** The **weak topology on $X$** induced by $Y$ (and $b$) is the weakest topology on $X$, denoted by $\text{𝜎}(X, Y, b)$ or simply $\text{𝜎}(X, Y)$, making all maps $b(\bullet, y) : X \to \mathbb{K}$ continuous, as $y$ ranges over $Y$.[^n2]

The weak topology on $Y$ is now automatically defined as described in the article [Dual system](wikipedia://en/63735167). However, for clarity, we now repeat it.

**Definition.** The **weak topology on $Y$** induced by $X$ (and $b$) is the weakest topology on $Y$, denoted by $\text{𝜎}(Y, X, b)$ or simply $\text{𝜎}(Y, X)$, making all maps $b(x, \bullet) : Y \to \mathbb{K}$ continuous, as $x$ ranges over $X$.[^n2]

If the field $\mathbb{K}$ has an [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) $\cdot$, then the weak topology $\text{𝜎}(X, Y, b)$ on $X$ is induced by the family of [seminorms](wikipedia://en/292067), $p_{y} : X \to \mathbb{R}$, defined by

$$p_{y}(x) := b(x, y)$$

for all $y \in Y$ and $x \in X$. This shows that weak topologies are [locally convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_convex_space).

**Assumption.** We will henceforth assume that $\mathbb{K}$ is either the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\mathbb{R}$ or the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\mathbb{C}$.

#### Canonical duality {#Canonical_duality}

We now consider the special case where $Y$ is a vector subspace of the [algebraic dual space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_dual_space) of $X$ (i.e. a vector space of linear functionals on $X$).

There is a pairing, denoted by $(X,Y,\langle\cdot, \cdot\rangle)$ or $(X,Y)$, called the [canonical pairing](wikipedia://en/63735167#Canonical_duality_on_a_vector_space) whose bilinear map $\langle\cdot, \cdot\rangle$ is the **canonical evaluation map**, defined by $\langle x,x'\rangle =x'(x)$ for all $x\in X$ and $x'\in Y$. Note in particular that $\langle \cdot,x'\rangle$ is just another way of denoting $x'$ i.e. $\langle \cdot,x'\rangle=x'(\cdot)$.

**Assumption.** If $Y$ is a vector subspace of the [algebraic dual space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_dual_space) of $X$ then we will assume that they are associated with the canonical pairing $\langle X, Y\rangle$.

In this case, the **weak topology on $X$** (resp. the **weak topology on $Y$**), denoted by $\text{𝜎}(X,Y)$ (resp. by $\text{𝜎}(Y,X)$), is the [weak topology](wikipedia://en/63735167#Weak_topology) on $X$ (resp. on $Y$) with respect to the canonical pairing $\langle X, Y\rangle$.

The topology $\sigma(X,Y)$ is the [initial topology](wikipedia://en/1197123) of $X$ with respect to $Y$.

If $Y$ is a vector space of linear functionals on $X$, then the continuous dual of $X$ with respect to the topology $\sigma(X,Y)$ is precisely equal to $Y$.[^n2](Rudin 1991, Theorem 3.10)

#### The weak and weak\* topologies {#The_weak_and_weak*_topologies}

Let $X$ be a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVS) over $\mathbb{K}$, that is, $X$ is a $\mathbb{K}$ [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) equipped with a [topology](wikipedia://en/30450) so that vector addition and [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373) are continuous. We call the topology that $X$ starts with the **original**, **starting**, or **given topology** (the reader is cautioned against using the terms "[initial topology](wikipedia://en/1197123)" and "[strong topology](wikipedia://en/589726)" to refer to the original topology since these already have well-known meanings, so using them may cause confusion). We may define a possibly different topology on $X$ using the topological or [continuous dual space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_dual_space) $X^*$, which consists of all [linear functionals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) from $X$ into the base field $\mathbb{K}$ that are [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) with respect to the given topology.

Recall that $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is the canonical evaluation map defined by $\langle x,x'\rangle =x'(x)$ for all $x\in X$ and $x'\in X^*$, where in particular, $\langle \cdot,x'\rangle=x'(\cdot)= x'$.

**Definition.** The **weak topology on $X$** is the weak topology on $X$ with respect to the [canonical pairing](wikipedia://en/63735167#Canonical_duality_on_a_vector_space) $\langle X,X^*\rangle$.[^n2]

**Definition**: The **weak topology on $X^*$** is the weak topology on $X^*$ with respect to the [canonical pairing](wikipedia://en/63735167#Canonical_duality_on_a_vector_space) $\langle X,X^*\rangle$.[^n2] This topology is also called the **weak\* topology**.

### Weak topology induced by the continuous dual space {#Weak_topology_induced_by_the_continuous_dual_space}

Alternatively, the **weak topology** on a TVS $X$ is the [initial topology](wikipedia://en/1197123) with respect to the family $X^*$. In other words, it is the [coarsest](wikipedia://en/393069) topology on X such that each element of $X^*$ remains a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122).

A [subbase](wikipedia://en/395395) for the weak topology is the collection of sets of the form $\phi^{-1}(U)$ where $\phi\in X^*$ and $U$ is an open subset of the base field $\mathbb{K}$. In other words, a subset of $X$ is open in the weak topology [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it can be written as the union of (possibly infinitely many) sets, each of which is the intersection of finitely many sets of the form $\phi^{-1}(U)$.

From this point of view, the weak topology is the coarsest [polar topology](wikipedia://en/1789978).

### Weak convergence {#Weak_convergence}

The weak topology is characterized by the following condition: a [net](wikipedia://en/22170) $(x_\lambda)$ in $X$ converges in the weak topology to the element $x$ of $X$ if and only if $\phi(x_\lambda)$ converges to $\phi(x)$ in $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ for all $\phi\in X^*$.

In particular, if $x_n$ is a [sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence_%28mathematics%29) in $X$, then $x_n$ **converges weakly to** $x$ if

$$\varphi(x_n) \to \varphi(x)$$

as $n \to \infty$ for all $\varphi \in X^*$. In this case, it is customary to write

$$x_n \overset{\mathrm{w}}{\longrightarrow} x$$

or, sometimes,

$$x_n \rightharpoonup x.$$

### Other properties {#Other_properties}

If $X$ is equipped with the weak topology, then addition and scalar multiplication remain continuous operations, and $X$ is a [locally convex topological vector space](wikipedia://en/518844).

If $X$ is a normed space, then the dual space $X^*$ is itself a normed vector space by using the norm

$$\|\phi\|=\sup_{\|x\|\le 1} |\phi(x)|.$$

This norm gives rise to a topology, called the **strong topology**, on $X^*$. This is the topology of [uniform convergence](wikipedia://en/50652). The uniform and strong topologies are generally different for other spaces of linear maps; see below.

## Weak-\* topology {#Weak-*_topology}

**Weak-\* convergence**

A [net](wikipedia://en/22170) $\phi_{\lambda}$ in $X^*$ is convergent to $\phi$ in the weak-\* topology if it converges pointwise:

$$\phi_{\lambda} (x) \to \phi (x)$$

for all $x\in X$. In particular, a [sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence_%28mathematics%29) of $\phi_n\in X^*$ converges to $\phi$ provided that

$$\phi_n(x)\to\phi(x)$$

for all $x \in X$. In this case, one writes

$$\phi_n \overset{w^*}{\to} \phi$$

as $n \to \infty$.

Weak-\* convergence is sometimes called the **simple convergence** or the **pointwise convergence**. Indeed, it coincides with the [pointwise convergence](wikipedia://en/340198) of linear functionals.

### Properties {#Properties}

If $X$ is a [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) (i.e. has a countable dense subset) [locally convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_convex) space and *H* is a norm-bounded subset of its continuous dual space, then *H* endowed with the weak\* (subspace) topology is a [metrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrizable) topological space.[^n2] However, for infinite-dimensional spaces, the metric cannot be translation-invariant.[^n3] If $X$ is a separable [metrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrizable_TVS) [locally convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_convex) space then the weak\* topology on the continuous dual space of $X$ is separable.[^n2]

**Properties on normed spaces**

By definition, the weak\* topology is weaker than the weak topology on $X^*$. An important fact about the weak\* topology is the [Banach–Alaoglu theorem](wikipedia://en/577366): if $X$ is normed, then the closed unit ball in $X^*$ is weak\*-[compact](wikipedia://en/6042) (more generally, the [polar](wikipedia://en/1764022) in $X^*$ of a neighborhood of 0 in $X$ is weak\*-compact). Moreover, the closed unit ball in a normed space $X$ is compact in the weak topology if and only if $X$ is [reflexive](wikipedia://en/210091).

In more generality, let $F$ be locally compact valued field (e.g., the reals, the complex numbers, or any of the [p-adic number](wikipedia://en/51423) systems). Let $X$ be a normed topological vector space over $F$, compatible with the absolute value in $F$. Then in $X^*$, the topological dual space $X$ of continuous $F$-valued linear functionals on $X$, all norm-closed balls are compact in the weak\* topology.

If $X$ is a normed space, a version of the [Heine-Borel theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heine-Borel_theorem) holds. In particular, a subset of the continuous dual is weak\* compact if and only if it is weak\* closed and norm-bounded.[^n2] This implies, in particular, that when $X$ is an infinite-dimensional normed space then the closed unit ball at the origin in the dual space of $X$ does not contain any weak\* neighborhood of 0 (since any such neighborhood is norm-unbounded).[^n2] Thus, even though norm-closed balls are compact, X\* is not weak\* [locally compact](wikipedia://en/48632).

If $X$ is a normed space, then $X$ is separable if and only if the weak\* topology on the closed unit ball of $X^*$ is metrizable,[^n2] in which case the weak\* topology is metrizable on norm-bounded subsets of $X^*$. If a normed space $X$ has a dual space that is separable (with respect to the dual-norm topology) then $X$ is necessarily separable.[^n2] If $X$ is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), the weak\* topology is not metrizable on all of $X^*$ unless $X$ is finite-dimensional.[^o28]

## Examples {#Examples}

### Hilbert spaces {#Hilbert_spaces}

Consider, for example, the difference between strong and weak convergence of functions in the [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) $L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^n)$. Strong convergence of a sequence $\psi_k\in L^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$ to an element $\psi$ means that

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} |\psi_k-\psi |^2\,{\rm d}\mu\, \to 0$$

as $k \to \infty$. Here the notion of convergence corresponds to the norm on $L^{2}$.

In contrast weak convergence only demands that

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \bar{\psi}_k f\,\mathrm d\mu \to \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \bar{\psi}f\, \mathrm d\mu$$

for all functions $f \in L^{2}$ (or equivalently, all *f* in a [dense subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_subset) of $L^{2}$ such as a space of [test functions](wikipedia://en/301505), if the sequence {*ψ*<sub>*k*</sub>} is bounded). For given test functions, the relevant notion of convergence only corresponds to the topology used in $\mathbb{C}$.

For example, in the Hilbert space $L^{2}(0,\pi)$, the sequence of functions

$$\psi_k(x) = \sqrt{2/\pi}\sin(k x)$$

forms an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063). In particular, the (strong) limit of $\psi_k$ as $k \to \infty$ does not exist. On the other hand, being an orthonormal sequence in a Hilbert space, it must converge weakly to zero, by the [Riesz representation theorem](wikipedia://en/26452), [Bessel's inequality](wikipedia://en/1446277), and the [nth-term test](wikipedia://en/9980598) for series. Alternatively, the [Riemann–Lebesgue lemma](wikipedia://en/1461209) can be used to show that the weak limit exists and is zero.

### Distributions {#Distributions}

One normally obtains spaces of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) by forming the strong dual of a space of test functions (such as the [compactly supported](wikipedia://en/381013) smooth functions on $\mathbb{R}^n$). In an alternative construction of such spaces, one can take the weak dual of a space of test functions inside a Hilbert space such as $L^{2}$. Thus one is led to consider the idea of a [rigged Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/393048).

### Weak topology induced by the algebraic dual {#Weak_topology_induced_by_the_algebraic_dual}

Suppose that $X$ is a vector space and *X*<sup>\#</sup> is the [algebraic dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_dual) space of $X$ (i.e. the vector space of all linear functionals on $X$). If $X$ is endowed with the weak topology induced by *X*<sup>\#</sup> then the continuous dual space of $X$ is $X^{\#}$, every bounded subset of $X$ is contained in a finite-dimensional vector subspace of $X$, every vector subspace of $X$ is closed and has a [topological complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_complement).[^n4]

## Operator topologies {#Operator_topologies}

If $X$ and $Y$ are topological vector spaces, the space $L(X,Y)$ of [continuous linear operators](wikipedia://en/1802169) $f : X \to Y$ may carry a variety of different possible topologies. The naming of such topologies depends on the kind of topology one is using on the target space $Y$ to define operator convergence (Yosida 1980, IV.7 Topologies of linear maps). There are, in general, a vast array of possible [operator topologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_topology) on $L(X,Y)$, whose naming is not entirely intuitive.

For example, the **[strong operator topology](wikipedia://en/458699)** on $L(X,Y)$ is the topology of *pointwise convergence*. For instance, if $Y$ is a normed space, then this topology is defined by the seminorms indexed by $x \in X$:

$$f\mapsto \|f(x)\|_Y.$$

More generally, if a family of seminorms *Q* defines the topology on $Y$, then the seminorms $p_{q, x}$ on $L(X,Y)$ defining the strong topology are given by

$$p_{q,x} : f \mapsto q(f(x)),$$

indexed by $q \in Q$ and $x \in X$.

In particular, see the [weak operator topology](wikipedia://en/458698) and [weak\* operator topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak*_operator_topology).
