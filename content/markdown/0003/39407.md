# Dirac equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dirac equation"}

In [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics), the **Dirac equation** is a [relativistic wave equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_wave_equation) derived by British physicist [Paul Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742) in 1928.[^o2] In its [free form](wikipedia://en/39407#Covariant_form_and_relativistic_invariance), or including electromagnetic interactions, it describes all [spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin-1/2) [massive particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massive_particle), called "Dirac particles", such as [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) and [quarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark) for which [parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_%28physics%29) is a [symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_%28physics%29). It is consistent with both the principles of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) and the theory of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962),[^o3] and was the first theory to fully account for special relativity in the context of quantum mechanics. The equation is validated by its rigorous accounting of the observed [fine structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_structure) of the [hydrogen spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_spectral_series) and has become vital in the building of the [Standard Model](wikipedia://en/47641).[^o4]

:::

The equation also implied the existence of a new form of matter, *[antimatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimatter)*, previously unsuspected and unobserved. The existence of antimatter was experimentally confirmed several years later. It also provided a *theoretical* justification for the introduction of several component wave functions in [Pauli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Pauli)'s [phenomenological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenology_%28particle_physics%29) theory of [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829). The wave functions in the Dirac theory are vectors of four [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) (known as [Dirac spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_spinor)), two of which resemble [the Pauli wavefunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauli_equation) in the non-relativistic limit, in contrast to the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874), which described wave functions of only one complex value. Moreover, in the limit of zero mass, the Dirac equation reduces to the [Weyl equation](wikipedia://en/33162584). In the context of [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), the Dirac equation is reinterpreted to describe quantum fields corresponding to spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ particles.

Dirac did not fully appreciate the importance of his results; however, the entailed explanation of spin as a consequence of the union of quantum mechanics and relativity—and the eventual discovery of the [positron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron)—represents one of the great triumphs of [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028). This accomplishment has been described as fully on par with the works of [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627), [James Clerk Maxwell](wikipedia://en/28989696), and [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736) before him.[^o5] The equation has been deemed by some physicists to be "the real seed of modern physics".[^o6] The Dirac equation has been described as the "centerpiece of relativistic quantum mechanics", and as "perhaps the most important \[equation\] in all of quantum mechanics".[^o7]

## History {#History}

### Early attempts at a relativistic formulation {#Early_attempts_at_a_relativistic_formulation}

The first phase in the development of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), lasting between 1900 and 1925, focused on explaining individual phenomena that could not be explained through [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586).[^o10] The second phase, starting in the mid-1920s, saw the development of two systematic frameworks governing quantum mechanics. The first, known as [matrix mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_mechanics), uses [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) to describe [physical observables](wikipedia://en/294248); it was developed in 1925 by [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg), [Max Born](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Born), and [Pascual Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascual_Jordan).[^o11] The second, known as wave mechanics, uses a [wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691) known as the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874) to describe the [state](wikipedia://en/30876419) of a [system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_system); it was developed the next year by [Erwin Schrödinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Schrödinger). While these two frameworks were initially seen as competing approaches, they would later be shown to be equivalent.[^o12]

Both these frameworks only formulated quantum mechanics in a [non-relativistic](wikipedia://en/26962) setting.[^o12] This was seen as a deficiency right from the start, with Schrödinger originally attempting to formulate a [relativistic version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_wave_equations) of the Schrödinger equation, in the process discovering the [Klein–Gordon equation](wikipedia://en/209627).[^o11] However, after showing that this [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) did not correctly reproduce the relativistic corrections to the [hydrogen atom](wikipedia://en/14225) [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emission_spectrum) for which an exact form was known due to [Arnold Sommerfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Sommerfeld), he abandoned his [relativistic formulation](wikipedia://en/30001).[^o13] The Klein–Gordon equation was also found by at least six other authors in the same year.[^o14]

During 1926 and 1927, there was a widespread effort to incorporate relativity into quantum mechanics, largely through two approaches. The first was to consider the Klein–Gordon as the correct relativistic generalization of the Schrödinger equation.[^o14] Such an approach was viewed unfavourably by many leading [theorists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist) since it failed to correctly predict numerous [experimental results](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_data), and more importantly it appeared difficult to reconcile with the principles of quantum mechanics as understood at the time.[^o15] These conceptual issues primarily arose due to the presence of a [second temporal derivative](wikipedia://en/3224219).[^o13]

The second approach introduced relativistic effects as corrections to the known non-relativistic formulas.[^o15] This provided many provisional answers that were expected to eventually be supplanted by some yet-unknown relativistic formulation of quantum mechanics. One notable result by Heisenberg and Jordan was the introduction of two terms for [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829) and relativity into the hydrogen [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381), allowing them to derive the [first-order approximation](wikipedia://en/346769) of the Sommerfeld [fine structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_structure) formula.[^o16]

A parallel development during this time was the concept of spin, first introduced in 1925 by [Samuel Goudsmit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Goudsmit) and [George Uhlenbeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Uhlenbeck).[^o17] Shortly after, it was conjectured by Schrödinger to be the missing link in acquiring the correct Sommerfeld formula.[^o15] In 1927, [Wolfgang Pauli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Pauli) used the ideas of spin to find an [effective theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_theory) for a nonrelativistic [spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin-1/2) [particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle), the [Pauli equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauli_equation).[^o18] He did this by taking the Schrödinger equation and, rather than just assuming that the wave function depends on the [physical coordinate](wikipedia://en/81931), he also assumed that it depends on a spin coordinate that can take only two values $\pm \tfrac{\hbar}{2}$. While this was still a non-relativistic formulation, he believed that a fully relativistic formulation possibly required a more complicated model for the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron), one that moved beyond a [point particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_particle).[^o15]

### Dirac's relativistic quantum mechanics {#Dirac's_relativistic_quantum_mechanics}

By 1927, many physicists no longer considered the fine structure of hydrogen as a crucial puzzle that called for a completely new relativistic formulation since it could effectively be solved using the Pauli equation or by introducing a spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839) [quantum number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_number) in the Klein–Gordon equation.[^o15] At the fifth [Solvay Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvay_Conference) held that year, [Paul Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742) was primarily concerned with the logical development of quantum mechanics. However, he realized that many other physicists complacently accepted the Klein–Gordon equation as a satisfactory relativistic formulation, which demanded abandoning basic principles of quantum mechanics as understood at the time, to which Dirac strongly objected.[^o15] After his return from [Brussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels), Dirac focused on finding a relativistic theory for electrons. Within two months he solved the problem and published his results on January 2, 1928.[^o2]

In his [paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_publishing#Scholarly_paper), Dirac was guided by two principles from [transformation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_theory_%28quantum_mechanics%29), the first being that the equation should be [invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_%28physics%29) under transformations of special relativity, and the second that it should transform under the transformation theory of quantum mechanics.[^o14] The latter demanded that the equation would have to be [linear](wikipedia://en/91591) in [temporal derivatives](wikipedia://en/1783069), so that it would admit a [probabilistic interpretation](wikipedia://en/4313746). His argument begins with the Klein–Gordon equation[^o20]

$$\left[\boldsymbol p^2+m^2c^2\right]\phi(t,x) = -\frac{\hbar^2}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2} \phi(t,x),$$

describing a particle using the wave function $\phi(t,x)$. Here $\boldsymbol p^2 = p_1^2+p_2^2+p_3^2$ is the square of the [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431), $m$ is the [rest mass](wikipedia://en/162321) of the particle, $c$ is the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light), and $\hbar$ is the [reduced Planck constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_constant). The naive way to get an equation linear in the time derivative is to essentially consider the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of both sides. This replaces $\boldsymbol p^2+m^2c^2$ with $\sqrt{\boldsymbol p^2+m^2c^2}$. However, such a [square root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_square_root) is mathematically problematic for the resulting theory, making it unfeasible.[^o12]

Dirac's first insight was the concept of linearization. He looked for some sort of [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109) $\alpha_i$ that are independent of momentum and spacetime coordinates for which the square root could be rewritten in a linear form[^o12]

$$\sqrt{p_1^2+p_2^2+p_3^2+(mc)^2} = \alpha_1p_1+\alpha_2p_2+\alpha_3p_3+\alpha_4 mc.$$

By squaring this [operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_%28physics%29) and demanding that it reduces to the Klein–Gordon equation, Dirac found that the variables must satisfy $\alpha_i^2=1$ and $\alpha_i\alpha_j + \alpha_j\alpha_i=0$ if $i \neq j$. Dirac initially considered the $2\times 2$ [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868) as a candidate, but then showed these would not work since it is impossible to find a set of four $2\times 2$ matrices that all [anticommute](wikipedia://en/294358) with each other.[^o11] His second insight was to instead consider [four-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) matrices. In that case the equation would be acting on a [four-component](wikipedia://en/23218956) wavefunction $\psi = (\psi_1, \psi_2, \psi_3, \psi_4)$.[^o21] Such a proposal was much more bold than Pauli's original generalization to a two-component wavefunction in the Pauli equation.[^o15] This is because in Pauli's case, this was motivated by the demand to encode the two spin states of the particle. In contrast, Dirac had no physical argument for a four-component wavefunction, but instead introduced it as a matter of mathematical necessity. When the quantum mechanical momentum operators are substituted for the classical momentum he arrived at what we call the Dirac equation[^o22]

$$\left( i\hbar \mathbf{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{\nabla} + \beta mc\right) \mathbf{\psi}(t,x)= i\frac{\hbar}{c}\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \mathbf{\psi}(t,x).$$

where the wave functions are now 4 dimensional vectors. Dirac constructed the correct matrices $\alpha_i$ without realizing that they form a mathematical structure known of since the early 1880s, the [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305).[^o23] By recasting the equation in a [Lorentz invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_covariance) form, he also showed that it correctly combines special relativity with his principle of quantum mechanical transformation theory, making it a viable candidate for a relativistic theory of the electron.[^o11]

To investigate the equation further, he examined how it behaves in the presence of an [electromagnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field).[^o15] To his surprise, this showed that it described a particle with a [magnetic moment](wikipedia://en/540979) arising due to the particle having spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$. Spin directly emerged from the equation, without Dirac having added it in by hand. Additionally, he focused on showing that the equation successfully reproduces the fine structure of the hydrogen atom, at least to first order. The equation therefore succeeds where all previous attempts have failed, in correctly describing relativistic phenomena of electrons from [first principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_principle) rather than through the ad hoc modification of existing formulas.[^o15]

### Consequences {#Consequences}

Except for his followup paper[^o25] deriving the [Zeeman effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeeman_effect) and Paschen–Back effect from the equation in the presences of a [magnetic fields](wikipedia://en/36563), Dirac left the work of examining the consequences of his equation to others, and only came back to the subject in 1930.[^o15] Once the equation was published, it was recognized as the correct solution to the problem of spin, relativity, and quantum mechanics. At first the Dirac equation was considered the only valid relativistic equation for a particle with [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass). Then in 1934 Pauli and [Victor Weisskopf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Weisskopf) reinterpreted the Klein–Gordon equation as the equation for a relativistic [spinless particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_boson).[^o11]

One of the first calculations was to reproduce the Sommerfeld fine structure formula exactly, which was performed independently by [Charles Galton Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Galton_Darwin) and [Walter Gordon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Gordon_%28physicist%29) in 1928.[^o26][^o27][^o12] This is the first time that the full formula has been derived from first principles. Further work on the mathematics of the equation was undertaken by [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) in 1929.[^o28] In this work he showed that the [massless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massless_particle) Dirac equation can be decomposed into a pair of [Weyl equations](wikipedia://en/33162584).

The Dirac equation was also used to study various scattering processes. In particular, the [Klein–Nishina formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein–Nishina_formula), looking at [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon)-electron [scattering](wikipedia://en/164483), was also derived in 1928.[^o29] [Mott scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mott_scattering), the scattering of electrons off a heavy target such as [atomic nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus), followed the next year.[^o30] Over the following years it was further used to derive other standard scattering processes such as [Moller scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moller_scattering) in 1932[^o31] and [Bhabha scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhabha_scattering) in 1936.[^o32]

A problem that gained more focus with time was the presence of [negative energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_energy) [states](wikipedia://en/30876419) in the Dirac equation, which led to many efforts to try to eliminate such states. Dirac initially simply rejected the negative energy states as unphysical,[^o15] but the problem was made more clear when in 1929 [Oskar Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Klein) showed that in static fields there exists inevitable mixing between the negative and positive energy states.[^o12] Dirac's initial response was to believe that his equation must have some sort of defect, and that it was only the first approximation of a future theory that would not have this problem.[^o12] However, he then suggested a solution to the problem in the form of the [Dirac sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_sea). This is the idea that the universe is filled with an infinite sea of negative energy electrons states. Positive energy electron states then live in this sea and are prevented from decaying to the negative energy states through the [Pauli exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/24669).[^o11]

Additionally, Dirac postulated the existence of positively charged [holes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_hole_theory) in the Dirac sea, which he initially suggested could be the [proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton). However, Oppenheimer showed that in this case stable [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom) could not exist[^o33] and Weyl further showed that the holes would have to have the same mass as the electrons.[^o34] Persuaded by Oppenheimer's and Weyl's argument, Dirac published a paper in 1931 that predicted the existence of an as-yet-unobserved particle that he called an "anti-electron" that would have the same mass and the opposite charge as an electron and that would mutually annihilate upon contact with an electron. He suggested that every particle may have an oppositely charged partner, a concept now called [antimatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimatter)[^o35][^o36]

In 1933 [Carl Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_David_Anderson) discovered the "positive electron", now called a [positron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron), which had all the properties of Dirac's anti-electron.[^o37][^o12] While the Dirac sea was later superseded by [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), its conceptual legacy survived in the idea of a [dynamical vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_vacuum_state) filled with [virtual particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_particle).[^o11] In 1949 [Ernst Stueckelberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Stueckelberg) suggested and [Richard Feynman](wikipedia://en/25523) showed in detail that the negative energy solutions can be interpreted as particles traveling backwards in [proper time](wikipedia://en/494418).[^o48][^o12][^o38][^o39] The concept of the Dirac sea is also realized more explicitly in some [condensed matter systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_matter_physics) in the form of the [Fermi sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_sea), which consists of a sea of filled [valence electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_electron) below some [chemical potential](wikipedia://en/218628).[^o40]

Significant work was done over the following decades to try to find [spectroscopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy) discrepancies compared to the predictions made by the Dirac equation, however it was not until 1947 that [Lamb shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamb_shift) was discovered, which the equation does not predict.[^o11] This led to the development of [quantum electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/25268) in the 1950s, with the Dirac equation then being incorporated within the context of quantum field theory.[^o41] Since it describes the dynamics of Dirac spinors, it went on to play a fundamental role in the [Standard Model](wikipedia://en/47641) as well as many other areas of physics. For example, within condensed matter physics, systems whose fermions have a near linear [dispersion relation](wikipedia://en/853778) are described by the Dirac equation. Such systems are known as [Dirac matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_matter) and they include [graphene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphene) and [topological insulators](wikipedia://en/23649871), which have become a major area of research since the start of the 21st century.[^o40]

The Dirac equation is inscribed upon a plaque on the floor of [Westminster Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Abbey). Unveiled on 13 November 1995, the plaque commemorates Dirac's life.[^o42] The equation, in its [natural units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_units) formulation, is also prominently displayed in the auditorium at the ‘Paul A.M. Dirac’ Lecture Hall at the Patrick M.S. Blackett Institute (formerly The San Domenico Monastery) of the [Ettore Majorana Foundation and Centre for Scientific Culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ettore_Majorana_Foundation_and_Centre_for_Scientific_Culture) in [Erice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erice), [Sicily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicily).

## Formulation {#Formulation}

### Covariant formulation {#Covariant_formulation}

In its modern [field theoretic](wikipedia://en/1293340) formulation, the Dirac equation in 3+1 dimensional [Minkowski spacetime](wikipedia://en/230488) is written in terms of a *Dirac field* $\psi(x)$. This is a [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28physics%29) that assigns a [complex vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_vector) from $\mathbb C^4$ to each point in [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758),[^o23] where the key property of the field is that it transforms as a [Dirac spinor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_spinor) under [Lorentz transformations](wikipedia://en/18404).[^o46] In [natural units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_units) where $\hbar = c = 1$, the [Lorentz covariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_covariant) formulation of the Dirac equation is given by[^o20]

::: {#o47 .equation cue=equation-box-1 name="Dirac equation"}

**Dirac equation.**

:::

where ${{\partial}\!\!\!/} = \gamma^\mu \partial_\mu$ is a [contraction](wikipedia://en/202722) between the [four-gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-gradient) $\partial_\mu$ and the [gamma matrices](wikipedia://en/2907966) $\gamma^\mu$. These are a set of four matrices [generating](wikipedia://en/3171371) the [Dirac algebra](wikipedia://en/10290343), which requires them to satisfy[^o41]

$$\{ \gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu \} =2\eta^{\mu\nu}I_4,$$

where $\{a,b\} = ab+ba$ is the [anticommutator](wikipedia://en/7193), $\eta^{\mu\nu}$ is the Minkowski [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_tensor_%28general_relativity%29) in a mainly [negative signature](wikipedia://en/463225), and $I_4$ is the $4\times4$ [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718). The Dirac algebra is a special case of the more general mathematical structure known as a [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305).[^o20] The Dirac algebra can also be seen as the real part of the [spacetime algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime_algebra). There is no unique choice of matrices for the gamma matrices, with different choices known as different [representations of the algebra](wikipedia://en/854978).[^o48] One common choice, originally discovered by Dirac, is known as the Dirac representation. Here the matrices are given by[^o40]

$$\gamma^0 = \begin{pmatrix} I_2 & 0 \\ 0 & -I_2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \gamma^i = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sigma_i \\ - \sigma_i & 0\end{pmatrix},$$

where $\sigma_i$ are the three [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868) for $i \in \{1,2,3\}$. There are two other common representations for the gamma matrices. The first is the [chiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality_%28physics%29) representation, which is useful when decomposing the Dirac equation into a pair of [Weyl equations](wikipedia://en/33162584).[^o41] The second is the Majorana representation, for which all gamma matrices are [imaginary](wikipedia://en/14554), so the [Dirac operator](wikipedia://en/1079448) is [purely real](wikipedia://en/20646438).[^o20] This representation is useful for studying [Majorana spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majorana_equation), which are purely real four-component [spinor](wikipedia://en/29276) solutions of the Dirac equation.[^o40]

By taking the [hermitian conjugate](wikipedia://en/667175) of the Dirac equation and multiplying it by $\gamma^0$ from the right, the adjoint Dirac equation can be found, with this being the [equation of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equation_of_motion) for the [Dirac adjoint](wikipedia://en/695917) $\bar \psi = \psi^\dagger \gamma^0$. It is given by[^o49]

$$\bar \psi(x)(-i \gamma^\mu \overleftarrow{\partial}_\mu -m)=0.$$

The adjoint spinor is useful in forming Lorentz invariant quantities. For example, the [bilinear](wikipedia://en/449477) $\psi^\dagger \psi$ is not Lorentz invariant, but $\bar \psi \psi$ is.[^o40] Here $\overleftarrow{\partial}_\mu$ is shorthand notation for a [partial derivative](wikipedia://en/52565) acting on the left. In regular notation, the adjoint Dirac equation is equivalent to[^o41]

$$-i\partial_\mu \bar \psi(x)\gamma^\mu -m\bar \psi(x)=0.$$

The Dirac equation can be rewritten in a non-covariant form similar to that of the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874)[^o11]

$$i \hbar \frac{\partial }{\partial t}\psi(t,\boldsymbol x) = -i \hbar c\boldsymbol \alpha \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla}\psi(t,\boldsymbol x) + \beta mc^2 \psi(t, \boldsymbol x).$$

The right-hand side of the equation is the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381) acting on the Dirac spinor $H\psi$. Here $\boldsymbol \alpha$ and $\beta$ are a set of four [Hermitian](wikipedia://en/189682) $4\times 4$ matrices that all anticommute with each other and square to the identity. They are related to the gamma matrices through $\gamma^0 = \beta$ and $\gamma^i = \beta \alpha_i$.[^o12] This form is useful in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), where the Hamiltonian can be easily modified to solve a wide range of problems, such as by introducing a [potential](wikipedia://en/23703)[^o11] or through a [minimal coupling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_coupling) to the [electromagnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field).[^o11]

### Dirac action {#Dirac_action}

The Dirac equation can also be acquired from a [Lagrangian formulation](wikipedia://en/40752010) of the field theory, where the Dirac [action](wikipedia://en/312881) is given by[^o40]

$$S = \int d^4 x \bar \psi(i{{\partial}\!\!\!/} - m)\psi.$$

The equation then arises as the [Euler–Lagrange equation](wikipedia://en/294995) of this action, found by varying the adjoint spinor $\bar \psi(x)$.[^o41] Meanwhile, the adjoint Dirac equation is acquired by varying the spinor $\psi(x)$. The action formulation of the Dirac equation has the advantage of making the [symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_%28physics%29) of the Dirac equation more explicit, since they leave its action invariant.[^o20] [Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159) then allows for the direct calculation of [currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-current) corresponding to these symmetries.[^o51] Additionally, the action is usually used to define the associated [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), such as through the [path integral formulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_integral_formulation).[^o20]

### Meaning of the Dirac fields {#Meaning_of_the_Dirac_fields}

In quantum mechanics, the Dirac spinor $\psi(x)$ corresponds to a four-component spinor [wave function](wikipedia://en/145343) describing the [state](wikipedia://en/30876419) of a [Dirac fermion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_fermion).[^o11] Its position [probability density](wikipedia://en/43487), the probability of finding the fermion in a region of space, is described by the zeroth component of its vector current, $\psi^\dagger \psi$.[^o11] In the case of a large number of [particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle), it can also be interpreted as the [charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_density).[^o23] An appropriate [normalization](wikipedia://en/77361219) is required to ensure that the total probability across all of space is equal to one, with [probability conservation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_current) following directly from the [conservation](wikipedia://en/1097925) of the vector current. The Dirac equation is the [relativistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_wave_equations) analogue of the Schrödinger equation for the Dirac fermion wavefunction.

In the [second quantization](wikipedia://en/418156) form of quantum field theory the Dirac spinor is [quantized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantization_%28physics%29) to be an operator-valued spinor field $\hat \psi(x)$.[^o53] In contrast to quantum mechanics, it no longer represents the state in the [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), but is rather the [operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_%28physics%29) that acts on states to create or destroy particles.[^o20][^o54] [Observables](wikipedia://en/294248) are formed using [expectation values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_function_%28quantum_field_theory%29) of these operators.[^o55] The Dirac equation then becomes an operator equation describing the state-independent evolution of the operator-valued spinor field[^o56]

$$(i{{\partial}\!\!\!/} - m)\hat \psi(x) = 0.$$

In the [path integral formulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_integral_formulation) of quantum field theory, the spinor field $\psi(x)$ is an anti-commuting [Grassmann-valued](wikipedia://en/3117887) field that only acts as an integration variable.[^o46] The Dirac equation then emerges as the classical [saddle point](wikipedia://en/640249) behaviour of the path integral. It also arises as an equation of the expectation value of the classical field variables[^o56]

$$\langle (i{{\partial}\!\!\!/} - m)\psi(x)\rangle = 0,$$

in the sense of the [Schwinger–Dyson equations](wikipedia://en/811030).[^o20] This version of the equation can also be acquired by taking the expectation value of the operator equation.

The Dirac equation also arises in describing the [time evolution](wikipedia://en/937739) of a spinor field in [classical field theory](wikipedia://en/1293340). Such a field theory would have to lift the Lorentz group to the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) $\text{SL}(2,\mathbb C)$ as its [spacetime symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime_symmetries) group, since the former does not admit [spinor representations](wikipedia://en/10160091).[^o51] This is in contrast to the quantum theory which does admit spinor representations even when the spacetime symmetries are described by the Lorentz group. This is because the states in a Hilbert space are defined only up to a complex phase,[^o57] so particles belong to [projective representations](wikipedia://en/348860) rather than regular representations, with the projective representations of the Lorentz group being equivalent to regular representations of $\text{SL}(2,\mathbb C)$.[^o20] Classical spinor fields do not ordinarily arise as macroscopic classical fields in our universe because the [Pauli exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/24669) prevents populating the field with a sufficient number of particles to reach the [classical limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_limit).[^o51] However, many-fermion systems can often be described classically using fermion bilinears or collective observables, such as the current or the [stress-energy tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress-energy_tensor).

## Properties {#Properties}

### Lorentz transformations {#Lorentz_transformations}

The [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489) $\text{SO}(1,3)$, describing the transformation between [inertial reference frames](wikipedia://en/14838), can admit many different [representations](wikipedia://en/19378200).[^o61] A representation is a particular choice of [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) that [faithfully](wikipedia://en/2617872) represent the action of the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) on some [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370), which is sometimes referred to as a [module](wikipedia://en/276410), where the [dimensionality](wikipedia://en/38267) of the matrices can differ between representations.[^o62] For example, the Lorentz group can be represented by $4\times 4$ real matrices $\Lambda$ acting on the vector space $\mathbb R^{1,3}$, corresponding to how [Lorentz transformations](wikipedia://en/18404) act on vectors or on [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758).[^o20] Another related representation is a set of $4\times 4$ complex matrices acting on [Dirac spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_spinor) in the complex vector space $\mathbb C^4$.[^o63] A smaller related representation is a set of $2\times 2$ complex matrices acting on [Weyl spinors](wikipedia://en/33162584) in the $\mathbb C^2$ vector space.[^o64]

[Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) elements can be generated using the corresponding [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944), which together with a [Lie bracket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_bracket), describes the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) of the group [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) around its identity element.[^o65] The [basis elements](wikipedia://en/18420) of this vector space are known as [generators](wikipedia://en/99945) of the group. A particular group element is then acquired by [exponentiating](wikipedia://en/672731) a corresponding tangent space vector.[^o66] The generators of the Lorentz Lie algebra must satisfy certain [anticommutation](wikipedia://en/294358) relations, known as a Lie bracket. The six vectors can be packaged into an antisymmetric object $X^{\mu\nu}$ indexed by $\{\mu,\nu\}$, with the bracket for the Lorentz algebra given by[^o41]

$$[X^{\mu\nu},X^{\rho\sigma}] = i(\eta^{\nu \rho}X^{\mu\sigma} - \eta^{\mu\rho}X^{\nu \sigma} + \eta^{\mu\sigma}X^{\nu \rho}-\eta^{\nu \sigma}X^{\mu\rho}).$$

This algebra admits numerous representation, where each generator is represented by a matrix, with each [algebra representation](wikipedia://en/854978) generating a corresponding [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) of the group.[^o66] For example, the representation acting on real vectors is given by the six matrices $X^{\mu\nu} = M^{\mu\nu}$ where[^o66]

$$\left(M^{\mu\nu}\right)^\rho{}_\sigma = i(\eta^{\mu\rho}\delta^\nu{}_\sigma - \eta^{\nu \rho}\delta^\mu{}_\sigma).$$

The Lorentz transformation matrix can then be acquired from these generators through an exponentiation[^o20]

$$\Lambda = \exp\left(\tfrac{i}{2}\omega_{\mu\nu}M^{\mu\nu}\right),$$

where $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ is an antisymmetric matrix encoding the six [degrees of freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_of_freedom_%28physics_and_chemistry%29) of the Lorentz group used to specify the particular group element. These correspond to the three boosts and three [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789).[^o41]

Another representation for the Lorentz algebra is the [spinor representation](wikipedia://en/10160091) where the generators are given by[^o20]

$$S^{\mu\nu} = \tfrac{i}{4}[\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu].$$

In this case the Lorentz group element, specified by $\omega_{\mu\nu}$, is given by

$$S[\Lambda] = \exp\left(\tfrac{i}{2}\omega_{\mu\nu}S^{\mu\nu}\right).$$

The mapping of $\Lambda \mapsto S[\Lambda]$ is not one-to-one since there are two consistent choices for $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ that give the same $\Lambda$ but a different $S[\Lambda]$.[^o65] This is a consequence of the spinor representation being [projective representations](wikipedia://en/348860) of the Lorentz group $\text{SO}(1,3)$.[^o57] Equivalently, they are regular representation of [$\text{SL}(2,\mathbb C)$](wikipedia://en/144823), which is a [double cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_cover_%28topology%29) of the Lorentz group.[^o51]

Under a Lorentz transformation, spacetime coordinates transform under the vector representation $x'^\mu=\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu$, while the spinors transform under the spinor representation

$$\psi'(x') = S[\Lambda]\psi(x).$$

The Dirac equation is a Lorentz covariant equation, meaning that it takes the form in all inertial reference frames.[^o63] That is, it takes the same form when for a spinor $\psi(x)$ with [coordinates](wikipedia://en/81931) $x$, as well as for a Lorentz transformed spinor $\psi'(x')$ in the Lorentz transformed coordinates $x' = \Lambda x$

$$(i{{\partial}\!\!\!/}'-m)\psi'(x') = 0,$$

where $\partial_\mu'$ is the [four-gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-gradient) for the new coordinates $x'$. Meanwhile, the Dirac action is Lorentz invariant, meaning that it is the same in all reference frames $S'=S$.[^o20]

::: {#o68 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Symmetries"}

### Symmetries {#Symmetries}

Dirac's theory is invariant under a global $\text{U}(1)$ [symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_%28physics%29) acting on the [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_factor) of the spinor[^o20]

$$\psi(x) \rightarrow e^{i\alpha} \psi(x), \qquad \bar \psi(x) \rightarrow e^{-i\alpha} \bar \psi(x).$$

This has a corresponding [conserved current](wikipedia://en/1097925) that can be derived from the action using [Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159), given by[^o40]

$$j^\mu = \bar \psi \gamma^\mu \psi.$$

This symmetry is known as the vector symmetry because its current transforms as a [vector](wikipedia://en/23218956) under Lorentz transformations. Promoting this symmetry to a [gauge symmetry](wikipedia://en/23912155) gives rise to [quantum electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/25268).

In the [massless limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massless_particle), the Dirac equation has a second inequivalent $\text{U}(1)$ symmetry known as the [axial symmetry](wikipedia://en/72988466), which acts on the spinors as[^o69]

$$\psi(x) \rightarrow e^{i\beta \gamma^5}\psi(x), \qquad \bar \psi(x) \rightarrow e^{i\beta \gamma^5}\bar \psi(x),$$

where $\gamma_5$ is the chiral matrix. This arises because in the massless limit the Dirac equation reduces to a pair of [Weyl equations](wikipedia://en/33162584).[^o20] Each of these is invariant under a $\text{U}(1)$ phase symmetry. These two symmetries can then be grouped into the vector symmetry where both Weyl spinors transform by the same phase, and the axial symmetry where they transform under the opposite signs of the phase.[^o20] The current corresponding to the axial symmetry is given by[^o40]

$$j^\mu_5 = \bar \psi \gamma^\mu \gamma^5 \psi.$$

This transforms as a [pseudovector](wikipedia://en/166356), meaning that its spatial part is odd under parity transformations.[^o64] [Classically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_physics), the single-fermion axial symmetry admits a well-formulated gauge theory, but at the [quantum level](wikipedia://en/25202) it has a [chiral anomaly](wikipedia://en/312229) that provides an obstruction towards gauging.[^o20]

The spacetime symmetries of the Dirac action correspond to the [Poincaré group](wikipedia://en/230487), a combination of spacetime [translations](wikipedia://en/204682) and the Lorentz group.[^o20] Invariance under the four spacetime translations yields the Dirac [stress-energy tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress-energy_tensor) as its [four-currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-current)[^o69]

$$T^{\mu\nu} = i \bar \psi \gamma^\mu \partial^\nu \psi - \eta^{\mu\nu}\mathcal L_\text{Dirac},$$

where the last term is the Dirac Lagrangian, which vanishes [on shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_shell_and_off_shell). Invariance under Lorentz transformations meanwhile yields a set of currents indexed by $\rho$ and $\sigma$, given by[^o69]

$$(\mathcal J^\mu)^{\rho \sigma} = x^\rho T^{\mu \sigma} - x^\sigma T^{\mu \rho} + \bar \psi \gamma^\mu S^{\rho \sigma}\psi,$$

where $S^{\rho \sigma}$ are the spinor representation generators of the Lorentz Lie algebra, used to define how spinors transform under Lorentz transformations.

:::

### Plane wave solutions {#Plane_wave_solutions}

Acting on the Dirac equation with the operator $(i{{\partial}\!\!\!/} +m)$ gives rise to the [Klein–Gordon equation](wikipedia://en/209627) for each component of the spinor[^o71]

$$(i{{\partial}\!\!\!/} +m)(i{{\partial}\!\!\!/} -m)\psi(x) = \left(\partial_\mu \partial^\mu + m^2\right)\psi(x) = 0.$$

As a result, any solution to the Dirac equation is also automatically a solution to the Klein–Gordon equation.[^o51] Its solutions can therefore be written as a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of [plane waves](wikipedia://en/41559).[^o72]

The Dirac equation admits positive [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779) plane wave solutions[^o72]

$$\psi(x) = u(\boldsymbol p)e^{ip_\mu x^\mu}$$

with a positive energy given by $p_0 = \sqrt{\boldsymbol p^2+m^2}>0$. It also admits [negative frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_frequency) solutions taking the same form except with $p_0 = -\sqrt{\boldsymbol p^2+m^2}<0$.[^o20] It is more convenient to rewrite these negative frequency solutions by flipping the sign of the momentum to ensure that they have a positive [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) $p_0>0$ and so take the form[^o41]

$$\psi(x) = v(\boldsymbol p)e^{-ip_\mu x^\mu}.$$

At the classical level these are positive and negative frequency solutions to a classical wave equation, but in the quantum theory they correspond to operators creating particles with spinor [polarization](wikipedia://en/41564) $u(\boldsymbol p)$ or [annihilating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creation_and_annihilation_operator) [antiparticles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiparticle) with spinor polarization $v(\boldsymbol p)$.[^o20] Both these spinor polarizations satisfy the [momentum space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_and_momentum_space) Dirac equation[^o72]

$$({{p}\!\!\!/} + m)u(\boldsymbol p) = 0,$$

$$({{p}\!\!\!/} - m)v(\boldsymbol p) = 0.$$

Since these are simple matrix equations, they can be solved directly once an explicit representation for the gamma matrices is chosen. In the chiral representation the general solution is given by[^o20]

$$u(\boldsymbol p) = {\sqrt{p\cdot \sigma}\xi_s \choose \sqrt{p\cdot \bar \sigma}\xi_s}, \qquad v(\boldsymbol p) = {\sqrt{p\cdot \sigma}\eta_s \choose - \sqrt{p\cdot \bar \sigma}\eta_s},$$

where $\xi_s$ and $\eta_s$ are arbitrary complex 2-vectors, describing the two spin degrees of freedom for the particle and two for the antiparticle.[^o41] In the massless limit, these spin states correspond to the possible [helicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helicity_%28particle_physics%29) states that the massless fermions can have, either being [left-handed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality_%28physics%29) or right-handed.[^o20]

:::

## Related equations {#Related_equations}

### Related Dirac equations {#Related_Dirac_equations}

While the standard Dirac equation was originally derived in a $3+1$ dimensional [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758), it can be directly generalized to arbitrary [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) and [metric signatures](wikipedia://en/463225), where it takes the same covariant form.[^o75] The crucial difference is that the [gamma matrices](wikipedia://en/2907966) must be changed to gamma matrices of the [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305) appropriate in those dimensions and metric signature, with the size of the [Dirac spinor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_spinor) corresponding to the dimensionality of the gamma matrices. While the Dirac equation always exists, since every dimension admits Dirac spinors, the properties of these spinors and their relation to other [spinor representations](wikipedia://en/10160091) differs significantly across dimensions.[^o46] Other differences include the absence of a [chirality matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality_%28physics%29) in odd dimensions.[^o40]

The equation can also be generalized from flat [Minkowski spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space) to [curved spacetime](wikipedia://en/1016422) through the introduction of a spinor [covariant derivative](wikipedia://en/431848)[^o46]

$$D_\mu = \partial_\mu - \tfrac{i}{4}\left(\omega_{\alpha \beta}\right)_\mu \gamma^\alpha \gamma^\beta,$$

where $\omega_{\alpha \beta}$ is the [spin connection](wikipedia://en/2915190) that can be defined using the [tetrad formalism](wikipedia://en/11141222). The [Dirac equation in curved spacetime](wikipedia://en/39171284) then takes the form[^o76]

$$(i\gamma^\mu D_\mu -m)\psi(x) = 0.$$

Adding self-interaction terms to the Dirac action gives rise to the [nonlinear Dirac equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_Dirac_equation), which allows for the [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion) to interact with themselves, such as in the [Thirring model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirring_model).[^o77] Interactions between fermions can also be introduced through [electromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism) effects. In particular, the [Breit equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breit_equation) describes multi-electron systems interacting electromagnetically to first order in [perturbation theory](wikipedia://en/297069).[^o78] The [two-body Dirac equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-body_Dirac_equation) is a similar multi-body equation.

A [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) reformulation of the Dirac equation is known as the Dirac–Hestenes equation.[^o79] In this formulation all the components of the Dirac equation have an explicit geometric interpretation. Another related geometric equation is the [Dirac–Kähler equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac–Kähler_equation), which is a geometric analogue of the Dirac equation that can be defined on any general [pseudo-Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/287555) and which acts on [differential forms](wikipedia://en/221519).[^o80] In the case of a [flat manifold](wikipedia://en/12756616), it reduces to four copies of the Dirac equation. However, on curved manifolds this decomposition breaks down and the equation fundamentally differs.[^o81] This equation is used in [lattice field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_field_theory) to describe the continuum limit of [staggered fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staggered_fermion).

### Weyl and Majorana equations {#Weyl_and_Majorana_equations}

The Dirac spinor can be decomposed into a pair of Weyl spinors of opposite chirality $\psi^T = (\psi_L,\psi_R)$.[^o51] Under [Lorentz transformations](wikipedia://en/18404), one transforms as a left-handed Weyl spinor $\psi_L$ and the other as a right-handed Weyl spinor $\psi_R$. In the chiral representation of the gamma matrices, the Dirac equation reduces to the pair of equations for the Weyl spinors[^o72]

$$i\sigma^\mu \partial_\mu \psi_R(x) = m\psi_L(x),$$

$$i \bar \sigma^\mu \partial_\mu \psi_L(x) = m\psi_R(x).$$

In particular, in the [massless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massless_particle) limit the Weyl spinors [decouple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupling_%28physics%29) and the Dirac equation is equivalent to a pair of [Weyl equations](wikipedia://en/33162584).[^o20]

This decomposition has been proposed as an intuitive explanation of [Zitterbewegung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zitterbewegung), as these massless components would propagate at the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) and move in opposite directions, since the [helicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helicity_%28particle_physics%29) is the projection of the [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829) onto the direction of [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion).[^o83] Here the role of the [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) is not to make the [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) less than the speed of light, but instead controls the [average](wikipedia://en/60167) rate at which these reversals occur; specifically, the reversals can be modelled as a [Poisson process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_process).[^o84]

A closely related equation is the [Majorana equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majorana_equation), with this formally taking the same form as the Dirac equation except that it acts on Majorana spinors.[^o20] These are spinors that satisfy a reality condition $\psi = C \bar \psi^T$, where $C$ is the [charge-conjugation operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_symmetry).[^o46] In [higher dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extra_dimensions), the Dirac equation has similar relations to the equations describing other spinor representations that arise in those dimensions.

### Pauli equation {#Pauli_equation}

In the non-relativistic limit, the Dirac equation reduces to the [Pauli equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauli_equation),[^o48] which when coupled to electromagnetism has the form

$$\left[\frac{1}{2m}\left(\boldsymbol \sigma \cdot (\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}-q \boldsymbol A)\right)^2+q\phi \right]|\psi\rangle = 0.$$

Here $\boldsymbol \sigma$ is the vector of [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868) and $\hat{\boldsymbol p} = -i\nabla$ is the [momentum operator](wikipedia://en/2288549). The equation describes a fermion of [charge](wikipedia://en/9804) $q$ coupled to the [electromagnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field) through a [magnetic vector potential](wikipedia://en/1191067) $\boldsymbol A$ and an [electric scalar potential](wikipedia://en/59615) $\phi$.[^o21] The fermion is described through the two-component [wave function](wikipedia://en/145343) $|\psi\rangle$, where each component describes one of the two spin [states](wikipedia://en/30876419) $|\psi\rangle = \psi_+|\uparrow\rangle + \psi_-|\downarrow\rangle$.[^o11]

The Pauli equation is often used in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) to describe phenomena where [relativistic effects](wikipedia://en/30001) are negligible but the spin of the fermion is important.[^o11] It can also be recast in a form which directly shows that the [gyromagnetic ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyromagnetic_ratio) of the fermion described by the Dirac equation is exactly $g=2$.[^o72] In [quantum electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/25268) there are additional quantum corrections that modify this value, give rise to a non-zero [anomalous magnetic moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anomalous_magnetic_dipole_moment).[^o11]

## Gauge symmetry {#Gauge_symmetry}

### Vector symmetry {#Vector_symmetry}

The vector and [axial symmetries](wikipedia://en/72988466) of the Dirac action are both global [symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_%28physics%29), in that they act the same everywhere in [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758).[^o20] In [classical field theory](wikipedia://en/1293340), the [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/40752010) can always be modified in a way to elevate a global symmetry to a local symmetry, which can act differently at different spacetime locations.[^o51] In the case of the vector symmetry, which corresponding to a global change of the [spinor](wikipedia://en/29276) [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28physics%29) by a [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_factor) $\psi \rightarrow e^{i\alpha}\psi$, [gauging](wikipedia://en/23912155) would result in an [action](wikipedia://en/312881) invariant under a local symmetry where the phase can take different values at different points $\psi \rightarrow e^{i\alpha(x)}\psi$.[^o72] While symmetries can always be gauged in classical field theory, this may not always be possible in the full [quantum theory](wikipedia://en/25202) due to various obstructions such as [anomalies](wikipedia://en/364731), which signal that the full quantum theory is not invariant under the local symmetry despite its classical Lagrangian being invariant.[^o63] For example, the axial symmetry in the [massless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massless_particle) Dirac theory with one [fermion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion) is anomalous due to the [chiral anomaly](wikipedia://en/312229) and cannot be gauged.[^o41]

Elevating the vector symmetry to a local symmetry means that the original action is no longer invariant under the symmetry due to the appearance of a $\partial_\mu \alpha(x)$ term arising from the [kinetic term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_term).[^o51] Instead, a new field $A_\mu(x)$, known as a gauge field, must be introduced. It must also transform under the local symmetry as[^o41]

$$A_\mu \rightarrow A_\mu + \tfrac{1}{e}\partial_\mu \alpha,$$

where $e$ plays the role of the [charge](wikipedia://en/9804) of the [Dirac spinor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_spinor) to the gauge field. The Dirac action can then be made invariant under the local symmetry by replacing the derivative term with a new [gauge covariant derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_covariant_derivative)[^o20]

$$D_\mu\psi = \partial_\mu \psi +ie A_\mu\psi.$$

The Dirac action then takes the form[^o72]

$$S = \int d^4x \bar \psi(i{{D}\!\!\!\!/}-m)\psi = \int d^4 x \left[\bar \psi(i{{\partial}\!\!\!/} - m)\psi -ej^\mu A_\mu\right].$$

This result can also be directly acquired through the Noether procedure, which is the general principle that a global symmetry can be gauged through the introduction of a term coupling the gauge field to the appropriate global symmetry [current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-current).[^o51] Additionally introducing the kinetic term for the gauge field results in the action for [quantum electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/25268).[^o41]

### General symmetries {#General_symmetries}

The symmetries that can be gauged can be greatly expanded by considering a theory with $N$ [identical](wikipedia://en/15352) Dirac spinors $\psi^a$ labelled by a new [index](wikipedia://en/320026) $a$. Together these spinors can be considered as being part of a single object $\psi$ with components $\psi^{i,a}$ where $i$ labels the four spin components, and $a$ the different spinors.[^o20] The largest global symmetry of this action is then given by the [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) $\text{U}(N)$.[^o41]

Any continuous [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of $\text{U}(N)$ can then be gauged. In particular, if one wishes to gauge the symmetry acting on all $N$ components, then the symmetry being gauged must admit an $N$-dimensional [unitary representation](wikipedia://en/292788) acting on the spinors.[^o64] That is, for every $g \in \text{G}\subseteq \text{U}(N)$, there exists an $N\times N$ dimensional matrix [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) $\rho(g)$ such that

$$\psi(x) \rightarrow \rho(g)\psi(x)$$

forms a [faithful representation](wikipedia://en/2617872) of the [group](wikipedia://en/19447). Gauging the largest continuous subgroup, $\text{SU}(N)$, requires the spinors to transform in the [fundamental](wikipedia://en/649743) or [antifundamental representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antifundamental_representation).[^o20] Gauging elevates the representation from being spacetime independent to being spacetime dependent $\rho(g(x))$.[^o41] It requires introducing a gauge field $A_\mu^a$, formally the [connection](wikipedia://en/470632) on the [principal bundle](wikipedia://en/310950),[^o89] which necessarily transforms in the [adjoint representation](wikipedia://en/302188) of the [gauge group](wikipedia://en/39240530). The covariant derivative then takes the form[^o51]

$$D_\mu \psi = \partial_\mu \psi + \tilde \rho (A_\mu)\psi.$$

One symmetry that frequently gets gauged is the [special unitary group](wikipedia://en/173997) $\text{SU}(N)$ symmetry. Spinors transforming in the fundamental representation transform as[^o64]

$$\psi(x) \rightarrow U(x)\psi(x),$$

where $U(x)$ is a $N \times N$ [unitary matrix](wikipedia://en/129222) corresponding to a particular group element. The gauge field is a matrix valued gauge field $A^a_\mu$ which transforms in the adjoint representation as[^o20]

$$A_\mu(x) \rightarrow U(x)A_\mu(x) U^{-1}(x) + \frac{1}{g}(\partial_\mu U(x))U^{-1}(x).$$

The covariant derivative then takes the form

$$D_\mu \psi = \partial_\mu \psi + i g A_\mu \psi.$$

By also introducing the kinetic term for the gauge field, one constructs the action for [quantum chromodynamics](wikipedia://en/25264)[^o41]

$$S_{\text{QCD}} = \int d^4 x \left[-\tfrac{1}{4}\text{Tr}(F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}) + \bar \psi(i{{D}\!\!\!/} -m)\psi\right],$$

where in the gauge field kinetic term, the [Yang–Mills fields](wikipedia://en/672202) [strength tensor](wikipedia://en/38184895) is defined as

$$F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu - ig [A_\mu, A_\nu].$$

The case of $\text{SU}(3)$ describes strong interactions of the [quark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark) sector of the [Standard Model](wikipedia://en/47641), with the gauge field corresponding to [gluons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluon) and the Dirac spinors to the quarks.[^o90] The $\text{SU}(2)$ case also plays a role in the Standard Model, describing the [electroweak sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroweak_theory). The gauge field in this case is the [W-boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W-boson), while the Dirac spinors are [leptons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepton).[^o20]
