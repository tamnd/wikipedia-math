# Elementary function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Elementary function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **elementary function** is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) of a single [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109) ([real](wikipedia://en/1268958) or [complex](wikipedia://en/5759#Complex_functions)) that is typically encountered by beginners. The basic elementary functions are [polynomial functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_function), [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210), the [trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function), the [exponential](wikipedia://en/9678) and [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) functions, the [n-th root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-th_root), and the [inverse trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_trigonometric_function), as well as those functions obtained by [addition](wikipedia://en/61338), [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845), [division](wikipedia://en/53696), and [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of these. Some functions which are encountered by beginners are *not* elementary, such as [piecewise-defined functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise-defined_function). More generally, in some modern treatments, elementary functions comprise the set of functions previously enumerated, all [algebraic functions](wikipedia://en/974169), and all functions obtained by [roots of a polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_a_polynomial) whose coefficients are elementary.

:::

The elementary functions were originally defined by [Joseph Liouville](wikipedia://en/341810) in 1833. A key property is that all elementary functions have [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) of any order, which are also elementary, and can be [algorithmically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmically) computed by applying the [differentiation rules](wikipedia://en/10308785) (or the rules for [implicit differentiation](wikipedia://en/470397) in the case of roots). The [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) of an elementary function converges in a neighborhood of every point of its domain. More generally, they are [global analytic functions](wikipedia://en/17211428), defined (possibly with [multiple values](wikipedia://en/222367), such as the elementary function $\sqrt z$ or $\log z$) for every [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) argument, except at [isolated points](wikipedia://en/390557). In contrast, [antiderivatives](wikipedia://en/2823) of elementary functions need not be elementary and is difficult to decide whether a specific elementary function has an elementary antiderivative.

[Liouville's result](wikipedia://en/9482601) is that, if an elementary function has an elementary antiderivative, then this antiderivative is a linear combination of logarithms, where the coefficients and the arguments of the logarithms are elementary functions involved, in some sense, in the definition of the function. The [Risch algorithm](wikipedia://en/289450) (1968) can decide whether an elementary function has an elementary antiderivative, and, if so, to compute it. However, , there is no full implementation.[^o2]

## Examples {#Examples}

### Basic examples {#Basic_examples}

Elementary functions of a single variable $x$ include:

- [Constant functions](wikipedia://en/320861): $2$, $\pi$, $e$, the [Euler–Mascheroni constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Mascheroni_constant), [Apéry's constant](wikipedia://en/2216711), [Khinchin's constant](wikipedia://en/435315), etc. Any constant real (or complex) number.
- [Powers of $x$](wikipedia://en/99491): $x^\alpha=e^{\alpha\log x}$, etc. (The exponent can be any real or complex constant.)
- [Exponential functions](wikipedia://en/9678): $e^x$, $\textstyle a^x=e^{x\log a}$
- [Logarithms](wikipedia://en/17860): $\log x$, $\textstyle \log_a x=\frac {\log x}{\log a}$
- [Trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function): $\textstyle \sin x=\frac{e^{ix}-e^{-ix} }{2i}$, $\textstyle \cos x=\frac{e^{ix}+e^{-ix} }{2}$, $\textstyle \tan x=\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}$, etc.
- [Inverse trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_trigonometric_function): $\arcsin x$, $\arccos x$, etc.
- [Hyperbolic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function): $\sinh x$, $\cosh x$, etc.
- [Inverse hyperbolic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_hyperbolic_function): $\operatorname{arsinh} x$, $\operatorname{arcosh} x$, etc.
- All functions obtained by adding, subtracting, multiplying or dividing a finite number of any of the previous functions[^o6]
- All functions obtained as [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) of a polynomial whose coefficients are elementary functions[^o7][^o8]
- All functions obtained by [composing](wikipedia://en/195947) a finite number of any of the previously listed functions

Certain elementary functions of a single complex variable $z$, such as $\sqrt{z}$ and $\log z$, may be [multivalued](wikipedia://en/222367). Additionally, certain classes of functions may be obtained by others using the final two rules. For example, the exponential function $e^{z}$ composed with addition, subtraction, and division provides the hyperbolic functions, while initial composition with $iz$ instead provides the trigonometric functions.

### Composite examples {#Composite_examples}

Examples of elementary functions include:

- Addition, e.g. ($x + 1$)
- Multiplication, e.g. ($2x$)
- [Polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) functions
- $$-i\log\left(x+i\sqrt{1-x^2}\right)$$

The last function is equal to $\arccos x$, the [inverse cosine](wikipedia://en/374220#Logarithmic_forms), in the entire [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628).

All [monomials](wikipedia://en/357416), [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) and [algebraic functions](wikipedia://en/974169) are elementary.

### Non-elementary functions {#Non-elementary_functions}

All elementary functions are [analytic](wikipedia://en/61478) in the following sense: they can be extended to [functions of a complex variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functions_of_a_complex_variable) (possibly [multivalued](wikipedia://en/222367)) that are analytic except at isolated points of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628).[^o11] Thus nonanalytic functions such as the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) function are not elementary,[^o12] nor are most other [piecewise-defined functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise-defined_function).

Not every analytic function is elementary. In fact, most [special functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_function) are not elementary. Non-elementary functions include:

- the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316)
- non-elementary [Liouvillian functions](wikipedia://en/47147130#Examples), including
  - the [exponential integral](wikipedia://en/648614) (*Ei*) [logarithmic integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_integral) (*Li* or *li*) and [Fresnel integrals](wikipedia://en/271143) (*S* and *C*)
  - the [error function](wikipedia://en/177111), $\mathrm{erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi} }\int_0^x e^{-t^2}\,dt$, a fact that may not be immediately obvious, but can be proven using the [Risch algorithm](wikipedia://en/289450)
- other [nonelementary integrals](wikipedia://en/1520379), including the [Dirichlet integral](wikipedia://en/1235977) and [elliptic integral](wikipedia://en/9960).

## Real-variables and analytic branches {#Real-variables_and_analytic_branches}

In elementary real-variable settings such as those in calculus and pre-calculus, expressions involving roots, logarithms, and inverse trigonometric functions are often interpreted using fixed real branches on specified real domains. This convention is distinct from the analytic convention used in the theory of elementary functions and integration in finite terms. Risch gives a precise definition of elementary functions "in the sense of analysis" by using functions of a complex variable rather than a real variable. In this setting the elementary functions are built using algebraic operations, exponentials, and logarithms, and are represented in differential fields of meromorphic functions on regions of the complex plane or on Riemann surfaces.[^n1]

An algebraic equation such as

$$y^2=x^2$$

has the local analytic branches $y=x$ and $y=-x$. The real identity

$$\sqrt{x^2}=|x|$$

uses the convention that $\sqrt{\cdot}$ denotes the nonnegative real square root, and so changes from one analytic branch to the other at $x=0$. Thus the restrictions of $|x|$ to $(0,\infty)$ and $(-\infty,0)$ are elementary, but the usual real absolute value function on an interval containing $0$ is not a single analytic branch.

The same distinction appears in complex analysis. Whittaker and Watson note that although $x-iy$ and $|z|$, where $z=x+iy$, are functions of $z$ in a general sense, they are not elementary functions of the analytic type under consideration.[^n2] In symbolic computation, functions such as absolute value, signum, and piecewise-defined functions can be treated instead by adjoining a step or conditional operation, which forms a separate class of piecewise function rings.[^n3]

## Closure {#Closure}

It follows directly from the definition that the set of elementary functions is [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under arithmetic operations, (algebraic) root extraction and composition. The elementary functions are closed under [differentiation](wikipedia://en/7921). They are not closed under [limits and infinite sums](wikipedia://en/15287). Importantly, the elementary functions are not closed under [integration](wikipedia://en/2823), as shown by [Liouville's theorem](wikipedia://en/9482601), see [nonelementary integral](wikipedia://en/1520379). The [Liouvillian functions](wikipedia://en/47147130) are defined as the elementary functions and, recursively, the integrals of the Liouvillian functions.

## Extensions {#Extensions}

In late-nineteenth-century analysis, elementary functions were often classified into successive kinds according to the number of independent integrations required for their definition. Functions expressible without any integration—those generated from rational functions by algebraic operations together with exponentiation, logarithms, and circular or hyperbolic trigonometric functions—were said to be elementary functions of the first kind (in the sense of Liouville). Functions defined by a single integration of an algebraic function, such as the error function and the elliptic integrals, were elementary functions of the second kind; their inverses, the elliptic functions, were considered of the same order. Higher "kinds" (third, fourth, etc.) corresponded to multiple integrals of algebraic functions, giving rise to hyperelliptic and more general Abelian functions.[^n4]

The essential point of the classification was that the class of elementary functions of any given kind be closed under the elementary operations—addition, multiplication, composition, and differentiation—so that differentiation never leads outside the same class, while integration may ascend to the next higher kind.

More recently, some have proposed extending the set of elementary functions by extending with certain [transcendental functions](wikipedia://en/297021), to include, for example, the [Lambert W function](wikipedia://en/92465)[^o21] or [elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/69939),[^o22] all of which are analytic. The key attribute, from the perspective of the Liouville theorem, is that as a class, they are closed under taking derivatives. For example, the Lambert function $w=W(z)$, which is defined implicitly by the equation $we^w=z$, has a derivative which can be obtained by [implicit differentiation](wikipedia://en/470397):

$$W'(z) = \frac{e^{-W(z)}}{1+W(z)},$$

which is again "elementary", provided that $W(z)$ is.

## Differential algebra {#Differential_algebra}

The mathematical definition of an *elementary function* is formalized in [differential algebra](wikipedia://en/1816587). A [differential field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_field) is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) with an extra operation of derivation (algebraic version of differentiation). Using the derivation operation new equations can be written and their solutions used in [extensions](wikipedia://en/11634) of the algebra. By starting with the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210), two special types of transcendental extensions (the logarithm and the exponential) can be added to the field building a tower containing elementary functions.

A *differential field* $F$ is a field together with a [derivation](wikipedia://en/897658) $u\mapsto \partial u$ that maps $F$ to itself. The derivation generalizes [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921), being linear (that is, $\partial (u + v) = \partial u + \partial v$) and satisfying the [Leibniz product rule](wikipedia://en/248988) (that is,$\partial(u\cdot v)=\partial u\cdot v+u\cdot\partial v$) for every two elements $u$ and $v$ in $F$. The [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) over $\mathbb{Q}$ of $\mathbb{C}$ form a basic examples of differential fields, when equipped with the usual derivative.

An element $h$ of $F$ is a constant if $\partial h=0$. The constants of $F$ form a differential field with zero derivative. Care must be taken that a differential field extension of a differential field may enlarge the field of constants.

A function $u$ of a differential extension $G$ of a differential field $F$ is an **elementary function** over $F$ if it belongs to a finite chain (for inclusion) of differential subfields of $G$ that starts from $F$ and is such that each is generated over the preceding one by a function that is either

- [algebraic](wikipedia://en/974169) over the preceding field, or
- an *exponential*, that is, $\partial u = u\partial a$ for some $a$ belonging to a prior subfield, or
- a *logarithm*, that is, $\partial u = \partial a/a$ for some $a$ belonging to a prior subfield, (see [Liouville's theorem](wikipedia://en/9482601))

With this definition, the usual elementary functions are exactly the function that are elementary over the field of the [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210). This generalized definition allows considering every transcendental function as elementary for applying Liouville's theorem.
